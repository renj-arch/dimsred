Mathematical Biology — Models of Life

Mathematical biology uses mathematical models to understand biological systems — from population dynamics and epidemiology to pattern formation, neural networks, and evolutionary game theory. The field combines differential equations, stochastic processes, dynamical systems, and computational methods to describe the mechanisms of life at scales from molecules to ecosystems.

"The essential fact is that all the phenomena of life are governed by mathematical laws."
— D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson, On Growth and Form (1917)
Part 1

🐺 Population Dynamics — Predators and Prey

Growth
Lotka-Volterra and Logistic Growth — The Classics
The simplest model of population growth is exponential dN/dt = rN, but real populations face limits. The logistic equation dN/dt = rN(1−N/K) introduces carrying capacity K. The Lotka-Volterra predator-prey model: dN/dt = N(a − bP), dP/dt = P(cN − d), where N is prey, P is predator. This system has a conserved quantity H = cN − d ln N + bP − a ln P, giving closed orbits in phase space — predator and prey populations oscillate. The model is structurally unstable (small perturbations change behavior). Competition models extend Lotka-Volterra to n species: dN_i/dt = N_i(r_i − Σ a_{ij}N_j). Allee effects add a minimum viable population size — below which extinction occurs. The reproductive number R₀ determines whether a population grows or declines. Age-structured models (Leslie matrices) track population by age class.
Even the simplest predator-prey equations produce elegant oscillations — but real ecosystems add complexity that makes prediction challenging.
Part 2

🦠 Epidemiology — The Mathematics of Disease Spread

SIR
SIR Models, R₀, and the Dynamics of Epidemics
The classic SIR model (Kermack-McKendrick, 1927): dS/dt = −βSI, dI/dt = βSI − γI, dR/dt = γI, where S = susceptible, I = infected, R = recovered. The basic reproduction number R₀ = βS₀/γ determines outbreak: if R₀ > 1, infection grows; if R₀ < 1, it dies out. The final size relation: S(∞) = S(0)e^{−R₀(1−S(∞)/N)} gives total infected in an unmitigated epidemic. SEIR adds an exposed (latent) compartment for diseases like measles and COVID-19. Spatial epidemic models (reaction-diffusion) describe spread through space: ∂S/∂t = −βSI + D_S∇²S. The Kermack-McKendrick threshold theorem: an epidemic occurs iff R₀ > 1. Stochastic SIR models account for chance effects in small populations — extinction can happen even with R₀ > 1. Vaccination thresholds: herd immunity requires vaccination coverage > 1 − 1/R₀. Branching processes model early epidemic growth as a birth-death process.
R₀ is the single most important number in epidemiology — it tells you whether a disease will spread and what vaccination coverage is needed.
Part 3

🔬 Pattern Formation — Turing Instabilities and Morphogenesis

Patterns
Turing Patterns — How Chemistry Creates Biological Form
How do zebras get stripes and leopards get spots? Alan Turing (1952): a system of two diffusing chemicals (morphogens) can spontaneously form spatial patterns from a uniform state through diffusion-driven instability. The activator-inhibitor model: ∂u/∂t = f(u,v) + D_u∇²u, ∂v/∂t = g(u,v) + D_v∇²v, where u (activator) promotes itself and v (inhibitor), v inhibits u. If D_v > D_u (inhibitor diffuses faster), the uniform state can destabilize, producing stationary patterns with characteristic wavelength. The Swift-Hohenberg equation ∂u/∂t = ru − (1+∇²)²u + u³ is a simpler pattern-forming PDE. Reaction-diffusion systems produce spots, stripes, labyrinths, and target patterns — the pattern depends on the domain geometry and parameters. The Gray-Scott model and Brusselator are canonical pattern-forming systems. Chemotaxis (Keller-Segel model) describes how cells move toward chemical signals — it can produce aggregation patterns through the chemotactic instability.
Turing showed that diffusion — usually a smoothing process — can actually create patterns when combined with reaction. This is the mathematics of how embryos develop form.
Part 4

🧬 Evolutionary Game Theory and Systems Biology

Evolution
Evolutionary Stable Strategies, Gene Circuits, and Neural Dynamics
Evolutionary game theory (Maynard Smith) models strategy evolution in populations. The replicator equation: dx_i/dt = x_i(f_i − φ), where x_i is the frequency of strategy i, f_i its fitness, φ the average fitness. An evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) cannot be invaded by rare mutants — it's a refinement of Nash equilibrium. The Hawk-Dove game and Prisoner's Dilemma are classic examples. Gene regulatory networks are modeled by ODEs: dX/dt = f(X) − γX where f is a Hill-type activation/repression function. The lac operon (Jacob-Monod) is the classic example — a switch with bistability. Neural dynamics: the Hodgkin-Huxley model describes action potentials via ion channel kinetics and gating variables. The FitzHugh-Nagumo model is a simpler 2-variable reduction. The Moran process models finite-population evolution with mutation and selection. Cancer modeling views tumors as evolving ecosystems with competition for resources, immune evasion, and therapy resistance.
Evolutionary game theory and systems biology show that the mathematics of life is the mathematics of feedback, selection, and nonlinear dynamics.
