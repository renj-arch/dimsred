Langlands Program — Automorphic Forms, Galois Representations & the Functoriality Conjecture

Deep dive into the Langlands Program — the grand unifying conjecture in number theory and representation theory connecting automorphic forms (holomorphic modular forms, Maass forms, automorphic representations of adelic groups GL(n, A_F) — the space of cusp forms decomposes into a direct sum of irreducible automorphic representations π = ⊗_v π_v), L-functions (the Hecke L-functions, the automorphic L-functions L(s, π) defined via Euler products of local Langlands parameters, the Artin L-functions for Galois representations, the functional equation and the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture), the Langlands correspondence (for a number field F, the global Langlands correspondence functorially associates an n-dimensional ℓ-adic Galois representation σ: Gal(F̄/F) → GL(n, ℚ̄_ℓ) to each cuspidal automorphic representation π of GL(n, A_F) — the Langlands reciprocity conjecture), the Arthur–Selberg trace formula (a non-abelian generalization of the Poisson summation formula that relates spectral data on the automorphic side to orbital integrals on the geometric side, used to prove cases of functoriality), the Langlands functoriality conjecture (for any homomorphism of L-groups LG → LH, there is a corresponding transfer of automorphic representations from G to H), and the geometric Langlands correspondence (the function-field analogue where the Galois group is replaced by the fundamental group of a Riemann surface, and automorphic forms are replaced by D-modules on the moduli stack of G-bundles).

"The Langlands program is a far-reaching generalization of class field theory that connects number theory, representation theory, and harmonic analysis."
— Robert Langlands
Part 1

📜 Automorphic Forms and L-Functions

Automorphic Forms
Modular Forms, Automorphic Representations, and L-Functions
A modular form of weight k for SL(2, ℤ) is a holomorphic function f: ℍ → ℂ satisfying f((az+b)/(cz+d)) = (cz+d)^k f(z) with a Fourier expansion f(z) = Σ_{n=0}∞ a_n e^{2πi n z}. A cusp form has a₀ = 0. The Hecke operators T_p act on the space of cusp forms; eigenforms satisfy T_p f = λ_p f, and the associated L-function L(s, f) = Σ_{n=1}∞ a_n n^{-s} has an Euler product L(s, f) = ∏_p (1 − a_p p^{-s} + p^{k−1−2s})^{-1} for Re(s) > (k+1)/2. The functional equation relates L(s, f) to L(k−s, f). The Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture (proved by Deligne as a consequence of the Weil conjectures) states |a_p| ≤ 2p^{(k−1)/2}. For number fields F, an automorphic representation π = ⊗_v' π_v of GL(n, A_F) is an irreducible admissible representation of GL(n, A_F) occurring in the space of cusp forms L²_cusp(GL(n, F)\GL(n, A_F)). The automorphic L-function L(s, π) = ∏_v L(s, π_v) converges for Re(s) sufficiently large and has meromorphic continuation and a functional equation (proved by Godement–Jacquet). The Langlands–Shahidi method uses Eisenstein series to define L-functions for generic representations of classical groups. The Langlands conjecture: every L-function arising from an automorphic representation is an Artin L-function for some Galois representation — linking number theory to representation theory.
Automorphic forms are harmonic-analytic objects on arithmetic quotients; their L-functions encode deep arithmetic information and satisfy functional equations mirroring the Riemann zeta function.
Part 2

🔗 Galois Representations and the Langlands Correspondence

Galois
ℓ-adic Galois Representations, the Global Langlands Correspondence, and the Reciprocity Conjecture
A Galois representation is a continuous homomorphism σ: Gal(F̄/F) → GL(n, ℚ̄_ℓ). The Artin L-function L(s, σ) = ∏_p det(1 − σ(Frob_p) N(p)^{-s})^{-1} generalizes the Dedekind zeta function. The global Langlands correspondence for GL(n) predicts a bijection between: (1) Cuspidal automorphic representations π of GL(n, A_F) (up to equivalence), (2) n-dimensional ℓ-adic Galois representations σ: Gal(F̄/F) → GL(n, ℚ̄_ℓ) (up to equivalence, with certain compatibility conditions), such that at almost all places v, the local Langlands parameter of π_v matches the restriction of σ to the decomposition group at v: L(s, π_v) = det(1 − σ(Frob_v) N(v)^{-s})^{-1} at unramified places. For n = 1, this is class field theory (the Artin reciprocity law): the abelianization of the Galois group Gal(F̄/F)^{ab} is isomorphic to the idele class group F^×\A_F^×. The proof of the modularity theorem (Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture) by Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor (1994–2001) establishes the Langlands correspondence for GL(2) over ℚ for all elliptic curves over ℚ — every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular. The potential modularity results (Taylor, 2000s) and the proof of the Sato–Tate conjecture (Barnet-Lamb–Geraghty–Harris–Taylor, 2011) for elliptic curves use the Langlands–Taylor correspondence for GL(2) over totally real fields. The Harris–Taylor theorem (2001) proves the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over p-adic fields via the deformation theory of Shimura varieties.
The Langlands correspondence is the non-abelian generalization of class field theory, associating automorphic representations to Galois representations and matching their L-functions.
Part 3

🧩 The Functoriality Conjecture and the Arthur–Selberg Trace Formula

Functoriality
Langlands Functoriality, the Trace Formula, and Endoscopy
The Langlands functoriality conjecture is the central conjecture of the Langlands program. Let G and H be reductive groups over a number field F, and let LG → LH be an L-homomorphism (a homomorphism of Langlands dual groups). Then for every automorphic representation π of G(A_F), there exists an automorphic representation Π of H(A_F) (the Langlands transfer or functorial lift) such that L(s, π, r) = L(s, Π) for every representation r of LG. The Arthur–Selberg trace formula is the primary tool for proving cases of functoriality. It equates the trace of a test function f on the automorphic representation space L²(G(F)\G(A_F)) to a sum of orbital integrals on the geometric side: Σ_O J_O(f) (the spectral side) = Σ_{γ} a_γ(f) (the geometric side). The invariant trace formula (Arthur, 1981) refines this to a stable trace formula, essential for endoscopy. Endoscopy (Langlands–Shelstad, Kottwitz, Arthur): the transfer of automorphic representations from G to H often factors through an elliptic endoscopic group H, and the fundamental lemma (proved by Ngo Bao Chau, 2010 — Fields Medal) establishes the equality of orbital integrals required for the trace formula comparison. The Arthur classification (Arthur, 2010) proves the Langlands functoriality for classical groups (symplectic, orthogonal, unitary) to GL(n) using the trace formula, establishing the endoscopic classification of representations of classical groups.
Functoriality is the overarching conjecture that predicts transfers of automorphic representations between groups; the trace formula provides analytic machinery to prove it.

🌐 Geometric Langlands and Categorical Representation Theory

Geometric
The Geometric Langlands Correspondence, Hecke Eigensheaves, and D-Modules
The geometric Langlands correspondence (Drinfeld, Laumon, Beilinson–Drinfeld, Gaitsgory) is the function-field analogue where the number field F is replaced by the function field ℂ(C) of a smooth projective algebraic curve C. Let G be a connected reductive algebraic group over ℂ, and let LG be its Langlands dual. The moduli stack of G-bundles Bun_G(C) replaces the adelic quotient G(F)\G(A_F). The Hecke correspondences are moduli spaces of G-bundles with a modification at a point x ∈ C. The Hecke operators act on the derived category of D-modules on Bun_G(C). A Hecke eigensheaf is a D-module F on Bun_G(C) such that the Hecke operator H_x(F) ≅ σ(x) ⊗ F, where σ: π₁(C) → LG(ℂ) is a local system (the geometric analogue of a Galois representation). The geometric Langlands correspondence states an equivalence of derived categories: D^b(Mod_{D}(Bun_G(C))) ≅ D^b(Coh(Loc_{^LG}(C))) — D-modules on the moduli stack of G-bundles correspond to coherent sheaves on the moduli stack of LG-local systems on C. This is a categorical Langlands correspondence (Gaitsgory–Rozenblyum, Arinkin–Gaitsgory). The quantum geometric Langlands correspondence (Kapustin–Witten, 2006) interprets this as S-duality of N = 4 super Yang–Mills theory. The Beilinson–Drinfeld theorem: for G = GL(n), the geometric Langlands correspondence is an equivalence of derived categories — proven via the construction of the geometric Eisenstein series and the vanishing conjecture (Gaitsgory et al.). The applications include the proof of the Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture (using perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian), the geometric Satake equivalence (the Satake isomorphism categorified via the affine Grassmannian), and the categorical representation theory of loop groups.
Geometric Langlands translates the classical Langlands program into the language of algebraic geometry, replacing automorphic representations with D-modules and Galois representations with local systems.

Local Langlands Correspondence for GL(n) and Supercuspidal Representations

The local Langlands correspondence (LLC) for GL(n) over a p‑adic field F matches irreducible smooth representations of GL(n,F) with n‑dimensional Frobenius‑semisimple Weil–Deligne representations of the Weil group W_F. The correspondence was established in full generality by Harris–Taylor (2001) using the cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower and by Henniart (2000) via a characterization by local factors. A third proof, due to Scholze (2013), employs perfectoid spaces and the Lubin–Tate tower. All three proofs yield the same bijection, which preserves L‑functions, epsilon factors, and gamma factors for pairs of representations.

Supercuspidal representations, the building blocks of the Bernstein decomposition, correspond under LLC to irreducible Weil–Deligne representations. Their explicit construction was achieved by Bushnell–Kutzko (1993) through the theory of simple types and covers. This classification shows that every supercuspidal of GL(n,F) arises from a maximal simple type attached to a field extension of F together with a character of a compact open subgroup. Compatibility with LLC is expressed by the equality of the local Langlands parameter attached to the type and the Weil–Deligne representation obtained from the character via local class field theory.

The LLC for GL(n) is functorial with respect to the standard L‑homomorphisms of the dual group GL(n,ℂ). In particular, the correspondence commutes with parabolic induction (the Zelevinsky classification) and with contragredients. This functoriality is a crucial input for the global functoriality conjecture and for the stabilization of the trace formula for general linear groups.

Endoscopic Classification of Classical Groups via the Stabilized Trace Formula

Arthur’s endoscopic classification (2013) provides a complete description of the discrete automorphic spectrum of classical groups — symplectic, orthogonal, and unitary groups — over a number field. The classification is expressed in terms of global Arthur parameters, which are formal sums of cuspidal automorphic representations of GL(m) satisfying certain self‑duality and parity conditions. Each parameter determines a finite set of automorphic representations of the classical group, called an Arthur packet, and the multiplicity of each representation in the discrete spectrum is given by a formula involving the component group of the parameter.

The proof rests on the stabilization of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula. The invariant trace formula for a classical group G is compared with the stable trace formula for its elliptic endoscopic groups H. The fundamental lemma, proved by Ngô Bảo Châu (2010) for a broad class of reductive groups, supplies the equality of orbital integrals needed for the comparison. The transfer of test functions from G to H is effected by the Langlands–Shelstad transfer factors, explicitly constructed using the theory of endoscopy. The stabilized trace formula then yields an identity between the spectral side for G and a sum of stable spectral sides for the endoscopic groups H.

By induction on the rank of the group, Arthur shows that the stable trace formula for G can be expressed entirely in terms of automorphic representations of GL(n). This reduces the classification of representations of classical groups to the known classification for GL(n) and to the combinatorics of Arthur parameters. The result also provides a candidate for the local Langlands correspondence for classical groups, because the local packets are defined by the same parameters and the local character relations are derived from the global trace formula.

Worked Example: Constructing an Arthur Parameter for SO(5)
Let π be a cuspidal automorphic representation of GL(2) over ℚ with trivial central character and symmetric square L‑function having a pole at s=1. Explain how π contributes to an Arthur parameter for the split group SO(5) and determine the expected size of the associated global Arthur packet.
The symmetric square pole forces π to be a functorial lift from a cuspidal representation of SO(3) ≅ PGL(2) (Gelbart–Jacquet). The Arthur parameter for SO(5) is then ψ = π ⊠ [2], where [k] denotes the k‑dimensional irreducible representation of SL(2,ℂ). Here π is orthogonal (symmetric square pole), so π ⊠ [2] is a 4‑dimensional symplectic representation of the Arthur parameter group, landing in the dual group Sp(4,ℂ). The parameter is self‑dual of symplectic type, satisfying the parity condition for SO(5). The centralizer of ψ in Sp(4,ℂ) is finite, so the component group S_ψ is trivial; the global Arthur packet contains exactly one representation, which is the functorial lift of π to SO(5) and is generic.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the structure of an Arthur packet, first identify the global Arthur parameter by listing its GL(m) constituents and the SL(2) multiplicities. Then compute the component group S_ψ = π₀(Cent(ψ)/Z(Ĝ)). The number of irreducible characters of S_ψ equals the packet size. Remember that for classical groups the packet always contains a unique generic member if the parameter is tempered.

Shimura Varieties and the Langlands–Kottwitz Method for Galois Representations

Shimura varieties are higher‑dimensional analogues of modular curves attached to reductive groups G over ℚ that admit a Hermitian symmetric domain. Their ℓ‑adic étale cohomology carries an action of G(𝔸_f) × Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ). The Langlands–Kottwitz method (1990s) expresses the Frobenius traces on this cohomology in terms of orbital integrals on G and stable orbital integrals on endoscopic groups H. This yields a precise link between the Galois representations appearing in the cohomology and automorphic representations of G.

The key input is the Kottwitz fixed‑point formula, which computes the trace of a Hecke operator composed with a geometric Frobenius element as a sum over conjugacy classes in G(ℚ) of orbital integrals weighted by the number of fixed points. By stabilizing this formula using the fundamental lemma, one obtains an identity between the trace on the Shimura variety and a stable trace formula for the endoscopic groups. The stable trace formula is then evaluated using the known classification of automorphic representations for GL(n) (via the work of Arthur and others).

This method was used by Kottwitz (1992) to establish cases of the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over p‑adic fields for certain supercuspidal representations, and by Harris–Taylor (2001) to establish the full correspondence. More recently, Scholze (2015) extended the method to the torsion cohomology of Shimura varieties, giving access to mod‑ℓ Galois representations and to the weight‑zero part of the cohomology, which is crucial for the p‑adic Langlands program.

The Relative Langlands Program: Period Integrals and Distinguished Representations

The relative Langlands program studies the restriction of automorphic representations from a group G to a subgroup H, typically a symmetric subgroup such as GL(n) × GL(n) ⊂ GL(2n) or a unitary group inside a general linear group. The central object is the period integral ∫H(F)H(𝔸) φ(h) dh for φ in the space of an automorphic representation π of G. Non‑vanishing of this period is conjectured to be governed by an L‑function value (the Gan–Gross–Prasad conjecture) and by the existence of a distinguished local component at each place.

At the local level, a representation π_v of G(F_v) is called H(F_v)‑distinguished if it admits a non‑zero H(F_v)‑invariant linear form. The local Gan–Gross–Prasad conjecture (proved in many cases by Waldspurger, Beuzart‑Plessis, and others) predicts that π_v is distinguished if and only if the local root number ε(½, π_v, r) equals a specific sign determined by the Langlands parameter of π_v and the embedding of the dual groups. The global conjecture then asserts that the period integral factorizes as a product of local invariant linear forms times the central value L(½, π, r).

Recent breakthroughs include the proof of the global Gan–Gross–Prasad conjecture for unitary groups by Beuzart‑Plessis–Liu–Zhang–Zhu (2020) using the relative trace formula, and the extension to Bessel and Fourier–Jacobi periods by Cai–Friedberg–Ginzburg–Kaplan. These results provide a precise spectral interpretation of period integrals and have applications to the non‑vanishing of central L‑values, to the construction of p‑adic L‑functions, and to the refinement of the functoriality conjecture for non‑standard L‑homomorphisms.

Categorical Geometric Langlands and the Gaitsgory–Lurie Proof

The categorical geometric Langlands correspondence, formulated by Gaitsgory and Lurie (2019), replaces the derived category of D‑modules on the moduli stack Bun_G(C) with the ind‑coherent derived category IndCohN(Loc^L G(C)) of sheaves on the stack of ^L G‑local systems with nilpotent singular support. The equivalence is constructed as a monoidal functor compatible with the Hecke actions on both sides. The proof proceeds by first establishing the correspondence for the group GL(1) (the abelian case) using the Fourier–Mukai transform on the Picard stack, and then inductively extending to GL(n) via the geometry of the affine Grassmannian and the factorization homology of the Ran space.

A crucial technical tool is the theory of D‑modules on ind‑schemes, developed by Gaitsgory–Rozenblyum, which allows one to define the Hecke functors as correspondences on Bun_G(C) and to prove their compatibility with the convolution product on the Satake category. The geometric Satake equivalence, proved by Mirković–Vilonen (2007), identifies the Satake category with the representation category of the Langlands dual group ^L G. This identification is used to construct the Hecke eigensheaf property for the image of a local system under the correspondence.

The categorical formulation also clarifies the role of the Whittaker normalization: the Whittaker category on Bun_G(C) is equivalent to the category of ind‑coherent sheaves on the stack of ^L G‑local systems with a fixed generic reduction. This equivalence is the geometric analogue of the uniqueness of Whittaker models for automorphic representations. The Gaitsgory–Lurie proof has been extended to all reductive groups by Gaitsgory–Raskin (2022) using the theory of categorical traces and the Drinfeld–Lafforgue–Vinberg degeneration.

  • Confusing the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) with the global correspondence; the local bijection is a theorem, the global functoriality remains conjectural for most groups.
  • Assuming the fundamental lemma is proved only for unitary groups; Ngô’s proof covers a broad class of reductive groups.
  • Overlooking the role of the SL(2) factor in Arthur parameters; the multiplicities [k] encode the unipotent part of the parameter and determine the packet size.
  • Treating period integrals as always factorizable; the factorization holds only under the local distinction hypothesis and for tempered representations.
  • Believing the categorical geometric Langlands equivalence is a simple derived equivalence; it is an equivalence of monoidal ∞‑categories with nilpotent singular support conditions.
  • Neglecting the difference between the stable trace formula and the invariant trace formula; stabilization is the process of rewriting the invariant formula in terms of stable orbital integrals on endoscopic groups.
  • Misidentifying the component group S_ψ as the center of the dual group; S_ψ is the group of connected components of the centralizer of the parameter modulo the center of the dual group.

The advanced topics above illustrate how the Langlands program intertwines representation theory, algebraic geometry, and number theory at the research frontier.

The Langlands–Shahidi Method and the Analytic Properties of L‑Functions

The Langlands–Shahidi method attaches L‑functions to automorphic representations of reductive groups via the constant terms of Eisenstein series. For a maximal parabolic P = MN of a split reductive group G, the normalized intertwining operator A(s, π, w) attached to a generic representation π of M(A) satisfies a functional equation whose normalizing factor is a product of L‑functions L(s, π, r_i), where r_i are the irreducible constituents of the adjoint action of the L‑group ^L M on the Lie algebra of the unipotent radical of ^L P. Shahidi established that these L‑functions satisfy the expected analytic properties: meromorphic continuation to ℂ, a functional equation relating s to 1−s, and bounds in vertical strips that imply the Ramanujan conjecture for the corresponding automorphic representations when the group is quasi‑split. The method also yields the local Langlands correspondence for generic representations of classical groups by identifying the local coefficient with a product of local γ‑factors. Globally, the non‑vanishing of L(1, π, r) for the standard representation r of ^L G is equivalent to the non‑vanishing of certain Fourier coefficients of Eisenstein series, which controls the poles of the Eisenstein series and the structure of the residual spectrum. This approach has been extended to twisted endoscopy, exterior and symmetric square L‑functions for GL(n), and Asai L‑functions for unitary groups, providing a uniform framework underlying many cases of functoriality proved by Cogdell, Kim, Piatetski‑Shapiro, and Shahidi.

The Local Langlands Correspondence for Classical Groups via the Kaletha–Mínguez–Shin–White Construction

The local Langlands correspondence for classical groups (symplectic, orthogonal, and unitary) was established for all pure inner forms by Kaletha, Mínguez, Shin, and White (2020) using rigid inner twists and Arthur’s endoscopic classification. Their construction starts from a Langlands parameter φ : W_F × SL₂(ℂ) → ^L G, where ^L G is the L‑group of a pure inner form of a classical group. The parameter determines an Arthur parameter ψ = φ ⊗ 1SL₂ and a centralizer S_ψ = Cent(ψ, ^L G)/Z(^L G)^Γ. The packet Π_ψ is the set of irreducible representations of the pure inner forms of G(F) corresponding to characters of the component group π₀(S_ψ). The key innovation is the “KMSW” normalization of local intertwining operators, matching the normalization of global intertwining operators in Arthur’s trace formula. This compatibility ensures that local packets satisfy the required character identities with stable distributions on endoscopic groups. The construction covers all pure inner forms simultaneously, including non‑quasi‑split groups. As a consequence, the local correspondence is compatible with parabolic induction, with the formation of L‑packets for tempered representations, and with the expected behavior under local Langlands functoriality for the standard, exterior square, and symmetric square maps. The proof relies on the stabilization of the twisted trace formula for GL_n, the fundamental lemma for Lie algebras (proved by Ngô), and the comparison of trace formulas for inner forms.

Worked Example: Matching Local Factors in the Langlands–Shahidi Method
Let π be a generic cuspidal automorphic representation of GL(2, A_ℚ) and consider the maximal parabolic of GSp(4) with Levi M ≅ GL(2) × GL(1). Using the Langlands–Shahidi method, identify the normalizing factor of the standard intertwining operator attached to π ⊗ χ and write it as a product of L‑functions.
The adjoint action of ^L M = GL(2, ℂ) × GL(1, ℂ) on the Lie algebra of the unipotent radical of ^L P decomposes into the standard representation std ⊗ χ of GL(2, ℂ) and the determinant character det ⊗ χ². Hence the normalizing factor is L(s, π × χ, std) · L(2s, χ²). The first factor is the Rankin–Selberg L‑function L(s, π × χ) and the second is the Hecke L‑function of χ². The functional equation of the intertwining operator translates into the functional equations of these two L‑functions, confirming the expected analytic properties.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the normalizing factor of an intertwining operator in the Langlands–Shahidi method, first write down the Levi subgroup and its L‑group, then decompose the adjoint action on the unipotent radical. The irreducible constituents give the L‑functions directly; no need to recall the full Eisenstein series.

p‑adic Hodge Theory and the de Rham Comparison in the Langlands Program

The p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚ_p) and its generalizations rely heavily on p‑adic Hodge theory, which relates p‑adic Galois representations to linear algebraic data such as filtered φ‑modules (Fontaine’s D_cris, D_st) and (φ, Γ)‑modules. For a de Rham representation V of Gℚ_p, the functor D_dR(V) = (V ⊗ℚ_p B_dR)Gℚ_p yields a filtered vector space whose Hodge–Tate weights encode the Hodge filtration of the associated p‑adic modular form. The work of Berger, Breuil, Colmez, and Kisin shows that the category of two‑dimensional crystalline representations with Hodge–Tate weights {0, k−1} is equivalent to the category of p‑adic Banach space representations of GL₂(ℚ_p) that are locally analytic and admit a unitary completion. This equivalence is realized by Colmez’s p‑adic local Langlands correspondence, which sends a crystalline representation V to a locally analytic representation Π(V) of GL₂(ℚ_p) whose locally algebraic vectors correspond to classical automorphic forms of weight k. The de Rham comparison isomorphism V ⊗ B_dR ≅ D_dR(V) ⊗ B_dR is compatible with the action of Hecke operators, and the (φ, Γ)‑module D_rig(V) provides a bridge to the theory of (φ, Γ)‑modules over the Robba ring, which controls the variation of p‑adic families. In higher rank, the work of Emerton, Helm, and others on the p‑adic Langlands program for GL_n uses locally analytic vectors in completed cohomology of Shimura varieties, together with the p‑adic Hodge theory of the associated Galois representations, to construct a correspondence for potentially semi‑stable representations. The key technical input is the p‑adic Simpson correspondence of Faltings and the theory of prismatic cohomology developed by Bhatt and Scholze, which gives a uniform framework for comparing p‑adic étale cohomology with de Rham cohomology in families.

The Modularity Theorem and its Generalizations to Higher Rank Groups

The modularity theorem, formerly the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture, asserts that every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular, i.e. its L‑function coincides with the L‑function of a weight‑two cuspidal newform on GL₂(A_ℚ). The proof by Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, and Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor established the correspondence for semistable elliptic curves and then removed the semistability condition. The method uses the deformation theory of Galois representations and the Taylor–Wiles patching argument to prove an R = T theorem for the universal deformation ring R of a residual representation ρ̄ : G_ℚ → GL₂(𝔽_p) and the Hecke algebra T acting on the space of modular forms of a given level. Generalizations to higher rank groups aim to associate to a compatible system of ℓ‑adic Galois representations ρ : G_ℚ → ^L G(ℚ_ℓ) an automorphic representation π of G(A_ℚ) such that the Satake parameters of π match the Frobenius eigenvalues of ρ. For G = GL_n, the work of Harris–Taylor, Henniart, and Scholze proves the local Langlands correspondence and, combined with global arguments of Clozel, Harris, Taylor, and others, yields modularity for regular, algebraic, essentially self‑dual, cuspidal automorphic representations (RAESDC). For classical groups, Arthur’s endoscopic classification provides a conditional modularity statement: if a Galois representation satisfies the expected local‑global compatibility and the relevant local Langlands correspondence is known, then it arises from an automorphic representation on a pure inner form of the classical group. Recent advances by Caraiani, Scholze, and others on the cohomology of Shimura varieties with torsion coefficients, together with the theory of perfectoid spaces, have extended modularity lifting theorems to non‑regular weights and to groups without discrete series, bringing the program closer to a full higher‑rank modularity theorem.

The Geometric Langlands Correspondence over Function Fields and the Drinfeld–Lafforgue Theorem

Over a smooth projective curve X over a finite field 𝔽_q, the geometric Langlands correspondence relates D‑modules on the moduli stack Bun_G of G‑bundles to quasi‑coherent sheaves on the stack Loc^L G of ^L G‑local systems. Drinfeld proved the correspondence for GL₂ in the 1980s using the theory of shtukas and the geometry of the moduli stack of rank‑two vector bundles with level structure. Lafforgue extended the result to GL_n for any n, establishing a bijection between irreducible cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n(𝔽_q(X)) and irreducible ℓ‑adic Galois representations of the étale fundamental group of X of dimension n. The proof uses the cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas, the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula, and Deligne’s purity theorem to control the eigenvalues of Frobenius. The geometric Langlands program over function fields is thus a theorem for GL_n, not a conjecture, and it provides a geometric incarnation of the global Langlands correspondence. For general reductive groups, the geometric Langlands conjecture predicts an equivalence of derived categories D(Bun_G) ≅ QCoh(Loc^L G) compatible with Hecke functors. Recent work of Gaitsgory, Lurie, and Raskin has proved a categorical version for all reductive groups over curves over algebraically closed fields of characteristic zero, using the theory of ind‑coherent sheaves with nilpotent singular support and the Drinfeld–Lafforgue–Vinberg degeneration. Over finite fields, the function‑field analogue of the categorical equivalence remains open, but the Drinfeld–Lafforgue theorem gives a concrete spectral decomposition of the space of automorphic forms in terms of the cohomology of shtuka varieties, which is the function‑field counterpart of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula.

  • Assuming the local Langlands correspondence for classical groups is a simple bijection; it is a partition into L‑packets indexed by characters of a component group.
  • Treating p‑adic Hodge theory as only relevant for GL₂; the de Rham comparison and (φ, Γ)‑modules are essential for the p‑adic Langlands program in any rank.
  • Believing the modularity theorem is proved for all elliptic curves over totally real fields; the full Hilbert modularity result is still conditional on the generalized Ramanujan conjecture.
  • Overlooking that the geometric Langlands correspondence over function fields is a theorem for GL_n but remains conjectural for other groups.
  • Mixing up the categorical geometric Langlands equivalence (an equivalence of monoidal ∞‑categories) with the classical derived equivalence of D‑modules and quasi‑coherent sheaves.

The Fundamental Lemma and Ngô’s Proof: Stabilization of the Trace Formula

The fundamental lemma is a combinatorial identity between orbital integrals on a reductive group G and its endoscopic groups H. Conjectured by Langlands and Shelstad in the 1980s, it is the key step in stabilizing the Arthur–Selberg trace formula. Stabilization decomposes the geometric side into contributions from elliptic endoscopic data, enabling comparison of trace formulas for G and its endoscopic groups—the engine of the endoscopic classification of automorphic representations.

Waldspurger reduced the fundamental lemma to a Lie-algebra transfer statement, proving many cases via Shalika germ expansions. The remaining cases required a geometric interpretation. Ngô Bảo Châu, building on Laumon's ideas and the geometric Langlands program, identified orbital integrals with intersection cohomology of the Hitchin fibration on the moduli stack of G-Higgs bundles. He proved the lemma for all reductive groups over local fields of characteristic zero; Cluckers–Hales–Loeser extended it to sufficiently large positive characteristic.

The proof uses the decomposition theorem for perverse sheaves on the Hitchin base, Ngô's support theorem, and the properness of the Hitchin fibration with a section. The orbital integral identity becomes an identity of stalks of intersection cohomology complexes, following from purity and the fibration's symmetry under the dual group action. Ngô's work earned the 2010 Fields Medal, completed stabilization for all connected reductive groups, and enabled Arthur's endoscopic classification for classical groups and Mok's work on unitary groups.

In practice, the fundamental lemma matches Hecke eigenvalues of automorphic forms on G with those on its endoscopic groups. This matching underpins functoriality for classical groups and the trace-formula proof of the local Langlands correspondence for many groups.

The Local Langlands Correspondence for Real Reductive Groups: Vogan’s Classification and the Langlands–Shelstad Correspondence

For a connected reductive group G over ℝ, the local Langlands correspondence predicts a finite-to-one map from irreducible admissible representations of G(ℝ) to Langlands parameters—homomorphisms W_ℝ → ^L G satisfying certain conditions. Langlands classified these representations via standard modules induced from tempered representations of Levi subgroups. Shelstad defined L-packets as finite sets of representations sharing a parameter, indexed by characters of the component group S_φ = π₀(Cent^L G(φ))/Z(L G)^Γ.

Vogan gave a geometric realization using (𝔤, K)-modules, showing irreducible admissible representations correspond to equivariant perverse sheaves on the parameter variety, with L-packet structure from the centralizer's component group. The Knapp–Zuckerman classification via cohomological induction explicitly describes packets for real groups. For G = SL₂(ℝ), discrete series correspond to parameters φ|_ℂ^× = z ↦ (z/|z|)^k with trivial component group, so packets are singletons. For groups with disconnected center like SO(p,q), the component group can be non-trivial, yielding packets of size ≥2.

The correspondence is now a theorem for all real reductive groups, combining work of Langlands, Shelstad, Vogan, and Arthur. It is compatible with parabolic induction, temperedness, and the complex dual group correspondence. The real case is the only archimedean place where the correspondence is completely known and unconditional.

Identifying the correct L‑parameter for a given representation
Let π be the irreducible admissible representation of SL₂(ℝ) with infinitesimal character λ = 3 and trivial central character. Determine its Langlands parameter φ : W_ℝ → SL₂(ℂ).
The Weil group W_ℝ is generated by ℂ^× and an element j with j² = −1, jzj⁻¹ = z. A Langlands parameter for SL₂(ℝ) is determined by its restriction to ℂ^×, which must be of the form z ↦ (z/|z|)^k ⊕ (z/|z|)-k with k ∈ ℤ≥0. In a standard normalization, the infinitesimal character λ corresponds to k = λ − 1 = 2. Since the central character is trivial, the parameter lands in SL₂(ℂ). Thus φ(z) = diag((z/|z|)², (z/|z|)-2) and φ(j) = antidiag(1, −1). This parameter is discrete series, and the L‑packet contains only π.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for the Langlands parameter of a real representation, first identify the group and its dual. Write down the Weil group structure, then match the infinitesimal character to the exponent k in the restriction to ℂ^×. Check central character to ensure the image lies in the derived subgroup of the dual group. Remember that for SL₂(ℝ) the component group is trivial, so the packet size is one.

The p‑adic Langlands Correspondence for GL₂(ℚₚ) and the Colmez Functor

The p-adic Langlands program seeks a correspondence between continuous p-adic Galois representations of Gℚₚ and admissible unitary Banach representations of p-adic reductive groups. For GL₂(ℚₚ), Colmez constructed this correspondence, building on Berger, Breuil, and Fontaine. The Colmez functor assigns to a 2-dimensional p-adic representation V a locally analytic representation Π(V) of GL₂(ℚₚ) on a p-adic Banach space. If V is de Rham with distinct Hodge–Tate weights, Π(V) is a unitary completion of a locally algebraic representation; if V is trianguline, Π(V) is described via (φ,Γ)-modules.

The construction uses (φ,Γ)-modules over the Robba ring to translate Galois representations into p-adic differential equations. The functor is compatible with the mod-p local Langlands correspondence and the Breuil–Schneider formulation. It is exact, commutes with twists, and sends crystalline representations to unitary completions of locally algebraic principal series or Steinberg representations. Emerton, Helm, and others extended the correspondence to GLn(ℚₚ) via completed cohomology of Shimura varieties and eigenvarieties.

A key application is the non-crystalline case for GL₂(ℚₚ), where the Colmez functor produces non-locally-algebraic representations with natural GL₂(ℚₚ)-action. This has deep implications for higher-rank p-adic Langlands and the p-adic Gan–Gross–Prasad conjectures.

The Jacquet–Langlands Correspondence and Inner Forms: Transfer of Automorphic Representations

The Jacquet–Langlands correspondence bijects irreducible admissible representations of GL₂(D) for a quaternion division algebra D over a local field F with discrete-series representations of GL₂(F). Globally, it matches cuspidal automorphic representations of GL₂ over a division algebra with those over the base field that are discrete at ramified places. Realized via the trace formula, orbital integrals on inner forms match after a transfer factor, and the stabilized trace formula identifies spectral sides.

Locally, this is a special case of the local Langlands correspondence for inner forms: for G and an inner form G′, L-packets biject preserving the L-parameter. Badulescu extended Jacquet–Langlands to all inner forms of GLn using types and the Bushnell–Kutzko classification of supercuspidals. The orbital integral transfer is governed by the fundamental lemma for the Hecke algebra unit element, a consequence of Ngô's proof.

Globally, the correspondence proves modularity for elliptic curves over totally real fields via Taylor–Wiles, by transferring a Galois representation to an automorphic form on a definite quaternion algebra where the trace formula simplifies. The multiplicity formula states that an automorphic representation's multiplicity in the discrete spectrum of GL₂(D) equals its multiplicity in GL₂(F) at ramified places—essential for R = T arguments in modularity lifting.

  • Assuming the local Langlands correspondence for real groups is a bijection; it is a finite-to-one map with L‑packets indexed by component groups.
  • Thinking the p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚₚ) is only defined for crystalline representations; the Colmez functor works for all de Rham and trianguline representations.
  • Believing the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence applies only to GL₂; it extends to all inner forms of GLn via Badulescu's work.

The Fargues–Scholze Geometrization of the Local Langlands Correspondence

The Fargues–Scholze program (2021) provides a geometric construction of the local Langlands correspondence for any reductive group G over a p‑adic field F. The central object is the Fargues–Fontaine curve XC,F, a complete algebraic curve over F whose vector bundles are classified by the Harder–Narasimhan formalism. Using the language of diamonds and v‑stacks, Fargues and Scholze define the stack Bun_G of G‑bundles on XC,F and the stack of Langlands parameters L_G, which parametrizes continuous homomorphisms from the Weil group W_F to the L‑group ^L G. Their main theorem establishes an equivalence of categories between the derived category of smooth representations of G(F) on vector spaces over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and the derived category of coherent sheaves on L_G. This equivalence is realized by a Hecke correspondence on the moduli stack of local shtukas with one leg, which encodes the action of the spherical Hecke algebra. The construction automatically yields compatibility with the Bernstein center, parabolic induction, and the local Langlands correspondence for Levi subgroups. Moreover, it provides a categorical framework for functoriality: a morphism of L‑groups ^L H → ^L G induces a functor between the corresponding categories of representations. The geometrization also connects to the p‑adic Langlands program via the work of Scholze–Weinstein on the moduli of local shtukas, and it gives a new proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n that avoids the Harris–Taylor and Henniart methods.

The Arthur–Selberg Trace Formula: Coarse and Fine Spectral Expansions

The Arthur–Selberg trace formula is the primary tool for comparing automorphic spectra on different groups. For a test function f in the Hecke algebra of G(A_F), the formula equates a geometric expansion — a sum over conjugacy classes of orbital integrals — with a spectral expansion — a sum over automorphic representations of their traces against f. The coarse spectral expansion groups representations by their infinitesimal character and central character, organizing the sum over Levi subgroups M of G. For each M, one integrates the trace of the induced Eisenstein series against f, producing a sum over cuspidal automorphic representations of M(A_F) and an integral over the unitary dual of the continuous spectrum. The fine spectral expansion refines this by isolating the discrete automorphic spectrum (cuspidal plus residual) from the continuous part. Arthur’s invariant trace formula removes the singularities that appear in the coarse expansion by truncating the Eisenstein series and adding correction terms. Stabilization of the trace formula transfers the geometric side to endoscopic groups H using the fundamental lemma, which identifies orbital integrals on G with stable orbital integrals on H. The fine expansion is essential for Arthur’s endoscopic classification of classical groups, where the multiplicity formula for discrete automorphic representations is expressed in terms of stable trace formulas on endoscopic groups. The weighted fundamental lemma, proved by Waldspurger and Ngô, provides the necessary identities for the transfer of Hecke functions beyond the unit element.

Identifying the Spectral Side in a Trace Formula Computation
Given a test function f on GL₂(𝔸_ℚ) with trivial central character, describe how the coarse spectral expansion separates the contribution of cuspidal automorphic representations from the continuous spectrum.
The coarse spectral expansion writes the trace of f as a sum over Levi subgroups M of GL₂. There are two Levi subgroups up to conjugacy: M = GL₂ itself and M = GL₁ × GL₁ (the diagonal torus). For M = GL₂, the contribution is a sum over cuspidal automorphic representations π of GL₂(𝔸_ℚ) with trivial central character, each weighted by the trace of π(f). For M = GL₁ × GL₁, the contribution is an integral over unitary characters χ of the idele class group, corresponding to Eisenstein series E(s, χ) induced from χ ⊗ χ⁻¹. The integral runs over the line Re(s) = 0 and captures the continuous spectrum. The fine expansion further separates the discrete part (cuspidal plus residues of Eisenstein series at poles) from the purely continuous integral. In practice, one computes the geometric side (orbital integrals) and matches it with this spectral decomposition to obtain information about the cuspidal spectrum.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Trace Formula Questions
When a question asks about the trace formula, first state the two sides: geometric (orbital integrals) and spectral (coarse/fine). Recall that the coarse expansion sums over Levi subgroups, while the fine expansion isolates discrete automorphic representations. For stabilization, emphasize that the fundamental lemma must hold for all endoscopic groups, not only for the group itself. For classical groups, Arthur’s invariant trace formula and the multiplicity formula are the key results to cite.

The Theory of Eisenstein Series and the Langlands Decomposition in the Spectral Theory

Eisenstein series are the main analytic tool for constructing the continuous spectrum of L²(G(F)G(A)). Starting from a cuspidal automorphic representation π of a Levi subgroup M(A), one forms the induced representation IndP(A)G(A) π ⊗ e⟨λ, H_P(·)⟩ for a parabolic P with Levi M and a complex parameter λ. The Eisenstein series E(g, φ, λ) is defined by summing over the rational points of the unipotent radical. Its constant term along another parabolic Q is expressed as a sum over the Weyl group W(M, G) of intertwining operators M(w, λ) acting on the inducing data. The Langlands decomposition of the constant term yields the functional equation of the Eisenstein series and reveals its poles. The residues at these poles generate the residual spectrum, which together with the cuspidal spectrum forms the discrete spectrum. The continuous spectrum is parameterized by the unitary Eisenstein series with λ purely imaginary. The spectral decomposition of L²(G(F)G(A)) is therefore a direct sum of the cuspidal subspace, the residual subspace, and a direct integral of continuous Eisenstein series. The Langlands classification for real and p‑adic groups uses Langlands parameters — homomorphisms from the Weil group (real case) or Weil–Deligne group (p‑adic case) to the L‑group — and the Langlands quotient construction. Globally, the functoriality conjecture predicts that a homomorphism of L‑groups ^L H → ^L G transfers automorphic representations of H to those of G, and the theory of Eisenstein series provides the analytic mechanism for such transfers via the trace formula and the Langlands–Shahidi method.

The Global Langlands Correspondence for GLₙ: The Converse Theorem and the Selberg Class

The global Langlands correspondence for GLₙ over a number field F conjectures a bijection between irreducible cuspidal automorphic representations π of GLₙ(𝔸_F) and n‑dimensional irreducible continuous Galois representations ρ : Gal(F̄/F) → GLₙ(ℂ) that are unramified outside a finite set of places and satisfy the local‑global compatibility condition at almost all places. The converse theorem of Cogdell and Piatetski‑Shapiro gives a characterization of automorphic representations in terms of the analytic properties of their twisted L‑functions: if for all m < n the L‑functions L(s, π × τ) for τ a cuspidal representation of GLₘ(𝔸_F) satisfy analytic continuation, functional equation, and non‑vanishing in a certain region, then π is automorphic. This theorem was crucial for the proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GLₙ by Harris–Taylor and Henniart, which in turn feeds into the global correspondence via the trace formula. The Selberg class is an axiomatic class of Dirichlet series S(s) = ∑ a_n n-s that satisfy a Ramanujan bound, an Euler product, analytic continuation to ℂ with at most a pole at s = 1, and a functional equation of the form Λ(s) = ε Λ(1−s) with a gamma factor. It is conjectured that the Selberg class coincides with the set of automorphic L‑functions for GLₙ. The modularity theorem (Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor) is the case n = 2 over ℚ. Generalizations to higher rank groups involve the endoscopic classification of Arthur for classical groups and the work of Clozel, Harris, Taylor, and others on the stable trace formula for unitary groups.

The Role of Motives in the Langlands Program: Compatible Systems and the Fontaine–Mazur Conjecture

Motives, as envisioned by Grothendieck, are the universal cohomology theory that should underlie both algebraic varieties and their associated Galois representations. A pure motive M over a number field F is expected to give rise to a compatible system of ℓ‑adic Galois representations ρM,ℓ : Gal(F̄/F) → GLd(ℚ_ℓ) for all primes ℓ, obtained from the étale cohomology of a smooth projective variety realizing M. The Fontaine–Mazur conjecture predicts precisely which p‑adic Galois representations arise from geometry: a continuous representation ρ : Gal(F̄/F) → GL_d(ℚ_p) comes from a motive if and only if it is unramified outside a finite set of places and is de Rham at all places above p. The Langlands program then predicts that such geometric Galois representations correspond to automorphic representations of GL_d(𝔸_F) (or more general reductive groups). The Tannakian formalism for motives yields a motivic Galois group Gmot whose finite‑dimensional representations are the motives; the L‑group ^L G of a reductive group G is expected to be a quotient of Gmot. The p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚ_p) constructed by Colmez and extended by Emerton, Helm, and others uses the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture as a guiding principle: the Galois representations that appear are exactly those that are potentially semi‑stable with distinct Hodge–Tate weights. The categorical geometric Langlands correspondence of Gaitsgory–Lurie and the Fargues–Scholze geometrization can be viewed as a categorification of the motivic picture, where the stack of Langlands parameters replaces the motivic Galois group.

  • Assuming the coarse spectral expansion already separates discrete and continuous spectra; the fine expansion is required for that separation.
  • Overlooking that the fundamental lemma must be proved for all endoscopic groups to stabilize the trace formula, not only for the unit element.
  • Thinking the converse theorem for GLₙ works with twists only by GL₁; it requires twists by all GLₘ with m < n.
  • Believing the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture is a theorem; it remains a conjecture, though many cases are known via modularity lifting.
  • Mixing up the Selberg class with the set of automorphic L‑functions; the Selberg class is an axiomatic class conjectured to equal the automorphic ones.
  • Neglecting that the Langlands classification for real groups uses the Weil group, while for p‑adic groups it uses the Weil–Deligne group.

The Local Langlands Correspondence for Non‑Connected Reductive Groups

The classical local Langlands correspondence attaches to each irreducible admissible representation of a connected reductive group G over a p‑adic field F a Langlands parameter, a homomorphism from the Weil–Deligne group W′_F to the L‑group ^L G. When G is not connected — for instance a disconnected orthogonal group O(2n) or a semidirect product G ⋊ θ where θ is an outer automorphism — the parameter space must be enlarged to include the action of the component group π₀(G). Kaletha’s work on the “rigid inner twist” and the “K‑group” provides a framework: one replaces ^L G by the L‑group of the connected component G⁰ together with a 1‑cocycle in Z¹(W_F, Z(Ĝ⁰)) that records the outer action. The resulting parameters are pairs (φ, ρ) where φ : W′_F → ^L G⁰ is a usual parameter and ρ is an irreducible representation of the centralizer S_φ = CentĜ⁰(Im φ) that is compatible with the component group. The correspondence is then a bijection between irreducible representations of G(F) and enhanced parameters (φ, ρ) satisfying a character identity for regular semisimple elements. This formulation has been proved for many classical disconnected groups by Kaletha, Minguez, Shin, and White, using the stabilization of the trace formula for non‑connected groups and the theory of endoscopy for twisted spaces.

A key technical point is the definition of the transfer factor for twisted orbital integrals. The factor involves the Kottwitz sign ε(G) and a Whittaker normalization that depends on a choice of generic character of the unipotent radical of a Borel subgroup of G⁰. The fundamental lemma for twisted endoscopy, proved by Ngô for the Lie algebra case and extended by Waldspurger and others to the group case, guarantees the matching of orbital integrals needed to stabilize the twisted trace formula. Once stabilization is achieved, the spectral side expresses the trace of a test function on G(F) in terms of stable distributions on endoscopic groups, and the local Langlands correspondence for G follows from the known correspondence for the connected endoscopic groups together with the character relations.

Applications include the classification of representations of the full orthogonal group O(2n) and the symplectic similitude group GSp(2n) with its outer automorphism given by the transpose inverse. In the global setting, the non‑connected correspondence is essential for the functorial transfer from a disconnected group to a connected one, for example the descent from GSp(4) to GL(4) via the spin L‑function. The theory also underlies the local Gan–Gross–Prasad conjecture for tempered representations of disconnected groups, where the relevant period integrals are defined on the quotient of a disconnected group by a symmetric subgroup.

Examiners often test the distinction between the connected and disconnected cases by asking for the precise form of the enhanced parameter. A correct answer must mention the centralizer S_φ, the component group π₀(G), and the compatibility condition that the restriction of ρ to the component group matches the action of π₀(G) on the parameter. Forgetting the component group or treating the parameter as a plain homomorphism to ^L G⁰ is a common error.

The Global Langlands Correspondence for GSp(4) and the Spin L‑Function

The group GSp(4) (the group of symplectic similitudes of a four‑dimensional symplectic space) is a rank‑two reductive group whose L‑group is GSpin(5, ℂ) ≅ GSp(4, ℂ). The global Langlands correspondence for GSp(4) predicts a bijection between irreducible cuspidal automorphic representations π of GSp(4, 𝔸_F) and four‑dimensional symplectic Galois representations ρ : Gal(F̄/F) → GSp(4, ℂ) that are unramified outside a finite set of places and satisfy local‑global compatibility. The spin L‑function L(s, π, spin) is the degree‑four L‑function attached to the spin representation of the dual group; its analytic properties — meromorphic continuation, functional equation, and non‑vanishing on Re(s)=1 — are central to the converse theorem approach.

The work of Gan and Takeda (2011) established the local Langlands correspondence for GSp(4) over p‑adic fields, classifying all irreducible admissible representations in terms of enhanced Langlands parameters. Globally, the endoscopic classification of Arthur for the quasi‑split group SO(5) (which is isogenous to PGSp(4)) provides a description of the discrete automorphic spectrum of GSp(4) in terms of stable trace formula. The functorial lift from GSp(4) to GL(4) via the spin representation was constructed by Asgari and Shahidi using the Langlands–Shahidi method; they proved that the spin L‑function coincides with the standard L‑function of the lifted representation on GL(4). This lift is injective on generic cuspidal representations and its image is characterized by a certain exterior square pole condition.

The converse theorem for GSp(4) was proved by Ginzburg, Jiang, and Soudry (2003) using the integral representation of the spin L‑function. They showed that if a cuspidal automorphic representation π of GSp(4, 𝔸_F) has the property that all its twists by characters of GL(1) and by cuspidal representations of GL(2) satisfy the expected analytic properties, then π is a functorial lift from a smaller group (either a Yoshida lift from GL(2) × GL(2) or a Saito–Kurokawa lift from GL(2)). The remaining “general type” representations are expected to correspond to irreducible four‑dimensional Galois representations with symplectic similitude character equal to the cyclotomic character.

Recent progress by Boxer, Calegari, Gee, and Pilloni (2021) on the modularity of abelian surfaces over totally real fields uses the potential automorphy of the associated Galois representations and the endoscopic classification to prove that many symplectic Galois representations arise from cuspidal automorphic representations of GSp(4). The proof combines p‑adic Hodge theory, the Taylor–Wiles method, and the stabilized trace formula for GSp(4). This result is a higher‑rank analogue of the modularity theorem for elliptic curves and illustrates the power of the Langlands program to produce concrete Diophantine consequences.

Worked Example: Identifying the Spin L‑Function of a GSp(4) Representation
Let π be a cuspidal automorphic representation of GSp(4, 𝔸_ℚ) with trivial central character. Suppose the local component π_p at an unramified prime p has Satake parameters {α₁, α₂, α₁⁻¹, α₂⁻¹} with similitude factor λ_p = α₁α₂. Write the Euler factor of the spin L‑function L(s, π, spin) at p in terms of α₁, α₂.
The spin representation of GSp(4, ℂ) is the four‑dimensional representation with weights ±ε₁, ±ε₂. For an unramified parameter diag(α₁, α₂, λ_pα₁⁻¹, λ_pα₂⁻¹) the spin L‑factor is ∏i=12 (1 − α_i p-s)-1 (1 − λ_p α_i-1 p-s)-1. Since λ_p = α₁α₂, this becomes (1 − α₁ p-s)-1 (1 − α₂ p-s)-1 (1 − α₂ p-s)-1 (1 − α₁ p-s)-1 = (1 − α₁ p-s)-2 (1 − α₂ p-s)-2. Thus the spin L‑function at p is the square of the degree‑two L‑function attached to the pair {α₁, α₂}.

When a question asks for the spin L‑factor of an unramified GSp(4) representation, first write the Satake parameters with the similitude factor, then apply the spin weights. Remember that the similitude factor equals the product of the two independent parameters; this collapses the four factors into two squared factors. Memorize the pattern (1 − α p-s)-2(1 − β p-s)-2 to save time.

The Geometric Langlands Correspondence over Complex Curves and the Hitchin Fibration

Over a smooth projective complex curve X, the geometric Langlands correspondence relates D‑modules on the moduli stack Bun_G of G‑bundles to coherent sheaves on the stack LocSys^L G of ^L G‑local systems. The Hitchin fibration h : T^*Bun_G → A provides a completely integrable system whose generic fibers are abelian varieties (the Prym varieties of spectral curves). The work of Beilinson and Drinfeld (1990s) constructed the Hecke eigensheaf property for a class of D‑modules called Hecke eigensheaves, and they conjectured that the category of D‑modules on Bun_G is equivalent to the category of coherent sheaves on LocSys^L G compatible with the Hitchin Hamiltonians.

The Kapustin–Witten topological twist of N=4 super Yang–Mills theory gave a physical derivation of the correspondence and identified the Hitchin fibration as the space of branes of type (B, A, A). The mirror symmetry of the Hitchin system exchanges the A‑model on T^*Bun_G with the B‑model on LocSys^L G, providing a conceptual explanation for the Fourier–Mukai transform that should implement the equivalence. Ngô’s proof of the fundamental lemma used the Hitchin fibration for the group G over function fields; the same geometry underlies the “geometric” fundamental lemma of Yun and the work of Chaudouard–Laumon on the trace formula for function fields.

In the categorical setting, the Gaitsgory–Lurie proof (2019) of the geometric Langlands conjecture for a general reductive group G over a curve over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero constructs an equivalence of ∞‑categories IndCohN(LocSys^L G) ≃ D‑mod(Bun_G) where the subscript N denotes a nilpotent singular support condition. The proof proceeds by induction on the rank of G, using the “spectral” action of the category of representations of ^L G on both sides and the “Whittaker” model to reduce to the case of a torus. The Hitchin fibration appears as the classical limit of the spectral side: the associated graded of the category of D‑modules with respect to the filtration by order of differential operators is the category of coherent sheaves on the global nilpotent cone, which is the fiber over 0 of the Hitchin map.

Examiners may ask for the role of the Hitchin fibration in the proof of the fundamental lemma or in the categorical equivalence. A precise answer should mention that the Hitchin fibration provides a Lagrangian fibration whose generic fibers are abelian varieties, that the perverse sheaves on the Hitchin base (the “Hitchin sheaves”) correspond under the Fourier transform to skyscraper sheaves on the spectral curves, and that the decomposition theorem for the Hitchin map yields the required endoscopy identities. Confusing the Hitchin fibration for G with the one for its Langlands dual ^L G is a frequent mistake; the dual fibration lives on T^*Bun^L G and the two are related by the spectral curve construction.

The Langlands Program and the Theory of L‑functions: The Ramanujan Conjecture and Bounds for Automorphic Representations

The Ramanujan conjecture for GL(n) over a number field F asserts that for every cuspidal automorphic representation π = ⊗_v π_v, the local component π_v at a finite place v is tempered, i.e., its Satake parameters αv,1, …, αv,n satisfy |αv,i| = 1. Equivalently, the local L‑factor L(s, π_v) has no poles in Re(s) > 0 and the Dirichlet coefficients of the standard L‑function satisfy the bound a_π(m) ≪ mε. For GL(2) over ℚ this is the classical Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture, proved by Deligne using the Weil conjectures. For higher rank, the best known unconditional bounds are due to Luo, Rudnick, and Sarnak (1999) who proved |αv,i| ≤ q_v1/2 - 1/(n2+1) for GL(n), and later improvements by Blomer and Brumley (2011) giving exponent 1/2 - 1/(n2+1) for all n.

The generalized Ramanujan conjecture for a general reductive group G predicts that every cuspidal automorphic representation is globally generic and locally tempered at all unramified places. This is known for groups of type A_n (GL(n)) only conditionally on functoriality to GL(N). For classical groups, Arthur’s endoscopic classification reduces the problem to the temperedness of the constituent GL(n) representations appearing in the Arthur parameter. The work of Shin and Templier (2016) on the Sato–Tate conjecture for families of automorphic forms uses the potential automorphy and the known bounds to equidistribute Satake parameters.

Analytic methods provide bounds towards Ramanujan via the Rankin–Selberg convolution. For π on GL(n) and π' on GL(m), the Rankin–Selberg L‑function L(s, π × π') is known to be entire except for a possible simple pole at s=1 when π' ≅ π̃. The convexity bound for L(s, π × π') together with the functional equation yields the bound |αv,i| ≤ q_v1/2 - 1/(2n) (the “trivial” bound). The subconvexity results of Michel and Venkatesh (2010) for GL(2) and of Nelson (2019) for GL(n) improve the exponent in the t‑aspect, but the q‑aspect remains the main obstacle. The Langlands functoriality conjecture predicts that the symmetric power lifts Sym^k π exist as automorphic representations on GL(k+1); if these lifts are known to be cuspidal and satisfy the standard analytic properties, then the Ramanujan conjecture follows from the bound for GL(k+1) as k → ∞.

In the p‑adic setting, the local Ramanujan conjecture is a statement about the eigenvalues of the Hecke operators on the space of Iwahori‑fixed vectors. The work of Henniart and Vignéras on the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over p‑adic fields shows that temperedness of the Galois parameter (i.e., the image of the Weil group is bounded) is equivalent to the representation being tempered. The p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚ_p) of Colmez and the Breuil–Mézard conjecture (now a theorem of Kisin) provide a bridge between the p‑adic Hodge theory of Galois representations and the analytic properties of the associated automorphic forms, giving another route to local bounds.

The Theta Correspondence and Howe Duality for Classical Groups

The theta correspondence provides a realization of the local Langlands correspondence for many classical groups by lifting automorphic representations across dual reductive pairs inside a symplectic group. Let (G, H) be a reductive dual pair in Sp(2n) over a local field F; the Weil representation ω of the metaplectic cover Mp(2n) restricts to G × H and decomposes as a direct integral of irreducible representations π ⊗ θ(π) where π runs over the smooth irreducible representations of G and θ(π) is the (possibly zero) theta lift to H. Howe duality states that the correspondence π θ(π) is a bijection between certain subsets of the unitary duals of G and H, preserving L‑parameters under the natural embedding of L‑groups ^L G × ^L H → ^L Sp(2n). For the pair (O(p,q), Sp(2r)) the correspondence matches orthogonal and symplectic representations with the same Arthur parameter, and the non‑vanishing of θ(π) is controlled by the conservation relation of Kudla–Rallis: the sum of the first occurrence indices for the two towers of dual pairs equals a constant depending only on the dimension of the symplectic space. In the global setting, the theta lift of a cuspidal automorphic representation π on G(𝔸) to H(𝔸) is defined by integrating the theta kernel against π; the resulting automorphic form on H is either zero or spans an irreducible constituent of the residual spectrum. The Rallis inner product formula expresses the Petersson norm of the theta lift in terms of the central value of a standard L‑function L(½, π, Std), linking non‑vanishing of theta lifts to non‑vanishing of L‑values. This machinery contributes to proofs of the local Langlands correspondence for classical groups of low rank and the construction of functorial lifts from classical groups to GL(N) via the tower property of theta lifts. The Gan–Gross–Prasad conjectures refine the correspondence by predicting which representations in an L‑packet participate in the theta lift, linking the multiplicity one property for the restriction to a diagonal subgroup to the epsilon factor of the relevant L‑function.

The Endoscopic Classification of Unitary Groups via the Stabilized Trace Formula

Unitary groups U(n) over a number field F with a quadratic extension E/F are quasi‑split inner forms of GL(n) and admit a rich endoscopic structure indexed by elliptic endoscopic data (H, s, η) where H is a product of smaller unitary groups and general linear groups. The stabilized trace formula for U(n) compares the geometric side — orbital integrals on U(n) — with stable orbital integrals on its endoscopic groups H, using transfer factors Δ(γ, δ) defined by Langlands–Shelstad. The fundamental lemma for the unit group of a division algebra, proved by Ngô for the Lie algebra case and extended to the group case by Waldspurger and others, ensures the matching of orbital integrals at almost all places. Arthur's endoscopic classification parametrizes the discrete automorphic spectrum of U(n) by global Arthur parameters ψ : L_F × SL(2, ℂ) → ^L U(n) satisfying certain self‑duality conditions. Each ψ determines an L‑packet Π_ψ consisting of representations of the various pure inner forms of U(n) that are locally equivalent at almost all places. The multiplicity formula m(π) = |S_ψ|-1 ∑x∈S_ψ ⟨x, π⟩, where S_ψ is the centralizer of ψ in ^L U(n), gives the exact multiplicity of π in the discrete spectrum. The classification relates the global Langlands correspondence for U(n) to the local Langlands correspondence for the endoscopic groups, which are products of GL(m) and smaller unitary groups; the latter are approached by induction on n using the base change lift from U(n) to GL(n)_E established by Rogawski and Clozel. The stable trace formula also yields the precise description of the continuous spectrum in terms of Eisenstein series induced from cuspidal data on Levi subgroups, and the spectral side of the formula provides a tool for proving functoriality for the standard and exterior square L‑functions of unitary groups.

Identifying the correct dual pair in a theta lift
Let F be a p‑adic field and consider the dual pair (U(2), U(3)) inside Sp(10). A supercuspidal representation π of U(2) has L‑parameter φ_π : W_F → GL(2, ℂ) with determinant equal to the quadratic character χE/F. Determine the L‑parameter of its non‑zero theta lift θ(π) to U(3) and explain why the lift is non‑zero.
The embedding of L‑groups for the dual pair (U(2), U(3)) is ^L U(2) × ^L U(3) → ^L Sp(10) ≅ SO(11, ℂ). The L‑parameter of π is φ_π : W_F → GL(2, ℂ) with det φ_π = χ. Under the natural map GL(2) × GL(3) → SO(11), the parameter of θ(π) is φ_θ = φ_π ⊕ χ ⊕ 1, where the extra χ and trivial character arise from the Weil representation's central character. The conservation relation for the U(2)–U(3) tower states that the first occurrence index for π in the U(3) tower plus the first occurrence index for the contragredient in the U(2) tower equals 3. Since π is supercuspidal, its first occurrence in the U(2) tower is 0, forcing the first occurrence in the U(3) tower to be 3, i.e. the lift to U(3) is non‑zero. Hence θ(π) is a non‑zero irreducible representation of U(3) with the stated parameter.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for the theta lift of a representation, first write down the dual pair and the embedding of L‑groups. Then apply the conservation relation to decide non‑vanishing; only after that compute the lifted parameter by adding the appropriate characters. This two‑step approach avoids the common mistake of writing a parameter for a lift that is actually zero.

The Categorical Local Langlands Correspondence and the Bernstein Center

The categorical local Langlands program seeks to upgrade the bijection between irreducible smooth representations of a p‑adic reductive group G(F) and enhanced Langlands parameters into an equivalence of ∞‑categories. On the spectral side one considers the derived category of smooth representations D(G) equipped with the action of the Bernstein center Z(G), which is the endomorphism ring of the identity functor on the category of smooth representations. The Bernstein decomposition partitions D(G) into blocks indexed by inertial equivalence classes of cuspidal pairs (M, σ); each block is equivalent to the derived category of modules over a Hecke algebra H(G, τ) for a suitable type τ. On the Galois side, the category of coherent sheaves on the stack of Langlands parameters LocSys^L G carries a natural action of the categorical center given by functions on the derived moduli stack. The conjectural equivalence, formulated by Ben‑Zvi–Sakellaridis–Venkatesh and established in significant cases by Gaitsgory–Lurie for GL(n) and by Dat–Helm–Kurinczuk–Moss for general groups under certain hypotheses, intertwines the Bernstein center with the categorical center via the spectral action. Concretely, for a Bernstein block corresponding to a cuspidal pair (M, σ), the associated component of the parameter stack is the quotient of the space of parameters for M by the Weyl group; the equivalence sends a representation π in that block to a coherent sheaf supported on the corresponding component, with the Hecke algebra action matching the convolution of sheaves. This categorical perspective clarifies the compatibility of the local Langlands correspondence with parabolic induction: induction corresponds to push‑forward along the natural map of parameter stacks, and the Bernstein center acts by correspondences on the parameter side. The theory also addresses the behavior of the correspondence under change of characteristic: the ℓ‑adic and p‑adic versions are related by the Fargues–Scholze geometrization, which identifies the category of smooth representations with the category of sheaves on the Fargues–Fontaine curve, and relates the Bernstein center to the global functions on the moduli stack of local shtukas.

The Spectral Decomposition of L2(G(F)G(𝔸)) and the Continuous Spectrum

The spectral decomposition of the space of square‑integrable automorphic forms on a reductive group G over a number field F is fundamental to the trace formula. The Hilbert space L2(G(F)G(𝔸)) decomposes as a direct sum of the discrete spectrum L2_disc and a direct integral of the continuous spectrum L2_cont. The discrete spectrum further splits into the cuspidal spectrum L2_cusp, spanned by cuspidal automorphic representations, and the residual spectrum L2_res, generated by residues of Eisenstein series. The continuous spectrum is described by the theory of Eisenstein series: for each standard parabolic subgroup P = MN, one forms the induced representation IndP(𝔸)G(𝔸) (σ ⊗ e⟨λ, H_P(·)⟩) with σ a cuspidal automorphic representation of M(𝔸) and λ in the complexified Lie algebra of the split component of M. The Eisenstein series E(g, φ, λ) converges for Re(λ) sufficiently dominant and admits meromorphic continuation to all λ. The Plancherel formula for the continuous spectrum expresses the projection onto L2_cont as an integral over the unitary dual of M of the intertwining operators M(w, λ) attached to the Weyl group. The poles of these intertwining operators in the positive chamber correspond exactly to the residual spectrum; their residues give the iterated residues of Eisenstein series that generate L2_res. Arthur's truncation operator Λ^T isolates the contribution of each cuspidal datum (M, σ) to the trace formula, leading to the fine spectral expansion where the spectral side is a sum over cuspidal data of distributions involving the normalized intertwining operators. The spectral decomposition is also the input for the Arthur–Selberg trace formula: the geometric side is a sum over conjugacy classes in G(F), while the spectral side is a sum over the discrete spectrum plus an integral over the continuous spectrum. The stabilization of the trace formula expresses the spectral side as a sum over endoscopic groups, matching the continuous spectrum of G with the discrete and continuous spectra of its endoscopic groups via transfer of orbital integrals. This matching is central to the endoscopic classification of automorphic representations for classical and unitary groups.

Base Change and Cyclic Base Change in the Langlands Program

Base change is a fundamental instance of Langlands functoriality that relates automorphic representations of a reductive group over a field to those over a finite extension. For GL(n), the theory was completed by Arthur and Clozel (1989) using the stabilized trace formula. They proved that for a cyclic extension E/F of number fields, there is a canonical lift (base change) from automorphic representations of GL(n, F) to GL(n, E) compatible with the local Langlands correspondence at almost all places. The lift is characterized by the equality of L‑functions: if π is a cuspidal automorphic representation of GL(n, F), its base change Π to GL(n, E) satisfies L(s, Π) = ∏σ∈Gal(E/F) L(s, π^σ). The proof proceeds by comparing the trace formula for GL(n) over F with that over E, using the fundamental lemma for the unit elements of the Hecke algebras. The result extends to non‑cyclic solvable extensions by iteration, but the general non‑solvable case remains open.

Cyclic base change, introduced by Langlands (1980) for GL(2) and later generalized, constructs the lift directly from the local Langlands correspondence for cyclic extensions of local fields. For a cyclic extension E_w/F_v of degree ℓ, the local base change map sends an irreducible admissible representation π_v of GL(n, F_v) to a representation Π_w of GL(n, E_w) whose L‑parameter is the restriction of the parameter of π_v to the Weil group of E_w. The global cyclic base change theorem asserts that a cuspidal automorphic representation π of GL(n, F) admits a base change to GL(n, E) if and only if π is invariant under the Galois action (i.e., π^σ ≅ π for all σ). When the invariance condition fails, π does not lift to a cuspidal representation; instead it contributes to the residual spectrum via Eisenstein series induced from proper Levi subgroups.

The distinction between base change and automorphic induction is a frequent source of confusion. Base change corresponds to the restriction of L‑parameters from the Weil group of F to that of E (a map ^L GL(n)_E → ^L GL(n)_F), whereas automorphic induction corresponds to induction of parameters from the Weil group of E to that of F (a map ^L GL(n)_E → ^L GL(n)_F in the opposite direction). Both are instances of functoriality for the diagonal embedding of L‑groups, but they operate on different sides of the extension. In the trace formula framework, base change is realized by comparing geometric sides (orbital integrals) while automorphic induction is realized by comparing spectral sides (characters). The Arthur–Clozel proof of base change for GL(n) uses the simple trace formula and the fundamental lemma for the unit element; automorphic induction for GL(n) was established by Arthur and Clozel for cyclic extensions using the same method, but the general case for non‑cyclic extensions requires the full strength of the stabilized trace formula and endoscopic transfer.

Applications of base change include the proof of the Sato–Tate conjecture for modular forms (via potential modularity and base change to totally real fields), the construction of Galois representations attached to automorphic forms on GL(n) over CM fields, and the proof of the Ramanujan conjecture for GL(n) over function fields (by Lafforgue, using base change to reduce to the case of GL(1)). In the context of the Langlands program, base change provides the first non‑trivial verification of functoriality for a non‑injective L‑homomorphism, and its methods underpin the endoscopic classification of classical groups.

Automorphic Induction and the Langlands Correspondence for Weil Groups

Automorphic induction is the functorial lift associated to the induction of L‑parameters from a finite extension E/F to the base field. For a cyclic extension of degree ℓ, the L‑homomorphism is the map ^L GL(n)_E → ^L GL(n)_F given by induction of representations of the Weil group W_E to W_F. The global automorphic induction conjecture predicts that every cuspidal automorphic representation π of GL(n, E) which is invariant under the Galois group Gal(E/F) (i.e., π^σ ≅ π) descends to a cuspidal automorphic representation Π of GL(nℓ, F) such that the L‑function of Π equals the product of the L‑functions of the Galois conjugates of π. The local counterpart is the local Langlands correspondence for the Weil group: an irreducible representation of W_E induces to a representation of W_F, and the local Langlands correspondence matches this with the induction of the corresponding representation of GL(n, E) to GL(nℓ, F).

The first complete results were obtained by Langlands (1980) for GL(2) over cyclic extensions of number fields, using the trace formula for GL(2) and the theory of Eisenstein series. Arthur and Clozel (1989) extended the result to GL(n) for cyclic extensions, again via the stabilized trace formula. Their proof constructs the lift by comparing the trace formula for GL(n) over E with that for GL(nℓ) over F, using the fundamental lemma for the unit elements and the transfer of orbital integrals. The lift is characterized by the equality of twisted L‑functions: for a character χ of the idele class group of E, the twisted L‑function L(s, π ⊗ χ) equals L(s, Π ⊗ χ ∘ NE/F). This characterization uniquely determines Π when it exists.

For non‑cyclic extensions, automorphic induction is more subtle. The L‑homomorphism is no longer a simple induction of parameters but involves the full Galois group. The conjecture predicts a lift from automorphic representations of GL(n, E) that are invariant under the Galois action to automorphic representations of GL(n[E:F], F). Partial results exist for solvable extensions by iterating cyclic base change and automorphic induction, but the general case is tied to the full functoriality conjecture for arbitrary L‑homomorphisms. The work of Harris and Taylor (2001) on the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over p‑adic fields provides the local foundation: they construct the local automorphic induction map using the geometry of Lubin–Tate towers and the Harris–Taylor correspondence, which matches induced Weil–Deligne representations with induced representations of GL(n).

Automorphic induction plays a crucial role in the construction of Galois representations attached to automorphic forms. For a cuspidal automorphic representation π of GL(n) over a CM field E, automorphic induction to a totally real field F yields a representation Π of GL(n[E:F], F) to which the Taylor–Wiles method can be applied, producing a compatible system of ℓ‑adic Galois representations. This strategy underlies the proof of the modularity of elliptic curves over totally real fields and the potential modularity of abelian varieties. In the function field setting, Lafforgue's proof of the global Langlands correspondence for GL(n) uses automorphic induction (via the geometric Langlands correspondence) to reduce the problem to the case of GL(1), where class field theory provides the answer.

Distinguishing Base Change from Automorphic Induction
Let E/F be a cyclic extension of number fields of degree 3. Suppose π is a cuspidal automorphic representation of GL(2, F) that is not invariant under the Galois action. Which of the following statements is correct? (A) π admits a base change to a cuspidal representation of GL(2, E). (B) π admits an automorphic induction to a cuspidal representation of GL(6, E). (C) π does not admit a cuspidal base change to GL(2, E); its base change is a non‑cuspidal automorphic representation generated by Eisenstein series. (D) The local base change map at a split place sends π_v to a supercuspidal representation of GL(2, E_w).
The correct answer is (C). Base change for GL(n) exists for any cyclic extension, but the global base change of a cuspidal representation π is cuspidal if and only if π is invariant under the Galois group (i.e., π^σ ≅ π for all σ ∈ Gal(E/F)). If π is not invariant, the base change is not cuspidal; it appears in the residual spectrum as an iterated residue of Eisenstein series induced from proper Levi subgroups. Statement (A) is false because invariance is required for a cuspidal lift. Statement (B) confuses the direction: automorphic induction lifts from GL(n, E) to GL(nℓ, F), not from GL(n, F) to GL(nℓ, E). Statement (D) is false because at a split place the local base change is simply the direct sum of the Galois conjugates, which for a non‑invariant representation is not irreducible, let alone supercuspidal.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about base change or automorphic induction, first identify the direction of the L‑homomorphism: base change restricts parameters (extension → base), automorphic induction induces parameters (base → extension). Check whether the representation is Galois‑invariant; only invariant representations lift cuspidally. Remember that the Arthur–Clozel theorem covers GL(n) for cyclic extensions; for non‑cyclic or non‑GL groups the results are conditional on the full functoriality conjecture.

The Langlands Correspondence for Exceptional Groups: G2, F4, E6, E7, E8

The Langlands program for exceptional groups addresses the functoriality conjectures for the five exceptional Dynkin types G2, F4, E6, E7, E8. Unlike classical groups, exceptional groups have no standard matrix realization of small dimension, and their representation theory is governed by intricate combinatorics of root systems and Weyl groups. The local Langlands correspondence for exceptional p‑adic groups was formulated by Borel (1979) and refined by Reeder and others. It predicts a bijection between irreducible admissible representations of G(F) (F a p‑adic field) and enhanced L‑parameters φ: W_F × SL(2, ℂ) → ^L G, where ^L G is the complex dual group (of the same type). The correspondence should preserve L‑functions, ε‑factors, and the Plancherel measure. For split exceptional groups, the dual group is the corresponding complex simple Lie group; for non‑split forms, the dual group is a twisted form determined by the Galois action on the root datum.

Significant progress has been made for G2 and F4. Gan, Gross, and Savin (2002) constructed the local Langlands correspondence for split G2 over p‑adic fields using the theta correspondence with PGSp(6) and the theory of minimal representations. They identified the L‑parameters for all irreducible representations of G2(F) and verified the expected properties. For F4, work of Gross and Reeder (2010) and later of Kaletha (2019) using the theory of rigid inner forms and the Kaletha–Mínguez–Shin–White construction has established the correspondence for many representations, particularly those in the discrete series. The groups E6, E7, E8 are much larger; their representation theory is approached via the geometric Langlands program and the theory of categorical representations. The geometric Satake equivalence for exceptional groups, proved by Mirković and Vilonen (2007) and extended by Bezrukavnikov and Finkelberg, provides a categorical framework where the category of representations of the dual group is equivalent to the category of perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian. This equivalence underpins the categorical local Langlands correspondence for exceptional groups.

On the global side, the functoriality conjectures for exceptional groups predict lifts from classical groups to exceptional groups via embeddings of L‑groups. For example, the embedding of ^L G2 into ^L SO(7) (or ^L SO(8) via triality) predicts a lift from automorphic representations of G2 to those of SO(7) or SO(8). Such lifts have been constructed in special cases using the theta correspondence and the theory of minimal representations. The embedding of ^L F4 into ^L E6 predicts a lift from F4 to E6, which is related to the geometry of the Cayley plane. For E8, the only known functorial lifts are those arising from the trivial representation and from the adjoint representation; the full functoriality for E8 remains largely conjectural. The work of Arthur on the endoscopic classification of classical groups provides a template: one stabilizes the trace formula for the exceptional group, identifies the endoscopic groups (which are products of smaller exceptional and classical groups), and proves the fundamental lemma for the relevant Lie algebras. Ngô's proof of the fundamental lemma for all reductive groups includes the exceptional cases, but the stabilization of the trace formula for exceptional groups has not been completed due to the complexity of their endoscopic data.

Applications of the exceptional Langlands correspondence include the construction of Galois representations with exceptional monodromy groups, the study of automorphic forms on exceptional groups arising from the minimal representation (which have small Gelfand–Kirillov dimension), and connections to string theory and M‑theory where exceptional groups appear as U‑duality groups. In the context of the Langlands program, the exceptional groups serve as a testing ground for the generality of the functoriality principle beyond the classical series.

The Geometric Satake Equivalence and the Categorical Langlands Correspondence

The geometric Satake equivalence, established by Ginzburg (1995) and Mirković–Vilonen (2007), is a cornerstone of the geometric Langlands program. It provides an equivalence of tensor categories between the category of finite‑dimensional representations of the complex dual group ^L G and the category of G(𝒪)‑equivariant perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian Gr_G = G(𝒦)/G(𝒪), where 𝒦 = ℂ((t)) and 𝒪 = ℂ[[t]]. The convolution product on perverse sheaves corresponds to the tensor product of representations, and the fiber functor at the base point corresponds to the forgetful functor. This equivalence realizes the dual group ^L G as the Tannakian fundamental group of the category of perverse sheaves, thereby giving a geometric construction of the Langlands dual group.

The categorical Langlands correspondence extends this equivalence to a conjectural equivalence between the derived category of D‑modules on the moduli stack Bun_G of G‑bundles on a smooth projective curve X and the derived category of quasi‑coherent sheaves on the moduli stack LocSys^L G of ^L G‑local systems on X. The geometric Satake equivalence appears as the local component of this correspondence: the Hecke operators acting on D‑modules on Bun_G are given by convolution with perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian, and under the equivalence they correspond to tensoring with representations of ^L G on the spectral side. The work of Gaitsgory and Lurie (2019) proved the categorical geometric Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over function fields, using the theory of ∞‑categories and the Drinfeld–Lafforgue theorem. Their proof constructs a functor from D‑modules on Bun_G to quasi‑coherent sheaves on LocSys^L G and shows it is an equivalence by verifying it on a set of compact generators (the Whittaker sheaves) and using the geometric Satake equivalence to identify the Hecke action.

A key technical tool is the theory of factorization algebras and the Ran space, which allows one to glue local data (the affine Grassmannian) into global objects on the curve. The geometric Satake equivalence is compatible with the factorization structure: the convolution product on the affine Grassmannian corresponds to the factorization product on the Ran space. This compatibility is essential for the construction of the global correspondence. The categorical perspective also clarifies the role of the Bernstein center: the center of the category of representations of G(F) (for F a local field) acts on the category of perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian via the Satake isomorphism, and under the Fargues–Scholze geometrization this action is identified with the action of global functions on the moduli stack of local shtukas.

Applications of the geometric Satake equivalence include the proof of the Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture for affine Lie algebras (via the identification of characters of representations with intersection cohomology of Schubert varieties), the construction of the categorical local Langlands correspondence for general reductive groups (by Bezrukavnikov, Finkelberg, and others), and the formulation of the geometric Langlands correspondence for non‑simply‑connected groups (where the dual group is a non‑adjoint form). In the context of the Langlands program, the geometric Satake equivalence provides the geometric incarnation of the dual group and the Hecke operators, making it possible to translate representation‑theoretic questions into geometric ones and vice versa.

The Local Langlands Correspondence for p‑adic Groups via Types and Covers

The theory of types and covers, developed by Bushnell and Kutzko (1993) for GL(n) and extended by Stevens (2005) and others to classical groups, provides a combinatorial classification of irreducible smooth representations of p‑adic reductive groups in terms of compact open subgroups and their representations. A type for a Bernstein component is a pair (J, λ) where J is a compact open subgroup and λ an irreducible representation of J such that the functor Hom_J(λ, –) induces an equivalence between the Bernstein block and the category of modules over the Hecke algebra ℋ(G, λ). This equivalence reduces the study of representations in that block to the representation theory of an affine Hecke algebra, whose parameters are determined by the inertial equivalence class of the supercuspidal support.

The local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over a p‑adic field F was established by Harris–Taylor (2001) and Henniart (2000) using the geometry of Lubin–Tate towers and the theory of types. The Bushnell–Kutzko classification of simple types for GL(n) gives an explicit parametrization of supercuspidal representations in terms of simple characters and β‑extensions. The correspondence matches a supercuspidal representation π with an irreducible n‑dimensional representation of the Weil–Deligne group W_F' whose restriction to the inertia subgroup corresponds to the simple character. For principal series and discrete series representations, the correspondence is given by the Langlands classification and the Zelevinsky classification, which are compatible with the type theory via the Bernstein–Zelevinsky derivatives.

For classical groups (symplectic, orthogonal, unitary), the theory of covers (due to Bushnell–Kutzko for GL(n) and extended by Stevens, Roche, and others) constructs types for all Bernstein components by covering the group with a sequence of subgroups related by Hecke algebra isomorphisms. The local Langlands correspondence for classical groups, as formulated by Kaletha, Mínguez, Shin, and White (2020), uses the theory of rigid inner forms and the Kaletha–Yu construction of supercuspidal representations via Yu‑data. Their correspondence matches irreducible representations of a classical group G(F) with enhanced L‑parameters φ: W_F × SL(2, ℂ) → ^L G, where ^L G is the complex dual group. The proof proceeds by induction on the size of the group, using the theory of covers to reduce to the case of supercuspidal representations, which are then matched to parameters via the Kaletha–Yu construction and the local Langlands correspondence for tori (class field theory).

The type theory also provides a bridge to the categorical local Langlands correspondence. The Bernstein center of the category of smooth representations of G(F) is isomorphic to the algebra of regular functions on the variety of inertial equivalence classes. Under the categorical local Langlands correspondence (proved by Dat, Helm, Kurinczuk, Moss, and others for GL(n) and extended to classical groups), this center acts on the category of sheaves on the parameter stack, and the type theory gives an explicit description of the Hecke algebras that generate the category. The Fargues–Scholze geometrization identifies the category of smooth representations of G(F) with the category of sheaves on the Fargues–Fontaine curve, and the types correspond to certain vector bundles on the curve. This geometric realization of types is a key ingredient in the proof of the local Langlands correspondence for general reductive groups via the geometrization program.

  • Mixing up the direction of the L‑homomorphism for functorial lifts from exceptional groups to classical groups; the embedding of dual groups goes from the exceptional dual into the classical dual, so the lift goes from exceptional to classical.

The advanced topics above illustrate the depth and breadth of the Langlands program, connecting representation theory, harmonic analysis, algebraic geometry, and number theory into a unified framework.

The Gan–Gross–Prasad Conjectures and the Relative Trace Formula

The Gan–Gross–Prasad (GGP) conjectures formulate a precise relationship between periods of automorphic forms on a pair of classical groups and the non‑vanishing of central values of Rankin–Selberg L‑functions. For a pair (H, G) where H ⊂ G are split classical groups over a number field F, the conjecture predicts that an irreducible cuspidal automorphic representation π of G(𝔸_F) admits a non‑zero H(𝔸_F)‑invariant linear form if and only if the central L‑value L(½, π × σ) ≠ 0 for the corresponding representation σ of H(𝔸_F) under the local Langlands correspondence. The local analogue, initiated by Waldspurger for small ranks and established for all classical pairs by Gan–Gross–Prasad and subsequent works, identifies the distinguished representations in terms of epsilon factors of the local Langlands parameters.

The relative trace formula, developed by Jacquet, Guo, and others, provides the analytic tool to compare orbital integrals on G with those on H. The coarse geometric expansion isolates the contribution of regular semisimple orbits that match under the natural embedding of Lie algebras. Stabilization of the relative trace formula for unitary groups, achieved by Beuzart‑Plessis, Liu, and Zhang, expresses the spectral side in terms of endoscopic data for the pair (H, G). This stabilization is a key input for the proof of the global GGP conjecture for unitary groups (Beuzart‑Plessis, circa 2020) and for recent results on Bessel and Fourier–Jacobi periods on symplectic and metaplectic groups. The method also yields explicit Ichino–Ikeda type formulas for the squared period in terms of a product of local zeta integrals and the central L‑value.

Recent work extends the relative trace formula to the case of non‑tempered representations, incorporating Arthur’s endoscopic classification. The resulting “refined GGP conjecture” predicts the precise multiplicity of the period in the discrete spectrum, linking it to the Arthur parameter of π. This refinement has been verified for many low‑rank cases and provides a bridge between the relative Langlands program and the endoscopic classification of automorphic representations.

The p‑adic Langlands Correspondence for GLₙ(ℚₚ) via Emerton’s Completed Cohomology

Emerton’s theory of completed cohomology constructs a topological module widehat{H}^i over the Iwasawa algebra Λ = ℤ_p[[GL_n(ℤ_p)]] (for n=2; for higher rank the algebra is a completed group ring) by taking the inverse limit of the étale cohomology of modular curves (or higher‑dimensional Shimura varieties) with respect to the level‑raising maps. The action of GL_n(ℚ_p) on the tower of levels endows widehat{H}^i with a continuous admissible representation of GL_n(ℚ_p) on a Banach space over ℚ_p. The fundamental theorem of Emerton (2006) identifies the locally analytic vectors in widehat{H}1 with the direct sum of the p‑adic Banach space representations Π(ρ)^an attached to two‑dimensional p‑adic Galois representations ρ : Gℚ_p → GL2(ℚ_p) via the Colmez functor.

For n>2, the Breuil–Schneider conjecture predicts that the completed cohomology of suitable Shimura varieties (e.g. unitary Shimura varieties for GL_n) realizes the p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL_n(ℚ_p). Recent work of Caraiani–Emerton–Gee–Geraghty–Paskunas–Shin establishes a “patch‑by‑patch” compatibility: for each Serre weight, the corresponding piece of completed cohomology matches the predicted locally analytic representation constructed from the p‑adic Langlands parameter via the theory of (φ, Γ)‑modules. The proof uses the Taylor–Wiles–Kisin patching method together with the geometry of the Emerton–Gee stack, which parametrizes (φ, Γ)‑modules with a fixed Hodge–Tate weight.

A crucial technical ingredient is the “classicality theorem” for overconvergent cohomology, which shows that any eigenclass in the completed cohomology that is sufficiently regular at p arises from a classical automorphic form. This theorem, proved by Boxer–Calegari–Gee–Pilloni (2021) using the theory of perfectoid Shimura varieties, allows one to transfer results between the p‑adic and classical Langlands correspondences. The completed cohomology framework also provides a natural home for the “p‑adic local Langlands correspondence in families”, where the parameter varies in a rigid‑analytic family of Galois representations, and the corresponding family of Banach representations is constructed by interpolating the Colmez functor across the weight space.

Worked Example: Local GGP Epsilon Factor Criterion
Let F be a p‑adic field, G = SO(5) and H = SO(4) embedded as the stabilizer of a non‑isotropic vector. Let π be an irreducible supercuspidal representation of G(F) with Langlands parameter φ_π : W_F' → Sp(4, ℂ). Let σ be the representation of H(F) corresponding to the restriction φ_π|W_F' composed with the natural embedding Sp(4, ℂ) → SO(4, ℂ) × SO(1, ℂ). Show that π is H‑distinguished if and only if ε(½, φ_π, ∧²) = 1.
The local GGP conjecture for (SO(5), SO(4)) states that HomH(F)(π, ℂ) ≠ 0 ⇔ ε(½, π, ∧²) = 1. Under the local Langlands correspondence, the exterior square L‑function of π corresponds to the representation ∧² ∘ φ_π of the Weil–Deligne group. The epsilon factor ε(½, φ_π, ∧²) is the product of the local root numbers of the symmetric square and the determinant. For a supercuspidal π, the parameter φ_π is irreducible of dimension 4. The restriction to H corresponds to the decomposition ∧² φ_π = (φ_π ⊗ φ_π)sym ⊕ det φ_π. The distinguished condition is exactly that the central character of π matches the quadratic character attached to the embedding, which translates to ε(½, φ_π, ∧²) = 1. This equality is verified by computing the local root number via the Langlands–Shahidi method or by using the explicit formula of Gan–Gross–Prasad for the epsilon factor of the pair (SO(5), SO(4)). Hence the criterion holds.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the distinction criterion in a relative Langlands setting, first identify the pair (H, G) and the relevant L‑function (Rankin–Selberg, exterior square, or standard). Then recall the precise epsilon‑factor condition: non‑vanishing of the period ⇔ ε(½) = 1. For p‑adic Langlands, remember that completed cohomology realizes the correspondence in families; the key theorems are Emerton’s locally analytic vectors = Colmez functor (n=2) and the patching compatibility for n>2 (Caraiani et al.). Cite the classicality theorem (Boxer–Calegari–Gee–Pilloni) when moving between overconvergent and classical eigenclasses.

The Cohomology of Shimura Varieties and the Realization of Galois Representations

Shimura varieties provide a geometric source of Galois representations attached to automorphic forms. For a Shimura datum (G, X) with G a reductive group over ℚ and X a G(ℝ)‑conjugacy class of homomorphisms 𝕊 → G_ℝ, the associated tower of Shimura varieties Sh_K(G, X) over the reflex field E carries an action of G(𝔸_f) × Gal(Ē/E). The ℓ‑adic étale cohomology groups H^i_et(Sh_K, ℚ_ℓ) decompose as a direct sum over automorphic representations π of G(𝔸_f) of π^K ⊗ ρ_π,ℓ, where ρ_π,ℓ is a continuous ℓ‑adic Galois representation of Gal(Ē/E). The Langlands–Kottwitz method computes the trace of Hecke operators on this cohomology via the stable trace formula, identifying the Frobenius eigenvalues with the Satake parameters of π.

For Hodge‑type Shimura varieties (e.g. Siegel modular varieties, unitary Shimura varieties), the work of Kisin, Caraiani–Scholze, and others establishes the “p‑adic comparison isomorphism” between the étale cohomology and the de Rham cohomology of the associated automorphic vector bundles. This isomorphism, compatible with the Hodge–Tate decomposition, yields the p‑adic Hodge‑theoretic properties of ρ_π,p: it is de Rham with Hodge–Tate weights determined by the infinitesimal character of π_∞. The recent proof of the “local‑global compatibility” at p for regular algebraic cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n (Caraiani–Emerton–Gee–Geraghty–Paskunas–Shin) uses the geometry of the infinite‑level Shimura variety and the Fargues–Fontaine curve to match the Weil–Deligne representation attached to ρ_π,p with the local Langlands parameter of π_p.

In the non‑compact case, the boundary cohomology contributes Eisenstein classes. The work of Harder, Franke, and Schwermer describes the spectral decomposition of L2(G(ℚ)G(𝔸)) and identifies the contribution of the continuous spectrum to the cohomology. The “Eisenstein cohomology” classes realize Galois representations induced from Levi subgroups, providing a geometric incarnation of the Langlands functoriality for Eisenstein series. Recent results on the “classicality of overconvergent cohomology” extend these realizations to p‑adic families, allowing one to interpolate Galois representations across the weight space and to prove cases of the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture for automorphic Galois representations.

The Minimal Representation of Exceptional Groups and its Automorphic Realization

For a split exceptional group G over a local field F, the minimal representation π_min is the unique irreducible unitary representation with the smallest possible Gelfand–Kirillov dimension. It can be constructed via the theta correspondence from the trivial representation of a suitable dual pair (G, G') inside a larger symplectic group, or by quantizing the minimal nilpotent coadjoint orbit. The global minimal representation appears as a residue of an Eisenstein series attached to a maximal parabolic subgroup whose Levi factor is a Heisenberg group. Its automorphic realization was first studied by Kazhdan–Savin for G₂ and by Ginzburg–Rallis–Soudry for F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈.

The Fourier coefficients of the minimal automorphic representation are supported on the minimal nilpotent orbit. The work of Ginzburg–Jiang–Soudry (2006) shows that the global L‑function L(s, π_min, std) equals the product of Riemann zeta functions shifted by the exponents of G. For G₂, the standard L‑function is ζ(s)ζ(s−1)ζ(s−2). The minimal representation also provides a concrete example of the “small” automorphic representations predicted by the Arthur conjectures: its Arthur parameter is the trivial representation of SL(2, ℂ) tensored with the standard representation of the dual group ^L G. The endoscopic classification of Arthur predicts that such representations occur in the discrete spectrum with multiplicity one, and they are precisely the ones that contribute to the “minimal” part of the spectral side of the trace formula.

Recent advances by Gan–Gross–Savin and by Loke–Ma construct the minimal representation for the non‑split forms of exceptional groups using the theory of “small” theta lifts from metaplectic covers. These constructions reveal a uniform pattern: the minimal representation is the unique irreducible quotient of a degenerate principal series induced from a character of a maximal parabolic whose Levi is a Heisenberg group times a smaller exceptional group. The automorphic realization then follows from the residue of the corresponding Eisenstein series, and the non‑vanishing of its Fourier coefficients on the minimal orbit is equivalent to the non‑vanishing of a certain period integral over a unipotent subgroup, linking back to the GGP conjectures for the pair (G, H) where H is the stabilizer of a generic element in the minimal orbit.

The Ichino–Ikeda Formula for Automorphic Periods and Central L‑Values

The Ichino–Ikeda formula gives an exact factorization of the squared period of a factorizable automorphic form on a classical group in terms of a product of local zeta integrals and the central value of a Rankin–Selberg L‑function. For a pair (H, G) = (SO(2n), SO(2n+1)) and a cuspidal automorphic representation π of G(𝔸) with trivial central character, the formula states

|P_H(φ)|² = ½ · L(½, π × σ) / L(1, π, Ad) · ∏_v Z_v(φ_v, φ_v^∨)

where P_H(φ) = ∫H(F)H(𝔸) φ(h) dh is the period integral, σ is the representation of H(𝔸) corresponding to π under the local Langlands correspondence, and Z_v are the local zeta integrals defined by the Rankin–Selberg method. The factor ½ arises from the volume of the quotient H(F)H(𝔸) with respect to the Tamagawa measure.

The proof, completed by Ichino–Ikeda (2010) for unitary groups and extended by Liu, Zhang, and others to all classical pairs, uses the relative trace formula. One constructs a kernel function on G(𝔸) × G(𝔸) whose spectral expansion yields the period squared, while the geometric expansion expresses it as a sum of orbital integrals. Matching the two sides and applying the fundamental lemma for the relevant endoscopic groups yields the factorization. The local zeta integrals Z_v are shown to equal the local Rankin–Selberg integrals of Jacquet–Piatetski‑Shapiro–Shalika, and their product over all places gives the completed L‑function L(s, π × σ).

The formula has profound consequences: it proves the non‑vanishing of the central L‑value implies the existence of a non‑zero period (the “if” direction of GGP), and it provides an explicit expression for the period in terms of special values of L‑functions. In the p‑adic setting, the formula interpolates to give a p‑adic L‑function for the family of automorphic representations, as developed in recent work of Hsieh, Liu, and Zhang. This p‑adic Ichino–Ikeda formula is a key input for approaches to the p‑adic Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture for certain abelian varieties with real multiplication.

  • Confusing the local GGP epsilon factor condition with the global non‑vanishing of the period; the local criterion is necessary but not sufficient for global distinction.
  • Assuming the completed cohomology of any Shimura variety realizes the full p‑adic Langlands correspondence; only Hodge‑type (or abelian‑type) Shimura varieties are known to work, and the correspondence is proved patch‑by‑patch.
  • Treating the minimal representation of an exceptional group as a generic representation; it is highly non‑generic, its Whittaker model is zero, and its Fourier coefficients are supported on the minimal nilpotent orbit.
  • Misapplying the Ichino–Ikeda formula to pairs where the central character of π is non‑trivial; the formula requires trivial central character or a suitable twist by a character of the center.
  • Believing that the relative trace formula for (SO(2n), SO(2n+1)) is fully stabilized for all endoscopic groups; stabilization is known for unitary groups and for some symplectic/metaplectic cases, but the general orthogonal case still requires the fundamental lemma for non‑standard endoscopy.
  • Overlooking that the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence in families (Emerton–Gee stack) depends on the choice of a Hodge–Tate weight; different weights give different locally analytic representations even for the same Galois parameter.
  • Confusing the Arthur parameter of the minimal representation (trivial SL(2) factor) with that of a generic representation (non‑trivial SL(2) factor); the former corresponds to a “small” A‑parameter, the latter to a “large” one.

The Kottwitz Conjecture and Point Counting on Shimura Varieties

The Kottwitz conjecture predicts that the number of points of a Shimura variety over a finite field can be expressed as a sum over certain elliptic endoscopic groups of stable orbital integrals on the associated reductive group. Formulated by Robert Kottwitz in the early 1990s, the conjecture provides a precise link between the geometry of Shimura varieties and the harmonic analysis of the group G over local and global fields. Kottwitz's fixed‑point formula rewrites the Lefschetz trace of Frobenius on the ℓ‑adic cohomology of the Shimura variety as a sum of terms indexed by conjugacy classes in G(ℚ) that are elliptic modulo the center. Each term factors into a product of a volume factor, a stable orbital integral at the finite places, and a factor at the infinite place given by the character of a representation of the real group.

Kottwitz completed the proof for PEL‑type Shimura varieties (1992, 1997) using the Arthur–Selberg trace formula and the stabilization of the geometric side. Shin (2011) and Caraiani–Scholze (2017) established it for Hodge‑type Shimura varieties, as part of proving the local Langlands correspondence for GLₙ over p‑adic fields. The point‑counting formula has become a central tool for realizing Galois representations in the cohomology of Shimura varieties: the alternating sum of the ℓ‑adic cohomology groups yields a virtual Galois representation whose trace at Frobenius matches the point count, and the Kottwitz formula identifies this virtual representation with a sum of automorphic representations transferred from endoscopic groups.

The formula allows computing the trace of Frobenius on the cohomology of a Shimura variety without knowing its full geometry. For the Siegel modular variety of genus g, the point count reduces to a sum over endoscopic groups isomorphic to products of smaller symplectic groups, and the stable orbital integrals are expressed in terms of the Satake transform of the Hecke operators. This explicit description underpins the Langlands–Kottwitz method, comparing the trace formula for G with that of its endoscopic groups to isolate the contribution of a given automorphic representation. The method has proved cases of the global Langlands correspondence for GLₙ and classical groups via endoscopic classification, and constructed Galois representations for regular algebraic cuspidal automorphic representations of GLₙ over CM fields.

The Relative Trace Formula for Spherical Varieties and the Gan–Gross–Prasad Conjectures

Introduced by Jacquet in the 1980s, the relative trace formula compares the spectral expansion of a kernel function integrated over a spherical variety HG with its geometric expansion as a sum of orbital integrals on H. For symmetric or wavefront spherical varieties, the geometric side stabilizes via the fundamental lemma for relevant endoscopic groups, yielding period factorizations in terms of L‑functions. This framework underlies the Gan–Gross–Prasad (GGP) conjectures: for a tempered automorphic representation π of a classical group G, the non‑vanishing of a period integral over a smaller classical group H is equivalent to the non‑vanishing of the central value L(½, π × σ), where σ corresponds to π under the local Langlands correspondence.

Ichino–Ikeda (2010) proved the GGP conjecture for (SO(2n), SO(2n+1)) for unitary groups; Liu, Zhang, and others extended it to all classical groups via the relative trace formula. The proof constructs a test function on G(𝔸) × G(𝔸) whose spectral side gives the squared period integral, while the geometric side expands as orbital integrals on H(𝔸). Matching sides and applying the fundamental lemma for H's endoscopic groups yields |P_H(φ)|² = ½ · L(½, π × σ) / L(1, π, Ad) · ∏_v Z_v(φ_v, φ_v^∨). The local zeta integrals Z_v are the Rankin–Selberg integrals of Jacquet–Piatetski‑Shapiro–Shalika; their product over all places gives the completed L‑function. The factor ½ arises from the Tamagawa volume of H(F)H(𝔸).

Sakellaridis and Venkatesh developed the relative trace formula for general spherical varieties, defining a "spherical variety" with a dense Borel orbit and a "wavefront" condition ensuring period factorization. Their framework includes the GGP conjectures as a special case and predicts a spectral decomposition of L²(H(F)G(𝔸)) in terms of H-distinguished automorphic representations. Beuzart‑Plessis, Liu, Zhang, and others have established the local GGP epsilon factor criterion for all classical groups and proved many global cases via the stabilized relative trace formula. The method also yields p‑adic L‑functions interpolating periods in families, essential for p‑adic Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer analogues for abelian varieties with real multiplication.

Worked Example: Period Factorization for (SO(4), SO(5))
Let π be a cuspidal automorphic representation of SO(5, 𝔸) with trivial central character, and let σ be the corresponding representation of SO(4, 𝔸) under the local Langlands correspondence. Assume the local epsilon factor ε(½, π_v × σ_v) = 1 for all places v. Using the Ichino–Ikeda formula, express the squared period |PSO(4)(φ)|² in terms of the central L‑value L(½, π × σ) and the adjoint L‑value L(1, π, Ad). Identify the local zeta integrals Z_v.
The Ichino–Ikeda formula for the pair (SO(4), SO(5)) reads |PSO(4)(φ)|² = ½ · L(½, π × σ) / L(1, π, Ad) · ∏_v Z_v(φ_v, φ_v^∨). The local zeta integral Z_v is the Rankin–Selberg integral Z_v(φ_v, φ_v^∨) = ∫SO(4, F_v) ⟨π_v(h)φ_v, φ_v^∨⟩ dh, which equals the local L‑factor L(½, π_v × σ_v) up to the normalizing factor given by the local functional equation. Because the global epsilon factor is the product of local epsilon factors and equals 1, the central L‑value does not vanish automatically; the formula shows that the period is non‑zero exactly when L(½, π × σ) ≠ 0. The factor ½ is the Tamagawa volume of SO(4, ℚ)SO(4, 𝔸). Thus the squared period factorizes completely into the product of the central Rankin–Selberg L‑value, the adjoint L‑value, and the local zeta integrals.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the factorization of a period integral, first identify the spherical pair (H, G) and the relevant L‑functions (Rankin–Selberg and adjoint). Write down the Ichino–Ikeda formula with the correct volume factor (often ½ for orthogonal groups). Remember that the local zeta integrals are the Rankin–Selberg integrals, not the local L‑factors themselves; they differ by the local epsilon factor. Check whether the central character of π is trivial; if not, a twist by a character of the center is required before the formula applies.

The Local Langlands Correspondence for Reductive Groups over Local Fields of Positive Characteristic

Let F be a non‑archimedean local field of characteristic p > 0, e.g., F = 𝔽_q((t)). The local Langlands correspondence for a connected reductive group G over F parametrizes irreducible smooth representations of G(F) by Langlands parameters—homomorphisms from the Weil–Deligne group W'_F to the L‑group ^L G. In characteristic zero, Harris–Taylor and Henniart established it for GLₙ; Arthur, Mok, and Kaletha–Mínguez–Shin–White did so for many classical groups. In positive characteristic, Lafforgue (2002) established it for GLₙ using the global Langlands correspondence over function fields and shtuka geometry. Genestier–Lafforgue (2019) constructed it for general reductive groups using the moduli stack of G‑shtukas and geometric Satake over function fields.

The Genestier–Lafforgue correspondence is compatible with parabolic induction, central characters, and the local Langlands correspondence for Levi subgroups. It also respects L‑function and ε‑factor local factors from the Langlands–Shahidi method. A key ingredient is "excursion operators" acting on the cohomology of the G‑shtuka moduli stack, realizing Hecke operators and matching the spectral decomposition of automorphic forms with the trace formula's geometric side. The correspondence is characterized by L(s, π, r) = L(s, r ∘ φ_π) for any algebraic representation r of ^L G.

Unlike in characteristic zero, the positive characteristic correspondence lacks a full classification of supercuspidals via types and covers. V. Lafforgue (2018) addressed the cuspidal part for GLₙ, and Chen–Zhu (2022) treated unramified groups via Fargues–Scholze geometrization, bridging the two settings. Fargues–Scholze constructs the correspondence for any reductive group over a p‑adic field via the Fargues–Fontaine curve; its mod p reduction yields a correspondence over 𝔽_q((t)) expected to match Genestier–Lafforgue. This compatibility remains a major open problem.

The Discrete Spectrum and Arthur Packets for Classical Groups

Arthur's endoscopic classification describes the discrete part of L²(G(F)G(𝔸)) for a classical group G over a number field F via global Arthur parameters. An Arthur parameter is a formal sum ψ = ⊞_i (π_i ⊠ [d_i]), where each π_i is a cuspidal automorphic representation of GLn_i(𝔸) and [d_i] is the d_i‑dimensional irreducible representation of SL₂(ℂ). ψ determines an L‑packet Π_ψ of irreducible admissible representations of G(𝔸); multiplicities in the discrete spectrum are given by a formula involving the component group S_ψ = Cent^L G(ψ)/Z(^L G)^Γ. Arthur (2013) proved it for symplectic and odd orthogonal groups; Mok (2015) and Kaletha–Mínguez–Shin–White (2020) completed it for even orthogonal and unitary groups.

The discrete spectrum decomposes as ⊕_ψ ⊕π ∈ Π_ψ m_ψ(π) π, where m_ψ(π) = 1 if the character of S_ψ attached to π is trivial, else 0. This multiplicity formula refines Arthur's earlier conjecture and follows from trace formula stabilization. Packets Π_ψ are constructed by lifting from endoscopic groups: ψ factors through an endoscopic group H of G, and the packet transfers the stable packet on H to G via the fundamental lemma. Local components are local Arthur packets, parametrized by local ψ_v with the same multiplicity formula for Sψ_v.

A key application is proving the local and global GGP conjectures for classical groups. GGP period integrals isolate representations in Π_ψ distinguished by a smaller classical group H. Distinction translates to ψ being "relevant" for (H, G): the restriction of ψ to ^L H contains the trivial SL₂ representation. The Ichino–Ikeda formula then expresses the period via the central value of the Rankin–Selberg L‑function attached to ψ. The classification also yields the Ramanujan conjecture for generic discrete-spectrum representations: if π is generic, its Arthur parameter lacks a non‑trivial SL₂ factor, so π is tempered everywhere.

  • Assuming the relative trace formula for a spherical variety is always stabilized; stabilization requires the fundamental lemma for relevant endoscopic groups, known for symmetric and wavefront spherical varieties but not all.
  • Treating the positive characteristic local Langlands correspondence as identical to characteristic zero; Genestier–Lafforgue uses G‑shtukas and excursion operators, while characteristic zero uses Harris–Taylor or Arthur–Selberg trace formula.
  • Believing every Arthur packet has multiplicity one; multiplicities are governed by S_ψ and can be zero for representations with non‑trivial associated character.
  • Overlooking that Ichino–Ikeda requires π's central character to be trivial (or suitably twisted); applying it without twisting gives incorrect factorization.
  • Confusing the local epsilon factor criterion for GGP distinction with global period non‑vanishing; the local condition is necessary but not sufficient.
  • Assuming Fargues–Scholze works for all reductive groups over positive characteristic local fields; it's proved for p‑adic fields, and its mod p reduction remains conjectural.
