Simple Harmonic Motion Fundamentals

1.1 Restoring Force and Hooke’s Law

In any system that executes simple harmonic motion the displacement from equilibrium produces a restoring force that is directly proportional to the displacement and directed toward the equilibrium position. This linear relationship is expressed by Hooke’s law, F = –k x, where k is the force constant of the spring measured in newton per metre and x is the instantaneous displacement. The negative sign indicates that the force always opposes the displacement, which is the essential cause of the oscillatory behaviour. Because the force is linear, the resulting acceleration a = F/m = –(k/m) x is also proportional to the displacement, leading to a second‑order differential equation whose solution is a sinusoidal function of time.

The proportionality constant k/m determines the natural angular frequency ω of the oscillator through ω = √(k/m). The period T, the time for one complete oscillation, follows as T = 2π/ω = 2π√(m/k). Frequency f, the reciprocal of the period, is f = 1/T = ω/(2π). These three quantities — angular frequency, period, and frequency — are the primary descriptors that appear in every examination question on simple harmonic motion, and they are all expressed in SI units: rad s⁻¹ for ω, seconds for T, and hertz for f.

1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion

The total mechanical energy of an ideal simple harmonic oscillator remains constant because the restoring force is conservative. At any instant the energy is shared between kinetic energy K = ½ m v² and elastic potential energy U = ½ k x². When the mass passes through the equilibrium position the displacement is zero, so the potential energy vanishes and the kinetic energy reaches its maximum value ½ m v_max² = ½ k A², where A is the amplitude. Conversely, at the turning points the velocity is zero and the entire energy resides as potential energy ½ k A². The constancy of the sum K + U = ½ k A² provides a powerful check on calculations and is frequently used to find the amplitude when the maximum speed is known.

Because the energy exchange is periodic, the average kinetic energy over a cycle equals the average potential energy, each being one quarter of the total energy. This equipartition result is a direct consequence of the sinusoidal nature of the motion and is often tested in conceptual questions that ask for the fraction of total energy that is kinetic at a given displacement.

1.3 Phase, Phase Constant and the General Solution

The complete description of a simple harmonic oscillator requires not only its amplitude and frequency but also its phase at t = 0. The general solution of the equation of motion can be written as x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ) or equivalently x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ′), where φ (or φ′) is the phase constant determined by the initial displacement and velocity. If the oscillator is released from rest at x = A, the phase constant is zero and the motion follows a pure cosine. If it is given an initial velocity from the equilibrium position, the phase constant becomes –π/2 and the motion follows a sine function. The phase constant therefore encodes the initial conditions and is essential when superposing several oscillations, because the relative phase determines whether the superposition is constructive or destructive.

In problems that involve two oscillators of the same frequency, the phase difference Δφ = φ₂ – φ₁ decides the resultant amplitude through the relation A_res² = A₁² + A₂² + 2A₁A₂ cos Δφ. This formula is a direct application of the phasor addition method and appears regularly in questions on interference of waves that share a common frequency.

Pendulum Motion and Energy

2.1 Simple Pendulum Approximation

A simple pendulum consists of a point mass suspended by a light, inextensible string of length L. For small angular displacements θ (typically θ ≤ 10°) the restoring torque is τ = –mgL sin θ ≈ –mgL θ, which is linear in the angular displacement. The equation of motion becomes I α = –mgL θ, where the moment of inertia I = mL² for a point mass. This yields an angular frequency ω = √(g/L) and a period T = 2π√(L/g) that is independent of the mass and the amplitude, provided the small‑angle approximation holds. The independence of mass is a classic demonstration of the equivalence principle and is often asked as a conceptual check.

The small‑angle approximation introduces a systematic error that grows with amplitude. For larger amplitudes the exact period involves an elliptic integral and can be expressed as a series T = 2π√(L/g) [1 + (1/16)θ₀² + (11/3072)θ₀⁴ + …], where θ₀ is the maximum angular displacement in radians. Examination questions sometimes ask for the percentage increase in period when the amplitude is increased from 5° to 20°, requiring the student to apply the first‑order correction term.

2.2 Physical Pendulum and Moment of Inertia

A physical pendulum is any rigid body swinging about a horizontal axis that does not pass through its centre of mass. The restoring torque is τ = –mgh sin θ, where h is the distance from the pivot to the centre of mass. For small angles the equation of motion is I α = –mgh θ, giving ω = √(mgh/I) and T = 2π√(I/(mgh)). The moment of inertia I must be evaluated about the pivot axis, often using the parallel‑axis theorem I = I_cm + mh². This formula reduces to the simple pendulum result when the body is a point mass at distance L = h, because then I = mL² and T = 2π√(L/g).

Typical problems provide a uniform rod of length L pivoted at one end, for which I = (1/3)mL² and h = L/2, yielding T = 2π√(2L/(3g)). Another common configuration is a circular disc of radius R pivoted at a point on its rim, where I = (3/2)mR² and h = R, giving T = 2π√(3R/(2g)). These specific results are frequently memorised because they appear in multiple‑choice questions that test the ability to apply the parallel‑axis theorem quickly.

2.3 Energy Considerations in Pendular Motion

Just as for the spring‑mass system, the total mechanical energy of a pendulum is conserved in the absence of damping. The potential energy relative to the lowest point is U = mgh(1 – cos θ) ≈ ½ mgh θ² for small angles, and the kinetic energy is K = ½ I ω². At the maximum angular displacement θ₀ the kinetic energy is zero and the total energy equals the potential energy mgh(1 – cos θ₀). At the bottom of the swing the potential energy is zero and the kinetic energy reaches its maximum ½ I ω_max² = mgh(1 – cos θ₀). This energy balance allows one to find the maximum angular speed ω_max = √[2gh(1 – cos θ₀)/I] without solving the differential equation.

When a pendulum is used as a timing device, the constancy of the period for small amplitudes is a direct consequence of the linear restoring torque. Any non‑linearity, such as large amplitude or a non‑uniform gravitational field, introduces amplitude dependence and thus limits the accuracy of a pendulum clock. This historical context explains why the development of the balance spring in watches was a crucial advance for portable timekeeping.

Worked Example: Period of a Physical Pendulum
A uniform rod of length 1.20 m and mass 0.80 kg is pivoted at one end and allowed to oscillate in a vertical plane. Calculate the period of small oscillations. Take g = 9.80 m s⁻².
The moment of inertia of a uniform rod about one end is I = (1/3) m L² = (1/3) × 0.80 kg × (1.20 m)² = 0.384 kg m². The distance from the pivot to the centre of mass is h = L/2 = 0.60 m. The angular frequency for a physical pendulum is ω = √(m g h / I) = √(0.80 kg × 9.80 m s⁻² × 0.60 m / 0.384 kg m²) = √(12.288 / 0.384) = √32.0 = 5.66 rad s⁻¹. The period is T = 2π/ω = 2π / 5.66 s⁻¹ = 1.11 s. Therefore the rod completes one small oscillation in approximately 1.11 seconds.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the period of a physical pendulum, always identify the pivot‑to‑centre‑of‑mass distance h and the moment of inertia about the pivot before substituting into T = 2π√(I/(mgh)). A common mistake is to use the moment of inertia about the centre of mass without applying the parallel‑axis theorem.

Wave Equation and Propagation

3.1 Derivation of the One‑Dimensional Wave Equation

Consider a taut string of linear mass density μ under tension T. A small segment of length Δx experiences a net transverse force due to the difference in the vertical components of tension at its ends. For small slopes the vertical component is T ∂y/∂x, so the net force is T(∂y/∂x|x+Δx – ∂y/∂x|_x) ≈ T ∂²y/∂x² Δx. Applying Newton’s second law, μ Δx ∂²y/∂t² = T ∂²y/∂x² Δx, which simplifies to ∂²y/∂t² = (T/μ) ∂²y/∂x². The constant v = √(T/μ) has dimensions of speed and is identified as the wave speed. The resulting partial differential equation ∂²y/∂t² = v² ∂²y/∂x² is the one‑dimensional wave equation, valid for any linear, non‑dispersive medium where the restoring force is proportional to the curvature.

The same equation governs longitudinal waves in a rod, where the tension is replaced by the Young’s modulus Y and the linear density by the volume density ρ, giving v = √(Y/ρ). For sound waves in a fluid the bulk modulus B replaces Y, yielding v = √(B/ρ). Recognising the analogous forms allows a student to transfer the mathematical techniques learned for a string to acoustic and seismic problems.

3.2 General Solution and Travelling Waves

The general solution of the one‑dimensional wave equation can be written as a superposition of a right‑travelling wave f(x – vt) and a left‑travelling wave g(x + vt). Any twice‑differentiable function of the argument (x ∓ vt) satisfies the equation, which means the shape of the disturbance is preserved as it propagates. A particularly important case is the sinusoidal travelling wave y(x,t) = A sin(kx – ωt + φ), where the wave number k = 2π/λ, the angular frequency ω = 2πf, and the phase velocity v = ω/k = λf. The parameters A, λ, f, and φ completely specify a monochromatic wave and are the quantities most often asked for in numerical problems.

Because the wave equation is linear, the principle of superposition holds: the resultant displacement at any point is the algebraic sum of the displacements produced by each wave individually. This linearity is the mathematical foundation for interference, beats, and the formation of standing waves, all of which are explored in the next section.

3.3 Standing Waves and Boundary Conditions

When two identical sinusoidal waves travel in opposite directions along a string fixed at both ends, their superposition produces a standing wave described by y(x,t) = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt). The spatial factor sin(kx) creates nodes at positions where sin(kx) = 0, i.e. x = nλ/2 (n = 0,1,2,…), and antinodes where sin(kx) = ±1. The fixed ends impose the boundary condition y(0,t) = y(L,t) = 0, which forces the allowed wavelengths to satisfy L = nλ/2. Consequently the permitted frequencies are f_n = n v/(2L) = n f₁, where f₁ = v/(2L) is the fundamental frequency. These discrete frequencies are called the normal modes of the string.

The same analysis applies to air columns in pipes. For a pipe closed at one end and open at the other, the closed end is a displacement node (pressure antinode) and the open end is a displacement antinode (pressure node). The allowed wavelengths are λ_n = 4L/(2n–1) and the frequencies are odd multiples of the fundamental: f_n = (2n–1)v/(4L). For a pipe open at both ends the condition is identical to the string, giving all integer multiples. These formulas are standard examination material and are often combined with end‑correction factors in more advanced problems.

Superposition, Interference and Beats

4.1 Principle of Superposition and Interference

The principle of superposition states that when two or more waves overlap in a linear medium, the resultant displacement at any point and time is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements. For two sinusoidal waves of the same frequency and amplitude travelling in the same direction, y₁ = A sin(kx – ωt) and y₂ = A sin(kx – ωt + Δφ), the resultant is y = 2A cos(Δφ/2) sin(kx – ωt + Δφ/2). The amplitude of the combined wave is therefore A_res = 2A cos(Δφ/2). When Δφ = 0 the waves are in phase and constructive interference yields an amplitude of 2A; when Δφ = π they are out of phase and destructive interference gives zero amplitude. This simple cosine dependence on phase difference is the basis for all interference phenomena, from thin‑film colours to the operation of interferometers.

In a typical examination problem the phase difference arises from a path difference Δx, with Δφ = (2π/λ) Δx. Constructive interference occurs when Δx = nλ and destructive interference when Δx = (n + ½)λ. The ability to convert between path difference, phase difference, and the resulting intensity (which is proportional to the square of the amplitude) is a core skill tested in both multiple‑choice and descriptive questions.

4.2 Beats: Amplitude Modulation from Nearby Frequencies

When two waves of slightly different frequencies f₁ and f₂ superpose, the resultant displacement can be expressed as y = 2A cos[π(f₁ – f₂)t] sin[π(f₁ + f₂)t]. The fast oscillation at the average frequency (f₁ + f₂)/2 is modulated by a slowly varying envelope of frequency |f₁ – f₂|. The listener perceives a periodic variation in loudness called beats, with a beat frequency f_beat = |f₁ – f₂|. The beat phenomenon is a direct consequence of the linear superposition of two nearly equal frequencies and does not require any non‑linearity in the medium.

Beats are used practically to tune musical instruments: a musician sounds a reference tone and the instrument’s tone simultaneously, then adjusts the tension or length until the beats disappear, indicating that the frequencies have been matched. In physics examinations, a common question provides two tuning forks of known frequencies and asks for the number of beats heard in a given time interval, which is simply f_beat × Δt. Another variant gives the beat frequency and one of the component frequencies, requiring the student to solve for the unknown frequency, remembering that two solutions are possible (f₂ = f₁ ± f_beat).

4.3 Standing Waves as a Special Case of Superposition

A standing wave can be viewed as the superposition of two identical travelling waves moving in opposite directions. The resultant displacement y = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt) shows that the amplitude varies with position but the phase is the same for all points between adjacent nodes. Energy is not transported along the string; instead it oscillates between kinetic and potential forms within each segment. The nodes are points of zero displacement at all times, while antinodes experience maximum amplitude. The distance between consecutive nodes or antinodes is λ/2, a fact that is often used to determine the wavelength from a photograph of a standing wave pattern.

In a laboratory setting, standing waves are produced on a string driven by a vibrator at one end and fixed at the other. By adjusting the driving frequency, the string can be made to resonate at one of its normal modes. The resonance condition f = n v/(2L) is identical to the condition derived from the boundary‑value problem, illustrating the equivalence of the forced‑oscillation and free‑oscillation viewpoints. Examination questions may ask for the length of the string given the frequency of the third harmonic and the wave speed, or vice versa.

Doppler Effect and Applications

5.1 Derivation of the Doppler Shift for Sound

The Doppler effect describes the change in observed frequency when there is relative motion between a source and an observer in a material medium. For sound waves the medium (air) provides a preferred reference frame. If the source moves toward a stationary observer with speed v_s, the wavelength in front of the source is compressed to λ′ = (v – v_s)/f, where v is the speed of sound in the medium. The observer receives these wavefronts at a rate f′ = v/λ′ = f v/(v – v_s). Conversely, if the observer moves toward a stationary source with speed v_o, the relative speed of the wavefronts is v + v_o, giving f′ = (v + v_o)/λ = f (v + v_o)/v. When both source and observer move along the line joining them, the combined formula is f′ = f (v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s), where the upper signs correspond to motion toward each other and the lower signs to motion away.

It is crucial to note that the speed of sound v depends on the medium’s temperature and composition, not on the motion of the source or observer. This distinguishes acoustic Doppler shifts from the relativistic Doppler effect for light, where the speed of light is invariant and the formula involves the Lorentz factor. In examinations, the acoustic formula is applied to problems involving moving vehicles, rotating sirens, or astronomical objects where the medium can be approximated as stationary air.

5.2 Doppler Effect for Light and Relativistic Considerations

For electromagnetic waves in vacuum there is no material medium, so the classical derivation using a preferred frame fails. The relativistic Doppler shift for light observed when the source and observer recede from each other with relative speed u is f′ = f √[(1 – β)/(1 + β)], where β = u/c and c is the speed of light. For approach the sign of β is reversed. In the low‑speed limit (β ≪ 1) this reduces to the familiar approximation f′ ≈ f (1 ∓ u/c), which mirrors the acoustic formula with v replaced by c. The relativistic formula also predicts the transverse Doppler shift (observed when the motion is perpendicular to the line of sight), a purely relativistic effect with no classical analogue, given by f′ = f/γ where γ = 1/√(1 – β²).

Although the relativistic Doppler effect is more appropriate for astrophysics, many introductory physics examinations restrict the Doppler discussion to sound waves. When a question explicitly mentions light or asks for the redshift of a galaxy, the relativistic formula must be used. Recognising the domain of applicability of each formula is a key discriminator in multiple‑choice questions.

5.3 Practical Applications and Problem‑Solving Strategies

The Doppler effect finds applications in radar speed guns, medical ultrasound, and astronomy. A police radar emits microwaves at a known frequency; the reflected wave from a moving vehicle experiences a double Doppler shift (once upon reception by the vehicle, once upon re‑emission), yielding a frequency shift Δf = 2f v_r/c, where v_r is the radial component of the vehicle’s velocity. In medical Doppler ultrasound, the frequency shift of sound reflected from moving blood cells provides a measure of blood flow velocity. The standard problem‑solving strategy is to identify the source and observer velocities relative to the medium, choose the correct sign convention, and substitute into the appropriate formula. Drawing a diagram with velocity vectors and indicating the direction of wave propagation helps avoid sign errors, which are the most common source of mistakes in Doppler calculations.

Another frequent examination scenario involves a source moving in a circle around a stationary observer. The observed frequency varies sinusoidally between a maximum when the source moves directly toward the observer and a minimum when it moves directly away. The maximum and minimum frequencies are given by f_max = f v/(v – v_s) and f_min = f v/(v + v_s). The difference f_max – f_min can be used to determine the source speed v_s if the emitted frequency and the speed of sound are known. This type of problem tests the student’s ability to apply the Doppler formula to a time‑varying geometry.

The study of oscillations and waves thus connects the simple harmonic motion of a mass on a spring to the propagation of disturbances in continuous media, the interference of coherent waves, the perception of beats, and the frequency shifts caused by relative motion, forming a coherent framework that underpins much of classical and modern physics.

Damped Harmonic Motion and Energy Dissipation

Real oscillators lose energy to their surroundings through viscous drag, internal friction, or radiation. The equation of motion for a linearly damped oscillator of mass m, spring constant k and damping coefficient b is mẍ + bẋ + kx = 0. Introducing the natural angular frequency ω₀ = √(k/m) and the damping ratio ζ = b/(2√(km)) yields the standard form ẍ + 2ζω₀ẋ + ω₀²x = 0. Three regimes appear: under‑damped (ζ < 1) where the motion is oscillatory with an exponentially decaying envelope x(t) = A e^(−ζω₀t) cos(ω_d t + φ) and damped frequency ω_d = ω₀√(1−ζ²); critically damped (ζ = 1) where the system returns to equilibrium without overshoot in the minimum time; and over‑damped (ζ > 1) where two distinct real exponentials govern a slow, non‑oscillatory decay. The mechanical energy E = ½mv² + ½kx² decays as dE/dt = −b v², so the average power dissipated in the under‑damped case is P_avg = ½b ω_d² A² e^(−2ζω₀t). The quality factor Q = 1/(2ζ) quantifies the number of cycles over which the energy falls by a factor e; high‑Q systems such as tuning forks have ζ ≪ 1, while automotive shock absorbers are designed near critical damping to suppress bounce. In examinations, a common twist is to give the logarithmic decrement δ = ln(x_n/xn+1) = 2πζ/√(1−ζ²) and ask for ζ or Q, requiring the student to invert the relation accurately.

Forced Oscillations, Resonance and Quality Factor

When a periodic driving force F(t) = F₀ cos(ωt) acts on a damped oscillator, the steady‑state solution is x(t) = X cos(ωt − φ) with amplitude X = (F₀/m) / √[(ω₀² − ω²)² + (2ζω₀ω)²] and phase lag φ = tan⁻¹[2ζω₀ω/(ω₀² − ω²)]. The amplitude reaches a maximum at the resonance frequency ω_r = ω₀√(1−2ζ²) (for ζ < 1/√2), which is slightly below the natural frequency. At exact resonance the amplitude is X_max = F₀/(2mζω₀²) = Q·F₀/(mω₀²), showing the direct proportionality to the quality factor. The power absorbed from the driver is P(ω) = ½F₀ωX sin φ = ½b ω² X²; its frequency dependence is a Lorentzian centred at ω₀ with full width at half maximum Δω = ω₀/Q. This bandwidth–Q relation Δω·τ = 1 (where τ = Q/ω₀ is the energy decay time) is a cornerstone of resonance phenomena in electrical, mechanical and optical systems. Examiners often test the distinction between amplitude resonance (maximum displacement) and velocity resonance (maximum kinetic energy), the latter occurring exactly at ω = ω₀ regardless of damping. Another frequent trap is to confuse the transient solution, which contains the homogeneous damped terms, with the steady‑state response; only the latter persists after times ≫ τ.

Worked Example: Determining Q from Bandwidth
A series RLC circuit has a resonant frequency f₀ = 1.00 MHz and a measured full‑width at half‑maximum of the current versus frequency curve of Δf = 10 kHz. Calculate the quality factor Q and the damping ratio ζ.
The quality factor for a resonant system is Q = f₀/Δf. Substituting the given values: Q = 1.00×10⁶ Hz / 1.0×10⁴ Hz = 100. The damping ratio is related to Q by ζ = 1/(2Q) = 1/(200) = 0.005. Thus the circuit is lightly damped (ζ ≪ 1) and the resonance peak is sharp. In a mechanical analogue the same Q would imply that the oscillator completes about Q/(2π) ≈ 16 cycles before its energy decays by a factor e.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives a resonance curve, first identify whether the plotted quantity is displacement, velocity or current; the peak frequency shifts for displacement but not for velocity. Write down the standard amplitude formula, note the given parameters, and solve for the unknown symbolically before inserting numbers. This avoids sign errors in the phase expression and makes the bandwidth–Q relation obvious.

Coupled Oscillators and Normal Modes

Two or more oscillators linked by springs or other coupling elements exhibit collective motion that can be decomposed into independent normal modes. For two identical masses m connected by a spring of constant k to fixed walls and coupled to each other by a spring k_c, the equations of motion are mẍ₁ = −k x₁ − k_c(x₁ − x₂) and mẍ₂ = −k x₂ − k_c(x₂ − x₁). Seeking solutions of the form x₁ = A₁ eiωt, x₂ = A₂ eiωt leads to the eigenvalue problem (k + k_c − mω²)A₁ − k_c A₂ = 0, −k_c A₁ + (k + k_c − mω²)A₂ = 0. The characteristic equation yields two normal‑mode frequencies: ω₁ = √(k/m) (the symmetric mode where A₁ = A₂, the coupling spring is unstretched) and ω₂ = √[(k + 2k_c)/m] (the antisymmetric mode where A₁ = −A₂, the coupling spring is maximally stretched). Energy exchanged between the masses beats at the difference frequency Δω = ω₂ − ω₁; this is the classic “beats in coupled pendulums” demonstration. For N identical coupled oscillators with nearest‑neighbour coupling, the normal‑mode frequencies form a band ω_q = √[ω₀² + 4κ sin²(qπ/2(N+1))] where q = 1…N and κ = k_c/m. The continuum limit N → ∞ gives the dispersion relation of a one‑dimensional lattice, a precursor to phonon physics. Examination questions often ask for the normal‑mode coordinates (sum and difference of displacements) and to show that the total energy separates into a sum of independent harmonic oscillator energies, each with its own frequency.

Wave Packets, Group Velocity and Dispersion

A monochromatic wave extends infinitely in space and carries no information. A localized disturbance is built by superposing a narrow band of frequencies around a carrier ω₀, producing a wave packet ψ(x,t) = ∫ A(k) ei(kx−ω(k)t) dk. If the spectral width Δk is small, the amplitude envelope moves with the group velocity v_g = dω/dk|k₀, while the carrier oscillations travel at the phase velocity v_p = ω/k. In a non‑dispersive medium ω = vk, so v_g = v_p = v and the packet retains its shape. In a dispersive medium the relation ω(k) is nonlinear; different spectral components travel at different speeds, causing the packet to spread. For deep‑water gravity waves ω = √(gk) giving v_p = √(g/k) and v_g = ½ v_p; the packet broadens as it propagates. For a quantum‑mechanical free particle ω = ħk²/2m, v_g = ħk/m equals the classical particle velocity, while v_p = ħk/2m is half that value. The spreading rate can be estimated from the second derivative d²ω/dk²; for a Gaussian packet the width grows as σ(t) = σ₀√[1 + (t/τ)²] with τ = 2σ₀²/|d²ω/dk²|. In optics, material dispersion leads to pulse broadening in fibres, quantified by the group‑velocity dispersion parameter β₂ = d²k/dω². Problems frequently provide a dispersion relation ω(k) and ask for v_g, v_p, and the condition for zero group‑velocity dispersion (β₂ = 0), which is crucial for high‑bandwidth communication.

Acoustic Impedance, Reflection and Transmission at Boundaries

When a sound wave encounters a change in medium, part of the wave is reflected and part transmitted. The specific acoustic impedance of a medium is Z = ρc, where ρ is the density and c the speed of sound. For a plane wave normally incident from medium 1 (Z₁) onto medium 2 (Z₂), the pressure reflection coefficient is R = (Z₂ − Z₁)/(Z₂ + Z₁) and the transmission coefficient is T = 2Z₂/(Z₂ + Z₁). The intensity reflection and transmission coefficients are |R|² and (Z₁/Z₂)|T|² respectively, ensuring energy conservation. A large impedance mismatch (e.g., air–water, Z_air ≈ 400 Pa·s/m, Z_water ≈ 1.5×10⁶ Pa·s/m) yields |R| ≈ 0.999, so almost all sound is reflected; this is why underwater sound does not easily enter air. In medical ultrasound, a coupling gel is used to match the impedance of the transducer to skin, reducing reflection losses. For oblique incidence, Snell’s law sinθ₁/c₁ = sinθ₂/c₂ governs the angles, and mode conversion can generate shear waves in solids. The concept of acoustic impedance also underlies the design of anti‑reflection coatings (quarter‑wave layers) and the analysis of standing‑wave patterns in tubes with open or closed ends, where the boundary condition corresponds to Z = 0 (pressure node) or Z → ∞ (pressure antinode). Examination problems often give two media and ask for the fraction of transmitted intensity, or provide a layered structure and require the use of the transfer‑matrix method to compute overall transmission.

  • Mixing up phase velocity and group velocity in dispersive media; they coincide only when ω ∝ k.
  • Using the normal‑incidence reflection formula for oblique incidence without applying Snell’s law and the appropriate impedance for the angle.
  • Forgetting that the intensity transmission coefficient includes the impedance ratio (Z₁/Z₂) factor, not just the pressure transmission coefficient squared.
  • Treating coupled oscillators as independent when the coupling spring constant is comparable to the individual spring constants, leading to incorrect normal‑mode frequencies.

Nonlinear Oscillations and the Onset of Chaos

When the restoring force is not strictly proportional to displacement, the simple harmonic oscillator becomes a nonlinear system. The Duffing oscillator, described by mẍ + cẋ + kx + αx³ = F₀ cos ωt, introduces a cubic stiffness term αx³ that can be either hardening (α > 0) or softening (α < 0). For small amplitudes the motion remains nearly sinusoidal, but as the drive amplitude grows the frequency‑response curve bends, producing a characteristic hysteresis loop: the system jumps between two stable branches at different drive frequencies. This jump phenomenon is a hallmark of nonlinear resonance; exam problems often ask students to sketch the amplitude versus frequency plot and identify the saddle‑node bifurcation points where the branches disappear.

Beyond forced Duffing dynamics, autonomous nonlinear oscillators such as the Van der Pol equation ẍ − μ(1 − x²)ẋ + x = 0 exhibit self‑sustained limit‑cycle oscillations. The parameter μ controls nonlinear damping; for μ ≫ 1 the phase‑space trajectory shows slow drifts along nullclines interrupted by fast jumps—a relaxation oscillation. Numerical integration (e.g., Runge‑Kutta 4) shows the period scales as T ≈ 1.61μ for large μ, a common computational physics result.

Adding a second degree of freedom allows coupled nonlinear oscillators to exhibit chaos. The driven, damped double pendulum or the Lorenz‑type truncation of the Rayleigh‑Bénard convection equations are classic examples. Chaos is diagnosed by a positive largest Lyapunov exponent (λ₁ > 0), a broadband power spectrum, and a strange attractor with fractal dimension. Exam problems often give a Poincaré map of a driven oscillator and ask students to identify periodic, quasiperiodic, or chaotic regimes from the point distribution.

Energy considerations in nonlinear systems differ from the linear case: the total mechanical energy is not a quadratic form, and the virial theorem takes the modified form ⟨T⟩ = (1/2)⟨x·∂V/∂x⟩. For a potential V(x) = ½kx² + ¼αx⁴ the time‑averaged kinetic energy satisfies ⟨T⟩ = ½k⟨x²⟩ + ½α⟨x⁴⟩, a relation used to estimate the amplitude‑dependent frequency via the method of averaging or the Lindstedt‑Poincaré perturbation technique.

Parametric Resonance and the Mathieu Equation

Parametric resonance arises when a parameter (e.g., stiffness or pendulum length) is modulated at twice the natural frequency. The canonical model is the Mathieu equation ẍ + [δ + 2ε cos(2t)]x = 0, where δ = (ω₀/ωₘ)² and ε measures the modulation depth. The stability chart in the (δ, ε) plane consists of alternating stable and unstable tongues emanating from δ = n² (n = 1, 2, …). The principal tongue (n = 1) corresponds to modulation at 2ω₀ and has the widest instability region; its boundaries are approximately δ = 1 ± ε for small ε.

A physical realization is a pendulum whose support oscillates vertically: ẍ + (g/L)[1 + (Aωₘ²/g) cos ωₘ t] sin x = 0. Linearising for small angles yields the Mathieu form with ε = Aωₘ²/(2g). If the drive frequency ωₘ is tuned near 2√(g/L), the upright equilibrium becomes unstable and the pendulum swings with exponentially growing amplitude until nonlinear saturation limits the motion. This principle underlies the Kapitza pendulum, where a rapidly vibrating pivot can stabilise the inverted position—a counter‑intuitive effect seen in advanced mechanics.

In electrical circuits, a varactor diode whose capacitance varies as C(t) = C₀[1 + m cos(ωₘ t)] produces a parametric amplifier. The small‑signal gain is maximal when the pump frequency ωₘ equals twice the signal frequency, and the bandwidth is determined by the damping (quality factor Q). Examination questions often ask for the threshold pump amplitude m_th = 1/Q for the onset of parametric oscillation, derived by equating the negative resistance generated by the pump to the circuit losses.

Floquet theory provides the rigorous framework: solutions are of the form x(t) = eμtp(t) where p(t) has the period of the modulation. The characteristic exponent μ is real in unstable tongues (exponential growth) and purely imaginary in stable regions (bounded oscillations). Computing the monodromy matrix over one modulation period and finding its eigenvalues yields the stability boundaries analytically for the Mathieu equation and numerically for more complex parametric systems.

Worked Example: Principal Parametric Instability Threshold
A simple pendulum of length L = 1.0 m has its support driven vertically with amplitude A = 2 mm. Determine the minimum drive frequency ωₘ (in rad s⁻¹) for which the principal parametric resonance tongue appears, and compute the corresponding modulation index ε. Use g = 9.8 m s⁻².
The natural frequency is ω₀ = √(g/L) = √9.8 ≈ 3.13 rad s⁻¹. Principal parametric resonance requires ωₘ ≈ 2ω₀ = 6.26 rad s⁻¹. The modulation index for vertical drive is ε = Aωₘ²/(2g). Substituting A = 0.002 m and ωₘ = 6.26 rad s⁻¹ gives ε = 0.002 × (6.26)² / (2 × 9.8) ≈ 0.0040. Since ε ≪ 1, the instability tongue is narrow; the exact boundaries are δ = 1 ± ε, i.e. (ω₀/ωₘ)² = 1 ± 0.0040, confirming the resonance condition.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem mentions a periodically varying parameter, immediately write the equation in Mathieu form and identify δ and ε. Sketch the stability chart qualitatively; the principal tongue is the most frequently tested. Remember the threshold condition m_th = 1/Q for parametric amplifiers — it appears in both mechanics and electronics contexts.

Solitons and the Korteweg–de Vries Equation

In dispersive nonlinear media, a balance between nonlinearity and dispersion can produce solitary waves that retain their shape and speed after collisions. The Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation uₜ + 6uuₓ + uₓₓₓ = 0 is the prototypical model for shallow‑water waves and ion‑acoustic pulses in plasmas. Its single‑soliton solution is u(x,t) = (c/2) sech²[√c (x − ct − x₀)/2], where c > 0 is the wave speed proportional to the amplitude. The soliton’s width scales as 1/√c, so taller pulses are narrower.

The inverse scattering transform (IST) provides the exact multi‑soliton solution and proves that the KdV equation is completely integrable. For N solitons the asymptotic state after interaction is a superposition of the original solitons with only phase shifts — no radiation is generated. This elastic collision property distinguishes true solitons from mere solitary waves in non‑integrable systems (e.g., the φ⁴ model), where inelastic collisions produce dispersive tails.

Conserved quantities of KdV include mass ∫u dx, momentum ∫u² dx, and energy ∫(uₓ² − 2u³) dx. The infinite hierarchy of conservation laws follows from the Lax pair formulation Lₜ = [L, A] with L = −∂ₓ² + u and A = −4∂ₓ³ + 6u∂ₓ + 3uₓ. Examination questions may ask to verify that a given functional is conserved by direct differentiation using the KdV equation, or to compute the phase shift Δx = (1/√c₁) ln|(√c₁ + √c₂)/(√c₁ − √c₂)| for a two‑soliton collision.

Physical applications extend beyond water waves: the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS) iψₜ + ψₓₓ + 2|ψ|²ψ = 0 governs envelope solitons in optical fibres. The fundamental bright soliton exists for anomalous dispersion (β₂ < 0) and has the form ψ = √P₀ sech(t/T₀) exp(iζz) with peak power P₀ = |β₂|/(γT₀²). The soliton period z₀ = πT₀²/(2|β₂|) determines the distance over which higher‑order effects (Raman scattering, third‑order dispersion) become significant — a key design parameter for wavelength‑division multiplexed systems.

Waveguides, Modes, and Cut‑off Frequencies

Electromagnetic and acoustic waveguides confine propagation to discrete modes, each with a characteristic cut‑off frequency below which the mode is evanescent. For a rectangular metallic waveguide of dimensions a × b (a > b) the transverse electric (TEₘₙ) and transverse magnetic (TMₘₙ) modes satisfy the dispersion relation ω² = c²(kₓ² + k_y² + k_z²) with kₓ = mπ/a, k_y = nπ/b. The cut‑off wavenumber is k_c = √[(mπ/a)² + (nπ/b)²] and the cut‑off frequency f_c = c k_c / (2π). The dominant mode is TE₁₀ with f_c = c/(2a); all higher modes have larger cut‑offs.

In dielectric optical fibres, the weakly guiding approximation leads to the scalar wave equation for the LPₗₘ modes. The normalized frequency V = (2πa/λ) NA, where a is the core radius and NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) the numerical aperture, determines the number of guided modes. Single‑mode operation requires V < 2.405; for V ≫ 1 the mode count ≈ V²/2. The group velocity dispersion parameter β₂ = d²β/dω² governs pulse broadening; zero‑dispersion wavelength λ₀ can be engineered by adjusting the core/cladding index profile (e.g., dispersion‑shifted fibre).

Acoustic waveguides such as ducts with rigid walls support plane‑wave modes below the first cut‑off f_c = c/(2H) (H = duct height). Above cut‑off, higher‑order modes propagate with phase velocities v_p = c/√[1 − (f_c/f)²] > c and group velocities v_g = c√[1 − (f_c/f)²] < c. The modal impedance Zₘₙ = ρc/√[1 − (f_c/f)²] differs from the free‑field value, affecting radiation coupling at openings. Problems often ask for the transmission loss through a sudden area expansion, which is computed by mode‑matching the pressure and volume velocity across the junction.

Surface acoustic wave (SAW) devices exploit Rayleigh waves on a piezoelectric substrate. The electromechanical coupling coefficient K² determines the velocity shift Δv/v ≈ K²/2 between open‑circuit and short‑circuit boundary conditions. Interdigital transducers (IDTs) launch and detect SAWs; their frequency response is shaped by the aperture and the number of finger pairs. The impulse response of an IDT is essentially the Fourier transform of its electrode weighting function, a concept that links waveguide theory to signal processing.

Quantum Harmonic Oscillator, Coherent States and Uncertainty

The quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian H = p²/(2m) + ½mω²x² is diagonalised by ladder operators a = √(mω/2ħ)(x + ip/mω) and a†, giving eigenvalues E_n = ħω(n + ½). The ground state |0⟩ is a Gaussian wavepacket with minimum uncertainty ΔxΔp = ħ/2. Coherent states |α⟩ = e−|α|²/2 Σ (αⁿ/√n!) |n⟩ are eigenstates of the annihilation operator a|α⟩ = α|α⟩ and remain minimum‑uncertainty packets whose centre follows the classical trajectory x_cl(t) = √(2ħ/mω) Re[α e−iωt].

The Glauber‑Sudarshan P‑representation expresses any density operator as ρ = ∫ P(α) |α⟩⟨α| d²α. For a thermal state P(α) is a Gaussian with variance ⟨n⟩ = 1/(eħω/k_BT − 1); for a number state |n⟩ the P‑function is highly singular (derivatives of δ). The Wigner function W(x,p) = (1/πħ) ∫ ⟨x+y|ρ|x−y⟩ e−2ipy/ħ dy provides a phase‑space quasiprobability; negativity of W signals non‑classicality (e.g., squeezed states).

Squeezed states |α, ξ⟩ = D(α)S(ξ)|0⟩ with S(ξ) = exp[½(ξ*a² − ξa†²)] reduce the variance of one quadrature below the vacuum level at the expense of the conjugate quadrature. The squeezing parameter ξ = r eiθ yields ΔX_θ = (½)e−r, ΔXθ+π/2 = (½)er. In gravitational‑wave interferometers, frequency‑dependent squeezing is injected to beat the standard quantum limit across the detection band.

Examination problems frequently ask to compute the time evolution of ⟨x⟩ and ⟨p⟩ for an initial coherent state displaced by a sudden force, or to derive the Mandel Q‑parameter Q = (⟨n²⟩ − ⟨n⟩²)/⟨n⟩ − 1, which is negative for sub‑Poissonian light (photon antibunching). The connection between the classical action‑angle variables (J, φ) and the quantum number n via J = ħ(n + ½) illustrates the Bohr‑Sommerfeld quantisation rule and appears in semiclassical analysis questions.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Duffing hardening/softening spring sign and drawing the hysteresis loop in the wrong direction.
  • Assuming parametric resonance occurs at the drive frequency equal to the natural frequency instead of twice the natural frequency.
  • Treating all solitary waves as solitons; only integrable models guarantee elastic collisions and exact multi‑soliton solutions.
  • Using the free‑space wavelength λ₀ instead of the guided wavelength λ_g = λ₀/√[1 − (f_c/f)²] when calculating phase shifts in waveguides.
  • Forgetting that coherent states are not orthogonal and that the overlap ⟨α|β⟩ = exp[−½|α−β|² + i Im(α*β)] matters in measurement theory.
  • Neglecting the zero‑point energy ħω/2 when applying the virial theorem to the quantum oscillator; the classical virial theorem has no zero‑point term.
  • Mixing up group velocity dispersion β₂ (ps²/km) with the dispersion parameter D (ps/nm·km); they are related by D = −(2πc/λ²)β₂.

Nonlinear Wave Steepening and Shock Formation

When a finite‑amplitude wave propagates in a medium whose wave speed depends on local amplitude, different parts of the waveform travel at different speeds. In a simple fluid the sound speed increases with compression, so the crest of a compressive pulse overtakes the trough. This leads to progressive steepening of the leading edge until the gradient becomes infinite — a shock front. The process is described by the inviscid Burgers equation ∂u/∂t + u ∂u/∂x = 0 for a one‑dimensional velocity field u(x,t). The method of characteristics shows that characteristics converge at a time t_s = −1 / min(∂u/∂x|t=0), after which a multivalued solution would appear. Physical dissipation (viscosity, thermal conduction) regularises the discontinuity, producing a thin shock layer whose thickness scales as the ratio of viscosity to the jump in velocity. The Rankine‑Hugoniot jump conditions across a steady shock follow from conservation of mass, momentum and energy: ρ₁v₁ = ρ₂v₂, p₁ + ρ₁v₁² = p₂ + ρ₂v₂², and h₁ + ½v₁² = h₂ + ½v₂², where ρ, v, p, h are density, velocity, pressure and specific enthalpy. For a weak shock the pressure jump Δp ≈ ρ₀c₀Δv, and the entropy increase is of third order in the Mach number, confirming that weak shocks are nearly isentropic. In solids, nonlinear elastic constants give rise to similar steepening of longitudinal and shear waves, and the formation of elastic shock waves is exploited in dynamic material testing. The same mathematics underlies traffic flow models, where the car density plays the role of the wave amplitude and the shock corresponds to a sudden traffic jam. Problems may ask to compute the shock formation distance for a given initial sinusoidal profile u(x,0) = u₀ sin(kx), yielding t_s = 1/(u₀k) and a corresponding distance x_s = c₀t_s. Another typical problem involves applying the Rankine‑Hugoniot relations to a normal shock in air with upstream Mach number M₁, deriving the downstream Mach number M₂² = [(γ−1)M₁²+2]/[2γM₁²−(γ−1)] and the pressure ratio p₂/p₁ = 1 + 2γ/(γ+1)(M₁²−1).

Thermal Noise and the Fluctuation‑Dissipation Theorem

Any linear dissipative system in thermal equilibrium exhibits spontaneous fluctuations whose spectrum is directly linked to the dissipative part of its response function. This is the content of the fluctuation‑dissipation theorem (FDT). For a mechanical oscillator with coordinate x, mass m, natural frequency ω₀ and damping coefficient γ, the equation of motion with a random force ξ(t) is mẍ + mγẋ + mω₀²x = ξ(t). The random force has zero mean and a correlation ⟨ξ(t)ξ(t')⟩ = 2mγk_BT δ(t−t') (classical limit). The power spectral density of the displacement is S_x(ω) = (2γk_BT/m) / [(ω₀²−ω²)² + (γω)²]. At resonance ω≈ω₀ this reduces to S_x(ω₀) ≈ 2k_BT/(mω₀²γ). The mean‑square displacement ⟨x²⟩ = ∫ S_x(ω) dω/2π = k_BT/(mω₀²), which is the equipartition result. The FDT also applies to electrical circuits: the voltage noise across a resistor R at temperature T has spectral density S_V(ω) = 4k_BTR (Johnson‑Nyquist noise). For a quantum treatment, the symmetrised noise spectrum becomes S_x(ω) = ħ coth(ħω/2k_BT) Im[χ(ω)], where χ(ω) is the mechanical susceptibility. This quantum FDT predicts zero‑point fluctuations that persist at T=0, with ⟨x²⟩_zp = ħ/(2mω₀). In precision measurements such as gravitational‑wave interferometers, thermal noise from mirror coatings and suspension fibres often dominates the noise budget at frequencies around 100 Hz. The coating Brownian noise spectrum follows from the FDT applied to the elastic loss angle φ(ω): S_x(ω) = (2k_BT/ω) (1−σ²)/(πwY) φ(ω), where σ is Poisson’s ratio, w the beam radius and Y the Young’s modulus. Reducing φ by using crystalline coatings or operating at cryogenic temperatures directly lowers the thermal noise floor. Problems may ask to derive the Johnson‑Nyquist formula from the FDT for an RC circuit, or to compute the thermal force noise on a cantilever of stiffness k and quality factor Q, giving S_F = 4k_BT k/(ω₀Q). Understanding the distinction between the classical white‑force approximation and the full frequency‑dependent quantum spectrum is essential for advanced noise budgeting.

Worked Example: Thermal Force Noise on a Microcantilever
A silicon microcantilever has effective mass m = 2×10⁻¹² kg, resonance frequency f₀ = 100 kHz, and quality factor Q = 10⁴ at room temperature T = 300 K. Compute the single‑sided thermal force noise spectral density S_F at resonance and the corresponding displacement noise spectral density S_x at resonance.
The angular resonance frequency ω₀ = 2πf₀ = 2π×10⁵ rad/s. The spring constant k = mω₀² = 2×10⁻¹² × (2π×10⁵)² ≈ 7.9 N/m. The thermal force noise spectral density (single‑sided) is S_F = 4k_BT k/(ω₀Q). Using k_B = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K, T = 300 K, ω₀ = 6.28×10⁵ rad/s, Q = 10⁴, we get S_F = 4×1.38×10⁻²³×300×7.9 / (6.28×10⁵×10⁴) ≈ 2.1×10⁻³⁰ N²/Hz. The mechanical susceptibility at resonance is |χ(ω₀)| = Q/k = 10⁴/7.9 ≈ 1.27×10³ m/N. The displacement noise spectral density is S_x = |χ|² S_F ≈ (1.27×10³)² × 2.1×10⁻³⁰ ≈ 3.4×10⁻²⁴ m²/Hz. The root‑mean‑square displacement in a 1 Hz bandwidth is √S_x ≈ 1.8×10⁻¹² m, illustrating the extreme sensitivity limited by thermal noise.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives Q, f₀ and m, first compute k = m(2πf₀)². Then use the compact formula S_F = 4k_BT k/(ω₀Q) for force noise and S_x = (Q/k)² S_F for displacement noise at resonance. Memorise the equipartition check ⟨x²⟩ = k_BT/k to verify your result quickly.

Optomechanical Coupling and Radiation Pressure Forces

Radiation pressure couples the electromagnetic field in a cavity to the mechanical motion of a movable mirror. The Hamiltonian for a single cavity mode of frequency ω_c and a mechanical mode of frequency ω_m is H = ħω_c a†a + ħω_m b†b − ħg₀ a†a (b + b†), where a (a†) and b (b†) are the annihilation (creation) operators for the optical and mechanical modes, and g₀ = (ω_c/L) √(ħ/2mω_m) is the single‑photon optomechanical coupling rate (L is the cavity length, m the effective mirror mass). The interaction term shows that each intracavity photon exerts a force F = ħg₀ (b + b†) on the mechanics. In the linearised regime with a strong coherent drive at frequency ω_L, the optical field is split into a large classical amplitude α and a quantum fluctuation δa. The linearised Hamiltonian becomes H_lin = −ħΔ δa†δa + ħω_m b†b + ħG (δa + δa†)(b + b†), where Δ = ω_L − ω_c is the detuning and G = g₀α is the enhanced coupling. For Δ = −ω_m (red detuning) the beam‑splitter interaction G(δa†b + δa b†) enables state‑swap operations and ground‑state cooling. The cooling limit is set by the quantum back‑action noise: the final phonon occupancy n_f ≈ (κ/4ω_m)² + (γ_m n_th)/Γ_opt, where κ is the cavity linewidth, γ_m the mechanical damping, n_th the thermal bath occupancy, and Γ_opt = 4G²/κ the optomechanical damping rate. For Δ = +ω_m (blue detuning) the two‑mode squeezing interaction G(δa†b† + δa b) generates mechanical amplification and parametric instability when Γ_opt > γ_m. The standard quantum limit (SQL) for continuous position measurement arises from the trade‑off between shot noise (imprecision) and radiation‑pressure noise (back‑action). The SQL displacement spectral density is S_x^SQL = ħ/(mω_mγ_m). Beating the SQL requires either squeezed‑light injection or a quantum‑non‑demolition measurement scheme. Experimental platforms range from macroscopic Fabry‑Pérot cavities with gram‑scale mirrors to nanophotonic crystal cavities with femtogram effective masses. Typical problems ask to derive the linearised optomechanical Hamiltonian, to compute the cooperativity C = 4G²/(κγ_m) and discuss the condition C > 1 for ground‑state cooling, or to evaluate the radiation‑pressure force noise spectral density S_F^rp = 2ħ²G²/κ for a given intracavity photon number.

Phononic Crystals and Acoustic Band Gaps

Periodic modulation of elastic properties creates phononic crystals, the acoustic analogues of photonic crystals. For a one‑dimensional stack of alternating layers with densities ρ₁, ρ₂ and sound speeds c₁, c₂, the transfer‑matrix method yields the Bloch condition cos(qΛ) = cos(k₁d₁)cos(k₂d₂) − ½(Z₁/Z₂ + Z₂/Z₁) sin(k₁d₁) sin(k₂d₂), where q is the Bloch wavevector, Λ = d₁+d₂ the lattice constant, k_i = ω/c_i, and Z_i = ρ_i c_i the acoustic impedances. When the right‑hand side exceeds 1 in magnitude, q becomes complex and propagation is forbidden — a band gap opens. The first gap appears near the Bragg condition ω ≈ πc_eff/Λ, where c_eff is an effective sound speed. The gap width scales with the impedance contrast ΔZ/Z. In two and three dimensions, band structures are computed using plane‑wave expansion or finite‑element methods. Complete band gaps (for all polarizations and directions) require sufficient contrast and appropriate lattice symmetry; for example, a square lattice of steel cylinders in epoxy exhibits a complete gap for longitudinal and shear waves when the filling fraction is around 0.3. Defects introduced by removing or altering a unit cell create localized defect modes inside the gap, enabling waveguiding and cavity effects. Phononic crystal slabs with engineered defects are used for high‑Q acoustic resonators and for thermal management by suppressing phonon transport. The same formalism applies to surface acoustic waves on periodically corrugated substrates, where the periodicity folds the dispersion relation and opens gaps at the Brillouin‑zone boundaries. Problems may ask to calculate the first band‑gap edges for a given bilayer using the transfer matrix, or to determine the condition for a complete gap in a 2D square lattice by comparing the lowest transverse band at the X point with the highest longitudinal band at the Γ point. Understanding the role of impedance mismatch and filling fraction is crucial for designing acoustic metamaterials with negative effective density or modulus.

Stochastic Resonance in Bistable Oscillators

Stochastic resonance (SR) is a counter‑intuitive phenomenon where the addition of noise enhances the response of a nonlinear system to a weak periodic signal. The canonical model is a particle in a symmetric double‑well potential V(x) = −½ax² + ¼bx⁴ (a,b > 0) driven by a weak sinusoidal force F(t) = A cos(ωt) and subject to Gaussian white noise ξ(t) with intensity D. The Langevin equation is ẋ = −V'(x) + A cos(ωt) + ξ(t). In the absence of noise, the particle remains trapped in one well if A is below a critical threshold A_c (which scales as a√(a/b)). With noise, the particle can hop between wells at a rate given by Kramers' formula r_K = (ω₀ω_b/2π) exp(−ΔV/D), where ω₀ = √(2a) is the intra‑well frequency, ω_b = √(a) the barrier frequency, and ΔV = a²/(4b) the barrier height. When the Kramers rate matches half the driving frequency (r_K ≈ ω/2π), the hops become synchronised with the periodic force, maximising the signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) at the output. The SNR as a function of D shows a peak — the hallmark of SR. For a bistable system with asymmetric wells, the resonance condition shifts and the SNR peak can be larger. SR has been observed in electronic circuits (Schmitt trigger with noise), in neural models (FitzHugh‑Nagumo), and in climate models (ice‑age cycles). In the quantum regime, a double‑well potential coupled to a thermal bath exhibits quantum stochastic resonance where tunnelling replaces thermal activation at low temperatures. Problems may ask to derive the Kramers rate for a given potential, to plot the SNR versus noise intensity and identify the optimal D, or to explain why SR does not violate the second law of thermodynamics (the noise supplies the energy for the amplified output). A variation may ask how the resonance condition changes if the driving force is not sinusoidal but a square wave, requiring the student to consider the Fourier components and their matching to the hopping rate.

  • Assuming the optomechanical coupling g₀ is enhanced by the intracavity photon number α instead of the amplitude α (G = g₀α).
  • Treating the first Bragg gap in a phononic crystal as a complete gap without checking all polarizations and directions.
  • Misapplying Kramers' rate by using the barrier curvature ω_b instead of the well curvature ω₀ in the prefactor.
  • Forgetting that stochastic resonance requires a sub‑threshold signal; above threshold the SNR decreases monotonically with noise.

Modulational Instability and the Nonlinear Schrödinger Equation

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS) emerges as a universal envelope equation for weakly nonlinear, weakly dispersive wave packets in conservative media. Starting from a generic dispersion relation ω(k) and a cubic nonlinearity, a multiple‑scale expansion yields the standard focusing NLS: i∂ₜA + ½β₂∂ₓ²A + γ|A|²A = 0, where A(x,t) is the complex envelope, β₂ = ∂²ω/∂k² is the group‑velocity dispersion (GVD) coefficient, and γ is the nonlinear coefficient proportional to the third‑order susceptibility. When β₂γ > 0 (focusing regime), a continuous‑wave background A₀ becomes unstable to sideband perturbations — the Benjamin‑Feir or modulational instability (MI). Linearising around A₀ gives the growth rate σ(K) = |A₀|√(2γ|A₀|²β₂K² − β₂²K⁴) for perturbation wavenumber K. The maximum gain occurs at Kₘ = √(γ|A₀|²/β₂) with σₘ = γ|A₀|². This mechanism underlies the breakup of optical pulses into soliton trains in fibers, the formation of rogue waves in deep water, and the self‑modulation of Langmuir waves in plasmas. In the defocusing regime (β₂γ < 0) the background is stable, but dark solitons and dispersive shock waves can appear. The NLS also admits exact breather solutions (Akhmediev, Kuznetsov‑Ma, Peregrine) that describe localized amplification events. Experimental verification of MI spans nonlinear optics (supercontinuum generation), hydrodynamics (wave‑tank measurements of the Peregrine breather), and ultracold atoms (matter‑wave MI in Bose‑Einstein condensates). A key exam‑relevant distinction is that MI requires anomalous GVD (β₂ < 0) for self‑focusing Kerr media, whereas normal GVD leads to stability unless higher‑order dispersion or Raman terms are included. Problems often ask to derive the MI gain spectrum from the linearised NLS, to compute the characteristic length L_MI = 1/σₘ for a given pump power, or to explain why the Peregrine breather is a rational solution that appears from nowhere and disappears without a trace.

Topological Phononics and Edge States in Acoustic Metamaterials

Topological phononics transfers concepts from electronic topological insulators to classical acoustic and elastic waves, yielding robust edge or interface modes protected by bulk topological invariants. The simplest platform is the one‑dimensional Su‑Schrieffer‑Heeger (SSH) chain of alternating spring‑mass couplings with intra‑cell stiffness κ₁ and inter‑cell stiffness κ₂. The Bloch Hamiltonian H(q) = (κ₁ + κ₂ cos q)σₓ + κ₂ sin q σᵧ (σ are Pauli matrices in the sublattice basis) possesses a chiral symmetry that quantises the Zak phase to 0 or π. When κ₁ < κ₂ the Zak phase is π, signalling a nontrivial topology; a finite chain then hosts zero‑frequency edge modes localised at the ends. In two dimensions, valley‑Hall topological insulators are realised by breaking inversion symmetry in a sonic crystal of coupled resonators (e.g., a honeycomb lattice with staggered on‑site potentials). The valley Chern numbers Cᵥ = ±½ at the K and K′ points lead to valley‑protected edge states along domain walls between regions of opposite valley Chern number. These states are immune to backscattering from sharp bends or disorder that does not mix valleys. A related spin‑Hall phase uses pseudo‑spin degeneracy (e.g., degenerate dipole modes) and a synthetic gauge field to open a full gap with spin‑Chern numbers ±1, yielding helical edge states where forward and backward propagation are locked to pseudo‑spin. Experimental demonstrations include 3D‑printed acoustic crystals with valley‑Hall edge waveguides, active circuits implementing non‑Hermitian SSH models, and mechanical metamaterials with rotating spinners that break time‑reversal symmetry to achieve a Chern insulator with chiral edge modes. Exam questions may ask to compute the Zak phase for a given SSH parameter set, to sketch the bulk‑edge correspondence for a valley‑Hall domain wall, or to explain why valley protection fails when intervalley scattering is introduced by atomic‑scale defects. The distinction between symmetry‑protected (chiral, valley, spin) and intrinsic (Chern) topology is crucial: the former requires the preserving symmetry to remain unbroken at the boundary, while the latter is robust against any perturbation that does not close the bulk gap.

Worked Example: Zak Phase of an Acoustic SSH Chain
Consider an infinite 1D acoustic SSH chain with intra‑cell spring constant κ₁ = 2 N/m and inter‑cell spring constant κ₂ = 5 N/m. The unit cell contains two masses m = 1 kg. Determine the topological phase and predict whether a finite chain with open boundaries will host zero‑frequency edge modes.
The Bloch Hamiltonian for the SSH model in the sublattice basis is H(q) = dₓ(q)σₓ + dᵧ(q)σᵧ with dₓ = κ₁ + κ₂ cos q, dᵧ = κ₂ sin q. The Zak phase is the Berry phase across the Brillouin zone: Z = i∫₋π^π ⟨u(q)|∂_q|u(q)⟩ dq, where |u(q)⟩ is the occupied eigenvector. For the SSH model, the Zak phase is quantised to 0 (trivial) when |κ₁| > |κ₂| and π (nontrivial) when |κ₁| < |κ₂|. Here κ₁ = 2, κ₂ = 5, so |κ₁| < |κ₂|. Therefore the Zak phase is π, indicating a nontrivial topological phase. By the bulk‑edge correspondence, a finite chain with open ends will support a zero‑frequency mode localised at each end. The mode shape decays exponentially into the bulk with decay length ξ = a/ln(κ₂/κ₁) where a is the lattice constant. Thus the chain hosts topologically protected edge modes.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for a topological invariant, first identify the symmetry class (chiral, time‑reversal, particle‑hole) and the spatial dimension. For 1D chiral‑symmetric systems, the Zak phase or winding number is the relevant invariant; compute it by checking the sign of the mass term at high‑symmetry points. For 2D valley‑Hall systems, evaluate the valley Chern number from the Berry curvature near K/K′ points. Always verify that the protecting symmetry is preserved at the boundary — if the boundary breaks the symmetry, the edge mode may become gapped. Sketching the bulk band structure and marking the gap‑closing points helps visualise the topological transition.

Non‑Hermitian Physics and PT‑Symmetric Oscillators

Non‑Hermitian Hamiltonians with balanced gain and loss can exhibit entirely real spectra if they respect parity‑time (PT) symmetry: the Hamiltonian commutes with the combined operator PT, where P reflects space (x → −x) and T performs complex conjugation (i → −i). A canonical example is a pair of coupled oscillators, one with gain +iγ and the other with loss −iγ, described by H = (p₁² + ω²x₁²)/2 + (p₂² + ω²x₂²)/2 + κx₁x₂ + iγ(x₁p₁ − x₂p₂)/2. In matrix form, the eigenvalue problem yields frequencies ω± = √(ω² + κ² − γ² ± 2κ√(ω² − γ²)). For γ < γ_EP = ω, the square root is real and the spectrum is entirely real (exact PT phase). At γ = γ_EP the two eigenvalues and eigenvectors coalesce — an exceptional point (EP) of order two. For γ > γ_EP the eigenvalues become complex conjugate pairs (broken PT phase), leading to exponential growth of one mode and decay of the other. EPs are branch‑point singularities where the Hamiltonian becomes defective (non‑diagonalisable). They enhance sensitivity: a small perturbation ε splits the eigenvalues as √ε rather than linearly, enabling ultra‑sensitive sensors. PT‑symmetric acoustics has been realised with paired resonators where one is actively pumped (gain) and the other is damped (loss). In optics, PT‑symmetric waveguide arrays show power oscillations and unidirectional invisibility near the EP. A key exam concept is the distinction between Hermitian degeneracies (diabolic points) where eigenvectors remain orthogonal, and non‑Hermitian EPs where eigenvectors become parallel. Problems may ask to locate the EP for a given gain‑loss parameter, to compute the Petermann factor (excess noise) near an EP, or to design a PT‑symmetric dimer that acts as a coherent perfect absorber at the EP. The non‑Hermitian skin effect — accumulation of all eigenmodes at one boundary under open boundary conditions — is another hallmark of non‑reciprocal systems with asymmetric hopping, leading to a breakdown of the conventional bulk‑edge correspondence.

Quantum Optomechanics: Ground‑State Cooling and Entanglement

Cavity optomechanics studies the interaction between a mechanical oscillator (frequency ωₘ, mass m) and an electromagnetic cavity mode (frequency ω_c) via radiation pressure. The Hamiltonian in a frame rotating at the laser drive frequency ω_L is H = −ℏΔ a†a + ℏωₘ b†b − ℏg₀ a†a (b + b†) + iℏE(a† − a), where Δ = ω_L − ω_c is the detuning, a (b) annihilates a cavity photon (phonon), g₀ = (ω_c/L)√(ℏ/2mωₘ) is the single‑photon coupling rate, and E is the drive amplitude. Linearising around the steady‑state intracavity field α = ⟨a⟩ gives the beam‑splitter interaction H_int = −ℏG(a†b + ab†) for red detuning Δ = −ωₘ (G = g₀α). This enables sideband cooling: anti‑Stokes scattering removes phonons, with final occupancy n_f ≈ (κ/4ωₘ)² + n_th γₘ/Γ_opt, where κ is the cavity linewidth, γₘ the mechanical damping, n_th the thermal bath occupancy, and Γ_opt = 4G²/κ the optomechanical damping. Ground‑state cooling (n_f < 1) requires the resolved‑sideband regime ωₘ ≫ κ and strong cooperativity C = 4G²/(κγₘ) ≫ n_th. Quantum backaction arises from the Stokes process (a†b† + ab) which adds noise; it is suppressed by the same sideband resolution. Entanglement between the mechanical and optical modes can be generated via two‑mode squeezing with blue detuning Δ = +ωₘ, yielding the interaction H_sq = −ℏG(a†b† + ab). The Duan criterion for continuous‑variable entanglement is ⟨(Δu)²⟩ + ⟨(Δv)²⟩ < 2, where u = x₁ − x₂, v = p₁ + p₂ are collective quadratures. Experiments with membrane‑in‑the‑middle setups and superconducting microwave circuits have achieved n_f ≈ 0.1 and demonstrated optomechanical entanglement. A typical exam problem asks to derive the linearised quantum Langevin equations, compute the steady‑state covariance matrix, and evaluate the logarithmic negativity as a function of cooperativity and thermal occupancy. Another common twist is to consider the effect of mechanical frequency modulation (parametric drive) on the cooling limit, which can lead to squeezed mechanical states even without a blue‑detuned drive.

Frequency Combs and Nonlinear Resonance in Microresonators

Kerr‑nonlinear microresonators convert a continuous‑wave pump into a broadband frequency comb via cascaded four‑wave mixing (FWM). The dynamics are governed by the Lugiato‑Lefever equation (LLE) for the intracavity field ψ(θ,τ): ∂_τψ = [−1 − iΔ + i∂_θ² + i|ψ|²]ψ + F, where θ is the azimuthal coordinate, τ the slow time, Δ the normalised detuning, and F the pump amplitude. The LLE admits homogeneous steady states and, above a parametric instability threshold, Turing patterns (primary combs) with equally spaced lines. Further increasing the pump leads to soliton solutions — dissipative Kerr solitons (DKS) — which correspond to mode‑locked pulse trains in the time domain and smooth, broadband combs in the frequency domain. The soliton existence range is bounded by the lower detuning limit Δ_min = √3 (for anomalous GVD) and the upper limit where the soliton collapses into chaos. The comb line spacing equals the free‑spectral range (FSR) of the resonator, typically 10–1000 GHz. Dispersion engineering (waveguide geometry, mode coupling) is critical: anomalous GVD (β₂ < 0) supports bright solitons, while normal GVD supports dark pulses or platicons. Higher‑order dispersion (β₃, β₄) induces dispersive waves (Cherenkov radiation) that extend the comb spectrum but can perturb soliton stability. Thermal effects (thermo‑optic coefficient) cause a dynamic detuning shift that must be compensated for stable soliton access; techniques include pump‑frequency sweeping, auxiliary heating, or self‑injection locking. Applications span optical clocks, astrophysical spectrometer calibration, and coherent communications. Exam questions often require deriving the parametric oscillation threshold P_th = (πn₀²V_eff)/(8n₂Q²λ) from the coupled‑mode equations, calculating the soliton pulse duration τ_s = √(−β₂/γP_s) from the balance of dispersion and nonlinearity, or explaining the role of the Raman self‑frequency shift in limiting the long‑wavelength edge of the comb. The distinction between primary combs (modulational instability) and soliton combs (mode‑locking) is a frequent conceptual trap.

  • Confusing the sign of the group‑velocity dispersion β₂ when identifying the focusing regime for modulational instability; MI requires β₂γ > 0, not merely γ > 0.
  • Assuming that a nontrivial Zak phase in an SSH chain guarantees edge modes for any boundary termination; the boundary must preserve the chiral symmetry that quantises the invariant.
  • Treating an exceptional point as a standard diabolic point and using non‑degenerate perturbation theory; near an EP the eigenvalue splitting scales as the square root of the perturbation.
  • Neglecting the quantum backaction term (Stokes scattering) when estimating the ground‑state cooling limit; the final phonon occupancy includes both the quantum backaction limit (κ/4ωₘ)² and the thermal contribution.
  • Equating the parametric oscillation threshold with the soliton existence threshold in a Kerr microresonator; the former marks the onset of primary combs, while solitons require higher pump power and specific detuning.
  • Overlooking the role of higher‑order dispersion β₃ in generating dispersive waves that can destabilise a dissipative Kerr soliton even when the second‑order dispersion is anomalous.
  • Misapplying the Duan entanglement criterion by using the wrong collective quadrature definitions for the optomechanical two‑mode state; the correct combinations are u = x₁ − x₂ and v = p₁ + p₂ for the beam‑splitter interaction.
