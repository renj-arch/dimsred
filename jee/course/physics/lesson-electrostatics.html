What is Electrostatics?

Electrostatics deals with stationary electric charges, the forces between them, and the electric fields they produce. Key topics for NEET include Coulomb's law, electric field and potential, Gauss's law, capacitors, and dielectrics. Understanding charge interactions and electric field concepts is fundamental for this topic.

Key Concepts

Coulomb's Law: F = kq1q2/r2, where k = 1/(4πε0) = 9 × 109 Nm2/C2. Electric Field: E = F/q = kQ/r2. Electric Potential: V = kQ/r, and potential difference ΔV = W/q. Gauss's Law: ΦE = &oblique8;E·dA = Qenc/ε0. Capacitance: C = Q/V. For parallel plate capacitor: C = ε0A/d. Energy stored in capacitor: U = (1/2)CV2 = Q2/(2C).

Solved Examples

Example 1
Two charges of +2 μC and +8 μC are placed 10 cm apart. Find the point on the line joining them where the electric field is zero.

a) 2.5 cm from smaller charge b) 3.33 cm from smaller charge c) 4 cm from smaller charge d) 5 cm from smaller charge
Solution: Let x cm from the 2 μC charge. The electric field is zero when E1 = E2. k(2)/x2 = k(8)/(10 - x)2. Taking square roots: √2/x = √8/(10 - x). √2/x = 2√2/(10 - x). 1/x = 2/(10 - x). 10 - x = 2x, so 3x = 10, x = 3.33 cm. Hence option (b) is correct.
Example 2
A parallel plate capacitor has plates of area 0.1 m2 separated by 2 mm. Find its capacitance. (ε0 = 8.85 × 10-12 C2/Nm2)

a) 44.25 pF b) 88.5 pF c) 22.125 pF d) 442.5 pF
Solution: C = ε0A/d = (8.85 × 10-12 × 0.1)/(2 × 10-3) = (8.85 × 10-13)/(2 × 10-3) = 4.425 × 10-10 F = 442.5 pF. Hence option (d) is correct.
Example 3
Find the potential at a distance of 30 cm from a point charge of 5 μC.

a) 1.5 × 105 V b) 1.5 × 104 V c) 4.5 × 105 V d) 4.5 × 104 V
Solution: V = kQ/r = (9 × 109 × 5 × 10-6)/(0.3) = (45 × 103)/0.3 = 1.5 × 105 V. Hence option (a) is correct.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For electric field zero problems, use the ratio method: the point is closer to the smaller charge.
  • For capacitors in series, use 1/Ceq = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + ...; for parallel, Ceq = C1 + C2 + ...
  • In problems involving dielectric insertion, remember that capacitance increases by factor K (dielectric constant).
  • For work done in moving a charge between two points, use W = q(VB - VA).
Pro Tip
For NEET, always convert all units to SI (meters, Coulombs, etc.) before plugging into formulas. Charge is typically given in μC, so remember to multiply by 10-6 to convert to Coulombs.

Gauss's Law in Non‑Uniform Fields and Conductors

Gauss's law in integral form states that the total electric flux through a closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by the permittivity of free space, ΦE = ∮ E·dA = Qenc/ε₀. The differential form, ∇·E = ρ/ε₀, applies when charge density varies continuously. In practice, the integral form is used by choosing a Gaussian surface matching the charge distribution's symmetry. For a point charge or spherically symmetric distribution, a concentric sphere yields E·4πr² = Qenc/ε₀, giving the inverse‑square field. For an infinitely long line charge with linear density λ, a coaxial cylinder of radius r and length L gives E·2πrL = λL/ε₀, so E = λ/(2πε₀r). A uniformly charged infinite plane sheet produces a constant field E = σ/(2ε₀) on each side, independent of distance.

When the field is non‑uniform but possesses planar, cylindrical, or spherical symmetry, the same Gaussian surfaces remain valid because the flux integral reduces to E times the area where E is constant and normal to the surface. If symmetry is broken — for example, a point charge near a conducting plane — the method of images restores symmetry by replacing the conductor with an image charge, permitting Gauss's law to find the field. In conductors at electrostatic equilibrium the electric field inside the material is zero; any excess charge resides on the outer surface. The surface charge density adjusts so that the field just outside is normal to the surface with magnitude σ/ε₀. A cavity inside a conductor carries no net charge on its inner surface unless a charge is placed inside; then an induced charge of opposite sign appears on the cavity wall, while the outer surface acquires the same total charge as the inserted charge.

These results yield practical rules. The field inside a hollow conductor is zero regardless of external fields — the basis of electrostatic shielding. The potential of a conductor is constant throughout its volume, so the work done moving a test charge between any two points on or inside it is zero. When a conductor is placed in an external uniform field, induced surface charges create a dipole‑like distribution that cancels the field inside; the total induced charge remains zero if the conductor was initially neutral. A common mistake is assuming the field just outside a conductor is always σ/ε₀; this holds only for the normal component, while the tangential component is zero by the equilibrium condition.

Limitations of Gauss's law arise when symmetry is insufficient to pull E out of the integral; one must then resort to Coulomb's law integration or numerical methods. Another pitfall is forgetting that ε₀ refers to vacuum permittivity; if a dielectric fills the Gaussian volume, the enclosed free charge must be used with ε = Kε₀, or bound charge accounted for separately. Finally, Gauss's law states the total flux, not the field at a point; it cannot directly give the field at a point without symmetry.

In summary, mastering Gauss's law requires recognizing the charge configuration's symmetry, selecting an appropriate Gaussian surface, and correctly handling conductors and dielectrics. The law is a powerful shortcut for highly symmetric problems and a conceptual tool for understanding shielding, capacitance, and the behavior of charges in materials.

Energy Storage and Dielectric Polarisation in Capacitors

The energy stored in a capacitor derives from the work required to transfer charge from one plate to the other against the growing potential difference. For a capacitor of capacitance C charged to final voltage V, the incremental work dW = V dq = (q/C) dq. Integrating from 0 to Q gives U = Q²/(2C) = ½ CV² = ½ QV. This energy resides in the electric field between the plates; the energy density is u = ½ ε₀E² in vacuum. When a linear dielectric of constant K fills the space, the capacitance increases to C = K C₀, and the energy density becomes u = ½ εE² = ½ Kε₀E². If the capacitor remains connected to a battery while the dielectric is inserted, the voltage stays constant, the charge increases to Q = K Q₀, and the stored energy rises to U = ½ K C₀V² = K U₀. The extra energy is supplied by the battery, which does work V dQ.

If the capacitor is charged and then disconnected before the dielectric is inserted, the charge Q remains fixed. The voltage drops to V = V₀/K, and the stored energy falls to U = U₀/K. The difference U₀ − U = U₀(1 − 1/K) is the mechanical work done by the electric field in pulling the dielectric into the capacitor. This force can be calculated from the gradient of the energy with respect to the insertion distance, F = −dU/dx. For a parallel‑plate capacitor partially filled, the force tends to draw the dielectric further in, a principle used in electrostatic actuators.

Dielectric polarisation is the microscopic origin of the increased capacitance. An external field aligns permanent dipoles or induces dipoles in the material, creating bound surface charges that partially cancel the free‑charge field. The polarisation vector P = ε₀χₑE, where χₑ = K − 1 is the electric susceptibility. The bound surface charge density is σb = P·n̂. The net field inside the dielectric is E = E₀/K, where E₀ is the field without the dielectric; this reduction for a given free charge is why the capacitance rises.

When capacitors are combined in series or parallel with dielectrics, the equivalent capacitance follows the usual rules but each capacitor’s capacitance already includes its dielectric constant. For series combinations, 1/Ceq = Σ 1/Ci; for parallel, Ceq = Σ Ci. If a single capacitor has multiple dielectric layers stacked normal to the field, it behaves as series capacitors with thicknesses di and constants Ki. If the dielectrics are side‑by‑side parallel to the plates, they act as parallel capacitors with areas Ai.

Practical limits include dielectric breakdown, where the field exceeds the material’s dielectric strength and a conductive path forms, and leakage currents due to finite resistivity. The maximum energy density a capacitor can store is roughly ½ ε₀K Ebreakdown². Choosing a dielectric with high K and high breakdown strength is crucial for high‑energy applications such as pulse power systems. Understanding these mechanisms allows one to predict how a capacitor behaves under varying voltage, frequency, and temperature conditions.

Worked Example: Energy Change on Dielectric Removal
A parallel‑plate capacitor of plate area A = 0.02 m² and separation d = 1 mm is filled with a dielectric of constant K = 4. It is charged to V = 100 V and then disconnected from the source. The dielectric is slowly withdrawn completely. Calculate the work done by the external agent in removing the dielectric.
Initial capacitance with dielectric: C₁ = Kε₀A/d = 4 × 8.85×10⁻¹² × 0.02 / 0.001 = 7.08×10⁻¹⁰ F. Initial charge Q = C₁V = 7.08×10⁻¹⁰ × 100 = 7.08×10⁻⁸ C. Initial energy U₁ = Q²/(2C₁) = (7.08×10⁻⁸)² / (2 × 7.08×10⁻¹⁰) = 3.54×10⁻⁶ J. After removal, capacitance C₂ = ε₀A/d = 1.77×10⁻¹⁰ F. Charge stays Q. Final energy U₂ = Q²/(2C₂) = (7.08×10⁻⁸)² / (2 × 1.77×10⁻¹⁰) = 1.416×10⁻⁵ J. The energy increases by ΔU = U₂ − U₁ = 1.062×10⁻⁵ J. Since the capacitor is isolated, this increase equals the mechanical work done by the external agent against the attractive electrostatic force. Therefore the work done is 1.06×10⁻⁵ J.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for work done in inserting or removing a dielectric, first decide whether the capacitor stays connected to a battery (constant V) or is isolated (constant Q). Write the energy expressions for the two states using the appropriate capacitance values. The work done by the external agent equals the change in stored energy if the charge is fixed; if the voltage is fixed, the battery supplies or absorbs energy, so the external work is the difference between the energy change and the battery work. Always keep track of signs: positive work means the agent must supply energy.

Transient Analysis of RC and RL Circuits

In an RC series circuit connected to a DC source V through a switch, the voltage across the capacitor vC(t) obeys the first‑order differential equation V = iR + vC with i = C dvC/dt. Substituting gives RC dvC/dt + vC = V. The solution for a capacitor initially uncharged is vC(t) = V(1 − e−t/τ) where τ = RC is the time constant. The charging current is i(t) = (V/R) e−t/τ. After a long time (t ≫ τ) the capacitor voltage reaches V and the current falls to zero. During discharging through the resistor alone, the equation becomes RC dvC/dt + vC = 0, yielding vC(t) = V₀ e−t/τ and i(t) = −(V₀/R) e−t/τ. The energy initially stored ½ CV₀² is dissipated as heat in the resistor.

For an RL series circuit with a DC source, the governing equation is V = iR + L di/dt. With zero initial current, the solution is i(t) = (V/R)(1 − e−t/τ) where τ = L/R. The inductor voltage is vL(t) = V e−t/τ. When the source is removed and the inductor discharges through the resistor, the current decays as i(t) = I₀ e−t/τ and the inductor voltage reverses polarity to maintain current continuity. The magnetic energy ½ LI₀² is again dissipated in the resistor.

The time constant τ determines how quickly the transient dies out. After one τ the charging capacitor reaches about 63 % of its final voltage; after five τ it is over 99 % charged. Similarly, the RL current reaches 63 % of its steady value in one τ. These rules of thumb are useful for estimating circuit response without solving the full exponential. In practical circuits, parasitic capacitances and inductances create additional time constants that can affect high‑speed switching.

Initial and final conditions are critical. The capacitor voltage cannot change instantaneously, so vC(0⁺) = vC(0⁻). The inductor current cannot change instantaneously, so iL(0⁺) = iL(0⁻). These continuity conditions provide the boundary values needed to solve the differential equations for any switching scenario. When multiple switches operate at different times, the circuit is analysed piecewise, using the final state of one interval as the initial state of the next.

Applications of RC and RL transients include timing circuits (e.g., 555 timer), debouncing switches, filter design (low‑pass RC, high‑pass RL), and snubber circuits that protect switches from voltage spikes. In each case the designer selects R, C, or L to achieve a desired τ. Understanding the exponential nature of the response allows quick estimation of rise times, fall times, and settling times, which are frequently tested in circuit analysis problems.

Kirchhoff's Laws in Multi‑Loop Networks with Dependent Sources

Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) states that the algebraic sum of currents entering a node is zero. Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) states that the algebraic sum of voltage drops around any closed loop is zero. These laws hold for any linear or non‑linear, time‑invariant or time‑varying network, provided the lumped‑element model is valid. When dependent (controlled) sources are present, the controlling variable (voltage or current) must be expressed in terms of the unknown node voltages or mesh currents before writing the equations.

A voltage‑controlled voltage source (VCVS) has output Vout = μ Vctrl, where μ is the gain. A current‑controlled current source (CCCS) has Iout = β Ictrl. Similarly, a voltage‑controlled current source (VCCS) gives Iout = g Vctrl (transconductance g), and a current‑controlled voltage source (CCVS) gives Vout = r Ictrl (transresistance r). In nodal analysis, each dependent source introduces an extra equation linking its output to the controlling variable. For example, a VCCS connected between nodes a and b with control voltage between nodes c and d adds a term g(Vc − Vd) to the KCL equations at nodes a and b.

In mesh analysis, a dependent voltage source appears as a voltage rise or drop in the KVL equation of the mesh it belongs to, expressed in terms of the mesh currents. A dependent current source that lies on a shared branch forces a constraint between the two mesh currents, reducing the number of independent mesh equations. One common technique is to assign a supermesh that excludes the dependent current source and write KVL around the supermesh, while using the source's controlling equation as an additional relation.

Superposition cannot be applied directly when dependent sources are present because they are not independent sources; they must remain active during each superposition step. However, one can still use superposition on the independent sources while keeping the dependent sources in the circuit, solving the resulting linear system for each independent source and summing the responses. This approach is valid because the overall system remains linear.

When solving large networks, it is often efficient to use matrix methods. The nodal admittance matrix Y is built from conductances and the contributions of VCCS and CCCS elements. The right‑hand side vector contains independent current sources and the effects of independent voltage sources (converted to Norton equivalents). Dependent sources add off‑diagonal terms that couple nodes. Solving Y V = I yields all node voltages. Similarly, the mesh impedance matrix Z incorporates CCVS and VCVS couplings. Modern circuit simulators perform these steps automatically, but understanding the manual formulation is essential for interpreting results and for exam problems that require symbolic expressions.

Electrostatic Shielding and Faraday Cage Principles

Electrostatic shielding relies on the property that a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is an equipotential volume. When an external static electric field is applied, free charges in the conductor redistribute on its surface until the internal field cancels the external field. The result is zero electric field everywhere inside the conductor material and in any cavity that contains no charge. This principle holds regardless of the conductor's shape, provided it forms a closed surface. A Faraday cage is a conductive enclosure — solid or mesh — that blocks external static fields from reaching its interior.

For a solid conductor, the shielding is perfect for static fields. For a mesh or perforated conductor, the shielding effectiveness depends on the size of the apertures relative to the wavelength of the field. For static (DC) fields, even a coarse mesh provides excellent attenuation because the field lines terminate on induced charges on the wires. As the frequency increases, the mesh begins to leak; the rule of thumb is that holes should be smaller than about one‑tenth of the wavelength for good attenuation. At microwave frequencies, a solid conductive sheet or a waveguide‑beyond‑cutoff design is required.

The interior of a Faraday cage remains at a constant potential if the cage itself is not grounded. Grounding the cage fixes its potential to earth and provides a path for any induced charge to flow, which can improve shielding against low‑frequency fields that might otherwise couple capacitively. In practice, coaxial cables use a cylindrical conductive shield around the central conductor; the shield carries the return current and prevents external fields from inducing noise on the signal line. The shield's effectiveness is quantified by its transfer impedance, which should be as low as possible.

Limitations of electrostatic shielding appear with time‑varying magnetic fields. A changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force (EMF) around any loop according to Faraday's law, ∮ E·dl = −dΦB/dt. This induced electric field is non‑conservative and can penetrate a conductive enclosure; eddy currents oppose it, but at low frequencies or with poor conductors significant penetration occurs. At high frequencies, skin effect confines currents to a thin surface layer, and the shield's thickness must exceed several skin depths to maintain attenuation. Magnetic shielding for low‑frequency fields requires high‑permeability materials (e.g., mu‑metal) that divert magnetic flux lines around the protected volume.

Real‑world applications include shielding sensitive measurement equipment (electrometers, SQUIDs), protecting electronic devices from electrostatic discharge (ESD), and ensuring electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) in automotive and aerospace systems. Designing an effective shield involves selecting conductive materials with high conductivity, ensuring electrical continuity at seams and joints, and providing adequate grounding. Understanding the distinction between electrostatic shielding (static fields) and electromagnetic shielding (time‑varying fields) is crucial for solving problems that mix both phenomena.

  • Assuming Gauss's law gives the field at a point without symmetry leads to incorrect magnitude and direction.
  • Forgetting that the energy change on dielectric removal equals external work only when the capacitor is isolated, not when it remains connected to a battery.
  • Using the RC time constant τ = RC for an RL circuit or vice‑versa confuses the exponential behaviour.
  • Applying superposition while turning off dependent sources violates linearity and yields wrong results.
  • Treating a mesh Faraday cage as perfect for all frequencies ignores the aperture‑size‑to‑wavelength criterion.
  • Neglecting the continuity conditions for capacitor voltage and inductor current at switching instants produces erroneous transient solutions.
  • Confusing the bound charge density σb = P·n̂ with free surface charge leads to errors in capacitance calculations with dielectrics.

Boundary Conditions at Dielectric Interfaces

At the boundary between two linear, isotropic dielectrics, the tangential component of E and the normal component of D satisfy continuity conditions from Maxwell’s equations. The tangential component of E is continuous: E1t = E2t. This follows from ∇ × E = 0, implying the line integral of E around a vanishingly small loop straddling the boundary is zero. The normal component of D satisfies D1n − D2n = σf, where σf is the free surface charge density on the interface. Without free surface charge, the normal component of D is also continuous. Combining these with D = ε1E and D = ε2E yields the refraction law for field lines: tan θ1 / tan θ2 = ε1 / ε2. This electrostatic analogue of Snell’s law is essential for layered-dielectric problems, e.g., a parallel-plate capacitor with two dielectrics or a point charge near a planar interface. The boundary conditions also dictate the bound surface charge σb = P·n̂, where P = ε₀χeE is the polarization. A common mistake is applying continuity of the normal component of E instead of D, yielding incorrect field magnitudes. If the interface carries free charge, it must be included in the normal‑D condition; otherwise Gauss’s law for a pillbox crossing the boundary is violated.

Method of Images for Point Charges near Conducting Planes and Spheres

The method of images replaces a conductor at fixed potential (usually zero) with fictitious charges placed outside the physical domain. For an infinite grounded conducting plane, a point charge q at distance d is mirrored by an image charge −q at distance d on the opposite side. The resulting potential in the half‑space containing the real charge satisfies Laplace’s equation and V = 0 on the plane. The force on the real charge is the Coulomb attraction toward its image: F = (1/4πε₀) q²/(2d)² directed toward the plane. For a grounded conducting sphere of radius R with a point charge q at distance r > R from the centre, the image system consists of a charge q′ = −qR/r at distance r′ = R²/r on the line joining the centre to the real charge, plus a charge q″ = +qR/r at the centre if the sphere is isolated and neutral. The sphere surface is an equipotential (zero for grounded, V₀ for a sphere held at V₀). The method extends to multiple charges and dielectric spheres using appropriate image charges and dipoles. A frequent exam question asks for the induced surface charge density σ(θ) = −ε₀ ∂V/∂r|r=R. The result σ(θ) = −q(R² − r²) / (4πR(R² + r² − 2Rrcos θ)³ᐟ²) shows the characteristic cos θ dependence. The image construction is valid only where the original boundary conditions hold; the field inside the conductor is not given by the images.

Image‑Charge Force Calculation
A point charge q = 2 µC is placed 5 cm from an infinite grounded conducting plane. Compute the magnitude of the electrostatic force on the charge.
The image charge is −q located 5 cm behind the plane, so the separation between real and image charges is 2 d = 10 cm = 0.10 m. Coulomb’s law gives F = (1/4πε₀) q²/(2d)². Using 1/4πε₀ = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C², q = 2 × 10⁻⁶ C, and (2d) = 0.10 m: F = 9 × 10⁹ × (2 × 10⁻⁶)² / (0.10)² = 9 × 10⁹ × 4 × 10⁻¹² / 0.01 = 36 × 10⁻³ / 0.01 = 3.6 N. The force is attractive, directed toward the plane.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem involves a conductor at a non‑zero potential, first solve the grounded case with images, then add a uniform potential term (or an additional central image charge for a sphere) to raise the conductor to the required potential. This superposition avoids re‑deriving the entire image system.

Displacement Current and Maxwell’s Correction to Ampere’s Law

The original Ampere’s law, ∮ B·dl = μ₀ Ienc, fails for time‑varying fields because conduction current is not conserved across a capacitor gap. Maxwell introduced the displacement current density Jd = ε₀ ∂E/∂t (plus ∂P/∂t in dielectrics) so that the total current density Jtot = J + Jd satisfies the continuity equation ∇·Jtot = −∂ρ/∂t. The corrected Ampere‑Maxwell law reads ∮ B·dl = μ₀ (Icond + ε₀ dΦE/dt), where ΦE is the electric flux through the surface bounded by the Amperian loop. In a parallel‑plate capacitor, the conduction current in the leads equals the displacement current between the plates: I = ε₀ A dE/dt. This guarantees the magnetic field between the plates matches that of a continuous conduction current. A typical exam question asks for the magnetic field at radial distance r < R from the axis of a circular capacitor with time‑varying voltage V(t). Using a circular Amperian loop of radius r gives B(2πr) = μ₀ ε₀ (πr²) dE/dt, yielding B = (μ₀ ε₀ r / 2) dE/dt. The direction follows the right‑hand rule with respect to the displacement current. Students often forget the ε₀ factor or incorrectly use the total plate area instead of the loop area. The displacement current concept also underpins electromagnetic waves, where time‑varying E generates B and vice‑versa, leading to the wave equation ∇²E = μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t² in free space.

Energy Density in Electric Fields and Poynting Vector in DC Circuits

The electrostatic energy stored in a volume V can be expressed as a sum over charges, U = ½ Σ qi V(ri), or as a volume integral of the energy density uE = ½ ε₀ E² (vacuum) or uE = ½ E·D (linear dielectrics). For a parallel‑plate capacitor, integrating uE reproduces U = ½ C V². In a steady‑state DC circuit, the electric field inside wires is small, but surface charges create an external E field that, with the magnetic field, yields a Poynting vector S = (1/μ₀) E × B directed from battery to load. The magnitude of S integrated over a cylindrical surface surrounding a resistor equals the Joule heating I²R, showing electromagnetic energy flows through the space around conductors, not inside the metal. This resolves the paradox of energy transfer when the electric field inside a perfect conductor is zero. A common trap: the energy flow in a battery-resistor loop is radially inward toward the resistor, not along the wire. The magnetic energy density uB = B²/(2μ₀) is also present, but in DC steady state the net energy flow is accounted for by the electric field component of the Poynting vector. When a capacitor charges through a resistor, the Poynting vector points from the resistor into the capacitor gap, delivering the energy stored as ½ C V². The time integral of the Poynting flux through the capacitor surface matches the increase in electrostatic energy, confirming energy conservation without a separate “displacement current” energy term.

Non‑Ohmic Conduction and Temperature Dependence of Resistivity

Ohm’s law, V = IR, holds for many metals over a wide range, but numerous materials exhibit non‑linear I‑V characteristics. In semiconductors, carrier generation‑recombination and drift‑diffusion lead to exponential I‑V relations such as the Shockley diode equation I = I₀ (eqV/kT − 1). In gas discharge tubes, the I‑V curve shows negative differential resistance after breakdown. For filament lamps, resistance rises sharply with temperature: tungsten’s resistivity follows ρ(T) ≈ ρ₀[1 + α(T − T₀)] with α ≈ 4.5 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹ near room temperature; at operating temperatures (~2500 K) resistance can be 10–15× the cold value. The temperature coefficient α is positive for metals (phonon scattering increases with T) and negative for intrinsic semiconductors (carrier concentration increases exponentially with T). Typical exam problems give a resistivity-temperature table to find α, or ask for power dissipation when a voltage step hits a cold filament, requiring solution of C dT/dt = I²R(T) − hA(T − Tamb). Students must distinguish the linear approximation for small temperature excursions from full non‑linear behaviour. Another twist: a thermistor (NTC/PTC) in a voltage divider gives non-linear output; linearization uses dR/dT at the operating point. The key is recognizing when V = IR applies versus when the device’s I-V curve or temperature-dependent resistivity is needed.

  • Applying the continuity of the normal component of E instead of D at a dielectric interface.
  • Using the total plate area rather than the Amperian‑loop area when calculating displacement‑current magnetic fields.
  • Forgetting to add the central image charge for an isolated neutral conducting sphere.
  • Assuming the Poynting vector points along the wire in a DC circuit instead of radially inward toward the resistor.
  • Treating a filament lamp as a constant‑resistance element when its temperature changes significantly.
  • Neglecting the bound surface charge σ_b = P·n̂ when computing the net field inside a dielectric slab.
  • Using the linear temperature coefficient α outside its valid range for large temperature swings.
