1. Ohm’s Law and the Concept of Resistance

1.1 Definition of Current, Potential Difference and Resistance

Electric current is the rate of flow of charge through a conductor and is measured in amperes (A). When a potential difference (voltage) V is applied across the ends of a conductor, charges experience an electric field that drives them from higher to lower potential, producing a current I. The opposition offered by the material to this flow of charge is called resistance R, measured in ohms (Ω). Resistance depends on the nature of the material, its dimensions and temperature.

1.2 Ohm’s Law – Statement and Scope

Ohm’s law states that, for a large class of conductors known as ohmic conductors, the current flowing through the conductor is directly proportional to the applied potential difference, provided the temperature remains constant. Mathematically, V = I R. This linear relationship is represented graphically by a straight line passing through the origin on a V–I plot. The law fails for devices such as diodes, transistors and filament lamps, where the V–I characteristic is non‑linear.

1.3 Derivation of the Resistivity Formula

The resistance of a uniform cylindrical conductor of length ℓ and cross‑sectional area A can be expressed as R = ρ ℓ/A, where ρ is the resistivity of the material (Ω·m). Resistivity is an intrinsic property that quantifies how strongly a material opposes the flow of electric current. Metals have low ρ, whereas insulators possess high ρ. The formula follows from considering the electric field E = V/ℓ and the current density J = I/A; using Ohm’s law in the differential form J = σE, where σ = 1/ρ, yields the macroscopic relation.

1.4 Temperature Dependence of Resistance

For most metallic conductors, resistance increases linearly with temperature according to R = R₀[1 + α(T – T₀)], where R₀ is the resistance at a reference temperature T₀, and α is the temperature coefficient of resistance (°C⁻¹). The increase occurs because lattice vibrations scatter conduction electrons more effectively at higher temperatures. In contrast, the resistance of semiconductors decreases with temperature because more charge carriers are thermally generated.

1.5 Practical Applications of Ohm’s Law

Ohm’s law is employed in designing electrical circuits, selecting appropriate wire gauges, and troubleshooting faulty connections. In laboratory settings, the law enables the determination of an unknown resistance by measuring V and I with a voltmeter and ammeter, respectively. The method is also the basis for the Wheatstone bridge, which provides high‑precision resistance measurement.

2. Cells, EMF, and Internal Resistance

2.1 Electrochemical Cells – Construction and Operation

An electrochemical cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy through redox reactions occurring at two electrodes immersed in an electrolyte. The electrode where oxidation takes place is the anode, while reduction occurs at the cathode. The potential difference generated between the two electrodes under open‑circuit conditions is called the electromotive force (EMF), denoted ε, and is measured in volts (V).

2.2 EMF versus Terminal Potential Difference

When a cell supplies current to an external circuit, the measured potential across its terminals, V, is less than the EMF because part of the energy is lost overcoming the cell’s internal resistance r. The relationship is given by V = ε – Ir, where I is the current drawn from the cell. As the load draws more current, the terminal voltage drops, which is why a fresh battery shows a higher voltage than an exhausted one.

2.3 Internal Resistance – Origin and Variation

Internal resistance arises from several sources: the resistance of the electrolyte, the electrode materials, and the contact resistance at the interfaces. It is not a fixed value; r typically increases as the cell discharges because the concentration of reactive species diminishes and the electrolyte becomes depleted. Temperature also influences r, generally decreasing it as temperature rises, similar to metallic conductors.

2.4 Series and Parallel Combination of Cells

When cells are connected in series, their EMFs add algebraically while the internal resistances also add: ε_total = Σε_i and r_total = Σr_i. In a parallel arrangement, the overall EMF equals the common EMF of the individual cells (provided they are identical), but the equivalent internal resistance is reduced according to 1/r_total = Σ1/r_i. This reduction allows a higher current to be drawn without a large drop in terminal voltage.

2.5 Measuring EMF and Internal Resistance

A practical method to determine ε and r uses a variable external resistance R. By measuring the terminal voltage V for two different values of R and plotting V against I, the intercept on the voltage axis gives ε, while the slope (negative) yields –r. Alternatively, a potentiometer can be employed to measure ε directly without drawing current from the cell, thereby eliminating the influence of r.

3. Kirchhoff’s Rules and Network Analysis

3.1 Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL)

KCL states that at any junction in an electric circuit, the algebraic sum of currents entering the junction equals the sum of currents leaving it. This law follows from the principle of charge conservation; charge cannot accumulate at a node in a steady‑state circuit. Mathematically, ΣI_in = ΣI_out, where the sign convention assigns positive values to currents entering the node.

3.2 Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL)

KVL asserts that the directed sum of potential differences around any closed loop in a circuit is zero. Traversing a loop, each rise in potential (such as across a battery from – to +) is taken as positive, while each drop (such as across a resistor in the direction of current) is taken as negative. The law is a manifestation of energy conservation: the net work done on a charge after completing a loop must be zero.

3.3 Application of Kirchhoff’s Rules to Complex Circuits

To solve a circuit containing multiple loops and junctions, one writes KCL equations for independent nodes and KVL equations for independent loops. The number of independent equations equals the number of unknown branch currents. Solving the simultaneous linear equations yields the current in each branch, from which voltage drops and power dissipations can be calculated.

3.4 Example: Solving a Three‑Loop Circuit

Consider a circuit with three meshes, two batteries of EMFs ε₁ and ε₂, and three resistors R₁, R₂, R₃ arranged such that mesh currents I₁, I₂, I₃ flow clockwise. Writing KVL for each mesh and KCL at a common node provides three equations:

  • Mesh 1: ε₁ – I₁R₁ – (I₁ – I₂)R₃ = 0
  • Mesh 2: ε₂ – I₂R₂ – (I₂ – I₁)R₃ = 0
  • Node: I₁ + I₂ – I₃ = 0 (if a fourth branch exists)

Solving these yields the numerical values of the mesh currents, which can be substituted back to find individual branch currents.

3.5 Limitations and Practical Considerations

Kirchhoff’s laws assume that the circuit elements are lumped and that electromagnetic radiation from the circuit is negligible. At very high frequencies or in circuits with significant parasitic inductance and capacitance, distributed‑parameter models become necessary, and the simple KVL may no longer hold because of induced electromotive forces.

4. Wheatstone Bridge and Potentiometer Methods

4.1 Wheatstone Bridge – Principle and Balance Condition

The Wheatstone bridge consists of four resistors forming a diamond shape, with a galvanometer connecting the two opposite junctions. When the bridge is balanced, no current flows through the galvanometer, and the ratio of the two resistances in one arm equals the ratio in the opposite arm: R₁/R₂ = R₃/Rₓ, where Rₓ is the unknown resistance. This condition follows directly from KVL applied to the two loops and KCL at the junctions.

4.2 Procedure for Determining an Unknown Resistance

To measure an unknown resistance Rₓ, one selects three known resistors R₁, R₂, R₃ such that the bridge can be balanced by adjusting a sliding contact on a uniform resistance wire (or by varying a known resistor). When the galvanometer reads zero, the balance condition is satisfied, and Rₓ can be calculated from the known values. The method provides high accuracy because the measurement depends on a null condition, eliminating the influence of the galvanometer’s internal resistance.

4.3 Sensitivity and Error Analysis of the Wheatstone Bridge

The sensitivity of the bridge, defined as the change in galvanometer current per unit change in Rₓ, is maximal when the bridge is near balance. Errors arise from contact resistance at the sliding junction, temperature variations affecting the resistors, and the finite resistance of the galvanometer. Using a Wheatstone bridge with a long uniform wire reduces the effect of contact resistance, and employing a high‑sensitivity galvanometer minimizes the detection limit.

4.4 Potentiometer – Principle of Null Measurement

A potentiometer is a device that measures an unknown voltage by balancing it against a known voltage drop along a calibrated resistance wire. The key advantage is that no current is drawn from the source of the unknown voltage, so the measurement does not load the source. The balance condition is V_unknown = (ℓ/ L) V_ref, where ℓ is the length of the wire between the zero point and the jockey, and L is the total length of the wire.

4.5 Determination of EMF Using a Potentiometer

To find the EMF of a cell, the cell is connected to a galvanometer and a jockey is moved along the potentiometer wire until the galvanometer shows zero deflection. The length ℓ at which balance occurs is recorded. Knowing the potential gradient (V/L) of the wire, the EMF is calculated as ε = (ℓ/L) V_ref. Because the cell supplies no current during the measurement, its internal resistance does not affect the result, making the potentiometer the most accurate method for EMF determination.

5. Meters and Their Connection in Circuits

5.1 Ammeter – Construction and Connection

An ammeter measures current by passing the circuit current through a low‑resistance moving‑coil galvanometer. To avoid altering the circuit significantly, the internal resistance of the ammeter must be much smaller than the resistance of the portion of the circuit it is measuring. Consequently, an ammeter is always connected in series with the element whose current is to be measured.

5.2 Voltmeter – Construction and Connection

A voltmeter measures the potential difference between two points by using a high‑resistance moving‑coil galvanometer. Because it draws only a tiny current, the voltmeter is connected in parallel across the component whose voltage is required. The high internal resistance ensures that the voltage across the component is not appreciably changed by the meter.

5.3 Ohmmeter – Principle of Operation

An ohmmeter determines resistance by applying a known small voltage across its terminals and measuring the resulting current. The device internally contains a battery and a galvanometer; the scale is calibrated in ohms using the relationship R = V/I. Modern digital ohmmeters use a microcontroller to perform the calculation and display the result directly.

5.4 Power Rating and Safety Considerations

When connecting meters, it is essential to respect their power ratings. An ammeter with a low current rating can be damaged if placed in a high‑current branch. Similarly, a voltmeter must not be connected across a voltage exceeding its maximum rating, otherwise its internal resistance may be insufficient to protect it from overload. Using appropriate protective fuses and following correct polarity for analog meters prevents damage.

Example 5.1 – Determining an Unknown Resistance Using a Wheatstone Bridge

A Wheatstone bridge is set up with R₁ = 100 Ω, R₂ = 150 Ω, and R₃ = 200 Ω. The bridge is balanced when the galvanometer shows zero current. Find the unknown resistance Rₓ.

At balance, the ratio of resistances in one arm equals the ratio in the opposite arm:

R₁ / R₂ = R₃ / Rₓ

Substituting the known values:

100 / 150 = 200 / Rₓ

Cross‑multiplying gives 100 Rₓ = 150 × 200 = 30 000

Hence, Rₓ = 30 000 / 100 = 300 Ω.

The unknown resistance is therefore 300 Ω.

Exam‑Day Insight

When a question asks for the terminal voltage of a cell delivering a known current, first write the expression V = ε – Ir and substitute the given internal resistance. This avoids the common mistake of using V = ε + Ir, which applies only when the cell is being charged.

6. Integrated Problems and Conceptual Links

6.1 Combining Ohm’s Law with Kirchhoff’s Rules

In many circuit problems, the first step is to assign symbolic currents to each branch and then apply KVL around each independent loop. Ohm’s law is used to replace each voltage drop with I R, converting the loop equations into linear algebraic equations in the unknown currents. Solving the system yields the complete current distribution, after which individual voltage drops and power dissipations follow directly.

6.2 Using Potentiometer Data to Verify EMF Measurements

A common experimental verification involves measuring the EMF of a cell with a potentiometer and then independently determining the same EMF using a Wheatstone bridge where the cell’s EMF replaces one of the known resistors. Consistency between the two methods confirms the reliability of the measurements and highlights the advantage of null‑balance techniques.

6.3 Effect of Internal Resistance on Power Delivered to a Load

The power delivered to an external resistance R_L from a cell of EMF ε and internal resistance r is P = I² R_L, where I = ε / (r + R_L). Differentiating P with respect to R_L shows that maximum power transfer occurs when R_L = r. This result, known as the maximum power transfer theorem, explains why matching the load to the source’s internal resistance is essential in audio amplifiers and transmission lines.

6.4 Practical Design of Measurement Set‑ups

When designing a laboratory set‑up to measure a small resistance, the Wheatstone bridge is preferred because the null condition eliminates the influence of the galvanometer’s resistance. For measuring a high voltage, a potentiometer is advantageous because it does not draw current from the source, preventing voltage sag that could otherwise distort the reading.

6.5 Summary of Key Formulae and Units

Below is a concise list of the most frequently required relationships in this lesson:

  • Ohm’s law: V = I R (V in volts, I in amperes, R in ohms)
  • Resistivity: R = ρ ℓ/A (ρ in Ω·m, ℓ in metres, A in m²)
  • Temperature coefficient: R = R₀[1 + α(T – T₀)]
  • Cell terminal voltage: V = ε – Ir
  • Series cells: ε_total = Σε_i, r_total = Σr_i
  • Parallel cells: 1/r_total = Σ1/r_i (ε common)
  • Wheatstone balance: Rₓ = R₃ R₂ / R₁ (when bridge is balanced)
  • Potentiometer EMF: ε = (ℓ/L) V_ref
  • Maximum power transfer: R_L = r

Mastering these expressions, their derivations, and the physical reasoning behind them equips the student to tackle any problem in current electricity with confidence.
