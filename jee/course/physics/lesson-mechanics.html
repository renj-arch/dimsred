What is Mechanics?

Mechanics is the branch of physics that deals with the motion of objects under the influence of forces. It forms the foundation of classical physics and is divided into statics (study of objects at rest), kinematics (study of motion without forces), and dynamics (study of motion with forces). For NEET, the core topics include Newton's laws of motion, work-energy theorem, rotational motion, and gravitation. Understanding vectors and calculus basics is essential for mastering mechanics.

Key Concepts

Newton's Laws of Motion: First law (inertia) states that an object remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by an external force. Second law: F = ma, where force equals mass times acceleration. Third law: Every action has an equal and opposite reaction. Work-Energy Theorem: W = ΔKE, where work done equals change in kinetic energy. Conservation of Energy: Total mechanical energy (KE + PE) is conserved in the absence of non-conservative forces. Circular Motion: Centripetal force Fc = mv2/r. Gravitation: F = Gm1m2/r2, escape velocity ve = √(2GM/R).

Solved Examples

Example 1
A block of mass 5 kg is pulled on a frictionless surface with a force of 20 N at an angle of 60° to the horizontal. Find the acceleration of the block.

a) 2 m/s2 b) 4 m/s2 c) 3 m/s2 d) 1 m/s2
Solution: The horizontal component of force is Fx = F cosθ = 20 × cos60° = 20 × 0.5 = 10 N. Using Newton's second law, a = Fx/m = 10/5 = 2 m/s2. Hence option (a) is correct.
Example 2
A ball of mass 0.5 kg is dropped from a height of 20 m. Find the velocity just before it hits the ground. (Take g = 10 m/s2)

a) 10 m/s b) 15 m/s c) 20 m/s d) 25 m/s
Solution: Using conservation of energy, mgh = (1/2)mv2. Cancelling m, v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 20) = √400 = 20 m/s. Hence option (c) is correct.
Example 3
A car of mass 1000 kg is moving in a circular path of radius 50 m with a speed of 20 m/s. Find the centripetal force acting on the car.

a) 4000 N b) 8000 N c) 6000 N d) 10000 N
Solution: Centripetal force Fc = mv2/r = (1000 × 202)/50 = (1000 × 400)/50 = 8000 N. Hence option (b) is correct.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For problems involving pulleys, always draw free body diagrams (FBD) separately for each mass and apply F = ma.
  • In work-energy problems, if only conservative forces act, directly apply ΔKE + ΔPE = 0.
  • For projectile motion, remember that time of flight T = 2u sinθ/g and range R = u2 sin2θ/g.
  • In circular motion, if speed is constant, tangential acceleration is zero and only centripetal acceleration exists.
Pro Tip
For NEET numericals in mechanics, always check if the system involves conservation of mechanical energy before applying equations of motion. Energy methods are often faster than kinematic equations, especially in problems with varying acceleration or multiple objects.

Non-Inertial Reference Frames and Pseudo Forces

When Newton's laws are applied in a frame that accelerates with respect to an inertial frame, the equations of motion acquire additional terms called pseudo forces (or fictitious forces). A frame moving with constant velocity is inertial; any frame with linear acceleration aframe, rotation with angular velocity ω, or a combination of both is non‑inertial. In a linearly accelerating frame the pseudo force on a particle of mass m is Fpseudo = −maframe, acting opposite to the frame’s acceleration. In a uniformly rotating frame two pseudo forces appear: the centrifugal force Fcf = mω × (ω × r) directed radially outward, and the Coriolis force Fcor = −2mω × vrel acting perpendicular to the particle’s velocity relative to the rotating frame. The Euler force −m(dω/dt) × r appears when the angular velocity changes with time.

These forces are not due to any physical interaction; they arise solely from the choice of coordinates. Nevertheless, they allow the use of Newton’s second law in the non‑inertial frame: Freal + Fpseudo = marel. A classic illustration is a block on the floor of a horizontally accelerating truck. In the truck frame the block experiences a backward pseudo force matruck. If the coefficient of static friction is μs, the block remains at rest relative to the truck provided matruck ≤ μsmg. In a rotating frame, the apparent weight of an object at latitude λ on Earth is reduced by the centrifugal component mω²R cos²λ, and the Coriolis force deflects moving objects to the right in the northern hemisphere.

Important exceptions: pseudo forces do not obey Newton’s third law — there is no equal and opposite reaction force acting on the frame. Energy conservation in a non‑inertial frame must include the work done by pseudo forces, which can be non‑zero even for closed systems. When transforming between frames, the kinetic energy changes by the work of the pseudo forces, so the work‑energy theorem retains its form only if pseudo work is accounted for. In problems involving both translation and rotation of the frame, the total pseudo force is the vector sum of the translational, centrifugal, Coriolis, and Euler contributions.

Central Force Motion and Effective Potential

A central force is a force whose magnitude depends only on the distance r from a fixed centre and whose direction is always along the radial line. Gravitational and electrostatic forces are the principal examples. Because the torque τ = r × F vanishes for a central force, the angular momentum L = r × mv is conserved. Motion therefore stays in a plane perpendicular to L. Using polar coordinates (r, θ) in that plane, the radial equation of motion becomes m(r̈ − rθ̇²) = F(r). Substituting the conserved angular momentum L = mr²θ̇ eliminates θ̇ and yields a one‑dimensional radial equation: mr̈ = F(r) + L²/(mr³). The term L²/(mr³) can be interpreted as the negative derivative of an effective potential Ueff(r) = U(r) + L²/(2mr²), where U(r) is the potential energy associated with F(r) (i.e., F = −dU/dr). The radial motion is then governed by mr̈ = −dUeff/dr.

The shape of Ueff determines the possible orbits. For an attractive inverse‑square force F = −k/r², U(r) = −k/r and Ueff(r) = −k/r + L²/(2mr²). This effective potential has a minimum at r0 = L²/(mk), corresponding to a stable circular orbit. If the total energy E equals the minimum value, the orbit is circular; if E is slightly higher, the radial coordinate oscillates about r0 producing an elliptical orbit. For E ≥ 0 the trajectory is unbound (parabolic or hyperbolic). The same analysis applies to the repulsive Coulomb force with k negative, where Ueff has no minimum and all orbits are hyperbolic.

Bertrand’s theorem states that only the inverse‑square law and the Hooke’s law force (F = −kr) produce closed bounded orbits for all initial conditions. For other central forces, the orbit precesses; the precession per revolution can be computed from the integral of dθ/dr over one radial period. In relativistic corrections to gravity, an additional term proportional to 1/r³ appears in the effective potential, leading to the famous perihelion precession of Mercury.

Worked Example: Block in an Accelerating Truck
A block of mass 2 kg rests on the horizontal floor of a truck that accelerates horizontally at 3 m/s². The coefficient of static friction between block and floor is 0.4. Determine whether the block slips, and if not, find the frictional force acting on the block in the truck frame.
In the truck frame the block experiences a pseudo force Fpseudo = −matruck = −2 kg × 3 m/s² = −6 N (directed opposite to the truck’s acceleration). The maximum static friction is fmax = μsmg = 0.4 × 2 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 7.84 N. Since |Fpseudo| = 6 N ≤ 7.84 N, the block does not slip. The static friction adjusts to exactly balance the pseudo force, so the frictional force on the block in the truck frame is +6 N (forward, i.e., in the direction of the truck’s acceleration). In the ground frame the same friction force accelerates the block forward at 3 m/s², consistent with Newton’s second law.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem involves an accelerating or rotating frame, first identify the frame’s acceleration and angular velocity. Write down all real forces, then add the appropriate pseudo forces (translational, centrifugal, Coriolis, Euler). Apply Newton’s second law in that frame. Remember that pseudo forces do not appear in free‑body diagrams drawn in an inertial frame; they are a bookkeeping device for non‑inertial frames only. Check whether the question asks for quantities measured in the inertial frame or the non‑inertial frame — velocities and accelerations transform by adding the frame’s motion.

Two‑Dimensional Collisions and Centre‑of‑Mass Frame

In a two‑dimensional collision between particles of masses m₁ and m₂, momentum conservation holds vectorially: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂. The centre‑of‑mass (CM) velocity Vcm = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/(m₁+m₂) is constant throughout the collision. Transforming to the CM frame simplifies the analysis because the total momentum in that frame is zero. The velocities in the CM frame are u′₁ = u₁ − Vcm, u′₂ = u₂ − Vcm, and similarly for the final velocities v′₁, v′₂. In the CM frame the particles approach each other along a line, collide, and separate along a line that is rotated by the scattering angle χ relative to the initial line of approach.

For an elastic collision kinetic energy is also conserved: ½m₁u₁² + ½m₂u₂² = ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂². In the CM frame this reduces to u′₁ = v′₁ and u′₂ = v′₂ (speeds unchanged), with only the direction changing. The scattering angle χ in the CM frame is related to the impact parameter b and the interaction potential. For hard‑sphere collisions of equal masses, the CM scattering angle is 90°, and in the laboratory frame the two particles move at right angles after collision. For unequal masses, the laboratory scattering angles can be derived from the CM angle using the velocity addition formulas.

In completely inelastic collisions the particles stick together, moving with the CM velocity after impact. The kinetic energy lost equals the initial kinetic energy in the CM frame: ΔK = ½μu′rel², where μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) is the reduced mass and u′rel is the relative speed before collision. This result holds for any dimension. For partially elastic collisions characterised by a coefficient of restitution e (0 < e < 1), the relative speed after collision in the CM frame is e times the relative speed before collision, and the energy loss is (1−e²) times the initial CM kinetic energy.

Stability of Equilibrium and Small Oscillations

A mechanical system in equilibrium satisfies Fnet = 0 and τnet = 0. To assess stability, consider a small displacement from equilibrium. If the potential energy U(q) has a local minimum at the equilibrium configuration, the equilibrium is stable; if it is a maximum, the equilibrium is unstable; a saddle point indicates neutral stability in some directions and instability in others. For a single coordinate x with equilibrium at x₀, expand U(x) in a Taylor series: U(x) ≈ U(x₀) + ½k(x−x₀)², where k = d²U/dx²|x₀. The equation of motion for small oscillations becomes mẍ = −k(x−x₀), giving simple harmonic motion with angular frequency ω = √(k/m). For multiple degrees of freedom, the potential energy near equilibrium is a quadratic form ½∑kijηiηj, where ηi are small displacements. The kinetic energy is ½∑mijη̇iη̇j. The normal modes are found by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem kijAj = ω²mijAj. The eigenvalues ω² are the squared normal‑mode frequencies; all must be positive for stable equilibrium.

Applications include the simple pendulum (stable for small angles, ω = √(g/L)), a mass on a vertical spring (equilibrium at x₀ = mg/k, ω = √(k/m)), and a rigid body pivoted at a point (physical pendulum). For a body on a curved surface, the effective curvature determines stability: a particle at the bottom of a concave surface (positive curvature) is stable, while at the top of a convex surface it is unstable. In rotating systems, the effective potential includes centrifugal terms; a particle in a rotating hoop can have stable equilibria at angles where the effective potential has minima, leading to oscillations about those angles with frequencies that depend on the rotation rate.

Gyroscopic Precession and Nutation

A rapidly spinning symmetric top (or gyroscope) subjected to a torque τ (e.g., due to gravity acting on its centre of mass offset from the pivot) exhibits precession: the spin axis rotates about the vertical with angular velocity Ωp. For a top with spin angular momentum Ls = Isωs along its symmetry axis, and a torque τ = r × mg perpendicular to Ls, the precession angular velocity is given by Ωp = τ/Ls (vectorially). In magnitude, Ωp = mgr/(Isωs) for a top of mass m, centre‑of‑mass distance r from the pivot, and spin ωs. This steady precession occurs when the spin is large enough that the angular momentum is nearly aligned with the symmetry axis.

If the initial conditions are not exactly those for steady precession, the axis undergoes nutation — a periodic nodding motion superimposed on the precession. Nutation arises from the exchange between gravitational potential energy and the kinetic energy of the precessional motion. The nutation frequency for small amplitudes is approximately ωnut = Isωs/I⊥, where I⊥ is the moment of inertia about a transverse axis through the pivot. In the limit of very fast spin, nutation is rapid and of small amplitude, often damped quickly by friction at the pivot. The Earth’s axis exhibits a similar precession (period ≈ 26 000 years) due to torques from the Sun and Moon on the equatorial bulge, and a small nutation (period ≈ 18.6 years) from the Moon’s orbital inclination.

Gyroscopic effects are exploited in navigation (gyrocompasses), stabilization (gyroscopic stabilizers on ships and spacecraft), and measurement (gyroscopes in inertial navigation systems). The key principle is conservation of angular momentum: a torque perpendicular to the spin axis changes the direction of L but not its magnitude, causing the axis to precess. If the torque has a component along the spin axis, the spin magnitude changes, leading to spin‑up or spin‑down.

  • Treating pseudo forces as real interaction forces — they have no reaction pair and do no work in an inertial frame.
  • Forgetting to include the Coriolis force when analysing motion in a rotating frame with non‑zero relative velocity.
  • Assuming the effective potential method applies to non‑central forces — it is valid only when the force is central and angular momentum is conserved.
  • Using the laboratory scattering angle formulas for unequal masses without transforming through the centre‑of‑mass frame.
  • Confusing the coefficient of restitution definition (relative speed after/before) with energy loss fraction — they differ by a factor of (1+e) for unequal masses.
  • Neglecting the second derivative of potential energy when testing stability — a zero first derivative alone does not guarantee a minimum.
  • Applying the steady‑precession formula Ωp = τ/Ls when the spin is not sufficiently large, ignoring nutation and possible toppling.

Lagrangian Mechanics and Generalized Coordinates

The Lagrangian formulation replaces Newton’s vector equations with a single scalar function L = T − V, where T is the total kinetic energy and V the potential energy expressed in terms of a set of independent generalized coordinates qi and their time derivatives dot qi. The number of coordinates equals the number of degrees of freedom, and constraints are automatically incorporated by choosing coordinates that satisfy them. The Euler–Lagrange equations

d/dt (∂L/∂dot qi) − ∂L/∂qi = 0

yield the equations of motion for each coordinate. This approach is especially powerful for systems with holonomic constraints (e.g., a bead on a rotating wire, a double pendulum) because constraint forces never appear explicitly. The generalized momentum conjugate to qi is defined as pi = ∂L/∂dot qi. If the Lagrangian does not depend explicitly on a coordinate qk, the corresponding momentum pk is conserved — a direct expression of Noether’s theorem linking symmetries to conservation laws. For a particle in a central potential, the cyclic coordinate is the azimuthal angle φ, giving conservation of angular momentum Lz = mr²dot φ. The Lagrangian method also extends naturally to field theory and to systems with non‑conservative forces by introducing a Rayleigh dissipation function F such that the generalized force is −∂F/∂dot qi.

Hamiltonian Mechanics and Phase Space

The Hamiltonian H(q, p, t) is obtained from the Lagrangian by a Legendre transformation: H = Σ pidot qi − L. It represents the total energy expressed as a function of coordinates and conjugate momenta. Hamilton’s canonical equations

dot qi = ∂H/∂pi, dot pi = −∂H/∂qi

form a first‑order system in the 2n‑dimensional phase space. Trajectories in phase space never intersect, reflecting the deterministic nature of classical mechanics. Liouville’s theorem states that the phase‑space volume occupied by an ensemble of systems is conserved under Hamiltonian flow, a cornerstone of statistical mechanics. Canonical transformations preserve the form of Hamilton’s equations; they are generated by functions of the old and new variables (type‑1 through type‑4 generating functions). A particularly important class is the set of transformations that render the Hamiltonian independent of the new coordinates, leading to action‑angle variables for integrable systems. The Poisson bracket {f, g} = Σ (∂f/∂qi ∂g/∂pi − ∂f/∂pi ∂g/∂qi) provides an algebraic structure: dot f = {f, H} + ∂f/∂t. Constants of motion correspond to functions with zero Poisson bracket with H. The Hamiltonian framework also underlies the transition to quantum mechanics via canonical quantization {·,·} → (iℏ)⁻¹[·,·].

Worked Example: Simple Harmonic Oscillator in Hamiltonian Form
A particle of mass m moves in a one‑dimensional potential V(x) = ½ k x². Write the Hamiltonian, derive Hamilton’s equations, and obtain the phase‑space trajectory equation.
The Lagrangian is L = ½ m dot x² − ½ k x². Conjugate momentum p = ∂L/∂dot x = m dot x. The Hamiltonian H = pdot x − L = p²/(2m) + ½ k x². Hamilton’s equations: dot x = ∂H/∂p = p/m, dot p = −∂H/∂x = −k x. Eliminating time gives dp/dx = (−k x)/(p/m) = −(km/p) x. Integrating yields p²/(2m) + ½ k x² = E, an ellipse in the (x, p) plane with semi‑axes √(2E/k) and √(2mE). The motion is periodic with angular frequency ω = √(k/m).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for conserved quantities, first check for cyclic coordinates in the Lagrangian; the associated momenta are immediate constants of motion. In Hamiltonian problems, identify whether the Hamiltonian is explicitly time‑dependent — if not, H itself is conserved and equals the total energy. Sketching the phase‑space portrait (ellipses for harmonic oscillators, hyperbolas for inverted potentials) often reveals the qualitative behaviour faster than solving differential equations.

Canonical Transformations and Action‑Angle Variables

A canonical transformation (q, p) → (Q, P) preserves the symplectic structure of phase space, i.e., the fundamental Poisson brackets {Qi, Pj} = δij, {Qi, Qj} = {Pi, Pj} = 0. Generating functions provide a systematic way to construct such transformations. For a type‑2 generating function F₂(q, P, t), the relations are pi = ∂F₂/∂qi, Qi = ∂F₂/∂Pi, and the new Hamiltonian K = H + ∂F₂/∂t. If a system is integrable (possesses n independent constants of motion in involution), there exists a canonical transformation to action‑angle variables (J, θ) where the Hamiltonian depends only on the actions Ji. The actions are defined as Ji = (1/2π) ∮ pi dqi over a closed orbit in the original phase space; they are adiabatic invariants. The angles evolve linearly: θi = ωi t + βi with frequencies ωi = ∂H/∂Ji. This formulation underlies perturbation theory for near‑integrable systems (KAM theorem) and the semiclassical quantization condition Ji = ni h (Bohr–Sommerfeld). For the one‑dimensional harmonic oscillator, J = E/ω and θ = ω t + const, reproducing the equally spaced quantum levels.

Nonlinear Oscillations and Perturbation Techniques

Real mechanical oscillators often contain nonlinear restoring forces, e.g., a pendulum with sin θ ≈ θ − θ³/6 or a spring with a cubic term F = −k x − α x³. The equation of motion becomes ddot x + ω₀² x + ε f(x, dot x) = 0 where ε is a small parameter. Standard perturbation methods include the Lindstedt–Poincaré technique, which removes secular terms by expanding the frequency ω = ω₀ + ε ω₁ + … and the solution x = x₀ + ε x₁ + …. For the Duffing oscillator ddot x + ω₀² x + ε α x³ = 0, the first‑order frequency correction is ω₁ = (3α A²)/(8 ω₀) where A is the amplitude. The method of multiple scales introduces a slow time T = ε t to capture amplitude and phase modulation, yielding the averaged equations dA/dT = 0, dφ/dT = (3α A²)/(8 ω₀). For weakly damped and forced systems, the Van der Pol oscillator ddot x − ε(1 − x²)dot x + ω₀² x = 0 exhibits a stable limit cycle; the method of averaging predicts an amplitude of approximately 2. The Melnikov function provides a criterion for the onset of chaotic motion in perturbed Hamiltonian systems by measuring the distance between stable and unstable manifolds of a saddle point. Numerical Poincaré sections complement analytical work, revealing islands of regular motion surrounded by a chaotic sea.

Relativistic Corrections to Classical Mechanics

When particle speeds approach a significant fraction of the speed of light c, Newtonian mechanics must be corrected. The relativistic Lagrangian for a free particle is L = −mc² √(1 − v²/c²), yielding the canonical momentum p = γ m v with γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). The Hamiltonian (total energy) becomes H = γ mc² = mc² + p²/(2m) − p⁴/(8 m³ c²) + …. The first correction term −p⁴/(8 m³ c²) accounts for the kinetic energy’s deviation from the quadratic form. For a particle in a potential V(r), the Hamiltonian is H = √(p²c² + m²c⁴) + V(r). Expanding for v ≪ c gives the familiar p²/(2m) + V plus relativistic corrections. In central‑force motion, the effective potential acquires an additional term −L²/(2 m r²) (v²/c²), leading to a precession of the periapsis. For the gravitational two‑body problem, the first post‑Newtonian correction predicts a perihelion advance of Δφ = 6π G M/(a(1−e²) c²) per revolution, matching the observed Mercury precession. In accelerator physics, the relativistic mass increase modifies the cyclotron frequency to ω = qB/(γ m), requiring frequency modulation (synchrocyclotron) or magnetic field ramping (synchrotron) to maintain resonance. These corrections are essential for precision experiments and high‑energy applications.

  • Treating a non‑cyclic coordinate as cyclic and claiming its momentum is conserved.
  • Applying the Hamiltonian H = T + V when the Lagrangian contains velocity‑dependent potentials (e.g., magnetic forces) without adding the appropriate term.
  • Assuming all canonical transformations are generated by a single type of generating function; mixed‑variable generators are often required.
  • Neglecting the adiabatic invariance of action variables when a parameter changes slowly, leading to incorrect predictions for frequency shifts.
  • Using the simple harmonic oscillator frequency formula for a nonlinear oscillator without computing the amplitude‑dependent correction.
  • Ignoring the γ factor in relativistic momentum when the kinetic energy exceeds ~10 % of the rest energy.
  • Confusing the Melnikov criterion for chaos with the mere presence of a nonlinear term; chaos requires a transverse homoclinic intersection.

Noether's Theorem and Conservation Laws in Classical Mechanics

Noether's theorem establishes a profound link between continuous symmetries of the action and conserved quantities. For a mechanical system described by a Lagrangian L(qi, dot qi, t), an infinitesimal transformation qi → qi + ε ηi(q, t) that leaves the action invariant up to a total time derivative yields a conserved current. The conserved quantity is Q = Σi (∂L/∂dot qi) ηi − F, where F is the function whose total derivative appears in the variation of L. Translational invariance in space (ηi = constant) gives conservation of linear momentum p = Σ ∂L/∂dot qi. Rotational invariance (ηi = εijk nj qk) yields conservation of angular momentum L = Σ qi × pi. Time‑translation invariance (η = 0, t → t + ε) produces energy conservation H = Σ dot qi ∂L/∂dot qi − L, provided L has no explicit time dependence. The theorem extends to field theories and to gauge symmetries, where the conserved charges become the sources of fundamental interactions. In practical problem solving, identifying a cyclic coordinate (∂L/∂qk = 0) is equivalent to recognising a symmetry; the corresponding conjugate momentum pk is then a constant of motion. This insight reduces the number of degrees of freedom and often allows the construction of first integrals that simplify integration of the equations of motion.

When the Lagrangian contains velocity‑dependent potentials, such as the electromagnetic term (q/c) A·v, the canonical momentum differs from the mechanical momentum. Noether's theorem still applies, but the conserved quantity associated with spatial translations becomes the canonical momentum, not the kinetic momentum. Care must be taken to distinguish these in problems involving charged particles in magnetic fields. Similarly, for systems with non‑holonomic constraints, the symmetry must be expressed in terms of virtual displacements compatible with the constraints; the resulting conserved quantity may involve Lagrange multipliers. The theorem also underpins the method of reduction: if a Lie group acts freely and properly on the configuration space, one can quotient out the symmetry and obtain a reduced phase space of lower dimension, a technique central to modern geometric mechanics.

Hamilton–Jacobi Theory and Separation of Variables

The Hamilton–Jacobi equation provides a bridge between Hamiltonian mechanics and the calculus of variations. For a system with Hamiltonian H(qi, pi, t), one seeks a generating function S(qi, αi, t) of type 2 such that the new Hamiltonian K vanishes identically. The condition K = H + ∂S/∂t = 0 yields the partial differential equation ∂S/∂t + H(qi, ∂S/∂qi, t) = 0. A complete integral S containing n independent constants αi (equal to the number of degrees of freedom) solves the dynamics: the equations of motion are obtained from pi = ∂S/∂qi and βi = ∂S/∂αi, where βi are the new constant coordinates. When the Hamiltonian is time‑independent, one can separate the time dependence by writing S = W(qi, αi) − E t, where E is the total energy. The reduced equation H(qi, ∂W/∂qi) = E is the time‑independent Hamilton–Jacobi equation for Hamilton's characteristic function W.

Separation of variables is the principal technique for solving the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. If the Hamiltonian can be written as a sum of terms each depending on a single coordinate and its conjugate momentum, the characteristic function splits additively: W = Σ Wi(qi, α). Classic examples include the central‑force problem in spherical coordinates, where the azimuthal angle φ is cyclic, giving pφ = constant, and the polar angle θ separates after introducing the separation constant L2 (the square of total angular momentum). The radial equation then reduces to a one‑dimensional effective‑potential problem. In the Kepler problem, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation separates in parabolic coordinates, leading directly to the Runge–Lenz vector as an additional constant of motion. For the harmonic oscillator, separation in Cartesian coordinates yields action variables Ji = Ei/ωi, reproducing the equally spaced quantum spectrum via the Bohr–Sommerfeld rule. The method also applies to systems with velocity‑dependent potentials, provided the Hamiltonian is expressed in terms of canonical momenta. Mastery of separation techniques is essential for tackling integrable models and for constructing action‑angle variables in perturbation theory.

Worked Example: Hamilton–Jacobi for a Central Potential
A particle of mass m moves in a central potential V(r) = −k/r. Write the Hamilton–Jacobi equation in spherical coordinates (r, θ, φ) and separate variables to obtain the radial action integral Jr.
The Hamiltonian is H = (pr2/2m) + (pθ2/2mr2) + (pφ2/2mr2 sin2θ) − k/r. Since φ is cyclic, pφ = Lz = constant. Set S = Wr(r) + Wθ(θ) + Lz φ − Et. The Hamilton–Jacobi equation becomes (1/2m)(dWr/dr)2 + (1/2mr2)[(dWθ/dθ)2 + Lz2/sin2θ] − k/r = E. The θ‑dependent part must equal a separation constant L2. Thus (dWθ/dθ)2 + Lz2/sin2θ = L2. The radial equation is (dWr/dr)2 = 2m(E + k/r) − L2/r2. The radial action is Jr = (1/2π) ∮ pr dr = (1/π) ∫rminrmax √[2m(E + k/r) − L2/r2] dr. Evaluating the integral yields Jr = √(mk2/−2E) − L, where L = √(L2). The total action J = Jr + L gives the energy spectrum E = −mk2/2J2, the classical analogue of the Bohr formula.
Exam Strategy
When a problem asks for action variables or the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, first identify all cyclic coordinates; each gives a trivial separation constant. Write the ansatz S = Σ Wi(qi) − Et, substitute into H + ∂S/∂t = 0, and collect terms that depend on a single coordinate. The separation constants are the conserved quantities (energy, angular momentum components). Practice the Kepler and harmonic‑oscillator separations until the algebraic steps are automatic; they appear frequently in advanced mechanics papers.

Euler's Equations for Rigid Body Rotation

The rotational dynamics of a rigid body about its centre of mass are governed by Euler's equations in the body‑fixed principal‑axis frame. Let the principal moments of inertia be I1, I2, I3 and the components of angular velocity be ω1, ω2, ω3. In the absence of external torque, the equations read I1 dot ω1 = (I2 − I3) ω2 ω3, I2 dot ω2 = (I3 − I1) ω3 ω1, I3 dot ω3 = (I1 − I2) ω1 ω2. These are a set of coupled nonlinear ordinary differential equations. Two quadratic integrals exist: the kinetic energy T = ½ (I1 ω12 + I2 ω22 + I3 ω32) and the squared angular momentum L2 = I12 ω12 + I22 ω22 + I32 ω32. The intersection of the energy ellipsoid and the momentum sphere in ω‑space defines the possible trajectories, which are closed curves (polhodes) on the inertia ellipsoid. For a symmetric top (I1 = I2 ≠ I3), the equations simplify: ω3 is constant, and the transverse components precess at the rate Ω = (I3 − I1) ω3/I1. This free precession is observed in rotating spacecraft and in the Earth's Chandler wobble.

When external torques act, the right‑hand sides acquire torque components Ni expressed in the body frame. For a heavy symmetric top with a fixed point, the gravitational torque leads to the well‑known Euler angles (θ, φ, ψ) description. The Lagrangian in terms of Euler angles yields two cyclic coordinates (φ, ψ), giving conserved momenta pφ = Lz (space‑fixed vertical component) and pψ = L3 (body‑fixed symmetry‑axis component). The reduced one‑dimensional problem for θ has an effective potential Veff(θ) = (Lz − L3 cos θ)2/2I1 sin2θ + Mgl cos θ. The motion of θ oscillates between turning points (nutation), while φ and ψ evolve according to dot φ = (Lz − L3 cos θ)/I1 sin2θ and dot ψ = L3/I3 − cos θ dot φ. Stability of steady precession (θ = constant) requires the spin L3 to exceed a threshold: L32 > 4 I1 Mgl cos θ0. This condition explains why a rapidly spinning top remains upright while a slow one falls.

Poincaré Sections and Onset of Chaos in Hamiltonian Systems

Poincaré sections provide a geometric tool to visualise the phase‑space structure of Hamiltonian systems with two or more degrees of freedom. For a 2‑DOF system, the energy surface is three‑dimensional; a Poincaré map is obtained by intersecting trajectories with a two‑dimensional surface of section (e.g., q2 = 0, p2 > 0) and plotting the successive intersection points (q1, p1). Integrable systems produce smooth invariant curves on the section, each corresponding to a torus in the full phase space. When a perturbation breaks integrability, resonant tori are destroyed and chaotic layers appear around the separatrices of the unperturbed resonances. The Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM) theorem guarantees that sufficiently irrational tori survive small perturbations, forming a Cantor set of positive measure. As the perturbation strength increases, more tori break up, and the chaotic sea expands, eventually leading to global chaos where trajectories wander ergodically across the energy surface.

Quantitative indicators of chaos include the Lyapunov exponent λ, which measures the exponential divergence of nearby trajectories: |δx(t)| ≈ |δx(0)| eλ t. For Hamiltonian flows, the sum of all Lyapunov exponents is zero (phase‑space volume preservation). The Melnikov method gives an analytical criterion for transverse homoclinic intersections in near‑integrable systems: if the Melnikov function M(t0) has simple zeros, the stable and unstable manifolds of a saddle intersect transversely, implying Smale horseshoe chaos. Numerical computation of Poincaré sections complements analytical work; a typical exam question may present a section plot and ask to identify regular islands, chaotic zones, and the location of periodic orbits. Understanding the transition from order to chaos is essential for problems in celestial mechanics (asteroid dynamics), plasma confinement, and nonlinear optics.

Maupertuis Principle and Abbreviated Action

Maupertuis' principle, also known as the principle of least action in the Jacobi form, states that for a conservative system with fixed total energy E, the actual trajectory between two configurations makes the abbreviated action W = ∫ pi dqi stationary among all paths with the same energy. Here pi = ∂L/∂dot qi and the integral is taken along the path in configuration space, not in time. The principle is equivalent to the Hamilton–Jacobi equation: the characteristic function W(q, α) satisfies H(q, ∂W/∂q) = E, and its variation δW = 0 yields the equations of motion. Unlike Hamilton's principle, which fixes the endpoints in both configuration and time, Maupertuis' principle fixes only the endpoints in configuration space and the energy. This makes it particularly useful for problems where the time of flight is not prescribed, such as determining the shape of a brachistochrone under gravity or the trajectory of a particle in a central potential with given energy.

The abbreviated action is directly related to the action variables of integrable systems: Ji = (1/2π) ∮ pi dqi over a closed orbit. In the semiclassical (EBK) quantization, the action integrals are quantised as Ji = (ni + μi/4) h, where μi are Maslov indices accounting for caustics. Maupertuis' principle also underlies the geometric formulation of mechanics: the trajectories are geodesics of the Jacobi metric ds2 = 2(E − V(q)) gij dqi dqj on configuration space. This equivalence allows the use of Riemannian geometry techniques (e.g., curvature criteria for stability) in mechanical problems. In examinations, a typical application is to derive the orbit equation for a central force by extremising W = ∫ √[2m(E − V(r))] √[dr2 + r2 dθ2], leading to the Binet equation without explicit time parametrisation.

  • Confusing canonical momentum with mechanical momentum when applying Noether's theorem to velocity‑dependent potentials.
  • Assuming every cyclic coordinate yields a conserved quantity without checking that the symmetry is a true invariance of the action, not just of the Lagrangian.
  • Neglecting the Maslov index in EBK quantisation, leading to an incorrect ground‑state energy for the harmonic oscillator.
  • Treating the Melnikov function as a sufficient condition for chaos without verifying that the unperturbed system possesses a homoclinic orbit.
  • Using the symmetric‑top precession formula for an asymmetric body, ignoring the distinct moments of inertia.
  • Misidentifying the surface of section in a Poincaré map, causing misinterpretation of regular versus chaotic regions.
  • Applying Maupertuis' principle to non‑conservative systems where the energy is not constant along the varied paths.

Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes of Rigid Bodies

The inertia tensor I is a symmetric 3×3 matrix that relates the angular momentum L of a rigid body to its angular velocity ω through L = I·ω. In Cartesian components,

Iij = ∫ ρ(r) (δij r² − xi xj) dV,

where ρ(r) is the mass density, r² = x² + y² + z², and δij is the Kronecker delta. The diagonal elements Ixx, Iyy, Izz are the moments of inertia about the coordinate axes; the off‑diagonal elements Ixy = Iyx = −∫ ρ x y dV, etc., are the products of inertia. Because the tensor is real and symmetric, it can be diagonalised by an orthogonal transformation. The eigenvectors define the principal axes and the eigenvalues are the principal moments of inertia I₁, I₂, I₃. In the principal‑axis frame the angular momentum simplifies to Li = Ii ωi (no sum), and Euler’s equations take their standard form.

The parallel‑axis theorem for the tensor states that if the inertia tensor about the centre of mass is ICM, then about a parallel axis displaced by a constant vector a the tensor becomes

I = ICM + M (a² 1 − a⊗a),

where M is the total mass and 1 is the identity matrix. This is essential when shifting the reference point from the centre of mass to a pivot.

Typical textbook examples: a uniform thin rectangular plate of mass M and sides a, b has principal moments I₁ = (1/12) M b², I₂ = (1/12) M a², I₃ = (1/12) M (a² + b²) about axes through its centre parallel to the sides and normal to the plate. A solid cylinder of mass M, radius R, height h has I₁ = I₂ = (1/12) M (3R² + h²) and I₃ = (1/2) M R². For an asymmetric body the principal axes are not aligned with any obvious geometric symmetry; they must be found by solving the characteristic equation det(I − λ1) = 0.

In examinations, a common task is to compute the inertia tensor for a composite body (e.g., a rod with a point mass at its end) and then determine the principal axes by diagonalisation. Another twist is to ask for the kinetic energy of rotation expressed in the principal‑axis frame: T = ½ (I₁ ω₁² + I₂ ω₂² + I₃ ω₃²). Misidentifying the products of inertia or forgetting the minus sign in the off‑diagonal elements leads to incorrect eigenvalues.

Liouville's Theorem and Phase Space Volume Conservation

In Hamiltonian mechanics the state of a system with n degrees of freedom is represented by a point in the 2n‑dimensional phase space with coordinates (q₁,…,qₙ, p₁,…,pₙ). The time evolution of an ensemble of such points is governed by the Hamiltonian vector field

v = (∂H/∂p₁, …, ∂H/∂pₙ, −∂H/∂q₁, …, −∂H/∂qₙ).

Liouville’s theorem states that the phase‑space flow is incompressible: the divergence of v vanishes identically,

∇·v = Σ (∂²H/∂qᵢ∂pᵢ − ∂²H/∂pᵢ∂qᵢ) = 0.

Consequently, the volume of any region in phase space is conserved as it evolves under the Hamiltonian dynamics. If ρ(q,p,t) is the density of representative points, the continuity equation ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρ v) = 0 reduces to the Liouville equation dρ/dt = 0, meaning ρ is constant along each trajectory.

This theorem underpins statistical mechanics: the microcanonical ensemble corresponds to a uniform density on the constant‑energy hypersurface, and the conservation of phase‑space volume justifies the equal‑a‑priori probability postulate. It also leads to Poincaré recurrence — for a bounded Hamiltonian system, almost every trajectory returns arbitrarily close to its initial condition infinitely often.

In practical problems, Liouville’s theorem is used to relate the density of states in phase space to the density of states in energy space. For a one‑dimensional harmonic oscillator with Hamiltonian H = p²/(2m) + ½ m ω² q², the phase‑space trajectories are ellipses. The area enclosed by an ellipse of energy E is 2πE/ω, which is the action variable J. The theorem guarantees that this area is invariant under slow (adiabatic) changes of ω, a fact that connects directly to adiabatic invariants.

Examiners often test the understanding that Liouville’s theorem holds only for Hamiltonian systems; dissipative forces (e.g., friction) introduce a non‑zero divergence and cause phase‑space volumes to contract. A typical trap is to apply the theorem to a damped oscillator and incorrectly claim that the phase‑space area is constant.

Worked Example: Principal Moments of a Rectangular Plate
A thin uniform rectangular plate of mass M has sides of length a (along x) and b (along y). Find the principal moments of inertia about axes through its centre of mass.

Because the plate is thin, we treat it as a two‑dimensional lamina in the xy‑plane with surface density σ = M/(ab). The inertia tensor components about the centre are:

Ixx = ∫ σ y² dA = σ ∫−a/2a/2 ∫−b/2b/2 y² dy dx = σ a (b³/12) = (M/ab) a (b³/12) = (1/12) M b².

Iyy = ∫ σ x² dA = (1/12) M a².

Izz = ∫ σ (x² + y²) dA = Ixx + Iyy = (1/12) M (a² + b²).

The products of inertia Ixy = −∫ σ x y dA vanish because the integrand is odd in both x and y over symmetric limits. Hence the coordinate axes are already principal axes, and the principal moments are I₁ = (1/12) M b², I₂ = (1/12) M a², I₃ = (1/12) M (a² + b²).

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for principal axes of a composite body, first compute the inertia tensor about the centre of mass of the whole system using the parallel‑axis theorem for each component. Then set up the characteristic equation; if the tensor is already diagonal in the chosen coordinates, the principal axes coincide with those coordinates. Always check the sign of the off‑diagonal elements: Iij = −∫ ρ xi xj dV for i ≠ j.

Adiabatic Invariants and the Hannay Angle

An adiabatic invariant is a quantity that remains nearly constant when the parameters of a Hamiltonian system are varied slowly compared to the natural periods of motion. For a one‑degree‑of‑freedom system with action variable J = ∮ p dq (integral over one period), the adiabatic theorem states that dJ/dt = O(ε) where ε is the ratio of the parameter‑variation timescale to the oscillation period. In the limit ε → 0, J is exactly conserved.

Consider a harmonic oscillator with slowly varying frequency ω(t). The Hamiltonian is H = p²/(2m) + ½ m ω(t)² q². The action J = E/ω, where E is the instantaneous energy. As ω changes, the energy scales as E ∝ ω, so J stays constant. This result is used in plasma physics (magnetic moment μ = mv⊥²/(2B) is an adiabatic invariant) and in celestial mechanics (the action variables of planetary orbits are adiabatically invariant under slow mass loss of the star).

When the system has more than one degree of freedom and the parameter variation is cyclic, the angle variables acquire a geometric phase known as the Hannay angle. For an integrable Hamiltonian H(q,p; λ) depending on parameters λ that are slowly carried around a closed loop in parameter space, the angle variable θᵢ changes by

Δθᵢ = −∂/∂Jᵢ ∮ A·dλ,

where A is the Hannay connection one‑form. This is the classical analogue of the Berry phase in quantum mechanics. For the harmonic oscillator with a slowly rotating anisotropic potential, the Hannay angle equals the solid angle subtended by the loop in parameter space.

In examinations, a typical question asks to show that the action J = ∮ p dq is adiabatically invariant for a particle in a slowly expanding box (the “piston” problem). Another common task is to compute the Hannay angle for a simple pendulum whose length is varied cyclically. The key is to recognise that the adiabatic invariant is the area enclosed by the trajectory in phase space, and that the Hannay angle arises from the curvature of the parameter‑space connection.

Rutherford Scattering and Central Force Cross Sections

Rutherford scattering describes the deflection of a charged particle by a Coulomb potential V(r) = k/r, where k = Z₁Z₂e²/(4πε₀). The trajectory is a hyperbola with the scattering centre at one focus. The relation between the impact parameter b and the scattering angle θ in the centre‑of‑mass frame is

b = (k/(2E)) cot(θ/2),

where E is the kinetic energy of the incident particle in the centre‑of‑mass frame. The differential cross section is obtained from

dσ/dΩ = |b db / sinθ dθ| = (k/(4E))² csc⁴(θ/2).

This famous 1/sin⁴(θ/2) dependence was the key evidence for the nuclear model of the atom. The total cross section diverges because the Coulomb force is long‑range; in practice a screening cutoff (e.g., atomic electrons) renders it finite.

For a general central potential V(r), the scattering angle is given by the integral

θ = π − 2 ∫rmin∞ (b/r²) [1 − b²/r² − V(r)/E]⁻¹ᐟ² dr,

where rmin is the distance of closest approach, determined by the root of the square‑root denominator. The differential cross section follows from the same Jacobian transformation. For a hard sphere of radius R, the result is isotropic: dσ/dΩ = R²/4. For an attractive 1/r⁴ potential (e.g., ion‑induced dipole), the cross section has a different angular dependence.

Examiners often test the derivation of the Rutherford formula from conservation of energy and angular momentum, and the identification of the impact parameter. A common trap is to confuse the laboratory‑frame scattering angle with the centre‑of‑mass angle; the conversion involves the mass ratio of projectile and target. Another twist is to ask for the distance of closest approach for a head‑on collision (b = 0), which is rmin = k/E.

Variational Principles: Hamilton's Principle and Lagrange's Equations

Hamilton’s principle states that the actual path q(t) taken by a system between fixed endpoints (q₁, t₁) and (q₂, t₂) makes the action integral S = ∫t₁t₂ L(q, q̇, t) dt stationary, where L = T − V is the Lagrangian. The stationarity condition δS = 0 yields the Euler–Lagrange equations

d/dt (∂L/∂q̇ᵢ) − ∂L/∂qᵢ = 0, i = 1,…,n.

These are equivalent to Newton’s laws for holonomic systems. If non‑conservative forces are present, they appear as generalized forces Qᵢ on the right‑hand side: d/dt (∂L/∂q̇ᵢ) − ∂L/∂qᵢ = Qᵢ.

Hamilton’s principle differs from Maupertuis’ principle (the principle of least abbreviated action) in that the former fixes the time interval while the latter fixes the total energy. Hamilton’s principle is more convenient for deriving equations of motion with time‑dependent constraints; Maupertuis’ principle is better suited for problems where the energy is prescribed but the time of flight is free, such as the brachistochrone or the shape of orbits in a central potential.

The principle extends naturally to field theory: the action becomes S = ∫ L(φ, ∂μφ) d⁴x, and stationarity gives the Euler–Lagrange field equations ∂μ(∂L/∂(∂μφ)) − ∂L/∂φ = 0. In classical mechanics, Noether’s theorem connects continuous symmetries of the action to conserved quantities; for example, invariance under time translation gives energy conservation, and invariance under spatial translation gives momentum conservation.

In examinations, a typical question asks to derive the equations of motion for a bead sliding on a rotating wire using Hamilton’s principle with a time‑dependent constraint. Another common task is to identify the cyclic coordinates in a given Lagrangian and write down the corresponding conserved momenta. A subtle trap is to forget that the Euler–Lagrange equations hold only for variations that vanish at the endpoints; if the endpoints are not fixed, boundary terms appear and lead to natural boundary conditions.

  • Confusing the inertia tensor’s off‑diagonal sign convention, leading to wrong principal axes.
  • Applying Liouville’s theorem to dissipative systems where phase‑space volume contracts.
  • Assuming the adiabatic invariant is exactly conserved for any slow parameter change, ignoring the O(ε) corrections.
  • Using the laboratory‑frame scattering angle in the Rutherford formula without transforming to the centre‑of‑mass frame.
  • Neglecting the boundary terms in Hamilton’s principle when the endpoints are not fixed, resulting in incorrect natural boundary conditions.
  • Misidentifying the Hannay angle as a dynamical phase rather than a geometric phase arising from cyclic parameter variation.
  • Remember that the adiabatic invariant is the phase‑space area enclosed by the trajectory, and its conservation underlies the quantum Bohr–Sommerfeld quantisation rule.

Constrained Systems and Lagrange Multipliers

Holonomic constraints of the form f(q1, …, qn, t) = 0 reduce the number of independent degrees of freedom. When it is inconvenient or impossible to choose a minimal set of generalized coordinates that automatically satisfy the constraints, the method of Lagrange multipliers incorporates the constraints directly into the variational principle. The modified action is S′ = ∫ [L(q, q̇, t) + Σk λk(t) fk(q, t)] dt, where λk(t) are undetermined multiplier functions. Stationarity with respect to independent variations δqi and δλk yields the Euler–Lagrange equations with constraint forces: d/dt (∂L/∂q̇i) − ∂L/∂qi = Σk λk ∂fk/∂qi, together with the constraint equations fk(q, t) = 0. The right‑hand side is the generalized constraint force Qi(c) associated with the k‑th constraint. The multiplier λk has a direct physical interpretation: it equals the magnitude of the constraint force (or a component thereof) when the constraint is written in a form where ∂fk/∂qi gives the direction cosines of the force. For a bead on a wire described by f(x, y, z) = 0, the constraint force is normal to the wire and λ is its magnitude. For a rolling without slipping constraint f = x − Rθ = 0, the multiplier gives the static friction force. The method extends to non‑holonomic constraints linear in velocities, Σ aik(q, t) q̇k + ai0(q, t) = 0, provided the constraints are ideal (virtual work of constraint forces vanishes). The multipliers then satisfy the same Euler–Lagrange structure but the variational principle requires the vakonomic versus nonholonomic distinction; for ideal nonholonomic constraints the correct equations are d/dt (∂L/∂q̇i) − ∂L/∂qi = Σk λk aki. A classic application is the particle constrained to move on a moving surface f(r, t) = 0; the multiplier yields the normal reaction force, and its time derivative gives the power delivered by the moving constraint. In examinations, a typical problem asks for the tension in a string of a pendulum whose support moves vertically, or the normal reaction on a bead sliding on a rotating hoop. The multiplier method avoids the need to solve for the constraint force by eliminating coordinates, and it provides the force directly as part of the solution.

Normal Modes and Coupled Oscillators

A system of N coupled oscillators with small displacements from stable equilibrium is described by a quadratic Lagrangian L = ½ Σi,j Tij q̇i q̇j − ½ Σi,j Vij qi qj, where Tij and Vij are symmetric positive‑definite matrices (the mass and stiffness matrices). The equations of motion are Tij q̈j + Vij qj = 0. Seeking normal‑mode solutions qi(t) = ai cos(ωt + φ) leads to the generalized eigenvalue problem (Vij − ω² Tij) aj = 0. Non‑trivial solutions exist when det(V − ω² T) = 0, yielding N real positive eigenvalues ωα² (α = 1, …, N) — the normal‑mode frequencies. The corresponding eigenvectors a(α) give the mode shapes. Orthogonality with respect to the mass matrix holds: a(α)T T a(β) = 0 for α ≠ β, and similarly for the stiffness matrix. The general motion is a superposition of normal modes: qi(t) = Σα Cα ai(α) cos(ωαt + φα). The coefficients Cα, φα are fixed by initial conditions using the orthogonality relations. For two identical masses m coupled by springs of constants k (to walls) and kc (between them), the frequencies are ω1 = √(k/m) (symmetric mode, masses move in phase) and ω2 = √((k + 2kc)/m) (antisymmetric mode, masses move out of phase). Energy exchange between the masses — beats — occurs when only one mass is initially displaced; the beat frequency is ω2 − ω1. For a continuous system like a loaded string or a linear chain of N masses with nearest‑neighbour coupling, the normal modes are standing waves with wave numbers kn = nπ/(N+1)a and dispersion relation ωn = 2√(κ/m) sin(nπ/2(N+1)). In the limit N → ∞ this becomes the wave equation. Examiners often test the ability to write the Lagrangian for a specific coupled system (e.g., double pendulum, coupled LC circuits, masses on a string), set up the eigenvalue problem, and interpret the mode shapes. A common twist is to ask for the normal coordinates — linear combinations of the original coordinates that diagonalize both T and V — and to show that each normal coordinate evolves as an independent harmonic oscillator.

Worked Example: Normal Modes of a Double Pendulum
Two point masses m are attached to massless rods of length l, forming a double pendulum. Find the normal‑mode frequencies and the ratio of amplitudes for small oscillations.
Let θ1, θ2 be the angles from vertical. For small angles, the kinetic energy is T ≈ ½ ml² (2θ̇1² + θ̇2² + 2θ̇1θ̇2) and the potential energy is V ≈ ½ mgl (2θ1² + θ2²). The mass and stiffness matrices are T = ml² [[2, 1], [1, 1]] and V = mgl [[2, 0], [0, 1]]. The eigenvalue problem det(V − ω² T) = 0 gives det([[2mgl − 2ω²ml², −ω²ml²], [−ω²ml², mgl − ω²ml²]]) = 0. Dividing by mgl and setting λ = ω²l/g: (2 − 2λ)(1 − λ) − λ² = 0 → 2 − 6λ + λ² = 0. The roots are λ = 3 ± √7. Thus ω1² = (3 − √7)g/l ≈ 0.354 g/l and ω2² = (3 + √7)g/l ≈ 5.646 g/l. For the lower mode (λ = 3 − √7), the eigenvector equation gives (2 − 2λ)a1 − λa2 = 0 → a2/a1 = 2(1 − λ)/λ = 2(√7 − 2)/(3 − √7) ≈ 1.82 (masses swing in phase, lower mass has larger amplitude). For the higher mode (λ = 3 + √7), a2/a1 = 2(1 − λ)/λ = −2(2 + √7)/(3 + √7) ≈ −1.18 (masses swing out of phase).
Exam Strategy: Normal Mode Problems
When asked for normal modes, always write the kinetic and potential energy matrices explicitly before setting up the determinant. Check that both matrices are symmetric. For the frequency equation, factor out common terms (like ml² or mgl) to simplify the algebra. If the determinant yields a quadratic in ω², verify that both roots are positive — a negative root signals an algebraic error or an unstable equilibrium. For the amplitude ratios, substitute each ω² back into one row of the eigenvalue equation; the ratio a2/a1 is often simpler than solving the full system. Remember that the number of normal modes equals the number of degrees of freedom. In coupled oscillator problems with identical subsystems, look for symmetric and antisymmetric combinations as a shortcut to guess the mode shapes before doing the algebra.

Symmetric Top: Free and Forced Precession

A symmetric top has two equal principal moments of inertia, I1 = I2 ≠ I3, with the symmetry axis along the 3‑direction. In the body frame, Euler's equations for torque‑free motion are I1ω̇1 = (I1 − I3)ω2ω3, I1ω̇2 = (I3 − I1)ω3ω1, I3ω̇3 = 0. Thus ω3 = constant. The transverse components precess about the 3‑axis with angular velocity Ωb = (I3 − I1)ω3/I1 in the body frame. In the space frame, the angular momentum L is fixed. The symmetry axis (body 3‑axis) precesses about L with angular velocity Ωs = L/I1. The angle θ between the symmetry axis and L is constant, given by tan θ = (I1/I3) tan α, where α is the angle between ω and the symmetry axis. For a prolate top (I3 < I1), Ωb is negative: the body‑frame precession is retrograde relative to ω3. For an oblate top (I3 > I1), it is prograde. The Earth is an oblate symmetric top; its free precession (Chandler wobble) has a period of about 433 days. When a gravitational torque τ = Mgl sin θ acts on a heavy symmetric top with one point fixed (distance l from the fixed point to the centre of mass), the motion is described by the Lagrangian in Euler angles (θ, φ, ψ): L = ½ I1(θ̇² + φ̇² sin² θ) + ½ I3(ψ̇ + φ̇ cos θ)² − Mgl cos θ. The cyclic coordinates φ and ψ give conserved momenta pφ = I1φ̇ sin² θ + I3(ψ̇ + φ̇ cos θ) cos θ = Lz (vertical component of L) and pψ = I3(ψ̇ + φ̇ cos θ) = L3 (component along symmetry axis). The effective potential for θ is Veff(θ) = (pφ − pψ cos θ)²/(2I1 sin² θ) + Mgl cos θ. Steady precession occurs at a constant θ = θ0 where Veff′(θ0) = 0, yielding the quadratic equation for the precession rate φ̇: I1φ̇² cos θ0 − I3ω3φ̇ + Mgl = 0, where ω3 = ψ̇ + φ̇ cos θ0. For rapid spin (ω3 large), the two roots are φ̇slow ≈ Mgl/(I3ω3) (slow precession) and φ̇fast ≈ I3ω3/(I1 cos θ0) (fast precession). Nutation is the oscillation of θ about θ0; its frequency for small nutation is ωnut = √(Veff″(θ0)/I1). A sleeping top (θ = 0) is stable if ω3² > 4I1Mgl/I3². Examiners frequently ask for the condition for steady precession, the slow‑precession approximation, and the stability criterion for a sleeping top.

Restricted Three‑Body Problem and Lagrange Points

The circular restricted three‑body problem models two massive bodies (primaries) of masses M1 and M2 in circular orbits about their common centre of mass, and a third body of negligible mass that does not affect the primaries' motion. In a rotating frame with origin at the centre of mass and angular velocity ω = √(G(M1+M2)/a³) (where a is the separation), the primaries are fixed on the x‑axis at x1 = −μa, x2 = (1−μ)a, with μ = M2/(M1+M2). The equations of motion for the test particle are ẍ − 2ωẏ = ∂U/∂x, ÿ + 2ωẋ = ∂U/∂y, where the effective potential is U(x, y) = −GM1/r1 − GM2/r2 − ½ ω²(x² + y²). The Coriolis terms (−2ωẏ, 2ωẋ) do no work. Equilibrium points (Lagrange points) occur where ∇U = 0. There are five: three collinear points L1, L2, L3 on the x‑axis, and two equilateral points L4, L5 at the vertices of equilateral triangles with the primaries. For L4 and L5, r1 = r2 = a, and the coordinates are x = a(½ − μ), y = ±√3/2 a. Linear stability analysis shows that L1, L2, L3 are always unstable (saddle points of U). L4 and L5 are stable if μ < μcrit = ½ − √(69)/18 ≈ 0.03852. For the Sun–Jupiter system (μ ≈ 0.001), L4 and L5 are stable and host the Trojan asteroids. For the Earth–Moon system (μ ≈ 0.012), they are also stable. The Jacobi integral C = 2U − (ẋ² + ẏ²) is a constant of motion in the rotating frame; it defines zero‑velocity curves that bound the particle's accessible region. For C > C1 (value at L1), the particle cannot cross between the primaries' vicinities. As C decreases, the forbidden regions shrink, allowing transfer between regions. This underlies the design of low‑energy transfer trajectories (e.g., Hiten, Genesis missions). Examiners test the derivation of the effective potential, the location of Lagrange points, the stability condition for L4/L5, and the physical meaning of the Jacobi constant. A common question asks for the approximate distance of L1 from the smaller primary for μ ≪ 1: r ≈ a(μ/3)1/3.

Hamiltonian Field Theory and Symplectic Structure

The Hamiltonian formulation extends from particle mechanics to continuous fields. For a scalar field φ(x, t) with Lagrangian density ℒ(φ, ∂μφ), the conjugate momentum density is π(x, t) = ∂ℒ/∂φ̇. The Hamiltonian density is ℋ = πφ̇ − ℒ, and the total Hamiltonian is H = ∫ ℋ d³x. Hamilton's equations become functional derivatives: φ̇(x) = {φ(x), H} = δH/δπ(x), π̇(x) = {π(x), H} = −δH/δφ(x), where the Poisson bracket for field functionals F[φ, π], G[φ, π] is {F, G} = ∫ [δF/δφ(x) δG/δπ(x) − δF/δπ(x) δG/δφ(x)] d³x. The equal‑time Poisson brackets of the fundamental fields are {φ(x), φ(y)} = 0, {π(x), π(y)} = 0, {φ(x), π(y)} = δ³(x − y). This infinite‑dimensional phase space carries a symplectic structure: the symplectic form is Ω = ∫ d³x δπ(x) ∧ δφ(x). Canonical transformations preserve this form. Noether's theorem in the Hamiltonian framework states that for every continuous symmetry generated by a functional G with {G, H} = 0, the quantity G is conserved. Spatial translation invariance gives momentum Pi = −∫ π ∂iφ d³x; time translation gives energy H; rotational invariance gives angular momentum Ji = ∫ εijk xj π ∂kφ d³x. For the free scalar field ℒ = ½ φ̇² − ½ (∇φ)² − ½ m²φ², the Hamiltonian is H = ½ ∫ [π² + (∇φ)² + m²φ²] d³x. Fourier expansion φ(x) = ∫ d³k/(2π)³ 1/√(2ωk) [a(k) eik·x + a†(k) e−ik·x] with ωk = √(k² + m²) diagonalizes H = ∫ d³k ωk a†(k)a(k). The Poisson brackets become {a(k), a†(k′)} = −i δ³(k − k′). This classical Hamiltonian structure is the direct precursor to canonical quantization, where Poisson brackets are replaced by commutators divided by iħ. In examinations, the field‑theoretic Hamiltonian formalism appears in the context of deriving the energy‑momentum tensor from Noether's theorem, verifying the Poisson bracket algebra of the generators of the Poincaré group, and setting up the Hamiltonian for a given Lagrangian density (e.g., electromagnetic field, fluid dynamics). A typical question asks to compute the Hamiltonian density for the electromagnetic field in Coulomb gauge and identify the physical degrees of freedom.

  • Confusing the vakonomic and nonholonomic equations for velocity‑dependent constraints; the correct nonholonomic equations use the constraint coefficients aki directly, not ∂f/∂qi.
  • Forgetting that the mass matrix T in coupled oscillators must be positive definite; a zero eigenvalue of T signals a redundant coordinate or a gauge degree of freedom.
  • Mixing up the body‑frame precession rate Ωb = (I3 − I1)ω3/I1 with the space‑frame precession rate Ωs = L/I1 for a free symmetric top.
  • Using the slow‑precession formula φ̇ ≈ Mgl/(I3ω3) without checking the condition I3ω3 ≫ √(I1Mgl cos θ).
  • Assuming L4 and L5 are always stable; the Routh criterion μ < 0.03852 is essential and often tested numerically.
  • Neglecting the Coriolis terms in the restricted three‑body equations when writing the effective potential; they are crucial for the Jacobi integral but do not appear in U.
  • Treating the field Poisson bracket {φ(x), π(y)} = δ³(x − y) as an ordinary function rather than a distribution; the delta function requires careful handling in integrals.
