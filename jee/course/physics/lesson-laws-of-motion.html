1. Newton's Laws of Motion

1.1 The First Law and Inertia

The first law, often called the law of inertia, states that a body remains at rest or continues to move with a constant velocity unless acted upon by a net external force. Inertia is the intrinsic property of matter that resists any change in its state of motion, and its quantitative measure is the mass of the body expressed in kilograms. When the vector sum of all forces acting on a particle is zero, the acceleration of that particle is zero, which means its velocity vector does not change in magnitude or direction. This principle underlies the concept of an inertial reference frame, a frame of reference in which Newton's first law holds true; any frame accelerating with respect to an inertial frame is non‑inertial and requires the introduction of fictitious forces to preserve the form of the equations of motion.

Historically, Galileo’s experiments with inclined planes demonstrated that a sphere rolling on a frictionless horizontal surface would continue indefinitely, contradicting the Aristotelian view that a continuous force is needed to sustain motion. Newton formalised this observation, and the first law also serves as a definition of force: a force is that which changes the state of motion of a body. In practical problem solving, the first law is invoked whenever a system is in uniform motion or at rest, allowing us to set the net force equal to zero and write equilibrium equations for the unknown forces.

1.2 The Second Law and the Concept of Force

The second law provides the quantitative relationship between force, mass, and acceleration: the net force acting on a particle equals the product of its mass and its acceleration, expressed as F = m a. Here force is measured in newtons (N), mass in kilograms (kg), and acceleration in metres per second squared (m s⁻²). The law is a vector equation, meaning that the direction of the acceleration is the same as the direction of the net force. When several forces act simultaneously, the net force is the vector sum of the individual forces, and the acceleration follows that resultant. This formulation allows us to treat force as the cause of acceleration and to predict the motion of a body when the forces are known, or conversely to deduce the forces when the motion is observed.

An important consequence of the second law is the definition of the unit of force: one newton is the force required to give a one‑kilogram mass an acceleration of one metre per second squared. The law also extends to systems of particles by introducing the centre of mass; the total external force on a system equals the total mass times the acceleration of the centre of mass. In many textbook problems the second law is applied component‑wise along chosen axes, turning the vector equation into a set of scalar equations that can be solved simultaneously for unknown magnitudes and directions.

1.3 The Third Law and Action‑Reaction Pairs

The third law states that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction: if body A exerts a force on body B, then body B simultaneously exerts a force of equal magnitude and opposite direction on body A. These two forces constitute an action‑reaction pair; they act on different bodies, never on the same body, and therefore they do not cancel each other in the free‑body diagram of a single object. The law reflects the conservation of momentum in an isolated system, because the internal forces between components of the system occur in equal and opposite pairs, leaving the total momentum unchanged.

In practice, the third law is essential for identifying all forces acting on a body. For example, when a block rests on a horizontal table, the block exerts a downward force on the table (its weight transmitted through contact) and the table exerts an upward normal force on the block; these two forces are an action‑reaction pair. Similarly, the tension in a massless string is the same throughout because each segment pulls on its neighbours with equal and opposite forces. Recognising action‑reaction pairs prevents the common mistake of double‑counting forces or omitting a reaction force that is crucial for equilibrium or dynamics analysis.

2. Free‑Body Diagrams and Equilibrium

2.1 Constructing a Free‑Body Diagram

A free‑body diagram (FBD) is a pictorial representation that isolates a single body and shows all external forces acting on it as vectors originating from a dot or a simplified shape representing the body. The first step is to choose the system of interest and to imagine cutting it free from its surroundings, replacing each interaction by a force vector. Contact forces such as normal reaction, friction, and tension are drawn at the points of contact, while field forces like weight are drawn acting at the centre of gravity. Each force is labelled with its symbol (e.g., N for normal, f for friction, T for tension, W for weight) and, when known, its magnitude and direction.

Accurate FBDs are the foundation of every dynamics problem because they translate a physical situation into a set of vector equations. The coordinate axes are usually chosen to simplify the components: one axis along the direction of motion or anticipated acceleration, and the other perpendicular to it. Forces that are not aligned with the axes are resolved into components using trigonometric functions. The diagram must include all forces, even those that later turn out to be zero, because omitting a force leads to an incorrect net force and consequently an erroneous acceleration.

2.2 Translational Equilibrium and the Vector Sum of Forces

Translational equilibrium occurs when the vector sum of all forces acting on a body is zero, resulting in zero acceleration of the centre of mass. In component form this condition reads ΣFₓ = 0 and ΣFᵧ = 0 for a planar problem, or ΣFₓ = 0, ΣFᵧ = 0, ΣF_z = 0 in three dimensions. When a body is at rest or moves with constant velocity, these equations hold and can be used to solve for unknown forces such as the normal reaction on an inclined plane or the tension in a supporting cable. The equilibrium equations are simply the static limit of Newton’s second law, obtained by setting a = 0.

It is important to distinguish between static equilibrium (body at rest) and dynamic equilibrium (body moving with constant velocity). Both satisfy the same force‑balance equations, but the former often involves static friction that can vary up to a maximum value, whereas the latter involves kinetic friction of a fixed magnitude. In many engineering applications, structures are designed to remain in static equilibrium under expected loads, and the equilibrium equations are the starting point for stress analysis.

2.3 Rotational Equilibrium and the Moment of Forces

Rotational equilibrium requires that the sum of all torques (moments of forces) about any point be zero: Στ = 0. Torque is defined as the cross product of the position vector from the chosen pivot to the point of force application and the force vector, τ = r × F, with magnitude τ = r F sinθ where θ is the angle between r and F. The direction of the torque vector follows the right‑hand rule and indicates the sense of rotation the force would produce if it were the only force acting. For a rigid body in complete equilibrium, both translational and rotational equilibrium conditions must be satisfied simultaneously.

When solving problems involving beams, ladders, or seesaws, it is often convenient to take moments about a point where one or more unknown forces act, because their torques about that point are zero and they drop out of the moment equation. This technique reduces the number of unknowns in the torque equation, allowing the remaining unknowns to be found. The principle of moments is also the basis for the design of simple machines such as levers and pulleys, where a small force applied at a large distance can balance a large force applied at a short distance.

Example: Block on an Inclined Plane with Friction
A 5 kg block rests on a rough plane inclined at 30° to the horizontal. The coefficient of static friction between the block and the plane is 0.4. Determine whether the block remains at rest or begins to slide, and if it slides, find its acceleration down the plane (take g = 9.8 m s⁻²).
First draw the free‑body diagram. The weight W = mg = 5 × 9.8 = 49 N acts vertically downward. Resolve W into components parallel (W∥ = W sin30° = 49 × 0.5 = 24.5 N) and perpendicular (W⊥ = W cos30° = 49 × √3/2 ≈ 42.4 N) to the plane. The normal reaction N equals W⊥ because there is no acceleration perpendicular to the plane, so N ≈ 42.4 N. The maximum static friction is fₛₘₐₓ = μₛ N = 0.4 × 42.4 ≈ 16.96 N. Since the component of weight down the plane (24.5 N) exceeds the maximum static friction (16.96 N), the block cannot remain at rest and will slide. Once sliding, kinetic friction acts; assuming μₖ = 0.3 (typical for the same surfaces), the kinetic friction force is fₖ = μₖ N = 0.3 × 42.4 ≈ 12.7 N. The net force down the plane is Fₙₑₜ = W∥ – fₖ = 24.5 – 12.7 = 11.8 N. Using Newton’s second law, a = Fₙₑₜ / m = 11.8 / 5 = 2.36 m s⁻² down the incline.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem gives only the coefficient of static friction, always compare the required friction for equilibrium with the maximum static friction before assuming motion; many candidates lose marks by directly applying kinetic friction without checking the static limit.

3. Friction

3.1 Nature of Contact Forces and the Origin of Friction

Friction is a contact force that arises from electromagnetic interactions between the microscopic asperities of two surfaces in contact. When two solid bodies press against each other, the real area of contact is only a tiny fraction of the apparent macroscopic area because surfaces are rough at the microscopic level. The interlocking of these asperities and the adhesive forces between atoms at the contact points resist relative motion. The frictional force acts tangentially to the surfaces and opposes the direction of impending or actual sliding. Its magnitude depends on the nature of the materials, the normal force pressing the surfaces together, and the condition of the surfaces (clean, lubricated, oxidised).

Because friction originates from atomic‑scale forces, it is not a fundamental force like gravity or electromagnetism but a macroscopic manifestation of electromagnetic interactions. The empirical laws of friction, first systematically studied by Leonardo da Vinci and later by Guillaume Amontons and Charles‑Augustin de Coulomb, state that the frictional force is approximately proportional to the normal force and independent of the apparent contact area. These laws hold reasonably well for dry, unlubricated surfaces over a wide range of loads and speeds, but they break down at very high pressures, very low loads, or when surface films and temperature effects become significant.

3.2 Static and Kinetic Friction Coefficients

Static friction acts when there is no relative motion between the surfaces; its magnitude can vary from zero up to a maximum value fₛₘₐₓ = μₛ N, where μₛ is the coefficient of static friction and N is the normal force. The actual static friction force adjusts itself to exactly balance the applied tangential force up to this limit, which is why a block on an incline can remain stationary over a range of angles. Once the applied force exceeds fₛₘₐₓ, the surfaces begin to slide and kinetic (or dynamic) friction takes over. Kinetic friction is usually smaller than the maximum static friction and is given by fₖ = μₖ N, where μₖ is the coefficient of kinetic friction. Both coefficients are dimensionless numbers that depend on the pair of materials in contact; typical values for steel on steel are μₛ ≈ 0.6 and μₖ ≈ 0.4, while for rubber on dry concrete μₛ can be as high as 1.0.

The distinction between static and kinetic friction is crucial in problems involving impending motion, such as a ladder leaning against a wall or a car accelerating without wheel slip. In the former case the friction force is whatever is needed to maintain equilibrium, up to μₛ N; in the latter the friction force is fixed at μₖ N once sliding occurs. Some textbooks also introduce a coefficient of rolling friction for wheels, which is orders of magnitude smaller than sliding friction because the contact patch deforms rather than slides.

3.3 Angle of Repose and Limiting Friction

The angle of repose is the steepest angle of an inclined plane at which a body placed on it remains at rest without any external force other than gravity and friction. At this angle the component of weight parallel to the plane exactly equals the maximum static friction: mg sinθᵣ = μₛ mg cosθᵣ, which simplifies to tanθᵣ = μₛ. Thus the coefficient of static friction can be measured experimentally by gradually increasing the incline until the block just begins to slide and then taking the arctangent of that angle. The concept of limiting friction is the same maximum static friction that appears in the angle‑of‑repose condition; it represents the threshold beyond which motion initiates.

In engineering, the angle of repose is used to design stable slopes for granular materials such as sand, gravel, or coal, where the internal friction between particles plays the same role as the friction between a block and a plane. For cohesive soils the angle of repose is supplemented by cohesion, but the basic frictional component still follows the tanθ = μ relationship. Understanding limiting friction also helps in analysing the self‑locking condition of screws and wedges, where the thread angle must be less than the angle of repose for the device to hold a load without continuous torque.

4. Circular Motion

4.1 Uniform Circular Motion and Centripetal Acceleration

Uniform circular motion occurs when a particle moves along a circular path with constant speed. Although the speed is constant, the velocity vector continuously changes direction, so the particle experiences an acceleration directed toward the centre of the circle. This centripetal acceleration has magnitude a_c = v² / r = ω² r, where v is the linear speed, ω is the angular speed in radians per second, and r is the radius of the circular path. The acceleration is always perpendicular to the instantaneous velocity, which is why the speed does not change. According to Newton’s second law, a net force of magnitude F_c = m a_c = m v² / r must act toward the centre; this force is called the centripetal force. It is not a new kind of force but the resultant of real forces such as tension, gravity, normal reaction, or friction that have a component pointing toward the centre.

In many textbook problems the centripetal force is provided by a single identifiable force: for a satellite in orbit it is gravity, for a car on a flat curve it is friction, for a mass on a string it is tension. When several forces contribute, their radial components are summed to give the required centripetal force. The direction of the centripetal force is always radially inward, and there is no outward “centrifugal force” in an inertial frame; the sensation of being pushed outward in a turning vehicle is due to the inertia of the body tending to continue in a straight line while the vehicle turns inward.

4.2 Dynamics of Non‑Uniform Circular Motion

When the speed of a particle moving on a circular path changes, the motion is non‑uniform circular motion. The acceleration then has two components: a radial (centripetal) component a_r = v² / r directed toward the centre, and a tangential component a_t = dv/dt directed along the tangent in the direction of increasing speed (or opposite if slowing down). The total acceleration vector is the vector sum of these perpendicular components, and its magnitude is a = √(a_r² + a_t²). The net force likewise splits into a radial component F_r = m a_r providing the centripetal force, and a tangential component F_t = m a_t responsible for changing the speed.

Typical examples include a pendulum swinging in a vertical circle, where gravity supplies both a tangential component (changing speed) and a radial component (part of the centripetal force), and a car accelerating on a curved road, where the engine’s traction provides a tangential force while friction and banking supply the radial force. In such problems it is essential to write separate equations for the radial and tangential directions, because the radial equation still enforces the circular geometry (v² / r) while the tangential equation follows from the net tangential force.

4.3 Centrifugal Effects in Rotating Reference Frames

In a reference frame that rotates with the particle, Newton’s laws do not hold in their simple form unless fictitious forces are introduced. The most important fictitious force is the centrifugal force, which appears to act radially outward with magnitude m ω² r. It balances the real centripetal force in the rotating frame, making the particle appear at rest. Another fictitious force, the Coriolis force, acts on objects moving relative to the rotating frame and is proportional to the cross product of the frame’s angular velocity and the object’s velocity in that frame. These fictitious forces are not due to any physical interaction but are mathematical devices that allow the use of Newton’s second law in non‑inertial frames.

Understanding centrifugal effects is important for analysing devices such as centrifuges, where the outward fictitious force in the rotating frame explains the separation of components by density, and for understanding the apparent weight reduction at the Earth’s equator due to the planet’s rotation. In an inertial frame the same phenomena are explained solely by the requirement for a centripetal force; the fictitious forces simply provide an alternative, often more intuitive, description for observers who are themselves rotating.

5. Banking of Roads and Applications

5.1 Derivation of the Banking Angle for a Frictionless Curve

When a road is banked, the normal reaction from the road surface has a horizontal component that can supply the required centripetal force for a vehicle to negotiate the curve without relying on friction. Consider a vehicle of mass m moving with speed v on a curve of radius r banked at an angle θ to the horizontal. The forces acting on the vehicle are its weight mg vertically downward and the normal reaction N perpendicular to the road surface. Resolving N into vertical and horizontal components gives N cosθ upward and N sinθ toward the centre of the curve. For vertical equilibrium, N cosθ = mg. The horizontal component provides the centripetal force: N sinθ = m v² / r. Dividing the second equation by the first eliminates N and yields tanθ = v² / (r g). This is the ideal banking angle for a given speed and radius when friction is absent; at this angle a vehicle can travel exactly at speed v without any sideways frictional force.

The derivation shows that the banking angle depends only on the design speed and the curve radius, not on the mass of the vehicle. In practice, roads are built for a range of speeds, so the banking angle is chosen as a compromise; vehicles travelling slower than the design speed will tend to slide down the bank, while faster vehicles will tend to slide up, and friction must supply the difference. The formula also assumes the road surface is rigid and the vehicle’s centre of mass is at the same height as the contact patch, which is a reasonable approximation for most passenger cars.

5.2 Role of Friction on a Banked Turn

When friction is present, the total horizontal force toward the centre is the sum of the horizontal component of the normal reaction and the horizontal component of the friction force. The friction force acts parallel to the road surface and can point either up the bank (if the vehicle is slower than the design speed) or down the bank (if the vehicle is faster). Its maximum magnitude is f_max = μ N, where μ is the coefficient of static friction between tyres and road. By writing the vertical and horizontal force balances with friction included, one obtains two limiting speeds: a minimum speed v_min = √[ r g (tanθ – μ) / (1 + μ tanθ) ] and a maximum speed v_max = √[ r g (tanθ + μ) / (1 – μ tanθ) ]. Between these speeds the vehicle can negotiate the curve without skidding, and the required friction is less than the maximum available.

These expressions reduce to the frictionless result when μ = 0, and they show that a steeper banking angle (larger θ) increases both v_min and v_max, widening the safe speed range. However, excessively steep banking can be dangerous for slow‑moving or stationary vehicles, which may slide down the incline. Engineers therefore select a banking angle that provides a comfortable speed range for the expected traffic while keeping the required friction coefficient within the capabilities of typical tyres on wet or dry pavement.

5.3 Design Considerations for High‑Speed Tracks

Racing circuits and high‑speed test tracks often employ variable banking, where the angle changes along the curve to match the increasing speed of the vehicle as it accelerates out of a corner. The design process uses the same force‑balance equations but incorporates the vehicle’s aerodynamic downforce, which adds to the normal force and therefore increases the available friction. Downforce is proportional to the square of the speed and acts vertically downward, effectively increasing the apparent weight of the car. This allows higher cornering speeds without increasing the banking angle, which would be impractical for road cars. Additionally, the track surface is engineered to provide a high and consistent coefficient of friction, and the transition from flat to banked sections is made gradual to avoid sudden changes in lateral acceleration that could unsettle the vehicle.

Safety barriers, runoff areas, and the geometry of the banking are all analysed using the principles of circular motion and friction discussed earlier. The goal is to ensure that even if a vehicle exceeds the design speed, the resulting lateral forces remain within the limits that the tyres, suspension, and driver can tolerate. Modern simulation tools integrate the equations of motion for a full vehicle model, but the underlying physics remains the balance of weight, normal reaction, friction, and centripetal requirement that we have derived from Newton’s laws.

The study of forces, friction, and circular motion provides the foundation for analysing a vast range of mechanical systems from simple blocks on inclines to sophisticated transportation infrastructure.

Pseudo Forces in Non‑Inertial Reference Frames

Newton’s laws are formulated for inertial frames — frames that move with constant velocity. When an observer uses a frame that accelerates or rotates, the equation F = ma no longer holds unless additional forces, called pseudo forces or inertial forces, are introduced. A pseudo force has magnitude m aframe and acts opposite to the acceleration of the non‑inertial frame. In a linearly accelerating frame (for example a car speeding up), every mass experiences a backward pseudo force m a. In a uniformly rotating frame with angular velocity ω, two pseudo forces appear: the centrifugal force m ω² r directed radially outward, and the Coriolis force 2 m (ω × vrel) acting perpendicular to the relative velocity vrel of the particle in the rotating frame. These forces are not the result of any physical interaction; they are mathematical devices that allow Newton’s second law to be applied in the accelerating frame.

The centrifugal force is responsible for the apparent outward push felt by a passenger in a turning vehicle. If the vehicle follows a circular path of radius r at speed v, the required centripetal acceleration is v² / r. In the vehicle’s frame the passenger is at rest, so the net radial force must be zero. The real forces are the normal reaction from the seat and the friction from the seat belt; the pseudo centrifugal force balances their inward components. The Coriolis force becomes important when an object moves radially on a rotating platform. For a particle sliding outward on a frictionless turntable, the Coriolis force deflects its trajectory sideways, producing a curved path in the rotating frame even though the inertial trajectory is a straight line.

When solving problems in non‑inertial frames, the standard procedure is: (1) identify the acceleration of the frame, (2) draw a free‑body diagram including all real forces, (3) add the appropriate pseudo forces, (4) write Newton’s second law in the non‑inertial frame. This method is especially powerful for systems where the frame’s motion is prescribed, such as a block on an accelerating wedge, a pendulum in an accelerating lift, or a bead on a rotating hoop. Care must be taken to distinguish between the frame’s angular velocity and the particle’s angular velocity relative to the frame; the Coriolis term involves the relative velocity only.

A common pitfall is to treat the centrifugal force as a real interaction force and to include it in action‑reaction pairs. It has no reaction counterpart because it arises solely from the choice of frame. Another error is to omit the Coriolis force when the particle has a velocity component perpendicular to the rotation axis. In many textbook problems the Coriolis term vanishes because the motion is purely radial or purely tangential, but in general it must be retained. Mastery of pseudo forces enables the analysis of weather systems (Coriolis effect on wind), the design of rotating machinery, and the interpretation of experiments performed in accelerating laboratories.

Rolling Motion and the Condition for Pure Rolling

Rolling without slipping is a constrained motion where the instantaneous point of contact between a body and a surface has zero velocity relative to the surface. For a cylinder or sphere of radius R rolling on a horizontal plane, the translational velocity of the centre of mass v and the angular velocity ω satisfy v = ω R. Differentiating gives the acceleration relation a = α R, where α is the angular acceleration. The static friction force at the contact point provides the necessary torque for angular acceleration but does no work because the contact point is instantaneously at rest. Consequently, mechanical energy is conserved in pure rolling on a fixed rough surface if no other dissipative forces act.

Consider a uniform solid cylinder of mass m and radius R released from rest on an inclined plane of angle θ. The forces are weight mg, normal reaction N = mg cosθ, and static friction f acting up the plane. The translational equation is mg sinθ − f = m a. The rotational equation about the centre is f R = I α, with moment of inertia I = ½ m R² for a solid cylinder. Using a = α R gives f = ½ m a. Substituting into the translational equation yields a = (2/3) g sinθ and f = (1/3) mg sinθ. The friction required is less than the maximum static friction μs N provided μs ≥ (1/3) tanθ. If the coefficient is smaller, slipping occurs and kinetic friction takes over, reducing the acceleration to a = g (sinθ − μk cosθ).

The direction of static friction in rolling is often misunderstood. On an incline, friction acts up the plane for a cylinder rolling down, because the weight component tends to spin the cylinder faster than translation would allow. Conversely, if a torque is applied to the cylinder (e.g., by a string wound around it) causing it to roll up the incline, friction acts down the plane. In all cases, static friction adjusts to enforce the no‑slip constraint v = ω R. The work‑energy theorem for rolling includes both translational and rotational kinetic energy: K = ½ m v² + ½ I ω². For a body rolling down a height h, energy conservation gives mgh = ½ m v² + ½ I (v² / R²), leading to v = √[ 2gh / (1 + I / (m R²)) ]. This shows that objects with larger moment of inertia (e.g., a hollow sphere) reach the bottom later than those with smaller moment of inertia (e.g., a solid sphere).

Rolling with slipping introduces kinetic friction, which does negative work and converts mechanical energy into heat. The equations of motion then become mg sinθ − μk mg cosθ = m a and μk mg cosθ R = I α, with no kinematic link between a and α. The transition from slipping to pure rolling occurs when the condition v = ω R is finally satisfied; after that instant static friction may take over if the required friction does not exceed μs N. Problems involving a ball projected onto a rough horizontal surface with initial spin but no translation, or a cylinder pulled by a horizontal force applied at its centre, are classic illustrations of this transition.

Exam‑day strategy
When a problem mentions a rotating or accelerating platform, immediately decide whether to work in the inertial frame (using centripetal acceleration) or the non‑inertial frame (adding pseudo forces). In the non‑inertial frame, draw the pseudo forces first; they are always opposite to the frame’s acceleration. This reduces sign errors and makes the equilibrium equations straightforward.

Vertical Circular Motion and Critical Speeds

Vertical circular motion differs from horizontal circular motion because the weight of the particle has a component along the radial direction that varies with the angular position. Consider a particle of mass m attached to a light inextensible string of length L moving in a vertical circle. At an angle θ measured from the lowest point, the radial forces are tension T toward the centre and the radial component of weight mg cosθ (directed outward when the particle is above the horizontal). The centripetal requirement is T − mg cosθ = m v² / L. Energy conservation gives ½ m v² = ½ m v₀² − mg L (1 − cosθ), where v₀ is the speed at the lowest point. Combining these yields T = m v₀² / L − mg (3 cosθ − 2).

The minimum speed at the lowest point for the particle to complete the circle is determined by the condition that tension never becomes zero. The most critical point is the top of the circle (θ = π), where cosθ = −1. Setting T ≥ 0 at the top gives m vtop² / L ≥ mg, i.e. vtop ≥ √(g L). Using energy conservation between bottom and top: ½ m v₀² = ½ m vtop² + 2 mg L. Substituting the minimum vtop gives v₀ ≥ √(5 g L). If the initial speed is less than this, the string goes slack before the top and the particle follows a projectile path.

For a particle on a spherical surface (e.g., a bead on a smooth wire hoop), the normal reaction replaces tension. The condition for maintaining contact is N ≥ 0. At the top, N + mg = m vtop² / L, so the minimum speed is again √(g L). However, if the particle is constrained to a circular track (like a roller‑coaster loop), the normal force can also push outward, and the design speed is chosen to keep N within comfortable limits for passengers. The concept of “critical speed” also appears in the conical pendulum, where a mass moves in a horizontal circle while the string sweeps out a cone. The vertical component of tension balances weight: T cosφ = mg. The horizontal component provides centripetal force: T sinφ = m ω² L sinφ. Eliminating T gives ω = √(g / (L cosφ)). The period is T = 2π √(L cosφ / g), independent of the mass.

Examiners often test the distinction between a string (which can only pull) and a rod (which can push). With a light rod, the particle can have zero speed at the top and still complete the circle because the rod can exert a compressive force. The minimum speed at the bottom for a rod is then √(4 g L), derived from energy conservation with vtop = 0. Another common twist is to ask for the angle at which the string goes slack for a given initial speed; this requires solving T = 0 for θ using the tension expression above.

Superelevation Design for Railway Tracks and Aircraft Turns

Superelevation (also called cant or banking) is the practice of raising the outer rail or roadway relative to the inner one to counteract the lateral acceleration experienced by a vehicle on a curve. For railways, the design balances the centrifugal force m v² / r with the component of weight due to the tilt angle θ. The ideal superelevation angle satisfies tanθ = v² / (r g), exactly as for a frictionless banked road. However, trains operate over a wide speed range, so a single fixed cant cannot satisfy the ideal condition for all speeds. The concept of “cant deficiency” D quantifies the shortfall: D = (v² / (r g)) − tanθ. A positive cant deficiency means the train experiences a net lateral acceleration toward the outside of the curve, which must be resisted by the friction between wheel and rail. Standards limit cant deficiency to values that ensure passenger comfort and prevent wheel climb (typically D ≤ 0.1 to 0.15 in many codes). Conversely, “cant excess” occurs when a slow train on a highly banked curve feels a net inward lateral acceleration, risking derailment toward the inside; this is limited by the minimum operating speed.

Transition curves (clothoids or Euler spirals) are used to vary the superelevation gradually from zero on straight track to the full cant on the circular curve. The curvature of a clothoid increases linearly with length, providing a constant rate of change of lateral acceleration (jerk) and a smooth increase in superelevation. The length of the transition is chosen so that the rate of change of cant does not exceed a prescribed value (often 1 in 360 per metre). This ensures that the forces on the vehicle and the track structure vary smoothly, reducing wear and improving ride quality.

In aviation, banking is achieved by tilting the lift vector. An aircraft in a coordinated turn banks at an angle φ such that the horizontal component of lift provides the centripetal force: L sinφ = m v² / r. The vertical component balances weight: L cosφ = mg. Dividing gives tanφ = v² / (r g), identical to the frictionless banking formula. The load factor n = L / (mg) = 1 / cosφ increases with bank angle; at φ = 60° the load factor is 2, meaning the pilot and airframe experience twice the normal weight. Structural limits and passenger comfort restrict the maximum bank angle in commercial aviation to about 25°–30°. Fighter aircraft can sustain much higher load factors (up to 9 g) and bank angles exceeding 80°. The turn radius for a given speed and bank angle is r = v² / (g tanφ). Pilots use the “standard rate turn” (3° per second) as a reference; the required bank angle for a standard rate turn at speed v (in knots) is approximately φ ≈ 0.15 v degrees.

Both railway and aircraft design must consider the variation of speed. For railways, the timetable speed, maximum permissible speed, and minimum speed determine the chosen cant and cant deficiency. For aircraft, the turn is usually executed at a constant airspeed, but wind gradients and altitude changes can alter the true airspeed and thus the required bank angle. Modern flight management systems compute the optimal bank angle continuously, while railway signalling systems enforce speed limits that keep the actual cant deficiency within safe bounds. The underlying physics — balancing weight, normal (or lift) reaction, and centripetal requirement — is the same Newtonian force analysis used for a simple banked road.

Impulse, Momentum and Friction in Collisions

The impulse–momentum theorem states that the change in linear momentum of a body equals the integral of the net external force over the collision time: Δp = ∫ F dt = J. For a system of particles, internal forces cancel in pairs, so the total momentum changes only due to external impulses. In a collision between two bodies, the contact force is large and acts for a short duration; the impulse delivered by this force determines the post‑collision velocities. If the collision is perfectly elastic, kinetic energy is also conserved, giving a second equation. The coefficient of restitution e (0 ≤ e ≤ 1) relates the relative speed of separation to the relative speed of approach along the line of impact: e = (v₂′ − v₁′) / (v₁ − v₂). For a perfectly elastic collision e = 1; for perfectly inelastic e = 0 and the bodies stick together.

When friction acts during an oblique collision, the contact force has both normal and tangential components. The normal impulse Jn governs the change in normal momentum, while the tangential impulse Jt changes the tangential momentum. If the surfaces are rough, the maximum tangential impulse is limited by the friction law: |Jt| ≤ μ Jn. In many problems the collision is assumed to be instantaneous, so the friction impulse is either sufficient to prevent relative tangential slip (sticking) or it reaches the limiting value (slipping). The condition for sticking is that the required tangential impulse to equalise the tangential velocities does not exceed μ Jn. If it does, slip occurs and the tangential impulse is Jt = −μ Jn sign(vt), where vt is the relative tangential velocity at contact.

Consider a sphere of mass m and radius R striking a rough horizontal floor with velocity components vx (horizontal) and vy (vertical downward). The normal impulse reverses the vertical momentum: Jn = m (vy′ + vy). If the collision is elastic in the normal direction, vy′ = e vy. The friction impulse changes the horizontal momentum and also exerts a torque about the centre, altering the angular velocity ω. The equations are m (vx′ − vx) = Jt and I (ω′ − ω) = −Jt R (sign depending on convention). For a solid sphere I = 2/5 m R². If the sphere initially has no spin (ω = 0) and the horizontal speed is large, the required Jt to bring the contact point to rest (no slip) may exceed μ Jn. In that case slip persists throughout the impact and the final horizontal speed is vx′ = vx − μ (1+e) vy. If the required impulse is within the limit, the contact point sticks, giving vx′ = (5 vx − 2 R ω) / 7 and ω′ = (5 ω + 2 vx / R) / 7 for a solid sphere.

These principles extend to collisions of rigid bodies with multiple contact points, such as a billiard ball striking a cushion. The cushion exerts a normal impulse perpendicular to its surface and a friction impulse parallel to it. The coefficient of restitution for the cushion is typically less than 1, and the friction coefficient determines the spin imparted to the ball. In sports physics, the “kick” of a football or the “swerve” of a cricket ball can be modelled by combining the impulse from the foot or the air (Magnus force) with the frictional interaction with the ground. Understanding the vector nature of impulse and the role of friction is essential for solving problems where the collision is not head‑on and where rotational motion is coupled to translation.

  • Confusing the direction of pseudo forces: they always oppose the acceleration of the non‑inertial frame, not the motion of the particle.
  • Assuming static friction always acts opposite to the direction of motion; in rolling it can act either up or down an incline depending on the torque applied.
  • Forgetting that the minimum speed for a vertical circle with a string is √(5gL), while with a rigid rod it is √(4gL).
  • Treating cant deficiency as a force rather than an acceleration; it is the unbalanced lateral acceleration after superelevation is accounted for.
  • Neglecting the tangential impulse limit μJn in oblique collisions, leading to impossible sticking conditions.
  • Using the coefficient of restitution for the tangential direction; it applies only to the normal component of relative velocity.
  • Overlooking that the Coriolis force vanishes only when the particle’s velocity is parallel to the rotation axis.

Systems of Particles and Centre‑of‑Mass Motion

The centre of mass (COM) of a system of particles is the mass‑weighted average position vector R = (∑ mᵢ **r**ᵢ) / (∑ mᵢ). Its velocity **V** = d**R**/dt and acceleration **A** = d**V**/dt obey Newton’s second law for the whole system: (∑ mᵢ) **A** = **F**ext, where **F**ext is the vector sum of all external forces. Internal forces cancel in pairs because of Newton’s third law, so they never affect the COM motion. This principle underpins the analysis of explosions, rocket propulsion, and collisions where the system’s total momentum **P** = (∑ mᵢ) **V** is conserved if **F**ext = 0.

The kinetic energy of the system splits into two parts: K = ½ (∑ mᵢ) V² + ½ ∑ mᵢ vᵢ′², where vᵢ′ are velocities relative to the COM. The first term is the translational kinetic energy of the COM; the second is the internal kinetic energy associated with motion about the COM. In an inelastic collision the internal kinetic energy decreases while the COM kinetic energy remains unchanged (provided no external impulse). For a rocket ejecting mass at relative speed u, the thrust force is u (dm/dt) and the COM acceleration follows from **F**ext = M d**V**/dt + u dm/dt, leading to the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation ΔV = u ln(M₀/M).

When solving problems, always draw a free‑body diagram of the entire system to identify external forces. Then write the COM equation (∑ mᵢ) **A** = **F**ext. If the problem asks for individual particle motions, supplement the COM equation with relative‑motion equations or constraints (e.g., rigid‑body distances). A common mistake is to apply the work‑energy theorem to the whole system while ignoring work done by internal non‑conservative forces; internal friction can change the internal kinetic energy without affecting the COM kinetic energy.

Work–Energy Theorem with Non‑Conservative Forces

The work–energy theorem states that the change in kinetic energy of a particle equals the net work done by all forces: ΔK = Wnet = ∑ ∫ **F**·d**r**. For conservative forces (gravity, spring) the work is path‑independent and can be expressed as −ΔU, giving mechanical energy conservation K + U = constant. Non‑conservative forces such as kinetic friction do path‑dependent work Wnc = −∫ fk ds = −μk ∫ N ds. Because friction always opposes relative motion, its work is negative and mechanical energy decreases: ΔK + ΔU = Wnc.

When friction varies with position (e.g., a block sliding on a surface with μ(x)), the work integral must be evaluated piecewise: Wfr = −∫ μ(x) N(x) dx. If the normal force also changes (as on a curved track), both μ and N become functions of the coordinate. The theorem remains valid provided the correct differential displacement is used. In problems involving a block on a rough incline of length L and angle θ, the work done by gravity is mgL sinθ, by friction is −μ mgL cosθ, and the final speed follows from ½ mv² = mgL(sinθ − μ cosθ).

A subtle point: static friction does no work in the ground frame because the point of application has zero instantaneous displacement. However, in a moving reference frame (e.g., a block on an accelerating belt) static friction can do work. Always check the frame of reference before assigning work to static friction. Another trap is to treat the work done by a variable friction force as −μ mgL cosθ even when μ changes; the integral must be performed explicitly.

Work‑Energy with Variable Friction
A 3 kg block slides from rest down a 4 m long rough plane inclined at 30°. The coefficient of kinetic friction varies linearly from 0.1 at the top to 0.3 at the bottom: μ(x) = 0.1 + 0.05x, where x is the distance measured from the top along the plane. Find the speed of the block at the bottom.
The work done by gravity: Wg = mgL sinθ = 3×9.8×4×sin30° = 58.8 J. The normal force N = mg cosθ = 3×9.8×cos30° ≈ 25.46 N (constant). Friction work: Wfr = −∫₀ᴸ μ(x) N dx = −N ∫₀⁴ (0.1 + 0.05x) dx = −25.46 [0.1x + 0.025x²]₀⁴ = −25.46 (0.4 + 0.4) = −20.37 J. Net work = 58.8 − 20.37 = 38.43 J. By work–energy theorem, ½ mv² = 38.43 → v = √(2×38.43/3) ≈ 5.06 m/s.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives a position‑dependent friction coefficient, write the integral immediately; do not substitute an average μ unless the question explicitly asks for an approximation. Check units of the integral result (joules) before equating to kinetic energy.

Coriolis Force in Rotating Reference Frames

In a frame rotating with constant angular velocity **Ω**, a particle of mass m moving with velocity **v**′ relative to the rotating frame experiences a Coriolis force **F**C = −2m (**Ω** × **v**′). This force is perpendicular to both the rotation axis and the particle’s velocity in the rotating frame, so it does no work ( **F**C·**v**′ = 0 ) but deflects the trajectory. The magnitude is 2mΩv′ sinφ, where φ is the angle between **Ω** and **v**′. On Earth, Ω ≈ 7.29×10⁻⁵ rad/s; for a projectile fired horizontally at 500 m/s at latitude 45°, the Coriolis acceleration is about 0.05 m/s², causing a noticeable sideways drift over long ranges.

The Coriolis effect explains the rotation direction of large‑scale weather systems: in the Northern Hemisphere cyclones rotate counter‑clockwise because the Coriolis force deflects moving air to the right. The Foucault pendulum demonstrates the effect directly; its plane of oscillation rotates at rate Ω sinλ, where λ is latitude. In engineering, the Coriolis force appears in rotating machinery (e.g., turbine blades) and must be accounted for in the design of gyroscopic navigation systems.

When solving dynamics in a rotating frame, write the equation of motion as m **a**′ = **F**real + **F**cf + **F**C, where **F**cf = −m **Ω** × (**Ω** × **r**′) is the centrifugal force. Remember that the Coriolis force depends on the velocity in the rotating frame, not the inertial velocity. A frequent error is to use the inertial velocity in the cross product, which gives the wrong direction and magnitude.

Vehicle Dynamics: Skidding, Rollover and Anti‑Lock Braking

On an unbanked horizontal curve of radius r, the maximum speed without skidding is set by the static friction limit: fs,max = μs N = μs mg. This friction provides the required centripetal force m v²/r, giving vmax = √(μs g r). If the road is banked at angle θ, the normal reaction has a horizontal component N sinθ that assists the centripetal force, and the friction force can act up or down the bank depending on speed. The no‑skid condition becomes v²/r = g (tanθ ± μs) / (1 ∓ μs tanθ), with the upper sign for friction acting down the bank (high speed) and the lower sign for friction acting up the bank (low speed).

Rollover occurs when the overturning moment about the outer wheels exceeds the restoring moment due to weight. For a vehicle of mass m, centre‑of‑gravity height h, and track width t, the lateral acceleration ay at the rollover threshold satisfies m ay h = mg (t/2), giving ay,roll = g t / (2h). On a curve, ay = v²/r, so the rollover speed is vroll = √(g r t / 2h). Modern vehicles lower h and widen t to increase this limit. Electronic stability control (ESC) monitors yaw rate and lateral acceleration, applying selective braking to keep the vehicle within the friction envelope.

Anti‑lock braking systems (ABS) prevent wheel lock‑up during hard braking. When a wheel locks, kinetic friction (μk) replaces static friction (μs), reducing the maximum deceleration from μsg to μkg and eliminating steering control. ABS modulates brake pressure to keep each wheel at the peak of the μ‑slip curve (typically 10‑20 % slip), maintaining near‑static friction and allowing the driver to steer while braking. The control algorithm uses wheel‑speed sensors to detect incipient lock‑up and a hydraulic modulator to pulse brake pressure at 10‑15 Hz.

Motion in a Resistive Medium: Linear and Quadratic Drag

When an object moves through a fluid, the drag force opposes the velocity. At low Reynolds numbers (Re ≪ 1) the drag is linear: **F**d = −b **v**, where b = 6πηR for a sphere of radius R in a fluid of viscosity η (Stokes’ law). The equation of motion for a falling sphere of mass m is m dv/dt = mg − bv. The solution with v(0)=0 is v(t) = (mg/b)(1 − e−bt/m). The terminal velocity vt = mg/b is reached exponentially with time constant τ = m/b.

At high Reynolds numbers (Re ≫ 1) the drag is quadratic: **F**d = −c v **v**, where c = ½ ρ Cd A, ρ is fluid density, Cd the drag coefficient, and A the cross‑sectional area. For a skydiver, mg = c vt² gives vt = √(mg/c). The velocity‑time relation is obtained by separating variables: ∫ dv/(g − (c/m)v²) = ∫ dt, yielding v(t) = vt tanh(gt/vt). The distance fallen is y(t) = (vt²/g) ln cosh(gt/vt).

In many practical problems the drag regime changes during the motion (e.g., a sphere falling from rest passes through a linear regime before reaching turbulent quadratic drag). One must then solve piecewise, matching velocity and position at the transition Reynolds number. A common mistake is to apply the quadratic formula for all speeds, which overestimates drag at low speeds and underestimates terminal velocity for small objects. Always estimate the Reynolds number Re = ρ v L / η to decide the appropriate model.

  • Assuming the centre‑of‑mass acceleration equals the acceleration of any individual particle in a system.
  • Using a constant coefficient of friction when the problem states it varies with position.
  • Applying the Coriolis force with the inertial velocity instead of the rotating‑frame velocity.
  • Neglecting the direction change of friction on a banked curve when speed is below the design speed.
  • Treating kinetic friction as the limiting value during ABS operation; ABS maintains static friction.
  • Using the quadratic drag law for a small droplet at low Reynolds number where Stokes’ law applies.

Constraint Forces and Generalized Coordinates in Planar Motion

When a particle moves on a prescribed curve or surface, the reaction forces that enforce the constraint do no work if the constraint is ideal and frictionless. By introducing generalized coordinates that automatically satisfy the geometric constraints, the equations of motion can be written without explicitly solving for the constraint forces. For a bead sliding on a smooth wire shaped as y = f(x), the single generalized coordinate can be taken as the arc length s or the horizontal coordinate x. The kinetic energy T = ½ m (ẋ² + ẏ²) becomes a function of the chosen coordinate and its time derivative, and the Lagrangian L = T – V yields the Euler–Lagrange equation d/dt(∂L/∂q̇) – ∂L/∂q = 0. This approach eliminates the normal reaction from the differential equation, leaving only the tangential component of gravity and any non‑conservative forces. The method extends to systems with several particles linked by rigid rods or strings, where the number of generalized coordinates equals the number of degrees of freedom. A common pitfall is to treat a constraint force as an applied force in the energy equation; the correct procedure is to exclude it because its virtual work vanishes for ideal constraints.

For a double pendulum consisting of two point masses m₁, m₂ attached to massless rods of lengths l₁, l₂, the generalized coordinates are the two angles θ₁, θ₂ measured from the vertical. The position vectors are r₁ = (l₁ sinθ₁, –l₁ cosθ₁) and r₂ = r₁ + (l₂ sinθ₂, –l₂ cosθ₂). The kinetic energy contains cross terms proportional to cos(θ₁ – θ₂) θ̇₁ θ̇₂, reflecting the coupling between the two degrees of freedom. The potential energy is V = –m₁ g l₁ cosθ₁ – m₂ g (l₁ cosθ₁ + l₂ cosθ₂). Applying the Euler–Lagrange equations yields two coupled nonlinear second‑order ODEs. Linearising about the stable equilibrium θ₁ = θ₂ = 0 gives the normal‑mode frequencies ω² = g/l₁ (1 ± √(1 + m₂/m₁)). This illustrates how generalized coordinates simplify the derivation of equations of motion for constrained systems and how small‑oscillation analysis follows naturally.

In problems where the constraint itself moves, such as a bead on a rotating hoop, the constraint is time‑dependent. The generalized coordinate can still be the angle along the hoop, but the kinetic energy acquires an explicit time dependence through the hoop’s angular velocity Ω. The Lagrangian then contains a term m R² Ω θ̇ sin²θ, which acts like a gyroscopic force. The resulting equation of motion includes a term proportional to Ω² sinθ cosθ that can stabilise the bead at an angle away from the bottom. This is the classic “bead on a rotating hoop” problem, often used to illustrate effective potentials in non‑inertial frames without invoking pseudo forces explicitly. The key is to recognise that the constraint forces do no work in the instantaneous rest frame of the hoop, so they remain absent from the Lagrangian.

When friction is present at the constraint, the constraint force acquires a tangential component that does work. In that case the friction force must be introduced as a generalized non‑conservative force Q_fric = –μ N sign(q̇) where N is the normal reaction obtained from the constraint equations. The normal reaction can be found a posteriori by substituting the solved motion into the original Newtonian equations or by using Lagrange multipliers. The multiplier method adds λ ∂f/∂q to the Euler–Lagrange equation, where f(q,t)=0 is the constraint. The multiplier λ equals the magnitude of the constraint force. This technique is essential for problems such as a block sliding on a moving wedge with friction, where the normal force depends on the acceleration of the wedge.

Examiners frequently test the ability to choose appropriate generalized coordinates and to identify when constraint forces can be ignored. A typical trap is to write the Newtonian equations for each mass and then attempt to eliminate the tension in a string by adding the equations, which works only for simple systems. The Lagrangian method provides a systematic route: write T and V in terms of the chosen coordinates, form L, and apply the Euler–Lagrange equations. Mastery of this technique reduces algebraic errors and reveals conserved quantities (e.g., cyclic coordinates) that are not obvious in the vector formulation.

Variable Mass Systems and the Rocket Equation

Systems that gain or lose mass, such as rockets expelling propellant or a raindrop accreting water vapour, require a modified form of Newton’s second law. The standard expression F = dp/dt remains valid if the momentum p of the system is defined as the total momentum of all mass that currently belongs to the system. For a rocket of instantaneous mass m moving with velocity v in an inertial frame, ejecting mass at rate ṁ (negative) with exhaust velocity u relative to the rocket, the momentum balance gives m dv/dt = ṁ u + F_ext. The term ṁ u is the thrust; it is positive when mass is ejected backward (ṁ < 0, u directed opposite to v). Integrating with F_ext = 0 yields the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation Δv = u ln(m₀/m_f), where m₀ is the initial mass and m_f the final mass after burnout. This result assumes constant exhaust velocity and no external forces; gravity and drag can be added as F_ext = –mg – ½ ρ C_d A v² for atmospheric flight.

When mass is added to the system, as in a snowball rolling down a slope and gathering snow, the incoming mass initially has zero horizontal velocity in the ground frame. The momentum equation becomes d(mv)/dt = v dm/dt + m dv/dt = F_ext. The term v dm/dt represents the momentum that must be supplied to the accreted mass to bring it up to the system’s velocity. If the accreted mass already moves with velocity v_in, the thrust term generalises to (v_in – v) dm/dt. For a rocket taking on fuel in flight (air‑breathing engine), v_in is the velocity of the incoming air relative to the ground, and the thrust expression must account for the momentum flux of the intake. A frequent error is to write F = m a and simply replace m by m(t), ignoring the ṁ v term; this leads to an incorrect prediction that a rocket could accelerate without expelling mass.

The rocket equation can be derived elegantly using the concept of centre‑of‑mass motion for a system of variable composition. Consider the rocket plus the expelled gas as a closed system with no external forces. The centre of mass of this combined system moves with constant velocity. Differentiating the centre‑of‑mass position gives m_rocket v_rocket + ∫ m_gas v_gas = constant. Since the gas velocity relative to the ground is v_rocket – u, the integral yields the same logarithmic relation. This derivation highlights that the rocket’s acceleration is a consequence of momentum conservation, not a mysterious “reaction force”. It also clarifies why the exhaust velocity u must be measured relative to the rocket, not the ground.

In multi‑stage rockets, the total Δv is the sum of the Δv contributed by each stage, provided the stages are discarded after burnout. The mass ratio of each stage is defined as the ratio of the stage’s initial mass (including upper stages) to its final mass (after propellant depletion and structural separation). Optimising stage mass ratios for a given total Δv and structural fraction leads to the well‑known result that equal mass ratios per stage are optimal when the structural coefficient is the same for all stages. Real launch vehicles deviate from this ideal due to varying engine specific impulse, aerodynamic loads, and thrust‑to‑weight constraints. Understanding these trade‑offs is essential for problems that ask for the minimum number of stages to achieve orbit.

Examiners often embed variable‑mass concepts in seemingly unrelated contexts, such as a leaking sandbag on a frictionless cart or a conveyor belt receiving sand at a constant rate. In the leaking sandbag, the sand leaves the bag with the same horizontal velocity as the bag, so ṁ u = 0 and the cart’s velocity remains constant despite the mass loss. In the conveyor belt problem, sand falls vertically onto a horizontally moving belt; the belt must exert a horizontal force on the sand to accelerate it to the belt speed, resulting in a required driving force F = v dm/dt. Recognising whether the added or ejected mass carries momentum relative to the system is the decisive step.

Worked Example: Rocket with Gravity and Linear Drag
A rocket of initial mass m₀ = 5000 kg burns fuel at a constant rate ṁ = –50 kg/s with exhaust velocity u = 2500 m/s relative to the rocket. It launches vertically from rest in a uniform gravitational field g = 9.8 m/s² and experiences a linear drag force F_d = –k v with k = 200 N·s/m. Find the velocity as a function of time until burnout at t_b = 80 s.
The equation of motion is m dv/dt = ṁ u – m g – k v, with m(t) = m₀ + ṁ t. Substitute ṁ = –50, u = 2500 → thrust = 125000 N. The ODE is (m₀ – 50 t) dv/dt + k v = 125000 – (m₀ – 50 t) g. This is a first‑order linear ODE in v. The integrating factor μ(t) = exp(∫ k/(m₀ – 50 t) dt) = (m₀ – 50 t)^(–k/50). With k = 200, exponent = –4. Multiply the ODE by μ and integrate from 0 to t. After integration and applying v(0)=0, the solution is v(t) = (m₀ – 50 t)⁴ ∫₀ᵗ [125000 – (m₀ – 50 τ) g] (m₀ – 50 τ)⁻⁴ dτ. Evaluating the integral yields v(t) = (125000/3·50) [1 – (m₀ – 50 t)³/m₀³] – g t + (k/50)⁻¹ g [1 – (m₀ – 50 t)⁴/m₀⁴]. Substituting numbers gives v(t) ≈ 833.3 [1 – (1 – 0.01 t)³] – 9.8 t + 0.25·9.8 [1 – (1 – 0.01 t)⁴] m/s. At burnout t = 80 s, m = 1000 kg, v ≈ 2.1 km/s.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a variable‑mass problem appears, first write the momentum balance for the system including the mass flux term (v_rel – v) dm/dt. Identify whether the incoming/outgoing mass has the same velocity as the system (then the term vanishes) or a different velocity (then it contributes thrust or drag). Next, decide if external forces (gravity, drag) are present and add them. Finally, check whether the mass rate ṁ is constant; if so, the equation often reduces to a linear first‑order ODE solvable with an integrating factor. Practice the standard forms: rocket (ṁ < 0, u opposite v), accretion (ṁ > 0, v_in = 0), and conveyor belt (ṁ > 0, v_in = 0 but system moves).

Stability of Equilibrium and Small Oscillations

The stability of a mechanical equilibrium can be assessed by examining the potential energy V(q) near the equilibrium configuration q₀ where ∂V/∂q = 0. If the second derivative ∂²V/∂q² evaluated at q₀ is positive, the equilibrium is stable; if negative, it is unstable; if zero, higher‑order terms decide. For a system with several degrees of freedom, the Hessian matrix H_ij = ∂²V/∂q_i ∂q_j at equilibrium must be positive definite for stability. This criterion follows from the fact that for small displacements the Lagrangian reduces to L ≈ ½ Σ m_ij q̇_i q̇_j – ½ Σ H_ij q_i q_j, leading to the linearised equations M q̈ + H q = 0. The normal‑mode frequencies are the square roots of the eigenvalues of M⁻¹ H. If any eigenvalue is negative, the corresponding mode grows exponentially, signalling instability.

Consider a particle of mass m constrained to move on a vertical circular hoop of radius R that rotates about its vertical diameter with constant angular speed Ω. The generalized coordinate is the angle θ measured from the lowest point. The effective potential is V_eff(θ) = –mgR cosθ – ½ m Ω² R² sin²θ. Equilibria satisfy dV_eff/dθ = mgR sinθ – m Ω² R² sinθ cosθ = 0, giving θ = 0, π, and cosθ = g/(Ω² R) when Ω² R > g. The second derivative d²V_eff/dθ² at θ = 0 is mgR – m Ω² R². Thus the bottom equilibrium is stable only if Ω² < g/R; above this critical speed it becomes unstable and two new symmetric stable equilibria appear at cosθ = g/(Ω² R). This pitchfork bifurcation is a classic example of how rotation can stabilise an otherwise unstable position.

For small oscillations about a stable equilibrium, the motion is simple harmonic with angular frequency ω = √(k_eff/m_eff), where k_eff is the relevant second derivative of V and m_eff the corresponding generalized mass. In the rotating hoop example, for the new equilibria the effective spring constant is k_eff = m Ω² R² (1 – (g/Ω² R)²) and the generalized mass is m R², giving ω = Ω √(1 – (g/Ω² R)²). Notice that ω → 0 as Ω approaches the critical value from above, a phenomenon known as critical slowing down. This behaviour is often tested by asking for the period of small oscillations as a function of Ω and identifying the bifurcation point.

When damping is present, the linearised equation becomes M q̈ + C q̇ + H q = 0, where C is the damping matrix. For proportional damping (C = α M + β H), the normal modes decouple and each obeys q̈_i + 2 ζ_i ω_i q̇_i + ω_i² q_i = 0 with damping ratio ζ_i = (α/2ω_i + β ω_i/2). Underdamped modes oscillate with frequency ω_d = ω_i √(1 – ζ_i²); overdamped modes decay without oscillation. In many exam problems, only a single degree of freedom is damped, and the student must write the equation of motion, identify the damping coefficient, and state the condition for underdamping (ζ < 1). A common mistake is to confuse the damping ratio with the quality factor Q = 1/(2ζ).

Energy methods provide an alternative stability test: if the total energy E = T + V has a strict minimum at equilibrium, the equilibrium is stable (Dirichlet’s theorem). For non‑conservative systems, Lyapunov functions generalise this idea. In the context of the syllabus, the examiner may ask to construct the effective potential for a particle in a central force field with angular momentum L, V_eff(r) = V(r) + L²/(2mr²). The condition for a stable circular orbit is dV_eff/dr = 0 and d²V_eff/dr² > 0. Applying this to the inverse‑square law V = –k/r yields the well‑known result that circular orbits are stable for all radii, whereas for a force law F ∝ –rⁿ with n < –3 the effective potential has a maximum and circular orbits are unstable. Recognising the role of the centrifugal barrier term L²/(2mr²) is crucial.

Non‑Uniform Circular Motion with Time‑Varying Radius

When a particle moves in a plane under a central force but the radial distance r(t) changes, the motion is no longer uniform circular motion. The acceleration in polar coordinates has radial component a_r = r̈ – r θ̇² and transverse component a_θ = r θ̈ + 2 ṙ θ̇. The term 2 ṙ θ̇ is the Coriolis acceleration arising from the coupling of radial and angular motion; it is a real inertial effect, not a pseudo force. If the force is purely central, F_θ = 0, then the transverse equation gives d/dt (m r² θ̇) = 0, i.e., angular momentum L = m r² θ̇ is conserved. This allows elimination of θ̇ = L/(m r²) from the radial equation, yielding m r̈ = F_r + L²/(m r³). The extra term L²/(m r³) is the centrifugal barrier that appears in the effective one‑dimensional radial motion.

A classic problem is a particle attached to a string passing through a hole in a smooth horizontal table, with the string pulled downward at a constant speed v₀. The radius decreases as r(t) = r₀ – v₀ t. Since the force is central (tension), angular momentum is conserved: θ̇ = L/(m r²). The tension T = m (r̈ – r θ̇²) = –m L²/(m² r³) because r̈ = 0. Thus T = –L²/(m r³), which grows as r⁻³. The work done by the pulling force equals the increase in kinetic energy: ΔK = ½ m (ṙ² + r² θ̇²) – ½ m r₀² θ̇₀². Substituting ṙ = –v₀ and θ̇ = L/(m r²) shows that the kinetic energy increases as r decreases, the extra energy being supplied by the agent pulling the string. This problem tests the ability to apply angular momentum conservation in a system with a moving constraint.

Another important case is a bead sliding on a frictionless rod that rotates about a vertical axis with prescribed angular velocity Ω(t). The rod imposes the constraint θ = Ω(t), so θ̇ = Ω, θ̈ = Ω̇. The radial equation becomes m r̈ = m r Ω² – mg sinα (if the rod is inclined at angle α). If the rod is horizontal (α = 0), the equation is r̈ – Ω² r = 0, whose solution is a linear combination of eΩ t and e–Ω t for constant Ω. For time‑varying Ω(t), the equation is a parametric oscillator; if Ω(t) varies periodically, parametric resonance can occur, leading to exponential growth of r. This is the principle behind the “rotating rod” demonstration of parametric amplification.

In the presence of non‑central forces, such as a drag force proportional to velocity, the angular momentum is no longer conserved. The transverse equation becomes m (r θ̈ + 2 ṙ θ̇) = –k r θ̇ (for linear drag). This can be rewritten as d/dt (m r² θ̇) = –k r² θ̇, showing that angular momentum decays exponentially if r is approximately constant. For a satellite experiencing atmospheric drag at high altitude, the orbit decays and the radius decreases slowly; the drag force has a small transverse component that reduces angular momentum, while the radial component does work against gravity. Analysing such problems requires writing both polar equations and often using the fact that the orbit remains nearly circular to simplify the algebra.

Examiners frequently ask for the tension in a string winding around a pole (the “involute” problem) or the motion of a particle on a rotating turntable with a radial groove. In the winding string problem, the string length decreases as it wraps, so r(t) is not a simple linear function; the constraint is that the string is always tangent to the pole. The geometry gives dr/dθ = –a (pole radius), leading to r = r₀ – a θ. Combining with angular momentum conservation yields a differential equation for θ(t). The key is to recognise the geometric relation between r and θ before writing the dynamics. In the rotating turntable with a radial groove, the groove forces the particle to move radially while the turntable rotates; the Coriolis force 2 m ṙ Ω appears as a real transverse force exerted by the groove walls. The radial equation includes the centrifugal term m r Ω². If the groove is frictionless, the particle slides outward under centrifugal force, and the Coriolis force causes it to press against one side of the groove.

Friction in Multi‑Contact Systems

Many practical problems involve bodies in contact at several points, each capable of transmitting both normal and frictional forces. The static friction at each contact satisfies |f_i| ≤ μ_i N_i, and the direction of each friction force opposes the impending relative motion at that contact. When the system is on the verge of slipping, one or more contacts reach the limiting value f_i = ± μ_i N_i. Determining which contacts slip first requires solving the equilibrium equations together with the friction inequalities. A systematic approach is to assume a set of active slip contacts, replace their friction forces by the limiting values with appropriate signs, and solve for the remaining unknowns. If the solution yields friction forces at the assumed sticking contacts that satisfy |f_j| < μ_j N_j, the assumption is consistent. Otherwise, a different combination of slipping contacts must be tried.

Consider a uniform ladder of length L and mass m leaning against a rough vertical wall (coefficient μ_w) and resting on a rough horizontal floor (coefficient μ_f). The ladder makes an angle θ with the horizontal. The four unknown reaction components are N_f, f_f at the floor and N_w, f_w at the wall. Equilibrium gives ΣF_x = 0 → f_w = N_f, ΣF_y = 0 → N_w + f_f = mg, and ΣM about the floor contact = 0 → N_w L sinθ + f_w L cosθ = mg (L/2) cosθ. The friction limits are f_f ≤ μ_f N_f and f_w ≤ μ_w N_w. Substituting f_w = N_f and eliminating N_w yields a condition on θ for equilibrium: tanθ ≥ (1 – μ_f μ_w) / (2 μ_f). If μ_f = μ_w = μ, this reduces to tanθ ≥ (1 – μ²)/(2μ). For a given μ, there is a minimum angle below which the ladder must slip regardless of the friction forces. This result is often asked in the form “find the minimum angle for a ladder with given coefficients”.

In a block‑on‑block system where a horizontal force F is applied to the lower block of mass M resting on a frictionless table, and a smaller block of mass m sits on top with coefficient μ between them, the maximum F without slipping is found by assuming the two blocks move together with acceleration a = F/(M+m). The friction on the upper block provides its acceleration: f = m a ≤ μ m g. Hence F ≤ μ g (M+m). If F exceeds this value, the upper block slips and its acceleration becomes a_u = μ g while the lower block accelerates at a_l = (F – μ m g)/M. The transition from sticking to slipping is a classic example of a bifurcation in the friction force direction. A common error is to assume the friction on the lower block is always μ m g opposite to F, even when the blocks stick together.

For a system of three blocks stacked vertically with different coefficients between each pair, the analysis proceeds from the top down. The top block experiences only the friction from the block below; the middle block experiences friction from both above and below; the bottom block experiences friction from above and the applied force. At each interface, the friction force is either the limiting value (if that interface slips) or an unknown less than the limit (if it sticks). The number of possible slip/stick combinations grows exponentially, but physical reasoning often reduces the possibilities: the interface with the smallest μ mg product tends to slip first. In exam questions, the configuration is usually simple enough that only one or two interfaces are at the point of slipping.

When a body contacts a surface along a line or area, the pressure distribution is not uniform, and the resultant friction force is the integral of μ p dA over the contact region. For a rectangular block of width b and length L pressed against a surface with a linearly varying pressure p(x) = p₀ (1 – x/L), the total normal force is N = ∫ p dA = ½ p₀ b L, and the maximum static friction is F_max = μ N only if the pressure distribution remains unchanged during loading. If the block is about to tip, the pressure shifts to the leading edge, reducing the effective normal force available for friction. This coupling between tipping and slipping is crucial in the design of brakes and clutches. The condition for simultaneous tipping and slipping is that the moment of the friction force about the centre of mass equals the moment of the normal reaction about the same point, leading to a relation between μ, the geometry, and the applied force height.

  • Assuming all contacts reach limiting friction simultaneously without checking consistency.
  • Neglecting the moment equilibrium when friction forces act at different heights.
  • Using the same coefficient of friction for all interfaces when the problem specifies different values.
  • Forgetting that the direction of friction on a block can reverse when the applied force exceeds the sticking limit.
  • Treating the normal reaction as uniformly distributed when the body is on the verge of tipping.
  • Applying the single‑block friction formula F_max = μ mg to a multi‑block stack without accounting for internal forces.
  • Ignoring the Coriolis term 2 ṙ θ̇ in polar coordinates when the radius changes.

These advanced topics extend the core syllabus and appear regularly in higher‑level physics examinations.
