What is Thermodynamics?

Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that deals with heat, work, temperature, and the conversion of energy between different forms. It explains how thermal energy is transferred and how it affects matter. Key concepts for NEET include the laws of thermodynamics, specific heat capacity, calorimetry, heat engines, and entropy. Thermodynamics is crucial for understanding processes in engines, refrigerators, and natural phenomena.

Key Concepts

Zeroth Law: If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. First Law of Thermodynamics: ΔU = Q - W, where ΔU is change in internal energy, Q is heat added, and W is work done by the system. Second Law: Heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body. Specific Heat: c = Q/(mΔT). Molar Specific Heats: Cv = (f/2)R and Cp = Cv + R, where f is degrees of freedom. Efficiency of Heat Engine: η = 1 - Tc/Th (Carnot efficiency).

Solved Examples

Example 1
A gas is compressed by doing 500 J of work on it, and it absorbs 300 J of heat. Find the change in internal energy of the gas.

a) 200 J b) 800 J c) -200 J d) -800 J
Solution: Using first law ΔU = Q - W. Since work is done ON the system, W = -500 J (negative). Heat absorbed Q = +300 J. So ΔU = 300 - (-500) = 300 + 500 = 800 J. Hence option (b) is correct.
Example 2
A Carnot engine operates between 127°C and 27°C. Find its efficiency.

a) 25% b) 50% c) 75% d) 33.3%
Solution: Convert temperatures to Kelvin: Th = 127 + 273 = 400 K, Tc = 27 + 273 = 300 K. Efficiency η = 1 - Tc/Th = 1 - 300/400 = 1 - 0.75 = 0.25 = 25%. Hence option (a) is correct.
Example 3
Calculate the work done when 2 moles of an ideal gas expand isothermally at 300 K from a volume of 10 L to 20 L. (R = 8.314 J/mol-K)

a) 3456 J b) 4157 J c) 4988 J d) 2885 J
Solution: For isothermal expansion, W = nRT ln(V2/V1). W = 2 × 8.314 × 300 × ln(20/10) = 2 × 8.314 × 300 × ln(2) = 2 × 8.314 × 300 × 0.693 = 3456 J. Hence option (a) is correct.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For adiabatic processes, remember PVγ = constant and use γ = Cp/Cv.
  • In calorimetry problems, apply heat lost = heat gained. Always check the final equilibrium phase (ice/water/steam).
  • For efficiency problems, always convert Celsius to Kelvin before using formulas.
  • In cyclic processes, ΔU = 0, so net work = net heat (Qin - Qout).
Pro Tip
For NEET, remember that the first law is essentially conservation of energy. The sign convention is crucial: work done BY the system is positive, work done ON the system is negative. Practice with sign conventions to avoid mistakes in exams.

Maxwell Relations and Their Applications

The four Maxwell relations arise from the equality of mixed second derivatives of the thermodynamic potentials. Starting from the fundamental relation for internal energy, dU = T dS − P dV, the natural variables are entropy S and volume V. By performing Legendre transforms we obtain the enthalpy H = U + PV (natural variables S, P), the Helmholtz free energy F = U − TS (natural variables T, V), and the Gibbs free energy G = H − TS (natural variables T, P). For each potential the differential form yields a pair of conjugate variables; equating the cross derivatives gives the Maxwell relations:

  • From dU: (∂T/∂V)S = −(∂P/∂S)V
  • From dH: (∂T/∂P)S = (∂V/∂S)P
  • From dF: (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V
  • From dG: (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P

These relations are powerful because they connect measurable quantities (thermal expansion coefficient α = (1/V)(∂V/∂T)P, isothermal compressibility κT = −(1/V)(∂V/∂P)T, heat capacities CP, CV) to entropy derivatives that are otherwise difficult to access experimentally. For instance, the third relation gives (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V. Using the ideal‑gas equation PV = nRT, (∂P/∂T)V = nR/V, so the entropy change on isothermal expansion of an ideal gas is ΔS = nR ln(V2/V1), a result that can also be obtained from the definition of entropy but emerges here without integrating dQ/T.

A frequent examination twist asks for the difference CP − CV in terms of α and κT. Starting from the definition CP − CV = T(∂P/∂T)V(∂V/∂T)P and substituting the Maxwell relation (∂P/∂T)V = (∂S/∂V)T together with the definitions of α and κT, one obtains the compact identity CP − CV = TVα²/κT. This expression holds for any simple compressible system and is a standard result in advanced thermodynamics problems.

Another application is the derivation of the Joule‑Thomson coefficient μJT = (∂T/∂P)H. Using the cyclic rule and the Maxwell relation from dH, μJT can be expressed as (1/CP)[T(∂V/∂T)P − V]. For an ideal gas the term in brackets vanishes, confirming that μJT = 0. For real gases the sign of μJT determines whether a throttling process produces cooling (μJT > 0) or heating (μJT < 0), a principle exploited in gas liquefaction.

Phase Equilibria and the Clapeyron Equation

When two phases of a pure substance coexist in equilibrium, their specific Gibbs free energies are equal: g1(T,P) = g2(T,P). Differentiating this equality along the coexistence curve gives the Clapeyron equation, which relates the slope of the phase boundary in the P‑T plane to the latent heat and the change in specific volume:

dP/dT = Δs/Δv = L/(T Δv)

Here Δs = s2 − s1 is the specific entropy change, Δv = v2 − v1 the specific volume change, and L = T Δs the specific latent heat. The equation is exact for any first‑order transition (solid‑liquid, liquid‑vapour, solid‑vapour). For the liquid‑vapour transition the volume change is dominated by the vapour specific volume vg ≫ vf, so Δv ≈ vg. Assuming the vapour behaves as an ideal gas, vg = RT/P, the Clapeyron equation reduces to the Clausius‑Clapeyron form:

d(ln P)/dT = L/(R T²)

Integrating between two temperatures (assuming L constant) yields the familiar logarithmic vapour‑pressure relation ln(P₂/P₁) = −(L/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁). This approximation works well for many substances over moderate temperature ranges but fails near the critical point where Δv → 0 and L → 0.

The solid‑liquid boundary for most substances has a positive slope because the solid is denser than the liquid (Δv < 0, L > 0 ⇒ dP/dT > 0). Water is a notable exception: ice has a larger specific volume than liquid water, so Δv > 0 and the melting curve slopes negatively (dP/dT < 0). This explains why applying pressure melts ice, a fact used in ice‑skating and in the regelation phenomenon.

At the triple point the three coexistence curves meet. The slopes of the three curves satisfy the vector sum condition derived from the Gibbs phase rule (F = C − P + 2). For a single component (C = 1) with three phases (P = 3) the degrees of freedom F = 0, so the triple point is a fixed (T,P) pair. The critical point terminates the liquid‑vapour curve; beyond it the distinction between liquid and vapour disappears and the Clapeyron equation no longer applies because the transition becomes continuous (second order).

Examiners often test the ability to estimate the boiling point at a pressure different from 1 atm using the integrated Clausius‑Clapeyron equation, or to compare the slopes of the solid‑liquid and liquid‑vapour curves qualitatively. A common trap is to use the ideal‑gas approximation for the solid‑liquid transition, where Δv is not dominated by a gas phase and the approximation is invalid.

Worked Example: Boiling Point at Reduced Pressure
Water boils at 100 °C (373.15 K) at 1 atm. The latent heat of vaporisation is 2257 kJ/kg. Estimate the boiling temperature at 0.5 atm assuming L is constant and the vapour behaves ideally. (R = 0.4615 kJ/kg·K for water vapour.)
Use the integrated Clausius‑Clapeyron equation: ln(P₂/P₁) = −(L/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁).
P₁ = 1 atm, T₁ = 373.15 K, P₂ = 0.5 atm, L = 2257 kJ/kg, R = 0.4615 kJ/kg·K.
ln(0.5) = −(2257/0.4615)(1/T₂ − 1/373.15).
−0.6931 = −4890.6 (1/T₂ − 0.002679).
1/T₂ − 0.002679 = 0.6931/4890.6 = 1.417×10⁻⁴.
1/T₂ = 0.002679 + 0.0001417 = 0.0028207.
T₂ = 1/0.0028207 ≈ 354.5 K = 81.3 °C.
Thus the boiling point at 0.5 atm is approximately 81 °C.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives latent heat in kJ/kg and asks for a temperature shift, convert everything to consistent SI units (J, kg, K) before substituting. Remember that the Clausius‑Clapeyron integration assumes constant L and ideal‑gas vapour; if the temperature range is large, the answer will be approximate. For solid‑liquid transitions, always use the full Clapeyron form dP/dT = L/(T Δv) because the ideal‑gas simplification does not hold.

Irreversibility, Availability, and Exergy Analysis

The second law introduces the concept of entropy generation Sgen ≥ 0 for any real process. For a control mass undergoing a change from state 1 to state 2 while interacting with a reservoir at temperature T₀, the maximum useful work (availability or exergy) that can be extracted is the decrease in the system’s availability function Φ = (U − U₀) + P₀(V − V₀) − T₀(S − S₀). Here the subscript 0 denotes the dead‑state properties (environment). The exergy balance for a closed system reads:

ΔΦ = Σ(1 − T₀/Tk)Qk − Wu − T₀ Sgen

where Qk are heat transfers at temperatures Tk, Wu is the useful work (excluding work done against the environment pressure P₀), and the term T₀ Sgen represents the irreversibility (lost work). For a reversible process Sgen = 0 and the useful work equals the decrease in availability.

In steady‑flow devices (turbines, compressors, heat exchangers) the specific flow exergy is ψ = (h − h₀) − T₀(s − s₀) + (V²/2) + gz. The exergy destruction rate in a component is Ḋ = T₀ Ṡgen. The second‑law efficiency (exergetic efficiency) is defined as the ratio of exergy output to exergy input, e.g., for a turbine ηII = (ṁ ψin − ṁ ψout − Ẇu)/ (ṁ ψin − ṁ ψout). This metric highlights where the largest irreversibilities occur, guiding design improvements.

A classic textbook problem asks for the exergy of a heat reservoir at temperature T > T₀ supplying heat Q. The maximum work obtainable is the Carnot work Wmax = (1 − T₀/T) Q, which is exactly the exergy of that heat. If the same heat is transferred through a finite temperature difference, the exergy destruction is T₀ ΔSuniverse = T₀ Q (1/T₀ − 1/T). This quantifies the penalty of irreversible heat transfer.

Examiners may combine exergy with the first law in a single problem: given a steam turbine inlet and outlet states, ambient conditions, and measured power output, compute the second‑law efficiency. The steps are: (1) determine specific enthalpies and entropies from steam tables, (2) calculate specific flow exergies at inlet and outlet, (3) find the reversible work = ṁ(ψin − ψout), (4) compute ηII = Ẇactual / Ẇrev. A frequent mistake is to use the first‑law efficiency (Ẇ/ṁ(hin−hout)) instead of the exergy‑based definition.

Real Gas Effects: Joule‑Thomson Coefficient and Inversion Curve

The Joule‑Thomson (throttling) process is an isenthalpic expansion of a real gas through a porous plug or valve. The temperature change is governed by the Joule‑Thomson coefficient μJT = (∂T/∂P)H. Using the thermodynamic identity for enthalpy, dH = T dS + V dP, and the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P, one derives:

μJT = (1/CP)[T(∂V/∂T)P − V]

For an ideal gas, V = RT/P, so T(∂V/∂T)P = V and μJT = 0. Real gases deviate because of intermolecular forces and finite molecular volume. The van der Waals equation (P + a/V²)(V − b) = RT provides an analytical expression for μJT. After algebra, the inversion condition μJT = 0 yields the inversion temperature Tinv = 2a/(Rb) for the low‑pressure limit. More generally, the inversion curve in the P‑T plane separates the cooling region (μJT > 0) from the heating region (μJT < 0). For nitrogen at room temperature, T < Tinv so throttling produces cooling, which is the basis of the Linde air‑liquefaction cycle.

The maximum inversion temperature for a van der Waals gas is Tinv,max = 2a/(Rb). Substituting the critical constants (Tc = 8a/(27Rb), Pc = a/(27b²)) gives Tinv,max = (27/4) Tc ≈ 6.75 Tc. For many gases the experimental maximum inversion temperature is about 5–6 times the critical temperature. Below the inversion curve, a throttling valve can be used as a refrigeration stage; above it, the gas warms on expansion.

In practical liquefaction cycles (Linde, Claude), the gas is first compressed, then cooled in a heat exchanger by the returning cold stream, and finally throttled. The pre‑cooling must bring the gas temperature below its inversion temperature at the throttling pressure; otherwise the throttling step would heat the gas and defeat liquefaction. This requirement explains why hydrogen and helium, which have very low inversion temperatures (≈ 200 K and 40 K respectively), must be pre‑cooled with liquid nitrogen before they can be liquefied by simple throttling.

Examination questions often provide van der Waals constants a and b and ask for the inversion temperature at a given pressure, or ask to sketch the inversion curve qualitatively. A common error is to confuse the Joule‑Thomson coefficient with the Joule coefficient (∂T/∂V)U, which governs free expansion into vacuum. The two are related but distinct; the Joule coefficient is zero for an ideal gas as well, but its expression involves CV rather than CP.

Heat Transfer in Composite Systems and Critical Insulation Radius

Steady one‑dimensional conduction through a composite slab or cylinder is analysed by treating each layer as a thermal resistance in series. For a plane wall of thickness L, conductivity k, and cross‑sectional area A, the resistance is R = L/(kA). For a cylindrical shell of inner radius r₁, outer radius r₂, length ℓ, and conductivity k, the resistance is R = ln(r₂/r₁)/(2πkℓ). The overall heat‑transfer rate is Q = ΔT / ΣRi. When convection occurs at the surfaces, the convective resistance Rconv = 1/(hA) is added in series.

A subtle but important phenomenon appears for cylindrical (and spherical) insulation: adding insulation does not always reduce heat loss. The total resistance of a pipe with insulation is Rtotal = ln(rins/rpipe)/(2πkℓ) + 1/(h 2π rins ℓ). Differentiating with respect to the insulation outer radius rins and setting dR/drins = 0 yields the critical radius rcr = k/h. For rins < rcr, adding insulation actually increases heat loss because the increase in outer surface area (which enhances convection) outweighs the added conductive resistance. For rins > rcr, further insulation reduces heat loss as expected. For typical pipe insulation (k ≈ 0.05 W/m·K, h ≈ 10 W/m²·K) the critical radius is about 5 mm, which is smaller than most pipe radii, so insulation is beneficial. However, for electric wires (small radius, low h), the critical radius can be larger than the wire radius, and a thin coating of insulation may increase the wire temperature.

In spherical geometry the critical radius is rcr = 2k/h. The factor 2 arises from the different area‑radius relationship (A = 4πr²). This result is relevant for insulated storage tanks and cryogenic vessels.

Composite walls with contact resistance between layers introduce an additional interfacial resistance Rc = 1/(hcA), where hc is the contact conductance. In high‑precision applications (electronic packaging, turbine blades) this resistance can dominate the overall thermal budget. The temperature drop across the interface is ΔTc = Q Rc.

Radiative heat transfer between parallel plates or concentric cylinders adds a radiative resistance Rrad = 1/(hradA) where hrad = εσ(T₁²+T₂²)(T₁+T₂) for gray surfaces. When radiation is significant (high temperatures, vacuum), it acts in parallel with conduction/convection, and the combined resistance is obtained from 1/Rtotal = 1/Rcond + 1/Rrad.

Examiners frequently test the critical radius concept with a multiple‑choice question: “Adding insulation to a steam pipe always reduces heat loss.” The correct answer is false; the statement holds only when the pipe radius exceeds the critical radius. Another trap is to apply the plane‑wall resistance formula to a cylinder, forgetting the logarithmic term.

  • Confusing the Maxwell relations’ subscripts (constant S vs constant T) when substituting into derivative identities.
  • Using the Clausius‑Clapeyron integrated form for solid‑liquid transitions where Δv is not approximated by the vapour volume.
  • Equating first‑law efficiency with second‑law (exergetic) efficiency in turbine or compressor problems.
  • Assuming the Joule‑Thomson coefficient is zero for all gases at room temperature, ignoring the inversion curve.
  • Applying the plane‑wall conduction resistance formula to cylindrical or spherical geometries.
  • Neglecting the critical insulation radius and stating that any insulation thickness reduces heat loss.
  • Forgetting to convert latent heat to per‑mole or per‑kg basis consistent with the gas constant used in the Clapeyron equation.

These advanced topics deepen the thermodynamic toolkit and are essential for solving the higher‑difficulty problems that appear in competitive examinations.

Thermodynamic Stability Criteria and Spinodal Decomposition

Thermodynamic stability of a homogeneous phase requires that the second derivatives of the appropriate thermodynamic potential be positive definite. For a simple compressible system at constant temperature and pressure the Gibbs free energy G(T,P) must be a convex function of its natural variables; equivalently the isothermal compressibility κT = −(1/V)(∂V/∂P)T and the specific heat at constant pressure CP must both be positive. In the P–V plane this translates to (∂P/∂V)T < 0 for mechanical stability. When a fluid is quenched into the two‑phase region the homogeneous state becomes metastable; the limit of metastability is the spinodal curve defined by (∂P/∂V)T = 0. Inside the spinodal the system is absolutely unstable and any infinitesimal composition fluctuation grows spontaneously — a process known as spinodal decomposition. The Cahn–Hilliard equation describes the time evolution of the concentration field c(r,t) by adding a gradient‑energy term κ(∇c)² to the free‑energy density, leading to a characteristic wavelength λmax = 2π√(2κ/|f''(c₀)|) that maximizes the growth rate. Experimentally, spinodal decomposition is observed in binary alloys (e.g., Al‑Zn), polymer blends, and even in the early universe as a model for structure formation. In examinations, a typical question supplies a van der Waals equation of state and asks to locate the spinodal analytically by solving (∂P/∂V)T = 0, then to compare the spinodal with the binodal (coexistence) curve obtained from the Maxwell construction. A common mistake is to confuse the spinodal with the binodal; the former marks the limit of linear stability, the latter the equilibrium phase boundary.

Chemical Potential and Phase Equilibrium in Multicomponent Systems

For a mixture of k components the fundamental relation for the Gibbs free energy is dG = −S dT + V dP + Σi=1k μi dni, where μi = (∂G/∂ni)T,P,nj≠i is the chemical potential of species i. At equilibrium between two phases α and β at the same T and P, the chemical potential of each component must be equal: μiα = μiβ for all i. This set of k equations, together with the Gibbs‑Duhem relation Σ xi dμi = −Sm dT + Vm dP, reduces the number of independent intensive variables to k + 1 (the Gibbs phase rule: F = C − P + 2). In a binary (C = 2) liquid–vapour system the phase diagram at constant pressure shows a tie‑line connecting the liquid composition x1L and vapour composition y1V. The lever rule gives the relative amounts of the two phases. For non‑ideal solutions the chemical potential is written μi = μi° + RT ln ai, where ai = γi xi is the activity and γi the activity coefficient. Models such as Margules, van Laar, Wilson, NRTL, and UNIQUAC provide γi as functions of composition and temperature. In ternary systems (C = 3) the equilibrium data are represented on triangular diagrams; the binodal surface separates one‑phase from two‑phase regions, and tie‑triangles connect the compositions of three co‑existing phases. Examination problems often give excess‑Gibbs‑energy parameters for a binary Margules model and ask to compute the bubble‑point pressure and vapour composition at a specified temperature. The solution requires solving the coupled equations yi P = xi γi Pi°(T) for i = 1,2 together with Σ yi = 1. A frequent error is to treat γi as constant or to neglect the Poynting correction when the total pressure deviates significantly from the pure‑component vapour pressures.

Worked Example: Binary VLE with Two‑Suffix Margules Model
A binary mixture of components 1 and 2 follows the two‑suffix Margules equation GE/RT = A x1 x2 with A = 1.2 at 350 K. Pure‑component vapour pressures are P1° = 120 kPa and P2° = 80 kPa. For a liquid composition x1 = 0.4, calculate the bubble‑point pressure and the vapour composition y1.
For the two‑suffix Margules model ln γ1 = A x2² and ln γ2 = A x1². With x1 = 0.4, x2 = 0.6: ln γ1 = 1.2 × 0.36 = 0.432 → γ1 = 1.540; ln γ2 = 1.2 × 0.16 = 0.192 → γ2 = 1.212. Modified Raoult’s law: y1 P = x1 γ1 P1° = 0.4 × 1.540 × 120 = 73.92 kPa; y2 P = x2 γ2 P2° = 0.6 × 1.212 × 80 = 58.18 kPa. Summing gives P = 73.92 + 58.18 = 132.10 kPa. Then y1 = 73.92 / 132.10 = 0.560. Bubble‑point pressure = 132.1 kPa, vapour mole fraction of component 1 = 0.560.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a VLE problem supplies an excess‑Gibbs‑energy model, first write the expressions for ln γi analytically; then compute γi at the given liquid composition before applying Raoult’s law. Keep a table of xi, γi, Pi°, and the product xiγiPi° to avoid arithmetic slips. If the total pressure is not given, the bubble‑point pressure is simply the sum of the partial pressures. For dew‑point calculations, invert the relation: xi = yi P / (γi Pi°) and iterate on P until Σ xi = 1.

Non‑Equilibrium Thermodynamics: Onsager Reciprocal Relations and Entropy Production

Classical equilibrium thermodynamics deals with states; non‑equilibrium thermodynamics extends the framework to irreversible processes by introducing fluxes Jα and conjugate thermodynamic forces Xα. The local entropy production rate per unit volume is σ = Σ Jα Xα ≥ 0, a statement of the second law for continuous media. Near equilibrium the fluxes are linear functions of the forces: Jα = Σ Lαβ Xβ, where the phenomenological coefficients Lαβ form a symmetric matrix (Lαβ = Lβα) — the Onsager reciprocal relations, derived from microscopic reversibility. Classic examples include thermoelectric effects: the Seebeck coefficient (electric current driven by temperature gradient) and the Peltier coefficient (heat current driven by electric current) are linked by L12 = L21. In a binary mixture the diffusion flux J1 and heat flux Jq couple through the Soret (thermal diffusion) and Dufour (diffusion‑thermo) coefficients, again satisfying Onsager symmetry. The entropy production can be written in matrix form σ = Xᵀ L X; positive‑definiteness of L guarantees σ > 0 for any non‑zero force vector. Far from equilibrium the linear laws break down and higher‑order terms or nonlinear constitutive relations (e.g., the Boltzmann equation for rarefied gases) are required. Examination questions often present a set of coupled linear equations for heat and mass flow and ask to verify Onsager symmetry or to compute the entropy production for given forces. A typical trap is to assume Lαβ = Lβα holds for all transport coefficients without checking that the forces and fluxes are chosen as conjugate pairs derived from the entropy production expression.

Advanced Power Cycles: Stirling, Ericsson, and Combined Cycles

The Carnot cycle sets the upper efficiency limit ηC = 1 − TC/TH, but practical engines approximate it with different process sequences. The Stirling cycle consists of two isothermal processes (heat addition at TH, rejection at TC) and two constant‑volume regeneration processes. With an ideal regenerator of effectiveness ε = 1 the heat transferred during the constant‑volume legs is internally recycled, and the cycle efficiency equals ηC. Real regenerators have ε < 1, so extra heat must be supplied from the hot reservoir, reducing efficiency to η = 1 − (TC/TH) / [1 + (1−ε)(CV/R) ln(r)], where r is the compression ratio. The Ericsson cycle replaces the constant‑volume legs with constant‑pressure regeneration; its ideal efficiency also reaches ηC. Both cycles are external‑combustion, allowing use of low‑grade heat sources. In combined‑cycle power plants a gas‑turbine (Brayton) topping cycle exhausts at ~600 °C into a heat‑recovery steam generator that drives a bottoming Rankine cycle. The overall efficiency ηoverall = ηGT + ηST − ηGTηST can exceed 60 % with modern materials. Examiners frequently ask to derive the net work of a Stirling cycle with imperfect regeneration, or to compute the overall efficiency of a combined cycle given component efficiencies and pressure ratios. A common error is to treat the regenerator heat transfer as an external heat input, thereby double‑counting the energy balance.

Thermodynamic Properties of Real Gases: Virial Coefficients and Corresponding States Principle

Real‑gas behaviour is systematically captured by the virial expansion of the compressibility factor Z = PVm/RT = 1 + B(T)/Vm + C(T)/Vm² + …, where B(T) is the second virial coefficient, C(T) the third, etc. For a pairwise additive intermolecular potential u(r) the second virial coefficient is B(T) = −2πNA ∫₀^∞ [exp(−u(r)/kT) − 1] r² dr. At high temperatures B(T) > 0 (repulsive dominance); at low temperatures B(T) < 0 (attractive dominance). The Boyle temperature TB is defined by B(TB) = 0, where the gas behaves ideally over a wide pressure range. The principle of corresponding states asserts that all fluids have the same Z when expressed as a function of reduced temperature Tr = T/Tc and reduced pressure Pr = P/Pc. The two‑parameter theorem works well for simple spherical molecules; for polar or associating fluids a third parameter, the acentric factor ω = −log₁₀(Prsat)Tr=0.7 − 1, is introduced (Pitzer correlation). Generalized compressibility charts (Nelson‑Obert, Lee‑Kesler) provide Z(Tr, Pr, ω) for rapid engineering calculations. In examinations, a typical problem gives critical constants and ω for a substance and asks to estimate the molar volume at a specified T and P using the Lee‑Kesler tables, or to compute the residual enthalpy HR = RTc [ (Tr ∂Z/∂Tr)Pr ] from the chart. A frequent mistake is to apply the ideal‑gas heat capacity to evaluate enthalpy changes without adding the residual correction, leading to errors of several percent near the critical region.

  • Confusing the spinodal curve with the binodal (coexistence) curve when analysing phase stability.
  • Treating activity coefficients as constants instead of composition‑dependent functions in VLE calculations.
  • Assuming Onsager reciprocity holds for any arbitrary choice of fluxes and forces without verifying conjugacy.
  • Double‑counting regenerator heat transfer as external heat input in Stirling or Ericsson cycle analysis.
  • Using ideal‑gas enthalpy changes for real gases near the critical point without residual property corrections.
  • Applying the two‑parameter corresponding‑states correlation to highly polar fluids without the acentric factor.
  • Neglecting the Poynting correction when total pressure deviates significantly from pure‑component vapour pressures.

Refrigeration and Heat‑Pump Cycles: Vapor‑Compression, Absorption, and Coefficient of Performance

The vapor‑compression cycle is the workhorse of modern refrigeration and air‑conditioning. Its four steady‑flow processes are: (1) isentropic compression of low‑pressure vapor in the compressor, (2) constant‑pressure heat rejection in the condenser, (3) isenthalpic expansion through a throttling valve, and (4) constant‑pressure heat absorption in the evaporator. The coefficient of performance for a refrigerator is COPR = QL / Wnet, while for a heat pump COPHP = QH / Wnet = COPR + 1. In an ideal cycle with a reversible compressor and no pressure drops, COPR,rev = TL / (TH − TL) where temperatures are absolute. Real cycles suffer from compressor inefficiency (isentropic efficiency ηc), sub‑cooling, super‑heating, and pressure losses, all of which reduce COP. The refrigeration capacity is ṁ (h1 − h4) where ṁ is the mass flow rate of refrigerant.

Absorption cycles replace the mechanical compressor with a thermal compressor consisting of an absorber, a generator, a pump, and a throttling valve. A common working pair is water‑ammonia (NH3‑H2O) or lithium‑bromide‑water (LiBr‑H2O). The generator uses heat at temperature TG to desorb refrigerant from the absorbent; the absorber rejects heat at TA. The COP of an ideal absorption refrigerator is COPabs,rev = (TL / (TH − TL)) × ((TG − TA) / TG). Because the pump work is negligible, absorption systems are attractive when low‑grade waste heat is available. However, they are bulkier and have lower COP than vapor‑compression units for the same temperature lift.

Examiners often ask to compare the two cycles on a T‑s diagram, to compute the mass flow rate required for a given cooling load, or to evaluate the effect of sub‑cooling and super‑heating on COP. A typical trap is to use the compressor inlet enthalpy after super‑heating as the evaporator outlet enthalpy, ignoring the fact that the evaporator only provides saturated vapor at the evaporator pressure. Another common error is to treat the throttling process as isentropic; it is isenthalpic, and the entropy increases.

Exergy Analysis of Chemical Reactions and Combustion Processes

Exergy (availability) quantifies the maximum useful work obtainable when a system comes to equilibrium with a defined reference environment (usually T0 = 298 K, P0 = 1 atm, and specified composition). For a reacting mixture the total exergy is the sum of physical exergy (due to T, P differences) and chemical exergy (due to composition difference from the environment). The standard chemical exergy of a species i, ech,i0, is the maximum work from bringing one mole of i from the environmental state to the dead state in a reversible process. For elements in their reference form (O2, N2, C (graphite), H2) the standard chemical exergy is zero; for compounds it is positive and tabulated (e.g., ech0(CH4) ≈ 831 kJ mol⁻¹).

In a steady‑flow combustion chamber the exergy balance reads: Σ ṁin ψin = Σ ṁout ψout + Ẇrev + İ, where ψ = (h − h0) − T0(s − s0) + ech is the specific flow exergy, Ẇrev is the reversible work (zero for adiabatic combustion), and İ = T0 Ṡgen is the exergy destruction. The second‑law efficiency is ηII = (Exergy of products − Exergy of reactants) / (Exergy of fuel). For methane combustion with air at stoichiometric conditions, ηII is typically 0.30–0.35, indicating that 65–70 % of the fuel exergy is destroyed as irreversibility, mainly due to the large temperature gradient in the flame.

When solving exam problems, first compute the standard chemical exergy of each reactant and product using tabulated values or the approximation ech ≈ ΔGf0 + Σ νj ech,j0 (elements). Then evaluate physical exergy at the actual T, P using ψph = (h − h0) − T0(s − s0). The exergy destruction follows from the Gouy‑Stodola relation İ = T0 ΔSgen. A frequent mistake is to neglect the chemical exergy of the products (especially CO2 and H2O) and to assume that the exergy of the exhaust equals its physical exergy only.

Exergy Destruction in Methane Combustion
Methane (CH₄) at 298 K, 1 atm burns with 120 % theoretical air at 298 K, 1 atm in an adiabatic steady‑flow combustor. The products leave at 2200 K, 1 atm. Using standard chemical exergies: e_ch⁰(CH₄)=831 kJ mol⁻¹, e_ch⁰(CO₂)=19.9 kJ mol⁻¹, e_ch⁰(H₂O(g))=9.5 kJ mol⁻¹, e_ch⁰(O₂)=e_ch⁰(N₂)=0. Assume constant specific heats: c_p,CO₂=50 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, c_p,H₂O=38 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, c_p,N₂=29 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, c_p,O₂=30 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Compute the exergy destruction per kmol of CH₄.
1. Write stoichiometric reaction: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. With 120 % air, O₂ supplied = 2.4 kmol, N₂ = 2.4×3.76 = 9.024 kmol. Products: 1 CO₂, 2 H₂O, 0.4 O₂, 9.024 N₂. 2. Chemical exergy of reactants: ψ_ch,react = 831 kJ (CH₄) + 0 (O₂,N₂) = 831 MJ per kmol CH₄. 3. Chemical exergy of products: ψ_ch,prod = 1×19.9 + 2×9.5 + 0.4×0 + 9.024×0 = 38.9 MJ. 4. Physical exergy of products at 2200 K: ψ_ph = Σ n_i [c_p,i (T−T₀) − T₀ c_p,i ln(T/T₀)]. For each species compute Δh = c_p (2200−298) and Δs = c_p ln(2200/298). Using T₀=298 K: CO₂: Δh=50×1902=95.1 MJ, Δs=50×ln(7.38)=50×1.999=99.9 kJ/K → ψ_ph=95.1−298×0.0999=95.1−29.8=65.3 MJ. H₂O: Δh=38×1902=72.3 MJ, Δs=38×1.999=75.9 kJ/K → ψ_ph=72.3−298×0.0759=72.3−22.6=49.7 MJ each, total 99.4 MJ. O₂: Δh=30×1902=57.1 MJ, Δs=30×1.999=60.0 kJ/K → ψ_ph=57.1−298×0.0600=57.1−17.9=39.2 MJ ×0.4 =15.7 MJ. N₂: Δh=29×1902=55.2 MJ, Δs=29×1.999=58.0 kJ/K → ψ_ph=55.2−298×0.0580=55.2−17.3=37.9 MJ ×9.024 =342.0 MJ. Sum ψ_ph,prod = 65.3+99.4+15.7+342.0 = 522.4 MJ. 5. Total product exergy = ψ_ch,prod + ψ_ph,prod = 38.9 + 522.4 = 561.3 MJ. 6. Exergy destruction İ = ψ_react − ψ_prod = 831 − 561.3 = 269.7 MJ per kmol CH₄. 7. Second‑law efficiency η_II = ψ_prod / ψ_react = 561.3 / 831 = 0.675 (67.5 %). The result shows that even with excess air, a large fraction of fuel exergy is destroyed in the high‑temperature combustion process.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When an exergy problem appears, first list all species and their standard chemical exergies. Separate chemical and physical contributions; compute physical exergy using the ψ_ph = Δh − T₀Δs formula with constant‑c_p approximations if tables are not given. Remember that the reference environment fixes T₀ and P₀; do not use the combustor inlet temperature as T₀. Check units: chemical exergy is usually in kJ mol⁻¹ or MJ kmol⁻¹, while physical exergy from c_pΔT yields kJ kmol⁻¹. A quick sanity check: the exergy destruction must be positive and the second‑law efficiency must lie between 0 and 1.

Fugacity, Activity Coefficients, and Real Solution Models

Fugacity fi extends the concept of pressure to real fluids so that the chemical potential retains the ideal‑gas form μi = μi⁰ + RT ln(fi/fi⁰). For a pure component the fugacity coefficient φ = f/P is obtained from the residual Gibbs energy: ln φ = (GR/RT) = ∫₀ᴾ (Z−1) dP/P. In mixtures the fugacity of component i is fi = yi φi P (vapor) or fi = xi γi fi⁰ (liquid), where γi is the activity coefficient and fi⁰ is the standard‑state fugacity (usually pure liquid at system T, P). The activity coefficient captures non‑ideality of the liquid phase; γi → 1 as xi → 1 (Raoult’s law) and γi → γi^∞ as xi → 0 (Henry’s law).

Several empirical and semi‑theoretical models provide γi as a function of composition and temperature. The two‑parameter Margules equations: ln γ₁ = x₂² [A₁₂ + 2 (A₂₁ − A₁₂) x₁], ln γ₂ = x₁² [A₂₁ + 2 (A₁₂ − A₂₁) x₂]. The Van Laar model: ln γ₁ = A₁₂ / (1 + (A₁₂ x₁)/(A₂₁ x₂))², ln γ₂ = A₂₁ / (1 + (A₂₁ x₂)/(A₁₂ x₁))². More sophisticated local‑composition models include Wilson (requires only binary interaction parameters Λij), NRTL (non‑random two‑liquid, adds non‑randomness parameter α), and UNIQUAC (combines combinatorial and residual terms using surface area and volume parameters). These models are fitted to VLE data and then used to predict bubble‑point, dew‑point, and azeotropic behavior.

In examinations, a typical question supplies binary VLE data at a given temperature and asks to determine the Margules parameters by linear regression of ln(γ₁/γ₂) versus composition, or to calculate the azeotropic composition and pressure using the Wilson equation. A common pitfall is to confuse the symmetric convention (γᵢ → 1 as xᵢ → 1) with the asymmetric convention (γᵢ → 1 as xᵢ → 0) used for Henry’s law standard state. Another trap is to apply the ideal‑gas fugacity coefficient φ = 1 for the vapor phase at high pressures without checking the compressibility factor Z; the correct approach is to compute φ from an equation of state (e.g., Peng‑Robinson) or from generalized charts.

Finite‑Time Thermodynamics and Endoreversible Engine Models

Classical Carnot efficiency ηC = 1 − TC/TH assumes reversible heat transfer, which requires infinite time and zero power output. Finite‑time thermodynamics (FTT) introduces realistic heat‑transfer laws between the working fluid and the reservoirs. The simplest endoreversible model (Curzon‑Ahlborn) assumes Newtonian heat transfer: Q̇H = αH (TH − TH,wf), Q̇C = αC (TC,wf − TC), where TH,wf and TC,wf are the working‑fluid temperatures during the hot and cold isotherms. The engine is internally reversible, so the entropy balance gives Q̇H/TH,wf = Q̇C/TC,wf. Power output is Ẇ = Q̇H − Q̇C. Maximizing Ẇ with respect to TH,wf (or the temperature ratio) yields the Curzon‑Ahlborn efficiency ηCA = 1 − √(TC/TH). This is lower than ηC but corresponds to maximum power, not maximum efficiency.

Extensions include different heat‑transfer laws (radiative Q̇ ∝ T⁴, convective Q̇ ∝ ΔTⁿ), finite‑capacitance reservoirs, and internal irreversibilities parameterized by an endoreversibility factor φ = (ΔSgen,int)/(ΔSgen,total). The efficiency at maximum power for a generalized heat‑transfer law Q̇ ∝ (ΔT)ⁿ becomes ηmp = 1 − (TC/TH)n/(n+1). For n = 1 (Newtonian) this reduces to ηCA. FTT also provides design guidelines: the optimal allocation of heat‑exchanger area between hot and cold sides follows αH/αC = √(TH/TC) for maximum power.

Exam problems often give the hot‑ and cold‑side conductances αH, αC and reservoir temperatures, then ask for the working‑fluid temperatures at maximum power, the corresponding power output, and the efficiency. A frequent error is to set the working‑fluid temperatures equal to the reservoir temperatures, which would give Carnot efficiency but zero power. Another mistake is to maximize efficiency instead of power, leading to the trivial solution TH,wf → TH, TC,wf → TC and zero power. Always verify whether the question asks for maximum power, maximum efficiency, or a specified power output.

Thermoelectric Effects: Seebeck, Peltier, and Thomson Relations

Thermoelectric phenomena couple heat and charge flows in conductors and semiconductors. The Seebeck effect: an open‑circuit voltage ΔV = S ΔT appears across a material with Seebeck coefficient S (V K⁻¹) when a temperature difference ΔT is applied. The Peltier effect: when a current I passes through a junction of two materials, heat is absorbed or released at a rate Q̇ = Π I, where Π = S T is the Peltier coefficient (V). The Thomson effect: in a homogeneous conductor with a temperature gradient and current, heat is generated or absorbed per unit volume at a rate q̇ = τ J ∇T, where τ = T dS/dT is the Thomson coefficient (V K⁻¹). The three coefficients satisfy the Kelvin relations: Π = S T and τ = T dS/dT, which follow from Onsager reciprocity and the second law.

The dimensionless figure of merit ZT = S² σ T / κ quantifies thermoelectric performance, where σ is electrical conductivity and κ is thermal conductivity (electronic + lattice). High ZT requires large S, high σ, and low κ — a challenging combination because σ and κ are linked via the Wiedemann‑Franz law for metals. Modern thermoelectric materials (Bi₂Te₃, PbTe, skutterudites, half‑Heuslers) achieve ZT ≈ 1–2 near room temperature and up to 2.5 at elevated temperatures by engineering nanostructures to scatter phonons more than electrons.

In a thermoelectric generator the efficiency is η = (ΔT/TH) (√(1+ZT̄) − 1) / (√(1+ZT̄) + TC/TH), where ZT̄ is the average figure of merit over the temperature range. For a cooler (Peltier refrigerator) the maximum temperature difference is ΔTmax = ½ ZTC². Exam questions may provide S, σ, κ data at two temperatures and ask to estimate ZT̄, the generator efficiency, or the cooling capacity of a Peltier module with given geometry. A typical trap is to use the electrical resistivity ρ = 1/σ but forget to convert units (Ω m vs Ω cm) or to neglect the temperature dependence of S when integrating for ZT̄. Another error is to treat the Thomson heat as a simple Joule heating term; it is reversible and changes sign with current direction.

  • Confusing the symmetric and asymmetric activity‑coefficient conventions when applying Henry’s law versus Raoult’s law.
  • Assuming the vapor‑phase fugacity coefficient is unity at high pressure without evaluating the compressibility factor.
  • Maximizing efficiency instead of power in finite‑time thermodynamics problems, yielding zero power output.
  • Neglecting the temperature dependence of the Seebeck coefficient when calculating the average figure of merit ZT̄.
  • Treating the Thomson heat as irreversible Joule heating rather than a reversible thermoelectric term.
  • Using the ideal‑gas chemical exergy for combustion products instead of the correct standard chemical exergy values.

Thermodynamic Geometry and Information Metric

Thermodynamic geometry provides a differential‑geometric framework in which the equilibrium state space of a thermodynamic system is endowed with a Riemannian metric derived from the second derivatives of a thermodynamic potential. The most common choice is the Ruppeiner metric, defined as the negative Hessian of the entropy with respect to the extensive variables: gij = –∂²S/∂Xi∂Xj. For a simple fluid with internal energy U, volume V and particle number N, the line element reads ds² = –(∂²S/∂U²)dU² – 2(∂²S/∂U∂V)dU dV – (∂²S/∂V²)dV². The scalar curvature R of this metric diverges at critical points and its sign distinguishes between attractive (R < 0) and repulsive (R > 0) dominant interactions. In the ideal‑gas limit the curvature vanishes, reflecting the absence of correlations. For a van der Waals fluid the curvature can be expressed analytically in terms of reduced temperature and density, providing a quantitative measure of the correlation volume. The geometric approach also extends to black‑hole thermodynamics, where the Ruppeiner curvature of the Kerr–Newman solution encodes information about the underlying microscopic degrees of freedom. In practical examinations, candidates may be asked to compute the metric components for a given fundamental equation, evaluate the curvature scalar, and interpret its physical meaning. A typical trap is to confuse the Ruppeiner metric (entropy representation) with the Weinhold metric (energy representation), which differs by a conformal factor 1/T and leads to a different curvature.

Fluctuation Theorems: Jarzynski Equality and Crooks Relation

Fluctuation theorems quantify the probability of observing entropy‑decreasing trajectories in small systems driven far from equilibrium. The Jarzynski equality states that for a system initially in canonical equilibrium at temperature T, the exponential average of the work W performed during an arbitrary protocol λ(t) satisfies ⟨e–W/kBT⟩ = e–ΔF/kBT, where ΔF is the free‑energy difference between the initial and final equilibrium states. This relation holds for any protocol speed, providing a route to equilibrium free energies from non‑equilibrium work measurements. The Crooks fluctuation theorem refines this by giving the ratio of forward and reverse work distributions: PF(W)/PR(–W) = e(W–ΔF)/kBT. Both theorems rely on microscopic reversibility and the assumption that the initial state is thermal. In experimental practice, one measures work histograms for pulling a molecular bond or stretching a polymer, then uses the Crooks crossing point (where PF(W)=PR(–W)) to extract ΔF without exponential averaging. A common examination question supplies a set of forward and reverse work values and asks for the free‑energy estimate using the Bennett acceptance ratio, which is statistically optimal. Candidates must remember that the theorems apply only when the dynamics obey detailed balance; any external driving that breaks time‑reversal symmetry (e.g., magnetic fields with non‑conservative forces) invalidates the simple form.

Worked Example: Free Energy from Crooks Crossing
A single‑molecule pulling experiment yields the following forward work values (in kBT units): 12, 15, 18, 20, 22. The corresponding reverse work values (negative of work done on the system during the reverse protocol) are: –8, –10, –12, –14, –16. Estimate ΔF/kBT using the Crooks crossing method.
Plot the forward histogram PF(W) and the reverse histogram PR(–W) on the same axis. The crossing occurs where the two histograms intersect. With the given discrete data, the forward values cluster around 17 kBT and the reverse values around –12 kBT. The crossing point is approximately at W* ≈ 2.5 kBT (midway between the two clusters). By Crooks theorem, at the crossing W* = ΔF. Hence ΔF ≈ 2.5 kBT. A more precise estimate using the Bennett acceptance ratio yields ΔF = 2.48 kBT, confirming the crossing approximation.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for ΔF from non‑equilibrium work data, first check whether the forward and reverse histograms are provided. If only forward work values are given, use the Jarzynski equality with exponential averaging, but be aware of the large‑variance bias. If both forward and reverse data are supplied, the Crooks crossing or Bennett acceptance ratio gives a far more reliable estimate. Always state the underlying assumptions (initial canonical equilibrium, microscopic reversibility) to secure full credit.

Quantum Thermodynamics: Landauer Principle and Maxwell's Demon

Quantum thermodynamics extends classical concepts to regimes where coherence, entanglement, and measurement back‑action become significant. The Landauer principle asserts that erasing one bit of information in a memory register at temperature T requires a minimum heat dissipation of Qmin = kBT ln 2. This bound follows from the requirement that the total entropy of system plus environment does not decrease. In a quantum setting, the erasure protocol can be modelled as a completely positive trace‑preserving map that takes an arbitrary input state to a fixed pure state; the entropy increase of the environment is then bounded by the von Neumann entropy change of the system. Maxwell's demon, a thought experiment in which a feedback controller sorts fast and slow molecules to extract work from a single heat bath, is resolved by recognizing that the demon's memory must be reset, incurring a Landauer cost that exactly compensates the extracted work. Modern experiments with trapped ions and superconducting qubits have verified the Landauer bound and demonstrated that quantum coherence can transiently reduce the average dissipation below kBT ln 2, but the second law holds when the full measurement‑feedback cycle is accounted for. Examination questions often present a quantum circuit implementing a feedback protocol and ask for the minimum work cost given the initial state's von Neumann entropy. A frequent error is to ignore the entropy of the measurement apparatus or to treat the demon's information as free.

Entropy Generation Minimization and Thermodynamic Optimization

Entropy generation minimization (EGM) is a design philosophy that seeks to reduce irreversibilities in thermal systems by distributing thermodynamic forces (temperature gradients, pressure drops, concentration gradients) as uniformly as possible. For a heat exchanger operating between two streams, the local entropy generation rate per unit length is σ = (ΔT)²/(ThTc) U P + (Δp)²/(ρ T) f (Re), where the first term represents thermal irreversibility and the second viscous dissipation. The optimal allocation of heat‑transfer area and pressure‑drop budget follows from the equipartition of entropy generation: the thermal and fluid‑friction contributions should be of the same order of magnitude. In a finite‑time Carnot‑like engine, the maximum power condition derived from EGM reproduces the Curzon–Ahlborn efficiency ηCA = 1 – √(Tc/Th). For more complex cycles (e.g., regenerative Brayton, combined cycles) the method yields a set of coupled algebraic equations for the optimal pressure ratios and temperature ratios. Examiners may provide a simplified model of a gas‑turbine cycle with given component efficiencies and ask for the pressure ratio that minimizes total entropy generation per unit net work. The solution proceeds by writing the total entropy generation as a function of the pressure ratio, differentiating, and solving the resulting polynomial. A common pitfall is to neglect the entropy generation in the regenerator or to treat component efficiencies as constant when they actually vary with operating point.

Thermodynamic Modeling of Fuel Cells and Electrochemical Energy Conversion

Fuel cells convert chemical energy directly into electrical work through controlled electrochemical reactions. The maximum reversible voltage (open‑circuit voltage) of a hydrogen‑oxygen fuel cell at temperature T and pressure p is given by the Nernst equation: E = E⁰ – (RT/2F) ln(pH₂O/(pH₂ pO₂¹ᐟ²)), where E⁰ = –ΔG⁰/(2F) is the standard potential derived from the Gibbs free energy of formation of water. The actual cell voltage under load is reduced by three main overpotentials: activation (ηact), ohmic (ηohm), and concentration (ηconc). The activation overpotential follows the Butler–Volmer relation, which in the high‑overpotential limit reduces to the Tafel equation ηact = (RT/αF) ln(i/i₀). Ohmic losses are linear in current density: ηohm = i Rint. Concentration overpotential arises from reactant depletion at the electrode surface and can be expressed as ηconc = (RT/nF) ln(1 – i/iL), where iL is the limiting current density. The overall efficiency is η = (i Ecell)/(ΔH · i/2F) = (Ecell/Eth) where Eth = ΔH/(2F) is the thermoneutral voltage. In solid‑oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) operating at 800–1000 °C, the Nernst potential is higher and the activation losses are lower, but material degradation becomes the dominant design constraint. Examination problems often give polarization data (voltage vs. current density) and ask to extract the exchange current density i₀, the internal resistance Rint, and the limiting current iL by fitting the three‑overpotential model. A typical mistake is to treat the open‑circuit voltage as the thermoneutral voltage, ignoring the entropy change of the reaction.

  • Confusing the Ruppeiner (entropy) metric with the Weinhold (energy) metric and using the wrong curvature formula.
  • Applying the Jarzynski equality without verifying that the initial ensemble is canonical and the protocol is time‑reversible.
  • Neglecting the Landauer cost of memory erasure when analyzing Maxwell's demon, leading to an apparent violation of the second law.
  • Assuming component efficiencies are constant in entropy‑generation minimization, whereas they usually depend on operating temperature and pressure ratio.
  • Using the thermoneutral voltage instead of the reversible Nernst voltage as the open‑circuit reference in fuel‑cell efficiency calculations.
  • Forgetting to convert units consistently (e.g., Ω cm vs Ω m) when evaluating the figure of merit ZT or the internal resistance of a fuel cell.
  • Treating the Thomson heat as irreversible Joule heating in thermoelectric problems, whereas it is a reversible contribution that changes sign with current direction.
