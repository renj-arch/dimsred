What is Modern Physics?

Modern physics covers the revolutionary developments in physics from the early 20th century, including quantum mechanics, relativity, atomic and nuclear physics. For NEET, key topics include photoelectric effect, Bohr's atomic model, radioactivity, nuclear fission and fusion, and dual nature of matter. These concepts explain phenomena at atomic and subatomic scales.

Key Concepts

Photoelectric Effect: Einstein's equation: hf = φ + KEmax, where φ is work function. Threshold frequency f0 = φ/h. Bohr's Model: Angular momentum mvr = nh/2ππ, Energy En = -13.6/n2 eV, Radius rn = 0.529 n2/Z Å. de Broglie Wavelength: λ = h/p = h/(mv). Radioactive Decay: N = N0e-λt, half-life T1/2 = 0.693/λ. Mass-Energy Equivalence: E = mc2. Nuclear Binding Energy: Δm × c2.

Solved Examples

Example 1
The work function of a metal is 2.3 eV. Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on it. Find the maximum kinetic energy of emitted photoelectrons. (h = 6.63 × 10-34 Js, c = 3 × 108 m/s, 1 eV = 1.6 × 10-19 J)

a) 0.8 eV b) 1.1 eV c) 1.5 eV d) 2.3 eV
Solution: Energy of photon E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10-34 × 3 × 108)/(400 × 10-9) = 4.97 × 10-19 J = 4.97 × 10-19/1.6 × 10-19 = 3.1 eV. KEmax = E - φ = 3.1 - 2.3 = 0.8 eV. Hence option (a) is correct.
Example 2
In a hydrogen atom, find the energy of the electron in the second excited state (n = 3).

a) -3.4 eV b) -1.51 eV c) -0.85 eV d) -13.6 eV
Solution: En = -13.6/n2 eV. For n = 3, E3 = -13.6/9 = -1.51 eV. Hence option (b) is correct.
Example 3
A radioactive sample has a half-life of 5 days. How much fraction of the sample remains after 15 days?

a) 1/2 b) 1/4 c) 1/8 d) 1/16
Solution: Number of half-lives n = t/T1/2 = 15/5 = 3. Fraction remaining = (1/2)n = (1/2)3 = 1/8. Hence option (c) is correct.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For photoelectric effect, remember that intensity affects number of photoelectrons (current), not their kinetic energy.
  • For atomic spectra, the number of spectral lines when an electron drops from n to 1 is n(n-1)/2.
  • In radioactive decay, after n half-lives, remaining fraction = (1/2)n and decayed fraction = 1 - (1/2)n.
  • For binding energy calculations, mass defect Δm is converted to energy using 1 amu = 931 MeV.

For NEET, memorize the key constants: h = 6.63 × 10-34 Js, 1 eV = 1.6 × 10-19 J, 1 amu = 931 MeV/c2. Also remember that the ground state energy of hydrogen is -13.6 eV.

Photoelectric Effect: Stopping Potential and Work Function Variations

Dependence of Stopping Potential on Frequency

The stopping potential V₀ is the minimum reverse voltage that reduces the photocurrent to zero. Einstein’s photoelectric equation gives eV₀ = hf − φ, where φ is the work function of the cathode material. A plot of V₀ versus frequency f is a straight line with slope h/e, universal for all metals, and intercept −φ/e, which shifts with the material. This linear relationship is the experimental basis for determining Planck’s constant and comparing work functions.

Effect of Intensity on Photocurrent

Increasing light intensity raises the number of incident photons per unit time, increasing the saturation photocurrent proportionally. However, the kinetic energy of emitted electrons, and hence the stopping potential, remains unchanged because each photon still transfers energy hf. This distinction between intensity (current) and frequency (energy) is the most frequently tested conceptual point.

Work Function Variations and Surface Conditions

The work function φ depends on crystal face, surface contamination, and adsorbed layers. Clean single‑crystal tungsten surfaces show φ ≈ 4.5 eV, while oxidised or contaminated surfaces can exhibit values differing by 0.5–1 eV. In practice, the measured stopping potential for a given frequency may differ by a few tenths of a volt if the cathode is not freshly prepared. Examiners often ask why two identical metals give different stopping potentials; the answer lies in surface preparation and the resulting work‑function shift.

Temperature Dependence

At room temperature thermal energy kT ≈ 0.025 eV is negligible compared with typical work functions (2–5 eV). At elevated temperatures thermionic emission adds a background current that can mask the true photoelectric current. The Richardson‑Dushman equation describes this thermionic component, and careful subtraction is required for accurate stopping‑potential measurements at high temperature.

Applications in Photodetectors

Photomultiplier tubes and vacuum photodiodes exploit the photoelectric effect. The choice of cathode material (e.g., Cs₃Sb, GaAs) is driven by a low work function and high quantum efficiency in the desired spectral region. The stopping‑potential characteristic determines the dynamic range and linearity of the detector.

Bohr Model Extensions: Fine Structure, Lamb Shift, and Hyperfine Splitting

Fine Structure from Relativistic Corrections

The Bohr model predicts energy levels En = −13.6 eV / n². Relativistic corrections to the electron’s kinetic energy and the spin‑orbit interaction split each level into sub‑levels characterised by the total angular momentum quantum number j. The fine‑structure splitting for hydrogen is of order α² Rydberg ≈ 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ eV, where α ≈ 1/137 is the fine‑structure constant. This produces closely spaced components in the Balmer‑α line (Hα) near 656.28 nm.

Lamb Shift: Quantum Electrodynamics Effect

The Lamb shift is a small additional splitting between the 2S₁/₂ and 2P₁/₂ levels, degenerate in the Dirac theory. Measured by Lamb and Retherford in 1947, the shift is about 1057 MHz (≈ 4.4 × 10⁻⁶ eV). It arises from the interaction of the bound electron with the quantised electromagnetic field (vacuum fluctuations). The Lamb shift provided the first experimental confirmation of quantum electrodynamics (QED) and remains a benchmark for high‑precision tests of the theory.

Hyperfine Splitting and the 21 cm Line

Interaction between the magnetic moments of the electron and the proton splits the ground state (1S₁/₂) into two hyperfine levels with total spin F = 0 and F = 1. The transition between them emits a photon of wavelength 21.1 cm (frequency 1420 MHz). This line is a cornerstone of radio astronomy, allowing mapping of neutral hydrogen in the galaxy. The hyperfine splitting energy is ≈ 5.9 × 10⁻⁶ eV, much smaller than fine structure.

Implications for Spectroscopy

High‑resolution spectroscopy of hydrogen reveals the hierarchy: gross structure (Bohr), fine structure (relativistic + spin‑orbit), Lamb shift (QED), hyperfine splitting (nuclear spin). Each successive correction is smaller by roughly a factor of α. Modern frequency‑comb techniques measure the 1S–2S transition with fractional uncertainty below 10⁻¹⁵, providing stringent tests of fundamental constants.

Worked Example: Stopping Potential Calculation
A clean sodium surface (work function φ = 2.28 eV) is illuminated by monochromatic light of wavelength 400 nm. Calculate the stopping potential V₀.
Photon energy E = hc/λ = (1240 eV·nm) / 400 nm = 3.10 eV. Using Einstein’s equation: eV₀ = E − φ = 3.10 eV − 2.28 eV = 0.82 eV. Therefore V₀ = 0.82 V. The stopping potential is independent of light intensity; only the saturation current would change with intensity.
Exam Day Strategy
When a question gives wavelength and work function, first convert wavelength to photon energy using 1240 eV·nm / λ(nm). Then subtract the work function to obtain the maximum kinetic energy in eV; the numerical value in eV equals the stopping potential in volts. Remember that the slope of the V₀ versus f graph is always h/e, so any change in the intercept directly reflects a change in work function.

Nuclear Shell Model, Magic Numbers, and Stability Trends

Shell Structure Analogous to Atomic Orbitals

The nuclear shell model treats nucleons as moving independently in a mean potential well created by all other nucleons. The potential is often approximated by a three‑dimensional harmonic oscillator plus a strong spin‑orbit term. Energy levels are labelled by principal quantum number n, orbital angular momentum l, and total angular momentum j = l ± ½. The spin‑orbit splitting is much larger in nuclei than in atoms, producing the observed sequence of magic numbers.

Magic Numbers and Extra Stability

Nuclei with proton number Z or neutron number N equal to 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, or 126 exhibit unusually high binding energy per nucleon, high first excitation energies, and large numbers of stable isotopes or isotones. These “magic numbers” arise from closed shells in the nuclear potential. Doubly magic nuclei such as ⁴He (Z=2, N=2), ¹⁶O (8,8), ⁴⁰Ca (20,20), and ²⁰⁸Pb (82,126) are exceptionally spherical and stable.

Deviations from the Simple Shell Model

For nuclei far from closed shells, collective effects such as deformation and pairing correlations become important. The Nilsson model extends the shell model by adding a deformed potential, explaining rotational bands in rare‑earth and actinide nuclei. Pairing leads to an even‑odd staggering in binding energies: even‑even nuclei are more bound than their odd‑A neighbours.

Experimental Signatures

Magic numbers are identified experimentally by (i) low neutron‑capture cross sections, (ii) high first excited 2⁺ energies, (iii) large two‑neutron separation energies, and (iv) abundance peaks in the solar system r‑process path. The persistence of the N = 126 shell closure in the neutron‑rich region around ²⁰⁸Pb confirms the robustness of the shell model far from stability.

Impact on Nuclear Reactions

In nuclear fission, fragments tend to form near magic numbers (e.g., Z ≈ 50, N ≈ 82) because these configurations are energetically favoured. In fusion, the path to heavier elements proceeds through waiting points at magic neutron numbers, influencing the astrophysical r‑process. Understanding shell closures is therefore essential for both energy applications and nucleosynthesis modelling.

Semiconductor Carrier Statistics: Fermi Level, Intrinsic Concentration, and Temperature Dependence

Intrinsic Carrier Concentration

For an intrinsic semiconductor the electron concentration n and hole concentration p are equal: n = p = nᵢ. The intrinsic concentration is given by nᵢ² = Nc Nv exp(−Eg / kT), where Nc and Nv are the effective density of states in the conduction and valence bands, Eg is the band gap, k is Boltzmann’s constant, and T is absolute temperature. For silicon at 300 K, Eg ≈ 1.12 eV, Nc ≈ 2.8 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, Nv ≈ 1.04 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, yielding nᵢ ≈ 1.0 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³.

Fermi Level Position in Intrinsic Material

The intrinsic Fermi level Ei lies near the middle of the band gap. Its exact position is Ei = (Ec + Ev)/2 + (kT/2) ln(Nv/Nc). Because Nc > Nv for Si, the logarithmic term is negative; at 300 K the offset is about −0.01 eV (closer to the valence band). This small shift is often neglected in introductory problems but becomes relevant in precision device modelling.

Extrinsic Doping and Fermi Level Shift

Donor doping (n‑type) raises the Fermi level toward the conduction band; acceptor doping (p‑type) lowers it toward the valence band. In the non‑degenerate limit, n = Nc exp[−(Ec − Ef)/kT] and p = Nv exp[−(Ef − Ev)/kT]. The product np = nᵢ² remains constant at a given temperature, a direct consequence of the law of mass action. This relation allows quick calculation of minority‑carrier concentration once the majority concentration is known.

Temperature Dependence of Carrier Concentrations

At low temperatures, carriers freeze out and the Fermi level moves toward the donor or acceptor level. In the extrinsic saturation region (room temperature for typical dopings), n ≈ Nd (donor concentration) for n‑type, and p ≈ Na for p‑type. At high temperatures, intrinsic carriers dominate and n ≈ p ≈ nᵢ, causing the Fermi level to return toward mid‑gap. The transition temperatures depend on doping density and band gap.

Degenerate Semiconductors and Band‑Gap Narrowing

When doping exceeds ~10¹⁹ cm⁻³, the Fermi level enters the conduction or valence band, and the semiconductor becomes degenerate. The simple Boltzmann approximation fails; Fermi‑Dirac statistics must be used. Heavy doping also reduces the effective band gap (band‑gap narrowing) by up to 0.1 eV in silicon, affecting device characteristics such as the forward voltage of a p‑n junction.

Logic Gate Synthesis Using Only NAND or NOR Gates

Universality of NAND and NOR

A NAND gate implements the function Y = ¬(A·B). By connecting its inputs together, a NAND becomes a NOT gate: Y = ¬(A·A) = ¬A. Two NAND gates in series produce an AND gate: first NAND gives ¬(A·B), second NAND (with both inputs tied to that output) gives ¬¬(A·B) = A·B. An OR gate is obtained by inverting both inputs before a NAND: Y = ¬(¬A·¬B) = A + B (De Morgan’s theorem). Thus any Boolean function can be built from NAND alone.

NOR‑Only Implementation

The NOR gate Y = ¬(A + B) is equally universal. A single NOR with tied inputs acts as NOT. Two NORs give an OR gate. An AND gate is realised by inverting inputs before a NOR: Y = ¬(¬A + ¬B) = A·B. The symmetry between NAND and NOR follows directly from De Morgan’s laws.

Minimal Gate Count for Common Functions

An XOR gate (Y = A⊕B) requires four NAND gates in the standard implementation: (1) N1 = ¬(A·B), (2) N2 = ¬(A·N1), (3) N3 = ¬(B·N1), (4) Y = ¬(N2·N3). Using NOR gates, XOR needs five gates. For a half‑adder (sum = A⊕B, carry = A·B), the NAND implementation typically uses five or six gates, while the NOR implementation uses six. Knowing these counts helps in estimating chip area and propagation delay.

Propagation Delay and Fan‑Out Considerations

Each gate adds a propagation delay (typically 1–5 ns for CMOS). Cascading many NAND/NOR gates increases total delay, which limits maximum clock frequency in synchronous circuits. Fan‑out (the number of inputs driven by a single output) also affects delay because of capacitive loading. In practice, designers mix gate types (e.g., use a dedicated XOR cell) to optimise speed and area rather than insisting on pure NAND/NOR universality.

Examination Relevance

Questions often ask to draw the NAND‑only circuit for a given Boolean expression or to prove universality by constructing NOT, AND, OR from NAND. A systematic approach: (i) write the expression in sum‑of‑products or product‑of‑sums, (ii) apply De Morgan’s theorem to convert all operations to NAND (or NOR), (iii) draw the resulting network. Practice with expressions like Y = A·B + C·D and Y = (A + B)·(C + D) builds confidence.

  • Confusing the direction of Fermi‑level shift: n‑type moves Ef up toward Ec, p‑type moves Ef down toward Ev.
  • Assuming the intrinsic Fermi level is exactly at mid‑gap for all semiconductors; the (kT/2) ln(Nv/Nc) term causes a small but measurable offset.
  • Forgetting that the product np = nᵢ² holds only under thermal equilibrium and non‑degenerate conditions.
  • Treating the Lamb shift as part of fine structure; it is a separate QED correction of different origin and magnitude.
  • Misidentifying magic numbers: 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126 are the standard set; 40 is not a magic number despite being a subshell closure in some models.
  • Believing that a NAND gate with tied inputs behaves as an AND gate; it actually behaves as a NOT gate.
  • Neglecting temperature‑dependent band‑gap narrowing in heavily doped devices, leading to errors in forward‑voltage prediction.

Compton Scattering and Photon Momentum

Compton scattering provides direct experimental evidence that photons carry momentum proportional to their energy. When a photon of wavelength λ collides with a free or loosely bound electron, the scattered photon emerges with a longer wavelength λ′ and the electron recoils. The wavelength shift Δλ = λ′ − λ is given by the Compton formula Δλ = (h / mₑc)(1 − cos θ), where θ is the scattering angle, h is Planck’s constant, mₑ the electron rest mass, and c the speed of light. The constant h/(mₑc) = 2.426 × 10⁻¹² m is the Compton wavelength of the electron. The derivation follows from conservation of energy and momentum applied to a photon‑electron two‑body system, treating the photon as a particle with energy E = hν and momentum p = h/λ. The maximum shift occurs at θ = 180°, yielding Δλₘₐₓ = 2h/(mₑc). In the laboratory, the scattered photon energy is measured with a spectrometer; the recoil electron can be detected in a cloud chamber or silicon detector. Compton scattering dominates the interaction of γ‑rays with matter in the 0.1–10 MeV range and is the basis of Compton cameras used in nuclear medicine and astrophysics. The effect also limits the resolution of X‑ray imaging because scattered photons add a background that reduces contrast. Understanding the angular distribution, described by the Klein‑Nishina differential cross‑section, is essential for shielding calculations in radiotherapy and for interpreting γ‑ray spectra from radioactive sources.

Pair Production, Annihilation, and Energy Thresholds

Pair production is the creation of an electron‑positron pair from a photon in the Coulomb field of a nucleus. Energy‑momentum conservation requires the photon energy to exceed the rest‑mass energy of the two particles, 2 mₑc² = 1.022 MeV. The excess energy appears as kinetic energy shared between the electron and positron. The process cannot occur in free space because a single photon cannot satisfy both energy and momentum conservation simultaneously; the heavy nucleus absorbs the recoil momentum. The cross‑section rises sharply just above threshold and grows approximately as Z², where Z is the atomic number of the material. In the reverse process, annihilation, an electron and positron at rest produce two 511 keV photons emitted back‑to‑back to conserve momentum. If the positron has kinetic energy, the annihilation photons are Doppler‑broadened, a feature exploited in positron annihilation spectroscopy to study defects in solids. Pair production becomes the dominant γ‑ray interaction mechanism above ~10 MeV in high‑Z materials such as lead or tungsten, and it determines the design of calorimeters in high‑energy physics detectors. The attenuation coefficient for pair production is often expressed as μ_pair = N_A ρ σ_pair / A, where σ_pair is the atomic cross‑section. In shielding applications, a combination of high‑Z material (to initiate pair production) followed by a thick low‑Z layer (to absorb the resulting electrons and positrons via bremsstrahlung and ionisation) provides optimal protection.

Worked Example: Minimum Photon Energy for Pair Production in Lead
A γ‑ray photon traverses a lead (Z = 82) shield. Calculate the minimum photon energy required for pair production and the wavelength of the two annihilation photons produced when the created positron comes to rest.
The threshold energy is 2 mₑc² = 1.022 MeV. Any photon with E ≥ 1.022 MeV can produce a pair in the field of a lead nucleus. When the positron annihilates at rest, two photons of energy 511 keV each are emitted. Their wavelength λ = hc/E = (1240 eV·nm)/(511 000 eV) ≈ 2.43 × 10⁻³ nm = 2.43 pm. Thus the minimum photon energy is 1.022 MeV and the annihilation photons have wavelength 2.43 pm.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the 1.022 MeV threshold and the 511 keV annihilation line; they appear in multiple‑choice questions on γ‑ray interactions, PET imaging, and shielding. When a problem gives a photon energy just above threshold, compute the kinetic energy shared by the pair as E_photon − 1.022 MeV. For Compton scattering, remember the maximum wavelength shift 2h/(mₑc) = 4.85 pm; use it to check whether a given scattered photon energy is physically possible.

Liquid Drop Model and Semi‑Empirical Mass Formula

The liquid drop model treats the nucleus as a droplet of incompressible nuclear fluid, allowing a macroscopic description of binding energy. The semi‑empirical mass formula (SEMF), also called the Bethe‑Weizsäcker formula, expresses the binding energy B(A,Z) as a sum of five terms: volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing. The volume term a_v A reflects the saturation of nuclear forces; the surface term −a_s A2/3 corrects for nucleons at the surface having fewer neighbours. The Coulomb term −a_c Z(Z−1)/A1/3 accounts for electrostatic repulsion between protons. The asymmetry term −a_a (A−2Z)²/A arises from the Pauli principle, which forces excess neutrons into higher energy levels. The pairing term ±a_p A−1/2 adds extra binding for even‑even nuclei, subtracts for odd‑odd, and is zero for odd‑A nuclei. Typical coefficients (in MeV) are a_v ≈ 15.8, a_s ≈ 18.3, a_c ≈ 0.714, a_a ≈ 23.2, a_p ≈ 12.0. The SEMF reproduces the overall trend of binding energy per nucleon, peaking near A ≈ 56 (iron‑56). It also predicts the valley of stability and the most stable Z for a given A by minimising B with respect to Z, yielding Z ≈ A/(2 + a_c A2/3/(4a_a)). The model explains why very heavy nuclei (A > 200) are unstable to spontaneous fission: the Coulomb term grows faster than the surface term, making the nucleus energetically favourable to split. However, the SEMF cannot account for shell effects that produce magic numbers; those require the nuclear shell model.

Radioactive Decay Series and Secular Equilibrium

Many naturally occurring heavy nuclei decay through a cascade of α and β emissions until a stable isotope is reached. The three classical series are the uranium‑238 series (ending in lead‑206), the uranium‑235 series (ending in lead‑207), and the thorium‑232 series (ending in lead‑208). Each series contains 10–14 members with half‑lives ranging from fractions of a second to billions of years. When a long‑lived parent (e.g., ²³⁸U, T½ = 4.47 × 10⁹ y) decays to a much shorter‑lived daughter, the activity of the daughter quickly approaches that of the parent. This condition, called secular equilibrium, occurs when the parent half‑life is at least ~100 times longer than the daughter’s. In secular equilibrium, the number of daughter atoms N_d satisfies λ_d N_d = λ_p N_p, so the activities are equal. The total activity of the sample remains essentially constant over geological timescales, which is the basis for uranium‑lead dating. If the half‑lives are comparable, transient equilibrium is reached, where the daughter activity exceeds the parent activity by a factor λ_d/(λ_d − λ_p). In a closed system, the ratios of isotopes in a decay series provide a clock; for example, the ²³⁰Th/²³⁸U ratio in coral yields ages up to 500 kyr. Understanding the buildup and decay of series members is also crucial for radiation protection, because short‑lived daughters (e.g., radon‑222 in the uranium series) can dominate the dose even when the parent activity is modest.

Zener and Tunnel Diodes: Breakdown Mechanisms

Zener diodes exploit two distinct breakdown mechanisms in heavily doped p‑n junctions. For breakdown voltages below ~5 V, quantum mechanical tunnelling of electrons from the valence band of the p‑side to the conduction band of the n‑side dominates; this is the Zener effect. The tunnelling probability increases exponentially with the electric field, which in a heavily doped junction can exceed 10⁶ V/cm even at low reverse bias. For breakdown voltages above ~5 V, avalanche multiplication prevails: carriers accelerated by the high field gain enough kinetic energy to create electron‑hole pairs by impact ionisation, leading to a runaway current. The temperature coefficient of the Zener voltage is negative for the tunnelling regime (voltage decreases with temperature) and positive for the avalanche regime (voltage increases with temperature). Around 5 V the two effects nearly cancel, giving a near‑zero temperature coefficient, which is exploited in precision voltage references. Tunnel diodes (Esaki diodes) are fabricated with extremely heavy doping (≈10¹⁹ cm⁻³) so that the depletion region is only a few nanometres wide. At zero bias, the Fermi level lies inside the conduction band on the n‑side and inside the valence band on the p‑side. A small forward bias aligns filled states on one side with empty states on the other, allowing a large tunnelling current. As voltage increases, the overlap decreases, producing a region of negative differential resistance (NDR) where current falls with increasing voltage. This NDR region enables high‑frequency oscillators and amplifiers up to the terahertz range. The peak‑to‑valley current ratio (PVCR) and the peak current density are key figures of merit; typical silicon tunnel diodes have PVCR ≈ 3–5, while GaAs/AlGaAs resonant‑tunnelling structures achieve PVCR > 10.

Small‑Signal Hybrid‑π Model of the Bipolar Transistor

The hybrid‑π model provides a linearised, small‑signal representation of a bipolar junction transistor (BJT) around a chosen quiescent point. The model consists of a transconductance g_m = I_C / V_T (where V_T = kT/q ≈ 25.9 mV at 300 K), an input resistance r_π = β / g_m between base and emitter, an output resistance r_o = V_A / I_C (V_A is the Early voltage) between collector and emitter, and parasitic capacitances C_π (diffusion capacitance) and C_μ (junction capacitance). The current source g_m v_π represents the controlled collector current. This model is valid for frequencies well below the transition frequency f_T = g_m / (2π(C_π + C_μ)). In the common‑emitter configuration, the voltage gain is approximately −g_m R_C‖r_o, and the input impedance is r_π‖(β+1) R_E if an emitter resistor R_E is present. The model also captures the Miller effect: C_μ appears multiplied by the voltage gain at the input, limiting bandwidth. For high‑frequency design, the hybrid‑π parameters are extracted from S‑parameter measurements or from the Gummel‑Poon model. The model simplifies to the T‑model (with emitter resistance r_e = 1/g_m) when the base is grounded, which is useful for analysing common‑base stages. Understanding the hybrid‑π model is essential for designing broadband amplifiers, calculating noise figure, and predicting stability in feedback circuits.

  • Confusing the Zener tunnelling regime (negative temperature coefficient) with the avalanche regime (positive coefficient) when selecting a voltage reference.
  • Assuming the hybrid‑π model remains accurate up to f_T; in practice, parasitic package inductances and substrate coupling degrade performance well before f_T.
  • Neglecting the Miller multiplication of C_μ in common‑emitter stages, leading to overestimation of bandwidth.
  • Treating the semi‑empirical mass formula as exact for magic‑number nuclei; shell corrections can shift binding energies by several MeV.
  • Forgetting that secular equilibrium requires the parent half‑life to be at least two orders of magnitude longer than the daughter’s; otherwise transient equilibrium applies.
  • Misapplying the Compton formula to bound electrons without considering the Doppler broadening from electron momentum distribution.
  • Overlooking the fact that pair production cannot occur in free space; a nucleus or atomic field is mandatory for momentum conservation.

Mössbauer Effect and Recoilless Gamma‑Ray Emission

The Mössbauer effect, discovered by Rudolf Mössbauer in 1958, describes the recoilless emission and resonant absorption of gamma rays by nuclei bound in a solid lattice. When a free nucleus emits a gamma photon of energy Eγ, momentum conservation forces the nucleus to recoil with kinetic energy ER = Eγ² / (2Mc²), where M is the nuclear mass. In a crystal, a fraction f of emission events occurs without transferring momentum to a single nucleus; instead the entire lattice takes up the recoil, making ER negligibly small. This recoilless fraction is given by the Debye‑Waller factor f = exp[−⟨x²⟩k²], where ⟨x²⟩ is the mean‑square vibrational amplitude and k = Eγ/ħc. For the 14.4 keV transition of 57Fe at room temperature, f ≈ 0.7, yielding a natural linewidth Γ₀ = 4.7 neV (≈ 10⁻⁸ eV), one of the narrowest known spectral lines.

Because the emitted photons have an extremely well‑defined energy, they can be resonantly absorbed by identical nuclei in a second sample. By moving the source relative to the absorber with a controlled Doppler velocity v (typically a few mm s⁻¹), the photon energy is shifted by ΔE/E = v/c. The resulting Mössbauer spectrum displays hyperfine interactions: isomer shift (electron density at the nucleus), quadrupole splitting (electric field gradient), and magnetic hyperfine splitting (internal magnetic field). These parameters provide quantitative information on oxidation state, spin state, lattice dynamics, and magnetic ordering in solids.

Applications extend beyond condensed‑matter spectroscopy. The gravitational redshift experiment of Pound and Rebka (1960) used the 14.4 keV line to verify the gravitational redshift predicted by general relativity. Modern Mössbauer spectrometers operate in transmission, conversion‑electron, and synchrotron‑radiation modes, enabling in‑situ studies of catalysis, battery materials, and geological samples under extreme pressure.

Laser Physics: Population Inversion, Coherence, and Rate Equations

Light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation (laser) requires a medium with a population inversion between two energy levels, an optical cavity for feedback, and a pump mechanism to sustain the inversion. In a three‑level system (e.g., ruby), the ground state is level 1, the pump excites atoms to a short‑lived level 3, which rapidly decays non‑radiatively to the metastable upper laser level 2. The laser transition occurs from level 2 to level 1. Because the lower laser level is the ground state, more than half the atoms must be pumped to achieve inversion, leading to high threshold pump powers.

A four‑level system (e.g., Nd:YAG, He‑Ne, semiconductor lasers) adds a fast‑decaying lower laser level 0 above the ground state. Atoms pumped to level 3 relax to the upper laser level 2; stimulated emission transfers them to level 0, which quickly empties to the ground state. Since level 0 is essentially unpopulated at thermal equilibrium, inversion is achieved with a modest pump rate. The rate equations for the upper laser level population N₂ and photon density φ in the cavity are:

dN₂/dt = Rₚ − N₂/τ₂ − σ φ (N₂ − N₁)
dφ/dt = σ c (N₂ − N₁) φ − φ/τ_c

where Rₚ is the pump rate per unit volume, τ₂ the spontaneous lifetime of level 2, σ the stimulated‑emission cross‑section, c the speed of light in the medium, and τ_c the cavity photon lifetime. At steady state above threshold, dφ/dt = 0 gives the threshold condition σ (N₂ − N₁) = 1/(c τ_c). The output power scales linearly with pump power above threshold: P_out = η_s (P_pump − P_th), where η_s is the slope efficiency.

Coherence properties follow from the phase relationship of the stimulated photons. The longitudinal coherence length L_c = c/Δν is inversely proportional to the spectral linewidth Δν. For a typical He‑Ne laser (λ = 632.8 nm, Δν ≈ 1 GHz), L_c ≈ 30 cm. Semiconductor lasers exhibit broader linewidths (tens of MHz) but can be narrowed by external cavities or distributed‑feedback gratings. The Schawlow‑Townes limit gives the fundamental quantum linewidth Δν_ST = (2π h ν (Δν_c)²) / P_out, where Δν_c is the cavity linewidth.

Worked Example: Threshold Pump Rate for a Four‑Level Laser
A Nd:YAG laser rod (length 10 cm, diameter 6 mm) has an upper‑level lifetime τ₂ = 230 µs, emission cross‑section σ = 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ cm², and cavity photon lifetime τ_c = 5 ns. The refractive index n = 1.82. Assuming the lower laser level is empty (N₁ ≈ 0), calculate the threshold pump power P_th if the pump wavelength is 808 nm and the quantum efficiency η_q = 0.9.
The threshold inversion density is ΔN_th = 1/(σ c τ_c). With c = c₀/n = 3.0 × 10¹⁰ cm s⁻¹ / 1.82 ≈ 1.65 × 10¹⁰ cm s⁻¹, ΔN_th = 1 / (2.8 × 10⁻¹⁹ cm² × 1.65 × 10¹⁰ cm s⁻¹ × 5 × 10⁻⁹ s) ≈ 4.3 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³. The active volume V = π (0.3 cm)² × 10 cm ≈ 2.83 cm³, so the total number of inverted ions N_th = ΔN_th × V ≈ 1.2 × 10¹⁷. The pump rate required is Rₚ = N_th / τ₂ ≈ 5.2 × 10²³ s⁻¹. Each pump photon at 808 nm carries energy E_p = hc/λ ≈ 1.53 eV = 2.45 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Accounting for quantum efficiency, the threshold pump power P_th = (Rₚ × E_p) / η_q ≈ (5.2 × 10²³ × 2.45 × 10⁻¹⁹) / 0.9 ≈ 1.4 × 10⁵ W = 140 kW. In practice, diode‑pumped Nd:YAG lasers achieve threshold at a few watts because the pump is absorbed in a much smaller mode volume and the cavity Q is higher; the calculation illustrates the scaling of the threshold with σ, τ₂, and τ_c.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a laser problem gives τ₂, σ, and τ_c, first compute the threshold inversion ΔN_th = 1/(σ c τ_c). Then multiply by the active volume to get the total inverted population. Convert the pump photon energy to joules and apply the quantum efficiency. Remember that the cavity photon lifetime τ_c = Q/ω, where Q is the quality factor; if Q is given instead of τ_c, use τ_c = Q/ω. Keep units consistent (cm, s, J) to avoid factor‑of‑10 errors.

Superconductivity: BCS Theory, Meissner Effect, and Type I/II Classification

Superconductivity is the complete loss of electrical resistance below a critical temperature T_c accompanied by the expulsion of magnetic flux (Meissner effect). The microscopic Bardeen‑Cooper‑Schrieffer (BCS) theory (1957) explains conventional superconductors as a condensate of Cooper pairs — electrons bound by an attractive interaction mediated by lattice phonons. The pairing opens an energy gap Δ at the Fermi surface; at zero temperature 2Δ = 3.53 k_B T_c. The critical magnetic field H_c(T) follows H_c(T) = H_c(0)[1 − (T/T_c)²]. The thermodynamic critical field is related to the condensation energy density U₀ = H_c²/8π.

Type I superconductors (e.g., Al, Pb, Hg) exhibit a single critical field H_c above which the normal state is restored abruptly. Type II superconductors (e.g., NbTi, Nb₃Sn, high‑T_c cuprates) possess two critical fields: a lower critical field Hc1 where magnetic flux first penetrates as quantised vortices, and an upper critical field Hc2 where superconductivity is destroyed. Between Hc1 and Hc2 the material is in the mixed (vortex) state. The Ginzburg‑Landau parameter κ = λ/ξ (penetration depth λ over coherence length ξ) determines the type: κ < 1/√2 → Type I, κ > 1/√2 → Type II. For NbTi, λ ≈ 200 nm, ξ ≈ 4 nm, giving κ ≈ 50.

Quantised flux vortices each carry a flux quantum Φ₀ = h/2e = 2.07 × 10⁻¹⁵ Wb. The vortex lattice melts at high temperatures or currents, leading to flux‑flow resistance. Pinning centres (defects, grain boundaries) immobilise vortices, enabling large critical current densities J_c. Practical superconducting wires (NbTi, Nb₃Sn) operate at 4.2 K in fields up to 10–15 T, while REBCO coated conductors function at 77 K in high magnetic fields. Applications include MRI magnets, particle‑accelerator dipoles, maglev propulsion, and superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) for ultra‑sensitive magnetometry.

Quantum Hall Effect and Topological Edge States

The quantum Hall effect (QHE) appears in a two‑dimensional electron gas (2DEG) subjected to a strong perpendicular magnetic field B at low temperature. The Hall resistance R_H = V_H/I exhibits plateaus at quantised values R_H = h/(ν e²), where the filling factor ν = n_s h/(e B) (n_s = sheet carrier density). The integer QHE (IQHE) shows plateaus at integer ν = 1, 2, 3, … with a precision of parts in 10⁹, defining the von Klitzing constant R_K = h/e² = 25 812.807 Ω. The fractional QHE (FQHE) reveals plateaus at rational fractions ν = p/q (e.g., 1/3, 2/5) due to electron‑electron interactions forming incompressible quantum liquids with quasiparticles of fractional charge e* = e/q.

Landau quantisation splits the 2DEG density of states into discrete Landau levels separated by ħ ω_c (cyclotron energy). Each level has a degeneracy per unit area eB/h. When the Fermi level lies in the gap between Landau levels, the bulk is insulating and conduction occurs only via chiral edge states that propagate unidirectionally along the sample boundary. The number of edge channels equals the integer ν, giving a Hall conductance σ_xy = ν e²/h. The topological nature of these edge states is captured by the Chern number C = ν, which is robust against disorder as long as the bulk gap remains open.

Experimental signatures include vanishing longitudinal resistance R_xx → 0 on plateaus, activated temperature dependence R_xx ∝ exp(−Δ/2k_B T) with gap Δ, and non‑local transport measurements confirming edge‑only conduction. The QHE provides a primary resistance standard and a candidate platform for topological quantum computation, with the ν = 5/2 state a potential host for non‑Abelian anyons.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance: Principles, Chemical Shift, and Imaging Applications

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) exploits the magnetic moment μ = γ ħ I of nuclei with non‑zero spin I (e.g., 1H, 13C, 31P). In a static magnetic field B₀, the Zeeman splitting yields Larmor frequency ω₀ = γ B₀. For protons, γ/2π = 42.58 MHz T⁻¹; at 1.5 T, ω₀/2π ≈ 63.9 MHz. A transverse radio‑frequency (RF) pulse at ω₀ tips the net magnetisation M₀ into the xy‑plane, where it precesses and induces a detectable voltage in a receiver coil — the free induction decay (FID).

The resonance frequency is shifted by the local electronic environment, giving the chemical shift δ = (ν_sample − ν_ref)/ν_ref × 10⁶ ppm. Typical proton shifts range 0–12 ppm. Scalar (J) coupling between neighbouring spins splits peaks into multiplets with spacing J (Hz), providing connectivity information. Relaxation processes return the system to equilibrium: longitudinal relaxation time T₁ (spin‑lattice) governs recovery of M_z, while transverse relaxation time T₂ (spin‑spin) governs decay of M_xy. The Bloch equations describe the magnetisation dynamics under RF fields and relaxation.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) adds spatially varying gradient fields G_x, G_y, G_z to encode position. Slice selection uses a frequency‑selective RF pulse simultaneous with a slice‑select gradient. Phase encoding applies a brief gradient along y before readout; frequency encoding applies a gradient along x during signal acquisition. The acquired k‑space data S(k_x, k_y) is the 2D Fourier transform of the spin density ρ(x, y). Image reconstruction via inverse FFT yields anatomical contrast weighted by T₁, T₂, or proton density. Advanced sequences (spin‑echo, gradient‑echo, inversion‑recovery, diffusion‑weighted, functional MRI) manipulate contrast for clinical diagnosis.

  • Confusing the recoilless fraction f with the Debye‑Waller factor in X‑ray diffraction; they have the same mathematical form but describe different physical processes.
  • Assuming a three‑level laser always has a higher threshold than a four‑level laser; some three-level systems (e.g., ruby) can operate with high peak powers due to a broad pump band and long metastable lifetime.
  • Treating the BCS gap ratio 2Δ/k_B T_c = 3.53 as universal; strong‑coupling superconductors (Pb, Hg) show ratios up to 4.5.
  • Mixing up the lower and upper critical fields Hc1 and Hc2 when describing the mixed state of a Type II superconductor.
  • Believing the quantum Hall plateaus arise from single‑particle Landau quantisation alone; the fractional QHE requires many‑body correlations and cannot be explained without electron‑electron interaction.
  • Neglecting the difference between T₁ and T₂ in MRI contrast; T₁‑weighted images use short TR, T₂‑weighted images use long TE.
  • Using the free‑space Larmor frequency for in‑vivo NMR without accounting for susceptibility‑induced field inhomogeneities that broaden lines and shift resonances.

These advanced topics deepen the quantitative toolkit required for high‑level problem solving in modern physics.

Auger Effect and Electron Spectroscopy

The Auger effect is a non‑radiative transition that follows the creation of a core‑hole in an atom. When an inner‑shell electron is removed — by photoionisation, electron impact, or X‑ray absorption — a higher‑shell electron drops into the vacancy, releasing energy. Instead of emitting a characteristic X‑ray photon, this energy can be transferred to a third electron, which is ejected from the atom as an Auger electron. The kinetic energy of the Auger electron is given by the difference between the initial core‑level binding energy and the sum of the two final‑state binding energies, making it essentially independent of the excitation source. This property makes Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) a powerful surface‑analysis technique: the escape depth of Auger electrons in solids is only a few nanometres, so the signal originates almost exclusively from the topmost atomic layers.

Selection Rules and Notation

Auger transitions are labelled by the three shells involved, for example KLL, LMM, or MNN. The first letter denotes the initial core hole, the second the shell of the electron that fills it, and the third the shell of the emitted electron. Because the process involves three electrons, the final state contains two holes, leading to multiplet splitting that reflects the coupling of the two hole angular momenta. The relative intensities of the multiplet components follow statistical weights modified by the overlap of the initial and final state wavefunctions. In solids, the presence of a continuum of valence states broadens the lines and can shift the kinetic energy by a few electronvolts due to extra‑atomic relaxation (screening by neighbouring atoms).

Applications in Materials Characterisation

AES is routinely used for elemental mapping, depth profiling (combined with ion sputtering), and chemical‑state identification. The chemical shift of Auger peaks — typically 1–5 eV — correlates with the oxidation state and local bonding environment, complementing X‑ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Because the Auger yield is high for light elements (C, N, O), AES is especially valuable for analysing contamination, oxidation layers, and thin‑film stoichiometry in semiconductor processing. Quantitative analysis requires sensitivity factors that account for the electron escape depth, analyser transmission function, and matrix effects; these are usually calibrated against standards of known composition.

Limitations and Complementary Techniques

The main limitation of AES is its surface sensitivity, which can be a disadvantage when bulk information is needed. Charging of insulating samples distorts the kinetic energy scale, requiring charge neutralisation or referencing to a known peak. Radiation damage from the primary electron beam can modify the surface chemistry, particularly for polymers and alkali halides. For deeper profiling, techniques such as secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) or XPS with variable photon energy (hard X‑ray photoelectron spectroscopy) are preferred. Nevertheless, the combination of high spatial resolution (down to ~10 nm in scanning Auger microscopes) and elemental specificity keeps AES a cornerstone of surface science.

Raman Scattering and Phonon Interactions

Raman scattering is an inelastic light‑scattering process in which a photon exchanges energy with elementary excitations in a material, most commonly optical phonons. When monochromatic light of frequency ω₀ illuminates a sample, the scattered light contains components at ω₀ (Rayleigh line), ω₀ − Ω (Stokes line), and ω₀ + Ω (anti‑Stokes line), where Ω is the phonon frequency. The Stokes process corresponds to photon energy loss by creating a phonon; the anti‑Stokes process requires a thermally populated phonon and results in photon energy gain. The intensity ratio of anti‑Stokes to Stokes lines follows the Bose‑Einstein factor exp(−ħΩ/k_BT), providing a direct measure of the local temperature in micro‑Raman thermometry.

Selection Rules and Polarisation Dependence

The Raman activity of a phonon mode is governed by the change in the polarizability tensor during the vibration. Group theory predicts which irreducible representations of the crystal point group are Raman‑active. For centrosymmetric crystals, modes that are infrared‑active are Raman‑inactive and vice‑versa (mutual exclusion rule). In non‑centrosymmetric materials, some modes can be both IR and Raman active. The scattered intensity depends on the polarisation vectors of the incident and scattered light relative to the crystal axes, allowing the symmetry of each mode to be identified by measuring polarised Raman spectra in different scattering geometries (e.g., back‑scattering from (001) face with parallel and crossed polarisers).

Resonance Raman Enhancement

When the excitation photon energy approaches an electronic transition (interband or excitonic), the Raman cross‑section can increase by orders of magnitude — the resonance Raman effect. This enhancement is mode‑selective: only phonons that couple strongly to the resonant electronic state are amplified. Resonance Raman spectroscopy is therefore a sensitive probe of electron‑phonon coupling, exciton‑phonon interactions, and the symmetry of excited electronic states. In semiconductors such as GaAs or MoS₂, tuning the laser wavelength across the band gap reveals the evolution of phonon linewidths and frequencies, providing insight into carrier‑phonon scattering mechanisms that limit mobility.

Disorder, Stress, and Temperature Effects

Phonon confinement in nanocrystals or quantum dots relaxes the q = 0 selection rule, leading to asymmetric broadening and downshift of the Raman peak. Hydrostatic or biaxial strain shifts phonon frequencies linearly with the strain tensor components; the shift rates (phonon deformation potentials) are tabulated for common semiconductors and are used for non‑destructive stress mapping in devices. Temperature increases cause anharmonic phonon‑phonon interactions, resulting in peak broadening and a redshift that follows a well‑established empirical relation (e.g., the Balkanski model). These dependencies make Raman spectroscopy a versatile diagnostic tool for process control in microelectronics, photovoltaics, and two‑dimensional material research.

Worked Example: Estimating Temperature from Anti‑Stokes/Stokes Ratio
A micro‑Raman measurement on a silicon sample at room temperature uses a 532 nm laser. The first‑order optical phonon in Si has a Raman shift of 520 cm⁻¹. The recorded anti‑Stokes to Stokes intensity ratio is 0.12. Assuming the instrument response is flat across the two lines, estimate the local temperature of the probed volume.
The intensity ratio R = I_AS / I_S = exp(−ħΩ / k_B T). The phonon energy ħΩ = h c Δν = (6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s)(3.00×10⁸ m/s)(520×100 m⁻¹) = 1.03×10⁻²⁰ J. k_B = 1.381×10⁻²³ J/K. Then ħΩ/k_B = 746 K. Taking natural logarithm: ln(R) = −746 K / T → T = −746 K / ln(0.12) = 746 K / 2.12 ≈ 352 K. The result (≈79 °C) indicates local heating by the focused laser, a common artefact in micro‑Raman experiments.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a Raman question gives a Stokes shift in cm⁻¹, convert to energy in joules or electronvolts before using the Boltzmann factor. Remember that the anti‑Stokes line is weaker at room temperature; if the problem states equal intensities, the implied temperature is very high (≫1000 K) and likely signals a non‑thermal population (e.g., laser‑induced carrier heating). Always check whether the instrument response correction is mentioned — if not, assume it is unity.

Quantum Tunneling in Resonant Tunneling Diodes

A resonant tunneling diode (RTD) exploits coherent quantum tunneling through a double‑barrier heterostructure to produce a region of negative differential resistance (NDR) in its current‑voltage characteristic. The active region consists of a narrow quantum well (typically 3–5 nm of GaAs or InGaAs) sandwiched between two thin barrier layers (AlAs or AlGaAs, 1–2 nm each). When a bias is applied, the Fermi level in the emitter aligns with a quantised energy level in the well. Electrons tunnel resonantly through the first barrier, occupy the quasi‑bound state, and then tunnel out through the second barrier into the collector. The transmission probability peaks sharply at the resonance, giving a large current. As the bias increases further, the level moves below the emitter Fermi level and the current drops, creating the NDR region.

Quantised Levels and Linewidth

The energy levels in the well are determined by solving the Schrödinger equation for a finite square well with effective mass m* appropriate to the well material. For a 5 nm GaAs well (m* = 0.067 m₀) the ground state lies ≈ 50 meV above the well bottom. The linewidth Γ of the resonance is set by the lifetime τ of the quasi‑bound state: Γ = ħ/τ. The lifetime is limited by tunneling through the barriers; thinner or lower barriers increase the tunneling rate, broadening the resonance and raising the peak current but reducing the peak‑to‑valley current ratio (PVCR). Typical PVCR values at 300 K range from 2–5 for AlAs/GaAs RTDs, improving to >10 at 77 K because phonon‑assisted scattering (which broadens the level) is suppressed.

Coherence and Phase‑Breaking Mechanisms

Resonant tunneling requires phase coherence across the double‑barrier structure. Elastic scattering (interface roughness, alloy disorder) and inelastic scattering (phonon emission, electron‑electron interactions) introduce phase breaking, which reduces the resonant transmission and fills the valley current. The valley current is often modelled as a sum of sequential tunneling (incoherent) and thermionic emission over the barriers. At high temperatures, thermionic emission dominates the valley, degrading NDR. Design strategies to improve PVCR include using wider bandgap barriers (AlAs, AlN), inserting spacer layers to reduce impurity scattering, and employing strain‑balanced InGaAs/AlAsSb systems for larger conduction‑band offsets.

Applications and Circuit Implications

The NDR of RTDs enables ultra‑high‑frequency oscillators (up to several THz), multi‑valued logic, and low‑power memory cells. In a simple series circuit with a load resistor, the RTD can be biased at the stable points on either side of the NDR region, forming a bistable latch. The small capacitance of the quantum well (fF range) and the picosecond tunneling transit time give RTDs intrinsic switching speeds far exceeding conventional transistors. However, the low peak current density (typically 10–100 kA/cm²) and the need for precise epitaxial growth limit large‑scale integration. Hybrid circuits combining RTDs with heterojunction bipolar transistors (HBTs) or high‑electron‑mobility transistors (HEMTs) are actively researched to leverage the speed of RTDs while providing gain and isolation.

Spintronics: Giant Magnetoresistance and Tunnel Magnetoresistance

Spintronics exploits the electron spin degree of freedom, in addition to its charge, for information storage and processing. Two landmark phenomena — giant magnetoresistance (GMR) and tunnel magnetoresistance (TMR) — underlie modern magnetic read heads and magnetic random‑access memory (MRAM). GMR occurs in metallic multilayers consisting of alternating ferromagnetic and non‑magnetic spacer layers (e.g., Co/Cu/Co). When the magnetisations of adjacent ferromagnetic layers are parallel, majority‑spin electrons experience low scattering in both layers, while minority‑spin electrons scatter strongly. The total resistance is low. When the magnetisations are antiparallel, both spin channels encounter high scattering in one of the layers, raising the resistance. The GMR ratio ΔR/R can exceed 100 % at room temperature for optimised Co/Cu superlattices.

Spin‑Dependent Scattering and the Two‑Current Model

The two‑current model treats spin‑up and spin‑down electrons as independent conduction channels with distinct resistivities ρ↑ and ρ↓. In a ferromagnet, the exchange splitting of the d‑band creates an asymmetry in the density of states at the Fermi level, leading to different scattering rates for the two spin channels. In a multilayer, the total resistance for a given magnetic configuration is obtained by adding the channel resistances in series (for current‑perpendicular‑to‑plane, CPP geometry) or in parallel (for current‑in‑plane, CIP geometry). The GMR effect is largest in CPP geometry because the spin‑dependent interface resistances add directly. Interface quality, spacer thickness (which controls interlayer exchange coupling via RKKY oscillations), and spin‑diffusion length are critical design parameters.

Tunnel Magnetoresistance in Magnetic Tunnel Junctions

TMR occurs in a magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ) where two ferromagnetic electrodes are separated by a thin insulating barrier (typically MgO, 1–2 nm). Electrons tunnel quantum‑mechanically through the barrier; the tunneling probability depends on the symmetry of the electron wavefunction and the spin‑polarised density of states at the Fermi level. In Fe/MgO/Fe junctions, the Δ₁ symmetry band provides a highly spin‑polarised tunneling channel, yielding TMR ratios >600 % at room temperature and >1000 % at low temperature. The TMR ratio is given by Jullière’s formula: TMR = 2P₁P₂/(1 − P₁P₂), where P₁,₂ are the spin polarisations of the two electrodes. MgO’s crystalline coherence filters the Δ₁ band, enhancing the effective polarisation far beyond the bulk value.

Spin‑Transfer Torque and MRAM

A spin‑polarised current passing through an MTJ can exert a torque on the free layer’s magnetisation — the spin‑transfer torque (STT). When the current exceeds a critical density (typically 10⁶–10⁷ A/cm²), the torque can switch the free layer between parallel and antiparallel states, enabling electrical writing in STT‑MRAM. The thermal stability factor Δ = E_b/k_BT (where E_b is the energy barrier between states) must exceed ≈ 60 for ten‑year data retention. This requirement conflicts with the desire for low switching current, driving research into perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA) materials (CoFeB/MgO, Co/Pt multilayers) and voltage‑controlled magnetic anisotropy (VCMA) to reduce the write energy. STT‑MRAM combines non‑volatility, endurance >10¹⁵ cycles, and sub‑nanosecond write speeds, positioning it as a leading candidate for universal memory.

Quantum Dots: Size Quantization and Optical Properties

Quantum dots (QDs) are semiconductor nanocrystals typically 2–10 nm in diameter, small enough that charge carriers experience three‑dimensional quantum confinement. The confinement energy scales inversely with the square of the dot size, so the effective band gap widens as the dot shrinks. For a spherical dot of radius R, the lowest electron‑hole transition energy in the effective‑mass approximation is E(R) = E_g + ħ²π²/(2R²)(1/m_e* + 1/m_h*) − 1.8e²/(4πεR), where the last term is the Coulomb binding energy (exciton). This size‑tunable band gap makes QDs attractive for light‑emitting diodes, lasers, and biological imaging.

Synthesis and Surface Chemistry

Colloidal synthesis in coordinating solvents (e.g., trioctylphosphine oxide) yields monodisperse QDs with narrow size distributions (σ/R < 5 %). The surface is passivated by organic ligands (oleic acid, amines) that determine solubility and prevent non‑radiative surface traps. Core‑shell structures (CdSe/ZnS, CdTe/CdSe) further reduce surface recombination by confining the wavefunction away from the interface, boosting photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQY) to >90 %. Ligand exchange can render QDs water‑soluble for bio‑applications, but often introduces trap states that lower PLQY; recent zwitterionic ligands mitigate this trade‑off.

Optical Spectra and Fine Structure

The absorption spectrum of a QD ensemble shows a series of discrete peaks corresponding to 1S, 1P, 1D… electron‑hole transitions, broadened by size dispersion. Single‑dot spectroscopy reveals sharp zero‑phonon lines (linewidth < 1 µeV at 4 K) and a fine structure splitting of the exciton due to electron‑hole exchange interaction (typically 0.1–1 meV). The splitting lifts the degeneracy of the bright exciton doublet, leading to linearly polarised emission. In charged QDs (trions), the additional carrier screens the exchange interaction, modifying the fine structure — a signature used to probe charging dynamics in QD‑based single‑photon sources.

Applications in Photonics and Quantum Information

QDs embedded in photonic cavities or waveguides serve as deterministic single‑photon emitters with high purity (g²(0) < 0.01) and indistinguishability > 95 % under resonant excitation. The Purcell effect in a cavity reduces the spontaneous‑emission lifetime, increasing the emission rate and suppressing dephasing. Electrically driven QD single‑photon diodes have been demonstrated by integrating QDs into p‑i‑n junctions. For quantum computing, the spin of a resident electron or hole in a charged QD acts as a stationary qubit; optical selection rules enable spin‑photon interfaces for distributed quantum networks. Challenges remain in achieving uniform emission wavelength across large arrays (inhomogeneous broadening) and in suppressing charge noise that broadens the spin coherence time (T₂* ≈ 1–10 ns).
