Magnetic Field and the Biot–Savart Law

1.1 Origin of the Magnetic Field

The magnetic field is a vector field that exerts a force on moving electric charges and on other magnets; it arises fundamentally from electric currents and from the intrinsic magnetic moments of elementary particles. In the early nineteenth century, Jean‑Baptiste Biot and Félix Savart performed systematic experiments that showed the magnetic effect of a current element is proportional to the current, to the length of the element, and to the sine of the angle between the element and the line joining the element to the observation point, while it falls off as the inverse square of the distance. This empirical discovery laid the groundwork for a quantitative description of magnetostatics and demonstrated that magnetism is not a separate force but a manifestation of electricity in motion.

1.2 Mathematical Formulation of the Biot–Savart Law

The Biot–Savart law states that the differential magnetic field dB produced at a point P by a current element I dl is given by dB = (μ₀/4π) I (dl × mathbf{r})/r², where μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A is the permeability of free space, mathbf{r} is the unit vector from the element to P, and r is the distance. The cross product ensures that the field circles the current according to the right‑hand rule, and the 1/r² dependence mirrors the Coulomb law for electric fields, reflecting the inverse‑square nature of the underlying interaction. Integrating this expression over the entire current distribution yields the total magnetic field, a procedure that is straightforward for highly symmetric geometries but becomes cumbersome for arbitrary shapes.

1.3 Applications to Simple Current Configurations

For an infinitely long straight wire carrying a steady current I, the integration of the Biot–Savart law gives B = μ₀ I/(2π r), a result that is also obtainable more directly from Ampère’s circuital law. A circular loop of radius R carrying current I produces on its axis a field B = (μ₀ I R²)/(2(R² + x²)3/2), which reduces to μ₀ I/(2R) at the centre. These textbook examples illustrate how the superposition principle allows the field of any planar current distribution to be built from elemental contributions, and they provide the standard reference fields used in laboratory calibrations of magnetometers.

Ampere’s Circuital Law and Its Applications

2.1 Statement and Integral Form

Ampère’s circuital law, in its original magnetostatic form, asserts that the line integral of the magnetic field B around any closed loop C equals μ₀ times the net current I_enc passing through the surface bounded by C: ∮_C B·dl = μ₀ I_enc. This law is a direct consequence of the Biot–Savart law and the fact that the magnetic field has zero divergence (∇·B=0). It provides a powerful shortcut for calculating B when the current distribution possesses sufficient symmetry, because the integral can often be evaluated by choosing an Amperian loop on which B is either constant in magnitude or parallel to the path element.

2.2 Symmetry Arguments and the Magnetic Field of a Long Straight Conductor

For an infinitely long straight wire, cylindrical symmetry dictates that the magnetic field lines form concentric circles centred on the wire and that the magnitude of B depends only on the radial distance r. Choosing a circular Amperian loop of radius r gives ∮ B·dl = B(2π r) = μ₀ I, leading immediately to B = μ₀ I/(2π r). The same reasoning applied to a coaxial cable shows that the field outside the outer conductor is zero when the net enclosed current vanishes, a principle exploited in shielding sensitive electronic equipment from external magnetic interference.

2.3 Magnetic Field Inside a Solenoid and a Toroid

A long solenoid with n turns per unit length carrying current I produces a nearly uniform magnetic field inside, directed along the axis, given by B = μ₀ n I, while the field outside is negligible. This result follows from applying Ampère’s law to a rectangular Amperian loop that straddles the interior and exterior of the solenoid. A toroidal coil, by contrast, confines the magnetic field entirely within its windings; applying the law to a circular path of radius r inside the toroid yields B = μ₀ N I/(2π r), where N is the total number of turns. The toroid’s self‑contained field makes it the preferred geometry for inductors in high‑frequency circuits where stray fields must be minimised.

Example: Magnetic Field of a Straight Wire
A long straight copper wire carries a steady current of 5 A. Calculate the magnitude of the magnetic field at a point 2 cm from the wire.
Using Ampère’s law for a long straight conductor, B = μ₀ I/(2π r). Substituting μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A, I = 5 A, and r = 0.02 m gives B = (4π × 10⁻⁷ × 5)/(2π × 0.02) = (2 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.02) = 1 × 10⁻⁴ T = 0.1 mT. The direction follows the right‑hand rule: if the current flows upward, the field circles counter‑clockwise when viewed from above.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem asks for the direction of an induced emf, always apply Lenz’s law first: the induced current creates a magnetic field that opposes the change in flux. Then use the right‑hand rule for the induced current to assign the polarity of the emf; this two‑step method avoids sign errors that are common under time pressure.

Magnetic Properties of Materials

3.1 Classification of Magnetic Materials

Materials respond to an external magnetic field H by acquiring a magnetisation M, and the total magnetic flux density is B = μ₀(H + M). Based on the sign and magnitude of the magnetic susceptibility χ = M/H, substances are grouped into diamagnetic (χ < 0, small), paramagnetic (χ > 0, small), and ferromagnetic (χ ≫ 1, strongly temperature‑dependent). Diamagnetism arises from induced atomic currents that oppose the applied field and is present in all matter; paramagnetism originates from the alignment of permanent atomic magnetic moments with the field; ferromagnetism results from a quantum‑mechanical exchange interaction that aligns moments spontaneously over macroscopic domains, producing a large net magnetisation even without an external field.

3.2 Microscopic Origin of Magnetisation

In diamagnetic materials the applied field modifies the orbital motion of electrons, generating a tiny induced magnetic moment opposite to the field in accordance with Lenz’s law at the atomic scale. Paramagnetic atoms possess unpaired electron spins that give each atom a permanent magnetic moment μ_B (Bohr magneton); thermal agitation randomises these moments, but an external field produces a partial alignment described by the Brillouin function, which reduces to Curie’s law χ = C/T at high temperatures. Ferromagnetism is explained by the Heisenberg exchange Hamiltonian, which favours parallel alignment of neighbouring spins; the resulting spontaneous magnetisation divides the material into domains separated by Bloch walls. An external field moves these walls and rotates domains, producing the characteristic hysteresis loop.

3.3 Hysteresis and Permanent Magnets

When a ferromagnetic specimen is subjected to a cyclic magnetising field H, the flux density B traces a closed loop known as the hysteresis loop. The area of the loop represents the energy dissipated per cycle as heat, a quantity crucial for transformer core design. The remanent flux density B_r (retentivity) is the magnetisation left when H returns to zero, while the coercive field H_c (coercivity) is the reverse field required to bring B back to zero. Hard magnetic materials such as Alnico or rare‑earth alloys exhibit large B_r and H_c, making them suitable for permanent magnets; soft magnetic materials like silicon steel have narrow loops, low losses, and are used in AC machines and transformer cores.

Electromagnetic Induction: Faraday’s and Lenz’s Laws

4.1 Faraday’s Law of Induction

Michael Faraday discovered in 1831 that a changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an electromotive force (emf) proportional to the rate of change of that flux. In integral form, the induced emf ε = − dΦ_B/dt, where Φ_B = ∫_S B·dA is the magnetic flux through a surface S bounded by the circuit. The negative sign, introduced by Lenz, encodes the direction of the induced emf. Faraday’s law is one of Maxwell’s equations in its differential form ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, showing that a time‑varying magnetic field creates a non‑conservative electric field whose circulation around a closed path equals the negative time derivative of the magnetic flux.

4.2 Lenz’s Law and the Direction of Induced Current

Lenz’s law states that the induced current flows in such a direction that its own magnetic field opposes the change in the original magnetic flux that produced it. This is a direct consequence of energy conservation: if the induced current reinforced the flux change, a positive feedback would create energy from nothing. Practically, to apply Lenz’s law one first determines whether the external flux is increasing or decreasing, then uses the right‑hand rule to find the direction of the magnetic field that would oppose that change, and finally deduces the sense of the induced current that produces the opposing field. This systematic approach eliminates sign ambiguities in problems involving moving conductors or varying currents.

4.3 Motional emf and the Concept of Magnetic Flux

When a conductor of length l moves with velocity v perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field B, the magnetic force q(v×B) on free charges separates them, creating a potential difference ε = B l v across the ends. This motional emf can be derived from Faraday’s law by noting that the area swept by the conductor per unit time is l v, so the rate of change of flux is B l v. The same expression emerges from the Lorentz force law, illustrating the deep unity between magnetic forces on charges and electromagnetic induction. In a rotating coil (as in an AC generator), the flux varies sinusoidally, Φ = B A cos ωt, giving an alternating emf ε = N B A ω sin ωt, where N is the number of turns. This principle underlies all commercial electric power generation.

Alternating Current Circuits and Resonance

5.1 Phasor Representation of Sinusoidal Quantities

In AC circuit analysis, sinusoidal voltages and currents of the same frequency are represented as rotating vectors called phasors. A voltage v(t) = V_m sin(ωt + φ) corresponds to a phasor V of magnitude V_m/√2 (the RMS value) making an angle φ with the reference axis. The time derivative becomes multiplication by jω, and integration becomes division by jω, where j = √(−1). This algebraic transformation converts differential equations governing LCR circuits into simple complex‑impedance equations, allowing the use of Ohm’s law in the form V = I Z, where Z = R + j(ωL − 1/ωC) is the complex impedance. The real part of Z represents resistive loss, while the imaginary part represents reactive storage of energy in the magnetic field of the inductor and the electric field of the capacitor.

5.2 Series LCR Circuit and Resonance Condition

For a series connection of a resistor R, an inductor L, and a capacitor C driven by a sinusoidal source, the total impedance is Z = R + j(ωL − 1/ωC). The current amplitude I = V/|Z| reaches a maximum when the reactive part vanishes, i.e., when ωL = 1/ωC. This defines the resonant angular frequency ω₀ = 1/√(LC), at which the circuit behaves purely resistively and the voltage across the inductor and capacitor can be many times larger than the source voltage, a phenomenon quantified by the quality factor Q = ω₀ L/R = 1/(ω₀ C R). High‑Q circuits exhibit sharp resonance peaks and are employed in radio tuners and filters; low‑Q circuits provide broad bandwidths suitable for power‑factor correction.

5.3 Power in AC Circuits and the Quality Factor

The instantaneous power p(t) = v(t)i(t) in an AC circuit consists of a constant average component P = V_rms I_rms cos φ and an oscillating component at twice the supply frequency. The factor cos φ is the power factor; it equals R/|Z| for a series LCR circuit. At resonance φ = 0, so the power factor is unity and the source delivers maximum real power to the load. The quality factor Q also relates to the energy stored versus energy dissipated per cycle: Q = 2π (energy stored)/(energy lost per cycle). In practical power systems, improving the power factor by adding shunt capacitors reduces line currents and I²R losses, a direct application of the resonance and impedance concepts developed for signal circuits.

The lesson concludes with a reminder that the unified framework of magnetostatics, electromagnetic induction, and AC circuit theory provides the foundation for understanding everything from electric motors to wireless communication.
