Transition Metals: General Characteristics and Periodic Trends

1.1 Electronic configuration and the d‑block definition

The d‑block elements are defined by the progressive filling of the (n‑1)d subshell while the ns orbital remains either fully occupied or contains one or two electrons. In the first transition series the 3d orbitals are filled after the 4s orbital, giving configurations such as [Ar] 3d¹4s² for scandium and [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s¹ for copper. This ordering arises because the 4s orbital is lower in energy for the neutral atom, but once electrons are removed the 3d orbitals become more stable, which explains why the common oxidation states involve loss of the 4s electrons first. The gradual increase in nuclear charge across the series contracts the d orbitals, making them more core‑like and less available for bonding, a trend that underlies many of the chemical properties discussed later.

1.2 Variation in atomic and ionic radii across the series

Atomic radii decrease steadily from scandium to zinc because each added proton pulls the electron cloud closer while the shielding provided by the d electrons is relatively poor. The contraction is not uniform; a pronounced dip occurs at chromium and copper where the half‑filled and fully‑filled d subshells confer extra stability, leading to a slight expansion of the metallic radius. Ionic radii for a given oxidation state follow the same pattern, but the magnitude of the decrease is larger for higher charges because the effective nuclear charge felt by the remaining electrons increases. This systematic size reduction influences lattice energies, complex stability and the ability of the metal centre to accommodate ligands of different steric demand.

1.3 Oxidation state diversity and its thermodynamic basis

Transition metals exhibit a wide range of oxidation states, from +1 in copper(I) to +7 in manganese(VII) as permanganate. The accessibility of multiple states stems from the comparable energies of the (n‑1)d and ns orbitals, allowing electrons to be removed from either set with relatively small energy penalties. Thermodynamically, the stability of a particular oxidation state is governed by the balance between ionisation energies, lattice or solvation energies, and the ligand field stabilisation energy (LFSE) that the metal gains in a given coordination environment. For example, the +2 state dominates for the late first‑row metals because the LFSE for a d⁸ configuration in an octahedral field is maximised, whereas early metals such as titanium favour +4 where a d⁰ configuration eliminates LFSE considerations altogether.

1.4 Magnetic behaviour and the role of unpaired electrons

The magnetic moment of a transition metal complex is directly related to the number of unpaired d electrons. In high‑spin octahedral complexes the electrons occupy the five d orbitals according to Hund’s rule before pairing, giving spin‑only magnetic moments that follow the formula μₛₒ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons. Low‑spin configurations arise when the crystal field splitting Δ₀ exceeds the pairing energy, forcing electrons to pair in the lower t₂g set. This spin‑state crossover is most common for d⁴–d⁷ metal ions with strong‑field ligands such as CN⁻ or CO, and it has profound consequences for reactivity, colour and catalytic cycles.

1.5 Catalytic activity and industrial relevance

Many transition metals serve as heterogeneous or homogeneous catalysts because they can adopt multiple oxidation states and provide vacant d orbitals for substrate binding. In the Haber‑Bosch process iron surfaces facilitate N₂ dissociation, while in the Wilkinson’s catalyst RhCl(PPh₃)₃ the rhodium centre cycles between Rh(I) and Rh(III) during hydrogenation of alkenes. The ability to form π‑complexes with unsaturated substrates, to undergo oxidative addition and reductive elimination, and to stabilise high‑energy intermediates through LFSE makes the d‑block uniquely suited for catalytic transformations that are otherwise kinetically inaccessible.

Lanthanoids and Actinoids: Electronic Structure and Chemistry

2.1 The 4f and 5f orbitals and the concept of lanthanoid contraction

The lanthanoid series corresponds to the filling of the 4f subshell from cerium (4f¹) to lutetium (4f¹⁴). Because the 4f orbitals are radially contracted and shielded by the filled 5s and 5p shells, they participate only weakly in bonding, and the chemistry of the lanthanoids is dominated by the +3 oxidation state. The progressive increase in nuclear charge across the series pulls the 6s electrons closer, causing a steady decrease in ionic radius known as the lanthanoid contraction. This contraction makes the later lanthanoids chemically similar to the early transition metals of the next period, influencing separation strategies and the properties of their complexes.

2.2 Common oxidation states and the stability of +3

While +3 is the overwhelmingly preferred oxidation state for all lanthanoids, a few elements exhibit accessible +2 or +4 states. Europium(II) and ytterbium(II) are stabilised by the half‑filled (f⁷) and fully‑filled (f¹⁴) configurations respectively, whereas cerium(IV) and terbium(IV) benefit from the empty or half‑filled f subshells. The redox potentials for these couples are modest, so the non‑+3 states are usually only observed in solid‑state compounds or in strongly reducing/oxidising media. In contrast, the actinoids display a much richer oxidation‑state chemistry because the 5f orbitals are less contracted and can engage in covalent bonding.

2.3 Spectroscopic properties and the origin of sharp f‑f bands

The electronic transitions within the 4fⁿ configuration are Laporte‑forbidden but gain weak intensity through mixing with opposite‑parity orbitals. Because the 4f electrons are shielded, the crystal field splitting is small (typically < 500 cm⁻¹) and the resulting absorption bands are extremely sharp, resembling atomic spectra. These sharp bands give rise to the characteristic colours of lanthanoid ions and are exploited in phosphors, lasers and up‑conversion materials. The energy levels can be described by the Russell‑Saunders coupling scheme, and the positions of the bands are largely independent of the ligand environment, which makes them useful as internal standards in spectroscopic analysis.

2.4 Actinoid chemistry: radioactivity, oxidation state range and complex formation

The actinoid series (thorium to lawrencium) involves filling of the 5f orbitals. The early actinoids (Th, Pa, U, Np, Pu) have relatively extended 5f orbitals that can overlap with ligand orbitals, allowing a variety of oxidation states from +3 up to +7 for uranium. As the series progresses the 5f orbitals contract, and the chemistry converges toward the lanthanoid‑like +3 dominance. Radioactivity introduces additional constraints: many actinoids are available only in trace amounts, and their complexes are studied using radiochemical techniques. Nevertheless, actinoid complexes such as uranyl (UO₂²⁺) display strong linear O=U=O units and intense charge‑transfer bands that are central to nuclear fuel reprocessing and environmental chemistry.

2.5 Separation techniques based on ionic radius differences

The lanthanoid contraction creates a systematic decrease in ionic radius of about 0.2 Å across the series, a difference that is exploited in ion‑exchange chromatography and solvent extraction. In cation‑exchange resins the smaller, later lanthanoids are retained more strongly, allowing elution in order of increasing atomic number. In solvent extraction, the distribution ratio of a lanthanoid between an aqueous phase and an organic phase containing a chelating extractant (e.g., HDEHP) varies smoothly with radius, enabling fractional separation. For the actinoids, similar principles apply, but the presence of multiple oxidation states adds a redox‑based separation dimension, as seen in the PUREX process where U(VI) and Pu(IV) are selectively extracted into tributyl phosphate.

Example
A sample contains a mixture of Eu³⁺ and Yb³⁺ ions. Explain how the difference in their 4f electron configurations influences their relative stability in the +2 oxidation state and predict which ion is more readily reduced to the +2 state under mild reducing conditions.
Europium has a 4f⁷ configuration in the +2 state (Eu²⁺: [Xe] 4f⁷) which corresponds to a half‑filled f subshell, a particularly stable arrangement due to exchange energy. Ytterbium in the +2 state attains a 4f¹⁴ configuration (Yb²⁺: [Xe] 4f¹⁴), a fully filled subshell that is also highly stable. However, the reduction potential for Eu³⁺/Eu²⁺ (E° ≈ –0.35 V) is less negative than that for Yb³⁺/Yb²⁺ (E° ≈ –1.05 V), indicating that Eu³⁺ is more easily reduced to Eu²⁺ under mild conditions. Therefore, europium is the ion more readily reduced to the +2 state.
Tip
When comparing redox behaviour of lanthanoids, always check the f‑electron count of the reduced ion; half‑filled (f⁷) and fully‑filled (f¹⁴) configurations give extra stability, but the actual reduction potential determines which ion is reduced more easily.

Coordination Chemistry: Nomenclature, Isomerism and Bonding

3.1 IUPAC nomenclature rules for coordination compounds

The systematic naming of coordination compounds follows a set of hierarchical rules: the cationic part is named before the anionic part; ligands are listed alphabetically ignoring multiplicative prefixes (di‑, tri‑, tetra‑); anionic ligands receive an –o suffix (chloro, cyano, oxalato) while neutral ligands retain their common names (aqua, ammine, carbonyl). The oxidation state of the central metal is indicated in Roman numerals in parentheses immediately after the metal name. For example, [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂ is named pentaamminechloridocobalt(III) chloride. Bridging ligands are indicated by the prefix μ‑, and the overall charge of a complex ion is shown as a superscript on the formula.

3.2 Types of isomerism: structural, geometrical and optical

Coordination compounds exhibit several classes of isomerism. Structural isomers differ in the connectivity of atoms: ionisation isomers (e.g., [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br vs [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄), hydrate isomers (different numbers of water molecules inside vs outside the coordination sphere), linkage isomers (ambidentate ligands such as SCN⁻ binding through S or N), and coordination isomers (distribution of ligands between cationic and anionic metal centres in a salt). Geometrical isomerism arises in square‑planar (cis/trans) and octahedral (cis/trans, fac/mer) complexes when ligands of different identity occupy distinct positions. Optical isomerism occurs when a complex lacks any improper rotation axis, giving rise to enantiomeric pairs (Δ and Λ) that rotate plane‑polarised light in opposite directions; classic examples are [M(AA)₃]ⁿ⁺ (AA = bidentate) and cis‑[M(AA)₂X₂].

3.3 Ligand field theory versus valence bond theory

Valence bond theory (VBT) describes metal‑ligand bonding by hybridisation of metal orbitals (sp³, dsp², d²sp³) to accommodate ligand donor pairs, and it accounts for magnetic properties by invoking inner‑orbital (low‑spin) versus outer‑orbital (high‑spin) complexes. Ligand field theory (LFT), an extension of crystal field theory, treats the metal‑ligand interaction as a perturbation of the metal d orbitals by the ligand field, incorporating covalent character through the nephelauxetic effect. LFT provides quantitative predictions of d‑d transition energies, magnetic moments, and the spectrochemical series, whereas VBT is largely qualitative and cannot explain the intensity of absorption bands or the variation of Δ with ligand type.

3.4 Chelate effect and thermodynamic stability of chelates

When a multidentate ligand replaces an equivalent number of monodentate ligands, the equilibrium constant for the substitution is markedly larger; this is the chelate effect. The origin is primarily entropic: the reaction increases the number of free particles in solution (e.g., 3 en + M²⁺ → [M(en)₃]²⁺ releases three NH₃ molecules), leading to a positive ΔS°. Additionally, the formation of five‑ or six‑membered chelate rings minimises ring strain and maximises orbital overlap, giving an enthalpic contribution. The overall stability constant (β) for a chelate complex can be orders of magnitude higher than for the corresponding monodentate complex, a principle exploited in analytical chemistry (EDTA titrations) and in the design of metallodrugs.

Crystal Field Theory: Octahedral, Tetrahedral and Square Planar Complexes

4.1 Splitting of d orbitals in an octahedral field and the magnitude of Δ₀

In an octahedral environment the five degenerate d orbitals split into a lower‑energy t₂g set (dxy, dyz, dzx) and a higher‑energy e_g set (dz², dx²‑y²). The energy gap Δ₀ (10 Dq) depends on the metal oxidation state, the nature of the ligand, and the metal‑ligand distance. For a given metal ion, Δ₀ increases with ligand field strength according to the spectrochemical series (I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < CN⁻ < CO). Higher oxidation states and 4d/5d metals give larger Δ₀ because of greater metal‑ligand orbital overlap and reduced shielding. The magnitude of Δ₀ determines whether a d⁴–d⁷ ion adopts a high‑spin or low‑spin configuration; if Δ₀ > P (pairing energy) the electrons pair in t₂g, giving low‑spin complexes.

4.2 Tetrahedral splitting, the relationship Δₜ ≈ 4/9 Δ₀ and high‑spin preference

In a tetrahedral field the d orbitals split into a lower e set (dz², dx²‑y²) and an upper t₂ set (dxy, dyz, dzx). Because only four ligands approach the metal, the ligand field is weaker and the splitting Δₜ is roughly 4/9 of the octahedral Δ₀ for the same metal‑ligand combination. Consequently Δₜ is almost always smaller than the pairing energy, so tetrahedral complexes are invariably high‑spin regardless of ligand strength. The reduced coordination number also leads to longer metal‑ligand bonds and smaller LFSE, which explains why tetrahedral geometry is favoured for d⁰, d⁵ (high‑spin) and d¹⁰ ions where LFSE is zero or minimal.

4.3 Square planar geometry, strong field ligands and low‑spin d⁸ configurations

Square planar complexes arise most commonly for d⁸ metal ions (Ni²⁺, Pd²⁺, Pt²⁺, Au³⁺) with strong‑field ligands such as CN⁻, CO, or phosphines. The d‑orbital splitting in a square planar field places the dx²‑y² orbital at the highest energy because it points directly at the four ligands, while the dxy orbital is lowest. The large gap between dx²‑y² and the next set forces the eight d electrons to occupy the lower four orbitals, giving a diamagnetic low‑spin configuration. This geometry is also favoured by the large LFSE gain (≈ 1.7 Δ₀) and by the ability of 4d and 5d metals to form strong π‑backbonding interactions with ligands such as CO, stabilising the planar arrangement.

Applications and Spectroscopic Behaviour of d‑ and f‑Block Complexes

5.1 Colour, absorption spectra and the spectrochemical series

The colour of a transition metal complex originates from d‑d transitions that promote an electron from a lower‑energy d orbital to a higher‑energy one; the wavelength absorbed corresponds to the crystal field splitting Δ. Because Δ varies systematically with ligand type, the spectrochemical series provides a predictive tool: complexes with weak‑field ligands (e.g., [Ti(H₂O)₆]³⁺) absorb in the red region and appear violet, whereas strong‑field complexes (e.g., [Co(CN)₆]³⁻) absorb in the blue‑green and appear orange‑red. Charge‑transfer bands (ligand‑to‑metal or metal‑to‑ligand) are usually much more intense and can dominate the visible spectrum, as seen in the deep purple of permanganate (MnO₄⁻) where an O 2p → Mn 3d transition occurs.

5.2 Magnetic susceptibility measurements and spin‑only formula

Experimental magnetic moments are obtained from molar magnetic susceptibility (χ_M) using the Gouy or Evans method. For many high‑spin first‑row complexes the measured moment closely matches the spin‑only value μₛₒ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons. Deviations arise from orbital angular momentum contributions (especially for ions with more than half‑filled d shells such as Co²⁺) and from spin‑orbit coupling, which become significant for heavier 4d/5d metals and for lanthanoids where the 4f orbitals retain considerable orbital character. In lanthanoid complexes the magnetic moment is better described by the Russell‑Saunders coupling scheme, giving values that are largely independent of the ligand field.

5.3 Role of transition metal complexes in catalysis, medicine and materials

Transition metal complexes are indispensable in homogeneous catalysis: the Monsanto acetic acid process uses a rhodium carbonyl iodide catalyst that cycles between Rh(I) and Rh(III); the Ziegler‑Natta polymerisation employs TiCl₄/AlEt₃ to generate active Ti‑alkyl sites for olefin insertion. In medicine, platinum(II) complexes such as cisplatin and its derivatives (carboplatin, oxaliplatin) exploit square planar geometry and ligand exchange kinetics to bind DNA and inhibit cancer cell division. Gold(I) phosphine complexes are investigated as anti‑arthritic agents. In materials science, metal‑organic frameworks (MOFs) built from Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺ or Zr⁴⁺ nodes and organic linkers provide porous structures for gas storage, separation and sensing. Lanthanoid‑doped phosphors (e.g., Eu³⁺ in Y₂O₃) give red emission for display technologies, while actinoid complexes such as uranyl nitrate are central to nuclear fuel cycles. The unifying theme across these applications is the ability of d‑ and f‑block elements to adopt variable coordination numbers, oxidation states and electronic configurations that can be tuned by ligand design.
