What is Organic Chemistry?

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon-containing compounds, their structure, properties, reactions, and synthesis. For NEET, key topics include IUPAC nomenclature, isomerism, reaction mechanisms (SN1, SN2, E1, E2), functional group transformations, hydrocarbons, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, and biomolecules. Understanding reaction mechanisms and electron movement is crucial.

Key Concepts

IUPAC Nomenclature: Identify the longest carbon chain, number the carbons to give the lowest locants to substituents, name substituents alphabetically. Isomerism: Structural isomers (chain, position, functional group) and stereoisomers (geometrical and optical). Reaction Mechanisms: SN1 (unimolecular, carbocation intermediate, racemization) and SN2 (bimolecular, backside attack, inversion). Markovnikov's Rule: In addition of HX to alkenes, the hydrogen adds to the carbon with more hydrogen atoms. Electrophilic Substitution: Benzene undergoes electrophilic substitution reactions (nitration, halogenation, sulfonation).

Solved Examples

Example 1
What is the IUPAC name of CH3-CH(CH3)-CH2-COOH?

a) 3-methylbutanoic acid b) 2-methylbutanoic acid c) 3-methylpentanoic acid d) 2-methylpentanoic acid
Solution: The longest chain containing the carboxylic acid group has 4 carbons (butanoic acid). Numbering starts from the carbon of COOH. The methyl group is on carbon 3. So the name is 3-methylbutanoic acid. Hence option (a) is correct.
Example 2
Which of the following shows optical isomerism?

a) CH3OH b) CH3CH(OH)COOH c) CH3COOH d) CCl4
Solution: Optical isomerism occurs in molecules with a chiral carbon (carbon with four different groups). In CH3CH(OH)COOH, the central carbon (CH) has four different groups: H, OH, CH3, and COOH. Hence option (b) is correct.
Example 3
When propene reacts with HBr in the presence of peroxide, the product is:

a) 1-bromopropane b) 2-bromopropane c) 1,2-dibromopropane d) 1,3-dibromopropane
Solution: In the presence of peroxide, the addition of HBr to alkenes follows anti-Markovnikov rule (peroxide effect). The Br adds to the terminal carbon. So propene forms 1-bromopropane. Hence option (a) is correct.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For distinguishing SN1 vs SN2: SN1 favors tertiary substrates and polar protic solvents; SN2 favors primary substrates and polar aprotic solvents.
  • For geometrical isomerism, if two identical groups are on the same side of the double bond, it is cis; on opposite sides, it is trans.
  • In naming organic compounds, aldehydes and carboxylic acids always get priority in numbering (lowest locant).
  • For the peroxide effect, remember that it only works with HBr, not HCl or HI.

Pericyclic Reactions: Cycloadditions, Electrocyclic Processes, and Sigmatropic Shifts

Pericyclic reactions proceed through a single, concerted cyclic transition state in which bonding changes occur simultaneously. The three principal classes — cycloadditions, electrocyclic reactions, and sigmatropic rearrangements — are governed by the Woodward–Hoffmann rules, which correlate the number of π‑electrons, the reaction mode (thermal or photochemical), and the stereochemical pathway (suprafacial or antarafacial). A [4+2] cycloaddition (Diels–Alder reaction) involves a diene (4 π electrons) and a dienophile (2 π electrons). Under thermal conditions the reaction is suprafacial on both components, giving a concerted, stereospecific formation of a six‑membered ring. The endo rule predicts that substituents on the dienophile orient themselves under the diene π‑system in the transition state, leading to the endo product as the kinetic major product. Photochemical [2+2] cycloadditions, by contrast, proceed suprafacial on one component and antarafacial on the other, a pathway that is symmetry‑allowed only under irradiation.

Electrocyclic reactions involve the ring‑opening or ring‑closing of a conjugated π‑system. The stereochemical outcome (conrotatory vs disrotatory) depends on the number of π‑electrons and the reaction conditions. For a 4 π‑electron system (e.g., cyclobutene → butadiene) thermal ring‑opening is conrotatory, whereas photochemical opening is disrotatory. For a 6 π‑electron system (e.g., 1,3,5‑hexatriene → cyclohexadiene) the thermal process is disrotatory and the photochemical process conrotatory. These predictions follow directly from the conservation of orbital symmetry.

Sigmatropic rearrangements entail the migration of a σ‑bond adjacent to a π‑system with simultaneous shift of the π‑bonds. The order [i,j] indicates the number of atoms over which the σ‑bond migrates. The [3,3]‑Claisen rearrangement of allyl vinyl ethers and the [3,3]‑Cope rearrangement of 1,5‑dienes are classic examples. Both proceed through a six‑membered, chair‑like transition state; the stereochemistry at the migrating centres is retained. The [1,5]‑hydrogen shift in pentadienyl systems is thermally allowed suprafacially, whereas a [1,3]‑shift would require an antarafacial component and is therefore forbidden under thermal conditions.

Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis provides a practical shortcut: the interaction between the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of one reactant and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the other dictates feasibility. In a normal‑electron‑demand Diels–Alder reaction the diene HOMO interacts with the dienophile LUMO; electron‑withdrawing groups on the dienophile lower its LUMO and accelerate the reaction. Inverse‑electron‑demand variants operate with a diene bearing electron‑withdrawing groups and a dienophile bearing electron‑donating groups, reversing the orbital energy alignment.

Understanding these symmetry‑controlled pathways enables prediction of product stereochemistry without resorting to stepwise ionic mechanisms. Examination questions frequently test the ability to apply the Woodward–Hoffmann selection rules to unfamiliar substrates, to distinguish between thermal and photochemical outcomes, and to rationalise the endo/exo selectivity in Diels–Alder adducts.

Cahn‑Ingold‑Prelog Priority Rules and Absolute Configuration Assignment

The Cahn‑Ingold‑Prelog (CIP) system provides an unambiguous method for assigning absolute configuration (R or S) to chiral centres and for describing double‑bond geometry (E/Z). The first step is to assign priority to the four substituents attached to the stereogenic atom based on atomic number: the higher the atomic number of the directly attached atom, the higher the priority. If a tie occurs, the substituent atoms are compared in descending order of atomic number along the chain (the “first point of difference” rule). For isotopes, the heavier isotope receives higher priority. Multiple bonds are treated by duplicating the attached atom: a carbonyl carbon is considered attached to two oxygens and one carbon.

Once priorities 1 > 2 > > 3 > 4 are established, the molecule is oriented so that the lowest‑priority group (4) points away from the observer. The sequence 1 → 2 → 3 is then traced: a clockwise arrangement corresponds to the R (rectus) configuration, a counter‑clockwise arrangement to the S (sinister) configuration. If the lowest‑priority group is directed toward the observer, the observed sense is reversed. For molecules containing more than one stereogenic centre, each centre is assigned independently; the overall stereochemical descriptor (e.g., (2R,3S)) lists the configurations in numerical order of the locants.

Double‑bond geometry uses the same priority rules on each sp² carbon. The higher‑priority substituents on each carbon are identified; if they lie on the same side of the double bond the configuration is Z (zusammen), if on opposite sides it is E (entgegen). In cumulenes and allenes, axial chirality is described by the same CIP principles applied to the orthogonal π‑systems.

Common pitfalls include mis‑assigning priority when a substituent contains a heteroatom further down the chain (e.g., –CH₂OH vs –CH₂CH₃) and forgetting to treat a double bond as two phantom atoms. Practice with increasingly complex substituents — such as –CH=CH₂, –C≡CH, –SO₂R, and –PO₃R₂ — builds confidence for exam‑level problems where the stereocentre is embedded in a polycyclic framework.

Absolute configuration is not merely a naming convention; it correlates directly with optical rotation only in a minority of cases. The (R)‑enantiomer may be dextrorotatory (+) or levorotatory (–) depending on the molecule. Therefore, experimental optical rotation cannot be used to deduce configuration without a known reference or computational prediction.

Technique
Predict the major stereochemical outcome of the thermal Diels–Alder reaction between cyclopentadiene and methyl acrylate. Indicate the configuration (endo/exo) and assign the absolute configuration at the newly formed bridgehead stereocentres using CIP rules.
The reaction is a normal‑electron‑demand [4+2] cycloaddition. Under thermal conditions the suprafacial‑suprafacial pathway is symmetry‑allowed. The endo transition state places the electron‑withdrawing ester group under the diene π‑system, giving the endo adduct as the kinetic product. In the bicyclo[2.2.1]hept‑5‑ene‑2‑carboxylate product, the two bridgehead carbons (C‑1 and C‑4) become stereogenic. Assign priorities: at each bridgehead, the substituent bearing the carbonyl carbon (C=O) outranks the bridge carbon bearing only carbon atoms. With the lowest‑priority hydrogen oriented away, the 1 → 2 → 3 sequence proceeds clockwise at C‑1 (R) and counter‑clockwise at C‑4 (S). Thus the major product is the endo‑(1R,4S) adduct.
Exam‑day strategy
When a pericyclic question appears, first count π‑electrons and note thermal vs photochemical conditions; then apply the Woodward–Hoffmann table (4n + 2 → suprafacial‑suprafacial thermal, 4n → suprafacial‑antarafacial thermal). For CIP assignments, write the substituent atomic numbers explicitly; a quick sketch with phantom atoms for multiple bonds eliminates the most common ranking errors.

Organometallic Reagents: Grignard, Organolithium, and Cuprate Chemistry

Organomagnesium (Grignard) and organolithium reagents are among the most versatile nucleophiles in synthetic organic chemistry. Both are prepared by oxidative insertion of magnesium or lithium into an alkyl or aryl halide in anhydrous ether (Grignard) or hydrocarbon (organolithium) solvents. The carbon–metal bond is highly polarised toward carbon, rendering the reagent a strong base (pKₐ of conjugate acid ≈ 50) and a powerful nucleophile. Grignard reagents (RMgX) typically exist as Schlenk equilibria (RMgX ⇌ R₂Mg + MgX₂) in solution, whereas organolithium compounds (RLi) aggregate into tetramers or hexamers in non‑coordinating solvents, affecting reactivity and selectivity.

Both reagent classes add to carbonyl groups (aldehydes, ketones, esters, acid chlorides) to give alcohols after aqueous work‑up. With esters and acid chlorides, two equivalents add, furnishing tertiary alcohols. They also perform nucleophilic addition to epoxides (ring‑opening at the less hindered carbon), to carbon dioxide (carboxylation), and to nitriles (after hydrolysis, ketones). However, their strong basicity leads to side reactions: deprotonation of acidic protons (α‑to carbonyl, terminal alkynes, phenols) and β‑hydride elimination from alkyl halides (Wurtz‑type coupling). Protecting groups or the use of less basic cuprates can circumvent these problems.

Lithium dialkylcuprates (Gilman reagents, R₂CuLi) are prepared by treating two equivalents of organolithium with copper(I) iodide. They are softer nucleophiles, displaying conjugate (1,4‑) addition to α,β‑unsaturated carbonyl compounds (Michael addition) and undergoing SN2‑type coupling with alkyl halides, vinyl halides, and aryl halides (including heteroaryl). Cuprates do not add to saturated carbonyls under normal conditions, providing chemoselectivity. Higher‑order cuprates (R₂Cu(CN)Li₂) further enhance reactivity toward hindered substrates and enable cross‑coupling with aryl triflates.

Transmetalation between organolithium or Grignard reagents and transition‑metal salts underlies many catalytic cycles (e.g., Negishi, Kumada, and Suzuki couplings). Understanding the aggregation state, solvent coordination, and the hard/soft character of the nucleophile allows the chemist to choose the appropriate reagent for a given transformation and to predict competing pathways such as β‑hydride elimination, homocoupling, or protonolysis.

Retrosynthetic Analysis: Disconnection Approach and Strategic Bond Formation

Retrosynthetic analysis, formalised by E. J. Corey, deconstructs a target molecule into simpler precursors by imaginary bond cleavages (disconnections) that correspond to known forward reactions. The process is iterative: each disconnection generates synthons, which are then converted into commercially available or easily prepared reagents (synthetic equivalents). Key concepts include the identification of functional‑group interconversions (FGI), the use of strategic bonds (those whose formation simplifies the skeleton), and the application of symmetry to reduce the number of steps.

A classic example is the retrosynthesis of a β‑hydroxy ketone. The C–C bond β to the carbonyl can be disconnected via an aldol reaction, yielding an aldehyde and a ketone enolate as synthons. The enolate synthetic equivalent is the corresponding ketone under basic conditions; the aldehyde is a direct reagent. If the target contains a quaternary centre, a Michael addition disconnection (1,4‑addition to an α,β‑unsaturated carbonyl) may be more appropriate. For aromatic targets, a Friedel‑Crafts acylation followed by reduction (Clemmensen or Wolff‑Kishner) is a standard two‑step sequence that avoids carbocation rearrangements inherent in direct alkylation.

Protecting‑group strategy is integral to retrosynthesis. When a molecule contains multiple reactive functionalities (e.g., an alcohol and an amine), orthogonal protecting groups (silyl ethers for alcohols, Boc for amines) allow selective deprotection. The retrosynthetic plan must anticipate the order of installation and removal to minimise steps and avoid protecting‑group incompatibility.

Modern retrosynthetic software (e.g., ASKCOS, RetroPath) uses reaction databases and machine‑learning models to propose routes, but the chemist’s intuition regarding stereochemical control, chemoselectivity, and scalability remains decisive. Examination questions often present a complex target and ask for a plausible three‑step retrosynthetic scheme, requiring the student to recognise key disconnections (e.g., a Diels–Alder adduct, a Claisen rearrangement precursor, or a Suzuki coupling partner) and to propose suitable reagents for each forward step.

Spectroscopic Methods for Structure Elucidation: IR, NMR, and Mass Spectrometry

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy identifies functional groups through characteristic vibrational frequencies. The carbonyl stretch appears as a strong band near 1700 cm⁻¹; its exact position shifts with conjugation (α,β‑unsaturated ≈ 1680 cm⁻¹), hydrogen bonding (carboxylic acids ≈ 1710 cm⁻¹ broad), and ring strain (cyclobutanone ≈ 1780 cm⁻¹). The broad O–H stretch of alcohols and phenols (3200–3600 cm⁻¹) and the sharp N–H stretch of amines (3300–3500 cm⁻¹) are diagnostic. Absence of a carbonyl band in a molecule expected to contain one signals either a non‑carbonyl functional group or a symmetric environment that renders the stretch IR‑inactive.

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H NMR) provides the number, chemical shift, integration, and coupling pattern of hydrogen nuclei. Typical chemical‑shift ranges: alkyl 0.9–1.5 ppm, allylic/benzylic 1.6–2.2 ppm, α‑to heteroatom 3.0–4.5 ppm, aromatic 6.5–8.0 ppm, aldehydic 9–10 ppm, carboxylic acid 10–12 ppm. Coupling constants (J) reveal dihedral angles: vicinal trans‑alkene J ≈ 15–18 Hz, cis‑alkene J ≈ 10–12 Hz, axial‑axial in cyclohexane J ≈ 12–14 Hz. Multiplicity follows the n + 1 rule for equivalent neighbours; non‑equivalent neighbours give doublets of doublets, etc. ¹³C NMR (broadband decoupled) yields the number of distinct carbon environments; DEPT experiments differentiate CH₃, CH₂, CH, and quaternary carbons.

Mass spectrometry (MS) determines molecular weight and fragmentation pattern. Electron‑impact (EI) ionisation produces a molecular ion (M⁺·) for many volatile compounds; electrospray (ESI) and atmospheric‑pressure chemical ionisation (APCI) give protonated [M+H]⁺ or deprotonated [M–H]⁻ ions for polar, non‑volatile molecules. The nitrogen rule states that a molecule with an odd number of nitrogens has an odd‑mass molecular ion. Isotopic patterns (Cl, Br, S) aid elemental composition assignment. Common fragments: α‑cleavage next to heteroatoms, McLafferty rearrangement (γ‑hydrogen transfer to a carbonyl), and loss of small neutrals (H₂O, CO, CH₃·).

Combined interpretation — matching IR functional groups, NMR connectivity and stereochemistry, and MS molecular formula — yields a complete structure. Examination problems frequently provide a set of spectral data for an unknown C₈H₁₀O₂ and ask for the structure; success requires systematic correlation of each datum rather than reliance on a single technique.

  • Assuming a Diels–Alder reaction always gives the endo product under thermal conditions without checking for steric hindrance that may favour the exo pathway.
  • Misapplying CIP priority by treating a double bond as a single attachment instead of duplicating the attached atom.
  • Using a Grignard reagent on a substrate containing an unprotected acidic proton (e.g., phenol, terminal alkyne) and expecting carbonyl addition.
  • Choosing a cuprate for direct addition to a saturated ketone, where it is unreactive, instead of a Grignard or organolithium.
  • Proposing a retrosynthetic disconnection that creates a high‑energy intermediate (e.g., a primary carbocation) without a viable forward reaction.
  • Ignoring the nitrogen rule or halogen isotopic pattern when deducing molecular formula from mass‑spectrometric data.
  • Overlooking the effect of solvent coordination on organolithium aggregation, leading to incorrect predictions of reactivity and selectivity.

Asymmetric Synthesis and Chiral Catalysis

Asymmetric synthesis creates a single enantiomer or diastereomer in excess, a requirement for pharmaceuticals where the biological activity often resides in one absolute configuration. The enantiomeric excess (ee) is defined as ee = (|R − S| / (R + S)) × 100 %. Catalytic asymmetric methods are preferred over stoichiometric chiral auxiliaries because they operate at sub‑stoichiometric loadings (0.1–10 mol %). Key catalyst classes include chiral transition‑metal complexes (Rh, Ru, Ir, Pd) bearing C₂‑symmetric diphosphine ligands such as BINAP, DuPhos, or SegPhos; chiral Lewis acids (Ti‑TADDOLate, Cu‑bisoxazoline) that activate carbonyls for enantioselective additions; and organocatalysts (proline‑derived secondary amines, cinchona alkaloid‑based phase‑transfer catalysts) that operate via enamine, iminium, or hydrogen‑bonding activation. The Sharpless asymmetric dihydroxylation (AD‑mix‑α/β) and the Sharpless epoxidation (Ti(OiPr)₄, (+)‑ or (−)‑DET) remain benchmark reactions for allylic alcohols and trans‑disubstituted alkenes, delivering >95 % ee under mild conditions. Noyori’s Ru‑BINAP/diamine catalysts achieve asymmetric hydrogenation of β‑ketoesters and imines with turnover numbers >10⁵. Modern developments include photoredox‑enabled enantioselective radical couplings (e.g., Ir‑phenanthroline complexes with chiral phosphoric acids) and nickel‑catalyzed cross‑electrophile coupling using chiral bisoxazoline ligands. Reaction optimisation focuses on ligand bite angle, electronic tuning, and counter‑ion effects; small changes can invert the sense of induction. For examination purposes, students must recognise the catalytic cycle (oxidative addition, migratory insertion, reductive elimination for metals; enamine formation, C‑C bond formation, hydrolysis for organocatalysis) and predict the absolute configuration of the product using the quadrant model for BINAP‑type ligands or the Houk‑List transition‑state model for proline catalysis.

Photochemical Reactions and Norrish Type Processes

Photochemistry accesses excited‑state potential energy surfaces that are unavailable thermally, enabling reactions such as cycloadditions, fragmentations, and isomerisations. The primary photophysical step is absorption of a photon (λ ≈ 200–400 nm) promoting a molecule to a singlet excited state (S₁); intersystem crossing (ISC) can populate the triplet state (T₁), which is longer‑lived and often the reactive species. Norrish type I cleavage involves α‑scission of a carbonyl compound from the n→π* excited state, generating an acyl radical and an alkyl radical; the subsequent decarbonylation of the acyl radical yields a second alkyl radical and CO. Norrish type II proceeds via a six‑membered cyclic transition state in which a γ‑hydrogen is abstracted by the carbonyl oxygen, forming a 1,4‑biradical that can undergo cyclisation to a cyclobutanol or fragmentation to an alkene and an enol. The competition between type I and type II depends on carbonyl substitution (α‑branching favours type I), solvent polarity, and wavelength. The Paterno‑Büchi reaction, a [2+2] cycloaddition between an excited carbonyl and an alkene, furnishes oxetanes with regio‑ and stereochemical control dictated by the alignment of the carbonyl π* orbital and the alkene π orbital. Photo‑induced electron transfer (PET) using aromatic ketones (benzophenone, thioxanthone) as sensitisers enables radical ion formation for C‑C bond construction under visible light. In the laboratory, quartz vessels, nitrogen degassing, and cut‑off filters (λ > 300 nm) are standard to avoid side reactions. Examination questions often present a substrate with multiple carbonyls and ask which Norrish pathway dominates; the answer requires drawing the relevant six‑membered transition state and evaluating the stability of the resulting radicals.

Worked Example: Predicting Norrish Outcome
A student is given 2‑methyl‑1‑phenyl‑1‑propanone (Ph‑CO‑CH₂‑CH(CH₃)₂) and asked to predict the major photoproduct upon irradiation at 300 nm in hexane.
The carbonyl is aryl‑alkyl; the α‑carbon bears a phenyl group that stabilises the acyl radical, favouring Norrish type I α‑cleavage. The cleavage yields Ph‑CO· (benzoyl radical) and ·CH₂‑CH(CH₃)₂ (a secondary alkyl radical). The benzoyl radical rapidly loses CO to give a phenyl radical, which abstracts hydrogen from solvent to form benzene. The secondary alkyl radical can dimerise or abstract hydrogen to give 2‑methyl‑1‑propane. Norrish type II would require a γ‑hydrogen on a six‑membered ring; the γ‑carbon is a quaternary centre (no H), so type II is impossible. Therefore the major products are benzene, CO, and 2‑methyl‑propane (isobutane) derived from the alkyl radical.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a photochemistry question appears, first identify the excited state (singlet vs triplet) and the presence of γ‑hydrogens. Sketch the six‑membered cyclic transition state for Norrish II; if a γ‑hydrogen is absent or sterically hindered, default to Norrish I. Remember that aryl carbonyls undergo rapid decarbonylation of the acyl radical, a frequent source of marks.

Heterocyclic Chemistry: Five‑ and Six‑Membered Rings

Heterocycles constitute the core of most bioactive molecules. Five‑membered aromatic heterocycles (pyrrole, furan, thiophene) possess 6 π‑electrons (4 from the two double bonds + 2 from the heteroatom lone pair) and are π‑excessive, undergoing electrophilic substitution at the 2‑position preferentially. Pyrrole’s nitrogen lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet, making the N‑H acidic (pKₐ ≈ 17) and the ring susceptible to polymerisation under strong acid; protection as N‑Boc or N‑Ts is common. Furan is less aromatic (resonance energy ≈ 16 kcal mol⁻¹) and behaves as a diene in Diels‑Alder reactions, giving oxabicyclic adducts that can be opened to 1,4‑dicarbonyls. Thiophene is more aromatic (≈ 29 kcal mol⁻¹) and undergoes electrophilic substitution at C‑2; the sulfur can be oxidised to the sulfone, which activates the ring toward nucleophilic substitution. Six‑membered heterocycles (pyridine, pyrimidine, pyrazine) are π‑deficient; the nitrogen withdraws electron density, directing electrophilic attack to the 3‑ and 5‑positions while nucleophilic attack occurs at C‑2 and C‑4 (Chichibabin reaction). Pyridine N‑oxide restores electron density, allowing electrophilic substitution at the 4‑position. Fused systems such as indole (benzopyrrole) and quinoline (benzopyridine) combine the reactivity patterns of the constituent rings; indole undergoes electrophilic substitution at C‑3 (the most nucleophilic carbon) and can be alkylated via the Vilsmeier–Haack formylation at C‑3. Synthesis of these heterocycles relies on classic named reactions: Paal‑Knorr (pyrroles from 1,4‑dicarbonyls), Knorr pyrrole synthesis (β‑ketoesters + α‑amino ketones), Hantzsch pyridine synthesis (β‑ketoesters + ammonia + aldehydes), and the Bischler–Napieralski cyclisation for isoquinolines. Modern methods include metal‑catalysed C‑H activation (Pd, Rh) for direct arylation of heterocycles and photoredox‑mediated Minisci-type alkylation of electron‑deficient heterocycles. For examinations, students must be able to draw the mechanistic arrows for each named reaction, predict regioselectivity based on heteroatom electronics, and recognise the effect of N‑oxidation or protection on reactivity.

Neighboring Group Participation and Anchimeric Assistance

Neighboring group participation (NGP) occurs when a nearby σ‑bond or lone pair stabilises a developing carbocation or transition state, accelerating the reaction and often dictating stereochemistry. Classic examples include the acetolysis of 2‑aryl‑ethyl tosylates where the aryl π‑system forms a phenonium ion, leading to retention of configuration at the reacting carbon. In the solvolysis of β‑acetoxy‑alkyl halides, the carbonyl oxygen attacks the carbon bearing the leaving group, generating a cyclic acyloxonium ion; subsequent nucleophilic attack opens the ring with overall retention (double inversion). The participation of a sulfur atom (thioether) is even stronger due to the polarizable 3p orbitals, giving rate enhancements of 10³–10⁴. NGP also manifests in the hydrolysis of cyclic sulfates and in the rearrangement of epoxides under acidic conditions (the “anchimeric assistance” of the epoxide oxygen). The stereochemical outcome is diagnostic: a substrate that would normally undergo SN2 inversion instead shows retention or a mixture if a neighboring group forms a bridged intermediate. Kinetic isotope effects (KIE) and Hammett ρ values provide experimental evidence; a large negative ρ (≈ −4) indicates strong cationic character stabilised by the neighboring group. In synthesis, NGP is exploited to achieve stereocontrolled glycosylations (participating 2‑O‑acyl groups give 1,2‑trans glycosides) and to direct ring‑opening of epoxides to anti‑diols. Examination problems often present a substrate with a potential participating group (e.g., a β‑silyl ether) and ask whether the reaction proceeds with retention or inversion; the answer requires drawing the cyclic intermediate and counting the number of inversions.

Green Chemistry Metrics and Sustainable Synthetic Design

Green chemistry evaluates the environmental impact of a synthetic route through quantitative metrics. The atom economy (AE) = (molecular weight of desired product / sum of molecular weights of all reactants) × 100 % measures the fraction of reactant atoms incorporated into the product; a Diels‑Alder reaction has AE = 100 % whereas a Wittig reaction (phosphine oxide by‑product) has AE ≈ 50 %. The E‑factor = (mass of waste / mass of product) includes solvents, reagents, and by‑products; typical pharmaceutical processes have E‑factors of 25–100, while bulk chemicals aim for < 1. The process mass intensity (PMI) = total mass input / mass of product = E‑factor + 1. Reaction mass efficiency (RME) = (mass of product / mass of all reactants) × 100 % combines yield and atom economy. Catalysis, especially heterogeneous or biocatalysis, reduces stoichiometric reagent waste; for example, enzymatic transamination replaces a stoichiometric chiral auxiliary with a catalytic pyridoxal‑5′‑phosphate dependent enzyme. Solvent selection follows the CHEM21 guide: water, supercritical CO₂, 2‑MeTHF, and cyclopentyl methyl ether are preferred over dichloromethane, DMF, or benzene. Energy efficiency is assessed by the reaction temperature and time; microwave or flow chemistry can lower the energy demand by an order of magnitude. Life‑cycle assessment (LCA) extends the analysis to raw‑material extraction, manufacturing, and disposal. In an exam context, a question may give a two‑step sequence (e.g., a Friedel‑Crafts acylation followed by a reduction) and ask to calculate the overall AE, E‑factor, and suggest a greener alternative (e.g., a direct C‑H acylation using a copper catalyst and benign oxidant). Students should be comfortable converting stoichiometric equations into mass balances and interpreting the metrics to rank routes.

  • Assuming that a high atom economy automatically guarantees a low E‑factor; solvent and work‑up waste dominate E‑factor in many lab‑scale reactions.
  • Confusing retention from neighboring group participation with a concerted SN2 mechanism; the key is the formation of a cyclic intermediate that undergoes two inversions.
  • Overlooking the requirement for a γ‑hydrogen in Norrish type II; a quaternary γ‑carbon forces type I cleavage even if the α‑radical is less stable.
  • Predicting electrophilic substitution at C‑2 of pyridine instead of C‑3/5; the nitrogen withdraws electron density, making C‑2/4 electron‑poor.
  • Neglecting the effect of N‑oxidation on pyridine reactivity; N‑oxide directs electrophiles to the 4‑position, a common trap in regioselectivity questions.
  • Using a chiral ligand that gives the wrong absolute configuration because the quadrant model was applied to the wrong enantiomer of the ligand.
  • Failing to account for the mass of the catalyst and its recovery when calculating PMI for a catalytic process; even sub‑stoichiometric amounts contribute to the total mass input.

Metal‑Catalyzed Cross‑Coupling Reactions

General Catalytic Cycle

Cross‑coupling reactions forge carbon–carbon or carbon–heteroatom bonds through a three‑step catalytic cycle: oxidative addition of an electrophile (usually an aryl or vinyl halide or pseudohalide) to a low‑valent transition‑metal complex, transmetalation with a nucleophilic organometallic partner, and reductive elimination to release the coupled product and regenerate the catalyst. Palladium(0) complexes such as Pd(PPh₃)₄ or Pd₂(dba)₃ with phosphine or N‑heterocyclic carbene ligands are the most common catalysts; nickel(0) and copper(I) systems are employed for cheaper or more challenging substrates.

Major Variants

  • Suzuki–Miyaura – aryl/vinyl boronic acids or esters couple with halides using a base (Na₂CO₃, K₃PO₄) in aqueous/organic biphasic media. Tolerates a wide range of functional groups and gives high stereoretention for vinyl boranes.
  • Heck – aryl/vinyl halides undergo migratory insertion into an alkene followed by β‑hydride elimination; no organometallic nucleophile is required. Regioselectivity favors the more substituted alkene carbon; chiral ligands can induce enantioselectivity in intramolecular versions.
  • Negishi – organozinc reagents (R‑ZnX) transmetalate rapidly, allowing coupling of alkyl, alkenyl, and aryl zinc species with minimal β‑hydride elimination.
  • Stille – organostannanes (R‑SnBu₃) are stable and reactive; however, tin by‑products demand careful removal. Fluoride activation (CsF) can accelerate transmetalation.
  • Sonogashira – terminal alkynes couple with halides using a Pd/Cu co‑catalyst system; the copper acetylide forms in situ and transmetalates to palladium.
  • Buchwald–Hartwig – aryl halides couple with amines, amides, or alcohols to form C–N or C–O bonds; bulky biaryl phosphine ligands (XantPhos, BrettPhos) enable coupling of sterically hindered partners.

Key Practical Considerations

Choice of ligand controls oxidative addition rate and reductive elimination selectivity. Electron‑rich, bulky phosphines accelerate oxidative addition of aryl chlorides. Base selection influences transmetalation efficiency; for Suzuki couplings, carbonate bases in aqueous ethanol or dioxane/water are standard. Reaction temperature typically ranges from 25 °C (active aryl iodides) to 100 °C (aryl chlorides). Microwave irradiation can reduce reaction times dramatically. Catalyst loading of 0.5–5 mol % is common; ligand‑to‑metal ratios of 2:1 to 4:1 ensure full coordination.

Carbene and Nitrene Chemistry

Carbenes: Electronic States and Generation

Carbenes are neutral divalent carbon species with six valence electrons. Singlet carbenes possess paired electrons in an sp² orbital and an empty p orbital, making them both nucleophilic (at the lone pair) and electrophilic (at the vacant p). Triplet carbenes have two unpaired electrons in orthogonal orbitals and behave as diradicals. Common precursors include diazo compounds (R₂C=N₂) which lose N₂ upon thermolysis, photolysis, or transition‑metal catalysis (Rh₂(OAc)₄, Cu(I) salts). The Simmons–Smith reagent (CH₂I₂/Zn(Cu)) generates a zinc‑carbenoid that transfers a methylene group to alkenes with retention of alkene geometry.

Typical Carbene Reactions

  • Cyclopropanation – concerted addition to alkenes; singlet carbenes give stereospecific syn addition, triplet carbenes lead to loss of stereochemistry via a diradical intermediate.
  • C–H Insertion – intramolecular insertion into activated C–H bonds (allylic, benzylic, α‑to‑heteroatom) proceeds with retention of configuration at the carbon center.
  • Ylide Formation – reaction with heteroatom lone pairs (O, S, N) gives carbonyl ylides, sulfonium ylides, or ammonium ylides that undergo [2,3]‑sigmatropic rearrangements.

Nitrenes: Generation and Reactivity

Nitrenes are the nitrogen analogues of carbenes (R–N:). They are generated from azides (R–N₃) by thermolysis or photolysis, from N‑haloamines under basic conditions (Hofmann rearrangement), or via metal‑catalysed decomposition of iminoiodinanes (PhI=NR). Singlet nitrenes undergo concerted insertion into C–H bonds and aziridination of alkenes; triplet nitrenes behave as diradicals, giving stepwise pathways with possible loss of stereochemistry. The Curtius rearrangement (acyl azide → isocyanate) proceeds through a nitrene‑like transition state with migration of the acyl group to nitrogen.

Stereochemical Control

Chiral dirhodium(II) carboxylates (Rh₂(S‑DOSP)₄) or copper‑bis(oxazoline) complexes enforce enantioselective cyclopropanation and C–H amination. The catalyst’s chiral pocket directs approach of the substrate to the metal‑carbene or metal‑nitrene intermediate, delivering high enantiomeric excesses (often >95 % ee).

Mechanistic Prediction
A substrate containing a terminal alkene and a benzylic C–H bond is treated with ethyl diazoacetate (N₂CHCO₂Et) and 1 mol % Rh₂(OAc)₄ in dichloromethane at 0 °C. Predict the major product and justify the selectivity.
The rhodium catalyst generates a rhodium‑carbene from ethyl diazoacetate. Singlet carbenes prefer cyclopropanation of electron‑rich alkenes over C–H insertion when both pathways are accessible. The terminal alkene is more nucleophilic than the benzylic C–H bond, and the reaction conditions (low temperature, non‑polar solvent) favor the concerted cyclopropanation. The major product is the cyclopropane bearing an ester substituent on the newly formed three‑membered ring; the benzylic C–H insertion product is a minor side‑product.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question presents a diazo compound with a transition‑metal catalyst, first identify the metal (Rh, Cu, Pd) and its typical carbene reactivity. Rh(II) gives singlet carbenes → cyclopropanation > C–H insertion. Cu(I) often yields triplet‑like behavior → more C–H insertion. Use this hierarchy to eliminate answer choices quickly.

Radical Chain Reactions and Atom Transfer

Chain Mechanism Fundamentals

Radical chain processes consist of initiation (generation of a radical from a stable precursor), propagation (radical reacts with a substrate to give a new radical), and termination (radical–radical combination or disproportionation). Efficient chains require a high propagation‑to‑termination ratio; typical chain lengths range from 10² to 10⁴ cycles. Common initiators: azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN, 60–70 °C), benzoyl peroxide (80–90 °C), and photoinitiators (Irgacure 2959 under UV).

Key Transformations

  • Halogenation – Br₂/hv or NBS/hv abstracts allylic or benzylic hydrogen via a bromine radical; the resulting carbon radical captures Br₂ to give the brominated product and regenerate Br·.
  • Barton–McCombie Deoxygenation – xanthate esters undergo Bu₃SnH‑mediated radical fragmentation to give alkanes; the tin radical abstracts the xanthyl group, generating a carbon radical that abstracts H from Bu₃SnH.
  • Giese Addition – carbon‑centered radicals add to electron‑deficient alkenes (α,β‑unsaturated carbonyls, acrylates); the adduct radical is quenched by H‑atom transfer from a thiol or silane.
  • Atom Transfer Radical Addition (ATRA) – a halogenated compound (R‑X) adds across an alkene via a transition‑metal catalyst (Cu(I)/Cu(II) or Ru) that mediates reversible halogen atom transfer; the process is catalytic in halogen and can be rendered enantioselective with chiral ligands.
  • Atom Transfer Radical Polymerization (ATRP) – controlled radical polymerization using alkyl halide initiators and Cu(I) complexes; the dormant–active equilibrium yields polymers with narrow dispersity (Đ ≈ 1.1–1.2).

Hydrogen‑Atom Transfer (HAT) Agents

Bu₃SnH (BDE ≈ 74 kcal mol⁻¹) is the classic H‑atom donor but is toxic; alternatives include (Me₃Si)₃SiH (TTMSS, BDE ≈ 79 kcal mol⁻¹), thiophenol (PhSH), and tris(trimethylsilyl)silane. The choice of HAT agent influences rate and selectivity: weaker donors favor abstraction from weaker C–H bonds, enabling site‑selective functionalization.

Organic Electrochemistry

Fundamental Electrochemical Steps

Organic electrosynthesis proceeds via electron transfer at an electrode surface. Anodic oxidation generates radical cations (or dications) from electron‑rich substrates (enol ethers, amines, arenes). Cathodic reduction produces radical anions from electron‑deficient substrates (alkyl halides, carbonyl compounds, aryl halides). The primary radical ion can undergo bond cleavage, dimerization, nucleophilic capture, or further electron transfer. Supporting electrolytes (Bu₄NBF₄, LiClO₄) ensure conductivity; solvents such as MeCN, DMF, or MeOH are chosen for wide electrochemical windows.

Classic Named Electrochemical Reactions

  • Kolbe Electrolysis – anodic decarboxylative dimerization of carboxylates (2 RCOO⁻ → R–R + 2 CO₂ + 2 e⁻). Works best for primary and secondary aliphatic acids; β‑substituted acids give elimination side‑products.
  • Non‑Kolbe Oxidation – anodic oxidation of carboxylates bearing α‑heteroatoms yields acyloxyl radicals that fragment to give carbocations trapped by nucleophiles (e.g., solvent).
  • Electroreductive Coupling – cathodic reduction of aryl halides to aryl radicals that couple (Ullmann‑type) or add to alkenes; mediated by Ni or Cu complexes to lower overpotential.
  • Paired Electrolysis – simultaneous anodic oxidation and cathodic reduction in a single cell, converting both half‑reactions into valuable products (e.g., anodic oxidation of benzyl alcohol to benzaldehyde paired with cathodic reduction of acrylonitrile to adiponitrile).

Mediated Electrochemistry and Green Metrics

Redox mediators (TEMPO, quinones, iodide) shuttle electrons between electrode and substrate, enabling reactions at lower potentials and improving selectivity. The atom economy of electrochemical steps is inherently high because electrons replace stoichiometric oxidants/reductants. The E‑factor is reduced when the electrolyte is recycled and the solvent is recovered. Recent advances include flow‑electrolysis cells that enhance mass transport and allow gram‑scale synthesis with minimal electrode fouling.

  • Assuming that all cross‑couplings proceed with complete retention of configuration; oxidative addition can invert stereochemistry at sp³ centers, and transmetalation may proceed with retention or inversion depending on the organometallic nucleophile.
  • Confusing singlet vs triplet carbene reactivity; singlet carbenes give stereospecific cyclopropanation, triplet carbenes lead to loss of stereochemistry via diradical intermediates.
  • Overlooking the need for a suitable hydrogen‑atom donor in radical deoxygenation; without an efficient HAT agent the carbon radical undergoes side reactions such as dimerization or β‑scission.
  • Neglecting the role of the supporting electrolyte in organic electrochemistry; a non‑coordinating electrolyte is essential to avoid undesired nucleophilic capture of radical cations.
  • Treating ATRA and ATRP as identical; ATRA is a stoichiometric addition across a double bond, while ATRP is a catalytic polymerization that relies on a dormant–active equilibrium.
  • Misapplying the Kolbe reaction to aromatic carboxylates; aromatic acids undergo decarboxylation but the resulting aryl radicals preferentially dimerize to biaryls only under specific conditions, often giving complex mixtures.
  • Forgetting that chiral ligands in cross‑coupling must match the oxidative addition geometry; a ligand that enforces a square‑planar Pd(II) intermediate may not induce enantioselectivity in a reaction that proceeds through a tetrahedral Ni(0) species.

These advanced topics provide a deeper mechanistic toolkit for solving complex organic synthesis problems.

Kinetic vs Thermodynamic Control in Organic Reactions

Many organic transformations can proceed through two competing pathways that lead to different products. The product distribution depends on whether the reaction is under kinetic control or thermodynamic control. Under kinetic control, the product that forms fastest — the one with the lowest activation energy — predominates. This situation is typical at low temperatures, with short reaction times, and when the reaction is irreversible. Under thermodynamic control, the more stable product — the one with the lower free energy — predominates. This regime is favored at higher temperatures, longer reaction times, and when the reaction is reversible, allowing equilibration.

Classic Examples

The addition of HBr to 1,3‑butadiene illustrates the principle. At −80 °C the 1,2‑addition product (3‑bromo‑1‑butene) forms rapidly because the allylic cation intermediate is stabilized and the transition state for attack at the terminal carbon is lower in energy. At 40 °C the 1,4‑addition product (1‑bromo‑2‑butene) dominates because the more substituted double bond in the product is thermodynamically more stable. Another textbook case is the alkylation of enolates. Kinetic enolates are generated with a strong, sterically hindered base such as LDA at −78 °C, giving the less substituted enolate which reacts faster with electrophiles. Thermodynamic enolates are formed with a weaker base (e.g., NaOEt) at higher temperature, allowing equilibration to the more substituted, more stable enolate.

Experimental Handles

Temperature is the primary lever: lowering the temperature suppresses the reverse reaction and traps the kinetic product. Reaction time matters because prolonged heating permits the kinetic product to revert to starting material and re‑equilibrate. The choice of solvent can also shift the balance; polar protic solvents often stabilize charged intermediates and accelerate equilibration. Catalysts that lower the barrier for the reverse reaction (e.g., acid or base catalysis) promote thermodynamic control. In practice, chemists often run a reaction at low temperature to isolate the kinetic product, then warm the mixture to see whether the product ratio changes, confirming reversibility.

Implications for Synthesis

Understanding which regime operates allows deliberate selection of conditions to obtain a desired regio‑ or stereoisomer. For example, in the synthesis of a target molecule requiring the less substituted alkene, a kinetic elimination (e.g., using a bulky base such as t‑BuOK at low temperature) is chosen. Conversely, when the more substituted alkene is needed, a thermodynamic elimination (e.g., using a smaller base like EtONa with heating) is preferred. The concept also underlies the use of protecting groups that can be removed under kinetic or thermodynamic conditions, providing orthogonal deprotection strategies.

Protecting Group Strategies in Multistep Synthesis

Complex molecule construction frequently requires temporary masking of reactive functional groups to prevent undesired side reactions. A protecting group must be installed efficiently, remain stable under the conditions of subsequent steps, and be removable selectively without affecting other functionalities. The choice of protecting group is guided by the orthogonal stability profile: acid‑labile, base‑labile, hydrogenolysis‑labile, fluoride‑labile, and oxidatively or reductively cleavable groups.

Common Protecting Groups and Their Orthogonality

Silyl ethers (TMS, TES, TBS, TIPS) are cleaved by fluoride sources (TBAF, HF·pyridine) and are stable to many basic and nucleophilic conditions, but labile to strong acid. Benzyl ethers and esters are removed by catalytic hydrogenolysis (H₂, Pd/C) and survive acidic and basic media. Acetals and ketals (e.g., dioxolanes) are acid‑labile but stable to base and nucleophiles. tert‑Butyl esters and carbamates (Boc) are cleaved by strong acid (TFA, HCl in EtOAc) while resisting base and hydrogenolysis. Fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (Fmoc) is base‑labile (piperidine) but stable to acid. Allyl ethers and esters can be removed by Pd(0) catalysts under neutral conditions, orthogonal to both acid and base.

Strategic Planning

In a retrosynthetic analysis, protecting groups are introduced at the disconnection stage. The synthetic chemist maps the sequence of reactions and assigns a protecting group to each functional group that must survive the planned transformations. Orthogonal sets are chosen so that each deprotection step can be performed without disturbing the others. For instance, a synthesis containing an alcohol, an amine, and a carboxylic acid might protect the alcohol as a TBS ether (fluoride labile), the amine as a Boc carbamate (acid labile), and the acid as a benzyl ester (hydrogenolysis labile). This three‑tier orthogonality permits sequential deprotection in any order.

Installation and Removal Considerations

Installation should be high‑yielding and scalable. Silylation of alcohols with TBSCl and imidazole proceeds at room temperature in minutes. Benzylation of alcohols uses NaH and BnBr in DMF. Boc protection of amines employs (Boc)₂O with a mild base. Deprotection conditions must be compatible with the rest of the molecule: TBAF can also desilylate sensitive silyl enol ethers; hydrogenolysis may reduce alkenes or remove other benzyl groups; strong acid can cleave acid‑labile protecting groups elsewhere. Therefore, the order of deprotection is often dictated by the most sensitive functionality present.

Modern Trends

Recent developments include photolabile protecting groups (e.g., o‑nitrobenzyl) that are removed by UV irradiation, and enzyme‑cleavable groups for biochemical applications. The concept of “traceless” protecting groups — where the protecting group fragment is incorporated into the final product — reduces step count. An example is the use of a silyl group that, after a key transformation, becomes part of the product’s silicon‑containing motif, eliminating a separate deprotection step.

Mechanistic Discrimination
A reaction of 2‑methyl‑2‑butene with HBr at −78 °C gives predominantly 2‑bromo‑2‑methylbutane, while the same reaction at 50 °C gives a mixture enriched in 2‑bromo‑3‑methylbutane. Explain the product distribution in terms of kinetic and thermodynamic control, identifying the intermediates and transition states involved.
At −78 °C the reaction is under kinetic control. Protonation of the alkene yields the more stable tertiary carbocation (2‑methyl‑2‑butyl cation) rapidly; bromide attacks this cation at the same carbon to give the kinetic product 2‑bromo‑2‑methylbutane. The activation barrier for this pathway is lower because the tertiary cation forms faster than the less stable secondary cation that would lead to the 1,2‑shift product. At 50 °C the reaction becomes reversible; the initially formed tertiary bromide can ionize back to the tertiary cation, which can undergo a hydride shift to give the more stable secondary cation (actually a more substituted allylic cation after rearrangement). The equilibrium favors the more substituted alkene‑derived product 2‑bromo‑3‑methylbutane because the resulting double bond in the elimination‑re‑addition sequence is more substituted, lowering the overall free energy. Thus the product ratio shifts from kinetic to thermodynamic control as temperature increases.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the major product at two different temperatures, first draw the possible carbocation intermediates. Identify which cation forms fastest (kinetic) and which leads to the more stable final product (thermodynamic). State explicitly whether the reaction is reversible under the given conditions; reversibility is the hallmark of thermodynamic control.

Isotope Effects and Mechanistic Probes

Kinetic isotope effects (KIEs) provide direct experimental evidence for bond‑making and bond‑breaking events in the rate‑determining step. A primary KIE occurs when the bond to the isotopically labeled atom is broken or formed in the transition state; typical values for C–H vs C–D cleavage range from 2 to 7 at room temperature. A secondary KIE arises when the labeled atom is not directly involved in bond cleavage but experiences a change in hybridization or hyperconjugation; values are usually close to 1 (0.9–1.2). The magnitude and temperature dependence of the KIE distinguish between concerted and stepwise mechanisms, and between tunneling‑dominated and classical over‑the‑barrier reactions.

Primary KIE in Elimination and Oxidation

In an E2 elimination, the C–H bond is broken in the rate‑determining step. Replacing the β‑hydrogen with deuterium gives a primary KIE (k_H/k_D ≈ 3–5). In contrast, an E1 elimination proceeds via carbocation formation; the C–H bond is broken after the rate‑determining ionization, so the KIE is near unity. Similarly, the oxidation of alcohols by Cr(VI) reagents shows a large primary KIE when the α‑C–H bond is cleaved in the chromate ester decomposition step, confirming that C–H cleavage is rate‑limiting.

Secondary KIE and Hybridization Change

During an SN1 reaction, the carbon bearing the leaving group changes from sp³ to sp² in the carbocation. A β‑deuterium substitution yields an inverse secondary KIE (k_H/k_D < 1) because the C–D bond is stronger in the sp²‑hybridized cation due to reduced hyperconjugation. In an SN2 reaction, the carbon remains sp³‑like in the transition state, giving a normal secondary KIE close to 1. Measuring these subtle effects requires precise kinetic measurements, often by competitive methods using a mixture of labeled and unlabeled substrate.

Solvent and Heavy‑Atom Isotope Effects

Solvent isotope effects (e.g., H₂O vs D₂O) probe proton transfer steps. A large solvent KIE indicates that proton transfer is rate‑determining. Heavy‑atom isotope effects (¹³C, ¹⁵N, ¹⁸O) are much smaller (typically 1.01–1.03) but can be measured by isotope‑ratio mass spectrometry. They are valuable for distinguishing between associative and dissociative pathways in phosphoryl transfer and for confirming the involvement of a carbonyl oxygen in acyl‑transfer reactions.

Application to Enzyme Mechanisms

Enzymatic reactions often exhibit large primary KIEs, sometimes exceeding the semiclassical limit due to quantum mechanical tunneling. Temperature‑dependence studies (Arrhenius plots) that show curved lines or unusually low activation energies for the light isotope are hallmarks of tunneling. These data, combined with mutagenesis, allow assignment of the chemical step that limits turnover.

Concerted vs Stepwise Reaction Pathways

Many pericyclic and addition reactions can proceed either through a single concerted transition state or via a stepwise sequence involving discrete intermediates. The distinction has profound consequences for stereochemistry, regioselectivity, and substituent effects. Experimental tools include stereochemical probes, kinetic isotope effects, radical clocks, and computational studies.

Diels–Alder Reaction

The classic Diels–Alder cycloaddition is a concerted [4+2] process that proceeds through a single aromatic transition state (six π‑electrons). It is stereospecific: the relative configuration of substituents on the diene and dienophile is preserved in the cycloadduct (suprafacial‑suprafacial). However, with strongly electron‑deficient dienophiles and electron‑rich dienes, a stepwise diradical or zwitterionic pathway can compete, leading to loss of stereospecificity. The observation of a normal secondary KIE at the diene termini supports a concerted pathway, while a large primary KIE at the dienophile β‑position suggests stepwise character.

Ene Reaction

The ene reaction transfers an allylic hydrogen to an enophile with a concomitant shift of the double bond. A concerted ene reaction proceeds through a six‑membered, envelope‑like transition state and is stereospecific with respect to the migrating hydrogen. Stepwise variants involve a diradical or polar intermediate, which can be detected by radical traps (e.g., TEMPO) or by observing racemization at a chiral allylic center.

1,3‑Dipolar Cycloadditions

These reactions are generally concerted, but with certain dipoles (e.g., nitrones) and dipolarophiles bearing strong electron‑withdrawing groups, a stepwise Michael‑type addition followed by ring closure can occur. The stepwise pathway often shows a solvent polarity effect on rate and can be intercepted by nucleophiles that trap the zwitterionic intermediate.

Diagnostic Experiments

Stereochemical probes: a cis‑substituted alkene that gives a trans‑product indicates a stepwise mechanism with bond rotation in an intermediate. Radical clocks: incorporation of a cyclopropylcarbinyl group that undergoes rapid ring opening if a radical intermediate forms. KIE measurements: a primary KIE at a position where no bond is broken in a concerted TS signals a stepwise process. Computational chemistry (DFT) can locate both concerted and stepwise transition states and compare their free energies.

Modern Oxidation and Reduction Reagents

Contemporary synthesis demands chemoselective, mild, and scalable redox reagents. Traditional reagents such as Jones oxidation (CrO₃/H₂SO₄) or LiAlH₄ reduction often lack functional‑group tolerance. Modern alternatives provide orthogonal reactivity, enabling late‑stage transformations on complex molecules.

Oxidation Reagents

Dess–Martin periodinane (DMP) oxidizes primary alcohols to aldehydes and secondary alcohols to ketones under neutral conditions, tolerating acid‑ and base‑sensitive groups. The reaction proceeds via a hypervalent iodine intermediate; the by‑product is iodobenzoic acid, easily removed by aqueous workup. IBX (2‑iodoxybenzoic acid) is a related reagent that can effect α‑oxidation of carbonyl compounds. TEMPO/NaOCl (or NaOCl/NaBr) catalytically oxidizes primary alcohols to aldehydes with high chemoselectivity; the nitroxyl radical cycles between oxoammonium and hydroxylamine forms. For allylic and benzylic oxidations, SeO₂ (selenium dioxide) performs allylic transposition to give allylic alcohols, while MnO₂ selectively oxidizes allylic and benzylic alcohols to carbonyls without over‑oxidation. The Pfitzner–Moffatt oxidation (DCC/DMSO) and Swern oxidation (oxalyl chloride/DMSO) are mild methods for acid‑sensitive substrates, though they generate malodorous by‑products.

Reduction Reagents

Sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) reduces aldehydes and ketones but not esters. Its reactivity is modulated by additives: CeCl₃·7H₂O (Luche conditions) enhances 1,2‑reduction of α,β‑unsaturated ketones to allylic alcohols. Sodium cyanoborohydride (NaBH₃CN) is a milder hydride donor used for reductive amination at neutral pH. Diisobutylaluminum hydride (DIBAL‑H) at −78 °C reduces esters to aldehydes; excess or higher temperature gives alcohols. Lithium tri‑tert‑butoxyaluminum hydride (LTBA) selectively reduces acid chlorides to aldehydes. For chemoselective reduction of amides to amines, borane·THF (BH₃·THF) is effective. Catalytic hydrogenation with Pd/C, PtO₂, or Rh/Al₂O₃ reduces alkenes, alkynes, benzyl ethers, and nitro groups; poisoned catalysts (Lindlar’s catalyst) stop at cis‑alkenes. Transfer hydrogenation using HCO₂NH₄/Pd‑C avoids high‑pressure H₂.

Orthogonal Redox Pairs

In multistep synthesis, pairing an oxidation that leaves a silyl ether untouched with a reduction that does not affect a benzyl ether is essential. For example, DMP oxidation of a primary alcohol in the presence of a TBS ether is clean, while subsequent Luche reduction of a ketone does not cleave the TBS group. Similarly, TEMPO oxidation of a primary alcohol in the presence of a benzyl ester is selective, and later hydrogenolysis removes the benzyl ester without affecting the newly formed aldehyde (if protected as an acetal). Knowledge of these compatibilities enables concise synthetic routes.

  • Assuming that a large primary KIE always proves a concerted mechanism; stepwise pathways with a rate‑determining bond cleavage also show primary KIEs.
  • Confusing the temperature dependence of kinetic vs thermodynamic control; kinetic products dominate at low temperature, not high temperature.
  • Overlooking the need for orthogonal protecting groups; using two acid‑labile groups in the same sequence leads to simultaneous deprotection.
  • Treating all silyl ethers as equally stable; TBS is acid‑labile while TIPS is more robust, affecting deprotection order.
  • Neglecting solvent isotope effects when proton transfer is implicated; a reaction in D₂O that slows by a factor >2 signals a rate‑determining proton transfer.
  • Believing that Dess–Martin periodinane works on all alcohols; hindered tertiary alcohols react sluggishly and may require alternative oxidants.
  • Forgetting that catalytic hydrogenation can reduce unintended functionalities such as alkenes or benzyl groups unless a poisoned catalyst is used.
