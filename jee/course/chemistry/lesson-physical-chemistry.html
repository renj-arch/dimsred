Foundations of Physical Chemistry: Mole Concept and Stoichiometry

1.1 The Mole as a Bridge Between Microscopic and Macroscopic Worlds

The mole is the fundamental counting unit that allows chemists to translate the number of atoms, molecules, or ions in a sample into a measurable mass. One mole of any substance contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities, a value fixed by the 2019 redefinition of the SI base units. Because the molar mass of an element expressed in grams per mole is numerically equal to its atomic weight, the relationship n = m/M connects the mass m of a sample to the amount of substance n. This equivalence makes it possible to weigh out reactants in the laboratory while knowing precisely how many particles will take part in a reaction.

1.2 Avogadro’s Law and the Molar Volume of Gases

Avogadro’s hypothesis states that equal volumes of all gases, measured at the same temperature and pressure, contain the same number of particles. At standard temperature and pressure (0 °C, 1 atm) one mole of an ideal gas occupies 22.414 L, a value that follows directly from the ideal‑gas equation PV = nRT. Real gases deviate from this volume because of intermolecular forces and finite molecular size; the deviation is quantified by the compressibility factor Z = PV/RT. Understanding these deviations is essential when high‑precision gas‑phase stoichiometry is required, for example in the calibration of gas‑chromatography standards.

1.3 Stoichiometric Calculations and Limiting Reagents

Balanced chemical equations provide the mole ratios that govern how reactants combine and products form. When the initial amounts of reactants are known, the limiting reagent is identified by comparing the mole ratio of each reactant to its stoichiometric coefficient; the reactant that yields the smallest amount of product controls the theoretical yield. The percent yield, calculated as (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100 %, reveals the efficiency of a process and is a key metric in both laboratory synthesis and industrial production. Mastery of these calculations enables a student to predict the mass of product obtainable from a given mass of starting material, a skill tested repeatedly in quantitative problems.

Atomic Structure and Quantum Mechanics

2.1 The Development of the Quantum Model

The failure of classical physics to explain black‑body radiation, the photoelectric effect, and atomic line spectra led to the quantum hypothesis introduced by Max Planck in 1900 and extended by Albert Einstein in 1905. Niels Bohr’s 1913 model quantised the angular momentum of the electron in hydrogen, reproducing the Rydberg formula for spectral lines. However, the Bohr model could not account for the fine structure of spectra or the behaviour of multi‑electron atoms. The Schrödinger equation, published in 1926, replaced the notion of defined orbits with wavefunctions ψ that describe the probability density of finding an electron in a region of space. The solutions to this equation are characterised by four quantum numbers: principal (n), azimuthal (l), magnetic (mₗ), and spin (mₛ), which together define the unique state of each electron in an atom.

2.2 Electron Configurations and the Periodic Table

The Aufbau principle, guided by the (n + l) rule, dictates the order in which atomic orbitals are filled as the atomic number increases. Hund’s rule states that electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly before pairing, maximising total spin and lowering electron‑electron repulsion. The Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons in the same atom from having identical sets of four quantum numbers. These rules generate the periodic recurrence of chemical properties because elements in the same group share similar valence‑electron configurations. Exceptions such as chromium (3d⁵4s¹) and copper (3d¹⁰4s¹) arise from the extra stability associated with half‑filled and fully‑filled d subshells.

2.3 Spectroscopic Evidence for Quantised Energy Levels

Atomic emission and absorption spectra provide direct experimental proof of quantised energy levels. When an electron transitions from a higher energy level E₂ to a lower level E₁, a photon of frequency ν = (E₂ − E₁)/h is emitted; the reverse process absorbs a photon of the same frequency. The Lyman, Balmer, and Paschen series of hydrogen correspond to transitions ending at n = 1, 2, and 3 respectively. In multi‑electron atoms, the presence of screening and penetration modifies the effective nuclear charge experienced by each electron, shifting line positions and giving rise to the complex spectra used in analytical techniques such as atomic absorption spectroscopy.

Example 1
A 0.500 g sample of an unknown metal M reacts completely with excess hydrochloric acid to produce 0.0250 mol of H₂ gas. The reaction follows the equation 2 M + 6 HCl → 2 MCl₃ + 3 H₂. Determine the molar mass of M and identify the metal from the periodic table.
Solution: From the stoichiometry, 3 mol H₂ are produced per 2 mol M, so moles of M = (2/3) × 0.0250 mol = 0.01667 mol. Molar mass = mass ÷ moles = 0.500 g ÷ 0.01667 mol = 30.0 g mol⁻¹. The element with a molar mass close to 30 g mol⁻¹ that forms a +3 oxidation state is aluminium (Al, 26.98 g mol⁻¹) but the calculated value is slightly higher; considering experimental uncertainty, the metal is most likely aluminium.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem gives a gas volume at non‑standard conditions, always convert to moles using the ideal‑gas law before applying stoichiometric ratios; this avoids the common mistake of using 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at the wrong temperature or pressure.

Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

3.1 Ionic, Covalent, and Metallic Bonding

Ionic bonds arise from the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by the transfer of electrons from a low‑ionisation‑energy metal to a high‑electron‑affinity non‑metal. The lattice energy, which can be estimated by the Born‑Landé equation, quantifies the stability of the resulting crystal. Covalent bonds involve the sharing of electron pairs between atoms; the degree of sharing is described by electronegativity differences, with a difference greater than about 1.7 indicating predominant ionic character. Metallic bonding is characterised by a delocalised “sea” of valence electrons that holds positively charged metal ions together, giving rise to properties such as electrical conductivity, malleability, and metallic lustre.

3.2 Valence‑Shell Electron‑Pair Repulsion Theory and Molecular Geometry

The VSEPR model predicts the three‑dimensional arrangement of electron domains around a central atom by minimising repulsions between bonding pairs and lone pairs. The order of repulsion strength is lone‑pair–lone‑pair > lone‑pair–bonding‑pair > bonding‑pair–bonding‑pair. Consequently, molecules such as CH₄ (four bonding pairs) adopt a tetrahedral geometry with bond angles of 109.5°, while NH₃ (three bonding pairs, one lone pair) is trigonal pyramidal with a reduced angle of about 107°, and H₂O (two bonding pairs, two lone pairs) is bent at roughly 104.5°. The model also rationalises the shapes of species with expanded octets, such as SF₆ (octahedral) and XeF₄ (square planar).

3.3 Hybridisation and the Description of σ and π Bonds

Hybridisation mixes atomic orbitals on the same atom to form a set of equivalent hybrid orbitals that can form σ bonds directed in space. sp hybridisation yields two linear orbitals (180°), sp² gives three trigonal‑planar orbitals (120°), and sp³ produces four tetrahedral orbitals (109.5°). Unhybridised p orbitals overlap side‑by‑side to form π bonds, which are weaker than σ bonds and restrict rotation about the bond axis. In ethene (C₂H₄) each carbon is sp² hybridised; the C=C double bond consists of one σ bond from sp²–sp² overlap and one π bond from p–p overlap. In ethyne (C₂H₂) each carbon is sp hybridised, giving a triple bond composed of one σ and two π bonds.

Thermodynamics and Energy Changes

4.1 The First Law: Conservation of Energy

The first law of thermodynamics states that the internal energy U of an isolated system is constant; for a closed system, the change in internal energy ΔU equals the heat q added to the system plus the work w done on the system (ΔU = q + w). In chemistry, work is most often pressure‑volume work w = −PΔV, so at constant pressure the heat exchanged equals the enthalpy change ΔH = ΔU + Δ(PV). Enthalpy is a state function, meaning its value depends only on the initial and final states, not on the path taken. This property allows the use of Hess’s law to calculate ΔH for a reaction by summing the enthalpy changes of a series of steps that lead from reactants to products.

4.2 Enthalpy Changes in Chemical Reactions

Standard enthalpy of formation ΔH_f° is defined as the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states at 1 bar and a specified temperature (usually 298 K). The standard reaction enthalpy ΔH_rxn° can be obtained from Σ n ΔH_f°(products) − Σ m ΔH_f°(reactants). Exothermic reactions have negative ΔH (heat released), while endothermic reactions have positive ΔH (heat absorbed). Calorimetry, either constant‑pressure (coffee‑cup) or constant‑volume (bomb), provides experimental ΔH or ΔU values. The temperature dependence of ΔH is described by Kirchhoff’s law: ΔH(T₂) = ΔH(T₁) + ∫ΔC_p dT, where ΔC_p is the difference in heat capacities between products and reactants.

4.3 The Second Law: Entropy and Spontaneity

The second law introduces entropy S, a measure of the number of microscopic configurations consistent with a macroscopic state. For any spontaneous process in an isolated system, the total entropy change ΔS_total > 0. In a non‑isolated system, the Gibbs free energy G = H − TS combines enthalpy and entropy to predict spontaneity at constant temperature and pressure: a process is spontaneous if ΔG < 0, non‑spontaneous if ΔG > 0, and at equilibrium if ΔG = 0. The standard Gibbs energy of formation ΔG_f° allows calculation of ΔG_rxn° analogously to ΔH_rxn°. The relationship ΔG° = −RT ln K links thermodynamics to chemical equilibrium, where K is the equilibrium constant.

Chemical Equilibrium and Kinetics

5.1 Dynamic Equilibrium and the Equilibrium Constant

At equilibrium the forward and reverse reaction rates are equal, so the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant in time. The equilibrium constant K_c is expressed in terms of molar concentrations, while K_p uses partial pressures for gas‑phase reactions; they are related by K_p = K_c(RT)Δn_gas, where Δn_gas is the change in moles of gas. The reaction quotient Q has the same form as K but uses instantaneous concentrations; comparing Q to K predicts the direction in which the system will shift to reach equilibrium (Q < K → forward, Q > K → reverse). Le Chatelier’s principle states that a system at equilibrium will respond to a change in concentration, pressure, or temperature by shifting in a direction that counteracts the imposed change.

5.2 Reaction Rates and Rate Laws

The rate of a reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. For a reaction aA + bB → products, the differential rate law is rate = k[A]^x[B]^y, where x and y are the reaction orders with respect to A and B, determined experimentally. The overall order is x + y. The rate constant k varies with temperature according to the Arrhenius equation k = A exp(−E_a/RT), where A is the pre‑exponential factor and E_a is the activation energy. A plot of ln k versus 1/T yields a straight line of slope −E_a/R, providing a method to determine E_a from kinetic data.

5.3 Integrated Rate Laws and Half‑Life

Integration of the differential rate law gives concentration‑time relationships that are linear for specific orders. For a first‑order reaction, ln[A] = −kt + ln[A]0; a plot of ln[A] versus t is linear with slope −k. The half‑life t1/2 = ln 2/k is independent of initial concentration, a hallmark of first‑order kinetics. For a second‑order reaction with a single reactant, 1/[A] = kt + 1/[A]0, and the half‑life depends on the initial concentration as t1/2 = 1/(k[A]0). Zero‑order reactions show a linear decrease in concentration with time, [A] = −kt + [A]0, and their half‑life is t1/2 = [A]0/(2k). Recognising the linear form appropriate to the order allows rapid determination of k from experimental data.

5.4 Catalysis and Reaction Mechanisms

A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, increasing the rate without being consumed. Homogeneous catalysts exist in the same phase as the reactants, while heterogeneous catalysts operate at a phase boundary, often a solid surface. The catalytic cycle involves adsorption of reactants, reaction on the surface, and desorption of products. Enzymes are biological catalysts that exhibit high specificity and operate under mild conditions; their kinetics are described by the Michaelis–Menten equation v = (V_max[S])/(K_m + [S]). Understanding the elementary steps of a mechanism, identifying the rate‑determining step, and applying the steady‑state approximation for reactive intermediates are essential for deriving rate laws that match experimental observations.

Mastery of these interconnected topics equips a student to analyse any physicochemical problem by moving fluidly between quantitative calculations and conceptual reasoning.

Electrochemistry: Galvanic Cells and the Nernst Equation

A galvanic (voltaic) cell converts the free energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical work. The cell is represented by a line notation: anode | anode electrolyte || cathode electrolyte | cathode. The anode is the site of oxidation, the cathode the site of reduction. Standard electrode potentials (E°) are tabulated for half‑reactions written as reductions at 298 K, 1 bar, and unit activity. The standard cell potential is E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. A positive E°cell indicates a spontaneous reaction under standard conditions.

The Nernst equation extends the prediction to non‑standard conditions: Ecell = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln Q, where n is the number of electrons transferred, F is the Faraday constant (96485 C mol⁻¹), R is the gas constant, T the absolute temperature, and Q the reaction quotient constructed from activities (approximated by concentrations for dilute solutions). At 298 K the factor RT/F ln 10 ≈ 0.05916 V, giving the convenient base‑10 form Ecell = E°cell − (0.05916/n) log₁₀ Q. This relationship allows calculation of cell potentials for concentration cells, determination of equilibrium constants (E°cell = (0.05916/n) log₁₀ K), and the design of batteries such as the lead‑acid, nickel‑cadmium, and lithium‑ion systems.

Practical considerations include the liquid‑junction potential, which is minimised by using a salt bridge with an inert electrolyte (e.g., KNO₃). Overpotential, the extra voltage required to drive an electrode reaction at a finite rate, distinguishes the thermodynamic potential from the observed working voltage. In corrosion, a metal acts as the anode of a local galvanic cell; the cathodic reaction is often oxygen reduction in neutral or acidic media. Cathodic protection (sacrificial anodes or impressed current) applies the same principles to prevent metal loss.

Standard Hydrogen Electrode and Reference Scales

The standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) defines the zero of the electrochemical series: 2 H⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻ ⇌ H₂(g) at 1 bar, aH⁺ = 1. All other potentials are measured relative to SHE. The electrochemical series orders half‑reactions by their reduction potentials; a species higher in the series is a stronger oxidising agent. This ordering predicts the direction of spontaneous redox reactions and guides the selection of electrodes for electrolysis.

Applications in Energy Storage

In a lead‑acid battery the overall reaction is Pb(s) + PbO₂(s) + 2 H₂SO₄(aq) → 2 PbSO₄(s) + 2 H₂O(l). The Nernst equation shows that the cell voltage decreases as the acid concentration drops during discharge, providing a built‑in state‑of‑charge indicator. Lithium‑ion cells rely on intercalation reactions; the open‑circuit voltage varies with the lithium content in the host electrodes, again described by the Nernst relation for the solid‑state redox couples.

Ionic Equilibrium: Acid‑Base Theory, Buffers, and Solubility Product

Acid‑base behaviour is described by three complementary theories. Arrhenius defines acids as H⁺ producers and bases as OH⁻ producers in water. Brønsted‑Lowry extends the concept to proton donors and acceptors, allowing acid‑base reactions in non‑aqueous solvents. Lewis theory further generalises to electron‑pair acceptors (acids) and donors (bases), encompassing metal‑ligand complexation. In water the auto‑ionisation equilibrium 2 H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ has Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 298 K, giving pH + pOH = 14.

Weak acids (HA) and weak bases (B) establish equilibria characterised by Ka and Kb, related by Ka Kb = Kw for conjugate pairs. The Henderson‑Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]), provides a rapid estimate for buffer solutions, which resist pH change upon addition of small amounts of strong acid or base. Buffer capacity is maximal when [A⁻] = [HA] (pH = pKa) and declines as the ratio deviates beyond 10:1 or 1:10. Common buffer systems include acetate (pKa ≈ 4.76), phosphate (pKa₂ ≈ 7.21), and carbonate (pKa₂ ≈ 10.33).

Solubility equilibria involve sparingly soluble salts. For a salt AₓBᵧ(s) ⇌ x Aʸ⁺ + y Bˣ⁻, the solubility product Ksp = [Aʸ⁺]ˣ[Bˣ⁻]ʸ. The common‑ion effect reduces solubility when a shared ion is added from a soluble source. Precipitation occurs when the ionic product Qsp exceeds Ksp; selective precipitation exploits differences in Ksp values to separate ions. Complex formation (e.g., Ag⁺ + 2 NH₃ ⇌ [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺) can increase apparent solubility by lowering the free ion concentration, a principle used in qualitative analysis and in the cyanide leaching of gold.

Polyprotic Acids and Amphiprotic Species

Polyprotic acids (H₂A, H₃A) dissociate stepwise with successive Ka values (Ka₁ ≫ Ka₂ ≫ Ka₃). For diprotic acids, the pH of a solution of the intermediate salt (e.g., NaHA) is approximated by pH ≈ (pKa₁ + pKa₂)/2, provided the concentration is not too low. Amphiprotic species such as HCO₃⁻ can act as both acid and base; their pH is governed by the same average formula. These approximations break down when Ka values are close or concentrations are very dilute, requiring full equilibrium treatment.

Acid‑Base Indicators and Titration Curves

Indicators are weak acids (HIn) with distinct colours for HIn and In⁻. The transition range is pKa ± 1. In a strong acid–strong base titration the equivalence point pH is 7; for weak acid–strong base it is >7 (basic salt hydrolysis), and for strong acid–weak base it is <7. The steepness of the pH jump near equivalence determines the suitability of an indicator. Gran plots (Vₐ × 10⁻ᵖᴴ vs. Vₐ) linearise titration data, allowing accurate endpoint determination even with imperfect indicators.

Nernst Equation Application
Calculate the cell potential at 298 K for a Daniell cell with [Zn²⁺] = 0.1 M and [Cu²⁺] = 0.01 M given standard potentials Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V, Cu²⁺/Cu = +0.34 V.
The cell reaction is Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s). n = 2. E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = 0.34 V − (−0.76 V) = 1.10 V. Reaction quotient Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] = 0.1/0.01 = 10. At 298 K, Ecell = E°cell − (0.05916/2) log₁₀ Q = 1.10 V − 0.02958 × 1 = 1.070 V. The cell potential is 1.07 V.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When solving electrochemical problems, always write the half‑reactions, identify the anode and cathode, and apply the Nernst equation using the reaction quotient Q. Remember that the sign of E°cell is positive for spontaneous galvanic cells; a negative value indicates a non‑spontaneous electrolytic cell.

Phase Equilibria: Gibbs Phase Rule and Phase Diagrams

The Gibbs phase rule, F = C − P + 2, relates the number of degrees of freedom (F) to the number of components (C) and phases (P) in a system at equilibrium. For a one‑component system (C = 1) such as water, the rule gives F = 3 − P. In a single‑phase region (P = 1) two variables (e.g., T and P) can be changed independently; along a two‑phase coexistence line (P = 2) only one variable is free; at the triple point (P = 3) F = 0, fixing both T and P uniquely. The critical point marks the end of the liquid‑vapour coexistence curve, beyond which the distinction between liquid and gas disappears.

Phase diagrams plot the stable phases as a function of temperature and pressure (or temperature and composition for binary systems). The water phase diagram shows the negative slope of the solid‑liquid line, reflecting the lower density of ice. Carbon dioxide lacks a liquid phase at 1 atm; its triple point is at 5.1 atm, −56.6 °C, so solid CO₂ sublimes at atmospheric pressure. Binary phase diagrams (e.g., Pb‑Sn, Cu‑Ni) display liquidus and solidus curves; the lever rule determines the relative amounts of co‑existing phases: fraction of α = (Cβ − C₀)/(Cβ − Cα), where C₀ is the overall composition and Cα, Cβ are the compositions of the phases at the tie‑line ends.

Eutectic, Peritectic, and Monotectic Reactions

A eutectic reaction (L ⇌ α + β) occurs at a fixed composition and temperature where a liquid solidifies into two solid phases of fixed composition. The eutectic composition melts at the lowest temperature in the system, a principle exploited in solder alloys. A peritectic reaction (L + α ⇌ β) involves a liquid and a solid forming a new solid phase on cooling. Monotectic reactions (L₁ ⇌ L₂ + α) involve liquid‑liquid phase separation. Understanding these invariant reactions is essential for designing heat‑treatment schedules and predicting microstructures in alloys.

Colligative Properties and Phase Diagrams

Adding a non‑volatile solute lowers the vapour pressure of the solvent (Raoult’s law), causing boiling‑point elevation (ΔTb = i Kb m) and freezing‑point depression (ΔTf = i Kf m), where i is the van’t Hoff factor, Kb and Kf are ebullioscopic and cryoscopic constants, and m is molality. These effects shift the liquid‑vapour and solid‑liquid coexistence lines on a T‑P diagram, providing a thermodynamic basis for determining molar masses and for practical applications such as antifreeze and road‑salt de‑icing.

Chemical Kinetics: Collision Theory and Transition‑State Theory

Collision theory models reaction rates by assuming that molecules must collide with sufficient kinetic energy and proper orientation. The rate constant is k = Z ρ exp(−Ea/RT), where Z is the collision frequency, ρ the steric factor (0 < ρ ≤ 1), and Ea the activation energy. For a bimolecular gas‑phase reaction, Z = σ √(8kBT/πμ) NA, with σ the collision cross‑section, μ the reduced mass, and NA Avogadro’s number. The theory predicts the temperature dependence of k but often overestimates rates because ρ is difficult to determine a priori.

Transition‑state theory (TST) provides a more rigorous statistical‑mechanical framework. It postulates a quasi‑equilibrium between reactants and an activated complex (‡) at the saddle point of the potential energy surface. The rate constant is k = (kBT/h) K‡, where K‡ is the equilibrium constant for formation of the activated complex. Expressing K‡ in terms of partition functions yields the Eyring equation: k = (kBT/h) exp(ΔS‡/R) exp(−ΔH‡/RT). Here ΔH‡ and ΔS‡ are the enthalpy and entropy of activation. A large negative ΔS‡ indicates a highly ordered transition state (e.g., bimolecular association), while a positive ΔS‡ suggests a dissociative or loosely bound complex.

Reaction Coordinate Diagrams and Kinetic Isotope Effects

A reaction coordinate diagram plots potential energy versus the progress of reaction. The difference in energy between reactants and the transition state is ΔH‡ (≈ Ea − RT for gas‑phase reactions). The Hammond postulate states that the transition state resembles the nearer stable species in energy: for exothermic steps it resembles reactants, for endothermic steps it resembles products. Kinetic isotope effects (KIE) arise because zero‑point vibrational energies differ for isotopologues; a primary KIE (kH/kD > 1) indicates bond cleavage to the isotopic atom in the rate‑determining step, providing mechanistic insight.

Steady‑State and Pre‑Equilibrium Approximations

For complex mechanisms, the steady‑state approximation sets d[intermediate]/dt ≈ 0, allowing elimination of reactive intermediates from the rate law. The pre‑equilibrium approximation assumes a fast reversible step precedes the rate‑determining step, so the equilibrium constant of the fast step can be used to express intermediate concentrations. Both methods yield rate laws that can be compared with experimental orders; discrepancies signal missing steps or incorrect assumptions.

Solid State Chemistry: Crystal Lattices, Defects, and Electrical Conductivity

Crystalline solids are described by a Bravais lattice (14 types) and a basis of atoms. The seven crystal systems (cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, hexagonal, rhombohedral, monoclinic, triclinic) differ in lattice parameters and symmetry. Common metallic structures are face‑centred cubic (FCC, packing efficiency 74 %), body‑centred cubic (BCC, 68 %), and hexagonal close‑packed (HCP, 74 %). Ionic crystals (e.g., NaCl, CsCl, ZnS) adopt structures dictated by radius‑ratio rules; the coordination number and geometry follow from the relative sizes of cation and anion.

Point defects control many properties. Schottky defects (cation‑anion vacancy pairs) preserve stoichiometry and dominate in alkali halides with similar ion sizes. Frenkel defects (cation vacancy‑interstitial pair) prevail when the cation is much smaller than the anion (e.g., AgCl). Non‑stoichiometric oxides (e.g., Fe₁₋ₓO) exhibit metal‑deficiency via cation vacancies compensated by higher oxidation states. Defect concentrations follow Boltzmann statistics: n/N = exp(−Ef/2kBT) for Schottky, where Ef is the formation energy.

Electrical Conductivity in Solids

Metals conduct via a partially filled conduction band; resistivity rises with temperature due to phonon scattering (ρ = ρ₀ + AT⁵ at low T, linear at high T). Ionic conductivity occurs by vacancy or interstitial migration; the conductivity σ = n q μ, where n is the mobile defect concentration, q the charge, and μ the mobility. In doped semiconductors (e.g., Si with P or B), donor or acceptor levels introduce electrons or holes; the temperature dependence of carrier concentration follows n ∝ T³ᐟ² exp(−Eg/2kBT) for intrinsic, and n ≈ Nd for extrinsic regimes. The Hall effect distinguishes n‑type from p‑type and measures carrier concentration and mobility.

Band Theory and Magnetic Properties

Band theory explains the distinction between conductors, semiconductors, and insulators by the size of the band gap Eg. In transition‑metal oxides, strong electron‑electron correlations can open a Mott‑Hubbard gap, leading to insulating behaviour despite partially filled d‑bands. Magnetic ordering (ferro‑, antiferro‑, ferrimagnetism) arises from exchange interactions between unpaired spins; the Néel temperature marks the antiferromagnetic‑paramagnetic transition. These phenomena are central to functional materials such as high‑temperature superconductors, multiferroics, and spintronic devices.

  • Assuming buffer capacity is infinite; it is effective only within ±1 pH unit of pKa and for reasonable concentrations.

Statistical Thermodynamics: Partition Functions and Thermodynamic Properties

The molecular partition function q is the sum over all quantum states i of exp(−Ei/kBT). For a molecule with separable degrees of freedom, q factorises into translational (qtrans), rotational (qrot), vibrational (qvib), and electronic (qelec) contributions. The translational partition function for a particle of mass m in volume V is qtrans = (2πmkBT/h²)3/2 V. The rotational partition function for a linear rigid rotor is qrot = T/σθrot where θrot = h²/(8π²IkB) and σ is the symmetry number; for non‑linear rotors qrot = √π (T³/σθAθBθC)1/2. The vibrational partition function for a harmonic oscillator is qvib = 1/[1 − exp(−hν/kBT)]; at high temperature this reduces to kBT/hν. The electronic partition function is usually dominated by the ground state degeneracy g0 because excited electronic states lie far above kBT.

The canonical partition function for N indistinguishable particles is Q = qN/N!. Using Stirling’s approximation, the Helmholtz free energy A = −kBT ln Q. From A all other thermodynamic functions follow: internal energy U = −∂lnQ/∂β, entropy S = (U − A)/T, Gibbs free energy G = A + pV, and heat capacity CV = ∂U/∂T. The equilibrium constant K for a reaction can be expressed as the ratio of product and reactant partition functions multiplied by exp(−ΔE0/kBT), where ΔE0 is the zero‑point energy difference. Questions often test the distinction between the molecular partition function q and the canonical Q, the correct inclusion of symmetry numbers, and the use of high‑temperature approximations for rotations and vibrations. A common question asks for the temperature at which a vibrational mode becomes fully excited, requiring hν ≈ kBT.

Non‑ideal Solutions: Activity, Fugacity, and Real Gas Equations

In real solutions the effective concentration is the activity ai = γi xi (mole‑fraction basis) or ai = γi mi/m° (molality basis). The activity coefficient γi approaches unity at infinite dilution. For electrolytes the Debye–Hückel limiting law gives log10 γ± = −A |z+z−| √I, where I = ½ Σ mizi² is the ionic strength and A = 0.509 mol−1/2 kg1/2 at 298 K. The extended Debye–Hückel equation adds a linear term in √I with an ion‑size parameter å: log10 γ± = −A|z+z−|√I/(1 + Bå√I). For concentrated solutions Pitzer’s equations incorporate binary and ternary interaction parameters.

For real gases the fugacity f replaces pressure in the chemical potential: μ = μ° + RT ln(f/p°). The fugacity coefficient φ = f/p is obtained from the compressibility factor Z = pV/RT via ln φ = ∫0p (Z−1)/p d

. Common equations of state include van der Waals (p = RT/(V−b) − a/V²), Redlich–Kwong, and Peng–Robinson, each providing an analytical Z for φ calculation. Questions frequently test conversion between activity and fugacity standard states, correct use of the Debye–Hückel limiting law versus the extended form, and interpretation of φ > 1 (repulsive dominance) versus φ < 1 (attractive dominance). A common error is applying the limiting law at ionic strengths above 0.01 mol kg−1 where it deviates significantly.

Calculating Activity Coefficients Using Debye–Hückel Limiting Law
Calculate the mean activity coefficient of 0.01 mol kg−1 NaCl at 298 K using the Debye–Hückel limiting law. (A = 0.509 mol−1/2 kg1/2)
For NaCl, z+ = +1, z− = −1, so |z+z−| = 1. Ionic strength I = ½ Σ mizi² = ½ (0.01×1² + 0.01×1²) = 0.01 mol kg−1. √I = 0.1. log10 γ± = −A |z+z−| √I = −0.509 × 1 × 0.1 = −0.0509. γ± = 10−0.0509 = 0.888. The mean activity coefficient is 0.888.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Non‑Ideal Systems
When a problem involves non‑ideal behaviour, first identify the standard state (mole fraction, molality, or pressure) and the corresponding activity or fugacity definition. Next, decide which model is appropriate: Debye–Hückel limiting law for very dilute electrolytes (I < 0.01 M), extended Debye–Hückel or Pitzer for higher concentrations, and a cubic equation of state for gases. Write the relevant equation explicitly before substituting numbers; this shows the examiner you understand the assumptions. Finally, check the magnitude of the result — activity coefficients should be ≤ 1 for attractive interactions, and fugacity coefficients near 1 for near‑ideal gases.

Electrochemical Kinetics: Butler–Volmer Equation and Tafel Analysis

The Butler–Volmer equation describes the current density j at an electrode as a function of overpotential η: j = j0 [exp(αaFη/RT) − exp(−αcFη/RT)], where j0 is the exchange current density, αa and αc are the anodic and cathodic transfer coefficients (αa + αc = 1 for a single‑step electron transfer), F is Faraday’s constant, R the gas constant, and T the temperature. At high anodic overpotentials (η > 0.1 V) the cathodic term becomes negligible, yielding the Tafel equation η = ba log10(j/j0) with ba = 2.303RT/(αaF). Similarly, at high cathodic overpotentials η = −bc log10(|j|/j0) with bc = 2.303RT/(αcF). A Tafel plot (η versus log10|j|) gives straight lines whose slopes yield αa and αc, and the intercept at η = 0 gives j0. The exchange current density reflects the intrinsic rate of electron transfer at equilibrium; a large j0 indicates a facile reaction (e.g., H2 on Pt), while a small j0 signals kinetic hindrance (e.g., O2 reduction on carbon). The sign convention is often tested: anodic current is positive, cathodic negative, and η = E − Eeq. A frequent question provides a Tafel plot with mixed kinetic and mass‑transport regions and asks to identify the kinetic Tafel slope. Another error is assuming α = 0.5 without justification; the symmetry factor depends on the activation barrier shape and can deviate significantly.

Surface Chemistry: Adsorption Isotherms and Heterogeneous Catalysis

The Langmuir isotherm assumes a uniform surface with a fixed number of identical sites, monolayer coverage, and no interactions between adsorbed molecules. The fractional coverage θ = Kp/(1 + Kp) for gas‑phase adsorption at pressure p, where K = ka/kd is the equilibrium constant for adsorption. Linearised forms (1/θ = 1 + 1/Kp or p/θ = 1/K + p) are used to extract K and the monolayer capacity. The BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) model extends Langmuir to multilayer adsorption: p/[V(p0−p)] = 1/(VmC) + (C−1)p/(VmC p0), where Vm is the monolayer volume and C is related to the heat of adsorption of the first layer. The Freundlich isotherm θ = KF p1/n is empirical and accounts for surface heterogeneity.

In heterogeneous catalysis, three principal mechanisms operate. The Langmuir–Hinshelwood mechanism involves adsorption of both reactants on the surface followed by surface reaction; the rate is proportional to θAθB. The Eley–Rideal mechanism has one reactant adsorbed and the other reacting from the gas phase; rate ∝ θA pB. The Mars–van Krevelen mechanism involves lattice oxygen participation, with the catalyst being reduced and re‑oxidised in a cyclic fashion. Turnover frequency (TOF) = (moles of product formed per unit time) / (moles of active sites) is the key metric for catalyst activity. Questions test the ability to derive rate laws from a proposed mechanism using the steady‑state approximation for surface intermediates, and to distinguish between linearised Langmuir and BET plots. A common error is applying the Langmuir model to data that clearly show multilayer uptake, or assuming the BET constant C equals the ratio of heats of adsorption without considering the entropy contribution.

Photochemistry: Quantum Yield, Jablonski Diagram, and Photochemical Reaction Mechanisms

The Jablonski diagram depicts the electronic states of a molecule: ground state S0, excited singlet states S1, S2…, and triplet states T1, T2…. Absorption promotes the molecule to a vibrationally excited level of S1 or S2 (Franck–Condon principle). Rapid internal conversion (IC) relaxes the molecule to the lowest vibrational level of S1 (Kasha’s rule). From S1 three pathways compete: fluorescence (radiative decay to S0), intersystem crossing (ISC) to T1, and non‑radiative internal conversion to S0. Phosphorescence is the spin‑forbidden radiative decay T1 → S0. The quantum yield of a process φ = (number of events) / (number of photons absorbed). For fluorescence φf = kf/(kf + kic + kisc); for phosphorescence φp = φisc × kp/(kp + knr). The Stern–Volmer equation describes quenching: φ0/φ = 1 + KSV[Q] where KSV = kqτ0.

Photochemical reactions often proceed via excited‑state reactivity. Norrish type I cleavage involves α‑cleavage of a carbonyl compound from the nπ* triplet state, yielding radicals. Norrish type II is an intramolecular γ‑hydrogen abstraction leading to a 1,4‑biradical that can cleave or cyclise. Photoisomerisation (e.g., stilbene cis–trans) occurs via rotation around a double bond in the excited state. Photoredox catalysis uses a photosensitiser (e.g., Ru(bpy)32+) to generate strong oxidising or reducing equivalents via electron transfer from the excited state. Questions frequently ask to calculate quantum yields from kinetic data, identify the operative excited state (singlet vs triplet) from quenching studies, and predict product distributions for Norrish reactions. A classic error is confusing quantum yield with quantum efficiency (the latter is often defined per photon incident, not absorbed), and assuming fluorescence quantum yield is unity for all fluorophores.

  • Using the Debye–Hückel limiting law at ionic strengths above 0.01 mol kg−1 where it loses accuracy.
  • Confusing the symmetry factor α with 0.5 in Butler–Volmer analysis without experimental justification.
  • Applying the Langmuir isotherm to multilayer adsorption data instead of the BET model.
  • Neglecting the entropy term when interpreting the BET constant C as purely an enthalpy ratio.
  • Equating quantum yield with quantum efficiency, leading to incorrect photochemical rate calculations.
  • Assuming fluorescence quantum yield is always high; many molecules have φf < 0.01 due to efficient intersystem crossing.
  • Forgetting that the Tafel slope changes when mass‑transport limitations become significant, and using the mixed region to extract kinetic parameters.

These advanced topics require careful attention to underlying assumptions and model limits to avoid common pitfalls.
