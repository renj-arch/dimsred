The Periodic Table and Periodic Properties

1.1 Historical Development and Modern Form

The periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number so that elements with similar chemical properties fall in the same vertical group. Dmitri Mendeleev's 1869 table left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties with remarkable accuracy, but the modern form follows Henry Moseley's 1913 discovery that atomic number, not atomic mass, is the fundamental ordering principle. The long form of the table contains eighteen groups and seven periods, with the f-block elements placed separately at the bottom to keep the table's width manageable. Each period corresponds to the filling of a principal quantum shell, while each group reflects a common valence electron configuration that dictates chemical behaviour.

1.2 Periodic Trends in Atomic and Ionic Radii

Atomic radius decreases across a period because the nuclear charge increases while electrons enter the same principal shell, so the effective nuclear charge experienced by the outermost electrons rises steadily. Down a group, a new principal shell is added, which outweighs the increased nuclear charge and causes the radius to increase. Ionic radii follow the same trends, but cations are always smaller than their parent atoms because the loss of electrons reduces electron-electron repulsion and often removes an entire shell, while anions are larger because added electrons increase repulsion without increasing nuclear charge. For isoelectronic species such as N3-, O2-, F-, Na+, Mg2+, and Al3+, the radius decreases as nuclear charge increases, giving the order N3- > O2- > F- > Na+ > Mg2+ > Al3+.

1.3 Ionization Energy, Electron Affinity, and Electronegativity

First ionization energy generally increases across a period because the increasing effective nuclear charge holds electrons more tightly, and it decreases down a group because the outermost electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded. Exceptions occur at Group 13 (B, Al, Ga) where removal of a p-electron is easier than removal of an s-electron from the preceding Group 12 element, and at Group 16 (O, S, Se) where electron-electron repulsion in a paired p-orbital lowers the energy needed to remove one electron. Electron affinity becomes more exothermic across a period and less exothermic down a group, though the trend is less regular because of small orbital sizes and repulsion effects. Electronegativity, measured on the Pauling scale, follows the same periodic pattern as ionization energy; fluorine (3.98) is the most electronegative element and caesium (0.79) the least, and the difference in electronegativity between bonded atoms determines the ionic character of the bond.

1.4 Metallic Character and Oxidation States

Metallic character decreases across a period and increases down a group, mirroring the trends in ionization energy and electronegativity. Elements on the left of the table lose electrons readily to form cations, while those on the right gain electrons to form anions. Transition metals exhibit variable oxidation states because the (n-1)d and ns orbitals are close in energy, allowing different numbers of electrons to be lost or shared. The highest oxidation state of a transition metal usually equals its group number up to manganese (Group 7, +7 in MnO4-), after which the increasing nuclear charge makes higher oxidation states less stable. In the p-block, the inert pair effect makes the oxidation state two less than the group valence increasingly stable down Groups 13-16, so Tl(I) is more stable than Tl(III), Pb(II) more stable than Pb(IV), and Bi(III) more stable than Bi(V).

Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

2.1 Ionic Bonding and Lattice Energy

Ionic bonds form when the electronegativity difference between two atoms is large enough (typically >1.7 on the Pauling scale) that electron transfer creates oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction. The stability of an ionic compound is quantified by its lattice energy, the energy released when gaseous ions combine to form one mole of solid crystal. The Born-Landé equation shows that lattice energy is proportional to the product of the ionic charges and inversely proportional to the sum of the ionic radii, so compounds with small, highly charged ions such as MgO (Mg2+ and O2-) have very high lattice energies and high melting points. The Born-Haber cycle applies Hess's law to relate lattice energy to measurable quantities: enthalpy of formation, enthalpy of atomization, ionization energies, electron affinities, and enthalpy of sublimation, allowing lattice energies to be determined indirectly.

2.2 Covalent Bonding and Lewis Structures

Covalent bonds arise from the sharing of electron pairs between atoms of similar electronegativity. A Lewis structure represents valence electrons as dots and shows how atoms achieve noble-gas configurations by sharing. The octet rule works well for second-period elements but fails for molecules with odd numbers of electrons (NO, NO2), electron-deficient species (BF3, BeCl2), and expanded octets in third-period and heavier elements (PF5, SF6, XeF4) where d-orbitals or, more accurately, hypervalent bonding involving three-centre four-electron bonds can accommodate extra electrons. Formal charge, calculated as valence electrons minus non-bonding electrons minus half the bonding electrons, helps select the most plausible Lewis structure; the structure with formal charges closest to zero and negative charges on the more electronegative atoms is preferred.

2.3 VSEPR Theory and Molecular Geometry

The Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) model predicts molecular shapes by assuming that electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to minimize repulsion. The order of repulsion strength is lone pair-lone pair > lone pair-bond pair > bond pair-bond pair, so lone pairs occupy more space and compress bond angles. The steric number (number of sigma bonds plus lone pairs) determines the electron-pair geometry: 2 gives linear, 3 trigonal planar, 4 tetrahedral, 5 trigonal bipyramidal, and 6 octahedral. The molecular geometry is the shape of the atoms only; for example, four electron pairs with one lone pair (steric number 4, one lone pair) give a tetrahedral electron-pair geometry but a trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry (NH3, bond angle 107°), while two lone pairs give a bent geometry (H2O, bond angle 104.5°). In trigonal bipyramidal arrangements, lone pairs always occupy equatorial positions to minimize 90° repulsions, leading to seesaw (SF4), T-shaped (ClF3), and linear (XeF2) molecular geometries.

2.4 Hybridization and Orbital Overlap

Hybridization mixes atomic orbitals on the same atom to form equivalent hybrid orbitals that overlap more effectively with orbitals on neighbouring atoms. The number of hybrid orbitals equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed, and the hybridization scheme corresponds to the steric number from VSEPR: sp (linear, 180°), sp2 (trigonal planar, 120°), sp3 (tetrahedral, 109.5°), sp3d (trigonal bipyramidal), and sp3d2 (octahedral). Sigma bonds form by head-on overlap of hybrid orbitals or s-orbitals, while pi bonds form by sideways overlap of unhybridized p-orbitals. In ethene (C2H4), each carbon is sp2 hybridized; three sp2 orbitals form sigma bonds (two C-H and one C-C), and the remaining p-orbitals overlap to form a pi bond, giving a rigid planar structure. In ethyne (C2H2), sp hybridization leaves two p-orbitals for two pi bonds, producing a linear molecule with a short, strong triple bond.

2.5 Molecular Orbital Theory for Homonuclear Diatomics

Molecular orbital (MO) theory treats electrons as delocalized over the entire molecule, forming bonding, antibonding, and non-bonding orbitals from linear combinations of atomic orbitals. For homonuclear diatomics of the second period, the ordering of MOs depends on the energy gap between 2s and 2p orbitals. For Li2 to N2, the order is σ2s, σ*2s, π2px=π2py, σ2pz, π*2px=π*2py, σ*2pz; for O2 and F2, the σ2pz drops below the π2p orbitals because of reduced s-p mixing. Bond order = ½ (bonding electrons - antibonding electrons) predicts stability and bond length: N2 (bond order 3) has a very short, strong triple bond; O2 (bond order 2) is paramagnetic with two unpaired electrons in degenerate π* orbitals, a result MO theory explains but Lewis structures cannot; F2 (bond order 1) has a weak single bond; Ne2 (bond order 0) does not exist.

Example 1
Predict the shape and bond angle of ClF3 using VSEPR theory, and state the hybridization of the central chlorine atom.
Solution: Chlorine in ClF3 has seven valence electrons. Three are used to form sigma bonds with three fluorine atoms, leaving four electrons (two lone pairs). The steric number is therefore 5 (three bond pairs + two lone pairs), giving a trigonal bipyramidal electron-pair geometry. To minimize repulsion, the two lone pairs occupy equatorial positions, resulting in a T-shaped molecular geometry. The bond angles are slightly less than 90° and 180° due to lone pair-bond pair repulsion being stronger than bond pair-bond pair repulsion. The hybridization corresponding to steric number 5 is sp3d. Thus ClF3 is T-shaped with sp3d hybridization.
Exam Insight
In NEET, questions on VSEPR often ask for the shape of ions such as ICl4- or XeF4. Remember that for steric number 6 with two lone pairs, the lone pairs occupy opposite axial positions in the octahedral electron-pair geometry, giving a square planar molecular geometry. The bond angles are exactly 90° because the lone pairs are trans to each other and their repulsions cancel in the equatorial plane.

Coordination Compounds

3.1 Werner's Coordination Theory

Alfred Werner's 1893 coordination theory revolutionized the understanding of complex compounds by proposing that metal ions exhibit two types of valence: primary valence (oxidation state), satisfied by anions, and secondary valence (coordination number), satisfied by ligands attached directly to the metal ion in a fixed geometry. Werner's experiments with cobalt(III) chloride-ammonia complexes, such as CoCl3·6NH3 (which precipitates all three chlorides as AgCl), CoCl3·5NH3 (two chlorides precipitated), and CoCl3·4NH3 (one chloride precipitated), demonstrated that only the chloride ions outside the coordination sphere are ionizable. He also resolved optical isomers of [Co(en)3]3+ and geometric isomers of [Co(NH3)4Cl2]+, proving that coordination compounds have definite three-dimensional structures, not chain structures as previously believed.

3.2 Nomenclature and Isomerism

IUPAC nomenclature for coordination compounds follows a systematic order: ligands are named alphabetically before the metal, with prefixes di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa- for simple ligands and bis-, tris-, tetrakis- for complex ligand names. Anionic ligands end in -o (chloro, cyano, hydroxo), neutral ligands keep their common names (aqua, ammine, carbonyl, nitrosyl), and cationic ligands end in -ium. The metal name takes its element name for cationic or neutral complexes and the -ate suffix for anionic complexes (ferrate, cuprate, cobaltate), with the oxidation state in Roman numerals in parentheses. Isomerism in coordination compounds includes structural isomerism (ionization, hydrate, linkage, coordination, and coordination position isomerism) and stereoisomerism (geometric and optical). Geometric isomerism arises in square planar (MA2B2 type) and octahedral (MA4B2, MA3B3) complexes; optical isomerism occurs in octahedral complexes lacking a plane of symmetry, such as [M(AA)3] and [M(AA)2B2] (cis form), where AA is a bidentate ligand like ethylenediamine.

3.3 Crystal Field Theory and Spectrochemical Series

Crystal Field Theory (CFT) treats ligands as point negative charges that split the degenerate d-orbitals of the central metal ion. In an octahedral field, the five d-orbitals split into a lower-energy t2g set (dxy, dyz, dzx) and a higher-energy eg set (dz2, dx2-y2), with a splitting energy Δo. In a tetrahedral field, the splitting is inverted and smaller (Δt ≈ 4/9 Δo). The magnitude of Δo depends on the metal ion (increasing down a group and with higher oxidation state) and the ligand. The spectrochemical series orders ligands by field strength: I- < Br- < SCN- (S) < Cl- < NO3- < F- < OH- < C2O42- < H2O < NCS- (N) < NH3 < en < bipy < phen < NO2- < PPh3 < CN- < CO. Strong-field ligands cause large splitting and favour low-spin configurations; weak-field ligands cause small splitting and favour high-spin configurations.

3.4 Magnetic Properties and Colour

The magnetic behaviour of a complex depends on the number of unpaired electrons. High-spin complexes follow Hund's rule, maximizing unpaired electrons, while low-spin complexes pair electrons in the lower t2g orbitals before occupying eg. For example, [Fe(CN)6]4- (d6, strong field) is low-spin t2g6eg0 with no unpaired electrons (diamagnetic), whereas [Fe(H2O)6]2+ (d6, weak field) is high-spin t2g4eg2 with four unpaired electrons (paramagnetic). The spin-only magnetic moment μ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons, gives a good approximation for first-row transition metals. Colour arises from d-d transitions: absorption of visible light promotes an electron from t2g to eg, and the complementary colour is observed. The wavelength absorbed corresponds to Δo; thus [Ti(H2O)6]3+ (d1) absorbs at about 20,000 cm-1 (green-yellow) and appears purple. Charge-transfer transitions, where an electron moves from ligand to metal (LMCT) or metal to ligand (MLCT), are often more intense and account for the deep colours of MnO4- (purple) and [Fe(phen)3]2+ (red).

s-Block Elements

4.1 Group 1: Alkali Metals

The alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) have a single ns1 valence electron, which they lose readily to form M+ ions, giving them a uniform +1 oxidation state. Their low ionization energies and low electronegativities make them the most electropositive elements. Lithium shows anomalous behaviour due to its small size and high charge density: it forms a stable nitride (Li3N) directly with nitrogen, its carbonate and fluoride are sparingly soluble, and it forms covalent organolithium compounds. The standard electrode potentials (E° M+/M) become more negative down the group (Li -3.04 V, Na -2.71 V, K -2.93 V, Rb -2.98 V, Cs -3.03 V), but lithium's highly negative value is due to its large hydration enthalpy rather than a low ionization energy. All alkali metals react vigorously with water to give MOH and H2, with reactivity increasing down the group. They form ionic oxides (M2O), peroxides (M2O2), and superoxides (MO2); the larger cations stabilize the larger anions, so Li forms mainly the oxide, Na forms the peroxide, and K, Rb, Cs form superoxides.

4.2 Group 2: Alkaline Earth Metals

The alkaline earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra) have an ns2 configuration and predominantly show a +2 oxidation state. Their first ionization energies are higher than those of alkali metals, but the second ionization energy is compensated by the high lattice energies or hydration enthalpies of the M2+ compounds. Beryllium is anomalous: it forms covalent compounds, its oxide and hydroxide are amphoteric, and it does not react with water even at high temperatures. Magnesium reacts slowly with hot water, while Ca, Sr, Ba react readily with cold water. The solubility of hydroxides increases down the group (Be(OH)2 and Mg(OH)2 are sparingly soluble, Ba(OH)2 is quite soluble), while the solubility of sulfates and carbonates decreases down the group due to the faster decrease in hydration enthalpy compared to lattice energy. Thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates increases down the group because larger cations stabilize the large anions better; BeCO3 is unstable, MgCO3 decomposes at 540°C, CaCO3 at 900°C, and BaCO3 at 1360°C.

4.3 Anomalous Behaviour and Diagonal Relationship

Lithium and magnesium exhibit a diagonal relationship due to their similar charge densities (Li+ 76 pm, Mg2+ 72 pm) and electronegativities (Li 0.98, Mg 1.31). Both form nitrides on heating in nitrogen, both form covalent organometallic compounds, both have carbonates that decompose on heating, and both have hydroxides and fluorides that are sparingly soluble. Beryllium and aluminium show a similar diagonal relationship: both form amphoteric oxides and hydroxides, both form covalent halides that are Lewis acids, both form complex ions like [BeF4]2- and [AlF6]3-, and both are rendered passive by concentrated nitric acid due to a protective oxide layer. These relationships arise because moving down a group increases size while moving across a period decreases size, so the two effects partially cancel along the diagonal.

p-Block Elements

5.1 Group 13: Boron Family

Group 13 elements (B, Al, Ga, In, Tl) have an ns2np1 configuration. Boron is a non-metal with a high ionization energy and forms only covalent compounds; it is electron-deficient and acts as a Lewis acid, forming adducts with Lewis bases (BF3·NH3) and complex anions like [BF4]-. Aluminium is a metal but forms a protective oxide layer (Al2O3) that makes it corrosion-resistant. The +3 oxidation state is common for B, Al, Ga, In, but the inert pair effect makes +1 increasingly stable down the group; Tl(I) is more stable than Tl(III), and Tl+ resembles alkali metal ions in size and chemistry. The trihalides are Lewis acids, with acid strength order BF3 < BCl3 < BBr3 (due to pπ-pπ back-bonding in BF3 reducing electron deficiency). Boron forms unusual hydrides (boranes) like B2H6 with three-centre two-electron bonds, and borax (Na2B4O7·10H2O) gives a bead test for metal ions.

5.2 Group 14: Carbon Family

Group 14 (C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb) shows a transition from non-metal to metal. Carbon's unique ability to catenate (form C-C bonds) leads to the vast field of organic chemistry. The +4 oxidation state dominates for C and Si, but the inert pair effect makes +2 increasingly stable for Ge, Sn, and Pb; Pb(II) is more stable than Pb(IV), and PbO2 is a strong oxidizing agent. All elements form tetrahalides (MX4), but stability decreases down the group; PbI4 does not exist. The dioxides (CO2, SiO2, GeO2, SnO2, PbO2) show decreasing acidity: CO2 and SiO2 are acidic, GeO2 and SnO2 are amphoteric, PbO2 is weakly acidic. Silicates, based on the SiO44- tetrahedron, form the bulk of the Earth's crust; they polymerize by sharing corners to give chains, sheets, and three-dimensional frameworks (zeolites).

5.3 Group 15: Nitrogen Family

Group 15 (N, P, As, Sb, Bi) has ns2np3 configuration. Nitrogen differs sharply from the others: it is a diatomic gas (N≡N, bond energy 945 kJ/mol), forms multiple bonds readily (pπ-pπ), and shows oxidation states from -3 to +5. Phosphorus exists as P4 tetrahedra (white P) and polymeric forms (red, black P). The hydrides (NH3, PH3, AsH3, SbH3, BiH3) show decreasing thermal stability and basicity down the group; NH3 is a strong ligand and hydrogen-bonds, while PH3 is a weak ligand and does not hydrogen-bond. Oxides include N2O, NO, N2O3, NO2, N2O5 for nitrogen and P4O6, P4O10 for phosphorus. The oxoacids of nitrogen (HNO2, HNO3) and phosphorus (H3PO2, H3PO3, H3PO4) show varying oxidation states and reducing/oxidizing properties; H3PO2 (hypophosphorous acid) is a strong reducing agent due to two P-H bonds.

5.4 Group 16: Oxygen Family

Group 16 (O, S, Se, Te, Po) has ns2np4. Oxygen is a diatomic gas with strong pπ-pπ bonding; it shows -2 oxidation state (except in OF2 and peroxides) and does not expand its octet. Sulphur forms S8 rings and catenates extensively (S-S bonds up to 20 atoms). The hydrides H2O, H2S, H2Se, H2Te show decreasing bond angles (104.5°, 92°, 91°, 90°) and increasing acidity. Oxides: SO2 (bent, reducing agent), SO3 (trigonal planar, anhydride of H2SO4). Sulphuric acid is manufactured by the Contact process (V2O5 catalyst, 450°C, 2 atm). The oxoacids of sulphur include H2SO3, H2SO4, H2S2O3 (thiosulphuric), H2S2O7 (disulphuric), and peroxoacids like H2SO5 (peroxymonosulphuric, Caro's acid).

5.5 Group 17: Halogens

Group 17 (F, Cl, Br, I, At) are the most electronegative elements, needing one electron to complete an octet.

d-Block Elements: Transition Metals and Their Chemistry

General Electronic Configuration and Oxidation States

The d-block comprises the ten groups 3 to 12, with the general outer configuration (n‑1)d1‑10 ns0‑2. Because the (n‑1)d and ns orbitals are close in energy, transition metals exhibit a wide range of oxidation states. Scandium shows only +3, titanium commonly +4 and +3, vanadium +5 to +2, chromium +6, +3, +2, manganese +7 to +2, iron +3 and +2, cobalt +3 and +2, nickel +2, copper +2 and +1, and zinc exclusively +2. The maximum oxidation state usually equals the group number for groups 3‑7, then declines due to increasing ionization energies and the stabilizing effect of half‑filled (d5) and fully‑filled (d10) subshells.

Atomic and Ionic Radii: Lanthanide Contraction Effect

Across a period the atomic radius contracts steadily because the effective nuclear charge increases while electrons enter the same (n‑1)d shell. The 4d and 5d series are remarkably similar in size (e.g., Zr 160 pm, Hf 159 pm) because the lanthanide contraction — the poor shielding of the 4f electrons — offsets the expected increase down a group. This contraction influences the chemistry of the post‑lanthanide elements, making 5d metals less reactive and more noble than their 4d counterparts.

Magnetic Properties and Colour

Unpaired d‑electrons give rise to paramagnetism. The spin‑only magnetic moment μso = √[n(n+2)] BM (Bohr magnetons) provides a first approximation; orbital contribution can increase the observed moment, especially for ions with T ground terms (e.g., Co2+ high‑spin). Colour in transition‑metal complexes originates from d‑d transitions that are Laporte‑forbidden but gain intensity through vibronic coupling. The energy gap Δo (octahedral) or Δt (tetrahedral) depends on the metal oxidation state, nature of the ligand (spectrochemical series), and geometry, producing the vast palette of colours seen in compounds such as [Cu(H2O)6]2+ (blue) and [Fe(CN)6]4‑ (pale yellow).

Catalytic Activity and Industrial Relevance

Variable oxidation states and the ability to form π‑complexes with unsaturated substrates make transition metals excellent catalysts. Iron (Haber process), vanadium(V) oxide (Contact process), nickel (hydrogenation), and platinum‑group metals (automotive catalytic converters) are textbook examples. Homogeneous catalysts such as Wilkinson’s catalyst [RhCl(PPh3)3] operate via oxidative addition/reductive elimination cycles that exploit the metal’s accessible oxidation states.

f-Block Elements: Lanthanides and Actinides

Lanthanide Series: Electronic Structure and the +3 Dominance

The fourteen lanthanides (Ce–Lu) fill the 4f subshell. The 4f orbitals are radially contracted and shielded by the filled 5s25p6 shells, so they participate little in bonding. Consequently, the +3 oxidation state (loss of 6s2 and one 5d/4f electron) is overwhelmingly stable across the series. Exceptions: Eu2+ (4f7, half‑filled) and Yb2+ (4f14, fully filled) are relatively stable; Ce4+ (4f0) and Tb4+ (4f7) are strong oxidizing agents.

Lanthanide Contraction and Its Consequences

Each successive addition of a 4f electron increases nuclear charge by +1 while shielding increases by only ~0.85, causing a steady decrease in ionic radius from La3+ (103 pm) to Lu3+ (86 pm). This contraction makes the chemistry of the 5d transition metals (Hf, Ta, W) resemble their 4d congeners (Zr, Nb, Mo) and complicates the separation of adjacent lanthanides, which is achieved industrially by solvent extraction or ion‑exchange chromatography exploiting minute differences in complex stability.

Actinide Series: Radioactivity and Variable Oxidation States

Actinides (Ac–Lr) fill the 5f orbitals, which are more extended than 4f and can engage in covalent bonding. Early actinides (Th, Pa, U, Np, Pu) display a rich oxidation‑state chemistry: U commonly +6 (uranyl UO22+), +5, +4, +3; Pu exhibits +3 to +7 in solution. Later actinides (Am onward) behave more like lanthanides, favouring +3. All actinides are radioactive; half‑lives range from 4.5 × 109 yr for 232Th to minutes for the heaviest synthetic isotopes. This radioactivity dictates handling protocols and underpins applications: 235U and 239Pu as nuclear fuel, 241Am in smoke detectors, 252Cf as a neutron source.

Separation and Analytical Techniques

Because of similar ionic radii and identical +3 charge for most lanthanides, separation relies on subtle differences in complex formation constants with ligands such as EDTA, DTPA, or organophosphates. For actinides, oxidation‑state adjustment (e.g., reducing Pu4+ to Pu3+ for selective precipitation) combined with ion‑exchange resins enables purification. Modern analytical methods include ICP‑MS for trace determination and X‑ray absorption spectroscopy (XANES/EXAFS) for speciation in environmental samples.

Technique: Applying the Irving‑Williams Series
Predict the relative order of stability constants (log K) for the octahedral aqua complexes [M(H2O)6]2+ of the first‑row transition metals Mn2+, Fe2+, Co2+, Ni2+, Cu2+, Zn2+.
The Irving‑Williams series states that for high‑spin octahedral M2+ complexes the stability increases across the period: Mn2+ < Fe2+ < Co2+ < Ni2+ < Cu2+ > Zn2+. The peak at Cu2+ arises from the additional Jahn–Teller stabilization of the d9 configuration, while Zn2+ (d10) lacks crystal‑field stabilization energy. Thus the expected log K order is Mn < Fe < Co < Ni < Cu > Zn.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorize the Irving‑Williams series and the nephelauxetic series (F‑ > H2O > NH3 > en > CN‑ > CO) together; they appear in multiple‑choice questions on complex stability and spectral shifts. A quick mental map of the series saves time and prevents sign‑reversal errors.

Advanced Coordination Chemistry: Ligand Field Theory, Stability Constants, and Kinetic Behaviour

From Crystal Field to Ligand Field Theory

Crystal Field Theory (CFT) treats ligands as point charges and accounts for d‑orbital splitting but ignores covalency. Ligand Field Theory (LFT) incorporates molecular‑orbital concepts, allowing π‑donor (e.g., halides) and π‑acceptor (e.g., CO, CN‑) interactions. π‑acceptor ligands lower the t2g set by back‑bonding, increasing Δo and producing strong‑field complexes (low‑spin). The nephelauxetic effect — reduction of interelectronic repulsion parameters (B, C) — quantifies covalency; a larger nephelauxetic ratio β = Bcomplex/Bfree ion indicates greater electron delocalisation onto the ligand.

Thermodynamic Stability: Chelate, Macrocyclic, and Irving‑Williams Effects

The chelate effect arises from the entropy gain when a multidentate ligand displaces several monodentate ligands (ΔS > 0). Macrocyclic ligands (e.g., crown ethers, porphyrins) add a pre‑organization enthalpy advantage, giving exceptionally high formation constants (log K up to 30 for [Fe(porphyrin)]2+). The Irving‑Williams series (Mn2+ < Fe2+ < Co2+ < Ni2+ < Cu2+ > Zn2+) reflects the combined influence of ionic radius, crystal‑field stabilization energy (CFSE), and Jahn–Teller distortion for d9 Cu2+.

Kinetic Lability vs. Inertness: Taube’s Classification

Complexes are labelled labile (fast ligand exchange, t1/2 < 1 min) or inert (slow exchange) based on d‑electron configuration and CFSE. High‑spin d4–d7 octahedral ions (e.g., [Fe(H2O)6]2+) are labile; low‑spin d6 (e.g., [Co(NH3)6]3+) and d3 (e.g., [Cr(H2O)6]3+) are inert because ligand substitution would require loss of CFSE. Square‑planar d8 complexes (e.g., [PtCl4]2‑) are also inert, a fact exploited in cisplatin chemotherapy.

Spectroscopic Probes: UV‑Vis, EPR, and Mössbauer

UV‑Vis absorption bands correspond to d‑d transitions (weak, Laporte‑forbidden) and charge‑transfer bands (intense). Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) detects species with unpaired electrons (e.g., Cu2+, low‑spin Fe3+) and provides g‑values that reveal geometry. Mössbauer spectroscopy (⁵⁷Fe) distinguishes oxidation states, spin states, and coordination environments through isomer shift and quadrupole splitting, making it indispensable for bioinorganic iron‑sulfur clusters.

Metallic Bonding, Band Theory, and Electrical Conductivity in Solids

Electron‑Sea Model and Its Limitations

The classical electron‑sea model pictures a lattice of positive metal ions immersed in a delocalised “sea” of valence electrons, explaining high electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability, and metallic lustre. However, it cannot account for the existence of semiconductors, the temperature dependence of resistivity, or the detailed electronic structure of transition metals where d‑electrons are partially localised.

Band Formation from Atomic Orbitals

When N atoms approach, each atomic orbital splits into N closely spaced molecular orbitals forming a band. The valence band derives from the outermost occupied orbitals (s, p, or d), while the conduction band originates from the next higher set. In conductors (e.g., Na, Cu) the valence band is partially filled or overlaps the conduction band, providing abundant carriers at the Fermi level. In insulators (e.g., diamond, NaCl) a wide band gap (> 5 eV) separates a filled valence band from an empty conduction band. Semiconductors (Si, Ge, GaAs) possess a narrow gap (0.5–2 eV); thermal excitation promotes electrons across the gap, giving conductivity that rises exponentially with temperature.

Fermi Level, Density of States, and Effective Mass

The Fermi level (EF) marks the highest occupied energy at 0 K. In metals EF lies within a band; in intrinsic semiconductors it sits near the middle of the gap. The density of states (DOS) near EF determines the electronic specific heat and Pauli paramagnetism. Curvature of the E(k) dispersion relation defines the effective mass m* = ħ2/(d2E/dk2); electrons near the bottom of a conduction band behave as if they have a mass different from the free electron mass, influencing mobility and Hall coefficient.

Alloys, Superconductivity, and Applications

Substitutional and interstitial alloys modify band structure by altering electron concentration and lattice periodicity. Hume‑Rothery rules predict solid‑solution formation based on atomic size, valency, and electronegativity. Superconductivity — zero resistivity below a critical temperature Tc — arises from Cooper pairing mediated by lattice vibrations (BCS theory) in conventional superconductors (e.g., NbTi, Tc ≈ 9 K) and by unconventional mechanisms in cuprates (YBa2Cu3O7‑δ, Tc > 90 K). These materials underpin MRI magnets, maglev trains, and quantum computing circuits.

Noble Gases: Chemistry of Group 18 Elements

Inertness and the Origin of Reactivity

Group 18 elements (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn, Og) possess closed‑shell configurations (ns2np6 except He 1s2), giving them the highest ionisation energies in each period. This electronic stability makes them chemically inert under normal conditions. Reactivity increases down the group as ionisation energy decreases (Xe = 1170 kJ mol‑1, Rn = 1037 kJ mol‑1) and the outer orbitals become more diffuse, allowing orbital overlap with highly electronegative partners such as fluorine and oxygen.

Known Compounds of Xenon and Krypton

Xenon forms a rich chemistry: XeF2 (linear), XeF4 (square planar), XeF6 (distorted octahedral), oxides XeO3 (explosive) and XeO4 (tetrahedral), and oxyfluorides (XeOF4). Krypton yields only KrF2 (linear) under extreme conditions (electric discharge at −196 °C). Radon compounds are inferred from radiochemical studies but are not isolable due to intense radioactivity. Helium, neon, and argon form no stable neutral compounds; they are trapped in clathrate hydrates (e.g., Ar·6H2O) where the noble gas occupies cages in a hydrogen‑bonded water lattice.

Clathrates, Inclusion Complexes, and Endohedral Fullerenes

Noble gases can be encapsulated in host frameworks without covalent bonding. Gas hydrates (clathrates) form at high pressure and low temperature; they are relevant to natural gas storage and pipeline blockage. Endohedral fullerenes such as He@C60 and Ne@C60 are synthesised by high‑pressure implantation; the confined atoms retain atomic‑like spectra, providing a unique probe of quantum confinement.

Industrial and Scientific Applications

Helium’s low boiling point (4.2 K) makes it indispensable for cryogenics (MRI magnets, superconducting magnets). Argon provides an inert atmosphere for welding and semiconductor crystal growth. Krypton and xenon are used in high‑intensity discharge lamps, excimer lasers (KrF, XeCl), and as propellants for ion thrusters (Xe). Radon’s radioactivity is a health hazard but also a tracer in geophysical studies. Oganesson (element 118) is predicted to be a relativistic noble metal rather than a gas, but its chemistry remains theoretical.

Organometallic Chemistry of Transition Metals: Bonding, Reactivity, and Catalysis

Organometallic compounds contain at least one direct metal–carbon bond. The 18‑electron rule, derived from the filling of nine valence orbitals (one s, three p, five d), guides the stability of many complexes, especially those of the middle and late transition series. Early‑transition‑metal alkyls (e.g., Ti(CH₂Ph)₄) often violate the rule because the metal centre is highly electrophilic and prefers σ‑bonding to carbon without extensive π‑back‑donation. In contrast, carbonyl complexes such as Fe(CO)₅, Mo(CO)₆ and W(CO)₆ obey the rule through strong π‑acceptor CO ligands that lower the metal d‑orbital energy and enable effective back‑bonding.

Key Bonding Models

The Dewar–Chatt–Duncanson model describes metal–ligand interaction for π‑acceptors: σ‑donation from the ligand lone pair to an empty metal d orbital, and π‑back‑donation from a filled metal d orbital into the ligand π* orbital. For σ‑donor/π‑donor ligands (alkyl, hydride, amide) the interaction is dominated by σ‑donation; the metal–carbon bond is largely covalent with significant ionic character when the metal is highly electropositive. Agostic interactions — three‑centre‑two‑electron bonds between a metal centre and a C–H bond of a ligand — are common in electron‑deficient early‑metal alkyls and influence reaction pathways such as β‑hydride elimination.

Fundamental Reaction Types

  • Oxidative addition: a metal centre increases its oxidation state by two and its coordination number by two (e.g., Rh(I) → Rh(III) in Vaska’s complex IrCl(CO)(PPh₃)₂ + H₂ → IrCl(H)₂(CO)(PPh₃)₂).
  • Reductive elimination: the reverse process, forming a new X–Y bond and decreasing the metal oxidation state by two.
  • Migratory insertion: a CO or alkene inserts into a metal–alkyl bond, extending the carbon chain (key step in hydroformylation).
  • β‑Hydride elimination: a hydrogen on the β‑carbon transfers to the metal, generating a metal‑hydride and an alkene; requires a vacant coordination site and a syn‑periplanar geometry.

Catalytic Cycles of Industrial Relevance

The Monsanto acetic acid process uses a Rh(I) carbonyl iodide catalyst that cycles through oxidative addition of CH₃I, migratory insertion of CO, and reductive elimination of acetyl iodide. The related Cativa process employs an Ir(I) catalyst with a promoter (Ru) to improve rate and selectivity. Ziegler–Natta polymerisation relies on TiCl₄/AlR₃ systems where the active site is a Ti‑alkyl that undergoes repeated ethylene insertion. Metallocene catalysts (e.g., Cp₂ZrCl₂ activated by methylaluminoxane) provide single‑site control, yielding polymers with narrow molecular‑weight distributions.

Understanding the electronic requirements of each step — 16‑electron intermediates for oxidative addition, 18‑electron resting states for stability — allows rational ligand design (electron‑rich phosphines, N‑heterocyclic carbenes) to tune activity and selectivity.

Bioinorganic Chemistry: Metalloproteins, Oxygen Transport, and Enzyme Catalysis

Metalloproteins exploit the variable oxidation states, coordination geometries, and Lewis acidity of transition metals to perform biological functions that are difficult for purely organic cofactors. The most studied systems are the heme and non‑heme iron proteins, copper oxidases, and the molybdenum/tungsten enzymes.

Oxygen Transport and Activation

Hemoglobin and myoglobin contain an Fe(II) centre in a porphyrin ring. In the deoxy state the iron is high‑spin (S = 2) and lies ~0.6 Å out of the porphyrin plane; O₂ binding converts it to low‑spin (S = 0) Fe(II)–O₂, pulling the iron into the plane and triggering the cooperative conformational change described by the Monod–Wyman–Changeux model. The distal histidine stabilises the bound O₂ through hydrogen bonding and prevents oxidation to Fe(III). In contrast, cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV) uses a binuclear CuB–Fea3 centre to reduce O₂ to water, coupling the four‑electron reduction to proton translocation across the mitochondrial membrane.

Non‑Heme Iron Enzymes

Enzymes such as methane monooxygenase (MMO) and ribonucleotide reductase (RNR) employ a di‑iron centre. In soluble MMO the di‑Fe(II) site reacts with O₂ to form a high‑valent Fe(IV)–Fe(IV) “diamond” core (often called the Q intermediate) capable of hydroxylating unactivated C–H bonds. The mechanism involves O–O bond cleavage assisted by a bridging carboxylate and a nearby glutamate residue that acts as a proton donor.

Copper Proteins

Type 1 (blue) copper sites (e.g., plastocyanin) have a distorted tetrahedral geometry with a strong Cys→Cu charge‑transfer band at ~600 nm, giving intense blue colour. Their reduction potentials are tuned (≈ +370 mV) for rapid electron transfer. Multicopper oxidases (laccase, ceruloplasmin) contain a trinuclear cluster that reduces O₂ to water via a four‑electron process without releasing reactive oxygen species.

Molybdenum and Tungsten Enzymes

The molybdopterin cofactor (MPT) binds Mo or W through two dithiolene sulfurs. In dimethyl sulfoxide reductase (DMSO reductase) the Mo(VI) centre cycles to Mo(IV) during oxygen‑atom transfer. The “oxo‑transfer” chemistry is central to the global sulfur and nitrogen cycles (e.g., nitrate reductase, sulfite oxidase). Tungsten analogues operate in anaerobic organisms, often at lower redox potentials.

Spectroscopic tools — Mössbauer (⁵⁷Fe), EPR (Cu²⁺, Mo⁵⁺), X‑ray absorption (XANES/EXAFS) — provide oxidation‑state and geometric information on the active sites under turnover conditions, linking structure to function.

Worked Example: Identifying the Oxidation State in a Metalloenzyme Intermediate
In the catalytic cycle of cytochrome c oxidase, the binuclear centre passes through a peroxy‑bridged Fe(III)–O–O–Cu(II) intermediate. Assign the formal oxidation states of Fe and Cu in this species and rationalise the overall charge assuming the protein ligands contribute a net –2 charge.
The peroxy bridge is O₂²⁻ (each O is –1). Let the oxidation states be Fe = x, Cu = y. The total charge of the metal–ligand core = x + y + (–2) + (–2) = x + y – 4. The protein environment supplies a net –2 charge, so the overall complex is neutral: x + y – 4 – 2 = 0 → x + y = 6. Spectroscopic data (EPR silent, Mössbauer δ ≈ 0.5 mm s⁻¹) indicate low‑spin Fe(III) (d⁵, S = ½) and Cu(II) (d⁹, S = ½) antiferromagnetically coupled. Thus x = +3, y = +2, satisfying x + y = 5? Wait, that sums to 5, not 6. The discrepancy is resolved by recognising that the peroxy bridge is better described as O₂⁻ (superoxide) in this intermediate, giving a –1 charge per O. Then total ligand charge = –2 (protein) –2 (peroxy) = –4, and x + y = 4. With Fe(III) and Cu(II) the sum is +5, so the complex carries a +1 charge, consistent with the observed cationic intermediate. Therefore the formal oxidation states are Fe(III) and Cu(II).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for oxidation states in a metalloprotein intermediate, first write the known ligand charges (porphyrin = –2, histidine = 0, cysteine = –1, peroxy/superoxide = –2/–1). Use the overall charge of the protein fragment (often given or implied as neutral) to set up an algebraic sum. Remember that antiferromagnetic coupling can make EPR silent even with odd‑electron metals, so do not rely solely on EPR to assign oxidation states.

Inorganic Reaction Mechanisms: Ligand Substitution, Electron Transfer, and Inner‑Sphere Pathways

Understanding how coordination complexes exchange ligands or transfer electrons is essential for predicting reactivity in catalysis, environmental chemistry, and metallodrug activation. Mechanistic classification follows the Taube‑Pearson framework: associative (A), dissociative (D), interchange (Iₐ, I_d) for substitution; outer‑sphere (OS) and inner‑sphere (IS) for electron transfer.

Substitution Mechanisms

Octahedral d³ (Cr³⁺) and low‑spin d⁶ (Co³⁺) complexes are kinetically inert (t₁/₂ ≈ hours‑days) because ligand field stabilization energy (LFSE) is maximal and the transition state for either A or D pathways loses significant LFSE. In contrast, high‑spin d⁴–d⁷ (e.g., Fe²⁺, Co²⁺) and d⁰/d¹⁰ (Ti⁴⁺, Zn²⁺) are labile (t₁/₂ < ms). Square‑planar d⁸ complexes (Pt²⁺, Pd²⁺) undergo associative substitution via a five‑coordinate trigonal‑bipyramidal transition state; the rate law is second order overall (first order in complex, first order in incoming ligand). The trans‑effect — the ability of a ligand to accelerate substitution trans to itself — follows the series CO > CN⁻ > PR₃ > NO₂⁻ > I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > NH₃ > OH⁻ > H₂O. Strong π‑acceptors stabilise the transition state by delocalising electron density from the metal.

Electron‑Transfer Pathways

Outer‑sphere electron transfer (OS‑ET) occurs without bond making/breaking between the metal centres; the rate depends on the Marcus reorganization energy λ = λin + λout. For self‑exchange reactions (e.g., [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻/⁴⁻) λ is small (≈ 0.5 eV) because the inner‑sphere geometry changes little. Cross‑reaction rates can be predicted from the Marcus cross relation: k₁₂ = (k₁₁ k₂₂ K₁₂ f)¹/², where K₁₂ is the equilibrium constant and f ≈ 1 for similar complexes.

Inner‑sphere electron transfer (IS‑ET) requires a bridging ligand (often halide, OH⁻, N₃⁻, CN⁻) that can coordinate to both metal centres. The classic example is the reduction of [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]²⁺ by [Cr(H₂O)₆]²⁺, where Cl⁻ bridges the two metals. The rate law is second order overall, and the reaction proceeds via a precursor complex formation (Kpre) followed by electron transfer within the bridge (ket). The bridging ligand must have a suitable orbital (π* for halides) to mediate super‑exchange.

Proton‑Coupled Electron Transfer (PCET)

In many biological and energy‑conversion systems (e.g., photosystem II, hydrogenases) electron transfer is coupled to proton movement. The thermodynamic driving force is expressed by the Pourbaix diagram (E vs pH). Concerted PCET avoids high‑energy intermediates (e.g., Fe(IV)=O + H⁺ + e⁻ → Fe(III)–OH) and often shows a kinetic isotope effect (KIE > 2) when H is replaced by D.

Experimental discrimination between OS‑ET and IS‑ET uses isotopic labelling of the bridging ligand, measurement of activation parameters (ΔH‡, ΔS‡), and observation of product distribution (e.g., ligand transfer in IS‑ET).

Solid‑State Defect Chemistry: Point Defects, Non‑Stoichiometry, and Ionic Conductivity

Real crystals deviate from ideal stoichiometry through point defects that govern electrical, optical, and catalytic properties. Kröger‑Vink notation (e.g., VO•• for a doubly positively charged oxygen vacancy, FeFeˣ for a neutral Fe on its lattice site) provides a systematic way to write defect reactions and apply mass‑action laws.

Intrinsic Defects

Schottky defects (cation + anion vacancy pairs) dominate in high‑coordination, highly ionic oxides such as MgO and NaCl. The formation enthalpy ΔHS ≈ 2–3 eV per pair. Frenkel defects (cation vacancy + interstitial cation) are favoured when the cation is much smaller than the anion (e.g., AgCl, ZrO₂). The equilibrium concentration follows [V] = exp(–ΔH/2kT) for Schottky and [V][I] = exp(–ΔH/kT) for Frenkel.

Extrinsic Defects and Doping

Aliovalent substitution creates charge‑compensating defects. Doping ZrO₂ with Y₂O₃ introduces YZr′ (effective –1 charge) and generates oxygen vacancies VO•• for charge neutrality: Y₂O₃ → 2 YZr′ + 3 OOˣ + VO••. The resulting yttria‑stabilised zirconia (YSZ) exhibits high oxide‑ion conductivity (σ ≈ 0.1 S cm⁻¹ at 1000 °C) because vacancies migrate via a vacancy‑hopping mechanism. Similarly, acceptor‑doped perovskites (La₁₋ₓSrₓMnO₃) show mixed electronic/ionic conduction used in solid‑oxide fuel‑cell cathodes.

Non‑Stoichiometry and Defect Equilibria

Transition‑metal oxides often exhibit variable composition (e.g., Fe₁₋ₓO, TiO₂₋ₓ). The defect reaction for wüstite (Fe₁₋ₓO) can be written as: 2 FeFeˣ + ½ O₂(g) ⇌ 2 FeFe• + VFe″ + OOˣ. The equilibrium constant K = [FeFe•]²[VFe″] / pO₂¹/². Measuring conductivity as a function of pO₂ yields the defect type: σ ∝ pO₂⁻¹/⁶ for n‑type (electron) conduction, σ ∝ pO₂¹/⁶ for p‑type (hole) conduction.

Ionic Conductivity and Applications

Fast‑ion conductors require a high concentration of mobile defects and a low migration barrier (< 0.5 eV). β‑alumina (Na₂O·11Al₂O₃) conducts Na⁺ via two‑dimensional planes; NASICON (Na₁₊ₓZr₂SiₓP₃₋ₓO₁₂) provides three‑dimensional Na⁺ pathways. In Li‑ion battery cathodes (LiCoO₂, LiFePO₄), Li⁺ diffusion occurs through vacancy mechanisms; the diffusion coefficient D ~ 10⁻¹¹–10⁻⁸ cm² s⁻¹ at room temperature. Understanding defect chemistry enables rational design of solid electrolytes (e.g., Li₇La₃Zr₂O₁₂ garnet) with high Li⁺ conductivity and electrochemical stability.

Inorganic Photochemistry and Charge‑Transfer Transitions

Fundamentals of Photoinduced Electron Transfer

Inorganic photochemistry is governed by the absorption of photons that promote electrons from ground‑state orbitals to excited states. The most common transitions in transition‑metal complexes are metal‑to‑ligand charge‑transfer (MLCT), ligand‑to‑metal charge‑transfer (LMCT), and intra‑ligand (π→π*) transitions. MLCT bands appear in complexes with low‑lying π* orbitals on ligands such as 2,2′‑bipyridine, phenanthroline, or CO; the classic example is [Ru(bpy)₃]²⁺, which shows a strong absorption at 452 nm (ε ≈ 14 000 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹) assigned to a dπ(Ru) → π*(bpy) transition. LMCT bands dominate when the metal is in a high oxidation state and the ligand possesses filled non‑bonding or π orbitals, as in [FeCl₄]⁻ (Cl⁻ → Fe³⁺) or [MnO₄]⁻ (O²⁻ → Mn⁷⁺). The energy of a charge‑transfer band can be estimated from the difference between the metal redox potential and the ligand redox potential, corrected for Coulombic and solvation terms (the Rehm–Weller equation).

Excited‑State Reactivity and Applications

After photoexcitation, the MLCT state of [Ru(bpy)₃]²⁺ lives for ~600 ns in aqueous solution, allowing it to act as both a strong oxidant (E°(*Ru²⁺/Ru⁺) ≈ +0.84 V vs NHE) and a strong reductant (E°(Ru³⁺/*Ru²⁺) ≈ –0.81 V). This dual redox character underpins its use in photoredox catalysis, dye‑sensitised solar cells (DSSCs), and light‑driven water splitting. In DSSCs, the excited dye injects an electron into the conduction band of TiO₂ (τ_inj < 100 fs), while the oxidised dye is regenerated by a redox mediator (I⁻/I₃⁻ or Co²⁺/Co³⁺ complexes). The overall efficiency depends on the alignment of the dye’s excited‑state energy with the TiO₂ conduction band edge and on the rate of charge recombination, which is suppressed by surface‑anchoring groups and co‑adsorbents.

Ligand‑Field Effects on Photophysics

Strong‑field ligands increase the d‑orbital splitting (Δ₀) and raise the energy of metal‑centered (MC) d‑d states, reducing non‑radiative decay pathways that quench luminescence. Consequently, complexes with π‑acceptor ligands (CN⁻, CO, phosphines) often show intense, long‑lived emission at room temperature (e.g., [Ir(ppy)₃] emits green light at 510 nm with Φ ≈ 0.4). In contrast, weak‑field ligands (H₂O, halides) place MC states low enough to provide efficient internal conversion, giving non‑emissive or weakly emissive species. The energy gap law predicts that the non‑radiative rate constant k_nr scales exponentially with the energy gap between the emitting state and the ground state, explaining why near‑IR emitters (e.g., Yb³⁺, Nd³⁺ lanthanide complexes) require rigid, deuterated ligand environments to achieve measurable quantum yields.

Cluster Chemistry and Metal‑Metal Bonding

Polyhedral Skeletal Electron Pair Theory (PSEPT)

Metal carbonyl clusters and related species obey the Wade–Mingos rules, which relate the total valence electron count (TVEC) to the polyhedral geometry. A closo cluster with n vertices requires 2n + 2 skeletal electron pairs (SEP), a nido cluster 2n + 4 SEP, and an arachno cluster 2n + 6 SEP. For example, [Rh₆(CO)₁₆] has TVEC = 6 × 9 (Rh) + 16 × 2 (CO) = 86 e⁻; subtracting 12 e⁻ per Rh for 18‑electron fragments leaves 86 – 72 = 14 e⁻ = 7 SEP, matching a closo octahedron (n = 6 → 2n + 2 = 14 e⁻). The Rh–Rh distances (~2.70 Å) are consistent with direct metal‑metal bonds supported by a delocalised skeletal bonding framework.

Metal‑Metal Multiple Bonds and Quadruple Bonds

Quadruple bonds (σ²π⁴δ²) are observed in d⁴–d⁴ dimers such as [Mo₂Cl₈]⁴⁻ and [Re₂Cl₈]²⁻. The δ bond arises from overlap of d_xy orbitals in a eclipsed conformation; rotation about the metal‑metal axis destroys δ overlap, leading to a low barrier to rotation in the excited state. The Mo–Mo distance in [Mo₂Cl₈]⁴⁻ is 2.14 Å, significantly shorter than a single bond (~2.70 Å). The electronic configuration can be probed by UV‑Vis (δ→δ* transition near 500 nm) and by photoelectron spectroscopy, which shows a low ionisation energy for the δ electron. Quadruple bonds are rare for first‑row metals because the smaller 3d orbitals give poor δ overlap; instead, Cr₂ and Mo₂ form quintuple bonds only in specially designed ligand environments (e.g., terphenyl‑supported Cr–Cr with a bond order of 5 and a distance of 1.83 Å).

Cluster Reactivity: CO Substitution and Oxidative Addition

Clusters often undergo CO loss to generate coordinatively unsaturated sites that can bind small molecules (H₂, N₂, alkenes). For instance, [Fe₃(CO)₁₂] loses CO thermally to give [Fe₃(CO)₁₁], which adds H₂ across an Fe–Fe edge to form a dihydride cluster. Oxidative addition of alkyl halides to low‑valent clusters increases the metal oxidation state and expands the cluster framework, sometimes converting a closo cluster to a nido geometry. The electron‑counting formalism remains a reliable predictor of the product structure, provided the total electron count is updated after each elementary step.

Worked Example: Electron Counting in a Mixed‑Metal Cluster
Determine the predicted geometry of the heterometallic cluster [FeRu₃(CO)₁₃]⁻ assuming all metals obey the 18‑electron rule and the cluster follows Wade–Mingos rules.
Step 1: Count valence electrons. Fe (group 8) contributes 8 e⁻, each Ru (group 8) contributes 8 e⁻, total metal electrons = 8 + 3 × 8 = 32 e⁻. Thirteen CO ligands donate 2 e⁻ each = 26 e⁻. The overall charge –1 adds 1 e⁻. TVEC = 32 + 26 + 1 = 59 e⁻. Step 2: Subtract 12 e⁻ per metal for 18‑electron fragments (4 metals × 12 = 48 e⁻). Skeletal electrons = 59 – 48 = 11 e⁻ = 5.5 SEP. Step 3: For a four‑vertex cluster, closo requires 2n + 2 = 10 e⁻ (5 SEP), nido requires 12 e⁻ (6 SEP). The count of 11 e⁻ lies between closo and nido, indicating a distorted closo tetrahedron with one open face (a “closo‑nido” intermediate). The predicted geometry is a tetrahedral FeRu₃ core with one Ru–Ru edge elongated, consistent with crystallographic data for related anions.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a cluster problem appears, first write the TVEC, then subtract 12 e⁻ per metal centre. The remainder gives skeletal electron pairs; match to Wade–Mingos (closo 2n+2, nido 2n+4, arachno 2n+6). This systematic approach avoids guesswork and earns full marks for electron‑counting questions.

Inorganic Materials for Energy Storage: Battery Cathodes and Solid Electrolytes

Layered Oxide Cathodes (LiCoO₂, NMC, NCA)

Commercial lithium‑ion batteries rely on layered transition‑metal oxides with the α‑NaFeO₂ structure (space group R‑3m). In LiCoO₂, Li⁺ occupies octahedral 3a sites, Co³⁺ occupies 3b sites, and O²⁻ forms a cubic close‑packed lattice. Upon charging, Li⁺ is extracted (Li₁₋ₓCoO₂) and Co³⁺ oxidises to Co⁴⁺; the theoretical capacity is 274 mAh g⁻¹ (x = 1), but practical cycling limits x ≤ 0.5 to avoid structural collapse to a spinel or rock‑salt phase. Nickel‑rich compositions LiNiₓMnᵧCo₂O₂ (NMC) increase capacity (Ni²⁺/Ni⁴⁺ redox) while Mn⁴⁺ stabilises the lattice. However, Ni²⁺/Li⁺ cation mixing (Ni on Li sites) blocks Li diffusion channels, raising impedance. Surface coatings (Al₂O₃, ZrO₂) and bulk doping (Mg, Ti) mitigate this effect.

Polyanion Cathodes: Olivine LiFePO₄ and NASICON Structures

LiFePO₄ (olivine, Pnma) offers a flat voltage plateau at 3.45 V vs Li⁺/Li, excellent thermal stability, and low cost. Its one‑dimensional Li⁺ diffusion channels along the b‑axis are prone to blockage by antisite defects (Fe on Li sites). Carbon coating and nanosizing reduce the diffusion length and improve rate capability. NASICON‑type phosphates (e.g., LiTi₂(PO₄)₃) provide three‑dimensional Li⁺ pathways with activation energies ~0.3 eV, enabling high‑power applications. The framework tolerates aliovalent substitution (Al³⁺ for Ti⁴⁺) to tune the lattice parameter and conductivity.

Solid Electrolytes: Garnet, Perovskite, and Sulfide Systems

Garnet‑type Li₇La₃Zr₂O₁₂ (LLZO) exhibits Li⁺ conductivity up to 1 mS cm⁻¹ at room temperature when doped with Ta⁵⁺ or Al³⁺ to stabilise the cubic phase. The conduction mechanism involves Li⁺ hopping between tetrahedral and octahedral sites; the bottleneck is the Li–O–Li bond angle. Perovskite Li₃ₓLa₂/₃₋ₓTiO₃ (LLTO) shows high bulk conductivity (~10⁻³ S cm⁻¹) but suffers from grain‑boundary resistance and reduction of Ti⁴⁺ at low potentials. Sulfide electrolytes (Li₁₀GeP₂S₁₂, LGPS) achieve >10 mS cm⁻¹ due to soft, polarisable lattices, yet they are chemically unstable against Li metal and moisture, requiring protective interlayers.

Environmental Inorganic Chemistry: Heavy Metal Speciation and Remediation

Speciation Controls Bioavailability and Toxicity

The toxicity of heavy metals (Hg, Pb, Cd, As, Cr) depends on oxidation state, coordination environment, and ligand type. Methylmercury (CH₃Hg⁺) bioaccumulates because the Hg–C bond resists hydrolysis and the cation crosses the blood–brain barrier via amino‑acid transporters. In contrast, inorganic Hg²⁺ binds strongly to thiol groups in proteins, causing enzyme inhibition. Chromium(VI) as chromate (CrO₄²⁻) is a strong oxidant and carcinogen; Cr(III) is far less toxic and precipitates as Cr(OH)₃ at neutral pH. Arsenic speciation shifts between arsenite (As(III), H₃AsO₃) and arsenate (As(V), H₂AsO₄⁻) with redox potential; As(III) is more mobile and toxic. Accurate risk assessment requires speciation analysis (HPLC‑ICP‑MS, XANES) rather than total elemental concentrations.

Remediation Strategies: Adsorption, Precipitation, and Phytoremediation

Activated carbon, iron‑oxide nanoparticles, and layered double hydroxides (LDHs) adsorb anionic species (CrO₄²⁻, AsO₄³⁻) via inner‑sphere complexation. For cationic metals (Pb²⁺, Cd²⁺), phosphate amendment induces precipitation of pyromorphite (Pb₅(PO₄)₃Cl) or cadmium phosphate, reducing leachability. Phytoremediation exploits hyperaccumulator plants (Thlaspi caerulescens for Zn/Cd, Pteris vittata for As) that translocate metals to shoots; chelating agents (EDTA, citrate) can enhance uptake but risk groundwater contamination. Electrokinetic remediation applies an electric field to migrate charged species toward electrodes, where they are precipitated or captured.

Redox‑Active Minerals in Natural Attenuation

Fe(II)-bearing minerals (magnetite, green rust) reduce Cr(VI) to Cr(III) and U(VI) to U(IV), immobilising them as insoluble oxides. Manganese oxides (birnessite) oxidise As(III) to As(V), which then adsorbs more strongly. The rates depend on surface area, pH, and competing ions (e.g., phosphate competes for adsorption sites). Understanding these mineral‑mediated pathways is essential for predicting plume evolution in contaminated aquifers.

Supramolecular Inorganic Chemistry: Host‑Guest Complexes and Coordination Polymers

Metallacrown Ethers and Metallacalixarenes

Metallacrown ethers incorporate metal ions into the crown‑ether ring, providing both size‑selective cation binding and additional coordination sites for anions or neutral guests. A 12‑MC‑4 metallacrown (12‑membered ring, 4 metal centres) binds Li⁺ with log K ≈ 5.5 in methanol, while the larger 15‑MC‑5 prefers Na⁺. The metal centres (often Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺) can be post‑synthetically functionalised to introduce catalytic activity, yielding bifunctional host‑guest catalysts.

Metal‑Organic Frameworks (MOFs) and Covalent Organic Frameworks (COFs)

MOFs are crystalline coordination polymers built from metal nodes (clusters or single ions) and multitopic organic linkers. The prototypical MOF‑5 (Zn₄O(BDC)₃, BDC = 1,4‑benzenedicarboxylate) has a BET surface area >3000 m² g⁻¹ and pore diameter ~8 Å. Gas adsorption isotherms follow the Langmuir model at low pressure; the isosteric heat of H₂ adsorption in MOF‑5 is ~5 kJ mol⁻¹, suitable for cryogenic storage. Water stability remains a challenge; Zr‑based MOFs (UiO‑66) resist hydrolysis due to strong Zr–O bonds and can be functionalised with –NH₂, –SO₃H groups for proton conduction. COFs, built from reversible covalent bonds (boronate esters, imines), lack metal nodes but achieve comparable porosity; they are explored for photocatalytic water splitting when incorporating porphyrin or triazine units.

Dynamic Covalent Chemistry and Postsynthetic Modification

Postsynthetic exchange (PSE) allows replacement of linkers or metal nodes in pre‑formed frameworks. In UiO‑66, missing‑linker defects create open Zr sites that can be capped with monocarboxylates, tuning pore chemistry. Solvent‑assisted linker exchange (SALE) replaces BDC with functionalised dicarboxylates (e.g., 2‑amino‑BDC) while retaining crystallinity. These strategies enable rapid screening of catalytic sites (e.g., Pd‑doped MOFs for Suzuki coupling) without de novo synthesis.
