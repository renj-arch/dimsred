General Characteristics of the p‑Block

1.1 Position and Electronic Configuration

The p‑block occupies the right‑hand side of the periodic table and comprises groups 13 to 18, where the differentiating electron enters the outermost p orbital. Elements in this block show a progressive increase in the principal quantum number from period 2 to period 7, which governs the size of the valence shell and the energy gap between the s and p subshells. Because the p orbitals are more diffuse than the s orbitals, the effective nuclear charge felt by the valence electrons rises less steeply across a period than in the s‑block, leading to a gradual rather than abrupt change in atomic radii and ionisation energies.

1.2 Trends in Atomic and Ionic Radii

Across a period the atomic radius contracts as the nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the same principal shell; however, the contraction is less pronounced than in the d‑block because the added p electrons shield each other poorly. Down a group the radius expands markedly because a new principal shell is added, and the shielding effect of the inner d and f electrons (from period 4 onward) further reduces the effective nuclear charge experienced by the outer electrons. Consequently, the covalent radius of boron (≈84 pm) is far smaller than that of thallium (≈170 pm), and a similar pattern is observed for the other groups.

1.3 Ionisation Energy and Electronegativity

The first ionisation energy generally rises across a period as the effective nuclear charge grows, but the presence of a half‑filled or fully filled p subshell (as in nitrogen and the noble gases) introduces extra stability that produces local maxima. Down a group the ionisation energy falls because the outer electrons are farther from the nucleus and more heavily shielded. Electronegativity follows the same periodic trend, with fluorine being the most electronegative element (3.98 on the Pauling scale) and the heavier p‑block metals such as lead and bismuth showing values near 1.8. These trends dictate the type of bonding—covalent for the lighter non‑metals, increasingly ionic or metallic for the heavier members.

Group 13 – The Boron Family

2.1 General Properties and Oxidation States

Group 13 elements possess an ns²np¹ valence configuration, giving a characteristic +3 oxidation state derived from loss of the three valence electrons. Boron, being a non‑metal, prefers covalent bonding and rarely attains a formal +3 ionic state; instead it forms electron‑deficient compounds such as BF₃ and BCl₃ that act as Lewis acids. Aluminium, gallium, indium and thallium are metals, and while the +3 state dominates for Al, Ga and In, thallium exhibits a pronounced +1 oxidation state because the 6s² electron pair becomes inert (the so‑called inert‑pair effect). This effect grows stronger down the group as relativistic stabilisation of the s orbital increases.

2.2 Important Compounds of Boron

Boron forms a rich chemistry of hydrides, halides, oxides and borates. Diborane (B₂H₆) is the simplest borane and displays three‑centre two‑electron (3c‑2e) bridging hydrogen atoms, a bonding motif that recurs in higher boranes such as B₅H₉ and B₁₀H₁₄. Boron trifluoride (BF₃) and boron trichloride (BCl₃) are planar trigonal molecules that accept electron pairs from donors like NH₃, forming adducts such as BF₃·NH₃. Boric oxide (B₂O₃) is an acidic oxide that reacts with water to give boric acid (H₃BO₃), a weak monobasic acid that acts as a Lewis acid by accepting OH⁻ to form the tetrahydroxyborate ion [B(OH)₄]⁻. Borates, built from BO₃ triangles and BO₄ tetrahedra, constitute the structural basis of many minerals such as borax (Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O) and colemanite (Ca₂B₆O₁₁·5H₂O).

2.3 Chemistry of Aluminium, Gallium, Indium and Thallium

Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) is amphoteric, dissolving in both acids and bases to give [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ and aluminate [Al(OH)₄]⁻ respectively; this dual behaviour underlies the extraction of aluminium by the Hall‑Héroult process, where Al₂O₃ is dissolved in molten cryolite and electrolysed. Gallium and indium form similar oxides (Ga₂O₃, In₂O₃) that are also amphoteric but less basic than Al₂O₃. Their halides (GaCl₃, InCl₃) are covalent and hydrolyse readily. Thallium(I) compounds such as TlCl, Tl₂O and TlNO₃ are stable and resemble alkali metal salts, whereas thallium(III) compounds (TlCl₃, Tl₂O₃) are strong oxidising agents and decompose to the +1 state. The inert‑pair effect thus not only stabilises the lower oxidation state but also influences the redox chemistry of the heavier group 13 elements.

Worked Example
Predict the structure and bonding in the adduct formed between boron trifluoride and dimethyl ether, and explain why the adduct is more stable than the corresponding adduct with boron trichloride.
Boron trifluoride is a planar trigonal molecule with an empty p orbital on boron that can accept a lone pair from the oxygen atom of dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃). The resulting adduct, BF₃·O(CH₃)₂, adopts a tetrahedral geometry around boron with B–O bond formation. Fluorine is highly electronegative and withdraws electron density from boron, increasing its Lewis acidity; consequently the B–F bonds are strongly polarised and the boron centre is more electron‑deficient than in BCl₃, where chlorine is less electronegative and donates electron density back to boron through pπ–pπ back‑bonding. This makes BF₃ a stronger Lewis acid, so its adduct with dimethyl ether is thermodynamically more stable than the BCl₃ analogue.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks you to compare Lewis acidity of BF₃ and BCl₃, remember that the key factor is the electronegativity of the halogen and the extent of π‑back‑donation; fluorine withdraws electron density and cannot back‑donate, whereas chlorine can, making BF₃ the stronger acid.

Group 14 – The Carbon Family

3.1 Valence Configuration and Oxidation States

Group 14 elements have an ns²np² configuration, allowing oxidation states of +4 (loss of all four valence electrons) and +2 (loss of the two p electrons only). Carbon, a non‑metal, almost exclusively exhibits the +4 state in covalent compounds, while silicon and germanium are metalloids that also favour +4 but can show +2 in some reduced species. Tin and lead are metals; the +2 state becomes increasingly stable down the group because of the inert‑pair effect, so lead(II) compounds such as PbO and PbCl₂ are far more common than lead(IV) analogues.

3.2 Allotropy and Catenation

Carbon displays remarkable allotropy: diamond (sp³ network), graphite (sp² layers), fullerenes (closed cages), carbon nanotubes and graphene. The ability of carbon to catenate—form strong C–C single, double and triple bonds—underlies the vast diversity of organic molecules. Silicon also forms Si–Si bonds but they are weaker (≈226 kJ mol⁻¹) and silicon analogues of organic chains (silanes) are far less stable, hydrolysing rapidly in water. Germanium, tin and lead show progressively diminishing catenation, and their chemistry is dominated by heteroatomic bonds to oxygen, halogens and sulfur.

3.3 Oxides, Halides and Hydrides

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is a linear, non‑polar molecule with double bonds, while silicon dioxide (SiO₂) forms a three‑dimensional network of SiO₄ tetrahedra, giving quartz its hardness and high melting point. Germanium dioxide (GeO₂) adopts both quartz‑type and rutile‑type structures. Tin(IV) oxide (SnO₂) and lead(IV) oxide (PbO₂) are amphoteric, whereas the +2 oxides (SnO, PbO) are basic. The tetrahalides (CCl₄, SiCl₄, GeCl₄, SnCl₄, PbCl₄) are covalent liquids or low‑melting solids; however, PbCl₄ decomposes to PbCl₂ and Cl₂, reflecting the instability of the +4 state for lead. Hydrides such as methane (CH₄), silane (SiH₄), germane (GeH₄) and stannane (SnH₄) become progressively less stable; plumbane (PbH₄) has never been isolated.

3.4 Organometallic Compounds and Applications

Organosilicon compounds (e.g., tetramethylsilane, polydimethylsiloxane) are thermally stable and find use as lubricants, sealants and in semiconductor processing. Organotin compounds such as tributyltin oxide have been employed as biocides and PVC stabilisers, though environmental concerns have restricted their use. Lead organometallics, notably tetraethyllead, were once added to gasoline as antiknock agents but have been phased out worldwide because of lead toxicity. The chemistry of group 14 organometallics illustrates how the decreasing bond strength down the group influences both stability and practical utility.

Group 15 – The Nitrogen Family

4.1 Electronic Structure and Oxidation States

Group 15 elements possess an ns²np³ configuration, giving a half‑filled p subshell that imparts extra stability. The common oxidation states range from –3 (as in NH₃, PH₃) to +5 (as in NO₃⁻, PO₄³⁻, SbCl₅, BiF₅). Nitrogen, being the most electronegative of the group, readily attains –3 in ammonia and +5 in nitrate; phosphorus shows a rich chemistry in +3 (PCl₃, P₂O₃) and +5 (PCl₅, P₂O₅) states. Arsenic, antimony and bismuth increasingly stabilise the +3 state because of the inert‑pair effect, while the +5 state becomes strongly oxidising (e.g., BiF₅ is a powerful fluorinating agent).

4.2 Hydrides, Oxides and Oxyacids

Ammonia (NH₃) is a pyramidal molecule with a lone pair that makes it a good Lewis base and a weak base in water (Kb ≈ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵). Phosphine (PH₃) is far less basic and more toxic, reflecting the poorer overlap of the larger 3p orbital with hydrogen 1s. The oxides N₂O, NO, N₂O₃, NO₂, N₂O₄ and N₂O₅ illustrate the multiple oxidation states of nitrogen; NO and NO₂ are odd‑electron species that dimerise to N₂O₄. Phosphorus forms P₄O₆ (phosphorus(III) oxide) and P₄O₁₀ (phosphorus(V) oxide), the latter being a powerful dehydrating agent that yields phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) on hydrolysis. Arsenic and antimony oxides (As₂O₃, Sb₂O₃) are amphoteric, while bismuth(III) oxide (Bi₂O₃) is basic. The corresponding oxyacids—nitrous acid (HNO₂), nitric acid (HNO₃), phosphorous acid (H₃PO₃), phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄), arsenic acid (H₃AsO₄) and antimonic acid (HSbO₃)—show decreasing acidity down the group.

4.3 Halides and Interhalogen Compounds

Nitrogen forms only NF₃ and NCl₃ (the latter is unstable and explosive), whereas phosphorus yields a full series PCl₃, PCl₅, PBr₃, PI₃ and PF₅. The pentahalides of phosphorus, arsenic and antimony (PF₅, AsF₅, SbF₅) are trigonal‑bipyramidal and act as strong Lewis acids; SbF₅ is a component of the superacid “magic acid” (SbF₅·FSO₃H). Bismuth forms only BiF₅ and BiCl₃; BiF₅ is a potent oxidiser. Interhalogen compounds such as ClF₃, BrF₅ and IF₇ involve group 15 central atoms in high oxidation states and illustrate the ability of the heavier pnictogens to expand their coordination sphere beyond the octet.

4.4 Allotropes and Structural Chemistry

Nitrogen exists as diatomic N₂ with a very strong triple bond (bond dissociation energy ≈ 945 kJ mol⁻¹), making it chemically inert under normal conditions. Phosphorus has several allotropes: white phosphorus (P₄ tetrahedra, highly reactive), red phosphorus (polymeric chains, much less reactive) and black phosphorus (layered structure analogous to graphite). Arsenic, antimony and bismuth adopt metallic layered structures (rhombohedral for As and Sb, hexagonal for Bi) with increasing metallic character. The progressive change from molecular to metallic bonding mirrors the decreasing electronegativity and increasing atomic size down the group.

Group 16 – The Oxygen Family

5.1 General Trends and Oxidation States

Group 16 elements have an ns²np⁴ configuration, typically showing oxidation states of –2 (as in H₂O, H₂S, H₂Se, H₂Te), +2, +4 and +6. Oxygen, the most electronegative element after fluorine, almost exclusively exhibits –2 (except in OF₂ where it is +2 and in peroxides where it is –1). Sulphur, selenium and tellurium readily expand their valence shells using d orbitals (or more accurately, through hypervalent bonding involving p‑orbital participation) to achieve +4 (SO₂, SeO₂, TeO₂) and +6 (SO₃, SeO₃, TeO₃) states. Polonium is a radioactive metal that predominantly shows +2 and +4 states.

5.2 Oxides, Oxoacids and Peroxides

Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) is a bent molecule with resonance‑stabilised S=O bonds; it dissolves in water to give sulphurous acid (H₂SO₃), a diprotic acid that disproportionates to sulphate and sulphide. Sulphur trioxide (SO₃) reacts vigorously with water to form sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), one of the strongest mineral acids and a key industrial chemical. Selenium and tellurium form analogous oxides (SeO₂, TeO₂) and oxoacids (H₂SeO₃, H₂TeO₃; H₂SeO₄, H₂TeO₄) but the acids are weaker and less stable. Peroxides such as H₂O₂, H₂S₂O₈ (peroxydisulphuric acid) and the corresponding salts contain O–O single bonds and act as oxidising agents; the O–O bond energy (≈146 kJ mol⁻¹) is low, making peroxides reactive.

5.3 Halides and Chalcogen Halides

Oxygen forms only OF₂ and O₂F₂ (dioxygen difluoride) under extreme conditions. Sulphur yields a rich series: SCl₂, S₂Cl₂, SCl₄ (unstable), SF₄ (see‑saw), SF₆ (octahedral, exceptionally inert). Selenium and tellurium form SeCl₂, SeCl₄, SeF₄, SeF₆ and TeCl₄, TeF₆ respectively. The hexafluorides SF₆, SeF₆ and TeF₆ are all octahedral and thermally stable; SF₆ is used as an insulating gas in high‑voltage equipment because of its high dielectric strength and chemical inertness. The heavier chalcogen halides are more readily hydrolysed, reflecting the decreasing bond strength and increasing polarizability down the group.

5.4 Allotropes and Structural Diversity

Oxygen exists as O₂ (triplet ground state) and O₃ (ozone, bent, strong oxidiser). Sulphur shows numerous allotropes: cyclic S₈ rings (orthorhombic α‑sulphur), polymeric chains (plastic sulphur) and high‑pressure forms. Selenium adopts helical chains in grey selenium (metallic conductor) and Se₈ rings in red selenium. Tellurium crystallises in a helical chain structure similar to grey selenium, giving it semiconducting properties. Polonium, being a metal, adopts a simple cubic lattice. The variety of allotropes stems from the ability of the chalcogens to form single, double and coordinate bonds and to polymerise through catenation, which diminishes down the group.

Group 17 – The Halogen Family

6.1 Electronic Configuration and Oxidation States

Halogens have an ns²np⁵ configuration, one electron short of a noble‑gas configuration. The most common oxidation state is –1 (as in halide ions X⁻). Positive oxidation states appear in interhalogen compounds and oxyhalides: +1 (ClO⁻), +3 (ClO₂⁻), +5 (ClO₃⁻), +7 (ClO₄⁻). Fluorine, being the most electronegative element, never exhibits a positive oxidation state. The ability to access higher oxidation states increases down the group because the larger, more polarizable atoms can accommodate more electron density in expanded valence shells.

6.2 Hydrogen Halides and Acid Strength

Hydrogen fluoride (HF) is a weak acid in water (Ka ≈ 6.8 × 10⁻⁴) because the H–F bond is very strong (≈565 kJ mol⁻¹) and the fluoride ion is heavily solvated. Hydrogen chloride (HCl), hydrogen bromide (HBr) and hydrogen iodide (HI) are strong acids, dissociating completely in aqueous solution; the acid strength increases down the group as the H–X bond weakens (H–Cl ≈ 432 kJ mol⁻¹, H–Br ≈ 366 kJ mol⁻¹, H–I ≈ 298 kJ mol⁻¹). The trend is a classic illustration of bond‑strength control over acidity in binary acids.

6.3 Interhalogen Compounds and Polyhalides

Interhalogens such as ClF, BrF₃, IF₅ and ICl₃ are formed by direct combination of the elements; they adopt geometries predicted by VSEPR (linear, T‑shaped, square‑pyramidal, etc.). The central atom is always the larger, less electronegative halogen. Polyhalide ions (e.g., I₃⁻, Br₃⁻, Cl₃⁻) exist in solution and in solid salts; they are linear and stabilized by charge delocalisation over the three halogen atoms. These species are important in halogen chemistry and in the extraction of iodine from brine.

6.4 Oxides, Oxyacids and Oxidising Power

Chlorine forms a series of oxides (Cl₂O, ClO₂, Cl₂O₆, Cl₂O₇) and corresponding oxyacids (HOCl, HClO₂, HClO₃, HClO₄). The oxidising power increases with oxidation state: perchloric acid (HClO₄) is a very strong acid but a relatively mild oxidiser, whereas chlorine dioxide (ClO₂) is a powerful oxidising agent used in bleaching. Bromine and iodine have analogous series (HBrO, HBrO₂, HBrO₃, HBrO₄; HIO, HIO₂, HIO₃, HIO₄) but the higher oxyacids are less stable. The standard reduction potentials for the X₂/X⁻ couples (F₂/F⁻ +2.87 V, Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36 V, Br₂/Br⁻ +1.07 V, I₂/I⁻ +0.54 V) quantify the decreasing oxidising ability down the group.

Group 18 – The Noble Gases

7.1 Inertness and Electronic Structure

The noble gases possess a closed‑shell ns²np⁶ configuration (except helium, 1s²), which gives them exceptionally high ionisation energies and very low electron affinities. This electronic stability makes them chemically inert under normal conditions. Helium has the highest first ionisation energy of all elements (24.6 eV), and the values decrease down the group (Ne 21.6 eV, Ar 15.8 eV, Kr 14.0 eV, Xe 12.1 eV, Rn 10.7 eV). The decreasing ionisation energy correlates with the increasing reactivity of the heavier noble gases.

7.2 Compounds of Xenon and Krypton

Xenon forms a well‑established chemistry, beginning with the discovery of xenon hexafluoroplatinate (Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻) by Neil Bartlett in 1962. Stable binary fluorides XeF₂ (linear), XeF₄ (square planar) and XeF₆ (distorted octahedral) are obtained by direct reaction of xenon with fluorine at elevated temperature and pressure. Xenon oxides (XeO₃, XeO₄) and oxyfluorides (XeOF₄, XeO₂F₂) are also known; XeO₃ is a colourless explosive solid. Krypton forms only KrF₂ (linear) under similar conditions, and its chemistry is far more limited because the Kr–F bond is weaker and the ionisation energy of krypton is higher than that of xenon. Argon compounds (e.g., HArF) have been observed only in matrix isolation at cryogenic temperatures.

7.3 Clathrates and Inclusion Compounds

Noble gases can be trapped in the cavities of host lattices to form clathrate hydrates (e.g., Ar·6H₂O, Kr·6H₂O, Xe·6H₂O) where the guest atoms occupy polyhedral water cages stabilized by hydrogen bonding. These inclusion compounds are important for gas storage and separation technologies. The size match between the noble‑gas atom and the cage determines the stability; xenon forms the most stable clathrates because its atomic radius fits the larger cages optimally.

7.4 Applications and Environmental Significance

Helium is used as a cryogenic coolant for superconducting magnets and as an inert shielding gas in welding. Neon lights exploit the bright red‑orange emission of excited neon atoms. Argon provides an inert atmosphere for metallurgical processes and for the preservation of historical documents. Krypton and xenon find use in high‑intensity discharge lamps, laser media and as anaesthetics (xenon). Radon, a radioactive decay product of uranium, is a health hazard because its α‑emitting daughters can be inhaled; monitoring indoor radon levels is a standard public‑health practice. The diverse applications of the noble gases illustrate how their once‑thought absolute inertness has been overcome by both chemical ingenuity and physical exploitation of their unique properties.

Anomalous Behaviour of the First Element in Each p‑Block Group

The first member of every p‑block group displays properties that deviate markedly from the rest of the group. The anomalies arise from three intertwined factors: (i) the absence of low‑lying d‑orbitals, (ii) a very high charge density due to the small atomic radius, and (iii) the unusually high electronegativity of the first element. In Group 13, boron is a non‑metal with a strong tendency to form covalent, electron‑deficient compounds such as B₂H₆ and BF₃, whereas aluminium and heavier congeners are metallic and readily form ionic Al³⁺ species. Boron also shows a maximum coordination number of four (tetrahedral sp³) while Al, Ga, In, Tl can expand to six using vacant d‑orbitals.

In Group 14, carbon’s ability to catenate (C–C single, double, triple bonds) and to form a vast array of π‑conjugated systems has no parallel in Si, Ge, Sn, Pb. Silicon prefers σ‑bonded networks (Si–O–Si) and does not form stable multiple bonds under normal conditions. The high ionisation energy of carbon (1086 kJ mol⁻¹) and its small size give rise to strong C–C and C–H bonds, making organic chemistry a distinct discipline.

Group 15’s nitrogen forms a very strong N≡N triple bond (bond dissociation energy 945 kJ mol⁻¹) and a wide range of oxidation states from –3 to +5. Heavier pnictogens (P, As, Sb, Bi) favour the +3 and +5 states but lack the ability to form stable multiple bonds with themselves; P₄ is a tetrahedral cage, not a chain. Nitrogen’s high electronegativity (3.04) also makes its hydride NH₃ a strong base, whereas PH₃ is a very weak base.

In Group 16, oxygen’s high electronegativity (3.44) and small size lead to strong O–H bonds (463 kJ mol⁻¹) and the unique ability to form hydrogen bonds, giving water its anomalous boiling point. Sulphur, selenium and tellurium form weaker chalcogen–hydrogen bonds and do not hydrogen‑bond to the same extent. Oxygen also exists as a diatomic gas O₂ with a double bond, while the heavier elements prefer polymeric or ring structures (S₈, Se₈).

Group 17’s fluorine is the most electronegative element (3.98) and forms the strongest H–F bond (565 kJ mol⁻¹). It does not exhibit positive oxidation states (except in the exotic F₂O) and its chemistry is dominated by the F⁻ ion. Chlorine, bromine and iodine readily show +1, +3, +5, +7 states in oxyacids and interhalogens. Fluorine’s small radius also leads to a very high lattice energy in fluorides, making many metal fluorides exceptionally stable.

Group 18’s helium and neon have closed‑shell configurations with no low‑lying vacant orbitals; they form no stable neutral compounds under normal conditions. Argon, krypton and xenon can be oxidised by powerful fluorinating agents because their ionisation energies drop sufficiently (Xe = 12.1 eV). The first element of each group thus sets a chemical “benchmark” that the heavier members approach only gradually.

Inert Pair Effect and Its Influence on Oxidation State Stability

The inert pair effect describes the reluctance of the ns² electron pair to participate in bonding for the heavier p‑block elements (especially Groups 13–16). It becomes pronounced from the fourth period onward because the (n‑1)d and (n‑2)f electrons shield the nucleus poorly, causing a contraction of the ns orbital (relativistic stabilisation) and an expansion of the np orbitals. Consequently, the energy gap between ns and np widens, making promotion of the ns² electrons to np energetically unfavourable.

In Group 13, thallium exhibits a stable +1 oxidation state (Tl⁺, 6s²) alongside the +3 state (Tl³⁺, 6s⁰). The standard potential Tl³⁺/Tl⁺ is +1.25 V, indicating that Tl³⁺ is a strong oxidising agent and readily reduced to Tl⁺. Thallium(I) compounds (TlCl, Tl₂O) are more stable than their Tl(III) analogues, which are strong oxidisers and hydrolyse in water.

Group 14 shows the effect in lead: Pb²⁺ (6s²) is far more stable than Pb⁴⁺ (6s⁰). Lead(IV) oxide (PbO₂) is a powerful oxidising agent used in lead‑acid batteries, whereas lead(II) oxide (PbO) is the common, stable form. The standard potential PbO₂/Pb²⁺ is +1.46 V, confirming the oxidising nature of Pb(IV). Tin also shows a +2 state (Sn²⁺) but the +4 state (Sn⁴⁺) remains accessible because the 5s² pair is less stabilised.

In Group 15, bismuth’s +3 state (Bi³⁺, 6s²) dominates; Bi(V) compounds (BiF₅, NaBiO₃) are strong oxidisers and exist only with highly electronegative ligands. The Bi⁵⁺/Bi³⁺ couple has a potential of +1.6 V. Antimony shows a less pronounced effect; Sb(III) and Sb(V) are both common, but Sb(V) is more oxidising.

Group 16 illustrates the effect in polonium: Po(IV) (PoO₂) is more stable than Po(VI) (PoO₃), which is only known in the gas phase. For tellurium, Te(IV) (TeO₂) is the prevalent oxide, while Te(VI) (TeO₃) requires strong oxidising conditions. The decreasing stability of the higher oxidation state down the group correlates with the increasing ionisation energy required to remove the ns² electrons.

The inert pair effect also influences structural chemistry: Tl⁺ and Pb²⁺ often adopt stereochemically active lone pairs, leading to distorted coordination geometries (e.g., hemidirected environments in TlCl and PbO). In contrast, the higher oxidation states (Tl³⁺, Pb⁴⁺) are typically regular (octahedral) because the lone pair is absent. Understanding this effect is essential for predicting redox behaviour, stability of oxides, and the design of lead‑free materials.

Worked Example: Predicting the Dominant Oxidation State
A student is asked: “Which oxidation state of thallium is more stable in aqueous solution, +1 or +3? Justify using the inert pair effect and standard electrode potentials.”
The +1 oxidation state (Tl⁺, 6s²) is more stable. The inert pair effect stabilises the 6s² electron pair in heavy Group 13 elements, making promotion to 6s⁰ (Tl³⁺) energetically costly. The standard reduction potential for Tl³⁺ + 2e⁻ → Tl⁺ is +1.25 V, indicating Tl³⁺ is a strong oxidising agent and will be reduced to Tl⁺ in water. Therefore, Tl⁺ predominates in aqueous chemistry, while Tl³⁺ exists only in strongly oxidising or complexed environments.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the “most stable oxidation state” of a heavy p‑block element, first check the group trend for the inert pair effect. Write the ns² configuration, state that the ns² pair is reluctant to ionise, and quote the relevant standard potential if known. This two‑step reasoning (electronic structure + thermodynamic data) earns full marks.

Thermodynamic and Kinetic Factors Governing the Stability of Higher Oxidation States

Higher oxidation states in the p‑block are stabilised by a combination of high ligand electronegativity, strong π‑donation, and favourable lattice or solvation energies. Fluorine and oxygen are the most effective ligands because they withdraw electron density strongly and can engage in π‑bonding with the central atom’s vacant d‑orbitals (for periods ≥ 4). For example, XeF₆, XeO₄, and IF₇ exist only with fluorine or oxygen donors; chlorine analogues (ClF₇, ClO₄) are unknown because the central atom cannot accommodate seven ligands sterically and electronically.

The thermodynamic cycle for forming a high‑oxidation‑state fluoride MXₙ (M = p‑block element, X = F) involves: (1) sublimation of M, (2) ionisation to Mⁿ⁺, (3) dissociation of F₂, (4) electron affinity of F, and (5) lattice energy of MXₙ. The large lattice energy of fluorides (due to small F⁻ radius) often compensates the high ionisation energies required for Mⁿ⁺. This is why BiF₅, PbF₄, and SbF₅ are stable, whereas the corresponding chlorides (BiCl₅, PbCl₄) are not; the larger Cl⁻ gives a much lower lattice energy.

Kinetic stability also matters. Many high‑oxidation‑state oxides (e.g., Cl₂O₇, Mn₂O₇) are powerful oxidisers but decompose explosively because the M–O bonds are weak and the O–O bond formation is favourable. In contrast, perchlorate (ClO₄⁻) is kinetically inert in aqueous solution despite Cl being in +7 state; the tetrahedral ClO₄⁻ ion has no low‑energy pathway for reduction without a strong reductant. This kinetic barrier is exploited in analytical chemistry where perchlorate serves as a non‑coordinating anion.

Relativistic effects further stabilise the highest oxidation states for the heaviest elements. For example, the 6s orbital contraction in gold (though a d‑block element) and in thallium, lead, bismuth makes the 6s² pair less available, but the 6p orbitals expand, allowing better overlap with ligand orbitals. In xenon, relativistic lowering of the 5p orbital energy facilitates Xe–F bonding, making XeF₂, XeF₄, XeF₆ accessible. Without relativistic corrections, the ionisation energy of xenon would be too high for practical fluorination.

Solvent effects can shift the apparent stability. In superacid media (HF/SbF₅), even normally inert species like Xe can be protonated to give XeH⁺, and high oxidation state cations such as ClO₃⁺ are observed. The extremely low nucleophilicity of the medium prevents reduction. Similarly, in molten salts, high oxidation state anions (e.g., PtF₆⁻) stabilise cationic xenon species (Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻). These non‑aqueous environments are essential for isolating otherwise fleeting high‑oxidation‑state compounds.

Comparative data: Standard potentials for the X₂/X⁻ couples (F₂/F⁻ +2.87 V, Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36 V, Br₂/Br⁻ +1.07 V, I₂/I⁻ +0.54 V) illustrate the decreasing thermodynamic driving force for halogen oxidation. For the p‑block central atoms, the Mⁿ⁺/M⁽ⁿ⁻²⁾⁺ potentials become more positive down a group, reflecting the increasing difficulty of accessing the higher state. Mastery of these thermodynamic cycles enables prediction of whether a given high oxidation state can be isolated in the solid state, in solution, or only in the gas phase.

Industrial Synthesis of Key p‑Block Compounds: Contact Process, Ostwald Process, and Chlor‑Alkali Industry

The Contact Process produces sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) on a megatonne scale. The core reaction is the catalytic oxidation of SO₂ to SO₃: 2 SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2 SO₃(g) ΔH = –198 kJ mol⁻¹. A vanadium(V) oxide (V₂O₅) catalyst on a silica support operates at 450 °C and 1–2 atm. The equilibrium is favoured by low temperature (Le Chatelier) but the rate requires a compromise temperature; excess O₂ and removal of SO₃ by absorption in 98 % H₂SO₄ drive conversion > 99.5 %. The absorbed SO₃ forms oleum (H₂S₂O₇), which is diluted to the desired acid concentration.

The Ostwald Process manufactures nitric acid (HNO₃) from ammonia. The first step is catalytic oxidation of NH₃ over a platinum‑rhodium gauze at 850–950 °C: 4 NH₃ + 5 O₂ → 4 NO + 6 H₂O. The NO is further oxidised to NO₂ (2 NO + O₂ → 2 NO₂) and absorbed in water to give HNO₃ (3 NO₂ + H₂O → 2 HNO₃ + NO). The NO is recycled. The overall yield is ~ 95 % based on NH₃. The high temperature is necessary for kinetic reasons; the Pt‑Rh alloy resists poisoning and sintering.

The Chlor‑Alkali Industry simultaneously produces chlorine, hydrogen, and sodium hydroxide (or potassium hydroxide) by electrolysis of brine (NaCl solution). Three cell technologies exist: mercury cell (phased out), diaphragm cell, and membrane cell. The membrane cell uses a cation‑exchange membrane (e.g., Nafion) that allows Na⁺ to pass but blocks Cl⁻ and OH⁻, yielding high‑purity NaOH (32 % w/w) and Cl₂ gas at the anode (2 Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻). The overall reaction: 2 NaCl + 2 H₂O → Cl₂ + H₂ + 2 NaOH. Energy consumption is ~ 2.5 MWh per tonne of Cl₂. The membrane process dominates new installations because of lower environmental impact.

These processes illustrate how thermodynamic equilibria (Contact, Ostwald) and electrochemical potentials (Chlor‑Alkali) are harnessed industrially. Catalysts lower activation barriers; temperature and pressure are tuned to balance rate and equilibrium; product removal shifts equilibria. The choice of materials (V₂O₅, Pt‑Rh, ion‑exchange membranes) reflects the need for corrosion resistance, selectivity, and longevity under harsh conditions.

Environmental considerations drive modern modifications: double‑contact double‑absorption (DCDA) in the Contact Process reduces SO₂ emissions to < 0.1 % of feed; selective catalytic reduction (SCR) of NOₓ in Ostwald tail gases; and zero‑mercury membrane cells eliminate Hg pollution. Understanding the underlying p‑block chemistry (redox of S, N, Cl) is essential for process optimisation and for developing greener alternatives such as electrochemical H₂SO₄ synthesis or plasma‑assisted NH₃ oxidation.

Environmental Impact and Green Chemistry Aspects of p‑Block Elements

Heavy p‑block metals (Pb, Hg, Cd, As, Sb) are persistent toxic pollutants. Lead from historic gasoline additives and paint contaminates soils; its bioavailability is controlled by pH and organic matter. The inert pair effect stabilises Pb²⁺, which mimics Ca²⁺ in biological systems, disrupting heme synthesis and neurodevelopment. Remediation uses phosphate amendments to precipitate pyromorphite (Pb₅(PO₄)₃Cl), a highly insoluble mineral.

Arsenic contamination of groundwater (especially in the Bengal basin) arises from reductive dissolution of Fe‑oxyhydroxides that release adsorbed As(III) and As(V). As(III) is more mobile and toxic; oxidation to As(V) followed by adsorption onto Fe‑oxides or coagulation with ferric salts is a standard treatment. The redox chemistry of As (As(III)/As(V) couple, E° = +0.56 V) is central to both mobility and remediation.

Mercury’s global cycle involves elemental Hg⁰ (volatile, long atmospheric residence), oxidation to Hg²⁺ (soluble, deposited), and methylation to methylmercury (CH₃Hg⁺) by anaerobic microbes. Methylmercury bioaccumulates in aquatic food webs. The Minamata Convention targets Hg emissions from artisanal gold mining, coal combustion, and chlor‑alkali plants (mercury‑cell process). Alternative membrane cells eliminate Hg use.

Green chemistry principles applied to p‑block synthesis include: (1) atom‑economy – the direct oxidation of SO₂ to SO₃ in the Contact Process has 100 % atom economy; (2) safer solvents – supercritical CO₂ replaces organic solvents in some fluorination reactions; (3) catalysis – heterogeneous V₂O₅ and Pt‑Rh catalysts are recycled; (4) design for degradation – perfluorinated compounds (PFCs) are being replaced by shorter‑chain fluorotelomers that degrade more readily; (5) renewable feedstocks – bio‑based methanol can replace fossil‑derived feedstock for formaldehyde (CH₂O) production.

Emerging technologies exploit p‑block chemistry for sustainability: (i) CO₂ capture using amine‑functionalised silica (N‑based sorbents); (ii) nitrogen fixation via electrochemical reduction of N₂ to NH₃ at ambient conditions using Mo‑ or Fe‑based catalysts (still laboratory scale); (iii) phosphorus recovery from wastewater as struvite (NH₄MgPO₄·6H₂O) to close the P cycle; (iv) halogen‑free flame retardants based on phosphorus‑nitrogen synergists, reducing persistent brominated diphenyl ethers.

Life‑cycle assessment (LCA) of p‑block products quantifies impacts from mining (e.g., bauxite for Al, phosphate rock for P) through manufacturing to end‑of‑life. Aluminium’s high recycling rate (≈ 75 %) and low energy requirement for secondary production (5 % of primary) make it a model for circular economy. Conversely, the low recycling rate of rare p‑block elements (Ga, In, Te) used in photovoltaics and LEDs poses supply‑risk and environmental challenges. Designing products for disassembly and developing hydrometallurgical recovery routes are active research areas.

  • Assuming the first element of a group follows the same oxidation‑state trend as the heavier members (e.g., expecting B to show a stable +1 state like Tl).
  • Confusing the inert pair effect with simple “lower oxidation state becomes more stable down the group” without invoking relativistic ns² stabilisation and ligand‑field arguments.
  • Neglecting kinetic barriers when judging the stability of high oxidation states (e.g., treating ClO₄⁻ as a strong oxidiser in aqueous solution because Cl is +7).
  • Overlooking the role of lattice energy in stabilising fluorides versus chlorides of heavy p‑block elements.
  • Misidentifying the industrial process that produces a given compound (e.g., attributing HNO₃ production to the Contact Process).
  • Failing to connect environmental toxicity to the underlying electronic structure (e.g., not linking Pb²⁺ toxicity to its 6s² inert pair mimicking Ca²⁺).
  • Treating green‑chemistry metrics as qualitative only; exam questions often require quantitative atom‑economy or E‑factor calculations for p‑block syntheses.
