🎭 Part 1: The Story That Measured the Earth

Story Time: How a Stick Cast a Shadow and Measured the World

Around 240 BC, a Greek scholar named Eratosthenes did something that sounds impossible: he measured the entire circumference of the Earth using nothing but a stick, a shadow, and his brain.

He heard a rumor: in the city of Syene (modern-day Aswan, Egypt), at noon on the summer solstice, the sun shone straight down a well — no shadow at all. The sun was directly overhead.

But in Alexandria, 800 km north, at the exact same moment, a stick (called a gnomon) cast a shadow. The angle of that shadow was about 7.2°.

🌍 Ancient Breakthrough (Eratosthenes, 240 BC)
The Stick That Weighed the World
Eratosthenes reasoned: If the Earth were flat, the sun's rays would be parallel — both cities would have the same shadow (or none). But Alexandria has a 7.2° shadow while Syene has none. That 7.2° is the Earth's curvature between the two cities.

The calculation: 7.2° is 1/50th of a full circle (360° ÷ 7.2° = 50). So the distance between the cities (800 km) is 1/50th of Earth's circumference. 800 km × 50 = 40,000 km — remarkably close to the actual value of 40,075 km!
💡 Triangles + Angles = Measure Anything. Height of a pyramid, distance to a ship, size of the Earth — all come down to the same idea: trigonometry.
☀️ Watch the Shadow Change with the Sun's Angle
As the sun's position changes (simulated above), the shadow length changes. At noon, the shadow is shortest. Eratosthenes measured this angle — just 7.2° — and used it to calculate Earth's circumference with remarkable accuracy.
🌟 First Big Idea
Trigonometry is the study of how angles and side lengths in triangles are connected.
Measure an angle, calculate a distance. That's ALL it is. Everything else is details.

From one angle (7.2°) and one distance (800 km), Eratosthenes measured the whole planet. That's the power of trigonometry — it's the origin of all measurement.

🕐 Part 3: The Unit Circle — The Clock That Tells Coordinates

Why the Circle is the Key to All Trig

A right triangle can only have angles between 0° and 90°. But what about 120°? 200°? 360°?

Enter the Unit Circle: a circle of radius 1, centered at the origin. For any angle θ, the point where the ray hits the circle has coordinates (cos θ, sin θ).

🔄 Watch the Point Trace the Circle — See Sin & Cos as Coordinates
The point on the unit circle at angle θ has coordinates (cos θ, sin θ). As θ goes from 0° to 360°, cos and sin smoothly cycle — this is why they're called circular functions. The sine wave below is just the y-coordinate as the angle increases!
🌟 Third Big Idea
sin θ = y-coordinate, cos θ = x-coordinate on the unit circle.
Cosine comes first because "co-" means "together" — like coordinates. Cosine = x, Sine = y. Always.
🧮 Part 4: Always True — The Three Identities

Pythagoras on the Circle

On the unit circle, the radius is 1. The point (cos θ, sin θ) is always on the circle. So by Pythagoras:

(cos θ)² + (sin θ)² = 1²  →  sin²θ + cos²θ = 1
📐 Pythagoras on the Circle — The Most Beautiful Equation in Trig
In the unit circle, the horizontal leg is cos θ (x-coordinate), the vertical leg is sin θ (y-coordinate), and the hypotenuse is 1 (the radius). Pythagoras says cos²θ + sin²θ = 1. It's just the Pythagorean theorem!
🧮 The Three Identities Are Actually ONE Identity ▶

Starting point: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1

Divide by cos²θ:

sin²θ/cos²θ + cos²θ/cos²θ = 1/cos²θ  →  tan²θ + 1 = sec²θ

Divide by sin²θ:

sin²θ/sin²θ + cos²θ/sin²θ = 1/sin²θ  →  1 + cot²θ = csc²θ

That's it! Three identities from ONE — just divided differently. No need to memorize all three!

The Angle Addition Machine — What's sin(75°)?

You know sin(30°) = 0.5 and sin(45°) ≈ 0.707. But what about sin(75°)? (75° = 30° + 45°). Can we figure it out from what we already know?

Yes! And the formula is surprisingly beautiful:

sin(A+B) = sinA·cosB + cosA·sinB
📐 The Geometric Proof — Two Triangles Stacked
Two right triangles stacked: one with angle A, one with angle B on top. The total height is sin(A+B). That height splits into two rectangles: the bottom part = (cos A)(sin B), the top part = (sin A)(cos B). Added together, they give sin(A+B)!
🧠 Memory Trick
Sine keeps the sign, Cosine changes it
sin(A + B): "Sine = sign-same" → plus stays plus in the middle.
sin(A + B) = sinA cosB + cosA sinB

cos(A + B): "Cosine = co-sign-changes" → plus becomes minus in the middle.
cos(A + B) = cosA cosB − sinA sinB

For sin(A−B) and cos(A−B), just flip the sign: sin(A−B) = sinA cosB − cosA sinB, cos(A−B) = cosA cosB + sinA sinB.
🧠 Don't memorize 4 formulas. Just remember: Sine keeps the sign, Cosine changes it.
🔄 Part 5: Inverse Trig — The Undo Button

Ctrl+Z for Trigonometry!

You know sin(30°) = ½. That's the forward journey: angle → ratio.

But what if you want to go backward? "Which angle gives me the ratio ½?" That's inverse trig: ratio → angle.

sin(30°) = ½   ⟹   sin⁻¹(½) = 30°
🔁 Forward (sin) and Backward (sin⁻¹)
sin takes an angle and gives a ratio. sin⁻¹ (also written as arcsin) takes a ratio and gives back the angle. Like pressing Ctrl+Z — it undoes the sin operation!
🌟 Fourth Big Idea
Inverse trig is just asking: "What angle produced this ratio?"
sin⁻¹(½) = 30° means "the angle whose sine is ½ is 30°." That's it.
🔄 Part 6: Which Trig Functions are Positive? — The ASTC Rule

All Students Take Coffee — The Sign of the Four Quadrants

On the unit circle, the sign of sin, cos, and tan changes depending on which quadrant the angle is in. There's a simple memory trick to keep track:

☕ Memory Trick
All Students Take Coffee
Q1 — All functions are positive.
Q2 — Students: Sine is positive (cos, tan negative).
Q3 — Take: Tangent is positive (sin, cos negative).
Q4 — Coffee: Cosine is positive (sin, tan negative).

Why? On the unit circle, cos = x-coordinate, sin = y-coordinate. In Q2, x is negative (cos negative) but y is positive (sin positive). Tan = sin/cos, so when sin and cos have opposite signs, tan is negative!
🧠 ASTC = "All Students Take Coffee" — the FIRST LETTER of each quadrant tells you which function is positive.

📈 Part 7: Trigonometric Graphs — The Waves of Life

Sine, Cosine, Tangent — The Rhythm of the Universe

Trig functions don't just live on triangles — they create beautiful repeating waves that describe everything from sound to tides to heartbeats.

📊 The Three Fundamental Waves
Sine (solid) starts at 0, rises to 1 at π/2, back to 0 at π. Cosine (dashed) is sine shifted left by π/2. Tangent (gold) has vertical asymptotes at π/2, 3π/2... where cos = 0 (division by zero!). Period of sin/cos = 2π, period of tan = π.
🔄 Frequency Changes — sin x vs sin 2x vs sin(x/2)
Period = 2π/|a| for sin(ax). When a=1, one cycle per 2π. When a=2, the wave squeezes — 2 cycles per 2π, so period = π. When a=½, the wave stretches — half a cycle per 2π, so period = 4π. The 'a' controls the frequency!
🌟 Key Graph Facts
Amplitude = |A| for A·sin(x). Period = 2π/|B| for sin(Bx). Phase shift = C/B for sin(Bx − C).
The general form: y = A·sin(Bx − C) + D — A controls height, B controls width, C shifts left/right, D shifts up/down.
♾️ Part 8: General Solutions — The Infinite Answers

When One Answer is Not Enough

If sinθ = ½, you might say θ = 30°. But what about 150°? Or 390°? Or -210°? All of these have sine = ½! Trig equations have INFINITE solutions because the unit circle wraps around forever.

♾️ Infinite Answers
"If sinθ = 0, θ could be 0, π, 2π, -π... INFINITE answers!"
Unlike algebraic equations (like x+2=5, one answer), trig equations usually have infinitely many solutions because trig functions repeat every 2π (or π for tan).

Example: sinθ = 0 → θ = 0, π, 2π, 3π, -π, -2π, ... → θ = nπ for any integer n!

The trick: Find ONE solution (the principal value), then add the period repeatedly. The general solution formula captures ALL answers in one line.
💡 When solving trig equations, NEVER cancel or divide by a trig function — you might lose solutions! Factor instead.
🔄 All Solutions of sinθ = ½ — Animated on the Unit Circle
The general solution formula captures ALL infinitely many answers. For sin, the (-1)ⁿ term handles the "mirror" property of sine about the y-axis. Never lose solutions!
📝 The Three General Solution Formulas ▶

For sin: sinθ = sinα → θ = nπ + (-1)ⁿα

For cos: cosθ = cosα → θ = 2nπ ± α

For tan: tanθ = tanα → θ = nπ + α

Where n is any integer (n ∈ ℤ). The ± for cosine gives two branches — one adding α, one subtracting α.

📐 Part 9: Solution of Triangles — Beyond Right Angles

Any Triangle, Any Angle — The Power of Sine and Cosine Rules

SOH CAH TOA only works for right triangles. But what about any triangle? The Sine Rule and Cosine Rule are the supercharged versions — they work for every triangle.

📐 The General Triangle — Labeled with Sides and Angles
Sine Rule: works when you know two angles + one side (ASA/AAS) or two sides + non-included angle (SSA). Cosine Rule: works when you know three sides (SSS) or two sides + included angle (SAS). Click a case above!
📝 Worked Example
Find the area of a triangle with two sides 7cm and 9cm, and included angle 40°
Given: a = 7, b = 9, and included angle C = 40° (this is an SAS case!).

Area formula: Δ = ½·a·b·sin(C)

Step 1: Δ = ½ × 7 × 9 × sin(40°)
Step 2: sin(40°) ≈ 0.6428
Step 3: Δ = ½ × 63 × 0.6428 = ½ × 40.4964
Answer: Δ ≈ 20.25 cm²

Check with Heron's formula: First find side c using cosine rule: c² = 7²+9²−2·7·9·cos40° = 49+81−126·0.7660 = 130−96.52 = 33.48 → c ≈ 5.79. Then s = (7+9+5.79)/2 = 10.895. Heron: Δ = √(10.895·3.895·1.895·5.105) ≈ √(410.2) = 20.25 cm² ✓
🧠 For area, you only need two sides and the included angle — Δ = ½ab sin C. Quick, clean, no need to find the third side!
Δ = ½ab sin C = ½bc sin A = ½ac sin B   =   √(s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c))  (Heron's formula)
🌊 Part 10: Sum-to-Product & Product-to-Sum — Waves Colliding

Adding Waves Creates Multiplication — The Most Beautiful Transformation

What happens when you add two sine waves? The result looks like a modulated wave — and it can be rewritten as a product of two simpler waves!

🌊 Two Sine Waves Adding Together — The "Beat" Pattern
The purple wave is sin A + sin B. It looks like a fast oscillation (the "carrier") inside a slow envelope (the "modulator"). The sum-to-product formula rewrites this sum as 2 sin(average) cos(half-difference) — turning addition into multiplication!
🧮 All Four Sum-to-Product Formulas ▶
sin C + sin D = 2 sin((C+D)/2) cos((C-D)/2)
sin C − sin D = 2 cos((C+D)/2) sin((C-D)/2)
cos C + cos D = 2 cos((C+D)/2) cos((C-D)/2)
cos C − cos D = −2 sin((C+D)/2) sin((C-D)/2)

The pattern: sum of sines → 2·sin(avg)·cos(half-diff). Sum of cosines → 2·cos(avg)·cos(half-diff). The "co" functions pair together!

🔄 Product-to-Sum (Reverse Direction) ▶
2 sin A cos B = sin(A+B) + sin(A-B)
2 cos A sin B = sin(A+B) − sin(A-B)
2 cos A cos B = cos(A+B) + cos(A-B)
2 sin A sin B = cos(A-B) − cos(A+B)

These are just the sum-to-product formulas read backwards! Helpful for integrating products of trig functions.

💡 Part 11: Problem-Solving Strategies — Think Like a Trig Master

Three Golden Rules for Solving Any Trig Problem

Most trig problems feel hard because you're staring at a pile of formulas. Here are three universal strategies that work for almost everything:

🧠 Strategy #1
Convert Everything to sin and cos First
Whenever you see tan, cot, sec, or csc, rewrite them as sin/cos:

tan x = sin x / cos x  |  cot x = cos x / sin x
sec x = 1 / cos x  |  csc x = 1 / sin x

Most identities become obvious once everything is in terms of sines and cosines. It's like translating a foreign language into your native tongue!
🧠 Strategy #2
Use the t = tan(θ/2) Substitution
For integrals or tricky equations, the Weierstrass substitution converts trig into algebra:

t = tan(θ/2)  ⟹  sin θ = 2t/(1+t²), cos θ = (1−t²)/(1+t²), tan θ = 2t/(1−t²)

This turns ANY trig expression into a rational function of t — which you can solve with plain algebra!
🧠 Strategy #3
Check the Range Before Applying Inverse Trig
sin⁻¹(x) returns values in [−π/2, π/2] (Quadrants I and IV)
cos⁻¹(x) returns values in [0, π] (Quadrants I and II)
tan⁻¹(x) returns values in (−π/2, π/2) (Quadrants I and IV)

Example: sin⁻¹(0.5) = 30°, but sin⁻¹(−0.5) = −30° (not 330°!). Always ask: "Is my answer in the correct range?"
⚠️ Part 12: Common Mistakes — Learn from Others' Errors

Three Traps That Trip Everyone Up

🚫 Mistake #1
Forgetting Inverse Trig Range Restrictions
The error: sin⁻¹(½) = 30° or 150°?
Wrong! sin⁻¹(½) = 30° ONLY (not 150°).

The inverse sine function is defined to return a single value in [−90°, 90°]. If you want ALL solutions, use the general solution formula instead: θ = nπ + (−1)ⁿ·30°.
🚫 Mistake #2
Confusing sin⁻¹x with (sin x)⁻¹ (Cosecant!)
This is the #1 notation trap in trigonometry!

sin⁻¹x = "inverse sine" or "arcsin" = the angle whose sine is x.
(sin x)⁻¹ = 1/sin x = cosecant (csc x).

Mnemonic: The "−1" exponent in sin⁻¹ means "inverse function" (undo), NOT "reciprocal." The reciprocal of sin has its own name: csc!
🚫 Mistake #3
Dropping Solutions — Dividing by sinθ Loses sinθ=0 Cases
Wrong approach: 2 sinθ cosθ = sinθ → divide by sinθ → 2 cosθ = 1 → cosθ = ½ → θ = 60°, 300°

What went wrong? You divided by sinθ, which is zero when θ = 0°, 180°, 360°... You lost those solutions!

Correct approach: 2 sinθ cosθ − sinθ = 0 → sinθ(2 cosθ − 1) = 0 → Either sinθ = 0 → θ = 0°, 180°, 360°, ... Or cosθ = ½ → θ = 60°, 300°

Golden rule: Never divide by a trig function. Factor instead!
⚠️ The #1 Rule
FACTOR, DON'T DIVIDE. If you ever reach for ÷sin or ÷cos, stop — factor it out instead.
Every solution matters. Don't let them slip away!

Multiple‑Angle and Half‑Angle Formulas — Deriving sin 2θ, cos 2θ, tan 2θ and Their Power‑Reduction Forms

The double‑angle identities follow directly from the angle‑addition formulas. Setting A = B = θ in sin(A+B) and cos(A+B) gives

  • sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ
  • cos 2θ = cos²θ − sin²θ

Because sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, the cosine double‑angle formula has two useful alternates:

  • cos 2θ = 2 cos²θ − 1
  • cos 2θ = 1 − 2 sin²θ

The tangent double‑angle formula is obtained by dividing the sine and cosine forms:

tan 2θ = 2 tan θ / (1 − tan²θ)

Half‑angle formulas are derived by solving the power‑reduction versions for sin θ and cos θ. From cos 2α = 1 − 2 sin²α we get sin²α = (1 − cos 2α)/2. Replacing α = θ/2 yields

  • sin(θ/2) = ±√[(1 − cos θ)/2]
  • cos(θ/2) = ±√[(1 + cos θ)/2]

The sign depends on the quadrant of θ/2. The tangent half‑angle formula, often called the Weierstrass substitution, is

tan(θ/2) = ±√[(1 − cos θ)/(1 + cos θ)] = sin θ/(1 + cos θ) = (1 − cos θ)/sin θ

These identities are indispensable for integrating powers of sine and cosine, for solving equations like sin 2θ = cos θ, and for reducing higher‑multiple angles (e.g., sin 3θ = 3 sin θ − 4 sin³θ) to polynomials in sin θ or cos θ.

General Solutions of Trigonometric Equations — From Principal Values to Infinite Families

Every trigonometric equation has infinitely many solutions because the functions are periodic. The standard approach is to isolate the basic function, find its principal value(s) within the defined range, and then add the appropriate period multiples.

For sin θ = k (|k| ≤ 1) the principal value is θ₀ = sin⁻¹k ∈ [−π/2, π/2]. The general solution is

θ = nπ + (−1)ⁿ θ₀, n ∈ ℤ

For cos θ = k the principal value is θ₀ = cos⁻¹k ∈ [0, π]. The general solution is

θ = 2nπ ± θ₀, n ∈ ℤ

For tan θ = k the principal value is θ₀ = tan⁻¹k ∈ (−π/2, π/2). The general solution is

θ = nπ + θ₀, n ∈ ℤ

When the equation contains multiple angles, e.g. sin 2θ = ½, first solve for the inner angle: 2θ = nπ + (−1)ⁿ π/6. Then divide by the coefficient of θ, remembering that division by an integer may introduce extraneous solutions if the period is not adjusted. The safe method is to write the full set for the inner angle and then substitute back.

Equations that factor, such as 2 sin θ cos θ − sin θ = 0, must be solved by factoring, never by dividing by a trigonometric factor. Factoring gives sin θ(2 cos θ − 1) = 0, leading to two independent families: θ = nπ and θ = 2nπ ± π/3.

Worked Example — Solving a Multiple‑Angle Equation
Find all solutions of cos 3θ = −½ in the interval [0, 2π).

Step 1: Identify the principal value for cosine. cos⁻¹(−½) = 2π/3 (since cos 2π/3 = −½ and 2π/3 ∈ [0, π]).

Step 2: Write the general solution for the inner angle 3θ: 3θ = 2nπ ± 2π/3, n ∈ ℤ.

Step 3: Divide by 3: θ = (2nπ)/3 ± 2π/9.

Step 4: Generate values for n = 0, 1, 2,… until θ ≥ 2π.

  • n = 0 → θ = 2π/9, −2π/9 (reject negative, add 2π → 16π/9)
  • n = 1 → θ = 2π/3 + 2π/9 = 8π/9; 2π/3 − 2π/9 = 4π/9
  • n = 2 → θ = 4π/3 + 2π/9 = 14π/9; 4π/3 − 2π/9 = 10π/9
  • n = 3 → θ = 2π + 2π/9 > 2π (stop)

Collecting the distinct solutions in [0, 2π): θ = 2π/9, 4π/9, 8π/9, 10π/9, 14π/9, 16π/9.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a multiple‑angle equation appears, first solve for the inner angle using the standard general‑solution patterns. Only after you have the complete family for the inner angle should you divide by the coefficient. This avoids missing solutions that arise from the periodicity change.

Inverse Trigonometric Identities and Principal Value Ranges — Beyond the Basic Definitions

The inverse functions sin⁻¹x, cos⁻¹x, tan⁻¹x are defined by restricting the domains of sine, cosine, and tangent to intervals where they are one‑to‑one. The principal ranges are:

  • sin⁻¹x ∈ [−π/2, π/2]
  • cos⁻¹x ∈ [0, π]
  • tan⁻¹x ∈ (−π/2, π/2)

From these ranges follow a set of complementary identities that hold for all admissible x:

  • sin⁻¹x + cos⁻¹x = π/2
  • tan⁻¹x + cot⁻¹x = π/2
  • sec⁻¹x + cosec⁻¹x = π/2 (for |x| ≥ 1)

Other useful identities involve double arguments:

  • 2 sin⁻¹x = sin⁻¹(2x√(1−x²)) for x ∈ [−1/√2, 1/√2]
  • 2 cos⁻¹x = cos⁻¹(2x²−1) for x ∈ [0, 1]
  • 2 tan⁻¹x = sin⁻¹(2x/(1+x²)) = cos⁻¹((1−x²)/(1+x²)) = tan⁻¹(2x/(1−x²)) for appropriate x

Sum and difference formulas for inverse functions are also examinable:

sin⁻¹x ± sin⁻¹y = sin⁻¹(x√(1−y²) ± y√(1−x²)) provided the result stays in [−π/2, π/2].

cos⁻¹x ± cos⁻¹y = cos⁻¹(xy ∓ √(1−x²)√(1−y²)) with the result in [0, π].

tan⁻¹x ± tan⁻¹y = tan⁻¹((x ± y)/(1 ∓ xy)) (adjust by π if the denominator is negative to keep the principal value).

These identities are frequently used to simplify expressions like tan⁻¹(1/2) + tan⁻¹(1/3) = tan⁻¹(1) = π/4.

Trigonometric Form of Complex Numbers — Euler’s Formula and De Moivre’s Theorem

Any non‑zero complex number z = x + iy can be written in polar form z = r(cos θ + i sin θ) where r = √(x²+y²) and θ = arg z. Euler’s formula states eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ, so z = r eiθ.

De Moivre’s theorem follows immediately: for any integer n,

(cos θ + i sin θ)ⁿ = cos nθ + i sin nθ

or (eiθ)ⁿ = einθ. This theorem provides a powerful way to derive multiple‑angle formulas. Expanding the left side with the binomial theorem and equating real and imaginary parts yields expressions for cos nθ and sin nθ as polynomials in cos θ and sin θ. For example, n = 3 gives

cos 3θ = 4 cos³θ − 3 cos θ, sin 3θ = 3 sin θ − 4 sin³θ.

Roots of unity are a direct application: the n‑th roots of 1 are e2πik/n for k = 0,…,n−1. They lie on the unit circle at equally spaced angles and satisfy zⁿ = 1. The sum of all n‑th roots of unity is zero, a fact used in evaluating trigonometric sums such as ∑k=0n-1 cos(2πk/n) = 0.

Complex exponentials also simplify products and powers of trigonometric functions. For instance, cos θ = (eiθ+e−iθ)/2 and sin θ = (eiθ−e−iθ)/(2i). Substituting these into integrals or series converts trigonometric problems into algebraic ones.

Solving Triangles in the Ambiguous Case — Law of Sines, Supplementary Angles, and Area Formulas

When two sides and a non‑included angle (SSA) are given, the triangle may have zero, one, or two solutions — the ambiguous case. Let the known data be side a, side b, and angle A opposite side a. Compute the height h = b sin A.

  • If a < h → no triangle (side a too short to reach the base).
  • If a = h → exactly one right triangle (A = 90°).
  • If h < a < b → two possible triangles: angle B can be acute (B₁ = sin⁻¹(b sin A/a)) or obtuse (B₂ = π − B₁). Both satisfy the sine law because sin B₁ = sin B₂.
  • If a ≥ b → only one triangle (the obtuse possibility would make the sum of angles exceed π).

After determining the possible values of B, find C = π − A − B and the remaining side c using the sine law c/sin C = a/sin A.

The area of any triangle can be expressed without Heron’s formula when two sides and the included angle are known: Δ = ½ ab sin C. This is often faster than computing the third side first. For the ambiguous case, compute the area for each valid triangle separately.

An alternative area formula using the circumradius R is Δ = abc/(4R), and using the inradius r is Δ = r s where s is the semiperimeter. These relations connect trigonometry with circle geometry and are useful in advanced problems.

  • Forgetting to test the obtuse supplement when using the sine law in SSA — always check whether π − B₁ yields a valid triangle.
  • Applying the principal value of an inverse trigonometric function directly as the final answer without adding the period multiples required by the problem statement.
  • Misusing the half‑angle sign: the ± in √[(1±cos θ)/2] must be chosen according to the quadrant of θ/2, not θ.
  • Dividing by a trigonometric factor (e.g., sin θ) instead of factoring, thereby losing the solutions where that factor equals zero.
  • Assuming De Moivre’s theorem holds for non‑integer exponents without introducing multi‑valued complex logarithms.
  • Neglecting to adjust the principal value when using tan⁻¹x ± tan⁻¹y formula if the denominator becomes negative.
  • Confusing the range of cos⁻¹x ([0, π]) with that of sin⁻¹x ([−π/2, π/2]) when converting between inverse functions.

Trigonometric Inequalities and Their Solution Sets

Trigonometric inequalities extend the equation‑solving toolkit by asking for all angles that satisfy a relation such as sin θ > ½ or 2 cos θ + 1 ≤ 0. The standard approach is to isolate a single trigonometric function, determine its principal interval where the inequality holds, and then replicate that interval by adding integer multiples of the function’s period. For sine and cosine the period is 2π; for tangent and cotangent it is π. Because the inequality sign may reverse when multiplying by a negative expression, it is safest to bring all terms to one side and factor, e.g. sin θ − ½ > 0, rather than dividing by a trigonometric factor that could be zero.

Consider sin θ > ½. The principal solution on [0, 2π) is θ ∈ (π/6, 5π/6). Adding 2kπ yields the general solution θ ∈ (π/6 + 2kπ, 5π/6 + 2kπ), k ∈ ℤ. For an inequality involving tangent, such as tan θ ≤ 1, the principal interval on (−π/2, π/2) is (−π/2, π/4]; the general solution becomes (−π/2 + kπ, π/4 + kπ], k ∈ ℤ. When the inequality contains a linear combination like 2 sin θ + cos θ > 0, rewrite it as R sin(θ + α) > 0 with R = √(2² + 1²) and α = arctan(1/2). The problem then reduces to a single‑function inequality.

Compound inequalities such as sin θ < cos θ < tan θ require solving each pairwise inequality and intersecting the resulting sets. Graphical insight on the unit circle or on the graphs of the functions helps avoid missing intervals where a function changes sign. Always verify endpoints: strict inequalities exclude points where the expression equals the boundary, while non‑strict inequalities include them provided the function is defined there (e.g., tan θ is undefined at π/2 + kπ).

In competitive examinations, trigonometric inequalities often appear in optimisation or range‑finding questions. A typical twist is to ask for the number of integer values of k for which a given angle lies in a specific interval, or to combine the inequality with a domain restriction such as θ ∈ [0, π]. Mastery of the periodic replication step and careful handling of undefined points are the keys to full marks.

Weierstrass Substitution: Solving Trigonometric Equations via Rational Functions

The Weierstrass substitution t = tan(θ/2) converts any rational equation in sin θ and cos θ into an algebraic equation in t. The identities sin θ = 2t/(1 + t²), cos θ = (1 − t²)/(1 + t²), and dθ = 2 dt/(1 + t²) are derived from the half‑angle formulas and the right‑triangle definition of tangent. This substitution is especially powerful for equations that mix sine and cosine in a non‑linear way, such as sin θ + cos θ = 1 or 3 sin θ − 4 cos θ = 2.

Applying the substitution to sin θ + cos θ = 1 gives 2t/(1 + t²) + (1 − t²)/(1 + t²) = 1. Multiplying by 1 + t² yields 2t + 1 − t² = 1 + t², which simplifies to 2t = 2t², i.e. t(1 − t) = 0. Hence t = 0 or t = 1. Back‑substituting, tan(θ/2) = 0 gives θ = 2kπ; tan(θ/2) = 1 gives θ/2 = π/4 + kπ, so θ = π/2 + 2kπ. Both families satisfy the original equation, and no extraneous roots appear because the substitution is bijective on each interval of length 2π where tan(θ/2) is defined.

A common pitfall is forgetting that t = tan(θ/2) is undefined when θ = π + 2kπ (i.e., when cos(θ/2) = 0). If the original equation is satisfied at those points, they must be checked separately. For instance, the equation sin θ = 0 has solutions θ = kπ. The substitution yields t = 0 only, giving θ = 2kπ; the missing odd multiples of π correspond to the undefined t values and must be added manually.

The method also handles equations of the form a sin θ + b cos θ = c efficiently. After substitution the equation becomes a quadratic in t: (a + c)t² − 2b t + (c − a) = 0. The discriminant condition (2b)² − 4(a + c)(c − a) ≥ 0 reproduces the well‑known solvability condition a² + b² ≥ c². This algebraic perspective clarifies why some trigonometric equations have no real solutions.

In integration, the Weierstrass substitution rationalises integrands containing sin θ and cos θ, turning ∫ dθ/(a + b sin θ) into a rational integral in t. The same algebraic structure appears in the evaluation of definite integrals over symmetric intervals, where the substitution maps the interval to the whole real line.

Worked Example: Solving a Mixed Trigonometric Equation
Solve for θ in [0, 2π): 2 sin θ − cos θ = 1.
Set t = tan(θ/2). Then sin θ = 2t/(1 + t²), cos θ = (1 − t²)/(1 + t²). Substitute: 2·(2t/(1 + t²)) − (1 − t²)/(1 + t²) = 1. Multiply by 1 + t²: 4t − (1 − t²) = 1 + t² → 4t − 1 + t² = 1 + t² → 4t = 2 → t = ½. Thus tan(θ/2) = ½. The principal value is θ/2 = arctan(½) ≈ 0.46365 rad, so θ ≈ 0.9273 rad. Adding the period π for tan(θ/2) gives a second solution in [0, 2π): θ/2 = arctan(½) + π → θ ≈ 0.9273 + 2π ≈ 7.2105 rad, which exceeds 2π, so only θ ≈ 0.9273 rad lies in the interval. Check the undefined point θ = π (where tan(θ/2) is undefined): 2 sin π − cos π = 0 − (−1) = 1, so θ = π is also a solution. Final answer: θ = arctan(½)·2 and θ = π.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a trigonometric equation contains both sine and cosine linearly, first attempt the R sin(θ + α) method; if the equation is quadratic or higher in sine and cosine, the Weierstrass substitution often yields a simple polynomial. Always list the values where tan(θ/2) is undefined and test them separately — this single step recovers the solutions that the algebraic manipulation silently drops.

Chebyshev Polynomials and Multiple‑Angle Identities

Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind, Tₙ(x), are defined by the recurrence T₀(x) = 1, T₁(x) = x, and Tₙ₊₁(x) = 2x Tₙ(x) − Tₙ₋₁(x). They satisfy the fundamental identity Tₙ(cos θ) = cos(nθ) for all integers n ≥ 0. This property provides a systematic way to express cos(nθ) as a polynomial in cos θ of degree n. For example, T₂(x) = 2x² − 1 gives cos 2θ = 2 cos²θ − 1; T₃(x) = 4x³ − 3x yields cos 3θ = 4 cos³θ − 3 cos θ; T₄(x) = 8x⁴ − 8x² + 1 produces cos 4θ = 8 cos⁴θ − 8 cos²θ + 1.

The polynomials of the second kind, Uₙ(x), defined by U₀(x) = 1, U₁(x) = 2x, and the same recurrence, satisfy sin((n+1)θ) = sin θ · Uₙ(cos θ). Consequently, sin(nθ) can be written as sin θ times a polynomial in cos θ of degree n−1. For instance, U₂(x) = 4x² − 1 gives sin 3θ = sin θ (4 cos²θ − 1) = 3 sin θ − 4 sin³θ, matching the familiar triple‑angle formula.

These polynomials appear naturally when solving equations such as cos 5θ = ½. Writing cos 5θ = T₅(cos θ) = 16 cos⁵θ − 20 cos³θ + 5 cos θ transforms the trigonometric equation into a quintic polynomial in x = cos θ. The roots of this polynomial are exactly the cosines of the angles satisfying the original equation. Because Tₙ(x) has n distinct real roots in [−1, 1], the equation cos nθ = c (with |c| ≤ 1) always yields n distinct values of cos θ in that interval, corresponding to the n solutions for θ modulo 2π.

Chebyshev polynomials also minimise the maximum deviation from zero on [−1, 1] among all monic polynomials of a given degree, a property exploited in approximation theory and numerical analysis. In competitive mathematics, they provide a rapid derivation of multiple‑angle formulas without repeatedly applying addition formulas. Recognising the pattern Tₙ(cos θ) = cos(nθ) allows a student to write down cos 7θ or sin 7θ in seconds during an exam.

An important extension is the identity Tₙ(x) = cosh(n arccosh x) for |x| > 1, which connects trigonometric and hyperbolic functions. This analytic continuation is useful when solving equations like cosh 5y = 2, where the same polynomial structure appears with hyperbolic cosine.

Trigonometric Form of Vectors: Dot Product, Cross Product, and Projection

Vectors in the plane or in space can be expressed using trigonometric functions of the angles they make with coordinate axes. For a vector **a** of magnitude a making an angle α with the positive x‑axis, its components are a cos α and a sin α. The dot product of two vectors **a** and **b** with magnitudes a, b and included angle θ is **a**·**b** = ab cos θ. This definition immediately yields the projection of **a** onto **b**: proj_**b** **a** = (**a**·**b**/b²) **b** = (a cos θ) **b̂**, where **b̂** is the unit vector along **b**. The scalar projection (signed length) is simply a cos θ.

The cross product (in three dimensions) has magnitude |**a** × **b**| = ab sin θ and direction given by the right‑hand rule. The area of the parallelogram spanned by **a** and **b** is exactly this magnitude. The sine of the angle can be extracted from the cross product: sin θ = |**a** × **b**|/(ab). Combining dot and cross products gives tan θ = |**a** × **b**|/(**a**·**b**), a formula that avoids computing θ explicitly when only the ratio of sine to cosine is needed.

In problems involving forces or velocities, resolving a vector into components parallel and perpendicular to a given direction is a direct application of projection. If a force **F** acts at an angle φ to a displacement **d**, the work done is **F**·**d** = Fd cos φ. The component of **F** perpendicular to **d** does no work; its magnitude is F sin φ, obtainable from the cross product magnitude.

The trigonometric form also simplifies the derivation of the law of sines and cosines for a triangle formed by vectors **a**, **b**, **c** with **a** + **b** + **c** = 0. Taking dot products with each vector yields relations among the side lengths and the cosines of the included angles, reproducing the cosine rule. The sine rule follows from the equality of the areas expressed as ½ ab sin C, ½ bc sin A, ½ ca sin B.

Advanced applications include the vector triple product identity **a** × (**b** × **c**) = (**a**·**c**)**b** − (**a**·**b**)**c**, which can be proved by writing each vector in a basis aligned with **b** and **c** and using trigonometric component expressions. This identity is indispensable in rigid‑body dynamics and electromagnetism, where it appears in the Lorentz force and in the expression for the curl of a cross product.

Optimization of Trigonometric Expressions Using AM‑GM and Cauchy‑Schwarz

Many competition problems ask for the maximum or minimum value of expressions such as sin θ + cos θ, sin θ cos θ, or a sin θ + b cos θ + c sin 2θ. While calculus (differentiation) works, algebraic inequalities often give faster, more elegant solutions. The AM‑GM inequality states that for non‑negative numbers, the arithmetic mean is at least the geometric mean. Since sin²θ and cos²θ are non‑negative and sum to 1, we can apply AM‑GM to products like sin²θ cos²θ ≤ (sin²θ + cos²θ)²/4 = 1/4, giving |sin θ cos θ| ≤ 1/2. Hence the maximum of sin θ cos θ is 1/2, attained when sin²θ = cos²θ = 1/2, i.e. θ = π/4 + kπ/2.

For linear combinations a sin θ + b cos θ, the Cauchy‑Schwarz inequality (a sin θ + b cos θ)² ≤ (a² + b²)(sin²θ + cos²θ) = a² + b² gives the bound |a sin θ + b cos θ| ≤ √(a² + b²). Equality occurs when (sin θ, cos θ) is proportional to (a, b), i.e. tan θ = a/b (with appropriate quadrant). This reproduces the R‑sin(θ + α) method without explicitly computing R and α.

Expressions involving higher multiples, such as sin θ + sin 2θ, can be handled by rewriting sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ and then using substitution x = sin θ, y = cos θ with x² + y² = 1. The problem becomes maximizing x + 2xy subject to x² + y² = 1. Lagrange multipliers or the method of Lagrange’s identity (a form of Cauchy‑Schwarz) yields the extremum. Alternatively, express the whole function as a single sinusoid using sum‑to‑product formulas: sin θ + sin 2θ = 2 sin(3θ/2) cos(θ/2). The product of a sine and a cosine is bounded by 1/2 in absolute value, so the maximum is 1, attained when sin(3θ/2) = cos(θ/2) = ±1/√2.

A classic optimisation is finding the maximum of sin θ + sin φ subject to θ + φ = constant. Using the identity sin θ + sin φ = 2 sin((θ+φ)/2) cos((θ−φ)/2), the factor sin((θ+φ)/2) is fixed, so the maximum occurs when cos((θ−φ)/2) = 1, i.e. θ = φ. This symmetry argument often replaces calculus entirely.

When the expression contains both sine and cosine of different multiples, such as sin θ + cos 2θ, use the double‑angle identity cos 2θ = 1 − 2 sin²θ to obtain a quadratic in sin θ: f(x) = x + 1 − 2x² with x = sin θ ∈ [−1, 1]. The vertex of this parabola gives the extremum within the interval, a purely algebraic step.

  • Forgetting to check the endpoints of the domain when using AM‑GM or Cauchy‑Schwarz, because equality conditions may require values outside the allowed range of the trigonometric variable.
  • Applying AM‑GM to expressions that can be negative without first squaring or taking absolute values, leading to an invalid inequality.
  • Misidentifying the equality condition for Cauchy‑Schwarz: the vectors must be linearly dependent, which translates to a specific tangent value, not merely any angle that makes the derivative zero.
  • Overlooking the periodicity when stating the angles at which the extremum occurs; the answer must include the general solution θ = θ₀ + 2kπ (or π periodicity for tangent‑based expressions).
  • Confusing the maximum of sin θ cos θ (which is 1/2) with the maximum of sin θ + cos θ (which is √2), a frequent mix‑up in timed tests.
  • Neglecting to verify that a critical point obtained from a quadratic substitution (e.g., x = sin θ) actually corresponds to a real angle; the vertex may lie outside [−1, 1].
  • Master these traps to avoid losing marks.

Summation of Trigonometric Series in Arithmetic Progression

Series of the form S = sin α + sin(α+β) + sin(α+2β) + … + sin(α+(n−1)β) and the analogous cosine series appear frequently in physics and engineering problems involving wave superposition, diffraction gratings, and discrete Fourier analysis. The closed‑form expressions are derived by multiplying the series by 2 sin(β/2) and using the product‑to‑sum identity 2 sin A sin B = cos(A−B) − cos(A+B). For the sine series this yields

2 sin(β/2) S = cos(α−β/2) − cos(α+(n−1/2)β).

Hence, provided sin(β/2) ≠ 0,

S = [cos(α−β/2) − cos(α+(n−1/2)β)] / [2 sin(β/2)].

A similar manipulation for the cosine series gives

C = cos α + cos(α+β) + … + cos(α+(n−1)β) = [sin(α+(n−1/2)β) − sin(α−β/2)] / [2 sin(β/2)].

When β is an integer multiple of 2π the denominator vanishes; the series then reduces to n sin α or n cos α respectively, which matches the limit of the formula as β → 2kπ. These formulas are also the discrete analogues of the Dirichlet kernel D_n(x) = sin((n+½)x)/sin(x/2) that governs convergence of Fourier series. In competitive problems the series often appears with α = 0 or α = β/2, leading to simplifications such as Σk=1n sin(kθ) = sin(nθ/2) sin((n+1)θ/2) / sin(θ/2). Recognising the pattern allows immediate evaluation without term‑by‑term addition.

Applications extend to finding the resultant amplitude of n equally spaced phasors, analysing the intensity pattern of a multi‑slit interferometer, and solving recurrence relations with trigonometric characteristic roots. A typical examiner twist is to ask for the sum of a series where the angles are not in arithmetic progression but can be transformed into one by using identities like sin 3θ = 3 sin θ − 4 sin³θ, thereby reducing a cubic trigonometric sum to a combination of arithmetic‑progression series.

Roots of Unity and Trigonometric Identities

The n‑th roots of unity are the complex solutions of zⁿ = 1, given by z_k = cos(2πk/n) + i sin(2πk/n) for k = 0,1,…,n−1. Their symmetric distribution on the unit circle yields a wealth of trigonometric identities. The polynomial factorisation zⁿ − 1 = ∏k=0n−1 (z − z_k) and the substitution z = 1 produce the identity ∏k=1n−1 (1 − z_k) = n. Taking absolute values and using |1 − eiθ| = 2|sin(θ/2)| gives the product formula ∏k=1n−1 sin(πk/n) = n / 2n−1. This classic result is often used to evaluate products of sines at rational multiples of π.

Further, the sum of the roots is zero, which translates to Σk=0n−1 cos(2πk/n) = 0 and Σk=0n−1 sin(2πk/n) = 0. By pairing conjugate roots one obtains identities for sums of cosines of multiples of an angle: Σk=1n−1 cos(2πk/n) = −1. More generally, for any integer m not divisible by n, Σk=0n−1 cos(2πmk/n) = 0. These orthogonality relations are the discrete counterpart of the continuous Fourier orthogonality and underlie the derivation of Chebyshev polynomials.

Roots of unity also provide a systematic way to express cos(nθ) and sin(nθ) as polynomials in cos θ and sin θ. Writing (cos θ + i sin θ)ⁿ = cos(nθ) + i sin(nθ) and expanding via the binomial theorem, then equating real and imaginary parts, yields the multiple‑angle formulas. Conversely, the factorisation of cos(nθ) − cos(nα) into linear factors in cos θ leads to the identity cos(nθ) − cos(nα) = 2n−1 ∏k=0n−1 [cos θ − cos(α + 2πk/n)]. Setting α = 0 gives a factorisation of cos(nθ) − 1, which is useful in solving equations like cos(nθ) = 1.

A frequent advanced problem asks for the value of Σk=1n−1 1/(1 − cos(2πk/n)). Using the identity 1 − cos φ = 2 sin²(φ/2) and the product formula for sines, the sum evaluates to (n²−1)/6. Such problems test the ability to move fluidly between complex exponentials, trigonometric products, and algebraic sums.

Worked Example
Evaluate the product P = ∏k=17 sin(kπ/8).
The angles kπ/8 for k = 1,…,7 are exactly the non‑zero multiples of π/8 up to 7π/8. Using the general formula ∏k=1n−1 sin(πk/n) = n / 2n−1 with n = 8 gives P = 8 / 27 = 8 / 128 = 1/16. Alternatively, pair sin(π/8) with sin(7π/8) = sin(π/8), sin(2π/8) with sin(6π/8) = sin(2π/8), etc., and use sin(π/4)=√2/2, sin(π/2)=1 to verify the same result.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the product formula ∏k=1n−1 sin(πk/n) = n/2n−1 and the sum‑of‑roots orthogonality Σ cos(2πmk/n)=0 for m not a multiple of n. When a problem presents a product of sines or cosines at equally spaced angles, immediately check whether the angles match the πk/n pattern. If they do, apply the formula directly; if they are scaled (e.g., sin(2πk/15)), adjust n accordingly. This single recognition step can replace several lines of algebraic manipulation.

Trigonometric Substitution in Definite Integrals

Definite integrals containing algebraic expressions of the forms √(a² − x²), √(a² + x²), or √(x² − a²) are standard candidates for trigonometric substitution. The substitutions x = a sin θ, x = a tan θ, and x = a sec θ respectively transform the square root into a simple trigonometric function: √(a² − a² sin²θ) = a cos θ, √(a² + a² tan²θ) = a sec θ, √(a² sec²θ − a²) = a tan θ. The differential dx becomes a cos θ dθ, a sec²θ dθ, or a sec θ tan θ dθ, and the limits of integration change according to the inverse trigonometric functions. For example, ∫₀¹ √(1 − x²) dx becomes ∫₀π/2 cos²θ dθ after substituting x = sin θ. The resulting trigonometric integral is then evaluated using power‑reduction identities such as cos²θ = (1+cos 2θ)/2.

A subtle point is the handling of absolute values when the trigonometric function changes sign over the integration interval. Since the original integrand is non‑negative (it is a square root), the substitution must preserve this sign. Choosing the principal branch of the inverse function (e.g., θ ∈ [−π/2, π/2] for arcsin) guarantees that cos θ ≥ 0, so √(cos²θ) = cos θ without an absolute value. For the tangent substitution, θ ∈ (−π/2, π/2) ensures sec θ > 0. For the secant substitution, one typically restricts θ ∈ [0, π/2) ∪ (π/2, π] so that tan θ has the correct sign matching the original variable’s domain.

Beyond the three basic forms, integrals involving √(ax² + bx + c) are handled by completing the square and then applying the appropriate substitution. For instance, √(x² + 4x + 5) = √((x+2)² + 1) suggests the substitution x+2 = tan θ. The method also extends to integrals of rational functions of sin θ and cos θ via the Weierstrass substitution t = tan(θ/2), which converts them into rational functions of t. However, the Weierstrass substitution is already covered elsewhere; here we focus on the geometric substitutions that exploit the Pythagorean identities directly.

Examiners often test the ability to recognise the correct substitution by presenting an integral where the quadratic under the root is not a perfect square but can be transformed. A typical trap is to forget to change the limits of integration, leading to an answer expressed in terms of the original variable after back‑substitution, which is acceptable but more error‑prone. Another common error is to drop the differential factor dx = a cos θ dθ and integrate only the transformed root, effectively omitting the Jacobian of the transformation.

Trigonometric Form of Rotation Matrices and Applications

A rotation in the plane by an angle θ is represented by the 2×2 matrix R(θ) = [[cos θ, −sin θ], [sin θ, cos θ]]. This matrix is orthogonal (RᵀR = I) and has determinant +1, characterising the special orthogonal group SO(2). The trigonometric entries encode the action on basis vectors: the first column is the image of (1,0) and the second column is the image of (0,1). Composition of rotations corresponds to matrix multiplication, and the identity R(α)R(β) = R(α+β) reproduces the angle‑addition formulas for sine and cosine. This matrix viewpoint provides a powerful algebraic proof of identities such as cos(α+β) = cos α cos β − sin α sin β by simply equating the (1,1) entry of the product matrix.

Eigenvalues of R(θ) are e±iθ, with complex eigenvectors (1, ∓i). The diagonalisation over the complex numbers connects the rotation matrix to the complex exponential representation of rotations. For any integer n, R(θ)ⁿ = R(nθ), which is the matrix form of De Moivre’s theorem. This property is used to compute powers of rotation matrices efficiently and to solve linear recurrence relations with trigonometric characteristic roots, such as the sequence defined by xk+1 = 2 cos θ x_k − xk−1, whose closed form involves sin(kθ)/sin θ.

In three dimensions, a rotation about a unit vector u = (u_x, u_y, u_z) by angle θ is given by the Rodrigues formula: R = I + sin θ K + (1−cos θ) K², where K is the cross‑product matrix of u. The entries of this 3×3 matrix are polynomials in the components of u and trigonometric functions of θ. The trace of the rotation matrix equals 1 + 2 cos θ, providing a coordinate‑free way to extract the rotation angle from a given orthogonal matrix with determinant +1. This trace formula is frequently used in computer graphics and robotics to interpolate between orientations (slerp).

Applications in competitive problems include finding the matrix of a reflection across a line making angle φ with the x‑axis, which is R(φ) [[1,0],[0,−1]] R(−φ) = [[cos 2φ, sin 2φ], [sin 2φ, −cos 2φ]]. The eigenvalues of a reflection matrix are +1 and −1, and its determinant is −1. Problems may ask for the angle of rotation resulting from the product of two reflections, which is twice the angle between the reflection lines — a direct consequence of the matrix multiplication R(α)R(β) = R(α+β) when one of the matrices is a reflection.

Solving Cubic Equations via Trigonometric Vieta Substitution

The depressed cubic t³ + pt + q = 0 with real coefficients and discriminant Δ = (q/2)² + (p/3)³ ≤ 0 has three real roots. When Δ < 0, Cardano’s formula involves cube roots of complex conjugates, which is algebraically correct but numerically inconvenient. Vieta’s trigonometric substitution t = 2√(−p/3) cos θ transforms the cubic into 4 cos³θ − 3 cos θ = cos 3θ after using the identity cos 3θ = 4 cos³θ − 3 cos θ. The equation becomes cos 3θ = −q / (2√(−p/3)³). Since the right‑hand side lies in [−1, 1] precisely when Δ ≤ 0, we can set 3θ = arccos(−q / (2√(−p/3)³)) + 2πk for k = 0,1,2. The three distinct roots are then t_k = 2√(−p/3) cos(θ + 2πk/3). This method yields all three real roots using only real arithmetic and the inverse cosine function.

If p > 0, the substitution t = 2√(p/3) sinh θ (hyperbolic sine) reduces the cubic to sinh 3θ = 3 sinh θ + 4 sinh³θ, giving a single real root expressed with hyperbolic functions. For p < 0 and Δ > 0, the substitution t = 2√(−p/3) cosh θ works similarly. The trigonometric/hyperbolic approach unifies the three cases of the cubic discriminant and avoids complex numbers entirely.

A classic problem asks to solve x³ − 3x + 1 = 0. Here p = −3, q = 1, so √(−p/3) = 1. The equation becomes cos 3θ = −1/2, so 3θ = 2π/3, 4π/3, 8π/3, giving θ = 2π/9, 4π/9, 8π/9. The roots are 2 cos(2π/9), 2 cos(4π/9), 2 cos(8π/9). Recognising that these angles are multiples of 2π/9 connects the cubic to the 9‑th roots of unity and explains why the roots are expressible in radicals involving cos(2π/9). This interplay between algebraic equations and trigonometric values of rational multiples of π is a recurring theme in advanced problem‑solving.

Examiners may present a cubic with a parameter and ask for the range of the parameter for which all roots are real. The condition Δ ≤ 0 translates directly into an inequality involving the parameter. Alternatively, they may give a trigonometric equation like cos 3θ = 1/2 and ask to find the cubic whose roots are 2 cos θ, 2 cos(θ+2π/3), 2 cos(θ+4π/3). The answer is t³ − 3t − 2 cos 3θ = 0, which follows from reversing the Vieta substitution.

  • Forgetting to restrict the domain of the inverse trigonometric function when applying the Vieta substitution, leading to missing roots or extraneous angles.
  • Misapplying the product formula for sines when the angles are not exactly πk/n but a scaled version; always factor out the scaling factor before using the standard identity.
  • Neglecting the absolute value in √(cos²θ) during trigonometric substitution in integrals, which can flip the sign of the integrand on part of the interval.
  • Confusing the rotation matrix R(θ) with the reflection matrix; the determinant distinguishes them (+1 for rotation, −1 for reflection).
  • Using the Weierstrass substitution t = tan(θ/2) for integrals that are more directly handled by the Pythagorean substitutions, resulting in unnecessarily complicated rational functions.
  • Overlooking that the sum of roots of unity is zero only when the sum runs over a complete set of n‑th roots; partial sums require the Dirichlet kernel formula.

Trigonometric Solutions of Linear Differential Equations with Constant Coefficients

Linear ordinary differential equations with constant coefficients appear in every branch of applied mathematics. The characteristic polynomial P(r) = anrn + an-1rn-1 + … + a1r + a0 determines the form of the general solution. When P(r) has a pair of complex conjugate roots r = α ± iβ (β ≠ 0), the corresponding real‑valued fundamental solutions are eαxcos(βx) and eαxsin(βx). This follows directly from Euler’s formula eiβx = cos(βx) + i sin(βx) and the linearity of the differential operator. If the complex roots are repeated with multiplicity m, the basis expands to {xkeαxcos(βx), xkeαxsin(βx)} for k = 0,…,m−1. The trigonometric factor therefore encodes the oscillatory part of the motion, while the exponential factor governs growth or decay.

A classic example is the damped harmonic oscillator x'' + 2γx' + ω02x = 0. Its characteristic equation r2 + 2γr + ω02 = 0 yields roots r = −γ ± i√(ω02−γ2) when γ < ω0 (underdamped case). The solution x(t) = e−γt(C1cos(ωt) + C2sin(ωt)) with ω = √(ω02−γ2) shows a sinusoid whose amplitude decays exponentially. In the critically damped case γ = ω0 the roots coincide at −γ and the trigonometric functions disappear, leaving a purely polynomial‑exponential solution. Examiners often ask for the condition on parameters that separates oscillatory from non‑oscillatory behaviour; the answer is the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial.

Higher‑order equations follow the same pattern. For a fourth‑order beam equation y(4) + k4y = 0 the characteristic roots are the fourth roots of −k4, i.e. k·eiπ/4, k·ei3π/4, k·ei5π/4, k·ei7π/4. Pairing conjugates gives two frequencies ω1 = k/√2 and ω2 = k/√2, so the general solution is a linear combination of cos(ω1x), sin(ω1x), cos(ω2x), sin(ω2x). Recognising that the roots lie on a circle in the complex plane and are equally spaced by π/2 is a shortcut that avoids solving the quartic explicitly.

When the forcing term is a sinusoid, the method of undetermined coefficients or the complex exponential ansatz reduces the particular integral to an algebraic problem. For L[y] = F0cos(Ωt) one substitutes yp = Re{A eiΩt} and solves for the complex amplitude A = F0/P(iΩ). The denominator P(iΩ) is precisely the characteristic polynomial evaluated at iΩ; its magnitude and phase give the steady‑state amplitude and phase lag. Resonance occurs when Ω coincides with a natural frequency, i.e. when P(iΩ) = 0, which is exactly the condition that the homogeneous equation admits a pure sinusoid of that frequency.

In summary, the trigonometric functions that appear in the solutions of constant‑coefficient linear ODEs are not accidental; they are the real and imaginary parts of complex exponentials that diagonalise the differential operator. Mastery of this connection lets a candidate move fluidly between algebraic characteristic equations and the oscillatory language of sine and cosine.

Ptolemy’s Theorem and Trigonometric Identities in Cyclic Quadrilaterals

Ptolemy’s theorem states that for a convex quadrilateral ABCD inscribed in a circle, the product of the diagonals equals the sum of the products of opposite sides: AC·BD = AB·CD + AD·BC. This purely geometric relation becomes a powerful generator of trigonometric identities when the sides are expressed as chords of the circumcircle. If the circle has radius R and the central angles subtended by the sides are 2α, 2β, 2γ, 2δ (with α+β+γ+δ = π), then each chord length equals 2R sin(angle). Substituting AB = 2R sin α, BC = 2R sin β, CD = 2R sin γ, DA = 2R sin δ, and the diagonals AC = 2R sin(α+β), BD = 2R sin(β+γ) into Ptolemy’s equality and cancelling the common factor 4R2 yields sin(α+β) sin(β+γ) = sin α sin γ + sin β sin δ. Using the angle‑sum condition δ = π − (α+β+γ) and the identity sin(π−θ) = sin θ, the right‑hand side becomes sin α sin γ + sin β sin(α+β+γ). Expanding the left‑hand side with the product‑to‑sum formula and simplifying reproduces the standard addition formulas for sine and cosine. Thus Ptolemy’s theorem is equivalent to the angle‑addition identities.

Beyond the basic addition formulas, Ptolemy’s theorem generates identities for multiple angles. Setting α = β = γ = θ gives a cyclic quadrilateral with three equal sides; the relation reduces to sin 2θ sin 2θ = sin θ sin θ + sin θ sin 3θ, i.e. sin22θ = sin2θ + sin θ sin 3θ. Using sin 3θ = 3 sin θ − 4 sin3θ one recovers the double‑angle formula sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ. More generally, choosing α = θ, β = 2θ, γ = 3θ produces an identity linking sin 5θ, sin 3θ, and sin θ, which is the Chebyshev recurrence for sine. This geometric viewpoint explains why Chebyshev polynomials appear naturally in the study of cyclic polygons.

Examiners frequently disguise Ptolemy’s theorem as a problem about a quadrilateral with given side lengths and a known circumradius, asking for a diagonal or an angle. The trigonometric translation turns the problem into solving a linear equation in sines of unknown angles. A typical trap is to forget that the quadrilateral must be cyclic; if the given data do not satisfy the necessary and sufficient condition (opposite angles sum to π), the theorem does not apply and any answer derived from it is invalid. Another common error is to use the chord formula 2R sin(θ/2) with the full central angle instead of the half‑angle, leading to a factor‑of‑two mistake.

The theorem also extends to the crossed (self‑intersecting) cyclic quadrilateral, where the sign of one product changes. In that configuration the relation becomes AC·BD = |AB·CD − AD·BC|, which corresponds to the sine subtraction formula sin(α−β) = sin α cos β − cos α sin β. Recognising the crossed case is essential when the problem statement mentions “a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle” without specifying convexity.

Deriving a Product‑to‑Sum Identity from Ptolemy
Let a cyclic quadrilateral have consecutive central angles 2α, 2β, 2γ, 2δ with α+β+γ+δ = π. Use Ptolemy’s theorem to prove that sin(α+β) sin(β+γ) = sin α sin γ + sin β sin δ.
In a circle of radius R, each side equals the chord of its central angle: AB = 2R sin α, BC = 2R sin β, CD = 2R sin γ, DA = 2R sin δ. The diagonals subtend the sums of adjacent central angles: AC subtends 2(α+β) so AC = 2R sin(α+β); BD subtends 2(β+γ) so BD = 2R sin(β+γ). Ptolemy gives AC·BD = AB·CD + AD·BC. Substituting the chord expressions yields (2R sin(α+β))(2R sin(β+γ)) = (2R sin α)(2R sin γ) + (2R sin β)(2R sin δ). Cancelling the common factor 4R2 produces the required identity sin(α+β) sin(β+γ) = sin α sin γ + sin β sin δ.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a geometry problem mentions a cyclic quadrilateral, immediately write down the chord‑length formulas in terms of the circumradius and half‑central angles. Convert the given side or diagonal lengths into sine equations; the resulting trigonometric system is usually linear in the sines of the unknown angles. Check the convexity condition (opposite angles sum to π) before applying the standard Ptolemy relation; if the quadrilateral is crossed, use the subtraction version. This systematic translation avoids the common pitfall of mixing chord lengths with full central angles.

Fourier Series: Trigonometric Basis and Convergence Criteria

A Fourier series represents a periodic function f(x) of period 2π as an infinite sum of sines and cosines: f(x) ∼ a0/2 + Σn=1∞ [ancos(nx) + bnsin(nx)], where the coefficients are given by the orthogonal projection integrals an = (1/π)∫−ππ f(x)cos(nx)dx and bn = (1/π)∫−ππ f(x)sin(nx)dx. The orthogonality relations ∫−ππ cos(mx)cos(nx)dx = πδmn (m,n≥1) and similarly for sine, together with ∫ cos(mx)sin(nx)dx = 0, guarantee that each coefficient isolates a single frequency component. This trigonometric basis is complete in the Hilbert space L2(−π,π), meaning any square‑integrable periodic function can be approximated arbitrarily well in the mean‑square sense by a finite trigonometric polynomial.

Pointwise convergence is more delicate. Dirichlet’s theorem states that if f is piecewise smooth (i.e. f and f' are piecewise continuous) then the Fourier series converges to f(x) at every point of continuity and to the average (f(x+)+f(x−))/2 at a jump discontinuity. The Gibbs phenomenon — an overshoot of about 9 % of the jump height near a discontinuity — is a universal feature that does not vanish as more terms are added; it only moves closer to the jump. Examiners often ask to identify the limit of the series at a specific point, requiring the candidate to compute the one‑sided limits of the original function.

Uniform convergence holds under stronger conditions: if f is continuous and piecewise smooth, and the periodic extension is also continuous (i.e. f(−π)=f(π)), then the Fourier series converges uniformly to f on ℝ. A useful sufficient condition is that f be of bounded variation and continuous; then the series converges everywhere. For functions with a discontinuous derivative, the series of derivatives converges in the mean‑square sense but not necessarily pointwise.

Parseval’s identity Σ (an2+bn2) = (1/π)∫ f(x)2dx links the energy of the signal to the sum of squared coefficients. It is frequently used to evaluate infinite series such as Σ 1/n2 = π2/6 by expanding f(x)=x2 on (−π,π). The complex exponential form f(x) ∼ Σ cneinx with cn = (1/2π)∫ f(x)e−inxdx simplifies algebraic manipulations and makes the symmetry c−n = cn for real f evident.

In competitive problems, the Fourier series of standard waveforms (square wave, sawtooth, triangle wave) are memorised because they appear as building blocks. The square wave of amplitude 1 has only odd sine terms: (4/π) Σk=0∞ sin((2k+1)x)/(2k+1). The sawtooth f(x)=x on (−π,π) yields 2 Σ (−1)n+1 sin(nx)/n. Recognising these series allows rapid evaluation of sums like Σ (−1)n+1/n = ln 2 or Σ 1/(2k+1)2 = π2/8.

Hyperbolic Trigonometric Functions and Their Identities

The hyperbolic functions sinh x = (ex−e−x)/2, cosh x = (ex+e−x)/2, and tanh x = sinh x / cosh x satisfy identities that mirror the circular trigonometric ones, with sign changes dictated by the fundamental relation cosh2x − sinh2x = 1. The addition formulas are sinh(x+y) = sinh x cosh y + cosh x sinh y and cosh(x+y) = cosh x cosh y + sinh x sinh y. The double‑angle formulas become sinh 2x = 2 sinh x cosh x and cosh 2x = cosh2x + sinh2x = 2 cosh2x − 1 = 1 + 2 sinh2x. These are obtained by replacing the circular angle iθ with a real argument x in Euler’s formula, i.e. cos(iθ) = cosh θ, sin(iθ) = i sinh θ.

The inverse hyperbolic functions have logarithmic forms: arcsinh x = ln(x + √(x2+1)), arccosh x = ln(x + √(x2−1)) for x ≥ 1, and artanh x = ½ ln((1+x)/(1−x)) for |x|<1. These expressions are derived by solving the quadratic equations that result from the definitions of sinh, cosh, tanh. They are indispensable for integrating rational functions containing √(x2±a2) or √(a2−x2) after an appropriate hyperbolic substitution.

Hyperbolic substitutions often simplify integrals that would otherwise require trigonometric substitutions with imaginary arguments. For ∫ √(x2+a2) dx, the substitution x = a sinh t gives dx = a cosh t dt and √(x2+a2) = a cosh t, turning the integrand into a2cosh2t dt, which integrates to (a2/2)(t + sinh t cosh t). Back‑substitution yields the standard result (x/2)√(x2+a2) + (a2/2) arcsinh(x/a). Similarly, ∫ √(x2−a2) dx uses x = a cosh t (x ≥ a), and ∫ √(a2−x2) dx uses the circular substitution x = a sin t. Recognising which substitution matches the sign under the radical is a frequent exam discriminator.

The Gudermannian function gd(x) = ∫0x sech t dt = 2 arctan(ex) − π/2 provides a bridge between circular and hyperbolic angles without complex numbers. It satisfies sinh x = tan(gd x), cosh x = sec(gd x), and tanh x = sin(gd x). This function appears in the theory of map projections (Mercator projection) and in the solution of the differential equation dy/dx = √(1+y2) which gives y = sinh(x+C). Problems that ask for the relation between the amplitude of a hyperbolic sine and a circular angle often reduce to the Gudermannian.

In the context of differential equations, the hyperbolic functions arise when the characteristic roots are real and distinct (overdamped oscillator) or when the discriminant is positive. The general solution of y'' − k2y = 0 is C1ekx + C2e−kx = A cosh(kx) + B sinh(kx). The hyperbolic basis is numerically more stable for large arguments because it avoids catastrophic cancellation between the two exponentials.

Trigonometric Form of Conic Sections in Polar Coordinates

Every conic section with a focus at the origin can be written in the polar equation r = ℓ / (1 + e cos θ) or r = ℓ / (1 + e sin θ), where e is the eccentricity and ℓ is the semi‑latus rectum (the chord through the focus perpendicular to the major axis). For an ellipse 0 ≤ e < 1, the denominator never vanishes and r is bounded; for a parabola e = 1 the denominator can become zero, giving r → ∞ as θ → π; for a hyperbola e > 1 the denominator changes sign, producing two separate branches corresponding to the two intervals where 1 + e cos θ > 0. The directrix is the line x = ℓ/e (for the cosine form) and the focus‑directrix property r = e·distance to directrix is exactly the polar equation.

The trigonometric form makes the symmetry of the conic evident: replacing θ by −θ reflects the curve across the polar axis, while replacing θ by π−θ reflects across the perpendicular axis. The vertices occur at θ = 0 and θ = π for the cosine form, giving rmin = ℓ/(1+e) (periapsis) and rmax = ℓ/(1−e) (apoapsis) for an ellipse. The semi‑major axis a = ℓ/(1−e2) and semi‑minor axis b = ℓ/√(1−e2) follow from the geometry of the ellipse. The area swept by the radius vector per unit time is constant (Kepler’s second law), which in polar coordinates reads dA/dt = ½ r2 dθ/dt = constant. This leads to the differential equation dθ/dt = h/r2 with h = √(μℓ) where μ is the gravitational parameter.

When the conic is rotated by an angle φ, the polar equation becomes r = ℓ / (1 + e cos(θ−φ)). Expanding the cosine gives r = ℓ / (1 + e cos φ cos θ + e sin φ sin θ). This linear combination of cos θ and sin θ in the denominator is the hallmark of a conic whose principal axes are not aligned with the polar axes. Problems that give a general second‑degree equation in Cartesian coordinates and ask for the polar form relative to a focus require completing the square to locate the focus, then translating the origin and rotating the axes. The eccentricity can be read directly from the discriminant of the quadratic form: e = √(1 − (b2/a2)) for an ellipse, e = √(1 + (b2/a2)) for a hyperbola.

The pedal equation p2 = r2 − (dr/dθ)2 (where p is the perpendicular from the origin to the tangent) simplifies for conics to p2 = ℓ r − (e2−1) r2 / (1+e cos θ)2. For a parabola (e=1) this reduces to p2 = ℓ r, a relation used in the design of parabolic reflectors. In competitive problems, the polar form is often the quickest route to find the distance between the foci, the length of the latus rectum, or the angle between the asymptotes of a hyperbola (given by cos ψ = 1/e).

A common examiner twist is to present the polar equation r = a / (1 + cos θ) and ask for the Cartesian equation. Multiplying both sides by the denominator and using r = √(x2+y2), r cos θ = x yields √(x2+y2) = a − x. Squaring and simplifying gives y2 = a2 − 2ax, a parabola opening leftwards with focus at the origin. Forgetting to check for extraneous solutions introduced by squaring is a frequent error; the original polar equation already restricts r ≥ 0, which translates to x ≤ a.
