1. Quadratic Equations and Their Roots

1.1 Quadratic Equations in Standard Form

A quadratic equation is any equation that can be rearranged into the standard form a x² + b x + c = 0 where a, b and c are real constants and a is not zero. The coefficient a determines the curvature of the associated parabola, while b and c shift the graph horizontally and vertically. Because the highest power of the variable is two, the equation is guaranteed to have at most two solutions in the complex number system, a fact that follows directly from the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. When a student writes the equation in this canonical shape, the subsequent application of the quadratic formula or factorisation becomes systematic and less prone to algebraic slips.

1.2 Solving by Factorisation

Factorisation exploits the zero‑product property: if the product of two expressions equals zero, at least one of them must be zero. To factor a quadratic, one searches for two numbers whose product equals a c and whose sum equals b; these numbers then split the middle term, allowing the expression to be grouped into binomial factors. This method is most efficient when the roots are rational, because the required integers exist only in that case. When the discriminant is not a perfect square, factorisation over the integers fails, signalling that the roots are irrational or complex and that an alternative method must be employed.

1.3 Completing the Square

Completing the square rewrites the quadratic as a perfect square trinomial plus a constant, yielding (x + b/(2a))² = (b² - 4ac)/(4a²). The derivation begins by dividing the whole equation by a, moving the constant term to the right side, and adding (b/(2a))² to both sides. This transformation reveals the vertex of the parabola at (−b/(2a), −Δ/(4a)) where Δ = b² − 4ac, and it provides a geometric interpretation of the roots as the horizontal intercepts of the shifted parabola. Moreover, completing the square is the algebraic foundation from which the quadratic formula itself is derived, so mastering it deepens conceptual understanding.

1.4 The Quadratic Formula

The quadratic formula x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / (2a) gives the two roots of any quadratic equation directly, regardless of whether they are real, rational, irrational or complex. The expression under the square root, Δ = b² − 4ac, is called the discriminant and it governs the nature of the roots: Δ > 0 yields two distinct real roots, Δ = 0 yields a repeated real root, and Δ < 0 yields a pair of complex conjugates. Because the formula is derived by completing the square, it inherits the same vertex information and can be used to locate the axis of symmetry at x = −b/(2a). In examinations, the formula is the most reliable tool when factorisation is not obvious, and it also serves as a check for solutions obtained by other methods.

2. Nature of Roots and Discriminant

2.1 Discriminant and Root Classification

The discriminant Δ = b² − 4ac acts as a decisive indicator of the root structure without requiring the explicit computation of the roots. When Δ is positive and a perfect square, the roots are rational and distinct; when Δ is positive but not a perfect square, the roots are irrational conjugates of the form p ± √q. If Δ equals zero, the quadratic touches the x‑axis at its vertex, producing a double root that is also the x‑coordinate of the vertex. A negative discriminant forces the square root of a negative number, introducing the imaginary unit i and giving a pair of complex conjugate roots. This classification is essential for problems that ask only for the nature of roots, allowing students to answer quickly by evaluating Δ alone.

2.2 Relationship Between Coefficients and Roots

Vieta’s formulas connect the coefficients of a quadratic to the sum and product of its roots: if α and β are the roots, then α + β = −b/a and αβ = c/a. These relations hold for any quadratic, even when the roots are complex, because they follow directly from expanding a(x − α)(x − β). They are powerful in reverse: given the sum and product, one can reconstruct the quadratic equation as x² − (sum)x + (product) = 0 (after normalising a = 1). In competitive problems, Vieta’s formulas often replace explicit root calculation, especially when the question asks for expressions like α² + β² or 1/α + 1/β, which can be rewritten in terms of the sum and product.

2.3 Graphical Interpretation of the Discriminant

The sign of the discriminant corresponds to the intersection of the parabola y = a x² + b x + c with the x‑axis. A positive discriminant means the curve cuts the axis at two distinct points; zero discriminant means the vertex lies exactly on the axis, so the parabola is tangent; a negative discriminant means the entire curve lies either above (a > 0) or below (a < 0) the axis, with no real intercepts. This visual link helps students anticipate the number of real solutions before any algebraic manipulation, and it also clarifies why the quadratic formula yields complex numbers when the graph does not meet the axis.

2.4 Applications to Inequalities

Quadratic inequalities such as a x² + b x + c > 0 are solved by first finding the roots of the corresponding equation. The sign of the quadratic expression on the intervals determined by the roots depends on the leading coefficient a: if a > 0, the expression is positive outside the roots and negative between them; if a < 0, the opposite holds. When the discriminant is negative, the quadratic never changes sign, so the inequality is either always true or always false depending on the sign of a. This interval method, rooted in the discriminant and the vertex, is a standard technique for solving optimisation and range problems in higher mathematics.

Example 1
Find the values of k for which the quadratic equation 2x² − 4kx + (k² + 3) = 0 has real and distinct roots.
The discriminant Δ = (−4k)² − 4·2·(k² + 3) = 16k² − 8k² − 24 = 8k² − 24. For real and distinct roots we require Δ > 0, i.e. 8k² − 24 > 0 ⇒ k² > 3 ⇒ k < −√3 or k > √3. Hence the required values of k are (−∞, −√3) ∪ (√3, ∞).
Exam Insight
When a problem asks for the range of a parameter that guarantees a certain root condition, always write the discriminant inequality first; solving it directly yields the answer without needing to compute the roots themselves.

3. Arithmetic Progressions

3.1 Definition and General Term

An arithmetic progression (AP) is a sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by adding a fixed constant d, called the common difference, to the preceding term. If the first term is a, the nth term is given by Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d. This linear formula reflects the fact that the terms lie on a straight line when plotted against their index n. The common difference can be positive, negative or zero; a zero difference produces a constant sequence, while a negative difference yields a decreasing progression. Recognising the linear nature of the nth term is crucial for converting word problems into algebraic equations.

3.2 Sum of the First n Terms

The sum Sₙ of the first n terms of an AP can be derived by pairing the first and last terms, the second and penultimate terms, and so on. Each pair sums to a + (a + (n − 1)d) = 2a + (n − 1)d, and there are n/2 such pairs, giving Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d]. An equivalent form Sₙ = n (first term + last term) / 2 is often more convenient when the last term is known directly. This formula is linear in n², which explains why the sequence of partial sums of an AP is itself a quadratic sequence.

3.3 Properties and Manipulations

Several useful properties follow from the definition: if a, b, c are three consecutive terms of an AP, then 2b = a + c; inserting any number of arithmetic means between two given numbers preserves the common difference; and the sum of terms equidistant from the beginning and end is constant. These properties allow one to solve problems that involve unknown numbers of terms or unknown common differences by setting up simple linear equations. For instance, if the sum of the first p terms equals the sum of the first q terms (p ≠ q), then the sum of the first (p + q) terms must be zero, a result that frequently appears in competitive examinations.

3.4 Applications to Real‑World Problems

Arithmetic progressions model situations with uniform incremental change: salary increments, depreciation by a fixed amount, seating arrangements in a theatre, or the distance covered by a body moving with constant acceleration in discrete time steps. Translating a verbal description into the parameters a and d is the first step; then the nth term or sum formula provides the quantitative answer. Care must be taken to identify whether the problem asks for a specific term, the number of terms needed to reach a certain sum, or the value of the common difference itself.

4. Geometric Progressions

4.1 Definition and General Term

A geometric progression (GP) is a sequence where each term after the first is obtained by multiplying the preceding term by a fixed non‑zero constant r, called the common ratio. With first term a, the nth term is Tₙ = a rⁿ⁻¹. The exponential nature of this formula means that the terms grow (or decay) multiplicatively, and the sign of the terms alternates when r is negative. When |r| < 1, the terms approach zero as n increases, a behaviour that underpins the convergence of infinite geometric series.

4.2 Sum of the First n Terms

The sum Sₙ of the first n terms of a GP is derived by multiplying the series by r and subtracting, yielding Sₙ = a (1 − rⁿ) / (1 − r) for r ≠ 1. If r = 1, the progression is constant and Sₙ = n a. This formula shows that the partial sums themselves form a sequence that can be expressed in closed form, and it is the basis for the sum to infinity when |r| < 1, where the term rⁿ tends to zero and S∞ = a / (1 − r). The condition |r| < 1 is both necessary and sufficient for convergence, a fact that is often tested in problems asking for the sum of an infinite series.

4.3 Properties and Special Cases

If a, b, c are three consecutive terms of a GP, then b² = a c; the product of terms equidistant from the ends of a finite GP is constant and equals a² rⁿ⁻¹. Inserting geometric means between two numbers creates a GP whose common ratio is the appropriate root of the ratio of the extremes. These properties are useful for solving problems where the product of certain terms is given, or where the terms themselves are expressed in terms of logarithms, converting the multiplicative structure into an additive one.

4.4 Applications to Finance and Growth Models

Geometric progressions naturally describe compound interest, population growth with a constant percentage rate, radioactive decay, and the bouncing of a ball that loses a fixed fraction of its height each impact. In finance, the future value of an annuity can be expressed as the sum of a GP, and the present value of a perpetuity corresponds to the infinite sum formula. Recognising the underlying GP allows the student to write down the relevant formula immediately, avoiding the need to derive it from first principles each time.

5. Special Series and Summation Techniques

5.1 Sum of Squares and Cubes of Natural Numbers

The series Σ n² from n = 1 to N equals N(N + 1)(2N + 1)/6, while Σ n³ equals [N(N + 1)/2]². These formulas can be proved by mathematical induction or by using the method of differences, where one observes that (n + 1)³ − n³ = 3n² + 3n + 1 and sums both sides telescopically. The sum of cubes being the square of the sum of the first N natural numbers is a striking identity that often simplifies problems involving higher powers. In examinations, these closed forms are expected knowledge and are frequently combined with AP or GP terms to form mixed series.

5.2 Telescoping Series

A telescoping series is one in which successive terms cancel each other partially, leaving only a few boundary terms. The classic example is Σ (1/n − 1/(n + 1)) which collapses to 1 − 1/(N + 1). The technique relies on expressing the general term as a difference f(n) − f(n + 1) or f(n) − f(n − 1). Identifying the appropriate decomposition is the key skill; it often involves partial fractions or algebraic manipulation such as writing 1/(n(n + 1)) = 1/n − 1/(n + 1). Telescoping series appear in problems that ask for the sum of rational functions of n, and they provide an elegant alternative to induction.

5.3 Arithmetico‑Geometric Series

An arithmetico‑geometric series has the form Σ (a + (n − 1)d) rⁿ⁻¹, combining an arithmetic factor with a geometric factor. Its sum can be found by multiplying the series by r, subtracting, and solving the resulting linear equation for the sum. The closed form is Sₙ = [a − (a + (n − 1)d) rⁿ] / (1 − r) + d r (1 − rⁿ⁻¹) / (1 − r)² for r ≠ 1. When |r| < 1, the infinite sum converges to a/(1 − r) + d r/(1 − r)². This series type is common in problems involving expected values in probability or present values of increasing annuities.

5.4 Method of Differences for Polynomial Sequences

If the nth term of a sequence is a polynomial in n of degree k, then the kth forward difference is constant. This property allows one to reconstruct the sum of the sequence by building a difference table and using the formula Σ Tₙ = Σ Δ⁻¹ Tₙ, where Δ⁻¹ denotes the discrete antiderivative. In practice, one writes the first few terms, computes successive differences until a constant row appears, and then uses binomial coefficients to express the sum as a polynomial of degree k + 1. This method is systematic and avoids guesswork, making it valuable for series that do not fit the standard AP, GP, or arithmetico‑geometric patterns.
