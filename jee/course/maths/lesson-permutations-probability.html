Fundamental Counting Principles

1.1 The Multiplication Principle

The multiplication principle states that if a task can be broken into a sequence of independent stages, and the first stage can be performed in m ways while the second stage can be performed in n ways for each outcome of the first stage, then the whole task can be performed in m × n ways. This principle follows directly from the idea that each choice in the first stage can be paired with every choice in the second stage, producing a Cartesian product of the two sets of possibilities. When more than two stages are involved, the total number of ways is the product of the numbers of ways for each individual stage, provided the choices at each stage do not depend on earlier choices except through the already fixed outcomes.

In practice, the multiplication principle is the engine behind counting arrangements, selections, and many probability calculations. For example, to form a three‑digit code where the first digit cannot be zero, there are 9 choices for the first digit, 10 for the second, and 10 for the third, giving 9 × 10 × 10 = 900 possible codes. The principle also underlies the derivation of factorial notation, because arranging n distinct objects in a line can be seen as n successive choices: n ways for the first position, n−1 for the second, and so on, yielding n × (n−1) × … × 1 = n!.

1.2 The Addition Principle

The addition principle applies when a task can be completed by exactly one of several mutually exclusive methods. If method A can be carried out in a ways and method B in b ways, and the two methods cannot both be used for the same outcome, then the total number of ways to complete the task is a + b. This principle is essentially a statement about the cardinality of a disjoint union of finite sets: the size of the union equals the sum of the sizes of the individual sets. It is crucial when a problem naturally splits into cases that do not overlap, such as counting the number of ways to choose a committee that either includes a particular person or excludes that person.

Care must be taken to ensure the cases are truly disjoint; otherwise the simple sum would double‑count outcomes that belong to more than one case. In such situations the principle of inclusion–exclusion extends the addition principle by subtracting the sizes of intersections. The addition principle also appears in probability when computing the probability of the union of mutually exclusive events, because the probability of the union equals the sum of the individual probabilities.

1.3 Applications to Arrangements

Arrangements of objects in a line, around a circle, or in a row with restrictions are classic applications of the multiplication and addition principles. For linear arrangements of n distinct objects, the multiplication principle gives n! directly. When some objects are identical, the number of distinct permutations reduces because swapping identical objects does not produce a new arrangement; the formula becomes n! divided by the product of the factorials of the multiplicities of each identical type. Circular arrangements introduce a further division by n, since rotating the whole circle does not change the relative order, leading to (n−1)! for n distinct objects around a circle.

Restrictions such as “two particular persons must not sit together” are handled by first counting the total arrangements without restriction and then subtracting the arrangements where the restriction is violated. The latter is often counted by treating the restricted pair as a single combined object, applying the multiplication principle to the reduced set, and then multiplying by the internal permutations of the pair. This complementary counting technique is a direct consequence of the addition principle applied to the partition of the sample space into “restriction satisfied” and “restriction violated” cases.

Permutations

2.1 Definition and Notation

A permutation is an ordered arrangement of a selection of objects taken from a larger set. The notation P(n, r) or ⁿPᵣ denotes the number of ways to choose r objects from n distinct objects and arrange them in a sequence. By the multiplication principle, the first position can be filled in n ways, the second in n−1 ways, continuing until the r‑th position, which can be filled in n−r+1 ways. Hence P(n, r) = n × (n−1) × … × (n−r+1) = n! / (n−r)!. This formula makes explicit the relationship between permutations and factorials, and it reduces to n! when r = n, i.e., when all objects are arranged.

The notation ⁿPᵣ is read as “n permute r” and is standard in combinatorial textbooks. It emphasizes that order matters: the arrangement (a, b, c) is considered different from (b, a, c). This distinction is the defining feature that separates permutations from combinations, where order is irrelevant. Understanding this notation is essential for translating word problems into algebraic expressions that can be evaluated or simplified.

2.2 Permutations of Distinct Objects

When all n objects are distinct, the number of permutations of all n objects is n!. This follows immediately from the multiplication principle applied to n successive choices. For a partial permutation of length r, the formula P(n, r) = n!/(n−r)! gives the count. For instance, the number of ways to award gold, silver, and bronze medals to 10 athletes is P(10, 3) = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720. The reasoning is that the gold medal can go to any of the 10 athletes, the silver to any of the remaining 9, and the bronze to any of the remaining 8.

Permutations of distinct objects also appear in problems involving seating arrangements, password formation, and scheduling. In each case, the key is to identify the number of positions to fill and the pool of available distinct items for each position, then apply the product rule. If additional constraints such as “person A must sit at the left end” are present, the constrained position is fixed first, reducing the effective pool for the remaining positions, and the multiplication principle is applied to the reduced problem.

2.3 Permutations with Repetition

When objects are not all distinct, the number of distinct permutations decreases because swapping identical objects does not create a new arrangement. If a multiset contains n objects with n₁ of type 1, n₂ of type 2, …, n_k of type k (where Σ n_i = n), the number of distinct permutations is n! / (n₁! n₂! … n_k!). This formula is derived by starting with n! permutations of n labelled objects and then dividing by the factorial of each multiplicity to eliminate the overcounting caused by permutations among identical objects.

A classic example is the word “MISSISSIPPI”, which has 11 letters with multiplicities M=1, I=4, S=4, P=2. The number of distinct arrangements is 11! / (1! 4! 4! 2!) = 34 650. The same principle applies to arranging coloured balls, distributing identical gifts, or any scenario where some items are indistinguishable. It is important to verify that the objects within each type are truly identical; if any subtle distinction exists, the simple division by factorials would undercount the true number of arrangements.

Example: Arranging Letters with Repetition
Find the number of distinct arrangements of the letters in the word “BANANA”.
The word BANANA has 6 letters. The letter A appears 3 times, N appears 2 times, and B appears once. Using the formula for permutations with repetition, the number of distinct arrangements is 6! / (3! 2! 1!) = 720 / (6 × 2 × 1) = 720 / 12 = 60. Therefore, there are 60 distinct arrangements of the letters in BANANA.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem asks for arrangements with repeated items, first write down the total number of items and the frequency of each distinct item. Then apply the division by factorials of the frequencies. A common mistake is to forget the factorial for items that appear only once; including 1! does not change the value but reinforces the habit of accounting for every type.

Combinations

3.1 Definition and Notation

A combination is a selection of objects from a larger set where the order of selection does not matter. The notation C(n, r) or ⁿCᵣ (also written as binom{n}{r}) denotes the number of ways to choose r objects from n distinct objects without regard to order. Since each combination of r objects corresponds to r! permutations of those same objects, the relationship between permutations and combinations is C(n, r) = P(n, r) / r! = n! / [r! (n−r)!]. This formula is symmetric: C(n, r) = C(n, n−r), reflecting the fact that choosing r objects to include is equivalent to choosing n−r objects to exclude.

The binomial coefficient binom{n}{r} appears in the binomial theorem, in Pascal’s triangle, and in many probability calculations. It is read as “n choose r”. The symmetry property is often used to simplify calculations, for example by replacing C(100, 97) with C(100, 3). Understanding the combinatorial interpretation of binom{n}{r} as the number of r‑element subsets of an n‑element set is fundamental for solving selection problems.

3.2 Combinations of Distinct Objects

When selecting r objects from n distinct objects, the number of possible selections is binom{n}{r}. For example, the number of ways to form a committee of 4 members from a group of 12 people is binom{12}{4} = 495. The reasoning is that we first count ordered selections (permutations) which would be P(12, 4) = 12 × 11 × 10 × 9 = 11 880, and then divide by 4! = 24 because each committee can be ordered in 4! ways, leaving 495 distinct committees.

Combinations are used whenever the problem asks for “groups”, “teams”, “subsets”, or “selections” without any ordering. If the problem later imposes an order on the chosen objects (e.g., assigning distinct roles to the chosen members), the count must be multiplied by the number of ways to order the chosen objects, effectively converting the combination back into a permutation. Recognizing whether order matters is the first decision point in any counting problem.

3.3 Combinations with Repetition

Sometimes we are allowed to choose the same type of object more than once, as in selecting r scoops of ice cream from n flavours where multiple scoops of the same flavour are permitted. The number of such combinations with repetition is binom{n+r-1}{r}. This result is derived by a stars‑and‑bars argument: represent the r selections as r stars and the n−1 dividers between flavours as bars; the total number of arrangements of r stars and n−1 bars is binom{n+r-1}{r}. Each arrangement corresponds uniquely to a multiset of size r drawn from n types.

For instance, the number of ways to buy 6 donuts from 4 varieties is binom{4+6-1}{6} = binom{9}{6} = 84. The stars‑and‑bars method also provides a visual intuition: the positions of the bars determine how many of each variety are chosen. This technique extends to solving integer equations of the form x₁ + x₂ + … + x_n = r with non‑negative integer solutions, where the number of solutions is exactly binom{n+r-1}{r}.

Probability Foundations

4.1 Sample Space and Events

The sample space of a random experiment is the set of all possible outcomes. An event is any subset of the sample space, including the empty set (impossible event) and the sample space itself (certain event). For a finite sample space with equally likely outcomes, the probability of an event E is defined as the ratio of the number of outcomes in E to the total number of outcomes in the sample space. This classical definition connects directly to counting principles: the numerator and denominator are often computed using permutations or combinations.

When outcomes are not equally likely, the probability measure must assign a non‑negative weight to each elementary outcome such that the sum of all weights equals 1. The probability of an event is then the sum of the weights of the outcomes it contains. This axiomatic approach, formalized by Kolmogorov, provides a rigorous foundation that accommodates continuous sample spaces, where outcomes are points in a continuum and probabilities are defined via density functions rather than simple counting.

4.2 Axioms of Probability

The three Kolmogorov axioms state: (1) For any event A, 0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1. (2) P(S) = 1, where S is the sample space. (3) For any countable sequence of mutually exclusive events A₁, A₂, …, the probability of their union equals the sum of their probabilities: P(∪ A_i) = Σ P(A_i). These axioms imply several useful rules, such as the complement rule P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A), the addition rule for non‑exclusive events P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B), and the monotonicity property that if A ⊆ B then P(A) ≤ P(B).

The axioms also justify the definition of conditional probability and the multiplication rule for intersections. They are the logical bedrock upon which all further probability theory, including Bayes’ theorem and the theory of random variables, is built. In problem solving, the axioms are rarely invoked explicitly, but they guarantee that any probability computed by legitimate counting or integration will satisfy the basic consistency requirements.

4.3 Classical and Empirical Probability

Classical probability applies when the sample space is finite and all outcomes are equally likely, as in tossing a fair coin, rolling a fair die, or drawing a card from a well‑shuffled deck. In such cases, probability reduces to a counting problem: P(E) = |E| / |S|. Empirical probability, on the other hand, is based on observed frequencies from repeated trials. If an experiment is performed N times and event E occurs f times, the empirical probability is f/N. As N grows, the law of large numbers ensures that the empirical probability converges to the true (theoretical) probability, provided the trials are independent and identically distributed.

Both viewpoints are essential. Classical probability gives exact answers for idealized models, while empirical probability provides estimates when the model is unknown or too complex. In many practical situations, such as quality control or medical trials, empirical probabilities are used to infer the underlying theoretical probabilities, often with confidence intervals derived from the binomial or normal approximations.

Conditional Probability, Bayes' Theorem and Distributions

5.1 Conditional Probability and Independence

Conditional probability quantifies the likelihood of an event A given that another event B has occurred. It is defined as P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), provided P(B) > 0. This definition reflects the idea of restricting the sample space to the outcomes in B and then measuring the proportion of those outcomes that also belong to A. The multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B) P(B) = P(B|A) P(A) follows directly and is a powerful tool for computing joint probabilities when conditional probabilities are known.

Two events A and B are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other, i.e., P(A|B) = P(A) and P(B|A) = P(B). Equivalently, independence means P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B). Independence simplifies calculations dramatically because the joint probability factorizes. However, independence is an assumption that must be justified by the physical context; many real‑world events exhibit dependence, and assuming independence incorrectly can lead to severe errors in risk assessment.

5.2 Bayes' Theorem

Bayes’ theorem provides a way to update the probability of a hypothesis H in light of new evidence E. It states P(H|E) = P(E|H) P(H) / P(E), where P(E) can be expanded using the law of total probability: P(E) = Σ P(E|H_i) P(H_i) over a partition {H_i} of the sample space. The theorem is a direct consequence of the definition of conditional probability and the multiplication rule. It is widely used in medical diagnosis, spam filtering, and machine learning, where prior probabilities are updated with observed data.

A classic illustration is the disease testing problem: suppose a disease affects 1 % of the population, a test has 99 % sensitivity (true positive rate) and 99 % specificity (true negative rate). If a randomly selected person tests positive, Bayes’ theorem gives the posterior probability of actually having the disease as (0.99 × 0.01) / (0.99 × 0.01 + 0.01 × 0.99) = 0.5, i.e., only a 50 % chance despite the high accuracy of the test. This counter‑intuitive result highlights the importance of the prior probability (prevalence) in interpreting diagnostic tests.

5.3 Common Discrete Distributions

Discrete probability distributions assign probabilities to countable outcomes. The binomial distribution models the number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials with constant success probability p. Its probability mass function is P(X = k) = binom{n}{k} p^k (1−p)n−k for k = 0, 1, …, n. The mean is np and the variance is np(1−p). The Poisson distribution models the number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space when events happen independently at a constant average rate λ. Its mass function is P(X = k) = e−λ λ^k / k! for k = 0, 1, 2, …, with mean and variance both equal to λ.

The geometric distribution describes the number of trials needed to obtain the first success in independent Bernoulli trials, with P(X = k) = (1−p)k−1 p for k = 1, 2, … . The negative binomial generalizes this to the number of trials needed for the r‑th success. The hypergeometric distribution models sampling without replacement from a finite population, giving the probability of k successes in n draws from a population of size N containing K successes. Each distribution arises from a specific random mechanism, and recognizing the underlying mechanism is the key to selecting the correct model for a given problem.

Inclusion–Exclusion Principle and Its Applications

The inclusion–exclusion principle systematically counts elements in the union of finite sets that may overlap. For two sets A and B, |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. For three sets A, B, C, |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A ∩ B| − |A ∩ C| − |B ∩ C| + |A ∩ B ∩ C|. In general, for n sets A₁, A₂, …, Aₙ,

|A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ … ∪ Aₙ| = Σ|Aᵢ| − Σ|Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ| + Σ|Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ ∩ Aₖ| − … + (−1)ⁿ⁺¹|A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ|,

where sums run over all distinct indices. The term for the intersection of k sets carries sign (−1)ᵏ⁺¹.

A standard application counts integers up to a bound not divisible by given primes. For positive integers ≤ 100 not divisible by 2, 3, or 5, let A₂, A₃, A₅ be the sets of multiples. Then |A₂| = ⌊100/2⌋ = 50, |A₃| = 33, |A₅| = 20. Pairwise intersections: |A₂ ∩ A₃| = ⌊100/6⌋ = 16, |A₂ ∩ A₅| = ⌊100/10⌋ = 10, |A₃ ∩ A₅| = ⌊100/15⌋ = 6. Triple intersection: |A₂ ∩ A₃ ∩ A₅| = ⌊100/30⌋ = 3. By inclusion–exclusion, numbers divisible by at least one of 2,3,5 total 50+33+20−16−10−6+3 = 74. Hence 100−74 = 26 are not divisible by any.

The principle extends to probability: for events E₁, …, Eₙ, P(E₁ ∪ … ∪ Eₙ) follows the same alternating‑sum formula with probabilities replacing cardinalities. This is useful when the union probability is hard to compute directly but individual and intersection probabilities are accessible.

Another application counts onto functions. The number of surjections from an m‑element set to an n‑element set is Σk=0n (−1)ᵏ C(n, k) (n−k)ᵐ, obtained by letting Aᵢ be the functions missing the i‑th codomain element and applying inclusion–exclusion.

When applying inclusion–exclusion, correctly identify all intersections and maintain the alternating sign pattern. A common error is stopping at pairwise intersections when triple (or higher) intersections are non‑empty, causing overcounting.

Derangements and Subfactorial Numbers

A derangement is a permutation with no element in its original position. The number of derangements of n distinct objects is denoted !n (subfactorial n) or Dₙ. Inclusion–exclusion gives

!n = n! Σk=0n (−1)ᵏ / k! = n! (1 − 1/1! + 1/2! − 1/3! + … + (−1)ⁿ/n!).

This sum is the truncated Maclaurin series for e⁻¹, so for large n, !n ≈ n!/e. Rounding n!/e to the nearest integer yields the exact value.

A useful recurrence is !n = (n−1)(!(n−1) + !(n−2)) for n ≥ 2, with !0 = 1, !1 = 0. It follows by placing element 1 in any of n−1 positions, say k. If element k goes to position 1, the remaining n−2 elements must be deranged; otherwise, element k must avoid position 1, equivalent to deranging n−1 elements with a modified forbidden position.

Circular Permutations and Necklace Problems

Arranging n distinct objects around a circle treats rotations as identical. The number of distinct circular permutations is (n−1)!, found by fixing one object and permuting the remaining n−1 linearly.

If objects are not all distinct, divide by the factorials of multiplicities. For example, circular arrangements of "ALGEBRA" (7 letters, A twice) total (7−1)! / 2! = 720 / 2 = 360.

When reflections are also identical, the objects form a necklace. For n > 2 distinct beads, the number is (n−1)!/2. For n = 1 or 2, division by 2 does not apply because a reflection coincides with a rotation. With colored beads (repetitions allowed), the problem becomes counting orbits under the dihedral group Dₙ. Burnside's lemma (Cauchy‑Frobenius lemma) gives the number of distinct colorings as (1/|G|) Σg∈G fix(g), where G is the symmetry group (cyclic for rotations, dihedral for rotations and reflections) and fix(g) is the number of colorings fixed by g.

For a binary necklace (two colors) with n beads, rotationally distinct necklaces number (1/n) Σd|n φ(d) 2n/d, where φ is Euler's totient function. If reflections are allowed, the formula for odd n is (1/(2n)) [ Σd|n φ(d) 2n/d + n·2⌈n/2⌉ ]; for even n an analogous expression with additional terms applies. These are standard results in Polya enumeration theory.

In examination problems, the symmetry group is usually explicit: "round table" implies rotations only; "necklace" or "bracelet" implies rotations and reflections. Misidentifying the group is a frequent error.

Fixed positions break circular symmetry: if certain objects must occupy specific seats (e.g., host at the head), the problem reduces to a linear permutation of the remaining objects.

Multinomial Coefficients and the Multinomial Theorem

The multinomial coefficient generalizes the binomial coefficient to partitioning a set into more than two subsets. The number of ways to divide n distinct objects into k labeled groups of sizes n₁, n₂, …, nₖ (Σ nᵢ = n) is

n! / (n₁! n₂! … nₖ!).

This coefficient appears as the coefficient of x₁n₁ x₂n₂ … xₖnₖ in the expansion of (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₖ)ⁿ, the multinomial theorem:

(x₁ + x₂ + … + xₖ)ⁿ = Σn₁+…+nₖ=n (n! / (n₁! … nₖ!)) x₁n₁ … xₖnₖ.

The sum runs over all k‑tuples of non‑negative integers summing to n. For k = 2 this reduces to the binomial theorem.

A direct combinatorial interpretation: the number of distinct permutations of a multiset with n total items, where item i appears nᵢ times, is n!/(n₁!…nₖ!). For "MISSISSIPPI" (M=1, I=4, S=4, P=2), distinct permutations = 11!/(1!4!4!2!) = 34,650.

Multinomial coefficients also give the probability mass function of the multinomial distribution: for n independent trials with outcomes 1,…,k having probabilities p₁,…,pₖ (Σ pᵢ = 1), the probability of exactly nᵢ occurrences of outcome i is (n!/(n₁!…nₖ!)) p₁n₁ … pₖnₖ, generalizing the binomial distribution.

An important identity: Σn₁+…+nₖ=n n!/(n₁!…nₖ!) = kⁿ, obtained by setting x₁ = … = xₖ = 1 in the multinomial theorem.

When solving problems, a multinomial coefficient can be computed sequentially as a product of binomial coefficients: choose n₁ items for the first group (C(n, n₁)), then n₂ from the remainder (C(n−n₁, n₂)), etc. This stepwise approach simplifies arithmetic and reduces overflow risk in manual calculations.

Advanced Probability: Expectation, Variance, and Generating Functions

The expectation of a discrete random variable X is E[X] = Σ x·P(X=x). Expectation is linear: E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y] for any constants a, b and random variables X, Y, regardless of independence. This simplifies expectations of sums, e.g., the number of successes in n Bernoulli trials: E[X] = Σ E[Iᵢ] = n·p, where Iᵢ indicates success on trial i.

Variance measures spread: Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²] = E[X²] − (E[X])². For independent variables, variances add: Var(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y). Covariance Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y] quantifies linear dependence; the correlation coefficient ρ = Cov(X,Y)/√(Var(X)Var(Y)) lies in [−1,1].

Moment generating functions (MGFs) encapsulate all moments. The MGF of X is M_X(t) = E[etX], defined for t near 0. The k‑th moment is E[Xᵏ] = M_X(k)(0). For independent X, Y, MX+Y(t) = M_X(t) M_Y(t), making MGFs ideal for sums of independent variables.

Common MGFs: Binomial(n,p): M(t) = (1−p + p e^t)ⁿ. Poisson(λ): M(t) = exp(λ(e^t−1)). Exponential(λ): M(t) = λ/(λ−t) for t<λ. Normal(μ,σ²): M(t) = exp(μt + ½σ²t²). The MGF of a sum of independent Poissons is Poisson with parameter equal to the sum of the individual parameters, confirming the reproductive property.

Probability generating functions (PGFs) are used for non‑negative integer‑valued variables: G_X(s) = E[s^X] = Σ P(X=k) s^k. The PGF of a sum of independent variables is the product of their PGFs. Factorial moments come from derivatives at s=1: E[X(X−1)…(X−k+1)] = G_X(k)(1). For Binomial(n,p), G(s) = (1−p + ps)ⁿ; for Poisson(λ), G(s) = eλ(s−1).

Generating functions also solve recurrence relations for probabilities. For example, the extinction probability in a branching process is the smallest non‑negative root of s = G(s), where G is the PGF of the offspring distribution.

In examinations, questions often ask for the expectation or variance of a function of a random variable, or to identify a distribution from its MGF or PGF. Recognizing standard MGFs/PGFs and using their algebraic properties (product for sums, composition for mixtures) can dramatically reduce computation.

Common Traps

  • Forgetting to divide by 2 when counting necklaces with reflection symmetry for n > 2.
  • Applying the binomial coefficient formula to multinomial problems without adjusting for multiple categories.
  • Confusing the inclusion–exclusion sign pattern: the term for k sets has sign (−1)k+1.
  • Using the derangement approximation n!/e without rounding to the nearest integer, leading to off‑by‑one errors.
  • Assuming independence when computing expectation of a product; E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] holds only for independent variables.
  • Misapplying the multinomial theorem by omitting the factorial denominator for repeated items.
  • Neglecting the condition P(B) > 0 when writing conditional probability formulas in multi‑step Bayes problems.

Mastering these advanced counting and probability techniques ensures a robust toolkit for tackling the most challenging combinatorial problems.

Probability Inequalities: Markov, Chebyshev, and Chernoff Bounds

Probability inequalities give deterministic upper bounds on tail probabilities without requiring the full distribution. They are indispensable when only moments or independence structure are known. The simplest is Markov’s inequality: for a non‑negative random variable X and any a > 0, P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a. The proof follows from a·I{X ≥ a} ≤ X and taking expectations. Chebyshev’s inequality sharpens this using variance: for any random variable with finite mean μ and variance σ², P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k² for k > 0. It is derived by applying Markov to (X − μ)². Both bounds are distribution‑free but often loose.

Chernoff bounds exploit the moment generating function to obtain exponentially decaying tails for sums of independent variables. Let X = Σᵢ Xᵢ where Xᵢ are independent Bernoulli(pᵢ) and μ = E[X] = Σ pᵢ. For any δ > 0, P(X ≥ (1+δ)μ) ≤ exp(−μδ²/(2+δ)) and for 0<δ<1, P(X ≤ (1−δ)μ) ≤ exp(−μδ²/2). For identical p, a simpler form is P(X ≥ (1+δ)μ) ≤ (e^δ/(1+δ)1+δ)μ. These bounds are tight up to constant factors in the exponent and underlie concentration of measure. A typical exam question asks to bound the deviation of a binomial(n, p) variable from its mean by more than εn; Hoeffding’s inequality (a special case of the Chernoff method) gives a bound of order exp(−2ε²n).

Other useful inequalities include the Paley–Zygmund inequality, which gives a lower bound on P(X > θE[X]) for non‑negative X using the second moment, and the union bound (Boole’s inequality) P(∪ Aᵢ) ≤ Σ P(Aᵢ), often combined with Chernoff to control the maximum of many random variables. Mastery of these tools allows rapid estimation of tail probabilities where exact computation is infeasible.

Conditional Expectation and the Law of Total Expectation

Conditional expectation E[X | Y] is a random variable that is a function of Y, defined by the property that for any measurable set B in the range of Y, ∫Y∈B E[X | Y] dP = ∫Y∈B X dP. For discrete variables, E[X | Y = y] = Σₓ x·P(X = x | Y = y). The law of total expectation states E[X] = E[E[X | Y]], which follows by taking B as the whole space. This identity reduces an unconditional expectation to a weighted average of conditional expectations.

A classic application is the random sum: let N be a non‑negative integer‑valued random variable independent of an i.i.d. sequence X₁, X₂, … with mean μ. Then E[Σi=1N X_i] = E[N]·μ. The proof uses E[Σ X_i | N = n] = nμ and then E[E[Σ X_i | N]] = E[N]μ. Another frequent scenario is a two‑stage experiment: first choose a box with probability pᵢ, then draw a ball. The overall probability of a red ball is Σ pᵢ·P(red | box i), the law of total probability for indicators.

Conditional variance decomposes as Var(X) = E[Var(X | Y)] + Var(E[X | Y]), separating variability due to Y from average internal variability. This is useful in hierarchical models and for the Rao–Blackwell theorem. In competitive problems, conditioning on a convenient variable (e.g., the first step of a random walk) often turns an intractable expectation into a simple recurrence.

Technique: Conditioning on the First Step
A fair coin is tossed repeatedly. Let X be the number of tosses until the pattern HTH appears for the first time. Find E[X].
Define states based on the longest suffix matching a prefix of HTH: state 0 (no match), state 1 (H), state 2 (HT), state 3 (HTH, absorbing). Let e_i be the expected additional tosses from state i. We have e3 = 0. From state 0, a toss gives H with probability 1/2 (move to state 1) or T with probability 1/2 (stay in state 0): e0 = 1 + (1/2)e1 + (1/2)e0 → e0 = 2 + e1. From state 1, H keeps us in state 1, T moves to state 2: e1 = 1 + (1/2)e1 + (1/2)e2 → e1 = 2 + e2. From state 2, H completes the pattern (state 3), T returns to state 0: e2 = 1 + (1/2)·0 + (1/2)e0 → e2 = 1 + (1/2)e0. Solve: substitute e1 = 2 + e2 into e0 = 2 + e1 → e0 = 4 + e2. Then e2 = 1 + (1/2)(4 + e2) = 1 + 2 + (1/2)e2 → (1/2)e2 = 3 → e2 = 6. Then e0 = 4 + 6 = 10. Hence E[X] = 10.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for an expectation of a stopping time or a sum with a random number of terms, first identify a natural filtration (e.g., the outcome of the first trial). Write the conditional expectation given that first outcome, then apply the law of total expectation. This reduces the problem to a system of linear equations that can be solved quickly. Always check whether the conditioning variable is independent of the future increments; if it is, the conditional expectation simplifies to a constant plus the original expectation.

Combinatorial Proofs and Bijective Techniques

Combinatorial proofs establish identities by counting the same set in two ways. The most common method is double counting: define a set S, count |S| directly for one expression, then partition S by a parameter for another. Equating yields the identity. A classic example is Vandermonde’s convolution: Σk=0r C(m, k) C(n, r−k) = C(m+n, r). The left side counts ways to choose r people from m men and n women by first choosing k men; the right side chooses r from the total m+n directly.

Bijective proofs construct an explicit one‑to‑one correspondence between two combinatorial families. For instance, the Catalan numbers C_n = (1/(n+1)) C(2n, n) count Dyck paths of length 2n, binary trees with n+1 leaves, and non‑crossing partitions of {1,…,n}. A standard bijection maps a Dyck path to a binary tree by reading the path as a sequence of pushes and pops on a stack. Another elegant bijection proves Σk=0n C(n, k)² = C(2n, n) by pairing subsets of an n‑set with their complements to form n‑element subsets of a 2n‑set.

The involution principle proves alternating sum identities. Define an involution (a self‑inverse bijection) on a signed set that pairs most elements with opposite signs, leaving only fixed points to contribute. This yields the inclusion–exclusion formula and the derangement formula D_n = n! Σk=0n (−1)^k/k!. In examinations, a problem may ask to prove Σk=0n (−1)^k C(n, k) C(m+k, r) = (−1)^n C(m, r−n). The standard approach interprets the left side as the coefficient of x^r in (1+x)^m (1 − (1+x))^n = (−1)^n x^n (1+x)^m, giving the result. A bijective proof using sign‑reversing involutions on lattice paths also exists.

Polya’s Enumeration Theorem and Burnside’s Lemma

Burnside’s lemma (Cauchy–Frobenius lemma) counts orbits of a finite group G acting on a set X: the number of orbits equals (1/|G|) Σg∈G |Fix(g)|, where Fix(g) = {x∈X : g·x = x}. This reduces enumeration of distinct colourings under symmetry to a sum over group elements. For example, the number of distinct colourings of the vertices of a square with k colours, up to rotations, is (k⁴ + k² + 2k)/4. The rotation group C₄ has elements: identity (k⁴ fixed), rotation by 90° (k fixed), 180° (k² fixed), 270° (k fixed). Sum = k⁴ + k² + 2k, divide by 4.

Polya’s enumeration theorem generalizes this with a generating function recording colourings by colour inventory. The cycle index polynomial of G acting on n objects is Z_G(z₁,…,z_n) = (1/|G|) Σg∈G z₁c₁(g) … z_nc_n(g), where c_i(g) is the number of cycles of length i in g. Substituting z_i = Σcolours w_colour^i yields the pattern inventory. For the necklace problem with n beads and k colours, the cyclic group C_n has cycle index Z = (1/n) Σd|n φ(d) z_dn/d. Setting each z_d = k gives the number of distinct necklaces (rotations only) as (1/n) Σd|n φ(d) kn/d. If reflections are allowed (dihedral group D_n), the cycle index includes additional terms depending on whether n is odd or even.

These tools solve problems such as: How many distinct 3‑colourings of the faces of a cube exist up to rotations? The rotation group of the cube has 24 elements with cycle structures on faces: identity (six 1-cycles); six 90° face rotations (two 1-cycles, one 4-cycle); three 180° face rotations (two 1-cycles, two 2-cycles); eight 120° vertex rotations (two 3-cycles); six 180° edge rotations (three 2-cycles). Plugging into the cycle index and substituting k=3 yields the answer. Mastery of cycle index computation for standard symmetry groups (cyclic, dihedral, symmetric, platonic solids) is a high‑yield skill.

Random Walks and Gambler’s Ruin

A simple symmetric random walk on the integers starts at 0 and at each step moves +1 or −1 with probability 1/2. Let S_n be the position after n steps. The walk is recurrent: it returns to 0 infinitely often with probability 1. The expected return time to 0 is infinite, illustrating the distinction between recurrence and positive recurrence. For an asymmetric walk with step +1 probability p ≠ 1/2, the walk is transient and drifts to +∞ if p > 1/2.

The gambler’s ruin problem considers a walk on {0, 1, …, N} with absorbing boundaries at 0 and N. Starting at i, the probability of reaching N before 0 is (1 − (q/p)^i) / (1 − (q/p)^N) for p ≠ q, and i/N for p = q = 1/2. The expected duration is i(N−i) for the fair case, and for p ≠ q it is i/(q−p) − N/(q−p) · (1 − (q/p)^i)/(1 − (q/p)^N). These formulas are derived by solving the linear recurrences u_i = p ui+1 + q ui−1 with boundary conditions u0 = 0, u_N = 1 for the probability, and a similar inhomogeneous recurrence for the expectation.

Random walks also model ballot problems and Catalan numbers. The ballot theorem states that if candidate A receives a votes and B receives b votes with a > b, the probability that A is always strictly ahead during the count is (a−b)/(a+b). This equals the probability that a simple random walk from (0,0) to (a+b, a−b) stays above the x‑axis, counted by the reflection principle. The reflection principle maps paths that touch the axis to paths from (0,−2) to (a+b, a−b), yielding the Catalan number C_n = (1/(n+1)) C(2n, n) for Dyck paths of length 2n. In examinations, a common twist asks for the probability that a random walk stays non‑negative given a final position, solved by the same reflection argument.

  • Confusing the conditions for Markov’s inequality (requires non‑negative variable) with Chebyshev’s (requires finite variance).
  • Applying Chernoff bounds to variables that are not sums of independent indicators without justification.
  • Forgetting that the law of total expectation requires the conditioning variable to be integrable; using it on a non‑integrable Y leads to undefined expressions.
  • Misidentifying the cycle structure of a symmetry group element when computing the cycle index for Polya’s theorem.
  • Using the fair‑coin gambler’s ruin formula i/N when the step probabilities are not equal.
  • Neglecting the reflection principle’s requirement that the walk starts at 0 and the barrier is at 0; shifting the barrier incorrectly changes the count.
  • Assuming that a bijective proof is complete without verifying that the constructed map is indeed a bijection (both injective and surjective).

Stirling Numbers and Set Partitions

Stirling numbers appear in two flavours, each counting a different combinatorial structure on an n‑element set. The unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind, denoted c(n,k) or ⎡n k⎤, count permutations of n distinct objects with exactly k cycles. They satisfy the recurrence c(n,k) = c(n‑1,k‑1) + (n‑1) c(n‑1,k) with boundary conditions c(0,0)=1 and c(n,0)=c(0,k)=0 for n,k>0. This recurrence reflects inserting element n either as a new 1‑cycle (first term) or into one of the n‑1 positions inside existing cycles of a permutation of n‑1 elements (second term). The signed Stirling numbers of the first kind s(n,k) = (−1)n‑k c(n,k) appear as coefficients in the expansion of the falling factorial x(x‑1)…(x‑n+1) = Σk=0^n s(n,k) x^k.

Stirling numbers of the second kind, denoted S(n,k) or ⎧n k⎫, count partitions of an n‑element set into k non‑empty, unlabelled blocks. Their recurrence is S(n,k) = S(n‑1,k‑1) + k S(n‑1,k), with S(0,0)=1 and S(n,0)=S(0,k)=0 for n,k>0. The first term corresponds to placing element n in a block by itself; the second to inserting n into one of the k blocks of a partition of n‑1 elements. The Bell numbers B_n = Σk=0^n S(n,k) give the total number of partitions of an n‑set. They satisfy Bn+1 = Σk=0^n C(n,k) B_k and have the exponential generating function exp(e^x‑1).

These numbers connect to many areas. The number of surjections from an n‑set onto a k‑set is k! S(n,k). The moments of a Poisson(λ) random variable are E[X^n] = Σk=0^n S(n,k) λ^k. The unsigned first‑kind numbers appear in the expansion of rising factorials: x(x+1)…(x+n‑1) = Σk=0^n c(n,k) x^k. The two families are inverse matrices: Σ_k s(n,k) S(k,m) = δn,m. This orthogonality is useful for converting between polynomial bases.

Explicit formulas exist but are rarely used in examinations. For the second kind, S(n,k) = (1/k!) Σi=0^k (−1)^i C(k,i) (k‑i)^n. For the first kind, c(n,k) is the coefficient of x^k in the rising factorial; for example, c(n,2) = (n‑1)! Hn‑1 where H_m = Σi=1^m 1/i. The asymptotic behaviour of Bell numbers is given by the de Bruijn approximation log B_n ∼ n log n − n log log n − n. Mastery of the recurrences and the ability to set up combinatorial arguments that yield these numbers is more valuable than memorising closed forms.

Typical examination questions ask for the number of ways to distribute n distinct balls into k identical boxes with no box empty (S(n,k)), or the number of permutations of n with exactly k cycles (c(n,k)). A common twist asks for the number of permutations where all cycles have even length, which can be tackled by exponential generating functions or by adapting the recurrence for c(n,k) with a parity constraint.

The Probabilistic Method and the Lovász Local Lemma

The probabilistic method, pioneered by Paul Erdős, is a non‑constructive technique for proving the existence of a combinatorial object with a desired property. Define a probability space of objects (often a random graph, colouring, or assignment) and show that the probability a randomly chosen object has the property is strictly positive. If the probability is positive, at least one such object exists. The method often reduces to showing that the expected number of “bad” events is less than 1, which implies an outcome with zero bad events.

A classic example is the lower bound on the diagonal Ramsey number R(k,k). Consider a random 2‑colouring of the edges of K_n, each edge independently red or blue with probability 1/2. For any fixed set of k vertices, the probability it forms a monochromatic K_k is 2 · 2−C(k,2) = 21−k(k‑1)/2. There are C(n,k) such k‑sets. By linearity of expectation, the expected number of monochromatic K_k is C(n,k) 21−k(k‑1)/2. If this expectation is less than 1, there exists a colouring with no monochromatic K_k, proving R(k,k) > n. Choosing n = ⌊2k/2⌋ yields R(k,k) > ⌊2k/2⌋ for k ≥ 3.

The Lovász Local Lemma (LLL) strengthens this approach when bad events are not independent but have limited dependence. Let A1,…,A_n be events in a probability space. Construct a dependency graph where vertices are events and edges connect dependent pairs. If each event A_i has probability at most p, and each vertex has degree at most d, and if e p (d+1) ≤ 1 (where e is the base of natural logarithms), then the probability that none of the events occur is positive. The symmetric version is often quoted as: if p ≤ 1/(e(d+1)), then P(∧ ¬A_i) > 0. The asymmetric version replaces the uniform bound with individual probabilities p_i and requires numbers x_i ∈ (0,1) such that P(A_i) ≤ x_i ∏j∈Γ(i) (1−x_j), where Γ(i) are the neighbours of i in the dependency graph.

Applications of LLL include proving the existence of proper colourings of graphs with large girth and high chromatic number, constructing packet routing schedules, and showing certain SAT formulas are satisfiable. In examinations, a typical question provides a set of bad events and asks to verify the LLL condition. The key steps are: (1) define the probability space, (2) identify the bad events and compute their probabilities, (3) build the dependency graph and bound the maximum degree d, (4) check the inequality e p (d+1) ≤ 1. If the condition holds, conclude existence.

A frequent pitfall is assuming independence where only limited dependence exists, or miscounting the degree in the dependency graph. Another is forgetting that LLL only guarantees existence; it does not provide an efficient algorithm to find the object (though algorithmic versions exist, they are beyond the standard syllabus). The method is powerful precisely because it bypasses explicit construction, which is often extremely difficult.

Probabilistic Method: Ramsey Lower Bound
Show that for any integer k ≥ 3, the Ramsey number R(k,k) satisfies R(k,k) > ⌊2k/2⌋.
Consider a random 2‑colouring of the edges of K_n where n = ⌊2k/2⌋. Each edge is independently coloured red or blue with probability 1/2. For a fixed set S of k vertices, the probability that all C(k,2) edges inside S have the same colour is 2 · (1/2)C(k,2) = 21−k(k‑1)/2. There are C(n,k) such k‑sets. By linearity of expectation, the expected number of monochromatic K_k is E = C(n,k) · 21−k(k‑1)/2. Using C(n,k) ≤ n^k/k! and n ≤ 2k/2, we have E ≤ (2k/2)^k / k! · 21−k(k‑1)/2 = 2k2/2 / k! · 21−k2/2 + k/2 = 21 + k/2 / k!. For k ≥ 3, k! ≥ 6 and 21+k/2 ≤ 22.5 ≈ 5.66, so E < 1. Hence there exists a colouring with no monochromatic K_k, proving R(k,k) > n = ⌊2k/2⌋.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Probabilistic Method
When a problem asks you to prove existence using the probabilistic method, first clearly state the random experiment (e.g., random colouring, random subset). Define the bad events precisely. Compute the probability of each bad event, then use linearity of expectation to bound the expected number of bad events. If the expectation is < 1, you are done. For the Lovász Local Lemma, explicitly draw or describe the dependency graph, bound the maximum degree d, and verify e p (d+1) ≤ 1. Do not forget to mention that the lemma guarantees a positive probability, hence existence.

Martingales and the Optional Stopping Theorem

A martingale is a sequence of random variables (X_n)n≥0 adapted to a filtration (F_n) such that E[|X_n|] < ∞ and E[Xn+1 | F_n] = X_n for all n. The filtration represents the information available up to time n. The simplest example is a simple symmetric random walk: let X0 = 0 and X_n = Σi=1^n ξ_i where ξ_i are independent Rademacher variables (±1 with probability 1/2). Then E[Xn+1 | F_n] = X_n + E[ξn+1] = X_n. Another example is the product of independent mean‑1 variables: if Y_i are independent with E[Y_i]=1, then M_n = ∏i=1^n Y_i is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration.

A stopping time T is a random variable taking values in {0,1,2,…} ∪ {∞} such that the event {T ≤ n} is determined by F_n for each n. The Optional Stopping Theorem (OST) states that if (X_n) is a martingale and T is a stopping time, then under certain conditions E[X_T] = E[X0]. Common sufficient conditions are: (i) T is bounded almost surely; (ii) X_n is bounded by an integrable random variable and T is finite almost surely; (iii) the increments Xn+1−X_n are bounded and E[T] < ∞. If none of these hold, the conclusion may fail, as illustrated by the simple symmetric random walk stopped at the first time it hits +1: the walk is a martingale, the stopping time is finite almost surely, but E[X_T] = 1 ≠ 0 = E[X0] because the expected stopping time is infinite.

OST is a powerful tool for computing hitting probabilities and expected durations. For the gambler’s ruin problem with absorbing boundaries at 0 and N, the position X_n is a martingale. Applying OST to the bounded stopping time T = min{n : X_n ∈ {0,N}} yields E[X_T] = i, which gives the probability of reaching N before 0 as i/N for the fair case. For the biased case, the process (q/p)X_n is a martingale, and OST yields the formula (1−(q/p)^i)/(1−(q/p)^N). Wald’s equation for the sum of a random number of i.i.d. variables is another consequence: if S_n = Σi=1^n ξ_i with E[ξ_i]=μ and T is a stopping time with E[T] < ∞ and some boundedness condition, then E[S_T] = μ E[T].

Martingales also lead to concentration inequalities. Azuma’s inequality states that if (X_n) is a martingale with |X_n − Xn‑1| ≤ c_n almost surely, then for any t > 0, P(|X_n − X0| ≥ t) ≤ 2 exp(−t2 / (2 Σi=1^n c_i2)). This is a martingale analogue of Hoeffding’s inequality and is proved by applying the exponential Markov inequality to the martingale exponential. It is widely used in the analysis of randomized algorithms and random graphs where the exposure martingale (Doob martingale) has bounded differences.

In examinations, you may be asked to verify that a given process is a martingale, to identify a suitable stopping time, and to apply OST after checking the required conditions. A common trap is to apply OST without justifying boundedness or integrability. Always state which condition you are using (bounded stopping time, bounded increments with finite expectation, or dominated convergence) and verify it explicitly.

Concentration Inequalities: Hoeffding, Azuma, and McDiarmid

Concentration inequalities bound the probability that a random variable deviates significantly from its mean. Hoeffding’s inequality applies to sums of independent bounded random variables. Let X1,…,X_n be independent with a_i ≤ X_i ≤ b_i almost surely. Let S_n = Σ X_i and μ = E[S_n]. Then for any t > 0, P(S_n − μ ≥ t) ≤ exp(−2 t2 / Σ (b_i − a_i)2) and the same bound holds for the lower tail. The two‑sided version is P(|S_n − μ| ≥ t) ≤ 2 exp(−2 t2 / Σ (b_i − a_i)2). This inequality is sharp for Rademacher variables and underpins many results in learning theory and statistics.

Azuma’s inequality extends Hoeffding’s bound to martingales with bounded differences. Let (X_n) be a martingale with respect to (F_n) such that |X_n − Xn‑1| ≤ c_n almost surely. Then for any t > 0, P(|X_n − X0| ≥ t) ≤ 2 exp(−t2 / (2 Σi=1^n c_i2)). The proof uses the fact that the exponential of a martingale with bounded increments is a supermartingale, then applies Markov’s inequality. Azuma’s inequality is particularly useful for the exposure martingale of a function of independent random variables: reveal the variables one by one and track the conditional expectation.

McDiarmid’s bounded differences inequality (also called the method of bounded differences) is a direct corollary of Azuma’s inequality for functions of independent variables. Let f : ∏i=1^n Ω_i → ℝ be a function such that changing the i‑th coordinate while keeping others fixed changes the value of f by at most c_i. If X1,…,X_n are independent random variables taking values in Ω_i, then for any t > 0, P(|f(X) − E[f(X)]| ≥ t) ≤ 2 exp(−2 t2 / Σ c_i2). The proof constructs the Doob martingale Y_k = E[f(X) | X1,…,X_k] and notes that |Y_k − Yk‑1| ≤ c_k. McDiarmid’s inequality is often easier to apply because it only requires checking the bounded differences condition on f, not constructing a martingale explicitly.

Typical applications include: bounding the chromatic number of a random graph G(n,p) (the exposure martingale has differences at most 1), analysing the length of the longest increasing subsequence in a random permutation, and proving concentration of the empirical risk in machine learning. In examinations, you may be given a function f of independent variables and asked to derive a concentration bound. The steps are: (1) verify the bounded differences condition, (2) compute or bound the sum of squares of the bounds c_i, (3) plug into McDiarmid’s inequality. If the variables are not independent but form a martingale, use Azuma’s inequality instead.

A common mistake is to apply Hoeffding’s inequality to variables that are not independent, or to use Azuma’s inequality without verifying the bounded differences condition. Another is to confuse the constants: Hoeffding’s exponent is −2 t2 / Σ (b_i−a_i)2; Azuma’s exponent is −t2 / (2 Σ c_i2); McDiarmid’s exponent is −2 t2 / Σ c_i2. Note the factor‑of‑2 differences. Use the correct version for the given setting.

  • Confusing Stirling numbers of the first kind (cycle counts) with those of the second kind (set partitions) and using the wrong recurrence.
  • Assuming the probabilistic method yields an explicit construction; it only proves existence.
  • Misidentifying the dependency graph when applying the Lovász Local Lemma, leading to an incorrect degree bound.
  • Applying the Optional Stopping Theorem without verifying boundedness, finite expectation, or dominated convergence conditions.
  • Using Azuma’s inequality on a process that does not have almost surely bounded increments.
  • Mixing up the constants in Hoeffding’s, Azuma’s, and McDiarmid’s inequalities, especially the factor of 2 in the exponent.
  • Forgetting that McDiarmid’s inequality requires the bounded differences condition to hold for every coordinate change, not just on average.

Catalan Numbers and Their Combinatorial Interpretations

The Catalan numbers Cn = (1/(n+1)) binom(2n, n) form one of the most ubiquitous sequences in enumerative combinatorics. They satisfy the quadratic recurrence C0 = 1, Cn+1 = Σi=0n CiCn-i, which reflects the decomposition of many Catalan objects into a left part and a right part. The ordinary generating function C(x) = Σ Cnxn satisfies C(x) = 1 + xC(x)2, yielding the closed form C(x) = (1 − √(1−4x))/(2x). This algebraic equation is the starting point for many analytic proofs, including the asymptotic Cn ~ 4n/(√π n3/2).

Classical interpretations include: (1) Dyck paths of semilength n — lattice paths from (0,0) to (2n,0) with steps (1,1) and (1,−1) that never go below the x-axis; (2) correctly matched parentheses strings of length 2n; (3) full binary trees with n+1 leaves; (4) triangulations of a convex (n+2)-gon; (5) non‑crossing partitions of an n-element set; (6) stack‑sortable permutations of length n. The bijections between these families are often constructed via the “first return” decomposition of a Dyck path or the “root edge” removal in a tree. For example, the number of ways to parenthesise a product of n+1 factors equals Cn because each parenthesisation corresponds to a binary tree whose internal nodes are the multiplication operations.

Catalan numbers also appear in the ballot problem: if candidate A receives p votes and candidate B receives q votes with p > q, the probability that A stays strictly ahead throughout the count is (p−q)/(p+q). Setting p = n+1, q = n gives the Catalan number Cn = (1/(2n+1)) binom(2n+1, n). A related array, the Catalan triangle T(n,k) = (k+1)/(n+1) binom(2n−k, n), refines the Catalan numbers by an additional parameter and arises in several Dyck path enumerations. In examinations, you may be asked to prove that a given combinatorial class is counted by Catalan numbers by exhibiting a bijection to Dyck paths or by verifying the recurrence. A common technique is to define a “canonical” decomposition of the object that mirrors the recurrence Cn+1 = Σ CiCn-i.

Exponential Generating Functions and Labelled Structures

While ordinary generating functions (OGFs) are natural for unlabelled structures, exponential generating functions (EGFs) are the correct tool for labelled objects where the labels are distinct and the order of labels matters. The EGF of a sequence (an) is A(x) = Σn≥0 an xn/n!. The factor 1/n! compensates for the n! ways to assign labels to an n-element structure. The fundamental operations on labelled classes translate into simple operations on EGFs: disjoint union corresponds to addition, labelled product (where labels are distributed between two components) corresponds to multiplication, and the sequence construction (SEQ) corresponds to 1/(1−A(x)). The set construction (SET) gives exp(A(x)), and the cycle construction (CYC) gives log(1/(1−A(x))). These rules constitute the symbolic method for labelled structures (Flajolet and Sedgewick).

Classic examples: permutations are sets of cycles, so their EGF is exp(log(1/(1−x))) = 1/(1−x) = Σ n! xn/n!. Set partitions are sets of non‑empty sets, giving EGF exp(exp(x)−1). Involutions (permutations with only cycles of length 1 or 2) are sets of 1‑cycles and 2‑cycles, yielding EGF exp(x + x2/2). The number of involutions In satisfies In = In−1 + (n−1)In−2 with I0=I1=1. Derangements (permutations with no fixed points) are sets of cycles of length ≥2, EGF exp(−x)/(1−x). The Lagrange inversion formula is a powerful tool for extracting coefficients from implicitly defined generating functions: if y = xΦ(y) with Φ(0)≠0, then [xn]y = (1/n)[tn−1]Φ(t)n. This yields the number of rooted labelled trees (nn−1) and the number of connected labelled graphs.

In examinations, you may be given a combinatorial specification and asked to write the EGF, then extract a recurrence or an asymptotic estimate. A typical trap is to confuse OGF and EGF operations: for labelled structures, the product is not the Cauchy product of OGFs but the exponential convolution. Always check whether the objects are labelled (distinct labels) or unlabelled (indistinguishable). Another trap is to forget the 1/n! factor when converting between EGF coefficients and actual counts.

Worked Example: Involutions via EGF
Find the exponential generating function for the number of involutions on an n-element set, and derive a closed form for the number of involutions In.
An involution is a permutation whose cycles have length 1 or 2. In the labelled framework, a 1‑cycle is a singleton set (EGF x), a 2‑cycle is an unordered pair of distinct labels (EGF x2/2). Since an involution is a set of such cycles, the EGF is exp(x + x2/2). To extract coefficients, expand: exp(x) exp(x2/2) = (Σk≥0 xk/k!) (Σj≥0 x2j/(2j j!)). The coefficient of xn is Σj=0⌊n/2⌋ 1/((n−2j)! 2j j!). Multiplying by n! gives In = Σj=0⌊n/2⌋ n!/((n−2j)! 2j j!). This matches the recurrence In = In−1 + (n−1)In−2 obtained by considering whether the element n is a fixed point or paired with one of the n−1 other elements.
Exam‑Day Strategy for EGFs
When a problem asks for the number of labelled structures, immediately write the EGF using the symbolic rules (SET → exp, CYC → log, SEQ → 1/(1−)). Then decide whether you need a closed form, a recurrence, or an asymptotic. For closed forms, expand the EGF as a product of known series and collect coefficients. For recurrences, differentiate the EGF or use the fact that multiplication by x corresponds to shifting indices. For asymptotics, locate the dominant singularity of the EGF (often a pole or branch point) and apply standard transfer theorems. Always verify the first few values by hand to catch algebraic slips.

The Second Moment Method and the Alteration Technique

The second moment method is a cornerstone of the probabilistic method for proving existence of combinatorial objects. Let X be a non‑negative integer‑valued random variable counting the number of desired structures (e.g., cliques, independent sets, arithmetic progressions). If we can show that E[X] > 0 and that the variance is not too large relative to the square of the mean, then P(X > 0) > 0. Chebyshev’s inequality gives P(X = 0) ≤ Var(X)/E[X]2. A sharper bound is the Paley–Zygmund inequality: P(X > 0) ≥ E[X]2/E[X2]. Thus it suffices to prove E[X2] ≤ (1+o(1))E[X]2. This is often achieved by expanding E[X2] = ΣA,B P(A and B both appear) and showing that the contribution from overlapping pairs A,B is negligible compared to the square of the sum over all pairs.

A classic application is the lower bound on the clique number of the Erdős–Rényi random graph G(n, 1/2). Let Xk be the number of k-cliques. Then E[Xk] = binom(n, k) 2−binom(k,2). Choosing k = 2 log2 n − 2 log2 log2 n + O(1) makes the expectation tend to infinity. The second moment calculation shows that the ratio E[Xk2]/E[Xk]2 → 1, so a k-clique exists with high probability. This yields the celebrated result that the clique number is concentrated on two values.

The alteration method (also called the deletion method) is a complementary technique: first construct a random object that has many “good” features but also some “bad” ones, then delete a small number of elements to remove all bad features. For example, to prove a lower bound on the Ramsey number R(k,k), consider a random 2‑colouring of the edges of Kn. The expected number of monochromatic k-cliques is 2 binom(n, k) 2−binom(k,2). If this expectation is less than 1/2, there exists a colouring with no monochromatic k-clique, giving R(k,k) > n. When the expectation is larger, one can delete one vertex from each monochromatic k-clique; the expected number of deleted vertices is at most the expected number of cliques. If after deletion the remaining graph still has many vertices, we obtain a larger lower bound. The alteration method often gives better constants than the naive first moment method because it tolerates a moderate number of bad events.

In examinations, you may be asked to apply the second moment method to a specific random variable (e.g., number of triangles in G(n, p)) or to design an alteration argument for a given extremal problem. Key steps: define the random variable clearly, compute the first moment, compute the second moment by splitting the sum over pairs according to the size of their intersection, and bound the ratio. A common mistake is to ignore the contribution from pairs with large overlap, which can dominate the second moment. Always check the “diagonal” terms (identical pairs) and the “off‑diagonal” terms separately.

Markov Chains: Coupling, Stationarity, and Mixing Times

A finite Markov chain is defined by a state space Ω and a transition matrix P where P(x,y) = P(Xt+1 = y | Xt = x). A distribution π is stationary if πP = π. If the chain is irreducible and aperiodic, the stationary distribution is unique and the chain converges to π regardless of the initial state. The total variation distance between the distribution at time t started from x and π is dTV(t) = maxx ½ Σy |Pt(x,y) − π(y)|. The mixing time tmix(ε) is the smallest t such that dTV(t) ≤ ε.

Coupling is a powerful technique to bound mixing times. A coupling of two copies of the chain is a joint process (Xt, Yt) such that each marginal evolves according to P. The coupling inequality states that dTV(t) ≤ P(Xt ≠ Yt). If we can design a coupling that meets (coalesces) quickly with high probability, we obtain a strong bound on the mixing time. For example, for the lazy random walk on the hypercube {0,1}n, a natural coupling flips the same coordinate in both chains; the Hamming distance between the two chains is a Markov chain that decreases by 1 with probability 1/(2n) and stays the same otherwise. This yields a mixing time of order n log n.

For reversible chains (those satisfying detailed balance π(x)P(x,y) = π(y)P(y,x)), the spectral gap 1−λ2 (where λ2 is the second largest eigenvalue of P) controls the mixing time: tmix(ε) ≤ (1/(1−λ2)) log(1/(ε πmin)). Cheeger’s inequality relates the spectral gap to the conductance Φ = minS: π(S)≤1/2 (Σx∈S,y∉S π(x)P(x,y))/π(S). This provides a geometric way to bound mixing times without computing eigenvalues. Path coupling, introduced by Bubley and Dyer, simplifies coupling arguments by only specifying couplings for pairs of states at distance 1 in a suitable metric; the coupling for arbitrary pairs is then obtained by concatenating paths.

Applications include card shuffling (the Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds model of riffle shuffles mixes in approximately 3/2 log2 n steps), Glauber dynamics for the Ising model, and random walks on expander graphs. In examinations, you may be asked to compute the stationary distribution of a given chain (often by solving detailed balance equations), to design a coupling and bound the meeting time, or to apply Cheeger’s inequality to a graph. A frequent trap is to assume that a chain is reversible without verifying detailed balance, or to use the spectral gap bound without checking that the chain is lazy (to avoid periodicity). Always confirm irreducibility and aperiodicity before invoking convergence theorems.

Talagrand's Concentration Inequality and the Entropy Method

Talagrand’s inequality is a far‑reaching concentration result for product probability spaces that applies to convex Lipschitz functions. Let Ω = Πi=1n Ωi be a product space equipped with a product measure μ. A function f : Ω → ℝ is convex if its extension to the convex hull of Ω is convex, and it is L-Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean metric if |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ L √(Σi 1{xi≠yi}) for all x,y. Talagrand’s inequality states that for any such f and any median Mf of f, μ(f ≥ Mf + t) ≤ exp(−t2/(4L2)) and similarly for the lower tail. This is stronger than McDiarmid’s bounded differences inequality because it does not require bounded differences coordinate‑wise; it only needs convexity and a global Lipschitz condition. The proof uses the entropy method: one considers the logarithmic moment generating function ψ(λ) = log ∫ exp(λf) dμ and shows that its derivative satisfies a differential inequality derived from a log‑Sobolev inequality for product measures (the Herbst argument).

The entropy method is a general framework for proving concentration inequalities. For a random variable Z, the entropy is Ent(Z) = E[Z log Z] − E[Z] log E[Z]. If one can establish a log‑Sobolev inequality of the form Ent(g2) ≤ 2C E[|∇g|2] for all smooth g, then by the Herbst argument one obtains sub‑Gaussian concentration for any 1‑Lipschitz function with variance factor C. For product measures on {0,1}n, the optimal constant is C = 1/4, yielding Talagrand’s inequality. The method also yields modified log‑Sobolev inequalities for other spaces (e.g., the symmetric group, the slice of the hypercube) and leads to concentration for functions with bounded influences.

Applications of Talagrand’s inequality include the concentration of the length of the longest increasing subsequence in a random permutation (the Ulam problem), the concentration of the largest eigenvalue of a Wigner matrix, and the chromatic number of a random graph G(n, p) where the function is convex and 1‑Lipschitz after a suitable scaling. In examinations, you may be given a function defined on a product space and asked to verify the convexity and Lipschitz conditions, then apply Talagrand’s inequality to obtain a tail bound. A common trap is to apply Talagrand’s inequality to a function that is not convex (e.g., the number of triangles in G(n, p) is not convex), in which case one must resort to McDiarmid’s inequality or the method of bounded differences. Another trap is to confuse the Euclidean Lipschitz constant with the Hamming Lipschitz constant; Talagrand’s inequality uses the Euclidean metric on the indicator vectors of coordinate changes.
