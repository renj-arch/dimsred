Vector Algebra Fundamentals

1 Definition and Representation of Vectors

A vector is a mathematical object that possesses both magnitude and direction, distinguishing it from a scalar which has magnitude only. In three‑dimensional space a vector can be represented by an ordered triple of real numbers (x, y, z) which are its components along the mutually perpendicular coordinate axes. The same vector may also be depicted as a directed line segment from the origin to the point (x, y, z) or as a free vector that can be translated without altering its properties. The notation a or a is commonly used, and the zero vector 0 has all components equal to zero and no definite direction.

2 Vector Addition and Scalar Multiplication

Vector addition follows the parallelogram law: the sum a+b is obtained by placing the tail of b at the head of a and drawing the resultant from the tail of a to the head of b. Component‑wise this means (a1,a2,a3)+(b1,b2,b3)=(a1+b1,a2+b2,a3+b3). Scalar multiplication by a real number k stretches or shrinks the vector and reverses its direction when k is negative, giving ka=(ka1,ka2,ka3). Both operations satisfy the axioms of a vector space, making the set of all three‑dimensional vectors a real vector space of dimension three.

3 Components and Direction Cosines

The components of a vector are its projections on the coordinate axes; if a=a1i+a2j+a3k then a1,a2,a3 are the scalar components. The direction cosines l,m,n are the cosines of the angles that the vector makes with the positive x, y, and z axes respectively, and they satisfy l2+m2+n2=1. They are obtained by dividing each component by the magnitude of the vector: l=a1/|a|, m=a2/|a|, n=a3/|a|. Direction ratios are any three numbers proportional to the direction cosines and are often used when the magnitude is not required.

4 Magnitude and Unit Vectors

The magnitude (or length) of a is |a|=√(a12+a22+a32), derived from the Pythagorean theorem in three dimensions. A unit vector has magnitude one and points in the same direction as the original vector; it is obtained by dividing the vector by its magnitude, a=a/|a|. The standard basis vectors i,j,k are unit vectors along the coordinate axes. Any vector can be expressed as a linear combination of these basis vectors, which is the foundation for all subsequent algebraic manipulations in three‑dimensional geometry.

Dot Product and Its Geometric Significance

5 Algebraic Definition of the Dot Product

The dot product (also called scalar product) of two vectors a=a1i+a2j+a3k and b=b1i+b2j+b3k is defined algebraically as acdotb=a1b1+a2b2+a3b3. This operation yields a scalar, not a vector, and it is commutative: acdotb=bcdota. The dot product distributes over addition, a·(b+c)=acdotb+acdotc, and it is compatible with scalar multiplication, (ka)cdotb=k(acdotb). These algebraic properties make the dot product a powerful tool for simplifying vector expressions.

6 Geometric Interpretation and Angle Between Vectors

Geometrically the dot product equals the product of the magnitudes of the two vectors and the cosine of the angle θ between them: acdotb=|a||b|cosθ. This relationship provides a direct method to compute the angle: cosθ=(acdotb)/(|a||b|). When the dot product is zero the vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular), because cosθ=0 implies θ=90^°. The sign of the dot product indicates whether the angle is acute (positive) or obtuse (negative), which is useful in determining the relative orientation of vectors in physics and engineering problems.

7 Projection of One Vector Onto Another

The scalar projection of a onto b is the length of the shadow of a on the line of b and is given by projba=(acdotb)/|b|. The vector projection, which retains direction, is big((acdotb)/|b|2big)b. These formulas follow directly from the geometric definition of the dot product and are essential for resolving forces, finding components of velocity, and solving optimisation problems where a quantity must be maximised or minimised along a given direction.

8 Applications in Work and Orthogonality

In physics the work done by a constant force F moving an object through a displacement d is W=Fcdotd; only the component of the force along the displacement contributes to the work. In geometry, two lines are perpendicular if their direction vectors have a zero dot product, and a vector is normal to a plane if it is orthogonal to every direction vector lying in the plane. The dot product therefore bridges algebraic computation and geometric reasoning, making it indispensable for both theoretical derivations and practical calculations.

Example: Angle Between Two Vectors
Find the angle between the vectors a=2i-3j+6k and b=-i+2j-2k.
First compute the dot product: acdotb=2(-1)+(-3)(2)+6(-2)=-2-6-12=-20. Next find the magnitudes: |a|=√(22+(-3)2+62)=√(4+9+36)=√(49)=7; |b|=√((-1)2+22+(-2)2)=√(1+4+4)=√(9)=3. Then cosθ=(acdotb)/(|a||b|)=-20/(7·3)=-20/21. Hence θ=cos-1(-20/21), which is approximately 162.3^°. The obtuse angle confirms that the vectors point in largely opposite directions.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for the angle between two vectors, always compute the dot product and the magnitudes first; the cosine formula is the most direct route and avoids the pitfalls of using the cross product magnitude, which gives the sine of the angle and requires an extra step to resolve the quadrant.

Cross Product and Vector Triple Product

9 Algebraic Definition of the Cross Product

The cross product (vector product) of a=a1i+a2j+a3k and b=b1i+b2j+b3k is defined as the determinant atimesb=begin{vmatrix}i&j&ka1&a2&a3b1&b2&b3end{vmatrix}, which expands to (a2b3-a3b2)i+(a3b1-a1b3)j+(a1b2-a2b1)k. The result is a vector perpendicular to both a and b. The cross product is anti‑commutative: atimesb=-(btimesa), and it distributes over addition but is not associative.

10 Geometric Meaning and Right‑Hand Rule

The magnitude of the cross product equals the area of the parallelogram formed by a and b: |atimesb|=|a||b|sinθ, where θ is the angle between them. The direction follows the right‑hand rule: if the fingers of the right hand curl from a to b through the smaller angle, the extended thumb points in the direction of atimesb. This geometric interpretation makes the cross product essential for computing torques, angular momentum, and surface normals in three‑dimensional graphics.

11 Scalar Triple Product and Volume of Parallelepiped

The scalar triple product of three vectors a,b,c is defined as a·(btimesc). Its absolute value gives the volume of the parallelepiped whose adjacent edges are the three vectors. The scalar triple product is invariant under cyclic permutations: a·(btimesc)=b·(ctimesa)=c·(atimesb). If the scalar triple product is zero, the three vectors are coplanar, which provides a quick test for linear dependence in three dimensions.

12 Vector Triple Product Identity

The vector triple product a×(btimesc) can be expanded using the identity a×(btimesc)=(acdotc)b-(acdotb)c. This identity, often remembered as “BAC minus CAB”, converts a double cross product into a linear combination of the original vectors and is extremely useful in simplifying expressions that arise in rigid‑body dynamics and electromagnetic theory. The identity also shows that the vector triple product lies in the plane spanned by b and c.

Lines in Three‑Dimensional Space

13 Vector and Parametric Equations of a Line

A line in space can be described by a point r0 through which it passes and a direction vector d. The vector equation is r=r0+lambdad, where λ is a real parameter. In component form this yields the parametric equations x=x0+λ d1, y=y0+λ d2, z=z0+λ d3. The parameter λ uniquely identifies each point on the line, and varying λ from -∞ to +∞ traces the entire line. This representation is especially convenient for finding intersections with other geometric objects because it reduces the problem to solving for the parameter.

14 Symmetric Form and Direction Ratios

Eliminating the parameter λ from the parametric equations gives the symmetric (or Cartesian) form (x-x0)/d1=(y-y0)/d2=(z-z0)/d3, provided none of the direction components d_i is zero. The numbers d1,d2,d3 are called direction ratios; any non‑zero scalar multiple of them represents the same line. Direction cosines are obtained by normalising the direction ratios. The symmetric form is useful for quickly checking whether a given point lies on the line and for computing the angle between two lines using their direction ratios.

15 Angle Between Two Lines

If two lines have direction vectors d1 and d2, the acute angle θ between them satisfies cosθ=|d1cdotd2|/(|d1||d2|). The absolute value ensures the angle is taken as the smaller of the two supplementary angles formed by the lines. When the lines are given in symmetric form, the direction ratios can be read directly and substituted into the formula. This concept is frequently tested in problems that ask whether two lines are perpendicular (dot product zero) or parallel (direction ratios proportional).

16 Shortest Distance Between Two Skew Lines

Two lines that are neither parallel nor intersecting are called skew lines. The shortest distance between them is the length of the common perpendicular segment. If the lines are r=a1+lambdab1 and r=a2+mub2, the distance d is given by d=|(a2-a1)·(b1timesb2)|/|b1timesb2|. The numerator is the absolute value of the scalar triple product of the vector joining a point on the first line to a point on the second line with the two direction vectors; the denominator is the magnitude of the cross product of the direction vectors, which equals the area of the parallelogram they span. This formula is derived by projecting the joining vector onto the unit vector perpendicular to both lines.

Planes in Three‑Dimensional Space

17 Vector and Cartesian Equations of a Plane

A plane can be defined by a point r0 on it and a normal vector n perpendicular to the plane. The vector equation is (r-r0)cdotn=0, which expands to the Cartesian form a(x-x0)+b(y-y0)+c(z-z0)=0 or ax+by+cz+d=0 where n=ai+bj+ck and d=-(ax0+by0+cz0). Alternatively, a plane can be described by three non‑collinear points or by a point and two direction vectors lying in the plane, leading to the parametric form r=r0+lambdau+muv. The Cartesian equation is most convenient for distance calculations and for determining the relative position of points and lines.

18 Normal Vector and Distance from a Point to a Plane

The coefficients a,b,c in the Cartesian equation ax+by+cz+d=0 are the components of a normal vector n. The perpendicular distance from a point P(x1,y1,z1) to the plane is D=|ax1+by1+cz1+d|/√(a2+b2+c2). This formula follows from projecting the vector from any point on the plane to P onto the unit normal vector. The sign of the numerator indicates on which side of the plane the point lies, a fact used in determining whether two points are on the same side of a plane.

19 Angle Between Two Planes and Line‑Plane Intersection

The angle between two planes is defined as the acute angle between their normal vectors. If the planes are a1x+b1y+c1z+d1=0 and a2x+b2y+c2z+d2=0, then cosθ=|a1a2+b1b2+c1c2|/(√(a12+b12+c12)√(a22+b22+c22)). A line intersects a plane unless its direction vector is orthogonal to the plane’s normal (i.e., the line is parallel to the plane). Substituting the parametric equations of the line into the plane equation yields a linear equation in the parameter; solving it gives the intersection point, while a contradiction indicates the line is parallel and either lies in the plane (infinitely many solutions) or is disjoint (no solution).

20 Shortest Distance Between Parallel Planes and Between a Line and a Plane

Two parallel planes have proportional normal vectors; their equations can be written as ax+by+cz+d1=0 and ax+by+cz+d2=0. The distance between them is |d2-d1|/√(a2+b2+c2), obtained by measuring the distance from any point on one plane to the other. For a line r=a+lambdab and a plane rcdotn=d, if the line is not parallel to the plane the intersection point gives zero distance. If the line is parallel (i.e., bcdotn=0), the distance equals the perpendicular distance from any point on the line to the plane, computed with the point‑to‑plane formula. These results complete the toolkit for solving all standard distance problems in three‑dimensional geometry.

Mastery of these concepts enables a student to translate geometric situations into algebraic equations, manipulate them using vector identities, and interpret the results back into spatial understanding, which is the essence of three‑dimensional analytic geometry.

Family of Planes Through the Intersection of Two Planes

When two non‑parallel planes π₁: a₁x + b₁y + c₁z + d₁ = 0 and π₂: a₂x + b₂y + c₂z + d₂ = 0 intersect, their intersection is a straight line. Every plane that contains this line can be represented by a linear combination of the two plane equations. The family of planes through the line of intersection is given by π₁ + λ π₂ = 0, where λ is a real parameter. Expanding, (a₁ + λ a₂)x + (b₁ + λ b₂)y + (c₁ + λ c₂)z + (d₁ + λ d₂) = 0. For each value of λ we obtain a distinct plane sharing the same line of intersection; the only exception is λ = –1, which yields the plane (a₁ – a₂)x + (b₁ – b₂)y + (c₁ – c₂)z + (d₁ – d₂) = 0, still passing through the line because the normal vector becomes n₁ – n₂, which is orthogonal to the direction vector of the intersection line (n₁ × n₂).

To find a particular plane of the family that satisfies an additional condition — for instance, passing through a given point P(x₀, y₀, z₀) — substitute the coordinates of P into the family equation and solve for λ. The resulting λ gives the required plane. If the condition is that the plane be perpendicular to a given vector v = (p, q, r), then the normal of the family plane n(λ) = (a₁ + λ a₂, b₁ + λ b₂, c₁ + λ c₂) must be parallel to v, i.e. n(λ) = k v for some scalar k. This yields two independent ratios that determine λ uniquely (provided v is not orthogonal to the intersection line). The family representation is also useful for finding the plane that bisects the angle between π₁ and π₂: the bisectors correspond to λ = ±√(a₁² + b₁² + c₁²) / √(a₂² + b₂² + c₂²) after normalising the equations.

A common pitfall is to treat the family equation as valid for all λ including the value that makes the coefficient of x, y, z all zero simultaneously; that would happen only if π₁ and π₂ are proportional, i.e. the same plane, in which case the intersection is not a line but the entire plane. In the standard situation of two distinct intersecting planes, the family covers every plane through their line exactly once. The parameter λ can be eliminated to obtain the Cartesian equation of the line of intersection itself by solving the two plane equations simultaneously, but the family form is often more convenient for problems that ask for a plane through the intersection with an extra geometric constraint.

Condition for Coplanarity of Two Lines and Their Intersection

Two lines in space, given in vector form as r = a₁ + λ b₁ and r = a₂ + μ b₂, are coplanar if and only if the scalar triple product of the vector joining a point on the first line to a point on the second line with the two direction vectors vanishes: (a₂ – a₁) · (b₁ × b₂) = 0. This condition is both necessary and sufficient. If the lines are coplanar, they either intersect at a unique point or are parallel (including the case of coincident lines). Parallelism is detected by b₁ × b₂ = 0; in that case the coplanarity condition reduces to (a₂ – a₁) being parallel to b₁, which means the lines lie on the same line or on distinct parallel lines within a plane.

When the lines are known to be coplanar and non‑parallel, their intersection point can be found by solving the vector equation a₁ + λ b₁ = a₂ + μ b₂ for the scalars λ and μ. This yields three scalar equations in two unknowns; because the lines intersect, the system is consistent and any two independent equations suffice. A compact vector formula for the intersection point uses the vector triple product: the position vector of the intersection is a₁ + λ b₁ where λ = [(a₂ – a₁) × b₂] · (b₁ × b₂) / |b₁ × b₂|². The denominator is the squared area of the parallelogram spanned by b₁ and b₂, and the numerator is the scalar triple product of (a₂ – a₁), b₂, and (b₁ × b₂). An equivalent symmetric expression can be written by swapping the roles of the two lines.

If the lines are skew, the scalar triple product is non‑zero and its absolute value divided by |b₁ × b₂| gives the shortest distance between them, as covered in the earlier section. The coplanarity test is therefore a quick diagnostic: compute (a₂ – a₁) · (b₁ × b₂). If the result is zero, proceed to find the intersection; if non‑zero, the lines are skew and the distance formula applies. In coordinate geometry, the same test appears as the vanishing of a 3×3 determinant formed by the direction ratios of the two lines and the direction ratios of the line joining a point on each line.

Worked Example: Coplanarity and Intersection
Determine whether the lines L₁: r = (1, 2, 3) + λ (2, –1, 1) and L₂: r = (4, 0, 1) + μ (1, 2, –1) are coplanar. If they are, find their point of intersection.
Compute a₂ – a₁ = (4–1, 0–2, 1–3) = (3, –2, –2). Compute b₁ × b₂ = (2, –1, 1) × (1, 2, –1) = determinant |i j k; 2 –1 1; 1 2 –1| = i( (–1)(–1) – (1)(2) ) – j( (2)(–1) – (1)(1) ) + k( (2)(2) – (–1)(1) ) = i(1 – 2) – j(–2 – 1) + k(4 + 1) = (–1, 3, 5). Now (a₂ – a₁) · (b₁ × b₂) = (3, –2, –2) · (–1, 3, 5) = –3 –6 –10 = –19 ≠ 0. Since the scalar triple product is not zero, the lines are skew, not coplanar. Therefore they do not intersect. The shortest distance between them is |–19| / |(–1, 3, 5)| = 19 / √(1+9+25) = 19 / √35.
Exam Strategy
When a problem asks for the intersection of two lines, first compute the scalar triple product (a₂ – a₁) · (b₁ × b₂). If it is non‑zero, the lines are skew and there is no intersection — state this clearly and, if required, give the shortest distance. Only when the triple product is zero should you solve for λ and μ. This two‑step check saves time and avoids algebraic errors on inconsistent systems.

Reflection of a Point and a Line in a Plane

The reflection (mirror image) of a point P(x₁, y₁, z₁) across a plane π: ax + by + cz + d = 0 is obtained by dropping a perpendicular from P to π, finding the foot of the perpendicular F, and then extending the segment PF by the same length beyond the plane. The foot F has coordinates (x₁ – a·k, y₁ – b·k, z₁ – c·k) where k = (ax₁ + by₁ + cz₁ + d) / (a² + b² + c²). The reflected point P′ is then P′ = P – 2k (a, b, c) = (x₁ – 2ak, y₁ – 2bk, z₁ – 2ck). This formula works for any plane, including those not passing through the origin. The sign of k indicates which side of the plane P lies on; the reflection automatically places P′ on the opposite side at the same perpendicular distance.

To reflect a line L: r = a + λ b in the plane π, there are two equivalent approaches. One method is to reflect two distinct points on L (for instance, the point corresponding to λ = 0 and λ = 1) using the point‑reflection formula, then write the line through the two reflected points. The direction vector of the reflected line is the reflection of the original direction vector b. Since reflection in a plane is a linear isometry that reverses the component of a vector normal to the plane while leaving the tangential component unchanged, the reflected direction vector b′ = b – 2 (b·n̂) n̂, where n̂ = (a, b, c) / √(a² + b² + c²) is the unit normal of π. In Cartesian terms, if b = (l, m, n) and the plane normal is (a, b, c), then b′ = (l, m, n) – 2 (al + bm + cn) / (a² + b² + c²) · (a, b, c). The reflected line passes through the reflected image of any point on the original line, so its vector equation is r = a′ + λ b′ where a′ is the reflected position vector of a.

An important special case occurs when the line is parallel to the plane (b·n = 0). Then b′ = b, so the direction is unchanged, and the reflected line is simply the original line shifted by twice the perpendicular distance from the line to the plane. If the line lies in the plane, every point reflects to itself and the line is invariant. These properties are frequently tested in problems that ask for the image of a line in a plane or for the equation of a plane that is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining a point and its reflection.

Volume of a Tetrahedron and Scalar Triple Product Applications

The volume of a tetrahedron with vertices A, B, C, D can be expressed elegantly using the scalar triple product. Let vectors AB = b – a, AC = c – a, AD = d – a, where a, b, c, d are the position vectors of the vertices. The volume V of the parallelepiped spanned by AB, AC, AD is |(AB × AC) · AD|. Since a tetrahedron occupies exactly one sixth of that parallelepiped, V_tetra = |(AB × AC) · AD| / 6. This formula is coordinate‑free and works in any dimension where the cross product is defined (in three dimensions it is the standard scalar triple product). In Cartesian coordinates, if A = (x₁, y₁, z₁), B = (x₂, y₂, z₂), C = (x₃, y₃, z₃), D = (x₄, y₄, z₄), then V = |det( x₂–x₁, y₂–y₁, z₂–z₁; x₃–x₁, y₃–y₁, z₃–z₁; x₄–x₁, y₄–y₁, z₄–z₁ )| / 6.

The scalar triple product also provides a quick test for coplanarity of four points: the points are coplanar iff the volume of the tetrahedron they form is zero, i.e. (AB × AC) · AD = 0. This is equivalent to the determinant of the 3×3 matrix of coordinate differences being zero. In vector form, four points with position vectors a, b, c, d are coplanar iff (b – a) · ((c – a) × (d – a)) = 0. This condition is often used in problems that ask whether a given point lies in the plane determined by three other points.

Beyond volume, the scalar triple product appears in the formula for the distance from a point to a plane (already covered) and in the expression for the shortest distance between two skew lines. It also gives the volume of a parallelepiped formed by three vectors, which is the absolute value of the scalar triple product. A useful identity is the cyclic permutation property: (u × v) · w = (v × w) · u = (w × u) · v. This allows one to choose the most convenient order of vectors when computing determinants. In examinations, the scalar triple product is frequently hidden inside a determinant evaluation; recognising it can reduce a 3×3 determinant to a simple scalar triple product computation.

Vector Equation of a Plane Through Three Non‑Collinear Points (Determinant Form)

Given three non‑collinear points A(x₁, y₁, z₁), B(x₂, y₂, z₂), C(x₃, y₃, z₃), the plane passing through them can be written in determinant form as | x – x₁, y – y₁, z – z₁; x₂ – x₁, y₂ – y₁, z₂ – z₁; x₃ – x₁, y₃ – y₁, z₃ – z₁ | = 0. This determinant expresses the condition that the vector from A to a variable point P(x, y, z) lies in the span of the two direction vectors AB and AC, i.e. the three vectors are coplanar. Expanding the determinant yields the Cartesian equation ax + by + cz + d = 0 where (a, b, c) = AB × AC is a normal vector to the plane. The constant term d = –(a x₁ + b y₁ + c z₁). This form is particularly useful when the coordinates of the three points are given numerically, because one can compute the determinant directly without first finding the normal vector explicitly.

The determinant form also generalises to the parametric vector equation r = a + λ (b – a) + μ (c – a), where a, b, c are the position vectors of A, B, C. The parameters λ, μ ∈ ℝ generate all points in the plane. The equivalence between the parametric form and the determinant form follows from the fact that the three vectors (r – a), (b – a), (c – a) are linearly dependent, which is exactly the vanishing of their scalar triple product. In practice, the determinant form is often faster for deriving the Cartesian equation, while the parametric form is more convenient for finding intersections with lines or for describing the plane in vector calculations.

A common examination question provides three points and asks for the equation of the plane in both vector and Cartesian forms. The recommended workflow: compute two direction vectors, take their cross product to obtain the normal, then write the Cartesian equation using the point‑normal form. As a check, substitute the coordinates of the three given points into the final equation; all three must satisfy it. If the points are collinear, the cross product will be the zero vector and the determinant will vanish identically, signalling that no unique plane exists. This collinearity test is another application of the scalar triple product: (b – a) × (c – a) = 0 iff the points are collinear.

  • Confusing the family of planes parameter λ with the parameter in the line of intersection; λ = –1 does not give a plane parallel to the intersection line.
  • Forgetting to check the scalar triple product before solving for intersection of two lines; attempting to solve an inconsistent system wastes time.
  • Using the point‑reflection formula with the wrong sign for k; the reflected point is P – 2k n, not P + 2k n.
  • Assuming the reflected direction vector of a line is simply the negative of the original; only the normal component changes sign.
  • Misapplying the tetrahedron volume formula by omitting the factor 1/6 or by using the wrong order of vectors in the scalar triple product.
  • Treating the determinant form of a plane as valid for collinear points; the determinant vanishes identically and yields no plane equation.
  • Neglecting to normalise the plane normal when computing the reflected direction vector; the formula b′ = b – 2 (b·n̂) n̂ requires a unit normal.

Equation of a Plane Through a Given Line and a Point Not on the Line

A line in space can be expressed in vector form as r = a + λ b, where a is the position vector of a point on the line and b is its direction vector. Let a point P with position vector p be given such that P does not lie on the line. The plane that contains the line and the point P is uniquely determined because the line supplies one direction vector b and the vector AP = p – a (where A is the point on the line corresponding to λ = 0) supplies a second, non‑parallel direction vector. Since P is not on the line, AP is not a scalar multiple of b; hence b and AP are linearly independent and span the plane.

The vector equation of the required plane is therefore r = a + λ b + μ (p – a), with λ, μ ∈ ℝ. This parametric form makes it clear that every point in the plane can be reached by moving from A along the line direction b and then along the direction from A to P. To obtain the Cartesian equation, compute a normal vector n = b × (p – a). The plane consists of all points r satisfying (r – a) · n = 0. Expanding gives n · r = n · a, which is the standard point‑normal form. If the line is given in symmetric form (x – x₁)/l = (y – y₁)/m = (z – z₁)/n and the point is P(x₀, y₀, z₀), then a = (x₁, y₁, z₁), b = (l, m, n), and p – a = (x₀ – x₁, y₀ – y₁, z₀ – z₁). The cross product b × (p – a) yields the normal components directly.

An important check: if the given point happens to lie on the line, then p – a becomes parallel to b, the cross product vanishes, and no unique plane exists — infinitely many planes contain the line. In examinations, this collinearity test is often embedded in a problem that asks for the equation of a plane through a line and a point; the first step should always be to verify that the point is not on the line by checking whether (p – a) × b = 0. If it is zero, the data are insufficient to define a single plane.

Applications of this construction appear in problems involving the reflection of a line in a plane, the determination of a plane that contains a given line and is parallel to another line, and the computation of the volume of a tetrahedron where one face is known to contain a specific line. The parametric form r = a + λ b + μ (p – a) is also convenient for finding the intersection of this plane with another plane or line, because one can substitute the parametric coordinates into the other object’s equation and solve for λ and μ.

Equation of a Plane Through the Intersection of Two Planes and Parallel to a Given Line

Let two planes be given in Cartesian form as π₁: a₁x + b₁y + c₁z + d₁ = 0 and π₂: a₂x + b₂y + c₂z + d₂ = 0. Their line of intersection L has direction vector d = n₁ × n₂, where n₁ = (a₁, b₁, c₁) and n₂ = (a₂, b₂, c₂) are the normals of the planes. Any plane passing through this line belongs to the family π₁ + λ π₂ = 0, i.e. (a₁ + λ a₂)x + (b₁ + λ b₂)y + (c₁ + λ c₂)z + (d₁ + λ d₂) = 0, with λ ∈ ℝ. The normal of a member of this family is n(λ) = n₁ + λ n₂.

Now suppose we need the particular plane from this family that is parallel to a given line with direction vector v. A plane is parallel to a line iff its normal is perpendicular to the line’s direction, i.e. n(λ) · v = 0. Substituting n(λ) gives (n₁ + λ n₂) · v = 0, which is a linear equation in λ: n₁·v + λ (n₂·v) = 0. Provided n₂·v ≠ 0, we obtain a unique λ = – (n₁·v) / (n₂·v). If n₂·v = 0 but n₁·v = 0, then every plane in the family is parallel to the line (the line is parallel to both π₁ and π₂, hence parallel to their intersection). If n₂·v = 0 and n₁·v ≠ 0, no plane of the family is parallel to the line — the data are inconsistent.

Once λ is found, the required plane equation is π₁ + λ π₂ = 0. In vector form, the plane can be written as r · n(λ) = – (d₁ + λ d₂). This method avoids finding an explicit point on the intersection line; it works directly with the normals and the given direction vector. A common variant asks for the plane through the intersection of two planes and perpendicular to a third plane. In that case the condition is n(λ) · n₃ = 0, where n₃ is the normal of the third plane, leading to the same linear equation for λ.

Examination questions often combine this with a request to find the distance of the resulting plane from a given point, or to verify that a given point lies on the plane. The family‑of‑planes parameter λ must not be confused with the parameter used in the parametric equation of the intersection line; they are independent. A useful shortcut: if the two given planes are written in vector form as r · n₁ = p₁ and r · n₂ = p₂, the family is r · (n₁ + λ n₂) = p₁ + λ p₂. The parallelism condition (n₁ + λ n₂) · v = 0 yields λ instantly.

Worked Example: Plane through intersection parallel to a line
Find the equation of the plane passing through the intersection of the planes 2x – y + 3z – 4 = 0 and x + 2y – z + 1 = 0 and parallel to the line (x – 1)/2 = (y + 3)/–1 = (z – 2)/4.
The normals are n₁ = (2, –1, 3) and n₂ = (1, 2, –1). The direction vector of the given line is v = (2, –1, 4). The family of planes through the intersection is π₁ + λ π₂ = 0, with normal n(λ) = n₁ + λ n₂ = (2+λ, –1+2λ, 3–λ). Parallelism requires n(λ)·v = 0. Compute (2+λ)·2 + (–1+2λ)·(–1) + (3–λ)·4 = 0 → 4+2λ + 1–2λ + 12–4λ = 0 → 17 – 4λ = 0 → λ = 17/4. Substitute λ into the family: (2x – y + 3z – 4) + (17/4)(x + 2y – z + 1) = 0. Multiply by 4: 8x – 4y + 12z – 16 + 17x + 34y – 17z + 17 = 0 → 25x + 30y – 5z + 1 = 0. This is the required plane.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for a plane through the intersection of two planes with an extra condition (parallel to a line, perpendicular to a plane, passing through a point), always write the family π₁ + λ π₂ = 0 first. The condition translates into a single linear equation in λ. Solve for λ, then substitute back. This avoids the longer route of finding a point on the intersection line and then imposing the condition. Remember that λ = –1 gives the second plane itself, not a plane parallel to the intersection line.

Equation of a Line Through a Point and Perpendicular to Two Given Lines

Given two lines in space with direction vectors b₁ and b₂, a line that is perpendicular to both must have its direction vector parallel to the cross product b₁ × b₂ (provided b₁ and b₂ are not parallel). If a point P with position vector p is given, the required line has vector equation r = p + λ (b₁ × b₂). This construction is the three‑dimensional analogue of finding a line perpendicular to two given lines in a plane, where the direction would be the normal to the plane containing them.

The cross product b₁ × b₂ yields a vector orthogonal to both b₁ and b₂. If the two given lines are skew, the line through P with this direction is the unique line through P that is perpendicular to both. If the given lines intersect, the line through P perpendicular to both is the normal to the plane containing them, provided P lies in that plane; otherwise it is simply a line through P orthogonal to the plane of the two lines. In either case, the direction vector is b₁ × b₂ (or any non‑zero scalar multiple).

To express the line in Cartesian (symmetric) form, compute the components of d = b₁ × b₂ = (d₁, d₂, d₃). If none of the components is zero, the symmetric equations are (x – x₀)/d₁ = (y – y₀)/d₂ = (z – z₀)/d₃, where P(x₀, y₀, z₀). If one component is zero, the corresponding coordinate is constant and the equality is written with the remaining two ratios. This line is often used as the common perpendicular to two skew lines; the shortest distance between the skew lines is measured along this direction.

A frequent examination twist: the two given lines are presented in symmetric form, and the point P is the intersection of one of them with a given plane. The student must first find the intersection point, then compute the cross product of the direction vectors. Another variant asks for the vector equation of the line through a given point that is perpendicular to a given plane and also perpendicular to a given line — this reduces to the cross product of the plane’s normal and the line’s direction vector.

Shortest Distance Between Two Parallel Lines in Vector Form

Two parallel lines have direction vectors that are scalar multiples of each other. Let the lines be L₁: r = a₁ + λ b and L₂: r = a₂ + μ b, where b is a common direction vector (non‑zero). The shortest distance between them is the length of the component of the vector a₂ – a₁ perpendicular to b. This is given by | (a₂ – a₁) × b | / |b|. The numerator is the magnitude of the cross product, which equals the area of the parallelogram formed by a₂ – a₁ and b; dividing by the base length |b| gives the perpendicular height — the required distance.

If the lines are given in Cartesian form, first extract a point on each line and the direction ratios. For example, L₁: (x – x₁)/l = (y – y₁)/m = (z – z₁)/n and L₂: (x – x₂)/l = (y – y₂)/m = (z – z₂)/n (same direction ratios because they are parallel). Then a₁ = (x₁, y₁, z₁), a₂ = (x₂, y₂, z₂), b = (l, m, n). Compute a₂ – a₁ = (x₂ – x₁, y₂ – y₁, z₂ – z₁), then the cross product with b, its magnitude, and divide by √(l² + m² + n²).

An alternative formula uses the projection: distance = | (a₂ – a₁) – ((a₂ – a₁)·b̂) b̂ |, where b̂ = b/|b| is the unit direction vector. This is the magnitude of the rejection of a₂ – a₁ from b. Both formulas are equivalent; the cross‑product version is often faster because it avoids normalising b.

A common pitfall is to apply the skew‑line distance formula | (a₂ – a₁)·(b₁ × b₂) | / |b₁ × b₂| to parallel lines. For parallel lines, b₁ × b₂ = 0, making the denominator zero and the formula invalid. Always check whether the direction vectors are parallel before choosing the formula. In examinations, a problem may give two lines in vector form and ask for the shortest distance; the first step is to test if b₁ × b₂ = 0. If yes, use the parallel‑line formula; if not, use the skew‑line formula.

Condition for a Line to Lie in a Plane and Its Vector Verification

A line L: r = a + λ b lies completely in a plane π: r · n = d (or Cartesian ax + by + cz + d = 0) if and only if two conditions hold: (1) the direction vector b is perpendicular to the plane’s normal n, i.e. b · n = 0; (2) at least one point of the line satisfies the plane equation, i.e. a · n = d. The first condition ensures the line is parallel to the plane (or lies in it); the second ensures the line is not merely parallel but actually embedded in the plane.

In vector form, if the plane is given as r = p + μ u + ν v (parametric with two direction vectors u, v), the line lies in the plane iff b is a linear combination of u and v (so b is in the plane’s direction space) and the point a can be expressed as p + α u + β v for some scalars α, β. Equivalently, the three vectors b, u, v must be coplanar (scalar triple product zero) and a – p must also be coplanar with u, v.

When the plane is given in Cartesian form Ax + By + Cz + D = 0 and the line in symmetric form (x – x₁)/l = (y – y₁)/m = (z – z₁)/n, the conditions become Al + Bm + Cn = 0 and Ax₁ + By₁ + Cz₁ + D = 0. The first is the orthogonality of direction ratios to the normal; the second is the point‑on‑plane check. If both hold, the line lies in the plane. If only the first holds, the line is parallel to the plane but does not intersect it (or lies entirely outside). If the first fails, the line intersects the plane at a unique point.

Examination questions often ask: “Find the value of k for which the line (x – 1)/2 = (y + 3)/k = (z – 2)/–1 lies in the plane 2x – y + 3z – 5 = 0.” The solution: set 2·2 + (–1)·k + 3·(–1) = 0 → 4 – k – 3 = 0 → k = 1. Then verify the point (1, –3, 2) satisfies the plane: 2·1 – (–3) + 3·2 – 5 = 2 + 3 + 6 – 5 = 6 ≠ 0. Since the point does not satisfy, no value of k makes the line lie in the plane — the line can be parallel but not embedded. This two‑step verification is essential; many candidates stop after finding k from the direction condition and forget the point condition.

  • Confusing the family‑of‑planes parameter λ with the parameter of the intersection line; λ = –1 gives the second plane, not a plane parallel to the intersection.
  • Applying the skew‑line distance formula to parallel lines, leading to division by zero.
  • Forgetting to check the point‑on‑plane condition after satisfying the direction‑normal orthogonality when testing if a line lies in a plane.
  • Using the cross product of direction vectors as the normal for a plane through a line and a point, but omitting the check that the point is not on the line (which would make the cross product zero).
  • Misidentifying the direction vector of the required line in “perpendicular to two lines” problems; the correct direction is the cross product of the two given direction vectors, not their sum or difference.
  • Neglecting to normalise the direction vector when using the projection formula for distance between parallel lines, while the cross‑product formula does not require normalisation.
  • Assuming that a plane through the intersection of two planes and parallel to a line always exists; if the line is parallel to both given planes, every plane of the family is parallel, and if it is parallel to only one, no such plane exists.
The advanced constructions above complete the toolkit for handling three‑dimensional geometry problems that combine lines, planes, and distance conditions in a single question.
