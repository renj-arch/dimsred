🏴‍☠️ Part 1: The Story That Invented Vectors

Story Time: The Pirate Who Needed Direction

Imagine a pirate finds an old treasure map. The map says: "Walk 10 steps."

The pirate walks 10 steps... and falls into a pit! Why? Because "10 steps" tells you how far but NOT where. Was it 10 steps North? East? Into the pit?

Now imagine a BETTER map that says: "Walk 10 steps NORTH." Now the pirate knows both how far AND where. The pirate finds the treasure! 🏆

⚓ The Birth of Vectors
"10 Steps" vs "10 Steps North"
Scalar = Just the number. "10 steps", "5 kg", "30°C". No direction.

Vector = Number + Direction. "10 steps NORTH", "5 km South-East", "3 m/s upward".

Vectors save pirates from pits. They tell you BOTH how much AND which way.
💡 Vector = Magnitude + Direction. A scalar is just a number. A vector is an arrow — it has length AND orientation!
🧭 See the Difference: Scalar vs Vector
Left: Scalar tells you "how far" but NOT "which way" — the poor pirate could go in ANY direction! Right: A Vector tells you BOTH magnitude (length) and direction — the pirate knows EXACTLY where to go.
🌟 First Big Idea
A vector is like an arrow: it has magnitude (length) and direction (where it points).
Scalar = just the number. Vector = number + direction. That's ALL you need to remember.

Welcome to 3D: The Room Corner

In the real world, we live in 3 dimensions. Think of the corner of a room where two walls meet the floor. That's your 3D coordinate system!

🏠 The Room Corner: x, y, z Axes
Think of the corner of a room. The floor is the xy-plane, the left wall is the xz-plane, the right wall is the yz-plane. Any point in the room can be found by its x, y, and z coordinates!
🏹 Part 2: Vectors — "Arrows With Purpose"

Representing Vectors: The Three Magical Arrows

In 3D, any vector can be broken into three components — one along each axis. We use three special unit vectors as our building blocks:

🧙 The Three Magical Arrows: i, j, k
The three magical arrows: î points along x, ĵ points along y, k̂ points along z. Each has length = 1. ANY vector in 3D can be built from these three!

Building Any Vector: a = a₁i + a₂j + a₃k

Think of it like a recipe: you take a₁ spoonfuls of i, a₂ spoonfuls of j, and a₃ spoonfuls of k, and BAM — you have your vector!

📐 See the Components in Action
Start at origin. Walk a₁ steps along x (green), then a₂ steps along y (purple), then a₃ steps along z (amber). The straight-line distance from start to finish is the magnitude: √(a₁² + a₂² + a₃²).
🌟 Second Big Idea
Any vector in 3D = a₁î + a₂ĵ + a₃k̂. The magnitude is just the straight-line distance from tail to tip.
Like the hypotenuse of a 3D right triangle: |a| = √(a₁² + a₂² + a₃²).
🧮 Why Magnitude = √(a₁² + a₂² + a₃²)? ▶

It's just the 3D version of the Pythagorean theorem! In 2D, the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides a₁ and a₂ is √(a₁² + a₂²). In 3D, you add the third dimension:

Step 1: First, the projection on the xy-plane has length √(a₁² + a₂²).

Step 2: Now you have a vertical triangle with base √(a₁² + a₂²) and height a₃.

Step 3: The hypotenuse = √(√(a₁² + a₂²)² + a₃²) = √(a₁² + a₂² + a₃²).

That's ALL there is to it! It's the Pythagorean theorem in 3D.

🌓 Part 3: Dot Product — "The Shadow Test"

How Much Do Two Vectors Point Together?

Imagine you have TWO vectors. The dot product answers one simple question: "How much do they point in the SAME direction?"

Picture a flashlight shining straight down onto vector b. The shadow that a casts on b is the dot product!

🔦 The Shadow Test: a·b = |a||b|cosθ
Shine a light straight down onto b. The shadow that a casts on b is |a|cosθ. Multiply by |b| and you get the dot product! It measures "how much of a points in b's direction."

The Two Special Cases

↔️ Perpendicular vs Parallel
Left: If a and b are perpendicular, there's ZERO shadow — they don't point together at all. Dot = 0. Right: If they're parallel, the shadow is FULL — maximum overlap. Dot = |a||b|.
🌟 Third Big Idea
The dot product measures "how much two vectors point together." It's a scalar (just a number).
Perpendicular → dot=0. Parallel → dot=|a||b|. Everything else is somewhere in between.

👶 Part 4: Cross Product — "The Perpendicular Baby"

When Two Vectors Have a Baby

If the dot product asks "how much together?", the cross product asks "how much APART?"

When two vectors a and b are NOT parallel, they form a parallelogram. The cross product gives us:

  • Magnitude: The AREA of that parallelogram = |a||b|sinθ
  • Direction: A NEW vector PERPENDICULAR to both a and b
📐 The Parallelogram: Area = |a×b|
Two vectors always form a parallelogram. The area of that parallelogram = base (|a|) × height (|b|sinθ) = |a||b|sinθ = magnitude of the cross product. The bigger the angle, the bigger the area!

The Right-Hand Rule: Which Way Does the Baby Point?

The cross product a×b gives a NEW vector that's perpendicular to both a and b. But which direction? Use your RIGHT hand:

✋ The Right-Hand Rule
Point your right-hand fingers along vector a, curl them toward b. Your thumb points in the direction of a×b. If a and b are parallel, there's NO area → cross product = 0!
🌟 Fourth Big Idea
The cross product gives a NEW vector perpendicular to both parents. Its length = the area of the parallelogram they form.
a×b = |a||b|sinθ × n̂, where n̂ is the perpendicular direction (right-hand rule).
🎪 Real-Life Analogy
The Door That Opens
Dot product: You push a door. If you push straight (parallel to the door), it doesn't open — dot = 0. If you push perpendicular, it opens fully — dot is maximum.

Cross product: The door's hinge is like a×b. The door swings in a plane, and the hinge is PERPENDICULAR to that plane. The area the door sweeps = the cross product magnitude!
🧠 Dot = "how much together". Cross = "the perpendicular baby". One is a number, the other is a NEW vector.
📐 Part 5: 3D Geometry — "The Extra Dimension"

Same as 2D, Just With One More Spice

Everything you know from 2D geometry works in 3D, just with an extra z-coordinate. Let's see how!

3D Distance: Just Add z!

In 2D, distance between (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂) = √[(Δx)² + (Δy)²]. In 3D: just add (Δz)²!

📍 Point in 3D Space
Point P(3,4,5) in 3D space. Its distance from the origin is just the 3D Pythagorean theorem: √(3² + 4² + 5²) = √50 = 5√2. Same as 2D but with an extra term!

Line in 3D: "For Each Step t, Move (a,b,c)"

A line in 3D is like a set of marching instructions: "For each step t, move a units in x, b in y, and c in z."

🪄 The 3D Line: (x-x₁)/a = (y-y₁)/b = (z-z₁)/c = t
A line in 3D is just a starting point + a direction vector. The parameter t tells you how far along the line you've gone. Like a train on tracks — at each moment t, you're at a specific position!

Plane: A Flat Sheet With a Normal

A plane is like a flat, infinite sheet. The key to understanding a plane is its normal vector — the vector that sticks out PERPENDICULAR to the surface.

🛬 The Plane: ax + by + cz = d
A plane is a flat sheet. Vector n = (a,b,c) is the normal — it sticks straight out from the plane, perfectly perpendicular. The equation ax + by + cz = d tells you which plane it is. If a point is on the plane, substituting its coordinates satisfies the equation!
🌟 Fifth Big Idea
A plane = a flat sheet. Its normal vector sticks out perpendicular. The equation ax + by + cz = d is like a password — only points ON the plane can satisfy it.
A line = a starting point + direction vector × t. Like a train on tracks at time t.
🌍 Real-Life 3D
The World Is 3D!
GPS: Your phone uses 3D coordinates (latitude, longitude, altitude) to find you — that's (x,y,z)!

Airplanes: A flight path is a 3D line: (x-x₁)/a = (y-y₁)/b = (z-z₁)/c tells the plane "for each step forward, go a East, b North, c up."

Construction: A wall is a plane. The normal vector points perpendicular to the wall. Engineers use this to ensure walls are straight!
🧠 3D Geometry isn't abstract. It's the math of how everything in the physical world works!
📐 Part 6: Section Formula & Position Vectors

Dividing a Line in a Given Ratio

If two points A and B have position vectors a and b, any point P on the line AB can be expressed as a weighted combination of them. This is the core idea behind the section formula.

📍 Section Formula: P = (n·a + m·b) / (m+n)
Closer to A Closer to B m:n=1:1
Drag the slider to change ratio m:n. Point P divides AB such that AP:PB = m:n. The position vector of P is a weighted average of a and b: P = (n·a + m·b)/(m+n). When m=n, P is the midpoint: (a+b)/2.

Centroid of a Triangle

The centroid (intersection of medians) of a triangle with vertices at position vectors a, b, c is simply the average of the three:

G = (a + b + c) / 3
🔺 Centroid = Average of Vertices
The centroid is the average of the three vertices. It's where all three medians intersect, and it divides each median in ratio 2:1.

Collinearity Condition

Three points A, B, C with position vectors a, b, c are collinear (lie on the same line) if one vector can be written as a linear combination of the other two. Specifically, there exist scalars λ + μ = 1 such that:

c = λ·a + μ·b, where λ + μ = 1

Equivalently, the vectors b - a and c - a are parallel — their cross product is zero!

🌟 Sixth Big Idea
Position vectors let you turn geometry into algebra. The centroid is just an average. Collinearity means one point is a weighted combination of the other two.
"Use position vectors to simplify any geometry problem — they turn lines into equations!"
🔄 Part 7: Vector Triple Product — a×(b×c)

The "bac-cab" Rule

What happens when you take the cross product of a vector with another cross product? You get the vector triple product: a×(b×c). The result is a vector that lies in the plane of b and c — always!

a × (b × c) = (a·c)b - (a·b)c
🎯 a×(b×c) Lies in the Plane of b and c
b×c is perpendicular to the plane of b and c. Then a×(b×c) swings back into the plane — it's a linear combination of b and c! That's why the formula only uses b and c, not a.
🧠 Memory Trick
"Bac-Cab" = (a·c)b - (a·b)c
bac-cab — say it out loud!

• bac = b × (a·c) — the "b" gets multiplied by (a·c)
• cab = c × (a·b) — the "c" gets multiplied by (a·b)
• Subtract: bac − cab = (a·c)b − (a·b)c

Notice the pattern: the middle vector (b×c or c×a) determines which vector gets + and which gets −.
💡 The result is ALWAYS in the plane of the two vectors INSIDE the parentheses!
🧮 Worked Example: Simplify a×(b×c) + b×(c×a) + c×(a×b) ▶

Step 1: Apply bac-cab to each term:

a×(b×c) = (a·c)b - (a·b)c

b×(c×a) = (b·a)c - (b·c)a

c×(a×b) = (c·b)a - (c·a)b

Step 2: Add all three:

= (a·c)b - (a·b)c + (b·a)c - (b·c)a + (c·b)a - (c·a)b

Step 3: Collect like terms. Notice (a·c)b and -(c·a)b cancel! Similarly the other pairs cancel too.

Answer: a×(b×c) + b×(c×a) + c×(a×b) = 0

This is a famous identity — the sum of cyclic triple products is always zero!

📦 Part 8: Scalar Triple Product — Volume of a Box

a·(b×c) = Volume of Parallelepiped

Take three vectors a, b, c. They form a slanted box called a parallelepiped. The scalar triple product [a b c] = a·(b×c) gives the volume of this box!

[a b c] = a · (b × c) = Volume of parallelepiped
📦 The Parallelepiped: Volume = |a·(b×c)|
The three vectors a, b, c form a slanted box. Base area = |b×c| (parallelogram of b and c). Height = |a|cosθ (how much a sticks up). Volume = base × height = |a·(b×c)|. If the volume is zero, the vectors lie in the same plane!

Coplanarity: When Three Vectors Lie in a Plane

If a, b, c are coplanar (all in the same plane), they can't form a 3D box — the volume is zero!

[a b c] = 0 ⇔ a, b, c are coplanar
⬇️ Coplanar = Zero Volume
If a, b, c are coplanar, the scalar triple product [a b c] = 0. Think of it as a box that got squashed flat — no height, no volume!

Four Points Coplanarity

Four points A, B, C, D are coplanar if the vectors AB, AC, AD are coplanar:

[(b-a) (c-a) (d-a)] = 0
🌟 Seventh Big Idea
The scalar triple product = volume of a box. If it's zero, everything is flat — the vectors are coplanar.
"Think 3D: three vectors either make a box or lie flat. The triple product tells you which!"
📏 Part 9: 3D Lines & Planes — Advanced

Skew Lines, Line-Plane Angles, and More

Shortest Distance Between Two Skew Lines

Two lines in 3D that are neither parallel nor intersecting are called skew lines. They don't meet, but there's a unique shortest distance between them — along a line perpendicular to BOTH.

📏 Shortest Distance Between Skew Lines
Two skew lines L₁ and L₂ — they don't meet, don't run parallel. The shortest distance is the length of the common perpendicular segment connecting them (shown in amber). It's the projection of the vector between any two points (one on each line) onto the direction perpendicular to both lines.

Angle Between a Line and a Plane

The angle between a line and a plane is NOT the angle between the line and the normal! It's the complement — the angle between the line and its projection onto the plane.

sin φ = |(direction vector of line) · (normal vector of plane)| / (|d| · |n|)
🪄 Line Piercing Through a Plane
A line piercing through a plane. The angle φ between the line and the plane is the complement of the angle between the line and the normal vector. If the line is parallel to the normal, it's perpendicular to the plane (φ = 90°). If the line is perpendicular to the normal, it lies in the plane (φ = 0°).

Condition for a Line to Lie in a Plane

A line with direction vector d passing through point A lies in a plane with normal n if and only if:

d · n = 0 AND A lies on the plane

The direction vector must be perpendicular to the normal (parallel to the plane), AND the point must satisfy the plane equation.

🌟 Eighth Big Idea
For any line-plane problem: the normal vector is your best friend. Project onto it for distances, take dot products for angles, use cross products for perpendicular directions.
"When in doubt, find the normal first. Everything else follows from that."
🎯 Part 10: Linear Programming — Optimization with Vectors

Finding the Best Solution in a Feasible Region

Linear programming is about optimizing (maximizing or minimizing) a linear objective function subject to linear constraints. The corner point method says: the optimum always occurs at a vertex of the feasible region!

📊 Feasible Region & Corner Points
The feasible region is the set of points satisfying all constraints. The optimum of the objective function Z always occurs at a corner point (vertex). Sweep the objective line outward — the last corner it touches gives the optimal value!
🧮 Worked Example: Maximize Z = 3x + 4y with Constraints ▶

Constraints:

x + y ≤ 50, 2x + y ≤ 80, x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0

Step 1: Plot the constraints and find the feasible region.

Step 2: Find the corner points:

O(0,0), A(50,0), B(30,20) — intersection of x+y=50 and 2x+y=80, C(0,50)

Wait — let me solve correctly: x+y=50 and 2x+y=80 → subtract: x=30, y=20. So B(30,20).

Step 3: Evaluate Z at each corner:

O: Z=0 | A: Z=150 | B: Z=3(30)+4(20)=170 | C: Z=100

Maximum Z = 170 at B(30,20). Minimum Z = 0 at O(0,0).

Unbounded case: If the region doesn't close, and Z can increase without bound, there's no finite maximum. If the region is empty, there's no solution at all!

🌟 Ninth Big Idea
Linear Programming = optimizing a straight line over a polygon. The answer is ALWAYS at a corner. Just check the vertices!
"When the region is unbounded, the optimum might not exist. When it's empty, there's no solution. Check both!"
💡 Part 11: Problem-Solving Strategies

How to Attack Any Vector & 3D Problem

🧠 Strategy 1
Use Position Vectors to Simplify Geometry
When you see a geometry problem with points, immediately assign position vectors a, b, c, etc. to the points. Now the problem is just vector algebra — no messy coordinate manipulation!

Example: "Prove the medians of a triangle are concurrent." Assign position vectors a, b, c. The centroid G = (a+b+c)/3 lies on each median. Done!
💡 Position vectors = coordinate-free geometry. They make proofs elegant and simple.
🧠 Strategy 2
Dot = Projection, Cross = Area
a·b = "how much of a points in b's direction" = projection of a on b. Use it when you need shadow length, overlap, or perpendicularity (dot=0).

a×b = area of the parallelogram they form. Use it when you need area, a perpendicular vector, or parallelism (cross=0).

Pro tip: Dot product = "togetherness meter". Cross product = "apartness meter".
💡 Dot = scalar (a number). Cross = vector (an arrow). They measure different things — don't mix them up!
🧠 Strategy 3
For Planes, Always Find the Normal First
The normal vector n = (a,b,c) is the key to EVERY plane problem:

• Equation: ax + by + cz = d — the coefficients ARE the normal.
• Distance from point to plane: |ax₁+by₁+cz₁-d| / √(a²+b²+c²)
• Angle with line: sinφ = |d·n| / (|d|·|n|)
• Parallel planes: Same normal, different d.
• Perpendicular planes: Dot product of normals = 0.
💡 The normal vector is a plane's "fingerprint." Find it first, and everything else falls into place.
⚠️ Part 12: Common Mistakes (Don't Fall for These!)

Trap Doors in Vector Problems

❌ Mistake 1
Order Matters in Cross Product!
Wrong: a×b = b×a
Right: a×b = − b×a

The cross product is anti-commutative. Swapping the vectors flips the direction (right-hand rule reverses). This is the #1 cause of sign errors in vector problems!

Memory trick: a before b → thumbs up. b before a → thumbs down.
⚠ Always check the order! a×b ≠ b×a — they're opposites!
❌ Mistake 2
Dot Product → Scalar. Cross Product → Vector.
Wrong: "a·b gives a vector perpendicular to both."
Right: a·b gives a number (scalar). a×b gives a vector.

The names tell you everything:
• Dot product → the dot is a small multiplication → scalar
• Cross product → the cross is a big X → vector
⚠ Dot = number. Cross = vector. Never confuse them again!
❌ Mistake 3
a×b = 0 Doesn't Mean a = 0 or b = 0!
Wrong: "a×b = 0, so either a or b must be zero."
Right: a×b = 0 means a and b are parallel (or one of them is zero).

Remember: |a×b| = |a||b|sinθ. If sinθ = 0, the cross product is zero — even if both vectors are huge! They just happen to be pointing the same (or opposite) way.
⚠ a×b = 0 means they're parallel, not that they're small! Don't jump to conclusions.
❌ Mistake 4
Distance Formula — Don't Forget the Absolute Value!
Wrong: Distance from point (x₁,y₁,z₁) to plane ax+by+cz+d=0 = (ax₁+by₁+cz₁+d)/√(a²+b²+c²)
Right: Distance = |ax₁+by₁+cz₁+d| / √(a²+b²+c²)

The absolute value is crucial! Distance can never be negative. The numerator might be negative (if the point is on the opposite side of the plane), but the distance is always positive.
⚠ Distance is always positive! Put absolute value bars around the numerator.

Vector Triple Product and Its Geometric Interpretation

The vector triple product a × (b × c) appears frequently in mechanics and geometry. Unlike the scalar triple product, it yields a vector that lies in the plane spanned by b and c. The identity a × (b × c) = (a·c)b − (a·b)c (often called the bac‑cab rule) can be derived by expanding both sides in components or by using the properties of the Levi‑Civita symbol. Geometrically, b × c is a vector perpendicular to the plane of b and c; crossing a with that normal returns a vector orthogonal to a but still in the b‑c plane. The coefficients (a·c) and (a·b) are the projections of a onto c and b respectively, so the result is a linear combination of b and c weighted by those projections. This interpretation is crucial when resolving forces in a plane or when finding the component of a vector perpendicular to another vector within a given plane. A common application is the derivation of the formula for the perpendicular distance from a point to a line expressed in vector form: if the line passes through point A with direction d, the distance from point P is |(AP × d)| / |d|, which follows directly from the magnitude of the vector triple product AP × (d × AP).

Scalar Triple Product Identities and Applications

The scalar triple product [a b c] = a·(b × c) equals the signed volume of the parallelepiped formed by the three vectors. It is invariant under cyclic permutations: [a b c] = [b c a] = [c a b], and changes sign under any swap: [a b c] = −[a c b]. These properties allow rapid simplification of expressions involving multiple scalar triple products. For instance, the identity (a × b)·(c × d) = (a·c)(b·d) − (a·d)(b·c) can be proved by writing each cross product as a determinant and using the scalar triple product expansion. Another powerful identity is the vector quadruple product: (a × b) × (c × d) = [a c d]b − [b c d]a, which reduces a product of two cross products to a linear combination of a and b. In coordinate geometry, the condition for four points A, B, C, D to be coplanar is [AB AC AD] = 0. This single scalar equation replaces three separate determinant conditions and is often the quickest way to test coplanarity in contest problems. The scalar triple product also appears in the formula for the volume of a tetrahedron: V = |[AB AC AD]| / 6. When vectors are expressed in components, the scalar triple product becomes the determinant of the 3×3 matrix whose rows (or columns) are the components, linking vector algebra directly to linear algebra.

Worked Example: Volume of a Tetrahedron from Coordinates
Find the volume of the tetrahedron with vertices A(1,2,3), B(4,0,−1), C(−2,5,2), D(3,−1,4).
Step 1: Form edge vectors from A: AB = (3,−2,−4), AC = (−3,3,−1), AD = (2,−3,1). Step 2: Compute the scalar triple product [AB AC AD] = determinant | 3 -2 -4; -3 3 -1; 2 -3 1 |. Expanding: 3*(3*1 - (-1)*(-3)) - (-2)*((-3)*1 - (-1)*2) + (-4)*((-3)*(-3) - 3*2) = 3*(3 - 3) + 2*(-3 + 2) -4*(9 - 6) = 0 + 2*(-1) -4*3 = -2 -12 = -14. Step 3: Volume = |−14| / 6 = 14/6 = 7/3 cubic units.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for a volume or a coplanarity check, immediately write down the relevant vectors and set up the 3×3 determinant. Evaluating a determinant is faster and less error‑prone than expanding cross products component‑wise. Keep a mental checklist: cyclic permutations preserve the value, a single swap flips the sign, and a zero determinant means the vectors are coplanar (volume zero).

Equation of a Plane Through Three Non‑Collinear Points

Given three non‑collinear points A, B, C with position vectors a, b, c, the plane passing through them can be expressed in several equivalent vector forms. The parametric form is r = a + λ(b−a) + μ(c−a), where λ, μ ∈ ℝ. The normal vector is n = (b−a) × (c−a). Using the point‑normal form, the Cartesian equation becomes n·(r−a) = 0, which expands to n·r = n·a. In coordinates, if A(x₁,y₁,z₁), B(x₂,y₂,z₂), C(x₃,y₃,z₃), the determinant form | x−x₁ y−y₁ z−z₁; x₂−x₁ y₂−y₁ z₂−z₁; x₃−x₁ y₃−y₁ z₃−z₁ | = 0 gives the plane equation directly. This determinant is exactly the scalar triple product [(r−a) (b−a) (c−a)] = 0. A useful shortcut: if the plane equation is required in the form ax + by + cz = d, compute n = (b−a) × (c−a) and then d = n·a. The condition that the three points are non‑collinear is precisely n ≠ 0. This method also yields the area of triangle ABC as ½|n|, linking the plane equation to geometric quantities.

Line of Intersection of Two Planes

Two non‑parallel planes π₁: n₁·r = d₁ and π₂: n₂·r = d₂ intersect in a line. The direction vector of this line is d = n₁ × n₂ (non‑zero because the planes are not parallel). To obtain a point on the line, solve the two linear equations together with a third convenient equation, e.g., set one coordinate to zero (provided the line is not parallel to that coordinate plane) and solve the resulting 2×2 system. Alternatively, use the formula for the line in vector form: r = ( (d₁ n₂ − d₂ n₁) × (n₁ × n₂) ) / |n₁ × n₂|² + t (n₁ × n₂). This expression is derived by finding the unique point on the line that is perpendicular to both normals (the foot of the perpendicular from the origin onto the line). In practice, the simplest approach is to write the planes in Cartesian form, eliminate one variable to get a relation between the other two, and then parameterise. For example, from 2x + y − z = 3 and x − y + 2z = 4, adding gives 3x + z = 7 → z = 7 − 3x. Substituting back yields y = 3 − 2x + z = 3 − 2x + 7 − 3x = 10 − 5x. Let x = t, then the line is r = (0,10,7) + t(1,−5,−3). The direction vector (1,−5,−3) is indeed proportional to n₁ × n₂.

Distance Between Parallel Lines and Parallel Planes

For two parallel lines L₁: r = a + λd and L₂: r = b + μd (same direction vector d), the shortest distance is the length of the component of (b−a) perpendicular to d. This equals |(b−a) × d| / |d|. The derivation uses the fact that the cross product magnitude gives the area of the parallelogram formed by (b−a) and d, and dividing by the base |d| yields the height. For parallel planes π₁: n·r = d₁ and π₂: n·r = d₂ (same normal n), the distance is simply |d₁ − d₂| / |n|. This follows from the point‑to‑plane distance formula applied to any point on one plane. A common trap is to forget the absolute value or to use the wrong normal magnitude when the plane equations are not in normalised form. If the planes are given as ax + by + cz + d₁ = 0 and ax + by + cz + d₂ = 0, the distance is |d₁ − d₂| / √(a²+b²+c²). For skew lines, the distance formula generalises to |(a₂−a₁)·(d₁ × d₂)| / |d₁ × d₂|, which reduces to the parallel‑line formula when d₁ × d₂ = 0 (the denominator would be zero, so the formula is not directly applicable). Always check whether lines are parallel before applying the skew‑line formula.

  • Confusing the vector triple product identity with the scalar triple product; remember that a × (b × c) yields a vector, while a·(b × c) yields a scalar.
  • Assuming the scalar triple product changes sign under cyclic permutation; it only changes sign under an odd permutation.
  • Using the determinant form for a plane through three points but forgetting to subtract the coordinates of the reference point, leading to an incorrect constant term.
  • Taking the cross product of normals to find the line of intersection but then failing to find a specific point on the line, leaving the line equation incomplete.
  • Applying the skew‑line distance formula to parallel lines, which makes the denominator zero; always test parallelism first.
  • Omitting the absolute value in the distance between parallel planes, which can give a negative distance.
  • When computing the volume of a tetrahedron, using the scalar triple product of edge vectors that do not share a common vertex, which does not give the correct volume.

Vector Projection, Rejection, and Component Along a Direction

The projection of a vector a onto a non‑zero vector b is the vector component of a that lies along b. It is given by projb a = ((a·b) / |b|²) b. The scalar projection (or component) is the signed length of this projection: compb a = (a·b) / |b|. The rejection (or perpendicular component) is a − projb a and is orthogonal to b. These three quantities satisfy a = projb a + rejb a and projb a·rejb a = 0.

In coordinate form, if a = (a₁,a₂,a₃) and b = (b₁,b₂,b₃), then projb a = ((a₁b₁+a₂b₂+a₃b₃)/(b₁²+b₂²+b₃²)) (b₁,b₂,b₃). The rejection is obtained by subtracting this from a. A frequent application is finding the foot of the perpendicular from a point to a line: given line r = c + λd and point p, the foot is c + projd(p−c). The distance from the point to the line equals |rejd(p−c)| = |(p−c) × d| / |d|.

Projection also appears in the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation process. Starting with a set of linearly independent vectors {v₁, v₂, …}, one constructs an orthogonal set by repeatedly subtracting projections onto previously obtained vectors. In three dimensions this yields an orthonormal basis {e₁, e₂, e₃} where each eᵢ = uᵢ/|uᵢ| and u₁ = v₁, u₂ = v₂ − proju₁ v₂, u₃ = v₃ − proju₁ v₃ − proju₂ v₃. The determinant of the matrix formed by the orthonormal basis vectors is ±1, preserving orientation.

When the direction vector b is a unit vector û, the formulas simplify: projû a = (a·û) û and compû a = a·û. This is the basis for direction cosines: if û = (l,m,n) with l²+m²+n² = 1, then the components of a along the coordinate axes are a₁ = a·i, a₂ = a·j, a₃ = a·k. The same idea extends to any orthonormal basis.

A common trap is to confuse the scalar projection with the vector projection; the former is a signed magnitude, the latter a vector. Another error is forgetting to divide by |b|² when b is not a unit vector. Always verify orthogonality of the rejection by checking the dot product with b equals zero.

Equation of a Plane Through a Point and Parallel to Two Non‑Parallel Vectors

A plane can be uniquely determined by a point a (position vector of a known point on the plane) and two non‑parallel direction vectors b and c that lie in the plane. The vector (parametric) form is r = a + λb + μc, λ,μ ∈ ℝ. The normal vector is n = b × c (non‑zero because b and c are not parallel). The Cartesian (point‑normal) equation is n·(r − a) = 0, i.e. n·r = n·a.

If the point is A(x₁,y₁,z₁) and the direction vectors are b = (b₁,b₂,b₃), c = (c₁,c₂,c₃), then n = (b₂c₃−b₃c₂, b₃c₁−b₁c₃, b₁c₂−b₂c₁). The plane equation becomes n₁(x−x₁) + n₂(y−y₁) + n₃(z−z₁) = 0, or n₁x + n₂y + n₃z = d where d = n₁x₁ + n₂y₁ + n₃z₁. This determinant form is equivalent to the scalar triple product [(r−a) b c] = 0.

The same plane can also be described by a single linear equation in Cartesian coordinates. To convert from parametric to Cartesian, eliminate λ and μ. One systematic method: write the three parametric equations x = x₁ + λb₁ + μc₁, y = y₁ + λb₂ + μc₂, z = z₁ + λb₃ + μc₃. Treat λ, μ as unknowns and solve the 2×2 subsystem for any two coordinates, then substitute into the third. The resulting linear relation is the Cartesian equation.

An important special case occurs when the plane passes through the origin: then a = 0 and the equation reduces to n·r = 0. The plane consists of all vectors orthogonal to n. If the plane is given in the form r = λb + μc, the normal is still b × c and the constant term d = 0.

When the two direction vectors are not given explicitly but the plane is known to be parallel to two lines with direction vectors d₁ and d₂, the same construction applies: n = d₁ × d₂. If the plane also contains a point a, the equation follows immediately. This is the standard way to find the plane containing a line and parallel to another line, or the plane through a point parallel to two given lines.

Worked Example: Plane Through a Point Parallel to Two Vectors
Find the Cartesian equation of the plane that passes through the point A(2,−1,3) and is parallel to the vectors b = (1,2,−1) and c = (3,0,2).
Compute the normal n = b × c = ( (2)(2) − (−1)(0), (−1)(3) − (1)(2), (1)(0) − (2)(3) ) = (4, −5, −6). The plane equation is 4(x−2) −5(y+1) −6(z−3) = 0. Expanding: 4x −8 −5y −5 −6z +18 = 0 → 4x −5y −6z +5 = 0. Check: substitute A: 4·2 −5·(−1) −6·3 +5 = 8+5−18+5 = 0. The equation is 4x −5y −6z +5 = 0.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a plane is described by a point and two direction vectors, always compute the cross product first. Write the normal components as integers if possible; this avoids fractions in the final Cartesian equation. Verify the answer by plugging the given point into the equation — a quick arithmetic check that catches sign errors.

Angle Between Two Planes and the Dihedral Angle

The angle between two planes is defined as the acute angle between their normal vectors. For planes π₁: n₁·r = d₁ and π₂: n₂·r = d₂, the cosine of the angle θ (0 ≤ θ ≤ π/2) is cos θ = |n₁·n₂| / (|n₁| |n₂|). The absolute value ensures the acute angle; if the obtuse angle is required, use cos(π−θ) = −|n₁·n₂| / (|n₁| |n₂|). The dihedral angle is the same geometric quantity, often encountered in solid geometry and crystallography.

If the planes are given in Cartesian form a₁x + b₁y + c₁z + d₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂z + d₂ = 0, then n₁ = (a₁,b₁,c₁), n₂ = (a₂,b₂,c₂) and cos θ = |a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ + c₁c₂| / √(a₁²+b₁²+c₁²) √(a₂²+b₂²+c₂²). Two planes are parallel iff their normals are parallel, i.e. n₁ × n₂ = 0, which is equivalent to a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂ (provided denominators non‑zero). They are perpendicular iff n₁·n₂ = 0, i.e. a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ + c₁c₂ = 0.

The line of intersection of two non‑parallel planes lies in both planes, so its direction vector is d = n₁ × n₂. The angle between the line of intersection and a third plane can be found by first computing the angle between d and the normal of the third plane, then taking the complement. This chain of angles appears in problems involving a line piercing a plane and the projection of that line onto the plane.

A useful identity: if three planes intersect pairwise in three lines, the angles between the lines are related to the angles between the planes. In particular, if the three planes are mutually perpendicular, their normals form an orthogonal triad and the three lines of intersection are also mutually perpendicular. This property underlies the construction of a rectangular coordinate system from three mutually perpendicular planes.

When the planes are expressed in vector form π₁: r = a + λb + μc and π₂: r = p + αq + βr, first extract normals n₁ = b × c, n₂ = q × r, then apply the cosine formula. The constants a, p do not affect the angle.

Shortest Distance from a Point to a Line in 3D

Given a line L: r = a + λd (with direction vector d ≠ 0) and a point P with position vector p, the shortest distance from P to L is the length of the perpendicular dropped from P onto L. The foot of the perpendicular is the point Q on L such that PQ ⟂ d. In vector terms, Q = a + λ₀d where λ₀ = ((p−a)·d) / |d|². The distance is |p − a − λ₀d| = |(p−a) × d| / |d|.

Derivation: the vector from A to P is AP = p−a. Decompose AP into components parallel and perpendicular to d. The parallel component is projd AP = (((p−a)·d)/|d|²) d. The perpendicular component is AP − projd AP. Its magnitude is the required distance. Using the identity |u × v| = |u| |v| sin φ, where φ is the angle between u and v, we obtain the compact formula.

If the line is given in Cartesian symmetric form (x−x₁)/l = (y−y₁)/m = (z−z₁)/n, then a = (x₁,y₁,z₁) and d = (l,m,n). The distance formula becomes √[ ((y−y₁)n − (z−z₁)m)² + ((z−z₁)l − (x−x₁)n)² + ((x−x₁)m − (y−y₁)l)² ] / √(l²+m²+n²). This is the magnitude of the cross product of AP and d divided by |d|.

A related problem is the distance from a point to a line segment. If the perpendicular foot falls outside the segment, the shortest distance is the minimum of the distances to the two endpoints. This requires checking whether λ₀ lies between 0 and 1 (for a segment parameterised from 0 to 1). In many optimisation problems, this distinction changes the answer.

When the line is expressed as the intersection of two planes, first find a point on the line and its direction vector (cross product of the plane normals), then apply the point‑to‑line distance formula. This two‑step process is a common pattern in multi‑part questions.

Vector Form of a Sphere and Its Intersection with a Plane

A sphere with centre C (position vector c) and radius R is the set of points r satisfying |r − c| = R, or equivalently (r − c)·(r − c) = R². Expanding gives r·r − 2c·r + c·c − R² = 0. In Cartesian coordinates (x,y,z) with centre (x₀,y₀,z₀), this is (x−x₀)² + (y−y₀)² + (z−z₀)² = R².

The intersection of a sphere and a plane is either empty, a single point (tangent plane), or a circle. Let the plane be n·r = d with |n| = 1 (unit normal). The distance from the centre to the plane is δ = |n·c − d|. If δ > R, no intersection; if δ = R, the plane is tangent and the intersection is the single point c + (d − n·c)n; if δ < R, the intersection is a circle of radius r = √(R² − δ²) whose centre is the foot of the perpendicular from C onto the plane: c′ = c + (d − n·c)n.

To obtain the vector equation of the intersection circle, choose two orthonormal vectors u, v in the plane (both orthogonal to n and to each other). Then the circle is r = c′ + r (cos θ u + sin θ v), θ ∈ [0,2π). The vectors u, v can be constructed by taking any vector not parallel to n, say w, and setting u = (w − (w·n)n) / |w − (w·n)n|, v = n × u.

The intersection of two spheres is also a circle (or a point, or empty). Subtracting the two sphere equations eliminates the quadratic terms and yields a plane equation — the radical plane. The circle lies in this plane and its centre is on the line joining the two sphere centres. This principle is used in trilateration and GPS positioning.

A common examination question asks for the radius of the circle of intersection given the sphere and plane equations. The steps: (1) find centre and radius of sphere; (2) compute distance δ from centre to plane; (3) apply r = √(R² − δ²). Ensure the plane normal is normalised before using the distance formula, or use the general distance formula |n·c − d| / |n|.

  • Confusing the acute angle between planes with the obtuse angle; always read whether the question asks for the acute or the dihedral angle.
  • Forgetting to take the absolute value in the cosine formula for the angle between planes, which can yield an obtuse angle when the acute one is required.
  • Using the non‑unit normal in the point‑to‑plane distance formula without dividing by its magnitude, leading to an incorrect distance.
  • Assuming the foot of the perpendicular from a point to a line always lies on the given line segment; check the parameter λ₀ against the segment bounds.
  • Neglecting to normalise the plane normal when computing the radius of a sphere‑plane intersection circle, causing the distance δ to be scaled incorrectly.
  • Mixing up the vector triple product identity a × (b × c) = (a·c)b − (a·b)c with the scalar triple product; the former yields a vector, the latter a scalar.
  • Treating the cross product of two direction vectors as the normal of a plane without verifying that the vectors are not parallel; a zero cross product indicates parallel vectors and no unique plane.

Reciprocal Vectors and Their Applications

Given three non‑coplanar vectors a, b, c, their reciprocal vectors a′, b′, c′ are defined by the relations a·a′ = b·b′ = c·c′ = 1 and a·b′ = a·c′ = b·a′ = b·c′ = c·a′ = c·b′ = 0. In compact form, if the matrix M = [a b c] has columns a, b, c, then the matrix of reciprocal vectors is M⁻ᵀ. Explicitly, a′ = (b × c) / [a b c], b′ = (c × a) / [a b c], c′ = (a × b) / [a b c], where [a b c] = a·(b × c) is the scalar triple product (volume of the parallelepiped). Reciprocal vectors are indispensable in crystallography: the primitive translation vectors of a direct lattice generate a reciprocal lattice whose vectors satisfy the orthogonality conditions above. In solid‑state physics, the diffraction condition k′ − k = G (where G is a reciprocal‑lattice vector) follows directly from the definition. In vector algebra, any vector v can be expanded in the non‑orthogonal basis {a, b, c} as v = (v·a′) a + (v·b′) b + (v·c′) c. This expansion avoids solving a linear system; the coefficients are simply the dot products with the reciprocal basis. The reciprocal basis also provides a clean proof of the vector triple product identity: a × (b × c) = (a·c) b − (a·b) c by writing b × c = [a b c] a′ and using orthogonality. When the three vectors are orthogonal, the reciprocal vectors are just the original vectors scaled by the inverse squared lengths. In the general case, the metric tensor gᵢⱼ = eᵢ·eⱼ and its inverse gⁱʲ give the transformation between covariant and contravariant components; the reciprocal vectors are the contravariant basis vectors. A common examination task is to compute the reciprocal basis for a given set of vectors and then express a fourth vector in that basis. The steps: (1) compute the scalar triple product V = a·(b × c); (2) if V = 0 the vectors are coplanar and no reciprocal basis exists; (3) otherwise form a′ = (b × c)/V, etc.; (4) the coefficients of v are α = v·a′, β = v·b′, γ = v·c′. This technique also simplifies the derivation of the formula for the volume of a tetrahedron with vertices at the origin and at a, b, c: the volume is |V|/6. In mechanics, reciprocal vectors appear when transforming between generalized coordinates and Cartesian coordinates in Lagrangian dynamics.

Vector Differentiation and the Frenet‑Serret Frame

Let r(t) be a smooth vector‑valued function describing a space curve. The derivative r′(t) = dr/dt is the tangent vector (velocity if t is time). Its magnitude v = |r′| is the speed. The unit tangent vector is T = r′/v. Differentiating T with respect to arc length s (where ds/dt = v) gives dT/ds = κ N, where κ = |dT/ds| is the curvature and N is the principal normal (unit vector). The binormal is defined as B = T × N. The three mutually orthogonal unit vectors T, N, B form the Frenet‑Serret frame. Their derivatives with respect to arc length satisfy the Frenet‑Serret formulas: dT/ds = κ N, dN/ds = −κ T + τ B, dB/ds = −τ N, where τ is the torsion. Torsion measures how the curve twists out of the osculating plane spanned by T and N. For a plane curve, τ = 0 and B is constant. The curvature and torsion can be computed directly from the parametric representation without explicit arc‑length parametrisation: κ = |r′ × r″| / |r′|³ and τ = (r′ × r″)·r‴ / |r′ × r″|². These formulas are derived by differentiating T = r′/|r′| and using the triple product identities. A helix r(t) = (a cos t, a sin t, bt) has constant curvature κ = a/(a²+b²) and constant torsion τ = b/(a²+b²). The Frenet‑Serret frame is fundamental in differential geometry and appears in the theory of elastic rods, computer graphics (parallel transport frames), and the motion of charged particles in magnetic fields (where the Lorentz force causes the velocity vector to rotate about the field direction). In examinations, a typical question provides a vector function and asks for the curvature and torsion at a given parameter value, or to verify that a given curve is a general helix (constant ratio τ/κ). The computation requires careful differentiation and simplification using vector identities such as (u × v)·w = u·(v × w). Another common task is to find the osculating plane at a point: its equation is (R − r)·B = 0, where R is the position vector of a generic point on the plane. The rectifying plane is (R − r)·N = 0 and the normal plane is (R − r)·T = 0. Understanding the geometric meaning of each plane helps in visualising the curve’s local behaviour.

Gradient, Divergence, and Curl in Cartesian Coordinates

For a scalar field φ(x, y, z), the gradient is the vector field ∇φ = (∂φ/∂x, ∂φ/∂y, ∂φ/∂z). It points in the direction of maximum rate of increase of φ, and its magnitude equals that maximum rate. For a vector field F = (F₁, F₂, F₃), the divergence is the scalar field ∇·F = ∂F₁/∂x + ∂F₂/∂y + ∂F₃/∂z. It measures the net outward flux per unit volume at a point; a positive divergence indicates a source, negative a sink. The curl is the vector field ∇×F = (∂F₃/∂y − ∂F₂/∂z, ∂F₁/∂z − ∂F₃/∂x, ∂F₂/∂x − ∂F₁/∂y). It represents the infinitesimal rotation of the field; its direction is the axis of rotation (right‑hand rule) and its magnitude is twice the angular velocity of a small paddle wheel placed in the flow. Two fundamental identities hold for sufficiently smooth fields: ∇×(∇φ) = 0 (the curl of a gradient vanishes) and ∇·(∇×F) = 0 (the divergence of a curl vanishes). These imply that any irrotational field (curl zero) is locally a gradient of some scalar potential, and any solenoidal field (divergence zero) is locally the curl of a vector potential. The Laplacian of a scalar field is ∇²φ = ∇·(∇φ) = ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y² + ∂²φ/∂z². For a vector field, the vector Laplacian is defined component‑wise: ∇²F = (∇²F₁, ∇²F₂, ∇²F₃). An important identity is ∇×(∇×F) = ∇(∇·F) − ∇²F, which is used in deriving the wave equation for electromagnetic fields. In Cartesian coordinates, the del operator ∇ = (∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z) behaves formally like a vector, allowing mnemonic determinant forms for curl. However, in curvilinear coordinates the unit vectors themselves vary, and the expressions acquire scale factors. Examination questions often ask to verify whether a given vector field is conservative (curl zero) or solenoidal (divergence zero), and if conservative, to find the potential function by integrating the components. Another common task is to compute the flux of a field through a closed surface using the divergence theorem: ∬_S F·n dS = ∭_V (∇·F) dV. Similarly, Stokes’ theorem relates circulation to curl: ∮_C F·dr = ∬_S (∇×F)·n dS. Mastery of these operators and theorems is essential for problems in electromagnetism, fluid dynamics, and heat conduction.

Family of Planes Through the Line of Intersection of Two Planes

Given two non‑parallel planes π₁: n₁·r = d₁ and π₂: n₂·r = d₂, their line of intersection L consists of all points satisfying both equations. Any plane containing L can be written as a linear combination π(λ): (n₁ + λ n₂)·r = d₁ + λ d₂, where λ ∈ ℝ is a parameter. This is the equation of the family of planes through L. The plane corresponding to λ = 0 is π₁; as λ → ∞, the plane approaches π₂ (more precisely, dividing by λ gives π₂). The normal vector of π(λ) is n(λ) = n₁ + λ n₂, which varies linearly with λ. The direction vector of the line L is d = n₁ × n₂, and indeed n(λ)·d = (n₁ + λ n₂)·(n₁ × n₂) = 0 for all λ, confirming that every plane in the family contains L. A frequent problem asks for the particular plane in this family that satisfies an extra condition, such as passing through a given point P, being perpendicular to a given vector v, or making a given angle with another plane. To find the plane through P, substitute the coordinates of P into the family equation and solve for λ. To find the plane perpendicular to v, impose n(λ)·v = 0 and solve for λ. To find the plane that makes an angle θ with a plane π₃: n₃·r = d₃, use the formula |cos θ| = |n(λ)·n₃| / (|n(λ)| |n₃|) and solve the resulting equation for λ (usually a quadratic). The family representation also simplifies the calculation of the distance from a point to the line L: the distance equals the minimum over λ of the distance from the point to the plane π(λ), which occurs when the plane’s normal is perpendicular to the vector from the point to the line. In vector form, if the planes are given as r·n₁ = d₁ and r·n₂ = d₂, the family is r·(n₁ + λ n₂) = d₁ + λ d₂. This formulation is coordinate‑free and works directly with position vectors. When the two planes are given in Cartesian form A₁x + B₁y + C₁z + D₁ = 0 and A₂x + B₂y + C₂z + D₂ = 0, the family is (A₁+λA₂)x + (B₁+λB₂)y + (C₁+λC₂)z + (D₁+λD₂) = 0. This is the standard “λ‑method” taught in analytic geometry. A subtle point: if the two planes are parallel (n₁ ∥ n₂), they do not intersect in a line, and the family degenerates to a set of parallel planes; the parameter λ then simply scales the normal. In examinations, always verify that the given planes are non‑parallel before applying the family formula.

Vector Form of a Right Circular Cylinder and Its Intersection with a Plane

A right circular cylinder is the set of points at a fixed perpendicular distance R (the radius) from a given line (the axis). Let the axis pass through point A with position vector a and have direction unit vector û (|û| = 1). For any point P with position vector r, the vector from A to P is AP = r − a. The perpendicular distance from P to the axis is the magnitude of the component of AP orthogonal to û: |AP − (AP·û) û| = |(r − a) × û| (since |û| = 1). The cylinder equation is therefore |(r − a) × û| = R. Squaring gives |(r − a) × û|² = R². Using the identity |u × v|² = |u|²|v|² − (u·v)² with v = û, we obtain |r − a|² − ((r − a)·û)² = R². This is a quadratic vector equation representing the cylindrical surface. The intersection of this cylinder with a plane π: n·r = d (where n is a unit normal) is generally an ellipse, a circle, a pair of parallel lines, or empty. To analyse the intersection, substitute the plane equation into the cylinder equation. A convenient method: choose an orthonormal basis {û, v, w} where v, w span the plane perpendicular to û. In this basis, the cylinder equation becomes y² + z² = R² (with coordinates along v, w). The plane equation becomes a linear relation among the coordinates. Eliminating one variable yields a quadratic in the remaining two, which can be identified as a conic section. The type of conic depends on the angle between the plane normal n and the cylinder axis û. If n·û = 0 (plane perpendicular to axis), the intersection is a circle of radius R. If 0 < |n·û| < 1, the intersection is an ellipse with semi‑minor axis R and semi‑major axis R / √(1 − (n·û)²). If |n·û| = 1 (plane parallel to axis), the intersection is either two parallel lines (if the plane distance from axis is less than R), a single line (tangent, distance equals R), or empty (distance greater than R). The distance from the axis to the plane is δ = |n·a − d| (since n is unit). For the parallel‑axis case, the condition for two lines is δ < R. In many optimisation problems, one needs the shortest distance from a point to the cylinder surface. This distance is || (r − a) × û | − R|. The foot of the perpendicular lies on the line through the point parallel to the vector (r − a) − ((r − a)·û) û. These formulas are frequently used in computer‑aided design and in problems involving the volume of intersection between a cylinder and a prism.

  • Confusing the reciprocal basis with the dual basis in non‑Euclidean metrics; the reciprocal vectors defined here assume the standard dot product.
  • Forgetting that the Frenet‑Serret formulas require the curve to be parametrised by arc length; using the parameter t directly without the factor ds/dt leads to incorrect curvature and torsion.
  • Misapplying the curl determinant in Cartesian coordinates by mixing up the order of partial derivatives; the correct cyclic order is (∂F₃/∂y − ∂F₂/∂z, ∂F₁/∂z − ∂F₃/∂x, ∂F₂/∂x − ∂F₁/∂y).
  • Assuming the family of planes through the intersection line always contains the plane at infinity; the parameter λ covers all finite planes, but the second original plane corresponds to the limit λ → ∞, not a finite λ.
  • Neglecting to normalise the cylinder axis direction vector û before using the distance formula |(r−a)×û| = R; if û is not unit, the cross product magnitude is scaled by |û|.
  • Treating the intersection of a cylinder and a plane as always an ellipse; when the plane is parallel to the axis the intersection degenerates to lines or is empty, a common trap in multiple‑choice questions.
  • Always verify the non‑parallel condition of the two given planes before writing the family equation, otherwise the line of intersection does not exist and the λ‑method fails.

Vector Form of a Cone and Its Intersection with a Plane

A right circular cone is the set of points whose position vectors r satisfy a constant angle α between the axis direction unit vector û and the generator vector r − a, where a is the vertex position vector. The condition is (r − a)·û = |r − a| cos α. Squaring and using |û| = 1 gives ((r − a)·û)² = |r − a|² cos² α. Rearranging yields |r − a|² − ((r − a)·û)² = |r − a|² sin² α. The left side is the squared magnitude of the component of r − a orthogonal to û, i.e. |(r − a) × û|². Hence the cone equation is |(r − a) × û| = |r − a| sin α. For a given half‑angle α, this is a quadratic vector equation representing a double‑napped cone. When a plane π: n·r = d (with unit normal n) cuts the cone, the intersection curve is a conic section. Substitute r from the plane equation into the cone equation, or eliminate one coordinate by choosing an orthonormal basis {û, v, w} where v, w span the plane orthogonal to û. In that basis the cone becomes y² + z² = (x tan α)² (with x along û). The plane becomes a linear relation among x, y, z. Eliminating one variable produces a quadratic in the remaining two, which can be identified as an ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola depending on the angle between n and û. If n·û = cos α the plane is parallel to a generator and the section is a parabola. If |n·û| < cos α the section is an ellipse (circle when n·û = 0). If |n·û| > cos α the section is a hyperbola. The degenerate cases (plane through the vertex) give a point, a line, or a pair of intersecting lines. These results are standard in analytic geometry and appear frequently in optimisation problems involving conical surfaces.

The distance from a point P to the cone surface can be expressed as the absolute difference between the perpendicular distance to the axis and the radius of the cross‑section at that axial coordinate. Let the axial coordinate be t = (r − a)·û. The cross‑section radius at t is |t| tan α. The perpendicular distance from P to the axis is |(r − a) × û|. The shortest distance to the cone is ||(r − a) × û| − |t| tan α|. The foot of the perpendicular lies on the generator through the projection of P onto the axis. This formula is useful in computer‑aided design for tool‑path generation on conical parts.

Vector Form of a Paraboloid and Its Intersection with a Plane

An elliptic paraboloid with vertex at a, axis along unit vector û, and focal parameter p > 0 consists of points r satisfying (r − a)·û = (1/(4p)) |(r − a) × û|². The derivation uses the definition: the distance to the focus equals the distance to the directrix plane. In vector form the focus is at a + p û and the directrix plane is (r − a)·û = −p. Equating distances and simplifying yields the quadratic equation above. A hyperbolic paraboloid (saddle) can be written as (r − a)·û = (1/(2p)) ((r − a)·v)² − (1/(2q)) ((r − a)·w)² where {û, v, w} is an orthonormal basis and p, q > 0. The intersection of a paraboloid with a plane n·r = d is again a conic. Choose the basis aligned with the paraboloid axis. For the elliptic paraboloid the equation becomes x = (y² + z²)/(4p) (with x along û). Substituting the plane equation gives a quadratic in y, z. The discriminant determines the type: if the plane is not parallel to the axis, the section is an ellipse (or circle when the plane is perpendicular to the axis). If the plane is parallel to the axis, the section is a parabola. For the hyperbolic paraboloid the intersection can be a hyperbola, a pair of intersecting lines, or a parabola depending on the orientation of the cutting plane relative to the asymptotic directions v and w. These classifications are obtained by examining the sign of the quadratic form after elimination.

The tangent plane to the elliptic paraboloid at a point r₀ is given by (r − r₀)·û = (1/(2p)) ((r₀ − a) × û)·((r − r₀) × û). This follows from the gradient of the implicit function F(r) = (r − a)·û − (1/(4p))|(r − a) × û|². The normal vector is ∇F = û − (1/(2p)) ((r − a) − ((r − a)·û) û). The curvature properties (principal curvatures, Gaussian curvature) can be derived from the second fundamental form. For the elliptic paraboloid the Gaussian curvature is positive everywhere and equals 1/(4p² (1 + (ρ/(2p))²)²) where ρ is the distance from the axis. These formulas are standard in differential geometry of surfaces and appear in problems on reflection properties of parabolic mirrors.

Worked Example: Intersection of a Cone and a Plane
Find the type of conic obtained by intersecting the cone with vertex at the origin, axis along the z‑axis, half‑angle α = 30°, with the plane x + y + z = 1.
The cone equation is |r × k| = |r| sin 30° = |r|/2, where k = (0,0,1). In coordinates r = (x,y,z) this gives √(x² + y²) = (1/2)√(x² + y² + z²). Squaring: x² + y² = (1/4)(x² + y² + z²) → 3(x² + y²) = z². The plane is z = 1 − x − y. Substitute: 3(x² + y²) = (1 − x − y)² = 1 + x² + y² + 2xy − 2x − 2y. Rearranging: 2x² + 2y² − 2xy + 2x + 2y − 1 = 0. The quadratic form matrix for (x,y) is [[2, -1],[-1,2]] with eigenvalues 1 and 3, both positive. Hence the conic is an ellipse. Because the plane is not parallel to the axis (k·n = 1/√3 ≠ cos 30°), the section is indeed an ellipse.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for the intersection type of a quadric and a plane, first write the quadric in a coordinate system aligned with its symmetry axis. Then substitute the plane equation to obtain a quadratic in two variables. Compute the discriminant (or eigenvalues of the 2×2 quadratic form matrix) to classify the conic without fully solving for the curve. This avoids lengthy algebra and reduces arithmetic errors.

Vector Form of an Ellipsoid and Its Principal Axes

An ellipsoid centred at c with semi‑axes lengths a, b, c along orthonormal directions u₁, u₂, u₃ is the set of points r satisfying ((r − c)·u₁)²/a² + ((r − c)·u₂)²/b² + ((r − c)·u₃)²/c² = 1. In matrix notation let U be the 3×3 orthogonal matrix whose columns are u₁, u₂, u₃ and let D = diag(1/a², 1/b², 1/c²). Then the equation is (r − c)ᵀ U D Uᵀ (r − c) = 1. The symmetric positive‑definite matrix A = U D Uᵀ encodes the shape and orientation. The principal axes are the eigenvectors of A, and the squared semi‑axis lengths are the reciprocals of the eigenvalues. Given a general quadratic equation rᵀ A r + 2 bᵀ r + c = 0 with A symmetric, the centre (if it exists) is at r₀ = −A⁻¹ b provided A is invertible. Translating to the centre eliminates the linear term, yielding (r − r₀)ᵀ A (r − r₀) = r₀ᵀ A r₀ − c. The right‑hand side must be positive for a real ellipsoid. Diagonalising A by an orthogonal transformation gives the canonical form. The volume of the ellipsoid is (4/3)π abc = (4/3)π / √(det A) multiplied by the factor from the constant term. The surface area has no elementary closed form but can be expressed via elliptic integrals. The intersection of an ellipsoid with a plane is always an ellipse (or a point or empty). To find its semi‑axes, project the ellipsoid onto the plane and solve the resulting 2×2 eigenvalue problem. This technique is used in statistics for confidence regions of multivariate normal distributions.

The tangent plane at a point r₀ on the ellipsoid is (r − r₀)ᵀ A (r₀ − c) = 0. The outward unit normal is proportional to A (r₀ − c). The curvature tensor can be derived from the second fundamental form; the principal curvatures at r₀ are the eigenvalues of the shape operator. For an ellipsoid the Gaussian curvature is positive everywhere and varies with position. These differential‑geometric quantities are essential in problems involving geodesics on ellipsoids, such as the calculation of shortest paths on the Earth’s reference ellipsoid.

Vector Form of a Quadric Surface Classification via Invariants

A general quadric surface in three dimensions is given by the homogeneous quadratic equation in homogeneous coordinates X = (x, y, z, 1)ᵀ: Xᵀ Q X = 0, where Q is a 4×4 symmetric matrix. The upper‑left 3×3 submatrix A corresponds to the quadratic terms, the next three entries form the vector b for the linear terms, and the bottom‑right entry is the constant c. Classification of the quadric (ellipsoid, hyperboloid of one sheet, hyperboloid of two sheets, elliptic paraboloid, hyperbolic paraboloid, cone, cylinder, or degenerate cases) can be performed using the ranks and signatures of A and the full matrix Q, together with the determinant of Q. The classical invariants are: Δ = det Q, δ = det A, and the eigenvalues of A. If δ ≠ 0 the quadric is a central quadric (ellipsoid, hyperboloids, cone). The sign of δ and the signature of A distinguish them: all eigenvalues same sign → ellipsoid (or imaginary ellipsoid if Δ/δ > 0); two positive one negative → hyperboloid of one sheet; one positive two negative → hyperboloid of two sheets; one eigenvalue zero → cone. If δ = 0 but rank A = 2 the quadric is a paraboloid (elliptic if the non‑zero eigenvalues have same sign, hyperbolic if opposite). If rank A = 1 the quadric is a cylinder (elliptic, hyperbolic, or parabolic). Degenerate cases occur when Δ = 0, giving pairs of planes, a single plane, a line, or a point. These invariants are coordinate‑free and provide a systematic way to identify the surface without completing squares. In examinations, computing the eigenvalues of a 3×3 matrix is often faster than rotating axes, especially when the matrix has a simple structure.

The affine classification (ignoring metric properties) uses only the rank and signature of A and the rank of Q. The projective classification further identifies quadrics up to projective transformations; all non‑degenerate quadrics with the same signature are projectively equivalent. The dual quadric is represented by the adjugate matrix Q* (or Q⁻¹ when invertible). The tangent plane at a point X₀ on the quadric is Q X₀ (in homogeneous coordinates). The polar plane of an external point Y is Q* Y. These projective concepts are useful in computer vision for camera calibration and in geometric modelling for surface intersection algorithms.

Vector Form of a Ruled Surface and Its Developable Condition

A ruled surface is a surface that can be generated by moving a straight line (the generator) along a curve (the directrix). In vector form, let the directrix be r(u) and the generator direction be v(u) (a unit vector). The surface parametrisation is R(u, v) = r(u) + v v(u), where v is a real parameter along the generator. The tangent plane at a point is spanned by R_u = r'(u) + v v'(u) and R_v = v(u). The surface is developable (i.e. can be flattened onto a plane without stretching) iff the Gaussian curvature is identically zero. For a ruled surface this is equivalent to the condition that the tangent plane is constant along each generator, which translates to the scalar triple product (r'(u), v(u), v'(u)) = 0 for all u. In other words, the vectors r'(u), v(u), v'(u) are coplanar. This condition characterises three classical types of developable ruled surfaces: cylinders (v'(u) = 0), cones (r(u) = a constant vertex), and tangent developables (v(u) = r'(u)/|r'(u)|, i.e. the generators are tangents to a space curve). For a general ruled surface the distribution parameter (or striction line) measures the deviation from developability. The line of striction is the curve on the surface where the distance between neighbouring generators is minimal; its position vector is r_s(u) = r(u) − ((r'(u)·v'(u))/(|v'(u)|²)) v(u) when v'(u) ≠ 0. The Gaussian curvature at a point is K = − (λ²) / (|R_u × R_v|²) where λ is the distribution parameter. These formulas are standard in differential geometry of surfaces and appear in problems on sheet‑metal design and architectural geometry.

The intersection of a ruled surface with a plane is generally a curve that can be analysed by substituting the plane equation into the parametrisation. For a developable surface the intersection curve is a planar curve whose curvature can be expressed in terms of the generator direction and the directrix curvature. In particular, the intersection of a tangent developable with a plane not containing the edge of regression yields a curve with a cusp when the plane is tangent to the surface along a generator. These properties are exploited in the design of developable surfaces for ship hulls and aircraft skins.

  • Forgetting to normalise the axis direction vector before applying the cone or cylinder distance formulas; an unnormalised vector scales the cross product magnitude incorrectly.
  • Misidentifying the conic type by using the sign of the discriminant of the 2×2 quadratic form without checking whether the plane passes through the vertex (which yields degenerate cases).
  • Assuming every quadric with δ = 0 is a paraboloid; rank A = 1 gives a cylinder, which has a different canonical form.
  • Neglecting the condition (r'(u), v(u), v'(u)) = 0 for developability; a ruled surface with non‑zero distribution parameter has non‑zero Gaussian curvature and cannot be flattened.

  • Using the volume formula for an ellipsoid (4/3)π abc without adjusting for the constant term after translating to the centre; the right‑hand side of the centred equation must equal 1 for the formula to apply directly.
