1. Foundations of Differentiation

The derivative of a function captures the instantaneous rate of change of the function’s output with respect to its input, and it is the cornerstone of differential calculus. When a function is differentiable at a point, the limit of the difference quotient exists and equals the slope of the tangent line to the graph at that point. This limit definition not only provides a rigorous foundation but also connects the algebraic notion of a rate of change with the geometric picture of a line that just touches the curve.

1.1 The Concept of a Derivative

In elementary terms, if y = f(x) then the derivative f′(x) measures how much y changes for an infinitesimal change in x. The notation f′(x) or dy/dx emphasises that the derivative itself is a function that assigns to each x the slope of the tangent line, provided the limit exists. Understanding this concept is essential because every subsequent rule of differentiation is derived from the limit definition, and many applications in physics, economics, and engineering rely on interpreting the derivative as a rate.

1.2 Limit Definition and Geometric Meaning

The formal definition states that f′(a) = limh→0 (f(a+h) – f(a))/h, assuming the limit exists. Geometrically, the quotient (f(a+h) – f(a))/h is the slope of the secant line through (a, f(a)) and (a+h, f(a+h)). As h approaches zero, the secant line rotates about the fixed point and approaches the tangent line, so the derivative equals the slope of that tangent. This picture explains why differentiability implies continuity: if the limit of the difference quotient exists, the function cannot have a jump or a cusp at that point.

1.3 Differentiability and Continuity

A function that is differentiable at a point is necessarily continuous there, but the converse is false; a classic counterexample is the absolute value function at x = 0, which is continuous yet not differentiable because the left‑hand and right‑hand limits of the difference quotient differ. Recognising points where a function fails to be differentiable—corners, cusps, vertical tangents, or discontinuities—is a key skill when analysing the behaviour of a function and when applying theorems such as the Mean Value Theorem.

2. Rules of Differentiation

Once the limit definition is understood, a collection of algebraic rules allows us to differentiate a vast array of functions without repeatedly evaluating limits. These rules are proved from the definition and they interact in predictable ways, so mastering them is a prerequisite for solving any problem that involves rates of change, optimisation, or curve sketching.

2.1 The Power Rule and Constant Multiple Rule

For any real exponent n, the derivative of xⁿ is n·xⁿ⁻¹. This rule follows directly from the binomial expansion of (x+h)ⁿ and the limit process. A constant factor can be pulled out of the derivative: d/dx [c·f(x)] = c·f′(x). Together, these two rules enable the differentiation of any polynomial term by term.

2.2 The Sum and Difference Rules

The derivative of a sum or difference of functions equals the sum or difference of their derivatives: d/dx [f(x) ± g(x)] = f′(x) ± g′(x). This linearity property reflects the fact that the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, and it allows us to break down complicated expressions into simpler pieces that can be handled individually.

2.3 The Product Rule

When two functions are multiplied, the derivative is not simply the product of the derivatives. Instead, d/dx [f(x)·g(x)] = f′(x)·g(x) + f(x)·g′(x). The rule can be remembered as “first times derivative of second plus second times derivative of first.” It is proved by adding and subtracting f(x+h)·g(x) in the numerator of the difference quotient and then taking limits.

2.4 The Quotient Rule

For a quotient f(x)/g(x) with g(x) ≠ 0, the derivative is (f′(x)·g(x) – f(x)·g′(x))/[g(x)]². This formula can be derived by applying the product rule to f(x)·[g(x)]⁻¹ and using the chain rule for the reciprocal. A common mistake is to forget the square in the denominator or to reverse the order of subtraction in the numerator.

2.5 The Chain Rule

If y = f(u) and u = g(x), then dy/dx = (dy/du)·(du/dx). In words, differentiate the outer function evaluated at the inner function, then multiply by the derivative of the inner function. The chain rule is indispensable for differentiating composite functions such as sin(x²) or e3x+1, and it extends naturally to multiple layers of composition.

Example: Differentiating a Composite Rational Function
Find the derivative of h(x) = (3x² + 2)⁴ / (5x – 1)³.
Apply the quotient rule with u(x) = (3x² + 2)⁴ and v(x) = (5x – 1)³. First compute u′(x) using the chain rule: u′(x) = 4(3x² + 2)³·(6x) = 24x(3x² + 2)³. Next compute v′(x) = 3(5x – 1)²·5 = 15(5x – 1)². Then h′(x) = [u′(x)v(x) – u(x)v′(x)] / [v(x)]² = [24x(3x² + 2)³·(5x – 1)³ – (3x² + 2)⁴·15(5x – 1)²] / (5x – 1)⁶. Factor common terms: (3x² + 2)³(5x – 1)²[24x(5x – 1) – 15(3x² + 2)] / (5x – 1)⁶. Simplify the bracket: 120x² – 24x – 45x² – 30 = 75x² – 24x – 30. The final derivative is h′(x) = (3x² + 2)³(75x² – 24x – 30) / (5x – 1)⁴.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem asks for the derivative of a quotient of two composite functions, always differentiate the numerator and denominator separately using the chain rule before substituting into the quotient formula; this reduces algebraic errors and saves time.

3. Tangents and Normals

The derivative at a point gives the slope of the tangent line, and the equation of that line can be written in point‑slope form. The normal line is perpendicular to the tangent, so its slope is the negative reciprocal of the tangent’s slope, provided the tangent is not horizontal or vertical. These lines are fundamental in curve sketching, in solving optimisation problems with geometric constraints, and in physics when analysing motion along a curved path.

3.1 Equation of a Tangent Line

If f is differentiable at x = a, the tangent line has equation y – f(a) = f′(a)(x – a). This follows directly from the definition of slope and the point (a, f(a)). In practice, one first computes f′(x), evaluates it at the given x‑coordinate, and then substitutes into the point‑slope form. When the derivative is zero, the tangent is horizontal; when the derivative does not exist but the function is continuous, the tangent may be vertical, in which case the equation is x = a.

3.2 Equation of a Normal Line

The normal line at (a, f(a)) has slope –1/f′(a) provided f′(a) ≠ 0. Its equation is y – f(a) = –(1/f′(a))(x – a). If the tangent is horizontal (f′(a) = 0), the normal is vertical and its equation is x = a. Conversely, if the tangent is vertical (derivative undefined but function continuous), the normal is horizontal with equation y = f(a). Recognising these special cases prevents division‑by‑zero errors.

3.3 Applications to Curve Sketching

Tangents and normals help identify the behaviour of a curve near a point. For instance, the tangent line approximates the function locally, which is the basis of linearisation and Newton’s method for root finding. Normals are used in problems involving reflection, such as determining the path of a light ray striking a curved mirror, where the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection measured relative to the normal.

4. Monotonicity and the First Derivative Test

The sign of the first derivative on an interval tells us whether the function is increasing or decreasing there. This information is crucial for locating local extrema, understanding the shape of the graph, and solving optimisation problems without resorting to higher‑order derivatives.

4.1 Increasing and Decreasing Functions

A function f is increasing on an interval I if for any x₁ < x₂ in I we have f(x₁) ≤ f(x₂); it is strictly increasing if the inequality is strict. If f′(x) > 0 for all x in I (except possibly at isolated points where the derivative is zero), then f is strictly increasing on I. Conversely, f′(x) < 0 on I implies f is strictly decreasing. The proof uses the Mean Value Theorem, which guarantees the existence of a point where the derivative equals the average rate of change.

4.2 Critical Points and Sign Charts

Critical points occur where f′(x) = 0 or where f′(x) does not exist but f is continuous. These points partition the domain into subintervals on which the sign of f′ is constant. By selecting a test point in each subinterval and evaluating the sign of the derivative, we construct a sign chart that summarises where the function rises or falls. This chart is the primary tool for applying the first derivative test.

4.3 The First Derivative Test for Local Extrema

If f′ changes from positive to negative at a critical point c, then f has a local maximum at c. If f′ changes from negative to positive at c, then f has a local minimum at c. If the sign does not change, c is neither a maximum nor a minimum (it could be an inflection point with horizontal tangent). The test is conclusive whenever the derivative exists on a punctured neighbourhood of c and changes sign, which is the typical situation for polynomial and rational functions.

5. Maxima, Minima and the Second Derivative Test

While the first derivative test relies on sign changes, the second derivative test uses concavity to classify critical points more directly. Concavity also provides insight into the shape of the graph and helps locate points of inflection, which are essential for accurate sketching and for understanding the behaviour of optimisation problems.

5.1 Concavity and the Second Derivative

If f″(x) > 0 on an interval, the graph of f is concave upward (shaped like a cup) on that interval; if f″(x) < 0, the graph is concave downward (shaped like a cap). Points where the concavity changes are called points of inflection. The second derivative is the derivative of the first derivative, so it measures the rate of change of the slope. This interpretation explains why a positive second derivative corresponds to an increasing slope, i.e., the tangent lines rotate counter‑clockwise as x increases.

5.2 The Second Derivative Test for Local Extrema

Suppose c is a critical point with f′(c) = 0 and f″ exists in a neighbourhood of c. If f″(c) > 0, then f has a local minimum at c; if f″(c) < 0, then f has a local maximum at c. If f″(c) = 0, the test gives no information and one must use the first derivative test or higher‑order derivatives. The test is a direct consequence of the Taylor expansion of f around c, where the quadratic term dominates the behaviour near the critical point.

5.3 Global Extrema on Closed Intervals

When a continuous function is defined on a closed interval [a, b], the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees the existence of absolute maximum and minimum values. These global extrema occur either at critical points inside (a, b) or at the endpoints a and b. To find them, evaluate f at all critical points and at the endpoints, then compare the values. This procedure is the standard method for solving constrained optimisation problems in calculus.

Mastering the interplay between derivatives, tangents, monotonicity, and extrema equips a student to analyse any differentiable function systematically and to apply calculus to real‑world optimisation and modelling tasks.
