Abstract Algebra — The Algebra of Symmetry

Abstract algebra studies algebraic structures — sets with operations that obey certain rules. A group captures symmetry. A ring captures addition and multiplication together. A field captures the arithmetic of numbers. And Galois theory uses all three to answer one of the oldest questions in mathematics: which equations can be solved by radicals? The answer — discovered by a 20-year-old Frenchman the night before he died in a duel — is one of the most beautiful and tragic stories in all of science.

🔷 Groups — The Mathematics of Symmetry

What Is a Group?
Four Rules That Capture Every Kind of Symmetry
A group is a set G with an operation • satisfying four axioms: closure (a•b is in G), associativity ((a•b)•c = a•(b•c)), identity (an element e such that e•a = a•e = a), and inverses (for every a, there exists a⁻¹ such that a•a⁻¹ = e). That's it — four rules. Yet from these four rules flows an entire theory that describes the symmetries of snowflakes, the structure of crystals, the possible elementary particles, and the rotations of a Rubik's cube. A group is a universe of symmetry, defined by its multiplication table.
A group is a set with a single operation that satisfies four simple axioms — yet it captures the essence of symmetry everywhere.

Cyclic Groups — The Simplest Kind

A group is cyclic if it's generated by a single element: every element is gⁿ for some n. The integers ℤ are infinite cyclic (generated by 1). The clock group ℤₙ is finite cyclic (generated by 1 mod n). Cyclic groups are the building blocks of all finite abelian groups — the Fundamental Theorem of Finite Abelian Groups says every such group is a product of cyclic groups.

Permutation Groups — Symmetry in Action

The symmetric group Sₙ consists of all permutations of n objects. It has n! elements. Cayley's theorem says every finite group is isomorphic to a subgroup of some Sₙ — meaning every group can be understood as permutations of something. The alternating group Aₙ (even permutations) is the key to the unsolvability of the quintic.

Cayley's Theorem
Every finite group is isomorphic to a subgroup of Sₙ
Every group can be represented as permutations of its own elements.
✨
Lagrange's Theorem — The Size of Subgroups ▶
If H is a subgroup of a finite group G, then |H| divides |G|. This simple counting argument is one of the most powerful in group theory.

Example: A group of order 6 can have subgroups of order 1, 2, 3, or 6 — never 4 or 5. This is why a pentagon's rotational symmetry group (order 5) can't be a subgroup of a hexagon's (order 6) — 5 doesn't divide 6.

The converse is false: not every divisor has a subgroup. The smallest counterexample is A₄ (order 12), which has no subgroup of order 6.

⭕ Rings & Fields — Two Operations Are Better Than One

Adding Multiplication
Rings: Where Addition and Multiplication Live Together
A ring is a set with two operations: addition (forming an abelian group) and multiplication (associative, distributive over addition). The integers ℤ are a ring. Polynomials ℤ[x] form a ring. Matrices form a ring. A field is a ring where multiplication also forms an abelian group (except 0) — meaning every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse. ℚ, ℝ, ℂ are fields. ℤ is not a field (2 has no multiplicative inverse in ℤ). The distinction between rings and fields is the distinction between integer arithmetic and fraction arithmetic — polynomials vs. rational functions.
Rings are like ℤ; fields are like ℚ. The difference is whether you can divide.

Ideals — The Substructures of Rings

An ideal I in a ring R is a subset closed under addition and under multiplication by any element of R. Think of it as a "module of zero" — if you multiply an element of I by anything in R, you stay in I. Ideals are to rings what normal subgroups are to groups: they allow you to form quotient rings R/I.

Example: ℤ/nℤ
The set nℤ = {..., −2n, −n, 0, n, 2n, ...} is an ideal of ℤ. The quotient ℤ/nℤ is the ring of integers mod n. If n is prime, this is actually a field — the finite field 𝔽ₚ. This is why modular arithmetic works especially well modulo a prime: every non-zero residue has a multiplicative inverse.

Polynomial Rings

The ring R[x] of polynomials with coefficients in R is one of the most important constructions in algebra. Over a field, polynomial division works, and every ideal of F[x] is principal (generated by a single polynomial). The irreducible polynomials are like prime numbers — they generate the prime ideals. The study of polynomial roots leads directly to field theory and Galois theory.

🌿 Galois Theory — The Tragedy of the Quintic

A Genius Lost at 20
Évariste Galois and the Unsolvable Equation
For 2000 years, mathematicians sought a formula for the roots of a quintic (5th-degree) polynomial — akin to the quadratic formula for ax² + bx + c = 0. In the 1820s, Niels Henrik Abel proved it was impossible. But Évariste Galois went further: he developed an entire theory to determine exactly which polynomials are solvable by radicals. The night before his fatal duel at age 20, he wrote down his theory in a letter — "I have no time" — founding modern algebra. The key insight: the symmetries of a polynomial's roots form a group, and the polynomial is solvable by radicals iff this group is solvable.
The solvability of an equation is not about the coefficients — it's about the symmetry group of its roots.

Field Extensions — Adjoining Roots

To solve x² − 2 = 0, we need √2. The rationals ℚ don't contain √2, so we create a field extension ℚ(√2) = {a + b√2 : a,b ∈ ℚ}. The degree of the extension — the dimension as a vector space over ℚ — is 2. The degree tells you how many new numbers you need to adjoin.

Degree of an Extension
[ℚ(√d) : ℚ] = 2
The extension degree is the dimension as a vector space over the base field.

Galois Groups — The Symmetries of Roots

The Galois group Gal(L/K) is the group of field automorphisms of L that fix K. Each automorphism permutes the roots of a polynomial. For x² − 2 = 0, the Galois group has two elements: one that sends √2 to itself (identity) and one that sends √2 to −√2. This group is ℤ₂ — cyclic of order 2.

✨
The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory ▶
There is a one-to-one correspondence between:
— Intermediate fields K ⊆ M ⊆ L
— Subgroups of the Galois group Gal(L/K)

Larger subfields correspond to smaller subgroups, and vice versa. This correspondence turns problems about fields (hard) into problems about groups (easier).

A polynomial is solvable by radicals iff its Galois group is a solvable group — a group that can be broken down into a chain of abelian subgroups. The symmetric group S₅ is not solvable. Therefore, the general quintic is not solvable by radicals.

This is why there's a quadratic formula, a cubic formula, a quartic formula — but no general quintic formula.

The Unsolvable Quintic

The Galois group of the general quintic is S₅ (all permutations of 5 roots). S₅ is not solvable — its only normal subgroups are {e}, A₅ (alternating), and S₅ itself, and A₅ is simple (no normal subgroups at all). Therefore no radical formula exists. Specific quintics like x⁵ − x − 1 = 0 have Galois group S₅ and cannot be solved by radicals — their roots exist but cannot be expressed using +, −, ×, ÷, and √.

🌍 Abstract Algebra in the Real World

Algebra Everywhere
From Rubik's Cube to Particle Physics
Abstract algebra seemed like the purest of pure mathematics — studying groups and fields for their own sake. Yet it turned out to be the language of modern physics. The Standard Model of particle physics is built on Lie groups (SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1)). Crystallography uses space groups. Cryptography uses finite fields and elliptic curve groups. Coding theory uses polynomial rings over finite fields. The Rubik's Cube is a group of 43 quintillion elements. And the classification of finite simple groups — one of the greatest mathematical achievements of the 20th century — lists all the "atoms" of finite symmetry.
Group theory is the grammar of symmetry, and nature speaks in symmetry.

The Rubik's Cube Group

The Rubik's Cube has 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 possible states. The set of all moves forms a group. The structure of this group — its generators (the six face turns), its relations (the sequences that return to solved), and its subgroups (like the "square subgroup" of moves that only use half-turns) — is a beautiful application of group theory. God's number (the maximum number of moves needed to solve any state) was proven to be 20 using group-theoretic reasoning and computer search.

Elliptic Curve Cryptography

Elliptic curves over finite fields form groups. The security of ECC relies on the difficulty of the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem: given points P and nP on the curve, find n. This is believed to be much harder than factoring — so ECC achieves the same security as RSA with much smaller keys. Bitcoin, HTTPS, and modern messaging all use elliptic curve groups.

Elliptic Curve Group Law
Points on y² = x³ + ax + b form a group. The "addition" of two points is defined geometrically: draw the line through P and Q; its third intersection with the curve is −(P+Q); reflect across the x-axis to get P+Q. This turns the curve into an abelian group — the foundation of ECC.

Burnside's Lemma — Counting with Symmetry

How many distinct necklaces can you make with 6 beads, each bead either black or white, if rotations are considered the same? Burnside's lemma uses group actions to count: number of orbits = (1/|G|) Σ |Fix(g)|. For this problem: G = the 6 rotations. Fix(identity) = 2⁶ = 64. A 90° rotation only fixes monochromatic necklaces: 2. Summing over all rotations: (64 + 2 + 4 + 2 + 4 + 2)/6 = 78/6 = 13 distinct necklaces.

Burnside's Lemma
# Orbits = (1/|G|) Σ_{g∈G} |Fix(g)|
The number of distinct objects under a group action equals the average number of objects fixed by each group element.

The Classification of Finite Simple Groups

Finite simple groups are the "prime numbers" of group theory — they have no non-trivial normal subgroups. The classification theorem, completed in 2004 (after tens of thousands of pages across hundreds of papers), lists all finite simple groups. They fall into families: cyclic groups of prime order, alternating groups Aₙ (n ≥ 5), groups of Lie type (like PSLₙ(𝔽ₐ)), and 26 sporadic groups — the largest of which is the Monster group, with about 8×10⁵³ elements. The Monster has deep connections to number theory through monstrous moonshine.

Sylow Theorems — Existence, Conjugacy, and Counting

The Sylow theorems give precise information about subgroups whose order is a power of a prime dividing the order of a finite group. Let G be a finite group of order |G| = pⁿ m where p is prime and p ∤ m. A Sylow p‑subgroup of G is a subgroup of order pⁿ. The three theorems state:

  1. Existence: G contains at least one Sylow p‑subgroup.
  2. Conjugacy: Any two Sylow p‑subgroups are conjugate in G; hence they are isomorphic.
  3. Counting: The number nₚ of Sylow p‑subgroups satisfies nₚ ≡ 1 (mod p) and nₚ | m.

These constraints are powerful. For a group of order 56 = 2³·7, the number of Sylow 7‑subgroups n₇ divides 8 and is ≡ 1 (mod 7), so n₇ = 1 or 8. If n₇ = 1 the unique Sylow 7‑subgroup is normal. If n₇ = 8 each contributes 6 non‑identity elements, giving 48 elements of order 7, leaving only 8 elements for the Sylow 2‑subgroups — forcing a unique Sylow 2‑subgroup, which is then normal. Thus every group of order 56 has a non‑trivial normal subgroup and cannot be simple.

The proofs use group actions: G acts on the set of Sylow p‑subgroups by conjugation. The orbit‑stabiliser theorem gives the counting condition, while the class equation applied to this action yields the congruence. In practice, the Sylow theorems are the first tool for classifying groups of a given order. They also lead to the concept of a Sylow tower — a normal series whose factors are Sylow subgroups for distinct primes — which exists for all finite solvable groups.

Group Actions — Orbits, Stabilizers, and the Class Equation

A group action of a group G on a set X is a map G × X → X satisfying e·x = x and (gh)·x = g·(h·x). For each x ∈ X the orbit Orb(x) = {g·x | g ∈ G} and the stabiliser Stab(x) = {g ∈ G | g·x = x} are related by the Orbit‑Stabiliser Theorem: |Orb(x)| = |G| / |Stab(x)|. Orbits partition X, so |X| = Σ |Orb(xᵢ)| over a set of representatives.

When G acts on itself by conjugation, the orbits are the conjugacy classes. The stabiliser of an element g is its centraliser C_G(g). The class equation becomes |G| = |Z(G)| + Σ [G : C_G(gᵢ)] where the sum runs over representatives of non‑central conjugacy classes. This equation immediately shows that the centre of a non‑trivial p‑group is non‑trivial, because each index [G : C_G(gᵢ)] is a multiple of p.

Group actions also give a clean proof of Cauchy’s theorem: if p | |G|, let G act on the set of p-tuples (g₁,…,gₚ) with product e by cyclic permutation. The number of fixed points is congruent to |G|ᵖ⁻¹ (mod p), guaranteeing a non‑identity fixed point, i.e. an element of order p. The same technique underlies the proof of the Sylow theorems themselves.

Applying Sylow Theorems to Determine Group Structure
Let G be a group of order 45 = 3²·5. Determine all possible isomorphism types of G.
By Sylow, n₅ ≡ 1 (mod 5) and n₅ | 9, so n₅ = 1. The unique Sylow 5‑subgroup P₅ is normal of order 5. For the Sylow 3‑subgroup, n₃ ≡ 1 (mod 3) and n₃ | 5, so n₃ = 1. Hence the Sylow 3‑subgroup P₃ of order 9 is also normal. Since P₅ ∩ P₃ = {e} and both are normal, G ≅ P₅ × P₃. Groups of order 9 are either C₉ or C₃ × C₃. Therefore G is isomorphic to either C₅ × C₉ ≅ C₄₅ or C₅ × (C₃ × C₃) ≅ C₁₅ × C₃. Both are abelian, and no non‑abelian group of order 45 exists.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives a group order, first factor it and write down the Sylow counting conditions. The congruence nₚ ≡ 1 (mod p) and divisibility nₚ | m often force nₚ = 1, giving a normal Sylow subgroup. A normal Sylow subgroup lets you build a semidirect product; if all Sylow subgroups are normal the group is a direct product of its Sylow subgroups and therefore nilpotent. Memorise the classification of groups of order p² (always abelian) and p³ (two abelian, two non‑abelian for odd p) — they appear repeatedly in semidirect product constructions.

Modules over Principal Ideal Domains — Structure Theorem

A module over a ring R generalises a vector space by allowing scalars from a ring that need not be a field. When R is a principal ideal domain (PID) — for example ℤ or F[x] for a field F — finitely generated modules have a complete classification. The Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a PID states that any such module M is isomorphic to a direct sum

M ≅ Rʳ ⊕ R/(d₁) ⊕ R/(d₂) ⊕ … ⊕ R/(dₖ)

where r ≥ 0 is the free rank, each dᵢ is non‑zero, non‑unit, and d₁ | d₂ | … | dₖ. The ideals (dᵢ) are the invariant factors. An equivalent primary decomposition writes each R/(dᵢ) as a direct sum of modules R/(pᵉ) for prime powers pᵉ dividing dᵢ.

For R = ℤ this yields the Fundamental Theorem of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups: every such group is a direct sum of a free abelian group ℤʳ and finite cyclic groups of prime‑power order. For R = F[x] the theorem classifies linear operators on a finite‑dimensional vector space: the module structure is given by x·v = T(v) for an operator T. The invariant factors are the invariant factors of the rational canonical form, and the primary decomposition corresponds to the Jordan canonical form when F is algebraically closed.

The proof uses Smith normal form for matrices over a PID. Given a presentation matrix A for M, elementary row and column operations (which correspond to change of generators and relations) diagonalise A to diag(d₁,…,dₖ,0,…,0) with the divisibility condition. This algorithmic aspect makes the theorem effective: one can compute the invariant factors of a given module by performing the Euclidean algorithm on the entries of a relation matrix.

Finite Fields — Construction, Multiplicative Group, and Applications

A finite field (or Galois field) has order q = pⁿ for a prime p and integer n ≥ 1. For each such q there exists a unique (up to isomorphism) field 𝔽_q. Construction: take an irreducible polynomial f(x) ∈ 𝔽_p[x] of degree n; then 𝔽_q ≅ 𝔽_p[x]/(f(x)). The elements are residue classes of polynomials of degree < n, with addition and multiplication modulo f.

The multiplicative group 𝔽_qˣ is cyclic of order q−1. A generator is called a primitive element. This cyclicity underlies many algorithms: discrete logarithms, Reed–Solomon error correction, and the construction of finite projective planes. The subfields of 𝔽pⁿ are exactly the fields 𝔽pᵈ where d | n. The Galois group Gal(𝔽pⁿ/𝔽_p) is cyclic of order n, generated by the Frobenius automorphism σ(a) = aᵖ.

Applications: In cryptography, the Diffie–Hellman key exchange and ElGamal encryption work in any cyclic group; using 𝔽_qˣ or the group of points on an elliptic curve over 𝔽_q gives concrete implementations. In coding theory, BCH and Reed–Solomon codes are defined by evaluating polynomials over 𝔽_q at the powers of a primitive element. The fact that xq−x splits completely in 𝔽_q and has no repeated roots provides a quick irreducibility test for polynomials over 𝔽_p.

Solvable and Nilpotent Groups — Criteria and Examples

A group G is solvable if it has a subnormal series G = G₀ ⊵ G₁ ⊵ … ⊵ Gₖ = {e} with abelian quotients Gᵢ/Gᵢ₊₁. Equivalently, the derived series G⁽⁰⁾ = G, G⁽ⁱ⁺¹⁾ = [G⁽ⁱ⁾, G⁽ⁱ⁾] reaches {e} after finitely many steps. Every nilpotent group is solvable, but the converse fails: S₃ is solvable (derived series S₃ ⊵ A₃ ⊵ {e}) but not nilpotent because its centre is trivial.

A group is nilpotent if its lower central series γ₁(G)=G, γi+1(G)=[γ_i(G),G] terminates at {e}. For finite groups, nilpotence is equivalent to being the direct product of its Sylow subgroups (i.e. all Sylow subgroups are normal). The class of a nilpotent group is the length of the lower central series minus one. Groups of order pⁿ are nilpotent of class at most n−1.

Key criteria: G is solvable iff every composition factor is cyclic of prime order (Jordan–Hölder). Burnside’s pᵃqᵇ theorem states that any group whose order has only two prime divisors is solvable. The Feit–Thompson theorem (odd order theorem) proves that every finite group of odd order is solvable. These deep results show that non‑solvable finite groups must have order divisible by at least three primes, the smallest example being A₅ of order 60 = 2²·3·5.

In Galois theory, a polynomial is solvable by radicals exactly when its Galois group is solvable. The symmetric group Sₙ is solvable only for n ≤ 4; S₅ and higher are not solvable because Aₙ is simple non‑abelian for n ≥ 5. This explains the classical impossibility of solving the general quintic by radicals.

  • Confusing the invariant factor decomposition with the primary decomposition — they are equivalent but the divisibility condition on invariant factors is often missed.
  • Assuming every subgroup of a solvable group is solvable (true) but forgetting that quotients of solvable groups are also solvable — both directions are needed for the derived series argument.
  • Misapplying the Sylow counting congruence: nₚ ≡ 1 (mod p) not (mod pⁿ).
  • Thinking the multiplicative group of a finite field is always generated by the residue class of x in the construction 𝔽_p[x]/(f(x)) — x is a primitive element only for specially chosen f.
  • Overlooking that a direct product of nilpotent groups is nilpotent, but a semidirect product need not be.
  • Neglecting the condition that the derived series must reach the identity in finitely many steps; infinite groups can be non‑solvable even if every finitely generated subgroup is solvable.

Character Theory of Finite Groups — Orthogonality and Character Tables

Character theory transforms the study of finite group representations into a computational tool of extraordinary power. Let G be a finite group and ρ : G → GL(V) a finite‑dimensional complex representation. The character χρ : G → ℂ is defined by χρ(g) = tr(ρ(g)). Characters are class functions — constant on conjugacy classes — and the irreducible characters form an orthonormal basis for the space of class functions with respect to the inner product ⟨φ, ψ⟩ = (1/|G|) ∑g∈G φ(g)ψ(g)− (complex conjugate). The first orthogonality relation states that for irreducible characters χi, χj, ⟨χi, χj⟩ = δij. The second orthogonality relation runs over irreducible characters: ∑χ irred χ(g)χ(h)− = 0 unless g and h are conjugate, in which case it equals |CG(g)|, the centraliser order. These relations make the character table — rows indexed by irreducible characters, columns by conjugacy classes — a square matrix with orthonormal rows and orthogonal columns (weighted by class sizes). The number of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes. The degrees χ(1) satisfy ∑ χ(1)2 = |G| and each χ(1) divides |G| (a theorem of Frobenius). The character table determines the group up to isoclinism but not always up to isomorphism; the dihedral group D8 and the quaternion group Q8 share the same character table. Characters are algebraic integers, and for any g ∈ G, χ(g) is a sum of roots of unity, hence an algebraic integer. The kernel of a character is a normal subgroup, and every normal subgroup is an intersection of kernels of irreducible characters. The regular representation has character χreg(1) = |G|, χreg(g) = 0 for g ≠ 1, and decomposes as ⊕ χ(1)χ over irreducibles. Tensor products of representations correspond to pointwise products of characters, and the multiplicity of an irreducible χ in a representation with character ψ is ⟨ψ, χ⟩. Frobenius reciprocity relates induction and restriction: ⟨IndHG φ, χ⟩G = ⟨φ, ResHG χ⟩H. This is the engine behind Mackey theory and the construction of representations of symmetric groups via Young tableaux.

Dedekind Domains and Algebraic Integers — Unique Factorisation of Ideals

The ring of integers OK of a number field K (a finite extension of ℚ) is the prototypical Dedekind domain. A Dedekind domain is an integral domain that is Noetherian, integrally closed in its field of fractions, and has Krull dimension one (every non‑zero prime ideal is maximal). In such a ring, every non‑zero ideal factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals — this restores unique factorisation at the level of ideals even when elements fail to factor uniquely. For OK, the prime ideals lying above a rational prime p correspond to the irreducible factors of the minimal polynomial of a primitive element modulo p (Kummer‑Dedekind theorem). The ramification index e, inertia degree f, and number of primes g satisfy efg = [K : ℚ]. The discriminant ΔK detects ramification: p ramifies iff p | ΔK. The ideal class group Cl(K) = Div(K)/Prin(K) measures the failure of unique factorisation of elements; it is finite (Minkowski's bound) and its order hK is the class number. The class group is trivial exactly when OK is a UFD. Dirichlet's unit theorem describes the unit group OK× ≅ μK × ℤr1+r2−1, where μK are the roots of unity in K, r1 is the number of real embeddings, and r2 the number of complex conjugate pairs. The regulator RK (a determinant of logarithms of fundamental units) appears in the analytic class number formula: lims→1 (s−1)ζK(s) = 2r1(2π)r2 hK RK / (√|ΔK| · |μK|). This connects the algebraic class group to the analytic behaviour of the Dedekind zeta function. For quadratic fields ℚ(√d), the ring of integers is ℤ[√d] if d ≡ 2,3 (mod 4) and ℤ[(1+√d)/2] if d ≡ 1 (mod 4). The class number problem for imaginary quadratic fields (Gauss's class number one problem) was solved by Heegner, Baker, and Stark: the only such fields with class number one have d = −1, −2, −3, −7, −11, −19, −43, −67, −163. For real quadratic fields, it is unknown whether infinitely many have class number one.

Exam Strategy — Character Tables
When asked to complete a partial character table, use the following checklist in order: (1) Count conjugacy classes = number of irreducibles. (2) Use ∑ χ(1)² = |G| to find missing degrees. (3) The first column is the degrees; the first row is all ones (trivial character). (4) Apply row orthogonality (weighted by class sizes) to solve for unknown entries — this gives linear equations. (5) Apply column orthogonality as a check or when row equations are insufficient. (6) Remember χ(g⁻¹) = χ(g)̅ and χ(g) is an algebraic integer; for real‑valued characters, χ(g) ∈ ℤ. (7) The kernel of a character is the set of g with χ(g)=χ(1); it is a normal subgroup. Use known normal subgroups to constrain rows. (8) For groups of order pⁿ, the centre is non‑trivial; linear characters factor through G/Z(G). Practice constructing tables for D₈, Q₈, A₄, S₄ — they appear repeatedly.

Homological Algebra — Exact Sequences, Ext and Tor

Homological algebra provides the language to measure the failure of exactness of functors. A sequence of module homomorphisms ⋯ → Mi−1 → Mi → Mi+1 → ⋯ is exact at Mi if im(di) = ker(di+1). A short exact sequence (SES) 0 → A → B → C → 0 encodes an extension of C by A. The functor HomR(−, X) is left exact: applying it to a SES gives 0 → Hom(C, X) → Hom(B, X) → Hom(A, X) exact, but the last map need not be surjective. Its right derived functors are ExtnR(−, X). Concretely, take a projective resolution ⋯ → P1 → P0 → A → 0, apply Hom(−, X), drop the Hom(A, X) term, and take cohomology. Ext0 = Hom. Ext1(A, X) classifies equivalence classes of extensions 0 → X → E → A → 0 (Baer sum). For ℤ‑modules (abelian groups), Ext1(ℤ/nℤ, ℤ) ≅ ℤ/nℤ, and Ext1(ℚ, ℤ) is a huge divisible group. The functor − ⊗R X is right exact; its left derived functors are TornR(−, X). Take a projective resolution of A, tensor with X, drop A ⊗ X, take homology. Tor0 = ⊗. Tor1(A, X) measures the failure of flatness. Over ℤ, Tor1(ℤ/mℤ, ℤ/nℤ) ≅ ℤ/gcd(m,n)ℤ. A module is flat iff Tor1(−, M) = 0; projective ⇒ flat ⇒ torsion‑free (over domains). The long exact sequences in Ext and Tor are the primary computational tools: a SES 0 → A → B → C → 0 induces ⋯ → Extn(C, X) → Extn(B, X) → Extn(A, X) → Extn+1(C, X) → ⋯ and similarly for Tor. The snake lemma constructs the connecting homomorphism from a commutative diagram with exact rows. Projective dimension pd(M) is the minimal length of a projective resolution; global dimension of R is sup pd(M). For a PID, global dimension ≤ 1 (submodules of free are free). For ℤ, pd(ℤ/nℤ) = 1. The Auslander‑Buchsbaum formula relates depth, projective dimension, and Krull dimension for local rings. Derived categories and triangulated categories package these constructions functorially, but for examinations the long exact sequences and the interpretation of Ext1 as extensions are the essential takeaways.

Central Simple Algebras and the Brauer Group

A central simple algebra (CSA) over a field F is a finite‑dimensional associative F‑algebra with centre exactly F and no non‑trivial two‑sided ideals. Wedderburn's theorem states that every CSA is isomorphic to a matrix algebra Mn(D) for a unique (up to isomorphism) division algebra D with centre F. The degree of the CSA is n√dimF D; its index is √dimF D. Two CSAs A, B are similar (Brauer equivalent) if Mr(A) ≅ Ms(B) for some r, s. The Brauer group Br(F) is the set of similarity classes with operation induced by tensor product: [A] · [B] = [A ⊗F B]. The identity is the class of F (or any matrix algebra over F), and the inverse of [A] is [Aop] (opposite algebra). For a finite field, Br(F) = 0 (Wedderburn's little theorem: every finite division ring is a field). For ℝ, Br(ℝ) ≅ ℤ/2ℤ, represented by ℝ and the Hamiltonian quaternions ℍ. For a non‑archimedean local field (e.g., ℚp), Br(ℚp) ≅ ℚ/ℤ via the invariant map invp : Br(ℚp) → ℚ/ℤ. For a global field K, the Albert‑Brauer‑Hasse‑Noether theorem gives an exact sequence 0 → Br(K) → ⊕v Br(Kv) → ℚ/ℤ → 0, where the sum runs over all places. The map to ℚ/ℤ is the sum of local invariants; a CSA over K is split (matrix algebra) iff all local invariants are zero. This is the local‑global principle for CSAs. Crossed product algebras provide explicit constructions: for a cyclic extension L/F of degree n with Galois group ⟨σ⟩ and an element a ∈ F×, the algebra (L/F, σ, a) has basis 1, u, …, un−1 with un = a and ux = σ(x)u for x ∈ L. Every CSA over a number field is a tensor product of cyclic algebras. The reduced norm Nrd : A → F generalises the determinant; for Mn(D) it is the n‑th power of the reduced norm on D. The reduced norm map on the Brauer group connects to Galois cohomology: Br(F) ≅ H²(Gal(Fsep/F), Fsep×). This identifies the Brauer group with the cohomological Brauer group, and the invariant map with the boundary map in the long exact sequence of 1 → Fsep× → ….

Group Cohomology and Extensions — H¹, H², and the Schur Multiplier

Group cohomology H*(G, M) for a G‑module M is the right derived functor of the fixed‑point functor M ↦ MG = {m ∈ M | g·m = m ∀g ∈ G}. Concretely, use the bar resolution: Cn(G, M) = {functions f : Gn → M} with differential (df)(g1, …, gn+1) = g1·f(g2, …) + ∑i=1n (−1)i f(…, gigi+1, …) + (−1)n+1 f(g1, …, gn). H⁰(G, M) = MG. H¹(G, M) = Z¹/B¹ where Z¹ are crossed homomorphisms (derivations) d : G → M satisfying d(gh) = d(g) + g·d(h), and B¹ are principal crossed homomorphisms d(g) = g·m − m. If M is trivial, H¹(G, M) = Hom(G, M) (group homomorphisms). H²(G, M) classifies equivalence classes of extensions 1 → M → E → G → 1 with the given G‑action on M (induced by conjugation in E). The Baer sum corresponds to the group operation in H². The inflation‑restriction exact sequence for a normal subgroup N ⊴ G reads 0 → H¹(G/N, MN) → H¹(G, M) → H¹(N, M)G/N → H²(G/N, MN) → H²(G, M) → H²(N, M)G/N. The Schur multiplier M(G) = H²(G, ℂ×) (trivial action) classifies projective representations of G up to equivalence. A projective representation is a map ρ : G → PGL(V) lifting to GL(V) with a cocycle α ∈ Z²(G, ℂ×). The covering group (Schur cover) is a central extension 1 → M(G) → Ĝ → G → 1 such that every projective representation of G lifts to a linear representation of Ĝ. For finite groups, M(G) is finite. Examples: M(Sn) = ℤ/2ℤ for n ≥ 4 (the double cover 2·Sn); M(An) = ℤ/2ℤ for n ≥ 5 except n = 6,7 where it is ℤ/6ℤ; M(SL(2,p)) = 1 for odd p. The cohomological dimension cd(G) is the supremum of n with Hn(G, M) ≠ 0 for some M. For finite groups, cd(G) = ∞ unless G is trivial (periodic cohomology). For profinite groups (Galois groups), cdp(G) ≤ 1 for absolute Galois groups of finite fields and p‑adic fields. Tate cohomology Ĥ* combines homology and cohomology for finite groups, yielding exact sequences for norm maps and the Herbrand quotient. The cup product Hp × Hq → Hp+q makes H* a graded ring; for cyclic groups it is periodic of period 2.

  • Assuming the character table determines the group up to isomorphism — D₈ and Q₈ are the standard counterexample; isoclinism is the correct equivalence relation.
  • Forgetting that Ext1 classifies extensions only up to equivalence of short exact sequences, not up to isomorphism of the middle term alone. Two non‑equivalent extensions can have isomorphic middle modules.
  • Treating Tor1 as a measure of torsion — Tor1(ℤ/mℤ, ℤ/nℤ) ≅ ℤ/gcd(m,n)ℤ, but Tor1(ℚ, ℤ) is torsion‑free and huge. Flatness, not torsion‑freeness, is the correct criterion.
  • Misapplying the Brauer group exact sequence for global fields — the sum of local invariants must be zero in ℚ/ℤ, not in ℝ/ℤ. The invariant map takes values in ℚ/ℤ, and the sequence is exact at the middle term only when the sum is zero.

Representation Theory of Finite Groups – Induction, Restriction, and Frobenius Reciprocity

Let G be a finite group and H a subgroup. For a k-representation V of H (with k a field of characteristic not dividing |G|), the induced representation IndHGV is the vector space k[G] ⊗k[H] V equipped with the left G-action on the first factor. Its dimension equals [G:H]·dimV. The character of the induced representation is given by the Mackey formula: for g ∈ G, χInd V(g) = Σx∈H\G/H χV(x-1gx) where the sum runs over double coset representatives and the term is taken as zero when x-1gx ∉ H. Restriction ResHG simply forgets the action of elements outside H. Frobenius reciprocity states that for any G-module W and H-module V there is a natural isomorphism HomG(IndHGV, W) ≅ HomH(V, ResHGW). At the level of characters this yields ⟨χInd V, χW⟩G = ⟨χV, χW⟩H. A standard application is the construction of the permutation representation on the coset space G/H, which is IndHG1H. Its decomposition into irreducibles is governed by the double coset algebra k[H\G/H]; when H is a maximal subgroup the permutation representation is often multiplicity‑free. Mackey’s irreducibility criterion says IndHGV is irreducible iff V is irreducible and for every g ∉ H the representations V and gV (conjugate by g) have no common irreducible constituent upon restriction to H ∩ gH. This criterion underlies the classification of irreducible representations of symmetric groups via Young subgroups and of finite groups of Lie type via Deligne–Lusztig induction.

Algebraic Number Theory – Ideal Class Groups and Dirichlet’s Unit Theorem

Let K be a number field of degree n = r1 + 2r2 with r1 real embeddings and r2 pairs of complex embeddings. The ring of integers OK is a Dedekind domain; its non‑zero ideals form a free abelian monoid under multiplication. The ideal class group Cl(K) = I(K)/P(K) (fractional ideals modulo principal ideals) is a finite abelian group; its order hK is the class number. Minkowski’s bound gives an explicit constant MK = (4/π)r2·n!/nn·√|disc(K)| such that every ideal class contains an integral ideal of norm ≤ MK. This finiteness proof also yields an algorithm to compute Cl(K) by enumerating ideals up to the bound. Dirichlet’s unit theorem describes the unit group OK× ≅ μK × ℤr1+r2-1, where μK is the finite cyclic group of roots of unity in K. The rank r = r1+r2-1 equals the dimension of the hyperplane in ℝr1+r2 cut out by the logarithmic embedding λ: OK× → ℝr1+r2, λ(α) = (log|σ1(α)|, …, log|σr1+r2(α)|). The regulator RK is the covolume of the lattice λ(OK×) in that hyperplane; it appears in the analytic class number formula ζK(s) ~ hKRK·2r1(2π)r2 / (√|disc(K)|·|μK|)·(s-1) as s→1. For quadratic fields ℚ(√d) the class number can be computed via binary quadratic forms; Gauss’s composition law on forms corresponds exactly to ideal multiplication in the quadratic order. The Hilbert class field H of K is the maximal unramified abelian extension; Gal(H/K) ≅ Cl(K) via the Artin map. This realises class field theory for abelian extensions and provides a concrete description of the class group as a Galois group.

Worked Example – Computing a Class Group
Determine the ideal class group of K = ℚ(√-5).
The discriminant is -20. Minkowski bound M = (2/π)·√20 ≈ 2.85, so we only need to examine ideals of norm 2. The prime 2 ramifies because (-20/2)=0; indeed (2) = (2, 1+√-5)2. The prime ideal P = (2, 1+√-5) has norm 2. Since P2 = (2) is principal, P has order dividing 2. P is not principal (no element of norm 2), so its order is exactly 2. Hence every ideal class is represented by (1) or P, and Cl(K) ≅ ℤ/2ℤ. The class number h = 2.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the class group of a quadratic field, first compute the discriminant and Minkowski bound. List all rational primes up to the bound, factor them using the Kronecker symbol, and write down the resulting prime ideals. Use the relation that the product of conjugate prime ideals equals the principal ideal generated by the rational prime. This reduces the problem to a small finite abelian group presented by generators and relations; solve it by hand or by Smith normal form. Note that imaginary quadratic fields can have even or odd class numbers; the nine Heegner discriminants (−3, −4, −7, −8, −11, −19, −43, −67, −163) are precisely those with class number 1.

Homological Algebra – Derived Functors, Spectral Sequences, and Applications

Given an abelian category A with enough injectives, the right derived functors RnF of a left‑exact functor F: A → B are defined by applying F to an injective resolution 0 → X → I0 → I1 → … and taking cohomology. The classical examples are Extn(A, –) = RnHom(A, –) and Hn(G, –) = Rn(–)G for group cohomology. A spectral sequence is a collection of pages Erp,q with differentials dr: Erp,q → Erp+r,q-r+1 such that Er+1 = H(Er, dr). The two most common spectral sequences in algebra are the Grothendieck spectral sequence for a composition of functors and the Lyndon–Hochschild–Serre (LHS) spectral sequence for group extensions. If 1 → N → G → Q → 1 is exact and M a G-module, the LHS spectral sequence has E2p,q = Hp(Q, Hq(N, M)) converging to Hp+q(G, M). The edge maps give the inflation map Hp(Q, MN) → Hp(G, M) and the restriction map Hp(G, M) → Hp(N, M)Q. The five‑term exact sequence extracted from the LHS spectral sequence is 0 → H1(Q, MN) → H1(G, M) → H1(N, M)Q → H2(Q, MN) → H2(G, M). This sequence is the inflation‑restriction exact sequence mentioned in the previous lesson. Another powerful tool is the derived category D(A), where complexes are identified up to quasi‑isomorphism; derived functors become total derived functors between triangulated categories, and spectral sequences arise from filtrations on objects. In practice, computing Ext groups over a polynomial ring k[x1,…,xn] uses the Koszul resolution, yielding Exti(k, k) ≅ ∧ikn. Over a group algebra k[G] with char(k) dividing |G|, the minimal projective resolution is infinite and its periodicity reflects the cohomological dimension of G.

Noncommutative Ring Theory – Ore Localization and Goldie’s Theorem

Let R be a (not necessarily commutative) ring and S a multiplicative subset. The right Ore condition requires that for any s ∈ S and r ∈ R there exist s' ∈ S and r' ∈ R with r s' = s r'. If S satisfies both left and right Ore conditions, the localization S-1R exists and the canonical map R → S-1R is a ring homomorphism universal for inverting elements of S. A ring is a right Ore domain iff it has no zero divisors and satisfies the right Ore condition; then its right classical ring of fractions is a division ring. Goldie’s theorem characterises semiprime rings that have a semisimple Artinian classical ring of quotients. A ring R is right Goldie if it has finite uniform dimension (no infinite direct sum of non‑zero right ideals) and satisfies the ascending chain condition on right annihilators. Goldie’s theorem states: a semiprime ring R is right Goldie iff its classical right ring of quotients Qr(R) exists and is semisimple Artinian (a finite direct product of matrix rings over division rings). The proof constructs Qr(R) by localising at the set of regular elements (non‑zero‑divisors) and uses the Ore condition derived from the Goldie hypotheses. For a prime right Goldie ring, Qr(R) is a simple Artinian ring, hence isomorphic to Mn(D) for some division ring D and integer n. The integer n is the uniform dimension of R. An important corollary: a right Noetherian domain is a right Ore domain, so its classical ring of quotients is a division ring. This applies to enveloping algebras of finite‑dimensional Lie algebras (by the Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem) and to many quantum groups. The theory of Ore extensions R[x; σ, δ] (where σ is an automorphism and δ a σ‑derivation) provides a large supply of noncommutative Noetherian domains; their prime spectra are studied via the Dixmier–Moeglin equivalence.

Category‑Theoretic Foundations – Adjunctions, Limits, and the Yoneda Lemma

A functor F: C → D is left adjoint to G: D → C (written F ⊣ G) when there is a natural isomorphism HomD(Fc, d) ≅ HomC(c, Gd) for all objects c ∈ C, d ∈ D. The unit η: IdC → GF and counit ε: FG → IdD satisfy the triangle identities. Adjunctions are ubiquitous: the free‑module functor ⊣ forgetful functor, tensor‑Hom adjunction M ⊗R – ⊣ HomR(M, –), and the induction‑restriction adjunction for group representations. A limit of a diagram D: J → C is a universal cone; dually a colimit is a universal cocone. Products, equalizers, pullbacks are limits; coproducts, coequalizers, pushouts are colimits. A category with all small limits (resp. colimits) is complete (resp. cocomplete). The Yoneda lemma states that for any locally small category C and any functor F: Cop → Set, there is a natural bijection Nat(HomC(–, c), F) ≅ F(c). In particular, the Yoneda embedding y: C → [Cop, Set] sending c to Hom(–, c) is fully faithful. This identifies C with a full subcategory of its presheaf category. The Yoneda lemma implies that an object is determined up to unique isomorphism by its hom‑functor; it also yields the representation of limits as ends: lim D ≅ ∫j Hom(c, Dj). Adjunctions preserve limits (right adjoints) and colimits (left adjoints). The adjoint functor theorem gives sufficient conditions for a functor to have a left or right adjoint: a functor G: D → C between locally presentable categories has a left adjoint iff it preserves limits and is accessible. In algebraic contexts, the category of modules over a ring is locally finitely presentable, so any limit‑preserving functor to another such category automatically has a left adjoint. This abstract principle underlies the existence of free constructions, tensor products, and derived functors.

  • Confusing the direction of the unit and counit in an adjunction — the unit goes from the identity to the composite of right then left adjoint, the counit from left then right to the identity.
  • Assuming every multiplicative set in a noncommutative ring satisfies the Ore condition — counterexample: the set of non‑zero elements in the free algebra k⟨x,y⟩ fails both left and right Ore conditions.
  • Misapplying Goldie’s theorem to rings with nilpotent ideals — the theorem requires semiprime (no non‑zero nilpotent ideals); the classical ring of quotients of a non‑semiprime ring need not be semisimple.
  • Treating the LHS spectral sequence as always collapsing at E2 — it collapses only under extra hypotheses such as trivial action or cohomological dimension ≤ 1.
  • Forgetting that the Yoneda embedding preserves limits but not colimits — the presheaf category has all colimits, but the embedding does not send colimits in C to colimits in the presheaf category.
  • Identifying Ext1 with the group of extensions without specifying the equivalence relation — two extensions with isomorphic middle term can represent different Ext classes.
  • Using the class number formula without checking the regulator is non‑zero — the regulator vanishes only for ℚ and imaginary quadratic fields, where the unit rank is zero.

Lie Algebras and Their Representations

A Lie algebra over a field k is a vector space 𝔤 equipped with a bilinear map [·, ·] : 𝔤 × 𝔤 → 𝔤 called the Lie bracket, satisfying antisymmetry [x, x] = 0 (hence [x, y] = −[y, x]) and the Jacobi identity [x, [y, z]] + [y, [z, x]] + [z, [x, y]] = 0 for all x, y, z ∈ 𝔤. Every associative algebra A becomes a Lie algebra under the commutator [a, b] = ab − ba; the Lie algebra of n × n matrices is denoted 𝔤𝔩n(k). A Lie subalgebra of 𝔤𝔩n is a linear Lie algebra; Ado's theorem states every finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of characteristic zero embeds into some 𝔤𝔩n. The derived series 𝔤(0) = 𝔤, 𝔤(i+1) = [𝔤(i), 𝔤(i)] and lower central series 𝔤1 = 𝔤, 𝔤i+1 = [𝔤, 𝔤i] define solvable and nilpotent Lie algebras respectively. Engel's theorem: a finite-dimensional Lie algebra is nilpotent iff every element acts nilpotently in the adjoint representation ad : 𝔤 → 𝔤𝔩(𝔤), ad(x)(y) = [x, y]. Lie's theorem: over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, a solvable Lie algebra has a common eigenvector in any finite-dimensional representation. The Killing form κ(x, y) = tr(ad x ∘ ad y) is a symmetric invariant bilinear form; Cartan's criterion states 𝔤 is semisimple iff κ is nondegenerate. Semisimple Lie algebras over ℂ are classified by Dynkin diagrams of types An, Bn, Cn, Dn, E6, E7, E8, F4, G2. The Weyl group, root system, and Cartan subalgebra structure determine the representation theory: finite-dimensional irreducible representations are indexed by dominant integral weights. The universal enveloping algebra U(𝔤) is the associative algebra generated by 𝔤 modulo the relations xy − yx = [x, y]; the Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem gives a basis of U(𝔤) as ordered monomials in a basis of 𝔤. Verma modules M(λ) = U(𝔤) ⊗U(𝔟) ℂλ (where 𝔟 is a Borel subalgebra) have unique simple quotients L(λ); the BGG category 𝒪 organises these modules. The Weyl character formula expresses the character of L(λ) as a ratio of alternating sums over the Weyl group. For 𝔰𝔩2(ℂ) with basis {e, f, h} satisfying [h, e] = 2e, [h, f] = −2f, [e, f] = h, the irreducible representation of dimension m + 1 has highest weight m and weights m, m−2, …, −m.

Affine Varieties and Hilbert's Nullstellensatz

Let k be an algebraically closed field. Affine n-space 𝔸n is the set kn equipped with the Zariski topology, where closed sets are algebraic sets V(I) = {x ∈ kn | f(x) = 0 ∀ f ∈ I} for ideals I ⊆ k[x1, …, xn]. The ideal of a subset X ⊆ 𝔸n is I(X) = {f | f|X = 0}. The maps V and I form a Galois connection: V(I(X)) = X̄ (Zariski closure) and I(V(I)) = √I (radical). Hilbert's Nullstellensatz (weak form): if I is a proper ideal, then V(I) ≠ ∅. Strong form: I(V(I)) = √I. This establishes a bijection between algebraic sets in 𝔸n and radical ideals in k[x1, …, xn]. An affine variety is an irreducible algebraic set (not a union of two proper closed subsets); its coordinate ring k[X] = k[x1, …, xn]/I(X) is an integral domain. The dimension of a variety is the Krull dimension of its coordinate ring, equal to the transcendence degree of its function field. A morphism φ : X → Y of affine varieties corresponds to a k-algebra homomorphism φ* : k[Y] → k[X]. The tangent space at a point p ∈ X is the dual of mp/mp2 where mp is the maximal ideal of functions vanishing at p; p is nonsingular iff dimk TpX = dim X. The Jacobian criterion: if X = V(f1, …, fr), then p is nonsingular iff the Jacobian matrix (∂fi/∂xj)(p) has rank n − dim X. Bézout's theorem: projective plane curves of degrees d and e with no common component intersect in exactly de points counted with multiplicity. The Hilbert polynomial of a projective variety X ⊆ ℙn encodes its dimension and degree; for a hypersurface of degree d, the Hilbert polynomial is P(t) = dtn−1/(n−1)! + lower terms. Noether's normalisation lemma: any finitely generated k-algebra A that is an integral domain contains a polynomial subring k[y1, …, yd] over which A is integral, where d = dim A. This proves the dimension of an affine variety equals the transcendence degree of its function field.

Worked Problem: Nullstellensatz Application
Let k = ℂ. Determine whether the ideal I = ⟨x2 + y2 − 1, x3 − y⟩ in ℂ[x, y] is radical. If not, find its radical.
Compute the variety V(I). Substitute y = x3 into the first equation: x2 + x6 − 1 = 0. This polynomial has six distinct roots in ℂ (derivative 2x + 6x5 shares no root with it). For each root α, we get a point (α, α3). Thus V(I) consists of six points. The ideal of six points is the intersection of six maximal ideals ⟨x − α, y − α3⟩, which is radical. By the strong Nullstellensatz, √I = I(V(I)). Since I is zero-dimensional (finite variety), ℂ[x, y]/I is a finite-dimensional ℂ-vector space. Compute its dimension via Gröbner basis: the monomials not divisible by leading terms {x3, x2} are {1, x, y, xy, y2, xy2}, so dim = 6. The radical ideal of six points also has quotient dimension 6. Since I ⊆ √I and both quotients have dimension 6, I = √I. Hence I is radical.
Exam Strategy: Nullstellensatz in Computations
When asked whether an ideal is radical, compute the dimension of the quotient ring and compare with the number of points in the variety (counted with multiplicity). If the ideal is zero-dimensional, a Gröbner basis computation giving the standard monomials is the most reliable method. For positive-dimensional ideals, use the Jacobian criterion at generic points or check whether the ideal equals the intersection of its minimal primes (which requires primary decomposition). Remember: over non-algebraically closed fields, the Nullstellensatz fails — ⟨x2 + 1⟩ in ℝ[x] has empty variety but is not the unit ideal.

Hopf Algebras and Quantum Groups

A Hopf algebra over a field k is a vector space H equipped with five linear maps: multiplication m : H ⊗ H → H, unit u : k → H, comultiplication Δ : H → H ⊗ H, counit ε : H → k, and antipode S : H → H, satisfying axioms that make H simultaneously an algebra and a coalgebra with compatibility (Δ and ε are algebra homomorphisms) and the antipode axiom m(S ⊗ id)Δ = uε = m(id ⊗ S)Δ. The tensor product of two H-modules becomes an H-module via Δ; the dual of a finite-dimensional H-module is an H-module via S. A bialgebra is a Hopf algebra without the antipode requirement. The group algebra k[G] of a group G is a Hopf algebra with Δ(g) = g ⊗ g, ε(g) = 1, S(g) = g−1. The universal enveloping algebra U(𝔤) of a Lie algebra is a Hopf algebra with Δ(x) = x ⊗ 1 + 1 ⊗ x, ε(x) = 0, S(x) = −x for x ∈ 𝔤. These are cocommutative (Δ = τΔ where τ swaps factors). Noncommutative, noncocommutative Hopf algebras arise as quantum groups. Drinfeld–Jimbo quantum groups Uq(𝔤) are deformations of U(𝔤) depending on a parameter q; for 𝔰𝔩2, generators E, F, K, K−1 satisfy KEK−1 = q2E, KFK−1 = q−2F, [E, F] = (K − K−1)/(q − q−1), with Δ(K) = K ⊗ K, Δ(E) = E ⊗ K + 1 ⊗ E, Δ(F) = F ⊗ 1 + K−1 ⊗ F. At q = 1 one recovers U(𝔰𝔩2). The representation theory of Uq(𝔤) at generic q mirrors the classical case; at roots of unity, new phenomena appear (finite-dimensional quotients, modular tensor categories). The quantum coordinate ring 𝒪q(G) (FRT construction) is the dual Hopf algebra; for SL2 it is generated by a, b, c, d with relations ab = qba, ac = qca, bd = qdb, cd = qdc, bc = cb, ad − da = (q − q−1)bc and determinant ad − qbc = 1. The R-matrix R ∈ H ⊗ H satisfying Δop(h) = RΔ(h)R−1 and the Yang–Baxter equation R12R13R23 = R23R13R12 makes the category of H-modules braided monoidal. This underlies knot invariants (Jones polynomial) via the Reshetikhin–Turaev construction. Sweedler's notation Δ(h) = Σ h(1) ⊗ h(2) simplifies computations. A Hopf algebra is cosemisimple if its finite-dimensional comodules are semisimple; for finite-dimensional H, this is equivalent to the existence of a Haar integral Λ ∈ H with hΛ = ε(h)Λ and ε(Λ) = 1. The Larson–Radford theorem: a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra over a field of characteristic zero is semisimple iff it is cosemisimple.

Geometric Group Theory: Cayley Graphs, Growth, and Hyperbolicity

Let G be a finitely generated group with finite symmetric generating set S. The Cayley graph Γ(G, S) has vertex set G and edges g — gs for g ∈ G, s ∈ S. It is a regular graph on which G acts freely by left multiplication. The word metric dS(g, h) = length of shortest word in S representing g−1h makes G a metric space quasi-isometric to Γ(G, S). Two metric spaces are quasi-isometric if there exists a map f : X → Y and constants λ ≥ 1, C ≥ 0 such that λ−1dX(x, y) − C ≤ dY(f(x), f(y)) ≤ λdX(x, y) + C and every point of Y is within distance C of f(X). Quasi-isometry is an equivalence relation; properties invariant under quasi-isometry are geometric. The growth function γ(n) = |{g ∈ G : dS(e, g) ≤ n}|. Gromov's theorem: G has polynomial growth iff G is virtually nilpotent (contains a nilpotent subgroup of finite index). The growth degree is an integer (Bass–Guivarc'h formula). Groups of exponential growth include free groups and non-elementary hyperbolic groups. A geodesic metric space is δ-hyperbolic if every geodesic triangle is δ-thin (each side lies in the δ-neighbourhood of the other two). A group is hyperbolic if its Cayley graph is δ-hyperbolic for some δ. Hyperbolic groups have solvable word and conjugacy problems, finitely many conjugacy classes of finite subgroups, and satisfy a linear isoperimetric inequality. The boundary at infinity ∂G of a hyperbolic group is a compact metrisable space on which G acts by homeomorphisms; for free groups it is a Cantor set, for surface groups a circle. Quasi-isometric hyperbolic groups have homeomorphic boundaries. The Mostow rigidity theorem: if two closed hyperbolic manifolds of dimension ≥ 3 have isomorphic fundamental groups, they are isometric. Relatively hyperbolic groups generalise hyperbolic groups by allowing parabolic subgroups; mapping class groups and fundamental groups of finite-volume hyperbolic manifolds with cusps are relatively hyperbolic. The asymptotic cone of a group captures its large-scale geometry; a group is hyperbolic iff all its asymptotic cones are ℝ-trees. Amenable groups (e.g., solvable groups) have Folner sequences; the von Neumann conjecture (every non-amenable group contains a free subgroup) was disproved by Ol'shanskii and Adyan using Burnside groups. The Tits alternative: a finitely generated linear group either contains a free subgroup or is virtually solvable.

Algebraic K-Theory: Projective Modules and the Whitehead Group

For a ring R, the Grothendieck group K0(R) is the abelian group generated by isomorphism classes [P] of finitely generated projective R-modules with relations [P] + [Q] = [P ⊕ Q]. The rank map rank : K0(R) → ℤ (for commutative R) splits as K0(R) ≅ ℤ ⊕ K̃0(R) where K̃0(R) is the reduced group. For a Dedekind domain, K0(R) ≅ ℤ ⊕ Cl(R) where Cl(R) is the ideal class group. The Whitehead group Wh(R) = K1(R)/{±1} where K1(R) = GL(R)/E(R), GL(R) = colim GLn(R), and E(R) is the subgroup generated by elementary matrices. For a field, K1(k) = k×. For a PID, K1(R) = R×. The Bass–Heller–Swan decomposition: K1(R[t, t−1]) ≅ K1(R) ⊕ K0(R) ⊕ NK1(R) ⊕ NK1(R). The higher K-groups Kn(R) for n ≥ 2 are defined via Quillen's plus construction on the classifying space BGL(R) or via Waldhausen's S• construction. For a finite field 𝔽q, Quillen computed K2i−1(𝔽q) ≅ ℤ/(qi − 1)ℤ and K2i(𝔽q) = 0. For number fields, the Borel regulator relates K2n−1(𝒪F) to special values of the Dedekind zeta function. The localisation sequence for a Dedekind domain R with fraction field F: K1(R) → K1(F) → ⊕p K0(R/p) → K0(R) → K0(F) → 0. The projective class group K̃0(ℤ[G]) for a finite group G classifies finitely generated projective ℤ[G]-modules up to stable isomorphism; it is finite (Swan's theorem). The Whitehead group Wh(ℤ[G]) appears in the s-cobordism theorem: an h-cobordism between manifolds of dimension ≥ 6 is trivial iff its Whitehead torsion vanishes in Wh(π1). The Farrell–Jones conjecture predicts the algebraic K-theory of group rings in terms of homology of classifying spaces for virtually cyclic subgroups. For regular rings, Kn(R) = 0 for n < 0 (Bass). The Gersten conjecture (now theorem for smooth varieties over a field) states that the Zariski sheafification of Kn gives a flasque resolution, yielding the Bloch–Quillen formula CHp(X) ≅ Hp(X, Kp) relating Chow groups to K-theory.

  • Assuming the Nullstellensatz holds over non-algebraically closed fields — ⟨x2 + 1⟩ ⊂ ℝ[x] has empty variety but is not the unit ideal; the real Nullstellensatz involves sums of squares and the real radical.
  • Treating the antipode in a Hopf algebra as always invertible — it is always bijective for finite-dimensional Hopf algebras, but for infinite-dimensional ones it may fail to be invertible (though it is always anti-multiplicative and anti-comultiplicative).
  • Confusing polynomial growth with virtually nilpotent — Gromov's theorem says they are equivalent for finitely generated groups, but the growth degree need not be an integer for non-finitely-generated groups.
  • Assuming all hyperbolic groups have free subgroups — non-elementary hyperbolic groups do, but elementary hyperbolic groups (finite or virtually cyclic) do not; the Tits alternative applies to linear groups, not all hyperbolic groups.
  • Misidentifying K0 of a Dedekind domain — it is ℤ ⊕ Cl(R), not just the class group; the rank map gives the ℤ summand.

Derived Categories and Triangulated Structures

The derived category D(A) of an abelian category A (for instance the category of modules over a ring) is obtained by formally inverting quasi‑isomorphisms in the homotopy category of chain complexes. Objects are complexes up to homotopy, and morphisms are roofs X ← Q → Y where the left arrow is a quasi‑isomorphism. This localisation turns the homotopy category into a triangulated category: it carries a shift functor [1] and a class of distinguished triangles X → Y → Z → X[1] satisfying Verdier’s axioms (TR1–TR4). Distinguished triangles encode long exact sequences of cohomology; for any cohomological functor H• the triangle yields an exact sequence … → Hi(X) → Hi(Y) → Hi(Z) → Hi+1(X) → … .

A key technical tool is the existence of enough K‑injective (or K‑projective) resolutions, which allows one to compute derived functors such as RHom and L⊗ inside D(A) without leaving the triangulated setting. For a ring R, the bounded derived category Db(mod‑R) of finitely generated modules is central to representation theory: its Grothendieck group recovers the usual K0, and its triangulated structure reflects exact sequences of modules. The derived Morita theorem states that two rings are derived equivalent iff their derived categories of modules are equivalent; this equivalence preserves many invariants (global dimension, Hochschild cohomology, etc.) but not the ring itself.

Triangulated categories also admit t‑structures, which are pairs of full subcategories (D≤0, D≥0) satisfying orthogonality and stability under shift. The heart of a t‑structure is an abelian category; for the standard t‑structure on D(A) the heart is A itself. Tilting theory studies equivalences between hearts of different t‑structures, providing a powerful method to relate module categories of different algebras. In algebraic geometry, the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves Db(Coh X) on a smooth projective variety X carries a Serre functor S(−) = (−) ⊗ ωX[dim X], and Bondal–Orlov reconstruction shows that X can be recovered from Db(Coh X) when the canonical or anti‑canonical bundle is ample.

Finally, the language of ∞‑categories (quasi‑categories) refines triangulated categories by retaining higher homotopical data; the stable ∞‑category underlying D(A) encodes mapping spectra and allows a functorial calculus of limits and colimits that is impossible in the purely triangulated setting. This higher‑categorical viewpoint underlies modern approaches to derived algebraic geometry and topological field theory.

Tensor Categories and Fusion Categories

A tensor category over a field k is a k‑linear abelian rigid monoidal category with finite‑dimensional Hom spaces, simple unit object, and exact tensor product. Rigidity means every object X has a left dual X* and a right dual *X with evaluation and coevaluation morphisms satisfying the snake identities. When the category is semisimple (every object is a direct sum of simples) and has finitely many isomorphism classes of simple objects, it is called a fusion category. The Grothendieck ring of a fusion category is a based ring with non‑negative integer structure constants (fusion rules).

Fundamental examples include the representation category Rep(G) of a finite group G (a symmetric fusion category), the category of finite‑dimensional comodules over a finite‑dimensional Hopf algebra, and the categories of representations of quantum groups at roots of unity (modular tensor categories). The Drinfeld centre Z(C) of a fusion category C is a braided fusion category; if C is spherical, Z(C) is modular, i.e. its S‑matrix is invertible. Modular tensor categories provide the algebraic data for (2+1)‑dimensional topological quantum field theories and anyon models in condensed matter physics.

Classification results are deep: fusion categories of rank ≤ 4 over ℂ have been classified. The classification of integral fusion categories (all Frobenius‑Perron dimensions integers) remains open beyond rank 5. Ocneanu rigidity implies that a fusion category has only finitely many inequivalent module categories (and hence finitely many fiber functors). The notion of a module category over a tensor category generalises group actions: a module category M over C is a category equipped with an exact bifunctor C × M → M satisfying associativity and unit constraints. The category of module endofunctors FunC(M, M) is again a fusion category, the “dual” of C with respect to M.

Weak Hopf algebras and Hopf algebroids arise as the Tannakian reconstruction of fiber functors on fusion categories; the reconstruction theorem says a fusion category with a fiber functor to Veck is equivalent to Rep(H) for a unique (up to isomorphism) semisimple weak Hopf algebra H. This bridges categorical and Hopf‑algebraic viewpoints and underlies the classification of low‑dimensional fusion categories via their representation theory.

Modular Representation Theory of Finite Groups

Modular representation theory studies representations of a finite group G over a field k of characteristic p dividing |G|. Unlike the semisimple case (characteristic zero or coprime to |G|), the group algebra kG is not semisimple; indecomposable modules need not be irreducible. The block theory of Brauer decomposes kG into a direct sum of indecomposable two‑sided ideals (blocks) B = kG·e where e are primitive central idempotents. Each block has a defect group D (a p‑subgroup of G) controlling its representation theory: the number of irreducible modules in the block equals the number of p‑regular conjugacy classes in the block, and the dimensions of projective indecomposable modules are multiples of |D|.

The Green correspondence gives a bijection between indecomposable modules of a block with defect group D and indecomposable modules of the Brauer correspondent block in NG(D) with the same vertex. Vertices and sources classify indecomposables up to projective equivalence; a module has vertex Q (a p‑subgroup) if it is a direct summand of IndQG(M) for some kQ-module M with no proper p‑subgroup vertex. The Alperin weight conjecture (proved for many families) predicts that the number of irreducible Brauer characters in a block equals the number of conjugacy classes of weights (pairs (Q, φ) where Q is a p‑subgroup and φ a projective character of NG(Q)/Q).

Projective covers and injective hulls exist for every module because kG is a finite‑dimensional algebra. The Cartan matrix C of a block records the composition multiplicities of projective indecomposables; its determinant is a power of p (the determinant conjecture, proved by Brauer). The decomposition matrix D relates ordinary irreducible characters (characteristic zero) to Brauer characters; the relation C = DTD holds. The Brauer character of a module is obtained by lifting eigenvalues of p‑regular elements to complex roots of unity; it is a class function on p‑regular elements and satisfies orthogonality relations analogous to the ordinary case.

Landmark results include the Fong–Swan theorem (every irreducible Brauer character lifts to an ordinary character in characteristic zero), the Alperin–Broué conjecture on local control of blocks, and the classification of blocks with cyclic defect groups (Brauer–Dade theory) where the Brauer tree encodes the entire module category. For blocks with Klein four defect groups, the classification by Erdmann shows three Morita equivalence classes (the principal block of A5, the principal block of A4, and a third exotic type). These classifications are essential for the modular representation theory of finite simple groups.

Noncommutative Geometry and Spectral Triples

Noncommutative geometry, pioneered by Connes, replaces a topological space X by a spectral triple (A, H, D) where A is a *‑algebra represented on a Hilbert space H and D is an unbounded self‑adjoint operator with compact resolvent such that [D, a] is bounded for all a in a dense subalgebra. The commutative prototype: A = C∞(M) for a compact spin manifold M, H = L2(M, S) the spinor bundle, and D the Dirac operator. Connes’ reconstruction theorem states that a commutative spectral triple satisfying certain axioms (orientability, Poincaré duality, regularity, finiteness, reality, first order) comes from a unique spinc manifold.

The distance formula d(x, y) = sup{|a(x) − a(y)| : ‖[D, a]‖ ≤ 1} recovers the geodesic distance on M. In the noncommutative setting this defines a metric on the state space of A. Cyclic cohomology plays the role of de Rham cohomology; the Chern character maps K‑theory of A to cyclic cohomology, and the index theorem becomes the pairing Index(D) = ⟨Ch(e), Ch(D)⟩ for an idempotent e in a matrix algebra over A. The local index formula expresses this pairing as a residue of a zeta function ζD(s) = Tr(|D|−s).

Key examples beyond manifolds include the noncommutative torus Aθ (generated by unitaries U, V with VU = e2πiθUV), the quantum group SUq(2), and the standard model of particle physics formulated as an almost‑commutative geometry A = C∞(M) ⊗ AF where AF = ℂ ⊕ ℍ ⊕ M3(ℂ). The spectral action Tr(f(D/Λ)) expands asymptotically to yield the Einstein–Hilbert action plus the Yang–Mills and Higgs terms, providing a geometric derivation of the Standard Model Lagrangian.

Recent developments: the classification of finite spectral triples (Krajewski diagrams), the interplay with twisted spectral triples for type III von Neumann algebras (relevant to quantum field theory at finite temperature), and the use of spectral triples in index theory for foliations and group actions (the Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients). The notion of a real spectral triple (with an antilinear isometry J implementing charge conjugation) encodes KO‑theory and is essential for the fermionic doubling problem in lattice gauge theory.

Higher Category Theory and Infinity‑Categories

Higher category theory generalises the notion of a category by allowing morphisms between morphisms (2‑morphisms), morphisms between those (3‑morphisms), and so on. An (∞,1)‑category (or ∞‑category) is a higher category where all k‑morphisms for k ≥ 2 are invertible. The leading model is Joyal’s quasi‑categories: simplicial sets satisfying the inner horn filling condition. Ordinary categories embed as nerves; the homotopy category of a quasi‑category is obtained by identifying 1‑simplices up to 2‑simplices (homotopies). Limits and colimits are defined via universal properties in the ∞‑categorical sense, yielding homotopy (co)limits that are invariant under equivalence.

The ∞‑category of spaces (Kan complexes) is the free cocompletion of the point; the ∞‑category of spectra stabilises it. Presentable ∞‑categories are those accessible and admitting all small colimits; they are precisely the localisations of presheaf ∞‑categories. The adjoint functor theorem holds: a functor between presentable ∞‑categories admits a right adjoint iff it preserves colimits. This underpins the theory of derived ∞‑categories: for a ring R, the derived ∞‑category D(R) is the ∞‑categorical localisation of chain complexes at quasi‑isomorphisms; it is stable (has a zero object, all fibers and cofibers, and the loop/suspension functors are equivalences).

Stable ∞‑categories provide the correct home for homological algebra: exact triangles are both fiber and cofiber sequences, and the mapping spectrum functor Map(X, Y) takes values in spectra, encoding higher Ext groups as homotopy groups π−nMap(X, Y). The ∞‑categorical Yoneda embedding identifies any small ∞‑category with a full subcategory of presheaves; the Ind‑completion yields the free cocompletion under filtered colimits. This machinery is essential for derived algebraic geometry, where structure sheaves take values in connective E∞‑ring spectra, and for topological field theory, where the cobordism hypothesis classifies fully extended TQFTs as symmetric monoidal functors from the (∞,n)‑category of cobordisms.

Model categories (Quillen) present ∞‑categories via a combinatorial framework: a model structure on a category provides weak equivalences, fibrations, cofibrations; the underlying ∞‑category is the simplicial localisation. The equivalence between the homotopy theory of model categories and ∞‑categories (via the Dwyer–Kan localisation) allows one to transfer results. For example, the projective model structure on chain complexes presents D(R), and the injective model structure on simplicial presheaves presents the ∞‑topos of ∞‑stacks. These tools are now standard in modern algebraic geometry, representation theory, and mathematical physics.

  • Assuming every fusion category is the representation category of a Hopf algebra — only those admitting a fiber functor to Vec are Tannakian; many fusion categories (e.g. Haagerup) have no fiber functor.
  • Treating Brauer characters as ordinary characters on all group elements — Brauer characters are defined only on p‑regular elements and take values in a characteristic‑zero field containing appropriate roots of unity.
  • Thinking the distance formula in a spectral triple always yields a genuine metric — it defines an extended metric on the state space; for non‑unital algebras or non‑compact spaces the distance may be infinite.
  • Believing that (∞,1)‑categories are just categories enriched in spaces — enrichment captures only the mapping spaces, not the higher coherence data; quasi‑categories encode all higher homotopies.
  • Assuming every stable ∞‑category comes from a triangulated category — the triangulated category is the homotopy category of the stable ∞‑category, but the ∞‑categorical structure (mapping spectra) contains strictly more information.
  • Overlooking the role of the reality operator J in a real spectral triple — J implements KO‑theory and is necessary for the correct fermionic action in the spectral Standard Model.
The advanced topics above extend the algebraic toolkit far beyond the classical syllabus and appear regularly in research‑level examinations.

Étale Cohomology and the Weil Conjectures

Étale cohomology, developed by Grothendieck and Artin in the 1960s, provides a cohomology theory for algebraic varieties over arbitrary fields that mimics singular cohomology of complex varieties. For a scheme X and a torsion sheaf ℱ on the étale site Xét, the groups Hiét(X, ℱ) are defined as derived functors of global sections. When X is a smooth projective variety over a finite field 𝔽q and ℱ = ℚℓ (ℓ ≠ p = char 𝔽q), these groups are finite-dimensional ℚℓ-vector spaces carrying a continuous action of the absolute Galois group Gal(𝔽̄q/𝔽q) ≅ Ẑ, generated by the geometric Frobenius Frobq. The Weil conjectures, proved by Deligne (1974), assert: (1) Rationality — the zeta function Z(X, t) = exp(∑n≥1 #X(𝔽qn) tn/n) is a rational function; (2) Functional equation — Z(X, t) satisfies a symmetry t ↦ 1/(qdt) where d = dim X; (3) Riemann hypothesis — the eigenvalues of Frobq on Hiét(X, ℚℓ) are algebraic integers all of whose complex absolute values equal qi/2; (4) Betti numbers — the ranks of Hiét equal the Betti numbers of a complex lift. The proof uses the Lefschetz trace formula #X(𝔽q) = ∑i (−1)i Tr(Frobq | Hiét) and the hard Lefschetz theorem plus the Hodge index theorem in the étale setting. Key tools: the comparison theorem Hiét(Xℂ, ℚℓ) ≅ Hising(Xℂ, ℚℓ), the proper base change theorem, and the purity theorem for weights. Applications include the proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for modular forms (Deligne, 1971) and the Langlands correspondence for GL2 over function fields (Drinfeld, Lafforgue).

The Langlands Program — Automorphic Forms and Galois Representations

The Langlands program, initiated by Robert Langlands in 1967, proposes a vast web of conjectures connecting number theory, representation theory, and algebraic geometry. For a reductive algebraic group G over a global field F, the conjectural Langlands correspondence asserts a bijection between (i) isomorphism classes of irreducible admissible representations of G(𝔸F) occurring in the discrete spectrum of L2(G(F)\G(𝔸F)) (automorphic representations), and (ii) equivalence classes of L-homomorphisms from the conjectural Langlands group LF to the L-group LG = ∨G ⋊ Gal(F̄/F) (Galois parameters), where ∨G is the complex dual group. For G = GLn over a number field, this predicts a correspondence between cuspidal automorphic representations π = ⊗v πv and n-dimensional semisimple ℓ-adic Galois representations ρπ,ℓ : Gal(F̄/F) → GLn(ℚ̄ℓ) such that for almost all finite places v, the Satake parameter of πv matches the Frobenius conjugacy class ρπ,ℓ(Frobv). The local Langlands correspondence (proved for GLn by Harris–Taylor and Henniart, 2000) matches irreducible smooth representations of GLn(Fv) with n-dimensional Frobenius-semisimple Weil–Deligne representations of the Weil group WFv. Functoriality predicts that for any L-homomorphism LH → LG, there is a transfer of automorphic representations from H to G. The endoscopic classification (Arthur, 2013) establishes this for classical groups. The geometric Langlands program replaces Galois representations with D-modules on the moduli stack of ∨G-bundles on a curve, and automorphic representations with perverse sheaves on the same stack; the categorical equivalence was proved by Gaitsgory–Raskin (2024) for GLn.

Perfectoid Spaces and p-adic Hodge Theory

Perfectoid spaces, introduced by Peter Scholze (2012), are adic spaces that provide a geometric framework for p-adic Hodge theory, enabling the comparison of étale, de Rham, and crystalline cohomologies in families. A perfectoid field K is a complete non-archimedean field of mixed characteristic (0, p) with non-discrete valuation such that the Frobenius map 𝒪K/p → 𝒪K/p is surjective. The tilt K♭ is a perfectoid field of characteristic p with 𝒪K♭ = lim←, x↦xp 𝒪K/p; there is a canonical isomorphism Gal(K̄/K) ≅ Gal(K̄♭/K♭). A perfectoid space is an adic space locally isomorphic to Spa(R, R+) for a perfectoid Tate–Huber pair. The key theorem: for a smooth proper rigid-analytic variety X over a perfectoid field K, there is a canonical GK-equivariant isomorphism Hiét(XK̄, ℤp) ⊗ℤp BdR ≅ HidR(X/K) ⊗K BdR (the p-adic Hodge–Tate decomposition), where BdR is Fontaine's period ring. This is proved via the almost purity theorem: the map 𝒪X+ → 𝒪X+/p is almost finite étale after adjoining p-power roots. Perfectoid Shimura varieties (Scholze, Caraiani–Scholze) realise the local Langlands correspondence and the Kottwitz conjecture in the cohomology of the tower at infinite level. The Fontaine–Mazur conjecture predicts that an irreducible p-adic Galois representation ρ : Gal(F̄/F) → GLn(ℚ̄p) unramified outside a finite set and de Rham at p arises from geometry (i.e., from the étale cohomology of a smooth projective variety). The p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚp) (Colmez, 2010) matches 2-dimensional p-adic Galois representations with unitary Banach space representations of GL2(ℚp), using the (φ, Γ)-module formalism over the Robba ring.

Intersection Cohomology and the Decomposition Theorem

Intersection cohomology, developed by Goresky and MacPherson (1980), provides a cohomology theory for singular algebraic varieties that satisfies Poincaré duality and the hard Lefschetz theorem. For a complex algebraic variety X of dimension d, the intersection cohomology complexes ICX(L) are objects in the derived category of constructible sheaves Dcb(X, ℚ) characterised by support and cosupport conditions relative to a stratification. The intersection cohomology groups IHi(X, L) = ℋi(ICX(L)) carry a pure Hodge structure of weight i (Saito, 1990). The Decomposition Theorem (Beilinson–Bernstein–Deligne–Gabber, 1982) states: for a proper map f : X → Y of complex algebraic varieties with X smooth, the derived pushforward Rf*ℚX[d] splits in Dcb(Y, ℚ) as a direct sum of shifted intersection cohomology complexes: Rf*ℚX[d] ≅ ⊕i ⊕α ICZα(Lα)[−i], where Zα are the strata of a stratification of Y and Lα are semisimple local systems. This implies the hard Lefschetz theorem for IH*(X) and the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations. Applications: (1) The Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture (proved by Beilinson–Bernstein and Brylinski–Kashiwara) expresses characters of irreducible highest-weight representations of a semisimple Lie algebra in terms of intersection cohomology of Schubert varieties in the flag variety. (2) The geometric Satake equivalence (Mirković–Vilonen, 2007) identifies the category of representations of the Langlands dual group ∨G with the category of G(𝒪)-equivariant perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian GrG, with tensor product given by convolution. (3) The decomposition theorem for the Hitchin fibration (Ngô, 2010) proves the fundamental lemma in the Langlands program for Lie algebras.

Topological Cyclic Homology and the Cyclotomic Trace

Topological cyclic homology (TC), introduced by Bökstedt–Hsiang–Madsen (1993) and refined by Nikolaus–Scholze (2018), is a refinement of topological Hochschild homology (THH) that approximates algebraic K-theory via the cyclotomic trace map K(R) → TC(R). For a ring R, THH(R) is the homotopy colimit of the cyclic bar construction in spectra, carrying an action of the circle group S1. The cyclotomic structure provides maps φp : THH(R)Cp → THH(R) for each prime p, where Cp ⊂ S1 is the subgroup of p-th roots of unity. TC(R) is defined as the homotopy limit TC(R) = lim(φ, R) THH(R)Cpn over the Frobenius φ and restriction R maps. The cyclotomic trace trc : K(R) → TC(R) factors the Dennis trace K(R) → HH(R) through the comparison THH(R) → HH(R). The Dundas–Goodwillie–McCarthy theorem (2012) states that for a nilpotent extension R → S of connective ring spectra, the square relating K, TC, and THH is cartesian. The main theorem (Hesselholt–Madsen, 1997; Geisser–Hesselholt, 2006; Bhatt–Morrow–Scholze, 2019) identifies TC of perfectoid rings with prismatic cohomology: for a perfectoid ring R with tilt R♭, there is an equivalence TC(R)∧p ≃ ΔR where ΔR is the prismatic complex. This yields a p-adic comparison isomorphism K(R)∧p ≃ TC(R)∧p for regular noetherian 𝔽p-algebras (the K(π,1) property). The cyclotomic trace is the p-adic analogue of the Chern character; it detects p-torsion in K-theory that the Chern character misses. Applications include the computation of K-theory of truncated polynomial algebras, the proof of the K(π,1) conjecture for smooth varieties over finite fields, and the formulation of the motivic spectral sequence converging to K-theory from motivic cohomology.

Cluster Algebras and Their Combinatorial Structure

Cluster algebras, introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky in 2002, provide a combinatorial framework for studying algebras generated by a distinguished set of variables called cluster variables. A cluster algebra is defined by an initial seed consisting of a cluster (a finite set of algebraically independent variables) and an exchange matrix encoding mutation rules. Mutating at a cluster variable replaces it with a new variable determined by the exchange relation, producing a new seed. The Laurent phenomenon guarantees that every cluster variable is a Laurent polynomial in the variables of any given cluster, with coefficients in the ground ring.

Classification of Finite Type

Fomin and Zelevinsky classified cluster algebras of finite type — those with finitely many cluster variables — by showing they correspond bijectively to Dynkin diagrams of finite‑dimensional simple Lie algebras. The exchange matrix of a finite‑type cluster algebra is mutation‑equivalent to a skew‑symmetrizable matrix whose associated Cartan matrix is of finite type. This classification links cluster algebras to representation theory of quivers, where cluster variables correspond to indecomposable rigid modules over the path algebra of the quiver.

Applications to Poisson Geometry and Teichmüller Theory

Cluster algebras arise naturally as coordinate rings of Poisson varieties. The cluster structure on the coordinate ring of a double Bruhat cell in a semisimple Lie group yields a compatible Poisson bracket. In Teichmüller theory, the lambda‑length coordinates of Penner on the decorated Teichmüller space of a surface form a cluster algebra of type determined by the triangulation of the surface. Mutations correspond to flips of triangulations, and the exchange relations encode Ptolemy relations for lambda‑lengths.

Tropical Geometry and Piecewise‑Linear Algebra

Tropical geometry replaces the usual addition and multiplication over a field by the operations of minimum (or maximum) and addition, respectively. The tropical semiring (ℝ ∪ {∞}, ⊕, ⊗) with a ⊕ b = min(a,b) and a ⊗ b = a + b turns algebraic varieties into polyhedral complexes. Given a polynomial f = Σ cᵢ x¹ⁱ … xⁿⁱ over a valued field, its tropicalization Trop(f) is the piecewise‑linear function obtained by replacing each monomial cᵢ x¹ⁱ … xⁿⁱ with the affine linear function val(cᵢ) + i₁ y₁ + … + iₙ yₙ and taking the minimum. The tropical hypersurface is the corner locus where the minimum is attained at least twice.

Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry

The fundamental theorem states that the tropicalization of an algebraic variety over a non‑archimedean field coincides with the set of valuations of its points, and it carries a natural polyhedral fan structure. For a subvariety X ⊂ (K*)ⁿ, Trop(X) is a rational polyhedral complex of pure dimension dim X, balanced at each codimension‑one face. This balance condition is the tropical analogue of the algebraic condition that the sum of residues of a rational function on a curve is zero.

Enumerative Applications

Tropical methods have solved enumerative problems such as counting plane curves of given degree and genus through prescribed points. Mikhalkin’s correspondence theorem equates the number of complex curves with the number of tropical curves weighted by multiplicities derived from lattice geometry. The tropical approach reduces the problem to counting certain lattice paths in a Newton polygon, making computations feasible for high degrees where classical methods become intractable.

Worked Example: Tropical Line Counting
Count the number of tropical lines in ℝ² passing through three generic points (0,0), (1,2), (3,1). A tropical line is the corner locus of a tropical polynomial of degree 1, i.e. min(a + x, b + y, c). Determine the number of such lines and their combinatorial types.
A tropical line is determined by three coefficients (a,b,c) up to simultaneous addition of a constant. The condition that the line passes through a point (x₀,y₀) means the minimum among a+x₀, b+y₀, c is attained at least twice. For three generic points this yields a system of piecewise‑linear equations. Solving shows there is exactly one tropical line through the three points, and its Newton polygon is the standard triangle with vertices (0,0), (1,0), (0,1). The unique line has a trivalent vertex at the intersection of the three rays, and its combinatorial type is the unique trivalent tree with three leaves. Hence the answer is 1.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a tropical count, first write the tropical polynomial with unknown coefficients, impose the incidence conditions as equalities of minima, and solve the resulting linear inequalities. The number of solutions equals the number of combinatorial types; multiplicities are computed from lattice areas of dual polygons.

Iwasawa Theory and p‑adic L‑functions

Iwasawa theory studies the growth of class groups in towers of number fields, most notably the cyclotomic ℤₚ‑extension of a number field K. Let K∞ be the union of fields Kₙ obtained by adjoining all pⁿ‑th roots of unity. The Galois group Γ = Gal(K∞/K) is isomorphic to the additive group of p‑adic integers ℤₚ. The projective limit of the p‑parts of the class groups, X = lim← Cl(Kₙ)[p^∞], becomes a compact module over the Iwasawa algebra Λ = ℤₚ[[Γ]] ≅ ℤₚ[[T]]. The structure theorem for finitely generated torsion Λ‑modules yields invariants λ, μ, ν such that the order of the p‑part of Cl(Kₙ) grows as pλ n + μ pⁿ + ν for large n.

Main Conjecture

The Iwasawa main conjecture, proved by Mazur and Wiles for ℚ and by Wiles for totally real fields, asserts that the characteristic ideal of X as a Λ‑module is generated by the p‑adic L‑function Lₚ(s, χ) interpolating the special values of complex Dirichlet L‑functions. In modern language, the conjecture identifies the Selmer group of a p‑adic Galois representation with the module generated by the p‑adic L‑function, providing a bridge between algebraic and analytic objects.

Extensions to Elliptic Curves

For an elliptic curve E over ℚ with good ordinary reduction at p, the Selmer group Selₚ^∞(E/K∞) is a Λ‑module whose characteristic ideal is conjecturally generated by the p‑adic L‑function Lₚ(E, s) constructed by Mazur, Tate, and Teitelbaum. The work of Kato, Skinner–Urban, and others has established one divisibility in many cases, and the full main conjecture is known for a large class of modular elliptic curves. These results underpin the proof of the Birch and Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture in rank zero and one cases.

Derived Deformation Theory and the Cotangent Complex

Derived deformation theory, developed by Illusie, Quillen, and later by Lurie, replaces classical deformation functors with functors on the ∞‑category of derived Artinian rings. The central object is the cotangent complex LA/k of a commutative algebra A over a base ring k, which lives in the derived category D(A). For a morphism A → B, the cotangent complex controls the obstruction theory for lifting maps along square‑zero extensions. Its cohomology groups satisfy H⁰(LA/k) ≅ ΩA/k (the module of Kähler differentials) and H⁻¹(LA/k) classifies infinitesimal automorphisms.

Obstruction Theory

Given a deformation problem represented by a functor F : Art_k → Set, the tangent space is F(k[ε]) and obstructions lie in Ext²_A(LA/k, M) for a square‑zero extension with kernel M. When the cotangent complex is perfect of Tor‑amplitude [−1,0], the deformation functor is pro‑representable by a derived Artinian ring, and the associated derived moduli stack carries a (−1)‑shifted symplectic structure in the sense of Pantev–Toën–Vaquié–Vezzosi. This derived enhancement resolves classical issues such as non‑reducedness of moduli spaces and provides a natural setting for virtual fundamental classes.

Applications to Moduli of Sheaves

For a smooth projective variety X, the derived moduli stack of perfect complexes Perf(X) has cotangent complex at an object E given by RHom(E,E)[1]. The (−1)‑shifted symplectic structure yields a Behrend‑function weighted Euler characteristic that computes Donaldson–Thomas invariants. Joyce’s wall‑crossing formula for these invariants is naturally expressed in the language of derived Artin stacks and their shifted symplectic structures.

Shimura Varieties and the Langlands Correspondence

Shimura varieties are higher‑dimensional analogues of modular curves, attached to a reductive algebraic group G over ℚ together with a conjugacy class of homomorphisms h : ℂ → Gℝ satisfying Deligne’s axioms. The complex points form a disjoint union of locally symmetric spaces Γ X where X = G(ℝ)/K is a Hermitian symmetric domain and Γ ⊂ G(ℚ) is an arithmetic subgroup. By the Baily–Borel theorem, Shimura varieties admit canonical models over number fields (the reflex fields), and their ℓ‑adic étale cohomology carries a natural action of G(𝔸_f) × Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ).

Langlands–Kottwitz Method

The Langlands–Kottwitz method expresses the trace of a Hecke operator on the cohomology of a Shimura variety as a sum over elliptic endoscopic groups, linking the cohomology to automorphic representations of G. The Kottwitz conjecture, proved by Kottwitz for PEL‑type Shimura varieties and by Shin for abelian type, gives a precise formula for the alternating trace of Frobenius on the ℓ‑adic cohomology in terms of stable orbital integrals on endoscopic groups. This formula is the geometric side of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula for G.

Realisation of the Local Langlands Correspondence

Harris–Taylor and Henniart used the cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower (a p‑adic analogue of a Shimura variety for GLₙ) to prove the local Langlands correspondence for GLₙ over a p‑adic field. The correspondence matches irreducible admissible representations of GLₙ(F) with n‑dimensional Frobenius‑semisimple Weil–Deligne representations of the Weil group W_F. The global Langlands correspondence for GLₙ over a number field would imply a decomposition of the cohomology of Shimura varieties into automorphic representations, a central goal of the Langlands program.

Gröbner Bases and Computational Commutative Algebra

Gröbner bases transform the ideal membership problem in a polynomial ring k[x1, …, xn] into a division algorithm with respect to a monomial order. Fix a monomial order > (lexicographic, graded reverse lexicographic, or a block order). For an ideal I = ⟨f1, …, fs⟩, a finite subset G ⊂ I is a Gröbner basis if the leading monomials LM(G) generate the leading ideal LM(I). Buchberger’s algorithm constructs G by iteratively adding S-polynomials S(f, g) = (LCM/LT(f))f − (LCM/LT(g))g until all S-polynomials reduce to zero. The algorithm terminates by Dickson’s lemma (equivalently, Hilbert’s basis theorem). A reduced Gröbner basis is unique for a fixed order, providing a canonical representation of I.

Lexicographic order enables elimination: if G is a Gröbner basis for I with x1 > … > xn, then G ∩ k[xk+1, …, xn] is a Gröbner basis for the elimination ideal I ∩ k[xk+1, …, xn]. This solves implicitization and projection problems. Graded reverse lexicographic (grevlex) typically yields smaller bases and faster computation; one computes in grevlex and converts to lexicographic via the FGLM algorithm when the quotient is zero-dimensional. The complexity of Buchberger’s algorithm is doubly exponential in the worst case (Mayr–Meyer), but Faugère’s F4 and F5 algorithms use linear algebra on Macaulay matrices to achieve single-exponential behaviour for generic systems. Syzygy modules are computed by lifting the syzygies of the leading monomials via Schreyer’s theorem; this yields free resolutions and enables computation of Ext, Tor, and local cohomology. Standard bases extend the theory to local rings (negative weights) and power series rings, essential for singularity theory. Implementations in Singular, Macaulay2, and SageMath make these algorithms routine for systems with dozens of variables and hundreds of polynomials.

Arakelov Theory and Arithmetic Intersection Theory

Arakelov theory compactifies the arithmetic surface Spec OK of a number field K by adding the archimedean places as “fibres at infinity”. An Arakelov divisor is a pair (D, g) where D is a finite divisor on Spec OK and g is a Green function on the Riemann surface Xσ = K ⊗σ ℂ for each embedding σ : K → ℂ, satisfying Δg = δD − μ with μ the canonical (Arakelov) volume form. The intersection pairing on Arakelov divisors is defined by (D1, g1) · (D2, g2) = ∑v finite ordv(f1) ordv(f2) log Nv + ∑σ ∫Xσ g1 Δg2. This pairing is symmetric, bilinear, and satisfies an arithmetic Hodge index theorem: the intersection form on the orthogonal complement of the canonical divisor is negative definite. Faltings used this to prove the Mordell conjecture (1983): a curve of genus ≥ 2 over a number field has only finitely many rational points. The arithmetic Riemann–Roch theorem of Gillet and Soulé (1990) computes the determinant of cohomology of a hermitian vector bundle Ē on an arithmetic variety: log ‖det RΓ(Ē)‖ = Ē · T + ∫X(ℂ) ch(Ē) Td(TX) + analytic torsion. Heights of subvarieties are defined via arithmetic intersection numbers; the Bogomolov conjecture (proved by Ullmo and Zhang) states that a subvariety of an abelian variety has small points only if it is a translate of an abelian subvariety by a torsion point. Arakelov theory also underlies the arithmetic intersection formulas for CM cycles on Shimura varieties (Kudla–Rapoport–Yang) and the Colmez conjecture on Faltings heights of CM abelian varieties.

Worked Computation: Arakelov Intersection on ℙ1ℤ
Let D1 = (0) − (∞) and D2 = (1) − (∞) be divisors on ℙ1ℤ. Equip the archimedean fibre ℙ1(ℂ) with the Fubini–Study metric. Compute the Arakelov intersection number (D̂1, D̂2) where D̂i = (Di, gi) and gi is the Green function with logarithmic singularity along Di normalised by ∫ gi μ = 0.
The finite intersection is zero because the divisors meet only at ∞ with multiplicity 1, but the local intersection multiplicity at ∞ is 1 and the fibre is ℤ, so the finite contribution is 0·log N = 0. On the archimedean fibre, the Green function for a point a ∈ ℙ1(ℂ) is ga(z) = −log |z − a|2 / (1 + |z|2) + constant. The Arakelov intersection is ∫ℙ1 g0 Δg1. Since Δg1 = δ1 − μ, the integral equals g0(1) − ∫ g0 μ. By normalisation ∫ g0 μ = 0. Direct computation gives g0(1) = −log |1|2/2 + log 2 = log 2. Hence (D̂1, D̂2) = log 2.
Exam Strategy: Recognising Arakelov Setups
When a problem mentions “archimedean places”, “Green functions”, “Fubini–Study metric”, or “arithmetic intersection”, immediately identify the underlying arithmetic variety (usually Spec OK or a regular model). Write the Arakelov divisor as a pair (finite part, Green function). The finite intersection is a sum over primes of valuation products times log norms; the archimedean part is an integral of a Green function against a Laplacian. For ℙ1, the Green function is always a logarithm of a distance function normalised by the canonical measure. Memorise the standard Green function on ℙ1(ℂ) and its integral properties — they appear repeatedly in height computations.

Motivic Cohomology and the Category of Mixed Motives

Motivic cohomology provides a universal cohomology theory for smooth schemes over a field k, unifying algebraic cycles, algebraic K-theory, and étale cohomology. Voevodsky constructed the triangulated category of geometric motives DMgm(k) and its derived version DM(k) using 𝔸1-homotopy theory and Nisnevich sheaves with transfers. For a smooth scheme X, motivic cohomology groups are defined as Hp,q(X, ℤ) = HomDM(k)(ℤ(q)[p], M(X)), where ℤ(q) is the Tate motive and M(X) is the motive of X. These groups coincide with Bloch’s higher Chow groups CHq(X, 2q−p) and satisfy the expected functorialities: contravariant for morphisms, covariant with transfers for finite correspondences, homotopy invariance, and a Mayer–Vietoris sequence for Nisnevich covers. The Beilinson–Lichtenbaum conjecture, proved by Voevodsky and Rost (using the norm residue isomorphism theorem), states that the motivic cohomology with finite coefficients ℤ/n agrees with étale cohomology in weights ≤ degree: Hp,q(X, ℤ/n) ≅ Hpét(X, μn⊗q) for p ≤ q. This identifies the motivic spectral sequence E2p,q = Hp,q(X, ℤ) ⇒ K−p−q(X) with the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence for algebraic K-theory. The conjectural abelian category of mixed motives MM(k) would be a Tannakian category over ℚ with a fibre functor to graded vector spaces given by Betti, de Rham, or ℓ-adic realisations. The motivic Galois group would control all relations among periods of algebraic varieties. The standard conjectures on algebraic cycles (Lefschetz type, Hodge type, Künneth type) imply the existence of MM(k) with the expected properties. Voevodsky’s category DM(k) provides a triangulated approximation; the heart of the motivic t-structure (if it exists) would be MM(k). The motivic cohomology of a point Spec k recovers Milnor K-theory: Hq,q(Spec k, ℤ) ≅ KqM(k), and the norm residue isomorphism KqM(k)/n ≅ Hq(k, μn⊗q) is the Milnor–Bloch–Kato conjecture.

Prismatic Cohomology and the Hodge–Tate Comparison

Prismatic cohomology, introduced by Bhatt and Scholze (2019), provides a unified integral p-adic cohomology theory for formal schemes over 𝒪K where K is a p-adic field. A prism is a pair (A, I) where A is a δ-ring (a ring with a Frobenius lift δ : A → A satisfying δ(x+y) = δ(x)+δ(y)+(xp+yp−(x+y)p)/p) and I ⊂ A is an ideal such that A is (p, I)-complete, p ∈ I + pA, and A/I is p-torsion-free. The key example is Ainf = W(𝒪C♭) with I = (ξ) where ξ = [π♭] − p for a compatible system π♭ of p-power roots of a uniformiser π. The prismatic site (𝔛/𝔸)△ has objects prisms (A, I) with a map 𝔸 → A/I; the structure sheaf 𝔒△ sends (A, I) to A. The prismatic cohomology Δ𝔛/𝔸 = RΓ((𝔛/𝔸)△, 𝔒△) is a derived (p, I)-complete complex of A-modules equipped with a Frobenius φ△ : Δ𝔛/𝔸 → Δ𝔛/𝔸 which is φ-linear and satisfies φ△(I·x) = p·φ△(x). The Hodge–Tate comparison theorem states that for a p-adic formal scheme 𝔛 over 𝔸 = (Ainf, (ξ)), there is a canonical isomorphism Δ𝔛/𝔸 ⊗𝔸𝕃 Ainf/(ξ) ≅ ⨁i Ω𝔛/𝒪Ki[−i] in the derived category, where the right side is the Hodge–Tate complex. The Nygaard filtration N≥jΔ𝔛/𝔸 = {x : φ△(x) ∈ IjΔ𝔛/𝔸} satisfies grjN Δ𝔛/𝔸 ≅ Ω𝔛/𝒪Kj[−j] after base change to Ainf/(ξ). This recovers the de Rham cohomology via the crystalline prism (Acrys, (p)) and the étale cohomology via the étale prism. Prismatic F-crystals (modules with Frobenius over the prismatic site) correspond to p-adic Galois representations via the prismatic F-crystal associated to a representation. The theory gives an integral version of the p-adic Hodge theory comparison isomorphisms and underlies the recent proof of the integral p-adic Hodge conjecture for smooth proper varieties over 𝒪K (Bhatt–Lurie–Mathew).

Algebraic Cycles, Chow Groups, and the Bloch–Beilinson Conjectures

The Chow group CHk(X) of a smooth projective variety X over a field k is the group of codimension-k algebraic cycles modulo rational equivalence. The intersection product makes ⊕k CHk(X) a graded ring. Cycle class maps to cohomology theories (Betti, de Rham, ℓ-adic étale, crystalline) are ring homomorphisms cl : CHk(X) → H2k(X)(k). The kernel consists of homologically trivial cycles; the Griffiths group Griffk(X) = ker(cl) / CHk(X)alg measures the difference between homological and algebraic equivalence. The Hodge conjecture predicts that for X over ℂ, the cycle class map to Hodge classes Hk,k(X) ∩ H2k(X, ℚ) is surjective. The Tate conjecture predicts surjectivity onto Galois-invariant classes in ℓ-adic cohomology for varieties over finitely generated fields. Bloch and Beilinson conjectured a descending filtration F• on CHk(X)ℚ such that F0 = CHk, F1 = ker(cl), and the graded pieces grjF CHk are controlled by motivic cohomology: grjF CHk(X)ℚ ≅ H2k−j, k(X, ℚ). The conjectural category of mixed motives would realise this filtration as the weight filtration. Murre’s conjectures (1990) give a more explicit splitting: there should exist orthogonal projectors πi in CHdim X(X × X) inducing a Künneth decomposition of the diagonal, such that πi acts as zero on Hj for j ≠ i and the induced filtration on Chow groups satisfies Fj CHk = ker(π0 + … + π2k−j). For curves, F2 CH1 = 0 and F1 CH1 = CH10 (degree-zero divisors). For surfaces, the Bloch–Beilinson filtration on CH2 is F0 = CH2, F1 = ker(deg), F2 = CH20 (Albanese kernel), F3 = 0. The regulator maps from higher Chow groups to Deligne cohomology (or syntomic cohomology in the p-adic case) compute the graded pieces and relate them to special values of L-functions via Beilinson’s conjectures. The finite-dimensionality of grjF CHk is equivalent to the finite generation of motivic cohomology groups, a major open problem.

  • Assuming the Arakelov intersection pairing is positive definite — the arithmetic Hodge index theorem states it is negative definite on the orthogonal complement of the canonical divisor, a crucial sign for height bounds.
  • Treating motivic cohomology as a single theory — it is bigraded Hp,q with weight q and degree p; the Beilinson–Lichtenbaum isomorphism with étale cohomology holds only for p ≤ q and finite coefficients.
  • Believing prismatic cohomology is just crystalline cohomology with a Frobenius — the prismatic site includes many prisms (crystalline, étale, Hodge–Tate, Breuil–Kisin) and the theory interpolates between them via base change along maps of prisms.
  • Assuming the Bloch–Beilinson filtration is known to exist — it is a deep conjecture; only the first two steps (F0, F1) are unconditional, and Murre’s conjectures provide a conditional splitting that implies the full filtration.
  • Neglecting the distinction between algebraic and homological equivalence — the Griffiths group can be non-zero (e.g., for certain threefolds), and the Hodge/Tate conjectures concern the image of the cycle class map, not the Chow group itself.
  • Using the wrong Green function normalisation in Arakelov theory — the canonical Green function satisfies ∫ g μ = 0 with μ the Arakelov (1,1)-form; omitting this normalisation changes intersection numbers by constants that affect height computations.

Hecke Algebras and the Kazhdan–Lusztig Basis

The Hecke algebra H(W) of a Coxeter group W with parameter q is the associative algebra over ℤ[q,q−1] generated by symbols Ts for each simple reflection s∈S, subject to the quadratic relation (Ts−q)(Ts+1)=0 and the braid relations TsTtTs⋯ = TtTsTt⋯ (with mst factors on each side). When q=1 the algebra specialises to the group algebra ℤ[W]. The Kazhdan–Lusztig basis {Cw | w∈W} is characterised by two properties: each Cw is invariant under the bar involution (which sends q↦q−1 and Ts↦Ts−1) and its expansion in the standard basis Tw has coefficients in qℤ[q] with leading term Tw. The transition polynomials Py,w(q) defined by Cw = ∑y≤w Py,w(q) Ty are the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials; they have non‑negative integer coefficients and satisfy Pw,w=1, deg Py,w ≤ (ℓ(w)−ℓ(y)−1)/2 for y<w. These polynomials control the composition factors of Verma modules in category 𝒪 for a complex semisimple Lie algebra: the multiplicity of the simple module L(y·λ) in the Verma module M(w·λ) is given by a coefficient of Py,w(q). For the symmetric group Sn, the Hecke algebra at generic q is semisimple and its irreducible representations are indexed by partitions of n; at a root of unity the representation theory becomes modular and connects to the representation theory of quantum groups Uq(𝔰𝔩n). The Kazhdan–Lusztig basis also gives a canonical basis for the quantum group Uq(𝔤) via Lusztig’s quantum enveloping algebra, and the structure constants of this basis are the same polynomials. In the geometric setting, the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials compute the stalk cohomology of intersection cohomology complexes on Schubert varieties in the flag variety G/B, providing a bridge between algebraic combinatorics, representation theory, and topology.

Geometric Langlands Correspondence and D‑Modules

The geometric Langlands program, initiated by Drinfeld for GL2 over function fields and formulated by Beilinson and Drinfeld for curves over ℂ, replaces the Galois group of a number field by the fundamental group of a smooth projective curve X over ℂ, and replaces automorphic representations by D-modules on the moduli stack BunG of principal G-bundles on X. For a reductive group G with Langlands dual G∨, the conjecture asserts an equivalence of derived categories D-mod(BunG) ≃ QCoh(LocSysG∨), where LocSysG∨ is the moduli stack of G∨-local systems (flat G∨-bundles) on X. The functor is expected to be given by a kernel — the Hecke eigensheaf — which is a D-module on BunG × LocSysG∨ satisfying Hecke eigenproperty: for each point x∈X and representation V of G∨, the Hecke correspondence Hx,V acting on BunG yields an isomorphism Hx,V(F) ≅ F ⊠ VE where E is the local system corresponding to the point in LocSysG∨. The abelian case G=GL1 reduces to the Fourier–Mukai transform on the Jacobian of X, a classical result of Rothstein and Laumon. For GLn, the classical Langlands correspondence over function fields was proved by Lafforgue; the geometric Langlands correspondence over ℂ has been established in the tamely ramified case by Gaitsgory and collaborators using the theory of ind‑coherent sheaves and the Whittaker model. The categorical formulation involves the Hecke category acting on D-mod(BunG) and the Satake equivalence identifying the spherical Hecke category with Rep(G∨). Recent work of Arinkin and Gaitsgory advances the equivalence for singular local systems using the nilpotent cone and the Springer resolution. The geometric Langlands correspondence also predicts a spectral decomposition of the space of automorphic forms in terms of periods of D-modules, linking to the classical Langlands program via the function‑field trace formula.

Worked Example: Kazhdan–Lusztig Polynomial for S₃
Compute the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomial Pe,w₀(q) for the longest element w₀ = (1 3) in the symmetric group S₃ with simple reflections s₁=(1 2), s₂=(2 3).
The Bruhat order on S₃ has elements e, s₁, s₂, s₁s₂, s₂s₁, w₀=s₁s₂s₁=s₂s₁s₂. The Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials satisfy Px,x=1 and for x<y are determined by a recursion involving the R‑polynomials. For y=w₀ (length 3) and x=e (length 0), the interval [e,w₀] is a hexagon. Since the Schubert variety for w₀ in the flag variety of SL₃ is smooth, its intersection cohomology is trivial, so Pe,w₀(q)=1. (A common incorrect recursion yields a rational function; the correct KL recursion gives the polynomial 1.)
Exam Strategy: Recognising Hecke Algebra Specialisations
When a problem mentions a deformation parameter q and quadratic relations (T - q)(T + 1)=0, immediately identify the Hecke algebra of a Coxeter group. If q is specialised to a root of unity, the representation theory becomes modular; look for connections to quantum groups at roots of unity or to modular representation theory of the corresponding finite group. For symmetric groups, the Hecke algebra at generic q is semisimple and its irreducibles are indexed by partitions — use the Okounkov–Vershik approach or the seminormal basis. In geometric Langlands questions, the key objects are D-modules on Bun_G and quasi-coherent sheaves on LocSysG^∨; the Hecke eigensheaf condition is the defining property. Remember that the abelian case reduces to Fourier–Mukai on the Jacobian, a concrete computable instance.

Derived Algebraic Geometry and Spectral Schemes

Derived algebraic geometry replaces commutative rings by connective E∞-ring spectra (or simplicial commutative rings) to encode higher homotopical information such as derived intersections and obstruction theories. A spectral scheme is a pair (X, 𝒪X) where X is a topological space and 𝒪X is a sheaf of E∞-ring spectra locally equivalent to Spec of a connective E∞-ring. The classical truncation π0𝒪X recovers an ordinary scheme. The cotangent complex LX/S of a morphism of spectral schemes is a connective quasi-coherent complex that controls deformations: for a square-zero extension A→A⊕M (with M an A-module), the space of lifts of a map Spec A→X is the mapping space MapA(LX/S, M[1]). In particular, the derived fibre product X×hZY of two maps X→Z←Y has cotangent complex given by the homotopy pullback of the individual cotangent complexes, and its homotopy groups compute Tor groups of the structure sheaves. This resolves the excess intersection problem: if two subvarieties intersect non‑transversely, the derived intersection carries the correct virtual fundamental class. The derived moduli stack of perfect complexes on a smooth projective variety, constructed by Toën and Vezzosi, is a spectral Artin stack whose tangent complex at a point E is RHom(E,E)[1]; its truncation is the classical moduli stack of sheaves. Derived algebraic geometry also provides a natural home for the derived category of quasi-coherent sheaves: QCoh(X) is a stable presentable ∞-category, and for a spectral scheme X the functor X↦QCoh(X) satisfies étale descent. Recent advances in p-adic Hodge theory, including work of Bhatt, Lurie, Mathew, and others on prismatic cohomology, illustrate the power of derived methods in arithmetic geometry.

Modular Representation Theory of Symmetric Groups and Young Tableaux

The modular representation theory of the symmetric group Sn over a field k of characteristic p > 0 is governed by the combinatorics of p-regular partitions and the representation theory of the Hecke algebra at a primitive p-th root of unity. The Specht modules Sλ (defined over ℤ) reduce modulo p to give the Specht modules for kSn; their simple quotients Dλ are indexed by p-regular partitions λ (no part repeated p or more times). The decomposition matrix dλμ = [Sλ : Dμ] records composition multiplicities and is unitriangular with respect to the dominance order on partitions. The James conjecture (proved for large p by Fayers; the general case is approached via Soergel bimodules and the work of Williamson) predicts that the decomposition numbers for Sn in characteristic p equal the coefficients of the canonical basis of the Fock space representation of the quantum affine algebra Uq(𝔰𝔩p) at q=0. The Mullineux involution describes the effect of tensoring with the sign representation on simple modules: Dλ ⊗ sgn ≅ Dμ where μ is the Mullineux conjugate of λ, computed by a combinatorial algorithm on the p-core and p-quotient of λ. The blocks of kSn are labelled by p-cores; each block is Morita equivalent to the principal block of a smaller symmetric group via the Scopes equivalence. The graded representation theory of the cyclotomic Khovanov–Lauda–Rouquier algebras (which are isomorphic to the blocks of kSn) provides a categorification of the Fock space, yielding a graded decomposition matrix whose entries are the graded multiplicities of simple modules in graded Specht modules. The dimension of a simple module Dλ is given by the hook-length formula for the corresponding p-regular partition only in the semisimple case (p > n); in general no closed formula is known, but the dimensions can be computed recursively using the Jantzen sum formula and the Mullineux map. The representation theory of Sn also controls the cohomology of the Hilbert scheme of n points in the plane via the Nakajima quiver variety construction, linking modular representation theory to geometric representation theory.

Computational Group Theory: Algorithms for Finitely Presented Groups

Computational group theory provides algorithms to answer algorithmic questions about groups given by finite presentations ⟨X | R⟩. The Todd–Coxeter coset enumeration algorithm, given a subgroup H generated by words in X, attempts to enumerate the cosets of H in G; if it terminates, it yields the index [G:H] and the permutation representation of G on the cosets. The algorithm may not terminate for infinite index subgroups, reflecting the unsolvability of the generalised word problem. The Knuth–Bendix completion procedure attempts to transform a set of rewriting rules derived from R into a confluent rewriting system; if successful, it solves the word problem by reducing any word to a unique normal form. For automatic groups (e.g., hyperbolic groups, braid groups), the fellow‑traveller property guarantees the existence of a finite state automaton accepting the language of geodesic words, yielding a quadratic-time solution to the word problem. The Reidemeister–Schreier process computes a presentation for a subgroup of finite index from a presentation of the ambient group and a transversal. For matrix groups over finite fields, the Meataxe algorithm (Parker’s algorithm) computes the composition factors of a module by finding proper submodules via the action of random elements; it is the backbone of the classification of finite simple groups in computational form. The quotient algorithm (Holt–Rees) constructs the largest quotient of a finitely presented group satisfying a given set of laws (e.g., nilpotency class c, exponent e) by iteratively adding consequences of the laws to the relators. In practice, systems like GAP and Magma implement these algorithms with sophisticated heuristics: coset enumeration uses the Felsch strategy with subgroup generators; Knuth–Bendix uses length‑lexicographic ordering and overlap criteria; the Meataxe uses the Norton irreducibility test. The complexity of the word problem for a finitely presented group can be arbitrarily high (by the Boone–Novikov theorem there exist presentations with unsolvable word problem), but for many naturally occurring groups (linear groups, mapping class groups, fundamental groups of 3‑manifolds) the word problem is solvable in polynomial time.

  • Assuming the geometric Langlands correspondence is a theorem for all reductive groups over ℂ — it is proved only in the tamely ramified case and for certain special groups; the general case remains a conjecture.
  • Treating derived schemes as ordinary schemes with nilpotents — derived structure encodes higher homotopy groups of the structure sheaf, not just nilpotent thickenings; the cotangent complex is a complex, not a sheaf.
  • Believing the decomposition matrix of Sₙ in characteristic p is given by the hook-length formula — the hook-length formula gives dimensions of Specht modules in characteristic zero; modular decomposition numbers are governed by Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials for the affine Weyl group.
  • Expecting the Todd–Coxeter algorithm to always terminate — it terminates iff the subgroup has finite index; for infinite index it may run forever, reflecting the undecidability of the generalised word problem.
  • Using the Meataxe algorithm for groups over infinite fields — the Meataxe requires a finite field to guarantee termination of the random search for submodules; over infinite fields one must use other methods.
  • Confusing the Mullineux involution with the transpose of a partition — the Mullineux map is a non‑trivial combinatorial involution on p‑regular partitions that depends on the p‑core and p‑quotient, not simply conjugation.
