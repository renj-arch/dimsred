Model Theory in Algebra — Ultraproducts, Quantifier Elimination & Categorical Theories

Deep dive into Model Theory in Algebra — ultraproducts and Łoś's theorem (the fundamental theorem of ultraproducts, for any first-order sentence φ, ∏_U 𝔄_i ⊧ φ iff {i | 𝔄_i ⊧ φ} ∈ U), nonstandard analysis (Robinson's hyperreals *ℝ as an ultrapower of ℝ, the transfer principle, infinitesimals and infinite numbers), quantifier elimination (the theory ACF of algebraically closed fields eliminates quantifiers — every formula is equivalent to a quantifier-free formula; the theory RCF of real closed fields also eliminates quantifiers — Tarski–Seidenberg), the Ax–Kochen theorem (for p-adic fields ℚ_p, ultraproducts ∏_U ℚ_p are elementarily equivalent to ∏_U 𝔽_p((t))), Morley's categoricity theorem (if a countable first-order theory is categorical in some uncountable cardinal, it is categorical in all uncountable cardinals), stable theories (Shelah's classification, the main gap theorem, the notion of forking independence), Zariski geometries (Hrushovski–Zilber, model-theoretic geometry), and the model theory of differential fields.

Part 1

🏗️ Structures, Theories, and Compactness

First-Order
Structures, Languages, Theories, and the Compactness Theorem
A first-order language ℒ consists of constant symbols, relation symbols (each with an arity), and function symbols. An ℒ-structure 𝔄 = (A, {c^𝔄}, {R^𝔄}, {f^𝔄}) assigns a domain A and interpretations. The satisfaction relation 𝔄 ⊧ φ[s] is defined recursively. A theory T is a set of ℒ-sentences closed under logical consequence. A theory T is complete if for every sentence φ, either φ ∈ T or ¬φ ∈ T. Gödel's completeness theorem gives the equivalence of syntactic provability and semantic consequence. The compactness theorem (the most powerful tool in model theory): a theory T has a model iff every finite subset T₀ ⊆ T has a model. This is used to construct nonstandard models of arithmetic and analysis. For example, add a new constant c to the language of arithmetic with axioms c > n for all n ∈ ℕ; by compactness, there is a model of PA containing an infinite natural number (a nonstandard element larger than all standard naturals). The Löwenheim–Skolem theorems: if T has an infinite model, it has models of every infinite cardinality. The upward Löwenheim–Skolem uses compactness to build arbitrarily large models. The downward Löwenheim–Skolem uses the Skolem hull construction to produce small elementary submodels. An elementary embedding f: 𝔄 → 𝔅 preserves all first-order formulas: 𝔄 ⊧ φ(a) iff 𝔅 ⊧ φ(f(a)). The elementary chain theorem (Tarski–Vaught): the union of an elementary chain is an elementary extension of each member.
The compactness theorem is the engine of model theory — it guarantees the existence of models with desired properties and underlies the construction of nonstandard elements.
Part 2

🔭 Ultraproducts and Nonstandard Analysis

Ultraproducts
Łoś's Theorem, the Hyperreals, and the Transfer Principle
Let I be an index set and U an ultrafilter on I (a finitely additive measure of subsets of I taking values 0 or 1, containing no finite sets for non-principal ultrafilters). Given ℒ-structures 𝔄_i (i ∈ I), the ultraproduct ∏_U 𝔄_i is the quotient of the Cartesian product by the equivalence relation (a_i) ∼ (b_i) iff {i | a_i = b_i} ∈ U. Łoś's theorem (1955): for any first-order formula φ(x₁,…,x_n) and elements a^1,…,a^n ∈ ∏_U 𝔄_i, we have ∏_U 𝔄_i ⊧ φ([a^1],…,[a^n]) iff {i ∈ I | 𝔄_i ⊧ φ(a^1_i,…,a^n_i)} ∈ U. This is proved by induction on formula complexity (the atomic case follows from the definition, the quantifier step uses the ultrafilter property). Consequences: the ultraproduct of fields is a field, of ordered fields is an ordered field, etc. Robinson's nonstandard analysis: take the ultrapower *ℝ = ∏_U ℝ (with respect to a non-principal ultrafilter on ℕ). The transfer principle: any first-order statement true about ℝ is also true about *ℝ. *ℝ contains infinitesimals: the equivalence class of (1, 1/2, 1/3, …) is a positive infinitesimal ε > 0 with ε < 1/n for all n ∈ ℕ. It contains infinite numbers: (1, 2, 3, …) is greater than all standard reals. The standard part function st: *ℝ_{finite} → ℝ maps a finite hyperreal to its nearest real. Applications: ε-δ definitions in analysis become simple algebraic manipulations with infinitesimals. The hyperfinite set *{1, …, H} for infinite H ∈ *ℕ models finite combinatorics internally. Loeb measure converts internal *-finite sets into standard measure spaces. Keisler's axiom system and the SAT (saturated) property of ultrapowers.
Ultraproducts provide a uniform method for constructing saturated extensions of structures; the hyperreals give a rigorous foundation for calculus with infinitesimals.
Part 3

🔍 Quantifier Elimination and Applications

QE
Quantifier Elimination for ACF, RCF, and the Ax–Kochen Theorem
A theory T has quantifier elimination (QE) if for every formula φ(x), there exists a quantifier-free formula ψ(x) such that T ⊧ ∀x (φ(x) ↔ ψ(x)). Algebraically closed fields (ACF): proved by Tarski (1948) — every formula in the language of rings {+, −, ·, 0, 1} is equivalent modulo ACF to a Boolean combination of polynomial equations. This implies the theory ACF is complete (for each characteristic p or 0) and strongly minimal (every definable subset of a model is either finite or cofinite). The Chevalley–Tarski theorem: the image of a constructible set (a Boolean combination of Zariski closed sets) under a projection is constructible — equivalently, the theory of algebraically closed fields eliminates quantifiers. Real closed fields (RCF): the theory RCF of ordered fields where every positive element has a square root and every odd-degree polynomial has a root eliminates quantifiers (Tarski–Seidenberg, 1940). This implies the theory is complete and the Tarski's theorem on the decidability of the theory of ℝ as an ordered field. The Ax–Kochen theorem (1965, 1966): for any non-principal ultrafilter U on ℕ, the ultraproducts ∏_U ℚ_p (over all primes p) and ∏_U 𝔽_p((t)) are elementarily equivalent. This yields the Ax–Kochen isomorphism theorem: for each sentence φ in the language of valued fields, φ holds in ℚ_p for all sufficiently large p iff φ holds in 𝔽_p((t)) for all sufficiently large p. Application: the Ax–Kochen theorem on the Artin conjecture — every homogeneous polynomial of degree d over ℚ_p in n > d² variables has a nontrivial zero (for all sufficiently large p). Elimination of imaginaries (Poizat): a theory eliminates imaginaries if every definable equivalence relation has a quotient object definable as a sort. ACF and RCF eliminate imaginaries.
Quantifier elimination reduces all definable sets to simple algebraic conditions — a powerful tool for proving completeness, decidability, and structural properties.
Part 4

🌐 Categorical Theories and Zariski Geometries

Geometry
Morley's Theorem, Stable Theories, and Hrushovski–Zilber Geometry
Morley's categoricity theorem (1965): if a countable first-order theory T is κ-categorical for some uncountable cardinal κ (has exactly one model of size κ up to isomorphism), then T is λ-categorical for every uncountable λ. The proof uses prime models, saturated models, and the Vaught's test for completeness. Totally transcendental theories (those admitting a Morley rank for each definable set). The Morley rank RM(X) is a notion of dimension for definable sets in a stable theory — the smallest ordinal α such that X can be partitioned into at most ℵ₀ sets each of Morley rank < α, with the property that infinite definable sets have rank ≥ 1. Stability theory (Shelah): a theory T is λ-stable if for every model M of size λ, |S₁(M)| ≤ λ (there are at most λ complete types over M). T is stable if it is λ-stable for some λ. The Shelah classification program: theories are classified as stable, superstable, ω-stable, etc. The main gap theorem: for a countable complete theory T, either I(T, κ) = 2^κ for all uncountable κ (maximal models), or I(T, ℵ_α) < ℶ_{ω₁}(ℵ_α) for all α (few models). Zariski geometries (Hrushovski–Zilber, 1993): a mathematical structure where the sets of points are endowed with a dimension function and a topology satisfying properties analogous to the Zariski topology on algebraic varieties. A Zariski geometry has the dimension theorem (dim(X ∩ Y) ≥ dim(X) + dim(Y) − dim(Z) for irreducible Z ⊇ X ∪ Y). Hrushovski–Zilber proved that any one-dimensional Zariski geometry is either a curve over an algebraically closed field or a special non-classical type (like the Zilber field). This provides a model-theoretic characterization of algebraic curves. The Weil–Hrushovski theorem: group definable in a stable field is definably isomorphic to an algebraic group.
Morley's theorem and stable classification reveal deep combinatorial structure in complete theories; Zariski geometries show model theory can reconstruct algebraic geometry.
