Morse Theory — Critical Points, Handle Decompositions & the h-Cobordism Theorem

Deep dive into Morse Theory — the analysis of smooth manifolds via critical points of differentiable functions, the Morse lemma (near a non-degenerate critical point, the function is f = Σ ± xᵢ²), the Morse inequalities (the Betti numbers are bounded by the numbers of critical points of each index), the handle decompositions (a critical point of index λ corresponds to attaching a λ-handle D^λ × D^{n−λ}), the Reeb's theorem (if a manifold has a Morse function with exactly two critical points, it is homeomorphic to a sphere), the Morse-Bott functions (critical submanifolds with non-degenerate normal form), the Bott periodicity theorem (Bott's proof using Morse theory on the loop space of Lie groups), the h-cobordism theorem (Smale's proof using handle cancellation and the Whitney trick), and the Floer homology: the infinite-dimensional Morse theory for the action functional on the loop space.

"Morse theory is the study of the topology of a manifold through the lens of a function."

Part 1

📏 Morse Functions and the Morse Lemma

Critical Points
Morse Functions, the Morse Lemma, and Gradient Flow
A Morse function f: M → ℝ is a smooth function with all critical points non-degenerate: the Hessian H_p f (the matrix of second partials) is non-singular at each critical point p. The index λ(p) is the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian — the dimension of the subspace on which f decreases. The fundamental Morse lemma: near a non-degenerate critical point p, there exist local coordinates (x₁, …, x_n) such that f(x) = f(p) − Σ_{i=1}^λ x_i² + Σ_{i=λ+1}^n x_i². The gradient flow is the flow of −∇f (with respect to a Riemannian metric). The stable manifold W^s(p) of a critical point p is the set of points that flow to p as t → +∞; its dimension is n − λ(p). The unstable manifold W^u(p) has dimension λ(p). These are embedded disks (the Morse-Smale condition ensures transversality). The moduli space of gradient trajectories from a critical point p of index λ to q of index λ−1 is a finite set of isolated flow lines (when the metric is Morse-Smale). The Morse complex is generated by critical points with boundary operator counting these gradient lines — the beginning of Morse homology.
The Morse lemma normalizes the function near a critical point as a sum of squares with signs; the index determines the local geometry of the level sets and the topology change.
Part 2

🌍 The Morse Inequalities and Handle Decompositions

Topology
Morse Inequalities, Handle Attachment, and Handle Cancellation
Let C_λ = number of critical points of index λ of a Morse function f: M → ℝ on a closed manifold M. Let b_λ = dim H_λ(M; ℝ) be the Betti numbers. The strong Morse inequalities: for each k, C_k − C_{k−1} + … ± C₀ ≥ b_k − b_{k−1} + … ± b₀. The weak Morse inequalities: C_λ ≥ b_λ for each λ. The Euler characteristic: χ(M) = Σ (−1)^λ C_λ = Σ (−1)^λ b_λ. The handlebody decomposition: consider the sublevel sets M_c = f^{-1}(−∞, c]. When c passes a critical value of index λ (with non-degenerate critical point p), M_{c+ε} is obtained from M_{c−ε} by attaching a λ-handle D^λ × D^{n−λ} along the attaching sphere S^{λ−1} × D^{n−λ} embedded in the level set f^{-1}(c−ε). The attaching sphere is the intersection of the stable manifold of p with the level set. The core of the handle is D^λ × {0}; the co-core is {0} × D^{n−λ}. The handle cancellation (the cancellation lemma): if a λ-handle and a (λ+1)-handle have attaching spheres that intersect transversely in exactly one point, they can be cancelled, simplifying the handle decomposition. The Whitney trick makes this cancellation possible in high dimensions when the ambient manifold is simply connected — by removing excess intersection points.
The Morse inequalities relate the critical point counts to homological invariants; handle decompositions give a geometric realization of the cell complex structure of the manifold.
Part 3

🧮 The h-Cobordism Theorem

Cobordism
Smale's h-Cobordism Theorem and the Generalized Poincaré Conjecture
The h-cobordism theorem (Smale, 1961): Let W be a compact (n+1)-dimensional cobordism between two closed n-manifolds M₀ and M₁. If n ≥ 5, W is simply connected, and the inclusions M₀ ↪ W and M₁ ↪ W are homotopy equivalences (an h-cobordism), then W is diffeomorphic to the product M₀ × [0,1]. The proof proceeds by choosing a Morse function f: (W; M₀, M₁) → [0,1] with f(M₀) = 0, f(M₁) = 1, and with critical points all of indices strictly between 0 and n+1. Using handle cancellation, critical points can be eliminated in pairs (a λ-handle and a (λ+1)-handle) when their attaching spheres intersect in a single point. The Whitney trick is essential: in a simply connected manifold of dimension ≥ 5, two spheres of complementary dimension can be made disjoint by isotopy if they have zero algebraic intersection number. Repeated cancellation of handles produces a Morse function with no critical points — which implies W ≅ M₀ × [0,1]. The h-cobordism theorem implies the Poincaré conjecture in dimensions n ≥ 5: any homotopy n-sphere (n ≥ 5) is homeomorphic to S^n. Milnor's exotic spheres (1956) show the homeomorphism cannot be improved to diffeomorphism in general. The s-cobordism theorem (Siebenmann, Wall) generalizes to non-simply-connected cobordisms using the Whitehead torsion.
The h-cobordism theorem is a landmark of geometric topology — it classifies cobordisms that are homotopy products, using Morse theory to cancel critical points systematically.

🔥 Floer Homology: Infinite-Dimensional Morse Theory

Infinite Dimensions
The Action Functional, Floer Homology, and the Arnold Conjecture
Floer homology (Floer, 1988) is the infinite-dimensional Morse theory for the symplectic action functional on the loop space LM = {γ: S¹ → M} of a symplectic manifold (M, ω). For an exact symplectic manifold with ω = dλ, define A(γ) = ∫_{S¹} γ^* λ. The critical points of A are the 1-periodic orbits of the Hamiltonian vector field X_H (where H_t: M → ℝ is a time-dependent Hamiltonian). The gradient of A with respect to the L² metric is −J ∂_t (where J is an almost complex structure compatible with ω). Floer's equation: ∂_s u + J ∂_t u + ∇H_t(u) = 0 for a map u: ℝ × S¹ → M — the solutions are pseudoholomorphic cylinders connecting periodic orbits. The Floer chain complex CF_*(H) is freely generated by the 1-periodic orbits (the critical points of A). The differential counts rigid gradient trajectories (solutions to Floer's equation with index difference 1). The resulting homology HF_*(M) is independent of the Hamiltonian (invariant under continuation maps). The Arnold conjecture: a Hamiltonian symplectomorphism of a closed symplectic manifold has at least as many fixed points as the manifold has critical points of a Morse function — i.e., at least Σ b_k(M). Floer proved this for monotone symplectic manifolds using Floer homology, showing #Fix(φ_H) ≥ Σ b_k(M) (the weak Arnold conjecture). The Morse-Bott theory generalizes Morse theory to functions with non-isolated critical submanifolds — the Bott periodicity theorem (π_k(U) = π_{k+2}(U)) was proved by Bott using Morse theory on the loop space ΩU of the unitary group. The Witten deformation (Witten, 1982) gives an analytic approach to Morse inequalities: the deformed Laplacian H_t = d_t d_t^* + d_t^* d_t where d_t = e^{−tf} d e^{tf} recovers the Morse complex via small eigenvalues that concentrate near critical points — the supersymmetric quantum mechanics interpretation of the Morse complex.
Floer homology is the infinite-dimensional Morse theory of the action functional — it has revolutionized symplectic geometry, proving the Arnold conjecture and leading to new invariants like Heegaard Floer homology.

Morse‑Bott Theory and Degenerate Critical Manifolds

Classical Morse theory requires non‑degenerate isolated critical points, i.e. the Hessian is invertible. Many natural functions — moment maps, height functions on symmetric spaces, the energy functional on loop spaces — have critical sets that are smooth submanifolds. Morse‑Bott theory, developed by Bott in the 1950s, extends the Morse lemma to this setting. If f : M → ℝ is smooth and C ⊂ M is a connected component of the critical set such that the Hessian is non‑degenerate in the normal directions to C, then C is a non‑degenerate critical manifold of index λ equal to the number of negative normal eigenvalues. A tubular neighbourhood of C is diffeomorphic to the normal bundle, and f takes the form f(c, v) = f(c) − ‖v⁻‖² + ‖v⁺‖² where v = v⁻ ⊕ v⁺ splits according to the negative and positive normal directions.

The Morse‑Bott inequalities replace the critical point counts by the Betti numbers of the critical manifolds. For a Morse‑Bott function with critical manifolds C₁,…,Cₖ of indices λ₁,…,λₖ, the Poincaré polynomial of M satisfies ∑i tλᵢ Pt(Cᵢ) = Pt(M) + (1+t) Q(t) with Q(t) a polynomial with non‑negative coefficients. This yields strong constraints on the topology of M; for example, the even‑dimensional complex projective space ℂPⁿ admits a perfect Morse‑Bott function with critical manifolds ℂP⁰, ℂP¹, …, ℂPⁿ of indices 0,2,…,2n, giving the well‑known cohomology ring.

Handle decompositions adapt naturally: a critical manifold of index λ yields a handle attachment of the form C × D^λ along its boundary C × Sλ−1. The attaching map is the inclusion of the sphere bundle of the negative normal bundle. Cancellation of handles now requires that the attaching sphere of a (λ+1)‑handle intersect the belt sphere of a λ‑handle transversely in a single point inside the critical manifold. The Whitney trick still works in high dimensions provided the critical manifolds are simply connected and the normal bundles satisfy appropriate triviality conditions.

Applications abound. The moment map for a Hamiltonian torus action on a symplectic manifold is a Morse‑Bott function; its critical manifolds are the fixed point components of the torus action. The Atiyah‑Bott‑Berline‑Vergne localisation formula computes integrals over the manifold by summing contributions from these critical manifolds. In gauge theory, the Yang‑Mills functional on the space of connections modulo gauge has critical manifolds corresponding to flat connections; Morse‑Bott theory underlies the construction of the Atiyah‑Floer conjecture relating instanton Floer homology to Lagrangian Floer homology.

Cerf Theory and the Classification of Morse Functions on High‑Dimensional Manifolds

While Morse functions are generic, the space of all Morse functions on a fixed manifold is not connected. Cerf theory, introduced by Jean Cerf in 1970, studies the stratification of the space of smooth functions by their singularity types. A generic one‑parameter family of functions fₜ : M → ℝ encounters only two types of non‑Morse events: a birth‑death of a cancelling pair of critical points of adjacent index (a Morse‑Bott singularity of type A₂) and a handle slide where the attaching sphere of a (λ+1)‑handle passes over the belt sphere of a λ‑handle. Cerf proved that for simply connected manifolds of dimension ≥ 5, any two Morse functions can be joined by a path that avoids worse singularities; the only obstructions are the Cerf diagrams recording the sequence of births, deaths, and handle slides.

The Cerf diagram is a planar graph whose vertices correspond to critical points and edges to gradient trajectories. A birth‑death creates or annihilates a pair of vertices connected by an edge; a handle slide corresponds to a Reidemeister‑type move on the diagram. The fundamental result is that the group of pseudo‑isotopies of a high‑dimensional manifold is isomorphic to the group of path components of the space of Morse functions modulo these moves. This leads to the pseudo‑isotopy theorem: for a simply connected manifold of dimension ≥ 6, every diffeomorphism isotopic to the identity is pseudo‑isotopic to the identity, and the space of such diffeomorphisms is connected.

Cerf theory also provides a combinatorial framework for the h‑cobordism theorem. The handle cancellation process in Smale’s proof can be viewed as a sequence of Cerf moves that simplify the Cerf diagram until no critical points remain. The Whitney trick guarantees that the necessary handle slides can be performed without creating new intersections. In dimension 4, Cerf theory breaks down because the Whitney trick fails; this is the root of the exotic smooth structures on ℝ⁴ and the difficulty of the smooth 4‑dimensional Poincaré conjecture.

Modern developments include the work of Igusa on the stability of Morse functions and the construction of the Cerf complex, a chain complex whose homology computes the homotopy groups of the diffeomorphism group. The Cerf complex has been used by Hatcher and Wagoner to study the algebraic K‑theory of spaces and by Galatius‑Randal‑Williams to prove homological stability for diffeomorphism groups of high‑dimensional manifolds.

Worked Example: Cerf Move on a Handle Diagram
Consider a 5‑dimensional cobordism with a Morse function having a single 2‑handle and a single 3‑handle whose attaching spheres intersect algebraically once but geometrically in three transverse points. Describe the Cerf moves needed to cancel the pair.
The three intersection points indicate that the attaching sphere of the 3‑handle wraps the belt sphere of the 2‑handle three times. First, perform a handle slide of the 3‑handle over itself (a Cerf move of type “handle slide”) to reduce the geometric intersection number by two, using the Whitney trick in the 4‑dimensional level set (dimension ≥ 5 ensures the Whitney disks are embedded). After the slide, the intersection number becomes one. Now the attaching sphere and belt sphere intersect in a single transverse point, so the pair forms a cancelling 2‑/3‑handle pair. A birth‑death move (the inverse of a cancellation) removes both critical points, leaving a Morse function with no critical points. The cobordism is therefore a product.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks to “simplify a handle decomposition”, always check whether the ambient dimension is ≥ 5 and the manifold is simply connected. If so, the Whitney trick is available and you can cancel any algebraically cancelling pair after at most one handle slide. In dimension 4, state explicitly that the Whitney trick fails and cancellation may be impossible.

Morse‑Smale Complexes and Gradient Flow Dynamics

A Morse function f on a closed Riemannian manifold (M, g) is called Morse‑Smale if the stable and unstable manifolds of its critical points intersect transversely. The gradient flow φₜ of −∇f then defines a finite‑dimensional dynamical system whose chain recurrent set consists exactly of the critical points. The Morse‑Smale complex is the chain complex generated by the critical points, graded by index, with differential ∂p = ∑q n(p,q) q where n(p,q) counts (with signs) the flow lines from p to q of index difference one. The homology of this complex is canonically isomorphic to the singular homology of M.

Transversality is a generic condition: for a fixed Morse function, a generic metric makes the pair Morse‑Smale. The compactified moduli spaces of flow lines are manifolds with corners; their boundaries correspond to broken trajectories, which give the relation ∂² = 0. The Morse‑Smale complex provides a concrete, finite‑dimensional model for the Floer complex in symplectic geometry, where the gradient flow is replaced by solutions of the Cauchy‑Riemann equation.

Morse‑Smale dynamics also yields the Conley index, a topological invariant of isolated invariant sets that generalises the Morse index. For an isolated invariant set S, the Conley index is the homotopy type of the quotient N/L where N is an isolating neighbourhood and L its exit set. When S consists of a single non‑degenerate critical point of index λ, the Conley index is the pointed sphere S^λ. The Conley index is robust under perturbations and extends Morse theory to non‑gradient flows and infinite‑dimensional settings.

Applications include the proof of the Arnold conjecture for surfaces via Morse‑Smale homology (by Floer), the construction of the Morse‑Novikov complex for closed 1‑forms, and the study of gradient‑like flows on manifolds with boundary where the flow is tangent to the boundary. In computational topology, discrete Morse theory (Forman) provides a combinatorial analogue that works on simplicial complexes and is used in persistent homology algorithms.

Witten’s Analytic Approach and Supersymmetric Quantum Mechanics

In 1982 Edward Witten gave an analytic proof of the Morse inequalities by deforming the de Rham complex. Let f : M → ℝ be a Morse function. Define the deformed differential dₜ = e−tf d etf = d + t df∧ on differential forms. Its adjoint with respect to the L² metric is dₜ* = d* + t ι∇f. The deformed Laplacian Δₜ = dₜ dₜ* + dₜ* dₜ is a Schrödinger‑type operator Δₜ = Δ₀ + t ℒ + t² |∇f|², where ℒ is a zeroth‑order operator. As t → ∞, the low‑lying spectrum of Δₜ concentrates near the critical points of f. Near a critical point of index λ, after rescaling coordinates by √t, Δₜ approximates the harmonic oscillator on ℝⁿ with λ negative directions. The number of eigenvalues in [0, ε] for small ε equals the number of critical points of index λ, and the corresponding eigenforms are exponentially localised.

Witten’s construction is precisely the supersymmetric quantum mechanics of a particle on M with superpotential f. The supercharges are Q = dₜ and Q* = dₜ*; the Hamiltonian is H = Δₜ. The Witten index Tr(−1)^F e−βH equals the Euler characteristic of M and is independent of t. This physical interpretation explains why the Morse complex computes homology: the supersymmetric ground states correspond to harmonic forms, and the instanton trajectories between critical points are the gradient flow lines that define the differential.

The analytic approach yields strong results. The Helffer‑Sjöstrand theory gives precise asymptotics for the eigenvalues of Δₜ, leading to the “Morse inequalities with remainder” that bound the difference between critical point counts and Betti numbers by the number of small eigenvalues. In the semiclassical limit, the tunnelling between wells is governed by the Agmon distance, which coincides with the minimal action of gradient flow lines. This perspective underlies the rigorous construction of Floer homology via the heat flow method and the proof of the Atiyah‑Floer conjecture for certain 3‑manifolds.

Applications to Topological Quantum Field Theory and Heegaard Floer Homology

Morse theory on infinite‑dimensional spaces is the engine behind several topological quantum field theories (TQFTs). The Chern‑Simons functional on the space of connections over a 3‑manifold has critical points corresponding to flat connections; its gradient flow (with respect to the L² metric) is the anti‑self‑dual Yang‑Mills equation on the 4‑manifold M × ℝ. The resulting Floer homology groups are the instanton Floer homology of the 3‑manifold, which Atiyah conjectured to be isomorphic to the Lagrangian Floer homology of the moduli space of flat connections on a surface — the Atiyah‑Floer conjecture.

Heegaard Floer homology, introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó in 2001, is a Morse‑theoretic invariant of 3‑manifolds constructed from a Heegaard splitting. The data is a symmetric product Sym^g(Σ) of a genus‑g surface Σ, equipped with a symplectic form and a pair of Lagrangian tori 𝕋_α, 𝕋_β coming from the α‑ and β‑curves. The Floer homology of these Lagrangians in Sym^g(Σ) is the Heegaard Floer homology ĤF(Y). It satisfies a surgery exact triangle, detects the Thurston norm, and gives a combinatorial algorithm for computing the knot Floer homology of links in S³. The theory has been extended to 4‑manifolds (Ozsváth‑Szabó 4‑manifold invariants) and to contact geometry (the contact invariant).

Both instanton and Heegaard Floer homologies fit into the framework of a (3+1)‑dimensional TQFT: to a closed 3‑manifold they assign a graded vector space, to a 4‑dimensional cobordism a linear map, and they satisfy functoriality under gluing. The proof of functoriality uses Cerf theory on the space of metrics and perturbations to show that the maps are independent of choices up to chain homotopy. The resulting TQFTs have deep connections to gauge theory, symplectic geometry, and low‑dimensional topology, and they provide the most powerful tools for distinguishing smooth structures on 4‑manifolds.

Morse Theory on Manifolds with Boundary and Relative Handle Decompositions

When a smooth manifold M has non‑empty boundary ∂M, a Morse function f : M → ℝ is usually required to have no critical points on ∂M and to be either constant on each boundary component or to have ∂M as a regular level set. The gradient‑like vector field X is then chosen so that it points strictly outward on the part of the boundary where f attains its maximum and strictly inward where it attains its minimum. This guarantees that the flow of X stays inside M for all time and that the unstable manifolds of interior critical points intersect the boundary transversely.

The handle decomposition of a cobordism (W; M0, M1) follows from a Morse function f : W → [0,1] with f−1(0) = M0, f−1(1) = M1 and no critical points on the boundary. Each critical point of index λ corresponds to the attachment of a λ‑handle Dλ × Dn−λ along its attaching sphere Sλ−1 × Dn−λ ⊂ ∂(Wc−ε). The relative Morse inequalities compare the number cλ of interior critical points of index λ with the relative Betti numbers bλ(W, M0) = rank Hλ(W, M0; ℤ2):

  • c0 ≥ b0
  • c1 − c0 ≥ b1 − b0
  • …
  • ∑i=0λ (−1)λ−i ci ≥ ∑i=0λ (−1)λ−i bi

Equality holds for the alternating sum, giving the relative Euler characteristic χ(W, M0) = ∑ (−1)λ cλ. Smale’s proof of the h‑cobordism theorem uses a Morse function with only critical points of index n and n+1 on a simply connected cobordism of dimension ≥ 6; the Whitney trick then cancels the handles pairwise, showing the cobordism is a product. When the boundary is not simply connected, the relative handle decomposition still exists but the cancellation may be obstructed by Whitehead torsion, leading to the s‑cobordism theorem of Barden‑Mazur‑Stallings.

Morse‑Novikov Theory for Closed 1‑Forms and the Novikov Inequalities

Classical Morse theory studies the critical points of a real‑valued function. In many geometric situations — for example, the study of foliations, circle‑valued maps, or the action functional on a loop space — the natural object is a closed 1‑form ω on a manifold M. A zero of ω is a point where ω vanishes; if ω = df locally, the zero is a critical point of a local primitive. Novikov (1981) developed a Morse theory for closed 1‑forms with non‑degenerate zeros, assuming the form is generic in the sense of Morse‑Bott. The key invariant is the Novikov homology H*(M, ω), defined as the homology of a chain complex generated by the zeros of ω with coefficients in the Novikov ring Λω (a completion of the group ring of the covering group associated to the periods of ω).

The Novikov inequalities relate the number cλ of zeros of index λ to the ranks of the Novikov homology modules:

  • c0 ≥ rank Λω H0(M, ω)
  • c1 − c0 ≥ rank Λω H1(M, ω) − rank Λω H0(M, ω)
  • …

When ω is exact (ω = df), the Novikov ring collapses to the ground field and the inequalities reduce to the classical Morse inequalities. Farber (1986) and Pajitnov (1990) extended the theory to compute the minimal number of zeros of a closed 1‑form in a given cohomology class; this minimal number is the Novikov number bλ(M, [ω]). The theory also provides a Morse‑Novikov complex whose differential counts gradient flow lines of a metric adapted to ω; the resulting homology is independent of the metric and the generic perturbation. Applications include lower bounds for the number of closed characteristics on a contact manifold and the study of the topology of foliations via the Godbillon‑Vey invariant.

Examiner's trap
A closed 1‑form ω on a compact 3‑manifold M has exactly 5 non‑degenerate zeros of index 1 and 2 zeros of index 2. The Novikov homology with coefficients in the Novikov ring Λω has ranks rank Λω H₁(M, ω) = 3 and rank Λω H₂(M, ω) = 1. Does this data violate the Novikov inequalities? Justify your answer.
The Novikov inequalities for λ = 1 read c₁ − c₀ ≥ rank H₁ − rank H₀. Here c₀ = 0 (no index‑0 zeros), c₁ = 5, rank H₁ = 3, rank H₀ = 0 (since H₀ vanishes for a non‑exact form on a connected manifold). The left side is 5, the right side is 3, so 5 ≥ 3 holds. For λ = 2: c₂ − c₁ + c₀ ≥ rank H₂ − rank H₁ + rank H₀ → 2 − 5 + 0 ≥ 1 − 3 + 0 → −3 ≥ −2, which is false. Therefore the given numbers violate the second Novikov inequality; such a closed 1‑form cannot exist.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks whether a set of critical point counts can be realised by a Morse function or a closed 1‑form, write down the relevant (Novikov) inequalities explicitly and test each alternating sum. A single violated inequality is enough to rule out the configuration; you do not need to construct an explicit function.

Lusternik‑Schnirelmann Category and Morse Theory

The Lusternik‑Schnirelmann (LS) category cat(M) of a topological space M is the smallest integer k such that M can be covered by k open sets each contractible in M. For a smooth closed manifold, the LS theorem (1934) states that any smooth function f : M → ℝ has at least cat(M) + 1 distinct critical points. If f is a Morse function, this gives a lower bound on the total number of critical points that is often sharper than the sum of Betti numbers. The proof uses the deformation lemma: if f has no critical values in [a, b], the sublevel set f−1(−∞, a] is a deformation retract of f−1(−∞, b]. A cover by k contractible sets yields a sequence of sublevel sets whose topology changes at least k times, forcing at least k critical values.

The cup‑length estimate provides a computable lower bound: cat(M) ≥ cl(M), where cl(M) is the maximal length of a non‑zero cup product in the cohomology ring H*(M; ℤ2). For complex projective space ℂℙn, cl = n, so any Morse function has at least n+1 critical points; the standard height function achieves exactly n+1. The LS category also interacts with the Morse inequalities: the weak Morse inequalities imply ∑ cλ ≥ cat(M) + 1. In symplectic geometry, the LS category of a symplectic manifold gives a lower bound for the number of fixed points of a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism (Arnold conjecture), and the proof uses Floer homology, which is the infinite‑dimensional Morse theory of the action functional.

Conley Index Theory and Isolated Invariant Sets

Conley (1978) introduced a topological index for isolated invariant sets of a flow φt on a metric space, generalising the Morse index of a non‑degenerate critical point. An isolated invariant set S is a compact invariant set that is the maximal invariant set in some neighbourhood N. An index pair (N, L) consists of compact sets L ⊂ N such that L is positively invariant in N and the quotient N/L (with L collapsed to a point) has the homotopy type of a pointed CW complex. The Conley index h(S) is the homotopy type of N/L; it is independent of the choice of index pair. For a non‑degenerate critical point of a gradient flow with Morse index λ, the Conley index is the homotopy type of a pointed sphere Sλ.

The Conley index satisfies a continuation property: if a family of flows has an isolated invariant set varying continuously, the index remains constant. This allows one to detect the existence of connecting orbits between invariant sets by comparing indices. The Morse decomposition of an isolated invariant set yields a sequence of invariant sets whose Conley indices fit into a long exact sequence (the Morse‑Conley sequence), analogous to the Morse complex. In infinite dimensions, the Conley index has been used by Salamon and Zehnder to give a rigorous foundation for Floer homology: the action functional on the loop space of a symplectic manifold has isolated invariant sets corresponding to periodic orbits, and the Conley index of the whole space recovers the Floer homology groups. The theory also provides a robust tool for proving the existence of chaotic dynamics in Hamiltonian systems via the existence of non‑trivial Conley indices for invariant sets in a Poincaré map.

Exotic Spheres and the Role of Morse Functions in High‑Dimensional Topology

Milnor’s discovery (1956) of exotic 7‑spheres — smooth manifolds homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to the standard sphere S7 — was achieved by constructing Morse functions with exactly two critical points (a minimum and a maximum) on certain S3‑bundles over S4. By Reeb’s theorem, a closed manifold admitting a Morse function with only two critical points is homeomorphic to a sphere. Milnor showed that the total spaces of these bundles are homeomorphic to S7 but have non‑trivial Pontryagin classes, hence are not diffeomorphic to the standard sphere. The classification of exotic spheres in dimension ≥ 5 was completed by Kervaire and Milnor (1963) using the group Θn of h‑cobordism classes of homotopy n‑spheres. The h‑cobordism theorem (Smale, 1962) states that if (W; M, M′) is an h‑cobordism between simply connected manifolds of dimension ≥ 6, then W is diffeomorphic to M × [0,1]. The proof constructs a Morse function on W with only critical points of index n and n+1, then uses the Whitney trick to cancel them pairwise. The Whitney trick requires dimension ≥ 5 to embed the Whitney disks; this is why the h‑cobordism theorem fails in dimension 4 (where exotic ℝ4 exist). The group Θn fits into an exact sequence involving the stable homotopy groups of spheres and the image of the J‑homomorphism; for n = 7, Θ7 ≅ ℤ28, giving 28 distinct smooth structures on S7. Morse theory thus provides the handle‑body language that translates differential topology into algebraic topology, enabling the computation of Θn for many dimensions.

  • Confusing the Novikov ring for a closed 1‑form with the ordinary group ring; the completion is essential for convergence of the Morse‑Novikov complex.
  • Applying the Lusternik‑Schnirelmann category bound cat(M)+1 to a function that is not continuous on the whole space; the theorem requires a continuous function on a compact metric space.
  • Treating the Conley index as a numerical invariant rather than a homotopy type; the index is a pointed homotopy type, and its Euler characteristic recovers the Morse index only for isolated non‑degenerate critical points.
  • Believing that the Whitney trick works in dimension 4 for simply connected manifolds; it fails because the Whitney disks cannot be made embedded in general, leading to exotic smooth structures on ℝ⁴.
  • Overlooking that the Kervaire‑Milnor exact sequence involves the cokernel of the J‑homomorphism, not just the stable homotopy groups; the group Θₙ is not simply πₙˢ.
  • Using the relative Morse inequalities without verifying that the Morse function has no critical points on the boundary; boundary critical points contribute extra terms that modify the inequalities.

Equivariant Morse Theory and the Moment Map

When a compact Lie group G acts smoothly on a manifold M preserving a Morse function f, the critical set splits into G-orbits. If each orbit is non‑degenerate in the sense of Morse‑Bott, the equivariant Morse inequalities relate the G-equivariant Betti numbers of M to the contributions of the critical orbits. The moment map μ : M → 𝔤* for a Hamiltonian G-action on a symplectic manifold provides a natural Morse‑Bott function: the square of its norm ‖μ‖² is invariant and its critical sets are precisely the symplectic quotients at various levels. Kirwan’s surjectivity theorem (1984) shows that the inclusion of a regular level set into M induces a surjection in equivariant cohomology, which can be proved by analysing the gradient flow of ‖μ‖² and applying the equivariant Morse lemma. The indices of the critical orbits are computed from the weights of the isotropy representation; each weight contributes ±1 to the Morse index depending on whether the corresponding direction is attracted or repelled by the flow. This machinery yields the Kirwan‑Witten localisation formula for integrals of equivariant cohomology classes and underlies the computation of the cohomology rings of moduli spaces of flat connections and of symplectic quotients. In the infinite‑dimensional setting, the same ideas appear in the Atiyah‑Bott Yang‑Mills functional on the space of connections, where the gauge group plays the role of G and the moment map is the curvature map.

Discrete Morse Theory and Combinatorial Topology

Forman’s discrete Morse theory (1998) replaces a smooth function on a manifold by a function f on the cells of a regular CW complex K satisfying a simple combinatorial condition: for each p-cell σ, at most one (p+1)-cell τ with σ ⊂ τ has f(τ) ≤ f(σ), and at most one (p−1)-cell ν with ν ⊂ σ has f(ν) ≥ f(σ). A cell is critical if neither inequality occurs. The discrete gradient vector field consists of the paired cells (σ, τ) with f(τ) ≤ f(σ); its flow lines are sequences of paired cells that mimic the gradient trajectories of a smooth Morse function. The main theorem states that K is homotopy equivalent to a CW complex having exactly one cell for each critical cell of f, with the same dimension. Consequently the discrete Morse inequalities hold: the number of critical p-cells is at least the p-th Betti number of K. This theory has become a standard tool in computational topology: it provides an algorithmic way to simplify large simplicial complexes (e.g. point‑cloud data) while preserving homology, and it underlies the construction of Morse matchings in persistent homology software. A discrete Morse function can be obtained from a generic smooth Morse function by restricting to a sufficiently fine triangulation, but many useful discrete Morse functions have no smooth counterpart, for instance the lexicographic ordering on the faces of a convex polytope.

Worked Example: Discrete Morse Function on a Triangulated Torus
Consider the minimal triangulation of the 2‑torus with 7 vertices, 21 edges and 14 triangles. Define a function f on vertices by assigning distinct integers 1,…,7; extend to edges by f(edge) = max(f(endpoints)) and to triangles by f(triangle) = max(f(edges)). Determine the critical cells and verify the discrete Morse inequalities.
Vertices: each vertex has a unique value, so no two incident edges share the same maximum; each vertex is paired with the incident edge of smallest higher value, leaving exactly one vertex (the global minimum) unpaired → 1 critical 0‑cell. Edges: an edge is paired with the triangle of smallest higher value unless it is already paired with a vertex; counting shows 3 edges remain unpaired → 3 critical 1‑cells. Triangles: each triangle is paired with the edge of largest lower value unless that edge is already paired; 2 triangles remain unpaired → 2 critical 2‑cells. Betti numbers of the torus are b₀=1, b₁=2, b₂=1. The critical cell counts (1,3,2) satisfy c₀≥b₀, c₁≥b₁, c₂≥b₂ and the alternating sum 1−3+2 = 0 equals χ(T²). Hence the discrete Morse inequalities hold.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for a discrete Morse function on a given complex, first construct a total order on the vertices (or 0‑cells) and then propagate it upward by the max rule. Check the pairing condition locally: each cell can be paired with at most one co‑face and one face. Count critical cells dimension by dimension and compare with known Betti numbers; the alternating sum must equal the Euler characteristic. This quick verification catches most construction errors.

Morse Theory on Stratified Spaces and Intersection Homology

Classical Morse theory assumes a smooth manifold, but many natural spaces (e.g. moduli spaces, algebraic varieties) are singular. Goresky and MacPherson (1980) extended Morse theory to Whitney‑stratified spaces by defining a stratified Morse function f whose restriction to each stratum is a Morse function in the usual sense and whose gradient flow respects the stratification: trajectories can only move from a stratum to one of lower dimension in the closure. The critical points are the critical points of f on each stratum together with points where the gradient of f is tangent to the stratum. Attaching data are no longer ordinary handles but conical handles modelled on the product of a disc with the link of the stratum. The resulting cellular chain complex computes the intersection homology groups IHₖ(X) (with middle perversity) rather than ordinary homology. The stratified Morse inequalities state that the number of critical points of index k on a stratum of dimension d is at least the rank of IHₖ(X). This framework yields a purely topological proof of the hard Lefschetz theorem for projective varieties and underlies the decomposition theorem for perverse sheaves. In practice, one often works with a Morse‑Bott version where critical sets are submanifolds of strata, and the normal Morse data are given by the representation of the link’s fundamental group.

Morse Theory on Manifolds with Corners and Polyhedral Morse Functions

A manifold with corners locally resembles [0,∞)ᵏ × ℝⁿ⁻ᵏ. A polyhedral Morse function is a continuous function which, in each corner chart, is the restriction of a smooth Morse function on ℝⁿ that is linear on each face of the corner. Critical points can lie in the interior of a face of any codimension; the index is defined as the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian restricted to the tangent space of that face. The gradient flow points outward on some faces and inward on others, producing a handle decomposition with corners where handles are attached along products of discs with faces of the corner. The resulting cellular complex computes the homology of the manifold relative to its boundary strata. This theory is essential for the study of compactifications of moduli spaces (e.g. the Deligne‑Mumford compactification of ℳg,n) where the boundary divisors meet transversely, creating corners. The polyhedral Morse lemma guarantees that near a critical point on a face of codimension c the function is equivalent to a quadratic form on ℝⁿ⁻ᶜ plus the sum of the coordinate functions defining the face. The associated corner handlebody picture yields a combinatorial description of the fundamental group and higher homotopy groups via a presentation by generators (critical points) and relations (gradient flow lines between critical points of adjacent index).

Parametric Morse Theory and the Space of Morse Functions

Cerf theory studies the topology of the space Morse(M) of all Morse functions on a fixed closed manifold M. A generic one‑parameter family fₜ (t ∈ [0,1]) encounters only two types of singularities: birth‑death of a cancelling pair of critical points of adjacent index, and a handle slide where the attaching sphere of a handle passes over the belt sphere of another handle of the same index. The fundamental group π₁(Morse(M)) is isomorphic to the group of pseudo‑isotopies of M modulo isotopy; for simply connected M of dimension ≥ 6 this group is trivial by the h‑cobordism theorem. Higher homotopy groups of Morse(M) are related to the groups of diffeomorphisms of M and to the stable homotopy groups of spheres via the work of Igusa (1980s). In parametric Morse theory one considers families parametrised by a manifold P; the discriminant (the set of parameters where fₚ fails to be Morse) is a stratified subset of P of codimension 1 (birth‑death) and codimension 2 (handle slide). The monodromy of the gradient flow around the discriminant gives a representation of π₁(P discriminant) on the Morse complex, leading to the notion of a Morse module over the fundamental groupoid of the parameter space. This perspective is crucial for understanding the topology of spaces of metrics with positive scalar curvature and for the construction of spectral invariants in symplectic topology.

Morse Theory on Hilbert Manifolds and the Palais–Smale Condition

Classical Morse theory assumes a finite‑dimensional manifold. When the underlying space is a Hilbert manifold — a manifold modeled on a separable Hilbert space — the gradient flow of a smooth function need not have compact orbits. The Palais–Smale condition (PS) restores compactness: a functional f satisfies (PS) if every sequence {x_k} with f(x_k) bounded and ∥∇f(x_k)∥ → 0 possesses a convergent subsequence. Under (PS) the deformation lemma holds, allowing one to push sublevel sets past non‑critical values exactly as in the finite‑dimensional case. The Morse lemma also extends: near a non‑degenerate critical point the functional is equivalent to a quadratic form on the Hilbert space, with the index equal to the dimension of the maximal negative subspace of the Hessian. This framework underlies the variational approach to nonlinear elliptic PDEs (e.g., the Yamabe problem, semilinear Schrödinger equations) where the energy functional lives on a Sobolev space H¹(M). Key references are Palais (1963) “Morse theory on Hilbert manifolds” and Smale (1964) “Morse theory for a class of infinite dimensional manifolds”. The (PS) condition is often verified by a concentration‑compactness argument (Lions, 1984) or by exploiting the compact embedding of H¹ into L^p for subcritical exponents.

The Morse Index Theorem for Geodesics and Conjugate Points

Let (M,g) be a complete Riemannian manifold and γ:[0,1]→M a geodesic. The index form I(V,W)=∫₀¹(⟨∇_t V,∇_t W⟩−⟨R(V,γ')γ',W⟩)dt defined on vector fields along γ vanishing at the endpoints is a symmetric bilinear form. The Morse index of γ is the maximal dimension of a subspace on which I is negative definite. The Morse index theorem (Morse, 1934; Bott, 1956) states that this index equals the number of conjugate points to γ(0) along γ, counted with multiplicity. A point γ(t₀) is conjugate to γ(0) if there exists a non‑trivial Jacobi field J with J(0)=J(t₀)=0; its multiplicity is the dimension of the space of such Jacobi fields. The proof uses the spectral flow of the family of Sturm–Liouville operators associated to the index form. Consequences include the Lyusternik–Fet theorem (every compact Riemannian manifold admits a closed geodesic) and the estimate that the index of an iterated closed geodesic grows linearly with the iteration number (Bott’s iteration formula). In the presence of conjugate points the exponential map fails to be a local diffeomorphism, and the cut locus is precisely the first conjugate locus for manifolds without focal points.

Worked Example
State the Morse index theorem for a geodesic on a Riemannian manifold and explain how conjugate points contribute to the index.
The Morse index theorem asserts that for a geodesic γ:[0,1]→M the Morse index ind(γ) — the maximal dimension of a subspace of the space of variation vector fields vanishing at the endpoints on which the index form I is negative definite — equals the total number of conjugate points to γ(0) along γ, each counted with its multiplicity. A conjugate point γ(t₀) occurs when there exists a non‑zero Jacobi field J along γ with J(0)=J(t₀)=0; the multiplicity is dim{J | J(0)=J(t₀)=0}. The proof proceeds by considering the family of index forms I_s on the restrictions γ|[0,s] and tracking the dimension of their negative eigenspaces as s increases; each time s passes a conjugate value the dimension jumps by the multiplicity of that conjugate point. Hence the final index at s=1 is exactly the sum of these multiplicities.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the Morse index theorem, write the index form explicitly, define conjugate points via Jacobi fields, and state the equality of index and total conjugate multiplicity. Do not confuse this with the Morse index of a critical point of an arbitrary function; the geodesic theorem is a special case where the functional is the energy on the path space.

Morse Theory of the Energy Functional on Loop Spaces and Bott Periodicity

The free loop space ΛM = C^∞(S¹,M) of a compact Riemannian manifold carries the energy functional E(γ)=½∫₀¹|γ'(t)|² dt. Critical points of E are precisely the closed geodesics (parametrized proportionally to arc length). The Hessian of E at a closed geodesic is the index form of the Morse index theorem, so the Morse index of a critical point equals the number of conjugate points along the geodesic. Because closed geodesics come in S¹‑families (reparametrizations), the critical sets are typically circles, making E a Morse‑Bott function. Bott (1956) applied this Morse‑Bott theory to the loop space of a compact Lie group G. The homology of ΩG (the based loop space) computed via the Morse complex of E yields the celebrated Bott periodicity theorem: ΩU(n) has the homotopy type of U(∞) and ΩSp(n) of Sp(∞), giving 2‑periodicity for unitary groups and 8‑periodicity for symplectic/orthogonal groups. Milnor (1963) gave a detailed account in “Morse Theory”. The Morse‑Bott spectral sequence collapses at the E² page because the critical manifolds are even‑dimensional and the normal Morse data are trivial, leading to an explicit description of H_*(ΩG) as a polynomial algebra on generators of even degree.

Morse Theory and the Topology of Configuration Spaces

For a manifold M of dimension ≥ 2, the ordered configuration space F(M,k) = {(x₁,…,x_k)∈M^k | x_i≠x_j} is an open submanifold of M^k. Morse functions on F(M,k) can be constructed by pulling back a Morse function f on M and adding a small perturbation that separates diagonals, or by using the distance to a fixed point in a high‑dimensional embedding. Farber (2003) introduced the topological complexity TC(M) — the minimal number of open sets covering M×M admitting continuous motion planning sections — and proved TC(M) ≥ cat(F(M,2)), where cat denotes the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category. Morse theory on F(M,k) provides lower bounds for cat(F(M,k)) via the Morse inequalities applied to a suitable Morse‑Bott function whose critical manifolds are products of diagonals. Cohen and Taylor (1995) computed the homology of F(ℝ^m,k) using a Morse function given by the sum of squared distances to a generic point, showing that the critical points correspond to partitions of {1,…,k} and that the resulting Morse complex is the Orlik–Solomon algebra of the braid arrangement. These calculations yield explicit formulas for the Betti numbers of configuration spaces and, via the Farber–Grant inequality, for TC(M). The Morse‑Bott condition is essential because the critical sets are typically submanifolds of positive dimension (e.g., coincident points).

Morse Theory in Surgery Theory and the s‑Cobordism Theorem

A handle decomposition of a smooth manifold W obtained from a Morse function f:W→[0,1] with no critical points of index 0 or n gives an h‑cobordism between the boundary components ∂₀W = f⁻¹(0) and ∂₁W = f⁻¹(1). The chain complex C_*(f) of the Morse function (with coefficients in ℤ[π₁(W)]) computes the Whitehead torsion τ(W,∂₀W) ∈ Wh(π₁(W)). The s‑cobordism theorem (Barden–Mazur–Stallings, 1960s) states that an h‑cobordism (W,∂₀W,∂₁W) of dimension ≥ 6 is diffeomorphic to a product ∂₀W×[0,1] if and only if its Whitehead torsion vanishes. In the Morse‑theoretic proof one arranges, by handle slides and cancellations, that the Morse function has exactly one critical point of each index i and n−i, and that the algebraic intersection matrix of the attaching spheres equals the identity. The vanishing of τ then guarantees that the handles can be cancelled geometrically, yielding a product structure. This interplay between Morse functions, handlebodies, and algebraic K‑theory is the cornerstone of high‑dimensional surgery theory (Wall, 1970). The theorem fails in dimension 4 (Freedman’s topological s‑cobordism theorem holds but the smooth version is open) and in dimension 5 the proof requires additional work on the fundamental group.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Palais–Smale condition with compactness of the underlying Hilbert manifold; (PS) is a property of the functional, not the manifold.
  • Assuming the Morse index theorem holds for any geodesic without the non‑degeneracy hypothesis; conjugate points must be counted with multiplicity.
  • Overlooking that Bott periodicity requires the loop space of a compact Lie group and the Morse‑Bott condition; it does not apply to arbitrary manifolds.
  • Treating configuration‑space Morse functions as ordinary Morse functions; they are typically Morse‑Bott with critical submanifolds of positive dimension.
  • Neglecting the role of Whitehead torsion in the s‑cobordism theorem; an h‑cobordism may be non‑trivial even when the Morse function has no index‑0 or index‑n critical points.
  • Misapplying the Morse lemma in infinite dimensions without verifying the Palais–Smale condition.
  • Forgetting that the Morse index of an iterated closed geodesic grows linearly with the iteration number, so the index can be infinite for a sequence of iterates.

Morse Theory on Manifolds with Cylindrical Ends and the Atiyah–Patodi–Singer Index Theorem

When a smooth manifold W has a boundary that is a disjoint union of closed manifolds, one can attach infinite cylinders ∂W × [0,∞) to obtain a complete non‑compact manifold Ŵ with cylindrical ends. A Morse function f on W that is constant on the boundary extends to a proper Morse function on Ŵ whose critical points lie in the interior. The gradient flow lines of f on Ŵ converge either to critical points or to periodic orbits on the ends, and the compactified moduli spaces of flow lines carry a natural stratification by broken trajectories. The analytic heart of the theory is the Atiyah–Patodi–Singer (APS) index theorem for the Dirac operator on Ŵ with APS boundary conditions. The index equals the spectral flow of the family of tangential operators on the cross‑sections of the ends, which is computed by the eta invariant η of the boundary Dirac operator. In Morse‑theoretic language, the index of the linearised gradient flow operator at a critical point equals the Morse index, and the APS theorem identifies the difference between the analytic index and the topological index with a boundary correction term. This correction is precisely the contribution of the cylindrical ends to the Morse complex, leading to a relative Morse homology that computes the homology of the pair (W, ∂W). The construction underlies the proof of the Novikov additivity of the signature, the gluing formula for the eta invariant, and the definition of the rho invariant for manifolds with boundary. In applications to gauge theory, the same cylindrical‑end set‑up appears in the definition of instanton Floer homology for 3‑manifolds, where the Chern–Simons functional plays the role of the Morse function and the APS index theorem computes the grading on the Floer chain complex.

Morse Theory for the Yang–Mills Functional and Donaldson Invariants

Let P be a principal SU(2)‑bundle over a smooth, closed, oriented 4‑manifold X. The Yang–Mills functional YM(A) = ∫_X |F_A|² dvol assigns to each connection A the L²‑norm of its curvature. Critical points of YM are precisely the solutions of the Yang–Mills equations d_A^* F_A = 0. On a 4‑manifold the Hodge star splits 2‑forms into self‑dual and anti‑self‑dual parts, and the absolute minima of YM are the anti‑self‑dual (ASD) connections satisfying F_A+ = 0. The moduli space M_k of irreducible ASD connections with fixed second Chern class k is, for generic Riemannian metrics, a smooth finite‑dimensional manifold of dimension 8k − 3(1 − b₁ + b₂⁺). The gradient flow of YM with respect to the L² metric on the space of connections is the Yang–Mills heat flow; its limit points are ASD connections. By perturbing the metric one achieves a Morse–Smale condition: the stable and unstable manifolds of the critical points intersect transversely. The resulting Morse complex, with coefficients in the orientation local system, computes the Donaldson invariants — polynomial invariants of the smooth structure of X. The differential counts gradient flow lines between ASD connections of index difference one; compactness of the 1‑dimensional moduli spaces follows from Uhlenbeck’s removable singularities theorem and the bubbling analysis of sequences of ASD connections. The wall‑crossing phenomenon occurs when the metric crosses a wall in the space of metrics where reducible ASD connections appear; the change in the Donaldson invariants is expressed by a universal formula involving the intersection form of X. This Morse‑theoretic construction was the first rigorous definition of invariants that distinguish exotic smooth structures on ℝ⁴ and on many simply‑connected 4‑manifolds.

Worked Example: Computing the Expected Dimension of an ASD Moduli Space
Let X be a simply‑connected 4‑manifold with b₂⁺ = 3 and b₂⁻ = 2. Consider an SU(2) bundle with c₂ = 2. What is the expected dimension of the moduli space of irreducible ASD connections for a generic metric?
The dimension formula for the ASD moduli space is dim M_k = 8k − 3(1 − b₁ + b₂⁺). Here k = c₂ = 2, b₁ = 0 (simply‑connected), b₂⁺ = 3. Substituting: dim = 8·2 − 3(1 − 0 + 3) = 16 − 3·4 = 16 − 12 = 4. Hence the expected dimension is 4.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the dimension of an ASD moduli space, write down the formula 8c₂ − 3(1 − b₁ + b₂⁺) immediately. Identify c₂, b₁, b₂⁺ from the data; plug in and simplify. Remember that b₂⁺ is the dimension of a maximal positive‑definite subspace of the intersection form, not the total b₂. A common trap is to use b₂ instead of b₂⁺.

Morse Theory for the Seiberg–Witten Equations and the Seiberg–Witten Invariants

On a closed, oriented, smooth 4‑manifold X with a chosen spinc structure 𝔰, the Seiberg–Witten equations couple a connection A on the determinant line bundle L to a spinor field ψ ∈ Γ(W⁺ ⊗ L). The equations are F_A+ = q(ψ) and D_A ψ = 0, where q(ψ) is a quadratic map and D_A is the Dirac operator. Solutions are critical points of the Chern–Simons–Dirac functional on the configuration space of pairs (A, ψ) modulo gauge. For generic perturbations the moduli space M_𝔰 is a compact, smooth manifold of dimension d = (c₁(L)² − 2χ(X) − 3σ(X))/4. The Seiberg–Witten invariant SW_X(𝔰) is defined by counting points in the zero‑dimensional moduli space with signs determined by an orientation of the determinant line bundle of the linearised operator. When d > 0 one evaluates cohomology classes on M_𝔰 to obtain polynomial invariants. The Morse‑theoretic interpretation uses the gradient flow of the Chern–Simons–Dirac functional on the 3‑manifold Y = ∂X (or on a cylindrical end) to define a Floer homology HF^SW(Y); the relative invariant of a cobordism X is a map between these Floer groups. The Seiberg–Witten invariants vanish for manifolds with b₂⁺ > 1 and a metric of positive scalar curvature, a consequence of the Weitzenböck formula for the Dirac operator. This vanishing theorem gives a powerful obstruction to the existence of metrics of positive scalar curvature and leads to the proof of the Thom conjecture and the Milnor conjecture on the slice genus of torus knots.

Morse Theory for the Space of Metrics of Positive Scalar Curvature and the Gromov–Lawson Surgery Theorem

Let M be a closed smooth manifold of dimension n ≥ 3. The space Riem⁺(M) of Riemannian metrics with positive scalar curvature is an open subset of the Fréchet manifold of all metrics. The scalar curvature functional S(g) = ∫_M s_g dvol_g is not a Morse function on Riem⁺(M), but the Yamabe functional Y(g) = (∫_M s_g dvol_g) / (vol_g)(n−2)/n restricted to a conformal class has critical points exactly at constant scalar curvature metrics. The Gromov–Lawson surgery theorem states that if M admits a metric of positive scalar curvature, then any manifold obtained from M by surgery of codimension at least 3 also admits such a metric. The proof constructs a Morse function on the space of metrics by considering the distance to a fixed reference metric in the C⁰ topology and uses the fact that the scalar curvature is a quadratic form in the second derivatives of the metric. The handle decomposition of the space of metrics induced by this Morse function reflects the surgery steps: each handle of index k corresponds to a surgery of codimension k+1. The obstruction to extending a positive scalar curvature metric across a surgery of codimension 2 is measured by the index of the Dirac operator on the surgery sphere, which is the Atiyah–Singer index theorem in disguise. The Stolz theorem (1992) completes the classification for simply‑connected spin manifolds of dimension ≥ 5: such a manifold admits a metric of positive scalar curvature iff its α‑invariant in KO‑theory vanishes. The Morse‑theoretic viewpoint also underlies the work of Marques (2012) on the topology of Riem⁺(M), where the homotopy groups of the space of positive scalar curvature metrics are related to the index theory of families of Dirac operators.

Morse Theory for the Space of Legendrian Submanifolds and Contact Homology

In a contact manifold (Y, ξ = ker α) a Legendrian submanifold Λ is an isotropic submanifold of maximal dimension n−1 (where dim Y = 2n−1). The space of Legendrian embeddings of a fixed manifold L into Y carries a natural Morse function given by the action functional A(γ) = ∫_γ α on the space of Reeb chords with endpoints on Λ. Critical points of A are precisely the Reeb chords; their Conley–Zehnder indices provide a grading. The Chekanov–Eliashberg differential graded algebra (DGA) is generated by Reeb chords, and its differential counts rigid pseudoholomorphic disks in the symplectisation ℝ × Y with boundary on ℝ × Λ and asymptotic to Reeb chords. This DGA is the Morse complex of the action functional on the infinite‑dimensional space of Legendrian paths, and its homology is the Legendrian contact homology HC_*(Λ). The DGA is invariant under Legendrian isotopy up to stable tame isomorphism, providing powerful invariants that distinguish Legendrian knots with the same classical invariants (Thurston–Bennequin number and rotation number). The Morse‑Bott version, where families of Reeb chords form clean critical manifolds, leads to a spectral sequence converging to the contact homology. In higher dimensions, the theory extends to Legendrian submanifolds with boundary, where the Morse function is the action on the space of paths with endpoints on the boundary, and the resulting invariants are related to the wrapped Fukaya category of the symplectic filling.

  • Assuming the Yang–Mills functional is Morse on the whole space of connections; it is only Morse–Bott after quotient by gauge, and transversality requires a generic metric perturbation.
  • Forgetting that the Seiberg–Witten invariant is defined only for spinc structures with c₁(L)² = 2χ + 3σ (mod 4); otherwise the moduli space is empty.

Morse Theory for the Space of Pseudoholomorphic Curves and Gromov–Witten Invariants

The modern formulation of Gromov–Witten invariants rests on a Morse‑theoretic analysis of the moduli space of stable pseudoholomorphic maps. Let (X, ω) be a compact symplectic manifold and J an ω‑compatible almost complex structure. For a fixed homology class A ∈ H₂(X; ℤ) and genus g, consider the space ℳg,n(X, A; J) of J‑holomorphic maps u : Σ → X from a connected nodal Riemann surface Σ of genus g with n marked points, representing the class A. Gromov’s compactness theorem (1985) shows that after adding stable maps the space becomes compact, but it is generally a singular orbifold of virtual dimension dim_ℝ ℳ = 2c₁(TX)·A + (dim_ℝ X − 6)(1−g) + 2n. The key insight of Ruan–Tian (1995) and Li–Tian (1998) is to treat the Cauchy–Riemann operator ∂̄_J as a section of a Banach bundle over the space of smooth maps; its linearisation is a Fredholm operator whose index equals the virtual dimension. When the moduli space is not cut out transversely, one constructs a virtual fundamental class [ℳ]vir ∈ Hdim ℳ(ℳ; ℚ) by perturbing the section (Kuranishi structures, polyfolds, or the algebraic approach of Behrend–Fantechi). The Gromov–Witten invariants are then defined as integrals of cohomology classes pulled back from X via evaluation maps against this virtual class. From a Morse‑theoretic viewpoint, the action functional 𝒜(u) = ∫_Σ u*ω on the infinite‑dimensional space of maps has critical points exactly at J‑holomorphic curves; the Morse index of a critical point is the spectral flow of the linearised ∂̄_J operator along a path of almost complex structures. This index coincides with the Maslov index of the associated Lagrangian boundary condition when one considers open curves. The resulting Morse complex, after a suitable compactification and perturbation, yields the quantum cohomology ring QH*(X) whose product is defined by three‑point genus‑zero invariants. The associativity of this product (the WDVV equations) follows from the gluing analysis of the boundary strata of the compactified moduli space, which in Morse language corresponds to the identification of broken gradient trajectories with the boundary of the 1‑dimensional moduli space of flow lines. The theory extends to open Gromov–Witten invariants (Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono) where the Morse function is the symplectic area on the space of maps with Lagrangian boundary conditions, and the resulting A∞‑structure on the Floer complex of the Lagrangian recovers the open‑closed string map.

Morse Theory for the Space of Harmonic Maps and the Energy Functional

Let (M, g) and (N, h) be compact Riemannian manifolds. The energy functional E(φ) = ½ ∫_M |dφ|² dvol_g on the Sobolev space W1,2(M, N) is the natural Morse function whose critical points are harmonic maps. Eells and Sampson (1964) proved that when N has non‑positive sectional curvature the negative gradient flow of E exists for all time and converges to a harmonic map, establishing the existence of harmonic representatives in each homotopy class. The Palais–Smale condition holds for E on W1,2(M, N) when dim M = 2 (Sacks–Uhlenbeck, 1981) and more generally under curvature assumptions on N. The Morse index of a harmonic map φ is the number of negative eigenvalues of the Jacobi operator J_φ = Δ_φ + R^N(dφ, ·)dφ acting on sections of φ*TN. The Morse index theorem for harmonic maps (Jost, 1988; Chang‑Ding‑Ye, 1992) relates this index to the number of conjugate points along geodesics in the pull‑back bundle, generalising the classical index theorem for geodesics. When M = S² the space of based maps ΩN carries a natural Morse function given by the energy; its critical points are harmonic spheres and the Morse complex computes the homology of the loop space, yielding a proof of Bott periodicity for the homotopy groups of classical groups (Uhlenbeck, 1982). For higher‑dimensional domains the Morse complex is infinite‑dimensional, but the finite‑dimensional approximation via the heat flow (Struwe, 1988) provides a Morse‑Bott decomposition of the space of maps into unstable manifolds of harmonic maps. This decomposition underlies the proof of the existence of multiple harmonic maps in a given homotopy class (the “mountain pass” theorem of Sacks–Uhlenbeck) and the construction of minimal surfaces via the min‑max method (Pitts–Rubinstein, 1987). The Morse‑theoretic perspective also clarifies the bubbling phenomenon: loss of compactness corresponds to the appearance of non‑trivial harmonic spheres at infinity, which are precisely the critical points at infinity in the sense of Bahri–Coron.

Worked Example: Morse Index of a Harmonic Map from S² to Sⁿ
Let φ : S² → Sⁿ (n ≥ 3) be a non‑constant harmonic map of degree d. Using the second variation formula, compute the Morse index of φ in terms of d and n.
The Jacobi operator on sections of φ*TSⁿ is J = Δ + |dφ|². For a harmonic map from S² the eigenvalues of Δ on S² are λ_k = k(k+1) with multiplicity 2k+1. The pull‑back metric satisfies |dφ|² = 2E(φ)/Vol(S²) = 8π|d|/4π = 2|d|. Hence the spectrum of J is shifted by 2|d|. Negative eigenvalues occur when λ_k + 2|d| < 0, which never happens because λ_k ≥ 0. However, the conformal variation yields an extra negative direction corresponding to the scaling of the domain; this contributes exactly one negative eigenvalue for each independent conformal Killing field on S², i.e. three. Therefore the Morse index of φ equals 3, independent of d and n (provided n ≥ 3). This matches the known result that any non‑constant harmonic sphere in Sⁿ has Morse index three.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the Morse index of a harmonic map, first identify the Jacobi operator and the spectrum of the Laplacian on the domain. Remember that conformal variations on S² always contribute three negative directions, while the target curvature term shifts the spectrum by the energy density. Write the index as a sum of contributions from domain and target; this avoids the common mistake of forgetting the conformal Killing fields.

Morse Theory on the Space of Knots and Vassiliev Invariants

Vassiliev’s theory of finite‑type (Vassiliev) invariants (1990) is built on a Morse‑theoretic stratification of the space of smooth immersions Imm(S¹, ℝ³). The discriminant Σ ⊂ Imm(S¹, ℝ³) consists of maps with self‑intersections; its complement is the space of knots K = Imm(S¹, ℝ³) Σ. Vassiliev observed that the cohomology of K can be computed by a spectral sequence whose E₁‑page is generated by the homology of the strata of Σ, each stratum corresponding to immersions with a prescribed singularity type (double points, triple points, etc.). The Morse function is the “distance to the discriminant” measured in the C¹‑topology; its critical manifolds are the strata of Σ, and the gradient flow lines correspond to resolutions of singularities. The resulting Vassiliev invariants are the coefficients of the Taylor expansion of the knot invariant when one perturbs a knot through the discriminant. Concretely, a Vassiliev invariant of order ≤ n vanishes on any singular knot with more than n double points. The space of chord diagrams modulo the 4T and 1T relations provides a combinatorial model for the dual of the space of Vassiliev invariants; the Kontsevich integral (1993) gives a universal finite‑type invariant taking values in the completed algebra of chord diagrams. From the Morse‑theoretic viewpoint, the Kontsevich integral is the partition function of a topological quantum field theory on the configuration space of points on the knot, and the 4T relation arises from the boundary of the 1‑dimensional moduli space of flow lines between strata of codimension one. The theory extends to knots in arbitrary 3‑manifolds (Habiro, 2000) and to higher‑dimensional knots (Vassiliev, 1992). The Morse‑Bott version, where families of singular knots form clean critical manifolds, leads to a spectral sequence converging to the homology of the space of long knots, which is known to be a free loop space on the embedding space. This perspective also underlies the proof that the space of long knots is formal over ℚ (Lambrechts–Volić, 2014).

Morse Theory for the Space of Minimal Submanifolds and the Morse Index Theorem

Let Σ^k ⊂ (M^n, g) be a compact minimal submanifold. The second variation of volume defines the Jacobi operator J = Δ_Σ + Ric^M(ν, ν) + |A|² acting on normal vector fields, where A is the second fundamental form and ν a unit normal. The Morse index ind(Σ) is the number of negative eigenvalues of J (counted with multiplicity). Simons (1968) proved that a stable minimal hypersurface (index 0) in a manifold of non‑negative Ricci curvature is totally geodesic. The Morse index theorem for minimal submanifolds (do Carmo–Cao, 1992; Savo, 1998) relates ind(Σ) to the number of conjugate points along normal geodesics emanating from Σ, generalising the classical index theorem for geodesics. In the context of the min‑max construction of minimal hypersurfaces (Almgren–Pitts, 1980s; Marques–Neves, 2014), one considers the space of cycles Z_k(M) with the flat topology and a Morse function given by the volume functional. The critical points are stationary varifolds; the Morse index of a min‑max minimal hypersurface equals the number of parameters in the sweepout family that can be varied to decrease volume. This index bound is crucial for the proof of the Willmore conjecture (Marques–Neves, 2012) and for the existence of infinitely many minimal hypersurfaces in manifolds with positive Ricci curvature (Irie–Marques–Neves, 2018). For higher codimension, the Morse index is defined via the normal bundle; the work of Sharp (2017) shows that the index of a min‑max minimal submanifold is bounded by the dimension of the parameter space. The Morse‑Bott situation occurs when the minimal submanifold belongs to a smooth family (e.g., the Clifford tori in S³); then the nullity of J equals the dimension of the family, and the Morse complex splits as a tensor product of the Morse complex of the family with the complex of the normal directions.

Morse Theory for the Space of Reeb Orbits and Cylindrical Contact Homology

Let (Y2n−1, ξ = ker α) be a closed contact manifold with a non‑degenerate contact form α. The Reeb vector field R_α is defined by α(R_α)=1, dα(R_α,·)=0. The action functional 𝒜(γ) = ∫_γ α on the free loop space ΛY has critical points exactly the closed Reeb orbits. The Conley–Zehnder index μCZ(γ) provides a grading. Cylindrical contact homology HC_*cyl(Y, α) (Eliashberg–Givental–Hofer, 2000) is the homology of the chain complex generated by good Reeb orbits, with differential counting rigid pseudoholomorphic cylinders in the symplectisation ℝ × Y asymptotic to Reeb orbits at the ends. This differential is the Morse differential of 𝒜 after a suitable perturbation that achieves transversality (the “abstract perturbation” scheme of Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder, 2003, or the polyfold framework of Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder, 2009). The resulting homology is independent of the choice of non‑degenerate contact form up to canonical isomorphism, yielding an invariant of the contact structure ξ. When the contact form is Morse–Bott (e.g., a Boothby–Wang bundle over a symplectic manifold), the critical manifolds are circles of Reeb orbits; the Morse–Bott spectral sequence (Bourgeois, 2002) converges to cylindrical contact homology and its E₁‑page is the Morse homology of the critical manifolds with coefficients in the homology of the loop space of the fibre. The grading shift by the Maslov index of the contact structure ensures that the differential has degree −1. Cylindrical contact homology is a building block for the full symplectic field theory (SFT) of Eliashberg–Givental–Hofer, which also counts curves with multiple positive and negative punctures. The Morse‑theoretic interpretation clarifies the compactness and gluing theorems: broken cylinders correspond to the boundary of the 1‑dimensional moduli space of gradient flow lines of 𝒜, and the SFT differential squares to zero because the boundary of the 1‑dimensional moduli space consists of two‑level buildings.

Morse Theory for the Space of Symplectic Structures and the Gromov–Eliashberg h‑Principle

The space Symp(M,ω) of symplectic forms on a fixed smooth manifold M carries a natural infinite‑dimensional Fréchet topology. A Morse function on this space can be built from the symplectic volume functional Vol(ω)=∫_M ω^n/n! (where dim M=2n). Critical points of Vol are precisely the symplectic forms that are Einstein in the sense of symplectic geometry: the Ricci form of any compatible almost‑complex structure is a constant multiple of ω. The Hessian of Vol at a critical form is the Lichnerowicz operator acting on primitive (1,1)‑forms; its negative eigenvalues give the Morse index. Gromov’s h‑principle implies that the inclusion of the subspace of integrable symplectic forms into the space of almost‑symplectic forms is a weak homotopy equivalence. Consequently, the Morse complex of Vol on the almost‑symplectic space computes the same homology as the genuine symplectic subspace. Eliashberg’s classification of overtwisted contact structures in dimension three translates, via the symplectisation construction, into a statement about the Morse theory of Vol on four‑manifolds: overtwisted contact boundaries correspond to negative‑index critical points that can be cancelled by a handle‑body move, yielding the flexibility part of the h‑principle. In higher dimensions, the work of Murphy (2012) shows that the space of loose Legendrian submanifolds is contractible, which again reflects the vanishing of certain Morse‑theoretic obstructions. The Morse‑Bott situation occurs when a whole family of symplectic forms (e.g., a cohomology class) consists of critical points; then the critical manifold is the space of compatible almost‑complex structures, and the Morse‑Bott spectral sequence collapses at the E₂‑page because the normal Hessian is non‑degenerate in the directions transverse to the family.

Morse Theory on the Space of Connections and the Atiyah–Floer Correspondence

Let G be a compact Lie group and P→Σ a principal G‑bundle over a closed oriented surface Σ of genus g≥2. The space 𝒜(P) of connections modulo gauge transformations is an infinite‑dimensional affine space. The Chern–Simons functional CS(A)=∫_Σ Tr(A∧dA+⅔ A∧A∧A) (after fixing a trivialisation over a 3‑manifold bounding Σ) descends to a circle‑valued Morse function on 𝒜(P)/𝒢. Its critical points are flat connections; the Hessian at a flat connection A is the twisted de Rham operator d_A⊕d_A^* acting on Ω¹(Σ,ad P). The Morse index equals the spectral flow of the family of operators obtained by varying the connection along a path to a reference flat connection. Atiyah (1988) conjectured, and Floer later proved, that the Morse homology of CS on 𝒜(P)/𝒢 is isomorphic to the instanton Floer homology of the mapping torus Σ×_φ S¹, where φ is the monodromy of a Heegaard splitting of a 3‑manifold Y. This Atiyah–Floer correspondence identifies the gradient flow lines of CS (which are instantons on Σ×ℝ) with the pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectic moduli space ℳ(Σ,G) of flat connections. The Morse‑Bott case appears when the centraliser of a flat connection is positive‑dimensional (e.g., reducible connections); then the critical manifold is a component of the representation variety Hom(π₁Σ,G)/G, and the Morse‑Bott spectral sequence computes the equivariant Floer homology. The correspondence has been extended by Dostoglou–Salamon (1994) to the case of symplectic manifolds with Hamiltonian G‑action, where the moment map provides a Morse‑Bott function on the symplectic quotient, and the resulting Floer homology matches the quantum cohomology of the quotient.

Worked Example: Morse Index of a Reducible Flat Connection
Let Σ be a genus‑2 surface and G=SU(2). Consider the trivial flat connection A₀ on the trivial bundle. Its stabiliser is the whole group SU(2), so the critical manifold is a copy of SU(2)≅S³. Compute the Morse index of A₀ for the Chern–Simons functional on 𝒜/𝒢, assuming the Hessian on the normal directions has exactly 6 negative eigenvalues.
The Morse‑Bott index of a critical manifold C is the sum of the Morse index of a Morse function on C (here we may take a perfect Morse function on S³ with indices 0 and 3) and the normal Morse index. A perfect Morse function on S³ has one minimum (index 0) and one maximum (index 3). Adding the normal index 6 yields critical points of total index 6 (from the minimum) and 9 (from the maximum). Thus the Morse‑Bott complex contributes generators in degrees 6 and 9, matching the known instanton Floer homology groups of the mapping torus of the identity map on Σ.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the Morse index of a degenerate critical manifold, first identify the critical manifold and its dimension, then choose a perfect Morse function on it (often given by the moment map or a generic perturbation). Add the normal index supplied in the problem statement. The total index is the sum of the two; this is the grading shift used in the Morse‑Bott spectral sequence.

Morse Theory for the Space of Metrics with Bounded Ricci Curvature and Perelman’s Entropy Functional

On a closed n‑manifold M, consider the space ℛ(M) of Riemannian metrics with Ricci curvature bounded below by a constant κ. Perelman’s 𝒲‑entropy 𝒲(g,f,τ)=∫_M [τ(|∇f|²+R)+f−n] (4πτ)-n/2 e-f dvol_g is a functional on the product ℛ(M)×C^∞(M)×ℝ>0. Its critical points are gradient shrinking Ricci solitons (g,∇f) with τ=1. The second variation of 𝒲 at a soliton is given by the Lichnerowicz Laplacian on symmetric 2‑tensors modulo diffeomorphisms; the number of negative eigenvalues is the Morse index of the soliton. Perelman proved that 𝒲 is non‑decreasing along the Ricci flow, which is precisely the negative gradient flow of 𝒲 after a suitable gauge fixing (DeTurck trick). This monotonicity yields a Morse‑theoretic interpretation of the Ricci flow: the flow lines are gradient trajectories of 𝒲 on the infinite‑dimensional manifold ℛ(M)/Diff(M). The Morse‑Bott situation occurs for the round sphere Sⁿ, where the space of shrinking solitons is the finite‑dimensional manifold of metrics conformal to the round metric; the nullity equals the dimension of the conformal group. The Morse complex of 𝒲 then recovers the topology of the moduli space of Ricci flow singularity models. In dimension three, the only non‑trivial shrinking solitons are the round sphere, the round cylinder S²×ℝ, and their quotients; their Morse indices are 0, 1, and 0 respectively, which matches the classification of κ‑solutions used in the proof of the geometrisation conjecture.

Morse Theory for the Space of Embeddings and the Smale–Hirsch Immersion Theory

Let Emb(M,N) denote the space of smooth embeddings of a compact manifold M into a manifold N with dim N>dim M. The Smale–Hirsch theorem identifies the homotopy type of the space of immersions Imm(M,N) with the space of bundle monomorphisms TM→TN. A Morse function on Emb(M,N) can be constructed from the embedding energy E(f)=∫_M |df|² dvol_M (the Dirichlet energy of the map). Critical points of E are harmonic embeddings; when M is a circle, these are closed geodesics in N, and the Morse index equals the classical Morse index of the geodesic. For higher‑dimensional M, the Hessian of E is the Jacobi operator acting on normal vector fields along the embedding; its negative eigenvalues give the Morse index. The work of Haefliger (1962) and later of Weiss (1996) shows that the inclusion Emb(M,N)→Imm(M,N) is (2n−m−2)‑connected, where n=dim N, m=dim M. Consequently, the Morse complex of E on Emb(M,N) computes the same homology as the Morse complex on Imm(M,N) up to that connectivity range. In the metastable range (2m+2≤n), the space of embeddings is highly connected, and the only critical points of E are the trivial ones coming from linear embeddings; their Morse indices are determined by the representation theory of the orthogonal group O(n−m). The Morse‑Bott manifolds appear when the embedding has a continuous symmetry group (e.g., a knot with a rotational symmetry); the critical manifold is then the orbit of the symmetry group, and the Morse‑Bott spectral sequence converges to the homology of the embedding space, which is related to the Vassiliev invariants via the Goodwillie–Weiss embedding calculus.

Morse Theory for the Space of Gradient Flows and the Conley–Morse Graph

Given a smooth Morse function f on a compact manifold M, consider the space 𝔽(f) of all gradient‑like vector fields for f (i.e., vector fields X such that X·f>0 away from critical points). This space is contractible, but the Conley–Morse graph Γ(f) encodes the combinatorial dynamics: vertices are the critical points, and directed edges correspond to heteroclinic orbits connecting critical points of index difference one. The graph can be equipped with a Morse function Φ:Γ(f)→ℝ defined by Φ(v)=f(v) on vertices and extended linearly on edges. Critical points of Φ on the graph are exactly the vertices; the Morse index of a vertex equals the Morse index of the corresponding critical point of f. The chain complex generated by the vertices with differential counting directed paths in Γ(f) is precisely the Morse complex of f. When f is perturbed within its Cerf equivalence class, the graph changes by elementary moves (birth/death of cancelling pairs, handle slides). Cerf theory shows that any two Morse functions on a simply‑connected manifold of dimension ≥5 are connected by a path of Morse functions with only birth/death singularities; the corresponding sequence of Conley–Morse graphs realises the algebraic cancellation of chain homotopy equivalences. The Conley index of an isolated invariant set in the flow of a gradient‑like vector field coincides with the homology of the subgraph spanned by the critical points in that set, providing a finite‑dimensional combinatorial model for the infinite‑dimensional Conley index theory. This graph‑theoretic viewpoint underlies the algorithmic computation of Morse homology in applied topological data analysis (e.g., the Mapper algorithm).

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Morse index of a symplectic form with the dimension of the space of compatible almost‑complex structures; the index is computed from the Lichnerowicz operator, not from the contractible space of almost‑complex structures.
  • Assuming the Atiyah–Floer correspondence holds for all gauge groups without checking the simply‑connectedness of the symplectic moduli space; reducible connections introduce Morse‑Bott degeneracies that must be handled by equivariant Floer homology.
  • Treating Perelman’s 𝒲‑entropy as a standard Morse function on the whole metric space; it is only Morse‑Bott on the quotient by diffeomorphisms after DeTurck gauge fixing, and its gradient flow is the Ricci flow, not a finite‑dimensional gradient descent.
  • Neglecting the metastable range condition 2m+2≤n when applying Smale–Hirsch theory to embeddings; outside this range the inclusion Emb→Imm is not highly connected and the Morse complex of the embedding energy does not compute the correct homology.
  • Identifying the Conley–Morse graph with the full Conley index; the graph captures only the Morse‑theoretic part (critical points and index‑one connections), while the Conley index includes higher‑order connection data and is a homotopy invariant of the isolated invariant set.
  • Forgetting that the Morse‑Bott spectral sequence for a family of flat connections collapses at E₂ only when the normal Hessian is non‑degenerate; if the family has further degeneracies (e.g., a wall‑crossing phenomenon), higher differentials may be non‑zero.
  • Misapplying the h‑principle to conclude that every formal symplectic structure is homotopic to a genuine one without verifying the open condition (non‑degeneracy) required for the convex integration step.

Morse Theory for the Space of Einstein Metrics and the Einstein–Hilbert Functional

The Einstein–Hilbert functional EH(g) = ∫M Rg dvolg on the space ℳ of Riemannian metrics of fixed volume on a closed manifold M is a natural Morse‑type functional whose critical points are precisely the Einstein metrics (Ricg = λ g). The second variation of EH at an Einstein metric is given by the Lichnerowicz Laplacian ΔL acting on symmetric 2‑tensors h with Trgh = 0 and δgh = 0 (transverse‑traceless gauge). The Morse index equals the number of negative eigenvalues of ΔL on this subspace. When the Einstein metric is a local minimum of EH (e.g., the round sphere Sn for n ≥ 3), the index is zero; for many Kähler–Einstein metrics with negative scalar curvature the index can be positive, reflecting instability under volume‑preserving deformations. The Palais–Smale condition fails on ℳ because of the diffeomorphism group action, but after slicing by the DeTurck gauge (or working on the quotient ℳ/Diff0(M)) one obtains a Hilbert manifold on which EH satisfies the Palais–Smale condition away from the zero scalar curvature level. The Morse inequalities then relate the Betti numbers of the moduli space of Einstein metrics to the critical points of EH. In dimension four, the work of Besse and later of Anderson shows that the moduli space can have multiple components with different Morse indices, and the topology of ℳ/Diff is reflected in the existence of distinct Einstein metrics on the same smooth manifold (e.g., on certain K3 surfaces). Examiners often test the distinction between the Lichnerowicz operator on all symmetric tensors and its restriction to the transverse‑traceless slice; forgetting the gauge‑fixing step leads to an overcount of negative directions.

Morse Theory for the Space of Lagrangian Submanifolds and Lagrangian Floer Homology

Let (X, ω) be a compact symplectic manifold and L ⊂ X a closed Lagrangian submanifold. The action functional 𝒜 on the space of paths γ: [0,1] → X with γ(0), γ(1) ∈ L and γ̇ − XH(γ) = 0 (for a Hamiltonian H) is a Morse‑Bott functional whose critical points are Hamiltonian chords from L to itself. After a generic perturbation of L (or of H) the chords become non‑degenerate and the functional becomes Morse. The Morse index of a chord is given by the Conley–Zehnder index of the associated linearised Hamiltonian flow, shifted by the Maslov index of L. The resulting Floer chain complex CF*(L, L) is generated by these chords, with differential counting rigid pseudo‑holomorphic strips with boundary on L. When L is monotone (ω|π₂(X,L) = λ μ|π₂(X,L) for λ > 0) and has minimal Maslov number ≥ 2, the differential squares to zero and the homology HF*(L, L) is independent of the perturbation. The Morse–Bott spectral sequence degenerates at E₂ when the clean intersection hypothesis holds; otherwise higher differentials encode wall‑crossing phenomena as the Hamiltonian varies. A key examiner trap is to confuse the Maslov index of a disc with the Conley–Zehnder index of a chord; the former appears in the grading shift for the pearl complex, the latter directly in the Floer grading. Another common error is to assume that HF*(L, L) ≅ H*(L) for every Lagrangian; this holds only under monotonicity and orientability assumptions, and fails for non‑monotone Lagrangians where bubbling of holomorphic discs obstructs the differential.

Worked Example: Computing the Morse Index of a Hamiltonian Chord
Let X = T* S¹ with standard symplectic form, and L = S¹ the zero section. Take the Hamiltonian H(q,p) = ½ p². Find the non‑degenerate Hamiltonian chords from L to L and compute their Conley–Zehnder indices.
The Hamiltonian flow is φt(q,p) = (q + t p, p). A chord from L to L satisfies φ1(q,0) = (q,0) ∈ L, so p = 0. The only chord is the constant path at any q ∈ S¹. After a C²‑small perturbation Hε(q,p) = ½ p² + ε cos(2π q), the critical points become non‑degenerate: there are two chords corresponding to the maxima and minima of cos(2π q). The linearised flow along a chord is given by the symplectic matrix exp(J Hess H). For the minimum, Hess H = diag( (2π)² ε, 1 ) > 0, so the path of symplectic matrices has no crossing of the Maslov cycle; its Conley–Zehnder index is 0. For the maximum, Hess H has one negative eigenvalue, giving a single crossing; the index is 1. Thus the Floer complex has generators in degrees 0 and 1, and HF*(L,L) ≅ H*(S¹) as expected.

When a question asks for the Morse index of a critical point in an infinite‑dimensional setting, first identify the correct Hessian operator (Lichnerowicz, linearised Cauchy–Riemann, or second variation of the energy). Then recall the gauge‑fixing or transversality condition that restricts the operator to a Fredholm domain. The index is the spectral flow of that restricted operator; write down the relevant index theorem (Atiyah–Singer for Dirac‑type, Conley–Zehnder for Hamiltonian paths, or Maslov for Lagrangian paths) rather than attempting a direct eigenvalue count.

Morse Theory for the Space of Contact Structures and Contact Homology

On a closed (2n−1)‑manifold Y, the space 𝒞 of co‑oriented contact structures ξ = ker α is an open subset of the space of hyperplane fields. The contact homology algebra is defined via a Morse‑Bott functional on the space of Reeb orbits of a contact form α. The action functional 𝒜(γ) = ∫γ α on the free loop space ΛY has critical manifolds given by the Reeb orbits; each orbit is a Morse‑Bott critical manifold diffeomorphic to S¹. After a generic perturbation of α (or a Morse function on each S¹) the orbits become non‑degenerate and the functional becomes Morse. The Conley–Zehnder index of a non‑degenerate Reeb orbit γ, computed from the linearised Reeb flow on ξ, provides the grading. The differential counts rigid J‑holomorphic cylinders in the symplectisation ℝ × Y asymptotic to Reeb orbits. The resulting contact homology HC*(Y, ξ) is invariant under contact isotopy and, by the work of Bourgeois–Eliashberg–Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder, under exact symplectic cobordisms. A subtle point: the Morse‑Bott spectral sequence collapses at E₂ only when the contact form is non‑degenerate and the almost complex structure J is generic; if the contact structure admits a family of Reeb orbits with non‑trivial normal Hessian degeneracy (e.g., a Morse‑Bott family of higher dimension), higher differentials may survive and encode the topology of the orbit space. Examiners frequently test the distinction between the grading by Conley–Zehnder index and the grading by the action value; the latter is not a homotopy invariant and cannot be used to define the differential.

Morse Theory for the Space of Foliations and the Haefliger Classifying Space

Let ℱq(M) denote the space of Cr codimension‑q foliations on a closed manifold M (r ≥ 2). The Godbillon–Vey invariant and other secondary characteristic classes arise from a Morse‑theoretic analysis of the Haefliger classifying space BΓq. One constructs a functional on ℱq(M) by pulling back a Morse function on BΓq via the classifying map of the foliation. Critical points correspond to foliations whose classifying map is a critical point of the Morse function on BΓq; these are precisely the foliations with a prescribed transverse structure (e.g., a Riemannian foliation). The Morse index equals the index of the critical point in BΓq plus the dimension of the space of leafwise deformations preserving the transverse structure. Thurston’s stability theorem implies that for a compact leaf with finite holonomy, the space of nearby foliations is a finite‑dimensional manifold, and the Morse lemma holds locally. In the non‑compact leaf case, the functional may fail the Palais–Smale condition because sequences of foliations can develop “bubbling” of holonomy. The Morse inequalities then give lower bounds on the number of distinct foliation types in a given homotopy class of Haefliger structures. A typical exam trap is to confuse the Haefliger cohomology H*(BΓq) with the leafwise cohomology of a specific foliation; the former classifies transverse invariants, the latter depends on the leaf topology.

Morse Theory for the Space of Diffeomorphisms and the Smale Conjecture

The space Diff(M) of diffeomorphisms of a closed manifold M carries a natural Morse‑type functional given by the L²‑norm of the velocity field of a path in Diff(M) (the Ebin–Marsden metric). Critical points of the energy functional on the path space ΩDiff(M) are geodesics of the right‑invariant L² metric; their Morse index equals the number of conjugate points along the geodesic, counted with multiplicity. For M = S³, Smale’s conjecture (proved by Hatcher) states that the inclusion O(4) → Diff(S³) is a homotopy equivalence. Morse theory on Diff(S³) with the energy functional shows that the only critical points in the identity component are the constant paths (index 0) and the one‑parameter subgroups generated by Killing fields (index 1). The Morse complex therefore has generators only in degrees 0 and 1, and its homology matches H*(O(4)). For higher‑dimensional spheres, the space Diff(Sⁿ) has more complicated topology; the work of Cerf and later of Igusa uses Morse theory on the space of embeddings Emb(Dⁿ, Sⁿ) to analyse the homotopy type of Diff(Sⁿ). A frequent examiner error is to assume that the L² metric on Diff(M) is complete; it is not, and the Palais–Smale condition fails, so one must work with the stronger Hs Sobolev metrics (s > n/2 + 1) to obtain a Hilbert manifold setting where Morse theory applies.

  • Assuming Lagrangian Floer homology always equals singular homology of the Lagrangian; monotonicity and minimal Maslov number ≥ 2 are essential hypotheses.
  • Mixing the Conley–Zehnder index of a Reeb orbit with the action value; only the former provides a stable grading for contact homology.

Morse Theory for the Yamabe Functional and Constant Scalar Curvature Metrics

The Yamabe functional assigns to each conformal class of Riemannian metrics on a closed manifold M^n the total scalar curvature normalized by the volume power (n-2)/(n). Critical points of this functional are precisely metrics of constant scalar curvature within the given conformal class. The functional is not bounded below on the whole conformal class, but after restricting to the unit volume slice it satisfies the Palais–Smale condition for dimensions n ≤ 5 and for locally conformally flat manifolds in higher dimensions. The Morse index of a critical metric equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the linearised scalar curvature operator L_g = (4(n-1))/(n-2)Δ_g + R_g acting on functions orthogonal to the kernel generated by the conformal Killing fields. When the kernel is non‑trivial the critical set forms a Morse‑Bott manifold; the Morse‑Bott spectral sequence then computes the topology of the space of constant scalar curvature metrics. Aubin and Schoen’s solution of the Yamabe problem shows that the global minimiser exists and has index zero. Higher critical points correspond to unstable constant scalar curvature metrics and their indices give lower bounds for the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category of the conformal class. In dimensions n ≥ 6 the failure of compactness (bubbling) creates sequences that concentrate at points; the blow‑up analysis yields a Morse‑theoretic description of the loss of compactness in terms of the topology of the space of Green’s functions of the conformal Laplacian.

Morse Theory for the Hitchin System on the Moduli Space of Higgs Bundles

The moduli space MHiggs(C,G) of semistable G-Higgs bundles on a compact Riemann surface C carries a natural hyperkähler metric. The Hitchin map h : MHiggs → bigoplusi=1^r H0(C, K_Cd_i) is a completely integrable system whose generic fibres are abelian varieties (the Prym varieties of spectral curves). The function f = \|h\|2 obtained from the L2 norm of the Hitchin map is a proper Morse‑Bott function on the smooth locus of MHiggs. Its critical manifolds are precisely the nilpotent cone (the fibre over 0) and the subvarieties where the spectral curve acquires extra automorphisms. The Morse index of a critical manifold equals twice the number of positive weights in the C^*-action on the normal bundle, which can be read off from the Harder–Narasimhan stratification of the underlying Higgs bundle. The resulting Morse‑Bott spectral sequence degenerates at the E2 page and computes the mixed Hodge numbers of MHiggs; this recovers the celebrated P = W conjecture for G = GL_n via the identification with the character variety. For non‑simply‑connected groups the nilpotent cone splits into several components indexed by the fundamental group, and the Morse indices differ by the dimension of the corresponding centraliser. The gradient flow of f with respect to the hyperkähler metric coincides with the negative gradient flow of the Yang–Mills–Higgs functional, providing a geometric bridge between Morse theory and the analytic proof of the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence.

Worked Example: Computing the Morse Index of a Nilpotent Higgs Bundle
Let (E,Φ) be a stable SL2(C)-Higgs bundle on a genus g≥ 2 curve with Φ nilpotent of rank one. Determine the Morse index of the corresponding critical point of f = \|h\|2 on the smooth part of the moduli space.

Morse Theory for Special Lagrangian Submanifolds and the Strominger–Yau–Zaslow Conjecture

On a Calabi–Yau manifold (X,ω,Ω) the volume functional restricted to the space of Lagrangian submanifolds has critical points exactly at special Lagrangians, i.e. Lagrangians calibrated by operatorname{Re}(eiθΩ). The second variation formula shows that the Morse index equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the Jacobi operator J = Δ + operatorname{Ric}^⊥ + |A|2 acting on normal vector fields, where operatorname{Ric}^⊥ is the normal Ricci curvature and A the second fundamental form. For a special Lagrangian torus fibration π : X → B the base B carries an affine structure with singularities; the discriminant locus Δ ⊂ B corresponds to fibres where the Morse‑Bott degeneracy jumps. Near a focus‑focus singularity the local model is the Harvey–Lawson cone, and the Morse index of the vanishing cycle is one. The gradient flow of the volume functional with respect to the L2 metric on the space of Lagrangian embeddings is the Lagrangian mean curvature flow; short‑time existence holds in the smooth category, but singularities develop precisely when a fibre crosses Δ. The SYZ conjecture predicts that mirror symmetry exchanges the Fukaya category of X (generated by special Lagrangians with flat unitary connections) with the derived category of coherent sheaves on the mirror check{X}. Morse theory on the space of Lagrangian branes provides a geometric realisation of the A_∞ operations: the moduli space of gradient flow trees with boundary on a collection of special Lagrangians is compactified by broken trees, and the signed count of rigid trees defines the higher products. The Maslov index of a flow tree equals the sum of the Morse indices of its asymptotic special Lagrangians, giving the grading on the Fukaya category.

Morse Theory for Gradient Flow Trees and the A_∞‑Structure on the Fukaya Category

Let L0,…,L_k be a tuple of transverse, graded, spin special Lagrangian submanifolds in a Calabi–Yau n-fold. The space of gradient flow trees with edges mapped to the Lagrangians and vertices at intersection points carries a natural Fredholm problem: the linearised Cauchy–Riemann operator with Lagrangian boundary conditions. Its index equals Σi=0^k μ(L_i) - n(k-1) where μ(L_i) is the Maslov index of the boundary condition. The moduli space of rigid trees (index zero) is a compact zero‑dimensional manifold; counting its points with signs determined by the spin structures yields the higher A_∞ product μ^k : CF^*(Lk-1,L_k) ⊗ … ⊗ CF^*(L0,L1) → CF^*(L0,L_k)[2-k]. The compactification includes broken trees, which correspond exactly to the A_∞ relations. When the Lagrangians are not transverse one perturbs by a Morse function on each intersection locus; the resulting pearl complex combines Morse theory on the clean intersection with the Floer differential. The Morse‑Bott spectral sequence for this pearl complex converges to the Lagrangian Floer homology, and its E1 page is the direct sum of the Morse homologies of the intersection manifolds. The differential on the E1 page counts gradient flow lines on the intersection manifolds together with holomorphic strips; this is the geometric incarnation of the A_∞ structure on the Morse model of the Fukaya category.

Morse Theory for the Space of Pseudoholomorphic Quilts and Generalized Lagrangian Correspondences

A quilted surface is a Riemann surface with seam lines labelled by Lagrangian correspondences Lij ⊂ M_i- × M_j. A pseudoholomorphic quilt is a map from the patches to the symplectic manifolds M_i that is J_i-holomorphic on each patch and satisfies the seam condition (u_i(z), u_j(z)) ∈ Lij on each seam. The space of such quilts with fixed asymptotic Hamiltonian chords at the strip‑like ends carries a Fredholm theory whose index is the sum of the Conley–Zehnder indices of the chords minus the Maslov indices of the correspondences. The gradient flow of the symplectic action functional on the space of Hamiltonian chords, combined with the quilt equation, yields a Morse‑Bott complex whose generators are the Hamiltonian chords and whose differential counts rigid quilts. When all correspondences are the graphs of symplectomorphisms the theory reduces to the usual Floer complex of the composite symplectomorphism. For a cyclic sequence of correspondences L01, L12, …, Lk0 the resulting homology is invariant under Hamiltonian isotopies of the correspondences and under the geometric composition of correspondences (the “quilted” version of the Donaldson–Floer gluing theorem). The Morse‑Bott spectral sequence for a clean intersection of correspondences has E1 page given by the Morse homology of the intersection manifolds, and the higher differentials encode the A_∞ operations on the Fukaya category of the product manifold. This framework provides a unified Morse‑theoretic proof of the functoriality of Lagrangian Floer homology under Lagrangian correspondences and of the homotopy associativity of the generalized Fukaya category.

Morse Theory for the Space of Metrics with Positive Ricci Curvature and the Hamilton–Perelman Ricci Flow

The space R+(M) of Riemannian metrics with positive Ricci curvature on a closed manifold M carries a natural Morse function: Perelman’s F-functional F(g,f) = ∫_M (R + |∇ f|2) e-f dV_g, where f is a smooth function normalised so that ∫_M e-f dV_g = 1. The critical points of F are gradient Ricci solitons satisfying operatorname{Ric}_g + ∇2 f = λ g for some constant λ. The Morse index of a critical point equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the linearised operator L = Δ_L + 2∇2 f acting on symmetric 2-tensors, where Δ_L is the Lichnerowicz Laplacian. Perelman proved that F is non-decreasing along the Ricci flow ∂_t g = -2operatorname{Ric}_g, and its monotonicity implies that the Ricci flow is a gradient flow for F on the quotient of R+(M) by the diffeomorphism group. The handle decomposition of R+(M) induced by F reflects the topology of the moduli space of positive Ricci curvature metrics: each handle corresponds to a surgery performed at a singular time of the Ricci flow with surgery. In dimension three, the only compact gradient shrinking solitons are the round sphere S3, the round cylinder S2 × R, and their quotients; the Morse indices are 0, 1, and higher respectively. The W-functional W(g,f,τ) = ∫_M [τ(R + |∇ f|2) + f - n](4πτ)-n/2 e-f dV_g provides a second Morse function whose critical points are gradient shrinking solitons with fixed scale τ. The second variation formula for W involves the operator L = Δ_L + 2∇2 f - (1)/(2τ) on the space of transverse-traceless tensors. The Morse inequalities for F and W constrain the Betti numbers of R+(M) and have been used to prove that R+(S^n) is contractible for n ≥ 3 (by work of Marques and others). The λ-functional λ(g) = inf_f F(g,f) is the lowest eigenvalue of -4Δ_g + R_g; its critical points are Einstein metrics with positive scalar curvature. The Morse index of an Einstein metric as a critical point of λ equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the Einstein operator Δ_E = Δ_L - 2mathring{R} on transverse-traceless tensors, where mathring{R} is the action of the curvature tensor. This index has been computed for symmetric spaces and is related to the stability of the Einstein metric under Ricci flow.

Morse Theory for the Space of Calibrated Submanifolds and the Harvey–Lawson Theory

A calibration on a Riemannian manifold (M,g) is a closed p-form φ such that φ|_xi ≤ operatorname{vol}_xi for every oriented p-plane xi ⊂ T_x M. A p-submanifold L ⊂ M is calibrated by φ if φ|TL = operatorname{vol}_L; calibrated submanifolds are absolutely volume-minimising in their homology class. The space C_φ(M) of φ-calibrated submanifolds carries a natural Morse function: the volume functional operatorname{Vol}(L) = ∫_L dV_L. The critical points of operatorname{Vol} restricted to C_φ(M) are the calibrated submanifolds themselves, and the Morse index equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the Jacobi operator J = Δ_L + |A|2 + operatorname{Ric}(nu,nu) acting on normal vector fields, where A is the second fundamental form and nu a unit normal. For special Lagrangian submanifolds in a Calabi–Yau manifold (calibrated by operatorname{Re} Ω), the Jacobi operator coincides with the Hodge Laplacian on 1-forms via the isomorphism NL ≅ T^*L given by the Kähler form; the Morse index equals the number of negative eigenvalues of Δ1 on exact 1-forms. For associative 3-folds in a G2-manifold (calibrated by the associative 3-form φ), the deformation theory is governed by a Dirac-type operator D : Γ(NL) → Γ(NL) whose index equals the expected dimension of the moduli space. The Morse–Bott theory for calibrated submanifolds arises when the moduli space has positive-dimensional components; the critical manifolds are the connected components of the moduli space, and the normal Morse index is given by the spectral flow of the family of Jacobi operators along paths in the moduli space. Harvey and Lawson proved that the space of calibrated currents (generalised submanifolds) is compact in the flat topology, and the volume functional satisfies the Palais–Smale condition on this compactification. The handle decomposition of the space of calibrated cycles induced by the volume functional yields a Morse complex whose homology is isomorphic to the calibrated homology group H_*^φ(M), a subgroup of the ordinary homology H_*(M;R) consisting of classes representable by φ-calibrated cycles. The Morse inequalities for this complex imply that the Betti numbers of the moduli space of calibrated submanifolds are bounded by the ranks of the calibrated homology groups. For special Lagrangian tori in a Calabi–Yau 3-fold, the moduli space is a Lagrangian submanifold of the intermediate Jacobian, and the volume functional corresponds to the central charge function Z(L) = ∫_L Ω; the critical points are the special Lagrangians, and the Morse index equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian of operatorname{Im} Z. The wall-crossing phenomenon for special Lagrangians is described by the Kontsevich–Soibelman formula, which relates the change in the Morse complex across walls of marginal stability to the product of automorphisms of the Fukaya category.

Worked Example: Morse Index of a Special Lagrangian Torus
Let L = T3 ⊂ (T6, g, J, Ω) be a special Lagrangian 3-torus in a flat Calabi–Yau 3-torus, calibrated by operatorname{Re} Ω. The normal bundle NL is trivial and identified with T^*L via the Kähler form ω. The Jacobi operator on normal vector fields corresponds to the Hodge Laplacian Δ1 on 1-forms on T3. Compute the Morse index of L as a critical point of the volume functional on the space of special Lagrangian deformations.
On a flat torus T3 = R3/Z3, the Hodge Laplacian on 1-forms has spectrum given by the eigenvalues of -Δ on functions. The 1-forms are spanned by dx^i and their Fourier modes e2π i k · x dx^i for k ∈ Z3 ∖ {0}. The eigenvalues are 4π2 |k|2 with multiplicity 3 for each k. All eigenvalues are strictly positive. The kernel of Δ1 consists of the harmonic 1-forms dx1, dx2, dx3, which correspond to infinitesimal special Lagrangian deformations (translations in the normal directions). Since there are no negative eigenvalues, the Morse index of L is 0. The nullity is 3, equal to the dimension of the moduli space of special Lagrangian tori near L (which is H1(L;R) ≅ R3). Thus L is a strict local minimum of the volume functional, and the critical manifold is Morse–Bott with index 0 and nullity 3.

Morse Theory for the Space of Instantons and the Donaldson–Segal Program

The space A(P) of connections on a principal G-bundle P → X over a closed 4-manifold X carries the Yang–Mills functional operatorname{YM}(A) = ∫_X |F_A|2 dV_g. The critical points are Yang–Mills connections satisfying d_A^* F_A = 0. The Morse index of a Yang–Mills connection A equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian H_A = d_A^* d_A + [F_A, ·] acting on Ω1(operatorname{ad} P) modulo gauge transformations. For an anti-self-dual (ASD) instanton (F_A+ = 0), the Hessian simplifies to H_A = d_A^* d_A on the Coulomb gauge slice d_A^* a = 0, and its negative eigenvalues correspond to the kernel of the deformation operator d_A+ : Ω1(operatorname{ad} P) → Ω+(operatorname{ad} P). The index of the deformation complex 0 → Ω0(operatorname{ad} P) xrightarrow{d_A} Ω1(operatorname{ad} P) xrightarrow{d_A+} Ω+(operatorname{ad} P) → 0 equals the expected dimension of the moduli space M_k of ASD connections with instanton number k = -(1)/(8π2) ∫_X operatorname{tr}(F_A wedge F_A). By the Atiyah–Singer index theorem, operatorname{ind}(d_A+) = 8k - 3(1-b1+b_+) for G = SU(2). The Morse–Bott theory for the Yang–Mills functional on the space of connections modulo gauge was developed by Atiyah and Bott for Riemann surfaces and by Donaldson for 4-manifolds. The critical manifolds are the moduli spaces M_k of ASD instantons, which are smooth of the expected dimension when the linearised operator is surjective. The normal Morse index of M_k is given by the spectral flow of the family of Hessians along paths in the space of connections. Donaldson used the gradient flow of the Chern–Simons functional on the 3-manifold boundary to define Floer homology groups HF_*(Y) for a homology 3-sphere Y, and proved that the Morse complex of the Yang–Mills functional on a 4-manifold with boundary Y computes the Donaldson invariants. The Donaldson–Segal program extends this to the space of all connections (not just ASD) by considering the gradient flow of the Yang–Mills functional on a 4-manifold with cylindrical end R × Y. The critical points at infinity correspond to instantons on R × Y, i.e., gradient flow lines of the Chern–Simons functional on Y. The resulting Morse–Bott–Floer complex has generators given by the critical points of the Chern–Simons functional (flat connections on Y) and differentials counting instantons on R × Y with prescribed asymptotics. The homology of this complex is the instanton Floer homology I_*(Y), which is isomorphic to the singular homology of the moduli space of flat connections on Y for certain 3-manifolds. The wall-crossing formula for Donaldson invariants under change of metric is derived from the Morse theory of the Yang–Mills functional on the space of metrics: the walls correspond to metrics for which there exist reducible ASD connections, and the jump in the invariants is computed by the spectral flow of the Hessian across the wall.

Morse Theory for the Space of Bridgeland Stability Conditions and the Kontsevich–Soibelman Wall-Crossing Formula

The space operatorname{Stab}(D) of Bridgeland stability conditions on a triangulated category D (such as the derived category of coherent sheaves on a Calabi–Yau 3-fold) is a complex manifold of dimension equal to the rank of the numerical Grothendieck group Knum(D). A stability condition σ = (Z, P) consists of a central charge Z : Knum(D) → C and a slicing P of D by semistable objects. The space operatorname{Stab}(D) carries a natural Morse function: the mass functional M(σ) = Σ_i |Z(γ_i)| for a fixed set of charges γ_i ∈ Knum(D). The critical points of M correspond to stability conditions where some objects become strictly semistable, i.e., where the central charge aligns the phases of two or more charges: arg Z(γ1) = arg Z(γ2). These are the walls of marginal stability. The Morse index of a wall is the number of charges whose phases cross the wall in a given direction. The Kontsevich–Soibelman wall-crossing formula describes how the Donaldson–Thomas invariants Ω(γ; σ) (which count semistable objects of charge γ) change as σ crosses a wall. The formula states that the product of quantum dilogarithms Πγ E(x_γ)Ω(γ; σ) is invariant under wall-crossing, where the product is ordered by the phase arg Z(γ). The Morse theory of the mass functional on operatorname{Stab}(D) provides a geometric interpretation: the walls are the critical manifolds of a Morse–Bott function, and the wall-crossing automorphisms are the monodromy of the gradient flow around the critical manifolds. The space operatorname{Stab}(D) is homotopy equivalent to a cell complex whose cells correspond to the chambers (connected components of the complement of the walls). The Morse inequalities for the mass functional imply that the Betti numbers of operatorname{Stab}(D) are bounded by the number of chambers. For the derived category of a quiver with potential, the space of stability conditions is related to the space of stability data on the associated 3-Calabi–Yau category, and the wall-crossing formula coincides with the mutation of quivers. The gradient flow of the mass functional on operatorname{Stab}(D) is related to the renormalisation group flow in the associated physical theory (e.g., N=2 supersymmetric gauge theory). The Morse complex of the mass functional has generators given by the chambers, and the differential counts gradient flow lines between chambers, which correspond to walls of marginal stability. The homology of this complex is the space of stability conditions modulo the action of the autoequivalence group of D. The Kontsevich–Soibelman formula can be derived by comparing the Morse complexes on either side of a wall and using the fact that the total complex is quasi-isomorphic to the complex of the whole space.

Morse Theory for the Space of Symplectic Cobordisms and Symplectic Field Theory

A symplectic cobordism from a contact manifold (Y_-, xi_-) to (Y_+, xi_+) is a compact symplectic manifold (W, ω) with boundary ∂ W = -Y_- sqcup Y_+ such that ω|Y_± = dα_± for contact forms α_± defining xi_±. The space C(Y_-, Y_+) of symplectic cobordisms carries a natural Morse function: the symplectic action functional A(W) = ∫_W ω^n (the symplectic volume). The critical points of A are symplectic cobordisms that are exact, i.e., ω = dλ for a Liouville form λ restricting to the contact forms on the boundary. The Morse index of an exact symplectic cobordism equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the linearised operator L = Δ_λ + R acting on 1-forms, where R is the curvature term arising from the Reeb dynamics on the boundary. Symplectic field theory (SFT), developed by Eliashberg, Givental, and Hofer, constructs a differential graded algebra whose generators are Reeb orbits on the contact boundaries and whose differential counts rigid pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectisation R × Y and in the cobordism W. The SFT differential is the Morse differential for the action functional on the space of Reeb orbits: the generators are the critical points of the action functional A(γ) = ∫_γ α on the loop space of Y, and the differential counts gradient flow lines, which are pseudoholomorphic cylinders in R × Y. The compactification of the moduli space of pseudoholomorphic curves includes broken curves, which correspond to the Morse–Bott boundary strata. The resulting SFT homology SH_*(Y) is the Morse homology of the action functional on the loop space of the contact manifold, and it is invariant under deformation of the contact structure. For a symplectic cobordism W, the SFT differential counts pseudoholomorphic curves with positive ends on Y_+ and negative ends on Y_-, and defines a chain map SH_*(Y_-) → SH_*(Y_+). The handle decomposition of the space of symplectic cobordisms induced by the symplectic volume functional corresponds to the decomposition of W into elementary cobordisms obtained by attaching symplectic handles (Weinstein handles). The Morse inequalities for the symplectic volume functional imply that the number of symplectic handles of index k is at least the k-th Betti number of the space of symplectic cobordisms. The Eliashberg–Gromov h-principle for symplectic cobordisms states that the space of symplectic structures on a given cobordism is homotopy equivalent to the space of almost complex structures compatible with the boundary conditions, and the Morse theory of the symplectic volume functional on this space reflects the topology of the space of almost complex structures. The contact homology algebra is the homology of the Morse complex of the action functional on the space of Reeb orbits, with the differential counting rigid pseudoholomorphic curves. The Bourgeois–Oancea isomorphism relates the contact homology of a contact manifold to the symplectic homology of its symplectisation, and the Morse theory of the action functional provides the geometric framework for this isomorphism.

Morse Theory for the Space of Gradient Flow Lines on Loop Spaces and Chas–Sullivan String Topology

The free loop space LM of a closed manifold M carries a natural Morse function given by the energy functional E(γ) = ½∫01|γ̇(t)|² dt. Critical points of E are precisely the closed geodesics on M, and the Morse index of a geodesic equals the number of conjugate points counted with multiplicity along the geodesic, as described by the classical Morse index theorem. The negative gradient flow of E with respect to the L² metric yields the heat flow on loops, which converges to constant loops after infinite time. By perturbing the metric on LM to achieve the Morse–Smale condition, one obtains a finite‑dimensional Morse complex whose homology is isomorphic to the singular homology of LM. Chas and Sullivan discovered that the intersection product on the homology of LM, induced by the concatenation of loops at a common base point, can be realised at the chain level by counting gradient flow lines that intersect the evaluation maps ev₀, ev₁ : LM → M. The resulting product, called the loop product, has degree −dim M and makes H*+dim M(LM) into a graded commutative algebra. Moreover, the S¹‑action on LM by rotating loops gives rise to a degree +1 operator Δ satisfying Δ² = 0 and the Batalin–Vilkovisky identity, turning the loop homology into a BV‑algebra. The Morse‑theoretic construction of these operations uses parametrised moduli spaces of gradient flow trees with marked points mapping to M via evaluation maps; compactifications of these moduli spaces correspond to broken flow lines and give the algebraic relations. A key technical point is the verification of the Palais–Smale condition for the energy functional on the Hilbert manifold LM, which holds because the energy is proper and bounded below. The resulting string topology operations have been computed for spheres, projective spaces, and Lie groups, and they detect non‑trivial geodesics via the Morse inequalities applied to the loop product. In particular, the existence of a non‑zero loop product class forces the existence of infinitely many geometrically distinct closed geodesics on any compact simply‑connected manifold, a result that extends the classical theorem of Gromoll and Meyer.

Morse Theory for the Space of Symplectic Forms and the Moser Stability Theorem

Let M be a closed manifold of dimension 2n. The space Symp(M) of symplectic forms on M is an open subset of the Fréchet space of closed 2‑forms, hence a smooth infinite‑dimensional manifold. The functional F(ω) = ∫M ωⁿ is the symplectic volume; its critical points are precisely the symplectic forms that are constant multiples of a fixed cohomology class. The Hessian of F at a critical form ω₀ can be identified with the operator L = *d* d acting on 1‑forms modulo exact forms, where * is the Hodge star of a compatible almost complex structure. The Morse index of ω₀ equals the number of negative eigenvalues of L, which is related to the topology of the space of compatible almost complex structures via the Gromov–Eliashberg h‑principle. Moser’s stability theorem states that if ωₜ is a smooth family of symplectic forms in the same cohomology class, then there exists a diffeotopy φₜ with φ₀ = id and φₜ* ωₜ = ω₀. In Morse‑theoretic language, this means that the sublevel sets of the volume functional are diffeomorphic as long as no critical value is crossed; the critical submanifolds correspond to the orbits of the diffeomorphism group acting on the cohomology class. The handle decomposition induced by the volume functional attaches handles of index equal to the Morse index of the critical symplectic forms. For a fixed cohomology class [ω] ∈ H²(M; ℝ), the space of symplectic forms representing [ω] is homotopy equivalent to the space of almost complex structures compatible with [ω], and the Morse inequalities imply that the number of critical symplectic forms of index k is at least the k‑th Betti number of this space. In dimension four, the Seiberg–Witten invariants provide constraints on the existence of symplectic forms, and the Morse theory of the volume functional interacts with the wall‑crossing phenomena for the Seiberg–Witten equations. The Moser argument itself can be viewed as a gradient flow for the functional ‖ω − ω₀‖² on the space of forms, where the flow lines are generated by a time‑dependent vector field solving the homotopy equation ιXₜ ωₜ = −α with dα = ωₜ − ω₀. This perspective connects the classical Moser trick with the modern infinite‑dimensional Morse theory of the symplectic volume functional.

Morse Theory for the Space of Contact Forms and the Contact Homology Algebra

On a closed contact manifold (Y, ξ = ker α) of dimension 2n−1, the space of contact forms C(Y, ξ) = {fα | f ∈ C∞(Y, ℝ+)} is an open convex cone in the Fréchet space of 1‑forms. The action functional A(γ) = ∫γ α on the free loop space LY has critical points exactly the Reeb orbits of α. The Morse index of a non‑degenerate Reeb orbit γ equals the Conley–Zehnder index μCZ(γ) computed from the linearised Reeb flow on the contact distribution ξ. The contact homology algebra CH*(Y, α) is the homology of the Morse complex generated by good Reeb orbits, with differential counting rigid pseudoholomorphic cylinders in the symplectisation ℝ × Y that are gradient flow lines of A. The grading is given by μCZ − n + 1. The compactification of the moduli space of cylinders includes broken trajectories, which correspond to the Morse–Bott boundary strata and give the differential the property ∂² = 0. The resulting algebra is independent of the choice of contact form α within the same contact structure, as shown by Bourgeois and Oancea via a continuation map induced by a monotone homotopy of contact forms. The Morse inequalities for the action functional imply that the number of Reeb orbits with Conley–Zehnder index k is at least the k‑th Betti number of the contact homology. In particular, if the contact homology vanishes, the contact manifold must be overtwisted; this recovers the Eliashberg classification of overtwisted contact structures in dimension three. The contact homology algebra carries a natural Batalin–Vilkovisky operator Δ induced by the S¹‑action on the loop space, and the pair (CH*, Δ) satisfies the BV‑identity. The Morse‑theoretic framework also yields the exact triangle relating contact homology, symplectic homology of the symplectisation, and the linearised contact homology, which is the algebraic counterpart of the Viterbo functoriality for Liouville domains.

Morse Theory for the Space of Foliations and the Godbillon–Vey Invariant

Let M be a closed manifold of dimension n and let Folq(M) denote the space of codimension‑q C² foliations on M, equipped with the C² topology. A foliation F can be described by a Haefliger cocycle, and the space Folq(M) is a Fréchet manifold whose tangent space at F consists of infinitesimal deformations of the tangent distribution. The Godbillon–Vey invariant GV(F) ∈ H²q+1(M; ℝ) is a secondary characteristic class defined for foliations with a transverse volume form. It can be realised as the critical value of a Morse functional on Folq(M) constructed as follows: choose a closed (2q+1)‑form Ω on the Haefliger classifying space BΓq representing the universal Godbillon–Vey class; pull it back via the classifying map cF : M → BΓq and integrate over M. The resulting functional G(F) = ∫M cF* Ω is invariant under leafwise homotopy and its critical points are precisely the foliations for which the Godbillon–Vey form is harmonic with respect to a leafwise metric. The Hessian of G at a critical foliation is a differential operator acting on the space of normal deformations; its Morse index equals the number of negative eigenvalues of this operator. Thurston’s stability theorem implies that for codimension‑one foliations without holonomy, the functional G is locally constant, so the Morse index is zero. In contrast, for Anosov foliations the index can be arbitrarily large, reflecting the richness of the deformation space. The handle decomposition of Folq(M) induced by G attaches handles of index equal to the Morse index of the critical foliations. The Morse inequalities then give lower bounds on the number of distinct foliations with a given Godbillon–Vey invariant in terms of the Betti numbers of the classifying space BΓq. In dimension three, the work of Thurston and Roussarie shows that the Godbillon–Vey invariant can take any real value, and the Morse theory of G provides a dynamical interpretation: gradient flow lines of G correspond to deformations that increase the transverse measure entropy, and the compactified moduli space of flow lines yields a stratification by the number of Reeb components created or destroyed along the flow.

Morse Theory for the Space of Embeddings and the Haefliger–Weber Theory

For a compact manifold N of dimension k and a manifold M of dimension n with n ≥ 2k+2, the space of smooth embeddings Emb(N, M) is an open subset of the Fréchet manifold C∞(N, M). The Haefliger–Weber theorem identifies the homotopy type of Emb(N, M) with the space of sections of a certain fibration over N whose fibre is the Stiefel manifold Vn−k(ℝn). A Morse function on Emb(N, M) can be obtained by pulling back a generic Morse function on M via the evaluation map ev : Emb(N, M) × N → M and integrating over N. The critical points of this functional are embeddings whose image is a union of critical points of the Morse function on M, and the Morse index equals the sum of the indices of those critical points weighted by the normal bundle data. The handle decomposition of Emb(N, M) induced by this functional corresponds to the classical surgery exact sequence for embeddings: attaching a handle of index i amounts to performing an i‑surgery on the embedded submanifold. The Morse inequalities imply that the number of isotopy classes of embeddings with a given normal invariant is at least the i‑th Betti number of the space of sections. In the metastable range n ≥ 2k+2, the Haefliger–Weber theorem guarantees that the embedding space is (n−2k−2)‑connected, so the first non‑trivial Morse critical points appear in index n−2k−1. The gradient flow lines of the functional are isotopies that move the embedding along the negative gradient of the Morse function on M; their compactification includes broken isotopies that correspond to the Whitney trick for eliminating double points. This Morse‑theoretic viewpoint recovers the Smale–Hirsch immersion theory as the index‑zero part of the handle decomposition and explains why the embedding space is highly connected in the metastable range. For knots (k=1, n=3), the functional reduces to the energy of a knot, and the Morse index of a critical knot equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian of the energy, which is related to the Vassiliev invariants via the Kontsevich integral.

Morse Theory for the Space of Metrics with Nonnegative Ricci Curvature and the Cheeger–Gromoll Splitting Theorem

The space MetRic≥0(M) of smooth Riemannian metrics on a closed manifold M satisfying Ric ≥ 0 is a convex subset of the Fréchet manifold of all metrics. A natural Morse function on this space is the Einstein–Hilbert functional E(g) = ∫M Rg dvolg restricted to the convex cone. Critical points of E in the interior are precisely the Ricci‑flat metrics; on the boundary they correspond to metrics with Ric ≥ 0 that admit a parallel vector field. The second variation formula shows that the Morse index of a Ricci‑flat metric equals the dimension of the space of infinitesimal Einstein deformations with negative Einstein constant, which by the work of Koiso and Besse is identified with the space of transverse‑traceless tensors satisfying the Lichnerowicz Laplacian eigenvalue condition λ < 0.

The Cheeger–Gromoll splitting theorem enters Morse theory through the observation that any metric in MetRic≥0(M) containing a line in its universal cover splits isometrically as a product ℝ × N. In the space of metrics this splitting manifests as a degenerate critical manifold: the set of product metrics forms a finite‑dimensional submanifold on which the Hessian of E has a kernel generated by the translational Killing field. Applying Morse–Bott theory, one attaches handles whose indices are shifted by the dimension of the kernel, yielding a handle decomposition of the stratum of splittable metrics. The Morse inequalities then give lower bounds on the Betti numbers of the moduli space of nonnegative Ricci curvature metrics in terms of the topology of the space of Ricci‑flat metrics and the splitting data.

Landmark results: Cheeger–Gromoll (1971) proved the splitting theorem; Perelman (2002) used the ℱ‑functional (a variant of the Einstein–Hilbert action) to analyse the space of metrics with Ric ≥ 0 on three‑manifolds, showing that the only non‑trivial critical points are flat metrics. In higher dimensions, the work of Anderson and Cheeger on the moduli space of Einstein metrics with Ric ≥ 0 shows that the Morse index of a Ricci‑flat metric is bounded above by the first Betti number of M. Examiners often test the distinction between the Morse index of the Einstein–Hilbert functional and the index of the Perelman ℱ‑functional; the latter includes a −n/2 grading shift coming from the volume normalisation.

Morse Theory for the Space of Symplectic Fillings and the Weinstein Conjecture

Let (Y, ξ) be a closed contact manifold of dimension 2n−1. A strong symplectic filling is a compact symplectic manifold (W, ω) with ∂W = Y and ω|ξ > 0. The space Fill(Y, ξ) of such fillings up to symplectic deformation equivalence carries a natural action functional A defined on the space of Reeb orbits in Y: for a Reeb orbit γ, A(γ) = ∫γ α where α is a contact form for ξ. Critical points of A are precisely the closed Reeb orbits; the Morse index of a non‑degenerate orbit γ equals its Conley–Zehnder index μCZ(γ) shifted by n−3, i.e. ind(γ) = μCZ(γ) − n + 3. This shift arises because the symplectic action functional lives on the loop space of W, whose tangent space splits into a contact part of dimension 2n−2 and a normal direction contributing the grading correction.

The Weinstein conjecture asserts that every contact manifold admits at least one closed Reeb orbit. In Morse‑theoretic language this is the statement that the action functional A has at least one critical point. When the contact form is non‑degenerate, the Morse complex generated by Reeb orbits with differential counting rigid holomorphic cylinders in the symplectisation ℝ × Y computes the cylindrical contact homology HC*(Y, ξ). The homology is independent of the choice of filling, so any filling provides a chain map from the Morse complex of A to the singular chain complex of the filling. The existence of a filling forces the contact homology to be non‑trivial, which in turn forces the existence of a Reeb orbit — a Morse‑theoretic proof of the Weinstein conjecture for fillable contact manifolds (Viterbo 1999, Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder 2003).

Degenerate contact forms lead to Morse–Bott families of Reeb orbits (typically circles or tori). The Morse–Bott complex replaces each critical manifold by its singular homology shifted by the Morse–Bott index, and the differential counts cascades of holomorphic curves with asymptotics on the critical manifolds. The resulting spectral sequence converges to the same contact homology. Examiners frequently ask for the precise grading shift in the contact homology differential: the differential lowers the Conley–Zehnder index by 1, but the overall grading on HC* is μCZ − n + 2, so the differential has degree −1 with respect to this grading.

Worked Example: Grading Shift in Contact Homology
Let (Y³, ξ) be a three‑dimensional contact manifold with a non‑degenerate contact form α. A Reeb orbit γ has Conley–Zehnder index μCZ(γ) = 3. Compute the grading of γ in cylindrical contact homology HC*(Y, ξ) and state the degree of the differential ∂ acting on γ.
In dimension 2n−1 = 3 we have n = 2. The grading on cylindrical contact homology is |γ| = μCZ(γ) − n + 2 = 3 − 2 + 2 = 3. The differential ∂ counts rigid holomorphic cylinders and lowers the Conley–Zehnder index by 1, hence it lowers the grading by 1. Therefore deg(∂) = −1, and ∂γ lives in grading 2.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for the Morse index of a Reeb orbit in a symplectic filling, first write down the dimension formula ind = μCZ − n + 3. Then check whether the contact form is non‑degenerate; if it is Morse–Bott, add the dimension of the critical manifold to the index. Remember that the grading on contact homology uses μCZ − n + 2, so the differential has degree −1. A quick mental check: for the standard contact sphere S2n−1 the simple Reeb orbit has μCZ = n, giving grading n − n + 2 = 2, which matches the known generator of HC2.

Morse Theory for the Space of Pseudoholomorphic Curves with Lagrangian Boundary Conditions and Open Gromov–Witten Invariants

Fix a symplectic manifold (M, ω) with a compact Lagrangian submanifold L. For a compatible almost complex structure J, consider the space Mapk((D², ∂D²), (M, L)) of J‑holomorphic maps from the disc with k boundary marked points. The energy functional E(u) = ∫D² u*ω is a Morse–Bott function on each component of the moduli space; its critical points are the constant maps at intersection points of L with itself (after a Hamiltonian perturbation) and the non‑constant J‑holomorphic discs. The Morse index of a non‑constant disc u equals the Maslov index μ(u) minus the dimension of the automorphism group of the domain (which is 3 for the disc). After quotienting by the automorphism group, the virtual dimension of the moduli space of discs with one boundary marked point is μ(u) − 1.

The gradient flow lines of E are solutions of the perturbed Cauchy–Riemann equation with Lagrangian boundary condition; compactification adds broken configurations consisting of a disc bubbling off a strip. This compactified moduli space carries a natural Kuranishi structure, and the resulting chain complex generated by intersection points with differential counting rigid discs is the Fukaya A∞ algebra CF*(L, L). The higher products mk count discs with k+1 boundary marked points and have degree 2−k, reflecting the Morse index shift of the evaluation maps. Open Gromov–Witten invariants are obtained by integrating cohomology classes over the virtual fundamental class of the compactified moduli space; they satisfy the open WDVV equations which are the A∞ relations.

Key results: Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono (2009) constructed the Kuranishi structure and proved the A∞ relations; Seidel (2008) showed that for exact Lagrangians in exact symplectic manifolds the energy functional satisfies the Palais–Smale condition, so the Morse complex is well defined without virtual techniques. Examiners often test the degree formula for the product mk: deg(mk) = 2 − k, and the fact that the unit in the Fukaya algebra is represented by the fundamental class of L shifted by the minimal Maslov number.

Morse Theory for the Space of Gradient Flows on Manifolds with Boundary and the Relative Morse Complex

Let M be a compact smooth manifold with boundary ∂M, and let f : M → ℝ be a Morse function that is constant on each boundary component and has no critical points on ∂M. Choose a gradient‑like vector field X that points outward on ∂M. The unstable manifolds Wu(p) of critical points p intersect ∂M transversely, giving a stratification of ∂M by the descending manifolds of the boundary critical points of the restriction f|∂M. The relative Morse complex C*(M, ∂M; f) is generated by interior critical points, with differential counting flow lines that stay in the interior; boundary critical points contribute to the relative homology via the long exact sequence of the pair.

The handle decomposition of M relative to ∂M attaches handles of index equal to the Morse index of interior critical points. The attaching maps land in the level set just below the critical value and are framed by the stable manifolds. When a critical point lies on the boundary, the corresponding handle is attached to ∂M × [0, ε) and the Morse index is computed with respect to the inward‑pointing normal direction. The relative Morse inequalities state that for each k, the number of interior critical points of index k is at least the k‑th relative Betti number bk(M, ∂M). Equality holds if and only if the gradient flow is Morse–Smale and the boundary restriction is a Morse function with no flow lines connecting boundary critical points of the same index.

Applications: The relative Morse complex computes the singular homology H*(M, ∂M) and underlies the proof of the h‑cobordism theorem for manifolds with boundary (Smale 1962). In Floer theory, the same construction appears for Lagrangian Floer homology where the boundary condition is a Lagrangian submanifold; the relative index is the Maslov index of the path of Lagrangian subspaces. Examiners frequently ask to distinguish between the absolute Morse complex of a closed manifold and the relative complex of a manifold with boundary, especially the shift in grading caused by the outward‑pointing gradient condition.

Morse Theory for the Space of Metrics with Positive Isotropic Curvature and the Brendle–Schoen Sphere Theorem

The Perelman F-functional F(g,f)=∫_M (R+|∇ f|2)e-fdV restricted to this subspace is a Morse–Bott function whose critical points are gradient Ricci solitons with PIC. The handle decomposition of MPIC(M) attaches handles of index equal to this Morse index; the attaching spheres are given by the unstable manifolds of the gradient flow of F. A key technical point is the verification of the Palais–Smale condition for F on the PIC subspace, which follows from Hamilton’s differential Harnack inequality and the compactness theorem for Ricci flow with surgery. Morse Theory for the Space of Minimal Hypersurfaces and the Almgren–Pitts Min‑Max Theory

Let (Mn+1,g) be a closed Riemannian manifold with 3≤ n+1≤ 7. The space Z_n(M) of integral n-currents modulo boundaries is a complete metric space under the flat norm. The area functional M:Z_n(M)toR is lower semicontinuous and its critical points are stationary integral varifolds, i.e. minimal hypersurfaces with possible singularities of codimension ≥7. Almgren (1965) and Pitts (1981) developed a min‑max theory that produces a non‑trivial critical point by considering sweepouts Φ:[0,1]toZ_n(M) and the min‑max width ω=inf_ΦsuptM(Φ(t)). The Morse index of the resulting minimal hypersurface Σ is bounded above by the number of parameters in the sweepout; for a one‑parameter family the index is at most one. Marques and Neves (2014) refined this to show that the min‑max limit is a smooth embedded minimal hypersurface of index ≤1 when n+1≤7. The handle decomposition of the sublevel sets {M≤ a} attaches handles of index equal to the Morse index of the critical varifolds; the attaching maps are given by the unstable manifolds of the gradient flow of the area functional in the sense of Brakke’s mean curvature flow. A crucial ingredient is the compactness theorem for integral varifolds with bounded area and genus, which guarantees that the min‑max sequence converges to a stationary varifold. Examiners frequently test the relationship between the min‑max width and the first eigenvalue of the Jacobi operator: ω = infΣM(Σ) where the infimum runs over all smooth embedded minimal hypersurfaces of index ≤1. The grading shift in the associated Morse complex is -operatorname{ind}(Σ), so the chain group in degree k is generated by critical varifolds of index k.

Morse Theory for the Space of Flat Connections and the Atiyah–Bott Symplectic Structure

Let Σ_g be a closed Riemann surface of genus g≥2 and G a compact Lie group. The moduli space Mflat(Σ_g,G) of flat G-connections modulo gauge equivalence is a finite‑dimensional symplectic manifold (possibly with orbifold singularities) of dimension (2g-2)dim G. Atiyah and Bott (1982) identified the Yang–Mills functional operatorname{YM}(A)=∫Σ_g\|F_A\|2 on the space of all connections as a Morse–Bott function whose critical sets are the strata of flat connections and higher‑energy Yang–Mills connections. The Morse index of a flat connection A equals twice the number of negative eigenvalues of the Laplacian d_A^*d_A on Ω1(Σ_g,g_P), which coincides with the dimension of the unstable manifold in the gradient flow of operatorname{YM}. The gradient flow lines are solutions of the heat equation ∂_t A = -d_A^*F_A; they converge to flat connections as t→∞. The Morse stratification of A(P) by unstable manifolds yields a decomposition of the infinite‑dimensional space into cells indexed by the Harder–Narasimhan type of the corresponding holomorphic bundle (via the Narasimhan–Seshadri theorem). The handle decomposition of the sublevel sets {operatorname{YM}≤ c} attaches handles of index equal to the Morse index; the attaching maps are given by the evaluation of the flow at a fixed time. A key result is that the equivariant cohomology of A(P) with respect to the gauge group computes the cohomology of Mflat via the Kirwan surjectivity theorem. Morse Theory for the Space of Holomorphic Vector Bundles and the Hitchin–Kobayashi Correspondence

On a compact Kähler manifold (X,ω) of complex dimension n, the space A1,1(E) of Hermitian connections on a holomorphic vector bundle E→ X carries the Yang–Mills–Higgs functional operatorname{YMH}(A,Φ)=∫_X (\|F_A0,2\|2+\|F_A1,1-λΦΦ^*\|2+\|bar∂_AΦ\|2)ω^n/n!, where Φ is a Higgs field. The functional operatorname{YMH} is a Morse–Bott function on the infinite‑dimensional space of pairs (A,Φ) modulo the complex gauge group. Its critical points correspond to solutions of the Hitchin equations (absolute minima) and to higher‑energy critical points classified by the Harder–Narasimhan filtration of the underlying Higgs bundle. The Morse index of a critical point equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the linearised Hitchin operator, which can be expressed in terms of the weights of the C^*-action on the associated graded Higgs bundle. The gradient flow of operatorname{YMH} is the Donaldson heat flow; its long‑time existence and convergence to a critical point were proved by Simpson (1988) for semistable Higgs bundles. The handle decomposition of the sublevel sets attaches handles of index equal to the Morse index; the cells are diffeomorphic to the unstable manifolds, which are affine bundles over the moduli space of the associated graded object. A crucial technical tool is the Uhlenbeck compactness for the sequence of connections along the flow, together with the Gieseker–Maruyama compactification of the moduli space of Higgs bundles.
