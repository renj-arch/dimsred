Morse Theory — Critical Points, Handle Decompositions & the h-Cobordism Theorem

Deep dive into Morse Theory — the analysis of smooth manifolds via critical points of differentiable functions, the Morse lemma (near a non-degenerate critical point, the function is f = Σ ± xᵢ²), the Morse inequalities (the Betti numbers are bounded by the numbers of critical points of each index), the handle decompositions (a critical point of index λ corresponds to attaching a λ-handle D^λ × D^{n−λ}), the Reeb's theorem (if a manifold has a Morse function with exactly two critical points, it is homeomorphic to a sphere), the Morse-Bott functions (critical submanifolds with non-degenerate normal form), the Bott periodicity theorem (Bott's proof using Morse theory on the loop space of Lie groups), the h-cobordism theorem (Smale's proof using handle cancellation and the Whitney trick), and the Floer homology: the infinite-dimensional Morse theory for the action functional on the loop space.

"Morse theory is the study of the topology of a manifold through the lens of a function."
— John Milnor
Part 1

📏 Morse Functions and the Morse Lemma

Critical Points
Morse Functions, the Morse Lemma, and Gradient Flow
A Morse function f: M → ℝ is a smooth function with all critical points non-degenerate: the Hessian H_p f (the matrix of second partials) is non-singular at each critical point p. The index λ(p) is the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian — the dimension of the subspace on which f decreases. The fundamental Morse lemma: near a non-degenerate critical point p, there exist local coordinates (x₁, …, x_n) such that f(x) = f(p) − Σ_{i=1}^λ x_i² + Σ_{i=λ+1}^n x_i². The gradient flow is the flow of −∇f (with respect to a Riemannian metric). The stable manifold W^s(p) of a critical point p is the set of points that flow to p as t → +∞; its dimension is n − λ(p). The unstable manifold W^u(p) has dimension λ(p). These are embedded disks (the Morse-Smale condition ensures transversality). The moduli space of gradient trajectories from a critical point p of index λ to q of index λ−1 is a finite set of isolated flow lines (when the metric is Morse-Smale). The Morse complex is generated by critical points with boundary operator counting these gradient lines — the beginning of Morse homology.
The Morse lemma normalizes the function near a critical point as a sum of squares with signs; the index determines the local geometry of the level sets and the topology change.
Part 2

🌍 The Morse Inequalities and Handle Decompositions

Topology
Morse Inequalities, Handle Attachment, and Handle Cancellation
Let C_λ = number of critical points of index λ of a Morse function f: M → ℝ on a closed manifold M. Let b_λ = dim H_λ(M; ℝ) be the Betti numbers. The strong Morse inequalities: for each k, C_k − C_{k−1} + … ± C₀ ≥ b_k − b_{k−1} + … ± b₀. The weak Morse inequalities: C_λ ≥ b_λ for each λ. The Euler characteristic: χ(M) = Σ (−1)^λ C_λ = Σ (−1)^λ b_λ. The handlebody decomposition: consider the sublevel sets M_c = f^{-1}(−∞, c]. When c passes a critical value of index λ (with non-degenerate critical point p), M_{c+ε} is obtained from M_{c−ε} by attaching a λ-handle D^λ × D^{n−λ} along the attaching sphere S^{λ−1} × D^{n−λ} embedded in the level set f^{-1}(c−ε). The attaching sphere is the intersection of the stable manifold of p with the level set. The core of the handle is D^λ × {0}; the co-core is {0} × D^{n−λ}. The handle cancellation (the cancellation lemma): if a λ-handle and a (λ+1)-handle have attaching spheres that intersect transversely in exactly one point, they can be cancelled, simplifying the handle decomposition. The Whitney trick makes this cancellation possible in high dimensions when the ambient manifold is simply connected — by removing excess intersection points.
The Morse inequalities relate the critical point counts to homological invariants; handle decompositions give a geometric realization of the cell complex structure of the manifold.
Part 3

🧮 The h-Cobordism Theorem

Cobordism
Smale's h-Cobordism Theorem and the Generalized Poincaré Conjecture
The h-cobordism theorem (Smale, 1961): Let W be a compact (n+1)-dimensional cobordism between two closed n-manifolds M₀ and M₁. If n ≥ 5, W is simply connected, and the inclusions M₀ ↪ W and M₁ ↪ W are homotopy equivalences (an h-cobordism), then W is diffeomorphic to the product M₀ × [0,1]. The proof proceeds by choosing a Morse function f: (W; M₀, M₁) → [0,1] with f(M₀) = 0, f(M₁) = 1, and with critical points all of indices strictly between 0 and n+1. Using handle cancellation, critical points can be eliminated in pairs (a λ-handle and a (λ+1)-handle) when their attaching spheres intersect in a single point. The Whitney trick is essential: in a simply connected manifold of dimension ≥ 5, two spheres of complementary dimension can be made disjoint by isotopy if they have zero algebraic intersection number. Repeated cancellation of handles produces a Morse function with no critical points — which implies W ≅ M₀ × [0,1]. The h-cobordism theorem implies the Poincaré conjecture in dimensions n ≥ 5: any homotopy n-sphere (n ≥ 5) is homeomorphic to S^n. Milnor's exotic spheres (1956) show the homeomorphism cannot be improved to diffeomorphism in general. The s-cobordism theorem (Siebenmann, Wall) generalizes to non-simply-connected cobordisms using the Whitehead torsion.
The h-cobordism theorem is a landmark of geometric topology — it classifies cobordisms that are homotopy products, using Morse theory to cancel critical points systematically.
Part 4

🔥 Floer Homology: Infinite-Dimensional Morse Theory

Infinite Dimensions
The Action Functional, Floer Homology, and the Arnold Conjecture
Floer homology (Floer, 1988) is the infinite-dimensional Morse theory for the symplectic action functional on the loop space LM = {γ: S¹ → M} of a symplectic manifold (M, ω). For an exact symplectic manifold with ω = dλ, define A(γ) = ∫_{S¹} γ^* λ. The critical points of A are the 1-periodic orbits of the Hamiltonian vector field X_H (where H_t: M → ℝ is a time-dependent Hamiltonian). The gradient of A with respect to the L² metric is −J ∂_t (where J is an almost complex structure compatible with ω). Floer's equation: ∂_s u + J ∂_t u + ∇H_t(u) = 0 for a map u: ℝ × S¹ → M — the solutions are pseudoholomorphic cylinders connecting periodic orbits. The Floer chain complex CF_*(H) is freely generated by the 1-periodic orbits (the critical points of A). The differential counts rigid gradient trajectories (solutions to Floer's equation with index difference 1). The resulting homology HF_*(M) is independent of the Hamiltonian (invariant under continuation maps). The Arnold conjecture: a Hamiltonian symplectomorphism of a closed symplectic manifold has at least as many fixed points as the manifold has critical points of a Morse function — i.e., at least Σ b_k(M). Floer proved this for monotone symplectic manifolds using Floer homology, showing #Fix(φ_H) ≥ Σ b_k(M) (the weak Arnold conjecture). The Morse-Bott theory generalizes Morse theory to functions with non-isolated critical submanifolds — the Bott periodicity theorem (π_k(U) = π_{k+2}(U)) was proved by Bott using Morse theory on the loop space ΩU of the unitary group. The Witten deformation (Witten, 1982) gives an analytic approach to Morse inequalities: the deformed Laplacian H_t = d_t d_t^* + d_t^* d_t where d_t = e^{−tf} d e^{tf} recovers the Morse complex via small eigenvalues that concentrate near critical points — the supersymmetric quantum mechanics interpretation of the Morse complex.
Floer homology is the infinite-dimensional Morse theory of the action functional — it has revolutionized symplectic geometry, proving the Arnold conjecture and leading to new invariants like Heegaard Floer homology.

Geometric Surgery and the Failure of the Whitney Trick in Dimension Four

The transition from dimension five down to dimension four represents a catastrophic breakdown in the standard techniques of geometric topology, specifically regarding handle cancellation. In dimensions n ≥ 5, Smale's h-Cobordism theorem relies heavily on the Whitney trick: if two spheres of complementary dimension intersect transversely, they can be separated by an isotopy provided their algebraic intersection number is zero. This allows one to remove pairs of handles without changing the homotopy type.

The critical exception occurs in dimension four. Here, the fundamental group π1 plays a decisive role. If the manifold is not simply connected, or even if it is simply connected but we are working with smooth structures rather than topological ones, the Whitney trick fails because the spheres cannot be isotoped to be disjoint without creating self-intersections that cannot be removed. This failure is the root cause of the existence of exotic R4s and exotic S4 candidates (the smooth Poincaré conjecture in dimension four remains open). The examiner often twists this concept by asking students to explain why a simply connected 4-manifold might not be diffeomorphic to its product cobordism despite satisfying the homotopy conditions of an h-cobordism. The answer lies in the distinction between topological and smooth categories; Freedman's work established the classification in the topological category, while Donaldson theory using gauge fields revealed that many smooth 4-manifolds are not homeomorphic to standard ones.

Technique: Analyzing the Failure of Whitney Isotopy
Consider a compact h-cobordism W between two homology spheres M0 and M1 in dimension 4. Suppose π1(W) = 0. Why does the standard handle cancellation argument fail to prove W ≅ M0 × [0,1]?
The argument fails because the Whitney trick requires an isotopy of the attaching spheres in a simply connected manifold. In dimension 4, the codimension of the intersection is 2 (since 2+2=4). The obstruction to performing the Whitney move lies in the second homotopy group π2. Unlike higher dimensions where the embedding space allows enough room to untangle intersections, in dimension 4, the "room" is insufficient. Specifically, the self-intersection number of a sphere in a 4-manifold is not well-defined in the same way as in higher dimensions without careful framing, and the algebraic intersection condition is not sufficient to guarantee an isotopy to disjointness because the normal bundle geometry is constrained by the signature of the intersection form. This leads to the fact that there are uncountably many smooth structures on R4.
Exam-Day Strategy: Dimensional Thresholds
When solving problems involving cobordisms or handle decompositions, immediately check the dimension. If n ≥ 5, assume the Whitney trick works for simply connected manifolds and focus on algebraic intersection numbers. If n = 4, switch your mental model to gauge theory invariants (Donaldson polynomials) or topological classification via the intersection form signature. The examiner loves to present a scenario that looks like an h-cobordism in dimension 5 but subtly implies dimension 4 to trap you into applying the Whitney trick blindly.

Morse-Bott Theory and Non-Isolated Critical Manifolds

Standard Morse theory assumes critical points are isolated. However, many geometric situations involve functions with non-isolated critical sets, such as the height function on a torus S1 × S1 embedded in R3, where entire circles of critical points exist. This leads to Morse-Bott theory. Here, the critical set is a submanifold N_λ. The index is defined as the dimension of the normal bundle restricted to N_λ. The local model near a critical submanifold N is given by φ(x,y) = -(1)/(2) Σi=1^λ x_i2 + (1)/(2) Σj=λ+1^n y_j2, where (x,y) are coordinates on N and its normal bundle.

The gradient flow lines now connect critical submanifolds rather than points. The Morse inequalities generalize to relate the Betti numbers of the manifold to the sum of Betti numbers of the critical submanifolds weighted by their indices. A key application is the study of loop spaces in symplectic geometry, where the action functional naturally has degenerate critical points (the periodic orbits). Understanding this generalization is crucial for advanced problems involving moduli spaces of pseudoholomorphic curves.

Technique: Computing Indices on Critical Submanifolds
Let M be a manifold and f: M → R be a Morse-Bott function. Let N be a connected critical submanifold of dimension k. The Hessian of f restricted to the normal bundle nu N has signature (λ, n-k-λ). If N = S1, calculate the contribution of this critical set to the Morse polynomial in terms of its index λ.
The contribution to the Morse polynomial is not a single term but a sum over the homology classes represented by the flow lines. However, for the purpose of counting generators in the chain complex, we associate the critical submanifold N with the homology group generated by its fundamental class (and potentially others if N wraps around). The index λ determines the degree of the generator in the homology of the sublevel set. Specifically, if the index is λ, the critical manifold contributes to the relative homology in dimension λ. Unlike isolated points where we count multiplicity by the Hessian determinant, here we must integrate over the normal bundle or use the Euler characteristic of the critical set weighted by the local index.

Witten Deformation and Supersymmetric Quantum Mechanics

The connection between Morse theory and quantum mechanics is profound, formalized through Edward Witten's deformation. Consider the Laplacian on differential forms Δ = d d^* + d^* d. Witten introduced a deformed operator Δ_t by twisting the exterior derivative with the exponential of a Morse function f: d_t = e-tf d etf. The new operator is Δ_t = (d + t df) (d^* - t df^*) + …, which can be interpreted as a Hamiltonian in supersymmetric quantum mechanics. As t → ∞, the spectrum of Δ_t concentrates near the critical points of f. The low-lying eigenvalues correspond to harmonic forms localized at the critical points.

This analytic approach provides a rigorous proof of Morse inequalities without relying solely on combinatorial counting. It shows that the Euler characteristic chi(M) is the alternating sum of the dimensions of the cohomology groups, which matches the alternating sum of the number of critical points in a perfect Morse function. The "Witten Laplacian" allows one to perturb non-Morse functions into Morse functions while controlling the error terms via spectral analysis. This is a standard advanced topic where examiners ask for the spectral interpretation of the Morse complex.

Technique: Spectral Interpretation of Critical Points
Explain how Witten deformation relates the number of critical points of index k to the dimension of the k-th cohomology group H^k(M). Why does this relation hold asymptotically as the deformation parameter t → ∞?
The deformed operator Δ_t has a kernel that consists of forms supported near the critical points. Specifically, in a neighborhood of a critical point of index λ, the eigenfunctions decay exponentially away from the critical point with rate proportional to t. The dimension of the eigenspace corresponding to small eigenvalues approximates the number of critical points of index λ (with multiplicity). By Lefschetz duality and the properties of the heat kernel, the limit as t → ∞ recovers the Morse inequalities: b_k(M) ≤ #{p mid index(p)=k}. The equality holds if and only if the function is a perfect Morse function.
  • Confusing Topological vs. Smooth Categories: In dimension 4, a topological h-cobordism implies a product structure (Freedman), but a smooth h-cobordism does not necessarily imply a diffeomorphism to the product due to exotic structures.
  • Misapplying the Whitney Trick: Never apply the Whitney trick in dimension 4 without checking the fundamental group and the specific embedding obstruction. The algebraic intersection number being zero is necessary but not sufficient in dim 4.
  • Ignoring Morse-Bott Indices: When a critical set is a submanifold, do not treat it as a single point with an arbitrary index; calculate the index via the normal bundle signature and account for the dimension of the submanifold itself in the spectral sequence.
  • Overlooking Witten's Parameter: In problems involving Morse inequalities, remember that the combinatorial count is an upper bound unless the function is perfect. The Witten deformation explains why the bound becomes tight asymptotically.
