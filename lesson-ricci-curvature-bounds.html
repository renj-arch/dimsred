Synthetic Ricci Curvature Bounds — CD(K,N) & the Lott–Sturm–Villani Theory

Deep dive into Synthetic Ricci Curvature Bounds — the program of defining Ricci curvature lower bounds on metric measure spaces without a smooth Riemannian structure. Topics covered include the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus (the Γ₂(f) = ½Δ|∇f|² − ⟨∇f, ∇Δf⟩ criterion characterizing Ric ≥ K via Γ₂(f) ≥ K|∇f|²), the equivalence between the Γ₂ criterion and the Wasserstein K-convexity of entropy for Riemannian manifolds, the Lott–Sturm–Villani CD(K,N) condition (the K-convexity of the Rényi entropy S_N along W₂-geodesics in the space of probability measures), the Sturm–von Renesse theorem (CD(K,∞) is equivalent to Ric ≥ K on Riemannian manifolds), the tensorization property (the product of CD(K,Nᵢ) spaces is CD(K, Σ Nᵢ)), the stability under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence (limits of Riemannian manifolds with Ric ≥ K satisfy CD(K,N) — this is the foundation for studying limits in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense), the Bishop–Gromov volume comparison theorem (vol(B(p,R)) / vol(B(p,r)) ≤ (R/r)^N for Ric ≥ K(N−1) with N the dimension), the Bonnet–Myers diameter bound (Ric ≥ K > 0 implies diam(M) ≤ π√((N−1)/K)), the Cheeger–Gromoll splitting theorem (a complete manifold with Ric ≥ 0 containing a line splits as ℝ × N), the Gromov–Hausdorff precompactness theorem (a collection of manifolds with uniform Ricci lower bound and diameter upper bound is precompact), and the RCD(K,N) condition (adding the infinitesimally Hilbertian condition to eliminate Finsler-like spaces) with the splitting theorem, rectifiability, and the regular set of full measure.

Part 1

📏 Ricci Curvature and Geodesic Convexity of Entropy

Ricci
The Bakry–Émery Γ₂ Calculus, the Wasserstein Interpretation, and the von Renesse–Sturm Theorem
Ricci curvature Ric is a (0,2)-tensor defined as the trace of the Riemann curvature: Ric(X, Y) = Σᵢ ⟨R(X, eᵢ)Y, eᵢ⟩ for an orthonormal frame {eᵢ}. On a Riemannian manifold (M, g), Ric ≥ K means Ric(v, v) ≥ K|v|² for all tangent vectors v. The Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus (1984): define Γ(f, g) = ½(Δ(fg) − fΔg − gΔf) = ⟨∇f, ∇g⟩, and Γ₂(f, g) = ½(ΔΓ(f, g) − Γ(f, Δg) − Γ(g, Δf)). For smooth functions, Γ₂(f) = Ric(∇f, ∇f) + ‖Hess f‖². The Γ₂ criterion: Ric ≥ K iff Γ₂(f) ≥ K|∇f|² = K Γ(f) for all smooth f. The Bakry–Émery–Ledoux inequality: if Ric ≥ K > 0, then for any Lipschitz function f, the variance satisfies Var_μ(f) ≤ (1/K) ∫ |∇f|² dμ (the Poincaré inequality), and also the log-Sobolev inequality Ent_μ(f²) ≤ (2/K) ∫ |∇f|² dμ. The von Renesse–Sturm theorem (2005): on a compact Riemannian manifold M, the following are equivalent: (i) Ric ≥ K, (ii) the Boltzmann–Shannon entropy H(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dvol is K-convex along Wasserstein-2 geodesics in P(M), (iii) the Monge–Ampère equation has a unique solution and W₂ satisfies the K-convexity inequality: H(μ_t) ≤ (1−t)H(μ₀) + tH(μ₁) − (K/2) t(1−t) W₂²(μ₀, μ₁). This is the bridge between classical Ricci curvature and the synthetic CD condition. The Γ₂ criterion on metric measure spaces: in the RCD setting, Γ₂ can be defined via the heat flow and the carré du champ operator, providing an analytic characterization of curvature bounds.
The equivalence between Ric ≥ K and K-convexity of entropy in the Wasserstein space is the fundamental insight that allows Ricci curvature to be defined synthetically on metric measure spaces without any smooth structure.
Part 2

🌐 The CD(K,N) Condition

Synthetic
The Lott–Sturm–Villani CD(K,N) Condition, Geodesic Convexity, and Displacement Convexity Classes
The CD(K,N) condition (Lott–Sturm–Villani, 2005) is defined for a metric measure space (X, d, m). Let P₂(X, m) be the space of probability measures with finite second moment that are absolutely continuous with respect to m. For μ₀ = ρ₀ m and μ₁ = ρ₁ m in P₂(X, m), define the Rényi entropy S_N(μ) = −∫ ρ^{1−1/N} dm (with N ∈ (1, ∞)) and the Boltzmann–Shannon entropy H(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dm for N = ∞. The space satisfies CD(K,N) if for every pair μ₀, μ₁ ∈ P₂(X, m), there exists a W₂-geodesic μ_t (an optimal transport interpolation) such that for all t ∈ [0, 1]: S_N(μ_t) ≤ (1−t) S_N(μ₀) + t S_N(μ₁) − (K/2) t(1−t) W₂²(μ₀, μ₁) in the case N = ∞; for finite N, the inequality involves the distortion coefficients τ_{K,N}^{(t)}(θ) = t^{1/N} (sin(tθ)/sin θ)^{N−1} when K > 0 and the hyperbolic sine when K < 0. The Sturm distortion coefficients τ_{K,N}^{(t)} encode the Jacobi field behavior in model spaces. For K = 0, τ₀,N^{(t)} = t. For K > 0, the coefficients come from spheres S^N_R with Ric = K(N−1). The CD(K,N) condition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence (Sturm, Lott–Villani): if (X_n, d_n, m_n) → (X, d, m) in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense and each X_n satisfies CD(K,N), then X satisfies CD(K,N). This stability is crucial for studying limits of Riemannian manifolds. The tensorization property: if (X₁, d₁, m₁) satisfies CD(K,N₁) and (X₂, d₂, m₂) satisfies CD(K,N₂), then the product X₁ × X₂ with the L² metric and product measure satisfies CD(K, N₁+N₂). The local-to-global property (Cavalletti–Milman): if (X, d, m) has the CD(K,N) property locally (every point has a neighborhood that is CD(K,N)), then the global space is CD(K,N). The CD(K,N) condition implies the Bishop–Gromov inequality: for any ball B(x, R), the volume ratio vol(B(x,R))/vol(B(x,r)) ≤ vol(B_K(N−1)(R))/vol(B_K(N−1)(r)) where B_K(N−1)(r) is a ball in the model space of constant curvature K/(N−1).
The CD(K,N) condition is the definitive synthetic notion of Ricci curvature bounded below by K and dimension bounded above by N — it is stable, tensorizable, and recovers all classical geometric consequences of Ricci curvature bounds.
Part 3

🔬 Stability and Tensorization

Consequences
Gromov–Hausdorff Stability, Tensorization, and Geometric Consequences of CD(K,N)
The measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence (Fukaya, 1985) extends Gromov–Hausdorff convergence to metric measure spaces by including the convergence of measures. A sequence (X_n, d_n, m_n) converges to (X, d, m) if there exist metric isometric embeddings into a common metric space such that the spaces converge in the Hausdorff sense and the pushforward measures converge weakly. The stability theorem (Sturm, Lott–Villani): the CD(K,N) condition is preserved under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. This is the foundation for the Gromov precompactness theorem: a collection of Riemannian manifolds M_n with Ric_{M_n} ≥ K(N−1) and diam(M_n) ≤ D is precompact in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology. The limits are metric measure spaces satisfying CD(K,N) but may have singularities. The Bishop–Gromov volume comparison for CD(K,N) spaces: the function r ↦ m(B(x,r)) / v_{K,N}(r) is non-increasing, where v_{K,N}(r) is the volume of a ball of radius r in the model space (the simply connected space form of constant curvature K/(N−1)). The Bonnet–Myers theorem for CD(K,N): if K > 0, then diam(X) ≤ π√((N−1)/K). The Abresch–Gromoll inequality (a version of the splitting theorem): in an CD(0,N) space with a line, the space splits as ℝ × Y with the product measure. The Lévy–Gromov isoperimetric inequality: for a CD(K,N) space with K > 0, the isoperimetric profile I(θ) ≥ I_{K,N}(θ) where I_{K,N} is the profile of the model space. The Poincaré inequality and the log-Sobolev inequality hold on CD(K,N) spaces when K > 0 with constants determined by K. The spectral gap λ₁ of the Laplacian on a CD(K,N) space satisfies λ₁ ≥ K/(N−1) when K > 0 (the Lichnerowicz estimate).
The geometric consequences of CD(K,N) include the classical Bonnet–Myers, Bishop–Gromov, and Lichnerowicz theorems — proving that the synthetic condition captures the full essence of Ricci curvature bounds.
Part 4

🏛️ RCD Spaces and the Synthetic Program

RCD
The RCD(K,N) Condition, the Splitting Theorem, Rectifiability, and the Future of Synthetic Curvature
The RCD(K,N) condition (Gigli, Ambrosio–Tresoldi, 2010) refines CD(K,N) by adding the requirement that the space is infinitesimally Hilbertian: the Sobolev space W^{1,2}(X) (defined via the Cheeger energy) is a Hilbert space. This excludes Finsler-like metric measure spaces that satisfy CD(K,N) but are not Riemannian — e.g., a finite-dimensional ℓ^p-space with p ≠ 2 satisfies CD(0,N) but not any RCD condition. The splitting theorem (Gigli, 2013): an RCD(0,N) space that contains a line is isometric to ℝ × Y for some RCD(0,N−1) space Y. This is the synthetic analogue of the Cheeger–Gromoll splitting theorem. The rectifiability theorem (Mondino–Naber, 2013): an RCD(K,N) space is rectifiable — there exists a regular set ℛ of full measure such that every point of ℛ has a tangent cone isometric to ℝ^k for some k (the dimension). The regular set is open in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense and has a C^{1,α} structure. The essential dimension of an RCD(K,N) space is an integer n ≤ N (the dimension of the regular set). The heat kernel on an RCD(K,N) space exists, is unique, and satisfies Gaussian estimates (the Li–Yau type bounds). The RCD program has led to major advances: the stability of the heat kernel under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence (Gigli–Mondino), the topological regularity of RCD spaces (De Philippis–Gigli–Pasqualetto), the Ahlfors regularity of the measure, and the Brenier theorem on RCD spaces (Gigli). The Cheeger–Colding theory of limits of Riemannian manifolds with Ricci bounds (pre-2005) already showed that the limit spaces have a regular-singular decomposition, are rectifiable, and that the singular set has codimension at least 2 — these results are now recovered and extended in the RCD framework. The synthetic program continues to grow: Ricci curvature bounds for metric measure spaces with variable curvature, the CD(K,N) with variable N, the Riemannian cone characterization of RCD spaces, and the connections to the Enriched Ricci flow (Sturm–Lott).
The RCD(K,N) condition is the definitive Riemannian synthetic Ricci curvature bound — it is stable, rectifiable, and carries all the analytic and geometric consequences of classical Ricci curvature, opening the door to understanding highly singular spaces that arise as limits.

The Bakry–Émery Γ₂ Calculus and the Equivalence to CD(K,N)

The Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus provides an analytic formulation of Ricci curvature lower bounds that is completely equivalent to the optimal‑transport definition of CD(K,N) on infinitesimally Hilbertian spaces. Introduced by Dominique Bakry and Michel Émery in 1985, the calculus starts from the carré du champ operator Γ(f,g)=½(Δ(fg)−fΔg−gΔf) and its iterated version Γ₂(f,g)=½(ΔΓ(f,g)−Γ(f,Δg)−Γ(g,Δf)). The curvature‑dimension inequality CD(K,N) is then expressed as Γ₂(f)≥KΓ(f)+1/N(Δf)² for all smooth f, where Γ(f)=Γ(f,f). On a smooth Riemannian manifold this inequality is exactly the Bochner formula Ric(∇f,∇f)≥K|∇f|² together with the dimension bound N. The equivalence theorem (Sturm 2006, Lott–Villani 2009, Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré 2014) states that for a metric measure space (X,d,m) which is infinitesimally Hilbertian, the following are equivalent: (i) the space satisfies the CD(K,N) condition in the sense of displacement convexity of the relative entropy; (ii) the Bakry–Émery inequality Γ₂(f)≥KΓ(f)+1/N(Δf)² holds for all f in the domain of the Cheeger energy; (iii) the heat flow is K‑contractive in the Wasserstein distance W₂. This triple equivalence is the cornerstone of the synthetic theory because it allows one to switch between geometric, analytic, and probabilistic viewpoints at will. In particular, the Γ₂ calculus yields immediate functional inequalities: the Poincaré inequality with constant 1/K for K>0, the log‑Sobolev inequality with constant 2/K, and the exponential integrability of Lipschitz functions. Moreover, the calculus extends naturally to the setting of CD(K,∞) by dropping the 1/N term, and to the refined RCD(K,N) condition by requiring the underlying Sobolev space W¹,² to be Hilbertian. The Γ₂ formalism also underlies the proof of the splitting theorem in the synthetic setting: if an RCD(0,N) space contains a line, the Γ₂ inequality forces the existence of a parallel gradient field, which integrates to an isometric splitting ℝ×Y. Thus the Bakry–Émery calculus is not merely a computational tool; it is the analytic backbone that makes the synthetic Ricci curvature program both rigorous and flexible.

Displacement Convexity of Entropy and the Lott–Villani–Sturm Theory

The Lott–Villani–Sturm theory recasts Ricci curvature lower bounds as convexity properties of the Boltzmann–Shannon entropy along Wasserstein geodesics. Given a metric measure space (X,d,m), the relative entropy of a probability measure ρm with respect to m is Entₘ(ρ)=∫ρlogρ dm if ρlogρ∈L¹(m) and +∞ otherwise. A space satisfies CD(K,N) if for every pair of probability measures μ₀,μ₁ with bounded support and densities ρ₀,ρ₁, there exists a W₂‑geodesic (μₜ)ₜ∈[0,1] such that the entropy satisfies the displacement convexity inequality Entₘ(μₜ)≤(1−t)Entₘ(μ₀)+tEntₘ(μ₁)−K/2 t(1−t)W₂²(μ₀,μ₁)−(1/N)∫₀¹ t(1−t) |∇ₜ|² dt, where the last term involves the metric speed of the geodesic. For N=∞ the dimension term disappears and one recovers the CD(K,∞) condition. The theory shows that on a smooth Riemannian manifold this convexity is equivalent to Ric≥Kg and dim≤N. The breakthrough of Lott–Villani (2009) and Sturm (2006) was to prove that this definition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, thereby providing a notion of Ricci curvature that makes sense on singular limit spaces. The displacement convexity formulation also yields sharp geometric inequalities: the Brunn–Minkowski inequality, the Bishop–Gromov volume comparison, and the Bonnet–Myers diameter bound all follow from the convexity of entropy along suitable geodesics. Furthermore, the theory identifies the precise distortion coefficients τK,N(t)(θ) that appear in the Jacobian of the optimal transport map; these coefficients are exactly the ratios of volumes of balls in the model space of constant curvature K/(N−1). The Lott–Villani–Sturm approach also clarifies the role of the reference measure m: the entropy is defined relative to m, and the CD condition forces m to behave like a volume measure with Ricci bounds. This insight led to the development of the RCD condition, where one additionally requires the Cheeger energy to be quadratic, thus excluding Finsler‑type spaces that satisfy CD(K,N) but lack a Riemannian structure. In summary, displacement convexity of entropy is the geometric heart of synthetic Ricci curvature, linking optimal transport, functional inequalities, and the structure theory of limit spaces.

Worked Example: Verifying CD(0,N) on a Cone
Let (X,d,m) be the metric measure cone over a compact RCD(N−2,N−1) space (Y,d_Y,m_Y) with the cone metric dr²+r²d_Y² and measure rN−1dr dm_Y. Show that (X,d,m) satisfies CD(0,N).
The cone construction is a standard model for CD(0,N) spaces. First, note that the base (Y,d_Y,m_Y) satisfies CD(N−2,N−1) by hypothesis. The warped product formula for the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus on a cone gives Γ₂^X(f) = Γ₂^Y(f) + (N−1)/r² Γ^X(f) for functions depending only on the radial variable. Since the base has Γ₂^Y ≥ (N−2)Γ^Y + 1/(N−1)(Δ^Y f)², a direct computation using the product structure shows that the cone satisfies Γ₂^X ≥ 0·Γ^X + 1/N (Δ^X f)², i.e. CD(0,N). Equivalently, one can use the displacement convexity definition: optimal transport on the cone reduces to transport on the base combined with radial scaling, and the entropy along such geodesics is convex because the base entropy is (N−2)-convex and the radial part contributes the missing 2/N term. The measured Gromov–Hausdorff stability of CD(K,N) then guarantees that any limit of such cones remains CD(0,N). This example illustrates how the synthetic theory captures the classical fact that a Riemannian cone over an Einstein manifold with Ric = (N−2)g has non‑negative Ricci curvature.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks to verify a CD(K,N) condition on a warped product or cone, first identify the base curvature‑dimension parameters. Then apply the known warped‑product formulas for Γ₂ or for the distortion coefficients τK,N(t). Remember that the cone over an RCD(N−2,N−1) space is CD(0,N); the suspension over an RCD(K,N) space yields CD(K+1,N+1). These transformation rules are frequently tested because they avoid heavy computation.

Heat Kernel Estimates and Functional Inequalities on RCD(K,N) Spaces

On an RCD(K,N) space the heat kernel exists, is unique, and satisfies two‑sided Gaussian estimates that mirror the classical Li–Yau bounds on smooth manifolds. The construction of the heat flow via the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in the L²‑Wasserstein space (Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré 2014) yields a Markov semigroup (Pₜ)ₜ≥₀ that is symmetric, mass preserving, and satisfies the Bakry–Émery gradient estimate |∇Pₜf|² ≤ e−2Kt Pₜ(|∇f|²). From this gradient estimate one deduces the parabolic Harnack inequality and the Gaussian upper bound pₜ(x,y) ≤ C t−N/2 exp(−d²(x,y)/(4t) + Kt) for the heat kernel pₜ. A matching lower bound holds on the regular set ℛ, which has full measure and where tangent cones are Euclidean. These estimates imply the full suite of functional inequalities: the Poincaré inequality ∫|f−⨍f|² dm ≤ Cₚ ∫|∇f|² dm with Cₚ = 1/K for K>0, the log‑Sobolev inequality Entₘ(f²) ≤ 2/K ∫|∇f|² dm, and the Sobolev inequality (∫|f|2N/(N−2) dm)(N−2)/N ≤ Cₛ ∫|∇f|² dm for N>2. The constants are sharp and depend only on K and N. Moreover, the heat kernel is Hölder continuous in both variables and satisfies the semigroup property PₜPₛ = Pₜ₊ₛ. The stability of the heat kernel under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence (Gigli–Mondino 2018) means that if (Xₙ,dₙ,mₙ) → (X,d,m) in the mGH sense and each Xₙ is RCD(K,N), then the heat kernels pₜⁿ converge locally uniformly to pₜ. This stability is crucial for passing to limits in geometric analysis problems, for instance in the study of the Ricci flow through singularities. The heat kernel also provides a probabilistic representation of the distance via Varadhan's formula limt→0 −4t log pₜ(x,y) = d²(x,y), which holds on any RCD(K,N) space. In summary, the analytic machinery of the heat kernel on RCD spaces reproduces the classical theory of Ricci curvature bounds and extends it to non‑smooth settings with optimal constants.

Variable Curvature Bounds and the CD(K,N) Condition with Variable Dimension

The classical CD(K,N) condition assumes constant lower Ricci bound K and constant dimension bound N. Recent work has developed a variable‑curvature version CD(K(·),N(·)) where K and N are functions on the space. This generalization is motivated by the need to describe spaces that arise as limits of manifolds with non‑constant Ricci curvature, or by the study of weighted manifolds where the effective dimension changes from point to point. The definition replaces the constant distortion coefficients τK,N(t)(θ) by pointwise coefficients τK(x),N(x)(t)(θ) that depend on the base point x of the geodesic. The resulting condition is still stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence provided the functions K,N converge uniformly on compact sets. A key result (Ketterer 2015, Cavalletti–Mondino 2017) shows that on an essentially non‑branching space the variable CD condition is equivalent to a pointwise Bochner inequality Γ₂(f)(x) ≥ K(x)Γ(f)(x) + 1/N(x) (Δf(x))² for all f in the domain of the Laplacian. This equivalence allows one to transfer the whole analytic toolkit — heat kernel estimates, functional inequalities, splitting theorems — to the variable setting, with constants that now depend on the local values of K and N. Applications include the study of metric measure spaces with boundary, where the dimension bound drops by one at the boundary, and the analysis of Ricci flow singularities where the curvature blows up and the effective dimension may change. The variable theory also clarifies the relationship between CD(K,N) and the measure‑contraction property MCP(K,N): MCP is exactly the CD condition with the dimension term evaluated only at the initial point of the geodesic, making it strictly weaker. Understanding the variable CD condition is essential for the next generation of synthetic geometry, where one aims to treat spaces with genuinely non‑constant curvature such as the limits of Kähler–Ricci flow or the metric measure spaces arising in optimal transport with non‑quadratic costs.

Synthetic Ricci Flow and the Evolution of Metric Measure Spaces

The synthetic Ricci flow program, initiated by Sturm and Lott, seeks to define a flow on the space of metric measure spaces that reduces to Hamilton's Ricci flow on smooth manifolds and continues past singularities. The central idea is to interpret the Ricci flow as the gradient flow of the Perelman entropy functional ℱ(g,f)=∫(R+|∇f|²)e−f dvol in the Wasserstein space of probability measures. In the synthetic setting, one replaces the smooth entropy by the relative entropy Entₘ(ρ) and considers the gradient flow of the functional ℱ(ρ)=Entₘ(ρ) + ∫V dρ for a suitable potential V. The resulting flow (Xₜ,dₜ,mₜ) satisfies a family of Evolution Variational Inequalities (EVI) that encode the CD(Kₜ,Nₜ) condition along the flow. A major theorem (Sturm 2018, Lott 2019) states that if the initial space is RCD(K,N), then there exists a unique synthetic Ricci flow for short time, and the flow preserves the RCD condition with time‑dependent bounds Kₜ,Nₜ. The flow also satisfies a synthetic version of Perelman's monotonicity formulas: the reduced volume and the ℒ‑length are non‑increasing along the flow. These monotonicities provide compactness and regularity theory for the flow, allowing one to analyze singularity formation. In particular, any blow‑up limit of the synthetic Ricci flow at a singular time is a metric measure space satisfying CD(0,N) that splits off a line, hence by the splitting theorem is a product ℝ×Y. This recovers the classical Hamilton–Ivey pinching and the classification of ancient solutions in the smooth case. The synthetic flow also interacts with the heat kernel: the conjugate heat kernel evolves by the adjoint equation and satisfies the same Gaussian bounds with time‑dependent constants. Recent advances (Bamler 2020, Haslhofer–Naber 2021) have extended the theory to the setting of RCD spaces with variable curvature bounds, opening the way to a unified treatment of Ricci flow and optimal transport. For exam purposes, the key takeaways are: (1) synthetic Ricci flow is defined via EVI gradient flows of entropy; (2) it preserves the RCD condition; (3) monotonicity formulas survive in the non‑smooth setting; (4) singularity models are CD(0,N) spaces that split. These points are frequently tested in advanced geometric analysis questions.

  • Confusing the constant‑dimension CD(K,N) with the variable‑dimension CD(K(·),N(·)) and applying the wrong distortion coefficients.
  • Assuming that every CD(K,N) space is RCD(K,N); Finsler manifolds provide counterexamples.
  • Forgetting that the heat kernel Gaussian bounds on RCD(K,N) hold globally only for the upper bound; the lower bound requires the regular set.
  • Misapplying the splitting theorem: it requires an RCD(0,N) space with a line, not merely CD(0,N).
  • Neglecting the role of the reference measure in the entropy definition; the CD condition is not invariant under arbitrary changes of the reference measure.
  • Overlooking that the synthetic Ricci flow preserves the RCD condition only for short time; long‑time existence requires additional curvature assumptions.
  • Remember that the equivalence between Γ₂ calculus, displacement convexity, and Wasserstein contraction holds precisely on infinitesimally Hilbertian spaces.

Local-to-Global Principles for the CD(K,N) Condition

The CD(K,N) condition was originally formulated as a global convexity requirement on the entropy functional along Wasserstein geodesics. A major breakthrough came with the realization that on essentially non‑branching metric measure spaces the condition can be localized: it suffices to verify a pointwise inequality involving the distortion coefficients on each geodesic segment. The localization technique, developed by Cavalletti and Mondino, reduces the global displacement convexity to a family of one‑dimensional CD(K,N) inequalities on the transport rays of an optimal transport plan. This yields the equivalence between the original CD(K,N) and the reduced curvature‑dimension condition CD*(K,N) on essentially non‑branching spaces. The reduced condition replaces the full distortion coefficients τK,N(t) by their simplified versions σK,N(t), which are easier to handle analytically. As a consequence, many global geometric and functional inequalities — Bishop–Gromov volume comparison, Lévy–Gromov isoperimetric inequality, Bonnet–Myers diameter bound — can be proved by integrating the one‑dimensional model inequalities along the disintegration of the reference measure. The local‑to‑global principle also clarifies the role of the non‑branching assumption: without it, CD*(K,N) is strictly weaker than CD(K,N), and counterexamples exist where the reduced condition holds but the full displacement convexity fails. For exam purposes, the key points are: (1) CD(K,N) ⇔ CD*(K,N) on essentially non‑branching spaces; (2) the localization method disintegrates the space into transport rays; (3) the one‑dimensional model spaces are completely classified; (4) global comparison theorems follow by integration. A common mistake is to apply the reduced condition on a branching space such as a Finsler manifold without checking the non‑branching hypothesis.

Metric Measure Spaces with Boundary and the Neumann CD Condition

Spaces with boundary appear naturally as limits of manifolds with convex boundary or as metric measure spaces arising from optimal transport with constraints. The synthetic Ricci curvature theory extends to this setting by imposing a Neumann‑type condition on the heat flow: the normal derivative of the heat semigroup vanishes at the boundary. Mondino and Naber introduced the CD^N(K,N) condition, which requires that the entropy is convex along Wasserstein geodesics that stay inside the space and that the boundary is convex in the sense of distance functions. Equivalently, the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus holds with a Neumann boundary condition on the domain of the Laplacian. The main geometric consequences are a Bishop–Gromov comparison for the interior measure, a Lévy–Gromov isoperimetric inequality where the model spaces are spherical caps, and a splitting theorem stating that an RCD^N(0,N) space with a line splits isometrically as a product ℝ × Y where Y also satisfies a Neumann CD condition. The boundary itself inherits a synthetic Ricci curvature bound of dimension N−1, which is crucial for induction arguments. Recent work by Cavalletti, Milman, and others has shown that the Neumann CD condition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, allowing one to pass to limits of manifolds with boundary. For exam questions, remember that the Neumann condition is not simply the restriction of the interior CD condition; it imposes an additional convexity requirement on the boundary distance function. Confusing the interior CD(K,N) with the Neumann CD^N(K,N) leads to incorrect applications of the splitting theorem or the volume comparison.

Localization Method Application
Let (X,d,m) be an essentially non‑branching metric measure space satisfying CD*(K,N) for some K∈ℝ and N∈[1,∞). The CD*(K,N) condition implies that for m‑a.e. point x the disintegration of m along the transport rays emanating from x yields a family of one‑dimensional measures m_α on intervals [0,ℓ_α] each satisfying the one‑dimensional CD(K,N) condition. Rigidity Theorems on RCD(K,N) Spaces: Obata, Splitting, and Maximal Diameter

Rigidity results characterize the equality cases in the fundamental comparison theorems. On an RCD(K,N) space with K>0 and N>1, the Lichnerowicz spectral gap states that the first non‑zero eigenvalue λ₁ of the Laplacian satisfies λ₁ ≥ K N/(N−1). The Obata theorem asserts that equality λ₁ = K N/(N−1) forces the space to be isomorphic (as a metric measure space) to the spherical suspension of an RCD(N−2,N−1) space; in particular, if N is an integer and the space is a smooth manifold, it is isometric to the round sphere of radius √((N−1)/K). The proof uses the Bochner inequality in its synthetic form and the fact that the eigenfunction achieving the gap must satisfy Γ₂(f) = K Γ(f) + (Δf)²/N everywhere, which forces the Hessian to be a multiple of the metric. The splitting theorem states that an RCD(0,N) space containing a line (a globally minimizing geodesic) splits isometrically as a product ℝ × Y where Y is an RCD(0,N−1) space. This is proved by constructing a Busemann function associated to the line and showing that its gradient has constant length one and zero Hessian, which yields a global product structure. The maximal diameter theorem says that if an RCD(K,N) space with K>0 attains the Bonnet–Myers diameter bound π√((N−1)/K), then it is a spherical suspension over an RCD(N−2,N−1) space. These rigidity statements are frequently tested in advanced geometric analysis exams; the key is to recognize that they require the infinitesimally Hilbertian (RCD) structure, not merely CD. On a Finsler manifold the splitting theorem can fail because the Cheeger energy is not quadratic.

Concentration of Measure and Isoperimetric Inequalities on CD(K,N) Spaces

The Lévy–Gromov isoperimetric inequality on CD(K,N) spaces asserts that among all subsets of a given measure, the boundary measure is minimized by metric balls in the model space of constant curvature K and dimension N. The model space is the sphere S^N_K for K>0, Euclidean space ℝ^N for K=0, and hyperbolic space H^N_K for K<0. The proof uses the localization method: the isoperimetric problem reduces to a one‑dimensional problem on each transport ray, where the optimal sets are intervals. Integrating the one‑dimensional isoperimetric profile yields the global inequality. A direct consequence is the concentration of measure phenomenon: for any 1‑Lipschitz function f on a CD(K,N) space with K>0, the measure of the set where f deviates from its median by more than t decays like exp(−c K t²) for small t and like exp(−c' K t) for large t, with constants depending only on N. This Gaussian‑type concentration is sharper than the exponential concentration available on general metric measure spaces with a Poincaré inequality. The isoperimetric inequality also implies the sharp Sobolev and log‑Sobolev inequalities on CD(K,N) spaces, with the same constants as on the model spaces. For exam purposes, remember that the Lévy–Gromov inequality holds for the full CD(K,N) condition without any non‑branching assumption, because the isoperimetric profile is defined via the Minkowski content which is lower semicontinuous under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. A common trap is to confuse the Lévy–Gromov inequality (which compares boundary measure) with the Bishop–Gromov inequality (which compares volumes); they are distinct but both follow from the same localization principle.

The Role of the Heat Flow in Characterizing CD(K,∞) and CD(K,N)

The dynamic approach to synthetic Ricci curvature, pioneered by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré, characterizes the CD(K,∞) condition by the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) for the heat flow in the Wasserstein space. A metric measure space satisfies CD(K,∞) if and only if the heat semigroup (P_t) is the gradient flow of the relative entropy and satisfies EVI_K: for any probability measure ν, ½ d/dt W₂²(P_t μ, ν) + K/2 W₂²(P_t μ, ν) ≤ Ent_m(ν) − Ent_m(P_t μ). This formulation is equivalent to the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ inequality Γ₂(f) ≥ K Γ(f) for all f in the domain of the Laplacian. For finite N, the CD(K,N) condition can be characterized by a modified EVI involving the distortion coefficients, or by the Γ₂ inequality with the dimension term: Γ₂(f) ≥ K Γ(f) + (Δf)²/N. The heat flow characterization is powerful because it provides a purely metric construction of the Laplacian and the heat semigroup via the minimizing movement scheme in the Wasserstein space. It also yields immediate stability: if a sequence of CD(K,N) spaces converges in measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense, the limit space satisfies CD(K,N) because the EVI passes to the limit. Moreover, the heat flow gives a natural definition of the Laplacian on non‑smooth spaces and allows one to prove functional inequalities (Poincaré, log‑Sobolev, Talagrand) by differentiating the entropy along the flow. In exams, a typical question asks to show that the EVI_K implies the contraction estimate W₂(P_t μ, P_t ν) ≤ e−Kt W₂(μ, ν). The proof is a direct integration of the EVI with ν = P_t ν. Another common point is that the CD(K,N) condition for N<∞ cannot be captured by a simple exponential contraction; one needs the distortion coefficients τK,N(t) in the EVI. Remember that the equivalence between Γ₂ and EVI holds precisely on infinitesimally Hilbertian spaces (RCD), while on general metric measure spaces the Γ₂ calculus is not available.

  • Confusing the Obata rigidity (maximal eigenvalue) with the maximal diameter rigidity; they have different model spaces (sphere vs. spherical suspension).

  • Claiming that the heat flow contraction W₂(P_t μ, P_t ν) ≤ e−Kt W₂(μ, ν) characterizes CD(K,N) for finite N; it only characterizes CD(K,∞).

The Measure Contraction Property and Its Equivalence to CD(K,N) on Non‑Branching Spaces

The measure contraction property (MCP) was introduced by Ohta and independently by Sturm as a synthetic lower Ricci curvature bound that is weaker than the full curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N). For a metric measure space (X,d,m) and parameters K∈ℝ, N∈[1,∞), MCP(K,N) requires that for every point o∈X and every measurable set A⊂X with 0<m(A)<∞, the measure of the t‑interpolation of A towards o satisfies a distortion inequality involving the coefficients σ<sub>K,N(t)(θ). Concretely, if we denote by F_t(A) the set of points at proportion t along geodesics from o to A, then m(F_t(A)) ≥ ∫_A σK,N(t)(d(o,x))^N dm(x). This condition is formulated entirely in terms of optimal transport maps from a Dirac mass to a general measure, and it does not require the displacement convexity of the relative entropy for all pairs of measures.

A fundamental result by Cavalletti and Mondino shows that on essentially non‑branching metric measure spaces the MCP(K,N) condition is equivalent to the reduced curvature‑dimension condition CD*(K,N). Moreover, on such spaces CD*(K,N) coincides with the full CD(K,N) condition. The proof uses the disintegration of the optimal transport plan along the geodesics of a suitable transport map and the localization technique (also known as needle decomposition). The non‑branching assumption guarantees that the transport map is uniquely defined m‑almost everywhere, which allows one to reduce the problem to one‑dimensional model spaces where the distortion coefficients can be computed explicitly. As a consequence, many geometric and functional inequalities that are known for CD(K,N) spaces — such as the Bishop–Gromov volume comparison, the Lévy–Gromov isoperimetric inequality, and the sharp Poincaré inequality — also hold under the a priori weaker MCP(K,N) on non‑branching spaces.

It is important to note that the equivalence fails without the non‑branching hypothesis. There exist metric measure spaces satisfying MCP(K,N) but not CD(K,N); a standard example is a suitable weighted Euclidean space where the weight is not log‑concave but still satisfies the measure contraction inequality. For exam purposes, remember that the MCP condition is strictly weaker in general, but it becomes a full synthetic Ricci curvature bound on the large class of essentially non‑branching spaces, which includes all RCD(K,N) spaces and all Alexandrov spaces with curvature bounded below.

The Theory of Tangent Modules and the Differential Structure on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The development of a first‑order differential calculus on non‑smooth metric measure spaces is one of the cornerstones of the RCD theory. Gigli introduced the notion of a tangent module L0(TX) over the algebra L^∞(X,m) as an L^∞‑module equipped with a pointwise norm that mimics the space of vector fields on a smooth manifold. On an RCD(K,N) space, the Cheeger energy Ch(f)=½∫|∇f|2 dm is a quadratic form, and its domain W1,2(X) carries a natural Hilbert module structure. The gradient operator ∇: W1,2(X)→L0(TX) is then a linear map satisfying the Leibniz rule and the chain rule, and it identifies the minimal weak upper gradient |∇f| with the pointwise norm of ∇f.

The tangent module allows one to define the covariant derivative ∇_v w for vector fields v,w in suitable Sobolev spaces, and to formulate a synthetic version of the Bochner inequality. In particular, the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus can be recast in terms of the Hessian Hess(f) as an element of the tensor product L0(TX)⊗L0(TX). The curvature‑dimension condition RCD(K,N) is then equivalent to the inequality ⟨Hess(f),Hess(f)⟩ + K|∇f|2 ≤ (Δf)2/N in a weak sense, where the Laplacian Δ is the generator of the heat flow. This differential structure also yields a well‑defined notion of parallel transport along geodesics, which is essential for the proof of the splitting theorem and for the construction of the Riemannian structure on the regular set of an RCD(K,N) space.

A key technical tool is the identification of the tangent module with the space of derivations on the algebra of Lipschitz functions. This identification shows that the differential calculus is intrinsic and does not depend on the particular representation of the space. Moreover, the dimension of the tangent module is constant m‑almost everywhere on the regular set, and it coincides with the essential dimension of the space. For exam questions, a typical task is to show that the quadraticity of the Cheeger energy implies the linearity of the gradient, and hence the space is infinitesimally Hilbertian. Another common point is that the tangent module construction works on any metric measure space, but the rich calculus (Hessian, covariant derivative, Bochner inequality) is only available under the RCD(K,N) assumption.

The Brunn–Minkowski Inequality and Its Role in Synthetic Ricci Curvature

The Brunn–Minkowski inequality is a classical geometric inequality that relates the volume of the Minkowski sum of two sets in Euclidean space to the volumes of the individual sets. In the synthetic Ricci curvature framework, a generalized Brunn–Minkowski inequality holds on any metric measure space satisfying CD(K,N). For two measurable sets A,B⊂X with positive finite measure, and for t∈[0,1], let Z_t be the set of points that lie at proportion t along geodesics from A to B (the t‑interpolation). Then the CD(K,N) condition implies m(Z_t)1/N ≥ σK,N(1-t)(θ) m(A)1/N + σK,N(t)(θ) m(B)1/N, where θ is the distance between the sets in a suitable sense and σ are the distortion coefficients. This inequality was first established in this generality by Cordero‑Erausquin, McCann, and Schmuckenschläger for smooth manifolds and later extended to the non‑smooth setting by Sturm and by Cavalletti–Mondino.

The Brunn–Minkowski inequality is not only a consequence of CD(K,N); it also characterizes the condition in the non‑branching setting. Indeed, if a metric measure space satisfies the Brunn–Minkowski inequality for all pairs of sets, then it satisfies CD(K,N). This equivalence provides a more geometric, set‑based formulation of synthetic Ricci curvature that avoids the language of optimal transport plans and entropy convexity. It is particularly useful for proving isoperimetric and functional inequalities, because the Brunn–Minkowski inequality directly implies the Lévy–Gromov isoperimetric inequality via the localization method. Moreover, the Brunn–Minkowski inequality yields the Bishop–Gromov volume comparison by taking one of the sets to be a small ball around a point and letting its radius tend to zero.

In examinations, a typical question may ask to derive the Bishop–Gromov inequality from the Brunn–Minkowski inequality, or to show that the Brunn–Minkowski inequality for K=0 reduces to the classical concavity of the N‑th root of the volume of Minkowski sums. Another common point is that the distortion coefficients σK,N(t)(θ) are defined via the solution of the Jacobi equation on the model space of constant curvature K and dimension N, and they encode the precise way in which volumes distort under interpolation. Memorizing the explicit form of these coefficients for K>0, K=0, and K<0 is often required for quantitative estimates.

Quantitative Stability of CD(K,N) under Measured Gromov–Hausdorff Convergence

The stability of the CD(K,N) condition under measured Gromov–Hausdorff (mGH) convergence is a foundational result: if a sequence of CD(K,N) spaces converges in the mGH sense, the limit space also satisfies CD(K,N). Beyond this qualitative statement, recent work by Gigli, Mondino, and Savaré, and independently by Cavalletti and Milman, has established quantitative stability estimates. These estimates control the deficit in the CD(K,N) condition of the limit space in terms of the mGH distance between the approximating spaces and the limit. Concretely, if (X_i,d_i,m_i) are CD(K,N) spaces converging to (X,d,m) with mGH distance ε_i, then for any two probability measures μ,ν on X with bounded support, the displacement convexity inequality for the entropy holds up to an error of order C ε_i^α, where C and α depend only on K,N and the diameters of the spaces.

The proof of quantitative stability relies on the equivalence between the CD(K,N) condition and the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) for the heat flow, together with the stability of the heat flow under mGH convergence. One constructs approximate optimal transport plans on the limit space by pulling back plans from the approximating spaces, and then uses the EVI to bound the entropy difference. The exponent α is typically 1/2 for general spaces, but can be improved to 1 under additional regularity assumptions such as non‑branching or the existence of a quadratic Cheeger energy. These quantitative estimates are crucial for applications in geometric analysis, for instance in the study of the convergence of eigenvalues of the Laplacian, the stability of the isoperimetric profile, and the construction of tangent cones as metric measure spaces.

For exam preparation, it is important to distinguish between qualitative stability (the limit of CD(K,N) spaces is CD(K,N)) and quantitative stability (explicit error bounds). A typical question might ask to explain why the EVI formulation yields stability, or to sketch how the mGH convergence of the heat flows implies the convergence of the entropy functionals. Another common trap is to assume that quantitative stability holds for the full CD(K,N) condition without the non‑branching assumption; the known quantitative results require either the reduced condition CD*(K,N) or the RCD(K,N) condition.

Synthetic Ricci Curvature for Finsler Manifolds and the Failure of the Splitting Theorem

Finsler manifolds provide a natural class of metric measure spaces where the synthetic Ricci curvature bounds can be studied beyond the Riemannian setting. On a Finsler manifold (M,F) equipped with the Busemann–Hausdorff measure (or the Holmes–Thompson measure), the curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) can be formulated using the optimal transport theory on the associated geodesic metric space. Ohta and Sturm showed that a Finsler manifold satisfies CD(K,N) if and only if its weighted Ricci curvature Ric_N satisfies Ric_N ≥ K in the sense of the Bakry–Émery tensor, where the weight is given by the logarithm of the volume density of the chosen measure. This equivalence extends the Riemannian theory to a genuinely non‑Riemannian context.

However, several key results of the RCD theory fail for general Finsler manifolds. The most striking is the failure of the splitting theorem: a Finsler manifold with non‑negative Ricci curvature and a line need not split isometrically as a product of a line and another Finsler manifold. The reason is that the Cheeger energy on a Finsler manifold is not quadratic; the space is not infinitesimally Hilbertian, so it does not satisfy the RCD condition. Consequently, the differential calculus based on tangent modules and the Bochner inequality are not available, and the proof of the splitting theorem — which relies on the existence of a parallel vector field constructed via the heat flow — breaks down. Indeed, there are explicit examples of Finsler manifolds (e.g., certain Randers metrics) with Ric ≥ 0 that contain lines but do not split.

Another important distinction is that the heat flow on a Finsler manifold is not the gradient flow of the relative entropy in the Wasserstein space with respect to the quadratic Wasserstein distance; instead, one must use an asymmetric transport cost adapted to the Finsler structure. This leads to a different notion of displacement convexity and a modified EVI. For exam purposes, remember that CD(K,N) makes sense on Finsler manifolds and is equivalent to a lower bound on the weighted Ricci curvature, but the richer RCD theory (quadratic Cheeger energy, splitting theorem, tangent module calculus) requires the infinitesimally Hilbertian property, which is exactly the condition that the Finsler metric comes from a Riemannian metric. A common question asks to identify which step in the proof of the splitting theorem uses the quadraticity of the Cheeger energy, and the answer is the construction of the harmonic function with linear growth via the heat flow, which relies on the Hilbert space structure of the tangent module.

The Eulerian and Lagrangian Formulations of the Heat Flow on RCD(K,N) Spaces

On an RCD(K,N) space the heat flow admits two equivalent variational descriptions. The Eulerian formulation views the heat flow as the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy Ch in the Hilbert space L²(X,m). The Cheeger energy is defined as the lower semicontinuous envelope of the Dirichlet energy ∫|∇f|² dm on Lipschitz functions, and its subdifferential yields the infinitesimally Hilbertian Laplacian Δ. The Lagrangian formulation, due to Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré, identifies the same flow as the gradient flow of the relative entropy Entₘ(μ)=∫ρ log ρ dm in the quadratic Wasserstein space (P₂(X),W₂). The equivalence of the two formulations is a cornerstone of the RCD theory: it holds precisely because the Cheeger energy is quadratic, which is the defining property of infinitesimal Hilbertianity. On a general CD(K,N) space the Cheeger energy need not be quadratic; consequently the heat flow is not the Wasserstein gradient flow of the entropy, and the EVI (Evolution Variational Inequality) formulation fails. The EVIK inequality ½ d/dt W₂²(μₜ,ν) + K/2 W₂²(μₜ,ν) ≤ Entₘ(ν) − Entₘ(μₜ) characterises the heat flow on RCD(K,∞) spaces and, with the appropriate distortion coefficients, on RCD(K,N) spaces. This duality underpins the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus, the Bochner inequality, and the whole differential structure on RCD spaces.

A practical consequence for exams is that any question asking to “show that the heat flow is the gradient flow of the entropy” implicitly assumes the RCD condition. If the space is only known to satisfy CD(K,N), one must either invoke the non‑branching assumption to upgrade to CD*(K,N) and then use the equivalence with the reduced heat flow, or explicitly state that the Lagrangian formulation is not available without quadratic Cheeger energy. The Eulerian formulation, however, exists on every metric measure space via the Cheeger energy, but the resulting semigroup may not be Markovian or may not preserve the entropy convexity along Wasserstein geodesics.

The Structure Theory of RCD(K,N) Spaces: Rectifiability and Tangent Cones

The deep geometric structure of RCD(K,N) spaces was established by Mondino–Naber, De Philippis–Gigli, and others. Every RCD(K,N) space is rectifiable in the sense of metric measure spaces: there exists a decomposition X = ∪ₖ Rₖ ∪ N where N is m-negligible, each Rₖ is a k-regular set, and on Rₖ the measure m is absolutely continuous with respect to the k-dimensional Hausdorff measure with a density bounded above and below by positive constants. Moreover, the essential dimension is an integer k ≤ N that is constant on each connected component of the regular set. At m-almost every point the tangent cone is unique and isometric to the Euclidean space ℝᵏ equipped with the Lebesgue measure. This uniqueness of tangents is a dramatic strengthening of the general metric measure space theory, where tangents may be non‑unique or non‑Euclidean. The proof combines the splitting theorem (which holds on RCD spaces because of the quadratic Cheeger energy), the volume cone implies metric cone principle, and the almost‑splitting theorem derived from the Bochner inequality.

For exam purposes, remember that the rectifiability result fails on general CD(K,N) spaces: Finsler manifolds provide counterexamples where tangents are normed spaces that are not Euclidean. Also, the integer dimension k is not necessarily equal to the synthetic dimension N; it is the essential dimension of the space, and the condition k ≤ N is sharp. A typical question may ask to explain why the splitting theorem is needed to prove that tangents are Euclidean, or to sketch how the Bochner inequality yields the almost‑splitting estimate that forces the tangent cone to split off a line when the space almost contains a line.

Worked Example: Tangent Cone Identification
Let (X,d,m) be an RCD(0,N) space and assume that at a point x the tangent cone is isometric to a metric cone C(Y) over a compact metric space Y. Show that Y must be an RCD(N−2,N−1) space and that the tangent cone is actually Euclidean.
Since X is RCD(0,N), the tangent cone at any point is an RCD(0,N) space by stability of the RCD condition under pointed measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. A metric cone C(Y) satisfies RCD(0,N) iff the cross section Y satisfies RCD(N−2,N−1) (this is the cone rigidity theorem). Moreover, the splitting theorem on RCD(0,N) spaces implies that any line in the tangent cone yields a splitting. The cone C(Y) contains a line only if Y has diameter π, which forces Y to be a spherical suspension. Iterating this argument shows that the only metric cone that is an RCD(0,N) space and has a unique tangent at the vertex is ℝᵏ. Hence the tangent cone is Euclidean.
Exam Strategy
When a question mentions “tangent cone” on an RCD(K,N) space, immediately recall the rectifiability theorem: tangents are Euclidean ℝᵏ a.e. If the problem gives a non‑Euclidean tangent, the space cannot be RCD; it is at best CD. Use the splitting theorem and the cone rigidity theorem as the two main tools to identify the structure of tangents.

The Lichnerowicz–Obata Eigenvalue Estimate and Its Rigidity on RCD(K,N) Spaces

On a smooth Riemannian manifold with Ricci curvature ≥ K > 0 and dimension ≤ N, the Lichnerowicz theorem states that the first non‑zero eigenvalue λ₁ of the Laplace–Beltrami operator satisfies λ₁ ≥ K·N/(N−1). Obata’s rigidity theorem characterises the equality case: equality holds iff the manifold is isometric to the round sphere of radius √((N−1)/K). The synthetic analogue on RCD(K,N) spaces was proved by Ketterer, Mondino–Wei, and others. The Cheeger energy on an RCD(K,N) space is a quadratic form whose domain is the Sobolev space W¹,²(X). The spectrum of the associated Laplacian is discrete when the space is compact, and the same lower bound λ₁ ≥ K·N/(N−1) holds. The rigidity statement is more subtle: if λ₁ = K·N/(N−1), then the space is isomorphic (as a metric measure space) to a spherical suspension over an RCD(N−2,N−1) space. In particular, when N is an integer and the space is non‑branching, the suspension is the round sphere Sᴺ. The proof uses the Bochner inequality in the form Γ₂(f) ≥ K Γ(f) + (Δf)²/N, integrated against the eigenfunction, together with the fact that equality forces the eigenfunction to be an affine function on the tangent cones, which then forces the space to be a spherical suspension.

A common examiner twist is to ask for the eigenvalue bound on an RCD(K,N) space with K < 0. In that case the Lichnerowicz bound does not apply; instead one has the trivial bound λ₁ ≥ 0, and no positive lower bound can be deduced from curvature alone. Another trap is to confuse the synthetic dimension N with the topological dimension; the bound uses the synthetic dimension N, which may be larger than the essential dimension. If the space is an RCD(K,N) space with essential dimension k < N, the sharp bound is still λ₁ ≥ K·N/(N−1), but rigidity forces the space to be a spherical suspension of dimension k, which is only possible if k = N. Hence equality can occur only when the essential dimension equals the synthetic dimension.

The Equivalence of CD(K,N) and the Reduced Condition CD*(K,N) on Essentially Non‑Branching Spaces

The CD(K,N) condition uses the full distortion coefficients σK,N(t)(θ) defined for all t∈[0,1] and θ≥0. The reduced condition CD*(K,N) replaces these coefficients by the smaller coefficients τK,N(t)(θ) = t1/N σK,N−1(t)(θ) for N>1, and by the usual σK,1 for N=1. The reduced condition is weaker: CD(K,N) ⇒ CD*(K,N) always holds. The converse is false in general metric measure spaces because branching can create geodesics that violate the full convexity while still satisfying the reduced one. However, on essentially non‑branching spaces (i.e., spaces where any optimal transport plan is concentrated on a set of non‑branching geodesics) the two conditions are equivalent. This equivalence was established by Cavalletti–Milman using the localization technique (also called needle decomposition): the space is disintegrated into one‑dimensional rays along which the measure satisfies a one‑dimensional CD(K,N) condition. On each ray the full and reduced coefficients coincide because the one‑dimensional CD condition is equivalent to the reduced one. Gluing the rays back together yields the full CD(K,N) condition. The essentially non‑branching assumption is crucial; without it, the disintegration may produce branching transport plans that cannot be reduced to rays, and counterexamples exist (e.g., certain glued spaces).

For exams, remember that the equivalence CD(K,N) ⇔ CD*(K,N) holds on essentially non‑branching spaces, and that the localization technique is the standard proof. A typical question may ask to explain why the reduced condition is easier to verify (the coefficients are simpler) or to give an example of a branching space where CD* holds but CD fails. The classic example is the “branching line” constructed by Rajala: a metric measure space that satisfies CD*(0,N) but not CD(0,N). Also note that the MCP(K,N) condition is equivalent to CD*(K,N) on essentially non‑branching spaces, but not to CD(K,N) without the non‑branching assumption.

The Role of the Reference Measure in the Definition of CD(K,N) and Weighted Ricci Curvature

The curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) is not a property of the metric alone; it depends crucially on the reference measure m. On a smooth weighted Riemannian manifold (M,g,e−V volg) the Bakry–Émery Ricci tensor is RicV = Ricg + Hess V − (1/(N−n)) dV⊗dV for the synthetic dimension N ≥ n = dim M. The condition CD(K,N) is equivalent to RicV ≥ K g. Changing the reference measure by a conformal factor e−W changes the weight to V+W and alters the weighted Ricci tensor accordingly. In the synthetic setting, if (X,d,m) satisfies CD(K,N) and we replace m by m̃ = f m with a positive density f, the new space (X,d,m̃) satisfies CD(K,N) only if f is of the form e−φ where φ is a function with suitable convexity properties. In general, the CD condition is not invariant under arbitrary measure changes. This is why the choice of the reference measure (Busemann–Hausdorff, Holmes–Thompson, or a smooth volume) on a Finsler manifold leads to different weighted Ricci curvature bounds. The synthetic theory therefore always works with a fixed reference measure, and statements like “a metric space has Ricci curvature ≥ K” are meaningless without specifying the measure.

A frequent exam error is to assume that if a metric measure space satisfies CD(K,N) then any other measure absolutely continuous with respect to the original one also satisfies CD(K,N). The correct statement is that the CD condition is stable under multiplication by a constant (which does not affect the distortion coefficients) but not under general densities. Another subtlety: the synthetic dimension N can be larger than the Hausdorff dimension of the space; the reference measure may be singular with respect to the Hausdorff measure, yet the CD condition can still hold (e.g., on a lower‑dimensional subset of ℝⁿ with a weighted measure). The weighted Ricci curvature bound is encoded in the behaviour of the entropy along Wasserstein geodesics, and the reference measure appears explicitly in the definition of the relative entropy Entm(μ). Therefore, any change of reference measure changes the entropy functional and consequently the displacement convexity properties.

The Bochner Inequality and Its Synthetic Formulation on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The Bochner inequality is the analytic heart of the Bakry–Émery calculus. On a smooth weighted Riemannian manifold (M,g,e−Vvolg) the inequality reads Γ2(f) ≥ K Γ(f) + (1/N)(Δf)2 for all smooth f, where Γ(f)=|∇f|2 and Γ2(f)=½Δ|∇f|2−⟨∇f,∇Δf⟩. In the synthetic setting of RCD(K,N) spaces, Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré proved that the same inequality holds for all f in the domain of the Cheeger energy, with the Laplacian understood as the generator of the heat flow. The proof uses the Γ‑calculus on the tangent module and the fact that the heat flow is the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in the L2‑Wasserstein space. A key consequence is the equivalence between the Bochner inequality and the CD(K,N) condition on infinitesimally Hilbertian spaces, which is the definition of RCD(K,N). This equivalence allows one to transfer all functional inequalities known from the smooth Bakry–Émery theory — Poincaré, log‑Sobolev, Talagrand — to the non‑smooth RCD setting.

One subtle point is the role of the dimension parameter N. In the synthetic Bochner inequality the term (1/N)(Δf)2 appears exactly as in the smooth case, but the Laplacian is only defined m‑a.e. and the inequality holds in the weak sense: ∫ φ Γ2(f) dm ≥ ∫ φ (K Γ(f) + (1/N)(Δf)2) dm for all non‑negative φ in the test function space. The proof of this weak formulation relies on the regularizing properties of the heat flow and on the fact that on RCD(K,N) spaces the heat flow maps L2 into the Sobolev space W1,2 with quantitative estimates. These estimates are uniform in time and provide the necessary compactness to pass to the limit in the discrete approximation of Γ2.

For exam purposes, remember that the Bochner inequality is the bridge between the Eulerian (Γ‑calculus) and Lagrangian (optimal transport) viewpoints. A typical question asks to show that the CD(K,N) condition implies the Bochner inequality on RCD spaces, or to explain why the inequality fails on a general CD(K,N) space without the infinitesimal Hilbertianity assumption. The answer hinges on the fact that the Γ2 operator is defined via the Carré du champ of the Cheeger energy, which is quadratic only on RCD spaces.

The Theory of Gradient Flows in the Wasserstein Space on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The Wasserstein space (P2(X),W2) over an RCD(K,N) space is a geodesic metric space with non‑positive curvature in the sense of Alexandrov. The relative entropy functional Entm(μ)=∫ ρ log ρ dm (with μ=ρ m) is geodesically convex precisely when the underlying space satisfies CD(K,N). On RCD(K,N) spaces the entropy is not only convex but also lower semicontinuous and its subdifferential coincides with the negative of the heat flow. This identification, due to Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré, shows that the heat flow is the gradient flow of the entropy in the Wasserstein space. The proof uses the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) formulation: a curve (μt) satisfies EVIK if ½ d/dt W22(μt,ν) + K/2 W22(μt,ν) ≤ Entm(ν)−Entm(μt) for all ν∈P2(X). On RCD(K,N) spaces the heat flow is the unique EVIK gradient flow of the entropy.

A practical consequence is the contractivity of the heat flow in Wasserstein distance: W2(Htμ, Htν) ≤ e−Kt W2(μ,ν). This estimate is stronger than the usual L2 contractivity and is equivalent to the CD(K,∞) condition. For finite N the contractivity holds with a dimension‑dependent correction term that appears in the refined EVI formulation. The gradient flow perspective also yields the Bakry–Émery gradient estimate |∇Htf| ≤ e−Kt Ht(|∇f|) m‑a.e., which is a direct corollary of the EVI property and the chain rule on RCD spaces.

Examiners often test the equivalence between the EVI formulation and the CD(K,N) condition. A standard exercise: given a metric measure space satisfying CD(K,N), prove that the heat flow satisfies EVIK. The solution uses the displacement convexity of the entropy along Wasserstein geodesics and the fact that the heat flow can be approximated by the minimizing movement scheme (JKO scheme). The JKO scheme constructs a discrete sequence μkτ minimizing μ ↦ (1/2τ)W22(μ,μk−1τ)+Entm(μ). Passing to the limit τ→0 yields the continuous gradient flow, and the convexity of the entropy gives the EVI inequality.

The Structure of Singular Sets and Stratification on RCD(K,N) Spaces

On an RCD(K,N) space the regular set ℛ consists of points where the tangent cone is isometric to ℝk for some integer k≤N. The singular set 𝒮 = X ℛ is stratified by the dimension of the tangent cone: 𝒮k = {x ∈ X : no tangent cone at x splits off a factor ℝk+1}. Mondino and Naber proved that the Hausdorff dimension of 𝒮k is at most k, and that the k‑dimensional Minkowski content of 𝒮k is finite. This stratification mirrors the Cheeger–Colding theory for Ricci limit spaces but holds in the fully synthetic RCD setting without any non‑collapsing assumption. The proof uses the quantitative differentiation technique: one shows that at almost every point the space is close to a metric cone, and then applies a Reifenberg‑type argument to control the size of the set where the symmetry is lower than expected.

A crucial tool is the splitting theorem on RCD(K,N) spaces: if a space contains a line, it splits isometrically as a product ℝ × Y with Y an RCD(K,N−1) space. This theorem, proved by Gigli, is used inductively to identify the maximal Euclidean factor in a tangent cone. The dimension of the regular set is constant m‑a.e. and equals the essential dimension of the space, which is an integer n ≤ N. The singular set has Hausdorff dimension at most n−1, and the top stratum 𝒮n−1 is (n−1)‑rectifiable. These results are essential for the structure theory of RCD spaces and for the definition of the measure‑valued Ricci curvature tensor.

In examinations, a common question is to explain why the singular set has codimension at least one. The answer: at any point where the tangent cone is not ℝn, the cone splits off a lower‑dimensional Euclidean factor, and the splitting theorem forces the dimension of the singular stratum to drop by at least one. Another typical trap is to confuse the synthetic dimension N with the essential dimension n; the stratification bounds use n, not N.

The Convergence of Finite‑Dimensional Approximations and Discretization Schemes for CD(K,N) Spaces

Approximating a CD(K,N) space by finite‑dimensional simplicial complexes or by point clouds is a central problem in computational optimal transport. The most studied scheme is the entropic regularization of the Wasserstein distance (Sinkhorn algorithm), which yields a smooth approximation of the heat flow. On RCD(K,N) spaces, the entropic regularization converges to the true heat flow as the regularization parameter ε→0, with a rate O(ε) in the Wasserstein distance. This result, due to Conforti, Léonard and others, relies on the fact that the Schrödinger problem is a convex optimization problem whose dual formulation involves the Hopf–Lax semigroup. The convergence is uniform on bounded subsets of P2(X) and preserves the CD(K,N) inequality at the discrete level.

Another discretization is the finite‑volume scheme on a graph approximating the space. If the graph satisfies a discrete CD(K,N) condition (in the sense of Erbar–Maas or Mielke), then the discrete heat flow converges to the continuous heat flow in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense. The discrete CD condition is defined via the convexity of the discrete entropy along discrete geodesics in the space of probability measures on the graph. The convergence proof uses the stability of CD(K,N) under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence and the fact that the discrete entropy Γ‑converges to the continuous entropy. This provides a rigorous foundation for numerical methods in geometric analysis on non‑smooth spaces.

For exam preparation, remember that the entropic regularization is not a CD(K,N) space itself; it only approximates the heat flow. The discrete CD condition on graphs is stronger than the usual Ollivier–Ricci curvature and is equivalent to the convexity of the discrete entropy. A typical question asks to state the convergence theorem for the Sinkhorn algorithm on RCD(K,N) spaces and to identify the assumptions needed (compactness, lower Ricci bound, finite entropy). The answer must mention the role of the Schrödinger bridge and the Γ‑convergence of the entropic cost.

The Role of the Entropy Functional in the Definition of CD(K,N) and Its Variational Properties

The relative entropy Entm(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dm (with the convention 0 log 0 = 0) is the central functional in the Lott–Sturm–Villani theory. On a metric measure space (X,d,m) the CD(K,N) condition is defined by requiring that for any two absolutely continuous measures μ0, μ1 with finite entropy there exists a Wasserstein geodesic (μt) such that Entm(μt) ≤ (1−t)Entm(μ0) + t Entm(μ1) − K/2 t(1−t) W22(μ0,μ1) for all t∈[0,1] when N=∞, and a similar inequality with the distortion coefficients σK,N(t) when N<∞. The entropy is lower semicontinuous with respect to narrow convergence and its sublevel sets are compact in the Wasserstein space if the space is proper. These variational properties make the entropy a natural energy functional for the gradient flow approach.

The entropy also satisfies a chain rule on RCD(K,N) spaces: for any Lipschitz function φ, Entm(φ#μ) = Entm(μ) + ∫ log φ' dμ when φ is a monotone map on ℝ. In the multi‑dimensional setting the chain rule is replaced by the Benamou–Brenier formula for the Wasserstein distance, which expresses W22(μ,ν) as the infimum of the action ∫01 ∫ |vt|2 dμt dt over all continuity equations ∂tμt+∇·(vtμt)=0. This formula is the Lagrangian counterpart of the Eulerian Γ‑calculus and is valid on any geodesic metric measure space. On RCD(K,N) spaces the minimal action is attained by the velocity field of the constant‑speed geodesic, and the entropy dissipation along the heat flow equals the Fisher information I(μ)=∫ |∇√ρ|2 dm.

Examiners often test the equivalence between the displacement convexity of the entropy and the CD(K,N) condition. A standard proof sketch: (⇒) Assume CD(K,N). Take any geodesic and use the definition of CD(K,N) to get the convexity inequality. (⇐) Assume the entropy is K‑convex along all geodesics. Then the heat flow, being the gradient flow of the entropy, satisfies the EVIK inequality, which implies the Bochner inequality and hence CD(K,N). The key step is the identification of the subdifferential of the entropy with the negative of the logarithmic density, which holds on RCD spaces because the Cheeger energy is quadratic.

The Martingale Approach to Synthetic Ricci Curvature and Its Equivalence to CD(K,N)

The martingale formulation of synthetic Ricci curvature, developed by Sturm, von Renesse, and others, characterises lower Ricci bounds through the behaviour of martingales on the Wasserstein space. On a metric measure space (X,d,m) the condition CD(K,∞) is equivalent to the statement that for every pair of probability measures μ₀, μ₁ with finite entropy there exists a martingale (μₜ)ₜ∈[0,1] in the Wasserstein space P₂(X) such that μ₀ and μ₁ are the marginals and the relative entropy Entₘ(μₜ) satisfies the convexity inequality Entₘ(μₜ) ≤ (1−t)Entₘ(μ₀) + t Entₘ(μ₁) − K/2 t(1−t) W₂²(μ₀,μ₁). The martingale property means that for any 0 ≤ s ≤ t ≤ 1 the conditional law of μₜ given the σ‑algebra generated by μₛ coincides with μₛ. This probabilistic viewpoint yields a powerful tool: the martingale optimal transport problem, where one minimises the expected quadratic cost over all martingale couplings. The equivalence theorem states that a geodesic metric measure space satisfies CD(K,N) if and only if for every μ₀, μ₁ there exists a martingale transport plan whose displacement interpolation is a geodesic and whose entropy obeys the CD(K,N) distortion coefficients σK,N(t)(θ). The proof uses the dynamic formulation of optimal transport and the fact that the heat flow is the gradient flow of the entropy in the Wasserstein space. On RCD(K,N) spaces the martingale representation is unique and coincides with the constant‑speed geodesic. This approach also clarifies the role of the dimension parameter N: the distortion coefficients arise from the conditional variance of the martingale increments. Examiners often ask to state the martingale characterisation of CD(K,∞) and to explain why the martingale property fails on spaces with branching geodesics. The answer must mention that branching creates multiple conditional laws, violating the martingale condition, and that the reduced condition CD*(K,N) restores a version of the martingale property on essentially non‑branching spaces.

The Geometry of the Wasserstein Space over RCD(K,N) Spaces and Its Sectional Curvature

The Wasserstein space P₂(X) over an RCD(K,N) space inherits a synthetic sectional curvature bound in the sense of Alexandrov. For a geodesic metric space (X,d,m) satisfying RCD(K,N), the space (P₂(X),W₂) is an Alexandrov space with curvature bounded below by K when N = ∞, and more generally it satisfies a curvature‑dimension condition that can be expressed through the behaviour of geodesic triangles in P₂(X). The key result, due to Sturm and later refined by Gigli, is that the map μ ↦ Entₘ(μ) is K‑convex along geodesics in P₂(X) if and only if (X,d,m) satisfies CD(K,∞). For finite N the convexity involves the distortion coefficients and the resulting space P₂(X) is not Alexandrov but satisfies a synthetic Ricci bound in the sense of Lott–Villani. The sectional curvature of P₂(X) can be computed via the second variation formula for the entropy: given a geodesic (μₜ) with velocity field vₜ, the second derivative of Entₘ(μₜ) equals ∫ |∇vₜ|² dμₜ + ∫ Ric(vₜ,vₜ) dμₜ, where Ric is the synthetic Ricci tensor defined via the Γ₂ calculus. On RCD(K,N) spaces this yields the inequality d²/dt² Entₘ(μₜ) ≥ K ∫ |vₜ|² dμₜ + 1/N (∫ ∇·vₜ dμₜ)². This inequality is exactly the synthetic Bochner inequality integrated along the geodesic. A typical exam question asks to derive the K‑convexity of the entropy from the Bochner inequality on an RCD(K,N) space and to explain why the argument fails on a general CD(K,N) space without the infinitesimal Hilbertianity assumption. The answer must identify the quadratic Cheeger energy as the ingredient that allows the identification of the tangent module and the definition of Γ₂, and note that without it the second variation formula cannot be justified.

The Theory of Dirichlet Forms and the Identification of the Cheeger Energy on RCD(K,N) Spaces

On an RCD(K,N) space the Cheeger energy Ch(f) = ½ ∫ |∇f|² dm coincides with the Dirichlet form associated to the heat flow. The identification proceeds through the theory of strongly local, regular Dirichlet forms on metric measure spaces. Given a metric measure space (X,d,m) satisfying the volume doubling property and a Poincaré inequality, the Cheeger energy is a lower semicontinuous, convex functional on L²(X,m). Its domain, the Sobolev space W¹,²(X), carries a natural Hilbert space structure when the space is infinitesimally Hilbertian, i.e. when the Cheeger energy satisfies the parallelogram law. On RCD(K,N) spaces this Hilbertianity is guaranteed by the quadraticity of the heat flow, which is equivalent to the linearity of the heat semigroup. The Dirichlet form ℰ(f,g) = ∫ ∇f·∇g dm is then the polarisation of Ch. The Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus can be developed entirely within this Dirichlet form framework: Γ(f) = |∇f|² and Γ₂(f) = ½ ΔΓ(f) − ∇f·∇Δf, where Δ is the generator of the heat semigroup. The curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) is equivalent to the inequality Γ₂(f) ≥ K Γ(f) + 1/N (Δf)² for all f in the domain of Δ. This equivalence is a cornerstone of the synthetic theory because it allows one to transfer analytic tools (spectral gap, log‑Sobolev inequalities, heat kernel bounds) from the smooth setting to non‑smooth spaces. A common exam task is to prove that on an RCD(K,N) space the heat flow is the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in the Wasserstein space, and to deduce the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) formulation. The proof uses the fact that the subdifferential of Ch in L² coincides with the negative of the generator Δ, and that the EVI_K inequality for the heat flow is equivalent to the K‑convexity of Ch along Wasserstein geodesics.

The Synthetic Ricci Curvature for Sub‑Riemannian Manifolds: The Heisenberg Group and the Failure of CD(K,N)

Sub‑Riemannian manifolds provide a natural class of metric measure spaces where the synthetic Ricci curvature bounds CD(K,N) fail in general. The Heisenberg group ℍ¹ equipped with the Carnot–Carathéodory distance and the Haar measure is the prototype. Although ℍ¹ satisfies a measure contraction property MCP(0,5) (with homogeneous dimension 5), it does not satisfy CD(K,N) for any K ∈ ℝ and N ∈ [1,∞). The obstruction is the presence of abnormal minimisers and the non‑uniqueness of geodesics, which destroys the displacement convexity of the entropy. More precisely, the entropy functional on ℍ¹ is not convex along any Wasserstein geodesic connecting two absolutely continuous measures; one can construct explicit counterexamples using the group translations and the non‑commutative structure. The failure of CD(K,N) is reflected in the fact that the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ operator does not satisfy the curvature‑dimension inequality: the horizontal Laplacian on ℍ¹ has a negative curvature term coming from the non‑integrability of the horizontal distribution. Recent work by Baudoin, Garofalo, and others introduced a modified curvature‑dimension condition CD_H(K,N) adapted to the sub‑Riemannian setting, where the dimension parameter is replaced by the homogeneous dimension and the curvature bound involves the horizontal Ricci tensor. However, this modified condition does not imply the standard CD(K,N) and the Lott–Sturm–Villani theory does not apply directly. Examiners may ask to explain why the Heisenberg group fails CD(K,N) despite satisfying MCP, and to describe the role of the abnormal geodesics in breaking the displacement convexity. The answer must mention that MCP only controls the volume distortion along a single geodesic, while CD(K,N) requires convexity along all geodesics, and that abnormal geodesics create branching in the space of geodesics, violating the essential non‑branching assumption needed for the equivalence between CD and CD*.

The Stability of the Spectral Gap and the Lichnerowicz Estimate under Measured Gromov–Hausdorff Convergence

The proof uses the Mosco convergence of the Cheeger energies Chₙ to Ch, which follows from the stability of the CD(K,N) condition under mGH convergence and the compactness of the embedding of the Sobolev spaces. A quantitative version gives a rate of convergence for λ₁ in terms of the mGH distance, provided a uniform Poincaré inequality holds. The Entropic Cost and the Dynamic Formulation of CD(K,N)

The dynamic formulation of the curvature‑dimension condition replaces the static geodesic convexity of the Rényi entropy by an evolution variational inequality for the heat flow. Given a metric measure space (X,d,m) and the relative entropy functional Ent(μ|m)=∫ρlogρ dm for μ=ρm, the entropic cost between two probability measures μ₀,μ₁ is defined as the infimum of ∫₀¹|μ̇ₜ|²/2 dt over all absolutely continuous curves (μₜ) in the Wasserstein space W₂(X) connecting μ₀ to μ₁, where |μ̇ₜ| denotes the metric derivative. Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré proved that on a proper metric measure space the CD(K,N) condition is equivalent to the statement that the heat flow (Pₜ) is the gradient flow of Ent with respect to the entropic cost and satisfies the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI)ₖ: for every ν∈D(Ent) and t>0, ½ d²(Pₜμ,ν) − ½ d²(μ,ν) ≤ t (Ent(ν|m)−Ent(Pₜμ|m)) − K/2 ∫₀ᵗ d²(Pₛμ,ν) ds. This dynamic characterisation bypasses the need for geodesics in the space of measures and works directly with the heat semigroup. It also yields the contraction estimate d(Pₜμ,Pₜν) ≤ e⁻ᴷᵗ d(μ,ν) and the Bakry‑Émery gradient estimate |∇Pₜf|² ≤ e⁻²ᴷᵗ Pₜ(|∇f|²). The entropic cost formulation is particularly powerful for proving stability under measured Gromov‑Hausdorff convergence because the EVI is preserved under Mosco convergence of the Cheeger energies. Examiners often ask to derive the EVI from the displacement convexity of the entropy and to explain why the entropic cost is the natural action functional for the gradient flow of Ent in W₂.

The Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) and Its Equivalence to CD(K,∞)

For the infinite‑dimensional case N=∞ the CD(K,∞) condition admits a clean equivalent formulation via the Evolution Variational Inequality. Let (X,d,m) be a complete separable metric measure space with a lower semicontinuous entropy functional Ent. The heat flow (Hₜ) is said to satisfy EVIₖ if for all μ∈D(Ent), ν∈P₂(X) and t>0 one has ½ d²(Hₜμ,ν) − ½ d²(μ,ν) ≤ t (Ent(ν)−Ent(Hₜμ)) − K/2 ∫₀ᵗ d²(Hₛμ,ν) ds. Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré (2014) proved that on a proper metric measure space the following are equivalent: (i) (X,d,m) satisfies CD(K,∞); (ii) the heat flow is the unique gradient flow of Ent in W₂ and satisfies EVIₖ; (iii) the entropy is K‑convex along every W₂‑geodesic. The proof uses the fact that the subdifferential of Ent in the sense of metric spaces coincides with the minimal relaxed slope, and that the EVI characterises the gradient flow uniquely. A key technical tool is the De Giorgi variational interpolation, which constructs a curve satisfying the EVI from the minimizing movement scheme. The equivalence fails if the space is not proper or if the entropy is not lower semicontinuous; in those cases one must work with the reduced curvature‑dimension condition CD*(K,∞). Examiners frequently test the distinction between the static geodesic convexity definition and the dynamic EVI definition, and they may ask to outline the proof that EVIₖ implies the contraction property d(Hₜμ,Hₜν) ≤ e⁻ᴷᵗ d(μ,ν).

Worked Example: Deriving the Contraction Estimate from EVI
Let (X,d,m) satisfy CD(K,∞) and let (Hₜ) be its heat flow. Show that for any μ,ν∈P₂(X) and t≥0, d(Hₜμ,Hₜν) ≤ e⁻ᴷᵗ d(μ,ν).
Apply the EVIₖ with μ replaced by Hₜμ and ν replaced by Hₜν. For any s>0, ½ d²(Hₛ(Hₜμ),Hₜν) − ½ d²(Hₜμ,Hₜν) ≤ s (Ent(Hₜν)−Ent(Hₛ(Hₜμ))) − K/2 ∫₀ˢ d²(Hᵣ(Hₜμ),Hₜν) dr. Since the heat flow is a semigroup, Hₛ(Hₜμ)=Hₜ₊ₛμ. Let s→0 and use the chain rule for the metric derivative to obtain d/dt ½ d²(Hₜμ,Hₜν) ≤ −K d²(Hₜμ,Hₜν). Grönwall's inequality yields d(Hₜμ,Hₜν) ≤ e⁻ᴷᵗ d(μ,ν). The same argument works for the reverse order by symmetry, giving the desired contraction.

When a question asks for the equivalence between CD(K,∞) and EVI, write down the three equivalent statements (geodesic convexity, gradient flow, EVI) and sketch the De Giorgi interpolation argument. Remember that the properness assumption is essential; if the space is not proper, mention the reduced condition CD*(K,∞).

Second‑Order Calculus on RCD(K,N) Spaces: Hessian, Covariant Derivative, and the Bochner Formula

On a smooth Riemannian manifold the Hessian of a function f is the (0,2)‑tensor ∇²f(X,Y)=X(Yf)−(∇ₓY)f, and the Bochner formula reads ½Δ|∇f|² = |∇²f|² + ⟨∇f,∇Δf⟩ + Ric(∇f,∇f). Gigli (2018) developed a second‑order differential calculus on RCD(K,N) spaces that reproduces these objects synthetically. The tangent module L²(TX) carries a pointwise norm and a symmetric covariant derivative ∇:W¹,²(TX)→L²(T*X⊗T*X) satisfying the Leibniz rule and the symmetry condition ∇X Y − ∇Y X = [X,Y] for vector fields in the domain. For f∈W²,²(X) the Hessian is defined as Hess f = ∇df, an element of L²(T*X⊗T*X). The Bochner inequality on RCD(K,N) takes the synthetic form ½Δ|∇f|² ≥ |Hess f|² + ⟨∇f,∇Δf⟩ + K|∇f|² + (Δf)²/N, valid in the weak sense against non‑negative test functions. This inequality is equivalent to the CD(K,N) condition and implies the Lichnerowicz spectral gap λ₁ ≥ K N/(N−1) for K>0. The construction uses the heat flow regularisation: for f∈Lip∩D(Δ) one defines Hess f as the L²‑limit of Hess Pₜf, where the Hessian of smooth functions is defined via the Γ₂ calculus. The resulting second‑order calculus is compatible with the measured Gromov‑Hausdorff convergence: if (Xₙ) are RCD(K,N) converging to X, then the Hessians of the limit functions are limits of the Hessians of the approximating functions. Examiners may ask to state the synthetic Bochner inequality, to explain how the Hessian is defined via the heat flow, or to derive the Lichnerowicz estimate from the Bochner inequality by integrating against the first eigenfunction.

The Application of CD(K,N) to Ricci Limit Spaces and the Structure of Tangent Cones

Ricci limit spaces are pointed measured Gromov‑Hausdorff limits of sequences of smooth Riemannian manifolds (Mₙ,gₙ) with Ric≥K and dim≤N. By the compactness theorem of Gromov and the stability of the CD(K,N) condition under mGH convergence (Lott‑Villani, Sturm), every Ricci limit space is an RCD(K,N) space. Conversely, the structure theory of RCD(K,N) spaces developed by Mondino‑Naber, De Philippis‑Gigli, and others shows that any RCD(K,N) space is rectifiable: it can be covered up to measure zero by countably many bi‑Lipschitz charts mapping into ℝᵏ with k≤N. The tangent cone at almost every point is a metric measure cone over an RCD(N−2,N−1) space, and the dimension k is constant on each chart. This rectifiability result is proved by combining the splitting theorem (which yields a Euclidean factor whenever a line exists) with the almost‑splitting theorem and the Reifenberg‑type topological arguments. The CD(K,N) condition is essential because it provides the Bochner inequality, which in turn gives the almost‑rigidity of the volume growth and the control of the heat kernel needed for the quantitative differentiation. A typical exam question asks to explain why the CD(K,N) condition is preserved under mGH limits and how the synthetic Bochner inequality replaces the classical Ricci curvature bound in the proof of the almost‑splitting theorem. Another common task is to describe the structure of the singular set: it has Hausdorff dimension at most N−2, and the regular set consists of points where the tangent cone is ℝᵏ.

Synthetic Ricci Curvature for Infinite‑Dimensional Spaces: The CD(K,∞) Condition and Its Functional Inequalities

The CD(K,∞) condition is the natural extension of the curvature‑dimension bound to spaces without an upper dimension bound. It is defined by requiring that the relative entropy Ent(·|m) is K‑convex along every W₂‑geodesic in the space of probability measures. Equivalently, the heat flow satisfies the EVIₖ as discussed above. On CD(K,∞) spaces the following functional inequalities hold: the logarithmic Sobolev inequality Ent(μ|m) ≤ (1/2K) I(μ|m) for K>0, where I is the Fisher information; the Talagrand inequality W₂²(μ,m) ≤ (2/K) Ent(μ|m); and the Poincaré inequality Var(f) ≤ (1/K) ∫|∇f|² dm. These inequalities are all equivalent to CD(K,∞) on infinitesimally Hilbertian spaces (i.e. RCD(K,∞)). The proof uses the gradient flow formulation: the EVIₖ implies the contraction of the heat flow in entropy, which yields the logarithmic Sobolev inequality via the Herbst argument. The Talagrand inequality follows from the HWI inequality of Otto‑Villani, which itself is a consequence of the displacement convexity of the entropy. In the infinite‑dimensional setting the dimension parameter does not appear, so the Bochner inequality simplifies to Γ₂(f) ≥ K Γ(f). This is exactly the Bakry‑Émery condition BE(K,∞). The equivalence between CD(K,∞) and BE(K,∞) on RCD spaces was established by Ambrosio‑Gigli‑Savaré and by Erbar‑Kuwada‑Sturm. Examiners often test the chain of equivalences: CD(K,∞) ⇔ EVIₖ ⇔ BE(K,∞) ⇔ log‑Sobolev ⇔ Talagrand ⇔ Poincaré, and they may ask to derive one inequality from another using the heat flow semigroup.

  • Confusing the entropic cost with the usual Wasserstein distance; the entropic cost is an action functional, not a metric.
  • Assuming the EVI formulation holds for CD(K,N) with finite N without the additional dimensional correction term.
  • Stating that the Hessian exists for every Lipschitz function; it is only defined on the Sobolev space W²,².
  • Believing that every RCD(K,N) space is a Ricci limit space; there exist non‑collapsed RCD spaces that are not limits of smooth manifolds.
  • Neglecting the properness hypothesis when invoking the equivalence between CD(K,∞) and EVIₖ.
  • Remember to check the precise hypotheses of each theorem before applying it.

The Theory of Nonlinear Potential Theory on CD(K,N) Spaces: Capacities and Fine Properties

Nonlinear potential theory on metric measure spaces satisfying the CD(K,N) condition extends the classical Euclidean theory of capacities, fine topology, and quasi‑continuity to a nonsmooth setting. The Cheeger energy Ch(f)=½∫|∇f|² dm provides the natural Dirichlet form, and its domain W¹,²(X) replaces the Sobolev space. For a set E⊂X the p‑capacity (p=2) is defined as Cap₂(E)=inf{Ch(f)+∫f² dm : f≥1 on a neighbourhood of E}. On CD(K,N) spaces the capacity is outer regular, subadditive, and satisfies the strong subadditivity property Cap₂(A∪B)+Cap₂(A∩B)≤Cap₂(A)+Cap₂(B). The fine topology is the coarsest topology making all quasi‑continuous functions continuous; a function is quasi‑continuous iff for every ε>0 there exists an open set U with Cap₂(U)<ε such that the restriction to XU is continuous. The CD(K,N) condition yields a Poincaré inequality and a doubling measure, which together imply that every function in W¹,² has a quasi‑continuous representative. Moreover, the capacity of a ball B(x,r) is comparable to rN-2 (or rN when N=2) uniformly in x, a consequence of the volume growth bounds derived from the Bishop–Gromov inequality. Fine properties such as the Lebesgue differentiation theorem for quasi‑continuous functions, the existence of precise representatives, and the quasi‑Lindelöf principle hold. These tools are essential for the study of obstacle problems, the regularity of minimizers of the Cheeger energy, and the construction of the heat flow via the Dirichlet form. Examiners may ask to define the capacity, to prove that Lipschitz functions are quasi‑continuous, or to explain how the doubling property and Poincaré inequality imply the existence of a quasi‑continuous representative for every Sobolev function.

The Synthetic Ricci Curvature for Non‑Reversible Finsler Spaces and the Asymmetric CD Condition

Classical CD(K,N) theory assumes a symmetric distance, but many geometric models (e.g., Randers metrics, navigation problems) give rise to non‑reversible Finsler manifolds where d(x,y)≠d(y,x). Ohta and Sturm introduced an asymmetric curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) for such spaces by replacing the Wasserstein distance W₂ with the asymmetric Wasserstein distance W₂^a built from the forward distance. The entropy functional Ent(μ|m)=∫ρ log ρ dm (with μ=ρm) is required to be K‑convex along forward W₂^a‑geodesics. Equivalently, the heat flow associated with the symmetrized Dirichlet form satisfies an Evolution Variational Inequality with respect to W₂^a. The resulting theory parallels the symmetric case: a Bochner inequality Γ₂(f)≥KΓ(f)+1/N (Lf)² holds for the symmetrized carré du champ, the measure contraction property holds for forward balls, and a splitting theorem holds under the additional assumption of reversibility of the limit space. However, the failure of symmetry leads to new phenomena: the forward and backward heat flows differ, the gradient flow of the entropy is not symmetric, and the isoperimetric profile may be asymmetric. The asymmetric CD condition has been verified for a class of non‑reversible Finsler manifolds with weighted Ricci curvature bounded below, and it is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence when the convergence respects the forward distance. A typical exam question asks to state the definition of the asymmetric CD condition, to explain why the symmetrized Dirichlet form is used, or to derive the measure contraction property for forward balls from the displacement convexity of the entropy.

Worked Example: Asymmetric CD Condition on a Randers Space
Let (M,F) be a Randers manifold with F(x,v)=√(g_x(v,v))+β_x(v) where g is Riemannian and β is a 1‑form with ‖β‖_g<1. The forward distance d+(x,y) is the infimum of ∫F(γ,γ̇) over curves from x to y. Show that if the weighted Ricci curvature Ric_N+≥K for the forward direction, then (M,d+,m) satisfies the asymmetric CD(K,N) condition.
The weighted Ricci curvature Ric_N+ is defined via the second variation of the forward distance along forward geodesics. Ohta proved that Ric_N+≥K implies the displacement convexity of the relative entropy along forward W₂+‑geodesics. The proof follows the Lott–Villani–Sturm scheme: one lifts the problem to the tangent bundle, uses the convexity of the entropy along geodesics in the Wasserstein space built from d+, and identifies the second derivative with Ric_N+. The key step is the verification that the forward heat flow (the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy with respect to the symmetrized Dirichlet form) satisfies the EVI_K with respect to W₂+. This yields the asymmetric CD(K,N) condition by the equivalence theorem of Erbar–Kuwada–Sturm adapted to the asymmetric setting. Therefore (M,d+,m) is an asymmetric CD(K,N) space.
Exam Strategy
When a question mentions non‑reversible Finsler or Randers metrics, immediately recall the asymmetric Wasserstein distance and the forward/backward distinction. Write down the definition of the asymmetric CD condition (entropy K‑convex along forward geodesics) and note the role of the symmetrized Dirichlet form. If asked for a proof sketch, outline the Ohta–Sturm argument: weighted Ricci bound ⇒ displacement convexity ⇒ EVI ⇒ asymmetric CD. Do not confuse the symmetric CD condition with the asymmetric one; the latter requires the forward distance in the Wasserstein metric.

The Role of the Heat Kernel in the Construction of the Ricci Flow on RCD Spaces

On smooth manifolds the Ricci flow ∂_t g = -2 Ric can be approached via the heat kernel of the evolving metric. In the synthetic setting, Gigli and Mantegazza initiated a program to define a Ricci flow on RCD(K,N) spaces using the heat kernel of the canonical Dirichlet form. The heat kernel p_t(x,y) on an RCD(K,N) space satisfies Gaussian upper and lower bounds: C-1 t-N/2 exp(-d(x,y)²/(5t)) ≤ p_t(x,y) ≤ C t-N/2 exp(-d(x,y)²/(5t)) for t≤1, with constants depending only on K,N. These bounds, proved by Jiang–Li–Zhang using the Bochner inequality and the Davies–Gaffney estimates, allow one to define a time‑dependent family of distances d_t by d_t(x,y)² = -4t log p_t(x,y) for small t. The map t↦d_t is shown to be locally Lipschitz and to satisfy a synthetic version of the Ricci flow equation: ∂_t d_t(x,y)² = -2 Ric_t(x,y) in a suitable weak sense, where Ric_t is the synthetic Ricci curvature derived from the CD(K,N) condition at time t. The flow preserves the RCD(K,N) condition for short time, and the resulting metric measure spaces remain infinitesimally Hilbertian. A key technical tool is the identification of the heat kernel with the transition density of the Brownian motion on the RCD space, which exists by the theory of Dirichlet forms. The construction also yields a synthetic Perelman entropy functional that is monotone along the flow. Examiners may ask to state the Gaussian bounds for the heat kernel on RCD(K,N) spaces, to explain how the distance d_t is defined from the heat kernel, or to outline the proof that the synthetic Ricci flow preserves the CD condition.

Stability of the Isoperimetric Profile under Measured Gromov‑Hausdorff Convergence

The isoperimetric profile I_X(v)=inf{Per(E) : m(E)=v} of a metric measure space X encodes the optimal boundary measure for a given volume. On CD(K,N) spaces the profile is known to be bounded below by the model profile IK,N(v) of the corresponding space form (sphere, Euclidean space, or hyperbolic space) — this is the Lévy–Gromov isoperimetric inequality proved by Cavalletti–Mondino. A deeper result is the stability of the profile under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence: if (X_i,d_i,m_i) are CD(K,N) spaces converging to X, then for every v∈(0,1) the isoperimetric profiles converge IX_i(v)→I_X(v). The proof uses the compactness of sets of finite perimeter in the limit space, the lower semicontinuity of the perimeter under mGH convergence, and the existence of isoperimetric regions on the approximating spaces (guaranteed by the compactness of the embedding BVL¹ on CD(K,N) spaces). Moreover, if the limit space is a smooth manifold, the convergence of profiles is uniform on compact subsets of (0,1). This stability is crucial for the rigidity part of the Lévy–Gromov inequality: if equality holds for some v, then the space must be a spherical suspension. A typical exam question asks to define the isoperimetric profile, to state the Lévy–Gromov inequality on CD(K,N) spaces, or to explain why the perimeter functional is lower semicontinuous under mGH convergence.

The Equivalence of the Entropic Curvature‑Dimension Condition and the Dynamic Schrödinger Problem Formulation

The entropic curvature‑dimension condition CD^e(K,N) introduced by Léonard and later refined by Mikami–Thieullen characterizes lower Ricci bounds via the large‑deviation behavior of the Schrödinger problem. Given a reference measure m and a prior Markov kernel R_t (the heat kernel), the Schrödinger problem minimizes the relative entropy H(π|R_t) over couplings π with prescribed marginals μ₀,μ₁. The value of this minimization is the entropic cost C_t(μ₀,μ₁). The space satisfies CD^e(K,N) if for all μ₀,μ₁ the function t↦C_t(μ₀,μ₁) is K‑convex after a suitable dimensional correction. This dynamic formulation is equivalent to the static CD(K,N) condition on essentially non‑branching spaces. The proof proceeds by showing that the entropic cost Γ‑converges to the squared Wasserstein distance as t→0, and that the displacement convexity of the entropy along W₂‑geodesics is equivalent to the convexity of the entropic cost along the Schrödinger bridges. The dimensional correction term involves the Fisher information and the logarithmic Sobolev inequality. This equivalence provides a probabilistic interpretation of synthetic Ricci curvature and connects optimal transport with large‑deviation theory. Examiners may ask to define the entropic cost, to state the equivalence theorem between CD(K,N) and CD^e(K,N), or to explain the role of the Schrödinger bridge in the dynamic formulation.

  • Confusing the asymmetric CD condition with the symmetric one; the asymmetric version uses the forward Wasserstein distance and requires a symmetrized Dirichlet form.
  • Assuming the heat kernel on an RCD space satisfies the same pointwise Gaussian bounds as on a smooth manifold without the constants depending on K,N.
  • Stating that the isoperimetric profile is always continuous; it is only lower semicontinuous in general, and continuity holds under additional non‑branching assumptions.
  • Neglecting the dimensional correction term in the entropic CD condition; the correction involves the Fisher information and is essential for finite N.
  • Believing that the synthetic Ricci flow constructed via the heat kernel exists for all time; it is only a short‑time flow and may develop singularities.
  • Mixing up the capacity definition with the perimeter; capacity uses the Cheeger energy while perimeter uses the total variation of the characteristic function.
  • Overlooking the requirement that the reference measure be a probability measure in the Schrödinger problem; the prior kernel R_t must be a Markov kernel with invariant measure m.

Quantitative Non‑Collapsing and Volume Convergence on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The quantitative non‑collapsing theory gives a precise lower bound on the volume of metric balls in an RCD(K,N) space in terms of the radius, the lower Ricci bound K, and the dimension bound N. The fundamental result, due to Colding and Naber and later refined by De Philippis–Gigli, states that for every ε>0 there exists δ=δ(K,N,ε)>0 such that if an RCD(K,N) space (X,d,m) satisfies m(B₁(p))≥(1−δ)ω_N for some point p, then the pointed measured Gromov–Hausdorff distance between (X,p,m) and the Euclidean unit ball in ℝ^N is at most ε. Here ω_N denotes the volume of the unit ball in the N‑dimensional model space of constant curvature K. This quantitative estimate is the synthetic analogue of the classical Cheeger–Colding volume convergence theorem and it underpins the rectifiability of RCD(K,N) spaces: the regular set ℛ_k of points having a unique k‑dimensional Euclidean tangent cone has full measure, and the singular set ℛ\ℛ_k has Hausdorff dimension at most k−1. The proof combines the almost rigidity of the Bishop–Gromov inequality with a careful analysis of the heat kernel and the gradient flow of the distance function. Examiners often ask to state the quantitative non‑collapsing theorem, to explain how the δ‑parameter depends on K,N,ε, or to outline the role of the Abresch–Gromoll excess estimate in the synthetic setting.

A key tool is the almost Euclidean splitting theorem: if a ball B_r(p) is (1−δ)‑close in volume to the model ball, then the space splits off an ℝ^k factor up to a controlled error. This splitting is obtained by constructing k almost harmonic functions with almost orthogonal gradients, using the heat flow to regularize distance functions. The quantitative control on the Hessian of these functions follows from the Bochner inequality on RCD(K,N) spaces. The volume convergence theorem then asserts that if a sequence of RCD(K,N) spaces (X_i,d_i,m_i) converges in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense to a limit space (X,d,m) and the volumes of unit balls converge to the model volume, then the convergence is actually smooth on the regular set and the limit space is a smooth Riemannian manifold with Ricci ≥ K. This result is essential for the compactness theory of RCD(K,N) spaces and for the proof of the structure theorem for tangent cones.

Almost Splitting Theorem and the Structure of Almost Maximally Volumed Spaces

The almost splitting theorem, proved by Gigli–Mondino–Savaré and later sharpened by De Philippis–Gigli, says that if an RCD(K,N) space (X,d,m) contains a point p such that the volume of the unit ball B₁(p) is within δ of the model volume ω_N, then there exists a map u:X→ℝ^k (with k≤N) that is a (1+ε)‑bi‑Lipschitz embedding on a large subset of B₁(p) and whose components are δ‑harmonic with almost orthogonal gradients. The integer k is the maximal number of independent almost splitting directions and coincides with the essential dimension of the space at p. The proof proceeds by first constructing a family of harmonic functions via the heat flow applied to distance functions from carefully chosen points, then using the Bochner inequality to control their Hessians, and finally applying a quantitative version of the Cheeger–Colding segment inequality to obtain the almost orthogonal gradient condition. The theorem yields a decomposition of the space into a regular part, where the map u is a bi‑Lipschitz chart, and a singular part of small measure. This decomposition is the starting point for the rectifiability theorem: the regular set is covered by countably many bi‑Lipschitz charts with values in ℝ^k, and the singular set has Hausdorff dimension at most k−1.

An important corollary is the volume rigidity statement: if equality holds in the Bishop–Gromov inequality for some radius, then the space is a metric cone over an RCD(N−2,N−1) space. In the almost rigidity regime, the space is close to a metric cone in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense. This almost cone structure is used to prove that tangent cones at almost every point are unique and isometric to ℝ^k. Examiners may ask to define the almost splitting map, to explain how the heat flow regularizes distance functions, or to state the relationship between the almost splitting theorem and the rectifiability of RCD(K,N) spaces.

Worked Example: Quantitative Non‑Collapsing Estimate
Let (X,d,m) be an RCD(0,N) space with N≥2. Suppose m(B₁(p)) ≥ 0.99 ω_N for some p∈X. Using the quantitative non‑collapsing theorem, estimate the pointed measured Gromov–Hausdorff distance between (X,p,m) and the Euclidean unit ball in ℝ^N.
The quantitative non‑collapsing theorem provides a function δ(ε) such that if the volume deficit is less than δ(ε), then the pmGH distance is ≤ ε. For a given deficit 0.01 ω_N, one inverts the modulus of continuity: choose ε such that δ(ε) = 0.01 ω_N. In the literature (De Philippis–Gigli 2018) the dependence is of the form δ(ε) ≍ εC(N) for some constant C(N). Hence ε ≍ (0.01)1/C(N). The exact constant C(N) is not universal but can be taken as C(N)=N+2 in the proof. Therefore the pmGH distance is bounded by a constant multiple of (0.01)1/(N+2). This illustrates how a small volume deficit forces the space to be close to Euclidean space.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for a quantitative estimate, always identify the modulus of continuity (δ as a function of ε) from the relevant theorem. Write the logical chain: hypothesis → volume deficit → δ(ε) → ε bound. Do not attempt to compute an explicit numeric constant unless the problem supplies it; a qualitative description of the dependence (e.g., polynomial in ε) is usually sufficient for full marks.

Regularity Theory of Harmonic Functions on RCD(K,N) Spaces

Harmonic functions on RCD(K,N) spaces are defined as minimizers of the Cheeger energy Ch(f)=½∫|∇f|² dm among functions with prescribed boundary values. The existence and uniqueness of solutions to the Dirichlet problem follow from the convexity and lower semicontinuity of Ch on the Sobolev space W1,2(X). A fundamental regularity result, due to Gigli–Han and later extended by Mondino–Naber, states that every harmonic function on an RCD(K,N) space is locally Lipschitz and its gradient satisfies a Caccioppoli inequality with constants depending only on K and N. Moreover, the Laplacian Δf (defined as the distributional derivative of the gradient) belongs to L^∞loc and satisfies the Bochner inequality in a weak sense: ½Δ|∇f|² ≥ |Hess f|² + K|∇f|². This inequality is the synthetic counterpart of the classical Bochner formula and it yields a Li–Yau type gradient estimate: for any positive harmonic function u on B2R(p), supB_R(p) |∇log u| ≤ C(K,N)/R. The proof uses the heat flow as a regularization tool: the heat semigroup P_t maps L2 functions to Lipschitz functions with quantitative gradient bounds, and the harmonic function is recovered as the limit of P_t f as t→∞. The Caccioppoli inequality is obtained by testing the minimality of f against suitable cut‑off functions, while the Bochner inequality is derived from the Γ₂ calculus on the tangent module.

A deeper regularity theorem asserts that the singular set of a harmonic function (the set where the gradient vanishes or the function fails to be C1,α) has Hausdorff dimension at most N−2. This is proved by a blow‑up argument: at a singular point one rescales the function and the metric, obtaining a harmonic function on a tangent cone which is a metric cone over an RCD(N−2,N−1) space. The homogeneity of the blow‑up limit forces the singular set to be contained in the vertex of the cone, which has codimension at least two. Examiners may ask to define the Cheeger energy, to state the Caccioppoli inequality for harmonic functions, or to explain how the Bochner inequality yields a gradient estimate.

The Heat Flow and Gradient Identification on Metric Measure Spaces with Lower Ricci Bounds

The heat flow on an RCD(K,∞) space is the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in the L2 Wasserstein space. It can be constructed either by the minimizing movement scheme (Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto) or by the theory of Dirichlet forms. A central identification theorem, proved by Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré, states that for any f∈L2(X) the heat flow u_t = P_t f satisfies the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI): ½ d/dt W₂²(u_t,ν) + Ch(u_t) ≤ Ch(ν) − (K/2) W₂²(u_t,ν) for all ν∈P₂(X). This EVI characterization is equivalent to the CD(K,∞) condition and it provides a powerful tool to identify the gradient of u_t. In particular, the metric speed |u'_t| coincides with the minimal relaxed slope |∇u_t|_*, and the heat flow satisfies the Bakry–Émery gradient estimate |∇P_t f| ≤ e-Kt P_t(|∇f|). The proof of the gradient estimate uses the Γ₂ calculus on the tangent module: one shows that the map t↦e2Kt∫|∇P_t f|² dm is non‑increasing, which follows from the Bochner inequality Γ₂(f) ≥ KΓ(f). On RCD(K,N) spaces with finite N the gradient estimate acquires a dimensional correction: |∇P_t f| ≤ (1−e-2Kt)−1/2 e-Kt P_t(|∇f|) when K>0, and a similar bound with the heat kernel when K≤0. These estimates are crucial for the regularity theory of the heat flow and for the proof of the Li–Yau inequality on synthetic spaces.

Another important aspect is the identification of the heat kernel. On an RCD(K,N) space the heat flow admits a symmetric kernel p_t(x,y) satisfying Gaussian upper and lower bounds with constants depending only on K and N. The kernel is Hölder continuous in both variables and satisfies the semigroup property. The existence of the kernel is proved by combining the ultracontractivity of the heat semigroup (which follows from the Sobolev inequality on CD(K,N) spaces) with the Davies–Gaffney estimates. Examiners may ask to state the EVI formulation, to derive the gradient estimate from the Bochner inequality, or to explain the role of the heat kernel in the construction of the synthetic Ricci flow.

The Measure Contraction Property on Branching Spaces and Its Relation to CD(K,N)

The Measure Contraction Property MCP(K,N) was introduced by Ohta and by Sturm as a weaker synthetic Ricci curvature condition that makes sense on branching metric measure spaces. It requires that for any point o∈X and any measurable set A⊂X with 0<m(A)<∞, the measure of the set of points reached by geodesics from o to A at time t∈[0,1] is at least the corresponding model measure in the N‑dimensional space form of curvature K. Formally, if S_t^o(A) denotes the set of points γ_t where γ is a geodesic with γ<sub>0=o and γ1∈A, then m(S_t^o(A)) ≥ τK,N(1−t)(d(o,A))^N m(A), where τK,N(t)(θ) is the distortion coefficient. On essentially non‑branching spaces, MCP(K,N) is equivalent to the reduced curvature‑dimension condition CD*(K,N), which in turn is equivalent to CD(K,N). However, on branching spaces (e.g., the Heisenberg group with its Carnot–Carathéodory metric) MCP(K,N) can hold while CD(K,N) fails. The failure of CD(K,N) on the Heisenberg group is due to the lack of displacement convexity of the entropy along Wasserstein geodesics, which is caused by the abundance of branching geodesics. Nevertheless, MCP(K,N) still implies a Bishop–Gromov volume comparison, a Bonnet–Myers diameter bound, and a Lichnerowicz spectral gap estimate. The proof of these consequences uses the same distortion coefficients but avoids the need for optimal transport geodesics by working directly with the measure contraction inequality.

A recent development is the characterization of MCP(K,N) via a dynamic formulation using the heat flow: a space satisfies MCP(K,N) if and only if the heat semigroup satisfies a certain gradient estimate with dimensional correction. This bridges the gap between the Eulerian (heat flow) and Lagrangian (optimal transport) viewpoints even on branching spaces. The Stability of the Sobolev Inequality under Measured Gromov–Hausdorff Convergence

On a metric measure space satisfying the RCD(K,N) condition the Sobolev inequality takes the form

‖f‖2N/(N−2) ≤ CS ( ‖∇f‖2 + ‖f‖2 )

for every f ∈ W1,2(X), where the constant CS depends only on K and N. A fundamental result of Gigli, Mondino and Savaré (2015) shows that this inequality is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence: if a sequence of RCD(K,N) spaces (Xi, di, mi) converges to an RCD(K,N) space (X, d, m) in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense, then the optimal Sobolev constants CS(Xi) converge to CS(X). The proof combines the stability of the heat kernel (which yields convergence of the Dirichlet forms) with the compactness of the embedding W1,2 L2N/(N−2) on RCD(K,N) spaces. A key technical tool is the Γ‑convergence of the Cheeger energies Chi to Ch, which guarantees that minimising sequences for the Sobolev quotient on Xi can be lifted to a minimising sequence on X. The limit space inherits the same dimensional bound N, so the critical exponent 2N/(N−2) does not change along the convergence. This stability fails if one only assumes CD(K,N) without the infinitesimal Hilbertianity required for RCD; counterexamples on Finsler manifolds show that the Sobolev constant can jump at the limit. Examiners often ask to state the precise dependence of CS on K and N, to explain why the RCD condition is essential for stability, or to sketch the Γ‑convergence argument that links the Cheeger energies.

The Theory of the Laplacian on RCD(K,N) Spaces: Domain, Self‑Adjointness, and Spectral Theory

The Laplacian Δ on an RCD(K,N) space is defined as the generator of the Cheeger energy Ch(f) = ½ ∫ |∇f|² dm. The domain D(Δ) consists of those f ∈ W1,2(X) for which there exists a function g ∈ L²(X) such that Ch(f, φ) = −∫ g φ dm for all φ ∈ W1,2(X); then Δf = g. The Cheeger energy is a strongly local, regular Dirichlet form on L²(X,m), hence by the general theory of Dirichlet forms the associated operator Δ is self‑adjoint, non‑positive, and has a compact resolvent when the space is compact. The spectrum of −Δ is a discrete set of eigenvalues 0 = λ₀ < λ₁ ≤ λ₂ ≤ … accumulating at +∞. The Lichnerowicz estimate λ₁ ≥ K N/(N−1) holds for K > 0 and is rigid: equality forces the space to be a spherical suspension. The heat semigroup Pt = etΔ is ultracontractive, mapping L¹ to L∞ with Gaussian bounds on the kernel pt(x,y). The eigenfunctions belong to the Sobolev space W1,2 and, by the Bochner inequality, satisfy a gradient estimate |∇u| ≤ C λ1/2 ‖u‖∞. On non‑compact RCD(K,N) spaces the essential spectrum may be non‑empty; the bottom of the spectrum is characterised by the volume growth via the Brooks–Sturm type criterion. A frequent exam question is to prove that the Laplacian defined via the Cheeger energy coincides with the distributional Laplacian on smooth Riemannian manifolds, and to explain why the RCD condition guarantees the density of D(Δ) in W1,2.

The Monge Problem and Optimal Maps on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The Monge problem asks for a map T : X → X that pushes a given probability measure μ₀ forward to μ₁ while minimising the transport cost ∫ d(x, T(x))² dμ₀(x). On RCD(K,N) spaces the existence and uniqueness of optimal maps for absolutely continuous marginals is a deep result due to Cavalletti–Mondino (2017) and Gigli (2018). The strategy uses the fact that on RCD(K,N) spaces the Wasserstein geodesics are unique and the entropy is displacement convex. One first solves the Kantorovich dual problem to obtain a Kantorovich potential φ, which is a c‑concave function with c(x,y)=½ d(x,y)². The optimal map is then given by T(x) = expx(∇φ(x)), where the exponential map is defined via the unique gradient flow of the squared distance. The regularity theory of the potential φ relies on the Sobolev-to-Lipschitz property and the fact that the Hessian of φ exists as an L²‑tensor on the tangent module. Uniqueness holds μ₀‑a.e. because the space is essentially non‑branching. If the marginals are not absolutely continuous, optimal maps may fail to exist, but optimal plans are still concentrated on a cyclically monotone set. Examiners may ask to state the regularity assumptions on the marginals, to explain why the RCD condition is needed for the existence of the exponential map, or to contrast the Monge problem on RCD spaces with the classical Brenier theorem on ℝⁿ.

The Synthetic Ricci Curvature for Non‑Compact Spaces: Volume Growth and the Bishop–Gromov Inequality

On a complete, non‑compact metric measure space satisfying CD(K,N) with N ∈ (1,∞), the Bishop–Gromov volume comparison holds in the form

m(BR(x)) / vK,N(R) ≤ m(Br(x)) / vK,N(r) for 0 < r ≤ R,

where vK,N(r) is the volume of a ball of radius r in the N‑dimensional simply connected space form of constant curvature K. This inequality implies that the asymptotic volume ratio AVR = limR→∞ m(BR(x)) / vK,N(R) exists and is independent of the base point x. When K = 0 the model volume is ωN RN, so AVR ≤ 1 and equality characterises the Euclidean cone over an RCD(N−2,N−1) space (the volume cone implies metric cone theorem of De Philippis–Gigli). For K < 0 the model volume grows exponentially, and the ratio gives a measure of the “volume entropy”. The Bishop–Gromov inequality is proved by applying the CD(K,N) condition to the entropy functional along the Wasserstein geodesic that contracts a ball to its centre, using the distortion coefficients τK,N(t). A common exam task is to derive the volume comparison from the CD(K,N) definition, to compute the model volume vK,N(r) explicitly for K = 1, 0, −1, and to explain why the asymptotic volume ratio is a bi‑Lipschitz invariant under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence.

Sobolev Spaces and Functional Inequalities on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The theory of Sobolev spaces, fundamental in the study of Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) and functional analysis, extends naturally to RCD(K,N) spaces. On a general metric measure space (X, d, m), the Sobolev space W1,2(X) is defined using the Cheeger energy, Ch(f), which serves as a synthetic analogue of the L²-norm of the gradient. Specifically, for a function f ∈ L²(X, m), its Cheeger energy is given by Ch(f) = ½ ∫ |∇f|² dm, where |∇f| is the minimal weak upper gradient of f. The space W1,2(X) is then the completion of the set of Lipschitz functions with respect to the norm ||f||W1,2 = (||f||L²² + Ch(f))½. This definition is robust and allows for the development of a rich calculus on these non-smooth spaces.

The RCD(K,N) condition plays a crucial role in establishing fundamental functional inequalities that are well-known from Riemannian geometry. These inequalities are not merely abstract results; they provide quantitative control over the behavior of functions and the geometry of the space. Key inequalities include:

  • Poincaré Inequality: For a compact RCD(K,N) space, there exists a constant C such that for any f ∈ W1,2(X), ∫ (f − ∫ f dm)² dm ≤ C ∫ |∇f|² dm. This inequality bounds the L²-oscillation of a function by its gradient, implying a certain "connectedness" or "non-pinching" of the space. Its constant is directly related to the first non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian, λ₁, through the Lichnerowicz-Obata estimate λ₁ ≥ K N/(N−1) for N < ∞, or λ₁ ≥ K for N = ∞.
  • Sobolev Inequality: For N > 2, there exists a constant C such that ||f||L2N/(N-2) ≤ C ||f||W1,2. This inequality relates Lp-norms of a function to its Sobolev norm, providing crucial embedding properties. It implies that W1,2 functions are "smoother" than L² functions in a quantifiable way.
  • Logarithmic Sobolev Inequality: For any f ∈ W1,2(X) with ∫ f² dm = 1, ∫ f² log(f²) dm ≤ C ∫ |∇f|² dm + D. This inequality is closely related to the exponential decay of the heat kernel and the convergence to equilibrium for the heat flow. It is a stronger inequality than the Poincaré inequality and is fundamental in information theory and statistical mechanics.

The existence of these inequalities on RCD(K,N) spaces is a cornerstone of the theory, enabling the study of heat flow, spectral theory, and the regularity of solutions to elliptic and parabolic PDEs. For instance, the compactness of Sobolev embeddings (e.g., W1,2(X) Lp(X) for p < 2N/(N−2) on compact spaces) is a direct consequence of the RCD condition and is essential for variational methods in analysis. These functional inequalities provide a bridge between the abstract curvature-dimension condition and concrete analytical properties of functions on these singular spaces.

Rectifiability and the Structure of Tangent Cones on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The RCD(K,N) condition endows metric measure spaces with a remarkable degree of regularity, despite their potential for singularities. One of the most profound aspects of this regularity is the concept of rectifiability and the existence of tangent cones. Rectifiability, in essence, means that an RCD(K,N) space is "almost everywhere" a smooth Riemannian manifold. More precisely, an RCD(K,N) space (X, d, m) is rectifiable in the sense that it can be decomposed into a countable union of Lipschitz images of subsets of ℝN, up to a set of m-measure zero. This implies that at m-almost every point, there exists a unique tangent space, which is isometric to ℝN. This is a powerful result, established by Cheeger and Colding for Ricci limit spaces and extended to RCD spaces by Gigli and De Philippis–Gigli.

Beyond rectifiability, the structure of tangent cones provides a deeper insight into the local geometry of RCD spaces. For any point x ∈ X, one can consider the sequence of rescaled spaces (X, ri-1 d, ri-N m) as ri → 0. A tangent cone at x is a limit of such rescaled spaces in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense. A fundamental result states that for any point x in an RCD(K,N) space, there exists at least one tangent cone, and any such tangent cone is itself an RCD(0,N) space. This means that locally, an RCD space looks like a space with non-negative Ricci curvature. Furthermore, at m-almost every point, the tangent cone is unique and isometric to ℝN, which is consistent with the rectifiability result.

The uniqueness of tangent cones at singular points is a more delicate matter. While tangent cones always exist, they are not necessarily unique at every point. However, for RCD(K,N) spaces, the structure theory shows that the set of points where the tangent cone is not unique or not isometric to ℝN has zero N-dimensional Hausdorff measure. This stratification of the singular set is crucial for understanding the local geometry. The tangent cone at a singular point can be a more complex RCD(0,N) space, such as a metric cone over an RCD(N-1,N-1) space. The study of these tangent cones allows for a fine-grained analysis of the local structure, revealing how curvature bounds constrain the geometry even at points where the space is not smooth.

This implies that at m-almost every point, there exists a unique tangent space isometric to ℝN. However, rectifiability does not imply smoothness everywhere. RCD spaces can (and often do) have singular points where the space is not locally isometric to a Euclidean space. At these singular points, the tangent cone may not be ℝN, but rather a more general RCD(0,N) metric cone. For example, a Euclidean cone over a sphere is an RCD(0,N) space with a singularity at the cone vertex. The Bochner Inequality and its Role in Rigidity Theorems on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The Bochner inequality is a cornerstone of Riemannian geometry, relating the Laplacian of the squared gradient of a function to its Ricci curvature and Hessian. On RCD(K,N) spaces, a synthetic version of the Bochner inequality holds, providing a powerful analytical tool to deduce geometric properties. The synthetic Bochner inequality, often stated as Δ |∇f|² ≥ 2 Ric(∇f, ∇f) + 2 |Hess f|² + 2 ⟨∇Δf, ∇f⟩, is formulated using the Cheeger energy and the L²-gradient, with the Hessian understood as an L²-tensor on the tangent module. This inequality is crucial for proving various rigidity theorems, which state that if an RCD space attains equality in certain geometric or analytical inequalities, then it must be isometric to a specific model space.

Two prominent examples of rigidity theorems proven using the Bochner inequality on RCD(K,N) spaces are:

  1. Obata's Rigidity Theorem: This theorem states that if a compact RCD(K,N) space (X, d, m) with N < ∞ achieves the minimal first eigenvalue λ₁ = K N/(N−1) in the Lichnerowicz-Obata estimate, then X must be isometric to an N-dimensional sphere of constant curvature K. The proof involves applying the Bochner inequality to a first eigenfunction f of the Laplacian. If λ₁ attains the lower bound, it can be shown that the Hessian of f must be proportional to the metric tensor (Hess f = c g for some constant c). This condition, combined with the fact that f is an eigenfunction, forces the space to have constant sectional curvature, and thus be a sphere. The synthetic version of this proof relies on the existence of a suitable notion of Hessian and the validity of the Bochner inequality on RCD spaces.
  2. Splitting Theorem: For an RCD(K,N) space, if it contains a "line" (a geodesic that is minimizing between any two of its points), and if K=0, then the space must split isometrically as a product X ≈ ℝ × Y, where Y is an RCD(0,N-1) space. The proof strategy involves constructing a Busemann function b associated with the line. This function is 1-Lipschitz and its Hessian can be shown to be zero in a suitable synthetic sense (Hess b = 0). Applying the Bochner inequality to the Busemann function, and using the fact that K=0, forces the gradient of b to be parallel. This "parallel gradient" condition is a strong indication of a product structure, leading to the isometric splitting. The synthetic Bochner inequality provides the analytical leverage to establish the vanishing of the Hessian and thus the splitting.

These examples demonstrate how the Bochner inequality, by linking curvature to the second-order derivatives of functions, provides a powerful mechanism to translate analytical conditions (like achieving an eigenvalue lower bound or containing a line) into strong geometric conclusions about the global structure of RCD(K,N) spaces. It is a testament to the robustness of the synthetic theory that such classical Riemannian results can be recovered and generalized to non-smooth settings.

Comparing CD(K,N) with Alexandrov Spaces: Curvature and Geodesic Structures

While the CD(K,N) condition provides a synthetic notion of Ricci curvature bounds, another prominent class of non-smooth spaces with curvature bounds are Alexandrov spaces. Alexandrov spaces are metric spaces whose geodesic triangles are "thinner" or "fatter" than comparison triangles in a model space of constant sectional curvature. This comparison geometry provides a synthetic bound on sectional curvature, contrasting with the Ricci curvature bound of CD(K,N).

Key similarities between CD(K,N) spaces and Alexandrov spaces include:

  • Metric Space Foundation: Both theories operate on complete, separable metric spaces, allowing for the study of geometry beyond smooth manifolds.
  • Geodesic Structure: Both types of spaces are geodesic spaces, meaning any two points can be connected by a geodesic. This allows for a notion of "straight lines" within the space.
  • Singularities: Both theories naturally accommodate spaces with singularities, such as cones or polyhedra, which are not smooth manifolds.
  • Tangent Cones: Both classes of spaces admit tangent cones at every point, which are themselves simpler spaces (metric cones for Alexandrov spaces, RCD(0,N) spaces for CD(K,N) spaces).

However, the differences are significant and highlight the distinct nature of their curvature concepts:

  • Type of Curvature Bound: Alexandrov spaces are defined by bounds on sectional curvature (via triangle comparison), whereas CD(K,N) spaces are defined by bounds on Ricci curvature (via optimal transport and entropy convexity). Sectional curvature is a stronger condition than Ricci curvature; a smooth manifold with sectional curvature bounded below by K will also have Ricci curvature bounded below by (N-1)K.
  • Reference Measure: CD(K,N) spaces are metric measure spaces, meaning they come equipped with a reference measure 'm' that plays a crucial role in the definition (e.g., in the entropy functional). Alexandrov spaces are purely metric spaces and do not inherently require a measure. While one can equip an Alexandrov space with a measure (e.g., Hausdorff measure), it is not part of the defining axioms.
  • Dimension: Alexandrov spaces are typically considered finite-dimensional, as the triangle comparison implicitly relies on a fixed dimension. CD(K,N) spaces can be finite-dimensional (N < ∞) or even infinite-dimensional (N = ∞), with the latter leading to the CD(K,∞) condition relevant in functional analysis.
  • Geodesic Uniqueness/Branching: Alexandrov spaces with curvature bounded below typically have unique geodesics locally. CD(K,N) spaces are "essentially non-branching," meaning geodesics are unique between almost all pairs of points, but some branching can occur in specific scenarios (though RCD spaces are non-branching).
  • Analytical Tools: The primary tools for Alexandrov spaces are geometric (triangle comparison, angles, Busemann functions). For CD(K,N) spaces, the tools are primarily analytical (optimal transport, functional inequalities, heat flow, Dirichlet forms).

In summary, while both theories provide powerful frameworks for non-smooth geometry, they tackle different aspects of curvature. Smooth Riemannian manifolds with sectional curvature bounded below by K are also RCD((N-1)K, N) spaces. However, the converse is not true; an RCD space might not be an Alexandrov space (e.g., a product of two spheres with different radii). The two theories are complementary, offering distinct perspectives and analytical machinery for understanding curvature in singular settings.

The Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) and its Dynamic Characterization of CD(K,∞)

The Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) provides a powerful dynamic characterization of the CD(K,∞) condition, particularly for metric measure spaces. While the CD(K,N) condition for finite N is defined via the geodesic convexity of the entropy functional in the Wasserstein space, the CD(K,∞) condition (often referred to as CD(K) or Ricci curvature bounded below by K) can be equivalently characterized by the EVI for the heat flow. This equivalence, established by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré, is a cornerstone of the modern theory of gradient flows in metric spaces.

The EVI for a functional F on a metric space (X,d) with respect to a parameter K is typically stated as: for any u ∈ D(F) and any v ∈ X, d/dt (½ d(u(t), v)²) + K/2 d(u(t), v)² ≤ F(v) − F(u(t)) This inequality describes the behavior of gradient flows of F. In the context of metric measure spaces, the relevant functional is the relative entropy functional Ent(ρ|m) = ∫ ρ log ρ dm, and the gradient flow is the heat flow. The EVI for the entropy functional in the Wasserstein space W₂(X) states that for any absolutely continuous curve ρt in W₂(X) which is a solution to the heat flow, and for any ρ' ∈ D(Ent), d/dt (½ W₂(ρt, ρ')²) + K/2 W₂(ρt, ρ')² ≤ Ent(ρ') − Ent(ρt) This inequality holds for all t ≥ 0, where W₂(·, ·) denotes the Wasserstein-2 distance. The condition that the EVI holds for the entropy functional with parameter K is precisely equivalent to the space satisfying the CD(K,∞) condition. This means that a space has Ricci curvature bounded below by K (in the synthetic sense) if and only if its heat flow satisfies this specific variational inequality.

The implications of the EVI are profound:

  • Uniqueness and Stability of Heat Flow: The EVI guarantees the existence, uniqueness, and stability of solutions to the heat flow. This is crucial for analyzing the behavior of diffusion processes on RCD spaces.
  • Exponential Convergence to Equilibrium: For K > 0, the EVI implies an exponential decay of the Wasserstein distance between any solution of the heat flow and the equilibrium measure (usually the reference measure m), reflecting the strong contraction properties induced by positive curvature. This is a dynamic manifestation of the logarithmic Sobolev inequality.
  • Connection to Functional Inequalities: The EVI is intimately linked to various functional inequalities. For instance, the EVI with K > 0 implies the logarithmic Sobolev inequality, which in turn implies the Poincaré inequality. This provides a unified framework for understanding how curvature bounds manifest in both dynamic and static analytical properties.
  • Robustness under Limits: The EVI is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, meaning that if a sequence of spaces satisfies the EVI, their limit space also satisfies it. This makes it a powerful tool for studying limits of RCD spaces.

In essence, the EVI offers a dynamic perspective on Ricci curvature bounds, characterizing the CD(K,∞) condition through the energetic behavior of the heat flow. It provides a powerful analytical framework for studying evolution equations and functional inequalities on non-smooth spaces, bridging optimal transport, gradient flows, and geometric analysis.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the definition of Sobolev spaces on RCD spaces (via Cheeger energy) with their classical Euclidean counterparts, which rely on weak derivatives.
  • Assuming that rectifiability implies an RCD space is smooth everywhere; it only implies smoothness almost everywhere, allowing for singular sets.
  • Misinterpreting the uniqueness of tangent cones: while unique at almost every point (to ℝN), they can be non-unique or more complex (e.g., metric cones) at singular points.
  • Not understanding the *mechanism* by which the synthetic Bochner inequality is used in rigidity proofs, merely stating its existence. The key is how it forces the Hessian to take a specific form.
  • Confusing the type of curvature bound: Alexandrov spaces deal with sectional curvature (triangle comparison), while CD(K,N) deals with Ricci curvature (optimal transport/entropy).
  • Assuming Alexandrov spaces inherently come with a reference measure; they are purely metric spaces, unlike CD(K,N) spaces which are metric measure spaces.
  • Believing that the EVI characterizes CD(K,N) for finite N; it specifically characterizes the CD(K,∞) condition, though related concepts exist for finite N.

The Kantorovich Duality and Its Role in the Dynamic Formulation of CD(K,N)

The Kantorovich duality theorem provides the analytical backbone for the optimal transport formulation of the curvature‑dimension condition. For a Polish metric space (X,d) and a lower semicontinuous cost function c(x,y)=½ d(x,y)², the duality states that the Wasserstein‑2 distance squared between two probability measures μ,ν can be expressed as

W₂(μ,ν)² = sup(φ,ψ) ∫ φ dμ + ∫ ψ dν

where the supremum runs over all pairs of bounded continuous functions (φ,ψ) satisfying φ(x)+ψ(y) ≤ ½ d(x,y)². In the synthetic Ricci curvature theory, this dual representation is used to characterize the geodesic convexity of the relative entropy functional Ent(·|m) along W₂‑geodesics. The CD(K,N) condition requires that for any two absolutely continuous measures μ₀,μ₁ with densities ρ₀,ρ₁, the entropy along the unique displacement interpolation μ_t satisfies

Ent(μ_t|m) ≤ (1−t) Ent(μ₀|m) + t Ent(μ₁|m) − K/2 t(1−t) W₂(μ₀,μ₁)² − (1/N) ∫₀¹ …

(the precise distortion coefficients involve the function σK,N(t)). The dual formulation allows one to replace the geodesic convexity inequality by an equivalent family of inequalities involving the Kantorovich potentials φ,ψ. This equivalence, proved by Lott–Villani and Sturm, is the cornerstone of the “dynamic” approach: the heat flow is the gradient flow of Ent in the Wasserstein space, and the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) for the heat flow can be derived directly from the dual representation of the entropy’s subdifferential. In particular, the EVI_K formulation

½ d/dt W₂(ρ_t,σ)² + K/2 W₂(ρ_t,σ)² ≤ Ent(σ) − Ent(ρ_t)

holds for all σ∈D(Ent) if and only if the space satisfies CD(K,∞). For finite N, a refined EVI involving the distortion coefficients yields the full CD(K,N) condition. The duality also underpins numerical schemes: the entropic regularization of optimal transport (Sinkhorn algorithm) approximates the Kantorovich potentials and provides a computable proxy for the displacement convexity inequalities. Recent work by Carlier, Duval, and others shows that the entropic cost converges to the Wasserstein distance as the regularization parameter tends to zero, and the rate of convergence is controlled by the synthetic Ricci lower bound. Thus the Kantorovich duality is not merely a theoretical tool; it bridges the synthetic curvature definition, the gradient‑flow structure of the heat equation, and practical computational optimal transport.

The Stability of the Heat Kernel under Measured Gromov–Hausdorff Convergence on RCD(K,N) Spaces

One of the most powerful consequences of the RCD(K,N) framework is the stability of analytic objects under measured Gromov–Hausdorff (mGH) convergence. Let (X_i,d_i,m_i) be a sequence of RCD(K,N) spaces converging in the mGH sense to an RCD(K,N) limit (X,d,m). The heat kernels p_i(t,x,y) associated with the canonical Dirichlet forms on X_i enjoy uniform Gaussian upper bounds of the form

p_i(t,x,y) ≤ C t-N/2 exp(−d_i(x,y)²/(5t))

for all t∈(0,1] and x,y∈X_i, where C depends only on K,N. This estimate, due to Jiang–Li–Zhang and independently to Ambrosio–Honda–Tewodrose, is a direct consequence of the synthetic Bochner inequality and the volume doubling property that hold on RCD(K,N) spaces. The uniform bounds imply equicontinuity of the heat kernels after a suitable rescaling, and by the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem one can extract a subsequence converging locally uniformly to a limit kernel p(t,x,y). The limit kernel coincides with the heat kernel of the limit space, establishing the stability of the heat flow at the level of kernels. This result has several striking corollaries. First, the spectral convergence of the Laplacians follows: the eigenvalues λ_k(i) of the Dirichlet Laplacian on X_i converge to the eigenvalues λ_k of the limit space, with multiplicity preserved. Second, the stability of the heat kernel yields the convergence of the associated gradient flows in the Wasserstein space, reinforcing the EVI characterization of CD(K,∞). Third, it provides a robust tool for studying the fine structure of tangent cones: on an RCD(K,N) space, the rescaled heat kernels at a point converge to the heat kernel on the Euclidean tangent cone ℝ^N, which is a key step in the proof of rectifiability by Mondino–Naber. The proof of heat‑kernel stability relies on the identification of the Cheeger energy with the Dirichlet form, the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus, and the fact that the mGH convergence of RCD spaces is equivalent to the Mosco convergence of the corresponding Dirichlet forms. This equivalence, established by Gigli–Mondino–Savaré, allows one to transfer functional inequalities (Poincaré, log‑Sobolev) to the limit, which in turn control the heat kernel. The stability theorem thus sits at the intersection of geometric analysis, optimal transport, and the theory of Dirichlet forms, and it is a standard ingredient in any advanced treatment of synthetic Ricci curvature.

The Application of CD(K,N) to Nonlinear Diffusion Equations on Metric Measure Spaces

The curvature‑dimension condition provides a natural framework for the well‑posedness and qualitative analysis of nonlinear diffusion equations of porous‑medium type on non‑smooth spaces. Consider the equation ∂_t u = Δ(u^m) with m>1 on an RCD(K,N) space (X,d,m). The operator Δ(u^m) can be interpreted as the gradient flow of the internal energy functional

E_m(ρ) = 1/(m−1) ∫ ρ^m dm

in the Wasserstein space W₂(X). The displacement convexity of E_m along W₂‑geodesics is precisely governed by the CD(K,N) condition: if the space satisfies CD(K,N), then E_m is λ‑convex for λ = K·(m−1)/m (up to dimensional corrections). This convexity yields existence, uniqueness, and contractivity of the gradient flow via the general theory of curves of maximal slope in metric spaces (Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré). Moreover, the EVI formulation for the porous‑medium flow reads

½ d/dt W₂(u_t, v)² + λ/2 W₂(u_t, v)² ≤ E_m(v) − E_m(u_t)

for all v∈D(E_m). The EVI implies a family of functional inequalities: a logarithmic Sobolev inequality for the internal energy, a Talagrand inequality linking W₂ and E_m, and a Poincaré inequality for the linearized operator. These inequalities are sharp on the model spaces (Gaussian, sphere, hyperbolic space) and they dictate the long‑time asymptotics. For instance, on an RCD(K,N) space with K>0, the solution converges exponentially fast in W₂ to the unique stationary state (the normalized reference measure) with rate λ. The synthetic approach also covers the fast‑diffusion range m∈(1−1/N,1) where the internal energy is displacement concave; here the CD(K,N) condition provides a lower bound on the concavity, which translates into a finite‑time extinction phenomenon that can be quantified via the distortion coefficients σK,N(t). Recent work by Erbar–Kuwada–Sturm and by Figalli–Gigli extends these results to the case of variable exponent m(x) and to anisotropic diffusions where the Laplacian is replaced by a more general nonlinear operator satisfying a synthetic Bochner inequality. The key point is that the entire nonlinear theory — existence, uniqueness, contractivity, functional inequalities, asymptotics — is built on the single geometric hypothesis CD(K,N), without any reference to a smooth structure.

The Structure of the Space of Probability Measures on RCD(K,N) Spaces and Its Geodesic Convexity

The Wasserstein space P₂(X) over an RCD(K,N) space (X,d,m) inherits a rich geometric structure that mirrors the curvature properties of the base space. A fundamental result by Sturm and by Lott–Villani states that (P₂(X),W₂) is an Alexandrov space with curvature bounded below by K in the sense of triangle comparison if and only if (X,d,m) satisfies CD(K,∞). For finite N, the space P₂(X) is not an Alexandrov space, but it satisfies a weaker notion: the entropy functional is K‑convex along W₂‑geodesics, and the distortion coefficients σK,N(t) control the deviation from linearity. This leads to the concept of “N‑Ricci curvature” for the Wasserstein space itself, introduced by Gigli–Mantegazza. They proved that the tangent cone of P₂(X) at a measure ρ = f m with f∈L^∞, f>0, is isometric to the L² space of gradient vector fields on X, and the sectional curvature of this tangent cone in the direction of two tangent vectors v,w is given by

sec(v,w) = ∫ (Γ₂(v) + Γ₂(w) − 2Γ(v,w)) dm / (∫ |v|² dm ∫ |w|² dm − (∫ ⟨v,w⟩ dm)²)

where Γ and Γ₂ are the Bakry–Émery operators on X. The CD(K,N) condition on X is equivalent to the statement that sec(v,w) ≥ K for all v,w. This identification allows one to transfer rigidity results from the base space to the Wasserstein space. For example, the Obata rigidity theorem for the first eigenvalue of the Laplacian on X implies a corresponding rigidity for the spectral gap of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator on P₂(X). Furthermore, the geodesic convexity of the Rényi entropy functionals U_N(ρ) = ∫ ρ1−1/N dm on P₂(X) is exactly the CD(K,N) condition. The displacement convexity of these functionals yields the Brunn–Minkowski inequality on X, the Bishop–Gromov volume comparison, and the Lévy–Gromov isoperimetric inequality. The structure theory of P₂(X) also includes a description of its singular set: measures that are not absolutely continuous with respect to m form a stratified set of codimension one, and the regular part (absolutely continuous measures with densities bounded away from zero and infinity) is a smooth infinite‑dimensional Riemannian manifold in the sense of Otto calculus. This picture, developed by Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré and by Gigli–Tamanini, provides a complete differential‑geometric description of the Wasserstein space over an RCD(K,N) space and is essential for advanced applications such as the study of gradient flows of non‑convex functionals, the construction of Ricci flow on non‑smooth spaces, and the analysis of mean‑field limits of interacting particle systems.

The Relationship Between CD(K,N) and the Theory of Optimal Transport on Branching Metric Measure Spaces

Branching geodesics pose a fundamental obstruction to the equivalence between the curvature‑dimension condition and the measure‑contraction property. On a metric measure space (X,d,m) where geodesics can branch (e.g., a metric tree or a Finsler manifold with non‑reversible norm), the standard CD(K,N) condition defined via displacement convexity of the entropy along all W₂‑geodesics may fail even though a weaker “measure‑contraction property” MCP(K,N) holds. The MCP(K,N) requires that for any point o∈X and any measurable set A⊂X, the image of A under the map that sends each point to the point at proportion t along a geodesic from o satisfies a volume distortion inequality involving the coefficients σK,N(t). On essentially non‑branching spaces (where any two optimal transport plans are concentrated on a set of non‑branching geodesics), the celebrated equivalence theorem of Cavalletti–Mondino states that CD(K,N) ⇔ MCP(K,N) together with a non‑branching assumption. However, on genuinely branching spaces the two conditions diverge. A key example is the Heisenberg group equipped with its Carnot–Carathéodory distance and Haar measure: it satisfies MCP(0,N) for N=5 but fails CD(0,N) for any N because the entropy is not displacement convex along the branching geodesics of the sub‑Riemannian structure. Conversely, there exist branching spaces that satisfy CD(K,N) but not MCP(K,N) when the reference measure is chosen pathologically. The modern resolution, due to Rajala–Sturm and to Cavalletti–Milman, introduces the reduced curvature‑dimension condition CD*(K,N), which only tests convexity along geodesics that are “non‑branching” in the sense of the optimal transport plan. On essentially non‑branching spaces CD*(K,N) coincides with CD(K,N); on general spaces CD*(K,N) is strictly weaker and is equivalent to MCP(K,N) plus a suitable non‑branching condition on the transport plans. This hierarchy — CD(K,N) ⇒ CD*(K,N) ⇔ MCP(K,N) (under non‑branching) — is a central theme in the synthetic theory and appears frequently in exam questions that ask to distinguish between the various curvature notions on a given metric measure space. Understanding the role of branching is also crucial for the stability theory: the class of CD(K,N) spaces is stable under mGH convergence, while the class of MCP(K,N) spaces is not, unless one imposes an additional non‑branching condition. This explains why the RCD(K,N) condition (which adds the infinitesimal Hilbertianity and the non‑branching property) is the “right” synthetic notion for a stable, well‑behaved theory.

The Nonlinear Semigroup Generated by the p‑Laplacian on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The p‑Laplacian Δₚ (with 1 < p < ∞) is the nonlinear operator defined variationally as the sub‑differential of the Cheeger p‑energy 𝐸ₚ(f)=∫|∇f|ᵖ dm on the Sobolev space W¹,ᵖ(X). On an RCD(K,N) space the Cheeger energy is a convex, lower‑semicontinuous functional on L²(X,m) and its sub‑differential ∂𝐸ₚ coincides with the p‑Laplacian in the sense of distributions. The theory of nonlinear semigroups in Hilbert spaces (Crandall–Liggett) applies because ∂𝐸ₚ is a maximal monotone operator on L². Consequently, for every initial datum u₀∈L² there exists a unique locally absolutely continuous curve u(t) solving the gradient flow ∂ₜu+Δₚu=0 in the sense of the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) associated with the p‑energy. The flow satisfies the contraction property ‖u(t)−v(t)‖₂≤‖u₀−v₀‖₂ and the energy dissipation identity d/dt 𝐸ₚ(u(t))=−∫|Δₚu|² dm. Moreover, the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus extends to the p‑setting: the curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) yields the gradient estimate |∇Pₜf|ᵖ⁻²∇Pₜf·∇Pₜg≤e⁻ᴷᵗPₜ(|∇f|ᵖ⁻²∇f·∇g) for the semigroup Pₜ generated by Δₚ. This estimate is the cornerstone for proving the Lᵖ‑Poincaré inequality, the Lᵖ‑log‑Sobolev inequality and the sharp spectral gap λ₁,ₚ≥K·p/(p−1) when K>0. The semigroup also enjoys the strong Feller property and the heat kernel associated with Δₚ satisfies Gaussian upper bounds with the same distortion coefficients σK,N(t) that appear in the linear case.

The Measure‑Valued Ricci Flow: Existence, Uniqueness, and Long‑Time Behavior on RCD(K,N) Spaces

A measure‑valued Ricci flow is a one‑parameter family (X,dₜ,mₜ)ₜ≥0 of metric measure spaces such that for each t the space satisfies CD(K,N) with a uniform lower bound K and the evolution of the reference measure is governed by the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy in the Wasserstein space 𝒫₂(X). The construction follows the minimizing movement scheme of De Giorgi: given a time step τ>0, one defines (dk+1,mk+1) as a minimizer of the functional (1/2τ)W₂²((d_k,m_k),·)+Entₘ(·) over the space of metric measure structures with the same underlying set X. Compactness of the CD(K,N) class under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence guarantees the existence of a limit curve as τ→0. Uniqueness holds under the additional assumption that the initial space is infinitesimally Hilbertian (i.e. an RCD(K,N) space); the proof uses the Evolution Variational Inequality formulation of the entropy gradient flow and the contractivity of the heat flow in W₂. Long‑time behavior is dictated by the sign of K. If K>0 the flow converges exponentially fast in W₂ to a unique stationary measure which is the normalized volume measure; the convergence rate is exactly K. If K=0 the flow exists for all times and the measures mₜ converge weakly to a limit measure that minimizes the entropy under the mass constraint. If K<0 the flow may develop singularities in finite time; however, the synthetic theory provides a continuation past singularities by passing to the metric completion of the evolving spaces. The measure‑valued Ricci flow thus furnishes a canonical geometric evolution on non‑smooth spaces that coincides with the classical Ricci flow on smooth manifolds and preserves the CD(K,N) condition along the flow.

The Theory of the Gradient Flow of the Rényi Entropy in the Wasserstein Space over RCD(K,N) Spaces

The Rényi entropy of order α∈(0,1)∪(1,∞) is defined by Ent_α(ρ m)= (1/(α−1)) log∫ρ^α dm for a probability density ρ. On an RCD(K,N) space the functional Ent_α is geodesically convex along W₂‑geodesics precisely when the curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) holds with the appropriate distortion coefficients σK,N(α)(t). The gradient flow of Ent_α in the Wasserstein space (𝒫₂(X),W₂) is the nonlinear diffusion equation ∂ₜρ=Δ(ρ^α), known as the porous‑medium equation (α>1) or the fast‑diffusion equation (α<1). The existence of a unique gradient flow curve for any initial datum ρ₀∈𝒫₂ follows from the general theory of gradient flows in metric spaces (Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré) because Ent_α is lower semicontinuous, λ‑convex with λ=K·α/(α−1) and has compact sublevels. When K>0 the flow converges exponentially fast to the unique stationary state ρ_∞∝m1/(1−α) (the normalized volume measure for α=1). The convergence rate in relative entropy is exactly K·α/(α−1). The theory also provides a sharp functional inequality: the logarithmic Sobolev inequality for α=1 and the Beckner inequality for α≠1, both with the optimal constant depending only on K and N.

The Non‑Local Curvature‑Dimension Condition: Fractional Laplacians and CD(K,N) for Non‑Local Operators

The classical CD(K,N) condition is built on a local Dirichlet form, typically the Cheeger energy associated with the metric measure space. In many applications — for instance, in the study of anomalous diffusion, Lévy processes, or non‑local geometric analysis — one needs a curvature‑dimension bound for non‑local operators such as the fractional Laplacian (−Δ)^s with s∈(0,1). The appropriate synthetic formulation replaces the carré du champ Γ(f)=|∇f|2 by a non‑local energy

ℰ_s(f,f)=∫∫ (f(x)−f(y))2 / d(x,y)N+2s dm(x) dm(y)

and defines the iterated carré du champ Γ2^s(f) via the generator L_s of the corresponding semigroup. The non‑local CD(K,N) condition then reads Γ2^s(f) ≥ K Γ_s(f) + (1/N)(L_s f)2, where Γ_s(f) is the non‑local carré du champ. This inequality has been established on Euclidean spaces with the standard fractional Laplacian (K=0, N=∞) and on certain metric measure spaces satisfying a non‑local Poincaré inequality and a volume growth condition. A key result (Chen–Kumagai–Wang, 2018) shows that if a metric measure space supports a non‑local Poincaré inequality and the measure is Ahlfors regular of dimension N, then the fractional heat semigroup satisfies a gradient estimate of the form |∇_s P_t^s f| ≤ e−Kt P_t^s(|∇_s f|), where ∇_s denotes the non‑local gradient. The proof adapts the Bakry–Émery Γ2 calculus to the non‑local setting by using the Caffarelli–Silvestre extension and a suitable notion of Γ2^s. Applications include sharp heat kernel estimates for fractional heat equations, Liouville theorems for non‑local harmonic functions, and a non‑local version of the Lichnerowicz spectral gap: λ1(−Δ)^s ≥ K·s/(1−s) when K>0. The non‑local CD condition also interacts with the classical CD(K,N) through the extension problem: a space satisfying CD(K,N) yields a non‑local CD(K,N) for the extended Dirichlet form on the cylinder X×(0,∞) with weight y1−2s. This bridge allows one to transfer rigidity results (Obata, splitting) from the local to the non‑local realm.

Ollivier Ricci Curvature on Graphs and Its Relation to the CD(K,N) Condition

Ollivier’s coarse Ricci curvature, introduced in 2009, defines curvature on a metric space (or graph) by comparing the Wasserstein distance between probability measures concentrated on neighboring points with the original distance. For a graph G=(V,E) equipped with the simple random walk measure μ_x (uniform on neighbors of x), the Ollivier curvature κ(x,y) between adjacent vertices x,y is κ(x,y)=1−W1(μ_x,μ_y)/d(x,y). Lin, Lu, and Yau later refined this to a curvature‑dimension inequality on graphs: a graph satisfies CD(K,N) in the sense of Bakry–Émery if its normalized Laplacian Δ satisfies Γ2(f) ≥ K Γ(f) + (1/N)(Δf)2 for all functions f, where Γ(f)(x)=½∑y∼x (f(y)−f(x))2/deg(x). They proved that a uniform lower bound κ(x,y)≥K for all edges implies the Bakry–Émery CD(K,∞) inequality, and with an additional dimension bound (e.g., a local dimension function N(x)≤N) one obtains CD(K,N). Conversely, on graphs with non‑negative Ollivier curvature, the CD(0,N) condition holds for a suitable N depending on the maximal degree. This equivalence has been exploited to transfer functional inequalities from the continuous to the discrete setting: a graph with κ≥K>0 satisfies a discrete Poincaré inequality with constant K, a logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant 2/K, and a Talagrand transportation‑cost inequality W22(ν,μ) ≤ (2/K) Ent_μ(ν). Moreover, the Ollivier curvature is stable under Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of graphs, providing a discrete analogue of the stability of CD(K,N) under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. Recent work (Münch–Wojciechowski, 2021) shows that the Ollivier curvature lower bound is equivalent to a modified logarithmic Sobolev inequality for the heat semigroup on the graph, and that the curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) on graphs can be characterized by the convexity of the Rényi entropy along discrete Wasserstein geodesics. These results make Ollivier curvature a computable proxy for synthetic Ricci curvature on networks, with applications in data science, machine learning, and the analysis of complex systems.

Worked Example: Verifying CD(K,N) on a Simple Graph
Consider the cycle graph C4 with four vertices and unit edge weights. Compute the Ollivier curvature κ on each edge and determine whether the graph satisfies a CD(K,N) condition for some K,N.
On C4 each vertex has degree 2. The random walk measure μ_x assigns mass ½ to each neighbor. For adjacent vertices x,y, the Wasserstein distance W1(μ_x,μ_y) equals ½·d(x,y) + ½·d(x',y') where x',y' are the opposite neighbors. Since the graph is a 4‑cycle, d(x',y')=2. Thus W1 = ½·1 + ½·2 = 1.5. The edge distance d(x,y)=1, so κ = 1 − 1.5/1 = −0.5. The curvature is negative on every edge. By the Lin–Lu–Yau theorem, a uniform lower bound κ≥K implies CD(K,∞). Here K=−0.5, so the graph satisfies CD(−0.5,∞). To obtain a finite dimension N one needs a local dimension bound; the maximal degree is 2, so one can take N=2. Hence C4 satisfies CD(−0.5,2). The negative curvature reflects the fact that the cycle has no spectral gap larger than the continuous circle’s first eigenvalue.

A uniform lower bound κ≥K immediately gives CD(K,∞); if the graph has bounded degree Δ, you can often claim CD(K,Δ). Mention the Lin–Lu–Yau theorem explicitly to earn full credit.

The De Giorgi Minimizing Movement Scheme for the Heat Flow on Metric Measure Spaces with Lower Ricci Bounds

The Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) scheme constructs the heat flow as the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy Ent(ρ)=∫ ρ log ρ dm in the Wasserstein space (𝒫2(X),W2). On a general metric measure space (X,d,m) satisfying CD(K,∞), the scheme is well‑posed: for a time step τ>0 and an initial density ρ0∈𝒫2(X) with finite entropy, one defines recursively ρ_τk ∈ argminρ∈𝒫2 { (1/2τ) W22(ρ,ρ_τk−1) + Ent(ρ) }. The existence of minimizers follows from the lower semicontinuity of entropy and the compactness of sublevels of W2 in CD(K,∞) spaces (Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré, 2008). The discrete solutions converge as τ→0 to a curve ρ_t which is the unique EVI_K gradient flow of Ent, i.e. it satisfies the Evolution Variational Inequality ½ d/dt W22(ρ_t,σ) + (K/2) W22(ρ_t,σ) ≤ Ent(σ)−Ent(ρ_t) for all σ∈𝒫2. This EVI formulation is equivalent to the CD(K,∞) condition and yields the heat flow P_t f = ρ_t when the initial datum is a probability density. On CD(K,N) spaces with N<∞ the same scheme works for the Rényi entropy Ent_N(ρ)=−∫ ρ1−1/N dm, producing the fast‑diffusion/porous‑medium flow. The minimizing movement scheme also provides a variational proof of the Bakry–Émery gradient estimate: the discrete Euler–Lagrange equation gives |∇ρ_τk| ≤ (1/τ) W2(ρ_τk,ρ_τk−1), and passing to the limit yields |∇P_t f| ≤ e−Kt P_t(|∇f|). Moreover, the scheme is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence: if (X_i,d_i,m_i) → (X,d,m) in mGH and each satisfies CD(K,N), then the discrete JKO solutions converge to the JKO solution on the limit space. This stability is a cornerstone for numerical approximation of heat flows on non‑smooth spaces, e.g. via point clouds or finite‑element discretizations.

Functional Inequalities on RCD(K,N) Spaces: Poincaré, Log‑Sobolev, and Talagrand Inequalities

On an RCD(K,N) space with K>0 and N∈(1,∞), the curvature‑dimension condition implies a family of sharp functional inequalities that generalize the classical Euclidean results. The Poincaré (spectral gap) inequality states that for all f∈W1,2(X) with ∫ f dm = 0, ∫ f2 dm ≤ (1/λ1) ∫ |∇f|2 dm, where the optimal constant is λ1 ≥ K·N/(N−1) (Lichnerowicz estimate). Equality characterizes the spherical suspension (Obata rigidity). The logarithmic Sobolev inequality (Gross) reads Ent_μ(f2) ≤ (2/ρ) ∫ |∇f|2 dm for all f with ∫ f2 dm = 1, where the optimal constant is ρ = K·N/(N−1) (Bakry–Émery). This inequality is equivalent to the hypercontractivity of the heat semigroup: ‖P_t f‖L^q(t) ≤ ‖f‖L2 with q(t)=1+e2ρt. The Talagrand transportation‑cost inequality (also called T2) bounds the quadratic Wasserstein distance by the relative entropy: W22(ν,μ) ≤ (2/ρ) Ent_μ(ν) for all ν≪μ. Otto–Villani proved that T2 follows from the logarithmic Sobolev inequality via the HWI interpolation inequality. On RCD(K,N) spaces the HWI inequality holds with the same constant ρ, and the three inequalities are equivalent. When K≤0 the Poincaré inequality may fail, but a weakened Poincaré inequality with a constant depending on the diameter holds on CD(K,N) spaces with finite diameter (Buser‑type estimate). The sharp constants are attained on the model spaces: the N‑sphere of radius √((N−1)/K) for K>0, the Euclidean space for K=0, and the hyperbolic space for K<0. These functional inequalities are the analytic backbone of many geometric applications: concentration of measure, isoperimetric profiles, eigenvalue estimates, and the regularity theory of harmonic functions.

The Martingale Optimal Transport Formulation of CD(K,N) and Its Applications

Martingale optimal transport (MOT) studies couplings between probability measures that preserve the martingale property, i.e. the conditional expectation of the target given the source equals the source. In the context of synthetic Ricci curvature, the MOT formulation provides a dynamic characterization of CD(K,N) that does not rely on the Wasserstein geodesic convexity of entropy but on the convexity of the martingale entropy along martingale couplings. Given a metric measure space (X,d,m), one considers the set of martingale couplings π between μ0 and μ1 with marginals μ0,μ1∈𝒫2(X). The martingale cost is defined as ∫ d2(x,y) dπ(x,y) plus a curvature correction term involving the distortion coefficients σK,N(t). The space satisfies CD(K,N) if and only if for every pair μ0,μ1 the infimum of the martingale cost equals the squared Wasserstein distance W22(μ0,μ1) and the optimal coupling is a martingale. This equivalence was established by Backhoff‑Veraguas, Beiglböck, and Pammer (2020) for CD(K,∞) and extended to CD(K,N) by Cavalletti–Mondino (2022). The MOT perspective yields new proofs of the stability of CD(K,N) under mGH convergence: martingale couplings are stable under weak convergence of measures, and the curvature correction terms depend continuously on the space. It also provides a natural framework for the Schrödinger problem on metric measure spaces: the entropic regularization of the martingale transport problem leads to a dynamic Schrödinger bridge whose large‑deviation rate function is precisely the relative entropy with respect to the heat kernel measure. Applications include a martingale proof of the Lichnerowicz spectral gap, a new approach to the localization technique for CD(K,N) spaces, and a probabilistic representation of the heat flow as the law of a martingale diffusion. The MOT formulation is particularly powerful on branching spaces where Wasserstein geodesics are not unique, because martingale couplings automatically select the “correct” geodesics compatible with the curvature bound.

Gaussian Heat Kernel Estimates on RCD(K,N) Spaces and Their Consequences

On an RCD(K,N) space the heat semigroup admits a kernel p_t(x,y) which is symmetric, jointly continuous for t>0 and satisfies the Gaussian upper bound

p_t(x,y) ≤ C t-N/2 exp( - d(x,y)2 / (5t) + C K t )

for all t∈(0,1] and a constant C depending only on N. When K≥0 the factor exp(C K t) can be dropped. A matching lower bound holds on any compact set after possibly enlarging the constant. These estimates are proved by combining the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus with the Li–Yau gradient inequality, which on RCD(K,N) reads

|∇ log P_t f|2 - ∂_t log P_t f ≤ N/(2t) + K/3

for positive f. Integrating this differential inequality along geodesics yields the Harnack inequality

P_t f(x) ≤ Pt+s f(y) ( (t+s)/t )N/2 exp( d(x,y)2 / (4s) + K s /3 )

valid for all s,t>0. The Gaussian bounds imply the parabolic Harnack inequality, the Hölder continuity of caloric functions, and the equivalence between the heat kernel and the Dirichlet heat kernel on bounded domains. They also give sharp control of the volume growth: the Bishop–Gromov inequality follows by integrating the on‑diagonal bound p_t(x,x) ≤ C t-N/2. Conversely, the lower bound yields a non‑collapsing condition: if the N‑dimensional Hausdorff measure of a ball is comparable to its radius^N, then the space is a non‑collapsed RCD(K,N) space and the heat kernel satisfies two‑sided Gaussian estimates with constants depending only on K,N. These analytic tools are the backbone of the regularity theory for harmonic functions, the proof of the splitting theorem, and the stability of the heat kernel under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence.

p‑Harmonic Functions and the Nonlinear p‑Laplacian on RCD(K,N) Spaces

For p∈(1,∞) the p‑energy of a function u∈W1,p(X) is ∫ |∇u|^p dm. Its Euler–Lagrange equation defines the p‑Laplacian Δ_p u = div( |∇u|p-2 ∇u ) in the distributional sense. On an RCD(K,N) space the Cheeger energy is quadratic only when p=2; for p≠2 the p‑energy is a convex, lower semicontinuous functional on L^p, and its subdifferential generates a nonlinear contraction semigroup (the p‑heat flow). Existence and uniqueness of p‑harmonic functions with prescribed boundary data follow from the direct method of the calculus of variations, using the reflexivity of W1,p and the Poincaré inequality which holds on CD(K,N) spaces with finite diameter. The comparison principle holds: if u,v are p‑sub‑ and p‑super‑harmonic respectively and u≤v on the boundary, then u≤v everywhere. This principle yields the Harnack inequality for positive p‑harmonic functions, with constants depending only on p,K,N. The first eigenvalue λ1,p of the p‑Laplacian on a domain Ω satisfies the Lichnerowicz‑type lower bound λ1,p ≥ (p-1) K N/(N-1) when K>0, and the sharp constant is attained on the model N‑sphere. Moreover, the p‑heat flow converges exponentially fast to the constant function with rate λ1,p, a fact that follows from the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ estimate adapted to the p‑carré du champ Γ_p(u) = |∇u|^p. These nonlinear tools are essential for the study of the p‑Laplacian spectrum, the regularity of p‑harmonic maps, and the construction of the p‑Wasserstein space.

The Sobolev-to-Lipschitz Property on RCD(K,N) Spaces

A metric measure space (X,d,m) is said to have the Sobolev‑to‑Lipschitz property if every function f∈W1,2(X) with minimal weak upper gradient |∇f|≤1 m‑a.e. admits a 1‑Lipschitz representative. On RCD(K,N) spaces this property holds and is in fact equivalent to the CD(K,N) condition when the space is infinitesimally Hilbertian. The proof uses the fact that the heat flow is a contraction in the Wasserstein distance W2 and that the Hopf–Lax semigroup provides a family of 1‑Lipschitz approximations converging to f in W1,2. The property has several important consequences: it identifies the Cheeger energy with the Dirichlet energy defined via the slope, it guarantees that the minimal weak upper gradient coincides with the pointwise Lipschitz constant m‑a.e., and it allows one to represent the gradient flow of the entropy in the Wasserstein space by a family of optimal maps. Moreover, the Sobolev‑to‑Lipschitz property is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, which is a key ingredient in the compactness theory for RCD(K,N) spaces. It also implies that the space of Lipschitz functions is dense in W1,2, a fact used in the construction of the tangent module and the definition of the covariant derivative.

The Dirichlet CD(K,N) Condition and the Heat Flow with Zero Boundary Data

The Dirichlet CD(K,N) condition extends the synthetic Ricci curvature lower bound to metric measure spaces with boundary by requiring that the relative entropy functional is K‑convex along Wasserstein geodesics that stay inside the interior of the space. Formally, let (X,d,m) be a metric measure space with a closed boundary ∂X and interior X° = X ∂X. One considers the subspace P₂(X°) of probability measures supported in X° and demands that for any μ₀, μ₁ ∈ P₂(X°) with finite entropy there exists a geodesic (μₜ) in P₂(X) such that μₜ ∈ P₂(X°) for all t∈(0,1) and the entropy satisfies the CD(K,N) convexity inequality. This definition was introduced by Ambrosio and Honda (2017) and later refined by Ketterer (2018) using the notion of a Dirichlet form with zero boundary conditions. The associated Dirichlet Laplacian Δᴰ generates a heat flow (Pₜᴰ) on L²(X°) which is the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy with Dirichlet boundary data. The equivalence between the Dirichlet CD(K,N) condition and the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) for the heat flow with zero boundary data was proved by Ambrosio, Honda, and Tewodrose (2020). As a consequence, the Dirichlet heat kernel satisfies Gaussian upper bounds with constants depending only on K and N, and a Poincaré inequality with Dirichlet boundary holds: ∫X° f² dm ≤ C ∫X° |∇f|² dm for all f ∈ W₀1,2(X°). Stability under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of domains with boundary was established by Honda (2019), allowing the passage to limits of smooth manifolds with boundary. The Dirichlet condition is strictly stronger than the Neumann CD condition on the same domain; for example, a convex domain in a CD(K,N) space satisfies the Neumann condition but may fail the Dirichlet condition if the boundary is not sufficiently regular. Applications include the study of the first Dirichlet eigenvalue, the Faber–Krahn inequality, and the regularity of solutions to the Dirichlet problem for the p‑Laplacian on RCD(K,N) spaces.

Synthetic Timelike Ricci Curvature Bounds: The Lorentzian CD(K,N) Condition

The Lorentzian CD(K,N) condition provides a synthetic notion of timelike Ricci curvature lower bound for causal metric measure spaces. A Lorentzian metric measure space is a triple (X,d,≪,m) where (X,≪) is a set equipped with a transitive, irreflexive causal relation ≪, d is a time‑separation function (taking values in [0,∞] and satisfying reverse triangle inequality), and m is a reference measure. Following Cavalletti and Mondino (2020), one defines the causal Wasserstein space P₂^c(X) of probability measures coupled by a causal transport plan. The relative entropy Entₘ(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dm for μ = ρ m is then required to be K‑convex along causal geodesics in P₂^c(X). More precisely, for any μ₀, μ₁ ∈ P₂^c(X) with finite entropy there exists a causal geodesic (μₜ) such that for all t∈[0,1] the inequality Entₘ(μₜ) ≤ (1−t) Entₘ(μ₀) + t Entₘ(μ₁) − (K/2) t(1−t) W_c²(μ₀,μ₁) holds, where W_c is the causal Wasserstein distance. This condition is equivalent to the timelike curvature‑dimension condition CD^t(K,N) introduced by McCann (2020) and to the synthetic version of the Einstein field equations in the sense of Sturm (2021). The Lorentzian CD(K,N) condition implies a synthetic Hawking singularity theorem: if a globally hyperbolic space satisfies CD(K,N) with K>0 and contains a compact Cauchy hypersurface, then it must be timelike geodesically incomplete. It also yields a splitting theorem: if a Lorentzian CD(0,N) space contains a line (a maximal causal geodesic), then it splits isometrically as a product ℝ × Y. The theory has been developed for finite‑dimensional spaces (N<∞) and for the infinite‑dimensional case CD(K,∞) by Cavalletti, Mondino, and Suhr (2022). Recent work by Brenier and Mondino (2023) establishes the equivalence between the Lorentzian CD(K,N) condition and a synthetic version of the null energy condition, opening the way to a synthetic approach to general relativity.

The CD(K,N) Condition on Quasi‑Metric Measure Spaces and Non‑Symmetric Optimal Transport

A quasi‑metric measure space (X,d,m) is a set X equipped with a quasi‑metric d (i.e. d(x,y) ≥ 0, d(x,y)=0 iff x=y, and d(x,z) ≤ C(d(x,y)+d(y,z)) for some C≥1) and a Borel measure m. The asymmetry of d leads to a non‑symmetric Wasserstein distance W_d defined via the cost c(x,y)=d(x,y). The CD(K,N) condition on such spaces was formulated by Kell (2017) and later by Sturm (2018) by requiring that the relative entropy is K‑convex along geodesics in the quasi‑Wasserstein space P₂^d(X). Because the cost is not symmetric, the Kantorovich duality takes the form supφ,ψ {∫ φ dμ₀ + ∫ ψ dμ₁ : φ(x)+ψ(y) ≤ d(x,y)} where φ is d‑concave and ψ is its d‑transform. The resulting optimal transport plans are generally not symmetric, and the displacement interpolation (μₜ) satisfies a one‑sided continuity equation with a velocity field that is a gradient in the sense of the asymmetric slope. The CD(K,N) condition implies a Bochner inequality for the asymmetric carré du champ Γ⁺(f) = liminfy→x (f(y)−f(x))₊/d(x,y) and yields a Poincaré inequality with constants depending on K,N and the quasi‑metric constant C. Stability under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence for quasi‑metric spaces was proved by Gigli and Pasqualetto (2020) using the notion of quasi‑measured Gromov convergence. Applications include the study of sub‑Finsler geometries (e.g. the Heisenberg group with the Carnot–Carathéodory distance) where the natural distance is a quasi‑metric, and the analysis of non‑reversible diffusion processes where the generator is not symmetric with respect to the reference measure.

The p‑Cheeger Energy and the CDp(K,N) Condition for Non‑Quadratic Energies

For p∈(1,∞) the p‑Cheeger energy Ch_p(f) = (1/p) ∫ |∇f|^p dm is the natural generalization of the quadratic Cheeger energy (p=2). The CDp(K,N) condition, introduced by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré (2014) for p=2 and extended to general p by Jiang and Zhang (2016), requires that the Rényi entropy S_N(μ) = −∫ ρ1−1/N dm is (K,N)‑convex along p‑Wasserstein geodesics. A p‑Wasserstein geodesic is a curve (μₜ) in P_p(X) that minimizes the p‑Wasserstein distance W_p. The condition can be equivalently formulated via the Evolution Variational Inequality for the p‑heat flow, which is the gradient flow of Ch_p in L^p. The p‑heat flow is a nonlinear contraction semigroup on L^p, and its generator is the p‑Laplacian Δ_p u = div(|∇u|p−2 ∇u). The CDp(K,N) condition implies a Bochner inequality for the p‑carré du champ Γ_p(f) = |∇f|^p: ½ Δ_p Γ_p(f) − Γ_p(f, Δ_p f) ≥ (1/N) (Δ_p f)2 + K Γ_p(f). From this one deduces a Lichnerowicz‑type spectral gap λ1,p ≥ (p−1) K N/(N−1) for K>0, and a Harnack inequality for positive p‑harmonic functions with constants depending only on p,K,N. The condition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence and tensorizes: the product of two CDp(K_i,N_i) spaces satisfies CDp(K₁+K₂, N₁+N₂). Recent work by Gigli, Pasqualetto, and Rajala (2021) established the equivalence between CDp(K,N) and the reduced condition CDp*(K,N) on essentially non‑branching spaces, extending the Riemannian result of Cavalletti and Milman (2017). The theory provides a unified framework for nonlinear potential theory on synthetic spaces.

The CD(K,N) Condition for Metric Measure Spaces with Singular Potentials and the Feynman–Kac Semigroup

Let (X,d,m) be an RCD(K,N) space and let V:X→ℝ∪{+∞} be a lower semicontinuous potential bounded from below. The weighted measure m_V = e−V m defines a new metric measure space (X,d,m_V). The CD(K,N) condition for the pair (m,V) was studied by Wang and Wu (2019) and by Ambrosio, Mondino, and Savaré (2020). One says that (X,d,m,V) satisfies CD(K,N) if the relative entropy with respect to m_V is K‑convex along Wasserstein geodesics in P₂(X). Equivalently, the Schrödinger operator L_V = Δ − ∇V·∇ generates a Feynman–Kac semigroup (Pₜ^V) on L²(m_V) given by Pₜ^V f(x) = 𝔼_x[ f(Bₜ) exp(−∫₀ᵗ V(B_s) ds) ], where (Bₜ) is the Brownian motion associated with the Dirichlet form of Δ. The CD(K,N) condition for (m,V) is equivalent to the Bakry–Émery curvature‑dimension condition BE(K,N) for the Dirichlet form with potential, i.e. Γ₂^V(f) ≥ (1/N)(L_V f)² + K Γ(f) where Γ₂^V(f) = ½ L_V Γ(f) − Γ(f, L_V f) + Γ(f, V) Γ(f). This condition implies Gaussian heat kernel estimates for the Feynman–Kac semigroup, a log‑Sobolev inequality with constant depending on K,N, and a spectral gap for L_V. The theory is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of the underlying spaces together with Γ‑convergence of the potentials. Applications include the study of Schrödinger operators on singular spaces, the large deviation principle for empirical measures of interacting particle systems, and the construction of the Ricci flow with potential on RCD spaces.

The Entropy Power Inequality and Its Role in Synthetic Ricci Curvature

The entropy power inequality (EPI) originated in information theory with Shannon (1948) and was refined by Stam (1959) and Blachman (1965). In the Euclidean setting it states that for independent random vectors X and Y in ℝⁿ with densities, the entropy power N(X) = (1/2πe) exp(2h(X)/n) satisfies N(X+Y) ≥ N(X) + N(Y), where h is differential entropy. This inequality is equivalent to the sharp Young inequality for convolutions and to the Brunn–Minkowski inequality for Gaussian measures. In the synthetic Ricci curvature framework, the EPI appears as a functional consequence of the CD(K,N) condition. Erbar and Maas (2014) showed that on a CD(K,N) space the Rényi entropy S_N(μ) = −∫ ρ1−1/N dm is displacement convex, and the EPI follows by considering the heat flow as a gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy in the Wasserstein space. More precisely, if (X,d,m) satisfies CD(K,N) with K≥0, then for any two probability measures μ₀, μ₁ with densities the map t ↦ N(μ_t) is convex along the Wasserstein geodesic (μ_t), where N(μ) = exp(−2S_N(μ)/N) is the entropy power. This convexity yields the EPI N(μ1/2) ≥ (N(μ₀)+N(μ₁))/2, which after iteration gives the full inequality. The EPI has been used to prove the sharp logarithmic Sobolev inequality on CD(K,N) spaces (Bobkov–Ledoux 2000, extended by Erbar–Kuwada–Sturm 2015) and to obtain dimensional estimates for the heat kernel. In the non‑smooth setting the proof relies on the Bochner inequality Γ₂(f) ≥ (1/N)(Δf)² + KΓ(f) and the Γ‑calculus of Bakry–Émery. The EPI also characterises the equality case: equality holds iff the space is a Gaussian space (i.e. a Euclidean space with Gaussian measure) or a quotient thereof. This rigidity result is a synthetic analogue of the classical fact that equality in the Euclidean EPI forces the densities to be Gaussian with proportional covariance matrices. Recent work by Cavalletti–Mondino (2020) extended the EPI to essentially non‑branching CD(K,N) spaces, showing that the inequality remains valid without the non‑branching assumption if one uses the reduced curvature‑dimension condition CD*(K,N). The EPI thus serves as a bridge between optimal transport, functional inequalities, and the geometric structure of synthetic Ricci curvature bounds.

Stability of the Log‑Sobolev Inequality under Measured Gromov‑Hausdorff Convergence

The logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI) is a fundamental functional inequality that implies Gaussian concentration, hypercontractivity of the heat semigroup, and a spectral gap. On a smooth Riemannian manifold with Ric ≥ K > 0, the LSI holds with constant 1/(2K) (Gross 1975). In the synthetic setting, the LSI is known to hold on any CD(K,∞) space with K > 0 (Bakry–Émery 1985) and on CD(K,N) spaces with K > 0 after a dimensional correction (Ledoux 2004). The stability of the LSI under measured Gromov‑Hausdorff (mGH) convergence was established by Gigli, Mondino, and Savaré (2015) for the class of RCD(K,∞) spaces. They proved that if a sequence of RCD(K,∞) spaces (X_i,d_i,m_i) converges in the mGH sense to an RCD(K,∞) space (X,d,m), then the optimal LSI constants converge: the LSI constant of the limit is the liminf of the constants of the approximating spaces. The proof uses the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) formulation of the heat flow, which is stable under mGH convergence, and the fact that the LSI constant can be expressed as the inverse of the optimal constant in the hypercontractivity estimate for the heat semigroup. For finite‑dimensional RCD(K,N) spaces, the stability of the LSI with the sharp dimensional constant was obtained by Cavalletti–Milman (2017) using the equivalence between CD(K,N) and the reduced condition CD*(K,N) on essentially non‑branching spaces. The argument proceeds by showing that the entropy functional is Mosco‑convergent along the mGH convergence, which yields convergence of the associated gradient flows and of the Fisher information. As a corollary, the sharp logarithmic Sobolev constant is lower semicontinuous with respect to mGH convergence. This stability result is crucial for compactness arguments in geometric analysis: it allows one to pass to limits in variational problems involving the entropy, for instance in the study of isoperimetric profiles or in the construction of Ricci flow on singular spaces via the De Giorgi minimizing movement scheme. A notable application is the proof that the Gaussian isoperimetric profile is stable under mGH convergence of RCD(K,N) spaces with K>0, which was used by Mondino–Naber (2019) to obtain quantitative almost‑rigidity statements for spaces with nearly maximal LSI constant.

Worked Example: Stability of LSI Constant
Let (X_i,d_i,m_i) be a sequence of RCD(K,∞) spaces with K>0 converging in the measured Gromov‑Hausdorff sense to an RCD(K,∞) space (X,d,m). Denote by C_i the optimal constant in the logarithmic Sobolev inequality Entm_i(f²) ≤ 2C_i ∫ Γ(f) dm_i for all f in the domain of the Cheeger energy. Show that liminfi→∞ C_i ≥ C, where C is the optimal constant for the limit space.
The logarithmic Sobolev inequality is equivalent to the hypercontractivity of the heat semigroup (P_t^i) on L²(m_i): ‖P_t^i f‖Lq(t) ≤ ‖f‖L² with q(t)=1+e2t/C_i. By the stability of the heat flow under mGH convergence (Gigli–Mondino–Savaré 2015), the semigroups P_t^i converge strongly in L² to the limit semigroup P_t. Hypercontractivity is preserved under strong convergence of semigroups: if each P_t^i satisfies the hypercontractivity estimate with constant C_i, then the limit semigroup satisfies it with constant C = liminf C_i. The optimal LSI constant is the reciprocal of the optimal hypercontractivity constant, hence C ≤ liminf C_i. This yields the desired lower semicontinuity.

Cite the key papers (Gigli–Mondino–Savaré 2015 for RCD(K,∞); Cavalletti–Milman 2017 for RCD(K,N)). Do not confuse lower semicontinuity with continuity; the constant may jump up in the limit.

The Structure of Gradient Flows in the Wasserstein Space over RCD(K,N) Spaces

The theory of gradient flows in metric spaces, developed by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré (2005), provides the analytic backbone for the synthetic Ricci curvature program. On an RCD(K,N) space (X,d,m), the Wasserstein space P₂(X) of probability measures with finite second moment is a geodesic metric space. The Boltzmann entropy Ent(μ|m) = ∫ ρ log ρ dm (with μ=ρm) is a lower semicontinuous, geodesically convex functional on P₂(X) precisely when (X,d,m) satisfies CD(K,∞). For CD(K,N) with N<∞, the Rényi entropy S_N(μ) = −∫ ρ1−1/N dm is (K,N)-convex. The gradient flow of Ent in P₂(X) is the heat flow (P_t), which coincides with the L²‑gradient flow of the Cheeger energy Ch(f)=½∫|∇f|² dm. The Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) characterization states that a curve (μ_t) is the gradient flow of Ent iff for all ν∈P₂(X) and a.e. t>0, ½ d/dt W₂²(μ_t,ν) + Ent(μ_t|m) ≤ Ent(ν|m) − (K/2) W₂²(μ_t,ν). This EVI formulation is equivalent to the CD(K,∞) condition (Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré 2014). For finite N, the gradient flow of the Rényi entropy satisfies a modified EVI with a dimensional correction term. The structure of these gradient flows is remarkably rigid: the heat flow is a contraction semigroup in both L² and W₂, it preserves mass and positivity, and it satisfies the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ inequality. Moreover, the gradient flow of any λ‑convex functional on P₂(X) can be constructed via the De Giorgi minimizing movement scheme (JKO scheme), and the resulting curve is the unique EVI solution. This variational approach yields existence and uniqueness of solutions to nonlinear diffusion equations such as the porous medium equation ∂_t ρ = Δ(ρ^m) on RCD(K,N) spaces, where the driving functional is the Rényi entropy with a different power. The JKO scheme also provides a natural discretization for numerical approximation of the heat flow on synthetic spaces, and its convergence rate has been quantified by Erbar (2010) using the displacement convexity of the entropy. The gradient flow structure is stable under mGH convergence: if (X_i,d_i,m_i) → (X,d,m) in the mGH sense, then the corresponding heat flows converge in the sense of curves in P₂, and the limit curve is the heat flow on the limit space. This stability is a cornerstone of the compactness theory for RCD(K,N) spaces and underlies the construction of the synthetic Ricci flow as a gradient flow of the Perelman entropy functional on the space of metric measure spaces.

Upper Curvature Bounds and the Synthetic Alexandrov–CD Comparison

While the CD(K,N) condition encodes a lower bound on Ricci curvature, synthetic upper curvature bounds are captured by the theory of Alexandrov spaces (CAT(K) spaces). The interplay between these two notions has been a central theme since Sturm (2006) and Lott–Villani (2009) introduced the CD condition. A metric measure space that is both an Alexandrov space with curvature ≥ K (in the sense of Alexandrov) and satisfies CD(K,N) is called a RCD(K,N) space with two‑sided curvature bounds. Petrunin (2011) showed that on a finite‑dimensional Alexandrov space the CD(K,N) condition is equivalent to the lower Ricci bound in the sense of the Bishop–Gromov inequality, and that the space is an RCD(K,N) space. Conversely, if an RCD(K,N) space has an upper curvature bound in the sense of Alexandrov (i.e. it is a CAT(K) space), then it is a smooth Riemannian manifold with sectional curvature bounded above by K and Ricci curvature bounded below by K (by the splitting theorem and rigidity results of Cavalletti–Mondino 2018). The synthetic comparison between CD and CAT conditions is mediated by the notion of the “curvature‑dimension” condition for the heat semigroup: the Bochner inequality Γ₂(f) ≥ (1/N)(Δf)² + KΓ(f) is the analytic counterpart of the lower Ricci bound, while the upper bound on sectional curvature translates into a reverse Poincaré inequality for the heat semigroup (Li–Yau 1986). In the non‑smooth setting, the equivalence between the CD(K,N) condition and the measure contraction property MCP(K,N) on non‑branching spaces (Cavalletti–Milman 2017) provides a bridge to the Alexandrov world, because MCP(K,N) is known to hold on Alexandrov spaces with curvature ≥ K. Recent work by Kell–Mondino (2020) introduced a synthetic notion of “sectional curvature bounded above” for RCD(K,N) spaces via the behavior of the heat kernel: they proved that if the heat kernel satisfies a Gaussian upper bound with the same exponent as on a model space of constant curvature K, then the space is a CAT(K) space. This result yields a purely analytic characterization of upper curvature bounds in terms of the heat flow, complementing the CD(K,N) lower bound. The combined lower and upper bounds lead to strong rigidity: an RCD(K,N) space that is also CAT(K) is isometric to a spherical suspension or a quotient of the sphere, by the generalized Obata theorem (Ketterer 2015). These comparisons are essential for understanding the geometry of singular limits of Ricci‑bounded manifolds, such as the Gromov–Hausdorff limits of sequences of manifolds with Ric ≥ K and sectional curvature ≤ Λ.

The Heat Kernel on RCD(K,N) Spaces with Boundary: Dirichlet and Neumann Estimates

The analysis of the heat kernel on metric measure spaces with boundary requires a careful distinction between Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions. On a smooth Riemannian manifold with boundary, the Dirichlet heat kernel p_D(t,x,y) vanishes on the boundary, while the Neumann heat kernel p_N(t,x,y) has zero normal derivative. In the synthetic setting, the Dirichlet CD(K,N) condition (introduced by Mondino–Semola 2020) requires that the entropy is convex along Wasserstein geodesics that stay in the interior of the space, whereas the Neumann CD(K,N) condition allows geodesics to reflect at the boundary. Both conditions imply the existence of a heat semigroup with the corresponding boundary behavior. For an RCD(K,N) space with boundary satisfying the Dirichlet condition, Jiang and Li (2021) proved Gaussian upper and lower bounds for the Dirichlet heat kernel: there exist constants C,c>0 depending only on K,N such that for all t∈(0,1] and x,y in the interior, c t-N/2 exp(−d(x,y)²/(4t) − C t) ≤ p_D(t,x,y) ≤ C t-N/2 exp(−d(x,y)²/(4t) + C t). The proof uses the Dirichlet form associated with the Cheeger energy with zero boundary data, the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus with Dirichlet boundary conditions, and a boundary Harnack inequality established via the method of reflected Brownian motion on the doubling metric measure space. For the Neumann condition, the heat kernel satisfies similar Gaussian bounds but with a different constant reflecting the boundary reflection; the Neumann heat kernel is comparable to the Dirichlet heat kernel up to a multiplicative factor depending on the distance to the boundary. These estimates are stable under measured Gromov‑Hausdorff convergence of spaces with boundary (in the sense of Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of the doubles). As an application, the Dirichlet heat kernel bounds yield a sharp Faber–Krahn inequality for the first Dirichlet eigenvalue on RCD(K,N) domains, and the Neumann bounds give a sharp Poincaré inequality for functions with zero mean. The boundary theory also connects to the study of the heat flow with singular potentials (Feynman–Kac semigroups) where the potential V encodes the boundary condition via a large penalty near the boundary. Recent work by Ambrosio–Honda–Tewodrose (2023) extended these results to the case of non‑smooth boundaries with Minkowski content, showing that the heat kernel estimates hold under a uniform exterior ball condition. This synthetic boundary theory is a key ingredient for the analysis of the Ricci flow on spaces with boundary and for the study of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map on singular spaces.

  • Confusing the Dirichlet CD condition with the Neumann CD condition; the Dirichlet condition requires geodesics to stay in the interior, while the Neumann condition allows reflection at the boundary.
  • Assuming the entropy power inequality holds with the same constant on all CD(K,N) spaces; the constant depends on the dimension parameter N and the curvature lower bound K.
  • Overlooking that stability of the logarithmic Sobolev constant gives only lower semicontinuity, not continuity; the constant may increase in the limit.
  • Thinking that the gradient flow of the Rényi entropy coincides with the heat flow for all N; it coincides only after a suitable time rescaling when N<∞.
  • Believing that an RCD(K,N) space with an upper Alexandrov bound is automatically a smooth manifold; rigidity requires additional non‑collapsing or maximal diameter assumptions.
  • Neglecting the role of the boundary Harnack inequality in proving Gaussian bounds for the Dirichlet heat kernel; the inequality fails without a uniform exterior ball condition.
  • Stating that the measure contraction property MCP(K,N) is equivalent to CD(K,N) on all metric measure spaces; the equivalence holds only on essentially non‑branching spaces.

The advanced topics above illustrate how synthetic Ricci curvature bounds unify optimal transport, functional inequalities, and geometric analysis on non‑smooth spaces.

The Heat Flow on RCD(K,N) Spaces with Singular Measures

When the reference measure of an RCD(K,N) space is allowed to have a singular part with respect to the Hausdorff measure, the heat flow must be defined via the Cheeger energy on the whole space, not only on the regular set. The Cheeger energy remains a convex, lower semicontinuous functional on L², and its gradient flow in the L²‑Wasserstein sense coincides with the heat semigroup generated by the (possibly non‑local) Dirichlet form associated with the singular measure. Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré showed that the heat flow preserves mass and satisfies the same contraction estimates in Wasserstein distance as in the non‑singular case, provided the singular part is absolutely continuous with respect to the reference measure with a density belonging to L¹∩L∞. The Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus extends to this setting by interpreting the carré du champ operator on the domain of the Cheeger energy; the Bochner inequality holds in the distributional sense and yields the same curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N). A key technical point is the identification of the minimal relaxed slope with the pointwise slope quasi‑everywhere, which requires a fine analysis of the capacity associated with the singular measure. As a consequence, the Gaussian heat kernel bounds of Jiang–Li remain valid with constants depending only on K and N, and the logarithmic Sobolev inequality holds with the same constant as in the regular case. This theory is essential for the study of Ricci flow on spaces with singular measures, where the evolving measure may develop singularities in finite time.

The Synthetic Ricci Curvature for Metric Measure Spaces with Both Lower and Upper Curvature Bounds

Combining a lower Ricci bound Ric ≥ K with an upper sectional curvature bound sec ≤ Λ leads to a synthetic condition that is stronger than CD(K,N) alone. On a smooth Riemannian manifold, the pair (K,Λ) implies a two‑sided bound on the Riemann curvature tensor, which in turn controls the behavior of Jacobi fields and the convexity of the distance function. In the synthetic framework, one can impose an upper curvature bound in the sense of Alexandrov (CAT(Λ)) together with the CD(K,N) condition. The resulting class of spaces, sometimes called RCD(K,N)∩CAT(Λ), enjoys a rigid structure: by the generalized Obata theorem (Ketterer 2015) any such space that attains the maximal diameter is isometric to a spherical suspension or a quotient of the sphere. Moreover, the Bishop–Gromov volume comparison holds with the model space of constant curvature Λ, while the Lichnerowicz spectral gap estimate holds with the lower bound K. The interplay between the two bounds yields sharp eigenvalue estimates for the Laplacian and a precise description of the tangent cones, which are metric cones over spherical buildings. Stability under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence is preserved: a sequence of RCD(K,N) spaces with uniform CAT(Λ) bounds converges to a limit that again satisfies both conditions. This combined theory is crucial for understanding singular limits of manifolds with both Ricci and sectional curvature bounds, such as collapsing sequences with bounded sectional curvature.

Worked Example: Identifying the Limit Space
Consider a sequence of n‑dimensional Riemannian manifolds (M_i,g_i) with Ricg_i ≥ (n-1)K and secg_i ≤ Λ, where K>0 and Λ>K. Assume the volumes are uniformly bounded below and the diameters converge to the maximal possible value π/√K. Show that the measured Gromov–Hausdorff limit is isometric to the spherical suspension over an (n-1)‑dimensional Alexandrov space with curvature ≥ 1.
By the Bishop–Gromov inequality the volume non‑collapsing assumption implies the limit is an RCD(K,N) space with N=n. The upper sectional curvature bound passes to the limit in the sense of CAT(Λ). Since the diameter attains the maximal value π/√K, the generalized Obata theorem for RCD(K,N)∩CAT(Λ) spaces (Ketterer 2015) forces the limit to be a spherical suspension over a space of curvature ≥ 1. The base of the suspension inherits the CAT(1) condition from the CAT(Λ) bound and the lower Ricci bound translates into a lower curvature bound on the base. Hence the limit is exactly the claimed spherical suspension.
Exam Strategy
When a problem asks for the structure of a limit space under both lower Ricci and upper sectional bounds, first recall the relevant rigidity theorem (Obata‑type). Then verify that the limit satisfies both synthetic conditions: CD(K,N) from the lower Ricci bound and CAT(Λ) from the upper sectional bound. The maximal diameter condition triggers the spherical suspension conclusion. Always check whether non‑collapsing is assumed; without it the limit could be a lower‑dimensional space.

The Entropy Dissipation Formula and the Convergence of Entropy along the Heat Flow on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The entropy functional Ent(μ|m) = ∫ ρ log ρ dm for μ = ρ m is displacement convex on the Wasserstein space over an RCD(K,N) space. Its gradient flow in the Wasserstein sense is precisely the heat flow (ρ_t) generated by the Cheeger energy. The entropy dissipation formula states that for a.e. t>0, d/dt Ent(ρ_t|m) = -∫ Γ(log ρ_t) ρ_t dm, where Γ is the carré du champ operator. On RCD(K,N) spaces the Bakry–Émery calculus yields the refined inequality d/dt Ent(ρ_t|m) ≤ -K Ent(ρ_t|m) - (2/N) (∫ √Γ(log ρ_t) ρ_t dm)², which integrates to the exponential decay Ent(ρ_t|m) ≤ e-Kt Ent(ρ0|m) when K>0. This decay is sharp and characterizes the spectral gap. Moreover, the entropy is convex along the heat flow: the second derivative satisfies d²/dt² Ent(ρ_t|m) ≥ 2∫ Γ₂(log ρ_t) ρ_t dm ≥ 2K ∫ Γ(log ρ_t) ρ_t dm + (4/N) (∫ Γ(log ρ_t) ρ_t dm)². These identities are the synthetic counterparts of the classical Bochner formula and are proved by approximating the heat flow with the De Giorgi minimizing movement scheme and passing to the limit using the stability of the Γ₂ calculus under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. The entropy dissipation formula is a key tool for proving functional inequalities such as the logarithmic Sobolev inequality and for establishing the long‑time asymptotics of the heat flow.

The p‑Wasserstein Space and its Curvature‑Dimension Conditions

For p∈(1,∞) the p‑Wasserstein space (P_p(X),W_p) over an RCD(K,N) space (X,d,m) inherits a synthetic curvature‑dimension condition CD_p(K,N) that is equivalent to the quadratic case p=2 when the space is infinitesimally Hilbertian. The definition replaces the quadratic transport cost ½d² by the p‑cost d^p/p and requires geodesic convexity of the Rényi entropy functional U_N(μ) = -∫ ρ1-1/N dm along W_p‑geodesics. Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré proved that on an RCD(K,N) space the heat flow is the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in the W2 sense, while for p≠2 the gradient flow of the same energy in W_p yields a nonlinear diffusion equation ∂_t ρ = div(|∇ρ|p-2∇ρ). The CD_p(K,N) condition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence and tensorizes: the product of two CD_p(K,N) spaces satisfies CD_p(K,N₁+N₂). A remarkable result by Erbar, Kuwada and Sturm shows that CD_p(K,∞) is equivalent to the exponential contraction of the W_p distance along the heat flow: W_p(μ_t,ν_t) ≤ e-Kt W_p(μ0,ν0). For finite N the equivalence holds under an additional non‑branching assumption. This theory extends the Lott–Sturm–Villani framework to a whole family of transport costs and provides a unified approach to nonlinear diffusion equations on non‑smooth spaces.

The Bakry–Émery Calculus on RCD(K,N) Spaces with Non‑Symmetric Dirichlet Forms

When the Dirichlet form associated with the Cheeger energy is not symmetric, the carré du champ operator Γ(f,g) is no longer symmetric in its arguments, and the Γ₂ calculus must be adapted. The non‑symmetric setting arises naturally for metric measure spaces equipped with a non‑reversible Markov process, such as those coming from a Finsler metric or a magnetic field. The synthetic condition CD(K,N) can be formulated using the asymmetric carré du champ Γ⁺(f) = limt→0 (P_t f² - (P_t f)²)/(2t), where P_t is the non‑symmetric heat semigroup. The Bochner inequality then reads Γ₂⁺(f) ≥ K Γ⁺(f) + (1/N)(L f)², where L is the generator of the semigroup. This inequality implies the same contraction estimates in the Wasserstein distance as in the symmetric case, provided the reference measure is invariant. The theory of tangent modules and the differential structure developed by Gigli extends to the non‑symmetric case by considering the cotangent module as a Banach module rather than a Hilbert module. The resulting synthetic Ricci curvature bounds have been applied to the study of the heat kernel on sub‑Riemannian manifolds (e.g., the Heisenberg group) where the natural Dirichlet form is non‑symmetric and the CD(K,N) condition fails, but a weaker curvature‑dimension condition holds. This line of research connects synthetic Ricci curvature with hypoelliptic operators and geometric control theory.

  • Confusing the symmetric and non‑symmetric carré du champ operators; the non‑symmetric Γ⁺ does not satisfy Γ⁺(f,g)=Γ⁺(g,f) and the Bochner inequality must be stated with the appropriate generator.
  • Assuming the entropy dissipation formula holds with the same constant for all p‑Wasserstein gradients; the constant depends on p and the dimension parameter N.
  • Overlooking that the CD_p(K,N) condition tensorizes only when the same p is used for both factors; mixing different p values breaks the product property.
  • Thinking that the heat flow on a space with singular measures is always the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in the quadratic Wasserstein space; for singular measures the gradient flow may live in a different transport metric.
  • Neglecting the role of the non‑collapsing assumption in the rigidity theorem for spaces with both lower Ricci and upper sectional bounds; without it the limit may be a lower‑dimensional spherical suspension.
  • Believing that the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus on non‑symmetric spaces yields a Hilbertian tangent module; the cotangent module is generally a Banach module and the Sobolev‑to‑Lipschitz property may fail.
  • Assuming the generalized Obata theorem applies to any RCD(K,N) space with an upper curvature bound; the theorem requires the space to be both RCD(K,N) and CAT(Λ) with the same dimension parameter N.

The Neumann CD(K,N) Condition and Heat Flow with Reflecting Boundary

The classical CD(K,N) condition is formulated for metric measure spaces without boundary. When a boundary is present, one must prescribe boundary behaviour for the heat flow. The Neumann CD(K,N) condition, introduced by Ketterer and later refined by Mondino and others, requires that the heat semigroup be the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in the quadratic Wasserstein space over the space of probability measures with the additional constraint that the flow preserves the total mass and reflects at the boundary. Concretely, a metric measure space (X,d,m) with a closed subset ∂X is said to satisfy the Neumann CD(K,N) condition if for every pair of probability measures μ₀,μ₁ supported in X there exists a W₂‑geodesic (μ_t) such that the Rényi entropy U_N(μ_t)=−∫ρ1−1/Ndm is K‑convex along the geodesic and the geodesic stays in the set of measures that do not charge the boundary. The associated heat flow ∂_t u = Δu with Neumann boundary condition ∂_ν u = 0 on ∂X is then the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy. This formulation yields the Bochner inequality with a boundary term that vanishes for test functions with vanishing normal derivative. As a consequence, the logarithmic Sobolev inequality, the Talagrand inequality and the Gaussian heat kernel estimates hold with the same constants as in the boundary‑less case, provided the boundary is convex in the sense of the distance function. The Neumann condition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of spaces with boundary, and it tensorizes: the product of two Neumann CD(K,N) spaces satisfies Neumann CD(K,N₁+N₂).

The CD(K,N) Condition on Warped Product Spaces and the Curvature‑Dimension Tensorization Formula

Warped products provide a flexible class of spaces where the curvature‑dimension condition can be computed explicitly. Let (B,d_B,m_B) and (F,d_F,m_F) be metric measure spaces satisfying CD(K_B,N_B) and CD(K_F,N_F) respectively, and let f:B→(0,∞) be a positive warping function. The warped product X = B ×_f F carries the metric d((b₁,f₁),(b₂,f₂))² = d_B(b₁,b₂)² + f(b₁)² d_F(f₁,f₂)² and the measure m = m_B ⊗ fN_F m_F. A theorem of Ketterer (2015) shows that X satisfies CD(K,N) with N = N_B + N_F provided the warping function satisfies the differential inequality Γ₂^B(log f) ≥ (K − K_B) Γ^B(log f) + (1/N_F)(Δ_B log f)². In the smooth Riemannian setting this reduces to the well‑known condition that the base has Ricci curvature ≥ K_B, the fiber has Ricci curvature ≥ K_F, and the Hessian of log f is bounded below by (K−K_B)g_B. The tensorization formula for the curvature‑dimension condition on products is recovered when f≡1, giving CD(K_B+N_F K_F, N_B+N_F). For non‑constant warping functions the effective curvature bound K depends on the interplay between the base curvature, the fiber curvature and the convexity of log f. This result is sharp: if the inequality on log f fails, one can construct a warped product that violates CD(K,N) for any K. The warped product theory is essential for the analysis of spherical suspensions, cones and model spaces that appear as tangent cones of RCD(K,N) spaces.

Technique
Given a warped product X = B ×_f F where B satisfies CD(0,2) and F satisfies CD(1,1), and the warping function f(b)=eφ(b) with φ a smooth function on B. Derive the condition on φ that guarantees X satisfies CD(K,3) for some K∈ℝ.
The warped product dimension is N = N_B+N_F = 3. The curvature bound K is determined by the inequality Γ₂^B(φ) ≥ (K−K_B)Γ^B(φ) + (1/N_F)(Δ_B φ)². Here K_B=0, N_F=1, so the condition becomes Γ₂^B(φ) ≥ K Γ^B(φ) + (Δ_B φ)². Since B satisfies CD(0,2), the Bochner inequality on B gives Γ₂^B(φ) ≥ (1/2)(Δ_B φ)². Therefore a sufficient condition is (1/2)(Δ_B φ)² ≥ K Γ^B(φ) + (Δ_B φ)², i.e. −(1/2)(Δ_B φ)² ≥ K Γ^B(φ). This holds for any K ≤ 0 provided φ is chosen so that Γ^B(φ) > 0. In particular, taking φ with Δ_B φ = 0 yields K=0. Hence X satisfies CD(0,3) whenever φ is harmonic on B.
Exam‑day strategy
When a warped product appears in a problem, first identify the base and fiber curvature‑dimension parameters. Then write down the differential inequality for the warping function; it is the only place where the effective curvature K can differ from the naive sum. Remember that the inequality involves the Γ₂ calculus on the base, so use the known Bochner inequality for the base to simplify.

The CD(K,N) Condition for Metric Measure Spaces with Rough Metrics and Stability under Gromov‑Hausdorff Convergence

In many applications the underlying metric is not a length metric but a rough metric, i.e. a metric that is bi‑Lipschitz equivalent to a length metric. The CD(K,N) condition can be extended to such spaces by requiring that the entropy convexity holds along geodesics of the length metric associated to the rough metric. A fundamental result of Gigli, Mondino and Savaré shows that if a sequence of metric measure spaces (X_i,d_i,m_i) satisfies CD(K,N) uniformly and converges in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense to a limit space (X,d,m), then the limit also satisfies CD(K,N) provided the convergence is non‑collapsing (i.e. the Hausdorff dimension of the limit equals N). The proof uses the stability of the heat flow and the Γ₂ calculus under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. For rough metrics, one first replaces each d_i by the induced length metric d_i^ℓ; the bi‑Lipschitz equivalence guarantees that the CD(K,N) condition for d_i^ℓ is equivalent to a CD(K',N) condition for d_i with a possibly different curvature bound K'. The stability theorem then applies to the length metrics, and the limit inherits a CD(K,N) condition with the same K. This framework is crucial for the analysis of limits of manifolds with lower Ricci bounds under only C⁰ convergence of the metrics, and it underpins the compactness theory for RCD(K,N) spaces.

The p‑Wasserstein Gradient Flow of the Rényi Entropy and the Porous Medium Equation on RCD(K,N) Spaces

For p∈(1,∞) the p‑Wasserstein space (P_p(X),W_p) over an RCD(K,N) space (X,d,m) carries a natural gradient flow structure. The Rényi entropy functional U_N(μ)=−∫ρ1−1/Ndm is geodesically convex in (P_p(X),W_p) precisely when the underlying space satisfies the CD_p(K,N) condition, which is equivalent to CD(K,N) for p=2 and to a family of curvature‑dimension conditions for p≠2. The gradient flow of U_N in the p‑Wasserstein metric yields the nonlinear diffusion equation ∂_t ρ = div(|∇ρ|p−2∇ρ), known as the porous medium equation when p>2 and the fast diffusion equation when p<2. Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré proved that on an RCD(K,N) space the heat flow (p=2) is the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in W₂. For p≠2, the same Cheeger energy generates a gradient flow in W_p that coincides with the weak solution of the porous medium equation constructed via the De Giorgi minimizing movement scheme. The CD_p(K,N) condition implies exponential contraction of the W_p distance along the flow: W_p(μ_t,ν_t) ≤ e−Kt W_p(μ0,ν0). This contraction property is equivalent to the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) formulation in the p‑Wasserstein space. Moreover, the CD_p(K,N) condition tensorizes: the product of two CD_p(K,N) spaces satisfies CD_p(K,N₁+N₂). The theory extends the Lott–Sturm–Villani framework to a whole family of transport costs and provides a unified approach to nonlinear diffusion equations on non‑smooth spaces.

The Stability of the Poincaré Inequality under Measured Gromov‑Hausdorff Convergence on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The Poincaré inequality is a fundamental functional inequality that controls the variance of a function by the integral of its gradient. On an RCD(K,N) space with K>0, the sharp Poincaré constant is given by the Lichnerowicz estimate λ₁ ≥ N K/(N−1). For K≤0 the optimal constant depends on the diameter or on the isoperimetric profile. A deep result of Honda (2019) and later refined by Cavaletti and Mondino shows that the Poincaré constant is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of non‑collapsing RCD(K,N) spaces. More precisely, if (X_i,d_i,m_i) are RCD(K,N) spaces converging to (X,d,m) in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense and the convergence is non‑collapsing, then the first non‑zero eigenvalue λ₁(X_i) converges to λ₁(X). The proof combines the stability of the heat kernel, the Mosco convergence of the Cheeger energies, and the compactness of the embedding of the Sobolev space W1,2 into L². As a corollary, the sharp Lichnerowicz bound passes to the limit, and any limit space that attains the equality λ₁ = N K/(N−1) must be a spherical suspension, yielding a rigidity theorem. This stability is essential for the spectral convergence of the Laplacian on RCD(K,N) spaces and for the analysis of the spectrum of tangent cones.

Multi‑Marginal Optimal Transport and the CD(K,N) Condition

The classical Lott–Sturm–Villani theory formulates lower Ricci curvature bounds through the displacement convexity of the Boltzmann entropy along geodesics in the quadratic Wasserstein space. A natural extension replaces the two‑marginal transport problem by a multi‑marginal one, where a cost function c(x₁,…,x_m) couples m probability measures. On a smooth Riemannian manifold with Ricci curvature bounded below by K, the multi‑marginal cost c(x₁,…,x_m)=∑i<j</sub> d²(x_i,x_j) yields a displacement convexity inequality for the sum of entropies that is equivalent to the CD(K,N) condition when m=2. For m≥3 the same convexity property characterises a stronger curvature‑dimension condition, often denoted CD_m(K,N), which implies CD(K,N) but is not equivalent in general. Recent work by Pass, Kim, and others shows that on essentially non‑branching metric measure spaces the multi‑marginal convexity for the symmetric quadratic cost is equivalent to the usual CD(K,N) condition, while on branching spaces it provides a strictly finer hierarchy. The proof uses the cyclical monotonicity of optimal multi‑plans and the fact that any optimal two‑plan can be embedded into an optimal multi‑plan by adding independent copies of the reference measure. This embedding transfers the convexity inequality from the multi‑marginal setting back to the two‑marginal one, establishing the equivalence under the non‑branching assumption. In the branching case, counterexamples based on metric trees demonstrate that CD(K,N) does not control the behaviour of three‑point configurations, so the multi‑marginal condition captures additional geometric information such as the absence of certain branching patterns. The theory also connects with the Martingale optimal transport formulation of CD(K,N): a multi‑marginal martingale transport plan corresponds to a discrete‑time martingale whose marginals follow the heat flow, and the convexity of the entropy along such martingales reproduces the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ inequality. These links make multi‑marginal transport a powerful tool for studying stability, tensorization, and the structure of singular sets on RCD(K,N) spaces.

From the analytic side, the multi‑marginal problem leads to a system of coupled Hamilton–Jacobi equations for the Kantorovich potentials. The displacement convexity of the entropy translates into a matrix‑valued Bochner inequality involving the Hessian of the potentials. On RCD(K,N) spaces the second‑order calculus developed by Gigli provides a rigorous meaning to these Hessians, and the multi‑marginal convexity can be expressed as a pointwise inequality for the Γ₂ operator applied to vector‑valued functions. This formulation is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, which allows one to pass multi‑marginal curvature bounds to limits of non‑collapsing sequences. Moreover, the tensorization property extends: the product of two spaces satisfying CD_m(K,N) again satisfies CD_m(K,N₁+N₂) with the same number of marginals m. This is proved by constructing optimal multi‑plans on the product from optimal multi‑plans on the factors, using the independence of the reference measures. The multi‑marginal perspective also clarifies the role of the reference measure in the definition of CD(K,N): the entropy functional is the relative entropy with respect to the reference measure, and the multi‑marginal cost naturally couples several copies of that reference measure, making the dependence on the reference measure explicit.

Heat Flow on Time‑Dependent Metric Measure Spaces and Synthetic Ricci Flow

In the smooth setting, the Ricci flow ∂_t g = −2 Ric(g) evolves the metric while preserving the volume measure up to a scalar factor. The synthetic counterpart, introduced by Sturm and later refined by Gigli–Mantegazza, considers a one‑parameter family of metric measure spaces (X,d_t,m_t) satisfying a dynamic CD(K,N) condition: for each t the space (X,d_t,m_t) is an RCD(K,N) space, and the map t↦d_t is absolutely continuous with a derivative controlled by the heat flow. The heat flow on such a time‑dependent space is defined as the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy Ch_t(f)=½∫|∇_t f|² dm_t in the Hilbert space L²(X,m_t). Because the metric and the measure change with time, the Cheeger energy itself becomes time‑dependent, and the gradient flow must be understood in the sense of evolving Hilbert spaces. The De Giorgi minimizing movement scheme adapts by using the time‑dependent Wasserstein distance W2,t at each step, leading to a curve of probability measures (μ_t) that satisfies the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) with respect to the dynamic entropy functional Entm_t(μ)=∫ρ log ρ dm_t. The EVI formulation is equivalent to the dynamic CD(K,∞) condition and provides a robust notion of synthetic Ricci flow that does not require a smooth differential structure.

A key result is the stability of this dynamic CD condition under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of the whole family. If a sequence of time‑dependent RCD(K,N) spaces converges in a suitable space‑time sense, the limit family again satisfies the dynamic CD(K,N) condition. The proof combines the stability of the static CD condition with the compactness of the heat flow in the space‑time Wasserstein metric. Applications include the construction of a synthetic Ricci flow starting from any RCD(K,N) space, the proof of short‑time existence and uniqueness, and the derivation of Perelman‑type entropy monotonicity formulas. The dynamic theory also yields a synthetic version of Hamilton’s Harnack inequality for the heat kernel on evolving RCD(K,N) spaces, which in turn implies Gaussian upper bounds that are uniform in time. These estimates are crucial for the analysis of singularities in the synthetic Ricci flow and for the study of the long‑time behaviour of the flow on non‑compact spaces.

Worked Example: Multi‑Marginal Convexity Implies CD(K,N)
Let (X,d,m) be an essentially non‑branching metric measure space. Assume that for every three probability measures μ₀,μ₁,μ₂ with finite entropy the multi‑marginal entropy functional Ent_m(μ₀)+Ent_m(μ₁)+Ent_m(μ₂) is convex along the geodesic in the three‑marginal quadratic Wasserstein space. Show that (X,d,m) satisfies the CD(K,N) condition for some K∈ℝ, N∈[1,∞].
Take any two measures μ₀,μ₁ with finite entropy. Choose a third measure μ₂=m (the reference measure). The three‑marginal geodesic connecting (μ₀,μ₁,m) projects onto the two‑marginal geodesic connecting μ₀ and μ₁ because the optimal three‑plan with marginals (μ₀,μ₁,m) has the property that its first two marginals form an optimal two‑plan (by the non‑branching assumption). Convexity of the sum of entropies along the three‑marginal geodesic then reduces to convexity of Ent_m(μ_t) along the two‑marginal geodesic, since Ent_m(m)=0 is constant. The convexity inequality with a quadratic modulation term yields exactly the CD(K,N) condition. The constants K,N are the same as those appearing in the multi‑marginal convexity assumption.

Stability of CD(K,N) under Measured Gromov–Hausdorff Convergence in p‑Wasserstein Metrics for p≠2

The classical stability theorem for the CD(K,N) condition states that if a sequence of RCD(K,N) spaces converges in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense and the convergence is non‑collapsing, then the limit space is again an RCD(K,N) space. The proof uses the quadratic Wasserstein distance W₂ because the CD(K,N) condition is defined via displacement convexity of the entropy in (P₂(X),W₂). For p≠2 the p‑Wasserstein space (P_p(X),W_p) carries a different geodesic structure, and the entropy is not generally displacement convex in that geometry. However, one can define a family of curvature‑dimension conditions CD_p(K,N) by requiring convexity of the Rényi entropy U_N(μ)=−∫ρ1−1/N dm along W_p‑geodesics. It is known that CD_p(K,N) is equivalent to CD(K,N) when p=2, and for p≠2 it yields a strictly weaker condition that nevertheless controls the behaviour of the p‑heat flow ∂_t ρ = div(|∇ρ|p−2∇ρ). Recent work by Gigli, Mondino, and Savaré establishes a stability theorem for CD_p(K,N) under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence: if (X_i,d_i,m_i) are CD_p(K,N) spaces converging non‑collapsing to (X,d,m), then the limit satisfies CD_p(K,N). The proof adapts the W₂ argument by using the fact that on RCD(K,N) spaces the p‑Wasserstein distance is bi‑Lipschitz equivalent to the W₂ distance with constants depending only on p and the diameter. This equivalence allows one to transfer convergence of measures and the lower semicontinuity of the entropy from the W₂ setting to the W_p setting. The tensorization property also holds: the product of two CD_p(K,N) spaces satisfies CD_p(K,N₁+N₂). As a consequence, the p‑heat flow on the limit space is the limit of the p‑heat flows on the approximating spaces, and the associated nonlinear semigroup converges in the Mosco sense. This stability result is essential for the analysis of p‑harmonic functions and the p‑Laplacian on limit spaces, and it underpins the compactness theory for the family of CD_p(K,N) spaces.

Collapsing Sequences of RCD(K,N) Spaces and the Limit Space Dimension

When a sequence of RCD(K,N) spaces converges in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense but the convergence is collapsing (i.e. the Hausdorff dimension of the limit is strictly smaller than N), the limit space need not satisfy a CD(K,N) condition with the same dimension parameter. Instead, the limit satisfies a CD(K,N') condition for some N'≤N, where N' is the essential dimension of the limit space. The essential dimension is defined as the maximal integer k such that the k‑dimensional Hausdorff measure of the regular set is positive. A deep theorem of Honda (2019) shows that for a non‑collapsing sequence the essential dimension equals N and the limit is RCD(K,N). In the collapsing case, the limit is an RCD(K,N') space with N'<N, and the curvature bound K is preserved. The proof uses the stability of the heat kernel and the fact that the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus passes to the limit in the sense of Mosco convergence of the Cheeger energies. However, the dimension parameter drops because the reference measures m_i concentrate on lower‑dimensional subsets. Quantitatively, the Bishop–Gromov inequality yields a uniform bound on the volume of balls, and the collapsing forces the volume to concentrate near a lower‑dimensional stratum. The limit space inherits a stratification by the dimension of tangent cones, and the top stratum has dimension N'. The almost splitting theorem and the structure theory of RCD(K,N) spaces then imply that the limit is rectifiable of dimension N' and satisfies the CD(K,N') condition. This phenomenon is illustrated by sequences of spherical suspensions S^k × S<sup>N−k with shrinking fibres, which converge to S^k with the same curvature bound but lower dimension. Understanding collapsing is crucial for the compactness theory of RCD(K,N) spaces, because the natural compactification of the class includes spaces of varying dimension.

The Schrödinger Problem on RCD(K,N) Spaces: Entropic Cost and Displacement Convexity

The dynamic Schrödinger problem asks for the most likely evolution of a diffusion process between two prescribed marginals, where the prior dynamics is given by the heat flow on the underlying space. On a smooth Riemannian manifold with Ricci curvature bounded below by K, the entropic cost — the minimal relative entropy of a path measure with respect to the law of the Brownian motion — is displacement convex along the geodesics of the entropic Wasserstein space. Léonard proved that this displacement convexity is equivalent to the CD(K,∞) condition. On RCD(K,N) spaces the same equivalence holds: the entropic cost functional is geodesically convex in the space of probability measures equipped with the entropic Wasserstein distance if and only if the space satisfies CD(K,N). The proof uses the identification of the heat flow with the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy in the quadratic Wasserstein space, and the fact that the Schrödinger problem can be formulated as a static optimal transport problem with a cost given by the logarithmic heat kernel. The displacement convexity of the entropic cost then translates into the convexity of the Rényi entropy along W₂‑geodesics, which is the definition of CD(K,N). This equivalence provides a probabilistic characterisation of synthetic Ricci curvature bounds that does not refer to the geometry of the Wasserstein space directly, but only to the heat kernel and the relative entropy. It also yields a new proof of the stability of CD(K,N) under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, because the heat kernel converges uniformly on compact sets and the entropic cost is lower semicontinuous with respect to the convergence of the underlying spaces. Moreover, the Schrödinger formulation extends naturally to the p‑Wasserstein setting and to the dynamic CD_p(K,N) conditions, linking the entropic cost for the p‑heat flow to the convexity of the Rényi entropy in (P_p(X),W_p).

The CD(K,N) Condition on Metric Measure Spaces with Rough Metrics: Stability under Uniform Convergence

The classical CD(K,N) theory assumes a complete, separable metric measure space (X,d,m) with a geodesic distance d. In many applications the metric is only known to be a uniform limit of smoother metrics, for instance when approximating a singular space by a sequence of Riemannian manifolds with bounded sectional curvature. Gigli, Mondino and Savaré proved that if a sequence of metric measure spaces (X,d_i,m) satisfies CD(K,N) uniformly and the distances d_i converge uniformly to a limit distance d, then the limit space (X,d,m) also satisfies CD(K,N). The proof uses the fact that the entropy functional Ent_m(μ)=∫ρ log ρ dm is lower semicontinuous with respect to narrow convergence of measures, and that the Wasserstein distance W2 depends continuously on the underlying metric when the convergence is uniform. A key technical point is that the geodesics in (X,d_i) converge uniformly to geodesics in (X,d) because uniform convergence of metrics implies Gromov–Hausdorff convergence with a controlled distortion of lengths. Consequently, the displacement convexity of the Rényi entropy along W2‑geodesics passes to the limit. This stability result is essential for the approximation of Alexandrov spaces, for the construction of synthetic Ricci flows on non‑smooth spaces, and for the analysis of limits of manifolds with only C0 control on the metric tensor. It also shows that the CD(K,N) condition is a closed condition in the topology of uniform metric convergence, which is strictly stronger than measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence.

The Neumann CD(K,N) Condition and the Heat Flow with Reflecting Boundary Data

When a metric measure space (X,d,m) has a non‑empty boundary ∂X in the sense of a closed subset with a well‑defined inward normal, one can impose a Neumann (reflecting) boundary condition on the heat flow. The Neumann CD(K,N) condition requires that the relative entropy Ent_m(μ|ν) is convex along Wasserstein geodesics that stay inside X and reflect at ∂X according to the law of geometric optics. Equivalently, the heat semigroup (P_t) generated by the Dirichlet form with Neumann boundary data satisfies the Bakry–Émery Γ2 inequality Γ2(f) ≥ K Γ(f) + (1/N)(Δf)2 for all f in the domain of the Neumann Laplacian. Mondino and Naber showed that this condition is equivalent to the displacement convexity of the Rényi entropy in the Wasserstein space over X with the additional constraint that optimal transport plans do not charge the boundary. The Neumann heat flow can be constructed via the De Giorgi minimizing movement scheme for the entropy functional restricted to the convex set of probability measures supported in X. The resulting gradient flow coincides with the semigroup generated by the Neumann Laplacian, and the Γ2 calculus yields Gaussian heat kernel estimates with Neumann boundary conditions. This framework extends the classical Neumann Laplacian on smooth manifolds with boundary to non‑smooth RCD(K,N) spaces, allowing the study of spectral gaps, isoperimetric inequalities and the behaviour of harmonic functions up to the boundary.

Worked Example: Verifying Neumann CD(K,N) on a Convex Domain
Let X be a compact convex domain in ℝ^n with the Euclidean distance and Lebesgue measure. Show that (X,d,m) satisfies the Neumann CD(0,n) condition.
On a convex domain the Euclidean distance is geodesically convex and the Lebesgue measure is the reference measure. The Neumann heat flow is the standard heat semigroup with reflecting boundary condition, which is known to preserve mass and satisfy the Bakry–Émery Γ2 inequality with K=0 and N=n because the underlying manifold is flat. The Γ2 operator reduces to Γ2(f)=|Hess f|2 ≥ 0 = K Γ(f) + (1/n)(Δf)2, where the last term is non‑negative by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Displacement convexity of the Rényi entropy along reflecting Wasserstein geodesics follows from the fact that optimal transport maps between measures supported in a convex set stay inside the set and the Euclidean entropy is convex along linear interpolation. Hence the Neumann CD(0,n) condition holds.

The Dynamic CD(K,N) Condition for Time‑Dependent Metric Measure Spaces

In the study of synthetic Ricci flow one considers a family of metric measure spaces (X,d_t,m_t)t∈[0,T] where the metric and the reference measure evolve in time. The dynamic CD(K,N) condition, introduced by Sturm and later refined by Haslhofer and Naber, requires that for each fixed t the space (X,d_t,m_t) satisfies the static CD(K,N) condition, and additionally the map t↦Entm_t(μ_t) is convex along solutions of the continuity equation ∂_t μ_t + ∇·(v_t μ_t)=0 where the velocity field v_t is the gradient of the Kantorovich potential for the W2‑geodesic at time t. Equivalently, the dynamic condition can be expressed as an Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) for the entropy functional in the time‑dependent Wasserstein space: for any two curves of measures (μ_t),(ν_t) one has ½ d/dt W2,t2(μ_t,ν_t) + (K/2) W2,t2(μ_t,ν_t) ≤ Entm_t(ν_t) - Entm_t(μ_t). This formulation captures the interplay between the diffusion of the heat flow and the deformation of the metric. It implies that the heat kernel evolves by a conjugate heat equation and that the Perelman entropy functionals are monotone along the flow. The dynamic CD(K,N) condition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence of the time‑dependent spaces, which is the cornerstone of the compactness theory for synthetic Ricci flows.

The CDp(K,N) Condition for Non‑Quadratic Costs and the p‑Wasserstein Geometry

The classical CD(K,N) condition is built on the quadratic Wasserstein distance W2. For p≠2 one can define a curvature‑dimension condition CD_p(K,N) by requiring that the Rényi entropy S_N(μ)=∫ρ1-1/N dm is geodesically convex in the p‑Wasserstein space (P_p(X),W_p). The p‑Cheeger energy Ch_p(f)=∫|∇f|^p dm replaces the Dirichlet energy, and the associated p‑Laplacian Δ_p generates a nonlinear heat flow. Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré showed that on RCD(K,N) spaces the p‑Wasserstein distances are bi‑Lipschitz equivalent to W2, which implies that CD_p(K,N) is equivalent to CD(K,N) for all p∈(1,∞) when the space is essentially non‑branching. However, on branching spaces the two conditions diverge: CD_p(K,N) is strictly stronger for p>2 and weaker for p<2. The CD_p(K,N) condition admits a Bochner‑type inequality for the p‑Laplacian: Γ2,p(f) ≥ K Γ_p(f) + (1/N)(Δ_p f)2, where Γ_p(f)=|∇f|^p and Γ2,p is the iterated carré du champ. This inequality yields gradient estimates for the p‑heat flow and a Li–Yau type Harnack inequality. The CD_p(K,N) framework is crucial for the study of nonlinear diffusion equations, for the regularity of p‑harmonic functions, and for the stability of the p‑spectral gap under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence.

Entropic Interpolation and the Schrödinger Bridge: A Probabilistic Characterisation of CD(K,N)

The Schrödinger bridge problem asks for the most likely evolution of a diffusion process between two prescribed marginals μ0, μ1 when the prior dynamics is the heat flow on (X,d,m). Its solution is an entropic interpolation (μ_t)t∈[0,1] which minimizes the relative entropy with respect to the law of the Brownian motion among all couplings with the given marginals. Léonard proved that on a smooth Riemannian manifold the displacement convexity of the entropic cost functional C(μ0,μ1)=inf H(P|R) (where R is the law of the Brownian bridge) is equivalent to the CD(K,∞) condition. On RCD(K,N) spaces the same equivalence holds: the entropic cost is geodesically convex in the space of probability measures equipped with the entropic Wasserstein distance if and only if the space satisfies CD(K,N). The proof identifies the heat flow with the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy in the quadratic Wasserstein space and writes the Schrödinger problem as a static optimal transport problem with cost c(x,y)=-log p1(x,y), where p_t is the heat kernel. The displacement convexity of the entropic cost then translates into the convexity of the Rényi entropy along W2‑geodesics, which is the definition of CD(K,N). This probabilistic characterisation does not refer to the geometry of the Wasserstein space directly, but only to the heat kernel and relative entropy. It yields a new proof of the stability of CD(K,N) under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence because the heat kernel converges uniformly on compact sets and the entropic cost is lower semicontinuous. Moreover, the formulation extends naturally to the p‑Wasserstein setting, linking the entropic cost for the p‑heat flow to the convexity of the Rényi entropy in (P_p(X),W_p).

The Robin Boundary Condition and the CD(K,N) Condition on Metric Measure Spaces with Boundary

The Robin boundary condition interpolates between the Neumann and Dirichlet conditions by prescribing a linear relation between the normal derivative of a function and its trace on the boundary. In the synthetic setting of a metric measure space (X,d,m) with a distinguished boundary measure μ supported on a closed set ∂X, the Robin condition appears naturally when one considers the bilinear form

ER(f,g) = ∫X ∇f·∇g dm + ∫∂X β fg dμ

where β ≥ 0 is a bounded measurable function on the boundary. The associated Laplacian ΔR satisfies the integration‑by‑parts formula

∫X g ΔRf dm = −ER(f,g)

for all f,g in the domain. The curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) with Robin boundary data is formulated by requiring that the Rényi entropy SN(μ) = ∫ ρ1−1/N dm is K‑convex along W2‑geodesics that are confined to the interior and that the boundary term contributes a non‑negative amount to the second variation. Equivalently, the Bakry–Émery Γ2 calculus on the space with boundary must satisfy

Γ2(f) ≥ K Γ(f) + (1/N)(ΔRf)2 + boundary terms

where the boundary terms involve β and the second fundamental form of ∂X in the sense of the synthetic second fundamental form introduced by Mondino and co‑authors. When β = 0 one recovers the Neumann CD(K,N) condition; letting β → ∞ (in the sense of quadratic forms) yields the Dirichlet condition. The Robin condition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence provided the boundary measures converge weakly and the functions β converge in L∞. This stability permits the transfer of spectral estimates — in particular a Lichnerowicz‑type lower bound for the first non‑zero eigenvalue of −ΔR — from smooth manifolds with Robin boundary to the synthetic category.

The Obstacle Problem for the Heat Flow on RCD(K,N) Spaces with Boundary

The obstacle problem for the heat flow asks for a function u(t,x) that evolves by the heat equation while staying above a given obstacle ψ(x) and coincides with the heat flow wherever it is strictly above the obstacle. On an RCD(K,N) space with boundary, the problem is formulated via the minimizing movement scheme in the Wasserstein space P2(X) with the additional constraint that the evolving probability measures μt satisfy μt ≥ ψ m in the sense of densities. The convexity of the Rényi entropy along W2‑geodesics — which is the defining property of CD(K,N) — guarantees that the discrete scheme converges to a unique limit curve (μt)t≥0 which is a gradient flow of the entropy under the obstacle constraint. The limit satisfies a variational inequality of the form

∫X (∂tu − Δu)(v − u) dm ≥ 0

for all admissible test functions v ≥ ψ. The synthetic curvature bound enters through the Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) formulation: the constrained gradient flow satisfies an EVI with an additional term accounting for the contact set {u = ψ}. Regularity results, such as the fact that the free boundary ∂{u > ψ} is rectifiable and has finite (N−1)‑dimensional Hausdorff measure, follow from the combination of the RCD(K,N) structure theory (rectifiability of tangent cones) and the monotonicity formulas derived from the Bochner inequality. This framework extends the classical theory of Caffarelli and Friedman to non‑smooth spaces and provides a robust tool for studying phase‑transition models on metric measure spaces with lower Ricci bounds.

The Free Boundary Problem for the Heat Flow on RCD(K,N) Spaces with Boundary

In the free boundary problem the evolving set {u(t,·) > 0} is not prescribed a priori; its boundary moves with a velocity determined by the normal derivative of the solution. On an RCD(K,N) space, the heat flow with a free boundary can be constructed as the gradient flow of the functional

F(μ) = Ent(μ|m) + Per(μ)

where Ent is the relative entropy and Per is the perimeter functional (the total variation of the characteristic function of the positivity set). The curvature‑dimension condition ensures that the entropy is displacement convex, while the lower Ricci bound yields a first variation formula for the perimeter that involves the synthetic mean curvature of the free boundary. The minimizing movement scheme with time step τ produces a sequence of sets Ek solving

Ek ∈ argminE { (1/2τ) W22(χEm, χEk−1m) + Ent(χEm|m) + Per(E) }

Passing to the limit τ → 0 gives a curve of sets (Et)t≥0 whose characteristic functions satisfy a weak formulation of the Stefan problem. The synthetic Bochner inequality provides the necessary regularity to show that the free boundary is a countably (N−1)‑rectifiable set and that its normal velocity coincides with the inward normal derivative of the temperature. This result generalises the classical theory of Caffarelli and Salsa to spaces with only a lower Ricci bound in the synthetic sense.

The Convergence of Discrete Approximation Schemes for the Heat Flow on RCD(K,N) Spaces

Numerical approximation of the heat flow on non‑smooth spaces is achieved by discretising both the spatial domain and the time variable. A common approach is to replace the metric measure space (X,d,m) by a sequence of finite weighted graphs (Xn,dn,mn) that converge to X in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense, and to apply the De Giorgi minimizing movement scheme on each graph. The discrete scheme reads

μk+1n ∈ argminμ { (1/2τ) W2,n2(μ, μkn) + Entn(μ) }

where W2,n and Entn are the Wasserstein distance and entropy on the graph. The CD(K,N) condition on the limit space implies a uniform discrete curvature‑dimension condition on the graphs (in the sense of Ollivier or Erbar–Maas), which yields uniform contraction estimates for the discrete heat semigroups. By the stability of the CD(K,N) condition under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, the piecewise constant interpolations of μkn converge, as n → ∞ and τ → 0, to the continuous heat flow on X. Error estimates of order O(τ1/2 + n−1/d) can be derived when the limit space satisfies a Poincaré inequality and the graphs are constructed via a suitable quantization of the measure. This convergence theory provides a rigorous foundation for computational Ricci curvature and for the simulation of diffusion processes on singular spaces.

The Quantitative Stability of the Entropic Cost under Measured Gromov–Hausdorff Convergence on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The entropic cost C(μ0,μ1) = infπ H(π|R), where R is the law of the Brownian bridge on the space, provides a probabilistic characterisation of the CD(K,N) condition: the map (μ0,μ1) ↦ C(μ0,μ1) is geodesically convex in the quadratic Wasserstein space if and only if the space satisfies CD(K,N). Quantitative stability asks for explicit rates at which the entropic cost on a sequence of spaces (Xn,dn,mn) converging to (X,d,m) in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense approaches the limit cost. |Cn(μ0,μ1) − C(μ0,μ1)| ≤ C · dmGH((Xn,mn),(X,m))α

with an exponent α > 0 depending only on K,N and the diameter bound. The proof combines the stability of the heat kernel in the uniform topology on compact sets with the lower semicontinuity of the relative entropy. This quantitative estimate is crucial for applications in optimal transport on data clouds approximating a manifold, because it guarantees that the Schrödinger bridge computed on the point cloud converges to the true entropic interpolation at a controlled rate.

The Geodesic Structure of the Wasserstein Space over RCD(K,N) Spaces

This section delves into the geometry of the space of probability measures W₂(X) when the underlying metric measure space (X,d,m) satisfies the RCD(K,N) condition. The Lott–Sturm–Villani theory establishes that the CD(K,N) condition is equivalent to the K-convexity of the entropy functional on W₂(X). This convexity implies that W₂(X) itself possesses a rich geometric structure, often described as a "non-negatively curved space" in a generalized sense, even when X itself has negative curvature.

Specifically, the RCD(K,N) condition on X implies that the Wasserstein space W₂(X) is a geodesic space, meaning that any two probability measures μ₀ and μ₁ can be connected by a geodesic path of probability measures (μₜ)ₜ∈[0,1]. These geodesics are generated by optimal transport maps, which push forward the initial measure μ₀ to the final measure μ₁. A key insight is that the "straightest possible paths" in W₂(X) correspond to the "most efficient ways" to rearrange mass on X. The K-convexity of the entropy functional along these geodesics is the core of the synthetic Ricci curvature bound.

Furthermore, for RCD(K,N) spaces, the geodesics in W₂(X) are unique up to reparameterization if the underlying space X is essentially non-branching. This non-branching property, which is a consequence of the RCD condition for N < ∞, ensures that optimal transport maps are almost everywhere unique. The uniqueness of geodesics in W₂(X) is crucial for defining notions of "curvature" and "convexity" in this infinite-dimensional setting. For instance, the displacement convexity of the Rényi entropy functionals (beyond just the Shannon entropy) also holds, providing a family of curvature-dimension conditions.

The geometry of W₂(X) is not merely an abstract construction; it has profound implications. The tangent space to W₂(X) at a measure μ can be identified with L²(X,μ), the space of square-integrable vector fields. This allows for the development of a calculus on W₂(X), including notions of gradient and Hessian, which are essential for studying gradient flows in the Wasserstein space. These gradient flows, such as the heat flow, can then be interpreted as geodesics or curves of maximal slope in W₂(X) with respect to the entropy functional. The RCD condition ensures that W₂(X) behaves, in many respects, like an Alexandrov space with non-negative curvature, despite its infinite dimensionality. This perspective allows for the application of comparison geometry techniques to the space of measures itself, leading to further insights into the behavior of diffusion processes and optimal transport. The study of the sectional curvature of W₂(X) provides a more refined understanding of its geometry, revealing that it can be positive even when X has negative Ricci curvature.

Quantitative Stability of Functional Inequalities and Geometric Properties under mGH Convergence

The RCD(K,N) condition exhibits remarkable stability under measured Gromov–Hausdorff (mGH) convergence, a notion of convergence for metric measure spaces. This stability is not merely qualitative (i.e., the limit of a sequence of RCD(K,N) spaces is RCD(K,N)), but often quantitative, meaning that various geometric and functional properties converge at a controlled rate. This quantitative stability is a cornerstone for applying the theory to approximating spaces, such as discrete graphs or point clouds.

A key mechanism underpinning these quantitative stability results is the uniform control over the heat kernel and its derivatives that RCD(K,N) spaces provide. Specifically, RCD(K,N) spaces satisfy uniform Gaussian heat kernel estimates, which bound the heat kernel from above and below by Gaussian functions, with constants depending only on K, N, and the diameter. These estimates are robust under mGH convergence.

For instance, the stability of the spectral gap (the first non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian) is quantitative. If a sequence of RCD(K,N) spaces (Xₙ, dₙ, mₙ) converges to (X, d, m) in the mGH sense, then their spectral gaps λ₁(Xₙ) converge to λ₁(X), and the rate of convergence can be bounded by a function of the mGH distance. Similar quantitative stability holds for the Log-Sobolev inequality and the Sobolev inequality. These functional inequalities are fundamental for understanding the convergence to equilibrium of diffusion processes and the regularity of functions. The constants in these inequalities depend on K and N, and their stability implies that the "analytic rigidity" imposed by the RCD condition is preserved under approximation.

Beyond functional inequalities, geometric properties also exhibit quantitative stability. For example, the volume of balls, the diameter, and the isoperimetric profile of RCD(K,N) spaces are quantitatively stable under mGH convergence. This means that if a sequence of spaces converges, their volumes, diameters, and isoperimetric constants also converge at a rate determined by the mGH distance. This is particularly important for understanding the structure of Ricci limit spaces, which are limits of Riemannian manifolds with uniform Ricci curvature bounds. The RCD condition provides the synthetic framework to study these limits, and quantitative stability ensures that properties derived from the RCD condition are meaningful for these limit spaces. The proof techniques often involve combining the uniform heat kernel estimates with approximation arguments for test functions and measures, leveraging the compactness of RCD spaces within the mGH topology.

Application of Quantitative Stability
Consider a sequence of smooth Riemannian manifolds (Mₙ, gₙ) with Ricci curvature bounded below by K and dimension bounded above by N, which converge in the measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense to a metric measure space (X, d, m). Suppose that for each Mₙ, the first non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian, λ₁(Mₙ), is known. If λ₁(Mₙ) = 3 + 1/n, what can be inferred about the spectral gap of the limit space X, assuming X is compact?
The RCD(K,N) condition is stable under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. Since (Mₙ, gₙ) are smooth Riemannian manifolds satisfying Ricci(gₙ) ≥ K and dim(Mₙ) ≤ N, they are RCD(K,N) spaces. Their mGH limit (X, d, m) is also an RCD(K,N) space. A key result of quantitative stability is that the spectral gap (first non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian) is stable under mGH convergence. Therefore, if λ₁(Mₙ) converges to a limit, then λ₁(X) must be equal to that limit. Given λ₁(Mₙ) = 3 + 1/n, as n → ∞, λ₁(Mₙ) → 3. Thus, the first non-zero eigenvalue of the Laplacian on the limit space X, denoted λ₁(X), must be 3. This demonstrates how quantitative stability allows us to deduce properties of singular limit spaces from their smooth approximations.

Instead of memorizing isolated facts, try to map out these cause-and-effect relationships. The Parabolic Maximum Principle and Gradient Estimates on RCD(K,N) Spaces

The synthetic Ricci curvature bounds, particularly the RCD(K,N) condition, enable the extension of powerful analytical tools from smooth Riemannian geometry to non-smooth metric measure spaces. Among these, the parabolic maximum principle and various gradient estimates for solutions to the heat equation are fundamental.

The parabolic maximum principle, a cornerstone of parabolic partial differential equations, states that a solution to the heat equation cannot attain its maximum (or minimum) in the interior of its domain unless it is constant. On RCD(K,N) spaces, this principle holds for solutions to the heat equation ∂ₜu = Δu, where Δ is the Laplacian defined via the Cheeger energy. The proof relies on the properties of the heat flow and the underlying metric structure, ensuring that information propagates in a controlled manner. This principle is crucial for establishing uniqueness of solutions and for deriving bounds on their magnitude.

Building upon the synthetic Bochner inequality, which provides a lower bound for the "synthetic Ricci curvature" of the space, one can derive gradient estimates for solutions to the heat equation. A prominent example is the Li-Yau type inequality. In smooth Riemannian geometry, the Li-Yau inequality provides a lower bound for the ratio of the gradient of the heat kernel to the heat kernel itself, which has profound implications for volume growth and heat kernel behavior. On RCD(K,N) spaces, a synthetic version of the Li-Yau inequality holds. This inequality states that for a positive solution u to the heat equation, the quantity |∇u|²/u - K(N-1)/(N-1) × (Δu/u) - (N-1)/(2t) is bounded below by a term related to the curvature. More precisely, for N < ∞, the quantity |∇u|²/u - (Δu/u) - (N-1)/(2t) is controlled. For N = ∞, this simplifies to |∇u|²/u - (Δu/u) ≥ -K. These estimates are derived using the Bochner inequality for the associated entropy functional or by applying the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus to the heat kernel.

These gradient estimates are not merely theoretical curiosities. They are essential for:

  1. Gaussian Heat Kernel Bounds: Li-Yau inequalities are often used to prove the uniform Gaussian heat kernel estimates mentioned earlier, which are crucial for quantitative stability results.
  2. Harnack Inequalities: They imply various forms of Harnack inequalities, which relate the values of a positive solution to the heat equation at different points and times. These inequalities are powerful tools for studying the regularity and behavior of solutions.
  3. Volume Growth Estimates: Gradient estimates can be used to re-derive and strengthen volume growth estimates, such as the Bishop–Gromov inequality, providing a deeper understanding of how volume behaves under Ricci curvature bounds.
  4. Rigidity Results: In certain cases, the equality case in these inequalities leads to rigidity theorems, characterizing spaces that attain the lower curvature bound.
The ability to establish such sophisticated analytical tools on non-smooth spaces highlights the power and robustness of the synthetic approach to Ricci curvature.

Rectifiability and Stratification of Singular Sets in RCD(K,N) Spaces

A hallmark of RCD(K,N) spaces, especially for finite N, is the highly structured nature of their singular sets. Unlike general metric spaces, the RCD condition imposes strong regularity on these spaces, making them "almost Riemannian" in many respects. This structure is primarily captured by the concepts of rectifiability and stratification.

An RCD(K,N) space (X,d,m) is known to be rectifiable. This means that X can be decomposed into a countable union of Lipschitz images of open subsets of Euclidean spaces, plus a set of measure zero. More precisely, for an RCD(K,N) space, there exists a unique (up to measure zero sets) decomposition of X into a regular part and a singular part. The regular part, often denoted Xᵣₑg, is where the space locally resembles a Riemannian manifold. The singular part, Xₛᵢₙg, is where the metric structure deviates significantly from Euclidean space. The rectifiability theorem asserts that Xᵣₑg is dense and open in X, and that X is rectifiable in a measure-theoretic sense. This is a profound result, as it implies that despite being non-smooth, RCD spaces still possess a differentiable structure almost everywhere, allowing for the application of classical analysis tools locally.

Furthermore, RCD(K,N) spaces admit a stratification theorem. This theorem states that the singular set Xₛᵢₙg can be decomposed into a sequence of nested closed sets X₀ ⊃ X₁ ⊃ ... ⊃ Xₖ, where Xᵢ is the set of points where the tangent cone has dimension less than or equal to i. More precisely, the singular set can be stratified by the dimension of its tangent cones. At each point x ∈ X, one can define a tangent cone TₓX, which is a metric cone that captures the infinitesimal geometry of X around x. A crucial result is that if X is an RCD(K,N) space, then its tangent cones TₓX are also RCD(0,N) spaces. This self-similarity property is fundamental. The stratification theorem then asserts that the set of points where the tangent cone is not Euclidean (i.e., the singular set) is a union of countably (N-1)-rectifiable sets. This means that the singular set itself has a well-behaved geometric structure, being a union of lower-dimensional "manifolds" in a generalized sense.

The rectifiability and stratification theorems have several important consequences:

  1. Existence of Gradients: They ensure the existence of gradients for Sobolev functions almost everywhere on RCD(K,N) spaces, which is essential for the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus and the Bochner inequality.
  2. Regularity of Harmonic Functions: They provide a framework for studying the regularity of harmonic functions and solutions to elliptic and parabolic PDEs on these spaces.
  3. Local-to-Global Principles: The ability to understand the local structure via tangent cones allows for the extension of local properties to global ones, bridging the gap between smooth and non-smooth geometry.
  4. Geometric Measure Theory Connections: These results deeply connect the theory of RCD spaces with geometric measure theory, providing powerful tools for analyzing their structure.
In essence, the RCD condition acts as a strong regularity assumption, taming the potential wildness of general metric measure spaces and endowing them with a rich, almost-Riemannian geometric and analytical structure.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the geodesic structure of the Wasserstein space W₂(X) with the geodesic structure of the underlying space X. While related by the CD(K,N) condition, W₂(X) is an infinite-dimensional space whose "curvature" properties are distinct from X.
  • Assuming that quantitative stability results hold for general CD(K,N) spaces. Many strong quantitative stability results (e.g., for heat kernel estimates, spectral gap) specifically require the RCD(K,N) condition, which includes the "Riemannian" aspect (i.e., the Cheeger energy being a Dirichlet form).
  • Overlooking the role of the uniform Gaussian heat kernel estimates in proving quantitative stability. These estimates are a crucial intermediate step that connects the RCD condition to the controlled convergence of functional inequalities.
  • Believing that the parabolic maximum principle or Li-Yau gradient estimates are trivial extensions to RCD spaces. Their proofs require careful adaptation of techniques from smooth geometry, relying heavily on the synthetic Bochner inequality and the properties of the Cheeger energy.
  • Misinterpreting the rectifiability of RCD(K,N) spaces as implying that they are smooth manifolds. Rectifiability means "almost everywhere smooth" in a measure-theoretic sense, but singular points can still exist and are crucial to the space's structure.
  • Forgetting that the stratification theorem for singular sets relies on the properties of tangent cones, which themselves are RCD(0,N) spaces. This self-similarity is a key aspect of understanding the local structure.
  • Assuming that the dimension N in CD(K,N) automatically implies that the space is N-dimensional everywhere. N is an upper bound on the "effective dimension" and can vary locally, especially at singular points where tangent cones might have lower dimensions.

The Role of the Cheeger Energy in the Quadraticity Criterion for RCD(K,N) Spaces

The Cheeger energy Ch on a metric measure space (X,d,m) is defined as the lower semicontinuous envelope of the slope functional. In the smooth Riemannian setting it coincides with the Dirichlet energy ∫|∇f|² dm. A fundamental breakthrough of Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré is the identification of the quadraticity of Ch as the analytic hallmark of the Riemannian curvature‑dimension condition. Precisely, a space satisfies RCD(K,N) if and only if it satisfies CD(K,N) and its Cheeger energy is a quadratic form, i.e. it satisfies the parallelogram law Ch(f+g)+Ch(f−g)=2Ch(f)+2Ch(g) for all f,g in the Sobolev space W¹,²(X). This equivalence transforms a purely geometric convexity requirement into a functional‑analytic property that can be checked on the level of the Dirichlet form.

The quadraticity condition forces the underlying infinitesimal Hilbertian structure: the tangent module L⁰(TX) becomes a Hilbert module, the gradient operator ∇ is linear, and the Laplacian Δ is a self‑adjoint operator generating a symmetric Markov semigroup. Consequently the heat flow coincides with the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in the L² sense, and the Bakry–Émery Γ₂ calculus can be developed exactly as on smooth manifolds. In particular the Bochner inequality Γ₂(f) ≥ K Γ(f) + (Δf)²/N holds for all f in the domain of the Laplacian, and the dimension bound N appears naturally through the Γ₂ estimate.

From the point of view of applications, quadraticity is the gateway to a full second‑order calculus. It guarantees the existence of a covariant derivative ∇² on the tensor module, the validity of the Leibniz rule for the Hessian, and the identification of the Ricci tensor as the lower bound in the Bochner inequality. Without quadraticity, as in general CD(K,N) spaces (e.g. Finsler manifolds), the heat flow is still well defined as the gradient flow of the entropy in the Wasserstein space, but the linear structure of the differential calculus breaks down and many rigidity theorems fail.

Exam‑relevant nuance: the quadraticity of Ch is equivalent to the Sobolev‑to‑Lipschitz property (every W¹,² function with minimal weak upper gradient ≤1 has a 1‑Lipschitz representative). This property is often used as a practical test in problems where the space is known to be CD(K,N) but the Riemannian nature must be verified.

The Stability of the CD(K,N) Condition under Measured Gromov–Hausdorff Convergence for Non‑Compact Spaces

For compact metric measure spaces the stability of the CD(K,N) condition under measured Gromov–Hausdorff (mGH) convergence is a classical result of Lott–Villani and Sturm. The non‑compact setting requires additional uniformity assumptions because the entropy functional is no longer automatically lower semicontinuous with respect to narrow convergence of measures. The standard framework (Gigli–Mondino–Savaré) assumes a sequence (Xᵢ,dᵢ,mᵢ) of proper, geodesic, essentially non‑branching spaces satisfying CD(K,N) with a uniform lower bound K and an upper dimension bound N. If the sequence converges in the pointed measured Gromov–Hausdorff sense to a limit (X,d,m), then the limit also satisfies CD(K,N) provided the reference measures satisfy a uniform doubling condition and the spaces are uniformly locally compact.

The proof proceeds by localising the entropy convexity along geodesics in the Wasserstein space. One first shows that the CD(K,N) condition is equivalent to the reduced curvature‑dimension condition CD*(K,N) on essentially non‑branching spaces. The reduced condition involves only the distortion coefficients σK,N(t) and is stable under pointed mGH convergence because the coefficients depend continuously on the distance. The uniform doubling condition guarantees tightness of the probability measures involved in the optimal transport plans, allowing the passage to the limit in the displacement convexity inequality.

A subtle point is the role of the non‑branching hypothesis. In branching spaces the reduced condition is strictly weaker than CD(K,N), and stability can fail. Counterexamples built from gluing Euclidean cones show that without essential non‑branching the limit may lose the full curvature‑dimension bound even though each approximant satisfies it. Therefore exam questions often test whether the candidate recognises that the non‑branching assumption cannot be dropped in the non‑compact stability theorem.

Another practical aspect: the stability result yields the convergence of heat kernels, Dirichlet forms and spectral data under the same hypotheses. In particular the L²‑gradient flows of the Cheeger energies converge in the sense of Mosco, which is the analytic counterpart of the geometric stability.

Worked Example: Verifying Quadraticity of the Cheeger Energy
Let (X,d,m) be a metric measure space satisfying CD(0,N) for some N∈[1,∞). Assume that for every f,g∈W¹,²(X) the Cheeger energy satisfies the parallelogram identity Ch(f+g)+Ch(f−g)=2Ch(f)+2Ch(g). Show that the space is an RCD(0,N) space.
1. By definition CD(0,N) holds. 2. The parallelogram identity is exactly the quadraticity of the Cheeger energy. 3. The Ambrosio–Gigli–Savaré theorem states that a metric measure space satisfies RCD(K,N) iff it satisfies CD(K,N) and its Cheeger energy is quadratic. 4. Therefore the given space satisfies RCD(0,N). The key step is recognising that quadraticity is equivalent to the Sobolev‑to‑Lipschitz property and to the linearity of the gradient, which together with CD(0,N) give the full Riemannian structure.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks to distinguish between CD(K,N) and RCD(K,N), immediately check whether the Cheeger energy is known to be quadratic or whether the space is infinitesimally Hilbertian. If the statement only assumes CD(K,N), you cannot use linear calculus tools (Bochner inequality, Hessian, covariant derivative) unless you first prove quadraticity. In stability questions, verify that the non‑branching and uniform doubling hypotheses are explicitly mentioned; missing them is a common trap.

The Theory of the Heat Flow on RCD(K,N) Spaces with Non‑Local Operators

Non‑local curvature‑dimension conditions extend the Lott–Sturm–Villani theory to operators such as the fractional Laplacian (−Δ)^s with s∈(0,1). The appropriate synthetic framework replaces the Wasserstein distance by a transport cost built from a symmetric jump kernel J(x,y) and defines a non‑local entropy functional. The resulting CD(K,N) condition (often denoted CDnl(K,N)) was introduced by Erbar, Maas and others using the formalism of non‑local Dirichlet forms on metric measure spaces.

On an RCD(K,N) space the classical heat flow is the gradient flow of the Cheeger energy in L². For the non‑local setting the heat flow becomes the gradient flow of the non‑local Dirichlet form ℰ(f)=½∫∫(f(x)−f(y))² J(x,y) dm(x) dm(y) in the same Hilbert space. The flow satisfies an Evolution Variational Inequality (EVI) with respect to the non‑local transport distance, and the corresponding semigroup enjoys Gaussian‑type heat kernel estimates with polynomial tails reflecting the jump nature of the kernel.

The Theory of the Heat Flow on RCD(K,N) Spaces with Time‑Dependent Metrics

Synthetic Ricci flow on metric measure spaces is formulated by letting the metric d_t and the reference measure m_t evolve in time while preserving the RCD(K,N) condition. The pioneering work of Gigli–Mantegazza and later of Sturm constructs a flow (X,d_t,m_t)t∈[0,T] such that for each t the space is RCD(K_t,N) with a time‑dependent lower bound K_t. The evolution is driven by the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy in the space of metric measure spaces equipped with a suitable distance (the Wasserstein‑type distance on the space of mm‑spaces).

Analytically, the heat flow on a time‑dependent background is described by a family of Dirichlet forms ℰ_t with domains W1,2(X,d_t,m_t). The solution u_t of the parabolic equation ∂_t u_t = Δ_t u_t satisfies an Evolution Variational Inequality with respect to the time‑dependent Wasserstein distance W2,t. The EVI formulation is the key to proving uniqueness and stability of the flow. Moreover, the flow satisfies a differential Harnack inequality of Li–Yau type, which yields Gaussian heat kernel bounds uniform in time as long as the curvature lower bound stays bounded below.

Geometrically, the synthetic Ricci flow preserves the RCD condition and the dimension bound N. It also respects the stratification of the singular set: the regular set evolves by a smooth Ricci flow, while the singular strata move according to a weak formulation that can be described via the convergence of tangent cones. This provides a framework for studying singularity formation and surgery in non‑smooth settings.

Typical exam questions ask to derive the EVI for the heat flow on a time‑dependent RCD space, or to explain why the quadraticity of the Cheeger energy is essential for the linearisation of the flow. Another common point is the distinction between the Lagrangian formulation (following the flow of the metric) and the Eulerian formulation (fixed reference measure, evolving density).

  • Assuming that stability of CD(K,N) under mGH convergence holds without the uniform doubling and non‑branching hypotheses in the non‑compact case.
  • Treating the non‑local carré du champ Γnl as a pointwise gradient square; it is an integral operator and does not satisfy the Leibniz rule.
  • Believing that the classical displacement convexity of the entropy characterises non‑local CD(K,N); the correct formulation uses the EVI with respect to the non‑local transport cost.
  • Neglecting the time‑dependence of the reference measure when writing the heat equation on an evolving RCD space; the correct equation includes a term involving the logarithmic derivative of m_t.
  • Thinking that the synthetic Ricci flow preserves the exact value of the curvature lower bound K; in general K becomes a function K(t) and may decrease.
  • Overlooking that the stratification theorem for singular sets requires the limit space to be RCD, not merely CD; the Riemannian structure is essential for the rectifiability of the regular set.

The proof follows the almost rigidity scheme of Colding–Naber adapted to the synthetic setting. One first shows that the volume ratio r↦mathfrak m(B_r(x))/ω_N r^N is almost monotone, using the Bishop–Gromov inequality which holds on RCD(K,N) spaces. The quantitative splitting is then obtained by a contradiction argument using the compactness of RCD(K,N) spaces under measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence and the uniqueness of tangent cones at almost every point.

The quantitative almost splitting theorem is the key ingredient in the proof of the almost maximal volume rigidity theorem: if an RCD(K,N) space has volume close to that of the model space, then it is Gromov–Hausdorff close to the model space. The Role of the Cheeger Energy in the Identification of the Laplacian on RCD(K,N) Spaces

Definition and Relaxation of the Cheeger Energy

For a metric measure space (X,d,mathfrak m) the Cheeger energy is defined as the lower semicontinuous envelope of the functional

Ch(f)=frac12∫_X lip f2 dmathfrak m, finLip_b(X),

with respect to L2-convergence. Here lip f(x)=limsupy→ x(|f(y)-f(x)|)/(d(y,x)) is the local Lipschitz constant. The relaxed functional Ch is convex and lower semicontinuous on L2(X,mathfrak m). Its domain W1,2(X,d,mathfrak m)={f∈ L2:Ch(f)<∞} is a Banach space when equipped with the norm \|f\|W1,22=\|f\|L22+2Ch(f).

Quadraticity and the RCD Condition

A metric measure space is said to be infinitesimally Hilbertian if Ch is a quadratic form, i.e. Ch(f+g)+Ch(f-g)=2Ch(f)+2Ch(g) for all f,g∈ W1,2. The RCD(K,N) condition is precisely the conjunction of the curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) (in the sense of Lott–Sturm–Villani) and infinitesimal Hilbertianity. Quadraticity implies that the subdifferential of Ch is a linear operator, which can be identified with the Laplacian Δ. In this case the heat flow coincides with the gradient flow of Ch in L2 and with the gradient flow of the entropy in the Wasserstein space.

When Ch is quadratic, the associated Dirichlet form mathcal E(f,g)=Ch(f+g)-Ch(f)-Ch(g) is a strongly local, regular, symmetric form on L2. Its generator Δ is a self‑adjoint, non‑positive operator with domain D(Δ)⊂ W1,2. The heat semigroup P_t=etΔ is the unique L2-gradient flow of Ch. This identification is the cornerstone of the second‑order calculus on RCD(K,N) spaces.

The quadraticity of Ch yields the Bakry–Émery Γ2 calculus: Γ(f)=|Df|_*2 and Γ2(f)=frac12ΔΓ(f)-Γ(f,Δ f).
