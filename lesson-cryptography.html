Cryptography — Secrets, Proofs, and Post-Quantum Security

Cryptography is the mathematics of secure communication — ensuring confidentiality, integrity, authentication, and non-repudiation even in the presence of adversaries. It spans symmetric ciphers (AES), public-key systems (RSA, ECC), cryptographic hash functions, zero-knowledge proofs, multiparty computation, and post-quantum cryptography. The subject is built on computational hardness assumptions that separate feasible from infeasible.

"Anyone who considers arithmetical methods of producing random digits is, of course, in a state of sin."
— John von Neumann
Part 1

🔐 Symmetric-Key Cryptography and Shannon's Theory

Secrecy
Perfect Secrecy, Block Ciphers, and AES
Shannon's perfect secrecy: the ciphertext reveals no information about the plaintext — achieved by the one-time pad (OTP) with key length = message length, key uniform random, never reused. The OTP is unconditionally secure but impractical. Modern symmetric ciphers use product ciphers combining substitution and permutation. AES (Advanced Encryption Standard, Rijndael) is a 128/192/256-bit block cipher with 10/12/14 rounds of SubBytes, ShiftRows, MixColumns, AddRoundKey. The Feistel structure (DES, Blowfish, Twofish) splits the block into halves and alternates round functions. Modes of operation (CBC, CTR, GCM) extend block ciphers to encrypt arbitrary-length messages. Differential and linear cryptanalysis are the main attacks — AES was designed with proven bounds against these. Side-channel attacks exploit timing, power, or electromagnetic leakage rather than mathematical weaknesses.
Perfect secrecy requires keys as long as messages — all practical ciphers trade unconditional security for computational security based on assumptions.
Part 2

🔑 Public-Key Cryptography — RSA and Diffie-Hellman

Asymmetric
RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and the Discrete Logarithm Problem
Public-key cryptography (Diffie-Hellman, 1976) revolutionized security: each party has a public key (encryption) and private key (decryption). RSA (Rivest-Shamir-Adleman, 1977): choose primes p,q, n=pq, φ(n)=(p−1)(q−1), e coprime to φ(n), d = e^{-1} mod φ(n). Encrypt c = m^e mod n, decrypt m = c^d mod n. Security relies on the hardness of factoring n. The current factoring record: RSA-250 (829-bit, 2020). Diffie-Hellman key exchange: Alice sends g^a, Bob sends g^b, shared secret = g^{ab}. Security relies on the computational Diffie-Hellman (CDH) assumption. Elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) uses the group of points on an elliptic curve E: y² = x³ + ax + b over a finite field. ECC offers equivalent security to RSA with much shorter keys (256-bit ECC ≈ 3072-bit RSA). The ECDLP (elliptic curve discrete log) has no known subexponential attacks — the basis of ECC's efficiency.
Public-key cryptography solves the key distribution problem — security rests on number-theoretic problems like factoring and discrete log that are easy one way but hard to invert.
Part 3

🧾 Zero-Knowledge Proofs and Secure Computation

Proofs
Zero-Knowledge Proofs — Proving Knowledge Without Revealing It
A zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) allows a prover to convince a verifier of a statement's truth without revealing anything beyond the statement's validity. The classic example: proving you know a Hamiltonian cycle in a graph. ZKPs must satisfy completeness (honest prover convinces), soundness (cheating prover can't), and zero-knowledge (verifier learns nothing about the witness). Interactive proofs (Goldwasser-Micali-Rackoff, 1985) use multiple rounds. Non-interactive ZK (NIZK) via Fiat-Shamir heuristic uses a hash function. zk-SNARKs (Succinct Non-interactive ARguments of Knowledge) produce short proofs verifiable in milliseconds — used in Zcash and blockchain privacy. Secure multiparty computation (MPC) (Yao's garbled circuits, GMW protocol) allows n parties to compute a function on their private inputs without revealing them. The honest-majority setting (Shamir secret sharing, SPDZ) tolerates up to n/2 corrupted parties.
Zero-knowledge proofs enable a prover to convince a verifier of a fact without leaking any information — the ultimate privacy-preserving primitive.
Part 4

⚡ Post-Quantum Cryptography

Quantum Threat
Lattice-Based, Code-Based, and Multivariate Cryptography
Shor's algorithm (1994) factors integers and computes discrete logs in polynomial time on a quantum computer — breaking RSA, DSA, ECDSA, and Diffie-Hellman. Post-quantum cryptography designs schemes secure against quantum adversaries. The leading candidates: lattice-based (Learning With Errors — LWE, Ring-LWE). LWE: given (A, As+e) with small noise e, find s. Security reduces to worst-case lattice problems (GapSVP, SIVP). The NIST-selected winner CRYSTALS-Kyber (KEM) and CRYSTALS-Dilithium (signatures) are lattice-based. Also selected: FALCON (lattice signature) and SPHINCS+ (hash-based signature). Code-based cryptography (McEliece, Niederreiter) uses error-correcting codes — the Goppa code variant has survived decades of cryptanalysis but has large keys (256KB+). Multivariate cryptography (Rainbow, UOV) uses the hardness of solving systems of quadratic equations over finite fields. The NIST PQC standardization (2024) selected Kyber and Dilithium as primary algorithms, with Falcon and SPHINCS+ as alternatives.
Shor's algorithm kills RSA/ECC — post-quantum crypto (LWE, code-based, multivariate) is being standardized now to secure communications against future quantum computers.
