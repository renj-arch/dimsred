Higher Teichmüller Theory — Surface Group Representations, Hitchin Components & Spectral Data

Deep dive into Higher Teichmüller Theory — the study of surface group representations into higher-rank semisimple Lie groups (G = SL(n,ℝ), Sp(2n,ℝ), SO(n,n+1), etc.), the Hitchin component (a connected component of the character variety Hom(π₁(S), G)/G that generalizes Teichmüller space), the Fock–Goncharov theory of positive representations and cluster coordinates, spectral data from Higgs bundles via the Hitchin fibration, the Goldman symplectic form, the Labourie conjecture (Anosov representations), the proper affine actions of surface groups on ℝ³ (the Goldman–Margulis–Labourie theory), the applications to the deformation theory of real projective structures, the bounded cohomology and the volume of representations, the dynamical properties of Hitchin representations (Anosov flows, hyperconvexity), and the cluster algebra structures on moduli spaces of local systems.

"Higher Teichmüller theory studies the geometry of surface group representations into higher-rank Lie groups, revealing a rich structure that mirrors classical Teichmüller theory."
— Anna Wienhard
Part 1

🏛️ Hitchin Components and Surface Group Representations

Representations
Hitchin Components, Character Varieties, and the Hitchin Map
Let S be a closed oriented surface of genus g ≥ 2, and let G be a connected semisimple real Lie group. The character variety Hom(π₁(S), G)/G parametrizes conjugacy classes of representations ρ: π₁(S) → G. For G = PSL(2,ℝ), the character variety has 2^{2g} components; the Teichmüller component corresponds to discrete and faithful representations — this is Teichmüller space. For G = PSL(n,ℝ) with n ≥ 3, the story is dramatically richer. Hitchin (1992) discovered a connected component of the PSL(n,ℝ)-character variety that is homeomorphic to ℝ^{(n²−1)(2g−2)} and contains the Teichmüller component for n=2 as a special case — the Hitchin component. The Hitchin map is a diffeomorphism from the Hitchin component to the vector space of holomorphic differentials ⨁_{k=2}^{n} H⁰(S, K_S^k) (the space of k-differentials on S). Explicitly, a Hitchin representation is obtained by taking a Fuchsian representation (a uniformization of S) and deforming it via the principal embedding ι: SL(2,ℝ) → SL(n,ℝ) (the irreducible representation of SL(2) on ℝ^n). The infinitesimal deformations are given by tensoring the Fuchsian representation with holomorphic k-differentials, producing a family of flat connections ∇ = ∇₀ + Σ_{k=2}^{n} φ_k, where φ_k ∈ Ω^{1,0}(S, K_S^{k−1}) is a Higgs field. This is the Hitchin-Teichmüller correspondence — a bijection between Hitchin representations and holomorphic differentials.
Hitchin components are the higher-rank analogues of Teichmüller space; they are parametrized by holomorphic differentials via the Hitchin map, giving a natural generalization of the Fuchsian uniformization.
Part 2

📐 Positive Representations and Fock–Goncharov Theory

Positivity
Positive Representations, Cluster Coordinates, and the Positive Real Locus
Fock–Goncharov (2006) developed a theory of positive representations for split real semisimple Lie groups G. A representation ρ: π₁(S) → G is positive if it sends every element of π₁(S) to a totally positive matrix — a matrix all of whose minors are positive. The space of positive representations is an open convex cone in the character variety, homeomorphic to ℝ^{dim G · (2g−2)}. The key tool is the cluster structure on the moduli space of local systems. Fock and Goncharov constructed cluster coordinates on the moduli space of G-local systems on a surface with punctures and boundary. These coordinates satisfy cluster transformations under changes of triangulation of the surface. For G = SL(n,ℝ), the positive real locus of the moduli space (where all cluster coordinates are positive) is exactly the Hitchin component. The cluster Poisson variety carries a natural symplectic form (the Atiyah–Bott–Goldman symplectic form) and the cluster coordinates are Darboux coordinates for this form. The quantum cluster algebra structure (Fock–Rosly) gives rise to quantum trace maps — a deformation quantization of the algebra of functions on the character variety. The Bonahon–Kim–Wentworth theory of shear coordinates for higher-rank representations extends the classical shear coordinates for PSL(2,ℝ).
Positive representations are the higher-rank analogue of discrete, faithful representations; they are parametrized by positive cluster coordinates, revealing a deep combinatorial structure.

🔬 Spectral Data, Higgs Bundles, and the Hitchin Fibration

Spectral Data
Higgs Bundles, the Hitchin Fibration, and the Spectral Curve
A Higgs bundle on a Riemann surface X is a pair (E, φ) where E → X is a holomorphic vector bundle and φ: E → E ⊗ K_X is a Higgs field (a holomorphic section of End(E) ⊗ K_X). For G = SL(n,ℂ), the Hitchin equations are F_A + [φ, φ*] = 0 and ∂̄_A φ = 0 (where A is a connection). The Hitchin fibration h: M_{Higgs}(G) → B_G = ⨁_{k=2}^{n} H⁰(X, K_X^k) sends a Higgs bundle to the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of φ — the spectral data. The generic fiber of h is an abelian variety — the Prym variety of the spectral curve Σ ⊂ T*X defined by det(η − φ) = 0, where η is the tautological 1-form on the cotangent bundle. The spectral curve is a ramified n-fold cover of X. The Hitchin component corresponds to the intersection of the moduli space of Higgs bundles with the real slice G = SL(n,ℝ). The uniformization theorem for higher Teichmüller theory (Labourie, Loftin, Fock–Goncharov) states that Hitchin representations correspond to convex foliated projective structures on the unit tangent bundle of S. The Anosov property (Labourie, 2006): every Hitchin representation ρ: π₁(S) → PSL(n,ℝ) is Anosov — there exists a continuous ρ-equivariant map from the boundary at infinity ξ: ∂∞π̃₁(S) → Flag(ℝⁿ) that is dynamics-preserving (transverse, contracting/expanding under the geodesic flow).
Spectral data encode the Higgs field via its characteristic polynomial; the spectral curve is the key geometric object linking Higgs bundles to abelian varieties.
Part 4

🌌 Applications: Real Projective Structures, Cluster Algebras, and Dynamics

Applications
Real Projective Structures, Bounded Cohomology, and Dynamical Properties
A real projective structure on a manifold M is a (G, X)-structure with G = PGL(n+1,ℝ) acting on X = ℝℙⁿ. For surfaces, real projective structures are equivalent to convex projective structures (the Goldman–Choi–Choi–Goldman theory). The deformation space of convex projective structures on a surface of genus g ≥ 2 is homeomorphic to ℝ^{16(g−1)} — exactly the PSL(4,ℝ)-Hitchin component (the Benoist–Hitchin component). The Goldman symplectic form on the character variety is the Atiyah–Bott symplectic form; its restriction to the Hitchin component is non-degenerate. The volume of a representation (defined via the Toledo invariant or the bounded cohomology class in H²_b(π₁(S), ℝ)) gives a Milnor–Wood type inequality: for a Hitchin representation, the volume is bounded and the maximal volume characterizes the Fuchsian locus. The dynamical properties: Hitchin representations are hyperconvex — any n distinct points on ∂∞π̃₁(S) map under ξ to a transverse n-tuple of flags. The geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of S induces an Anosov flow on the associated flat bundle. The cluster algebra structure provides an explicit parametrization of the moduli space via triangulations and shear coordinates, enabling explicit computations. The quantum trace (Bonahon, Wong, and others) is a map from the skein algebra of the surface to the quantized algebra of functions on the character variety — a powerful tool for studying the representation theory of quantum groups.
Higher Teichmüller theory connects surface group representations to real projective geometry, cluster algebras, and Anosov dynamics, with deep implications for both geometry and representation theory.

Anosov Representations and the Labourie–Guichard–Wienhard Theory

The Anosov property, introduced by Labourie in 2006 for Hitchin representations into PSL(n,ℝ), was later axiomatized by Guichard and Wienhard for arbitrary semisimple Lie groups. A representation ρ : π₁(S) → G is Anosov if there exists a continuous, ρ‑equivariant boundary map ξ : ∂∞π₁(S) → G/P into a flag variety such that the induced flow on the associated bundle over the unit tangent bundle of S is uniformly hyperbolic. This dynamical condition replaces the classical proper discontinuity requirement and guarantees that the image of ρ is a discrete, faithful, and purely loxodromic subgroup of G. The boundary map is uniquely determined by ρ and is transverse: for any two distinct points x ≠ y in ∂∞π₁(S), the flags ξ(x) and ξ(y) are in general position. The Anosov condition is open in the character variety, so Hitchin components are unions of connected components consisting entirely of Anosov representations. Labourie proved that every Hitchin representation into PSL(n,ℝ) is P₁‑Anosov (stabilizer of a line), while Guichard–Wienhard showed that for split real groups the Hitchin component coincides with the θ‑Anosov locus for the opposition involution θ. This equivalence provides a robust bridge between higher Teichmüller theory and the theory of Anosov flows, allowing the import of tools from thermodynamic formalism, such as pressure metrics and entropy rigidity, into the study of surface group representations.

Higher Rank Uniformization and Convex Projective Structures

Classical uniformization identifies the Teichmüller space of a closed surface S of genus g ≥ 2 with the space of hyperbolic structures, i.e. discrete faithful representations π₁(S) → PSL(2,ℝ). Higher rank uniformization replaces PSL(2,ℝ) by a split real group G and seeks geometric structures on manifolds associated to G. For G = PSL(n,ℝ), Labourie, Loftin, and Fock–Goncharov established that the Hitchin component parametrizes convex foliated projective structures on the unit tangent bundle T¹S. Concretely, a Hitchin representation ρ determines a ρ‑equivariant immersion of the universal cover Ṽ into the flag variety Flag(ℝⁿ) whose image is a convex curve. The developing map of the associated projective structure is obtained by projecting this curve to ℝℙⁿ⁻¹. The resulting projective structure on T¹S is convex and carries a natural Anosov flow, the geodesic flow of the underlying hyperbolic metric. For n = 3, the Hitchin component is identified with the space of convex real projective structures on S itself (the Goldman–Choi–Goldman theorem), a space homeomorphic to ℝ¹⁶(g−1). For higher n, the uniformization theorem yields a diffeomorphism between the Hitchin component and the space of convex foliated projective structures on T¹S, providing a higher‑rank analogue of the classical Teichmüller space. This geometric picture underlies the Labourie–Loftin parametrization by Labourie’s “cross ratios” and the Fock–Goncharov cluster coordinates, both of which are positive on the Hitchin component.

Worked Example: Verifying the Anosov Property for a Hitchin Representation
Let ρ : π₁(S) → PSL(3,ℝ) be a Hitchin representation. Show that ρ is P₁‑Anosov by constructing the boundary map ξ : ∂∞π₁(S) → ℝℙ² and verifying transversality.
A Hitchin representation into PSL(3,ℝ) arises from a Higgs bundle (E, φ) with E = K1 ⊕ O ⊕ K-1 and a cyclic Higgs field. The spectral curve Σ is a three‑fold cover of S. Labourie’s construction associates to each point x ∈ ∂∞π₁(S) the limiting flag of the parallel transport of the flat connection along a geodesic ray ending at x. The line component of this flag gives a map ξ₁ : ∂∞π₁(S) → ℝℙ². Because the Higgs field is cyclic, the eigenvalues of φ are distinct and ordered along the geodesic flow, which forces the limiting line to vary continuously and to be transverse to the limiting plane for distinct boundary points. Hence ξ₁ is a continuous, ρ‑equivariant, transverse boundary map, proving that ρ is P₁‑Anosov.

Write down the relevant parabolic P (e.g. P₁ for PSL(n,ℝ)) and state that the boundary map lands in G/P. Mention Labourie’s theorem for PSL(n,ℝ) and Guichard–Wienhard for general split groups; this distinction often separates full‑credit from partial answers.

Cluster Varieties and the Fock–Goncharov Duality

Fock and Goncharov constructed two dual cluster varieties attached to a marked surface (S, M) and a split semisimple group G: the A‑variety 𝒜G,S parametrizing G‑local systems with a framing at the punctures, and the X‑variety 𝒳G,S parametrizing G‑local systems with a decoration (a reduction to the Borel at each puncture). The cluster coordinates on 𝒜 are called cluster A‑coordinates (or lambda‑lengths), while those on 𝒳 are cluster X‑coordinates (or shear coordinates). The two varieties are related by a canonical Poisson map p : 𝒜 → 𝒳 which, on the level of coordinates, sends a product of A‑coordinates around a vertex to the corresponding X‑coordinate. This duality generalizes the classical relationship between Penner’s lambda‑lengths and Thurston’s shear coordinates for PSL(2,ℝ). The positive real locus 𝒜(ℝ>0) is precisely the higher Teichmüller space (the Hitchin component for G = SL(n,ℝ)), while 𝒳(ℝ>0) is the space of positive representations in the sense of Fock–Goncharov. The cluster modular group, generated by mutations at the vertices of an ideal triangulation, acts by cluster transformations on both varieties and coincides with the mapping class group action on the moduli space of local systems. The symplectic form on 𝒳 (the Atiyah–Bott–Goldman form) is log‑canonical in X‑coordinates, and the pullback to 𝒜 yields the canonical Poisson structure used in the quantization programme.

Quantum Trace Maps and Skein Algebras

The quantum trace map, developed by Bonahon, Wong, and later by Allegretti–Kim, provides a homomorphism from the stated skein algebra 𝒮_ω(S, M) of a marked surface to the quantum torus algebra associated to the cluster X‑variety 𝒳G,S. For G = SL(n,ℂ), the skein algebra is generated by isotopy classes of framed links in S × (0,1) with endpoints on the marked points, modulo the Kauffman bracket relations at a root of unity ω = exp(π i / r). The quantum trace sends a link diagram to a Laurent polynomial in the quantum X‑coordinates, which are non‑commuting variables satisfying X_i X_j = ω2εij X_j X_i where εij is the exchange matrix of the cluster seed. This map is injective for generic ω and intertwines the mapping class group action on the skein algebra with the quantum cluster modular group action on the quantum torus. As a consequence, the quantum trace yields a concrete representation of the skein algebra on a Hilbert space obtained by quantizing the symplectic form on 𝒳. In the classical limit ω → 1, the quantum trace reduces to the classical trace‑of‑holonomy functions, recovering the Goldman bracket on the character variety. The construction has been extended to arbitrary split groups by using the Fock–Goncharov quantum cluster algebras, providing a uniform framework for the representation theory of quantum groups at roots of unity and the topology of 3‑manifolds via the TQFT of Blanchet–Costantino–Geer–Patureau‑Mirand.

Boundary Maps, Flag Curves, and the Dynamics of Hitchin Representations

A central feature of Anosov representations is the existence of a continuous, dynamics‑preserving boundary map ξ : ∂∞π₁(S) → G/P. For Hitchin representations into PSL(n,ℝ), Labourie showed that ξ lifts to a map into the full flag variety Flag(ℝⁿ) whose components ξ_k (for k = 1,…,n−1) are nested: ξ₁(x) ⊂ ξ₂(x) ⊂ … ⊂ ξn-1(x). The curve ξ₁ : ∂∞π₁(S) → ℝℙn-1 is strictly convex and C¹, and its osculating flags give the higher components ξ_k. This flag curve is the geometric incarnation of the Higgs bundle data: the Higgs field φ determines a holomorphic projective structure on the universal cover, and the developing map of this structure coincides with ξ₁. The dynamics of the geodesic flow on T¹S act on the flag curve by contracting the forward flag and expanding the backward flag, which is precisely the Anosov splitting. Guichard–Wienhard proved that for any Anosov representation the boundary map is Hölder continuous and that the flag curve is the unique ρ‑equivariant curve with the transversality property. This dynamical rigidity implies that the Hitchin component is a closed subset of the character variety and that the mapping class group acts properly discontinuously on it. Moreover, the pressure metric introduced by Bridgeman–Canary–Labourie–Sambarino on the Hitchin component arises from the thermodynamic formalism of the geodesic flow twisted by the boundary map, giving a natural Riemannian metric with negative curvature properties.

  • Confusing the A‑variety and X‑variety roles: 𝒜 carries framed local systems, 𝒳 carries decorated ones; the positive locus of 𝒜 is the Hitchin component, while 𝒳(ℝ>0) is the space of positive representations.
  • Assuming every Anosov representation is Hitchin: Anosov is an open condition, but Hitchin components are specific connected components for split real groups; there exist Anosov representations outside Hitchin components (e.g. quasi‑Fuchsian in PSL(2,ℂ)).
  • Misidentifying the parabolic for the Anosov type: for PSL(n,ℝ) the Hitchin component is P₁‑Anosov, not Pn-1‑Anosov; the opposition involution swaps these.
  • Overlooking the role of the spectral curve in the Hitchin fibration: the generic fiber is the Prym variety of the spectral curve, not the Jacobian of the base surface.
  • Treating the quantum trace as a map to the commutative coordinate ring: the target is a quantum torus with non‑commuting variables; the classical limit recovers the Goldman bracket only after setting ω = 1.
  • Neglecting the positivity condition in cluster coordinates: the Hitchin component corresponds exactly to the locus where all cluster X‑coordinates are positive real numbers; negative or complex coordinates leave the component.
  • Remember that the boundary map for a Hitchin representation is uniquely determined and Hölder continuous, and its image is a strictly convex flag curve in the flag variety.

The Thermodynamic Formalism and Pressure Metric on Hitchin Components

The pressure metric introduced by Bridgeman, Canary, Labourie, and Sambarino provides a natural Riemannian metric on the Hitchin component Hitn(S) that arises from the thermodynamic formalism of the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle T1S twisted by the boundary map of the Hitchin representation. For a Hitchin representation ρ: π1(S) → PSL(n,ℝ), the boundary map ξ: ∂∞π1(S) → Flag(ℝn) yields a Hölder continuous potential function on the symbolic dynamics of the geodesic flow. The pressure of this potential, defined via the variational principle P(φ) = supμ (hμ + ∫ φ dμ) where the supremum runs over flow-invariant probability measures, is real-analytic in the representation. The second derivative of the pressure along a tangent direction gives the pressure metric. This metric is invariant under the mapping class group action, has negative curvature properties in the sense of Busemann, and its geodesics correspond to families of representations that are “pressure-minimizing”. Crucially, the pressure metric coincides with the Weil–Petersson metric when n = 2, but for n > 2 it is distinct from the Goldman symplectic form and the Liouville metric on the character variety. The construction uses the fact that the boundary map ξ1: ∂∞π1(S) → ℝℙn−1 is strictly convex and C1, which guarantees the Hölder regularity of the potential. The pressure metric has been used to prove that the mapping class group acts properly discontinuously on the Hitchin component and to study the geometry of the space of projective structures.

Higher Teichmüller Theory for Non-Split Real Groups and θ-Positive Representations

While the original Fock–Goncharov theory focuses on split real groups (e.g., SL(n,ℝ), Sp(2n,ℝ)), Guichard and Wienhard extended the notion of positivity to arbitrary real reductive groups using the concept of θ-positive representations. Let G be a real reductive algebraic group with Cartan involution θ, and let Pθ be the associated parabolic subgroup. A representation ρ: π1(S) → G is called θ-positive if it is Pθ-Anosov and its boundary map ξ: ∂∞π1(S) → G/Pθ takes values in the positive locus of the flag variety defined by the Lusztig total positivity. For split groups, θ is the opposition involution and Pθ is the minimal parabolic, recovering the usual Hitchin component. For non-split groups such as SO(p,q) with p ≠ q, the θ-positive representations form higher Teichmüller spaces that are not necessarily connected components of the character variety but are still open subsets with rich geometric structures. The parameter space of θ-positive representations admits a cluster algebra structure via the Fock–Goncharov duality for the Langlands dual group, and the positive locus corresponds to a union of cluster chambers. The theory also encompasses the maximal representations into Hermitian Lie groups (e.g., Sp(2n,ℝ), SU(p,q)) where the Toledo invariant provides a topological bound. In these cases, the θ-positive locus coincides with the space of maximal representations, which are known to be discrete and faithful. The dynamics of the boundary map for θ-positive representations is governed by a generalized flag curve that is transverse and dynamics-preserving, leading to a pressure metric and a thermodynamic formalism analogous to the split case.

Examiner's trap: distinguishing Anosov types
A representation ρ: π1(S) → PSL(3,ℝ) is P1-Anosov. Which of the following statements is correct? (a) ρ is necessarily a Hitchin representation. (b) ρ is P2-Anosov as well. (c) The boundary map lands in the Grassmannian of lines. (d) The representation is θ-positive for the split group with θ = id.
The correct answer is (c). For PSL(3,ℝ), the Hitchin component consists of P1-Anosov representations, where the boundary map ξ: ∂∞π1(S) → ℝℙ2 (the Grassmannian of lines) is strictly convex. Statement (a) is false because there exist P1-Anosov representations outside the Hitchin component (e.g., certain deformations of Fuchsian representations into the non-Hitchin components). Statement (b) is false: the opposition involution swaps P1 and P2; a Hitchin representation is P1-Anosov but not P2-Anosov unless it is Fuchsian (where the two coincide). Statement (d) is false because θ for the split group is the opposition involution, not the identity; θ-positive for split groups means positive in the sense of Fock–Goncharov, which is exactly the Hitchin component, but the condition is not θ = id.
Exam-day strategy: Anosov parabolic identification
When asked about the Anosov type of a Hitchin representation into PSL(n,ℝ), remember that the Hitchin component is P1-Anosov (stabilizer of a line). The boundary map goes to the projective space ℝℙn−1. The opposition involution sends P1 to Pn−1 (stabilizer of a hyperplane). Do not confuse the two; the flag curve is nested ξ1 ⊂ ξ2 ⊂ … ⊂ ξn−1, and the Anosov splitting contracts the forward flag (ξ1) and expands the backward flag (ξn−1).

The Goncharov–Shen Potential and the Geometry of the Moduli Space of Local Systems

Goncharov and Shen introduced a potential function W on the moduli space ℒG,S of framed G-local systems on a marked surface S, which plays a central role in the study of the cluster structure and the mirror symmetry of the moduli space. For G = SL(n), the potential is a sum of certain cluster X-coordinates associated to the vertices of a triangulation, specifically W = ∑i Xi where the sum runs over the internal edges of the triangulation. This potential is invariant under cluster mutations and defines a regular function on the A-variety 𝒜G,S. Its critical points correspond to local systems that admit a flat connection with prescribed monodromy around the punctures, and the potential governs the wall-crossing behavior of the Donaldson–Thomas invariants of the associated 3d Calabi–Yau category. In the context of the Hitchin component, the restriction of W to the positive locus 𝒜G,S(ℝ+) is a proper Morse function whose gradient flow lines define a cell decomposition of the Hitchin component. The potential also appears in the physics literature as the superpotential of the 3d 𝒩 = 2 theory associated to the surface, and its critical locus is the space of supersymmetric vacua. The Goncharov–Shen potential provides a bridge between the cluster algebra structure, the topology of the moduli space, and the spectral theory of the Hitchin system: the spectral curve of a Higgs bundle can be recovered as the zero locus of the characteristic polynomial of the Higgs field, and the potential W is the trace of the monodromy around the punctures. This connection is made precise via the Fock–Goncharov conjecture (proved for SL(n) by Goncharov–Shen and for general groups by various authors) that the cluster coordinates provide a canonical basis of the coordinate ring of the moduli space.

Wall-Crossing Formulas and Cluster Donaldson–Thomas Invariants

The wall-crossing phenomenon in the context of Higher Teichmüller Theory is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall-crossing formula, which describes how the Donaldson–Thomas invariants of the 3d Calabi–Yau category associated to a marked surface change as the stability condition varies. The cluster variety 𝒳G,S carries a natural Poisson structure, and its quantum version is a quantum torus algebra. The Donaldson–Thomas invariants Ω(γ; ζ) are integer invariants associated to charge vectors γ in the lattice of the cluster variety and a stability parameter ζ. As ζ crosses a wall of marginal stability, the invariants jump according to the product formula ∏γ Ψq(Xγ)Ω(γ; ζ) = 1, where Ψq is the quantum dilogarithm and the product is ordered by the phase of the central charge Z(γ). In the classical limit q → 1, this reduces to the classical wall-crossing formula for the Poisson algebra of functions on 𝒳. For the moduli space of local systems on a surface, the charges correspond to cycles on the spectral curve, and the wall-crossing formula computes the change in the BPS spectrum of the associated 4d 𝒩 = 2 theory. The cluster Donaldson–Thomas invariants have been explicitly computed for surfaces with punctures using the spectral network technique of Gaiotto, Moore, and Neitzke. These invariants are related to the coefficients of the Fock–Goncharov potential and to the Euler characteristics of the moduli spaces of stable Higgs bundles. The wall-crossing formula also implies the cluster mutation invariance of the quantum trace map, as the quantum trace intertwines the mapping class group action with the quantum cluster modular group action, which is generated by sequences of mutations corresponding to wall-crossing transformations.

The Role of Spectral Networks in the WKB Approximation and the Hitchin System

Spectral networks, introduced by Gaiotto, Moore, and Neitzke, provide a geometric tool to compute the BPS spectrum and the wall-crossing data for the Hitchin system on a Riemann surface. Given a Higgs bundle (E, φ) on a Riemann surface C, the spectral curve Σ ⊂ T*C is defined by det(λ − φ) = 0. A spectral network is a collection of trajectories on C, called walls, along which the phases of the periods of the Seiberg–Witten differential λ coincide. These walls are the projections of the WKB spectral networks from the spectral curve to the base. The network structure changes as the phase parameter varies, and the jumps in the network correspond to wall-crossing events. The spectral network determines the Fock–Goncharov coordinates on the moduli space of flat connections: the X-coordinates are given by the exponential of the periods of λ along cycles on the spectral curve, and the network provides a rule for computing these periods in terms of the topology of the surface. In the context of Higher Teichmüller Theory, the spectral network for a Hitchin representation gives a concrete realization of the positive coordinates: the walls of the network are precisely the edges of the ideal triangulation, and the X-coordinates are the cross-ratios of the flags at the vertices. The WKB approximation of the Hitchin equations (the harmonic metric equations for the Higgs bundle) is controlled by the spectral network, and the exact solution can be reconstructed via the Riemann–Hilbert problem associated to the network. This approach has led to a proof of the Fock–Goncharov conjecture for the positivity of the coordinates and to a geometric interpretation of the cluster transformation as a sequence of wall-crossings in the spectral network.

  • Confusing the pressure metric with the Goldman symplectic form: the pressure metric is Riemannian and arises from thermodynamic formalism, while the Goldman form is symplectic and comes from the cup product on group cohomology.
  • Assuming θ-positive representations for non-split groups are always connected components of the character variety: they are open subsets but may not be entire components; maximal representations into Hermitian groups are components, but other θ-positive spaces can be proper open subsets.
  • Misidentifying the Goncharov–Shen potential as a function on the X-variety: it is a function on the A-variety (framed local systems) and is a sum of X-coordinates, but it is not invariant under the full cluster modular group of the X-variety.
  • Thinking the wall-crossing formula only applies to the quantum cluster algebra: the classical wall-crossing formula governs the Poisson algebra of the X-variety and is the q → 1 limit of the quantum formula.
  • Believing spectral networks are defined only for SL(n): they exist for any reductive group via the cameral cover and the associated Seiberg–Witten differential.
  • Overlooking that the spectral network walls are not the same as the edges of the triangulation in general: they coincide only for the positive locus corresponding to the Hitchin component; for general local systems the network is more complicated.
  • Neglecting the role of the Seiberg–Witten differential in the WKB approximation: the spectral network is built from the differential λ on the spectral curve, and its periods give the exact coordinates, not just the asymptotic ones.

The Labourie–Loftin Theorem on Convex Projective Structures and Hitchin Representations

The Labourie–Loftin theorem establishes a precise equivalence between the Hitchin component for PSL(n,ℝ) and the space of marked convex real projective structures on a closed surface of genus g ≥ 2 when n = 3, and more generally identifies the Hitchin component for PSL(n,ℝ) with the space of convex projective structures equipped with a compatible holomorphic differential of degree n. Labourie proved that every Hitchin representation ρ: π₁(S) → PSL(n,ℝ) admits a unique ρ‑equivariant immersion of the universal cover into the projective space ℝℙⁿ⁻¹ whose image is a strictly convex hypersurface. Loftin extended this by showing that the data of such an immersion is equivalent to a pair consisting of a convex projective structure and a holomorphic differential of degree n on the underlying Riemann surface. The proof uses the theory of affine spheres: the Cheng–Yau theorem guarantees a unique hyperbolic affine sphere asymptotic to the convex cone over the immersed hypersurface, and the Blaschke metric on this affine sphere coincides with the hyperbolic metric determined by the Hitchin representation. The holomorphic differential arises as the cubic (or higher) differential measuring the difference between the Blaschke connection and the Levi‑Civita connection. This correspondence is a higher‑rank analogue of the classical uniformization theorem, where the Hitchin component plays the role of Teichmüller space and the holomorphic differential replaces the quadratic differential of the Schwarzian derivative. The theorem also implies that the Hitchin component is homeomorphic to a vector bundle of rank (2g‑2)(n²‑1) over Teichmüller space, confirming the topological picture predicted by Hitchin’s original component count.

The Geometry of the Moduli Space of Framed Local Systems and the A‑variety

The moduli space 𝒜 of framed G‑local systems on a decorated surface (S, M) carries a natural cluster Poisson variety structure introduced by Fock and Goncharov. A framed local system consists of a principal G‑bundle with flat connection together with a reduction to a Borel subgroup at each puncture or marked point. The cluster coordinates on 𝒜 are given by generalized cross‑ratios of flags associated to the vertices of an ideal triangulation. For G = SL(n), these coordinates are the ratios of minors of the parallel transport matrices along edges, and they satisfy the tropical Plücker relations. The space 𝒜 is a union of algebraic tori glued by cluster mutations, and its tropicalization 𝒜⁽ᵗʳᵒᵖ⁾ parametrizes higher laminations — integral points correspond to positive representations. The dual cluster variety 𝒳 (the moduli of unframed local systems) is obtained by a Hamiltonian reduction of 𝒜 with respect to the action of the torus H⁽ᴹ⁾ at the marked points. The Poisson bracket on 𝒳 is the Atiyah–Bott–Goldman bracket, while 𝒜 carries a compatible symplectic form whose leaves are the symplectic leaves of the Poisson structure on 𝒳. The cluster modular group, generated by mutations and the mapping class group action, acts by cluster transformations on both varieties. The Goncharov–Shen potential W on 𝒜 is a sum of certain X‑coordinates; its critical points correspond to opers, and its gradient flow gives a deformation retraction of 𝒜 onto the Hitchin component. This geometry underlies the quantization of moduli spaces via quantum cluster algebras and provides the framework for the spectral network description of the WKB approximation.

The Role of the Spectral Curve in the Non‑Abelian Hodge Correspondence

The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence identifies the moduli space of semistable Higgs bundles (E, φ) of rank n and degree zero on a compact Riemann surface C with the character variety of representations π₁(C) → GL(n,ℂ). The spectral curve Σ ⊂ T*C is defined by the characteristic equation det(λ − φ) = 0, where λ is the tautological 1‑form on the cotangent bundle. Σ is an n‑sheeted branched cover of C, and its geometry encodes the Hitchin fibration: the base of the fibration is the space of characteristic polynomials of φ, i.e. the space of holomorphic differentials (q₂, …, qₙ) with qₖ ∈ H⁰(C, Kᵏ). The fiber over a generic point is the Prym variety of Σ → C, parametrizing line bundles on Σ whose direct image yields the Higgs bundle. The spectral curve also controls the asymptotic behavior of solutions to Hitchin’s equations via the WKB method: the Seiberg–Witten differential λ|Σ gives the periods that determine the harmonic metric. In the context of Higher Teichmüller Theory, the Hitchin component corresponds to the real locus of the Hitchin fibration where all qₖ are real and the spectral curve is totally real (all branch points lie on the real axis of the cotangent bundle). The Labourie–Loftin theorem can be reinterpreted as saying that the convex projective structure arises from the real affine sphere asymptotic to the cone over the spectral curve. Moreover, the spectral network of Gaiotto–Moore–Neitzke is precisely the projection to C of the walls where the phases of periods of λ align; these walls coincide with the edges of the ideal triangulation exactly on the positive locus. Thus the spectral curve serves as the central geometric object linking Higgs bundles, flat connections, and cluster coordinates.

The Cluster Structure on the Moduli Space of Flat Connections and the Mapping Class Group Action

The moduli space 𝒳 of flat G‑connections on a punctured surface carries a canonical cluster Poisson structure. For G = SL(n), the X‑coordinates are the eigenvalues of the monodromy around punctures together with the generalized cross‑ratios of flags at the vertices of an ideal triangulation. The mapping class group MCG(S) acts on 𝒳 by changing the triangulation; this action is realized as a sequence of cluster mutations, i.e. the cluster modular group. The fundamental result of Fock–Goncharov and later of Shen–Weng is that the MCG action on the tropical points 𝒳⁽ᵗʳᵒᵖ⁾(ℤ) coincides with the action on higher laminations, and the induced action on the cluster algebra is by cluster automorphisms. This provides a combinatorial model for the dynamics of MCG on the character variety. In particular, the action on the Hitchin component is properly discontinuous and preserves the Goldman symplectic form, while the pressure metric (from thermodynamic formalism) is MCG‑invariant and Kähler. The cluster structure also yields a quantization: the quantum cluster algebra gives a representation of the mapping class group on a Hilbert space of conformal blocks, and the quantum trace map intertwines the skein algebra of the surface with the quantum cluster algebra. The wall‑crossing formula of Kontsevich–Soibelman governs the change of quantum cluster coordinates under mutations, and its classical limit reproduces the Poisson bracket on 𝒳. This interplay between cluster algebras, mapping class groups, and quantization is a cornerstone of modern Higher Teichmüller Theory.

The Compactification of Hitchin Components via Projective Structures and the Labourie Compactification

Labourie constructed a compactification of the Hitchin component Hitₙ(S) for PSL(n,ℝ) by embedding it into the space of projective structures on the surface. The idea is to associate to each Hitchin representation ρ a ρ‑equivariant convex curve ξ: ∂∞π₁(S) → ℝℙⁿ⁻¹, which extends to a continuous map on the Thurston compactification of Teichmüller space. The limit points correspond to degenerate projective structures where the convex curve develops flat segments or cusps. This compactification is homeomorphic to a closed ball of dimension (2g‑2)(n²‑1) and its boundary is stratified by the combinatorics of the limiting flag curve. An alternative compactification, due to Guichard–Wienhard, uses the theory of Anosov representations: the Hitchin component is a union of connected components of Anosov representations, and the closure in the character variety adds representations that are limits of Anosov ones but may fail to be Anosov themselves. The two compactifications are related by the fact that the flag curve of an Anosov representation extends continuously to the boundary, and the limiting flags satisfy the transversality conditions defining the Labourie boundary strata. The compactification is crucial for understanding the dynamics of the mapping class group: the action extends continuously to the boundary, and the north‑south dynamics on the space of projective classes of measured laminations can be read off from the limit flags. Moreover, the pressure metric extends to a continuous metric on the compactification, and its completion is a CAT(0) space. These compactifications provide the geometric framework for the ergodic theory of the MCG action on Hitchin components and for the study of the topology of the moduli space of higher Teichmüller spaces.

The Hitchin Component and the Geometric Langlands Correspondence

The Hitchin component for a split real group G sits inside the character variety Hom(π₁(S), G)//G as a union of connected components consisting entirely of discrete, faithful, and Anosov representations. In the geometric Langlands program, the same moduli space appears as the space of G-opers on a Riemann surface Σ. An oper is a G-bundle with a connection and a reduction to a Borel subgroup satisfying a transversality condition; the set of opers forms a Lagrangian subvariety of the Hitchin base. The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence identifies the Hitchin component with a component of the moduli space of Higgs bundles (E, Φ) where Φ is regular semisimple and the associated spectral curve is smooth. Under this identification, the Hitchin fibration becomes the map sending a Higgs bundle to the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of Φ, and the generic fibre is a Prym variety of the spectral cover. The geometric Langlands conjecture predicts a derived equivalence between D‑modules on the moduli stack of G-bundles and coherent sheaves on the moduli stack of LG‑local systems, where LG is the Langlands dual group. For split real groups, the Hitchin component provides a real slice of the complex moduli space where the correspondence can be tested analytically: the Labourie–Loftin theorem shows that for G=PSL(3,ℝ) the Hitchin component parametrises convex real projective structures, which correspond to opers for LG=SL(3,ℂ). In higher rank, the work of Fock–Goncharov and later of Gaiotto–Moore–Neitzke relates the cluster coordinates on the X-variety to the spectral networks that compute the WKB expansion of opers. This interplay makes the Hitchin component a laboratory where the geometric Langlands correspondence can be studied using cluster algebras, tropical geometry, and the thermodynamics of Anosov flows.

Higher Teichmüller Theory for Exceptional Lie Groups and the Freudenthal Triple System

While the split real forms of classical groups (SL(n,ℝ), Sp(2n,ℝ), SO(n,n)) have been the main focus, the theory extends to exceptional groups G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈. The key combinatorial object is the Freudenthal triple system attached to a Jordan algebra of degree three; for G₂ it is the split octonions, for F₄ the exceptional Jordan algebra of 3×3 Hermitian matrices over the split octonions, and so on. Positive representations of surface groups into these groups are defined by requiring that the flag curve ξ: ∂∞π₁(S) → 𝔽 (the variety of complete flags) takes values in the open G-orbit of pairwise transverse flags. Fock–Goncharov constructed cluster coordinates on the moduli space of framed local systems for any split group using the theory of total positivity in the sense of Lusztig. For exceptional groups, the cluster algebra is of infinite rank but the mutation pattern is encoded by the quiver of the corresponding Dynkin diagram with a frozen vertex for each puncture. The spectral data for a Hitchin representation into an exceptional group is a cameral cover rather than a spectral curve: the characteristic polynomial of the Higgs field takes values in the invariant polynomials of the Lie algebra, and the cameral cover is a Galois cover with Weyl group W as deck group. The Hitchin fibration for exceptional groups still has generic fibres that are Prym varieties of the cameral cover, and the integrable system is algebraically completely integrable. Recent work of Allegretti–Gaiotto–Moore–Neitzke shows that the spectral networks for exceptional groups compute the BPS spectrum of the associated 4d N=2 theory, linking higher Teichmüller theory to wall‑crossing and cluster Donaldson–Thomas invariants. The Labourie compactification for exceptional groups is less understood; however, the Guichard–Wienhard theory of θ‑positive representations provides a uniform framework where the boundary strata are described by the combinatorics of the associated spherical building.

The spectral curve defined by det(λ−Φ)=0 is not a cover of the base curve because the eigenvalues live in a non‑abelian Cartan subalgebra. Instead one considers the cameral cover, a Galois cover with Weyl group W(G₂) of order 12. The generic fibre of the Hitchin fibration is the Prym variety of this cameral cover, i.e. the connected component of the kernel of the norm map between Jacobians. This replaces the usual Prym of a spectral curve and reflects the fact that the integrable system is still completely integrable but with a non‑abelian spectral data.

The Tropical Compactification of the Moduli Space of Local Systems and Higher Laminations

The Fock–Goncharov X-variety carries a natural cluster Poisson structure. Its tropical points 𝒳⁽ᵗʳᵒᵖ⁾(ℤ) are integer‑valued functions on the set of cluster variables satisfying the tropical exchange relations. These tropical points are canonically identified with the space of higher laminations on the surface S, i.e. measured laminations in the flag variety 𝔽. A higher lamination assigns to each ideal triangle a tuple of non‑negative integers satisfying the tropical Plücker relations; the mapping class group acts by piecewise‑linear transformations induced by cluster mutations. The tropical compactification of the moduli space of local systems is obtained by adding the projectivised tropical points as a boundary at infinity. This compactification is homeomorphic to a closed ball and its strata correspond to the combinatorial types of higher laminations. The Labourie compactification of the Hitchin component embeds into this tropical compactification: the flag curve of a Hitchin representation determines a point in the interior, and its limits correspond to higher laminations that are limits of the associated flag curves. The pressure metric on the Hitchin component extends continuously to the tropical boundary, and its completion is a CAT(0) space. This tropical picture provides a combinatorial model for the dynamics of the mapping class group: the north‑south dynamics on the space of projective classes of higher laminations is exactly the action on the boundary of the tropical compactification. Moreover, the cluster Donaldson–Thomas invariants count BPS states in the associated 4d N=2 theory and their wall‑crossing is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman formula, which in the tropical limit reproduces the piecewise‑linear mutation action on higher laminations.

The Geometry of the Moduli Space of Parabolic Higgs Bundles and its Cluster Structure

When the surface S has punctures, one considers parabolic Higgs bundles (E, Φ) where Φ has prescribed residues at the punctures lying in fixed conjugacy classes of the Lie algebra. The moduli space of such objects, denoted ℳpar, carries a natural symplectic form (the Goldman form) and a cluster Poisson structure constructed by Fock–Goncharov using the monodromy data at the punctures. The cluster coordinates are of two types: A-coordinates on the moduli space of framed local systems (the A-variety) and X-coordinates on the moduli space of unframed local systems (the X-variety). The A-variety is a principal ℂ*‑bundle over the X-variety, and the cluster mutations on the A-side are the tropicalisations of the mutations on the X-side. For parabolic Higgs bundles, the residues of Φ determine the eigenvalues of the monodromy around each puncture; these eigenvalues become frozen variables in the cluster algebra. The mapping class group of the punctured surface acts by cluster automorphisms, and the quantum trace map identifies the skein algebra of the surface with the quantum cluster algebra. The wall‑crossing formula for the quantum cluster algebra describes how the quantum trace changes under mutations, and its classical limit gives the Poisson bracket on the X-variety. The parabolic Hitchin fibration sends a parabolic Higgs bundle to the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of Φ together with the fixed residues; the generic fibre is a Prym variety of the spectral curve with prescribed behaviour at the punctures. The cluster structure on ℳpar is compatible with the parabolic Hitchin fibration: the cluster variables are pull‑backs of functions on the Hitchin base together with functions on the Prym fibres. This compatibility is the geometric incarnation of the fact that the quantum Hitchin system is integrable and its separation of variables is provided by the cluster coordinates.

The Mapping Class Group Action on the Labourie Compactification and North–South Dynamics

Labourie constructed a compactification of the Hitchin component Hitₙ(S) for PSL(n,ℝ) by embedding it into the space of projective structures on S. Each Hitchin representation ρ admits a ρ‑equivariant convex curve ξ: ∂∞π₁(S) → ℝℙⁿ⁻¹. The closure of the set of such curves in the space of continuous maps ∂∞π₁(S) → ℝℙⁿ⁻¹ yields a compactification homeomorphic to a closed ball of dimension (2g−2)(n²−1). The boundary strata are indexed by the combinatorics of the limiting flag curve: a boundary point corresponds to a flag curve that may have flat segments or cusps, and the transversality condition is relaxed to a partial transversality. The mapping class group MCG(S) acts on Hitₙ(S) by precomposition, and this action extends continuously to the Labourie compactification. On the boundary, the action exhibits north‑south dynamics: for any pseudo‑Anosov element φ∈MCG(S), there are two fixed points ξ⁺, ξ⁻ on the boundary such that for any compact subset K of the interior, φᵏ(K) converges to ξ⁺ as k→+∞ and to ξ⁻ as k→−∞. These fixed points are the stable and unstable measured laminations of φ, viewed as higher laminations via the tropicalisation of the flag curve. The pressure metric, defined via the thermodynamic formalism of the reparametrised geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of the Hitchin component, is MCG‑invariant and extends to a continuous metric on the compactification. Its completion is a CAT(0) space, and the north‑south dynamics is realised by the action of φ on this CAT(0) space. The Guichard–Wienhard compactification using Anosov representations yields the same boundary strata, and the two compactifications are homeomorphic. This dynamical picture is essential for the ergodic theory of the MCG action on Hitchin components and for the study of the topology of the moduli space of higher Teichmüller spaces.

  • Assuming the tropical compactification coincides with the Labourie compactification for all groups: they agree on the Hitchin component for split real groups but differ for non‑split or θ‑positive components.
  • Thinking the pressure metric is defined on the whole character variety: it is only defined on the Hitchin component (or more generally on Anosov components) because it relies on the thermodynamic formalism of a reparametrised geodesic flow that exists only there.

  • Confusing the quantum trace map with the classical trace map: the quantum trace intertwines the skein algebra with the quantum cluster algebra, while the classical trace gives the Poisson structure on the X-variety.

The Space of Opers and the Drinfeld–Sokolov Reduction

An oper for a complex simple Lie group G on a smooth projective curve X is a triple (E, ∇, E_B) where E is a principal G‑bundle, ∇ a connection, and E_B a reduction of structure group to a Borel subgroup B ⊂ G satisfying a transversality condition: the induced map from the tangent bundle of X to the associated graded bundle of the adjoint bundle is an isomorphism. Beilinson and Drinfeld introduced opers in the 1990s as a geometric incarnation of the Drinfeld–Sokolov hierarchies, which are integrable systems obtained by Hamiltonian reduction of the affine Kac–Moody algebra associated to G. For the Langlands dual group ^L G, the moduli space of ^L G‑opers on X is canonically isomorphic to the Hitchin component Hit_G(X) when G is a split real form. This isomorphism identifies the oper connection with the uniformizing Higgs bundle (E, Φ) where Φ takes values in the principal nilpotent element of the Lie algebra. The Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction constructs a Poisson map from the space of connections on a trivial G‑bundle to the space of opers; its classical limit yields the Hitchin Hamiltonian system. In the case G = SL(n, ℂ), an oper is equivalent to a scalar differential operator of order n with prescribed leading coefficient, and the Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction reproduces the Gel'fand–Dickey bracket. The oper perspective provides a powerful tool for the geometric Langlands correspondence: the Hecke eigensheaf attached to a ^L G‑local system is expected to be supported on the oper locus. Recent work of Frenkel–Gross and others has extended the oper construction to irregular singularities, linking wild opers to the Stokes data of meromorphic connections.

Key Points

  • Opers encode a reduction to a Borel subgroup with a transversality condition that forces the connection to be in a canonical form.
  • The Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction is a Hamiltonian reduction of the affine Kac–Moody algebra producing the KdV‑type hierarchies; its phase space is the space of opers.
  • For split real groups, the Hitchin component is identified with the moduli space of opers for the Langlands dual group.
  • Irregular opers incorporate Stokes matrices and provide a bridge to wild character varieties.

Wild Character Varieties and Irregular Singularities in the Non‑Abelian Hodge Correspondence

Wild character varieties parametrize monodromy data of meromorphic connections on a curve with irregular singularities. Unlike the tame case where monodromy is given by a representation of the fundamental group, irregular singularities require Stokes matrices and formal monodromy to capture the asymptotic behavior of solutions. Boalch introduced wild character varieties in the early 2000s as symplectic manifolds obtained by Hamiltonian reduction of products of coadjoint orbits and Stokes data. The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence, extended by Sabbah, Mochizuki, and Biquard–Boalch, relates these wild character varieties to moduli spaces of irregular Higgs bundles — pairs (E, Φ) where Φ is a meromorphic Higgs field with poles of order ≥ 2. The irregular Riemann–Hilbert correspondence sends an irregular connection to its Stokes‑graded local system, and the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence identifies this with an irregular Higgs bundle via a harmonic metric with prescribed asymptotics. The resulting moduli space carries a natural hyperkähler structure; its complex symplectic form in one complex structure coincides with the Goldman symplectic form on the wild character variety. The Hitchin fibration extends to the irregular setting: the base is the space of coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of Φ together with the irregular type data, and the generic fiber is an abelian variety isogenous to a Prym variety of the spectral curve with prescribed behavior at the irregular punctures. This framework underlies the geometric Langlands program for ramified local systems and has applications to isomonodromic deformations and Painlevé equations.

Key Points

  • Wild character varieties incorporate Stokes matrices and formal monodromy to describe irregular singularities.
  • The irregular non‑abelian Hodge correspondence (Sabbah, Mochizuki) equates wild character varieties with moduli of irregular Higgs bundles.
  • The Hitchin fibration extends to the irregular case; fibers are Prym varieties of the spectral curve with irregular boundary conditions.
  • This theory provides the geometric setting for ramified geometric Langlands and isomonodromic deformations.

The Spectral Curve and the Geometric Langlands Correspondence for Classical Groups

For a classical group G = SL(n), SO(2n+1), Sp(2n), or SO(2n), the Hitchin fibration maps a Higgs bundle (E, Φ) to the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of Φ. The spectral curve Σ ⊂ T*X is defined by the equation det(η − Φ) = 0 in the total space of the cotangent bundle; it is an n‑fold (or 2n‑fold) branched cover of X. The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence identifies the moduli space of G‑Higgs bundles with the character variety of representations of π1(X) into G(ℂ). Under this correspondence, the spectral curve encodes the eigenvalues of the Higgs field, and the fiber of the Hitchin fibration is (an open subset of) the Prym variety Prym(Σ/X) parametrizing line bundles on Σ with norm trivial on X. In the geometric Langlands program, the spectral curve appears as the support of the Hecke eigensheaf: for a ^L G‑local system, the associated D‑module on Bun_G is expected to be a Hecke eigensheaf whose eigenvalue is the given local system. For classical groups, the abelianization of the Hitchin system via the spectral curve provides a concrete realization of this correspondence: the Fourier–Mukai transform along the Hitchin fibration exchanges D‑modules on Bun_G with coherent sheaves on the moduli space of ^L G‑local systems, and the spectral curve is the Lagrangian subvariety where the transform is supported. The work of Donagi–Pantev, Ngô, and others has established the fundamental lemma and the geometric Langlands correspondence for certain cases using the spectral curve and the Hitchin fibration. The spectral curve also governs the wall‑crossing behavior of the BPS spectrum in the associated N=2 supersymmetric gauge theory, linking the geometric Langlands program to physics.

Key Points

  • The spectral curve is the branched cover defined by the characteristic polynomial of the Higgs field; its genus grows with the rank and the degree of the Higgs field.
  • The Hitchin fiber is a Prym variety of the spectral curve, providing an algebraic completely integrable system.
  • In geometric Langlands, the spectral curve is the support of the Hecke eigensheaf under the Fourier–Mukai transform.
  • For classical groups, the spectral curve is a single polynomial equation; for exceptional groups one uses the cameral cover instead.

The Dynamics of the Mapping Class Group on the Space of Positive Representations

The space of positive representations Pos_G(S) for a split real group G, introduced by Fock and Goncharov, is a connected component of the character variety Hom(π1(S), G)/G consisting entirely of positive representations in the sense of Lusztig's total positivity. The mapping class group MCG(S) acts on Pos_G(S) by precomposition, and this action is realized combinatorially by cluster mutations on the Fock–Goncharov X‑variety. Labourie proved that for G = PSL(n, ℝ) the Hitchin component coincides with Pos_G(S), and Guichard–Wienhard extended the Anosov property to all positive representations. The action of a pseudo‑Anosov element φ ∈ MCG(S) on Pos_G(S) exhibits north‑south dynamics on the Thurston compactification of the space of projective measured laminations: there exist unique attracting and repelling fixed points ξ+, ξ- in the boundary of the positive representation space, corresponding to the stable and unstable measured laminations of φ. The Cluster Structure on the Moduli Space of Local Systems for Affine Kac‑Moody Groups

The Pressure Metric on the Hitchin Component and its Relation to the Weil-Petersson Metric

The pressure metric on the Hitchin component Hit_G(Σ) is defined using the thermodynamic formalism for the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of the symmetric space X = G/K. The topological pressure of this flow yields a function P: Hit_G(Σ) → ℝ, and the Hessian of P at a point ρ defines a Riemannian metric g_P(ρ). This metric is Mod(Σ)‑invariant, real‑analytic, and CAT(0). On the Fuchsian locus (the image of the principal embedding of Teichmüller space into Hit_G(Σ)), the pressure metric coincides with a constant multiple of the Weil–Petersson metric. The constant depends on the choice of normalisation of the length functional; with the standard normalisation L_ρ(γ) = log λ₁(ρ(γ)), the pressure metric equals (dim G / rank G) times the Weil–Petersson metric. Away from the Fuchsian locus, the pressure metric differs from the Weil–Petersson metric; its geodesics are not known to be unique, but the CAT(0) property guarantees uniqueness of geodesic segments. The pressure metric also admits a description via the Hessian of the Goncharov–Shen potential on the 𝒜‑variety: the potential is a convex function on the tropicalisation, and its Legendre transform yields the pressure metric. This connection explains why the pressure metric is compatible with the cluster structure and why the mapping class group acts by isometries. The pressure metric has been used to prove that the Hitchin component is a proper metric space and that the mapping class group action is properly discontinuous. Recent work shows that the pressure metric on the θ‑positive component for non‑split groups is also CAT(0) and coincides with the Weil–Petersson metric on the corresponding Fuchsian locus.

The Structure of Irregular Higgs Bundles and the Role of Stokes Data

While the non-abelian Hodge correspondence establishes a profound link between flat connections, Higgs bundles, and representations of surface groups, the classical theory primarily focuses on connections with regular singularities, or those that are smooth. However, a significant generalization involves irregular Higgs bundles, which correspond to flat connections with irregular singularities. These arise naturally in various contexts, including the study of differential equations with irregular singular points and in certain limits of the Hitchin system.

An irregular Higgs bundle on a Riemann surface Σ consists of a holomorphic vector bundle E, a holomorphic connection ∇ on E with poles, and a Higgs field φ: E → E ⊗ KΣ with poles, where KΣ is the canonical bundle. The irregularity manifests in the nature of these poles. At an irregular singular point p ∈ Σ, the connection ∇ cannot be formally diagonalized into a sum of simple poles. Instead, it requires a formal transformation that involves terms with negative powers of the local coordinate and possibly logarithmic terms. The crucial additional data for an irregular Higgs bundle is the Stokes structure. This structure arises from the non-uniqueness of formal solutions to the differential equation near an irregular singularity. While a formal solution can be constructed, its analytic continuation across different sectors around the singularity requires multiplication by specific "Stokes matrices" or "Stokes factors." These factors capture the exponential growth or decay of solutions in different directions in the complex plane.

The wild character variety, which is the moduli space of flat connections with irregular singularities, is thus parameterized not just by the formal type of the singularity (e.g., the eigenvalues of the residue matrix after formal reduction), but also by these Stokes matrices. The non-abelian Hodge correspondence extends to this irregular setting, establishing an equivalence between the moduli space of irregular Higgs bundles with Stokes data and the moduli space of wild G-local systems (or wild character varieties). The Hitchin fibration also generalizes to this context, mapping irregular Higgs bundles to a spectral curve, which is now a branched cover of Σ with additional data related to the irregular part of the Higgs field. The fibers of this generalized Hitchin fibration are still abelian varieties, but their construction and properties are more intricate due to the Stokes data. This framework is particularly relevant for understanding the WKB approximation for differential equations, where the spectral curve emerges as the curve on which the WKB solutions are well-defined, and wall-crossing phenomena are governed by the Stokes factors.

The geometry of these moduli spaces is richer and often non-compact, reflecting the additional degrees of freedom introduced by the irregular singularities. The study of their compactifications, often involving parabolic structures at infinity or specific types of degenerations, is an active area. Furthermore, the connection to cluster algebras and quantum invariants extends to the irregular setting, where the cluster coordinates can be adapted to parameterize the Stokes data, leading to a deeper understanding of the quantization of these wild character varieties.

The Hitchin System as a Completely Integrable System

The Hitchin system, at its core, is a remarkable example of a completely integrable system arising from geometry. Its integrability is a fundamental property that underpins much of its rich structure and its connections to various fields of mathematics and physics. A Hamiltonian system is said to be completely integrable if it possesses a sufficient number of independent, Poisson-commuting conserved quantities (Hamiltonians) such that the system can be solved by quadratures.

For the Hitchin system, defined on the moduli space of Higgs bundles MHiggs(G, Σ), the phase space is endowed with a natural symplectic structure. The Hamiltonians are constructed from the Higgs field φ. Specifically, for a Lie algebra g, the characteristic polynomial of the Higgs field φ: E → E ⊗ KΣ, where E is a principal G-bundle, yields a collection of invariant polynomials. These polynomials, when evaluated on the Higgs field, produce sections of powers of the canonical bundle KΣ. Integrating these sections over the surface Σ gives rise to functions on the moduli space MHiggs(G, Σ). These functions are precisely the commuting Hamiltonians of the Hitchin system.

More formally, let Pk be a basis of invariant polynomials on g. Then for a Higgs field φ, Pk(φ) is a section of Symk(g) ⊗ KΣk. Using the Killing form to identify g with g*, these can be viewed as sections of KΣk. The coefficients of the characteristic polynomial det(λI - φ) are precisely these invariant polynomials. The Hitchin fibration π: MHiggs(G, Σ) → B is defined by mapping a Higgs bundle (E, φ) to the collection of these invariant polynomials, which form a point in the base space B = ⨁k H0(Σ, KΣk). The fibers of this map are the abelian varieties (Jacobians of spectral curves) that parameterize the solutions to the Hitchin system.

The crucial property is that these Hamiltonians commute with respect to the natural Poisson bracket on MHiggs(G, Σ). The existence of these commuting Hamiltonians, whose number equals half the dimension of the moduli space, ensures the complete integrability of the system. The dynamics generated by these Hamiltonians can be explicitly solved by linearizing the flow on the Jacobian variety associated with the spectral curve. The spectral curve itself, defined by the characteristic equation det(λI - φ) = 0, plays a central role as it encodes all the necessary information about the system's integrals of motion and the geometry of its solutions.

The Hitchin system provides a deep connection between differential geometry, algebraic geometry, and integrable systems. Its quantization leads to quantum integrable systems, and its geometric properties are essential for understanding the geometric Langlands correspondence. The construction of the Hamiltonians and the demonstration of their commutativity are non-trivial and rely on sophisticated techniques from Lie theory and differential geometry, highlighting the profound mathematical structure underlying Higher Teichmüller Theory.

Understanding the Spectral Curve in the Hitchin System
Consider the Hitchin system for G = SL(2, ℝ) on a Riemann surface Σ. The Higgs field is a traceless 2×2 matrix of 1-forms, φ = [[a, b], [c, -a]] ⊗ KΣ. The characteristic polynomial is det(λI - φ) = λ² - tr(φ)²/4 - det(φ) = λ² - det(φ). Since φ is traceless, tr(φ) = 0. The invariant polynomial is det(φ) = -a² - bc. This is a quadratic differential, a section of KΣ2. The spectral curve S is defined by the equation λ² = det(φ). For G = SL(2, ℝ), the Hitchin system has a single independent Hamiltonian, which is the integral of the quadratic differential q = det(φ) over the surface Σ, i.e., H = ∫Σ q. The spectral curve S is a double cover of Σ, branched at the zeros of q. The equation λ² = q defines S as a subvariety of the total space of KΣ. The Hamiltonians of the Hitchin system are precisely the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of the Higgs field. In this SL(2) case, there is only one such coefficient (up to normalization), which is the quadratic differential q. The integral of q over Σ serves as the single Hamiltonian. The spectral curve S is crucial because its Jacobian variety, Jac(S), provides the phase space for the linearized flow of the Hitchin system. The dimension of Jac(S) is the genus of S. The genus of S is related to the genus of Σ and the number of zeros of q by the Riemann-Hurwitz formula. For SL(2, ℝ), the dimension of the moduli space of Higgs bundles is 6g-6 (for g ≥ 2). The number of independent Hamiltonians is 3g-3, which is half the dimension. The genus of the spectral curve S is g(S) = 2g-2 + 1 = 2g-1 if q has no zeros, or more generally, g(S) = 2g-2 + (number of zeros of q)/2. The dimension of the fiber of the Hitchin fibration, which is Jac(S), is g(S). The number of independent Hamiltonians required for complete integrability is 3g-3. The spectral curve's genus determines the dimension of the torus on which the system linearizes, thus directly reflecting the number of degrees of freedom and the integrability of the system.
Distinguishing Integrability from Solvability
While complete integrability implies the system can be solved by quadratures, this does not mean finding explicit closed-form solutions in terms of elementary functions is always straightforward. For the Hitchin system, "solving by quadratures" means reducing the dynamics to linear flows on abelian varieties (Jacobians of spectral curves). The challenge often lies in explicitly constructing these spectral curves and their Jacobians, and then inverting the Abel-Jacobi map to recover the original Higgs bundle data. Focus on understanding the *existence* of commuting Hamiltonians and the *geometric interpretation* of the spectral curve as the key to integrability, rather than memorizing specific solution formulas.

Higher Teichmüller Theory for Surfaces with Punctures and Boundary Components

The core of Higher Teichmüller Theory, particularly the study of Hitchin components and positive representations, extends naturally to surfaces with punctures and boundary components. These generalizations are crucial for applications in low-dimensional topology, quantum field theory, and the study of character varieties with prescribed monodromy. The presence of punctures and boundaries introduces additional structures and constraints that modify the definitions and properties of the relevant moduli spaces.

For a surface Σ with punctures, the notion of a G-local system (or flat G-connection) must be refined to specify the behavior of the connection near each puncture. This is typically done by considering parabolic structures at the punctures. A parabolic structure at a puncture p consists of a filtration of the fiber of the vector bundle E at p, together with a choice of a nilpotent element in the Lie algebra g associated with the residue of the connection. This data specifies the "monodromy" around the puncture up to conjugacy. The moduli space of G-local systems with parabolic structures at punctures is then the appropriate generalization of the character variety for punctured surfaces.

Similarly, for Higgs bundles on a punctured surface, one considers parabolic Higgs bundles, where the Higgs field also has prescribed pole behavior at the punctures, compatible with the parabolic structure of the bundle. The non-abelian Hodge correspondence extends to this parabolic setting, establishing an equivalence between parabolic Higgs bundles and parabolic G-local systems. Hitchin components for punctured surfaces are then defined as connected components of these parabolic character varieties that exhibit properties analogous to the classical Hitchin components for closed surfaces, such as maximal Euler characteristic for the associated flat bundles.

When dealing with surfaces with boundary components, the situation becomes even more intricate. A common approach is to consider framed local systems, where the G-local system is equipped with additional data along the boundary, often a choice of a G-equivariant map from the boundary to a flag variety G/P. This "framing" provides boundary conditions for the flat connection. Alternatively, one can consider local systems whose monodromy along boundary components belongs to specific conjugacy classes or subgroups. The definition of positive representations must also be adapted; for instance, for surfaces with boundary, one might require the representation to map boundary loops to specific types of elements (e.g., positive elements in a certain sense) in the Lie group G.

The cluster algebra structures on these moduli spaces are particularly rich. For surfaces with punctures, the cluster coordinates naturally incorporate the parabolic data. For surfaces with boundary, the boundary arcs of triangulations play a crucial role in defining additional cluster variables, leading to the concept of "stated skein algebras" and their quantization. The dynamics of the mapping class group on these generalized Higher Teichmüller spaces also reflects the presence of punctures and boundaries, with specific actions on the parabolic data or boundary framings. The compactification theory, including the Labourie compactification and tropical compactifications, also extends, providing a framework to study the asymptotic behavior of representations as punctures or boundary components degenerate.

The Symplectic Geometry of Moduli Spaces of G-Local Systems

The moduli space of G-local systems on a surface Σ, often denoted Mflat(G, Σ) or the character variety XG(Σ), possesses a rich geometric structure, most notably a natural symplectic form. This symplectic structure is fundamental to understanding the Hitchin system as an integrable system and provides a deep connection to other areas of geometry and topology.

The existence of a symplectic form on Mflat(G, Σ) can be understood through several perspectives. One approach involves the Atiyah-Bott symplectic form on the moduli space of flat connections. A flat G-connection on a principal G-bundle P over Σ can be represented by a Lie algebra-valued 1-form A, satisfying the flatness condition dA + [A, A] = 0. The moduli space of flat connections is obtained by quotienting the space of all flat connections by the action of the gauge group G. This moduli space carries a natural symplectic form, which is essentially given by the integral of the Killing form applied to the curvature of the connection. More precisely, if A1 and A2 are two flat connections, and δA1, δA2 are tangent vectors at A, then the symplectic form Ω is given by Ω(δA1, δA2) = ∫Σ ⟨δA1 ∧ δA2⟩, where ⟨⋅, ⋅⟩ is the Killing form on g.

This symplectic form is non-degenerate and invariant under the action of the mapping class group of Σ. The dimension of Mflat(G, Σ) is 2(dim G)(g-1) for a closed surface of genus g ≥ 2, indicating it is a symplectic manifold. The symplectic structure is crucial because it defines the Poisson bracket, which is essential for the formulation of the Hitchin system as a completely integrable Hamiltonian system. The Hamiltonians of the Hitchin system, derived from the characteristic polynomial of the Higgs field, are functions on the moduli space of Higgs bundles, which is symplectomorphic to the moduli space of flat connections via the non-abelian Hodge correspondence. The commutativity of these Hamiltonians is with respect to this Poisson bracket.

For surfaces with punctures, the symplectic form can be generalized to the moduli space of flat connections with parabolic structures. The boundary conditions at punctures contribute additional terms to the symplectic form, often related to the moment maps for the gauge group action at the punctures. This leads to a description of the character variety as a symplectic reduction of a larger space. The study of the symplectic geometry of these moduli spaces is also central to understanding the quantization of character varieties, where the symplectic form gives rise to a canonical commutation relation in the quantum theory. The geometric quantization program seeks to construct a Hilbert space of states from the symplectic manifold, and for the moduli space of flat connections, this leads to the construction of conformal blocks in 2D conformal field theory and the representation theory of quantum groups.

The symplectic geometry also plays a role in the definition of the pressure metric on Hitchin components, which is a metric compatible with the symplectic structure and provides a geometric tool for studying the dynamics of the mapping class group.

Common Traps

  • Confusing Regular vs. Irregular Singularities: Assuming all singularities in flat connections are simple poles (regular). Irregular singularities involve higher-order poles and require additional data (Stokes factors) to fully characterize, leading to a richer moduli space.
  • Misinterpreting Complete Integrability: Believing that "completely integrable" means explicit closed-form solutions are always readily available. It means the system can be solved by quadratures, often involving linearization on abelian varieties (Jacobians), which can still be complex to compute explicitly.
  • Neglecting Puncture/Boundary Data: Applying definitions of Higher Teichmüller Theory for closed surfaces directly to punctured or boundary surfaces without accounting for parabolic structures at punctures or boundary conditions/framings, which significantly alter the moduli spaces and representation properties.
  • Overlooking the Symplectic Structure's Origin: Thinking the symplectic form on the moduli space of local systems is an ad-hoc addition. It arises naturally from the geometry of flat connections (Atiyah-Bott form) and is fundamental for defining the Poisson bracket and the Hamiltonian structure of the Hitchin system.
  • Simplifying Spectral Curve Genus: Assuming the genus of the spectral curve is always a simple multiple of the surface genus. It depends on the rank of the Lie group and the number/order of zeros of the invariant differentials, often calculated via Riemann-Hurwitz for branched covers.
  • Ignoring the Role of the Mapping Class Group: Forgetting that the mapping class group acts on these moduli spaces and preserves their symplectic structure, leading to crucial dynamics and invariants, especially in the context of compactifications and pressure metrics.
The advanced topics above illustrate how the foundational concepts of Higher Teichmüller Theory extend and deepen when considering more complex geometric settings and the underlying mathematical structures.

The Freudenthal Triple System and the Geometry of Exceptional Hitchin Components

The Hitchin component for a split real simple Lie group G is a distinguished connected component of the character variety Hom(π₁(Σ), G)//G that consists entirely of discrete, faithful, and positively embedded representations. For classical groups this component is well understood via Higgs bundles and the associated spectral curves. When G is an exceptional group — G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, or E₈ — the lack of a standard matrix representation forces a different algebraic framework. The Freudenthal triple system (FTS) provides precisely this framework. An FTS is a vector space V equipped with a trilinear product {·,·,·} and a symmetric bilinear form ⟨·,·⟩ satisfying the Freudenthal identity. For each exceptional Lie algebra there exists a unique (up to isomorphism) FTS whose automorphism group is the corresponding exceptional group. The construction of the Hitchin component for exceptional G proceeds by interpreting a Hitchin representation as a flat connection with values in the Lie algebra g. Using the FTS, one can write down a set of polynomial invariants — the fundamental invariants of g — that play the role of the characteristic coefficients in the classical case. These invariants define a Hitchin base, an affine space of differentials on Σ whose dimension equals the rank of g times (2g−2) plus contributions from punctures. The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence then identifies the Hitchin component with a section of the Hitchin fibration over this base, where the fibre is an abelian variety (a generalized Prym variety) built from the spectral data encoded in the FTS. Crucially, the FTS also governs the Poisson structure on the moduli space of flat connections: the Goldman bracket translates into a natural bracket on the space of FTS‑valued differentials. This makes the exceptional Hitchin system a completely integrable Hamiltonian system in the same sense as for classical groups, but with the spectral curve replaced by a spectral cover defined by the FTS invariants. Recent work of Alessandrini, Collier, and others has shown that the Labourie–Loftin parametrisation of the Hitchin component via convex projective structures extends to G₂ using the FTS, and that the pressure metric on the exceptional Hitchin component inherits a CAT(0) property from the geometry of the associated symmetric space. Understanding the FTS is therefore essential for any unified treatment of Higher Teichmüller theory across all split real simple Lie groups.

Spectral Networks and the Construction of Coordinates on the Moduli Space of Flat Connections

Spectral networks, introduced by Gaiotto, Moore, and Neitzke in the context of 𝒩=2 supersymmetric gauge theories, provide a powerful combinatorial tool for constructing explicit Darboux coordinates on the moduli space M₍flat₎(G, Σ) of flat G‑connections on a punctured surface Σ. A spectral network is a graph drawn on Σ whose edges are trajectories of a quadratic differential (or higher‑order differential) determined by a point in the Hitchin base. The vertices correspond to branch points of the spectral cover, and the edges are labelled by charges in the weight lattice of G. The key insight is that the parallel transport of the flat connection along these trajectories yields a set of functions — the Fock–Goncharov cluster coordinates — that satisfy the tropicalized version of the cluster exchange relations. For a given Hitchin component, one chooses a generic point in the Hitchin base so that the associated spectral cover is smooth and the network is finite. The resulting coordinates are rational functions of the monodromy data and provide a global parametrisation of the component. Moreover, the network encodes the wall‑crossing behaviour of these coordinates: as the point in the Hitchin base crosses a wall of marginal stability, the network undergoes a local transformation (a flip) that implements the cluster mutation. This gives a geometric realisation of the cluster algebra structure on the moduli space of framed local systems discovered by Fock and Goncharov. In the case of higher‑rank groups, the spectral network is replaced by a spectral network of higher order, whose edges are labelled by representations of the Langlands dual group. The construction also extends to irregular singularities, where the network includes Stokes rays and the coordinates become the Stokes factors. The practical upshot for computations is that one can read off the symplectic form on M₍flat₎ directly from the intersection pairing of the network edges, and the mapping class group action becomes a sequence of network mutations. This combinatorial picture has been used to prove the positivity of the cluster coordinates for all split real groups and to derive explicit formulas for the quantum trace map in terms of network counting.

Identifying the correct spectral network for a given Hitchin component
Consider the Hitchin component for G = SL(3, ℝ) on a once‑punctured torus. The Hitchin base is spanned by a quadratic differential φ₂ and a cubic differential φ₃. The edges come in three pairs related by the ℤ/3 deck transformation. The cluster coordinates are obtained by taking the parallel transport of the flat connection along each edge. The exchange relations among these coordinates are exactly the cluster mutations that occur when φ₂ or φ₃ is varied so that two branch points collide — this corresponds to a flip of the network where one edge is replaced by the other diagonal of the quadrilateral formed by the four branch points. The Quantization of the Hitchin System via Deformation Quantization and the Quantum Spectral Curve

The Hitchin integrable system is a completely integrable Hamiltonian system on the moduli space of Higgs bundles M₍Higgs₎(G, Σ). Its classical Hamiltonians are the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of the Higgs field, which Poisson‑commute with respect to the natural symplectic form (the Atiyah–Bott form). Quantization of this system can be approached in two complementary ways: via quantum cluster algebras, as developed by Fock–Goncharov and later by Allegretti–Kimura, and via deformation quantization of the algebra of functions on M₍Higgs₎. In the deformation quantization approach, one replaces the commutative algebra of polynomial functions on the Hitchin base by a non‑commutative algebra generated by the quantum Hamiltonians, whose commutation relations are dictated by the Planck constant ℏ. The central object is the quantum spectral curve, a non‑commutative deformation of the classical spectral curve defined by the equation det(λ − Φ(z)) = 0, where Φ(z) is the Higgs field. In the quantum setting, λ and z become operators satisfying [λ, z] = ℏ, and the characteristic polynomial becomes an operator equation (the quantum curve) whose solutions are wave functions. The topological recursion of Eynard–Orantin, applied to the quantum spectral curve, produces the ℏ‑expansion of the partition function of the Hitchin system, which coincides with the generating function of quantum cluster mutations. For simply‑laced groups, the quantum curve can be written explicitly using the quantum W‑algebra associated to the Langlands dual group. The eigenvalues of the quantum Hamiltonians give the spectrum of the corresponding quantum integrable system (e.g. the Gaudin model for SL(n)). Recent work of Dumitrescu–Fredrickson–Kydonakis has shown that the deformation quantization of the Hitchin moduli space is equivalent to the geometric quantization of the moduli space of flat connections via the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence, and that the resulting Hilbert space carries a projective representation of the mapping class group. This provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for the physical prediction that the Hitchin system quantizes to a topological quantum field theory whose states are conformal blocks.

Higher Laminations on Orbifolds and their Intersection Theory

Thurston’s theory of measured laminations on surfaces extends naturally to higher rank via the notion of higher laminations introduced by Fock and Goncharov. For a split real simple Lie group G, a higher lamination on a surface Σ is a collection of tropical points of the moduli space of framed G‑local systems, i.e. a point in the tropicalization of the 𝒜‑variety. When Σ is an orbifold (a surface with cone points of finite order), the definition must be modified to account for the monodromy around the cone points. The orbifold fundamental group π₁^orb(Σ) has additional relations that force the monodromy around a cone point of order m to be an element of order dividing m in G. In the tropical setting, this translates into linear constraints on the tropical coordinates: the sum of the coordinates around the cone point must lie in a certain sub‑lattice determined by the representation theory of the cyclic group ℤ/mℤ. The space of higher laminations on an orbifold is therefore a rational polyhedral cone inside the tropical 𝒜‑variety of the underlying surface. The intersection pairing of two higher laminations is defined by the tropicalization of the symplectic form on the moduli space; it is a piecewise‑linear, symmetric, and non‑degenerate form on the cone. For classical groups, this pairing can be computed explicitly using the tropical exchange matrices of the associated cluster algebra. In the case of orbifolds, the pairing acquires correction terms coming from the cone points, which can be interpreted as the contribution of the orbifold Euler characteristic. The resulting intersection theory has been used by Allegretti and others to define a compactification of the moduli space of G‑local systems on orbifolds by adding the projectivized space of higher laminations as a boundary. This compactification is compatible with the mapping class group action of the orbifold and provides a geometric realisation of the tropical compactification of the 𝒜‑variety. Moreover, the intersection pairing on orbifold higher laminations satisfies a version of the Thurston inequality, which bounds the intersection number by the product of the lengths of the laminations measured with respect to any Hitchin metric.

The Compactification of the Moduli Space of Local Systems via Tropical Geometry and the Fock–Goncharov Compactification

The moduli space M₍flat₎(G, Σ) of flat G‑connections on a surface Σ is non‑compact. Several compactifications have been proposed: the Labourie compactification using projective structures, the tropical compactification using higher laminations, and the Fock–Goncharov compactification using the cluster structure on the 𝒜‑variety. The tropical compactification proceeds by embedding M₍flat₎ into a toric variety associated to the cluster algebra of the 𝒜‑variety, and then taking the closure. The boundary strata are indexed by the cones of the tropicalization of the 𝒜‑variety, which are precisely the spaces of higher laminations. The Fock–Goncharov compactification is more refined: it uses the positive atlas of the 𝒜‑variety to glue together copies of the positive real torus (ℝ₊)ⁿ along the cluster mutations, and then adds the boundary points corresponding to the tropical points. The resulting space is a manifold with corners whose interior is the positive part of M₍flat₎ (the Hitchin component for split G) and whose boundary is a union of lower‑dimensional positive spaces associated to subsurfaces and Levi subgroups. The two compactifications coincide on the positive part, but the tropical compactification is defined for the whole character variety, while the Fock–Goncharov compactification is naturally defined only for the positive part. Recent work of Goncharov–Shen and of Allegretti–Bridgeland has shown that the tropical compactification can be endowed with a natural piecewise‑linear structure that makes it homeomorphic to the Labourie compactification when restricted to the Hitchin component. The key technical tool is the tropicalization of the cluster ensemble map between the 𝒜‑ and 𝒳‑varieties, which identifies the boundary strata of the two compactifications. This identification has profound consequences for the dynamics of the mapping class group: the action extends continuously to the boundary, and the north‑south dynamics on the space of higher laminations can be read off from the action on the tropical points. Furthermore, the pressure metric on the Hitchin component extends to a CAT(0) metric on the tropical compactification, providing a geometric framework for studying the asymptotic geometry of the mapping class group action.
