What is Critical Reasoning?

Critical Reasoning questions assess your logical thinking and ability to evaluate arguments, assumptions, and conclusions. These questions appear in RBI Grade B phase 2 exams and test your analytical skills at a higher level. Common types include statement-assumption, statement-argument, statement-conclusion, cause-effect, and course of action.

Types of Critical Reasoning Questions

1. Statement-Assumption: A statement is given followed by two assumptions. You must determine which assumption(s) is/are implicit in the statement. An assumption is something that must be true for the statement to be valid.

2. Statement-Argument: A statement followed by two arguments (for and against). You evaluate the strength of each argument. A strong argument is directly related to the statement, logical, and substantial.

3. Statement-Conclusion: A passage is given followed by conclusions. You determine which conclusion(s) logically follow(s) from the information given.

4. Cause-Effect: Two events are given. You determine whether one is the cause of the other, both are effects of a common cause, or they are independent.

5. Course of Action: A problem situation is described, followed by proposed actions. You evaluate which action(s) is/are appropriate and practical.

6. Strengthening/Weakening Arguments: A conclusion is given with supporting evidence. You identify which option strengthens or weakens the argument.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Statement-Assumption)
Statement: 'The government should make voting compulsory to increase voter turnout.'
Assumptions:
I. Making voting compulsory will increase voter turnout.
II. Voter turnout currently is lower than desired.

a) Only I is implicit b) Only II is implicit c) Both are implicit d) Neither is implicit
Solution: c) Both are implicit. The statement suggests compulsory voting as a solution to increase turnout, which implies that voter turnout needs to increase (II) and that compulsory voting will achieve this (I).
Example 2 (Course of Action)
Situation: Waterlogging in the city after every heavy rainfall causes traffic jams and inconvenience to citizens.
Courses of Action:
I. The municipal corporation should clean and desilt the drainage system before the monsoon.
II. Citizens should be advised to stay indoors during heavy rainfall.

a) Only I follows b) Only II follows c) Both follow d) Neither follows
Solution: a) Only I follows. I is a practical and preventive action — cleaning drains addresses the root cause. II is impractical — citizens cannot stay indoors every time it rains heavily, and it does not solve the problem.
Example 3 (Cause-Effect)
Event A: The company's profits increased by 40% this quarter.
Event B: The company launched a new product line that became very popular.

a) A is the cause and B is the effect b) B is the cause and A is the effect c) Both are effects of a common cause d) No relationship
Solution: b) B is the cause and A is the effect. Launching a popular new product (B) likely led to increased sales and hence higher profits (A).

Shortcut Techniques

  • For statement-assumption: An assumption is implicit if its negation would make the statement invalid.
  • For cause-effect: Check temporality — the cause must precede the effect. Also check if there is a third factor causing both.
  • For course of action: The action must be practical, feasible, and address the root cause — not a temporary fix.
  • Read the question stem carefully — 'strong' vs 'weak' arguments require judgment about relevance and impact.
Pro Tip
In RBI Grade B, critical reasoning questions in the descriptive paper may ask you to write short essays evaluating an argument. Practice identifying logical fallacies — hasty generalization, false cause, circular reasoning — as they are often tested indirectly.

Advanced Problem Types

Competitive exams feature advanced variations combining multiple reasoning concepts. Master these to score high.

Multi-Step Problems

Some questions require applying multiple reasoning techniques sequentially. Break problems into clear steps.

Common Traps

  • Assuming a pattern continues without checking all elements
  • Misinterpreting negative statements in syllogisms
  • Forgetting wrapping in alphabet coding (Z to A transition)
  • Confusing directions (East vs West)
  • Overlooking either-or cases in syllogisms
  • Not considering all possible arrangements in puzzles
Pro Tip
For puzzles, start with the most concrete clue and build around it. Draw diagrams for seating arrangements and family trees for blood relations.

Structure of Complex Arguments: Premises, Sub‑Conclusions, and Hidden Links

In competitive examinations, arguments rarely appear as a single premise‑conclusion pair. A typical stimulus contains a reasoning chain where intermediate claims serve both as conclusions for earlier premises and as premises for later conclusions. Recognising this layered architecture is essential for answering assumption, inference, and flaw questions accurately. The first step is to map every declarative sentence and label it as a premise, a sub‑conclusion, or the main conclusion. Premises are statements offered without support; sub‑conclusions are supported by earlier premises and in turn support the main conclusion; the main conclusion is the ultimate claim the author wishes the reader to accept.

Hidden links — often signalled by words such as “therefore”, “thus”, “hence”, “because”, “since”, or “given that” — reveal the direction of support. However, examiners frequently omit explicit connectors, forcing the test‑taker to infer the logical flow from content alone. A reliable technique is to ask, “What does this statement try to prove?” for each sentence. If the answer points to another statement in the passage, the former is a premise or sub‑conclusion for the latter. Diagramming the argument with arrows (→) helps visualise the structure and prevents the common error of treating a sub‑conclusion as the main conclusion.

Consider a passage that begins with a statistical claim, follows with a causal explanation, and ends with a policy recommendation. The statistical claim is a premise; the causal explanation is a sub‑conclusion drawn from the statistic; the recommendation is the main conclusion supported by the causal explanation. Misidentifying the recommendation as a premise would lead to selecting an assumption that actually belongs to the causal link. Practice with varied argument maps builds the habit of tracing support both forward and backward, a skill that directly improves speed on multi‑step reasoning items.

Evaluating Assumptions: Necessary vs Sufficient Conditions and the Negation Test

Assumption questions ask for an unstated premise that must be true for the argument to hold. The logical distinction between necessary and sufficient conditions governs the strength of an assumption. A necessary assumption is one without which the conclusion cannot follow; if it is false, the argument collapses. A sufficient assumption guarantees the conclusion but is not required — the argument could still succeed through a different route. Competitive exams almost always target necessary assumptions because they are uniquely identifiable.

The negation test is the standard tool for confirming necessity. To apply it, negate the candidate assumption and check whether the argument’s conclusion still follows. If the conclusion no longer follows, the assumption is necessary and therefore implicit. If the conclusion remains intact, the assumption is either sufficient or irrelevant. For example, an argument that “The new tax policy will increase revenue because it broadens the tax base” assumes that the broadened base will not be offset by massive evasion. Negating this — “The broadened base will be offset by massive evasion” — destroys the revenue claim, confirming the assumption’s necessity.

Examiners often disguise necessary assumptions as “background facts” or “common knowledge”. A statement such as “The company’s profits rose after the marketing campaign” implicitly assumes that no other major event (e.g., a competitor’s exit) occurred simultaneously. The negation test exposes this hidden premise. When evaluating answer choices, discard any that are merely sufficient (they make the conclusion certain but are not required) and any that are irrelevant (they do not affect the logical link). Mastery of the negation test reduces guesswork and raises accuracy on assumption‑type items.

Negation Test Walkthrough
Argument: “If the city installs more streetlights, night‑time accidents will decrease because drivers can see hazards earlier.” Which of the following is a necessary assumption?
1. Drivers will not increase speed simply because visibility improves.
2. The city has sufficient budget for the installation.
3. Night‑time accidents are currently higher than day‑time accidents.
Negate each choice.
1. “Drivers will increase speed simply because visibility improves.” If true, the safety benefit of streetlights could be nullified, so the conclusion that accidents will decrease no longer follows. Hence choice 1 is a necessary assumption.
2. “The city does not have sufficient budget.” This affects feasibility, not the logical link between streetlights and accident reduction. The conclusion could still be logically valid even if the project is unaffordable. Not necessary.
3. “Night‑time accidents are not higher than day‑time accidents.” The argument does not compare night vs day rates; it only claims a reduction from the current night level. Negation does not break the conclusion. Not necessary.
Correct answer: 1.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Assumption Items
Read the stem first to know whether you need a necessary or sufficient assumption. Then apply the negation test mentally to each option; the one that destroys the conclusion is the answer. Practice the test on at least twenty arguments before the exam so the process becomes automatic.

Inference Chains: Distinguishing Deductive Validity from Inductive Strength

Inference questions require selecting a statement that logically follows from the given information. The first decision point is whether the stimulus supports a deductive inference (certainty) or an inductive inference (probability). Deductive validity demands that the conclusion be impossible to falsify if the premises are true; inductive strength only requires that the conclusion be likely. Competitive exams label deductive items with stems such as “must be true” or “logically follows”, while inductive items use “most strongly supported” or “can be inferred”.

To evaluate deductive validity, translate the premises into a formal structure (e.g., categorical syllogism, conditional chain) and test for logical form. Common valid forms include modus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism, and disjunctive syllogism. Invalid forms — affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, illicit conversion — appear frequently as distractors. For inductive strength, assess the representativeness of evidence, sample size, and the presence of confounding factors. A statement that “most surveyed voters favour the policy” supports the inference “the policy is popular” only if the survey sample is random and sufficiently large.

Examiners often blend both types in a single passage: a deductive core (e.g., “All engineers must pass the licensing exam”) surrounded by inductive evidence (e.g., “Recent pass rates have risen”). The test‑taker must isolate the deductive backbone for “must be true” questions and the inductive periphery for “most strongly supported” questions. Practising the separation of logical layers sharpens the ability to pick the correct inference under time pressure.

Conclusion Types: Main Conclusion, Intermediate Conclusions, and Implicit Conclusions

Identifying the exact conclusion an argument defends is a prerequisite for every critical‑reasoning task. The main conclusion is the ultimate claim; intermediate conclusions (also called sub‑conclusions) are claims that receive support and also provide support for the main conclusion. Implicit conclusions are not stated outright but can be derived from the explicit structure. Misclassifying any of these leads to wrong answers in “main point”, “role of a statement”, and “flaw” questions.

Signal words help but are not decisive. “Therefore”, “thus”, “hence” often precede the main conclusion, yet authors may place the main conclusion at the opening for rhetorical effect. A reliable method is the “why” test: for each candidate statement, ask “Why does the author believe this?” If the answer points to other statements in the passage, the candidate is not the main conclusion. The statement that no other statement supports is the main conclusion. Intermediate conclusions will have both incoming and outgoing support arrows in the argument map.

Implicit conclusions arise when the author expects the reader to draw a final inference without stating it. For instance, a passage detailing rising costs, falling revenues, and failed mitigation measures implicitly concludes that the project will be cancelled. Recognising implicit conclusions requires synthesising the entire chain and asking what ultimate claim the evidence collectively points toward. Training with passages that omit the final sentence builds this synthesis skill.

Logical Fallacies in Competitive Exams: Identification and Counter‑Strategies

Fallacy recognition is a high‑yield area because many flaw‑type questions are essentially “name the fallacy” items. The most frequently tested fallacies include hasty generalisation, false cause (post hoc ergo propter hoc), circular reasoning, straw man, false dilemma, appeal to authority, and slippery slope. Each has a structural signature that can be spotted without deep content knowledge.

Hasty generalisation appears when a broad claim rests on an insufficient sample. The stimulus will present a few anecdotes or a small survey and then assert a universal rule. The counter‑strategy is to look for a quantifier mismatch: “All”, “Every”, “Never” versus “Some”, “A few”. False cause assumes temporal succession implies causation; the fix is to check for a plausible mechanism or a third variable. Circular reasoning restates the conclusion as a premise; the giveaway is semantic repetition. Straw man misrepresents an opponent’s position; the remedy is to compare the described position with the actual argument. False dilemma forces a binary choice; the solution is to identify a viable middle ground. Appeal to authority cites an expert outside the relevant domain; verify the expertise match. Slippery slope predicts a chain of catastrophic events without evidence for each link; demand proof of each step.

When a question asks “Which of the following best describes the flaw?”, map the argument onto the fallacy templates above. Eliminate choices that describe a different structural error. Practice with a catalogue of labelled fallacies accelerates pattern recognition and reduces reliance on intuition.

  • Treating a sub‑conclusion as the main conclusion and selecting an assumption that belongs to the wrong link.
  • Confusing a sufficient assumption with a necessary one and choosing an answer that merely guarantees the conclusion.
  • Applying the negation test to a statement that is not an assumption but a premise, leading to a false positive.
  • Assuming inductive support equals deductive certainty and marking a “most strongly supported” answer for a “must be true” stem.
  • Overlooking an implicit conclusion and answering a “main point” question with an intermediate claim.
  • Labeling a valid deductive form as a fallacy because of unfamiliar terminology.
  • Failing to check for a third variable when evaluating a causal claim, resulting in a false‑cause selection.

Argument Mapping and Visual Representation

Argument mapping translates a prose passage into a diagram that makes the logical architecture explicit. Each node represents a distinct proposition — premise, sub‑conclusion, or main conclusion — and directed edges show the inferential flow. A common convention uses solid arrows for explicit support and dashed arrows for hidden or assumed links. Colour coding (e.g., green for premises, amber for intermediate conclusions, red for the final claim) helps identify the “why” chain quickly. For multiple independent lines of reasoning, draw separate trees side by side and merge them at a common conclusion node. This separation prevents misassigning a premise from one line as support for another. Mapping also reveals circularity: if a node points back to itself, the argument is viciously circular. Practising with a blank sheet or digital tool trains recognition of an argument's backbone within the short reading window typical of high‑stakes reasoning sections.

Mapping Procedure

  1. Identify every declarative sentence that makes a claim.
  2. Label each claim with a unique identifier (P1, P2, C1, …).
  3. Ask “What does this claim support?” and draw an arrow from the supporter to the supported.
  4. Mark any claim that receives no incoming arrows as a premise; any claim with no outgoing arrows as the main conclusion.
  5. Insert dashed arrows for any inferential step the author leaves implicit.

After the map is complete, the “why” test becomes mechanical: trace arrows backward from the main conclusion until you reach premises. Any answer choice not on this trace can be eliminated for assumption, flaw, or role‑of‑statement questions.

Conditional Reasoning and Contrapositive Strategies

Conditional statements (“If A, then B”) are central to deductive reasoning items. The form A → B yields two valid inferences: the contrapositive ¬B → ¬A and the original conditional. The converse (B → A) and inverse (¬A → ¬B) are not entailed; they underlie the common traps of Affirming the Consequent and Denying the Antecedent. A chain of conditionals (A → B, B → C) permits the transitive inference A → C and its contrapositive ¬C → ¬A. When a stimulus mixes sufficient/necessary language (“only if”, “unless”, “requires”), translate each clause into canonical A → B form before chaining. For example, “Only engineers may sign the report” becomes “If a person signs the report, then that person is an engineer.” The phrase “unless” is equivalent to “if not”: “Unless the fee is paid, the application is rejected” → “If the fee is not paid, then the application is rejected.” Mastering these translations helps spot hidden conditionals.

Contrapositive as a Search Tool

For “must be true” questions with a conditional premise, construct the contrapositive and scan choices for a match. If the stem gives a negated consequent (¬B), the contrapositive yields the negated antecedent (¬A). Conversely, an answer resembling the converse can be rejected: the contrapositive of the converse is the inverse, which is not guaranteed. Drills with one conditional and four choices (contrapositive, converse, inverse, unrelated) sharpen pattern recognition for timed sections.

Technique: Contrapositive Mapping
Premise: If the committee approves the proposal, then the budget will be increased. The budget was not increased. Which of the following must be true? (A) The committee approved the proposal. (B) The committee did not approve the proposal. (C) The proposal was rejected by the board. (D) The budget will be increased next quarter.
Translate the premise: Approve → Increase. The contrapositive is ¬Increase → ¬Approve. The stimulus gives ¬Increase (budget not increased). Applying the contrapositive yields ¬Approve — the committee did not approve the proposal. Choice (B) matches exactly. Choice (A) affirms the antecedent, which is not forced. Choice (C) introduces a new actor (board) not present in the premise. Choice (D) predicts a future event with no logical link. Therefore (B) is the only statement that must be true.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When you see an “if…then” stem, immediately write the contrapositive in shorthand (¬B → ¬A) on your scratch pad. Then read each answer choice once, checking only for a direct match to either the original conditional or its contrapositive. Discard any choice that resembles the converse or inverse without further support.

Quantifier Logic: Universal, Existential, and Mixed Quantifiers

Quantifier logic governs statements with “all”, “some”, “no”, and “some…not”. The four categorical forms — A (All S are P), E (No S are P), I (Some S are P), O (Some S are not P) — constitute the traditional Square of Opposition. The A and E forms are contraries (cannot both be true, but can both be false); I and O are subcontraries (cannot both be false, but can both be true); A and O, E and I are contradictories (exactly one true). Distribution rules identify universal terms: subject of A and E, predicate of E and O. Illicit conversion (e.g., inferring “All P are S” from “All S are P”) and illicit contraposition (e.g., inferring “All non‑P are non‑S” from “All S are P” without conversion) are classic error patterns. Mixed‑quantifier sentences (e.g., “Every student who passed received a certificate”) contain an embedded universal and an existential implication (at least one student passed). For multiple quantified claims, draw and overlay Venn diagrams; the overlap represents individuals satisfying all conditions. This prevents the quantifier‑shift fallacy, where the order of “all” and “some” is swapped.

Common Quantifier Traps

  • Assuming “Some A are B” entails “Some B are A” (valid) but “All A are B” entails “All B are A” (invalid).
  • Treating “No A are B” as “All A are non‑B” (valid) and then converting to “All non‑B are A” (invalid).
  • Overlooking existential import: in modern logic, “All unicorns are white” does not assert that unicorns exist, whereas traditional Aristotelian logic does.

Analogy and Comparative Reasoning in Arguments

Analogical arguments infer that shared relevant properties imply a further shared property. The structure: Case 1 has attributes {a, b, c, …, x}; Case 2 has attributes {a, b, c, …}; therefore Case 2 probably has attribute x. Strength depends on: (1) number and relevance of shared attributes, (2) absence of critical dissimilarities, (3) specificity of the projected attribute. A false analogy arises when shared attributes are superficial (e.g., both “round”) but the projected attribute depends on a deep structural difference (e.g., “edible”). Test items may present a policy in Country A and ask if the same outcome follows in Country B. Evaluation checks whether cited similarities (GDP per capita, legal framework, demographics) are causally linked to the outcome, and whether disclosed differences (political stability, resources) break the causal chain. Checklist: list shared factors, label each as causally relevant or incidental, then verify no unmentioned outcome‑affecting factor differs.

Evaluating Analogy Questions

  1. Identify the source and target cases.
  2. Extract the explicit similarities cited by the author.
  3. Determine whether each similarity is a known cause of the projected property.
  4. Search the passage for acknowledged differences; if none, consider plausible hidden differences.
  5. Choose the answer that strengthens the causal relevance of similarities or weakens the analogy by exposing a critical dissimilarity.

Evidence Evaluation: Relevance, Sufficiency, and Weight

Critical‑reasoning passages embed evidence (statistics, expert testimony, case studies, anecdotes) for evaluation. Relevance asks whether evidence makes the claim more probable; data from a different population, period, or definition is irrelevant despite numerical impressiveness. Sufficiency concerns whether evidence amount and quality sustain the inference; a single small‑sample study rarely suffices for a universal claim. Weight reflects credibility: peer‑reviewed meta‑analyses outweigh isolated expert opinions; recent data outweigh outdated figures for evolving phenomena. Common tricks: large‑scale surveys with selection bias, authorities cited outside their expertise, vivid anecdotes masking lack of systematic data. For strengthen/weaken questions, map each choice onto three dimensions: does it add relevant, sufficient, high‑weight evidence, or expose a relevance gap, sufficiency shortfall, or credibility flaw?

Quick Evidence Audit Checklist

  • Source: primary data vs. secondary summary; peer‑reviewed vs. popular press.
  • Sample: size, representativeness, randomisation.
  • Measurement: operational definitions, reliability, validity.
  • Context: temporal proximity, geographic applicability, population match.
  • Logical fit: does the evidence directly address the causal claim or merely correlate?
  • Confusing necessary and sufficient conditions when the stimulus uses “only if” language.
  • Treating the converse of a conditional as logically equivalent and selecting it as a must‑be‑true answer.
  • Illicitly converting a universal affirmative (“All A are B”) into “All B are A” in quantifier‑shift items.
  • Accepting an analogy based on superficial similarities while ignoring a disclosed structural difference.
  • Overvaluing a single anecdote or non‑representative statistic as sufficient proof for a broad conclusion.
  • Failing to construct the contrapositive before evaluating answer choices in conditional‑reasoning questions.
  • Neglecting to check existential import when moving between traditional and modern interpretations of categorical statements.
