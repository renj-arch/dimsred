What are Fillers & Cloze Test?

Fillers and Cloze Test questions evaluate your vocabulary, grammar, and contextual understanding. In fillers, a sentence with one or two blanks is given and you choose the correct word(s) from options. In cloze tests, a passage with multiple blanks is given and you fill each blank by selecting the most appropriate word. These appear in competitive exams.

Types of Fillers

1. Single Fillers: One blank in a sentence. You choose the word that best fits the meaning and grammar of the sentence. Tests vocabulary (synonyms, antonyms, collocations) and grammar (parts of speech, tense consistency).

2. Double Fillers: Two blanks in a sentence. You select a pair of words that fill both blanks appropriately. Both words must fit grammatically and contextually.

3. Phrase Fillers: A phrase or idiom is missing from the sentence. You choose the correct phrase that completes the sentence meaningfully.

Cloze Test Strategy

1. Read the Entire Passage: Before attempting any blank, read the whole passage to understand the context, tone, and main idea.

2. Identify the Part of Speech: Determine what type of word fits in each blank — noun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction.

3. Check Collocations: Certain words naturally go together (e.g., 'heavy rain', 'strong coffee', 'make a decision', 'take a break'). Recognizing collocations helps narrow choices.

4. Use Elimination: If the context is positive, eliminate negative words. If it's formal, eliminate informal/colloquial words.

5. Read After Filling: Once you've filled all blanks, read the entire passage to ensure it is coherent and grammatically correct.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Single Filler)
The new government is taking several measures to _______ the economy from the current slowdown.

a) revive b) collapse c) ignore d) accelerate
Solution: a) revive. The sentence talks about measures against a 'slowdown', so the government wants to 'revive' (bring back to good condition) the economy.
Example 2 (Double Fillers)
The company's _______ in innovation has _______ it to stay ahead of competitors.

a) investment, enabled b) failure, forced c) lack, helped d) disinterest, allowed
Solution: a) investment, enabled. To 'stay ahead of competitors', the company would need a positive quality — 'investment' in innovation, and this 'enabled' them to lead.
Example 3 (Cloze Test)
Read the passage and fill the blank: 'The banking sector has undergone significant _______ in recent years. The introduction of digital technologies has made banking more _______.'

Blank 1: a) stagnation b) transformation c) decline d) confusion
Blank 2: a) complex b) expensive c) accessible d) restrictive
Solution: Blank 1: b) transformation (significant change). Blank 2: c) accessible (digital tech makes banking easy to use). The passage has a positive tone about digital technologies improving banking.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For single fillers, first identify the tone (positive/negative) and eliminate opposite-toned words.
  • For double fillers, check each word in the pair independently — both must fit.
  • For cloze tests, pay attention to transition words (however, therefore, moreover) as they indicate the direction of the passage.
  • Read the sentence after the blank — it often contains clues about what the blank should contain.
Pro Tip
In mains, cloze tests are often from financial or economic contexts. Build a strong vocabulary related to banking, economics, and business. Words like 'acquisition', 'deregulation', 'insolvency', 'liquidity', 'collateral' appear frequently.

Contextual Vocabulary and Register Shifts

Fillers and cloze items are rarely neutral; they embed a register — formal, semi‑formal, journalistic, or academic — that governs which lexical items are acceptable. A word that is semantically correct may be rejected because its register clashes with the surrounding tone. For instance, “commence” fits a formal policy document, while “kick off” belongs to a conversational register. Recognising register shifts requires attention to three signals: (1) the presence of passive constructions and nominalisations, which usually indicate a formal register; (2) the use of contractions, phrasal verbs, and colloquial idioms, which signal informality; (3) the domain‑specific terminology (e.g., “liquidity”, “fiscal deficit”) that anchors the passage in a professional field. When a blank appears near a domain term, the answer must belong to the same lexical field and register. A practical drill is to rewrite a short paragraph in two registers — formal and informal — and then compare the lexical choices that become permissible or impermissible. This trains the eye to spot register mismatches instantly, a skill that separates high scorers from average candidates.

Another layer is the “semantic prosody” of a word — the habitual connotation it carries in authentic usage. “Cause” often collocates with negative outcomes (“cause damage”, “cause concern”), whereas “bring about” is neutral or positive. In a cloze passage describing a reform that improves access, “bring about” or “facilitate” will be preferred over “cause”. Examiners deliberately plant distractors that are synonyms in isolation but carry the wrong prosody. Building a mental inventory of such prosodic patterns for high‑frequency verbs (e.g., “trigger”, “spark”, “prompt”, “instigate”) sharpens elimination speed. Finally, register awareness extends to punctuation and sentence length: formal passages favour longer, complex sentences with subordinate clauses; informal ones use shorter, paratactic structures. Aligning the candidate word with the surrounding syntactic rhythm is a reliable, often overlooked, cue.

Syntactic Parallelism in Double Fillers

Double‑filler items test the ability to maintain parallel structure across two blanks that often belong to the same clause or to coordinated clauses. Parallelism operates at three levels: (a) part‑of‑speech parity — both blanks may require nouns, both verbs, or both adjectives; (b) morphological symmetry — if the first blank takes a gerund, the second frequently takes a gerund or an infinitive depending on the governing verb; (c) semantic balance — the two words should reflect a logical relationship such as cause‑effect, contrast, or addition. Consider the pattern “The committee’s ______ in transparency ______ public trust.” The first blank demands a noun denoting a quality (e.g., “commitment”, “lack”), the second a verb that agrees with that noun and conveys the resulting impact (“enhanced”, “eroded”). The correct pair respects both grammatical agreement and logical coherence. A systematic approach is to first isolate the syntactic frame: identify the subject, verb, and any coordinating conjunctions. Then list the grammatical category each blank must fulfil. Next, test each option pair against the frame, discarding any that break agreement or parallelism. This method reduces the search space dramatically, especially when the answer choices contain mixed parts of speech.

Examiners also embed “false parallelism” traps where the surface structure suggests symmetry but the semantics demand asymmetry. For example, “The policy ______ inflation but ______ growth.” The first blank expects a verb like “curbed” (negative), the second a verb like “stimulated” (positive). The conjunction “but” signals contrast, so the two verbs must be antonymic in effect. Recognising discourse markers such as “however”, “moreover”, “consequently” inside the sentence guides the semantic polarity of each blank. Practising with sentences that contain explicit markers trains the candidate to anticipate the required lexical contrast before looking at options. Over time, the parallelism check becomes an automatic pre‑filter that eliminates at least two of four option pairs in under ten seconds.

Parallel Structure Mapping
Choose the pair that best completes the sentence: The new regulation ______ compliance costs for small firms while ______ innovation in the sector.
Step 1: Identify the syntactic frame — “The new regulation” is the subject; two coordinated verb phrases linked by “while” (indicating contrast). Step 2: Determine part of speech — both blanks need finite verbs in past tense (or present participle if the structure were “reducing … fostering”). Step 3: Apply semantic contrast — first verb should convey increase or burden (negative for firms), second verb should convey encouragement (positive). Option analysis: (a) raised, stifling — both negative, no contrast. (b) lowered, fostering — first negative? “lowered” reduces costs (positive), second positive — not contrast. (c) increased, encouraging — first negative (increase costs), second positive (encourage innovation) — fits contrast and parallel verb forms. (d) cut, hampering — first positive, second negative — opposite contrast. Correct answer: (c) increased, encouraging.
Exam‑Day Parallelism Drill
Before the test, spend five minutes writing ten two‑blank sentences that use contrasting conjunctions (while, whereas, but). For each, list the required verb tense and polarity. During the exam, when a double filler appears, mentally map the conjunction to the expected polarity pair; this cuts option evaluation time by half.

Discourse Markers and Cohesion in Cloze Tests

Cloze passages are engineered to test the reader’s grasp of textual cohesion. Discourse markers — conjunctions, adverbials, lexical ties — act as signposts that dictate the logical flow between sentences. Mastery of these markers allows a candidate to predict the semantic role of a missing word even before scanning the options. The most frequent markers in banking and economic texts include “consequently”, “furthermore”, “nevertheless”, “in contrast”, “as a result”, and “for instance”. Each marker imposes a specific relational constraint: “consequently” demands a cause‑effect link where the blank often fills the effect; “nevertheless” signals a concession, so the blank typically introduces a counter‑point; “for instance” precedes an exemplifying noun phrase. A practical technique is to underline every discourse marker in the passage during the first read. Then, for each blank, note the nearest preceding and following markers. The intersection of their constraints narrows the lexical field dramatically. For example, if a blank follows “however” and precedes “therefore”, the missing word must express a contrast that leads to a conclusion — often a noun like “setback” or “challenge”.

Beyond explicit markers, cohesive devices such as lexical repetition, synonym chains, and pronoun reference create invisible threads. A cloze blank may be the only place where a key concept is lexicalised; the surrounding sentences may refer to it via pronouns (“it”, “this”) or synonyms (“the measure”, “the initiative”). Tracking the referential chain reveals the required part of speech and semantic content. Consider a passage where “The central bank announced a rate cut. ______ is expected to boost lending.” The pronoun “it” in the second sentence refers back to the rate cut, so the blank must be a noun phrase denoting the rate cut (“The move”, “This decision”). Recognising such anaphoric links eliminates options that are semantically unrelated. Training with authentic financial reports — highlighting markers and referential chains — builds an intuitive sense of cohesion that translates directly into higher cloze accuracy.

Advanced Elimination Using Semantic Fields

Semantic field theory posits that vocabulary clusters around conceptual domains (e.g., “banking”, “risk”, “regulation”). In fillers and cloze items, the correct answer almost always belongs to the same semantic field as the surrounding context words. Advanced elimination exploits this by constructing a rapid field map: list the domain‑specific nouns, verbs, and adjectives that appear within a two‑sentence window of the blank. Then, for each option, check whether it shares the field. Options from unrelated fields — such as “harvest” in a monetary policy passage — are discarded instantly. This method is especially powerful for “word replacement” questions where a highlighted word must be substituted with a context‑appropriate synonym. The replacement must preserve not only denotation but also collocational behaviour. For instance, “mitigate” collocates with “risk”, “impact”, “damage”; “alleviate” collocates with “pain”, “burden”, “stress”. If the original sentence reads “The framework aims to mitigate systemic risk,” the only viable replacement is “reduce” or “lessen” — words that also collocate with “risk”. “Alleviate” would be a field mismatch.

To operationalise this, maintain a personal lexical notebook organised by field: banking (capital adequacy, liquidity, Basel), monetary policy (repo, reverse repo, inflation targeting), fiscal policy (deficit, borrowing, stimulus). Review the notebook weekly, focusing on high‑frequency collocations. During practice, force yourself to articulate the field of each blank before looking at options. This habit converts semantic field awareness from a conscious strategy into an automatic filter, shaving precious seconds per question. Over a full test, the cumulative time saved can be the difference between attempting all items and leaving blanks unanswered.

  • Assuming a synonym works in every register — register mismatch is a primary distractor.
  • Ignoring discourse markers that dictate logical relations between sentences.
  • Overlooking pronoun or lexical reference chains that define the required referent.
  • Selecting a word with correct meaning but wrong collocational partner.
  • Failing to enforce parallel grammatical form across double‑filler blanks.
  • Treating “while” and “whereas” as interchangeable without checking polarity.
  • Neglecting semantic‑field consistency when replacing highlighted words.

Master these advanced techniques to consistently secure high accuracy in fillers and cloze tests.

Collocational Constraints and Lexical Priming in Fillers

Collocational constraints operate at the interface of syntax and lexis: certain verbs select specific nouns, adjectives bind to particular nouns, and prepositions govern fixed complements. In single‑filler items, the blank often sits inside a collocational frame such as “mitigate ______”, “exert ______”, or “subject to ______”. The correct option must not only fit the grammatical slot but also preserve the conventional pairing that native speakers have internalised through massive exposure. Lexical priming theory explains this by proposing that each encounter with a word activates its frequent neighbours, creating a probabilistic expectation. When a candidate reads “The policy aims to ______ inflation”, the verb “curb”, “contain”, or “tame” is primed because they co‑occur with “inflation” in economic corpora far more often than “reduce” or “lower”, which are grammatically acceptable but collocationally weaker.

Advanced elimination exploits this by building a micro‑corpus of high‑frequency collocates for the target domain. For banking passages, the verb “tighten” collocates with “monetary policy”, “liquidity”, “norms”; “ease” collocates with “rates”, “conditions”, “stance”. A filler that offers “tighten” as an option for a blank preceded by “The central bank decided to ______ the repo rate” is instantly preferred over “modify” because “tighten the repo rate” is a fixed phrase in policy discourse. Conversely, “modify the repo rate” is rare and signals a distractor.

Exceptions arise when the test deliberately uses a marked collocation to test register awareness. A passage written in a journalistic style may employ “slash” with “rates” (“The bank slashed rates”) whereas a formal report prefers “cut”. Both are grammatical, but only one matches the register. The examinee must therefore map the collocational strength onto the stylistic level of the passage. A practical drill is to extract 20‑sentence windows from authentic financial reports, underline every verb‑noun pair, and classify them by register (formal, semi‑formal, journalistic). Over time the mental lexicon aligns with the test’s expectations, turning collocational knowledge into an automatic filter.

Another layer is the interaction between collocation and negation. Negative polarity items such as “any”, “ever”, “at all” are licensed only in downward‑entailing environments. A filler like “The committee did not ______ any objection” forces the verb to be one that permits a negative polarity object: “raise”, “voice”, “register” work; “accept” does not because “accept any objection” is semantically odd. Recognising polarity licensing narrows the field before semantic considerations are even applied.

Finally, collocational constraints interact with syntactic parallelism in double fillers. When two blanks share a coordinate structure — “The measure will ______ risk and ______ confidence” — the verbs must belong to the same collocational set for each object. “Mitigate risk” and “boost confidence” are both strong collocations; “reduce risk” and “raise confidence” are weaker but acceptable. The test often includes a pair where one verb collocates well with the first noun but poorly with the second, creating a cross‑collocational trap. Systematic practice with paired collocation tables eliminates this vulnerability.

Negation Scope and Polarity Sensitivity in Double Fillers

Negation scope determines which constituents fall under the semantic influence of a negative operator such as “not”, “never”, “without”, or “no”. In double‑filler sentences, the two blanks may lie on opposite sides of a negation boundary, requiring the examinee to track whether each blank is inside or outside the scope. For example, “The regulator did not ______ the proposal nor ______ the dissent” places both blanks within the scope of “did not”. The verbs must therefore be compatible with a negative polarity context: “approve” and “acknowledge” are positive polarity verbs that become infelicitous under negation (“did not approve” is fine, but “did not acknowledge” is acceptable; however “did not reject” flips the meaning). The test exploits this by offering one verb that is a negative polarity item (e.g., “budge”, “lift a finger”) which is only licensed under negation, and another that is a positive polarity item (e.g., “welcome”, “embrace”) which sounds unnatural when negated.

Polarity sensitivity extends to determiners and adverbs. Items like “any”, “ever”, “at all”, “in the least” are negative polarity items (NPIs) that require a downward‑entailing environment. In a double filler such as “The audit revealed no ______ nor ______ of misconduct”, the blanks must be filled with NPIs or nouns that can appear in a negative context: “trace”, “evidence”, “indication”. Options like “proof” or “confirmation” are positive polarity and create a semantic clash because “no proof” is acceptable but “no confirmation” is less idiomatic in formal audit language. The examiner often pairs a strong NPI with a distractor that is grammatically plural but polarity‑incompatible.

Scope ambiguities arise with quantifiers. “Not every bank ______ the guideline” has a different truth condition from “Every bank did not ______ the guideline”. The former means “some banks did not”, the latter means “no bank did”. Double fillers may embed a quantifier in the first clause and a negation in the second: “Not all stakeholders ______ the reform, and the board ______ the objections”. The first blank expects a verb that can be partially true (e.g., “supported”), while the second blank, under a positive verb “addressed”, must be compatible with a direct object. Misreading the scope leads to selecting a verb that forces a universal reading where only a partial one is intended.

Licensing of NPIs also depends on the presence of affective predicates such as “doubt”, “deny”, “prevent”. In “The ministry doubts that the scheme will ______ any ______”, both blanks fall under the scope of “doubts”, licensing “yield” and “benefit” as NPIs. If the matrix verb were “believes”, the NPIs would be unlicensed and the sentence would become ungrammatical. Recognising the matrix predicate’s polarity is therefore a prerequisite for filler selection.

A reliable technique is to annotate each sentence with polarity markers: underline the negative operator, draw an arrow to the furthest constituent it governs, and label each blank as “IN‑SCOPE” or “OUT‑OF‑SCOPE”. Then filter options by polarity compatibility before checking collocation. This two‑stage filter — polarity first, collocation second — reduces the decision space dramatically and mirrors the logical structure the examiner expects.

Technique: Polarity Item Licensing
Choose the pair that best completes the sentence: “The committee did not ______ the amendment nor ______ any objection from members.” Options: (A) approve / entertain (B) reject / entertain (C) approve / dismiss (D) reject / dismiss
Step 1: Identify the negation operator “did not”. Its scope extends over the coordinated verb phrases “______ the amendment” and “______ any objection”. Both blanks are IN‑SCOPE. Step 2: Determine polarity requirements. The second blank contains the NPI “any objection”, which is licensed only in a downward‑entailing environment — satisfied by “did not”. The verb in the second blank must be compatible with a negative context and with the object “any objection”. “Entertain any objection” is a conventional collocation in formal proceedings; “dismiss any objection” is also possible but “dismiss” is a positive polarity verb that sounds marked under negation (“did not dismiss any objection” implies they kept the objection, which is logically odd). Step 3: Evaluate the first blank. “Approve the amendment” under negation yields “did not approve the amendment”, a standard phrasing. “Reject the amendment” under negation yields “did not reject the amendment”, meaning they accepted it — a possible reading but less likely in a sentence that also says they did not entertain objections. The most coherent pair is (A) approve / entertain. Both verbs are negative‑polarity‑compatible and collocate naturally with their objects.
Exam‑Day Strategy
During the first read, circle every negation word (not, never, without, no, neither, nor). Draw a bracket to the end of the clause it governs. Label each blank inside the bracket as NEG‑IN. For NEG‑IN blanks, discard any option that is a strong positive polarity item (e.g., welcome, embrace, celebrate) unless the sentence logic explicitly requires a double negative. Then apply collocational filtering. This two‑pass method prevents the common error of selecting a grammatically correct but polarity‑incompatible word.

Coordination, Ellipsis, and Structural Ambiguity in Double Fillers

Double‑filler items frequently employ coordinate structures — “and”, “or”, “but”, “nor” — that impose parallelism constraints across the two blanks. The parallelism is not merely syntactic; it extends to thematic roles, aspect, and argument structure. When the coordinate conjunction is “and”, the two verbs typically share the same subject and aspectual class. For instance, “The policy aims to ______ inflation and ______ growth” expects two verbs that can both take an economic indicator as direct object and that belong to the same aspectual class (both telic or both atelic). “Curb inflation and stimulate growth” satisfies this; “curb inflation and boost growth” also works because “boost” is telic. However, “curb inflation and maintain growth” mixes a change‑of‑state verb with a stative verb, creating a subtle aspectual mismatch that examiners use as a distractor.

Ellipsis adds another layer. In “The bank will ______ rates and ______ liquidity”, the second verb may be elided if it repeats the first: “The bank will cut rates and ______ liquidity”. The blank then requires a verb that can coordinate with “cut” under the same auxiliary “will”. Options like “ease” (will ease liquidity) are parallel; “provide” (will provide liquidity) changes the transitivity pattern and breaks the ellipsis licence. Recognising whether the coordinate structure is a full conjunction or a gapped coordination determines whether the second blank must match the first verb’s subcategorisation frame.

Structural ambiguity arises when a prepositional phrase can attach to either conjunct. “The committee reviewed the proposal with the experts and ______ the draft” — the phrase “with the experts” could modify “reviewed” (instrumental) or “the proposal” (accompaniment). The second blank’s verb must be compatible with the intended reading. If the intended reading is instrumental, the second verb often shares the same instrumental adjunct: “reviewed the proposal with the experts and revised the draft with the experts”. The test may omit the repeated adjunct, forcing the examinee to infer the shared structure. A reliable heuristic is to test each option by mentally inserting the adjunct after the second verb; if the resulting phrase is infelicitous, the option is likely wrong.

Coordination with “or” introduces a choice reading that often triggers a modal or future orientation. “The regulator may ______ the licence or ______ the application” expects verbs that are mutually exclusive actions. “Grant” and “reject” are natural opposites; “grant” and “process” are not mutually exclusive. The examiner exploits this by offering a pair where one verb is a superordinate of the other, violating the exclusivity implied by “or”.

Finally, “nor” coordinates two negative clauses and requires subject‑auxiliary inversion in formal registers: “The board did not approve the plan, nor ______ it ______ the budget”. The second blank must be filled with an auxiliary (“did”) and a verb that matches the first clause’s polarity. Recognising the “nor” inversion pattern eliminates options that lack the required auxiliary.

Practice with annotated coordinate structures — marking shared subjects, auxiliaries, adjuncts, and argument roles — builds an automatic parser for these constraints. Over time the examinee internalises the parallelism template and spots violations instantly.

Genre-Specific Lexical Selection in Cloze Tests

Cloze passages are drawn from identifiable genres — central bank minutes, regulatory circulars, financial journalism, academic abstracts — each with a distinct lexical fingerprint. Genre‑specific selection means that the same semantic slot may be filled by different lexical items depending on the register. A monetary policy passage uses “tighten” for “increase the policy rate”, while a news report prefers “hike”. A regulatory circular employs “prescribe” for “set a requirement”, whereas a press release uses “mandate”. The examinee who recognises the genre can predict the lexical item before seeing options.

Corpus studies show that certain verbs have a strong genre bias. In central bank communications, “anchor” collocates with “inflation expectations” at a frequency ten times higher than in general business news. “Calibrate” appears almost exclusively in policy‑rate contexts. “Unwind” is reserved for balance‑sheet reduction narratives. When a cloze blank appears in a sentence like “The MPC decided to ______ the accommodative stance”, the genre‑appropriate verb is “withdraw” or “normalize”, not “remove” or “delete”. The latter are grammatically possible but genre‑incongruent.

Nominalisation density is another genre marker. Formal regulatory texts favour heavy nominalisation: “The implementation of the framework necessitates the recalibration of parameters.” A cloze blank in such a passage often requires a nominalised form (“recalibration”) rather than a verb (“recalibrate”). Journalistic texts, by contrast, prefer verbal forms and lighter noun phrases. Detecting the nominalisation level guides part‑of‑speech selection.

Modal verb distribution also varies. Central bank minutes use “shall” for binding commitments, “may” for discretionary actions, and “should” for recommendations. A blank preceded by “The framework ______ be reviewed annually” expects “shall” in a regulatory circular but “should” in a consultative paper. Misreading the modal force leads to a register mismatch.

Discourse markers differ by genre. Minutes employ “accordingly”, “consequently”, “furthermore” in a rigid hierarchical order. News articles use “meanwhile”, “additionally”, “however” more flexibly. A cloze blank that follows a discourse marker inherits its genre‑specific continuation pattern. For example, after “accordingly” in a minutes text, the next clause typically states a concrete action (“accordingly, the rate was raised”). After “however” in a news piece, the clause often introduces a contrasting viewpoint (“however, analysts warned of risks”).

A practical preparation method is to compile a genre‑lexicon notebook: for each genre, list the top 30 verbs, nouns, modals, and discourse markers with their collocates. Review weekly and practice cloze passages labelled by source. This trains the brain to activate the appropriate lexical set automatically, reducing reliance on trial‑and‑error elimination.

Pragmatic Inference and Implicature in Cloze Completion

Beyond syntax and collocation, cloze items often test the ability to recover the speaker’s intended meaning — the pragmatic layer. Gricean maxims (quantity, quality, relation, manner) generate implicatures that constrain lexical choice. When a passage states “The inflation target remains unchanged, ______ the committee sees no need for immediate action”, the blank must convey a causal or justificatory relation that satisfies the maxim of relation. “Because” or “since” are explicit; “as” is acceptable; “although” would violate the expected coherence because it introduces a concession contrary to the implicature that stability justifies inaction.

Scalar implicature operates with quantifiers. “Some banks have adopted the new standard” implicates “not all”. If a later sentence reads “______ banks have adopted the new standard”, the blank cannot be “all” without cancelling the earlier implicature. The test may present a cloze where the preceding context uses “some” and the blank requires a quantifier consistent with the scalar hierarchy. Options like “most”, “many”, “a few” are compatible; “all” is a trap.

Presupposition triggers such as “again”, “stop”, “regret”, “manage” impose background assumptions. “The governor managed to ______ the crisis” presupposes the crisis was difficult. The blank must be a verb denoting successful handling (“contain”, “resolve”, “navigate”). “Avoid” would contradict the presupposition because “manage to avoid” implies the crisis never materialised, clashing with the presupposed existence of the crisis.

Conventional implicatures attached to discourse particles (“even”, “only”, “just”) further narrow the field. “Even the smallest banks ______ the requirement” implies that compliance by small banks is surprising. The verb must be one that can be scaled for difficulty (“met”, “satisfied”, “fulfilled”). “Ignored” would reverse the implicature.

Speech act theory distinguishes assertives, directives, commissives, and expressives. A cloze blank in a directive sentence (“The regulator ______ banks to submit reports by Friday”) expects a performative verb like “requires”, “mandates”, “directs”. “Suggests” weakens the illocutionary force and is inappropriate in a binding circular. Recognising the speech act type of the host sentence guides the verb selection.

To develop pragmatic sensitivity, annotate practice passages with implicature tags: mark scalar items, presupposition triggers, and speech act labels. Then, for each blank, articulate the pragmatic constraint before scanning options. This habit transforms cloze solving from a lexical matching task into a discourse comprehension exercise, aligning with the examiner’s design.

  • Assuming a verb that collocates in one genre works equally in another — genre mismatch is a primary distractor.
  • Ignoring the scope of negation when two blanks share a coordinate structure — polarity errors cascade.
  • Overlooking ellipsis licensing — the second verb must match the first verb’s subcategorisation frame.
  • Selecting a quantifier that cancels a scalar implicature established earlier in the passage.
  • Choosing a verb that violates the presupposition triggered by particles such as “manage” or “again”.
  • Misidentifying the speech act type and picking a verb with incompatible illocutionary force.
  • Neglecting to track referential chains across sentences leads to pronoun‑antecedent mismatches in cloze blanks.
