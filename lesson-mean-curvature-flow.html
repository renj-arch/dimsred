Mean Curvature Flow — the Huisken Flow, the Brakke Flow & the Singularity Analysis

Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) is a parabolic evolution equation for hypersurfaces: each point moves in the direction of the mean curvature vector with speed equal to the mean curvature. Given an immersion X₀: Mⁿ → ℝ^{n+1}, the flow is ∂_t X = −H·n where H is the mean curvature (the sum of the principal curvatures) and n is the unit normal. This is a quasilinear parabolic system — it smooths surfaces while preserving the topology until a singularity forms. The analysis of singularities is the central theme: Huisken's monotonicity formula shows that the Gaussian density is non-increasing, and the blow-up analysis classifies Type I singularities (spheres, cylinders, planes). The Brakke flow extends MCF past singularities using varifolds, while the level set approach (Evans-Spruck, Chen-Giga-Goto) gives a unique viscosity solution. The Huisken-Sinestrari theorem provides a complete topological classification for two-convex hypersurfaces using MCF with surgery.

"Mean curvature flow smooths surfaces while preserving the topology until the singularity."
— Gerhard Huisken
Part 1

📏 The Flow and its Short-Time Existence

Parabolic Flow
The Mean Curvature Flow: ∂_t X = −H·n
Let X(p,t): Mⁿ × [0,T) → ℝ^{n+1} be a one-parameter family of smooth immersions of an n-dimensional hypersurface. The mean curvature flow is defined by ∂_t X = −H·n where H = λ₁ + ⋯ + λ_n is the mean curvature (the sum of the principal curvatures) and n is the outward unit normal. The negative sign ensures that convex surfaces shrink to a point (the flow moves in the direction of the inward normal for convex surfaces). The flow is parabolic — the linearization gives the heat operator ∂_t − Δ on the normal bundle, making it a quasilinear parabolic system of PDEs. Short-time existence: if the initial surface M₀ is smooth (or at least C^{2,α}) and locally strictly convex, then there exists a unique smooth solution for a short time T > 0 (this follows from the parabolic PDE theory of quasilinear systems, using the DeTurck trick to break diffeomorphism invariance). The evolution equations: the mean curvature evolves by ∂_t H = ΔH + |A|²H where |A|² = λ₁² + ⋯ + λ_n² is the squared norm of the second fundamental form. This is a heat equation with a potential term: the potential |A|² may blow up at the singularity. The Gauss map evolution: on a convex surface, the unit normal n evolves by ∂_t n = Δn + |A|²n, which implies the normal vector moves by the heat equation on the sphere. Preservation of convexity: if the initial surface is convex (all λ_i ≥ 0), then it remains convex under MCF. More generally, if M₀ is mean convex (H > 0), then H > 0 for all t < T. The maximum principle plays a crucial role: the minimum mean curvature is non-decreasing.
The MCF is a quasilinear parabolic flow that preserves convexity and mean convexity; the evolution of the mean curvature follows a reaction-diffusion equation with a potentially singular source term.
Part 2

🌍 Huisken's Monotonicity Formula and Singularities

Singularity Analysis
Gaussian Density, Type I and Type II Singularities
The Huisken monotonicity formula (1990) is the central tool for analyzing singularities of MCF. For a family of hypersurfaces M_t evolving by MCF, the Gaussian function centered at (x₀, T) is ρ(x,t) = (4π(T−t))^{−n/2} exp(−|x−x₀|² / (4(T−t))). Huisken proved that (d/dt) ∫_{M_t} ρ dA ≤ 0 — the Gaussian weighted area is non-increasing. The Gaussian density at a point (x₀, T) is the limit θ(M_t, (x₀, T)) = lim_{t→T⁻} ∫_{M_t} ρ dA. The monotonicity implies that at each point of the first singularity, the density is well-defined and at least 1 (for a smooth point, the density is 1). Classification of Type I singularities: a singularity is Type I if there exists C > 0 such that max |A|² ≤ C/(T−t). At a Type I singularity, the blow-up (parabolic rescaling) converges to a self-shrinking solution — a smooth solution of MCF that evolves only by scaling: X(p,t) = √(T−t) Y(p) where Y is a hypersurface satisfying H = ⟨Y, n⟩/2 (the self-shrinker equation). Huisken's theorem (1984): the only smooth self-shrinking convex surfaces are spheres (round spheres). For mean convex MCF, the Huisken-Sinestrari theorem (1999) shows that the only Type I singularities are shrinking spheres, shrinking cylinders S^{n−1}×ℝ, and shrinking planes. Type II singularities: these are singularities where |A|² blows up faster than 1/(T−t). The prototypical Type II singularity is the neck-pinch — a dumbbell-shaped surface develops a thin neck that pinches off before the rest of the surface shrinks to a point. The Type II blow-up produces a translating solution (a grim reaper in 2D, or a translating bowl in higher dimensions). The Angenent's torus (Angenent, 1992) is a smooth self-shrinking torus that shrinks to a circle — it is the first non-trivial example of a self-shrinker that is not a sphere, cylinder, or plane. It provides a counterexample to the naive expectation that only spheres can appear as Type I singularities, though it is non-convex.
Gaussian density is monotone under MCF; Type I blow-ups are self-shrinkers classified as spheres, cylinders, and planes for convex surfaces, while Type II blow-ups produce translators like the grim reaper.
Part 3

🧮 The Brakke Flow and the Level Set Approach

Weak Solutions
Varifold Formulation and Level Set MCF
The Brakke flow (Brakke, 1978) is a varifold formulation of MCF that can continue through singularities. A varifold V is a Radon measure on the Grassmannian of n-planes in ℝ^{n+1}, encoding both the area and the tangent plane information of a possibly singular surface. A family of varifolds {V_t} is a Brakke flow if for all test functions φ ∈ C_c¹(ℝ^{n+1}, ℝ₊), the time derivative of the area satisfies (d/dt) ∫ φ dμ_{V_t} ≤ −∫ φ H² dμ_{V_t} + ∫ |∇^⊥ φ| dμ_{V_t}, where H is the generalized mean curvature of V_t in the sense of first variation. This inequality captures the dissipative nature of MCF: the area decreases at least as fast as the integral of squared mean curvature. Existence: for any initial integer rectifiable varifold with mean curvature in L², there exists a Brakke flow (Brakke's existence theorem). The flow is not unique in general, but it is the canonical weak solution. The level set method (Evans-Spruck, 1991; Chen-Giga-Goto, 1991) provides an alternative approach: let u: ℝ^{n+1}×[0,T] → ℝ be a function such that the zero level set {u(·,t) = 0} represents the evolving surface. The level set MCF equation is ∂_t u = |∇u|·div(∇u/|∇u|) = Σ_{i,j=1}^{n+1} (δ_{ij} − (∂_i u ∂_j u)/|∇u|²) ∂_{ij}u. This is a degenerate elliptic-parabolic equation that can be rewritten as u_t = Δu − (∇²u(∇u,∇u))/|∇u|², where ∇²u is the Hessian. The level set equation is well-posed in the viscosity solution sense: the comparison principle holds (if u₀ ≤ v₀ then u ≤ v for all t), and solutions are unique. The level set approach allows topological changes: when a neck pinches, the zero level set changes topology continuously, and the flow continues uniquely past the singularity. The fattening phenomenon: for some initial data, the level set solution develops interior (the zero level set has non-empty interior), corresponding to non-uniqueness of the geometric evolution.
Brakke flow provides a varifold formulation that exists past singularities, while the level set method gives a unique viscosity solution through topological changes; both are essential for understanding MCF beyond the first singular time.
Part 4

🔥 The Two-Convex MCF and the Huisken-Sinestrari Theorem

Topological Classification
Two-Convexity, MCF with Surgery, and the Classification Theorem
A hypersurface Mⁿ ⊂ ℝ^{n+1} is two-convex if at every point, the sum of the two smallest principal curvatures is positive: λ₁ + λ₂ > 0 (with λ₁ ≤ λ₂ ≤ ⋯ ≤ λ_n). This condition includes convex surfaces (all λ_i ≥ 0) and hypersurfaces with one small negative curvature that is dominated by the next principal curvature. Two-convexity is preserved under MCF and ensures that singularities are mild. The Huisken-Sinestrari theorem (1999, 2009): for a two-convex solution of MCF in ℝ^{n+1} with n ≥ 2, the flow becomes strictly convex before the singular time. Moreover, all singularities are of Type I and are modeled on shrinking spheres. The theorem provides a complete topological classification: any two-convex hypersurface Mⁿ ⊂ ℝ^{n+1} (n ≥ 2) is diffeomorphic to a sphere Sⁿ or a finite connected sum of spheres and copies of S¹×S^{n−1}. This is proved using MCF with surgery (also called the flow with surgery, developed by Huisken-Sinestrari). The idea: as the MCF evolves, when the curvature becomes too large in a region (the neck becomes too thin), one performs surgery — cut out the highly curved region and cap off the two ends with smooth caps. The flow is then restarted. After finitely many surgeries, the remaining pieces are all convex and shrink to round points. The surgery procedure tracks the topology: each surgery removes either a sphere component or an S¹×S^{n−1} component. Applications: the classification theorem shows that in dimensions n ≥ 2, the only topological types that admit two-convex embeddings into Euclidean space are spheres and connected sums of spheres and S¹×S^{n−1} — this is a striking rigidity result. The Angenent's torus (T²) is not two-convex (it has negative curvature points), which is why it can persist as a self-shrinker but cannot appear in the two-convex classification. The higher dimensions: the results extend to n ≥ 2, with the surgery construction requiring careful curvature estimates and neck detection. The Brendle-Huisken theorem (2016): the flow with surgery also works for mean convex MCF, with the same topological conclusions when the flow is two-convex from the start.
Two-convex MCF becomes convex before singularities; MCF with surgery provides a complete topological classification of two-convex hypersurfaces as spheres and connected sums with S¹×S^{n−1}.
