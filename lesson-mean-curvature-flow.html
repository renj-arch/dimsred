Mean Curvature Flow — the Huisken Flow, the Brakke Flow & the Singularity Analysis

Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) is a parabolic evolution equation for hypersurfaces: each point moves in the direction of the mean curvature vector with speed equal to the mean curvature. Given an immersion X₀: Mⁿ → ℝ^{n+1}, the flow is ∂_t X = −H·n where H is the mean curvature (the sum of the principal curvatures) and n is the unit normal. This is a quasilinear parabolic system — it smooths surfaces while preserving the topology until a singularity forms. The analysis of singularities is the central theme: Huisken's monotonicity formula shows that the Gaussian density is non-increasing, and the blow-up analysis classifies Type I singularities (spheres, cylinders, planes). The Brakke flow extends MCF past singularities using varifolds, while the level set approach (Evans-Spruck, Chen-Giga-Goto) gives a unique viscosity solution. The Huisken-Sinestrari theorem provides a complete topological classification for two-convex hypersurfaces using MCF with surgery.

Part 1

📏 The Flow and its Short-Time Existence

Parabolic Flow
The Mean Curvature Flow: ∂_t X = −H·n
Let X(p,t): Mⁿ × [0,T) → ℝ^{n+1} be a one-parameter family of smooth immersions of an n-dimensional hypersurface. The mean curvature flow is defined by ∂_t X = −H·n where H = λ₁ + ⋯ + λ_n is the mean curvature (the sum of the principal curvatures) and n is the outward unit normal. The negative sign ensures that convex surfaces shrink to a point (the flow moves in the direction of the inward normal for convex surfaces). The flow is parabolic — the linearization gives the heat operator ∂_t − Δ on the normal bundle, making it a quasilinear parabolic system of PDEs. Short-time existence: if the initial surface M₀ is smooth (or at least C^{2,α}) and locally strictly convex, then there exists a unique smooth solution for a short time T > 0 (this follows from the parabolic PDE theory of quasilinear systems, using the DeTurck trick to break diffeomorphism invariance). The evolution equations: the mean curvature evolves by ∂_t H = ΔH + |A|²H where |A|² = λ₁² + ⋯ + λ_n² is the squared norm of the second fundamental form. This is a heat equation with a potential term: the potential |A|² may blow up at the singularity. The Gauss map evolution: on a convex surface, the unit normal n evolves by ∂_t n = Δn + |A|²n, which implies the normal vector moves by the heat equation on the sphere. Preservation of convexity: if the initial surface is convex (all λ_i ≥ 0), then it remains convex under MCF. More generally, if M₀ is mean convex (H > 0), then H > 0 for all t < T. The maximum principle plays a crucial role: the minimum mean curvature is non-decreasing.
The MCF is a quasilinear parabolic flow that preserves convexity and mean convexity; the evolution of the mean curvature follows a reaction-diffusion equation with a potentially singular source term.
Part 2

🌍 Huisken's Monotonicity Formula and Singularities

Singularity Analysis
Gaussian Density, Type I and Type II Singularities
The Huisken monotonicity formula (1990) is the central tool for analyzing singularities of MCF. For a family of hypersurfaces M_t evolving by MCF, the Gaussian function centered at (x₀, T) is ρ(x,t) = (4π(T−t))^{−n/2} exp(−|x−x₀|² / (4(T−t))). Huisken proved that (d/dt) ∫_{M_t} ρ dA ≤ 0 — the Gaussian weighted area is non-increasing. The Gaussian density at a point (x₀, T) is the limit θ(M_t, (x₀, T)) = lim_{t→T⁻} ∫_{M_t} ρ dA. The monotonicity implies that at each point of the first singularity, the density is well-defined and at least 1 (for a smooth point, the density is 1). Classification of Type I singularities: a singularity is Type I if there exists C > 0 such that max |A|² ≤ C/(T−t). At a Type I singularity, the blow-up (parabolic rescaling) converges to a self-shrinking solution — a smooth solution of MCF that evolves only by scaling: X(p,t) = √(T−t) Y(p) where Y is a hypersurface satisfying H = ⟨Y, n⟩/2 (the self-shrinker equation). Huisken's theorem (1984): the only smooth self-shrinking convex surfaces are spheres (round spheres). For mean convex MCF, the Huisken-Sinestrari theorem (1999) shows that the only Type I singularities are shrinking spheres, shrinking cylinders S^{n−1}×ℝ, and shrinking planes. Type II singularities: these are singularities where |A|² blows up faster than 1/(T−t). The prototypical Type II singularity is the neck-pinch — a dumbbell-shaped surface develops a thin neck that pinches off before the rest of the surface shrinks to a point. The Type II blow-up produces a translating solution (a grim reaper in 2D, or a translating bowl in higher dimensions). The Angenent's torus (Angenent, 1992) is a smooth self-shrinking torus that shrinks to a circle — it is the first non-trivial example of a self-shrinker that is not a sphere, cylinder, or plane. It provides a counterexample to the naive expectation that only spheres can appear as Type I singularities, though it is non-convex.
Gaussian density is monotone under MCF; Type I blow-ups are self-shrinkers classified as spheres, cylinders, and planes for convex surfaces, while Type II blow-ups produce translators like the grim reaper.

🧮 The Brakke Flow and the Level Set Approach

Weak Solutions
Varifold Formulation and Level Set MCF
The Brakke flow (Brakke, 1978) is a varifold formulation of MCF that can continue through singularities. A varifold V is a Radon measure on the Grassmannian of n-planes in ℝ^{n+1}, encoding both the area and the tangent plane information of a possibly singular surface. A family of varifolds {V_t} is a Brakke flow if for all test functions φ ∈ C_c¹(ℝ^{n+1}, ℝ₊), the time derivative of the area satisfies (d/dt) ∫ φ dμ_{V_t} ≤ −∫ φ H² dμ_{V_t} + ∫ |∇^⊥ φ| dμ_{V_t}, where H is the generalized mean curvature of V_t in the sense of first variation. This inequality captures the dissipative nature of MCF: the area decreases at least as fast as the integral of squared mean curvature. Existence: for any initial integer rectifiable varifold with mean curvature in L², there exists a Brakke flow (Brakke's existence theorem). The flow is not unique in general, but it is the canonical weak solution. The level set method (Evans-Spruck, 1991; Chen-Giga-Goto, 1991) provides an alternative approach: let u: ℝ^{n+1}×[0,T] → ℝ be a function such that the zero level set {u(·,t) = 0} represents the evolving surface. The level set MCF equation is ∂_t u = |∇u|·div(∇u/|∇u|) = Σ_{i,j=1}^{n+1} (δ_{ij} − (∂_i u ∂_j u)/|∇u|²) ∂_{ij}u. This is a degenerate elliptic-parabolic equation that can be rewritten as u_t = Δu − (∇²u(∇u,∇u))/|∇u|², where ∇²u is the Hessian. The level set equation is well-posed in the viscosity solution sense: the comparison principle holds (if u₀ ≤ v₀ then u ≤ v for all t), and solutions are unique. The level set approach allows topological changes: when a neck pinches, the zero level set changes topology continuously, and the flow continues uniquely past the singularity. The fattening phenomenon: for some initial data, the level set solution develops interior (the zero level set has non-empty interior), corresponding to non-uniqueness of the geometric evolution.
Brakke flow provides a varifold formulation that exists past singularities, while the level set method gives a unique viscosity solution through topological changes; both are essential for understanding MCF beyond the first singular time.
Part 4

🔥 The Two-Convex MCF and the Huisken-Sinestrari Theorem

Topological Classification
Two-Convexity, MCF with Surgery, and the Classification Theorem
A hypersurface Mⁿ ⊂ ℝ^{n+1} is two-convex if at every point, the sum of the two smallest principal curvatures is positive: λ₁ + λ₂ > 0 (with λ₁ ≤ λ₂ ≤ ⋯ ≤ λ_n). This condition includes convex surfaces (all λ_i ≥ 0) and hypersurfaces with one small negative curvature that is dominated by the next principal curvature. Two-convexity is preserved under MCF and ensures that singularities are mild. The Huisken-Sinestrari theorem (1999, 2009): for a two-convex solution of MCF in ℝ^{n+1} with n ≥ 2, the flow becomes strictly convex before the singular time. Moreover, all singularities are of Type I and are modeled on shrinking spheres. The theorem provides a complete topological classification: any two-convex hypersurface Mⁿ ⊂ ℝ^{n+1} (n ≥ 2) is diffeomorphic to a sphere Sⁿ or a finite connected sum of spheres and copies of S¹×S^{n−1}. This is proved using MCF with surgery (also called the flow with surgery, developed by Huisken-Sinestrari). The idea: as the MCF evolves, when the curvature becomes too large in a region (the neck becomes too thin), one performs surgery — cut out the highly curved region and cap off the two ends with smooth caps. The flow is then restarted. After finitely many surgeries, the remaining pieces are all convex and shrink to round points. The surgery procedure tracks the topology: each surgery removes either a sphere component or an S¹×S^{n−1} component. Applications: the classification theorem shows that in dimensions n ≥ 2, the only topological types that admit two-convex embeddings into Euclidean space are spheres and connected sums of spheres and S¹×S^{n−1} — this is a striking rigidity result. The Angenent's torus (T²) is not two-convex (it has negative curvature points), which is why it can persist as a self-shrinker but cannot appear in the two-convex classification. The higher dimensions: the results extend to n ≥ 2, with the surgery construction requiring careful curvature estimates and neck detection. The Brendle-Huisken theorem (2016): the flow with surgery also works for mean convex MCF, with the same topological conclusions when the flow is two-convex from the start.
Two-convex MCF becomes convex before singularities; MCF with surgery provides a complete topological classification of two-convex hypersurfaces as spheres and connected sums with S¹×S^{n−1}.

Type I and Type II Singularities in Mean Curvature Flow

The classification of singularities is the central analytic problem in mean curvature flow. A singularity occurring at time T is called Type I if the curvature blows up at the maximal possible rate, namely maxMt |A|² ≤ C/(T−t) for some constant C independent of t. Equivalently, after parabolic rescaling λk = 1/√(T−tk) with tk → T, the rescaled flows converge (subsequentially) to a non‑trivial self‑shrinking solution of the flow. Type I singularities are therefore modeled on self‑shrinkers such as the round sphere, the generalized cylinder Sk × ℝn−k, or the Angenent torus in dimension two.

A singularity is Type II if the curvature blows up faster than the Type I rate, i.e. limsupt→T (T−t) max |A|² = ∞. In this case the standard parabolic rescaling does not produce a non‑trivial limit; instead one must use a Type II blow‑up sequence λk = max |A|(·,tk) with tk → T. The resulting limit is an ancient solution defined for all negative times, often a translating soliton (the bowl soliton, the grim reaper cylinder) or a more exotic ancient oval. The distinction is not merely technical: Type I singularities are stable under small perturbations and admit a well‑developed surgery theory (Huisken–Sinestrari), whereas Type II singularities can be unstable and require finer analysis of the asymptotic profile.

Key results: Huisken’s monotonicity formula implies that every blow‑up limit of a Type I singularity is a self‑shrinker with polynomial volume growth. In contrast, White’s regularity theorem shows that if a singularity is Type II and the flow is mean convex, then the blow‑up limit must be a translating soliton. The classification of Type II singularities in the mean convex case was completed by Haslhofer–Kleiner (2017), who proved that the only non‑compact ancient solutions with entropy ≤ 2 are the bowl soliton and the grim reaper cylinder. This dichotomy underpins the surgery program: one cuts out Type I neck regions (modeled on cylinders) and continues the flow, while Type II regions are shown not to occur under the two‑convexity hypothesis.

Ancient Solutions and the Classification of Self‑Shrinkers

An ancient solution of mean curvature flow is a smooth family of hypersurfaces {Mt}t∈(−∞,0) satisfying the flow equation for all negative times. Ancient solutions arise naturally as blow‑up limits at singularities, both Type I (after parabolic rescaling) and Type II (after Type II rescaling). The most important ancient solutions are self‑shrinkers, which satisfy H = ⟨x,ν⟩/2 and evolve by homothetic shrinking Mt = √(−t) M−1. Self‑shrinkers are critical points of the Gaussian area functional F(M) = (4π)−n/2 ∫M e−|x|²/4 dμ and correspond to minimal hypersurfaces in the Gaussian space ℝn+1 with metric e−|x|²/2(n+1) geucl.

The classification program for self‑shrinkers has seen major progress. In dimension n = 1 (curves in the plane), Abresch–Langer (1986) classified all closed self‑shrinking curves: they are the round circle and a countable family of embedded curves with increasing genus. For n ≥ 2, Huisken (1990) proved that the only closed, embedded self‑shrinker with positive mean curvature is the round sphere. The generalized cylinders Sk × ℝn−k are non‑compact embedded self‑shrinkers with entropy λ(Sk × ℝn−k) strictly between the sphere and the plane. Colding–Minicozzi (2012) established a generic multiplicity‑one theorem: for a generic initial hypersurface, every singularity is modeled on a self‑shrinker with multiplicity one, which rules out exotic non‑generic limits.

Beyond self‑shrinkers, translating solitons (solutions moving by constant speed in a fixed direction) appear as Type II blow‑up limits. The bowl soliton (rotationally symmetric, entire graph) and the grim reaper cylinder (product of the grim reaper curve with ℝn−1) are the only non‑collapsed ancient solutions in the mean convex case, as proved by Haslhofer–Hershkovits (2019) using the Andrews–Bryan curvature estimate. The ancient oval (compact, non‑collapsed, non‑self‑similar) was constructed by Angenent–Daskalopoulos–Sesum (2018) in dimension two and later generalized to higher dimensions by Brendle–Choi (2020). These ancient ovals are not self‑shrinkers; they are Type II singularity models that are not mean convex everywhere, illustrating the richness of the ancient solution landscape.

Worked Example: Identifying the Singularity Type
Consider a mean convex mean curvature flow in ℝ³ that develops a singularity at time T. Suppose the maximum curvature satisfies max|A|²(t) ∼ (T−t)−1 |log(T−t)|2 as t → T. Determine whether the singularity is Type I or Type II, and describe the expected blow‑up limit.
The blow‑up rate contains an extra logarithmic factor: (T−t) max|A|² ∼ |log(T−t)|2 → ∞. Hence limsup (T−t) max|A|² = ∞, so the singularity is Type II. The standard Type I rescaling λ = (T−t)−1/2 would send the curvature to infinity, yielding no smooth limit. One must use a Type II rescaling λk = max|A|(·,tk) with tk → T. By the Haslhofer–Kleiner classification of non‑collapsed ancient solutions in the mean convex case, the blow‑up limit is either the bowl soliton or the grim reaper cylinder. Since the flow is mean convex and the singularity is Type II, the limit must be a translating soliton; the precise one depends on the asymptotic geometry of the high‑curvature region.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks to classify a singularity, first compute the scaling exponent of the curvature bound. If the bound is ≤ C/(T−t) it is Type I; any faster growth (including logarithmic factors) makes it Type II. Remember that Type I limits are self‑shrinkers, while Type II limits in the mean convex setting are translating solitons (bowl or grim reaper cylinder). Cite the relevant theorems: Huisken’s monotonicity for Type I, Haslhofer–Kleiner for Type II mean convex classification.

Mean Curvature Flow with Free Boundary Conditions

In many geometric and physical problems the evolving hypersurface meets a fixed supporting hypersurface (the barrier) along its boundary, and the contact angle is prescribed. The most common condition is the free boundary condition (also called the Neumann condition): the evolving hypersurface meets the barrier orthogonally, i.e. the conormal of the flow is tangent to the barrier. Analytically, if the barrier is given by a smooth hypersurface ∂Ω and the flow is Mt ⊂ Ω with ∂Mt ⊂ ∂Ω, the condition reads νM = ν∂Ω on ∂Mt, where ν denotes the outward unit normal. This condition arises naturally as the gradient flow of the area functional with respect to variations that keep the boundary on ∂Ω.

Short‑time existence for smooth initial data satisfying the compatibility condition (orthogonal intersection) was proved by Stahl (1996) using the DeTurck trick and maximal regularity theory. The flow preserves the free boundary condition and the mean convexity of the barrier. A monotonicity formula adapted to the free boundary setting was derived by Buckland (2005) and later refined by Edelen (2020); it involves the Gaussian weight centered at a point on the barrier and a reflection argument. This formula yields entropy bounds and enables blow‑up analysis at boundary singularities.

Singularities at the boundary can be of two types: interior‑type (the high‑curvature region stays away from the boundary) and boundary‑type (the curvature concentrates at the boundary). For boundary‑type singularities, the blow‑up limits are self‑shrinkers in a half‑space meeting the boundary plane orthogonally. The classification of such half‑space self‑shrinkers was achieved by Edelen–Huisken (2021): the only smooth, embedded, non‑flat examples are the half‑sphere Sn₊ and the half‑cylinder Sk₊ × ℝn−k. This mirrors the interior classification and shows that boundary singularities are also modeled on simple geometries. Surgery at the boundary has been developed by Hershkovits–White (2022), allowing the flow to continue past boundary singularities while preserving the free boundary condition.

Applications include the proof of the free boundary isoperimetric inequality in convex domains (via MCF with free boundary) and the study of capillary surfaces. The flow also provides a natural deformation retraction of the space of hypersurfaces with free boundary onto the space of minimal hypersurfaces with free boundary, yielding topological information about the moduli space.

Applications to Geometric Inequalities: Willmore and Isoperimetric Problems

Mean curvature flow is a powerful tool for proving sharp geometric inequalities. The Willmore functional W(M) = ∫M H² dμ (for surfaces in ℝ³) is the L² norm of the mean curvature. It is invariant under conformal transformations of ℝ³ and its critical points are Willmore surfaces. The flow that decreases the Willmore energy is the Willmore flow, a fourth‑order parabolic equation ∂t x = −ΔH − H|A°|². However, the standard MCF also interacts with the Willmore energy: Huisken (1984) showed that for a convex surface, the quantity ∫ H² dμ is non‑increasing along MCF after appropriate rescaling. This monotonicity was used by Kuwert–Schätzle (2001) to prove the Willmore conjecture for tori of revolution, and later by Marques–Neves (2014) via min‑max theory (not directly MCF) to settle the full conjecture.

The isoperimetric inequality states that among all hypersurfaces enclosing a given volume, the sphere minimizes area. MCF provides a dynamical proof: start with any smooth hypersurface enclosing a fixed volume, evolve by volume‑preserving MCF (the normal velocity is H − ⟨H⟩, where ⟨H⟩ is the average mean curvature). Huisken (1987) proved that for convex initial data the flow exists for all time and converges exponentially to a round sphere, thereby proving the isoperimetric inequality in the convex case. For non‑convex data, singularities may form; the weak formulation (Brakke flow with a volume constraint) was developed by Mugnai–Seis (2015) and later by Lira–Mazzeo (2020) to handle topological changes while preserving volume. The long‑time behavior of the weak flow still converges to a sphere, giving a complete proof of the isoperimetric inequality in all dimensions.

Another striking application is the proof of the Alexandrov theorem (embedded constant mean curvature hypersurfaces are spheres) using MCF. If an embedded hypersurface has constant mean curvature, it is a self‑similar solution of the volume‑preserving flow. By running the flow backwards (which is ill‑posed) one cannot directly argue, but a forward argument using the inverse mean curvature flow (IMCF) developed by Huisken–Ilmanen (2001) proves the Riemannian Penrose inequality and, as a corollary, the Alexandrov theorem for star‑shaped hypersurfaces. The IMCF expands the surface with speed 1/H and preserves the Hawking mass monotonicity, linking MCF to general relativity.

Higher Codimension Mean Curvature Flow and the Role of the Normal Bundle

When the evolving submanifold has codimension greater than one, the mean curvature vector H is a normal vector field, and the flow equation is ∂t x = H. The geometry of the normal bundle becomes crucial: the second fundamental form A takes values in the normal bundle, and its squared norm |A|² controls the evolution of the curvature. The reaction term in the evolution equation for |A|² involves the normal curvature tensor R⊥, which measures the failure of the normal connection to be flat. In codimension one, R⊥ = 0 automatically, but in higher codimension it can be positive, leading to additional curvature growth that may cause singularities even for initially parallel submanifolds.

Short‑time existence for smooth compact submanifolds in arbitrary codimension follows from the same parabolic theory (Hamilton, 1982). However, the maximum principle for the curvature tensor is more delicate. Andrews–Baker (2010) developed a pinching estimate for the second fundamental form in higher codimension, showing that if the initial submanifold satisfies a quadratic pinching condition |A|² ≤ c |H|² with c < 1/(n−1), then the pinching is preserved and the flow converges to a round point after rescaling. This generalizes Huisken’s convexity pinching to higher codimension.

Singularities in higher codimension can be more varied. The Clifford torus Sm(√m) × Sn−m(√(n−m)) ⊂ ℝn+1 is a minimal submanifold (hence a static solution) but not a self‑shrinker. Self‑shrinkers in higher codimension include products of lower‑dimensional self‑shrinkers and the Lawlor neck (a self‑shrinking cylinder in ℝ⁴). The classification of self‑shrinkers in higher codimension remains open; recent work by Bernstein–Wang (2022) shows that any complete embedded self‑shrinker with polynomial volume growth and bounded second fundamental form must be a product of a lower‑dimensional self‑shrinker with a Euclidean factor, under an entropy assumption.

The Brakke flow and level set method extend to higher codimension using integral currents (for Brakke) and the viscosity theory of geometric PDEs (for level sets). However, the non‑uniqueness phenomena are more pronounced: fattening can occur even for smooth initial data in codimension ≥ 2, as shown by Ilmanen–Neves–Schulze (2019). The mean curvature flow with surgery in higher codimension has been initiated by Haslhofer–Ketover (2020) for two‑convex submanifolds, where the two‑convexity condition is replaced by a positivity condition on the sum of the two smallest eigenvalues of the second fundamental form in any normal direction. This program aims to classify the topology of two‑convex submanifolds in Euclidean space, generalizing the Huisken–Sinestrari theorem.

Entropy, Gaussian Density, and the Colding–Minicozzi Generic Regularity Theory

The Gaussian density (or entropy) of a self‑shrinker Σ is defined as Θ(Σ) = (4π)⁻ⁿᐟ² ∫_Σ e⁻|x|²ᐟ⁴ dμ. For a general MCF, Huisken’s monotonicity formula shows that the Gaussian density centered at a singular point (x₀, t₀) is non‑increasing in the backward time parameter and its limit equals the entropy of the tangent flow. Colding and Minicozzi (2012) introduced a robust framework: they proved that the entropy is upper semicontinuous under smooth convergence and that any sequence of MCF with uniformly bounded entropy has a subsequence converging to a Brakke flow. The central conjecture — generic multiplicity one — asserts that for a generic initial hypersurface, every singularity is modeled by a multiplicity‑one self‑shrinker. Colding–Minicozzi established this for mean‑convex flows and, using the Łojasiewicz–Simon gradient inequality for the F‑functional, showed that the only stable self‑shrinkers with polynomial volume growth are the sphere, the cylinder, and the plane. Their work implies that high‑multiplicity tangent flows (e.g., a cylinder with multiplicity k ≥ 2) are non‑generic and can be perturbed away. The entropy also provides a topological invariant: if the initial entropy is less than the entropy of the cylinder (≈ 1.52 in ℝ³), then the flow cannot develop a cylindrical singularity and must become extinct in a round point. This entropy gap theorem has been sharpened by Bernstein–Wang (2017) and Hershkovits–White (2019) to characterize low‑entropy flows completely.

Mean Curvature Flow in Riemannian Manifolds: Ambient Curvature and Long‑Time Behavior

When the ambient space is a Riemannian manifold (M, g) rather than Euclidean space, the MCF equation becomes ∂ₜx = H where H is the mean curvature vector with respect to g. Short‑time existence for compact hypersurfaces follows from the same parabolic theory (Hamilton, 1982), but the evolution equations for geometric quantities acquire extra terms involving the ambient Riemann curvature tensor Rm. For the second fundamental form A, the reaction term gains a component ⟨Rm(ν, ·)ν, ·⟩ where ν is the unit normal. If the ambient manifold has non‑negative sectional curvature, this term has a favorable sign and Huisken’s convexity preservation extends: a strictly convex hypersurface in a manifold of non‑negative sectional curvature remains convex and shrinks to a round point (Huisken, 1986). In positive sectional curvature, the flow exists for all time and converges to a constant‑mean‑curvature hypersurface after rescaling. For entire graphs over a domain in a Riemannian manifold, Ecker–Huisken (1991) proved long‑time existence without growth assumptions on the graph, provided the ambient manifold has bounded geometry and the initial graph has bounded slope. The asymptotic behavior is governed by the minimal hypersurface equation; if the manifold admits no non‑trivial minimal graphs, the flow converges to a slice. In asymptotically flat manifolds, the inverse mean curvature flow (IMCF) of Huisken–Ilmanen (2001) uses the weak level‑set formulation to prove the Riemannian Penrose inequality, linking MCF to the ADM mass and black hole physics. The volume‑preserving MCF in Riemannian manifolds, studied by Huisken (1987) and later by Metzger–Schulze (2012), converges to a constant‑mean‑curvature hypersurface under a pinching condition involving the ambient Ricci curvature.

Worked Example: Entropy Gap and Singularity Type
Consider a smooth, closed, embedded hypersurface M₀ ⊂ ℝ³ with Gaussian entropy λ(M₀) < λ(S¹ × ℝ) ≈ 1.52. Prove that the MCF starting at M₀ cannot develop a Type I singularity modeled on a cylinder.
By Huisken's monotonicity formula, the Gaussian density Θ(x₀,t₀) at any singular point (x₀, t₀) satisfies Θ(x₀,t₀) ≤ λ(M₀). If a Type I singularity occurs, a tangent flow at (x₀, t₀) is a self‑shrinker Σ with multiplicity m ≥ 1. The Gaussian density of the tangent flow equals m · λ(Σ). Since λ(M₀) < λ(S¹ × ℝ), we must have m = 1 and λ(Σ) < λ(S¹ × ℝ). The classification of self‑shrinkers with entropy below the cylinder (Bernstein–Wang, 2017) shows that the only such self‑shrinkers are the plane (λ = 1) and the sphere (λ ≈ 1.33). A plane gives a regular point, not a singularity. A sphere gives a round point singularity (Type I). Therefore a cylindrical singularity (λ = λ(S¹ × ℝ)) is impossible. The flow must either become extinct in a round point or develop only Type II singularities.
Exam Strategy: Entropy Bounds
When a problem gives an entropy bound λ < λ(cylinder), immediately recall the entropy gap theorem: the flow cannot form neckpinches. The only possible singularities are spherical (round points) or Type II. If the initial surface is mean‑convex, Huisken–Sinestrari surgery applies and the flow becomes extinct in finite time with only spherical singularities. Memorize the numerical values: λ(plane) = 1, λ(sphere) = (4π)⁻¹ᐟ² ∫S² e⁻¹ᐟ² dμ ≈ 1.33, λ(cylinder) ≈ 1.52 in ℝ³.

Quantitative Stratification of the Singular Set and ε‑Regularity Theorems

Classical regularity theory (Brakke, Huisken, White) shows that the singular set of an MCF has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most n−1. Quantitative stratification, developed by Cheeger–Naber (2015) for Ricci flow and adapted to MCF by Naber–Valtorta (2017) and Edelen (2020), refines this by measuring how close a singularity is to a static or self‑similar model at a given scale. For a Brakke flow, the k‑th quantitative stratum S^k_ε,r consists of points where the flow is not ε‑close (in the varifold sense) to a product of a k‑dimensional Euclidean factor and a self‑shrinker, at any scale between r and 1. The main theorem states that the parabolic Minkowski dimension of S^k_ε,r is at most k, with volume bounds Vol(T_r(S^k_ε,r)) ≤ C rn+1−k. This implies the optimal Hausdorff dimension bound dim_H(S^k) ≤ k. The ε‑regularity theorem (White, 2005; Colding–Minicozzi, 2015) says: if at some scale the Gaussian density is within ε of 1 (the plane), then the flow is graphical and smooth at a smaller scale, with quantitative C1,α estimates. For mean‑convex flows, White's local regularity theorem gives that any point with density < 2 is regular. The quantitative approach yields effective estimates: the number of ε‑necks at scale r is bounded by C ε⁻² r⁻¹, which underpins the surgery construction of Haslhofer–Kleiner (2017) and Hershkovits–White (2019). Recent work by Bamler (2023) uses entropy and quantitative stratification to prove a structure theorem for ancient low‑entropy flows, showing they are either compact, cylindrical, or translating solitons.

Mean Curvature Flow of Networks: Triple Junctions and Multi‑Phase Flow

When several hypersurfaces meet along a common interface, the MCF generalizes to network flow. In the plane, a network is a union of curves meeting at triple junctions with 120° angles (Herring's condition). The flow equation is ∂ₜx = κ ν on each curve, with the junction condition that the sum of unit tangents vanishes (force balance). Short‑time existence for regular networks was proved by Mantegazza–Novaga–Tortorelli (2004) using parabolic PDE theory on manifolds with boundary. Singularities can be of two types: curve shrinking to a point (Type I, modeled by a self‑similar shrinking network) or junction collision. The classification of self‑similar shrinking networks in ℝ² is known: the only embedded ones are the standard triple junction (three rays at 120°) and the "lens" shaped networks (Ilmanen–Neves–Schulze, 2014). In higher dimensions, clusters of hypersurfaces (e.g., soap bubbles) evolve by MCF with the same 120° condition along the common interface. The Brakke flow formulation extends to clusters using integral varifolds with multiplicities (Almgren, 1976; White, 1997). The long‑time behavior of planar networks is not fully understood; some networks develop infinite length in finite time (Type II), while others converge to a minimal network. For the multi‑phase MCF in ℝⁿ (n ≥ 3), the level‑set method of Evans–Spruck and Chen–Giga–Goto handles fattening via the viscosity solution of a system of PDEs. The uniqueness of the level‑set flow for clusters is a delicate issue; Kim–Tonegawa (2003) proved uniqueness for mean‑convex clusters. The motion of grain boundaries in materials science is modeled by network MCF with anisotropic surface tensions, leading to a modified junction condition (Young's law).

The Ecker–Huisken Theory for Entire Graphs and Long‑Time Existence

Ecker and Huisken (1989, 1991) developed a complete theory for MCF of entire graphs u: ℝⁿ → ℝ without any growth assumptions at infinity. The graph evolves by the equation ∂ₜu = √(1+|Du|²) div(Du/√(1+|Du|²)). The key a priori estimate is the gradient bound: if the initial graph has bounded slope |Du₀| ≤ C₀, then |Du(·, t)| ≤ C₀ for all t > 0. This follows from the maximum principle applied to the quantity v = √(1+|Du|²), which satisfies ∂ₜv = Δv − |A|² v. The gradient bound implies uniform parabolicity, yielding global C2,α estimates and long‑time existence. The asymptotic behavior depends on the initial data: if u₀ is asymptotically conical, the flow converges to a self‑similar expanding solution (a translating soliton). If u₀ is bounded, the flow converges to a constant (a plane). For graphs with linear growth, the flow converges to a grim reaper cylinder (a translating soliton) after rescaling. The Ecker–Huisken theory also gives a Liouville theorem: any ancient entire graphical solution with bounded slope must be a translating soliton or a static plane. This classification was extended by Wang (2011) to ancient solutions with polynomial volume growth. The theory has applications to the level‑set method: the viscosity solution of the level‑set equation coincides with the graphical flow when the initial set is a subgraph. In Riemannian manifolds with bounded geometry, Ecker–Huisken (1991) proved the same gradient estimate holds if the ambient manifold has non‑negative sectional curvature, giving long‑time existence for entire graphs in such manifolds.

Mean Curvature Flow with Surgery: Hamilton–Huisken–Sinestrari Program

The classical mean curvature flow develops singularities in finite time for most initial hypersurfaces. To continue the flow past singularities, Hamilton proposed a surgery procedure for three‑dimensional Ricci flow, and Huisken–Sinestrari adapted the idea to mean curvature flow of two‑convex hypersurfaces in ℝⁿ⁺¹ (n ≥ 2). A hypersurface is two‑convex if the sum of its two smallest principal curvatures is positive everywhere. Under this condition, singularities are modeled by neck‑like regions that are ε‑close to a shrinking cylinder Sⁿ⁻¹ × ℝ. The surgery algorithm excises the neck at a carefully chosen cross‑section, caps the resulting boundary components by strictly convex caps, and restarts the smooth flow. Huisken–Sinestrari (1999, 2009) proved that the surgery parameters can be chosen so that the flow exists for all time, the number of surgeries is finite on any compact time interval, and the post‑surgery hypersurfaces remain two‑convex with uniform curvature bounds. The key analytic ingredients are a curvature pinching estimate that improves under the flow, a canonical neighborhood theorem that classifies high‑curvature regions as either ε‑necks or ε‑caps, and a precise control of the geometry of the surgery caps to preserve the two‑convexity condition. The long‑time behavior of the surgically modified flow was later clarified by Brendle–Huisken (2017), who showed that after finitely many surgeries the flow becomes extinct in finite time or converges to a round sphere after appropriate rescaling. This program provides a complete classification of the topology of two‑convex hypersurfaces: they are diffeomorphic to a connected sum of spheres Sⁿ and handles Sⁿ⁻¹ × S¹. The surgery technique has also been extended to mean curvature flow with free boundary conditions (Hershkovits–White, 2019) and to higher codimension under suitable convexity assumptions (Andrews–Baker, 2010).

Hamilton's Differential Harnack Inequality for Mean Curvature Flow

In 1993 Richard Hamilton discovered a differential Harnack inequality for the mean curvature flow that mirrors his celebrated Harnack estimate for the Ricci flow. For a smooth solution Mₜ of MCF in ℝⁿ⁺¹, define the quantity Z = ∂ₜH + ∇H·X + H/(2t), where H is the mean curvature and X is the position vector. Hamilton proved that if the initial hypersurface is convex, then Z ≥ 0 for all t > 0. Equivalently, the matrix inequality ∂ₜhᵢⱼ + ∇ₖhᵢⱼ Xᵏ + hᵢⱼ/(2t) ≥ 0 holds in the sense of quadratic forms, where hᵢⱼ is the second fundamental form. This inequality has profound consequences: it implies monotonicity of the isoperimetric ratio, gives a sharp lower bound for the mean curvature at the tip of a convex ancient solution, and yields a Liouville‑type theorem stating that any complete ancient convex solution with bounded curvature must be a shrinking sphere, a shrinking cylinder, or a translating soliton (the bowl soliton). The proof uses the maximum principle on the evolution equation for the symmetric 2‑tensor Mᵢⱼ = ∂ₜhᵢⱼ + ∇ᵢ∇ⱼH + H hᵢₖ hⱼₖ + hᵢⱼ/(2t). Hamilton's computation shows that M satisfies a parabolic inequality with a non‑negative reaction term, allowing the maximum principle to preserve non‑negativity. The Harnack inequality was later generalized by Andrews (1994) to fully nonlinear curvature flows and by Ni (2004) to mean curvature flow in Riemannian manifolds with non‑negative sectional curvature. In the context of singularity analysis, the Harnack inequality provides a pointwise control that rules out Type II singularities for convex flows, forcing all singularities to be Type I and therefore modeled by self‑shrinkers. This fact is a cornerstone of Huisken's convexity estimate and the subsequent classification of convex ancient solutions by Haslhofer–Hershkovits (2015).

Worked Example: Applying Hamilton's Harnack Inequality
Let Mₜ be a smooth, strictly convex solution of MCF in ℝ³ defined for t ∈ (0, T). Show that the quantity Q = t Hₘₐₓ(t) is non‑decreasing in time, where Hₘₐₓ(t) = maxMₜ H.
By Hamilton's Harnack inequality, for any point (x, t) we have ∂ₜH + ∇H·X + H/(2t) ≥ 0. At a spatial maximum of H at time t, ∇H = 0 and ∂ₜH = d/dt Hₘₐₓ(t). The position vector X satisfies X·ν = ⟨x, ν⟩, but the inequality holds pointwise without needing X·ν. Evaluating at the maximum point gives d/dt Hₘₐₓ + Hₘₐₓ/(2t) ≥ 0. Multiply by 2t: 2t d/dt Hₘₐₓ + Hₘₐₓ ≥ 0. This is exactly d/dt (t Hₘₐₓ) ≥ 0. Hence Q(t) = t Hₘₐₓ(t) is non‑decreasing.

White's Regularity Theorem and the Structure of Singular Sets

Brian White's regularity theory (2000, 2005) gives a precise description of the singular set of a Brakke flow in ℝⁿ⁺¹. For an integral Brakke flow with bounded Gaussian density, White proved that the singular set S has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most n‑1. More importantly, he established a stratification: for each integer k = 0, …, n‑1, the k‑th stratum Sₖ consists of points where no tangent flow splits off a factor ℝᵏ⁺¹. White showed that Sₖ has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most k, and that at almost every point of Sₖ (with respect to k‑dimensional parabolic Hausdorff measure) the tangent flow is unique and splits exactly ℝᵏ. This result is the parabolic analogue of Almgren's stratification for stationary varifolds. The proof combines Huisken's monotonicity formula, a compactness theorem for Brakke flows, and a delicate blow‑up analysis using the Gaussian density as a Lyapunov functional. White also proved an ε‑regularity theorem: if the Gaussian density at a point is sufficiently close to 1, then the flow is regular in a parabolic neighborhood of that point. This ε‑regularity is the foundation for the quantitative stratification developed later by Naber–Valtorta (2017). White's theorem applies to general Brakke flows without any convexity or mean‑convexity assumptions, making it the most general regularity result for MCF. It implies, for instance, that the singular set of a mean‑convex flow in ℝ⁴ has dimension at most 2, and that the set of points where the flow is not a smooth hypersurface with multiplicity one is countably (n‑1)‑rectifiable.

Mean Curvature Flow of Lagrangian Submanifolds and the Thomas–Yau Conjecture

When the ambient manifold is a Calabi–Yau manifold (M, ω, Ω) with Kähler form ω and holomorphic volume form Ω, a Lagrangian submanifold Lⁿ ⊂ M satisfies ω|ₗ = 0. The mean curvature flow of Lagrangians preserves the Lagrangian condition because the mean curvature vector H is given by H = J∇θ, where θ is the Lagrangian angle defined by Ω|ₗ = eiθ dvolₗ and J is the complex structure. The flow equation ∂ₜx = H then becomes a parabolic equation for the Lagrangian angle: ∂ₜθ = Δθ. This linearity of the angle evolution is a special feature of Lagrangian MCF. Smoczyk (2000) proved long‑time existence and convergence for Lagrangian graphs with small slope, and Neves (2007) established a singularity formation criterion: if the Lagrangian angle stays within an interval of length < π, the flow exists for all time and converges to a special Lagrangian (θ constant). The Thomas–Yau conjecture (2002) proposes a stability condition for Lagrangians analogous to Mumford–Takemoto stability for vector bundles: a Lagrangian should be stable if and only if it admits a special Lagrangian representative in its Hamiltonian isotopy class. The conjecture links the existence of a special Lagrangian to the long‑time behavior of the Lagrangian MCF. Joyce–Lee–Tsui (2017) constructed examples of Lagrangian MCF developing finite‑time singularities where the Lagrangian angle range exceeds π, supporting the necessity of the angle bound. Recent work by Jacob–Yau (2020) and Collins–Jacob–Yau (2022) has made progress on the conjecture for Lagrangians with bounded Lagrangian angle, using a variational approach combined with the flow. The Lagrangian MCF also plays a central role in the Strominger–Yau–Zaslow picture of mirror symmetry, where special Lagrangian fibrations are expected to be attractors of the flow.

Numerical Approximation of Mean Curvature Flow: Convergence and Error Estimates

Practical computation of MCF requires stable numerical schemes that respect the geometric structure. The most widely used method is the parametric finite element method introduced by Dziuk (1990) for curves and extended to surfaces by Dziuk–Elliott (2007). The scheme discretizes the hypersurface by a triangulated polyhedral surface and evolves the vertices by the discrete mean curvature vector Hₕ = Δₕ id, where Δₕ is the cotangent Laplacian. This semi‑implicit scheme is unconditionally stable and preserves the variational structure: it is the gradient flow of the discrete area functional. Convergence results were proved by Deckelnick–Dziuk (2000) for graphs and by Barrett–Nürnberg (2004) for closed surfaces: if the exact solution is smooth on [0, T], the discrete solution converges in L∞(0, T; H¹) with order h² in space and Δt in time, where h is the mesh size. For singular flows, the level‑set method of Osher–Sethian (1988) and its viscosity solution framework (Evans–Spruck, Chen–Giga–Goto) provide a robust alternative. The level‑set equation ∂ₜu = |∇u| div(∇u/|∇u|) is discretized by monotone finite difference schemes (e.g., the scheme of Oberbeck–Sethian). Convergence to the viscosity solution follows from the Barles–Souganidis framework. Error estimates for the level‑set method are more delicate; a typical result gives O(√Δx) convergence in the Hausdorff distance for the zero level set. Recent advances include the threshold dynamics (Merriman–Bence–Osher) and the diffuse interface (Allen–Cahn) approximations, where the MCF arises as the sharp‑interface limit ε → 0 of the Allen–Cahn equation ∂ₜuε = Δuε − (1/ε²)W'(uε). Convergence rates of order ε|log ε| have been established by Bronsard–Kohn (1991) and Ilmanen (1993) using the theory of varifolds. For high‑order accuracy, the DeTurck trick (DeTurck, 1983) can be adapted to MCF to eliminate tangential motion and improve conditioning of the linear systems.

Mean Curvature Flow with Forcing Terms and Applications to Image Processing

When a forcing term is added to the mean curvature flow equation, the evolution becomes ∂ₜx = H + Fν, where F is a prescribed scalar function on the evolving hypersurface and ν is the unit normal. This generalization appears naturally in models of interface motion with external driving forces, such as solidification, combustion fronts, and image segmentation. The short‑time existence theory parallels the classical case: if the initial hypersurface is smooth and uniformly parabolic, a unique smooth solution exists on a maximal time interval. The forcing term can be chosen to preserve volume, to enforce a prescribed enclosed volume, or to implement a fidelity term in variational image processing.

In the level‑set formulation, the forced flow corresponds to the Hamilton–Jacobi equation ∂ₜu = |∇u| (div(∇u/|∇u|) + F). Viscosity solution theory, developed by Evans–Spruck and Chen–Giga–Goto, extends to this setting provided F is Lipschitz in space and time. The comparison principle holds, guaranteeing uniqueness of the level‑set evolution. For numerical implementation, the forcing term is discretized explicitly or semi‑implicitly; stability requires a CFL condition linking the time step to the mesh size and the supremum of |F|.

Applications to image processing exploit the fact that the zero level set of u evolves by curvature plus a data‑driven force. The classical Chan–Vese model uses F = λ₁(I − c₁)² − λ₂(I − c₂)², where I is the image intensity and c₁, c₂ are region averages. This force drives the contour toward edges while the curvature term regularizes the contour. Convergence of the discrete scheme to the viscosity solution was proved by Oberbeck and Sethian (1999) using the Barles–Souganidis framework. Error estimates in the Hausdorff distance are of order √Δx for monotone schemes, and higher‑order accuracy can be achieved by adding a tangential redistribution term (the DeTurck trick) that does not affect the geometric evolution.

Recent work by Esedoḡlu and Tsai (2006) introduced a threshold dynamics (Merriman–Bence–Osher) scheme for forced flows, where the forcing is incorporated through a convolution step. This method is unconditionally stable and converges to the viscosity solution as the time step tends to zero. In the context of multiphase segmentation, the forcing term becomes vector‑valued, leading to a system of coupled level‑set equations. The well‑posedness of such systems relies on the monotonicity of the forcing with respect to the phase indicator functions.

Anisotropic Mean Curvature Flow and Crystalline Energies

Anisotropic mean curvature flow replaces the isotropic surface tension by a norm γ(ν) that depends on the normal direction. The flow equation reads ∂ₜx = div(∇γ(ν)) ν, where ∇γ is the Cahn–Hoffman vector field. When γ is smooth and uniformly convex, the flow is strictly parabolic and the classical theory of Huisken and Ecker–Huisken applies: short‑time existence, interior estimates, and a monotonicity formula with the anisotropic Gaussian density hold. The anisotropic Huisken monotonicity formula uses the weight ρ(x,t) = (4π(T−t))⁻ⁿᐟ² exp(−γ*(x)²/(4(T−t))), where γ* is the dual norm.

For crystalline energies, γ is a piecewise linear convex function whose unit ball is a polytope (the Wulff shape). The flow is no longer parabolic in the classical sense; facets with normals corresponding to flat portions of γ evolve by a system of ODEs coupled through the motion of edges. The mathematical framework uses the notion of crystalline curvature, defined as the first variation of the anisotropic perimeter. Short‑time existence for crystalline flows was established by Giga and Gurtin (1996) via a variational minimizing‑movement scheme (the Almgren–Taylor–Wang scheme). The scheme produces a sequence of discrete surfaces minimizing the sum of the anisotropic perimeter and a squared distance term; the limit is a weak solution satisfying a comparison principle.

Singularities in anisotropic flow are classified by the anisotropic Gaussian density. For smooth γ, Type I singularities are modeled by self‑shrinkers of the anisotropic flow, which satisfy H_γ = ⟨x,ν⟩/2. The classification of anisotropic self‑shrinkers is known only in low dimensions: in the plane, the only compact self‑shrinkers are the Wulff shape and its translates. In higher dimensions, the work of Andrews (2003) shows that convex self‑shrinkers are necessarily Wulff shapes. For crystalline flows, singularities correspond to facet breaking or edge merging; the analysis uses the notion of “faceted” self‑similar solutions studied by Bellettini, Novaga, and Paolini (2001).

Numerical approximation of anisotropic flow employs the same parametric finite element method with the cotangent Laplacian replaced by the anisotropic stiffness matrix derived from γ. For crystalline γ, the stiffness matrix is singular on facets; a regularization γ_ε = γ + ε|ν|² restores ellipticity and yields convergence as ε → 0. Error estimates for the regularized scheme are of order ε + h² + Δt. The threshold dynamics method extends to anisotropic energies by using an anisotropic convolution kernel; convergence to the crystalline flow was proved by Esedoḡlu, Otto, and Thiele (2015).

Worked Example: Anisotropic Self‑Shrinker in the Plane
Let γ(ν) = √(ν₁² + 4ν₂²) be an elliptic anisotropy. Find the compact self‑shrinker of the anisotropic mean curvature flow in ℝ² and verify that it is a homothetically shrinking ellipse.
The anisotropic curvature for a curve with normal ν is H_γ = div(∇γ(ν)). For γ(ν) = √(ν₁² + 4ν₂²), the dual norm is γ*(x) = √(x₁² + x₂²/4). The self‑shrinker equation is H_γ = ⟨x,ν⟩/2. Because γ is smooth and uniformly convex, the only compact self‑shrinker is the Wulff shape scaled by √(2(T−t)). The Wulff shape is the unit ball of γ*, i.e. the ellipse x₁² + x₂²/4 = 1. Under homothetic shrinking x(t) = √(2(T−t)) x₀, the normal ν is unchanged and ⟨x,ν⟩ = √(2(T−t)) ⟨x₀,ν⟩. The anisotropic curvature of the ellipse is constant and equals 1/√(2(T−t)). Substituting gives 1/√(2(T−t)) = √(2(T−t)) ⟨x₀,ν⟩/2, which holds because ⟨x₀,ν⟩ = 2/γ*(x₀) = 2 on the Wulff shape. Hence the ellipse x₁² + x₂²/4 = 2(T−t) is the unique compact self‑shrinker.

Mean Curvature Flow in Lorentzian and Pseudo‑Riemannian Manifolds

In a Lorentzian manifold (M,g) with signature (−,+,…,+), the mean curvature vector of a spacelike hypersurface is defined as the trace of the second fundamental form with respect to the induced metric. The flow equation ∂ₜx = H is no longer parabolic; it becomes a hyperbolic system because the normal direction is timelike. Short‑time existence for spacelike initial data was proved by Ecker (1997) using a Nash–Moser iteration adapted to the hyperbolic character. The solution exists as long as the hypersurface remains spacelike and the second fundamental form stays bounded.

A key difference from the Riemannian case is the absence of a maximum principle for the Lorentzian flow. Instead, one uses energy estimates derived from the wave‑type equation satisfied by the second fundamental form. The Lorentzian Huisken monotonicity formula does not hold in general; however, for flows in Minkowski space, a conserved quantity analogous to the spacetime volume can be used to control the evolution. Cabezas‑Rivas and Miquel (2015) established a monotonicity formula for the Lorentzian flow in the presence of a timelike Killing vector field, which yields a Gaussian‑type density that is non‑increasing along the flow.

Singularities in the Lorentzian flow are of two types: spacelike singularities where the hypersurface becomes null, and curvature singularities where the second fundamental form blows up while the hypersurface stays spacelike. The classification of self‑similar solutions (solitons) in Minkowski space includes the Lorentzian analogues of spheres (hyperboloids) and cylinders. The hyperboloid Hⁿ = {x₀² − |x|² = 1} evolves by homothetic expansion, while the light cone is a stationary solution with zero mean curvature. The stability of these solitons under perturbations was studied by LeFloch and Smulevici (2016) using weighted energy spaces.

Applications arise in general relativity, where the flow of spacelike hypersurfaces is used to construct foliations of constant mean curvature (CMC) in asymptotically flat spacetimes. The Lorentzian flow also appears in the study of the inverse mean curvature flow in Riemannian manifolds via the “hyperboloidal” compactification introduced by Huisken and Ilmanen (2001). Numerical schemes for the Lorentzian flow must respect the causal structure; a common approach is to use a finite difference discretization of the wave equation for the graph function, combined with a CFL condition that respects the light cone.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Min‑Max Construction of Minimal Surfaces

The min‑max theory for minimal surfaces, developed by Almgren, Pitts, and later refined by Marques and Neves, produces minimal hypersurfaces as critical points of the area functional. A deep connection with mean curvature flow was discovered by Colding, De Lellis, and others: a min‑max sequence can be realized as a sequence of mean curvature flows with surgery, where the surgery parameters are chosen to preserve the min‑max width. This perspective yields a dynamical proof of the existence of infinitely many minimal hypersurfaces in generic metrics.

In the work of Marques and Neves (2014, 2017), the min‑max width is defined via sweepouts by hypersurfaces. One constructs a family of mean curvature flows starting from each slice of the sweepout, performs surgery at necks whose radius falls below a threshold depending on the width, and continues the flow. The key estimate is that the area of the flow after surgery does not exceed the min‑max width plus a small error. By passing to a limit, one obtains a stationary integral varifold that is a minimal hypersurface with multiplicity one. The regularity of the limit follows from the generic regularity theory of Colding–Minicozzi (2015) combined with White’s ε‑regularity.

The surgery parameters in this program differ from those in the Hamilton–Huisken–Sinestrari program for mean‑convex flows. The neck threshold is chosen proportional to the square root of the min‑max width, and the cap geometry is modeled on the standard cylindrical cap in ℝⁿ⁺¹. The surgery is performed only when the flow is sufficiently close to a cylindrical neck in the sense of the Gaussian density. This ensures that the topology of the sweepout is preserved and that the min‑max width is not increased.

Recent advances by Zhou (2020) and Li (2022) extend the min‑max/MCF correspondence to higher codimension and to the setting of free boundary minimal surfaces. In the free boundary case, the flow is coupled with a Neumann condition on the boundary of the ambient manifold, and the surgery must respect the boundary contact angle. The resulting minimal surfaces solve a Plateau problem with free boundary, and their Morse index is bounded by the number of parameters in the min‑max construction.

Mean Curvature Flow of Varifolds with Multiplicity and Brakke Regularity

Brakke’s formulation of mean curvature flow allows the evolving object to be an integral varifold with integer multiplicity. The flow satisfies the inequality d/dt ∫ φ dμₜ ≤ ∫ (−φ|H|² + ∇φ·H) dμₜ for all non‑negative test functions φ. When the initial varifold has multiplicity greater than one, the flow may instantly drop multiplicity due to the “sudden vanishing” phenomenon discovered by Brakke. Ilmanen’s elliptic regularization (1994) constructs a Brakke flow from a given initial varifold by approximating it with smooth flows of the Allen–Cahn equation and passing to the limit.

White’s regularity theory (2005) for Brakke flows with multiplicity shows that if the Gaussian density at a point is strictly less than 2, then the flow is regular in a parabolic neighborhood and the multiplicity is one. The proof uses a blow‑up argument and the classification of tangent flows with density < 2, which are either a smooth multiplicity‑one flow or a static plane with multiplicity one. This ε‑regularity theorem is the cornerstone of the quantitative stratification of the singular set developed by Naber and Valtorta (2017).

For flows with higher multiplicity, the singular set can be larger. In the mean‑convex case, the multiplicity is non‑increasing and the singular set has dimension at most n−2. In the general case, the singular set may have dimension n−1, but the set of points where the multiplicity jumps is countably (n−1)‑rectifiable. The work of Edelen (2020) on the “multiplicity‑one conjecture” for generic initial data shows that for a dense set of smooth initial hypersurfaces, the Brakke flow has multiplicity one everywhere. This result relies on the generic regularity theory of Colding–Minicozzi and a perturbation argument using the implicit function theorem in Banach spaces.

Numerical approximation of varifold flows with multiplicity is an active area. The threshold dynamics method naturally produces multiplicity when the zero level set fattens; the resulting varifold can be interpreted as a Brakke flow with multiplicity equal to the number of phases meeting at a point. Convergence of the threshold dynamics scheme to a Brakke flow with multiplicity was proved by Esedoḡlu and Otto (2015) using the theory of De Giorgi’s minimizing movements. Error estimates in the flat norm are of order √Δt for the Allen–Cahn approximation and of order Δt for the threshold dynamics scheme.

Inverse Mean Curvature Flow: Properties and Applications

While Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) drives a surface in the direction of its mean curvature vector, the Inverse Mean Curvature Flow (IMCF) propels the surface with a velocity proportional to the inverse of the magnitude of its mean curvature vector. Specifically, if Σt is a family of hypersurfaces evolving in ℝⁿ⁺¹, its velocity v in the normal direction N is given by v = (1/|H|) N, where H is the mean curvature vector. This seemingly minor change in the flow law leads to dramatically different behavior and analytical challenges compared to MCF.

One of the most striking differences is the nature of the parabolicity. MCF is a strictly parabolic equation for smooth surfaces, ensuring short-time existence and regularity. IMCF, however, is a degenerate parabolic equation. This degeneracy occurs when the mean curvature H approaches zero, which can lead to infinite velocities or ill-posedness. To handle this, generalized solutions, often formulated using level set methods or weak formulations in the context of geometric measure theory, are crucial. For instance, the level set formulation for IMCF involves evolving a function u such that Σt = {u=0} by the equation ∂u/∂t = |∇u| div(∇u/|∇u|), which simplifies to ∂u/∂t = |∇u| H. For IMCF, the velocity is 1/|H|, so the level set equation becomes ∂u/∂t = |∇u| / H. This is not directly parabolic and requires careful regularization.

Key properties of IMCF, particularly for star-shaped initial surfaces in Euclidean space, include:

  • Outward Propagation: The flow always moves outwards, expanding the enclosed volume.
  • Monotonicity of Enclosed Volume: For a closed surface, the enclosed volume V(t) is strictly increasing, unlike MCF where volume can decrease.
  • Area Behavior: For convex surfaces, the area A(t) can decrease, which is counter-intuitive compared to the expansion.
  • Preservation of Convexity: If the initial surface is convex, it generally remains convex under IMCF, though this requires careful proof due to the degeneracy.
  • Long-Time Existence and Convergence: Under suitable conditions (e.g., initial surface being star-shaped and strictly convex), the flow exists for all time and converges to a round sphere as t → ∞. This is a significant contrast to MCF, which often develops singularities in finite time.

The most celebrated application of IMCF is its pivotal role in the proof of the Riemannian Penrose Inequality by Huisken and Ilmanen (2001). This inequality, a fundamental result in mathematical General Relativity, relates the mass of an asymptotically flat spacetime to the area of its outermost apparent horizon. The proof involves flowing an initial surface (representing an apparent horizon) outwards using a weak formulation of IMCF. As the surface expands, its area decreases (a non-trivial result for IMCF), and the enclosed volume increases. The flow is shown to eventually converge to a round sphere, which corresponds to the Schwarzschild black hole solution. By carefully tracking the mass and area along the flow, Huisken and Ilmanen established the inequality, demonstrating that the area of the apparent horizon is bounded below by a quantity related to the ADM mass of the spacetime. This work highlighted the power of geometric flows in tackling deep problems in differential geometry and mathematical physics, particularly in contexts where classical smooth solutions are not guaranteed.

The Allen-Cahn Equation as a Regularization of Mean Curvature Flow

The Mean Curvature Flow, while geometrically intuitive, presents significant analytical challenges due to the formation of singularities. To overcome these, various regularization techniques have been developed. One of the most powerful and widely studied is the connection to the Allen-Cahn equation, a reaction-diffusion partial differential equation that models phase separation in binary mixtures.

The Allen-Cahn equation is given by ∂u/∂t = Δu - (1/ε²)f(u), where u(x,t) is a scalar phase field variable, Δ is the Laplacian, ε is a small positive parameter representing interface thickness, and f(u) is a non-linear potential function, typically a double-well potential like f(u) = u³ - u. This potential has two stable minima (e.g., at u = ±1), representing two distinct phases. The term Δu drives diffusion, while -(1/ε²)f(u) drives the system towards one of the stable phases, creating sharp interfaces between regions where u ≈ +1 and u ≈ -1.

The profound connection to MCF arises in the sharp interface limit as ε → 0. In this limit, the diffuse interface separating the two phases becomes infinitesimally thin, and its evolution is governed by Mean Curvature Flow. More precisely, the zero-level set of the solution u(x,t) (i.e., the set {x : u(x,t) = 0}) converges to a surface evolving by MCF. This relationship is rigorously established through the theory of Gamma-convergence. The energy functional associated with the Allen-Cahn equation, often called the Cahn-Hilliard energy, is given by Eε(u) = ∫ (ε/2)|∇u|² + (1/ε)F(u) dx, where F(u) is an anti-derivative of f(u). As ε → 0, this energy functional Γ-converges to a multiple of the perimeter functional (area functional) for sets. The Modica-Mortola theorem (1977) provides the foundational result for this convergence, showing that the limit of Eε(u) is proportional to the perimeter of the set {u > 0}.

This connection means that the Allen-Cahn equation effectively regularizes MCF. Instead of tracking a sharp interface that can develop singularities, one solves a smooth PDE for the phase field u. Topological changes, such as pinching off or merging, are handled naturally by the phase field without explicit intervention. Ilmanen's seminal work (1993, 1994) utilized this approach to construct global weak solutions for MCF, known as Brakke flows, by taking the limit of solutions to the Allen-Cahn equation. This provided a powerful tool for studying MCF beyond the regime of smooth solutions.

While offering significant advantages in terms of existence and handling topological changes, the Allen-Cahn approach also presents computational challenges. The small parameter ε necessitates very fine spatial discretizations to resolve the thin interface, leading to high computational costs. Numerical schemes for Allen-Cahn, such as finite difference or finite element methods, must be carefully designed to maintain stability and accuracy. Despite these challenges, the Allen-Cahn equation remains a cornerstone in the theoretical and numerical study of Mean Curvature Flow, providing a robust framework for understanding its complex dynamics and generalized solutions.

Analyzing Flow Behavior
Consider a closed, smooth, strictly convex hypersurface Σ₀ in ℝⁿ⁺¹. Describe the qualitative long-time behavior of this surface under:
  1. Mean Curvature Flow (MCF)
  2. Inverse Mean Curvature Flow (IMCF)
Explain the key differences in how the area and enclosed volume evolve in each case.
  1. Mean Curvature Flow (MCF):

    For a closed, smooth, strictly convex hypersurface in ℝⁿ⁺¹, MCF (∂X/∂t = -H N) causes the surface to shrink and eventually disappear in finite time. This is a classical result by Huisken (1984) and Grayson (1987). The surface remains convex throughout the flow. As it shrinks, it becomes increasingly spherical, eventually collapsing to a point. The area A(t) of the surface strictly decreases over time, and the enclosed volume V(t) also strictly decreases. The flow is driven by the curvature, causing regions of higher curvature to shrink faster, which tends to make the surface rounder as it collapses.

  2. Inverse Mean Curvature Flow (IMCF):

    For a closed, smooth, strictly convex hypersurface in ℝⁿ⁺¹, IMCF (∂X/∂t = (1/|H|) N) causes the surface to expand outwards. Under this flow, the surface exists for all time and converges to a round sphere of infinite radius as t → ∞. The surface remains convex throughout the flow. The enclosed volume V(t) strictly increases over time, growing without bound. Surprisingly, the area A(t) of the surface strictly decreases for a convex surface, approaching a limit related to the initial area or potentially zero, depending on the precise formulation and ambient space. This decreasing area for an expanding surface is a distinguishing feature of IMCF compared to MCF. The flow is degenerate when H approaches zero, but for strictly convex surfaces, H is always positive, allowing for a well-behaved flow in the smooth setting.

Key Differences:

  • Direction of Flow: MCF shrinks inwards; IMCF expands outwards.
  • Long-Time Existence: MCF leads to finite-time extinction; IMCF leads to infinite-time existence and convergence to an infinite sphere.
  • Volume Evolution: MCF decreases volume; IMCF increases volume.
  • Area Evolution: MCF decreases area; IMCF also decreases area for convex surfaces (a counter-intuitive but crucial distinction).
  • Singularities: MCF forms singularities (e.g., pinching) before extinction; IMCF can also form singularities (e.g., spikes or degeneracy if H=0) but for convex initial data, it typically remains smooth and converges to a sphere.

Distinguishing Flow Types
When encountering questions about different geometric flows, always pay close attention to the sign and functional form of the velocity term. A common mistake is to assume that all "curvature flows" behave similarly. MCF (velocity proportional to -H) and IMCF (velocity proportional to 1/|H|) have fundamentally different long-time behaviors and implications for area/volume evolution, even for simple initial geometries like convex surfaces. Memorize the core properties (shrink/expand, volume/area increase/decrease, finite/infinite time existence) for standard initial data (e.g., convex, star-shaped) for each major flow type.

Mean Curvature Flow as a Gradient Flow for Area

A powerful way to understand Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) is to view it as the L²-gradient flow of the area functional. This perspective connects MCF directly to variational calculus and provides deep insights into its behavior, particularly why it tends to simplify surfaces and reduce their area. In essence, MCF is the steepest descent path for a surface seeking to minimize its area.

Let Σ be a hypersurface in ℝⁿ⁺¹. Its area functional is given by A(Σ) = ∫Σ 1 dμ, where dμ is the induced volume form on the surface. To show that MCF is the L²-gradient flow of this functional, we consider a one-parameter family of surfaces X(t,s) = X(t) + sY(t)N(t), where X(t) is the position vector of the surface at time t, N(t) is the unit outward normal vector, and Y(t) is a smooth function representing a normal variation of the surface. The velocity of the flow is ∂X/∂t = -H N, where H is the mean curvature vector.

The first variation of the area functional with respect to a normal perturbation YN is given by: δA(Σ, YN) = d/ds [A(X(t,s))]s=0 = ∫Σ H Y dμ. Here, H is the scalar mean curvature (sum of principal curvatures). The mean curvature vector is H N, so H is the magnitude of the mean curvature vector. The gradient of a functional F with respect to an L²-inner product is defined such that δF(Σ, YN) = ⟨∇F, YN⟩L². For surfaces, the L²-inner product is ∫Σ Y1 Y2 dμ. Thus, if we identify the normal vector N as the direction, the scalar component of the gradient of the area functional in the normal direction is precisely H.

A gradient flow for a functional F is typically written as ∂X/∂t = -∇F. In our context, this means that the velocity of the surface in the normal direction is given by the negative of the mean curvature. Therefore, ∂X/∂t = -H N, which is precisely the definition of Mean Curvature Flow. This establishes MCF as the L²-gradient flow of the area functional.

This interpretation has several profound implications:

  • Area Reduction: Since MCF is a gradient descent, it naturally seeks to reduce the area of the surface. The rate of area decrease is given by d/dt A(Σt) = -∫Σt |H|² dμ. This shows that area strictly decreases as long as H is not identically zero. If H=0, the surface is a minimal surface, which is a critical point (stationary point) of the area functional.
  • Simplification of Geometry: By reducing area, MCF tends to simplify the geometry of surfaces, making them rounder and smoother, as observed in the convergence of convex surfaces to spheres.
  • Connection to Minimal Surfaces: Minimal surfaces are precisely the stationary points of the area functional, meaning they are the "equilibrium" states of MCF where the flow stops. MCF can thus be seen as a dynamic process that evolves surfaces towards minimal surfaces.
  • Energy Dissipation: The quantity ∫Σt |H|² dμ can be interpreted as an energy dissipation rate. The flow dissipates "curvature energy" as it reduces area.

While this gradient flow picture is clear for smooth solutions, its extension to generalized solutions (like Brakke flows) requires careful formulation. For Brakke flows, the area functional is replaced by the perimeter functional for sets, and the flow satisfies an energy dissipation inequality in a weaker sense. Nevertheless, the fundamental idea that MCF is driven by the desire to minimize area remains central to understanding its analytical and geometric properties.

Stability and Linearization of Mean Curvature Flow

Understanding the stability of solutions to Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) is crucial for predicting their long-term behavior and for analyzing the nature of singularities. Stability analysis involves linearizing the flow equation around a known solution, typically a stationary solution (a minimal surface) or a self-similar solution (a self-shrinker).

Let X(x,t) be a solution to the MCF equation ∂X/∂t = -H N. We consider a small perturbation of this solution in the normal direction. Let the perturbed surface be given by X̃(x,t) = X(x,t) + εY(x,t)N(x,t), where ε is a small parameter and Y is a scalar function on the surface. Substituting X̃ into the MCF equation and keeping only terms linear in ε, we obtain the linearized equation for Y. For a surface evolving in Euclidean space, the linearized equation takes the form:

∂Y/∂t = ΔΣY + |A|²Y

where ΔΣ is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the evolving surface Σt, and |A|² is the squared norm of the second fundamental form (extrinsic curvature tensor). Note that for hypersurfaces, |A|² = H² + Σi≠j (κi - κj)², where κi are the principal curvatures. In the context of a stationary solution (a minimal surface, where H=0), this simplifies to ∂Y/∂t = ΔΣY + |A|²Y. The operator L = ΔΣ + |A|² is known as the Jacobi operator or stability operator for minimal surfaces.

The eigenvalues of the Jacobi operator determine the stability of a minimal surface. If all eigenvalues are non-negative, the minimal surface is stable. If there is at least one negative eigenvalue, it is unstable. For example, a sphere is a stable minimal surface (in the sense that it is a stable critical point of the area functional), while a catenoid is an unstable minimal surface.

For evolving solutions of MCF, the stability analysis is more complex because the operator itself changes with time. However, the linearized equation provides insight into how small perturbations grow or decay. For instance, if a flow approaches a self-shrinker, the stability of that self-shrinker (as a solution to the self-shrinker equation) dictates how the flow behaves in its neighborhood. The sphere is a stable self-shrinker, which explains why many MCFs become spherical near singularities of Type I.

Key aspects of stability analysis in MCF:

  • Short-Time Regularity: The parabolic nature of the linearized equation ensures that small perturbations remain smooth for short times.
  • Singularity Analysis: Near a singularity, the flow often rescales to a self-similar solution (a self-shrinker). The stability of these self-shrinkers under the linearized flow helps classify the type of singularity (Type I vs. Type II) and understand the local geometry of the singular set.
  • Uniqueness: Stability analysis can be used to prove uniqueness of solutions for certain initial data or under specific conditions.
  • Perturbation Theory: It allows for the study of how solutions change under small variations of initial data or ambient geometry. For example, the stability of a minimal surface under MCF in a Riemannian manifold depends on the ambient Ricci curvature.

Hamilton's differential Harnack inequality for MCF is a powerful tool related to stability, providing a lower bound on the evolution of certain quantities related to curvature. White's regularity theorem, which states that singularities of MCF are "generic" and well-behaved, also implicitly relies on stability arguments and the analysis of blow-up limits, which are often stable self-shrinkers. The study of the spectrum of the Jacobi operator on self-shrinkers is an active area of research, providing a deeper understanding of the geometry of singularities.

Mean Curvature Flow with External Potentials and Constraints

The standard Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) describes the evolution of a surface driven solely by its intrinsic curvature. However, in many physical and mathematical contexts, surfaces are also influenced by external forces or subject to specific constraints. Incorporating these elements leads to richer dynamics and broader applications.

Mean Curvature Flow with External Potentials

When an external potential function V(x) is present in the ambient space, the flow equation is modified to include a force term derived from this potential. The modified flow equation becomes: ∂X/∂t = -H N - ∇V⊥ where ∇V⊥ denotes the component of the gradient of V that is normal to the surface Σt. This term represents an external force pushing or pulling the surface. The surface evolves not only to reduce its curvature energy but also to move towards regions of lower potential energy.

Applications of MCF with external potentials are diverse:

  • Image Processing (Active Contours/Snakes): In computer vision, MCF with a potential is used for image segmentation. The potential V(x) is typically constructed from image features, such as intensity gradients. The flow drives the contour towards strong edges in the image, effectively segmenting objects. The term ∇V⊥ acts as an "edge detector" force.
  • Material Science: Modeling crystal growth, phase transitions, or the evolution of interfaces in heterogeneous materials. The potential V can represent chemical potentials, elastic energies, or anisotropic surface energies.
  • Biological Systems: Simulating the dynamics of cell membranes, vesicles, or other biological interfaces. External potentials can model forces from the cytoskeleton, osmotic pressure, or interactions with the extracellular matrix.

Mean Curvature Flow with Volume Constraint

Another important variation is MCF under a volume constraint, where the enclosed volume of a closed surface is required to remain constant. This is achieved by introducing a Lagrange multiplier λ(t) into the flow equation: ∂X/∂t = -(H + λ)N Here, λ(t) is a scalar function of time that is determined by the constraint ∫Ωt 1 dx = V0 (constant volume). The Lagrange multiplier acts as a uniform pressure term on the surface. To find λ(t), we differentiate the volume constraint with respect to time and substitute the flow equation. This leads to: d/dt V(t) = ∫Σt ∂X/∂t · N dμ = ∫Σt -(H + λ) dμ = 0 From this, λ(t) = -(∫Σt H dμ) / (∫Σt 1 dμ) = -(∫Σt H dμ) / A(t), where A(t) is the area of the surface. Thus, λ(t) is the negative of the average mean curvature of the surface.

This volume-preserving MCF is a gradient flow for the area functional subject to a volume constraint. It seeks to minimize the surface area while keeping the enclosed volume fixed. For an initial convex surface, this flow converges to a round sphere, which is the unique minimizer of area for a given enclosed volume (the classical isoperimetric problem). This flow is crucial in understanding the formation of bubbles and droplets, where surface tension (curvature) drives the shape, but volume is conserved.

Other constraints can also be imposed, such as fixed boundary conditions (where the boundary of the surface is held fixed), or area-preserving flows (where the total surface area is conserved, often leading to more complex dynamics and different types of singularities). These variations of MCF highlight its flexibility and applicability to a wide range of problems in geometry, physics, and engineering.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the long-time behavior of MCF and IMCF: MCF shrinks to a point (for convex surfaces), while IMCF expands to an infinite sphere (for convex/star-shaped surfaces).
  • Assuming IMCF always increases area: For convex surfaces, IMCF actually decreases area while increasing volume, which is counter-intuitive.
  • Overlooking the degeneracy of IMCF: The 1/|H| term means IMCF is degenerate when H=0, requiring weak solutions or level set formulations, unlike the strictly parabolic MCF.
  • Misinterpreting the Allen-Cahn equation's role: It's a *regularization* that provides a smooth phase field, whose zero-level set *approximates* MCF in the sharp interface limit, not an exact equivalent for all ε.
  • Forgetting that the Jacobi operator for MCF includes the |A|² term (or H² for minimal surfaces), not just the Laplacian, which fundamentally affects stability.
  • Assuming a volume-constrained MCF always converges to a sphere regardless of initial topology: While true for convex initial data, complex topologies might lead to different stable configurations or singularities before reaching a sphere.
  • Confusing the external potential term ∇V⊥ with a Lagrange multiplier for a constraint; the potential term is an externally prescribed force, while a Lagrange multiplier arises from an internal constraint.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Evolution of the Gauss Map

The Gauss map of an immersed hypersurface Σ ⊂ ℝⁿ⁺¹ assigns to each point its unit normal ν. Under mean curvature flow ∂ₜX = –H ν the Gauss map satisfies a coupled parabolic system. Differentiating ν along the flow yields ∂ₜν = ∇H, where ∇H is the tangential gradient of the mean curvature. This relation follows from the Weingarten equations and the fact that the normal bundle evolves by the shape operator. Consequently the pull‑back of the round metric on the sphere Sⁿ evolves by ∂ₜg_G = –2H A_G, where A_G is the second fundamental form of the Gauss image. The evolution equation for the Gauss map is therefore a heat‑type equation with a lower‑order term involving the squared norm of the second fundamental form |A|².

A key consequence is the monotonicity of the spherical image area when the flow is convex. For a strictly convex hypersurface the Gauss map is a diffeomorphism onto Sⁿ, and the area of the spherical image equals the total curvature ∫_Σ H dμ. Huisken’s monotonicity formula applied to the Gauss map yields a differential inequality for the entropy of the spherical image, which can be used to prove that the rescaled flow converges to a round sphere. In the non‑convex case the Gauss map may develop folds; the evolution of its Jacobian determinant J = det(dν) satisfies ∂ₜJ = ΔJ – |A|²J, showing that J can vanish only at singular times. This provides a geometric interpretation of Type I singularities: the Gauss map collapses on regions where the curvature blows up.

The Gauss map evolution also underlies the proof of the differential Harnack inequality for mean curvature flow. Hamilton’s matrix Harnack estimate for the second fundamental form can be rewritten as a positivity condition for a certain quadratic form on the space of symmetric 2‑tensors pulled back via the Gauss map. This perspective connects the Harnack inequality to the convexity of the spherical image and yields sharp decay estimates for |A|² along ancient solutions.

Applications of the Gauss map dynamics include the classification of self‑shrinkers with polynomial volume growth. For a self‑shrinker Σ satisfying H = ⟨X, ν⟩/2, the Gauss map is an eigenfunction of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator on Σ with eigenvalue 1/2. This spectral property forces the Gauss map to be a linear combination of coordinate functions when Σ is a cylinder or a sphere, and it rules out exotic self‑shrinkers with non‑trivial topology under suitable entropy bounds.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Classification of Self‑Shrinkers via the Łojasiewicz–Simon Gradient Inequality

Self‑shrinkers are critical points of the Gaussian area functional F(Σ) = ∫_Σ e–|X|²/4 dμ. The Łojasiewicz–Simon gradient inequality provides a quantitative rate of convergence for the mean curvature flow to a self‑shrinker when the flow exists for all time and the entropy is sufficiently close to that of a given shrinker. In the setting of Brakke flows, one considers the L²‑gradient flow of F in the space of integral varifolds. The inequality states that there exist constants θ ∈ (0, ½] and C > 0 such that for any varifold V sufficiently close to a smooth self‑shrinker Σ in the varifold topology, |F(V) – F(Σ)|1–θ ≤ C ‖δF(V)‖, where δF(V) is the first variation of F at V.

The exponent θ is determined by the spectral gap of the linearised operator L = Δ – ½⟨X, ∇⟩ + |A|² + ½ on Σ. For the round sphere Sⁿ the spectrum is explicit: the first non‑zero eigenvalue is 1, giving θ = ½. For the cylinder Sᵏ × ℝⁿ⁻ᵏ the spectrum contains a zero eigenvalue corresponding to translations, so the inequality holds only after modding out by the symmetry group; the effective exponent remains ½. For more complicated shrinkers (e.g. the Angenent torus) the spectral analysis is more delicate, but the inequality still holds with some θ < ½.

Using the Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality one can prove that any ancient solution with bounded entropy and which is non‑collapsed must converge, after appropriate parabolic rescaling, to a self‑shrinker at a polynomial rate. This result is a cornerstone of the Colding–Minicozzi generic regularity theory: it shows that the only possible blow‑up limits at a generic singularity are the sphere and the cylinder. The proof proceeds by establishing a differential inequality for the Gaussian area along the flow, integrating it, and applying the inequality to obtain a decay estimate for the distance to the shrinker in the varifold metric.

The inequality also yields uniqueness of the tangent flow at a singular point for generic initial data. If two distinct tangent flows existed, one could construct a sequence of rescaled flows violating the Łojasiewicz–Simon decay, contradicting the entropy bound. This argument has been extended to the level‑set flow by Evans–Spruck and to the Brakke flow by White, providing a unified framework for singularity analysis.

Worked Example: Łojasiewicz–Simon Exponent for the Cylinder
Compute the Łojasiewicz–Simon exponent θ for the self‑shrinking cylinder Σ = S¹ × ℝ in ℝ³. The linearised operator on Σ is L = Δ_Σ – ½⟨X, ∇⟩ + |A|² + ½. Determine the lowest positive eigenvalue of L on functions orthogonal to the translation modes.
On S¹ × ℝ the metric splits as ds² = dθ² + dz². The second fundamental form has |A|² = ½ (since the principal curvatures are 1 and 0). The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator on ℝ gives eigenvalues k/2 for Hermite polynomials; the translation mode in the z‑direction corresponds to the first Hermite polynomial with eigenvalue ½. On S¹ the Laplacian eigenvalues are m². The operator L acts as L = ∂_θ² + ∂_z² – ½(z∂_z) + ½ + ½. Separating variables u(θ,z)=eimθh_k(z) yields eigenvalues λ = m² + k/2. The zero eigenvalue occurs for m=0, k=1 (translation in z). The next eigenvalue is for m=±1, k=0 giving λ = 1. Hence the spectral gap is 1, and the Łojasiewicz–Simon exponent is θ = ½.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the Łojasiewicz–Simon exponent, first identify the self‑shrinker, write down its linearised operator, and compute the lowest positive eigenvalue after factoring out symmetries. The exponent is always ½ for shrinkers with a spectral gap of 1; if the gap is λ > 0, then θ = ½ still holds because the inequality exponent is determined by the analytic structure of the gradient flow, not the precise gap. Memorise the spectra for sphere, cylinder, and plane.

Mean Curvature Flow with Obstacle Constraints and the Variational Inequality Formulation

An obstacle problem for mean curvature flow prescribes a closed set O ⊂ ℝⁿ⁺¹ that the evolving hypersurface Σₜ must not penetrate: Σₜ ⊂ ℝⁿ⁺¹ O for all t. The flow is then the gradient flow of the area functional in the convex set of admissible surfaces. In the viscosity solution framework this is encoded by a variational inequality: for any smooth test function φ touching Σₜ from above at a point x ∉ ∂O, one requires ∂ₜφ ≤ –H(φ)ν; if the contact point lies on ∂O, the inequality is replaced by a Neumann‑type condition reflecting the obstacle. The existence of a unique viscosity solution follows from the comparison principle for the level‑set equation with obstacles, proved by Ishii and Souganidis using the doubling‑variables technique.

In the Brakke flow setting, the obstacle constraint is incorporated by restricting the class of test functions in the Brakke inequality to those vanishing on O. The resulting varifold solution satisfies the same avoidance principle: if the initial surface lies outside O, the flow never enters O. Regularity theory for obstacle‑constrained MCF shows that the free boundary ∂Σₜ ∩ ∂O is smooth for almost every time, provided ∂O is smooth and the initial surface meets ∂O transversally. The contact angle is always 90°, a consequence of the first variation formula with a Lagrange multiplier supported on the contact set.

Applications include the evolution of capillary surfaces in containers, where the container wall acts as an obstacle, and the modeling of grain boundaries in polycrystalline materials, where the obstacle represents an impenetrable phase. The variational inequality formulation also yields a natural numerical scheme: the minimizing movement algorithm (De Giorgi’s scheme) with an obstacle constraint converges to the viscosity solution, and error estimates of order √Δt can be obtained using the monotonicity of the discrete energy.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Formation of Neckpinch Singularities in Rotationally Symmetric Solutions

Neckpinch singularities are the prototypical Type I singularities for non‑convex mean curvature flow. Consider a rotationally symmetric hypersurface Σₜ ⊂ ℝⁿ⁺¹ given by a profile function r(z,t) > 0 satisfying ∂ₜr = rzz/(1+r_z²) – (n–1)/r. The flow develops a neckpinch when r(z,t) → 0 at some point z₀ as t → T. Formal matched asymptotics show that near the pinch the profile resembles a shrinking cylinder Sⁿ⁻¹ × ℝ with radius ρ(t) ∼ √(2(T–t)). The rescaled flow converges to the self‑similar cylinder, and the singularity is Type I because the maximal curvature blows up like (T–t)–½.

Rigorous analysis by Altschuler, Angenent, and Giga established that for n ≥ 2 the neckpinch is generic among rotationally symmetric initial data with a single neck. The proof uses a Lyapunov functional (the entropy) and a centre‑manifold reduction to show that the flow near the singularity is attracted to the cylinder. The linearised operator around the cylinder has a one‑dimensional unstable manifold corresponding to the neck radius; the stable manifold corresponds to higher modes that decay exponentially. This spectral picture explains why the neckpinch is stable under small perturbations preserving rotational symmetry.

In higher dimensions (n ≥ 3) the neckpinch can be non‑generic: there exist rotationally symmetric ancient solutions (the “bowl” solitons) that do not pinch but instead converge to a translating soliton. The transition between pinching and non‑pinching behaviour is governed by the sign of a certain integral invariant (the “neck energy”). For n = 2 the neckpinch is the only possible singularity for embedded rotationally symmetric curves, a result of Grayson’s theorem.

Neckpinch analysis also informs the surgery program of Huisken–Sinestrari. The surgery parameters are chosen so that the neck region is replaced by a pair of standard caps before the curvature reaches the surgery threshold. The asymptotic profile of the neck guarantees that the post‑surgery surface remains mean‑convex and that the surgery does not increase the entropy.

Mean Curvature Flow in the Viscosity Solution Framework: Comparison Principles and Uniqueness

The level‑set formulation of mean curvature flow represents the evolving hypersurface as the zero level set of a continuous function u: ℝⁿ⁺¹ × [0,∞) → ℝ satisfying the degenerate parabolic equation ∂ₜu = |∇u| div(∇u/|∇u|). This equation is understood in the viscosity sense: a subsolution (resp. supersolution) satisfies the inequality with the operator evaluated on smooth test functions touching from above (resp. below). The fundamental comparison principle of Evans–Spruck and Chen–Giga–Goto states that if u is a subsolution and v a supersolution with u(·,0) ≤ v(·,0), then u ≤ v for all time. The proof uses the doubling‑variables technique and the structure of the operator, which is geometric (invariant under adding constants and monotone in the gradient).

Uniqueness of the viscosity solution follows immediately from comparison. Moreover, the level‑set flow coincides with the Brakke flow for mean‑convex initial data, as shown by Ilmanen using the elliptic regularisation method. For general initial data the level‑set flow may develop “fattening” where the zero level set acquires interior; this phenomenon is precisely the non‑uniqueness of the Brakke flow. The viscosity framework also handles obstacles, forcing terms, and anisotropic energies by modifying the operator accordingly.

Numerical schemes based on the viscosity formulation (e.g. finite‑difference schemes with monotone discretisations) are guaranteed to converge to the unique viscosity solution by the Barles–Souganidis theorem. Error estimates of order Δx1/2 have been proved for the standard upwind scheme. The viscosity approach thus provides a robust analytical and computational foundation for mean curvature flow beyond the smooth regime.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Evolution of the Second Fundamental Form

The second fundamental form A of a hypersurface evolving by mean curvature flow satisfies a tensorial reaction‑diffusion equation that is central to all regularity and singularity analyses. In local coordinates the evolution reads ∂ₜAᵢⱼ = ΔAᵢⱼ + |A|² Aᵢⱼ − 2 H Aᵢₖ Aᵏⱼ + H Aᵢⱼ, where H is the mean curvature and Δ is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the evolving surface. Taking the trace yields the well‑known scalar evolution ∂ₜ|A|² = Δ|A|² − 2|∇A|² + 2|A|⁴. The positive reaction term 2|A|⁴ is the analytic source of curvature blow‑up; it forces any non‑trivial solution to develop singularities in finite time unless the flow is a self‑shrinker. Huisken’s 1984 thesis exploited this equation to prove that convexity is preserved: if the initial hypersurface is strictly convex (all principal curvatures positive), then the pinching ratio λₘₐₓ/λₘᵢₙ remains bounded and the flow converges to a round point after parabolic rescaling. The proof uses the maximum principle on the quantity Q = |A|²/H², showing that its maximum cannot increase. Later, Andrews refined the pinching estimate to a quantitative bound λₘₐₓ ≤ C λₘᵢₙ for mean‑convex flows, which is a key ingredient in the Huisken–Sinestrari surgery program. The evolution of A also underlies the classification of Type I singularities: after blow‑up at the maximal curvature rate (T−t)⁻¹ᐟ², the rescaled second fundamental form converges to that of a self‑shrinker, and the limiting equation ΔA − ½⟨x,∇A⟩ + |A|²A = 0 characterises the possible profiles. In higher codimension the system acquires normal‑bundle terms, and the reaction term becomes |A|²A − 2∑ₖ A(·,eₖ)A(eₖ,·), reflecting the richer algebraic structure of the normal connection.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Huisken–Ilmanen Inverse Mean Curvature Flow Connection

Inverse mean curvature flow (IMCF) was introduced by Huisken and Ilmanen in 2001 to prove the Riemannian Penrose inequality for asymptotically flat 3‑manifolds with non‑negative scalar curvature. While MCF moves a hypersurface in the direction of its mean curvature vector with speed H, IMCF moves it in the opposite normal direction with speed 1/H. Formally, if a family of hypersurfaces Σₜ satisfies ∂ₜx = (1/H) ν, then the area evolves by d/dt |Σₜ| = ∫Σₜ 1 = |Σₜ|, so area grows exponentially. The two flows are linked by a simple time reversal and a conformal change of the ambient metric: if g(t) = e2t g₀, then MCF for g(t) corresponds to IMCF for g₀. This duality allows one to transfer monotonicity formulas. Huisken’s monotonicity formula for MCF becomes the Geroch monotonicity of the Hawking mass along IMCF. The weak formulation of IMCF, developed via the level‑set method by Huisken–Ilmanen, uses a function u solving div(∇u/|∇u|) = |∇u|, which is the elliptic counterpart of the parabolic level‑set equation for MCF. The existence of a weak solution for any initial outward‑minimising surface relies on the elliptic regularisation technique of Ilmanen. Applications extend beyond the Penrose inequality: IMCF provides a geometric proof of the Minkowski inequality for convex bodies, yields sharp isoperimetric inequalities in Schwarzschild space, and gives a variational characterisation of the Bartnik mass. The connection also informs numerical schemes: a semi‑implicit discretisation of MCF can be transformed into an elliptic problem resembling the IMCF level‑set equation, leading to unconditionally stable algorithms for curvature‑driven interface motion.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Classification of Ancient Solutions via Entropy

Ancient solutions are complete MCF solutions defined for all negative times t ∈ (−∞,0). They arise as blow‑up limits at singularities and as translating or rotating solitons. The Gaussian entropy functional ℰ(Σ) = (4π)⁻ⁿᐟ² ∫_Σ e−|x|²/4 dμ, introduced by Huisken and later refined by Colding–Minicozzi, is monotone non‑increasing along MCF and constant exactly on self‑shrinkers. For an ancient solution, the entropy is bounded above by the entropy of the initial time slice and below by the entropy of any tangent flow at −∞. Colding–Minicozzi proved that low‑entropy ancient solutions in ℝⁿ⁺¹ are either flat planes, round shrinking spheres, or round shrinking cylinders Sᵏ × ℝⁿ⁻ᵏ. The proof uses the Łojasiewicz–Simon gradient inequality for the entropy functional near a self‑shrinker, together with a compactness theorem for integral Brakke flows with uniformly bounded entropy. The classification has been extended to higher entropy regimes by Bernstein–Wang and others, who showed that any ancient solution with entropy less than that of the cylinder S¹ × ℝⁿ⁻¹ must be a sphere or a plane. For entropy between the cylinder and the sphere, the only possibilities are the translating bowl soliton and its rotations. These results are crucial for the generic regularity theory: they imply that at a generic singularity the tangent flow is a multiplicity‑one cylinder, and the singular set is (n−1)‑rectifiable. The entropy also controls the number of ends of an ancient solution; a theorem of Hershkovits–White states that an ancient low‑entropy flow in ℝ³ has at most two ends, and if it has two ends it must be the cylinder.

Mean Curvature Flow with Boundary Conditions: Neumann and Dirichlet Problems

When a hypersurface evolves inside a domain with boundary, the interaction with the container is encoded by boundary conditions. The Neumann (free‑boundary) condition requires the evolving surface to meet the container orthogonally: ⟨ν,μ⟩ = 0 on ∂Σₜ ∩ ∂Ω, where μ is the outward normal of the container. This condition arises naturally from the first variation of area with a fixed boundary and preserves the volume enclosed by the surface and the container. Stahl (1996) proved short‑time existence and regularity for smooth initial data meeting the container at a right angle, and showed that the orthogonal contact angle is preserved as long as the flow remains smooth. For convex containers, the flow exists until the surface shrinks to a point or develops a singularity on the boundary. The Dirichlet condition fixes the boundary of the surface on a prescribed submanifold Γ ⊂ ∂Ω. Ecker and Huisken (1991) established long‑time existence for entire graphs over a domain with Dirichlet data, provided the initial graph is a Lipschitz function. The maximum principle yields gradient estimates that prevent the formation of interior singularities; the only possible singularities are at the boundary where the gradient may blow up. In the varifold setting, Brakke’s inequality is supplemented by a boundary term ∫∂Σₜ φ ⟨ν,μ⟩ dσ, and the free‑boundary Brakke flow was constructed by Edelen using elliptic regularisation. Applications include the evolution of capillary surfaces, the motion of grain boundaries in polycrystals, and the geometric proof of the Heintze–Karcher inequality via a free‑boundary flow in a half‑space.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Role of the Maximum Principle in Preserving Convexity

The parabolic maximum principle is the primary tool for deriving curvature pinching estimates that guarantee convexity preservation. For a convex hypersurface, the principal curvatures λ₁ ≤ … ≤ λₙ are positive. Huisken’s 1984 argument considers the ratio Q = λₙ/λ₁ (or the scale‑invariant quantity |A|²/H²). Using the evolution equations for the curvature tensor and the mean curvature, one computes ∂ₜQ = ΔQ + 2⟨∇log H,∇Q⟩ + (λₙ−λ₁)²/λ₁² (|A|² − Hλₙ). The reaction term is non‑positive because |A|² ≤ Hλₙ for convex hypersurfaces (a consequence of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the eigenvalues). Hence the maximum of Q cannot increase, and the pinching ratio remains bounded by its initial value. Andrews later refined this to a quantitative estimate λₙ ≤ C λ₁ for mean‑convex flows, where C depends only on the initial pinching and the dimension. The maximum principle also yields the differential Harnack inequality of Hamilton type: ∂ₜH + ⟨X,∇H⟩ + ½ H/t ≥ 0 for any vector field X tangent to the flow. This inequality implies that the mean curvature is non‑decreasing along space‑time curves moving with the flow, and it is a key ingredient in the classification of ancient solutions. In the viscosity framework, the comparison principle replaces the maximum principle; it guarantees that the level‑set flow of a mean‑convex initial set remains mean‑convex, and that the arrival time function is a viscosity solution of the corresponding Hamilton–Jacobi equation. The interplay between the classical maximum principle for smooth flows and the viscosity comparison principle for weak flows underpins the entire regularity theory of MCF.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Blow‑up Analysis of Singularities: Tangent Flows and Self‑Shrinkers

When a smooth mean curvature flow develops a singularity at time T, the standard technique is to rescale the flow around the singular point by the parabolic factor λ = 1/√(T−t). The rescaled flows Σt(λ) = λ(ΣT−t/λ² − x₀) converge subsequentially to a limiting ancient solution called a tangent flow. Huisken’s monotonicity formula guarantees that any tangent flow is a self‑shrinker, i.e. it satisfies H = ⟨x, ν⟩/2. The classification of self‑shrinkers is therefore central to understanding singularity models. In codimension one, the only smooth, complete, embedded self‑shrinkers with polynomial volume growth are the plane, the sphere, and the cylinder Sk × ℝn−k (for 1 ≤ k ≤ n−1), a result proved by Colding and Minicozzi using the Łojasiewicz–Simon gradient inequality for the Gaussian area functional. For non‑embedded or higher‑codimension shrinkers, the landscape is richer: Angenent’s torus, the Abresch–Langer curves, and numerous non‑compact examples with higher genus exist. The blow‑up procedure also yields the Gaussian density Θ(x₀,T) = limt→T ∫Σt Φ(x₀,T) dμt, where Φ is the backward heat kernel. White’s regularity theorem shows that if Θ(x₀,T) < 2, the singularity is removable; if Θ = 2, the tangent flow is a multiplicity‑one cylinder. This density gap is the cornerstone of the ε‑regularity theory and of the generic regularity program of Colding–Minicozzi.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Partial Regularity Theory for Brakke Flows: Allard's Theorem and ε‑Regularity

Brakke’s formulation of mean curvature flow as a family of integral varifolds satisfying the inequality d/dt ∫ φ dμt ≤ ∫ (−φ|H|² + ∇φ·H) dμt allows weak solutions that can undergo topological changes. The partial regularity theory asks: how large is the singular set of a Brakke flow? Allard’s regularity theorem for stationary varifolds extends to the parabolic setting via the work of Brakke, Ilmanen, and later White. The key ε‑regularity statement asserts that there exists a universal ε₀ > 0 such that if a Brakke flow satisfies the Gaussian density bound Θ(x₀,t₀) < 1 + ε₀ in a parabolic cylinder, then the flow is smooth in a smaller cylinder. The proof uses a blow‑up argument: assuming a sequence of counterexamples, one obtains a tangent flow that is a stationary integral varifold with density < 1 + ε₀, which by Allard’s theorem must be a smooth multiplicity‑one plane, contradicting the assumption of singularity. White’s stratification theorem further shows that the singular set has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most n−1, and the set of points where the tangent flow is not a cylinder has dimension at most n−2. This stratification underpins the quantitative regularity estimates used in the Colding–Minicozzi generic regularity theory and in the construction of mean curvature flow with surgery.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Coupled Ricci–Mean Curvature Flow: Geometric Applications

The coupled Ricci–mean curvature flow evolves a Riemannian metric g(t) on a manifold M by the Ricci flow ∂tg = −2 Ric and simultaneously evolves a submanifold Σt by mean curvature flow with respect to the evolving metric. This system appears naturally in the study of geometric transitions, such as the formation of singularities in the Ricci flow where surgery is performed along necks that are themselves evolving by mean curvature flow. The coupled flow was introduced by Müller and later studied by Lotz, who proved short‑time existence and a monotonicity formula analogous to Perelman’s 𝒲‑functional. The coupled monotonicity quantity 𝒲(g,Σ,f,τ) = ∫M [τ(|∇f|² + R) + f − n] (4πτ)−n/2 e−f dμ + ∫Σ [τ(|∇Σf|² + H²) + f − k] (4πτ)−k/2 e−f dσ is non‑decreasing in τ. This functional controls the formation of singularities in both the ambient metric and the submanifold. Applications include a proof of the multiplicity‑one conjecture for tangent flows of mean curvature flow in Ricci‑flow backgrounds, and a new approach to the Willmore conjecture via the evolution of minimal surfaces under Ricci flow. The coupled flow also provides a framework for studying the stability of minimal submanifolds under metric deformations.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Viscosity Solution Framework for Non‑Graphical Initial Data

The level‑set method of Evans–Spruck and Chen–Giga–Goto defines mean curvature flow for arbitrary closed sets by representing the evolving hypersurface as the zero level set of a viscosity solution u(x,t) of the degenerate parabolic equation ut = |∇u| div(∇u/|∇u|). For initial data that are not graphs, the comparison principle guarantees uniqueness of the viscosity solution, and the resulting level‑set flow coincides with the classical flow as long as the latter remains smooth. A major advance is the work of Ambrosio and Soner, who extended the theory to sets of finite perimeter, showing that the level‑set flow agrees with the Brakke flow for mean‑convex initial data. For non‑mean‑convex data, fattening can occur: the zero level set develops interior, and the level‑set flow is not a hypersurface but a set with positive measure. The viscosity framework also handles anisotropic and crystalline flows by replacing the isotropic operator with a Finsler‑type operator. Recent results by Kim and Tonegawa establish a uniqueness theorem for the level‑set flow of sets with bounded mean curvature in the sense of varifolds, bridging the gap between viscosity solutions and geometric measure theory.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Sub‑Riemannian Setting: Heisenberg Group and Horizontal Mean Curvature

In sub‑Riemannian geometry, the horizontal mean curvature flow evolves a hypersurface in the direction of its horizontal mean curvature vector, which is the projection of the Riemannian mean curvature onto the horizontal distribution. The Heisenberg group ℍ¹, with its standard contact structure, is the model case. The horizontal mean curvature H0 of a surface Σ is given by div0(ν0), where ν0 is the horizontal unit normal. The flow ∂tx = −H0 ν0 is a degenerate parabolic system because the horizontal distribution is not bracket‑generating in the normal direction. Cheng, Hwang, Malchiodi, and Yang proved short‑time existence for smooth initial data satisfying the non‑characteristic condition (ν0 ≠ 0). Singularities can be of two types: characteristic points where ν0 vanishes, and genuine curvature blow‑up. The blow‑up analysis at characteristic points leads to the study of horizontal self‑shrinkers, which are solutions of H0 = ⟨x,ν0⟩/2. The only complete, embedded horizontal self‑shrinkers in ℍ¹ are the vertical planes and the horizontal planes, a classification due to Montefalcone. The horizontal flow also appears in the study of the sub‑Riemannian isoperimetric problem and in the regularity theory for minimal surfaces in the Heisenberg group.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Evolution of the Willmore Energy

The Willmore energy of an immersed hypersurface Σ in Euclidean space is defined by 𝒲(Σ)=∫_Σ H² dμ, where H is the mean curvature. Under mean curvature flow the area element evolves by ∂_t dμ = −H² dμ and the mean curvature satisfies ∂_t H = Δ H + |A|² H, where A is the second fundamental form. A direct computation gives the evolution of the Willmore energy:

∂_t 𝒲 = ∫_Σ (2H ∂_t H − H³) dμ = −2∫_Σ |∇H|² dμ + 2∫_Σ H²|A|² dμ − ∫_Σ H³ dμ.

Unlike the area functional, the Willmore energy is not monotone along the flow. However, Kuwert and Schätzle (2001) proved that for a smooth MCF with a Type I singularity the quantity 𝒲(Σ_t) + 2∫0^t ∫Σ_s |∇H|² dμ ds is non‑increasing. This modified monotonicity is the key to their proof of the existence of a smooth MCF through singularities for surfaces of genus zero with small Willmore energy. Later, Rivière (2008) extended the analysis to arbitrary genus using the language of varifolds and the ε‑regularity theory of Allard. The evolution formula also appears in the study of the Willmore flow (the L²‑gradient flow of 𝒲), where the additional term −2∫|∇H|² provides the parabolic smoothing that makes the Willmore flow fourth order.

In the context of geometric inequalities, the monotonicity of the modified Willmore energy yields a new proof of the Willmore inequality 𝒲(Σ) ≥ 4π for immersed spheres, with equality only for the round sphere. The argument uses the fact that a Type I blow‑up limit of a sphere‑type MCF is a self‑shrinker, and the only embedded self‑shrinker with genus zero and entropy ≤ 2 is the round cylinder or the sphere; the cylinder has entropy > 2, forcing the limit to be the sphere and giving the lower bound.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Classification of Self‑Expanders

Self‑expanders are ancient solutions of MCF that move by homothetic expansion: Σ_t = √(−t) Σ−1 for t<0, and they satisfy the elliptic equation H = −⟨x,ν⟩/2 on Σ−1. Unlike self‑shrinkers, which correspond to Type I singularities, self‑expanders describe the asymptotic profile of a flow emerging from a conical singularity. The first non‑trivial examples were constructed by Ilmanen (1994) using a shooting method for rotationally symmetric hypersurfaces in ℝn+1. He produced a one‑parameter family of embedded self‑expanders asymptotic to a given cone, proving that every smooth cone admits at least one smooth expander asymptotic to it.

Subsequent work by Drugan and Nguyen (2015) classified all complete embedded self‑expanders with polynomial volume growth in ℝ³. They showed that any such expander is either a plane, a cylinder ℝ×S¹, or one of Ilmanen’s rotationally symmetric expanders. The proof combines the maximum principle for the expander equation with a careful analysis of the asymptotic cone at infinity, using the fact that the Gaussian density of an expander equals the density of its asymptotic cone. In higher dimensions, the classification remains open; however, Bernstein‑type results by Wang (2011) prove that any entire graphical self‑expander in ℝn+1 with bounded slope must be a hyperplane.

Self‑expanders also appear as tangent flows at the initial time of a Brakke flow starting from a non‑smooth initial set. White (2000) proved that if the initial set is a cone, then the tangent flow at time zero is a self‑expander asymptotic to that cone. This connection underlies the uniqueness theory for MCF with conical initial data developed by Hershkovits and White (2019).

Worked Example: Identifying a Self‑Expander
Consider the rotationally symmetric hypersurface in ℝ³ given by the profile curve r(z) = √(z²+1). Show that this surface is a self‑expander for mean curvature flow.
The surface is generated by revolving the curve (r(z),z) around the z‑axis. Its unit normal pointing outward is ν = (1/√(1+r'²))(−r',1) in the (r,z)‑plane. The mean curvature of a rotationally symmetric surface is H = (1/r) (r'/√(1+r'²))' + (1/√(1+r'²)) (1/r). For r(z)=√(z²+1) we have r' = z/√(z²+1) and √(1+r'²) = √(1+z²/(z²+1)) = √((2z²+1)/(z²+1)). A direct computation yields H = −z/(2√(z²+1)). The position vector is x = (r,0,z) in cylindrical coordinates, so ⟨x,ν⟩ = (r·(−r')+z·1)/√(1+r'²) = (−z+ z)/√(1+r'²) = 0? Wait, careful: x in the (r,z) plane is (r,z). The dot product with ν = (−r',1)/√(1+r'²) gives (−r r' + z)/√(1+r'²). Since r r' = z, the numerator vanishes, giving ⟨x,ν⟩ = 0. That would imply H=0, which is false. The error is that the normal used must be the outward normal of the hypersurface in ℝ³, which has an extra angular component. The correct mean curvature for a rotationally symmetric hypersurface in ℝ³ is H = (1/r) (r'/√(1+r'²))' + (1/√(1+r'²)) (1/r). Substituting r(z) gives H = −z/(2(z²+1)). Meanwhile ⟨x,ν⟩ = (r·(−r')+z·1)/√(1+r'²) = (−z+z)/√(1+r'²)=0. This suggests the surface is not a self‑expander. In fact the standard self‑expander asymptotic to the cone r=|z| is given implicitly by r(z) = √(z²+2) (the bowl expander). The correct profile satisfies the ODE r''/(1+r'²)3/2 + 1/(r√(1+r'²)) = −z/2. Solving this ODE yields the Ilmanen expander, not r=√(z²+1). Therefore the given surface is not a self‑expander.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Asymptotic Behavior of the Flow on Cones

If the initial hypersurface is a cone C ⊂ ℝn+1 (i.e., invariant under positive scaling), the classical smooth MCF does not exist because the tip is a singularity. Ilmanen (1994) constructed a Brakke flow starting from C by elliptic regularization: he solved the approximate equation εΔ u_ε = √(1+|∇u_ε|²) div(∇u_ε/√(1+|∇u_ε|²)) with boundary data given by the cone, then passed to the limit ε→0. The resulting Brakke flow is instantaneously smooth for t>0 and its tangent flow at t=0 is a self‑expander Σ asymptotic to C. The asymptotic shape of Σ_t as t→∞ is also a self‑expander; in fact Σ_t = √t Σ1, so the flow is self‑similar.

White (2000) proved that the Brakke flow from a cone is unique among integral Brakke flows with the same initial cone, provided the flow has unit multiplicity. The proof uses the monotonicity formula for the Gaussian density and the fact that any two such flows must have the same tangent flow at the origin, which forces them to coincide. Later, Hershkovits and White (2019) extended uniqueness to the class of all Brakke flows with bounded Gaussian density, showing that the Ilmanen expander is the only possible tangent flow at the tip.

The asymptotic behavior of the flow on a cone is closely related to the stability of the corresponding self‑expander. Colding and Minicozzi (2012) introduced the entropy functional λ(Σ)=supx₀,t₀ ∫_Σ Φx₀,t₀ dμ, where Φ is the backward heat kernel. They proved that the entropy of a self‑expander equals the Gaussian density of its asymptotic cone. Moreover, if a cone has entropy strictly less than that of the cylinder S¹×ℝn−1, then the Ilmanen expander is dynamically stable: any MCF starting sufficiently close to the cone (in the varifold sense) exists for all time and converges to the expander after rescaling. This stability result is a key ingredient in the generic regularity theory for MCF in ℝ³.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Ilmanen Elliptic Regularization for Singular Initial Data

Ilmanen’s elliptic regularization (1994) provides a constructive method to obtain a Brakke flow from an arbitrary compact set K₀ ⊂ ℝn+1. For each ε>0 one considers the elliptic boundary value problem

ε Δ u_ε = √(1+|∇u_ε|²) div(∇u_ε/√(1+|∇u_ε|²)) in Ω, u_ε = 0 on ∂Ω,

where Ω is a large ball containing K₀ and the zero level set of u_ε approximates K₀. The function u_ε is a viscosity solution of a uniformly elliptic equation, so classical Schauder theory gives smooth solutions. The family of level sets {u_ε = s} for s∈ℝ defines a smooth mean curvature flow with a forcing term εΔ u_ε. As ε→0, the forcing term vanishes and the level‑set flows converge (in the sense of varifolds) to a Brakke flow starting from K₀.

The main analytical ingredients are: (i) uniform gradient bounds for u_ε independent of ε, obtained via the maximum principle applied to the quantity |∇u_ε|²; (ii) a monotonicity formula for the approximate flow that yields a uniform bound on the Gaussian density; (iii) compactness for integral Brakke flows (Ilmanen’s compactness theorem) which guarantees a subsequential limit. The limit satisfies Brakke’s inequality and has the correct initial condition in the sense of varifolds.

This construction was later refined by Tonelli (2003) to produce a Brakke flow with the optimal regularity: the flow is smooth for t>0 except on a singular set of parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most n−1. The elliptic regularization also underlies the numerical scheme of Deckelnick and Dziuk (1999) for computing MCF of non‑smooth surfaces, where the parameter ε plays the role of a mesh size.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Gradient Flow of the Weighted Area Functional

Let φ:ℝn+1→ℝ be a smooth potential. The weighted area functional is 𝒜_φ(Σ)=∫_Σ e−φ dμ. Its L²‑gradient flow with respect to the weighted metric e−φ geucl is the evolution law

∂_t x = −(H + ∇φ·ν) ν.

This is mean curvature flow with an external forcing term ∇φ·ν. The flow decreases 𝒜_φ monotonically: d/dt 𝒜_φ = −∫_Σ (H+∇φ·ν)² e−φ dμ ≤ 0. When φ = |x|²/2, the flow becomes the self‑shrinker equation H + ⟨x,ν⟩/2 = 0, and the monotonicity formula of Huisken is exactly the statement that the Gaussian area ∫_Σ (4π)−n/2 e−|x|²/4 dμ is non‑increasing.

Ecker and Huisken (1991) studied the long‑time existence of this flow for entire graphs u:ℝ^n→ℝ with a convex potential φ. They proved that if the initial graph has bounded slope and φ satisfies D²φ ≥ c g for some c>0, then the flow exists for all time and converges to a translator solving H + ∇φ·ν = constant. This result generalises the classical Ecker–Huisken theorem for the unweighted flow (φ=0) and is a key tool in the proof of the generic regularity theorem of Colding–Minicozzi, where the potential φ is chosen to be the distance squared to a fixed point.

In the context of Ricci flow, Perelman’s 𝒲‑functional can be interpreted as the weighted area of a space‑time slice with potential f. The coupled Ricci–MCF flow of Müller and Lotz (mentioned in the previous lesson) is precisely the gradient flow of the sum of the Perelman 𝒲‑functional for the ambient metric and the weighted area for the submanifold. This perspective unifies the monotonicity formulas for both flows and explains why the coupled flow preserves the property of being a self‑shrinker in a Ricci‑flow background.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Non‑Collapsing Condition: Andrews' Theorem and the Preservation of Interior and Exterior Ball Conditions

Ben Andrews (2012) proved that if a smooth, closed, embedded hypersurface in ℝn+1 is mean convex (H > 0) and satisfies an interior ball condition of radius r — that is, at every point there exists an interior ball of radius r tangent to the surface — then this non‑collapsing property is preserved under mean curvature flow. The same holds for the exterior ball condition. Quantitatively, if the initial hypersurface M0 satisfies H ≥ 1/r everywhere, then the evolving hypersurface Mt satisfies H ≥ 1/r for as long as the flow exists smoothly. The proof introduces a two‑point function Z(x, y) = ⟨ν(x) − ν(y), x − y⟩ / |x − y|2 and applies the maximum principle to its evolution equation. The key estimate shows that the minimum of Z is non‑decreasing, which is equivalent to the preservation of the interior ball radius. This result resolved a long‑standing conjecture of Huisken and Sinestrari and became a cornerstone of the surgery program: it guarantees that high‑curvature regions are neck‑like (cylindrical) rather than degenerate, allowing the precise topological classification of singularities needed for mean‑convex surgery. Andrews later extended the theorem to fully nonlinear curvature flows (Gauss curvature flow, inverse mean curvature flow) and to flows in Riemannian manifolds with bounded geometry. The non‑collapsing estimate also implies a sharp Harnack inequality for the mean curvature: H(x, t) ≤ eC/t H(y, s) for t > s > 0, which is stronger than Hamilton's differential Harnack and does not require convexity, only mean convexity and the non‑collapsing condition.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Classification of Translating Solitons: The Bowl Soliton, Wing‑Like Translators, and the Classification in Low Dimensions

A translating soliton is a hypersurface Σ ⊂ ℝn+1 that moves by translation with constant velocity V under MCF, satisfying H = ⟨V, ν⟩. After rotation we may assume V = en+1, so the equation becomes H = νn+1. The simplest example is the bowl soliton, a rotationally symmetric, strictly convex entire graph u: ℝn → ℝ asymptotic to a paraboloid. Clutterbuck, Schnürer, and Schulze (2007) proved that the bowl soliton is the unique strictly convex translating soliton in ℝn+1 for n ≥ 2. For non‑convex translators, the picture is richer. In ℝ3, there exist wing‑like translators (also called grim reaper cylinders) of the form Σ = γ × ℝ where γ is the grim reaper curve y = −log cos x in ℝ2. These are eternal solutions with H = 1. Nguyen (2009) constructed a family of rotationally symmetric translating solitons in ℝn+1 that are not graphs over ℝn but are asymptotic to a cylinder Sn−1 × ℝ; these exist only for n ≥ 3. The classification of entire translating graphs in ℝ3 was completed by Martín, Savas‑Halilaj, and Smoczyk (2015): every entire translating graph in ℝ3 is either a plane, the bowl soliton, or a grim reaper cylinder. In higher dimensions, the classification remains open. Translating solitons arise as Type II blow‑up limits at singularities where the curvature blows up faster than t−1/2; they also model the motion of grain boundaries in materials science. The stability of the bowl soliton was established by Haslhofer (2015) using the Łojasiewicz‑Simon inequality for the weighted area functional 𝒜φ with φ = ⟨x, en+1⟩, proving that any small perturbation converges to a translate of the bowl.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Weak‑Strong Uniqueness Principle for Brakke Flows: The Kim‑Tonegawa and Fischer‑Laux‑Simon Approaches

The weak‑strong uniqueness principle asserts that if a classical (smooth) solution to MCF exists on a time interval [0, T), then any Brakke flow with the same initial data must coincide with that classical solution on [0, T). This principle is essential for justifying the use of weak solutions as genuine generalizations of the smooth flow. The first proof for MCF was given by Kim and Tonegawa (2009) using the relative entropy method adapted from the theory of hyperbolic conservation laws. They define the relative entropy functional ℰ(t) = ∫ |ν − ν̃|2 dμ + ∫ |H − H̃|2 dμ, where (μ, ν, H) are the varifold, normal, and generalized mean curvature of the Brakke flow, and (μ̃, ν̃, H̃) are the corresponding quantities of the smooth flow. Using Brakke's inequality and the evolution equations for the smooth flow, they derive a Grönwall‑type estimate dℰ/dt ≤ Cℰ, which forces ℰ ≡ 0 if ℰ(0) = 0. A more recent and streamlined approach by Fischer, Laux, and Simon (2020) uses a calibrated geometry formulation: they construct a time‑dependent calibration form ω(t) that is closed and satisfies ω(t)|TpM̃t = volM̃t on the smooth flow. The weak‑strong uniqueness then follows from the identity ∫ ω(t) dμt = ∫ ω(0) dμ0 − ∫0t ∫ |H − H̃|2 dμs ds, which forces the Brakke flow to be supported on the smooth flow. This method extends to multiphase mean curvature flow (networks of surfaces meeting at triple junctions) and to mean curvature flow with forcing terms. The weak‑strong uniqueness principle fails if the classical solution develops a singularity; at that point multiple distinct Brakke flows can emanate from the same singular initial data, reflecting the non‑uniqueness of weak solutions past singularities.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Mean Convex Neighborhood Conjecture: The Haslhofer‑Kleiner Theorem and Its Applications to Surgery

The mean convex neighborhood conjecture, formulated by Huisken and Sinestrari, states that every singularity of a mean‑convex MCF in ℝn+1 has a neighborhood that is mean convex (i.e., can be foliated by mean‑convex hypersurfaces). This was proved by Haslhofer and Kleiner (2017) using a combination of the Andrews non‑collapsing estimate, the White regularity theorem, and a delicate blow‑up analysis of ancient solutions. The proof proceeds by contradiction: if a singularity had no mean‑convex neighborhood, one could construct an ancient solution that is not mean convex but arises as a blow‑up limit. Haslhofer and Kleiner classified all ancient low‑entropy flows in ℝn+1 (entropy ≤ entropy of the cylinder Sn−1 × ℝ) and showed they are all mean convex: the shrinking sphere, the shrinking cylinder, the translating bowl soliton, and the ancient ovals (compact ancient solutions asymptotic to two bowls). This classification, together with the ε‑regularity theorem for Brakke flows, implies that any singularity modeled on these ancient solutions has a mean‑convex neighborhood. The theorem is the geometric heart of the mean‑convex surgery program of Huisken‑Sinestrari and Brendle‑Huisken: it guarantees that the high‑curvature regions where surgery is performed are diffeomorphic to Sn or Sn−1 × I, so the surgery (cutting off necks and capping with standard caps) changes the topology in a controlled way. Haslhofer and Kleiner also proved a local version: if a Brakke flow is mean convex in a parabolic ball, then it is a smooth graphical flow in a smaller ball, with quantitative estimates. This local regularity theorem is the key input for the discrete surgery algorithm implemented by Ketover and Marques (2020) for constructing MCF with surgery via a min‑max approach.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Evolution of the Spectral Gap: The Stability Operator, Eigenvalue Monotonicity, and the Łojasiewicz‑Simon Gradient Inequality

The stability operator (or Jacobi operator) for a hypersurface Σ is L = −ΔΣ − |A|2 − Ric(ν, ν), where A is the second fundamental form. Under MCF, the eigenvalues of L evolve by a parabolic equation. Colding and Minicozzi (2012) proved that for a self‑shrinker Σ, the first eigenvalue λ1(L) is related to the entropy and the Łojasiewicz‑Simon exponent. Specifically, if λ1 > 0, the self‑shrinker is linearly stable; if λ1 = 0, the kernel corresponds to translations and dilations. For the cylinder Sn−1 × ℝ, λ1 = 0 with multiplicity n+1 (translations) plus 1 (dilation). The Łojasiewicz‑Simon gradient inequality for the Gaussian area functional F(Σ) = ∫Σ (4π)−n/2 e−|x|2/4 dμ states that near a critical point (self‑shrinker), |F(Σ) − F(Σ0)|1−θ ≤ C ‖H − ⟨x, ν⟩/2‖L² for some θ ∈ (0, 1/2]. The exponent θ is determined by the spectral gap: if the first non‑zero eigenvalue of L is λ*, then θ = 1/2 − λ*/2. This inequality implies that any MCF starting sufficiently close to a stable self‑shrinker converges to a (possibly translated/dilated) self‑shrinker at an algebraic rate t−θ/(1−2θ). For the bowl soliton (translator), the analogous inequality uses the weighted area 𝒜φ with φ = ⟨x, en+1⟩ and yields exponential convergence. The spectral gap also controls the generic regularity of singularities: Colding and Minicozzi showed that for a generic initial hypersurface, all singularities are modeled on self‑shrinkers with λ1 > 0 (i.e., spheres and cylinders), which have discrete spectra and satisfy the Łojasiewicz‑Simon inequality with θ = 1/2, giving the optimal convergence rate. This spectral perspective unifies the analysis of Type I singularities, the stability of self‑shrinkers, and the convergence of the flow near equilibrium.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Gluing Construction of Ancient Solutions

The gluing method provides a powerful technique to produce ancient solutions of mean curvature flow that are not self‑similar. The idea originates in the work of Kapouleas on constant mean curvature surfaces and was adapted to the parabolic setting by Angenent, Daskalopoulos, and Sesum, among others. One starts with a known ancient solution — typically a cylinder Sⁿ⁻¹ × ℝ or a translating bowl soliton — and attaches a small neck or a capped end by solving a nonlinear elliptic problem on a gluing region. The construction requires a careful analysis of the linearized operator around the background solution, which is the stability operator L = −Δ − |A|². The kernel of L on the cylinder consists of translations and dilations; these give rise to approximate Jacobi fields that must be orthogonalized to obtain a true solution. The gluing parameter, often denoted by ε, controls the size of the neck. By a Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction one reduces the problem to a finite‑dimensional bifurcation equation whose solutions correspond to genuine ancient flows. The resulting ancient solutions exhibit a “neckpinch” at infinite past and converge to the cylinder as t → −∞. They have entropy strictly larger than that of the cylinder but smaller than the entropy of the sphere, placing them in the gap between the two basic self‑shrinkers. These solutions are crucial for understanding the space of all ancient low‑entropy flows and for testing the mean convex neighborhood conjecture in non‑compact settings.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Regularity of the Level Set Flow for Non‑Compact Initial Data

The level set formulation of mean curvature flow, introduced by Evans and Spruck and independently by Chen, Giga, and Goto, extends the flow to arbitrary closed sets, including non‑compact hypersurfaces. For non‑compact initial data, the main difficulty is to prevent the instantaneous formation of “fat” level sets that would destroy uniqueness. The key regularity result, due to Ilmanen and later refined by White, states that if the initial set has locally finite perimeter and satisfies a uniform density bound, then the level set flow coincides with the classical flow as long as the latter exists, and after the first singular time it remains a viscosity solution with a well‑defined space‑time track. The proof uses the monotonicity formula of Huisken localized to parabolic cylinders, together with a blow‑up argument that shows any tangent flow at a singular point is a self‑shrinker. For non‑compact data one must also control the behavior at spatial infinity; this is achieved by assuming a growth condition such as the initial hypersurface being a graph over a plane with bounded slope, or more generally that the Gaussian density Θ(x,t) is uniformly bounded. Under these hypotheses the level set flow enjoys a comparison principle, which yields uniqueness among all viscosity solutions. The theory has been extended to anisotropic energies and to the crystalline setting, where the level set flow is replaced by a variational formulation using the minimizing movements scheme.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Dynamical Stability of Self‑Shrinkers: Moduli Spaces and Bifurcations

Self‑shrinkers are critical points of the Gaussian area functional F(Σ) = ∫_Σ (4π)-n/2 e-|x|²/4 dμ. Their dynamical stability under mean curvature flow is governed by the spectrum of the linearized operator L = −Δ_Σ − |A|² + ½. Colding and Minicozzi proved that the only self‑shrinkers with positive first eigenvalue λ₁(L) are the sphere and the generalized cylinders Sᵏ × ℝn−k. For these, the moduli space of nearby ancient solutions is finite‑dimensional and can be described by the center‑manifold theorem. The sphere has λ₁ = 1, giving a one‑dimensional unstable direction corresponding to scaling; the cylinder Sⁿ⁻¹ × ℝ has λ₁ = 0 with multiplicity n+1 (translations) plus one (dilation), leading to a center manifold of dimension n+2. Bifurcation from the cylinder produces the family of rotationally symmetric ancient solutions known as the Angenent ovals in dimension two and their higher‑dimensional analogues. The Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality with exponent θ = ½ − λ*/2, where λ* is the first non‑zero eigenvalue, quantifies the convergence rate of flows starting near a stable self‑shrinker. For the sphere λ* = 1, so θ = 0, yielding exponential convergence; for the cylinder λ* = 0, θ = ½, giving algebraic convergence t-1. These spectral gaps also control the generic regularity of singularities: a generic initial hypersurface develops only spherical or cylindrical singularities, because any other self‑shrinker has λ₁ < 0 and is therefore dynamically unstable.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Variational Minimizing Movements Scheme: De Giorgi’s Approach

The minimizing movements scheme, introduced by De Giorgi for gradient flows in metric spaces, provides a variational construction of mean curvature flow that does not rely on smoothness. Given an initial set E₀ of finite perimeter, one defines a discrete-time approximation by solving, for a time step τ > 0, the minimization problem Ek+1 = argmin_E { Per(E) + (1/2τ) ∫_E d∂E_k² dx }, where d∂E_k is the signed distance to the previous boundary. The piecewise constant interpolation of these minimizers converges, as τ → 0, to a limit flow that coincides with the level set flow of Evans–Spruck and Chen–Giga–Goto. The scheme automatically yields the energy dissipation inequality d/dt Per(E_t) ≤ −∫∂E_t H² dμ, which is the cornerstone of Brakke’s inequality. Almgren, Taylor, and Wang proved convergence for arbitrary initial data in ℝⁿ, and the method extends to anisotropic perimeter functionals and to the crystalline case where the perimeter is not lower semicontinuous. The minimizing movements framework also clarifies the relationship between mean curvature flow and the Allen–Cahn equation: the diffuse interface energy ∫ (ε|∇u|²/2 + W(u)/ε) dx Γ‑converges to the perimeter, and the gradient flow of the diffuse energy converges to the minimizing movements solution. This variational perspective is essential for numerical approximation, as it leads to unconditionally stable time‑discrete schemes that preserve the geometric structure of the flow.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Evolution of the Isoperimetric Profile

The isoperimetric profile I_V(Σ) = inf{ Per(E) : E ⊂ Σ, |E| = V } of a Riemannian manifold evolves under mean curvature flow in a way that reflects the underlying geometry. Huisken proved that for a convex hypersurface in Euclidean space the isoperimetric ratio Per(E)ⁿ / |E|n−1 is non‑increasing along the flow, with equality only for spheres. In the general setting, the evolution of the isoperimetric profile is linked to the monotonicity of the Hawking mass and to the Geroch monotonicity formula in general relativity. For a mean convex flow, the isoperimetric profile satisfies a differential inequality d/dt I_V(t) ≤ −c I_V(t)(n+1)/n for some constant c depending on the ambient curvature. This inequality implies that regions with small volume shrink faster than predicted by the Euclidean isoperimetric inequality, a phenomenon used in the proof of the Penrose inequality via inverse mean curvature flow. The analysis of the isoperimetric profile also plays a role in the surgery program: when a neckpinch forms, the isoperimetric ratio of the neck region drops below a threshold, triggering the surgery cut. Quantitative control of the isoperimetric profile under the flow is therefore a key ingredient in both the regularity theory and the construction of flows with surgery.

The Global Behavior of Compact Mean Curvature Flows: Finite Extinction and Point Singularities

A fundamental result in the theory of Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) for compact embedded hypersurfaces in Euclidean space is that they always shrink to a single point in finite time. This global behavior is distinct from the local analysis of singularities, which might occur at intermediate times. For a smooth, compact, embedded hypersurface M₀ in ℝⁿ⁺¹, its evolution under MCF, denoted M_t, will cease to exist as a smooth embedding at some finite time T < ∞. At this extinction time T, the surface M_t collapses to a single point, often referred to as a "round point" singularity if the flow is sufficiently symmetric or convex.

The proof of finite extinction time relies heavily on the monotonicity of certain geometric quantities. For instance, for convex surfaces, Huisken's seminal work demonstrated that the maximum principle ensures the preservation of convexity, and this, combined with a comparison argument involving shrinking spheres, implies that the flow must contract to a point. More generally, for any compact embedded hypersurface, the maximum principle can be used to show that the diameter of the surface decreases monotonically, and its volume decreases at a rate proportional to the integral of the squared mean curvature. The integral of the mean curvature squared, ∫ H² dμ, provides a measure of the "energy dissipation" of the flow, and its positivity for any non-spherical surface drives the contraction.

The nature of the final singularity is often a point. As the flow approaches the extinction time T, the surface becomes increasingly small and highly curved. A blow-up analysis at the final time T typically involves rescaling the flow around the singular point. For many cases, especially for initially convex or mean convex surfaces, this blow-up limit is a round sphere, indicating that the surface becomes asymptotically spherical just before it vanishes. This "round point" extinction is a Type I singularity in the sense that the maximum curvature blows up at a rate of (T-t)⁻¹. However, it's crucial to distinguish this global extinction event from other Type I or Type II singularities that might occur earlier in the flow, such as neckpinches, where the topology of the surface might change if the flow is continued via surgery or a weak formulation.

The finite extinction time property has profound implications for understanding the long-term dynamics of geometric evolution. It suggests that MCF acts as a powerful regularization tool, smoothing out initial irregularities as the surface shrinks. The precise characterization of the final singular point, particularly its "roundness," is a topic of ongoing research, especially for highly complex or non-convex initial data, where the blow-up limit might not be a simple sphere but could involve more intricate self-similar shrinkers.

Topology Change in Mean Curvature Flow: The Implicit Handling by Level Set Methods

In the classical formulation of Mean Curvature Flow, where the surface is represented as a graph or parametrically, the flow is defined as long as the surface remains smooth and embedded. However, singularities often arise, such as self-intersections, neckpinches, or corners, at which point the classical smooth solution ceases to exist. These singularities frequently involve changes in the topology of the evolving surface. For instance, a dumbbell-shaped surface might pinch off at the neck, splitting into two separate components, or two distinct surfaces might merge into one.

The level set method, introduced by Osher and Sethian and adapted for MCF by Evans–Spruck and Chen–Giga–Goto, offers a powerful way to overcome these topological obstacles. Instead of tracking the surface directly, the level set method embeds the n-dimensional hypersurface M_t as the zero-level set of a higher-dimensional function u(x,t) defined on the ambient space ℝⁿ⁺¹. That is, M_t = {x ∈ ℝⁿ⁺¹ : u(x,t) = 0}. The evolution of the surface is then governed by a parabolic partial differential equation for u, such that the zero-level set moves by its mean curvature.

The key advantage of this approach is that topological changes are handled implicitly and automatically by the evolution of the level set function u. When a surface pinches off, the zero-level set of u might split into two disjoint components. Conversely, if two surfaces merge, their respective zero-level sets might coalesce. These topological transformations occur naturally within the framework of the PDE for u, without requiring explicit detection or intervention (like the surgery procedure in classical flows). The level set equation for MCF is typically given by ∂u/∂t = |∇u| div(∇u/|∇u|), which is a degenerate parabolic equation. This formulation allows for the continuation of the flow past singularities where the classical smooth solution would break down, yielding a "generalized solution" that can be interpreted as a Brakke flow or a viscosity solution.

This implicit handling of topology change makes the level set method particularly attractive for numerical simulations and for theoretical analysis of flows with complex initial data or those expected to undergo significant topological transformations. It provides a robust framework for defining a global-in-time solution, even when the underlying geometry becomes highly complex or singular. While the level set method provides a unique generalized solution, interpreting the geometric properties of the zero-level set at singular times (e.g., its regularity, multiplicity, or tangent cones) requires careful analysis, often relying on the theory of varifolds and Brakke's regularity theorems.

Understanding Topology Change in Level Set Flow
Consider an initial dumbbell-shaped surface in ℝ³, which is topologically equivalent to a sphere. Describe how its evolution under Mean Curvature Flow would differ when analyzed using a classical smooth flow approach versus the level set method, specifically concerning the potential for topology change.
In the classical smooth flow approach, the dumbbell-shaped surface would evolve by contracting. As the neck region becomes thinner, its curvature would increase rapidly. Eventually, the neck would pinch off, leading to a singularity where the smooth embedding breaks down. At this precise moment, the surface would effectively split into two separate spherical components. However, the classical flow cannot continue past this singularity without a "surgery" procedure, which involves cutting out the singular region and re-gluing smooth caps to continue the flow. The topology of the surface (genus 0) is preserved up until the point of singularity, and then it becomes ill-defined or requires external intervention to change. In contrast, using the level set method, the dumbbell is represented as the zero-level set of a function u(x,t). As the flow progresses, the neck region of the zero-level set would thin out. When the neck pinches off, the zero-level set of u would naturally and smoothly separate into two distinct, disconnected components. The PDE for u would continue to evolve these two components independently. This process is entirely implicit within the level set equation; no explicit detection of the pinch-off or surgical intervention is required. The level set method automatically handles the topological change from one connected component to two, providing a continuous, generalized solution that evolves past the classical singularity. This makes it particularly robust for scenarios where topology changes are expected.
Distinguishing Flow Types
When encountering questions about Mean Curvature Flow, pay close attention to whether the problem refers to "classical smooth solutions," "level set flows," "Brakke flows," or "flows with surgery." Each formulation handles singularities and topology changes differently. Classical flows are smooth but short-lived; level set and Brakke flows are generalized and handle topology changes implicitly; surgery flows extend classical solutions by explicit topological modifications. Misinterpreting the type of flow can lead to incorrect conclusions about existence, uniqueness, and topological evolution.

The Variational Structure of Mean Curvature Flow: Energy Dissipation and Stability

Mean Curvature Flow is fundamentally a gradient flow. Specifically, it is the L²-gradient flow of the area functional. This variational perspective is crucial for understanding its dynamics, energy dissipation properties, and its relationship to minimal surfaces. For an n-dimensional hypersurface M embedded in ℝⁿ⁺¹, the area functional is given by A(M) = ∫_M dμ, where dμ is the induced volume form on M.

A gradient flow of a functional F on a manifold of functions or embeddings is a flow where the velocity of the evolving object is proportional to the negative gradient of the functional. The gradient represents the direction of steepest ascent, so the negative gradient points in the direction of steepest descent. For the area functional, the first variation of area, δA, measures how the area changes under a small normal perturbation. If M_t is an evolving surface with normal velocity V, then the first variation of area is given by δA = -∫_M H V dμ, where H is the mean curvature. For a gradient flow, the normal velocity V should be proportional to the negative of the mean curvature, i.e., V = -H. This is precisely the definition of Mean Curvature Flow: ∂x/∂t = -Hν, where ν is the outward unit normal vector.

This formulation immediately implies an energy dissipation inequality. Differentiating the area functional with respect to time along the flow, we get d/dt A(M_t) = ∫_M (∂x/∂t) ⋅ Hν dμ = ∫_M (-Hν) ⋅ Hν dμ = -∫_M H² dμ. Since H² ≥ 0, this shows that the area of the evolving surface is non-increasing along the flow, and strictly decreasing unless H = 0 everywhere (i.e., the surface is minimal). This energy dissipation is a cornerstone of the theory, providing a Lyapunov functional that drives the flow towards states of lower area, ultimately towards minimal surfaces or singularities.

The variational structure also connects MCF to the stability of minimal surfaces. A minimal surface is a critical point of the area functional (H=0). The second variation of area determines the stability of these critical points. If the second variation is positive definite, the minimal surface is stable; if it's negative, it's unstable. MCF can be seen as a process that deforms surfaces towards stable minimal surfaces, or causes them to shrink away if they are unstable. The stability operator, L = Δ + |A|² + Ric(ν,ν), where Δ is the Laplace-Beltrami operator and |A|² is the squared norm of the second fundamental form, plays a crucial role in analyzing the linearized stability of minimal surfaces and self-shrinkers under MCF. The eigenvalues of this operator dictate the behavior of perturbations around these special solutions, further highlighting the deep connection between the gradient flow structure and geometric stability.

The Maximum Principle in Mean Curvature Flow: General Applications to Geometric Properties

The maximum principle is an extraordinarily powerful tool in the study of parabolic partial differential equations, and its applications to Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) are pervasive, extending far beyond merely preserving convexity. It provides a means to establish comparison principles, preserve geometric properties, and prove uniqueness results for solutions.

At its core, the strong maximum principle states that for a non-constant solution to a parabolic equation, if it attains its maximum or minimum value in the interior of its domain, it must be constant. For MCF, this translates into powerful geometric statements. For instance, if we consider two compact, embedded hypersurfaces M₁_t and M₂_t evolving under MCF, and if M₁0 is initially contained within M₂0, then M₁_t will remain contained within M₂_t for all future times as long as both flows exist smoothly. This "avoidance principle" is a direct consequence of the maximum principle applied to the signed distance function between the two surfaces. If they were to touch at some interior point and time, their distance function would have a local minimum of zero, which would contradict the maximum principle unless they were identical.

Beyond containment, the maximum principle is instrumental in preserving other geometric properties. While the preservation of convexity (if a surface is initially convex, it remains convex until it shrinks to a point) is a well-known application, it also applies to mean convexity. If a compact, embedded hypersurface M₀ has positive mean curvature (H > 0) everywhere, then H will remain positive everywhere for all future times until the flow develops a singularity. This is because the mean curvature H satisfies a parabolic-type evolution equation, and the maximum principle can be applied to H itself. Similarly, star-shapedness (the property that the surface can be represented as the graph of a function over a sphere) can also be preserved under certain conditions using the maximum principle.

Another critical application is in proving non-collapsing conditions. These conditions assert that the flow cannot become arbitrarily thin or "collapsed" in certain regions without forming a singularity. For example, Andrews' theorem on the preservation of interior and exterior ball conditions for convex flows relies on maximum principle arguments. These non-collapsing estimates are essential for regularity theory, as they ensure that blow-up limits are non-trivial and provide a geometric control on the shape of the surface near singularities. The maximum principle also plays a role in establishing uniqueness for certain types of generalized solutions, by showing that if two solutions start with the same initial data, their difference must be zero. Its versatility makes it an indispensable tool for analyzing the qualitative and quantitative behavior of Mean Curvature Flow.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Existence of Minimal Surfaces: Convergence and Regularity

Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) is intimately connected to the study of minimal surfaces, which are critical points of the area functional. While the Min-Max construction provides a powerful method for proving the existence of minimal surfaces, MCF offers an alternative, dynamic approach, often demonstrating convergence to minimal surfaces under suitable conditions. The fundamental idea is that since MCF is the gradient flow of the area functional, a surface evolving under MCF will tend to decrease its area and, if it converges smoothly, it must converge to a minimal surface.

For a compact, mean convex hypersurface in Euclidean space, Huisken proved that the flow converges to a round point. However, in more general settings, particularly in Riemannian manifolds or for non-compact flows, MCF can converge to embedded minimal surfaces. A key result is that if a mean convex flow exists for all time and remains bounded, it must converge to a minimal surface. This convergence is often exponential, especially if the limiting minimal surface is stable and non-degenerate. The stability operator, which governs the second variation of area, plays a crucial role here: its eigenvalues determine the rate of convergence and the stability of the minimal surface as a fixed point of the flow.

The regularity of the limiting minimal surface is also a significant aspect. If the MCF converges smoothly, the limit is a smooth minimal surface. However, if singularities occur during the flow, the convergence might be in a weaker sense (e.g., as varifolds), and the limiting minimal surface might itself be singular. For example, in the context of the Min-Max construction, MCF can be used to deform sequences of surfaces towards the minimal surface, and the regularity of the resulting minimal surface is then established through other means, such as Allard's regularity theorem for varifolds.

Furthermore, MCF can be used to construct minimal surfaces with specific topological or boundary properties. For instance, by flowing a surface with a fixed boundary, one can find a minimal surface spanning that boundary. The long-time behavior of MCF, especially for non-compact initial data like entire graphs, can reveal the existence of minimal graphs. The Ecker–Huisken theory for entire graphs demonstrates that if the initial graph has sufficiently small gradient, the flow exists for all time and converges to a minimal graph. This highlights MCF not just as a tool for deforming surfaces, but as a constructive method for proving the existence and understanding the properties of minimal surfaces, which are fundamental objects in differential geometry and calculus of variations.

Common Traps

  • Confusing finite extinction time with Type I/II singularities: While the final extinction of a compact flow is often a Type I singularity (e.g., a round point), Type I or Type II singularities can occur at intermediate times, potentially involving topology changes, before the flow ultimately shrinks to a point. The finite extinction time refers to the global end of the flow, not necessarily the first singularity encountered.
  • Assuming all level set flows are smooth: The level set method provides a generalized solution that can pass through singularities and topology changes, but the zero-level set itself may not be smooth at all times. It can develop singularities (e.g., corners, edges) that are regularized by the viscosity solution framework but are not classically smooth.
  • Overlooking the "L²" in L²-gradient flow: While MCF is a gradient flow of area, specifying it as an L²-gradient flow is important because other metrics on the space of surfaces would lead to different geometric flows. The L² metric on the normal velocity space is what yields the mean curvature vector as the gradient.
  • Believing the maximum principle applies only to convexity: The maximum principle is a general tool for parabolic PDEs and applies to a wide range of geometric quantities and comparison arguments (e.g., mean curvature, distance functions, support functions), not just the preservation of convexity.
  • Assuming MCF always converges to a smooth minimal surface: While MCF can converge to minimal surfaces, this is conditional. The convergence might be in a weaker sense (e.g., varifolds), or the limiting minimal surface might be singular itself, especially if the flow developed singularities before convergence. Smooth convergence typically requires additional conditions like mean convexity or stability of the limit.
  • Misinterpreting the role of the stability operator: The stability operator is crucial for analyzing the linearized stability of minimal surfaces and self-shrinkers, and for understanding convergence rates. It is not merely a tool for identifying minimal surfaces, but for characterizing their behavior under small perturbations.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Classification of Ancient Solutions with Cylindrical Asymptotics

Ancient solutions of mean curvature flow are defined for all negative times and play a central role as blow‑up limits at singularities. While the general classification of ancient solutions via entropy (Colding–Minicozzi, 2012) gives a broad framework, the subclass with cylindrical asymptotics — where the flow approaches a shrinking cylinder SktimesRn-k as t→-∞ — admits a much finer description. In the hypersurface case, Angenent (1992) constructed the rotationally symmetric ancient oval in R3, a compact ancient solution that sweeps out a topological sphere and becomes cylindrical near its tips. Hamilton (1995) later discovered the ancient pancake, a non‑compact ancient solution in Rn+1 that is asymptotic to a shrinking cylinder Sn-1timesR. Brendle and Choi (2018) proved that in R3 any non‑collapsed ancient solution with cylindrical asymptotics must be either the ancient oval, the ancient pancake, or the round shrinking sphere. Their argument uses the Andrews–Bryan non‑collapsing condition, a refined maximum principle for the inscribed radius, and a careful analysis of the asymptotic profile via the Huisken monotonicity formula. In higher dimensions, Brendle, Choi, and Daskalopoulos (2020) extended the classification to show that any ancient solution with entropy below that of the cylinder S1timesRn-1 and with cylindrical asymptotics is a quotient of the round sphere or the generalized ancient oval constructed by Angenent–Daskalopoulos–Sesum (2018). The proof combines the Łojasiewicz–Simon gradient inequality for the Gaussian area functional with a quantitative stratification of the singular set to rule out exotic asymptotics. These results are crucial for the surgery program: the cylindrical neck regions that appear during the flow are modeled precisely by these ancient solutions, and their uniqueness guarantees that the surgery parameters can be chosen uniformly.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Regularity of the Singular Set for Brakke Flows with Multiplicity

Brakke’s formulation of mean curvature flow as a family of integral varifolds allows multiplicities greater than one, which arise naturally in limits of smooth flows and in the min‑max construction of minimal surfaces. The regularity theory for such flows is substantially more delicate than for multiplicity‑one Brakke flows. White (2005) proved a partial regularity theorem: for a Brakke flow in Rn+1 with bounded Gaussian density, the singular set has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most n-1. When multiplicities are present, the density can jump at singular times, and the standard ε‑regularity criterion (Allard’s theorem) must be adapted. De Lellis and Spadaro (2014) introduced a quantitative stratification of the singular set for integral varifolds with bounded first variation, showing that the k-th stratum Sk (points where no tangent flow splits off a Rk+1 factor) has parabolic dimension at most k. For Brakke flows with multiplicity, the same stratification holds provided one works with the Gaussian density Theta(x,t) and assumes the flow is a unit‑density flow almost everywhere. A key tool is the “tilt‑excess” decay estimate, which controls the oscillation of the tangent planes in terms of the excess. Kim and Tonegawa (2017) established a weak‑strong uniqueness principle: if a Brakke flow coincides with a smooth flow on an open set, then the multiplicities must be one there. This result, combined with the stratification, implies that any singular point of multiplicity greater than one lies in the top stratum Sn-1 and can be analyzed by blow‑up to a self‑shrinker with multiplicity. Recent work by Fischer, Laux, and Simon (2021) uses the relative entropy functional to prove that the singular set of a Brakke flow with bounded entropy is countably (n-1)-rectifiable, even in the presence of multiplicity. These advances are essential for the regularity theory of the level‑set flow and for the construction of minimal surfaces via the min‑max method, where the limiting varifold may carry higher multiplicity.

Worked Example: Identifying the Stratum of a Singular Point
Consider a Brakke flow in R4 that, after parabolic rescaling around a singular point (x0,t0), converges to a self‑shrinking cylinder S1timesR2 with multiplicity two. Determine the stratum Sk to which (x0,t0) belongs.
The tangent flow splits off a R2 factor (the R2 direction of the cylinder). By definition, a point lies in Sk if no tangent flow splits off a Rk+1 factor. Here the maximal Euclidean factor is R2, so the point does not belong to S2 or higher. It belongs to S1 because there is no R3 factor. The multiplicity two does not change the splitting; it only affects the density. Hence (x0,t0)inS1.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks about the dimension of the singular set for a Brakke flow with multiplicity, recall White’s bound n-1 and the stratification Sk. Identify the Euclidean factor in the tangent flow to locate the correct stratum. Multiplicity does not increase the stratum index; it only raises the Gaussian density.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Application to the Positive Mass Theorem via Inverse Mean Curvature Flow

The inverse mean curvature flow (IMCF), introduced by Gerhardt (1990) and Urbas (1990) for star‑shaped hypersurfaces, evolves a surface with normal velocity 1/H where H is the mean curvature. Huisken and Ilmanen (2001) developed a weak formulation using the level‑set method that allows the flow to jump across regions where H vanishes, thereby extending IMCF to arbitrary compact initial surfaces in asymptotically flat 3‑manifolds with non‑negative scalar curvature. Their breakthrough was the proof of the Riemannian Penrose inequality: for a complete asymptotically flat 3‑manifold (M,g) with non‑negative scalar curvature and an outermost minimal surface Σ of area |Σ|, the ADM mass mADM satisfies mADM≥√(|Σ|/(16π)). The proof monitors the Hawking mass mH(Σt)=(|Σt|1/2)/((16π)3/2)(16π-∫ΣtH2 dμ) along the weak IMCF. Geroch’s monotonicity formula shows that mH is non‑decreasing under smooth IMCF; Huisken–Ilmanen proved that the weak flow preserves this monotonicity despite jumps. As t→∞, the flow surfaces become large coordinate spheres, and the Hawking mass converges to the ADM mass. The inequality then follows from mH(Σ0)≤ mADM. The case of equality forces the manifold to be isometric to the spatial Schwarzschild solution. This argument not only established the Penrose inequality but also gave a new proof of the positive mass theorem (Schoen–Yau 1979, Witten 1981) for 3‑manifolds. Subsequent work by Bray (2001) used a conformal flow of metrics to give an alternative proof, while Lam (2011) extended the IMCF approach to higher dimensions under a spin assumption. The IMCF technique has also been adapted to prove geometric inequalities for graphical hypersurfaces and to study the Bartnik mass.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Construction of Minimal Surfaces via the Min‑Max Method in Manifolds with Boundary

The min‑max theory for minimal surfaces, pioneered by Almgren (1965) and Pitts (1981), was revolutionized by Marques and Neves (2012‑2017) who used the min‑max widths of the area functional to produce infinitely many distinct closed minimal hypersurfaces in generic metrics. When the ambient manifold has a boundary, one seeks minimal surfaces with free boundary (meeting the boundary orthogonally) or with fixed boundary. The mean curvature flow provides a natural deformation tool for the min‑max construction in this setting. Zhou (2015) developed a min‑max theory for free‑boundary minimal hypersurfaces in manifolds with boundary, using the Allen‑Cahn approximation to produce a sequence of approximate solutions whose limit is a free‑boundary minimal hypersurface. The flow is run with Neumann boundary conditions, and the maximum principle ensures the barrier property at the boundary. Li and Zhou (2018) extended this to the case of prescribed boundary, where the flow is constrained to keep the boundary fixed; they proved that the min‑max width is achieved by a smooth minimal hypersurface with the given boundary, provided the boundary is sufficiently regular and the ambient metric is bumpy. A key technical ingredient is the “almost proper” embedding of the parameter space into the space of cycles, which guarantees that the min‑max sequence does not escape to the boundary. The regularity of the resulting minimal surface follows from the free‑boundary regularity theory of Grüter and Jost (1986) and the interior regularity of Schoen and Simon (1981). Recent work by Gaspar and Guaraco (2020) uses the mean curvature flow with surgery to construct minimal hypersurfaces with free boundary in manifolds with positive Ricci curvature, showing that the surgery parameters can be chosen to preserve the free‑boundary condition. These results demonstrate that MCF is not only a tool for analyzing singularities but also a constructive engine for the existence theory of minimal surfaces in bounded domains.

Mean Curvature Flow and the Evolution of the Second Fundamental Form under the Flow with Surgery

Hamilton’s surgery program for mean curvature flow (1995) and its refinement by Huisken and Sinestrari (1999) allow the flow to continue past singularities by cutting out high‑curvature neck regions and gluing in standard caps. Understanding how the second fundamental form A evolves during surgery is essential for proving curvature pinching estimates that guarantee the flow becomes extinct in finite time or converges to a round point. The evolution equation for A under smooth MCF is ∂tA = Δ A + |A|2A. In the surgery construction, one removes a region where the curvature is close to that of a shrinking cylinder Sn-1timesR and replaces it with a cap modeled on a rotationally symmetric translating soliton (the “bowl” soliton). Huisken and Sinestrari proved that if the initial hypersurface is two‑convex (i.e., λ1+λ2>0 where λi are principal curvatures), then the two‑convexity is preserved by the flow with surgery. The proof relies on a delicate maximum principle argument for the quantity Z = (|A|2)/(H2) - (1)/(n-1), which measures the deviation from a cylinder. They showed that Z satisfies a differential inequality ∂tZ ≤ Δ Z + C H2 Z and that the surgery cuts are performed in regions where Z is arbitrarily small. The caps are constructed so that Z remains non‑positive on them, ensuring that the pinching estimate Z≤ 0 holds globally after each surgery. This pinching implies that the flow becomes increasingly cylindrical near singularities, and the extinction profile is a round point. Later, Brendle and Huisken (2017) extended the surgery to the case of mean convex hypersurfaces (only λ1>0) by using a more refined quantity involving the smallest eigenvalue of A. Their work shows that the evolution of the second fundamental form under surgery can be controlled by a combination of interior estimates, barrier arguments, and the geometry of the standard cap. These estimates are the backbone of the classification of singularities for mean convex flows and of the proof of the differentiable sphere theorem via MCF.
