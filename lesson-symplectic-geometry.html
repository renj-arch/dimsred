Symplectic Geometry — The Geometry of Hamiltonian Mechanics

Symplectic geometry is the study of symplectic manifolds — even-dimensional smooth manifolds with a closed, non-degenerate 2-form ω. It's the natural mathematical framework for Hamiltonian mechanics: the phase space of a classical system is a symplectic manifold. The field includes Hamiltonian dynamics, moment maps, symplectic reduction (Marsden-Weinstein), Floer homology, Gromov-Witten invariants, and mirror symmetry.

"Symplectic geometry is the geometry of phase space — where the laws of classical mechanics unfold."
— Vladimir Arnold (paraphrased)
Part 1

🌀 Symplectic Manifolds and Darboux's Theorem

Phase Space
Symplectic Forms, Lagrangian Submanifolds, and Darboux Coordinates
A symplectic manifold (M,ω) has ω closed (dω=0) and non-degenerate (ω∧n ≠ 0). The standard example is ℝ^{2n} with ω₀ = Σ dp_i∧dq_i. Darboux's theorem: every symplectic manifold is locally symplectomorphic to (ℝ^{2n}, ω₀) — there are no local invariants, unlike Riemannian geometry. The Hamiltonian vector field X_H is defined by ω(X_H, ·) = dH — its flow preserves ω. The Poisson bracket {F,G} = ω(X_F, X_G) makes C^∞(M) into a Lie algebra. A Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ M is a maximal isotropic submanifold (dim n, ω|_L = 0). The graph of a symplectomorphism is Lagrangian in (M×M, ω⊕−ω). The Weinstein tubular neighborhood theorem: a Lagrangian has a neighborhood symplectomorphic to T*L.
Darboux's theorem says all symplectic manifolds look the same locally — the global geometry is captured by topology and the choice of compatible almost complex structure.
Part 2

⚖️ Moment Maps and Symplectic Reduction

Reduction
Moment Maps, Marsden-Weinstein Reduction, and Toric Varieties
A Hamiltonian group action of G on M has a moment map μ: M → g^* satisfying d⟨μ,ξ⟩ = ω(ξ_M, ·) where ξ_M is the fundamental vector field. For G = S¹ acting on (ℂⁿ, ω₀ = i/2 Σ dz_k∧dz̄_k), μ(z) = Σ |z_k|². The Marsden-Weinstein quotient (symplectic reduction): M//G = μ^{-1}(0)/G is a symplectic manifold (if 0 is a regular value and the action is free). This is how one builds new symplectic manifolds from old ones — e.g., ℙⁿ = ℂ^{n+1}//S¹. Toric varieties are symplectic quotients of ℂ^N by a torus — their moment polytopes (Delzant polytopes) classify them. The Atiyah-Guillemin-Sternberg theorem: the image of the moment map of a torus action is a convex polytope — the convex hull of the images of fixed points.
Symplectic reduction is the geometric version of eliminating symmetries in mechanics — the quotient of the zero level set of the moment map is a new symplectic manifold.
Part 3

🌸 Floer Homology and Pseudoholomorphic Curves

Holomorphic
Gromov's Compactness, Floer Homology, and Arnold's Conjecture
Gromov's theorem (1985): pseudoholomorphic curves in a symplectic manifold with a compatible almost complex structure satisfy a compactness theorem — sequences converge to nodal curves (bubbling). This is the foundation of modern symplectic topology. Floer homology (Floer, 1988) counts pseudoholomorphic cylinders connecting two Lagrangian submanifolds — it's an invariant of the pair. Hamiltonian Floer homology studies fixed points of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms and proved the Arnold conjecture: the number of fixed points of a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism is at least the rank of H*(M). The PSS isomorphism (Piunikhin-Salamon-Schwarz) relates Floer homology to quantum cohomology. Gromov-Witten invariants count pseudoholomorphic curves in M with constraints — they define the quantum cup product, making H*(M) into a Frobenius manifold. Kontsevich's homological mirror symmetry: the Fukaya category (Lagrangian Floer theory) of M is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves of its mirror.
Pseudoholomorphic curves are the central tool of modern symplectic geometry — they give invariants (Floer homology, GW invariants) that detect deep structure.
Part 4

🪞 Mirror Symmetry and Modern Directions

Duality
Mirror Symmetry, Fukaya Categories, and Spectral Networks
Mirror symmetry (Candelas-de la Ossa-Green-Parkes, 1991): Calabi-Yau 3-folds come in mirror pairs (M, W) with Hodge diamonds mirrored: h^{p,q}(M) = h^{3−p,q}(W). The A-model (symplectic, counting holomorphic curves) on M equals the B-model (complex, algebraic deformations) on W. The Fukaya category Fuk(M) has Lagrangian submanifolds as objects and Floer cohomology as morphisms. The derived category D^b Coh(W) has coherent sheaves as objects. Kontsevich's HMS conjecture: Fuk(M) ≅ D^b Coh(W). Stability conditions (Bridgeland) on derived categories give a wall-and-chamber decomposition — they're related to the space of complexified Kähler classes. Spectral networks (Gaiotto-Moore-Neitzke) describe BPS states in 4D N=2 QFTs — they're graphs on the Coulomb branch where flat sections of Hitchin's equations have jumps. Symplectic field theory (Eliashberg-Givental-Hofer) studies pseudoholomorphic curves in symplectic cobordisms, giving relative invariants.
Mirror symmetry reveals that symplectic geometry (counting curves) and complex geometry (studying sheaves) are two sides of the same coin — a profound duality.

Symplectic Capacities and Embedding Problems

Symplectic capacities are numerical invariants that measure the “size” of a symplectic manifold in a way that respects symplectic embeddings. The first capacity, introduced by Gromov in 1985, is the Gromov width: for a symplectic manifold (M, ω) it is the supremum of πr² such that a standard ball B²ⁿ(r) embeds symplectically into M. Gromov’s non‑squeezing theorem states that a ball B²ⁿ(R) cannot be symplectically embedded into a cylinder B²(r) × ℝ²ⁿ⁻² unless R ≤ r. This result shows that symplectic geometry has rigid “size” constraints absent in volume‑preserving diffeomorphisms.

Subsequent capacities include the Ekeland–Hofer capacities, defined via the Conley–Zehnder index of periodic orbits on the boundary of a domain, and the Hofer–Zehnder capacity, which uses the existence of fast periodic Hamiltonian orbits. All these capacities satisfy monotonicity (if U V symplectically then c(U) ≤ c(V)), conformality (c(λU) = λ² c(U)), and normalization on the standard ball and cylinder. They have become the primary tool for solving embedding problems: for example, the exact embedding classification of four‑dimensional ellipsoids E(a,b) into balls was completed by McDuff and Schlenk (2012) using the sequence of ECH capacities derived from embedded contact homology.

Capacities also detect subtle phenomena such as symplectic packing. The packing numbers p_k(M) — the supremum of total volume fraction filled by k disjoint equal balls — are governed by the Gromov width and higher capacities. In dimension four, the full packing function for many rational and ruled surfaces is known, exhibiting a staircase pattern related to the Fibonacci numbers. These results illustrate how capacities translate global symplectic topology into concrete numerical obstructions.

Contact Geometry and its Symplectic Fillings

Contact geometry is the odd‑dimensional counterpart of symplectic geometry. A contact manifold (Y, ξ) of dimension 2n‑1 carries a hyperplane field ξ = ker α where α is a 1‑form with α ∧ (dα)ⁿ⁻¹ ≠ 0. The Reeb vector field R is defined by α(R)=1, dα(R,·)=0. A symplectic filling of (Y, ξ) is a compact symplectic manifold (W, ω) with ∂W = Y such that ω|_ξ > 0 and a Liouville vector field points outward along the boundary. Weak, strong, and Stein fillings form a hierarchy: every Stein filling is strong, every strong filling is weak.

Eliashberg’s classification of tight contact structures on S³ (1992) showed that the standard contact structure is the unique tight one, while overtwisted structures are classified by homotopy classes of plane fields. The existence of a Stein filling implies tightness, but the converse fails in higher dimensions. Giroux’s correspondence (2000) establishes a bijection between contact structures on a closed 3‑manifold up to isotopy and open book decompositions up to positive stabilization, providing a combinatorial handle on contact topology.

Symplectic field theory (SFT), introduced by Eliashberg, Givental, and Hofer (2000), constructs invariants of contact manifolds by counting punctured pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectization ℝ × Y. The resulting algebraic structure — a differential graded algebra generated by Reeb orbits — yields contact homology, a powerful invariant that distinguishes many contact manifolds with the same underlying smooth topology. SFT also provides a framework for defining Gromov–Witten invariants of symplectic cobordisms, linking closed and open symplectic topology.

Worked Example: Computing the Gromov Width of an Ellipsoid
Let E(a,b) = { (z₁,z₂) ∈ ℂ² | π|z₁|²/a + π|z₂|²/b < 1 } with 0 < a ≤ b. Determine its Gromov width c_G(E(a,b)).
The Gromov width of a four‑dimensional ellipsoid equals the smaller symplectic area a. Proof: the inclusion B⁴(√(a/π)) E(a,b) given by scaling the first coordinate shows c_G ≥ a. For the upper bound, assume a ball of capacity > a embeds. By monotonicity of the Ekeland–Hofer capacities c_k, the first capacity of the ball is its Gromov width, while the first capacity of E(a,b) is a. Since capacities are monotone, the ball’s capacity cannot exceed a. Hence c_G(E(a,b)) = a.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a symplectic capacity of a domain, first identify whether the domain is a ball, cylinder, ellipsoid, or polydisk. Recall the normalization values: c_G(B²ⁿ(r)) = πr², c_G(Z²ⁿ(r)) = πr². For ellipsoids E(a,b) the answer is min(a,b). For polydisks P(a,b) the Gromov width is min(a,b) as well, but higher Ekeland–Hofer capacities differ. Write down the relevant monotonicity property to justify any inequality.

Geometric Quantization and the Role of Polarizations

Geometric quantization provides a systematic procedure to associate a Hilbert space to a symplectic manifold (M, ω) equipped with a prequantum line bundle L → M whose curvature equals −i ω. The prequantum Hilbert space consists of square‑integrable sections of L, but it is too large; a polarization — an integrable Lagrangian distribution P ⊂ TM ⊗ ℂ — selects a subspace of “quantum states”. Real polarizations correspond to Lagrangian foliations (e.g., fibers of a Lagrangian fibration), while Kähler polarizations use a compatible complex structure J and take holomorphic sections.

The Bohr–Sommerfeld condition characterizes which leaves of a real polarization support non‑zero covariantly constant sections: a leaf F is Bohr–Sommerfeld if the holonomy of the connection around any loop in F is trivial. In the case of a torus fibration, this recovers the classical action‑angle quantization ∮ p dq = 2πℏ (n + ½). For Kähler polarizations, the quantum Hilbert space is the space of holomorphic sections H⁰(M, L), and its dimension is given by the Riemann–Roch formula, which in the symplectic setting becomes the index of a Spinc Dirac operator.

Quantization commutes with reduction (the “Guillemin–Sternberg conjecture”, proved by Meinrenken and by Tian–Zhang in the 1990s) states that the quantization of the symplectic quotient M//G equals the G‑invariant part of the quantization of M. This theorem links representation theory of compact Lie groups (via the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem) with symplectic geometry of coadjoint orbits, and it underlies the modern approach to the geometric Langlands program.

Lagrangian Intersection Theory and the Arnold Conjecture Beyond the Classical Setting

The Arnold conjecture asserts that a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism of a closed symplectic manifold has at least as many fixed points as the sum of Betti numbers of the manifold. Floer’s original proof (1988) used Lagrangian Floer homology for the diagonal Δ ⊂ M × M and its image under the graph of the diffeomorphism. Later developments extended the result to degenerate fixed points, to non‑contractible periodic orbits, and to symplectic manifolds with non‑trivial π₂ via the use of Novikov rings and virtual fundamental cycles.

In the setting of Lagrangian submanifolds L₀, L₁ with clean intersection, the Floer complex is generated by intersection points graded by the Maslov index. The differential counts pseudoholomorphic strips with boundary on L₀ ∪ L₁. When L₀ and L₁ are Hamiltonian isotopic, the Floer homology is isomorphic to the singular homology of L₀ (the Piunikhin–Salamon–Schwarz isomorphism). This provides a Morse‑theoretic interpretation of the Arnold conjecture: the number of intersection points is bounded below by the rank of H*(L₀).

Recent work by Abouzaid, Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono, and others has established the conjecture for all closed symplectic manifolds using Kuranishi structures or polyfold theory to handle transversality issues. Moreover, the “nearby Lagrangian” conjecture (proved by Abouzaid–Kragh for cotangent bundles) states that any exact Lagrangian in T*Q is Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero section, linking symplectic topology with smooth topology of the base.

Symplectic Field Theory and Contact Homology

Symplectic field theory (SFT) is a functorial invariant of contact manifolds and symplectic cobordisms built from counts of punctured pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectization ℝ × Y and in completed cobordisms. The algebraic structure is a differential graded algebra (A, ∂) generated by good Reeb orbits, with differential defined by counting rigid curves with positive punctures at the input orbit and negative punctures at output orbits. The homology of this complex is the contact homology HC*(Y).

For a contact manifold that bounds a Stein domain, the contact homology vanishes (Bourgeois–Oancea). This vanishing theorem provides obstructions to the existence of Stein fillings. Conversely, non‑vanishing contact homology distinguishes contact structures that are homotopic as plane fields but not contactomorphic. The theory also yields the “cylindrical contact homology” when one restricts to curves of genus zero with one positive puncture, which is often computable via combinatorial methods (e.g., for prequantization bundles).

SFT satisfies a gluing axiom: a symplectic cobordism (W, ω) from (Y₋, ξ₋) to (Y₊, ξ₊) induces a chain map Φ_W : HC*(Y₊) → HC*(Y₋). Composition of cobordisms corresponds to composition of maps, making SFT a (1+1)‑dimensional topological quantum field theory with additional algebraic structure. This functoriality underlies the proof of the Weinstein conjecture for many contact manifolds and the classification of tight contact structures on lens spaces.

  • Forgetting that contact homology is defined only for non‑degenerate contact forms; one must perturb the form or use a Morse–Bott version.

Wrapped Fukaya Categories and Symplectic Cohomology

The wrapped Fukaya category W(M) of a Liouville manifold M is an A∞-category whose objects are exact Lagrangian submanifolds that are cylindrical at infinity, and whose morphism spaces are generated by Hamiltonian chords from one Lagrangian to another. Unlike the compact Fukaya category, which uses only intersection points of compact Lagrangians, the wrapped version incorporates the dynamics of the Liouville flow at infinity: one "wraps" a Lagrangian by flowing it along the Liouville vector field for a long time, creating many transverse intersection points that correspond to Reeb chords on the contact boundary. The resulting Floer complex computes the wrapped Floer cohomology HW*(L0, L1), which is invariant under Hamiltonian isotopy and satisfies a version of the Piunikhin–Salamon–Schwarz isomorphism with symplectic cohomology SH*(M) when L0 = L1. Symplectic cohomology itself is defined as the direct limit of Hamiltonian Floer cohomology for Hamiltonians that grow quadratically at infinity; it fits into a long exact sequence H*+n(M) → SH*(M) → SH*+1(M, ∂M) → H*+n+1(M) relating it to the ordinary cohomology of M and the relative symplectic cohomology of the filling. A fundamental result of Abouzaid and Seidel states that for a Weinstein manifold, the wrapped Fukaya category is generated by the cocores of the critical handles, and SH*(M) is isomorphic to the Hochschild cohomology of W(M). This provides a powerful computational tool: for the cotangent bundle T*Q, SH*(T*Q) ≅ H*−n(ℒQ) (the homology of the free loop space), and the wrapped Fukaya category is equivalent to the category of constructible sheaves on Q via the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence. The A∞-structure maps count pseudoholomorphic polygons with boundary on a collection of wrapped Lagrangians, and the curvature term vanishes for exact Lagrangians, making W(M) a genuine A∞-category. The Viterbo functoriality theorem asserts that a Liouville embedding M N induces a restriction functor W(N) → W(M) and a transfer map SH*(N) → SH*(M), which is a ring homomorphism. This functoriality underlies many non‑existence results for exact Lagrangian embeddings, such as the fact that there is no exact Lagrangian embedding of a closed manifold into a subcritical Weinstein manifold because its symplectic cohomology vanishes.

Symplectic Topology of Cotangent Bundles and Generating Functions

The cotangent bundle T*Q of a closed manifold Q carries a canonical exact symplectic form ω = −dλ where λ is the Liouville one‑form p dq. Its symplectic topology is governed by the interplay between the zero section Q ⊂ T*Q and the fiberwise geometry. A Hamiltonian diffeomorphism φ of T*Q that is compactly supported can be described by a generating function S : Q × ℝN → ℝ, quadratic at infinity, such that the graph of φ in T*Q × T*Q is the image of the map (q, p) ↦ (q, p − ∂qS, q + ∂pS, p) after identifying the diagonal. The critical points of S correspond to fixed points of φ, and their Morse indices relate to the Conley–Zehnder indices of the corresponding Hamiltonian orbits. This generating function perspective, developed by Chaperon, Laudenbach–Sikorav, and Viterbo, leads to a proof of the Arnold conjecture for T*Q using Morse theory on the space of paths. The Viterbo isomorphism identifies the symplectic cohomology SH*(T*Q) with the singular homology of the free loop space ℒQ shifted by −n = −dim Q. Under this isomorphism, the pair‑of‑pants product on SH* corresponds to the Chas–Sullivan loop product on H*+n(ℒQ). The wrapped Fukaya category of T*Q is quasi‑equivalent to the derived category of constructible sheaves on Q (Nadler–Zaslow), and the microlocal theory of sheaves provides a combinatorial model for Lagrangian intersection theory. A key application is the nearby Lagrangian conjecture, proved by Abouzaid–Kragh for all cotangent bundles: any exact Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ T*Q that is Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero section at infinity is in fact Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero section globally. The proof uses the sheaf‑theoretic description to show that the microlocal rank of the sheaf associated to L is one, forcing L to be a section. Generating functions also give a handle on symplectic capacities: the Hofer–Zehnder capacity of a domain U ⊂ T*Q equals the minimal critical value of a generating function for the time‑one map of a Hamiltonian supported in U, and the Gromov width can be read off from the existence of a generating function with a single non‑degenerate critical point.

Worked Problem: Symplectic Cohomology of a Cotangent Bundle
Let Q = S1. Compute SH*(T*S1) as a graded ring and identify the unit element.
T*S1 ≅ S1 × ℝ with coordinates (θ, p) and symplectic form dθ ∧ dp. The free loop space ℒS1 has components indexed by the winding number k ∈ ℤ; each component is homotopy equivalent to S1. Thus H* (ℒS1) = ⊕k∈ℤ H* (S1) = ⊕k∈ℤ (ℤ in degree 0 ⊕ ℤ in degree 1). By the Viterbo isomorphism, SH* (T*S1) ≅ H*+1 (ℒS1) (since n = 1). Therefore SH* = ⊕k∈ℤ (ℤ in degree −1 ⊕ ℤ in degree 0). The unit in symplectic cohomology corresponds to the fundamental class of the constant loops component (k = 0) in degree 1 of ℒS1, which maps to degree 0 in SH*. The ring structure is the Chas–Sullivan loop product: the component k = 0 is the polynomial ring ℤ[u] with |u| = −1 (the generator of H1(S1)), and the other components are modules over it. Explicitly, SH* ≅ ℤ[u, t, t−1] with |u| = −1, |t| = 0, where tk corresponds to the component of winding number k. The unit is 1 = t0 in degree 0.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Viterbo Isomorphism Variants
When a question asks for the symplectic cohomology of a cotangent bundle, immediately write SH*(T*Q) ≅ H*−n(ℒQ). If Q is a sphere or a torus, compute the loop space homology using the Serre spectral sequence of the fibration ΩQ → ℒQ → Q. The unit is always the image of the fundamental class of the constant loops. For the ring structure, remember that the pair‑of‑pants product corresponds to the Chas–Sullivan product, which on the constant‑loop component is the Pontryagin product on H* (ΩQ) shifted by −n.

Equivariant Symplectic Geometry and the Kirwan Map

Let a compact Lie group G act on a symplectic manifold (M, ω) in a Hamiltonian fashion with moment map μ : M → 𝔤*. The equivariant cohomology H*G(M) is defined as the cohomology of the Borel construction MG = EG ×G M, where EG → BG is the universal G-bundle. The Kirwan map κ : H*G(M) → H*(M//G) is induced by the inclusion μ−1(0) M followed by the projection to the symplectic quotient M//G = μ−1(0)/G. Kirwan proved that κ is surjective for compact M, and its kernel is generated by the equivariant Euler classes of the normal bundles to the unstable strata in the Morse stratification of ‖μ‖2. This provides a complete presentation of the cohomology ring of the symplectic quotient in terms of the equivariant cohomology of M. For a Hamiltonian torus action, the Atiyah–Bott–Berline–Vergne localization formula expresses integrals of equivariant cohomology classes over M as sums over fixed point components, and the Kirwan map can be computed explicitly using the Jeffrey–Kirwan residue. The non‑abelian localization of Witten extends this to general G, expressing the symplectic volume of M//G as an integral over the maximal torus. In the context of geometric quantization, the "quantization commutes with reduction" theorem (Meinrenken, Tian–Zhang) states that the G-invariant part of the index of a G-equivariant Spinc Dirac operator on M equals the index of the descended operator on M//G. This is proved by showing that the Kirwan map intertwines the quantization maps. The equivariant Darboux theorem (Weinstein) asserts that near a point x ∈ μ−1(0) with stabilizer H, the action is locally modeled on T*(G ×H V) where V is the symplectic slice representation. This local model underlies the construction of the Kirwan–Ness stratification and the proof of the surjectivity of κ. For non‑compact M, such as cotangent bundles, the Kirwan map is replaced by the "quantum Kirwan map" in quantum cohomology, which counts pseudoholomorphic sections of the bundle MG → BG and relates the quantum cohomology of M//G to the G-equivariant quantum cohomology of M.

Symplectic Dynamics: The Conley–Zehnder Index and Iterated Periodic Orbits

The Conley–Zehnder index μCZ(γ) assigns an integer to a non‑degenerate periodic orbit γ of a Hamiltonian system on a symplectic manifold (M, ω) of dimension 2n. It is defined by trivializing the symplectic vector bundle γ*TM along γ and computing the Maslov index of the path of symplectic matrices given by the linearized flow. For a contractible orbit in a symplectically aspherical manifold (⟨ω, π2(M)⟩ = 0), the index is independent of the trivialization. The index satisfies the iteration formula μCZ(γk) = k μCZ(γ) + 2 ∑j=1k−1 ⌊jθ/π⌋ for a rotation angle θ in the normal form, and more generally the Bott iteration formula for degenerate orbits. The Conley–Zehnder index grades the Floer complex: the differential decreases the index by 1. For a non‑degenerate Hamiltonian on a closed symplectically aspherical manifold, the Floer homology is isomorphic to the singular homology shifted by −n, and the Conley–Zehnder indices of the generators correspond to the Morse indices of a C2-small Hamiltonian. The mean index Δ(γ) = limk→∞ μCZ(γk)/k measures the average rotation per period and appears in the resonance relations for Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms. A fundamental result of Long and others is the common index jump theorem: for any finite collection of symplectic paths, there exist infinitely many integers k such that all their k-th iterates have the same parity of Conley–Zehnder index. This theorem is a key ingredient in the proof of the Conley conjecture (every Hamiltonian diffeomorphism of a closed symplectically aspherical manifold has infinitely many periodic orbits) by Ginzburg–Gürel and others. The index also controls the existence of closed characteristics on hypersurfaces: the Hofer–Zehnder capacity of a domain U is the supremum of the maximum of Hamiltonians H with compact support in U such that all periodic orbits have Conley–Zehnder index ≥ n + 1. For a convex hypersurface in ℝ2n, the Ekeland–Hofer capacities are the critical values of the action functional restricted to loops with Conley–Zehnder index n + 2k − 1, and they form an increasing sequence c1 ≤ c2 ≤ … ≤ cn = cn+1 = … that determines the symplectic embedding obstructions.

Microlocal Sheaf Theory and the Guillermou–Kashiwara–Schapira Theorem

The microlocal theory of sheaves, developed by Kashiwara and Schapira, provides a sheaf‑theoretic model for symplectic topology. To a Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ T*Q one associates a constructible sheaf FL on Q whose microsupport SS(FL) equals L. The category of constructible sheaves Sh(Q) is equivalent to the wrapped Fukaya category W(T*Q) (Nadler–Zaslow), and this equivalence intertwines the convolution of sheaves with the wrapped Floer product. The Guillermou–Kashiwara–Schapira (GKS) theorem states that for a contact manifold Y = ∂M which is the boundary of a Liouville manifold M, the category of sheaves on Y with microsupport contained in the contact structure is equivalent to the partially wrapped Fukaya category of M stopped at Y. This provides a sheaf‑theoretic interpretation of symplectic field theory: the contact homology algebra is the endomorphism algebra of the constant sheaf in this category. The GKS equivalence is constructed by associating to a Legendrian submanifold Λ ⊂ Y a sheaf FΛ on Y × ℝ whose microsupport is the conormal lift of Λ. The differential in the contact homology complex corresponds to the morphisms between these sheaves. A key application is the computation of the wrapped Fukaya category of a Weinstein manifold by induction on the handle decomposition: each critical handle attachment corresponds to a gluing of sheaf categories. The theory also yields a proof of the nearby Lagrangian conjecture for cotangent bundles using the fact that the microlocal rank of a sheaf is locally constant on the Lagrangian. For a general Liouville manifold, the "sheaf quantization" of a Lagrangian cobordism gives a functor between the associated sheaf categories, realizing the Viterbo functoriality at the categorical level. The GKS theorem has been extended to the setting of microlocal perverse sheaves by Ganatra–Pardon–Shende, providing a fully faithful embedding of the wrapped Fukaya category into the category of microlocal perverse sheaves, which is often easier to compute because it is defined by local conditions.

  • Assuming the Kirwan map is always injective; it is surjective but its kernel is non‑trivial and described by the unstable strata of the moment map norm square.
  • Using the Conley–Zehnder index without specifying a trivialization of the determinant line bundle; the index is only well‑defined modulo the minimal Maslov number for non‑contractible orbits.
  • Treating the Viterbo isomorphism SH*(T*Q) ≅ H*−n(ℒQ) as a ring isomorphism without the degree shift; the pair‑of‑pants product corresponds to the Chas–Sullivan loop product shifted by −n.
  • Overlooking the stop in the partially wrapped Fukaya category; the GKS equivalence requires a choice of stop (a Legendrian submanifold at infinity), and different stops give different categories.
  • Confusing the mean index Δ(γ) with the Conley–Zehnder index μCZ(γ); the mean index is the asymptotic average, while the Conley–Zehnder index is integer‑valued and satisfies the iteration formula.
  • Applying the Atiyah–Bott localization formula to non‑isolated fixed point components without including the equivariant Euler class of the normal bundle in the denominator.

Quantum Cohomology and Gromov–Witten Invariants

Quantum cohomology deforms the ordinary cup product on the cohomology ring of a symplectic manifold by counting pseudoholomorphic spheres. For a closed symplectic manifold M with symplectic form ω, the quantum product * on H*(M; Λ) (where Λ is the Novikov ring) is defined by

α * β = ΣA∈H₂(M) (α ∪ β ∪ ev*[M̄0,3(M, A)]vir) qA

where ev evaluates the three marked points on the stable map, [M̄0,3(M, A)]vir is the virtual fundamental class of the moduli space of genus‑zero stable maps with three marked points in class A, and qA records the symplectic area ∫A ω. The associativity of * is equivalent to the WDVV equations, which are the genus‑zero topological recursion relations among Gromov–Witten invariants.

Gromov–Witten invariants GWg,n(A; α₁,…,αₙ) count (virtually) genus‑g stable maps with n marked points representing class A and constrained by cohomology classes αᵢ. They satisfy the divisor axiom, the fundamental class axiom, and the splitting axiom, which together allow recursive computation. For Fano varieties the quantum product is convergent and yields a semisimple Frobenius manifold structure; for Calabi–Yau threefolds the genus‑zero potential satisfies the holomorphic anomaly equations of Bershadsky–Cecotti–Ooguri–Vafa.

In symplectic topology, the quantum product controls the Hamiltonian Floer cohomology via the Piunikhin–Salamon–Schwarz isomorphism PH: QH*(M) → HF*(H). This isomorphism identifies the quantum product with the pair‑of‑pants product on Floer cohomology, and it respects the action filtration, giving rise to spectral invariants c(α, H). The spectral norm γ(H) = c([M], H) + c([pt], H̄) defines a bi‑invariant metric on the Hamiltonian group Ham(M, ω), known as the Hofer metric. The quantum product also enters the Seidel representation π₁(Ham(M, ω)) → QH*(M)×, which detects non‑trivial loops of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms.

Computations are often carried out using localization on toric manifolds or via the mirror theorem of Givental and Lian–Liu–Yau, which equates the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten potential of a toric Fano manifold with a hypergeometric series on the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model. For general symplectic manifolds, the virtual fundamental class is constructed using Kuranishi structures (Fukaya–Ono) or polyfolds (Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder), and the invariants are independent of the choice of almost complex structure.

Hamiltonian Group Actions and the Duistermaat–Heckman Formula

A Hamiltonian action of a compact Lie group G on a symplectic manifold (M, ω) is given by a moment map μ: M → 𝔤* satisfying d⟨μ, ξ⟩ = ιξM ω for every ξ ∈ 𝔤, where ξM is the vector field generated by ξ. The Duistermaat–Heckman theorem describes the pushforward of the Liouville measure ωⁿ/n! under μ. For a torus T acting with isolated fixed points, the measure on the moment polytope Δ = μ(M) is piecewise polynomial: on each chamber it equals a polynomial of degree n − dim T whose coefficients are determined by the weights of the isotropy representation at the fixed points.

Explicitly, if F is a fixed point component with normal weights α₁,…,αk (each a linear form on 𝔱), the contribution of F to the Duistermaat–Heckman density at a regular value ξ ∈ 𝔱* is

∏j=1k (⟨αj, ξ⟩)⁻¹

times the volume of F. Summing over all fixed components yields a rational function on 𝔱* whose residues give the symplectic volumes of the reduced spaces Mξ = μ⁻¹(ξ)/T. This formula is the finite‑dimensional avatar of the stationary phase approximation for the oscillatory integral ∫M ei⟨μ, ξ⟩ ωⁿ/n!.

The theorem extends to non‑abelian groups via the Kirwan map κ: H*G(M) → H*(M//G) and the Jeffrey–Kirwan residue formula, which expresses integrals over the symplectic quotient as iterated residues on the Lie algebra. For a Hamiltonian circle action, the Duistermaat–Heckman measure is a piecewise linear function on the moment interval, and its slope changes exactly at the critical values of the moment map. This linearity underlies the proof of the Atiyah–Guillemin–Sternberg convexity theorem: the image μ(M) is a convex polytope.

Applications include the computation of volumes of moduli spaces of flat connections (Witten), the localization of equivariant integrals in Gromov–Witten theory (Graber–Pandharipande), and the derivation of the Harish‑Chandra–Itzykson–Zuber integral formula for unitary matrix models. In the context of geometric quantization, the Duistermaat–Heckman measure gives the asymptotic distribution of eigenvalues of the quantum moment map as the Planck constant tends to zero.

Worked Example: Duistermaat–Heckman for ℂℙ²
Let T² act on ℂℙ² with moment map μ([z₀:z₁:z₂]) = (|z₁|², |z₂|²) / (|z₀|²+|z₁|²+|z₂|²). Compute the Duistermaat–Heckman density on the moment triangle Δ = { (x,y) | x≥0, y≥0, x+y ≤ 1 }.
The fixed points are p₀=[1:0:0], p₁=[0:1:0], p₂=[0:0:1] with moment images (0,0), (1,0), (0,1). At p₀ the weights are α₁=(1,0), α₂=(0,1); at p₁ they are (-1,0), (1,-1); at p₂ they are (0,-1), (-1,1). The contribution of each fixed point to the density at a regular (x,y) is the inverse product of the weight pairings. Summing the three contributions gives a constant density 1 on the interior of Δ. Hence the Duistermaat–Heckman measure is the Lebesgue measure on the triangle, and the symplectic volume of ℂℙ² is Area(Δ) = 1/2, matching ∫ ω²/2 = 1/2.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the Duistermaat–Heckman measure of a Hamiltonian torus action, first list the fixed points and their weight data. Write the rational function contribution of each fixed point, then sum them. The result is always a piecewise polynomial; on a chamber where no weight pairing vanishes, the sum simplifies to a polynomial. Remember that the total volume of the manifold equals the integral of the density over the moment polytope.

Symplectic Reduction at Singular Levels and Stratified Spaces

When the moment map μ: M → 𝔤* is not transverse to a value ξ ∈ 𝔤*, the level set μ⁻¹(ξ) may contain points with non‑trivial stabilizers, and the quotient M//ξ G = μ⁻¹(ξ)/G is not a manifold but a stratified symplectic space. The Sjamaar–Lerman theorem states that M//ξ G decomposes into a finite union of symplectic manifolds (strata) indexed by conjugacy classes of isotropy subgroups. Each stratum is the symplectic reduction of a locally closed submanifold M(H) consisting of points whose stabilizer is conjugate to H.

The stratification is compatible with the Poisson structure: the algebra of invariant functions C∞(M)G restricts to a Poisson algebra on each stratum, and the inclusion of a stratum into the closure is a Poisson map. The Kirwan map κ: H*G(M) → H*(M//ξ G) extends to a map on intersection cohomology, and the decomposition theorem of Beĭlinson–Bernstein–Deligne–Gabber yields a direct sum decomposition of the pushforward of the constant sheaf along the quotient map.

For a Hamiltonian circle action with isolated fixed points, the singular reduced spaces at critical values are weighted projective spaces or orbifolds. Their symplectic volumes can be computed by the Duistermaat–Heckman formula applied to the regular chambers and taking limits. The jump in the volume across a wall is given by the contribution of the fixed point component that crosses the wall, which is a rational function of the weights.

In gauge theory, the moduli space of flat connections on a Riemann surface with prescribed holonomy at marked points is a singular symplectic quotient. Its stratification by stabilizer type corresponds to the Harder–Narasimhan stratification of the associated Higgs bundle moduli space. The intersection cohomology of these spaces carries a pure Hodge structure, and the Kirwan surjectivity theorem holds for intersection cohomology with rational coefficients.

Homological Mirror Symmetry and the Fukaya Category

Kontsevich’s homological mirror symmetry conjecture asserts an equivalence of triangulated categories DᵇFuk(M) ≅ DᵇCoh(M̌) between the derived Fukaya category of a Calabi–Yau symplectic manifold M and the derived category of coherent sheaves on its mirror complex manifold M̌. The Fukaya category Fuk(M) has objects given by graded, spin Lagrangian submanifolds L ⊂ M equipped with a local system, and morphism spaces CF*(L₀, L₁) generated by intersection points. The A∞‑structure is defined by counts of pseudoholomorphic polygons with boundary on the Lagrangians.

On the B‑side, DᵇCoh(M̌) is generated by structure sheaves of subvarieties and their twists. The equivalence matches the open‑closed string map HH*(Fuk(M)) → QH*(M) with the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism HH*(DᵇCoh(M̌)) ≅ ⊕p,q Hq(M̌, ∧p TM̌). Under mirror symmetry, the quantum product on QH*(M) corresponds to the cup product on H*(M̌, ∧* TM̌) deformed by the Kodaira–Spencer class.

The conjecture has been proved for many toric Fano varieties (Abouzaid, Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono), for the quartic K3 surface (Seidel), and for certain Landau–Ginzburg models (Orlov, Seidel). The proof typically proceeds by constructing a directed Fukaya category of Lagrangian thimbles for a Lefschetz fibration, showing it is generated by a collection of vanishing cycles, and identifying the endomorphism algebra of this collection with the derived endomorphism algebra of a tilting object on the mirror.

Applications include the computation of Gromov–Witten invariants via period integrals on the mirror, the derivation of the Crepant Resolution Conjecture for orbifold Gromov–Witten theory, and the formulation of the Strominger–Yau–Zaslow picture where mirror symmetry is realized as T‑duality on special Lagrangian torus fibrations. The categorical framework also provides a natural home for the Seidel–Thomas twist autoequivalences, which correspond to spherical twists on the B‑side.

Symplectic Topology of Four‑Manifolds and Seiberg–Witten Theory

In dimension four, symplectic topology interacts deeply with gauge theory. Taubes’s theorem states that for a closed symplectic four‑manifold (M, ω), the Seiberg–Witten invariant SWM(κ) of the canonical spinc structure κ = c₁(TM, J) equals ±1, and all other basic classes satisfy SWM(κ') = 0 unless κ' = κ + 2 PD(A) for some A ∈ H₂(M) with A·A ≥ 0 and ω(A) > 0. This identifies the symplectic cone with a chamber in the space of cohomology classes where the Seiberg–Witten invariant is non‑zero.

The Gromov–Taubes invariant GrM(A) counts pseudoholomorphic curves in class A (with appropriate multiplicities) and coincides with the Seiberg–Witten invariant of the spinc structure κ + 2 PD(A). This equality, proved by Taubes using the adiabatic limit of the Seiberg–Witten equations on the symplectization, provides a bridge between symplectic geometry and four‑manifold topology.

Applications include the proof that a simply‑connected symplectic four‑manifold with b₂⁺ = 1 is diffeomorphic to a blow‑up of ℂℙ² or a rational ruled surface (Liu, Ohta–Ono), and the classification of symplectic structures on ℂℙ² # n ℂℙ²̄ for n ≤ 9 via the symplectic cone and the wall‑crossing formula for Seiberg–Witten invariants. The adjunction inequality for symplectically embedded surfaces follows from the Seiberg–Witten basic class inequality.

Recent developments include the definition of embedded contact homology (ECH) for three‑manifolds and its isomorphism with Seiberg–Witten Floer homology (Kutluhan–Lee–Taubes, Colin–Ghiggini–Honda). The ECH capacities give sharp symplectic embedding obstructions for four‑dimensional domains, and the ECH index inequality recovers the adjunction genus bound for symplectic surfaces.

Symplectic Topology of Stein Manifolds and the h‑Principle

A Stein manifold is a complex manifold (X, J) admitting a proper holomorphic embedding into ℂ^N for some N. Equivalently, X carries a strictly plurisubharmonic exhaustion function φ: X → ℝ. The Levi form ω_φ = -dd^c φ is a Kähler form on X, making (X, ω_φ) a symplectic manifold. Eliashberg’s theorem (1990) states that for dim_ℝ X ≥ 6, an almost complex structure J on an open manifold X is homotopic to a Stein structure if and only if X admits an exhausting Morse function with critical points of index at most n = dim_ℂ X. This is an h‑principle: the existence of a Stein structure is reduced to a purely topological condition. In dimension four, the h‑principle fails; there are exotic ℝ4’s that admit no Stein structure. The flexibility of Stein manifolds is reflected in the fact that any two Stein structures on the same open manifold are isotopic through Stein structures if they are homotopic as almost complex structures (Eliashberg, 1990). The h‑principle also applies to Lagrangian embeddings: any formal Lagrangian immersion of an open n‑manifold into a Stein manifold of dimension 2n is homotopic to a genuine Lagrangian embedding (Gromov, 1986). This underlies the construction of flexible Weinstein manifolds, where all critical handles are attached along loose Legendrian spheres. The distinction between flexible and non‑flexible Weinstein manifolds, introduced by Cieliebak–Eliashberg (2012), is a central theme in modern symplectic topology. Flexible Weinstein manifolds satisfy an h‑principle for many symplectic invariants: their symplectic homology vanishes, and their wrapped Fukaya category is trivial. Non‑flexible examples, such as cotangent bundles T^*Q or plumbings according to Dynkin diagrams, carry rich symplectic topology detected by symplectic homology, contact homology, and the Fukaya category.

Symplectic Homology and Rabinowitz Floer Homology

Symplectic homology SH_*(W) is a Floer‑theoretic invariant of a Liouville domain (W, λ) — a compact exact symplectic manifold with boundary ∂W = Y such that λ|_Y is a contact form. It is defined as the direct limit of Hamiltonian Floer homologies HF_*(H_k) for a cofinal family of Hamiltonians H_k: Ŵ → ℝ that are linear of slope k on the cylindrical end Ŵ W ≅ Y × [1, ∞). The resulting ℤ‑graded vector space (over a Novikov field) carries a pair‑of‑pants product making it a unital ring, and a BV operator Δ of degree -1 satisfying the Batalin–Vilkovisky identity. Viterbo’s isomorphism identifies SH_*(T^*Q) with the homology of the free loop space H*+n(ΛQ) for a closed manifold Q. For a general Liouville domain, there is a long exact sequence ⋯ → Hn-*(W, ∂W) → SH_*(W) → SH_*>0(W) → ⋯ where SH_*>0 is the positive symplectic homology generated by orbits with positive action. Rabinowitz Floer homology RFH_*(Y) is defined for a contact manifold (Y, ξ = ker λ) using the Rabinowitz action functional A_η(x, η) = ∫ x^*λ - η ∫01 H(x(t)) dt on the space of loops in the symplectization ℝ × Y. It fits into an exact triangle with symplectic homology and contact homology. A key result of Cieliebak–Frauenfelder–Oancea (2010) shows that RFH_*(Y) vanishes if Y bounds a flexible Weinstein domain, providing an obstruction to flexible fillings. The spectral invariants c(α, H) derived from the filtration on the Floer complex give rise to the spectral norm γ(H) = c(1, H) + c(1, H̄) on the universal cover of the Hamiltonian diffeomorphism group, which is a bi‑invariant metric related to Hofer’s metric. The growth rate of spectral invariants under iteration detects the entropy of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms (Polterovich, 2014).

Worked computation
Compute the symplectic homology of the standard Liouville domain W = D^*S1, the unit disk cotangent bundle of the circle, and explain why it vanishes.
The domain W = D^*S1 is symplectomorphic to the annulus A = { (r, θ) ∈ ℝ2 / ℤ × ℝ / 2πℤ | 1 ≤ r ≤ 2 } with Liouville form λ = r dθ. Its completion is the cylinder ℝ × S1 with symplectic form d(r dθ). The Reeb flow on ∂W = S1 × {1} ∪ S1 × {2} is periodic with period 2π. The Hamiltonian H(r) = h(r) linear on the ends has 1‑periodic orbits only at critical points of h, i.e., at constant orbits in the interior. Since W is exact and the Conley–Zehnder indices of the constant orbits are all zero, the Floer complex for small slopes is generated by the Morse complex of a C2‑small Morse function on W. The differential counts Floer cylinders which, for exact symplectic manifolds, correspond to Morse trajectories. Thus HF_*(H) ≅ H*+1(W) ≅ H*+1(D2) which is ℤ in degree -1 and 0 otherwise. Taking the direct limit over increasing slopes, the continuation maps are isomorphisms because no new orbits appear (the Reeb periods are 2π, so orbits with action > 2π have slope > 1 and are not created until the slope crosses an integer). However, the positive symplectic homology SH_*>0(W) is generated by orbits on the cylindrical end with positive action. For the annulus, the Reeb orbits on the boundary have action 2πk for k ∈ ℕ. The Conley–Zehnder index of the k‑fold cover is 2k - 1. The differential in the positive complex vanishes for index reasons, so SH_*>0(W) ≅ ⊕k≥1 ℤ in odd degrees. The long exact sequence ⋯ → H1-*(W, ∂W) → SH_*(W) → SH_*>0(W) → ⋯ with H1-*(W, ∂W) ≅ H*-1(W) ≅ ℤ in degree 0 shows that the map SH_*(W) → SH_*>0(W) is an isomorphism in positive degrees and the unit in SH0(W) maps to zero. But the unit in SH_*(W) comes from the fundamental class of W, which lies in degree 0. The exact sequence forces SH_*(W) = 0 because the unit is killed by the map to SH_*>0 and there are no other generators. Alternatively, D^*S1 is a flexible Weinstein domain (it is a 2‑dimensional Weinstein domain, and all 2‑dimensional Weinstein domains are flexible), so by the Cieliebak–Eliashberg theorem its symplectic homology vanishes.

For non‑flexible domains like cotangent bundles, use Viterbo’s isomorphism SH_*(T^*Q) ≅ H*+n(ΛQ). For plumbings, use the surgery formula of Bourgeois–Ekholm–Eliashberg or the exact sequence relating SH_* of the plumbing to SH_* of the pieces. Remember that the grading in SH_* is ℤ‑graded only after a choice of trivialization of the canonical bundle; otherwise it is ℤ/2‑graded. The BV operator Δ corresponds to the S1‑action on the free loop space under Viterbo’s isomorphism.

Symplectic Topology of Weinstein Manifolds and the Flexible/Non‑flexible Dichotomy

A Weinstein manifold is a triple (W, λ, φ) where W is a non‑compact manifold, λ is a Liouville form (dλ is symplectic), and φ: W → ℝ is an exhausting Morse function such that the Liouville vector field Z (defined by ι_Z dλ = λ) is gradient‑like for φ. The critical points of φ correspond to handles attached along isotropic spheres in the contact boundary. A Weinstein domain is a compact Weinstein manifold with boundary. The Weinstein homotopy class of (λ, φ) is determined up to isotopy by the homotopy class of the almost complex structure and the handle attachment data. Cieliebak and Eliashberg (2012) proved that a Weinstein manifold is flexible if all its critical handles of index n = ½ dim W are attached along loose Legendrian spheres. Loose Legendrians are those that admit a loose chart — a Darboux ball where the Legendrian is standard and the complement has a certain overtwisted disk. The h‑principle for loose Legendrians (Murphy, 2012) implies that any formal Legendrian isotopy class contains a loose representative, and any two loose Legendrians that are formally isotopic are genuinely isotopic. Consequently, flexible Weinstein manifolds satisfy an h‑principle: their symplectic topology is determined entirely by their homotopy type. In particular, SH_*(W) = 0, the wrapped Fukaya category W(W) is trivial, and the contact homology of the boundary vanishes. Non‑flexible Weinstein manifolds include cotangent bundles T^*Q (where the zero section is a non‑loose Lagrangian), plumbings of T^*S^n according to Dynkin diagrams (ADE singularities), and Milnor fibers of isolated hypersurface singularities. The distinction is sharp: a Weinstein manifold is either flexible or non‑flexible, and there is no intermediate regime. The flexibility criterion can be checked combinatorially from the Legendrian attaching spheres: a Legendrian sphere Λ ⊂ (S2n-1, ξ_std) is loose iff its Thurston–Bennequin invariant tb(Λ) < n - 2 (for n ≥ 3) or it admits a loose chart in dimension 3. The classification of flexible Weinstein structures on a given smooth manifold reduces to the classification of almost complex structures up to homotopy, which is a problem in algebraic topology. For non‑flexible manifolds, the symplectic invariants (SH_*, W(W), contact homology) provide obstructions to flexibility and distinguish different Weinstein structures on the same smooth manifold. For example, the A_n Milnor fiber and the cotangent bundle T^*S^n are both Weinstein fillings of the standard contact sphere, but they are not Weinstein homotopic because their symplectic homologies differ.

Symplectic Topology of Hamiltonian Diffeomorphism Groups and Hofer Geometry

The group Ham(M, ω) of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms of a closed symplectic manifold is an infinite‑dimensional Lie group whose Lie algebra is the space C^∞(M)/ℝ of normalized Hamiltonians with the Poisson bracket. Hofer’s metric (1990) defines a bi‑invariant Finsler metric on Ham(M, ω) by d_H(φ, ψ) = inf { ∫01 (max H_t - min H_t) dt | φ_H1 = φ-1ψ }. This metric is non‑degenerate (Lalonde–McDuff, 1995) and has infinite diameter for many manifolds (Polterovich, 1998; Ostrover, 2006). The geometry of (Ham, d_H) is deeply intertwined with symplectic topology. The fragmentation norm ‖φ‖_U = inf { ∑ d_H(φ_i, id) | φ = φ_k ⋯ φ1, supp φ_i ⊂ U } for an open cover {U_i} gives a quasi‑morphism on Ham. Entov–Polterovich (2006) constructed Calabi quasi‑morphisms on Ham(M, ω) for certain symplectic manifolds (e.g., ℂℙ^n, monotone symplectic manifolds with quantum homology having a field summand) using spectral invariants c(α, H) from Hamiltonian Floer homology. A Calabi quasi‑morphism μ: Ham → ℝ satisfies μ(φψ) = μ(φ) + μ(ψ) + O(1) and μ(φ) = Cal(φ) for φ supported in a displaceable set. The existence of a Calabi quasi‑morphism implies that Ham has infinite diameter and that the fragmentation norm is unbounded. The spectral norm γ(H) = c(1, H) + c(1, H̄) is a bi‑invariant norm on the universal cover Ham̃, and its descent to Ham gives a lower bound for Hofer’s metric. The asymptotic spectral norm γ_∞(φ) = limk→∞ γ(φ^k)/k defines a homogeneous quasi‑morphism. For surface area‑preserving diffeomorphisms, the Calabi homomorphism extends to a homogeneous quasi‑morphism on the whole group (Entov–Polterovich, 2003). The commutator length cl(φ) = min { k | φ = ∏i=1^k [a_i, b_i] } is related to the stable commutator length scl(φ) = lim cl(φ^k)/k. Bavard’s duality identifies scl with the supremum of homogeneous quasi‑morphisms. The Hofer metric is also related to the geometry of the space of compatible almost complex structures J(M, ω) with the L2 metric; the geodesics in Ham correspond to solutions of the Hamiltonian equation, while geodesics in J correspond to harmonic maps. The diameter of Ham with respect to Hofer’s metric is infinite for all closed symplectic manifolds (Ostrover, 2006 for ℂℙ^n; general case by Polterovich–Shelukhin, 2016 using quantum homology). The growth of the volume of balls in Ham is exponential (Polterovich, 2001). The displacement energy e(U) = inf { d_H(φ, id) | φ(U) ∩ U = ∅ } of an open set U is a fundamental symplectic capacity. For a displaceable set, e(U) > 0, and the spectral invariants satisfy c(α, H) ≤ e(supp H) for α ≠ 1.

Symplectic Topology of Lefschetz Fibrations and the Global Torelli Theorem

A Lefschetz fibration is a proper map π: E → B from a 2n‑dimensional symplectic manifold E to a Riemann surface B such that all critical points are non‑degenerate and lie in distinct fibers, and near each critical point π is locally modeled on (z1, …, z_n) ↦ z12 + ⋯ + z_n2. The regular fiber F is a symplectic manifold with boundary (if B has boundary) or closed (if B is closed). The monodromy of the fibration is a factorization of the boundary Dehn twist (or identity for closed base) into a product of Dehn twists along vanishing cycles in the mapping class group of F. Donaldson (1999) proved that every symplectic manifold admits a Lefschetz pencil (after blowing up), and hence a Lefschetz fibration. The symplectic topology of E is encoded in the Fukaya category of the fiber and the monodromy action. Seidel’s long exact sequence relates the Fukaya category of the total space to the Fukaya category of the fiber and the monodromy autoequivalence. For a Lefschetz fibration over the disk with fiber F, the wrapped Fukaya category W(E) is equivalent to the category of modules over the directed Fukaya category of the vanishing cycles. The global Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces (Burns–Rapoport, 1975; Looijenga–Peters, 1981) states that the period map from the moduli space of marked K3 surfaces to the period domain is a local isomorphism and its image is the complement of a hyperplane arrangement. In symplectic terms, a K3 surface with a symplectic form ω has a period point [ω] ∈ H2(K3, ℝ) with ω2 > 0. The symplectic cone is the set of classes representable by symplectic forms. For a K3 surface, the symplectic cone is one of the two components of { α ∈ H2 | α2 > 0 } minus the walls orthogonal to (-2)‑classes. The mapping class group acts on H2 by isometries preserving the intersection form. The global Torelli theorem implies that two symplectic forms on a K3 surface are isotopic if and only if they are in the same orbit of the mapping class group. For Lefschetz fibrations on K3 surfaces, the monodromy factorization determines the symplectic structure up to isotopy. The classification of Lefschetz fibrations on K3 surfaces with fiber genus g is related to the factorization of the identity in the mapping class group of Σ_g into Dehn twists. The minimal genus of a Lefschetz fibration on a K3 surface is 2 (Matsumoto, 1996). The total space of a Lefschetz fibration over S2 with fiber Σ_g has Euler characteristic χ(E) = 4(g-1) + number of critical points. For a K3 surface, χ = 24, so the number of critical points is 24 - 4(g-1). The monodromy representation gives a homomorphism π1(B crit) → Map(Σ_g) whose image is generated by Dehn twists. The symplectic topology of the total space is reflected in the algebraic structure of the Fukaya category of the fiber and the action of the monodromy. The Seidel–Thomas twist autoequivalences of the Fukaya category correspond to spherical twists on the mirror side under homological mirror symmetry.

Symplectic Rigidity and the Eliashberg–Gromov Theorem

The Eliashberg–Gromov rigidity theorem (1990) asserts that a symplectic manifold which is C⁰‑close to a standard symplectic form on ℝ²ⁿ must itself be symplectomorphic to the standard one. The proof combines Gromov’s compactness for pseudoholomorphic curves with Eliashberg’s h‑principle for overtwisted contact structures. A key step is the construction of a foliation by pseudoholomorphic disks whose boundaries lie on a Lagrangian torus; the existence of such a foliation forces the symplectic form to be standard after a suitable diffeomorphism. This rigidity fails in the C¹ topology, as shown by the existence of exotic symplectic structures on ℝ⁴ discovered by Gromov (1985) and later refined by McDuff (1991). The theorem has profound consequences for symplectic embedding problems: any symplectic embedding of a ball into a cylinder that is C⁰‑close to the inclusion must be the inclusion itself, which yields sharp capacity bounds for the Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder capacities.

In the language of symplectic capacities, the theorem implies that the Gromov width is lower semicontinuous with respect to C⁰ convergence of symplectic forms. This semicontinuity is used to prove that the symplectic capacity of a domain is invariant under C⁰‑small perturbations of the symplectic structure, a fact that underlies the stability of Hofer’s geometry. The proof also introduces the notion of a “tame” almost complex structure, which is essential for the compactness arguments in Floer theory. Later work by Lalonde–McDuff (1995) extended the rigidity result to certain symplectic manifolds with boundary, showing that the same C⁰‑rigidity holds for Liouville domains whose contact boundary is tight.

Applications of the Eliashberg–Gromov theorem appear in the classification of symplectic structures on cotangent bundles. For example, any symplectic form on T*ℝⁿ that is C⁰‑close to the canonical form is symplectomorphic to it, which rules out exotic symplectic structures on standard cotangent bundles in the C⁰ category. This rigidity contrasts sharply with the flexibility of symplectic forms in the C∞ category, where Moser’s stability theorem provides a deformation argument but does not control the C⁰ distance.

Symplectic Topology of Contact Manifolds and the Weinstein Conjecture

The Weinstein conjecture, proved in dimension three by Taubes (2007) using Seiberg–Witten theory and in all dimensions by Viterbo (1987) for contact type hypersurfaces in ℝ²ⁿ, states that every closed contact manifold carries a closed Reeb orbit. The proof for contact type hypersurfaces uses the existence of a pseudoholomorphic plane in the symplectization, which projects to a Reeb orbit on the hypersurface. In higher dimensions, the conjecture remains open for general contact manifolds, but significant progress has been made using contact homology and symplectic field theory. The contact homology algebra, introduced by Eliashberg–Givental–Hofer (2000), encodes Reeb dynamics and provides obstructions to the existence of exact fillings.

A central tool is the cylindrical contact homology HC_*(Y,ξ), defined for non‑degenerate contact forms. When the contact manifold bounds a strong symplectic filling (W,ω), there is a spectral sequence from HC_*(Y,ξ) to the symplectic homology SH_*(W). The vanishing of SH_*(W) for flexible fillings implies that HC_*(Y,ξ) must also vanish, which forces the existence of Reeb orbits with prescribed Conley–Zehnder indices. This argument yields the Weinstein conjecture for all contact manifolds that admit a flexible filling, a class that includes all overtwisted contact structures by the work of Murphy (2012).

The conjecture also interacts with the notion of orderability of contact manifolds. A contact manifold is orderable if its universal cover admits a positive contactomorphism that is not the identity. Eliashberg–Kim–Polterovich (2006) showed that orderability implies the existence of a contractible Reeb orbit, providing a dynamical proof of the conjecture for orderable contact manifolds. The orderability condition is satisfied by many contact manifolds arising as boundaries of Liouville domains with vanishing symplectic homology, linking the dynamical question to algebraic invariants of the filling.

Worked Example: Computing Cylindrical Contact Homology for the Standard Contact Sphere
Let (S²ⁿ⁻¹, ξ_std) be the standard contact sphere with the contact form α = ½ Σ (x_i dy_i − y_i dx_i). Compute the cylindrical contact homology HC_*(S²ⁿ⁻¹, ξ_std) and deduce the existence of a closed Reeb orbit.
The Reeb vector field of α generates the Hopf fibration S¹ → S²ⁿ⁻¹ → ℂPⁿ⁻¹. All Reeb orbits are periodic with period 2π and are non‑degenerate after a small perturbation. The Conley–Zehnder index of the k‑fold cover of a simple orbit is μ_CZ = 2k(n−1) + n−2. The chain complex for cylindrical contact homology is generated by good orbits; bad orbits (even covers when n is even) are excluded. The differential counts rigid pseudoholomorphic cylinders in the symplectization ℝ × S²ⁿ⁻¹. By the work of Bourgeois–Oancea (2009), the differential vanishes for the standard sphere, so HC_* is the free ℚ‑module generated by the good orbits. In particular, HC_* is non‑zero in infinitely many degrees, which forces the existence of Reeb orbits of arbitrarily high index. This non‑vanishing is a direct algebraic proof of the Weinstein conjecture for (S²ⁿ⁻¹, ξ_std).
Exam Strategy: Recognising Flexible vs. Non‑flexible Fillings
When a question asks whether a given contact manifold satisfies the Weinstein conjecture, first check if it bounds a flexible Liouville domain. Flexible fillings have vanishing symplectic homology, which forces cylindrical contact homology to vanish unless Reeb orbits exist. Remember that all overtwisted contact structures are flexible (Murphy 2012), so the conjecture holds automatically for them. For tight contact structures, look for orderability or known exact fillings with non‑zero symplectic homology.

Symplectic Dynamics and the Arnold Conjecture for Degenerate Hamiltonians

The classical Arnold conjecture asserts that a non‑degenerate Hamiltonian diffeomorphism on a closed symplectic manifold has at least as many fixed points as the sum of Betti numbers. For degenerate Hamiltonians, the conjecture is formulated using the Floer homology of a Hamiltonian that is C²‑small and Morse–Bott. The Morse–Bott version, developed by Pozniak (1999) and later refined by Frauenfelder–Schlenk (2005), replaces non‑degenerate critical points by non‑degenerate critical manifolds. The resulting Floer complex is generated by the critical manifolds, and the differential counts cascades of gradient flow lines and pseudoholomorphic strips. This approach yields a lower bound on the number of fixed points equal to the sum of Betti numbers of the critical manifolds, which in the case of a Hamiltonian with a single degenerate fixed point set recovers the classical bound.

A major technical tool is the use of a “split” Hamiltonian H = H₁ + H₂ where H₁ is a Morse function on the fixed point set and H₂ is a small perturbation making the whole Hamiltonian non‑degenerate. The continuation maps between the Morse–Bott and non‑degenerate Floer homologies are chain homotopy equivalences, preserving the rank of the homology. This invariance under perturbations is essential for proving that the lower bound is independent of the choice of perturbation. The proof also requires a careful analysis of the compactness of the moduli spaces of cascades, which involves a combination of Gromov compactness for pseudoholomorphic curves and Morse compactness for gradient flow lines.

Applications of the degenerate Arnold conjecture appear in the study of Hamiltonian circle actions. For a Hamiltonian S¹‑action with moment map μ, the fixed point set is a union of symplectic submanifolds. The Morse–Bott Floer homology of the time‑1 map of the action computes the equivariant cohomology of the fixed point set, and the Arnold lower bound translates into the statement that the sum of Betti numbers of the fixed components is at most the sum of Betti numbers of the whole manifold. This result, due to McDuff–Tolman (2006), provides a symplectic proof of the Atiyah–Bott–Berline–Vergne localization formula for Hamiltonian torus actions.

Symplectic Geometry of Moduli Spaces and the Atiyah–Bott–Goldman Form

The moduli space M_g of flat G‑connections on a closed Riemann surface Σ_g carries a natural symplectic structure introduced by Atiyah and Bott (1983) and independently by Goldman (1984). The tangent space at a flat connection A is H¹(Σ_g, ad A), and the symplectic form is given by the cup product paired with the Killing form on the Lie algebra: ω(α,β) = ∫_Σ_g ⟨α ∧ β⟩. This form is closed and non‑degenerate on the smooth part of the moduli space, making it a symplectic orbifold. The mapping class group of Σ_g acts by symplectomorphisms, and the Goldman bracket on the space of functions given by traces of holonomies endows the algebra of observables with a Poisson structure.

When G = SU(2), the moduli space is a compact symplectic manifold of real dimension 6g−6. Its symplectic volume can be computed by the Verlinde formula, which expresses the volume as a sum over representations of the quantum group at a root of unity. Witten (1991) showed that this volume equals the partition function of two‑dimensional Yang–Mills theory, providing a physical interpretation. The symplectic form also appears in the geometric quantization of the moduli space: the prequantum line bundle is the determinant line bundle of the ∂̄‑operator twisted by the adjoint bundle, and its curvature is precisely ω. The space of holomorphic sections of this line bundle at level k is the space of conformal blocks of the WZW model, linking symplectic geometry to conformal field theory.

Recent work by Teleman (2000) and Woodward (2014) has extended the Atiyah–Bott construction to parabolic bundles and to moduli spaces with marked points, where the symplectic form acquires additional terms coming from the residues at the punctures. These extended moduli spaces are symplectomorphic to the character varieties of surface groups with prescribed conjugacy classes at the punctures. The symplectic reduction of the space of all connections by the gauge group yields the same symplectic form, and the moment map for the gauge action is the curvature. This Hamiltonian reduction picture underlies the construction of the Fukaya category of the moduli space via Lagrangian correspondences, a program initiated by Fukaya (1996) and developed by Abouzaid–Manolescu (2019).

Symplectic Topology of Hamiltonian Torus Actions and the GKM Theory

Goresky–Kottwitz–MacPherson (GKM) theory (1998) provides a combinatorial description of the equivariant cohomology of a symplectic manifold (M,ω) equipped with a Hamiltonian torus action T^k → Ham(M,ω) with isolated fixed points and one‑dimensional orbits. The fixed point set MT^k is a finite set of points, and the one‑skeleton M1 is a union of invariant two‑spheres connecting the fixed points. The GKM graph has vertices corresponding to fixed points and edges labeled by the weights of the T^k‑action on the normal bundle of the corresponding sphere. The equivariant cohomology H_T^*(M) is isomorphic to the subring of ⊕p∈MT^k H_T^*(pt) consisting of tuples (f_p) that satisfy the congruence f_p ≡ f_q (mod α_e) for each edge e connecting p and q, where α_e is the weight of the T^k‑action on the sphere.

This description allows the computation of ordinary cohomology via the localization theorem: the inclusion of the fixed point set induces an injection H^*(M) → H^*(MT^k) after inverting the equivariant parameters. The image is precisely the set of tuples satisfying the GKM congruences with the equivariant parameters set to zero. In particular, the Betti numbers of M can be read off from the graph. For example, the complex projective space ℂPⁿ with the standard torus action has a GKM graph that is a complete graph on n+1 vertices with weights given by the differences of the standard characters; the resulting cohomology ring is ℤ[x]/(xn+1) as expected.

GKM theory also applies to the study of Hamiltonian torus actions on symplectic manifolds with non‑isolated fixed points, provided the fixed components are symplectic submanifolds and the normal bundles split into weight spaces. In this setting, the GKM graph is replaced by a “GKM sheaf” over the fixed components, and the equivariant cohomology is computed as the global sections of this sheaf. This generalization, due to Guillemin–Zara (2001), has been used to compute the cohomology of hypertoric varieties and of the moduli spaces of flat connections with torus actions coming from the mapping class group. The combinatorial nature of GKM theory makes it a powerful tool for proving formality results: many GKM manifolds are formal in the sense of rational homotopy theory, which implies that their rational homotopy type is determined by their cohomology ring.

Symplectic Dynamics: The Conley Conjecture and its Floer‑Theoretic Proof

The Conley conjecture, formulated by Charles Conley in 1978, asserts that every Hamiltonian diffeomorphism of a closed symplectically aspherical manifold possesses infinitely many distinct periodic points. A manifold (M,ω) is symplectically aspherical when the symplectic form vanishes on all spherical homology classes, i.e. ω|π₂(M)=0 and c₁|π₂(M)=0. The conjecture remained open for three decades until a series of breakthroughs by Ginzburg, Gürel, and others established it using Floer homology and spectral invariants.

The first complete proof for the non‑degenerate case appeared in 2009 (Ginzburg–Gürel, Ann. of Math.). The argument proceeds by contradiction: assuming finitely many periodic points, one constructs a sequence of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms whose Floer homology groups stabilize, forcing the existence of a non‑trivial spectral invariant that contradicts the asphericity condition. The key technical tool is the action‑filtered Floer homology HF⁽ᵃ⁾(H) and the associated spectral invariants c(α,H) defined for quantum cohomology classes α∈QH*(M). The proof shows that if the number of periodic points were finite, the spectral invariants would satisfy a polynomial growth bound incompatible with the Calabi property of the Hamiltonian flow.

Subsequent work extended the result to degenerate Hamiltonians (Ginzburg–Gürel 2010, Hein 2012) and to certain non‑aspherical manifolds under additional hypotheses (e.g. negative monotone symplectic manifolds). The Conley conjecture also has a Conley–Zehnder index refinement: for a non‑degenerate Hamiltonian, the mean index of periodic orbits grows linearly with iteration, guaranteeing infinitely many geometrically distinct orbits. This index‑theoretic perspective links the conjecture to the Arnold conjecture and to the study of Hofer–Zehnder capacity.

Applications of the Conley conjecture include rigidity results for Hamiltonian diffeomorphism groups, lower bounds on the number of periodic orbits for Reeb flows on contact boundaries, and constraints on the existence of exact Lagrangian submanifolds in cotangent bundles. The Floer‑theoretic proof has become a template for using filtered Floer homology to extract dynamical information from algebraic invariants.

Symplectic Group Actions: The Kirwan Surjectivity Theorem and the Martin Compactification

Let a compact torus T act on a compact symplectic manifold (M,ω) in a Hamiltonian fashion with moment map μ:M→𝔱*. The Kirwan map κ:H_T^*(M)→H^*(M//T) from the equivariant cohomology of M to the ordinary cohomology of the symplectic quotient M//T=μ⁻¹(0)/T is a central object in equivariant symplectic geometry. Kirwan proved in 1984 that κ is surjective when 0 is a regular value of μ. The proof uses the Morse–Bott stratification of the norm square ‖μ‖² and the fact that the inclusion of the minimal stratum induces an isomorphism in equivariant cohomology after inverting the equivariant parameters.

The surjectivity fails in general when 0 is a singular value. To understand the image of κ in the singular case, Kirwan introduced a partial desingularization of the quotient by blowing up the unstable strata. The resulting space carries a natural torus action whose fixed point set is a union of projective bundles over the fixed components of M. The cohomology of the desingularization maps onto the intersection cohomology of the singular quotient, and the Kirwan map factors through this intersection cohomology.

A complementary combinatorial description is provided by the Martin compactification of the moment polytope Δ=μ(M)∩𝔱*_+. Martin (1996) showed that the closure of the principal orbit type stratum in the symplectic quotient is homeomorphic to a compactification of the interior of Δ obtained by adding boundary strata corresponding to the faces of Δ. The cohomology ring of the symplectic quotient can then be computed as the ring of piecewise polynomial functions on this compactified polytope satisfying certain compatibility conditions along the faces. This description generalizes the classical Danilov–Jurkiewicz theorem for toric varieties to arbitrary Hamiltonian torus actions.

The Kirwan surjectivity theorem and the Martin compactification have been instrumental in computing the cohomology of moduli spaces of flat connections, hypertoric varieties, and symplectic quotients arising from quiver representations. They also underpin the abelianization technique in non‑abelian localization, where the integral over a non‑abelian quotient is reduced to a sum over abelian fixed point contributions via the surjectivity of the Kirwan map for the maximal torus.

Worked Example: Kirwan Surjectivity for ℂP² with a Circle Action
Consider ℂP² with the standard symplectic form and the Hamiltonian S¹‑action given by λ·[z₀:z₁:z₂]=[z₀:λz₁:λ⁻¹z₂]. The moment map is μ([z])=(|z₁|²−|z₂|²)/(|z₀|²+|z₁|²+|z₂|²). Compute the image of the Kirwan map κ:HS¹^*(ℂP²)→H^*(μ⁻¹(0)/S¹) and verify surjectivity.
The fixed points are p₀=[1:0:0], p₁=[0:1:0], p₂=[0:0:1] with weights 0, 1, −1 respectively. The equivariant cohomology HS¹^*(ℂP²) is generated by the hyperplane class x and the equivariant parameter u with relation x(x−u)(x+u)=0. The moment map image is [−1,1]; the zero level set μ⁻¹(0) is a smooth 3‑sphere, and the quotient μ⁻¹(0)/S¹ is ℂP¹. The Kirwan map sends x↦h (the hyperplane class of ℂP¹) and u↦0. Since H^*(ℂP¹)=ℤ[h]/(h²), the map is clearly surjective. The relation x(x−u)(x+u)=0 maps to h·h·h=0, which holds because h²=0. Thus the Kirwan map is surjective, confirming the theorem for this regular value.

When a question asks for the image of the Kirwan map, first identify whether the moment map value is regular. If it is regular, surjectivity is automatic; you only need to describe the generators of the target cohomology ring. If the value is singular, recall that the image equals the intersection cohomology of the quotient, which can be computed via the Martin compactification of the moment polytope. Sketch the polytope, label its faces with the corresponding isotropy weights, and write down the piecewise polynomial conditions. Relative Gromov–Witten Theory and Open String Invariants

Relative Gromov–Witten invariants count pseudoholomorphic curves in a symplectic manifold X with prescribed tangency conditions along a smooth divisor D⊂X. Introduced by Li–Ruan (2001) and Ionel–Parker (2003), they are defined by integrating virtual fundamental classes over the moduli space ℳg,k(X/D,β,→μ) of stable maps whose intersection with D has multiplicities given by a partition →μ. The degeneration formula expresses the absolute invariants of X in terms of relative invariants of the pair (X,D) and of the normal bundle ND/X, enabling inductive computations.

The virtual dimension of the relative moduli space is dim^vir = (dim X−3)(1−g) + ∫_β c₁(TX) + k + ℓ(→μ), where ℓ(→μ) is the length of the partition. The obstruction theory is built from the relative deformation–obstruction complex, which fits into an exact sequence involving the normal bundle of D. The resulting virtual class satisfies a splitting axiom compatible with gluing curves along D, and a divisor axiom that relates insertions of the Poincaré dual of D to the tangency data.

Open Gromov–Witten invariants, developed by Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono (2000s) and later by Solomon (2006), count pseudoholomorphic disks with boundary on a Lagrangian submanifold L⊂X. The moduli space ℳk,ℓ(X,L,β) of stable maps from bordered Riemann surfaces carries a Kuranishi structure (or a polyfold structure in the Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder framework). The invariants take values in the group ring of H₁(L) and satisfy an A∞‑algebra structure (the Fukaya algebra). When L is exact and spin, the disk potential W_L defines a Landau–Ginzburg superpotential whose critical points correspond to objects in the Fukaya category.

Relative and open invariants are linked by the open‑closed string map and by the removable singularity theorem for pseudoholomorphic curves with boundary on a Lagrangian that intersects a divisor transversely. In mirror symmetry, the relative invariants of a toric variety with respect to its toric boundary compute the closed string sector, while the open invariants of the mirror Lagrangian torus fiber compute the superpotential. The Gross–Siebert program uses logarithmic Gromov–Witten theory, a simultaneous generalization of relative and open theories, to construct mirror pairs for arbitrary Calabi–Yau varieties.

Polyfold Theory and the Compactness of Moduli Spaces of Pseudoholomorphic Curves

Polyfold theory, introduced by Hofer, Wysocki, and Zehnder (2009–2017), provides a functional‑analytic framework that resolves the transversality and compactness difficulties inherent in the study of pseudoholomorphic curves in symplectic field theory (SFT). Classical approaches using Banach manifolds and Fredholm theory fail because the Cauchy–Riemann operator is not Fredholm on the natural Sobolev completions when the domain curve is allowed to degenerate (nodes, bubbling, breaking). Polyfolds replace Banach manifolds by M‑polyfolds, which are locally modeled on sc‑Banach spaces equipped with a scale calculus (sc‑calculus) that captures the loss of derivatives at each level of regularity.

An sc‑Banach space is a nested sequence E₀⊃E₁⊃E₂⊃… of Banach spaces with compact inclusions. A map between sc‑Banach spaces is sc‑smooth if it is continuous and its derivative maps each level E_m to Em−1 continuously. The Cauchy–Riemann operator ∂̄_J becomes an sc‑Fredholm section of a strong polyfold bundle over the polyfold of maps from a weighted Riemann surface to the symplectization of a contact manifold. The sc‑Fredholm property guarantees that the linearization is a Fredholm operator between the appropriate sc‑levels, and the implicit function theorem for sc‑smooth maps yields a local finite‑dimensional reduction (an M‑polyfold chart) around each solution.

Compactness in SFT is formulated as the convergence of sequences of pseudoholomorphic buildings to a stable building. In the polyfold setting, the compactness theorem states that the zero set of an sc‑Fredholm section over a compact M‑polyfold is compact up to the action of the reparametrization group. The proof uses the sc‑smoothness of the gluing maps for broken trajectories and the exponential decay estimates for asymptotic eigenfunctions of the asymptotic operator. This yields a rigorous foundation for the construction of the SFT differential, the definition of contact homology, and the proof of the Weinstein conjecture for dynamically convex contact forms in dimension three.

Beyond SFT, polyfold theory has been applied to the Fukaya category (by Abouzaid–Ganatra–Pardon–Shende) to achieve transversality for moduli spaces of pseudoholomorphic polygons with Lagrangian boundary conditions, and to the symplectic cohomology of Liouville domains. The framework also accommodates logarithmic maps needed for the Gross–Siebert program, where the target is a log symplectic manifold and the domain carries a log structure. The ongoing development of polyfold Fredholm theory for families of operators promises a unified treatment of all enumerative invariants in symplectic topology.

Symplectic Cut and Symplectic Sum: The Gompf–Stipsicz Construction and Applications

The symplectic cut, introduced by Lerman (1995), provides a way to decompose a Hamiltonian S1‑space along a regular level set of the moment map. Given a symplectic manifold (M,ω) with a Hamiltonian circle action and moment map μ:MtoR, choose a regular value c. The reduced space M_c=μ-1(c)/S1 is a symplectic orbifold. The cut replaces a neighbourhood of μ-1(c) by two symplectic manifolds with boundary, each carrying a residual S1 action whose moment map image lies on one side of c. Gluing the two pieces along their common boundary recovers M. This operation is the symplectic analogue of the algebraic‑geometric blow‑up at a divisor and underlies the construction of toric varieties from Delzant polytopes.

The symplectic sum, developed by Gompf (1995) and later refined by Gompf–Stipsicz, is the inverse operation: given two symplectic manifolds (M1,ω1) and (M2,ω2) containing codimension‑two symplectic submanifolds V1⊂ M1, V2⊂ M2 with opposite normal bundles, one removes tubular neighbourhoods of V_i and glues the resulting manifolds along their contact‑type boundaries. The gluing uses a symplectomorphism of the boundaries that exchanges the S1 fibres of the normal circle bundles. The resulting manifold M1#_V M2 carries a natural symplectic form provided the symplectic areas of the normal fibres match. This construction is central to the proof that any two simply‑connected symplectic 4‑manifolds with the same intersection form become diffeomorphic after sufficiently many blow‑ups, and it underlies the classification of symplectic Lefschetz fibrations.

Key technical points: the cut requires a Hamiltonian S1 action with proper moment map; the sum requires a symplectic submanifold of codimension two with trivial normal bundle up to a sign. Both operations preserve the symplectic class up to a controlled change in the cohomology class of the symplectic form. In the cut, the cohomology class of the new symplectic form on each piece is the restriction of [ω] plus a multiple of the Poincaré dual of the exceptional divisor. In the sum, the symplectic class is the sum of the classes pulled back from each side, with the matching condition ensuring the form extends smoothly across the gluing region.

Applications include the construction of exotic symplectic structures on R4 (Akbulut–Matveyev), the proof that the symplectic mapping class group of a K3 surface is generated by Dehn twists along Lagrangian spheres (Seidel), and the computation of Gromov–Witten invariants via the symplectic sum formula (Ionel–Parker, Li–Ruan). The cut also provides a geometric realisation of the wall‑crossing phenomenon for symplectic quotients: as the level c crosses a critical value, the topology of the reduced space changes by a blow‑up or blow‑down, exactly mirroring the variation of GIT quotients.

Symplectic Blow‑up and Blow‑down: Exceptional Divisors and the McDuff–Salamon Classification

Symplectic blow‑up at a point replaces a Darboux ball B2n(r) by the total space of the line bundle O(-1)toCPn-1 with a symplectic form that coincides with the standard form outside a compact set. The exceptional divisor EcongCPn-1 is a symplectic submanifold of codimension two with normal bundle O(-1). The blow‑up increases the second Betti number by one and changes the symplectic class by [ωblow‑up]=π^*[ω]-π^*PD(p)+ε PD(E) for a small ε>0. The inverse operation, symplectic blow‑down, contracts an embedded symplectic CPn-1 with normal bundle O(-1) to a point, provided the symplectic area of the lines in E is sufficiently small.

McDuff and Salamon (1996) proved that any symplectic 4‑manifold containing a symplectic sphere of self‑intersection -1 can be blown down, and that the blow‑down is unique up to symplectomorphism. This result is the symplectic counterpart of Castelnuovo’s criterion in algebraic geometry. In higher dimensions, the classification of symplectic blow‑ups is more subtle: a symplectic submanifold V with normal bundle O(-1) can be blown down if and only if the symplectic form satisfies a certain positivity condition on the exceptional divisor (Lalonde–McDuff). The blow‑up formula for Gromov–Witten invariants (Hu–Li–Ruan) expresses the invariants of the blow‑up in terms of those of the original manifold and relative invariants of the pair (V,E).

Examiners often test the distinction between symplectic and complex blow‑up: the symplectic blow‑up exists for any point in any symplectic manifold, whereas the complex blow‑up requires an integrable complex structure. Moreover, the size of the exceptional divisor is a symplectic invariant; two blow‑ups of the same manifold at the same point with different sizes are not symplectomorphic (McDuff’s “size of the exceptional divisor” invariant). This leads to the concept of symplectic packing and the computation of Gromov width for blow‑ups.

Worked Example: Symplectic Sum of Two Blow‑ups
Let (M1,ω1) and (M2,ω2) be the symplectic blow‑ups of CP2 at one point, each with exceptional divisor E_icongCP1 of self‑intersection -1 and symplectic area a>0. Form the symplectic sum M=M1#E M2 by identifying E1 with E2 via a symplectomorphism that reverses the normal bundle orientation. Compute the second Betti number b2(M) and describe the symplectic class [ω_M] in terms of the pull‑backs of the hyperplane class H from each CP2 and the class of the glued divisor.
Each blow‑up has b2=2 with basis {H_i, E_i} where H_i2=1, E_i2=-1, H_i· E_i=0. The symplectic sum glues along E1≅ E2; the resulting manifold has b2 = b2(M1)+b2(M2)-2 = 2 because the two exceptional classes become identified and the gluing kills one dimension. A basis for H2(M) is given by the diagonal class H1+H2 and the class of the glued divisor E (which satisfies E2=-2). The symplectic class is [ω_M]=π1^*[ω1]+π2^*[ω2] restricted to the complement of the gluing region; in cohomology this is a(H1+H2)+aE with the matching condition that the areas of the normal fibres agree, which forces the coefficient of E to be the common area a. Thus [ω_M]=a(H1+H2+E).

For blow‑up/blow‑down, keep track of the exceptional class E and its self‑intersection; the symplectic area of E is a free parameter that distinguishes non‑symplectomorphic blow‑ups of the same underlying manifold.

The Seidel Representation and Quantum Homology: π1(Ham) Actions on QH_*

The Seidel representation, introduced by Seidel (1997), is a group homomorphism S:π1(Ham(M,ω))→ QH2n(M)^× from the fundamental group of the Hamiltonian diffeomorphism group to the group of units in the quantum homology ring. For a loop {φ_t}t∈ S1 of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms generated by a normalized Hamiltonian H_t, one constructs a Hamiltonian fibration E→ S2 with fibre M and a section s of the associated bundle of Lagrangian Grassmannians. Counting pseudoholomorphic sections of E in a given homology class A∈ H2(M) with one marked point mapping to a cycle representing a quantum homology class yields the Seidel element S([φ_t])∈ QH2n(M). The product in π1(Ham) corresponds to the quantum product in QH_*, making S a ring homomorphism after inverting the Novikov variable.

Key properties: S is a morphism of Λ-algebras where Λ is the Novikov ring. Its image lies in the subgroup of units of degree 2n (the top degree). For monotone symplectic manifolds, the Seidel element can be computed via the quantum product with the first Chern class c1(TM). In particular, for CP^n the Seidel representation is injective and its image generates the whole unit group of QH_*(CP^n). For symplectically aspherical manifolds, S takes values in the ordinary homology ring H_*(M) because quantum corrections vanish.

The representation detects non‑trivial loops in Ham. For example, the loop of rotations of S2 gives the Seidel element q (the Novikov variable), showing that π1(Ham(S2))congZ maps non‑trivially. On a symplectic torus T2n, π1(Ham) is huge (contains Z2n from translations) but the Seidel representation is trivial because the quantum product is the ordinary intersection product and the units are only ± 1. The Seidel representation also controls the action of π1(Ham) on the Fukaya category: a loop of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms induces an auto‑equivalence of the Fukaya category, and its effect on the Hochschild cohomology (which is isomorphic to QH_*) is precisely multiplication by the Seidel element.

Examiner twists: confusing the Seidel representation with the flux homomorphism (which maps π1(Symp0) to H1(M)). The flux homomorphism is defined for symplectomorphisms, not Hamiltonian ones, and takes values in cohomology, not quantum homology. Another trap is assuming the Seidel element always lies in the small quantum homology (coefficients in the Novikov field); for non‑monotone manifolds one must work with the large quantum homology and the element may not be a unit.

Symplectic Groupoids and Poisson Geometry: Integration of Poisson Manifolds

A symplectic groupoid is a Lie groupoid Grightrightarrows P equipped with a symplectic form Ω such that the graph of the multiplication map is a Lagrangian submanifold of GtimesG×overline{G}. The base P inherits a Poisson structure π uniquely determined by the condition that the source map s:G→ P is Poisson and the target map t:G→ P is anti‑Poisson. Conversely, a Poisson manifold (P,π) is integrable (i.e. arises from a symplectic groupoid) if and only if its monodromy groups are uniformly discrete (Crainic–Fernandes, 2003). The integration procedure constructs G as the quotient of the path space of the cotangent Lie algebroid T^*P by the action of the fundamental groupoid of the symplectic foliation.

When P is a symplectic manifold, the pair groupoid PtimesP with the product symplectic form is a symplectic groupoid integrating P. For a regular Poisson manifold, the symplectic groupoid is the symplectic groupoid of the symplectic foliation. For a linear Poisson structure on g^* (the dual of a Lie algebra), the integration is the cotangent bundle T^*G of the simply‑connected Lie group G integrating g, with the canonical symplectic form and the groupoid structure given by the action of G on g^* by the coadjoint representation. This recovers the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau symplectic forms on coadjoint orbits as the symplectic leaves.

Symplectic groupoids provide a geometric framework for deformation quantization: the convolution algebra of half‑densities on G yields a star‑product on C^∞(P)[[ℏ]] (Weinstein–Xu). They also underlie the theory of Hamiltonian group actions on Poisson manifolds: a Hamiltonian G‑action on a Poisson manifold P lifts to a symplectic groupoid action on G, and the moment map takes values in the dual of the Lie algebroid. The reduction of a symplectic groupoid by a Hamiltonian groupoid action yields the symplectic groupoid of the reduced Poisson manifold, generalising the Marsden–Weinstein reduction.

Important subtlety: not every Poisson manifold integrates to a smooth symplectic groupoid; the obstruction is the monodromy of the cotangent Lie algebroid. For example, the Poisson structure on R2 given by π = (x2+y2)∂_xwedge∂_y has non‑discrete monodromy at the origin and does not admit a smooth symplectic groupoid integration. This phenomenon is the Poisson analogue of the fact that not every Lie algebroid integrates to a Lie groupoid.

The Viterbo Transfer Map and Functoriality of Symplectic Cohomology

Symplectic cohomology SH^*(W) of a Liouville domain W is defined as the direct limit of Hamiltonian Floer cohomology for Hamiltonians that are linear at infinity with increasing slope. Viterbo (1999) constructed a transfer map SH^*(W2)→ SH^*(W1) for an exact symplectic embedding W1 W2 of Liouville domains. The map is induced by a continuation homomorphism between the Floer complexes of Hamiltonians that are C2-small on W1 and have slope large enough on the complement W2∖ W1. The key analytical input is the maximum principle, which prevents Floer trajectories from escaping the region where the Hamiltonian is linear, ensuring the continuation map is well defined and independent of choices up to homotopy.

The transfer map makes symplectic cohomology a functor from the category of Liouville domains with exact embeddings to the category of Z-graded vector spaces (or modules over the Novikov ring). It satisfies the following properties: (1) it is the identity for the inclusion of a domain into itself; (2) it is compatible with composition of embeddings; (3) it intertwines the long exact sequence relating SH^*, the ordinary cohomology H^*(W), and the Rabinowitz Floer homology RFH^* of the contact boundary. In particular, the transfer map fits into a commutative diagram with the Viterbo restriction map H^*(W2)→ H^*(W1) on the cohomology level.

Applications include the proof that the symplectic homology of a subcritical Stein manifold vanishes (Cieliebak), the computation of SH^* for cotangent bundles T^*Q where the transfer map corresponds to the map induced by the inclusion of the zero section, and the detection of non‑existence of exact embeddings via the non‑vanishing of SH^* (e.g. the non‑embedding of a ball into a cylinder of smaller radius). The transfer map also plays a central role in the proof of the Arnold conjecture for non‑degenerate Hamiltonians on closed symplectic manifolds via the isomorphism SH^*(T^*M)≅ H-*(LM) (Viterbo, Abbondandolo–Schwarz).

Common pitfalls: the transfer map is defined only for exact embeddings; a non‑exact symplectic embedding does not induce a map on symplectic cohomology in general. Also, the map goes from the larger domain to the smaller one (contravariant), which is opposite to the covariance of ordinary cohomology. Finally, the grading shift: the transfer map preserves the Z-grading only after a suitable choice of trivialisation of the canonical bundle; otherwise it may shift the grading by the Maslov index of the embedding.

Symplectic Toric Manifolds and Delzant Polytopes

A symplectic toric manifold is a compact connected symplectic manifold (M,ω) of dimension 2n equipped with an effective Hamiltonian action of the n‑torus Tⁿ and a choice of moment map μ: M → ℝⁿ. The image Δ = μ(M) is a convex polytope, and the Delzant theorem (1988) states that Δ is a Delzant polytope: it is simple (exactly n facets meet at each vertex), rational (edge directions are primitive integer vectors), and smooth (the primitive edge vectors at each vertex form a ℤ‑basis of ℤⁿ). Conversely, every Delzant polytope determines a unique (up to equivariant symplectomorphism) symplectic toric manifold. The construction proceeds by symplectic reduction of ℂᵈ at a regular value of the moment map for a subtorus Td‑n, yielding a Kähler form whose cohomology class corresponds to the choice of a point in the interior of the dual cone. Standard examples include complex projective space ℂPⁿ (Δ is the standard simplex), Hirzebruch surfaces F_k (Δ is a trapezoid with slope k), and iterated blow‑ups of ℂP² at torus‑fixed points (Δ obtained by corner chopping). The classification extends to orbifolds via the Lerman–Tolman theorem, where the smoothness condition is relaxed to allow finite isotropy. The moment polytope encodes the Duistermaat–Heckman measure: the pushforward of the Liouville volume is the Lebesgue measure on Δ, so symplectic volumes of invariant subsets are computed by Euclidean volumes of the corresponding polyhedral regions. Moreover, the equivariant cohomology ring H*Tⁿ(M) is isomorphic to the Stanley–Reisner ring of the fan of Δ, providing a combinatorial model for GKM theory. In practice, the Delzant correspondence reduces many questions about Hamiltonian torus actions to convex geometry: existence of invariant Kähler metrics, classification of symplectic circle actions, and computation of Gromov–Witten invariants via localization.

Lagrangian Cobordisms and the Arnold‑Chord Conjecture

A Lagrangian cobordism is an exact Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ (ℝ × Y, d(eᵗα)) of the symplectization of a contact manifold (Y,α) that is cylindrical over Legendrian submanifolds Λ₋ at t → −∞ and Λ₊ at t → +∞. When Λ₋ = ∅ the cobordism is called a Lagrangian filling. The Arnold‑chord conjecture (proved by Ekholm, Etnyre, and Sullivan for Legendrians in the standard contact ℝ2n+1 using generating functions, and later generalized by Chantraine, Dimitroglou Rizell, Ghiggini, and Golovko) asserts that the number of Reeb chords of a Legendrian submanifold is bounded below by the sum of its Betti numbers with coefficients in a field. The proof constructs a Morse‑Bott chain complex on the space of paths with endpoints on the Legendrian, whose differential counts pseudoholomorphic strips in the symplectization. An exact Lagrangian cobordism induces a chain map between the Legendrian contact homology algebras of its ends, preserving the augmentation category. This functoriality yields obstructions to the existence of cobordisms: if Λ₋ admits an augmentation but Λ₊ does not, no exact cobordism can exist. The theory also produces a Lagrangian cobordism group whose elements are concordance classes of Legendrians; the group operation is disjoint union, and the zero element is the class of Legendrians that bound exact Lagrangian fillings. Recent work of Ekholm–Honda–Kalman shows that the cobordism group is isomorphic to the symplectic homology of the Weinstein domain obtained by attaching handles along the Legendrians, linking the Arnold‑chord conjecture to the non‑vanishing of symplectic homology for subcritical Stein manifolds.

Worked Example

Indeed, the standard front projection shows exactly two chords (the two cusps). However, the Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA of the unknot has no augmentations over ℤ₂ (its homology is non‑trivial), contradicting the existence of such a filling. Hence no exact Lagrangian filling exists.

Symplectic Mapping Class Groups and the Flux Homomorphism

The symplectic mapping class group is the group of connected components π₀(Symp(M,ω)) = Symp(M,ω)/Symp₀(M,ω). The identity component Symp₀ fits into the exact sequence 1 → Ham(M,ω) → Symp₀(M,ω) → H¹(M;ℝ)/Γ → 1, where Ham is the Hamiltonian diffeomorphism group and the quotient is the flux group Γ. The flux homomorphism Flux: π₁(Symp₀) → H¹(M;ℝ) is defined by integrating the symplectic form over the 2‑cycle traced by a loop of symplectomorphisms; its image is precisely Γ. The flux conjecture, proved by Ono (2006) and independently by Lalonde–McDuff–Polterovich, states that Γ is a discrete subgroup of H¹(M;ℝ). Consequently, the universal cover of Symp₀ is a central extension of Ham by H¹(M;ℝ). For surfaces of genus g ≥ 2, π₀(Symp) is the mapping class group of the surface, and the flux group is trivial because H¹ is zero for the standard symplectic form. For ℂP² and its blow‑ups, the flux group is generated by the classes of the exceptional divisors, and π₀(Symp) is known to be finite (often trivial) by work of Gromov, McDuff, and Lalonde–McDuff. The Seidel representation π₁(Ham) → QH_*× lifts the flux information to quantum homology, detecting non‑trivial elements in π₁(Ham) that lie in the kernel of flux. In dimension four, the classification of symplectic circle actions via the Karshon–Tolman theorem shows that the flux of a Hamiltonian circle action equals the cohomology class of the moment map, linking the flux homomorphism to the moment polytope of toric manifolds.

Symplectic Field Theory for Higher Genus and the Compactness Theorem

Symplectic field theory (SFT) is a homology theory for contact manifolds (Y,ξ) built from punctured pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectization ℝ × Y with respect to an ℝ‑invariant almost complex structure. The algebraic structure is a differential graded algebra generated by good Reeb orbits, with differential counting curves of genus zero and one positive puncture. The compactness theorem of Bourgeois–Eliashberg–Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder extends Gromov compactness to curves of arbitrary genus and multiple positive/negative punctures: a sequence of curves with uniformly bounded energy has a subsequence converging to a holomorphic building — a collection of curves in the symplectization and in the completed cobordism, joined at asymptotic Reeb orbits. The limit building satisfies a matching condition on the asymptotic markers, ensuring that the differential squares to zero. The polyfold framework of Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder provides a global analytic setup where transversality is achieved by perturbing the Cauchy–Riemann operator in a scale‑smooth Banach manifold, and compactness follows from the polyfold Fredholm theory. SFT recovers Gromov–Witten invariants of a closed symplectic manifold by stretching the neck along a contact hypersurface; the resulting SFT Hamiltonian encodes the relative invariants. Applications include the proof of the Weinstein conjecture for many contact manifolds (existence of a closed Reeb orbit) via the non‑vanishing of the contact homology differential, and the computation of the contact homology of Brieskorn manifolds using the Morse–Bott technique of Bourgeois.

Symplectic Invariants from Persistent Homology and Topological Data Analysis

Recent work by Polterovich–Shelukhin, Usher, and Buhovsky–Humilière–Seyfaddini has established a dictionary between symplectic topology and persistent homology. Given a Hamiltonian isotopy φᵗ generated by a normalized Hamiltonian H on a closed symplectic manifold, one considers the filtration of the manifold by sublevel sets of the action functional on the path space. The resulting persistence module yields a barcode — a multiset of intervals — whose endpoints are spectral invariants c(α,H) for quantum homology classes α. The isometry theorem states that the Hofer distance between two Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms equals the bottleneck distance between their barcodes, providing a metric interpretation of symplectic rigidity. The spectral capacity cspec(U) of an open set U is defined as the supremum of spectral invariants of Hamiltonians supported in U; it satisfies the axioms of a symplectic capacity and coincides with the displacement energy for many domains. Persistent homology also detects non‑displaceability of Lagrangian submanifolds: if the barcode of a Lagrangian's Floer complex has an infinite bar, the Lagrangian cannot be displaced by a Hamiltonian isotopy. This recovers the Arnold conjecture for non‑degenerate Hamiltonians and gives quantitative versions of the non‑squeezing theorem. The theory extends to relative settings, producing invariants of Legendrian submanifolds via the persistence of their generating functions.

Symplectic Geometry of Integrable Systems and Action‑Angle Variables

An integrable Hamiltonian system on a 2n‑dimensional symplectic manifold (M,ω) possesses n independent, Poisson‑commuting first integrals F₁,…,Fₙ. The Liouville–Arnold theorem asserts that a compact, connected level set of the momentum map F = (F₁,…,Fₙ) is a Lagrangian torus Tⁿ, and there exist action‑angle coordinates (I,θ) on a neighbourhood of that torus such that ω = Σ dIᵢ ∧ dθᵢ and the Hamiltonian depends only on the actions I. The actions are given by Iᵢ = (1/2π)∮γᵢ λ where λ is a primitive of ω and γᵢ form a basis of H₁(Tⁿ;ℤ). The angles θᵢ are conjugate coordinates defined modulo 2π. The frequency vector ω(I) = ∂H/∂I determines the linear flow θ(t) = θ(0) + t ω(I). Non‑degeneracy (det ∂²H/∂I² ≠ 0) guarantees that the KAM theorem applies: for sufficiently small perturbations most invariant tori survive, while resonant tori break up into chains of alternating elliptic and hyperbolic periodic orbits. The monodromy of the action lattice over the base of the Lagrangian fibration is a topological obstruction to global action‑angle coordinates; it is measured by the Chern class of the torus bundle and appears, for example, in the spherical pendulum and the champagne bottle potential. In the semiclassical limit, the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization conditions Iᵢ ∈ ℏ(ℕ + ½ μᵢ) (with Maslov indices μᵢ) select the joint spectrum of the commuting quantum integrals, providing a bridge between classical integrable systems and spectral theory.

The Duistermaat–Heckman formula gives an exact stationary‑phase expression for the Fourier transform of the Liouville measure on a toric symplectic manifold, showing that the push‑forward of the symplectic volume under the moment map is a piecewise polynomial function on the moment polytope. This polynomiality underlies the computation of equivariant cohomology via the Atiyah–Bott–Berline–Vergne localization formula. In the context of integrable systems, the same localization technique yields the exact semiclassical trace formula for the joint spectrum, as developed by Colin de Verdière and Guillemin. The presence of focus‑focus singularities in the momentum map introduces non‑trivial monodromy and modifies the Bohr–Sommerfeld rules by a fractional shift of ½ in the action variables, a phenomenon first observed in the quantum spherical pendulum by Cushman and Duistermaat. Modern work by Vũ Ngọc and others extends these ideas to semitoric systems, where a combination of toric and focus‑focus singularities yields a complete set of symplectic invariants: the Taylor series invariant, the twisting index, and the height invariant. These invariants classify semitoric integrable systems up to symplectomorphism and have been used to distinguish non‑isomorphic quantum systems with identical classical limits.

Symplectic Topology of Symplectic Manifolds with Boundary: Relative Invariants and the Weinstein Conjecture

A Liouville domain (W,λ) is a compact symplectic manifold with boundary ∂W such that the Liouville vector field Z defined by ι_Z ω = λ points strictly outward along ∂W. The boundary inherits a contact structure ξ = ker(λ|∂W) and the Reeb vector field R satisfies ι_R dλ = 0, λ(R) = 1. The completion Ŵ = W ∪∂W ([0,∞) × ∂W) is a non‑compact symplectic manifold obtained by attaching the positive half of the symplectization. Symplectic homology SH_*(W) is defined as the Floer homology of a Hamiltonian that grows linearly on the cylindrical end; it is invariant under Liouville deformation equivalence and fits into a long exact sequence with the Hamiltonian Floer homology of the completion. The Viterbo transfer map SH_*(W) → H*+n(W,∂W) relates symplectic homology to relative singular homology and is a key tool for detecting non‑displaceable Lagrangian submanifolds. The Weinstein conjecture asserts that every closed contact manifold admits a closed Reeb orbit. For contact boundaries of Liouville domains, the conjecture follows from the non‑vanishing of the unit in SH_*(W) (Viterbo, 1999) or from the existence of a non‑trivial contact homology class (Bourgeois–Oancea, 2009). In dimension three, Taubes’ proof using Seiberg–Witten theory (2007) establishes the conjecture for all closed contact 3‑manifolds. Higher‑dimensional proofs rely on symplectic field theory: the contact homology differential counts punctured pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectization, and its non‑triviality forces the existence of a Reeb orbit. Relative Gromov–Witten invariants of (Ŵ,∂W) with Lagrangian boundary conditions on the cylindrical end compute open string invariants that satisfy an open‑closed topological quantum field theory structure (Ekholm–Lekili–Lipshitz). These invariants are instrumental in the proof of the Arnold chord conjecture for exact Lagrangians in cotangent bundles and in the construction of the wrapped Fukaya category.

Technique
Explain how the Viterbo transfer map is used to prove that a closed exact Lagrangian submanifold L in a Liouville domain W cannot be displaced by a Hamiltonian isotopy.
The Viterbo map V: SH_*(W) → H*+n(W,∂W) sends the unit 1 ∈ SH_*(W) to the fundamental class [W,∂W]. If L were displaceable, the wrapped Floer homology HW_*(L) would vanish, and the unit in SH_*(W) would factor through the zero map in the long exact sequence of the pair (W,∂W), forcing 1 = 0 in SH_*(W), a contradiction. Hence L is non‑displaceable.

Symplectic Geometry of Poisson Manifolds and Dirac Structures

A Poisson manifold (M,π) carries a bivector field π satisfying [π,π] = 0 (Schouten bracket). The symplectic leaves are the maximal integral submanifolds of the distribution Im(π^♯: T*M → TM); each leaf inherits a symplectic form ω_leaf = π-1. The Weinstein splitting theorem states that near any point there is a local product decomposition into a symplectic factor and a transverse Poisson factor with zero Poisson structure at the point. A Dirac structure on M is a maximally isotropic subbundle L ⊂ TM ⊕ T*M (with respect to the natural symmetric pairing) that is closed under the Courant bracket. Graphs of Poisson bivectors and of closed 2‑forms are examples; more generally, a Dirac structure encodes both a presymplectic foliation and a Poisson structure on the leaf space. The integration problem for Poisson manifolds asks whether a given Poisson manifold is the base of a symplectic groupoid (G ⇒ M) with source and target maps s,t: G → M that are Poisson and anti‑Poisson respectively. Crainic and Fernandes (2003) gave necessary and sufficient conditions: the monodromy groups of the symplectic leaves must be uniformly discrete. When integration is possible, the symplectic groupoid provides a global object whose symplectic form integrates the Poisson structure and whose convolution algebra quantizes the Poisson algebra. Dirac structures arise naturally in reduction: if a Lie group acts on a Poisson manifold with an equivariant momentum map, the reduced space inherits a Dirac structure (Bursztyn–Crainic–Weinstein–Zhu, 2004). In the context of topological field theory, the AKSZ construction produces a Courant sigma model whose target is a Dirac manifold, linking Poisson geometry to the Batalin–Vilkovisky formalism.

Symplectic Topology of Hamiltonian PDEs and Infinite‑Dimensional Symplectic Manifolds

Many evolution equations of mathematical physics (KdV, nonlinear Schrödinger, Euler’s equation for ideal fluids) are Hamiltonian systems on infinite‑dimensional symplectic manifolds. The phase space is often a Fréchet or Hilbert manifold equipped with a weak symplectic form ω(u,v) = ∫ ⟨u, J v⟩ dx where J is a skew‑adjoint operator. The Hamiltonian vector field X_H satisfies ω(X_H,·) = dH. For the KdV equation u_t = uxxx + 6 u u_x, the Gardner–Zakharov–Faddeev bracket {F,G} = ∫ (δF/δu) ∂_x (δG/δu) dx defines a Poisson structure; the second Hamiltonian structure (Magri, 1978) yields a bi‑Hamiltonian formulation and an infinite hierarchy of commuting integrals, establishing complete integrability via the inverse scattering transform. The symplectic geometry of the loop group LG and its coadjoint orbits underlies the Adler–Kostant–Symes scheme: the KdV hierarchy is the restriction of the linear flow on a coadjoint orbit of the Virasoro group to a symplectic leaf. In finite‑dimensional approximations (Galerkin truncations), the symplectic form becomes non‑degenerate and the flow preserves a symplectic volume (Liouville’s theorem). However, the infinite‑dimensional setting lacks a Darboux theorem in general; the existence of global action‑angle variables is a deep question solved for KdV by the Birkhoff coordinates of Kappeler–Pöschel (2003), which provide a canonical transformation to a sequence of harmonic oscillators. For the cubic nonlinear Schrödinger equation on the torus, the recent work of Grébert–Kappeler–Pöschel constructs Birkhoff normal forms and proves almost global existence of solutions. The symplectic topology of the space of loops in a symplectic manifold (the free loop space) is studied via symplectic homology and the Chas–Sullivan string topology operations, which are compatible with the pair‑of‑pants product on SH_*(T*M). These structures give a rigorous framework for the Floer‑theoretic interpretation of the Arnold conjecture for Hamiltonian PDEs.

Symplectic Geometry of Mirror Symmetry for Fano Varieties and Landau‑Ginzburg Models

Homological mirror symmetry (Kontsevich, 1994) predicts an equivalence between the derived Fukaya category of a symplectic manifold X and the derived category of coherent sheaves on a mirror complex manifold X^∨. For Fano varieties, the mirror is a Landau‑Ginzburg model (Y,W) where Y is a non‑compact Calabi‑Yau and W: Y → ℂ is a superpotential with isolated critical points. The Fukaya–Seidel category FS(Y,W) is generated by vanishing cycles (Lagrangian thimbles) associated to the critical values of W. The Seidel–Smith construction identifies the quantum cohomology QH^*(X) with the Jacobian ring Jac(W) = ℂ[∂W]/(∂W), and the small quantum product corresponds to the multiplication in Jac(W). The Gamma conjecture (Galkin–Golyshev–Iritani) relates the asymptotic expansion of the J‑function of X to the Gamma class of X^∨ and the Stokes data of the quantum differential equation. In the toric Fano case, the mirror superpotential is given by the Hori–Vafa formula W = Σ z_i on the algebraic torus (ℂ^*)^n with coordinates determined by the moment polytope. The SYZ conjecture (Strominger–Yau–Zaslow, 1996) provides a geometric recipe: X admits a special Lagrangian torus fibration with singular fibres; the dual fibration constructs X^∨. Gross–Siebert’s program uses logarithmic geometry and tropical degeneration to construct the mirror algebraically, proving mirror symmetry for a large class of log Calabi‑Yau varieties. Recent advances by Abouzaid–Ganatra–Katzarkov–Orlov establish the equivalence of categories for punctured Riemann surfaces and for certain cluster varieties, using wrapped Fukaya categories and the theory of scattering diagrams. The closed‑open string map from quantum cohomology to Hochschild cohomology of the Fukaya category is an isomorphism for many Fano varieties (Ganatra–Pardon–Shende), providing a powerful computational tool for enumerative invariants.

Symplectic Geometry of the Nadler–Zaslow Correspondence and Constructible Sheaves

The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence (2009) establishes a precise equivalence between the wrapped Fukaya category of a cotangent bundle T*M and the derived category of constructible sheaves on the base manifold M. This theorem realises a long‑standing expectation that Lagrangian intersection theory in T*M is governed by the microlocal geometry of sheaves on M. The functor sends a Lagrangian brane L ⊂ T*M with a local system to the sheaf whose microsupport is contained in L, and whose stalk cohomology at a point x ∈ M computes the Floer cohomology HF*(L, Tx*M). The proof uses the Guillermou–Kashiwara–Schapira sheaf quantisation of Hamiltonian isotopies and the fact that every object in the wrapped Fukaya category is generated by cotangent fibres. A key technical input is the non‑characteristic deformation lemma of Kashiwara–Schapira, which allows one to push sheaves forward along Hamiltonian flows. The correspondence extends to relative settings: for a Legendrian submanifold Λ ⊂ ∂T*M, the category of sheaves with microsupport in Λ is equivalent to the partially wrapped Fukaya category of T*M stopped at Λ. This relative version underpins the Ganatra–Pardon–Shende theorem on sectorial covers. The Nadler–Zaslow equivalence has become a computational engine: the constructible sheaf category admits a combinatorial description via stratification and exit paths, making Floer cohomology groups algorithmically accessible for many Lagrangians. It also provides a conceptual proof of the Arnold conjecture for cotangent bundles, since the wrapped Fukaya category of T*M is Morita equivalent to the singular chains on the free loop space LM via the Viterbo isomorphism.

Symplectic Topology of Symplectic Manifolds with Divisors and the Donaldson–Auroux Program

Donaldson’s 1996 theorem asserts that every symplectic manifold (X, ω) with integral cohomology class [ω] admits symplectic submanifolds D ⊂ X of real codimension two representing the Poincaré dual of k[ω] for all sufficiently large k. These submanifolds are called symplectic divisors. Auroux (2000) extended this by constructing symplectic Lefschetz pencils: after blowing up the base locus of a generic pair of sections of L⊗k, one obtains a fibration X̃ → ℂℙ1 whose fibres are symplectic divisors and whose singular fibres have nodal singularities. The Donaldson–Auroux program uses these pencils to reduce questions about symplectic 4‑manifolds to the study of mapping class groups and braid monodromy. The symplectic normal bundle of a divisor D is isomorphic to the line bundle L⊗k|D, and the adjunction formula c1(TX)|D = c1(TD) + PD(D)|D holds as in algebraic geometry. A central application is the proof that every symplectic 4‑manifold is a branched cover of ℂℙ2 (Auroux–Donaldson–Katzarkov, 2005), with branch locus a symplectic curve. This result translates symplectic topology problems into the language of braid groups and Hurwitz spaces. The program also yields constraints on the geography of symplectic 4‑manifolds: the genus of a symplectic divisor representing k[ω] satisfies the adjunction inequality g(D) ≥ ½(D·D + |c1·D|) + 1, which is a symplectic analogue of the Castelnuovo–de Franchis inequality. Recent work by Gay–Kirby and others uses broken Lefschetz fibrations to extend these techniques to manifolds with boundary and to the study of trisections.

Do not confuse this with the algebraic geometry version involving the canonical class K = −c₁; the sign convention in symplectic topology is c₁(TX), not c₁(K). Symplectic Geometry of the Calabi Homomorphism, Entropy, and the Geometry of Ham(M, ω)

The Calabi homomorphism, introduced by Eugenio Calabi in 1970, is a group homomorphism Cal : Ham₀(M, ω) → ℝ defined on the universal cover of the Hamiltonian diffeomorphism group. For a Hamiltonian Hₜ with zero mean, Cal([Hₜ]) = ∫₀¹ ∫M Hₜ ωⁿ dt. It measures the average displacement of volume by the Hamiltonian flow and descends to a homomorphism on the subgroup of Ham generated by Hamiltonians supported in a displaceable open set. The Calabi invariant is a quasi‑morphism on the full group Ham(M, ω) and plays a central role in the study of the fragmentation norm and Hofer geometry. Topological entropy of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms, introduced by Katok and developed by Polterovich, Yomdin, and Gromov, provides a dynamical invariant that is bounded below by the exponential growth rate of the number of periodic orbits. A landmark result of Polterovich (1992) and later refined by Shelukhin (2018) shows that the topological entropy of a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism is bounded above by its Hofer norm. The Calabi homomorphism and entropy are linked through the notion of mean action: for a Hamiltonian isotopy generated by Hₜ, the mean action of a contractible periodic orbit equals the Calabi invariant of the isotopy in the limit of large iterations. Recent work by Viterbo, Humilière–Leclercq–Seyfaddini, and others uses symplectic homology and the spectral invariants of the action functional to prove that positive topological entropy implies the existence of infinitely many periodic orbits, a symplectic analogue of the Morse–Novikov theory for geodesic flows. The geometry of Ham(M, ω) as a metric space with the Hofer metric is deeply influenced by these invariants: the diameter of Ham is infinite for many manifolds (Ono, Polterovich), and the Calabi homomorphism detects non‑trivial elements in the kernel of the flux homomorphism.

Symplectic Topology of Lagrangian Skeletons, Arboreal Singularities, and the Nadler–Shende–Zaslow Program

A Weinstein manifold (W, ω, Z, φ) admits a Lagrangian skeleton Λ ⊂ W, a closed subset which is the union of the stable manifolds of the critical points of the Lyapunov function φ with respect to the Liouville vector field Z. Cieliebak and Eliashberg (2012) proved that every Weinstein manifold deformation retracts onto its skeleton, and that the skeleton determines the Weinstein structure up to homotopy. Nadler (2017) introduced the class of arboreal Lagrangians: singular Lagrangians whose singularities are locally modelled on the arboreal singularities Ak, Dk, E6,7,8 (the same ADE classification as simple surface singularities). The Nadler–Shende–Zaslow program establishes an equivalence between the wrapped Fukaya category of a Weinstein manifold and the category of constructible sheaves on its arboreal skeleton with microsupport contained in the skeleton. This is a global, singular version of the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence. The key technical tool is the arborealisation theorem: every Lagrangian skeleton of a Weinstein manifold can be perturbed to an arboreal one through a sequence of Lagrangian handle attachments and births/deaths of Reeb chords. The category of sheaves on an arboreal space admits a combinatorial description via the exit path category of the stratification, making the wrapped Fukaya category computable in terms of quivers with relations. Applications include a proof of the Arnold chord conjecture for Weinstein manifolds, a computation of the symplectic cohomology of affine varieties via their tropical skeletons, and a geometric interpretation of the cluster algebra structure on the coordinate ring of a cluster variety as the wrapped Fukaya category of its mirror.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category with Stops, Sectorial Covers, and the Ganatra–Pardon–Shende Theorem

The notion of a stop, introduced by Sylvan (2016), generalises the idea of a Legendrian boundary condition for the wrapped Fukaya category. A stop 𝔰 in a Liouville manifold M is a closed, possibly singular, isotropic subvariety of the contact boundary ∂M. The partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔰) is generated by Lagrangian branes that do not intersect 𝔰 at infinity. Ganatra, Pardon, and Shende (2018) proved that for a Liouville manifold with an arboreal skeleton Λ and a stop 𝔰 corresponding to a union of strata, there is an equivalence W(M, 𝔰) ≃ Sh𝔰(Λ) where the right‑hand side is the category of sheaves on Λ with microsupport disjoint from 𝔰. The proof uses sectorial covers: a cover of the skeleton by sectors (conical neighbourhoods of strata) such that the stop is a union of sector boundaries. The GPS theorem implies that the wrapped Fukaya category satisfies a form of descent with respect to sectorial covers, making it a sheaf of categories on the skeleton. This descent property is the symplectic counterpart of the gluing formula for categories of sheaves. A major application is the computation of the wrapped Fukaya category of the complement of a divisor in a Weinstein manifold: if D ⊂ M is a symplectic divisor, then W(M D) is equivalent to the category of sheaves on the skeleton of M with microsupport disjoint from the conormal of D. The GPS theorem also underlies the proof of homological mirror symmetry for cluster varieties and for punctured Riemann surfaces, where the stop encodes the boundary conditions at the punctures.

  • Confusing the Calabi homomorphism (defined on the universal cover of Ham) with the flux homomorphism (defined on the fundamental group of Ham); they live on different covering spaces and measure different quantities.
  • Assuming every Lagrangian skeleton is automatically arboreal; arborealisation requires a perturbation through singularities and is not true for arbitrary singular Lagrangians.
  • Treating a stop as merely a Legendrian submanifold; stops can be singular isotropic varieties, and the GPS theorem requires the stop to be a union of strata in an arboreal stratification.
  • Overlooking the role of the mean action in linking Calabi invariants to entropy; the mean action of iterated orbits converges to the Calabi invariant, which is the bridge between dynamics and symplectic topology.
  • Believing the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence holds for arbitrary symplectic manifolds; it is specific to cotangent bundles (or more generally, Weinstein manifolds with a Lagrangian skeleton).
  • Neglecting the adjunction inequality for symplectic divisors; it gives a necessary condition for a homology class to be represented by a symplectic submanifold and is strictly stronger than the homological condition.
  • Equating the wrapped Fukaya category of a Liouville domain with its symplectic cohomology; SH* is the Hochschild cohomology of the wrapped Fukaya category, not the category itself.

These advanced topics illustrate the interplay between microlocal sheaf theory, dynamical invariants, and the combinatorial topology of singular Lagrangians that defines the current frontier of symplectic geometry.

Symplectic Geometry of Cluster Varieties and Scattering Diagrams

Cluster varieties, introduced by Fock and Goncharov (2006), provide a class of algebraic varieties equipped with a distinguished atlas of toric charts related by birational mutations. Their symplectic geometry is governed by the cluster Poisson structure, a log-symplectic form with simple poles along a divisor at infinity. The mirror of a cluster variety is another cluster variety, and the homological mirror symmetry equivalence identifies the wrapped Fukaya category of one with the category of coherent sheaves on the other. The key combinatorial object is the scattering diagram, a collection of walls in a tropical space carrying automorphisms of a torus algebra. Gross, Hacking, Keel, and Kontsevich (2018) constructed the canonical scattering diagram from the tropical vertex group, proving that its wall-crossing automorphisms generate the cluster mutations. The symplectic form on the cluster variety is obtained by integrating the tropical symplectic form against the piecewise-linear functions defined by the scattering diagram. The wrapped Fukaya category of a cluster variety is generated by Lagrangian sections of the SYZ fibration corresponding to the tropical points; their Floer cohomology computes the cluster algebra structure constants. The scattering diagram encodes the wall-crossing of counts of pseudoholomorphic disks with boundary on these Lagrangians, and the consistency of the scattering diagram is equivalent to the associativity of the wrapped Fukaya category. Applications include a proof of the Laurent phenomenon for cluster algebras, a computation of the quantum cohomology of cluster varieties via the tropical vertex, and a geometric interpretation of the Donaldson–Thomas invariants of the associated quiver with potential as counts of stable objects in the wrapped Fukaya category.

Symplectic Topology of Liouville Sectors and the Partially Wrapped Fukaya Category

A Liouville sector is a Liouville manifold with boundary whose contact boundary is decomposed into a union of a Liouville subdomain (the stop) and a sectorial region. The partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔰) is generated by Lagrangian branes that are disjoint from the stop 𝔰 at infinity. Ganatra, Pardon, and Shende (2018) proved that for a Liouville sector with an arboreal skeleton, the partially wrapped Fukaya category is equivalent to the category of constructible sheaves on the skeleton with microsupport disjoint from the stop. The sectorial cover of the skeleton provides a descent decomposition: the category is the limit of categories associated to sectors, with gluing functors given by microlocal monodromy. The stop can be singular, allowing the theory to handle divisors with normal crossings. The partially wrapped Fukaya category satisfies a localization property: if the stop is a union of strata, then the inclusion of the complement induces a localization of the wrapped Fukaya category at the objects supported on the stop. This localization is the symplectic counterpart of the Verdier quotient of sheaf categories. The theory of Liouville sectors provides a framework for relative symplectic geometry: the relative Fukaya category of a symplectic divisor D ⊂ M is equivalent to the partially wrapped Fukaya category of M D with stop given by the conormal of D. The sectorial cover also yields a spectral sequence computing the Hochschild homology of the partially wrapped Fukaya category in terms of the topology of the skeleton and the stop.

Worked Problem: Sectorial Cover and Descent
Let M be a Liouville sector with arboreal skeleton Λ and stop 𝔰 corresponding to a union of strata. Suppose Λ is covered by two sectors S₁ and S₂ whose intersection is a sector S₁₂. Describe the descent diagram for the partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔰) in terms of the categories of sheaves on the sectors.
By the GPS theorem, W(M, 𝔰) ≃ Sh_𝔰(Λ). The sectorial cover {S₁, S₂} induces a cover of the category of sheaves: Sh_𝔰(Λ) is the homotopy limit of the diagram Sh_𝔰(S₁) ← Sh_𝔰(S₁₂) → Sh_𝔰(S₂), where the functors are restrictions of microsupport. Since each sector is contractible, Sh_𝔰(Sᵢ) is equivalent to the category of modules over the endomorphism algebra of the generator, which is the path algebra of the quiver of the sector. The gluing functors are given by the microlocal monodromy along the intersection, which corresponds to the mutation of the quiver at the shared stratum. The descent property means that an object in W(M, 𝔰) is uniquely determined by its restrictions to the sectors together with a gluing isomorphism on the overlap satisfying the cocycle condition.
Exam Strategy: Sectorial Covers
When a question asks about the partially wrapped Fukaya category of a Liouville sector, first identify the arboreal skeleton and the stop as a union of strata. The sectorial cover is then the cover by conical neighbourhoods of the strata. The descent formula expresses the category as a limit of categories on sectors. Remember that the stop being a union of strata is essential for the GPS equivalence; arbitrary isotropic stops do not necessarily admit a sectorial cover compatible with the arboreal stratification.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category of Plumbings and the ADE Classification

The Fukaya category of a plumbing of cotangent bundles of spheres according to an ADE Dynkin diagram is a fundamental example where the wrapped Fukaya category can be computed explicitly. The plumbing manifold is a Weinstein manifold whose skeleton is the union of the zero sections, intersecting according to the Dynkin diagram. The wrapped Fukaya category is generated by the cotangent fibers at the intersection points, and its endomorphism algebra is the Ginzburg dg-algebra of the corresponding quiver with potential. The ADE classification of simple singularities appears as the classification of arboreal singularities of Lagrangian skeleta: the A_k singularity corresponds to the plumbing of k+1 spheres in a chain, the D_k to a fork, and the E6, E7, E8 to the exceptional diagrams. The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence identifies the wrapped Fukaya category with the category of constructible sheaves on the skeleton, which for ADE plumbings is equivalent to the derived category of representations of the corresponding quiver. The symplectic cohomology of the plumbing is the Hochschild cohomology of this category, which is isomorphic to the Jacobian algebra of the quiver with potential. The Fukaya–Seidel category of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model is generated by vanishing cycles corresponding to the simple roots, and its endomorphism algebra is the preprojective algebra of the quiver. The monodromy of the Landau–Ginzburg model acts on the Fukaya–Seidel category by the Coxeter element of the Weyl group, and the Seidel–Thomas braid group action on the derived category of the quiver matches the action of the symplectic mapping class group on the Fukaya category.

Symplectic Topology of Symplectic Mapping Class Groups and the Action on Fukaya Categories

The symplectic mapping class group π₀(Symp(M, ω)) acts on the Fukaya category by autoequivalences. For a symplectic manifold with a Lagrangian skeleton, this action factors through the action on the category of constructible sheaves on the skeleton. The flux homomorphism Flux: π₁(Symp₀(M, ω)) → H¹(M; ℝ) measures the obstruction to a symplectic isotopy being Hamiltonian; its kernel is the universal cover of Ham(M, ω). The Calabi homomorphism Cal: Ham(M, ω) → ℝ is a homomorphism on the universal cover of the Hamiltonian group, and its homogenization gives the mean action of iterated periodic orbits. The action of the symplectic mapping class group on quantum homology is given by the Seidel representation, which assigns to each loop of symplectomorphisms an invertible element of quantum homology. For a symplectic four-manifold, the action of the symplectic mapping class group on the Fukaya category is related to the action on Seiberg–Witten invariants via the Taubes isomorphism between Seiberg–Witten and Gromov invariants. The Nielsen realization problem asks whether a finite subgroup of the symplectic mapping class group can be realized by a group of symplectomorphisms; for K3 surfaces, this is related to the Mukai lattice and the action on the derived category of coherent sheaves. The symplectic mapping class group of a surface of genus g acts on the Fukaya category of the surface, which is equivalent to the derived category of the wrapped Fukaya category of the punctured surface; this action is related to the action of the mapping class group on the cluster algebra of the surface.

Symplectic Geometry of the Wrapped Fukaya Category and the Localization Theorem

The localization theorem for the wrapped Fukaya category, proved by Ganatra, Pardon, and Shende (2020), states that for a Liouville manifold M with a stop 𝔰, the inclusion of the complement M 𝔰 induces a localization of the wrapped Fukaya category W(M) at the set of objects supported on 𝔰. The localized category is equivalent to the partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔰). This is the symplectic analogue of the Verdier localization of the category of sheaves at the subcategory of sheaves supported on a closed subset. The proof uses the sectorial cover of the skeleton: the stop 𝔰 corresponds to a union of strata, and the sectors not intersecting 𝔰 generate the partially wrapped category. The localization functor is given by the microlocal projection onto the complement of the conormal of 𝔰. The theorem implies that the wrapped Fukaya category satisfies a form of excision: if M = M₁ ∪ M₂ is a decomposition along a contact hypersurface, then W(M) is the gluing of W(M₁) and W(M₂) along the category of the contact hypersurface. The localization theorem also yields a long exact sequence in symplectic cohomology relating SH*(M), SH*(M, 𝔰), and the symplectic cohomology of the stop. For a symplectic divisor D ⊂ M, the relative symplectic cohomology of the pair (M, D) is the symplectic cohomology of the complement M D localized at the conormal of D. This provides a computational tool for symplectic cohomology of affine varieties via their tropical compactifications.

  • Confusing the partially wrapped Fukaya category with the wrapped Fukaya category of the complement; the stop encodes boundary conditions at infinity that are not captured by simply removing the stop.
  • Assuming the scattering diagram of a cluster variety is always finite; it can be infinite, and consistency requires an infinite collection of walls in general.
  • Treating the ADE classification of arboreal singularities as identical to the ADE classification of simple surface singularities; the former classifies singularities of Lagrangian skeleta, the latter classifies quotient singularities ℂ²/Γ, and the correspondence is via the McKay quiver.
  • Overlooking the role of the flux homomorphism in distinguishing symplectic isotopy classes; two symplectomorphisms can be smoothly isotopic but not symplectically isotopic if their flux differs.
  • Believing the localization theorem applies to arbitrary isotropic stops; it requires the stop to be a union of strata in an arboreal stratification to admit a sectorial cover.
  • Equating the action of the symplectic mapping class group on the Fukaya category with its action on quantum homology; the former is a categorical action by autoequivalences, the latter is a linear representation on a vector space, and the Seidel representation is the induced action on Hochschild homology.
  • Neglecting the distinction between the wrapped Fukaya category of a plumbing and the Fukaya–Seidel category of its mirror; the former is the category of constructible sheaves on the skeleton, the latter is the category of vanishing cycles, and they are related by the homological mirror symmetry equivalence.

Symplectic Geometry of Symplectic Manifolds with Corners and the Relative Fukaya Category

Symplectic manifolds with corners arise naturally when one studies Liouville domains whose boundary is a union of smooth hypersurfaces meeting transversely along codimension‑two strata. The corner structure forces the symplectic form to be exact near each boundary face, and the Liouville vector field is required to be outward pointing on every face. This setting generalises the usual Liouville domain (a single smooth contact boundary) and provides a geometric framework for the relative Fukaya category introduced by Ganatra–Pardon–Shende. Objects are exact Lagrangian submanifolds with boundary on the corner strata, equipped with a grading and a spin structure. Morphisms are given by Floer complexes that count pseudoholomorphic polygons with boundary on the Lagrangians and with corners mapping to the strata. The key analytical input is a compactness theorem for such polygons, which uses a stretching‑the‑neck argument along each boundary face and a polyfold‑type gluing analysis at the corners. The resulting A∞‑category is invariant under symplectic deformations that preserve the corner structure, and it satisfies a version of the Künneth formula for products of cornered manifolds. When the corner is a product of two contact manifolds, the relative Fukaya category recovers the tensor product of the wrapped Fukaya categories of the factors, providing a categorical interpretation of the symplectic sum formula for symplectic cohomology.

Cornered Liouville Domains and the Sectorial Cover

A cornered Liouville domain (M, ω = dλ) admits a sectorial cover by open subsets each diffeomorphic to a product of a Liouville domain with a standard corner model [0, ε)². The stop 𝔰 is a union of some of the boundary faces; the complement M 𝔰 inherits a Liouville structure with a single smooth boundary. The partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔰) is defined as the wrapped Fukaya category of this complement, but with the additional requirement that Hamiltonian chords asymptotic to Reeb orbits on the stop are excluded. The localisation theorem of Ganatra–Pardon–Shende asserts that the inclusion W(M, 𝔰) → W(M) exhibits W(M, 𝔰) as the Verdier localisation of W(M) at the subcategory generated by objects supported on 𝔰. This result extends to cornered manifolds by iterating the localisation along each face, yielding a multi‑localisation sequence that computes the relative Fukaya category of a cornered domain in terms of the wrapped categories of its smooth faces.

Applications to Symplectic Cohomology and Mirror Symmetry

The relative Fukaya category provides a computational tool for symplectic cohomology of affine varieties with normal‑crossing divisors. If X is a smooth affine variety and D = D₁ ∪ … ∪ D_k a simple normal‑crossing divisor, the pair (X, D) can be compactified to a cornered Liouville domain whose faces correspond to the components D_i. The symplectic cohomology SH*(X D) is then the Hochschild homology of the relative Fukaya category. On the mirror side, the Landau–Ginzburg model (Y, W) with a superpotential having critical locus along a divisor mirrors this corner structure, and the category of matrix factorisations of W is expected to be equivalent to the relative Fukaya category. This picture underlies the recent proof of homological mirror symmetry for log Calabi–Yau pairs by Abouzaid–Ganatra–Pardon–Shende.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Non‑compact Manifolds and the Extended Moment Map

For a Hamiltonian action of a compact Lie group G on a non‑compact symplectic manifold (M, ω), the classical moment map μ : M → 𝔤* may fail to be proper, and its level sets can be non‑compact. The extended moment map, introduced by Harada–Karshon and developed further by Lerman–Meinrenken–Tolman–Woodward, replaces μ by a map to the dual of the Lie algebra of a larger group that incorporates the asymptotic behaviour of the action. Concretely, one considers the symplectic cut of M at a regular value ξ ∈ 𝔤*; the cut space M_ξ is a compact symplectic orbifold with a residual torus action. The extended moment map is the composition M → M_ξ → 𝔱*, where 𝔱 is the Lie algebra of the residual torus. This construction yields a proper map whose fibres are the symplectic quotients at all levels simultaneously. The Duistermaat–Heckman measure on 𝔤* extends to a piecewise polynomial measure on the extended space, and its wall‑crossing behaviour is governed by the geometry of the cut. When G = S¹, the extended moment map reduces to the moment map of the S¹‑action on the symplectic cut, and the resulting polytope is the moment polytope of the original non‑compact manifold, possibly with some facets removed.

Equivariant Cohomology and the Kirwan Map for Non‑compact Quotients

The Kirwan map κ : H*_G(M) → H*(M//G) in equivariant cohomology is defined for compact quotients by pulling back to the level set μ⁻¹(0) and pushing forward to the quotient. For non‑compact quotients, the extended moment map provides a compactification of the quotient stack [μ⁻¹(0)/G] by the symplectic cut. The Kirwan map then factors through the equivariant cohomology of the cut space, and its kernel is generated by the equivariant Euler classes of the normal bundles to the unstable strata in the sense of geometric invariant theory. This description matches the algebraic Kirwan surjectivity theorem of Martin, which identifies the kernel as the ideal generated by the weights of the G‑action on the normal directions to the semistable locus. The extended moment map also allows one to define a residue formula for integrals of equivariant cohomology classes over the non‑compact quotient, generalising the Jeffrey–Kirwan residue to the non‑compact setting.

Examples: Coadjoint Orbits and Hyperkähler Quotients

For a coadjoint orbit O_λ ⊂ 𝔤* of a non‑compact semisimple group, the Hamiltonian action of a maximal compact subgroup K is non‑compact. The extended moment map embeds O_λ into a compact symplectic cut that is a projective variety, and the Kirwan map computes the ring of K‑invariant polynomials on O_λ. In hyperkähler geometry, the extended moment map for a tri‑Hamiltonian action of a torus on a hyperkähler manifold yields a hyperkähler quotient that is non‑compact but admits a compactification by a sequence of symplectic cuts. The resulting extended polytope is the intersection of the moment polytopes for the three complex structures, and its wall‑crossing corresponds to flops in the hyperkähler quotient.

Worked Example: Extended Moment Map for ℂ² with S¹‑action
Consider the standard symplectic ℂ² with coordinates (z₁, z₂) and the Hamiltonian S¹‑action eiθ·(z₁, z₂) = (eiθz₁, e-iθz₂). The moment map is μ(z₁, z₂) = (|z₁|² - |z₂|²)/2. Compute the extended moment map and describe the symplectic cut at level ξ = 0.
The moment map μ is not proper because the level set μ⁻¹(0) = {|z₁| = |z₂|} is non‑compact. To construct the extended moment map, perform the symplectic cut at ξ = 0. Introduce an auxiliary copy of ℂ with coordinate w and the diagonal S¹‑action eiθ·w = eiθw. The moment map for this action is ν(w) = |w|²/2. The product ℂ² × ℂ carries the diagonal S¹‑action with moment map μ + ν. The cut at level 0 is the quotient (μ + ν)⁻¹(0)/S¹. The equation |z₁|² - |z₂|² + |w|² = 0 forces w = 0 and |z₁| = |z₂|, so the cut space is the quotient of the cone {|z₁| = |z₂|} by the S¹‑action, which is a single point. The extended moment map is the composition ℂ² → point → ℝ, i.e. the constant map. The Duistermaat–Heckman measure on ℝ is the pushforward of the Liouville measure; it is a piecewise linear function with a jump at 0 reflecting the non‑compactness of the original level set.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for the extended moment map of a non‑compact Hamiltonian action, first identify a regular value where the symplectic cut yields a compact space. Write down the auxiliary ℂ‑factor and the diagonal action, then compute the cut quotient. The extended moment map is the map to the moment polytope of the cut space. Remember that the Kirwan map kernel is generated by equivariant Euler classes of unstable strata; this is a frequent source of short‑answer questions.

Symplectic Geometry of the Symplectic Minimal Model Program and Symplectic Birational Geometry

The symplectic minimal model program (MMP) seeks to classify symplectic manifolds up to symplectic birational equivalence by running a sequence of symplectic blow‑ups and blow‑downs analogous to the algebraic MMP. A symplectic birational map between two symplectic manifolds (M₁, ω₁) and (M₂, ω₂) is a diffeomorphism between the complements of codimension‑two symplectic submanifolds that pulls back ω₂ to ω₁. The program was initiated by McDuff–Polterovich and developed by Biran–Cieliebak–Mohnke, who introduced the notion of a symplectic minimal model: a symplectic manifold that contains no exceptional symplectic spheres of self‑intersection -1 with respect to a given symplectic form. The existence of a minimal model for any closed symplectic four‑manifold follows from the work of Li–Liu and McDuff–Salamon, using the inflation technique to adjust the symplectic form while preserving the symplectic class. In higher dimensions, the situation is more subtle because exceptional divisors can have higher codimension and the inflation method does not directly apply. Recent work by Borman–Li–Wu uses the theory of symplectic capacities and the Gromov width to obstruct the existence of certain exceptional submanifolds, providing a partial MMP for symplectic manifolds of dimension six and above.

Symplectic Blow‑up and the Exceptional Divisor

The symplectic blow‑up of a symplectic manifold (M, ω) at a point p replaces a Darboux ball B2n(ε) by the projectivisation of its normal bundle, which is a ℂPn-1 bundle over the exceptional divisor E ≅ ℂPn-1. The symplectic form on the blow‑up is constructed by gluing a standard form on the total space of the line bundle O(-1) over ℂPn-1 to ω outside the ball. The cohomology class of the new form is [ω] - ε PD[E], where PD[E] is the Poincaré dual of the exceptional divisor. An exceptional symplectic submanifold is a symplectically embedded ℂPn-1 with normal bundle O(-1) and symplectic area equal to the minimal positive area of a symplectic sphere in its homology class. Blowing down such a submanifold reverses the blow‑up and reduces the symplectic volume. The symplectic MMP asserts that any sequence of blow‑downs terminates in a minimal model, and that the minimal model is unique up to symplectomorphism and symplectic deformation. In dimension four, this is equivalent to the classification of symplectic four‑manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1 by their symplectic cone.

Symplectic Birational Invariants and the Cone of Symplectic Forms

Symplectic birational invariants include the symplectic Kodaira dimension, defined via the growth rate of the volume of multiples of the symplectic class, and the symplectic Iitaka dimension, which measures the dimension of the image of the map to projective space given by sections of powers of the symplectic line bundle. For a symplectic four‑manifold, the symplectic cone (the set of cohomology classes representable by symplectic forms) is a convex cone bounded by the walls corresponding to exceptional classes. The MMP predicts that the cone is rational polyhedral and that its extremal rays are generated by classes of symplectic minimal models. This has been proved for rational and ruled surfaces by Li–Liu and for manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1 by McDuff. In higher dimensions, the cone is not necessarily polyhedral, but the work of Borman–Li–Wu shows that the set of symplectic forms with a given minimal model is a convex subset of the cone.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category of Symplectic Manifolds with Stops and the Nadler–Zaslow Correspondence for Singular Lagrangians

The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence identifies the wrapped Fukaya category of a cotangent bundle T*L with the category of constructible sheaves on L. When the base L is a manifold with boundary or a stratified space, the correspondence extends to partially wrapped Fukaya categories with stops given by the conormal bundles of the strata. For a Liouville sector (M, 𝔰) where the stop 𝔰 is a union of Legendrian submanifolds in the contact boundary, the partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔰) is equivalent to the category of constructible sheaves on a skeleton Λ that is a Lagrangian with singularities along the stop. The singularities of Λ are modelled on arboreal singularities, which are classified by the ADE Dynkin diagrams. The Ganatra–Pardon–Shende localisation theorem states that W(M) localised at the subcategory generated by objects supported on 𝔰 is equivalent to W(M, 𝔰). On the sheaf side, this localisation corresponds to Verdier localisation of the category of constructible sheaves at the subcategory of sheaves supported on the singular locus of Λ. The equivalence is realised by the microlocalisation functor, which sends a Lagrangian brane to its microlocal sheaf of sections. For singular Lagrangians, the microlocal sheaf is a constructible complex with non‑trivial cohomology on the singular strata, and the A∞‑structure on the Fukaya category matches the derived tensor product of constructible sheaves.

Arboreal Singularities and the ADE Classification

An arboreal singularity of a Lagrangian skeleton is a local model that is symplectomorphic to the conormal bundle of a union of coordinate hyperplanes in ℂⁿ. The combinatorial type of the singularity is encoded by a rooted tree whose vertices correspond to the strata and edges to incidence relations. The ADE classification arises when the tree is a Dynkin diagram of type A, D, or E. For example, an A_k singularity corresponds to a chain of k+1 strata, a D_k singularity to a tree with a central node of valence three, and an E6, E7, E8 singularity to the exceptional Dynkin diagrams. The wrapped Fukaya category of a plumbing of cotangent bundles according to an ADE graph is equivalent to the derived category of representations of the corresponding quiver. This equivalence is a geometric realisation of the McKay correspondence: the quotient singularity ℂ²/Γ for a finite subgroup Γ ⊂ SU(2) has a minimal resolution whose exceptional divisor is a union of ℂP¹'s intersecting according to the ADE diagram, and the Fukaya category of the resolution is equivalent to the category of Γ‑equivariant sheaves on ℂ².

Sectorial Covers and the Localization Theorem

A sectorial cover of a Liouville manifold with stop 𝔰 is an open cover by sectors (Liouville domains with corners) such that each sector either avoids 𝔰 or contains a single stratum of 𝔰 as a face. The partially wrapped Fukaya category is generated by the sectors that avoid 𝔰. The localisation functor is constructed by taking the microlocal projection onto the complement of the conormal of 𝔰. This projection is a functor from the wrapped Fukaya category of the whole manifold to the partially wrapped category, and it is a Verdier localisation. The proof uses the fact that the stop is a union of strata in an arboreal stratification, which guarantees that the sectorial cover exists and that the microlocal projection is well defined. The theorem implies a long exact sequence in symplectic cohomology relating SH*(M), SH*(M, 𝔰), and the symplectic cohomology of the stop, which is a powerful computational tool for affine varieties with divisors.

Symplectic Geometry of Symplectic Capacities in Higher Dimensions and the Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder Invariants

Symplectic capacities are numerical invariants of symplectic manifolds that measure the size of a domain in a symplectically invariant way. In dimension two, the only capacity is the area. In higher dimensions, the Gromov width c_G(M, ω) = sup{πr² | B2n(r) symplectically embeds into (M, ω)} is the most basic capacity. Ekeland–Hofer introduced a sequence of capacities c_k for convex domains in ℝ2n using the minimax principle on the action functional of the Hamiltonian system. Zehnder extended these to general symplectic manifolds by defining c_k(M, ω) as the infimum of the maximum action of a Hamiltonian with a given number of periodic orbits. These capacities satisfy monotonicity, conformality, and non‑triviality, and they are symplectic invariants. For a convex domain, the Ekeland–Hofer capacities coincide with the action values of the closed characteristics on the boundary. In particular, for an ellipsoid E(a₁, …, a_n) = {∑ |z_j|²/a_j < 1}, the capacities are the numbers π a_j with multiplicities given by the lattice points in the positive orthant. The sequence c_k(E) determines the symplectic embedding problem for ellipsoids: E(a) embeds into E(b) if and only if c_k(E(a)) ≤ c_k(E(b)) for all k. This result, proved by McDuff–Schlenk, is a cornerstone of symplectic embedding theory.

Higher‑Dimensional Capacities and the Volume Capacity

Beyond the Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder sequence, there are capacities defined via symplectic homology, such as the spectral invariants c(α, H) for α ∈ SH*(M). The volume capacity c_vol(M, ω) = (n! vol(M, ω))1/n is a homogenous capacity that gives a lower bound for the Gromov width. For a Liouville domain, the symplectic cohomology SH*(M) carries a filtration by action, and the spectral invariants are the minimal action levels at which a given homology class appears. These invariants satisfy the triangle inequality and are monotone under symplectic embeddings. The Viterbo functoriality map SH*(M) → SH*(N) for an exact embedding M → N preserves the filtration, yielding inequalities between the spectral capacities of M and N. This functoriality is a key ingredient in the proof of the symplectic non‑squeezing theorem in higher dimensions and in the computation of the embedding capacity of polydiscs.

Applications to Embedding Problems and the Symplectic Camel

The symplectic camel problem asks whether a ball can be moved through a hole in a hypersurface without decreasing its radius. In dimension four, the answer is negative by Gromov's non‑squeezing theorem. In higher dimensions, the problem is more subtle because the hole can have codimension greater than two. The Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder capacities provide obstructions: if a ball B2n(R) can be symplectically embedded into a domain with a hole of radius r, then c_k(B2n(R)) ≤ c_k(domain) for all k. For a cylindrical hole ℝ² × B2n-2(r), the capacities are πr² times the lattice points, and the inequality forces R ≤ r. This recovers the non‑squeezing theorem in all dimensions. For more general holes, the capacities give sharp embedding criteria. The recent work of Cristofaro‑Gardiner–Holm–Mandini–Pires uses the full sequence of EHZ capacities to solve the embedding problem for four‑dimensional ellipsoids into polydiscs, and the higher‑dimensional analogue remains an active area of research.

  • Believing the Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder capacities are defined for all symplectic manifolds; they are originally defined for convex domains and extended via symplectic homology, but the extension requires technical conditions such as exactness or monotonicity.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Non‑compact Manifolds and the Extended Moment Map: Properness and Cuts

The classical moment map μ: M → 𝔤* for a Hamiltonian G‑action on a compact symplectic manifold (M, ω) is proper when G is compact, and its level sets are compact. On non‑compact manifolds the moment map often fails to be proper, which obstructs the construction of symplectic quotients μ⁻¹(0)/G as Hausdorff spaces. Guillemin and Sternberg (1990) introduced the extended moment map by embedding the non‑compact manifold into a compact one via a symplectic cut. The cut replaces a neighbourhood of a Hamiltonian circle action with a divisor, producing a compact symplectic manifold (M̃, ω̃) together with a proper moment map μ̃: M̃ → 𝔤* that coincides with the original μ on the complement of the cut. The construction depends on a choice of a regular value ξ ∈ 𝔤* and a Hamiltonian circle action generated by a function H with proper moment map; the cut is performed at the level H = ξ. The resulting space carries a residual Hamiltonian G‑action and the quotient μ̃⁻¹(0)/G is a compact symplectic orbifold. This technique underlies the proof of the Kirwan surjectivity theorem for non‑compact quotients and provides a geometric realisation of the Martin compactification of the quotient.

Key properties: (1) The cut is functorial with respect to Hamiltonian morphisms. (2) The cohomology class [ω̃] equals [ω] + PD(D) where D is the exceptional divisor. (3) The Duistermaat–Heckman measure on the cut space is the push‑forward of the original measure plus a contribution from the divisor. These facts are used in the computation of equivariant cohomology of non‑compact symplectic manifolds and in the definition of the extended Kirwan map κ̃: H_G*(M) → H*(μ̃⁻¹(0)/G).

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Non‑compact Manifolds and the Extended Moment Map: Non‑proper Moment Maps

When the moment map μ is not proper, the zero‑level set μ⁻¹(0) may be non‑compact and the quotient μ⁻¹(0)/G can be non‑Hausdorff. Meinrenken and Sjamaar (1999) showed that one can still define a symplectic quotient by passing to the symplectic cut at a regular value ξ and then taking the quotient of the proper extended moment map μ̃⁻¹(0)/G. The resulting space is independent of the choice of ξ up to symplectomorphism, provided the cut is performed in a neighbourhood where the circle action is free. The construction yields a stratified symplectic space whose strata correspond to the orbit types of the original G‑action. The extended moment map is proper by construction, and its critical points are precisely the points where the original moment map fails to be transverse to the zero level. This framework allows the definition of the Kirwan map for non‑compact quotients as the composition H_G*(M) → H_G*(M̃) → H*(μ̃⁻¹(0)/G), where the first map is induced by the inclusion of the complement of the cut and the second is the usual Kirwan map for the compact cut space. The map is surjective in many cases, and its kernel is generated by the equivariant Euler classes of the normal bundles of the unstable strata.

Applications include the computation of the cohomology of moduli spaces of flat connections on non‑compact surfaces (via the Atiyah–Bott symplectic structure) and the study of the topology of hyperkähler quotients arising from non‑compact quiver varieties. The extended moment map also appears in the work of Harada and Holm (2004) on the equivariant cohomology of hypertoric varieties, where the cut corresponds to the deletion of a hyperplane arrangement.

Examiner's trap
A typical question asks: "Explain why the ordinary moment map for a Hamiltonian torus action on a non‑compact symplectic manifold may fail to be proper, and how the symplectic cut restores properness. Give an explicit example where the cut changes the topology of the quotient."
The ordinary moment map μ: M → 𝔱* is proper iff the preimage of a compact set is compact. On a non‑compact manifold such as T*𝕋ⁿ with the standard torus action, μ is the projection onto the fibre, which is not proper because the base is non‑compact. The symplectic cut at a regular value ξ replaces the non‑compact end by a divisor D ≅ ℙⁿ⁻¹, yielding a compact manifold M̃. The extended moment map μ̃: M̃ → 𝔱* is proper because M̃ is compact. The quotient μ̃⁻¹(0)/𝕋ⁿ is then a compact orbifold, whereas the naive quotient μ⁻¹(0)/𝕋ⁿ is non‑compact and non‑Hausdorff. In the example T*𝕋ⁿ, the cut produces the projective bundle ℙ(T*𝕋ⁿ ⊕ ℂ) and the quotient becomes a point, illustrating the topological change.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mentions “extended moment map” or “symplectic cut”, immediately recall the three‑step recipe: (1) choose a Hamiltonian circle action with proper moment map H; (2) cut at a regular value ξ to obtain a compact manifold with divisor D; (3) the extended moment map is the original μ on the complement and extends continuously across D. Memorise the cohomology formula [ω̃] = [ω] + PD(D) and the surjectivity statement for the extended Kirwan map. These are the facts examiners test most often.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Non‑compact Manifolds and the Extended Moment Map: Applications to Hyperkähler Quotients

Hyperkähler quotients provide a rich source of non‑compact symplectic manifolds with Hamiltonian group actions. Given a hyperkähler manifold (X, ω_I, ω_J, ω_K) with a tri‑Hamiltonian action of a compact group G, the hyperkähler moment map μ = (μ_I, μ_J, μ_K): X → 𝔤* ⊗ ℝ³ is rarely proper. The extended moment map construction can be applied simultaneously to the three symplectic forms by performing a symplectic cut with respect to a generic circle subgroup S¹ ⊂ G that acts with proper moment map for one of the forms, say ω_I. The cut yields a compact hyperkähler orbifold X̃ with a residual G‑action and an extended hyperkähler moment map μ̃. The hyperkähler quotient μ̃⁻¹(0)/G is then a compact hyperkähler orbifold, which coincides with the usual hyperkähler quotient when the original moment map is proper. This technique was used by Bielawski and Dancer (2000) to compactify the moduli space of monopoles on ℝ³ and by Nakajima (1994) to construct quiver varieties as hyperkähler quotients of non‑compact representation spaces. The extended moment map also controls the wall‑crossing behaviour of the Betti numbers of the quotient as the stability parameter varies, because the cut divisor corresponds to the unstable locus in the sense of geometric invariant theory.

In the context of the ADHM construction of instantons, the non‑compact moment map for the U(k) action on the space of ADHM data is made proper by a symplectic cut at the level of the determinant of the moment map matrix. The resulting compact quotient is the Uhlenbeck compactification of the instanton moduli space. The extended Kirwan map in this setting computes the equivariant cohomology of the compactified moduli space and matches the predictions of the Vafa–Witten S‑duality conjecture.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Non‑compact Manifolds and the Extended Moment Map: The Kirwan Map for Non‑compact Quotients

The Kirwan map κ: H_G*(M) → H*(μ⁻¹(0)/G) is a cornerstone of equivariant symplectic topology. For non‑compact M with non‑proper μ, the target space is not a manifold and the map may not be defined. The extended moment map μ̃: M̃ → 𝔤* on the symplectic cut M̃ provides a proper moment map, and the ordinary Kirwan map κ̃: H_G*(M̃) → H*(μ̃⁻¹(0)/G) is well defined. The inclusion i: M D → M̃ induces a map i*: H_G*(M̃) → H_G*(M D) ≅ H_G*(M) (since D has codimension two). The composition κ̃ ∘ (i*)⁻¹ defines the extended Kirwan map κ_ext: H_G*(M) → H*(μ̃⁻¹(0)/G). Harada and Holm (2004) proved that κ_ext is surjective for hypertoric varieties, and its kernel is generated by the equivariant Euler classes of the normal bundles of the unstable strata, exactly as in the compact case. The proof uses the localisation theorem for equivariant cohomology and the fact that the fixed point set of the torus action on M̃ is the union of the fixed points on M and the fixed points on the divisor D. The extended Kirwan map is functorial with respect to Hamiltonian reductions: if H ⊂ G is a subgroup, the diagram involving the extended Kirwan maps for G and H commutes. This functoriality is essential for the inductive computation of the cohomology rings of non‑compact symplectic quotients such as the moduli space of parabolic Higgs bundles.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Non‑compact Manifolds and the Extended Moment Map: Examples and Counterexamples

Concrete examples illustrate the necessity of the extended moment map. (1) The cotangent bundle T*G of a compact Lie group G carries a Hamiltonian G × G action. The moment map for the diagonal G‑action is μ(g, ξ) = Ad*_g ξ, which is not proper because the fibres are non‑compact. The symplectic cut at a regular value ξ₀ ∈ 𝔤* replaces the non‑compact fibres by the coadjoint orbit Oξ₀ and yields the compact manifold G ×Gξ₀ Oξ₀. The extended quotient is the symplectic reduction of this cut space, which is a point. (2) The hypertoric variety associated to a hyperplane arrangement in ℝⁿ is obtained as a hyperkähler quotient of a non‑compact vector space by a torus. The ordinary moment map is not proper; the symplectic cut at a generic level produces the projective hypertoric variety, whose cohomology ring is the Orlik–Solomon algebra of the arrangement. (3) A counterexample: the Hamiltonian S¹ action on ℂ² {0} with moment map μ(z) = |z₁|² − |z₂|² has non‑proper moment map and the zero level is a cone. The symplectic cut at ξ = 1 yields the Hirzebruch surface ℱ₁, and the extended quotient is a point, whereas the naive quotient μ⁻¹(0)/S¹ is the singular cone ℂ²/ℤ₂. This shows that the extended quotient resolves the singularity and changes the topology. These examples are standard in the literature of Guillemin–Sternberg, Meinrenken–Sjamaar, and Harada–Holm, and they appear frequently in examination questions on symplectic reduction of non‑compact spaces.

  • Equating the symplectic cut with the GIT quotient; they coincide for linear actions on projective varieties but differ for general non‑compact symplectic manifolds.
The extended moment map and symplectic cut provide a systematic way to obtain proper moment maps and well‑behaved quotients for Hamiltonian actions on non‑compact symplectic manifolds.

Symplectic Implosion and the Universal Symplectic Quotient

Symplectic implosion, introduced by Guillemin, Jeffrey and Sjamaar in 1998, provides a canonical compactification of the symplectic quotient of a Hamiltonian G‑space by a non‑abelian compact Lie group G. The construction replaces the ordinary quotient μ⁻¹(0)/G, which can be singular and non‑Hausdorff when the moment map μ is not proper, by a stratified symplectic space called the universal symplectic quotient. The imploded cross‑section is built from the symplectic cut at a generic regular value ξ in the positive Weyl chamber of the Lie algebra 𝔤, followed by a collapse of the fibres of the projection onto the quotient of the level set by the maximal torus T. The resulting space carries a natural symplectic form on each stratum and a residual Hamiltonian action of the Weyl group W = N(T)/T. The universal property states that for any Hamiltonian G‑space M, the symplectic quotient M//G is homeomorphic to the quotient of the imploded cross‑section by the diagonal W‑action. This functoriality makes implosion a powerful tool for computing equivariant cohomology rings of symplectic quotients via the abelian localisation theorem applied to the torus action on the cut space. In particular, the Kirwan map for non‑abelian quotients factors through the equivariant cohomology of the imploded cross‑section, and its kernel is generated by the equivariant Euler classes of the unstable strata, exactly as in the abelian case. Applications include the description of the cohomology of moduli spaces of flat connections on Riemann surfaces (the Jeffrey–Kirwan residue formula) and the construction of the universal symplectic quotient for the action of U(n) on the space of n‑tuples of matrices, which underlies the geometric invariant theory of quiver representations.

Quantum Kirwan Map and the Verlinde Formula

The quantum Kirwan map, developed by Martin, Woodward and others in the early 2000s, lifts the classical Kirwan map κ: H_G*(M) → H*(M//G) to quantum cohomology. For a Hamiltonian G‑space M with proper moment map, the quantum Kirwan map Qκ: QH_G*(M) → QH*(M//G) is a homomorphism of Frobenius algebras that intertwines the quantum product on the equivariant quantum cohomology of M with the quantum product on the ordinary quantum cohomology of the symplectic quotient. The construction uses the symplectic cut to reduce the problem to the abelian case, where the quantum Kirwan map can be expressed in terms of the Givental J‑function of the toric variety associated to the cut. The Verlinde formula, which computes the dimension of the space of conformal blocks in a Wess–Zumino–Witten model, appears as the evaluation of the quantum Kirwan map on the fundamental class of the moduli space of flat G‑bundles on a Riemann surface. Teleman (1998) proved that the Verlinde algebra is isomorphic to the fusion ring of the loop group LG at a fixed level, and Woodward (2005) showed that this isomorphism is realised by the quantum Kirwan map for the Hamiltonian LG‑action on the based loop group ΩG. The quantum Kirwan map also satisfies a wall‑crossing formula: as the level (or the symplectic form) varies, the map changes by contributions from stable maps that intersect the exceptional divisor of the cut. This wall‑crossing is governed by the quantum Lefschetz hyperplane theorem and matches the factorisation rules of the Verlinde algebra under the fusion product.

Worked Example: Quantum Kirwan Map for ℂℙ¹ // U(1)
Consider the standard Hamiltonian U(1) action on ℂℙ¹ with moment map μ([z₀:z₁]) = |z₁|²/(|z₀|²+|z₁|²). The symplectic quotient at level ξ = 1/2 is a point. Compute the quantum Kirwan map Qκ: QHU(1)*(ℂℙ¹) → QH*(pt) and verify that it sends the equivariant quantum parameter q to the Verlinde number for SU(2) at level k = 1.
The equivariant quantum cohomology of ℂℙ¹ is QHU(1)*(ℂℙ¹) ≅ ℚ[H, u, q]/(H² = uH + q), where H is the hyperplane class, u the equivariant parameter, and q the quantum parameter. The ordinary quantum cohomology of a point is QH*(pt) ≅ ℚ. The quantum Kirwan map is defined by Qκ(H) = 0 and Qκ(u) = 0, while Qκ(q) = 1. The relation H² = uH + q maps to 0 = 0 + Qκ(q), so Qκ(q) = 1. For SU(2) at level k = 1, the Verlinde formula gives dim V₁ = 1. Hence Qκ(q) equals the Verlinde number, confirming the compatibility of the quantum Kirwan map with the Verlinde algebra.

Write down the equivariant quantum cohomology ring of the original space (often a projective space or a Grassmannian). Then apply the abelian localisation formula on the symplectic cut: the map is determined by its values on the equivariant parameters and the quantum parameter. The Verlinde number appears as the image of the quantum parameter when the quotient is a point.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Orbifolds and Chen–Ruan Cohomology

When a compact Lie group G acts on a symplectic orbifold (X, ω) with a proper moment map μ: X → 𝔤*, the symplectic quotient X//G = μ⁻¹(0)/G inherits an orbifold structure. Chen and Ruan (2000) introduced the orbifold cohomology H*CR(X) which incorporates contributions from the twisted sectors indexed by conjugacy classes of isotropy groups. For a Hamiltonian G‑action, the equivariant Chen–Ruan cohomology H*G,CR(X) carries a ring structure defined by the Chen–Ruan cup product, which deforms the ordinary cup product by degree‑shifting numbers (ages) associated to the twisted sectors. The Kirwan map extends to a surjective homomorphism κCR: H*G,CR(X) → H*CR(X//G) whose kernel is generated by the equivariant Euler classes of the normal bundles of the unstable strata, now taken in the Chen–Ruan sense. This result, proved by Goldin and Holm (2004) for torus actions and by Jiang (2007) for general compact groups, uses the symplectic cut to reduce to the abelian case and the localisation theorem for equivariant Chen–Ruan cohomology. The fixed point set of the torus action on the cut orbifold includes not only the fixed points on X but also the orbifold strata coming from the exceptional divisor. The ages of these strata are computed from the weights of the torus action on the normal bundles, and they determine the degree shifts in the Chen–Ruan product. An important application is the computation of the Chen–Ruan cohomology of hypertoric orbifolds, which are hyperkähler quotients of vector spaces by tori. The resulting ring is the orbifold Orlik–Solomon algebra of the associated hyperplane arrangement, with generators corresponding to the twisted sectors labelled by the intersection lattice of the arrangement.

Symplectic Sum Formula and Degeneration of Gromov–Witten Invariants

The symplectic sum formula, established by Ionel and Parker (2003) and independently by Li and Ruan (2001), expresses the Gromov–Witten invariants of a symplectic manifold obtained by gluing two symplectic manifolds along a common symplectic divisor in terms of relative Gromov–Witten invariants of the pieces. Let (M₁, ω₁) and (M₂, ω₂) be symplectic manifolds containing a codimension‑two symplectic submanifold V with normal bundles N₁ and N₂ satisfying c₁(N₁) + c₁(N₂) = 0. The symplectic sum M = M₁ #_V M₂ carries a symplectic form obtained by stretching the neck along V. For a homology class A ∈ H₂(M) that splits as A = A₁ + A₂ with Aᵢ ∈ H₂(Mᵢ) and matching intersection numbers with V, the formula states that the absolute Gromov–Witten invariant ⟨τk₁(γ₁)…τk_n(γ_n)⟩AM equals a sum over splittings of the insertions and over relative invariants of (M₁, V) and (M₂, V) with matching contact orders. The relative invariants count pseudoholomorphic curves with prescribed tangency conditions along V, encoded by partitions of the intersection numbers. The degeneration formula, a special case where one piece is a projective bundle over V, yields a recursive algorithm for computing Gromov–Witten invariants of blow‑ups and of projective bundles. In the algebraic category, the formula matches the degeneration formula of Jun Li for stable maps to expanded degenerations. The symplectic sum formula is a cornerstone of the symplectic field theory programme: it provides the gluing axiom for the topological quantum field theory defined by Gromov–Witten invariants and underlies the proof of the symplectic invariance of the quantum cohomology ring under symplectic deformations that preserve the divisor V.

Symplectic Quotient Stacks and the Extended Kirwan Map in Derived Geometry

In derived symplectic geometry, a Hamiltonian G‑action on a derived symplectic manifold (X, ω) with moment map μ: X → 𝔤*[1] gives rise to a derived symplectic quotient stack [μ⁻¹(0)/G]. The classical Kirwan map is replaced by a morphism of derived stacks κ: [X/G] → [μ⁻¹(0)/G] which induces a pullback on derived cohomology (or on the Hochschild homology of the category of perfect complexes). The extended Kirwan map for non‑compact actions, developed by Harada, Holm and others using the symplectic cut, lifts to a morphism of derived stacks between the extended quotient stack [M̃/G] and the derived symplectic quotient of the cut. The derived structure captures the higher homotopical information of the unstable strata: the cotangent complex of the quotient stack at a point corresponding to a non‑free orbit is concentrated in degrees −1 and 0, with the degree −1 part given by the Lie algebra of the stabiliser. The derived Kirwan map is compatible with the derived localisation theorem of Gaitsgory and Rozenblyum, which expresses the derived global sections of a quasi‑coherent sheaf on the quotient stack in terms of the fixed point loci of a torus action. In the context of the geometric Langlands programme, the derived symplectic quotient of the moduli stack of G‑bundles on a curve by the gauge group yields the derived stack of local systems, and the derived Kirwan map corresponds to the spectral decomposition of D‑modules on the moduli stack. Recent work of Brav, Bussi and Joyce (2020) constructs a derived symplectic structure on the moduli stack of Lagrangians in a Calabi–Yau 3‑fold and shows that the derived Kirwan map for the Hamiltonian action of the group of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms recovers the wall‑crossing formula for Donaldson–Thomas invariants.

Symplectic Homogenization and Averaging Theory

Symplectic homogenization and averaging theory address the long-term behavior of Hamiltonian systems that exhibit multiple time scales. These systems typically involve a "slow" component and a "fast" component, often represented by a Hamiltonian H(q, p, t, εt) where ε is a small parameter. The goal is to approximate the dynamics of the original system over long times (of order 1/ε) by a simpler, averaged Hamiltonian system. This approximation is crucial in celestial mechanics, plasma physics, and accelerator design, where rapid oscillations can be averaged out to reveal underlying secular drifts.

The core idea is to perform a canonical transformation that eliminates the rapidly oscillating terms from the Hamiltonian, at least to a certain order in ε. This transformation is typically constructed using Lie series or generating functions that depend on the fast variables. For a Hamiltonian H(q, p, t) = H₀(q, p) + εH₁(q, p, t) + O(ε²), where H₁ contains rapidly oscillating terms, the averaging theorem states that, under certain non-resonance conditions, the system's trajectory can be approximated by that of an averaged Hamiltonian H̄₀(q, p) derived from H₀, plus a small perturbation. The key challenge lies in ensuring that the canonical transformation itself does not introduce new large terms or destroy the symplectic structure.

A fundamental result in this area is the Nekhoroshev theorem, which provides exponential stability estimates for Hamiltonian systems over very long, though finite, time scales, particularly for nearly integrable systems. While classical averaging provides polynomial estimates for stability, Nekhoroshev's theorem, formulated in the context of action-angle variables, shows that if the system is sufficiently convex (satisfies a steepness condition), the actions drift by an exponentially small amount over exponentially long times. This goes beyond simple averaging by providing rigorous bounds on the drift, crucial for understanding the stability of planetary orbits or particle accelerators. The theorem relies on the existence of invariant tori in the unperturbed system and the persistence of "whiskered" tori in the perturbed system, where trajectories can slowly diffuse.

The process of homogenization can be viewed as finding an effective Hamiltonian for the slow dynamics. This often involves solving a homological equation to remove the fast oscillations. When resonances occur (i.e., frequencies of the fast motion become commensurable), the averaging process becomes more complex, leading to phenomena like resonance zones and separatrix crossings, where the dynamics can become chaotic. In such cases, higher-order averaging or specific resonance-adapted canonical transformations are required. The interplay between averaging, resonance, and long-term stability is a central theme, connecting symplectic geometry with KAM theory and chaotic dynamics.

Coisotropic Submanifolds and Constrained Hamiltonian Systems

In symplectic geometry, submanifolds are classified by how their tangent spaces interact with the symplectic form ω. A submanifold S ⊂ (M, ω) is coisotropic if its symplectic orthogonal complement T S⊥ω is contained within its tangent space T S. This means that for any vector field X tangent to S, the set of all vector fields Y such that ω(X, Y) = 0 for all X ∈ T S is a subspace of T S. Equivalently, S is coisotropic if the kernel of the restriction of ω to S, denoted ω|S, has constant rank equal to dim(T S⊥ω). The dimension of T S⊥ω is called the coisotropic defect.

Coisotropic submanifolds are fundamental in the theory of constrained Hamiltonian systems. When a physical system is subject to constraints, its dynamics are often restricted to a coisotropic submanifold of the original phase space. For instance, in Dirac's theory of constrained systems, primary constraints define a coisotropic submanifold. The symplectic orthogonal complement T S⊥ω forms an integrable distribution on S, whose integral leaves are the characteristic leaves of S. These leaves are isotropic submanifolds. The quotient space S / T S⊥ω, obtained by collapsing these characteristic leaves to points, naturally inherits a symplectic structure, provided the quotient is a manifold. This process is known as coisotropic reduction.

Consider a Hamiltonian H defined on M. If the dynamics are constrained to a coisotropic submanifold S, the Hamiltonian vector field XH may not be tangent to S. However, the dynamics on S are generated by a modified Hamiltonian vector field that preserves S. The characteristic foliation of S plays a crucial role: the Hamiltonian flow on S descends to a well-defined Hamiltonian flow on the reduced symplectic manifold S / T S⊥ω. This reduction procedure provides a geometric framework for understanding how constraints reduce the phase space and simplify the dynamics, often leading to a lower-dimensional symplectic manifold where the dynamics can be studied more effectively. This is particularly relevant in gauge theories and classical field theories where constraints arise from gauge invariance.

The relationship between coisotropic submanifolds and Dirac structures is also profound. A Dirac structure on a manifold P is a subbundle L of TP ⊕ T*P that is maximally isotropic with respect to a natural symmetric bilinear form on TP ⊕ T*P. Symplectic manifolds are special cases of Poisson manifolds, which in turn are special cases of Dirac manifolds. Coisotropic submanifolds of a symplectic manifold induce a Dirac structure on the submanifold itself, providing a unified framework for understanding Poisson and presymplectic structures arising from constraints. This perspective is powerful for analyzing systems with both first-class and second-class constraints, where the reduction process can be more intricate than simple symplectic reduction.

Application of Coisotropic Reduction
Consider a particle moving on the unit sphere S² in ℝ³. The phase space for a free particle in ℝ³ is T*ℝ³ ≈ ℝ⁶ with coordinates (x, y, z, pₓ, pᵧ, p₂). The standard symplectic form is ω = dpₓ∧dx + dpᵧ∧dy + dp₂∧dz. The constraints for motion on S² are given by: 1. x² + y² + z² = 1 (position constraint) 2. x pₓ + y pᵧ + z p₂ = 0 (velocity constraint, meaning velocity is tangent to the sphere) Show that the submanifold defined by these two constraints is coisotropic, and describe the resulting reduced phase space.
Let M = T*ℝ³ = ℝ⁶. The constraints are given by functions C₁ = x² + y² + z² - 1 and C₂ = x pₓ + y pᵧ + z p₂. The constraint submanifold S is defined by C₁ = 0 and C₂ = 0. To check if S is coisotropic, we need to examine the Poisson bracket of the constraint functions. The standard Poisson bracket on ℝ⁶ is {f, g} = ∂f/∂x ∂g/∂pₓ - ∂f/∂pₓ ∂g/∂x + ... Let's compute the Poisson bracket of C₁ and C₂: ∂C₁/∂x = 2x, ∂C₁/∂y = 2y, ∂C₁/∂z = 2z ∂C₁/∂pₓ = 0, ∂C₁/∂pᵧ = 0, ∂C₁/∂p₂ = 0 ∂C₂/∂x = pₓ, ∂C₂/∂y = pᵧ, ∂C₂/∂z = p₂ ∂C₂/∂pₓ = x, ∂C₂/∂pᵧ = y, ∂C₂/∂p₂ = z {C₁, C₂} = (∂C₁/∂x ∂C₂/∂pₓ - ∂C₁/∂pₓ ∂C₂/∂x) + (∂C₁/∂y ∂C₂/∂pᵧ - ∂C₁/∂pᵧ ∂C₂/∂y) + (∂C₁/∂z ∂C₂/∂p₂ - ∂C₁/∂p₂ ∂C₂/∂z) {C₁, C₂} = (2x ⋅ x - 0 ⋅ pₓ) + (2y ⋅ y - 0 ⋅ pᵧ) + (2z ⋅ z - 0 ⋅ p₂) {C₁, C₂} = 2x² + 2y² + 2z² = 2(x² + y² + z²) On the constraint submanifold S, we have C₁ = 0, so x² + y² + z² = 1. Therefore, on S, {C₁, C₂} = 2(1) = 2. Since {C₁, C₂} ≠ 0 on S, the constraints are second-class in Dirac's terminology. This implies that the constraint submanifold S is coisotropic. The characteristic distribution T S⊥ω is spanned by the Hamiltonian vector fields XC₁ and XC₂. The dimension of M is 6. The number of independent constraints is 2. The dimension of S is dim(M) - rank(dC) = 6 - 2 = 4. The dimension of the characteristic distribution is 2 (since {C₁, C₂} ≠ 0, the constraints are independent and span a 2-dimensional space of Hamiltonian vector fields). The reduced phase space will have dimension dim(S) - dim(T S⊥ω) = 4 - 2 = 2. This 2-dimensional reduced phase space is precisely T*S², the cotangent bundle of the sphere S². The symplectic form on T*S² is the canonical one. This demonstrates how coisotropic reduction naturally yields the correct phase space for constrained motion.
Mastering Reduction Techniques
When encountering problems involving constrained systems or symmetries, immediately consider the various reduction techniques: symplectic reduction for Hamiltonian group actions (Moment Maps), coisotropic reduction for general constraints, and Poisson reduction for Poisson manifolds. Understand the conditions under which each applies and the nature of the resulting reduced space. Pay close attention to whether constraints are first-class (Poisson commuting) or second-class (non-Poisson commuting), as this dictates the dimension and structure of the characteristic foliation and the final reduced manifold.

Symplectic Geometry of Loop Spaces and Gauge Theory

The concept of a loop space, denoted LM, for a manifold M, consists of all smooth maps from the circle S¹ to M. When M is a symplectic manifold, its loop space LM can often be endowed with an infinite-dimensional symplectic structure. This construction is particularly significant in gauge theory and quantum field theory, where the phase space of certain theories is naturally identified with a loop space or a related space of connections.

For a finite-dimensional symplectic manifold (M, ω), the tangent space at a point γ ∈ LM (a loop) is given by the space of vector fields along γ, i.e., sections of γ*TM. The symplectic form on LM, often denoted Ω, is typically constructed by integrating the original symplectic form ω along the loop. A common construction involves a 2-form Ω(X, Y) = ∫S¹ ω(X(t), Y(t)) dt, where X and Y are tangent vectors to LM at γ. However, this naive form is generally degenerate. A non-degenerate symplectic structure often requires an additional term related to the curvature or a choice of connection on M, or by considering a specific subspace of LM, such as the space of based loops or loops with a fixed initial point.

A more robust approach involves considering the cotangent bundle of the loop space, T*LM, or by using the framework of prequantization. For example, in Chern-Simons theory, the phase space is the moduli space of flat connections on a surface, which can be understood as a symplectic quotient of the space of connections on a principal bundle over the surface. The infinite-dimensional symplectic form arises from the Chern-Simons functional. The symplectic structure on the loop space of a Lie group G, LG, is particularly important. It can be constructed using the Kirillov-Kostant-Souriau theory for coadjoint orbits, where the coadjoint orbit of LG (which is related to the space of connections) carries a natural symplectic structure.

The study of pseudoholomorphic curves in infinite-dimensional symplectic manifolds, such as loop spaces, is central to Floer theory. For instance, the symplectic action functional on the loop space of a cotangent bundle T*Q plays a key role in defining Floer homology for Lagrangian submanifolds. The critical points of this action functional correspond to geodesics or solutions to Hamiltonian equations, and the gradient flow lines are pseudoholomorphic curves in the loop space. These ideas extend to the study of moduli spaces of connections in gauge theory, where the symplectic structure on these spaces allows for the application of geometric quantization techniques and the definition of topological invariants.

Symplectic Geometry and the Theory of Caustics

Caustics are geometric singularities that arise in various physical contexts, most notably in optics, where they represent envelopes of light rays, and in mechanics, as envelopes of trajectories. In symplectic geometry, caustics are intimately linked to Lagrangian submanifolds and their projections. A Lagrangian submanifold L of a symplectic manifold (M, ω) is a submanifold such that dim(L) = (1/2)dim(M) and the symplectic form ω vanishes identically on L. When we project a Lagrangian submanifold L from M to a base manifold Q (e.g., if M = T*Q is a cotangent bundle), the singularities of this projection map are precisely the caustics.

More formally, consider a Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ T*Q, where T*Q is the cotangent bundle of a manifold Q, with the canonical symplectic form. The projection map π: T*Q → Q sends (q, p) to q. The caustic associated with L is the set of points q ∈ Q such that the fiber π⁻¹(q) intersects L non-transversally. This means that at such a point q, the tangent space T(q,p)L is not transverse to the vertical tangent space T(q,p)(π⁻¹(q)). In other words, the projection map π restricted to L has critical points at these locations. The critical values of π|L form the caustic set in Q.

The local structure of caustics is classified by singularity theory, particularly by Arnold's A-D-E classification of simple singularities. For example, a generic caustic in a 3-dimensional space (e.g., for a 2-dimensional Lagrangian in a 4-dimensional symplectic manifold) locally resembles a cusp or a swallowtail. These classifications are universal and appear across different physical systems, highlighting the deep mathematical structure underlying these phenomena. The generating function of a Lagrangian submanifold plays a crucial role here: if L is locally given by the zero set of the differential of a function S(q, x) (where x are auxiliary variables), then the caustic corresponds to the points q where the Hessian of S with respect to x is degenerate.

In Hamiltonian dynamics, caustics arise as envelopes of characteristics (trajectories) of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation. The Hamilton-Jacobi equation provides a way to find canonical transformations that flatten the Hamiltonian. The singularities of the solutions to this equation correspond to caustics. The presence of caustics can lead to multi-valued solutions for the action function, which is a central concept in semiclassical quantization. The Maslov index, a topological invariant associated with Lagrangian submanifolds, measures the winding number of the Lagrangian plane along a path and changes value when crossing a caustic. This index is crucial for understanding phase shifts in wave mechanics and for the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization conditions.

Symplectic Geometry of Non-Autonomous and Periodically Driven Systems

Most classical Hamiltonian mechanics focuses on autonomous systems, where the Hamiltonian function H does not explicitly depend on time. However, many physical systems are non-autonomous, meaning their Hamiltonian H(q, p, t) has an explicit time dependence. Examples include systems subjected to external time-varying forces or periodically driven oscillators. The symplectic framework needs adaptation to handle this explicit time dependence.

One common approach is to autonomize the system by extending the phase space. We introduce an additional canonical pair (t, E), where t is the "time" coordinate and E is its conjugate momentum, representing energy. The extended phase space becomes M × ℝ², with coordinates (q, p, t, E) and a new symplectic form Ω = ω + dt∧dE. The original non-autonomous Hamiltonian H(q, p, t) is then incorporated into an extended, autonomous Hamiltonian K(q, p, t, E) = H(q, p, t) - E. The Hamiltonian equations for K on the extended phase space reproduce the original non-autonomous equations for (q, p) and also yield dE/dτ = -∂H/∂t and dt/dτ = 1, where τ is an auxiliary evolution parameter. This allows us to treat non-autonomous systems within the standard autonomous symplectic framework, albeit in a higher-dimensional space.

For periodically driven systems, where H(q, p, t) is periodic in t with period T, the concept of a Poincaré map becomes central. Instead of analyzing the continuous flow, one studies the discrete map that takes a point in phase space at time t₀ to its position at time t₀ + T. This Poincaré map is a symplectomorphism from the phase space to itself. The fixed points of the Poincaré map correspond to periodic orbits of the original system. The stability of these periodic orbits can be analyzed by linearizing the Poincaré map around its fixed points. This leads to the study of the eigenvalues of the linearized map, which for a symplectomorphism come in reciprocal pairs (λ, 1/λ). If all eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, the orbit is stable; otherwise, it is unstable. This is a direct application of symplectic linear algebra to dynamics.

Another important tool for periodically driven systems is Floquet theory, which extends the concept of eigenvalues to linear periodic differential equations. In a symplectic context, Floquet theory provides a way to characterize the stability of periodic solutions. The Floquet exponents (or characteristic exponents) are related to the eigenvalues of the monodromy matrix (the linearized Poincaré map). For a Hamiltonian system, the Floquet exponents appear in pairs (γ, -γ), reflecting the symplectic structure. The study of these exponents is crucial for understanding phenomena like parametric resonance, where small periodic perturbations can lead to large amplitude oscillations.

Common Traps

  • Confusing Averaging with Exact Solutions: Averaging theory provides approximations for long-term behavior, not exact solutions. The accuracy is typically valid for times up to O(1/ε) or exponentially longer for Nekhoroshev-type results, but not indefinitely.
  • Ignoring Resonance Conditions in Averaging: Simple averaging theorems often assume non-resonance. Failing to account for resonances (when frequencies are commensurable) can lead to incorrect predictions, as resonance zones exhibit qualitatively different dynamics.
  • Misidentifying Coisotropic vs. Isotropic/Lagrangian: While related, coisotropic submanifolds are distinct. An isotropic submanifold has T S⊥ω ⊃ T S, while a coisotropic has T S⊥ω ⊂ T S. A Lagrangian is both. Incorrectly applying reduction methods based on these distinctions is a common error.
  • Assuming All Constraints are First-Class: In Dirac's theory, constraints can be first-class (Poisson commuting with all other constraints) or second-class (non-Poisson commuting). The reduction procedure differs significantly, especially in the dimension of the reduced phase space.
  • Overlooking the Extended Phase Space for Non-Autonomous Systems: Failing to correctly autonomize a non-autonomous Hamiltonian system by adding (t, E) can lead to incorrect application of standard symplectic theorems that assume time-independence.
  • Simplifying Caustics to Mere Singularities: Caustics are specific types of geometric singularities tied to the projection of Lagrangian submanifolds and have a rich classification (A-D-E series). They are not just any point of non-smoothness.
  • Neglecting the Symplectic Nature of Poincaré Maps: The Poincaré map for a Hamiltonian system is always a symplectomorphism. This property imposes strong constraints on its eigenvalues (appearing in reciprocal pairs) and is crucial for stability analysis.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category with Stops and the Categorical Enumerative Invariants

The Fukaya category of a Liouville sector equipped with a collection of stops provides a powerful framework for organizing Lagrangian Floer theory in the presence of boundary conditions. Stops are subsets of the ideal contact boundary that prescribe where Lagrangians are allowed to end; they encode a sectorial version of the wrapped Fukaya category. The categorical enumerative invariants arise from the fact that the Fukaya category with stops carries a natural Calabi–Yau structure of dimension n, and its Hochschild homology computes symplectic cohomology of the sector. This structure allows one to define open Gromov–Witten invariants as counts of pseudoholomorphic polygons with boundary on a collection of Lagrangians, which are invariant under Hamiltonian isotopies that preserve the stops. The Ganatra–Pardon–Shende theorem establishes an equivalence between the partially wrapped Fukaya category of a Liouville sector and the category of constructible sheaves on the skeleton with microsupport disjoint from the stops. This equivalence translates enumerative questions into microlocal sheaf theory, where the categorical invariants become computable via combinatorial models such as strand algebras or quivers with relations. In particular, for Weinstein sectors with arboreal singularities, the Fukaya category with stops is generated by a finite collection of thimbles, and the categorical enumerative invariants are encoded in the A∞‑structure constants of the endomorphism algebra of the generating set. These constants satisfy the homological mirror symmetry predictions for Landau–Ginzburg models, providing a bridge between symplectic topology and algebraic geometry.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category of Plumbings and the ADE Classification

Plumbings of cotangent bundles of spheres according to a simply‑laced Dynkin diagram give rise to Weinstein manifolds whose wrapped Fukaya categories are equivalent to the derived categories of representations of the corresponding preprojective algebras. The ADE classification of simple singularities appears naturally: the Milnor fiber of an ADE surface singularity is symplectomorphic to the plumbing of T*S² according to the same Dynkin diagram. The wrapped Fukaya category of such a plumbing is generated by the core spheres, and its endomorphism algebra is the Ginzburg dg‑algebra of the preprojective algebra. The categorical invariants, such as the Hochschild cohomology, recover the coordinate ring of the Slodowy slice, which carries a natural Poisson structure. The symplectic geometry of these plumbings also controls the topology of the link of the singularity, which is a contact manifold whose contact homology is isomorphic to the symplectic homology of the filling. The McKay correspondence relates the ADE Dynkin diagrams to finite subgroups of SU(2), and the symplectic quotient of C² by such a subgroup yields a symplectic resolution whose Fukaya category matches the derived category of the corresponding Kleinian singularity. This circle of ideas connects symplectic topology, representation theory, and algebraic geometry, and provides a testing ground for homological mirror symmetry in the non‑compact Calabi–Yau setting.

Consider the A₂ plumbing, i.e. the union of two cotangent bundles T*S² glued along a single intersection point. Describe the wrapped Fukaya category generators and compute the cohomology of the endomorphism algebra of the direct sum of the two core spheres.
The A₂ plumbing has two Lagrangian spheres L₁ and L₂ intersecting transversely at one point. In the wrapped Fukaya category, the morphism complex hom(Lᵢ, Lⱼ) is generated by intersection points together with Reeb chords at infinity. This matches the Ginzburg dg‑algebra description.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category of Symplectic Manifolds with Divisors and the Donaldson–Auroux Program

The Donaldson–Auroux program proposes that the Fukaya category of a symplectic manifold M with a symplectic divisor D can be understood by studying the Fukaya category of the complement MD together with a monodromy datum around D. The complement is a Liouville manifold whose wrapped Fukaya category carries an action of the fundamental group of the divisor complement, which is generated by a Dehn twist along the boundary of a tubular neighborhood of D. This monodromy is a symplectomorphism of the complement, and its action on the wrapped Fukaya category encodes the gluing data needed to recover the Fukaya category of the closed manifold. In the case where D is an anticanonical divisor, the complement is a Calabi–Yau manifold, and the monodromy is expected to be a spherical twist associated to a Lagrangian sphere in the complement. The program predicts an equivalence between the Fukaya category of M and the category of modules over the monodromy functor on the wrapped Fukaya category of MD. This perspective has been used to compute Fukaya categories of Fano varieties by degenerating them to normal crossing divisors and analyzing the resulting Landau–Ginzburg models. The categorical invariants, such as the Hochschild homology of the Fukaya category of M, are then expressed in terms of the symplectic cohomology of the complement and the monodromy action, providing a powerful computational tool.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category with Stops, Sectorial Covers, and the Localization Theorem

Sectorial covers provide a method to compute the partially wrapped Fukaya category of a Liouville sector by covering it with simpler sectors whose Fukaya categories are known. A sectorial cover is a collection of Liouville sectors with stops that map into the target sector such that the union of their images covers the target and the overlaps are compatible with the stop structure. The localization theorem of Ganatra–Pardon–Shende states that the Fukaya category of the target sector is the limit of the diagram of Fukaya categories of the covering sectors. In practice, one often uses a cover by elementary sectors such as the disk with one stop (which gives the category of vector spaces) or the annulus with two stops (which gives the category of modules over the A∞‑algebra of a single Reeb chord). The limit can be computed using a Čech‑type spectral sequence whose E₂ page involves Hochschild cohomology of the covering categories. This technique reduces the computation of wrapped Fukaya categories of complicated Weinstein manifolds to combinatorial data encoded in a quiver with relations. The localization theorem also implies that the wrapped Fukaya category satisfies descent with respect to sectorial covers, which is a key ingredient in proving the equivalence with constructible sheaves on the skeleton.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category of Symplectic Manifolds with Corners and the Relative Fukaya Category

When a symplectic manifold has corners, i.e. its boundary is a union of smooth hypersurfaces meeting transversely along codimension‑2 strata, the standard wrapped Fukaya category must be replaced by a relative Fukaya category that keeps track of Lagrangians intersecting the boundary strata in a controlled way. Objects are Lagrangian submanifolds with boundary on the corner strata, equipped with brane structures (gradings and spin structures) and a choice of sectorial conditions at each corner. Morphisms are defined by counting pseudoholomorphic polygons with boundary on the Lagrangians and with corners mapping to the prescribed boundary strata. The resulting A∞‑category is expected to be equivalent to the category of constructible sheaves on the singular skeleton with microsupport conditions adapted to the corners. This relative Fukaya category satisfies a gluing formula: if a cornered manifold is decomposed along a hypersurface, the relative Fukaya category of the whole is the fiber product of the relative Fukaya categories of the pieces over the Fukaya category of the hypersurface. This gluing property is the symplectic counterpart of the descent property for constructible sheaves and is essential for the proof of the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence for singular Lagrangians.

  • Assuming the endomorphism algebra of a plumbing is always the path algebra of the Dynkin quiver without the preprojective relations; the wrapped Floer product introduces the relations θᵢ = Σ xᵢⱼxⱼᵢ.
  • Neglecting the monodromy action in the Donaldson–Auroux program; the Fukaya category of the closed manifold is not simply the wrapped category of the complement but the category of modules over the monodromy functor.
  • Misapplying the localization theorem by using a cover that does not satisfy the sectorial compatibility conditions; the stops must be preserved under the covering maps.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Non‑compact Manifolds and the Extended Moment Map: The Moment Map as a Morse‑Bott Function

When a compact Lie group G acts on a non‑compact symplectic manifold (M, ω) with a proper moment map μ : M → 𝔤*, the critical set of μ is a union of smooth submanifolds on which the Hessian of μ is non‑degenerate in the normal directions. This Morse‑Bott property, established by Kirwan and later refined by Lerman, allows one to analyse the topology of the symplectic quotient μ⁻¹(0)/G by a stratification indexed by the critical manifolds. Each critical manifold C is a G‑invariant symplectic submanifold; its normal bundle splits into positive and negative weight spaces for the circle action generated by a generic element ξ ∈ 𝔤. The Morse‑Bott indices are the dimensions of the negative weight spaces, and the equivariant cohomology of M can be computed by a spectral sequence whose E₁ page is the direct sum of the equivariant cohomology of the critical manifolds shifted by their indices. This machinery underlies the proof of the Kirwan surjectivity theorem for non‑compact quotients and provides explicit formulas for the Betti numbers of hyperkähler quotients such as the Hilbert scheme of points on ℝ⁴.

Key technical points

  • The extended moment map μ̃ : M → 𝔤* ⊕ ℝ incorporates the Hamiltonian function for the scaling action on the conical end; properness of μ̃ is equivalent to the condition that the G‑action is of “linear growth” at infinity.
  • For a generic ξ, the function μ^ξ = ⟨μ, ξ⟩ is a Morse‑Bott function whose critical manifolds are precisely the fixed point components of the circle subgroup exp(tξ).
  • The Morse‑Bott lemma gives a G‑equivariant diffeomorphism between a neighbourhood of a critical manifold C and a neighbourhood of the zero section in its normal bundle, preserving the symplectic form up to a closed 2‑form that vanishes on C.
  • The Kirwan map κ : H_G^*(M) → H^*(μ⁻¹(0)/G) is induced by the inclusion of the zero level set followed by projection; its kernel is generated by the equivariant Euler classes of the negative normal bundles of the critical manifolds with positive index.

Applications include the computation of the cohomology ring of the moduli space of Higgs bundles on a Riemann surface (via the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence) and the verification of the topological mirror symmetry conjecture for certain hypertoric varieties.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian PDEs and Infinite‑Dimensional Symplectic Manifolds: The KdV Equation as a Hamiltonian System

The Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation u_t + uxxx + 6 u u_x = 0 admits a bi‑Hamiltonian structure on the loop space Lℝ = C^∞(S¹, ℝ). The first Poisson bracket is the constant symplectic structure {u(x), u(y)}₁ = δ'(x−y), giving the Hamiltonian functional H₁ = ∫ (½ u_x² − u³) dx. The second Poisson bracket is the Gardner–Zakharov–Faddeev bracket {u(x), u(y)}₂ = 2 u(x) δ'(x−y) + u_x(x) δ(x−y), with Hamiltonian H₂ = ∫ ½ u² dx. The recursion operator R = ∂_x² + 4 u + 2 u_x ∂_x⁻¹ maps the gradient of H₂ to the gradient of H₁ and generates an infinite hierarchy of commuting Hamiltonians H_n. The phase space is the Fréchet manifold of smooth periodic functions, equipped with the weak symplectic form ω₁(δu, δv) = ∫ δu ∂_x⁻¹ δv dx. Well‑posedness of the Hamiltonian flow in this weak symplectic setting requires the use of the inverse scattering transform, which linearises the flow on the infinite‑dimensional torus of action variables. The Lax pair formulation L = −∂_x² + u, ∂_t L = [L, A] with A = 4 ∂_x³ − 6 u ∂_x − 3 u_x identifies the spectrum of L as the complete set of integrals of motion. The symplectic geometry of the KdV hierarchy thus provides a prototype for the study of infinite‑dimensional completely integrable systems, and its geometric quantisation leads to the Virasoro algebra and the representation theory of the Kac–Moody affine Lie algebra sl̂(2).

Structural highlights

  • The two Poisson brackets are compatible, i.e. any linear combination is again a Poisson bracket; this is the definition of a bi‑Hamiltonian system.
  • The recursion operator R is hereditary, implying that the hierarchy of vector fields X_n = R^n X₀ consists of commuting symmetries.
  • The symplectic form ω₁ is weakly non‑degenerate but not strong; its Darboux theorem fails, reflecting the infinite‑dimensional nature of the phase space.
  • The action‑angle variables are constructed via the spectral curve of the Lax operator; the angles evolve linearly on the Jacobian of the spectral curve.

These features illustrate how finite‑dimensional symplectic techniques — moment maps, reduction, and toric geometry — extend to the infinite‑dimensional setting, albeit with analytical subtleties concerning the choice of function spaces and the definition of the symplectic form.

Worked Example: Computing the Morse‑Bott Index for a Circle Action on T*ℂⁿ
Let S¹ act on T*ℂⁿ ≅ ℂⁿ × ℂⁿ by eiθ·(z, w) = (eiθz, e-iθw). The moment map is μ(z,w) = ½(|z|² − |w|²). Determine the critical manifolds of μ and their Morse‑Bott indices.
The critical points of μ are the fixed points of the S¹ action, i.e. (z,w) with z=0 or w=0. The zero level set μ⁻¹(0) consists of pairs with |z|=|w|. The critical manifolds are C₊ = {z=0} ≅ ℂⁿ (index 0) and C₋ = {w=0} ≅ ℂⁿ (index 2n). The Hessian of μ at a point of C₊ has n positive eigenvalues (directions in w) and n negative eigenvalues (directions in z), giving Morse‑Bott index 2n for C₋ and 0 for C₊. The Kirwan map in equivariant cohomology is surjective with kernel generated by the Euler class of the negative normal bundle of C₋, which is the top Chern class of the tautological bundle over ℂⁿ.

The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence establishes an equivalence between the wrapped Fukaya category of a cotangent bundle T*L and the category of constructible sheaves on L with microsupport in the zero section. Symplectic Geometry of Symplectic Capacities in Higher Dimensions and the Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder Invariants: Computations for Ellipsoids and Polydiscs

The Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder (EHZ) capacities c_k(M, ω) for a symplectic manifold (M, ω) of dimension 2n are defined via the minimax principle on the action functional of the Hamiltonian loop space. The higher‑dimensional capacities satisfy monotonicity, conformality, and the product inequality c_k(M₁ × M₂) ≤ c_k(M₁) + c_k(M₂). The computation of these capacities for general convex domains uses the theory of symplectic homology and the filtered Floer complex; the EHZ capacities coincide with the spectral invariants of the Hamiltonian Floer homology. Recent work by Irie and others has extended the definition to non‑convex domains via the notion of “symplectic capacity from the positive loop group”, providing a unified framework that recovers the EHZ capacities for convex domains and the Gutt–Hutchings capacities for star‑shaped domains.

Symplectic Geometry of the Symplectic Minimal Model Program and Symplectic Birational Geometry: The Role of Symplectic Flops

The symplectic minimal model program (MMP) seeks to classify symplectic manifolds up to symplectic birational equivalence by performing a sequence of elementary transformations: symplectic blow‑ups, blow‑downs, and flops. A symplectic flop is a birational map between two symplectic manifolds that is an isomorphism outside a Lagrangian sphere S² with normal bundle O(−1)⊕O(−1); the flop replaces the neighbourhood of the sphere by its “dual” neighbourhood, changing the symplectic form by a deformation that preserves the cohomology class on the complement of the exceptional locus. Ruan proved that any two minimal symplectic models of a given uniruled symplectic manifold are connected by a sequence of flops. The cone of symplectic forms on a projective manifold is a rational polyhedral cone whose extremal rays correspond to classes that can be represented by symplectic forms with a Lagrangian sphere of minimal area. Contracting such a sphere yields a symplectic variety with terminal singularities; the flip replaces the contracted locus by a new Lagrangian sphere, producing a new smooth symplectic manifold. The symplectic MMP terminates for manifolds of dimension ≤ 4, but in higher dimensions the existence of flips is an open problem. The program interacts with the theory of symplectic capacities: the volume capacity is a birational invariant, and the EHZ capacities transform in a predictable way under flops, providing numerical criteria for the existence of symplectic embeddings between birational models.

  • A symplectic flop preserves the symplectic cone and the set of symplectic capacities up to a permutation of the EHZ sequence.

  • The Kirwan map for the Hamiltonian torus action on a toric symplectic manifold identifies the symplectic cone with the cone of ample line bundles on the corresponding toric variety.
  • The symplectic MMP provides a geometric framework for the “symplectic birational invariants” introduced by McDuff, such as the symplectic Kodaira dimension.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Circle Actions and the GKM Graph

When a compact torus acts on a symplectic manifold with isolated fixed points and one‑dimensional orbits, the equivariant cohomology can be computed combinatorially via the Goresky–Kottwitz–MacPherson (GKM) graph. The vertices correspond to fixed points, edges to invariant 2‑spheres, and each edge carries a weight equal to the isotropy character of the circle subgroup that fixes the sphere. The GKM theorem states that the equivariant cohomology ring injects into the direct sum of polynomial rings at the vertices, with the image consisting of tuples satisfying congruence relations along edges. This description makes it possible to read off the ordinary cohomology, the Chern classes of the tangent bundle, and the moment‑map image from purely graph‑theoretic data. In particular, the moment polytope of a Hamiltonian torus action is recovered as the convex hull of the fixed‑point moment values, and the Duistermaat–Heckman measure becomes a piecewise polynomial whose chambers are determined by the GKM graph. The method extends to non‑abelian group actions by passing to a maximal torus and imposing Weyl‑invariance, and it underlies the computation of quantum cohomology for many toric and flag varieties.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category of Liouville Sectors with Stops

A Liouville sector is a Liouville domain equipped with a distinguished subset of its contact boundary, called a stop, which prevents Reeb chords from crossing that region. The partially wrapped Fukaya category of a sector with stops is generated by Lagrangian branes that are disjoint from the stop, and its morphism spaces count pseudoholomorphic strips with boundary on the Lagrangians and with interior punctures asymptotic to Reeb chords that avoid the stop. The stop introduces a sectorial cover of the contact boundary, and the resulting A∞‑structure satisfies a localization theorem: inverting the morphisms associated to the stop yields the fully wrapped Fukaya category of the underlying Liouville domain. This framework, developed by Ganatra, Pardon and Shende, provides a categorical incarnation of the symplectic cut and sum operations. It also gives a clean formulation of the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence for singular Lagrangians: constructible sheaves on a skeleton with singularities correspond to objects in the partially wrapped category of the associated sector. The stop data encodes the arboreal singularities of the skeleton, and the sectorial cover reflects the stratification of the singular locus.

Worked Example: Computing a Partially Wrapped Morphism Space
Let (M,λ) be the standard Liouville sector T*[0,1] with stop at the endpoint {1}×ℝ. Consider the two Lagrangian branes L₀ = {0}×ℝ and L₁ = {1/2}×ℝ, both equipped with the trivial grading and spin structure. Compute the wrapped Floer cohomology HW*(L₀,L₁) in this sector.
In the sector T*[0,1] with stop at q=1, Reeb chords correspond to geodesic segments in the base [0,1] that do not hit the stop. The only Reeb chord from L₀ to L₁ is the constant chord at the intersection point (q=1/2, p=0), which has degree 0. There are no non‑constant chords because any path from q=0 to q=1/2 that avoids q=1 is homotopically trivial and has zero action. Hence the wrapped Floer complex is generated by a single degree‑0 generator, and the differential vanishes. Therefore HW*(L₀,L₁) ≅ k concentrated in degree 0, where k is the ground field. This matches the morphism space in the partially wrapped Fukaya category, which is equivalent to the category of constructible sheaves on the interval [0,1] with singular support away from the stop.

For an inclusion of Liouville domains UV, the Viterbo transfer map SH*(V)→SH*(U) is a unital ring homomorphism that respects the pair‑of‑pants product and the Batalin–Vilkovisky operator. Symplectic Geometry of the Symplectic Topology of Hamiltonian Diffeomorphism Groups and Hofer Geometry

The Donaldson–Auroux Program and Fukaya Categories of Divisor Complements

The Donaldson–Auroux program, initiated by Donaldson in 1996 and developed by Auroux, Katzarkov, Orlov, and others, constructs a symplectic analogue of the minimal model program for algebraic varieties. Given a symplectic manifold M with a symplectic divisor D representing a multiple of the symplectic class, the program studies the Fukaya category of the complement M D and its relation to the derived category of coherent sheaves on D. The key insight is that the wrapped Fukaya category W(M D) is quasi-equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on a Landau–Ginzburg mirror of M with superpotential determined by the divisor. For a Fano manifold M and an anticanonical divisor D, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model (Y, W) where Y is a toric variety and W a Laurent polynomial. The program predicts that the Fukaya category of M D splits into a direct sum of categories associated to the critical points of W, each corresponding to a component of the symplectic skeleton of M D. This skeleton is a Lagrangian torus fibration over a base with singularities, and its topology encodes the cluster structure of the mirror. The program has been verified for toric Fano varieties, where the divisor is the toric boundary, and for certain del Pezzo surfaces. In these cases, the wrapped Fukaya category is generated by a collection of Lagrangian thimbles associated to the critical points of the superpotential, and the morphisms between them compute the quantum cohomology of M via the Seidel representation. The program also predicts wall-crossing formulas for the Fukaya category as the symplectic form varies across the Kähler cone, which have been confirmed using scattering diagrams and the Gross–Siebert program. A central technical tool is the construction of a sectorial cover of the complement M D by Liouville sectors, each associated to a stratum of the divisor, and the application of the Ganatra–Pardon–Shende localization theorem to relate the partially wrapped Fukaya categories of these sectors to the fully wrapped category of the complement.

Sectorial Covers, Stops, and the Nadler–Zaslow Correspondence for Singular Lagrangians

The theory of sectorial covers, developed by Ganatra, Pardon, and Shende, provides a framework for computing the wrapped Fukaya category of a Liouville manifold by decomposing it into Liouville sectors with stops. A Liouville sector is a Liouville manifold with boundary equipped with a stop, a Legendrian submanifold in the contact boundary that removes certain Reeb chords from the wrapped Floer complex. The partially wrapped Fukaya category W(X, f) of a sector (X, f) is defined by counting pseudoholomorphic disks with boundary on Lagrangians that do not intersect the stop f. The localization theorem states that if a Liouville manifold M is covered by sectors (Xi, fi) such that the stops fi are pairwise disjoint and their union is a stop for M, then the fully wrapped Fukaya category W(M) is equivalent to the colimit of the partially wrapped categories W(Xi, fi) over the intersections. This theorem has been applied to compute the wrapped Fukaya category of plumbings of cotangent bundles of spheres, where the sectors correspond to the vertices of the plumbing graph and the stops to the edges. The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence extends this picture to singular Lagrangians: for a Liouville sector with stop, the partially wrapped Fukaya category is equivalent to the category of constructible sheaves on the Lagrangian skeleton with microsupport disjoint from the stop. When the skeleton has arboreal singularities, modeled on the ADE singularities, the category of constructible sheaves can be described combinatorially using quiver representations. The ADE classification of arboreal singularities matches the classification of simple singularities in singularity theory, and the corresponding wrapped Fukaya categories are equivalent to the derived categories of representations of the associated Dynkin quivers. This provides a powerful computational tool: the wrapped Fukaya category of a plumbing of T*Sn according to an ADE graph is equivalent to the derived category of the corresponding preprojective algebra. The sectorial cover technique also applies to the complement of a divisor in a Fano variety, where the sectors are the preimages of the toric strata under the moment map, and the stops are the conormal bundles of the divisor components. The localization theorem then expresses the wrapped Fukaya category of the complement as a colimit of categories associated to the strata, recovering the cluster structure predicted by mirror symmetry.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: "Let M be a Liouville manifold covered by two Liouville sectors (X1, f1) and (X2, f2) with disjoint stops. Is it true that W(M) ≃ W(X1, f1) ⊕ W(X2, f2)?" Explain why the answer is no and state the correct relationship.
The wrapped Fukaya category of M is not the direct sum of the partially wrapped categories of the sectors. The localization theorem states that W(M) is the colimit (homotopy pushout) of the diagram W(X1 ∩ X2, f1 ∪ f2) → W(X1, f1) and W(X1 ∩ X2, f1 ∪ f2) → W(X2, f2). The intersection sector (X1 ∩ X2, f1 ∪ f2) encodes the gluing data, and its category is generally non-zero. The direct sum would ignore this gluing data, which is essential for recovering the correct morphisms between Lagrangians that cross the sector boundary. For example, in the plumbing of two cotangent bundles T*Sn along a point, the two sectors are the individual cotangent bundles with stops at the zero section intersection, and their intersection is a neighborhood of the intersection point with both stops. The colimit recovers the A2 quiver category, while the direct sum would give two copies of the category of a point, missing the morphism between the two Lagrangian spheres.

Cornered Liouville Domains and the Relative Fukaya Category

Cornered Liouville domains, introduced by Ganatra, Pardon, and Shende, generalize Liouville sectors by allowing the boundary to have corners of arbitrary codimension, each equipped with a stop. A cornered Liouville domain is a manifold with corners X equipped with a Liouville form λ such that the Liouville vector field is transverse to each boundary stratum, and a stop f which is a Legendrian submanifold of the contact boundary of the smooth part of ∂X that extends to a stratified Legendrian in the corners. The partially wrapped Fukaya category W(X, f) is defined by counting pseudoholomorphic disks with boundary on Lagrangians that are disjoint from the stop and satisfy appropriate boundary conditions at the corners. The key result is the relative localization theorem: if a cornered Liouville domain X is decomposed into a union of cornered subdomains Xi along a common boundary stratum, then W(X, f) is the colimit of the categories W(Xi, fi) over the intersection. This theorem applies to the Weinstein handlebody decomposition of a Liouville manifold: each handle attachment corresponds to a cornered domain, and the stops encode the attaching spheres. For a Weinstein manifold built by attaching handles of index ≤ n to a subcritical domain, the wrapped Fukaya category is generated by the cocores of the handles, and the morphisms are computed by the Legendrian contact homology of the attaching spheres. The relative Fukaya category of a pair (M, L) where L is a Lagrangian submanifold with boundary can be modeled by a cornered domain where the stop is the conormal boundary of L. This provides a framework for open-string invariants and relative Gromov–Witten theory. The category W(X, f) carries a natural A∞ structure, and the localization theorem is an equivalence of A∞ categories. The stops can be used to define a filtration on the wrapped Fukaya category by the number of intersections with the stop, leading to a spectral sequence that computes the fully wrapped category from the partially wrapped ones. This spectral sequence degenerates at the E2 page for Weinstein manifolds with flexible handles, but can have non-trivial differentials for non-flexible manifolds, detecting the difference between flexible and non-flexible symplectic structures.

Relative Symplectic Cohomology and the Fukaya Category of Manifolds with Contact Boundary

For a Liouville domain U with contact boundary ∂U, the symplectic cohomology SH*(U) is defined as the Floer homology of a Hamiltonian that is linear at infinity with positive slope. The relative symplectic cohomology SH*(U, V) for an inclusion of Liouville domains V ⊂ U fits into a long exact sequence with SH*(V) and SH*(U), and the Viterbo transfer map SH*(V) → SH*(U) is a unital ring homomorphism. When U is the completion of a Weinstein domain W, the symplectic cohomology SH*(W) is isomorphic to the Hochschild cohomology of the wrapped Fukaya category W(W). For a Liouville domain with contact boundary, the wrapped Fukaya category can be defined using Hamiltonians that are quadratic at infinity, and its Hochschild cohomology computes the symplectic cohomology of the completion. The relative Fukaya category of a pair (U, L) where L is a Lagrangian submanifold with Legendrian boundary in ∂U is defined by counting pseudoholomorphic disks with boundary on L and interior punctures asymptotic to Reeb chords on ∂U. This category is equivalent to the category of modules over the Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra of the Legendrian boundary. The symplectic cohomology of U relative to L is then the Hochschild cohomology of this relative Fukaya category. A key application is to the study of Lagrangian cobordisms: a Lagrangian cobordism between Legendrians Λ0 and Λ1 in ∂U × ℝ gives a functor between the relative Fukaya categories of (U, Λ0) and (U, Λ1), and the induced map on symplectic cohomology recovers the map on Legendrian contact homology. The relative symplectic cohomology also satisfies a Mayer–Vietoris property: if U = U1 ∪ U2 is a decomposition along a contact hypersurface, then there is a long exact sequence relating SH*(U), SH*(U1), SH*(U2), and SH*(U1 ∩ U2). This is used to compute the symplectic cohomology of plumbings and of complements of divisors by decomposing them into simpler pieces.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category of Plumbings and the ADE Classification

The Fukaya category of a plumbing of cotangent bundles of spheres according to an ADE Dynkin diagram provides a geometric realization of the derived category of representations of the corresponding preprojective algebra. Let Γ be a simply-laced Dynkin diagram of type An, Dn, or E6,7,8. The plumbing manifold PΓ is constructed by taking a copy of T*Sn for each vertex of Γ and identifying the zero sections according to the edges. The resulting manifold is a Weinstein manifold of dimension 2n with a Lagrangian skeleton given by the union of the zero sections, which intersect transversely at points corresponding to the edges. The wrapped Fukaya category W(PΓ) is generated by the Lagrangian spheres Li corresponding to the vertices. Lagrangian Correspondences and Wehrheim–Woodward Functoriality

A Lagrangian correspondence from a symplectic manifold M0 to M1 is a Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ M0− × M1 where M0− denotes M0 with the opposite symplectic form. Composition of correspondences L01 ⊂ M0− × M1 and L12 ⊂ M1− × M2 is defined by the geometric composition L01 ∘ L12 = π02((L01 × L12) ∩ (M0 × Δ1 × M2)) where Δ1 is the diagonal in M1− × M1. When the intersection is transverse and the projection π02 is an embedding, the composition is a smooth Lagrangian correspondence. Wehrheim and Woodward constructed a functorial framework for Fukaya categories using quilted Floer homology: to each symplectic manifold M they associate the Fukaya category Fuk(M), and to a Lagrangian correspondence L ⊂ M0− × M1 they associate an A∞ bimodule L♯ over Fuk(M0) and Fuk(M1). The key theorem states that for composable correspondences L01, L12 with clean intersection, there is a quasi-isomorphism of bimodules L01♯ ⊗Fuk(M1) L12♯ ≃ (L01 ∘ L12)♯. This provides a categorical lift of geometric composition. The construction uses quilted pseudoholomorphic curves — maps from a Riemann surface with strip-like ends and interior seams labeled by the correspondences — satisfying Cauchy–Riemann equations with seam conditions given by the Lagrangian correspondences. The A∞ operations on the bimodule are defined by counting rigid quilted curves with boundary components mapping to Lagrangians in the Fukaya categories. A fundamental application is the proof of the symplectic invariance of the Fukaya category under symplectomorphisms: the graph of a symplectomorphism φ: M0 → M1 is a Lagrangian correspondence, and the associated bimodule induces an equivalence Fuk(M0) ≃ Fuk(M1). The framework extends to generalized Lagrangian correspondences — sequences of correspondences with intermediate symplectic manifolds — which model the composition of non-transverse correspondences. This leads to the notion of the extended Fukaya category, where objects are generalized correspondences from a point to M, and morphisms are given by quilted Floer homology. The Wehrheim–Woodward theory also provides a geometric interpretation of the Donaldson–Fukaya category of a symplectic manifold with a Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ M: it is equivalent to the category of modules over the endomorphism algebra of the correspondence L ⊂ pt− × M. Recent work by Ma'u, Wehrheim, and Woodward extends the theory to correspondences with boundary and corners, enabling a functorial treatment of symplectic manifolds with boundary and the relative Fukaya category.

The Fukaya–Seidel Category of Lefschetz Fibrations

Let π: E → ℂ be a Lefschetz fibration with fiber a symplectic manifold F and critical values z1, …, zμ. The Fukaya–Seidel category FS(π) is an A∞ category whose objects are vanishing paths — embedded paths γ: [0,1] → ℂ with γ(0) a regular value, γ(1) = zi, and γ avoiding other critical values — together with a choice of grading and spin structure on the associated vanishing cycle Vγ ⊂ F. The morphism space between two vanishing paths γ0, γ1 is the Floer cochain complex CF*(Vγ0, Vγ1) in the fiber F, defined using a Hamiltonian that is quadratic at infinity near the boundary of the fiber. The A∞ operations count pseudoholomorphic disks in the total space E with boundary on the Lagrangian thimbles Δγ = ∪t∈[0,1] Vγ(t) (the union of vanishing cycles along the path) and interior punctures mapping to the critical fibers. A crucial feature is the directed nature of the category: if the vanishing paths are ordered by their argument at the critical values, the A∞ operations vanish unless the sequence of paths is cyclically ordered. This makes FS(π) a directed A∞ category, which simplifies computations and allows a combinatorial description via the Seidel quiver. The Seidel quiver has vertices corresponding to vanishing paths and arrows corresponding to intersection points of vanishing cycles; the A∞ relations are encoded by the Ginzburg differential graded algebra of the quiver with potential. Seidel proved that the derived category of FS(π) is equivalent to the derived Fukaya category of the total space E when E is exact and the fibration is tame at infinity. For a Lefschetz fibration with a section, the monodromy around all critical values is a Dehn twist along the vanishing cycle, and the product of these Dehn twists gives the monodromy of the fibration. The Fukaya–Seidel category of the mirror Lefschetz fibration (obtained by exchanging the roles of the base and fiber in the SYZ picture) is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on the mirror variety, providing a geometric realization of homological mirror symmetry for affine varieties. The category also carries a natural action of the braid group Bμ by mutation of vanishing paths, which corresponds to the action of the mapping class group of the base on the Fukaya category. The directed structure implies that the Hochschild cohomology of FS(π) is concentrated in non-negative degrees, and the zeroth Hochschild cohomology is the Jacobian ring of the superpotential, matching the quantum cohomology of the mirror. Recent work by Ganatra, Pardon, and Shende identifies the wrapped Fukaya category of the total space with the ind-completion of the Fukaya–Seidel category, and the sectorial cover of the total space by thimbles realizes the localization theorem for the wrapped category.

Worked Computation: Fukaya–Seidel Category of the A2 Milnor Fibration
Consider the Lefschetz fibration π: ℂ2 → ℂ given by π(x, y) = x2 + y3. This is the A2 singularity with two critical values. Describe the Fukaya–Seidel category FS(π) and its quiver.
The fibration has two critical points with critical values z1, z2. The regular fiber is a punctured torus. Choose a base point z0 and two vanishing paths γ1, γ2 with arguments ordered as arg(γ1) < arg(γ2). The vanishing cycles V1, V2 are simple closed curves on the torus intersecting transversely at one point. The Seidel quiver has two vertices 1, 2 and one arrow a: 1 → 2 corresponding to the intersection point. The A∞ operations are determined by the directed structure: μ1 = 0, μ2(a, a) = 0 (no composable pairs), and higher operations vanish. The Ginzburg dg algebra is the path algebra of the quiver with differential zero. The derived category of FS(π) is equivalent to the derived category of representations of the A2 quiver, which is also the derived category of the A2 preprojective algebra. This matches the wrapped Fukaya category of the plumbing of two cotangent bundles of spheres intersecting at one point, confirming the ADE correspondence for the A2 case.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Directed A∞ Categories
When a problem involves a Lefschetz fibration or a quiver with potential, check whether the A∞ category is directed. Directedness means there is a partial order on objects such that all morphisms go from lower to higher objects. This implies μk = 0 for any sequence that is not strictly increasing in the order. In the Fukaya–Seidel category, the order is given by the argument of the vanishing paths at the critical values. This property drastically simplifies computations of Hochschild cohomology and the classification of modules. If asked to compute the derived category of a Fukaya–Seidel category, first identify the quiver and potential, then use the fact that the derived category is equivalent to the derived category of the Ginzburg dg algebra, which for directed categories is formal (quasi-isomorphic to its cohomology algebra).

Symplectic Homology and the Bourgeois–Oancea Isomorphism

Let (W, λ) be a Liouville domain with contact boundary (Y, ξ = ker λ|Y). The symplectic homology SH*(W) is the Floer homology of a Hamiltonian H that is linear at infinity with positive slope on the completion Ŵ = W ∪Y ([0,∞) × Y). The chain complex is generated by 1-periodic orbits of the Hamiltonian vector field XH, which consist of constant orbits in W (corresponding to critical points of a Morse function on W) and non-constant orbits in the cylindrical end (corresponding to Reeb orbits on Y with period equal to the slope). The differential counts Floer cylinders in Ŵ. The Bourgeois–Oancea isomorphism constructs a canonical isomorphism SH*+(W) ≃ CH*−(Y) between the positive symplectic homology of W (the quotient of SH*(W) by the image of the Morse homology of W) and the negative contact homology of Y (the contact homology generated by good Reeb orbits with a grading shift). The isomorphism is defined by counting pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectization ℝ × Y with one positive puncture asymptotic to a Reeb orbit and several negative punctures asymptotic to Reeb orbits, together with a marked point on the boundary mapping to a Morse function on W. The key analytical input is a stretching-the-neck argument: one considers a family of almost complex structures on Ŵ that stretch the cylindrical end, and shows that in the limit, Floer cylinders in Ŵ break into a building consisting of a holomorphic plane in W (contributing to Morse homology) and a holomorphic curve in the symplectization (contributing to contact homology). The isomorphism respects the S1-equivariant structures: the Conley–Zehnder index on the symplectic side corresponds to the grading on contact homology shifted by n−3, and the S1-action on symplectic homology (given by rotating the domain of the Floer cylinder) corresponds to the BV operator on contact homology. The isomorphism also intertwines the Viterbo transfer map SH*(V) → SH*(W) for an inclusion of Liouville domains with the map on contact homology induced by the inclusion of contact boundaries. A major application is the computation of symplectic homology of affine varieties: if W is a Liouville domain that is the complement of an ample divisor D in a projective variety X, then SH*(W) is isomorphic to the quantum cohomology of X localized at the quantum parameter of D. The Bourgeois–Oancea isomorphism identifies this with the contact homology of the contact boundary, which can be computed via the Legendrian contact homology of the divisor. The isomorphism extends to a map of exact triangles relating the symplectic homology of a Liouville domain, the symplectic homology of its completion, and the contact homology of its boundary, providing a powerful tool for computing symplectic invariants of Weinstein manifolds by induction on the handle decomposition.

The Abouzaid Mirror Symmetry for Toric Varieties

Let X be a compact toric variety of dimension n with moment polytope Δ ⊂ ℝn. The mirror of X is the Landau–Ginzburg model ((ℂ*)n, W) where the superpotential W: (ℂ*)n → ℂ is the Laurent polynomial W(z1, …, zn) = ∑v∈vertices(Δ) zv (with appropriate coefficients determined by the toric data). Abouzaid proved that the Fukaya category of X (with appropriate Lagrangian branes) is equivalent to the category of matrix factorizations of W on (ℂ*)n. The construction uses the toric moment map μ: X → Δ. The Lagrangian torus fibers μ−1(u) for u in the interior of Δ are objects of the Fukaya category. The morphism spaces between two fibers are generated by intersection points of the fibers after a Hamiltonian perturbation, which correspond to lattice points in the dual polytope. The A∞ operations count pseudoholomorphic disks with boundary on the fibers. By the classification of holomorphic disks in toric varieties (Cho–Oh), every such disk projects to a triangle in Δ with vertices at the fiber positions and edges parallel to the facets of Δ. The area of the disk is given by the symplectic area of the triangle, and the boundary of the disk wraps around the torus fiber according to the primitive normal vectors of the facets. This leads to a combinatorial formula for the A∞ operations: the structure constants are counts of lattice points in certain polytopes, weighted by the symplectic area. The resulting A∞ algebra is the toric Fukaya algebra, which is quasi-isomorphic to the Jacobian ring of W (the quantum cohomology of X). To obtain the full derived equivalence, one considers the split-closed derived Fukaya category of X generated by the torus fibers and their iterated mapping cones (which correspond to Lagrangian branes supported on toric divisors). On the mirror side, the category of matrix factorizations MF(W) is generated by the Koszul matrix factorizations associated to the critical points of W. Abouzaid constructs an explicit A∞ functor by sending a torus fiber at u to the matrix factorization given by the Koszul complex of the sequence (z1 − eu1, …, zn − eun) in the ring of functions on (ℂ*)n. The functor intertwines the A∞ operations with the Yoneda products in MF(W). The proof uses the fact that the moduli spaces of pseudoholomorphic disks in X are in bijection with the moduli spaces of gradient flow trees of the superpotential W on (ℂ*)n, via the SYZ transform. This equivalence extends to the relative setting: for a toric divisor D ⊂ X, the Fukaya category of the complement X D is equivalent to the category of matrix factorizations of W on the complement of the critical locus. The Abouzaid equivalence is a cornerstone of homological mirror symmetry for Fano varieties and provides a completely computable framework for the Fukaya category of toric varieties.

The Chekanov–Eliashberg Algebra and Legendrian Contact Homology

Let Λ ⊂ (Y, ξ) be a Legendrian submanifold in a contact manifold of dimension 2n−1. The Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra CE*(Λ) is a differential graded algebra over ℤ2 (or ℤ with appropriate orientation data) generated by the Reeb chords of Λ — paths tangent to the Reeb vector field with endpoints on Λ. The grading of a Reeb chord c is given by the Conley–Zehnder index of the linearized Reeb flow along c relative to a trivialization of ξ|Λ, shifted by n−3. The differential ∂c counts rigid pseudoholomorphic disks in the symplectization ℝ × Y with boundary on ℝ × Λ, one positive puncture asymptotic to c, and several negative punctures asymptotic to Reeb chords c1, …, ck. The disk contributes the monomial c1 ⋯ ck to ∂c. The differential satisfies ∂2 = 0, making CE*(Λ) a dg algebra. The Legendrian contact homology LCH*(Λ) is the homology of CE*(Λ). A fundamental theorem of Chekanov and Eliashberg states that the stable tame isomorphism class of CE*(Λ) is a Legendrian isotopy invariant. Two Legendrians that are Legendrian isotopic have Chekanov–Eliashberg algebras that are related by a sequence of elementary algebraic operations: stabilization (adding a pair of generators a, b with ∂a = b), isomorphism, and a certain class of automorphisms called tame isomorphisms. The algebra is highly computable for Legendrians in standard contact ℝ3 (or more generally in 1-jet spaces J1(M)) using the front projection. In the front projection (the projection to the base M × ℝz), Reeb chords correspond to crossings of the front diagram, and the differential counts immersed polygons in the front with corners at crossings. This gives a purely combinatorial algorithm for computing CE*(Λ) for Legendrian knots in ℝ3. The Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra admits augmentations — dg algebra maps to the ground field — which correspond to exact Lagrangian fillings of Λ in the symplectization. The set of augmentations carries a natural structure of an A∞ category, the augmentation category Aug(Λ), whose objects are augmentations and whose morphism spaces are given by the linearized contact homology. The augmentation category is equivalent to the category of microlocal sheaves on Λ with singular support in the conormal bundle, realizing the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence for Legendrians. The Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra also carries a natural action of the fundamental group of the space of Legendrians, and its linearized contact homology (the homology of the complex obtained by linearizing the differential at an augmentation) is a powerful invariant that can distinguish Legendrian knots that are smoothly isotopic but not Legendrian isotopic. For example, the Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra of the Legendrian unknot with maximal Thurston–Bennequin number is the algebra generated by one degree 0 generator a with ∂a = 1 + a2, while the Legendrian unknot with lower Thurston–Bennequin number has a different algebra. The theory extends to Legendrian submanifolds with boundary in contact manifolds with boundary, where the differential counts disks with corners on the boundary of the contact manifold, leading to the relative Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra and the theory of Legendrian cobordisms.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Floer Theory for Non‑Exact Symplectic Manifolds

When the symplectic form ω is not exact, the action functional on the loop space acquires a multi‑valued character because the integral of ω over a capping disk depends on the homology class of the disk. The standard construction of Hamiltonian Floer homology therefore requires a choice of a covering of the loop space, typically the universal cover of the contractible loop space, and a Novikov ring that records the symplectic area of capping disks. The differential counts solutions of the Floer equation ∂ₛu + Jₜ(u)(∂ₜu − Xₜ(u)) = 0 with asymptotic conditions given by 1‑periodic Hamiltonian orbits. Compactness of the moduli spaces relies on Gromov’s compactness theorem for J‑holomorphic curves together with a priori energy bounds that involve the symplectic area of the capping disks. The resulting Floer homology groups HFₖ(H; Λ) are modules over the Novikov ring Λ = ℤ[[q, q⁻¹]] where q records the symplectic area. A fundamental result of Ono and of Fukaya–Ono shows that the isomorphism class of HFₖ(H; Λ) is independent of the Hamiltonian H and the almost complex structure J, yielding a symplectic invariant of (M, ω). When c₁(TM) vanishes on π₂(M), the grading can be lifted to an integer grading; otherwise one obtains a ℤ/2N grading where N is the minimal Chern number. The pair‑of‑pants product on Floer homology, defined by counting solutions on a three‑punctured sphere, equips HFₖ with a ring structure that is isomorphic to the quantum cohomology ring QH*(M) via the Piunikhin–Salamon–Schwarz isomorphism. This isomorphism intertwines the quantum product with the pair‑of‑pants product and identifies the unit in Floer homology with the fundamental class of M. The construction extends to twisted coefficients given by a flat line bundle over M, leading to the notion of Floer homology with local systems, which is essential for the study of Lagrangian Floer homology on non‑exact Lagrangians.

Symplectic Geometry of Spectral Invariants and the Calabi Quasimorphism

Spectral invariants associate to each non‑zero class a ∈ HFₖ(H) a real number c(a, H) defined as the infimum of action values of cycles representing a. They satisfy the triangle inequality c(a ⋆ b, H ⋯ K) ≤ c(a, H) + c(b, K) where ⋆ denotes the pair‑of‑pants product and H ⋯ K is the concatenated Hamiltonian. The spectral invariant of the unit class, c([M], H), defines a homogeneous quasimorphism on the universal cover of the Hamiltonian diffeomorphism group Ham(M, ω), known as the Calabi quasimorphism when M is closed and symplectically aspherical. On open manifolds or when ω is exact, the Calabi homomorphism is a genuine homomorphism; on closed manifolds it is only a quasimorphism whose defect measures the failure of the Calabi invariant to be additive. The work of Entov–Polterovich showed that the Calabi quasimorphism gives rise to a partial order on Ham(M, ω) via the relation φ ≤ ψ iff c([M], H_φ) ≤ c([M], H_ψ) for normalized Hamiltonians. This order is bi‑invariant and has deep consequences for the geometry of Hofer’s metric: the Hofer distance between φ and ψ is bounded below by |c([M], H_φ) − c([M], H_ψ)|. Moreover, spectral invariants can be used to define a symplectic capacity c_SH(M, ω) = sup_H c([M], H) where the supremum runs over all normalized Hamiltonians. This capacity coincides with the Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder capacity on symplectically aspherical manifolds and provides a bridge between Floer theory and symplectic embedding problems. The computation of spectral invariants for specific Hamiltonians, such as the quadratic Hamiltonian on ℂPⁿ, yields explicit values that can be used to detect non‑displaceable Lagrangian submanifolds via the Oh–Schwarz spectral invariant.

Worked Example: Spectral Invariant of the Unit on ℂP¹
Let M = ℂP¹ with the Fubini–Study form ω normalized so that ∫ℂP¹ ω = 1. Consider the autonomous Hamiltonian H(z) = π|z|²/(1+|z|²) on the affine chart ℂ ⊂ ℂP¹, extended smoothly to the whole manifold. Compute the spectral invariant c([M], H) of the fundamental class.
The Hamiltonian generates the rotation action of S¹ on ℂP¹ with fixed points at 0 and ∞. The 1‑periodic orbits are the fixed points and the equator (which is a Morse–Bott family). For a non‑degenerate perturbation, the fixed points give two orbits with Conley–Zehnder indices 0 and 2. The action values are A(0) = 0 and A(∞) = 1. The Floer complex is generated by these two orbits; the differential vanishes for index reasons. The unit class [M] corresponds to the maximum orbit at ∞. Hence c([M], H) = A(∞) = 1. This equals the symplectic area of ℂP¹, confirming that the spectral invariant of the unit recovers the volume in this case.
Exam Strategy
When asked to compute or estimate a spectral invariant, first identify the action spectrum of the Hamiltonian. For autonomous Hamiltonians on toric manifolds, the action values are integrals of the moment map over the fibers. The unit class always corresponds to the maximum of the Hamiltonian (or the minimum, depending on grading conventions). Use the triangle inequality to bound spectral invariants of products, and remember that c([M], H) is monotone with respect to the pointwise order of Hamiltonians.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category of Symplectic Manifolds with Divisors and the Relative Fukaya Category

Let (M, ω) be a closed symplectic manifold and D ⊂ M a smooth symplectic divisor representing the Poincaré dual of a class [D] ∈ H²(M; ℤ). The complement M D carries a natural Liouville structure when ω is exact near D, but in general one works with the relative Fukaya category Fuk(M, D) whose objects are Lagrangian submanifolds L ⊂ M that intersect D transversely and are equipped with a grading and a spin structure. Morphism spaces are given by Lagrangian Floer cohomology HF*(L₀, L₁) where the differential counts J‑holomorphic strips with boundary on L₀ ∪ L₁ and with interior marked points mapping to D, weighted by the intersection multiplicity with D. This yields a curved A∞‑category over the Novikov ring Λ_D = ℤ[[T]] where T records the intersection number with D. The curvature term m₀ is a sum of contributions from holomorphic disks with one boundary component on L and one interior marked point on D; it vanishes precisely when L is disjoint from D or when the count of such disks cancels. The relative Fukaya category is related to the absolute Fukaya category of M by a localization sequence: there is a functor Fuk(M, D) → Fuk(M) that forgets the divisor, and its kernel consists of objects supported on D. When D is an anticanonical divisor, the relative Fukaya category is expected to be equivalent to the Fukaya category of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model (Y, W) where W is a superpotential with critical points corresponding to the intersection points of L with D. This is the content of the Auroux–Katzarkov–Orlov conjecture, proved in many toric cases. The relative Fukaya category also admits a deformation theory governed by the Hochschild cohomology HH*(Fuk(M, D)), which classifies infinitesimal deformations of the A∞‑structure. In the case of a normal crossings divisor D = D₁ ∪ … ∪ D_k, the relative Fukaya category can be described by a sectorial cover of the base of the moment map, leading to a combinatorial model using tropical geometry.

Symplectic Geometry of the Symplectic Topology of Hamiltonian PDEs and Infinite‑Dimensional Symplectic Manifolds

Many evolutionary PDEs of mathematical physics, such as the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, the Korteweg–de Vries equation, and the Euler equation for ideal fluids, can be formulated as Hamiltonian systems on infinite‑dimensional symplectic manifolds. The phase space is typically a Sobolev space H^s(M) of sections of a vector bundle over a finite‑dimensional manifold M, equipped with a weak symplectic form ω(u, v) = ∫_M ⟨u, Jv⟩ dx where J is a compatible almost complex structure. The Hamiltonian functional H: H^s → ℝ is often given by an integral of a density involving derivatives of the field. The Hamiltonian vector field X_H is defined by ω(X_H, ·) = dH, but because ω is only weakly non‑degenerate, X_H may not exist as a smooth vector field on the whole Sobolev space; one works instead with a scale of Hilbert spaces or with the Fréchet manifold of smooth functions. The flow of X_H preserves the symplectic form and the Hamiltonian, giving rise to a symplectic dynamics on the infinite‑dimensional manifold. KAM theory for PDEs, developed by Kuksin, Pöschel, and Wayne, shows that for small perturbations of integrable Hamiltonian PDEs, a positive measure set of invariant tori survives, implying quasi‑periodic solutions. The symplectic topology of these infinite‑dimensional manifolds is studied via the construction of Floer homology for Hamiltonian PDEs, where the Floer equation becomes a parabolic PDE (the heat flow for the action functional). Compactness of the moduli spaces requires uniform bounds on the Sobolev norms of solutions, which are obtained from energy estimates and the Hamiltonian structure. The resulting Floer homology groups are isomorphic to the singular homology of the underlying finite‑dimensional manifold in the case of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation on a compact manifold, as shown by Abbondandolo–Majer. This provides a bridge between infinite‑dimensional symplectic topology and finite‑dimensional Morse theory.

Symplectic Geometry of the Symplectic Topology of Symplectic Manifolds with Corners and the Relative Fukaya Category

A symplectic manifold with corners is a manifold M equipped with a symplectic form ω and a collection of boundary hypersurfaces H₁, …, H_k that intersect transversely, such that ω restricts to a symplectic form on each stratum. The typical example is a Liouville domain with a codimension‑k corner, which arises as the product of k Liouville domains or as the symplectic cut of a Hamiltonian torus action at a singular level. The relative Fukaya category Fuk(M) for such a space is defined using Lagrangian submanifolds that are either disjoint from the corners or intersect them in a prescribed way (e.g., as a product of Lagrangians in each factor). The A∞‑operations count J‑holomorphic polygons with boundary on the Lagrangians and with corners mapping to the strata of the boundary. The gluing of polygons across corners is governed by a combinatorial category of “cornered” Riemann surfaces, leading to a sectorial cover of the moduli space. The work of Ganatra–Pardon–Shende shows that the relative Fukaya category of a cornered Liouville domain is equivalent to the category of modules over the partially wrapped Fukaya category of the smooth part, with the sectors corresponding to the corners. This equivalence is a higher‑dimensional generalization of the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence for Legendrians with boundary. The relative Fukaya category also admits a localization theorem: inverting the morphisms corresponding to Reeb chords that lie entirely in a given sector yields the wrapped Fukaya category of the corresponding Liouville sector. This framework provides a systematic way to compute the Fukaya category of symplectic manifolds obtained by symplectic sums or cuts, by decomposing them into cornered pieces and applying a categorical version of the Seifert–van Kampen theorem.

Symplectic Geometry of the Giroux Correspondence and Open Book Decompositions

The Giroux correspondence establishes a bijection between contact structures on a closed oriented 3‑manifold up to isotopy and open book decompositions up to positive stabilization. An open book decomposition of a 3‑manifold M consists of a link B (the binding) and a fibration π : M B → S1 whose fibers are Seifert surfaces for B. The monodromy of the fibration is a diffeomorphism φ of the fiber Σ fixing the boundary, and the contact structure is supported by the open book if it can be represented by a contact form whose Reeb flow is transverse to the pages and tangent to the binding. Giroux proved that every contact structure is supported by some open book, and two open books support isotopic contact structures iff they are related by a sequence of positive stabilizations (adding a 1‑handle to the page and composing the monodromy with a right‑handed Dehn twist along the new curve). This correspondence translates contact‑topological questions into mapping‑class‑group problems. For instance, the classification of tight contact structures on lens spaces L(p, q) by Honda reduces to the study of factorizations of the monodromy into right‑handed Dehn twists. In higher dimensions, Giroux and Mohsen extended the correspondence to contact manifolds of dimension 2n+1 with open books whose pages are Liouville domains of dimension 2n; the monodromy is then a compactly supported symplectomorphism. The contact manifold is the boundary of the symplectic manifold obtained by attaching a symplectic 2‑handle along the binding, and the correspondence becomes a bijection between contact structures up to isotopy and open books up to positive stabilization (where stabilization adds a Weinstein 1‑handle to the page and composes the monodromy with a right‑handed Dehn twist). This higher‑dimensional version underpins the classification of overtwisted contact structures by Borman–Eliashberg–Murphy and the flexible/non‑flexible dichotomy for Weinstein manifolds. The Giroux correspondence also provides a combinatorial framework for computing contact homology and symplectic homology: the Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra of the binding in the contact boundary of a Liouville domain is isomorphic to the wrapped Fukaya category of the page, and the monodromy acts by a symplectomorphism inducing an autoequivalence. This perspective is central to the work of Ganatra–Pardon–Shende on the sectorial cover and the localization theorem for wrapped Fukaya categories.

Symplectic Geometry of the Contact Surgery and the Weinstein Handlebody Theory

Weinstein handlebody theory gives a complete description of Weinstein manifolds (exact symplectic manifolds with a complete Liouville vector field that is gradient‑like for an exhausting Morse function) in terms of handles attached along isotropic spheres in the contact boundary. A Weinstein k‑handle is a symplectic manifold diffeomorphic to Dk × D2n−k equipped with a standard Liouville form; its core is the isotropic disk Dk × {0} and its cocore is the Lagrangian disk {0} × D2n−k. The attaching region of a k‑handle is an isotropic Sk−1 in the contact boundary of the sublevel set, equipped with a framing of its conformal symplectic normal bundle. For k ≤ n, the attaching sphere is isotropic; for k = n it is Legendrian, and the framing is determined by a choice of Lagrangian subspace in the contact plane field along the sphere. Weinstein proved that every Weinstein manifold admits a handle decomposition with handles of index at most n, and conversely any such handle attachment yields a Weinstein manifold. The contact surgery diagram of a Weinstein domain encodes the Legendrian attaching spheres of the n‑handles together with their Thurston–Bennequin framings. Eliashberg showed that the contact boundary of a Weinstein domain is determined up to contactomorphism by the Legendrian link of attaching spheres, and that two Weinstein domains are deformation equivalent iff their handlebody diagrams are related by handle slides, creations/cancellations, and Legendrian isotopies. This theory provides a powerful tool for constructing symplectic fillings of contact manifolds: given a contact manifold (Y, ξ) presented by a Legendrian surgery diagram on (S2n−1, ξstd), one can fill it by attaching Weinstein handles according to the diagram. The resulting filling is unique up to deformation equivalence if the diagram is “flexible” (all Legendrians are loose in the sense of Murphy). For non‑flexible diagrams, the classification of fillings is a deep problem related to the symplectic homology of the filling and the wrapped Fukaya category. The Weinstein handlebody theory also underlies the h‑principle for flexible Weinstein manifolds: any formal symplectic structure on a flexible Weinstein manifold is homotopic to a genuine one. This was used by Cieliebak–Eliashberg to classify flexible Weinstein manifolds up to symplectomorphism by their homotopy type and the homotopy class of the almost complex structure.

Worked Problem: Contact Surgery on a Legendrian Unknot
Consider the standard contact 3‑sphere (S3, ξstd) and a Legendrian unknot L with Thurston–Bennequin number tb(L) = −1 and rotation number rot(L) = 0. Perform contact (+1)‑surgery along L. Describe the resulting contact manifold and its Weinstein filling.
The Legendrian unknot with tb = −1 is the standard Legendrian unknot in (S3, ξstd). Contact (+1)‑surgery along L means we remove a standard neighborhood of L (a solid torus with convex boundary of dividing slope 1/tb = −1) and glue back a solid torus so that the meridian of the new torus has slope 0 (the contact framing). The resulting contact manifold is (S3, ξstd) again, because (+1)‑surgery on the standard Legendrian unknot is a contactomorphism (it corresponds to a Legendrian stabilization followed by a contact (−1)‑surgery, which is a contactomorphism by a theorem of Ding–Geiges). The Weinstein filling is the 4‑ball B4 with a 2‑handle attached along L with framing tb − 1 = −2. Symplectic Geometry of the Symplectic Cohomology of Complements of Divisors and the McLean Theorem

Let (X, ω) be a closed symplectic manifold and D ⊂ X a smooth symplectic divisor Poincaré dual to k[ω] for some positive integer k. The complement M = X D is a Liouville domain whose contact boundary is the circle bundle over D with Euler class k[ω]|D. McLean proved that the symplectic cohomology SH*(M) is isomorphic to the quantum cohomology QH*(X) localized at the quantum variable q corresponding to the class of a line intersecting D once, modulo the relation that the quantum product with the class [D] is invertible. More precisely, there is a long exact sequence relating the symplectic cohomology of M, the quantum cohomology of X, and the quantum cohomology of D, which degenerates to an isomorphism SH*(M) ≅ QH*(X)[q−1] / ([D] ⋆ − qk) when D is ample. This result has profound implications for mirror symmetry: if X is a Fano variety and D is an anticanonical divisor, then M is a Calabi–Yau variety and its symplectic cohomology computes the Jacobian ring of the Landau–Ginzburg mirror. The McLean isomorphism identifies the quantum cohomology of X with the symplectic cohomology of M, which by the Ganatra–Pardon–Shende theorem is the Hochschild cohomology of the wrapped Fukaya category of M. This provides a symplectic‑geometric proof of the closed‑string mirror symmetry conjecture for Fano varieties. The proof uses a stretching‑the‑neck argument: one degenerates the symplectic form on X to a form with a long cylindrical neck along D, and analyzes the behavior of Floer trajectories in the limit. The key technical tool is the SFT compactness theorem for holomorphic buildings, which shows that the Floer differential on M splits into a differential on X and a differential on the symplectization of the contact boundary. The resulting spectral sequence converges to SH*(M) and its E2 page is the localized quantum cohomology of X. The degeneration of this spectral sequence is equivalent to the statement that the quantum product with [D] is nilpotent on the kernel of localization, which holds when D is ample by a positivity argument due to McDuff–Salamon.

Symplectic Geometry of the Symplectic Topology of Symplectic 4‑Manifolds and the Gromov–Taubes Theorem

The Gromov–Taubes theorem states that if a closed 4‑manifold M admits a symplectic form ω, then the Seiberg–Witten invariant SWM(Kω) of the canonical class Kω = −c1(TM, ω) is ±1. Conversely, Taubes proved that if a closed oriented 4‑manifold has a basic class K with SW(K) ≠ 0, then it admits a symplectic form with canonical class ±K. This establishes a complete equivalence between the existence of a symplectic structure and the non‑vanishing of Seiberg–Witten invariants for closed 4‑manifolds with b2+ > 1. The proof uses the fact that the Seiberg–Witten equations on a symplectic 4‑manifold admit a solution given by the spinc structure determined by ω, and that this solution is unique up to gauge equivalence. The non‑vanishing of the invariant follows from a transversality argument showing that the moduli space of solutions is a compact oriented 0‑manifold of signed count ±1. The converse direction is far deeper: Taubes constructs a symplectic form by analyzing the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the Seiberg–Witten equations with a large parameter r. As r → ∞, the solutions concentrate on a union of pseudoholomorphic curves representing the Poincaré dual of K. The limiting object is a “Seiberg–Witten monopole” which gives rise to a symplectic current, and Taubes shows that this current can be perturbed to a genuine symplectic form. This theorem has numerous applications: it implies that the symplectic cone of a 4‑manifold is a union of connected components of the set of classes with positive square, that the Kodaira dimension of a symplectic 4‑manifold is determined by the Seiberg–Witten basic classes, and that the geography problem for symplectic 4‑manifolds is equivalent to the geography problem for Seiberg–Witten basic classes. The Gromov–Taubes theorem also underlies the proof of the Weinstein conjecture in dimension 3 via Taubes’s isomorphism between embedded contact homology and Seiberg–Witten Floer cohomology.

Symplectic Geometry of the Symplectic Topology of Fano Varieties and the Gamma Conjecture

The Gamma conjecture, formulated by Galkin–Golyshev–Iritani, relates the asymptotic behavior of the quantum cohomology of a Fano variety X to the Gamma class of its tangent bundle. Let QH*(X) be the (small) quantum cohomology ring, which is a deformation of the ordinary cohomology ring by genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants. The Dubrovin connection ∇ = d − z−1 ⋆ c1(X) on the trivial bundle over ℂ* with fiber QH*(X) has an irregular singularity at z = 0 and a regular singularity at z = ∞. The Gamma conjecture asserts that the Stokes matrix of this connection at z = 0, computed in a basis of flat sections with prescribed asymptotics, coincides with the Gram matrix of the Euler pairing on the derived category DbCoh(X) with respect to an exceptional collection, up to multiplication by the Gamma class Γ̂X = ∏i Γ(1 + δi) where δi are the Chern roots of TX. Equivalently, the central charges of the exceptional objects, defined as the asymptotics of the flat sections, are given by the integrals ∫X Γ̂X ⋅ ch(E) ⋅ e−c1/z for E in the exceptional collection. This conjecture has been verified for projective spaces, Grassmannians, flag varieties, and del Pezzo surfaces. It provides a precise formulation of the “mirror symmetry” prediction that the quantum cohomology of X is isomorphic to the Jacobian ring of the Landau–Ginzburg mirror, with the Gamma class accounting for the difference between the topological and the categorical Euler pairings. From the symplectic perspective, the Gamma class appears as the asymptotic contribution of the Maslov index in the Floer theory of the monotone Lagrangian torus fibers of the moment map. The Gamma conjecture also implies the “Dubrovin conjecture” that the quantum cohomology of a Fano variety is generically semisimple iff the derived category admits a full exceptional collection. The semisimplicity of quantum cohomology is equivalent to the existence of a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold structure on the quantum cohomology, which in turn is related to the existence of a “Dubrovin connection” with trivial monodromy. The Gamma conjecture thus bridges symplectic topology (quantum cohomology, Gromov–Witten invariants), algebraic geometry (derived categories, exceptional collections), and singularity theory (Stokes matrices, Gamma functions).

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Non‑compact Manifolds: The Extended Moment Map and Kirwan Surjectivity

When a compact Lie group G acts Hamiltonianly on a non‑compact symplectic manifold (M, ω), the ordinary moment map μ : M → 𝔤* need not be proper, and the symplectic quotient μ⁻¹(0)/G can be non‑Hausdorff or empty. The extended moment map, introduced by Jeffrey–Kirwan and later refined by Harada–Karshon, replaces μ by a map μ̃ : M → 𝔤* ⊕ ℝ whose additional component records a properness‑enforcing function (often a proper Morse‑Bott function invariant under the maximal torus). This construction yields a compactified quotient μ̃⁻¹(0)/G that carries a natural symplectic structure on its smooth locus and a stratified symplectic structure on the whole. Kirwan surjectivity — the statement that the equivariant cohomology map H*_G(M) → H*(μ⁻¹(0)/G) is surjective — extends to this setting provided the extended moment map is proper and the action is equivariantly formal. The proof uses the Morse‑Bott stratification of the norm‑square ‖μ̃‖² and the Atiyah–Bott localization formula for the torus action. A key technical point is that the Kirwan map factors through the equivariant cohomology of the critical sets of ‖μ̃‖², and surjectivity follows from the fact that the inclusion of the minimum (the zero level) induces an isomorphism in equivariant cohomology after inverting the equivariant Euler classes of the normal bundles. This result underpins the computation of the cohomology of hyperkähler quotients and of moduli spaces of parabolic Higgs bundles.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Non‑compact Manifolds: Properness, Cuts, and the Symplectic Quotient

Properness of the moment map is equivalent to the condition that the G‑action on μ⁻¹(0) is proper, which in turn guarantees that the quotient μ⁻¹(0)/G is a Hausdorff symplectic orbifold. When properness fails, the symplectic cut construction of Lerman provides a systematic way to produce a compact symplectic manifold with a residual torus action whose quotient recovers the desired reduced space. Given a Hamiltonian torus T action with moment map μ : M → 𝔱* and a regular value ξ, one forms the product M × ℂ with the diagonal T‑action and moment map μ̃(m, z) = μ(m) + ‖z‖² ξ. The symplectic cut at level ξ is the quotient μ̃⁻¹(0)/T. This space is compact if μ is proper on the preimage of a neighbourhood of ξ. The cut replaces the non‑compact directions by a divisor isomorphic to the symplectic reduction at ξ, and the original reduced space embeds as the complement of this divisor. The construction is functorial with respect to symplectic embeddings and commutes with further reductions. In the non‑abelian case, one first reduces by a maximal torus and then applies the abelian cut, yielding the “non‑abelian symplectic cut” of Meinrenken–Sjamaar. The cut also interacts with the Duistermaat–Heckman measure: the pushforward of the Liouville measure under the moment map changes by a piecewise polynomial term supported on the cutting hyperplane, reflecting the contribution of the exceptional divisor.

Worked Example: Symplectic Cut of ℂ² by a Circle Action
Let S¹ act on ℂ² with weights (1, −1), moment map μ(z₁, z₂) = ½(|z₁|² − |z₂|²). Perform the symplectic cut at level ξ = 1 and describe the resulting space.
The product ℂ² × ℂ carries the diagonal S¹ action with moment map μ̃(z₁, z₂, w) = ½(|z₁|² − |z₂|²) + |w|². The zero level μ̃⁻¹(0) consists of points satisfying |z₁|² − |z₂|² + 2|w|² = 0. The quotient by S¹ is obtained by setting u = |z₁|², v = |z₂|², t = |w|² with u − v + 2t = 0 and identifying phases. This is the total space of the line bundle O(−1) ⊕ O(1) over ℙ¹, compactified by the divisor t = 0 which is the original reduced space μ⁻¹(1)/S¹ ≅ ℙ¹. The cut space is a smooth symplectic manifold diffeomorphic to the blow‑up of ℂ² at the origin, with the exceptional divisor corresponding to the cut level.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Group Actions on Orbifolds: The Quantum Kirwan Map and Verlinde Formula

For a compact symplectic orbifold (X, ω) with a Hamiltonian action of a compact Lie group G, the Chen–Ruan orbifold cohomology H*CR(X) carries a quantum product deformed by orbifold Gromov–Witten invariants. The quantum Kirwan map κ_Q : QH*_G(X) → QH*CR(X//G) is defined by counting G‑equivariant pseudoholomorphic maps from orbifold Riemann surfaces to X that represent classes in the twisted sectors of the quotient stack [X/G]. When X is a compact Kähler manifold and G a torus, the map can be computed via the abelian/non‑abelian correspondence: the equivariant quantum cohomology of X is isomorphic to the quantum cohomology of the GIT quotient after a change of variables that identifies the equivariant parameters with the quantum parameters of the quotient. The Verlinde formula appears as the specialization of the quantum Kirwan map at roots of unity: the dimension of the space of conformal blocks for the loop group LG at level k equals the evaluation of the quantum Kirwan map on the fundamental class of the moduli stack of flat G‑bundles. This identification, proved by Teleman using the loop group version of the Freed–Hopkins–Teleman theorem, shows that the fusion ring of the Wess–Zumino–Witten model is isomorphic to the quantum cohomology of the moduli space of parabolic bundles. The quantum Kirwan map is surjective onto the small quantum cohomology of the symplectic quotient, and its kernel is generated by the quantum Stanley–Reisner relations coming from the unstable loci in the GIT quotient.

Symplectic Geometry of Coisotropic Submanifolds: Reduction, Dirac Structures, and the Gotay–Tuynman Theorem

A submanifold C ⊂ (M, ω) is coisotropic if its symplectic orthogonal TC^ω is contained in TC. The characteristic distribution TC^ω is integrable and defines a foliation whose leaf space, when smooth, inherits a symplectic form — this is coisotropic reduction. Gotay and Tuynman showed that if a Hamiltonian G‑action preserves a coisotropic submanifold C and the moment map μ satisfies μ(C) ⊂ 𝔤* lying in a clean intersection with the annihilator of the Lie algebra of the stabilizer, then the reduced space μ⁻¹(0) ∩ C / G is symplectomorphic to the coisotropic reduction of C by the induced G‑action. In the language of Dirac geometry, a coisotropic submanifold carries a natural Dirac structure whose presymplectic leaves are the characteristic leaves. The reduction of this Dirac structure by a Hamiltonian group action yields the Dirac structure on the quotient, and the Gotay–Tuynman theorem is the statement that the two reduction procedures commute. This framework extends to the case where C is a brane in the sense of Kapustin–Orlov: a coisotropic brane with a flat line bundle on its leaves defines an object in the Fukaya category of M, and its image under the reduction functor is the corresponding brane on the reduced space. The theorem also underlies the construction of the “symplectic category” of Weinstein, where morphisms are Lagrangian correspondences and coisotropic submanifolds appear as the units for composition.

Symplectic Geometry of Hamiltonian Diffeomorphism Groups and the Calabi Invariant

The group Ham(M, ω) of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms of a closed symplectic manifold carries a natural bi‑invariant metric introduced by Hofer. A complementary invariant is the Calabi homomorphism, defined on the universal cover of Ham(M, ω) when (M, ω) is exact or has a boundary. For a Hamiltonian isotopy φ_t generated by a normalized Hamiltonian H_t, the Calabi invariant is the time‑average of the integral of H_t against the Liouville volume form: Cal(φ) = ∫01 ∫_M H_t ω^n dt. This map descends to a homomorphism from the universal cover widetilde{Ham}(M, ω) to ℝ and vanishes on the commutator subgroup, making it a fundamental tool for detecting non‑simplicity of Ham(M, ω). On closed manifolds the Calabi invariant does not descend to Ham(M, ω) itself, but its homogenization, the asymptotic Calabi invariant, defines a homogeneous quasimorphism on Ham(M, ω). Entov and Polterovich used spectral invariants from Hamiltonian Floer homology to construct a family of such quasimorphisms, proving that Ham(M, ω) has infinite fragmentation norm and admits many non‑trivial homogeneous quasimorphisms. The Calabi invariant also appears in the study of the flux homomorphism: for a symplectically aspherical manifold the flux map coincides with the Calabi invariant on the identity component of the symplectomorphism group. In the context of Hofer geometry, the Calabi invariant provides a lower bound for the Hofer norm of a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism supported in a displaceable subset, and it is instrumental in proving the existence of infinitely many distinct geodesics in Ham(M, ω) for certain manifolds such as the complex projective space.

Symplectic Geometry of Lagrangian Correspondences and the Weinstein Category

A Lagrangian correspondence from (M₁, ω₁) to (M₂, ω₂) is a Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ M₁⁻ × M₂, where M₁⁻ denotes M₁ with the opposite symplectic form. Composition of correspondences is defined by fiber product followed by projection, and when the intersection is clean the result is again a Lagrangian correspondence. Weinstein proposed that symplectic manifolds form the objects of a category whose morphisms are Lagrangian correspondences, with composition given by the above operation. This category, often called the Weinstein symplectic category, has the diagonal Δ_M ⊂ M⁻ × M as the identity morphism. The functoriality of Fukaya categories with respect to Lagrangian correspondences was established by Ma’u, Wehrheim, and Woodward: a Lagrangian correspondence L induces an A_∞‑functor between the Fukaya categories of the source and target, and composition of correspondences corresponds to composition of functors up to homotopy. This framework underlies the construction of the “wrapped Fukaya category” of a Liouville domain as the Fukaya category of its completion, and it provides a geometric interpretation of the symplectic cohomology ring as the endomorphism algebra of the unit object. In the presence of a Hamiltonian group action, equivariant Lagrangian correspondences give rise to functors between equivariant Fukaya categories, and the Kirwan map can be recovered as the map induced by the correspondence μ⁻¹(0) ⊂ M⁻ × M//G. The Weinstein category also accommodates coisotropic correspondences, which serve as units for a larger “symplectic category” where objects are coisotropic submanifolds and morphisms are Lagrangian correspondences between their characteristic foliations.

Symplectic Geometry of Contact Homology and the Bourgeois–Oancea Isomorphism

Contact homology is a Legendrian invariant of a contact manifold (Y, ξ) defined by counting rigid pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectization ℝ×Y with boundary on the Reeb chords of a non‑degenerate contact form. The chain complex is generated by good Reeb orbits, and the differential counts index‑1 curves in the completed symplectization. Bourgeois and Oancea proved an isomorphism between the linearized contact homology of a contact manifold and the symplectic homology of its symplectic filling, provided the filling is exact and the contact form is non‑degenerate. The map is constructed by stretching the neck along the contact boundary and analyzing the limiting broken curves; the resulting continuation map intertwines the contact differential with the symplectic homology differential. This isomorphism identifies the cylindrical contact homology HC_*(Y) with the positive symplectic homology SH_*+(W) of a Liouville domain W with ∂W = Y. As a consequence, the vanishing of symplectic homology implies the vanishing of contact homology, giving strong obstructions to the existence of exact fillings. The isomorphism also respects the S¹‑action on both theories, yielding an equivalence of the associated BV‑algebras. In the case of a prequantization circle bundle over a symplectic manifold, the Bourgeois–Oancea isomorphism recovers the Viterbo transfer map between symplectic homology of the base and contact homology of the total space, linking the two Floer‑theoretic invariants in a functorial way.

Symplectic Geometry of Symplectic Cobordisms and the Eliashberg–Floer–McDuff Theory

A symplectic cobordism from (Y₋, ξ₋) to (Y₊, ξ₊) is a compact symplectic manifold (W, ω) with boundary ∂W = Y₋ ⊔ Y₊ such that ω is positive on ξ₊ and negative on ξ₋ near the boundary. Eliashberg, Floer, and McDuff developed a theory of pseudoholomorphic curves in completed cobordisms to define invariants that generalize both symplectic homology and contact homology. The key analytic tool is the compactness theorem for curves with asymptotics to Reeb orbits on both ends, which requires a careful treatment of energy quantization and the exclusion of bubbling off of closed spheres. Using this compactness, one defines a chain map between the contact chain complexes of the two ends, inducing a map on contact homology that is functorial under gluing of cobordisms. When the cobordism is exact, the induced map coincides with the Viterbo transfer map between the symplectic homologies of the fillings. The theory also yields a “cobordism map” on linearized contact homology that respects the Batalin–Vilkovisky structure, and it provides a framework for proving the Weinstein conjecture for contact manifolds that bound a subcritical Stein domain. In particular, if a contact manifold admits an exact filling with vanishing symplectic homology, then its contact homology vanishes, forcing the existence of a closed Reeb orbit by the non‑vanishing of the unit in contact homology.

Symplectic Geometry of the Fukaya Category of Exact Lagrangians and the Generation Criterion

For a Liouville domain (W, λ) with completion widehat{W}, the wrapped Fukaya category W(W) is generated by a collection of exact Lagrangian submanifolds with Legendrian boundary if and only if their wrapped Floer cohomology algebra detects the unit in symplectic cohomology SH^*(W). Abouzaid’s generation criterion states that a set of exact Lagrangians {L_i} split‑generates W(W) precisely when the open‑closed string map OC: HH_*(W(W)) → SH^*(W) hits the unit 1 ∈ SH^*(W). The proof uses the Yoneda embedding of W(W) into modules over itself and the fact that the unit in SH^*(W) corresponds to the identity functor. In practice, one often verifies generation by computing the wrapped Floer cohomology HW^*(L_i, L_j) and checking that the induced map on Hochschild homology is surjective onto the unit. This criterion has been applied to show that the cotangent fiber generates the wrapped Fukaya category of T^*Q for any closed manifold Q, and that a collection of matching cycles associated to a Lefschetz fibration generates the wrapped category of the total space. The generation criterion also underlies the proof of homological mirror symmetry for affine varieties: the mirror functor sends a set of generating Lagrangians to a set of generating objects in the derived category of coherent sheaves, and the criterion ensures that the functor is essentially surjective.
