Part 1

🔊 Sound Waves and Wave Speed

Wave Propagation
Longitudinal Waves, the Wave Equation, and Speed of Sound
Sound propagates as longitudinal mechanical waves — alternating compressions and rarefactions — through elastic media. In a fluid, the speed of sound is v = √(K/ρ) where K is the bulk modulus and ρ is the density. In an ideal gas, v = √(γRT/M) where γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ is the adiabatic index, R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, and M is the molar mass. In solids, the speed depends on the elastic moduli: v = √(E/ρ) for longitudinal waves in a thin rod (where E is Young's modulus). The wave equation for sound pressure p(x, t) is ∂²p/∂t² = v² ∇²p. The acoustic impedance Z = ρv determines the reflection and transmission at boundaries: the intensity reflection coefficient is R = ((Z₂ − Z₁)/(Z₂ + Z₁))². The plane wave solution is p(x, t) = p₀ sin(kx − ωt) where k = 2π/λ is the wavenumber and ω = 2πf is the angular frequency. The superposition principle gives rise to interference — constructive when path difference is an integer multiple of λ, destructive when it is a half-integer multiple.
The speed of sound is a material property determined by the medium's elasticity and density; the wave equation unifies all linear acoustic phenomena.
Part 2

📊 Sound Intensity and the Decibel Scale

Loudness
Intensity, Decibels, and the Inverse-Square Law
The sound intensity I is the power per unit area carried by the wave, measured in W/m². For a plane wave, I = p²rms/(ρv) where p_rms is the root-mean-square pressure amplitude. The sound intensity level (SIL) is measured in decibels (dB): β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀), where I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m² is the reference intensity (threshold of human hearing at 1 kHz). A factor of 10 in intensity corresponds to 10 dB; a factor of 2 corresponds to approximately 3 dB. The inverse-square law: for a point source radiating uniformly in free space, the intensity at distance r is I = P/(4πr²), where P is the source power. The loudness perceived by the human ear is not linear — it follows the Weber-Fechner law approximately, where equal ratios of intensity produce equal increments in perceived loudness. The phon and sone scales account for frequency-dependent sensitivity of the ear (the Fletcher-Munson equal-loudness contours). The sound pressure level (SPL) uses pressure rather than intensity: Lₚ = 20 log₁₀(p_rms/p₀) with p₀ = 20 μPa (threshold of hearing).
The decibel scale compresses the enormous dynamic range of human hearing (10¹² in intensity) into a manageable logarithmic scale; every 3 dB doubles the intensity.
Part 3

🚗 The Doppler Effect and Ultrasound

Frequency Shift
The Doppler Effect, Shock Waves, and Medical Ultrasound
The Doppler effect: when a source of sound moves relative to an observer, the observed frequency f' differs from the emitted frequency f. For a source moving at speed vₛ toward a stationary observer: f' = f v/(v − vₛ). For an observer moving toward a stationary source: f' = f (v + vₒ)/v. When both move: f' = f (v + vₒ)/(v − vₛ). The fractional frequency shift Δf/f = Δv/v for low speeds. When the source exceeds the wave speed, a shock wave (Mach cone) forms with half-angle sin θ = v/vₛ = 1/M where M is the Mach number. Ultrasound uses frequencies above 20 kHz (typically 2–18 MHz in medical imaging). Piezoelectric transducers convert electrical signals to mechanical vibrations and vice versa. In pulse-echo imaging, short ultrasonic pulses are transmitted, and echoes from tissue interfaces are detected. The axial resolution is approximately half the spatial pulse length; the lateral resolution depends on beam width. The Doppler ultrasound measures blood flow velocity using the frequency shift of echoes from moving red blood cells. The attenuation of ultrasound in tissue follows I(z) = I₀ exp(−αz), where α is the absorption coefficient (approximately 0.5 dB/cm/MHz in soft tissue).
The Doppler effect is essential for both astronomy (redshift) and medicine (blood flow velocity measurement); ultrasound provides non-invasive imaging by exploiting the reflection of high-frequency sound at tissue boundaries.
Part 4

🏛️ Architectural Acoustics

Room Acoustics
Reverberation, Sabine's Formula, Absorption, and Room Modes
Architectural acoustics is the science of controlling sound in enclosed spaces. The most important quantity is the reverberation time T₆₀ — the time required for the sound pressure level to decay by 60 dB after the source stops. Sabine's formula: T₆₀ = 0.161 V/A (SI units), where V is the room volume (m³) and A = Σ αᵢ Sᵢ is the total absorption in sabins (αᵢ is the absorption coefficient of surface i with area Sᵢ). The Eyring-Norris formula improves accuracy for highly absorptive rooms: T₆₀ = 0.161 V/(−S ln(1 − ᾱ)), where ᾱ is the average absorption coefficient. The critical distance d_c = √(Q V/(4πT₆₀)) is where direct and reverberant sound are equal (Q is the directivity factor). Room modes (standing wave resonances) occur at frequencies f = (v/2) √((nₓ/Lₓ)² + (nᵧ/Lᵧ)² + (n₂/L₂)²) for a rectangular room of dimensions Lₓ, Lᵧ, L₂. Absorption materials (porous absorbers, panel absorbers, Helmholtz resonators) reduce reflected sound at specific frequency ranges. The sound transmission class (STC) rates how well a partition blocks airborne sound. The speech intelligibility index (SII) and rapid speech transmission index (RASTI) quantify clarity. Diffusion (scattering of sound by irregular surfaces) creates a diffuse field where sound energy is uniformly distributed.
Reverberation time is the single most important acoustic parameter of a room; Sabine's formula enables architects to design spaces with optimal acoustics for speech, music, or performance.

Psychoacoustics: The Non-Linear Perception of Frequency and Loudness

The previous sections covered the physical propagation of sound and basic intensity measurements, but they omitted the critical bridge between physical reality and human perception: psychoacoustics. While physics dictates that a 10 dB increase doubles intensity, the human ear does not perceive this as "twice as loud." This non-linearity is the foundation of audio engineering and advanced acoustic exams.

The cornerstone concept here is the Equal-Loudness Contour, historically known as Fletcher-Munson curves. These graphs plot sound pressure level (SPL) against frequency, showing that human hearing is not flat across the spectrum. At low volume levels (e.g., 20–30 dB SPL), the ear is significantly less sensitive to low frequencies (bass) and very high frequencies. To perceive a 100 Hz tone at 40 Hz as equally loud as a 1 kHz reference tone, you must increase the bass frequency's physical intensity by roughly 20–30 dB. As overall volume increases, this sensitivity curve flattens, meaning low frequencies become audible without massive power boosts. This phenomenon explains why "bass boost" settings on car stereos or headphones are necessary at listening volumes below 60 dB.

A related but distinct concept is the Maslow's Peak (or critical band) and the phenomenon of Masking. A loud sound can completely obscure a quieter sound, even if they are at different frequencies. The bandwidth of this masking effect depends on the frequency; low-frequency sounds tend to mask high-frequency sounds more effectively than vice versa. This is why a jet engine roar (broadband noise) makes it difficult to hear a bird chirp, but a bird chirp does not make it hard to hear the jet engine.

Technique: Calculating Perceived Loudness Shifts
A 1 kHz tone at 40 dB SPL is perceived as equal loudness to a 100 Hz tone. According to the Fletcher-Munson curves, what approximate SPL is required for the 100 Hz tone to be perceived as equally loud? Furthermore, if the 1 kHz tone is increased by 20 dB (to 60 dB), how does the required level for the 100 Hz tone change?
Solution:
At 40 dB SPL, the Fletcher-Munson curve indicates that a 100 Hz tone requires approximately 55–60 dB SPL to match the loudness of a 40 dB 1 kHz tone. This is a difference of roughly 15–20 dB.
Scenario Change: If the reference 1 kHz tone is raised to 60 dB, the ear's sensitivity improves, and the equal-loudness contour flattens. The required level for the 100 Hz tone drops significantly compared to the low-volume scenario. It would now require only about 45–50 dB SPL to match the 60 dB reference.
Key Takeaway: Low frequencies are "harder" to hear at low volumes but become relatively easier as volume increases, a fact often exploited in audio compression algorithms (Loudness War era) to maintain perceived balance.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Volume" Trap
Examiners frequently present a scenario where a student must calculate the intensity required for a low-frequency sound to match a high-frequency one. If the problem does not specify the overall volume level (SPL), you cannot give a single numerical answer. Always look for keywords like "at low volumes" or "threshold of hearing." If unspecified, assume the standard reference condition (1 kHz at 0 dB) but explicitly state that the answer depends on the Fletcher-Munson curve context. Never assume a linear relationship between frequency and perceived loudness.

Atmospheric Attenuation and Meteorological Acoustics

The existing lesson covered the inverse-square law for geometric spreading, which assumes an idealized free-field environment. However, sound traveling over long distances in the real atmosphere is subject to Atmospheric Attenuation, a mechanism independent of distance geometry but dependent on frequency and weather. This is a high-yield topic for competitive exams involving environmental physics or remote sensing.

Sound absorption in air is not uniform; it is highly frequency-dependent. High-frequency components are absorbed much more rapidly than low-frequency ones due to molecular relaxation processes involving oxygen (O₂) and nitrogen (N₂). At standard temperature and pressure, the attenuation coefficient increases with the square of the frequency. Consequently, over a distance of several kilometers, a sharp gunshot or high-pitched whistle will fade away quickly, leaving only the deep rumble of thunder or bass notes from distant explosions audible. This is why you can hear the low-frequency "boom" of a distant explosion but not the high-frequency crack.

Meteorological conditions drastically alter this propagation. Temperature gradients cause refraction. Sound travels faster in warmer air. On a hot day, the ground layer is hotter than the air above it. Sound waves traveling upward into cooler air slow down and bend back toward the ground (ducting), allowing sound to travel much further on hot summer days. Conversely, at night or during winter inversions (cold ground, warm air aloft), sound bends upward away from the listener, creating "acoustic shadows" where distant sounds are inaudible despite being physically present.

Technique: Analyzing Weather Effects on Propagation
A student observes that a siren from 5 km away is audible clearly at noon but inaudible at midnight. The source power and distance are constant. Explain the physical mechanism responsible for this difference, considering temperature gradients and atmospheric absorption.
Solution:
At noon (hot day), the air near the ground is warmer than the air above. Since sound speed increases with temperature (v ∝ √(T)), the wavefront bends upward away from the cooler upper layers? No, wait: The wave travels faster in the warm layer. If the source is on the ground and the gradient is positive (temp decreases with height), rays bend UP. However, typically on hot days, the surface is hot, causing refraction that keeps sound near the ground? Let's correct the physics logic for the exam context.
Correction: On a hot day, the surface is warm. Sound travels faster near the ground. Rays bend AWAY from the region of higher speed (upward). Wait, Snell's Law analogy: v1/v2 = sinθ1/sinθ2. If v increases with height, rays bend upward. This creates a shadow zone? Actually, standard meteorological acoustics states: Hot day -> Surface warm -> Sound bends UP -> Shadow zone near ground at distance. Cold night -> Surface cold -> Sound bends DOWN (towards ground) -> Enhanced propagation.
Re-evaluating the scenario: The prompt says audible at noon, inaudible at midnight. This implies better propagation at noon. This contradicts standard "shadow zone" theory for hot days unless there is a temperature inversion layer or wind shear assisting. However, a simpler explanation often tested involves wind gradients. Wind speed usually increases with height. Sound traveling downwind travels faster in the upper layers and bends back to the ground. If it is noon, winds are stronger. If it is midnight, winds might be calm or opposing.
Standard Exam Answer Logic: Usually, sound carries further at night (inversion) than day. If the question implies better hearing at noon, consider that the "noon" scenario might involve a specific wind direction carrying sound to the observer, whereas midnight has no wind. Alternatively, if the question is flawed or refers to a specific inversion breaking up at noon.
Strict Physics Answer: Standard theory: Sound carries further at night (inversion bends sound down). If audible at noon and not midnight, consider wind shear. Strong winds blowing from source to observer bend sound down (faster air aloft), enhancing range. Calm nights or opposing winds reduce range.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Wind" Variable
In problems involving long-distance sound propagation, always check for wind gradients. Sound travels faster downwind than upwind. If the wind blows from the source to the observer, the effective speed of sound is increased in the upper atmosphere, bending the wavefront downward and increasing intensity at the receiver. Conversely, an upwind condition creates a shadow zone. Do not ignore wind; it can be the deciding factor between "audible" and "inaudible" in advanced problems.

Non-Linear Acoustics and Shock Wave Formation

The existing lesson touched upon the Doppler effect and shock waves (Mach cones) briefly. However, a deep dive into Non-Linear Acoustics is essential for understanding how sound waves distort over distance, a concept crucial for supersonic flight and underwater acoustics. In linear acoustics, we assume small amplitudes where pressure variations are negligible compared to ambient pressure (p' ≪ p0). This allows us to treat the wave equation as linear.

When sound intensity is very high (e.g., explosions, jet engines), the assumption breaks down. The speed of sound in a gas depends on temperature, and temperature increases with pressure. Therefore, the crest of a sound wave (high pressure) travels slightly faster than the trough (low pressure). Over long distances, this causes the wavefront to steepen. A sinusoidal wave gradually transforms into a sawtooth wave. Eventually, the slope becomes infinite, forming a discontinuity known as a Shock Wave.
