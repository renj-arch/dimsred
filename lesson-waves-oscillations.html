Waves & Oscillations — SHM, Damped/Driven Oscillations, Wave Equation & Standing Waves

Waves and oscillations are ubiquitous in physics — from the vibration of a guitar string to the propagation of light and sound. Simple harmonic motion (SHM) is the prototypical oscillation: a mass m on a spring (restoring force F = −kx) satisfies the differential equation d²x/dt² + ω₀²x = 0, where ω₀ = √(k/m) is the natural angular frequency. The solution is x(t) = A cos(ω₀t + φ). Energy in SHM alternates between kinetic (K = ½mv²) and potential (U = ½kx²); total E = ½kA² is constant. The simple pendulum approximates SHM for small angles (θ < 15°): ω₀ = √(g/L). The physical pendulum (extended body) has ω₀ = √(Mgd/I). Damped oscillations include a velocity-dependent damping force F = −bv: the equation becomes d²x/dt² + (b/m)dx/dt + ω₀²x = 0, leading to underdamped (oscillatory decay), critically damped (fastest return to equilibrium), and overdamped (slow exponential decay) regimes. Driven oscillations with a sinusoidal driving force produce resonance — maximum amplitude at ω = ω₀, with a quality factor Q = ω₀/γ characterizing the sharpness of resonance. The wave equation ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²)∂²y/∂t² describes waves on a string, with wave speed v = √(T/μ) (tension/linear density). Traveling waves y(x,t) = f(x ∓ vt) propagate without changing shape. Standing waves on a fixed string of length L satisfy λ_n = 2L/n, f_n = nv/(2L). Fourier analysis decomposes any periodic waveform into a sum of sinusoidal harmonics.

Part 1

🔄 Simple Harmonic Motion

Oscillations
The Harmonic Oscillator, Energy in SHM, the Simple and Physical Pendulums
Simple harmonic motion occurs when the restoring force is proportional to the displacement (Hooke's law: F = −kx). The equation of motion: m d²x/dt² = −kx, or d²x/dt² + ω₀²x = 0 with ω₀ = √(k/m). The general solution: x(t) = A cos(ω₀t + φ), where A is the amplitude and φ is the phase constant determined by initial conditions. The velocity v(t) = −ω₀A sin(ω₀t + φ) and acceleration a(t) = −ω₀²A cos(ω₀t + φ) = −ω₀²x(t). The period T = 2π/ω₀ = 2π√(m/k); the frequency f = 1/T = ω₀/(2π). Energy in SHM: kinetic energy K = ½mv² = ½mω₀²A² sin²(ω₀t + φ), potential energy U = ½kx² = ½kA² cos²(ω₀t + φ). Total energy E = K + U = ½kA² = constant. At equilibrium (x = 0): U = 0, K = E (max). At extreme displacement (x = ±A): K = 0, U = E. The simple pendulum has restoring torque τ = −mgL sin θ. For small angles sin θ ≈ θ, giving d²θ/dt² + (g/L)θ = 0, so ω₀ = √(g/L) and T = 2π√(L/g). The physical pendulum (extended rigid body pivoted at O, center of mass at distance d): τ = −Mgd sin θ, I d²θ/dt² + Mgd θ = 0, ω₀ = √(Mgd/I). The torsional pendulum: τ = −κθ (where κ is the torsion constant), ω₀ = √(κ/I). The SHM approximation fails for large amplitudes where the restoring force is anharmonic — the motion becomes nonlinear with amplitude-dependent frequency (the pendulum period increases with amplitude via elliptic integrals).
Simple harmonic motion is the fundamental periodic motion in physics; its mathematical description applies to pendulums, springs, molecular vibrations, and countless other oscillating systems.
Part 2

📉 Damped and Driven Oscillations

Transient and Forced Motion
Damping Regimes, the Quality Factor, Driven Oscillators, and Resonance
Damped oscillations include a dissipative force proportional to velocity: F_damp = −bv = −b dx/dt. The equation: m d²x/dt² + b dx/dt + kx = 0. Dividing by m: d²x/dt² + γ dx/dt + ω₀²x = 0, where γ = b/m is the damping coefficient. The characteristic equation: r² + γr + ω₀² = 0, with roots r = −γ/2 ± √(γ²/4 − ω₀²). Three regimes: Underdamped (γ < 2ω₀): x(t) = A₀ e^{−γt/2} cos(ω_d t + φ) where ω_d = √(ω₀² − γ²/4) — the system oscillates with exponentially decaying amplitude. The time constant τ = 2/γ; the amplitude drops by factor e in each τ. Critically damped (γ = 2ω₀): x(t) = (A + Bt)e^{−γt/2} — the system returns to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without oscillating — used in shock absorbers and meter needles. Overdamped (γ > 2ω₀): x(t) = A₁e^{r₁t} + A₂e^{r₂t} (both r negative) — slow exponential decay. The quality factor Q = ω₀/γ = ω₀/Δω characterizes the sharpness of resonance — related to the number of oscillations for the energy to decay to 1/e of its initial value (N_e = Q/π). Driven (forced) oscillations: the equation with a sinusoidal driving force F(t) = F₀ cos(ωt) is d²x/dt² + γ dx/dt + ω₀²x = (F₀/m) cos(ωt). The steady-state solution is x(t) = A(ω) cos(ωt − δ), where A(ω) = F₀/m / √((ω₀² − ω²)² + γ²ω²) and tan δ = γω/(ω₀² − ω²). Resonance: maximum amplitude occurs at ω_r = √(ω₀² − γ²/2) ≈ ω₀ (for small γ). The amplitude at resonance: A_max = F₀/(mγω₀). The sharpness of the resonance peak is characterized by the full width at half-maximum Δω = γ. Resonance disasters: the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse (1940) due to wind-induced aeroelastic flutter. Practical applications: tuning circuits, MRI, atomic clocks, and musical instruments.
Damping determines how oscillations decay; driven oscillations reveal resonance — a phenomenon where even a small driving force can produce large amplitude oscillations at the natural frequency.
Part 3

🌊 The Wave Equation and Traveling Waves

Wave Propagation
The Wave Equation, Wave Speed, Traveling Waves, Superposition, and Reflection/Transmission
A wave is a disturbance that propagates through a medium without transporting matter. For a wave on a string, the wave equation is ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²) ∂²y/∂t², where v = √(T/μ) — T is the tension, μ is the linear mass density. The general solution is y(x,t) = f(x − vt) + g(x + vt) — any function of (x − vt) represents a wave traveling to the right, and any function of (x + vt) represents a wave traveling to the left. For a sinusoidal traveling wave: y(x,t) = A sin(kx − ωt + φ), where k = 2π/λ is the wavenumber, ω = 2πf is the angular frequency, λ is the wavelength, and the wave speed v = ω/k = λf. The power transmitted by a wave on a string: P = ½μvω²A². The principle of superposition: the net displacement at any point is the sum of the displacements of individual waves. This leads to interference: constructive when waves are in phase, destructive when out of phase. Reflection and transmission: at a boundary between two media with different wave speeds, part of the wave is reflected and part transmitted. For a string with a discontinuity in linear density: if the second medium has lower impedance (μ₂v₂ < μ₁v₁), the reflected pulse is inverted (phase shift of π). If higher impedance, no inversion. At a fixed end, the reflected wave is inverted; at a free end, it is not. Sound waves are longitudinal pressure waves in a medium. The wave speed for sound in a fluid: v = √(B/ρ) (B is bulk modulus). In an ideal gas: v = √(γRT/M) (γ = C_P/C_V). The Doppler effect: observed frequency f' = f(v ± vₒ)/(v ∓ v_s) for moving observer and source. The Mach number M = v_s/v; when v_s > v, a shock wave (sonic boom) forms at the Mach angle sin θ = v/v_s.
The wave equation unifies the description of all types of waves; the principle of superposition governs interference, reflection, and transmission phenomena across all wave systems.

🎵 Standing Waves, Fourier Analysis, and Nonlinear Waves

Boundary Effects
Standing Waves on Strings and in Pipes, Fourier Series, the Wave Equation in Higher Dimensions, and Solitons
Standing waves arise from the interference of two waves traveling in opposite directions: y(x,t) = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt). The spatial part sin(kx) has nodes (zero displacement) at x = 0, λ/2, λ, 3λ/2, ... and antinodes (maximum displacement) at x = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4, ... For a string fixed at both ends of length L, the boundary conditions y(0,t) = y(L,t) = 0 give kL = nπ, so λ_n = 2L/n and f_n = nv/(2L) = nf₁, where f₁ = v/(2L) is the fundamental frequency. The harmonics: n = 1 (fundamental), n = 2 (first overtone, octave above fundamental), n = 3 (second overtone), etc. The general motion is a superposition of normal modes: y(x,t) = Σ_n A_n sin(nπx/L) cos(ω_n t + φ_n). For a pipe open at both ends: antinodes at both ends → λ_n = 2L/n, f_n = nv/(2L). For a pipe closed at one end: node at closed end, antinode at open end → λ_n = 4L/n (n odd), f_n = nv/(4L). Fourier analysis: any periodic function f(t) with period T can be expanded as a Fourier series: f(t) = a₀/2 + Σ_{n=1}^∞ [a_n cos(nω₀t) + b_n sin(nω₀t)], where ω₀ = 2π/T. The Fourier coefficients a_n and b_n are extracted using orthogonality integrals. The Fourier transform generalizes this to non-periodic functions: F(ω) = ∫ f(t) e^{−iωt} dt. The bandwidth theorem: Δω Δt ≥ ½. The wave equation in 2D and 3D: ∇²u = (1/v²)∂²u/∂t². In 2D, circular symmetry leads to Bessel functions; the 2D wave equation governs drumhead vibrations with normal modes determined by zeros of Bessel functions. In 3D, spherical waves: u = (1/r) f(r ∓ vt). Nonlinear waves: the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation ∂_t u + u ∂_x u + δ² ∂_x³ u = 0 describes shallow water waves and exhibits soliton solutions — solitary waves that preserve their shape and speed upon collision. Solitons arise from the balance of nonlinearity (which steepens the wave) and dispersion (which spreads it). Applications: fiber optic communications (solitons as data bits), rogue waves, and the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou recurrence.
Standing waves are the fundamental vibration patterns of bounded systems; Fourier decomposition reveals any vibration as a superposition of harmonics, while nonlinear waves exhibit soliton behavior with remarkable stability.

Phase Velocity, Group Velocity, and Dispersion Relations

In a dispersive medium the wave speed depends on frequency. The phase velocity vp = ω/k describes the speed of a single sinusoidal component, while the group velocity vg = dω/dk governs the propagation of a wave packet’s envelope and, in most physical situations, the transport of energy and information. For a string under tension the dispersion relation is linear, ω = v k, so vp = vg = √(T/μ) and pulses travel without distortion. In contrast, deep‑water gravity waves obey ω² = g k, giving vp = √(g/k) and vg = ½√(g/k) = ½vp; the packet moves slower than its crests. For a plasma the relation ω² = ωp² + c²k² yields vp > c while vg < c, preserving causality. The sign of the second derivative d²ω/dk² determines whether a pulse broadens (normal dispersion) or sharpens (anomalous dispersion). In optical fibres the group‑velocity dispersion parameter β₂ = d²k/dω² quantifies pulse broadening per unit length; typical single‑mode fibre at 1550 nm has β₂ ≈ −20 ps²/km (anomalous), enabling soliton formation when balanced by Kerr nonlinearity. The bandwidth theorem Δω Δt ≥ ½ follows directly from the Fourier transform of a finite‑duration packet and sets a fundamental limit on the product of spectral width and temporal duration. Examiners often ask for the group velocity of a wave packet whose dispersion relation is given implicitly; the correct procedure is to differentiate the relation ω(k) and evaluate at the carrier wavenumber, not to substitute the phase velocity formula.

Impedance Matching and Energy Transmission at Boundaries

When a wave encounters a discontinuity in medium properties, the fraction of power transmitted depends on the characteristic impedances of the two media. For a transverse wave on a string the impedance is Z = μ v = √(μ T). If a wave of amplitude Ai travels from medium 1 (Z₁) to medium 2 (Z₂), the reflected and transmitted amplitudes are Ar = (Z₁−Z₂)/(Z₁+Z₂) Ai and At = 2Z₁/(Z₁+Z₂) Ai. The power transmission coefficient is T = 4Z₁Z₂/(Z₁+Z₂)², which reaches unity only when Z₁ = Z₂ (perfect impedance matching). In acoustics the specific acoustic impedance is Z = ρ c; a quarter‑wave transformer of thickness λ/4 and impedance √(Z₁Z₂) can match two dissimilar media over a narrow bandwidth. For electromagnetic waves in transmission lines the characteristic impedance Z₀ = √(L/C) plays the same role; a mismatched load produces standing waves quantified by the voltage standing‑wave ratio VSWR = (1+|Γ|)/(1−|Γ|) where Γ = (ZL−Z₀)/(ZL+Z₀) is the reflection coefficient. In optical coatings a single‑layer anti‑reflection coating of refractive index nc = √(n₀ ns) and thickness λ/4 eliminates reflection at the design wavelength. The same principle underlies the design of horn antennas, where a gradual flare provides a continuous impedance taper from waveguide to free space. A common trap is to confuse amplitude transmission coefficient with power transmission coefficient; the former can exceed unity when Z₂ < Z₁, but the latter never exceeds one because energy is conserved.

Worked Example: Quarter‑Wave Transformer
A lossless transmission line of characteristic impedance 50 Ω is terminated by a load of 200 Ω. Design a single‑section quarter‑wave transformer to match the load at 2 GHz. State the required characteristic impedance and physical length of the transformer section, assuming the propagation velocity on the line is 2 × 10⁸ m/s.
The transformer impedance Zt must satisfy Zt = √(Z₀ ZL) = √(50 × 200) = √10000 = 100 Ω. The wavelength on the line at 2 GHz is λ = v/f = (2 × 10⁸)/(2 × 10⁹) = 0.1 m. A quarter‑wave section has length λ/4 = 0.025 m = 2.5 cm. Therefore the transformer is a 100 Ω line 2.5 cm long.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives a dispersion relation ω(k), always differentiate to obtain group velocity; do not assume vg = vp unless the relation is explicitly linear. For impedance problems, write the reflection coefficient Γ first, then compute power transmission as 1−|Γ|². Remember that a quarter‑wave transformer works only at a single frequency; broadband matching requires multi‑section or tapered designs.

Coupled Oscillators, Normal Modes, and Energy Exchange

Two identical masses m connected by three identical springs of constant k form the simplest coupled system. The equations of motion are mẍ₁ = −k x₁ + k(x₂−x₁) and mẍ₂ = −k x₂ − k(x₂−x₁). Seeking solutions x₁ = A₁ eiωt, x₂ = A₂ eiωt leads to the eigenvalue problem (2k−mω²)A₁ − k A₂ = 0, −k A₁ + (2k−mω²)A₂ = 0. The characteristic equation (2k−mω²)² − k² = 0 yields normal‑mode frequencies ω₁ = √(k/m) (in‑phase mode, A₁ = A₂) and ω₂ = √(3k/m) (out‑of‑phase mode, A₁ = −A₂). Any initial condition is a superposition of these modes; if only one mass is displaced initially, the motion exhibits beats with beat frequency Δω = ω₂−ω₁, and energy oscillates completely between the two masses. For N masses with fixed ends the normal‑mode frequencies are ωp = 2√(k/m) sin(pπ/2(N+1)), p = 1…N, and the mode shapes are sinusoidal. In the continuum limit this reproduces the standing‑wave spectrum of a string. Coupled electrical oscillators (LC circuits linked by mutual inductance M or capacitance Cc) obey identical mathematics; the normal‑mode frequencies split by an amount proportional to the coupling coefficient. In molecular physics the same formalism describes vibrational normal modes of polyatomic molecules; the number of modes equals 3N−6 (non‑linear) or 3N−5 (linear). A frequent examiner twist is to ask for the time at which the initially displaced mass first comes to rest; the answer is half the beat period Tbeat/2 = π/Δω.

Nonlinear Wave Evolution: Modulational Instability and the Nonlinear Schrödinger Equation

Weakly nonlinear, narrowly‑banded wave packets in dispersive media are governed by the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation i∂A/∂t + ½β₂ ∂²A/∂x² + γ|A|²A = 0, where A(x,t) is the complex envelope, β₂ = d²k/dω² is the group‑velocity dispersion, and γ is the nonlinear coefficient (Kerr effect in optics, cubic nonlinearity in water waves). For anomalous dispersion (β₂ < 0) and focusing nonlinearity (γ > 0) the NLS equation admits bright soliton solutions A = √(P₀) sech(x/vgt) eiκz, where the peak power P₀ balances dispersion and nonlinearity. Modulational instability (Benjamin‑Feir instability) occurs when a continuous wave of amplitude A₀ is perturbed by sidebands at frequency offset Ω; the perturbation grows exponentially with gain g = √(γP₀|β₂|Ω² − (β₂Ω²/2)²) provided Ω < 2√(γP₀/|β₂|). This instability underlies the breakup of a uniform wave train into a train of solitons and is observed in fibre optics, deep‑water waves, and Bose‑Einstein condensates. The inverse scattering transform provides an exact solution method for the integrable NLS equation, mapping the initial envelope to scattering data that evolve linearly. In non‑integrable perturbations (higher‑order dispersion, Raman scattering) solitons radiate dispersive waves and experience a self‑frequency shift. Examiners may ask for the condition for soliton existence in a fibre with given β₂ and γ; the fundamental soliton order N = √(γP₀T₀²/|β₂|) must equal 1, where T₀ is the pulse width parameter.

Quantum Harmonic Oscillator, Coherent States, and Classical Correspondence

The quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian H = p²/2m + ½mω²x² has eigenvalues En = ħω(n+½) and eigenfunctions ψn(x) = (mω/πħ)1/4 (2ⁿ n!)-½ Hn(√(mω/ħ) x) e−mωx²/2ħ, where Hn are Hermite polynomials. The zero‑point energy ½ħω has no classical analogue but is essential for the stability of matter. Coherent states |α⟩ are eigenstates of the annihilation operator a = √(mω/2ħ)(x + ip/mω) with eigenvalue α ∈ ℂ. They minimize the Heisenberg uncertainty product Δx Δp = ħ/2 and have Poissonian photon‑number distribution P(n) = e−|α|² |α|2n/n!. The expectation values ⟨x⟩ = √(2ħ/mω) Re(α e−iωt) and ⟨p⟩ = √(2ħmω) Im(α e−iωt) follow exactly the classical equations of motion, providing the clearest demonstration of the correspondence principle. A displaced vacuum |α⟩ = D(α)|0⟩ with D(α) = eαa†−α*a is generated by a classical driving force. In quantum optics the quadrature operators X₁ = (a+a†)/2, X₂ = (a−a†)/2i have variances ¼ for the vacuum; squeezed states reduce one quadrature variance below ¼ at the expense of the other. The Wigner function of a coherent state is a Gaussian centred at (Re α, Im α) in phase space, while that of a number state shows negative regions signalling non‑classicality. A typical advanced question asks to compute the overlap ⟨β|α⟩ = exp(−½|α|²−½|β|²+β*α) and to show that coherent states form an overcomplete basis resolving the identity ∫|α⟩⟨α| d²α/π = 1.

Wave Packets, Group Velocity, and Dispersion in Real Media

A monochromatic plane wave extends infinitely in space and time, but any physical signal is a superposition of frequencies centred around a carrier ω₀. The resulting wave packet has an envelope that travels with the group velocity vg = dω/dk evaluated at the central wave‑number k₀, while the carrier oscillations move with the phase velocity vp = ω₀/k₀. In a non‑dispersive medium ω = ck, so vg = vp = c and the packet retains its shape. Real media exhibit dispersion: the refractive index n(ω) varies with frequency, giving ω(k) = ck/n(ω). The Taylor expansion ω(k) ≈ ω₀ + vg(k−k₀) + ½β₂(k−k₀)² introduces the group‑velocity dispersion parameter β₂ = d²ω/dk². A Gaussian packet of initial temporal width τ₀ broadens as τ(z) = τ₀√[1 + (zβ₂/τ₀²)²] after propagating distance z. In optical fibres β₂ can be positive (normal dispersion) or negative (anomalous dispersion); the sign determines whether chirped pulses compress or broaden. In water waves the deep‑water dispersion relation ω² = gk yields vg = ½vp, so energy travels slower than phase crests. In plasma the relation ω² = ωp² + c²k² gives vg = c²k/ω < c, while vp > c, illustrating that phase velocity can exceed c without violating relativity because information travels at vg. The concept of group velocity breaks down near absorption lines where the dispersion curve has strong curvature; there the packet distorts asymmetrically and the peak may appear to move faster than c or even backward, but the front velocity (the speed of the earliest non‑analytic point) remains ≤ c. Examiners often ask for the distance at which a pulse broadens to twice its initial width; solving τ(z) = 2τ₀ gives z = √3 τ₀²/|β₂|. Another common twist is to confuse group velocity with energy velocity; in lossless media they coincide, but in absorptive media the energy velocity defined by the ratio of time‑averaged Poynting vector to energy density differs from dω/dk.

Energy Flux, Poynting Vector, and Intensity in Electromagnetic Waves

For a monochromatic plane wave in a linear, isotropic, non‑magnetic medium the instantaneous Poynting vector is **S** = **E** × **H**. With **E** = E₀ cos(kz−ωt) **x̂** and **H** = H₀ cos(kz−ωt) **ŷ**, the time‑averaged intensity (power per unit area) is I = ⟨S⟩ = ½ Re(E₀ H₀*) = ½ E₀² / Z, where Z = √(μ/ε) is the wave impedance. In vacuum Z₀ = μ₀c ≈ 377 Ω, so I = ½ ε₀c E₀² = ½ c B₀²/μ₀. For a Gaussian beam the on‑axis intensity is I(r,z) = I₀ (w₀/w(z))² exp[−2r²/w(z)²] with beam radius w(z) = w₀√[1+(z/zR)²] and Rayleigh range zR = πw₀²/λ. The total power P = ∫ I dA = ½ π w₀² I₀ is independent of z, confirming energy conservation in free space. At a dielectric interface the normal component of the time‑averaged Poynting vector is continuous, leading to the familiar reflectance R = |(Z₂−Z₁)/(Z₂+Z₁)|² and transmittance T = 4Z₁Z₂/(Z₁+Z₂)² for normal incidence. For oblique incidence the Poynting vector has both normal and tangential components; the normal component gives the power crossing the boundary, while the tangential component is associated with the evanescent field in total internal reflection. In waveguides the time‑averaged Poynting vector integrated over the cross‑section yields the guided power P = ½ Re∫ (**E** × **H***)·**ẑ** dA. For TE modes in a rectangular guide the power flow is carried entirely by the transverse electric and magnetic fields; the longitudinal component of **S** is zero for pure TE/TM modes but non‑zero for hybrid modes. A frequent examiner trap is to use the peak electric field E₀ in the intensity formula I = ½ ε₀c E₀² for a wave in a medium of refractive index n; the correct expression is I = ½ n ε₀c E₀² because the impedance changes to Z = Z₀/n. Another trap is to assume the Poynting vector points exactly along the wave‑vector **k** in anisotropic crystals; in birefringent media the energy flow (ray direction) deviates from the normal to the wavefronts, a phenomenon known as walk‑off.

Worked Example: Pulse Broadening in Optical Fibre
A transform‑limited Gaussian pulse of central wavelength 1550 nm and initial full‑width at half‑maximum (FWHM) duration 100 fs propagates in a standard single‑mode fibre with group‑velocity dispersion β₂ = −20 ps²/km. Calculate the propagation distance at which the pulse FWHM doubles.
For a Gaussian pulse the intensity FWHM τFWHM = 2√(ln2) τ₀ where τ₀ is the 1/e field half‑width. The broadening formula τ(z) = τ₀√[1+(zβ₂/τ₀²)²] applies to τ₀. Doubling the FWHM means τ(z) = 2τ₀. Solve 2τ₀ = τ₀√[1+(zβ₂/τ₀²)²] → 4 = 1+(zβ₂/τ₀²)² → z = τ₀²√3/|β₂|. Convert 100 fs FWHM to τ₀: τ₀ = 100 fs / (2√(ln2)) ≈ 42.5 fs = 4.25×10⁻¹⁴ s. Then τ₀² ≈ 1.81×10⁻²⁷ s². β₂ = 20 ps²/km = 20×10⁻²⁴ s²/m. z = (1.81×10⁻²⁷ × √3) / (20×10⁻²⁴) ≈ 1.57×10⁻⁴ m = 0.157 mm. The pulse broadens to twice its width after only 0.16 mm, illustrating the extreme dispersion at 1550 nm in standard fibre.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question gives β₂ in ps²/km and pulse width in fs, convert everything to SI (seconds, metres) before plugging into the broadening formula. Remember that the formula uses the 1/e field half‑width τ₀, not the intensity FWHM. A quick mental check: if β₂ is negative (anomalous dispersion) the pulse still broadens in the linear regime; the sign only matters for soliton formation when nonlinearity is included.

Waveguides, Cutoff Conditions, and Mode Structure

A waveguide confines electromagnetic waves by total internal reflection (dielectric guides) or conducting boundaries (metallic guides). In a rectangular metallic waveguide of dimensions a × b (a > b) the fields satisfy the Helmholtz equation (∇² + k²)ψ = 0 with boundary conditions Etangential = 0 on the walls. Separation of variables yields TEmn and TMmn modes with transverse wave‑numbers kx = mπ/a, ky = nπ/b. The longitudinal propagation constant is β = √(k₀² − kc²) where k₀ = ω√(με) and the cutoff wave‑number kc = √[(mπ/a)² + (nπ/b)²]. A mode propagates only if ω > ωc = kc/√(με); below cutoff β becomes imaginary and the field decays exponentially (evanescent). The dominant mode in a rectangular guide is TE10 with cutoff wavelength λc = 2a. Its field pattern has a single half‑wave variation across the broad dimension and uniform across the narrow dimension. The guide wavelength λg = 2π/β = λ₀/√[1−(λ₀/λc)²] exceeds the free‑space wavelength λ₀, while the phase velocity vp = ω/β = c/√[1−(λ₀/λc)²] exceeds c. The group velocity vg = dω/dβ = c√[1−(λ₀/λc)²] is less than c, and the product vpvg = c² holds for lossless guides. Power transmitted by a TE10 mode is P = (a b / 4 ZTE) |E₀|² where ZTE = Z₀/√[1−(λ₀/λc)²] is the wave impedance of the mode. In dielectric slab guides the eigenvalue equation for TE modes is tan(κ d) = 2κγ/(κ²−γ²) with κ = √(n₁²k₀²−β²) in the core and γ = √(β²−n₂²k₀²) in the cladding. The number of guided modes is approximately V/π for large V, where the normalized frequency V = k₀ d √(n₁²−n₂²). Single‑mode operation requires V < π/2. A common examiner twist is to ask for the cutoff frequency of the TE20 mode in a guide that also supports TE10; the answer is simply twice the TE10 cutoff because kc scales with m. Another trap is to confuse the guide impedance ZTE with the free‑space impedance Z₀ when calculating power; using Z₀ overestimates the power by the factor √[1−(λ₀/λc)²].

Scattering, Diffraction, and the Huygens–Fresnel Principle

When a wave encounters an obstacle or aperture, each point on the wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets (Huygens’ principle). Fresnel added the crucial ingredient of interference: the field at an observation point is the superposition of these wavelets with appropriate amplitude and phase. The Kirchhoff diffraction integral formalises this: U(P) = (1/4π) ∫∫ [U ∂G/∂n − G ∂U/∂n] dS, where G = eikr/r is the free‑space Green’s function. In the far‑field (Fraunhofer) limit the integral reduces to the Fourier transform of the aperture function: U(x,y) ∝ ∫∫ A(x',y') e−ik(xx'+yy')/z dx' dy'. For a rectangular slit of width a the far‑field intensity is I(θ) = I₀ [sin(β)/β]² with β = (π a sinθ)/λ. The first minima occur at sinθ = ±λ/a. For a circular aperture of radius a the pattern is the Airy pattern I(θ) = I₀ [2 J₁(ka sinθ)/(ka sinθ)]², where J₁ is the Bessel function of the first kind. The Rayleigh criterion for resolution of two point sources states that the principal maximum of one Airy pattern coincides with the first minimum of the other, giving angular resolution θR ≈ 1.22 λ/D for a circular aperture of diameter D. In the near‑field (Fresnel) regime the quadratic phase factor cannot be neglected; the Fresnel number N = a²/(λz) determines the number of visible fringes. For N ≫ 1 geometric optics applies; for N ≪ 1 Fraunhofer diffraction dominates. Scattering from a small particle (size ≪ λ) is described by Rayleigh scattering: the scattered intensity varies as Isca ∝ (1+cos²θ)/λ⁴, explaining the blue sky. For particles comparable to λ, Mie theory gives a series expansion in spherical harmonics with coefficients determined by boundary conditions. A frequent exam question asks for the angular width of the central maximum of a single slit; the answer is 2λ/a (between first minima). Another trap is to apply the Fraunhofer formula when the observation screen is at a distance z that does not satisfy z ≫ a²/λ; the correct approach is to use the Fresnel integrals C(u) and S(u) with u = x√(2/λz).

Parametric Resonance, Mathieu Equation, and Stability Charts

Parametric resonance occurs when a system parameter is modulated at twice the natural frequency, leading to exponential growth of oscillations even without external forcing at the resonance frequency. The canonical model is the Mathieu equation: d²x/dt² + [ω₀² + 2ε cos(2ω t)] x = 0, where ε ≪ ω₀² is the modulation depth. Using the method of Floquet theory, solutions are of the form x(t) = eμt p(t) with p(t) periodic of period π/ω. The characteristic exponent μ determines stability: Re(μ) = 0 gives bounded (stable) motion; Re(μ) > 0 gives parametric instability. The stability diagram in the (δ, ε) plane, where δ = ω₀²/ω² − 1, shows alternating stable and unstable tongues emanating from δ = n² (n = 0,1,2,…). The principal instability tongue (n = 1) is centred at ω = ω₀ (i.e., modulation at 2ω₀) and has width Δε ≈ ε/2 for small ε. Higher‑order tongues (n = 2,3,…) correspond to subharmonic resonances at ω = ω₀/n. In a physical pendulum with vertically oscillating support, the effective gravity becomes geff = g + Aω² cos(ω t), leading to the Mathieu equation with ε = Aω²/(2l). The inverted pendulum can be stabilised by rapid vertical vibration (Kapitza’s pendulum) when the modulation frequency exceeds a threshold ω > √(2g/l). In electrical circuits a varactor diode modulates the capacitance of an LC tank, producing parametric amplification; the gain bandwidth is limited by the width of the instability tongue. A common examiner twist is to ask for the condition for the onset of parametric resonance in a system described by ẍ + ω₀²[1 + h cos(γ t)] x = 0; the answer is γ ≈ 2ω₀/n with h exceeding a threshold proportional to the damping. Another trap is to confuse parametric resonance with ordinary forced resonance: in parametric resonance the energy is supplied by the parameter modulation, not by a direct driving force at the natural frequency, and the growth rate is proportional to ε, not to the driving amplitude.

Common Traps

  • Using the intensity FWHM directly in the Gaussian pulse broadening formula instead of converting to the 1/e field half‑width τ₀.
  • Assuming the Poynting vector in a dielectric medium is S = E × H with vacuum impedance Z₀; the correct impedance is Z = Z₀/n.
  • Confusing guide wavelength λg with free‑space wavelength λ₀ when calculating phase velocity in a waveguide.
  • Applying the Fraunhofer diffraction formula when the Fresnel number N = a²/(λz) is not ≪ 1.
  • Treating the Mathieu equation instability tongues as symmetric about δ = 0; they are centred at δ = n² and have different widths for each order.
  • Neglecting the walk‑off angle in birefringent crystals and assuming energy flows along the wave‑vector.
  • Using the peak electric field in the intensity formula for a wave in a medium without multiplying by the refractive index n.

Surface Acoustic Waves and Rayleigh Wave Dispersion

Surface acoustic waves (SAWs) propagate along the free surface of an elastic solid with particle motion confined to a depth of roughly one wavelength. The classic Rayleigh wave is a non‑dispersive solution for an isotropic half‑space: the phase velocity vR satisfies the Rayleigh equation vR3 − 8vR2 + 8(3−2σ)vR − 16(1−σ) = 0, where σ is Poisson’s ratio. For typical metals (σ ≈ 0.3) the root gives vR ≈ 0.92 vS, with vS the shear‑wave speed. In layered or anisotropic media the Rayleigh wave becomes dispersive: the phase velocity depends on the product kh (wave‑number times layer thickness). The dispersion relation is obtained from the secular determinant of the Stroh formalism or the transfer‑matrix method, yielding multiple branches (Sezawa modes) that approach the bulk shear velocity at high kh. Energy confinement is quantified by the penetration depth δ ≈ 1/κ, where κ = √(k2 − ω2/vS2) for the shear component. SAW devices (delay lines, filters) exploit the strong electromechanical coupling of piezoelectric substrates such as LiNbO₃ or quartz; the coupling coefficient K² determines the fractional velocity shift Δv/v under metallization. A common examiner twist asks for the condition for the existence of a true Rayleigh wave in a coated half‑space: the coating must be slower than the substrate (vcoat < vR,sub) otherwise the wave leaks energy into the bulk as a leaky SAW.

Nonlinear Optical Wave Mixing and Phase Matching

In a medium with a second‑order susceptibility χ⁽²⁾, three‑wave mixing couples fields at frequencies ω₁, ω₂, ω₃ satisfying ω₃ = ω₁ + ω₂ (sum‑frequency generation) or ω₁ = ω₃ − ω₂ (difference‑frequency generation). The coupled‑wave equations for slowly varying envelopes Aj(z) are dA₃/dz = iκ A₁ A₂ eiΔk z, dA₁/dz = iκ A₃ A₂* e−iΔk z, dA₂/dz = iκ A₃ A₁* e−iΔk z, where κ = ω₃ deff/(n₃ c) and Δk = k₃ − k₁ − k₂ is the phase‑mismatch. Perfect phase matching (Δk = 0) yields coherent energy transfer over the interaction length L, giving conversion efficiency η ∝ (κ L)². In birefringent crystals, angle tuning (type‑I or type‑II) or temperature tuning adjusts the refractive indices no(θ), ne(θ) to satisfy n₃/λ₃ = n₁/λ₁ + n₂/λ₂. Quasi‑phase matching (QPM) uses a periodically poled domain structure with period Λ = 2π/Δk, introducing a reciprocal‑lattice vector G = 2π/Λ that compensates Δk. The effective nonlinearity is reduced by the Fourier coefficient of the domain profile (2/π for a square‑wave poling). Higher‑order QPM (m = 3,5…) uses the m‑th Fourier component, giving Λ = m·2π/Δk but with lower efficiency. Walk‑off between Poynting vector and wave‑vector in anisotropic media limits the effective aperture; the walk‑off angle ρ = arctan[(ne²−no²) tanθ/(ne²+no² tan²θ)] must be accounted for in high‑power designs. A frequent exam question asks for the acceptance bandwidth Δλ of a QPM device: Δλ ≈ λ²/(L·|dΔk/dλ|) for a given crystal length L.

Worked Example: Phase‑Matching Bandwidth
A 10 mm long periodically poled KTP crystal is used for second‑harmonic generation of a 1064 nm pump. The phase‑mismatch derivative dΔk/dλ at the design wavelength is 0.02 µm⁻¹ mm⁻¹. Estimate the full‑width at half‑maximum (FWHM) acceptance bandwidth Δλ in nm.
The acceptance bandwidth for a QPM device is approximately Δλ ≈ 0.44·λ²/(L·|dΔk/dλ|) for a sinc² intensity profile. Substituting λ = 1.064 µm, L = 10 mm, |dΔk/dλ| = 0.02 µm⁻¹ mm⁻¹ gives Δλ ≈ 0.44·(1.064)²/(10·0.02) ≈ 0.44·1.132/0.2 ≈ 2.49 nm. The FWHM acceptance bandwidth is therefore about 2.5 nm.

Remember the 0.44 factor comes from the sinc² FWHM; if the question asks for the first zero instead, use 0.88. Thermoacoustic Instabilities in Combustion Chambers

Thermoacoustic oscillations arise when unsteady heat release couples constructively with acoustic pressure fluctuations, satisfying the Rayleigh criterion: ∮ p'(t) q'(t) dt > 0 over a cycle, where p' is pressure perturbation and q' is heat‑release rate perturbation. In a premixed flame, the flame transfer function (FTF) relates q'/q̅ to the incoming velocity perturbation u'/u̅: q'/q̅ = F(ω) u'/u̅. The FTF magnitude and phase are measured experimentally or modelled with the n‑τ model (F = n e−iωτ) where n is the interaction index and τ the time delay. The acoustic field in a cylindrical chamber of length L with a choked nozzle at x = L and a flame at x = xf obeys the wave equation with a source term: ∂²p'/∂t² − c² ∂²p'/∂x² = (γ−1) ∂q'/∂t δ(x−xf). Solving with appropriate impedance boundary conditions yields a complex eigenfrequency ω = ωr + iωi. Instability occurs when ωi > 0, i.e. the growth rate exceeds damping. The growth rate can be expressed as ωi = (γ−1) q̅ n sin(ωrτ + φ) / (2ρ₀c²V) where φ is the acoustic phase between pressure and velocity at the flame location. Practical suppression strategies include Helmholtz resonators tuned to the unstable mode, fuel staging to modify the FTF phase, and passive liners that increase acoustic damping. A typical exam question provides the chamber geometry, flame position, and an n‑τ model; the student must compute the critical interaction index ncrit for marginal stability (ωi = 0) and comment on the effect of moving the flame upstream.

Wave Chaos and Scarring in Quantum Billiards

Quantum billiards are bounded domains where the Helmholtz equation (∇² + k²)ψ = 0 with Dirichlet or Neumann boundaries models non‑interacting particles. The classical counterpart is a point particle undergoing specular reflections. When the classical dynamics is chaotic (e.g. stadium, Sinai billiard), the eigenfunctions exhibit universal statistical properties described by random‑matrix theory: level spacing follows the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE) distribution P(s) = (π/2)s exp(−πs²/4). However, individual eigenstates may show scarring — enhanced probability density along unstable periodic orbits. The scar strength is quantified by the overlap integral S = ∫orbit |ψ|² ds / ∫domain |ψ|² dA. Heller’s semiclassical theory predicts S ∝ 1/√(λLyap T) where λLyap is the Lyapunov exponent of the orbit and T its period. In the stadium billiard, the “bouncing‑ball” orbit family produces strong scars at low k, while the “diamond” orbit scars appear at higher k. Experimental realizations include microwave cavities (copper plates) where the field distribution is mapped with a perturbation bead, and semiconductor quantum dots where conductance fluctuations reflect the underlying wave‑function morphology. A common examiner trap asks for the difference between scarring and Anderson localisation: scarring is a single‑particle interference effect in a clean chaotic system, whereas localisation requires disorder and leads to exponential decay of the wave function.

  • Neglecting the walk‑off angle in birefringent phase‑matching calculations and treating the Poynting vector as parallel to the wave‑vector.
  • Using the n‑τ flame model without checking that the time delay τ is much smaller than the acoustic period; otherwise higher‑order terms become important.
  • Confusing the GOE level‑spacing distribution with the Poisson distribution that characterises integrable billiards.
  • Applying the simple sinc² bandwidth formula to a chirped QPM grating where the poling period varies along the crystal.
  • Treating thermoacoustic growth rate as purely real and ignoring the frequency shift that accompanies the imaginary part of the eigenfrequency.

Bloch Waves, Band Gaps, and Wave Propagation in Periodic Media

When a wave travels through a medium whose material parameters vary periodically in space, the solutions of the wave equation acquire a characteristic structure described by Bloch's theorem. For a one‑dimensional periodic modulation of the refractive index n(x) = n(x + a) with lattice constant a, any eigenmode of the Helmholtz equation can be written as ψ(x) = uk(x) ei k x, where uk(x) shares the periodicity of the lattice. The wave‑vector k is restricted to the first Brillouin zone −π/a ≤ k ≤ π/a. The dispersion relation ω(k) develops allowed bands separated by forbidden gaps; at the zone boundaries k = ±π/a Bragg reflection opens a gap whose width is proportional to the Fourier component of the periodic perturbation. In a photonic crystal the gap corresponds to a range of frequencies for which no propagating electromagnetic mode exists, leading to total reflection. In a phononic crystal the same mechanism creates acoustic band gaps that can be engineered for vibration isolation. The transfer‑matrix method provides a practical way to compute the band structure: for each layer j of thickness dj and wave impedance Zj, the field vector (ψ, ψ') is related across the layer by a 2×2 matrix Mj. The product over one period yields the Bloch condition cos(k a) = ½ Tr(Mperiod). When |cos(k a)| > 1 the Bloch wave‑vector becomes complex and the wave decays exponentially — the hallmark of a band gap. Defects introduced by breaking the periodicity give rise to localized defect modes inside the gap, a principle exploited in cavity quantum electrodynamics and in waveguide bends with negligible radiation loss. The density of states near a band edge exhibits a Van Hove singularity, enhancing light‑matter interaction and modifying spontaneous‑emission rates (Purcell effect). In two‑ and three‑dimensional periodic structures the band diagram depends on the propagation direction; complete gaps require overlap of gaps for all polarizations and all high‑symmetry directions. Design rules such as the “quarter‑wave stack” condition n1d1 = n2d2 = λ/4 maximize the gap width for a given index contrast. Experimental verification uses angle‑resolved transmission or reflection spectroscopy; the measured gap edges agree with plane‑wave expansion calculations to within a few percent.

Nonlinear Acoustic Wave Steepening and Shock Formation

Finite‑amplitude sound waves in a fluid do not obey the linear superposition principle because the local sound speed depends on the particle velocity. For a plane wave propagating in the +x direction the governing equation can be reduced to the lossless Burgers equation ∂u/∂t + (c0 + β u) ∂u/∂x = 0, where u is the particle velocity, c0 the small‑signal sound speed, and β = 1 + B/(2A) the coefficient of nonlinearity (B/A is the medium’s nonlinearity parameter). The characteristic curves satisfy dx/dt = c0 + β u; points of higher velocity overtake lower‑velocity points, causing the waveform to steepen. For an initial sinusoidal wave u(x,0) = u0 sin(ω t − k x) the steepening continues until a gradient catastrophe occurs at the shock formation distance xs = c0²/(β ω u0). Beyond xs a discontinuous shock front forms; its thickness is limited by dissipative mechanisms (viscosity, thermal conduction, molecular relaxation) that are omitted in the lossless model. Including a diffusive term ν ∂²u/∂x² yields the viscous Burgers equation, whose travelling‑wave solution is a sawtooth‑like shock profile with thickness δ ≈ ν/(β u0). The Rankine‑Hugoniot jump conditions across the shock give the pressure rise Δp = ρ0 c0 Δu (1 + β Δu/(2 c0)). In air at room temperature B/A ≈ 0.4, so β ≈ 1.2. For a 1 kHz tone of 140 dB SPL (u0 ≈ 0.5 m/s) the shock formation distance is about 30 m, consistent with outdoor propagation measurements. In water B/A ≈ 5, giving β ≈ 3.5 and much shorter xs. Nonlinear steepening also generates higher harmonics; the n‑th harmonic amplitude grows as (x/xs)n−1 for x ≪ xs. This harmonic cascade is the basis of parametric arrays and of medical ultrasound imaging where tissue nonlinearity improves contrast. When a shock reflects from a rigid boundary the pressure doubles, a fact used in lithotripsy to focus energy on kidney stones. Numerical simulation of the full Navier‑Stokes equations with appropriate equation of state reproduces the shock structure and the subsequent decay due to thermoviscous absorption.

Worked Example: Shock Formation Distance in Air
A 2 kHz sinusoidal sound wave in air at 20 °C has a pressure amplitude of 200 Pa. The small‑signal sound speed is 343 m/s, density 1.204 kg/m³, and B/A = 0.4. Compute the shock formation distance.
First find the particle velocity amplitude u0 = p0/(ρ0 c0) = 200/(1.204 × 343) ≈ 0.485 m/s. The nonlinearity coefficient β = 1 + B/(2A) = 1 + 0.4/2 = 1.2. Angular frequency ω = 2π × 2000 ≈ 12566 rad/s. Shock formation distance xs = c0²/(β ω u0) = 343²/(1.2 × 12566 × 0.485) ≈ 117649/(7320) ≈ 16.1 m. Therefore a shock front is expected to form after roughly 16 m of propagation.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives pressure amplitude, frequency, and medium parameters, immediately write u0 = p0/(ρ0 c0) and β = 1 + B/(2A). Then apply xs = c0²/(β ω u0). Remember that the lossless formula overestimates the distance if absorption is strong; if the question asks for the distance to the first shock in a dissipative medium, state that the actual distance will be larger because absorption reduces the effective amplitude.

Quantum Optomechanical Coupling and Radiation Pressure Effects

Cavity optomechanics studies the interaction between a confined electromagnetic field and a movable mirror that forms one end of the cavity. The Hamiltonian in the rotating frame of the laser drive reads H = ℏ Δ a†a + ℏ ωm b†b − ℏ g0 a†a(b + b†) + i ℏ E(a† − a), where a (a†) annihilates (creates) a cavity photon, b (b†) a phonon of the mechanical mode, Δ = ωL − ωc the laser‑cavity detuning, ωm the mechanical resonance frequency, g0 = (ωc/L) √(ℏ/2 m ωm) the single‑photon optomechanical coupling rate, and E the drive amplitude. Linearising around the steady‑state intracavity field α = ⟨a⟩ yields the beam‑splitter‑type interaction ℏ G(a†b + ab†) with enhanced coupling G = g0 |α|. The cooperativity C = 4 G²/(κ γm) (κ cavity decay rate, γm mechanical damping) quantifies the relative strength of coherent coupling versus dissipation. In the resolved‑sideband regime κ ≪ ωm and with red detuning Δ = −ωm, the anti‑Stokes process dominates, leading to ground‑state cooling of the mechanical resonator. The final phonon occupancy is nf ≈ (γm nth + κ²/(4 ωm²) γm)/ (γm + γopt) where γopt = 4 G²/κ is the optomechanical damping rate and nth the thermal bath occupancy. Conversely, blue detuning Δ = +ωm amplifies the mechanical motion and can drive self‑sustained oscillations (phonon lasing) when γopt > γm. The standard quantum limit (SQL) for continuous position measurement is reached when the measurement imprecision noise equals the quantum back‑action noise; this occurs at an optomechanical cooperativity C ≈ 1. Ponderomotive squeezing of the output light quadratures appears for C > 1 and appropriate detuning, providing a resource for quantum‑enhanced sensing. Experimental platforms include membrane‑in‑the‑middle setups, microtoroids, and superconducting microwave circuits where the mechanical element is a drumhead capacitor. Recent achievements include entanglement between microwave and optical fields mediated by a mechanical resonator, and measurement‑based feedback cooling below the SQL.

Thermoelastic Damping in Micro‑ and Nano‑Mechanical Resonators

Thermoelastic damping (TED) arises because cyclic strain in a vibrating solid produces temperature gradients that drive irreversible heat flow, converting mechanical energy into entropy. Zener’s classical model for a homogeneous isotropic beam of thickness h gives the inverse quality factor QTED⁻¹ = (E α² T0)/(ρ Cp) × (ω τ)/(1 + ω² τ²), where E is Young’s modulus, α the linear thermal expansion coefficient, T0 the equilibrium temperature, ρ the density, Cp the specific heat at constant pressure, ω the angular frequency of the flexural mode, and τ = (ρ Cp h²)/(π² κ) the thermal relaxation time across the thickness (κ thermal conductivity). The loss peaks when ω τ = 1, i.e. when the vibration period matches the thermal diffusion time across the beam. For silicon at 300 K, E ≈ 169 GPa, α ≈ 2.6×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, ρ ≈ 2330 kg/m³, Cp ≈ 700 J/(kg·K), κ ≈ 150 W/(m·K); a 1 µm thick beam has τ ≈ 1.1 µs, so the peak occurs near 145 kHz. In the low‑frequency limit ω τ ≪ 1, QTED⁻¹ ∝ ω; in the high‑frequency limit ω τ ≫ 1, QTED⁻¹ ∝ 1/ω. This non‑monotonic behaviour distinguishes TED from other loss mechanisms such as clamping loss or surface loss, which typically scale as ω⁰ or ω¹. For nanoscale beams where the thickness approaches the phonon mean free path, the Fourier heat‑conduction model breaks down and the Boltzmann transport equation must be used, leading to a modified τ that depends on the Knudsen number. In composite or multilayer resonators, the mismatch of thermal expansion coefficients between layers introduces additional TED channels; the effective loss can be computed by solving the coupled thermoelastic equations with continuity of temperature and heat flux at interfaces. Experimental determination of TED uses ring‑down measurements in vacuum to eliminate gas damping; the measured Q versus frequency is fitted to the Zener formula to extract material parameters. Design strategies to suppress TED include operating far from the peak (either ω τ ≪ 1 or ω τ ≫ 1), using materials with low α (e.g. silicon carbide), or engineering the geometry so that the strain field is uniform (e.g. torsional modes in a cylindrical rod have zero TED because the strain is pure shear). In MEMS gyroscopes and accelerometers, TED often sets the ultimate noise floor, making its accurate modelling essential for performance prediction.

Wave Turbulence, Kinetic Equation, and Energy Cascade

Wave turbulence describes the statistical behaviour of a large ensemble of weakly nonlinear dispersive waves. The starting point is a Hamiltonian system with a quadratic part H0 = Σk ωk |ak|² and a weak interaction term Hint of order three or four in the canonical amplitudes ak. Under the assumptions of random phases, weak nonlinearity, and scale separation, one derives the wave‑kinetic equation for the wave action spectrum nk = ⟨|ak|²⟩: ∂ nk/∂ t = St[n], where the collision integral St[n] involves resonant manifolds defined by frequency and wave‑vector matching conditions ωk + ωk₁ = ωk₂ + ωk₃ and k + k1 = k2 + k3 for four‑wave interactions. For gravity waves on deep water the dispersion relation ω = √(g k) leads to a Kolmogorov‑Zakharov (KZ) spectrum nk ∝ k−23/6 corresponding to an energy flux ε toward high k (direct cascade) and a wave‑action flux toward low k (inverse cascade). The KZ exponent is obtained by dimensional analysis of the kinetic equation assuming locality of interactions in k‑space. In capillary waves (ω ∝ k3/2) the direct‑cascade spectrum is nk ∝ k−17/4. For plasma Langmuir waves the three‑wave decay process yields a different kinetic equation with a KZ spectrum nk ∝ k−2. The validity of the weak‑turbulence description requires the nonlinear frequency shift ΔωNL ∼ ε1/2 ω to be much smaller than the linear frequency, i.e. the wave steepness parameter ≪ 1. When this condition fails, coherent structures (solitons, wave collapses) appear and the kinetic equation no longer applies. Numerical simulations of the kinetic equation reproduce the predicted power laws over several decades of k, and laboratory experiments on vibrating fluid surfaces confirm the KZ exponents within experimental uncertainty. The wave‑turbulence framework also underpins the description of weak turbulence in Bose‑Einstein condensates, where the Gross‑Pitaevskii equation provides the microscopic dynamics, and in optical fibres where four‑wave mixing drives a cascade toward higher frequencies (supercontinuum generation). A practical diagnostic is the measurement of the third‑order correlation function (bispectrum) to verify the random‑phase assumption; a non‑zero bispectrum signals phase coherence and breakdown of the kinetic description.

  • Applying the Zener thermoelastic damping formula to a beam whose thickness is comparable to the phonon mean free path without invoking the Boltzmann transport correction.
  • Treating the wave‑kinetic equation as valid for strong turbulence where the nonlinear frequency shift is comparable to the linear frequency.

Acoustic Metamaterials and Negative Effective Moduli

Acoustic metamaterials are engineered composites whose sub‑wavelength unit cells produce effective dynamic mass density ρeff(ω) and bulk modulus Keff(ω) that can become negative over finite frequency bands. The phenomenon originates from local resonances — typically Helmholtz resonators, membrane‑type inclusions, or split‑ring resonators — that store kinetic and potential energy out of phase with the driving field. When the driving frequency lies just above the resonance, the induced dipole moment opposes the applied pressure gradient, yielding a negative dynamic mass density; similarly, a resonant compliance can render the effective bulk modulus negative. A double‑negative regime (both ρeff and Keff < 0) yields a negative acoustic refractive index n = √(ρeff/Keff) and enables sub‑diffraction imaging, acoustic cloaking, and super‑lensing. The effective medium description is valid only when the lattice constant a ≪ λ, and the retrieval of ρeff and Keff from scattering parameters (S11, S21) requires careful branch selection to satisfy causality (Im n ≥ 0) and passivity (Im z ≥ 0). Losses, inevitable in resonant structures, limit the figure of merit |Re n|/Im n and set a practical bandwidth. Recent designs mitigate loss by employing high‑Q mechanical resonators (e.g., silicon nitride membranes) or by using active gain elements, but stability constraints (Routh‑Hurwitz criteria) must be respected. Experimental verification typically measures transmission through a finite slab and retrieves the effective parameters via the Nicolson‑Ross‑Weir algorithm, confirming negative refraction angles consistent with Snell’s law for n < 0.

Nonlinear Wave Interactions in Magnetized Plasmas: Parametric Decay Instabilities

In a magnetized plasma, a high‑amplitude pump wave (often an electromagnetic wave near the electron cyclotron frequency or a lower‑hybrid wave) can decay parametrically into two daughter waves satisfying the three‑wave matching conditions ω0 = ω1 + ω2 and **k**0 = **k**1 + **k**2. The most studied channel is the decay of an extraordinary (X‑mode) pump into an electron Bernstein wave (EBW) and an ion acoustic wave (IAW), which underlies anomalous absorption in electron cyclotron resonance heating (ECRH) of fusion devices. The growth rate γ for the parametric decay instability (PDI) in the weak‑pump limit is γ ≈ (|E0|/2) √(χ1χ2), where χj are the linear susceptibilities of the daughter modes evaluated at the matching frequencies. Threshold pump power scales as Pth ∝ (ν1ν2)/|χ1χ2|, with νj the linear damping rates. In tokamaks, the PDI threshold is often exceeded, leading to spectral broadening and reduced current drive efficiency. Mitigation strategies include frequency modulation of the pump (to detune the resonance), launching at oblique angles to increase the mismatch, or tailoring the density profile to shift the resonance layer. A related process, the oscillating two‑stream instability, involves decay into two electron plasma waves and dominates when the pump frequency is near the plasma frequency. Nonlinear saturation is frequently described by a predator‑prey model coupling the pump envelope to the daughter wave energies, yielding limit‑cycle oscillations observed in reflectometry diagnostics. Accurate prediction of PDI thresholds requires kinetic treatment (Vlasov‑Maxwell) because fluid models underestimate Landau damping of the daughter waves, especially for short‑wavelength EBWs.

Worked Example: Parametric Decay Threshold
A 140 GHz X‑mode pump (E0 = 5 kV/m) propagates in a deuterium plasma with ne = 5×1019 m−3, Te = 5 keV, B = 5 T. The pump decays into an EBW (ω1 ≈ 1.2 ωce) and an IAW (ω2 ≈ 0.05 ωci). Soliton Perturbation Theory: Adiabatic Invariants and Radiation Loss

The integrable nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLSE) iψt + ½ψxx + |ψ|²ψ = 0 supports fundamental solitons ψ(x,t) = η sech[η(x−vt)] exp[i(vx/2 + (η²−v²/4)t + φ₀)] with four conserved quantities: energy (norm) N = ∫|ψ|²dx, momentum P = (i/2)∫(ψψx*−ψ*ψx)dx, Hamiltonian H, and the eigenvalue η of the associated Zakharov‑Shabat spectral problem. Wave Propagation in Anisotropic Elastic Media: Christoffel Equation and Quasi‑Shear Waves

Topological Edge States in Photonic and Acoustic Crystals

Periodic structures with engineered symmetries can support wave modes that are immune to back‑scattering from disorder. The underlying principle is a bulk‑boundary correspondence: a non‑trivial topological invariant defined for the infinite crystal (e.g., a Chern number, a valley‑Chern number, or a Z2 index) guarantees the existence of propagating states at any interface with a topologically trivial medium. In photonic crystals, breaking time‑reversal symmetry with magneto‑optical materials yields a non‑zero Chern number and chiral edge modes that travel unidirectionally around sharp bends. In acoustic analogues, pseudo‑spin or valley degrees of freedom are created by designing unit cells with C6v or C3v symmetry and then breaking inversion symmetry, producing valley‑Hall edge states that are robust against certain defects but can be scattered by intervalley mixing. The dispersion of these edge states lies inside the bulk band gap and connects the valence and conduction bands, a feature that can be verified experimentally by near‑field scanning or by measuring transmission through a waveguide with a 90° bend. Practical implementations include silicon‑on‑insulator photonic platforms operating at 1550 nm and 3D‑printed acoustic metamaterials operating in the 10–20 kHz range. The topological protection does not eliminate all loss; material absorption, radiation leakage, and fabrication‑induced symmetry breaking still limit the propagation length, but the reduction in back‑scattering can be orders of magnitude compared with conventional waveguides.

Wave Propagation in Viscoelastic Media: Complex Moduli and Fractional Models

Real solids and polymers exhibit frequency‑dependent stiffness and attenuation that cannot be captured by a single real elastic modulus. Classical models such as the Maxwell, Kelvin‑Voigt, and Standard Linear Solid combine springs and dashpots to produce rational functions of iω. Numerical implementation often uses a diffusive representation or a recursive convolution scheme to avoid the non‑locality of the fractional derivative in time‑domain simulations. Experimental validation comes from ultrasonic spectroscopy on polymer gels, where the fractional Zener model reproduces both the dispersion and the attenuation with only four parameters, whereas a conventional Prony series would require ten or more terms.

Anderson Localization and Wave Transport in Disordered Media

When waves propagate through a medium with random fluctuations of refractive index, density, or elastic constants, interference between multiply scattered paths can suppress diffusion and confine energy in spatially localized modes. This phenomenon, first described by P. W. Anderson in 1958 for electron wave functions in a disordered lattice, applies equally to electromagnetic, acoustic, and matter waves. The key parameter is the dimensionless conductance g = (kℓ)d‑2, where k is the wavenumber, ℓ the mean free path, and d the spatial dimension. In one and two dimensions, any amount of disorder leads to exponential localization of all eigenstates (g → 0). In three dimensions a metal‑insulator transition occurs at a critical disorder strength where the localization length ξ diverges as ξ ∝ |W‑W_c|‑ν with ν ≈ 1.57 for the orthogonal symmetry class.

Experimentally, Anderson localization has been observed in microwave networks, cold‑atom speckle potentials, photonic crystals with controlled disorder, and elastic plates with random scatterers. The hallmark signatures are an exponential decay of the average intensity ⟨I(r)⟩ ∝ exp(‑r/ξ) and a log‑normal distribution of transmission coefficients. In the localized regime the Thouless conductance g_T = δω/Δω (ratio of typical level spacing to level width) falls below unity, and the wave dynamics becomes a set of independent resonances. The scaling theory of localization predicts that the dimensionless conductance scales with system size L as d ln g/d ln L = β(g), with β(g) ≈ ln g for g ≪ 1 and β(g) ≈ d‑2 for g ≫ 1.

Beyond linear localization, interactions or nonlinearity can induce delocalization. In disordered nonlinear Schrödinger models, a weak Kerr nonlinearity leads to sub‑diffusive spreading of wave packets, characterized by a second moment m₂(t) ∝ tα with α ≈ 1/3 for one‑dimensional systems. This regime is often termed “weak chaos” and is relevant for light propagation in nonlinear disordered photonic lattices and for matter‑wave experiments with repulsive Bose‑Einstein condensates in speckle potentials. The interplay of disorder, nonlinearity, and dimensionality remains an active research frontier, with implications for quantum information transport, random lasers, and the design of disorder‑robust waveguides.

Nonlinear Wave Dynamics in Bose‑Einstein Condensates: Gross‑Pitaevskii Equation and Dark/Bright Solitons

At temperatures near absolute zero, a dilute gas of bosonic atoms condenses into a macroscopic quantum state described by a complex order parameter ψ(r,t) normalized to the total atom number N. The mean‑field dynamics is governed by the Gross‑Pitaevskii equation (GPE) iℏ∂_tψ = [‑(ℏ²/2m)∇² + V_ext(r) + g|ψ|²]ψ, where g = 4πℏ²a_s/m quantifies the s‑wave contact interaction (a_s is the scattering length). For repulsive interactions (a_s > 0) the GPE supports dark solitons — localized density dips with a π phase jump — while attractive interactions (a_s < 0) admit bright solitons, self‑trapped density peaks that propagate without dispersion in one dimension.

In a quasi‑one‑dimensional trap with tight transverse confinement (ω_⊥ ≫ ω_∥), the GPE reduces to the integrable 1D nonlinear Schrödinger equation. The dark soliton solution ψ(x,t) = √n₀[ i(v/c) + √(1‑v²/c²) tanh(√(1‑v²/c²)(x‑vt)/ξ) ] moves with velocity v < c, where c = √(gn₀/m) is the Bogoliubov sound speed and ξ = ℏ/√(2mgn₀) the healing length. The soliton energy E_s = (4/3) n₀ mc² (1‑v²/c²)3/2 and effective mass m* = ‑2ℏ n₀/c (negative for dark solitons) lead to oscillatory motion in a harmonic trap with frequency ω_s = ω_∥/√2, a result confirmed in experiments with 87Rb and 23Na condensates.

Bright solitons in attractive condensates are described by the sech profile ψ(x,t) = √(N/2ξ) sech[(x‑vt)/ξ] exp[i(mvx‑(mv²/2‑ℏ²/2mξ²)t)/ℏ], where ξ = ℏ²/(m|g|N) sets the width. Because the 1D attractive GPE is integrable, multi‑soliton collisions are elastic, preserving particle number and momentum. In higher dimensions the attractive GPE suffers collapse above a critical atom number N_c ≈ 0.574 a_⊥/|a_s| (a_⊥ = √(ℏ/mω_⊥)), limiting the lifetime of bright solitons. Experimental realization of stable bright soliton trains was achieved by quenching the scattering length via a Feshbach resonance in 7Li, producing matter‑wave solitons that propagate over millimeters without dispersion.

Beyond single solitons, the GPE predicts a rich spectrum of nonlinear excitations: vortex lines and rings in 3D, vortex‑soliton composites, and dispersive shock waves generated by sudden changes of the trapping potential. The Bogoliubov‑de Gennes linearization around a stationary solution yields the elementary excitation spectrum ω(k) = √[ (ℏk²/2m)² + c²k² ], which interpolates between phonon‑like (ω ≈ ck) and free‑particle (ω ≈ ℏk²/2m) regimes. This spectrum underlies the Landau criterion for superfluidity and determines the stability of solitons against snake and transverse instabilities. The GPE framework, extended with Lee‑Huang‑Yang corrections for beyond‑mean‑field effects, also describes quantum droplets — self‑bound clusters stabilized by quantum fluctuations — observed in dipolar 164Dy and 166Er gases.

Wave systems with balanced gain and loss are described by non‑Hermitian Hamiltonians that can exhibit parity‑time (PT) symmetry. A PT‑symmetric Hamiltonian satisfies [H, PT] = 0, where P is spatial reflection and T is time reversal (complex conjugation). When the gain‑loss parameter γ is below a threshold γ_EP, the eigenvalues are entirely real and the eigenvectors form a complete bi‑orthogonal basis — the unbroken PT phase. At γ = γ_EP two eigenvalues and their eigenvectors coalesce at an exceptional point (EP), a branch‑point singularity of order two. For γ > γ_EP the eigenvalues become complex conjugate pairs — the broken PT phase — leading to exponential amplification or decay of specific modes.

In coupled waveguide arrays, the simplest PT dimer consists of one gain and one loss waveguide with coupling κ. The eigenvalues are λ = ±√(κ² ‑ γ²). The EP occurs at γ = κ, where the Hamiltonian becomes defective (only one eigenvector). Encircling an EP in parameter space produces a non‑trivial geometric phase and eigenvector exchange, a phenomenon exploited for chiral mode switching and enhanced sensing. The sensitivity of eigenvalue splitting to a perturbation ε scales as ε1/2 near a second‑order EP, ε1/3 near a third‑order EP, offering a fundamental enhancement over the linear response of Hermitian systems.

Unidirectional invisibility arises in PT‑symmetric scattering potentials V(x) = V_R(x) + iV_I(x) with V_R even and V_I odd. For a specific class of potentials (e.g., V(x) = iγ sech(x) tanh(x)), the reflection coefficient vanishes for incidence from one side while remaining finite from the opposite side, and the transmission coefficient is unity for both directions. This effect has been demonstrated in coupled acoustic resonators, optical waveguide gratings with tailored gain‑loss profiles, and microwave circuits. Practical implementations must contend with saturation of gain, noise, and the requirement of precise balance; nevertheless, non‑Hermitian engineering provides a new toolbox for isolators, lasers with single‑mode selection, and topological lasers based on non‑Hermitian skin effects.

Wave Propagation in Granular Media: Force Chains and Nonlinear Dispersion

Granular media consist of discrete particles that interact through Hertzian contact forces. When a static precompression is applied, the contact stiffness scales as the 3/2 power of the overlap, leading to a strongly nonlinear force–displacement relation. In a one‑dimensional chain of identical spheres, the equation of motion for the nth particle reduces to a discrete nonlinear lattice model. For small amplitudes the system supports linear sound waves with a speed proportional to the square root of the precompression force. As the amplitude grows, the wave steepens and forms a solitary pulse whose width scales inversely with the square root of the peak force. The dispersion relation for linearized perturbations about a precompressed state is ω = 2√(k/m) |sin(qa/2)|, where k is the effective contact stiffness, m the particle mass, a the equilibrium spacing, and q the wavenumber. In the long‑wavelength limit this yields a phase velocity v_p = a√(k/m) and a group velocity v_g = v_p cos(qa/2). Nonlinear corrections introduce amplitude‑dependent dispersion, so that higher‑amplitude components travel faster, producing shock‑like fronts that are regularized by the discrete nature of the chain. Experiments on photoelastic particles visualize force chains as bright filamentary networks; these chains carry the majority of the stress and guide wave energy along preferential paths. In two‑ and three‑dimensional packings, the coordination number and fabric anisotropy modify the effective moduli, leading to direction‑dependent wave speeds and attenuation that scales with frequency squared in the Rayleigh scattering regime. Understanding granular wave propagation is essential for seismic wave attenuation in soils, impact mitigation in protective materials, and the design of acoustic metamaterials based on granular crystals.

Acoustic Holography and Time‑Reversal Mirrors

Acoustic holography records the complex pressure field on a measurement surface and reconstructs the source distribution by back‑propagating the field using the Helmholtz–Kirchhoff integral. In the time domain, a time‑reversal mirror (TRM) consists of an array of transducers that record the impulse response from a source, time‑reverse the signals, and re‑emit them. Because the wave equation is time‑reversal invariant in lossless media, the re‑emitted waves converge on the original source location, achieving diffraction‑limited focusing even in strongly heterogeneous media. The focusing quality is quantified by the temporal compression ratio and the spatial focal spot size, which approaches the Rayleigh limit λ/2 for a full aperture. In practice, finite aperture, limited bandwidth, and absorption degrade the focus; the latter introduces a non‑reciprocal component that can be compensated by iterative time‑reversal or by using a loss‑compensated Green’s function. Applications include lithotripsy, where a TRM focuses shock waves on kidney stones through the rib cage; non‑destructive evaluation of cracks in metal plates; and underwater communication in multipath environments. Recent advances combine TRM with compressive sensing to reduce the number of transducers, and with machine‑learning‑based phase conjugation to correct for nonlinear propagation effects in bubbly liquids. The theoretical foundation rests on the reciprocity theorem and the representation of the field as a superposition of normal modes; the time‑reversal operator is Hermitian in lossless media, and its eigenvalues correspond to the singular values of the multichannel transfer matrix.

Wave Energy Harvesting: Piezoelectric and Electromagnetic Transduction

Wave energy harvesters convert mechanical wave energy into electrical energy using either piezoelectric or electromagnetic transduction. In a piezoelectric harvester, a vibrating beam or plate with a bonded piezoelectric layer generates a voltage proportional to the strain rate. The constitutive equations couple mechanical stress σ and electric displacement D through the piezoelectric coefficient e: σ = c^E ε − e E, D = e ε + ε^S E, where c^E is the elastic stiffness at constant electric field, ε the strain, E the electric field, and ε^S the permittivity at constant strain. The harvested power for a resonant cantilever of effective mass m, stiffness k, and mechanical quality factor Q_m under base acceleration a₀ is P_max = (1/2) (k² Q_m / (ω C_p)) a₀², where k² is the electromechanical coupling factor, ω the resonance frequency, and C_p the clamped capacitance. Electromagnetic harvesters use a moving magnet or coil to induce a voltage via Faraday’s law: V = −N dΦ/dt. For a linear generator with coil resistance R_c and load resistance R_l, the maximum power transfer occurs at R_l = R_c, yielding P_max = (B l v)² / (4 R_c), where B is the magnetic flux density, l the coil length, and v the relative velocity. Both transduction mechanisms benefit from resonance tuning; however, broadband harvesting requires either an array of resonators, nonlinear stiffness tailoring, or active impedance matching. Recent designs exploit parametric amplification by modulating the stiffness at twice the resonance frequency, achieving effective quality factors exceeding 10⁴ and enabling energy extraction from low‑amplitude ambient vibrations. The figure of merit for a harvester is the power density normalized by the acceleration spectral density, typically expressed in µW/g²/Hz. Practical limits arise from material fatigue, dielectric breakdown in piezoelectrics, and magnetic saturation in electromagnetic devices.

Quantum Wave Packet Revivals and Fractional Revivals

In a quantum system with a discrete, non‑degenerate spectrum, an initially localized wave packet evolves according to ψ(x,t) = Σ c_n φ_n(x) e^(−iE_n t/ħ). For a particle in an infinite square well of width L, the energy eigenvalues are E_n = (π² ħ² n²)/(2mL²). The revival time T_rev = 4mL²/(πħ) is the period after which the wave packet reconstructs its initial shape exactly, because all phase factors e^(−iE_n T_rev/ħ) become unity. At rational fractions of T_rev, the wave packet splits into multiple copies — fractional revivals. For example, at t = T_rev/2 the packet forms two mirror‑image copies; at t = T_rev/3 it forms three copies, each with a phase shift. The phenomenon is a direct consequence of the quadratic dependence of E_n on n. In systems with anharmonic spectra, such as the Morse potential or Rydberg atoms, the revival structure becomes more complex: full revivals may be absent, but near‑revivals occur at times determined by the Taylor expansion of E_n around the central quantum number n₀. The autocorrelation function A(t) = |⟨ψ(0)|ψ(t)⟩|² exhibits peaks at revival times, providing an experimental signature. Wave packet revivals have been observed in molecular vibrational wave packets, Rydberg electron wave packets, and Bose‑Einstein condensates in optical lattices. They also underpin quantum carpets — spatiotemporal interference patterns that reveal the underlying energy spectrum. Understanding revivals is crucial for quantum control schemes that aim to reassemble a wave packet at a desired time, and for quantum information protocols that encode information in the revival dynamics.

Thermoacoustic Engines and Refrigerators: Limit‑Cycle Oscillations

Thermoacoustic devices convert heat into acoustic power (engine) or use acoustic power to pump heat (refrigerator) by exploiting the interaction between a temperature gradient and an oscillating gas in a resonator. The core element is a stack of parallel plates or a porous medium placed in a standing‑wave resonator. When a temperature gradient ∇T is imposed along the stack, the gas parcels undergo a thermodynamic cycle: compression at the hot end, expansion at the cold end, and heat exchange with the plates. If the gradient exceeds a critical value ∇T_crit = (γ−1) T_m ω / (v_s k), where γ is the ratio of specific heats, T_m the mean temperature, ω the angular frequency, v_s the speed of sound, and k the thermal conductivity of the gas, the acoustic wave grows exponentially until nonlinear saturation limits the amplitude, establishing a limit cycle. The saturated amplitude is determined by the balance between thermoacoustic gain and dissipative losses (viscous, thermal, and radiation). The acoustic power output is P_ac = (1/2) Re(p̂ û*) integrated over the cross‑section, where p̂ and û are the complex pressure and velocity amplitudes. For a refrigerator, an external acoustic driver imposes a standing wave; the resulting temperature difference across the stack is ΔT = (γ−1) T_m (p̂/ρ_m v_s) (δ_κ / y₀), where δ_κ is the thermal penetration depth and y₀ the half‑plate spacing. The coefficient of performance (COP) approaches the Carnot limit as the drive ratio (p̂/ρ_m v_s²) becomes small, but practical devices operate at drive ratios of a few percent, achieving COPs of 0.2–0.4 of Carnot. Key design parameters include the stack geometry (plate spacing ≈ 2δ_κ), the resonator length (quarter‑wave or half‑wave), and the working gas (helium or helium‑argon mixtures for high Prandtl number). Recent advances employ traveling‑wave resonators with a regenerator, achieving higher efficiencies by approximating the Stirling cycle. Thermoacoustic engines have no moving parts, making them attractive for waste‑heat recovery and space‑power applications.

Nonlinear Wave Breaking and Gradient Catastrophe in Shallow Water

Shallow‑water waves are governed by the depth‑averaged Euler equations, often written in conservative form as ∂h/∂t + ∂(hu)/∂x = 0 and ∂u/∂t + u ∂u/∂x + g ∂h/∂x = 0, where h(x,t) is the local depth, u(x,t) the depth‑averaged velocity, and g the gravitational acceleration. The characteristic speeds are u ± c with c = √(g h). Along the forward characteristic dx/dt = u + c the Riemann invariant R⁺ = u + 2c is constant, while along the backward characteristic dx/dt = u − c the invariant R⁻ = u − 2c is constant. When an initial compressive profile makes ∂u/∂x negative, the forward characteristics converge and intersect after a finite time, producing a gradient catastrophe — the mathematical signature of wave breaking.

The breaking time for a smooth initial condition can be estimated from the method of characteristics. If the initial velocity gradient is (∂u/∂x)₀ < 0, the earliest intersection occurs at t_b = −1 / (∂u/∂x)₀, assuming the depth variation is weak. More generally, the condition for breaking is that the Jacobian ∂x/∂ξ of the characteristic mapping vanishes, where ξ labels the initial position. In dimensional form, the wave steepens until the free surface becomes vertical; at that moment the shallow‑water model ceases to be valid and a hydraulic jump or bore forms, dissipating energy through turbulence.

Practical consequences appear in tsunami run‑up, river bores, and dam‑break floods. For a solitary wave of amplitude a on depth h₀, the breaking criterion is a/h₀ ≳ 0.78, consistent with laboratory observations. Numerical schemes that capture the shock — such as Godunov‑type finite‑volume methods with a Riemann solver — are required for predictive modelling. The Rankine‑Hugoniot conditions across the bore give the jump relations: [h u] = 0 and [h u² + ½ g h²] = 0, where brackets denote the difference between downstream and upstream states.

Wave Propagation in Relativistic Plasmas: Wakefield Acceleration

In an underdense plasma the electron plasma frequency ω_p = √(n_e e² / ε₀ m_e) sets the natural oscillation scale. When a short, intense laser pulse (or a relativistic electron bunch) propagates through the plasma, it expels electrons via the ponderomotive force, creating a charge‑separation field that trails the driver. In the linear regime the wakefield amplitude is E_z ≈ (m_e c ω_p / e) (a₀² / 2) for a laser with normalized vector potential a₀ = e E₀ / (m_e c ω₀). In the nonlinear “blow‑out” regime the bubble radius r_b ≈ 2 √a₀ c/ω_p and the accelerating gradient can reach E_z ≈ m_e c ω_p / e, i.e. tens of GV/m for n_e ≈ 10¹⁸ cm⁻³.

The dephasing length L_d = λ_p (ω₀/ω_p)² (for laser drivers) or L_d = γ_p² λ_p (for particle‑bunch drivers) limits the energy gain, where λ_p = 2πc/ω_p is the plasma wavelength and γ_p the Lorentz factor of the wake phase velocity. Beam loading — the back‑reaction of the accelerated bunch on the wake — flattens the accelerating field when the bunch charge Q satisfies Q ≈ (ε₀ E_z π r_b²) / e. Optimal loading yields a quasi‑uniform field and minimal energy spread. Recent experiments have demonstrated multi‑GeV electron beams in centimetre‑scale plasma channels, confirming the scaling laws.

Key design parameters include the plasma density profile (tailored to extend L_d), the driver duration (matched to λ_p/2 for resonant excitation), and the transverse focusing fields that provide simultaneous betatron oscillation control. The interplay between self‑focusing, self‑modulation, and hosing instability determines the ultimate beam quality. Analytical models based on the quasi‑static approximation and particle‑in‑cell simulations are the standard tools for optimizing future plasma‑based accelerators.

Quantum Tunneling of Wave Packets through Time‑Dependent Barriers

The time‑dependent Schrödinger equation iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = [−ℏ²∇²/2m + V(x,t)] ψ governs the evolution of a wave packet incident on a barrier whose height or width varies with time. When the ramp rate α exceeds the characteristic tunneling frequency ω_t ≈ (E_gap/ℏ) the process becomes non‑adiabatic and the wave packet splits into transmitted, reflected, and trapped components.

Resonant enhancement occurs when a sideband aligns with a quasi‑bound state of the static barrier, leading to peaks in the transmitted energy distribution. This photon‑assisted tunneling has been observed in semiconductor superlattices driven by terahertz fields and in ultracold atoms in shaken optical lattices.

Experimental control of the barrier phase allows coherent manipulation of the transmitted wave packet. The condition Ω τ_d ≫ 1 ensures that many drive cycles occur before decoherence destroys the interference.

Acoustic Wave Propagation in Porous Media: Biot Theory

Biot’s poroelastic model describes a fluid‑saturated porous solid by two coupled displacement fields: the solid matrix displacement u_s and the fluid displacement u_f relative to the solid. The equations of motion in the frequency domain are −ω² (ρ₁₁ u_s + ρ₁₂ u_f) = ∇·σ_s and −ω² (ρ₁₂ u_s + ρ₂₂ u_f) = −∇p_f, where ρ_ij are the effective mass densities (including tortuosity) and σ_s, p_f are the solid stress and fluid pressure. The constitutive relations involve the drained bulk modulus K_d, the solid grain modulus K_s, the fluid modulus K_f, and the shear modulus μ of the frame.

The theory predicts three compressional wave modes: a fast P‑wave (P1) whose phase velocity approaches the Gassmann low‑frequency limit at ω → 0, a slow P‑wave (P2) that is highly attenuated and diffusive at low frequencies but becomes propagating above a characteristic frequency ω_c = η φ / (κ ρ_f) (η fluid viscosity, φ porosity, κ permeability), and a shear S‑wave that does not involve fluid motion directly. The slow wave carries most of the fluid‑relative motion and is responsible for the strong attenuation observed in seismic logging and medical ultrasound of cancellous bone.

At frequencies well above ω_c the two compressional waves propagate with distinct velocities V_P1 ≈ √[(K_d + 4μ/3 + α² M)/ρ] and V_P2 ≈ √[M/ρ_f] where α = 1 − K_d/K_s is the Biot‑Willis coefficient and M = [φ/K_f + (α−φ)/K_s]⁻¹. The quality factor Q⁻¹ for the fast wave peaks near ω_c, providing a diagnostic for permeability. Inversion of measured V_P1(ω) and attenuation α(ω) yields estimates of φ, κ, and frame moduli, making Biot theory a cornerstone of quantitative acoustic characterization of porous materials.

Topological Insulators for Elastic Waves: Valley‑Hall Effect

Phononic crystals with broken inversion symmetry but preserved time‑reversal symmetry can host valley‑contrasting Berry curvature near the K and K′ points of a hexagonal Brillouin zone. By engineering a mass term that opens a gap at these Dirac points — for instance, by varying the radius of holes in a triangular lattice — the valley Chern numbers C_v = ±½ acquire opposite signs in the two valleys. A domain wall between two crystals with opposite mass signs supports topologically protected edge modes that propagate unidirectionally along the interface, immune to back‑scattering from smooth disorder that does not couple the valleys.

The edge‑state dispersion lies inside the bulk gap and connects the valence and conduction bands of opposite valleys. Its group velocity is determined by the slope of the Dirac cone and the magnitude of the mass gap Δ. For a typical silicon phononic crystal with lattice constant a = 500 µm and hole radius contrast Δr = 0.05 a, the gap opens at f ≈ 1.2 MHz with Δ ≈ 0.1 MHz, yielding edge‑mode group velocities of order 10³ m/s. The valley‑Hall protection fails only when intervalley scattering is strong, i.e., when disorder varies on the scale of the lattice constant or when sharp corners mix K and K′.

Experimental realizations include 3D‑printed polymer plates, aluminum plates with etched holes, and MEMS‑based resonators. Measurements of transmission across a sharp bend show >90 % power retention, confirming the robustness. Applications under investigation are topological waveguides for acoustic logic, vibration isolation in aerospace structures, and non‑reciprocal devices when combined with active modulation that breaks time‑reversal symmetry. The valley‑Hall platform is attractive because it requires only geometric patterning, not magnetic bias or spatiotemporal modulation.

Nonlinear Wave Interaction in Plasma: Three‑Wave Resonance and Parametric Decay

In a magnetized plasma the dielectric response becomes strongly anisotropic, allowing distinct wave branches — such as Langmuir, ion‑acoustic, and electromagnetic modes — to satisfy simultaneous frequency and wave‑vector matching conditions. When three waves with frequencies ω₁, ω₂, ω₃ and wave‑vectors k₁, k₂, k₃ obey ω₁ = ω₂ + ω₃ and k₁ = k₂ + k₃, a resonant three‑wave coupling occurs. The interaction Hamiltonian can be written as H_int ∝ χ⁽²⁾ a₁ a₂† a₃† + c.c., where χ⁽²⁾ is the second‑order susceptibility tensor derived from the fluid or kinetic plasma model. Energy exchange among the waves leads to parametric decay instability: a high‑frequency pump wave (often an electromagnetic wave near the plasma frequency ω_pe) decays into a lower‑frequency daughter wave (e.g., ion‑acoustic) and a scattered electromagnetic sideband. The growth rate γ for the decay is γ ≈ (|χ⁽²⁾| E₀ / 2) √(ω₂ ω₃ / ω₁) where E₀ is the pump electric field amplitude. Threshold conditions require the pump intensity to exceed I_th ∝ (ν₂ ν₃) / |χ⁽²⁾|², with ν₂, ν₃ the linear damping rates of the daughter waves. In laser‑fusion relevant plasmas, stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) and stimulated Brillouin scattering (SBS) are the dominant parametric decay channels, limiting laser‑energy coupling to the target. The Manley‑Rowe relations guarantee that the photon number fluxes satisfy N₁ = N₂ + N₃, providing a useful check for numerical simulations. Nonlinear frequency shifts, trapped‑particle effects, and wave‑breaking modify the simple three‑wave picture at high amplitudes, requiring kinetic Vlasov‑Poisson or particle‑in‑cell treatments for quantitative predictions.

Experimental verification uses Thomson scattering diagnostics to resolve the daughter‑wave spectra and measure growth rates. In tokamak edge plasmas, parametric decay of lower‑hybrid waves into ion‑Bernstein and ion‑acoustic modes degrades current‑drive efficiency. The theory also underpins plasma‑based amplifiers where a strong pump transfers energy to a weak signal via the decay channel, achieving gains of 10–20 dB in millimetre‑wave regimes. Understanding the interplay between linear dispersion (including finite‑Larmor‑radius corrections) and nonlinear coupling coefficients is essential for designing high‑power microwave sources and for interpreting space‑plasma observations such as auroral kilometric radiation.

Acoustic Metasurfaces: Generalized Snell’s Law and Anomalous Reflection

Acoustic metasurfaces are sub‑wavelength engineered interfaces that impose a spatially varying phase discontinuity φ(x) on an incident pressure field. By tailoring the local surface impedance Z_s(x) or by arranging resonant unit cells (e.g., Helmholtz resonators, labyrinthine channels), the metasurface imparts a prescribed phase gradient dφ/dx. The generalized Snell’s law for pressure waves reads sin θ_t − sin θ_i = (1/k₀) dφ/dx, where θ_i and θ_t are the incident and transmitted (or reflected) angles measured from the surface normal, and k₀ = ω/c₀ is the free‑space wavenumber. When the phase gradient is constant, the reflected beam is steered to an anomalous angle θ_r satisfying sin θ_r = sin θ_i + (1/k₀) dφ/dx. This enables negative reflection (θ_r on the same side of the normal as θ_i) and perfect beam deflection without the need for bulky phased arrays. The unit‑cell design must provide a 2π phase coverage while maintaining near‑unity amplitude transmission or reflection; typical implementations use coiled‑up channels whose effective acoustic length tunes the phase delay. Losses arise from viscous boundary layers and thermoviscous dissipation in narrow necks, limiting the maximum achievable efficiency to ≈80 % in air at audible frequencies.

Beyond beam steering, a quadratic phase profile φ(x) = (k₀/2f) x² focuses acoustic energy to a focal line at distance f, realizing a flat acoustic lens. Gradient metasurfaces can also convert propagating waves into surface‑bound modes, enabling sub‑wavelength imaging and acoustic cloaking by canceling scattered fields. The design methodology employs inverse design or topology optimization to meet broadband performance, often targeting a 10 % fractional bandwidth around a centre frequency. Recent experiments with 3D‑printed polymer metasurfaces demonstrated anomalous reflection at 8 kHz with a deflection angle of 60° and measured pressure‑field maps confirming the generalized Snell’s law. In underwater applications, water‑loaded metasurfaces require impedance matching layers to suppress spurious reflections at the fluid‑solid interface. The same formalism extends to elastic metasurfaces controlling shear and longitudinal wave conversion, opening pathways for seismic wave manipulation and non‑destructive evaluation.

Worked Example: Anomalous Reflection Angle
A metasurface imposes a linear phase gradient dφ/dx = 2π / Λ with Λ = 0.04 m. An airborne plane wave at f = 5 kHz (c₀ = 340 m/s) impinges at θ_i = 20°. Compute the reflected angle θ_r.
First compute k₀ = 2π f / c₀ = 2π × 5000 / 340 ≈ 92.4 rad/m. The phase gradient term is (1/k₀) dφ/dx = (1/92.4) × (2π / 0.04) ≈ 1.70. Generalized Snell’s law for reflection: sin θ_r = sin θ_i + (1/k₀) dφ/dx. sin 20° ≈ 0.342. Adding 1.70 gives 2.04, which exceeds 1, indicating that the chosen gradient is too large for a propagating reflected wave; the energy couples into an evanescent surface mode. To obtain a real θ_r, the gradient must satisfy |sin θ_i + (1/k₀) dφ/dx| ≤ 1. For this frequency, the maximum usable gradient magnitude is ≈ 0.66 rad/m, corresponding to Λ_min ≈ 0.10 m. Thus the given metasurface would not produce a propagating anomalous reflection at 5 kHz; it would instead generate a surface‑bound wave.
Exam Strategy
When a problem asks for the anomalous reflection angle, first compute the free‑space wavenumber k₀ and the phase‑gradient term (1/k₀) dφ/dx. Check the magnitude of sin θ_i plus this term; if it lies outside [−1, 1], the physical solution is an evanescent or surface wave, not a propagating reflected beam. This quick sanity check eliminates impossible answer choices and earns partial credit for recognizing the physical constraint.

Quantum Noise in Mechanical Oscillators: Standard Quantum Limit and Backaction Evading Measurements

A mechanical resonator of effective mass m and resonance frequency ω_m is described by the Hamiltonian H = ℏ ω_m a† a, where a (a†) annihilates (creates) a phonon. Continuous position measurement couples the resonator to a meter (e.g., an optical cavity field) via the interaction H_int = ℏ g₀ a† a (b + b†), with g₀ the single‑photon optomechanical coupling rate and b the cavity annihilation operator. The Heisenberg‑Langevin equations yield the output field quadratures containing both the imprecision noise (shot noise) and the quantum backaction force noise (radiation‑pressure fluctuations). The symmetrized position noise spectral density referred to the input is S_xx(ω) = S_xx^imp(ω) + |χ_m(ω)|² S_FF^ba(ω), where χ_m(ω) = [m (ω_m² − ω² − i ω Γ_m)]⁻¹ is the mechanical susceptibility, Γ_m the intrinsic damping, S_xx^imp = ℏ / (4 m ω_m g₀² n_cav) the imprecision, and S_FF^ba = ℏ m ω_m g₀² n_cav the backaction force noise (n_cav intracavity photon number). The product S_xx^imp S_FF^ba = ℏ²/4 saturates the Heisenberg uncertainty relation, defining the standard quantum limit (SQL) for a force measurement: S_FF^SQL = 2 ℏ m ω_m Γ_m. At the SQL the imprecision and backaction contributions are equal. Beating the SQL requires quantum correlations between the meter quadratures, achieved by injecting squeezed vacuum into the cavity or by performing a variational measurement that reads out a quadrature orthogonal to the backaction‑dominated one. Backaction‑evading (BAE) schemes measure only a single mechanical quadrature X_θ = (a e-iθ + a† eiθ)/2, which commutes with itself at different times, allowing repeated measurements without added backaction. Experimentally, BAE has been demonstrated in microwave‑optomechanical systems using a two‑tone drive that creates an effective negative‑mass reference frame, yielding a 3 dB improvement over the SQL. The ultimate limit is set by the mechanical quality factor Q_m = ω_m/Γ_m and the thermal occupancy n_th = k_B T / (ℏ ω_m); ground‑state cooling (n_th < 1) is a prerequisite for observing quantum‑limited noise.

In gravitational‑wave detectors, the test masses are kilogram‑scale pendulums with ω_m/2π ≈ 100 Hz. Quantum radiation‑pressure noise dominates at low frequencies, while shot noise limits high frequencies. Frequency‑dependent squeezing, realized by filter cavities, rotates the squeezed quadrature to match the optomechanical response across the detection band, achieving broadband sub‑SQL sensitivity. The same principles apply to macroscopic quantum superposition tests, where the mechanical resonator is prepared in a non‑Gaussian state and its decoherence rate is monitored via continuous measurement. Understanding the trade‑off between measurement strength, quantum efficiency η, and thermal noise is essential for designing next‑generation quantum sensors and for interpreting precision‑measurement data in competitive physics examinations.

Wave Propagation in Nonlocal Media: Spatial Dispersion and Hydrodynamic Models

In media where the constitutive relation between polarization P and electric field E (or stress σ and strain ε) depends on the field at neighboring points, the response is nonlocal. The Fourier‑space susceptibility becomes a function of both frequency ω and wave‑vector k: χ(ω, k) = χ₀(ω) / [1 + (k/k_c)²] for a simple hydrodynamic model, where k_c = ω_p / v_F is a characteristic wave‑vector set by the plasma frequency ω_p and the Fermi velocity v_F (or thermal velocity in classical gases). The resulting dispersion relation for transverse electromagnetic waves reads k² c² = ω² [1 + χ(ω, k)], leading to a k‑dependent refractive index n(ω, k). Spatial dispersion introduces additional boundary conditions: besides the usual continuity of tangential E and H, the normal component of the polarization current must satisfy a surface‑impedance condition derived from the microscopic current‑conservation law. This gives rise to longitudinal bulk plasmons (k·E ≠ 0) that can be excited at interfaces, and to surface waves such as the hydrodynamic surface plasmon polariton with dispersion ω_sp(k) ≈ ω_p / √2 [1 + (β k / ω_p)²] where β = √(3/5) v_F for a degenerate electron gas. In the extreme nonlocal limit (k ≫ k_c), the dielectric function approaches ε(ω, k) ≈ 1 − ω_p² / (ω² − β² k²), predicting a blueshift of the plasmon resonance and a reduction of field enhancement in nanogaps compared to local‑response theory. Nonlocal effects become measurable when the feature size is comparable to the Thomas‑Fermi screening length (≈0.1 nm in metals) or the electron mean free path (≈10–50 nm at room temperature). Experiments using electron‑energy‑loss spectroscopy (EELS) on silver nanowires and tip‑enhanced Raman scattering have confirmed the predicted blueshift and linewidth broadening. In acoustic analogues, nonlocal elasticity (gradient elasticity) introduces a length scale ℓ such that the stress depends on the strain gradient: σ = C ε − ℓ² C ∇² ε. The resulting dispersion for longitudinal waves is ω² = (C/ρ) k² / (1 + ℓ² k²), exhibiting a cutoff at k = 1/ℓ where the group velocity vanishes. This model captures size‑dependent stiffening in nanostructured phononic crystals and informs the design of metamaterials with tailored dispersion.

From a computational perspective, nonlocal hydrodynamic equations can be solved using the additional boundary condition (ABC) method or by introducing a scalar potential for the longitudinal current. The ABC states that the normal component of the total current vanishes at a hard wall, leading to a surface charge layer that screens the normal electric field. In quantum‑hydrodynamic models, the Bohm potential term (ℏ²/2m) ∇(∇²√n / √n) adds a dispersive pressure that regularizes singularities at sharp corners. These frameworks are essential for predicting the performance of plasmonic waveguides, spasers, and quantum‑plasmonic circuits where the mode confinement approaches the nonlocal length scale.

Elastic Wave Scattering by Cracks: Fracture Mechanics and Ultrasonic Nondestructive Evaluation

Scattering of elastic waves by a planar crack in an isotropic solid is a canonical problem in fracture mechanics and ultrasonic NDE. For a penny‑shaped crack of radius a subjected to a normally incident longitudinal wave of wavenumber k_L = ω / c_L, the low‑frequency (ka ≪ 1) scattering cross‑section for mode‑converted shear waves is σ_LS ≈ (64/9π) a⁶ k_L⁴ (c_L / c_S)⁴, where c_S is the shear wave speed. The far‑field scattered displacement amplitude for the reflected longitudinal wave is u_L^sc ≈ (k_L³ a³ / 4π r) (1 + cos θ) ei k_L r in the forward direction, while the mode‑converted shear amplitude scales as u_S^sc ∝ k_L³ a³ sin θ cos θ. At higher frequencies (ka ∼ 1), the Kirchhoff approximation (physical optics) treats the crack faces as locally flat reflectors, yielding a specular reflection coefficient R ≈ −1 for the longitudinal component and a mode‑conversion coefficient T_LS ≈ 2 (c_S / c_L) sin θ_i cos θ_i for incident angle θ_i. The exact solution uses the boundary‑integral equation method with the elastodynamic Green’s tensor, leading to a system of singular integral equations for the crack‑opening displacement (COD). The COD near the crack tip exhibits the characteristic square‑root singularity δ(r) ∝ √(a − r), which determines the stress‑intensity factor K_I = limr→a √(2π (a−r)) σyy(r). In ultrasonic testing, the time‑of‑flight diffraction (TOFD) technique exploits the diffracted waves from the crack tips; the tip‑diffracted signal arrival time t_d = 2 √(x² + a²) / c_L provides a direct measure of crack depth a. The amplitude of the tip‑diffracted pulse scales with the stress‑intensity factor, enabling quantitative sizing. For fatigue cracks with rough faces, the effective reflection coefficient is reduced by a factor exp(−2 k_L² σ_h²) where σ_h is the RMS surface height, an example of the Rayleigh roughness criterion. Modern phased‑array probes synthesize focused beams to illuminate the crack at multiple angles, and the full matrix capture (FMC) data are processed with the total focusing method (TFM) to reconstruct the crack geometry. Inverse scattering algorithms, such as the linear sampling method or the topological derivative approach, can locate and shape‑reconstruct cracks from multi‑static response matrices without prior knowledge of the crack orientation. These techniques are standard in aerospace inspection of turbine blades and in nuclear pressure‑vessel surveillance, where detection of sub‑millimetre cracks is required for safety certification.

  • Confusing the low‑frequency Rayleigh scattering scaling (a⁶) with the high‑frequency Kirchhoff scaling (a²) leads to order‑of‑magnitude errors in cross‑section estimates.
  • Neglecting mode conversion at non‑normal incidence; the shear‑wave diffracted field often dominates the received signal in TOFD.
  • Assuming the crack‑opening displacement is uniform; the √(a−r) singularity controls the stress‑intensity factor and the diffracted amplitude.
  • Overlooking the Rayleigh roughness factor exp(−2 k² σ_h²) when interpreting amplitude loss from fatigue cracks.
  • Applying the scalar acoustic scattering formulas to elastic solids without accounting for both longitudinal and shear wave speeds.
  • Forgetting that the Kirchhoff approximation requires ka ≫ 1 and a locally planar crack front; it fails for curved or branched cracks.

Wave Propagation in Piezoelectric Media: Coupled Electromechanical Waves

Piezoelectric crystals support waves in which mechanical strain and electric field are intrinsically coupled through the constitutive relations T = cES − eTE and D = eS + εSE, where T is stress, S strain, E electric field, D electric displacement, cE the elastic stiffness at constant electric field, e the piezoelectric stress tensor, and εS the permittivity at constant strain. Combining these with Newton’s second law ρ∂²u/∂t² = ∇·T and Gauss’s law ∇·D = 0 (for free‑charge‑free propagation) yields a coupled wave equation for the mechanical displacement u and electric potential φ. For a plane wave propagating along a principal axis of a 6 mm class crystal (e.g., ZnO, AlN) the dispersion relation separates into a quasi‑longitudinal branch and a quasi‑shear branch. The quasi‑longitudinal phase velocity is vL = √[(c33E + e33²/ε33S)/ρ], showing the stiffening due to the piezoelectric coupling. The electromechanical coupling coefficient k² = e33²/(c33Eε33S) quantifies the fraction of mechanical energy convertible to electrical energy; typical values are 0.05–0.1 for AlN and 0.1–0.15 for PZT‑5H. In surface acoustic wave (SAW) devices the Rayleigh‑type wave velocity is reduced by the factor √(1 − k²) when the surface is metallized (short‑circuit boundary) compared with a free surface (open‑circuit). This velocity shift underpins the operation of SAW delay lines, resonators, and sensors. For bulk acoustic wave (BAW) resonators used in RF filters, the thickness‑extensional mode frequency is f = vL/(2h) where h is the plate thickness; the series and parallel resonance frequencies split by Δf/f ≈ k²/2. Examiner traps often involve confusing the open‑circuit and short‑circuit elastic constants, or neglecting the fact that the coupling coefficient is defined with the *clamped* permittivity εS rather than the free permittivity εT. Another common error is applying the isotropic wave speed formula √(c/ρ) to piezoelectric media without adding the e²/ε term.

Acoustic Wave Propagation in Fluid‑Filled Elastic Tubes: Water Hammer and Dispersion

When a fluid (density ρf, bulk modulus Kf) fills a thin‑walled elastic tube (wall thickness h, Young’s modulus E, Poisson’s ratio ν), the coupled fluid‑structure system supports pressure waves whose speed is lower than the pure fluid sound speed c0 = √(Kf/ρf). The classical water‑hammer (or Korteweg) wave speed for a tube anchored against longitudinal motion is c = c0/√[1 + (KfD)/(Eh)], where D is the tube inner diameter. If the tube is free to move axially, the denominator becomes 1 + (KfD)/[Eh(1 − ν²)], reflecting the additional compliance from Poisson contraction. The derivation follows from linearising the fluid continuity equation ∂ρ/∂t + ρf∂v/∂x = 0, the fluid momentum equation ρf∂v/∂t = −∂p/∂x, and the tube radial equilibrium p = (Eh/D)(1 − ν²) (ΔD/D) for a thin shell. Combining these gives a single wave equation for pressure p with the above phase velocity. At higher frequencies (wavelength comparable to tube radius) the thin‑shell assumption breaks down and the full elastodynamic equations for the tube wall must be solved, leading to a set of coupled dispersion curves: a fluid‑dominated branch that asymptotes to c0 at very high k, and structural branches corresponding to shell breathing, longitudinal, and flexural modes. The fluid‑structure coupling also introduces attenuation through radiation of elastic waves into the surrounding medium and through viscous boundary layers in the fluid; the viscous attenuation per unit length for a Newtonian fluid is αvisc ≈ (ω²μ)/(2ρfc³) where μ is dynamic viscosity. In practical pipeline transients (valve closure, pump trip) the Joukowsky pressure rise Δp = ρfcΔv provides the peak over‑pressure, but the dispersed wave front shape depends on the frequency‑dependent wave speed and attenuation. Examiner traps include using the free‑field sound speed c0 instead of the coupled speed c, forgetting the (1 − ν²) factor for unrestrained tubes, and neglecting the distinction between the low‑frequency water‑hammer speed and the high‑frequency asymptotic speed when estimating surge arrival times.

Worked Example: Coupled Wave Speed in a Steel Pipe
A steel pipe (E = 200 GPa, ν = 0.3, h = 10 mm, D = 0.5 m) carries water (ρ_f = 1000 kg/m³, K_f = 2.2 GPa). The pipe is anchored at both ends. Compute the water‑hammer wave speed and the Joukowsky pressure rise for a sudden valve closure that stops a 2 m/s flow.
First compute the free‑field sound speed in water: c₀ = √(K_f/ρ_f) = √(2.2×10⁹ / 1000) ≈ 1483 m/s. The coupling term is (K_f D)/(E h) = (2.2×10⁹ × 0.5) / (200×10⁹ × 0.01) = 0.55. Since the pipe is anchored, the denominator is 1 + 0.55 = 1.55. The coupled wave speed c = c₀ / √1.55 ≈ 1483 / 1.245 ≈ 1191 m/s. The Joukowsky pressure rise Δp = ρ_f c Δv = 1000 × 1191 × 2 = 2.38 MPa. If the pipe were free to move axially, the denominator would be 1 + 0.55/(1−ν²) = 1 + 0.55/0.91 ≈ 1.604, giving c ≈ 1483/1.267 ≈ 1171 m/s and Δp ≈ 2.34 MPa. The anchored case yields a slightly higher pressure surge because the pipe cannot relieve strain by axial expansion.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives pipe material properties and asks for surge pressure, first identify whether the pipe is anchored or free. Write the denominator explicitly: 1 + (K_f D)/(E h) for anchored, 1 + (K_f D)/[E h (1−ν²)] for free. Compute c₀ from fluid properties, then apply the square‑root correction. Remember that the Joukowsky formula uses the *coupled* wave speed, not the free‑field sound speed. A quick sanity check: the coupled speed is always lower than c₀, so the pressure rise is lower than ρ_f c₀ Δv.

Nonlinear Wave Dynamics in Josephson Junction Arrays: Discrete Breathers and Synchronization

A one‑dimensional array of identical Josephson junctions biased by a constant current Ib is described by the resistively and capacitively shunted junction (RCSJ) model coupled through the superconducting loop inductances. The gauge‑invariant phase difference across the n‑th junction, φn, obeys C∂²φn/∂t² + (1/R)∂φn/∂t + Ic sin φn = Ib + (Φ0/2πL)(φn+1 − 2φn + φn−1), where C is junction capacitance, R shunt resistance, Ic critical current, L loop inductance, and Φ0 = h/2e the flux quantum. In the lossless, dispersionless limit this reduces to the discrete sine‑Gordon equation, which supports topological solitons (fluxons) and, crucially, *discrete breathers* — time‑periodic, spatially localised excitations that exist because the nonlinear on‑site potential (the Josephson cosine) creates frequencies outside the linear phonon band. The linear dispersion relation for small‑amplitude plasma oscillations is ω² = ωp² + (2/√LC)(1 − cos k), with plasma frequency ωp = √(2πc/Φ0C). A breather with frequency ωb > ωmax = √(ωp² + 4/√LC) can be exponentially localised; its core oscillates at ωb while the tails decay as exp(−κ|n|) with κ = arccosh[1 + (ωb² − ωp²)√LC/2]. When a weak AC drive Iac cos(ωdt) is added, the array can phase‑lock to the drive, exhibiting Shapiro steps in the current‑voltage characteristic at voltages V = n(Φ0ωd/2π). Synchronization of breathers across the array leads to coherent radiation at the drive frequency, a principle exploited in Josephson voltage standards and terahertz emitters. In the presence of disorder (spread in Ic or C) the breathers can become pinned, and the array may show a transition from coherent to incoherent dynamics as the drive amplitude is varied. Examiner traps include confusing the plasma frequency ωp (on‑site) with the bottom of the linear band, neglecting the factor 2π in the flux quantum when converting voltage to frequency, and assuming that discrete breathers exist for any nonlinearity — they require the breather frequency to lie *outside* the linear spectrum, which imposes a minimum amplitude threshold.

Wave Propagation in Chiral and Bianisotropic Media: Optical Activity and Negative Refraction

Chiral media are characterised by a magnetoelectric coupling that links the electric displacement D to the magnetic field H and the magnetic induction B to the electric field E. The constitutive relations for a reciprocal, isotropic chiral medium are D = εE + iκ√(εμ)H and B = μH − iκ√(εμ)E, where κ is the dimensionless chirality parameter (|κ| < 1 for passive media). Substituting into Maxwell’s curl equations yields two uncoupled circularly polarised eigenwaves with refractive indices n± = √(εμ) ± κ. The difference n+ − n− = 2κ produces optical activity: a linearly polarised wave rotates its polarisation plane by an angle θ = (π/λ)(n+ − n−)z = (2πκ/λ)z after propagating distance z. If the chirality is strong enough that κ > √(εμ), one of the indices becomes negative, leading to *negative refraction* for that circular polarisation while the other remains positive. This phenomenon does not require simultaneously negative ε and μ; it arises purely from the magnetoelectric coupling. In bianisotropic media the most general linear constitutive tensor includes four 3×3 tensors: ε, μ, ξ, ζ linking D, B to E, H. The dispersion relation for plane waves becomes a quartic in the wavevector, yielding up to four eigenmodes with distinct polarisation and propagation characteristics. For a Tellegen medium (ξ = ζ = χI) the eigenwaves are still circularly polarised but with indices n± = √(εμ − χ²) ± χ. The condition for negative refraction in a Tellegen medium is χ > √(εμ). Practical chiral metamaterials achieve large κ by arranging metallic helices or split‑ring resonators with broken mirror symmetry; the effective chirality peaks near the electric and magnetic resonances, often accompanied by strong dispersion and loss. Examiner traps include using the non‑chiral Fresnel formulas at an interface between chiral and achiral media (the boundary conditions couple the two circular polarisations), forgetting that the wave impedance for each eigenmode is Z± = √(μ/ε) (1 ∓ κ/√(εμ))⁻¹, and assuming that negative n implies backward power flow for *both* polarisations — only the mode with negative index exhibits a Poynting vector opposite to the wavevector.

Quantum Wave Packet Dynamics in Time‑Dependent Quadratic Potentials: Squeezing and Revivals

A quantum particle in a harmonic trap with a time‑dependent frequency ω(t) is governed by the Hamiltonian H = p²/2m + ½mω(t)²x². Because the Hamiltonian remains quadratic, an initial Gaussian wave packet stays Gaussian for all times; its evolution is fully described by the complex width parameter σ(t) = ⟨x²⟩ + i⟨xp+px⟩/2m. The equation of motion for σ is the Ermakov equation: d²σ/dt² + ω(t)²σ = ℏ²/(4m²σ³). A particular solution is obtained from two independent real solutions u₁(t), u₂(t) of the classical equation ü + ω(t)²u = 0 with Wronskian W = u₁u̇₂ − u̇₁u₂ = 1. Then σ(t) = (u₁ + iu₂)²/(2mω₀) for an initial ground state of frequency ω₀. The position variance is ⟨x²⟩ = Reσ, and the momentum variance ⟨p²⟩ = m²|σ̇|²/Reσ + ℏ²/(4 Reσ). Squeezing occurs when ⟨x²⟩ falls below the ground‑state value ℏ/(2mω₀); this requires the classical solutions to develop a phase‑space correlation such that the Wigner ellipse rotates and compresses along one quadrature. For a sudden quench ω(t) = ω₁ Θ(t) the variances oscillate at 2ω₁, producing periodic squeezing and anti‑squeezing. If the frequency is modulated periodically, ω²(t) = ω₀²[1 + ε cos(2ω₀t)], the system exhibits parametric resonance: the classical solutions grow exponentially, and the quantum variances grow without bound (in the ideal lossless model), signalling the breakdown of the Gaussian approximation when higher‑order terms become relevant. For an anharmonic perturbation λx⁴ the Gaussian ansatz fails at long times; however, for short times the wave packet exhibits *fractional revivals* at rational fractions of the classical period T = 2π/ω₀, where the autocorrelation function |⟨ψ(0)|ψ(t)⟩|² shows peaks. The revival time for a quartic oscillator is Trev = 2πℏ/(3λ⟨x⁴⟩₀). Examiner traps include confusing the classical Ermakov invariant with the quantum adiabatic invariant, neglecting the ℏ²/(4m²σ³) term when the packet is narrow, and assuming that a sudden frequency change produces a coherent state — it produces a squeezed vacuum unless the quench is accompanied by a displacement.

Nonlinear Wave Dynamics in Optical Cavities: Kerr Comb Generation and Dissipative Solitons

In high‑Q whispering‑gallery or microring resonators the intracavity field obeys a driven, damped nonlinear Schrödinger equation known as the Lugiato‑Lefever equation. The equation reads ∂ₜA = [−α − iΔ + iβ₂∂²_θ + iγ|A|²]A + √(θₑ)S_in, where A(θ,t) is the slowly varying envelope, α the total loss rate, Δ the pump‑cavity detuning, β₂ the group‑velocity‑dispersion coefficient, γ the Kerr nonlinearity, θₑ the coupling coefficient and S_in the pump amplitude. When the pump is red‑detuned (Δ>0) the homogeneous steady state becomes modulationally unstable and a periodic train of dissipative Kerr solitons forms. Each soliton corresponds to a single pulse circulating in the cavity; the repetition rate equals the free‑spectral range (FSR) and the comb lines are spaced by the FSR. The number of solitons N is determined by the balance between parametric gain and loss, roughly N ≈ √(P_inγ/α³) for anomalous dispersion. Experimental platforms such as Si₃N₄ microrings (Q≈10⁶) and crystalline CaF₂ resonators (Q>10⁸) have demonstrated octave‑spanning combs with >10⁴ lines. Key examiner points: the soliton existence range is bounded by the so‑called “soliton existence curve” in the (Δ, P_in) plane; crossing the lower boundary triggers a chaotic breathing regime, while the upper boundary leads to a Turing‑pattern (primary comb) state. The Raman self‑frequency shift adds an effective term iτ_R A∂ₜ|A|² that red‑shifts the soliton spectrum and can limit bandwidth. Thermal nonlinearity (thermo‑optic coefficient) introduces a slow detuning drift that must be compensated by active feedback or by operating in the “self‑injection locking” regime where the pump laser follows the cavity resonance.

Wave Propagation in Magnetoelastic Media: Magnetoacoustic Coupling

In a ferromagnetic solid the elastic displacement u and the magnetisation deviation m are coupled through the magnetoelastic energy density W_me = B₁(ε_xx m_x² + ε_yy m_y² + ε_zz m_z²) + 2B₂(ε_xy m_x m_y + ε_yz m_y m_z + ε_zx m_z m_x), where ε_ij are strain components and B₁, B₂ are magnetoelastic coupling constants. Linearising the Landau‑Lifshitz‑Gilbert equation together with the Navier equation yields two coupled wave equations: ρ∂ₜ²u = (λ+2μ)∇(∇·u) − μ∇×(∇×u) + ∇·σ_me and ∂ₜm = −γ m×H_eff + α_G m×∂ₜm + γ m×h_me. Here σ_me = ∂W_me/∂ε is the magnetoelastic stress and h_me = −∂W_me/∂m the effective magnetoelastic field. For a wave propagating along the x‑axis with magnetisation along z, the dispersion relation factorises into a pure shear branch ω² = μk²/ρ and a coupled magneto‑longitudinal branch given by the determinant |ρω² − (λ+2μ)k² −iB₁kM_s; iγB₁kM_s ω² − ω_m²| = 0, where ω_m = γ(H₀ + 2K_u/M_s) is the ferromagnetic resonance frequency. The avoided crossing near ω≈ω_m produces a hybrid magnetoacoustic mode with mixed elastic‑magnetic character; the group velocity can be reduced by an order of magnitude, enabling slow‑sound devices. In polycrystalline ferrites the effective coupling is reduced by texture averaging, but in single‑crystal YIG films (B₁≈−5×10⁶ J/m³) the hybridisation gap can reach several GHz. Examiner traps include neglecting the Gilbert damping term α_G when estimating the linewidth of the hybrid mode, using the isotropic elastic constants for a cubic crystal without rotating the stiffness tensor, and assuming the magnetoelastic stress is purely longitudinal — the off‑diagonal B₂ terms generate shear‑magnon coupling for oblique propagation.

Deriving the coupled magnetoelastic wave equations
Starting from the magnetoelastic energy density W_me = B₁ ε_xx m_z² for a wave travelling along x with equilibrium magnetisation along z, obtain the linearised equations of motion for the longitudinal displacement u_x and the transverse magnetisation component m_y. Show that the coupled dispersion relation reduces to (ρω² − (λ+2μ)k²)(ω² − ω_m²) = (γB₁kM_s)².
The magnetoelastic stress σ_me_xx = ∂W_me/∂ε_xx = B₁ m_z² ≈ B₁ M_s² + 2B₁ M_s m_z. The linearised Navier equation for u_x gives ρ∂ₜ²u_x = (λ+2μ)∂ₓ²u_x + ∂ₓσ_me_xx. Since m_z is small, the driving term is 2B₁ M_s ∂ₓm_z. The Landau‑Lifshitz equation for m_y (perpendicular to M_s) reads ∂ₜm_y = −γ M_s h_me_y + α_G M_s ∂ₜm_x. The magnetoelastic field h_me_y = −(1/M_s)∂W_me/∂m_y = 0 for this geometry, but the torque from σ_me produces an effective field h_me_x = −2B₁ ε_xx M_s/μ₀. Using ε_xx = ∂ₓu_x and eliminating m_z via the precession equation yields the coupled algebraic system (−ρω² + (λ+2μ)k²) u_x = 2iB₁ M_s k m_z and (−ω² + ω_m²) m_z = iγB₁ k M_s u_x. Solving the 2×2 determinant gives the stated dispersion relation.
Exam‑day strategy
When a problem asks for the hybrid magnetoacoustic dispersion, first write the uncoupled elastic and magnetic branches, then identify the off‑diagonal coupling term from the magnetoelastic energy. The determinant of the 2×2 matrix is the quickest route; avoid re‑deriving the full vector equations unless explicitly required.

Quantum Field Theory of Phonons: Second Quantization and Thermal Conductivity

The lattice Hamiltonian H = Σ_q ħω_q (a_q† a_q + ½) is obtained by expanding the displacement field in normal modes u(r) = Σ_q √(ħ/2ρVω_q) ε_q (a_q eiq·r + a_q† e−iq·r), where ε_q is the polarisation vector. Phonon‑phonon interactions arise from the anharmonic terms in the potential energy; the lowest order is the three‑phonon vertex Vq1q2q3 ∝ Σijk Φijk εq1,i εq2,j εq3,k with momentum conservation q1+q2+q3 = G (reciprocal lattice vector). At low temperatures (T≪Θ_D) only long‑wavelength acoustic phonons are excited, τ_U ∝ eΘ_D/3T leading to κ ∝ T³. Nonlinear Wave Propagation in Superconducting Transmission Lines: Kinetic Inductance and Parametric Amplification

A superconducting coplanar waveguide (CPW) exhibits a kinetic inductance L_k = μ₀λ_L² / (t w) per unit length, where λ_L is the London penetration depth, t the film thickness and w the centre‑conductor width. The total inductance L = L_g + L_k (geometric + kinetic) can be dominated by L_k in high‑kinetic‑inductance materials such as NbN (λ_L≈200 nm) or granular Al. The nonlinear current‑phase relation of a Josephson junction embedded in the line adds a term −E_J cos(φ) to the Lagrangian, where φ = (2π/Φ₀)∫V dt is the superconducting phase difference. Expanding to fourth order yields a Kerr nonlinearity coefficient K = (E_J/Φ₀²) (Φ_zpf/Φ₀)² with zero‑point flux Φ_zpf = √(ħZ/2) and characteristic impedance Z = √(L/C). The resulting Hamiltonian for a single mode is H = ħω a†a + (ħK/2) a†a†aa. When the line is pumped at 2ω_p ≈ 2ω, the three‑wave mixing term (a†a†a_p + h.c.) enables phase‑sensitive parametric amplification with gain G = 1/|1 − (P_p/P_th)|², where P_th = ħω²/(4K) is the threshold pump power. In the quantum‑limited regime the added noise approaches the standard quantum limit of half a photon. Traveling‑wave parametric amplifiers (TWPAs) use a periodic modulation of L_k (e.g. by a series array of Josephson junctions) to achieve phase matching Δk = 2k_p − k_s − k_i = 0 over a bandwidth >1 GHz. Wave Propagation in Active Media: Gain, Lasing, and Amplified Spontaneous Emission

In a medium with population inversion the susceptibility acquires a negative imaginary part χ''(ω) < 0, leading to a complex refractive index n = n' + i n'' with n'' < 0. The wave equation for the slowly varying envelope E(z,t) in a one‑dimensional gain medium reads ∂_z E = (g/2) E + i (β₂/2) ∂_t² E + i γ |E|² E, where g = −ω n''/c is the power gain coefficient. For a homogeneously broadened two‑level system g(ω) = g₀ / [1 + (ω−ω₀)²/Δω² + P/P_sat], with g₀ the small‑signal gain, Δω the homogeneous linewidth and P_sat the saturation power. In a laser cavity the threshold condition is g_th = α_tot + (1/2L) ln(1/R₁R₂), where α_tot includes internal loss and output coupling. Wave Propagation in Nonlinear Elastic Media: Higher‑Order Elastic Constants and Acoustic Nonlinearity Parameter

In a perfectly linear elastic solid the stress–strain relation is governed by the second‑order elastic constants (the Lamé parameters λ and μ). Real materials, however, exhibit measurable nonlinearity when the strain amplitude exceeds ~10⁻⁴. The next order in the strain energy expansion introduces the third‑order elastic constants (TOECs), often denoted by the Murnaghan constants l, m, n. For an isotropic solid the strain energy density W up to third order reads

W = ½ λ (tr ε)² + μ tr(ε²) + ⅓ l (tr ε)³ + m tr ε tr(ε²) + n tr(ε³)

where ε is the infinitesimal strain tensor. These constants modify the wave speed for finite‑amplitude longitudinal waves. The acoustic nonlinearity parameter β (sometimes written B/A) quantifies the relative change of the longitudinal wave speed c with particle velocity u:

c(u) = c₀ (1 + β u / c₀) with β = 1 + (B / 2A)

For an isotropic medium B/A = (l + 2m) / (λ + 2μ). Typical values for metals lie between 4 and 10, while for soft polymers β can exceed 100. The parameter β governs harmonic generation: a plane wave of frequency ω develops a second harmonic at 2ω whose amplitude grows linearly with propagation distance x until shock formation. The shock formation distance (the distance at which the waveform first becomes multivalued) is

xₛ = c₀² / (β ω u₀)

where u₀ is the peak particle velocity of the fundamental. In nondestructive evaluation, measuring the second‑harmonic amplitude as a function of x provides a direct estimate of β and thus of the TOECs, which are sensitive to microstructural damage such as dislocation density or fatigue cracks. Examiner focus: distinguishing the Murnaghan constants from the Brugger constants (which use a different strain measure), applying the weak‑shock theory to estimate the shock distance, and recognising that β for shear waves involves a different combination of TOECs (often denoted βₛ).

Quantum Fluctuations in Optomechanical Systems: Squeezed States and Backaction Evading Measurements

Cavity optomechanics couples a mechanical resonator (frequency ωₘ, effective mass m) to an optical cavity mode (frequency ω_c) via radiation pressure. The linearised Hamiltonian in a frame rotating at the laser drive frequency ω_L reads

H = ħ Δ a†a + ħ ωₘ b†b + ħ g (a† + a)(b† + b)

where Δ = ω_c − ω_L is the detuning, a (b) annihilates a cavity photon (phonon), and g = g₀ √n_c is the enhanced optomechanical coupling with single‑photon coupling g₀ and intracavity photon number n_c. Quantum Langevin equations for the quadratures X₁ = (a + a†)/√2, X₂ = (a − a†)/(i√2) and the mechanical quadratures q = (b + b†)/√2, p = (b − b†)/(i√2) reveal that the radiation‑pressure force introduces a backaction noise term proportional to X₁. When the cavity is driven on the red sideband (Δ = −ωₘ) the interaction becomes a beam‑splitter‑type Hamiltonian H_int = ħ g (a†b + ab†), enabling ground‑state cooling. Conversely, a blue‑detuned drive (Δ = +ωₘ) yields a two‑mode squeezing Hamiltonian H_int = ħ g (a†b† + ab) that generates entangled photon‑phonon pairs.

Squeezed states of the mechanical resonator are produced by driving the cavity with two tones symmetrically placed around the cavity resonance (Δ = ±ωₘ). The resulting effective Hamiltonian contains a single‑mode squeezing term ħ r (b†² + b²)/2 with squeezing parameter r ∝ g²/κ, where κ is the cavity linewidth. The mechanical quadrature variance can be reduced below the zero‑point level: ⟨Δq²⟩ = (½) e⁻²ʳ. This backaction‑evading (BAE) measurement scheme circumvent the standard quantum limit (SQL) on continuous position detection because the measured quadrature commutes with the Hamiltonian at the measurement frequency. The SQL for a force measurement is F_SQL = √(2 ħ m ωₘ γₘ), where γₘ is the mechanical damping rate. BAE techniques have demonstrated force sensitivities within a factor of 2 of F_SQL in membrane‑in‑the‑middle and microtoroid platforms. Examiner emphasis: deriving the linearised quantum Langevin equations, identifying the conditions for two‑mode versus single‑mode squeezing, and calculating the added noise of a phase‑sensitive amplifier in the presence of thermal bath occupancy n_th.

Worked Example: Squeezing Parameter from Experimental Parameters
A membrane resonator with ωₘ/2π = 1 MHz, m = 50 ng, and γₘ/2π = 10 Hz is coupled to a cavity with κ/2π = 100 kHz and single‑photon coupling g₀/2π = 200 Hz. The cavity is driven with two tones such that the intracavity photon number is n_c = 10⁵. Compute the single‑mode squeezing parameter r and the resulting mechanical quadrature variance ⟨Δq²⟩ in units of the zero‑point variance.
The enhanced coupling is g = g₀ √n_c = 2π × 200 Hz × √10⁵ ≈ 2π × 6.32 × 10⁴ Hz. For a two‑tone drive the effective squeezing rate is λ = 2g²/κ. With κ = 2π × 10⁵ Hz, λ = 2 (2π × 6.32 × 10⁴)² / (2π × 10⁵) ≈ 2π × 5.0 × 10⁴ Hz. The steady‑state squeezing parameter is r = λ / (γₘ + λ) ≈ λ/γₘ for λ ≫ γₘ. Here γₘ = 2π × 10 Hz, so r ≈ (5.0 × 10⁴) / 10 = 5 × 10³. This enormous r is unphysical because the linearised theory breaks down; in practice the maximum r is limited by the mechanical quality factor and optical absorption. A realistic r ≈ 1–2 yields ⟨Δq²⟩ = ½ e⁻²ʳ ≈ 0.07–0.14 times the zero‑point variance.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the squeezing parameter, first check whether the system is in the resolved‑sideband regime (κ ≪ ωₘ). If not, the simple formula r = 2g²/(κ γₘ) overestimates squeezing. Always state the validity condition and, if the numbers violate it, comment on the breakdown rather than producing an unrealistic r.

Nonlinear Wave Dynamics in Ferroelectric Domain Walls: Soliton‑Like Switching and Hysteresis

Ferroelectric materials possess a spontaneous polarization P that can be reversed by an applied electric field. The continuum description uses the Landau‑Ginzburg‑Devonshire (LGD) free‑energy density

F = α P² + β P⁴ + γ P⁶ + ½ g (∇P)² − E P

where α, β, γ are temperature‑dependent coefficients, g is the gradient‑energy coefficient, and E is the electric field. A 180° domain wall is a static solution connecting P = +Pₛ to P = −Pₛ. In the absence of an external field the wall profile is a hyperbolic tangent: P(x) = Pₛ tanh(x/δ) with wall width δ = √(g / 2|α|). When a time‑dependent field E(t) is applied, the wall moves. The dynamics can be mapped onto a driven sine‑Gordon equation for the phase φ = 2 arctan[exp(x/δ)] of the polarization:

∂²φ/∂t² + Γ ∂φ/∂t − c² ∂²φ/∂x² + ω₀² sin φ = f(t)

where Γ is a damping constant, c = √(g/χ) is the domain‑wall “sound” speed (χ is the susceptibility), ω₀ is the pinning frequency due to defects, and f(t) ∝ E(t). This equation admits soliton (kink) solutions that travel without dispersion. Under a constant field above the coercive threshold, the wall accelerates until limited by phonon emission (Cherenkov radiation) and reaches a terminal velocity v_term ≈ c √(1 − (E_c/E)²). The hysteresis loop arises because the wall nucleation field E_nuc is larger than the propagation field E_prop; the difference is quantified by the pinning potential landscape. In thin films, the wall width δ can be comparable to the film thickness, leading to a crossover from Ising‑type to Bloch‑type walls with a chiral structure. Examiner focus: deriving the static wall profile from the LGD functional, linearising the dynamics to obtain the domain‑wall mass and mobility, and explaining why the sine‑Gordon model predicts a maximum sustainable wall velocity (the “Walker breakdown” analogue).

Wave Propagation in Spatiotemporally Modulated Metamaterials: Nonreciprocal Bandgaps and Frequency Conversion

Metamaterials whose constitutive parameters (permittivity ε, permeability μ, or elastic moduli) are modulated in both space and time break Lorentz reciprocity and enable novel wave‑control functionalities. A one‑dimensional modulation of the refractive index n(x,t) = n₀ + Δn cos(Kx − Ωt) couples forward‑ and backward‑propagating waves via a travelling‑wave phase‑matching condition. The Floquet‑Bloch dispersion relation for a wave of frequency ω and wavevector k becomes

ω = ω₀(k) ± m Ω with k = k₀ ± m K

where m is an integer diffraction order. For a modulation travelling in the +x direction (Ω/K > 0), the coupling between the m = 0 and m = +1 harmonics opens a nonreciprocal bandgap: a forward wave at ω₀ can be Bragg‑reflected into a backward wave at ω₀ − Ω, while a backward wave at the same frequency sees no gap because the phase‑matching condition k₀ + K = k₁ is not satisfied. The width of the nonreciprocal gap is proportional to Δn. This principle underlies optical isolators without magnetic bias and parametric amplifiers that convert a signal at ω_s to an idler at ω_i = ω_s + Ω with gain G = sinh²(κ L), where κ ∝ Δn is the coupling coefficient and L the interaction length. In acoustic metamaterials, a travelling‑wave modulation of the bulk modulus B(x,t) = B₀ + ΔB cos(Kx − Ωt) produces analogous nonreciprocal bandgaps for longitudinal waves. Experimental implementations include spatiotemporal modulation of varactor‑loaded transmission lines, piezoelectric surface‑acoustic‑wave devices with interdigital transducers driven by phase‑shifted RF signals, and optomechanical arrays with a travelling‑wave optical pump. Examiner emphasis: writing the coupled‑mode equations for the Floquet harmonics, identifying the condition for unidirectional amplification (phase‑matching with a single diffraction order), and calculating the isolation ratio in dB from the imaginary part of the complex Bloch wavevector inside the gap.

  • Neglecting the pinning frequency ω₀ in the sine‑Gordon model of ferroelectric domain walls, which leads to an overestimate of the wall mobility.
  • Treating the spatiotemporal modulation as a static grating; the travelling‑wave nature is essential for nonreciprocity and frequency conversion.

  • Forgetting that the shock formation distance formula xₛ = c₀²/(β ω u₀) assumes a lossless medium; attenuation dramatically increases the effective shock distance.
  • Misidentifying the Walker breakdown field in ferroelectric walls as the coercive field; the former is a dynamic instability, the latter a static thermodynamic threshold.

These advanced topics illustrate how nonlinearity, quantum fluctuations, and spatiotemporal engineering extend the classical wave‑oscillation framework into regimes that are central to modern research and competitive examinations.

Wave Propagation in Superfluid Helium: Second Sound, Rotons, and Quantum Turbulence

Superfluid 4He below the lambda point (Tλ ≈ 2.17 K) supports two distinct hydrodynamic modes: ordinary first sound (density/pressure wave) and second sound (temperature/entropy wave). The two‑fluid model treats the liquid as a mixture of a normal component (density ρn, velocity **v**n) carrying entropy and a superfluid component (density ρs, velocity **v**s) with zero viscosity. Linearising the conservation laws yields coupled wave equations. First sound propagates at c1 ≈ 240 m s−1 (near 1 K) with both components moving in phase; second sound travels at c2 ≈ 20 m s−1 with the components moving out of phase, so the total mass current vanishes while an entropy current oscillates. The dispersion relation for second sound in the low‑frequency limit is ω = c2k, but at higher frequencies the roton minimum in the elementary excitation spectrum (Δ ≈ 8.65 K, p0/ħ ≈ 1.9 Å−1) introduces a strong k‑dependence. Rotons are gapped excitations with quadratic dispersion near the minimum: ε(p) = Δ + (p−p0)2/2μ, where μ ≈ 0.16 mHe. Their thermal population governs the temperature dependence of ρn and the attenuation of second sound (α ∝ ω2T−1e−Δ/kBT).

Quantum turbulence in superfluid helium consists of a tangle of quantised vortex lines, each carrying circulation κ = h/mHe ≈ 9.97 × 10−8 m2 s−1. At length scales larger than the intervortex spacing ℓ, the vortex tangle mimics classical Kolmogorov turbulence with an energy spectrum E(k) ∝ k−5/3. At scales below ℓ, the Kelvin‑wave cascade on individual vortices dominates, leading to a k−7/5 or k−3 spectrum depending on the reconnection dynamics. The mutual friction force **F**mf = Bρsκ**L** × (**v**n − **v**s) (where **L** is the vortex line density vector and B a temperature‑dependent coefficient) couples the normal and superfluid components and provides a dissipation channel for second sound. Experiments using second‑sound attenuation measure **L** and have confirmed the Vinen equation dL/dt = αvvnsL3/2 − βvκL2 for decaying turbulence.

Examiner focus: deriving the two‑fluid wave equations from the entropy and momentum conservation laws, obtaining the first‑ and second‑sound speeds in terms of ρ, ρn, ρs, and the specific heat ratio, and explaining why second sound is a pure temperature wave in the limit ρn ≪ ρs. Typical numerical problems ask for the temperature at which c2 = c1/10 given the empirical ρn(T) curve, or for the attenuation length of a 1 kHz second‑sound wave at 1.5 K using the roton‑phonon scattering formula.

Nonlinear Wave Dynamics in Dusty Plasmas: Dust‑Acoustic Waves, Solitons, and Instabilities

Dusty (complex) plasmas contain massive, negatively charged micron‑sized dust grains (mass md ≈ 10−12–10−9 kg, charge Zde ≈ 103–105e) immersed in an electron‑ion plasma. The large mass disparity introduces a low‑frequency dust‑acoustic wave (DAW) with phase speed Cd = √(ZdkBTi/md) (typically 1–10 cm s−1), where Ti is the ion temperature and electrons are assumed Boltzmann‑distributed. Linearising the dust fluid equations (continuity, momentum) together with Poisson’s equation and Boltzmann responses for electrons and ions yields the dispersion relation ω2 = k2Cd2/(1 + k2λD2), where λD is the effective Debye length. For kλD ≪ 1 the wave is acoustic; for kλD ≫ 1 it becomes a dust‑plasma oscillation at ωpd = √(Zd2e2nd0/ε0md).

Nonlinearity arises from the convective derivative in the dust momentum equation and from the quadratic dependence of the charging current on the grain potential. Using the reductive perturbation method (stretched coordinates ξ = ε(x − λt), τ = ε3t) one derives the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation for the dust density perturbation nd1: ∂nd1/∂τ + A nd1∂nd1/∂ξ + B ∂3nd1/∂ξ3 = 0, where A = (3λ/2)(1 − λ2/Cd2) and B = λ3/2ωpd2. For typical laboratory parameters (nd0 ≈ 108 m−3, Zd ≈ 104, Ti ≈ 0.03 eV) the soliton width is a few millimetres and the amplitude can reach 10–30 % of the equilibrium density.

Instabilities appear when streaming ions or electrons drive the DAW unstable. The ion‑streaming instability growth rate γ ≈ (k·**v**i0) (ωpi2/ωpd2)1/3 (for kλD ≪ 1) can exceed the dust‑neutral collision frequency, leading to the formation of dust‑density voids and self‑excited dust‑acoustic shocks. Lamb Waves and Harmonic Generation in Nonlinear Elastic Plates

Lamb waves are guided elastic waves in a plate of thickness h with traction‑free surfaces. The dispersion relation follows from the Rayleigh‑Lamb equations: for symmetric (S) modes tan(qh/2)/tan(ph/2) = −4k2pq/(q2 − k2)2 and for antisymmetric (A) modes the inverse ratio, where p2 = ω2/cL2 − k2, q2 = ω2/cT2 − k2, cL and cT are the longitudinal and shear bulk wave speeds. At low frequencies (kh ≪ 1) the fundamental S0 mode behaves as an extensional plate wave with phase velocity cp ≈ cL (thin‑plate limit), while the fundamental A0 mode is a flexural wave with ω ∝ k2 (cp ∝ √ω). Higher‑order modes have cut‑off frequencies at multiples of πcT/h and πcL/h.

Nonlinearity in isotropic plates is captured by the third‑order elastic constants (Murnaghan constants l, m, n) or equivalently the acoustic nonlinearity parameter β = (3cL2 + 4cT2 + …)/2cL2 for longitudinal waves. For Lamb waves the quadratic nonlinearity couples a primary mode at (ω, k) to a second‑harmonic mode at (2ω, 2k) provided phase matching holds: k(2ω) = 2k(ω). Because of dispersion, exact phase matching occurs only at discrete “internally resonant” frequencies where the dispersion curves intersect. For example, the S0 mode at a frequency‑thickness product f·h ≈ 1.2 MHz·mm in aluminium (cL ≈ 6320 m s−1, cT ≈ 3130 m s−1) satisfies kS0(2ω) = 2kS0(ω), enabling cumulative second‑harmonic growth. The amplitude evolution follows dA2/dx = iΓA12eiΔkx, where Γ ∝ βω2/ρcp3 and Δk = k(2ω) − 2k(ω). At perfect phase matching Δk = 0, the second harmonic grows linearly with distance: A2(x) = ΓA12x.

Higher‑order effects include third‑harmonic generation (via cascaded second‑harmonic + fundamental interaction) and the generation of shear‑horizontal (SH) modes through mode conversion at plate edges or material anisotropy. In weakly anisotropic composites the Christoffel equation yields quasi‑Lamb modes with polarization‑dependent nonlinearity, allowing selective harmonic generation by choosing the excitation angle. Experimental detection uses laser‑ultrasonics or piezoelectric transducers; the measured second‑harmonic amplitude versus propagation distance provides a direct estimate of β, which is sensitive to microstructural damage (dislocation density, fatigue cracks).

Examiner focus: writing the Rayleigh‑Lamb equations, identifying the low‑frequency asymptotes for S0 and A0, deriving the phase‑matching condition for second‑harmonic generation, and explaining why the A0 mode cannot generate a cumulative second harmonic in an isotropic plate (its dispersion is quadratic, so k(2ω) ≠ 2k(ω)). Numerical problems often give a plate material and thickness and ask for the lowest f·h product where S0–S0 internal resonance occurs.

Convected Wave Equation and Acoustic Doppler Shift in Moving Media

When the propagation medium moves with a steady velocity **U** (|**U**| ≪ c0), the acoustic pressure perturbation p′ satisfies the convected wave equation: (∂/∂t + **U**·∇)2p′ − c02∇2p′ = 0. In a uniform flow this reduces to a simple Galilean transformation: in the fluid frame the wave equation is ∂2p′/∂t′2 − c02∇′2p′ = 0 with t′ = t, **x**′ = **x** − **U**t. A plane wave p′ = Re{P ei(ωt − **k**·**x**)} in the laboratory frame has frequency ω and wavevector **k** related by the convected dispersion relation (ω − **U**·**k**)2 = c02k2. Solving for ω gives the Doppler‑shifted frequency ω = **U**·**k** ± c0k. For a source at rest in the lab emitting frequency ωs, the observed frequency in the moving medium is ω = ωs/(1 ∓ **U**·**n**/c0) where **n** is the unit propagation direction; the upper sign corresponds to downstream propagation.

In a sheared flow **U**(y) = U(y)**e**x, the convected wave equation becomes (∂/∂t + U∂/∂x)2p′ − c02(∂2/∂x2 + ∂2/∂y2)p′ = 0. Assuming a modal form p′ = φ(y)ei(ωt − kx) leads to the Rayleigh equation: (U − c)2φ″ − (U − c)U″φ − c02k2φ = 0, where c = ω/k is the complex phase speed. Critical layers occur where U(yc) = cr (real part of c); the singularity is regularised by viscosity or by a small imaginary part of c, leading to wave absorption or over‑reflection (the Miles‑Howard mechanism). For a linear shear U = Sy, the equation reduces to Airy’s equation and the dispersion relation can be expressed in terms of Airy functions.

Acoustic Doppler velocimetry exploits the frequency shift Δω = **k**·**U** to measure flow velocity. In a typical laser‑Doppler anemometer two intersecting beams create a fringe pattern with spacing δ = λ/(2sinθ); particles crossing the fringes scatter light at a Doppler frequency fD = **U**·**k**fringe = 2U sinθ/λ. For sound waves in a pipe with mean flow Mach number M = U/c0, the upstream and downstream cut‑off frequencies for a given duct mode shift by ±Mω, which must be accounted for in turbofan engine noise certification.

Examiner emphasis: deriving the convected wave equation from the linearised Euler and continuity equations in a moving medium, obtaining the Doppler‑shifted dispersion relation, and applying the Rayleigh equation to a simple shear profile. A frequent trap is to confuse the convected derivative (∂/∂t + **U**·∇) with the material derivative D/Dt when the mean flow is non‑uniform; the correct linearisation retains only the mean‑flow advection of perturbations, not the perturbation advection of the mean flow.

Gap Solitons and Bandgap Engineering in Nonlinear Photonic Crystals

Photonic crystals are periodic dielectric structures with a photonic bandgap (PBG) where linear Bloch waves cannot propagate. Introducing a Kerr nonlinearity (χ(3)) allows the formation of gap solitons — self‑localised pulses whose carrier frequency lies inside the PBG. The envelope of a weakly nonlinear Bloch wave near a band edge obeys the nonlinear coupled‑mode equations (or the massive Thirring model) for the forward (E+) and backward (E−) envelopes: i(∂E+/∂t + vg∂E+/∂z) + κE− + γ(|E+|2 + 2|E−|2)E+ = 0, and the symmetric equation for E−. Here vg is the group velocity at the band edge, κ is the coupling coefficient proportional to the Fourier component of the dielectric modulation, and γ = ω0n2/cAeff is the effective Kerr coefficient (n2 nonlinear index, Aeff effective mode area).

Stationary gap‑soliton solutions have the form E±(z,t) = A±(ξ)e−iΩt with ξ = z − vt, where the detuning Ω lies inside the gap (|Ω| < κ). The soliton exists for any velocity |v| < vg and its peak intensity scales as Ipeak ∝ (κ − Ω)/γ. The width Δξ ≈ vg/√(κ2 − Ω2) diverges as the soliton frequency approaches the band edge (Ω → ±κ), reflecting the vanishing group velocity. Moving gap solitons can be accelerated by a frequency chirp or by a gradient in the lattice period (adiabatic compression). Collisions between two gap solitons are nearly elastic for low velocities but become inelastic near the band edge, generating radiation into the allowed bands.

Bandgap engineering uses tailored superlattices (e.g., chirped Bragg gratings, Moiré patterns, or quasi‑periodic Fibonacci sequences) to create multiple overlapping gaps or to flatten the dispersion curve (slow‑light regime). In a χ(3) medium, a flat band enhances γeff ∝ 1/vg, reducing the soliton power threshold to the microwatt level in silicon‑on‑insulator waveguides (n2 ≈ 6 × 10−18 m2 W−1, Aeff ≈ 0.1 µm2). Two‑photon absorption (TPA) and free‑carrier absorption (FCA) introduce loss and an effective nonlinear loss coefficient βTPA, modifying the coupled‑mode equations with an imaginary γ. The figure of merit for all‑optical switching is FOM = n2/(λβTPA); silicon at 1550 nm has FOM ≈ 0.5, while chalcogenide glasses reach FOM > 100.

Examiner focus: writing the coupled‑mode equations from the Maxwell‑Bloch system with a Kerr nonlinearity, deriving the gap‑soliton existence condition (|Ω| < κ), and explaining how the soliton velocity relates to the group‑velocity dispersion sign. Numerical questions often give a Bragg grating period Λ = 500 nm, index modulation Δn = 10−3, and ask for the minimum peak power to form a fundamental gap soliton of width 10 ps in a silica fibre (n2 = 2.6 × 10−20 m2 W−1).
