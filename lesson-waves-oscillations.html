Waves & Oscillations — SHM, Damped/Driven Oscillations, Wave Equation & Standing Waves

Waves and oscillations are ubiquitous in physics — from the vibration of a guitar string to the propagation of light and sound. Simple harmonic motion (SHM) is the prototypical oscillation: a mass m on a spring (restoring force F = −kx) satisfies the differential equation d²x/dt² + ω₀²x = 0, where ω₀ = √(k/m) is the natural angular frequency. The solution is x(t) = A cos(ω₀t + φ). Energy in SHM alternates between kinetic (K = ½mv²) and potential (U = ½kx²); total E = ½kA² is constant. The simple pendulum approximates SHM for small angles (θ < 15°): ω₀ = √(g/L). The physical pendulum (extended body) has ω₀ = √(Mgd/I). Damped oscillations include a velocity-dependent damping force F = −bv: the equation becomes d²x/dt² + (b/m)dx/dt + ω₀²x = 0, leading to underdamped (oscillatory decay), critically damped (fastest return to equilibrium), and overdamped (slow exponential decay) regimes. Driven oscillations with a sinusoidal driving force produce resonance — maximum amplitude at ω = ω₀, with a quality factor Q = ω₀/γ characterizing the sharpness of resonance. The wave equation ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²)∂²y/∂t² describes waves on a string, with wave speed v = √(T/μ) (tension/linear density). Traveling waves y(x,t) = f(x ∓ vt) propagate without changing shape. Standing waves on a fixed string of length L satisfy λ_n = 2L/n, f_n = nv/(2L). Fourier analysis decomposes any periodic waveform into a sum of sinusoidal harmonics.

Part 1

🔄 Simple Harmonic Motion

Oscillations
The Harmonic Oscillator, Energy in SHM, the Simple and Physical Pendulums
Simple harmonic motion occurs when the restoring force is proportional to the displacement (Hooke's law: F = −kx). The equation of motion: m d²x/dt² = −kx, or d²x/dt² + ω₀²x = 0 with ω₀ = √(k/m). The general solution: x(t) = A cos(ω₀t + φ), where A is the amplitude and φ is the phase constant determined by initial conditions. The velocity v(t) = −ω₀A sin(ω₀t + φ) and acceleration a(t) = −ω₀²A cos(ω₀t + φ) = −ω₀²x(t). The period T = 2π/ω₀ = 2π√(m/k); the frequency f = 1/T = ω₀/(2π). Energy in SHM: kinetic energy K = ½mv² = ½mω₀²A² sin²(ω₀t + φ), potential energy U = ½kx² = ½kA² cos²(ω₀t + φ). Total energy E = K + U = ½kA² = constant. At equilibrium (x = 0): U = 0, K = E (max). At extreme displacement (x = ±A): K = 0, U = E. The simple pendulum has restoring torque τ = −mgL sin θ. For small angles sin θ ≈ θ, giving d²θ/dt² + (g/L)θ = 0, so ω₀ = √(g/L) and T = 2π√(L/g). The physical pendulum (extended rigid body pivoted at O, center of mass at distance d): τ = −Mgd sin θ, I d²θ/dt² + Mgd θ = 0, ω₀ = √(Mgd/I). The torsional pendulum: τ = −κθ (where κ is the torsion constant), ω₀ = √(κ/I). The SHM approximation fails for large amplitudes where the restoring force is anharmonic — the motion becomes nonlinear with amplitude-dependent frequency (the pendulum period increases with amplitude via elliptic integrals).
Simple harmonic motion is the fundamental periodic motion in physics; its mathematical description applies to pendulums, springs, molecular vibrations, and countless other oscillating systems.
Part 2

📉 Damped and Driven Oscillations

Transient and Forced Motion
Damping Regimes, the Quality Factor, Driven Oscillators, and Resonance
Damped oscillations include a dissipative force proportional to velocity: F_damp = −bv = −b dx/dt. The equation: m d²x/dt² + b dx/dt + kx = 0. Dividing by m: d²x/dt² + γ dx/dt + ω₀²x = 0, where γ = b/m is the damping coefficient. The characteristic equation: r² + γr + ω₀² = 0, with roots r = −γ/2 ± √(γ²/4 − ω₀²). Three regimes: Underdamped (γ < 2ω₀): x(t) = A₀ e^{−γt/2} cos(ω_d t + φ) where ω_d = √(ω₀² − γ²/4) — the system oscillates with exponentially decaying amplitude. The time constant τ = 2/γ; the amplitude drops by factor e in each τ. Critically damped (γ = 2ω₀): x(t) = (A + Bt)e^{−γt/2} — the system returns to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without oscillating — used in shock absorbers and meter needles. Overdamped (γ > 2ω₀): x(t) = A₁e^{r₁t} + A₂e^{r₂t} (both r negative) — slow exponential decay. The quality factor Q = ω₀/γ = ω₀/Δω characterizes the sharpness of resonance — related to the number of oscillations for the energy to decay to 1/e of its initial value (N_e = Q/π). Driven (forced) oscillations: the equation with a sinusoidal driving force F(t) = F₀ cos(ωt) is d²x/dt² + γ dx/dt + ω₀²x = (F₀/m) cos(ωt). The steady-state solution is x(t) = A(ω) cos(ωt − δ), where A(ω) = F₀/m / √((ω₀² − ω²)² + γ²ω²) and tan δ = γω/(ω₀² − ω²). Resonance: maximum amplitude occurs at ω_r = √(ω₀² − γ²/2) ≈ ω₀ (for small γ). The amplitude at resonance: A_max = F₀/(mγω₀). The sharpness of the resonance peak is characterized by the full width at half-maximum Δω = γ. Resonance disasters: the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse (1940) due to wind-induced aeroelastic flutter. Practical applications: tuning circuits, MRI, atomic clocks, and musical instruments.
Damping determines how oscillations decay; driven oscillations reveal resonance — a phenomenon where even a small driving force can produce large amplitude oscillations at the natural frequency.
Part 3

🌊 The Wave Equation and Traveling Waves

Wave Propagation
The Wave Equation, Wave Speed, Traveling Waves, Superposition, and Reflection/Transmission
A wave is a disturbance that propagates through a medium without transporting matter. For a wave on a string, the wave equation is ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²) ∂²y/∂t², where v = √(T/μ) — T is the tension, μ is the linear mass density. The general solution is y(x,t) = f(x − vt) + g(x + vt) — any function of (x − vt) represents a wave traveling to the right, and any function of (x + vt) represents a wave traveling to the left. For a sinusoidal traveling wave: y(x,t) = A sin(kx − ωt + φ), where k = 2π/λ is the wavenumber, ω = 2πf is the angular frequency, λ is the wavelength, and the wave speed v = ω/k = λf. The power transmitted by a wave on a string: P = ½μvω²A². The principle of superposition: the net displacement at any point is the sum of the displacements of individual waves. This leads to interference: constructive when waves are in phase, destructive when out of phase. Reflection and transmission: at a boundary between two media with different wave speeds, part of the wave is reflected and part transmitted. For a string with a discontinuity in linear density: if the second medium has lower impedance (μ₂v₂ < μ₁v₁), the reflected pulse is inverted (phase shift of π). If higher impedance, no inversion. At a fixed end, the reflected wave is inverted; at a free end, it is not. Sound waves are longitudinal pressure waves in a medium. The wave speed for sound in a fluid: v = √(B/ρ) (B is bulk modulus). In an ideal gas: v = √(γRT/M) (γ = C_P/C_V). The Doppler effect: observed frequency f' = f(v ± vₒ)/(v ∓ v_s) for moving observer and source. The Mach number M = v_s/v; when v_s > v, a shock wave (sonic boom) forms at the Mach angle sin θ = v/v_s.
The wave equation unifies the description of all types of waves; the principle of superposition governs interference, reflection, and transmission phenomena across all wave systems.
Part 4

🎵 Standing Waves, Fourier Analysis, and Nonlinear Waves

Boundary Effects
Standing Waves on Strings and in Pipes, Fourier Series, the Wave Equation in Higher Dimensions, and Solitons
Standing waves arise from the interference of two waves traveling in opposite directions: y(x,t) = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt). The spatial part sin(kx) has nodes (zero displacement) at x = 0, λ/2, λ, 3λ/2, ... and antinodes (maximum displacement) at x = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4, ... For a string fixed at both ends of length L, the boundary conditions y(0,t) = y(L,t) = 0 give kL = nπ, so λ_n = 2L/n and f_n = nv/(2L) = nf₁, where f₁ = v/(2L) is the fundamental frequency. The harmonics: n = 1 (fundamental), n = 2 (first overtone, octave above fundamental), n = 3 (second overtone), etc. The general motion is a superposition of normal modes: y(x,t) = Σ_n A_n sin(nπx/L) cos(ω_n t + φ_n). For a pipe open at both ends: antinodes at both ends → λ_n = 2L/n, f_n = nv/(2L). For a pipe closed at one end: node at closed end, antinode at open end → λ_n = 4L/n (n odd), f_n = nv/(4L). Fourier analysis: any periodic function f(t) with period T can be expanded as a Fourier series: f(t) = a₀/2 + Σ_{n=1}^∞ [a_n cos(nω₀t) + b_n sin(nω₀t)], where ω₀ = 2π/T. The Fourier coefficients a_n and b_n are extracted using orthogonality integrals. The Fourier transform generalizes this to non-periodic functions: F(ω) = ∫ f(t) e^{−iωt} dt. The bandwidth theorem: Δω Δt ≥ ½. The wave equation in 2D and 3D: ∇²u = (1/v²)∂²u/∂t². In 2D, circular symmetry leads to Bessel functions; the 2D wave equation governs drumhead vibrations with normal modes determined by zeros of Bessel functions. In 3D, spherical waves: u = (1/r) f(r ∓ vt). Nonlinear waves: the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation ∂_t u + u ∂_x u + δ² ∂_x³ u = 0 describes shallow water waves and exhibits soliton solutions — solitary waves that preserve their shape and speed upon collision. Solitons arise from the balance of nonlinearity (which steepens the wave) and dispersion (which spreads it). Applications: fiber optic communications (solitons as data bits), rogue waves, and the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou recurrence.
Standing waves are the fundamental vibration patterns of bounded systems; Fourier decomposition reveals any vibration as a superposition of harmonics, while nonlinear waves exhibit soliton behavior with remarkable stability.
