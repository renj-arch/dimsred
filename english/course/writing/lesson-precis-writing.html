Précis Writing

Updated 17 Nov 2026
Reduce a passage to one-third length while keeping the argument, figures and tone.
Précis · Summarising · Compression · Tone

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the fundamental concepts of Précis Writing
  • Apply key formulas and techniques to solve problems
  • Practice with exam-level questions to build speed and accuracy

1. Understanding Précis Writing

1.1 Definition and Purpose

A précis is a concise restatement of a passage that preserves the author’s central argument, the logical development of that argument, and the distinctive tone in which it is expressed, while reducing the original word count to roughly one‑third. The purpose of the exercise is to demonstrate that the writer can identify the essential structure of a text, discard superfluous material, and reproduce the core meaning in a fresh, formal idiom. Because the précis must stand alone, a reader who has never seen the source should be able to grasp the main claim, the supporting reasons, and the author’s attitude without any loss of nuance.

1.2 Distinction from Summary and Paraphrase

Although the terms summary, paraphrase, and précis are often used interchangeably in casual conversation, they denote different operations in descriptive English. A summary may be any shortened version, sometimes allowing the writer to reorganise ideas or to omit minor points without strict length constraints. A paraphrase rewrites the passage in different words but usually retains the original length and may keep illustrative details. A précis, by contrast, imposes a quantitative target — about one‑third of the source — and a qualitative demand that the logical skeleton and the author’s stance remain intact. This dual requirement makes the précis a more disciplined test of comprehension and expression.

1.3 The One‑Third Rule and Word‑Count Management

The one‑third rule is not an arbitrary figure; it reflects the typical proportion of essential content in well‑structured expository writing. When a passage of three hundred words is reduced to one hundred, the writer must decide which clauses carry the argumentative weight and which serve only to elaborate, exemplify, or embellish. Effective word‑count management begins with a precise count of the original, division by three to obtain a target, and a disciplined drafting process that constantly checks the running total against that target. Exceeding the limit by a wide margin signals that illustrative material has been retained; falling far short suggests that a crucial premise or the concluding implication has been lost.

1.4 Tone as a Non‑Negotiable Element

Tone conveys the author’s attitude — critical, tentative, celebratory, ironic — and is communicated through lexical choice, sentence rhythm, and the placement of qualifiers. A précis that flattens a scathing critique into a neutral description fails the test because it misrepresents the source’s rhetorical purpose. Preserving tone therefore requires the writer to mirror the author’s evaluative vocabulary (for example, “flawed”, “commendable”, “questionable”) and to avoid introducing a personal stance that would shift the register toward advocacy or apology.

2. The Process of Constructing a Précis

2.1 First Reading for Gist

The initial reading should be rapid yet attentive, aiming to capture the overall thrust of the passage without pausing over unfamiliar vocabulary. The reader asks: what is the central claim? What problem does the author address? What conclusion is reached? This macro‑level grasp prevents the later mistake of treating a supporting illustration as the main idea.

2.2 Second Reading for Structure and Argument

A slower second pass maps the internal architecture: the thesis statement, each major premise, the evidence offered, any concessions, and the final inference. Marking paragraph boundaries, transition signals, and recurring key terms creates a skeletal outline that will guide the drafting stage. The outline also reveals digressions — anecdotes, historical asides, rhetorical questions — that are candidates for omission.

2.3 Identification of Essential Elements

From the outline the writer extracts three categories of material that must survive compression: the central thesis, the logical reasons that support it, and any indispensable data such as proper nouns, dates, or quantitative figures that the argument depends upon. The author’s tonal markers — adjectives that signal judgement, modal verbs that indicate certainty or doubt — are also recorded because they cannot be paraphrased away without altering the stance.

2.4 Drafting in Own Words

Using the outline, the writer composes a first draft in continuous prose, employing third‑person indirect speech and a formal register. Sentences are combined where the original used several to express a single logical step, and subordinate clauses replace coordinate strings to achieve density. The draft is written without looking back at the source, ensuring genuine paraphrase rather than patchwork copying.

2.5 Revision for Tone, Length and Completeness

The revision cycle begins with a word count. If the draft exceeds the target, the writer trims redundant modifiers, merges adjacent clauses, and replaces phrases with single precise verbs. If it falls short, the writer checks whether a premise or the concluding implication has been inadvertently dropped and restores it in compressed form. A final read‑aloud confirms that the tone mirrors the source and that the précis reads as a coherent, self‑contained paragraph.

Worked Example: Compression and Tone Preservation
Original (78 words): “The new agricultural policy, announced last month, promises to double farmer incomes within five years by guaranteeing minimum support prices for twenty‑three crops and by expanding credit facilities through regional cooperatives. Critics argue that the price guarantees will distort market signals, leading to over‑production of water‑intensive crops, while the credit expansion may benefit only large landholders who already possess collateral.”
Précis (26 words): “The agricultural policy aims to double farmer incomes in five years via minimum support prices for twenty‑three crops and expanded cooperative credit, though critics warn of market distortion and unequal credit access.” The précis retains the policy’s twin instruments, the five‑year horizon, the critics’ two specific objections, and the cautious tone signalled by “warn”.
Examination‑Day Insight
Before writing, jot the original word count in the margin, divide by three, and circle the target. During drafting, keep a running tally every few sentences; this prevents the common last‑minute scramble to cut or pad. After the final full stop, read the précis once more solely for tonal fidelity — ask whether a reader would attribute the same attitude to the author.

3. What to Retain and What to Discard

3.1 Core Argument and Supporting Reasons

The backbone of any précis is the logical chain that links the thesis to its conclusion. Each major reason that the author advances must appear, albeit in condensed form, because omitting a premise breaks the argument and renders the précis incoherent. The writer therefore treats the argument as a series of indispensable links rather than a menu of selectable points.

3.2 Key Facts, Figures and Proper Nouns

Specific data — names of legislation, statistical magnitudes, geographical references — are retained only when they function as evidence for a claim. A figure cited merely to illustrate magnitude may be replaced by a qualitative descriptor (“a substantial increase”) if the exact number is not pivotal to the reasoning. However, when the argument hinges on a precise quantity — for example, a threshold that triggers a policy change — the number must survive compression.

3.3 Author’s Tone and Stance

Tone is conveyed through the author’s choice of evaluative language and through the framing of concessions. The précis must reproduce the same evaluative adjectives or their close synonyms, and must preserve the balance between assertion and qualification. If the source ends with a cautious “may” rather than a definitive “will”, the précis must reflect that tentativeness.

3.4 Material That Must Be Omitted

Examples, anecdotes, extended quotations, rhetorical flourishes, repetitions, and any passage that serves only to entertain or to illustrate without advancing the logical chain are excluded. The writer also removes first‑person references, direct speech, and any personal commentary that the source does not contain. The guiding principle is simple: if the sentence can be deleted without weakening the argument, it belongs in the discard pile.

4. Language Conventions and Structural Norms

4.1 Third‑Person Indirect Speech

A précis never uses “I”, “we”, or “you”. The writer reports the author’s ideas as “The author argues that…”, “It is contended that…”, or “The passage suggests…”. This convention maintains objectivity and signals that the précis is a representation, not an extension, of the original thought.

4.2 Tense Consistency and Formal Register

When the source discusses past events, the précis typically adopts the past tense; when it presents timeless principles, the present tense is appropriate. Shifting tenses arbitrarily creates confusion about the temporal status of the claims. The register remains formal throughout — contractions are avoided, colloquialisms are replaced by standard equivalents, and sentence structures favour subordination over coordination.

4.3 Paragraph Organisation

Although a précis may be a single paragraph, its internal organisation mirrors the source: an opening sentence that names the author (or the work) and states the thesis, a middle section that compresses the supporting reasoning in logical order, and a closing sentence that captures the conclusion or implication. Transitional phrases such as “furthermore”, “conversely”, “consequently” guide the reader through the condensed argument.

5. Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

5.1 Insertion of Personal Opinion

The most frequent lapse is the unconscious addition of the writer’s judgement — “the policy is wise” or “the argument is unconvincing”. Such insertions betray the requirement of fidelity. The safeguard is to ask after each sentence whether the claim originates in the source; if not, it must be removed.

5.2 Verbatim Copying and Inadequate Paraphrase

Copying whole clauses or merely substituting synonyms for isolated words produces a patchwork that fails the “own words” test. Effective paraphrase restructures the syntax, changes the grammatical subject, and replaces noun‑heavy phrases with strong verbs. Practising sentence‑level rewriting on unrelated texts builds this skill.

5.3 Distortion of Tone

Neutralising a critical passage or intensifying a tentative one alters the author’s rhetorical intent. The remedy is to highlight every evaluative term in the source before drafting and to ensure each appears, in some form, in the précis.

5.4 Length Deviations

Exceeding the one‑third ceiling usually signals retained illustrations; falling well below suggests missing premises. The disciplined word‑count check described in the process section eliminates both extremes.

6. Practice and Examination Strategy

6.1 Word‑Count Calculation Technique

At the start of the allotted time, count the words of the given passage (or use the provided count) and write the target figure prominently. Divide the target by the number of logical units identified in the outline to obtain a rough budget per unit; this prevents any single section from monopolising the allowance.

6.2 Timed Drafting Drill

Regular practice under strict time limits — for example, twenty minutes for a three‑hundred‑word passage — trains the mind to move swiftly from reading to outline to draft. The drill should include a final two‑minute review dedicated solely to tone verification and word‑count adjustment.

6.3 Self‑Review Checklist

After the draft is complete, the writer runs through a mental checklist: does the opening name the source and state the thesis? Are all major premises present in logical order? Is the conclusion captured? Has every evaluative adjective been mirrored? Is the word count within the acceptable band? Is the language third‑person, formal, and free of contractions? Answering each question affirmatively signals a précis ready for submission.

Mastery of précis writing rests on the disciplined interplay of analytical reading, logical extraction, and controlled expression, and the consistent application of the principles outlined above will enable any candidate to produce a faithful, concise, and tonally accurate précis under examination conditions.

Handling Nested Arguments and Counter‑Arguments in Précis

When a source embeds a primary claim within a series of qualifications, concessions, and rebuttals, the précis must preserve the logical hierarchy without reproducing every clause. Begin by mapping the argument tree: identify the thesis, each supporting premise, every conceded point, and each rebuttal. Represent concessions with verbs such as “acknowledges”, “grants”, or “admits” and rebuttals with “counters”, “refutes”, or “demonstrates”. This mapping prevents the common error of flattening a dialectical structure into a simple list of statements. In the draft, allocate word‑budget proportionally: the thesis and its strongest premises receive the largest share; concessions and rebuttals are compressed to a single clause each, provided their logical function remains clear. Preserve the author’s evaluative adjectives (e.g., “unconvincing”, “compelling”) because they signal the weight the writer assigns to each move. If the original uses a “although … nevertheless” pattern, render it as “Although the author concedes X, the argument maintains Y”. Such restructuring keeps the dialectic intact while meeting the one‑third length constraint.

Special care is required when the source contains multiple layers of counter‑argument (a counter‑counter‑argument). In these cases, collapse the innermost layer into a single evaluative phrase — “the rebuttal is ultimately dismissed” — unless the innermost layer introduces new evidence that the author treats as decisive. The guiding principle is fidelity to the author’s final stance, not to every intermediate turn. When the passage ends with a qualified conclusion (“the evidence suggests, but does not prove”), the précis must mirror that qualification; omitting it would distort tone.

Compressing Technical and Data‑Heavy Passages

Technical texts often present dense clusters of figures, terminology, and procedural detail. The précis writer must decide which data points are essential to the argument and which serve only as illustration. Retain any statistic that directly supports the thesis or a major premise — for example, a prevalence rate that underpins a policy recommendation. Omit peripheral numbers such as sample sizes, confidence intervals, or intermediate calculations unless the author explicitly argues from them. Replace noun‑heavy strings (“the measurement of the concentration of particulate matter”) with strong verbs (“measured particulate concentration”). Use standard abbreviations only after the first full expansion in the précis (e.g., “World Health Organization (WHO)”). When the source lists a series of comparable results, compress them into a range or a summary statistic (“values ranged from 12 to 18 µg/m³”). Preserve the author’s hedging language (“approximately”, “indicates”) because it conveys epistemic stance. If the passage includes a methodological description that the author later cites as a limitation, keep the limitation but drop the procedural minutiae. The goal is a précis that a reader unfamiliar with the discipline can follow without consulting the original.

Worked Compression of a Technical Excerpt
Original (120 words): “The study measured ambient PM2.5 concentrations at five urban monitoring stations over a twelve‑month period. The mean annual concentration was 35 µg/m³, with a standard deviation of 8 µg/m³. Seasonal peaks occurred in winter (mean 48 µg/m³) and summer (mean 22 µg/m³). The authors note that the monitoring network does not capture indoor exposure, which may account for up to 30 % of total personal exposure. They conclude that outdoor levels alone underestimate health risk.”
Précis (38 words): The study reports a mean annual PM2.5 level of 35 µg/m³ across five urban stations, with winter peaks of 48 µg/m³. The authors acknowledge that unmeasured indoor exposure may add 30 % to personal dose, arguing that outdoor data alone underestimate health risk.
Exam‑Day Data‑Selection Rule
Before drafting, underline every numeral and proper noun in the passage. Ask: does this figure advance the thesis or a major premise? If the answer is no, strike it. This two‑minute scan prevents the common trap of copying illustrative tables into the précis.

Resolving Implicit Premises and Ambiguous References

Many passages rely on unstated assumptions or pronouns whose antecedents are several sentences away. The précis writer must make these links explicit without adding interpretation. First, trace each pronoun (“it”, “this”, “such”) to its referent in the source. In the précis, replace the pronoun with the full noun phrase or a concise synonym (“the policy”, “the observed trend”). Second, identify any logical gaps: a claim that “therefore the program should be expanded” may rest on an implicit premise that “the program’s benefits outweigh its costs”. If the source never states that premise, the précis should not invent it; instead, render the conclusion as “the author recommends expansion, citing the reported benefits”. This preserves the author’s reasoning while flagging the missing link. When the passage contains vague quantifiers (“many”, “several”, “a number of”), retain the vagueness — do not substitute precise numbers. If the author uses a metaphor (“the economy is a ship without a rudder”), keep the metaphor only if it carries evaluative weight; otherwise, replace it with a literal paraphrase (“the economy lacks direction”). The overarching rule: clarify structure, never amplify content.

Précis of Multi‑Voice Texts: Dialogues, Reports, and Collaborative Documents

Texts that present multiple speakers — interview transcripts, panel discussions, or joint reports — require the précis to attribute each distinct position to its source. Begin by segmenting the passage into voice blocks. For each block, note the speaker’s role (e.g., “the chair”, “the dissenting expert”) and the stance taken. In the précis, use reporting verbs that reflect the speech act: “argues”, “contends”, “questions”, “concedes”. When two speakers share a view, combine them: “Both the chair and the economist argue that fiscal stimulus is necessary.” When they diverge, preserve the contrast: “While the chair supports stimulus, the dissenting expert warns of inflationary pressure.” Avoid merging opposing views into a false consensus. If the document concludes with a collective recommendation, state it as “the panel recommends…” and list any recorded dissent. Omit procedural dialogue (“shall we move to the next item?”) unless it reveals power dynamics relevant to the argument. The précis must remain a single, coherent narrative that a reader can follow without the original’s turn‑taking cues.

Advanced Syntactic Compression: Nominalisation, Ellipsis, and Clause Reduction

Beyond vocabulary substitution, expert précis writers exploit syntactic devices to shrink sentences while retaining meaning. Nominalisation converts verb‑centric clauses into dense noun phrases: “The committee decided to postpone the vote” becomes “The committee’s decision to postpone the vote”. Ellipsis removes recoverable elements: “The first study found a correlation; the second study found none” compresses to “The first study found a correlation; the second, none.” Clause reduction turns relative clauses into participial phrases: “The policy, which was introduced in 2015, reduced emissions” becomes “The 2015 policy reduced emissions.” Combine these techniques judiciously; over‑nominalisation can produce opaque prose. A practical drill: take a 150‑word paragraph and rewrite it in three successive passes — first replacing verbs with nouns, then applying ellipsis, then reducing clauses — counting words after each pass. The final version should sit within the one‑third target while preserving every logical relation. Remember that each compression step must be reversible in principle; if a reader cannot reconstruct the original claim, the compression has gone too far.

  • Flattening a dialectical structure into a simple list of statements.
  • Retaining peripheral statistics that do not support the thesis.
  • Replacing vague quantifiers with invented precise numbers.
  • Merging opposing voices in multi‑speaker texts into a false consensus.
  • Over‑nominalising to the point where logical relations become opaque.
  • Omitting the author’s hedging language, thereby distorting epistemic stance.
  • Applying these checks consistently ensures a faithful, concise précis.
