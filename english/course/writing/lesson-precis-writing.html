Précis Writing

Updated 23 Nov 2026
Reduce a passage to one-third length while keeping the argument, figures and tone.
Précis · Summarising · Compression · Tone

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the fundamental concepts of Précis Writing
  • Apply key formulas and techniques to solve problems
  • Practice with exam-level questions to build speed and accuracy

1. Understanding Précis Writing

1.1 Definition and Purpose

A précis is a concise restatement of a passage that preserves the author’s central argument, the logical development of that argument, and the distinctive tone in which it is expressed, while reducing the original word count to roughly one‑third. The purpose of the exercise is to demonstrate that the writer can identify the essential structure of a text, discard superfluous material, and reproduce the core meaning in a fresh, formal idiom. Because the précis must stand alone, a reader who has never seen the source should be able to grasp the main claim, the supporting reasons, and the author’s attitude without any loss of nuance.

1.2 Distinction from Summary and Paraphrase

Although the terms summary, paraphrase, and précis are often used interchangeably in casual conversation, they denote different operations in descriptive English. A summary may be any shortened version, sometimes allowing the writer to reorganise ideas or to omit minor points without strict length constraints. A paraphrase rewrites the passage in different words but usually retains the original length and may keep illustrative details. A précis, by contrast, imposes a quantitative target — about one‑third of the source — and a qualitative demand that the logical skeleton and the author’s stance remain intact. This dual requirement makes the précis a more disciplined test of comprehension and expression.

1.3 The One‑Third Rule and Word‑Count Management

The one‑third rule is not an arbitrary figure; it reflects the typical proportion of essential content in well‑structured expository writing. When a passage of three hundred words is reduced to one hundred, the writer must decide which clauses carry the argumentative weight and which serve only to elaborate, exemplify, or embellish. Effective word‑count management begins with a precise count of the original, division by three to obtain a target, and a disciplined drafting process that constantly checks the running total against that target. Exceeding the limit by a wide margin signals that illustrative material has been retained; falling far short suggests that a crucial premise or the concluding implication has been lost.

1.4 Tone as a Non‑Negotiable Element

Tone conveys the author’s attitude — critical, tentative, celebratory, ironic — and is communicated through lexical choice, sentence rhythm, and the placement of qualifiers. A précis that flattens a scathing critique into a neutral description fails the test because it misrepresents the source’s rhetorical purpose. Preserving tone therefore requires the writer to mirror the author’s evaluative vocabulary (for example, “flawed”, “commendable”, “questionable”) and to avoid introducing a personal stance that would shift the register toward advocacy or apology.

2. The Process of Constructing a Précis

2.1 First Reading for Gist

The initial reading should be rapid yet attentive, aiming to capture the overall thrust of the passage without pausing over unfamiliar vocabulary. The reader asks: what is the central claim? What problem does the author address? What conclusion is reached? This macro‑level grasp prevents the later mistake of treating a supporting illustration as the main idea.

2.2 Second Reading for Structure and Argument

A slower second pass maps the internal architecture: the thesis statement, each major premise, the evidence offered, any concessions, and the final inference. Marking paragraph boundaries, transition signals, and recurring key terms creates a skeletal outline that will guide the drafting stage. The outline also reveals digressions — anecdotes, historical asides, rhetorical questions — that are candidates for omission.

2.3 Identification of Essential Elements

From the outline the writer extracts three categories of material that must survive compression: the central thesis, the logical reasons that support it, and any indispensable data such as proper nouns, dates, or quantitative figures that the argument depends upon. The author’s tonal markers — adjectives that signal judgement, modal verbs that indicate certainty or doubt — are also recorded because they cannot be paraphrased away without altering the stance.

2.4 Drafting in Own Words

Using the outline, the writer composes a first draft in continuous prose, employing third‑person indirect speech and a formal register. Sentences are combined where the original used several to express a single logical step, and subordinate clauses replace coordinate strings to achieve density. The draft is written without looking back at the source, ensuring genuine paraphrase rather than patchwork copying.

2.5 Revision for Tone, Length and Completeness

The revision cycle begins with a word count. If the draft exceeds the target, the writer trims redundant modifiers, merges adjacent clauses, and replaces phrases with single precise verbs. If it falls short, the writer checks whether a premise or the concluding implication has been inadvertently dropped and restores it in compressed form. A final read‑aloud confirms that the tone mirrors the source and that the précis reads as a coherent, self‑contained paragraph.

Worked Example: Compression and Tone Preservation
Original (78 words): “The new agricultural policy, announced last month, promises to double farmer incomes within five years by guaranteeing minimum support prices for twenty‑three crops and by expanding credit facilities through regional cooperatives. Critics argue that the price guarantees will distort market signals, leading to over‑production of water‑intensive crops, while the credit expansion may benefit only large landholders who already possess collateral.”
Précis (26 words): “The agricultural policy aims to double farmer incomes in five years via minimum support prices for twenty‑three crops and expanded cooperative credit, though critics warn of market distortion and unequal credit access.” The précis retains the policy’s twin instruments, the five‑year horizon, the critics’ two specific objections, and the cautious tone signalled by “warn”.
Examination‑Day Insight
Before writing, jot the original word count in the margin, divide by three, and circle the target. During drafting, keep a running tally every few sentences; this prevents the common last‑minute scramble to cut or pad. After the final full stop, read the précis once more solely for tonal fidelity — ask whether a reader would attribute the same attitude to the author.

3. What to Retain and What to Discard

3.1 Core Argument and Supporting Reasons

The backbone of any précis is the logical chain that links the thesis to its conclusion. Each major reason that the author advances must appear, albeit in condensed form, because omitting a premise breaks the argument and renders the précis incoherent. The writer therefore treats the argument as a series of indispensable links rather than a menu of selectable points.

3.2 Key Facts, Figures and Proper Nouns

Specific data — names of legislation, statistical magnitudes, geographical references — are retained only when they function as evidence for a claim. A figure cited merely to illustrate magnitude may be replaced by a qualitative descriptor (“a substantial increase”) if the exact number is not pivotal to the reasoning. However, when the argument hinges on a precise quantity — for example, a threshold that triggers a policy change — the number must survive compression.

3.3 Author’s Tone and Stance

Tone is conveyed through the author’s choice of evaluative language and through the framing of concessions. The précis must reproduce the same evaluative adjectives or their close synonyms, and must preserve the balance between assertion and qualification. If the source ends with a cautious “may” rather than a definitive “will”, the précis must reflect that tentativeness.

3.4 Material That Must Be Omitted

Examples, anecdotes, extended quotations, rhetorical flourishes, repetitions, and any passage that serves only to entertain or to illustrate without advancing the logical chain are excluded. The writer also removes first‑person references, direct speech, and any personal commentary that the source does not contain. The guiding principle is simple: if the sentence can be deleted without weakening the argument, it belongs in the discard pile.

4. Language Conventions and Structural Norms

4.1 Third‑Person Indirect Speech

A précis never uses “I”, “we”, or “you”. The writer reports the author’s ideas as “The author argues that…”, “It is contended that…”, or “The passage suggests…”. This convention maintains objectivity and signals that the précis is a representation, not an extension, of the original thought.

4.2 Tense Consistency and Formal Register

When the source discusses past events, the précis typically adopts the past tense; when it presents timeless principles, the present tense is appropriate. Shifting tenses arbitrarily creates confusion about the temporal status of the claims. The register remains formal throughout — contractions are avoided, colloquialisms are replaced by standard equivalents, and sentence structures favour subordination over coordination.

4.3 Paragraph Organisation

Although a précis may be a single paragraph, its internal organisation mirrors the source: an opening sentence that names the author (or the work) and states the thesis, a middle section that compresses the supporting reasoning in logical order, and a closing sentence that captures the conclusion or implication. Transitional phrases such as “furthermore”, “conversely”, “consequently” guide the reader through the condensed argument.

5. Common Errors and How to Avoid Them

5.1 Insertion of Personal Opinion

The most frequent lapse is the unconscious addition of the writer’s judgement — “the policy is wise” or “the argument is unconvincing”. Such insertions betray the requirement of fidelity. The safeguard is to ask after each sentence whether the claim originates in the source; if not, it must be removed.

5.2 Verbatim Copying and Inadequate Paraphrase

Copying whole clauses or merely substituting synonyms for isolated words produces a patchwork that fails the “own words” test. Effective paraphrase restructures the syntax, changes the grammatical subject, and replaces noun‑heavy phrases with strong verbs. Practising sentence‑level rewriting on unrelated texts builds this skill.

5.3 Distortion of Tone

Neutralising a critical passage or intensifying a tentative one alters the author’s rhetorical intent. The remedy is to highlight every evaluative term in the source before drafting and to ensure each appears, in some form, in the précis.

5.4 Length Deviations

Exceeding the one‑third ceiling usually signals retained illustrations; falling well below suggests missing premises. The disciplined word‑count check described in the process section eliminates both extremes.

6. Practice and Examination Strategy

6.1 Word‑Count Calculation Technique

At the start of the allotted time, count the words of the given passage (or use the provided count) and write the target figure prominently. Divide the target by the number of logical units identified in the outline to obtain a rough budget per unit; this prevents any single section from monopolising the allowance.

6.2 Timed Drafting Drill

Regular practice under strict time limits — for example, twenty minutes for a three‑hundred‑word passage — trains the mind to move swiftly from reading to outline to draft. The drill should include a final two‑minute review dedicated solely to tone verification and word‑count adjustment.

6.3 Self‑Review Checklist

After the draft is complete, the writer runs through a mental checklist: does the opening name the source and state the thesis? Are all major premises present in logical order? Is the conclusion captured? Has every evaluative adjective been mirrored? Is the word count within the acceptable band? Is the language third‑person, formal, and free of contractions? Answering each question affirmatively signals a précis ready for submission.

Mastery of précis writing rests on the disciplined interplay of analytical reading, logical extraction, and controlled expression, and the consistent application of the principles outlined above will enable any candidate to produce a faithful, concise, and tonally accurate précis under examination conditions.

Handling Nested Arguments and Counter‑Arguments in Précis

When a source embeds a primary claim within a series of qualifications, concessions, and rebuttals, the précis must preserve the logical hierarchy without reproducing every clause. Begin by mapping the argument tree: identify the thesis, each supporting premise, every conceded point, and each rebuttal. Represent concessions with verbs such as “acknowledges”, “grants”, or “admits” and rebuttals with “counters”, “refutes”, or “demonstrates”. This mapping prevents the common error of flattening a dialectical structure into a simple list of statements. In the draft, allocate word‑budget proportionally: the thesis and its strongest premises receive the largest share; concessions and rebuttals are compressed to a single clause each, provided their logical function remains clear. Preserve the author’s evaluative adjectives (e.g., “unconvincing”, “compelling”) because they signal the weight the writer assigns to each move. If the original uses a “although … nevertheless” pattern, render it as “Although the author concedes X, the argument maintains Y”. Such restructuring keeps the dialectic intact while meeting the one‑third length constraint.

Special care is required when the source contains multiple layers of counter‑argument (a counter‑counter‑argument). In these cases, collapse the innermost layer into a single evaluative phrase — “the rebuttal is ultimately dismissed” — unless the innermost layer introduces new evidence that the author treats as decisive. The guiding principle is fidelity to the author’s final stance, not to every intermediate turn. When the passage ends with a qualified conclusion (“the evidence suggests, but does not prove”), the précis must mirror that qualification; omitting it would distort tone.

Compressing Technical and Data‑Heavy Passages

Technical texts often present dense clusters of figures, terminology, and procedural detail. The précis writer must decide which data points are essential to the argument and which serve only as illustration. Retain any statistic that directly supports the thesis or a major premise — for example, a prevalence rate that underpins a policy recommendation. Omit peripheral numbers such as sample sizes, confidence intervals, or intermediate calculations unless the author explicitly argues from them. Replace noun‑heavy strings (“the measurement of the concentration of particulate matter”) with strong verbs (“measured particulate concentration”). Use standard abbreviations only after the first full expansion in the précis (e.g., “World Health Organization (WHO)”). When the source lists a series of comparable results, compress them into a range or a summary statistic (“values ranged from 12 to 18 µg/m³”). Preserve the author’s hedging language (“approximately”, “indicates”) because it conveys epistemic stance. If the passage includes a methodological description that the author later cites as a limitation, keep the limitation but drop the procedural minutiae. The goal is a précis that a reader unfamiliar with the discipline can follow without consulting the original.

Worked Compression of a Technical Excerpt
Original (120 words): “The study measured ambient PM2.5 concentrations at five urban monitoring stations over a twelve‑month period. The mean annual concentration was 35 µg/m³, with a standard deviation of 8 µg/m³. Seasonal peaks occurred in winter (mean 48 µg/m³) and summer (mean 22 µg/m³). The authors note that the monitoring network does not capture indoor exposure, which may account for up to 30 % of total personal exposure. They conclude that outdoor levels alone underestimate health risk.”
Précis (38 words): The study reports a mean annual PM2.5 level of 35 µg/m³ across five urban stations, with winter peaks of 48 µg/m³. The authors acknowledge that unmeasured indoor exposure may add 30 % to personal dose, arguing that outdoor data alone underestimate health risk.
Exam‑Day Data‑Selection Rule
Before drafting, underline every numeral and proper noun in the passage. Ask: does this figure advance the thesis or a major premise? If the answer is no, strike it. This two‑minute scan prevents the common trap of copying illustrative tables into the précis.

Resolving Implicit Premises and Ambiguous References

Many passages rely on unstated assumptions or pronouns whose antecedents are several sentences away. The précis writer must make these links explicit without adding interpretation. First, trace each pronoun (“it”, “this”, “such”) to its referent in the source. In the précis, replace the pronoun with the full noun phrase or a concise synonym (“the policy”, “the observed trend”). Second, identify any logical gaps: a claim that “therefore the program should be expanded” may rest on an implicit premise that “the program’s benefits outweigh its costs”. If the source never states that premise, the précis should not invent it; instead, render the conclusion as “the author recommends expansion, citing the reported benefits”. This preserves the author’s reasoning while flagging the missing link. When the passage contains vague quantifiers (“many”, “several”, “a number of”), retain the vagueness — do not substitute precise numbers. If the author uses a metaphor (“the economy is a ship without a rudder”), keep the metaphor only if it carries evaluative weight; otherwise, replace it with a literal paraphrase (“the economy lacks direction”). The overarching rule: clarify structure, never amplify content.

Précis of Multi‑Voice Texts: Dialogues, Reports, and Collaborative Documents

Texts that present multiple speakers — interview transcripts, panel discussions, or joint reports — require the précis to attribute each distinct position to its source. Begin by segmenting the passage into voice blocks. For each block, note the speaker’s role (e.g., “the chair”, “the dissenting expert”) and the stance taken. In the précis, use reporting verbs that reflect the speech act: “argues”, “contends”, “questions”, “concedes”. When two speakers share a view, combine them: “Both the chair and the economist argue that fiscal stimulus is necessary.” When they diverge, preserve the contrast: “While the chair supports stimulus, the dissenting expert warns of inflationary pressure.” Avoid merging opposing views into a false consensus. If the document concludes with a collective recommendation, state it as “the panel recommends…” and list any recorded dissent. Omit procedural dialogue (“shall we move to the next item?”) unless it reveals power dynamics relevant to the argument. The précis must remain a single, coherent narrative that a reader can follow without the original’s turn‑taking cues.

Advanced Syntactic Compression: Nominalisation, Ellipsis, and Clause Reduction

Beyond vocabulary substitution, expert précis writers exploit syntactic devices to shrink sentences while retaining meaning. Nominalisation converts verb‑centric clauses into dense noun phrases: “The committee decided to postpone the vote” becomes “The committee’s decision to postpone the vote”. Ellipsis removes recoverable elements: “The first study found a correlation; the second study found none” compresses to “The first study found a correlation; the second, none.” Clause reduction turns relative clauses into participial phrases: “The policy, which was introduced in 2015, reduced emissions” becomes “The 2015 policy reduced emissions.” Combine these techniques judiciously; over‑nominalisation can produce opaque prose. A practical drill: take a 150‑word paragraph and rewrite it in three successive passes — first replacing verbs with nouns, then applying ellipsis, then reducing clauses — counting words after each pass. The final version should sit within the one‑third target while preserving every logical relation. Remember that each compression step must be reversible in principle; if a reader cannot reconstruct the original claim, the compression has gone too far.

  • Flattening a dialectical structure into a simple list of statements.
  • Retaining peripheral statistics that do not support the thesis.
  • Replacing vague quantifiers with invented precise numbers.
  • Merging opposing voices in multi‑speaker texts into a false consensus.
  • Over‑nominalising to the point where logical relations become opaque.
  • Omitting the author’s hedging language, thereby distorting epistemic stance.
  • Applying these checks consistently ensures a faithful, concise précis.

Integrating Visual Data: Tables, Charts, and Graphs into Précis

Examination passages increasingly embed quantitative displays — tables, bar charts, line graphs, or pie diagrams — that support the author’s argument. A précis must convey the evidential weight of such visuals without reproducing them. The first step is to read the accompanying caption and any explanatory prose; these usually state the variable measured, the time span, and the comparative units. Next, identify the single most salient trend or contrast that the author cites in the text (e.g., “exports rose 12 % between 2015 and 2020 while imports fell 3 %”). In the précis, render that trend as a concise clause: “Exports increased by twelve percent over the period, whereas imports declined by three percent.” Peripheral figures — such as the exact value for each year — are omitted unless the argument hinges on a specific datum (e.g., a threshold crossing). When a chart illustrates a correlation that the author discusses qualitatively, preserve the qualitative claim (“the data suggest a positive correlation between education spending and literacy rates”) and drop the graphic itself. If the passage contains multiple visuals, treat each independently and then decide whether the précis can combine them into a single comparative sentence (“Both the bar chart and the line graph show a steady decline in rural employment”). The guiding principle: the précis reflects the author’s use of the visual, not the visual’s raw content.

When the source text references a table without reproducing it, the précis writer must infer the relevant figures from the surrounding discussion. For instance, if the author writes “Table 2 shows that the mortality rate dropped from 8.4 to 5.1 per thousand,” the précis should retain the two rates and the direction of change. No additional calculation is required; the writer simply mirrors the author’s summary. However, if the author performs a calculation on the table (e.g., “the average annual decline is 0.55 per thousand”), that derived figure must be kept because it constitutes part of the argument. In all cases, the précis must attribute the data to the source (“the study reports…”) rather than presenting it as the writer’s own finding.

A common pitfall is to transcribe the visual’s legend or axis labels verbatim. This inflates word count and breaks the third‑person indirect‑speech convention. Instead, paraphrase the legend into a noun phrase: “the chart’s vertical axis measures annual rainfall in millimetres” becomes “annual rainfall (millimetres)”. Similarly, avoid describing visual formatting (“the blue bars represent…”) unless the colour coding carries analytical meaning (e.g., “blue bars denote projected figures”). The précis should be readable without the original graphic; a reader who never sees the chart must still grasp the evidential point.

Finally, verify that the compressed data still respects the one‑third length rule. Count the words devoted to visual evidence in the source, then allocate roughly one‑third of that budget in the précis. If the source devotes 120 words to a table and its discussion, the précis may spend about 40 words. This budgeting prevents the précis from becoming a data dump while preserving the argument’s quantitative backbone.

Précis of Legal Judgments and Statutory Excerpts

Legal texts differ from ordinary prose in three structural respects: they contain a ratio decidendi (the binding reason for the decision), obiter dicta (remarks not essential to the holding), and a dense citation network. A précis of a judgment must isolate the ratio, because that is the proposition that carries precedential weight. Begin by locating the court’s explicit statement of the legal principle — often signalled by phrases such as “we hold that”, “the principle is”, or “it follows that”. Extract that sentence and re‑express it in indirect speech: “The court held that a contractual clause limiting liability for negligence is unenforceable when it contravenes public policy.” All obiter passages, even if eloquent, are omitted unless the examination question explicitly asks for the court’s broader reasoning.

Statutory excerpts present a different challenge: the language is prescriptive, often conditional, and heavily cross‑referenced. The précis writer should first identify the operative provision — the clause that creates a right, duty, or penalty. Conditional limbs (“if”, “provided that”, “subject to”) are retained only when they define the scope of the rule. For example, “Section 12(1) provides that a company shall not issue shares at a discount unless authorised by a special resolution” compresses to “Section 12(1) prohibits the issue of shares at a discount without a special resolution.” Ancillary definitions, commencement dates, and repeal schedules are dropped unless the argument turns on them.

Citation chains (e.g., “see Smith v. Jones [2001] 2 AC 345 at 352”) are never reproduced in full. The précis may note “the court relied on Smith v. Jones” if that case is pivotal to the ratio. When multiple authorities are cited in support of a single proposition, combine them: “The court cited Smith v. Jones and Brown v. Green in support of the principle that…”. This preserves the evidential hierarchy without inflating the word count.

Tone in legal précis must remain neutral and authoritative. The writer must not inject evaluative language (“the court rightly held”) nor soften the holding (“the court suggested”). The indirect‑speech frame (“the court held”, “the statute provides”) maintains the original’s formal register. Finally, verify that the précis does not exceed the one‑third limit; legal passages are often concise, so the compression target may be as low as 30 % of the original word count.

Worked Example: Compressing a Judicial Holding
Original (180 words): “In the present appeal, the Supreme Court considered whether a clause in a standard‑form contract that excludes liability for personal injury caused by negligence is enforceable. The Court reviewed the precedent of White v. Black [1998] 1 All ER 456, where it was held that such clauses are void as against public policy. The Court also examined the statutory provision of the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977, section 2(1), which renders ineffective any term that excludes liability for death or personal injury resulting from negligence. The Court concluded that the clause in question is unenforceable both at common law and under the Act. Accordingly, the appeal is dismissed.”
Précis (58 words): The Supreme Court held that a standard‑form contract clause excluding liability for personal injury caused by negligence is unenforceable. The Court applied the common‑law rule from White v. Black that such clauses are void as against public policy and cited section 2(1) of the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977, which invalidates terms excluding liability for death or personal injury from negligence. The appeal was dismissed.
Examination‑Day Strategy
When a legal passage appears, underline the holding sentence and the statutory provision number in the first read. In the second read, mark any case names that are explicitly linked to the holding. Draft the précis by stitching together the holding, the governing statute, and the pivotal precedent — each in a single clause. This three‑element template guarantees coverage of the ratio while staying within the word limit.

Handling Footnotes, Endnotes, and Parenthetical Citations

Academic and policy passages frequently embed footnotes, endnotes, or parenthetical citations that supply source authority, methodological detail, or tangential commentary. The précis writer must decide which of this apparatus serves the central argument. A footnote that merely cites a source for a quoted statistic (“See World Bank, 2022”) is redundant in the précis because the statistic itself is already represented. Conversely, a footnote that qualifies a claim (“The 2022 figure excludes informal‑sector employment”) carries substantive weight and must be reflected, either by embedding the qualification in the main sentence or by adding a brief clause (“the figure excludes informal‑sector employment”).

Parenthetical citations within the text (e.g., “(Smith, 2020)”) function similarly. If the citation supports a claim that the précis retains, the citation can be omitted; the précis’s indirect‑speech attribution (“Smith argues…”) suffices. However, when the passage contrasts two studies (“Smith (2020) finds X, whereas Jones (2021) finds Y”), the précis must preserve the contrast and the attribution to each author. In such cases, the parenthetical names become part of the reporting verbs: “Smith argues X; Jones contends Y.”

Endnotes that contain extended methodological discussion (sample size, regression specification) are usually peripheral. Include them only if the author’s conclusion explicitly rests on a methodological choice (“Because the model controls for regional fixed effects, the estimate is robust”). In that event, compress the methodological note to its logical function: “the estimate controls for regional fixed effects”.

When the source uses a mixture of footnotes and in‑text citations, treat them uniformly: extract the informational content, discard the bibliographic formatting, and integrate the essential qualifiers into the précis’s narrative. This avoids the appearance of a reference list within a compressed text and keeps the focus on argumentative structure.

Word‑count budgeting for notes follows the same one‑third rule applied to the main body. If the original devotes 60 words to footnotes that are all peripheral, the précis may allocate zero words to them. If a single footnote carries a crucial limitation, allocate roughly one‑third of its length (e.g., 20 words become 7). This disciplined approach prevents the précis from becoming a bibliography.

Précis for Comparative Passages: Multiple Texts on a Single Theme

Some examinations present two or more short passages that address the same issue from different perspectives — for example, an editorial and a rebuttal, or a policy brief and a stakeholder response. The précis task may require a single compressed text that captures the dialogue between the sources. The first analytical step is to segment each passage into its thesis, supporting reasons, and evidence. Create a two‑column map: left column for Passage A, right column for Passage B. Align comparable points (e.g., both discuss funding mechanisms; Passage A cites a World Bank study, Passage B cites a national audit).

In the précis, organise the material thematically rather than sequentially. Open with a framing sentence that identifies the shared topic and the existence of divergent views: “The two texts examine the effectiveness of conditional cash transfers in reducing child malnutrition.” Then, for each thematic cluster, present the positions side by side using contrastive conjunctions: “While the editorial argues that targeting errors undermine impact, the rebuttal cites recent audit data showing a 92 % targeting accuracy.” Preserve each source’s tone: if the editorial is sceptical, the précis reflects that scepticism (“the editorial questions…”); if the rebuttal is confident, use “the rebuttal asserts…”.

When the passages converge on a point, merge them: “Both texts agree that administrative capacity is a prerequisite for scale‑up.” Avoid creating a false consensus by glossing over disagreements. If one passage introduces a counter‑argument that the other does not address, note the asymmetry: “The editorial raises the risk of fiscal leakage; the rebuttal does not respond to this concern.” This fidelity to the dialectic is essential for a faithful précis.

Attribution verbs must be varied to avoid monotony and to reflect speech‑act nuance: “contends”, “maintains”, “highlights”, “warns”, “concedes”. The précis should remain a single paragraph or a short series of paragraphs, not a dialogue script. Finally, apply the one‑third rule to the combined word count of all source passages; the target length is one‑third of the total, not one‑third of each individually.

Strategic Use of Signposting and Cohesive Devices in Compressed Prose

Compression amplifies the risk of incoherence because logical connectors are often the first casualties of word‑count pressure. Expert précis writers deliberately retain a minimal set of signposting devices that map the argument’s architecture. The most efficient markers are single‑word or two‑word discourse adverbials: “therefore”, “however”, “consequently”, “moreover”, “nevertheless”. These occupy negligible space yet signal cause, contrast, addition, or concession. For example, “The study finds a positive correlation; however, the sample is non‑representative” preserves the adversative relation in twelve words.

Clause‑level cohesion can be achieved through syntactic embedding rather than explicit connectors. A relative clause turned into a participial phrase (“The policy, which was introduced in 2015, reduced emissions” → “The 2015 policy reduced emissions”) removes the need for a separate sentence and its attendant transition. Similarly, nominalisation (“The committee decided to postpone” → “The committee’s decision to postpone”) creates a noun phrase that can serve as the subject of the next clause, allowing a seamless flow: “The committee’s decision to postpone delayed implementation.”

Paragraph breaks in a précis should mirror the macro‑structure of the source: one paragraph for the thesis and main premises, a second for evidence, a third for conclusion or recommendation. If the source is a single paragraph, the précis may remain a single paragraph; forced paragraphing wastes words on blank lines. Within a paragraph, use a single coordinating conjunction (“and”, “but”) to link two major claims when the logical relation is simple; reserve subordinating conjunctions (“although”, “because”) for more complex dependencies.

Lexical cohesion — repetition of key terms or their close synonyms — also guides the reader. In a compressed text, repeat the central concept (e.g., “the reform”) rather than substituting a vague pronoun (“it”). This prevents ambiguity when the antecedent is several clauses away. However, avoid excessive repetition that inflates the count; a single strategic repeat per paragraph is usually sufficient.

Finally, test the précis for readability by reading it aloud. Any point where the logical jump feels abrupt signals a missing signpost. Insert the smallest possible connector (often a single adverb) and recount words. This iterative tightening yields a précis that is both concise and logically transparent.

  • Treating visual data as raw numbers rather than the author’s interpreted trend.
  • Including obiter dicta or peripheral statutory schedules in a legal précis.
  • Copying footnote bibliographic details instead of extracting substantive qualifications.
  • Presenting comparative passages as a simple list without preserving contrastive structure.
  • Omitting all discourse markers, producing a fragmented logical flow.
  • Over‑nominalising to the point where the agent of an action becomes unrecoverable.
  • These traps illustrate the most frequent errors that reduce marks.

Précis of Scientific Research Articles

Scientific articles follow the IMRaD architecture — Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion — and a précis must mirror this logical skeleton while compressing each segment to its evidential core. The introduction typically states a research gap, a hypothesis, and the study’s objective; retain the hypothesis verbatim or in a close paraphrase because it anchors the entire argument. The methods section is often the most expendable: summarise the design (e.g., randomised controlled trial, longitudinal cohort), the population, and the primary measurement instruments in a single clause. Omit procedural minutiae such as reagent concentrations or software versions unless the passage explicitly argues that a methodological choice determines the outcome. In the results, preserve only statistically significant findings, effect sizes, and the direction of relationships; discard raw p‑values, confidence‑interval limits, and subsidiary analyses that do not bear on the main claim. The discussion usually restates the hypothesis, interprets the key result, acknowledges limitations, and proposes implications; keep the interpretation and the most consequential limitation, but drop speculative future‑work sentences. Throughout, maintain the author’s epistemic stance: if the original hedges (“suggests”, “may indicate”), the précis must hedge identically. Use nominalised verbs (“The trial demonstrated…” → “The trial’s demonstration of…”) to compress without losing agency. Finally, verify that the word count falls within one‑third of the source total, counting the abstract as part of the source if it is included in the examination passage.

Précis of Policy Briefs and Executive Summaries

Policy briefs are purpose‑built for decision‑makers; they foreground a problem definition, a set of vetted options, a recommended course, and the evidence base that justifies the recommendation. A précis of such a document must preserve the decision logic: the problem statement, the criteria used to evaluate options (cost‑effectiveness, equity, feasibility), the comparative assessment, and the final recommendation with its implementation caveats. Begin by isolating the “policy question” — often a single interrogative sentence — and render it in indirect speech (“The brief asks whether…”). Next, catalogue the options in a parallel series, each linked to its evaluative verdict (“Option A, judged cost‑effective but politically sensitive; Option B, equitable but fiscally demanding”). The recommendation follows, together with any conditionalities (“The brief recommends Option A provided that stakeholder consultation precedes rollout”). Evidence citations (e.g., “World Bank 2022”, “meta‑analysis of 14 RCTs”) should be retained as attributive tags, not expanded. Tone is crucial: a brief may be advocacy‑oriented (“urges”, “strongly recommends”) or neutral (“presents”, “outlines”); the précis must mirror that stance. Avoid converting a conditional recommendation into an absolute one. Because briefs often embed tables or infographics, treat any quantitative claim (e.g., “a 12 % reduction in out‑of‑pocket expenditure”) as a key fact that must survive compression. Finally, respect the one‑third rule on the full brief, including annexes if they are part of the examination text.

Worked Example: Policy Brief Précis
A 420‑word policy brief argues that urban air‑quality standards should be tightened. It defines the problem as “persistent PM2.5 exceedances causing 4,800 premature deaths annually”. Three options are evaluated: (1) stricter emission limits for industry, (2) congestion pricing for private vehicles, (3) expanded green‑belt zoning. The brief cites a 2021 WHO meta‑analysis showing a 9 % mortality reduction per 10 µg/m³ PM2.5 drop, and a 2020 city‑level simulation predicting a 12 µg/m³ reduction from Option 1 alone. Option 2 yields a 7 µg/m³ drop but faces political resistance; Option 3 yields a 3 µg/m³ drop with high public acceptance. The brief recommends adopting Option 1 immediately and piloting Option 2 in two districts, contingent on a stakeholder‑consultation framework. The tone is urgent but evidence‑based.
The brief urges tightening urban air‑quality standards to avert 4,800 annual premature deaths from PM2.5. It evaluates three options: stricter industrial emission limits (projected 12 µg/m³ PM2.5 reduction, supported by a 2021 WHO meta‑analysis linking a 10 µg/m³ drop to 9 % mortality decline), congestion pricing (7 µg/m³ reduction, politically contentious), and green‑belt expansion (3 µg/m³ reduction, publicly popular). The brief recommends immediate adoption of Option 1 and a piloted rollout of Option 2 in two districts, conditional on a stakeholder‑consultation framework.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a policy brief appears, first underline the policy question and the recommendation. Then number the options in the margin. Draft the précis by writing the question, the recommendation, and a single comparative sentence for each option. This scaffold guarantees that the decision logic survives compression and that the word count stays on target.

Précis of Historical Narratives

Historical narratives intertwine chronology, causality, and interpretation. A précis must preserve the temporal backbone — key dates, sequence of events, and periodisation — while condensing descriptive detail. Identify the thesis: the historian’s central argument about why a development occurred (e.g., “The 1857 revolt was primarily a military mutiny triggered by cartridge grievances, not a nationalist uprising”). Retain the thesis verbatim or in a close paraphrase. Next, map the causal chain: each major event should appear as a node linked by causal connectors (“precipitated”, “enabled”, “undermined”). Omit anecdotal episodes, biographical minutiae, and extended quotations unless they serve as evidence for the thesis. When the narrative presents competing interpretations, keep the historian’s adjudication (“Most scholars now reject the ‘great man’ view because…”). Preserve the historian’s epistemic modality: “suggests”, “contends”, “demonstrates” must survive. Numerical data (troop strengths, casualty figures, economic indices) are treated as key facts; round only if the source rounds. Finally, ensure the précis reads as a coherent analytical paragraph, not a timeline list; use subordinate clauses to embed chronology (“Following the 1856 annexation of Awadh, the 1857 cartridge issue ignited widespread mutiny”).

Précis of Philosophical and Theoretical Texts

Philosophical prose advances through definitions, premises, inferential steps, and conclusions, often embedding counter‑arguments and rebuttals. A précis must reconstruct the argument map rather than the rhetorical flow. Start by isolating the central claim (the thesis) and the key terms the author defines (e.g., “qualia”, “supervenience”). Retain definitions only if they are stipulative and essential to the argument. Next, list the premises in logical order, using conditional phrasing (“If mental states supervene on physical states, then…”). Preserve the author’s treatment of objections: note the objection, the author’s response, and whether the response succeeds in the author’s view. Avoid turning a nuanced “may be compatible with” into a flat “is compatible with”. Modal verbs (“must”, “could”, “might”) are non‑negotiable tone markers. When the text references a canonical debate (e.g., the Gettier problem), keep the reference as an attributive tag (“drawing on Gettier 1963”) rather than expanding the debate. Because philosophical texts are dense, nominalisation and clause reduction are especially valuable: “The argument that knowledge requires justified true belief is challenged by Gettier cases” → “Gettier cases challenge the justified‑true‑belief account of knowledge”. Verify that the final précis does not exceed one‑third of the source word count, counting footnotes only if they contain substantive argumentation.

Précis of Literary Criticism and Close Reading

Literary criticism blends textual evidence, theoretical framework, and interpretive claim. A précis must keep the critic’s reading strategy: the chosen lens (formalist, historicist, psychoanalytic, etc.), the specific passages analysed, and the interpretive conclusion. Begin by stating the critical question (“How does the novel’s fragmented narrative structure encode post‑colonial trauma?”). Retain the theoretical label if it is explicit (“drawing on Bhabha’s concept of hybridity”). Summarise the close‑reading evidence in a single clause per major textual segment (“The recurring mirror motif in Chapter 3 reflects the protagonist’s fractured identity”). Preserve the critic’s evaluative language (“convincingly demonstrates”, “overstates”, “neglects”). If the essay compares two works, maintain the comparative structure (“While Novel A uses…, Novel B employs…”). Omit extended plot summary; only the textual details that serve the argument survive. Tone fidelity is paramount: a tentative reading (“suggests a possible reading of…”) must not become assertive. Finally, apply the one‑third rule to the entire critical essay, including any block quotations that are part of the examination passage.

  • Treating a scientific abstract as the whole article and omitting the discussion’s limitations.
  • Converting a policy brief’s conditional recommendation into an absolute directive.
  • Collapsing a historical narrative’s causal chain into a mere chronological list.
  • Flattening a philosophical argument’s modal qualifications into categorical statements.
  • Replacing a literary critic’s theoretical lens with a generic “analysis”.
  • Discarding key quantitative evidence (effect sizes, mortality figures) because they appear in tables.
  • Exceeding the one‑third limit by retaining extensive quotations instead of paraphrasing.

These traps illustrate the most frequent errors that reduce marks.
