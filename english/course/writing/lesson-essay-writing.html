Learning Objectives

Key Concepts

What the Examiner Rewards

A descriptive essay is marked for content, organisation, expression and accuracy. In UPSC Mains Paper A, RBI Phase 2 and other descriptive papers you have limited time, so a clear structure matters more than a rare vocabulary. Choose a side, state it in the introduction, support it with two or three developed paragraphs, and close with a balanced conclusion. Do not try to cover every dimension; depth on three points beats a list of ten.

The Structure

  • Introduction (2-3 sentences) — define the issue, give the context, and state your thesis. Avoid opening with a dictionary definition unless it adds something.
  • Body paragraph 1 — the strongest argument, with a fact, example or illustration. Explain the link to the thesis.
  • Body paragraph 2 — a second argument, or the counter-argument and your response. Use a transition.
  • Body paragraph 3 — a practical dimension: policy, economy, society, ethics or implementation.
  • Conclusion (2-3 sentences) — restate the thesis in different words and suggest a way forward. Do not introduce a new argument.

Types of Essay

Argumentative — take a position and defend it. Expository — explain a topic neutrally. Descriptive — describe a place, person or experience with sensory detail. Narrative — tell a story with a clear arc. Reflective — connect an experience to a larger lesson. Read the prompt carefully: Discuss asks for both sides, Critically examine asks for a judgement, Comment asks for your view with reasons.

Language That Scores

Use concrete nouns and active verbs. Replace very important with critical, a lot of with substantial, and because of this with consequently. Vary sentence length: a short sentence after two long ones lands a point. Use connectors to show the shape of the argument: firstly, moreover, however, therefore, nevertheless, in conclusion. Avoid clichés such as since time immemorial and in today's fast-paced world.

Model outline — "Should social media be regulated?"
Thesis: regulation is needed, but it must protect speech rather than control it.

1. Harm: misinformation, mental health, election interference — one concrete example.
2. Counter-argument: regulation can become censorship; answer with independent oversight and transparency.
3. Design: age verification, algorithmic transparency, rapid takedown, appeal rights.
Conclusion: smart regulation, not a ban.
Why it works: the thesis is arguable, each paragraph has one job, the counter-argument is answered, and the conclusion proposes a practical way forward.
Exam tip
Spend the first three minutes on a one-line thesis and a three-point outline. A planned essay is easier to write, easier to read and less likely to repeat itself under time pressure.

Introductions and Conclusions

A strong introduction does three things in two or three sentences: it names the issue, gives the context, and states the thesis. Avoid a long historical survey. A useful opening move is a precise contrast or a fact: India added more internet users in five years than it added bank accounts in fifty; the question is whether the state can govern that speed. The examiner should know your position by the end of the first paragraph.

A conclusion should not repeat the introduction word for word. Restate the thesis in fresh language, acknowledge the strongest counter-point in one clause, and end with a practical direction — a policy, an institution, or a principle. Avoid introducing a new argument or a new example in the last paragraph; it reads as an unfinished essay.

Word count and time: a 250-word essay is roughly 15 to 18 sentences. If you write 300 words in the same time, you will lose marks for length and for the errors that hurry produces. Practise with a timer and count words once; after five attempts you will know how much space your handwriting fills for 250 words.
