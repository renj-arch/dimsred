Key Concepts

Formal and Informal Letters

A formal letter goes to an official, institution or business contact; an informal letter goes to a friend or relative. The difference is not only tone but structure: a formal letter has a subject line, a salutation with the recipient's designation, a body in short paragraphs, and a formal closing. An informal letter has a warmer opening, personal detail and a conversational close.

Formal Letter Format

  • Sender's address — top left, without the name in most exam formats.
  • Date — below the address, written in full.
  • Receiver's address — name, designation, organisation and address.
  • Subject — one line, specific: Subject: Request for issuance of a duplicate marksheet.
  • Salutation — Dear Sir/Madam or Dear Mr Sharma.
  • Body — paragraph 1 states the purpose, paragraph 2 gives details, paragraph 3 states the action requested.
  • Closing — Yours faithfully with Dear Sir/Madam; Yours sincerely with a named person.

Letters You Must Practise

Complaint — state the problem, give the order or reference number, describe the inconvenience, ask for a specific remedy. Application — name the post, refer to the advertisement, summarise relevant qualifications, enclose documents, request an interview. Request — ask for information, permission or a document, and say why it is needed. Editor — raise a public issue, give evidence, suggest a solution. Informal — share news, invite, thank, congratulate or console.

Report Writing

A report is factual and impersonal. Use a title, a one-line introduction (what, where, when), a body of findings in logical order, and a recommendation. Past tense for completed events, passive voice where the doer is unknown, and no personal opinion unless the prompt asks for it. Keep paragraphs short and use headings if the format allows.

Model opening — complaint
Subject: Defective laptop delivered against order no. 4821

Dear Sir/Madam,

I placed order no. 4821 on 12 August for a laptop (model X). The unit delivered on 18 August does not switch on, and the screen has a visible crack. I request a replacement or a full refund within seven working days.
Why it works: the subject names the issue and the order, the first paragraph states the facts, and the request is specific and time-bound.
Exam tip
Do not use contractions (I'm, don't), slang or exclamation marks in a formal letter. One clear request at the end is stronger than three vague complaints.

Email and Common Errors

A formal email follows the letter structure with a shorter subject line and no sender address: subject, salutation, body in two or three short paragraphs, closing and signature. Use Dear Sir/Madam when the name is unknown and Dear Ms Sharma when it is known. Keep the subject specific: Request for a duplicate marksheet, not Request.

Common errors cost more marks than format: contractions in a formal letter, a missing subject line, a salutation that does not match the closing, a complaint with no specific request, and a body that mixes two unrelated issues. Write one purpose per letter. If you must mention a second issue, put it in a separate short paragraph and make the main request clear.

Tone is firm but polite. Replace You people never respond with I have not received a response despite two reminders. Replace Do the needful immediately with I request you to process the refund within seven working days. A specific, time-bound request is more persuasive than an angry one and reads as mature English.
