1. Foundations of Sentence Structure

1.1 The Core Elements of a Sentence

A sentence in English is built around a subject and a predicate, and the relationship between them determines whether the construction is grammatically sound. The subject may be a simple noun phrase, a pronoun, a gerund phrase, or a clause, while the predicate must contain a finite verb that agrees with the subject in number and person. When a writer omits the subject, uses a non‑finite verb as the main verb, or inserts an extra clause without proper subordination, the result is a fragment or a run‑on that examiners flag as an error. Understanding that every complete sentence requires at least one independent clause, and that dependent clauses cannot stand alone, provides the diagnostic framework for spotting structural faults in error‑spotting questions.

1.2 Clause Types and Their Functions

Independent clauses express a complete thought and can function as standalone sentences, whereas dependent clauses — whether nominal, adjectival, or adverbial — rely on a main clause for meaning. Nominal clauses often serve as subjects or objects, adjectival clauses modify nouns, and adverbial clauses indicate time, cause, condition, or concession. Errors arise when a dependent clause is punctuated as a sentence, when a relative pronoun is omitted in a restrictive clause, or when a subordinating conjunction is used incorrectly, such as placing “because” at the start of a sentence without a following main clause. Recognising the syntactic role of each clause type allows a test‑taker to locate the exact point where the grammar breaks down.

1.3 Sentence Patterns and Word Order

English follows a relatively fixed Subject‑Verb‑Object (SVO) order, but variations appear in questions, inversions, and emphatic constructions. Inversion after negative adverbials (“Never have I seen such a mess”) or in conditional clauses without “if” (“Had he known, he would have acted”) is a frequent source of error because candidates often retain the declarative order. Similarly, misplaced adverbs can change the meaning of a sentence (“He only eats apples” versus “Only he eats apples”). Mastery of the standard patterns and their licensed deviations equips the learner to judge whether a given word order is permissible or constitutes a grammatical violation.

2. Agreement and Concord

2.1 Subject‑Verb Agreement with Simple Subjects

The most basic concord rule requires a singular subject to take a singular verb and a plural subject a plural verb. This principle operates even when the subject is separated from its verb by intervening phrases such as prepositional phrases (“The box of chocolates is open”) or relative clauses (“The student who won the prize is here”). The verb must agree with the head noun of the subject phrase, not with the noun nearest to it. Errors of attraction occur when the writer mistakenly matches the verb to the closer noun, producing forms like “The quality of the mangoes are good.” Recognising the head noun and ignoring distractors is the first line of defence in error detection.

2.2 Agreement with Compound and Collective Subjects

When two or more nouns are joined by “and”, the subject is usually plural and demands a plural verb (“The director and the producer have arrived”). However, if the coordinated nouns refer to a single entity or a unified concept, a singular verb is appropriate (“Bread and butter is my favourite breakfast”). Collective nouns such as “team”, “committee”, “family”, or “audience” can take either singular or plural verbs depending on whether the group is viewed as a unit or as individuals; British usage often prefers the plural (“The committee are divided”), while American usage leans toward the singular (“The committee is divided”). The test‑taker must infer the intended sense from context, because the exam rarely supplies an explicit cue.

2.3 Agreement with Indefinite Pronouns and Quantifiers

Indefinite pronouns fall into three categories: those that are always singular (“each”, “everyone”, “somebody”, “nothing”), those that are always plural (“both”, “few”, “many”, “several”), and those that vary with the noun they refer to (“all”, “any”, “most”, “none”, “some”). Quantifiers such as “a number of” take a plural verb (“A number of students are absent”), whereas “the number of” takes a singular verb (“The number of students is rising”). The distinction hinges on whether the phrase emphasises the individual members or the collective quantity. Misidentifying the category leads to errors like “Each of the boys have a pen” instead of “has”.

2.4 Proximity and Attraction Errors

Proximity attraction is the tendency of a verb to agree with a nearby noun rather than the true subject. This phenomenon appears frequently in sentences where a singular subject is followed by a plural noun in a prepositional phrase (“The list of items are long”) or where a plural subject precedes a singular noun in a relative clause (“The players who is injured”). The rule is absolute: the verb must agree with the grammatical subject, not with any noun that happens to be closer. Training the eye to locate the subject head noun before checking the verb eliminates the majority of attraction errors in competitive examinations.

3. Tense, Aspect, and Voice

3.1 Consistency of Tense within a Discourse

A coherent passage maintains a consistent time frame unless a shift is signalled by a time adverbial or a change in narrative perspective. Unmotivated tense shifts — such as moving from past simple to present perfect without a clear reason — create confusion and are marked as errors. For instance, “He entered the room and sees the letter” mixes past and present; the correct form is either “entered … saw” or “enters … sees”. The principle of tense consistency extends to subordinate clauses: when the main clause is in the past, subordinate clauses typically backshift (“She said she was tired”), except when expressing a universal truth (“He said the earth revolves around the sun”).

3.2 Sequence of Tenses in Reported Speech

Reported speech follows a systematic backshifting pattern: present simple becomes past simple, present perfect becomes past perfect, past simple becomes past perfect, and modal verbs shift to their past equivalents (“can” → “could”, “will” → “would”). The reporting verb’s tense governs the shift; a present reporting verb (“He says”) does not trigger backshift. However, if the reported clause contains a timeless fact or a still‑valid future arrangement, the original tense may be retained (“She said she will join us tomorrow”). Mastery of this sequence prevents the common error of leaving the reported verb in its original form when the reporting verb is past.

3.3 Conditional Structures and Their Time References

English conditionals are classified by the reality and time of the condition. The first conditional (real present/future) uses “if + present simple, will + base verb” (“If it rains, I will stay”). The second conditional (unreal present/future) uses “if + past simple, would + base verb” (“If I were you, I would go”). The third conditional (unreal past) uses “if + past perfect, would have + past participle” (“If he had studied, he would have passed”). Mixed conditionals combine time references, such as “If I had taken the job, I would be living in London” (past condition, present result). A frequent trap is inserting “will” or “would” into the if‑clause, which is never permitted in standard grammar.

3.4 Active and Passive Voice Transformations

Changing voice requires the object of the active sentence to become the subject of the passive, the verb to adopt a form of “be” plus the past participle, and the original subject to appear in a “by” phrase if retained. Errors arise when the past participle is omitted (“The letter was send”), when the auxiliary “be” does not match the tense (“The work is completed yesterday”), or when intransitive verbs are forced into passive (“The accident was happened”). Only transitive verbs with a direct object can be passivised, and the tense of the passive must mirror the tense of the active original.

4. Articles, Determiners, and Prepositions

4.1 Definite and Indefinite Articles with Countable and Uncountable Nouns

The indefinite article “a/an” appears only before singular countable nouns, signalling non‑specific reference (“a book”). The definite article “the” marks specific or previously mentioned nouns, whether countable or uncountable (“the water in this bottle”). Uncountable nouns never take “a/an” (“an advice” is incorrect; “advice” or “a piece of advice” is correct). Plural countable nouns use “the” for specific reference and zero article for generic reference (“Dogs are loyal” versus “The dogs next door bark”). Errors such as “The milk is spoilt” (when speaking generically) or “The Delhi is big” (proper nouns usually take zero article) stem from misapplying these rules.

4.2 Zero Article and Generic Reference

Zero article is used with plural countable nouns and uncountable nouns when making generalisations (“Gold is precious”, “Children need love”). It also appears with institutions and meals when they are referred to in their primary function (“at school”, “in hospital”, “at breakfast”). However, when the same nouns are particularised, “the” returns (“The school he attends is famous”). The distinction between generic and specific reference is a frequent source of error because the same noun can appear with or without an article depending on context.

4.3 Preposition Selection after Verbs and Adjectives

Many verbs and adjectives govern specific prepositions, and substituting another preposition changes meaning or creates an ungrammatical construction. “Depend on” (not “depend in”), “good at” (not “good in”), “interested in” (not “interested on”), “afraid of” (not “afraid from”) are fixed collocations. The preposition is part of the lexical entry of the verb or adjective; learning these collocations as whole units reduces errors. Some verbs allow two prepositions with a shift in meaning (“arrive at” a building, “arrive in” a city), and the test may probe this nuance.

4.4 Since versus For, In versus On versus At, Between versus Among

“Since” marks a point in time (“since 2010”), while “for” marks a duration (“for ten years”). “In” is used with months, years, seasons, and parts of the day (“in July”, “in the morning”), “on” with days and dates (“on Monday”, “on 15 August”), and “at” with precise times (“at 5 p.m.”). “Between” applies to two distinct entities (“between you and me”), whereas “among” distributes over three or more (“among the members”). Misusing these prepositions is a hallmark of non‑native writing and appears regularly in error‑spotting sections.

5. Pronouns, Modifiers, and Parallelism

5.1 Case Forms and Reflexive Pronouns

Pronouns must appear in the correct case: subjective (“I”, “he”, “she”, “we”, “they”) for subjects and subject complements, objective (“me”, “him”, “her”, “us”, “them”) for objects of verbs and prepositions, and possessive (“my”, “his”, “her”, “our”, “their”) for determiners. A common error is using the subjective case after a preposition (“between you and I”) where the objective “me” is required. Reflexive pronouns (“myself”, “yourself”, “himself”) are used only when the subject and object refer to the same entity (“I hurt myself”) or for emphasis (“The manager himself approved”). Using a reflexive as a subject (“Myself invited him”) is ungrammatical.

5.2 Relative Pronouns: Who, Whom, Which, That

“Who” and “whom” refer to people; “who” functions as subject, “whom” as object. In formal writing, “whom” appears after prepositions (“the person to whom I spoke”), but in everyday usage “who” often replaces it. “Which” refers to things and animals, and “that” can refer to both people and things in restrictive clauses. Non‑restrictive clauses require “who” or “which” and are set off by commas. Errors include using “which” for people, omitting the relative pronoun in a restrictive clause where it is the subject, or inserting a comma before “that”.

5.3 Dangling and Misplaced Modifiers

A modifier must be placed next to the word it modifies. A dangling modifier occurs when the intended subject is absent from the main clause (“Walking on the road, a dog bit him” implies the dog was walking). The correction supplies the correct subject (“While I was walking on the road, a dog bit me”). A misplaced modifier changes meaning (“He only eats apples” versus “Only he eats apples”). The adverb “only” is especially mobile; its position determines which element it limits. Careful placement of participial phrases, prepositional phrases, and adverbs eliminates ambiguity.

5.4 Parallel Structure in Lists and Correlative Conjunctions

Elements joined by coordinating conjunctions or correlative pairs (“not only … but also”, “either … or”, “neither … nor”) must share the same grammatical form. A list mixing gerunds, infinitives, and nouns (“She likes reading, to swim, and dancing”) violates parallelism; the corrected version uses a uniform form (“reading, swimming, and dancing”). With correlatives, the structure after each part must match (“not only honest but also hardworking” — both adjectives). Failure to maintain parallelism is a frequent error because writers focus on meaning rather than form.

6. Conditionals, Reported Speech, and Question Tags

6.1 First, Second, and Third Conditionals

The three canonical conditionals each encode a distinct degree of reality. The first conditional presents a likely future outcome (“If you study, you will pass”). The second conditional imagines a hypothetical present or future (“If I won the lottery, I would travel”). The third conditional regrets a past impossibility (“If she had called, I would have answered”). The verb forms are rigid: the if‑clause never contains “will” or “would”. Mixing the patterns — for example, “If I would know, I will tell you” — signals a fundamental misunderstanding of conditional logic and is always marked wrong.

6.2 Mixed Conditionals and Their Nuances

Mixed conditionals combine the time frames of the second and third conditionals. A past condition with a present result uses “if + past perfect, would + base verb” (“If I had taken the medicine, I would feel better now”). A present condition with a past result uses “if + past simple, would have + past participle” (“If he were smarter, he would have solved it”). These structures allow speakers to express counterfactual reasoning across time boundaries. The exam may test the ability to select the correct mixed form from distractors that misuse simple past or present perfect in the if‑clause.

6.3 Reporting Verbs and Backshifting Rules

Beyond “say” and “tell”, a variety of reporting verbs (“ask”, “wonder”, “promise”, “suggest”) govern different complement patterns: some take a that‑clause with backshift, others take an infinitive (“promised to come”), and others a gerund (“suggested going”). The tense of the reporting verb determines whether backshift occurs. When the reporting verb is in the past, the reported clause typically backshifts; when it is present or future, the original tense remains. Errors arise when candidates apply backshift indiscriminately or fail to adjust pronouns and time adverbials (“tomorrow” → “the next day”).

6.4 Formation and Inversion of Question Tags

A question tag mirrors the auxiliary of the main clause, reverses polarity, and matches the subject pronoun. Positive statements take negative tags (“He is coming, isn’t he?”), negative statements take positive tags (“She doesn’t know, does she?”). Special cases include “I am” → “aren’t I?”, imperatives → “will you?” or “won’t you?”, and “let’s” → “shall we?”. The tag must use the same auxiliary (or a form of “do” if none exists) and the appropriate pronoun. Errors such as “He can swim, can he?” (double positive) or “You’re ready, aren’t you?” (incorrect pronoun) are common.

7. Redundancy, Word Choice, and Common Confusions

7.1 Redundant Expressions and Pleonasms

Redundancy occurs when a word adds no new information because its meaning is already contained in another word. Phrases like “return back”, “revert back”, “free gift”, “past history”, “end result”, and “very unique” are pleonastic; the adverb or adjective repeats the inherent sense of the noun or verb. In formal writing and examinations, such constructions are considered errors of style and are corrected by deleting the redundant element (“return”, “revert”, “gift”, “history”, “result”, “unique”). Recognising these fixed collocations saves time during error spotting.

7.2 Adjective versus Adverb Forms

Adjectives modify nouns and pronouns; adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs. The suffix “‑ly” typically forms adverbs from adjectives (“quick → quickly”), but some adjectives end in “‑ly” already (“friendly”, “lovely”) and have no adverbial form; they require a phrase (“in a friendly manner”). Conversely, flat adverbs (“fast”, “hard”, “late”) share the same form as their adjectives. Errors such as “She runs quick” (should be “quickly”) or “He is very fastly running” (should be “very fast”) reveal confusion between the two categories. The rule “very + adjective” never takes “‑ly” is a useful shortcut.

7.3 Frequently Confused Word Pairs

Certain word pairs differ by a single letter or by part of speech, yet their meanings diverge sharply. “Accept” (verb) versus “except” (preposition), “affect” (verb) versus “effect” (noun), “altar” (noun) versus “alter” (verb), “complement” (noun/verb) versus “compliment” (noun/verb), “stationary” (adjective) versus “stationery” (noun), “principal” (adjective/noun) versus “principle” (noun), “advice” (noun) versus “advise” (verb), “practice” (noun) versus “practise” (verb). The exam often embeds the wrong member of a pair in a sentence, requiring the candidate to spot the semantic mismatch. Learning each pair with a mnemonic sentence (“The effect of the medicine was immediate”) cements the distinction.

7.4 Comparative and Superlative Constructions

Comparatives of one‑syllable adjectives add “‑er” (“taller”), those of two syllables ending in “‑y” change “y” to “i” and add “‑er” (“happier”), and longer adjectives use “more” (“more beautiful”). Superlatives follow the parallel pattern (“tallest”, “happiest”, “most beautiful”). Double comparatives (“more better”) and double superlatives (“most tallest”) are always incorrect. When comparing two entities, “than” follows the comparative; when comparing more than two, “the” precedes the superlative and “of” or “in” indicates the group (“the tallest of the three”, “the best in the class”). Comparisons must be logical: “The climate of Delhi is better than Mumbai” is faulty because a climate is compared to a city; the correction inserts “that of” (“better than that of Mumbai”).

Subjunctive Mood, Mandative Constructions, and Hypothetical Conditionals

The subjunctive mood survives in modern English mainly in three environments: mandative (or “that‑clause”) constructions after verbs such as recommend, insist, demand, suggest, require; formulaic expressions like be that as it may; and counterfactual conditionals introduced by if or as if. In a mandative clause the verb appears in its base form regardless of the subject’s person or number: The committee recommends that the proposal be reviewed. The indicative is reviewed would be an error in formal writing and is a frequent trap in error‑spotting items. The past subjunctive were is used for all persons in present‑time counterfactuals: If I were you, I would accept. A common mistake is to write If I was you, which is acceptable only in informal speech. The past perfect subjunctive (had + past participle) marks past counterfactuals: Had she known the deadline, she would have submitted the report. This inverted form (Had she known) is a concise alternative to If she had known and is often tested for recognition of inversion in conditional sentences. Mixed conditionals combine a past‑time condition with a present‑time result or vice‑versa: If he had studied harder, he would be a doctor now. The examiner may embed a mixed conditional inside a longer sentence and ask which clause carries the subjunctive marker. Recognising the mandative subjunctive also requires distinguishing it from the indicative after verbs of reporting: He suggested that she goes (incorrect) versus He suggested that she go (correct). The presence of should in British usage (He suggested that she should go) is permissible but the bare infinitive remains the standard form in most competitive‑exam rubrics.

Inversion, Fronting, and Emphatic Structures

Inversion—placing the auxiliary or verb before the subject—functions as a grammatical marker of emphasis, negation, or conditionality. The most frequent exam patterns are: (1) negative or restrictive adverbials at the front of a clause (Never have I seen such chaos, Seldom does he complain, Only after the audit did the error surface); (2) conditional clauses with had, were, should where the if is omitted (Had the data been accurate, the model would have converged); (3) comparative constructions introduced by so, such, neither, nor (So intense was the heat that the rails buckled, Neither the manager nor the staff were aware); and (4) fronted participial or prepositional phrases that trigger subject‑auxiliary inversion when the main verb is a form of be (Into the room walked the director). Errors arise when candidates fail to invert after a fronted negative adverbial (Rarely he visits instead of Rarely does he visit) or when they invert unnecessarily after a simple adverbial of time (Yesterday went he to the market). The “do‑support” rule applies only when the main verb is not an auxiliary or a form of be: Little did they know (correct) versus Little they knew (incorrect). Inversion also appears in “so/neither” tag‑like responses: She can swim; so can he. A subtle trap is the use of not only … but also where inversion follows not only if it begins the sentence: Not only did he finish early, but he also helped others. Mastery of these patterns allows rapid identification of the single‑word error (missing auxiliary, wrong verb form, or misplaced subject) that the exam typically hides in a long sentence.

Error‑Spotting Worked Example
Identify the error in the following sentence:
“Scarcely had the meeting begun when the fire alarm rang, and everyone evacuated the building.”
The sentence contains a coordination error. After the fronted negative adverbial Scarcely the auxiliary had correctly inverts with the subject the meeting. However, the conjunction when is inappropriate; the standard correlative is than (Scarcely had the meeting begun than the fire alarm rang). The corrected version: Scarcely had the meeting begun than the fire alarm rang, and everyone evacuated the building.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Inversion Items
When a sentence opens with a negative or restrictive adverbial (never, rarely, seldom, hardly, scarcely, barely, only, little, not only), immediately check for subject‑auxiliary inversion. If the main verb is not an auxiliary or be, insert the appropriate form of do. Circle the adverbial, locate the verb, and verify the order before reading the rest of the sentence. This two‑second scan eliminates the majority of inversion‑related errors.

Ellipsis, Substitution, and Gapping in Coordinated Structures

Ellipsis permits the omission of material that is recoverable from the linguistic context, reducing redundancy while preserving meaning. Three major types appear in competitive‑exam sentences: (1) verb‑phrase ellipsis after an auxiliary or modal (She can play the violin, and he can <u>play the violin</u> too → he can too); (2) gapping, where a repeated verb is omitted from the second conjunct while its arguments remain (Ravi bought a pen; Priya, a notebook); and (3) pseudogapping, a hybrid where a lexical verb is partially retained (She will bake a cake, and he will <u>bake</u> cookies → he will bake cookies). Substitution replaces a constituent with a pro‑form: do so for verb phrases (He promised to call, and he did so), so for clauses after verbs of thinking or saying (I think so), and one/ones for noun phrases (I prefer the red shirt to the blue one). Errors surface when the ellipsis site lacks a licensor (no auxiliary to support verb‑phrase ellipsis) or when the remnant does not match the antecedent in case or number (She likes apples, and he does <u>like</u> oranges – the auxiliary does requires bare infinitive like, not likes). Gapping demands parallel syntactic structure; a mismatch such as Ravi bought a pen; Priya, a notebook was bought is ungrammatical because the second conjunct retains a passive verb while the first is active. The exam often embeds a long coordinate sentence and asks which clause contains an illicit ellipsis. The test‑taker must reconstruct the omitted material mentally and verify that the licensor (auxiliary, modal, or to) is present and that the remnant’s form matches the antecedent’s.

Nominalization, Clause Reduction, and Participial Phrases

Nominalization turns verbs or adjectives into nouns, often shifting the syntactic weight of a sentence. Typical suffixes include -tion, -ment, -ance, -al, -ing (gerunds). While nominalizations can create concise academic prose, they also generate classic error‑spotting traps: (1) subject‑verb disagreement when a nominalized phrase becomes the subject (The analysis of the data <u>are</u> complete → is); (2) misplaced modifiers when a participial phrase intended to modify the logical subject attaches to the grammatical subject (Having finished the report, the computer was shut down – the computer did not finish the report). Clause reduction converts a finite relative or adverbial clause into a non‑finite participial or infinitive phrase. A defining relative clause with a passive verb can be reduced to a past‑participle phrase: The proposal <u>which was submitted</u> yesterday → The proposal submitted yesterday. An active relative clause reduces to a present‑participle phrase: The student <u>who is writing</u> the thesis → The student writing the thesis. Errors arise when the reduction is applied to a clause whose subject differs from the head noun (The book <u>which I read</u> was interesting cannot become The book reading was interesting). In adverbial clauses, because + clause can reduce to a present‑participle phrase only when the subjects are identical: Because she was tired, she left early → Being tired, she left early. If the subjects differ, reduction is impossible and the full clause must be retained. The exam frequently presents a sentence with a dangling participle or a nominalized subject that disagrees in number; spotting the mismatch requires identifying the true logical subject and the verb that agrees with it.

Advanced Punctuation: Comma Splices, Semicolons, Dashes, and Sentence Boundaries

Punctuation errors are a staple of sentence‑correction sections because they test the ability to recognise independent‑clause boundaries. A comma splice occurs when two independent clauses are joined only by a comma: The data were incomplete, the analysis was delayed. The correction options are a period, a semicolon, a coordinating conjunction with a comma, or a subordinating conjunction. A semicolon links closely related independent clauses without a conjunction: The data were incomplete; the analysis was delayed. It also separates items in a complex list where the items themselves contain commas: The committee included Dr. Rao, cardiologist; Ms. Patel, neurologist; and Mr. Singh, oncologist. A dash (em dash) can replace a colon, semicolon, or parentheses for emphasis or to set off an appositive that contains internal commas: The winner—an underdog from a small town—received a standing ovation. Overuse of dashes or using a hyphen instead of an em dash is penalised. Colons introduce a list, an explanation, or a quotation, but only after a complete independent clause: She brought three items: a pen, a notebook, and a laptop. A common trap is placing a colon after a verb or preposition (The items are: pen, notebook – incorrect). Parentheses enclose supplementary information that can be removed without affecting grammaticality; the surrounding sentence must remain punctuated correctly if the parenthetical is omitted. The exam often embeds a long sentence with multiple punctuation marks and asks which mark is misused. The systematic approach: (1) bracket each independent clause; (2) verify that each boundary is marked by a period, semicolon, coordinating conjunction + comma, or subordinating conjunction; (3) check that colons follow complete clauses and that dashes appear in pairs or singly for terminal emphasis; (4) ensure no comma separates a subject from its verb or a verb from its object unless a non‑restrictive element intervenes.

  • Treating a mandative subjunctive as indicative (e.g., “recommend that he goes” instead of “recommend that he go”).
  • Failing to invert after fronted negative adverbials (“Rarely he visits” vs. “Rarely does he visit”).
  • Using “when” instead of “than” after “scarcely/hardly” (“Scarcely had he arrived when” → “than”).
  • Ellipsis without a licensor (“She likes tea, and he does likes coffee” – missing bare infinitive).
  • Dangling participial phrases where the logical subject differs from the grammatical subject (“Having finished the report, the computer was shut down”).

Verb Complementation: Gerunds, Infinitives, and Raising Constructions

English verbs select specific non‑finite complements: a gerund (‑ing form), a to‑infinitive, or a bare infinitive. Some verbs admit both but with a meaning shift — stop + gerund means “cease an activity” (stop smoking), whereas stop + to‑infinitive means “pause in order to do something” (stop to smoke). Verbs such as remember, forget, regret, try behave similarly; the test‑maker often places the wrong complement after the verb to create a subtle semantic error that does not violate surface syntax.

A second layer of complexity involves raising versus control verbs. Raising verbs (seem, appear, happen, turn out) have no semantic subject of their own; the subject of the embedded clause “raises” to become the matrix subject (He seems to be tired). Control verbs (want, decide, promise, manage) assign a thematic role to their subject, which then controls the understood subject of the infinitive (He wants to leave). Errors arise when a raising verb is treated as a control verb (e.g., *It seems him to be tired) or when a control verb is used with a passive infinitive that mismatches the controller.

Perception verbs (see, hear, watch, feel) license either a bare infinitive or a gerund, but the choice changes aspect: I saw him cross the street (completed event) versus I saw him crossing the street (ongoing). Causative make, let, have require a bare infinitive in the active (She made him apologize) but a to‑infinitive in the passive (He was made to apologize). The exam frequently tests the passive transformation of causatives and the aspectual distinction with perception verbs.

When analysing a sentence, first identify the matrix verb, then consult its complementation pattern. If the verb appears in a list of “verb + gerund” or “verb + infinitive” items, verify that the form matches the intended meaning. For raising verbs, check that the matrix subject can logically serve as the subject of the embedded predicate; for control verbs, ensure the controller is the semantic agent of the infinitive.

Negative Polarity Items and the Scope of Negation

Negative polarity items (NPIs) — any, anyone, anything, ever, at all, a bit, a single — are licensed only in downward‑entailing environments: negation, questions, conditionals, comparatives, and certain adverbials such as before, without, rarely, hardly, scarcely. In a simple negative clause, She didn’t see anyone is grammatical because not c‑commands the NPI. In a positive declarative, *She saw anyone is ungrammatical; the correct form is someone.

The scope of negation determines whether an NPI in an embedded clause is licensed. If the negation resides in the matrix clause, it can license an NPI in a complement clause only when the complement is non‑finite or a subjunctive that shares the same polarity context: I don’t think he has ever been there (licensed). However, in I think he hasn’t ever been there, the negation is inside the embedded clause and still licenses ever. The trap appears when a higher‑clause negative does not c‑command the NPI because of an intervening finite boundary: *She said that nobody saw anyone is fine, but *She didn’t say that anyone saw the accident is illicit because anyone is not in the scope of not.

Common errors include using any in affirmative statements (*Any student can solve this → Any student can solve this is actually acceptable as a free‑choice any, but *He bought any books is not), substituting ever for never in negative contexts (*He hasn’t ever done it is acceptable, but *He has ever done it is not), and misplacing only so that it fails to license a following NPI (*Only John didn’t see anyone licenses anyone, but *John only didn’t see anyone may not). The examiner often embeds an NPI inside a relative clause whose antecedent is negated, testing whether the candidate recognises that the relative clause constitutes a separate licensing domain.

A reliable diagnostic: replace the NPI with a non‑polar counterpart (someone, something, always). If the sentence remains grammatical, the original NPI was likely unlicensed. Also, check whether a licensor (negation, question operator, conditional if, comparative than) c‑commands the NPI without an intervening finite clause boundary that blocks scope.

Technique
Identify the error in the following sentence: "The committee didn't approve any proposal, but they approved any budget increase."
The first clause correctly licenses the NPI any with didn't. The second clause is affirmative; any cannot appear there. Replace the second any with some or a: "The committee didn't approve any proposal, but they approved a budget increase."
Exam‑day strategy
When a sentence contains an NPI, locate the nearest potential licensor (negation, question, conditional, comparative). Verify that the licensor c‑commands the NPI and that no finite clause boundary intervenes. If the licensor is absent or blocked, the NPI is the error.

Cleft Sentences, Pseudoclefts, and Extraposition

An it‑cleft has the form It is/was X that/who Y, where X is the focused constituent and the relative clause supplies the presupposed background. The focused element can be a noun phrase (It was the manager who approved the plan), a prepositional phrase (It was in June that the meeting occurred), or an adverbial clause. The verb of the cleft clause agrees with it (singular), not with the focused noun: It is the players who are responsible (plural verb in the relative clause, but is in the matrix).

A wh‑cleft (pseudocleft) uses a free relative clause as subject: What the team needs is a new coach. The reversed pseudocleft places the focus first: A new coach is what the team needs. In both types, the copula agrees with the subject of the main clause (singular what or the focused NP). Errors often involve case mismatch in the focused pronoun: *It is me who am late (prescriptive grammar prefers It is I who am late), and verb agreement errors such as *It are the students who left.

Extraposition moves a heavy subject clause to the end and inserts dummy it: It is essential that the report be submitted (from That the report be submitted is essential). The extraposed clause can be a that-clause, an infinitive (It is easy to forget), or a gerund (It is no use arguing). A frequent trap is using extraposition with a verb that does not allow it (*It happened that he left early is acceptable, but *It seemed that he left early is marginal; He seemed to leave early is preferred). Also, the dummy it must not be confused with a referential pronoun.

To spot errors, first decide whether the sentence is a cleft, pseudocleft, or extraposition. Check that the copula agrees with the matrix subject (it or what), that the focused constituent bears the correct case, and that the relative or complement clause is well‑formed. In extraposition, verify that the predicate adjective or noun licenses the construction (e.g., important, necessary, possible, easy).

Comparative Correlatives and Double Comparatives

The correlative comparative construction pairs two comparative clauses introduced by the: The more you practise, the better you become. Both clauses must contain a comparative form (more, less, better, worse, etc.) and the definite article the precedes each comparative. Omitting the first the (*More you practise, the better you become) or using a non‑comparative (*The more you practise, the good you become) is ungrammatical.

Double comparatives — forms such as *more better, *more taller, *most fastest — are universally rejected in standard English. The correct forms are the synthetic comparatives better, taller, fastest or the periphrastic more + adjective only when the adjective does not have a synthetic comparative (e.g., more beautiful, not *beautifuller). The error often appears in sentences that mix a synthetic and a periphrastic comparative for the same adjective.

Comparatives with as…as and so…as follow distinct polarity rules: as…as is used in affirmative and negative clauses, while so…as is restricted to negative contexts (*He is not as tall as his brother vs. He is not so tall as his brother). After a comparative adjective, than introduces the standard of comparison; substituting then or when is a classic error. With less and fewer, the count/mass distinction applies: fewer mistakes (count) versus less water (mass).

Examiners frequently embed a correlative comparative inside a longer sentence and delete one the or replace a comparative with a positive degree adjective. The quick check: locate each the + comparative pair; ensure both are present, both are true comparatives, and the clauses are parallel in structure.

Modal Perfects: Epistemic versus Deontic Readings

Modal perfects combine a modal auxiliary with have + past participle: must have, should have, could have, might have, may have, need not have, ought to have. They locate the modal judgment in the past. The same form can convey an epistemic reading (speaker’s deduction about a past event) or a deontic reading (obligation, advisability, or permission concerning a past action).

Epistemic examples: He must have left already (strong deduction), She could have missed the train (possibility), They might have forgotten (weaker possibility). Deontic examples: You should have consulted the manual (advice about a past omission), We ought to have warned them (moral obligation), You need not have bought a ticket (lack of necessity, but the action was performed). The modal must lacks a deontic perfect; *You must have done it cannot express past obligation — had to is used instead.

Common errors include using must have for past obligation (*You must have submitted the form yesterday → You had to submit), confusing could have (past ability or possibility) with was able to (actualized ability), and treating need not have as equivalent to did not need to (the former implies the action was performed unnecessarily, the latter that it was not performed). The negative cannot have is epistemic only (He cannot have known), while could not have can be epistemic or deontic (past inability).

When analysing a modal perfect, first decide whether the context expresses the speaker’s certainty about a past fact (epistemic) or a normative judgment about a past action (deontic). Then verify that the modal chosen admits the intended reading in the perfect form. If the sentence demands a past deontic obligation, replace must have with had to or should have.

  • Using a gerund after a verb that requires a to‑infinitive (or vice‑versa) without a meaning change.
  • Placing an NPI in a clause that is not c‑commanded by a licensor, especially across a finite clause boundary.
  • Omitting the definite article the in one half of a correlative comparative or inserting a positive‑degree adjective where a comparative is required.
  • Applying a double comparative form such as *more better or *most fastest.
  • Treating must have as a past deontic obligation instead of using had to or should have.
  • Mis‑agreeing the copula in a cleft sentence (e.g., *It are the players who…) or using the wrong pronoun case for the focused element.
  • Extraposing a clause with a predicate that does not license dummy it (e.g., *It happened that he left is acceptable, but *It seemed that he left is not).

Mastering these advanced patterns enables precise error detection and confident sentence correction.

Apposition and Appositive Constructions: Restrictive versus Non‑Restrictive Uses

An appositive is a noun phrase placed beside another noun phrase to identify, explain, or rename it. The critical distinction for error detection lies in restrictiveness. A restrictive (defining) appositive narrows the reference of the head noun and is not set off by commas: The poet Kalidasa wrote Shakuntala implies there are multiple poets and the appositive Kalidasa picks out one. A non‑restrictive (non‑defining) appositive adds supplementary information about an already uniquely identified referent and requires commas (or dashes/parentheses): Kalidasa, the poet, wrote Shakuntala. Examiners frequently test punctuation by omitting commas around a non‑restrictive appositive (*My brother Rahul lives in Delhi when the speaker has only one brother) or inserting commas around a restrictive one (*The author, Shakespeare, wrote Hamlet).

Case agreement between the head noun and the appositive follows the syntactic function of the head. In We elected Rama, captain, both Rama and captain are objective because Rama is the object of elected. In Rama, captain of the team, led the parade, both are nominative. Errors arise when the appositive retains a default nominative form despite the head being objective (*They appointed him, leader instead of leader — though English lacks morphological case on common nouns, pronouns reveal the rule: They appointed him, *he is impossible; the appositive must be him).

Appositives introduced by namely, that is, i.e., e.g., for example, such as, including are typically non‑restrictive and require preceding punctuation (comma, dash, or semicolon) and following comma. A common trap: The committee selected three members, namely, A, B, and C — the comma after namely is optional but the comma before it is mandatory. When the appositive is a clause (a clausal appositive), it usually follows an abstract noun such as fact, idea, belief, rumor, news and is introduced by that: The news that he resigned shocked everyone. This that-clause is restrictive (no commas) because it specifies the content of the noun. Replacing that with which (*The news which he resigned) is ungrammatical; which introduces relative clauses, not appositive clauses.

Multiple appositives can stack: Delhi, the capital of India, a megacity of over twenty million, faces severe pollution. Each non‑restrictive layer requires its own pair of commas (or dashes). Omitting the second comma (*Delhi, the capital of India, a megacity of over twenty million faces severe pollution) creates a garden‑path reading where megacity appears to be the main verb. Examiners also test false appositives: My friend, the doctor, called versus My friend the doctor called. The first means the speaker has one friend who is a doctor; the second means the speaker has multiple friends and the one who is a doctor called. The punctuation changes the truth conditions.

Absolute Constructions and Nominative Absolutes: Structure and Punctuation

An absolute construction (also called a nominative absolute) consists of a noun phrase followed by a non‑finite verb form (participle, infinitive, or bare predicate) that stands grammatically detached from the main clause. It has no explicit syntactic link — no conjunction, no preposition — yet it conveys a circumstantial relationship such as cause, time, condition, or attendant circumstance. The classic pattern is Noun + Present Participle: The weather permitting, we shall depart. Here The weather permitting is equivalent to If the weather permits or Since the weather permits. The noun (weather) is the logical subject of the participle (permitting), but it is not the subject of the main clause (we). This independence is the defining feature.

Variants include Noun + Past Participle (passive sense): The work completed, they went home (= After the work was completed). Noun + Adjective: He stood there, arms folded. Noun + Prepositional Phrase: The meeting over, everyone left. Noun + Infinitive (rare, formal): The contract to be signed, we waited. In all cases, the absolute phrase is set off by commas (or dashes) because it is a parenthetical adjunct. Omitting the commas (*The weather permitting we shall depart) is a punctuation error; inserting a conjunction (*The weather permitting, and we shall depart) destroys the absolute structure.

A frequent examiner trap is the dangling absolute, where the logical subject of the participle is missing or mismatched. *Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful is a dangling participle, not an absolute, because trees cannot walk. A true absolute would be We walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful (awkward but grammatical) or better As we walked down the street, the trees looked beautiful. The absolute construction requires an overt noun phrase as its subject. Another trap: using a finite verb (*The weather permits, we shall depart — comma splice) or a gerund without a possessive subject (*The weather permitting, we shall depart is correct; *Weather permitting, we shall depart is acceptable as a fixed elliptical absolute where the noun is generic).

Pronoun case in absolutes follows the nominative (subject) form because the pronoun is the subject of the non‑finite predicate: He being the eldest, he inherited the property (first he is nominative). In informal usage, accusative forms appear (*Him being the eldest), but formal written English — and therefore exam answers — requires the nominative. The absolute construction is a hallmark of elevated register; examiners contrast it with subordinate clauses to test concision and style. Because the rain had stopped, the match resumed (subordinate clause) versus The rain having stopped, the match resumed (absolute). Both are correct; the absolute is more compact. Errors include double marking (*With the rain having stopped, the match resumed — with makes it a prepositional phrase, not an absolute) and tense mismatch (*The rain stops, the match resumed).

Error Detection: Absolute Construction vs Dangling Participle
Identify the error, if any, in the following sentence:

The committee having rejected the proposal, the chairman resigned.
No error. This is a well‑formed nominative absolute. The committee is the noun phrase, having rejected is the perfect participle (active), and the phrase is correctly set off by a comma. The logical subject of having rejected is the committee; the main clause subject the chairman is different — this independence is precisely what makes it an absolute construction. The perfect participle indicates anteriority (the rejection preceded the resignation). A common incorrect alternative would be *The committee having rejected the proposal, it was withdrawn where the main clause subject it does not match the absolute's subject, but that is still grammatical; the absolute simply provides background. The sentence as given is correct.
Exam‑Day Strategy: Spotting Absolutes
When you see a noun phrase followed by a participle at the start of a sentence, set off by a comma, ask: (1) Does the noun phrase function as the subject of the participle? (2) Is it different from the subject of the main clause? (3) Is there no conjunction or preposition linking it? If yes to all three, it is an absolute construction. Check that the participle form matches the voice (active having rejected vs passive having been rejected) and that the pronoun case (if any) is nominative. If the noun phrase is missing and the participle appears to modify the main clause subject, it is a participial phrase — verify it does not dangle.

Focus Particles: Scope of Only, Even, and Just in Complex Sentences

Focus particles (only, even, just, merely, simply, exactly, precisely) associate with a specific constituent — the focus — and their placement determines which part of the sentence falls within their scope. In spoken English, prosodic stress disambiguates; in writing, linear position is the primary cue. The standard prescriptive rule: place the focus particle immediately before the constituent it modifies. Only John ate the apple (no one else ate it). John only ate the apple (he did nothing else with it). John ate only the apple (he ate nothing else). John ate the apple only (same as previous, but less common). Examiners exploit the ambiguity of medial placement: She only speaks French could mean She speaks only French (language restriction) or Only she speaks French (person restriction). The safe correction moves only adjacent to the intended focus.

Even introduces a scalar implicature: the focused element is an extreme or surprising case on a contextually relevant scale. Even John passed implies John was the least likely to pass. John even passed is ambiguous: it could mean Even John passed (focus on John) or John passed, and that is surprising (focus on the whole VP). John passed even is ungrammatical. Just behaves similarly to only but often carries a temporal or minimising nuance: He just arrived (recently) versus Just he arrived (only he, no one else). In negative contexts, just can be a Negative Polarity Item: He didn't just leave; he slammed the door (minimising the leaving).

Scope interactions with negation create classic traps. Not only ... but also is a correlative pair requiring parallel structure: Not only did he sing, but he also danced (inversion after not only). *Not only he sang, but also he danced lacks inversion. Only in negative sentences: He didn't only sing; he danced (he did more than sing). He only didn't sing (the only thing he didn't do was sing). The position of only relative to not flips the meaning. Examiners also test only with infinitives: He tried only to help (his sole purpose was helping) versus He only tried to help (he did not succeed). With even, the focus must be a scalar endpoint: *Even the tallest student is short is infelicitous because tallest is not a low point on a height scale; Even the shortest student is tall works.

In complex sentences, the focus particle can appear in the main clause but take scope over an embedded constituent (long‑distance association). He only said that he likes Mary most naturally means The only thing he said was that he likes Mary (focus on the complement clause). To force focus on Mary, write He said that he only likes Mary or He said that he likes only Mary. However, Only he said that he likes Mary unambiguously focuses the subject. The exam tests whether you can identify the intended focus and reposition the particle without altering the truth conditions. A useful heuristic: read the sentence with contrastive stress on each candidate focus; the reading that matches the context tells you where the particle belongs.

Quantifier Scope and Distributivity: Each, Every, All, and Negation Interaction

Quantifiers differ in their distributive force and their interaction with negation, collective predicates, and plural morphology. Each is strongly distributive: it forces a one‑by‑one reading and requires a singular verb. Each student has a book (each student has his/her own book). Every is also distributive but more abstract; it resists collective predicates (*Every student gathered in the hall is odd; All the students gathered is natural). All with a plural noun (all students) permits both distributive and collective readings: All students passed (each passed) or All students surrounded the building (collective). With a mass noun (all water), it is non‑distributive. Both is dual distributive: Both students passed entails each passed.

Negation scope creates systematic ambiguities. All students didn't pass has two readings: (1) Partial negation (not all passed; some did) — the preferred reading in standard English, paraphrased as Not all students passed. (2) Total negation (none passed) — available in some dialects but often considered illogical because all ... not structurally places negation under the universal quantifier. Examiners prefer Not all students passed for partial negation and No student passed or None of the students passed for total negation. Every student didn't pass strongly favours total negation (none passed) because every resists wide‑scope negation; *Not every student passed is the correct form for partial negation. Each student didn't pass is similarly awkward; Not each student passed is marginal. The safe forms: Not all / Not every / Not both for partial; None / Neither / No for total.

Quantifier floating tests whether the quantifier can detach from its noun phrase. The students have all passed (floated all to adverbial position) is grammatical. The students have each passed is also acceptable. The students have every passed is ungrammatical — every cannot float. Both the students have passed versus The students have both passed — both work. Examiners test this by offering *The students have every passed as an error. Another trap: Each of the students have passed — the verb must agree with each (singular), so has passed. Every one of the students has passed (singular). All of the students have passed (plural). The of-phrase does not change the quantifier's inherent number specification.

Distributivity with each other and one another requires a plural antecedent that is semantically distributive. The two brothers love each other (reciprocal). *Every student loves each other fails because every student is singular; the reciprocal needs a plural group. Correction: All the students love each other or The students love one another. With each as subject, a reciprocal is impossible (*Each student loves each other). However, Each student loves the other works in a two‑member context. Examiners also test each versus every in time expressions: Each day (individual days, emphasis on separation) vs Every day (habitual, undifferentiated). Each of the three days is fine; Every of the three days is ungrammatical — every cannot take an of-phrase.

Preposition Stranding, Pied‑Piping, and Relative Clause Formality

When a relative pronoun or interrogative pronoun is the object of a preposition, English offers two structures: pied‑piping (the preposition moves with the pronoun to the front) and preposition stranding (the preposition remains in situ at the end of the clause). Pied‑piping: The colleague with whom I spoke; To whom did you give it?. Stranding: The colleague whom I spoke with; Who did you give it to?. Both are grammatical in standard English, but register differs. Pied‑piping is formal, often required in academic and legal writing; stranding is neutral to informal and dominates spoken usage. Examiners test two things: (1) whether you recognise that stranding is not an error per se, and (2) whether you can identify contexts where pied‑piping is obligatory or strongly preferred.

Pied‑piping is obligatory when the preposition is part of a fixed collocation or idiom that cannot be split: The situation in which we find ourselves (not *The situation which we find ourselves in). The grounds on which the decision was based (not *The grounds which the decision was based on). The extent to which he succeeded. The manner in which she spoke. These are prepositional verbs or noun‑preposition complexes where the preposition is selected by the head noun/adjective/verb. Stranding them sounds ungrammatical or at best highly marked. A second obligatory context: relative clauses with which as the object of a preposition when the antecedent is a whole clause. He lied, which I was not aware of (stranding acceptable) but He lied, of which I was not aware (pied‑piping) is more formal. However, *He lied, which I was not aware (omitting the preposition) is an error — the preposition cannot be dropped.

Stranding is blocked in non‑restrictive relative clauses with which when the preposition is of expressing a partitive or quantitative relation: The team, most of which were exhausted, continued — *The team, which most of were exhausted is impossible. The quantifier most of must stay together. Similarly, all of which, some of which, none of which require pied‑piping. With whom, stranding is always possible but often avoided in formal prose: The person whom I spoke to vs The person to whom I spoke. In interrogatives, Who did you speak to? is standard; To whom did you speak? is formal. *Whom did you speak to? mixes formal whom with stranded preposition — a style clash examiners flag.

A subtle trap involves preposition omission with verbs that require a prepositional complement. *This is the book I told you about is correct (stranded about). *This is the book I told you drops the required preposition — error. *This is the book about which I told you pied‑pipes correctly. Another trap: pied‑piping with that. *The book that I told you about is fine (stranding). *The book about that I told you is ungrammatical — that cannot be pied‑piped. Only which and whom allow pied‑piping. That and zero relative require stranding or preposition deletion (if the verb permits). Examiners also test preposition choice in pied‑piping: The reason for which he left (not *The reason of which). The preposition must match the head noun (reason for, basis for, grounds for, exception to).

  • Treating a non‑restrictive appositive as restrictive (or vice versa) by misplacing or omitting commas, altering the sentence's truth conditions.
  • Writing a dangling participle and calling it an absolute construction; forgetting that an absolute requires its own overt noun phrase subject.
  • Placing only, even, or just in a default medial position without verifying which constituent actually bears the focus, creating scope ambiguity.
  • Using all ... not for total negation (none) instead of no/none, or using not every when not all is needed for partial negation with plural universals.
  • Stranding a preposition that belongs to a fixed noun‑preposition or adjective‑preposition collocation (e.g., *the reason which he left for instead of the reason for which he left).
  • Pied‑piping with that (*the book about that I read) or omitting the preposition entirely when the verb/adjective requires it.
  • Using every with an of-phrase (*every of the students) or floating every (*the students have every passed), both of which are categorically ungrammatical.
