Foundations of Concord: Number, Person, and the Basic Rule

1.1 The Concept of Agreement in English

Concord, or subject-verb agreement, is the grammatical principle that requires a finite verb to match its subject in both number and person. In English, this matching is most visible in the present tense, where the verb changes form to signal whether the subject is singular or plural and whether it speaks in the first, second, or third person. The rule operates because the verb carries the primary burden of indicating the action or state of the subject; when the verb form clashes with the subject, the sentence loses clarity and grammatical correctness. Examiners test this principle heavily because it reveals whether a candidate can identify the true subject of a sentence, especially when that subject is separated from the verb by modifiers, phrases, or clauses. A failure of concord often signals a deeper confusion about sentence structure, making it a reliable discriminator in competitive assessments.

1.2 Singular and Plural Verb Forms in the Present Tense

In the simple present tense, the distinction between singular and plural verbs is marked by the presence or absence of the inflectional suffix -s or -es on the main verb. A singular third-person subject — he, she, it, or any singular noun phrase — takes a verb ending in -s (for example, walks, watches, goes, has). All other subjects — first person singular I, second person you, first person plural we, second person plural you, and third person plural they — take the base form of the verb without the suffix (for example, walk, watch, go, have). This asymmetry exists because English historically lost most of its person-and-number endings, leaving only the third-person singular marker as a relic. The verb be is exceptional: it distinguishes am for first person singular, is for third person singular, and are for all plural subjects and for second person singular you. Mastery of these forms is the prerequisite for applying every subsequent concord rule.

1.3 Person Distinctions and Their Effect on Verb Shape

Person determines which verb form the grammar selects once number is established. First person (I, we) and second person (you) always pair with the base verb in the present tense, regardless of whether the subject is singular or plural. Third person is where the singular-plural split occurs: singular third-person subjects require the -s ending, while plural third-person subjects (they or plural nouns) take the base form. This means that the pronoun you, though historically plural, now functions for both singular and plural reference but consistently governs a plural verb form (you are, you go). The practical consequence is that identifying the person of the subject is as important as counting its number; a sentence such as The committee has decided works because committee is treated as a singular third-person entity, whereas The committee are divided works because the same noun is reinterpreted as a plural third-person group. The verb form, therefore, is the surface evidence of a deeper decision about how the subject is conceptualized.

Coordination and the Proximity Principle

2.1 Subjects Joined by Coordinating Conjunctions

When two or more subjects are linked by the coordinating conjunction and, the resulting compound subject is ordinarily plural and therefore takes a plural verb. The logic is additive: Ram and Shyam denotes two distinct individuals, so the verb must be plural (are, go, have). However, this additivity collapses when the two nouns refer to a single person, a single entity, or a single concept. In Bread and butter is my favourite breakfast, the two nouns name one combined dish; in The horse and carriage is waiting, they denote a single conveyance; in The poet and philosopher has arrived, a single individual holds both titles. The grammatical signal of this unity is often the absence of a second article — the poet and philosopher versus the poet and the philosopher — though context can override the article test. Recognizing whether and creates a genuine plurality or a notional singularity is essential for correct verb selection.

2.2 The Proximity Rule with Disjunctive Conjunctions

When subjects are joined by or, either…or, neither…nor, or not only…but also, the verb does not automatically become plural. Instead, English applies the proximity principle: the verb agrees with the subject nearest to it. This rule exists because the disjunction presents alternatives rather than a sum; only one of the alternatives is realised, so the verb matches the alternative that is grammatically closest. In Neither the teacher nor the students were present, the plural noun students sits next to the verb, triggering were; reversing the order to Neither the students nor the teacher was present shifts the agreement to the singular teacher. The same principle governs Either the principal or the teachers are responsible versus Either the teachers or the principal is responsible. The proximity rule is a frequent examination target because it tests the candidate's ability to locate the true subject amidst distractors.

2.3 Correlative Constructions and Verb Choice

Correlative pairs such as not only…but also behave identically to either…or and neither…nor with respect to proximity. The verb agrees with the second element, the one immediately preceding it. Thus Not only the students but also the teacher was surprised takes a singular verb because teacher is nearest, while Not only the teacher but also the students were surprised takes a plural verb. A subtle complication arises when the correlative joins noun phrases of different persons: Not only you but also I am to blame sounds awkward because the verb am agrees with I but the construction places you first. Careful writers often rephrase to avoid the clash, but examiners may present the unrevised form to test strict application of the proximity rule. The underlying reason remains the same: the grammar treats the disjunction as offering a single realised subject, and proximity decides which one that is.

Worked Example: Proximity and Person Clash
Select the correct verb form for the sentence: "Neither the director nor the actors ______ satisfied with the rehearsal."
The subject closer to the verb is "the actors", which is plural and third person. By the proximity rule, the verb must agree with "actors". The correct plural verb for third person in the present tense is "were". Therefore the completed sentence reads: "Neither the director nor the actors were satisfied with the rehearsal."
Exam-Day Insight
In multiple-choice questions testing either…or or neither…nor, the distractor option often matches the first subject rather than the second. Train yourself to circle the noun immediately preceding the blank before reading the options; this single habit eliminates the most common proximity trap.

Collective Nouns and the Notion of Unity

3.1 Collective Nouns Acting as a Single Unit

Collective nouns — words such as committee, jury, team, family, audience, government, crowd, flock, herd — denote a group of individuals but can be treated as singular when the group acts as a unified whole. In The committee has submitted its report, the emphasis is on the collective decision; the verb has and the pronoun its both reflect singular agreement. This singular treatment is standard in American English and common in formal British English when the focus is on the group as an institution. The grammatical consequence extends beyond the verb: singular verbs attract singular pronouns (its, itself) and singular determiners (this, that). Candidates must recognise that the singular verb is not an error but a deliberate choice signalling collective unity.

3.2 Collective Nouns Highlighting Individual Members

When the context shifts to the members acting separately or holding differing opinions, the same collective noun takes a plural verb. The committee are divided in their opinions uses are and their because the sentence describes internal disagreement among individuals. British English favours this plural agreement more readily than American English, which often retains the singular even for divided groups. Examination questions set in a British-English framework (as is typical for UPSC and similar Indian competitive exams) expect candidates to detect contextual cues — words such as divided, disagree, argue, differ — that trigger plural agreement. The verb choice, therefore, becomes a diagnostic of meaning: singular for unity, plural for plurality of action.

3.3 Contextual Shifts in Formal and Informal Usage

Certain collective nouns exhibit a strong preference for one number regardless of context. Police and people are inherently plural (The police are investigating; People are waiting), while news, mathematics, physics are inherently singular despite their plural form (discussed in a later section). Family, team, staff, crew fluctuate: The team is flying to London (unit) versus The team are arguing among themselves (individuals). In formal writing, consistency within a passage matters; switching from singular to plural for the same collective noun without a clear shift of focus is considered a concord error. Examiners may present a paragraph where the collective noun appears twice, once with a singular verb and once with a plural verb, and ask whether the shift is justified. The answer hinges on whether the context supplies a reason for the change.

Indefinite Pronouns and Quantifying Expressions

4.1 Singular Indefinite Pronouns and Their Invariable Verbs

A large set of indefinite pronouns — everyone, everybody, anyone, anybody, someone, somebody, no one, nobody, each, either, neither — are grammatically singular and invariably take a singular verb. This rule holds even when the pronoun appears to refer to multiple entities notionally. Everyone is expected to attend uses is because everyone is singular in form; Neither of the candidates has withdrawn uses has because neither is singular. The singular agreement persists despite the plural noun in the following of-phrase (of the candidates). The grammatical reason is that these pronouns are historically compounds of every, any, some, no with one, body, thing, all of which are singular heads. Examiners frequently test this by inserting a plural noun between the pronoun and the verb (Each of the books are versus Each of the books is) to lure candidates into proximity-based error.

4.2 Quantifiers Governed by the Noun Following 'of'

Quantifying expressions such as most, some, all, any, none, half, part, rest, a lot of, a great deal of do not carry inherent number. Instead, the verb agrees with the noun that follows the preposition of. Most of the work is finished takes a singular verb because work is uncountable and singular; Most of the students are present takes a plural verb because students is plural. This principle, sometimes called the "noun-after-of" rule, applies uniformly across the quantifier set. The logic is that the quantifier merely specifies a portion of the noun; the noun itself remains the semantic and syntactic head of the subject phrase. Candidates must therefore look past the quantifier to the noun it quantifies.

4.3 The Special Cases of 'None', 'All', and 'Most'

None deserves particular attention because it can be singular or plural depending on meaning. Traditional grammar treats none as a contraction of not one and prescribes a singular verb (None of the students was late). Modern usage, however, widely accepts plural agreement when the sense is "not any" (None of the students were late). Both forms are now considered standard in reputable style guides, though formal examinations may still prefer the singular in "not one" contexts. All follows the noun-after-of rule strictly: All of the money is gone (singular), All of the coins are missing (plural). Most behaves identically. The expression more than one is an exception: it takes a singular noun and a singular verb (More than one student was absent) despite its plural meaning, because the head one is singular. Similarly, many a governs a singular noun and verb (Many a candidate has failed). These idioms are high-yield examination items because they contradict the notional plurality.

Special Noun Categories: Form versus Meaning

5.1 Nouns Plural in Form but Singular in Meaning

Several nouns end in -s — the typical plural marker — yet function as singular concepts and take singular verbs. These include names of academic disciplines (mathematics, physics, economics, politics, statistics, linguistics, ethics, civics), diseases (measles, mumps, rickets, shingles), games (billiards, darts, dominoes), and the word news. Mathematics is a difficult subject and The news was shocking illustrate the pattern. The plural form is a historical accident: many of these words derive from Greek or Latin neuter plurals that were reanalysed as singular in English. A few, notably economics and politics, can shift to plural when they refer to separate aspects rather than the discipline as a whole: The economics of the project are uncertain (meaning the financial details). Candidates should memorise the core list and the exceptional plural use for economics/politics.

5.2 Nouns Always Plural in Form and Agreement

Conversely, a set of nouns exists only in plural form and always governs plural verbs. These are typically items consisting of two joined parts (scissors, trousers, pants, shorts, jeans, spectacles, glasses, binoculars, tongs, pliers) or collective nouns lacking a singular counterpart (goods, clothes, alms, premises, thanks, regards, riches, wages, outskirts, surroundings, belongings). The scissors are blunt and His clothes are dirty are correct; The scissor is or A clothe is are impossible. To express singular quantity, English uses a pair of: A pair of scissors is on the table. Here the subject becomes the singular pair, so the verb shifts to singular. This pair of construction is a favourite examination device: A pair of trousers was bought versus The trousers were bought.

5.3 Expressions of Quantity: 'A Number of' versus 'The Number of'

The phrases a number of and the number of illustrate how the definite article can flip verb number. A number of functions as a quantifier meaning "several" or "many"; the true head is the plural noun following of, so the verb is plural: A number of students are absent. The number of refers to a statistical total; the head is the singular number, so the verb is singular: The number of students is fifty. The same contrast appears with a majority of (plural when meaning "most of the members") versus the majority of (singular when referring to the collective majority as a unit), though usage varies. The article test — a for plural, the for singular — provides a reliable heuristic for these expressions.

Inversion, Intervening Phrases, and Relative Clauses

6.1 Intervening Phrases and the Real Subject

A common source of concord error is the insertion of a prepositional phrase or other modifier between the subject and the verb. The verb must agree with the head noun of the subject phrase, not with the noun inside the intervening phrase. In The box of chocolates is expensive, the subject head is box (singular), so the verb is is; the plural chocolates is merely the object of the preposition of. Similarly, The quality of the mangoes is good takes is because quality is singular. Longer interruptions — The collection of rare stamps, which he inherited from his uncle, is valuable — do not change the rule. The grammatical reason is that agreement is a relation between the verb and the subject head; modifiers are structurally subordinate and cannot control verb form. Examination sentences often pile multiple phrases between subject and verb to test whether the candidate can isolate the head noun.

6.2 Inverted Sentences Beginning with 'Here' and 'There'

Sentences that open with here or there followed by a form of be (or occasionally other verbs) place the real subject after the verb. This inversion does not alter the agreement rule: the verb still agrees with the post-verbal subject. Here comes the bus (singular) versus Here come the buses (plural); There is a book on the table versus There are two books on the table. The expletive there is not the subject; it is a placeholder that allows the true subject to follow. In questions, the same principle applies: Where is the key? versus Where are the keys? Candidates must identify the noun after the verb and match the verb to it, ignoring the introductory here/there.

6.3 Relative Pronouns and Antecedent Agreement

When a relative pronoun (who, which, that) functions as the subject of a relative clause, its verb agrees with its antecedent — the noun or pronoun the relative clause modifies. In This is one of the best books that have been written, the antecedent of that is books (plural), so the verb is have. In This is the only book that has been written, the antecedent is book (singular), so the verb is has. The distinction between one of the (plural antecedent) and the only (singular antecedent) is a classic examination trap. The rule extends to other antecedents: He is one of those leaders who inspire confidence (plural leaders → inspire) versus He is the only one of the leaders who inspires confidence (singular one → inspires). The verb in the relative clause must reflect the number of the specific noun the relative pronoun refers to, not the number of the main-clause subject.

Mastery of subject-verb agreement requires not merely memorising rules but developing the habit of locating the true subject head in every sentence, regardless of intervening material, inversion, or quantifying expressions. The principles outlined here — basic number and person matching, coordination and proximity, collective noun nuance, indefinite pronoun invariability, quantifier dependence on the noun after of, form-meaning mismatches, and inversion or relative-clause structures — constitute the complete framework tested in competitive examinations. Consistent application of these principles, reinforced by practice with sentences that layer multiple complexities, will secure accuracy in this high-weight grammar topic.

Agreement with Complex Quantifiers and Partitive Constructions

Quantifying expressions that contain a partitive of phrase — such as a lot of, a majority of, a fraction of, a proportion of, a variety of, a range of, none of, all of, most of, some of, any of — follow a single governing principle: the verb agrees with the noun that immediately follows of. When that noun is plural, the verb is plural; when it is singular or uncountable, the verb is singular. For example, A lot of the evidence is circumstantial (uncountable evidence), but A lot of the witnesses are unreliable (plural witnesses). The same rule applies to a majority of: A majority of the committee has voted (collective singular sense) versus A majority of the members have voted (plural sense). In formal writing, none of traditionally takes a singular verb when the noun after of is singular or uncountable (None of the information is accurate) and a plural verb when the noun is plural (None of the candidates are qualified). However, modern usage increasingly accepts plural verbs with singular nouns in informal contexts; examiners usually expect the traditional singular agreement. All of and most of behave identically: All of the cake is gone (singular), All of the cakes are gone (plural). Some of and any of follow the same pattern. A frequent examination twist inserts a long modifier between the quantifier and the of phrase, e.g., A significant proportion of the students who applied for the scholarship are eligible. The head noun remains students, so the verb stays plural. Candidates must locate the noun after of and ignore intervening material.

Compound Subjects Linked by Additive Phrases

Additive phrases such as as well as, along with, together with, in addition to, besides, and including do not create a compound subject. The verb agrees solely with the first noun (the head subject). For instance, The captain, as well as the players, is ready — the head subject captain is singular, so the verb is singular. Reversing the order changes the agreement: The players, together with the captain, are ready. The same principle applies to not only … but also constructions, where the proximity rule governs: Not only the students but also the teacher is responsible (verb agrees with the nearer subject teacher). When the additive phrase is set off by commas, it is a parenthetical modifier and never controls verb number. A common trap places a plural noun in the additive phrase and a singular head subject, tempting the test‑taker to match the verb to the plural noun. The correct strategy is to underline the first noun before any additive phrase and match the verb to that noun alone. This rule holds regardless of the length or complexity of the additive phrase, and it also applies when the additive phrase precedes the subject: Along with the documents, the report was submitted.

Technique: Identify the Head Subject
Choose the correct verb: The director, along with the actors, ___ (is/are) attending the premiere.
The head subject is director (singular); the phrase along with the actors does not change number. Correct verb: is.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When you see additive phrases, underline the first noun and ignore the rest; match the verb to that noun only.

Elliptical and Comparative Structures in Concord

Ellipsis — the omission of words that are recoverable from context — frequently appears in comparative clauses introduced by than or as. The verb in the elliptical clause must agree with its own subject, even though the verb itself is omitted. In She runs faster than he, the full clause is than he runs; the subject he is singular, so the understood verb is singular. When the comparative subject is a plural pronoun, the understood verb is plural: They work harder than we (understood do). The same principle operates with as … as: He is as tall as she (understood is). In formal writing, the auxiliary may be retained for clarity: She runs faster than he does. A related construction is the reduced relative clause: The book (that) I read is interesting — the relative pronoun that is the object of read, so the verb in the main clause agrees with book. When the relative pronoun functions as subject, agreement follows the antecedent: The books that are on the shelf are mine. Examiners often test the distinction between than I and than me; the prescriptive rule requires the nominative pronoun because the elliptical clause contains a subject, but many modern tests accept the objective form in informal registers. Candidates should apply the formal rule unless the question explicitly signals a colloquial context.

Greek and Latin Plurals: Form‑Meaning Mismatches

English retains a substantial set of nouns borrowed from Greek and Latin that keep their original plural inflections. The most frequently tested pairs include criterion / criteria, phenomenon / phenomena, datum / data, medium / media, curriculum / curricula, syllabus / syllabi, appendix / appendices, index / indices, matrix / matrices, and vertex / vertices. In each case the singular form ends in -on, -um, -us, or -ex/-ix, while the plural ends in -a, -i, -ices, or -es. The verb must agree with the grammatical number of the form used: The criterion is clear (singular), The criteria are clear (plural). A persistent error is treating criteria or phenomena as singular because they look like singular nouns ending in -a. Data is a special case: in scientific and academic writing it remains plural (The data are significant), but in general usage it is often treated as a mass noun taking a singular verb (The data is inconclusive). Examiners typically expect the traditional plural agreement in formal contexts. Agenda, originally a plural of agendum, has become a singular noun in modern English (The agenda is long). Media as the plural of medium is still plural in formal writing (The media are reporting), though singular use appears in casual speech. Mastery of these forms requires memorising the singular‑plural pairs and applying the agreement rule without being misled by the unfamiliar endings.

Subjunctive and Conditional Mood Effects on Verb Number

The subjunctive mood in English uses invariant verb forms that do not change for person or number. In the present subjunctive (mandative), the base form be appears for all subjects: It is essential that he be present; It is essential that they be present. The past subjunctive (counterfactual) uses were for every person: If I were you; If she were here; If they were ready. Because these forms are invariant, the usual subject‑verb number agreement does not apply. Conditional sentences with would, could, should, or might also employ invariant modals, so number agreement is irrelevant in the conditional clause. However, the main clause of a conditional sentence follows normal agreement: If the plan were approved, the committee would meet (singular committee → would meet is invariant, but the committee meets would be singular). A common examination item mixes a subjunctive clause with a main clause that contains a collective noun, testing whether the candidate incorrectly forces plural agreement in the subjunctive. The rule to remember: subjunctive be and were never take an -s ending, regardless of the subject. This invariance also extends to the formulaic expressions be it, come what may, and so be it.

  • Treating a majority of as always plural, ignoring the collective‑unit sense.
  • Ignoring the noun after of when the quantifier is none in formal writing.
  • Applying the proximity rule to additive phrases instead of the head subject.
  • Using a singular verb with Greek/Latin plural forms such as criteria or phenomena.
  • Forgetting that subjunctive be and were are invariant for number.
  • Overlooking elliptical comparative clauses where the omitted verb must agree with the subject of the clause.

Distributive Constructions: Each, Every, Many a, and Singular Agreement

The distributive determiners each, every, and the fixed phrase many a impose singular agreement on the verb regardless of the plural sense they may convey. Each distributes attention to individual members of a group one by one; every generalises the distribution across the entire set; many a is an archaic but examinable construction meaning "a large number of" yet grammatically singular. In Each of the candidates has submitted the form, the subject is the singular pronoun each; the prepositional phrase of the candidates does not alter the number. The same rule applies when each follows a plural subject: The candidates each have a copy — here each is an appositive adverb, and the verb agrees with the plural subject candidates. Every never appears with of; it directly modifies a singular noun: Every student is present. Many a always takes a singular noun and singular verb: Many a soldier has fallen. A frequent error is allowing the plural noun in the of-phrase after each to attract a plural verb. Examiners also test the distinction between each as determiner (Each boy runs) and each as pronoun (Each of the boys runs) — both demand singular verbs. When each or every coordinates two singular nouns, the verb remains singular: Each man and each woman was asked; Every boy and every girl receives a prize. If the nouns differ in number, the construction is avoided in formal writing; the rewrite Each man and all women were asked uses plural agreement because all women is plural.

The 'One of Those Who' vs 'The Only One of Those Who' Paradox

Relative clauses beginning with who, that, or which after partitive constructions create a reliable examination trap in concord. The verb inside the relative clause must agree with the antecedent of the relative pronoun, not with the noun immediately preceding it. In He is one of those students who work hard, the antecedent of who is those students (plural), so the verb is plural work. The logic: among the group of hard-working students, he is one. Contrast He is the only one of those students who works hard. Here the only one is the antecedent of who; the meaning restricts the hard-working subset to a single person, so the verb is singular works. The same principle operates with superlatives: This is one of the best novels that have appeared this year (plural have because novels is antecedent) versus This is the best novel that has appeared this year (singular has because novel is antecedent). A parallel construction uses one of the few, one of the many, one of the several — all take plural verbs in the relative clause because the antecedent is the plural noun after of. The insertion of only before one shifts the antecedent to one and forces singular agreement. Candidates must identify the true antecedent by asking "who/that does the verb belong to?" — the answer is never the singular one unless only (or a similar restrictive modifier such as sole) precedes it. This rule holds even when the main clause subject is singular: She is one of the managers who approve the budget (plural approve); She is the only one of the managers who approves the budget (singular approves).

Worked Example: Antecedent Identification
Select the correct verb form: "He is one of those leaders who ______ (inspire / inspires) confidence."
Step 1: Locate the relative pronoun — who. Step 2: Determine its antecedent. The phrase one of those leaders contains two nouns: one (singular) and leaders (plural). Because one is not modified by only, sole, or a superlative, the antecedent of who is the plural noun leaders. Step 3: The verb in the relative clause must agree with leaders → plural inspire. Correct sentence: He is one of those leaders who inspire confidence. Contrast: He is the only one of those leaders who inspires confidence (antecedent = one → singular).
Exam-Day Strategy: The 'Only' Toggle
Circle the word only (or sole, very in the very one) whenever it appears before one in a partitive construction. If it is present, the relative verb is singular; if absent, the relative verb is plural. This binary toggle resolves the item in under five seconds without parsing the full clause.

Agreement with Fractions, Percentages, and Mathematical Expressions

Quantitative expressions involving fractions, percentages, decimals, and proportions follow the same of-phrase rule as other quantifiers: the verb agrees with the noun that follows of. One-third of the cake is eaten (singular cake → singular is); One-third of the cakes are eaten (plural cakes → plural are). Percentages behave identically: Fifty percent of the work is done; Fifty percent of the workers are absent. When the of-phrase is omitted because the referent is clear from context, the verb defaults to singular for fractions and percentages treated as a single quantity: Two-thirds is a large proportion; Ten percent has been allocated. However, if the implied noun is countable and plural, plural agreement is acceptable: Two-thirds (of them) are here. Decimals and exact numbers followed by a unit of measurement take singular verbs because the measurement is conceived as a single quantity: 5.5 kg is the weight limit; 3.14 metres is the circumference; 100 km is a long distance. The same applies to monetary amounts and time spans: Rs 500 is the fee; Two hours is enough. When the number refers to individual items rather than a collective measure, plural agreement returns: Five hundred rupees were found on the table (individual notes); Two hours have passed since we started (emphasising each hour). Mathematical expressions used as subjects — Two plus two equals four, Three times five is fifteen — take singular verbs because the operation yields a single result. Examiners often contrast More than one student is absent (singular) with More students than one are absent (plural) and One or more students are absent (plural by proximity). The phrase a majority of follows the of-noun: A majority of the votes was invalid (singular verb with majority as collective unit) versus A majority of the voters were angry (plural voters).

Titles, Names, and Proper Nouns: Singular Agreement Despite Plural Form

Titles of books, films, plays, paintings, organisations, and proper nouns denoting single entities take singular verbs even when their form is plural. The Canterbury Tales is a medieval masterpiece; The Three Musketeers was written by Dumas; The United Nations has its headquarters in New York; The Philippines is an archipelago; The Netherlands is a low-lying country. This rule extends to names of diseases (Measles is infectious), games (Billiards is a game of skill), fields of study (Mathematics is compulsory), and newspapers (The Times publishes a daily edition). Collective proper nouns such as the government, the committee, the jury follow the collective-noun rules already covered, but when they are part of a fixed title they are almost always treated as singular units: The Committee on Public Accounts has submitted its report. Brand names and trademarks used as subjects are singular: Sony produces electronics; Microsoft dominates the software market. A special case arises with plural-form proper nouns that denote a single geographical or political entity: The Andes is a mountain range; The Alps is a popular destination; The United States is a federal republic. However, when the plural form refers to the individual components, plural agreement appears: The Andes are snow-capped (the peaks); The United States are represented by fifty stars (the states individually). Examiners test the candidate's ability to distinguish the entity-as-whole from the entity-as-parts. The same distinction applies to the police (always plural in standard English: The police are investigating) versus the police force (singular: The police force is recruiting).

Agreement with Nouns Ending in -ics and Abstract Plurals

Nouns ending in -ics that denote fields of study, sciences, arts, or activities are singular when they refer to the discipline as a whole: Mathematics is the language of science; Physics explains natural phenomena; Economics studies resource allocation; Politics is the art of the possible; Statistics deals with data analysis; Gymnastics requires flexibility. When the same word refers to a set of facts, performances, or specific instances, it may take a plural verb: The statistics are misleading (the numerical data points); His politics are questionable (his political views or manoeuvres); The acoustics of the hall are excellent (the acoustic properties). The distinction mirrors the collective-noun unity-versus-individuals split. Ethics is usually singular as a branch of philosophy (Ethics is a required course) but plural when meaning moral principles (His ethics are beyond reproach). Linguistics, phonetics, semantics, robotics, genetics — all follow the pattern. A related group comprises abstract nouns that are plural in form but singular in meaning when denoting a concept: News travel fast (incorrect; correct: News travels fast); Information is power; Advice is welcome; Furniture is expensive; Luggage was lost. These are mass nouns and never take plural verbs. However, some abstract plurals have no singular form and are always plural: Thanks are due; Proceeds go to charity; Remains were discovered; Outskirts are dangerous; Surroundings influence behaviour. Candidates must memorise the short list of invariant plural abstracts and the -ics words that shift number according to sense.

  • Treating each of + plural noun as plural — the verb must be singular.
  • Using singular verb in one of those who clauses when only is absent — the relative verb agrees with the plural antecedent.
  • Applying plural agreement to percentages or fractions without checking the of-noun — the verb follows the noun after of.
  • Assuming titles with plural form take plural verbs — titles of single works are singular.
  • Using singular verb with the police, the poor, the rich — these are always plural in standard English.
  • Forgetting that statistics, politics, economics can be plural when referring to data points or views rather than the discipline.
  • Overlooking the singular default for mathematical expressions (Two plus two equals) and measurements (Five kilometres is).

Agreement with Clausal Subjects: That‑Clauses, Gerund Phrases, and Infinitival Phrases

When a clause functions as the subject of a sentence, the verb is almost invariably singular because the clause itself is a single syntactic unit. A that‑clause serving as subject — for example, That the proposal was rejected surprised everyone — takes a singular verb (surprised) regardless of the number of nouns inside the clause. The same principle governs gerund phrases (Swimming in the lake refreshes me) and infinitival phrases (To finish the project on time requires effort). In each case the head of the subject phrase is a non‑finite verb form (gerund or infinitive) or a complementiser (that), and the agreement target is that head, not any embedded noun.

Major reference grammars (e.g., Quirk et al., A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language; Huddleston & Pullum, The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language) describe clausal subjects as singular by default. Exceptions arise only when the clause is a free relative introduced by what, whatever, or whoever and the referent is semantically plural: What were once rare species are now protected. Here the verb agrees with the plural antecedent implied by the free relative, not with the clause as a formal unit. Another marginal case occurs with pseudo‑cleft constructions where the subject is a wh‑clause and the verb may match the complement: What the team need are more resources. In formal writing, however, the singular verb (is) is often preferred because the clause remains a singular entity.

Intervening material between the clausal subject and the verb does not change the rule. In That the committee has delayed the decision, despite pressure from stakeholders, is regrettable, the singular verb is agrees with the that‑clause. Candidates should therefore locate the clausal boundary (the complementiser or the non‑finite verb) and treat everything up to the main verb as a single subject.

Agreement with Quantificational Adverbs and Focus Particles: Only, Even, Just

Focus particles such as only, even, and just can precede a subject and create the illusion that the particle itself influences number. In reality, the verb must agree with the true subject that follows the particle. Consider Only the director has signed the memo versus Only the directors have signed the memo. The particle only does not carry number; the verb mirrors the head noun (director singular, directors plural). The same holds for even: Even the youngest participant understands the rule (singular) versus Even the youngest participants understand the rule (plural). Just behaves identically: Just the manager is responsible versus Just the managers are responsible.

A common testing pattern places the focus particle between a compound subject and the verb, e.g., The director and the assistant only have authority. Here only modifies the verb phrase, not the subject, so the plural subject (director and assistant) still demands a plural verb (have). When the particle precedes the first conjunct, as in Only the director and the assistant have authority, the verb remains plural because the subject is still a coordination of two nouns. The proximity principle does not apply because the particle is not a conjunction.

In inverted or fronted structures the particle may appear at clause‑initial position: Only after the audit did the board approve the budget. The subject (the board) follows the auxiliary, and the verb agrees with that subject. Recognising that focus particles are adjuncts, not heads, prevents the error of matching the verb to the particle.

Subject isolation with focus particles
Choose the correct verb form: Only the committee ___ (has/have) approved the proposal.
The true subject is the committee, a singular collective noun treated as a unit in this context. The focus particle only does not affect number. Therefore the singular verb has is correct: Only the committee has approved the proposal.
Exam‑day strategy
When a sentence begins with only, even, or just, bracket the particle and locate the first noun phrase that follows. That noun phrase is the subject; match the verb to its number, ignoring the particle entirely.

Agreement with Collective Quantifiers: A Variety of, A Range of, A Host of, A Multitude of

Expressions such as a variety of, a range of, a host of, and a multitude of function as quantifiers that precede a plural noun. Their agreement behaviour parallels a number of: the verb is plural because the quantifier emphasises the plurality of the following noun. Thus A variety of options are available and A host of questions remain unanswered both take plural verbs. The head of the subject phrase is the plural noun (options, questions), not the singular quantifier noun (variety, host).

However, when the quantifier itself is the focus of the statement — referring to the abstract collection as a single concept — a singular verb may appear. For example, A variety of apple is on display (meaning one type of apple) versus A variety of apples are on display (multiple types). In formal academic prose the plural verb is overwhelmingly preferred whenever the of-phrase contains a countable plural noun, because the construction is analysed as a partitive quantifier rather than a nominal head. Standard grammars (e.g., Biber et al., Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English) note that the singular variant is restricted to contexts where the quantifier denotes a taxonomic class rather than a set of individuals.

Test questions often test the distinction by embedding a singular mass noun after the quantifier: A range of equipment is required (singular equipment forces a singular verb). The rule, therefore, is: identify the noun immediately following of; if that noun is plural countable, use a plural verb; if it is singular or mass, use a singular verb.

Agreement with Partitive Expressions: The Rest of, The Remainder of, The Bulk of

Partitive phrases headed by the rest of, the remainder of, the bulk of, the majority of, and the minority of follow the same agreement principle as a number of versus the number of. The verb agrees with the noun that follows of. When that noun is a plural countable, the verb is plural: The rest of the students are waiting, The remainder of the votes were invalid, The bulk of the data are corrupted (note: data is traditionally plural in formal usage). When the of-noun is a singular mass or collective noun, the verb is singular: The rest of the money is missing, The remainder of the cake has been eaten, The bulk of the work remains unfinished.

A subtle trap occurs with the majority of when the of-noun is a collective noun that can be singular or plural depending on sense. The majority of the committee has decided (committee as a unit) versus The majority of the committee have disagreed (emphasising individual members). The same flexibility applies to the minority of and the plurality of. Candidates must read the surrounding context for cues — words such as unanimously (singular) or individually (plural) — to decide which agreement the examiner expects.

In legal and bureaucratic registers, the bulk of frequently appears with singular mass nouns (the bulk of the legislation is procedural) and is treated as singular. The principle remains invariant: the verb mirrors the number of the of-complement, not the partitive head.

Agreement with Correlative Additives: Not Only … But Also

The correlative pair not only … but also links two subjects (or other constituents) and triggers the proximity rule: the verb agrees with the subject nearest to it. In Not only the students but also the teacher is responsible, the nearer subject is the teacher (singular), so the verb is singular. Reversing the order yields Not only the teacher but also the students are responsible, where the plural students controls a plural verb. This mirrors the behaviour of either … or and neither … nor, which are covered elsewhere, but the additive meaning of not only … but also often leads candidates to assume a plural verb because two entities are mentioned.

When the correlative connects non‑subject elements (e.g., objects, complements), the verb still agrees with the grammatical subject of the clause, which may be outside the correlative. For instance, The report not only analyses the data but also recommends action — the subject the report is singular, so the verbs analyses and recommends are both singular. The correlative does not create a compound subject unless both conjuncts occupy the subject position.

Test items sometimes embed a focus particle inside the first conjunct: Not only the director but also the board members have approved. The proximity rule still applies; the verb agrees with the board members. Recognising that not only … but also is a coordinating correlative, not a quantifier, prevents the error of defaulting to plural agreement.

  • Treating a clausal subject as plural because it contains a plural noun inside the clause.
  • Letting a focus particle (only, even, just) dictate verb number instead of the true subject.
  • Applying singular agreement to a variety of + plural countable noun when the context clearly indicates a set of individuals.
  • Ignoring the of-noun in partitive expressions and matching the verb to the partitive head (the rest, the bulk).
  • Defaulting to plural verb with not only … but also regardless of which conjunct is nearer the verb.
  • Using singular verb with the majority of + plural noun when the sense is distributive rather than collective.
  • Overlooking that data, media, criteria are traditionally plural in formal registers and often require plural verbs after partitives.

Mastering these patterns ensures reliable concord decisions under examination conditions.

Existential There‑Constructions with Coordinated and Quantified Subjects

The dummy subject there never determines verb number; agreement is controlled by the logical subject that follows the verb. When the logical subject is a simple noun phrase, the rule is straightforward: There is a problem (singular) versus There are several problems (plural). Complexity arises when the logical subject is a coordination, a quantifier phrase, or a partitive construction.

With coordinated noun phrases joined by and, standard grammars treat the whole coordination as plural, so the verb is plural: There are a teacher and two students in the hall. Some prescriptive guides allow a singular verb when the first conjunct is singular and the speaker views the set as a single entity (There is a teacher and two students…), but many examinations expect the plural verb because the coordination denotes more than one entity. When the coordinator is or or nor, the proximity principle applies: the verb agrees with the nearest conjunct. Thus There is a pen or two pencils on the desk (singular pen nearest) but There are two pencils or a pen on the desk (plural pencils nearest).

Quantifier phrases such as a lot of, lots of, plenty of, a great deal of, and a large amount of behave like partitives: the verb matches the noun that follows of. There is a lot of water in the tank (mass noun → singular) versus There are a lot of books on the shelf (count plural → plural). The same principle governs a number of (plural) and the number of (singular), which are treated elsewhere.

Partitive expressions headed by the rest of, the remainder of, the bulk of, the majority of, and the minority of also follow the of-noun. There is the rest of the cake (singular mass) but There are the rest of the students (plural count). When the of-noun is a collective noun capable of singular or plural interpretation (committee, team), the surrounding context decides: There is the majority of the committee (unit reading) versus There are the majority of the committee (distributive reading).

Inverted existential sentences beginning with here or there obey the same agreement logic; the only difference is the fronted locative. Here comes the bus (singular) versus Here come the buses (plural). Test items often embed a long prepositional phrase between there and the logical subject to obscure the true head; the examinee must locate the head noun after the verb and apply the rules above.

Free Relative Clauses as Subjects: What, Whatever, Whoever

A free relative clause (also called a fused relative) functions as a nominal clause without an explicit antecedent. The most common introducers are what, whatever, whoever, whichever, and wherever. Because the clause itself occupies the subject position, verb agreement depends on whether the clause is interpreted as singular or plural.

When the free relative can be paraphrased with a singular head noun such as the thing that or the person who, the verb is singular. Example: What matters is the outcome (≈ The thing that matters is the outcome). Whatever you decide is fine (≈ The thing that you decide is fine). Whoever arrives first gets the prize (≈ The person who arrives first gets the prize).

When the clause denotes a plural set, the paraphrase uses a plural head: the things that, the people who. Then the verb is plural. Example: What matter are the details (≈ The things that matter are the details). Whatever the reasons are, they must be documented (≈ The reasons that exist are…). Whoever the candidates are, they will be interviewed (≈ The people who are the candidates…).

The decision hinges on the semantic number of the referent, not on the form of the introducer. What and whatever are neutral; whoever and whichever often imply a singular person or choice, but they can be plural when the context supplies a plural set (Whoever the winners are, they will be announced). Whichever follows the same pattern: Whichever books are needed will be ordered (plural) versus Whichever book is needed will be ordered (singular).

A common pattern places a singular verb after a free relative that is clearly plural because a plural noun appears later in the sentence. For instance, What are the criteria? is correct; What is the criteria? is wrong because criteria is plural (the singular is criterion). Similarly, Whatever the data show (plural data in formal usage) requires a plural verb. The examinee must identify the notional number of the free relative’s referent before selecting the verb form.

Worked Example
Choose the correct verb form: What ___ (is / are) the primary causes of the delay?
The free relative What refers to the primary causes, a plural noun phrase. The paraphrase The things that are the primary causes shows plural agreement. Therefore the correct verb is are: What are the primary causes of the delay?
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a free relative clause opens a sentence, instantly paraphrase it with the thing(s) that or the person(s) who. The number of the paraphrase head dictates the verb. Do not be misled by a singular introducer (what, whoever) or by a later singular noun; the referent’s notional number rules.

Inversion after Negative and Restrictive Adverbials

English requires subject‑auxiliary inversion when a clause begins with a negative or restrictive adverbial such as never, rarely, seldom, hardly, scarcely, barely, not only, not until, only then, only after, only when, only if, only by, and little. The inverted auxiliary agrees with the true subject that follows it; the main verb remains in its base or participle form as required by the auxiliary.

With simple past or present tense verbs that lack an auxiliary, the dummy auxiliary do/does/did is inserted. Never did she complain (subject she, singular → did). Rarely do they arrive on time (subject they, plural → do). When a modal auxiliary is present, it inverts directly: Seldom can we predict the outcome (subject we, plural → can). Hardly had he finished when the phone rang (subject he, singular → had).

The adverbial not only triggers inversion only when it fronts the clause and is followed by a verb phrase; the second conjunct introduced by but also does not invert. Not only did the manager approve the plan, but the board also endorsed it. The subject of the first clause is the manager (singular), so the auxiliary is did. The second clause retains normal order because its subject the board follows the conjunction.

Restrictive only phrases behave similarly: Only after the audit did the errors surface (subject the errors, plural → did). Only when the data are verified will the report be released (subject the data, plural in formal usage → are in the subordinate clause; the main clause inverts will with subject the report, singular). The key is to locate the subject of the inverted clause and match the auxiliary to its number and person.

Examination items often embed a long adverbial phrase before the inversion point to hide the subject. The test‑taker must strip away the fronted adverbial, identify the subject that follows the auxiliary, and apply standard agreement. Errors arise when candidates let the adverbial’s apparent singularity (e.g., only one reason) dictate the verb, ignoring the true plural subject.

Quantifiers of the “A Lot Of” Family: A Lot Of, Lots Of, Plenty Of, A Great Deal Of

The quantifiers a lot of, lots of, plenty of, a great deal of, a good deal of, a large amount of, and a large number of all function as partitive determiners. Their agreement rule is uniform: the verb agrees with the noun that immediately follows of. This noun is the head of the partitive phrase and determines number.

When the of-noun is a singular mass or abstract noun, the verb is singular. A lot of equipment is required. Plenty of time remains. A great deal of effort was wasted. A large amount of data is stored (formal data treated as plural, but many style guides accept singular here; the safer formal choice is are).

When the of-noun is a plural countable noun, the verb is plural. A lot of students are waiting. Lots of books have been donated. Plenty of reasons exist. A large number of errors were found. Note that a large number of always takes a plural verb because number in this construction is a quantifier, not the head noun.

The quantifier a great deal of is restricted to singular mass nouns; it sounds odd with plural countables (*a great deal of books). Conversely, a large number of is restricted to plural countables (*a large number of water). Plenty of and lots of are neutral and appear with both mass and count nouns.

In existential there constructions the same rule applies: There is a lot of noise versus There are a lot of people. Test designers frequently pair a singular quantifier form (a lot of) with a plural of-noun to lure candidates into singular agreement. The correct response is always to look at the of-noun.

Post‑Subject Each and Every: Agreement When the Distributive Follows a Plural Noun

The distributives each and every normally precede a singular noun and enforce singular agreement: Each student has a locker. When they appear after a plural noun phrase, they function as appositive distributives and do not alter the number of the main subject. The verb agrees with the plural antecedent.

Example: The participants each received a certificate. The subject is The participants (plural); each merely distributes the action. The verb received is plural (past tense shows no number, but in present tense The participants each receive a certificate). Similarly, The machines each require regular maintenance (plural require).

Every cannot follow a plural noun in standard English; *The students every have a book is ungrammatical. The correct form is Every student has a book or All the students have a book. This restriction is a reliable diagnostic: if every appears post‑nominally, the sentence is flawed.

When each of or every one of introduces a partitive phrase, the head is each or one, both singular, so the verb is singular: Each of the candidates is qualified. Every one of the proposals has merit. This contrasts with the post‑subject use where the plural noun remains the syntactic subject.

Examination items may present a sentence such as The reports each contain an executive summary and ask whether contain or contains is correct. The plural subject The reports controls the verb, so contain is correct. Candidates who mistakenly treat each as the subject will select the singular contains and lose marks.

  • Treating the dummy subject there as the agreement controller in existential sentences.
  • Applying singular agreement to a free relative clause because the introducer what or whoever looks singular.
  • Letting a fronted negative adverbial dictate verb number instead of the true subject after inversion.
  • Matching the verb to the quantifier head (a lot of, plenty of) rather than the of-noun.
  • Assuming each or every forces singular agreement even when they follow a plural noun.
  • Using singular verb with a large number of + plural noun because number appears singular.
  • Overlooking that every cannot appear post‑nominally and marking a sentence with post‑nominal every as correct.

Mastery of these advanced concord patterns enables precise verb selection under timed examination conditions.
