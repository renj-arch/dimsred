Articles, Prepositions & Conjunctions

Updated 28 Nov 2026
A/an/the, fixed prepositions, prepositional phrases and conjunction pairs tested in error spotting.
Articles · Prepositions · Conjunctions · Correlatives

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the fundamental concepts of Articles, Prepositions & Conjunctions
  • Apply key formulas and techniques to solve problems
  • Practice with exam-level questions to build speed and accuracy

Articles (a, an, the)

  • Indefinite article 'a/an': Used before singular countable nouns when mentioning something for the first time or when the noun is not specific. 'a' before consonant sound (a boy, a university — 'u' sounds like 'yu'), 'an' before vowel sound (an apple, an hour — 'h' silent, vowel sound). Choice depends on sound, not spelling letter.
  • Definite article 'the': Used before a particular person/thing already mentioned or known (the book I bought), unique things (the sun, the moon, the earth), superlatives (the best, the tallest), musical instruments (the piano), rivers/seas/ranges (the Ganga, the Indian Ocean, the Himalayas), newspapers (the Hindu), holy books (the Gita), and nationalities (the Indians).
  • Omission of articles: No article before proper nouns (Delhi, John), abstract nouns used generally (Honesty is best policy), material nouns (Gold is precious), languages (Hindi is easy), meals (breakfast, lunch, dinner), bed/church/college/hospital/prison/school when used for primary purpose (go to school = study; go to the school = visit building), plural countable nouns used generally (Cars are expensive).
  • 'A/an' vs 'The': 'A man and a woman were walking. The man was tall.' (first mention = a/an, second = the).

Prepositions

  • Time: at (exact point — at 5 o'clock, at noon, at night), in (period — in May, in 2024, in the morning), on (day/date — on Monday, on 15th August, on Christmas day). 'In' for longer periods, 'on' for specific days, 'at' for exact points.
  • Place: at (point — at the door, at the station), in (enclosed space — in the room, in Delhi, in the box), on (surface — on the table, on the wall, on the floor).
  • Movement/direction: to (towards — go to school), into (enter — go into the room), onto (on top — jump onto the bed), towards (direction — walk towards the market), through (pass from one side to other — walk through the tunnel), across (from one side to other — swim across the river), along (follow length — walk along the road), against (touching and opposing — lean against the wall), among (more than two — divide among three), between (two — divide between Ram and Shyam).
  • Instrument/agent: by (agent — killed by a thief; mode — travel by bus), with (instrument — cut with a knife; accompaniment — go with a friend), in (manner — write in ink), on (basis — depend on).
  • Other common prepositions: about (concerning — talk about politics), above (higher than — above the roof), below (lower than — below the poverty line), over (covering — a roof over the head), under (below — under the table), before (earlier than — before 5 pm), after (later than — after lunch), since (point in past — since Monday), for (duration — for 3 hours), from (starting point — from Monday to Friday), till/until (up to — wait till 6), during (within a period — during the holidays), within (before end — within a week), without (lacking — without water), unlike (different from — unlike his brother), like (similar to — like his father), beside (next to — sit beside me), besides (in addition to — besides English, she knows French), except (excluding — all except John), including (including — including tax).

Important Preposition Pairs/Rules

  • In vs On vs At (place): at (point) < in (enclosed) — I live at No. 5, in Delhi, in India.
  • In vs On vs At (time): at (exact) < on (day) < in (month/year) — at 5 pm, on Monday, in January.
  • Between vs Among: between = two (between you and me), among = more than two (distribute among the poor).
  • Since vs For: since = point (since 2010), for = duration (for 10 years). Both used with perfect tenses.
  • By vs With: by = agent/doer (killed by a thief), with = instrument (cut with a knife).
  • Beside vs Besides: beside = next to (sit beside me), besides = in addition to (besides English, she knows Hindi).
  • From vs Since: from = starting point (works from 9 to 5), since = point in past continued to present (works here since 2010 — with perfect tense).
  • Into vs In: into = movement entering (jump into the well), in = position (swim in the pool).
  • On vs Upon: upon is more formal/literary (once upon a time; upon reflection).
  • Above vs Over: above = higher than, no contact (flags above the building); over = covering/moving across (a roof over us, flew over the city).
  • Below vs Under: below = lower than, no contact (below the poverty line); under = directly below/contact (under the table).
  • Between vs From...to: between two points (between Delhi and Mumbai), from...to (from Monday to Friday).

Conjunctions

  • Coordinating conjunctions: join equal elements — and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet (FANBOYS).
  • Subordinating conjunctions: join dependent clause — because, although, though, since, if, unless, until, while, when, before, after, as, as if, as though, lest, so that.
  • Correlative conjunctions (pairs): either...or, neither...nor, both...and, not only...but also, whether...or, scarcely...when, no sooner...than, rather...than.
  • Important rules:
    • No sooner...than (No sooner did he arrive than it started raining — inversion).
    • Scarcely/Hardly...when (Scarcely had he reached when it rained — inversion, past perfect).
    • Lest = that...not, followed by should (Walk carefully lest you should fall).
    • Unless = if not (Unless you work hard, you will fail — no 'not' with unless).
    • Both...and takes plural verb (Both Ram and Shyam are present).
    • Not only...but also — verb agrees with nearer subject (Not only the teacher but also the students were present).
Technique: Article choice
Question: "___ honest man is respected by all." Choose article.
Answer: 'honest' starts with silent 'h' but vowel sound /ɒn/ → use 'an'. "An honest man is respected by all."
Technique: Since vs For
Question: "She has been living here ___ 2015." Choose preposition.
Answer: 2015 is a point in time → 'since'. "She has been living here since 2015."

Common Traps

  • Using 'a/an' before uncountable nouns (a milk, a furniture — wrong; milk is uncountable).
  • Using 'the' before proper nouns (the Delhi — wrong; Delhi is correct).
  • Confusing 'in' and 'at' for time/place.
  • Using 'between' for more than two people/things.
  • Using 'unless' with 'not' (unless you don't work — wrong; unless you work — correct).
  • Using 'the' before names of meals used generally (I take the breakfast — wrong; I take breakfast — correct).

Memory Tricks

  • Articles: a/an = first mention, general; the = specific, unique, second mention.
  • Sound rule: 'a university' (consonant sound), 'an hour' (vowel sound) — depends on sound, not letter.
  • Preposition place/time: at → on → in (smallest → largest unit).
  • Between vs Among: Between = 2; Among = 3+.
Articles/prepositions exam strategy
In error spotting, check articles before countable nouns, preposition choice (since/for, in/on/at), and 'unless' without 'not'. Read the sentence for meaning — wrong prepositions often change meaning entirely.

Zero Article and Null Determiner Contexts

The zero article — the absence of any determiner before a noun — operates in a tightly constrained set of syntactic environments. Plural count nouns used generically (Dogs bark), uncountable nouns denoting substance or abstract quality (Water boils at 100 °C), proper nouns naming unique entities (Paris is beautiful), names of meals when referring to the routine event (We have breakfast at eight), names of languages (She speaks French), and institutional nouns such as school, college, hospital, prison, church when the focus is on the activity rather than the building (He goes to school) all trigger the zero article. The same nouns admit the when the speaker points to a specific instance (The dogs next door bark, The water in this bottle is cold, The Paris of my childhood, The breakfast we had yesterday, The French spoken in Quebec, The school on the hill). A further layer appears with nouns that alternate between count and mass senses: experience (uncountable) versus an experience (countable event), knowledge (mass) versus a knowledge of Sanskrit (specific body). Examiners test this by embedding a noun in a prepositional phrase that forces a generic reading (by car, on foot, at night) and then offering a distractor with a or the. The rule of thumb: if the noun denotes a class, substance, or routine activity without individuation, the zero article is mandatory; any determiner signals a shift to specificity or countability.

Prepositional Verbs and Phrasal Verbs: Particle vs Preposition

Distinguishing prepositional verbs from phrasal verbs is a recurring error‑spotting target because both surface as verb + small word, yet their syntactic behaviour diverges. A prepositional verb consists of a lexical verb and a preposition that licenses a noun phrase complement (depend on the weather, look at the map, refer to the manual). The preposition cannot move away from its object; adverbs cannot intervene (*depend carefully on the weather is marginal). In contrast, a phrasal verb pairs a verb with a particle that behaves like an adverb: the particle can follow a noun object (turn the light off) and must follow a pronoun object (turn it off, not *turn off it). The particle also admits stress and can be fronted in clefts (Off went the light). Diagnostic tests: (1) Movement — if the small word can appear after a full NP, it is a particle; (2) Pronoun placement — pronoun must precede particle; (3) Adverb insertion — adverbs may separate verb and particle (turn quickly off) but not verb and preposition (*depend quickly on). Common prepositional verbs tested: account for, adhere to, approve of, consist of, rely on. Frequent phrasal verbs: give up, put off, run into, set up, take after. Examiners often present a sentence where a prepositional verb is incorrectly treated as phrasal (He looked the document up — wrong; correct He looked up the document) or vice‑versa (She depends her brother on — wrong). Mastery of the three tests eliminates the majority of such traps.

Technique: Particle Identification
Identify the particle in the following sentence and rewrite the sentence placing a pronoun object correctly: "The manager turned down the proposal."
The particle is down. With a pronoun object the particle must follow the pronoun: "The manager turned it down." The prepositional‑verb test fails because down cannot precede a noun phrase (*turned down the proposal is acceptable only as phrasal). The movement test confirms particle status: The manager turned the proposal down is grammatical.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a verb + small word appears, first try substituting a pronoun for the object. If the pronoun must precede the small word, you have a phrasal verb; if the small word stays before the pronoun, it is a prepositional verb. Apply the three‑second movement test mentally before marking an answer.

Correlative Conjunctions in Complex Sentence Architecture

Correlative conjunctions impose strict parallelism across coordinated constituents. The pairs either…or, neither…nor, both…and, not only…but also, whether…or demand that the grammatical category following the first element matches the category following the second. Violations appear as mismatched parts of speech (Either you must study hard or playing games — incorrect; correct Either you must study hard or you must play games). Subject‑verb agreement follows the proximity rule for neither…nor and either…or: the verb agrees with the nearer subject (Neither the teacher nor the students are ready). With both…and the verb is plural because the conjunction creates a plural subject (Both the manager and the clerk are present). Not only…but also triggers inversion when the clause begins with Not only (Not only did he arrive late, but he also forgot the files). The pair scarcely…when and no sooner…than require past perfect in the first clause and inversion (Scarcely had the train left when the storm broke). Lest introduces a subjunctive‑like clause with should (Walk carefully lest you should slip). Examiners embed these structures in long sentences where a hidden agreement error or missing inversion lurks. The reliable checklist: (1) verify parallel form; (2) locate the nearest subject for agreement; (3) check for required inversion after negative fronting; (4) ensure lest is followed by should + bare infinitive.

Article Usage with Abstract Nouns and Generic Reference

Abstract nouns — beauty, honesty, information, progress — default to zero article when used generically (Honesty is the best policy). The definite article appears when the abstract noun is particularised by a following of-phrase, a relative clause, or contextual specificity (The honesty of the witness was questioned, The information you gave me is outdated). The indefinite article surfaces when the abstract noun is treated as a countable instance or a type (A deep sadness settled over the room, An unusual courage characterises her). Superlative and ordinal modifiers force the (The best advice, The first attempt). Certain fixed expressions retain the despite generic sense: the rich, the poor, the young, the old (adjectival nouns). Conversely, names of meals, diseases, and languages resist the unless specified (He has malaria vs The malaria he contracted was severe). Exam items often present a sentence where an abstract noun is preceded by a in a generic context (A knowledge of history is essential — wrong; correct Knowledge of history is essential) or where the is omitted before a particularised abstract noun (Honesty of the witness — wrong). The decision matrix: generic → zero; particularised → the; countable instance → a/an; superlative/ordinal → the.

Preposition Choice in Temporal and Spatial Metaphors

English extends concrete spatial prepositions into metaphorical domains, creating collocations that are opaque to learners. Temporal metaphors: in the long run, at the moment, on time, by then, during the process, within an hour. Spatial metaphors: under pressure, over budget, above suspicion, below standard, beside the point, beyond doubt. The governing principle is the underlying image schema: in for containment (periods, states), at for point‑like instants, on for surfaces or lines (deadlines, schedules), under for subjection, over for exceeding a limit, above for superiority without contact, below for inferiority, beside for irrelevance, beyond for transcendence. Errors arise when a learner selects the literal preposition instead of the metaphorical one (He is in pressure instead of under pressure; The project is at budget instead of over budget). A second trap is mixing metaphors: above the limit (acceptable) versus over the limit (also acceptable but with nuance of excess). Examiners test these by offering a sentence with a blank and four preposition options, only one of which matches the conventional metaphor. Mastery requires memorising the high‑frequency metaphorical collocations and recognising the image schema that licenses each preposition.

  • Using a zero article with a singular count noun in a generic statement (e.g., Tiger is endangered instead of The tiger is endangered or Tigers are endangered).
  • Treating a prepositional verb as a phrasal verb and moving the particle after a pronoun (e.g., She depended it on).
  • Failing to maintain parallel structure with correlative pairs (e.g., Not only he sings but also dances).
  • Omitting the definite article before a particularised abstract noun (e.g., Beauty of the sunset instead of The beauty of the sunset).
  • Selecting a literal preposition in a metaphorical collocation (e.g., in pressure for under pressure).
  • Neglecting inversion after fronted negative correlatives (e.g., No sooner he arrived than it rained).
  • Using lest without should or with a negative (e.g., Lest you fall instead of Lest you should fall).

Article Interaction with Quantifiers and Partitives

Quantifiers such as all, both, each, every, either, neither, many, few, several, a number of, a lot of impose distinct article requirements on the nouns they modify. When a quantifier precedes a plural count noun, the article is normally omitted: All students passed, Both options are viable. With singular count nouns, each and every take a zero article (Each candidate submitted a form), whereas either and neither require the singular noun without an article (Either answer is acceptable). The partitive construction of the forces the definite article: Many of the participants, Few of the books. However, a number of and a lot of behave as plural quantifiers and combine with zero article plural nouns (A number of errors were found). The quantifier some admits both article‑less plural (Some apples) and singular with a when indicating an unspecified singular entity (Some person called). Examiners frequently test the contrast between All the team (definite, specific group) and All team members (generic plural). A reliable decision matrix: (1) identify quantifier type; (2) check noun countability; (3) determine whether the noun phrase is specific (definite article) or generic (zero article).

Preposition Selection in Idiomatic Collocations and Register Variation

English prepositions often lose their spatial sense when they enter fixed collocations, and the register (formal, academic, journalistic, colloquial) determines which preposition is licensed. In formal writing, in respect of, with regard to, and by virtue of are preferred over their colloquial counterparts about, concerning, and because of. The preposition on appears in on condition that, on the grounds that, and on account of, whereas upon is restricted to elevated style (upon receipt, upon completion). The pair in/on with temporal nouns follows a register split: in the morning (neutral), on the morning of the exam (specific event). With abstract nouns, under signals subjection (under consideration, under review), while over signals authority or excess (over budget, over the limit). The preposition at is fixed in at risk, at stake, at variance; substituting in or on produces an error. A second dimension is the verb‑preposition complementation pattern: consist of, depend on, object to, subscribe to. Learners often replace the required preposition with a semantically similar one (depend *in*), which is a high‑frequency trap. Mastery requires memorising the top 150 verb‑preposition and adjective‑preposition collocations and recognising the register cue (e.g., in accordance with vs. according to).

Error‑Spotting Worked Example
Identify the error: The committee decided on the proposal in respect to the new guidelines.
The phrase in respect to is non‑standard; the correct collocation in formal register is in respect of or with respect to. The sentence should read: The committee decided on the proposal in respect of the new guidelines. The error is a preposition‑choice mistake driven by register mismatch.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a blank tests a preposition in a formal‑sounding sentence, first check whether the surrounding vocabulary signals elevated register (e.g., committee, guidelines, accordance, virtue). If so, select the preposition that belongs to the formal collocation set (in respect of, by virtue of, on condition that) rather than the everyday alternative.

Conjunction Coordination Across Clause Boundaries: Ellipsis and Gapping

Coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet) can link not only full clauses but also reduced structures where material is omitted under identity. Gapping deletes the verb in the second conjunct when the verb is identical: She likes tea, and he coffee. Stripping retains only a single constituent (often a subject or object) with a conjunction: Only John passed; Mary, too. Ellipsis in not only … but also constructions permits omission of the auxiliary in the second clause when the first clause contains an inverted auxiliary: Not only did she arrive early, but she also prepared the agenda (no ellipsis) versus Not only did she arrive early, but also prepared the agenda (elliptical, acceptable in informal registers). With either … or and neither … nor, the verb may be omitted in the second conjunct if the modal or auxiliary is shared: You can either stay or leave (full) vs. You can either stay or leave (no ellipsis needed). However, examiners embed a hidden agreement error when the coordinated subjects differ in number: Neither the manager nor the clerks is available (incorrect; proximity rule demands are). The conjunction for (meaning because) coordinates independent clauses but never triggers inversion; it is often misidentified as a preposition. A checklist for coordination items: (1) verify parallel syntactic category; (2) apply proximity agreement for either/or, neither/nor; (3) confirm that any fronted negative element (not only, scarcely, no sooner) triggers subject‑auxiliary inversion in the first clause; (4) ensure that ellipsis does not create ambiguity (e.g., She likes him more than me vs. She likes him more than I).

Zero Article in Institutional and Title Contexts

Proper nouns that denote institutions, offices, or titles frequently appear without an article when used in a generic or role‑denoting sense. Parliament, Congress, Cabinet, Court, University, Hospital, School take zero article when referring to the institution as a concept: Parliament debated the bill. The definite article appears when the noun is particularised by a post‑modifier: The Parliament of 1998, The University where she teaches. Titles such as President, Prime Minister, Director, Professor drop the article when used as a vocative or role label: President signed the order, Professor Smith lectured. However, when the title functions as a common noun describing a person, the article returns: A president must be elected, The director of the department. Fixed expressions like at school, in hospital, at university (British usage) retain zero article, whereas American English prefers in the hospital, at the university. Examiners test this by presenting a sentence such as He was appointed Director of Finance (correct) versus He was appointed the Director of Finance (incorrect in role‑denoting context). The decision rule: if the noun denotes a unique office held by one person at a time and is used as a title, omit the article; if the noun is modified by a restrictive relative clause or an of-phrase that identifies a specific instance, insert the.

Prepositional Complementation of Adjectives and Nouns: Licensing Patterns

Adjectives and nouns license specific prepositions, and the choice is not predictable from semantics alone. Common adjective‑preposition pairs include aware of, capable of, dependent on, indifferent to, prone to, responsible for, similar to, superior to. Noun‑preposition pairs include advantage of, access to, attitude towards, cause of, effect on, exception to, increase in, reason for. Errors arise when learners substitute a semantically related preposition: interested *in* (correct) vs. interested *on*; good *at* (correct) vs. good *in*. Some adjectives accept two prepositions with a meaning shift: angry with (person) vs. angry about (situation); different from (standard) vs. different to (British informal) vs. different than (American informal). Nouns derived from verbs often retain the verb’s preposition: reliance on, insistence on, objection to. A high‑frequency trap is the noun preference, which licenses for (a preference for tea) not to. Another is solution, which takes to (solution to the problem) not for. Examiners embed these in long noun phrases: His insistence on the proposal was noted (correct) vs. His insistence *to* the proposal (incorrect). Mastery requires a memorised list of the 80 most tested adjective‑preposition and noun‑preposition collocations and the ability to spot the head adjective or noun that governs the preposition.

  • Using in respect to instead of in respect of or with respect to in formal register.
  • Omitting the required article after a partitive quantifier (e.g., Many of participants instead of Many of the participants).
  • Applying proximity agreement incorrectly with either … or when the nearer subject is plural (e.g., Either the manager or the clerks is responsible).
  • Failing to trigger inversion after fronted Not only (e.g., Not only she arrived late but also forgot the files).
  • Inserting an article before a role‑denoting title (e.g., The President signed when used as a title).
  • Choosing a semantically similar but unlicensed preposition with adjectives (e.g., interested on, good in).
  • Using preference to or solution for instead of the licensed preference for and solution to.

These advanced patterns complete the core toolkit for high‑stakes error‑spotting and sentence‑completion items.

Article Distribution in Appositive and Parenthetical Constructions

When a noun phrase is followed by an appositive or a parenthetical insertion, the article that precedes the head noun often governs the entire construction, but the presence of a second determiner inside the appositive can create a conflict that examiners exploit. In a restrictive appositive, the head noun retains its original article: The poet Keats (definite) versus A poet, Keats, (indefinite). In a non‑restrictive appositive set off by commas, the article of the head noun is usually preserved, while the appositive itself appears without an article if it is a proper name: My brother, a doctor, (indefinite article inside the appositive) but My brother, Dr. Singh, (zero article before the title). When the appositive contains a common noun modified by a determiner, the determiner must agree in definiteness with the head: The city, the capital of France, is correct; The city, a capital of France, signals a different information structure and is marked as non‑restrictive. Parenthetical clauses introduced by that is, i.e., or namely follow the same rule: the article of the antecedent noun phrase remains unchanged, and the parenthetical does not introduce a new determiner unless it is a full noun phrase with its own article. Errors arise when a test item inserts an unnecessary definite article before a proper‑name appositive (The Shakespeare, the playwright,) or omits the required article before a common‑noun appositive that is restrictive (President, the leader of the nation, should be The president, the leader of the nation, when the title is used as a common noun). The decision procedure: identify the head noun, determine whether the appositive is restrictive or non‑restrictive, then copy the head’s article to the whole construction unless the appositive itself is a full noun phrase requiring its own determiner.

Preposition Choice in Complex Nominalizations and Derivational Morphology

Nominalizations derived from verbs, adjectives, or other nouns often retain the prepositional complement of their source, but the mapping is not uniform. Verbs that select on (e.g., insist on) yield nouns that license on (insistence on), whereas verbs that select for (e.g., apply for) produce nouns that take for (application for). However, a sizable class of nominalizations shifts the preposition: rely on → reliance on (no shift), but depend on → dependence on (same), while object to → objection to (same). The shift appears with prefer → preference for (not to), solve → solution to (not for), and refer → reference to (not for). Adjectival nominalizations such as aware → awareness of, capable → capability of, and prone → propensity to illustrate that the preposition may change from of to to or vice‑versa. Derivational suffixes -tion, -ment, -ance, -ency do not predict the preposition; the lexical entry of the base verb or adjective determines it. Examiners frequently embed a long noun phrase with a mismatched preposition: His insistence to the proposal (incorrect) versus His insistence on the proposal (correct). A reliable strategy is to isolate the head noun, recall the base verb or adjective, and retrieve the licensed preposition from a memorised collocation list of the 80 most tested items. When the nominalization is preceded by a quantifier (many of the objections to), the article after the partitive must be definite (many of the objections), and the preposition remains governed by the head noun, not by the quantifier.

Error‑Spotting Technique
Identify the error in the following sentence: The committee's preference to the new policy was noted by the board.
The noun preference licenses the preposition for, not to. The correct form is The committee's preference for the new policy was noted by the board. The error is a preposition‑selection mistake driven by the nominalization of prefer (which takes to as a verb) but shifting to for in the noun form.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a sentence contains a long noun phrase headed by a nominalization, first locate the head noun, then mentally revert to its verb or adjective base. Ask which preposition the base selects; if the base selects a different preposition, the noun will usually follow the noun‑specific collocation (e.g., prefer → preference for). Mark the preposition and verify against a mental checklist of the top 80 noun‑preposition pairs.

Conjunction Reduction in Non‑Finite and Small Clause Environments

Conjunction reduction (also called coordination ellipsis) permits the omission of repeated material in coordinated clauses, but the licensing conditions differ for finite, non‑finite, and small‑clause conjuncts. In finite coordination, the shared element must be syntactically identical: She will write the report and will submit it → She will write the report and submit it. With non‑finite conjuncts, the auxiliary or infinitive marker can be omitted only when the subject is identical and the verb forms are parallel: He wants to sing and to dance → He wants to sing and dance. If the first conjunct is a bare infinitive after a modal, the second must also be bare: They can swim and can dive → They can swim and dive. Small clauses (subject + predicate without an overt verb) allow reduction of the predicate when the subject is shared: I consider him intelligent and consider him honest → I consider him intelligent and honest. However, reduction is blocked when the conjuncts differ in case assignment or when a complementizer is required: She believes that he is honest and that he is kind cannot drop the second that in formal writing because the complementizer marks the subordinate clause boundary. Examiners test this by inserting an illicit reduction: The manager approved the plan and approved the budget (acceptable) versus The manager approved the plan and the budget (ambiguous: could be read as a single NP coordination). The safe rule: reduction is licensed only when the ellipted material is recoverable from the first conjunct without altering the grammatical function of the remaining elements. Parallelism of syntactic category (VP with VP, NP with NP, AdjP with AdjP) is mandatory; mismatched categories produce ungrammatical or ambiguous structures.

Determiner‑Preposition Compatibility in Quantified Partitive Constructions

Partitive constructions such as some of the students, none of my friends, each of the candidates impose strict determiner‑preposition compatibility rules. The preposition of is obligatory after the quantifier, and the noun phrase that follows must be definite (i.e., preceded by the, a possessive, a demonstrative, or a proper noun in the genitive). Many of participants is ungrammatical; the correct form is Many of the participants. Quantifiers that are inherently definite (all, both, each, every) can appear without of when they directly modify a plural noun (All students passed), but when of is inserted the following NP must be definite: All of the students. The quantifier none patterns with singular or plural agreement depending on the noun after of: None of the water is (singular) versus None of the books are (plural). A frequent trap is the use of a lot of with a bare plural (A lot of people) which is acceptable in informal registers but considered non‑standard in formal error‑spotting items; the prescriptive form prefers Many people or A large number of people. When a partitive is embedded inside a larger NP, the article of the embedded NP does not project outward: The majority of the votes takes a singular verb if majority is the head, but the of-phrase remains definite. Examiners also test the interaction with few and a few: Few of the applicants (negative polarity) versus A few of the applicants (positive). The rule set: (1) quantifier + of → definite NP; (2) agreement follows the head quantifier for none, all, most but follows the embedded noun for a number of, a variety of; (3) zero article after of is never licensed.

Definiteness Projection Across Relative Clause Boundaries

The definiteness of a noun phrase can be influenced by a following relative clause, especially when the relative clause contains a superlative, a unique identifier, or a restrictive modifier that singles out a single referent. In The book that I bought yesterday, the definite article the is licensed by the restrictive relative clause, which narrows the set to one specific book. If the relative clause is non‑restrictive, the antecedent must already be definite: My brother, who lives in Delhi, (antecedent my brother is definite via the possessive). When the antecedent is an indefinite NP, a restrictive relative clause can make it definite in subsequent discourse: A man who knows the answer → later The man who knows the answer. However, the article inside the relative clause does not change the article of the antecedent; the antecedent’s article is determined at the point of first mention. Errors arise when a test item forces a mismatch: An only child who is talented (incorrect because only forces uniqueness, requiring The only child). Similarly, superlatives inside a relative clause demand a definite antecedent: The student who scored the highest (correct) versus A student who scored the highest (incorrect unless the context explicitly allows multiple highest scorers). The projection rule: if the relative clause contains a uniqueness‑implying expression (only, sole, unique, the first, the last, the best), the head NP must be definite. If the relative clause is merely descriptive, the head NP may remain indefinite. This interaction is a high‑frequency error‑spotting target because it combines article choice with relative‑clause syntax.

  • Using an indefinite article before a head noun modified by a restrictive relative clause that contains a uniqueness marker (e.g., An only solution).
  • Omitting the required definite article after a partitive quantifier (e.g., Many of participants instead of Many of the participants).
  • Applying conjunction reduction across non‑parallel categories (e.g., reducing a finite clause to a non‑finite VP).
  • Selecting the verb’s preposition for a nominalization instead of the noun‑specific collocation (e.g., preference to for preference for).
  • Stranding a preposition in a formal register where pied‑piping is required (e.g., The report which I referred to in a formal essay).
  • Failing to trigger subject‑auxiliary inversion after a fronted negative correlative (e.g., Not only he arrived late but also forgot the files).
  • Treating a non‑restrictive appositive as restrictive and dropping the article (e.g., My brother, doctor, instead of My brother, a doctor,).

These advanced patterns complete the toolkit for high‑stakes error‑spotting and sentence‑completion items.
