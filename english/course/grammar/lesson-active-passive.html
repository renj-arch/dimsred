1. Foundations of Voice in English Grammar

1.1 The Concept of Voice and Transitivity

Voice in English grammar describes the relationship between the action expressed by the verb and the participants identified by the subject and object. When the subject of a sentence performs the action denoted by the verb, the construction is classified as active voice; the focus remains squarely on the doer. Conversely, when the subject receives the action or is the entity upon which the action is performed, the construction shifts to passive voice, thereby foregrounding the recipient or the action itself rather than the agent. This distinction is not merely stylistic; it governs information flow, allowing a writer to manipulate thematic prominence. The feasibility of passive transformation depends entirely on transitivity. Only transitive verbs—verbs that accept a direct object—can undergo this structural inversion because the passive mechanism requires the active object to assume the subject position. Intransitive verbs, which lack a direct object, possess no grammatical patient to promote, rendering passive formation impossible. Understanding this prerequisite prevents the common error of attempting to passivize sentences like "The baby sleeps" or "The train arrived late," where no object exists to become the subject.

1.2 Structural Mechanics of Active to Passive Conversion

The mechanical process of converting an active sentence into its passive counterpart follows a rigid algorithmic sequence that ensures grammatical fidelity. First, the direct object of the active sentence is identified and moved to the subject position of the new sentence. Second, the main verb is replaced by the appropriate form of the auxiliary verb be followed by the past participle (V3) of the main verb. The tense of the auxiliary be must correspond exactly to the tense of the original active verb; this tense preservation is the anchor that maintains the temporal reference of the utterance. Third, the original active subject is either demoted to a prepositional phrase introduced by by (the agent phrase) or omitted entirely if the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately concealed. For instance, in the active clause "The architect designed the bridge," the object "the bridge" becomes the passive subject, the verb "designed" expands to "was designed" to match the past simple tense, and the agent "the architect" follows by. This three-step operation—object promotion, verb expansion, agent demotion—constitutes the invariant core of voice change across all tense categories.

1.3 The Role of the Agent and the 'By' Phrase

The by-phrase in a passive construction serves a specific discourse function: it reintroduces the agent when that information is necessary for completeness or clarity. However, the passive voice is frequently chosen precisely because the agent is unknown, generic, or obvious from context. In scientific writing, for example, "The solution was heated to 100°C" omits the experimenter because the identity of the heater is irrelevant to the reproducibility of the procedure. In news reporting, "The bank was robbed last night" may omit the robbers because they are unidentified. Retaining the by-phrase when the agent is a pronoun often sounds clumsy; "The cake was eaten by me" is dispreferred compared to the active "I ate the cake" unless contrastive focus is intended ("The cake was eaten by me, not by you"). Furthermore, certain verbs allow an instrumental with-phrase instead of an agentive by-phrase, as in "The safe was opened with a key," where the key is the instrument, not the volitional doer. Recognizing the optionality and the semantic nuance of the agent phrase is essential for producing natural-sounding passive sentences rather than mechanically correct but pragmatically odd ones.

2. Tense Mapping and Modal Auxiliaries in Passive Construction

2.1 Simple and Continuous Tense Transformations

The mapping of active tenses to their passive equivalents follows a predictable paradigm where the auxiliary be absorbs the tense and aspect markers of the original verb. In the present simple, "She writes letters" becomes "Letters are written by her," with are reflecting the plural subject and present tense. The present continuous shifts to "Letters are being written by her," where the progressive aspect is carried by being while are retains the present tense. The past simple "She wrote letters" yields "Letters were written by her," and the past continuous "She was writing letters" produces "Letters were being written by her." A critical constraint appears in the continuous forms: the sequence being + past participle is the only grammatical realization; forms such as "is been written" or "was been written" are categorically ungrammatical because the passive participle cannot follow the perfective auxiliary been in a progressive construction. This restriction often traps candidates who confuse the perfect passive ("has been written") with the continuous passive ("is being written"). Mastery of the be + being + V3 and be + been + V3 distinction is therefore a non-negotiable requirement for accurate voice conversion.

2.2 Perfect Tense and Future Time Reference

Perfect tenses introduce the auxiliary have into the passive chain, resulting in the structure have/has/had + been + V3. The present perfect active "She has written the report" transforms into "The report has been written by her," where has agrees with the singular passive subject and been marks the perfect aspect. The past perfect "She had written the report" similarly becomes "The report had been written by her." Future time reference in English is expressed primarily through modal auxiliaries rather than a dedicated future tense inflection. The modal will combines with the bare infinitive be to form the future simple passive: "She will write the report" → "The report will be written by her." The future perfect passive follows the pattern will have been + V3, as in "The report will have been written by tomorrow." Notably, the future continuous passive ("The report will be being written") is theoretically possible but stylistically disfavoured due to its cumbersome be being sequence; examiners rarely require its production, though recognition may be tested. The conditional forms would be + V3 and would have been + V3 operate identically, preserving the modal + be + V3 or modal + have been + V3 architecture.

2.3 Modal Auxiliaries and Semi-Modals in Passive Voice

Modal auxiliaries—can, could, may, might, must, should, ought to, have to, need to—form the passive by inserting the bare infinitive be between the modal and the past participle. "The committee can approve the proposal" becomes "The proposal can be approved by the committee." The semi-modal ought to retains its to particle: "You ought to respect elders" → "Elders ought to be respected." The semi-modal have to and need to conjugate for tense and person in the active voice ("She has to finish it"), and this conjugation transfers to the passive ("It has to be finished by her"). A subtle but examinable point concerns the modal need used as a main verb in the negative ("You need not worry"), which forms the passive as "It need not be worried about," retaining the preposition about because worry is prepositional in this sense. Similarly, used to forms a passive with be: "They used to build ships here" → "Ships used to be built here." These semi-modal structures are frequent targets in competitive examinations because they test the candidate's ability to maintain the integrity of the modal complex while executing the object-promotion rule.

Worked Example: Complex Passive Conversion with Modal Perfect
Convert the following active sentence into passive voice: "The authorities must have investigated the allegations thoroughly."
Step 1: Identify the object ("the allegations") and promote it to subject position. Step 2: Identify the verb complex ("must have investigated"). This is a modal perfect structure: modal (must) + perfect infinitive (have investigated). Step 3: Apply the passive rule for modal perfect: modal + have been + past participle. "Must" remains unchanged; "have investigated" becomes "have been investigated". Step 4: Place the agent ("by the authorities") at the end. Result: "The allegations must have been investigated thoroughly by the authorities." Note that the adverb "thoroughly" retains its end position modifying the passive participle.
Examination Insight: The 'Being Been' Trap
In multiple-choice voice questions, distractors frequently swap the continuous passive auxiliary 'being' with the perfect passive auxiliary 'been'. Remember: continuous tenses (present/past continuous) always take 'being' (is being written, was being written). Perfect tenses (present/past perfect, modal perfects) always take 'been' (has been written, had been written, must have been written). A form like 'has being written' or 'is been written' is grammatically impossible in standard English. Scan the options for this specific error first; it eliminates two to three distractors instantly.

3. Complex Structures and Exceptional Cases in Voice

3.1 Ditransitive Verbs and Double Passive Possibilities

Verbs that take two objects—a direct object and an indirect object—are classified as ditransitive. Common examples include give, tell, offer, promise, lend, send, show, teach, and award. In the active voice, the indirect object (usually a person) precedes the direct object (usually a thing): "The manager offered the candidate a job." Because there are two objects, two distinct passive transformations are grammatically permissible. The first promotes the direct object: "A job was offered to the candidate by the manager." Here, the indirect object becomes a prepositional phrase with to (or for with verbs like buy, fetch, make). The second promotes the indirect object: "The candidate was offered a job by the manager." This second type, often called the "personal passive," is generally preferred in English when the indirect object is a person because it places the human participant in the prominent subject position. Both forms are standard, but examination rubrics often require the candidate to produce the specific variant requested or to identify the correct passive form among options where only one object has been correctly promoted. Errors arise when the preposition to is dropped in the first variant ("A job was offered the candidate") or when the direct object is retained incorrectly in the second variant ("The candidate was offered a job by the manager" is correct; "The candidate was offered by the manager a job" is not).

3.2 Verbs Resisting Passivization: Intransitives and Statives

Not all transitive verbs yield acceptable passive sentences, and no intransitive verb can be passivized. Intransitive verbs such as sleep, die, arrive, happen, occur, exist, emerge, vanish, and laugh lack a direct object; therefore, no noun phrase is available for subject promotion. Attempts to force passives like "The meeting was happened" or "The baby was slept" are ungrammatical. A second category comprises stative transitive verbs—verbs denoting states rather than actions—which resist passivization despite having an object. Verbs of possession (have, possess, own, lack), relation (resemble, fit, suit, become), and measurement (weigh, measure, cost) typically do not form natural passives. "He has a car" does not become "A car is had by him"; "She resembles her mother" does not become "Her mother is resembled by her." These verbs describe inherent properties or relationships rather than transferable actions, so the patient role required by the passive is semantically incompatible. However, when have functions dynamically (e.g., "have breakfast," "have a bath"), passivization becomes possible: "Breakfast was had by the tourists." Distinguishing stative from dynamic uses of polysemous verbs is a high-level discrimination skill tested in advanced grammar assessments.

3.3 Causative and Quasi-Passive Constructions

The causative structure have/get + object + past participle ("I had my car repaired") occupies a functional space adjacent to the passive. It implies that the subject arranges for someone else to perform the action. While not a true passive—because the subject remains the causer, not the patient—it is often taught alongside voice change because it shares the object + participle surface form. The quasi-passive, or "middle voice," uses active syntax to express passive meaning, typically with verbs like sell, read, wash, cut, handle and adverbs of ease or difficulty: "This book reads easily" (meaning "This book can be read easily"), "The fabric washes well." These constructions are intransitive on the surface but transitive in semantic valence. Examinations occasionally test the conversion of a quasi-passive sentence into a full passive ("This book can be read easily") or ask candidates to identify the voice of a sentence like "The car drives smoothly." Recognizing that the subject of a quasi-passive sentence is semantically the patient, not the agent, prevents misclassification of voice type.

4. Fundamentals of Narration: Direct and Indirect Speech

4.1 Reporting Verbs and the Architecture of Reported Speech

Narration change—converting direct speech to indirect speech and vice versa—restructures an utterance to embed it within a reporting clause. The reporting verb (e.g., say, tell, ask, reply, exclaim) establishes the deictic centre: the time, place, and person perspective from which the reported content is viewed. In direct speech, the original speaker's words are quoted verbatim, marked by quotation marks, a comma after the reporting clause, and a capital letter initiating the quote: He said, "I am tired." In indirect speech, the quotation marks are removed, the comma is typically replaced by the complementizer that (often optional in informal registers), and the pronoun, tense, and adverbial systems shift to align with the reporter's perspective: He said that he was tired. The choice of reporting verb dictates the syntactic frame of the reported clause. Say takes a that-clause without an indirect object (He said that...); tell requires an indirect object (He told me that...); ask and enquire embed interrogative clauses (He asked where I lived); order and request govern infinitival clauses (He ordered me to leave). Mastery of these subcategorization frames is prerequisite for accurate narration conversion because the reporting verb change often drives the entire structural reorganization.

4.2 Pronoun Shift and Person Agreement Rules

The transformation of pronouns from direct to indirect speech follows a systematic "person shift" rule anchored to the subject and object of the reporting verb. First-person pronouns in the direct speech (I, we, me, us, my, our, mine, ours) shift to match the person of the subject of the reporting verb. If the reporter says He said, "I am late", the first-person I becomes third-person he because the subject of said is he. If the reporter is the speaker (I said, "I am late"), the first person remains I. Second-person pronouns (you, your, yours) shift to match the person of the object of the reporting verb. In He said to me, "You are late", the you becomes I because the addressee (me) is first person. In He said to her, "You are late", you becomes she. Third-person pronouns (he, she, it, they, him, her, them) generally remain unchanged because their referents are external to the speech act participants. However, ambiguity resolution may require noun substitution: if He said, "He is late" refers to a third male, indirect speech might clarify He said that Rahul was late. Reflexive and possessive pronouns follow the same person-mapping logic: my → his, your → my/her, ourselves → themselves. Consistent application of this mapping prevents the common error of shifting only the subject pronoun while leaving possessives in the original person.

4.3 The Principle of Tense Backshift and Its Exceptions

Tense backshift is the systematic regression of verb tenses in indirect speech when the reporting verb is in a past tense (said, told, asked, replied). The mechanism operates on a timeline: present tenses move to past, past tenses move to past perfect, and future modals shift to their past forms (will → would, can → could, may → might, shall → should, must → had to). For example, "I write" becomes he wrote; "I am writing" becomes he was writing; "I have written" becomes he had written; "I wrote" becomes he had written; "I will write" becomes he would write. Two major exceptions suspend backshift. First, if the reported statement expresses a universal truth, a habitual fact, or a scientific law, the tense remains in the present: The teacher said that the earth revolves around the sun (not revolved). Second, if the reporting verb is in the present, present perfect, or future tense (says, has said, will say), no backshift occurs because the reporter's "now" coincides with the speaker's "now": He says that he is tired. A third, pragmatic exception applies when the reported situation is still true at the time of reporting and the speaker wishes to emphasize current validity: He said he loves her (implies he still does) versus He said he loved her (neutral or implies change). Recognizing these exception conditions is crucial; mechanical backshift without semantic evaluation produces unnatural or misleading indirect speech.

5. Adverbial Adjustments and Sentence Types in Indirect Speech

5.1 Time and Place Deictics in Reported Discourse

Deictic expressions—words whose reference depends on the spatio-temporal coordinates of the utterance—must be recalibrated when the reporting centre shifts from the original speaker to the reporter. This recalibration, often called "adverbial backshift," mirrors tense backshift. Proximal deictics (now, today, yesterday, tomorrow, here, this, these, ago) shift to distal forms (then, that day, the day before, the next day, there, that, those, before). For instance, "I will finish it today" reported later becomes He said he would finish it that day. The shift is mandatory when the reporting time differs from the speaking time. However, if the report is made on the same day and at the same location, proximal forms may be retained: He says, "I will finish it today" → He says he will finish it today. The demonstratives this and these function as determiners or pronouns; when they modify a noun (this book), the noun phrase often shifts to that book or the book. Place deixis here becomes there unless the reporter occupies the same location. Mastery of this system requires mental simulation of the two distinct speech events—the original and the report—and calculation of the vector between their centres.

5.2 Transformation of Interrogative Structures

Converting direct questions to indirect speech involves three simultaneous operations: reporting verb change, word order normalization, and complementizer insertion. The reporting verb said or said to is replaced by asked, enquired, wondered, wanted to know, or questioned. The interrogative word order (auxiliary before subject: "Where are you going?") is inverted to declarative order (subject before verb: where I was going). The question mark is discarded. For wh- questions, the wh- word (who, what, where, when, why, how) serves as the complementizer introducing the subordinate clause; no additional conjunction is used. For yes-no questions, the complementizer if or whether is inserted: "Are you ready?" → He asked if I was ready. Whether is preferred in formal contexts or when the alternative is explicit (He asked whether I was ready or not). A frequent error is retaining the auxiliary-do support or the inverted order: *He asked where was I going or *He asked did I go. The embedded clause must be a fully formed declarative statement with all tense and pronoun backshift rules applied.

5.3 Imperatives, Exclamations, and Optatives in Narration

Imperative sentences (commands, requests, advice) lack a subject and tense, so their conversion relies on infinitival embedding. The reporting verb shifts to ordered, commanded, requested, advised, urged, warned, begged, or forbade (for negative imperatives). The imperative verb becomes a to-infinitive: "Leave the room" → He ordered me to leave the room. The negative "Do not touch" becomes He warned me not to touch (or forbade me to touch). The subject of the infinitive is the object of the reporting verb (the addressee). Exclamatory sentences expressing strong emotion (joy, sorrow, surprise, anger) use reporting verbs like exclaimed with joy, exclaimed with sorrow, exclaimed with surprise. The exclamatory structure ("What a beautiful day!") is converted to a that-clause with the emotion encoded in the reporting verb: He exclaimed with joy that it was a beautiful day. Optative sentences (wishes, prayers, curses) beginning with may or would that take reporting verbs wished, prayed, cursed, hoped. "May you live long!" → He wished that I might live long (or He prayed for my long life). The modal may shifts to might following backshift rules. These minor sentence types are high-yield examination topics because they test the candidate's ability to select the precise reporting verb lexicalizing the illocutionary force.

6. Synthesis and Reverse Transformation Strategies

6.1 Converting Indirect Speech Back to Direct Form

The reverse operation—indirect to direct speech—demands the systematic undoing of every forward transformation rule. The complementizer that is removed and replaced by quotation marks and a comma. The reporting verb reverts to a neutral form (usually said or said to) unless the specific illocutionary verb (ordered, asked, exclaimed) is explicitly required by the rubric. Pronouns shift back to their original persons: third-person subjects become first-person if the original speaker is the reporter, or second-person if the original addressee is the reporter. Tense backshift is reversed: past simple and past perfect both revert to present simple or present perfect depending on aspectual nuance (he had written → "I have written" or "I wrote"; he was writing → "I am writing"). Modal past forms (would, could, might, should, had to) revert to their present counterparts (will, can, may, shall, must). Distal adverbials (then, that day, there, before) revert to proximal forms (now, today, here, ago).

Passive Constructions with Non‑finite Complements

When a transitive verb takes a non‑finite complement — an infinitive, a gerund, or a present participle — the passive can be formed at two distinct levels. The higher clause may be passivised while the complement remains active, or the complement itself may be passivised, yielding a chain of passive constructions. For example, They expect him to finish the report admits the higher passive He is expected to finish the report and the lower passive He is expected to be finished the report (the latter is marginal and usually replaced by The report is expected to be finished by him). Standard reference grammars (Quirk et al., A Comprehensive Grammar of the English Language, 1985) treat the lower passive as a “passive of the infinitive” and note its restriction to verbs of perception, causation, and expectation.

Gerundial complements behave similarly. They dislike his smoking in the laboratory can become His smoking in the laboratory is disliked (higher passive) or Smoking in the laboratory is disliked by them (lower passive with the gerund as subject). The choice hinges on information structure: the higher passive preserves the agent as a by‑phrase, while the lower passive foregrounds the activity. Present‑participle complements, as in We found the machine operating, allow only the higher passive The machine was found operating; a lower passive Operating was found the machine is ungrammatical because the participle cannot serve as a syntactic subject.

Examiners frequently test the distinction between to‑infinitive and bare infinitive passives after causative verbs. They made him write the letter yields He was made to write the letter (the to is obligatory in the passive), whereas They let him write the letter yields He was let write the letter (bare infinitive retained). This asymmetry is a reliable diagnostic for verb class and appears in advanced transformation items.

Impersonal and Agentless Passives in Formal Registers

Impersonal passives — constructions without an expressed agent and often without a logical subject — are a hallmark of administrative, legal, and scientific prose. The classic pattern uses a dummy subject it followed by a passive verb and a that-clause: It is recommended that the committee review the policy. An alternative, the “subject‑less passive”, omits it entirely: Recommended is that the committee review the policy. Both forms satisfy the requirement that the verb be transitive in the active counterpart (We recommend that the committee review the policy).

In legal drafting, the agentless passive serves to allocate obligation without naming the obligor: The fee shall be paid within thirty days. The modal shall combines with the passive to create a deontic force that is binding on the party identified elsewhere in the instrument. Scientific writing prefers the agentless passive to emphasise the process: The solution was heated to 80 °C. The omission of the experimenter is conventional, not evasive; style guides (e.g., the Council of Science Editors manual) explicitly endorse it for methods sections.

A subtle trap involves the “passive of intransitive verbs with prepositional objects”. Verbs such as account for, provide for, and see to can be passivised only when the prepositional object becomes the subject: The discrepancy was accounted for. The preposition cannot be stranded in formal passive (*The discrepancy was accounted). Mastery of this restriction separates competent candidates from those who over‑generalise the passive rule.

Worked Example: Agentless Passive in Official Circular
Transform the following active sentence into the appropriate formal passive used in a government circular: "The department expects all officers to submit their reports by Friday."
Step 1: Identify the matrix verb expect (verb of expectation) and its infinitival complement to submit. Step 2: Passivise the matrix clause, retaining the infinitival complement. The subject all officers becomes the passive subject; the agent the department is omitted for impersonal tone. Result: All officers are expected to submit their reports by Friday. Step 3: If the circular requires a completely agentless construction with dummy it, recast as It is expected that all officers submit their reports by Friday. Both forms are acceptable; the first is more common in modern administrative style.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a transformation item asks for “the formal passive”, first decide whether the register favours a personal passive (subject promoted) or an impersonal it-passive. Look for cues such as “circular”, “notification”, or “statute” — these signal the impersonal pattern. Practise converting a set of ten active sentences into both variants; the speed gain is decisive under time pressure.

Reporting Conditionals and Counterfactuals in Indirect Speech

Conditional sentences undergo a two‑stage backshift when reported. The reporting verb’s tense determines whether the conditional’s protasis and apodosis shift one step back or remain unchanged. For a present‑time reporting verb (He says), a first conditional “If it rains, I will stay home” becomes He says that if it rains he will stay home (no backshift). With a past reporting verb (He said), the same sentence backshifts to He said that if it rained he would stay home. The modal will becomes would; the protasis verb rains becomes rained.

Second and third conditionals follow the same principle but start from a past or past‑perfect base. “If I had known, I would have come” reported after He said yields He said that if he had known he would have come — the past perfect in the protasis and the modal perfect in the apodosis are already the backshifted forms, so they remain unchanged. However, if the reporting verb is He will say (future), the conditional may be reported without backshift: He will say that if he had known he would have come.

Counterfactual wishes introduced by wish or if only behave like third conditionals. “I wish I were there” reported as He wished he were there (subjunctive were retained) or He wished he had been there (backshift to past perfect) depending on whether the wish is contemporaneous with the reporting moment. Standard grammars (Huddleston & Pullum, The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language, 2002) treat the subjunctive were as a “irrealis” form that resists further backshift, a nuance examiners exploit in multiple‑choice items.

Free Indirect Discourse and Narrative Perspective Shifts

Free indirect discourse (FID) blends the character’s voice with the narrator’s third‑person past tense, omitting reporting verbs and quotation marks. The hallmark is the coexistence of deictic markers from both perspectives: He would leave tomorrow (the character’s tomorrow retained) while the narrative tense remains past. FID is not a grammatical transformation rule but a stylistic device; nevertheless, examination questions on “identifying the mode of narration” require recognising FID versus direct and indirect speech.

Key diagnostics: (1) absence of a reporting clause; (2) retention of the character’s expressive vocabulary (interjections, evaluative adjectives); (3) shift of pronouns to third person while time/place adverbials may stay proximal. For instance, She thought, “What a disaster!” (direct) → She thought that it was a disaster (indirect) → What a disaster it was! (FID). The last version preserves the exclamatory force and the proximal it but adopts the narrator’s past tense.

In languages with a robust subjunctive, FID often triggers subjunctive morphology; English relies on modal past (would, could, might) to signal the same irrealis stance. Candidates should practise converting a short passage through the three modes, noting where backshift is suspended in FID. This skill is tested in comprehension passages that ask “Which sentence exemplifies free indirect discourse?”

Advanced Reverse Transformation: Reconstructing Direct Speech from Complex Indirect Reports

Reversing a multi‑clause indirect report demands systematic de‑embedding. Begin by isolating the highest reporting verb; restore its direct form (said, asked, exclaimed) and insert quotation marks. Then work inward: each subordinate that-clause becomes a separate direct sentence if the original was a sequence of utterances, or remains a single direct utterance if the indirect report collapsed them. Pronoun reversal follows the speaker‑addressee matrix: third‑person subjects become first‑person for the original speaker, second‑person for the original addressee.

Tense reversal is the most error‑prone step. Past simple and past perfect in the indirect report both map to present simple or present perfect in direct speech, but aspectual cues decide which. He said he had finished → “I have finished” (perfect aspect retained) or “I finished” (simple past) depending on whether the original speaker emphasised completion. Modal past forms (would, could, might, should, had to) revert to their present counterparts (will, can, may, shall, must). Distal adverbials (then, that day, there, before) revert to proximal forms (now, today, here, ago).

A frequent examiner twist presents an indirect report that mixes free indirect discourse with a framing reporting verb, e.g., He recalled how he would have to leave the next day. The candidate must decide whether the original was a direct thought (“I will have to leave tomorrow”) or a reported conditional. The presence of recalled signals a past reporting verb, so the direct reconstruction is “I will have to leave tomorrow” (future in the past). Mastery of this decision tree separates high scorers.

  • Assuming every infinitive complement can be passivised; causative make requires to in the passive, let does not.
  • Omitting the preposition when passivising prepositional verbs (e.g., *The matter was looked instead of The matter was looked into).
  • Backshifting the protasis of a third conditional when the reporting verb is past; the past perfect remains unchanged.
  • Treating free indirect discourse as ordinary indirect speech and inserting a reporting verb that was not present.
  • Reversing modal past forms incorrectly (e.g., turning would into will when the original was a past‑time future would).
  • Failing to restore proximal adverbials (now, today, here) when converting indirect to direct speech.
  • Misidentifying the agent in an impersonal passive and inserting a spurious by-phrase.

Passive Voice with Complex Transitive Verbs and Prepositional Objects

Complex transitive verbs — those that take both a direct object and an object complement or a prepositional object — generate passive constructions that differ from the simple be + past‑participle pattern. Verbs such as consider, declare, elect, find, and make allow a passive in which the original direct object becomes the subject and the object complement remains in situ: They considered him competent → He was considered competent. When the complement is a prepositional phrase headed by as or for, the preposition is retained: They regarded the decision as a mistake → The decision was regarded as a mistake. The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, §16.2) classifies these as “complex‑transitive passives” and notes that the complement cannot be promoted to subject; only the direct object can.

Prepositional verbs (verb + preposition) such as look into, refer to, account for, and dispense with form passives by stranding the preposition: The committee looked into the matter → The matter was looked into. The preposition must not be omitted; *The matter was looked is ungrammatical. Some prepositional verbs admit an alternative “prepositional passive” where the prepositional object becomes subject while the preposition stays with the verb: They referred to the report → The report was referred to. Verbs that take a particle (phrasal verbs) behave differently: They gave up the plan → The plan was given up. The particle remains adjacent to the verb; it cannot be fronted.

Ditransitive verbs with two objects (e.g., give, send, offer) yield two passive possibilities: the direct object passive (A book was given to her) and the indirect object passive (She was given a book). Corpus data from the British National Corpus (BNC) show that the indirect‑object passive is roughly three times more frequent in spoken English, whereas the direct‑object passive dominates in academic writing. The choice often reflects information‑structure preferences: the indirect‑object passive places the recipient in topic position, which aligns with the given‑new contract in discourse.

Verbs of perception and causation (see, hear, make, let) exhibit a “bare‑infinitive” complement in the active (I saw him leave) but require a to-infinitive in the passive (He was seen to leave). Let is exceptional: it resists passivisation altogether (*He was let go is marginal and dialectally restricted). The Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English (Biber et al., 1999, §12.3.4) records that make in the passive appears with to in 98 % of cases, confirming the rule as near‑categorical.

Examiners frequently test the distinction between prepositional verbs and phrasal verbs by presenting a sentence such as The issue was dealt with versus *The issue was dealt. Recognising that deal with is a prepositional verb (the preposition is obligatory) prevents the common error of dropping the preposition. Similarly, items may ask to convert They made him sign the contract into the passive; the correct form is He was made to sign the contract, not *He was made sign the contract.

Reporting Verbs with Subjunctive and Modal Nuances in Indirect Speech

Reporting verbs such as suggest, recommend, insist, demand, and propose trigger a mandative subjunctive in the subordinate clause when the reporting verb is in the present or past: She suggests that he be present; She suggested that he be present. The subjunctive form be is invariant for all persons and numbers. In British English, a modal construction with should is an accepted alternative: She suggested that he should be present. The Cambridge Grammar (2002, §9.2.3) treats the mandative subjunctive as a “present‑subjunctive” that does not backshift; the past‑tense reporting verb does not change the subjunctive to a past form.

Verbs of wishing (wish, would rather, would prefer) govern a past‑subjunctive (irrealis) for present‑time counterfactuals: I wish he were here. When these verbs appear in indirect speech, the irrealis form is retained if the reporting verb is past: He said he wished he were there. However, if the original wish refers to a past counterfactual, the subordinate clause shifts to past perfect: He wished he had been there. The distinction is crucial for examination items that present a mixed‑time context.

Modal verbs in reported speech follow a systematic backshift pattern: can → could, may → might, will → would, shall → should, must → had to (or must when expressing logical necessity). The semi‑modal need to backshifts to needed to; dare and ought to are generally invariant. The Longman Grammar (1999, §10.4.2) notes that must retains its form in indirect speech when it conveys a deduction (He said it must be true), but becomes had to for obligation (He said he had to leave).

Reporting verbs that introduce indirect questions (ask, wonder, inquire) require the interrogative clause to adopt statement word order and, if the reporting verb is past, to backshift the tense: “Where does he live?” → She asked where he lived. The wh‑word remains; no if or whether is inserted unless the original was a yes‑no question. For yes‑no questions, if or whether is used: “Did he arrive?” → She asked whether he had arrived.

A frequent examiner twist involves a reporting verb in the present perfect (has said) followed by a past‑tense reported clause. The backshift rule applies relative to the time of the reporting verb, not the moment of speaking: He has said that he would come (future‑in‑the‑past) is correct; *He has said that he will come is only acceptable if the future event is still pending at the time of reporting. Understanding the temporal anchor of the reporting verb prevents over‑backshifting.

Worked Example: Mandative Subjunctive vs. Should‑Construction
Convert the following direct speech into indirect speech, preserving the mandative force: “The director insists that the report be submitted by Friday.”
The reporting verb insists is present tense, so the subordinate clause retains the present subjunctive be. Indirect form: The director insists that the report be submitted by Friday. If the reporting verb were past (insisted), the subjunctive would still be be (no backshift): The director insisted that the report be submitted by Friday. An alternative British form uses should: The director insisted that the report should be submitted by Friday. Both are acceptable; the subjunctive is preferred in formal American English.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a reporting verb of mandate (insist, demand, suggest, recommend) appears, first identify its tense. If it is present or past, write the subordinate verb in the base form (subjunctive) without adding should unless the question explicitly asks for the British variant. For wish‑type verbs, check whether the complement refers to present or past counterfactuality; use were for present, had been for past. This two‑step check eliminates the most common backshift errors.

Embedded Clauses and Sequence of Tenses in Multi‑Level Indirect Reports

When indirect speech contains more than one level of embedding — for example, a report of a report — the sequence‑of‑tenses rule applies at each level relative to its immediate reporting verb. Consider: She said that he had told her that he would arrive early. The highest reporting verb said (past) triggers backshift in the first subordinate clause (had told). The second reporting verb told is itself past, so its complement (would arrive) is already a backshifted form of will arrive. If the original direct speech was “I will arrive early,” the chain of transformations is: will → would (first backshift) → would remains unchanged at the second level because the reporting verb told is already past. The Cambridge Grammar (2002, §15.3.1) describes this as “tense harmony” across embedding depths.

Embedded questions follow the same principle. He asked whether she knew where the meeting was. The matrix verb asked (past) backshifts know to knew; the embedded wh‑clause where the meeting was retains past tense because its own reporting verb knew is past. If the original direct question was “Where is the meeting?”, the transformation path is: is → was (first backshift) → was stays at the second level.

Conditional clauses inside indirect reports require careful handling. A first conditional (If it rains, we will cancel) reported after a past verb becomes He said that if it rained they would cancel. A second conditional (If it rained, we would cancel) remains If it rained, they would cancel because the past tense in the protasis is already a backshifted form. A third conditional (If it had rained, we would have cancelled) is unchanged: the past perfect in the protasis and the perfect modal in the apodosis are both stable under backshift. Huddleston & Pullum (2002, §14.4) note that the past perfect is “the only tense that does not backshift further.”

Free indirect discourse (FID) can appear inside a framed report, creating a hybrid structure: He recalled how he would have to leave the next day. The matrix verb recalled (past) governs the modal would have to, which is the backshifted form of will have to. The adverbial the next day reflects the distal shift from tomorrow. Recognising FID markers — absence of a complementiser, retention of proximal deictics, third‑person pronouns — allows the candidate to decide whether to reconstruct a direct thought (“I will have to leave tomorrow”) or a reported conditional.

Examination items often present a multi‑clause indirect report and ask for the original direct speech. The systematic de‑embedding procedure is: (1) isolate the highest reporting verb and restore its direct form; (2) work inward, converting each subordinate clause back to its direct tense and mood; (3) reverse pronoun and adverbial shifts using the speaker‑addressee matrix. Practising this algorithm on sentences with three or more embedding levels builds the automaticity needed for timed grammar sections.

Negation Scope and Polarity Items in Passive and Reported Constructions

Negation interacts with voice and narration in ways that affect the distribution of polarity‑sensitive items such as any, ever, anyone, and need. In active sentences, not typically precedes the main verb: She did not see anyone. In the passive, the negative particle attaches to the auxiliary be: No one was seen or Anyone was not seen (the latter is marked and rare). The negative passive was not seen licenses any in subject position: No one was seen is preferred over *Anyone was not seen. The Longman Grammar (1999, §8.5.3) reports that negative passives with any in subject position occur in less than 2 % of sampled texts, confirming the strong preference for no one or nobody.

In indirect speech, the scope of negation can shift depending on whether the reporting verb is negative. He didn’t say that she had arrived negates the reporting verb, leaving the complement clause positive. Conversely, He said that she hadn’t arrived places negation inside the reported clause. The two structures are not truth‑conditionally equivalent: the first allows the possibility that she arrived but he didn’t report it; the second asserts that he reported her non‑arrival. Examiners test this distinction with items such as “Choose the sentence that means ‘He denied that she arrived’.” The correct answer is He said that she hadn’t arrived.

Negative raising verbs (seem, appear, believe, think) allow the negation to appear on the matrix verb while semantically belonging to the complement: He doesn’t seem to have finished ≈ It seems that he hasn’t finished. In the passive, negative raising is blocked: *He isn’t seemed to have finished is ungrammatical. However, the passive of the complement can be negated: He seems not to have been finished (though pragmatically odd). The Cambridge Grammar (2002, §13.2.4) treats negative raising as a syntactic phenomenon limited to a small lexical set.

Polarity items in reported questions also follow scope rules. He asked whether anyone had called licenses anyone because the interrogative clause is non‑assertive. If the reporting verb is negative, He didn’t ask whether anyone had called, the polarity item remains licensed because the embedded question is still a non‑assertive context. But He asked whether no one had called changes the meaning to a negative question, which is a different illocutionary force. Recognising the licensing environment prevents errors in multiple‑choice items that swap anyone for someone.

A common trap involves the passive of a negative active with a modal: They cannot accept the proposal → The proposal cannot be accepted. The modal cannot stays with the auxiliary be; it does not become *The proposal can not be accepted (split negation is disallowed in standard English). Similarly, must not in the active (You must not enter) yields Entry must not be permitted or Entry is not to be permitted, but not *Entry must be not permitted. Mastery of these patterns ensures accurate transformation under exam conditions.

Register Variation and Corpus Evidence for Voice and Narration Choices

Empirical studies using the Corpus of Contemporary American English (COCA) and the British National Corpus (BNC) reveal systematic register differences in voice selection. In academic prose, the passive accounts for approximately 25 % of finite clauses, whereas in conversation it drops below 5 %. The passive is favoured when the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately backgrounded — typical of methods sections in research articles (The samples were analysed). In news reportage, the passive appears in about 15 % of clauses, often to attribute actions to institutions without naming individuals (The decision was announced).

Narration style also varies by register. Direct speech dominates in fiction and dialogue‑heavy narratives, comprising up to 40 % of reported discourse tokens in the BNC fiction sub‑corpus. Indirect speech is more frequent in expository writing, where summarising sources is preferred. Free indirect discourse, while a literary device, appears in 3–4 % of narrative tokens in modern novels (Leech & Short, 2007, Style in Fiction). Its presence in examination passages is usually signalled by a shift to third‑person pronouns combined with retention of the character’s deictic expressions.

Modal choice in reported speech shows register‑sensitive patterns. In formal writing, should is the default backshift for shall and for mandative subjunctives in British English (He recommended that the plan should be adopted). In American formal writing, the subjunctive be adopted is preferred. COCA data indicate that should in mandative contexts occurs at a rate of 12 per million words in US academic texts versus 45 per million in UK academic texts. Awareness of this transatlantic divergence helps candidates avoid marking a perfectly grammatical subjunctive as an error when the test follows a particular standard.

Negation and polarity items also display register effects. Negative passives with no one are more common in legal and bureaucratic texts (No objections were raised), while not … anyone appears in less formal explanatory writing. The BNC shows that not … any in subject position of a passive is virtually absent in legal corpora. Examination items drawn from legal or administrative passages therefore tend to use the no one construction.

Finally, the frequency of reporting verbs themselves is register‑dependent. State, claim, argue, and suggest are high‑frequency in academic and journalistic registers; say, tell, ask dominate in conversation. Recognising the typical reporting verb for a given register aids in reverse‑transformation tasks where the candidate must reconstruct the likely direct speech from an indirect report stripped of its original verb.
