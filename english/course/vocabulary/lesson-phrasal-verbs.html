Learning Objectives

  • Apply key formulas and techniques to solve problems

Key Concepts

What a Phrasal Verb Is

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle (preposition or adverb) that together carry a meaning different from the verb alone. Give means transfer; give up means quit. Look means direct the eyes; look after means take care of. Exams test the meaning in context, so learn the whole phrase with a short example.

Phrasal Verbs by Verb

  • Break — break down (stop working), break out (start suddenly), break up (end a relationship), break into (enter by force), break off (stop abruptly).
  • Bring — bring up (raise a child or mention), bring about (cause), bring out (publish or reveal), bring in (introduce), bring down (reduce or overthrow).
  • Call — call off (cancel), call on (visit), call for (demand), call up (telephone or summon), call out (shout).
  • Carry — carry on (continue), carry out (perform), carry away (lose control), carry off (win or manage).
  • Come — come across (find by chance), come up with (produce an idea), come down (decrease), come about (happen), come round (recover or agree).
  • Get — get over (recover), get away with (escape punishment), get along (be friendly), get rid of (remove), get through (finish or pass).
  • Give — give up (quit), give in (surrender), give away (donate or reveal), give out (distribute or fail), give back (return).
  • Go — go through (examine or experience), go over (review), go off (explode or stop working), go on (continue), go without (manage without).
  • Look — look after (take care of), look into (investigate), look for (search), look forward to (anticipate), look down on (despise).
  • Put — put off (postpone), put up with (tolerate), put on (wear), put out (extinguish), put down (suppress or write).
  • Run — run out of (exhaust), run into (meet unexpectedly), run over (hit with a vehicle), run away (flee), run through (review).
  • Set — set up (establish), set off (start a journey), set aside (reserve), set out (begin with an aim), set back (delay).
  • Take — take off (leave the ground or remove), take over (assume control), take after (resemble), take up (begin a hobby), take in (understand or deceive).
  • Turn — turn down (reject or reduce), turn up (arrive), turn out (prove to be), turn into (become), turn against (become hostile).

Separable and Inseparable

Some phrasal verbs allow the object between the verb and the particle: turn the offer down or turn down the offer. Others do not: look after the child, never look the child after. When the object is a pronoun, it must go between the verb and particle for separable verbs: turn it down, not turn down it. Learn the common separable verbs: turn on/off, put on/off, take off, give up, bring up, call off, carry out, set up.

Example 1
The meeting was ________ because of the storm.

a) called off b) called on c) called up d) called out
Solution: a) Called off. To call off is to cancel.
Example 2
She ________ her mother in appearance.

a) takes after b) takes off c) takes over d) takes up
Solution: a) Takes after. To take after someone is to resemble them.
Example 3
We have ________ sugar; please buy some.

a) run into b) run out of c) run over d) run through
Solution: b) Run out of. To run out of something is to exhaust the supply.
Exam tip
Group phrasal verbs by the particle as well as by the verb. Off often means separation or cancellation (call off, set off, take off); up often means completion or increase (give up, set up, turn up). The pattern helps when the exact phrase is unfamiliar.

Three-Part Phrasal Verbs

Some phrasal verbs take both a particle and a preposition and cannot be separated: put up with (tolerate), get along with (be friendly with), come up with (produce an idea), look forward to (anticipate), catch up with (reach the same level), run out of (exhaust), put down to (attribute to), make up for (compensate), stand up for (defend), get round to (finally do something).

Idiomatic meaning shifts: take off can mean remove clothing, leave the ground, or become successful suddenly. Turn up can mean arrive or increase the volume. Put out can mean extinguish or inconvenience. The sentence context, not the particle, decides which meaning applies; read the object and the subject before choosing.

Register: phrasal verbs are usually informal. In a formal letter, prefer postpone to put off, tolerate to put up with, investigate to look into, and cancel to call off. Descriptive papers mark register, so a phrasal verb that is correct in conversation can be wrong in a formal report.
