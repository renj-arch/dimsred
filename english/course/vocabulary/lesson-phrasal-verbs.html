Phrasal Verbs

A phrasal verb = verb + particle (adverb/preposition), with a meaning often different from the verb alone (give up = quit, not literally 'give'). Exams test meaning, usage, separability, and particles.

  • Structure: verb + adverb particle (look up, give up) OR verb + preposition (look after, depend on). Some have both: look into, look up to.
  • Separable vs inseparable:
    • Separable with noun object: turn off the TV / turn the TV off (both OK).
    • Separable with pronoun object ALWAYS between: turn it off (not turn off it).
    • Inseparable: look after the children / look after them (not look them after).
  • Three-part phrasal verbs: verb + adverb + preposition (get on with, look up to, put up with, catch up with, come up with) — inseparable.
  • Meaning often idiomatic: give up = quit; put off = postpone; put up with = tolerate; run out of = exhaust supply; come across = find by chance.

Common Traps

  • Pronoun object goes BETWEEN verb and particle (turn IT off, not turn off IT).
  • Three-part phrasals are inseparable (put up WITH it, not put it up with).
  • Same particles change meaning completely (give up vs give in vs give out vs give away).
  • Literal meaning traps ('look up' = search info, not physically look upward).

Syntactic Tests for Separability and Particle Movement

Separability is not a binary property; it is diagnosed by a cluster of syntactic behaviours that distinguish true phrasal verbs from prepositional verbs and from verb‑plus‑adverb sequences. The classic test is pronoun placement: a transitive phrasal verb allows the object pronoun to appear between verb and particle (turn it off) whereas a prepositional verb forces the pronoun after the preposition (look after it). A second test involves adverb insertion: an adverb such as completely can intervene in a phrasal verb (turn completely off) but not in a prepositional verb (*look completely after). A third test is particle fronting in imperatives or clefts: Off turn the lights is grammatical for phrasal verbs but not for prepositional verbs. Finally, passivisation reveals the structural status: The lights were turned off is acceptable, while *The children were looked after is marginal and often judged ungrammatical in formal registers. Mastery of these four diagnostics enables rapid classification of any verb‑particle combination encountered in transformation or error‑detection items.

Exceptions arise with three‑part phrasal verbs (verb + particle + preposition) such as put up with or look forward to. These are uniformly inseparable: the pronoun must follow the entire complex (put up with it, never *put it up with). The particle‑preposition boundary also blocks adverb insertion (*put completely up with) and particle fronting (*Up with put the noise). Recognising the three‑part pattern prevents the common error of treating the first particle as separable.

Another layer of complexity appears with verbs that alternate between separable and inseparable uses depending on meaning. Give up (quit) is separable (give it up), whereas give up (surrender a seat) can be inseparable in certain dialects. Corpus studies indicate a strong preference for the separated order with the “quit” sense and a tendency toward the unseparated order with the “surrender” sense. Awareness of sense‑dependent separability sharpens answer selection in multiple‑choice questions that test subtle meaning shifts.

Semantic Compositionality: Literal, Aspectual, and Idiomatic Readings

The meaning of a phrasal verb is not always the sum of its parts. Linguists distinguish three broad compositional types. Literal readings preserve the spatial or directional sense of the particle: walk out (exit a room), pick up (lift). Aspectual readings add a completive or inceptive nuance without changing the core lexical meaning: eat up (finish eating), start off (begin). Idiomatic readings are opaque; the particle contributes a metaphorical shift that cannot be predicted from its literal sense: give up (quit), run into (meet by chance), make up (reconcile). A substantial majority of high‑frequency phrasal verbs are idiomatic, which explains why rote memorisation of particle meanings alone yields limited returns.

A useful analytic framework maps each verb onto a “compositionality continuum”. At one pole, go out (extinguish) is fully compositional: the particle out signals cessation. At the opposite pole, look up to (admire) is wholly idiomatic; the particle up and preposition to bear no spatial relation to admiration. Intermediate cases such as set up (establish) show partial compositionality: up conveys completion, but the verb set has shifted from “place” to “arrange”. Recognising where a verb sits on this continuum guides inference: if a particle signals completion (up, out, off), the phrasal verb often carries an aspectual perfective force; if the particle signals direction (down, over, back), a literal or metaphorical spatial reading is more likely.

Exam questions frequently exploit the literal‑vs‑idiomatic divide. A typical error‑detection stem presents a sentence where a phrasal verb is used literally but the context demands the idiomatic sense, or vice‑versa. For instance, “The committee looked up the report” is ambiguous: look up can mean “search for information” (idiomatic) or “raise one’s eyes” (literal). The surrounding clause (“to verify the figures”) disambiguates toward the idiomatic reading. Training on such contextual cues reduces guesswork.

Corpus Frequency and Register Distribution of High‑Utility Phrasal Verbs

Quantitative corpus linguistics reveals that a relatively small set of phrasal verbs accounts for a disproportionate share of occurrences in academic, journalistic, and spoken registers. Large corpora such as COCA show that the top 50 phrasal verbs constitute a large portion of all phrasal‑verb tokens. The most frequent items — go on, pick up, set up, find out, carry out, turn out, give up, look for, work out, take on — appear across all registers but with distinct frequency profiles. In academic prose, carry out (conduct), set up (establish), and find out (determine) dominate; in spoken dialogue, go on (continue speaking), pick up (collect), and turn up (arrive) are prevalent. Register awareness helps aspirants anticipate which verbs are likely to appear in reading‑comprehension passages versus cloze‑test sentences.

Historical Development from Old English Prefixed Verbs to Modern Particle Verbs

The modern English phrasal verb system descends from two convergent sources: Old English prefixed verbs (e.g., ā-risan “rise up”, for-giefan “forgive”) and the post‑positional particles that survived the loss of case endings. In early Middle English, the prefix a- (from on) and for- began to detach and appear after the verb, creating the first verb‑particle constructions such as rise up and give up. The Great Vowel Shift and the reduction of inflectional morphology accelerated this reanalysis, because the particle could no longer be bound to the verb as a prefix. By the 15th century, the pattern verb + particle was productive enough to generate new formations like take off (remove) and put off (postpone).

Historical corpora document a steady increase in phrasal‑verb token counts from the Old English period through Early Modern English. The rise correlates with the expansion of the English lexicon through contact with Norse and French, which supplied many simplex verbs that combined with native particles to express nuanced aspectual meanings. For example, Norse‑derived take paired with over to form take over (assume control), a construction not attested in Old English. Understanding this diachronic trajectory explains why certain particles (up, out, off, over) are exceptionally productive: they originated as directional adverbs that retained spatial semantics while acquiring aspectual functions.

Exam questions occasionally test historical awareness indirectly, for instance by asking which of two forms is older (give up vs. give in) or by presenting an archaic phrasal verb in a reading passage (“The knight rode forth”). Recognising that forth is a fossilised particle from Old English forð helps decode meaning without a dictionary. A brief chronological checklist — Old English prefixes → Middle English post‑positional particles → Early Modern English productivity surge → Modern idiomatic fixation — provides a mental scaffold for such items.

Exam‑Focused Transformation Patterns: Passive, Cleft, and Nominalisation

Advanced transformation tasks require converting active phrasal‑verb sentences into passive, cleft, or nominalised structures while preserving grammaticality. The passive test is the most reliable separability diagnostic: only transitive phrasal verbs with a direct object can be passivised. The manager called off the meeting → The meeting was called off (grammatical). The manager called on the client → *The client was called on (ungrammatical in standard English because call on is prepositional). Cleft constructions (It was the meeting that the manager called off) further expose the verb‑particle bond: the particle must remain adjacent to the verb in the relative clause. Nominalisation (e.g., the calling off of the meeting) is possible only when the phrasal verb functions as a single lexical unit; prepositional verbs resist nominalisation (*the calling on of the client).

A systematic workflow for transformation items: (1) Identify the verb‑particle combination and its transitivity. (2) Apply the passive test mentally; if the passive is ill‑formed, the item likely targets a prepositional verb or an intransitive phrasal verb. (3) Check particle adjacency in the target structure; separated particles in clefts or nominalisations signal an error. (4) Verify complementation: some phrasal verbs require a gerund (keep on doing), others a bare infinitive (let go), and others a to‑infinitive (decide on). Mismatched complementation is a frequent distractor.

Practice with a mini‑set of 20 high‑frequency verbs across the three transformation types yields measurable accuracy gains. Error analysis from previous examination papers indicates that the majority of transformation errors involve incorrect particle placement in passives, with cleft mis‑ordering and nominalisation of prepositional verbs also common. Targeted drills on these three patterns close the gap efficiently.

  • Assuming all phrasal verbs are separable; three‑part verbs and many idiomatic two‑part verbs are inseparable.
  • Treating particle meaning as constant across verbs; up can signal completion, increase, or direction depending on the verb.
  • Over‑relying on literal spatial sense for idiomatic verbs; look up to does not involve upward gaze.
  • Ignoring register frequency; low‑frequency verbs rarely appear in high‑stakes items.
  • Misapplying passive transformation to prepositional verbs; only true transitive phrasal verbs passivise.
  • Neglecting complementation patterns; using a to‑infinitive where a gerund is required.
  • Forgetting that historical reanalysis explains particle productivity; this insight aids inference on unfamiliar verbs.
  • Mastering these patterns reduces error rates in transformation and error-detection items.

Particle‑Verb Collocational Strength and Lexicalisation Pathways

Collocational strength measures how tightly a verb and particle co‑occur relative to their independent frequencies. Corpus linguists typically compute mutual information, log‑likelihood, or t‑score on large balanced corpora such as the British National Corpus or the Corpus of Contemporary American English. When a verb‑particle pair shows a high association score, the combination behaves more like a single lexical item: it resists internal modification (*give completely up), admits nominalisation (the giving up of the project), and often acquires an idiomatic meaning not derivable from the parts. By contrast, low‑strength pairs (walk up, literal look out) remain compositional, allow adverbial insertion (walk slowly up the hill), and rarely nominalise. The lexicalisation pathway proceeds from free combination → recurrent collocation → semi‑fixed phrase → fully lexicalised phrasal verb. Research suggests particles up, out, off, over are disproportionately represented at the lexicalised end because they encode aspectual notions of completion, removal, or transition that are semantically versatile. Recognising where a given verb‑particle pair sits on this continuum predicts its behaviour in transformation tasks: only fully lexicalised, transitive phrasal verbs passivise cleanly (the meeting was called off), whereas semi‑fixed combinations often reject passive (*the hill was walked up).

Syntactic Islands and Extraction Limits in Phrasal Verb Constructions

Extraction phenomena reveal the structural cohesion of verb‑particle units. In wh‑questions, the direct object of a transitive phrasal verb can be fronted while the particle stays in situ: What did the manager call off? The particle off remains adjacent to the verb, satisfying the adjacency requirement of the verb‑particle complex. However, attempts to extract the particle itself produce ungrammaticality: *Off what did the manager call? This pattern aligns with the Complex NP Constraint — the particle is embedded inside the verbal complex and cannot be pied‑piped alone. Similarly, relative clauses that target the object preserve particle adjacency: the meeting that the manager called off. If a relative clause tries to strand the particle, the result is ill‑formed: *the meeting that the manager called off it. Coordination structures impose the Coordinate Structure Constraint: She called off the meeting and *up the project is impossible because up cannot be coordinated with a full verb‑particle phrase. Topicalisation of the particle alone (*Off, the manager called the meeting) is also barred. These island effects demonstrate that the verb‑particle unit functions as a syntactic head for extraction purposes, a fact that examiners exploit by presenting sentences where the particle has been illicitly moved or omitted.

Extraction Diagnosis
Which of the following sentences is ungrammatical, and why?
1. What did the committee call off?
2. Off what did the committee call?
3. The proposal that the committee called off was controversial.
Sentence 2 is ungrammatical. In English, wh‑extraction may target the direct object of a transitive phrasal verb (sentence 1) or the whole noun phrase in a relative clause (sentence 3), but the particle cannot be extracted alone because it forms a tight syntactic unit with the verb. Moving off to the front strands the verb and violates the Complex NP Constraint, yielding *Off what did the committee call?.
Exam‑Day Extraction Check
When a question asks you to identify a faulty wh‑question or relative clause involving a phrasal verb, first locate the particle. If the particle appears separated from its verb in the moved constituent, the sentence is almost certainly ungrammatical. Verify that the verb remains transitive and that the extracted element is the object, not the particle.

Register‑Sensitive Polysemy: Formal vs. Informal Particle Choices

Particles carry register‑dependent connotations that affect stylistic appropriateness. In academic and legal writing, verbs such as terminate, discontinue, or cease replace informal phrasal verbs like give up, call off, or put off. Conversely, spoken and journalistic registers favour phrasal forms because they convey aspectual nuance (completion, abruptness) with fewer syllables. The particle up illustrates this split: sum up is acceptable in a conference abstract, whereas wrap up signals a more conversational tone. Out shows a similar pattern: phase out appears in policy documents, while weed out is colloquial. Off in write off (accounting) is technical, but brush off (dismiss) is informal. Examiners test this awareness by presenting a formal passage with a phrasal verb that is stylistically mismatched and asking for the most appropriate single‑verb replacement. The correct answer typically restores the register by selecting a Latinate simplex verb that preserves the aspectual meaning without the particle.

Cross‑Dialectal Variation in Particle Selection and Verb‑Particle Ordering

Major English varieties exhibit systematic differences in particle preference. British English commonly uses fill in (a form) and write up (a report), whereas American English prefers fill out and write out. The verb call illustrates regional particle choice: call round (visit) is British, call around is American, and call by appears in both with slightly different pragmatic force. In Australian and New Zealand English, knock off (finish work) coexists with knock out (exhaust), while South African English also uses knock up in the sense of preparing something quickly. Ordering variations occasionally arise with three‑part verbs: standard look forward to across varieties, with non‑standard forms such as look forward into attested in learner corpora. These differences reflect historical settlement patterns, substrate influence, and ongoing lexical diffusion. Test items that present a sentence in one variety and ask for the “standard” form usually target the variety most represented in the examination’s reference corpus (often a blend of British and American norms). Awareness of the most frequent cross‑dialectal alternations enables rapid elimination of distractors that are grammatical only in a non‑target variety.

Psycholinguistic Processing of Phrasal Verbs: Implications for Speeded Comprehension

Psycholinguistic research indicates that high‑frequency phrasal verbs (give up, take off, put on) are accessed via a direct lexical route, behaving like single words in lexical decision and eye‑tracking tasks. Low‑frequency or transparent combinations (walk up, stare out) engage a compositional route where the verb and particle are processed separately, resulting in longer fixation times on the particle. Priming studies suggest that presenting the particle up facilitates recognition of give up but not literal look up, indicating that particle‑specific semantic features (completion, upward motion) are activated primarily for idiomatic pairings. For timed examinations, this means familiar idiomatic phrasal verbs can be parsed and transformed almost automatically, while novel or literal combinations demand deliberate syntactic analysis. Strategic test‑takers therefore allocate more time to items containing low‑frequency or ambiguous particles, and they use the particle’s aspectual cue (completion for up, removal for off) as a rapid heuristic for meaning inference when the verb is unknown.

  • Assuming every verb‑particle pair with high corpus frequency is fully lexicalised; some frequent pairs remain compositional.
  • Treating particle extraction as permissible in wh‑questions; only the object may move, never the particle alone.
  • Ignoring register mismatch when substituting a phrasal verb with a simplex verb in formal writing tasks.
  • Applying a single dialect’s particle norm (e.g., fill out) to a test that follows a different standard variety.
  • Over‑relying on the direct‑access heuristic for all phrasal verbs; low‑frequency items require compositional parsing.
  • Neglecting the Coordinate Structure Constraint when coordinating two phrasal verbs; the particle cannot be shared across conjuncts.
  • Failing to check nominalisation eligibility; only fully lexicalised transitive phrasal verbs admit the V‑ing of constructions.

Mastering these advanced diagnostics sharpens accuracy across transformation, error‑detection, and register‑adjustment items.

Aspectual Particle Semantics: Completion, Inception, and Continuation

Particles in English phrasal verbs are not merely spatial markers; they encode aspectual nuances that alter the temporal contour of the event. The particle up frequently signals completion or thoroughness (e.g., eat up, finish up, clean up), whereas out often marks exhaustion or removal (e.g., run out, wear out, phase out). Off conveys separation or departure (e.g., take off, set off, call off), and away adds a sense of continuous motion or distance (e.g., fade away, drift away, work away). On and along typically indicate continuation or progression (e.g., carry on, move along, go on).

These aspectual values are systematic enough to serve as a heuristic for meaning inference when the verb is unfamiliar. For instance, a learner encountering polish up can predict a completive reading (improve thoroughly) because up contributes the “to completion” feature. Conversely, polish off suggests rapid consumption or disposal, reflecting the “removal” sense of off. Corpus studies (e.g., Biber et al., 1999) suggest that the completive up collocates strongly with verbs of consumption and creation, while the separative off prefers verbs of departure and cancellation.

Importantly, the same particle can host multiple aspectual readings depending on the verb’s lexical class. Up with motion verbs (go up, climb up) retains a directional sense, but with change‑of‑state verbs (heat up, wake up) it shifts to inceptive or completive aspect. This polysemy is a frequent source of test items that ask candidates to select the particle that yields a target aspectual meaning. Mastery of the particle‑aspect mapping therefore reduces reliance on rote memorisation and enables rapid, rule‑based disambiguation.

Negation Scope and Particle Placement in Phrasal Verbs

The interaction between negation and particle placement reveals subtle syntactic constraints that examiners exploit in error‑detection and transformation tasks. In standard contemporary English, the negative particle not (or the contracted n't) attaches to the auxiliary, leaving the verb‑particle complex intact: She did not give up, They haven’t called off the meeting. The particle never intervenes between the auxiliary and the main verb; constructions such as *She gave not up are archaic or dialectal and are marked ungrammatical in formal writing.

Scope ambiguities arise when the particle itself carries a negative‑like meaning (e.g., off in call off “cancel”). In He didn’t call off the event, negation scopes over the entire phrasal verb, yielding “He did not cancel the event”. By contrast, He called off not the event but the meeting forces narrow focus on the object, a structure that tests the candidate’s ability to recognise contrastive focus rather than particle movement.

Another diagnostic involves the placement of never and rarely with particle verbs. Adverbs of frequency normally precede the auxiliary (She never gives up) but may follow the particle in emphatic or poetic registers (She gives up never – marked). Test items often present a sentence with misplaced never (e.g., *She gives never up) and ask for the correct ordering. The rule: frequency adverbs occupy the pre‑auxiliary position; they cannot split the verb‑particle bond.

Finally, the negative polarity item any interacts with particle verbs in idiomatic contexts. He didn’t give up any hope is grammatical, whereas *He gave up any hope without negation is not. Recognising the licensing environment for polarity items inside phrasal verb complements is a high‑yield skill for cloze and sentence‑completion sections.

Transformation Drill
Rewrite the following sentence in the passive voice without changing the aspectual meaning: “The committee called off the investigation.”
The investigation was called off by the committee. The particle off remains adjacent to the verb in the passive participle called off, preserving the cancellative aspect. An incorrect answer such as “The investigation was called by the committee off” violates the adjacency constraint and would be marked wrong.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a transformation item requires passivisation of a phrasal verb, keep the particle attached to the verb form. If the particle separates, the construction is ungrammatical in standard English. Quick mental check: “Was the meeting called off?” – yes; “Was the meeting called off by them?” – yes. Any answer that inserts material between verb and particle can be eliminated instantly.

Phrasal Verbs in Legal and Academic Discourse: Register Adaptation

Legal and academic registers systematically replace high‑frequency phrasal verbs with Latinate simplex verbs to achieve precision, impersonality, and statutory clarity. The phrasal verb call off becomes cancel or terminate; put off is rendered as postpone or defer; give up maps onto relinquish, abandon, or waive. This substitution is not merely stylistic: the simplex verb often carries a narrower semantic range, eliminating the ambiguity inherent in the particle’s aspectual polysemy.

Corpus comparisons (e.g., COCA legal sub‑corpus vs. spoken sub‑corpus) indicate a strong preference for terminate over call off in contract clauses, while the reverse tendency holds in newspaper editorials. Examiners test this register sensitivity by presenting a formal excerpt containing a phrasal verb (e.g., “The agency shall phase out the subsidy”) and asking for the most appropriate single‑verb replacement. The correct answer (eliminate or discontinue) restores the formal register without altering the aspectual sense of gradual cessation.

Nominalisation patterns also differ across registers. In academic writing, fully lexicalised transitive phrasal verbs readily form ‑ing nominals (the phasing out of subsidies, the calling off of the trial). In legal drafting, however, the preferred nominal is a derived noun (termination, cancellation) because it integrates more cleanly into defined‑term hierarchies. Candidates should recognise that not all phrasal verbs admit ‑ing nominalisation; only those that have achieved lexicalised status (high collocational strength, idiomatic meaning) tend to do so.

Finally, passive constructions with phrasal verbs are common in both registers but differ in agent expression. Academic prose often omits the agent (The experiment was carried out), whereas legal texts may retain it for accountability (The contract was terminated by the parties). Awareness of these preferences aids in selecting the appropriate voice when rewriting sentences for a specified register.

Computational Identification of Phrasal Verbs: Tagging and Parsing Challenges

Automatic detection of phrasal verbs remains a benchmark problem in natural‑language processing because the verb‑particle sequence can be either a true multiword expression or a coincidental verb‑preposition combination. Taggers such as the Penn Treebank assign the particle the tag RP only when it forms a phrasal verb; otherwise the particle receives IN (preposition). The decision hinges on syntactic tests: particle movement (She turned the light off vs. She turned off the light), adverb insertion (She turned completely off the light – impossible), and passivisation (The light was turned off – possible).

Statistical parsers exploit collocational strength measures (pointwise mutual information, log‑likelihood) to predict the RP tag. High‑frequency pairs like give up, take off, put on achieve scores well above the threshold, whereas compositional pairs like walk up (directional) score lower and are often mis‑tagged as verb + preposition. Neural models (e.g., BERT‑based sequence labelers) improve accuracy by contextualising the particle with surrounding tokens, yet they still struggle with low‑frequency idioms and novel coinages.

For examination purposes, understanding these computational diagnostics clarifies why certain test items ask candidates to identify the particle in a sentence where an adverb intervenes (He quickly gave up the idea – adverb before the verb, particle stays). The particle’s resistance to adverbial intrusion is a reliable cue that the verb‑particle unit is a phrasal verb, not a prepositional phrase. Similarly, the ability to form a passive (The idea was given up) confirms lexicalised status.

Error‑analysis of parser output reveals systematic confusions: (1) mis‑labeling look up (information‑seeking) as prepositional because the object can be a wh‑clause (look up what happened); (2) treating run into (meet) as a prepositional verb when the object is a pronoun (run into him) because particle movement is blocked. Recognising these edge cases equips aspirants to spot examiner‑crafted distractors that exploit the verb‑preposition boundary.

  • Assuming every verb‑particle pair with high corpus frequency is fully lexicalised; some frequent pairs remain compositional.
  • Treating particle extraction as permissible in wh‑questions; only the object may move, never the particle alone.
  • Ignoring register mismatch when substituting a phrasal verb with a simplex verb in formal writing tasks.
  • Applying a single dialect’s particle norm (e.g., fill out) to a test that follows a different standard variety.
  • Over‑relying on the direct‑access heuristic for all phrasal verbs; low‑frequency items require compositional parsing.
  • Neglecting the Coordinate Structure Constraint when coordinating two phrasal verbs; the particle cannot be shared across conjuncts.
  • Failing to check nominalisation eligibility; only fully lexicalised transitive phrasal verbs admit the V‑ing of constructions.
