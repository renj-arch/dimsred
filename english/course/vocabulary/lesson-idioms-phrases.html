Learning Objectives

Key Concepts

How Idioms Are Asked

An idiom is a fixed expression whose meaning cannot be worked out from the individual words. Exams give the idiom in a sentence and ask for its meaning, or give a meaning and ask for the idiom. The common trap is the literal meaning: break the ice has nothing to do with ice, and pull someone's leg has nothing to do with legs. Learn the idiom as one unit, with one short example sentence.

Idioms About Effort and Work

  • Burn the midnight oil — study or work late into the night.
  • Bite the bullet — face a difficult situation bravely.
  • Pull up one's socks — prepare to work harder.
  • Put one's shoulder to the wheel — start working with determination.
  • Leave no stone unturned — try every possible method.
  • Go the extra mile — make more effort than expected.
  • Back to the drawing board — start again after a failure.
  • Burn one's fingers — suffer from one's own actions.
  • Make hay while the sun shines — use an opportunity while it lasts.
  • Strike while the iron is hot — act at the right moment.

Idioms About Speech and Secrets

  • Spill the beans — reveal a secret.
  • Let the cat out of the bag — disclose a secret accidentally.
  • Beat around the bush — avoid the main point.
  • Call a spade a spade — speak plainly and honestly.
  • Hit the nail on the head — say exactly the right thing.
  • Talk through one's hat — talk nonsense.
  • Bury the hatchet — end a quarrel and make peace.
  • Give someone the cold shoulder — ignore someone deliberately.
  • In hot water — in trouble.
  • Out of the frying pan into the fire — from one bad situation to a worse one.

Idioms About Money and Character

  • A blessing in disguise — something that seems bad but turns out good.
  • A drop in the ocean — a very small amount compared with what is needed.
  • Cost an arm and a leg — be very expensive.
  • Feel the pinch — suffer from a shortage of money.
  • Turn a deaf ear — refuse to listen.
  • Apple of one's eye — someone greatly loved.
  • At the drop of a hat — immediately, without hesitation.
  • Once in a blue moon — very rarely.
  • Teach someone a lesson — punish someone so they do not repeat a mistake.
  • Let sleeping dogs lie — avoid bringing up an old problem.

Phrases Often Confused

In the nick of time means just in time; at the eleventh hour means at the last possible moment. On the spur of the moment means without planning; at a snail's pace means very slowly. All in all means considering everything; all the same means nevertheless. In lieu of means instead of; in view of means considering. Learn the preposition with the phrase, because the examiner often changes it to create a wrong option.

Example 1
The new intern was asked to pull up his socks after a poor first week.

a) buy new socks b) work harder c) resign d) complain
Solution: b) Work harder. The idiom means to improve one's effort, not to buy clothing.
Example 2
Choose the meaning of to let sleeping dogs lie.

a) to wake animals b) to avoid an old controversy c) to sleep early d) to tell a secret
Solution: b) To avoid an old controversy. The idiom advises against reopening a settled problem.
Example 3
The donation was only a drop in the ocean for the flood victims.

a) a large help b) a very small help c) a dangerous gift d) a useless object
Solution: b) A very small help. The amount is tiny compared with the need, not worthless in itself.
Exam tip
Do not translate an idiom word by word. If an option repeats the literal words of the idiom, it is almost always the trap. Choose the option that states the figurative meaning in plain language.

How to Revise

Group idioms by theme (work, money, speech, conflict) rather than alphabetically. Write one sentence of your own for each idiom; an idiom you have used once is easier to recall than one you have only read. In the exam, if two options look similar, choose the one that matches the tone of the sentence and the grammar of the surrounding clause.

Idioms in Context

Idioms carry tone as well as meaning. To pass away is neutral and respectful; to kick the bucket is informal and can be flippant. To be in the family way is old-fashioned and gentle; to be pregnant is direct. In a formal letter or an essay, choose the neutral option even if both meanings are correct. Exams usually test the neutral meaning, but a tone question can turn on this difference.

Business and government idioms appear in banking and UPSC papers: red tape (excessive bureaucracy), to cut corners (do something cheaply or carelessly), to bail out (rescue financially), a level playing field (fair competition), to tighten the belt (reduce spending), to break the bank (cost too much), in the red (in debt), in the black (profitable).

Preposition changes the idiom. To look after is to care for; to look for is to search; to look into is to investigate; to look down on is to despise. When two options differ only in the particle, the particle is the question. Learn the phrase with its particle, never the verb alone.
