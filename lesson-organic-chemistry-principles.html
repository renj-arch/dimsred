Organic Chemistry Principles — Classification, IUPAC Nomenclature, Isomerism & Mechanisms

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon compounds. Carbon forms four covalent bonds (tetravalency) and catenates extensively — bonding with itself to form chains, branches, and rings. Organic compounds are classified by functional groups — specific atoms or groups that determine chemical reactivity. Hydrocarbons contain only C and H (alkanes C₂Hₖ₊₂, alkenes C₂Hₖ, alkynes C₂Hₖ−₂, arenes). Functional groups include: halogens (R-X), alcohols (R-OH), ethers (R-O-R'), aldehydes (R-CHO), ketones (R-CO-R'), carboxylic acids (R-COOH), esters (R-COOR'), amines (R-NH₂), amides (R-CONH₂), and nitro compounds (R-NO₂). IUPAC nomenclature systematically names compounds: select the longest carbon chain (parent), number to give substituents the lowest locants, name substituents alphabetically, and indicate stereochemistry (E/Z, R/S). Isomerism: structural isomers (same molecular formula, different connectivity) and stereoisomers (same connectivity, different spatial arrangement — geometrical cis/trans and optical enantiomers). Reaction mechanisms describe bond breaking and forming: homolytic cleavage (radicals) and heterolytic cleavage (carbocations/carbanions). Major types: substitution (SN1, SN2), elimination (E1, E2), addition (electrophilic, nucleophilic), and rearrangement.

Part 1

📖 Classification and IUPAC Nomenclature

Fundamentals
Classification by Functional Groups, IUPAC Rules, Common Nomenclature, and Trivial Names
Organic compounds are broadly classified as acyclic (open-chain) or cyclic (closed-ring). Cyclic compounds include alicyclic (saturated rings), aromatic (planar rings with conjugated π-electron systems following Hückel's rule: 4n+2 π electrons), and heterocyclic (ring contains O, N, S). Functional groups determine chemical properties: alkenes (C=C), alkynes (C≡C), arenes (Ar-H), alkyl halides (R-X), alcohols (R-OH), phenols (Ar-OH), ethers (R-O-R'), aldehydes (R-CHO), ketones (R-CO-R'), carboxylic acids (R-COOH), esters (R-COOR'), acyl halides (R-COX), amides (R-CONH₂), amines (R-NH₂), nitriles (R-C≡N), and nitro compounds (R-NO₂). IUPAC nomenclature (IUPAC 1979/1993): (1) identify the parent chain — the longest continuous carbon chain containing the principal functional group. (2) Number the chain to give the principal functional group the lowest possible locant (OH > CHO > CO > COOH > COOR > CONH₂ > CN > C=C > C≡C > R > X > NO₂). (3) Name substituents as prefixes in alphabetical order (ignoring di-, tri-). (4) Use suffixes to indicate the principal functional group (-ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -oate, -amide, -nitrile, -amine). (5) For alkenes/alkynes, use -ene/-yne; indicate position with locants. (6) Stereochemistry: E/Z for alkenes, R/S for chiral centers. Common names: formaldehyde (methanal), acetic acid (ethanoic acid), acetone (propanone).
Systematic IUPAC naming gives each organic compound a unique, unambiguous name based on parent chain, functional group priority, and substituent positions.
Part 2

🔀 Structural and Stereoisomerism

Isomerism
Chain, Position, Functional Group, Metamerism, Tautomerism, Geometrical and Optical Isomerism
Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different bond connectivity. Types: chain isomerism (different carbon skeletons — e.g., n-butane vs isobutane), position isomerism (same functional group at different positions — e.g., 1-propanol vs 2-propanol), functional group isomerism (different functional groups — e.g., ethanol CH₃CH₂OH vs dimethyl ether CH₃OCH₃), metamerism (different alkyl groups around a functional group — e.g., diethyl ether vs methyl propyl ether), and tautomerism (rapid interconversion between isomers via proton transfer — e.g., keto-enol tautomerism in carbonyl compounds). Stereoisomers have the same bond connectivity but different spatial arrangement. Geometrical isomerism (cis-trans): arises from restricted rotation around a double bond or in cyclic compounds. For alkenes, cis (same side) and trans (opposite side); E/Z notation based on Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priority rules. Optical isomerism: molecules that are non-superimposable on their mirror images are chiral. A carbon with four different substituents is a chiral center (stereocenter). Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images — they rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions (dextrorotatory +, levorotatory −). A 1:1 mixture of enantiomers is a racemic mixture (racemate), which is optically inactive. Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images (e.g., cis/trans isomers, or compounds with multiple chiral centers). Meso compounds have multiple chiral centers but are achiral due to an internal plane of symmetry.
Structural isomers differ in connectivity; stereoisomers differ in spatial arrangement — the latter includes cis/trans and chiral enantiomers crucial in biological systems.
Part 3

🔥 Reaction Mechanisms: Substitution and Elimination

Mechanisms
SN1, SN2, E1, E2 Reactions — Kinetics, Stereochemistry, and Factors Affecting Reactivity
SN2 (bimolecular nucleophilic substitution): one-step concerted mechanism. The nucleophile attacks from the back side, and the leaving group departs simultaneously. Inversion of configuration (Walden inversion). Kinetics: rate = k[RX][Nu⁻] — second order. Favored by: primary substrates, strong nucleophiles, polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, DMF, acetone), good leaving groups (I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻). SN1 (unimolecular nucleophilic substitution): two-step mechanism. Step 1: slow ionization to form a carbocation (rate-determining). Step 2: fast attack by nucleophile. Racemization occurs (partial inversion + partial retention). Kinetics: rate = k[RX] — first order. Favored by: tertiary substrates, weak nucleophiles, polar protic solvents (H₂O, ROH), good leaving groups. Carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl. E2 (bimolecular elimination): one-step concerted mechanism — base abstracts a β-hydrogen while the leaving group departs, forming an alkene. Anti-periplanar geometry required (H and leaving group must be 180° apart). Kinetics: rate = k[RX][base] — second order. Favored by: strong bulky bases (t-BuO⁻), high temperature, aprotic solvents. Follows Zaitsev's rule: the more substituted alkene (more stable) is the major product. E1 (unimolecular elimination): two-step mechanism — ionization to carbocation (slow), then deprotonation (fast). Kinetics: rate = k[RX] — first order. Favored by: tertiary substrates, weak bases, polar protic solvents, high temperature. Carbocation rearrangements may occur (hydride or alkyl shift) to form a more stable carbocation. Substitution vs elimination competition: strong base/nucleophile + primary = SN2; bulky base + tertiary = E2; weak nucleophile + tertiary = SN1/E1 mixture; high temperature favors elimination.
SN2 inverts stereochemistry with second-order kinetics; SN1 gives racemization with first-order kinetics; E2 requires anti-periplanar geometry; Zaitsev's rule predicts the major alkene product.
Part 4

⚡ Addition Reactions, Rearrangements, and Functional Group Transformations

Transformations
Electrophilic Addition to Alkenes, Markovnikov's Rule, Carbocation Rearrangements, and Oxidation-Reduction
Electrophilic addition to alkenes: the C=C π-bond is electron-rich and attracts electrophiles. General mechanism: electrophile attacks, forming a carbocation, then nucleophile attacks. Markovnikov's rule: in HX addition, the H adds to the less substituted carbon (the one with more H's), giving the more stable carbocation intermediate. Anti-Markovnikov addition occurs with HBr in the presence of peroxides (radical mechanism — Kharasch effect). Addition of halogens (X₂): anti addition via cyclic halonium ion intermediate — gives vicinal dihalides (e.g., Br₂ in CCl₄). Addition of H₂O (acid-catalyzed hydration): follows Markovnikov, gives alcohols. Hydroboration-oxidation: anti-Markovnikov addition of H₂O (BH₃ then H₂O₂/OH⁻) — syn addition, gives alcohols. Oxidation of alcohols: primary → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (using PCC or CrO₃/H₂SO₄); secondary → ketone; tertiary — no oxidation. Reduction: aldehydes/ketones → alcohols (NaBH₄, LiAlH₄); carboxylic acids → alcohols (LiAlH₄); esters → alcohols (LiAlH₄); nitriles → amines (H₂/Ni). Carbocation rearrangements: 1,2-hydride shift or 1,2-alkyl shift to form a more stable carbocation (e.g., in dehydration of alcohols or addition of HX). Electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS): nitration, halogenation, sulfonation, Friedel-Crafts alkylation/acylation. The aromatic ring acts as a nucleophile; the π-complex (σ-complex, Wheland intermediate) is the key intermediate. Directing effects: activating (—OH, —NH₂, —R) are ortho/para directing; deactivating (—NO₂, —CN, —CHO, —COOH) are meta directing; halogens are deactivating but ortho/para directing.
Electrophilic addition follows Markovnikov's rule via stable carbocation intermediates; oxidation, reduction, and EAS reactions enable systematic functional group interconversion in organic synthesis.
