General Relativity — Equivalence Principle, Curved Spacetime, Einstein Equations & Black Holes

General relativity replaces the Newtonian idea of a gravitational force with the geometry of spacetime. Matter and energy tell spacetime how to curve; freely falling bodies follow the straightest possible paths — geodesics — through that curved geometry. The theory has passed every precision test so far, from the precession of Mercury’s perihelion to the direct detection of gravitational waves.

“Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve.”
John Archibald Wheeler
Part 1

The Equivalence Principle and Curved Spacetime

Equivalence Principle
Why gravity is geometry

The weak equivalence principle states that all test bodies fall with the same acceleration in a gravitational field, independent of their composition. This is equivalent to the equality of gravitational and inertial mass, and has been verified to extraordinary precision by torsion-balance experiments such as Eöt-Wash. The Einstein equivalence principle extends the idea: in a sufficiently small freely falling laboratory, the laws of physics reduce to those of special relativity, and uniform acceleration is locally indistinguishable from a uniform gravitational field.

The fundamental object is the metric tensor gμν(x), which defines the spacetime interval:

ds² = gμν dxμ dxν

In flat Minkowski spacetime, ημν = diag(−1, +1, +1, +1) in the (−,+,+,+) signature. In curved spacetime the metric varies from point to point. Free particles follow geodesics,

d²xμ/dτ² + Γμνρ (dxν/dτ)(dxρ/dτ) = 0,

where τ is proper time and Γμνρ are the Christoffel symbols. Gravitational time dilation follows directly: clocks deeper in a gravitational potential run slower, with dt/dτ = 1/√(1 − 2GM/(rc²)) in the Schwarzschild geometry.

Part 2

The Riemann Tensor and Einstein’s Field Equations

Curvature
From tidal forces to the Einstein tensor

Curvature is encoded in the Riemann curvature tensor,

Rρσμν = ∂μΓρνσ − ∂νΓρμσ + ΓρμλΓλνσ − ΓρνλΓλμσ.

It measures the failure of second covariant derivatives to commute and therefore the tidal stretching that cannot be removed by a change of coordinates. Contractions give the Ricci tensor Rμν = Rρμρν and the Ricci scalar R = gμνRμν. The Einstein tensor

Gμν = Rμν − ½ gμνR

is divergence-free, ∇μGμν = 0, by the contracted Bianchi identity. This makes it the natural geometric partner of a conserved stress-energy tensor Tμν. The field equations are

Gμν = 8πG Tμν/c⁴.

For a perfect fluid, Tμν = (ρ + p/c²)uμuν + p gμν. In the weak-field, slow-motion limit the 00-component reduces to Poisson’s equation ∇²Φ = 4πGρ, recovering Newtonian gravity. The same framework predicts gravitational redshift, light bending and the precession of Mercury’s orbit.

Part 3

Black Holes: Schwarzschild and Kerr Solutions

Black Holes
Event horizons, singularities and rotating spacetime

The Schwarzschild solution is the unique spherically symmetric vacuum metric:

ds² = −(1 − rs/r)c²dt² + (1 − rs/r)⁻¹dr² + r²dΩ²,

with Schwarzschild radius rs = 2GM/c². The surface r = rs is an event horizon: it is a coordinate singularity, not a curvature singularity, and can be removed by a coordinate transformation such as Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates. Inside the horizon, the radial coordinate becomes timelike and the singularity at r = 0 is unavoidable for infalling matter. The photon sphere lies at r = 3GM/c², and the innermost stable circular orbit for a test particle is at r = 6GM/c².

Real black holes rotate. The Kerr solution is characterised by mass M and angular momentum J. It possesses an event horizon at r+ = GM/c² + √((GM/c²)² − (J/(Mc))²) and an ergosphere outside the horizon where frame dragging forces even light to co-rotate. The no-hair theorems state that an astrophysical black hole is described by just M, J and electric charge Q.

Part 4

Gravitational Waves and Modern Cosmology

Waves and Cosmology
Ripples in spacetime and the expanding universe

Weak perturbations hμν around flat spacetime obey a wave equation. The leading radiation is quadrupolar: the luminosity scales as (G/c⁵)(d³Q/dt³)², where Q is the mass quadrupole moment. LIGO’s detection of GW150914 in 2015 — the merger of two stellar-mass black holes — confirmed this prediction directly and opened gravitational-wave astronomy.

On cosmological scales, the homogeneous and isotropic Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric is

ds² = −c²dt² + a(t)²[dr²/(1 − kr²) + r²dΩ²].

The Friedmann equations relate the scale factor a(t) to the energy density and pressure of the cosmic fluid. Observations of the cosmic microwave background, distant supernovae and large-scale structure support a universe about 13.8 billion years old, currently dominated by dark energy and cold dark matter — the ΛCDM model. The accelerated expansion is attributed to a cosmological constant or a dark-energy field, while the nature of dark matter remains one of the central open problems in physics.
