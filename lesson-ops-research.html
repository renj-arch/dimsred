Operations Research — Linear Programming, Duality & the Simplex Method

Operations Research (OR) is the discipline of applying mathematical methods to make optimal decisions in complex systems. At its core lies linear programming (LP): minimize c^T x subject to Ax ≤ b, x ≥ 0. The simplex method (Dantzig, 1947) navigates the vertices of the feasible polyhedron to find the optimum, while duality theory provides a powerful economic interpretation via shadow prices. Beyond LP, OR encompasses network flows, integer programming, convex optimization, interior point methods (Karmarkar, 1984), and dynamic programming (Bellman). Together these tools underpin modern logistics, finance, manufacturing, and artificial intelligence.

"Operations research is the art of making bad decisions with good mathematics."
— George Dantzig
Part 1

📏 Linear Programming and the Simplex Method

Foundations
Standard LP, Polyhedra, Extreme Points, and the Simplex Algorithm
The standard form LP is: minimize c^T x subject to Ax = b, x ≥ 0, where A ∈ ℝ^{m×n} with rank(A) = m and n ≥ m. The feasible set {x ≥ 0: Ax = b} is a convex polyhedron. A point x is an extreme point (vertex) iff the columns of A corresponding to positive entries are linearly independent — equivalently, x is a basic feasible solution (BFS): n−m variables (non-basic) are zero, and the m basic variables satisfy A_B x_B = b, x_B ≥ 0. The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Programming: if an LP has an optimal solution, it has an optimal BFS (extreme point). The simplex method (Dantzig, 1947) starts at a BFS and moves along an edge by increasing one non-basic variable until a constraint becomes tight. The pricing step selects the entering variable with the most negative reduced cost: c̄_j = c_j − c_B^T A_B^{-1} A_j. The ratio test determines the leaving variable: min over i with (A_B^{-1} A_j)_i > 0 of (x_B)_i / (A_B^{-1} A_j)_i. The tableau representation maintains the canonical form: the objective row expresses z = c_B^T A_B^{-1} b + Σ_{j∈N} c̄_j x_j. Degeneracy occurs when a BFS has fewer than m positive variables; cycling is possible but avoided by Bland's rule (the smallest-index rule for entering and leaving variables). Although the simplex method is exponential in worst-case (Klee-Minty cube), it is polynomial in practice and remains the workhorse of LP solvers.
Linear programming optimizes a linear objective over a convex polyhedron; the simplex method efficiently explores extreme points to find the optimum.
Part 2

🌍 Duality Theory

Duality
Dual LP, Weak and Strong Duality, Complementary Slackness, Sensitivity
Given the primal LP (P): minimize c^T x subject to Ax ≥ b, x ≥ 0, the dual LP (D) is: maximize b^T y subject to A^T y ≤ c, y ≥ 0. The pair is symmetric: the dual of the dual is the primal. Weak duality: for any feasible primal x and dual y, we have c^T x ≥ b^T y — the primal objective is always at least the dual objective. Strong duality: if (P) has an optimal solution x*, then (D) has an optimal solution y* with c^T x* = b^T y* (provided either (P) or (D) is feasible and bounded). This holds under a constraint qualification (e.g., Slater's condition). Complementary slackness: for optimal x* and y*, we have (c_j − a_j^T y*) x*_j = 0 for all j, and y*_i (a_i^T x* − b_i) = 0 for all i — a variable is positive only if its corresponding dual constraint is tight. Economic interpretation: y*_i is the shadow price of constraint i — the marginal increase in the optimal objective value when the right-hand side b_i is increased. The dual simplex method maintains dual feasibility and drives toward primal feasibility; it is especially useful for re-optimizing after adding constraints. Sensitivity analysis studies the range of coefficients (c_j, b_i, a_{ij}) for which the optimal basis remains optimal: the allowable increase/decrease for each cost coefficient c_j (without changing the optimal basis), the shadow prices and their ranges, and the right-hand side ranging. Farkas' lemma: Ax ≤ b, x ≥ 0 is infeasible iff there exists y ≥ 0 such that A^T y ≥ 0 and b^T y < 0 — a certificate of infeasibility dual to the original system.
Duality pairs every LP with a mirror problem; strong duality ensures their optimal values coincide, and shadow prices give marginal economic value to each constraint.
Part 3

🧮 Network Flow and Integer Programming

Networks & IP
Transportation Problem, Max Flow, Branch and Bound, Cutting Planes
The transportation problem minimizes Σ_i Σ_j c_{ij} x_{ij} subject to Σ_j x_{ij} = s_i (supply), Σ_i x_{ij} = d_j (demand), x_{ij} ≥ 0 — a classic network-structured LP that can be solved efficiently via specialized algorithms (the transportation simplex). The assignment problem (a special case where s_i = d_j = 1) is solved by the Hungarian algorithm (Kuhn-Munkres) in O(n³) time. The maximum flow problem: given a directed graph with capacities, find the maximum flow from source s to sink t. The Ford-Fulkerson algorithm repeatedly finds augmenting paths; the max-flow-min-cut theorem states the maximum flow equals the capacity of the minimum s-t cut. The minimum cost flow problem generalizes both transportation and max flow; the network simplex exploits the graph structure for efficiency. Moving to discrete decisions, integer programming (IP) adds integrality constraints: minimize c^T x subject to Ax ≤ b, x ∈ ℤ₊ⁿ. The branch and bound method: solve the LP relaxation, branch on a fractional variable x_j = v into two subproblems (x_j ≤ ⌊v⌋ and x_j ≥ ⌈v⌉), compute bounds to prune. Cutting planes tighten the LP relaxation: a Gomory cut is derived from a fractional row of the optimal simplex tableau — if x_B_i + Σ_j ā_{ij} x_{N_j} = b̄_i with b̄_i fractional, then x_B_i + Σ_j ⌊ā_{ij}⌋ x_{N_j} ≤ ⌊b̄_i⌋ is valid. The Chvátal-Gomory closure is the set of points satisfying all Gomory cuts — it is a polyhedron (the Chvátal rank is finite). Lagrangian relaxation dualizes complicating constraints, yielding a problem that decomposes into tractable subproblems; the subgradient method iteratively solves the dual. Stochastic programming handles uncertainty via two-stage formulation: here-and-now decisions x followed by wait-and-see recourse decisions y(ω) that depend on the realized scenario ω.
Network flow problems exploit graph structure for efficiency; integer programming extends LP to discrete decisions via branch-and-bound and cutting planes.
Part 4

🔥 Interior Point Methods and Convex Optimization

Advanced
Karmarkar's Algorithm, Primal-Dual Path-Following, SDP, and Beyond
Karmarkar's projective algorithm (1984) was the first interior point method for LP with polynomial complexity O(n^{3.5} L), igniting a revolution. Modern solvers use the primal-dual path-following framework: solve the KKT conditions via Newton's method applied to the perturbed system. For the LP: minimize c^T x subject to Ax = b, x ≥ 0, the KKT conditions are: A^T y + s = c, Ax = b, X·s·e = 0 with (x, s) ≥ 0. The perturbed system replaces complementarity with X·s·e = μ·e, where μ > 0 is the barrier parameter. The central path is the set of solutions (x(μ), y(μ), s(μ)) parameterized by μ → 0. The duality gap c^T x(μ) − b^T y(μ) = n·μ, so driving μ → 0 converges to optimality. The predictor-corrector method (Mehrotra) uses an affine predictor step to estimate μ, then a corrector step for centrality. The complexity is O(√n·log(1/ε)) iterations for μ → ε. Self-concordant barriers (Nesterov-Nemirovskii, 1994) generalize the logarithmic barrier x ↦ −Σ ln x_j to arbitrary convex cones, providing a universal theory of polynomial-time interior point methods for all convex optimization problems: linear, quadratic, second-order cone, semidefinite, and conic programming. Semidefinite programming (SDP): minimize C·X subject to A_i·X = b_i, X ⪰ 0 (X is symmetric positive semidefinite). SDP duality: max b^T y subject to Σ y_i A_i ⪯ C. The complementarity condition: X·Z = 0 for optimal primal-dual pair (X, Z = C − Σ y_i A_i). A landmark application is the MAX CUT approximation (Goemans-Williamson, 1995): relax MAX CUT to an SDP, then use randomized rounding to achieve a 0.878 approximation ratio — a dramatic improvement over naive methods. Beyond these, dynamic programming (Bellman's principle of optimality) solves sequential decision problems via the Hamiltonian-Jacobi-Bellman equation, linking OR to optimal control and reinforcement learning.
Interior point methods traverse the interior of the feasible region via Newton's method; their polynomial complexity extends to all convex optimization through self-concordant barriers.

Advanced Sensitivity Analysis, Degeneracy & The Shadow Price Nuances

While standard textbooks introduce sensitivity analysis as reading values from the final simplex tableau, advanced competitive exams test your ability to interpret the range of feasibility and range of optimality when multiple constraints interact. A critical exception occurs at degenerate vertices. In a degenerate solution, one or more basic variables are zero. This creates a scenario where the Simplex method cycles (repeats the same tableau without improving the objective function) or terminates prematurely with an unbounded-looking ray that is actually bounded by geometry. The examiner often presents a problem where the reduced cost of a non-basic variable is exactly zero. This indicates alternative optima. If you pivot on this variable, you move to another optimal vertex without changing the objective value. However, if the entering variable has a negative reduced cost (in minimization) but all coefficients in its column are non-positive, the LP is unbounded. The trap lies in distinguishing between mathematical unboundedness and a degenerate case where the algorithm gets stuck.

Consider the economic interpretation of shadow prices. In a standard non-degenerate optimal solution, the shadow price of a constraint represents the rate of change in the objective function per unit increase in the right-hand side (RHS). However, if the constraint is non-binding (slack variable is basic), its shadow price is zero. Advanced questions ask: "If I increase the RHS of a binding constraint by 1 unit, but this causes another constraint to become non-binding, what happens?" The answer requires recalculating the basis change. Furthermore, in integer programming, shadow prices lose their strict marginal interpretation because the objective function changes discretely. A fractional relaxation might suggest a gain of 0.5 per unit, but an integer solution might yield a jump of 2 or 0, making the LP relaxation bound loose.

Technique: Diagnosing Degeneracy and Alternative Optima
Question: In a minimization problem, the final tableau shows a non-basic variable with a reduced cost of zero. The column for this variable contains both positive and negative entries. What does this imply about the solution set? How would you handle this in an exam scenario where time is limited?
Answer: A reduced cost of zero for a non-basic variable implies that there are multiple optimal solutions (alternative optima). The line segment connecting the current vertex to the adjacent vertex defined by entering this variable consists entirely of optimal points. Since the column has mixed signs, pivoting will move you along this edge to a different corner point with the same objective value. In an exam, if asked for "the" solution, providing the current basic feasible solution is sufficient unless the question asks for the range of values or specific integer solutions. If degeneracy is suspected (a basic variable is zero), check the pivot row selection ratio test; if multiple rows give the minimum non-negative ratio, any choice leads to a degenerate pivot where the objective value does not improve.
Exam-Day Strategy: The Degeneracy Check
When solving simplex problems by hand in an exam, always check if any basic variable equals zero before declaring the solution optimal. If a reduced cost is zero, do not ignore it; explicitly state that alternative optima exist. For sensitivity analysis questions involving RHS changes, never assume linearity holds indefinitely; remember that the shadow price is valid only within the calculated range of feasibility. If a constraint becomes non-binding during this range expansion, the shadow price drops to zero immediately.

Network Flow Algorithms: Beyond Ford-Fulkerson and Min-Cost Circulation

The standard network flow curriculum covers Max-Flow (Ford-Fulkerson) and Min-Cost Flow. However, advanced sections require mastery of min-cost circulation problems where there is no source or sink, but rather lower and upper bounds on flow for every edge. The algorithm here combines the cycle-canceling method with successive shortest path augmentations. Another critical topic is the Transportation Problem specifically when supply and demand are not balanced. You must first add a dummy row or column to balance them before applying the Vogel's Approximation Method (VAM) for an initial basic feasible solution, which often yields a better starting point than the Northwest Corner Rule. The examiner frequently twists this by introducing transshipment nodes where flow is conserved (inflow = outflow), requiring you to set up node balance equations correctly: Flow_in - Flow_out = 0 for transshipment nodes, Supply for sources, and Demand for sinks.

A landmark case in network theory is the Maximum Flow-Minimum Cut Theorem. While Ford-Fulkerson finds the flow, the Min-Cut theorem provides a certificate of optimality by partitioning vertices into two sets (S containing source, T containing sink) such that the sum of capacities of edges from S to T equals the max flow. In competitive exams, you might be given a graph and asked to identify the min-cut directly without running the full algorithm, testing your ability to visualize cuts. Furthermore, for large-scale problems, the Cost Scaling Algorithm or Successive Shortest Path using Dijkstra's with potentials is preferred over simple Ford-Fulkerson because it handles negative costs (via potential functions) and runs in polynomial time O(V E log V + VE log C), whereas standard FF can be exponential if capacities are large integers.

Technique: Handling Unbalanced Transportation Problems
Question: A transportation problem has total supply of 100 units and total demand of 90 units. The cost matrix is given. How do you formulate this, and what is the impact on the optimal solution if a new route opens with infinite capacity but high cost?
Answer: First, add a dummy destination column with demand = 10 (the surplus supply) and zero transportation costs to all sources. Solve using VAM or Least Cost Method. The dummy column represents storage or disposal. If a new route opens, check if it connects to the most expensive cells in the current basis. If the new cost is lower than the current marginal cost of shipping via a specific path, the optimality condition (u_i + v_j <= c_ij) will be violated for that cell, triggering a pivot. The dummy column's costs are zero; any real route opening with positive cost will generally not displace the dummy unless it offers a cheaper way to utilize excess supply.

Dynamic Programming & Stochastic Optimization: Bellman Equations and Recourse

Dynamic programming (DP) extends beyond simple sequential decision making to solving complex stochastic optimization problems. The core mechanism is the Bellman Principle of Optimality: an optimal policy has the property that whatever the initial state and initial decision are, the remaining decisions must constitute an optimal policy with regard to the state resulting from the first decision. In competitive exams, you often encounter the Shortest Path Problem in a DAG solved via DP, which is equivalent to Dijkstra's algorithm but formulated recursively: f_k(x) = min { cij + fk-1(y) } over all predecessors y. A crucial distinction is between deterministic and stochastic DP. In stochastic settings (Markov Decision Processes), the value function V(s) satisfies the Bellman equation: V(s) = max_a { R(s,a) + γ Σ P(s'|s,a) V(s') }. The discount factor γ ensures convergence in infinite horizon problems.

Stochastic programming introduces recourse. In a two-stage problem, you make "here-and-now" decisions x (first stage), then observe random scenarios ω and make recourse decisions y(ω) (second stage). The objective is min { c^T x + E_ω [ Q(x, ω) ] }, where Q is the expected cost of recourse. If the first-stage decision restricts the second stage too much, you incur high recourse costs. The Sample Average Approximation method estimates the expectation by averaging over a finite set of scenarios. A common exam trap involves the Lagrangian Relaxation applied to stochastic problems, where complicating constraints (like capacity limits) are dualized to decompose the problem into smaller subproblems solvable for each scenario, linked by the dual variables (Lagrange multipliers).

Technique: Formulating a Two-Stage Stochastic Model
Question: You must decide how much inventory to order today (x) at cost c, knowing demand D will be realized tomorrow with probability distribution P(D). Holding cost is h per unit, shortage cost is p per unit. Write the objective function including recourse.
Answer: Let x be the order quantity (first stage). The recourse decision y(D) represents additional production or backlogging after demand D is known. The objective is to minimize Expected Total Cost: Minimize c·x + E_D [ h·max(0, x - D) + p·max(0, D - x) ]. Note that if we assume unlimited capacity later, the recourse cost is purely holding/shortage. If there are capacity limits in stage 2, the recourse function becomes more complex (min-cost flow). The expectation E_D is taken over all possible demand scenarios weighted by their probabilities.
  • Confusing Reduced Cost with Shadow Price: Reduced cost applies to non-basic variables (marginal gain of entering), while shadow price applies to basic constraints (marginal value of RHS). Do not mix them in sensitivity analysis.
  • Ignoring Degeneracy in Simplex: Assuming a zero reduced cost always means unboundedness is false; it usually means alternative optima. Always check the column signs.
  • Forgetting Dummy Variables in Transportation: Never apply the standard transportation algorithm directly if supply does not equal demand; the solution will be geometrically invalid.
  • Misinterpreting Stochastic Recourse: Assuming the first-stage decision is fixed regardless of cost. In reality, the optimal x depends on the expected future costs (recourse).
