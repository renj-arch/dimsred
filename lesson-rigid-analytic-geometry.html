Rigid Analytic Geometry — Non-Archimedean Spaces, the Tate Curve & Berkovich Spaces

Rigid analytic geometry is the theory of analytic geometry over non-Archimedean fields such as ℙp, ℂp, or 𝔽q((t)). Developed by John Tate in the 1960s, it overcomes the obstacles posed by the total disconnectedness of p-adic fields by introducing a Grothendieck topology (the G-topology) on affinoid spaces, allowing sheaf theory and cohomology to function analogously to complex analytic geometry. The central objects are rigid analytic spaces — locally G-ringed spaces obtained by gluing affinoids Sp(A) where A is an affinoid algebra (a quotient of a Tate algebra of convergent power series). The theory has profound applications: the Tate curve Eq ≅ 𝔺m/qℤ gives a non-Archimedean uniformization of elliptic curves with j-invariant j(q) = 1/q + 744 + 196884q + …; Mumford's theory of Schottky uniformization extends this to higher-genus curves. Berkovich's reformulation (1990) enriches rigid spaces by including all multiplicative seminorms, producing connected, locally compact, Hausdorff spaces that are far more tractable topologically. Modern developments include Huber's adic spaces, the Fargues-Fontaine curve in p-adic Hodge theory, Scholze's perfectoid spaces, and the geometrization of the local Langlands program.

"Rigid geometry is the study of p-adic analysis in the sense of algebraic geometry."
— John Tate
Part 1

📏 Non-Archimedean Fields and Affinoid Algebras

Foundations
Non-Archimedean Fields, Tate Algebras, and Affinoid Algebras
A non-Archimedean field K is a field complete with respect to an absolute value |·|: K → ℝ≥0 satisfying |x + y| ≤ max(|x|, |y|) (the ultrametric inequality). Equivalently, one has a discrete valuation v: K✗ → ℤ with |x| = p−v(x) in the p-adic case. Examples: ℙp, its completion ℂp, and 𝔽q((t)). The Tate algebra Tn = K⟨ζ1, …, ζn⟩ consists of formal power series ∑ aνζν with coefficients aν ∈ K such that |aν| → 0 as |ν| → ∞ — these converge on the unit polydisc {|ζi| ≤ 1}. An affinoid algebra is a quotient A = Tn/I by an ideal I. The Gauss (supremum) norm on Tn is ||∑ aνζν||sup = maxν |aν|, making Tn a K-Banach algebra. Affinoid algebras satisfy a maximum modulus principle: for f ∈ A, the supremum of |f| on Sp(A) equals the spectral radius. The Berkovich unit disc classifies points into types: the Gauss point (type II), classical points (type I), points with √r (type III), and limit points (type IV).
Non-Archimedean fields satisfy the ultrametric inequality; the Tate algebra of convergent power series is the building block of all rigid analytic geometry.
Part 2

🌍 Rigid Analytic Spaces and the G-Topology

Spaces
The G-Topology, Affinoid Spaces, and Coherent Sheaves
Tate's key insight was that the naive topology on a p-adic field is totally disconnected, so one needs a G-topology (Grothendieck topology) on affinoid spaces. An admissible open subset of Sp(A) is a finite union of rational domains (sets where certain functions satisfy inequalities). An admissible covering is a finite covering by admissible opens, or more generally a covering that admits a refinement by a finite covering (the "overconvergence" condition). An affinoid space Sp(A) is the set of maximal ideals of an affinoid algebra A, equipped with a structure sheaf OSp(A) that is a sheaf on the G-topology. A rigid analytic space is a locally G-ringed space obtained by gluing affinoids. The Tate acyclicity theorem states that the structure sheaf is a sheaf on the G-topology — i.e., the Čech complex of an admissible covering is exact. Kiehl's theorem: coherent sheaves on rigid analytic spaces satisfy the usual exactness properties (the category is abelian, and the cohomology of coherent sheaves on affinoids vanishes). Properness and separatedness are defined analogously to algebraic geometry: a morphism is proper if it is universally closed and separated, with the additional condition of being "overconvergent".
The G-topology replaces the usual topology to salvage sheaf theory; affinoid spaces are the local models, and rigid spaces are their gluings.
Part 3

🧮 The Tate Curve and Non-Archimedean Uniformization

Uniformization
The Tate Curve, q-Parameter, and Mumford Curves
Over ℂ, an elliptic curve is uniformized as E ≅ ℂ/(ℤ + τℤ). Over a non-Archimedean field K, there is an analogous Tate uniformization: for q ∈ K✗ with |q| < 1, the Tate curve Eq is defined as the rigid analytic quotient 𝔺m/qℤ, where 𝔺m is the multiplicative group and qℤ = {qn : n ∈ ℤ} is the infinite cyclic subgroup generated by q. The resulting quotient is an elliptic curve with j-invariant j(q) = 1/q + 744 + 196884q + 21493760q2 + … — the same q-expansion as the classical j-function, now convergent p-adically. The map q ↦ j(q) gives a bijection between elements q with |q| < 1 and j-values with |j| > 1 (the non-integral j-invariants). Mumford curves generalize this: any smooth projective curve of genus g ≥ 2 whose Schottky group Γ ⊂ PGL(2, K) is free of rank g and finitely generated gives a Schottky uniformization X ≅ (Ω)/Γ, where Ω is the complement of the limit set in ℘1. The Jacobian of a Mumford curve is a split torus (K✗)g — the p-adic analogue of the Jacobian of a once-punctured Riemann surface.
The Tate curve is the non-Archimedean analogue of the classical uniformization of elliptic curves by the upper half-plane; Mumford curves extend this to higher genus.
Part 4

🔥 Berkovich Spaces and Modern Developments

Modern
Berkovich Spaces, Analytification, and Perfectoid Geometry
Vladimir Berkovich's approach (1990) redefines the underlying set of an affinoid space as the set of all multiplicative seminorms on the affinoid algebra A that are bounded by the sup norm. The resulting space M(A) is locally compact, Hausdorff, connected, and even arcwise connected — a dramatic improvement over the totally disconnected maximal-ideal space. The Berkovich unit disc M(K⟨ζ⟩) has a rich structure of points: type I (classical points from maximal ideals), type II (the Gauss point |∑ anζn|η(0, r) = max |an|rn for r ∈ |K✗|), type III (with radii r ∉ |K✗|), and type IV (limit points of decreasing chains of discs). The Berkovich projective line ℘1, anK is homeomorphic to a planar tree and is simply connected. The analytification functor X ↦ Xan from algebraic varieties to Berkovich analytic spaces is faithful, preserves properness, and for projective varieties X ⊂ ℘N gives Xan as the set of seminorms on the homogeneous coordinate ring. Modern applications: the Fargues-Fontaine curve in p-adic Hodge theory and the local Langlands correspondence; Scholze's perfectoid spaces (2012) with the tilting correspondence relating characteristic-0 and characteristic-p geometries; Huber's adic spaces unifying rigid, Berkovich, and formal geometry by working with pairs of valuation rings.
Berkovich spaces restore topological connectedness to non-Archimedean geometry, unlocking powerful cohomological tools and deep connections to the Langlands program.
