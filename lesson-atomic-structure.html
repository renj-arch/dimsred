Atomic Structure — Bohr Model, Quantum Numbers & Electronic Configuration

Deep dive into Atomic Structure — the Bohr model of the hydrogen atom (quantized orbits, energy levels Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV, the Rydberg formula for spectral lines), the wave-mechanical model (Schrödinger equation, the wavefunction ψ, probability density |ψ|²), the four quantum numbers: principal n (shell size and energy), azimuthal ℓ (subshell shape, ℓ = 0, 1, …, n−1 corresponding to s, p, d, f), magnetic mℓ (−ℓ, …, +ℓ, orbital orientation in space), and spin ms (±½, intrinsic angular momentum). The Aufbau principle (orbitals filled in order of increasing energy: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p), the Pauli exclusion principle (no two electrons in an atom can share the same set of four quantum numbers), Hund's rule (for degenerate orbitals, electrons fill singly with parallel spins first), orbital shapes (s is spherical, p is dumbbell-shaped with three orientations pₓ, p_y, p_z, d has four-lobed cloverleaf shapes, f has complex multi-lobed shapes), the effective nuclear charge Z_eff = Z − S (Slater's rules for shielding), the periodic trends in atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, and electronegativity, and spectroscopic notation (e.g., 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ for sodium).

Part 1

⚛️ The Bohr Model and Quantum Numbers

Quantum Numbers
Bohr's Postulates, Energy Quantization, and the Four Quantum Numbers
Niels Bohr's 1913 model of the hydrogen atom postulated that electrons travel in circular orbits around the nucleus with quantized angular momentum: mvr = nħ where n = 1, 2, 3, … is the principal quantum number. The total energy of the electron in orbit n is Eₙ = −(mₑ e⁴)/(8 ε₀² h²)·(1/n²) = −13.6/n² eV. Transitions between orbits produce spectral lines: ΔE = hν = R_H(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) where R_H = 1.097×10⁷ m⁻¹ is the Rydberg constant. The quantum mechanical model replaced definite orbits with the wavefunction ψ(𝐫), whose square |ψ(𝐫)|² gives the probability density of finding the electron. The principal quantum number n determines the shell energy and average distance from the nucleus. The azimuthal quantum number ℓ = 0, 1, …, n−1 determines the subshell (s, p, d, f) and the orbital angular momentum L = √(ℓ(ℓ+1)) ħ. The magnetic quantum number mℓ = −ℓ, …, +ℓ determines the orientation of the orbital in space (the z-component of angular momentum L_z = mℓ ħ). The spin quantum number ms = ±½ in units of ħ describes the intrinsic spin of the electron, discovered by the Stern-Gerlach experiment and formalized by Pauli. The complete specification of an electronic state requires all four quantum numbers (n, ℓ, mℓ, ms) — no two electrons can share all four (Pauli exclusion principle).
The Bohr model first revealed the quantization of atomic energy levels, but the full quantum-mechanical description uses four quantum numbers (n, ℓ, mℓ, ms) to uniquely specify each electron's state in an atom.
Part 2

📊 Electronic Configuration and the Aufbau Principle

Configuration
Aufbau Principle, Pauli Exclusion, Hund's Rule, and Slater's Rules
The Aufbau principle (German: building up) states that electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy. The filling order is determined by the (n + ℓ) rule: orbitals with lower n + ℓ are filled first; when n + ℓ is equal, the orbital with lower n fills first. The sequence is: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, 5f, 6d, 7p. The Pauli exclusion principle: each orbital can hold at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spins (ms = +½ and ms = −½). Hund's rule: for degenerate orbitals (same n and ℓ, e.g., the three 2p orbitals), electrons first occupy each orbital singly with parallel spins before pairing. This maximizes the total spin (the exchange energy stabilizes the high-spin configuration). Slater's rules provide a method to estimate the screening constant S for an electron: for an ns or np electron, electrons in the same group contribute 0.35 each (except 1s where 0.30), electrons in the n−1 shell contribute 0.85, and electrons in n−2 or lower contribute 1.00. The effective nuclear charge Z_eff = Z − S. This explains periodic trends: atomic radius increases down a group (adding shells) and decreases across a period (increasing Z_eff pulls electrons inward). Ionization energy increases across a period and decreases down a group. Electron affinity is generally exothermic across the p-block (except for filled and half-filled subshells).
The Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion principle, and Hund's rule together determine how electrons fill orbitals, which in turn determines the chemical properties and periodic trends of elements.
Part 3

🌐 Orbital Shapes and Energy Levels

Orbitals
s, p, d, f Orbital Shapes, Nodal Surfaces, and Energy Splitting
s orbitals (ℓ = 0) are spherically symmetric. The 1s orbital has no radial nodes; the 2s orbital has one spherical radial node; the 3s has two radial nodes. The number of radial nodes is n − ℓ − 1. p orbitals (ℓ = 1) are dumbbell-shaped with two lobes and one angular node (a nodal plane through the nucleus). There are three p orbitals: pₓ, p_y, p_z, each oriented along a Cartesian axis. The number of angular nodes equals ℓ. d orbitals (ℓ = 2) have four-lobed cloverleaf shapes (except d_{z²}, which has two lobes along the z-axis with a doughnut-shaped ring in the xy-plane). The five d orbitals are: d_{xy}, d_{xz}, d_{yz}, d_{x²−y²}, and d_{z²}. Each d orbital has two angular nodes. f orbitals (ℓ = 3) have complex multi-lobed shapes with three angular nodes; there are seven f orbitals: f_{z³}, f_{xz²}, f_{yz²}, f_{xyz}, f_{z(x²−y²)}, f_{x(x²−3y²)}, f_{y(3x²−y²)}. In multi-electron atoms, the energy of orbitals depends on both n and ℓ due to penetration and shielding: for a given n, energy order is s < p < d < f because s electrons penetrate closer to the nucleus and experience less shielding. The crystal field theory describes how the d-orbital energies split when a transition metal ion is placed in an octahedral field: the e_g set (d_{z²}, d_{x²−y²}) is destabilized and the t_{2g} set (d_{xy}, d_{xz}, d_{yz}) is stabilized, with splitting Δ_O = 10 Dq. In tetrahedral fields, the splitting is reversed (e set lower, t₂ set higher) with Δ_tet = 4/9 Δ_O.
The shapes of atomic orbitals (s spherical, p dumbbell, d cloverleaf, f complex) and their nodal structure are direct consequences of the angular part of the wavefunction, given by the spherical harmonics.
Part 4

🧮 Periodic Trends and Spectroscopic Notation

Periodicity
Atomic Radius, Ionization Energy, Electron Affinity, Electronegativity, and Spectroscopic Notation
Atomic radius: half the distance between adjacent nuclei in a diatomic molecule (or the van der Waals radius for non-bonded atoms). Radius increases down a group (n increases, larger shells) and decreases across a period (Z_eff increases, pulling electrons inward). Ionization energy (IE): the energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from a gaseous atom. IE increases across a period and decreases down a group. Successive IEs increase sharply after removing all valence electrons (e.g., Na: 496, 4562, 6912 kJ/mol). The electron affinity (EA): the energy change when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom. Most exothermic for halogens (Cl: −349 kJ/mol), endothermic for noble gases and group 2 metals. Electronegativity (Pauling scale): a measure of an atom's ability to attract bonding electrons. Ranges from 0.79 (Fr) to 3.98 (F). The difference in electronegativity between bonded atoms determines bond polarity: Δχ > 1.7 generally yields ionic bonds, Δχ < 0.4 yields nonpolar covalent bonds, intermediate yields polar covalent. Spectroscopic notation (or electron configuration notation) summarizes the distribution of electrons in atomic orbitals. Examples: H: 1s¹, He: 1s², Li: 1s² 2s¹, C: 1s² 2s² 2p², Ne: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, Na: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹, Fe: [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s², Br: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵. The noble gas core (e.g., [He], [Ne], [Ar]) is used as shorthand. Exceptional configurations arise for chromium (Cr: [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ rather than 3d⁴ 4s²) and copper (Cu: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹) due to the extra stability of half-filled and fully filled d subshells.
Periodic trends in atomic radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, and electronegativity are rationalized by effective nuclear charge and the principal quantum number; spectroscopic notation provides a compact description of an atom's electron configuration.
