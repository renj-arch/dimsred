The Current Affairs module in the General Ability Test (Paper 2) is a critical component that assesses your awareness of the dynamic world around you, ranging from recent national developments to global geopolitical shifts. Unlike static subjects like Mathematics or Physics, this section requires you to synthesize information about defence news, prestigious awards, and major sporting events into coherent knowledge. This lesson will equip you with the strategies to recall these facts accurately under exam pressure, ensuring you can answer questions on specific dates, names, and locations without hesitation.

National Events

In the context of General Ability tests, "National Events" refers to significant occurrences within a country that have political, social, or economic impact. The examiner typically asks for the date of occurrence, the location, or the key figure associated with the event.

Technique: The Chronological Anchor
Question: In which year was the 'Bharat Ratna' award first instituted in India? A) 1954 B) 1950 C) 1962 D) 1952
Solution: The Bharat Ratna was established by the Government of India in 1954. It is important to note that while the award exists today, it was not given immediately upon independence (1947). The first recipients were C. Rajagopalachari and S. Radhakrishnan. Therefore, option A is correct.
Technique: The Contextual Link
Question: Which of the following events was NOT held in New Delhi during the last decade? A) Republic Day Parade B) G20 Summit (recently) C) Commonwealth Games D) India International Trade Fair
Solution: The Republic Day Parade and the India International Trade Fair are annual events held in New Delhi. While the G20 Summit was recently hosted by India, it took place in Goa. The Commonwealth Games were last held in India in 1982 (New Delhi). Thus, the Commonwealth Games is the correct answer for an event not held in New Delhi in the recent decade.

Defence News

Defence news encompasses updates on military hardware acquisitions, strategic partnerships, and significant exercises conducted by nations. The examiner often tests your knowledge of indigenous weapons systems or major international joint drills.

Technique: The Acronym Breakdown
Question: What does the acronym 'DRDO' stand for in Indian defence context? A) Defence Research and Development Organization B) Department of Rural Development Office C) Delhi Railway Development Order D) Defence Revenue Data Online
Solution: DRDO stands for Defence Research and Development Organization. It is the premier R&D organization in India, responsible for designing and developing weapon systems like the Akash missile and BrahMos. Option A is the correct expansion of the acronym.
Technique: The Exercise Association
Question: Which multinational naval exercise involves India, Japan, and the United States? A) Malabar B) Garuda C) Cobra Gold D) Caribou
Solution: The Malabar exercise is a trilateral naval drill involving India, Japan, and the USA. It focuses on anti-submarine warfare and air-sea cooperation. Garuda is an Indonesian exercise, Cobra Gold is Thai-led, and Caribou is Canadian. Therefore, option A is correct.

Awards

Awards in this context refer to prestigious recognitions given for contributions in science, arts, literature, or sports. The examiner usually asks about the year of establishment, the criteria for selection, or the latest recipient.

Technique: The Category Match
Question: Which award is given by the Government of India for contributions in the field of literature and education? A) Dadasaheb Phalke Award B) Jnanpith Award C) Ramon Magsaysay Award D) Bharat Ratna
Solution: The Jnanpith Award is the highest literary award in India, given for outstanding contribution to literature in any of the scheduled languages. The Dadasaheb Phalke Award is for cinema, Ramon Magsaysay is an international award, and Bharat Ratna covers all fields. Option B is correct.
Technique: The Recipient Recall
Question: Who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2019 for contributions to black hole physics? A) Roger Penrose and Andrea Ghez B) James Peebles and Michel Mayor C) William Nordhaus and Paul Krugman D) Tu Youyou and William C. Campbell
Solution: The 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Roger Penrose, Reinhard Genzel, and Andrea Ghez for discoveries related to black holes. Option A contains the correct names associated with this specific field and year.

Sports

Sporting news covers major tournaments, record-breaking performances, and changes in governing bodies. The examiner focuses on recent winners of global championships or significant rule changes in popular sports like Cricket, Football, and Badminton.

Technique: The Tournament Winner
Question: Which country won the FIFA World Cup in 2022? A) France B) Argentina C) Brazil D) Germany
Solution: The 2022 FIFA World Cup was held in Qatar. Argentina defeated France in the final match to win their third title. Therefore, option B is correct.
Technique: The Record Holder
Question: Who holds the record for the most Olympic Gold Medals won by an individual athlete? A) Usain Bolt B) Michael Phelps C) Carl Lewis D) swimmer Mark Spitz
Solution: Michael Phelps, an American swimmer, holds the record with 23 Olympic gold medals. While Mark Spitz won 7 in one Olympics, and Usain Bolt won 8 total, Phelps has the highest lifetime count. Option B is correct.

Common Traps

Students often fall into these specific pitfalls when answering Current Affairs questions:

  • Confusing Similar Names: For example, confusing 'Bharat Ratna' (civilian award) with 'Param Vir Chakra' (military gallantry award).
  • Misremembering Dates: Assuming an event happened in the same year as a similar-sounding historical event. Always verify the specific year.
  • Location Errors: Knowing the name of a summit but forgetting if it was held in New Delhi, Mumbai, or Goa.
  • Assumption Bias: Assuming that the most famous person always wins the award, ignoring recent winners who might be less known to the general public.

Memory Tricks

To retain this vast amount of information, use these shortcuts and mnemonics:

  • The "Firsts" List: Create a mental list of the first recipients of major awards (e.g., first Bharat Ratna in 1954). This anchors the date to the person.
  • Acronym Visualization: When learning defence acronyms like DRDO or HAL, visualize what each letter stands for physically. Imagine a factory (HAL) building planes and a lab (DRDO) testing missiles.
  • Tournament Geography: Associate the host city with the tournament. For example, link 'Qatar' directly to '2022 World Cup' and 'Japan' to '2019 Cricket WC'.
  • Categorization: Group awards by field (Science, Arts, Literature) rather than memorizing them randomly. This reduces cognitive load during the exam.
Exam-Day Strategy for Current Affairs
On the day of the exam, do not panic if you forget a specific name or date. Use the process of elimination: eliminate options that are factually impossible (e.g., an award given before it was established). If you know the approximate year, choose the option closest to it. Always read the question carefully to see if it asks for the "first" recipient or the "latest" one, as this changes the answer entirely. Stay calm and trust your general knowledge base.

Summit Diplomacy and Multilateral Outcomes

Summit diplomacy is the primary vehicle for shaping global norms, and examiners test the ability to link a summit’s theme, host, and concrete outcomes. The G20 New Delhi Summit (September 2023) adopted the “New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration” emphasizing inclusive growth, digital public infrastructure, and climate finance. The 2023 SCO summit, hosted by India, emphasized counter‑terrorism cooperation and regional connectivity, with a joint statement endorsing the SCO Regional Anti‑Terrorist Structure (RATS). The 2023 BRICS summit in Johannesburg announced the expansion to include Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE, effective 2024, altering the bloc’s economic weight. The 2023 ASEAN‑India Commemorative Summit in Jakarta upgraded the strategic partnership to a “Comprehensive Strategic Partnership” and launched the “ASEAN‑India Plan of Action (2024‑2028)”. Questions may ask which summit first adopted a declaration on digital public infrastructure; the G20 New Delhi Summit is the key reference. Candidates must also distinguish between “joint statements” (non‑binding political commitments) and “treaties” (legally binding), a nuance that appears in match‑the‑following items.

Defence Procurement and Indigenisation Milestones

India’s defence acquisition framework shifted from the Defence Procurement Procedure (DPP) 2016 to the Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020, introducing the “Make‑I”, “Make‑II”, and “Strategic Partnership” categories to accelerate indigenous production. The 2021 contract for 83 Light Combat Aircraft (LCA) Tejas Mk‑1A marked the largest single order for an indigenous fighter. The 2022 commissioning of INS Vikrant (IAC‑1), India’s first domestically built aircraft carrier, demonstrated the “Atmanirbhar Bharat” trajectory in naval shipbuilding. The approval of the “Future Ready Combat Vehicle” (FRCV) programme under the Strategic Partnership model aims to replace the T‑72 fleet with an indigenous platform. The Project‑75I submarine programme, involving shortlisted foreign OEMs for technology transfer, illustrates the hybrid import‑plus‑transfer approach. Examiners frequently ask for the chronological order of these milestones or the specific DAP category each programme falls under. A typical trap is confusing the “Make‑I” (government‑funded prototype) with “Make‑II” (industry‑funded prototype) – for example, misclassifying a production contract like Tejas Mk‑1A under a prototype category instead of its correct “Buy (Indian‑IDDM)” category, while the DRDO‑led “Abhyas” target drone is a Make‑I programme.

Technique: Chronology‑Category Matching
Arrange the following defence programmes in the order they were formally approved/contracted, and identify the DAP category for each: (1) INS Vikrant commissioning, (2) Tejas Mk‑1A contract (83 aircraft), (3) FRCV Strategic Partnership approval, (4) Project‑75I RFP issuance.
Correct chronological order: (1) Tejas Mk‑1A contract signed February 2021; (2) INS Vikrant commissioned September 2022; (3) FRCV approval (2023); (4) Project‑75I RFP issued (2023). Categories: Tejas Mk‑1A – Buy (Indian‑IDDM); INS Vikrant – Indigenous Aircraft Carrier programme (not a DAP category); FRCV – Strategic Partnership; Project‑75I – Strategic Partnership. The key is to note that the Tejas order predates the carrier’s commissioning, and both FRCV and Project‑75I fall under Strategic Partnership.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Defence Procurement
Create a two‑column cheat sheet: left column = programme name, right column = DAP category and year. Memorise only the flagship programmes (Tejas Mk‑1A, INS Vikrant, FRCV, Project‑75I, AMCA). In the exam, eliminate any option that pairs a programme with the wrong category (e.g., assigning a production contract to a prototype category). If a question asks for “the first indigenous aircraft carrier”, answer INS Vikrant; the carrier’s construction began before DAP 2020 but remains the flagship indigenous achievement cited in exams.

Science and Technology Awards Landscape

The Indian science award ecosystem spans national honours, academy fellowships, and private‑sector prizes. The Shanti Swarup Bhatnagar Prize (SSB) – instituted 1958 – recognises outstanding research in seven disciplines for scientists below 45 years; recent awardees include leading researchers in Physical and Biological Sciences. The Infosys Prize (established 2008) covers six categories and carries a monetary award of USD 100,000; recent laureates feature distinguished scientists across Engineering, Computer Science, and other fields. The Vigyan Ratna (proposed 2023, not yet operational) aims to be the highest national science honour, distinct from the Padma awards which are civilian. The Ramanujan Fellowship targets early‑career researchers returning to India; the J.C. Bose Fellowship supports senior scientists with proven track records. Examiners test the ability to match an award to its sponsoring body, eligibility age, and monetary value. A common confusion is between the SSB (government, age‑capped) and the Infosys Prize (private, no age cap). Another trap is assuming the “Vigyan Ratna” has already been conferred – it remains a proposal as of 2024.

Major Sporting Governance Reforms

Global sports governance has undergone structural reforms that appear in current‑affairs questions. The International Cricket Council (ICC) introduced a new “Future Tours Programme” (FTP) cycle 2023‑2027, expanding the World Test Championship to nine teams and adding a dedicated women’s FTP. FIFA’s governance reforms (2023) mandated independent ethics committees, term limits for council members, and a transparent bidding process for the 2030 World Cup, awarded jointly to Spain, Portugal, Morocco, Uruguay, Argentina, and Paraguay. The Badminton World Federation (BWF) revised its ranking system in 2022 to weight Grade 1 tournaments higher, affecting Olympic qualification pathways. The International Olympic Committee (IOC) adopted the “Olympic Agenda 2020+5” in 2021, emphasizing sustainability, gender equality, and digital engagement; the 2024 Paris Games will be the first with full gender parity in athlete quotas. Questions often ask which body introduced a specific reform and the year; candidates must differentiate between ICC’s FTP (cricket‑specific) and FIFA’s bidding transparency (football‑wide).

Data‑Driven Current Affairs Analysis Techniques

High‑scoring aspirants move beyond rote memorisation by applying a systematic data‑capture framework. The “PIB‑PRS‑Sansad TV” triangle – Press Information Bureau releases, PRS Legislative Research summaries, and Sansad TV debates – provides primary, legislative, and analytical layers for any policy event. For defence, the “MoD‑DRDO‑DDP” chain (Ministry of Defence press notes, DRDO technology bulletins, Defence Production Department procurement notices) yields verifiable procurement milestones. In sports, the “IOC‑IF‑NADO” hierarchy (International Olympic Committee communications, International Federation circulars, National Anti‑Doping Organisation bulletins) tracks governance changes and doping cases. A practical workflow: (1) Identify the event keyword (e.g., “Project‑75I”). (2) Search the PIB archive for the official press release date. (3) Cross‑check with PRS for any parliamentary committee report. (4) Note the DAP category from the MoD procurement notice. (5) Record in a structured spreadsheet with columns: Event, Date, Authority, Category, Outcome. This method reduces reliance on memory and enables quick elimination of options that contradict the primary source. Examiners increasingly frame questions as “According to the PIB release dated …, which category was assigned?” – a direct test of source‑based recall.

Advanced Common Traps

  • Confusing the G20 “New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration” with the earlier “Bali Declaration” – they address different thematic pillars.
  • Misclassifying the Tejas Mk‑1A production order under a prototype category (Make‑I or Make‑II) rather than the correct Buy (Indian‑IDDM) category.
  • Assuming the Vigyan Ratna has been awarded; it remains a proposal as of 2024.
  • Mixing ICC’s FTP reforms with FIFA’s governance reforms; each belongs to a distinct sport governing body.
  • Treating a joint statement from a summit as a legally binding treaty.
  • Using secondary news summaries as primary evidence for procurement dates instead of the MoD press release.
  • Overlooking the age‑cap criterion for the Shanti Swarup Bhatnagar Prize when matching awardees.

Climate Finance and Green Bond Framework

India’s climate finance architecture rests on three pillars: budgetary allocations, multilateral climate funds, and market‑based instruments such as green bonds. The Union Budget 2023‑24 allocated ₹35,000 crore for energy transition priorities, including the National Green Hydrogen Mission (₹19,744 crore) and the PLI scheme for high‑efficiency solar PV modules (₹24,000 crore). The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) issued the Sovereign Green Bond Framework in November 2022, aligning with the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) Green Bond Principles. The first tranche of ₹8,000 crore was auctioned in January 2023, followed by a second tranche of ₹8,000 crore in July 2023. Proceeds are ring‑fenced for projects in renewable energy, energy efficiency, pollution prevention, sustainable agriculture, and clean transportation.

Eligibility and Reporting

  • Projects must meet the taxonomy defined in the framework – a positive list of activities and a negative list excluding fossil‑fuel extraction.
  • Annual allocation and impact reports are published by the Ministry of Finance, verified by an external reviewer (currently the Climate Bonds Initiative).
  • The framework mandates a “use‑of‑proceeds” disclosure within 12 months of issuance and a post‑issuance impact assessment after 24 months.

Multilateral Complement

India accesses the Green Climate Fund (GCF) and the Global Environment Facility (GEF) for adaptation projects in the Himalayan states and coastal resilience in Odisha and West Bengal. As of March 2024, India has secured over USD 1 billion in GCF approvals. The National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC) channels central assistance to state‑level projects, with a corpus of ₹5,000 crore for the 2021‑26 cycle.

Examination Focus

Questions test the distinction between sovereign green bonds and corporate green bonds, the role of the external reviewer, and the mapping of budgetary heads to the framework’s project categories. A typical distractor conflates the PLI scheme for solar modules with the green bond proceeds, which are separate financing streams.

National Cyber Security Strategy and Institutional Architecture

The draft National Cyber Security Strategy (NCSS) 2020, formulated by the National Security Council Secretariat (NSCS), outlines a 2020‑2025 roadmap built on five pillars: governance, resilience, capacity building, cooperation, and indigenous technology development. The strategy designates the National Cyber Coordination Centre (NCCC) as the apex operational body for situational awareness and incident response, while the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT‑In) remains the statutory nodal agency under the Information Technology Act, 2000.

Legislative Enhancements

The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 impose due‑diligence obligations on significant social media intermediaries, including the appointment of a Chief Compliance Officer resident in India. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) introduces a consent‑based framework for personal data processing and establishes the Data Protection Board of India for adjudication. Both statutes intersect with the NCSS by mandating breach notification timelines (as prescribed under the DPDP Act, 6 hours for critical sectors under CERT‑In directions).

Capacity Building and Indigenous Tools

The Cyber Surakshit Bharat initiative trains government officials across ministries, while the Cyber Swachhta Kendra provides free botnet cleaning tools for end‑users. The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) has delivered the “Netra” network traffic analysis platform and the “Vajra” intrusion detection system for deployment in critical infrastructure.

Examination Focus

Examiners probe the hierarchy: NCCC for strategic coordination, CERT‑In for operational incident handling, NCIIPC for sector‑specific protection. A common trap is to attribute breach notification timelines incorrectly (e.g., citing 72 hours for CERT‑In), or to state that the NCSS 2020 has been superseded by a 2023 version (no revised strategy has been publicly released as of 2024).

Technique: Mapping Overlapping Mandates
A question asks: “Which agency is the statutory nodal point for cyber incident reporting under the IT Act, and which body provides strategic coordination at the national level?”
Statutory nodal agency: CERT‑In (Section 70B, IT Act, 2000). Strategic coordination: National Cyber Coordination Centre (NCCC) under NSCS. The NCIIPC protects critical information infrastructure but does not serve as the national reporting hub. The Defence Cyber Agency operates exclusively within the military domain.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Create a one‑page matrix of cyber institutions (name, parent ministry, mandate, key legal basis). When a question cites a timeline (e.g., 6‑hour vs prescribed DPDP period), match it to the correct statute: CERT‑In directions for critical sectors (6 h) and DPDP Act for personal data breaches. This eliminates the most frequent confusion.

Digital Public Infrastructure: UPI, Aadhaar, DigiLocker, and Governance

India’s Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) stack comprises three foundational layers: identity (Aadhaar), payments (Unified Payments Interface – UPI), and data exchange (DigiLocker and the Account Aggregator framework). Aadhaar, enacted under the Aadhaar (Targeted Delivery of Financial and Other Subsidies, Benefits and Services) Act, 2016, provides a 12‑digit unique identity to over 1.35 billion residents as of March 2024. The Supreme Court’s 2018 judgment (Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India) upheld the Act’s constitutionality while striking down mandatory linking for private services.

UPI Architecture and Growth

UPI, developed by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), operates on a four‑party model (payer PSP, payee PSP, remitter bank, beneficiary bank) with a single‑click, interoperable QR code. Transaction volume crossed 10 billion in August 2023, and value exceeded ₹15 lakh crore in a single month (December 2023). The UPI 2.0 upgrade (2018) introduced mandate‑based recurring payments, invoice‑in‑the‑box, and signed intent for higher security.

DigiLocker and Account Aggregator

DigiLocker, launched in 2015 under the Digital India programme, offers a cloud‑based repository for verified documents (driving licence, marksheets, insurance policies). As of 2024, over 150 million users have linked more than 5 billion documents. The Account Aggregator (AA) framework, regulated by the Reserve Bank of India, enables consent‑based sharing of financial data across banks, insurers, and fintechs. Eight licensed AAs (including CAMS, Finvu, and OneMoney) were operational by early 2024.

Governance and Interoperability

The Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC) leverages the DPI stack to create an open e‑commerce protocol, while the National Health Stack (NHS) uses Aadhaar‑based verification and AA‑enabled health‑record exchange. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) coordinates standards through the National e‑Governance Division (NeGD). Interoperability is enforced via open APIs (e.g., UPI’s /pay, /collect, /mandate endpoints) and the Data Empowerment and Protection Architecture (DEPA) for consent management.

Examination Focus

Questions test the legal basis of each layer (Aadhaar Act, Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007 for UPI, IT Act for DigiLocker), the role of NPCI as a not‑for‑profit company under Section 8 of the Companies Act, and the distinction between the AA framework (financial data) and DEPA (cross‑sector consent). A frequent distractor claims UPI is operated by the RBI; in reality, NPCI runs UPI under RBI’s regulatory oversight.

Energy Transition: Renewable Capacity Targets, Green Hydrogen Mission, and Grid Integration

India’s updated Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) under the Paris Agreement commits to 500 GW of non‑fossil fuel capacity by 2030 and net‑zero emissions by 2070. As of March 2024, installed renewable capacity stands at approximately 180 GW (solar ≈ 70 GW, wind ≈ 44 GW, large hydro ≈ 46 GW, biomass ≈ 10 GW, small hydro ≈ 5 GW). The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE) drives the trajectory through competitive bidding, PLI schemes, and the Green Energy Corridor (GEC) for transmission.

National Green Hydrogen Mission

Approved in January 2023 with an outlay of ₹19,744 crore, the mission targets 5 million metric tonnes per annum (MMTPA) of green hydrogen production by 2030. It comprises two sub‑schemes: the Strategic Interventions for Green Hydrogen Transition (SIGHT) programme for electrolyser manufacturing and hydrogen hubs, and a demand‑creation component for refineries, fertiliser plants, and steel mills. The first electrolyser manufacturing PLI tranche (₹4,500 crore) was awarded in June 2023 to three consortia.

Grid Integration and Storage

The Green Energy Corridor‑II (GEC‑II), sanctioned in 2022, adds 10,750 km of intra‑state transmission lines and 27,500 MVA of substation capacity across seven renewable‑rich states. Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) are being procured through viability‑gap funding; a 1 GW/4 GWh tender was floated by Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI) in late 2023. Pumped hydro storage projects (e.g., at Tehri and Kadamparai) complement lithium‑ion storage for long‑duration balancing.

Policy Instruments

  • Renewable Purchase Obligation (RPO) trajectory: 21 % solar, 10 % wind, 3 % other non‑fossil by 2029‑30 for obligated entities.
  • Waiver of inter‑state transmission charges for solar and wind projects commissioned before June 2025.
  • Production Linked Incentive for high‑efficiency solar PV modules (₹24,000 crore) and advanced chemistry cell batteries (₹18,100 crore).

Examination Focus

Expect questions on the distinction between the Green Hydrogen Mission’s supply‑side (SIGHT) and demand‑side components, the RPO trajectory percentages, and the role of GEC‑II versus the original GEC‑I (inter‑state vs intra‑state). A common trap is to attribute the 500 GW target solely to solar; the target includes wind, hydro, biomass, and nuclear.

International Trade Agreements: India‑UAE CEPA, India‑Australia ECTA, and RCEP Decision

India’s recent trade diplomacy reflects a shift toward comprehensive economic partnership agreements (CEPAs) with strategic partners while opting out of the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP). The India‑UAE Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA), signed in February 2022 and effective from May 2022, eliminates tariffs on 90 % of tariff lines within five years, includes a services chapter with mutual recognition of professional qualifications, and establishes a digital trade annex covering e‑signatures and data flows.

India‑Australia Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement (ECTA)

ECTA, signed in April 2022 and operational from December 2022, provides duty‑free access for 96 % of Indian exports to Australia (by value) and 85 % of Australian exports to India. Key sectors: textiles, leather, gems and jewellery, and pharmaceuticals from India; coal, critical minerals (lithium, cobalt), and wine from Australia. The agreement contains a labour‑mobility annex facilitating temporary entry for skilled professionals and a chapter on government procurement.

RCEP Decision and Rationale

India withdrew from RCEP negotiations in November 2019, citing inadequate market access for services, insufficient protection against import surges (especially from China), and concerns over rules of origin that could enable circumvention. The decision is framed around the “atmanirbhar” (self‑reliant) strategy and the need to safeguard domestic manufacturing, particularly in dairy, textiles, and electronics.

Semiconductor Mission and Design‑Linked Incentive Scheme

Policy Genesis and Institutional Architecture

The India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) was launched in December 2021 under the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) with a corpus of ₹76,000 crore to catalyse a full‑stack semiconductor ecosystem. The mission is governed by a high‑level advisory committee chaired by the Minister of Electronics and IT, with representatives from the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research, the Department of Space, and DRDO. The ISM operates through three verticals: (1) fabrication (fab) and assembly, testing, marking and packaging (ATMP) facilities; (2) compound semiconductor and silicon photonics; (3) design and innovation support.

Design‑Linked Incentive (DLI) Scheme Mechanics

The DLI scheme, notified in April 2022, targets domestic and global companies engaged in semiconductor design for integrated circuits (ICs), chipsets, system‑on‑chip (SoC), and intellectual property (IP) cores. Eligible applicants receive fiscal support of up to 50 % of eligible expenditure, capped at ₹15 crore per project for a maximum of three years. The scheme also provides product deployment linked incentives of 6 % to 4 % of net sales over five years, subject to a ceiling of ₹30 crore per applicant. A mandatory condition is that the design must be owned by an Indian entity or a joint venture with at least 51 % Indian equity.

Fab and ATMP Incentive Structure

For greenfield fab projects, the central government offers a uniform 50 % capital expenditure (CAPEX) support on a pari‑passu basis with state governments, which may contribute an additional 25 % of project cost. ATMP units receive 50 % CAPEX support from the centre and up to 25 % from states. The scheme covers silicon CMOS, compound semiconductors (GaN, SiC), and silicon photonics. As of March 2024, three fab proposals — Tata Electronics (with PSMC), CG Power (with Renesas), and a consortium led by Vedanta‑Foxconn — have received in‑principle approval, while multiple ATMP proposals are under evaluation.

Strategic Significance and Examination Angles

Questions often test the distinction between the ISM’s capital support for manufacturing versus the DLI’s operational expenditure (OPEX) focus for design. Another frequent angle is the equity threshold for DLI eligibility (51 % Indian ownership) and the cap on product deployment incentives (₹30 crore). The interplay between central and state fiscal contributions for fab projects is also a recurring trap.

National Logistics Policy and PM Gati Shakti Integration

Policy Framework and Objectives

The National Logistics Policy (NLP) was unveiled in September 2022 with the aim of reducing logistics cost from 13‑14 % of GDP to the global benchmark of 8 % by 2030. The policy rests on four pillars: (1) Integration of Digital Systems (IDS) — a unified logistics interface platform (ULIP) connecting 30+ ministries; (2) Standardisation of physical assets and processes; (3) Human resource development and skilling; (4) State‑level logistics action plans. The NLP is operationalised through the PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan (NMP), launched in October 2021, which provides a GIS‑based multimodal planning tool covering 1,500+ data layers across railways, highways, ports, airports, and inland waterways.

Institutional Coordination Mechanism

A three‑tier governance structure steers implementation: (i) Empowered Group of Secretaries (EGoS) chaired by the Cabinet Secretary for inter‑ministerial clearances; (ii) Network Planning Group (NPG) comprising heads of infrastructure ministries for project prioritisation; (iii) Technical Support Unit (TSU) housed in DPIIT for data analytics and monitoring. States are required to constitute State Logistics Coordination Committees (SLCCs) and prepare State Logistics Plans aligned with the NMP.

Key Performance Indicators and Monitoring

The Logistics Performance Index (LPI) dashboard, integrated with ULIP, tracks metrics such as average dwell time at ports, freight train speed, and last‑mile delivery cost. The NLP targets a 10 % reduction in average port dwell time and a 15 % increase in rail freight share by 2025. The Unified Logistics Interface Platform (ULIP) APIs enable real‑time visibility of container movement, e‑way bill integration, and electronic proof of delivery.

Examination Focus Areas

Examiners frequently ask to differentiate the NLP (policy document) from PM Gati Shakti (planning and execution platform). The role of ULIP as the digital backbone, the three‑tier governance (EGoS, NPG, TSU), and the specific cost‑reduction target (8 % of GDP by 2030) are high‑yield facts. A common distractor conflates the NLP’s 2030 horizon with the NMP’s 2024‑25 project completion milestones.

Technique: Distinguishing Policy vs Platform
A question states: “The National Logistics Policy provides a GIS‑based multimodal planning tool for infrastructure projects.” Identify the error and supply the correct statement.
The error lies in attributing the GIS‑based planning tool to the NLP. The National Logistics Policy is a strategic document outlining cost‑reduction targets and reform pillars. The GIS‑based multimodal planning tool is the PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan. Correct statement: “The PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan provides a GIS‑based multimodal planning tool, while the National Logistics Policy sets the target of reducing logistics cost to 8 % of GDP by 2030.”
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mixes NLP and PM Gati Shakti, first identify whether the statement describes a target/principle (NLP) or a technology/implementation mechanism (Gati Shakti). Write the two entities side‑by‑side on your rough sheet to avoid conflation.

Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023: Key Provisions and Implementation Timeline

Legislative Journey and Commencement

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP Act) received Presidential assent on 11 August 2023. The central government notified the commencement of substantive provisions on 1 January 2024, while the Data Protection Board (DPB) and the appellate tribunal were constituted by March 2024. The Act replaces the Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices) Rules, 2011, and establishes a consent‑centric framework for processing digital personal data within India and extraterritorially when linked to offering goods or services to Indian data principals.

Core Principles and Data Principal Rights

The Act enumerates principles including lawfulness, fairness, transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality, and accountability. Data principals enjoy rights to access, correction, erasure, grievance redressal, and nomination of a consent manager. Notably, the right to data portability and the right to be forgotten, present in earlier drafts, were omitted in the final version.

Obligations of Data Fiduciaries and Significant Data Fiduciaries

Every data fiduciary must appoint a Data Protection Officer (DPO) and publish a privacy notice in clear language. Significant Data Fiduciaries (SDFs) — designated by the central government based on volume, sensitivity, and risk — are required to undertake Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs), periodic audits, and appoint an independent data auditor. The Act prescribes a penalty ceiling of ₹250 crore per instance for non‑compliance, with the DPB empowered to impose penalties after an inquiry.

Cross‑Border Data Transfers and Exemptions

Cross‑border transfers are permitted to countries or territories notified by the central government as providing an adequate level of protection. In the absence of such notification, transfers may occur under standard contractual clauses approved by the DPB. The Act exempts processing for sovereignty, security, public order, and certain research, archival, or statistical purposes, provided safeguards are met.

Examination Relevance

Key exam points include the distinction between data fiduciary and significant data fiduciary obligations, the penalty cap (₹250 crore), the absence of data portability and right to be forgotten, and the role of the Data Protection Board as an adjudicatory body rather than a regulator. The timeline — assent August 2023, commencement January 2024 — is a frequent factual query.

India’s Maritime Security Architecture: Information Fusion Centre and Coastal Radar Network

Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region (IFC‑IOR)

Established in December 2018 at Gurugram under the Indian Navy, the IFC‑IOR serves as a multinational maritime domain awareness hub. It hosts liaison officers from 21 partner nations and international organisations, facilitating real‑time sharing of white‑shipping (commercial vessel) data, incident reporting, and collaborative response to piracy, smuggling, and illegal fishing. The centre integrates inputs from the Automatic Identification System (AIS), Long‑Range Identification and Tracking (LRIT), and coastal radar feeds.

Coastal Radar Chain and National Command Control Communication Intelligence (NC3I) Network

The coastal radar network comprises 46 static radar stations (including 10 high‑frequency over‑the‑horizon radars) covering the mainland coastline, Andaman & Nicobar, and Lakshadweep. These radars feed the NC3I network, a secure, satellite‑linked communication backbone connecting the Navy, Coast Guard, and coastal state marine police. The NC3I enables fused track correlation, automated anomaly detection, and rapid dissemination of actionable intelligence to operational commanders.

White‑Shipping Agreements and Information Sharing Protocols

India has concluded white‑shipping agreements with 36 countries and three multilateral constructs (Indian Ocean Naval Symposium, Indian Ocean Commission, and BIMSTEC). Under these agreements, merchant vessel position reports are exchanged in near‑real time via the IFC‑IOR. The protocols specify data standards (AIS Class‑A, LRIT), update frequency (minimum 6‑minute intervals), and confidentiality clauses restricting military use of commercial data.

Strategic Outcomes and Exam Pointers

The architecture has significantly reduced response times to maritime incidents in the Indian Ocean. Examination questions often test the distinction between the IFC‑IOR (multinational fusion centre) and the NC3I (national command network), the number of coastal radar stations (46), and the nature of white‑shipping agreements (commercial vessel data only). A typical trap is to attribute military‑grade surveillance capabilities to the white‑shipping feed.

India’s Nuclear Energy Expansion: Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors and Fast Breeder Programme

Pressurised Heavy Water Reactor (PHWR) Fleet and 700 MWe Series

India’s indigenous PHWR programme, operated by the Nuclear Power Corporation of India Limited (NPCIL), currently comprises 22 reactors with a combined capacity of 6,780 MWe. The 700 MWe PHWR design, first commissioned at Kakrapar Unit‑3 (January 2021) and Kakrapar Unit‑4 (March 2024), incorporates enhanced safety features: passive decay heat removal, steel‑lined containment, and higher fuel burn‑up. Four additional 700 MWe units are under construction at Rawatbhata (Rajasthan) and Gorakhpur (Haryana), targeting commissioning by 2027‑28.

Fast Breeder Reactor (FBR) and the Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR)

The PFBR, a 500 MWe sodium‑cooled fast breeder reactor located at Kalpakkam, is the cornerstone of India’s three‑stage nuclear programme. The reactor achieved first criticality in September 2024 and is slated for commercial operation by 2025. The FBR breeds fissile plutonium‑239 from uranium‑238, extending the fuel resource base. The second stage also includes the Fast Reactor Fuel Cycle Facility (FRFCF) for reprocessing and fuel fabrication, currently under commissioning.

Thorium Utilisation and Advanced Heavy Water Reactor (AHWR)

The third stage aims at commercial thorium utilisation. The 300 MWe AHWR design, validated by the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC), uses a thorium‑plutonium mixed oxide fuel and light water coolant with heavy water moderator. A technology demonstration unit is planned for the 2030s, contingent on the successful closure of the second stage fuel cycle.

Regulatory Oversight and International Cooperation

The Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB) licenses all nuclear facilities, while the Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) sets policy. India’s civil nuclear cooperation agreements (with the United States, France, Russia, Canada, Australia, and others) facilitate uranium supply, reactor technology transfer, and joint safety research. The 2023 amendment to the Atomic Energy Act enables joint ventures between NPCIL and public sector undertakings for reactor construction, accelerating capacity addition.

Examination Focus

High‑yield facts: current PHWR count (22), 700 MWe unit commissioning dates, PFBR capacity (500 MWe) and criticality milestone, three‑stage programme logic (U‑235 → Pu‑239 → Th‑232), and the 2023 Atomic Energy Act amendment allowing NPCIL‑PSU joint ventures. Distractors often confuse the PFBR (fast breeder) with the AHWR (thorium) or misstate the number of operational reactors.

  • Confusing the Design‑Linked Incentive’s OPEX support with the fab scheme’s CAPEX support.
  • Attributing the GIS‑based planning tool to the National Logistics Policy instead of PM Gati Shakti.
  • Assuming the DPDP Act includes a right to data portability or a right to be forgotten.
  • Mixing the multinational IFC‑IOR with the national NC3I network in maritime security questions.
  • Stating that the Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor is a thorium‑based reactor rather than a plutonium‑breeding sodium‑cooled fast reactor.
  • Believing the 700 MWe PHWR units are imported designs instead of indigenous.
  • These traps highlight the nuanced areas where examiners test depth of understanding.

India's Space Diplomacy: Gaganyaan Programme, International Partnerships, and Commercial Launch Services

The Gaganyaan human spaceflight programme, approved in 2018, aims to demonstrate indigenous crewed orbital capability by 2025. The programme is executed by the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) with the Human Space Flight Centre (HSFC) as the nodal agency. Critical milestones include the Pad Abort Test (July 2018), the Integrated Air Drop Test (2022), and the first uncrewed orbital test flight (G1) scheduled for late 2024, followed by a second uncrewed flight (G2) and the crewed mission (H1). The crew module incorporates a life‑support system, crew escape system, and an environmental control and life support system (ECLSS) developed in collaboration with the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) and the Indian Air Force’s Institute of Aerospace Medicine.

International partnerships form a strategic pillar. In 2021, ISRO signed a Memorandum of Understanding with the French space agency CNES for cooperation on space medicine, astronaut training, and microgravity experiments. A similar agreement with the Russian space agency Roscosmos (2022) covers training of Indian astronauts at the Yuri Gagarin Cosmonaut Training Centre and joint development of a docking mechanism for future space station missions. The United States, through NASA, has engaged in data‑sharing on radiation environment modelling and has offered a slot on the International Space Station for an Indian astronaut, pending policy clearance. These collaborations are framed under the United Nations Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space (COPUOS) guidelines and the Artemis Accords, which India joined in 2023.

Commercial launch services have expanded through NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), the commercial arm of ISRO. NSIL markets the Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV), Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle (GSLV) Mk II, and the new Small Satellite Launch Vehicle (SSLV). In 2023, NSIL secured a contract with OneWeb for 36 satellites launched on two GSLV Mk III missions, marking the first commercial use of the heavy‑lift vehicle. The SSLV‑D2 flight (February 2023) demonstrated on‑demand launch capability for small satellites, attracting customers from Europe and Southeast Asia. The 2024 Space Policy encourages private sector participation in end‑to‑end launch services, satellite manufacturing, and ground segment operations, with a single‑window clearance mechanism under IN‑SPACe.

Key Examination Pointers

  • Gaganyaan timeline: Pad Abort Test (2018), G1 (2024), G2 (2025), H1 (2025).
  • International MoUs: CNES (2021), Roscosmos (2022), NASA data‑sharing (2023).
  • Commercial milestones: OneWeb GSLV Mk III launches (2023), SSLV‑D2 (2023), NSIL revenue crossing ₹1,000 crore in FY 2023‑24.
  • Policy framework: Indian Space Policy 2023, IN‑SPACe single‑window, NSIL as commercial operator.

India's Climate Finance Architecture: Sovereign Green Bonds, Climate Finance Taxonomy, and International Climate Funds

India issued its first sovereign green bonds in January 2023, raising ₹16,000 crore across two tranches (5‑year and 10‑year). The proceeds are earmarked for projects classified under the Green Finance Taxonomy notified by the Ministry of Finance in 2022, which aligns with the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) Green Bond Principles. Eligible categories include renewable energy, energy efficiency, pollution prevention, sustainable agriculture, and climate‑resilient infrastructure. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) acts as the issuing agent, and the Public Debt Management Cell monitors allocation through a dedicated Green Bond Framework.

The Climate Finance Taxonomy, released in March 2022, defines technical screening criteria for each sector, thresholds for carbon intensity, and a “do no significant harm” (DNSH) principle. It also introduces a taxonomy‑aligned reporting template for issuers, enabling comparability with the EU Taxonomy and the ASEAN Taxonomy. The taxonomy is voluntary for private issuers but mandatory for sovereign green bond allocation reporting.

On the multilateral front, India accesses the Green Climate Fund (GCF) through the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD) as the accredited entity. As of March 2024, India has secured USD 1.2 billion in GCF financing for projects such as the Solar Parks Programme, the Climate Resilient Agriculture Initiative in Maharashtra, and the Urban Flood Management Project in Chennai. The Adaptation Fund and the Global Environment Facility (GEF) also channel resources through the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC). India’s contribution to the GCF replenishment (USD 100 million pledged in 2023) positions it as both a contributor and a recipient, a nuance often tested in examinations.

Examination Focus Areas

  • Sovereign green bond issuance dates, tenors, and amount (₹16,000 crore, Jan 2023).
  • Taxonomy sectors, DNSH principle, and alignment with ICMA principles.
  • GCF projects, accredited entity (NABARD), and India’s dual role as contributor and recipient.
  • Distinction between sovereign green bonds and corporate green bonds under SEBI’s 2021 guidelines.
Technique: Mapping Financial Instruments to Policy Objectives
A question asks: "Which of the following statements about India's sovereign green bonds issued in January 2023 is correct? (a) They were issued in three tranches of 3‑year, 5‑year, and 10‑year maturities. (b) The proceeds are allocated exclusively to renewable energy projects. (c) The issuance follows the Green Finance Taxonomy notified in March 2022 and aligns with ICMA Green Bond Principles. (d) The Reserve Bank of India is the sole subscriber to the bonds."
Correct answer: (c). The sovereign green bonds were issued in two tranches (5‑year and 10‑year), not three. The taxonomy covers multiple eligible sectors beyond renewable energy, including energy efficiency and climate‑resilient infrastructure. The RBI acts as the issuing agent, not the sole subscriber; the bonds are sold to a broad investor base through primary auction. The Green Finance Taxonomy (March 2022) provides the classification framework and the issuance explicitly references alignment with ICMA Green Bond Principles.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question lists multiple financial instruments (sovereign green bonds, corporate green bonds, GCF grants), first identify the issuer and the governing framework. Sovereign bonds follow the Ministry of Finance taxonomy; corporate bonds follow SEBI’s 2021 guidelines; GCF funds are channelled through accredited entities like NABARD. Matching the instrument to its regulatory anchor eliminates distractors quickly.

India's Border Infrastructure Development: Border Roads Organisation, Vibrant Villages Programme, and Strategic Connectivity

The Border Roads Organisation (BRO), under the Ministry of Defence, is the primary agency for construction and maintenance of strategic roads in the Himalayan and northeastern frontiers. As of March 2024, BRO maintains over 60,000 km of roads, including the 255 km Darbuk‑Shyok‑DBO (DS‑DBO) road in Ladakh, the 8.8 km Atal Tunnel (Rohtang) commissioned in October 2020, and the 1.8 km Sela Tunnel in Arunachal Pradesh (inaugurated March 2024). The organisation also constructs bridges, airfields, and tunnels to reduce travel time for military logistics and civilian access.

The Vibrant Villages Programme (VVP), launched in February 2023, targets 2,967 border villages across 19 districts in Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, and Ladakh. The programme converges central schemes — Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana (PMGSY), Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Gram Jyoti Yojana (DDUGJY), and the National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM) — with dedicated VVP funds of ₹4,800 crore for 2023‑24 to 2025‑26. Key interventions include all‑weather road connectivity, 4G/5G mobile coverage, renewable energy micro‑grids, tourism infrastructure, and skill development centres. The programme is monitored through a GIS‑based dashboard integrated with the PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan.

Strategic connectivity projects extend beyond roads. The 4.1 km Shinkun La Tunnel (under construction) will provide all‑weather access to the Zanskar valley. The 135 km Nimmu‑Padam‑Darcha road, nearing completion, links Ladakh to Himachal Pradesh via a new axis. In the northeast, the 2,000 km Trans‑Arunachal Highway (NH‑13) is being upgraded to double‑lane standards, and the 1,300 km East‑West Industrial Corridor connects Assam to Arunachal Pradesh. These projects are financed through the Central Road and Infrastructure Fund (CRIF) and the Special Accelerated Road Development Programme for the North East (SARDP‑NE).

Examination Pointers

  • BRO road length (>60,000 km) and flagship tunnels: Atal Tunnel (2020), Sela Tunnel (2024), Shinkun La (under construction).
  • VVP coverage: 2,967 villages, 19 districts, ₹4,800 crore allocation (2023‑26).
  • Convergence of PMGSY, DDUGJY, NRLM under VVP; GIS dashboard linked to PM Gati Shakti.
  • Key strategic corridors: DS‑DBO road, Nimmu‑Padam‑Darcha, Trans‑Arunachal Highway, East‑West Industrial Corridor.

India's Defence Export Promotion: Defence Export Strategy, Offset Policy, and Major Export Contracts

The Defence Export Strategy (DES) 2020‑25 sets a target of ₹35,000 crore (approximately USD 5 billion) in defence exports by 2025. The strategy is implemented by the Department of Defence Production (DDP) through the Defence Export Promotion Council (DEPC) and the newly created Defence Export Facilitation Cell (DEFC). Key enablers include the revised Offset Policy (2020), which mandates a 30 % offset obligation for contracts above ₹2,000 crore, with a multiplier for technology transfer and manufacturing in India. The policy also permits offset fulfilment through defence exports, creating a direct link between import contracts and export growth.

Major export contracts signed since 2021 illustrate the strategy’s traction. In 2021, Bharat Dynamics Limited (BDL) secured a USD 100 million contract for the Akash missile system to the Philippines. In 2022, Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) signed a USD 300 million deal for 12 Dornier‑228 maritime patrol aircraft to the Maldives and a USD 150 million contract for the Advanced Light Helicopter (ALH) Mk III to Mauritius. In 2023, the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) licensed the Pinaka multi‑barrel rocket launcher system to Armenia under a USD 250 million agreement. The BrahMos Aerospace joint venture (India‑Russia) concluded a USD 375 million contract for the shore‑based anti‑ship variant to the Philippines in 2022, marking the first export of a supersonic cruise missile system.

The Defence Production and Export Promotion Policy (DPEPP) 2022 further liberalises the No‑Objection Certificate (NOC) process, reduces the number of items on the Negative List for exports, and introduces a fast‑track clearance for exports to friendly foreign countries. The policy also encourages private sector participation through the Innovations for Defence Excellence (iDEX) framework, which has funded over 300 start‑ups since 2018, several of which have secured export orders for unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and electronic warfare suites.

Examination Focus

  • DES target: ₹35,000 crore by 2025; DEPC and DEFC as implementing bodies.
  • Offset Policy 2020: 30 % offset for contracts >₹2,000 crore, multiplier for tech transfer, offset fulfilment via exports.
  • Key export contracts: Akash to Philippines (2021), Dornier‑228 to Maldives (2022), ALH Mk III to Mauritius (2022), Pinaka to Armenia (2023), BrahMos to Philippines (2022).
  • DPEPP 2022: NOC liberalisation, Negative List reduction, fast‑track for friendly countries, iDEX start‑up export linkage.

India's Digital Health Mission: Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, Health IDs, and Interoperability

The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM), launched in September 2021, aims to create a national digital health ecosystem through unique Health IDs (ABHA numbers), a Health Facility Registry (HFR), a Healthcare Professionals Registry (HPR), and a Unified Health Interface (UHI). As of March 2024, over 450 million ABHA numbers have been generated, 250,000 health facilities registered, and 150,000 healthcare professionals enrolled. The ABHA number is a 14‑digit randomly generated identifier linked to the citizen’s Aadhaar or mobile number, enabling consent‑based sharing of longitudinal health records across public and private providers.

Interoperability is achieved through the ABDM Sandbox, which validates compliance with the FHIR R4 (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) standard and the India‑specific Clinical Document Architecture (CDA) profiles. The National Health Authority (NHA) operates the ABDM Gateway, a consent manager that logs every data access request, ensuring auditability under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. The UHI, modelled on the Unified Payments Interface (UPI), allows any certified application to initiate health service transactions — appointment booking, teleconsultation, pharmacy dispensing — without proprietary integrations.

Governance structures include the ABDM Steering Committee (chaired by the Union Health Minister), the ABDM Implementation Council (led by the NHA CEO), and State Health Agencies (SHAs) as nodal implementation units. The mission leverages the existing Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM‑JAY) IT backbone for claims processing, enabling seamless portability of insurance benefits across states. Pilot projects in Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Odisha have demonstrated reduction in duplicate diagnostic tests by 30 % and improvement in referral turnaround time by 40 %.

Examination Relevance

  • ABDM launch date (September 2021) and core building blocks: ABHA, HFR, HPR, UHI.
  • Scale metrics (as of March 2024): 450 million ABHA, 250 k facilities, 150 k professionals.
  • Technical standards: FHIR R4, CDA profiles, ABDM Sandbox, consent manager (ABDM Gateway).
  • Governance: Steering Committee, Implementation Council, SHAs; linkage with PM‑JAY IT platform.
  • Data protection alignment: DPDP Act 2023 consent framework, audit logs, purpose limitation.
  • Confusing the Gaganyaan uncrewed test flights (G1, G2) with the crewed mission (H1) and misstating their scheduled years.
  • Attributing the Green Finance Taxonomy to the Reserve Bank of India instead of the Ministry of Finance.
  • Assuming the Vibrant Villages Programme covers all border states; it excludes Jammu & Kashmir and the northeastern states beyond the 19 designated districts.
  • Mixing the Offset Policy’s 30 % obligation threshold (₹2,000 crore) with the earlier 2005 policy’s ₹300 crore threshold.
  • Stating that the ABHA number is derived directly from Aadhaar without the randomisation layer; the ABHA is a distinct 14‑digit identifier linked to Aadhaar or mobile.

  • Overlooking the role of the ABDM Sandbox in certifying FHIR R4 compliance; many candidates cite only the UHI as the interoperability layer.
