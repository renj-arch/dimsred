The English section of the General Ability Test is a rigorous assessment designed to test your command over the language through grammar, vocabulary, reading comprehension, and sentence correction. Success in this module does not rely on rote memorization but rather on understanding the underlying logic of how sentences are constructed and how words interact with one another. This lesson provides a comprehensive guide to mastering these four pillars, offering clear rules, practical examples, and strategies to tackle even the most tricky questions under time pressure.

Grammar

Grammar is the structural framework of a sentence, ensuring that thoughts are conveyed logically and accurately. The fundamental rule governing agreement is subject-verb concord: the verb must always match the number (singular or plural) of its subject. For instance, if the subject is "The list of items," which is singular because it refers to one list, the verb must be singular as well, such as "is." Conversely, a compound subject joined by 'and' usually requires a plural verb, like "Tom and Jerry are friends." The examiner typically asks you to identify errors in these agreements or to select the correct verb form based on the tense indicated by time words like 'yesterday' (past) or 'tomorrow' (future). You must scan for the subject first before looking at the verb to avoid being misled by intervening phrases.

Vocabulary

Vocabulary tests your ability to understand the precise meaning of words and their contextual usage within a sentence. A key concept here is synonymy, where words with similar meanings are used to vary style, but antonymy, where opposite meanings create contrast, is often tested in fill-in-the-blank questions. The examiner frequently presents a sentence with a missing word or an incorrect word and expects you to choose the option that best fits the tone and logic of the paragraph. To solve these, read the entire sentence first to grasp the context; then, look for clue words like 'however' (indicating contrast) or 'therefore' (indicating result). If a word seems ambiguous, eliminate options that are too extreme or unrelated to the main theme.

Reading Comprehension

Reading comprehension measures your ability to extract specific information, understand the author's intent, and infer meaning from text. The core principle is that every answer must be directly supported by the passage; you cannot use outside knowledge. The examiner typically asks questions about the main idea, the author's attitude, or specific details mentioned in a paragraph. To approach these effectively, read the question before reading the options to keep your focus sharp. Look for keywords in the question and locate them in the text, paying attention to pronouns like 'it' or 'they' which refer back to a noun mentioned earlier. Always verify that your chosen answer does not contradict any part of the passage.

Sentence Correction

Sentence correction focuses on identifying grammatical faults such as misplaced modifiers, dangling participles, and run-on sentences. A modifier is a word or phrase that describes another word, and it must be placed immediately next to the word it describes to avoid ambiguity. For example, in "Running down the street, the dog chased the ball," the phrase 'running' correctly modifies 'the dog.' However, if you wrote "Running down the street, the ball was caught by the boy," the modifier incorrectly suggests the ball was running. The examiner often presents a sentence with an underlined part and asks which option improves clarity or correctness. You must check for parallelism in lists (e.g., "He likes swimming, hiking, and to run" is incorrect; it should be "swimming, hiking, and running") and ensure that the tense remains consistent throughout the sentence.

Technique: Subject-Verb Agreement Check
Question: Identify the error in the following sentence:
"The number of students who are present in the class is increasing every day."
Solution:
The subject of the sentence is "The number," which is singular. Therefore, the verb must be singular ("is"). However, the phrase "who are present" contains a relative clause where "who" refers to "students" (plural), so "are" is correct there. The main error lies in the agreement between "number" and "is." If the sentence were "The numbers of students," it would be plural, but usually, we say "The number of..." implying one collective count. A common trap is confusing "The number" (singular) with "A number of" (meaning several, which is plural).
Technique: Contextual Word Choice
Question: Choose the correct word:
"He was accused _____ stealing the money."
Options: A) of, B) for, C) with, D) on.
Solution:
The correct preposition to use after the verb "accuse" is "of." This is a fixed collocation in English: "accuse someone of something." Using 'for' implies reason, 'with' implies possession or accompaniment, and 'on' implies location. Therefore, option A is the only grammatically correct choice that fits the standard idiom.

Common Traps

  • Misplaced Modifiers: Students often place descriptive phrases too far from the noun they describe, leading to confusing sentences like "She saw the man with the telescope" (did she use the telescope or did the man have it?). Always ensure the modifier touches the word it modifies.
  • Confusing 'Few' and 'A Few': 'Few' means not many (negative connotation), while 'a few' means some (positive connotation). Students often mix these up when describing quantities.
  • Dangling Participles: Using a participle phrase without a clear subject, such as "Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful" (trees cannot walk).
  • Run-on Sentences: Combining two independent clauses with only a comma instead of a conjunction or semicolon.

Memory Tricks

  • The 'A Few' Rule: Remember that "a" usually adds positivity. "A few friends" means you have some friends (good). "Few friends" means you are lonely (bad).
  • Parallelism Mnemonic: Think of a train track; both rails must be identical in shape. In lists, keep parts of speech the same: nouns with nouns, verbs with verbs.
  • Subject-Verb Shortcut: Cross out everything between the subject and the verb to see if they match directly. Ignore words like "and," "with," or "who" that might distract you.
Exam-Day Strategy
On the exam day, read the instructions for each section carefully before starting. For grammar and vocabulary questions, do not spend more than 45 seconds per question; if you are stuck, mark it and move on to return later. In reading comprehension, scan the passage for keywords rather than reading every single word linearly if time is tight. Always double-check your answers for sentence correction by reading the full sentence aloud in your head to catch awkward phrasing.

Subjunctive Mood and Counterfactual Conditionals

The subjunctive mood expresses wishes, demands, suggestions, or conditions contrary to fact. In modern English it survives mainly in fixed formulas and in the past‑subjunctive form were for all persons (If I were you). The present subjunctive uses the base form of the verb regardless of subject (It is essential that he attend). Counterfactual conditionals combine a past‑subjunctive if‑clause with a modal perfect in the main clause: If she had studied, she would have passed. The examiner often tests the distinction between was (indicative) and were (subjunctive) after if, as if, as though, and wish. A common error is to write If he was here, he would help when the situation is hypothetical; the correct form is If he were here. The same rule applies to suppose and imagine: Suppose he were late. In formal writing, the present subjunctive appears after verbs of mandate (recommend, insist, require, propose) and adjectives of necessity (imperative, vital, necessary). The structure is that + subject + base verb. For example, The committee recommended that the proposal be reviewed. Note that the passive base form be reviewed is required, not is reviewed. Mastery of these patterns eliminates a large class of sentence‑correction errors.

Collocation Chains and Lexical Cohesion in Formal Writing

Collocation refers to the statistically significant co‑occurrence of words. In academic and official texts, certain verb‑noun, adjective‑noun, and adverb‑verb pairs are fixed: make a decision (not do a decision), heavy rain (not strong rain), profound impact (not deep impact). Lexical cohesion is achieved when a writer repeats or varies these chains across paragraphs, creating a semantic thread that guides the reader. For instance, a passage on climate policy may introduce mitigation strategies, then refer to mitigation measures, mitigation efforts, and finally mitigation outcomes. The examiner tests this by offering four options that are synonyms in isolation but only one fits the established chain. Recognising the chain requires two steps: first, identify the head noun that anchors the lexical set; second, recall the conventional collocate that partners with that head. A useful technique is to build a personal collocation notebook grouped by head nouns (risk: assess, manage, mitigate, tolerate; analysis: conduct, perform, undertake, present). When a cloze item appears, locate the head noun in the surrounding sentences and select the option that completes the established chain. This approach also helps in sentence‑correction items where a preposition or particle is wrong because it breaks a fixed collocation (e.g., comply to instead of comply with).

Technique: Collocation Completion
Choose the word that best completes the sentence: "The board decided to ______ a comprehensive review of the policy." Options: A) conduct, B) make, C) perform, D) execute.
The head noun is review. In formal English the conventional verb collocating with review is conduct (conduct a review). Make a review is non‑standard, perform a review is possible but less idiomatic in administrative contexts, and execute a review suggests carrying out a plan rather than an evaluation. Therefore option A is correct.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Vocabulary
When a vocabulary question appears, first scan the surrounding two sentences for the head noun or verb that anchors the collocation. Eliminate any option that does not pair with that anchor in standard usage. If two options seem plausible, choose the one that appears more frequently in official reports and academic abstracts; frequency correlates with examiner preference.

Argument Mapping: Premise, Evidence, and Warrant in Reading Comprehension

Advanced comprehension passages are structured arguments rather than mere information dumps. An argument consists of a claim (the author's position), one or more premises (reasons offered), evidence (data, examples, citations) supporting each premise, and an implicit warrant (the general principle linking premise to claim). The examiner frequently asks: "Which of the following, if true, would most strengthen the author's argument?" To answer, map the argument first: underline the claim, bracket each premise, and note the evidence. Then identify the warrant — often a causal or analogical principle. A strengthening statement either supplies missing evidence for a premise or validates the warrant. Conversely, a weakening statement introduces a counter‑example that breaks the warrant or shows the evidence is unrepresentative. Practice by converting a paragraph into a diagram: Claim → Premise 1 (Evidence A) → Warrant X; Premise 2 (Evidence B) → Warrant Y. This visualisation makes it easy to spot the logical gap the question targets. Remember that the correct answer must be directly supported by the passage; outside knowledge cannot substitute for a missing warrant.

Ellipsis, Gapping, and Comparative Deletion in Sentence Correction

Ellipsis omits words that are recoverable from context. Gapping removes a repeated verb in coordinate clauses: She likes tea; he, coffee (the verb likes is gapped). Comparative deletion drops the repeated element after than or as: She runs faster than he (runs). The examiner tests whether the remaining structure stays grammatical and unambiguous. A frequent trap is faulty parallelism after ellipsis: He prefers reading novels to watch movies (should be to watching or to watch with parallel gerunds). Another trap is ambiguous reference when the elided verb has multiple possible antecedents: John helped Mary more than Susan — does it mean John helped Mary more than John helped Susan, or John helped Mary more than Susan helped Mary? The correct revision adds the missing verb or pronoun: John helped Mary more than he helped Susan or John helped Mary more than Susan did. In sentence‑correction items, always reconstruct the full clause mentally before judging the underlined portion. Check that the subject of the elided verb matches the intended subject, and that the tense and voice are consistent with the antecedent clause.

Punctuation Hierarchy: Semicolons, Colons, Dashes, and Parentheses

Punctuation marks signal the relationship between clauses and the hierarchy of information. A semicolon joins two independent clauses that are closely related but not linked by a coordinating conjunction: The data were inconclusive; further trials are required. It also separates items in a complex list where items themselves contain commas. A colon introduces an explanation, a list, or a quotation that follows an independent clause: The committee identified three priorities: funding, staffing, and timeline. The clause before the colon must be complete. An em dash (—) marks a sudden break, an appositive with internal commas, or emphasis: The results — unexpected and unprecedented — changed the policy. Dashes are more emphatic than parentheses and less formal than colons. Parentheses enclose supplementary, non‑essential information that can be omitted without altering the sentence's core meaning: The budget (approved in March) exceeds projections. The examiner often presents a sentence where a semicolon is incorrectly used for a dependent clause, or a colon follows a fragment. The rule of thumb: if the material before the mark cannot stand alone as a sentence, a colon is wrong; if the two clauses are not independent, a semicolon is wrong. Dashes and parentheses are interchangeable only when the enclosed material is truly parenthetical; dashes add rhetorical weight, parentheses de‑emphasise.

  • Confusing was and were in hypothetical if‑clauses.
  • Choosing a synonym that breaks an established collocation chain.
  • Misidentifying the warrant and selecting a strengthening answer that only adds evidence.
  • Leaving an ambiguous comparative deletion that allows two readings.
  • Using a semicolon before a dependent clause or a colon after a fragment.
  • Overusing dashes where parentheses would keep the tone neutral.
  • Mastering these traps will raise your accuracy significantly.
