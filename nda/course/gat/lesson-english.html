The English section of the General Ability Test is a rigorous assessment designed to test your command over the language through grammar, vocabulary, reading comprehension, and sentence correction. Success in this module does not rely on rote memorization but rather on understanding the underlying logic of how sentences are constructed and how words interact with one another. This lesson provides a comprehensive guide to mastering these four pillars, offering clear rules, practical examples, and strategies to tackle even the most tricky questions under time pressure.

Grammar

Grammar is the structural framework of a sentence, ensuring that thoughts are conveyed logically and accurately. The fundamental rule governing agreement is subject-verb concord: the verb must always match the number (singular or plural) of its subject. For instance, if the subject is "The list of items," which is singular because it refers to one list, the verb must be singular as well, such as "is." Conversely, a compound subject joined by 'and' usually requires a plural verb, like "Tom and Jerry are friends." The examiner typically asks you to identify errors in these agreements or to select the correct verb form based on the tense indicated by time words like 'yesterday' (past) or 'tomorrow' (future). You must scan for the subject first before looking at the verb to avoid being misled by intervening phrases.

Vocabulary

Vocabulary tests your ability to understand the precise meaning of words and their contextual usage within a sentence. A key concept here is synonymy, where words with similar meanings are used to vary style, but antonymy, where opposite meanings create contrast, is often tested in fill-in-the-blank questions. The examiner frequently presents a sentence with a missing word or an incorrect word and expects you to choose the option that best fits the tone and logic of the paragraph. To solve these, read the entire sentence first to grasp the context; then, look for clue words like 'however' (indicating contrast) or 'therefore' (indicating result). If a word seems ambiguous, eliminate options that are too extreme or unrelated to the main theme.

Reading Comprehension

Reading comprehension measures your ability to extract specific information, understand the author's intent, and infer meaning from text. The core principle is that every answer must be directly supported by the passage; you cannot use outside knowledge. The examiner typically asks questions about the main idea, the author's attitude, or specific details mentioned in a paragraph. To approach these effectively, read the question before reading the options to keep your focus sharp. Look for keywords in the question and locate them in the text, paying attention to pronouns like 'it' or 'they' which refer back to a noun mentioned earlier. Always verify that your chosen answer does not contradict any part of the passage.

Sentence Correction

Sentence correction focuses on identifying grammatical faults such as misplaced modifiers, dangling participles, and run-on sentences. A modifier is a word or phrase that describes another word, and it must be placed immediately next to the word it describes to avoid ambiguity. For example, in "Running down the street, the dog chased the ball," the phrase 'running' correctly modifies 'the dog.' However, if you wrote "Running down the street, the ball was caught by the boy," the modifier incorrectly suggests the ball was running. The examiner often presents a sentence with an underlined part and asks which option improves clarity or correctness. You must check for parallelism in lists (e.g., "He likes swimming, hiking, and to run" is incorrect; it should be "swimming, hiking, and running") and ensure that the tense remains consistent throughout the sentence.

Technique: Subject-Verb Agreement Check
Question: Identify the error in the following sentence:
"The number of students who are present in the class is increasing every day."
Solution:
The subject of the sentence is "The number," which is singular. Therefore, the verb must be singular ("is"). However, the phrase "who are present" contains a relative clause where "who" refers to "students" (plural), so "are" is correct there. The main error lies in the agreement between "number" and "is." If the sentence were "The numbers of students," it would be plural, but usually, we say "The number of..." implying one collective count. A common trap is confusing "The number" (singular) with "A number of" (meaning several, which is plural).
Technique: Contextual Word Choice
Question: Choose the correct word:
"He was accused _____ stealing the money."
Options: A) of, B) for, C) with, D) on.
Solution:
The correct preposition to use after the verb "accuse" is "of." This is a fixed collocation in English: "accuse someone of something." Using 'for' implies reason, 'with' implies possession or accompaniment, and 'on' implies location. Therefore, option A is the only grammatically correct choice that fits the standard idiom.

Common Traps

  • Misplaced Modifiers: Students often place descriptive phrases too far from the noun they describe, leading to confusing sentences like "She saw the man with the telescope" (did she use the telescope or did the man have it?). Always ensure the modifier touches the word it modifies.
  • Confusing 'Few' and 'A Few': 'Few' means not many (negative connotation), while 'a few' means some (positive connotation). Students often mix these up when describing quantities.
  • Dangling Participles: Using a participle phrase without a clear subject, such as "Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful" (trees cannot walk).
  • Run-on Sentences: Combining two independent clauses with only a comma instead of a conjunction or semicolon.

Memory Tricks

  • The 'A Few' Rule: Remember that "a" usually adds positivity. "A few friends" means you have some friends (good). "Few friends" means you are lonely (bad).
  • Parallelism Mnemonic: Think of a train track; both rails must be identical in shape. In lists, keep parts of speech the same: nouns with nouns, verbs with verbs.
  • Subject-Verb Shortcut: Cross out everything between the subject and the verb to see if they match directly. Ignore words like "and," "with," or "who" that might distract you.
Exam-Day Strategy
On the exam day, read the instructions for each section carefully before starting. For grammar and vocabulary questions, do not spend more than 45 seconds per question; if you are stuck, mark it and move on to return later. In reading comprehension, scan the passage for keywords rather than reading every single word linearly if time is tight. Always double-check your answers for sentence correction by reading the full sentence aloud in your head to catch awkward phrasing.

Collective Nouns, Quantifiers, and Subject‑Verb Agreement

Collective nouns such as team, committee, jury, audience, family can take either a singular or a plural verb depending on whether the group acts as a single unit or as individuals. When the collective noun is preceded by a quantifier like a number of, a variety of, a majority of, the verb agrees with the noun that follows the quantifier, not with the quantifier itself. For example, A number of candidates are waiting (plural) because candidates is plural, whereas The number of candidates is rising (singular) because the number is the head noun. Similarly, expressions of quantity such as half of, a third of, a proportion of follow the same rule: the verb matches the noun after of. When the noun after of is uncountable, the verb is singular (Half of the water is contaminated); when it is countable plural, the verb is plural (Half of the students have arrived).

Indefinite pronouns also create agreement challenges. Each, every, either, neither, one, everyone, somebody, nobody are always singular and require singular verbs. Both, few, many, several are plural. All, any, most, none, some can be singular or plural depending on the noun they refer to. A frequent examiner trap is the phrase none of the followed by a plural noun; traditional grammar treats none as singular, but modern usage accepts plural agreement when the sense is clearly plural (None of the answers are correct). In formal testing, the safest approach is to follow the noun after of unless the test explicitly demands the traditional singular rule.

Compound subjects joined by and normally take a plural verb, but when the two nouns refer to a single entity or a combined concept, a singular verb is used (Bread and butter is my favourite snack). With or, nor, either…or, neither…nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject (Neither the manager nor the employees are responsible). When a singular and a plural subject are joined by or/nor, place the plural subject last to avoid awkwardness and ensure correct agreement.

Relative clauses introduced by who, which, that inherit the number of their antecedent. In The only one of the students who has passed is Rahul, the antecedent of who is one (singular), so the verb is singular. Conversely, He is one of the students who have passed makes students the antecedent, requiring a plural verb. Mastery of these patterns eliminates the majority of subject‑verb agreement errors in high‑difficulty items.

Prepositional Collocations and Idiomatic Verb Patterns

English verbs often bind to specific prepositions, forming fixed collocations that cannot be deduced from meaning alone. The verb accuse takes of (accuse someone of theft), apologize takes for (apologize for the delay), comply takes with (comply with the regulations), and insist takes on (insist on paying). Some verbs allow two prepositions with a shift in meaning: compare to (liken) versus compare with (examine similarities and differences); consist of (composition) versus consist in (essence). The examiner frequently tests these by presenting a sentence with a blank and four preposition options, only one of which matches the established collocation.

Phrasal verbs add another layer: the particle can be a preposition or an adverb, and the meaning often changes dramatically. Give up (quit), give in (surrender), give out (distribute or exhaust), give away (reveal or donate). When a phrasal verb is transitive, the object pronoun must precede the particle (give it up, not give up it), whereas a noun object may appear on either side (give up the habit or give the habit up). This rule is a reliable marker for sentence‑correction items that test word order.

Adjective‑preposition pairs follow similar rigidity: interested in, good at, responsible for, capable of, dependent on, aware of. Errors arise when candidates substitute a semantically similar preposition (interested on, good in). A systematic way to internalise these pairs is to group them by the adjective’s semantic field: emotions (afraid of, proud of, fond of), ability (capable of, adept at, skilled in), relation (similar to, different from, contrary to).

Noun‑preposition collocations are equally tested: advantage of, solution to, reason for, cause of, effect on, attitude toward. The preposition is dictated by the head noun, not by the following noun phrase. For instance, the solution to the problem is correct; the solution for the problem is non‑standard in formal writing. Recognising the head noun and retrieving its canonical preposition eliminates guesswork.

Technique: Selecting the Correct Preposition after a Verb
Choose the preposition that correctly completes the sentence: "The committee decided to ______ the proposal because it lacked feasibility." Options: A) reject, B) reject to, C) reject from, D) reject of.
The verb reject is transitive and does not take a preposition when followed directly by its object. Therefore the correct form is simply "reject the proposal". Options B, C, and D each insert an unnecessary preposition, creating ungrammatical structures. The correct answer is A) reject.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Preposition Items
When a blank follows a verb, first identify whether the verb is transitive (takes a direct object) or intransitive (requires a preposition). If the verb is transitive, the answer is often the base verb with no preposition. If the verb is intransitive, recall the fixed collocation list you have memorised. Eliminate any option that adds a preposition to a transitive verb or uses a preposition not listed in the standard collocation for that verb.

Inference, Tone, and Argument Mapping in Reading Comprehension

Advanced comprehension questions move beyond literal retrieval to require the test‑taker to infer the author’s implicit stance, detect subtle shifts in tone, and reconstruct the logical architecture of the passage. An inference question asks what can be logically concluded from the information given, not what is explicitly stated. The correct answer must be a necessary consequence of the passage; any choice that introduces external knowledge or over‑generalises is a distractor. For example, if a passage notes that the new policy reduced emissions by 12 % in the first year, a valid inference is the policy had a measurable impact on emissions, whereas the policy will solve climate change is an unwarranted extrapolation.

Tone identification hinges on lexical cues: words such as alarmingly, regrettably, surprisingly, commendably signal the author’s attitude. A passage may adopt a cautious tone (hedging with may, suggests, appears), a critical tone (using flawed, inadequate, overlooks), or a neutral tone (presenting facts without evaluative adjectives). When a question asks for the author’s attitude toward a specific proposal, locate the sentences discussing that proposal and catalogue the modifiers. The answer choice that mirrors the cumulative evaluative language is correct.

Argument mapping involves recognising the passage’s structural components: claim, evidence, counter‑argument, rebuttal, and conclusion. A typical academic passage opens with a thesis, follows with supporting data, acknowledges opposing views, and ends with a qualified conclusion. Questions may ask which paragraph serves as the rebuttal or what function a particular sentence performs. To answer efficiently, annotate the passage on first reading: label each paragraph with its rhetorical role (e.g., Thesis, Evidence 1, Counter‑argument, Rebuttal, Conclusion). This map lets you locate the relevant section instantly when a function‑based question appears.

Comparative passages add a layer: two texts may present contrasting perspectives on the same issue. The examiner may ask how the authors differ in their use of evidence or in their underlying assumptions. The strategy is to create a two‑column table while reading: list each author’s main claim, type of evidence (empirical, anecdotal, theoretical), and tone. Then match the question’s focus to the appropriate column. This systematic approach reduces the cognitive load of holding two arguments simultaneously.

Parallelism, Ellipsis, and Subordination in Sentence Correction

Parallelism demands that items in a list, series, or coordinated structure share the same grammatical form. The rule extends beyond simple noun‑noun or verb‑verb matching to include matching clause types, infinitive versus gerund forms, and voice. For instance, The project requires planning, executing, and to monitor violates parallelism because the first two are gerunds and the third is an infinitive. The corrected version is planning, executing, and monitoring or to plan, to execute, and to monitor. When a correlative conjunction pair (not only…but also, either…or, neither…nor, both…and) is used, the elements following each connector must be parallel: She not only sings but also dances (both bare infinitives after the modal).

Ellipsis — the omission of words understood from context — is permissible only when the omitted material is recoverable without ambiguity. In She can play the piano better than he can, the second play the piano is ellipted. However, She likes apples more than oranges is ambiguous: it could mean more than she likes oranges or more than oranges like apples. The test often presents a sentence where an elliptical construction creates a faulty comparison; the correct answer restores the missing verb or changes the structure to a clear comparative clause (She likes apples more than she likes oranges).

Subordination hierarchy influences clarity and emphasis. A sentence overloaded with coordinate clauses (and, but, or) can be streamlined by subordinating less important ideas using because, although, while, since. The examiner may test the ability to choose the subordinate conjunction that reflects the logical relationship: cause (because, since), concession (although, even though), condition (if, unless), time (when, while, after). Misusing while to mean although is a common error; while primarily indicates simultaneity, though concessive use is accepted in formal writing. The safest choice in a test setting is the conjunction that unambiguously conveys the intended logical link.

Complex sentences with multiple embeddings (relative clauses inside noun clauses, etc.) are prone to misplaced modifiers and ambiguous pronoun reference. The correction strategy: identify the main clause, then verify each subordinate clause attaches to the intended noun. If a relative clause follows a noun phrase but logically modifies a different noun, restructure the sentence to place the modifier next to its target. For pronoun reference, ensure the antecedent is singular/plural and gender‑appropriate, and that no other noun of the same number and gender intervenes.

Register, Connotation, and Morphological Clues for Vocabulary in Context

Vocabulary questions in high‑stakes tests rarely ask for a dictionary definition; they probe the ability to discern the precise shade of meaning a word carries in a specific register — formal, academic, journalistic, or colloquial. A word such as ameliorate (to improve) belongs to a formal register and typically appears in policy or medical texts, whereas better is neutral and fix up is colloquial. When a passage uses ameliorate, the correct synonym among options will share that formal tone (e.g., mitigate, enhance) rather than a neutral or slang alternative.

Connotation — the emotional or cultural association a word evokes — can differentiate near‑synonyms. Stubborn (negative), tenacious (positive), and persistent (neutral) all describe continued effort, but the author’s attitude toward the subject determines which fits. If the passage praises a researcher’s determination, tenacious is appropriate; if it criticises a negotiator’s inflexibility, stubborn is the intended choice. Test items often present a sentence with a blank and four words that are denotatively similar; the discriminating factor is connotation aligned with the surrounding evaluative language.

Morphological analysis provides a rapid, reliable method for narrowing options. Recognising prefixes (un‑, dis‑, non‑, anti‑, pre‑, post‑), roots (port, spect, dict, fer, struct), and suffixes (-tion, -ity, -ous, -ive, -al) allows reconstruction of meaning. For example, inscrutable = in‑ (not) + scrut (examine) + -able (capable of) → “not capable of being examined”. If the context describes a facial expression that reveals nothing, inscrutable fits. Similarly, benevolent = bene (good) + vol (wish) + -ent → “wishing good”. Knowledge of a core set of Latin and Greek roots covers a large proportion of advanced vocabulary.

Collocational probability — the likelihood that two words co‑occur — further guides selection. Corpus data shows that make a decision is far more frequent than do a decision; heavy rain outnumbers strong rain. When a blank sits inside a fixed phrase, the answer that restores the conventional collocation is correct. Developing an ear for these pairings through extensive reading of quality non‑fiction (editorials, scientific abstracts, policy briefs) builds an intuitive sense that outperforms rote memorisation.

  • Assuming a collective noun is always singular; ignore the quantifier rule and the distinction between group‑as‑unit and group‑as‑individuals.
  • Choosing a preposition based on semantic similarity rather than the fixed verb‑preposition collocation (e.g., using interested on instead of interested in).
  • Treating an inference as a restatement of explicit text; the correct inference must be a logical necessity, not a mere paraphrase.
  • Misidentifying tone by focusing on a single adjective while the passage’s overall evaluative language points the opposite way.
  • Applying parallelism only to surface word forms (noun vs. verb) and neglecting deeper structural parallelism such as matching clause types or voice.
  • Allowing elliptical comparisons to remain ambiguous; the test expects the full comparative clause or a clearly parallel structure.
  • Selecting a synonym that matches denotation but clashes with register or connotation (e.g., using fix up in a formal academic passage where ameliorate is required).

Mastering these advanced patterns equips you to handle the most demanding items with confidence and precision.
