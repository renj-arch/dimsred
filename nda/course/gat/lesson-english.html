Learning Objectives

  • Understand the fundamental concepts of English
  • Apply key formulas and techniques to solve problems
  • Practice with exam-level questions to build speed and accuracy

The English section of the General Ability Test is a rigorous assessment designed to test your command over the language through grammar, vocabulary, reading comprehension, and sentence correction. Success in this module does not rely on rote memorization but rather on understanding the underlying logic of how sentences are constructed and how words interact with one another. This lesson provides a comprehensive guide to mastering these four pillars, offering clear rules, practical examples, and strategies to tackle even the most tricky questions under time pressure.

Grammar

Grammar is the structural framework of a sentence, ensuring that thoughts are conveyed logically and accurately. The fundamental rule governing agreement is subject-verb concord: the verb must always match the number (singular or plural) of its subject. For instance, if the subject is "The list of items," which is singular because it refers to one list, the verb must be singular as well, such as "is." Conversely, a compound subject joined by 'and' usually requires a plural verb, like "Tom and Jerry are friends." The examiner typically asks you to identify errors in these agreements or to select the correct verb form based on the tense indicated by time words like 'yesterday' (past) or 'tomorrow' (future). You must scan for the subject first before looking at the verb to avoid being misled by intervening phrases.

Vocabulary

Vocabulary tests your ability to understand the precise meaning of words and their contextual usage within a sentence. A key concept here is synonymy, where words with similar meanings are used to vary style, but antonymy, where opposite meanings create contrast, is often tested in fill-in-the-blank questions. The examiner frequently presents a sentence with a missing word or an incorrect word and expects you to choose the option that best fits the tone and logic of the paragraph. To solve these, read the entire sentence first to grasp the context; then, look for clue words like 'however' (indicating contrast) or 'therefore' (indicating result). If a word seems ambiguous, eliminate options that are too extreme or unrelated to the main theme.

Reading Comprehension

Reading comprehension measures your ability to extract specific information, understand the author's intent, and infer meaning from text. The core principle is that every answer must be directly supported by the passage; you cannot use outside knowledge. The examiner typically asks questions about the main idea, the author's attitude, or specific details mentioned in a paragraph. To approach these effectively, read the question before reading the options to keep your focus sharp. Look for keywords in the question and locate them in the text, paying attention to pronouns like 'it' or 'they' which refer back to a noun mentioned earlier. Always verify that your chosen answer does not contradict any part of the passage.

Sentence Correction

Sentence correction focuses on identifying grammatical faults such as misplaced modifiers, dangling participles, and run-on sentences. A modifier is a word or phrase that describes another word, and it must be placed immediately next to the word it describes to avoid ambiguity. For example, in "Running down the street, the dog chased the ball," the phrase 'running' correctly modifies 'the dog.' However, if you wrote "Running down the street, the ball was caught by the boy," the modifier incorrectly suggests the ball was running. The examiner often presents a sentence with an underlined part and asks which option improves clarity or correctness. You must check for parallelism in lists (e.g., "He likes swimming, hiking, and to run" is incorrect; it should be "swimming, hiking, and running") and ensure that the tense remains consistent throughout the sentence.

Technique: Subject-Verb Agreement Check
Question: Identify the error in the following sentence:
"The number of students who are present in the class is increasing every day."
Solution:
The subject of the sentence is "The number," which is singular. Therefore, the verb must be singular ("is"). However, the phrase "who are present" contains a relative clause where "who" refers to "students" (plural), so "are" is correct there. The main error lies in the agreement between "number" and "is." If the sentence were "The numbers of students," it would be plural, but usually, we say "The number of..." implying one collective count. A common trap is confusing "The number" (singular) with "A number of" (meaning several, which is plural).
Technique: Contextual Word Choice
Question: Choose the correct word:
"He was accused _____ stealing the money."
Options: A) of, B) for, C) with, D) on.
Solution:
The correct preposition to use after the verb "accuse" is "of." This is a fixed collocation in English: "accuse someone of something." Using 'for' implies reason, 'with' implies possession or accompaniment, and 'on' implies location. Therefore, option A is the only grammatically correct choice that fits the standard idiom.

Common Traps

  • Misplaced Modifiers: Students often place descriptive phrases too far from the noun they describe, leading to confusing sentences like "She saw the man with the telescope" (did she use the telescope or did the man have it?). Always ensure the modifier touches the word it modifies.
  • Confusing 'Few' and 'A Few': 'Few' means not many (negative connotation), while 'a few' means some (positive connotation). Students often mix these up when describing quantities.
  • Dangling Participles: Using a participle phrase without a clear subject, such as "Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful" (trees cannot walk).
  • Run-on Sentences: Combining two independent clauses with only a comma instead of a conjunction or semicolon.

Memory Tricks

  • The 'A Few' Rule: Remember that "a" usually adds positivity. "A few friends" means you have some friends (good). "Few friends" means you are lonely (bad).
  • Parallelism Mnemonic: Think of a train track; both rails must be identical in shape. In lists, keep parts of speech the same: nouns with nouns, verbs with verbs.
  • Subject-Verb Shortcut: Cross out everything between the subject and the verb to see if they match directly. Ignore words like "and," "with," or "who" that might distract you.
Exam-Day Strategy
On the exam day, read the instructions for each section carefully before starting. For grammar and vocabulary questions, do not spend more than 45 seconds per question; if you are stuck, mark it and move on to return later. In reading comprehension, scan the passage for keywords rather than reading every single word linearly if time is tight. Always double-check your answers for sentence correction by reading the full sentence aloud in your head to catch awkward phrasing.

Subjunctive Mood and Conditional Constructions

The subjunctive mood expresses wishes, demands, suggestions, or hypothetical situations rather than factual statements. In modern English the present subjunctive survives mainly in mandative clauses that follow verbs such as recommend, insist, require, suggest and adjectives like essential, vital, imperative. The form is the base verb for all persons: “The committee recommends that he submit the report.” The past subjunctive, identical to the past indicative except for the verb be (which becomes were for every subject), appears in contrary‑to‑fact conditions: “If I were you, I would accept.” Conditional sentences are classified by time reference and reality status. The zero conditional (If water reaches 100 °C, it boils) states universal truths. The first conditional (If it rains, we will cancel) predicts a likely future. The second conditional (If I had a car, I would drive) imagines a present or future situation that is unlikely. The third conditional (If she had studied, she would have passed) rewrites a past event. Mixed conditionals combine time frames, e.g., If I had taken the job, I would be living abroad now. Examiners frequently test the ability to match the correct verb forms across the two clauses and to spot illicit shifts such as “If he would have known, he would act differently.” Mastery requires recognising the mandative subjunctive, the invariant were in hypotheticals, and the strict tense sequencing of each conditional type.

Beyond the basic patterns, several exceptions and stylistic nuances appear in formal writing. The verb be in the present subjunctive can also appear as be for all persons (“It is crucial that he be present”), while the past subjunctive were is retained even for singular subjects in counterfactuals. In reported speech, the subjunctive may be back‑shifted: “She insisted that he be on time” becomes “She insisted that he be on time” (no change) because the subjunctive lacks a past form. Some dialects replace the mandative subjunctive with a modal construction (“The rule requires that he should submit”), but standard examinations treat the bare infinitive as the correct form. Conditional sentences also admit inverted structures for emphasis: “Had I known, I would have acted” (third conditional) and “Were I you, I would refuse” (second conditional). These inversions omit if and place the auxiliary before the subject, a pattern that examiners use to test recognition of elliptical conditionals. Finally, the distinction between would and could in the main clause signals ability versus willingness, a subtle point that appears in higher‑difficulty items.

Lexical Cohesion: Collocation, Register, and Semantic Fields

Vocabulary testing at an advanced level moves beyond single‑word definitions to the way words habitually co‑occur. A collocation is a statistically significant pairing such as “make a decision” rather than “do a decision”, or “heavy rain” versus “strong rain”. Examiners embed these patterns in gap‑fill or error‑spotting items, expecting candidates to select the verb that naturally partners with the noun. Register — the level of formality dictated by context — further restricts lexical choice. Academic prose prefers “commence” over “start”, “utilise” over “use”, while journalistic style tolerates “kick off” or “wrap up”. A single passage may shift registers, and questions often ask for the word that best maintains the prevailing tone. Semantic fields group words by shared meaning (e.g., joy, delight, elation, euphoria), and test items may require identifying the term that fits a specific nuance: “The victory filled him with ___” where elation conveys a higher intensity than joy. Understanding the gradable nature of synonyms, the connotative load of near‑synonyms, and the syntactic frames they occupy (transitive vs. intransitive, prepositional complementation) enables precise selection.

Advanced exercises also probe the boundaries of collocational flexibility. Some pairings are fixed (“rancid butter”), others are strong but allow limited variation (“fast food” vs. “quick food” — the latter is odd), and a few are free (“big house”, “large house”). Learners must recognise that de‑lexicalised verbs (make, do, take, have) generate a wide range of collocations whose meaning is carried by the noun (make a mistake, do research, take a break, have a look). Errors often arise when a candidate transfers a collocation from L1, producing forms like “do a decision” or “make a photo”. Register mismatches appear when a formal essay contains colloquial idioms (“The data shows a huge spike” instead of “a substantial increase”). Semantic‑field questions may present a set of synonyms and ask which one collocates with a given adjective (“_____ criticism” — constructive fits, positive does not). Mastery of these three dimensions — collocation, register, semantic field — transforms vocabulary from a list of definitions into a tool for coherent, context‑appropriate expression.

Technique: Identifying the Correct Conditional Form
Question: Choose the sentence that correctly uses the third conditional.
Solution: The third conditional expresses a hypothetical past situation and its imagined result, using "had + past participle" in the if‑clause and "would have + past participle" in the main clause. Option B — "If she had studied harder, she would have passed the exam." — follows this pattern, while the other options mix tenses incorrectly.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a conditional question appears, first label the time frame (past, present, future) and then match the verb forms to the standard patterns; this eliminates answer choices that blend incompatible tenses.

Critical Reading: Inference, Tone, and Argument Structure

Reading comprehension at the advanced level demands the ability to move beyond literal retrieval. Inference questions ask the test‑taker to derive a conclusion that is not explicitly stated but follows logically from the passage. The key is to locate the textual evidence — often a cluster of sentences — that jointly support the inference, and to avoid importing external knowledge. Tone identification requires sensitivity to diction, syntax, and rhetorical devices: a passage that uses words like “regrettably,” “alarmingly,” “unprecedented” signals a concerned or urgent tone, whereas “notably,” “remarkably,” “encouragingly” indicates approval. Argument structure analysis involves mapping the author’s claim, the supporting premises, and any concessions or rebuttals. Examiners frequently present a passage with a clear thesis in the opening paragraph, followed by evidence, a counter‑argument, and a synthesis. Questions may ask which sentence functions as the main claim, which provides a concession, or what logical fallacy (e.g., hasty generalisation, false cause) undermines a particular argument. Recognising signpost words — however, therefore, consequently, in contrast — helps trace the flow of reasoning.

Advanced passages often embed multiple perspectives, requiring the reader to distinguish the author’s voice from quoted sources. A question might ask, “The author’s attitude toward the proposed policy is best described as…” and the correct answer hinges on detecting subtle evaluative adjectives or the balance of positive versus negative evidence. Inference items sometimes use the phrase “It can be inferred that…” and the correct choice is the one that is *necessarily* true given the passage, not merely *possibly* true. A common trap is selecting an answer that is plausible in the real world but unsupported by the text. Tone questions may offer options like “optimistic,” “cautiously optimistic,” “skeptical,” “indifferent”; the distinction between “optimistic” and “cautiously optimistic” rests on the presence of qualifiers such as “provided that,” “if,” or “may.” Argument‑structure questions benefit from a quick outline: label each paragraph as Claim, Evidence, Counter‑argument, Rebuttal, Conclusion. This scaffold makes it easy to answer “Which paragraph presents the counter‑argument?” or “What is the function of the final paragraph?” without re‑reading the entire text.

Advanced Error Detection: Parallelism, Ellipsis, and Comparative Structures

Parallelism errors extend beyond simple lists. In complex sentences, coordinate structures may involve clauses, infinitives, gerunds, or prepositional phrases, and each must match in grammatical form. For example, “The project requires planning the schedule, allocating resources, and to monitor progress” mixes gerunds with an infinitive; the correction is “planning…, allocating…, and monitoring…”. Ellipsis — the omission of words understood from context — is permissible only when the omitted material is recoverable without ambiguity. In “She can play the piano better than he”, the full form is “than he can play the piano”; the elliptical version is acceptable because the auxiliary can is repeated. However, “She likes apples more than oranges” is ambiguous: it could mean “more than she likes oranges” or “more than oranges like apples”. The unambiguous form adds the pronoun or verb: “more than she likes oranges”. Comparative structures also test the distinction between ‑er/more and ‑est/most, the use of than versus as…as, and the avoidance of double comparatives (“more better”) or double superlatives (“most fastest”). Examiners embed these errors in long sentences where the faulty element is separated from its partner by intervening clauses, demanding careful tracking of the syntactic skeleton.

Another layer involves correlative conjunctions (both…and, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also) which require strict parallelism: “Not only did he finish the report, but also he presented it” is faulty because the first clause is inverted while the second is not; the corrected version aligns both as “Not only did he finish the report, but he also presented it” or “He not only finished the report but also presented it.” Ellipsis with correlatives can be tricky: “She will either call or send an email” is fine, but “She will either call or email” omits the object after email (which can be a verb), creating a shift in parts of speech. Comparative clauses introduced by as or than often trigger subject‑verb agreement issues when the subject is a pronoun: “He runs faster than me” (informal) versus “He runs faster than I” (formal, implying “than I run”). Advanced items may combine parallelism, ellipsis, and comparison in a single sentence, requiring the candidate to isolate each coordinate element, restore any ellipted material mentally, and verify that the grammatical form and logical comparison are both sound.

Punctuation Nuances: Semicolons, Colons, Dashes, and Parentheses

Precise punctuation signals the relationships between ideas that syntax alone cannot convey. A semicolon links two independent clauses that are closely related in thought but not joined by a coordinating conjunction: “The data were inconclusive; further experiments are planned.” It also separates items in a complex list where the items themselves contain commas: “The committee included Dr. Patel, the chair; Ms. Lee, the treasurer; and Mr. Khan, the secretary.” A colon introduces an explanation, a list, or a quotation that follows an independent clause: “The hypothesis rests on three assumptions: linearity, independence, and homoscedasticity.” The clause preceding the colon must be complete; a fragment such as “The reasons are: …” is considered incorrect in formal examinations. Dashes — both the em dash (—) and the en dash (–) — serve distinct purposes. The em dash marks a sudden break, an appositive with internal commas, or an emphatic parenthetical: “The results — unexpected and contradictory — forced a redesign.” The en dash indicates ranges (pages 10–15) or connects compound adjectives where each element carries equal weight (the New York–London flight). Parentheses enclose supplementary information that is grammatically independent of the main sentence; the surrounding sentence must remain complete if the parenthetical is removed. Overuse or misuse — such as placing a period inside parentheses when the parenthetical is a fragment — is a frequent error.

Examiners test these marks through correction items where a sentence contains a punctuation error and the candidate must choose the correctly punctuated version. Common traps include using a comma where a semicolon is required (comma splice), inserting a colon after a verb phrase (“The ingredients include: flour, sugar, eggs”), replacing an em dash with a hyphen, and nesting parentheses incorrectly. A useful diagnostic is to read the sentence aloud and note the natural pause: a brief pause suggests a comma, a longer pause a semicolon or dash, a full stop a period. For lists, check whether each item is a simple noun phrase (commas suffice) or contains internal commas (semicolons needed). For colons, verify that the preceding clause can stand alone as a sentence. For dashes, ensure the interrupted element is an independent clause or a non‑restrictive appositive. Mastery of these nuances not only avoids mechanical errors but also demonstrates the ability to manipulate sentence rhythm for clarity and emphasis — a skill valued in both objective and descriptive sections.

  • Treating the mandative subjunctive as optional and using a modal instead.
  • Mixing conditional time frames, e.g., using a second‑conditional if‑clause with a first‑conditional main clause.
  • Choosing a collocation that fits the meaning but violates register expectations.
  • Drawing an inference that is plausible in reality but lacks textual support.
  • Failing to restore ellipted material before checking parallelism, leading to missed mismatches.
  • Using a colon after a non‑independent clause or omitting the required independent clause before a colon.
  • Confusing the em dash with a hyphen or using parentheses when the enclosed text is essential to the sentence’s grammar.

Cleft Sentences, Fronting, and Inversion: Information Structure

Cleft constructions split a single clause into two parts to foreground a specific constituent. The it‑cleft (It was the manager who approved the proposal) and the wh‑cleft (What the manager approved was the proposal) both serve the same pragmatic function: they mark the focused element as new or contrastive information while relegating the rest to presupposed background. Fronting achieves a similar effect by moving an adjunct, object, or complement to clause‑initial position (Yesterday the committee met; The proposal the manager approved). Inversion, triggered by negative or restrictive adverbials (Never have I seen such confusion; Only after the audit did the errors surface), forces the auxiliary before the subject, signalling emphasis or a shift in thematic progression. Examiners test these structures by asking candidates to identify the focused element, to rewrite a sentence preserving the original information structure, or to spot violations such as double focus (*It was the manager who it was that approved) or illicit inversion (*Only after the audit the errors surfaced). Mastery requires recognising that the cleft pronoun it is expletive, that the relative clause in an it‑cleft must be finite, and that fronted elements must be constituents, not fragments.

Discourse Markers and Cohesive Devices: Beyond Conjunctions

Discourse markers — however, therefore, moreover, consequently, in fact, on the contrary — operate at the textual level, signalling relations such as contrast, cause‑effect, addition, and concession. Unlike coordinating conjunctions, they are syntactically detachable and often set off by commas or semicolons. Cohesive devices also include lexical chains (reiteration, synonymy, hyponymy), reference (personal, demonstrative, comparative), substitution and ellipsis (e.g., She prefers tea; he does so), and conjunction proper. A sophisticated comprehension item may present a paragraph where the logical flow depends on an implicit therefore that the candidate must infer, or where a marker is misused (e.g., Moreover, the data contradict the hypothesis when However is required). Error‑detection questions frequently embed a marker that clashes with the surrounding rhetorical structure, such as using in addition to introduce a counter‑argument. Recognising the semantic‑pragmatic profile of each marker — its polarity, scope, and register — enables rapid diagnosis of coherence breaks.

Technique: Marker‑Mismatch Identification
Choose the sentence in which the discourse marker is used incorrectly.
A. The experiment failed; consequently, the team revised the protocol.
B. The results were promising; nevertheless, the funding was withdrawn.
C. The data were incomplete; moreover, the conclusions remain tentative.
D. The model predicts a rise; on the contrary, the observed trend is downward.
Option D misuses on the contrary, which signals a direct opposition to a preceding negative claim (e.g., It is not true that X; on the contrary, Y). Here the preceding clause is affirmative, so the appropriate contrast marker is however or nevertheless. Options A, B, and C correctly pair cause‑effect, concession, and addition respectively.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a passage contains multiple discourse markers, underline each and label its relational function (contrast, cause, addition, etc.). Then read the surrounding clauses to verify that the logical relation matches the marker. A mismatch is the most common source of coherence‑error questions.

Advanced Vocabulary Strategies: Morphology, Etymology, and Word Formation

Deep lexical knowledge rests on three pillars: morphological analysis, etymological awareness, and word‑formation rules. Morphemes — roots, prefixes, suffixes — combine systematically; recognising the bound root ‑cred‑ (believe) yields credible, incredulous, credence, accreditation. Prefixes such as dis‑, un‑, non‑, anti‑, counter‑ convey negation or opposition but differ in register (un‑ neutral, anti‑ often technical). Suffixes determine part of speech: ‑tion (noun), ‑ive (adjective), ‑ize (verb). Etymology clarifies semantic nuances: Latin‑derived paternal versus Germanic fatherly carry distinct connotations of formality. Word‑formation processes — derivation, compounding, conversion, blending, clipping, back‑formation — generate new lexical items; for instance, editor → edit (back‑formation) and smog (blend of smoke + fog). Test items may present a nonsense stem and ask for the correct affixation (‑able vs. ‑ible), or require selecting the word that fits a given morphological family. A systematic approach: isolate the root, identify the affixes, determine the resulting part of speech, and verify semantic coherence with the context.

Figurative Language and Stylistic Analysis: Metaphor, Metonymy, Irony

Figurative language operates by mapping a source domain onto a target domain. Metaphor asserts an identity (Time is a thief), simile makes the comparison explicit with like or as, metonymy substitutes an associated entity (The White House issued a statement for the administration), and synecdoche uses part for whole (All hands on deck). Irony conveys a meaning opposite to the literal utterance, often signaled by contextual incongruity or prosodic cues. Personification attributes human traits to non‑human entities. Examiners embed these devices in comprehension passages and ask for the implied meaning, the rhetorical purpose, or the effect on tone. A metaphor may structure an entire argument (e.g., the economy as a machine), while a sustained metonymy can reveal authorial stance. Detecting irony requires evaluating the gap between propositional content and situational expectations. Stylistic analysis also covers parallelism, antithesis, chiasmus, and zeugma, which manipulate syntactic symmetry for emphasis. Candidates should practice annotating passages for figurative clusters and articulating the conceptual mapping in plain language.

Pragmatic Inference: Implicature, Presupposition, and Speech Acts

Pragmatics studies meaning in use. Conversational implicature (Grice) arises when a speaker flouts a maxim, prompting the hearer to infer an unstated meaning: Can you pass the salt? implicates a request, not a query about ability. Conventional implicature attaches to lexical items such as but (contrast) or even (scalar expectation). Presupposition is backgrounded information taken for granted; triggers include definite descriptions (the king of France), factive verbs (regret), and clefts. A sentence like She stopped smoking presupposes she used to smoke. Speech‑act theory classifies utterances by illocutionary force: assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, declarations. Recognising the force helps in sentence‑completion tasks where the blank must match the intended act (e.g., I promise to submit the report vs. I predict …). Examination items often present a dialogue and ask which implicature is licensed, which presupposition fails, or which speech act is performed. A reliable method: isolate the utterance, list the maxims, test for cancellability (implicature) or defeasibility (presupposition), and match the illocutionary point to the answer choices.

  • Assuming every fronted element triggers inversion; only negative or restrictive adverbials do.
  • Treating all discourse markers as interchangeable conjunctions, ignoring their specific relational semantics.
  • Applying a single affix rule (e.g., ‑able for all verbs) without checking etymological class.
  • Confusing metaphor with metonymy; metaphor maps across domains, metonymy stays within a domain.
  • Overlooking presupposition triggers in clefts and factive verbs, leading to false‑positive entailment judgments.
  • Equating conversational implicature with entailment; implicatures are cancellable, entailments are not.
  • Misidentifying the illocutionary force of an utterance because the syntactic form (interrogative) masks the pragmatic function (directive).
