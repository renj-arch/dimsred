Chemical Equilibrium — K, Le Chatelier, Acids/Bases, pH & Buffers

Deep dive into Chemical Equilibrium — the equilibrium constant K = Π a_i^{ν_i} (the ratio of product activities to reactant activities at equilibrium, each raised to the power of its stoichiometric coefficient), K_c (concentration-based) and K_p (pressure-based) with the relation K_p = K_c(RT)^{Δn} where Δn = Σ ν_gas(products) − Σ ν_gas(reactants). The reaction quotient Q (same form as K but for non-equilibrium conditions): if Q < K the reaction proceeds forward, if Q > K it proceeds in reverse, if Q = K it is at equilibrium. Le Chatelier's principle: when a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts in the direction that partially offsets the disturbance — concentration changes (adding reactant shifts right, adding product shifts left), pressure/volume changes (increasing pressure by decreasing volume shifts toward fewer moles of gas), temperature changes (exothermic reaction shifts left when heated, right when cooled; endothermic shifts right when heated, left when cooled). The van't Hoff equation: d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/RT², giving ln(K₂/K₁) = −ΔH°/R(1/T₂ − 1/T₁). Acid-base theories: Arrhenius (acids produce H⁺, bases produce OH⁻ in water), Brønsted-Lowry (acids donate H⁺, bases accept H⁺), Lewis (acids accept electron pairs, bases donate electron pairs). The autoionization of water: 2H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻, K_w = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. The pH scale: pH = −log[H⁺], pOH = −log[OH⁻], pH + pOH = 14. Strong acids/bases dissociate completely; weak acids/bases have K_a (acid dissociation constant, e.g., CH₃COOH: K_a = 1.8×10⁻⁵) and K_b (base dissociation constant, K_b = K_w/K_a for conjugate pairs). Percent ionization = ([HA]_ionized/[HA]_initial)×100%. Buffer solutions: a mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base and conjugate acid) that resists pH change. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pK_a + log([A⁻]/[HA]). Buffer capacity: the amount of strong acid or base that can be added before significant pH change, maximized when [A⁻] = [HA] (pH = pK_a).

"If a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change, the system will adjust to partially oppose the change."
— Henri Louis Le Chatelier
Part 1

⚖️ The Equilibrium Constant and the Reaction Quotient

K and Q
K_c, K_p, the Relation Between Them, and Predicting the Direction of Reaction
For the general reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the equilibrium constant K_c = ([C]^c[D]^d)/([A]^a[B]^b) where square brackets denote molar concentrations at equilibrium. For gas-phase reactions, K_p = (P_C^c P_D^d)/(P_A^a P_B^b) where P_i are partial pressures. The relation between K_p and K_c is: K_p = K_c(RT)^{Δn}, where Δn = (c + d) − (a + b) (the change in moles of gas). For reactions involving solids or pure liquids, their activities are effectively constant (taken as 1) and omitted from the equilibrium expression. For example, for CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g), K = P_{CO₂}. The reaction quotient Q has the same form as K but uses current (non-equilibrium) concentrations. Comparing Q to K determines the direction of spontaneous change: Q < K → forward reaction favored (net conversion of reactants to products); Q > K → reverse reaction favored (net conversion of products to reactants); Q = K → system is at equilibrium. The magnitude of K indicates the extent of reaction: K >> 1 (reaction strongly favors products, goes nearly to completion), K << 1 (reaction strongly favors reactants, very little product forms). The van't Hoff equation gives the temperature dependence of K: d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/RT². Integrated: ln(K₂/K₁) = −ΔH°/R (1/T₂ − 1/T₁). For exothermic reactions (ΔH° < 0), K decreases with increasing temperature. For endothermic (ΔH° > 0), K increases with increasing temperature.
The equilibrium constant quantitatively relates the concentrations of reactants and products at equilibrium; comparing Q to K predicts the direction in which a reaction will proceed.
Part 2

🔄 Le Chatelier's Principle

Le Chatelier
Concentration, Pressure, Volume, Temperature, and Catalyst Effects on Equilibrium
Le Chatelier's principle: when a stress is applied to a system at equilibrium, the equilibrium shifts to relieve the stress. Concentration changes: adding more of a reactant (or removing a product) shifts equilibrium to the right (toward products). Adding more of a product (or removing a reactant) shifts equilibrium to the left. The equilibrium constant K remains unchanged. Pressure and volume changes: for gas-phase reactions, decreasing the volume (increasing the total pressure) shifts the equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas (to reduce pressure). Increasing the volume (decreasing pressure) shifts toward the side with more moles of gas. If Δn = 0 (equal moles of gas on both sides), pressure changes have no effect. Temperature changes: increasing temperature shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction (absorbs heat to offset the increase). Decreasing temperature shifts in the exothermic direction. Unlike concentration and pressure changes, temperature changes alter the value of K. Catalysts speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally — they do not shift the equilibrium position but help the system reach equilibrium faster. Adding an inert gas at constant volume does not change the equilibrium position (partial pressures remain unchanged). Adding an inert gas at constant pressure increases the volume, which shifts equilibrium toward the side with more moles of gas (if Δn ≠ 0). Applications of Le Chatelier: the Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃) uses high pressure (favors product, fewer gas moles) and optimal temperature (compromise between equilibrium yield and reaction rate).
Le Chatelier's principle provides a qualitative framework for predicting how an equilibrium responds to disturbances in concentration, pressure, volume, and temperature.
Part 3

🧪 Acids, Bases, and pH

Acids & Bases
Brønsted-Lowry Theory, K_w, pH Scale, Strong and Weak Acids/Bases, and K_a/K_b
The Arrhenius theory defines acids as substances that produce H⁺ in water and bases as those that produce OH⁻. The Brønsted-Lowry theory generalizes: an acid is a proton (H⁺) donor, a base is a proton acceptor. An acid and its conjugate base differ by one proton (e.g., CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻, NH₄⁺/NH₃). The Lewis theory further generalizes: an acid is an electron pair acceptor, a base is an electron pair donor. Autoionization of water: H₂O + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻, with K_w = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. In pure water, [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0×10⁻⁷ M. The pH scale: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻], pH + pOH = 14 at 25°C. Neutral: pH = 7; acidic: pH < 7; basic: pH > 7. Strong acids (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HI, HBr, HClO₄) dissociate completely — [H⁺] = initial concentration of acid. Strong bases (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂) dissociate completely. Weak acids (e.g., CH₃COOH, HF, HCN) partially dissociate, with K_a = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. For acetic acid, K_a = 1.8×10⁻⁵, pK_a = 4.74. Weak bases (e.g., NH₃, CH₃NH₂) have K_b = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B]. For conjugate pairs: K_a × K_b = K_w, and pK_a + pK_b = 14. Percent ionization = ([HA]_dissociated/[HA]_initial) × 100%. For weak acids, percent ionization increases as the acid is diluted (Le Chatelier: dilution shifts dissociation equilibrium to the right, producing more H⁺ relative to initial). The leveling effect: in water, the strongest possible acid is H₃O⁺ and the strongest possible base is OH⁻; any stronger acid or base reacts completely with water.
The pH scale provides a logarithmic measure of H⁺ concentration; weak acids and bases establish equilibrium between the molecular and ionic forms, quantified by K_a and K_b.
Part 4

🧴 Buffer Solutions and the Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

Buffers
Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation, Buffer Capacity, and Titration Curves
A buffer solution resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. Buffers consist of a weak acid and its conjugate base (e.g., CH₃COOH/CH₃COONa) or a weak base and its conjugate acid (e.g., NH₃/NH₄Cl). The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pK_a + log([A⁻]/[HA]), where pK_a = −log K_a. A similar equation for basic buffers: pOH = pK_b + log([BH⁺]/[B]), with pH = 14 − pOH. A buffer works best (maximum buffer capacity) when [A⁻] = [HA] (pH = pK_a), and is generally effective for pH = pK_a ± 1. Buffer capacity is the amount of strong acid or base that can be neutralized per unit volume before the pH changes significantly. Capacity depends on the absolute concentrations of HA and A⁻ (more concentrated buffers have higher capacity). When a strong acid is added to a buffer, H⁺ reacts with A⁻ to form HA; when a strong base is added, OH⁻ reacts with HA to form A⁻. The pH change is small as long as the buffer capacity is not exceeded. Titration curves show pH versus volume of titrant added. For a strong acid/strong base titration, the equivalence point is at pH 7. For a weak acid/strong base titration, the equivalence point is above 7 (pH > 7) because the conjugate base hydrolyzes. The half-equivalence point (half the volume needed to reach equivalence) is where [HA] = [A⁻], so pH = pK_a — this is the optimal buffer region. For polyprotic acids (e.g., H₃PO₄), there are multiple equivalence points and buffer regions, one for each proton. Indicators are weak acids with different colors for the acid and conjugate base forms; they change color over a pH range roughly pK_a(Indicator) ± 1, chosen to bracket the equivalence point.
The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation relates the pH of a buffer to the pK_a and the ratio of conjugate base to acid concentrations — the foundation of pH control in chemical and biological systems.
