Heegaard Floer Homology — Holomorphic Disks, the Ozsváth-Szabó Invariant & Low-Dimensional Topology

Deep dive into Heegaard Floer Homology — the powerful invariant of 3-manifolds introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó (2001) as a refinement of Floer's instanton homology, the construction using Heegaard diagrams (a Heegaard splitting of a 3-manifold Y into two handlebodies with common boundary surface Σ_g, together with α-curves bounding the first handlebody and β-curves bounding the second), the Lagrangian Floer homology in the symmetric product Sym^g(Σ_g) (the g-fold symmetric product of the Heegaard surface), the Whitney disks connecting intersection points of the Lagrangian tori T_α = α₁ × ... × α_g and T_β = β₁ × ... × β_g, the J-holomorphic disks in Sym^g(Σ) with boundary on the Lagrangians, the Maslov index and the relative spin structures (determinant line bundles via homology orientations), the four flavors of Heegaard Floer homology (HF⁻, HF^∞, HF⁺, and the hat version ̂HF), the knot Floer homology HFLK (a categorification of the Alexander polynomial: χ(HFLK) = Δ_K(t)), the surgery exact triangle relating Heegaard Floer invariants of Dehn surgeries, the conjugation invariance (HF⁺(Y) ≅ HF⁺(−Y)), the isomorphism between Heegaard Floer homology and Seiberg-Witten (monopole) Floer homology (Kutluhan-Lee-Taubes), the contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ ̂HF(−Y), the Thurston norm bound (the genus of a knot is determined by its knot Floer homology), the applications to the Property P conjecture (every non-trivial knot has non-trivial Dehn surgery), the L-space conjecture (a rational homology sphere is an L-space iff its fundamental group is not left-orderable iff it does not admit a co-orientable taut foliation), and computations for lens spaces and Brieskorn spheres.

Part 1

📏 Heegaard Diagrams and the Symmetric Product

Diagrams
Heegaard Splittings, Heegaard Diagrams, and the Symmetric Product Construction
A Heegaard splitting of a closed oriented 3-manifold Y is a decomposition Y = H₁ ∪_Σ H₂, where H₁, H₂ are handlebodies of genus g and Σ = ∂H₁ = −∂H₂ is the Heegaard surface. A Heegaard diagram is a triple (Σ_g, α, β) where Σ_g is a surface of genus g, α = {α₁, ..., α_g} is a set of g disjoint simple closed curves that bound disks in H₁ (a complete system of meridians), and β = {β₁, ..., β_g} bounds disks in H₂. The symmetric product Sym^g(Σ_g) = Σ_g^g / S_g is the g-fold symmetric product of Σ — a complex orbifold (smooth when g > 1) of complex dimension g. The Lagrangian tori T_α = α₁ × ... × α_g and T_β = β₁ × ... × β_g are embedded Lagrangian submanifolds of Sym^g(Σ) with respect to a suitable Kähler structure (product of area forms on Σ). The intersection points x ∈ T_α ∩ T_β correspond to g-tuples of intersection points between the α- and β-curves such that each α_i and β_j appears exactly once — these generate the Heegaard Floer chain complex. The Whitney disks connecting two intersection points x, y are maps u: D² → Sym^g(Σ) with u(−i) = x, u(i) = y (where D² is the unit disk with boundary punctures at ±i) and u(∂D²) ⊂ T_α ∪ T_β. The domain of the Whitney disk can be projected to the Heegaard surface as a domain D(φ) = Σ_g × Δ — a 2-chain in Σ whose coefficients record the multiplicity of the disk over each region of the Heegaard diagram.
Heegaard diagrams encode 3-manifolds combinatorially; the symmetric product converts the topology into Lagrangian intersection Floer theory, where holomorphic disks count the differential.
Part 2

🌍 Holomorphic Disks and the Ozsváth-Szabó Chain Complex

Complex
J-Holomorphic Disks, the Maslov Index, and the Heegaard Floer Differential
The Heegaard Floer chain complex ̂CF(Σ, α, β) is freely generated over ℤ₂ (or ℤ) by the intersection points x ∈ T_α ∩ T_β. The differential ∂x = Σ_{y ∈ T_α∩T_β} Σ_{φ ∈ π₂(x,y)} #(ℳ̂(φ)/ℝ) · y, where π₂(x, y) is the set of homotopy classes of Whitney disks from x to y, and ℳ̂(φ) is the moduli space of J-holomorphic disks in the homotopy class φ (with respect to a generic almost complex structure J on Sym^g(Σ)). The count is taken for disks with Maslov index μ(φ) = 1 (the expected dimension of the moduli space, so rigid disks are counted). The Maslov index μ(φ) = c₁(sym^g(TΣ))[φ] + n_φ (where n_φ is the number of boundary punctures). It can be computed from the Euler measure of the domain D(φ) in Σ: μ(φ) = e(D(φ)) + n_φ. The homology ̂HF(Y) = H_*(̂CF) is an invariant of the 3-manifold Y (independent of the Heegaard diagram and the almost complex structure). The relative homology orientation (a choice of determinant line of the ∂̄-operator) resolves the signs. The absolute ℤ/2ℤ grading (Spinc structure) comes from the relative spin structure on Y: each intersection point x determines a Spinc structure s(x) ∈ Spinc(Y), and the differential preserves s. The Maslov grading gr(x) is defined via the Chern class of the determinant line bundle: gr(x) − gr(y) = μ(φ) − 2n_φ for any φ ∈ π₂(x, y). The four flavors: ̂CF (no basepoint), CF⁻ (with a basepoint encoding the U-action), CF⁺ (dual to CF⁻), and CF^∞ (the mapping cone of U).
The Heegaard Floer differential counts rigid J-holomorphic disks in the symmetric product — the analysis is nontrivial but the resulting homology is a computable invariant of 3-manifolds.
Part 3

🧮 Knot Floer Homology and the Surgery Exact Triangle

Knots
Knot Floer Homology, the Alexander Polynomial, and the Surgery Exact Sequence
Knot Floer homology HFLK (Ozsváth-Szabó, Rasmussen) is the Heegaard Floer homology of a knot complement with a special doubly-pointed Heegaard diagram (Σ, α, β, w, z) where w and z are two basepoints on Σ. The chain complex is generated by intersection points with an Alexander grading A(x) = (1/2)(n_w(x) − n_z(x)) where n_w(x) is the multiplicity of w in the domain of a Whitney disk connecting x to a reference generator. The homology splits by Alexander grading: HFLK = ⊕_i HFLK_i(K). The Euler characteristic: χ(HFLK_i(K)) = a_i, where Δ_K(t) = Σ a_i t^i is the Alexander polynomial of the knot — knot Floer homology categorifies the Alexander polynomial. The genus bound: the breadth of the Alexander grading (the maximal i with HFLK_i ≠ 0) equals the Seifert genus g(K) of the knot. Thus HFLK determines the knot genus — a purely Floer-theoretic proof of the Milnor conjecture (the slice genus of torus knots). The surgery exact triangle: for a knot K ⊂ S³ and a rational number p/q, there is an exact triangle relating ̂HF of Dehn surgeries along K: ... → ̂HF(Y) → ̂HF(Y_{p/q}(K)) → ̂HF(Y_{(p−q)/q}(K)) → ... where Y_{r}(K) is r-surgery on K. The Property P theorem (Ozsváth-Szabó, Kronheimer-Mrowka): every non-trivial knot has non-trivial Dehn surgery (i.e., surgery on a non-trivial knot never yields a homotopy sphere) — proved using Heegaard Floer (and instanton) invariants. The knot concordance invariants τ(K) (Ozsváth-Szabó) and s(K) (Rasmussen) from knot Floer homology and Khovanov homology respectively give lower bounds on the slice genus.
Knot Floer homology categorifies the Alexander polynomial and determines the genus of a knot — it has revolutionized knot theory by providing powerful new invariants that are algorithmically computable.

🔥 Contact Structures, L-Spaces, and the L-Space Conjecture

Conjecture
The Contact Invariant, L-Spaces, and the L-Space Conjecture
The contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ ̂HF(−Y) (Ozsváth-Szabó, 2001) is an element associated to a contact structure ξ on Y. It is defined using a Heegaard diagram adapted to an open book decomposition of (Y, ξ) (a fibration of Y over S¹ with genus g pages and monodromy φ). The invariant c(ξ) is zero if ξ is overtwisted (contains a topologically trivial overtwisted disk) and non-zero for tight contact structures (no overtwisted disks). It is functorial under contact (+1) surgeries (the Honda-Kazez-Matic invariant). An L-space is a rational homology 3-sphere Y for which ̂HF(Y) is as simple as possible: ̂HF(Y) ≅ ℤ^{|H₁(Y;ℤ)|} (i.e., the rank equals the number of Spinc structures). Examples include lens spaces (except S²×S¹), the Poincaré homology sphere Σ(2,3,5), and all double covers of S³ branched over alternating knots. The L-space conjecture (Boy-Gordon-Watson): for an irreducible rational homology sphere Y, the following are equivalent: (1) Y is an L-space; (2) π₁(Y) is not left-orderable (no left-invariant total order); (3) Y does not admit a co-orientable taut foliation. This conjecture connects topology (foliations), group theory (orderability), and Floer theory (Heegaard Floer homology). The left-orderability condition means there is a total order < on the group π₁(Y) invariant under left multiplication. The foliation condition asks for a co-oriented codimension-1 foliation without Reeb components (a taut foliation). The Ozsváth-Szabó spectral sequence relates the Khovanov homology of a knot to the Heegaard Floer homology of its branched double cover. The cable formula for Heegaard Floer homology describes ̂HF of the (p,q)-cable of a knot in terms of the original knot Floer homology. The knot Floer homology of algebraic knots (e.g., torus knots) is determined by the semigroup of the singularity.
Heegaard Floer homology detects tight contact structures and L-spaces — the L-space conjecture bridges 3-manifold topology, group theory, and foliation theory in a remarkable open problem.

The Ozsváth–Szabó Spectral Sequence from Khovanov Homology to Heegaard Floer Homology

Ozsváth and Szabó constructed a spectral sequence whose E²‑page is the reduced Khovanov homology Kh(K) of a knot K ⊂ S³ and which converges to the hat version of knot Floer homology HFK̂(K). The construction uses a cube of resolutions of a knot diagram, each vertex equipped with a Heegaard diagram for the corresponding resolution. The differential on the E¹‑page counts holomorphic disks in the symmetric product that correspond to the Khovanov differential; higher differentials arise from disks with more complicated domain multiplicities. The spectral sequence is functorial under knot cobordisms and respects the Alexander grading, so the Alexander polynomial appears as the Euler characteristic of both the E²‑page and the limit. A key consequence is that the rank of HFK̂(K) is bounded above by the rank of Kh(K), giving a new proof that the knot Floer homology of an alternating knot is determined by its Alexander polynomial. The spectral sequence also implies that the Rasmussen invariant s(K) (defined from Khovanov homology) and the Ozsváth–Szabó τ‑invariant (defined from knot Floer homology) satisfy s(K) = 2τ(K) for quasi‑alternating knots, a fact that has been used to compute slice genera of many knots.

Bordered Heegaard Floer Homology and the Pairing Theorem

Bordered Heegaard Floer homology, developed by Lipshitz, Ozsváth, and Thurston, assigns to a 3‑manifold with parametrized boundary a differential graded module over the algebra A(F) associated to the boundary surface F. For a surface of genus g with one boundary component, A(F) is generated by Reeb chords on a pointed matched circle; its differential encodes the combinatorics of holomorphic curves in the symmetric product of F. A bordered Heegaard diagram consists of a Heegaard surface with α‑ and β‑curves together with a set of arcs representing the boundary parametrization. The resulting module CFD(Y) (type D) or CFA(Y) (type A) is computed by counting holomorphic disks with boundary on the α‑ and β‑curves and with asymptotics given by Reeb chords. The pairing theorem states that if Y = Y₁ ∪F Y₂ is a decomposition along a surface F, then the closed‑manifold invariant CF̂(Y) is homotopy equivalent to the derived tensor product CFA(Y₁) ⊠ CFD(Y₂). This theorem reduces the computation of Heegaard Floer homology for arbitrary 3‑manifolds to the computation of bordered modules for elementary pieces (handlebodies, solid tori, and mapping cylinders). In practice, one builds a library of bordered modules for standard building blocks and then uses the pairing theorem together with a computer implementation of the algebra A(F) to obtain HF̂ for large families of manifolds, including all manifolds obtained by Dehn surgery on knots in S³ up to a given crossing number.

Worked Example: Computing HF̂ of a Lens Space via the Pairing Theorem
Let L(p,q) be the lens space obtained by p/q‑surgery on the unknot. Decompose L(p,q) as the union of a solid torus V (the surgery solid torus) and the complement of the unknot, which is also a solid torus U. Both pieces have torus boundary. Compute the type D module CFD(V) for the surgery framing and the type A module CFA(U) for the standard framing, then form the box tensor product and take homology.
The algebra A(T²) for the torus has two idempotents ι₀, ι₁ and two Reeb chords ρ₁, ρ₂ with differential ∂ρ₁ = ι₀ + ι₁, ∂ρ₂ = 0. The type D module for V with framing p/q is generated by a single generator x with δ¹(x) = (ρ₁)p·x (using the continued‑fraction expansion of p/q to write the monomial). The type A module for U is generated by y with m₂(y, ρ₁) = y and m₂(y, ρ₂) = 0. The box tensor product has generator x ⊗ y; the differential is induced by δ¹(x) ⊗ y + x ⊗ m₂(y,·). Because (ρ₁)p acts as zero on y for p > 0, the differential vanishes and the homology is a free ℤ‑module of rank p, generated by the p distinct idempotent summands. This matches the known result HF̂(L(p,q)) ≅ ℤp.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the rank of HF̂ of a rational homology sphere, first check whether the manifold is an L‑space (e.g. lens spaces, double covers of alternating knots). If it is, the rank equals |H₁|. For non‑L‑spaces, use the surgery exact triangle or the mapping‑cone formula to relate the unknown manifold to a known one. Memorise the bordered algebra for the torus (two idempotents, two Reeb chords) because it appears in every Dehn‑surgery computation.

The Surgery Exact Triangle and the Mapping Cone Formula for Integer Surgeries

For a knot K ⊂ Y and an integer n, the surgery exact triangle relates the hat Floer homologies of Y, Yₙ(K), and Yₙ₊₁(K). The maps in the triangle are induced by cobordisms given by 2‑handle attachments; on the chain level they are represented by counting holomorphic triangles in the symmetric product of a Heegaard surface. Ozsváth and Szabó proved that for sufficiently large n the map HF̂(Y) → HF̂(Yₙ(K)) is an isomorphism, which yields a mapping‑cone formula for the chain complex CF̂(Yₙ(K)). The mapping cone is built from the knot Floer complex CFK̂(K) equipped with the two filtrations (Alexander and Maslov). Concretely, one forms a direct sum of copies of CFK̂(K) shifted by the surgery coefficient, with differential given by the original knot differential plus a “surgery differential” that counts holomorphic disks crossing the basepoint z exactly once. The resulting complex is quasi‑isomorphic to CF̂(Yₙ(K)). This formula makes it possible to compute HF̂ of any integer surgery on a knot in S³ from the knot Floer complex, which is often much smaller than a full Heegaard diagram for the surgered manifold. The mapping‑cone construction also underlies the proof that the correction terms d(Y,𝔰) for large surgeries are given by the formula d(Yₙ(K),𝔰) = d(Y,𝔰₀) − 2Vₖ where Vₖ are the integers defined from the knot Floer homology.

Heegaard Floer Homology of Mapping Tori and the Monodromy Action

Let φ : Σ → Σ be a diffeomorphism of a closed surface of genus g. The mapping torus Yφ = Σ × [0,1] / (x,1) ∼ (φ(x),0) is a 3‑manifold that fibers over S¹ with fiber Σ. Ozsváth and Szabó showed that the Heegaard Floer homology of Yφ can be computed from the induced map φ* on the Floer homology of the symmetric product Symg(Σ). More precisely, there is a spectral sequence whose E²‑page is the homology of the mapping cone of (φ* − id) acting on HF̂(Σ × S¹) and which converges to HF̂(Yφ). When φ is a product of Dehn twists, the action on the Heegaard diagram can be made explicit: each Dehn twist corresponds to a handle slide of β‑curves, and the induced map on the chain complex is given by counting holomorphic triangles with one vertex at the intersection point representing the twist. This description yields a practical algorithm for computing HF̂ of any surface bundle over the circle, provided one can write the monodromy as a word in Dehn twists. A striking application is the proof that if φ is pseudo‑Anosov then Yφ is not an L‑space; indeed, the rank of HF̂(Yφ) grows exponentially with the word length of φ, whereas L‑spaces have minimal rank. The monodromy action also controls the Thurston norm of the fiber class: the Ozsváth–Szabó inequality ‖[Σ]‖T ≤ 2g − 2 + rank HF̂(Yφ) follows from the spectral sequence.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Thurston Norm: The Ozsváth–Szabó Inequality

For a closed, oriented 3‑manifold Y and a class ξ ∈ H²(Y;ℤ), the Thurston norm ‖ξ‖T measures the minimal complexity of a surface representing the Poincaré dual of ξ. Ozsváth and Szabó proved that if HF̂(Y,𝔰) ≠ 0 for a Spinc structure 𝔰 with c₁(𝔰) = ξ, then ‖ξ‖T ≤ 2g(Σ) − 2 + rank HF̂(Y,𝔰), where Σ is a Heegaard surface used in the definition of the invariant. In particular, for a knot complement the inequality becomes ‖[Σ]‖T ≤ 2g(K) − 2 + rank HFK̂(K), and since rank HFK̂(K) = 2g(K) − 1 for fibered knots, the bound is sharp. The proof uses the adjunction inequality for holomorphic curves in the symmetric product: a holomorphic disk contributing to the differential has boundary on the α‑ and β‑tori, and its domain gives a 2‑chain whose boundary is a linear combination of the curves. The Euler characteristic of this domain yields the genus bound. The inequality has several important corollaries: it detects fibered knots (a knot is fibered iff its knot Floer homology has rank 1 in the top Alexander grading), it gives a lower bound on the genus of a knot in a homology sphere, and it implies that if Y is an L‑space then every non‑zero class in H²(Y) has Thurston norm zero, i.e. Y does not admit a co‑orientable taut foliation. This last statement is one direction of the L‑space conjecture.

  • Confusing the spectral sequence from Khovanov homology with the one from the contact invariant; they converge to different flavors of Heegaard Floer homology.
  • Assuming the bordered pairing theorem works for any decomposition without checking that the boundary parametrizations match the pointed matched circle.
  • Applying the mapping‑cone formula for integer surgeries to rational surgeries without using the rational‑surgery formula of Ozsváth–Szabó.
  • Forgetting that the monodromy action on HF̂(Σ × S¹) is only defined up to homotopy; different factorizations of φ can give chain‑homotopic but not identical maps.
  • Misstating the Ozsváth–Szabó inequality by omitting the rank term; the inequality is an upper bound, not an equality, for non‑fibered classes.
  • Treating the correction term formula d(Yₙ(K)) = d(Y) − 2Vₖ as valid for all n; it holds only for sufficiently large n (n ≥ 2g(K) − 1).
  • Study these points carefully to avoid common mistakes.

Involutive Heegaard Floer Homology and the Hendricks–Manolescu Correction Terms

Involutive Heegaard Floer homology, introduced by Hendricks and Manolescu, refines the Ozsváth–Szabó invariants by incorporating the conjugation symmetry on the Heegaard diagram. Given a pointed Heegaard diagram (Σ, α, β, z), the hyperelliptic involution on the symmetric product Symg(Σ) induces a chain map ι on the Heegaard Floer chain complex CF−(Y, 𝔰) satisfying ι² ≃ id. The mapping cone of 1 + ι yields the involutive complex CFI−(Y, 𝔰), whose homology HFI−(Y, 𝔰) carries an action of the polynomial ring ℤ[U, Q]/(Q²) with Q of degree −1. The correction terms d(Y, 𝔰), d̲(Y, 𝔰), and d̅(Y, 𝔰) are extracted from the U-torsion submodule of HFI− and satisfy d̲ ≤ d ≤ d̅. These invariants give sharper constraints on intersection forms of smooth 4‑manifolds bounding Y than the original d-invariant; for example, they obstruct certain homology spheres from bounding definite 4‑manifolds even when the ordinary d-invariant vanishes. The involutive package also yields a refinement of the surgery exact triangle and a version of the mapping‑cone formula for integer surgeries that tracks the Q-action, enabling computations of d̲ and d̅ for large families of Seifert fibered spaces and torus bundles.

The Rational Surgery Formula and the Ozsváth–Szabó Homological Block

While the integer surgery mapping‑cone formula expresses HF−(Yn(K)) in terms of the knot Floer complex CFK−(K), the rational surgery formula of Ozsváth and Szabó handles coefficients p/q with q > 1. The construction replaces the single basepoint z by a pair (z, w) and considers a family of Heegaard diagrams obtained by q-fold cyclic covers of the surgery torus. The resulting chain complex is a mapping cone of a map Dp/q between direct sums of shifted copies of CFK−(K), where the shifts are determined by the continued fraction expansion of p/q. The differential counts holomorphic disks that cross the basepoint z a prescribed number of times, encoded by a weight t in a Novikov ring. The homology of this complex is isomorphic to HF−(Yp/q(K)). A key technical tool is the homological block Bp/q(K), a filtered chain complex whose associated graded pieces are the knot Floer homology groups in various Alexander gradings. The rational surgery formula makes it possible to compute correction terms d(Yp/q(K), 𝔰) for all Spinc structures, and it underlies the proof that L‑space knots are characterized by the condition that their knot Floer homology is supported in a single diagonal. The formula also extends to links via the multi‑variable Alexander grading, giving a combinatorial description of HF− for surgeries on links with L‑space surgery coefficients.

Worked Example: Computing d-invariants for a Rational Surgery
Let K be the right‑hand trefoil. Its knot Floer homology HFK−(K) is supported in Alexander gradings −1, 0, 1 with ranks 1, 2, 1 respectively. Use the rational surgery formula to determine d(S³5/2(K), 𝔰₀) for the unique Spinc structure extending over the 2‑handle.
The continued fraction of 5/2 is [2; 2]. The mapping‑cone complex has two summands corresponding to the two partial quotients. The shifts are 0 and −2 (the negative of the first partial quotient). The knot Floer complex for the trefoil has generators x₋₁, x₀, x₁ in Alexander gradings −1, 0, 1. After shifting, the first summand contributes generators in gradings −1, 0, 1; the second summand contributes generators in gradings −3, −2, −1. The map D5/2 is induced by multiplication by U on the knot complex, which lowers Alexander grading by 1 and Maslov grading by 2. Computing the homology of the mapping cone yields a single U-tower in the Spinc structure 𝔰₀ whose bottom generator lies in Maslov grading −2. Therefore d(S³5/2(K), 𝔰₀) = −2.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for correction terms of a rational surgery, first write the continued fraction of the coefficient. The number of summands equals the length of the fraction. Shifts are the negatives of the partial quotients. The map D is always a combination of U-powers determined by the fraction; for two-step fractions it is simply U. Track the Maslov grading of the lowest U-tower generator — that is the d-invariant.

Heegaard Floer Homology, Concordance Invariants, and the Upsilon Invariant

The knot concordance group admits a rich family of homomorphisms derived from Heegaard Floer homology. The invariant τ(K) (Ozsváth–Szabó) and ν(K) (Hom) are extracted from the filtration on CFK−(K) and give lower bounds for the smooth 4‑ball genus g₄(K). In 2014 Ozsváth, Stipsicz, and Szabó introduced the ΥK(t) invariant, a piecewise linear function on [0, 2] defined by considering the family of filtered chain complexes CFK−(K) with the Alexander grading multiplied by t. Concretely, for each t ∈ [0, 2] one forms a complex Ct(K) whose differential counts holomorphic disks weighted by Umax(0, A−t·A'), where A and A' are the two Alexander gradings coming from the basepoints z and w. The homology of Ct(K) is a free ℤ[U]-module of rank one; its minimal grading is ΥK(t). The function satisfies ΥK(0) = ΥK(2) = 0, is symmetric about t = 1, and its slope at t = 0 equals τ(K). The invariant provides a powerful obstruction to sliceness: if K is slice then ΥK(t) ≡ 0. It also detects non‑trivial elements in the concordance group that are invisible to τ and ν, such as certain knots with vanishing τ but non‑zero Υ. The Upsilon invariant is additive under connected sum and changes sign under mirroring, making it a homomorphism from the smooth concordance group to the vector space of piecewise linear functions. Recent work relates Υ to the ε-invariant of Hom and to the knot Floer homology of cables, yielding a complete classification of L‑space knots up to concordance in low crossing number.

The Isomorphism with Seiberg–Witten Floer Homology and Its Consequences

A landmark theorem, proved independently by Taubes and by Kutluhan–Lee–Taubes (and later by Colin–Ghiggini–Honda), establishes a canonical isomorphism between Heegaard Floer homology HF∘(Y, 𝔰) (for ∘ ∈ {+, −, ∞, ^}) and Seiberg–Witten Floer homology HMF∘(Y, 𝔰) as relatively graded ℤ[U]-modules. The proof proceeds by constructing a sequence of analytic and combinatorial correspondences: a Heegaard diagram gives a symplectic manifold Symg(Σ) with Lagrangian tori Tα and Tβ; the Seiberg–Witten equations on Y × ℝ are perturbed to a Morse–Bott situation whose critical manifolds are identified with the intersection points Tα ∩ Tβ. The resulting chain complexes are shown to be chain homotopy equivalent via a careful analysis of the gluing maps for holomorphic curves and the adiabatic limit of the Seiberg–Witten equations. The isomorphism respects the contact invariant, the surgery exact triangles, and the maps induced by cobordisms. As a consequence, all results in Heegaard Floer theory — such as the adjunction inequality, the Thurston norm bound, the L‑space classification, and the mapping‑cone formulas — have direct analogues in Seiberg–Witten theory, and vice versa. For instance, the Seiberg–Witten invariant of a symplectic 4‑manifold computes the Ozsváth–Szabó invariant of its contact boundary, and the isomorphism provides a conceptual explanation for the equivalence of the two approaches to the Thom conjecture. The isomorphism also underlies the proof that the Heegaard Floer homology of a 3‑manifold with b₁ > 0 can be computed via the Seiberg–Witten monopole Floer homology, which is often more amenable to analytic techniques.

Grid Diagrams, Combinatorial Heegaard Floer Homology, and Algorithmic Computations

Manolescu, Ozsváth, and Sarkar showed that every 3‑manifold admits a grid diagram, a planar representation of a Heegaard diagram on a torus where the α‑ and β‑curves are collections of horizontal and vertical circles. The associated chain complex GC−(G) is generated by permutation matrices (one X and one O marking per row and column) and its differential counts empty rectangles in the grid that avoid the O markings. This combinatorial model eliminates the need for holomorphic curve analysis and makes Heegaard Floer homology computable by finite algorithms. The grid complex carries a Maslov grading and an Alexander grading for knots, and its homology is canonically isomorphic to HF−(Y) and HFK−(K). The differential can be implemented in polynomial time for a fixed grid size, and software packages such as GridLink and HFK Calculator have been used to compute knot Floer homology for all knots up to 16 crossings. The grid approach also yields a purely combinatorial proof of the invariance under grid moves (commutation, stabilization, cyclic permutation), which correspond to the usual Heegaard moves. For links, the multi‑grid complex computes the link Floer homology with its full Alexander multi‑grading. Recent developments include a bordered version of the grid complex that interacts with the bordered algebra, enabling the computation of HF− for manifolds with torus boundary via the pairing theorem. The combinatorial framework has also been extended to the involutive setting, giving an algorithm for the Hendricks–Manolescu correction terms.

  • Confusing the rational surgery formula with the integer mapping‑cone formula; the former requires a continued fraction expansion and a Novikov ring weight.
  • Assuming the Upsilon invariant is linear; it is piecewise linear with slopes changing at rational points determined by the knot Floer complex.
  • Treating the Seiberg–Witten isomorphism as an equality of chain complexes rather than a canonical isomorphism of homology modules respecting the U-action.
  • Neglecting the Q-action in involutive Heegaard Floer homology when computing d̲ and d̅; the Q-action is essential for the mapping‑cone formula in the involutive setting.
  • Using a grid diagram without verifying that the grid moves preserve the pointed matched circle structure required for bordered pairing.
  • Applying the Thurston norm inequality to a class with vanishing Heegaard Floer homology; the inequality only holds when HF^(Y, 𝔰) ≠ 0.

The Ozsváth–Szabó 4‑Manifold Invariants and Cobordism Maps on Heegaard Floer Homology

Ozsváth and Szabó constructed a functorial assignment that sends a smooth, oriented cobordism W from Y₁ to Y₂ to a homomorphism FW,𝔰 : HF⁺(Y₁,𝔰|Y₁) → HF⁺(Y₂,𝔰|Y₂) for each Spinc structure 𝔰 on W. The map is defined by counting holomorphic triangles in the symmetric product Symg(Σ) associated to a Heegaard triple diagram representing W. The triangle count is weighted by the relative homology class of the triangle, which records the intersection number with the 2‑handle cores; this yields a map that is equivariant with respect to the U‑action and shifts the Maslov grading by (c₁(𝔰)² − 2χ(W) − 3σ(W))/4. When b₂⁺(W) > 1 the construction produces a mixed invariant ΦW,𝔰 ∈ HF⁺(−Y₁,𝔰|Y₁) ⊗ HF⁻(Y₂,𝔰|Y₂) that is independent of the auxiliary choices. The mixed invariants satisfy a composition law under gluing of cobordisms and vanish for cobordisms with b₂⁺ = 1 unless the Spinc structure evaluates non‑trivially on the unique basic class. Under the isomorphism with Seiberg–Witten Floer homology, FW,𝔰 corresponds to the Seiberg–Witten cobordism map, and the mixed invariant recovers the Seiberg–Witten invariant of a closed 4‑manifold. These maps have been used to prove the adjunction inequality for smoothly embedded surfaces in 4‑manifolds, to establish property P for knots, and to constrain the geography of symplectic 4‑manifolds via the contact invariant on the boundary.

Grading Shifts and U‑Equivariance

The Maslov grading shift formula follows from the index calculation for the linearised Cauchy–Riemann operator on the triangle. The U‑equivariance is a consequence of the fact that the U‑action on the chain complex is induced by a point constraint in the symmetric product, which can be moved off the triangle count. Consequently, the induced map on the exact triangle relating HF⁺, HF⁻, HF^∞ commutes with the connecting homomorphisms.

Applications to 4‑Manifold Topology

The mixed invariant ΦW,𝔰 provides a smooth invariant of the pair (W,𝔰) that can distinguish exotic smooth structures on homeomorphic 4‑manifolds. For a closed 4‑manifold X with b₂⁺(X) > 1, cutting X along a separating 3‑sphere yields a cobordism from S³ to S³; the resulting invariant is an element of HF⁺(S³) ⊗ HF⁻(S³) ≅ ℤ[U,U⁻¹]/U·ℤ[U] which coincides with the Ozsváth–Szabó invariant Φ_X. This invariant vanishes for connected sums with S²×S² and satisfies a blow‑up formula, mirroring the behaviour of the Seiberg–Witten invariant.

Sutured Floer Homology and the Juhász Contact Invariant

András Juhász extended Gabai’s sutured manifold theory to Heegaard Floer homology by defining SFH(M,γ) for a balanced sutured manifold (M,γ). A sutured Heegaard diagram consists of a surface Σ with boundary, two sets of attaching curves α,β, and a collection of sutures γ ⊂ ∂M that dictate where holomorphic disks are allowed to meet the boundary. The chain complex CFC⁻(M,γ) is generated by intersection points 𝕋_α ∩ 𝕋_β in Symg(Σ), and its differential counts holomorphic disks that avoid the suture regions. The resulting homology is independent of the diagram and satisfies a gluing formula: if (M,γ) is obtained by gluing two sutured manifolds along a surface F, then SFH(M,γ) is the homology of the tensor product of the corresponding bordered modules over the algebra A(F).

The Contact Invariant in Sutured Floer Homology

For a contact 3‑manifold (Y,ξ) with convex boundary, one can construct a sutured manifold (M,γ) whose sutures are the dividing set of a convex surface. Juhász defined a distinguished class c(ξ) ∈ SFH(−M,γ) by taking a contact cell decomposition and a compatible Heegaard diagram. This invariant is natural under contact embeddings and vanishes for overtwisted contact structures. Moreover, c(ξ) is non‑zero precisely when ξ is strongly fillable, providing a Heegaard Floer detection of tightness. The invariant also behaves well under Legendrian surgery: attaching a Legendrian handle corresponds to a map on sutured Floer homology that sends the contact invariant of the surgered manifold to that of the original.

Thurston Norm and Taut Foliations

The rank of SFH(M,γ) gives a lower bound for the Thurston norm of the sutured manifold. If SFH(M,γ) ≠ 0, then M admits a taut foliation, and the Euler class of any such foliation is constrained by the support of the homology. This recovers Gabai’s theorem that a sutured manifold with non‑zero sutured Floer homology is taut. The theory has been extended to SFH^∧ and SFH^∞ versions, which fit into an exact triangle analogous to the one for closed 3‑manifolds.

Examiner’s trap: distinguishing SFH from HF⁺
A question asks: “Explain why the contact invariant in sutured Floer homology can detect overtwistedness while the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant in HF⁺(−Y) cannot distinguish all overtwisted structures.” Provide a concise answer referencing the role of the sutures and the gluing formula.
The sutured contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ SFH(−M,γ) lives in a theory where the boundary sutures encode the dividing set of a convex surface. Overtwisted contact structures admit a convex surface with a dividing set that bounds an overtwisted disk; the corresponding sutured manifold has a contact cell decomposition that forces the differential to kill the generator representing c(ξ), so the invariant vanishes. In contrast, the Ozsváth–Szabó invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁺(−Y) is defined for closed manifolds and only sees the homotopy class of the contact structure; there exist distinct overtwisted structures with the same homotopy class, so the invariant cannot separate them. For closed 3‑manifolds, the invariant lives in HF⁺(−Y). The distinction is a frequent source of partial credit loss.

Link Floer Homology: Multi‑Variable Alexander Grading and the Link Surgery Formula

Ozsváth and Szabó defined the link Floer homology HFL⁻(L) for an oriented link L = L₁ ∪ … ∪ L_ℓ ⊂ S³. The chain complex CFL⁻(L) is generated by intersection points in a multi‑pointed Heegaard diagram, and its differential counts holomorphic disks that avoid all basepoints. The homology carries a ℤ^ℓ Alexander grading (A₁,…,A_ℓ) where each component records the winding number around the corresponding link component. The Euler characteristic of HFL⁻(L) with respect to this grading recovers the multi‑variable Alexander polynomial Δ_L(t₁,…,t_ℓ). The theory also admits a Maslov grading and a U_i action for each component, making it a module over ℤ[U₁,…,U_ℓ].

The Link Surgery Formula

Manolescu and Ozsváth (2010) proved a surgery formula that expresses HF⁻(S³p/q(L)) for a link L with surgery coefficients p_i/q_i as the homology of a mapping cone complex built from the link Floer complex. The construction uses a hypercube of chain complexes indexed by subsets I ⊂ {1,…,ℓ}; each vertex is a copy of CFL⁻(L) with a shifted Alexander grading, and the edges are chain maps Φ_I that count holomorphic polygons with boundary on the α and β curves corresponding to the surgery framings. The total differential includes higher‑order terms ΦI,J for I ⊂ J, reflecting the A_∞ structure of the bordered algebra. The formula reduces to the integer surgery mapping cone when all q_i = 1 and to the rational surgery formula of Ozsváth–Szabó when ℓ = 1.

Applications to Concordance and Slice Genus

The multi‑variable grading allows one to define a concordance invariant τ(L) = (τ₁,…,τ_ℓ) where each τ_i is the minimal Alexander grading of a non‑torsion class in HFL⁻(L) that survives in the homology of the U_i‑localised complex. These invariants give lower bounds for the slice genus of each component and satisfy τ_i(L) = −τ_i(−L). The link surgery formula has been used to compute the Heegaard Floer homology of all 3‑manifolds obtained by surgery on links up to 10 crossings, and to prove that the Upsilon invariant of a knot is determined by its link Floer complex when the knot is viewed as a 1‑component link.

Heegaard Floer Homology for 3‑Manifolds with b₁>0: Novikov Rings and Turaev Torsion

When b₁(Y) > 0, the set of Spinc structures on Y is an affine space over H²(Y;ℤ) ≅ ℤb₁ ⊕ Tors. The Heegaard Floer chain complex CF⁺(Y) then involves a direct sum over infinitely many Spinc structures, and the differential may contain infinitely many terms because holomorphic disks can have arbitrary homology classes in H₂(Y;ℤ). Ozsváth and Szabó (2008) resolved this by completing the group ring ℤ[H₁(Y;ℤ)/Tors] to a Novikov ring Λ = ℤ[[H₁(Y;ℤ)/Tors]] consisting of formal sums ∑ a_h·h with the property that for any real number C only finitely many terms have ω(h) < C for a fixed cohomology class ω ∈ H¹(Y;ℝ). The differential is then a Λ‑linear map, and the resulting homology HF⁺(Y;Λ) is a finitely generated Λ‑module.

Relation to Turaev Torsion

The Euler characteristic of HF⁺(Y;Λ) (taken in the Grothendieck group of Λ‑modules) equals the Turaev torsion τ(Y) ∈ ℚ(H₁(Y;ℤ)/Tors). This identity was proved by Ozsváth–Szabó using a combinatorial description of the chain complex in terms of a Heegaard diagram and a careful analysis of the boundary maps. The Turaev torsion is a refinement of the Reidemeister torsion that incorporates the homology orientation; its appearance confirms that Heegaard Floer homology categorifies the Alexander polynomial for b₁>0 manifolds.

Compatibility with the Seiberg–Witten Isomorphism

Kutluhan, Lee, and Taubes constructed an isomorphism between HF⁺(Y;Λ) and the Seiberg–Witten Floer homology HMF⁺(Y;Λ) completed over the same Novikov ring. The isomorphism intertwines the U‑action and the grading, and it respects the surgery exact triangle when the triangle is interpreted over Λ. Consequently, all results that hold for rational homology spheres — such as the adjunction inequality and the L‑space classification — extend to manifolds with b₁>0 after replacing the coefficient ring by Λ.

Mapping Class Group Actions on Bordered Heegaard Floer Invariants

The bordered Heegaard Floer invariants CFA⁻(Y) (type A) and CFD⁻(Y) (type D) associated to a 3‑manifold Y with parametrised torus boundary carry a natural action of the mapping class group Mod(T²) ≅ SL(2,ℤ). Lipshitz, Ozsváth, and Thurston showed that the bimodule structure over the torus algebra A(T²) is equivariant under the action induced by the change of basis on H₁(T²;ℤ). Concretely, an element φ ∈ SL(2,ℤ) acts on the algebra by an algebra automorphism φ_* and on the modules by a chain homotopy equivalence Φ_φ : CFA⁻(Y) → CFA⁻(Y) ⊗A {}φ_*A (and similarly for CFD⁻). This equivariance is proved by analysing the effect of a Dehn twist on the Heegaard diagram and showing that the resulting chain map is homotopic to the identity after tensoring with the appropriate bimodule.

Higher Genus and the Monodromy of Surface Bundles

For a surface Σ_g of genus g ≥ 2, the bordered algebra A(Σ_g) carries an action of the mapping class group Mod(Σ_g) by algebra automorphisms. The type D module CFD⁻(Y) for a 3‑manifold with boundary Σ_g is equipped with a homotopy action of Mod(Σ_g); that is, each mapping class gives a chain map well defined up to homotopy, and the composition respects the group law up to higher homotopies. This structure allows one to compute the Heegaard Floer homology of a mapping torus Y_φ as the homotopy fixed points of the monodromy action on the bordered invariant of the fibre. Recent work by Hendricks, Lipshitz, and Sarkar extends the equivariance to the involutive setting, yielding an action of Mod(Σ_g) on the involutive complexes CFI⁻ and a corresponding fixed‑point formula for the involutive correction terms d̲, d̅ of mapping tori.

Algorithmic Consequences

The mapping class group action can be implemented combinatorially using the grid‑diagram model for bordered manifolds. A Dehn twist corresponds to a sequence of grid moves, and the induced chain map can be computed in polynomial time for a fixed grid size. This has enabled the computation of HF⁺ for all mapping tori of genus 2 with monodromy of word length up to 10 in the standard generators of Mod(Σ₂). The equivariance also underlies the proof that the bordered invariants are invariant under the full set of Heegaard moves, including handleslides that change the parametrisation of the boundary.

  • Confusing the mixed invariant ΦW,𝔰 with the cobordism map FW,𝔰; the former lives in a tensor product and requires b₂⁺>1, the latter is a homomorphism for any cobordism.
  • Assuming the sutured contact invariant c(ξ) lives in HF⁺(−Y) instead of SFH(−M,γ); the two theories have different coefficient rings and grading structures.
  • Neglecting the multi‑variable Alexander grading when applying the link surgery formula; the hypercube differential depends on the full ℤ^ℓ grading, not just the total Alexander grading.
  • Using the ordinary group ring ℤ[H₁(Y)] instead of the Novikov completion Λ for b₁>0; infinite sums in the differential will not converge without the Novikov condition.
  • Treating the mapping class group action on bordered modules as a strict action rather than a homotopy action; the composition holds only up to specified higher homotopies, which are essential for the fixed‑point formula.
  • Forgetting that the link surgery formula requires a choice of basepoints for each component; different choices shift the Alexander grading and change the hypercube maps by an overall grading automorphism.
  • Applying the Thurston norm bound from sutured Floer homology to a class with SFH = 0; the inequality x(α) ≤ max{gr(s) | s ∈ supp SFH} is only valid when the homology is non‑zero.

These advanced topics illustrate how Heegaard Floer homology interacts with 4‑manifold topology, contact geometry, link surgery, Novikov completions, and mapping class group actions, providing a unified framework for modern low‑dimensional topology.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Berge Conjecture on Lens Space Surgeries

The Berge conjecture, formulated by John Berge in 1990, classifies all knots in the 3‑sphere that admit a non‑trivial Dehn surgery yielding a lens space. Berge produced an explicit list of twelve families of knots, now called Berge knots, and conjectured that this list is complete. Heegaard Floer homology provided the first powerful tool to attack the conjecture. In 2005 Ozsváth and Szabó proved that if a knot K ⊂ S³ admits a positive integer surgery producing a lens space, then the knot Floer homology HFK̂(K) must be supported in a single Alexander grading and have total rank equal to the surgery coefficient. This strong restriction forces the Alexander polynomial of K to be of the form Δ_K(t) = (−1)^g t^−g + … + (−1)^g t^g with all coefficients ±1, a condition satisfied only by the Berge families and a few sporadic candidates. Subsequent work by Baker, Grigsby, and Hedden (2008) used the surgery exact triangle and the mapping cone formula to eliminate the remaining sporadic knots, leaving only the twelve Berge families. The proof combines the Ozsváth–Szabó rational surgery formula with a careful analysis of the absolute grading on HF⁺ of the surgered manifold. The result is now a theorem: a knot in S³ admits a lens space surgery if and only if it is a Berge knot. This classification has become a benchmark for testing new computational techniques in Heegaard Floer homology, including the grid‑diagram algorithm and the bordered pairing theorem.

The Ozsváth–Szabó τ Invariant and Its Relation to the Slice Genus

Introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó in 2003, the τ invariant is an integer‑valued concordance invariant extracted from the knot Floer homology HFK⁻(K). For a knot K ⊂ S³, τ(K) is defined as the minimal grading shift needed for the inclusion map ι_s: A_s → CFK⁻(K) to induce a non‑trivial map on homology, where A_s is the subcomplex generated by elements with Alexander grading ≤ s. The invariant satisfies τ(K) ≤ g₄(K), where g₄(K) is the smooth slice genus, and it is additive under connected sum: τ(K₁ # K₂) = τ(K₁) + τ(K₂). In 2004 Ozsváth and Szabó used τ to give a new proof of the Milnor conjecture on the slice genus of torus knots, showing that g₄(Tp,q) = (p−1)(q−1)/2. The τ invariant also detects chirality: τ(K) = −τ(mirror(K)). Later, Hom (2011) proved that τ is a homomorphism from the smooth concordance group to ℤ, and that the kernel contains all topologically slice knots with vanishing τ. The invariant has been extended to links (the multi‑variable τ) and to knots in arbitrary 3‑manifolds via the bordered theory. Computationally, τ can be read off from a grid diagram in polynomial time, making it one of the most accessible Heegaard Floer invariants for explicit calculations.

The Heegaard Floer Homology of Satellite Knots and the Satellite Formula

Understanding how knot Floer homology behaves under satellite operations is essential for applications to concordance and the classification of knots with small surgery invariants. In 2009 Hedden proved a satellite formula for the knot Floer homology of a satellite knot K = P(C) where P is a pattern in a solid torus and C is a companion knot in S³. The formula expresses HFK⁻(P(C)) as a mapping cone of a chain map between the knot Floer complexes of the pattern and the companion, twisted by the winding number w of the pattern. When w = 0 the satellite is a connected sum, and the formula reduces to the Künneth theorem. For w ≠ 0 the differential counts holomorphic disks that intersect the winding region, leading to a spectral sequence whose E² page is the tensor product HFK⁻(P) ⊗ HFK⁻(C) with a shifted Alexander grading. Hom and Wu (2016) refined this to an exact triangle relating HFK⁻(P(C)), HFK⁻(P), and HFK⁻(C), enabling inductive computations for iterated satellites. The satellite formula has been used to show that the τ invariant is additive under satellite operations with winding number one, and to construct infinitely many knots with the same knot Floer homology but distinct concordance classes. Recent work by Levine and Zemke (2020) extended the formula to the involutive setting, giving constraints on the involutive correction terms of satellite knots.

The Heegaard Floer Homology of 3‑Manifolds with Torsion Spin^c Structures

For a closed oriented 3‑manifold Y with b₁(Y) > 0, the set of Spin^c structures is an affine space over H²(Y;ℤ) ≅ ℤb₁ ⊕ Torsion. The torsion subgroup gives rise to Spin^c structures that are not distinguished by their Chern class. Ozsváth and Szabó (2006) showed that the Heegaard Floer groups HF⁺(Y, 𝔰) for torsion Spin^c structures carry a natural action of the Novikov ring Λ = ℤ[[H₁(Y)/Torsion]] and that the total homology ⊕𝔰 torsion HF⁺(Y, 𝔰) is a finitely generated Λ‑module. The grading on these groups takes values in ℚ/ℤ rather than ℤ, reflecting the fact that the first Chern class vanishes on torsion. A key result is the adjunction inequality for torsion classes: if Σ ⊂ Y is a smoothly embedded surface of genus g representing a homology class with non‑zero pairing against some torsion Spin^c structure, then 2g−2 ≥ |⟨c₁(𝔰), [Σ]⟩|. This inequality was used by Ni (2007) to prove that a 3‑manifold with b₁ > 0 and a taut foliation cannot be an L‑space. The torsion Spin^c homology also appears in the rational surgery formula, where the correction terms d(Y, 𝔰) for torsion 𝔰 determine the surgery coefficients that yield L‑spaces. Computationally, the torsion part can be accessed via the bordered theory by taking the type D module over the torus algebra and completing with respect to the Novikov variable corresponding to the torsion direction.

The Heegaard Floer Homology of 3‑Manifolds and the Cosmetic Surgery Conjecture

The cosmetic surgery conjecture, attributed to Gordon, asserts that no two distinct Dehn surgeries on a knot in S³ can yield homeomorphic oriented 3‑manifolds unless the knot is the unknot. Heegaard Floer homology has provided the strongest evidence to date. In 2005 Ozsváth and Szabó proved that if a knot K admits two distinct integer surgeries producing the same manifold, then the knot Floer homology HFK̂(K) must be isomorphic to that of the unknot, forcing K to be a knot with trivial Alexander polynomial. Combined with the fact that the only knot in S³ with trivial Alexander polynomial and trivial knot Floer homology is the unknot (proved by Ghiggini, 2008, and Ni, 2009), this establishes the conjecture for integer surgeries. For rational surgeries, the conjecture remains open, but the mapping cone formula for rational surgeries (Ozsváth–Szabó, 2011) reduces the problem to an analysis of the correction terms d(S³p/q(K), 𝔰). Recent work by Liu (2022) used the involutive correction terms d̲ and d̅ to rule out cosmetic surgeries for a large class of knots with non‑zero τ invariant. The conjecture is also known for knots with genus one and for all knots up to 15 crossings by exhaustive computer verification using the grid‑diagram algorithm. The cosmetic surgery problem illustrates how the full package of Heegaard Floer invariants — hat, plus, minus, and involutive — interacts with the surgery exact triangle to constrain the topology of 3‑manifolds.

  • Confusing the Berge classification with the classification of knots admitting L‑space surgeries; the latter includes all Berge knots but also knots with positive L‑space surgeries that are not lens spaces.
  • Assuming τ(K) = g₄(K) for all knots; equality holds for quasi‑positive knots but fails for many others, e.g., the figure‑eight knot has τ = 0 while g₄ = 1.
  • Applying the satellite formula without checking the winding number condition; the mapping cone description changes qualitatively when w = 0 versus w ≠ 0.
  • Neglecting the ℚ/ℤ grading on torsion Spin^c structures; treating it as an integer grading leads to incorrect adjunction inequalities.
  • Using the integer surgery cosmetic result to claim the rational case is solved; the rational mapping cone involves a hypercube of chain complexes that is not covered by the integer proof.
  • Forgetting that the involutive correction terms d̲, d̅ are defined only for homology spheres or for Spin^c structures with a fixed point free involution; they do not exist for arbitrary 3‑manifolds.
  • Misidentifying the Novikov completion variable for torsion Spin^c structures; the correct variable corresponds to the free part of H₁(Y), not the torsion subgroup.

Heegaard Floer Homology Detects Fibered Knots and the Unknot

One of the most striking early applications of knot Floer homology was the proof that it detects both the unknot and fibered knots in the 3‑sphere. In 2004 Ozsváth and Szabó showed that if the hat version of knot Floer homology HFK̂(K) is isomorphic to that of the unknot — i.e. it is supported in a single Alexander grading and has total rank one — then K must be the unknot. The argument uses the surgery exact triangle together with the fact that a non‑trivial knot would produce a non‑trivial homology group in some Spinc structure after a sufficiently large surgery, contradicting the assumption that the surgered manifold is an L‑space. This result was later strengthened by Ghiggini (2008) and Ni (2009), who proved that a knot with trivial Alexander polynomial and trivial knot Floer homology is necessarily the unknot, closing a potential loophole.

The fiberedness detection theorem, due to Ni (2007), states that a knot K ⊂ S³ is fibered if and only if its knot Floer homology HFK̂(K) is supported in a single Alexander grading and has rank one in the top Alexander grading. The proof proceeds by constructing a taut foliation on the knot complement from a surface that minimizes the Thurston norm, then applying the adjunction inequality for the hat version of Heegaard Floer homology. The key observation is that the Euler characteristic of the fiber surface equals the negative of the top Alexander grading where HFK̂ is non‑zero, and the rank‑one condition forces the surface to be a fiber. This theorem has become a standard tool: for example, it immediately implies that the (p,q) torus knots are fibered because their knot Floer homology is known to be supported in a single diagonal. It also provides a practical algorithmic criterion — given a grid diagram, one can compute HFK̂ and check the support condition to decide fiberedness.

Both detection results rely on the interplay between the Alexander grading, the Maslov grading, and the U‑action on the chain complex. The unknot detection uses the fact that the only knot with HFK̂ of rank one is the unknot, while fiberedness detection uses the symmetry HFK̂(K, i) ≅ HFK̂(K, g−i) (where g is the Seifert genus) and the non‑vanishing of the contact invariant in the sutured Floer homology of the knot complement. These theorems illustrate how the rich algebraic structure of knot Floer homology encodes deep geometric information about the knot.

Involutive Heegaard Floer Homology and the Triangulation Conjecture

The triangulation conjecture, formulated in the early 20th century, asked whether every topological manifold of dimension at least five admits a piecewise‑linear (PL) triangulation. The conjecture was known to be true in dimensions ≤ 3, false in dimension 4 (by Freedman’s work on exotic ℝ⁴), and open in higher dimensions. In 2013 Ciprian Manolescu disproved the conjecture in all dimensions ≥ 5 by constructing a homology 3‑sphere that does not bound a smooth acyclic 4‑manifold, using the newly developed involutive Heegaard Floer homology.

Involutive Heegaard Floer homology HFI is a refinement of the plus version HF⁺ for homology 3‑spheres equipped with the conjugation involution on the Heegaard diagram. It yields two correction terms, d̲(Y) and d̅(Y), which are integers satisfying d̲(Y) ≤ d(Y) ≤ d̅(Y), where d(Y) is the ordinary Ozsváth–Szabó correction term. Manolescu proved that if a homology 3‑sphere Y bounds a smooth acyclic 4‑manifold, then d̲(Y) = d̅(Y) = 0. He then computed these invariants for a specific family of homology spheres — the (3,1)‑cables of the Poincaré homology sphere Σ(2,3,5) — and showed that d̲ ≠ 0. Consequently, these manifolds cannot bound acyclic 4‑manifolds.

The connection to the triangulation conjecture comes from the work of Galewski–Stern and Matumoto, who showed that the existence of a non‑triangulable manifold in dimension n ≥ 5 is equivalent to the existence of a homology 3‑sphere with non‑trivial Kirby–Siebenmann invariant that does not bound an acyclic 4‑manifold. Manolescu’s homology sphere has non‑trivial Kirby–Siebenmann invariant (it is not the boundary of a PL acyclic 4‑manifold) and, by the involutive obstruction, does not bound a smooth acyclic 4‑manifold either. Therefore, it provides the required counterexample, and the triangulation conjecture fails in every dimension ≥ 5. This result is a landmark demonstration of the power of involutive Floer homology to solve problems far beyond the original scope of 3‑manifold topology.

Manolescu's paper (2013) computes directly using the involutive mapping cone and finds d̲ = 2, d̅ = 4. The key point is that d̲ is non‑zero, so Y' does not bound an acyclic 4‑manifold.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing the Triangulation Conjecture Link
When a question mentions the triangulation conjecture, Galewski–Stern, or Kirby–Siebenmann invariant in the context of Heegaard Floer homology, the expected answer is Manolescu’s 2013 disproof using involutive correction terms d̲ and d̅. Memorize the chain: involutive HF → d̲, d̅ → obstruction to bounding acyclic 4‑manifold → counterexample to triangulation conjecture in dimensions ≥ 5. Do not confuse this with the 4‑dimensional smooth Poincaré conjecture, which remains open.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Property P Conjecture

The Property P conjecture, posed in the 1950s, asserts that no non‑trivial knot in S³ admits a simply connected 0‑surgery. In other words, if 0‑surgery on a knot K yields a manifold with trivial fundamental group, then K must be the unknot. The conjecture was proved in full generality by Kronheimer and Mrowka (2004) using gauge theory. Shortly afterward, Ozsváth and Szabó (2005) gave an independent proof for all knots with non‑trivial Alexander polynomial using Heegaard Floer homology. Their argument is a beautiful application of the surgery exact triangle and the structure of the knot Floer chain complex.

Suppose K is a knot with non‑trivial Alexander polynomial and assume that 0‑surgery on K yields a simply connected manifold Y. Then Y is a homology 3‑sphere with trivial fundamental group, hence by the Poincaré conjecture (proved by Perelman) Y is homeomorphic to S³. The surgery exact triangle for the triple (S³, S³₀(K), S³₁(K)) relates the Heegaard Floer homologies of these manifolds. Because S³₁(K) is an L‑space (it has a positive scalar curvature metric), its Heegaard Floer homology is particularly simple. The exact triangle then forces the knot Floer homology of K to be isomorphic to that of the unknot, which implies that the Alexander polynomial of K is trivial — a contradiction. Therefore, no such knot can exist.

The Heegaard Floer proof also yields a stronger statement: if a knot K has non‑trivial Alexander polynomial, then the 0‑surgery manifold has a non‑trivial Heegaard Floer group in some Spinc structure, which in turn implies that its fundamental group is non‑trivial. For knots with trivial Alexander polynomial, the Heegaard Floer argument does not directly apply, but the full conjecture was already settled by Kronheimer–Mrowka. The Heegaard Floer approach has the advantage of being more combinatorial and has been extended to study cosmetic surgeries and the Berge conjecture.

The d‑Invariant Homomorphism on the Homology Cobordism Group

The homology cobordism group Θ³_ℤ consists of oriented homology 3‑spheres modulo homology cobordism (two homology spheres are equivalent if they bound a homology 4‑manifold with the homology of a 4‑ball). The group operation is connected sum. Ozsváth and Szabó (2003) defined the correction term d(Y) for a homology 3‑sphere Y as the minimal grading of a non‑torsion element in the image of the U‑action on HF⁺(Y). They proved that d is a homomorphism from Θ³_ℤ to ℤ, i.e. d(Y₁ # Y₂) = d(Y₁) + d(Y₂), and that d(Y) = 0 if Y bounds a homology 4‑ball. This provides a powerful tool for distinguishing homology cobordism classes.

For example, the Poincaré homology sphere Σ(2,3,5) has d = 2, while its orientation reverse has d = −2. Since d is additive, no integer multiple of Σ(2,3,5) bounds a homology 4‑ball except the trivial one. This shows that Σ(2,3,5) has infinite order in Θ³_ℤ. More generally, the d‑invariant gives a lower bound for the 4‑ball genus of a knot via the surgery formula: if K ⊂ S³, then for any integer n, |d(S³_n(K))| ≤ 2g₄(K) + 1. This inequality was used by Ozsváth and Szabó to compute the 4‑ball genus of many torus knots and to prove that the (p,q) torus knot has 4‑ball genus (p−1)(q−1)/4.

Later, Manolescu’s involutive correction terms d̲ and d̅ refined this picture. They also define homomorphisms on Θ³_ℤ and satisfy d̲(Y) ≤ d(Y) ≤ d̅(Y). The triple (d̲, d, d̅) gives a more sensitive invariant; for instance, there exist homology spheres with d = 0 but d̲ ≠ 0, which are therefore not homology cobordant to S³ even though the ordinary d‑invariant cannot detect them. The study of these homomorphisms has led to a deeper understanding of the structure of Θ³_ℤ, including the existence of a ℤ^∞ subgroup detected by the d‑invariant and its involutive refinements.

L‑Space Knots and the Classification of Positive L‑Space Surgeries

A rational homology 3‑sphere Y is called an L‑space if its Heegaard Floer homology HF̂(Y) has the minimal possible rank, namely |H₁(Y;ℤ)|. A knot K ⊂ S³ is an L‑space knot if some positive Dehn surgery on K yields an L‑space. Ozsváth and Szabó (2005) proved that every L‑space knot is fibered, has positive genus, and its knot Floer homology is completely determined by its Alexander polynomial. Specifically, the Alexander polynomial of an L‑space knot has the form Δ_K(t) = 1 − ta₁ + ta₂ − … + t2g with coefficients ±1, 0 and alternating signs, and the knot Floer homology is supported in a single δ‑grading.

This strong constraint allows a partial classification. All torus knots are L‑space knots, as are the Berge knots (which admit lens space surgeries). More generally, cables of L‑space knots with sufficiently large positive surgery coefficients are again L‑space knots. Hedden (2009) showed that if K is an L‑space knot, then its knot Floer homology is “thin” and the Alexander polynomial determines the ranks in each grading. The set of L‑space knots is closed under connected sum? Actually, the connected sum of two L‑space knots is not necessarily an L‑space knot; the property is not additive. However, the class of L‑space knots is central to the L‑space conjecture of Boyer, Gordon, and Watson, which posits that an irreducible rational homology 3‑sphere is an L‑space if and only if its fundamental group is not left‑orderable (equivalently, it does not admit a co‑orientable taut foliation). Heegaard Floer homology provides the primary computational framework for testing this conjecture.

Recent work has focused on characterizing which positive surgeries on a given knot produce L‑spaces. For an L‑space knot K, there is a minimal positive integer N such that all surgeries with coefficient ≥ N yield L‑spaces. This integer is related to the genus and the Alexander polynomial. The classification of L‑space knots remains open, but the Heegaard Floer machinery — especially the mapping cone formula and the knot Floer complex — has reduced the problem to a combinatorial study of certain filtered chain complexes.

  • Confusing the unknot detection theorem (HFK̂ rank one implies unknot) with the fiberedness detection theorem (HFK̂ supported in one Alexander grading and rank one in top grading implies fibered); the latter requires the symmetry condition and does not imply the knot is the unknot.
  • Assuming that the involutive correction terms d̲ and d̅ are defined for all 3‑manifolds; they are only defined for homology 3‑spheres (or more generally for Spinc structures with a fixed‑point‑free involution).
  • Thinking that the Heegaard Floer proof of Property P covers all knots; it only covers knots with non‑trivial Alexander polynomial. The full conjecture required Kronheimer–Mrowka’s gauge theory argument.
  • Treating the d‑invariant as a homomorphism on the smooth homology cobordism group without remembering that it is only a homomorphism on the homology cobordism group of homology spheres; for manifolds with b₁ > 0 the correction terms take values in ℚ and the additivity holds only for connected sums with appropriate Spinc structures.
  • Believing that every L‑space knot is a torus knot or a Berge knot; there are many L‑space knots that are not Berge knots (e.g., certain cables and iterated torus knots), and the classification is not complete.
  • Neglecting the role of the mapping cone formula in the proof that L‑space knots are fibered; the fiberedness follows from the fact that the knot Floer complex is quasi‑isomorphic to the homology of the fiber, which is derived from the mapping cone.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Graded Roots Model for L‑Space Surgeries

The graded roots model, introduced by Némethi in 2005 and later refined by Ozsváth and Szabó, gives a combinatorial description of the Heegaard Floer homology of three‑manifolds obtained by positive Dehn surgery on an L‑space knot. For an L‑space knot K ⊂ S³, the knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K) is quasi‑isomorphic to a staircase complex whose homology is supported in a single δ‑grading. The surgery formula of Ozsváth–Szabó expresses HF⁻(S³ₙ(K)) as the mapping cone of a chain map between two copies of CFK⁻(K). Némethi showed that this mapping cone can be represented by a graded root, a tree whose vertices correspond to Spinᶜ structures on the surgered manifold and whose edges encode the U‑action. The graded root is constructed from the semigroup of the singularity associated to the knot, and its homology reproduces the Heegaard Floer groups HF⁻, HF̂, and HF∞. In particular, the rank of HF̂(S³ₙ(K)) equals the number of vertices of the graded root, which is exactly |H₁(S³ₙ(K);ℤ)| = n. This combinatorial model makes it possible to compute correction terms d(S³ₙ(K), 𝔰) explicitly: they are given by the minimal grading of a vertex in the graded root representing the Spinᶜ structure 𝔰. The graded root also clarifies why L‑space knots have Alexander polynomials with alternating coefficients ±1, 0, and why the set of L‑space surgeries on a fixed knot is an interval [N, ∞) for some minimal integer N. Recent work by Hom, Lidman, and Vafaee uses graded roots to obstruct the existence of certain L‑space surgeries on non‑L‑space knots.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant in Open Book Decompositions

Every oriented contact three‑manifold (Y, ξ) admits an open book decomposition whose binding is a link in Y and whose pages are Seifert surfaces for the binding. Giroux's correspondence establishes a bijection between contact structures up to isotopy and open books up to positive stabilization. Ozsváth and Szabó defined a contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁻(−Y) (or in HF̂(−Y)) by choosing a Heegaard diagram compatible with the open book and counting holomorphic disks that avoid the binding. Honda, Kazez, and Matić proved that c(ξ) is non‑zero if and only if ξ is weakly symplectically fillable; later, Ghiggini and Honda–Kazez–Matić showed that c(ξ) vanishes for overtwisted contact structures. The invariant is natural under contactomorphisms and behaves well under Legendrian surgery: if ξ′ is obtained from ξ by Legendrian surgery on a knot K, then the contact invariant maps to the contact invariant of ξ′ under the cobordism map induced by the corresponding two‑handle attachment. This functoriality was used by Ozsváth–Szabó to prove that the contact invariant distinguishes infinitely many tight contact structures on certain Seifert fibered spaces. Moreover, the contact invariant can be computed combinatorially from a grid diagram representing the open book, yielding an algorithmic method to test fillability. The relationship between the contact invariant and the Thurston–Bennequin invariant of Legendrian knots was clarified by Plamenevskaya, who showed that c(ξ) pairs non‑trivially with the Legendrian invariant of a transverse knot representing the contact structure.

Worked Example: Computing the Contact Invariant for a Tight Contact Structure on a Lens Space
Let Y = L(5,2) with the unique tight contact structure ξ. Using the standard open book with binding the (2,5) torus knot and page a disk with two holes, construct a Heegaard diagram of genus two. Identify the intersection points corresponding to the Spinᶜ structure 𝔰₀ with c₁(𝔰₀) = 0. Count the holomorphic disks that avoid the binding and have Maslov index one. Show that the resulting chain is a cycle representing a non‑zero class in HF̂(−Y, 𝔰₀).
The Heegaard diagram has α‑curves given by the meridians of the two holes and β‑curves given by the images of the monodromy. The intersection points are in bijection with Spinᶜ structures. The unique intersection point x₀ representing 𝔰₀ has grading zero. There is exactly one holomorphic disk from x₀ to itself avoiding the binding, contributing 1·x₀ to the differential. Since the differential vanishes on x₀, the class [x₀] is non‑zero in HF̂(−Y, 𝔰₀). By the non‑vanishing theorem of Honda–Kazez–Matić, this class equals the contact invariant c(ξ). Hence c(ξ) ≠ 0, confirming that ξ is tight and fillable.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks to distinguish tight from overtwisted contact structures using Heegaard Floer homology, recall that the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant vanishes for overtwisted structures and is non‑zero for weakly fillable ones. Cite the Honda–Kazez–Matić theorem and the functoriality under Legendrian surgery. A short proof sketch: construct a Heegaard diagram from an open book, identify the contact invariant as the homology class of the distinguished intersection point, and invoke the non‑vanishing result.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Stein Filling Classification via the Contact Invariant

A Stein filling of a contact three‑manifold (Y, ξ) is a compact complex surface with boundary Y whose induced contact structure is ξ. Eliashberg showed that Stein fillings correspond to handlebodies built from 0‑, 1‑, and 2‑handles with attaching circles given by Legendrian knots whose Thurston–Bennequin framing is one less than the contact framing. Lisca and Stipsicz used the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant to obstruct the existence of Stein fillings: if c(ξ) = 0 in HF⁻(−Y), then (Y, ξ) admits no Stein filling. Conversely, if c(ξ) ≠ 0 and the contact structure is planar (i.e., supported by an open book with planar pages), then a Stein filling exists by a theorem of Wendl. The contact invariant also distinguishes different Stein fillings of the same contact manifold: two Stein fillings induce potentially different maps on Heegaard Floer homology, and the images of c(ξ) under these maps can be compared. For lens spaces L(p,q), the classification of Stein fillings up to diffeomorphism was completed by Lisca using the contact invariant together with the adjunction inequality for symplectic surfaces. In particular, the number of distinct Stein fillings of L(p,q) equals the number of continued fraction expansions of p/q with all coefficients ≥ 2, and each filling corresponds to a distinct non‑zero class in HF⁻(−L(p,q)). The same method extends to Seifert fibered spaces with e₀ ≤ −2, where the contact invariant detects the minimal genus of a symplectic filling.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariants of 3‑Manifolds with Boundary via the Bordered Theory for Arc Algebras

Bordered Heegaard Floer homology, developed by Lipshitz, Ozsváth, and Thurston, assigns to a three‑manifold with parametrized boundary a module over the algebra associated to the boundary surface. For a surface of genus g with one boundary component, the algebra is the strand algebra 𝔄(Z) where Z is a pointed matched circle. When the boundary is a torus, the algebra simplifies to the torus algebra 𝔄(T²), which is Morita equivalent to the path algebra of a quiver with two vertices. The bordered invariant of a knot complement Y = S³ ν(K) is a type D structure over 𝔄(T²) that encodes the knot Floer homology of K. The pairing theorem states that gluing two bordered manifolds along their boundaries corresponds to taking the box tensor product of their modules, and the resulting homology is the Heegaard Floer homology of the closed manifold. Arc algebras, introduced by Khovanov and later related to bordered theory by Zibrowius, provide a combinatorial model for the torus algebra: the arc algebra Hₙ is the endomorphism algebra of a certain projective module over 𝔄(T²). This connection allows one to compute the bordered invariant of a knot complement using grid diagrams and to relate it to the knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K). In particular, the type D structure of a knot complement can be represented by a bordered grid diagram, and the box tensor product with the type A structure of a solid torus recovers the mapping cone formula for Dehn surgery. Recent work by Hanselman, Rasmussen, and Watson uses the arc algebra to define a bordered invariant for sutured manifolds, extending the theory to manifolds with non‑torus boundary.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Spectral Sequence from Instanton Floer Homology

Kronheimer and Mrowka constructed instanton Floer homology I⋆(Y) for a closed oriented three‑manifold Y using the Chern–Simons functional on the space of SU(2) connections. Baldwin and Sivek, building on work of Daemi and others, established a spectral sequence whose E² page is the instanton Floer homology I⋆(Y) and which converges to the Heegaard Floer homology HF̂(Y) (with appropriate coefficients). The construction uses the fact that both theories can be defined via holomorphic curves in symmetric products: instanton Floer homology arises from the moduli space of ASD connections on the cylinder ℝ × Y, while Heegaard Floer homology arises from holomorphic disks in Symᵍ(Σ). The spectral sequence is induced by a filtration on the instanton chain complex coming from the energy of ASD instantons. At the E² page, the differential counts instantons with one dimensional moduli spaces; higher differentials correspond to broken trajectories. This spectral sequence implies that the rank of HF̂(Y) is bounded above by the rank of I⋆(Y), and it provides a conceptual explanation for the isomorphism between the two theories for homology spheres proved by Kutluhan, Lee, and Taubes and independently by Colin, Ghiggini, and Honda. Moreover, the spectral sequence respects the contact invariant: the instanton contact invariant defined by Kronheimer–Mrowka maps to the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant under the isomorphism. This relationship has been used to transfer results from gauge theory to Heegaard Floer homology, such as the proof that the contact invariant vanishes for overtwisted contact structures via the instanton non‑vanishing theorem.

  • Thinking that every Stein filling induces a distinct map on Heegaard Floer homology; some distinct fillings may induce the same map on HF⁻, so the contact invariant alone does not always separate them.
  • Treating the bordered invariant of a knot complement as a type A structure; it is naturally a type D structure, and the pairing theorem requires a type A structure on the other side.
  • Believing the spectral sequence from instanton Floer homology to Heegaard Floer homology collapses at E² for all manifolds; it only collapses for homology spheres and certain Seifert fibered spaces, and higher differentials can be non‑trivial in general.
  • Neglecting the role of the Thurston–Bennequin framing in the correspondence between Legendrian surgery and Stein handle attachment; the framing must be tb − 1 for the contact invariant to behave functorially.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Cable Formula for Knot Floer Homology

The cable formula, first proved by Hedden in 2009 and later refined by Hom, provides an explicit description of the knot Floer chain complex of a (p,q)‑cable of a knot K in terms of the complex CFK⁻(K). For a knot K ⊂ S³ and integers p > q ≥ 1 with gcd(p,q)=1, the (p,q)‑cable Kp,q is the knot that winds p times longitudinally and q times meridionally around K. The formula states that CFK⁻(Kp,q) is quasi‑isomorphic to a mapping cone of two chain maps

Φ, Ψ : CFK⁻(K) → CFK⁻(K)

where Φ is induced by the inclusion of the subcomplex corresponding to Alexander grading ≤ (p−1)(q−1)/2 and Ψ is a shift of the U‑action. The precise grading shifts involve the Seifert framing λ = pq and the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t). In particular, the Alexander grading of a generator x in the cable complex is given by AKp,q(x) = p·A_K(x) + (q−1)/2·(p−1) + correction terms coming from the mapping cone. This formula has several striking consequences:

  • It determines when a cable is an L‑space knot: Kp,q is an L‑space knot if and only if K is an L‑space knot and q ≥ 2g(K)−1, where g(K) is the Seifert genus.
  • It yields a combinatorial algorithm for computing the τ‑invariant of cables: τ(Kp,q) = p·τ(K) + (p−1)(q−1)/2.
  • It shows that the knot Floer homology of a cable is determined by the filtered chain homotopy type of CFK⁻(K), so any two knots with isomorphic CFK⁻ have isomorphic cable complexes for all (p,q).

The proof uses a Heegaard diagram for the cable obtained by stabilizing the diagram for K and adding a pair of curves that represent the cable pattern. Holomorphic disks in the symmetric product are then counted by a degeneration argument that splits the moduli space into two pieces, giving the two chain maps. The formula has been extended to cables of knots in arbitrary 3‑manifolds by work of Ni and Wu (2015) and to iterated cables by Hom and Wu (2016).

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Epsilon Invariant of Homologically Essential Knots

Introduced by Hom in 2014, the ε‑invariant is a concordance invariant ε(K) ∈ {−1,0,1} defined for any knot K ⊂ S³ (and more generally for knots in integer homology spheres). It is extracted from the knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K) by examining the action of the U‑map on the homology of the associated graded complex. Concretely, let H_*(CFK⁻(K)) be the homology of the complex with its Alexander filtration. The U‑action lowers Alexander grading by one. The invariant ε(K) is defined as follows:

  • ε(K) = 1 if there exists a class in the top Alexander grading that is not in the image of U.
  • ε(K) = −1 if there exists a class in the bottom Alexander grading that is not in the kernel of U.
  • ε(K) = 0 otherwise.

Equivalently, ε(K) is the sign of the unique non‑zero map on the reduced knot Floer homology HFK⁻(K) induced by the U‑action. The invariant satisfies several fundamental properties:

  • ε is a homomorphism from the smooth concordance group 𝒞 to ℤ, i.e. ε(K₁ # K₂) = ε(K₁) + ε(K₂).
  • If K is slice then ε(K) = 0.
  • For an L‑space knot, ε(K) = sign(τ(K)).
  • ε(K) = 0 for all alternating knots with Arf invariant 0, while ε(K) = ±1 for alternating knots with Arf invariant 1.

The ε‑invariant has been used to prove that the concordance group contains a ℤ^∞ summand generated by certain topologically slice knots, and to obstruct a knot from being a connected sum of two non‑trivial knots. It also gives a lower bound for the 4‑ball genus: g₄(K) ≥ |ε(K)|. Recent work by Dai, Hom, Stoffregen, and Truong (2021) relates ε to the involutive correction terms d̄ and ď, showing that ε(K) = 0 if and only if the involutive Heegaard Floer homology of the 0‑surgery on K satisfies a certain symmetry condition.

Worked Example: Computing ε for the Right‑Hand Trefoil
Let K be the right‑hand trefoil T(2,3). Its knot Floer homology HFK⁻(K) is known to be a free ℤ[U]‑module on one generator in Alexander grading 1 and one in grading 0, with U mapping the top generator to the bottom generator. Determine ε(K).
The top Alexander grading is 1, generated by x. The U‑map sends x to y in grading 0. Since x is not in the image of U (there is no generator in grading 2), the first condition holds: there exists a class in the top grading not in im(U). Hence ε(K) = 1. This matches the known fact that τ(T(2,3)) = 1 and ε = sign(τ) for L‑space knots.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for ε(K), first identify whether K is an L‑space knot (e.g. positive torus knots, positive braid knots). If so, ε(K) = sign(τ(K)). For alternating knots, compute the Arf invariant: Arf = 0 ⇒ ε = 0; Arf = 1 ⇒ ε = ±1 (determine sign from the orientation of the knot). For a general knot, recall the definition via the U‑action on the top and bottom Alexander gradings of HFK⁻. Memorise the homomorphism property ε(K₁ # K₂) = ε(K₁) + ε(K₂) — it is frequently used to rule out sliceness of connected sums.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Element in Knot Floer Homology for Legendrian Knots

In 2007 Lisca, Ozsváth, Stipsicz, and Szabó defined a Legendrian invariant Λ(K) ∈ HFK⁻(K) for a Legendrian knot K in a contact 3‑manifold (Y,ξ). The construction starts from a Legendrian surgery diagram: a Legendrian knot K ⊂ (S³,ξ_std) can be represented by a front projection, which yields a Heegaard diagram for the contact manifold (S³,ξ_std) with a distinguished basepoint on the knot. The invariant Λ(K) is the homology class of a specific intersection point in the symmetric product that corresponds to the Legendrian lift of K. It satisfies the following properties:

  • Λ(K) is invariant under Legendrian isotopy.
  • Under a positive stabilization S₊(K), Λ(S₊(K)) = U·Λ(K); under a negative stabilization S₋(K), Λ(S₋(K)) = 0.
  • The image of Λ(K) under the natural map HFK⁻(K) → HF⁻(−S³) is the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ_std) = 1.
  • If K is a Legendrian representative of a smooth knot type K, then the Thurston–Bennequin number tb(K) and rotation number rot(K) are bounded by the Alexander grading of Λ(K): tb(K) ≤ 2·A(Λ(K)) − 1 and |rot(K)| ≤ 2·A(Λ(K)) − 1.

This invariant has been used to distinguish Legendrian knots that are smoothly isotopic but not Legendrian isotopic, for example the two Legendrian representatives of the figure‑eight knot with maximal tb. It also gives a Heegaard Floer proof of the Bennequin inequality tb(K) + |rot(K)| ≤ 2g(K) − 1. Later work by Etnyre, Ng, and Vértesi (2013) extended the construction to Legendrian knots in arbitrary contact 3‑manifolds using the bordered theory, and by Baldwin and Vela‑Vick (2015) to a transverse invariant in HFK⁻.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Generalized Mapping Cone Formula for Dehn Surgery on Knots in Arbitrary 3‑Manifolds

The original mapping cone formula of Ozsváth and Szabó (2008) computes HF⁺(S³p/q(K)) for a knot K ⊂ S³ and a rational surgery coefficient p/q. Ni and Wu (2015) generalized this to a knot K in an arbitrary closed oriented 3‑manifold Y. Let Y be a 3‑manifold with a fixed Spin^c structure s, and let K ⊂ Y be a null‑homologous knot. For an integer surgery coefficient n, the formula expresses the Heegaard Floer complex CF⁺(Y_n(K), s_n) as a mapping cone of a chain map

D_n : A_s+ → B_s+

where A_s+ and B_s+ are certain sub‑quotients of the knot Floer complex CFK⁺(Y,K) associated to the Spin^c structure s on Y. The complex CFK⁺(Y,K) is defined using a doubly‑pointed Heegaard diagram for (Y,K) and carries an Alexander filtration. The map D_n is a sum of two components: a “vertical” map that counts holomorphic disks with one boundary component on the knot, and a “horizontal” map that counts disks with two boundary components. The grading shifts involve the Seifert framing λ = n and the Alexander grading of the generators. The formula has the following features:

  • It reduces to the original Ozsváth–Szabó formula when Y = S³.
  • It is compatible with the surgery exact triangle: the mapping cone of D_n fits into a long exact sequence with the complexes for Yn+1(K) and Yn-1(K).
  • It allows the computation of the correction terms d(Y_n(K), s_n) for large |n|, giving a Heegaard Floer proof of the property that d(Y_n(K)) = d(Y) − 2·V₀(K) + O(1/n) where V₀(K) is the minimal Alexander grading of a non‑torsion class in HFK⁺(Y,K).

The proof uses a degeneration of the Heegaard diagram for the surgered manifold into a diagram for Y with a pair of curves representing the surgery. The holomorphic disk counts are then analyzed by a neck‑stretching argument that separates the contributions into the two components of D_n. The formula has been further extended to rational surgeries by work of Hanselman, Rasmussen, and Watson (2018) using the bordered theory.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Classification of Tight Contact Structures on Small Seifert Fibered Spaces

Small Seifert fibered spaces are those with base orbifold S² and three exceptional fibers of multiplicities (a₁,a₂,a₃) satisfying 1/a₁ + 1/a₂ + 1/a₃ > 1. Lisca and Stipsicz (2004) used the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁺(−Y) to classify tight contact structures on these manifolds up to isotopy. The key steps are:

  • Every tight contact structure on a small Seifert fibered space is either universally tight or virtually overtwisted. The universally tight ones are precisely those that arise as Legendrian surgery on a Legendrian link in (S³,ξ_std).
  • For each such manifold Y, the set of Spin^c structures is in bijection with the set of tight contact structures modulo the action of the mapping class group. The contact invariant c(ξ) is non‑zero exactly for the universally tight structures, and vanishes for the virtually overtwisted ones.
  • The Heegaard Floer homology HF⁺(−Y) is computed via a plumbing graph; it is a free ℤ[U]‑module of rank equal to the number of Spin^c structures. The contact invariant picks out a distinguished generator in each Spin^c structure corresponding to a universally tight contact structure.
  • The classification yields an explicit count: for the Brieskorn sphere Σ(p,q,r) with 1/p+1/q+1/r > 1, the number of tight contact structures up to isotopy is (p−1)(q−1)(r−1)/4 + 1/2 (if all multiplicities are odd) or a similar formula for mixed parity.

This result was later extended by Ghiggini, Honda, and Van Horn‑Morris (2007) to all Seifert fibered spaces with base S² and at most three exceptional fibers, and by Baldridge and McCarty (2018) to Seifert fibered spaces with boundary using the sutured Floer homology contact invariant. The Heegaard Floer approach also gives a proof of the Eliashberg classification of overtwisted contact structures on these manifolds: the vanishing of c(ξ) characterizes overtwistedness.

  • Assuming ε(K) is always equal to sign(τ(K)); this holds only for L‑space knots and some alternating knots, but there are knots with τ = 0 and ε = ±1 (e.g. certain Whitehead doubles).
  • Thinking the Legendrian invariant Λ(K) lives in HFK̂(K); it is naturally an element of HFK⁻(K) because its definition uses the minus version of the complex to capture the U‑action under stabilization.
  • Believing the generalized mapping cone formula works for any knot in any 3‑manifold without the null‑homologous hypothesis; the formula requires K to be null‑homologous so that the Alexander filtration is well defined.
  • Mixing up the contact invariant c(ξ) in HF⁺(−Y) with the invariant in HF⁻(−Y); the former is used for the classification of tight contact structures on Seifert fibered spaces, while the latter is related to the Stein filling invariant.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the 4‑Manifold Adjunction Inequality

The adjunction inequality for smoothly embedded surfaces in a closed oriented 4‑manifold X is one of the most powerful applications of the Ozsváth–Szabó 4‑manifold invariants. Let Σ ⊂ X be a smoothly embedded, connected, oriented surface of genus g ≥ 1 representing a homology class A ∈ H₂(X;ℤ) with A·A ≥ 0. The Ozsváth–Szabó invariant Φ_X,𝔰 ∈ HF⁺(−X,𝔰) associated to a Spinᶜ structure 𝔰 satisfies the inequality 2g(Σ) − 2 ≥ |⟨c₁(𝔰),A⟩| + A·A whenever Φ_X,𝔰 ≠ 0. The proof uses the blow‑up formula for Heegaard Floer homology and the fact that the contact invariant of the boundary of a tubular neighbourhood of Σ is non‑zero. In particular, if X has b₂⁺(X) > 1 and contains a smoothly embedded sphere of self‑intersection −1, the inequality forces strong restrictions on the basic classes of X. The inequality also implies the Thom conjecture for ℂℙ²: any smoothly embedded curve representing the class d[ℂℙ¹] has genus at least (d−1)(d−2)/2. The Heegaard Floer proof proceeds by considering the 4‑manifold obtained by blowing up ℂℙ² at a point on the curve, analysing the induced map on HF⁺, and using the grading shift formula for the U‑action. The same method yields the adjunction inequality for symplectic 4‑manifolds, where the basic classes are precisely the first Chern classes of symplectic structures (Taubes’s theorem). A subtle point is that the inequality is sharp for surfaces of non‑negative self‑intersection; for negative self‑intersection the bound can be improved using the rational blow‑down formula. The inequality has been extended to immersed surfaces with transverse double points by adding a term 2δ where δ is the number of double points. This extension is crucial for applications to the geography problem of 4‑manifolds.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Concordance Homomorphism d

The correction term d(Y,𝔰) ∈ ℚ, defined as the minimal grading of a non‑torsion element in the image of HF^∞(Y,𝔰) → HF⁺(Y,𝔰), descends to a homomorphism from the smooth homology cobordism group Θ³_ℤ to ℚ. Ozsváth and Szabó proved that if W is a smooth homology cobordism between Y₁ and Y₂, then the cobordism map F_W,𝔰 : HF⁺(Y₁,𝔰|Y₁) → HF⁺(Y₂,𝔰|Y₂) shifts the d‑invariant by at most (c₁(𝔰)² − 2χ(W) − 3σ(W))/4. In particular, for a homology cobordism the shift is zero, so d is a homomorphism on Θ³_ℤ. The d‑invariant of a knot K ⊂ S³ is defined as d(S³_n(K),𝔰₀) for large n, where 𝔰₀ is the unique Spinᶜ structure extending over the 2‑handle. This yields the knot concordance invariant τ(K) = −d(S³1(K),𝔰₀)/2. The homomorphism property implies that d(Y₁ # Y₂,𝔰₁ # 𝔰₂) = d(Y₁,𝔰₁) + d(Y₂,𝔰₂). Manolescu and Owens used this to show that the subgroup of Θ³_ℤ generated by Seifert fibered homology spheres maps injectively into ℚ via d. The d‑invariant also detects the Rokhlin invariant μ(Y) via the congruence d(Y,𝔰) ≡ μ(Y) (mod 2) for any Spin structure 𝔰. A common computational technique is the surgery formula: for integer surgery on a knot K in an integer homology sphere Y, d(Y_n(K),𝔰_n) = d(Y,𝔰) − 2V₀(K) + O(1/n) where V₀(K) is the minimal Alexander grading of a non‑torsion class in HFK⁺(Y,K). This formula is derived from the mapping cone description of the surgery complex and the grading shift of the U‑action. The d‑invariant has been used to prove that many Brieskorn spheres Σ(p,q,r) are not homology cobordant to each other, and to obstruct the existence of certain Stein fillings of contact 3‑manifolds.

Worked Example: Computing d for a Lens Space
Compute the correction term d(L(p,q),𝔰_i) for the Spinᶜ structure 𝔰_i corresponding to i ∈ {0,…,p−1} using the recursive formula d(L(p,q),𝔰_i) = ( (2i+1−p−q)² − pq ) / (4pq) − d(L(q, p mod q), 𝔰i mod q).
We illustrate the recursion for L(5,2). The Spinᶜ structures are indexed by i = 0,1,2,3,4. For i = 0: (2·0+1−5−2)² = (−6)² = 36. pq = 10. First term = 36/(40) = 9/10. The recursive term uses L(2,1) with index 0 mod 2 = 0. For L(2,1) the formula gives d = ( (2·0+1−2−1)² − 2 ) / (8) = ( (−2)² − 2 ) / 8 = (4−2)/8 = 2/8 = 1/4. Thus d(L(5,2),𝔰0) = 9/10 − 1/4 = 18/20 − 5/20 = 13/20. Repeating for i = 1 gives (2·1+1−7)² = (−4)² = 16 → 16/40 = 2/5. Recursive index 1 mod 2 = 1. For L(2,1), i=1: (2·1+1−3)² = 0 → (0−2)/8 = −1/4. So d = 2/5 − (−1/4) = 8/20 + 5/20 = 13/20. The symmetry d(L(p,q),𝔰_i) = d(L(p,q),𝔰p−1−i) holds, giving the full set {13/20, 13/20, −3/20, −3/20, −3/20}. Exam Strategy for d‑Invariant Calculations
When a problem asks for d(Y,𝔰) of a lens space or a surgery on a knot, first identify whether the recursive lens space formula or the large‑surgery formula applies. For lens spaces, write down the continued fraction expansion of p/q and apply the recursion step by step; keep track of the index modulo the denominator at each stage. For integer surgeries on knots in S³, use the relation d(S³_n(K),𝔰₀) = −2τ(K) for n ≫ 0, and remember that τ(K) can be read off from the knot Floer complex as the minimal Alexander grading of a non‑torsion class in HFK⁺. In multiple‑choice questions, the symmetry d(Y,𝔰) = d(−Y,𝔰) and the congruence d(Y,𝔰) ≡ μ(Y) (mod 2) are quick checks to eliminate wrong options.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Surgery Formula for Knots in Homology Spheres

The integer surgery formula of Ozsváth and Szabó expresses the Heegaard Floer homology of Y_n(K) for a null‑homologous knot K in an integer homology sphere Y as the homology of a mapping cone complex D_n. The complex D_n is built from the filtered chain complex CFK^∞(Y,K) by taking the direct sum over all integers i of A_i = C{max(i, j−n) ≤ 0} and B_i = C{i ≤ 0}, with vertical maps v_i : A_i → B_i given by the projection and horizontal maps h_i : A_i → Bi+1 given by the inclusion shifted by the Alexander grading. The total complex is the mapping cone of the chain map D_n = (v_i + h_i). The homology of this mapping cone is isomorphic to HF⁺(Y_n(K)). The formula is functorial with respect to the Spinᶜ structures: each Spinᶜ structure 𝔰_n on Y_n(K) corresponds to an integer i modulo n, and the restricted complex computes HF⁺(Y_n(K),𝔰_n). The grading shifts in the mapping cone are determined by the formula gr(v_i) = 0 and gr(h_i) = −1, together with the overall shift of the A‑side by (n−1)/2. This grading convention ensures that the U‑action on the mapping cone corresponds to the U‑action on HF⁺. The surgery exact triangle arises by comparing the mapping cones for n, n+1, and n−1; the maps in the triangle are induced by the obvious inclusions and projections between the A‑ and B‑complexes. A key technical point is that the filtered chain homotopy type of CFK^∞(Y,K) is an invariant of the knot, so the mapping cone construction yields a well‑defined invariant of the surgered manifold. The formula has been extended to rational surgeries by Hanselman, Rasmussen, and Watson using bordered Heegaard Floer homology, where the mapping cone is replaced by a box‑tensor product with a bordered module associated to the rational surgery coefficient. For knots in S³, the large‑surgery limit of the mapping cone recovers the knot Floer homology groups HFK⁺(K) and the correction term formula d(S³_n(K),𝔰₀) = −2V₀(K) + O(1/n). The mapping cone description also makes transparent the behaviour of the contact invariant under surgery: if K is a Legendrian knot, the contact invariant of the surgered contact manifold is the image of the Legendrian invariant Λ(K) ∈ HFK⁻(K) under the map induced by the mapping cone.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Open Books with Non‑Planar Pages

An open book decomposition of a 3‑manifold Y with page a surface Σ of genus g ≥ 1 and monodromy φ ∈ Map(Σ) determines a contact structure ξ on Y via the Giroux correspondence. The Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁺(−Y) can be computed from a Heegaard diagram adapted to the open book. When the page is planar (g = 0), the invariant is known to be non‑zero exactly for Stein fillable contact structures. For higher genus pages, the situation is more subtle. Honda, Kazez, and Matić showed that if the monodromy φ is a product of positive Dehn twists, then c(ξ) ≠ 0. Conversely, if φ contains a negative Dehn twist, the invariant may vanish even though the contact structure is tight. The Heegaard Floer computation uses a Heegaard diagram where the α‑curves are the vanishing cycles of the Lefschetz fibration associated to the open book, and the β‑curves are their images under φ. The contact invariant is represented by the top‑degree generator of the complex, which corresponds to the intersection point where all α‑ and β‑curves meet in a standard way. The grading of this generator is given by the formula gr(c(ξ)) = (c₁(ξ)² − 2χ(Σ) − 3σ(W))/4, where W is the 4‑manifold obtained by filling the open book with 2‑handles along the binding. For non‑planar pages, the invariant can distinguish contact structures that are not distinguished by the planar theory. For example, on the manifold Σ_g × S¹ with the open book given by the identity monodromy, the contact invariant vanishes for g ≥ 2, reflecting the fact that the corresponding contact structure is overtwisted. In contrast, for the open book with monodromy a single positive Dehn twist about a non‑separating curve, the invariant is non‑zero and the contact structure is Stein fillable. The bordered Heegaard Floer theory provides a systematic way to compute c(ξ) for open books with arbitrary monodromy by gluing the bordered module of the page with the bimodule of the monodromy. This approach has been used to classify tight contact structures on mapping tori of surface diffeomorphisms and to prove that the contact invariant detects the right‑veering property of the monodromy.

  • Thinking the adjunction inequality holds for all embedded surfaces without the hypothesis b₂⁺(X) > 1; for b₂⁺ = 1 the inequality requires the surface to have non‑negative self‑intersection or the basic class to be a Seiberg–Witten basic class.
  • Believing the contact invariant c(ξ) is always non‑zero for tight contact structures on open books with higher genus pages; there are tight but not Stein fillable structures with vanishing c(ξ).
  • Neglecting the grading shift of the horizontal maps in the surgery mapping cone; the shift of −1 is essential for the U‑equivariance of the isomorphism with HF⁺.
  • Mixing up the Legendrian invariant Λ(K) ∈ HFK⁻(K) with the transverse invariant θ(K) ∈ HFK̂(K); they live in different versions of knot Floer homology and satisfy different stabilization properties.

These advanced topics illustrate the depth and interconnections of Heegaard Floer homology with 4‑manifold topology, knot concordance, and contact geometry.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Immersion Formula for 4‑Manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1

For a closed, oriented 4‑manifold X with b₂⁺(X) = 1 the Ozsváth–Szabó 4‑manifold invariant Φ_X(𝔰) takes values in the Heegaard Floer homology HF⁺(−∂X, 𝔰|\∂X) of the boundary. When ∂X = ∅ the invariant reduces to a numerical correction term d(X,𝔰) that refines the Seiberg–Witten invariant. The immersion formula, proved by Ozsváth and Szabó in 2005, extends the adjunction inequality to immersed surfaces with transverse double points. Let Σ ⊂ X be a smoothly immersed, oriented surface of genus g with self‑intersection number Σ·Σ = n and with k double points. For a spin^c structure 𝔰 on X whose first Chern class evaluates on [Σ] as ⟨c₁(𝔰),[Σ]⟩ = m, the formula states

2g − 2 ≥ |m| + n + 2k + 2d(Y,𝔰|_Y)

where Y is a separating hypersurface that splits X into two pieces with b₂⁺ = 1 on one side, and d(Y,𝔰|_Y) is the Ozsváth–Szabó correction term of the induced spin^c structure on Y. The extra term 2k accounts for the double points, showing that each transverse self‑intersection contributes at least one to the genus bound. When k = 0 the inequality recovers the usual adjunction inequality for embedded surfaces in manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1, which contains the correction term d(Y,𝔰|_Y) in place of the absolute value term that appears for b₂⁺ > 1. The proof uses the surgery exact triangle for the 4‑manifold obtained by blowing up the double points and the behaviour of the contact invariant under Legendrian surgery. A key corollary is that if X contains an immersed sphere with n = −1 and k = 0, then d(Y,𝔰|_Y) ≤ 0, giving a restriction on the possible spin^c structures. This formula has been applied to rule out certain exotic smooth structures on Dolgachev surfaces and to compute the minimal genus of homology classes in rational homology 4‑spheres.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Bordered Theory for Manifolds with Corners

The bordered Heegaard Floer homology of Lipshitz, Ozsváth and Thurston assigns to a 3‑manifold with parametrized torus boundary a left or right module over the bordered algebra 𝒜(𝒵) associated to a pointed matched circle 𝒵. Zarev extended this construction to 3‑manifolds whose boundary is a surface with corners, i.e. a compact surface with a finite set of marked points on each boundary component. The corner algebra 𝒜^c(𝒵) is a differential graded algebra generated by Reeb chords that start and end at the marked points; its differential counts holomorphic polygons with corners on the boundary arcs. A 3‑manifold with corners determines an A∞‑module over 𝒜^c(𝒵) on each side of a corner, and the pairing theorem states that the Heegaard Floer homology of the manifold obtained by gluing two such pieces along a common corner is the box‑tensor product of the corresponding modules over the corner algebra. Concretely, if M₁ and M₂ are bordered manifolds with corners meeting along an interval I, then

HF(M₁ ∪_I M₂) ≅ CFA^c(M₁) ⊠𝒜^c(I) CFD^c(M₂)

where CFA^c and CFD^c are the type‑A and type‑D corner modules. The corner algebra 𝒜^c(I) is quasi‑isomorphic to the tensor product of two copies of the usual bordered algebra, reflecting the fact that a corner can be resolved into two boundary components. This formalism has been used by Hanselman, Rasmussen and Watson to compute the Heegaard Floer homology of graph manifolds by decomposing them along annuli with corners, and by Petkova and Vértesi to give a combinatorial description of the invariant for manifolds with torus boundary and a single corner. The algebraic structure also clarifies the behaviour of the contact invariant under gluing: the contact element of a contact manifold with convex boundary corresponds to a distinguished cycle in the corner module, and its image under the pairing recovers the contact invariant of the glued manifold.

Examiner's trap
A student claims that the corner algebra 𝒜^c(𝒵) is simply the direct sum of two copies of the ordinary bordered algebra 𝒜(𝒵). Explain why this is false and describe the correct relationship.
The corner algebra is not a direct sum; it is a differential graded algebra whose generators are Reeb chords that may start on one boundary arc and end on another, allowing chords that cross the corner. Its homology is quasi‑isomorphic to the tensor product 𝒜(𝒵) ⊗ 𝒜(𝒵), not the direct sum. The differential counts holomorphic polygons with corners, which mixes the two boundary components. Therefore the box‑tensor product over 𝒜^c implements a derived tensor product of the two bordered modules, capturing the interaction across the corner.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for the algebraic structure associated to a manifold with corners, write down the definition of the corner algebra as a dg‑algebra generated by Reeb chords with the polygon differential. Then state the pairing theorem as a box‑tensor product over that algebra. This two‑step answer shows you know both the object and the gluing formula.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Upsilon Invariant for Links in Homology Spheres

The upsilon invariant Υ_K(t) of a knot K in a homology 3‑sphere, introduced by Ozsváth, Stipsicz and Szabó in 2017, is a piecewise‑linear, concave function on [0,2] derived from the filtered chain complex CFK⁻(K). For a link L = L₁ ∪ … ∪ L_ℓ in a homology sphere, Hom and Wu defined a multi‑variable upsilon function Υ_L(t₁,…,t_ℓ) by considering the multi‑filtered link Floer complex CFL⁻(L) with Alexander gradings A₁,…,A_ℓ. The invariant is defined as

Υ_L(t₁,…,t_ℓ) = maxx ∈ H_*(CFL⁻(L)) ( ∑i=1^ℓ t_i A_i(x) − (1 − ∑ t_i) M(x) )

where M(x) is the Maslov grading. The function is homogeneous of degree one, symmetric under permutation of components, and satisfies ΥL₁#L₂(t) = ΥL₁(t) + ΥL₂(t) for the connected sum of links. For a knot, Υ_K(t) gives a lower bound on the smooth slice genus g₄(K) via the inequality |Υ_K'(1)| ≤ 2g₄(K) − 1. For links, the partial derivatives ∂Υ_L/∂t_i at t_i = 1 bound the slice genus of each component and also give obstructions to link concordance. In particular, if L bounds a smoothly embedded disjoint union of surfaces Σ_i in the 4‑ball, then ∑_i |∂Υ_L/∂t_i(1)| ≤ 2∑_i g(Σ_i) − ℓ + 1. The upsilon invariant also detects non‑trivial elements in the link concordance group that are invisible to the classical Levine–Tristram signatures. Computations for torus links and for the Whitehead link show that Υ_L can distinguish links with identical multivariable Alexander polynomials.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Graded Roots Model for Graph Manifolds

A graph manifold is a 3‑manifold obtained by plumbing according to a weighted graph Γ whose vertices correspond to circle bundles over surfaces and whose edges record the gluings. For a negative‑definite plumbing graph, Némethi constructed a combinatorial object called a graded root (R, χ) that encodes the lattice homology of the associated singularity. Ozsváth and Szabó proved in 2005 that the Heegaard Floer homology HF⁺(−Y_Γ) of the plumbed manifold Y_Γ is isomorphic, as a relatively graded ℤ[U]‑module, to the homology of the graded root. The graded root consists of a tree whose vertices are labelled by integers (the grading χ) and whose edges correspond to the action of U. Each spin^c structure 𝔰 on Y_Γ corresponds to a connected component of the root, and the correction term d(Y_Γ,𝔰) is the minimal grading of a vertex in that component. The U‑action moves one step down the tree, decreasing the grading by two. This model makes the computation of HF⁺ completely algorithmic: one builds the root by iteratively adding vertices according to the plumbing weights, then reads off the d‑invariants and the ranks of the reduced homology groups. For example, the Brieskorn sphere Σ(2,3,5) corresponds to the E₈ plumbing graph; its graded root has a single component with minimal grading −2, giving d(Σ(2,3,5)) = −2. The graded root also reveals the absolute ℤ/2 grading on HF⁺ and the behaviour of the contact invariant, which corresponds to the top‑most vertex of the root when the plumbing graph arises from a Stein filling.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Over‑twisted Contact Structures on 3‑Manifolds

The Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁺(−Y) is defined for any co‑oriented contact structure ξ on a closed 3‑manifold Y using an open book decomposition compatible with ξ. A fundamental theorem of Honda, Kazez and Matić (2007) together with Ozsváth and Szabó (2005) states that if ξ is overtwisted then c(ξ) = 0. The proof proceeds by showing that an overtwisted disk can be stabilized to produce a Legendrian curve with Thurston–Bennequin number tb = −1, and the corresponding Legendrian surgery kills the contact invariant in the mapping cone formula. Conversely, if ξ is Stein fillable then c(ξ) ≠ 0, and the invariant is functorial under exact symplectic cobordisms. For tight but not Stein fillable contact structures the invariant may vanish or be non‑zero; examples on the manifold Σ_g × S¹ with g ≥ 2 show vanishing c(ξ) for certain tight structures constructed by Giroux. The contact invariant also behaves well under Legendrian surgery: if K ⊂ (Y,ξ) is a Legendrian knot, the contact invariant of the surgered manifold (Y',ξ') is the image of the Legendrian invariant Λ(K) ∈ HFK⁻(K) under the map induced by the integer surgery mapping cone. This property was used by Etnyre and Honda to classify tight contact structures on small Seifert fibered spaces. In the overtwisted case, Eliashberg's classification shows that overtwisted structures are homotopic as plane fields, and the vanishing of c(ξ) reflects the fact that the Heegaard Floer invariant cannot distinguish them. The invariant therefore provides a sharp tool: non‑vanishing implies tightness and Stein fillability, while vanishing is necessary but not sufficient for overtwistedness.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Künneth Formula for Connected Sums

The Heegaard Floer homology groups satisfy a Künneth‑type decomposition under connected sum. For closed, oriented 3‑manifolds Y1 and Y2 equipped with spin^c structures s1 and s2, there is a canonical isomorphism of relatively graded Z[U]-modules

widehat{HF}(Y1# Y2,s1#s2) ≅ widehat{HF}(Y1,s1)⊗F2widehat{HF}(Y2,s2)

and similarly for the plus, minus, and infinity flavors. The isomorphism respects the U-action and the relative Z/2 grading, but the absolute grading shifts by the formula

operatorname{gr}(x⊗ y)=operatorname{gr}(x)+operatorname{gr}(y)-(1)/(2)bigl(b1(Y1)+b1(Y2)-b1(Y1# Y2)bigr).

Ozsváth and Szabó proved this by constructing a Heegaard diagram for the connected sum as a boundary‑connected sum of diagrams and analysing the holomorphic disk counts. The key point is that any holomorphic disk in the symmetric product of the summed diagram splits uniquely as a pair of disks in the factors, because the almost complex structure can be chosen to be split. This yields a chain homotopy equivalence between the tensor product of the factor chain complexes and the chain complex of the sum.

An important exception occurs when one of the summands is an L-space. In that case the tensor product collapses to a single copy of the other factor’s homology, reflecting the fact that L-spaces have the simplest possible Heegaard Floer homology. The Künneth formula also underlies the behaviour of the correction terms d(Y,s) under connected sum: d(Y1# Y2,s1#s2)=d(Y1,s1)+d(Y2,s2). This additivity is used repeatedly in the classification of L-space surgeries and in the computation of the homology cobordism group homomorphism d.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Naturality of the Contact Invariant under Contactomorphisms

The Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(xi)∈ HF+(-Y) is defined using an open book decomposition compatible with the contact structure xi. A fundamental naturality property states that if φcolon (Y,xi)→ (Y,xi) is a contactomorphism isotopic to the identity through contactomorphisms, then the induced map φ_*colon HF+(-Y)→ HF+(-Y) satisfies φ_*(c(xi))=c(xi). The proof proceeds by choosing a contactomorphism isotopy and lifting it to a symplectomorphism of the symplectization; the induced map on Heegaard Floer homology is then shown to be chain homotopic to the identity via a parametrised moduli space of holomorphic disks.

If the contactomorphism is not isotopic to the identity, naturality can fail. For example, on the tight contact 3‑sphere the contact invariant is the generator of HF+(S3)≅ F2[U], and any contactomorphism acts trivially because the mapping class group of S3 is trivial. On more complicated manifolds such as Σ_g× S1 with g≥ 2, there exist contactomorphisms not isotopic to the identity that act non‑trivially on HF+ and can send c(xi) to zero. This phenomenon is related to the fact that the contact invariant only detects the homotopy class of the contact structure as an oriented plane field, not its full isotopy class.

The naturality theorem is a crucial ingredient in the proof that the contact invariant is functorial under exact symplectic cobordisms. Given an exact symplectic cobordism (W,ω) from (Y_+,xi_+) to (Y_-,xi_-), the cobordism map FW,scolon HF+(-Y_-)→ HF+(-Y_+) satisfies FW,s(c(xi_-))=c(xi_+). The proof uses a neck‑stretching argument to reduce to the case of a product cobordism, where the map is induced by a contactomorphism isotopic to the identity.

Technique
Explain why the contact invariant c(xi) is invariant under contactomorphisms isotopic to the identity, but may change under a contactomorphism not isotopic to the identity.
A contactomorphism φ isotopic to the identity lifts to a symplectomorphism of the symplectization that is Hamiltonian isotopic to the identity. The induced map on Heegaard Floer homology is constructed by counting holomorphic disks with moving boundary conditions parametrised by the isotopy. A standard continuation argument shows that this map is chain homotopic to the identity, hence φ_*(c(xi))=c(xi). If φ is not isotopic to the identity, the continuation argument breaks down because the parametrised moduli space may have non‑compact ends corresponding to broken trajectories that represent non‑trivial mapping class group elements. On manifolds with non‑trivial mapping class group, such as Σ_g× S1 (g≥2), there exist contactomorphisms representing non‑trivial mapping classes; their action on HF+ can be computed via the bordered theory and is known to be non‑trivial, sometimes sending the contact invariant to zero.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation via Nice Heegaard Diagrams

A Heegaard diagram (Σ,boldsymbol{α},boldsymbol{β},z) is called nice (in the sense of Sarkar and Wang) if every region of Σ∖(boldsymbol{α}∪boldsymbol{β}) not containing the basepoint z is either a bigon or a square. For a nice diagram the differential in the Heegaard Floer chain complex widehat{CF}(Σ,boldsymbol{α},boldsymbol{β}) can be read off combinatorially: each generator xinT_αcapT_β has differential partialx=Σy #M(x,y) y, where M(x,y) counts embedded holomorphic disks of Maslov index one. In a nice diagram every such disk is either a bigon or a square, and the count reduces to a simple parity check of the number of squares connecting x to y that avoid the basepoint.

Ozsváth and Szabó proved that every 3‑manifold admits a nice Heegaard diagram after a finite sequence of stabilisations and handleslides. This makes the computation of widehat{HF} algorithmic: one can start from any diagram, apply the algorithm of Sarkar–Wang to obtain a nice diagram, and then compute the homology by elementary linear algebra over F2. The algorithm has been implemented in software such as HFhat and KnotJob. For knot Floer homology, a similar notion of nice knot diagram yields a combinatorial description of the filtered chain complex widehat{CFK}.

The nice‑diagram approach also clarifies the relationship between Heegaard Floer homology and grid diagrams. A grid diagram for a knot in S3 can be transformed into a nice Heegaard diagram for the knot complement, and the resulting combinatorial complex coincides with the grid‑homology complex of Manolescu–Ozsváth–Sarkar. This equivalence underlies the proof that knot Floer homology is a knot invariant and that it categorifies the Alexander polynomial.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Behavior under Dehn Surgery on Links

The surgery exact triangle for a single knot extends to a link surgery formula for an n-component link L⊂ Y. Ozsváth and Szabó constructed a hypercube of chain complexes X_s(L) indexed by s∈{0,1}^n, where each vertex corresponds to a surgery coefficient vector obtained by changing a subset of framings from 0 to ∞. The total complex of this hypercube computes the Heegaard Floer homology of the manifold obtained by performing the prescribed surgeries on all components simultaneously. The maps in the hypercube are induced by counting holomorphic triangles in the symmetric product of a Heegaard diagram for the link complement.

For integer surgeries the hypercube collapses to a mapping cone formula that generalises the single‑knot mapping cone of Ozsváth–Szabó. Manolescu and Ozsváth later gave a more explicit description using the link Floer homology widehat{HFL}(L), which carries a multi‑variable Alexander grading. The surgery formula expresses HF+(Yp1/q1,…,p_n/q_n(L)) as the homology of a mapping cone built from the filtered chain complex widehat{CFL}(L) and a collection of chain maps that encode the surgery coefficients. This formula has been used to compute Heegaard Floer homology of many families of 3‑manifolds, including all surgeries on the Whitehead link and on torus links.

An important subtlety is that the link surgery formula requires a choice of framing for each component; changing the framing by a full twist corresponds to tensoring with a certain U-equivariant chain complex that shifts the Alexander grading. The formula also respects the spin^c structures on the surgered manifold: each spin^c structure on Yp/q(L) corresponds to a set of relative spin^c structures on the link complement, and the hypercube decomposes accordingly.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Handleslide and Stabilization

The original definition of Heegaard Floer homology by Ozsváth and Szabó used a fixed Heegaard diagram. To prove that the resulting groups are topological invariants, one must show independence of the diagram. The two elementary moves on Heegaard diagrams are handleslides (sliding one α-curve over another) and stabilisation (increasing the genus by adding a cancelling pair of α and β curves intersecting once). Ozsváth and Szabó proved invariance under handleslides by constructing a chain homotopy equivalence between the chain complexes before and after the slide. The proof analyses the moduli spaces of holomorphic disks in the symmetric product and uses a neck‑stretching argument to show that the only new disks that appear are those that cross the sliding region; these are shown to cancel in pairs.

Invariance under stabilisation is more delicate because the symmetric product changes dimension. The argument proceeds by embedding the original symmetric product as a subvariety of the stabilised one and showing that the inclusion induces a quasi‑isomorphism on the chain level. A key technical tool is the holomorphic triangle count that defines the map associated to a cobordism given by a 2‑handle attachment; the stabilisation move corresponds to attaching a cancelling 2‑/3‑handle pair, and the associated cobordism map is shown to be a homotopy equivalence.

These invariance proofs also establish that the Heegaard Floer homology groups carry a natural action of the mapping class group of the 3‑manifold. A mapping class can be represented by a sequence of handleslides and stabilisations, and the induced map on homology is independent of the chosen sequence. This mapping class group action is trivial on the hat version widehat{HF} for many manifolds (e.g. S3), but can be non‑trivial on the plus version HF+ for manifolds with b1>0. The action has been computed explicitly for surface bundles over the circle using the bordered theory.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Tight Contact Structures on Lens Spaces

The Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) lives in the plus version of Heegaard Floer homology HF+(−Y) for a closed oriented 3‑manifold Y equipped with a contact structure ξ. For lens spaces L(p,q) the classification of tight contact structures is complete: up to isotopy there is exactly one tight contact structure when p>1, and it is the standard one induced by the tight contact structure on S³ via the p‑fold cyclic covering. Ozsváth and Szabó proved that c(ξ) is non‑zero for this unique tight structure and vanishes for every overtwisted contact structure on the same manifold. The proof uses the surgery exact triangle together with the fact that L(p,q) can be obtained by −p/q surgery on the unknot in S³. The contact invariant of the surgered manifold is identified with the image of the contact invariant of S³ under the cobordism map induced by the 2‑handle attachment. Because the unknot carries the standard tight contact structure, the resulting invariant is the generator of the U‑torsion submodule of HF+(−L(p,q)). This computation shows that the contact invariant distinguishes the tight contact structure on any lens space from all overtwisted ones, and it also provides a practical criterion: a contact structure on a lens space is tight if and only if its Ozsváth–Szabó invariant is non‑zero in HF+. The same method extends to small Seifert fibered spaces, where the contact invariant can be read off from the graded root associated to the surgery diagram.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the d‑Invariant for Seifert Fibered Spaces

The correction term (or d‑invariant) d(Y,𝔰) ∈ ℚ is a rational number associated to a spinc structure 𝔰 on a rational homology 3‑sphere Y. For Seifert fibered spaces the d‑invariant can be computed explicitly using the rational surgery formula of Ozsváth and Szabó together with the lattice homology description of Némethi. A Seifert fibered space M = M(b; (a₁,b₁), …, (aₙ,bₙ)) is obtained by surgery on a framed link consisting of a central unknot with framing b and n Hopf‑linked unknots with framings aᵢ/bᵢ. The Heegaard Floer chain complex of M splits as a direct sum over spinc structures, each of which corresponds to a vertex of the associated plumbing graph. The d‑invariant for a given spinc structure equals the minimal grading of a non‑torsion element in the corresponding summand of HF+. Némethi’s lattice homology provides a combinatorial algorithm: one constructs the negative‑definite plumbing graph, computes the set of characteristic vectors, and evaluates the formula d(Y,𝔰) = maxK∈Char (K² + |V|)/4 where the maximum runs over characteristic vectors K that represent the given spinc structure. This algorithm has been implemented in software such as HFhat and yields the d‑invariants for all Seifert fibered homology spheres up to any desired complexity. The values satisfy the symmetry d(Y,𝔰) = −d(−Y,𝔰) and the inequality d(Y,𝔰) ≤ 0 for any rational homology sphere that bounds a negative‑definite 4‑manifold, a consequence of the Ozsváth–Szabó 4‑manifold adjunction inequality.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Relationship with the Rasmussen s‑Invariant

The Rasmussen invariant s(K) ∈ ℤ is a knot concordance invariant defined via the Khovanov homology of a knot K ⊂ S³. Ozsváth and Szabó’s τ‑invariant τ(K) arises from the knot Floer homology HFK(K). A fundamental theorem, proved independently by Rasmussen and by Livingston–Naik, states that for every knot K in S³ the equality s(K) = 2 τ(K) holds. The proof uses the spectral sequence from Khovanov homology to Heegaard Floer homology constructed by Ozsváth and Szabó; the E² page of this spectral sequence is the reduced Khovanov homology, and it converges to the hat version of knot Floer homology. The filtration on Khovanov homology that defines s corresponds under the spectral sequence to the Alexander filtration on HFK that defines τ. Because the spectral sequence respects the filtrations up to an overall shift, the two invariants are proportional with factor 2. This relationship has several important consequences: (1) the slice genus bound g₄(K) ≥ |τ(K)| = |s(K)|/2 follows from either invariant; (2) the concordance homomorphism τ: 𝒞 → ℤ and s: 𝒞 → 2ℤ have identical kernels; (3) for quasi‑alternating knots both invariants are determined by the signature σ(K) via τ(K) = −σ(K)/2 and s(K) = −σ(K). The equality also implies that any knot with τ(K) = 0 has s(K) = 0, and therefore such knots are not detected by either invariant as non‑slice. Recent work by Hom and Wu shows that the equality persists for knots in arbitrary homology spheres after appropriate grading normalisations.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization via the Cylindrical Reformulation

Lipshitz’s cylindrical reformulation replaces the symmetric product Symg(Σ) by a product of a cylinder with a lower‑dimensional symmetric product, making the holomorphic curve equation more amenable to analysis. In this setting a Heegaard diagram of genus g is represented by a collection of α‑ and β‑curves on a surface Σ with a distinguished basepoint z. Stabilization adds a cancelling pair of α and β curves intersecting once in a small neighbourhood of z. In the cylindrical picture this corresponds to attaching a trivial 1‑handle to the domain of the holomorphic curves. The invariance proof proceeds by constructing an explicit chain homotopy equivalence between the original chain complex CF⁻(Σ,α,β,z) and the stabilized complex CF⁻(Σ#,α∪{α'},β∪{β'},z). The key observation is that the new intersection point created by the stabilizing curves contributes a contractible summand generated by the unique intersection point of α' and β'. The differential on this summand is exactly the U‑action, and the inclusion of the original complex as the complement of this summand is a quasi‑isomorphism. The cylindrical reformulation makes the neck‑stretching argument transparent: as the neck length tends to infinity, holomorphic curves that cross the stabilizing region break into a pair of curves, one of which is a trivial cylinder, and the resulting count yields the identity map on the original complex. This approach also yields a clean proof that the stabilization map commutes with the cobordism maps induced by 2‑handle attachments, which is essential for the functoriality of the theory under composition of cobordisms.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Mapping Class Group Action on the Hat Version for Manifolds with b₁=0

For a closed oriented 3‑manifold Y with first Betti number b₁(Y)=0 (i.e. a rational homology sphere), the mapping class group MCG(Y) acts on the set of Heegaard diagrams and therefore on the Heegaard Floer homology groups. Ozsváth and Szabó showed that the induced action on the hat version HF̂(Y) is trivial. The proof uses the fact that any mapping class can be represented by a sequence of handleslides and stabilizations; the invariance isomorphisms constructed for these moves are canonical up to chain homotopy, and their composition yields the identity on homology. For the plus and minus versions the action can be non‑trivial when b₁>0, but for rational homology spheres the U‑action on HF⁺(Y) is nilpotent and the mapping class group acts trivially on the U‑torsion submodule as well. This triviality has been exploited to define the Ozsváth–Szabó 4‑manifold invariants ΦX,𝔰 for cobordisms between rational homology spheres: the invariance under diffeomorphisms of the boundary follows from the trivial mapping class group action. In contrast, for manifolds with b₁>0 the action on HF⁺ can be detected via the bordered theory; for example, the mapping class group of a surface bundle over the circle acts non‑trivially on the plus version, and the resulting representation factors through the symplectic group Sp(2g,ℤ) acting on the homology of the fiber.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Large Surgery Formula for Knots in S³

The large surgery formula, proved by Ozsváth and Szabó, expresses the Heegaard Floer homology of the three‑manifold obtained by p/q‑surgery on a knot K ⊂ S³ when |p| is sufficiently large in terms of the knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K). The formula identifies the chain complex for the surgered manifold with a mapping cone built from the filtered chain homotopy type of CFK⁻(K). In particular, for integer surgeries with p > 2g(K)−1, the plus version HF⁺(S³_p(K)) splits as a direct sum of a “large” piece isomorphic to HF⁺(S³) and a “small” piece determined by the knot Floer homology groups HFK⁻(K, i) for Alexander gradings i between −g(K) and g(K). The differential in the mapping cone is induced by the U‑action and the inclusion maps between the filtered subcomplexes. This description makes it possible to compute the correction terms d(S³_p(K), 𝔰) for all Spin^c structures 𝔰 when p is large, and it underlies the proof that L‑space knots are precisely those admitting a positive L‑space surgery.

The proof proceeds by analyzing the Heegaard diagram for the surgered manifold obtained by adding a 2‑handle along the knot with framing p. One stretches the neck along the knot complement and counts holomorphic disks that intersect the new β‑curve. The neck‑stretching limit yields a holomorphic curve equation on the symplectization of the knot complement, whose solutions correspond to the filtered differential on CFK⁻(K). The large surgery condition guarantees that no holomorphic disks cross the neck more than once, so the resulting chain map is a quasi‑isomorphism onto the mapping cone. The formula has been extended to rational surgeries by Ni and Wu, who introduced a rational surgery formula that works for any p/q with p > 0, using a graded root model to keep track of the Spin^c structures.

Applications of the large surgery formula include the classification of knots with lens space surgeries, the determination of the knot genus from the Heegaard Floer homology of large surgeries, and the proof that the Ozsváth–Szabó τ invariant equals the maximal Alexander grading of a non‑torsion class in HFK⁻(K). The formula also provides a practical algorithm for computing HF⁺ of large surgeries, which has been implemented in the software package “HFhat” for explicit examples.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Correction Terms for Rational Homology Spheres via the d‑Invariant

The correction term d(Y, 𝔰) ∈ ℚ, also called the Ozsváth–Szabó d‑invariant, is defined for a rational homology sphere Y equipped with a Spin^c structure 𝔰 as the minimal grading of a non‑torsion element in the image of the natural map HF⁻(Y, 𝔰) → HF⁺(Y, 𝔰). Equivalently, it is the grading shift needed to identify the bottom of the U‑tower in HF⁺(Y, 𝔰) with the standard tower in HF⁺(S³). The d‑invariant is a homology cobordism invariant: if W is a negative‑definite cobordism from Y₁ to Y₂, then d(Y₁, 𝔰₁) ≥ d(Y₂, 𝔰₂) for any Spin^c structures extending over W. This inequality yields powerful obstructions to the existence of metrics of positive scalar curvature and to the sliceness of knots.

For Seifert fibered spaces, the d‑invariant can be computed combinatorially from the plumbing graph. Ozsváth and Szabó gave an explicit formula in terms of the continued fraction expansions of the Seifert invariants. The formula involves a lattice homology calculation on the associated negative‑definite plumbing tree, which reduces to finding the minimum of a quadratic form over characteristic vectors. For a Seifert fibered space Σ(b; (a₁,b₁), …, (a_n,b_n)) with b < 0, the d‑invariant for the unique Spin^c structure with c₁ = 0 is d = (c₁² + |V|)/4 − 2·mink∈Char (Q(k) + Σ_i ⌈k·E_i / a_i⌉), where Q is the intersection form on the plumbing lattice and E_i are the exceptional fibers. This expression has been used to determine which Seifert fibered spaces are L‑spaces and to verify the L‑space conjecture for graph manifolds.

The d‑invariant also appears in the surgery exact triangle. For a knot K ⊂ Y in a rational homology sphere, the triangle relating HF⁺(Y), HF⁺(Y₀(K)), and HF⁺(Y₁(K)) induces a relation among the corresponding d‑invariants. In particular, for integer surgeries on a knot in S³, the d‑invariants of S³_p(K) for p > 0 determine the knot Floer homology groups HFK⁻(K, i) via the large surgery formula. This interplay makes the d‑invariant a central tool for extracting topological information from Heegaard Floer homology.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Open Books with Planar Pages

An open book decomposition of a 3‑manifold Y with planar pages (genus zero) corresponds to a contact structure that is supported by a planar open book. Ozsváth, Szabó, and later Honda–Kazez–Matić showed that the contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁺(−Y) is non‑zero for any tight contact structure supported by a planar open book. The proof uses a Heegaard diagram adapted to the open book: the α‑curves are the boundaries of the pages, the β‑curves are the vanishing cycles of the monodromy, and the basepoint lies on the binding. In this diagram the contact invariant is represented by the top‑degree generator of the hat version HF̂(−Y), which survives in the plus version because the differential cannot lower the grading past the minimal Alexander grading of the page.

For overtwisted contact structures, the contact invariant vanishes. The distinction is detected by the existence of a bypass attachment that changes the monodromy by a positive Dehn twist along a curve parallel to the boundary. In the Heegaard Floer setting, this bypass corresponds to a holomorphic disk that kills the contact class. Consequently, the non‑vanishing of c(ξ) gives a necessary condition for tightness, and for planar open books it is also sufficient. This result has been used to classify tight contact structures on lens spaces and on small Seifert fibered spaces with e₀ > −2.

The planar open book condition is equivalent to the existence of a Stein filling with a handlebody decomposition containing only 0‑ and 1‑handles. The contact invariant then coincides with the Ozsváth–Szabó invariant of the Stein filling, providing a bridge between contact topology and 4‑manifold invariants. Recent work by Etnyre–Ozbagci extends the non‑vanishing result to open books with pages of genus one under additional hypotheses on the monodromy.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Surgery Exact Triangle for Links in S³

The surgery exact triangle for a link L = L₁ ∪ … ∪ L_m in S³ relates the Heegaard Floer homology of the manifolds obtained by performing 0‑surgery on a subset of components. For a single component, the triangle connects HF⁺(S³), HF⁺(S³0(L₁)), and HF⁺(S³1(L₁)). For a link, one can iterate the triangle to obtain a hypercube of chain complexes whose total homology is HF⁺ of the manifold obtained by 0‑surgery on all components. The maps in the hypercube are induced by 2‑handle attachments corresponding to changing the surgery coefficient from 0 to 1 on each component. This construction yields the link surgery formula of Ozsváth–Szabó, which expresses HF⁺(S³0(L)) as the homology of a mapping cone built from the link Floer complex CFL⁻(L).

The link Floer complex CFL⁻(L) carries a multi‑variable Alexander grading (A₁,…,A_m) and a Maslov grading. The differential counts holomorphic disks in the symmetric product of the Heegaard surface that intersect the β‑curves associated to each component. The surgery exact triangle is proved by analyzing the ends of the moduli spaces of holomorphic disks when the surgery coefficient varies, using a neck‑stretching argument along the torus boundary of the link complement. The resulting chain maps are quasi‑isomorphisms onto the mapping cones, and the triangle commutes up to chain homotopy.

Applications include the computation of HF⁺ for 0‑surgeries on alternating links, the detection of fibered links via the rank of the top Alexander grading, and the proof that the link Floer homology of an L‑space link is determined by its multi‑variable Alexander polynomial. The triangle also underlies the proof of the Berge conjecture for knots in S³, where the classification of lens space surgeries reduces to analyzing the mapping cone for a single knot.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the τ Invariant for Alternating Knots

For an alternating knot K ⊂ S³, the knot Floer homology HFK⁻(K) is determined by the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t) and the signature σ(K). Ozsváth and Szabó proved that HFK⁻(K) is supported in a single δ‑grading, and the τ invariant equals −σ(K)/2. The proof uses a grid diagram representing the alternating projection and the fact that the associated Heegaard diagram is “nice”: all regions are bigons or squares. In a nice diagram the differential in CFK⁻ counts only holomorphic disks that are embedded bigons or squares, which can be enumerated combinatorially. The resulting chain complex is isomorphic to the reduced Khovanov complex after a suitable grading shift, and the homology is thin, meaning it lies on two adjacent diagonals in the (Maslov, Alexander) plane.

The thinness implies that the spectral sequence from Khovanov homology to HFK⁻ collapses at the E² page, and the τ invariant can be read off from the Alexander polynomial via the formula τ(K) = max{ i | the coefficient of t^i in Δ_K(t) is non‑zero and i ≤ −σ(K)/2 }. For alternating knots this maximum is exactly −σ(K)/2. Consequently, τ provides a lower bound for the slice genus g₄(K) that is sharp for alternating knots: g₄(K) = (1−σ(K))/2. This equality was used by Ozsváth–Szabó to give a new proof of the Milnor conjecture for torus knots, which are alternating.

The combinatorial algorithm for τ from a grid diagram runs in polynomial time in the grid number. It has been implemented in the program “GridLink” and used to compute τ for all knots up to 16 crossings. The algorithm also yields the ε invariant of Hom, which refines τ and detects chirality for alternating knots. The relationship between τ, ε, and the concordance group remains an active area of research.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the d‑Invariant for Plumbed Manifolds via Lattice Homology

For a negative‑definite plumbing graph Γ describing a three‑manifold Y, the Ozsváth–Szabó correction term d(Y,𝔰) can be read from the lattice homology groups ℍ⁻(Γ,𝔰) introduced by Némethi. The lattice homology is the homology of a chain complex generated by characteristic vectors K ∈ Char(Γ) with a filtration given by the weight function w(K) = (K² + |V|)/4, where |V| is the number of vertices. The differential counts certain paths in the lattice of characteristic vectors, and the resulting graded ℤ[U]‑module is isomorphic to HF⁻(Y,𝔰) as a relatively graded module. The d‑invariant is then the minimal grading of a non‑torsion element in the image of the U‑action, i.e. d(Y,𝔰) = 2·min{ gr(x) | x ∈ HF⁻(Y,𝔰), Uⁿx ≠ 0 for all n } − 2. In practice one constructs the graded root associated to the plumbing graph: vertices correspond to spinᶜ structures, edges correspond to U‑multiplication, and the root’s height function gives the d‑invariants. For a rational homology sphere obtained by plumbing according to a star‑shaped graph (e.g. Brieskorn spheres Σ(p,q,r)), the graded root can be computed by a recursive algorithm that resolves the graph into simpler pieces. The resulting d‑invariants agree with the formulas derived from the surgery exact triangle and provide a purely combinatorial method that avoids holomorphic curve counts. This approach also yields the Ozsváth–Szabó inequality relating d(Y,𝔰) to the Thurston norm of classes in H₂(Y;ℤ) when Y bounds a smooth four‑manifold.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Contact Structures with Giroux Torsion

Giroux torsion is a measure of the failure of a contact structure to be tight in a neighborhood of a torus. A contact manifold (Y,ξ) has Giroux torsion ≥ k if it contains a contact embedding of T² × [0,1] with dividing curves of slope 0 on both boundary components and with k full twists of the contact planes. Honda, Kazez and Matić proved that if ξ has positive Giroux torsion then the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁻(−Y) vanishes. The proof uses the open book decomposition supporting ξ: positive Giroux torsion forces the monodromy to contain a product of right‑handed Dehn twists about parallel curves, which in turn creates a bypass attachment that kills the contact element in the sutured Floer homology of the complement. Conversely, for contact structures without Giroux torsion (e.g. Stein fillable or universally tight structures) the contact invariant is often non‑zero and can distinguish different tight structures on the same manifold. In the bordered theory, the contact invariant corresponds to a distinguished element in the type‑D module associated to the contact boundary, and its vanishing can be detected by pairing with the type‑A module of a filling. This provides a practical criterion: given a contact structure presented by an open book, one computes the monodromy’s fractional Dehn twist coefficients; if any coefficient exceeds 1, Giroux torsion is present and c(ξ) = 0. The vanishing result has been extended to the plus and hat versions of Heegaard Floer homology, and it plays a central role in the classification of tight contact structures on Seifert fibered spaces and on manifolds obtained by Legendrian surgery.

Worked Example: Vanishing of the Contact Invariant
Consider the contact structure ξ on the 3‑manifold Y = T² × S¹ obtained by taking the standard tight contact structure on T² × [0,1] and inserting two full twists (Giroux torsion = 2) before gluing the ends. Determine whether the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁻(−Y) is zero.
The manifold Y fibers over S¹ with fiber T² and monodromy the identity. Inserting two full twists means the monodromy becomes the product of two right‑handed Dehn twists about the same curve, i.e. a Dehn twist squared. This monodromy has fractional Dehn twist coefficient 2 > 1, so by the Honda–Kazez–Matić criterion the contact structure has positive Giroux torsion. The theorem states that any contact structure with positive Giroux torsion has vanishing contact invariant in all versions of Heegaard Floer homology. Therefore c(ξ) = 0 in HF⁻(−Y) (and also in HF̂ and HF⁺). No further computation of the Heegaard Floer groups is needed; the vanishing follows directly from the Giroux torsion condition.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Surgery Exact Triangle for Knots in Homology Spheres

Let Y be an integer homology sphere and K ⊂ Y a null‑homologous knot. For an integer n, denote by Yₙ(K) the result of n‑surgery on K. Ozsváth and Szabó constructed an exact triangle relating the Heegaard Floer homologies of Yₙ(K), Yₙ₊₁(K) and Y. The three vertices are HF⁺(Yₙ(K)), HF⁺(Yₙ₊₁(K)) and HF⁺(Y), and the maps are induced by the two‑handle cobordisms corresponding to changing the surgery coefficient by one. The triangle is proved by analyzing the ends of the moduli spaces of holomorphic disks in the symmetric product of a Heegaard surface for the knot complement, using a neck‑stretching argument along the torus boundary. The key algebraic input is the mapping cone formula: the chain complex CF⁺(Yₙ(K)) is quasi‑isomorphic to the mapping cone of a chain map Φₙ: CF⁺(Y) → CF⁺(Yₙ₊₁(K)) that counts holomorphic triangles with one vertex at the basepoint. When Y = S³, the triangle reduces to the familiar surgery exact triangle for knots in S³, and the maps can be described explicitly in terms of the knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K). For a general homology sphere, the knot Floer complex is replaced by the relative invariant CFK⁻(Y,K), which carries a single Alexander grading. The exact triangle implies that the rank of HF⁺(Yₙ(K)) is bounded by the sum of the ranks of HF⁺(Y) and HF⁺(Yₙ₊₁(K)). This inequality is a crucial ingredient in the proof of Property P for knots in homology spheres: if n‑surgery on K yields a manifold with the same homology as S¹ × S², then the triangle forces the knot Floer homology to be trivial, which by the detection theorem implies K is the unknot.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Behavior under Mutation

Mutation is an operation on a link diagram that cuts a tangle bounded by a four‑punctured sphere, rotates the tangle by 180° about one of three axes, and reglued. The Conway and Kinoshita–Terasaka knots are a classic mutant pair. Heegaard Floer homology exhibits a subtle relationship with mutation. Ozsváth and Szabó proved that the hat version HF̂(Y) of a closed three‑manifold is invariant under mutation of a knot inside Y, provided the mutation is performed along a Conway sphere that separates the knot into two tangles. However, the full knot Floer homology HFK⁻(K) is not mutation invariant in general; the Conway and Kinoshita–Terasaka knots have isomorphic HF̂ but distinct HFK⁻. The difference appears in the Alexander grading: the mutant pair have the same Alexander polynomial but different knot Floer homology groups in higher Alexander gradings. In the bordered theory, mutation corresponds to an automorphism of the type‑D module associated to the four‑punctured sphere, and the invariance of the hat version follows from the fact that this automorphism acts trivially on the homology of the pairing with any type‑A module. For the minus and plus versions, the automorphism acts non‑trivially on the U‑action, leading to different filtered chain homotopy types. Recent work by Baldwin and Levine shows that the ε‑invariant of Hom is mutation invariant, while the τ‑invariant can change. This pattern mirrors the behavior of Khovanov homology, where the reduced theory is mutation invariant but the unreduced theory is not. Understanding which versions of Heegaard Floer homology detect mutation is essential for applications to concordance and slice genus, because mutation preserves the slice genus but can change τ.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the ε Invariant for Knots in S³

The ε‑invariant, introduced by Hom, takes values in {−1,0,1} and refines the τ‑invariant. It is defined using the knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K) equipped with its two filtrations (algebraic and Alexander). One considers the subcomplexes A⁻_s = C{i < 0 or j < s} and the quotient complexes B_s = CFK⁻/A⁻_s. The invariant ε(K) = 1 if the map on homology induced by inclusion A⁻0 → CFK⁻ is non‑zero; ε(K) = −1 if the map induced by projection CFK⁻ → B0 is non‑zero; otherwise ε(K) = 0. Equivalently, ε(K) records whether the knot Floer homology has a non‑torsion element in the top or bottom Alexander grading. The ε‑invariant is a concordance homomorphism: ε(K₁ # K₂) = ε(K₁) + ε(K₂) (with the convention that 1 + (−1) = 0). It also satisfies |τ(K)| ≤ g₄(K) and ε(K) = sign(τ(K)) when τ(K) ≠ 0. For alternating knots, ε(K) = sign(−σ(K)/2). The computation of ε can be carried out combinatorially from a grid diagram: the grid complex GC⁻(K) is filtered chain homotopic to CFK⁻(K), and the maps on homology can be read from the grid homology groups. In practice, one computes the grid homology groups GH⁻(K) and the induced maps of the inclusion and projection on the E² page of the spectral sequence from the associated graded complex. The algorithm runs in polynomial time in the grid number and has been implemented in the software package “GridLink”. For the right‑handed trefoil, ε = 1; for the figure‑eight knot, ε = 0; for the (3,4) torus knot, ε = 1 while τ = 3. The ε‑invariant detects chirality for many knots where τ vanishes, and it provides a lower bound for the four‑dimensional clasp number.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization via the Cylindrical Reformulation

The cylindrical reformulation of Heegaard Floer homology, introduced by Lipshitz, replaces the symmetric product of a Heegaard surface by a cylindrical almost‑complex manifold built from the Heegaard diagram. In this setting a stabilization of the diagram corresponds to attaching a 1‑handle to the cylindrical end and extending the almost‑complex structure by a standard model. The invariance proof proceeds by constructing a chain homotopy equivalence between the original and stabilized cylindrical chain complexes. The key analytic input is a neck‑stretching argument: as the length of the cylindrical neck tends to infinity, holomorphic curves in the stabilized diagram converge to broken curves consisting of a curve in the original diagram together with a trivial cylinder over the new α‑β intersection. The gluing theorem for holomorphic curves then provides a quasi‑inverse to the inclusion map. Because the cylindrical model eliminates the need for admissibility perturbations, the proof works uniformly for all Spinc structures, including torsion ones. The resulting chain homotopy equivalence is canonical up to homotopy and respects the U‑action, the Alexander filtration, and the grading shift by one half. Consequently the hat, minus, plus, and infinity versions of Heegaard Floer homology are all invariant under stabilization. This approach also clarifies the behavior of the contact invariant: the cylindrical contact element is carried by the obvious inclusion of the original diagram into the stabilized one, so its non‑vanishing is preserved. The cylindrical reformulation has become the standard framework for proving naturality of cobordism maps and for developing the bordered theory, because the type‑A and type‑D modules are defined directly on the cylindrical chain complex.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Handleslide and Stabilization

Handleslide invariance is the statement that the Heegaard Floer chain complex does not change when an α‑curve is slid over another α‑curve (or a β‑curve over another β‑curve) in the Heegaard diagram. The proof uses a sequence of elementary moves: a handleslide can be decomposed into a stabilization, an isotopy, and a destabilization. Since stabilization invariance is already established via the cylindrical reformulation, it suffices to show that the chain homotopy equivalence associated to a handleslide is well defined up to homotopy. Ozsváth and Szabó constructed an explicit chain map by counting holomorphic triangles in a triple Heegaard diagram that encodes the handleslide. The triangle count satisfies the A∞ relations because the boundary of the 1‑dimensional moduli space of holomorphic triangles corresponds to broken configurations that exactly reproduce the composition of the handleslide map with the differential. The resulting map is a filtered chain homotopy equivalence preserving the Maslov and Alexander gradings. In the bordered setting, a handleslide of an α‑curve in the interior of a bordered diagram induces an automorphism of the associated type‑D module; this automorphism is homotopic to the identity, which is the algebraic shadow of handleslide invariance. The combination of handleslide and stabilization invariance implies that the Heegaard Floer homology groups are invariants of the underlying 3‑manifold (or knot) independent of the chosen Heegaard diagram. This invariance is the foundation for all subsequent applications, including the surgery exact triangle, the mapping cone formula, and the definition of the contact invariant.

Worked Example: Handleslide Map on a Genus‑Two Diagram
Consider a genus‑two Heegaard diagram for the 3‑torus T³ with α‑curves α₁, α₂ and β‑curves β₁, β₂. Perform a handleslide of α₁ over α₂, producing a new curve α₁′. Write down the triple diagram (α₁, α₂, α₁′; β₁, β₂) and describe the chain map Φ induced by counting holomorphic triangles. Explain why Φ is a chain homotopy equivalence.
The triple diagram has three α‑curves and two β‑curves; the intersection points are x ∈ α₁∩β, y ∈ α₂∩β, and z ∈ α₁′∩β. A holomorphic triangle contributing to Φ has vertices at x, y, and z. Because the handleslide is supported in a neighbourhood of α₂, the triangle count reduces to a single rigid triangle representing the identity on the intersection points not involved in the slide. The map Φ sends the generator corresponding to x to the generator corresponding to z and acts as the identity on the other generators. The boundary of the 1‑dimensional moduli space of triangles gives Φ∘∂ + ∂∘Φ = 0, so Φ is a chain map. Its inverse is given by the handleslide of α₁′ back over α₂, and the composition is chain homotopic to the identity by a standard homotopy of triangles. Hence Φ is a filtered chain homotopy equivalence preserving Maslov and Alexander gradings.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization via the Cylindrical Reformulation for Manifolds with Boundary

For 3‑manifolds with boundary, the cylindrical reformulation extends to the bordered theory by attaching cylindrical ends corresponding to the boundary components. A stabilization of a bordered Heegaard diagram adds a pair of α‑ and β‑curves in the interior of the surface, away from the boundary arcs. The resulting type‑A and type‑D modules are related by a chain homotopy equivalence that is constructed exactly as in the closed case, but now the holomorphic curves are counted in the cylindrical manifold with boundary conditions given by the Reeb chords on the boundary. The neck‑stretching argument still applies because the new curves are disjoint from the boundary, so the limiting broken curves consist of a curve in the original bordered diagram and a trivial cylinder over the new intersection point. The gluing theorem for bordered holomorphic curves provides the quasi‑inverse. This invariance implies that the bordered invariants CFA and CFD are well defined up to homotopy equivalence of A∞ modules, independent of the choice of bordered Heegaard diagram. Consequently the pairing theorem, which recovers the closed Heegaard Floer homology from the tensor product of a type‑A and a type‑D module, is independent of the diagrams used on each side. The cylindrical reformulation also makes the grading refinement by the relative Spinc structure transparent, because the grading is computed from the homology class of the cylindrical curve relative to the boundary.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization via the Cylindrical Reformulation for Links in S³

When a link L ⊂ S³ is represented by a multi‑pointed Heegaard diagram, a stabilization adds a pair of basepoints together with a cancelling pair of α‑ and β‑curves. The link Floer chain complex CFL⁻(L) is defined as the chain complex associated to the diagram with all basepoints. Stabilization invariance for links follows from the same cylindrical argument, because the new basepoints lie on the new curves and the holomorphic disks that contribute to the differential are unchanged away from the stabilization region. The Alexander multi‑filtration is preserved because the new basepoints contribute trivially to the filtration levels. As a result, the link Floer homology HFL⁻(L) and the associated concordance invariants τ(L), ε(L), and the multivariable Alexander polynomial are independent of the diagram. This invariance is essential for the surgery formula for links, where one must compare the knot Floer complexes of different surgery descriptions of the same 3‑manifold. The cylindrical reformulation also simplifies the proof that the link surgery formula is well defined, because the chain homotopy equivalences induced by stabilizations commute with the maps appearing in the surgery exact triangle.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization via the Cylindrical Reformulation for Contact Structures

The contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF̂(−Y) is defined using a Heegaard diagram adapted to an open book supporting the contact structure ξ. A stabilization of the open book corresponds to a positive stabilization of the Heegaard diagram, which adds a 1‑handle and a pair of curves intersecting once. In the cylindrical reformulation, this stabilization is exactly the cylindrical stabilization described earlier. The contact element is represented by the top‑degree generator of the stabilized diagram, and the chain homotopy equivalence between the original and stabilized complexes carries this generator to the top‑degree generator of the stabilized complex. Therefore the non‑vanishing of c(ξ) is invariant under positive stabilization of the open book. Combined with the fact that any two open books supporting the same contact structure are related by a sequence of positive stabilizations and destabilizations (Giroux correspondence), this proves that c(ξ) is a well‑defined invariant of the contact structure. Moreover, the cylindrical reformulation shows that the contact invariant is natural under contactomorphisms: a contactomorphism induces a diffeomorphism of the open book, which in turn induces a chain homotopy equivalence of the cylindrical complexes preserving the contact element.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Surgery Exact Triangle for Knots in S³ with Non‑Integral Surgeries

The Ozsváth–Szabó rational surgery formula expresses the Heegaard Floer homology of a 3‑manifold obtained by p/q surgery on a knot K ⊂ S³ in terms of the knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K). For non‑integral slopes the surgery exact triangle relates the three manifolds obtained by surgeries with coefficients r, r′, r″ satisfying 1/r + 1/r′ + 1/r″ = 0. The triangle is built from the mapping cone of chain maps induced by the elementary cobordisms between the surgeries. The maps are defined by counting holomorphic triangles in the symmetric product of the Heegaard surface with boundary conditions given by the three sets of attaching curves corresponding to the three slopes. The grading shifts in the triangle are determined by the relative Spinc structures on the cobordisms; the absolute grading on HF⁻ is fixed by the correction term d(Y,𝔰). A key technical point is that the triangle is exact only after completing the complexes with respect to the U‑action, i.e. working with the minus version HF⁻. The hat version HF̂ fits into a long exact sequence only after a grading shift of ½, which reflects the fact that the cobordisms have b₂⁺ = 0. The rational surgery formula also yields an explicit description of the knot Floer homology of the core of the surgery solid torus, which is essential for the proof of the L‑space conjecture for graph manifolds.

In practice, to compute HF⁻(S³p/q(K)) one constructs a filtered chain complex C{p/q} whose filtration levels correspond to the Alexander grading on CFK⁻(K). The differential counts holomorphic disks that cross the β‑curves representing the surgery slope. The mapping cone formula identifies C{p/q} with the mapping cone of a chain map v + h between two copies of the knot Floer complex, where v and h are the vertical and horizontal maps in the knot Floer grid. The exact triangle then follows from the short exact sequence of mapping cones associated to the three slopes. This framework has been used to compute the d‑invariants of all rational homology spheres obtained by surgery on alternating knots, and to prove that the Heegaard Floer homology of a rational homology sphere determines its Casson–Walker invariant.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Handleslide and Stabilization for Bordered Manifolds

Bordered Heegaard Floer homology assigns to a 3‑manifold Y with parametrized boundary Σ a type‑A A∞ module CFA(Y) and a type‑D structure CFD(Y) over the bordered algebra A(Σ). Handleslide invariance for bordered manifolds is proved by constructing a chain homotopy equivalence between the modules associated to two diagrams that differ by a handleslide of an α‑curve over another α‑curve. The proof uses the same holomorphic triangle counts as in the closed case, but the boundary conditions now involve Reeb chords on the boundary of the cylindrical end. The triangle map is an A∞ module homomorphism that respects the idempotent decomposition determined by the boundary parametrization. Stabilization invariance for bordered manifolds follows from the cylindrical reformulation: adding a 1‑handle and a pair of curves intersecting once corresponds to tensoring with the bordered module of the standard solid torus, which is known to be a homotopy equivalence. The pairing theorem states that for a decomposition Y = Y₁ ∪Σ Y₂, the closed Heegaard Floer complex CF⁻(Y) is homotopy equivalent to the box tensor product CFA(Y₁) ⊠ CFD(Y₂). The grading on the tensor product is the sum of the relative gradings on the two modules, shifted by the Euler class of the glued Spinc structure. This grading refinement is transparent in the cylindrical model because the grading is computed from the homology class of the cylindrical curve relative to the boundary. The invariance of the pairing under handleslides and stabilizations on either side is a direct consequence of the invariance of the individual bordered modules.

Applying the Bordered Pairing Theorem
Let Y₁ be a bordered 3‑manifold with boundary a genus‑2 surface Σ, and suppose its type‑A module CFA(Y₁) is given by a single generator x with idempotent I₀ and differential ∂x = 0. Let Y₂ be a solid torus with a framing corresponding to the slope 1/0, whose type‑D module CFD(Y₂) has a single generator y with idempotent I₀ and differential δ¹(y) = 0. Compute the hat Heegaard Floer homology HF̂(Y₁ ∪ Y₂) using the pairing theorem.
The box tensor product CFA(Y₁) ⊠ CFD(Y₂) is generated by x ⊗ y over the ground ring 𝔽₂. Since both differentials vanish, the tensor product differential is zero. The idempotents match (I₀·I₀ = I₀), so the generator survives. The grading of x ⊗ y is gr(x) + gr(y) + ½·e(𝔰), where e(𝔰) is the Euler class of the glued Spinc structure. For the standard framing the Euler class is zero, and the relative gradings of the unique generators are both zero by definition. Hence the resulting complex has a single generator in grading zero, so HF̂(Y₁ ∪ Y₂) ≅ 𝔽₂ in degree zero. This matches the known fact that gluing a solid torus with the 0‑framing to a manifold with torus boundary yields a closed manifold whose hat homology has rank one in the Spinc structure extending the boundary condition.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Contact Structures with Positive Giroux Torsion on Seifert Fibered Spaces

Giroux torsion measures the failure of a contact structure to be tight in a neighborhood of a torus leaf of a foliation. A contact 3‑manifold (Y,ξ) has positive Giroux torsion if it contains a contact embedding of T² × [0,1] with the standard contact structure ker(cos(2πt) dx + sin(2πt) dy). Honda, Kazez, and Matić proved that if ξ has positive Giroux torsion then the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF̂(−Y) vanishes. The proof uses an open book decomposition supporting ξ; positive Giroux torsion forces the monodromy to contain a Dehn twist about a curve that is homologically non‑trivial in the page. In the Heegaard diagram coming from the open book, this Dehn twist introduces a pair of curves intersecting in a single point, which creates a differential that kills the top‑degree generator representing c(ξ). For Seifert fibered spaces, the classification of tight contact structures by Lisca and Stipsicz shows that the only tight structures without Giroux torsion are those with Euler class satisfying certain inequalities. The contact invariant distinguishes these: it is non‑zero exactly for the tight structures with maximal Euler class, and zero for all others. This result has been extended to the case of contact structures with Giroux torsion on graph manifolds by Ghiggini, where the vanishing of c(ξ) is used to prove that certain graph manifolds do not admit symplectic fillings.

The computation of c(ξ) for Seifert fibered spaces proceeds by choosing a Heegaard diagram adapted to a horizontal open book. The page is a punctured surface of genus g, and the monodromy is a product of Dehn twists along the regular fibers. The contact element is the unique generator in the top Alexander grading of the hat complex. When the Euler class is maximal, the differential vanishes on this generator because there are no holomorphic disks with the required boundary conditions; the non‑vanishing of c(ξ) then follows from the invariance of the contact invariant under positive stabilization of the open book. Conversely, if the Euler class is not maximal, a holomorphic disk appears that connects the top generator to a lower one, forcing c(ξ) = 0. This dichotomy provides a complete algorithm for deciding tightness of contact structures on Seifert fibered spaces using Heegaard Floer homology.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Spectral Sequence from Khovanov Homology to Heegaard Floer Homology for Links

Ozsváth and Szabó constructed a spectral sequence whose E² page is the reduced Khovanov homology Kh(L) of a link L ⊂ S³ and which converges to the link Floer homology HFL̂(L). The spectral sequence is built from a filtered chain complex associated to a grid diagram of the link. The filtration is given by the Alexander multi‑grading on the grid complex, and the differential on the E¹ page is the sum of the vertical and horizontal differentials of the grid. The E² page is then the homology of this differential, which is known to be isomorphic to Kh(L) after a suitable grading shift. The higher differentials dʳ for r ≥ 2 count holomorphic polygons in the symmetric product with boundary conditions determined by the grid. The convergence to HFL̂(L) follows from the fact that the total complex computes the hat version of link Floer homology. A crucial feature is that the spectral sequence respects the multi‑variable Alexander grading, so each page carries a bigrading (homological, Alexander). This allows one to read off the Thurston norm of the link complement from the support of the E∞ page. The spectral sequence has been used to prove that the Rasmussen s‑invariant of a knot is a lower bound for the slice genus, and that the τ invariant from knot Floer homology coincides with the s‑invariant for quasi‑alternating knots. For links, the spectral sequence provides a bridge between the combinatorial world of Khovanov homology and the analytic world of Heegaard Floer homology, enabling the transfer of results such as the detection of the unlink and the classification of fibered links.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization for the Full Infinity Complex

The full infinity complex CF∞(Y,𝔰) is a doubly infinite chain complex over the polynomial ring 𝔽[U,U−1] whose homology is the invariant HF∞(Y,𝔰). Ozsváth and Szabó proved in 2004 that HF∞ is invariant under stabilization of the Heegaard diagram. The proof uses the cylindrical reformulation: a stabilized diagram is obtained by adding a trivial handle and a pair of curves that intersect once. The symmetric product then splits as a product of the original symmetric product with a cylinder, and the holomorphic disk counts factor accordingly. The chain homotopy equivalence is constructed by a explicit map that inserts or removes the extra intersection point, and it respects the U‑action up to a grading shift of ½. Lipshitz later gave a purely combinatorial proof using the cylindrical reformulation and the fact that the differential on the stabilized complex is the tensor product of the original differential with the identity on the cylinder. The key point is that the extra generator contributes a contractible summand, so the homology is unchanged. This invariance is essential for defining the absolute grading on HF∞ and for the surgery exact triangle, where the maps between different Spinc structures are induced by cobordisms that may involve stabilization.

The grading shift of ½ appears because the Maslov index of the constant disk at the new intersection point is 1, while the U‑action lowers grading by 2. Consequently the chain homotopy equivalence is U‑equivariant only after shifting the grading by ½ on one side. This subtlety is often overlooked and leads to an incorrect absolute grading on HF∞. The cylindrical reformulation also shows that the invariance holds for the hat, plus, and minus versions simultaneously, because they are obtained from CF∞ by standard algebraic truncations. In particular, the map induced by a handleslide is a chain homotopy equivalence on each version, and the composition of a handleslide followed by a stabilization is homotopic to the identity. This fact underlies the proof that the contact invariant is well defined under stabilization of the supporting open book.

When working with the full complex, one must keep track of the two gradings: the homological grading (Maslov) and the Alexander grading (when a knot is present). The stabilization map preserves the Alexander grading because the new curves are disjoint from the knot. The U‑equivariance up to shift implies that the isomorphism HF∞(Y,𝔰) ≅ HF∞(Y,𝔰) induced by stabilization is not strictly grading preserving but becomes so after the ½ shift. This is the same shift that appears in the surgery exact triangle when comparing HF+ and HF−. A common mistake is to assume that the isomorphism is grading preserving without the shift, which would contradict the known grading of the generator of HF∞(S3) ≅ 𝔽[U,U−1] in grading 0.

The cylindrical reformulation also provides a clean framework for proving the invariance of the bordered theory under stabilization of the boundary. In that setting the extra Reeb chords introduced by stabilization are paired with the new intersection points, and the same contractible summand argument applies. The result is that the type D and type A modules associated to a bordered manifold are invariant under stabilization of the Heegaard diagram with boundary, a fact used crucially in the pairing theorem. The proof requires checking that the almost complex structure on the cylindrical end can be chosen so that the extra holomorphic curves are exactly the trivial strips, which is guaranteed by the neck‑stretching argument of Lipshitz–Ozsváth–Thurston.

In summary, the invariance of CF∞ under stabilization is a foundational result that rests on the cylindrical reformulation, the contractibility of the extra summand, and the U‑equivariance up to a ½ grading shift. It guarantees that all versions of Heegaard Floer homology are well defined invariants of the 3‑manifold and Spinc structure, and it underpins the functoriality under cobordisms and the behavior of the contact invariant.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Surgery Exact Triangle for Knots in Arbitrary 3‑Manifolds with Torsion Spinc Structures

The surgery exact triangle relates the Heegaard Floer homology groups of a 3‑manifold Y, the 0‑surgery Y0(K), and the 1‑surgery Y1(K) along a null‑homologous knot K ⊂ Y. For rational homology spheres the triangle was established by Ozsváth and Szabó in 2005 for the plus version HF+. The maps are induced by the cobordisms given by the 2‑handle attachments corresponding to the surgeries. When Y has torsion in H1(Y;ℤ), the Spinc structures on Y, Y0, and Y1 are not in one‑to‑one correspondence; instead they are related by the surgery cobordism which induces an affine identification of Spinc structures modulo the action of H2(W;ℤ) where W is the cobordism. Ni and Wu (2015) extended the triangle to the case where Y is an arbitrary closed oriented 3‑manifold and K is null‑homologous, showing that the triangle holds for each torsion Spinc structure on Y after summing over the appropriate Spinc structures on the surgered manifolds.

The exact triangle takes the form
⋯ → HF+(Y,𝔰) → HF+(Y0(K),𝔰0) → HF+(Y1(K),𝔰1) → HF+(Y,𝔰) → ⋯
where the maps are U‑equivariant and respect the relative ℤ/2 grading. The grading shift between the three terms is determined by the Chern class of the Spinc structures on the cobordism; for integer surgeries the shift is 0 for the map from Y to Y0 and −1 for the map from Y0 to Y1. When torsion Spinc structures are present, the triangle splits into a direct sum of triangles indexed by the torsion subgroup of H2(Y;ℤ). The knot Floer homology HFK−(Y,K) appears as the mapping cone of the map HF−(Y) → HF−(Y0), and the large surgery formula expresses HF+(Y1) as a mapping cone of a map from HFK− to HF+(Y). This mapping cone description is the basis for the rational surgery formula of Ozsváth–Szabó (2011).

A subtle point is that the triangle is not always split exact; the connecting homomorphism can be non‑trivial and detects the torsion in H1. For example, if Y = L(p,q) and K is a knot representing a generator of H1(Y), the triangle relates the d‑invariants of the lens spaces obtained by surgery. The non‑triviality of the connecting map is equivalent to the non‑vanishing of the correction term d(Y,𝔰) for some torsion Spinc structure. In the bordered theory, the surgery exact triangle is realized as a triangle of type D modules, and the maps are given by the DA bimodules associated to the elementary cobordisms. This perspective clarifies the grading shifts and the role of the Reeb chords on the boundary.

When applying the triangle to compute HF+ of a manifold obtained by surgery on a knot in a homology sphere, one often uses the fact that HF+(Y) is known (e.g., Y = S3) and the knot Floer homology of K is computable via grid diagrams. The triangle then determines HF+(Y1) up to an extension problem. The extension is resolved by the U‑equivariance and the absolute grading, which fixes the d‑invariant. A common error is to ignore the grading shift of ½ when passing from HF+ to HF− in the triangle; the shift is essential for the correct identification of the d‑invariant of the surgered manifold.

The surgery exact triangle also has a version for the hat and infinity flavors. The hat version is obtained by setting U = 0, and the infinity version by inverting U. The maps are the same, but the exactness properties differ: the hat triangle is exact only after tensoring with the field 𝔽, while the infinity triangle is always exact because HF∞ is a free 𝔽[U,U−1]‑module. The triangle for HF∞ is particularly simple: it splits as a direct sum of copies of the standard triangle for S3 indexed by the torsion Spinc structures. This splitting is used in the proof of the isomorphism between Heegaard Floer homology and Seiberg–Witten Floer homology, where the surgery exact triangle corresponds to the blow‑up formula in Seiberg–Witten theory.

Identifying the correct Spinc structure in the surgery exact triangle
Let Y be the lens space L(5,2) and let K ⊂ Y be a knot representing the generator of H1(Y) ≅ ℤ/5. Consider the 1‑surgery Y1(K). The Spinc structures on Y are indexed by i ∈ {0,1,2,3,4} with d‑invariants d(Y,i) = ((2i+1−5)2−5)/(20) − 2·s(2,5). The surgery cobordism W from Y to Y1 has H2(W;ℤ) ≅ ℤ generated by the Poincaré dual of the core of the 2‑handle. If 𝔰i on Y corresponds to 𝔰'j on Y1 under the affine identification 𝔰' = 𝔰 + k·PD[core], determine the integer k such that the map HF+(Y,𝔰i) → HF+(Y1,𝔰'j) in the surgery exact triangle is non‑zero.
The surgery exact triangle for integer surgeries identifies Spinc structures on Y1 with those on Y by the rule 𝔰' = 𝔰 + PD[core] (i.e., k = 1). This follows from the fact that the 2‑handle is attached with framing 1 relative to the 0‑framing, so the first Chern class of the Spinc structure on the cobordism evaluates on the core as 1. Consequently the map in the triangle connects HF+(Y,𝔰i) to HF+(Y1,𝔰'i+1 mod 5). The grading shift of the map is 0, so the d‑invariants satisfy d(Y1,i+1) = d(Y,i) + ½. Using the explicit formula for d(L(5,2),i) one checks that this equality holds, confirming k = 1.
Exam‑day strategy
When a surgery exact triangle question appears, first write down the indexing set for Spinc structures on each manifold. Then recall the affine identification rule: for p/q‑surgery the shift is given by the continued fraction expansion; for integer surgery it is simply +1. Always include the ½ grading shift when comparing d‑invariants of HF+ and HF−. A quick check is to verify the formula on the simplest case S3 → L(p,q) where the d‑invariants are known explicitly.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the d‑Invariant for Homology Spheres via the Mapping Cone Formula

The correction term d(Y,𝔰) ∈ ℚ is defined for any rational homology sphere Y equipped with a Spinc structure 𝔰 as the minimal grading of a non‑torsion element in the image of the map HF−(Y,𝔰) → HF∞(Y,𝔰). For an integer homology sphere Y obtained by p‑surgery on a knot K ⊂ S3 with p > 0, Ozsváth and Szabó (2008) gave a mapping cone formula that expresses CF−(Y) as the mapping cone of a chain map
𝔸+ → 𝔸−
between two subcomplexes of the knot Floer complex CFK−(K). The complex 𝔸+ is generated by elements with Alexander grading ≥ 0, while 𝔸− is generated by elements with Alexander grading ≤ 0. The map is the sum of the vertical differential and a horizontal differential that counts holomorphic disks crossing the basepoint. The homology of the mapping cone is isomorphic to HF−(Y). The d‑invariant is then read off from the grading of the generator of the tower in HF−(Y) that maps to the generator of HF∞(Y) ≅ 𝔽[U,U−1].

Concretely, if the knot Floer homology of K is known (e.g., from a grid diagram), one can compute the chain map explicitly. The mapping cone complex is a finite‑dimensional filtered complex over 𝔽[U]. Its homology is a direct sum of a tower 𝔽[U] and a finite‑dimensional torsion part. The tower generator has a well defined absolute grading, which is exactly d(Y). The formula for d(Y) in terms of the knot Floer homology is
d(Y) = maxs ∈ ℤ ( 2·Vs(K) − s )
where Vs(K) are the integers defined by the filtration on CFK−(K). This is the large surgery formula specialized to p → ∞. For finite p the formula involves a maximum over a finite set of Spinc structures on the surgery manifold, each corresponding to an integer s modulo p. The mapping cone description also yields the rational surgery formula for arbitrary rational homology spheres: one replaces the integer p by a rational number p/q and uses the continued fraction expansion to build a sequence of mapping cones corresponding to the plumbing graph of the surgery cobordism.

The mapping cone formula is proved by analyzing the Heegaard diagram for the surgery. The diagram is obtained by adding a pair of curves that intersect p times, and the symmetric product splits into a union of pieces that correspond to the subcomplexes 𝔸+ and 𝔸−. The holomorphic disks that contribute to the differential in the mapping cone are exactly those that cross the new basepoint a prescribed number of times. The proof uses the neck‑stretching argument to show that the count of such disks is independent of the almost complex structure and matches the algebraic mapping cone construction. The U‑equivariance of the map is built in because the vertical differential commutes with U, and the horizontal differential lowers the Alexander grading by 1 while preserving the U‑action.

An important application is the computation of d‑invariants for Seifert fibered homology spheres. For a Seifert fibered space Σ(a1,…,an) the knot Floer homology of the regular fiber can be computed via the lattice homology of the associated plumbing graph. The mapping cone formula then reduces to the lattice homology computation of Némethi, and the d‑invariant coincides with the lattice homology correction term. This agreement is a key piece of evidence for the isomorphism between Heegaard Floer homology and lattice homology for graph manifolds. The formula also implies that d(Y) is a concordance invariant of the knot K when Y is obtained by large surgery, leading to the definition of the τ and ε invariants.

When using the mapping cone formula in practice, one must be careful with the grading conventions. The absolute grading on CFK− is normalized so that the generator of the top Alexander grading has grading 0. The map 𝔸+ → 𝔸− has degree −1, and the mapping cone shifts the grading of 𝔸+ by +1. The resulting tower generator in HF−(Y) then has grading d(Y). A common mistake is to forget the +1 shift in the mapping cone, which would give d(Y) off by 1. Another pitfall is to confuse the rational surgery formula (which involves a sequence of mapping cones for each step of the continued fraction) with the integer surgery formula; the rational formula requires keeping track of the Spinc structures at each stage, and the final d‑invariant is a maximum over a set of intermediate values.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Contact Structures with Non‑Zero Giroux Torsion on Non‑Seifert Fibered Spaces

The contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF+(−Y,𝔰ξ) was introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó (2005) for a closed contact 3‑manifold (Y,ξ). It is defined by choosing an open book decomposition supporting ξ, constructing a Heegaard diagram from the open book, and taking the homology class of the top‑degree generator. The invariant vanishes for overtwisted contact structures (Honda–Kazez–Matić, 2009). For tight contact structures, c(ξ) is often non‑zero, but there are tight contact structures with vanishing invariant. A major result of Ghiggini (2010) and later of Honda–Kazeh–Matić (2013) states that if ξ has positive Giroux torsion, then c(ξ) = 0, even when Y is not a Seifert fibered space. Giroux torsion means that there is an embedded T2 × [0,1] in Y with a contact structure isomorphic to the standard Giroux torsion model. The proof uses the fact that a contact structure with Giroux torsion can be decomposed along a convex torus into two pieces, one of which is a Giroux torsion domain. The contact invariant is then shown to factor through the contact invariant of the torsion domain, which is known to be zero by a direct computation in the Heegaard diagram of T2 × [0,1] with the torsion contact structure.

On non‑Seifert fibered spaces, the vanishing of c(ξ) for positive Giroux torsion does not imply that all tight contact structures have vanishing invariant. For example, on the manifold obtained by 0‑surgery on the figure‑eight knot, there are tight contact structures without Giroux torsion whose invariant is non‑zero (Ghiggini–Van Horn‑Morris, 2011). The distinction is crucial for the classification of tight contact structures: the contact invariant provides a necessary condition for tightness (non‑vanishing) but not a sufficient one. The presence of Giroux torsion is a sufficient condition for vanishing, and the converse is false in general. The proof of vanishing for Giroux torsion uses the fact that the open book supporting the contact structure can be stabilized so that the monodromy contains a Dehn twist along a curve that bounds a subsurface with fractional Dehn twist coefficient > 1. The corresponding Heegaard diagram then contains a pair of curves that intersect in a pattern that forces the top generator to be a boundary.

The contact invariant also behaves naturally under connected sum: c(ξ1 # ξ2) = c(ξ1) ⊗ c(ξ2) in HF+(−(Y1 # Y2)). This follows from the Künneth formula for HF+ and the fact that the open book for the connected sum is the boundary connected sum of the open books. Consequently, if either factor has Giroux torsion, the invariant of the sum vanishes. This property is used to construct infinitely many tight contact structures with vanishing invariant on a fixed manifold by taking connected sums with a Giroux torsion domain.

In the bordered theory, the contact invariant is refined to an element in the type D module associated to the contact manifold with boundary. The vanishing of the invariant for Giroux torsion is reflected in the fact that the corresponding type D module is homotopically trivial. This perspective allows one to glue contact manifolds along convex boundaries and compute the invariant of the glued manifold via the pairing theorem. The result is that the contact invariant of a glued manifold is the pairing of the invariants of the pieces, and if one piece has Giroux torsion, the pairing vanishes. This gives a powerful tool for detecting Giroux torsion in decomposed contact manifolds.

A common misconception is that the contact invariant detects all tight contact structures. The existence of tight contact structures with vanishing invariant (e.g., on certain hyperbolic manifolds) shows that the invariant is not a complete invariant. However, for many classes — such as contact structures on Seifert fibered spaces with non‑positive Giroux torsion, or on manifolds obtained by Legendrian surgery on knots in S3 — the invariant is known to be non‑zero for all tight structures. The precise classification of when c(ξ) ≠ 0 remains an active area of research, and the interplay with the ε invariant of the binding knot in the open book is a current focus.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Behavior under Connected Sum for the Minus and Infinity Versions

The Künneth formula for Heegaard Floer homology states that for closed oriented 3‑manifolds Y1 and Y2 there is an isomorphism of relatively graded 𝔽[U]‑modules
HF−(Y1 # Y2, 𝔰1 # 𝔰2) ≅ HF−(Y1, 𝔰1) ⊗𝔽[U] HF−(Y2, 𝔰2).
The isomorphism is induced by the connected sum of Heegaard diagrams: the symmetric product of the connected sum diagram splits as a fiber product of the symmetric products of the summands, and the holomorphic disk counts factor as a product. The absolute grading on the tensor product is the sum of the absolute gradings of the factors, and the U‑action is diagonal. For the infinity version, the formula is similar but the modules are over 𝔽[U,U−1] and the grading shift is 0 because HF∞ is a free module of rank 1 over 𝔽[U,U−1] for each Spinc structure. The hat version also satisfies a Künneth formula, but the tensor product is over 𝔽 and the grading shift is −½.

The proof of the Künneth formula for HF− uses the cylindrical reformulation to stretch the neck along the connecting sphere of the connected sum. In the limit, the holomorphic disks in the symmetric product of the connected sum diagram break into pairs of disks in the symmetric products of the summands, with matching boundary conditions on the neck. The gluing theorem for holomorphic curves then gives a bijection between the moduli spaces, establishing that the differential on the connected sum complex is the tensor product of the differentials. The U‑equivariance follows because the U‑action corresponds to a point constraint that can be placed in either summand, giving the diagonal action on the tensor product.

An immediate corollary is the additivity of the d‑invariant under connected sum:
d(Y1 # Y2, 𝔰1 # 𝔰2) = d(Y1, 𝔰1) + d(Y2, 𝔰2).
This follows because the tower generator in HF−(Y1 # Y2) is the tensor product of the tower generators of the factors, and its grading is the sum of the gradings. The additivity of d is a key ingredient in the proof that the homology cobordism group maps to ℚ via the d‑invariant homomorphism. It also implies that the correction terms of a connected sum of lens spaces are the sums of the individual correction terms, which can be verified directly from the Dedekind sum formula.

The behavior of the contact invariant under connected sum is also governed by the Künneth formula. If (Y1,ξ1) and (Y2,ξ2) are contact manifolds, then the contact invariant of the connected sum contact structure ξ1 # ξ2 is the tensor product of the individual contact invariants in HF+(−(Y1 # Y2)). This is proved by taking open books for each contact structure, forming their boundary connected sum, and observing that the Heegaard diagram for the sum is the connected sum of the diagrams. The top generator of the sum diagram is the tensor product of the top generators, and its homology class is the tensor product of the classes. Consequently, if either c(ξ1) or c(ξ2) vanishes, then c(ξ1 # ξ2) = 0. This gives a method to produce tight contact structures with vanishing invariant by taking connected sums with a Giroux torsion domain.

For the plus and hat versions, the Künneth formula holds with a grading shift of −½ for the hat version because the absolute grading on HF̂ is normalized differently. The shift arises from the fact that the top generator of HF̂(S3) has grading 0, while the tower generator of HF−(S3) has grading 0 as well, but the map HF− → HF̂ lowers grading by ½. In practice, when computing the d‑invariant of a connected sum, one uses the minus version and then applies the shift if needed. A common trap is to apply the hat version Künneth formula without the −½ shift, leading to an incorrect d‑invariant by ½. Another trap is to assume that the isomorphism is canonical; it depends on the choice of the connecting sphere and the almost complex structure, but the induced map on homology is independent of these choices.

  • Confusing the grading shift in the surgery exact triangle: the map HF+ → HF− carries a shift of ½ that must be included when comparing d‑invariants.
  • Assuming the contact invariant is non‑zero for all tight contact structures; positive Giroux torsion forces vanishing even on non‑Seifert fibered spaces.
  • Forgetting the +1 grading shift in the mapping cone formula for integer surgeries, which leads to an off‑by‑one error in the d‑invariant.
  • Neglecting the −½ shift in the hat version Künneth formula when computing connected sums of d‑invariants.
  • Treating the surgery exact triangle as split exact for torsion Spinc structures; the connecting homomorphism can be non‑trivial and encodes the torsion linking form.
  • Using the rational surgery formula without tracking the Spinc structures at each continued fraction step, resulting in an incorrect maximum for the d‑invariant.
  • Believing that the cylindrical reformulation is only needed for bordered invariance; it is also essential for the closed stabilization invariance proof and for the grading shift analysis.

The Analytical Foundations: Moduli Spaces of Holomorphic Disks

The rigorous construction of Heegaard Floer Homology relies critically on the properties of moduli spaces of holomorphic disks. While the concept of holomorphic disks forming the differential is fundamental, the analytical machinery ensuring the well-definedness and invariance of the theory is complex. The differential ∂ in the Ozsváth-Szabó chain complex is defined by counting rigid (dimension zero) holomorphic disks in the symmetric product of the Heegaard surface. This count must be finite, well-defined up to sign, and invariant under various choices.

The moduli space M(x, y) for two intersection points x and y in the symmetric product consists of equivalence classes of holomorphic disks whose boundary maps to the Lagrangian submanifolds associated with the Heegaard diagram. For the count to be meaningful, several analytical challenges must be addressed:

  1. Compactness: The moduli spaces of holomorphic disks must be compact in a suitable sense. This is crucial for ensuring that the count is finite and that the boundary operator ∂² = 0. Gromov's compactness theorem for holomorphic curves, adapted to the setting of symmetric products and Lagrangian boundary conditions, provides this. It states that sequences of holomorphic disks with bounded energy converge, possibly after bubbling off spheres or disks. The absence of bubbling in the symmetric product setting for generic almost complex structures is key.
  2. Transversality: For the count to be well-defined and independent of small perturbations, the moduli spaces must be smooth manifolds, and their dimension must match the expected dimension (zero for the differential). This is achieved by choosing a generic almost complex structure J on the symmetric product. By Sard-Smale type theorems, such generic J exist for which the relevant Fredholm operators are surjective, ensuring transversality and that the moduli spaces are smooth manifolds of the expected dimension.
  3. Orientation: To count disks with signs, each rigid disk must be assigned a consistent orientation. This involves orienting the determinant line bundles of the linearized Fredholm operators. These orientations are typically chosen based on a coherent system of orientations for the tangent spaces of the Lagrangian submanifolds and the Spinc structure of the 3-manifold. The choice of orientation must be consistent across all moduli spaces to ensure ∂² = 0 and invariance.
  4. Gluing: When considering connected sums of 3-manifolds, or the surgery exact triangle, the construction involves "gluing" holomorphic disks. Analytically, this means showing that the moduli space of disks on a connected sum diagram can be approximated by products of moduli spaces on the component diagrams. This is a delicate analytical procedure that involves stretching the neck of the connecting region and analyzing the limiting behavior of holomorphic curves.

These analytical foundations ensure that Heegaard Floer Homology is a robust invariant, independent of the choice of Heegaard diagram (up to stabilization) and the almost complex structure. The technical details often involve advanced techniques from symplectic geometry and geometric analysis, making the full proof of invariance a significant undertaking.

Spinc Structures and the Absolute Grading in Heegaard Floer Homology

A fundamental aspect of Heegaard Floer Homology is its dependence on Spinc structures, which are essential for defining both the chain complex and its gradings. A Spinc structure on a 3-manifold Y is an equivalence class of trivializations of the determinant line bundle of the tangent bundle, or equivalently, a lifting of the frame bundle to a principal Spinc(3) bundle. For a given 3-manifold Y, the set of Spinc structures is an affine space over H¹(Y; ℤ), meaning it can be identified with H¹(Y; ℤ) once a base Spinc structure is chosen.

In the context of a Heegaard diagram (Σ, α, β), a Spinc structure 𝔰 on Y can be uniquely identified with a pair of homology classes (zα, zβ) in H¹(Σ; ℤ) that satisfy certain compatibility conditions, or more directly, with a choice of a path between the two Lagrangian tori Tα and Tβ in the symmetric product Symg(Σ). Each intersection point x ∈ Tα ∩ Tβ corresponds to a specific Spinc structure 𝔰x on Y. The Ozsváth-Szabó chain complex CF(Y, 𝔰) is then constructed as a direct sum of modules over 𝔽[U] (or 𝔽 for the hat version), where each summand corresponds to a specific Spinc structure.

The grading of the chain complex is crucial. It is a ℤ-grading, but it is defined in two stages:

  1. Relative Grading: For a fixed Spinc structure 𝔰, the relative grading between two generators x and y (intersection points in the symmetric product) is given by the Maslov index of a holomorphic disk connecting them. The Maslov index of a disk is an integer that measures the winding of its boundary around the Lagrangian submanifolds. Specifically, for a holomorphic disk φ in Symg(Σ) with boundary on Tα and Tβ, connecting x to y, the relative grading difference is given by μ(φ), which is an integer. This ensures that the differential ∂ is degree-preserving.
  2. Absolute Grading: To make the grading absolute, a reference point is needed. For a 3-manifold Y with a Spinc structure 𝔰, the absolute grading of a generator x corresponding to 𝔰 is defined using a combination of the Maslov index and the Chern class of the Spinc structure. A common formula for the absolute grading of a generator x is:
    gr(x) = (c₁(𝔰) ⋅ J(x)) / 2 + (g - 1) / 2
    where c₁(𝔰) is the first Chern class of the Spinc structure, J(x) is a homology class associated with x (often related to the Maslov index of a specific reference disk), and g is the genus of the Heegaard surface. This formula ensures that the grading is an integer (or half-integer for some versions) and is independent of the choice of Heegaard diagram and reference path. The specific normalization varies between the different versions of Heegaard Floer Homology (HF̂, HF⁻, HF⁺, HF∞). For example, HF̂ typically uses a half-integer grading, while HF⁻ uses an integer grading.

The U-action, which corresponds to "puncturing" a holomorphic disk with a point constraint, shifts the grading by -2. This U-equivariance is essential for the structure of HF⁻ and HF∞ modules over 𝔽[U, U⁻¹]. Understanding the precise definition and normalization of these gradings is critical for computing invariants like the d-invariant, which is defined as the minimal grading of a specific generator in HF⁻(Y, 𝔰).

Understanding Grading Shifts in HFH
Consider a 3-manifold Y with a Spinc structure 𝔰. Let x be a generator in the Heegaard Floer chain complex CF⁻(Y, 𝔰) with absolute grading gr(x). The U-action on CF⁻(Y, 𝔰) is defined such that U⋅x is also a generator (or a sum of generators). If gr(x) is the grading of x, what is the grading of U⋅x? How does this relate to the definition of the d-invariant?
The U-action in Heegaard Floer Homology corresponds to adding a point constraint to the holomorphic disks that define the differential. Analytically, this means considering disks that pass through a specified point in the symmetric product. Geometrically, this action shifts the Maslov index by -2. Therefore, if x is a generator with absolute grading gr(x), then the generator U⋅x has an absolute grading of gr(x) - 2.

This relationship is crucial for understanding the structure of the HF⁻(Y, 𝔰) module. HF⁻(Y, 𝔰) is a free 𝔽[U]-module, meaning it has a basis {xᵢ} such that every element can be written as Σ pᵢ(U)xᵢ, where pᵢ(U) are polynomials in U. The U-action ensures that if x is a generator, then U⋅x, U²⋅x, etc., are distinct elements in the chain complex, forming a "tower" of generators with decreasing gradings.

The d-invariant, d(Y, 𝔰), is defined as the minimal grading of the unique generator in the image of the map from HF⁺(Y, 𝔰) to HF⁻(Y, 𝔰) that generates the U-tower. More precisely, it is the minimal grading of an element in the image of the map from the "top" part of the complex to the "bottom" part. If this generator is denoted by xtower, then d(Y, 𝔰) = gr(xtower). Since U shifts grading by -2, the minimal grading of any element in the U-tower generated by xtower is precisely gr(xtower), as applying U further would only decrease the grading. Thus, the U-action directly informs the definition and computation of the d-invariant by establishing the "bottom" of the grading spectrum for a given Spinc structure.
Grading Consistency Check
When working with Heegaard Floer Homology problems involving different versions (HF̂, HF⁻, HF⁺, HF∞) or operations like connected sums and surgeries, always double-check the grading conventions and shifts. The most common errors arise from misapplying the ±½ shift between HF̂ and HF⁻, or forgetting the -2 shift for the U-action, or the +1 shift in the mapping cone formula for integer surgeries. A quick mental check: HF̂ has half-integer gradings, while HF⁻ and HF⁺ typically have integer gradings (after appropriate normalization). The U-action *always* decreases grading by 2.

Connecting Heegaard Floer Homology to Symplectic Field Theory

Heegaard Floer Homology (HFH) and Symplectic Field Theory (SFT) represent two powerful, yet distinct, approaches to constructing invariants of 3-manifolds and contact structures. While HFH is combinatorial and gauge-theoretic in spirit (via its isomorphism with Seiberg-Witten Floer Homology), SFT is inherently geometric, relying on holomorphic curves in symplectizations of contact manifolds. A deep connection exists, particularly between HF+ and a component of cylindrical contact homology (CCH).

Symplectic Field Theory, pioneered by Eliashberg, Givental, and Hofer, aims to define invariants for contact manifolds (Y, ξ) by counting holomorphic curves in the symplectization (ℝ × Y, d(etα)), where α is a contact form for ξ. The resulting algebra, called the contact homology algebra, captures information about the Reeb dynamics of the contact structure. Cylindrical Contact Homology (CCH) is a specific version of SFT that counts holomorphic cylinders and half-cylinders in the symplectization, yielding a chain complex whose homology is an invariant of the contact structure.

The key connection lies in the work of Hutchings, who established an isomorphism between the Heegaard Floer Homology HF+(Y, 𝔰) and a specific part of the cylindrical contact homology CCH(Y, ξ) for certain contact 3-manifolds (Y, ξ). This isomorphism is particularly strong for contact structures that admit a compatible open book decomposition. An open book decomposition of a 3-manifold Y consists of a surface Σ (the page) and a diffeomorphism φ: Σ → Σ (the monodromy), such that Y is obtained by gluing the boundary of Σ × [0,1] to Σ × {0} and Σ × {1} via φ, and then identifying the two ends. Every contact 3-manifold admits a compatible open book decomposition.

The isomorphism can be understood conceptually:

  1. Common Underlying Geometry: Both theories count holomorphic curves. HFH counts disks in the symmetric product of a Heegaard surface, while CCH counts cylinders and half-cylinders in a symplectization. The symmetric product of a surface can be viewed as a symplectic manifold, and the Heegaard diagram defines Lagrangian boundary conditions.
  2. Open Book Decomposition as a Bridge: For a contact manifold (Y, ξ) with an open book decomposition (Σ, φ), the Heegaard diagram for Y can be constructed directly from (Σ, φ). The contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF+(−Y, 𝔰ξ) is a specific element in HF+ that is non-zero if and only if ξ is tight (under certain conditions).
  3. Identification of Generators and Differentials: The generators of HF+ correspond to intersection points in the symmetric product, while generators of CCH correspond to Reeb orbits on the contact manifold. The differentials in both theories are defined by counting specific types of holomorphic curves. The isomorphism essentially shows that these counts are equivalent under the right correspondence.

This connection provides a powerful tool for studying contact structures. It allows researchers to use the combinatorial and algebraic tools of HFH to compute invariants that have deep geometric meaning in SFT, and vice-versa. For instance, the contact invariant c(ξ) in HF+ can be interpreted as a count of specific holomorphic curves in the symplectization. This cross-pollination of ideas has led to significant progress in low-dimensional topology, particularly in understanding the classification of tight contact structures and the properties of L-space contact structures.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Classification of Exotic 4-Manifolds

Exotic 4-manifolds are smooth 4-manifolds that are homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to a standard manifold, such as S⁴ or ℂP². The existence of such manifolds, first demonstrated by Donaldson in the 1980s, highlights the richness and complexity of smooth topology in dimension four. Heegaard Floer Homology provides powerful invariants that can distinguish these exotic smooth structures, complementing earlier gauge-theoretic invariants like Donaldson and Seiberg-Witten invariants.

The primary tool from HFH for studying 4-manifolds is the set of Ozsváth-Szabó 4-manifold invariants, denoted by SWF(X, 𝔰) or more generally by the cobordism maps on Heegaard Floer Homology. For a closed, oriented 4-manifold X with b₂⁺ > 0 (where b₂⁺ is the dimension of the maximal positive definite subspace of H₂(X; ℝ)), these invariants are elements in the HF⁺ of a 3-manifold obtained by surgery on X. More generally, for a 4-manifold X with boundary Y, HFH defines a module over an algebra associated with Y, which can be thought of as a "boundary invariant."

The ability of HFH to detect exotic structures stems from several key properties:

  1. Adjunction Inequality: The Ozsváth-Szabó adjunction inequality provides constraints on the genus of embedded surfaces in 4-manifolds. Specifically, for an embedded surface Σ in a 4-manifold X with a Spinc structure 𝔰, the inequality relates the genus of Σ, its self-intersection number, and the d-invariant of the 3-manifold obtained by surgery along Σ. This inequality can be used to show that certain surfaces cannot be smoothly embedded in a given 4-manifold, thus distinguishing smooth structures.
  2. d-invariants and Rational Blow-downs: Many constructions of exotic 4-manifolds involve rational blow-downs, which are specific types of surgeries that replace a configuration of spheres with a rational homology ball. The d-invariants of the resulting 3-manifolds (which form the boundary of the 4-manifold pieces) play a crucial role in determining whether the gluing yields an exotic manifold. The d-invariant is an invariant of rational homology spheres, and its behavior under connected sums and surgeries is well-understood.
  3. Cobordism Maps: For a 4-manifold X that is a cobordism between two 3-manifolds Y₁ and Y₂, HFH defines a map HF(Y₁) → HF(Y₂) (or its dual). These maps encode information about the 4-manifold. If two 4-manifolds are diffeomorphic, their associated cobordism maps must be the same. By demonstrating that the cobordism maps for two homeomorphic 4-manifolds are different, one can prove they are exotic.
  4. Relation to Seiberg-Witten Theory: The isomorphism between Heegaard Floer Homology and Seiberg-Witten Floer Homology means that HFH invariants can be used to re-interpret and compute Seiberg-Witten invariants. Since Seiberg-Witten invariants are known to distinguish exotic 4-manifolds (e.g., the K3 surface has infinitely many exotic smooth structures), HFH provides an alternative, often more computable, pathway to these distinctions.

For example, the existence of exotic ℝ⁴s (smooth structures on ℝ⁴ not diffeomorphic to the standard one) is a profound result. While HFH directly applies to closed 4-manifolds, its techniques, particularly via the d-invariant and its behavior under surgery, are instrumental in understanding the boundaries of these exotic pieces. The ability to compute these invariants using combinatorial Heegaard diagrams makes HFH a powerful and practical tool in the ongoing effort to classify smooth 4-manifolds, a problem far from being solved.

Common Traps

  • Confusing Moduli Space Compactness with Transversality: While both are crucial, compactness (Gromov compactness) ensures sequences of disks converge, preventing "loss of energy" at infinity, whereas transversality ensures the moduli spaces are smooth manifolds of the correct dimension, making the count well-defined and generic. Assuming one implies the other is a common mistake.
  • Misinterpreting Spinc Structure Uniqueness: A Spinc structure on a 3-manifold is *not* unique; its set is an affine space over H¹(Y; ℤ). However, for a given Heegaard diagram, each intersection point *uniquely* corresponds to a specific Spinc structure on the 3-manifold. Confusing these two aspects can lead to errors in setting up the chain complex.
  • Incorrect Absolute Grading Normalization: Different versions of HFH (HF̂, HF⁻, HF⁺, HF∞) have different normalizations for their absolute gradings. Neglecting the half-integer shift for HF̂ or the specific reference point for the d-invariant can lead to off-by-half or off-by-integer errors.
  • Assuming SFT-HFH Isomorphism is Universal: The isomorphism between HF+ and CCH is powerful but often holds under specific conditions, such as the existence of a compatible open book decomposition or for specific types of contact structures. Assuming it applies universally to all contact 3-manifolds without checking conditions is a trap.
  • Overlooking the Role of b₂⁺ for 4-Manifold Invariants: The Ozsváth-Szabó 4-manifold invariants are typically defined for 4-manifolds with b₂⁺ > 0. For 4-manifolds with b₂⁺ = 0 (e.g., definite manifolds), different techniques or interpretations are needed. Applying the b₂⁺ > 0 framework indiscriminately is a common error.
  • Ignoring the Smooth vs. Topological Distinction: Exotic 4-manifolds are homeomorphic but not *diffeomorphic*. HFH invariants distinguish smooth structures. Assuming that an HFH invariant differentiating two manifolds means they are not even homeomorphic is incorrect; it only means they are not smoothly equivalent.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Isomorphism with Embedded Contact Homology

The isomorphism between Heegaard Floer homology (HFH) and embedded contact homology (ECH) is one of the most profound structural results in low-dimensional topology. Proven independently by Kutluhan–Lee–Taubes and Colin–Ghiggini–Honda, this isomorphism establishes a canonical equivalence between the Heegaard Floer chain complex of a contact 3-manifold (Y, ξ) and the embedded contact homology chain complex defined by Hutchings. The ECH chain complex is generated by orbit sets of the Reeb vector field associated to a contact form for ξ, with differential counting holomorphic curves in the symplectization ℝ × Y. The isomorphism respects the contact invariant: the ECH contact class c(ξ) corresponds to the Ozsváth–Szabó contact class c(ξ) ∈ HF̂(−Y, 𝔰_ξ). Crucially, the isomorphism is not merely an isomorphism of homology groups but an isomorphism of filtered chain complexes, where the filtration on the ECH side is given by the symplectic action of orbit sets, and on the HFH side by the Alexander grading or the filtration induced by the contact structure. This filtered isomorphism implies that the ECH spectral sequence from the contact form to HFH collapses at the E² page, and it provides a symplectic-geometric interpretation of the Heegaard Floer differential. The proof uses the "folding" construction to relate holomorphic curves in the symmetric product Sym^g(Σ) to holomorphic curves in the symplectization, mediated by a family of almost complex structures that stretch the neck along the binding of an open book decomposition supporting ξ. A key technical ingredient is the "ECH index" inequality, which matches the Fredholm index of holomorphic curves in the symplectization with the Maslov index of holomorphic disks in the symmetric product. The isomorphism also extends to the "hat," "minus," "plus," and "infinity" versions of HFH, corresponding to different versions of ECH (e.g., ECH with a marked point, or ECH of the symplectization with a cylindrical end). This equivalence has far-reaching consequences: it transfers the computational power of HFH (grid diagrams, bordered theory) to ECH, and it brings the dynamical tools of ECH (Reeb dynamics, volume bounds) to HFH. For instance, the ECH proof of the Weinstein conjecture for stable Hamiltonian structures on 3-manifolds implies the non-vanishing of the contact invariant in HFH for such structures. The isomorphism also respects the cobordism maps: a symplectic cobordism between contact 3-manifolds induces a map on ECH that corresponds under the isomorphism to the Ozsváth–Szabó cobordism map on HFH. This compatibility is essential for applications to symplectic fillings and the classification of tight contact structures.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Fukaya Category of the Symmetric Product

The Heegaard Floer chain complex admits a natural interpretation as the morphism complex between two Lagrangian submanifolds in the Fukaya category of the symmetric product Sym^g(Σ). Given a Heegaard diagram (Σ, α, β, z), the tori 𝕋_α = α1 × ⋯ × α_g and 𝕋_β = β1 × ⋯ × β_g are Lagrangian submanifolds of Sym^g(Σ) (away from the diagonal). The intersection points 𝕋_α ∩ 𝕋_β correspond to generators of the Heegaard Floer chain complex CF̂(Σ, α, β, z). The differential counts holomorphic disks in Sym^g(Σ) with boundary on 𝕋_α ∪ 𝕋_β, which are precisely the J-holomorphic curves counted in the Fukaya category Fuk(Sym^g(Σ)). This perspective, developed by Perutz and others, embeds HFH into the broader framework of homological mirror symmetry and symplectic topology. The symmetric product Sym^g(Σ) is a Kähler manifold (for a suitable complex structure on Σ), and its Fukaya category is expected to be equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on the dual abelian variety (the Jacobian Jac(Σ)) via homological mirror symmetry. Under this correspondence, the Lagrangian tori 𝕋_α and 𝕋_β correspond to skyscraper sheaves or more general objects on Jac(Σ), and the Heegaard Floer homology HF̂(Y) computes the Ext groups between these objects. This categorical viewpoint explains the functoriality of HFH under cobordisms: a 4-dimensional 2-handle attachment corresponds to a Lagrangian correspondence (a Lagrangian submanifold in the product of symmetric products), and the induced map on HFH is the composition with the associated Fourier–Mukai kernel. The Fukaya category perspective also clarifies the role of the basepoint z: it determines a divisor D_z = {z} × Symg-1(Σ) in Sym^g(Σ), and the "hat" version HF̂ corresponds to the wrapped Fukaya category of the complement Sym^g(Σ) D_z, while the "minus" and "plus" versions correspond to different completions of the Fukaya category relative to this divisor. The U-action is realized by intersection with the divisor D_z. This framework has been made rigorous by Lipshitz, who constructed a curved A_∞-category associated to a Heegaard diagram, and by Seidel and Smith, who related the symplectic Khovanov homology to the Fukaya category of symmetric products. The categorical approach also provides a natural setting for the bordered theory: a bordered 3-manifold with boundary a surface F gives a module over the algebra associated to F, which is the A_∞-algebra of the Lagrangian 𝕋_α in Sym^g(F). The pairing theorem then becomes a statement about the derived tensor product of modules over this A_∞-algebra.

Worked Example: Contact Invariant under the ECH-HFH Isomorphism
Let (Y, ξ) be a contact 3-manifold with an open book decomposition (Σ, φ) supporting ξ. The contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF̂(−Y, 𝔰_ξ) is represented by the homology class of the intersection point x0 = α ∩ β corresponding to the identity element in the mapping class group factorization of φ. Under the ECH-HFH isomorphism, describe the corresponding ECH generator and explain why it represents the ECH contact class c(ξ).
The open book decomposition (Σ, φ) gives a Heegaard diagram for −Y by taking two copies of Σ and gluing them via φ. The binding of the open book corresponds to the basepoint z. The contact invariant c(ξ) in HF̂(−Y) is the class of the generator x0 that lies in the "top" grading and is the unique intersection point of the Lagrangians 𝕋_α and 𝕋_β that is disjoint from the divisor D_z in a suitable sense. Under the ECH-HFH isomorphism, the ECH chain complex is generated by orbit sets of the Reeb vector field on Y associated to a contact form adapted to the open book. The Reeb dynamics near the binding consist of a single elliptic orbit for each binding component, and the pages of the open book give a family of holomorphic curves in the symplectization. The ECH contact class c(ξ) is represented by the empty orbit set (or the orbit set consisting of the binding orbits with multiplicity one, depending on conventions). The isomorphism maps the Heegaard Floer generator x0 to this empty orbit set because both are characterized by being the unique generator in the highest grading that survives in the homology of the contact invariant. The non-vanishing of c(ξ) in HF̂ corresponds to the non-vanishing of the ECH contact class, which is equivalent to the existence of a tight contact structure. The filtered isomorphism ensures that the action filtration on the ECH side (which is zero for the empty orbit set) matches the Alexander grading filtration on the HFH side (which is zero for x0 in the appropriate normalization).
Exam Strategy: Recognizing the ECH-HFH Isomorphism in Questions
When a question mentions both Reeb orbits and Heegaard diagrams, or asks about the contact invariant in the context of symplectizations, the ECH-HFH isomorphism is the underlying framework. Key phrases: "orbit set," "symplectic action," "ECH index," "symplectization," "open book decomposition," "Reeb vector field." Remember that the isomorphism is filtered and respects the contact invariant. The ECH differential counts holomorphic curves in ℝ × Y with ECH index 1; the HFH differential counts holomorphic disks in Sym^g(Σ) with Maslov index 1. The isomorphism matches these counts via the neck-stretching argument. For computational questions, the ECH side is often more accessible for contact structures with simple Reeb dynamics (e.g., prequantization bundles), while the HFH side is more computable via grid diagrams or bordered theory. The isomorphism transfers computability both ways.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant in the Full Minus and Plus Complexes

The Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) is originally defined in the "hat" version HF̂(−Y, 𝔰_ξ). However, its behavior in the "minus" HF⁻, "plus" HF⁺, and "infinity" HF^∞ versions carries additional information about the contact structure ξ. The natural maps HF̂ → HF⁺ and HF⁻ → HF̂ (and the long exact sequence relating them) allow one to track the image of c(ξ). A fundamental result of Honda–Kazez–Matić and independently of Ghiggini–Honda–Van Horn-Morris states that for a tight contact structure ξ, the contact invariant c(ξ) is non-zero in HF̂ and its image in HF⁺ is also non-zero. Moreover, the element U^k ⋅ c(ξ) in HF⁻ (for appropriate k) detects the Giroux torsion of ξ. Specifically, if ξ has Giroux torsion, then c(ξ) = 0 in HF̂ (a theorem of Gay–Ghiggini–Honda–Van Horn-Morris), but the "minus" version can still carry information: the contact invariant in HF⁻ (defined as a class in the homology of the mapping cone of the U-action) can be non-zero even when the hat version vanishes. The "plus" version HF⁺ is related to the contact invariant in sutured Floer homology via the isomorphism HF⁺(−Y) ≅ SFH(−M, γ) for a suitable sutured manifold (M, γ) obtained by removing a neighborhood of a Legendrian knot. The contact invariant in HF^∞ is always non-zero for any contact structure (since HF^∞ ≅ ℤ[U, U-1] for rational homology spheres), but its image under the map HF^∞ → HF⁺ recovers the HF⁺ contact invariant. The grading of the contact invariant in HF⁻ and HF⁺ is shifted relative to HF̂ by the formula gr(c(ξ)) = (c1(𝔰_ξ)2 − 2χ(Σ) − 3σ(X))/4 for a symplectic filling X of (Y, ξ), where Σ is a Seifert surface. This grading shift is crucial for the adjunction inequality applications. The behavior of the contact invariant under the U-action also distinguishes between different tight contact structures on the same 3-manifold: two tight contact structures ξ1, ξ2 with the same Spinc structure may have c(ξ1) = c(ξ2) in HF̂ but differ in HF⁻ by a power of U. This phenomenon is related to the "contact invariant in the full theory" defined by Colin–Ghiggini–Honda using ECH, which takes values in the completed ECH chain complex and captures the full Reeb dynamics.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Graded Roots Model for Plumbed 3-Manifolds

For 3-manifolds obtained by plumbing according to a negative-definite graph (plumbed 3-manifolds), the Heegaard Floer homology can be computed combinatorially via the "graded roots" model developed by Némethi and Ozsváth–Szabó. A graded root is a tree (V, E) with a grading function gr: V → ℤ satisfying certain local conditions: each vertex has degree at most 3, and along each edge the grading changes by exactly 1. The graded root encodes the lattice homology of the plumbed 3-manifold, which is isomorphic to the Heegaard Floer homology HF⁻ (and HF̂, HF⁺) by a theorem of Ozsváth–Szabó and independently of Némethi. The vertices of the graded root correspond to characteristic vectors K ∈ Char(Γ) of the plumbing graph Γ, modulo the action of H2(Y; ℤ). The grading of a vertex is given by the formula gr(K) = (K2 + |V|)/4, where K2 is the self-intersection in the lattice. The edges correspond to the "adjacent" characteristic vectors differing by twice a vertex class. The U-action on HF⁻ corresponds to moving "down" the graded root (decreasing the grading by 2). The graded root model provides an extremely efficient algorithm for computing the d-invariant: d(Y, 𝔰) = −2 max{gr(v) | v ∈ V, v is a "leaf" in the appropriate sense}. For a rational homology sphere, the graded root is a finite tree with a unique "top" vertex of maximal grading, and the d-invariant is simply minus twice the grading of this top vertex. The graded root also computes the HF̂ groups: the rank of HF̂(Y, 𝔰) equals the number of vertices in the graded root with grading equal to the maximal grading minus the number of "downward" edges from those vertices. The model extends to plumbed 3-manifolds with b1 > 0 by considering the "graded root with a cycle" or by using the Novikov ring version. The graded root is also closely related to the "spectral sequence" from the lattice chain complex to the Heegaard Floer complex, and it provides a geometric interpretation of the correction terms in terms of the topology of the plumbing graph. For example, for a Seifert fibered space with base S2 and three exceptional fibers, the plumbing graph is a star-shaped graph, and the graded root can be drawn explicitly, yielding closed formulas for the d-invariants in terms of the Seifert invariants. The graded root model has been generalized to "almost rational" plumbings and to the "involutive" Heegaard Floer homology, where the involution ι acts on the graded root by a reflection symmetry.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Surgery Formula for the Contact Invariant

The behavior of the contact invariant under Dehn surgery is governed by a surgery exact triangle that relates the contact invariants of the surgered manifolds. Let (Y, ξ) be a contact 3-manifold and K ⊂ Y a Legendrian knot. Performing contact (+1)-surgery (Legendrian surgery) on K yields a new contact manifold (Y', ξ'). The contact invariant c(ξ') ∈ HF̂(−Y') is related to c(ξ) ∈ HF̂(−Y) via the contact surgery exact triangle of Honda–Kazez–Matić. This triangle involves the knot Floer homology HFK̂(Y, K) and the map induced by the Legendrian surgery. Specifically, there is an exact triangle: ⋯ → HF̂(−Y) → HF̂(−Y0) → HF̂(−Y1) → ⋯ where Y0 is the 0-surgery and Y1 is the +1-surgery (Legendrian surgery). The contact invariant c(ξ) maps to c(ξ0) in HF̂(−Y0) and to c(ξ1) in HF̂(−Y1). The map HF̂(−Y) → HF̂(−Y1) is induced by the 2-handle attachment corresponding to the surgery, and it sends c(ξ) to c(ξ1). A key result is that if K is a Legendrian knot with maximal Thurston–Bennequin number, then the contact invariant c(ξ1) is non-zero if and only if c(ξ) is non-zero and the knot Floer homology class [K] ∈ HFK̂(Y, K) (the "contact class" of the Legendrian knot) satisfies a certain non-vanishing condition. For transverse knots, the contact invariant of the contact structure obtained by transverse surgery is related to the transverse invariant ψ(K) ∈ HFK̂(Y, K) defined by Ozsváth–Szabó–Thurston. The surgery formula for the contact invariant in the "minus" version HF⁻ involves the mapping cone formula of Ozsváth–Szabó for integer surgeries, where the contact invariant corresponds to a specific element in the mapping cone complex. In the bordered theory, the contact invariant of a contact manifold with boundary is an element of the type D module associated to the boundary, and the pairing theorem computes the contact invariant of the closed manifold obtained by gluing. This bordered perspective is essential for understanding the contact invariant under cutting and pasting operations, such as the Giroux correspondence between contact structures and open book decompositions. The surgery formula also implies constraints on the existence of tight contact structures: if a contact manifold (Y, ξ) has c(ξ) = 0, then any contact manifold obtained by Legendrian surgery on a Legendrian knot in (Y, ξ) also has vanishing contact invariant. This is used to prove that certain 3-manifolds admit no tight contact structures (e.g., some Seifert fibered spaces with negative Euler number).

  • Confusing the ECH-HFH isomorphism with the Seiberg-Witten isomorphism: The ECH-HFH isomorphism is a symplectic/contact geometry equivalence (Taubes, Kutluhan-Lee-Taubes, Colin-Ghiggini-Honda), while the Seiberg-Witten isomorphism is a gauge-theoretic equivalence (Kutluhan-Lee-Taubes, Colin-Ghiggini-Honda, Taubes). They are distinct theorems, though both involve Taubes. The ECH isomorphism is filtered and respects the contact invariant directly; the SW isomorphism relates HFH to Seiberg-Witten Floer homology.
  • Assuming the Fukaya category of Sym^g(Σ) is formal or easy to compute: The Fukaya category of the symmetric product is highly non-trivial. While Sym^g(Σ) is Kähler, its Fukaya category is not formal in general. The Lagrangians 𝕋_α and 𝕋_β are not necessarily exact, and the A_∞-structure is essential. The categorical viewpoint is a powerful conceptual framework, but explicit computations usually revert to the combinatorial Heegaard Floer chain complex.
  • Thinking the contact invariant in HF⁻ is just the image of the hat version: The contact invariant in HF⁻ is not simply the image of c(ξ) ∈ HF̂ under the map HF̂ → HF⁻ (which is often zero). It is defined as a class in the mapping cone of the U-action, or equivalently as an element of the homology of the full CF⁻ complex that projects to c(ξ) in HF̂. For overtwisted contact structures, c(ξ) = 0 in HF̂ but the HF⁻ contact invariant can be non-zero and detect Giroux torsion.
  • Misapplying the graded root model to non-plumbed manifolds: The graded root model is specifically for 3-manifolds that are boundaries of negative-definite plumbings (or more generally, for manifolds with a negative-definite plumbing graph). It does not apply to arbitrary 3-manifolds. For Seifert fibered spaces with base S2 and at most three exceptional fibers, the plumbing graph is a star, and the model applies. For graph manifolds with more complicated JSJ decompositions, one must use the bordered theory or the mapping cone formula.
  • Overlooking the role of the Thurston-Bennequin number in the contact surgery formula: The surgery formula for the contact invariant under Legendrian surgery depends critically on the Thurston-Bennequin number tb(K). The contact (+1)-surgery is Legendrian surgery with framing tb(K) − 1. If the knot is not Legendrian, or if the framing is not tb(K) − 1, the resulting contact structure is not well-defined, and the contact invariant surgery triangle does not apply. The transverse surgery formula uses the self-linking number sl(K) instead.
  • Confusing the grading shift for the contact invariant in HF⁺ vs HF̂: The absolute grading of the contact invariant differs between versions. In HF̂, gr(c(ξ)) = (c1(𝔰_ξ)2 − 2χ(Σ) − 3σ(X))/4 for a symplectic filling X. In HF⁺, the grading is shifted by the U-action. The map HF̂ → HF⁺ lowers grading by 1/2 (or raises, depending on conventions). Forgetting this shift leads to incorrect adjunction inequality applications.
  • Assuming the bordered contact invariant is always computable from the open book: While the bordered theory provides a framework for the contact invariant of a contact manifold with boundary, explicit computation of the type D module for a given open book decomposition is highly non-trivial. The "nice" Heegaard diagrams for open books (due to Baldwin–Vela-Vick) allow computation in some cases, but the general algorithm is not as straightforward as the hat version for closed manifolds.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Ozsváth–Szabó 4‑Manifold Invariants for Manifolds with b₂⁺ > 1

The Ozsváth–Szabó mixed invariants ΦX,𝔰 for a smooth, closed, oriented 4‑manifold X with b₂⁺(X) > 1 are defined by counting pseudo‑holomorphic triangles in the symmetric product Sym^g(Σ) associated to a Heegaard splitting of a 3‑manifold Y that bounds X. When b₂⁺ > 1 the invariant is independent of the choice of metric and perturbation, and it takes values in the Heegaard Floer homology group HF⁺(−Y, 𝔰|_Y). The construction uses a stretched neck along Y and a generic almost complex structure on the symplectization ℝ × Y. The resulting map is a homomorphism ΦX,𝔰 : HF⁺(−Y, 𝔰|_Y) → ℤ[U] (or ℤ[U, U⁻¹] for the full theory) that satisfies a blow‑up formula: if X̃ = X # ℂℙ² with the exceptional sphere E, then ΦX̃, 𝔰 ± PD[E] = ΦX, 𝔰. This formula is proved by analyzing the degeneration of the triangle counts when the neck length goes to infinity and identifying the contribution of the exceptional curve with the U‑action on HF⁺. The mixed invariants vanish for manifolds that admit a metric of positive scalar curvature, and they give obstructions to the existence of symplectic structures via the adjunction inequality: for any smoothly embedded surface Σ ⊂ X of genus g with Σ·Σ ≥ 0, one has 2g − 2 ≥ |⟨c₁(𝔰), [Σ]⟩| + Σ·Σ. The proof uses the fact that the contact invariant of the boundary of a tubular neighborhood of Σ is non‑zero in HF⁺, and the mixed invariant evaluates on it to give the inequality. These invariants also distinguish exotic smooth structures on certain 4‑manifolds, for example on Dolgachev surfaces E(1)p,q where the mixed invariant detects the exotic ℝ⁴’s constructed by Akbulut–Matveyev.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Classification of L‑Space Knots via the Alexander Polynomial and the Surgery Formula

A knot K ⊂ S³ is an L‑space knot if some positive Dehn surgery on K yields an L‑space, i.e. a 3‑manifold Y with rank HF̂(Y) = |H₁(Y)|. Ozsváth and Szabó proved that for an L‑space knot the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t) has the form ∑i=−gg (−1)^i ta_i where the exponents a_i are strictly decreasing and the coefficients are ±1. Moreover, the knot Floer homology HFK̂(K) is determined entirely by Δ_K(t): it is supported in a single δ‑grading and its ranks in each Alexander grading are given by the absolute values of the coefficients of Δ_K(t). The surgery exact triangle together with the large surgery formula shows that the Heegaard Floer homology of the surgered manifold S³p/q(K) can be computed from the knot Floer complex CFK∞(K). For L‑space knots the mapping cone complex collapses to a staircase complex, and the d‑invariants of the surgered manifolds are given by a simple formula involving the Alexander polynomial. This leads to a classification: a knot in S³ is an L‑space knot if and only if its knot Floer homology is a staircase complex, which is equivalent to the condition that the Alexander polynomial has the alternating form above and the knot is fibered with monodromy a product of positive Dehn twists. The Berge conjecture, which predicts that the only knots admitting a lens space surgery are the Berge knots, is known to hold for L‑space knots: any L‑space knot with a lens space surgery must be a Berge knot, and its Alexander polynomial is a product of cyclotomic polynomials. The classification also implies that the genus of an L‑space knot equals the degree of its Alexander polynomial, and the τ invariant equals the genus.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Behavior under Dehn Surgery on Knots in L‑Spaces

Let Y be an L‑space and K ⊂ Y a null‑homologous knot. The surgery exact triangle for Dehn surgery on K in Y relates the Heegaard Floer homologies of Y, Y₀(K) (0‑surgery), and Yp/q(K) for any slope p/q. When Y is an L‑space, HF⁺(Y) is a free ℤ[U]‑module of rank |H₁(Y)|, and the maps in the triangle are induced by the cobordisms given by 2‑handle attachments. Ozsváth and Szabó’s mapping cone formula expresses CF⁺(Yp/q(K)) as the mapping cone of a chain map Dp/q : A⁺0 → B⁺, where A⁺0 is a direct sum of copies of CF⁺(Y) indexed by the Alexander grading, and B⁺ is a quotient of the knot Floer complex CFK∞(K). For L‑spaces the complex A⁺0 is particularly simple because CF⁺(Y) has no torsion. The large surgery formula shows that for sufficiently large p the Heegaard Floer homology of Yp/q(K) is determined by the knot Floer homology of K and the d‑invariants of Y. In particular, if K is an L‑space knot in an L‑space Y, then Yp/q(K) is again an L‑space for all sufficiently large p. The correction terms d(Yp/q(K), 𝔰) can be computed from the graded root associated to the knot Floer complex; this root is a tree whose vertices correspond to the Alexander gradings and whose edges encode the U‑action. The behavior under negative surgeries is more subtle: the surgery exact triangle implies that if Y−1(K) is an L‑space, then the knot Floer homology of K must be a staircase complex, and K is an L‑space knot in Y. This gives a characterization of L‑space knots in arbitrary L‑spaces analogous to the S³ case.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization for the Bordered Theory with Corners

The bordered Heegaard Floer theory assigns to a 3‑manifold with parametrized boundary a type D module over the torus algebra 𝒜(∂Y). When the boundary has corners (i.e., the parametrization includes a set of marked points on the boundary circles), the algebra is enlarged to the cornered algebra 𝒜^c, and the invariant becomes a type DD bimodule. Stabilization of a bordered Heegaard diagram means adding a 1‑handle and a 2‑handle in a canceling pair, which corresponds to increasing the genus of the Heegaard surface by one. Lipshitz, Ozsváth, and Thurston proved that the bordered invariant is invariant under stabilization: if (Σ, α, β, z) and (Σ', α', β', z') are two bordered Heegaard diagrams for the same 3‑manifold with corners, related by a sequence of stabilizations, handleslides, and isotopies, then the associated type DD bimodules are homotopy equivalent over 𝒜^c. The proof uses the cylindrical reformulation of the differential, where the moduli spaces of holomorphic curves are counted in the symplectization ℝ × (Σ {z}). Stabilization introduces a new pair of curves αg+1, βg+1 that intersect in a single point; the holomorphic disks contributing to the differential either avoid the new curves or pass through the unique intersection point. The latter contribute a factor of the identity bimodule for the cornered algebra, which is homotopically trivial. The invariance under handleslide is proved by constructing a chain homotopy equivalence using the triangle counts in the symmetric product Symg+1(Σ'). This invariance is essential for the pairing theorem: if Y₁ and Y₂ are bordered manifolds with corners whose boundaries are identified by a diffeomorphism, the Heegaard Floer homology of the closed manifold Y₁ ∪ Y₂ is computed by the box tensor product of the corresponding type DD and type AA modules. The stabilization invariance guarantees that the result does not depend on the choice of bordered diagrams.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Contact Structures with Positive Giroux Torsion on Graph Manifolds

Giroux torsion is a measure of the failure of a contact structure to be tight in a neighborhood of a torus. A contact 3‑manifold (Y, ξ) has positive Giroux torsion if it contains a contact embedding of T² × [0,1] with the standard contact structure ker(cos(2πt) dx + sin(2πt) dy) for t ∈ [0,1]. For graph manifolds (3‑manifolds whose JSJ decomposition consists of Seifert fibered pieces), the presence of positive Giroux torsion can be detected by the contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁺(−Y). Honda, Kazez, and Matić showed that if ξ has positive Giroux torsion, then c(ξ) = 0 in HF̂(−Y), but the invariant in HF⁺(−Y) may be non‑zero and carries information about the amount of torsion. In the bordered theory, a contact structure on a manifold with torus boundary gives a type D module over the torus algebra, and the contact invariant is an element of the homology of that module. For a graph manifold with a JSJ decomposition along tori, the contact invariant of the whole manifold is computed by pairing the bordered invariants of the pieces along the JSJ tori. If a piece has positive Giroux torsion, its bordered contact invariant lies in the kernel of the map induced by the inclusion of the boundary torus, which forces the total contact invariant to vanish in HF̂ but can produce a non‑zero class in HF⁺ after taking the mapping cone of the U‑action. This phenomenon was used by Ghiggini, Honda, and Van Horn‑Morris to classify tight contact structures on certain graph manifolds: they showed that the contact invariant distinguishes the finitely many tight contact structures up to isotopy, and that the vanishing of c(ξ) in HF̂ exactly characterizes those with positive Giroux torsion. The computation relies on the fact that the bordered invariants of Seifert fibered pieces with convex boundary can be expressed in terms of the lattice homology of the associated plumbing graph, and the pairing reduces to a combinatorial calculation on the graded root.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Contact Structures with Negative Giroux Torsion

Negative Giroux torsion is a condition on a contact 3‑manifold (Y, ξ) that is dual to the more familiar positive Giroux torsion. A contact structure has negative Giroux torsion if it contains a contact embedding of T² × [0,1] with the standard contact form ker(cos(2πt) dx − sin(2πt) dy) for t ∈ [0,1]. The sign reversal changes the monodromy of the characteristic foliation on the torus slices and leads to distinct behavior of the Ozsváth–Szabó contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁺(−Y). Honda, Kazez, and Matić proved that if ξ has negative Giroux torsion then c(ξ) = 0 in HF̂(−Y), but the invariant in HF⁺(−Y) can be non‑zero and often detects the order of torsion. In the bordered theory, a contact structure with torus boundary gives a type D module over the torus algebra; the contact invariant is a cycle in that module. For a manifold with negative Giroux torsion, the bordered invariant lies in the image of the U‑action, which forces vanishing in the hat version but yields a non‑trivial class in the plus version after taking the mapping cone of U. This phenomenon was exploited by Ghiggini and Van Horn‑Morris to distinguish tight contact structures on certain Seifert fibered spaces that admit both positive and negative Giroux torsion. The computation reduces to a graded root associated to the plumbing graph of the Seifert fibration; the negative torsion contributes a shift in the grading of the root that is visible in the d‑invariant of the contact invariant. A key technical point is that the cylindrical reformulation of the bordered differential is required to see the U‑equivariance of the contact class; without it the stabilization argument fails because the new α and β curves intersect the basepoint. The upshot is that negative Giroux torsion is not merely the absence of positive torsion — it carries its own Floer‑theoretic signature that can be read off from the HF⁺ contact invariant.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Surgery Formula for Knots in S³ with Non‑Integral Surgeries

The Ozsváth–Szabó rational surgery formula expresses the Heegaard Floer homology of a rational homology sphere obtained by p/q surgery on a knot K ⊂ S³ in terms of the knot Floer complex CFK∞(K). The formula constructs a mapping cone whose vertices are the spinc structures on the surgered manifold, indexed by integers i modulo p. The horizontal differential is the knot differential ∂, while the vertical differential is a combination of the U‑action and the Alexander grading shift determined by the continued fraction expansion of p/q. When q = 1 the formula reduces to the integer surgery exact triangle; for general q the mapping cone has |p| columns and the differential counts holomorphic triangles in the symmetric product Symg(Σ) with boundary on the α, β, and γ curves coming from the Heegaard diagram of the surgery. The rational surgery formula is compatible with the large surgery formula: for |p/q| > 2g(K)−1 the mapping cone collapses to a single column and the homology is determined by the τ invariant. For small surgeries the full mapping cone must be computed, and the result can be used to detect L‑space surgeries. A knot K is an L‑space knot if and only if its knot Floer homology is “thin” (supported in a single diagonal) and the rational surgery formula yields an L‑space for all sufficiently large positive surgeries. The formula also gives a practical algorithm for computing HF⁺ of rational homology spheres: one builds the filtered chain complex CFK∞(K) from a grid diagram, then applies the mapping cone construction with the appropriate p/q weights. The resulting complex is a finite‑dimensional ℤ[U]‑module whose homology can be computed by standard linear algebra over ℤ/2. This algorithm has been implemented in the software package HFKcalc and used to verify the L‑space conjecture for many families of knots.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Behavior under Connected Sum for the Minus Version

The Künneth formula for Heegaard Floer homology states that for closed oriented 3‑manifolds Y₁, Y₂ there is a quasi‑isomorphism CF⁻(Y₁ # Y₂) ≃ CF⁻(Y₁) ⊗ℤ[U] CF⁻(Y₂) that respects the U‑action and the absolute ℤ‑grading on the minus version. The tensor product is taken over the polynomial ring ℤ[U] where U acts diagonally. The grading shift is given by gr(x ⊗ y) = gr(x) + gr(y) + 1; the extra +1 comes from the connected sum neck contributing a single intersection point in the symmetric product. This formula was proved by Ozsváth and Szabó using a Heegaard diagram for the connected sum obtained by joining the diagrams of Y₁ and Y₂ along a small disk and analyzing the holomorphic disks that cross the neck. The key observation is that any holomorphic disk in the connected sum diagram either stays entirely in one summand or passes through the neck exactly once; the latter contribute the tensor product differential. The formula implies that the d‑invariant (the correction term) is additive under connected sum: d(Y₁ # Y₂, s₁ # s₂) = d(Y₁, s₁) + d(Y₂, s₂). For the hat version the Künneth formula holds with a grading shift of +1/2 and requires a correction term coming from the U‑torsion submodule. The minus version is the cleanest because CF⁻ is a free ℤ[U]‑module, so the tensor product is homologically well‑behaved. This additivity is crucial for computing the d‑invariants of connected sums of lens spaces and for proving that the d‑invariant defines a homomorphism from the homology cobordism group to ℚ.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Handleslide for the Bordered Theory with Corners

In the bordered theory with corners, a 3‑manifold with boundary decomposed into a union of surfaces with corners is represented by a Heegaard diagram with several α and β curves meeting at corner points. A handleslide of an α curve over another α curve in the presence of corners must be accompanied by a corresponding slide of the corner arcs to preserve the algebraic structure of the type DD, type AA, and type DA modules. The invariance proof constructs a chain homotopy equivalence between the modules before and after the slide by counting holomorphic triangles in the symmetric product Symg(Σ) with boundary on the three sets of curves α, α', β. The corners introduce additional boundary conditions: the triangles must have vertices at the corner points, and the counts are weighted by the algebra elements associated to the Reeb chords at the corners. The resulting chain map is a bimodule homomorphism over the cornered algebra 𝒜(Z). One shows that this map is a homotopy equivalence by constructing an inverse using the reverse handleslide and verifying the triangle identities up to homotopy. The cylindrical reformulation is essential here because the standard symmetric product argument fails when the new α curve intersects the basepoint; the cylindrical model moves the basepoint to infinity and replaces the symmetric product with a Hilbert manifold of holomorphic curves in the symplectization. This invariance under handleslide, together with invariance under stabilization (which adds a canceling pair of α, β curves intersecting once), guarantees that the bordered invariants with corners are well‑defined up to homotopy equivalence of the associated modules. The pairing theorem for manifolds with corners then follows: if Y₁ and Y₂ are bordered manifolds with corners whose boundaries are identified by a diffeomorphism respecting the corner structure, the Heegaard Floer homology of the closed manifold Y₁ ∪ Y₂ is computed by the box tensor product of the corresponding type DD and type AA modules.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the τ Invariant for Torus Knots

The Ozsváth–Szabó τ invariant of a knot K ⊂ S³ is defined as the minimal grading of a homogeneous element in the knot Floer homology HFK⁻(K) that maps to a non‑zero class in HF⁻(S³) under the inclusion induced by the zero surgery. For a torus knot Tp,q with p, q > 0 coprime, the knot Floer homology is supported in a single diagonal and its ranks are given by the coefficients of the Alexander polynomial. The τ invariant has the closed form τ(Tp,q) = (p−1)(q−1)/2. This formula can be derived by analyzing the filtered chain complex CFK∞(Tp,q) obtained from a standard genus‑one Heegaard diagram on the torus. The diagram has p α curves and q β curves intersecting in pq points. The holomorphic disks contributing to the differential are in bijection with the lattice points in the rectangle [0,p] × [0,q]. The Alexander grading of a generator corresponding to a lattice point (i,j) is A = i q + j p − pq/2, and the Maslov grading is M = 2(i q + j p) − pq + 1. The τ invariant is the maximum over all generators of the quantity −A for those generators that survive in the homology of the associated graded complex. A direct combinatorial count shows that the maximum is attained at the lattice point closest to the line i/p = j/q, yielding the formula above. The τ invariant for torus knots satisfies τ(Tp,q) = g(Tp,q), where g = (p−1)(q−1)/2 is the Seifert genus, confirming that torus knots are fibered and have maximal τ. This computation also feeds into the large surgery formula: for n ≥ 2g−1, the Heegaard Floer homology of +n surgery on Tp,q is determined entirely by τ and the Alexander polynomial. The same method extends to iterated torus knots (cables of torus knots) using the cable formula for knot Floer homology, giving a recursive algorithm for τ of any cable of a torus knot.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Tight Contact Structures on Circle Bundles over Surfaces

The contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF̂(−Y) introduced by Ozsváth and Szabó assigns to a co‑oriented contact structure ξ on a closed oriented 3‑manifold Y a distinguished homology class. For circle bundles over a closed surface Σg of genus g ≥ 1, the classification of tight contact structures is known: up to isotopy there are exactly 22g tight structures when the Euler number e satisfies |e| ≤ 2g−2, and a unique tight structure when |e| > 2g−2 (Honda–Kazez–Matić). Heegaard Floer homology detects this classification completely. Using a Heegaard diagram adapted to the open book decomposition induced by the fibration Σg → Y → S1, one can compute HF̂(−Y) as a relatively graded ℤ/2‑module of rank 22g in the relevant Spinc structures. The contact invariant of each tight structure corresponds to a distinct generator in this module, and the vanishing of c(ξ) characterises over‑twisted structures. The proof proceeds by constructing a family of Heegaard diagrams obtained by stabilising a genus‑g diagram for the surface bundle; the holomorphic disk counts are then identified with the combinatorics of dividing curves on the pages. A key technical point is that the differential in the hat complex vanishes for these diagrams, so the homology is the chain complex itself. Consequently the map from the set of tight contact structures to HF̂(−Y) is injective, and the Ozsváth–Szabó invariant recovers the full classification. This result also implies that the contact invariant is natural under contactomorphisms that preserve the fibration, and it provides a computable invariant for distinguishing tight structures on any circle bundle over a surface.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the ε Invariant for Homologically Essential Knots via Grid Diagrams

The ε invariant, introduced by Hom, is a concordance invariant taking values in {−1,0,1} that refines the τ invariant. For a knot K ⊂ S³, ε(K) = 1 if τ(K) > 0 and the knot is not slice, ε(K) = −1 if τ(K) < 0, and ε(K) = 0 otherwise. When K is homologically essential in a rational homology sphere, the definition extends using the knot Floer complex CFK∞(Y,K). Grid diagrams give a completely combinatorial model for CFK∞: a grid diagram of index n for K yields a chain complex generated by n! permutations, with differential counting empty rectangles. The Alexander and Maslov gradings are read off from the grid coordinates. The ε invariant can be extracted from the filtered chain homotopy type of this complex. In practice one computes the homology of the associated graded complex with respect to the Alexander filtration; the ε invariant is the sign of the lowest Alexander grading in which a non‑torsion class appears. For alternating knots the grid diagram coming from a reduced alternating projection is nice, meaning all rectangles contributing to the differential are empty, and the complex simplifies to a direct sum of staircases. In that case ε(K) = sign(σ(K)/2) where σ is the signature. For non‑alternating knots one can still use a grid diagram, but the differential may contain higher order terms; the algorithm of Manolescu–Ozsváth–Sarkar reduces the computation to a sequence of grid moves (commutation, stabilization, destabilization) that preserve the filtered homotopy type. The resulting ε invariant is independent of the chosen grid diagram and agrees with the original definition via the knot Floer complex. This combinatorial approach makes ε computable for any knot with a known grid presentation, and it has been implemented in software such as the Python package gridlink.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Large Surgery Formula for Knots in Homology Spheres

The large surgery formula of Ozsváth and Szabó expresses the Heegaard Floer homology of +n surgery on a knot K ⊂ S³ for n ≥ 2g(K)−1 entirely in terms of the knot Floer complex CFK∞(K). For a knot K in an arbitrary integer homology sphere Y, the same principle holds: if n is sufficiently large compared to the genus of K and the correction terms of Y, then HF+(Yn(K)) is determined by the filtered chain homotopy type of CFK∞(Y,K) together with the absolute grading shift coming from the d‑invariant of Y. The proof uses the mapping cone formula for integer surgeries, which builds a chain complex Cone(𝔸 → 𝔹) where 𝔸 and 𝔹 are truncated versions of CFK∞. When n is large, the maps in the cone become quasi‑isomorphisms onto their images, and the homology splits as a direct sum of copies of HF+(Y) shifted by the Alexander gradings of the knot. The precise threshold is n ≥ 2g(K)−1 + max{|d(Y,𝔰)| : 𝔰 ∈ Spinc(Y)}. In this range the surgery formula simplifies to HF+(Yn(K), 𝔰) ≅ ⊕i∈ℤ HF+(Y, 𝔰i) where the sum runs over Alexander gradings i with non‑zero homology in H*(CFK∞(Y,K){i}). The formula also yields the correction terms d(Yn(K), 𝔰) = d(Y, 𝔰0) − 2 max{i : H*(CFK∞{i}) ≠ 0}. This result is crucial for computing Heegaard Floer homology of many 3‑manifolds obtained by surgery on knots in homology spheres, and it underpins the proof that L‑space knots in homology spheres are fibered and have simple knot Floer homology.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the Upsilon Invariant for Knots in S³ via Grid Diagrams

The upsilon invariant ΥK(t) is a piecewise linear concordance homomorphism from the knot concordance group to the space of continuous functions on [0,2], introduced by Ozsváth, Stipsicz and Szabó. For each t ∈ [0,2] it is defined as the minimal grading of a homogeneous element in the knot Floer complex CFK∞(K) that lies in the image of the map induced by multiplication by U⌈t/2⌉. Grid diagrams provide a finite‑dimensional model for CFK∞ that is amenable to computer calculation. Given a grid diagram of index n, one constructs the chain complex Cgrid generated by permutations with differential counting empty rectangles. The Alexander filtration and the U‑action are both visible on the grid: U corresponds to moving an O‑mark one step north‑west. The upsilon invariant at a rational slope t = p/q can be read off from the filtered chain homotopy type of the associated graded complex with respect to a two‑parameter filtration (Alexander, U‑power). In practice one computes the homology of the subcomplexes Fs = {x : A(x) ≤ s, Ukx = 0 for k > ⌈t/2⌉} and finds the smallest Maslov grading of a non‑zero class. The piecewise linear function ΥK(t) has slopes that are integers determined by the ranks of the knot Floer homology groups. For alternating knots the grid diagram from a reduced projection is nice, and ΥK(t) coincides with the function derived from the Alexander polynomial: ΥK(t) = (t/2)σ(K) for t ∈ [0,2]. For non‑alternating knots the grid‑based algorithm of Manolescu–Ozsváth–Sarkar reduces the computation to a sequence of grid moves that preserve the filtered homotopy type, yielding an effective procedure. This combinatorial method has been implemented and used to tabulate Υ for all knots up to 12 crossings.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the d‑Invariant for Rational Homology Spheres via the Mapping Cone Formula

The Ozsváth–Szabó d‑invariant (correction term) of a rational homology sphere Y equipped with a Spinc structure 𝔰 is defined as the minimal grading of a non‑torsion element in the image of HF−(Y,𝔰) → HF∞(Y,𝔰). For a rational homology sphere obtained by p/q surgery on a knot K ⊂ S³, the mapping cone formula expresses the Heegaard Floer complex CF−(Y,𝔰) as a mapping cone of a chain map between direct sums of truncated knot Floer complexes. The construction uses the continued fraction expansion p/q = [a1, a2, …, ak] and builds a multi‑column complex where each column corresponds to a term ai. The vertical differential in column i involves multiplication by Uai, while the horizontal differentials are induced by the knot Floer differential and the inclusion maps between Alexander filtration levels. The resulting chain complex is quasi‑isomorphic to CF−(Y,𝔰). The d‑invariant is then extracted by computing the homology of this finite‑dimensional complex and finding the minimal grading of a class that survives under U‑multiplication. For L‑space knots the knot Floer homology is particularly simple (a staircase), and the mapping cone collapses to a single staircase whose bottom grading gives d(Y,𝔰) = −2V0 + (p−1)/4 where V0 is the τ invariant of K. For general knots the algorithm proceeds by constructing the mapping cone explicitly from a grid diagram of K, then running a Smith normal form computation over ℤ[U] to obtain the homology. This method has been used to compute d‑invariants for all rational homology spheres arising from surgery on knots up to 10 crossings, and it provides the primary tool for testing the L‑space conjecture in the rational surgery setting.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Naturality of Cobordism Maps under Diffeomorphisms

The cobordism maps induced by a smooth, oriented 4‑dimensional cobordism W between closed 3‑manifolds Y₀ and Y₁ are central to the functoriality of Heegaard Floer homology. Ozsváth and Szabó defined maps FW,𝔰 : HF⁻(Y₀,𝔰|Y₀) → HF⁻(Y₁,𝔰|Y₁) for each Spin^c structure 𝔰 on W. A fundamental question is whether these maps are invariant under diffeomorphisms of the cobordism relative to the boundary. Zemke proved that if φ : W → W is a diffeomorphism restricting to the identity on ∂W, then the induced map on Heegaard Floer homology satisfies FW,𝔰 = FW,φ*𝔰 ∘ (φ|Y₀)_* = (φ|Y₁)_* ∘ FW,𝔰. The proof uses the cylindrical reformulation of the theory, where the cobordism is decomposed into elementary pieces (1‑handles, 2‑handles, 3‑handles) and the maps are constructed by counting holomorphic triangles in the symmetric product of the Heegaard surface. The diffeomorphism invariance follows from the fact that the chain homotopy type of the triangle counts is unchanged under handleslides and stabilizations that implement φ. Consequently, the cobordism maps descend to well‑defined maps on the mapping class group orbits of Spin^c structures, a property that is essential for defining 4‑manifold invariants such as the Ozsváth–Szabó mixed invariants for manifolds with b₂⁺ = 1. In practice, naturality allows one to compute the effect of a diffeomorphism on HF⁻ by tracking its action on the Heegaard diagram and the associated Spin^c structures, rather than re‑counting holomorphic curves from scratch.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Bypass Exact Triangle for Over‑twisted Contact Structures

Over‑twisted contact structures on a closed 3‑manifold Y are classified by their homotopy class of plane fields, and the contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ HF⁻(−Y) vanishes for every over‑twisted ξ. Nevertheless, the bypass exact triangle provides a powerful tool for relating the Heegaard Floer invariants of contact structures that differ by a single bypass attachment. Given a Legendrian knot L ⊂ (Y,ξ) with Thurston–Bennequin number tb(L) and rotation number rot(L), a bypass attachment along a convex surface Σ changes the contact structure to ξ′. The bypass exact triangle, established by Honda–Kazez–Matić and interpreted in Heegaard Floer homology by Etnyre–Ozbagci–Vela‑Vick, reads … → HF⁻(Y,ξ) → HF⁻(Y,ξ′) → HF⁻(Y,ξ″) → … where ξ″ corresponds to a second bypass attachment. The maps in the triangle are induced by cobordisms obtained by attaching a 2‑handle along L with framing tb(L)−1. The triangle is exact at the level of chain complexes, and the contact invariants satisfy c(ξ) = F(c(ξ′)) and c(ξ′) = F(c(ξ″)). For over‑twisted structures the invariant is zero, so the triangle reduces to a short exact sequence of homology groups. This exactness can be used to compute HF⁻ of a manifold obtained by a sequence of bypasses starting from a known tight structure, and it underlies the proof that the contact invariant detects tightness on many Seifert fibered spaces. In algorithmic terms, one can realize a bypass attachment as a handleslide of the β‑curves in a Heegaard diagram adapted to the Legendrian knot, making the triangle computable via the bordered theory.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the Contact Invariant for Tight Contact Structures on Graph Manifolds

Graph manifolds are 3‑manifolds that decompose along tori into Seifert fibered pieces. The result is non‑zero precisely when the gluing data satisfy the Honda–Lisca–Stipsicz combinatorial criteria, providing an algorithmic verification of tightness. Heegaard Floer Homology and the Involutive Heegaard Floer Homology for 3‑Manifolds with Boundary

Involutive Heegaard Floer homology HF_I⁻(Y) was introduced by Hendricks and Manolescu for closed 3‑manifolds Y using the conjugation involution ι on the Heegaard Floer chain complex CF⁻(Y). This framework allows the computation of the involutive correction terms d̄(Y,𝔰) and ď(Y,𝔰) for rational homology spheres with boundary by gluing a standard filling. The involutive correction terms also give a lower bound for the 4‑dimensional slice genus of a knot in a homology sphere via the inequality d̄(S³_n(K)) ≤ −2ν⁺(K) for large n. These results demonstrate that the involutive theory retains the computational power of the bordered theory while providing finer obstructions to sliceness and to the existence of certain 4‑dimensional cobordisms.

The invariant ν⁺(K) is defined as the maximal integer s such that there exists a homogeneous element x ∈ HF_I⁻(K) with Alexander grading s which is not U‑torsion. Heegaard Floer Homology and the Immersed Curve Formalism for Bordered Invariants

The immersed curve formalism, introduced by Hanselman, Rasmussen and Watson (2018), gives a geometric description of the bordered Heegaard Floer invariants for 3‑manifolds with torus boundary. A bordered manifold Y with parametrized boundary T² is assigned a type D structure ĈFD(Y) over the torus algebra 𝒜(T²). Each curve represents a summand of the type D structure; the local system encodes the action of the algebra generators. The pairing theorem then becomes a count of intersection points of the curves representing the two sides of a gluing, with the local systems determining the differential on the resulting chain complex. This reformulation makes the invariance under handleslides and stabilizations manifest, because the curves are invariant under the mapping class group action on the torus. It also yields an algorithmic way to compute the Heegaard Floer homology of Dehn surgeries: the surgery coefficient corresponds to a slope on the torus, and the homology of the surgered manifold is the Lagrangian Floer homology of the curve with the standard curve of that slope. The formalism has been extended to manifolds with higher genus boundary by work of Zarev and others, but the torus case remains the most complete and computationally effective.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Classification of L‑Space Knots via Staircase Complexes

An L‑space knot in S³ is a knot that admits a positive L‑space surgery. Ozsváth and Szabó proved that such knots are fibered and that their knot Floer homology HFK⁻(K) is a staircase complex: after a suitable change of basis the differential connects generators in a single diagonal staircase pattern. The Alexander grading of the generators is determined by the Alexander polynomial Δ_K(t); the coefficients of Δ_K must be 1, 0, 1, 0, …, 1 with alternating signs, and the genus g(K) equals the degree of Δ_K. Conversely, any knot whose knot Floer homology is a staircase complex is an L‑space knot. The staircase shape forces the τ invariant to equal the genus, and the ε invariant to be +1. The classification has been used to prove that the only L‑space knots with tunnel number one are torus knots and the (−2,3,7) pretzel knot. Moreover, the staircase model gives a simple formula for the Heegaard Floer homology of any positive surgery on an L‑space knot: the result is an L‑space whose correction terms d are computed by a lattice path in the staircase. This combinatorial description underlies the proof of the Berge conjecture for many families of lens space surgeries.

Worked Example: Immersed Curves for the Solid Torus

The differential counts holomorphic strips, which in this model are empty because the curves are linear. Hence the resulting homology is a direct sum of |p| copies of ℤ/2, matching the known Heegaard Floer homology of the lens space L(p,q). The absolute grading shift is given by the symplectic area of the triangles, which reproduces the standard d‑invariants of lens spaces.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Bordered Sutured Theory of Zarev

Zarev’s bordered sutured Floer homology extends the bordered theory to 3‑manifolds whose boundary is a sutured surface (F, Λ). To each such manifold Y one associates a type D structure ĈFDD(Y) over the bordered sutured algebra 𝒜(F, Λ), which is a differential graded algebra generated by Reeb chords on the sutured surface. The construction uses Heegaard diagrams with boundary arcs that meet the sutures, and the differential counts holomorphic curves with boundary on the arcs. The pairing theorem states that gluing two bordered sutured manifolds along a common sutured boundary corresponds to the derived tensor product of their type D structures, yielding the sutured Floer homology of the closed manifold. This framework recovers Juhász’s sutured Floer homology as the special case where one side is a product sutured manifold. It also provides a natural setting for the contact invariant: a contact structure with convex boundary gives a distinguished element in the bordered sutured invariant, and gluing preserves the contact invariant. The theory has been used to compute the contact invariant for contact structures on manifolds with boundary and to prove naturality under contactomorphisms that fix the boundary.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Involutive Concordance Invariants V and underline{V}

Involutive Heegaard Floer homology, developed by Hendricks and Manolescu (2017), equips the Heegaard Floer complexes with a homotopy involution ι induced by the hyperelliptic involution on the symmetric product. For a knot K ⊂ S³ the involutive knot Floer complex CFK⁻(K) carries an ι‑action that commutes with the differential and the U‑map. From this structure one extracts two integer‑valued concordance invariants V(K) and underline{V}(K) (sometimes denoted ν and underline{ν}). They satisfy underline{V}(K) ≤ V(K) ≤ g₄(K), where g₄(K) is the smooth slice genus. Moreover, V and underline{V} give lower bounds for the 4‑ball genus and detect non‑slice knots that have vanishing τ and ε. For example, the knot 12n₄₅₈ has τ = 0, ε = 0 but V = 1, proving it is not slice. The invariants are computed from the mapping cone formula for involutive knot Floer homology, which involves the ι‑action on the filtered complex. They also satisfy a connected sum formula V(K₁ # K₂) = V(K₁) + V(K₂) and change predictably under concordance. These properties make V and underline{V} powerful tools for studying the smooth concordance group.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Knot Floer Homology for Knots in Lens Spaces

Ozsváth and Szabó (2005) extended knot Floer homology to knots in arbitrary rational homology spheres, including lens spaces L(p,q). For a knot K ⊂ L(p,q) one can lift K to a link tilde{K} in the universal cover S³ (a p‑component link). The knot Floer homology HFK⁻(L(p,q), K) is then isomorphic to the equivariant knot Floer homology of tilde{K} with respect to the deck transformation group ℤ/p. This equivariant theory carries an action of the group ring ℤ[U, ℤ/p] and its homology decomposes according to Spin^c structures on the lens space. The τ invariant for knots in lens spaces is defined as the minimal Alexander grading of a non‑torsion class in HFK⁻; it satisfies the same bounds on the slice genus in the lens space. The contact invariant for a Legendrian knot in a lens space lives in the hat version widehat{HFK} and can be computed via grid diagrams adapted to the lens space. These invariants have been applied to the Berge conjecture: a knot in a lens space that admits an L‑space surgery must have knot Floer homology of a particularly simple form, analogous to the staircase condition in S³. Recent work by Baker, Grigsby and Hedden classifies L‑space knots in lens spaces using this equivariant staircase model.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Adjunction Inequality for 4‑Manifolds with b₂⁺ > 1

Ozsváth and Szabó proved that the Heegaard Floer homology groups of a closed, oriented 4‑manifold X with b₂⁺(X) > 1 satisfy an adjunction inequality for smoothly embedded surfaces. If Σ ⊂ X is a connected, oriented surface of genus g representing a homology class A ∈ H₂(X;ℤ) with A·A ≥ 0, then the inequality 2g − 2 ≥ |⟨c₁(s), A⟩| + A·A holds for every Spin^c structure s on X for which the Ozsváth–Szabó invariant ΦX,s is non‑zero. The invariant ΦX,s is defined by counting pseudo‑holomorphic triangles in the symmetric product associated to a Heegaard triple diagram for a decomposition X = X₁ ∪_Y X₂. The proof uses the fact that the cobordism map induced by X on Heegaard Floer homology respects the grading shift determined by c₁(s) and the intersection form. When b₂⁺(X) = 1 the inequality can fail because the relevant moduli spaces are not compact; the blow‑up formula for Φ shows that the inequality is sharp for symplectic 4‑manifolds and recovers the classical adjunction inequality of Kronheimer–Mrowka for Seiberg–Witten theory via the isomorphism of the two Floer homologies. The inequality has been applied to prove that certain homology classes cannot be represented by smoothly embedded spheres, to constrain the geography of simply‑connected 4‑manifolds, and to give new proofs of the Thom conjecture for complex surfaces.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the τ Invariant for Knots in Homology Spheres via the Mapping Cone Formula

For a knot K in an integer homology sphere Y, the knot Floer complex CFK⁻(Y,K) is a filtered chain complex over ℤ[U] whose homology is HFK⁻(Y,K). Ozsváth and Szabó showed that the τ invariant, defined as the minimal Alexander grading of a non‑torsion class in HFK⁻, can be read off from the mapping cone formula for the surgery exact triangle. Given a Heegaard diagram for Y adapted to K, the complex for the 0‑surgery Y₀(K) is the mapping cone of a chain map v₀ + h₀: A₀ → B₀, where A₀ and B₀ are the sub‑ and quotient complexes of CFK⁻ corresponding to the two Spin^c structures on Y₀(K) that extend the unique Spin^c structure on Y. The map v₀ counts holomorphic disks that cross the basepoint w, while h₀ counts those that cross z. The τ invariant equals the negative of the maximal grading shift for which the inclusion A_s → B_s induces an isomorphism on homology after inverting U. In practice one computes the filtered chain homotopy type of CFK⁻ using a nice Heegaard diagram or a grid diagram, then applies the mapping cone algorithm implemented in software such as HFKcalc. The resulting τ satisfies τ(K) ≤ g₄(K) and τ(K) = g(K) for fibered knots, and it determines the correction term d(Yp/q(K)) for large surgeries via the large surgery formula. The mapping cone approach also yields the ε invariant, which refines τ by recording the local equivalence class of the filtered complex.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Relationship with the Rasmussen s‑Invariant for Links

The Rasmussen s‑invariant, originally defined for knots via Khovanov homology, extends to links in S³ by considering the Lee deformation of the Khovanov complex. Ozsváth and Szabó constructed a spectral sequence from the reduced Khovanov homology of a link L to the hat version of link Floer homology ĤFL(L). This spectral sequence respects the bigrading (homological, quantum) on Khovanov homology and the (Alexander, Maslov) bigrading on ĤFL. For a knot, the s‑invariant equals twice the τ invariant: s(K) = 2τ(K). For links with more than one component, the relationship is more subtle. The link Floer homology ĤFL(L) carries a multi‑variable Alexander grading indexed by H₁(S³ L;ℤ) ≅ ℤ|L|. The spectral sequence collapses at the E₂ page for alternating links, and the s‑invariant of each component can be recovered from the τ invariant of that component together with the linking numbers. In particular, if L = L₁ ∪ L₂ is a two‑component link, then s(L₁) = 2τ(L₁) + lk(L₁,L₂) and similarly for L₂, where τ(L_i) is the τ invariant of the knot L_i considered inside the homology sphere obtained by 0‑surgery on the other component. This formula follows from the behaviour of the spectral sequence under the surgery exact triangle and the fact that the contact invariant in ĤFL detects the Thurston–Bennequin number of Legendrian representatives. The relationship has been used to prove that the s‑invariant gives a lower bound for the 4‑ball genus of each component of a link and to construct examples of links with vanishing τ but non‑zero s.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization via the Cylindrical Reformulation for 3‑Manifolds with b₁ > 0

The cylindrical reformulation of bordered Heegaard Floer homology, developed by Lipshitz, Ozsváth, and Thurston, replaces the symmetric product Sym^g(Σ) with a cylindrical end ℝ × [0,1] × α ∪ β, where α and β are the attaching curves on the Heegaard surface Σ. For a 3‑manifold Y with b₁(Y) > 0, the Heegaard diagram has genus g > b₁, and the α and β curves do not span the full homology of Σ. The cylindrical model introduces a family of almost complex structures J_t that stretch the neck along the Reeb orbits corresponding to the non‑trivial homology classes. Invariance under stabilization (adding a trivial handle to the Heegaard diagram) is proved by constructing a chain homotopy equivalence between the cylindrical complexes before and after stabilization. The key step is to analyse the moduli spaces of holomorphic curves with boundary on the new α and β curves; these curves are either constant or have a single positive puncture asymptotic to a Reeb chord that represents the new homology class. The differential in the stabilized complex splits as a direct sum of the original differential and an acyclic complex generated by the new Reeb chords. This splitting uses the fact that the new α and β curves intersect in a single point, so the corresponding bordered algebra is a matrix algebra over the original algebra. The invariance proof also requires a careful treatment of the Novikov ring coefficients that keep track of the periods of the symplectic form on the cylindrical end. The result extends the invariance theorem for bordered manifolds with b₁ = 0 to all oriented 3‑manifolds with boundary, and it is a crucial ingredient in the proof that the bordered invariant is a well‑defined functor from the extended cobordism category to the category of A_∞ modules.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the ε Invariant for Links in S³

The ε invariant, introduced by Hom for knots, refines the τ invariant by recording the local equivalence class of the filtered knot Floer complex CFK⁻. For a link L = L₁ ∪ … ∪ L_m in S³, the link Floer complex CFL⁻(L) carries a multi‑variable filtration indexed by the Alexander gradings A₁,…,A_m. The ε invariant of L is an m‑tuple (ε₁,…,ε_m) where each ε_i ∈ {−1,0,1} is defined by examining the homology of the subcomplexes CFL⁻(L)A_i ≤ s as s varies. Concretely, ε_i = 1 if there exists a class in H_*(CFL⁻)A_i ≤ 0 that maps non‑trivially to H_*(CFL⁻)A_i ≤ 1 under the inclusion, ε_i = −1 if the dual statement holds for the quotient complex, and ε_i = 0 otherwise. The computation uses a grid diagram for L, which gives a combinatorial description of CFL⁻ as a complex of free ℤ[U₁,…,U_m]‑modules generated by grid states. The differential counts empty rectangles in the grid, and the Alexander gradings are read off from the winding numbers of the grid state around the basepoints. Software such as gridlink implements this algorithm and has been used to compute ε for all links up to 11 crossings. The ε invariant satisfies a connected sum formula ε(L₁ # L₂) = ε(L₁) + ε(L₂) and gives a lower bound for the 4‑ball genus of each component: |ε_i| ≤ g₄(L_i). Moreover, ε detects non‑slice links that have vanishing τ and s invariants, for example the link L10n79 where τ = 0 but ε = (1,−1). The invariant also controls the behaviour of the link Floer homology under the link surgery formula, providing a combinatorial criterion for when a link surgery yields an L‑space.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization for the Full Infinity Complex with Non‑Trivial Boundary

The full infinity complex CFI⁻ associated to a bordered 3‑manifold with non‑trivial first homology carries a filtration by the spinc structures on the boundary. When a stabilization is performed by adding a cancelling pair of 1‑ and 2‑handles to the Heegaard diagram, the new α and β curves intersect in a single transverse point. The cylindrical reformulation of the bordered theory shows that the stabilized complex splits as a direct sum of the original CFI⁻ and an acyclic complex generated by the new Reeb chords. The chain homotopy equivalence is constructed by counting holomorphic curves in the symmetric product that have exactly one positive puncture asymptotic to the new Reeb chord and no negative punctures. The Novikov ring coefficients record the symplectic area of these curves; because the new curves have zero area, the Novikov variable does not appear in the differential of the acyclic summand. This splitting proves that the bordered invariant is unchanged under stabilization for all oriented 3‑manifolds with boundary, extending the earlier result for b₁ = 0. The argument also requires a careful analysis of the A_∞ module structure: the new algebra element acts as a matrix unit that isolates the acyclic summand, and the higher A_∞ operations vanish on it by degree reasons. Consequently, the functor from the extended cobordism category to the category of A_∞ modules is well defined on objects with arbitrary boundary topology.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization for the Full Infinity Complex with Non‑Trivial Boundary and Torsion Spinc Structures

When the ambient 3‑manifold admits torsion spinc structures, the full infinity complex carries an additional grading by the torsion subgroup of H²(Y;ℤ). Stabilization introduces a new pair of curves whose homology class is trivial in H₁(Σ) but non‑trivial in the relative homology of the Heegaard surface with boundary. The moduli spaces of holomorphic disks contributing to the differential now have boundary components mapping to the new α and β curves; their Maslov index is computed using the relative grading formula that incorporates the torsion Chern class. The chain homotopy equivalence is built by a filtered mapping cone that respects the torsion grading. The key technical point is that the new Reeb chords have zero torsion grading, so the acyclic summand lives entirely in grading zero. The inclusion and projection maps are defined by counting holomorphic curves with one positive puncture and any number of negative punctures, weighted by the Novikov variable raised to the symplectic area. Because the area is zero, the Novikov variable drops out, and the maps are chain maps over the ground ring ℤ[U]. The proof also uses the fact that the bordered algebra for the stabilized diagram is Morita equivalent to the original algebra, preserving the A_∞ module category up to quasi‑isomorphism. This result is essential for defining the bordered invariant for manifolds with torsion spinc structures and for the subsequent proof of the surgery exact triangle in the presence of torsion.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization for the Full Infinity Complex with Non‑Trivial Boundary and Higher Genus Boundary

For a bordered 3‑manifold whose boundary has genus g > 1, the stabilization adds a pair of curves that intersect in a single point on a subsurface of genus one. The bordered algebra becomes a tensor product of the original algebra with the matrix algebra M₂(ℤ) supported on the stabilized subsurface. The full infinity complex is a module over this tensor product algebra. The invariance proof proceeds by showing that the new matrix factor is contractible as an A_∞ bimodule. One constructs a bimodule homotopy equivalence between the identity bimodule and the stabilized bimodule by counting holomorphic curves with boundary on the new α and β curves and with inputs and outputs in the original algebra. The key combinatorial input is that the new curves form a standard genus‑one stabilization inside the higher genus surface, so the local picture is identical to the genus‑one case. The global differential splits as a tensor product of the original differential with the identity on the matrix factor, plus a term that is a differential on the matrix factor alone. The latter is acyclic because the matrix algebra with the standard differential is a resolution of the ground ring. This argument extends the invariance theorem to arbitrary genus boundary and is a crucial step in proving that the bordered theory defines a functor on the full extended cobordism category.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization for the Full Infinity Complex with Non‑Trivial Boundary and Non‑Separating Stabilization

A non‑separating stabilization adds a 1‑handle that connects two distinct boundary components of the Heegaard surface. The new α and β curves each run once over the new handle and intersect in a single point. The bordered algebra acquires a new idempotent corresponding to the new region, and the A_∞ module structure is modified by adding a new generator that pairs with this idempotent. The differential on the new generator is the original generator multiplied by the new Reeb chord. The acyclic summand is again generated by the new Reeb chord and its differential. The chain homotopy equivalence is built by a mapping cone that collapses the new idempotent. The Novikov ring coefficients are unchanged because the new curves bound no holomorphic disks with positive area. This version of stabilization invariance is required when proving that the bordered invariant is independent of the choice of Heegaard diagram for manifolds with multiple boundary components, and it underlies the proof of the pairing theorem for gluing along non‑connected boundaries.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Invariance under Stabilization for the Full Infinity Complex with Non‑Trivial Boundary and Relative Grading Refinement

The relative grading on the full infinity complex is refined by the homotopy class of paths in the symmetric product. Stabilization introduces a new relative grading shift of one for the new generator. The chain homotopy equivalence respects this shift because the homotopy map has degree −1. In the cylindrical formulation, the grading shift corresponds to the fact that the new Reeb chord has Conley–Zehnder index one. The differential on the acyclic summand raises the grading by one, making the summand a graded acyclic complex. The inclusion and projection maps are grading‑preserving, and the homotopy lowers grading by one. This graded invariance is essential for the definition of the d‑invariant on bordered manifolds and for the proof that the contact invariant is well defined under stabilization of the open book decomposition. The argument also uses the fact that the bordered algebra carries a natural grading by the Maslov index of Reeb chords, and the stabilization adds a degree‑one generator that does not affect the graded Morita equivalence class of the algebra.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Ozsváth–Szabó 4‑Manifold Invariants for Manifolds with b₂⁺>1

The original Ozsváth–Szabó 4‑manifold invariants were defined for smooth, closed, oriented 4‑manifolds with b₂⁺=1 using the Heegaard Floer homology of the boundary 3‑manifold and a choice of Spinc structure. When b₂⁺>1 the invariant takes values in the Heegaard Floer homology of the boundary with coefficients in the Novikov ring associated to the second homology. The construction proceeds by choosing a generic path of metrics and perturbations on the 4‑manifold, counting solutions to the Seiberg–Witten equations with a fixed Spinc structure, and then translating the count into a map on Heegaard Floer homology via the isomorphism with Seiberg–Witten Floer homology. The resulting invariant is independent of the auxiliary choices and satisfies a blow‑up formula that generalizes the b₂⁺=1 case. In particular, for a 4‑manifold X with b₂⁺>1 the invariant ΦX,𝔰 lies in HF+(−∂X,𝔰|∂X)⊗Λ*H₁(X;ℤ) and obeys a wall‑crossing formula when the metric crosses a chamber determined by the period point of the Spinc structure. This extension is crucial for applications to the geography problem of symplectic 4‑manifolds and for proving adjunction‑type inequalities for surfaces of higher genus.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Contact Invariant for Contact Structures with Non‑Zero Giroux Torsion on General 3‑Manifolds

Giroux torsion measures the failure of a contact structure to be tight in a neighborhood of a torus. For a contact 3‑manifold (Y,ξ) with Giroux torsion greater than zero, the contact invariant c(ξ) in the hat version of Heegaard Floer homology vanishes. The proof uses the open book decomposition compatible with ξ and the fact that positive Giroux torsion forces the monodromy to contain a Dehn twist about a curve that is homologically non‑trivial in the page. In the Heegaard diagram arising from the open book, this Dehn twist creates a region where the differential counts holomorphic disks with boundary on the twisted curves, producing a cancellation that kills the contact class. The vanishing result extends to the full infinity complex and to the sutured Floer homology invariant of the complement of a neighborhood of the torus. Consequently, any contact structure with non‑zero Giroux torsion cannot be Stein fillable, and the contact invariant provides an effective obstruction to the existence of symplectic fillings. This theorem has been used to classify tight contact structures on many Seifert fibered spaces and on torus bundles over the circle.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Graded Roots Model for L‑Space Surgeries on Graph Manifolds

Graph manifolds are 3‑manifolds obtained by plumbing along a graph whose vertices correspond to Seifert fibered pieces. For a graph manifold that is an L‑space, the Heegaard Floer homology HF+(Y,𝔰) is a free ℤ[U]‑module of rank one in each Spinc structure. Némethi and Ozsváth–Szabó showed that the entire HF+ can be encoded by a graded root, a combinatorial object consisting of a tree with vertices labeled by integers (the grading) and edges representing the U‑action. The graded root is constructed from the plumbing graph by assigning to each vertex a weight equal to the Euler number of the corresponding Seifert fibration and then performing a sequence of blow‑downs that correspond to surgery exact triangles. The resulting root determines the d‑invariants (correction terms) of Y and provides a complete algorithm for computing HF+ of any L‑space graph manifold. This model also yields a simple criterion for a graph manifold to be an L‑space: the graded root must have no branching, i.e., it must be a linear chain. The graded roots perspective has been instrumental in proving the L‑space conjecture for graph manifolds and in relating Heegaard Floer homology to lattice homology.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Computation of the d‑Invariant for Seifert Fibered Spaces via Lattice Homology

For a rational homology sphere Y that is a Seifert fibered space, the d‑invariant (Ozsváth–Szabó correction term) can be computed using lattice homology associated to a negative definite plumbing graph that bounds Y. The lattice homology groups ℍ+(Γ) are defined as the homology of a chain complex generated by characteristic vectors in the lattice H₂(W;ℤ) of the plumbing 4‑manifold W, with differential counting certain paths in the lattice. Némethi proved that ℍ+(Γ) is isomorphic to HF+(−Y) as relatively graded ℤ[U]‑modules. The d‑invariant is then extracted as the minimal grading of a non‑torsion element in ℍ+(Γ). In practice, one chooses a plumbing graph with vertices weighted by the Seifert invariants, computes the set of characteristic vectors, and runs a shortest‑path algorithm on the associated graded root to obtain the d‑invariant for each Spinc structure. This method has been implemented in computer programs and has yielded explicit formulas for the d‑invariants of all Seifert fibered homology spheres, confirming the conjecture that they are L‑spaces if and only if the base orbifold has positive Euler characteristic.

Heegaard Floer Homology and the Ozsváth–Szabó 4‑Manifold Invariants for Manifolds with Boundary

When a smooth, oriented 4‑manifold X has non‑empty boundary ∂X=Y, the Ozsváth–Szabó invariant takes the form of a map ΦX,𝔰 from the Heegaard Floer homology of Y to the Heegaard Floer homology of a point (i.e., the ground ring). The construction uses a relative version of the Seiberg–Witten equations on X with cylindrical end modeled on Y, and the isomorphism with Seiberg–Witten Floer homology identifies the moduli space of solutions with a chain map on the Heegaard Floer complex of Y. The resulting invariant is a homomorphism ΦX,𝔰: HF+(Y,𝔰|Y)→ℤ[U] that satisfies a gluing formula: if X=X₁∪YX₂ then ΦX,𝔰=ΦX₂,𝔰₂∘ΦX₁,𝔰₁. For b₂⁺(X)>1 the map lands in the Novikov completion of ℤ[U] and depends on a choice of chamber. This relative invariant has been used to obstruct the existence of Stein fillings of contact 3‑manifolds and to prove the Thom conjecture for surfaces in symplectic 4‑manifolds with boundary.
