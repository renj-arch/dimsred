Mastering General Science

General science is the foundation of the competitive exam syllabus, covering fundamental concepts from physics, chemistry, and biology. In these exams, questions are typically framed around high school level knowledge, focusing on everyday scientific phenomena and essential facts. This lesson will systematically break down these topics into manageable sections to ensure you understand not just the answers, but the underlying principles.

Physics: The Science of Matter and Motion

Physics in this context deals with how matter moves and interacts with energy. The cornerstone is Newton's Second Law, which states that Force equals Mass times Acceleration (F = ma). This formula explains why a heavier object requires more force to accelerate at the same rate as a lighter one. Another critical concept is Gravitation, where the acceleration due to gravity on Earth is approximately 9.8 meters per second squared (g = 9.8 m/s²). Light behaves according to laws of reflection and refraction, which govern how mirrors and lenses form images. Sound travels through air at a standard speed of 340 meters per second, creating echoes when it bounces off surfaces. Finally, electricity is governed by Ohm's Law (V = IR), linking Voltage, Current, and Resistance in circuits.

Chemistry: The Study of Substances

Chemistry explains the composition of matter and how substances change. At the atomic level, an atom consists of protons, neutrons, and electrons. Matter exists as elements (pure substances), compounds (chemically bonded elements like H₂O or CO₂), and mixtures (physically combined substances). Acids are defined by a pH value less than 7, while bases have a pH greater than 7; neutral solutions sit at exactly 7. Oxidation-Reduction reactions involve the transfer of electrons between molecules, a process vital for combustion and respiration.

Biology: Life and the Human Body

Biology focuses on living organisms, starting with the cell as the basic unit of life. The human body operates through complex systems like the digestive, respiratory, circulatory, and nervous systems. A crucial part of biology for exams is understanding Vitamins; these are essential organic compounds required in small quantities. For instance, Vitamin A is vital for vision, while Vitamin C prevents scurvy. Deficiencies lead to specific diseases, such as Rickets caused by a lack of Vitamin D or Calcium.

Solved Examples

Example 1: Applying Ohm's Law
A circuit has a voltage of 12 Volts and a resistance of 4 Ohms. Calculate the current flowing through it.

a) 3 Amps
b) 48 Amps
c) 0.33 Amps
d) 16 Amps
Solution: a) 3 Amps. We use Ohm's Law: V = I × R. To find Current (I), we rearrange the formula to I = V / R. Substituting the values: I = 12V / 4Ω = 3A. This confirms that 3 Amps of current flows through the circuit.
Example 2: Vitamin Deficiency
Which vitamin deficiency leads to night blindness?

a) Vitamin B1
b) Vitamin C
c) Vitamin A
d) Vitamin D
Solution: c) Vitamin A. The retina of the eye requires Vitamin A to convert light into signals for the brain. Without sufficient Vitamin A, the eye cannot see properly in low-light conditions, leading to night blindness. Vitamin B1 is for nerve function, C for immunity/scurvy, and D for bone health.

Common Traps

Students often fall into specific traps that lead to incorrect answers during the exam:

  • Confusing Units: Mixing up SI units like confusing Watts (Power) with Volts (Voltage) or Amps (Current). Always check if the question asks for force, energy, or power.
  • pH Scale Misconception: Thinking that a higher number means more acidic. Remember, high pH is basic/alkaline, low pH is acidic.
  • Speed of Sound vs Light: Assuming sound travels faster than light. In reality, light is instantaneous for practical distances, while sound takes time (hence thunder after lightning).
  • Vitamin Sources: Confusing sources, such as thinking Vitamin A comes from citrus fruits (it does not; that is Vitamin C). Vitamin A is found in carrots and liver.

Exam-Day Strategy
When facing a General Science question, first identify the branch: Is it Physics (motion/energy), Chemistry (substances/reactions), or Biology (life/cells)? If you are unsure of a specific fact, eliminate options based on units. For example, if a question asks for energy and one option is in Watts, discard it immediately because Watts measure power, not energy (Joules). This process of elimination saves valuable time.

Mechanics of Fluids: Bernoulli’s Principle and Applications

Bernoulli’s principle follows from the conservation of energy applied to an ideal fluid flowing along a streamline. For an incompressible, non‑viscous, steady flow the sum of pressure energy, kinetic energy per unit volume, and potential energy per unit volume remains constant: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant. Here P is static pressure, ρ fluid density, v flow speed, g acceleration due to gravity, and h height above a reference level. The equation assumes no energy loss to friction and that the flow is irrotational.

Typical applications illustrate the trade‑off between pressure and velocity. In a Venturi meter a constriction forces the fluid to accelerate, lowering the pressure; the pressure difference is then used to determine flow rate. An aircraft wing (airfoil) is shaped so that air moves faster over the upper surface than the lower, creating a pressure difference that generates lift. Carburettors in older internal‑combustion engines exploit the same effect: fast‑moving air in a narrow throat draws fuel into the airstream. In medical practice, a Venturi mask delivers a precise oxygen concentration by entraining room air through a high‑velocity oxygen jet.

Limitations are important for exam questions. Real fluids have viscosity, so a boundary layer forms where velocity drops to zero at the wall; Bernoulli’s equation does not hold inside this layer. Compressibility becomes significant at Mach numbers above ~0.3, requiring the full energy equation with enthalpy. Turbulent or unsteady flows also violate the steady‑flow assumption. When a problem states “ideal fluid” or “negligible viscosity”, Bernoulli may be applied; otherwise the examiner expects you to recognise the need for the Navier‑Stokes equations or empirical loss coefficients.

Dimensionless numbers help decide the regime. The Reynolds number Re = ρvL/μ compares inertial to viscous forces; low Re (laminar) flows approximate the ideal conditions better than high Re (turbulent) flows. The Mach number Ma = v/a (where a is the speed of sound) signals compressibility effects. A typical multiple‑choice trap gives a high‑speed gas flow and asks for the pressure drop using Bernoulli; the correct answer is “cannot be determined without compressibility corrections”.

Thermodynamics: Laws, Entropy, and Heat Engines

The four laws of thermodynamics form the backbone of energy analysis. The zeroth law establishes thermal equilibrium and the concept of temperature. The first law, ΔU = Q – W, states that the change in internal energy U of a closed system equals heat Q added to the system minus work W done by the system. Sign conventions vary; in physics W is work done by the system, while in chemistry it is often work done on the system. The second law introduces entropy S, a measure of energy dispersal: for any spontaneous process in an isolated system ΔS ≥ 0. The Clausius inequality ∮ δQ/T ≤ 0 quantifies this for cyclic processes. The third law asserts that the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as temperature approaches absolute zero.

Heat engines convert heat into work by operating between a hot reservoir at temperature Tₕ and a cold reservoir at T𝒸. The Carnot cycle, consisting of two isothermal and two adiabatic reversible steps, defines the maximum possible efficiency: ηCarnot = 1 – T𝒸/Tₕ (temperatures in kelvin). No real engine can exceed this limit. Practical engines (Otto, Diesel, Brayton) have lower efficiencies due to irreversibilities such as friction, finite temperature differences during heat transfer, and non‑ideal gas behaviour. Refrigerators and heat pumps are reverse heat engines; their coefficient of performance (COP) for a Carnot refrigerator is COPR = T𝒸/(Tₕ – T𝒸) and for a heat pump COPHP = Tₕ/(Tₕ – T𝒸).

Entropy change for a reversible process is ΔS = ∫ δQrev/T. For an ideal gas undergoing an isothermal expansion, ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁). In irreversible processes the actual entropy increase is larger than ∫ δQ/T. The concept of availability (exergy) combines the first and second laws to assess the maximum useful work obtainable from a system as it comes to equilibrium with the environment.

Common exam twists include: (i) asking for the efficiency of a cycle described by a PV diagram — you must identify the heat input and work output from the area enclosed; (ii) giving a real‑engine efficiency and asking whether it violates the second law — compare with Carnot efficiency at the same reservoir temperatures; (iii) mixing units (Celsius instead of kelvin) in the Carnot formula — a frequent source of arithmetic errors.

Dimensional Analysis Check
A student writes the efficiency of a Carnot engine as η = (Tₕ – T𝒸)/Tₕ. Verify whether the expression is dimensionally consistent.
Both numerator and denominator are temperatures, so their ratio is dimensionless. Efficiency is defined as a pure number (or percentage), therefore the expression is dimensionally correct. The same check would fail if the student wrote η = (Tₕ – T𝒸) because the result would have units of temperature.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the Carnot efficiency formula η = 1 – T𝒸/Tₕ and the COP expressions. When a question gives a claimed efficiency, instantly compute the Carnot limit using the reservoir temperatures in kelvin; any value higher than the Carnot limit is automatically impossible, allowing you to eliminate options quickly.

Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure: VSEPR, Hybridisation, and Intermolecular Forces

The Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) model predicts molecular geometry by minimising repulsion between electron domains (bonding pairs and lone pairs) around a central atom. The sequence of electron‑domain geometries for 2 to 6 domains is: linear (180°), trigonal planar (120°), tetrahedral (109.5°), trigonal bipyramidal (90°/120°), and octahedral (90°). Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs, so they compress adjacent bond angles; for example, water (two bonding pairs, two lone pairs) has a bent shape with an H–O–H angle of about 104.5°, less than the tetrahedral angle.

Hybridisation provides an orbital picture consistent with VSEPR geometries. sp hybridisation (one s + one p) yields two linear orbitals (e.g., BeCl₂). sp² (one s + two p) gives three trigonal planar orbitals (e.g., BF₃). sp³ (one s + three p) forms four tetrahedral orbitals (e.g., CH₄). For five domains, sp³d (one s + three p + one d) produces trigonal bipyramidal orbitals (e.g., PCl₅). Six domains use sp³d² (one s + three p + two d) for octahedral geometry (e.g., SF₆). The number of hybrid orbitals always equals the number of electron domains.

Intermolecular forces (IMFs) govern physical properties such as boiling point, viscosity, and solubility. The weakest are London dispersion forces, present in all molecules, arising from instantaneous dipoles; their magnitude increases with polarisability (larger electron clouds). Dipole‑dipole forces act between permanent dipoles (e.g., HCl). Hydrogen bonding is a special, strong dipole‑dipole interaction occurring when hydrogen is covalently bonded to N, O, or F and interacts with a lone pair on another N, O, or F. It explains the anomalously high boiling points of water, ammonia, and hydrogen fluoride.

Relative strength order: hydrogen bonding > dipole‑dipole > London dispersion. However, for large non‑polar molecules (e.g., I₂) dispersion forces can outweigh dipole‑dipole forces in small polar molecules. This nuance appears in questions comparing boiling points of isomers: n‑pentane (linear, larger surface area) boils higher than neopentane (spherical) despite both being non‑polar. Solubility follows “like dissolves like”: polar solvents dissolve polar/ionic solutes via ion‑dipole or hydrogen‑bonding interactions; non‑polar solvents dissolve non‑polar solutes via dispersion forces.

Electrochemistry: Redox Reactions, Electrochemical Cells, and Applications

Redox (reduction‑oxidation) reactions involve electron transfer. Oxidation is loss of electrons (increase in oxidation number), reduction is gain of electrons (decrease in oxidation number). The half‑reaction method balances redox equations in acidic or basic media: separate into oxidation and reduction half‑reactions, balance atoms other than O and H, balance O with H₂O, balance H with H⁺ (acidic) or H₂O/OH⁻ (basic), balance charge with electrons, equalise electron count, and add half‑reactions. The oxidising agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidised.

A galvanic (voltaic) cell converts spontaneous redox energy into electrical work. The anode is the site of oxidation (negative electrode), the cathode the site of reduction (positive electrode). Cell notation writes the anode on the left, cathode on the right, with phase boundaries indicated by a single vertical line and a salt bridge by a double line, e.g., Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s). The standard cell potential E°cell = E°cathode – E°anode. The Nernst equation relates the instantaneous cell potential to reaction quotient Q: E = E° – (RT/nF) ln Q. At 298 K, RT/F ≈ 0.0257 V, so E = E° – (0.0591/n) log₁₀ Q.

Standard electrode potentials are tabulated relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE, defined as 0 V). A more positive reduction potential indicates a stronger oxidising agent. The electrochemical series predicts spontaneity: a redox reaction is spontaneous if E°cell > 0. Electrolytic cells drive non‑spontaneous reactions using external voltage; the minimum voltage required is the decomposition potential, slightly higher than the thermodynamic E°cell due to overpotential.

Faraday’s laws quantify electrolysis: (1) mass of substance liberated is proportional to charge passed (m = ZIt, where Z is electrochemical equivalent); (2) masses liberated by the same charge are proportional to their equivalent weights. Applications include rechargeable batteries (lead‑acid: PbO₂ + Pb + 2H₂SO₄ ⇌ 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O; lithium‑ion: LiCoO₂ + C₆ ⇌ LiC₆ + CoO₂), corrosion (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ at anode, O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ at cathode), and electroplating (e.g., Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) on cathode).

Optics: Wave‑Particle Duality, Interference, Diffraction, and Polarisation

Light exhibits both wave and particle characteristics. The photon energy is E = hν = hc/λ, where h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s. The de Broglie wavelength for matter is λ = h/p, but for light the wave description suffices for interference and diffraction phenomena.

Young’s double‑slit experiment produces bright fringes where the path difference equals an integer multiple of the wavelength: d sinθ = mλ (m = 0, ±1, ±2…). For small angles, fringe width on a screen at distance D is β = λD/d. Coherence (constant phase relationship) and monochromaticity are essential; white light yields coloured fringes with a central white maximum.

Single‑slit diffraction minima occur at a sinθ = mλ (m = ±1, ±2…), where a is slit width. The central maximum is twice as wide as the secondary maxima. The intensity distribution follows I(θ) = I₀ (sinα/α)² with α = (πa sinθ)/λ. A diffraction grating with N slits produces principal maxima at the same angles as double‑slit but with much sharper peaks; the resolving power is R = mN.

Polarisation restricts the electric‑field vector to a single plane. Unpolarised light passing through a polariser emerges with intensity I = I₀/2. For a second polariser (analyser) at angle θ to the first, Malus’s law gives I = I₀ cos²θ. Brewster’s angle θB satisfies tanθB = n₂/n₁; at this angle reflected light is perfectly s‑polarised. Applications include polarising sunglasses (reduce glare), LCDs (twisted nematic cells rotate polarisation), and optical activity measurements (sugar solutions rotate plane of polarisation).

Thermodynamics: Laws, Heat Engines, Entropy, and Real‑World Cycles

The zeroth law establishes thermal equilibrium as a transitive relation, allowing temperature to be defined as a state function. The first law, ΔU = Q – W (sign convention: work done by the system is positive), expresses energy conservation for any process. For a cyclic process the net change in internal energy is zero, so the net heat absorbed equals the net work delivered. The second law introduces entropy (S) and states that for any spontaneous process in an isolated system ΔS ≥ 0. The Clausius inequality, ∮ δQ/T ≤ 0, leads to the definition of entropy change for a reversible path: dS = δQ_rev/T. The third law asserts that the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as temperature approaches absolute zero, providing an absolute reference for entropy values.

Heat engines operate between two reservoirs at temperatures T_H (hot) and T_C (cold). The Carnot cycle, composed of two isothermal and two adiabatic reversible steps, sets the maximum possible efficiency: η_Carnot = 1 – T_C/T_H (temperatures in kelvin). Real engines (Otto, Diesel, Brayton, Rankine) deviate because of irreversibilities — friction, finite temperature differences, non‑ideal gas behaviour — so their efficiencies are lower. The Otto cycle (spark‑ignition) has efficiency η = 1 – 1/r^(γ‑1) where r is the compression ratio and γ = C_p/C_v. The Diesel cycle (compression‑ignition) adds a constant‑pressure heat‑addition step, giving η = 1 – (1/r^(γ‑1))·[(ρ^γ‑1)/(γ(ρ‑1))] with ρ the cut‑off ratio. The Brayton cycle (gas turbine) uses two adiabatic and two isobaric processes; its ideal efficiency depends only on the pressure ratio: η = 1 – (1/r_p)^((γ‑1)/γ). The Rankine cycle (steam power) incorporates phase change; superheating and reheating raise the average temperature of heat addition, improving efficiency.

Entropy change for an ideal gas undergoing a reversible process from (T₁, V₁) to (T₂, V₂) is ΔS = nC_v ln(T₂/T₁) + nR ln(V₂/V₁). For a reversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas, ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁) = Q_rev/T. In irreversible free expansion (Joule expansion) into vacuum, Q = 0, W = 0, ΔU = 0, but ΔS > 0 because the process is not quasi‑static; the entropy increase equals nR ln(V₂/V₁). The concept of availability (exergy) quantifies the maximum useful work obtainable when a system comes to equilibrium with a reference environment at T₀, P₀: Φ = (U – U₀) + P₀(V – V₀) – T₀(S – S₀). This underpins second‑law analysis of power plants and refrigeration cycles.

Refrigerators and heat pumps are reverse heat engines. Their coefficient of performance (COP) for a Carnot refrigerator is COP_R = T_C/(T_H – T_C); for a Carnot heat pump COP_HP = T_H/(T_H – T_C). Real vapour‑compression cycles use a refrigerant that undergoes evaporation, compression, condensation, and throttling. The throttling (Joule‑Thomson) valve is an isenthalpic irreversible step that generates entropy. Sub‑cooling and super‑heating improve COP by moving the cycle closer to the Carnot limit.

Chemical Kinetics: Rate Laws, Mechanisms, Catalysis, and Temperature Dependence

The rate of a reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time, typically expressed as rate = –(1/ν_i) d[ i ]/dt where ν_i is the stoichiometric coefficient. For an elementary step the rate law follows directly from molecularity: a unimolecular step is first order (rate = k[A]), a bimolecular step is second order (rate = k[A][B] or k[A]²). The overall rate law for a complex reaction is determined experimentally and may not match the stoichiometry; the reaction order with respect to each species is the exponent in the empirical rate law.

The Arrhenius equation, k = A exp(–E_a/RT), relates the rate constant k to temperature T, activation energy E_a, and pre‑exponential factor A (frequency factor). Taking natural logs gives ln k = ln A – E_a/(RT); a plot of ln k versus 1/T yields a straight line of slope –E_a/R. The Eyring equation from transition‑state theory expresses k = (k_B T/h) exp(–ΔG‡/RT) where ΔG‡ = ΔH‡ – TΔS‡ is the Gibbs free energy of activation. This formulation separates enthalpic and entropic contributions to the barrier.

Reaction mechanisms are sequences of elementary steps that sum to the overall stoichiometry. The rate‑determining step (RDS) is the slowest step and controls the overall rate law. For a mechanism with a fast pre‑equilibrium followed by a slow step, the steady‑state approximation or the equilibrium approximation can be used to eliminate intermediates. Example: the decomposition of N₂O₅ proceeds via N₂O₅ ⇌ NO₂ + NO₃ (fast), NO₂ + NO₃ → NO₂ + O₂ + NO (slow), NO + NO₃ → 2 NO₂ (fast). The observed rate law is rate = k[N₂O₅] (first order).

Catalysts provide an alternative pathway with lower activation energy without being consumed. Homogeneous catalysts operate in the same phase (e.g., acid‑catalysed ester hydrolysis), while heterogeneous catalysts act at a surface (e.g., Fe in Haber‑Bosch ammonia synthesis). Enzyme catalysis follows Michaelis‑Menten kinetics: v₀ = (V_max [S])/(K_M + [S]), where V_max = k_cat[E]_total and K_M = (k_‑1 + k_cat)/k1. Competitive inhibitors increase apparent K_M; non‑competitive inhibitors decrease V_max; uncompetitive inhibitors decrease both.

Temperature also affects equilibrium constants via the van ’t Hoff equation: d ln K_eq / dT = ΔH°/(RT²). For exothermic reactions (ΔH° < 0) K_eq decreases with temperature; for endothermic reactions it increases. This thermodynamic constraint is distinct from the kinetic Arrhenius behaviour, but both must be considered in reactor design.

Worked Example: Determining Activation Energy
A reaction has rate constants k₁ = 2.5 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at 300 K and k₂ = 1.2 × 10⁻² s⁻¹ at 320 K. Calculate the activation energy E_a in kJ mol⁻¹.
Use the two‑point Arrhenius form: ln(k₂/k₁) = –E_a/R (1/T₂ – 1/T₁).
ln(1.2×10⁻² / 2.5×10⁻³) = ln(4.8) ≈ 1.5686.
1/T₂ – 1/T₁ = 1/320 – 1/300 = (300 – 320)/(320·300) = –20/96000 = –2.0833×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.
Thus 1.5686 = –E_a/8.314 × (–2.0833×10⁻⁴) → E_a = 1.5686 × 8.314 / 2.0833×10⁻⁴ ≈ 62,600 J mol⁻¹ = 62.6 kJ mol⁻¹.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question gives two rate constants at two temperatures, write the two‑point Arrhenius equation immediately; it avoids solving for A and reduces algebraic errors. Remember that R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and convert the final answer to kJ mol⁻¹ if the options are in those units.

Nuclear Physics: Radioactive Decay, Nuclear Reactions, and Applications

Radioactive decay follows first‑order kinetics: N(t) = N₀ e^(–λt) where λ is the decay constant. Half‑life t₁/₂ = ln 2 / λ. Activity A = λN (becquerel, Bq). Common decay modes: α‑emission (He nucleus, reduces Z by 2, A by 4), β⁻‑emission (neutron → proton + e⁻ + ν̅_e, Z increases by 1), β⁺‑emission / electron capture (proton → neutron + e⁺ + ν_e or p + e⁻ → n + ν_e, Z decreases by 1), γ‑emission (photon, no change in Z or A). Decay series (e.g., U‑238 → Pb‑206) involve multiple α and β steps until a stable nuclide is reached.

Nuclear binding energy per nucleon peaks near Fe‑56 (~8.8 MeV). For light nuclei fusion releases energy; for heavy nuclei fission releases energy. The semi‑empirical mass formula (Weizsäcker) approximates binding energy: B = a_v A – a_s A^(2/3) – a_c Z(Z‑1)/A^(1/3) – a_a (A‑2Z)²/A + δ(A,Z). The volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing terms explain trends in stability.

Induced nuclear reactions are written as a + X → Y + b. The Q‑value = (m_initial – m_final)c². Positive Q means exothermic. For neutron‑induced fission of U‑235: n + ²³⁵U → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3 n + ~200 MeV. The released neutrons sustain a chain reaction if the multiplication factor k ≥ 1. Critical mass depends on geometry, purity, and reflector. Control rods (B₄C, Hf, Gd) absorb neutrons to regulate k.

Fusion reactions of interest: D + T → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV (most feasible for magnetic confinement), D + D → ³He + n (3.27 MeV) or T + p (4.03 MeV). The Lawson criterion for a self‑sustaining plasma: nτ_E T > 3×10²¹ keV·s·m⁻³ (for D‑T). Magnetic confinement (tokamak, stellarator) and inertial confinement (laser‑driven) pursue this condition.

Applications: Radiocarbon dating uses ¹⁴C (t₁/₂ = 5730 yr) with the assumption of constant atmospheric ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio; calibration curves correct for historic variations. Medical isotopes: ⁹⁹ᵐTc (t₁/₂ = 6 h) for imaging, ¹³¹I (t₁/₂ = 8 d) for thyroid therapy. Industrial gauging uses β or γ sources for thickness measurement. Nuclear power reactors (PWR, BWR, PHWR) convert fission heat to electricity via Rankine cycles; fast breeder reactors aim to convert ²³⁸U to ²³⁹Pu.

Biochemistry: Central Metabolic Pathways, Regulation, and Clinical Correlates

Glycolysis converts one glucose to two pyruvate, yielding a net gain of 2 ATP (substrate‑level) and 2 NADH. Key regulated steps: hexokinase (inhibited by glucose‑6‑phosphate), phosphofructokinase‑1 (PFK‑1, activated by AMP, fructose‑2,6‑bisphosphate; inhibited by ATP, citrate), pyruvate kinase (activated by fructose‑1,6‑bisphosphate; inhibited by ATP, alanine). In anaerobic conditions pyruvate → lactate (via lactate dehydrogenase) regenerates NAD⁺ for glycolysis to continue.

The citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) oxidises acetyl‑CoA to 2 CO₂, producing 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, and 1 GTP per turn. Regulation: citrate synthase (inhibited by ATP, NADH, succinyl‑CoA), isocitrate dehydrogenase (activated by ADP, Ca²⁺; inhibited by ATP, NADH), α‑ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (inhibited by succinyl‑CoA, NADH). Anaplerotic reactions (e.g., pyruvate carboxylase) replenish oxaloacetate.

Oxidative phosphorylation couples electron transport (Complex I–IV) to ATP synthesis via chemiosmotic proton gradient. P/O ratios: NADH ≈ 2.5 ATP, FADH₂ ≈ 1.5 ATP. Uncouplers (e.g., 2,4‑dinitrophenol) dissipate the gradient, releasing heat. Inhibitors: rotenone (Complex I), antimycin A (Complex III), cyanide (Complex IV), oligomycin (ATP synthase).

Gluconeogenesis synthesises glucose from non‑carbohydrate precursors (lactate, amino acids, glycerol). It bypasses three irreversible glycolytic steps using pyruvate carboxylase/PEP carboxykinase, fructose‑1,6‑bisphosphatase, and glucose‑6‑phosphatase. Reciprocal regulation ensures glycolysis and gluconeogenesis do not run simultaneously: high insulin/low glucagon favours glycolysis; low insulin/high glucagon favours gluconeogenesis.

Glycogen metabolism: glycogen synthase (activated by insulin via dephosphorylation) and glycogen phosphorylase (activated by glucagon/epinephrine via phosphorylation cascade). Glycogen storage diseases (e.g., von Gierke disease, glucose‑6‑phosphatase deficiency) cause hypoglycaemia, hepatomegaly, hyperlipidaemia.

Lipid metabolism: β‑oxidation of fatty acids in mitochondria yields acetyl‑CoA, NADH, FADH₂. Each cycle shortens the chain by two carbons. Ketogenesis in liver mitochondria produces acetoacetate, β‑hydroxybutyrate, acetone during prolonged fasting or uncontrolled diabetes. Regulation: malonyl‑CoA (product of acetyl‑CoA carboxylase) inhibits carnitine palmitoyltransferase‑I, the rate‑limiting step for fatty‑acid entry into mitochondria.

Mineral Nutrition: Essential Minerals, Trace Elements, Deficiency, and Toxicity

Macrominerals (required >100 mg/day): calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na), potassium (K), chloride (Cl), sulfur (S). Calcium: 99% in bone as hydroxyapatite; serum Ca²⁺ tightly regulated by parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcitriol, calcitonin. Deficiency → rickets (children), osteomalacia (adults), osteoporosis. Excess → hypercalcaemia, nephrolithiasis, vascular calcification. Phosphorus: component of ATP, nucleic acids, phospholipids; balance with Ca regulated by PTH and FGF‑23. Magnesium: cofactor for >300 enzymes (e.g., Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase, kinases); deficiency causes neuromuscular irritability, arrhythmia.

Thermodynamics and Statistical Mechanics in Chemical Reactions

The first law (ΔU = Q – W) and the second law (ΔS ≥ Q/T) together define the direction and extent of chemical change. For a reaction at constant temperature and pressure the Gibbs free energy ΔG = ΔH – TΔS determines spontaneity: ΔG < 0 proceeds forward, ΔG = 0 at equilibrium, ΔG > 0 requires coupling. The equilibrium constant K is linked to standard Gibbs energy by ΔG° = –RT ln K, where R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. The Van’t Hoff equation, d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/(RT²), shows how K varies with temperature; an exothermic reaction (ΔH° < 0) has K decreasing as T rises.

Statistical mechanics connects macroscopic thermodynamics to molecular states. The canonical partition function Q = Σ e^(–Eᵢ/kT) sums over all microstates i with energy Eᵢ. From Q one obtains internal energy U = –∂ln Q/∂β (β = 1/kT), entropy S = k(ln Q + βU), and Helmholtz free energy A = –kT ln Q. For an ideal gas of N indistinguishable particles, Q = qᴺ/N! where q is the molecular partition function (translational, rotational, vibrational, electronic). The Boltzmann distribution gives the population ratio of two states: n₂/n₁ = (g₂/g₁) e^(–ΔE/kT). Transition‑state theory expresses the rate constant k = (kT/h) e^(–ΔG‡/RT), where ΔG‡ is the activation free energy and h is Planck’s constant. The Eyring equation thus links the Arrhenius pre‑exponential factor A to entropy of activation: A = (kT/h) e^(ΔS‡/R).

In practice, the temperature dependence of rate constants is often fitted to the Arrhenius form k = A e^(–Ea/RT). The activation energy Ea relates to ΔH‡ by Ea = ΔH‡ + RT. For solution reactions, the solvent reorganization energy λ appears in Marcus theory: ΔG‡ = (λ + ΔG°)²/(4λ). This predicts the inverted region where very exergonic electron‑transfer reactions become slower. Understanding these relationships allows one to predict how changing temperature, pressure, or solvent will shift both equilibrium position and reaction rate, a frequent theme in advanced chemistry questions.

Electrochemistry: Redox Potentials, Batteries, and Corrosion

Standard electrode potentials (E°) are measured versus the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) at 25 °C, 1 M activity, 1 atm gas pressure. The electrochemical series orders half‑reactions by their reduction potentials; a more positive E° indicates a stronger oxidising agent. The Nernst equation, E = E° – (RT/nF) ln Q, calculates the actual potential under non‑standard conditions, where n is electrons transferred, F = 96 485 C mol⁻¹, and Q is the reaction quotient. For a full cell, Ecell = Ecathode – Eanode = E°cell – (RT/nF) ln Q. At equilibrium Ecell = 0 and Q = K, giving ΔG° = –nFE°cell.

Primary cells (non‑rechargeable) include the zinc‑carbon (Leclanché) cell (E° ≈ 1.5 V) and the alkaline MnO₂/Zn cell (E° ≈ 1.5 V). Secondary cells are rechargeable: lead‑acid (PbO₂/PbSO₄, E°cell ≈ 2.0 V per cell), nickel‑cadmium (NiOOH/Cd, 1.2 V), nickel‑metal‑hydride (NiOOH/MH, 1.2 V), and lithium‑ion (LiCoO₂/graphite, 3.6–3.7 V). Lithium‑ion batteries dominate portable electronics because of high energy density (150–250 Wh kg⁻¹) and low self‑discharge. Fuel cells convert chemical energy directly to electricity; the proton‑exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) uses H₂ oxidation at the anode (2H₂ → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻) and O₂ reduction at the cathode (O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O), delivering ~0.7 V per cell under load.

Corrosion is an electrochemical process where a metal acts as an anode (M → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻) and a cathodic reaction (often O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻) occurs on the same surface. The corrosion rate follows the mixed‑potential theory: the corrosion potential Ecorr is where anodic and cathodic current densities are equal. Protective measures include cathodic protection (sacrificial anodes of Zn or Mg, or impressed current), coatings that block electrolyte access, and alloying (e.g., stainless steel with Cr forming a passive Cr₂O₃ layer). The Pourbaix diagram maps the thermodynamic stability of metal, oxide, and ion species as a function of pH and potential, guiding material selection for specific environments.

Molecular Biology Techniques: PCR, Sequencing, and Gene Editing

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplifies a target DNA segment exponentially. Each cycle comprises denaturation (94–98 °C), annealing (50–65 °C), and extension (72 °C for Taq polymerase). The reaction mix contains template DNA, two primers flanking the target, dNTPs, thermostable DNA polymerase, and Mg²⁺ buffer. After n cycles the theoretical yield is 2ⁿ copies. Variants include reverse‑transcription PCR (RT‑PCR) for RNA templates, quantitative PCR (qPCR) using fluorescent dyes (SYBR Green) or hydrolysis probes (TaqMan) to monitor amplification in real time, and digital PCR for absolute quantification by partitioning.

DNA sequencing determines the nucleotide order. Sanger (chain‑termination) sequencing uses dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs) labelled with fluorophores; fragments are separated by capillary electrophoresis, yielding reads up to ~1 kb. Next‑generation sequencing (NGS) platforms (Illumina, Ion Torrent) massively parallelise sequencing by clustering DNA fragments on a flow cell, achieving gigabases per run. Third‑generation single‑molecule methods (PacBio SMRT, Oxford Nanopore) produce long reads (10–100 kb) without amplification, enabling detection of structural variants and base modifications.

CRISPR‑Cas9 gene editing exploits a guide RNA (gRNA) that base‑pairs with a target DNA sequence adjacent to a protospacer‑adjacent motif (PAM, typically NGG for Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9). The Cas9‑gRNA complex introduces a double‑strand break, repaired by non‑homologous end joining (error‑prone, generating indels) or homology‑directed repair (precise editing with a donor template). Off‑target effects arise from gRNA mismatches; high‑fidelity Cas9 variants (eSpCas9, HiFi Cas9) and truncated gRNAs reduce them. Applications span functional genomics (knock‑out screens), disease modelling, and therapeutic development (ex vivo editing of haematopoietic stem cells for sickle‑cell disease).

Ecological Interactions and Biogeochemical Cycles

Species interactions shape community structure. Predation (+/–) transfers energy upward; classic Lotka‑Volterra equations describe predator‑prey oscillations. Competition (–/–) for limiting resources leads to competitive exclusion or niche partitioning (resource‑use differentiation). Mutualism (+/+) benefits both partners, e.g., mycorrhizal fungi exchanging phosphorus for plant carbohydrates. Commensalism (+/0) benefits one without affecting the other, such as epiphytes on tree trunks. Parasitism (+/–) resembles predation but the parasite usually does not kill the host immediately.

Energy flows through trophic levels with ~10 % transfer efficiency (Lindeman’s rule). Primary producers fix solar energy via photosynthesis; primary consumers (herbivores) ingest producers; secondary and tertiary consumers are carnivores. Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) recycle nutrients, closing the loop. The carbon cycle moves CO₂ between atmosphere, oceans, biosphere, and lithosphere; human combustion of fossil fuels adds ~9 Gt C yr⁻¹, raising atmospheric CO₂. The nitrogen cycle converts N₂ to NH₃ (nitrogen fixation), then to NO₃⁻ (nitrification), and back to N₂ (denitrification). Anthropogenic Haber‑Bosch fixation (~120 Mt N yr⁻¹) exceeds natural fixation, causing eutrophication. Phosphorus lacks a gaseous phase; weathering releases phosphate, which cycles through biota and sediments. Sulphur cycles via volcanic emission, microbial sulphate reduction, and oxidation, influencing acid rain formation.

Nanomaterials: Synthesis, Properties, and Applications

Nanomaterials are classified by dimensionality: zero‑dimensional (quantum dots, nanoparticles), one‑dimensional (nanowires, nanotubes), two‑dimensional (graphene, transition‑metal dichalcogenides), and three‑dimensional (nanocomposites, porous networks). Top‑down methods (ball milling, lithography) carve bulk material; bottom‑up methods (chemical vapour deposition, sol‑gel, hydrothermal synthesis) assemble atoms or molecules. Quantum confinement in particles < 10 nm discretises electronic states, shifting absorption/emission to higher energies (blue shift) — exploited in quantum‑dot displays. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) in noble‑metal nanoparticles (Au, Ag) yields intense visible absorption tunable by size, shape, and dielectric environment, used in colorimetric sensors and photothermal therapy.

Mechanical properties improve dramatically: carbon nanotubes exhibit tensile strength > 50 GPa and Young’s modulus ~1 TPa. Graphene’s thermal conductivity (~5 000 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) and electron mobility (> 200 000 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹) enable high‑frequency transistors. In catalysis, high surface‑to‑volume ratios and exposed crystal facets lower activation barriers; Pt nanoparticles on carbon support are standard in PEM fuel‑cell electrodes. Biomedical applications include drug‑delivery vehicles (liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles) that exploit the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect in tumours, and magnetic iron‑oxide nanoparticles for MRI contrast and magnetic hyperthermia. Environmental uses involve photocatalytic TiO₂ nanoparticles degrading organic pollutants under UV light, and nano‑zero‑valent iron for groundwater remediation of chlorinated solvents.

Thermodynamics and Chemical Equilibrium

The first law of thermodynamics states that energy cannot be created or destroyed; the change in internal energy ΔU of a system equals the heat q added to the system minus the work w done by the system (ΔU = q – w). The second law introduces entropy S, a measure of disorder, and asserts that for any spontaneous process the total entropy of the universe increases (ΔS_universe > 0). The third law defines the entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero as zero. Gibbs free energy G = H – TS combines enthalpy H and entropy S at constant temperature and pressure; a process is spontaneous when ΔG < 0. At equilibrium ΔG = 0, giving the relationship ΔG° = –RT ln K, where K is the equilibrium constant, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, and T is absolute temperature. Le Chatelier’s principle predicts the direction of shift when a system at equilibrium experiences a change in concentration, pressure, or temperature: increasing reactant concentration drives the reaction forward; raising pressure favours the side with fewer gas moles; for an exothermic reaction, increasing temperature shifts equilibrium toward reactants. The van’t Hoff equation (d ln K / dT = ΔH° / RT²) quantifies the temperature dependence of K. In industrial synthesis, the Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃) operates at 400–500 °C and 150–250 atm with an iron catalyst; high pressure favours ammonia formation, while moderate temperature balances rate and equilibrium yield. The contact process for sulfuric acid (2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃) uses V₂O₅ catalyst at 450 °C and 1–2 atm; removal of SO₃ drives the reaction forward. Understanding these principles allows prediction of reaction feasibility, optimisation of conditions, and design of energy‑efficient chemical plants.

Electrochemistry: Redox Reactions and Electrochemical Cells

Redox reactions involve transfer of electrons; oxidation is loss of electrons (increase in oxidation state) and reduction is gain (decrease). Balancing redox equations in acidic solution uses the half‑reaction method: separate oxidation and reduction half‑reactions, balance atoms other than O and H, add H₂O to balance O, add H⁺ to balance H, then add electrons to balance charge. In basic solution, after balancing in acid, add OH⁻ to both sides to neutralise H⁺, forming water. The standard electrode potential E° measures the tendency of a half‑reaction to occur as reduction under standard conditions (1 M, 1 atm, 25 °C). The standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) is defined as 0 V. A galvanic (voltaic) cell couples two half‑cells; the cell potential E°cell = E°cathode – E°anode. The Nernst equation relates the actual cell potential to concentrations: E = E° – (RT / nF) ln Q, where n is electrons transferred, F = 96485 C mol⁻¹, and Q is the reaction quotient. At 25 °C, (RT/F) ln 10 ≈ 0.05916 V, giving E = E° – (0.05916 / n) log₁₀ Q. Primary cells (e.g., alkaline Zn/MnO₂) are non‑rechargeable; secondary cells (lead‑acid, Ni‑Cd, Li‑ion) are rechargeable. The lead‑acid battery reaction: PbO₂ + Pb + 2H₂SO₄ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O, E° ≈ 2.05 V per cell. Lithium‑ion cells intercalate Li⁺ between graphite anode and LiCoO₂ cathode, delivering 3.6–3.7 V with high energy density. Fuel cells convert chemical energy directly to electricity; the proton‑exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cell uses H₂ oxidation at the anode (H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻) and O₂ reduction at the cathode (½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O), producing water and ~1.23 V theoretical voltage. Corrosion is an electrochemical process; iron rusting involves Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ at anodic sites and O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ at cathodic sites, forming Fe(OH)₂ which oxidises to Fe₂O₃·nH₂O. Cathodic protection (sacrificial anodes or impressed current) suppresses corrosion by making the metal a cathode.

Mechanics: Rotational Dynamics and Angular Momentum

Rotational motion analogues of linear quantities: angular displacement θ (rad), angular velocity ω = dθ/dt (rad s⁻¹), angular acceleration α = dω/dt (rad s⁻²). Moment of inertia I = Σ mᵢ rᵢ² for discrete masses; for continuous bodies I = ∫ r² dm. Common values: solid sphere I = 2/5 MR², hollow sphere I = 2/3 MR², solid cylinder I = 1/2 MR², thin rod about centre I = 1/12 ML², about end I = 1/3 ML². Torque τ = r × F = rF sin φ; rotational analogue of Newton’s second law: τ = Iα. Work done by torque: W = ∫ τ dθ = τΔθ (constant τ). Rotational kinetic energy K_rot = ½ I ω². Angular momentum L = Iω for a rigid body about a fixed axis; for a particle L = r × p. Conservation of angular momentum holds when net external torque is zero: L_initial = L_final. Gyroscopic precession: a spinning wheel with angular momentum L subjected to torque τ perpendicular to L precesses with angular velocity Ω = τ / L. The period of precession T = 2π / Ω. Applications include stabilisation of satellites (reaction wheels), navigation (gyrocompass), and the behaviour of a spinning top. In collisions, both linear and angular momentum are conserved if no external forces/torques act. For a rolling object without slipping, v = ωR and total kinetic energy = ½ mv² + ½ I ω². The acceleration of a cylinder rolling down an incline of angle θ: a = g sin θ / (1 + I/mR²). For a solid cylinder I = ½ mR² → a = 2/3 g sin θ; for a hoop I = mR² → a = ½ g sin θ.

Optics: Wave Optics and Optical Instruments

Wave optics treats light as an electromagnetic wave; interference arises from superposition of coherent waves. Young’s double‑slit experiment: fringe spacing β = λD / d, where λ is wavelength, D distance to screen, d slit separation. Constructive interference when path difference = mλ; destructive when = (m + ½)λ. Thin‑film interference: reflected light from top and bottom surfaces interferes; phase change of π occurs on reflection from a medium of higher refractive index. For a film of thickness t and index n, constructive reflection (bright) when 2nt = (m + ½)λ (if one phase reversal) or 2nt = mλ (if two or zero reversals). Diffraction: single‑slit minima at a sin θ = mλ (m ≠ 0); central maximum width ≈ 2λD / a. Diffraction grating: principal maxima at d sin θ = mλ; resolving power R = λ/Δλ = mN, where N is number of illuminated slits. Polarisation: transverse waves can be polarised; Malus’s law I = I₀ cos² θ for intensity after a polariser at angle θ. Brewster’s angle θ_B satisfies tan θ_B = n₂/n₁; reflected light is perfectly s‑polarised. Optical instruments: simple microscope (magnifying glass) angular magnification M = D/f + 1 (near point D ≈ 25 cm). Compound microscope: objective forms real, inverted, magnified image; eyepiece acts as simple microscope. Total magnification M_total = (L / f_obj) × (D / f_eye), where L is tube length. Telescope: astronomical (refracting) uses objective focal length f_o and eyepiece f_e; angular magnification M = –f_o / f_e (negative indicates inverted image). Reflecting telescope (Newtonian) uses a parabolic primary mirror; same magnification formula. Resolving power of a telescope (Rayleigh criterion) θ_min = 1.22 λ / D, where D is aperture diameter. Aberrations: spherical aberration (marginal rays focus closer), chromatic aberration (different λ focus at different points), coma, astigmatism. Achromatic doublet combines converging and diverging lenses of different glasses to bring two wavelengths to common focus.

Wave‑particle duality: de Broglie wavelength λ = h / p, where h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. Davisson‑Germer experiment confirmed electron diffraction. Thermodynamics: Laws, Entropy, and Real‑World Cycles

The four laws of thermodynamics form the quantitative backbone of energy conversion. The zeroth law establishes thermal equilibrium and defines temperature as a transitive property. The first law, ΔU = Q – W, states that the change in internal energy of a closed system equals heat added to the system minus work done by the system; sign conventions vary, but the convention used in most physics textbooks treats work done by the system as positive. The second law introduces entropy (S) and asserts that for any spontaneous process in an isolated system ΔS ≥ 0. The Clausius inequality, ∮ δQ/T ≤ 0, leads to the definition of entropy change for a reversible path: dS = δQ_rev/T. The third law states that the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as temperature approaches absolute zero, providing an absolute reference for entropy values.

Entropy is a state function; its change between two equilibrium states depends only on the endpoints. For an ideal gas undergoing a reversible isothermal expansion from V₁ to V₂, ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁). In a reversible adiabatic process ΔS = 0, while irreversible adiabatic expansion generates entropy. The Carnot cycle, composed of two isotherms and two adiabats, sets the maximum possible efficiency for a heat engine operating between reservoirs at temperatures T_h and T_c: η_Carnot = 1 – T_c/T_h (temperatures in kelvin). Real engines (Otto, Diesel, Brayton) fall short because of irreversibilities such as friction, finite‑rate heat transfer, and non‑ideal gas behaviour.

Thermodynamic potentials — Helmholtz free energy (F = U – TS) and Gibbs free energy (G = H – TS) — are useful for constant‑volume and constant‑pressure processes respectively. At constant temperature and pressure, a process is spontaneous if ΔG < 0. The Maxwell relations, derived from the equality of mixed second derivatives of the potentials, connect measurable quantities: (∂T/∂V)_S = –(∂P/∂S)_V, (∂T/∂P)_S = (∂V/∂S)_P, (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂P/∂T)_V, (∂S/∂P)_T = –(∂V/∂T)_P. These relations enable the calculation of entropy changes from PVT data.

Phase equilibria are governed by the Clapeyron equation, dP/dT = ΔS/ΔV = ΔH/(TΔV), and its integrated form, the Clausius–Clapeyron equation, ln(P₂/P₁) = –ΔH_vap/R (1/T₂ – 1/T₁), which describes the vapour‑pressure curve of a pure substance. The triple point (for water, 0.01 °C, 611.657 Pa) and critical point (for water, 374 °C, 22.064 MPa) are key reference states. Real‑world power cycles (Rankine, combined‑cycle) exploit phase change of water/steam; their thermal efficiencies are analysed using T‑s and h‑s diagrams, where the area enclosed by the cycle represents net work.

Chemical Kinetics: Rate Laws, Mechanisms, and Catalysis

Chemical kinetics quantifies how fast reactions proceed and elucidates the sequence of elementary steps — the reaction mechanism. The rate of a reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time, typically expressed as rate = –(1/ν_i) d[ i ]/dt, where ν_i is the stoichiometric coefficient. For an elementary reaction A + B → products, the rate law follows the molecularity: rate = k[A][B]. The rate constant k depends on temperature according to the Arrhenius equation, k = A exp(–E_a/RT), where A is the pre‑exponential factor, E_a the activation energy, R the gas constant, and T the absolute temperature. A plot of ln k versus 1/T yields a straight line of slope –E_a/R.

Complex reactions often exhibit rate laws that do not match overall stoichiometry. The method of initial rates, integrated rate laws, and half‑life analysis are standard tools to determine reaction order. For a first‑order process, ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt and t₁/₂ = ln 2/k; for a second‑order process, 1/[A] – 1/[A]₀ = kt and t₁/₂ = 1/(k[A]₀). The steady‑state approximation assumes that the concentration of a reactive intermediate remains low and constant (d[intermediate]/dt ≈ 0), allowing algebraic elimination of the intermediate from the rate expression. The rate‑determining step (RDS) is the slowest elementary step; the overall rate law is governed by the molecularity of the RDS and the concentrations of species that appear before it.

Catalysis provides an alternative pathway with lower activation energy. Homogeneous catalysts exist in the same phase as reactants (e.g., transition‑metal complexes in solution), while heterogeneous catalysts operate at a phase boundary (e.g., solid Pt in the Haber‑Bosch synthesis of ammonia). Enzyme catalysis follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics: v₀ = (V_max [S])/(K_m + [S]), where V_max = k_cat[E]_total and K_m = (k₋₁ + k_cat)/k₁. Lineweaver–Burk double‑reciprocal plots (1/v₀ versus 1/[S]) linearise the data for determination of K_m and V_max. Inhibitors modify these parameters: competitive inhibitors increase apparent K_m without affecting V_max; non‑competitive inhibitors decrease V_max while K_m remains unchanged; uncompetitive inhibitors decrease both.

Temperature, pressure, solvent, and ionic strength influence rate constants. The Eyring equation, derived from transition‑state theory, expresses k = (k_B T/h) exp(–ΔG‡/RT), where ΔG‡ = ΔH‡ – TΔS‡ is the Gibbs free energy of activation. Isotope effects (e.g., k_H/k_D ≈ 6–7 for C–H bond cleavage) provide mechanistic insight because zero‑point energy differences alter ΔH‡. Photochemical reactions obey the Stark–Einstein law: each photon absorbed activates one molecule; quantum yield Φ = (number of events)/(number of photons absorbed) can exceed unity in chain reactions.

Worked Example: Determining Activation Energy from Two Rate Constants
A reaction has rate constants k₁ = 2.5 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at 300 K and k₂ = 1.2 × 10⁻² s⁻¹ at 320 K. Calculate the activation energy E_a in kJ mol⁻¹.
Use the two‑point Arrhenius form: ln(k₂/k₁) = –E_a/R (1/T₂ – 1/T₁).
ln(1.2×10⁻² / 2.5×10⁻³) = ln(4.8) ≈ 1.5686.
1/T₂ – 1/T₁ = 1/320 – 1/300 = (300 – 320)/(320×300) = –20/96000 = –2.0833×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.
Thus 1.5686 = –E_a/8.314 × (–2.0833×10⁻⁴) → E_a = 1.5686 × 8.314 / 2.0833×10⁻⁴ ≈ 62,600 J mol⁻¹ = 62.6 kJ mol⁻¹.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a kinetics question supplies two temperatures and two rate constants, immediately write the two‑point Arrhenius equation; it avoids solving for the pre‑exponential factor and reduces algebraic errors. Keep R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ in mind and convert the final answer to kJ mol⁻¹ as required.

Human Metabolic Pathways: Glycolysis, Citric Acid Cycle, and Oxidative Phosphorylation

Cellular respiration converts glucose into ATP through three interconnected stages. Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol and splits one glucose (C₆) into two pyruvate (C₃) molecules, yielding a net gain of 2 ATP (substrate‑level phosphorylation) and 2 NADH. The ten enzymatic steps are regulated at three irreversible points: hexokinase (or glucokinase in liver), phosphofructokinase‑1 (PFK‑1), and pyruvate kinase. PFK‑1 is the principal flux‑controlling enzyme; it is allosterically activated by AMP and fructose‑2,6‑bisphosphate and inhibited by ATP and citrate.

Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion where pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDH) oxidatively decarboxylates it to acetyl‑CoA, producing one NADH per pyruvate. The citric acid (Krebs) cycle then oxidises the two‑carbon acetyl group to two CO₂, generating per acetyl‑CoA: 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, and 1 GTP (equivalent to ATP). Key regulatory enzymes are citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and α‑ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, all inhibited by high NADH/NAD⁺ and ATP/ADP ratios. Anaplerotic reactions (e.g., pyruvate carboxylase forming oxaloacetate) replenish cycle intermediates withdrawn for biosynthesis.

Oxidative phosphorylation couples electron transport to ATP synthesis. The mitochondrial inner membrane hosts four respiratory complexes (I–IV) and ATP synthase (Complex V). NADH donates electrons to Complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase), FADH₂ to Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase). Electrons flow through ubiquinone (Q), Complex III (cytochrome bc₁), cytochrome c, and Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) to reduce O₂ to H₂O. The exergonic electron flow drives proton pumping from matrix to intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient (Δp = ΔΨ – 2.303RT/F ΔpH). Protons re‑enter the matrix through ATP synthase, rotating the F₀ subunit and catalysing ADP + Pi → ATP in the F₁ sector. The P/O ratios are approximately 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH₂, reflecting the number of protons translocated per electron pair (10 for NADH, 6 for FADH₂) and the ~4 protons required per ATP (3 for synthesis, 1 for transport).

Regulation of oxidative phosphorylation matches ATP demand. High ADP stimulates respiration (respiratory control); uncouplers (e.g., 2,4‑dinitrophenol) dissipate Δp, increasing O₂ consumption without ATP synthesis. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) arise mainly at Complex I and III; superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase mitigate oxidative damage. Mitochondrial DNA encodes 13 protein subunits of the respiratory complexes; mutations cause diseases such as MELAS and Leber hereditary optic neuropathy.

Vitamin Biochemistry: Coenzyme Roles, Deficiency Syndromes, and Therapeutic Windows

Vitamins function primarily as precursors of coenzymes that assist enzymes in catalysis. Water‑soluble B‑vitamins are converted to coenzymes rapidly; fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) require dietary lipid for absorption and are stored in liver and adipose tissue. Thiamine (B₁) becomes thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), essential for pyruvate dehydrogenase, α‑ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and transketolase in the pentose phosphate pathway. Deficiency causes beriberi (wet: cardiovascular; dry: peripheral neuropathy) and Wernicke‑Korsakoff syndrome in chronic alcoholism.

Riboflavin (B₂) forms flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), prosthetic groups for Complex I, Complex II, and acyl‑CoA dehydrogenase in β‑oxidation. Niacin (B₃) yields NAD⁺/NADH and NADP⁺/NADPH, central to redox reactions and biosynthetic reductions. Pantothenic acid (B₅) is a component of Coenzyme A and acyl‑carrier protein, indispensable for acyl‑group transfer. Pyridoxine (B₆) becomes pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), the coenzyme for transamination, decarboxylation, and glycogen phosphorylase. Biotin (B₇) attaches to carboxylases (acetyl‑CoA carboxylase, pyruvate carboxylase) via a lysine‑biotin linkage. Folate (B₉) as tetrahydrofolate (THF) carries one‑carbon units for purine and thymidylate synthesis; deficiency leads to megaloblastic anaemia and neural‑tube defects. Cobalamin (B₁₂) as methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin participates in methionine synthase and methylmalonyl‑CoA mutase; deficiency causes subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord and megaloblastic anaemia.

Fat‑soluble vitamins have distinct non‑coenzyme roles. Vitamin A (retinol) forms retinal for rhodopsin vision cycle and retinoic acid for gene transcription via nuclear receptors. Vitamin D₃ (cholecalciferol) is hydroxylated to 25‑OH‑D₃ in liver and 1,25‑(OH)₂‑D₃ (calcitriol) in kidney; calcitriol binds the vitamin D receptor to regulate calcium homeostasis. Vitamin E (α‑tocopherol) is a lipid‑soluble antioxidant that terminates lipid‑peroxyl radicals in membranes. Vitamin K (phylloquinone, menaquinones) acts as a cofactor for γ‑glutamyl carboxylase, enabling γ‑carboxylation of glutamate residues on clotting factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C and S.

Therapeutic windows vary: water‑soluble vitamins exhibit low toxicity because excess is excreted, but high‑dose niacin can cause flushing and hepatotoxicity; vitamin B₆ > 100 mg/day may produce sensory neuropathy. Fat‑soluble vitamins accumulate; chronic vitamin A > 3000 µg RAE/day causes hypervitaminosis A (hepatotoxicity, teratogenicity); vitamin D excess leads to hypercalcaemia and soft‑tissue calcification. Recommended dietary allowances (RDAs) are set to meet the needs of 97–98 % of healthy individuals; tolerable upper intake levels (ULs) define the maximum unlikely to cause adverse effects.

Electromagnetic Wave Propagation: Maxwell’s Equations, Waveguides, and Antenna Fundamentals

Maxwell’s equations in differential form unify electricity and magnetism: ∇·D = ρ_f, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = –∂B/∂t, ∇×H = J_f + ∂D/∂t. In a source‑free, linear, isotropic medium (ρ_f = 0, J_f = 0) with permittivity ε and permeability μ, these reduce to the wave equations ∇²E – με ∂²E/∂t² = 0 and ∇²H – με ∂²H/∂t² = 0. Plane‑wave solutions have E, H, and propagation vector k mutually orthogonal; the intrinsic impedance η = √(μ/ε) relates field magnitudes (E = η H). In free space η₀ ≈ 377 Ω, phase velocity c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s.

At a planar interface between media 1 and 2, boundary conditions require continuity of tangential E and H, and normal D and B. For normal incidence, the reflection coefficient Γ = (η₂ – η₁)/(η₂ + η₁) and transmission coefficient τ = 2η₂/(η₂ + η₁). For oblique incidence, Fresnel equations give separate coefficients for transverse electric (TE, s‑polarisation) and transverse magnetic (TM, p‑polarisation) waves. Brewster’s angle θ_B satisfies tan θ_B = n₂/n₁; at this angle TM reflection vanishes. Total internal reflection occurs when n₁ > n₂ and incidence exceeds the critical angle θ_c = arcsin(n₂/n₁); the reflected wave acquires a phase shift that depends on polarisation.

Waveguides confine electromagnetic energy. Rectangular metallic waveguides support transverse electric (TE_mn) and transverse magnetic (TM_mn) modes; the dominant mode is TE₁₀ with cutoff wavelength λ_c = 2a (a = broad dimension). Below cutoff the mode is evanescent (exponential decay). Phase velocity v_p = c/√(1 – (f_c/f)²) exceeds c, while group velocity v_g = c√(1 – (f_c/f)²) is less than c; their product v_p v_g = c². Dielectric waveguides (optical fibres) guide light by total internal reflection; step‑index fibres have a core of index n₁ and cladding n₂ < n₁. The normalized frequency V = (2πa/λ) NA, where numerical aperture NA = √(n₁² – n₂²). Single‑mode operation requires V < 2.405; multimode fibres support many modes, causing modal dispersion.

Antennas convert guided waves to free‑space radiation. The Hertzian dipole (length ℓ ≪ λ) has far‑field E_θ = j η₀ k I₀ ℓ sinθ e-jkr/(4πr) and radiation resistance R_rad = 80π²(ℓ/λ)². A half‑wave dipole (ℓ = λ/2) exhibits R_rad ≈ 73 Ω and a gain of 2.15 dBi. Array antennas (e.g., Yagi‑Uda, phased arrays) shape the radiation pattern through constructive interference; the array factor multiplies the element pattern. Friis transmission equation relates received power P_r = P_t G_t G_r (λ/4πR)², where G_t, G_r are antenna gains and R is distance. Polarisation mismatch loss is cos²ψ, where ψ is the angle between incident E‑field and receiving antenna polarisation.
