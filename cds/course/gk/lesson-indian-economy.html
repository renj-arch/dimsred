1. National Income Accounting and Measurement

1.1 The Concept of Domestic Territory and Residence

National income accounting begins with a precise definition of the economic territory because the boundary determines which production activities are counted. The domestic territory of India includes the political frontiers, territorial waters, airspace, and Indian embassies, consulates, and military establishments located abroad; it excludes foreign embassies and international organisations located within India. This geographical definition underpins Gross Domestic Product, which measures the value of final goods and services produced within this territory regardless of whether the producers are Indian nationals or foreigners. In contrast, the concept of residence is based on the centre of economic interest: a person or institution is a resident if they ordinarily reside in the territory for more than one year and conduct economic transactions from there. This distinction explains why the profits earned by a foreign-owned factory in Gujarat enter India's GDP but not its GNP, while the wages of an Indian engineer working in Dubai enter India's GNP but not its GDP.

1.2 Gross Domestic Product and Its Variants

Gross Domestic Product at market prices is the aggregate money value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory during an accounting year. The term "gross" signifies that no deduction has been made for the consumption of fixed capital, commonly called depreciation, which represents the wear and tear of machinery and infrastructure. "Market prices" indicate that the valuation includes indirect taxes such as GST and excludes subsidies; therefore GDP at market prices equals GDP at factor cost plus net indirect taxes. The Central Statistics Office, now subsumed under the National Statistical Office, estimates GDP using three equivalent approaches: the production method sums gross value added across agriculture, industry, and services; the income method aggregates compensation of employees, operating surplus, and mixed income of the self-employed; and the expenditure method adds private final consumption expenditure, government final consumption expenditure, gross fixed capital formation, change in stocks, and net exports of goods and services. Because the three methods measure the same circular flow from different angles, they yield identical totals barring statistical discrepancies.

1.3 Real versus Nominal GDP and the Deflator

Nominal GDP is calculated at the prevailing prices of the current year, so a rise in nominal GDP may reflect higher output, higher prices, or both. To isolate the change in physical output, economists compute Real GDP by valuing current-year quantities at the constant prices of a chosen base year, which for the current series is 2011-12. The ratio of Nominal GDP to Real GDP, multiplied by one hundred, yields the GDP deflator, a broad measure of price inflation covering all domestically produced goods and services. Unlike the Consumer Price Index, which uses a fixed basket of consumption items, the GDP deflator automatically adjusts its basket every year to reflect the changing composition of output, making it a more comprehensive though less timely indicator of inflationary pressures in the economy.

1.4 Gross National Product and Net National Product

Gross National Product extends the domestic concept by adding net factor income from abroad, which is the difference between factor income earned by Indian residents from the rest of the world and factor income paid to non-residents operating in India. In India's case, remittances from the large diaspora and investment income on foreign assets typically exceed outflows, so GNP has historically been larger than GDP. Net National Product at factor cost, often referred to as National Income, is derived by subtracting depreciation from GNP at market prices and then adjusting for net indirect taxes. Per capita income, obtained by dividing NNP at factor cost by the mid-year population, serves as a rough proxy for average living standards, though it masks inequality and does not capture non-market welfare.

1.5 The Production, Income, and Expenditure Methods

The production approach calculates Gross Value Added for each sector by subtracting intermediate consumption from the value of output, thereby avoiding double counting of raw materials. The income approach distributes this value added among the factors of production: wages and salaries to labour, rent to land, interest to capital, and profit to entrepreneurship, with mixed income accruing to own-account workers such as farmers and artisans. The expenditure approach views GDP from the demand side, classifying final demand into consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports. In India, private final consumption expenditure consistently accounts for roughly fifty-five to sixty percent of GDP, making domestic demand the primary engine of growth, while gross fixed capital formation has hovered around twenty-eight to thirty-two percent, reflecting the investment rate necessary to sustain high growth.

2. Economic Growth, Development, and Structural Transformation

2.1 Distinguishing Growth from Development

Economic growth is a quantitative concept measured by the rate of increase in real GDP or per capita income over time, whereas economic development is a qualitative and multidimensional process encompassing improvements in health, education, nutrition, gender equity, and environmental sustainability. A country can experience growth without development if the gains are concentrated in a narrow sector or if they degrade the natural resource base; conversely, development without growth is difficult to sustain because public finances for social spending depend on an expanding tax base. The distinction matters for policy: growth-oriented reforms focus on removing supply-side bottlenecks and improving factor productivity, while development-oriented interventions target capability expansion through public provision of merit goods such as primary healthcare and elementary education.

2.2 The Human Development Index and India's Position

The United Nations Development Programme introduced the Human Development Index in 1990 as a composite measure combining life expectancy at birth, mean and expected years of schooling, and gross national income per capita adjusted for purchasing power parity. India's HDI value has risen steadily from 0.427 in 1990 to 0.644 in the 2022 report, placing the country in the medium human development category and ranking it 134 out of 193 nations. The improvement has been driven largely by gains in life expectancy, which crossed seventy years, and gross enrolment ratios in secondary education, though learning outcomes measured by surveys such as ASER remain a concern. The Inequality-adjusted HDI, which discounts the average value according to the depth of inequality, reveals a loss of approximately twenty-five percent, signalling that the benefits of growth are unevenly distributed across states and social groups.

2.3 Sectoral Composition of the Indian Economy

Structural transformation describes the shift of output and employment from agriculture to industry and services as per capita income rises. At independence, agriculture contributed over fifty percent of GDP; by 2023-24 its share had fallen to roughly eighteen percent, while the services sector expanded to contribute more than fifty percent, led by trade, hotels, transport, communication, financial services, and public administration. Industry, comprising manufacturing, mining, construction, and utilities, has remained stuck around twenty-five percent, a phenomenon economists describe as "premature deindustrialisation" because the manufacturing share peaked at about sixteen percent before the income level typical of mature industrialisers was reached. The employment structure has lagged behind output: agriculture still engages over forty-two percent of the workforce, implying low labour productivity and widespread disguised unemployment, while modern services employ a relatively small fraction despite their large output share.

2.4 Occupational Structure and the Informal Economy

The Periodic Labour Force Survey conducted by the National Statistical Office reveals that more than ninety percent of India's workforce is employed in the informal sector, characterised by lack of written contracts, social security coverage, and regulatory protection. This informality persists across both rural and urban areas and spans self-employment, casual wage labour, and informal jobs within formal enterprises. The dominance of informal employment constrains tax revenues, limits the reach of labour laws, and makes workers vulnerable to shocks such as the COVID-19 lockdown, which caused a sharp spike in unemployment and reverse migration. Recent labour codes passed in 2020 aim to extend social security to gig and platform workers and simplify compliance, but implementation remains a state-level challenge because labour falls under the Concurrent List of the Constitution.

Worked Example: Calculating Real GDP Growth
An economy produces only wheat and cloth. In the base year 2011-12, it produces 100 tonnes of wheat at Rs 1,000 per tonne and 50 metres of cloth at Rs 2,000 per metre. In the current year 2023-24, it produces 120 tonnes of wheat at Rs 1,500 per tonne and 60 metres of cloth at Rs 2,500 per metre. Calculate the Real GDP for 2023-24 at 2011-12 prices and the Real GDP growth rate.
Real GDP uses base-year prices. Base year GDP = (100 × 1,000) + (50 × 2,000) = Rs 1,00,000 + Rs 1,00,000 = Rs 2,00,000. Real GDP for 2023-24 = (120 × 1,000) + (60 × 2,000) = Rs 1,20,000 + Rs 1,20,000 = Rs 2,40,000. Real GDP growth = (2,40,000 − 2,00,000) ÷ 2,00,000 × 100 = 20%. Note that Nominal GDP for 2023-24 would be (120 × 1,500) + (60 × 2,500) = Rs 3,30,000, giving a GDP deflator of (3,30,000 ÷ 2,40,000) × 100 = 137.5, indicating 37.5% cumulative inflation since the base year.
Exam Insight: Base Year Revision
The CSO revises the national accounts base year periodically (1993-94, 2004-05, 2011-12). When a new base year is adopted, the level of GDP usually rises because of better coverage of the services sector and updated methodology. CDS questions often ask for the current base year (2011-12) or the impact of base-year change on growth rates; remember that growth rates are recalculated backwards for comparability, so the growth rate for a past year may differ between the old and new series.

3. Money, Banking, and Monetary Policy

3.1 Evolution and Functions of the Reserve Bank of India

The Reserve Bank of India was established on 1 April 1935 under the Reserve Bank of India Act 1934 as a shareholders' bank and was nationalised on 1 January 1949, transferring all share capital to the central government. As the monetary authority, the RBI formulates and implements monetary policy to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth, a mandate formalised by the 2016 amendment to the RBI Act which created the Monetary Policy Committee. The RBI acts as banker to the central and state governments, managing their public debt and providing ways and means advances; as banker to banks, it maintains the cash reserves of scheduled commercial banks and acts as lender of last resort; as currency authority, it has the sole right to issue banknotes under Section 22 of the RBI Act; and as regulator, it supervises commercial banks, cooperative banks, non-banking financial companies, and payment systems under the Banking Regulation Act 1949 and the Payment and Settlement Systems Act 2007.

3.2 Instruments of Monetary Policy

The Monetary Policy Committee, headed by the RBI Governor and comprising three RBI deputies and three external members appointed by the government, meets at least four times a year to set the policy repo rate, which is the rate at which the RBI lends overnight to banks against government securities under the Liquidity Adjustment Facility. The reverse repo rate, at which the RBI borrows from banks, typically remains one hundred basis points below the repo rate and serves as the floor of the interest rate corridor. The Cash Reserve Ratio requires banks to maintain a fraction of their net demand and time liabilities as non-interest-bearing balances with the RBI, directly affecting the money multiplier. The Statutory Liquidity Ratio mandates investment in approved government securities, influencing credit availability for the private sector. Open market operations, including outright purchase and sale of securities and the Market Stabilisation Scheme, are used for durable liquidity management. In 2020, the RBI introduced the Standing Deposit Facility, an uncollateralised absorption tool, to manage surplus liquidity more flexibly.

3.3 Commercial Banking Structure and Reforms

The Indian banking system comprises scheduled commercial banks, which are listed in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act, and non-scheduled banks. Scheduled commercial banks are classified into public sector banks, where the government holds a majority stake; private sector banks, including old and new generation entities; foreign banks operating through branches or wholly owned subsidiaries; regional rural banks sponsored by public sector banks to serve rural credit needs; and specialised institutions such as small finance banks and payments banks licensed under differentiated guidelines to promote financial inclusion. The Narasimham Committee reports of 1991 and 1998 laid the blueprint for banking reforms: reduction of statutory pre-emptions, introduction of prudential norms for income recognition, asset classification, and provisioning, capital adequacy requirements aligned with Basel norms, and autonomy for public sector banks through board-driven governance. The Indradhanush framework launched in 2015 addressed the seven key areas of appointments, capitalisation, de-stressing, empowerment, framework of accountability, governance reforms, and bank board bureau.

3.4 Non-Performing Assets and the Insolvency Framework

A non-performing asset is a loan or advance where interest or principal remains overdue for more than ninety days. The gross NPA ratio of scheduled commercial banks peaked at 11.2 percent in March 2018 following the asset quality review initiated by the RBI in 2015, which forced transparent recognition of stressed assets. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 created a time-bound, creditor-driven resolution process under the adjudication of the National Company Law Tribunal, replacing the fragmented earlier regime of SICA, RDDBFI Act, and SARFAESI Act. The Code distinguishes between the resolution process, which aims to revive the firm as a going concern, and liquidation, which maximises asset recovery. The establishment of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India as regulator and the creation of information utilities to store financial data have improved the information asymmetry that previously hampered recovery. As of March 2024, the gross NPA ratio has fallen below three percent, reflecting both resolution of large accounts and write-offs.

4. Public Finance, Budgeting, and Fiscal Policy

4.1 Constitutional Provisions and the Budget Cycle

Article 112 of the Constitution requires the President to cause to be laid before Parliament an Annual Financial Statement, commonly called the Union Budget, showing estimated receipts and expenditure for the financial year beginning 1 April. Until 2017, the budget was presented on the last working day of February; since then it has been advanced to 1 February to enable passage before the new fiscal year begins, eliminating the need for a vote on account. The budget goes through six stages: presentation by the Finance Minister, general discussion, departmental scrutiny by standing committees, voting on demands for grants, passage of the Appropriation Bill authorising withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund, and passage of the Finance Bill giving effect to tax proposals. The Railway Budget, presented separately since 1924, was merged with the General Budget in 2017-18 following the recommendations of the Bibek Debroy Committee.

4.2 Revenue and Capital Accounts

The budget distinguishes between revenue and capital accounts on both the receipt and expenditure sides. Revenue receipts comprise tax revenue (direct and indirect) and non-tax revenue (interest, dividends, fees, spectrum auctions) and do not create liabilities or reduce assets. Capital receipts include market borrowings, external loans, recoveries of loans, and disinvestment proceeds; they either create a liability (borrowing) or reduce an asset (disinvestment). Revenue expenditure covers interest payments, salaries, pensions, subsidies, and grants to states; it is recurring and does not create assets. Capital expenditure creates physical or financial assets such as roads, bridges, machinery, and equity investment in public enterprises, or reduces liabilities through loan repayment. The classification matters because the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act targets the revenue deficit, which is the excess of revenue expenditure over revenue receipts, as a measure of current fiscal profligacy.

4.3 Deficit Concepts and the FRBM Act

Fiscal deficit is the difference between total expenditure and total receipts excluding borrowings; it indicates the total borrowing requirement of the government from all sources. Primary deficit equals fiscal deficit minus interest payments, showing the borrowing needed to meet current expenditure net of past debt service. Effective revenue deficit, introduced in the 2012-13 budget, subtracts grants for creation of capital assets from the revenue deficit to focus on consumption expenditure. The FRBM Act 2003, amended in 2018, mandates the central government to reduce the fiscal deficit to three percent of GDP and the revenue deficit to zero, with an escape clause allowing a deviation of up to 0.5 percent of GDP in case of structural reforms or severe economic contraction. The 2021-22 budget invoked this clause to set a glide path targeting 4.5 percent by 2025-26. The Act also requires the government to place a Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement and a Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement before Parliament along with the budget.

4.4 Tax Structure: Direct and Indirect Taxes

Direct taxes are levied on the income, wealth, or profit of persons and corporations and cannot be shifted to another party; the incidence and impact fall on the same entity. The principal direct taxes are income tax on individuals, Hindu Undivided Families, and firms under the Income Tax Act 1961, and corporate tax on domestic and foreign companies under the Finance Act. The Direct Taxes Code, intended to simplify the law, has been under discussion for over a decade but not yet enacted. Indirect taxes are levied on the manufacture, sale, or consumption of goods and services; the impact is on the producer or seller, but the incidence is shifted to the consumer through higher prices. Before 2017, the indirect tax landscape was fragmented: the centre levied central excise on manufacture, service tax on services, and customs duties on imports, while states levied VAT on intra-state sales, central sales tax on inter-state sales, and entry taxes. This multiplicity created cascading effects, compliance burden, and barriers to a common market.

4.5 The Goods and Services Tax Architecture

The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act 2016 paved the way for the Goods and Services Tax, which subsumed most central and state indirect taxes into a single dual GST effective 1 July 2017. The GST is a destination-based consumption tax levied at every stage of value addition with seamless input tax credit across the supply chain. It operates through three components: Central GST levied by the centre on intra-state supplies, State GST levied by states on intra-state supplies, and Integrated GST levied by the centre on inter-state supplies and imports, with revenue apportioned between the centre and the destination state. The GST Council, a constitutional body chaired by the Union Finance Minister and comprising state finance ministers, decides rates, exemptions, thresholds, and dispute resolution by a three-fourths majority of weighted votes (centre one-third, states two-thirds). The four-tier rate structure (5%, 12%, 18%, 28%) plus a cess on sin and luxury goods aims to balance revenue neutrality with progressivity, though frequent rate changes have complicated compliance.

5. Economic Reforms since 1991 and External Sector

5.1 The Crisis of 1991 and the LPG Strategy

The balance of payments crisis of 1990-91, triggered by the Gulf War oil shock, loss of remittances from the Gulf, and political instability, brought India's foreign exchange reserves down to 1.1 billion dollars, barely enough for two weeks of imports. The Chandra Shekhar government pledged gold to the Bank of England and the Union Bank of Switzerland to secure emergency loans, and the incoming Narasimha Rao government, with Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister, negotiated a structural adjustment programme with the IMF. The 1991 budget dismantled the licence raj by abolishing industrial licensing for all but eighteen industries, reduced the peak customs duty from 150 percent to 65 percent, announced automatic approval for foreign direct investment up to fifty-one percent in thirty-four high-priority industries, and initiated the phased reduction of the statutory liquidity ratio and cash reserve ratio to free resources for the private sector. This trinity of liberalisation, privatisation, and globalisation shifted the development paradigm from state-led import substitution to market-oriented integration with the world economy.

5.2 Industrial Policy and Disinvestment

The 1991 Industrial Policy Statement reduced the number of industries reserved for the public sector from seventeen to eight and later to three: atomic energy, space, and defence equipment. The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act was amended to remove the threshold asset limit for large firms, eliminating the need for prior government approval for expansion, mergers, and new undertakings. Disinvestment began with the sale of minority stakes in public sector undertakings through auction and later through the Offer for Sale route on stock exchanges. The National Investment Fund was created in 2005 to channel disinvestment proceeds into social sector schemes. In 2021, the government announced a new Public Sector Enterprise Policy classifying sectors as strategic and non-strategic; in strategic sectors, a bare minimum presence will be maintained, while in non-strategic sectors, privatisation or closure will be pursued. The strategic sale of Air India to the Tata Group in 2022 marked the first full privatisation of a major airline in two decades.

5.3 Balance of Payments and Foreign Exchange Management

The Balance of Payments is a systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of India and the rest of the world in an accounting year. It comprises the current account, which records exports and imports of goods (trade balance), services (invisibles), primary income (investment income, compensation of employees), and secondary income (remittances, grants); and the capital and financial account, which records foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, external commercial borrowings, banking capital, and reserve assets. India has run a persistent merchandise trade deficit, financed by a surplus in services (especially software and business services) and private transfers (remittances), resulting in a current account deficit that averaged 1-2 percent of GDP in the pre-pandemic decade. The Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999 replaced the draconian FERA 1973, shifting the focus from conservation to management of foreign exchange and making violations civil rather than criminal offences. The RBI manages the exchange rate through a managed float, intervening to curb excessive volatility without targeting a specific level.

5.4 Foreign Direct Investment versus Portfolio Investment

Foreign Direct Investment implies a lasting interest and significant influence over the management of an enterprise, typically defined as ownership of ten percent or more of voting power. FDI brings technology, managerial know-how, and access to global markets, and is considered stable because it involves physical assets. The government permits FDI under the automatic route (no prior approval) or the government route (approval required) depending on the sector; sensitive sectors such as defence, media, and multi-brand retail have caps and conditions. Foreign Portfolio Investment refers to investment in financial assets such as equity and debt securities without management control; it is more volatile, driven by global risk appetite and interest rate differentials. The SEBI (Foreign Portfolio Investors) Regulations 2019 streamlined registration and investment limits. The distinction matters for policy: FDI is encouraged through liberalisation, while FPI flows are monitored for macro-financial stability, and the RBI may impose temporary restrictions on debt inflows during periods of excessive volatility.

6. Social Sector, Poverty Alleviation, and Key Institutions

6.1 Poverty Estimation Methodologies

Poverty measurement in India has evolved from the calorie-based poverty lines of the Alagh Committee (1979) and Lakdawala Committee (1993) to the consumption-expenditure-based lines of the Tendulkar Committee (2009) and the Rangarajan Committee (2014). The Tendulkar Committee shifted the reference basket from calories to a mixed basket of food, education, health, and transport, and used a uniform poverty line basket across rural and urban areas, yielding a poverty ratio of 21.9 percent for 2011-12. The Rangarajan Committee recommended higher expenditure thresholds and separate rural-urban baskets, estimating poverty at 29.5 percent for the same year. Since 2011-12, no official consumption expenditure survey has been finalised; the 2017-18 survey was withheld due to data quality concerns. The NITI Aayog introduced the Multidimensional Poverty Index in 2021, aligned with the global MPI, measuring deprivations across health, education, and living standards using twelve indicators; the 2023 progress review reported a decline in the multidimensionally poor from 24.85 percent in 2015-16 to 14.96 percent in 2019-21.

6.2 Employment Generation and Labour Reforms

The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005 provides a legal guarantee of one hundred days of wage employment per year to every rural household whose adult members volunteer for unskilled manual work. The scheme is demand-driven, self-targeting, and creates durable assets such as water conservation structures and rural roads; wages are linked to the Consumer Price Index for Agricultural Labourers. The Periodic Labour Force Survey, launched in 2017-18, replaced the quinquennial Employment-Unemployment Survey and provides annual estimates of labour force participation, worker population ratio, and unemployment rate by usual status and current weekly status.

Fiscal Federalism and the Role of Finance Commissions

India’s fiscal architecture rests on a constitutional division of revenue powers between the Union and the States. The Union collects the bulk of direct taxes (income tax, corporate tax) and indirect taxes (customs, excise, service tax before GST), while States rely heavily on taxes on land, stamps, state excise, and the Goods and Services Tax (GST) share. To address vertical and horizontal imbalances, Article 280 mandates the constitution of a Finance Commission every five years. The Commission recommends the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Centre and the States (vertical devolution) and the allocation among States (horizontal devolution) based on criteria such as population, income distance, area, forest cover, and demographic performance.

The Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015‑20) raised the States’ share in the divisible pool from 32 % to 42 %, the largest single jump in history, and introduced a “performance‑based” grant component for local bodies. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25) retained the 41 % vertical share (adjusted for the creation of the Union Territories of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh) and added a 1 % weight for demographic performance to reward States that have controlled population growth. It also recommended a separate grant for health, education, and disaster relief, and a fiscal consolidation roadmap that targets a combined debt‑to‑GDP ratio of 60 % by 2025‑26.

Horizontal devolution formulas have evolved from a heavy reliance on 1971 population (used until the Fourteenth Commission) to the 2011 Census base in the Fifteenth Commission, reducing the advantage of high‑population States. The “income distance” criterion — the gap between a State’s per‑capita GSDP and the highest per‑capita GSDP — remains the dominant equalising factor, accounting for roughly 45 % of the weight. Forest cover and ecology receive 10 % weight, recognising the opportunity cost of conservation. The Commission also recommends grants‑in‑aid under Article 275 for States with revenue deficits after devolution, and specific purpose grants for nutrition, rural roads, and urban infrastructure.

Implementation of Commission recommendations is not automatic; the Union government accepts them through a presidential order. While the Centre has largely honoured the vertical share, disagreements arise over the treatment of cesses and surcharges, which are excluded from the divisible pool. The introduction of GST in 2017 created a new fiscal federalism layer: the GST Council, a constitutional body, decides rates and compensation. The Centre guaranteed a 14 % annual growth in States’ GST revenue for five years, financed through a compensation cess. The pandemic‑induced shortfall led to a dispute over borrowing options, eventually resolved by allowing States to borrow under a special window with the Centre servicing the interest.

Emerging challenges include the growing share of non‑shareable cesses (e.g., health, education, road cesses) that erode the divisible pool, and the demand for a permanent “fiscal council” to monitor compliance with FRBM targets at both levels. The Sixteenth Finance Commission, to be constituted for 2026‑31, will need to balance the imperatives of equity, efficiency, and fiscal sustainability amid rising debt levels and climate‑finance requirements.

Inflation Targeting Framework and the Monetary Policy Committee

India formally adopted a flexible inflation‑targeting regime in February 2015 through an agreement between the Government and the Reserve Bank of India. The framework was given statutory backing by the Finance Act 2016, which amended the RBI Act to establish the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC). The MPC comprises six members — three from the RBI (including the Governor as chair) and three external members appointed by the Government for a four‑year term. Decisions are taken by majority vote; the Governor has a casting vote in case of a tie.

The inflation target is set at 4 % measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI) Combined, with an upper tolerance band of 6 % and a lower band of 2 %. The target is reviewed every five years; the current mandate runs until March 2026. The MPC meets at least four times a year, publishing a resolution statement and minutes after each meeting. Since its inception, the MPC has shifted the policy stance from “accommodative” to “neutral” and then to “withdrawal of accommodation” as inflation dynamics evolved.

Transmission of policy rates to bank lending rates has improved with the introduction of the external benchmark‑linked lending rate (EBLR) system in October 2019, requiring banks to link new floating‑rate loans to the repo rate, the 91‑day Treasury bill yield, or the 182‑day Treasury bill yield. The RBI also conducts variable‑rate reverse repo auctions and open market operations to manage liquidity within the corridor defined by the standing deposit facility (SDF) and the marginal standing facility (MSF).

Supply‑side shocks — such as food price spikes, fuel price adjustments, and global commodity cycles — test the credibility of the framework. The MPC’s communication strategy includes forward guidance through the “Monetary Policy Report” and the “State of the Economy” speeches. Empirical studies suggest that inflation expectations have become better anchored since 2016, but the persistence of core inflation above 4 % in several periods indicates the need for complementary fiscal and structural measures.

The framework also interacts with the external sector: capital flow volatility can force the RBI to intervene in the foreign exchange market, affecting domestic liquidity. The RBI’s “managed float” exchange rate regime, combined with the inflation target, creates a trilemma where monetary autonomy, exchange rate stability, and capital mobility cannot be simultaneously perfected. The MPC’s minutes routinely discuss the trade‑off between supporting growth and containing inflation, especially during the COVID‑19 pandemic when the repo rate was cut to a historic low of 4 % and subsequently raised in a calibrated manner to 6.5 % by early 2023.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: "Which of the following statements about the Monetary Policy Committee is correct? (a) It has seven members. (b) External members are appointed by the RBI. (c) The Governor has a casting vote. (d) The inflation target is 5 % with a ±1 % band."
The correct answer is (c). The MPC has six members (three RBI, three external). External members are appointed by the Central Government, not the RBI. The inflation target is 4 % with a ±2 % band. The Governor’s casting vote is explicitly provided in the amended RBI Act.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the MPC composition, voting rule, and the exact inflation target (4 % ±2 %). Questions often test the distinction between the RBI’s internal members and government‑appointed external members, and the casting vote provision. Keep a one‑line note: “6 members, 3+3, Governor casts tie‑breaker, target 4 % CPI ±2 %”.

Financial Sector Stability: Basel Norms, NBFC Regulation, and the Insolvency Regime

India’s banking stability framework aligns with the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision standards. The RBI adopted Basel II in 2009 and began phased implementation of Basel III capital, leverage, and liquidity requirements from 2013. The Capital Adequacy Ratio (CAR) minimum was raised to 9 % (11.5 % including capital conservation buffer) for scheduled commercial banks, with a Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) minimum of 5.5 %. The leverage ratio floor is 4 % and the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) reached 100 % in 2019. The Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) became applicable from October 2021.

Non‑Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs) have grown to hold roughly 25 % of total credit. The RBI introduced a scale‑based regulatory framework in October 2021, classifying NBFCs into Base, Middle, Upper, and Top layers based on asset size, interconnectedness, and supervisory inputs. Upper‑layer NBFCs face bank‑like norms: higher CAR, LCR, and mandatory board‑level risk committees. The framework also caps exposure to a single borrower at 20 % of Tier 1 capital and to a group at 25 %.

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) 2016 created a unified resolution mechanism for corporate debtors, including financial service providers (via a separate notification in 2019). The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) adjudicate cases. The Code mandates a 180‑day resolution timeline (extendable by 90 days), after which liquidation is triggered. As of March 2024, over 5,000 corporate insolvency resolution processes have been initiated, with a recovery rate averaging 30‑35 % of admitted claims.

Recent amendments (2020, 2021) introduced a pre‑packaged insolvency resolution process for MSMEs, a threshold of ₹1 crore for default initiation, and provisions for cross‑border insolvency under the UNCITRAL Model Law. The RBI’s Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework for banks, revised in 2017, triggers restrictions on dividend distribution, branch expansion, and lending when capital, asset quality, or profitability breach thresholds.

Challenges remain: the large stock of legacy NPAs in public sector banks, the “evergreening” risk in NBFC‑bank lending chains, and the need for a robust secondary market for distressed assets. The establishment of the National Asset Reconstruction Company (NARCL) and the India Debt Resolution Company (IDRCL) in 2021 aims to aggregate and resolve stressed assets above ₹500 crore, complementing the IBC.

Agricultural Price Policy: Minimum Support Prices, Procurement, and Market Reforms

The Minimum Support Price (MSP) regime, administered by the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP), announces prices for 23 mandated crops before each sowing season. MSP is calculated using the A2+FL cost concept (actual paid‑out costs plus imputed value of family labour) and a 50 % margin over it, as recommended by the Swaminathan Commission. Procurement at MSP is undertaken primarily by the Food Corporation of India (FCI) and State agencies for wheat and paddy, which together account for over 80 % of total procurement volume.

Procurement operations are concentrated in a few States — Punjab, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, and Uttar Pradesh for wheat; Punjab, Haryana, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and Odisha for paddy. This geographic skew creates fiscal burdens (food subsidy bill exceeded ₹2.5 lakh crore in 2023‑24) and storage pressures. The Open Market Sale Scheme (OMSS) and the Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PMGKAY) are the main disposal channels.

Market reforms aim to diversify selling options. The Farmers’ Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act 2020 allowed barrier‑free inter‑State trade and electronic trading platforms, but was repealed in 2021 after farmer protests. The Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act 2020 deregulated stock‑holding limits for cereals, pulses, oilseeds, and onions except under extraordinary circumstances. The Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act 2020 provided a framework for contract farming; it was also repealed.

Current policy emphasises “MSP guarantee” through legal backing, decentralised procurement, and promotion of Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs). The Agriculture Infrastructure Fund (₹1 lakh crore) finances post‑harvest infrastructure. The e‑NAM (National Agriculture Market) platform integrates 1,000 mandis across 18 States, enabling online price discovery. However, only a fraction of transactions occur on e‑NAM due to infrastructure gaps and trader resistance.

Price deficiency payment schemes (e.g., Bhavantar Bhugtan Yojana in Madhya Pradesh) and direct income support (PM‑KISAN, ₹6,000 per year per farmer family) complement MSP. The challenge is to align MSP incentives with crop diversification (pulses, oilseeds, millets) to reduce water‑intensive paddy‑wheat cycles and improve nutritional outcomes.

Infrastructure Financing: Public‑Private Partnerships, the National Investment and Infrastructure Fund, and Bond Market Development

India’s infrastructure investment requirement is estimated at 7‑8 % of GDP annually. Public‑Private Partnership (PPP) models — Build‑Operate‑Transfer (BOT), Build‑Own‑Operate‑Transfer (BOOT), Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM), and Toll‑Operate‑Transfer (TOT) — have been the primary vehicles for road, port, airport, and power projects. The HAM, introduced in 2016 for national highways, shares construction risk (40 % government, 60 % developer) and provides annuity payments during operation, reducing traffic risk for private partners.

The National Investment and Infrastructure Fund (NIIF) was set up in 2015 as a sovereign‑backed fund with an initial corpus of ₹40,000 crore. It operates through three funds: Master Fund (core infrastructure), Fund of Funds (investing in sectoral funds), and Strategic Opportunities Fund (equity in stressed assets). The Government holds 49 % stake; the rest is raised from domestic and international investors including the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority and the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank.

Bond market development is critical for long‑term financing. The RBI has permitted banks to issue infrastructure bonds with a minimum maturity of 7 years, exempt from Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) requirements. The Corporate Bond Market Development Fund (2022) and the “on‑tap” issuance of Government Securities (G‑Secs) for retail investors aim to deepen the yield curve. The Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest (SARFAESI) Act enables asset‑backed securitisation, but the market remains shallow with corporate bonds outstanding at roughly 17 % of GDP.

Recent initiatives include the “Infrastructure Investment Trusts” (InvITs) and “Real Estate Investment Trusts” (REITs) for monetising operational assets, and the “National Monetisation Pipeline” (2021‑25) targeting ₹6 lakh crore through asset leasing. The RBI’s “Framework for Financing of Infrastructure Projects” (2023) allows banks to classify certain infrastructure exposures as “priority sector” and provides relaxed provisioning norms for projects with proven cash flows.

Key constraints: land acquisition delays, regulatory uncertainty, and the absence of a deep secondary market for long‑dated bonds. The proposed Development Finance Institution (DFI) — the National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development (NaBFID), capitalised at ₹20,000 crore — aims to provide long‑tenor credit and catalyse bond market liquidity.
