Mastering Indian Geography

Welcome to your comprehensive guide on Indian Geography. This module is designed for candidates preparing for competitive exams, where questions focus on fundamental facts regarding India's physical landscape, climate patterns, agricultural cycles, and mineral resources. We will break down complex geographical concepts into simple, memorizable rules that you can apply instantly during the exam.

Rivers: The Lifelines of India

River: A river is a large natural stream of water flowing in a channel to the sea, a lake, or another river. In the context of Indian geography, understanding rivers is crucial because they define the boundaries between states and determine agricultural potential.

India's river systems are classified into two main groups: Himalayan Rivers (Perennial) and Peninsular Rivers (Non-perennial). Himalayan Rivers, such as the Ganga, Yamuna, and Brahmaputra, originate in the snow-capped mountains. They flow year-round because they receive water from melting glaciers and heavy rainfall. Peninsular Rivers, like the Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri, rise in the Western Ghats or Eastern Ghats. Unlike their northern cousins, these rivers depend entirely on monsoon rains; if it does not rain, these rivers dry up.

Key Rule for Exams: Always remember that the Ganga is the longest river in India (approx. 2525 km), followed by the Godavari as the longest peninsular river. The Brahmaputra is known for its massive volume of water and frequent flooding.

What the examiner typically asks: Questions often ask you to identify the longest river, the largest river basin (Ganga Basin), or which river flows through a specific state (e.g., Narmada flows through Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra).

Mountains: The Backbone of the Country

Mountain Range: A mountain range is a series of mountains connected by high ground. India's geography is dominated by its massive mountain systems which act as a barrier against cold winds from Central Asia.

The Himalayas are divided into three parallel ranges: the Great Himalayas (Himadri) in the north, the Lesser Himalayas (Himachal) in the middle, and the Outer Himalayas (Shivaliks) at the foothills. To the west lies the Aravalli Range, which is the oldest fold mountain system in India.

Key Rule for Exams: The highest peak in India is Kanchenjunga (8,598 m), located in Sikkim. Note that Kangchenjunga is not in India but on the border; the highest peak entirely within Indian territory is Nanda Devi. For mountain passes, remember that the Bhor Ghat pass connects Mumbai to Pune.

What the examiner typically asks: Questions frequently test the location of specific peaks (e.g., K2 is in Pakistan), the oldest mountain range (Aravallis), or which state a specific peak belongs to.

Climate: The Monsoon System

Climate: Climate refers to the average weather conditions in a region over a long period. India has a Tropical Monsoon climate, meaning it experiences distinct wet and dry seasons driven by seasonal wind shifts.

The Southwest Monsoon (June to September) brings the majority of India's rainfall from the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. The Northeast Monsoon (October to December) affects the southeastern coast, particularly Tamil Nadu. Understanding this is vital because agriculture in India is directly linked to these monsoon patterns.

Key Rule for Exams: The Indian Summer Monsoon enters from the southwest in June and withdraws by September. The retreating monsoon (October-December) brings rain to Tamil Nadu, unlike the rest of India which experiences winter.

Agriculture: Crops and Seasons

Agriculture: Agriculture is the science or practice of farming, including cultivation of the soil for growing crops. In India, crops are categorized based on the season in which they are sown and harvested.

Kharif Crops: These are sown with the onset of the monsoon (June) and harvested in autumn (September-October). Examples include Rice, Maize, Cotton, Jute, and Sugarcane. Rabi Crops: These are sown in winter (October-November) after the monsoon retreats and harvested in spring (April-May). Examples include Wheat, Mustard, Gram, and Peas.

Key Rule for Exams: Remember that Rice is a Kharif crop requiring high water, while Wheat is a Rabi crop requiring cooler temperatures. The Green Revolution transformed Punjab and Haryana into the breadbasket of India by introducing high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice.

Common Traps

  • Mistaking Peninsular River Flow: Students often think all Indian rivers flow south to sea. Remember, Narmada and Tapi flow west into the Arabian Sea, while Godavari and Krishna flow east.
  • Confusing Peak Locations: Do not assume the highest peak is in your home state. Kanchenjunga is in Sikkim, but Kangchenjunga is on the border with Nepal.
  • Season Confusion: Never confuse Rabi and Kharif seasons. If you see "Wheat," think Winter (Rabi). If you see "Rice," think Summer Monsoon (Kharif).

Memory Tricks

  • The 'G' Rule: For rivers flowing East (Ganga, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri), think of the letter 'G' standing for 'Go East'. Narmada and Tapi are exceptions that go West.
  • Monsoon Mnemonic: "Southwest Monsoon brings the rain; Northeast Monsoon saves Tamil Nadu."
  • Crop Seasons: Think of Kharif as "Kharo" (Summer) and Rabi as "Rabi" (Winter in Hindi context).

Exam-Day Strategy
If you do not know a specific river's length, eliminate options based on flow direction. If you forget a crop season, visualize the weather: Is it hot and rainy (Kharif) or cool and dry (Rabi)? Always read the question twice to ensure you are answering about location vs. source.
Example 1: Identifying the Longest River
Question: Which is the longest river in India?

a) Ganga
b) Brahmaputra
c) Yamuna
d) Godavari
Solution: The correct answer is (a) Ganga.

Reasoning: While the Brahmaputra has a larger volume of water, the Ganga is geographically longer within India at approximately 2525 km. The Godavari is the longest river in peninsular India but shorter than the Ganga. The Yamuna is a tributary of the Ganga.
Example 2: Identifying Kharif Crops
Question: Which of the following crops is NOT a Kharif crop?

a) Rice
b) Cotton
c) Wheat
d) Jute
Solution: The correct answer is (c) Wheat.

Reasoning: Kharif crops are sown with the monsoon. Rice, Cotton, and Jute all require heavy rainfall during their growing season. Wheat is a Rabi crop, sown in winter after the monsoon rains recede, making it unsuitable for the Kharif season.

River Regimes and Inter‑Basin Transfers

The Indian river system is split into two fundamentally different hydrological regimes. Himalayan rivers — Ganga, Brahmaputra, Indus and their tributaries — are perennial because they receive meltwater from permanent snowfields and glaciers in addition to monsoon rain. Their mean annual discharge is high; the Ganga at Farakka reportedly averages roughly 12 000 m³ s⁻¹, while the Brahmaputra at Pandu can exceed 19 000 m³ s⁻¹ during peak flow. Peninsular rivers — Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi, Narmada, Tapi — are rain‑fed, showing a sharp seasonal pulse: 70‑80 % of their annual runoff occurs between June and September, and many reaches become dry in the lean season.

Because surplus basins (mainly the Brahmaputra and the lower Ganga) coexist with deficit basins (the Pennar, the Cauvery delta, the Sabarmati), the National Perspective Plan (1980) and the National Water Development Agency (established 1982) identified 30 link projects. The most advanced is the Ken‑Betwa link (Madhya Pradesh–Uttar Pradesh), for which a detailed project report was cleared in 2021 and a tripartite agreement signed in March 2023. The Godavari‑Krishna link (Polavaram project) aims to transfer surplus Godavari water to the Krishna basin; spillway construction has been underway since 2019. Other priority links include the Mahanadi‑Godavari, the Par‑Tapi‑Narmada, and the Damanganga‑Pinjal. Each link requires clearance under the Inter‑State River Water Disputes Act 1956, environmental impact assessment under the Environment (Protection) Act 1986, and techno‑economic appraisal by the Central Water Commission.

Key operational parameters that examiners test: (i) the distinction between snow‑fed and rain‑fed regimes; (ii) the concept of “surplus” versus “deficit” basins; (iii) the institutional framework — NWDA, Central Water Commission, and the River Boards Act 1956; (iv) the status of flagship links (Ken‑Betwa, Polavaram, Par‑Tapi‑Narmada). Remember that the Ken‑Betwa link is the first to reach the implementation stage under the revised 2021 guidelines for inter‑basin transfer.

Himalayan Tectonics and Seismic Hazard Zonation

The Himalaya is the surface expression of the ongoing convergence between the Indian Plate and the Eurasian Plate at a rate of ~5 cm yr⁻¹. This convergence is accommodated along three major thrust systems from north to south: the Main Central Thrust (MCT), the Main Boundary Thrust (MBT), and the Main Frontal Thrust (MFT). The MFT marks the active deformation front and coincides with the Siwalik foothills. Seismicity is concentrated along these thrusts and the associated transverse faults such as the Karakoram Fault and the Mishmi Thrust.

India’s seismic zonation map (IS 1893 Part 1, 2016) divides the country into four zones. Zone V (Very High Damage Risk) covers the entire Kashmir Valley, western Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, the Rann of Kutch, and the North‑East states (Assam, Arunachal, Nagaland, Manipur, Mizoram, Tripura). Zone IV (High Damage Risk) includes the National Capital Territory of Delhi, parts of Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, and the Indo‑Gangetic plains adjacent to the Himalaya. Zone III (Moderate) covers central India, while Zone II (Low) covers the stable peninsular shield.

Historical earthquakes illustrate the hazard: the 1905 Kangra earthquake (M 7.8) on the MBT killed ~20 000; the 1934 Bihar‑Nepal earthquake (M 8.0) ruptured the MFT; the 2001 Bhuj earthquake (M 7.7) in the Kutch rift zone (Zone V) caused widespread damage despite being intraplate; the 2015 Gorkha (Nepal) earthquake (M 7.8) triggered landslides across the central Himalaya. The National Seismic Risk Mitigation Programme (approved 2020) mandates retrofitting of lifeline structures in Zones IV and V and the preparation of micro‑zonation maps for major cities.

Examiner's twist
Which seismic zone includes the National Capital Territory of Delhi according to the IS 1893 (2016) zonation map?
Delhi falls in Zone IV (High Damage Risk). The map places the NCT in Zone IV because it lies on the alluvial plains close to the Himalayan frontal thrust system, making it vulnerable to moderate‑to‑large magnitude events originating in the Himalaya. Zone V is restricted to the Himalayan belt and the Kutch‑Rann region; Zone III covers central India. Therefore the correct answer is Zone IV.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for a seismic zone, first locate the region on the mental map of the four zones. Eliminate Zone V for any area outside the Himalaya, Kutch, or North‑East. Delhi, Chandigarh, and the western Gangetic plains are classic Zone IV examples. If the option list contains “Zone III” for Delhi, discard it immediately.

Monsoon Onset, Breaks, and Teleconnections

The Southwest Monsoon normally sets in over Kerala around 1 June, advancing north‑westward at 10‑15 km day⁻¹ and covering the entire country by mid‑July. The onset is triggered by the northward shift of the Inter‑Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) and the establishment of a low‑level jet (the Somali Jet) that transports moisture from the Arabian Sea. A “break” in the monsoon occurs when the mid‑tropospheric dry air from the subtropical ridge intrudes over the Indian subcontinent, suppressing convection for 3‑7 days. Breaks are most frequent in late July and August and are associated with a northward displacement of the monsoon trough.

Large‑scale ocean‑atmosphere modes modulate the monsoon on interannual timescales. El Niño (warm phase of ENSO) typically weakens the monsoon by reducing the land‑sea thermal contrast and shifting the Walker circulation eastward; the 2002 and 2009 droughts coincided with strong El Niño events. La Niña (cold phase) tends to enhance rainfall, as seen in the excess monsoon years of 2010 and 2011. The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) acts independently: a positive IOD (warmer western Indian Ocean) can partially offset an El Niño‑induced deficit, a mechanism evident in 1997 and 2019. The Madden‑Julian Oscillation (MJO) influences intra‑seasonal variability, with its active phase over the Indian Ocean favouring monsoon bursts.

Operational forecasting by the India Meteorological Department (IMD) uses a combination of statistical models (based on historical predictors such as Nino 3.4, IOD, and Eurasian snow cover) and dynamical global circulation models (e.g., the NCEP‑CFS, the UKMO‑GloSea). Since 2017 IMD issues extended‑range forecasts (up to 4 weeks) and a seasonal outlook in April, updated in June. Examiners often test the timing of onset, the definition of a break, and the sign of ENSO/IOD impacts on all‑India rainfall.

Cropping Systems, Irrigation Efficiency, and Climate Adaptation

India’s cropping intensity (gross cropped area ÷ net sown area) stands at ~140 %, with the rice‑wheat system dominating the Indo‑Gangetic plains, cotton‑wheat in the Deccan plateau, and maize‑wheat in the central highlands. The Green Revolution (mid‑1960s) raised wheat yields from 0.8 t ha⁻¹ to >3 t ha⁻¹ in Punjab and Haryana through semi‑dwarf varieties (e.g., Kalyan Sona, HD 2285) and assured irrigation. However, groundwater depletion in the north‑west (average decline 0.3 m yr⁻¹) and soil salinity in the command areas of the Indira Gandhi Canal have emerged as sustainability concerns.

Irrigation coverage is ~48 % of net sown area. Canal irrigation (major and medium projects) contributes ~30 %, while tube‑wells (electric and diesel) account for ~60 % of the irrigated area. Micro‑irrigation (drip and sprinkler) covers only ~10 % but offers water‑use efficiency of 80‑90 % versus 40‑50 % for flood irrigation. The Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY, launched 2015) targets “Har Khet Ko Pani” through accelerated completion of major/medium projects, command area development (CAD), and promotion of micro‑irrigation with a 55 % subsidy for smallholders.

Climate‑resilient varieties are being deployed under the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA, 2014). Drought‑tolerant rice “Sahbhagi Dhan” (released 2010) yields 4‑5 t ha⁻¹ under rainfed conditions; heat‑tolerant wheat “HD 3086” (2015) maintains grain fill at terminal temperatures >35 °C. The Soil Health Card scheme (2015) provides site‑specific nutrient recommendations, reducing indiscriminate urea use. Examiners focus on the linkage between cropping pattern, irrigation source, and the policy instruments (PMKSY, CAD, NMSA) that aim to raise water productivity and adapt to temperature extremes.

Mineral Belts, Strategic Resources, and Policy Framework

India’s mineral wealth is concentrated in well‑defined geological provinces. The Chotanagpur Plateau (Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal) hosts the largest iron‑ore (hematite) reserves (~25 Gt), high‑grade coal (Jharia, Raniganj), manganese, and bauxite. The Dharwar Craton (Karnataka) contains the Kolar gold field (historic), the Bababudan iron‑ore range, and significant chromite. The Singhbhum Shear Zone (Jharkhand) is the principal copper‑uranium province (e.g., Jaduguda uranium mine). The Aravalli Range (Rajasthan) supplies lead‑zinc (Rampura‑Agucha), wollastonite, and gypsum. The Himalayan belt provides limestone and dolomite; strategic rare‑earth bearing monazite sands occur in the Kerala coastal placer deposits.

Strategic minerals identified in the National Mineral Policy 2019 include lithium (recently discovered in the Reasi district of Jammu & Kashmir), cobalt (associated with copper in Singhbhum), and rare‑earth elements (monazite, xenotime). The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act 1957, amended in 2021, introduced a transparent auction regime for major minerals, mandatory district mineral foundation (DMF) contributions (10 % of royalty for major minerals, 30 % for minor minerals), and a provision for composite licences covering prospecting and mining. The 2021 amendment also enabled the central government to reserve certain blocks for strategic minerals.

Key exam points: (i) match each belt with its flagship mineral (Chotanagpur – iron ore & coal; Dharwar – gold & iron; Singhbhum – copper & uranium; Aravalli – lead‑zinc; Himalaya – limestone & dolomite; Kerala coast – rare earths); (ii) recall the auction route introduced in 2015 for coal and 2021 for all major minerals; (iii) know the DMF share percentages; (iv) recognise that lithium in J&K and rare earths in Kerala are the only domestic sources currently under evaluation for commercial extraction.

  • Confusing the perennial nature of Himalayan rivers with the seasonal flow of Peninsular rivers and misidentifying a river’s regime in a map‑based question.
  • Assigning Delhi to Seismic Zone V instead of Zone IV because of its proximity to the Himalaya.
  • Stating that El Niño always causes a monsoon failure, ignoring the moderating role of a positive Indian Ocean Dipole.
  • Treating micro‑irrigation coverage as >30 % of irrigated area, while the actual figure is near 10 %.
  • Linking the Kolar gold field to the Chotanagpur belt rather than the Dharwar Craton.
  • Assuming the 2021 MMDR amendment removed the District Mineral Foundation, whereas it retained and revised the contribution rates.
  • Believing that the Ken‑Betwa link is already operational; it is still under construction with the first phase expected by 2027.

Groundwater Governance and Aquifer Mapping

India extracts roughly 250 km³ of groundwater each year, making it the largest user of groundwater in the world. Annual recharge is estimated at about 430 km³, but the spatial distribution is highly uneven: the Indo‑Gangetic plains receive high recharge (locally exceeding 1 m yr⁻¹), while the hard‑rock terrains of the Deccan plateau often record less than 200 mm yr⁻¹. The Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) classifies assessment units into safe, semi‑critical, critical and over‑exploited categories based on the stage of groundwater development (ratio of extraction to recharge). As per the latest assessment, over 1 000 of nearly 7 000 units are over‑exploited, concentrated in Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Delhi and parts of Tamil Nadu.

The National Aquifer Mapping Programme (NAMP), launched in 2012, aims to delineate aquifer geometry, hydraulic properties and water‑quality parameters at a 1:50 000 scale. Significant progress has been made in mapping, enabling the design of area‑specific recharge structures such as percolation tanks, check dams and injection wells. The Atal Bhujal Yojana (2020‑2025), a World Bank‑supported scheme, incentivises community‑led water budgeting in 7 800 gram panchayats across seven states. It links financial grants to measurable outcomes: reduction in extraction, rise in water‑table levels, and adoption of micro‑irrigation.

Regulatory instruments include the Model Groundwater (Control and Regulation) Bill circulated by the Ministry of Jal Shakti, which proposes a permit regime for new wells, mandatory metering for commercial users, and a penalty framework for exceeding the sustainable yield. Several states (e.g., Maharashtra, Karnataka) have enacted their own groundwater acts, but enforcement remains uneven. The Composite Water Management Index (CWMI) of NITI Aayog ranks states on groundwater sustainability, providing a performance‑based lever for central funding.

Key exam points: (i) distinguish between renewable recharge and fossil groundwater in the Thar desert; (ii) recall that the CGWB’s “stage of groundwater development” >100 % signals over‑exploitation; (iii) know the three pillars of Atal Bhujal – community participation, demand‑side management, and convergence with MGNREGA; (iv) recognise that the 2021 amendment to the MMDR Act does not affect groundwater, which remains a state subject under Entry 17 of the State List.

Coastal Geomorphology and Sea‑Level Rise

India’s coastline stretches about 7 517 km, comprising roughly 5 423 km of mainland shore and 2 094 km of island coasts. The western coast is a narrow, steep, rocky shoreline backed by the Western Ghats, while the eastern coast is broad, low‑lying and dominated by large deltas – Ganga‑Brahmaputra, Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri. Major coastal landforms include estuaries (e.g., Hooghly, Narmada), lagoons (Chilika, Pulicat), mangrove‑dominated deltaic islands (Sundarbans), and coral reefs (Gulf of Mannar, Lakshadweep).

Satellite altimetry indicates a mean sea‑level rise of around 3 mm yr⁻¹ along the Indian coast since 1993, close to the global average. Regional variations arise from steric effects, vertical land motion (subsidence in the Bengal delta of several mm yr⁻¹) and ocean‑dynamic topography. The Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS) operates a real‑time sea‑level monitoring network of tide‑gauges, feeding data into the Storm Surge and Tsunami Early Warning System.

Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) notifications (1991, 2011, 2019) regulate development within 500 m of the high‑tide line (HTL) and 100 m along tidal‑influenced water bodies. The 2019 notification introduced a “hazard line” mapping erosion‑prone stretches and mandated a 20 m no‑development buffer for ecologically sensitive areas (ESAs) such as mangroves, coral reefs and turtle nesting beaches. The Integrated Coastal Zone Management Project (ICZMP), supported by the World Bank, has piloted shoreline protection using geo‑tubes, beach nourishment and mangrove restoration in Gujarat, Odisha and West Bengal.

Examiners often test: (i) the difference between a tide‑dominated delta (Ganga‑Brahmaputra) and a wave‑dominated delta (Mahanadi); (ii) the role of the Indian Ocean Dipole in modulating coastal sea‑level anomalies; (iii) the distinction between CRZ‑I (ecologically sensitive), CRZ‑II (urban), CRZ‑III (rural) and CRZ‑IV (water area) categories; (iv) the fact that the 2019 CRZ amendment permits temporary tourism facilities in CRZ‑III with state‑level approval.

Map‑Based Interpretation
A question shows a satellite image of the Sundarbans with highlighted mangrove loss between 2000 and 2020. The candidate must identify the primary driver and name the policy instrument that directly addresses it.
The dominant driver is relative sea‑level rise compounded by reduced sediment supply due to upstream dams (notably Farakka). The policy instruments are the 2019 CRZ notification’s hazard‑line mapping and the centrally sponsored scheme for Conservation and Management of Mangroves, which together mandate buffer zones and fund restoration.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a coastal question appears, first locate the coast (west vs east) and the dominant process (wave, tide, river). Then match the landform to the correct CRZ category. Keep a mental table of the four CRZ zones and the 2019 hazard‑line rule – it is a frequent source of 1‑mark MCQs.

Forest Types, Biodiversity Hotspots, and Conservation Policy

India’s forest cover stood at 21.71 % of the geographical area in the 2021 India State of Forest Report, with very dense forest (VDF) accounting for 3.04 %, moderately dense forest (MDF) 9.33 % and open forest (OF) 9.34 %. The major forest types follow the Champion & Seth classification: tropical wet evergreen (Western Ghats, Andaman & Nicobar), tropical moist deciduous (central India, Chotanagpur), tropical dry deciduous (Deccan plateau, central highlands), subtropical pine (Himalaya 1 000‑2 000 m), temperate broadleaf (Himalaya 2 000‑3 000 m), sub‑alpine coniferous (3 000‑3 500 m) and alpine scrub (>3 500 m).

Four globally recognised biodiversity hotspots lie partly in India: the Himalaya, the Western Ghats, the Indo‑Burma region (covering the Andaman & Nicobar islands and the north‑eastern states) and Sundaland (Nicobar islands). Together they harbour a large proportion of the country’s endemic flowering plants and vertebrate species. The Western Ghats alone host over 5 000 flowering plant species, hundreds of globally threatened species, and the highest amphibian endemism in the subcontinent.

The legislative backbone comprises the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972 (amended 2006, 2022), the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, the Biological Diversity Act 2002 and the Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act 2006. The Compensatory Afforestation Fund Management and Planning Authority (CAMPA), operationalised in 2019, channels the Net Present Value (NPV) of diverted forest land into afforestation, catchment‑area treatment and wildlife corridor creation. As of recent years, the CAMPA corpus exceeds ₹ 50 000 crore, with 60 % earmarked for state‑level afforestation and 40 % for central‑level projects.

Key exam points: (i) match each hotspot to its flagship endemic (e.g., Nilgiri tahr – Western Ghats; Sangai deer – Indo‑Burma); (ii) recall that the 2022 amendment to the Wildlife Act introduced provisions for species recovery plans for critically endangered species; (iii) know that the Forest Rights Act recognises individual and community rights over forest land up to 4 ha, but does not apply to protected areas notified under the Wildlife Act; (iv) understand the distinction between “reserved forest” (state control) and “protected forest” (limited community use) under the Indian Forest Act 1927.

Urban Heat Islands and Metropolitan Climate Resilience

Urban heat island (UHI) intensity in Indian megacities typically ranges from 2 °C to 5 °C during night‑time, with Delhi recording values up to 5 °C in peak summer. The primary drivers are high impervious surface fraction (>70 % in core districts), reduced evapotranspiration, anthropogenic heat from vehicles and air‑conditioning, and altered boundary‑layer dynamics. Satellite‑derived land‑surface temperature (LST) maps from MODIS and Landsat show a strong correlation between built‑up density and LST anomalies.

The National Mission on Sustainable Habitat (NMSH), one of the eight missions under the National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008), promotes cool‑roof technologies, urban greening, and climate‑resilient building codes. The Energy Conservation Building Code (ECBC) 2017 mandates a minimum roof solar reflectance of 0.7 for commercial buildings in climate zones 1‑3; recent updates have introduced thermal‑comfort metrics for residential buildings. Cities such as Hyderabad and Pune have piloted “cool pavement” coatings that reduce surface temperature by several degrees.

Green‑cover targets under the Nagar Van (city forest) programme aim to increase urban tree canopy. The programme has established hundreds of urban forests across multiple states. Real‑time heat‑health warning systems, integrated with the India Meteorological Department’s (IMD) heat‑wave alerts, have been deployed in cities including Ahmedabad, Bhubaneswar and Chennai, contributing to reduced heat‑related mortality in recent summers.

Examiners focus on: (i) the difference between surface UHI (LST) and atmospheric UHI (air temperature); (ii) the role of the “sky view factor” in canyon geometry; (iii) the linkage between UHI intensity and increased ozone formation; (iv) the introduction of thermal‑comfort metrics (PMV‑PPD) in updated building codes for residential buildings.

Disaster Management: Flood Plains, Cyclone Tracks, and Early Warning Systems

India’s flood‑prone area covers roughly 40 million hectares, affecting an average of 75 million people annually. The Ganga‑Brahmaputra‑Meghna basin accounts for about 60 % of flood damage, with the Kosi, Gandak and Brahmaputra rivers exhibiting high avulsion frequency. The Central Water Commission (CWC) operates a network of over 1 000 flood‑forecasting stations, issuing stage‑based warnings (yellow, orange, red) with lead times of 12‑48 hours for major rivers.

Cyclone activity is concentrated in the Bay of Bengal (5‑6 cyclones per year) and the Arabian Sea (1‑2 per year). The IMD’s cyclone warning system follows a four‑stage protocol: pre‑cyclone watch (72 h), cyclone alert (48 h), cyclone warning (24 h) and post‑landfall outlook. The Doppler Weather Radar (DWR) network, expanded to over 30 stations, provides volumetric scans every 10 minutes, enabling rapid intensity estimation. The Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS) runs the Indian Tsunami Early Warning System (ITEWS), which disseminates bulletins within 10 minutes of an undersea earthquake >6.5 Mw.

The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) released the National Disaster Management Plan (NDMP) 2019, aligning with the Sendai Framework. It mandates district‑level disaster management plans (DDMPs), capacity‑building of State Disaster Response Forces (SDRFs), and the integration of community‑based early warning (e.g., “Aapda Mitra” volunteers). The Flood Risk Mitigation Scheme (FRMS) funds structural measures (embankments, reservoirs) and non‑structural measures (floodplain zoning, insurance). As of 2022, the Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) covers around 30 % of the gross cropped area, providing a financial safety net for flood‑affected farmers.

Key exam points: (i) differentiate between “flash flood” (response time <6 h) and “riverine flood” (response time >24 h); (ii) recall that the 1999 Odisha super‑cyclone (Category 5) led to the creation of the Odisha State Disaster Management Authority (OSDMA), a model for other states; (iii) know the colour‑code thresholds for IMD cyclone warnings (red warning indicates severe to extremely severe cyclone intensity); (iv) understand that the NDMP 2019 assigns the Ministry of Home Affairs as the nodal ministry for disaster response, while the Ministry of Jal Shakti leads flood‑specific mitigation.

  • Confusing the stage of groundwater development with the water‑table depth; the former is a ratio, the latter an absolute measure.
  • Assuming the 2019 CRZ notification removed the 500 m HTL buffer; it retained it and added a hazard line.
  • Linking the Western Ghats hotspot exclusively to the Nilgiri tahr while ignoring the high amphibian endemism.
  • Treating UHI intensity as a daytime phenomenon only; night‑time UHI is often stronger and more relevant for heat‑health impacts.
  • Believing that the IMD’s cyclone warning stages are based solely on wind speed; they also incorporate storm‑surge potential and rainfall forecasts.
  • Stating that the CAMPA fund is used only for afforestation; it also finances wildlife corridors and catchment‑area treatment.
  • Assuming the NDMP 2019 replaced the Disaster Management Act 2005; it operationalises the Act but does not repeal it.
