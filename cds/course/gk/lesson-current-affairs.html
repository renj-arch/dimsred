1. National News & Developments

Government Schemes & Programmes

Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB PM-JAY)

Launched on 23 September 2018 by PM Narendra Modi from Ranchi, Jharkhand, AB PM-JAY is the world’s largest government-funded healthcare programme. It provides a health cover of ₹5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary care hospitalization to ~10.7 crore poor and vulnerable families (~50 crore beneficiaries). The scheme is cashless and paperless at empanelled hospitals — over 27,000 public and private hospitals are empanelled nationwide. As of 2026, over 11.69 crore hospital admissions worth ₹1.73 lakh crore have been authorized.

Key Fact: In September 2024, the scheme was extended to cover all citizens aged 70 years and above, irrespective of economic status — each senior gets a separate ₹5 lakh top-up cover.

Background and Genesis

The concept of universal health coverage (UHC) in India dates back to the Bhore Committee (1946), which first recommended comprehensive healthcare for all. In 2017-18, out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) on health pushed ~5.5 crore Indians into poverty annually. NITI Aayog’s Three-Year Action Agenda (2017-20) recommended merging existing health insurance schemes (RSBY, SHIS) into a single comprehensive programme. The National Health Policy 2017 set a target of increasing public health expenditure to 2.5% of GDP. Against this backdrop, AB PM-JAY was announced in the Union Budget 2018-19 and formally launched later that year, subsuming the existing Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana (RSBY, 2008) and the Senior Citizen Health Insurance Scheme (SCHIS, 2016).

Objectives

  • Reduce out-of-pocket health expenditure and catastrophic health spending among poor and vulnerable families
  • Increase access to quality secondary and tertiary healthcare
  • Create a culture of formal healthcare-seeking behaviour among the poor
  • Leverage public-private partnership to expand healthcare delivery infrastructure
  • Generate health data for evidence-based policy making under the National Digital Health Mission (NDHM)

Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-KISAN)

Launched in February 2019, PM-KISAN provides income support of ₹6,000 per year to all landholding farmer families, paid in three equal installments of ₹2,000 each directly into bank accounts via DBT. The scheme covers ~11.8 crore beneficiaries with an annual budget of ₹75,000 crore. The 18th installment was released in October 2024. Uttar Pradesh has the highest number of beneficiaries (~2.25 crore), followed by Maharashtra (~91 lakh) and Madhya Pradesh (~81 lakh).

Background

The scheme was announced in the Interim Budget 2019-20 by then-FM Piyush Goyal. It was retroactively made effective from December 2018. The scheme emerged from the context of agrarian distress — falling farm incomes, rising input costs, and the demand for a universal MSP guarantee. Several states had already launched their own income support schemes: Telangana’s Rythu Bandhu (2018), Odisha’s KALIA (2018), and Andhra’s Annadatha Sukhibhava.

Eligibility and Exclusions

Initially limited to small and marginal farmers (up to 2 hectares), the scheme was expanded to all landholding farmers in June 2019. Exclusions include: income tax payers, government employees (excluding Class IV/Group D), pensioners with pension >₹10,000/month, professionals (doctors, engineers, lawyers, CA), and those who paid income tax in the previous assessment year.

UPSC Tip: PM-KISAN is frequently compared with state-level schemes like Telangana’s Rythu Bandhu (₹10,000/acre/season) and Odisha’s KALIA. Note the key difference: PM-KISAN is landowner-based, excluding tenant farmers and sharecroppers.

Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY)

PMAY is a flagship housing scheme with two components: PMAY-Urban (PMAY-U) and PMAY-Gramin (PMAY-G). The combined target is 2.95 crore houses. PMAY-U (2015) provides assistance of ₹1-2.5 lakh per house under BLC, CLSS, ISSR, and AHP. PMAY-G (2016) provides ₹1.20 lakh in plain areas and ₹1.30 lakh in hilly areas. Over 2.68 crore houses have been completed as of 2024. PMAY-U 2.0 announced for an additional 1 crore houses.

Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM)

Launched in August 2019 under the Ministry of Jal Shakti, JJM aims to provide Functional Household Tap Connection (FHTC) to every rural household. Coverage increased from ~18% (3.23 crore) in August 2019 to ~78% (15.11 crore) by August 2024. Budget allocation: ₹60,000+ crore annually. JJM 2.0 approved in March 2026 with a target of 100% coverage by 2028. The WHO estimates ~4 lakh diarrhoeal deaths prevented due to safe drinking water access.

Swachh Bharat Mission (SBM)

Launched on 2 October 2014 at Rajghat, New Delhi. Phase 1 (2014-2019): ODF status for 600,000+ villages, 10.9 crore IHHLs. Phase 2 (2020-2025): ODF+ and ODF++ with SLWM focus. SBM-U 2.0 (2021-2026) budget: ₹30,980 crore. India’s open defecation rate declined from 73% (2000) to ~7% (2024). The mission drew from the CLTS approach pioneered by Kamal Kar and Bangladesh’s success (ODF by 2003). Criticism: quality of construction (CAG 2020), behaviour vs infrastructure gap (SQUAT study), and manual scavenging persistence. The National Annual Rural Sanitation Survey found 96.5% of rural households had access to toilets.

National Education Policy (NEP) 2020

Approved on 29 July 2020, replacing the 34-year-old NPE 1986. Key reforms: 5+3+3+4 curricular structure (Foundational 3-7 yrs, Preparatory 8-10, Middle 11-13, Secondary 14-18). Vocational education from Class 6. Multiple entry/exit in higher education via Academic Bank of Credits. PARAKH sets board exam standards. Target: 6% of GDP public spending on education (currently ~3%). The policy was drafted by a committee led by former ISRO chief K. Kasturirangan. The National Research Foundation (NRF) was established in 2023 with ₹50,000 crore outlay over 5 years.

Economic Developments

GDP Growth and Macroeconomic Performance

India’s real GDP growth for FY24 was 8.2%, the fastest among major economies. For FY25, the Economic Survey projected 6.5-7%, while the IMF projected 7.0%. India is the 5th largest economy (nominal GDP ~.7 trillion in FY24), surpassing the UK in 2022. India is projected to reach trillion by 2027 and trillion by 2030. India’s share in global GDP (PPP) is ~8%, projected to reach 10% by 2030. The services sector contributes ~55% to GVA, industry ~28%, and agriculture ~17%.

Inflation Trends

CPI inflation declined from 6.7% (2022-23) to 5.4% (2023-24) and further to ~4.6% in 2024-25. Core inflation has moderated to ~3.5%. The RBI MPC maintained repo rate at 6.5% since February 2023 (11 consecutive status quo meetings as of mid-2025). Food inflation remains the primary concern. The flexible inflation targeting framework (CPI target 4% ± 2%) has been in place since 2016. The government retained the 4% target for 2026-2031.

Employment and Labour Market

PLFS data: unemployment rate declined to 6.6% (2023-24) from 8.3% (2019-20). LFPR improved to 57.2% (urban). Female LFPR at ~25% remains the lowest in South Asia. EPFO net additions reached 14 million in FY24. The government launched the Employment-Linked Incentive (ELI) scheme in Budget 2024-25, providing DBT of one month’s salary (up to ₹15,000) to first-time formal sector employees. Gig and platform workers (7.7 million) brought under social security via the Code on Social Security, 2020.

Social Justice & Welfare

Waqf Amendment Act 2025

Parliament passed the Waqf Amendment Act in April 2025, significantly reforming Waqf Board administration. Key provisions: mandatory inclusion of women and non-Muslim members in Waqf Boards, digitisation of all 6+ lakh Waqf properties (9.5 lakh acres), enhanced audit mechanisms, and removal of the Waqf Tribunal’s exclusive jurisdiction. Aims to address encroachment (30% estimated) and improve revenue (current ~₹200 cr vs potential ₹12,000 cr). JPC received 1.2 crore+ submissions — highest for any bill.

One Nation One Election (ONOE)

The High-Level Committee chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind submitted its report in March 2024. The committee recommended synchronised elections in two phases with 18 constitutional amendments. The government introduced two bills in the 2025 Winter Session, referred to JPC. Estimated savings: ~₹5,000 crore per cycle vs the staggered schedule (2019-24 cost ~₹1.5 lakh crore).

Uniform Civil Code — Uttarakhand Leads

Uttarakhand became the first state to implement a UCC, with the Uttarakhand Uniform Civil Code Act, 2024 receiving Presidential assent on 12 March 2024. Key provisions: minimum marriage age 18 (women) and 21 (men), ban on polygamy, compulsory registration of marriages and divorces, equal inheritance rights. Exemptions for tribals under Article 371F. Article 44 of the Constitution mandates the state to endeavour to secure a UCC.

New Criminal Laws (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita)

Three new criminal codes came into effect on 1 July 2024, replacing colonial-era laws: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) replaced IPC (1860), Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) replaced CrPC (1973), and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) replaced Indian Evidence Act (1872). Key changes: sedition replaced by treason (Section 152 BNS); organised crime and terrorism defined; zero FIR and electronic FIR mandatory; videography of crime scenes mandatory; timelines for investigation (60-90 days) and trial (1-2 years); community service as punishment for petty offences; trials in absentia for organised crime.

UPSC PYQ (2024): With reference to the new criminal laws (2023-24), which statements are correct? 1. BNS replaces IPC. 2. Sedition has been completely removed. 3. Community service introduced as punishment. Answer: 1 and 3 only. Sedition was replaced by treason, not removed.
UPSC Tip: The three new laws emphasise victim-centric justice, technology adoption (digital evidence, videoconferencing), and speedy trials. Criticism centres on expanded police powers and lack of judicial infrastructure. The new laws were drafted after the Law Commission’s 2017 report on the need to reform colonial-era criminal laws.

CAA Implementation (March 2024)

The Citizenship Amendment Act (CAA) 2019 was notified on 11 March 2024. Rules enable persecuted non-Muslim migrants (Hindus, Sikhs, Jains, Buddhists, Parsis, Christians) from Pakistan, Afghanistan, Bangladesh arriving before 31 December 2014 to apply for Indian citizenship. As of 2026, over 8,000 applications granted. Residence requirement relaxed from 11 to 5 years. The Act does not affect existing Indian citizens’ rights. Supreme Court hearings on constitutional validity ongoing.

2024 General Elections — 18th Lok Sabha

Held in 7 phases (April 19-June 1, 2024). NDA won 293 seats (BJP 240, allies 53) — BJP lost absolute majority (down from 303 in 2019). INDIA alliance won 234 (INC 99, allies 135). PM Modi became PM for third consecutive term (third after Nehru). Record 83 women MPs (15.2%). Rahul Gandhi became LoP in Lok Sabha — first since 2014. Key factors in BJP’s reduced tally: strong opposition coordination, caste arithmetic in UP/Maharashtra/Rajasthan, unemployment and inflation concerns.

2. International Relations

India's Foreign Policy Framework

India’s foreign policy is characterised by strategic autonomy and multi-alignment. Key pillars: Neighbourhood First (SAARC, BIMSTEC, Act East), extended neighbourhood (Indo-Pacific, Indian Ocean rim), great power relations (US, Russia, China, Japan, EU), multilateral activism (UN, G20, SCO, BRICS, Quad, I2U2), and Global South leadership (Voice of Global South Summits). India’s foreign policy evolution: Non-Alignment (1947-1991) → Multi-Alignment (1991-present). The Modi Doctrine emphasises “Vishwa Bandhu” (World Friend) and development diplomacy.

Key Concept: India's foreign policy doctrine has evolved from Non-Alignment to Multi-Alignment/Strategic Autonomy. Key frameworks: Panchsheel (1954, PM Nehru), Gujral Doctrine (1996-97, unilateral goodwill to neighbours), and Modi Doctrine (2014-present, development diplomacy, diaspora engagement, multi-alignment). India's Act East Policy (2014) upgraded the Look East Policy (1991).

India-United States Relations

India-US relations under the Global Comprehensive Strategic Partnership have reached their most strategic level. Bilateral trade: + billion. Defence deals: + billion cumulatively. Key agreements: LEMOA (2016, logistics exchange), COMCASA (2018, communications security), BECA (2020, geospatial intelligence). The US is India’s largest defence supplier. Indian diaspora in the US: 4.8 million (second-largest immigrant group).

iCET (Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technologies)

Launched January 2023, co-led by NSA Ajit Doval and US NSA Jake Sullivan. Focus: AI, quantum, semiconductors, biotech, space, 5G/6G. Under iCET, GE Aerospace signed landmark MoU with HAL to co-produce GE F414-INS6 jet engines (80% technology transfer). Deal valued at .06 billion. Applied Materials opened million semiconductor centre in Bengaluru. Micron invested .75 billion for ATMP facility in Gujarat.

UPSC PYQ (2023): Which agreement enables sharing of geospatial intelligence between India and the US? 1. LEMOA 2. COMCASA 3. BECA 4. GSOMIA. Answer: 3 (BECA). LEMOA = logistics; COMCASA = communications security. GSOMIA is a South Korea-Japan agreement.

Defence Cooperation

Major US-origin platforms: C-17 (11), C-130J (12), P-8I (12), Apache (22), Chinook (15), MQ-9B Predator (.99B for 31). Joint exercises: Yudh Abhyas (army), Malabar (naval), Vajra Prahar (SF), Cope India (air force). The US designated India as a “Major Defence Partner” in 2016.

India-Russia Relations

Built on a Special and Privileged Strategic Partnership. Despite Western pressure post-Ukraine invasion, India has maintained strategic autonomy. Russia remains India’s largest defence supplier (~60% of equipment is Russian-origin). Key systems: S-400 Triumf (5 regiments), T-90S, Su-30MKI, AK-203 (joint production at Korwa, UP). INS Tushil (upgraded Talwar-class frigate) commissioned December 2024.

Oil Imports — Strategic Shift

India became the largest buyer of Russian crude after China. Russian oil share rose from <2% (pre-2022) to ~35% (2024), saving India -10 billion annually. Payments in non-dollar currencies. Bilateral trade ~ billion (2024), target billion by 2030. PM Modi visited Russia in July 2024 for the 22nd Annual Summit — told Putin that “war cannot solve problems” and “children are dying.” The India-Russia Annual Summit was not held in 2022 and 2023 — first breaks in the 20+ year tradition.

UPSC Angle: Russia-Ukraine war has tested India's strategic autonomy. India continues defence imports, discounted oil, and diplomatic engagement while abstaining on UN votes condemning Russia. This has implications for India's relationships with the US (CAATSA sanctions risk for S-400) and Europe.

India-China Relations

At their lowest since the 1962 war following the Galwan Valley clash (June 2020, 20 Indian soldiers killed). Over 30 rounds of Corps Commander-level talks held. Unresolved friction points: Depsang Plains, Demchok. India has deployed ~1,00,000 troops in Eastern Ladakh and upgraded airfields (Nyoma, Daulat Beg Oldie, Fukche). Rafale, Su-30MKI, and Apache helicopters deployed. India has operationalised integrated theatre commands.

Trade and Economic Decoupling

Bilateral trade ~ billion (2024) with India’s largest trade deficit of ~ billion. Key Indian imports: electronics, APIs, fertilisers, steel, coal. India blocked 230+ Chinese apps (TikTok, PUBG), tightened FDI norms from land-bordering countries (2020), imposed Quality Control Orders. PLI schemes reduce import dependence. PM Modi and Xi Jinping have not held a bilateral summit since Mamallapuram (October 2019). China continues to claim 90,000 sq km in Arunachal as “South Tibet.”

UPSC PYQ (2022): Consider: 1. Galwan Valley clash was in Karakoram sector. 2. India-China Corps Commander talks ongoing. 3. China claims Arunachal as "South Tibet." Correct statements? Answer: 2 and 3 only. Galwan Valley is in Aksai Chin (Ladakh), not Karakoram.

India-Pakistan Relations

Relations frozen after Article 370 abrogation (August 2019). No high-level dialogue since 2015. Indus Water Commission continues as a technical body. India issued notice in 2024 seeking modification of the Indus Water Treaty (1960), citing demographic and environmental changes. Kartarpur Corridor (2019) remains the only people-to-people link. Trade negligible (~ million). 2003 LoC ceasefire has largely held since February 2021.

India-Neighbourhood Relations

India-Nepal

Bilateral ties improved after PM Prachanda’s visits. India is Nepal’s largest trade partner (~) and development partner (~ committed). Motihari-Amlekhgunj petroleum pipeline (first cross-border pipeline in South Asia). Arun-3 hydro (900 MW). Kalapani-Lipulekh border dispute under discussion.

India-Bhutan

Closest neighbour under the 1949 Treaty (revised 2007). India is Bhutan’s largest development partner (~). Hydropower: Chukha (336 MW), Tala (1020 MW), Mangdechhu (720 MW) operational. Punatsangchhu-I (1020 MW) and II (600 MW) under construction.

India-Bangladesh

Historic high under PM Sheikh Hasina. LBA (2015) resolved enclave issues. Bilateral trade ~. India supplies 1,600 MW electricity. Akhaura-Agartala rail link (2023), Maitree Express operational. Teesta water sharing agreement pending. India granted transit rights to Northeast via Chittagong and Mongla ports.

India-Maldives

Relations suffered under President Muizzu but stabilised by 2025. India withdrew military personnel, replaced with civilians. India extended budget support, currency swap. Greater Malé Connectivity Project is flagship.

India-Myanmar

Strained since 2021 coup. India condemned violence but engages with junta. FMR scrapped (2024), border fencing initiated. Kaladan Multi-Modal Project near completion. India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway (1,360 km) delayed.

India-Sri Lanka

Strengthened after Sri Lanka’s 2022 economic crisis. India provided ~ assistance. President Anura Kumara Dissanayake (elected 2024) visited India. 13th Amendment (Tamil provincial autonomy) under discussion.

India-Afghanistan

India does not recognise the Taliban regime. Humanitarian assistance: 50,000 MT wheat, 500 tonnes medicines. No country has formally recognised the Taliban as of 2026.

African Union included as permanent member — first expansion since 1999. IMEEC and Global Biofuels Alliance launched. Brazil hosted G20 in 2024 (Rio). South Africa to host in 2025.

Global Conflicts & Diplomacy

Russia-Ukraine War

India’s calibrated position: calls for “dialogue and diplomacy,” abstains on UN votes, continues oil/defence ties. PM Modi’s direct criticism to Putin (July 2024). 22,500+ citizens evacuated under Operation Ganga (2022). The war accelerated India’s defence indigenisation and altered global oil trade.

Israel-Palestine & Gaza War (Oct 2023-)

Deep Dive: MSP — The Legal Guarantee Debate

The Minimum Support Price (MSP) system, operational since the 1960s, covers 23 crops (7 kharif, 6 rabi, and other commercial crops) recommended by the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) and approved by the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA). The government’s declared objective is to ensure MSP at 50% above the A2+FL cost (comprehensive cost including family labour) as announced in Union Budget 2018-19. However, only 6% of farmers actually sell at MSP (NSSO 70th Round), with paddy and wheat accounting for 90%+ of MSP procurement. Farmer organisations have demanded a legal guarantee of MSP (during the 2020-21 farm laws protest). The government appointed a committee under former Agriculture Secretary Sanjay Agarwal to study the issue; its report has not been made public. In 2024, the MSP for paddy was increased to ₹2,300/quintal and wheat to ₹2,425/quintal. The Punjab government has been purchasing all crops at MSP through its procurement agencies.

UPSC Insight: MSP is a price intervention mechanism under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 and the Agricultural Produce Market Committee (APMC) Acts. CACP recommends MSP based on: cost of production, demand-supply, price trends, terms of trade, inter-crop parity, and effect on cost of living. The Shanta Kumar Committee (2015) recommended MSP only for crops with major government procurement (rice, wheat) and a direct benefit transfer for others.

Deep Dive: India’s Financial Sector Reforms — IBC, ARC, and NARCL

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016 has been a landmark reform. Key features: time-bound resolution (330 days), creditor-in-control, NCLT as adjudicating authority, resolution professionals as intermediaries. The Indian Banks’ Association (IBA) provides oversight. The Debt Recovery Tribunals (DRTs) and SARFAESI Act (2002) provide alternative recovery mechanisms.

Deep Dive: India’s Five-Year Plans vs NITI Aayog’s Approach

The Planning Commission (1950-2014) implemented 12 Five-Year Plans until its replacement by NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India) on 1 January 2015. Deep Dive: India’s Demographic Dividend — Opportunities and Challenges

Deep Dive: India’s Nuclear Doctrine — No First Use and Credible Minimum Deterrence

India’s nuclear doctrine was formally adopted in January 2003 and is based on the principles of Credible Minimum Deterrence and No First Use (NFU). Key elements: (1) India will not be the first to use nuclear weapons against any adversary. (2) Retaliation will be massive and designed to inflict unacceptable damage on the adversary if India is attacked with nuclear weapons. (3) Nuclear retaliation to a first strike will be decided by the Political Council (headed by the PM) and executed by the Nuclear Command Authority (NCA). (4) The NCA consists of a Political Council (approval authority) and an Executive Council (execution authority, headed by NSA). (5) India maintains a triad of nuclear delivery systems: land-based ballistic missiles (Agni series), sea-based (INS Arihant, INS Arighat SSBNs with K-4/K-15 SLBMs), and air-based (Rafale, Su-30MKI, Mirage 2000 with nuclear-capable bombs). (6) India maintains a no-use of nuclear weapons against non-nuclear weapon states (negative security assurance). (7) India supports the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT) but has not signed it (consistent with its voluntary moratorium on nuclear testing since 1998). (8) India supports negotiations on a Fissile Material Cut-off Treaty (FMCT) but has concerns about existing stocks. India is not a signatory to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT, 1968) which it considers discriminatory. India’s civil nuclear separation plan (2006) placed 14 reactors under IAEA safeguards. India’s nuclear doctrine has been criticised by strategic analysts for lacking clarity on the “massive retaliation” threshold — the 2024 MIRV test (Agni-5 with multiple warheads) suggests India is enhancing second-strike capabilities while maintaining NFU.

Deep Dive: India’s Smart Cities Mission — Achievements and Critique

Deep Dive: India’s Competition Law — CCI and the New Competition Amendment Act 2023

The Competition Act, 2002 (amended 2007, 2009, and 2023) established the Competition Commission of India (CCI) to prevent anti-competitive practices. The Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023 (effective April 2024) brought major reforms: (1) Gun-Jumping Provisions: Deal value threshold introduced — transactions above ₹2,000 crore where the target has substantial business operations in India require CCI approval (catching acquisitions of digital startups with high valuation but low turnover). (2) Settlements and Commitments: Introduced provisions for voluntary settlements and commitments to reduce litigation and speed up resolution. (3) Timelines: CCI must form its prima facie opinion within 30 days, investigation must be completed in 6 months (extendable by 6 more), and final order within 12 months. (4) Penalties: Enhanced penalties for anti-competitive conduct (up to 10% of turnover/global income). (5) Leniency Plus: Expanded leniency programme for cartel breakers — the first informant gets full immunity from penalties. (6) Cross-border Mergers: Extraterritorial application strengthened — CCI can review global combinations that have an appreciable adverse effect on competition in India. Key CCI decisions: Google (₹1,337 crore fine for Android abuse, 2022), Amazon-Future (CCI approved but later set aside by SC on different grounds), and several cement, airline, and pharma cartel cases. The CCI is also the designated authority for conducting competition advocacy and market studies.

Deep Dive: India’s MSME Sector — Backbone of the Economy

Deep Dive: India’s Trade Policy — From Protectionism to FTAs

India’s trade-to-GDP ratio declined from 55% (FY13) to ~43% (FY24), indicating relative closure (partly due to stronger GDP growth). Deep Dive: India’s Public Distribution System — NFSA, PMGKAY, and One Nation One Ration Card

The Targeted Public Distribution System (TPDS) is the world’s largest food distribution network. The National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013 provides a legal entitlement to 5 kg of foodgrains per person per month (₹3/kg rice, ₹2/kg wheat, ₹1/kg coarse grains) to 67% of the population (75% rural, 50% urban — approximately 81.35 crore beneficiaries). Coverage: Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY, poorest of poor) households get 35 kg/month. States also provide additional quantities and items (Tamil Nadu, Andhra, Karnataka provide free rice). During COVID, PM Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana (PMGKAY, March 2020-December 2022) provided an additional 5 kg free foodgrains + 1 kg chana per family, benefitting 81.35 crore people at a cost of ₹3.90 lakh crore. PMGKAY was the world’s largest food security programme during the pandemic. The One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC) scheme (implemented 2023 across all states) enables interstate portability — NFSA beneficiaries can get subsidised foodgrains anywhere in India through Aadhaar-based authentication. 80+ crore portability transactions have been recorded since launch. The Food Corporation of India (FCI) manages procurement (at MSP), storage (700+ lakh MT storage capacity), and distribution. The Open Market Sale Scheme (OMSS) for wheat was used in 2023-24 to control prices (sold at ₹2,350/quintal, below market). The PDS faces challenges: high leakage (30%+ before Aadhaar seeding, now reduced to ~10%), poor quality grain, exclusion errors (NFSA coverage based on 2011 Census), and the economic cost of FCI operations (₹4,500/quintal for rice vs CIP of ₹3/kg). The 2023-24 food subsidy was ₹2.05 lakh crore. The Economic Survey 2023-24 recommended reducing the NFSA coverage to better-targeted beneficiaries (potentially 50% of population) and shifting to cash transfers for non-priority groups.

Deep Dive: India’s Intellectual Property Regime — Patents, Trademarks, and Copyrights

India’s IP regime has evolved significantly since joining the WTO (1995) and implementing the TRIPS Agreement. Key legislation: Patents Act, 1970 (amended 1999, 2002, 2005 — product patents for all inventions), Trademarks Act, 1999 (replaced Trade and Merchandise Marks Act, 1958), Copyright Act, 1957 (amended 2012), Designs Act, 2000, Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999, and the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers’ Rights Act, 2001. The Office of the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trademarks (CGPDTM) is the nodal authority under DPIIT. The Patent (Amendment) Rules, 2024 introduced: expedited examination for startups, women inventors, and government-funded research; reduced compliance burden; and enhanced fee for delayed filings. India has been active in protecting its traditional knowledge through the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL, 2001) — preventing bio-piracy of 2.5+ lakh medicinal formulations (Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Yoga). The Department of Industrial Policy and Promotion (DPIIT) launched the National IPR Policy (2016) with 7 objectives: (1) IPR awareness, (2) creation of IP assets, (3) legal and legislative framework strengthening, (4) administration and management, (5) commercialisation of IP (technology transfer, licensing), (6) enforcement and adjudication, and (7) human capital development. Deep Dive: India’s Central & State Fiscal Relations — Finance Commission and GST Council

India’s federal fiscal architecture is governed by the Constitution (Articles 264-293). The Finance Commission (FC) is a constitutional body (Article 280) appointed every 5 years to recommend: (1) distribution of net tax proceeds between Union and states (vertical devolution), (2) distribution among states (horizontal devolution, based on population, area, income distance, demographic performance, forest & ecology, tax effort), (3) grants-in-aid to states (revenue deficit grants, sector-specific grants, local body grants), and (4) measures to augment state resources. The 15th Finance Commission (Chair: NK Singh, for 2021-26) recommended: vertical devolution at 41% of divisible pool (down from 42% of 14th FC), horizontal devolution based on 12.5% population (2011 Census), 45% income distance, 15% area, 10% demographic performance, 10% forest & ecology, 7.5% tax effort. The 16th Finance Commission (Chair: Arvind Panagariya, for 2026-31) is currently deliberating. The GST Council (Article 279A, 2016) is a joint forum of the Centre and states (33 members: Union FM + MoS Finance + state FMs). It makes recommendations on GST rates, exemptions, model laws, and disputes. As of 2025, the GST Council has held 50+ meetings. The 7th Schedule (Union List, State List, Concurrent List) allocates taxing powers — union list includes income tax, corporation tax, customs, excise; state list includes land revenue, state GST, stamp duty, entertainment tax; concurrent list includes stamp duties, taxes on electricity. India’s combined (Centre+state) tax-to-GDP ratio is ~22% (state share ~8%, central ~14%). The Fraternity and Fiscal Responsibility Budget Management (FRBM) targets apply to both Centre and states — most states have their own Fiscal Responsibility Acts.

Deep Dive: India’s Labour Codes — The 4 Labour Codes and Their Impact

In 2020, Parliament passed 4 codes consolidating 44+ central labour laws: (1) Code on Wages, 2019 — consolidates the Payment of Wages Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Bonus Act, and Equal Remuneration Act. Establishes a uniform minimum wage across India (₹178/day to ₹1,245/day depending on skill level and region). Introduces a National Minimum Wage (₹178/day initially). All employees (organised + unorganised + gig workers) covered. (2) Industrial Relations Code, 2020 — consolidates Trade Unions Act, Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, and Industrial Disputes Act. Introduces: retrenchment threshold for prior government approval increased from 100 to 300 workers; fixed-term employment (hiring flexibility without permanent benefits); re-skilling fund (15 days’ wages from employer for retrenched workers); and simplified trade union recognition (7 members for registration, 14% for recognition). (3) Social Security Code, 2020 — consolidates 9 laws including EPF Act, ESI Act, Maternity Benefit Act, and Payment of Gratuity Act. Introduces: social security coverage for gig workers and platform workers (first-ever legal recognition of gig workers); Universal Account Number (UAN) portability for EPF; ESI coverage expanded to 50+ employee establishments; and national social security board for unorganised workers. (4) Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code (OSHWC), 2020 — consolidates 13 laws including Factories Act, Plantations Labour Act, Mines Act, and Contract Labour Act. Introduces: applicability to establishments with 10+ workers (reduced from 20); mandatory annual health check-ups for all workers; provision for one cre`che per 50 women workers (also applicable to establishments employing 50+ workers); inter-State migrant workers covered under the same labour law framework as regular workers (mandatory portability). The 4 Codes have been passed by Parliament but rules are still being finalised. State governments also have to frame state-specific rules. The codes were opposed by trade unions (who called them pro-employer) and industry associations (who flagged complexity of compliance despite simplification). The codes simplify compliance through: single registration, single return, single licence, and digitised inspections (web-based jurisdiction-free inspection scheme). Implementation will be through a centralised portal (Shram Suvidha Portal).

Deep Dive: India’s Data Protection & Digital Privacy Regime — DPDP Act, 2023

The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 is India’s first comprehensive data protection law. Key provisions: (1) Applicability: The Act applies to processing of digital personal data within India where data is collected online or offline and digitised. It also applies to processing outside India if related to offering goods/services to individuals in India. (2) Consent: Data fiduciaries must obtain consent from data principals (individuals) for processing personal data. Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous with a clear affirmative action. Consent managers (registered with the Data Protection Board) can be appointed. (3) Rights of Data Principal: Right to access (know what data is collected, purpose, and recipients), right to correction and erasure, right to grievance redressal, and right to nominate a representative to exercise their rights after death or incapacity. (4) Obligations of Data Fiduciary: Implement appropriate technical and organisational measures, publish notice (purpose and manner of processing), establish grievance redressal mechanism, report personal data breaches to the Board and affected data principals, and appoint a Data Protection Officer (for significant data fiduciaries). (5) Significant Data Fiduciaries: Designated by the central government based on volume, sensitivity, risk of harm, or impact on national sovereignty. Additional obligations: appoint an independent data auditor, conduct Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), carry out periodic audits, and implement measures for algorithmic transparency. (6) Exemptions: The central government can exempt government agencies from provisions in the interest of security, sovereignty, public order, and friendly relations with foreign states. Research, statistical, and archival purposes also get some exemptions. (7) Data Localisation: The Act does NOT mandate data localisation explicitly. However, the government can restrict the transfer of personal data to certain countries (by notification) — essentially allowing free cross-border flows except for specified territories. (8) Penalties: Up to ₹250 crore per instance of non-compliance. The Data Protection Board of India (quasi-judicial body) will adjudicate penalties. The DPDP Act balances data protection with innovation. It overrides the earlier Personal Data Protection Bill (2019) which lapsed. The Information Technology Act (2000) Section 43A and the IT Rules (2011, SPDI Rules) will be subsumed. The Act establishes the Digital India Trust Agency (DIGITA) to verify digital service providers. The Act does NOT cover offline non-digital data processing, manual records, or processing for personal/domestic purposes.

UPSC Analysis: Key criticisms of DPDP Act 2023: (1) Broad exemptions for government agencies (privacy concerns post-Puttaswamy judgement — reasonable restrictions must be proportionate). (2) No Right to Data Portability (unlike GDPR). (3) No classification of sensitive personal data (health, financial, biometric, genetic, caste, religion, sexual orientation). (4) No independent regulatory mechanism — the Data Protection Board is a government-appointed body (concerns about independence). (5) Very high penalties (₹250 crore) may disproportionately affect MSMEs and startups.

Deep Dive: India’s Agriculture Marketing Reforms — From APMC to e-NAM and Beyond

The agricultural marketing system in India has historically been governed by state-level APMC (Agricultural Produce Market Committee) Acts (1950s-60s). The APMC system created monopolistic mandis where farmers were forced to sell at potentially exploitative prices (high commission charges, delayed payments, collusion among traders). Major reforms: (1) Model APMC Act, 2003: Encouraged contract farming, direct marketing, and private markets. Only 13 states adopted it meaningfully. (2) e-NAM (National Agriculture Market, 2016): Electronic trading platform integrating 1,360+ mandis across 23 states. Unified license, single-point levy, online payment. However, only 20% of mandis actively trade on e-NAM due to infrastructure gaps and trader resistance. (3) Farmers’ Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, 2020: Allowed intra-state and inter-state trade outside APMC mandis, removing barriers. Repealed in 2021 after prolonged farmer protests (Demand for MSP guarantee, loan waivers). (4) Model Contract Farming Act, 2018: Provided for contract farming (sponsor-farmer agreement) with dispute resolution mechanisms. (5) Warehouse-based Sales: PM-Kisan Sampaada Yojana and AIF encourage negotiable warehouse receipts (NWRs) for farmers to sell through exchanges. (6) Operation Greens (2018): Price stabilisation scheme for TOP (Tomato, Onion, Potato) crops — extends to all perishable horticulture crops (2022). Sub-schemes: Market Intervention Scheme (MIS) for regulated price support, Price Stabilisation Fund (PSF) for buffer stocking. (7) Kisan Rail (2020): Dedicated refrigerated rail transport for perishable agri-produce — 500+ services. (8) FPO (Farmer Producer Organisation) promotion: The government launched a dedicated scheme for establishing 10,000 FPOs (2020-25) with ₹6,865 crore. 7,500+ FPOs promoted. Each FPO gets ₹18 lakh equity grant and ₹15 lakh credit guarantee. FPOs enhance market access, reduce input costs, and improve price realisation. (9) MSP System: Minimum Support Price (MSP) announced for 23 Kharif and Rabi crops. The government promises MSP at 1.5x the cost of production (since 2018-19 Budget). However, MSP benefits only 6% of farmers (mainly wheat and paddy in Punjab, Haryana, UP, MP, Andhra). The Swaminathan Commission (2006) recommended MSP at 1.5x C2 cost (comprehensive cost with imputed rent and interest), but C2 is not centrally used. The government procured ₹4+ lakh crore worth of crops in 2024-25. The PM-AASHA scheme (2018) provides Price Support Scheme (PSS) for pulses/oilseeds and Price Deficiency Payment (PDPS). (10) Digital Agriculture Mission (2021-25): Agri-stack (digital infrastructure), Krishi Decision Support System (DSS), soil health cards (27 crore+ issued), and the Unified Portal for Agricultural Services (UPAG).

Deep Dive: India’s Energy Security — Oil & Gas, Strategic Reserves, and Diversification

India is the 3rd largest oil consumer globally (5.5+ million barrels/day, 90% imported) and 4th largest LNG importer (30+ million metric tonnes/year). Energy security is a critical national security issue. Key dimensions: (1) Oil Imports: India’s oil import bill was ₹16+ lakh crore (2024). Russia became India’s top oil supplier in 2023 (35%+ of crude imports, up from 2% in 2021) — due to discounted crude post-Ukraine war. Iraq (2nd, 20%), Saudi Arabia (3rd, 15%), UAE (10%), USA (5%), Kuwait, Nigeria. India has diversified from Middle Eastern to Russian, US, and African crudes. (2) Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR): India has 5.33 million tonnes of SPR (9 days of consumption) at 3 locations: Visakhapatnam (1.33 MT), Mangalore (1.5 MT), Padur (2.5 MT). Phase-2 SPR at Chandikhol (Odisha, 2.5 MT) and Padur II (2.5 MT) will take total to 12 days. The government allows private sector participation (strategic storage leases). (3) Natural Gas: Share in primary energy mix: 6% (target 15% by 2030). The government launched the National Gas Grid (unified pipeline network, 18,000+ km operational, target 32,000 km). The City Gas Distribution (CGD) network covers 500+ districts (88% of population). The government introduced the Unified Tariff (pipeline tariffs capped nationwide). India is expanding LNG import terminals: 45+ MMTPA capacity (Dahej, Hazira, Dabhol, Kochi, Ennore, Mundra, Jaigarh). The Helium recovery, coal-bed methane (CBM), coal gasification (Syngas to methanol) and hydrogen blending in gas grids are emerging priorities. (4) Renewable Energy Integration: The Variable Renewable Energy (VRE) integration requires grid balancing — India has 25+ GW of pumped storage and battery storage (target 50 GW by 2030). The government launched the National Power System Plan (PSP) and Green Energy Corridors for RE evacuation. (5) Energy Efficiency: The Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT) scheme (Bureau of Energy Efficiency/BEE) covers 1,000+ industrial units — energy savings of 30+ million tonnes of oil equivalent. The UJALA scheme (LED distribution) distributed 36+ crore LED bulbs saving 50+ billion kWh/year. The PAT Cycle VII (2023-25) expands coverage. The Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act, 2022 mandates: minimum renewable energy purchase, carbon trading, and energy conservation code for commercial buildings. (6) Biofuels & E-Mobility: Ethanol blending in petrol reached 15% (target 20% by 2025). The SATAT scheme promotes 5,000 CBG plants. India’s EV market crossed 4+ million EVs (FAME II, 2019-24, ₹10,000 crore, subsidised 2+ million EVs). The PM e-Drive (2024, ₹10,900 crore, replacing FAME III) accelerates EV adoption for two-wheelers, three-wheelers, e-buses, and e-trucks. (7) Energy Transition: India’s energy transition requires + trillion investment by 2070 (net-zero target). India has committed to 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030, 50% energy from RE by 2030, and 1 billion tonnes CO2 emission reduction by 2030.

Deep Dive: India’s Internal Security — Left-Wing Extremism (LWE), J&K, and Counter-Terrorism

India’s internal security landscape has multiple challenges: Left-Wing Extremism (LWE, Naxalism), insurgency in the North East (Nagaland, Manipur, Assam, Mizoram), Jammu & Kashmir militancy, cross-border terrorism, and urban terrorism (ISIS-inspired lone-wolf attacks). LWE (Naxalism): LWE affected areas reduced from 90 districts (2014) to ~30 districts (2024). Incidents declined from 1,200+ (2014) to 400+ (2024). Deaths declined from 400 (2014) to 100 (2024). The LWE corridor (Red Corridor) is shrinking to core areas of Bijapur, Sukma, Dantewada, Bastar, Gadchiroli. The government’s strategy: security (CRPF, CoBRA, Greyhounds, special forces), development (₹59,000 crore under LWE scheme, Aspirational Districts programme, road connectivity, mobile towers, banking facilities), and surrender/rehabilitation policy. The National Policy and Action Plan on LWE (2015) is a four-pronged approach: (1) security-related measures, (2) development, (3) ensuring rights/entitlements, (4) public perception management. Kashmir: Abrogation of Article 370 (5 August 2019) reorganised J&K state into Union Territories of J&K and Ladakh. Security situation improved significantly: infiltration across LoC declined by 60%+, stone-pelting incidents reduced from 1,600 (2016) to near zero, militant recruitment declined. The government has restored statehood (not yet done as of 2025, but indicated). Assembly elections were held in J&K (2024) with high voter turnout (60%+). G20 Tourism meet held in Srinagar (2023). Terror financing: the NIA and ED have seized ₹500+ crore assets under UAPA. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA, 1967) was strengthened (2019 amendment allows designation of individuals as terrorists — 100+ individuals designated). The multi-agency centre (MAC) under the Intelligence Bureau (IB) coordinates counter-terrorism. North East Insurgency: Major insurgencies: NSCN-IM (Nagaland), ULFA (Assam), PLA/UNLF (Manipur), NDFB (Bodoland — Assam). The government has signed peace accords (NSCN-IM Framework Agreement 2015, Bodo Peace Accord 2020, Bru-Reang Agreement 2018, Karbi Anglong Agreement 2021, Assam-Meghalaya border agreement 2022, Assam-Arunachal boundary pact 2023). The suspension of operations (SoO) with Kuki militants in Manipur is under strain due to ethnic violence (May 2023+). The AFSPA (Armed Forces Special Powers Act, 1958) was removed from most of the North East (except 3 districts of Nagaland and 8 districts in Assam/Manipur). The Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA) plans to bring the whole North East under AFSPA-free zone. Cyber Security: The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In, 2004) handles cyber incidents. The National Cyber Security Strategy (2020) and the National Cyber Coordination Centre (NCCC) are being operationalised. The National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC) protects strategic sectors. India has an estimated 5+ lakh cybercrime incidents/year (UP, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, Gujarat, Delhi). The cyber security market in India is ₹1+ lakh crore. Coastal Security: The Indian Coast Guard (1,700+ officers, 150+ ships, 70+ aircraft) — expanded significantly (12+ ships/year commissioning). Coastal radar chain: 46 stations operational. The AIS (Automatic Identification System) transponders on 1.5+ lakh fishing boats enhance coastal surveillance. The Sagar Kaval (Sea Guard) joint coastal patrol by Navy and Coast Guard.

UPSC PYQ (2023): Consider the following statements regarding the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA): 1. It was originally enacted in 1967. 2. It allows the designation of individuals as terrorists. 3. The maximum period of detention under UAPA is 90 days. 4. UAPA cases are adjudicated by special NIA courts. Which is/are correct? Answer: 1, 2, and 4 only. The maximum detention period is 180 days (not 90), extendable by 90 days on judicial approval.

Deep Dive: India’s Water Resources Management — Interlinking of Rivers, Jal Shakti, and Groundwater

India has 18% of the world’s population but only 4% of global freshwater resources. Per capita water availability declined from 5,200 cubic metres (1951) to 1,400 cubic metres (2024) — entering water-stressed territory (below 1,700). The government’s comprehensive approach includes: (1) National River Linking Project (NRLP): Interlinking of rivers — 30 links (14 Himalayan, 16 Peninsular) connecting 37 rivers at an estimated cost of ₹5.5 lakh crore. Benefits: 35 million hectares of irrigation, 34 GW of hydropower, 7,500 km navigation, drought mitigation, and flood control. Ken-Betwa Link (2021) is the first project ( ₹44,605 crore), linking MP’s Ken river to Betwa in UP — 10.6 lakh hectare irrigation, 103 MW hydro, 27 MW solar. Godavari-Cauvery link (KRIS) under planning. The NRLP is being executed by the National Water Development Agency (NWDA, 1982 under Ministry of Jal Shakti). (2) Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY, 2015): "Har Khet Ko Paani" — aim to expand irrigation coverage (micro-irrigation, drip/sprinkler) and improve water use efficiency. The PMKSY- Accelerated Irrigation Benefit Programme (PMKSY-AIBP) completed 33 projects out of 99 prioritised. 10+ lakh hectares additional irrigation created. The PMKSY-Per Drop More Crop (micro-irrigation, ₹8,000 crore, 2022) promotes drip/sprinkler irrigation on 15+ lakh hectares. (3) Atal Bhujal Yojana (2019, ₹6,000 crore, World Bank assisted): Groundwater management in 8,500+ water-stressed gram panchayats (7 states — Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, MP, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, UP). Community-led groundwater monitoring, aquifer mapping (NAQUIM), and water budgeting. (4) Jal Shakti Abhiyan (2019, JSA 2.0 2021): Water conservation and rainwater harvesting campaigns in all districts (focus on 256 water-stressed districts). 5-point intervention: rainwater harvesting, renovation of traditional water bodies, reuse & recharge, watershed development, and intensive afforestation. Groundwater recharge: 1.5 lakh check dams, 2+ lakh farm ponds, 2+ lakh percolation tanks constructed. (5) Aquifer Mapping: The National Aquifer Mapping and Management (NAQUIM) programme by Central Ground Water Board (CGWB) — mapping 25 lakh sq km of aquifer systems (60% completed). (6) Dam Safety and Clean Ganga: National Ganga Council (2019) and Namami Gange programme (2014, ₹20,000+ crore). Sewage treatment capacity increased from 1,200 MLD to 2,000+ MLD. River surface cleaning, ghat development, biodiversity (dolphin conservation, Ganga prawns). The Dam Safety Act (2021) provides for dam safety review panels, emergency action plans, and institutional mechanisms for 6,700+ large dams in India. (7) Desalination & Water Recycling: India has 10+ desalination plants (largest at Nemmeli, Chennai — 110 MLD, Minjur — 100 MLD). The government launched the Swachh Bharat Mission (SBM) treated wastewater reuse policy (20% reuse target by 2025, 50% by 2030). The Rejuvenation of Water Bodies scheme under AMRUT 2.0.

UPSC Analysis: India's water crisis is acute — 21 major cities are expected to run out of groundwater by 2030 (NITI Aayog). Critical issues: (1) Groundwater extraction rate 62% (highest in the world). (2) Water productivity is low (.5 per cubic metre vs Israel ). (3) 70% of irrigation is from groundwater. (4) Water conflicts are rising (Cauvery, Krishna, Godavari, Mahanadi, Ravi-Beas). (5) The Inter-State River Water Disputes Act (1956) has been amended (2017) with a single tribunal mechanism. The Ken-Betwa link faces forest clearance issues (Panna Tiger Reserve). The NRLP faces ecological, displacement, and financial hurdles.
