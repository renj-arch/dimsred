What is English Grammar?

Grammar forms the foundation of the English language section in banking exams, and the exam. Questions test your understanding of parts of speech, tenses, subject-verb agreement, articles, prepositions, conjunctions, and sentence structure. Mastery of these rules helps you score well in error detection, sentence improvement, and fill-in-the-blanks questions.

Key Grammar Topics

1. Tenses: Present (Simple, Continuous, Perfect, Perfect Continuous), Past (Simple, Continuous, Perfect, Perfect Continuous), Future (Simple, Continuous, Perfect, Perfect Continuous). Learn the correct form for each tense and time expressions used with each.

2. Subject-Verb Agreement: Singular subject takes singular verb; plural subject takes plural verb. Special cases: 'either/or', 'neither/nor', collective nouns (team, committee), indefinite pronouns (everyone, somebody), and phrases between subject and verb.

3. Articles (A, An, The): 'A' before consonant sounds, 'An' before vowel sounds. 'The' for specific references, unique things, superlatives, and before names of rivers, oceans, mountain ranges, etc.

4. Prepositions: Correct usage of in, on, at, for, since, during, by, with, among, between, etc. Each preposition has specific uses with time, place, and movement.

5. Conjunctions: Coordinating (FANBOYS — for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) and subordinating (because, although, unless, if, when, while, etc.).

6. Voice (Active/Passive): Active: Subject acts. Passive: Subject receives action. Conversion rules for different tenses.

7. Speech (Direct/Indirect): Rules for converting direct speech to indirect — changes in tense, pronouns, time expressions.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Subject-Verb Agreement)
Choose the correct option: The committee _______ divided on the issue of budget allocation.

a) is b) are c) has d) have
Solution: a) is. 'Committee' is a collective noun that is treated as singular when the committee acts as a single unit. Here 'is divided' refers to the group as one unit.
Example 2 (Articles)
Fill in the blank with the correct article: He is _______ honest man.

a) a b) an c) the d) no article
Solution: b) an. 'Honest' begins with a consonant letter 'h' but is pronounced with a vowel sound ('on-est'). Therefore, the article 'an' is used.
Example 3 (Tense)
Identify the error: She has been working here since five years.

a) She has been b) working here c) since d) five years
Solution: c) since. 'Since' is used with a point in time (e.g., since 2019, since Monday). For a duration of time, use 'for' (e.g., for five years). The correct sentence: She has been working here for five years.

Shortcut Techniques

  • When in doubt about subject-verb agreement, remove the phrase between the subject and verb to see the core subject-verb pair.
  • 'Each', 'every', 'either', 'neither', 'anyone', 'everyone' take singular verbs.
  • Use 'since' with a point in time, 'for' with a duration of time.
  • 'Between' is used for two items; 'among' for three or more.
  • 'Fewer' for countable nouns; 'less' for uncountable nouns.

The Subjunctive Mood and Its Triggers

The subjunctive mood expresses wishes, demands, suggestions, necessity, or hypothetical situations that are contrary to fact. In modern English it survives in two main forms: the present subjunctive, which uses the base form of the verb for all persons (e.g., I suggest that he be present), and the past subjunctive, which for most verbs is identical to the past indicative except for the verb be, where were replaces was for all subjects (e.g., If I were you, I would apply). The present subjunctive is triggered by a limited set of verbs and adjectives that convey mandate, recommendation, or necessity — recommend, insist, require, demand, propose, essential, vital, imperative. These triggers are followed by a that-clause in which the verb appears in its base form regardless of the subject’s number or person. The past subjunctive appears primarily in conditional clauses introduced by if, as if, as though, suppose, wish and in constructions such as it is time + past subjunctive (It is time we left). A common error is to use the indicative was after if in counterfactual conditions; the correct form is were for all persons. The subjunctive also appears in fixed formulas such as God save the Queen or Long live the King, where the base form conveys a wish. In formal writing, the subjunctive is preferred over modal alternatives (should + infinitive) because it signals a higher degree of formality and precision. Examiners often test the distinction between If he was late (indicative, real possibility) and If he were late (subjunctive, hypothetical). Mastery of the trigger list and the base‑form rule eliminates the majority of subjunctive‑related errors.

Perfect Progressive Tenses: Form, Meaning, and Common Errors

The perfect progressive (also called perfect continuous) tenses combine the perfect aspect (have/has/had + been) with the progressive aspect (verb‑ing). There are three primary forms: present perfect progressive (have/has been + -ing), past perfect progressive (had been + -ing), and future perfect progressive (will have been + -ing). Each emphasizes the duration of an action up to a reference point. The present perfect progressive (She has been studying for three hours) highlights an activity that began in the past and continues into the present, often with a time‑duration phrase (for, since). The past perfect progressive (They had been waiting for an hour when the bus arrived) stresses the length of an action that was ongoing before another past event. The future perfect progressive (By next June, I will have been working here for a decade) projects the duration of an activity up to a future moment. A frequent mistake is confusing since (point in time) with for (duration) in perfect progressive contexts; since must accompany a specific starting point (since 2019), while for accompanies a length of time (for five years). Another error is using the simple perfect where the progressive is required to convey ongoing nature (He has written the report vs. He has been writing the report). The progressive form also interacts with stative verbs: verbs such as know, believe, own rarely appear in progressive forms, so I have been knowing him is ungrammatical; the correct form is I have known him. In conditional sentences, the perfect progressive can appear in the result clause of a third conditional (If she had been practicing, she would have been performing better), but the form is rare and often replaced by simpler constructions. Recognizing the time markers (by the time, for, since, how long) and the aspectual nuance (ongoing vs. completed) allows rapid identification of the correct tense in error‑detection items.

Identifying the correct perfect progressive form
Choose the option that correctly completes the sentence: By the time the manager arrives, the team _______ the presentation for two hours.
a) will have prepared
b) will have been preparing
c) have been preparing
d) had been preparing
The sentence contains the future time marker By the time the manager arrives, which sets a future reference point. The phrase for two hours indicates duration of an ongoing action up to that point. The future perfect progressive (will have been + -ing) is the only tense that simultaneously projects forward and emphasizes continuous duration. Option (b) will have been preparing matches this requirement. Option (a) is future perfect simple, which stresses completion rather than continuity. Option (c) is present perfect progressive, anchored to the present, not the future. Option (d) is past perfect progressive, anchored to a past reference. Therefore, the correct answer is (b).
Exam‑day strategy for perfect progressive items
When a sentence includes a future‑point marker such as by the time, by next year, before combined with a duration phrase (for, since), immediately anticipate the future perfect progressive. Scan the answer choices for will have been + -ing; if it appears, it is almost always the intended answer. Conversely, if the marker is a past point (by the time he left) with a duration, look for had been + -ing. This pattern‑recognition shortcut reduces analysis time to under five seconds per item.

Determiners Beyond Articles: Quantifiers, Demonstratives, and Possessives

While articles (a, an, the) are the most frequent determiners, a full command of English grammar requires precise use of quantifiers, demonstratives, and possessive determiners. Quantifiers such as some, any, much, many, few, little, a few, a little, each, every, either, neither, all, both, half differ in countability sensitivity and polarity. Many and few modify plural count nouns; much and little modify uncountable nouns. The addition of the indefinite article creates a positive nuance: a few (some, but not many) versus few (almost none); a little versus little. Some appears in affirmative statements and offers; any appears in negatives, questions, and conditional clauses. Each and every are singular distributive quantifiers; they take singular verbs (Each of the candidates has submitted a form). Either and neither refer to two alternatives and also take singular verbs. All and both can be used with plural count nouns and uncountable nouns; all may precede the (all the students) or stand alone (all students). Demonstratives this, that, these, those indicate proximity and number; they function as determiners when followed by a noun (this book) and as pronouns when standing alone (This is mine). Possessive determiners (my, your, his, her, its, our, their) precede nouns and never co‑occur with articles (my book, not the my book). A frequent error is the double determiner construction (the my car) or mismatched quantifier‑noun agreement (much books). In formal writing, each and every are preferred over each and every as a redundant pair. Examiners test the distinction between few and a few in sentence‑completion items, and the correct choice hinges on whether the context implies a negative or positive connotation.

Preposition Selection with Adjectives, Verbs, and Nouns

Preposition choice is governed by collocational patterns rather than pure logic. Certain adjectives license specific prepositions: interested in, good at, responsible for, aware of, capable of, dependent on, similar to, different from (or than in American usage), afraid of, fond of. Verbs also dictate prepositions: rely on, insist on, apologize for, object to, contribute to, lead to, result in, consist of, consist in. Nouns derived from these verbs or adjectives often retain the same preposition: interest in, reliance on, apology for, objection to, contribution to, similarity to, difference from. A systematic approach is to learn the adjective/verb + preposition pairs as lexical chunks. Errors arise when learners transfer L1 preposition patterns (e.g., married with instead of married to) or overgeneralize a single preposition across a semantic field. The prepositions in, on, at for time and place follow hierarchical specificity: at for precise points (at 5 p.m., at the corner), on for days and surfaces (on Monday, on the table), in for longer periods and enclosed spaces (in July, in the room). For movement, to indicates direction toward a destination, into emphasizes entry into an enclosed space, onto emphasizes movement onto a surface. The distinction between between (two entities) and among (three or more) is tested frequently; however, between can also be used when distinct individual relationships are highlighted among multiple parties (negotiations between the five nations). Mastery of these collocations reduces guesswork in fill‑in‑the‑blank and error‑detection sections.

Complex Subject‑Verb Agreement: Collective, Compound, and Inverted Structures

Beyond the basic rule that a singular subject takes a singular verb and a plural subject takes a plural verb, several structures create agreement traps. Collective nouns (team, committee, family, audience, staff) can be singular or plural depending on whether the group acts as a unit or as individuals. In British English, plural verbs are common when the focus is on members (The committee are divided); in American English, the singular is preferred (The committee is divided). Compound subjects joined by and are plural (The director and the producer have arrived), but when the two nouns refer to a single entity, the verb is singular (Bread and butter is my breakfast). With or, nor, either…or, neither…nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject (proximity rule): Neither the manager nor the employees are responsible. Indefinite pronouns such as everyone, somebody, each, either, neither are singular; both, few, many, several are plural; all, any, most, some vary with the noun they quantify. Inverted sentences (where the verb precedes the subject) often obscure the true subject: There are many reasons (plural), Here comes the bus (singular). Questions beginning with What or Who follow the same principle: What are the criteria? vs. What is the criterion?. Relative clauses can also mislead: One of the students who have passed (plural verb because who refers to students) versus The only one of the students who has passed (singular because the only one is the antecedent). Recognizing the head noun, the conjunction type, and the inversion pattern enables accurate verb selection in even the most convoluted sentences.

  • Using was instead of were in counterfactual if-clauses.
  • Confusing since (point in time) with for (duration) in perfect progressive tenses.
  • Applying many to uncountable nouns or much to countable nouns.
  • Inserting an article before a possessive determiner (the my book).
  • Choosing between for more than two items when the context does not emphasize pairwise relations.
  • Treating a collective noun as plural in American‑style formal writing where singular is standard.
  • Remember that the subjunctive mood never takes a third‑person singular ‑s ending in present constructions.

Conditional Sentences: Real, Unreal, Mixed, and Inverted Forms

Conditional sentences express a hypothesis and its consequence. The four canonical types are zero, first, second, and third conditionals, each distinguished by tense‑aspect combinations in the if‑clause and the main clause. Zero conditionals use present simple in both clauses to state universal facts (If water reaches 100 °C, it boils). First conditionals pair present simple with will + bare infinitive to predict likely future outcomes (If it rains, the match will be cancelled). Second conditionals employ past simple in the if‑clause and would + bare infinitive in the main clause for present or future unreal situations (If I knew the answer, I would tell you). Third conditionals combine past perfect with would have + past participle for past counterfactuals (If she had studied, she would have passed).

Mixed conditionals blend time references: a past condition with a present result (If I had taken the job, I would be living in Mumbai) or a present condition with a past result (If he were more careful, he would not have lost the file). In formal writing, inversion replaces if for stylistic elevation: Had I known, I would have acted (third conditional), Were she here, she would agree (second conditional), Should you need help, call me (first conditional). The inverted forms never retain if and require the auxiliary (had, were, should) to precede the subject. Examiners frequently test the distinction between were (subjunctive) and was in second conditionals; the prescriptive rule mandates were for all persons, though was appears in informal speech.

Common error patterns include using would in the if‑clause (*If I would know), mixing will with past simple (*If it rained, it will stop), and omitting the perfect auxiliary in third conditionals (*If he studied, he would have passed). Mastery of the tense‑aspect matrix and the inversion rule enables rapid identification of the correct conditional type in sentence‑completion and error‑detection items.

Reported Speech: Backshifting Rules, Modal Transformations, and Reporting Verb Patterns

Reported (indirect) speech shifts the deictic centre from the original speaker to the reporter. The primary mechanical rule is backshifting: present simple becomes past simple, present continuous becomes past continuous, present perfect becomes past perfect, and will becomes would. Modal verbs follow a predictable ladder: can → could, may → might, must → had to (or must when expressing logical necessity), shall → should (for offers) or would (for future reference). The semi‑modals have to and need to shift to had to and needed to respectively. When the reporting verb is in the present, present perfect, or future (He says, He has said, He will say), backshifting is optional; the original tenses may be retained.

Pronouns, demonstratives, and time/place adverbials also adjust: I → he/she, this → that, now → then, today → that day, here → there. Reporting verbs dictate clause structure: say and tell take a that‑clause (with optional that), ask and wonder introduce embedded questions with if or whether, order, request, advise license a to‑infinitive complement (He ordered me to leave), while suggest and recommend trigger a subjunctive that‑clause (She suggested that he go). The verb promise patterns with a to‑infinitive (He promised to help) and deny with a gerund (He denied stealing).

Typical traps involve failing to backshift when the reporting verb is past (*He said he is coming), retaining will instead of would (*She said she will call), and mis‑aligning pronoun case (*He told me that I should go when the original was You should go). In exam items, the correct answer often hinges on recognizing whether the reporting verb permits a that‑clause or requires an infinitive/gerund complement, and on applying the modal ladder consistently.

Worked Example: Reported Speech Transformation
Convert the following direct speech into indirect speech: “I will finish the report by tomorrow,” she said.
The reporting verb said is past, so backshift applies. will becomes would. The pronoun I shifts to she. The time adverbial tomorrow becomes the next day (or the following day). Result: She said that she would finish the report by the next day.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a reported‑speech question appears, first note the tense of the reporting verb. If it is past, automatically backshift all modals and tenses; if it is present/present perfect/future, keep the original forms. Then scan for pronoun, demonstrative, and time‑place shifts. Finally, verify that the reporting verb’s complement pattern (that‑clause, infinitive, gerund, embedded question) matches the transformed clause.

Parallelism, Ellipsis, and Comparative Correlatives

Parallelism demands that coordinated elements share the same grammatical form. In lists, each item must be a noun phrase, gerund phrase, infinitive phrase, or full clause consistently (She likes reading, writing, and to edit is faulty; correct: reading, writing, and editing). Correlative conjunctions (both…and, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also) enforce strict symmetry: the structure following the first correlative must mirror the structure following the second. Not only did he arrive late, but also he forgot the files is parallel because both clauses are inverted; Not only did he arrive late, but also forgetting the files breaks parallelism.

Ellipsis omits repeated material when the meaning remains recoverable. In comparative clauses, the verb phrase is often deleted: She runs faster than he (runs). The omitted verb must be identical in form to the antecedent; mismatched auxiliaries create errors (*She has finished earlier than he did should be She finished earlier than he did or She has finished earlier than he has). Gapping removes the verb from the second conjunct of a coordinate structure: John bought apples, and Mary pears. Stripping removes the subject and auxiliary from a subordinate clause: While (he was) walking, he listened. Examiners test whether the ellipted material can be uniquely reconstructed; ambiguous ellipsis is penalised.

Comparative correlatives (the more…the more, the less…the less) require parallel comparative phrases: The more you practise, the better you become. Both clauses must be comparative (often with the + comparative adjective/adverb). A frequent mistake is mixing a comparative with a positive form (*The more you study, the good you get). Additionally, the order of the two clauses is fixed; reversing them changes the logical relation and is considered ungrammatical in standard usage.

Advanced Punctuation: Comma Splices, Semicolons, Colons, and Dashes in Complex Constructions

A comma splice occurs when two independent clauses are joined only by a comma (The data was incomplete, the analysis failed). Corrections include inserting a coordinating conjunction (The data was incomplete, so the analysis failed), replacing the comma with a semicolon (The data was incomplete; the analysis failed), or splitting into two sentences. Semicolons also separate items in a series when the items themselves contain commas (The committee comprised Dr. Rao, the chair; Ms. Patel, the secretary; and Mr. Singh, the treasurer).

Colons introduce an explanation, list, or quotation that follows an independent clause. The clause preceding the colon must be complete (She brought three items: a pen, a notebook, and a laptop). A colon must not separate a verb from its direct object or a preposition from its object (*The ingredients are: flour, sugar, eggs is incorrect; correct: The ingredients are flour, sugar, and eggs). Dashes (em dashes) mark a sharp break or an appositive that contains internal commas: The winner—who had trained for years—celebrated quietly. A pair of dashes functions like parentheses but with greater emphasis.

In complex sentences, a semicolon may precede a conjunctive adverb (however, therefore, moreover) linking two independent clauses: The budget was tight; however, the project proceeded. The conjunctive adverb is followed by a comma. Misplacing the semicolon after the adverb (*The budget was tight, however; the project proceeded) is a common error. Mastery of these punctuation rules directly impacts scores in error‑detection and sentence‑improvement sections.

  • Using would in the if‑clause of a conditional instead of the required past simple or past perfect.
  • Failing to backshift modals when the reporting verb is past, leaving will or can unchanged.
  • Breaking parallelism with correlative conjunctions by mixing a clause with a phrase.
  • Ellipsis that leaves the omitted verb ambiguous or mismatched in tense/aspect.
  • Treating a colon as a separator between a verb and its object or a preposition and its object.
  • Placing a semicolon after a conjunctive adverb rather than before it.
  • Confusing the fixed order of comparative correlatives, reversing the more…the more to the more…the more with swapped clauses.

Modal Verbs: Core Modals, Semi‑Modals, and Perfect Modal Constructions

The nine core modals — can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must — are defective verbs: they lack infinitive, participle, and third‑person singular forms. Their semantic range covers ability, permission, possibility, necessity, obligation, prediction, and volition. A frequent examination focus is the distinction between epistemic (speaker’s judgment about truth) and deontic (obligation/permission) readings. For example, He must be tired (epistemic deduction) versus He must finish the report (deontic obligation).

Semi‑modals — need, dare, ought to, used to, have to, had better — behave like modals in some contexts but retain non‑modal forms elsewhere. Need and dare operate as modals only in negative and interrogative sentences (Need he go?, He dare not refuse); in affirmative statements they require the full infinitive with to (He needs to go). Ought to always takes the to-infinitive and has no past form; past obligation is expressed by should have + past participle. Used to exists only in the past; its negative and interrogative forms vary between did not use to and used not to (the latter more formal). Have to and had better are periphrastic: have to inflects for tense and person (She has to leave), while had better is invariant and followed by the bare infinitive (You had better check).

Perfect modal constructions combine a modal with have + past participle to locate the modal meaning in the past. The pattern modal + have + V3 yields distinct nuances: could have done (past ability not exercised), should have done (unfulfilled obligation), must have done (strong deduction about a past event), may/might have done (past possibility), would have done (hypothetical result in a counterfactual past). Examiners often test the incorrect substitution of would have for could have in ability contexts, or the omission of have after must in deductions (*He must done it).

Negation placement alters meaning: cannot have been (impossibility in the past) versus could not have been (same, slightly more formal). Need not have + V3 indicates an unnecessary action that was performed, while did not need to + V1 signals that the action was not required and typically not performed. This contrast is a staple of error‑detection items.

In reported speech, modals backshift according to the reporting verb tense: can → could, may → might, will → would, shall → should, must → had to (for obligation) or remains must (for deduction). Semi‑modals follow the same principle: have to → had to, ought to → ought to (unchanged), used to → used to (unchanged). Mastery of these shifts prevents the common error of leaving will or can unchanged after a past reporting verb.

Non‑Finite Verb Forms: Gerunds, Infinitives, Participles, and Their Syntactic Roles

English non‑finite forms — gerund (‑ing), to‑infinitive, bare infinitive, present participle (‑ing), and past participle (‑ed/en) — function as nouns, adjectives, or adverbs without showing tense or agreement. The gerund and present participle share the ‑ing form but differ syntactically: a gerund heads a noun phrase (Swimming improves stamina), while a present participle modifies a noun or forms a participial clause (The swimming child laughed, Swimming across the lake, he felt cold).

Infinitives appear in two guises. The to‑infinitive follows many verbs (decide to go), adjectives (easy to learn), and nouns (the decision to leave). The bare infinitive occurs after modal auxiliaries, causative verbs (make, let, have), perception verbs (see, hear, watch) in active voice, and the idioms had better, would rather. A frequent trap is the misuse of the to‑infinitive after make in the active (*They made him to apologise → They made him apologise) while the passive restores to (He was made to apologise).

Perfect and passive non‑finite forms extend the temporal and voice dimensions. The perfect gerund (having + V3) and perfect infinitive (to have + V3) denote anteriority relative to the main verb: He denied having taken the file, She claims to have finished. The passive gerund (being + V3) and passive infinitive (to be + V3) shift voice: He dislikes being interrupted, The report is expected to be submitted. Combining both yields having been + V3 and to have been + V3 (He recalled having been warned).

Participle clauses (also called reduced clauses) replace full adverbial or relative clauses when the subject is identical. A present participle clause expresses simultaneous or cause‑effect action (Knowing the rules, she played well). A past participle clause carries passive meaning (Written in simple language, the manual is accessible). A perfect participle clause (having + V3) marks anteriority (Having finished the work, he left). Dangling participles arise when the implied subject of the participle differs from the main clause subject (*Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful → Walking down the street, I saw beautiful trees).

Gerunds and infinitives can both serve as subjects, objects, or complements, but certain verbs select one exclusively. Verbs of preference (enjoy, avoid, mind) take gerunds; verbs of intention (decide, plan, hope) take infinitives. Some verbs accept both with a meaning shift: remember to do (future obligation) versus remember doing (past memory); stop to do (pause for a new action) versus stop doing (cease an ongoing action). Examiners test these alternations in sentence‑improvement and fill‑in‑the‑blank items.

Error Detection – Non‑Finite Selection
Identify the error: She avoided to meet him because she feared to be recognised.
Error: avoided to meet and feared to be recognised. Correction: She avoided meeting him because she feared being recognised. Reason: Avoid and fear (in the sense of ‘worry about’) require a gerund, not a to‑infinitive. The passive gerund being recognised correctly follows fear.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Non‑Finites
When a verb is underlined, first classify it: main verb, modal, or non‑finite. If non‑finite, check the governing verb or adjective for its complement pattern (gerund vs infinitive). Memorise the high‑frequency verb lists: enjoy, avoid, mind, consider, suggest → gerund; decide, plan, hope, agree, refuse → infinitive. For perception and causative verbs, note voice: active → bare infinitive, passive → to‑infinitive. Spot dangling participles by asking “Who performs the ‑ing action?” — the answer must be the subject of the main clause.

Relative Clauses: Defining, Non‑Defining, Reduced, and Cleft Structures

Relative clauses modify nouns and are introduced by relative pronouns (who, whom, whose, which, that) or relative adverbs (where, when, why). A defining (restrictive) clause supplies essential identification and takes no commas: The candidate who scored highest will be promoted. A non‑defining (non‑restrictive) clause adds supplementary information and is set off by commas: My brother, who lives in Mumbai, is visiting. The pronoun that cannot introduce a non‑defining clause, nor can it follow a preposition; the report in that we trust is ungrammatical — use which instead.

Choice between who/whom and which/that follows animacy and register. Whom is the object form of who and appears after prepositions (the person to whom I spoke) or as direct object in formal writing (the candidate whom we selected). In everyday usage who often replaces whom, but examinations preserve the distinction. Whose functions as a possessive relative for both animate and inanimate antecedents (the house whose roof leaked).

Reduced relative clauses delete the relative pronoun and the auxiliary be, converting the verb to a participle. A defining clause with a passive verb becomes a past participle phrase: The documents (which were) required are ready → The documents required are ready. An active progressive clause becomes a present participle phrase: Students (who are) waiting outside may enter → Students waiting outside may enter. Reduction is impossible when the relative clause contains a modal or a perfect tense (*The files which have been sent cannot become *The files sent without ambiguity).

Cleft sentences (it‑cleft and wh‑cleft) foreground a constituent by placing it after it is/was or at the start of a wh‑clause. It was the manager who approved the loan emphasises the subject; What the manager approved was the loan emphasises the object. The verb in the relative part agrees with the focused element: It is the players who are responsible (plural). Clefts are tested for subject‑verb agreement and for the correct use of who/which/that after the focus.

Preposition placement in relative clauses offers two patterns: pied‑piping (the department in which he works) and stranding (the department which he works in). Pied‑piping is mandatory in formal writing when the relative pronoun is whom or which and the preposition is monosyllabic; stranding is acceptable in less formal contexts but never with whose or where. Examiners often present a sentence with a stranded preposition after whom (*the officer whom he spoke to) and expect correction to the officer to whom he spoke.

Voice Transformations: Causative, Get‑Passive, and Modal Passive Patterns

English voice alternations extend beyond the simple active‑passive pair. The causative construction uses have or get followed by an object and a past participle to indicate that the subject arranges for someone else to perform the action: She had her car repaired, He got his hair cut. Have is neutral; get is more informal and often implies effort or result. The agent may be expressed with a by-phrase (She had the report written by her assistant) but is frequently omitted.

The get‑passive replaces be with get in passive clauses, typically highlighting an adverse or unexpected outcome: He got fired, The window got broken. It is restricted to dynamic verbs (actions) and rarely appears with stative verbs (*The fact got known is marginal). In questions and negatives, get follows the auxiliary do: Did he get promoted?, She didn’t get invited. The get‑passive cannot be used with modal perfects (*He must have got chosen → He must have been chosen).

Modal passive forms combine a modal auxiliary with be + past participle: can be done, should be avoided, might have been seen. The perfect modal passive (modal + have been + V3) expresses past obligation, possibility, or deduction in the passive voice: The letter should have been sent yesterday. A common error is the insertion of to after the modal (*must to be done) or the omission of been in the perfect form (*should have sent when passive is required).

Double passives — a passive infinitive embedded under a passive matrix verb — appear in formal prose: The project is expected to be completed. The subject of the matrix clause (project) is also the logical object of the infinitive. Transformation exercises often require converting an active sentence with a complex object (They expect the team to finish the project) into the double passive (The project is expected to be finished by the team).

Impersonal passives with reporting verbs (It is said that…, He is believed to…) allow the subject of the subordinate clause to become the subject of the main clause: People say he is honest → He is said to be honest. The infinitive after the passive reporting verb is typically the to‑infinitive (present or perfect). When the subordinate clause contains a modal, the modal is retained in the infinitive: They think he can win → He is thought to be able to win (not *to can win).

Adverbial Clause Connectors: Concession, Purpose, Result, and Conditional Variants

Adverbial clauses are introduced by subordinating conjunctions that signal logical relations. Concession uses although, though, even though, while, whereas. Though can appear in a reduced clause at the end of a sentence (He accepted the offer, though reluctantly). Even if and even though differ: even if introduces a hypothetical concession (Even if it rains, we will go), while even though concedes a known fact (Even though it rained, we went).

Purpose clauses employ so that, in order that, so as to, in order to, to. The finite forms (so that, in order that) require a modal (can, could, will, would) in the subordinate clause: She left early so that she could catch the train. The non‑finite forms (to, in order to, so as to) share the subject with the main clause; a different subject forces a finite clause (She gave him a map so that he could find the way). So as not to and in order not to express negative purpose.

Result clauses follow so…that, such…that. So modifies an adjective or adverb (so tired that he slept); such modifies a noun phrase (such a noise that we woke). The that may be omitted in informal usage but is required in formal writing. A frequent error is using so with a noun (*so a good player that → such a good player that).

Conditional variants extend beyond the three basic types. Unless equals if not and takes a present tense for future reference (Unless he arrives soon, we will leave). Provided (that), providing (that), as long as, so long as impose a condition with a stronger sense of requirement. Suppose (that), supposing (that), imagine (that) introduce hypothetical scenarios, often with would in the main clause. In case expresses precaution and is followed by a present or past tense, never would (Take an umbrella in case it rains). Even if and whether…or convey exhaustive conditions (Whether you like it or not, you must attend).

Inversion in conditional clauses occurs when if is omitted and the auxiliary is fronted: Had I known, I would have told you (past perfect), Were he to arrive, we would start (past subjunctive), Should you need help, call me (present). This formal construction is a favourite in sentence‑reordering and error‑spotting items. The inverted form never co‑occurs with if; *Had if I known is ungrammatical.

  • Treating need and dare as modals in affirmative statements (e.g., *He need go instead of He needs to go).
  • Using a to‑infinitive after causative make or perception verbs in the active voice (*They made him to sign).
  • Omitting been in perfect modal passives (*The work should have completed instead of should have been completed).
  • Stranding a preposition after whom or in a non‑defining clause (*the officer whom I spoke to).
  • Confusing even if (hypothetical) with even though (factual) in concession clauses.
  • Dropping that in result clauses with so/such in formal writing (*He was so tired he slept).
  • Failing to invert the auxiliary when if is omitted in conditional clauses (*Had I known I would have told is correct; *If had I known is not).

Negative and Restrictive Inversion: Fronted Adverbials and Auxiliary Fronting

When a negative or restrictive adverbial phrase occupies clause‑initial position, the finite auxiliary must precede the subject. Typical triggers include never, rarely, seldom, hardly, scarcely, barely, little, no sooner, not only, not until, only after, only when, only if, only by. The inversion applies only when the fronted element modifies the whole clause; if the fronted phrase is a subject complement or a simple adverb of manner, inversion does not occur. For example, Never have I seen such chaos shows inversion, whereas Only John left early does not, because only modifies the subject, not the clause.

The auxiliary that inverts is the first auxiliary of the verb phrase. In simple tenses the dummy auxiliary do/does/did is inserted: Rarely does she complain. In perfect or progressive forms the existing auxiliary moves: Hardly had they arrived when the storm broke. With modal verbs the modal itself inverts: Seldom can we predict the outcome. A common error is to invert a lexical verb directly (*Never goes he there) or to omit the auxiliary in perfect constructions (*Hardly they had arrived).

Restrictive inversion with only follows a distinct pattern. When only precedes a prepositional phrase, an adverbial clause, or a non‑subject NP, inversion is obligatory: Only after the meeting did he speak. If only modifies the subject, the word order remains canonical: Only the manager approved the plan. The same restriction applies to not only … but also constructions: Not only did he arrive late, but he also forgot the files.

Inversion also appears with so and such in result clauses when they are fronted for emphasis: So intense was the heat that the rails buckled. This mirrors the conditional inversion pattern but is triggered by degree adverbials rather than conditionals. The auxiliary inversion rule remains identical: the first auxiliary (or dummy do) precedes the subject.

Examiners frequently test the distinction between clause‑initial negative adverbials and subject‑focus particles. A sentence such as Little did he know the truth is grammatical because little functions as a negative adverbial; Little he knew the truth is ungrammatical. Mastery of the auxiliary‑fronting rule across tense, aspect, and modality is essential for error‑spotting and sentence‑reordering items.

Verb Complementation Patterns: Control, Raising, and Exceptional Case Marking

English verbs select specific complement types, and the syntactic relationship between the matrix verb and the embedded subject determines whether the construction is control, raising, or exceptional case marking (ECM). In subject control the matrix subject is understood as the subject of the non‑finite clause: She promised to leave (the understood subject of to leave is she). In object control the matrix object controls the embedded subject: She persuaded him to leave (the understood subject of to leave is him). Both control verbs assign a thematic role to the controller and require an obligatory PRO in the embedded subject position.

Raising verbs such as seem, appear, happen, turn out, be likely, be certain do not assign a thematic role to their syntactic subject. Instead, the subject of the embedded clause raises to become the subject of the matrix clause: He seems to be tired (underlying It seems [he to be tired]). The raised DP receives case from the matrix finite verb (nominative) and no PRO is present. Raising can also be object‑to‑subject (e.g., I believe him to be honest), where the embedded subject him receives accusative case from the matrix verb believe.

Exceptional Case Marking (ECM) verbs — believe, consider, expect, find, prove, want — assign accusative case to the subject of a non‑finite clause without raising it to matrix subject position. The embedded subject remains in the lower clause but bears accusative case: We expect the team to win. The matrix verb does not assign a theta‑role to the embedded subject; the subject receives its role from the embedded predicate. ECM differs from object control because the matrix verb does not semantically select the embedded subject as an argument.

Diagnostics distinguish the three patterns. Passivization of the matrix verb preserves the embedded subject in raising and ECM but not in control: He is believed to be honest (raising) vs. *He was persuaded to leave (control — ungrammatical because the controller is the object). Expletive it can appear with raising verbs (It seems that he is tired) but not with control or ECM verbs. Adverbial modification of the embedded clause (e.g., quickly) attaches to the embedded predicate in all three, but only control allows a purpose adjunct linked to the controller.

Typical exam traps involve misidentifying expect as a control verb (it is ECM) or treating seem as an ECM verb (it is raising). Recognising the case‑assignment properties — nominative for raised subjects, accusative for ECM subjects, and null PRO for controlled subjects — enables rapid classification in sentence‑correction and fill‑in‑the‑blank items.

Complementation Classification
Identify the complementation type in the sentence: "The director expects the actors to rehearse tomorrow."
The matrix verb expects is an ECM verb. It assigns accusative case to the embedded subject the actors, which remains in the lower clause as the subject of the infinitive to rehearse. No raising to matrix subject occurs, and there is no controller PRO. Therefore the construction exemplifies Exceptional Case Marking.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a sentence contains a matrix verb followed by an NP + to‑infinitive, first ask whether the NP receives a theta‑role from the matrix verb. If yes → object control. If the NP receives no theta‑role but gets accusative case → ECM. If the NP moves to matrix subject position and receives nominative case → raising. Use passivization and expletive it tests mentally to confirm.

Determiner Phrase Architecture: Predeterminers, Central Determiners, Postdeterminers, and Quantifier Float

The internal structure of a Determiner Phrase (DP) follows a fixed linear order: Predeterminer → Central Determiner → Postdeterminer → Noun. Predeterminers include all, both, half, double, twice, three times and occur before articles or demonstratives: all the students, both my friends, half an hour. They cannot co‑occur with each other (*all both the books). Central determiners comprise articles (a, the), demonstratives (this, that, these, those), possessives (my, your, his), and quantificational determiners such as some, any, each, every, no, either, neither. Only one central determiner may appear in a DP.

Postdeterminers follow the central determiner and include cardinal numerals (one, two, three), ordinal numerals (first, second), and quantificational adjectives such as many, few, several, numerous, various. Example: the three first chapters (central the + postdeterminers three first). Postdeterminers can stack, but the order is generally numeral → ordinal → quantifier: the two first many attempts is marginal; preferred is the first two many attempts (though many usually precedes numerals in practice).

Quantifier Float allows certain quantifiers (all, both, each) to appear separated from the DP they quantify, typically after the auxiliary or main verb: The students have all passed (float of all from all the students). Floating is possible only with quantifiers that are predeterminers or central determiners in their base position. Each can float only when it originates as a central determiner: The children have each received a gift. Every and no do not float (*The students have every passed). The floated quantifier must be adjacent to the verb phrase; intervening adverbs block the construction (*The students have quickly all passed).

Agreement rules follow the head noun, not the quantifier. All the water is contaminated (singular) versus All the bottles are broken (plural). With both the noun is always plural: Both the doors are locked. Half agrees with the noun it quantifies: Half the cake is gone vs. Half the cakes are gone. Errors arise when test‑takers force plural verbs after all with singular mass nouns.

Exam items often present a DP with multiple determiners and ask which ordering is grammatical. The only licit sequence is Predeterminer → Central Determiner → Postdeterminer(s) → Noun. Any deviation (e.g., *the all students, *my both friends) signals an error. Recognising the slot each determiner occupies enables rapid elimination of impossible options in multiple‑choice questions.

Tense‑Aspect‑Modality Interactions: Future in the Past, Habitual Aspect, and Modal Perfects

Future in the past expresses a future event from a past perspective. The primary forms are would + bare infinitive (She said she would call), was/were going to + infinitive (They were going to leave), and was/were to + infinitive for scheduled or destined events (The president was to arrive at noon). Would is the past‑tense counterpart of will and retains its modal nuances (volition, prediction). Was/were going to conveys intention or plan, while was/were to often implies arrangement or fate. A common error is mixing would with going to (*She would going to call).

Habitual aspect in the past is expressed by used to + bare infinitive (He used to swim daily) and by would + bare infinitive when accompanied by a frequency adverbial (Every summer we would camp by the lake). Used to has no present‑tense form; present habitual uses simple present (He swims daily) or will for characteristic behaviour (A cat will always land on its feet). Would cannot replace used to without a temporal anchor (*He would smoke is ungrammatical unless a past time frame is established).

Modal perfects combine a modal verb with have + past participle to locate modality in the past. The core set includes must have, could have, may have, might have, should have, ought to have, need not have, dare not have. Each conveys a distinct epistemic or deontic reading: must have = strong deduction (He must have left); could have = past possibility not realized (She could have won); should have = unfulfilled obligation (You should have warned me). The perfect progressive modal (must have been working) adds ongoing aspect to the modal judgment.

Interaction of tense, aspect, and modality creates subtle meaning shifts. He would have been working can be a future‑in‑the‑past perfect progressive (predicted ongoing action) or a conditional perfect progressive (counterfactual). Disambiguation relies on context: a conditional clause (If he had stayed, he would have been working) forces the counterfactual reading. She will have finished by then (future perfect) differs from She would have finished by then (future‑in‑the‑past perfect). Examiners test the ability to select the correct modal perfect for a given temporal‑modal scenario.

Key traps: using would have for simple past deduction (*He would have left instead of He must have left), omitting been in modal perfect progressive (*He must have working), and confusing used to with would in present habitual contexts. Mastery of the three‑way distinction — future in the past, habitual, modal perfect — is essential for sentence‑completion and error‑detection items.

Preposition Stranding versus Pied‑Piping in Wh‑Clauses and Relative Clauses

English permits two strategies when a wh‑phrase is the complement of a preposition. Pied‑piping moves the preposition together with the wh‑word to the front of the clause: To whom did you speak?, The report on which we relied. Preposition stranding leaves the preposition in situ, typically at clause end: Who did you speak to?, The report which we relied on. Both are grammatical, but register and syntactic environment dictate preference.

In formal writing and in non‑defining relative clauses, pied‑piping is strongly preferred, especially with whom, which, whose. Stranding whom (*The person whom I spoke to) is considered non‑standard in formal contexts. With who and which in defining relatives, stranding is widely accepted: The book which I referred to. However, when the preposition is part of a fixed collocation (e.g., look after, rely on, consist of), pied‑piping preserves the idiom: The child after whom she looked versus the awkward The child who she looked after.

Interrogative clauses show a similar split. Direct questions in formal style use pied‑piping: For what reason did he resign?. Informal speech favours stranding: What did he resign for?. In indirect questions, the same register considerations apply: She asked to whom the letter was addressed (formal) vs. She asked who the letter was addressed to (informal).

Certain syntactic configurations block stranding. In relative clauses introduced by that, the preposition cannot be stranded: *The house that I live in is acceptable, but *The house in that I live is ungrammatical because that cannot be pied‑piped. With zero relativizer (contact clauses), stranding is obligatory: The house I live in. In comparative clauses (than), pied‑piping is rare; stranding is the norm: She is taller than I am (no preposition). When a preposition appears in a comparative (more interested in), the preposition stays: He is more interested in music than she is.

Exam traps include forcing pied‑piping with who in informal contexts (*To who did you give it?), stranding whom in formal prose, and attempting to pied‑pipe that. Recognising the register‑sensitivity and the syntactic restrictions on each relativizer enables accurate error‑spotting and sentence‑reordering decisions.

  • Treating only as a clause‑initial negative adverbial when it modifies the subject, leading to illicit inversion.
  • Misclassifying ECM verbs as object‑control verbs, resulting in incorrect passivization predictions.
  • Placing a postdeterminer before the central determiner (e.g., *three the books) violating DP order.
  • Using would for present habitual without a past time frame, confusing it with used to.
  • Stranding whom in formal writing or pied‑piping that in relative clauses.
  • Omitting been in modal perfect progressive constructions (e.g., *must have working).
  • Floating quantifiers that do not permit floating, such as every or no.

Cleft and Pseudo‑cleft Constructions: Focus, Information Structure, and Exam Patterns

Cleft sentences split a single clause into two parts to place a constituent in sharp focus. The canonical it‑cleft has the form it + be + focused element + relative clause: It was the manager who approved the proposal. The focused element can be a noun phrase, a prepositional phrase, an adverbial, or even a clause. A wh‑cleft (or pseudo‑cleft) uses a free relative headed by what (or who, where, when, why, how) as subject, followed by be and the focused complement: What the manager approved was the proposal. Both structures are information‑packaging devices: they mark the focused constituent as new or contrastive information while relegating the rest to presupposed background.

Examiners test three dimensions. First, case assignment: in an it‑cleft the focused NP retains the case it would have in the corresponding non‑cleft sentence (It was *he/him* who called → prescriptive he, colloquial him). Second, verb agreement: the verb be agrees with the focused element, not with the relative clause (It is the students who are responsible). Third, extraction constraints: a cleft cannot focus an element that is inside an island (e.g., a complex NP or adjunct clause). *It was the book that I met the author who wrote is illicit because the relative clause who wrote is an island.

Pseudo‑clefts allow a wider range of focused categories, including infinitival and gerundial complements: What she did was (to) rewrite the report. The to may appear or be omitted; both are acceptable, but the bare infinitive is more common in spoken registers. A reverse pseudo‑cleft places the what-clause after be: The proposal is what the manager approved. This order is frequent in written exposition where the topic precedes the comment.

Common error patterns in competitive items include: (1) using a that-clause instead of a relative clause after the focused element (*It was the manager that approved the proposal is acceptable only when that functions as a relative pronoun, but many keys treat it as a cleft‑specific error); (2) mismatching number between be and the focus (*It were the manager who approved); (3) inserting an extra it in pseudo‑clefts (*What it was the manager approved was the proposal). Mastery of these patterns enables rapid identification of the correct option in sentence‑completion and error‑spotting tasks.

Negation Scope, Polarity Items, and the Licensing of Negative Contexts

Negation in English is not a monolithic operator; its scope determines which constituents are semantically negated and which polarity‑sensitive items are licensed. A negative polarity item (NPI) such as any, ever, at all, a wink, lift a finger requires a licensing environment — typically a clause‑mate negation, a downward‑entailing quantifier (few, at most three), a conditional antecedent, or a comparative clause. For example, She didn’t see any birds is grammatical because not c‑commands any. In contrast, *She saw any birds fails because the positive clause lacks a licenser.

Scope ambiguities arise when multiple operators interact. In Every student didn’t pass, the preferred reading is not > every (i.e., Not every student passed), but a surface‑scope reading every > not (each student failed) is marginally available with heavy stress on didn’t. Examiners often present a sentence with an NPI and ask whether it is licensed; the test‑taker must trace the c‑command relation from the nearest negation or downward‑entailing operator to the NPI. Few students have ever visited the museum licenses ever because few is downward‑entailing. Many students have ever visited the museum does not, because many is upward‑entailing.

Negative concord (multiple negations yielding a single semantic negation) is non‑standard in formal English but appears in many dialects. Standard English treats I didn’t see nothing as a double negative yielding a positive meaning, which is considered an error in prescriptive testing. However, I didn’t see anything is the correct single‑negation form. Items that test “negative concord” usually require the candidate to replace nothing with anything or to restructure the sentence.

Another licensing context is the interrogative clause: Did you see any errors? licenses any because questions are semantically non‑assertive. Similarly, conditional antecedents (If you ever need help, call me) and comparatives (She runs faster than anyone expected) license NPIs. Recognising these environments allows quick elimination of answer choices that place an NPI in an unlicensed positive declarative clause.

Error‑Spotting Worked Example
Identify the segment that contains a grammatical error:
(A) Hardly had the meeting begun / (B) when the manager noticed / (C) that none of the participants / (D) had brought any documents.
The sentence uses the negative adverbial hardly which triggers subject‑auxiliary inversion (Hardly had the meeting begun) — correct. When introduces a temporal clause — correct. None of the participants is a negative quantifier; it licenses the NPI any in the following clause. However, none already carries negation, so the clause had brought any documents is doubly negated in standard English. The licensed form would be had brought no documents or hadn’t brought any documents. Therefore segment (D) contains the error.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When an NPI appears, instantly locate the nearest potential licenser (negation, few, at most, if, comparative). If none c‑commands the NPI, the sentence is faulty. Circle the licenser and the NPI; this visual link prevents overlooking scope mismatches under time pressure.

Coordination, Correlative Conjunctions, and Parallelism in Complex Sentences

Coordination joins constituents of identical syntactic category using and, or, but, nor, for, so, yet. Correlative pairs — both…and, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, whether…or — impose stricter parallelism: each correlative must precede a constituent of the same grammatical type. She not only sings but also dances coordinates two verb phrases; Not only the teacher but also the students were surprised coordinates two noun phrases. A frequent error is mixing categories: *She not only sings but also a dancer (verb phrase vs. noun phrase).

When coordination occurs inside a larger structure, the Coordinate Structure Constraint blocks extraction from a single conjunct. *What did you eat pizza and? is ungrammatical because what attempts to extract from only the second conjunct. However, Across‑the‑Board (ATB) extraction is permitted when the same element is extracted from all conjuncts: What did you eat and drink? (understood as What did you eat and what did you drink?). Test items often present a wh‑question with a coordinated verb phrase and ask whether the extraction is licit; the correct answer hinges on ATB compliance.

Parallelism also governs ellipsis in coordinated clauses. She can play the piano, and he can play the violin may be reduced to She can play the piano, and he can play the violin (no ellipsis) or She can play the piano, and he can (VP‑ellipsis) or She can play the piano, and he the violin (gapping). The ellipsis site must be recoverable from the antecedent conjunct; mismatched auxiliaries (She has finished, and he will) are acceptable because the auxiliary matches the tense‑aspect requirements of each conjunct.

Correlative conjunctions interact with negation. Neither the manager nor the staff was informed follows the proximity rule: the verb agrees with the nearest conjunct (staff → plural were in careful usage, but many prescriptive keys accept singular was when the first conjunct is singular). Either the manager or the staff are responsible likewise follows proximity. Examiners test both agreement and parallelism simultaneously, so candidates must check category matching first, then apply the proximity principle for subject‑verb agreement.

Phrasal Verbs: Separable, Inseparable, and Idiomatic Collocation Patterns

A phrasal verb consists of a lexical verb plus a particle (e.g., give up) or a preposition (e.g., look after) that together yield a meaning not predictable from the parts. The critical distinction for testing is separability. With a transitive phrasal verb that takes a particle, a noun‑phrase object may appear either between verb and particle (turn the light off) or after the particle (turn off the light). A pronoun object must intervene: turn it off (not *turn off it). Inseparable phrasal verbs (often prepositional verbs) never allow the object to intervene: look after the child (not *look the child after).

Three diagnostic tests identify the type. (1) Particle movement: if the particle can follow a full NP object, the verb is separable. (2) Adverb insertion: an adverb may appear between verb and particle only in separable verbs (turn quickly off is marginal but possible). (3) Passivization: prepositional verbs readily passivize (The child was looked after); particle verbs resist passivization unless the particle is stranded (The light was turned off is acceptable because off is a particle, but *The child was looked after is standard).

Idiomatic collocations create further traps. Make up can mean “reconcile” (separable: make it up) or “constitute” (inseparable: make up the team — object cannot intervene). Run into meaning “meet by chance” is inseparable (run into him, not *run him into). Examiners often present a sentence with a pronoun object placed incorrectly, or they ask to choose the correct particle/preposition for a given meaning. Mastery requires memorising the separability status of the most frequent 150‑200 phrasal verbs and recognising meaning‑dependent shifts.

Adjective Order, Post‑nominal Modification, and Determiner Interaction

English prenominal adjectives follow a relatively fixed hierarchy: opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → purpose (e.g., a lovely small old round red Italian leather racing car). Deviations sound marked or poetic. When multiple adjectives of the same semantic class appear, they are usually coordinated (a red and blue flag) or separated by commas if they are non‑coordinate (a long, winding road).

Post‑nominal modification (relative clauses, prepositional phrases, participial phrases) occurs after the head noun and does not obey the prenominal order. However, the presence of a post‑modifier can affect determiner choice. Definite article the is strongly preferred when a restrictive post‑modifier uniquely identifies the referent: the book that you recommended. Indefinite a/an appears with non‑restrictive or generic post‑modifiers: a book that interests me. Quantifiers such as every, each resist post‑modification by relative clauses (*every book that you read is acceptable, but *every book which you read is often flagged in prescriptive keys because which is non‑restrictive).

Determiner‑adjective interaction also governs the use of own, very, same. My own car (possessive + own) is grammatical; *the own car is not. The very idea uses very as an intensifier preceding a noun; *a very idea is impossible. The same mistake requires the definite article; *a same mistake is ungrammatical. These collocational restrictions are high‑yield error‑spotting targets.

Finally, adjective stacking with multiple determiners follows the DP architecture: predeterminers (all, both, half) → central determiners (the, a, my) → postdeterminers (many, few, three) → adjectives → noun. All the three large boxes respects the order; *the all three large boxes violates it. Recognising the slot each element occupies allows rapid correction of determiner‑order errors in sentence‑reordering tasks.

  • Treating a pseudo‑cleft what-clause as a free relative that can be omitted, leading to fragments like *Is what she wants.
  • Placing an NPI such as any in a clause licensed only by a non‑c‑commanding quantifier (e.g., *Many people have any idea).
  • Mixing syntactic categories in correlative coordination (*Not only the plan but also we must act).
  • Stranding a pronoun object after the particle in a separable phrasal verb (*pick up it instead of pick it up).
  • Reversing the canonical adjective order (*a red small car) or inserting a postdeterminer before the central determiner (*three the books).
  • Using which in a restrictive relative clause after a quantifier like every where prescriptive norms demand that or zero relativizer.
  • Assuming all phrasal verbs are separable, resulting in errors like *look the problem after.
The sections above equip candidates with the structural insight needed to navigate the most demanding grammar items.
