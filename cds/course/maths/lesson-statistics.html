Welcome to the Statistics and Probability module, where we transform raw numbers into meaningful insights and predict future outcomes based on chance. In this lesson, you will master the four pillars of data analysis: Mean, Median, Mode, and Variance, alongside the fundamental rules of probability. By the end of this session, you will be able to calculate central tendencies, understand data spread, and solve complex probability problems with confidence.

Mean

The mean is often called the average, but it is specifically defined as the sum of all values divided by the total count of those values. This formula works because it distributes the total value equally among every data point, creating a balance point for the dataset. Examiners typically ask you to calculate the mean from a list of numbers or find an unknown variable when the mean is given.

To calculate the mean, add up every single number in your set and then divide that total by how many numbers there are. If one value changes, the mean shifts immediately because it depends on the sum of all parts. This makes the mean sensitive to extreme values, or outliers, which can pull the average higher or lower than the typical data point.

Median

The median is the middle number in a dataset that has been arranged from smallest to largest, representing the exact center of the distribution. Unlike the mean, the median does not change if you add an extremely large or small number to the list, making it robust against outliers. The examiner usually provides a list of numbers and asks for the median, often testing your ability to handle sets with an odd count versus an even count.

If your dataset has an odd number of items, the median is simply the single number sitting exactly in the middle of the sorted list. If your dataset has an even number of items, there are two numbers in the middle, so you must find their average to get the median. Always remember to sort the data first; without sorting, identifying the middle position is impossible.

Mode

The mode is the value that appears most frequently within a specific dataset, indicating the most common occurrence in your group of numbers. A dataset can have one mode, multiple modes if several values share the highest frequency, or no mode at all if every number appears exactly once. Examiners love to trick students by presenting datasets with two modes, known as bimodal distributions, or asking you to identify that a mode does not exist.

To find the mode, count how many times each unique number appears in your list and select the one with the highest count. If two numbers appear the same amount of times and that count is higher than any other, the dataset has two modes. It is crucial to distinguish between a set with no repeating numbers, which has no mode, and a set where all numbers repeat equally.

Variance

Variance measures how spread out the data points are from the mean, providing a numerical value for the dispersion of your dataset. It is calculated by finding the difference between each data point and the mean, squaring those differences to avoid negative values canceling out positives, averaging them, and taking the square root for standard deviation. The examiner often asks for the variance or standard deviation to determine if a dataset is tightly clustered or widely scattered.

The process begins by calculating the mean, then subtracting the mean from each individual data point to find the deviation. Square each of these deviations to ensure they are positive, sum them up, and divide by the number of items for population variance or by one less than the count for sample variance. Taking the square root of this result gives you the standard deviation, which is in the same units as your original data.

Probability Basics

Probability is a measure of how likely an event is to occur, expressed as a number between zero and one where zero means impossible and one means certain. The fundamental rule states that probability equals the number of favorable outcomes divided by the total number of possible outcomes in the sample space. Examiners frequently test your understanding of independent events versus dependent events and ask you to calculate probabilities for multiple steps.

For a single event, count all the ways the specific outcome can happen and divide that by the total number of distinct possibilities available. If two events are independent, meaning the first does not affect the second, you multiply their individual probabilities together to find the combined probability. If events are mutually exclusive, meaning they cannot happen at the same time, you add their probabilities together.

Calculating Mean and Median from a List
Find the mean and median of the following set of test scores: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25.
To find the mean, sum the values: 5 + 10 + 15 + 20 + 25 equals 75. Divide this sum by the count of 5 numbers to get a mean of 15. To find the median, look at the list; it is already sorted from smallest to largest. Since there are 5 numbers (an odd count), the middle number is the third one, which is 15. In this specific case, the mean and median are identical because the data is perfectly symmetric.
Dealing with Outliers in Mean vs Median
Compare the mean and median of two sets. Set A: 10, 20, 30. Set B: 10, 20, 100.
For Set A, the sum is 60 divided by 3, giving a mean of 20. The middle number in the sorted list is also 20. For Set B, the sum is 130 divided by 3, giving a mean of approximately 43.3. However, the median for Set B is still 20 because it is the middle number regardless of the extreme value 100. This demonstrates that the mean is heavily influenced by the outlier 100, while the median remains stable at 20.

Common Traps

  • Forgetting to sort data: Students often try to find the median without arranging numbers in order, leading to incorrect identification of the middle value.
  • Miscounting the divisor for variance: Using 'n' instead of 'n-1' when calculating sample variance is a frequent error that lowers the final result artificially.
  • Ignoring negative deviations in variance: Failing to square the differences before averaging them causes positive and negative numbers to cancel out, resulting in a false zero variance.
  • Multiplying dependent events incorrectly: Assuming independence when one event changes the conditions for the next, such as drawing cards without replacement.

Memory Tricks

  • The "Balance Scale" for Mean: Imagine a seesaw; the mean is the fulcrum where the total weight balances perfectly. If you add a heavy weight (outlier), the fulcrum must move toward it.
  • The "Middle Seat" for Median: Visualize students sitting in a row. The median is simply the student sitting in the exact middle seat, regardless of how tall or short they are.
  • The "Most Popular" for Mode: Think of a popularity contest; the mode is the candidate who receives the most votes, even if they do not win by a landslide.
  • The "Spread Checker" for Variance: Variance tells you how far the students are standing from the center line. If everyone stands right next to the center, variance is low. If they scatter everywhere, variance is high.
Exam-Day Strategy
When facing a statistics question on exam day, always write down the mean first if you need to find the variance or standard deviation. This saves time and prevents calculation errors later. For probability questions, draw a tree diagram mentally or on scratch paper to visualize independent versus dependent steps. If asked for the median, take one second to sort your numbers; it is faster than recalculating an average that might be skewed by outliers. Finally, read the question carefully to see if it asks for sample variance (divide by n-1) or population variance (divide by n), as this single detail determines your final answer.

Weighted Mean and Combined Mean

In many examination problems the data points do not carry equal importance. A weighted mean assigns a weight wi to each observation xi and computes the average as Σ(wi·xi) ÷ Σwi. The weights may represent frequencies, credit hours, reliability factors, or any proportional influence. When two or more groups are merged, the combined mean of the pooled data equals the weighted mean of the group means, where each group’s weight is its size. For example, if Group A has 30 students with mean 65 and Group B has 20 students with mean 75, the overall mean is (30·65 + 20·75) ÷ (30+20) = 69. This shortcut avoids reconstructing the raw list and is a favourite of examiners testing speed.

A common twist is to give the combined mean and one group’s mean, then ask for the other group’s mean or its size. Rearranging the formula yields the missing quantity. Suppose the overall mean of 50 items is 40, the first 30 items have mean 35; the mean of the remaining 20 items is ((50·40) – (30·35)) ÷ 20 = 47.5. Mastery of this algebraic manipulation separates candidates who merely memorise the formula from those who can apply it under time pressure.

Weighted means also appear in index numbers, cost‑of‑living calculations, and portfolio returns. In a portfolio, each asset’s return is weighted by its proportion of total capital. The same principle underlies the calculation of a student’s cumulative grade point average where each course credit acts as a weight. Recognising the underlying structure — sum of products divided by sum of weights — allows rapid identification of the correct approach regardless of the narrative.

When weights are not explicitly given but implied by frequencies in a grouped frequency table, the weighted mean coincides with the ordinary mean of the expanded data set. This link connects the weighted‑mean concept to the next section on grouped data, reinforcing a unified view of averaging techniques.

Grouped Data: Mean, Median, and Mode from Frequency Distributions

Raw data are often summarised in class intervals with frequencies. The mean of grouped data uses the class mark (mid‑point) as a representative value: Σ(fi·xi) ÷ Σfi, where xi is the mid‑point of the i‑th class. This is exactly a weighted mean with frequencies as weights. The median requires locating the median class — the class where the cumulative frequency first reaches or exceeds N/2 — and then applying the interpolation formula: Median = L + ((N/2 – CF) ÷ f)·h, where L is the lower boundary of the median class, CF the cumulative frequency before that class, f the frequency of the median class, and h the class width.

The mode for grouped data is estimated by the modal class (highest frequency) using the formula: Mode = L + ((f1 – f0) ÷ (2f1 – f0 – f2))·h, where f1 is the modal class frequency, f0 the preceding class frequency, and f2 the succeeding class frequency. This formula assumes a smooth histogram and gives a reasonable estimate when the distribution is roughly unimodal.

Examiners frequently test the ability to switch between the three measures. A typical question provides a frequency table with one missing frequency and states that the mean equals a given value. Solving for the missing frequency uses the mean equation. Another variant asks which measure is most affected by an open‑ended class; the mean is sensitive because the mid‑point of an open class is ambiguous, whereas the median and mode depend only on cumulative frequencies and the modal class respectively.

When class intervals are unequal, the mid‑point method for the mean remains valid, but the median and mode formulas still use the class width h of the specific median or modal class. Care must be taken to use the correct h for each calculation. Additionally, if the data are inclusive (e.g., 10‑20, 20‑30), convert to exclusive boundaries (9.5‑20.5, 20.5‑30.5) before applying formulas; otherwise the class width and boundaries will be off by 0.5 units, leading to systematic errors.

Worked Example: Median from Grouped Data
The following frequency distribution gives the marks of 60 students. Find the median mark.
Marks   Frequency
0‑10   5
10‑20  12
20‑30  20
30‑40  15
40‑50  8
Total N = 60, so N/2 = 30. Cumulative frequencies: 5, 17, 37, 52, 60. The median class is 20‑30 because its cumulative frequency 37 first exceeds 30. L = 20, CF = 17, f = 20, h = 10. Median = 20 + ((30 – 17) ÷ 20)·10 = 20 + (13 ÷ 20)·10 = 20 + 6.5 = 26.5.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Grouped Data
Write the cumulative frequency column immediately after reading the table; it costs seconds but prevents mis‑identifying the median class. For the mode, check that the modal class is not at the extreme ends — if it is, the mode formula cannot be applied and the question may expect “mode cannot be determined”. Keep a mental checklist: mean uses all classes, median uses only the median class, mode uses modal class and its neighbours.

Standard Deviation, Coefficient of Variation, and Their Interpretation

While variance measures average squared deviation, the standard deviation (σ or s) restores the original units by taking the square root. For a population, σ = √(Σ(x – μ)² ÷ N); for a sample, s = √(Σ(x – x̄)² ÷ (n‑1)). The denominator n‑1 (Bessel’s correction) makes s an unbiased estimator of σ. In examinations, the distinction between population and sample is often signalled by phrases such as “the data represent the entire batch” versus “a random sample of 30 items”.

The coefficient of variation (CV) expresses relative dispersion as CV = (σ ÷ μ) × 100 % for a population or (s ÷ x̄) × 100 % for a sample. It enables comparison of variability across data sets with different means or units. For instance, a CV of 5 % for heights (mean 170 cm) indicates far less relative spread than a CV of 20 % for weights (mean 70 kg), even if the absolute standard deviations are comparable. Questions may ask which of two series is more consistent; the lower CV wins.

A frequent trap is to compute the standard deviation of a grouped distribution by using class marks and frequencies but forgetting to apply the correction factor for sample data. The grouped‑data formula for sample variance is s² = (Σf·x² ÷ (n‑1)) – (n ÷ (n‑1))·x̄², where x is the class mark. An equivalent shortcut: compute the mean, then Σf·(x – x̄)², divide by (n‑1), and take the square root. Both routes give the same result if arithmetic is exact.

Interpretation questions often link standard deviation to the empirical rule: for approximately normal data, about 68 % of observations lie within μ ± σ, 95 % within μ ± 2σ, and 99.7 % within μ ± 3σ. Even when normality is not stated, examiners may ask “what percentage of data lies within two standard deviations of the mean?” expecting the Chebyshev bound of at least 75 % for any distribution. Knowing both the empirical rule and Chebyshev’s inequality covers all phrasing.

Conditional Probability and Bayes’ Theorem

Conditional probability P(A|B) quantifies the chance of event A given that B has occurred, defined as P(A∩B) ÷ P(B) provided P(B) > 0. The multiplication rule P(A∩B) = P(A|B)·P(B) = P(B|A)·P(A) is the engine behind many multi‑stage problems. When events are independent, P(A|B) = P(A) and the rule collapses to simple multiplication.

Bayes’ theorem inverts the conditioning: P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) ÷ P(B). The denominator P(B) is often expanded via the law of total probability: P(B) = Σ P(B|Ai)·P(Ai) over a partition {Ai}. Classic textbook scenarios include medical testing (disease prevalence, sensitivity, specificity), quality control (defective items from different machines), and urn problems (drawing coloured balls from multiple boxes).

A typical exam question: “A factory has three machines M1, M2, M3 producing 30 %, 45 %, 25 % of output. Their defect rates are 2 %, 3 %, 1 % respectively. An item is found defective. Find the probability it came from M2.” Here Ai = “produced by Mi”, B = “defective”. Compute P(B) = 0.30·0.02 + 0.45·0.03 + 0.25·0.01 = 0.022. Then P(M2|defective) = 0.03·0.45 ÷ 0.022 ≈ 0.6136. The steps — identify partition, write total probability, apply Bayes — are mechanical once the structure is recognised.

Examiners also test the distinction between P(A|B) and P(B|A). A common wording: “Given that a student passed, what is the probability they studied?” versus “Given that a student studied, what is the probability they passed?” The numbers differ dramatically unless the events are symmetric. Drawing a tree diagram with branches labelled by conditional probabilities prevents reversal errors.

Discrete Probability Distributions: Binomial and Poisson

The binomial distribution models the number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials with constant success probability p. Its probability mass function is P(X = k) = C(n,k)·pk·(1‑p)n‑k for k = 0,1,…,n. Mean = np, variance = np(1‑p). The Poisson distribution approximates the binomial when n is large, p is small, and np = λ is moderate. Its pmf is P(X = k) = e‑λ·λk ÷ k! for k = 0,1,2,… with mean = variance = λ.

Examination questions often ask for the probability of “at most”, “at least”, or “exactly” a certain number of successes. For binomial, cumulative probabilities can be summed directly for small n; for larger n, normal approximation with continuity correction may be expected. The Poisson distribution appears in scenarios such as number of calls per minute at a call centre, defects per metre of cloth, or accidents per day at an intersection. Recognising the “rare event over a fixed interval” pattern triggers the Poisson model.

A subtle point: the Poisson distribution can also be derived as the limit of a binomial with n → ∞, p → 0, np = λ. This theoretical link is rarely tested computationally but may appear in a conceptual multiple‑choice item asking which distribution is appropriate for a given description. The key discriminators are: fixed number of trials vs. fixed interval; constant probability per trial vs. constant average rate; independence of trials vs. independence of disjoint intervals.

When both distributions are plausible, the exam may ask to compute the probability using both and comment on the closeness of the results. For instance, n = 100, p = 0.02 gives λ = 2. The binomial P(X = 0) = 0.98100 ≈ 0.1326; the Poisson P(X = 0) = e‑2 ≈ 0.1353. The small difference illustrates the quality of the approximation.

  • Confusing population and sample formulas for variance and standard deviation, especially when the question uses the word “sample” but provides the entire data set.
  • Applying the grouped‑data median formula with the wrong class width when intervals are unequal.
  • Forgetting to convert inclusive class boundaries to exclusive ones before calculating mid‑points or class widths.
  • Reversing conditional probabilities in Bayes’ theorem problems, i.e., computing P(B|A) instead of P(A|B).
  • Using the binomial formula when the situation describes a Poisson process (rare events over a continuous interval) or vice‑versa.
  • Neglecting the continuity correction when approximating a binomial with a normal distribution.
  • Assuming independence in multi‑stage draws without replacement, leading to an inflated probability.
