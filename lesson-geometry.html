Geometry — Shapes, Spaces & Theorems

Geometry is the study of shapes and their properties. From the pyramids of Egypt to modern architecture, geometry describes the physical world. Every competitive exam tests geometry — SSC CGL has 8-10 questions, Banking 5-6, CDS/NDA 10-12, and CTET 8-10.

📐 Lines & Angles

Where It All Begins
The Egyptian Rope-Stretchers
Every year the Nile flooded, washing away farmland boundaries. The Egyptians needed to re-mark fields. They used stretched ropes — straight lines — to measure and divide land. They discovered that a rope with knots at 3, 4, and 5 units made a perfect right angle. That was geometry's birth: measuring the earth (geo = earth, metry = measure).
Geometry started as a practical tool: dividing land, building structures, measuring distances.

🔺 Triangles — The Strongest Shape

Nature's Favorite Shape
Why Bridges Use Triangles
A square frame wobbles. Add a diagonal — two triangles — and it's rigid. Triangles are the only polygon that cannot change shape without changing side lengths. That's why bridges, cranes, and roof trusses use triangles. A triangle is the simplest rigid structure in engineering.
Triangles are uniquely rigid. That's why they're everywhere in construction.

Types of Triangles (by sides)

  • Equilateral: all sides equal, all angles = 60°
  • Isosceles: two sides equal, base angles equal
  • Scalene: all sides different

Types (by angles)

  • Acute: all angles < 90°
  • Right: one angle = 90° (hypotenuse opposite right angle)
  • Obtuse: one angle > 90°

⭕ Circles — The Perfect Shape

Wheels & Cycles
Why Circles Are Everywhere
A circle is the set of all points at a fixed distance (radius) from a center. The wheel — humanity's greatest invention — works because every point on a circle's rim is the same distance from the axle. That gives smooth rotation. The Babylonians divided the circle into 360° because it's divisible by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, 45, 60, 72, 90, 120, and 180 — the most divisible number possible.
The circle's symmetry and the divisibility of 360 make it the natural unit for angles.

📏 Mensuration & 3D Geometry

Measuring Everything
The King's Dilemma
A king ordered a gold crown. He gave the goldsmith pure gold. The crown came back the right weight, but was it pure gold? Archimedes realized: submerge the crown in water. The volume of water displaced equals the crown's volume. Compare density = mass/volume with pure gold's density. He ran naked through the streets shouting "Eureka!" — Greek for "I found it!" That was the birth of mensuration: measuring volume by displacement.
Density = mass/volume. Volume of irregular objects = water displaced.

Coordinate Geometry – Distance, Section Formula, and Locus

Coordinate geometry translates geometric problems into algebraic equations, allowing the use of algebraic manipulation to solve for lengths, mid‑points, and loci. The distance between two points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂) in the Cartesian plane is derived from the Pythagorean theorem: PQ = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]. This formula extends to three dimensions as √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² + (z₂−z₁)²]. The section formula gives the coordinates of a point dividing a line segment internally in the ratio m:n as ((mx₂+nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂+ny₁)/(m+n)); for external division the signs of m and n are opposite. The midpoint is the special case m=n, yielding ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2).

A locus is the set of all points satisfying a given geometric condition. For example, the locus of points equidistant from two fixed points A and B is the perpendicular bisector of AB. The locus of points at a constant distance r from a fixed point O is a circle with equation (x−h)² + (y−k)² = r² where O(h,k). The locus of points where the sum of distances to two fixed points (foci) is constant is an ellipse; the difference of distances constant gives a hyperbola. These definitions lead directly to the standard equations of conics.

The slope of a line through P(x₁,y₁) and Q(x₂,y₂) is m = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁). Two lines are parallel iff their slopes are equal; they are perpendicular iff the product of their slopes is −1 (provided neither is vertical). The equation of a line in slope‑intercept form is y = mx + c; in point‑slope form y−y₁ = m(x−x₁); and in intercept form x/a + y/b = 1. The general form Ax + By + C = 0 is useful for finding the distance from a point (x₀,y₀) to the line: |Ax₀+By₀+C|/√(A²+B²).

Applications include finding the area of a triangle given vertices (x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂), (x₃,y₃) via the determinant formula Area = ½|x₁(y₂−y₃)+x₂(y₃−y₁)+x₃(y₁−y₂)|. The condition for collinearity of three points is that this area equals zero. The centroid of a triangle with vertices (x₁,y₁), (x₂,y₂), (x₃,y₃) is ((x₁+x₂+x₃)/3, (y₁+y₂+y₃)/3). The circumcenter and orthocenter can also be located by solving perpendicular bisector and altitude equations respectively.

Examiners often combine coordinate geometry with other topics: a circle may be defined by three points, requiring the solution of a system of three equations to find its centre and radius; a triangle’s area may be asked after a transformation such as a rotation of axes. Mastery of algebraic manipulation, especially completing the square to convert general second‑degree equations into standard forms, is essential for speed.

Conic Sections – Parabola, Ellipse, Hyperbola

Conic sections arise from intersecting a plane with a double‑napped right circular cone. The angle of the plane relative to the cone’s axis determines the curve: a plane parallel to a generator yields a parabola; a plane cutting both nappes yields a hyperbola; a plane cutting one nappe at an angle less than the generator yields an ellipse; a plane perpendicular to the axis yields a circle (a special ellipse). Each conic can be defined by a focus‑directrix property: the set of points where the distance to a fixed point (focus) equals e times the distance to a fixed line (directrix). The eccentricity e characterises the conic: e = 1 for parabola, 0 < e < 1 for ellipse, e > 1 for hyperbola, and e = 0 for circle.

The standard equation of a parabola with vertex at the origin and axis along the x‑axis is y² = 4ax (focus (a,0), directrix x = −a). If the axis is along the y‑axis the equation is x² = 4ay. The latus rectum length is |4a|. For an ellipse centred at the origin with major axis along the x‑axis: x²/a² + y²/b² = 1 where a > b. The foci are at (±c,0) with c² = a²−b². Eccentricity e = c/a. The latus rectum length is 2b²/a. For a hyperbola centred at the origin with transverse axis along the x‑axis: x²/a² − y²/b² = 1. Foci at (±c,0) with c² = a²+b². Eccentricity e = c/a > 1. The asymptotes are the lines y = ±(b/a)x.

Parametric representations simplify many problems: parabola (at², 2at), ellipse (a cosθ, b sinθ), hyperbola (a secθ, b tanθ) or (a cosh t, b sinh t). The equation of the tangent to a conic at a point (x₁,y₁) on it can be written by replacing x² → xx₁, y² → yy₁, x → (x+x₁)/2, y → (y+y₁)/2 in the standard equation (the “T = 0” method). The chord of contact from an external point (x₀,y₀) is obtained similarly. The director circle of an ellipse is x²+y² = a²+b²; for a hyperbola it is x²+y² = a²−b² (real only if a > b).

Important properties: the reflection property of a parabola – rays parallel to the axis reflect through the focus; for an ellipse, rays from one focus reflect to the other focus; for a hyperbola, rays directed toward one focus reflect as if coming from the other focus. These properties underlie applications in optics and satellite dish design. The polar equation of a conic with one focus at the pole is r = ed/(1+e cosθ) where d is the distance from the focus to the directrix.

Examiners frequently ask for the locus of the midpoint of a chord of a conic that passes through a fixed point, or the equation of a common tangent to two conics. Solving these requires eliminating parameters or using the condition that a line y = mx + c touches a conic (discriminant zero). For a parabola y² = 4ax, the condition of tangency is c = a/m. For an ellipse x²/a² + y²/b² = 1, the condition is c² = a²m² + b². For a hyperbola x²/a² − y²/b² = 1, the condition is c² = a²m² − b².

Worked Example – Common Tangent to Parabola and Ellipse
Find the equation of a line that is tangent to both the parabola y² = 8x and the ellipse x²/9 + y²/4 = 1.
For the parabola y² = 8x, we have 4a = 8 ⇒ a = 2. A tangent in slope form is y = mx + a/m = mx + 2/m. For the ellipse x²/9 + y²/4 = 1, the tangency condition for y = mx + c is c² = a²m² + b² = 9m² + 4. Substitute c = 2/m: (2/m)² = 9m² + 4 ⇒ 4/m² = 9m² + 4. Multiply by m²: 4 = 9m⁴ + 4m² ⇒ 9m⁴ + 4m² − 4 = 0. Let u = m² ≥ 0: 9u² + 4u − 4 = 0. Solve: u = [−4 ± √(16 + 144)]/(18) = [−4 ± √160]/18 = [−4 ± 4√10]/18 = [−2 ± 2√10]/9. Only the positive root is valid: u = (−2 + 2√10)/9 = 2(√10−1)/9. Thus m = ±√u. The corresponding c = 2/m. Hence there are two common tangents symmetric about the x‑axis. Their equations are y = mx + 2/m with m = ±√[2(√10−1)/9].
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the tangency conditions for the three standard conics in slope form; they turn a locus or common‑tangent problem into a simple algebraic equation in m. When a problem gives a point and asks for the chord of contact, write the “T = 0” equation directly instead of finding the two tangents separately. For ellipse and hyperbola, keep the director‑circle equations handy to test whether a given point lies outside the conic (real tangents exist only if the point satisfies the director‑circle inequality).

Advanced Circle Theorems – Power of a Point and Radical Axis

The power of a point P with respect to a circle of radius r and centre O is defined as Pow(P) = OP² − r². If P lies outside the circle, the power equals the square of the length of the tangent from P to the circle; if P lies inside, the power is negative and its absolute value equals the product of the segments of any chord through P. For a secant through P intersecting the circle at A and B, PA × PB = |Pow(P)|. This theorem unifies tangent‑secant and intersecting‑chord results.

The radical axis of two circles is the locus of points having equal power with respect to both circles. Given circles C₁: x²+y²+2g₁x+2f₁y+c₁=0 and C₂: x²+y²+2g₂x+2f₂y+c₂=0, subtracting the equations eliminates the quadratic terms and yields the linear equation of the radical axis: 2(g₁−g₂)x + 2(f₁−f₂)y + (c₁−c₂) = 0. This line is perpendicular to the line joining the centres. If the circles intersect, the radical axis is the common chord; if they touch, it is the common tangent at the point of contact; if they are separate, it lies between them.

The radical centre of three circles (taken in pairs) is the unique point where the three radical axes concur, provided the centres are not collinear. This point has equal power with respect to all three circles. It is the centre of the unique circle orthogonal to all three given circles. Orthogonal circles satisfy 2g₁g₂ + 2f₁f₂ = c₁ + c₂ (derived from the condition that the radius of one circle drawn to the intersection point is tangent to the other).

Coaxial systems are families of circles sharing the same radical axis. The limiting points of a coaxial system are the two points (real or imaginary) that have zero power with respect to every circle in the system; they are the point circles of the family. Any circle orthogonal to two circles of a coaxial system is orthogonal to the whole system. These concepts appear in problems asking for the equation of a circle cutting three given circles orthogonally, or for the locus of centres of circles touching two given circles.

Applications: Given two intersecting circles, the length of the common chord can be found using the distance between centres d and radii r₁, r₂: chord length = 2√[r₁² − ((r₁²−r₂²+d²)/(2d))²]. The radical axis also provides a quick way to find the equation of a circle passing through the intersection points of two given circles: C₁ + λC₂ = 0 (where C₁=0 and C₂=0 are the circle equations) represents the family of circles through their intersections; the radical axis corresponds to λ = −1.

Solid Geometry – Frustum, Prism, Pyramid, and Composite Solids

A frustum is the portion of a cone or pyramid that remains after the top is cut off by a plane parallel to the base. For a conical frustum with radii R (lower base) and r (upper base) and height h, the slant height l = √[(R−r)² + h²]. Volume V = (πh/3)(R² + Rr + r²). Curved surface area CSA = π(R+r)l. Total surface area adds the areas of the two circular bases: TSA = π(R+r)l + πR² + πr². For a pyramidal frustum with base areas A₁ and A₂ and height h, volume V = (h/3)(A₁ + A₂ + √(A₁A₂)). The lateral surface area is the sum of the areas of the trapezoidal faces.

A prism is a polyhedron with two congruent parallel polygonal bases and rectangular lateral faces. For a right prism, the lateral edges are perpendicular to the bases. Volume V = Base Area × Height. Lateral surface area LSA = Perimeter of Base × Height. Total surface area TSA = LSA + 2 × Base Area. A right circular cylinder is a prism with a circular base; its formulas match the cylinder formulas already known.

A pyramid has a polygonal base and triangular faces meeting at a common vertex. For a right pyramid (vertex projected onto the centroid of the base), the slant height of each triangular face can be found using the apothem of the base and the pyramid height. Volume V = (1/3) × Base Area × Height. Lateral surface area is the sum of the areas of the triangular faces; for a regular pyramid with base perimeter P and slant height l, LSA = ½Pl. The total surface area adds the base area.

Composite solids are formed by combining or removing basic solids. Typical exam problems: a cylinder with a conical cavity drilled through its axis; a hemisphere mounted on a cylinder; a cube with a hemispherical depression on each face. The key principle is that volumes add or subtract, while surface areas require careful accounting of which faces become internal and are no longer exposed. For example, when a cone is removed from a cylinder sharing the same base and height, the remaining volume is V_cyl − V_cone = πr²h − (1/3)πr²h = (2/3)πr²h. The new total surface area is the cylinder’s curved surface plus the cone’s curved surface plus the base of the cylinder (the top base is removed).

Another common scenario: a solid sphere of radius R is melted and recast into a number of identical smaller spheres or into a wire (cylinder). Volume conservation gives n × (4/3)πr³ = (4/3)πR³ ⇒ r = R/∛n. For a wire of length L and radius r, πr²L = (4/3)πR³ ⇒ r = √[(4R³)/(3L)]. These problems test algebraic manipulation and unit consistency.

Transformation Geometry – Reflection, Rotation, Translation, and Scaling

Transformation geometry studies how figures move in the plane or space while preserving certain properties. An isometry (rigid motion) preserves distances and angles; the four basic plane isometries are translation, rotation, reflection, and glide reflection. A translation by vector v = (a,b) maps (x,y) → (x+a, y+b). A rotation about the origin by angle θ (counter‑clockwise) maps (x,y) → (x cosθ − y sinθ, x sinθ + y cosθ). A reflection across the x‑axis maps (x,y) → (x,−y); across the y‑axis (−x,y); across the line y = x maps (x,y) → (y,x); across y = −x maps (−y,−x). A glide reflection is a reflection followed by a translation parallel to the mirror line.

These transformations can be represented by 2×2 matrices (for linear part) plus a translation vector. Rotation matrix: [[cosθ, −sinθ],[sinθ, cosθ]]. Reflection across a line through the origin with unit direction vector u = (cosα, sinα) has matrix [[cos2α, sin2α],[sin2α, −cos2α]]. Composition of transformations corresponds to matrix multiplication (for the linear parts) and vector addition for translations. The determinant of the linear part is +1 for rotations and translations (orientation‑preserving) and −1 for reflections and glide reflections (orientation‑reversing).

Scaling (dilation) about the origin by factor k maps (x,y) → (kx, ky). It is not an isometry unless |k| = 1. A similarity transformation is a composition of an isometry and a scaling; it preserves angles and ratios of lengths. The matrix of a similarity is k × R where R is an orthogonal matrix (rotation or reflection). In coordinate geometry, a homothety with centre C(x₀,y₀) and ratio k maps P(x,y) → (x₀ + k(x−x₀), y₀ + k(y−y₀)).

Invariants under isometries: distances, angles, area, orientation (for proper isometries). Under similarities: angles, ratios of lengths, ratio of areas = k². Under affine transformations (which include shear), parallelism and ratios of segments on parallel lines are preserved, but angles and lengths are not. Exam questions often ask: “Triangle ABC is reflected in the line y = 2x + 1 and then rotated 90° about the origin. Find the coordinates of the image of vertex A.” The efficient method is to apply the transformation matrices sequentially or to find the single equivalent transformation matrix.

In three dimensions, the basic isometries are translation, rotation about an axis, reflection in a plane, and rotatory reflection (improper rotation). A rotation about the z‑axis by angle θ has matrix [[cosθ, −sinθ, 0],[sinθ, cosθ, 0],[0,0,1]]. Reflection in the xy‑plane is diag(1,1,−1). The composition of two reflections in intersecting planes is a rotation about their line of intersection by twice the angle between the planes. These facts are useful for solving problems involving symmetry of polyhedra or for finding the image of a point after multiple reflections.

  • Confusing the power of a point formula with the tangent length formula; remember power = OP² − r², tangent² = power only when P is outside.
  • Forgetting that the radical axis is a line, not a segment; it extends infinitely even if the circles do not intersect.
  • Using the frustum volume formula for a truncated pyramid without adjusting for base shapes; the pyramidal frustum uses base areas, not radii.
  • Neglecting to subtract the area of the base that becomes internal when two solids are joined; total surface area is not simply the sum of individual surface areas.
  • Applying 2D rotation matrices to 3D problems without adding the third coordinate; always embed the 2D matrix in a 3×3 identity block for the unchanged axis.
  • Assuming a similarity transformation preserves area; area scales by k², not by k.
  • Master these pitfalls to avoid losing marks on geometry questions.
