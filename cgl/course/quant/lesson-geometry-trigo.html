What is Geometry & Trigonometry?

Geometry deals with the properties and relationships of points, lines, angles, and shapes. Trigonometry deals with the relationships between angles and sides of triangles. SSC CGL Tier 1 includes basic geometry concepts (lines, angles, triangles, circles) and fundamental trigonometric ratios.

Key Concepts (Geometry)

1. Lines & Angles: Vertically opposite angles are equal. Corresponding angles are equal when lines are parallel. Sum of interior angles of a triangle = 180 degrees. Exterior angle = sum of opposite interior angles.

2. Triangle Properties: Pythagorean theorem: a^2 + b^2 = c^2 for right triangles. In an isosceles triangle, angles opposite equal sides are equal. In an equilateral triangle, all angles = 60 degrees. Centroid divides medians in the ratio 2:1.

3. Circles: Angle subtended by a diameter is 90 degrees (Thales theorem). Angles in the same segment are equal. The tangent at any point is perpendicular to the radius at that point.

Key Concepts (Trigonometry)

1. Basic Ratios: sin theta = opposite/hypotenuse. cos theta = adjacent/hypotenuse. tan theta = opposite/adjacent. cosec theta = 1/sin theta. sec theta = 1/cos theta. cot theta = 1/tan theta.

2. Standard Angles: sin 0 = 0, sin 30 = 1/2, sin 45 = 1/sqrt2, sin 60 = sqrt3/2, sin 90 = 1. cos values are reverse of sin. tan 45 = 1.

3. Identities: sin^2 theta + cos^2 theta = 1. sec^2 theta - tan^2 theta = 1. cosec^2 theta - cot^2 theta = 1.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Geometry — Triangle)
In a right triangle ABC with right angle at B, if AB = 6 and BC = 8, find AC.

a) 9 b) 10 c) 12 d) 14
Solution: b) 10. By Pythagoras: AC^2 = AB^2 + BC^2 = 36 + 64 = 100. AC = sqrt100 = 10.
Example 2 (Trigonometry — Value)
Find the value of sin 60 cos 30 + cos 60 sin 30.

a) 0 b) 1/2 c) 1 d) sqrt3/2
Solution: c) 1. sin 60 = sqrt3/2, cos 30 = sqrt3/2, cos 60 = 1/2, sin 30 = 1/2. Expression = (sqrt3/2)*(sqrt3/2) + (1/2)*(1/2) = 3/4 + 1/4 = 1.
Example 3 (Geometry — Circle)
The angle subtended by a chord at the centre is 120 degrees. What is the angle subtended by it at the circumference?

a) 30 degrees b) 45 degrees c) 60 degrees d) 90 degrees
Solution: c) 60 degrees. The angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference. So angle at circumference = 120/2 = 60 degrees.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For geometry, remember common Pythagorean triplets: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 7-24-25, 8-15-17, 9-40-41.
  • For trigonometry, use the mnemonic SOH CAH TOA to recall sin=opp/hyp, cos=adj/hyp, tan=opp/adj.
  • For complementary angles: sin(90 - theta) = cos theta, tan(90 - theta) = cot theta.
  • When facing complex trigonometric expressions, convert everything to sin and cos first, then simplify.

Triangle Centers and Their Properties

The centroid, orthocenter, circumcenter, and incenter are the four classical triangle centers. The centroid is the intersection of the three medians and divides each median in the ratio 2:1, with the longer segment adjacent to the vertex. The orthocenter is the concurrency point of the three altitudes; its position varies with the triangle type — inside for acute, at the right‑angle vertex for right, and outside for obtuse triangles. The circumcenter is the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the sides and is the center of the circumscribed circle; it lies inside for acute triangles, on the hypotenuse midpoint for right triangles, and outside for obtuse triangles. The incenter is the intersection of the internal angle bisectors and is the center of the inscribed circle; it is always inside the triangle.

Euler’s line is a remarkable collinearity: the centroid, orthocenter, and circumcenter are always collinear, with the centroid dividing the segment joining the orthocenter and circumcenter in the ratio 2:1. The nine‑point circle passes through the midpoints of the sides, the feet of the altitudes, and the midpoints of the segments from each vertex to the orthocenter; its center lies at the midpoint of the Euler segment. These relationships are frequently tested through coordinate geometry or pure geometry proofs, and recognizing them can reduce a multi‑step problem to a single application of a known ratio.

In an equilateral triangle all four centers coincide, and the distance from the common center to any vertex equals the circumradius R, while the distance to any side equals the inradius r, with R = 2r. For an isosceles triangle the centers lie on the axis of symmetry, which simplifies many locus problems. The distance between the circumcenter O and the incenter I satisfies Euler’s formula OI² = R(R − 2r), a result that appears in advanced questions linking triangle geometry with algebraic manipulation.

When a problem asks for the coordinates of a center given vertex coordinates, the centroid is immediate: (x₁+x₂+x₃)/3, (y₁+y₂+y₃)/3. The circumcenter can be found by solving the perpendicular bisector equations, but a faster route uses the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of any two sides. The orthocenter coordinates can be obtained by intersecting two altitudes, or by using the vector relation H = A + B + C − 2O (with origin at the circumcenter). The incenter coordinates are weighted by side lengths: (ax₁+bx₂+cx₃)/(a+b+c), (ay₁+by₂+cy₃)/(a+b+c). Mastery of these formulas allows rapid computation under time pressure.

Examiners often combine centers with other concepts: for instance, a question may give the circumradius and inradius and ask for the distance between the circumcenter and orthocenter, requiring the use of Euler’s line and the relation OH = 3·OG. Another common twist is to ask for the radius of the nine‑point circle, which is simply R/2. Recognizing these standard results and their derivations prevents unnecessary algebraic expansion and saves valuable minutes.

Circle Theorems Beyond the Basics

The intersecting chords theorem states that if two chords AB and CD intersect at point P inside the circle, then AP·PB = CP·PD. The tangent‑secant theorem extends this: if a tangent from external point P touches the circle at T and a secant through P meets the circle at A and B, then PT² = PA·PB. The power of a point P with respect to a circle is defined as the product of the distances from P to the two intersection points of any line through P with the circle; it is constant for all such lines and equals PO² − R², where O is the center and R the radius. This unified concept simplifies many problems that otherwise appear as separate chord, tangent, or secant cases.

The radical axis of two circles is the locus of points having equal power with respect to both circles; it is a straight line perpendicular to the line of centers. If the circles intersect, the radical axis is the common chord line. If they are tangent, the radical axis is the common tangent at the point of tangency. For three circles taken pairwise, the three radical axes are concurrent at the radical center, a fact used in problems involving coaxial systems or in proving concurrency of lines derived from circle intersections.

Cyclic quadrilaterals bring additional angle relations: opposite angles sum to 180°, and the exterior angle equals the interior opposite angle. Ptolemy’s theorem provides a metric relation: for a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, AC·BD = AB·CD + AD·BC. This theorem is a powerful tool for converting angle conditions into side‑length equations, and it often appears in problems that ask for a missing side length when the quadrilateral is known to be cyclic. The converse also holds: if the product of the diagonals equals the sum of the products of opposite sides, the quadrilateral is cyclic.

The angle between a chord and a tangent at the point of contact equals the angle in the alternate segment. This alternate segment theorem is frequently combined with the intersecting chords theorem to create multi‑step problems where an unknown angle is first found via the tangent‑chord relation, then used in a triangle to apply the sine rule or cosine rule. Another advanced result is the Miquel point: given a triangle and three points on its sides, the circles through each vertex and the two adjacent points are concurrent at the Miquel point, which lies on the circumcircle of the original triangle.

In coordinate geometry, the equation of a circle (x−h)²+(y−k)²=R² allows algebraic verification of the power of a point: substitute the point coordinates into the left‑hand side and subtract R². The radical axis of two circles given by equations S₁=0 and S₂=0 is simply S₁−S₂=0, a linear equation. These algebraic forms are indispensable when a problem mixes synthetic geometry with coordinate calculations, a pattern that appears regularly in higher‑difficulty sets.

Worked Example: Power of a Point and Radical Axis
Two circles have equations x²+y²−6x−8y+9=0 and x²+y²−2x−4y−11=0. Find the equation of their radical axis and the length of the common chord.
First rewrite each circle in standard form. Circle 1: (x−3)²+(y−4)² = 3²+4²−9 = 16, so center C₁(3,4), radius R₁=4. Circle 2: (x−1)²+(y−2)² = 1²+2²+11 = 16, so center C₂(1,2), radius R₂=4. The radical axis is obtained by subtracting the equations: (x²+y²−6x−8y+9) − (x²+y²−2x−4y−11) = 0 → −4x−4y+20 = 0 → x+y−5 = 0. Distance between centers d = √[(3−1)²+(4−2)²] = √8 = 2√2. Since R₁=R₂=4, the common chord length L = 2√[R₁² − (d/2)²] = 2√[16 − ( (2√2)/2 )²] = 2√[16 − 2] = 2√14. Thus radical axis: x+y−5=0, common chord length = 2√14.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a circle problem gives two equations, immediately write the radical axis as the difference of the equations; this yields a linear equation in seconds. For common chord length, compute the distance between centers, then apply the formula L = 2√[R² − (d/2)²] (valid when radii are equal). If radii differ, use the general formula L = (1/d)√[ (−d+R₁+R₂)(d−R₁+R₂)(d+R₁−R₂)(d+R₁+R₂) ]. Keep these formulas on your rough sheet for instant recall.

Trigonometric Identities and Transformations

The sum and difference formulas — sin(A±B) = sin A cos B ± cos A sin B, cos(A±B) = cos A cos B ∓ sin A sin B, tan(A±B) = (tan A ± tan B)/(1 ∓ tan A tan B) — are the foundation for deriving all other identities. From them follow the double‑angle formulas: sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ, cos 2θ = cos²θ − sin²θ = 2cos²θ−1 = 1−2sin²θ, tan 2θ = 2tan θ/(1−tan²θ). The half‑angle formulas are obtained by solving the double‑angle cosine forms for sin²(θ/2) and cos²(θ/2): sin(θ/2) = ±√[(1−cos θ)/2], cos(θ/2) = ±√[(1+cos θ)/2], tan(θ/2) = ±√[(1−cos θ)/(1+cos θ)] = sin θ/(1+cos θ) = (1−cos θ)/sin θ. The sign depends on the quadrant of θ/2.

Product‑to‑sum and sum‑to‑product transformations convert products of sines and cosines into sums, which are easier to integrate or to solve in equations: sin A sin B = ½[cos(A−B)−cos(A+B)], cos A cos B = ½[cos(A−B)+cos(A+B)], sin A cos B = ½[sin(A+B)+sin(A−B)]. Conversely, sin C + sin D = 2 sin[(C+D)/2] cos[(C−D)/2], cos C + cos D = 2 cos[(C+D)/2] cos[(C−D)/2]. These are indispensable when simplifying expressions like sin 20° sin 40° sin 80° or solving equations such as sin x + sin 3x = cos 2x.

The identities sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, 1+tan²θ = sec²θ, 1+cot²θ = csc²θ are used to replace higher powers with first powers. For example, sin⁴θ + cos⁴θ = (sin²θ+cos²θ)² − 2sin²θcos²θ = 1 − ½ sin²2θ. The maximum and minimum values of a sin θ + b cos θ are ±√(a²+b²), achieved when tan θ = a/b. This result follows from writing a sin θ + b cos θ = √(a²+b²) sin(θ+α) where α satisfies sin α = b/√(a²+b²), cos α = a/√(a²+b²). Such optimisation problems appear frequently in the advanced section.

Conditional identities, valid only under a given relation among angles, are a special class. The most common is A+B+C = π (or 180°). Then sin 2A + sin 2B + sin 2C = 4 sin A sin B sin C, cos 2A + cos 2B + cos 2C = −1 − 4 cos A cos B cos C, and tan A + tan B + tan C = tan A tan B tan C. These are proved by repeatedly applying sum‑to‑product formulas and the condition A+B = π−C. Memorising the final forms saves derivation time, but understanding the proof helps when the condition is altered, e.g., A+B+C = 2π.

Inverse trigonometric functions add another layer: sin⁻¹x + cos⁻¹x = π/2, tan⁻¹x + cot⁻¹x = π/2, and the addition formula tan⁻¹x + tan⁻¹y = tan⁻¹[(x+y)/(1−xy)] (with quadrant adjustments). Problems often ask to simplify expressions like sin(2 tan⁻¹x) or to solve equations involving inverse functions. The key is to set an angle θ = tan⁻¹x, then express sin θ, cos θ in terms of x using a right triangle with opposite x, adjacent 1, hypotenuse √(1+x²). This converts the inverse problem into an algebraic one.

Height and Distance Problems: Multi‑Step Strategies

Height and distance questions combine right‑triangle trigonometry with geometric insight. The basic relation tan θ = height / horizontal distance is the starting point. When two observers at different points measure angles of elevation to the same object, the object’s height h and its horizontal distances from the observers form a system of equations: h = d₁ tan α = d₂ tan β, with d₁ + d₂ = known separation (if observers are on the same side) or |d₁ − d₂| = separation (if on opposite sides). Solving yields h = (separation·tan α·tan β) / (tan α ± tan β), where the sign depends on the configuration.

Problems often introduce a moving observer: a person walks towards a tower, the angle of elevation changes from α to β after covering distance s. The height h satisfies h = s·tan α·tan β / (tan β − tan α) (assuming the walk is directly toward the tower). If the walk is at an angle to the line of sight, the horizontal component of the walk must be used. Drawing a clear diagram with labelled right triangles is essential; many candidates lose marks by misidentifying which side is opposite or adjacent.

When the object is not a point but a vertical pole on a hill, the problem splits into two right triangles sharing the hill slope. Let the hill inclination be φ, the pole height p, and the angles of elevation to the top and bottom of the pole be α and β from a point on the horizontal ground. Then the total height from ground to top is h = p cos φ + (horizontal distance)·tan α, while the height to the bottom is (horizontal distance)·tan β. Eliminating the horizontal distance gives p = (tan α − tan β)·horizontal distance / cos φ. Recognising the decomposition into slope and vertical components prevents algebraic confusion.

Shadow problems are a variant: the length of a shadow cast by a vertical object under a light source at angle θ satisfies shadow length = height / tan θ. If the light source moves (e.g., the sun), the shadow length changes. For two instants with angles θ₁ and θ₂, the difference in shadow lengths Δs = h (cot θ₁ − cot θ₂). This relation is often used to find the height of a tower given the change in shadow length over a known time interval, assuming the sun’s angular speed is known or the angles are given directly.

Advanced questions combine height‑distance with circle theorems: for instance, a tower stands at the centre of a circular field; from two points on the circumference the angles of elevation are α and β. The chord between the points subtends an angle at the centre, and the distances from the centre to the points are the radius R. Using the law of sines in the triangle formed by the two points and the tower top, one can relate R, h, α, β. Such synthesis of geometry and trigonometry is a hallmark of the most challenging items.

Coordinate Geometry Integration with Trigonometry

The distance formula √[(x₂−x₁)²+(y₂−y₁)²] and the slope formula (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁) are the bridge between algebra and trigonometry. The angle θ between two lines with slopes m₁ and m₂ satisfies tan θ = |(m₁−m₂)/(1+m₁m₂)|. This formula is derived from the tangent subtraction identity and is valid unless the lines are perpendicular (m₁m₂ = −1) where tan θ is undefined and θ = 90°. The acute angle between a line and the x‑axis is simply arctan|m|.

Rotation of axes is a powerful technique: rotating the coordinate system by angle φ transforms coordinates (x,y) to (x′,y′) where x = x′ cos φ − y′ sin φ, y = x′ sin φ + y′ cos φ. This simplifies equations of conics or lines that are aligned with the new axes. For example, the line y = mx + c becomes y′ = (m cos φ + sin φ)/(cos φ − m sin φ) x′ + c/(cos φ − m sin φ). Choosing φ such that the new slope is zero (i.e., tan φ = −1/m) makes the line horizontal, turning distance calculations into simple vertical differences.

The parametric form of a line through point (x₀,y₀) with direction angle θ is x = x₀ + r cos θ, y = y₀ + r sin θ, where r is the signed distance from the point. This is directly derived from the definitions of sine and cosine. It is extremely useful for finding the intersection of a line with a circle: substitute the parametric equations into the circle equation to obtain a quadratic in r; the roots give the distances to the intersection points. The sum and product of the roots relate to the chord length and the power of the point.

Complex numbers provide an alternative trigonometric representation: a point (x,y) corresponds to z = x + iy = r(cos θ + i sin θ). Multiplication by eiφ rotates the point by φ, and division scales and rotates. The distance between two points is |z₁−z₂|, and the angle between vectors is arg(z₁/z₂). While complex numbers are not always in the syllabus, the underlying trigonometric relations are identical to the rotation formulas above, and familiarity with them can give a speed advantage in problems involving multiple rotations or reflections.

Finally, the equation of a circle in parametric form is x = h + R cos θ, y = k + R sin θ. This is the direct trigonometric definition of a circle and is the basis for many locus problems: for example, the locus of a point P such that the angle APB is constant (with A,B fixed) is an arc of a circle (the Apollonius circle). Expressing the condition in coordinates and using the cosine rule leads to the circle equation. Recognising these standard loci — circle, line, parabola — from trigonometric conditions is a high‑yield skill.

  • Confusing the centroid ratio (2:1) with the orthocenter‑circumcenter division on Euler’s line (also 2:1 but opposite orientation).
  • Applying the intersecting chords theorem to a tangent‑secant configuration without squaring the tangent segment.
  • Forgetting the quadrant sign when using half‑angle formulas, leading to an incorrect sign for sin(θ/2) or cos(θ/2).
  • Using tan θ = height/distance when the angle given is the angle of depression instead of elevation, which swaps opposite and adjacent sides.
  • Neglecting to convert the angle between lines formula to absolute value, producing a negative angle for obtuse intersections.
  • Misidentifying the radical axis as the line joining the centers instead of the line perpendicular to it.
  • Assuming the nine‑point circle radius equals the inradius rather than half the circumradius.

Menelaus and Ceva Theorems in Triangle Geometry

Menelaus’ theorem gives a necessary and sufficient condition for three points on the extended sides of a triangle to be collinear. For triangle ABC let points D, E, F lie on lines BC, CA, AB respectively (or their extensions). Then D, E, F are collinear iff

(BD / DC) × (CE / EA) × (AF / FB) = 1,

where the segments are directed (signed) lengths. The sign convention makes the product equal to −1 for a transversal intersecting all three sides internally; many textbooks state the absolute value version with product = 1 and note the sign separately.

Ceva’s theorem is the dual statement for concurrency. For the same triangle and points on the sides (or extensions), the lines AD, BE, CF are concurrent (or all parallel) iff

(BD / DC) × (CE / EA) × (AF / FB) = 1

with the same directed‑segment convention. The two theorems are linked by projective duality: a transversal in Menelaus corresponds to a point of concurrency in Ceva.

Both theorems are proved elegantly using area ratios. For Menelaus, drop perpendiculars from the vertices to the transversal and compare the areas of the six small triangles; the ratio of areas equals the ratio of the corresponding bases, yielding the product relation. Ceva’s proof follows by applying Menelaus to the triangle formed by the cevians and the opposite sides.

Typical applications include finding unknown segment ratios when a cevian or transversal is drawn, proving that the three medians, altitudes, or angle bisectors are concurrent (centroid, orthocenter, incenter), and solving problems where a line through a vertex meets the opposite side and the extensions of the other two sides. In coordinate geometry the directed‑segment condition translates into a determinant condition for collinearity or concurrency, which can be checked algebraically.

Advanced variants involve the trigonometric form of Ceva: (sin ∠BAD / sin ∠CAD) × (sin ∠CBE / sin ∠ABE) × (sin ∠ACF / sin ∠BCF) = 1. This version is especially useful when only angles are given, allowing one to bypass length calculations entirely.

Power of a Point and Radical Axis

The power of a point P with respect to a circle of radius R and centre O is defined as Pow(P) = OP² − R². If P lies outside the circle, the power equals the square of the length of the tangent from P to the circle; if P lies inside, it is the negative of the product of the segments of any chord through P. For a secant intersecting the circle at A and B, the intersecting‑chords theorem states PA × PB = |Pow(P)|. For a tangent from P touching at T, PT² = Pow(P).

The radical axis of two non‑concentric circles is the locus of points having equal power with respect to both circles. It is a straight line perpendicular to the line of centres. If the circles intersect, the radical axis is their common chord; if they are tangent, it is the common tangent at the point of contact; if they are separate, it lies between them and can be constructed by drawing any two intersecting chords and joining the intersection points of the pairs of circles.

Three circles taken pairwise have three radical axes. Provided the centres are not collinear, these three axes are concurrent at the radical centre. This concurrency is a powerful tool for solving systems of circle equations: the radical centre coordinates satisfy the equal‑power equations, yielding a linear system that is often simpler than solving the quadratic circle equations directly.

Applications include finding the length of a common external tangent (using the right triangle formed by the centres, the tangent point, and the foot of the perpendicular), determining the circle orthogonal to two given circles (its centre lies on the radical axis and its radius satisfies the orthogonality condition d² = R₁² + R₂²), and solving geometry problems where a point moves so that the difference of its powers to two fixed circles is constant — the locus is a line parallel to the radical axis.

In coordinate form, the power of P(x, y) to the circle x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 is x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c. The radical axis of two circles is obtained by subtracting their equations, which eliminates the quadratic terms and leaves a linear equation. This algebraic shortcut is frequently used in competitive problems to avoid heavy computation.

Technic: Using Radical Axis to Find a Circle Through Two Points Orthogonal to a Given Circle
A circle passes through points A(1,2) and B(4,6) and cuts the circle x² + y² − 6x − 8y + 15 = 0 orthogonally. Find its equation.

Let the required circle be x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0. Since it passes through A and B:

1² + 2² + 2g·1 + 2f·2 + c = 0 → 5 + 2g + 4f + c = 0 … (1)

4² + 6² + 2g·4 + 2f·6 + c = 0 → 52 + 8g + 12f + c = 0 … (2)

Orthogonality condition with given circle (g₁ = −3, f₁ = −4, c₁ = 15): 2gg₁ + 2ff₁ = c + c₁ → 2g(−3) + 2f(−4) = c + 15 → −6g − 8f = c + 15 … (3)

Subtract (1) from (2): 47 + 6g + 8f = 0 → 6g + 8f = −47.

From (3): −6g − 8f = c + 15 → 6g + 8f = −c − 15.

Equate the two expressions for 6g + 8f: −47 = −c − 15 → c = 32.

Substitute c = 32 into (1): 5 + 2g + 4f + 32 = 0 → 2g + 4f = −37 → g + 2f = −18.5.

We also have 6g + 8f = −47 → divide by 2: 3g + 4f = −23.5.

Solve the linear system: multiply first by 2: 2g + 4f = −37. Subtract from second: (3g + 4f) − (2g + 4f) = −23.5 − (−37) → g = 13.5.

Then 13.5 + 2f = −18.5 → 2f = −32 → f = −16.

Required circle: x² + y² + 27x − 32y + 32 = 0.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for a circle satisfying orthogonality and point conditions, write the general circle equation, impose the point conditions to get linear equations in g, f, c, then use the orthogonality relation 2gg₁ + 2ff₁ = c + c₁. This yields a linear system that is solved in seconds, avoiding the quadratic expansion of the full circle equation.

General Solutions of Trigonometric Equations

Solving equations such as sin x = a, cos x = a, or tan x = a requires the principal value and the periodicity of the function. For sin x = a (|a| ≤ 1) the principal solution is x = arcsin a ∈ [−π/2, π/2]. The general solution is x = nπ + (−1)ⁿ arcsin a, n ∈ ℤ. For cos x = a the principal value is x = arccos a ∈ [0, π] and the general solution is x = 2nπ ± arccos a. For tan x = a the principal value is x = arctan a ∈ (−π/2, π/2) and the general solution is x = nπ + arctan a.

When the equation involves multiple angles, e.g. sin 2x = ½, first solve for the inner angle: 2x = nπ + (−1)ⁿ π/6, then divide by 2: x = nπ/2 + (−1)ⁿ π/12. Care must be taken that the division does not introduce extraneous solutions; checking each family against the original equation is advisable.

Equations reducible to quadratic form in a trigonometric function, such as 2 sin² x − 3 sin x + 1 = 0, are solved by substituting t = sin x, solving the quadratic for t, then applying the general solution for each admissible t (|t| ≤ 1). If the quadratic yields a value outside [−1, 1], that root is discarded.

Using identities to transform the equation can simplify it. For example, sin x + cos x = 1 can be rewritten as √2 sin(x + π/4) = 1, giving sin(x + π/4) = 1/√2, whose general solution is x + π/4 = nπ + (−1)ⁿ π/4. Similarly, equations containing both sin x and cos x can be converted to a single function using the tangent half‑angle substitution t = tan(x/2), where sin x = 2t/(1+t²), cos x = (1−t²)/(1+t²). This turns the trigonometric equation into a rational equation in t, which is then solved algebraically.

Extraneous roots often arise when squaring both sides or when multiplying by an expression that could be zero. Always verify each candidate solution in the original equation, especially when the domain is restricted (e.g., 0 ≤ x < 2π).

Trigonometric Form of Complex Numbers and Rotation

A complex number z = x + iy can be represented in polar form as z = r(cos θ + i sin θ), where r = √(x² + y²) is the modulus and θ = arg(z) is the argument, usually taken in (−π, π]. Multiplication of two complex numbers corresponds to multiplying their moduli and adding their arguments: (r₁(cos θ₁ + i sin θ₁))·(r₂(cos θ₂ + i sin θ₂)) = r₁r₂(cos(θ₁+θ₂) + i sin(θ₁+θ₂)). This is a direct consequence of the addition formulas for sine and cosine.

De Moivre’s theorem states (cos θ + i sin θ)ⁿ = cos nθ + i sin nθ for any integer n. It extends to rational exponents to give the n‑th roots of unity: the solutions of zⁿ = 1 are cos(2kπ/n) + i sin(2kπ/n), k = 0,1,…,n−1. These roots lie at the vertices of a regular n‑gon on the unit circle.

Rotation of a point (x, y) about the origin by angle φ is achieved by multiplying its complex representation by cos φ + i sin φ. The new coordinates are (x cos φ − y sin φ, x sin φ + y cos φ), which matches the standard rotation matrix. This complex‑number approach simplifies problems involving successive rotations: the net rotation is the product of the individual rotation factors, i.e., the sum of the angles.

Reflection across a line through the origin making angle α with the x‑axis can be expressed as z → e2iα z, where z is the complex conjugate. Combining rotations and reflections yields the full group of planar isometries, useful for solving geometry problems that involve symmetry.

In coordinate geometry, the equation of a line through the origin with direction angle θ is Im(z e−iθ) = 0, and a circle centred at the origin is |z| = R. Translating the centre to a point c gives |z − c| = R. The intersection of a line and a circle becomes a quadratic in the real parameter along the line, whose discriminant determines the number of intersection points.

Locus Problems Using Trigonometric Conditions

A locus is the set of all points satisfying a given geometric condition. Many classic loci are described naturally by trigonometric relations. The most frequent is the constant‑angle locus: given two fixed points A and B, the set of points P such that ∠APB = θ (0 < θ < π) is an arc of a circle (the Apollonius circle) whose chord AB subtends angle θ at the circumference. The centre lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB, and the radius R satisfies AB = 2R sin θ.

If the condition is a constant ratio of distances, PA / PB = k (k > 0, k ≠ 1), the locus is a circle (the circle of Apollonius). Using the cosine rule in triangles PAB and the law of sines, one can derive the Cartesian equation: (x−x₁)² + (y−y₁)² = k²[(x−x₂)² + (y−y₂)²], which simplifies to a circle equation after expanding and collecting terms.

A constant sum of distances, PA + PB = constant, defines an ellipse; a constant difference |PA − PB| = constant defines a hyperbola. While these are conic sections, they can be approached trigonometrically by introducing the eccentric angle parameter: for an ellipse x = a cos φ, y = b sin φ, the sum of distances to the foci is 2a, independent of φ.

Another useful trigonometric locus arises from the condition that the angle between two lines from P to fixed points equals a given value. Writing the slopes in terms of coordinates and using the formula tan θ = |(m₁−m₂)/(1+m₁m₂)| yields an equation that, after clearing denominators, is quadratic in x and y — confirming the locus is a circle or a line (when θ = π/2).

In competitive problems, recognising the standard loci — circle (constant angle or constant ratio), line (angle = π/2 or ratio = 1), ellipse/hyperbola (sum/difference constant) — allows immediate identification of the curve and its parameters, saving time on algebraic derivation.

Inversion in Plane Geometry

Inversion is a transformation that maps a point P (different from a fixed centre O) to a point P′ on the ray OP such that OP·OP′ = r², where r is the radius of the inversion circle. The centre O has no image; points on the inversion circle are fixed. A line not passing through O becomes a circle through O, and a circle through O becomes a line not passing through O. Circles orthogonal to the inversion circle are invariant as sets. This duality converts many intersection problems into simpler ones: intersecting circles become intersecting lines, and tangency is preserved because angles are preserved (inversion is conformal). The power of a point with respect to a circle is exactly the signed product OP·OP′, so inversion gives a geometric interpretation of the power. When solving problems, choose the inversion centre at a point where several circles or lines meet; the radius can be taken arbitrarily, often r = 1 for algebraic convenience. After inversion, apply elementary Euclidean facts, then invert back. A classic application is the proof of the radical axis theorem: invert about a point on the common chord of two circles; the circles become lines, and the chord becomes the line at infinity, showing the radical axis is a straight line. Another use is the construction of the Apollonian circle: given two points A, B and a ratio k, invert with centre A; the locus of points P with PA/PB = k becomes a line, whose pre‑image is the required circle. Inversion also simplifies the proof of Ptolemy’s theorem for cyclic quadrilaterals: invert about one vertex, the circumcircle becomes a line, and the theorem reduces to the triangle inequality on a line. Care must be taken with orientation: directed distances change sign when the point lies inside the inversion circle, which affects the sign of the power. In competitive problems, recognising a configuration that becomes linear after inversion (e.g., three circles mutually tangent) often yields a solution in a few lines.

Spiral Similarity and Miquel Configurations

A spiral similarity is a composition of a rotation and a homothety with the same centre. It sends any segment AB to a segment A′B′ such that triangles OAB and OA′B′ are similar and similarly oriented. The centre O is the unique point where the circles through A, A′ and B, B′ intersect (other than the trivial intersection at infinity). This centre is often called the Miquel point of the complete quadrilateral formed by the four lines AB, A′B′, AA′, BB′. In a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, the intersection of the diagonals AC and BD is the centre of a spiral similarity sending AB to CD and AD to BC. More generally, given four points A, B, C, D on a circle, the Miquel point of the complete quadrilateral formed by the lines AB, BC, CD, DA lies on the circumcircle and is the centre of the spiral similarity that maps AB to CD. The Miquel theorem states that for any four lines forming a complete quadrilateral, the four circumcircles of the triangles formed by taking three of the lines concur at a single point — the Miquel point. This point has equal power with respect to the four circles, and the angles subtended by opposite sides at the Miquel point are equal. Spiral similarity also explains the existence of the centre of the Apollonian circle: the locus of points P with PA/PB = k is a circle whose centre is the centre of the spiral similarity sending A to B with ratio k. In problems involving intersecting circles, look for a point that sees two chords under equal angles; that point is often a spiral similarity centre. The configuration of two intersecting circles and their common external tangents yields a spiral similarity centred at the intersection of the tangents, mapping one circle to the other. Recognising this allows immediate computation of lengths using the similarity ratio, which equals the ratio of the radii. When a problem gives several circles passing through a common point, the other intersections often lie on a circle (the Miquel circle), and the common point is the centre of a spiral similarity sending one chord to another. This principle underlies many geometry olympiad lemmas and appears in advanced competitive exams as a shortcut to avoid heavy coordinate work.

Technique: Spiral Similarity Centre Identification
Two circles ω₁ and ω₂ intersect at points X and Y. A line through X meets ω₁ again at A and ω₂ again at B. The tangents to ω₁ at A and to ω₂ at B intersect at T. Prove that T, Y, and the midpoint M of AB are collinear.
Let O₁, O₂ be the centres of ω₁, ω₂. The tangents at A and B are perpendicular to O₁A and O₂B respectively. Since ∠AXY = ∠ABY (angles in the same segment) and ∠BXY = ∠BAY, triangles AXY and BYX are similar. Hence there is a spiral similarity centred at Y sending A to B and X to X (fixed). This similarity also sends the tangent at A to the tangent at B, so it maps the line through A perpendicular to O₁A to the line through B perpendicular to O₂B. Therefore the intersection T of the two tangents lies on the line through Y that is the image of the line at infinity under the similarity, i.e., the line YT is the axis of the spiral similarity. The midpoint M of AB is the image of the midpoint of the segment joining the centres of the two circles under the same similarity, and it is well known that the centre of a spiral similarity, the midpoint of the corresponding segment, and the intersection of the corresponding tangents are collinear. Hence T, Y, M are collinear.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem presents two intersecting circles and a line through one intersection, immediately sketch the spiral similarity centred at the other intersection. Mark equal angles and look for the centre of the similarity; it often coincides with a given point (e.g., the other intersection). Use the fact that the similarity sends tangents to tangents and midpoints to midpoints. This visual cue converts a lengthy angle chase into a single similarity statement, saving precious minutes.

Trigonometric Substitutions for Algebraic Equations

Many algebraic equations become tractable after a trigonometric substitution that exploits identities such as sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, 1 + tan²θ = sec²θ, or 1 + cot²θ = csc²θ. The classic substitution x = a sin θ (or a cos θ) removes a square root √(a² − x²) and converts the integral or equation into a rational function of sin θ and cos θ. For expressions √(x² + a²) use x = a tan θ; for √(x² − a²) use x = a sec θ (with domain restrictions). In solving polynomial equations, the substitution x = 2 cos θ transforms the cubic x³ − 3x + 1 = 0 into 8 cos³θ − 6 cos θ + 1 = 0, i.e., 2 cos 3θ + 1 = 0, giving cos 3θ = −½ and thus θ = (2π/3 + 2kπ)/3. This yields the three real roots 2 cos(2π/9), 2 cos(4π/9), 2 cos(8π/9). The same idea solves any depressed cubic with three real roots (Casus irreducibilis) by using the triple‑angle formula cos 3θ = 4 cos³θ − 3 cos θ. For quartic equations that are symmetric, set x + 1/x = 2 cos θ; then x² + 1/x² = 2 cos 2θ, etc., reducing the equation to a polynomial in cos θ. In inequalities, the substitution a = tan A, b = tan B, c = tan C with A + B + C = π converts the condition ab + bc + ca = 1 into tan A tan B + tan B tan C + tan C tan A = 1, which is equivalent to A + B + C = π. This turns algebraic inequalities into trigonometric ones that can be handled with Jensen’s inequality or known bounds such as sin A + sin B + sin C ≤ 3√3/2. When the domain of a variable is [−1, 1], the substitution x = cos θ is natural and often simplifies expressions containing √(1 − x²). Always check the range of the substitution to avoid extraneous solutions; for instance, x = sec θ requires |x| ≥ 1. After solving for θ, back‑substitute and verify each candidate in the original equation, because squaring steps or the periodic nature of trigonometric functions can introduce spurious roots.

Spherical Trigonometry Essentials

Spherical trigonometry deals with triangles drawn on the surface of a sphere, where sides are arcs of great circles measured by the central angles they subtend. Let a, b, c be the side lengths (in radians) opposite angles A, B, C. The fundamental laws are the spherical law of cosines for sides: cos a = cos b cos c + sin b sin c cos A, and for angles: cos A = −cos B cos C + sin B sin C cos a. The spherical law of sines states sin a / sin A = sin b / sin B = sin c / sin C. These reduce to the planar laws when the sides are small (using cos x ≈ 1 − x²/2, sin x ≈ x). A right spherical triangle (C = π/2) obeys Napier’s rules: the five parts (two legs, complement of hypotenuse, complements of the two angles) arranged in a circle satisfy that the sine of any middle part equals the product of the tangents of the adjacent parts, and also equals the product of the cosines of the opposite parts. For example, sin a = tan b cot B. The area Δ of a spherical triangle on a unit sphere is given by the spherical excess: Δ = A + B + C − π (Girard’s theorem). On a sphere of radius R, the area is R²(A + B + C − π). This excess is always positive, reflecting the fact that the sum of angles exceeds π. In navigation, the great‑circle distance between two points with latitudes φ₁, φ₂ and longitude difference Δλ is given by the spherical law of cosines: cos d = sin φ₁ sin φ₂ + cos φ₁ cos φ₂ cos Δλ. The initial bearing satisfies sin d sin α = cos φ₂ sin Δλ. Spherical trigonometry also appears in the study of the celestial sphere: the altitude and azimuth of a star are obtained from its declination δ and hour angle H using the same formulas with φ as observer latitude. In competitive problems, a common trick is to project a planar configuration onto a sphere (e.g., via stereographic projection) where circles become circles and angles are preserved, allowing the use of spherical laws to derive planar results. Conversely, a planar problem involving a circle and a point can be lifted to a spherical triangle by placing the point at the sphere’s centre; the chord lengths become side angles, and the inscribed angles become dihedral angles. Recognising when a problem is essentially spherical (e.g., given three angles of a triangle on a sphere, find the side) avoids futile attempts to apply planar formulas.

Cross Ratio and Projective Invariants

The cross ratio of four collinear points A, B, C, D is defined as (A, B; C, D) = (AC/BC) / (AD/BD), using directed segments. It is invariant under projective transformations (perspectivities and projectivities), making it a fundamental tool in projective geometry. For four concurrent lines through a point O meeting a transversal at A, B, C, D, the cross ratio of the points equals the cross ratio of the lines, defined via sines of angles: (OA, OB; OC, OD) = (sin ∠AOC / sin ∠BOC) / (sin ∠AOD / sin ∠BOD). This invariance extends to conics: for four points on a conic, the cross ratio of the lines joining them to a fifth point on the conic is constant (Chasles’ theorem). In coordinate terms, if points have homogeneous coordinates, the cross ratio can be computed as a ratio of determinants. A harmonic bundle occurs when the cross ratio equals −1; this characterises pole‑polar relationships: if a point P lies on the polar of Q with respect to a circle, then the cross ratio (P, Q; X, Y) = −1 where X, Y are the intersections of line PQ with the circle. The complete quadrilateral yields three diagonal points; each pair of opposite sides and the line joining the other two diagonal points form a harmonic set. In problems, identifying a harmonic division often allows immediate length ratios without solving equations. For instance, if a line through a vertex of a triangle meets the opposite side and the circumcircle again, the cross ratio of the four points (vertex, foot, intersection with side, intersection with circle) is −1, giving a simple relation between segment lengths. The cross ratio also appears in the theory of Möbius transformations: a transformation z → (az + b)/(cz + d) preserves the cross ratio of four complex numbers. This links complex numbers, inversion, and projective geometry. When a problem involves a pencil of lines or a set of points on a circle, compute the cross ratio using sines of angles or directed lengths; if it matches a known value (−1, 2, 1/2), the configuration is harmonic or has a special symmetry, often yielding the answer directly. Be careful with orientation: the cross ratio changes under permutation of the four points according to the symmetric group S₄, producing six possible values related by λ, 1−λ, 1/λ, 1/(1−λ), (λ−1)/λ, λ/(λ−1). Recognising these relations prevents sign errors.

  • Assuming inversion preserves distances instead of angles; only angles are preserved, lengths change by factor r²/(OP·OQ).
  • Misidentifying the centre of a spiral similarity; it is the intersection of the circles through corresponding points, not the intersection of the segments themselves.
  • Forgetting domain restrictions when using trigonometric substitutions (e.g., using x = sin θ for |x| > 1).
  • Applying planar law of cosines to a spherical triangle without converting side lengths to central angles.
  • Treating cross ratio as an unsigned quantity; directed segments are essential for invariance under projection.
  • Overlooking the periodicity of trigonometric functions when back‑substituting, leading to missing or extraneous roots.
  • Confusing the spherical excess formula with planar area formulas; the excess is measured in radians, not square units, unless multiplied by R².
