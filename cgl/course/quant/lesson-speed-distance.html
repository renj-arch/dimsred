What is Speed, Distance & Time?

Speed, Distance, and Time form the backbone of one of the most high-scoring topics in the exam Quantitative Aptitude. Every year, 4–6 questions appear from this chapter in Tier 1 and Tier 2. The basic relationship is Distance = Speed × Time. Problems span trains, boats & streams, circular tracks, races, and clocks — making this a versatile topic that rewards conceptual clarity and shortcut mastery.

the exam Weightage
In the last 5 years, Speed-Distance-Time contributes 5–6 questions in Tier 1 and 4–5 in Tier 2. Average speed, train-platform, boats & streams, and relative speed are the most frequently tested sub-topics.

1. Basic Formula & Unit Conversion

Distance = Speed × Time. This can be rearranged as Speed = Distance / Time and Time = Distance / Speed. Always ensure units are consistent before plugging values.

Unit Conversion: 1 km/h = (1000 m) / (3600 s) = 5/18 m/s. Conversely, 1 m/s = 18/5 km/h.

Tip 1 — Quick Unit Conversion
To convert km/h to m/s, multiply by 5/18. To convert m/s to km/h, multiply by 18/5. Memorise: 36 km/h = 10 m/s, 54 km/h = 15 m/s, 72 km/h = 20 m/s, 90 km/h = 25 m/s, 108 km/h = 30 m/s. These repeating values save time.
Tip 2 — DST Triangle
Cover the quantity you need: D over S×T. If you need speed, cover S → D/T. If you need time, cover T → D/S. This visual trick prevents formula errors under exam pressure.
Example 1 — Unit Conversion
A train runs at 25 m/s. What is its speed in km/h?
Solution: Speed = 25 × (18/5) = 5 × 18 = 90 km/h. Quick check: 25 m/s = 90 km/h.

2. Average Speed

Average speed is not the arithmetic mean of speeds unless times are equal. It is Total Distance ÷ Total Time.

Case 1 — Equal Distances: A body travels from A to B at speed a and returns at speed b. Average speed = 2ab / (a + b).

Case 2 — Equal Times: A body travels at speed a for time t and at speed b for another t. Average speed = (a + b) / 2.

Tip 3 — Average Speed Shortcut
When distances are equal (common in the exam), never average the speeds directly. Use 2ab/(a+b). Example: 40 km/h and 60 km/h give average = 2×40×60/(100) = 48 km/h, not 50.
Tip 4 — When Times Are Equal
If the body moves at speed a for t hours and speed b for the next t hours, average speed is simply (a+b)/2. This is rarely tested directly but appears in multi-leg journey problems.
Example 2 — Average Speed (Equal Distances)
A person covers half the distance at 30 km/h and the other half at 50 km/h. Find the average speed for the entire journey.
Solution: Here distances are equal (each is half). Average speed = 2ab/(a+b) = 2×30×50/(30+50) = 3000/80 = 37.5 km/h.
Example 3 — Average Speed (Equal Times)
A car travels at 40 km/h for the first 2 hours and at 60 km/h for the next 2 hours. Find the average speed.
Solution: Total distance = 40×2 + 60×2 = 80 + 120 = 200 km. Total time = 4 hours. Average speed = 200/4 = 50 km/h.

3. Relative Speed

When two objects move, their relative speed determines how fast the distance between them changes.

Opposite Direction: Relative speed = S₁ + S₂. Time to meet = Initial distance / (S₁ + S₂).

Same Direction: Relative speed = |S₁ − S₂|. Time to catch up / overtake = Initial separation / |S₁ − S₂|.

Tip 5 — Relative Speed Caution
Always convert km/h to m/s before multiplying with time in seconds, or keep everything in km and hours. Mixing units is the #1 the exam trap in this chapter.
Example 4 — Opposite Direction
Two friends start from points A and B 450 m apart and walk towards each other at 3 m/s and 2 m/s respectively. How long will they take to meet?
Solution: Relative speed = 3 + 2 = 5 m/s. Time = 450/5 = 90 seconds = 1.5 minutes.
Example 5 — Same Direction (Chase)
A thief runs at 8 km/h. A policeman spots him 200 m away and chases at 10 km/h. How far does the policeman run before catching the thief?
Solution: Relative speed = 10 − 8 = 2 km/h = 2 × 5/18 = 5/9 m/s. Time to catch = 200 ÷ (5/9) = 360 s. Distance run by policeman = 10 × (5/18) × 360 = 10 × 100 = 1000 m.

4. Train Problems

Train problems hinge on one key insight: when a train passes an object, the distance covered equals the train's length plus the object's length (if the object has length).

  • Passing a pole / person: Distance = Length of train. Time = Length / Speed.
  • Passing a platform / bridge / tunnel: Distance = Length of train + Length of platform.
  • Passing another train (opposite direction): Distance = Sum of lengths. Relative speed = Sum of speeds.
  • Passing another train (same direction): Distance = Sum of lengths. Relative speed = Difference of speeds.
Tip 6 — Train Platform Shortcut
For train + platform problems, The exam often asks speed in km/h. Solve in m/s first, then multiply by 18/5. Remember: Total distance = L_train + L_platform.
Tip 7 — Two Trains Crossing
When two trains cross each other (opposite direction), time = (L₁+L₂)/(S₁+S₂). For same direction, time = (L₁+L₂)/|S₁−S₂|. Keep lengths in metres and speeds in m/s.
Example 6 — Train + Platform
A 240 m long train crosses a 360 m long platform in 40 seconds. Find the speed of the train in km/h.
Solution: Total distance = 240 + 360 = 600 m. Time = 40 s. Speed = 600/40 = 15 m/s = 15 × 18/5 = 54 km/h.
Example 7 — Train Passing a Pole
A 180 m long train passes a pole in 9 seconds. What is its speed in km/h?
Solution: Distance = 180 m (only train length). Time = 9 s. Speed = 180/9 = 20 m/s = 20 × 18/5 = 72 km/h.
Example 8 — Two Trains Crossing
Two trains of length 200 m and 300 m run towards each other at 54 km/h and 72 km/h. How long will they take to cross each other?
Solution: Relative speed = 54 + 72 = 126 km/h = 126 × 5/18 = 35 m/s. Total length = 200 + 300 = 500 m. Time = 500/35 = 100/7 ≈ 14.3 seconds.

5. Boats & Streams

Still water speed of a boat is its speed when there is no current. Stream speed adds or subtracts depending on direction.

Downstream (D): Speed along the current = Boat speed + Stream speed.

Upstream (U): Speed against the current = Boat speed − Stream speed.

Boat speed (in still water): (D + U) / 2.

Stream speed: (D − U) / 2.

Tip 8 — Boats Formula Memory
Downstream: D = B + S (both help). Upstream: U = B − S (boat minus stream). Boat speed = (D+U)/2 (average). Stream speed = (D−U)/2 (half the difference).
Example 9 — Downstream & Upstream Time
A boat's speed in still water is 12 km/h and stream speed is 4 km/h. How long will it take to go 48 km downstream and return?
Solution: Downstream speed = 12 + 4 = 16 km/h. Time downstream = 48/16 = 3 h. Upstream speed = 12 − 4 = 8 km/h. Time upstream = 48/8 = 6 h. Total = 9 h.
Example 10 — Find Stream Speed
A boat covers 36 km downstream in 6 hours and 36 km upstream in 9 hours. Find the speed of the stream.
Solution: Downstream speed = 36/6 = 6 km/h. Upstream speed = 36/9 = 4 km/h. Stream speed = (6 − 4)/2 = 1 km/h.

6. Circular Track Problems

On a circular track of length L, two runners start from the same point.

Meeting for the first time (opposite direction): Time = L / (S₁ + S₂).

Meeting for the first time (same direction): Time = L / |S₁ − S₂|.

Meeting at the starting point again: LCM of individual lap times = LCM(L/S₁, L/S₂).

Tip 9 — Circular Track LCM
To find when runners meet again at the starting point, compute time each takes for one lap (L/S), then take LCM of those times. This is a common the exam Tier 2 question.
Example 11 — Circular Track
Two runners start from the same point on a 400 m circular track at 8 m/s and 12 m/s. When will they meet for the first time if they run in opposite directions? Same direction?
Solution: Opposite: relative speed = 8 + 12 = 20 m/s. Time = 400/20 = 20 s. Same direction: relative speed = 12 − 8 = 4 m/s. Time = 400/4 = 100 s.

7. Race Problems

In races, one runner may give another a head start or beat them by a distance. The key ratio is distance : time = speed.

Tip 10 — Race Problems
When A beats B by d metres in a race of L metres, in the time A covers L, B covers (L − d). Use this proportion to find speeds or times.
Example 12 — Race Problem
In a 1000 m race, A beats B by 200 m. Find the ratio of their speeds.
Solution: When A runs 1000 m, B runs 800 m in the same time. Speed ratio = Distance ratio = 1000 : 800 = 5 : 4.

8. Clock Problems as Speed Problems

The minute hand and hour hand move at constant speeds. Minute hand speed = 6° per minute. Hour hand speed = 0.5° per minute. Relative speed = 5.5° per minute.

Tip 11 — Clock Hand Speeds
Minute hand: 360° in 60 min = 6°/min. Hour hand: 360° in 720 min = 0.5°/min. Relative speed = 5.5°/min. Hands meet every 360/5.5 = 720/11 = 65 5/11 minutes.
Example 13 — Clock Problem
At what time between 2 and 3 o’clock will the hands of a clock be together?
Solution: At 2:00, hour hand is 60° ahead. Relative speed = 5.5°/min. Time to cover 60° = 60/5.5 = 120/11 = 10 10/11 min. So time = 2:10:54.5 approximately.

9. the exam Shortcuts & Tricks Compilation

Tip 12 — Average Speed (Equal Distances)
Average speed when distances are equal: 2ab/(a+b). This is the most heavily tested the exam formula in this chapter.
Tip 13 — Average Speed (Equal Times)
Average speed when times are equal: (a+b)/2. Use when a body travels at speed a for t hours and speed b for the next t hours.
Tip 14 — Relative Speed Table
km/h to m/s: multiply by 5/18. Common values: 36→10, 54→15, 72→20, 90→25, 108→30, 126→35, 144→40. Memorise for instant recall.
Tip 15 — Train & Platform
If a train passes a platform in t seconds, speed = (L_train + L_platform)/t. To get km/h, multiply result by 18/5.
Tip 16 — Time to Overtake
If two bodies start simultaneously from the same point in the same direction, time to meet again on a circular track = L/|S₁−S₂|. Opposite direction = L/(S₁+S₂).
Tip 17 — Boat Speed Shortcut
If a boat takes t₁ hours downstream and t₂ hours upstream for the same distance D, boat speed = D(t₁+t₂)/(2 t₁ t₂). Stream speed = D(t₂−t₁)/(2 t₁ t₂).
Tip 18 — the exam Exam Strategy
In the exam, always write the formula first, convert units to matching pairs (km with h or m with s), then plug numbers. This structured approach prevents 90% of careless errors.

10. Solved Examples — Advanced Level

Example 14 — Train + Man (Same Direction)
A 180 m long train travelling at 72 km/h overtakes a man walking at 6 km/h in the same direction. How long will the train take to pass the man?
Solution: Relative speed = 72 − 6 = 66 km/h = 66 × 5/18 = 55/3 m/s. Time = 180 ÷ (55/3) = 180 × 3/55 = 540/55 = 108/11 ≈ 9.82 seconds.
Example 15 — Two Trains Passing (Same Direction)
Two trains of length 160 m and 240 m run on parallel tracks at 60 km/h and 42 km/h in the same direction. How long will they take to cross each other?
Solution: Relative speed = 60 − 42 = 18 km/h = 18 × 5/18 = 5 m/s. Total length = 160 + 240 = 400 m. Time = 400/5 = 80 seconds.

11. Advanced Practice Problems

Advanced 1 — Average Speed Variation
A person travels from A to B at 30 km/h and returns at 20 km/h. Next day, he travels A to B at 40 km/h and returns at 30 km/h. Compare the average speeds.
Solution: Day 1 average = 2×30×20/50 = 24 km/h. Day 2 average = 2×40×30/70 ≈ 34.3 km/h.
Advanced 2 — Train Crossing a Tunnel
A 300 m long train enters a 500 m long tunnel at 72 km/h. How long from the moment the engine enters till the last coach exits?
Solution: Distance = 300 + 500 = 800 m. Speed = 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s. Time = 800/20 = 40 seconds.
Advanced 3 — Boat Intermediate
A boat takes 9 hours downstream from A to B and 12 hours upstream. Stream speed is 2 km/h. Find AB.
Solution: Let boat speed = b. (b+2)/(b−2) = 12/9 = 4/3. 3b+6 = 4b−8 → b = 14 km/h. AB = (14+2)×9 = 144 km.
Advanced 4 — Circular Track Three Runners
Three runners start from the same point on a 600 m circular track at 6 m/s, 8 m/s, and 10 m/s. When will all three meet again at the starting point?
Solution: Lap times: 600/6=100 s, 600/8=75 s, 600/10=60 s. LCM(100,75,60)=300 s = 5 min.
Advanced 5 — Race with Head Start
In a 200 m race, A gives B a 20 m head start and beats B by 5 seconds. A's speed is 10 m/s. Find B's speed.
Solution: A’s time = 200/10 = 20 s. B covers 180 m in 25 s. B’s speed = 180/25 = 7.2 m/s.
Advanced 6 — Relative Speed with Head Start
Train leaves Delhi at 6:00 AM at 60 km/h. Another train leaves at 7:30 AM at 75 km/h same direction. At what distance from Delhi will the second overtake the first?
Solution: Head start = 1.5×60 = 90 km. Relative speed = 15 km/h. Time = 90/15 = 6 h. Distance = 75×6 = 450 km.
Advanced 7 — Boat with Total Time
A man rows 24 km downstream and back in 10 hours. Stream speed is 2 km/h. Find his rowing speed in still water.
Solution: Let boat speed = b. 24/(b+2) + 24/(b−2) = 10. 24(2b)/(b²−4) = 10 → 48b = 10b²−40 → 5b²−24b−20=0 → (5b+4)(b−5)=0 → b=5 km/h.
Advanced 8 — Clock Angle
Find the angle between the hour and minute hands at 3:40.
Solution: Minute hand at 40 min = 240° from 12. Hour hand at 3:40 = 3×30 + 40×0.5 = 90+20 = 110°. Difference = 130°. Smaller angle = 130°.

Uniformly Accelerated Motion in Aptitude Questions

Although most speed‑distance‑time problems assume constant speed, a noticeable minority of advanced questions introduce uniform acceleration. The standard equations of motion — v = u + at, s = ut + ½ at², v² = u² + 2as — remain valid, but the examiner expects you to recognise when the average speed over an interval can be replaced by the arithmetic mean of initial and final speeds. For a body starting from rest (u = 0) and accelerating uniformly, the distance covered in the nth second is u + a(n – ½); when u = 0 this simplifies to a(n – ½). A frequent pattern is a vehicle that accelerates from rest to a maximum speed, cruises, then decelerates uniformly to rest. The total distance equals the area of a trapezoid on a speed‑time graph, which can be computed as ½ × (t₁ + t₂) × v_max where t₁ and t₂ are acceleration and deceleration durations. If the acceleration and deceleration magnitudes are equal, the time to reach maximum speed equals the time to stop, and the distance during each phase is ½ v_max × t_acc. Candidates should also be comfortable converting “uniformly increasing speed” into “average speed = (initial + final) ÷ 2” for any sub‑interval, because the speed‑time graph is a straight line. A common twist: the problem gives the distance covered in the first k seconds and in the next k seconds, asking for the acceleration. Using s₁ = ½ a k² and s₂ = ½ a (2k)² – ½ a k² = 3 × ½ a k², the ratio s₂ : s₁ = 3 : 1 is a quick diagnostic. When numbers do not follow this ratio, the motion is not uniformly accelerated from rest, signalling either an initial speed or a piecewise‑constant acceleration. Always verify the “from rest” condition before applying the 1:3:5… pattern for successive equal‑time intervals.

Rest Periods and Waiting Times in Meeting Problems

Meeting problems become significantly harder when one or both moving entities pause. The fundamental principle remains: the sum of distances covered by the two parties equals the initial separation (for opposite directions) or the difference equals the separation (for same direction). A rest period simply adds a time interval during which the resting party contributes zero distance. If person A walks at speed v_A and rests for t_r minutes, the effective distance covered by A in total elapsed time T is v_A × (T – t_r). When both parties have rest periods, the equation becomes v_A (T – trA) + v_B (T – trB) = D for opposite‑direction meetings. Solving for T yields T = (D + v_A trA + v_B trB) ÷ (v_A + v_B). A subtle variation: the rest occurs after a certain distance has been covered, not at the start. In that case, split the journey into “moving” and “resting” phases, compute the time at which the rest begins, then treat the remaining distance with the reduced effective speed. Another examiner favourite is a “waiting for the other” scenario: a faster person reaches the meeting point early and waits. The meeting time is then the arrival time of the slower person, and the waiting time is the difference. To avoid algebraic errors, draw a timeline marking each phase (move, rest, move) for both parties; the visual separation of moving intervals makes the distance‑sum equation transparent. Remember that rest periods do not affect relative speed while both are moving; they only shift the effective start or end of the moving windows.

Worked Example: Opposite Directions with a Rest
Two cyclists start 120 km apart and ride toward each other. Cyclist A rides at 20 km/h but rests for 30 minutes after riding for 1 hour. Cyclist B rides continuously at 15 km/h. After how many hours from the start do they meet?

Let total elapsed time be T hours. Cyclist A moves for 1 h, rests 0.5 h, then moves again for T – 1.5 hours (provided T > 1.5). Distance by A = 20 × 1 + 20 × (T – 1.5) = 20T – 10. Distance by B = 15 × T = 15T. Sum = 120 ⇒ 20T – 10 + 15T = 120 ⇒ 35T = 130 ⇒ T = 130/35 = 26/7 ≈ 3.714 h. Check: A moves 1 h (20 km), rests 0.5 h, moves 2.214 h (44.28 km) total 64.28 km. B moves 3.714 h (55.71 km). Sum ≈ 120 km. Meeting occurs at 3 hours 42.9 minutes.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem includes rest or waiting, first compute the “pure moving” meeting time ignoring rests (using relative speed). Then add the rest durations of the party that rests before the meeting. If both rest, add the weighted rest times as shown in the formula T = (D + Σ v_i tri) ÷ Σ v_i. This two‑step mental shortcut avoids setting up a full piecewise equation under time pressure.

Weighted Average Speed for Unequal Segments

The classic results — harmonic mean for equal distances, arithmetic mean for equal times — are special cases of the universal definition: average speed = total distance ÷ total time. When a journey consists of n segments with distances d_i and speeds v_i, the total time is Σ (d_i / v_i). The average speed is therefore (Σ d_i) ÷ Σ (d_i / v_i). This expression is a weighted harmonic mean where the weights are the distances. If the problem gives times t_i and speeds v_i, the total distance is Σ (v_i t_i) and total time is Σ t_i, yielding a weighted arithmetic mean with time weights. A common examiner device is to present a journey where the first half of the distance is covered at speed v₁ and the second half at speed v₂, but then ask for the average speed if the first half of the *time* is at v₁ and the second half of the *time* at v₂. The first case gives 2v₁v₂/(v₁+v₂); the second gives (v₁+v₂)/2. Mixing the two conditions in a single question (e.g., “first 1/3 of distance at v₁, next 1/3 of time at v₂, remainder at v₃”) forces you to compute each segment’s distance and time explicitly. Always convert every segment to a (distance, time) pair before summing. A useful shortcut: if the journey is described in fractions of total distance, let total distance = LCM of denominators × a convenient unit (say 1 km), compute each segment’s time, then apply the universal formula. This avoids algebraic fractions and reduces arithmetic errors.

Train Coupling and Uncoupling Scenarios

Standard train problems assume a fixed train length. Advanced variants involve coupling (two trains joining) or uncoupling (a train splitting) while in motion. When two trains couple, their lengths add, but the speed after coupling is determined by conservation of momentum if the question states “they move together without jerk”. In aptitude tests, however, the simpler assumption is that the coupled train continues at the speed of the faster train (or a given common speed). The time to cross a stationary object after coupling becomes (L₁ + L₂) / v_common. If the coupling occurs while both trains are moving toward each other, the relative speed before coupling is v₁ + v₂; the moment they couple, the combined length is L₁ + L₂ and the relative speed becomes zero (they move as one). The meeting point is where the fronts meet; the time from start to coupling is initial separation ÷ (v₁ + v₂). After coupling, if they continue to a platform, the crossing time uses the combined length and the new speed. Uncoupling problems typically ask: a train of length L splits into two parts of lengths L₁ and L₂; the front part continues at speed v₁, the rear at v₂. The time for the two parts to separate by a distance D is D ÷ |v₁ – v₂| (if moving same direction) or D ÷ (v₁ + v₂) (if opposite). A frequent trap: forgetting that the initial separation at the moment of uncoupling is zero, so the distance to be covered is purely the required gap D. Always identify the reference frame (ground or one train) before writing the relative‑speed equation.

Variable Stream Speed in Boat Problems

Standard boat‑and‑stream problems assume a constant stream speed s. In advanced sets, the stream speed may change with distance (e.g., faster in the middle of the river) or with time (tidal variation). The usual approach is to divide the journey into stretches where the stream speed is constant. For each stretch, downstream speed = b + s_i, upstream speed = b – s_i, where b is the boat’s speed in still water (assumed constant). The total time for a round trip is Σ (d_i / (b + s_i)) + Σ (d_i / (b – s_i)). If the stream speed varies linearly with distance, the integral form appears, but aptitude questions discretise it into a few segments. A typical pattern: a boat travels 30 km downstream where the stream is 2 km/h for the first 10 km and 4 km/h for the next 20 km, then returns upstream over the same stretches. Compute downstream time = 10/(b+2) + 20/(b+4); upstream time = 20/(b–4) + 10/(b–2). The average speed for the round trip = total distance (60 km) ÷ total time. When the stream speed is given as a function of time (e.g., s = s₀ + kt), the problem usually asks for the time when the boat reaches a certain point, requiring integration of 1/(b ± s(t)). In multiple‑choice format, the numbers are chosen so that the integral simplifies (often s(t) is piecewise constant). The key is to treat each constant‑stream segment independently and then sum times; never attempt to use a single “average stream speed” unless the stream speed is constant throughout.

  • Applying the harmonic‑mean formula for equal distances when the journey segments have unequal distances.
  • Treating a rest period as a reduction in speed rather than a subtraction from moving time.
  • Using the combined length of coupled trains but retaining the original individual speeds for crossing calculations.
  • Assuming the stream speed is constant across the whole river when the problem explicitly states different speeds for different stretches.
  • Confusing the weighted arithmetic mean (time weights) with the weighted harmonic mean (distance weights) when both distances and times are given in mixed fractions.
  • Neglecting to convert all time units to a common base before summing times in variable‑stream round‑trip problems.
  • Forgetting that the relative speed after coupling becomes zero, so the meeting point is exactly where the fronts meet.

Clock Hand Relative Speed and Coincidence Problems

The motion of the hour, minute and second hands of an analog clock is a classic application of relative speed on a circular track of 360°. The minute hand moves at 6° per minute (360°/60), the hour hand at 0.5° per minute (360°/12/60), and the second hand at 360° per minute. The relative speed of the minute hand with respect to the hour hand is therefore 5.5° per minute. Starting from coincidence at 12:00, the hands coincide again each time the minute hand gains a full 360° on the hour hand, i.e. every 360° ÷ 5.5° min⁻¹ = 720/11 minutes ≈ 65 5/11 minutes. This yields the well‑known series of coincidence times: 1:05 5/11, 2:10 10/11, …, 10:54 6/11, and again at 12:00.

For “opposite” positions (180° apart) the minute hand must gain 180° on the hour hand, giving a period of 180° ÷ 5.5° min⁻¹ = 360/11 minutes ≈ 32 8/11 minutes. Right‑angle occurrences (90° or 270°) happen twice in each 360/11‑minute interval, so there are 22 right‑angle events in 12 hours. To find the exact clock time for a given event between hour H and H+1, set up the equation |30H – 5.5M| = desired angle, where M is minutes past the hour. Solve the two linear equations (one for +angle, one for –angle) and keep the solution with 0 ≤ M < 60.

When the second hand is involved, treat the clock as a three‑body problem. The relative speed of the second hand to the minute hand is 354° per minute, and to the hour hand 359.5° per minute. Coincidence of all three hands occurs only at 12:00 (and 24:00) because the rational ratios of their speeds do not align elsewhere. Problems often ask for the number of times the second hand bisects the angle between the hour and minute hands in a given interval; this reduces to counting solutions of a linear congruence modulo 360.

A useful shortcut: let the total minutes elapsed since 12:00 be t. The hour‑hand angle = 0.5t, minute‑hand angle = 6t (mod 360). The condition for coincidence is 6t ≡ 0.5t (mod 360) → 5.5t ≡ 0 (mod 360) → t = 720k/11, k integer. This modular formulation avoids piecewise casework and works for any number of revolutions.

Ping‑Pong Motion Between Two Moving Objects

Consider two bodies A and B starting simultaneously from opposite ends of a straight track of length D. They move toward each other with constant speeds v₁ and v₂, meet, instantly reverse direction, and continue shuttling between the ends. The motion is periodic and the meetings follow a simple pattern. The first meeting occurs after time t₁ = D/(v₁+v₂) at a distance v₁t₁ from A’s start. After meeting, the bodies together must cover an additional 2D to meet again (each travels to an end and back toward the other). Hence the interval between successive meetings is constant: Δt = 2D/(v₁+v₂). The nth meeting (n ≥ 1) occurs at time tₙ = (2n‑1)D/(v₁+v₂).

To locate the nth meeting point measured from A’s starting end, compute the total distance travelled by A up to tₙ: s₁ = v₁ tₙ. Because A reflects at the ends, its effective position on the track is given by the “folded” distance: let L = 2D. Compute r = s₁ mod L. If r ≤ D, the meeting point is at distance r from A’s start; otherwise it is at distance L‑r from A’s start (i.e., D‑(r‑D) from B’s start). This folding method works for any number of meetings and avoids tracking each leg separately.

If the bodies do not reverse but simply pass through each other (ghost‑passing), the meeting times are identical because the relative motion is unchanged. The only difference is that the bodies continue in their original directions; the folding step is then omitted and the position of A is simply s₁ mod D (with direction indicated by the parity of the quotient). Many exam questions disguise the ping‑pong as “two trains shuttling between stations” or “a ball bouncing between two approaching walls”. Recognising the constant meeting interval Δt = 2D/(v₁+v₂) is the key to solving such problems in seconds.

When speeds change after a certain number of meetings, treat each phase separately: compute the meeting time and position at the phase boundary, then use the new speeds as initial conditions for the next phase. The total distance covered by the pair up to the boundary is (2k‑1)D for the kth meeting, so the time at the boundary is known exactly.

Worked Example: Ping‑Pong Meeting
Two runners start from opposite ends of a 400 m track. Runner A runs at 5 m/s, runner B at 3 m/s. They run toward each other, instantly turn around on meeting, and continue. Find the time and position (measured from A’s start) of their 4th meeting.

Given D = 400 m, v₁ = 5 m/s, v₂ = 3 m/s. Relative speed v₁+v₂ = 8 m/s.

Time of nth meeting: tₙ = (2n‑1)D/(v₁+v₂). For n = 4: t₄ = (7 × 400)/8 = 2800/8 = 350 s.

Distance covered by A: s₁ = v₁ t₄ = 5 × 350 = 1750 m.

Folded track length L = 2D = 800 m. Compute r = 1750 mod 800 = 150 m (since 800×2 = 1600, remainder 150).

Because r = 150 ≤ D = 400, the 4th meeting occurs 150 m from A’s starting end.

Answer: 350 seconds after start, at 150 m from A’s start.

Exam‑Day Strategy
For ping‑pong problems, memorise the two formulas: tₙ = (2n‑1)D/(v₁+v₂) and Δt = 2D/(v₁+v₂). Write them on your rough sheet before the test. When the question asks for the nth meeting, plug n directly; when it asks for the time between the 3rd and 7th meetings, compute (7‑3)×Δt. The folding step (mod 2D) is a one‑liner: position = r if r ≤ D else 2D‑r. Practise this with a few numbers so it becomes automatic.

Train Problems with Intermediate Stoppages and Speed Changes

Standard train‑crossing questions assume constant speed. In advanced variants a train may halt at intermediate stations, change speed after a certain distance, or undergo a combination of both. The total journey time is the sum of moving times plus all stoppage durations. If a train of length L travels a distance D at speed v₁, stops for tₛ seconds, then continues the remaining distance at speed v₂, the overall average speed is total distance ÷ (D/v₁ + tₛ + (total‑D)/v₂). When the problem asks for the time when the train crosses a platform or another train, treat each constant‑speed segment separately: compute the position of the front of the train as a piecewise linear function of time, then solve for the instant when the front (or rear) coincides with the platform edge or the other train’s front.

A common pattern: “Train A leaves station X at 10:00, runs at 60 km/h for 120 km, stops 10 min, then runs at 80 km/h. Train B leaves station Y (300 km from X) at 10:30 at 70 km/h nonstop. When and where do they meet?” Convert all times to a common reference (e.g., minutes after 10:00). Write the distance‑from‑X function for each train. For Train A: from 0 to 120 min (120 km at 60 km/h = 2 h = 120 min) it moves; then a flat segment of 10 min; then slope 80 km/h. For Train B: starts at 30 min, slope 70 km/h from 300 km toward X. Equate the two distance functions and solve for the meeting time. Because the functions are piecewise linear, the solution lies in one of the intervals; check each interval sequentially.

When a train changes speed without stopping, the distance‑time graph has a kink. The meeting point with another train is found by intersecting the two piecewise lines. If the meeting occurs exactly at the kink, both the pre‑kink and post‑kink equations give the same time; verify consistency. For crossing a platform of length P, the condition is front‑position = platform‑start + P (for the rear to clear) or front‑position = platform‑start (for the front to enter). Use the appropriate segment’s equation.

Average speed over the whole route with stoppages is not the arithmetic mean of the segment speeds. It is total distance divided by total elapsed time (including stops). A frequent trap is to compute (v₁+v₂)/2 or to weight by distance instead of time. Remember: average speed = Σdᵢ / (Σdᵢ/vᵢ + Σt_stopᵢ).

Relative Speed with Three Moving Bodies on a Straight Line

Problems involving three bodies A, B, C moving on the same straight line can be reduced to a series of two‑body relative‑speed calculations by choosing a convenient reference frame. The most efficient frame is usually that of the middle body (by position) or the slowest body. Suppose at t=0 the positions are x_A < x_B < x_C with speeds v_A, v_B, v_C (positive to the right). The relative speed of A with respect to B is v_A‑v_B; of C with respect to B is v_C‑v_B. If v_A > v_B, A will catch B from behind; if v_C < v_B, C will be caught by B from behind. The meeting times are t_AB = (x_B‑x_A)/(v_A‑v_B) and t_BC = (x_C‑x_B)/(v_B‑v_C) provided the denominators are positive. The earliest positive meeting determines the first event. After that event, the two bodies involved may coalesce (if they stick) or pass through; the problem statement clarifies. In the “pass‑through” case, continue with the same speeds and update positions at the meeting instant, then recompute pairwise relative speeds for the remaining bodies.

When all three move in the same direction, the only possible meetings are overtaking events. The body with the highest speed will eventually overtake the others. The order of overtaking is determined by the ratios of speed differences to initial gaps. A useful invariant: the centre of mass of the three bodies moves at constant velocity (v_A+v_B+v_C)/3 if no external forces act. In aptitude questions, this invariant rarely appears directly but can be used to check consistency of computed meeting times.

If the bodies move in mixed directions (some left, some right), treat leftward speeds as negative. The relative speed between any pair is the absolute difference of their velocities. The meeting condition for a pair is that the distance between them becomes zero. Because there are three pairs, at most three meeting times exist. The problem often asks for the time when all three are at the same point (triple meeting). This requires solving t such that x_A+v_A t = x_B+v_B t = x_C+v_C t, which is possible only if the three velocities are not all distinct or the initial positions satisfy a linear relation. In most exam questions, a triple meeting is impossible and the answer is “never”.

A practical shortcut: convert the three‑body problem into two independent two‑body problems by fixing one body as the origin. For example, subtract v_B from all velocities and x_B from all positions. Body B becomes stationary at the origin. The problem reduces to finding when A and C (now moving with speeds v_A‑v_B and v_C‑v_B) meet the origin or each other. This eliminates one variable and simplifies algebra.

Optimal Angle for Minimum Time River Crossing

When a boat of still‑water speed b crosses a river of width W with a uniform current of speed s, the time to reach the opposite bank depends on the heading angle θ measured from the perpendicular to the banks. The component of the boat’s velocity across the river is b cos θ, so the crossing time is T = W/(b cos θ). This is minimised when cos θ = 1, i.e. θ = 0°: the boat should point straight across. The downstream drift during this minimum‑time crossing is s T = sW/b. If the problem asks for the heading that lands the boat at a point directly opposite the start (zero drift), the upstream component must cancel the current: b sin θ = s → θ = arcsin(s/b). The crossing time then becomes T = W/(b cos θ) = W/√(b²‑s²), which is longer than the minimum‑time crossing.

In many aptitude questions the current is not uniform but varies across the width (e.g., faster in the middle). The optimal strategy for minimum time is still to keep the heading perpendicular to the banks at every instant, because any upstream component reduces the cross‑river speed without reducing the total downstream displacement (which is irrelevant for minimum time). The total time is the integral of dW/(b cos θ) = W/b if θ≡0. Thus the minimum‑time crossing time is simply width divided by still‑water speed, independent of the current profile. This counter‑intuitive result holds provided the boat can instantaneously adjust heading and the current has no component perpendicular to the banks.

When the boat must reach a specific point on the opposite bank (not necessarily opposite the start), the problem becomes a calculus of variations exercise: choose θ(y) to minimise ∫ dy/(b cos θ) subject to ∫ (b sin θ + s(y)) dy/b cos θ = required downstream offset. In multiple‑choice format, the current profile is piecewise constant, and the optimal path consists of straight segments in each region with constant θ satisfying Snell’s law: (sin θ)/v_cross = constant, where v_cross = b cos θ. This reduces to solving a small system of equations for the angles in each region.

A frequent trap is to apply the uniform‑current formula θ = arcsin(s/b) when the current varies, or to assume the minimum‑time heading is upstream. Remember: minimum time ⇒ head straight across; zero drift ⇒ head upstream at arcsin(s/b); given drift ⇒ solve the two equations for θ and T. Always check whether the question asks for “least time”, “least drift”, or “reach a given point”.

  • Confusing the minimum‑time heading (θ=0) with the zero‑drift heading (θ=arcsin(s/b)).
  • Using arithmetic mean of segment speeds instead of total distance ÷ total time when stoppages are present.
  • Forgetting to fold the distance modulo 2D in ping‑pong problems, leading to positions beyond the track length.
  • Treating a triple meeting of three bodies as always possible; it requires a specific linear relation among initial positions and velocities.
  • Applying the constant‑current drift formula when the current varies across the river width.
  • Neglecting to convert all time units to a common base before summing moving and stoppage times in train problems.
  • Assuming the relative speed after a meeting in a ping‑pong scenario changes; it remains v₁+v₂ for the approach phase and |v₁‑v₂| for the separation phase only if the bodies reverse direction.

Relative Motion on a Moving Platform

When a vehicle such as a train, a ship, or a conveyor belt serves as a moving platform, the motion of an object on that platform must be resolved into two components: the velocity of the platform relative to the ground and the velocity of the object relative to the platform. The ground‑frame velocity of the object is the vector sum of these two velocities. In one‑dimensional problems the sum reduces to algebraic addition when the object moves in the same direction as the platform and to subtraction when it moves opposite. A typical examiner twist is to give the speed of a person walking inside a train and ask for the time taken to cross a stationary pole on the ground. The correct approach is to first compute the person’s ground speed (train speed ± walking speed) and then apply the basic distance‑speed‑time relation using the length of the train as the distance. If the person walks from the rear to the front, the effective speed relative to the ground is train speed + walking speed; if the walk is from front to rear, it is train speed – walking speed. The same principle applies to a boat crossing a river that itself is drifting on a larger current: the boat’s velocity relative to the earth is the vector sum of its velocity relative to the water and the water’s velocity relative to the earth. In problems where the platform changes speed mid‑journey (e.g., a train accelerates after a station), the journey must be split into segments, each with its own constant platform speed, and the object’s ground speed recomputed for each segment. A common pitfall is to average the platform speeds before adding the object’s relative speed; the correct method is to compute the time for each segment separately and then sum the times.

Another layer of complexity appears when the platform itself has length and the object must traverse that length while the platform moves. For instance, a person walking the length of a moving train of length L at speed v_p relative to the train, while the train moves at speed v_t relative to ground. The time to walk from one end to the other as measured by a ground observer is L ÷ v_p (the same as measured on the train) because the person’s motion relative to the train is independent of the train’s motion. However, the distance covered relative to ground during that walk is (v_t ± v_p) × (L ÷ v_p). Examiners often ask for the distance between the person’s starting point on the ground and the point where the person reaches the other end of the train; this requires the ground‑frame distance, not the train‑frame distance. The key is to keep reference frames distinct and to convert only when the question explicitly asks for a ground‑frame quantity.

Meeting Problems with Direction Reversals

In many aptitude questions two bodies move toward each other, meet, and then continue to the ends of a track where they instantly reverse direction and move back. The relative speed during the approach phase is the sum of their speeds (v₁ + v₂). After the first meeting, the bodies separate; the relative speed during separation is the absolute difference of their speeds (|v₁ – v₂|) if they continue in the same direction, but if they reverse, the situation resets to an approach phase again with relative speed v₁ + v₂. The crucial insight is that the pattern of meetings repeats periodically. For a straight track of length D with perfectly elastic reversals at the ends, the positions of the bodies as functions of time can be mapped onto a single straight line by “unfolding” the track: reflect the track each time a body hits an end. In the unfolded line the bodies move with constant velocities v₁ and v₂ without ever reversing, and meetings correspond to the times when their positions coincide. The distance between successive meetings in the unfolded line is 2D, so the time interval between consecutive meetings is 2D ÷ (v₁ + v₂). This holds regardless of whether the speeds are equal or different. If the bodies have different speeds, the meeting points on the original track are not equally spaced; they follow a pattern that can be derived by taking the unfolded meeting positions modulo 2D and then mapping back to the interval [0, D] with a reflection for odd multiples of D. A frequent examiner trap is to ask for the location of the nth meeting. The shortcut: compute the total distance covered by the faster body until the nth meeting in the unfolded line, which is n × 2D × v₁ ÷ (v₁ + v₂). Reduce this distance modulo 2D; if the remainder ≤ D, that is the meeting point measured from the faster body’s starting end; otherwise the meeting point is 2D – remainder measured from the same end. This method avoids simulating each reversal.

Technique: Unfolded Track for Repeated Meetings
Two runners A and B start simultaneously from opposite ends of a 400 m straight track. A runs at 6 m/s, B at 4 m/s. They run to the ends, instantly turn, and continue. Find the distance from A’s starting end where they meet for the 3rd time.
Unfold the track: each meeting in the unfolded line occurs every 2D ÷ (v_A + v_B) = 800 ÷ 10 = 80 s. The 3rd meeting occurs at t = 3 × 80 = 240 s. In the unfolded line, A’s distance from its start = v_A × t = 6 × 240 = 1440 m. Reduce modulo 2D = 800 m: 1440 mod 800 = 640 m. Since 640 m > D (400 m), the actual meeting point on the original track is 2D – 640 = 800 – 640 = 160 m from A’s starting end. Answer: 160 m.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem involves repeated meetings with instantaneous reversals, always unfold the track. Compute the meeting interval as 2D ÷ (v₁ + v₂), find the total time for the required meeting, multiply by the speed of the body whose starting end is used as reference, reduce modulo 2D, and reflect if the remainder exceeds D. This single formula replaces a lengthy step‑by‑step simulation and eliminates the risk of missing a reversal.

Composite River Systems with Confluences

Standard boat‑and‑stream problems assume a single uniform current. In advanced questions a river may split into two branches with different current speeds, or two rivers with distinct currents may join. The boat’s still‑water speed remains constant, but the effective downstream or upstream speed changes at the confluence. The journey must be divided into segments, each with its own current speed. For a downstream segment of length L₁ with current s₁, the time is L₁ ÷ (b + s₁). For an upstream segment of length L₂ with current s₂, the time is L₂ ÷ (b – s₂). If the boat crosses from one branch to another at an angle, the current vectors add vectorially; however, aptitude exams usually restrict motion to along the river axis, so only scalar addition or subtraction is needed. A typical twist: the boat travels downstream on the main river, then turns into a tributary flowing at a different speed, goes upstream a certain distance, and returns. The total time is the sum of the four segment times. The average speed for the whole trip is total distance ÷ total time, not the arithmetic mean of the segment speeds. Another common variant asks for the ratio of the two current speeds given the total round‑trip time and the distances. Set up the equation Σ L_i ÷ (b ± s_i) = T_total and solve for the unknown current(s). Because the equation is rational, cross‑multiplication yields a quadratic in the unknown current; only the positive root less than b (for upstream feasibility) is admissible. Examiners also test the concept of “effective current” when the boat moves across a confluence at an angle: the component of the current along the boat’s heading adds to or subtracts from the boat’s speed. In such cases, resolve the current into parallel and perpendicular components relative to the boat’s path; only the parallel component affects the time along that segment.

Non‑Uniform Acceleration in Time‑Distance Problems

While uniformly accelerated motion (constant acceleration) appears occasionally, some questions feature piecewise constant acceleration or acceleration that changes at known instants. The standard kinematic equations (v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as) apply only within intervals where acceleration is constant. The overall motion is solved by chaining the intervals: the final velocity of one interval becomes the initial velocity of the next. For a journey with three phases — acceleration a₁ for time t₁, constant speed for time t₂, deceleration a₂ for time t₃ — the total distance is the sum of the three phase distances. The constant‑speed phase distance equals the velocity at the end of the acceleration phase multiplied by t₂. The deceleration phase distance uses the same kinematic formulas with a negative acceleration. A frequent examiner trap is to give the total distance and total time and ask for the maximum speed. The maximum speed occurs at the end of the acceleration phase (or start of deceleration). Set up the distance equation in terms of the unknown maximum speed v_max, using t₁ = v_max ÷ a₁ and t₃ = v_max ÷ a₂ (since deceleration brings speed from v_max to zero). The constant‑speed time t₂ = T_total – t₁ – t₃. Substitute into the distance sum and solve for v_max. This reduces to a linear equation in v_max because the distance during acceleration and deceleration are each ½ v_max t₁ and ½ v_max t₃ respectively. The result is v_max = 2 × total distance ÷ (T_total + v_max/a₁ + v_max/a₂) which simplifies to v_max = (2 × total distance) ÷ (T_total + v_max(1/a₁ + 1/a₂)). Solving yields v_max = (2 × total distance) ÷ (T_total + (v_max)(1/a₁ + 1/a₂)). Rearranging gives a quadratic in v_max; the positive root is the answer. Another variant provides a velocity‑time graph consisting of straight‑line segments; the distance is the area under the graph. Compute the area of each trapezoid or triangle and sum. If the graph includes a curved segment (e.g., parabolic), the problem will state the functional form (e.g., v = k√t) and expect integration; however, such calculus‑based questions are rare in aptitude tests and usually the curve is approximated by given data points.

  • Treating a moving platform’s speed as additive for all quantities without distinguishing between ground‑frame and platform‑frame distances.
  • Assuming the relative speed after a reversal remains the difference of speeds; it reverts to the sum for the next approach phase.
  • Using arithmetic mean of segment speeds instead of total distance ÷ total time when currents differ across river branches.
  • Applying a single kinematic equation across an interval where acceleration changes, ignoring the need to chain final‑to‑initial velocities.
  • Forgetting to reduce the unfolded‑track distance modulo 2D before mapping back to the original track, leading to meeting points beyond the track length.
  • Taking the positive root of a quadratic for current speed without checking that it is less than the boat’s still‑water speed (upstream feasibility).
  • Confusing the component of current parallel to the boat’s heading with the total current magnitude when the boat crosses a confluence at an angle.

Pursuit and Interception Problems with Constant Bearing

Pursuit problems appear when one moving object (the pursuer) continuously adjusts its heading to point directly at a target that moves along a known straight line at constant speed. The defining feature is the constant bearing condition: the line of sight from pursuer to target does not rotate. In the ground frame this translates to the relative velocity vector of the pursuer with respect to the target being collinear with the instantaneous line joining them. If the pursuer speed is vₚ and the target speed is vₜ at an angle θ to the initial line of sight, the interception condition is vₚ sin α = vₜ sin θ, where α is the angle between the pursuer’s velocity and the line of sight. The time to intercept is the initial separation divided by the component of relative velocity along the line of sight: t = D ÷ (vₚ cos α − vₜ cos θ). Examiners often give vₚ, vₜ, θ and ask for the minimum vₚ that guarantees interception; the answer is vₚ ≥ vₜ sin θ ÷ sin α with α chosen to maximise the denominator, i.e. α = 90° giving vₚ ≥ vₜ sin θ. A common twist is to provide a piecewise‑constant target speed (e.g., the target accelerates after a certain time). The pursuer’s heading must be recomputed at the instant of speed change; the problem then splits into two pursuit phases with different constant‑bearing angles. The total interception time is the sum of the two phase times, and the pursuer’s path becomes a broken curve. Another variant asks for the distance travelled by the pursuer, which is simply vₚ × total time because the pursuer’s speed is constant. When the pursuer’s speed is also variable, the problem reduces to solving a differential equation; however, aptitude tests restrict to constant speeds and piecewise constant target motion, so the algebraic method above suffices.

Key examiner traps: (1) assuming the pursuer must head directly toward the target’s initial position rather than continuously updating the bearing; (2) using the arithmetic mean of speeds instead of the relative‑velocity component along the line of sight; (3) forgetting that interception is impossible if the pursuer’s speed component perpendicular to the target’s path is less than the target’s perpendicular component. The constant‑bearing condition is equivalent to stating that the relative velocity vector points from pursuer to target at every instant; this geometric insight often yields a quicker solution than setting up parametric equations.

Relative Motion in Two Dimensions Using Vector Components

When two bodies move in a plane with velocities that are not collinear, the relative velocity is the vector difference vᵣ = v₁ − v₂. The magnitude of vᵣ determines the rate at which the distance between them changes, while its direction gives the bearing of one body as seen from the other. To solve meeting or closest‑approach problems, resolve each velocity into orthogonal components (usually east–north or along/perpendicular to the initial line joining them). Let the initial separation vector be s₀. The separation at time t is s(t) = s₀ + vᵣ t. The distance squared is |s(t)|² = (s₀ + vᵣ t)·(s₀ + vᵣ t). This is a quadratic in t; its minimum occurs at tₘᵢₙ = −(s₀·vᵣ) ÷ |vᵣ|², provided tₘᵢₙ ≥ 0. The minimum distance is √(|s₀|² − (s₀·vᵣ)² ÷ |vᵣ|²). If the bodies are to meet, the quadratic must have a real root t ≥ 0, which requires the discriminant to be non‑negative and the root to be positive. Examiners frequently give speeds and directions as bearings (e.g., 030°, 210°) and ask for the time of closest approach or the minimum separation. Converting bearings to Cartesian components (east = speed × sin bearing, north = speed × cos bearing) is the standard first step. A twist is to introduce a third body whose motion influences the relative geometry, such as a moving reference platform; the relative velocity of body 1 with respect to body 2 as observed from the platform is v₁ − v₂ − vₚ, where vₚ is the platform velocity. Another variant asks for the angle between the relative velocity vector and the initial separation vector; this angle determines whether the bodies are approaching (cosine positive) or receding. The dot product s₀·vᵣ gives the signed rate of change of distance at t = 0. If this dot product is zero, the initial distance is momentarily stationary (closest approach at t = 0). Problems may also provide the minimum distance and ask for an unknown speed or direction; then the quadratic discriminant condition yields an equation in the unknown.

Common pitfalls: (1) mixing bearing conventions (clockwise from north vs. anticlockwise from east); (2) treating relative speed as the scalar difference of magnitudes instead of the vector magnitude; (3) ignoring the sign of tₘᵢₙ and reporting a negative time as the meeting instant; (4) forgetting to check that the discriminant is non‑negative before concluding that a meeting occurs. The vector‑component method is systematic and avoids the geometric ambiguities of sine‑law approaches.

Technique: Vector‑Component Interception
A boat A moves at 12 km/h on a bearing of 045°. Boat B moves at 10 km/h on a bearing of 150°. At 08:00 the position of A relative to B is 8 km due east. Find the time of closest approach and the minimum separation.

Step 1: Convert bearings to Cartesian components (east = x, north = y).
A: vₐₓ = 12 sin 45° = 12 × √2/2 ≈ 8.485 km/h; vₐᵧ = 12 cos 45° = 8.485 km/h.
B: v_bₓ = 10 sin 150° = 10 × 0.5 = 5 km/h; v_bᵧ = 10 cos 150° = 10 × (−√3/2) ≈ −8.660 km/h.

Step 2: Relative velocity vᵣ = vₐ − v_b.
vᵣₓ = 8.485 − 5 = 3.485 km/h.
vᵣᵧ = 8.485 − (−8.660) = 17.145 km/h.

Step 3: Initial separation vector s₀ = (8, 0) km (A is 8 km east of B).

Step 4: Compute dot product s₀·vᵣ = 8 × 3.485 + 0 × 17.145 = 27.88 km²/h.
|vᵣ|² = 3.485² + 17.145² ≈ 12.14 + 293.95 = 306.09 (km/h)².

Step 5: Time of closest approach tₘᵢₙ = −(s₀·vᵣ) ÷ |vᵣ|² = −27.88 ÷ 306.09 ≈ −0.091 h. Since tₘᵢₙ < 0, the distance is increasing from the start; the closest approach occurs at t = 0.

Step 6: Minimum separation = |s₀| = 8 km.

Answer: Closest approach at 08:00 (t = 0), minimum separation 8 km.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives bearings, immediately sketch a rough diagram and convert to east/north components. Write the relative velocity vector explicitly; the dot product with the initial separation tells you whether the bodies are initially approaching (negative dot product) or receding (positive). If the dot product is positive, the minimum distance is at t = 0 — no further calculation needed. This quick check saves time on questions that only ask for the minimum separation.

Speed‑Time Graphs with Non‑Linear Segments and Area Approximation

In many aptitude questions the speed‑time graph consists of straight‑line segments (constant acceleration or deceleration) and horizontal segments (constant speed). The distance travelled is the area under the graph. When a segment is described by a non‑linear function — for example v = k√t or v = at² — the exact area requires integration. Examiners rarely expect calculus; instead they provide a table of speed values at equally spaced times and ask for an approximate distance using the trapezoidal rule or Simpson’s 1/3 rule. The trapezoidal rule for n subintervals of width h is Distance ≈ h/2 [v₀ + 2(v₁+…+vₙ₋₁) + vₙ]. Simpson’s rule (requiring an even number of subintervals) is Distance ≈ h/3 [v₀ + 4(v₁+v₃+…) + 2(v₂+v₄+…) + vₙ]. A typical twist: the graph is piecewise — a parabola from t=0 to t=2, then a straight line to t=5. The candidate must compute the exact area under the parabola (∫k√t dt = 2k/3 t^(3/2)) and add the trapezoidal area of the linear part. Another variant gives a speed‑time curve that is symmetric about a vertical axis; the total distance is twice the area of one half. Examiners also test the concept of average speed over the whole journey: v_avg = total distance ÷ total time, where total distance is the summed area. If the graph includes a period of rest (speed = 0), that interval contributes zero area but adds to total time, lowering the average speed. A common error is to average the speeds at the given time points instead of using the area. When the graph is a velocity‑time graph (velocity can be negative), the net displacement is the signed area, while total distance is the sum of absolute areas. Questions may ask for the time when the particle returns to the starting point; this is the instant when the signed area becomes zero.

Key examiner traps: (1) applying the trapezoidal rule to a segment that is known to be a parabola and not using the exact integral when the function is given; (2) forgetting that Simpson’s rule requires an even number of intervals; (3) confusing displacement with distance when the graph dips below the time axis; (4) using the arithmetic mean of the endpoint speeds for a non‑linear segment. The safest approach is to identify each segment’s mathematical form, compute its exact area if possible, and resort to numerical rules only when the function is not integrable in elementary terms or when only tabular data are supplied.

Aircraft Ground Speed and Wind Correction Angle

Aircraft navigation problems are a direct application of relative velocity in two dimensions. The aircraft’s air speed (speed relative to the surrounding air) and heading (direction the nose points) combine with the wind velocity (speed and direction of the air mass relative to the ground) to produce the ground speed and track (actual path over the ground). In vector form: v_ground = v_air + v_wind. The magnitude of v_ground is the ground speed; its direction is the track. The wind correction angle (WCA) is the angle between the heading and the track, i.e., the angle the pilot must turn into the wind to maintain the desired track. Given desired track θ_T, air speed v_A, wind speed v_W and wind direction θ_W (the direction from which the wind blows), the heading θ_H satisfies the sine law in the velocity triangle: v_W ÷ sin(θ_H − θ_T) = v_A ÷ sin(180° − θ_W + θ_T) = v_G ÷ sin(θ_W − θ_H). A simpler component method: resolve wind into components parallel and perpendicular to the desired track. The perpendicular component must be cancelled by the aircraft’s heading component: v_A sin(WCA) = v_W sin(θ_W − θ_T). Hence WCA = arcsin[v_W sin(θ_W − θ_T) ÷ v_A]. The ground speed is then v_G = v_A cos(WCA) + v_W cos(θ_W − θ_T). Examiners often give air speed, wind speed/direction, and desired track, and ask for heading and ground speed. A twist is to provide the heading and ask for the resulting track and ground speed (the direct problem). Another variant: the aircraft must fly a round trip along the same track with a constant wind; the average ground speed for the round trip is not the arithmetic mean of the two ground speeds but the harmonic mean: v_avg = 2 v_G1 v_G2 ÷ (v_G1 + v_G2). This mirrors the boat‑stream round‑trip formula. A further complication: wind speed varies with altitude; the aircraft climbs through a layer where wind changes linearly. The problem then splits into two legs with different wind vectors, and the overall track is the vector sum of the two ground‑velocity vectors weighted by time.

Common pitfalls: (1) using wind direction as the direction the wind blows toward instead of the direction it comes from (meteorological convention); (2) mixing up heading and track in the sine‑law triangle; (3) applying the arithmetic mean for round‑trip average ground speed; (4) neglecting to check that the argument of arcsin lies within [−1, 1] — if it exceeds 1, the desired track cannot be maintained with the given air speed and wind. The component method is less error‑prone because it works directly with the desired track axis.

Problems Involving Time Gaps and Simultaneous Departures

Many meeting‑type questions introduce a time gap: one object starts earlier, or two objects start from different points at different times. The standard technique is to shift the time origin to the later start and compute the effective separation at that instant. Suppose object A starts from point X at t = 0 with speed v_A, and object B starts from point Y at t = Δt with speed v_B moving toward A. At t = Δt, A has already covered v_A Δt, so the remaining separation is D − v_A Δt. From that moment the relative speed is v_A + v_B (if moving toward each other) and the meeting time after B’s start is (D − v_A Δt) ÷ (v_A + v_B). The total time from A’s start is Δt plus this value. If the objects move in the same direction, the relative speed is |v_A − v_B| and the condition for meeting is that the faster object starts behind and the initial separation (adjusted for the time gap) is positive. A more complex variant involves three bodies with staggered starts: A starts at t=0, B at t=Δt₁, C at t=Δt₂. The meeting of any pair is analysed by considering the time interval during which both are in motion. Examiners also ask for the position of meeting measured from a fixed point; this is simply the distance travelled by the earlier starter up to the meeting instant. Another twist: the later starter moves with a speed that changes after a certain time (e.g., accelerates). The problem then splits into phases, each with its own relative speed, and the meeting may occur in any phase. The candidate must test each phase sequentially: compute the time needed to close the gap at the current relative speed; if that time exceeds the phase duration, subtract the distance closed in that phase from the gap and proceed to the next phase. A related concept is the “ghost” method: imagine the later starter had started at t=0 with the same speed; its position at any time would be ahead of its actual position by v_B Δt. The meeting condition then becomes the meeting of two objects that started simultaneously but with an initial offset. This mental model simplifies the algebra.

Key examiner traps: (1) forgetting to reduce the initial separation by the distance covered during the time gap; (2) using the wrong relative speed sign for same‑direction motion; (3) assuming the meeting always occurs after the later start without checking whether the earlier object already passed the meeting point before the later one started; (4) mixing up the order of subtraction when the faster object starts later. The systematic “shift‑origin” approach eliminates these errors.

  • Assuming the pursuer can intercept a target moving perpendicular to the initial line of sight without checking the perpendicular‑component condition.
  • Treating relative velocity in two dimensions as a scalar difference of speeds instead of a vector difference.
  • Applying the trapezoidal rule to a known parabolic segment when the exact integral is elementary.
  • Using wind direction as the direction the wind blows toward, reversing the wind‑correction angle sign.
  • Calculating round‑trip average ground speed as the arithmetic mean of the two leg ground speeds.
  • Neglecting to reduce the initial separation by the distance covered during a time gap before applying relative speed.
  • Forgetting that the argument of arcsin in wind‑correction must lie within [−1, 1]; otherwise the desired track is impossible.
