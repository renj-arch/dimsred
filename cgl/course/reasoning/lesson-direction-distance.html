What is Direction & Distance?

Direction and distance questions test your spatial awareness â€” you must track movements on a plane using cardinal directions (North, South, East, West) and calculate final position relative to the starting point. These questions are a regular feature in the reasoning section.

Key Concepts

1. Cardinal Directions: There are four main directions (North, South, East, West) and four intermediate directions (North-East, North-West, South-East, South-West). The angle between two adjacent main directions is 90Â°, and between a main and an intermediate direction is 45Â°.

2. Path Plotting: Draw a rough sketch showing each movement as an arrow. Mark distances. The final displacement is the straight-line distance from start to end, calculated using the Pythagoras theorem: dÂ² = (total East-West)Â² + (total North-South)Â².

3. Shadow Problems: In the morning (sunrise), the sun is in the East, so shadows fall towards the West. In the evening (sunset), the sun is in the West, so shadows fall towards the East. At noon, the sun is directly overhead, so there is no shadow.

4. Direction Changes: A right turn = +90Â° clockwise. A left turn = +90Â° counter-clockwise (anti-clockwise).

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Basic Path)
Rohan walks 10 km North, turns right and walks 5 km, then turns right again and walks 10 km. How far and in which direction is he from his starting point?

a) 5 km East b) 5 km West c) 10 km North d) 10 km West
Solution: a) 5 km East. Start at O. Walk 10 km North to A. Turn right (East), walk 5 km to B. Turn right (South), walk 10 km to C. Net displacement: East = 5 km, North = 10-10 = 0. So C is 5 km East of O.
Example 2 (Displacement)
A man walks 20 m East, then turns left and walks 15 m, then turns right and walks 25 m, then turns right and walks 15 m. How far is he from his starting point?

a) 40 m b) 45 m c) 50 m d) 55 m
Solution: b) 45 m. East: 20 + 25 = 45 m (first East 20, then after various turns another East 25). North-South: 15 North - 15 South = 0. Net displacement = 45 m East.
Example 3 (Shadow Problem)
One morning, Ravi and Raju are standing facing each other. Ravi's shadow falls to his left. Which direction is Raju facing?

a) North b) South c) East d) West
Solution: a) North. In the morning, sun is in the East, so shadows fall towards the West. If Ravi's shadow is to his left, then his left side is West. So Ravi is facing South (because when facing South, left is East...

Vector Decomposition and Net Displacement in Multi‑Leg Journeys

When a path consists of several straight segments, the most reliable way to obtain the final displacement is to resolve each leg into orthogonal components. Choose a Cartesian frame: let the positive x‑axis point East and the positive y‑axis point North. A movement of d metres at an angle θ measured clockwise from North contributes d·sin θ to the x component and d·cos θ to the y component. Summing all x components yields the net East‑West offset; summing all y components yields the net North‑South offset. The magnitude of the resultant displacement is √(Σx² + Σy²) and its bearing is given by arctan(Σx / Σy) with the appropriate quadrant correction. This method eliminates the need to redraw the whole route and works even when the number of legs exceeds ten.

Consider a candidate who walks 30 m at 030°, then 40 m at 120°, then 25 m at 210°. The component table is:

Σx = 15 + 34.64 ‑ 12.5 = 37.14 m (East). Σy = 25.98 ‑ 20 ‑ 21.65 = ‑15.67 m (South). Resultant distance = √(37.14² + 15.67²) ≈ 40.3 m. Bearing = arctan(37.14 / ‑15.67) ≈ 112° (measured clockwise from North), i.e. South‑East quadrant. The vector approach also makes it trivial to answer “how far East of the start?” – simply read Σx.

Examiners often hide the bearing in words such as “turns right by 45°” or “changes course to the left by 30°”. Convert each instruction into an absolute bearing before tabulating. A right turn adds the angle to the current bearing; a left turn subtracts it. Keep bearings in the 0°–360° range by adding or subtracting 360° when necessary. This systematic conversion prevents the common error of mixing relative and absolute angles.

Bearing Calculations and Clockwise Angle Conventions

Bearings are expressed as three‑digit angles measured clockwise from true North (000°). The four cardinal points correspond to 000° (North), 090° (East), 180° (South) and 270° (West). Intermediate directions follow the same rule: North‑East is 045°, South‑West is 225°, etc. When a problem states “turns right by 90°”, the new bearing = (old bearing + 90°) mod 360°. A left turn subtracts 90°. If the instruction uses “turns right by 45°” repeatedly, the bearing sequence is an arithmetic progression modulo 360°.

A frequent twist is the use of “bearing of B from A” versus “bearing of A from B”. The former is the direction you must travel from A to reach B; the latter is the reciprocal bearing, obtained by adding or subtracting 180° (mod 360°). For example, if the bearing of a lighthouse from a ship is 060°, the bearing of the ship from the lighthouse is 240°. Candidates who forget the reciprocal step lose marks on otherwise straightforward questions.

Another layer appears when the problem mixes bearings with distances and asks for the shortest distance between two moving objects. Treat each object’s trajectory as a parametric line: position = initial + velocity × time, where velocity components are derived from the bearing and speed. The distance between them at time t is the magnitude of the difference vector. Minimising this distance with respect to t (by setting the derivative to zero) yields the closest approach. In exam conditions, the quadratic that results often factorises neatly, giving an integer time value.

Tabular representation of bearing changes helps avoid sign errors. A compact table with columns “Step”, “Initial Bearing”, “Turn”, “Resultant Bearing” can be drawn in the rough sheet. For a sequence: start 030°, right 45°, left 120°, right 90°, the table reads:

Worked Example – Bearing and Minimum Separation
Two cyclists start from the same point. Cyclist A rides at 12 km/h on a bearing of 040°. Cyclist B rides at 15 km/h on a bearing of 130°. After how many hours will they be closest to each other, and what is that minimum distance?
Resolve velocities into components (East = +x, North = +y).
v_Ax = 12·sin 40° ≈ 7.71 km/h, v_Ay = 12·cos 40° ≈ 9.19 km/h.
v_Bx = 15·sin 130° ≈ 11.49 km/h, v_By = 15·cos 130° ≈ –9.64 km/h.
Relative velocity components: Δvx = v_Bx – v_Ax ≈ 3.78 km/h, Δvy = v_By – v_Ay ≈ –18.83 km/h.
Initial separation = 0. Distance at time t: D(t) = √[(Δvx·t)² + (Δvy·t)²] = t·√(Δvx² + Δvy²). Since the initial separation is zero, the distance increases monotonically; the minimum occurs at t = 0. However, if the problem had a non‑zero start offset, set derivative dD/dt = 0 → t = –(Δx·Δvx + Δy·Δvy) / (Δvx² + Δvy²). In this specific wording the answer is 0 h and 0 km.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Bearing Problems
Draw a tiny compass rose (N‑E‑S‑W) on the corner of your rough sheet. Write the three‑digit bearing next to each leg as you read the question. Convert every “right/left” instruction into an absolute bearing immediately; do not keep a running mental picture. When a question asks for the bearing of X from Y, remember to add 180° (mod 360°) to the bearing of Y from X. For minimum‑distance questions, write the relative velocity vector first; the time of closest approach is a simple dot‑product formula that fits on one line.

Shadow Geometry with Variable Sun Elevation

Shadow problems normally assume the Sun is either due East (morning) or due West (evening). Advanced versions introduce the Sun’s altitude angle, which changes the shadow length but not its direction on a flat horizontal plane. The direction of a shadow is always opposite to the Sun’s azimuth. At solar noon in the northern hemisphere the Sun is due South, so shadows point North. At any other time, the azimuth can be approximated by the hour angle: each hour before/after noon shifts the azimuth by 15°. For a morning problem at 09:00 local solar time, the Sun’s azimuth is 090° – 45° = 045° (North‑East), so shadows fall towards 225° (South‑West). If a person faces a certain direction, their left/right is defined relative to their facing, not the observer’s.

Consider a question: “At 10:00 am, a pole casts a shadow that points exactly to the left of a man standing facing the pole. Which direction is the man facing?” At 10:00 am the Sun’s azimuth ≈ 060° (East‑North‑East). Shadow direction = 240° (West‑South‑West). The man’s left side points to 240°. When a person faces bearing β, their left points to (β – 90°) mod 360°. Set (β – 90°) ≡ 240° → β ≡ 330° (North‑North‑West). Hence the man faces 330°.

Examiners may combine shadow direction with a subsequent movement: “The man then walks 20 m straight ahead, turns right and walks 15 m. Find his distance from the pole.” After determining the initial facing (330°), the first walk is along 330°, the right turn leads to 060°, and the second walk is along 060°. Resolve both legs into components and compute the resultant displacement from the pole. This tests both shadow reasoning and vector addition in a single item.

A subtle trap is the assumption that “morning” always means the Sun is exactly East. In competitive exams, “morning” is a qualitative cue; the precise azimuth must be inferred from the given clock time or from the statement “the Sun rises in the East”. If no time is given, adopt the standard convention: morning → Sun in East (azimuth 090°), evening → Sun in West (azimuth 270°). Never invent an intermediate azimuth unless the problem supplies a specific hour.

Relative Motion and Meeting Point Determination

When two persons start from different points and move along straight lines, the meeting point (if any) is found by equating their parametric position vectors. Let person P start at (x₁, y₁) with velocity components (v₁x, v₁y) and person Q start at (x₂, y₂) with (v₂x, v₂y). Their positions at time t are (x₁ + v₁x t, y₁ + v₁y t) and (x₂ + v₂x t, y₂ + v₂y t). Setting the coordinates equal gives two linear equations in t. A consistent solution yields the meeting time; substituting back gives the meeting coordinates. If the equations are inconsistent (different t values), the paths cross but the persons are not there simultaneously – a classic “crossing paths” distractor.

Example: A starts at (0, 0) moving North at 5 km/h. B starts at (10, 0) moving North‑West at 5√2 km/h (bearing 315°). Velocity components: A (0, 5). B (‑5, 5). Equate: 0 = 10 ‑ 5t → t = 2 h. y: 5t = 5t → satisfied. Meeting point = (0, 10). Distance each travelled: A = 10 km, B = 10 km. The symmetry often appears in exam options.

If speeds differ, the meeting time may be fractional. Always keep the time in hours (or minutes) consistent with the speed units. A common mistake is to mix km/h with m/s without conversion. Convert all speeds to the same unit before forming equations.

Another variation: one person moves, the other remains stationary but rotates a searchlight. The “meeting” becomes the instant the beam aligns with the moving person. Treat the beam direction as a rotating vector with angular speed ω. The condition is that the bearing from the stationary point to the moving person equals the beam bearing at that instant. This reduces to solving a trigonometric equation, which in exam questions simplifies to a standard angle (30°, 45°, 60°).

Three‑Dimensional Path Projection on a Plane

Some advanced items describe a person climbing a hill or moving in a building with floors, then ask for the horizontal distance between start and end points. The vertical component is irrelevant for the planar displacement. Extract only the horizontal projections of each segment. For a segment described as “walks 10 m up a slope inclined at 30° to the horizontal”, the horizontal advance = 10·cos 30° ≈ 8.66 m. The vertical gain = 10·sin 30° = 5 m, which is discarded for the final planar answer.

When a path includes a staircase of known rise and run, treat each step as a right triangle. If a staircase has 20 steps each 0.25 m rise and 0.30 m run, total horizontal = 20 × 0.30 = 6 m, total vertical = 5 m. Combine the horizontal components with other planar movements using the vector method from Section 1.

Examiners may phrase the question as “A person walks 12 m North, then climbs a ladder 5 m high leaning against a wall at 60° to the ground, then walks 8 m East. Find the straight‑line distance from the start.” The ladder’s horizontal projection = 5·cos 60° = 2.5 m towards the wall (assume wall faces East). If the wall is East, the ladder adds 2.5 m East. Net East = 2.5 + 8 = 10.5 m. Net North = 12 m. Distance = √(10.5² + 12²) ≈ 15.9 m. The vertical 5·sin 60° ≈ 4.33 m is ignored.

Be alert for “height of a tower” or “depth of a well” data that are extraneous. The moment the question asks for “distance on the ground” or “horizontal separation”, discard all vertical numbers. This filtering skill saves time and avoids the trap of inserting a vertical component into the Pythagorean sum.

  • Assuming the Sun is exactly East in every “morning” statement without checking for a given clock time.
  • Forgetting to add 180° when converting “bearing of A from B” to “bearing of B from A”.
  • Mixing relative turns (left/right) with absolute bearings without updating the current bearing after each turn.
  • Using the full slant length of an inclined segment instead of its horizontal projection for planar displacement.
  • Equating meeting times from x‑ and y‑coordinates separately and ignoring inconsistency, leading to a false “meeting point”.
  • Neglecting to convert all speeds to a common unit before forming relative‑motion equations.
  • Treating the shadow direction as the Sun’s azimuth rather than the opposite azimuth.

Rotating Reference Frames and Apparent Direction Changes

When a navigator or a moving platform continuously changes its heading, the direction of a fixed external object appears to rotate relative to the platform. This phenomenon is modelled by attaching a rotating reference frame to the moving body and expressing the bearing of the target in that frame. If the platform turns at a constant angular speed ω (degrees per unit time) and the target lies at a fixed absolute bearing β, the apparent bearing θ(t) measured from the platform’s forward axis evolves as θ(t) = β − (heading₀ + ωt) (mod 360°). The rate of change of the apparent bearing is simply −ω, independent of the target’s distance. In examination problems this principle is used to determine the instant when a target crosses a given relative bearing, for example “the lighthouse is observed at 045° relative to the ship’s bow; after how many minutes will it be dead ahead?” The solution requires converting the ship’s turn rate to consistent units, setting θ(t) = 0°, and solving for t. A common variation introduces a second moving object; the relative bearing between the two moving bodies then follows the difference of their heading functions. The key step is to write both headings as functions of time, subtract, and reduce modulo 360°. Because only the difference of headings matters, the absolute orientation of the reference frame cancels out, simplifying the algebra. Candidates must remember to normalise angles to the 0°–360° range after each arithmetic operation; failure to do so produces spurious negative bearings that are not present in the answer options.

Another layer appears when the platform’s turn rate is not constant but piecewise constant (e.g., a vessel executes a 90° turn in 5 minutes, then proceeds straight). The apparent bearing function becomes piecewise linear, and the crossing time may lie in any segment. The standard technique is to compute the apparent bearing at each segment boundary, check whether the target bearing lies between the boundary values, and if so solve the linear equation within that segment. This mirrors the “meeting point” logic used in relative‑motion problems but applied to angular quantities. Examiners often disguise the piecewise nature by describing the manoeuvre in words (“turns right at 30° per minute for 3 minutes, then resumes course”). Translating the narrative into a heading‑versus‑time table is the most reliable way to avoid missing a segment.

Composite Path Optimisation Using Reflection Principle

The reflection principle transforms a problem of finding the shortest path that touches a line (or a set of lines) into a straight‑line distance problem in a mirrored coordinate system. Suppose a person must start at point A, touch a straight road represented by line L, and then reach point B. The shortest such broken line A‑P‑B (with P on L) is obtained by reflecting B across L to B′ and drawing the straight segment A‑B′. The intersection of A‑B′ with L gives the optimal touch point P, and the minimal total distance equals |A‑B′|. This works because the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, a direct consequence of Fermat’s principle for light. In competitive‑exam questions the “road” may be a river bank, a wall, or a boundary that the path must cross exactly once. When multiple lines must be touched in a prescribed order, successive reflections are applied: reflect the final destination across the last line, then reflect that image across the previous line, and so on. The straight line from the start to the final image crosses each line at the required points in the correct sequence. The total minimal distance is the length of that straight line. A frequent twist is to ask for the coordinates of the touch points rather than the distance; these are obtained by intersecting the straight line with each line in the original (unreflected) plane. Candidates should practice reflecting points across vertical, horizontal, and oblique lines (e.g., y = mx + c) using the formula for mirror image: if line is ax + by + c = 0, the image of (x₀, y₀) is (x₀ − 2a(ax₀+by₀+c)/(a²+b²), y₀ − 2b(ax₀+by₀+c)/(a²+b²)). Mastery of this algebraic reflection eliminates the need for iterative trial‑and‑error and guarantees an exact answer.

Reflection Principle Application
A person stands at point A(2, 3) and must reach point B(10, 7) after touching the line y = 2x + 1 exactly once. Find the minimum total distance travelled and the coordinates of the touching point.
Reflect B across the line y = 2x + 1. Write line in standard form: 2x − y + 1 = 0 (a = 2, b = −1, c = 1). For B(10, 7), compute d = a·x₀ + b·y₀ + c = 2·10 + (−1)·7 + 1 = 20 − 7 + 1 = 14. Denominator a²+b² = 4+1 = 5. Image B′ = (x₀ − 2a·d/5, y₀ − 2b·d/5) = (10 − 2·2·14/5, 7 − 2·(−1)·14/5) = (10 − 56/5, 7 + 28/5) = ( (50−56)/5 , (35+28)/5 ) = (−6/5 , 63/5 ) = (−1.2 , 12.6). Distance A‑B′ = √[(2 − (−1.2))² + (3 − 12.6)²] = √[(3.2)² + (−9.6)²] = √[10.24 + 92.16] = √102.4 ≈ 10.12 units. The straight line A‑B′ has equation passing through (2,3) and (−1.2,12.6). Slope m = (12.6−3)/(−1.2−2) = 9.6/(−3.2) = −3. Equation: y − 3 = −3(x − 2) → y = −3x + 9. Intersect with y = 2x + 1: −3x + 9 = 2x + 1 → 5x = 8 → x = 1.6, y = 2·1.6 + 1 = 4.2. Touch point P = (1.6, 4.2). Minimum distance = 10.12 units (rounded to two decimals).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for a shortest path that must touch a line, immediately sketch the reflection. Write the line in ax+by+c=0 form, compute the mirror image of the destination, and measure the straight‑line distance. This converts a two‑segment optimisation into a single distance calculation, saving 30–40 seconds per question.

Time‑Dependent Speed Profiles and Piecewise Displacement

Many direction‑distance items describe a traveller whose speed varies with time, for example “walks at 4 km/h for the first 30 minutes, then at 6 km/h for the next 20 minutes, then rests for 10 minutes”. The displacement vector for each interval is the product of the constant velocity vector (speed × direction unit vector) and the duration of that interval. Because the direction may also change at interval boundaries, the overall trajectory is a polyline whose vertices are obtained by cumulatively adding the interval displacement vectors. The standard solution procedure: (1) convert all times to a common unit (hours or minutes); (2) for each interval, resolve the speed into north‑south and east‑west components using the bearing given for that interval; (3) multiply each component by the interval duration to get the component displacements; (4) sum all north components and all east components separately to obtain the net displacement vector; (5) compute the magnitude and bearing of the resultant. A frequent examiner trap is to give speeds in km/h and times in minutes without explicit conversion; the candidate must multiply speed (km/h) by time (h) = time(min)/60. Another trap is to include a “rest” interval with zero speed; the displacement for that interval is zero, but the time still counts for any subsequent average‑speed calculation. When the question asks for average speed over the whole journey, use total path length (sum of absolute distances of each interval) divided by total elapsed time (including rests). When it asks for average velocity, use net displacement magnitude divided by total elapsed time. The distinction between scalar average speed and vector average velocity is a classic multiple‑choice discriminator.

Piecewise constant acceleration appears in more advanced variants: “starts from rest, accelerates uniformly at 2 m/s² for 5 s, then moves at constant speed”. Here the displacement during the acceleration phase is ½ a t² in the direction of motion, and the final speed v = a t becomes the constant speed for the next phase. The same vector addition logic applies, but the candidate must first compute the distance covered during acceleration using the kinematic formula, then treat that distance as a straight segment with the appropriate direction. Because the acceleration is uniform and the direction does not change during that phase, the path remains a straight line. If the direction changes simultaneously with acceleration, the problem splits into two independent vector calculations: one for the accelerated segment (using the average velocity (0+v)/2 = v/2 over the acceleration time) and one for the constant‑velocity segment. Keeping a clear table with columns for interval, duration, speed profile, direction, north component, east component prevents mixing up the phases.

Geometric Locus Constraints in Direction Problems

Some questions do not give a complete path but instead state that a moving point satisfies a geometric condition at all times, for example “a person walks so that his distance from a fixed pole is always twice his distance from a straight road”. The set of all positions satisfying such a condition is a locus (a circle, line, parabola, ellipse, or hyperbola). Recognising the locus allows the candidate to replace a potentially infinite family of paths with a single curve equation, from which the required distance or bearing can be derived analytically. The most common locus in direction‑distance problems is the Apollonius circle: the set of points P such that PA / PB = k (k > 0, k ≠ 1) is a circle whose centre lies on line AB. If k = 1 the locus is the perpendicular bisector of AB (a straight line). Another frequent locus is the parabola defined by equal distance to a point (focus) and a line (directrix); this appears when a problem says “the person remains equidistant from a tree and a fence”. The standard form of a parabola with focus (h, k) and directrix y = c is (x−h)² = 4p(y−k) where p = (k−c)/2. For an ellipse or hyperbola the condition involves sum or difference of distances to two fixed points being constant. In exam questions the constant is usually chosen so that the resulting conic has integer or simple radical parameters, making the algebra tractable. The candidate should be comfortable converting the verbal condition into an algebraic equation, completing the square to identify the centre, axes, and orientation, and then intersecting the locus with a given line or circle to find the possible positions. A typical final step is to compute the bearing from the start point to the intersection point(s) using the arctangent of the coordinate differences, taking care to place the angle in the correct quadrant.

Error Propagation in Multi‑Stage Navigation

Real‑world navigation data often contain rounding or measurement errors, and competitive exams test the ability to estimate how these errors affect the final reported position. Suppose each leg of a journey is recorded with a bearing rounded to the nearest degree and a distance rounded to the nearest 0.1 km. The true bearing of a leg lies within ±0.5° of the recorded value, and the true distance lies within ±0.05 km. For a single leg the positional uncertainty region is a sector of an annulus. When several legs are concatenated, the uncertainty regions combine via vector addition of the error vectors. A conservative bound on the final position error is obtained by assuming the worst‑case alignment of all error vectors: sum the maximum radial errors (distance uncertainties) and the maximum transverse errors (distance × sin(0.5°) ≈ distance × 0.00873). The total radial error ≤ Σ Δd_i, and the total transverse error ≤ Σ (d_i × Δθ_i) where Δθ_i is in radians. The overall positional error radius ≤ √( (Σ Δd_i)² + (Σ d_i Δθ_i)² ). This bound is often used in multiple‑choice questions that ask “the final position could be off by at most …”. A tighter estimate uses root‑sum‑square (RSS) assuming independent random errors: radial RSS = √(Σ Δd_i²), transverse RSS = √(Σ (d_i Δθ_i)²). The exam may ask which method gives the “maximum possible error” (worst‑case) versus the “probable error” (RSS). Candidates must read the wording carefully: “maximum possible deviation” → worst‑case sum; “likely error” or “standard error” → RSS. Another nuance: if bearings are given as “N 30° E” the rounding interval is still ±0.5°, but the conversion to azimuth must be done before applying the error. Also, when a leg distance is given as “approximately 5 km”, the implied Δd may be larger (e.g., ±0.5 km). Always extract the rounding precision from the phrasing (“to the nearest 0.1 km”, “about 5 km”, “exactly 5 km”). The final answer should be expressed with the same precision as the least precise input, typically rounded up to the next significant figure.

  • Assuming the reflection principle works for curved boundaries without verifying the angle‑of‑incidence condition.
  • Forgetting to convert mixed time units (minutes vs hours) before multiplying by speed, leading to displacement errors of factor 60.
  • Treating average speed and average velocity as interchangeable when the path is not a straight line.
  • Misidentifying the locus type (circle vs parabola) from the verbal distance ratio, producing an incorrect equation.
  • Applying root‑sum‑square error combination when the question explicitly asks for the maximum possible deviation.
  • Neglecting to normalise bearings to 0°–360° after each turn, causing sign errors in component calculations.
  • Using the full slant length of an inclined segment instead of its horizontal projection when the question asks for ground distance.

Coordinate Transformation Between True, Magnetic, and Grid North

Navigation problems frequently give a bearing referenced to one north convention and require the answer in another. True north points to the geographic pole, magnetic north follows the Earth’s magnetic field, and grid north aligns with the vertical grid lines of a projected map (e.g., UTM). The angular difference between true and magnetic north is the magnetic declination (or variation), which changes with location and epoch; the difference between true and grid north is the grid convergence, a function of the projection and the point’s easting. To convert a bearing Btrue to magnetic, subtract the declination if it is east (magnetic north east of true) and add if west: Bmag = Btrue − Deast + Dwest. Converting to grid north uses convergence C: Bgrid = Btrue − C (convergence is positive when grid north lies east of true north). When a problem supplies a magnetic bearing and asks for the grid bearing, combine the two steps: Bgrid = Bmag + D − C. All angles must be normalised to the 0°–360° range after each operation. A common twist is to give declination as “3° E” and convergence as “2° W”; the signs must be applied consistently. Candidates should tabulate the three norths, the two correction angles, and the required conversion direction before computing components, because a sign error of 1° can shift the final position by several hundred metres over a 10 km leg.

Shadow Length Variation with Latitude, Declination, and Time of Day

The length of a vertical stick’s shadow on a horizontal plane is a classic proxy for solar elevation. At a given latitude φ, solar declination δ (positive north of the celestial equator), and hour angle H (0° at local solar noon, 15° per hour), the solar altitude α satisfies sin α = sin φ sin δ + cos φ cos δ cos H. The shadow length L of a stick of height h is L = h cot α = h cos α / sin α. Because cot α = √(1−sin²α) / sin α, the expression can be written entirely in terms of φ, δ, H, and h without evaluating α explicitly. In exam questions the date fixes δ (e.g., δ ≈ +23.44° on 21 June, −23.44° on 21 December, 0° on equinoxes). The hour angle is derived from the stated local time after correcting for the equation of time and longitude offset from the standard meridian; however, most competitive‑exam problems ignore the equation of time and assume the clock reads local solar time. A typical twist: “At 10 am on 21 March at latitude 30° N, a 2 m pole casts a shadow of length ___.” Here δ = 0°, H = −30° (two hours before noon), sin α = cos 30° cos 30° = 0.75, α ≈ 48.6°, L = 2 cot 48.6° ≈ 1.78 m. Candidates must remember that cot α = tan(90°−α) and that the shadow points due north when the sun is south of the zenith (common in the northern hemisphere). When the sun is north of the zenith (possible in the tropics), the shadow points south; the bearing of the shadow tip is then 180° opposite the solar azimuth. The solar azimuth A (measured clockwise from true north) follows tan A = sin H / (cos H sin φ − tan δ cos φ). Knowing both L and A lets the candidate place the tip coordinates relative to the pole base, which is often the final step in a multi‑leg direction problem.

Worked Example: Shadow Tip Coordinates
A 1.5 m vertical pole stands at the origin of a Cartesian grid (x east, y north). On 21 June at 09:00 local solar time, latitude 40° N. Find the coordinates of the shadow tip.
On 21 June δ = +23.44°. Hour angle H = −45° (three hours before noon). Compute sin α = sin 40° sin 23.44° + cos 40° cos 23.44° cos (−45°) ≈ 0.6428·0.3978 + 0.7660·0.9175·0.7071 ≈ 0.2556 + 0.5000 = 0.7556. α = arcsin 0.7556 ≈ 49.1°. Shadow length L = 1.5 cot 49.1° = 1.5·(cos 49.1°/sin 49.1°) ≈ 1.5·0.655/0.756 ≈ 1.30 m. Solar azimuth: tan A = sin (−45°) / (cos (−45°) sin 40° − tan 23.44° cos 40°) = −0.7071 / (0.7071·0.6428 − 0.4335·0.7660) = −0.7071 / (0.4545 − 0.3321) = −0.7071 / 0.1224 ≈ −5.78. A = arctan(−5.78) ≈ −80.2°; add 360° → 279.8° (measured clockwise from north). The shadow points opposite the sun, so shadow bearing = (279.8° + 180°) mod 360° = 99.8° (almost due east, slightly south). Components: Δx = L sin 99.8° ≈ 1.30·0.985 ≈ 1.28 m (east), Δy = L cos 99.8° ≈ 1.30·(−0.174) ≈ −0.23 m (south). Tip coordinates ≈ (1.28, −0.23).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a shadow problem appears, first list the three knowns: latitude, date (→ declination), and clock time (→ hour angle). Write the altitude formula in sine form; it avoids an explicit arcsin until the final step. Keep all angles in degrees, but convert to radians only if your calculator demands it. Normalise the final azimuth to 0°–360° before deciding the shadow bearing (add 180°). A quick sanity check: in the northern hemisphere before noon the shadow points west of north; after noon it points east of north. If your bearing contradicts this, re‑check the sign of the hour angle.

Minimum Distance from a Point to a Line Segment in Grid Navigation

Many direction‑distance questions ask for the shortest distance between a moving object’s path (a line segment between two waypoints) and a fixed landmark. The segment endpoints are A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂); the landmark is P(x₀, y₀). The perpendicular foot from P to the infinite line AB has parameter t = ((x₀−x₁)(x₂−x₁) + (y₀−y₁)(y₂−y₁)) / ((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²). If 0 ≤ t ≤ 1 the foot lies on the segment and the minimum distance is the perpendicular distance |(x₂−x₁)(y₁−y₀) − (x₁−x₀)(y₂−y₁)| / √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²). If t < 0 the closest point is A; distance = √((x₀−x₁)² + (y₀−y₁)²). If t > 1 the closest point is B; distance = √((x₀−x₂)² + (y₀−y₂)²). Examiners often hide the segment nature by describing a “road from village X to village Y” and asking “how close does the traveller come to the temple?” The candidate must compute t, not simply use the point‑to‑line formula. A frequent twist adds a speed profile: the traveller moves at 5 km/h for the first half of the segment and 8 km/h for the second half; the question asks for the time when the distance to the temple is minimal. Because the position along the segment is a piecewise‑linear function of time, the minimum may occur at the speed‑change point or at the perpendicular foot if that foot lies within the corresponding time interval. Solve by expressing the traveller’s coordinates as functions of time, then minimise the squared distance (a quadratic) on each interval, checking endpoints.

Relative Motion in River Current and Wind Drift Problems

When a boat or aircraft moves in a medium that itself flows, the ground‑track velocity is the vector sum of the craft’s velocity relative to the medium and the medium’s velocity relative to the ground. Let the craft’s speed in still water be v, its heading (direction it points) be θ measured from true north, and the current velocity be vector C = (Cₓ, Cᵧ). The ground velocity components are vₓ = v sin θ + Cₓ, vᵧ = v cos θ + Cᵧ. The ground speed is √(vₓ² + vᵧ²) and the track bearing is atan2(vₓ, vᵧ) normalised to 0°–360°. A classic problem: “A boat can do 6 km/h in still water. The river flows east at 2 km/h. What heading must the captain steer to reach a point directly north of the start?” Here the required ground track is due north, so vₓ = 0 → 6 sin θ + 2 = 0 → sin θ = −1/3 → θ ≈ −19.5° (i.e., 19.5° west of north). The resulting ground speed is vᵧ = 6 cos θ ≈ 5.68 km/h. If the question instead asks for the minimum time to cross a river of width W, the optimal heading maximises the northward component vᵧ subject to the constraint that the resultant x‑displacement is zero (or a prescribed landing offset). This is a constrained optimisation solvable by setting derivative of crossing time T = W / vᵧ to zero, yielding sin θ = −Cₓ / v when the target is straight across. Wind drift for aircraft follows the same vector addition; the only difference is that wind velocity is usually given as “from 270° at 30 kt”, meaning it blows toward 090°. Convert “from” bearings by adding 180° before using them as vector components. Candidates must also distinguish between true airspeed (relative to air) and ground speed (relative to earth); the former is the magnitude of the craft’s velocity vector in the moving medium.

Optimization of Multi‑Point Visits Using Nearest‑Neighbour Heuristic and Lower Bound

Some advanced direction‑distance sets present a travelling‑salesman‑type scenario: a delivery person must start at depot O, visit a set of n locations {P₁,…,Pₙ} exactly once, and return to O, minimising total distance. Exact solution is NP‑hard, but competitive exams only require the candidate to compute a reasonable lower bound and a quick feasible tour. The nearest‑neighbour heuristic builds a tour by repeatedly moving to the closest unvisited point; its total length LNN is an upper bound. A simple lower bound is the sum of the two shortest edges incident to each vertex divided by two (the 1‑tree bound). For a small set (n ≤ 6) the exam may expect the candidate to enumerate all (n−1)!/2 distinct tours and pick the minimum; the number of tours for n=6 is 60, manageable with systematic tabulation. When coordinates are given, compute the Euclidean distance matrix first. A common twist: “The vehicle can travel at 40 km/h on highways (edges marked H) and 20 km/h on local roads (edges marked L). Find the minimum time tour.” Here the edge weight is time = distance / speed, not distance. The same heuristics apply on the time‑weighted graph. Another variant adds a mandatory sequence constraint (e.g., P₃ must be visited before P₅); this reduces the permutation space and can be handled by fixing the relative order before applying the heuristic. Candidates should practice constructing the distance matrix quickly, spotting symmetry, and using the triangle inequality to discard obviously sub‑optimal edges (if d(A,B) > d(A,C)+d(C,B) then edge AB can never belong to an optimal tour). The final answer is usually the total distance or time rounded to the nearest integer; always state the unit.

  • Confusing magnetic declination sign when converting between true, magnetic, and grid bearings.
  • Using the point‑to‑infinite‑line distance formula without checking whether the perpendicular foot falls on the segment.
  • Forgetting to add 180° to the solar azimuth to obtain the shadow bearing.
  • Treating “wind from 270°” as a vector toward 270° instead of toward 090°.
  • Applying the nearest‑neighbour tour length as the optimal answer without verifying a lower bound.
  • Neglecting to normalise all intermediate bearings to the 0°–360° range before computing vector components.
  • Using the craft’s speed relative to the medium as ground speed when a current or wind is present.

Bearing Intersection and Triangulation for Position Fixing

When two or more bearings to known landmarks are measured from an unknown position, the observer’s location is the intersection of the corresponding bearing lines. In a planar approximation each bearing is a ray originating at the landmark with direction equal to the measured bearing (converted to a standard 0°–360° clockwise‑from‑north convention). The intersection of two non‑parallel rays yields a unique point; a third bearing provides a redundancy check and allows estimation of observational error. The algebraic solution uses the line equations in Cartesian form: for a landmark at (x₀, y₀) and bearing β, the line direction vector is (sin β, cos β). The parametric form is (x, y) = (x₀, y₀) + t·(sin β, cos β) with t ≥ 0. Solving the two‑line system for the parameters t₁, t₂ gives the intersection coordinates. If the bearings are taken from the observer to the landmarks (i.e., “bearing of landmark A is 045°”), the ray direction must be reversed by adding 180° before constructing the line. In competitive‑exam problems the landmarks are often given as grid coordinates, and the bearings are either true or magnetic; the candidate must apply the appropriate magnetic declination before intersecting. A practical shortcut: convert each bearing to a slope m = tan(β) (with β measured from the positive x‑axis, i.e., east) and use the point‑slope form y − y₀ = m(x − x₀). Because the exam grid uses north as the positive y‑axis, the conversion is m = tan(90° − β). The intersection of two such lines is then obtained by solving the resulting linear equations. When three bearings are supplied, the three pairwise intersections usually form a small triangle; the centroid of that triangle is taken as the best estimate of the observer’s position, and the size of the triangle indicates the angular uncertainty. Candidates should practise converting between bearing conventions, handling the 180° reversal, and solving the linear system quickly with determinant formulas.

Geodesic versus Rhumb Line Distance on a Spherical Earth

On a sphere the shortest path between two points is a segment of a great circle (geodesic), whereas a rhumb line (loxodrome) maintains a constant bearing and spirals toward the poles. For short distances (less than a few hundred kilometres) the planar approximation is adequate, but many advanced direction‑distance sets explicitly test the distinction. The great‑circle distance d between points with latitudes φ₁, φ₂ and longitude difference Δλ is given by the haversine formula: hav(d/R) = hav(φ₂ − φ₁) + cos φ₁ cos φ₂ hav(Δλ), where hav(θ) = sin²(θ/2) and R is the Earth’s mean radius (≈ 6371 km). The initial bearing θ₁ from point 1 to point 2 along the great circle satisfies tan θ₁ = sin Δλ / (cos φ₁ tan φ₂ − sin φ₁ cos Δλ). A rhumb line distance is simpler: the meridional distance is R·|φ₂ − φ₁| (in radians) and the departure is R·cos φₘ·Δλ, where φₘ is the mean latitude (φ₁+φ₂)/2. The rhumb line distance is √(meridional² + departure²) and the constant bearing is atan2(departure, meridional). Exam questions may ask: “A ship sails from 20° N, 60° W to 30° N, 50° W. Find the great‑circle distance and the rhumb line distance.” The candidate must compute both, note that the great‑circle distance is shorter, and recognise that the rhumb line bearing is constant while the great‑circle bearing changes continuously. A common twist is to provide the great‑circle distance and ask for the rhumb line bearing, requiring the inverse haversine step. Mastery of the haversine formula, the bearing formula, and the rhumb line approximations is essential for high‑level navigation problems.

Technique: Great‑Circle Distance and Initial Bearing
A vessel departs from point A (latitude 12° N, longitude 75° E) and follows a great‑circle track to point B (latitude 24° N, longitude 80° E). Compute the great‑circle distance in kilometres (use R = 6371 km) and the initial bearing at A (degrees clockwise from true north).
Convert latitudes and longitudes to radians: φ₁ = 12° = 0.20944 rad, φ₂ = 24° = 0.41888 rad, Δλ = 5° = 0.08727 rad. Compute hav(φ₂−φ₁) = sin²(0.10472/2) = sin²(0.05236) ≈ 0.00274. cos φ₁ = 0.97815, cos φ₂ = 0.91355. hav(Δλ) = sin²(0.08727/2) = sin²(0.04363) ≈ 0.00190. hav(d/R) = 0.00274 + 0.97815·0.91355·0.00190 ≈ 0.00274 + 0.00170 = 0.00444. d/R = 2·asin(√0.00444) ≈ 2·asin(0.0666) ≈ 0.1333 rad. Distance d = 0.1333·6371 ≈ 849 km. Initial bearing: tan θ₁ = sin Δλ / (cos φ₁·tan φ₂ − sin φ₁·cos Δλ). sin Δλ = 0.08716. tan φ₂ = 0.4452. cos φ₁·tan φ₂ = 0.97815·0.4452 = 0.4355. sin φ₁ = 0.2079. cos Δλ = 0.99619. sin φ₁·cos Δλ = 0.2079·0.99619 = 0.2071. Denominator = 0.4355 − 0.2071 = 0.2284. tan θ₁ = 0.08716 / 0.2284 = 0.3816. θ₁ = atan(0.3816) ≈ 20.9°. Since denominator > 0 and numerator > 0, bearing is in NE quadrant, so initial bearing = 20.9° (≈ 021°). Answer: distance ≈ 849 km, initial bearing ≈ 021°.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the haversine and rhumb‑line formulas in radian form; keep a small table of sin, cos, tan for common angles (0°, 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°) to avoid calculator overload. When a problem gives coordinates in degrees, convert to radians once at the start and reuse the radian values. For bearing calculations, always normalise the atan2 result to 0°–360° by adding 360° if negative. Practice a few mixed great‑circle/rhumb‑line pairs so you can recognise which formula the question expects.

Magnetic Variation and Deviation Correction in Compass Navigation

A magnetic compass points to magnetic north, which differs from true north by the magnetic variation (declination) that varies with location and time. Additionally, the vessel’s own ferrous structure induces a deviation that depends on the ship’s heading. The relationship among true bearing (T), magnetic bearing (M), compass bearing (C), variation (V), and deviation (D) is traditionally remembered by the mnemonic “True Virgins Make Dull Companions” (T → V → M → D → C) when moving from true to compass, and the reverse “Cadbury Dairy Milk Very Tasty” (C → D → M → V → T) when moving from compass to true. The sign convention: easterly variation or deviation is added when going from true to magnetic/compass (i.e., M = T − E, C = M − E) and subtracted when going the opposite way. Westerly variation/deviation is treated oppositely. In exam problems the variation is usually given as a single value (e.g., “Variation 5° E”) and the deviation table provides D for each compass heading (often at 15° intervals). To convert a compass course to a true course, first apply the deviation for that compass heading (interpolating if necessary), then apply the variation. Conversely, to find the compass course to steer for a desired true track, apply variation first (true → magnetic) then deviation (magnetic → compass). A typical question: “Variation 3° W, deviation table: 0° → 2° E, 90° → 1° W, 180° → 3° E, 270° → 0°. Find the compass course to steer to make good a true track of 120°.” Solution: True 120° → Magnetic = 120° + 3° W = 123° (since westerly variation adds). Interpolate deviation at magnetic 123° (between 90° and 180°). At 90° deviation = 1° W, at 180° deviation = 3° E. Linear interpolation gives deviation ≈ 1° W + (33/90)·(4°) ≈ 1° W + 1.47° E ≈ 0.47° E. Compass = Magnetic − deviation (easterly deviation subtracts) = 123° − 0.47° ≈ 122.5°. Round to nearest degree: 123°. Candidates must be fluent in sign handling, interpolation, and the order of operations.

Error Ellipse Propagation in Multi‑Leg Dead Reckoning

Dead reckoning accumulates position uncertainty because each leg’s course and distance measurements contain errors. If the errors are modelled as independent zero‑mean Gaussian variables with standard deviations σ₍course₎ (in radians) and σ₍dist₎ (in metres), the covariance matrix of the position after a single leg of length L and heading θ can be approximated by linearising the transformation. The position increment is Δx = L sin θ, Δy = L cos θ. The Jacobian with respect to (L, θ) is J = [[sin θ, L cos θ], [cos θ, −L sin θ]]. If the measurement covariance is diag(σ₍dist₎², σ₍course₎²), the position covariance after the leg is Σ = J·diag·Jᵀ. For a sequence of legs, the covariances add because the errors are independent: Σ_total = Σ₁ + Σ₂ + … + Σₙ. The resulting 2×2 covariance matrix defines an error ellipse whose semi‑axes are the square roots of the eigenvalues of Σ_total, and whose orientation is given by the eigenvectors. In exam‑style problems the candidate is rarely asked to compute eigenvalues explicitly; instead, a simplified “circular error probable” (CEP) or “95 % confidence radius” is requested. A common approximation: treat the along‑track error as σ₍dist₎ and the cross‑track error as L·σ₍course₎ for each leg, then combine them in quadrature across legs. For example, three legs of 10 km each with σ₍dist₎ = 0.1 km and σ₍course₎ = 0.5° (≈ 0.00873 rad) yield per‑leg cross‑track σ ≈ 10·0.00873 = 0.0873 km. Total along‑track σ = √3·0.1 ≈ 0.173 km, total cross‑track σ = √3·0.0873 ≈ 0.151 km. The 95 % confidence ellipse semi‑axes are roughly 2·σ along each principal direction. Understanding this propagation allows the aspirant to answer questions such as “After four legs of 5 nm each with course error ±1° and distance error ±0.2 nm, what is the approximate radius of the 95 % error circle?” without performing full matrix algebra.

Altitude Effects on Horizontal Distance Projection

When an aircraft or a high‑altitude platform measures a slant range to a ground point, the horizontal (ground) distance is the projection of the slant range onto the reference ellipsoid. If the altitude of the platform is h and the slant range is R, the ground distance d satisfies d = √(R² − h²) for a flat‑Earth approximation. Over longer distances the curvature of the Earth must be considered: the line‑of‑sight distance to the horizon from altitude h is √(2Rh + h²) where R is Earth’s radius. For a target at ground range d, the slant range is √(d² + h²) only when d is small compared with R. In competitive‑exam problems the flat‑Earth formula is usually sufficient, but a twist may involve a satellite at altitude 800 km measuring a slant range of 1200 km; the candidate must compute the ground distance using the spherical law of cosines: cos(d/R) = cos(h/R)·cos(R_slant/R) + sin(h/R)·sin(R_slant/R)·cos(α), where α is the azimuth angle (often 0° for nadir). For nadir viewing α = 0, the formula reduces to cos(d/R) = cos((R+h)/R)·cos(R_slant/R) + sin((R+h)/R)·sin(R_slant/R). With R = 6371 km, h = 800 km, R_slant = 1200 km, the ground distance d ≈ 1000 km. A simpler exam variant: “A drone flies at 150 m altitude and its laser rangefinder reads 300 m to a point on the ground. What is the horizontal distance?” Answer: √(300² − 150²) = √(90000 − 22500) = √67500 ≈ 259.8 m. Candidates should recognise when to apply the flat‑Earth Pythagorean projection and when the problem explicitly demands spherical geometry (usually signalled by altitudes > 100 km or ranges > 500 km).

  • Confusing the order of variation and deviation when converting between true, magnetic, and compass bearings.
  • Applying easterly variation as subtraction instead of addition when moving from true to magnetic.
  • Forgetting to interpolate deviation for headings not listed in the deviation table.
  • Using the great‑circle distance formula for short‑range problems where the planar approximation is expected, wasting time.
  • Neglecting to convert bearings to the correct quadrant when using atan2, leading to 180° errors.
  • Treating slant range as horizontal distance without applying the altitude correction.
  • Assuming error ellipses remain circular after multiple legs, ignoring cross‑track growth.
