What are Calendar & Clock Problems?

Calendar and clock problems test your ability to calculate dates, weekdays, angles, and time-related phenomena using modular arithmetic and basic spatial reasoning. SSC CGL typically asks 1-2 questions from this topic in Tier 1.

Calendar Concepts

1. Odd Days: The number of days beyond a complete week (7 days) is called odd days. For example, 365 days = 52 weeks + 1 odd day. 366 days (leap year) = 52 weeks + 2 odd days.

2. Leap Year Rules: A year divisible by 4 is a leap year. Exception: century years must be divisible by 400 to be leap years (e.g., 1900 is not a leap year, but 2000 is).

3. Weekday Calculation: If you know the weekday of a reference date, you can calculate any other date by adding odd days and getting modulo 7 (0=Sunday, 1=Monday, ..., 6=Saturday).

1. Angle Between Hands: In 60 minutes, the minute hand covers 360Â° (6Â° per minute), and the hour hand covers 30Â° (0.5Â° per minute). The angle between hands = |30H - 5.5M|, where H is the hour and M is minutes.

2. Clock Gains/Losses: A watch that gains or loses time does so at a constant rate. Calculate the net gain/loss per hour and multiply by elapsed time.

3. Mirror Images: The mirror image of a clock shows the time as (12:00 âˆ’ actual time). If it's hh:mm, the mirror time is 11:60 - hh:mm.

Leap Year Cycle and Century Exceptions

The Gregorian calendar inserts a leap day on 29 February every year that is divisible by 4, except for century years that are not divisible by 400. This rule creates a 400‑year cycle containing 97 leap years and 303 ordinary years. The total number of days in 400 years is 400 × 365 + 97 = 146 097, which is exactly 20 871 weeks; therefore the calendar repeats every 400 years with zero odd days.

For shorter spans the odd‑day count is derived from the composition of leap and ordinary years. A normal year contributes 1 odd day (365 ≡ 1 (mod 7)). A leap year contributes 2 odd days (366 ≡ 2 (mod 7)). In a 100‑year block there are 24 leap years (years 4, 8, …, 96) and 76 ordinary years, giving 24 × 2 + 76 × 1 = 124 odd days ≡ 5 (mod 7). Consequently a 100‑year period adds 5 odd days. A 200‑year period adds 10 ≡ 3 odd days, a 300‑year period adds 15 ≡ 1 odd day, and a 400‑year period adds 20 ≡ 0 odd days because the century year 400 is a leap year.

When calculating the weekday for a date far in the past or future, break the interval into complete 400‑year cycles (zero odd days), then add the odd days for the remaining centuries, decades, and single years. For example, to move from 1 January 2000 (Saturday) to 1 January 2100, note that 2000 is a leap century, so the 100‑year block 2000‑2099 contains 25 leap years (including 2000) and 75 ordinary years → 25 × 2 + 75 × 1 = 125 odd days ≡ 6 (mod 7). Adding 6 odd days to Saturday gives Friday, which matches the known weekday of 1 January 2100.

A common shortcut is to memorise the century‑odd‑day table: 1600 → 0, 1700 → 5, 1800 → 3, 1900 → 1, 2000 → 0, 2100 → 5, 2200 → 3, 2300 → 1, 2400 → 0. This table follows the 5‑3‑1‑0 pattern and eliminates the need to recount leap years for every century.

When the target date lies inside a century, add the odd days for each completed year after the century start. For months, use the standard month‑odd‑day values for a non‑leap year (Jan = 3, Feb = 0, Mar = 3, Apr = 2, May = 3, Jun = 2, Jul = 3, Aug = 3, Sep = 2, Oct = 3, Nov = 2, Dec = 3) and add one extra odd day for February in a leap year. Summing all contributions modulo 7 yields the required weekday.

Clock Gain and Loss Mechanics

A clock that gains or loses time at a constant rate can be modelled by a linear relationship between the indicated time and the true elapsed time. If a clock gains g minutes per hour, then in t true hours it will show an advance of t × (60 + g) minutes. Conversely, if it loses l minutes per hour, the shown advance is t × (60 − l) minutes. The ratio of shown minutes to true minutes is therefore (60 ± rate)/60.

To find the true time when the faulty clock shows a particular reading, set up the proportion: shown minutes / true minutes = (60 ± rate)/60. For instance, a clock that gains 5 minutes per hour shows 65 minutes for every 60 true minutes. If it reads 3 hours = 180 minutes, the true elapsed minutes = 180 × 60/65 = 166.15 minutes ≈ 2 hours 46 minutes.

The moment when a gaining clock again shows the correct time occurs when the accumulated gain equals a multiple of 12 hours (720 minutes) for a 12‑hour dial, or 24 hours (1440 minutes) for a 24‑hour dial. If the gain is g minutes per hour, the gain per true hour is g minutes. The number of true hours needed for a 12‑hour gain is 720/g. For a loss of l minutes per hour, the clock falls behind; it will show the correct time again when the total loss reaches 720 minutes, i.e. after 720/l true hours.

When two faulty clocks are compared, their relative gain/loss per hour is the algebraic sum of their individual rates. If clock A gains 3 min/h and clock B loses 2 min/h, the relative rate is 5 min/h. They will show the same reading whenever the relative gain accumulates to a multiple of 720 minutes, i.e. every 720/5 = 144 true hours.

Problems often ask for the correct time at a given instant when the faulty clock was set right at a known moment. Compute the true elapsed interval, multiply by the gain/loss rate to obtain the total error, then add (for gain) or subtract (for loss) that error from the faulty reading to recover the true time. Always keep units consistent: convert hours to minutes before applying the rate.

Gain/Loss Shortcut
A clock gains 5 minutes every hour. It is set right at 6:00 AM. What time will the clock show at 6:00 PM on the same day?
True elapsed time = 12 hours = 720 minutes. Gain per hour = 5 minutes → total gain = 12 × 5 = 60 minutes = 1 hour. Faulty clock reading = true time + gain = 6:00 PM + 1 hour = 7:00 PM. Answer: 7:00 PM.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Gain/Loss
Memorise the formula: error = (rate × true hours). For a 12‑hour dial, the clock repeats its correct reading every 720/rate hours. Convert all given times to minutes past a reference (e.g., 12:00) before applying the rate; this avoids hour‑minute carry errors. When two clocks are involved, work with their relative rate to find meeting points quickly.

Relative Speed Method for Clock Problems

The minute hand moves 360° in 60 minutes → 6° per minute. The hour hand moves 360° in 12 hours = 720 minutes → 0.5° per minute. Their relative angular speed is 6 − 0.5 = 5.5° per minute. This single figure solves all coincidence, opposition, and right‑angle questions without memorising separate formulas.

At any given hour H (in 12‑hour format) and minute M, the hour hand angle from 12 o’clock is 30H + 0.5M degrees, and the minute hand angle is 6M degrees. The absolute difference is |30H − 5.5M|. If this exceeds 180°, subtract from 360° to obtain the smaller angle.

To find when the hands coincide after a known hour H, set the angle difference to zero: 30H − 5.5M = 0 → M = (30H)/5.5 = (60H)/11 minutes past H. For example, after 4 o’clock the coincidence occurs at 4 + 240/11 = 4 : 21 9/11 minutes.

For opposition (180° apart), set |30H − 5.5M| = 180. This yields two solutions per 12‑hour cycle: M = (30H ± 180)/5.5. The “+” gives the first opposition after H, the “−” gives the one before H (or after the previous hour). For right angles (90°), use 90 instead of 180.

The relative speed method also handles “how many times do the hands form a right angle in a day?” Since the relative speed is 5.5°/min, the hands gain 360° every 360/5.5 = 720/11 ≈ 65.45 minutes. In 12 hours there are 11 coincidences, 11 oppositions, and 22 right angles. Doubling for 24 hours gives 22 coincidences, 22 oppositions, and 44 right angles.

When the clock is faulty (gaining or losing), adjust the effective speeds. A gaining clock’s minute hand moves faster than 6°/min by a factor (60 + g)/60, and the hour hand moves faster by the same factor. Their relative speed becomes 5.5 × (60 + g)/60 degrees per true minute. Use this adjusted relative speed in the same equations to find apparent coincidences on the faulty dial.

Calendar Repetition Patterns and the 28‑Year Cycle

For any non‑century year, the calendar repeats after 28 years because the leap‑year pattern (every 4 years) and the weekday shift (1 odd day per ordinary year, 2 per leap year) align every 28 years: 28 × 1 + 7 × 1 = 35 odd days ≡ 0 (mod 7). The seven leap years in a 28‑year block contribute an extra 7 odd days, totalling 35 ≡ 0.

Century years break the 28‑year cycle because the century year is not a leap year unless divisible by 400. For example, 1900 is not a leap year, so the 28‑year repetition that would map 1872 to 1900 fails; the calendar of 1872 repeats in 1900 only after a 12‑year gap (1872 → 1884 → 1896 → 1908). The general rule: a year Y (non‑century) repeats in Y + 28 unless a non‑leap century falls in between; then the next repetition is Y + 40 (or Y + 12 if the century is a leap year like 2000).

To find the next year with the same calendar as a given year, first check if the given year is a leap year. If it is a leap year, the next identical leap‑year calendar occurs after 28 years, provided no non‑leap century intervenes. If the given year is ordinary, the next identical ordinary calendar also occurs after 28 years under the same condition. When a non‑leap century lies in the interval, add 12 years to the 28‑year step (i.e., 40 years total) because the missing leap day shifts the weekday pattern by one extra day.

A practical shortcut: compute the year’s “calendar code” = (year + ⌊year/4⌋ − ⌊year/100⌋ + ⌊year/400⌋) mod 7. Two years share the same calendar iff they have the same calendar code and the same leap‑year status. This formula automatically accounts for century exceptions and works for any Gregorian year.

For example, 2023 is ordinary. Calendar code = (2023 + 505 − 20 + 5) mod 7 = 2513 mod 7 = 2. The next ordinary year with code 2 is 2034 (2023 + 11) because 2024 is leap, 2025‑2033 shift codes, and 2034 matches. Indeed 2023 and 2034 have identical calendars. The 28‑year rule would give 2051, but the intervening leap‑year pattern makes 2034 the earlier repeat.

Non‑Standard Clocks and Mirror‑Image Variations

Examiners often test clocks that lack numbers, run on a 24‑hour dial, or are viewed in a mirror or water reflection. The core geometry remains the same; only the reference frame changes.

For a 24‑hour analog clock, the hour hand completes one revolution in 24 hours → 15° per hour = 0.25° per minute. The minute hand still moves 6° per minute. Relative speed = 6 − 0.25 = 5.75° per minute. Coincidence interval = 360/5.75 = 1440/23 ≈ 62.61 minutes. There are 23 coincidences in 24 hours. The angle formula becomes |15H − 5.75M|, where H is in 0‑23 range.

Mirror image of a 12‑hour clock: the apparent time is 11:60 − actual time (hours:minutes). For a 24‑hour clock, the mirror axis is at 12:00/00:00, so the mirrored time = 24:00 − actual time. Example: actual 14:30 → mirror = 24:00 − 14:30 = 09:30.

Water image (vertical reflection) swaps the positions of 12 and 6, 1 and 5, 2 and 4, etc. The transformation for a 12‑hour clock is: water time = 12:00 − actual time (if actual ≤ 12:00) or 24:00 − actual time (if actual > 12:00). In practice, compute the mirror time first, then apply the water rule, or use the direct formula: water hour = (12 − hour) mod 12, water minute = (60 − minute) mod 60, with special handling when minute = 0.

Clocks with missing numbers (e.g., only 12, 3, 6, 9 marked) require the candidate to infer the hour hand position from the nearest marked hour. The angle between the hour hand and the nearest marked hour is 0.5° × minutes past that hour. This is often combined with gain/loss: a faulty clock showing 3:20 with only 12, 3, 6, 9 visible – the hour hand is 10° past 3 (20 × 0.5). If the clock gains 2 min/h, the true minute is slightly less; adjust using the gain ratio.

When multiple distortions combine (mirror + gain), solve sequentially: first find the true time on the faulty clock using gain/loss, then apply the mirror transformation. Always keep a clear timeline: reference moment → true elapsed → faulty reading → transformed reading.

  • Assuming the 28‑year repetition works across a non‑leap century without adding the extra 12 years.
  • Using the 12‑hour mirror formula (11:60 − time) for a 24‑hour clock.
  • Forgetting to convert gain/loss rates to a per‑minute basis before applying relative speed.
  • Treating the hour hand speed as 0.5°/min on a 24‑hour dial.
  • Neglecting the extra odd day contributed by a leap century (e.g., 2000) when summing century odd days.
  • Applying the water‑image rule without first normalising the minute to the 0‑59 range.
  • Confusing the coincidence count for a 24‑hour clock (23 per day) with the 12‑hour count (11 per 12 hours).

Mastering these advanced patterns ensures accurate solutions even when the examiner layers multiple distortions in a single question.

Zeller’s Congruence and the Doomsday Algorithm for Weekday Determination

Zeller’s congruence gives a direct arithmetic formula for the day of the week of any Gregorian date. For a date with day q, month m (March = 3, …, January = 13, February = 14 of the previous year), year of the century K = year mod 100, and zero‑based century J = ⌊year/100⌋, the weekday index h = (q + ⌊13(m+1)/5⌋ + K + ⌊K/4⌋ + ⌊J/4⌋ + 5J) mod 7. The result maps 0 = Saturday, 1 = Sunday, …, 6 = Friday. Because the formula uses only integer arithmetic, it is ideal for mental calculation once the month adjustment is memorised.

The Doomsday algorithm, devised by John Conway, works with a memorised “anchor day” for each century. For the Gregorian calendar the anchor days repeat every 400 years: 1800 → Friday, 1900 → Wednesday, 2000 → Tuesday, 2100 → Sunday. For a given year, compute the year’s offset: take the last two digits y, add ⌊y/4⌋, then add the century anchor (mod 7). The resulting day is the year’s Doomsday — the weekday on which 4/4, 6/6, 8/8, 10/10, 12/12, and the last day of February fall. Any target date is then compared to the nearest Doomsday in its month.

Both methods are equivalent; Zeller’s congruence is a single closed form, while Doomsday breaks the computation into smaller, memorable steps. In examinations, Doomsday is often faster for mental work because the anchor days for recent centuries (1900, 2000) are well known. Zeller’s congruence shines when a direct implementation in code or a single‑line calculation is required.

Leap‑year handling is automatic in both systems. In Zeller’s formula the term ⌊K/4⌋ adds one for each leap year within the century, and ⌊J/4⌋ adds the century‑leap correction. In Doomsday, the anchor day for a century already incorporates the Gregorian 400‑year rule, and the ⌊y/4⌋ term accounts for leap years inside the century. For dates before 1582 the Julian calendar must be used; Zeller’s congruence for the Julian calendar simply drops the ⌊J/4⌋ and 5J terms, replacing them with 5J (or equivalently using a different constant).

A practical shortcut: memorise the month codes for Zeller (March=3, April=4, …, January=13, February=14) and the Doomsday dates for each month (e.g., 1/3, 2/28 or 2/29, 3/14, 4/4, 5/9, 6/6, 7/11, 8/8, 9/5, 10/10, 11/7, 12/12). With these tables, any weekday question reduces to a few additions and a modulo‑7 operation.

Synchronization of Multiple Faulty Clocks and Relative Gain/Loss

When two or more clocks run at different erroneous rates, the problem reduces to a relative‑speed exercise on a circular dial. Express each clock’s rate as minutes gained or lost per true hour. A clock that gains g minutes per hour shows 60+g minutes for every 60 true minutes; its displayed minute hand moves at (60+g)/60 = 1 + g/60 times the true speed. A losing clock with loss l moves at 1 − l/60.

The relative speed between two clocks is the difference of their display speeds. If clock A gains g₁ and clock B loses l₂, the relative gain per true hour is g₁ + l₂ minutes. They will show the same reading whenever the accumulated relative gain equals a multiple of 12 hours (720 minutes) for a 12‑hour dial, or 24 hours (1440 minutes) for a 24‑hour dial. The meeting interval T (in true hours) = 720 / (g₁ + l₂) for a 12‑hour dial.

If the question asks when both clocks again show the correct time simultaneously, compute each clock’s individual period to return to correctness: a clock gaining g minutes per hour needs 720/g true hours (12‑hour dial) to accumulate a full 12‑hour error cycle. The simultaneous correctness occurs at the least common multiple of the individual periods.

When three or more clocks are involved, pairwise relative rates can be used to build a system of congruences. For example, clock A gains 2 min/h, clock B loses 1 min/h, clock C gains 3 min/h. The pairwise relative gains are 3, 5, and 1 min/h respectively. The time when all three show the same reading is the smallest true‑hour T satisfying 3T ≡ 0 (mod 720), 5T ≡ 0 (mod 720), 1T ≡ 0 (mod 720). This reduces to T = LCM(720/3, 720/5, 720/1) = LCM(240, 144, 720) = 720 hours.

A common twist: the clocks are set correctly at different starting moments. Convert each start to a common reference (e.g., true minutes elapsed since a fixed epoch), then apply the same relative‑gain logic. The displayed time of clock i at true elapsed t is (start_i + (1 + rate_i/60)·t) mod 720. Equating displayed times yields linear congruences in t that solve by standard modular arithmetic.

Relative Gain Method for Two Faulty Clocks
Clock A gains 2 minutes per hour. Clock B loses 1 minute per hour. Both are set correctly at 12:00 noon. After how many true hours will they show the same time again on a 12‑hour dial?
Relative gain per true hour = 2 + 1 = 3 minutes. They show the same reading when the relative gain accumulates 12 hours = 720 minutes. Required true hours = 720 ÷ 3 = 240 hours. 240 hours = 10 days. At that moment both clocks display the same wrong time (each has drifted 480 minutes = 8 hours from true time).
Exam‑Day Strategy
Convert every gain/loss rate to minutes per true hour first. Then work with relative rates; the dial size (12‑hour or 24‑hour) tells you the modulus (720 or 1440 minutes). For simultaneous correctness, compute each clock’s individual error‑cycle period and take the LCM.

Exact Time Calculation for a Given Angle Between Clock Hands

The angle between the hour and minute hands at H hours M minutes (12‑hour dial) is |30H − 5.5M| degrees, where the hour hand moves 0.5° per minute and the minute hand 6° per minute. For a prescribed angle θ (0° ≤ θ ≤ 180°), solve 30H − 5.5M = ±θ. This yields two linear equations: M = (30H − θ)/5.5 and M = (30H + θ)/5.5. Because M must lie in [0, 60), each hour typically gives two valid solutions, except when θ = 0° or 180° where the two coincide.

To enumerate all occurrences in a 12‑hour cycle, iterate H = 0,1,…,11 (with H = 0 representing 12). For each H compute the two candidate minutes. Accept a candidate if 0 ≤ M < 60. The total number of solutions is 22 for 0° < θ < 180°, 11 for θ = 0° (coincidences) and 11 for θ = 180° (opposite). This matches the known fact that the hands form any non‑extreme angle 22 times every 12 hours.

When the problem asks for the exact time (including seconds), keep the fractional minute. For example, for θ = 90° at H = 3, M = (90 − 90)/5.5 = 0 and M = (90 + 90)/5.5 = 180/5.5 = 32.727… minutes = 32 minutes 43.636… seconds. The two times are 3:00:00 and 3:32:43.6.

If the clock is a 24‑hour analog dial, the hour hand speed is 15° per hour = 0.25° per minute. The angle formula becomes |15H − 5.75M| with H in 0‑23. The same algebraic method applies, but the modulus for coincidence is 1440 minutes and there are 23 coincidences per day.

A frequent examiner twist: “Find the first time after 2:00 when the angle is 100°.” Compute the two solutions for H = 2; discard any M ≤ 0 (since we need after 2:00). If both are ≤ 0, advance to H = 3 and repeat. This sequential search guarantees the earliest occurrence.

Calendar Computations Across the Julian‑Gregorian Transition

The Julian calendar, introduced 45 BCE, adds a leap day every four years without exception. The Gregorian reform (1582) omitted three leap days every 400 years by making century years non‑leap unless divisible by 400. The transition date varied: Catholic countries adopted 15 October 1582 (Friday) after 4 October 1582 (Thursday), a 10‑day jump. Britain and colonies switched in 1752 (11‑day jump), Russia in 1918 (13‑day jump). For any historical date, first determine which calendar was in force.

For Julian dates, Zeller’s congruence simplifies to h = (q + ⌊13(m+1)/5⌋ + K + ⌊K/4⌋ + 5J) mod 7, where J = ⌊year/100⌋ and K = year mod 100, using the same month shift (March = 3). No century‑leap correction term appears. The result maps 0 = Saturday … 6 = Friday as before.

To convert a Julian date to its Gregorian equivalent, add the accumulated offset: 10 days for 1582‑1700, 11 days for 1700‑1800, 12 days for 1800‑1900, 13 days for 1900‑2100, etc. The offset increases by one day each time a Gregorian non‑leap century passes. For example, 1 January 1700 (Julian) = 11 January 1700 (Gregorian). After conversion, apply the Gregorian Zeller formula.

The Doomsday algorithm also has a Julian version. The century anchor for Julian centuries advances by 5 days per century (since 365.25 × 100 ≡ 5 mod 7). The anchor for 100 CE is Sunday, 200 CE Friday, 300 CE Wednesday, etc. The year‑offset calculation (y + ⌊y/4⌋) remains unchanged. This allows rapid weekday determination for any Julian date without conversion.

Examiners often test the 10‑day jump of 1582: “What was the day of the week on 15 October 1582?” Using Gregorian Zeller directly gives Friday. A trap is to apply the Julian formula to 15 October 1582, which would give a different weekday because the Julian calendar was not in use in the adopting regions. Always verify the calendar authority for the region and year.

Combined Mirror‑Water Transformations with Rotated Dials

A mirror image (horizontal axis) of a 12‑hour clock shows the time 11:60 − actual time. A water image (vertical axis) swaps the 12‑6 and 1‑5, 2‑4, 3‑3 positions; the transformation is water time = 12:00 − actual time for times ≤ 12:00, and 24:00 − actual time for times > 12:00. When a dial is rotated by 90° clockwise, the 12 moves to the 3‑position, 3 to 6, 6 to 9, 9 to 12. The effective mapping adds 3 hours (mod 12) to the hour hand reading while the minute hand reading is unchanged.

To solve a combined distortion, apply transformations sequentially in the order the physical effects occur. Example: actual time 3:40 on a standard clock. First mirror → 11:60 − 3:40 = 8:20. Then water → since 8:20 ≤ 12:00, water = 12:00 − 8:20 = 3:40. The net result returns to the original because mirror and water are inverses on a symmetric dial. If the dial is also rotated 90° clockwise after the mirror, add 3 hours to the mirrored hour: 8:20 → 11:20.

When gain/loss is present, first compute the true time shown by the faulty clock using the relative‑gain method, then apply the geometric transformations. For instance, a clock gains 2 min/h and shows 3:40 (mirror‑water‑rotated). Determine the true elapsed since setting, find the actual correct time, then apply the inverse transformations to recover the displayed reading.

A systematic approach: represent each transformation as a function on the pair (hour, minute) modulo 12 (or 24). Mirror: (h,m) → (11−h, 60−m) with carry handling. Water: (h,m) → (12−h, 60−m) for h≠0, with 12→0. Rotation by k hours: (h,m) → ((h+k) mod 12, m). Compose functions algebraically; the composition of mirror and water is a 180° rotation (add 6 hours). Adding a 90° rotation yields a 270° rotation (add 9 hours). This algebraic view eliminates case‑by‑case errors.

Examiners may ask: “A clock viewed in a mirror shows 4:50. The clock loses 5 minutes per hour. What is the true time?” First invert the mirror: actual displayed = 11:60 − 4:50 = 7:10. Then correct for loss: the clock loses 5 min per true hour, so displayed minutes = true minutes × (55/60). Let true elapsed = t minutes. Displayed = t × 55/60 = 7×60+10 = 430 minutes. Hence t = 430 × 60/55 = 469.09 minutes = 7 hours 49.09 minutes. If the clock was set correctly at 12:00, true time = 7:49.

  • Using Gregorian Zeller for pre‑1582 dates without applying the Julian correction.
  • Forgetting to convert gain/loss rates to a common true‑hour basis before computing relative rate.
  • Assuming the angle formula yields a unique minute value; there are two symmetric solutions per hour.
  • Applying the 12‑hour mirror formula (11:60 − time) to a 24‑hour or rotated dial.
  • Neglecting the century‑anchor shift in the Doomsday algorithm for years crossing a non‑leap century.
  • Treating water‑image transformation as simple minute subtraction without handling the hour wrap‑around.
  • Overlooking that two faulty clocks showing the same wrong time does not imply they show the correct time.
