1. Subject-Verb Agreement Errors

Subject-verb agreement is the single most frequently tested error type in the exam. The core rule: a singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb. the exam questions often hide the subject or place it far from the verb, making identification tricky.

Basic Rule Review

A verb must agree with its subject in number and person. The subject is the doer of the action, not the noun closest to the verb.

Subject-Verb Agreement — Quick Reference
Subject TypeExampleCorrect Verb
Singular nounThe boyplays / is playing
Plural nounThe boysplay / are playing
Third-person singularHe / She / Itruns / has / does
Third-person pluralTheyrun / have / do
Uncountable nounInformation / Furniture / Sceneryis / was / has

Singular vs Plural: Quick Identification

The fastest way to identify the subject is to locate the main verb and ask "who/what is performing this action?" Everything between the actual subject and the verb is usually an intervening phrase.

the exam 2018 — Basic SV Agreement
Identify the error:
The quality of the products (A)/ are not up to (B)/ the expected standards (C)/ set by the company. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. The subject is quality (singular). "Of the products" is an intervening phrase. The verb should be is, not are. Correct: "The quality of the products is not up to the expected standards."

Tricky Subjects: Mathematics, News, Politics

Words that end in "-s" but are always singular: news, mathematics, physics, economics, politics, ethics, gymnastics, billiards, measles, mumps, rickets. These take singular verbs despite their plural appearance. the exam uses these as traps.

the exam 2017 — Tricky Singular
Identify the error:
The news of the accidents (A)/ were broadcast on (B)/ all major television (C)/ channels this morning. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. "News" is singular. The verb should be was broadcast, not were broadcast. "The news was broadcast."

Subjects Joined by "And"

Two or more subjects joined by "and" take a plural verb, unless the compound subject is treated as a single unit (e.g., "bread and butter," "rice and curry," "law and order").

the exam 2019 — Compound Subject
Identify the error:
Bread and butter (A)/ are my usual (B)/ breakfast every (C)/ morning. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. "Bread and butter" is treated as a single dish (singular). Correct: "Bread and butter is my usual breakfast." However, if the subjects are distinctly separate, use plural: "Bread and butter are available in the store."

Distance & Intervening Phrases

the exam frequently places a prepositional phrase between the subject and verb to confuse test-takers. The verb must agree with the head noun of the subject, not the noun inside the intervening phrase.

the exam 2021 — Spot the Error
Find the error part in the sentence:

The owner of the restaurants (A)/ are planning to renovate (B)/ the interiors before the (C)/ festive season. (D) / No error (E)

a) A  b) B  c) C  d) D  e) E
Solution: b) B. The real subject is owner (singular). "Of the restaurants" is an intervening phrase. The verb should be is planning, not are planning.
the exam Trick — Intervening Phrase
Mentally bracket or remove the intervening phrase (of + noun, along with, together with, as well as) to isolate the real subject. The verb must agree with the subject outside the bracket. E.g., "The box of chocolates is (not are) on the table."

Indefinite Pronouns

These are high-yield in the exam. Indefinite pronouns are classified into three groups:

  • Always singular: each, every, either, neither, someone, anybody, nobody, everyone, anything
  • Always plural: several, both, few, many, others
  • Depends on the noun: some, any, none, all, most, half — agree with the noun they refer to
the exam 2019 — Indefinite Pronoun
Identify the error:
Neither of the candidates (A)/ were able to answer (B)/ the question asked by (C)/ the interviewing panel. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. "Neither" is always singular. The correct verb is was — "Neither of the candidates was able to answer."

Collective Nouns

Collective nouns (team, committee, audience, crowd, jury, government, staff, family) take a singular verb when the group acts as a single unit, and a plural verb when members act individually. In the exam, treat them as singular unless the sentence explicitly indicates individual action.

Collective Nouns in the exam
Collective NounSingular Use (Unit)Plural Use (Individuals)
CommitteeThe committee has decided.The committee have differing opinions.
JuryThe jury has reached a verdict.The jury are divided on this issue.
AudienceThe audience was enormous.The audience were clapping wildly.
StaffThe staff is well-trained.The staff are arguing among themselves.
the exam Trick — Collective Noun Test
Replace the collective noun with "it" (singular) or "they" (plural). If "it" fits, use a singular verb; if "they" fits, use a plural verb. This trick works in 95% of the exam questions.

Either / Or, Neither / Nor

With correlative conjunctions (either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also), the verb agrees with the subject closest to the verb (proximity rule). This is a guaranteed the exam question every year.

the exam 2020 — Proximity Rule
Identify the error:
Neither the teacher nor the students (A)/ was present in the (B)/ classroom when the (C)/ principal arrived. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. The nearest subject to the verb is students (plural), so the verb should be were, not was. Correct: "Neither the teacher nor the students were present."
the exam Trick — Switch Test
In "either/or" and "neither/nor" constructions, swap the subjects. If the plural subject comes first, the verb remains plural. E.g., "Neither the students nor the teacher was present" (teacher = singular → was). The proximity rule applies regardless of order.

2. Pronoun Agreement & Case

Pronoun errors are the second most common error type in the exam. They fall into three categories: agreement (number/gender), case (subjective/objective/possessive), and reflexive misuse.

Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement

A pronoun must agree with its antecedent (the noun it replaces) in number, gender, and person.

Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement Rules
AntecedentCorrect PronounExample
Each / Every / Either / NeitherSingular (he/she/it)Every student must submit his/her assignment.
Indefinite (everyone, nobody)SingularEveryone should bring his notebook. (or their in modern usage — but the exam prefers his)
Collective nounSingular (it) or plural (they)The team celebrated its victory.
Two singular nouns joined by "and"Plural (they)Ravi and Sohan completed their work.
Two singular nouns joined by "or/nor"SingularEither Ravi or Sohan will bring his camera.
the exam 2022 — Pronoun Agreement
Identify the error:
Each of the girls have submitted (A)/ their assignments to (B)/ the class teacher before (C)/ the deadline. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: Both A and B have errors. A: "Each" is singular → has submitted. B: The correct pronoun for "each of the girls" is her (not their). In the exam, "each" + "their" is a common trap.

Who vs Whom

"Who" is the subjective case (acts as the subject). "Whom" is the objective case (acts as the object of a verb or preposition). the exam tests this distinction in error detection and sentence improvement.

  • Who = he / she / they (subject) — "Who called you?" (He called me.)
  • Whom = him / her / them (object) — "Whom did you call?" (I called him.)
the exam Trick — The He/Him Test
Replace the who/whom with "he" or "him". If "he" fits, use who. If "him" fits, use whom. For example: "(Who/Whom) did you see?" → "You did see him." → Whom.

Reflexive Pronouns

Reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, themselves) must refer back to the subject of the sentence. They cannot be used as subjects or as replacements for personal pronouns.

the exam 2020 — Reflexive Error
Identify the error:
The manager and myself (A)/ will handle the (B)/ client meeting scheduled (C)/ for next Monday. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: a) A. "Myself" cannot be used as a subject. Replace it with "I". Correct: "The manager and I will handle the client meeting."

Common Pronoun Errors in the exam

Frequently Tested Pronoun Errors
Error TypeIncorrectCorrect

Between you and IBetween you and IBetween you and me

3. Tense Consistency

the exam frequently tests tense sequencing — especially in sentences with two clauses where the tenses must be logically consistent. The three golden rules: past in the past (past perfect), sequence of tenses, and time expressions.

Past Perfect with Simple Past

When two actions happened in the past, the earlier action takes the past perfect (had + V3), and the later action takes the simple past (V2). This is the number one tense error in the exam.

the exam 2021 — Past Perfect
Identify the error:
When we reached the station (A)/ the train already left (B)/ so we had to wait (C)/ for the next one. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. The train left before we reached the station (two past actions, one earlier). The earlier action needs past perfect: "the train had already left".

Sequence of Tenses

When the main clause is in the past tense, the subordinate clause must also be in a past tense.

Tense Consistency Rules
Main ClauseSubordinate ClauseExample
Past tensePast tense (not present)He said that he was (not is) tired.
Present tenseAny tense (as per meaning)He says that he will come.
Future tensePresent tenseI will call you when I reach (not will reach) home.
Universal truthAlways present tenseThe teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.
the exam Trick — Timeline Method
Draw a mental timeline. If Action A happened before Action B (both in past), Action A = past perfect, Action B = simple past. If you see "had" without a subsequent simple past action, check for incorrect usage.

Time Expression Errors

Specific time expressions require specific tenses. the exam tests these combinations:

  • Since / For → Present perfect or past perfect (not simple past)
  • Yesterday / Last week / Ago → Simple past (not present perfect)
  • Already / Yet / Just → Present perfect
  • When / While → Past continuous for longer action
the exam 2019 — Time Expression
Identify the error:
I am living in Delhi (A)/ since 2015 and I (B)/ have never faced any (C)/ major problem here. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: a) A. "Since 2015" requires present perfect or present perfect continuous. Correct: "I have been living in Delhi since 2015."

Present Perfect vs Simple Past

A common the exam trap is using present perfect with a specific past time reference, or using simple past when the time is not specified. Present perfect connects past to present (no specific time). Simple past is for a completed action at a specific time.

the exam 2022 — Tense Trap
Improve the sentence:
"I have visited the museum yesterday."

a) I have visited the museum yesterday.
b) I visited the museum yesterday.
c) I had visited the museum yesterday.
d) No improvement needed
Solution: b) B. "Yesterday" is a specific past time, so simple past is required, not present perfect.

4. Misplaced & Dangling Modifiers

A modifier must be placed close to the word it modifies. A misplaced modifier is placed too far from its target, causing ambiguity. A dangling modifier has no clear target at all. Both are tested in the exam sentence improvement questions.

Misplaced Modifiers

Adjectives, adverbs, and modifying phrases must be placed next to the word they modify.

the exam 2020 — Misplaced Modifier
Improve the sentence:
"She nearly spent all her savings on the new car."

a) She spent nearly all her savings on the new car.
b) She nearly spent all her savings on the new car. (same)
c) She spent all her savings nearly on the new car.
d) No improvement needed
Solution: a) A. "Nearly" should modify "all" (the amount), not "spent". The original sentence implies she almost spent (but didn't), which is not the intended meaning.

Dangling Modifiers

A dangling modifier occurs when the subject of the modifier is not stated in the sentence. Fix by adding the missing subject.

Dangling Modifier Fixes
Incorrect (Dangling)Correct
Walking through the park, the flowers were beautiful.Walking through the park, I found the flowers beautiful.
Having finished the exam, the results were announced.Having finished the exam, the students waited for the results.
To pass the exam, hard work is essential.To pass the exam, you need to work hard.
Written in haste, the teacher found many errors.Written in haste, the essay had many errors.
the exam Trick — Who Is Doing?
For every introductory phrase followed by a comma, check: Is the next noun the one performing the action in the phrase? If not, you have a dangling modifier. E.g., "Being a holiday, the office was closed" → The office is not "being a holiday"! Correct: "Being a holiday, nobody was at the office."

5. Parallelism

Parallelism means using the same grammatical form for items in a list, comparison, or series. the exam tests this in error detection and sentence improvement, especially with correlative conjunctions and lists.

Lists in a Series

All items in a list must share the same grammatical form (all nouns, all gerunds, all infinitives, or all verb phrases).

the exam 2022 — Parallelism
Identify the error:
She enjoys reading books, (A)/ to write short stories, (B)/ and painting landscapes (C)/ in her free time. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. The list has three items: "reading books", "painting landscapes" (both gerunds), but "to write short stories" (infinitive). Correct: "writing short stories" for parallelism.

Correlative Conjunctions

Correlative pairs (not only…but also, either…or, neither…nor, both…and) must be followed by the same grammatical structure.

Parallelism with Correlative Conjunctions
PairIncorrectCorrect
not only…but alsoHe is not only tall but also he is strong.He is not only tall but also strong.
either…orYou can either pay by cash or using a card.You can either pay by cash or by card.
rather…thanI would rather to walk than taking a taxi.I would rather walk than take a taxi.
both…andThe film was both entertaining and it had suspense.The film was both entertaining and suspenseful.
the exam Trick — The "And" Check
Replace the comma before "and" with another "and". If the resulting phrase sounds unnatural, there is a parallelism error. For example, "reading, writing, and to paint" becomes "reading and writing and to paint" — the shift is obvious.

6. Comparison Errors

Comparisons in the exam require careful attention to logic, completeness, and parallel structure. The three common errors are: illogical comparison, incomplete comparison, and double comparatives.

Illogical Comparisons

You can only compare comparable items (apples to apples, not apples to oranges).

the exam 2020 — Illogical Comparison
Identify the error:
The population of Mumbai (A)/ is larger than (B)/ any city in (C)/ India. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: c) C. This compares "population of Mumbai" with "any city" (illogical). Correct: "larger than that of any other city in India" or "larger than any other city in India".

Double Comparatives and Superlatives

Never use "more" + "-er" or "most" + "-est" together. Also avoid "more better", "more taller", "most easiest" — all are incorrect.

Common Comparison Errors
ErrorIncorrectCorrect
Double comparativeThis is more better than that.This is better than that.
Double superlativeShe is the most tallest girl.She is the tallest girl.
Comparative + than (missing)He is more intelligent among the two.He is more intelligent than the other.
Superlative + among (two items)He is the best of the two.He is the better of the two. (comparative for two)
the exam Trick — Two vs Many
Use comparative (-er / more) when comparing TWO items. Use superlative (-est / most) when comparing THREE or MORE. Watch for "between" (two) vs "among" (many). E.g., "She is the taller of the two sisters."

Incomplete Comparisons

When using "than", ensure the comparison is complete. Some the exam questions test missing "any other" or "else" in comparative sentences.

the exam 2021 — Incomplete Comparison
Identify the error:
Gold is more precious (A)/ than any metal (B)/ found in the (C)/ earth’s crust. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. Gold is itself a metal, so "any other metal" is needed. Comparative sentences require "any other" or "else" when comparing with the same category.

8. Conjunction Errors

Conjunction errors in the exam involve incorrect use of coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS), subordinating conjunctions, and correlative conjunctions. The most common errors involve though/but, because/so, and unless/without combinations.

Though / Although + But (Double Conjunction)

"Though" and "but" cannot be used together in the same sentence. Use one or the other.

the exam 2019 — Double Conjunction
Identify the error:
Though he was tired (A)/ but he completed (B)/ the assignment before (C)/ the deadline. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. Remove "but". Use either "Though he was tired, he completed…" or "He was tired but he completed…". Both conjunctions in one sentence is incorrect.

Because / Since + So

Similarly, "because" and "so" cannot be used together. Use "because" (reason) or "so" (result), not both.

Conjunction Errors to Avoid
ErrorIncorrectCorrect
Though + butThough it rained but we went out.Though it rained, we went out. / It rained but we went out.
Because + soBecause he was late so he missed the bus.Because he was late, he missed the bus. / He was late so he missed the bus.
Unless + notUnless you do not work, you will fail.Unless you work, you will fail. / If you do not work, you will fail.
No sooner + thanNo sooner did he arrive when the meeting started.No sooner did he arrive than the meeting started.
Hardly + whenHardly had he arrived than the meeting started.Hardly had he arrived when the meeting started.
the exam Trick — One Conjunction Per Sentence
If a sentence has two clauses expressing contrast, use EITHER "though/although" OR "but" — never both. The same applies to "because/so" (cause-effect). This is a guaranteed the exam error detection pattern.

No Sooner & Hardly Constructions

These are high-frequency the exam structures that follow specific patterns:

  • No sooner + did/had + subject + verb + than + clause
  • Hardly + had + subject + verb (past participle) + when + clause
  • Scarcely + had + subject + verb (past participle) + when + clause
the exam 2022 — No Sooner
Identify the error:
No sooner did the teacher enter (A)/ the class when (B)/ the students stood (C)/ up to greet her. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. The correct structure is "No sooner…than". Replace "when" with "than".

10. Article Errors (A / An / The)

Article errors are a consistent feature of the exam error detection questions. The key is understanding the sound (not just the letter) that follows the article, and knowing the rules for definite vs. indefinite articles.

Indefinite Articles: A vs An

The choice between "a" and "an" depends on the sound that follows, not the spelling:

  • A is used before words that begin with a consonant sound: a university (you-ni-ver-si-ty), a European (you-ro-pe-an), a one-eyed man (wun-eyed), a house (hau-se)
  • An is used before words that begin with a vowel sound: an hour (our), an honest man, an M.A. degree (em-ay), an F.I.R. (ef-eye-ar), an umbrella (um-brel-la)
the exam Trick — Sound Rule
Always pronounce the word silently in your head before choosing a/an. the exam traps you with: "an university" (wrong — "university" starts with "you", a consonant sound), "a honest man" (wrong — "honest" starts with a vowel sound "on").
the exam 2021 — Article Sound Rule
Identify the error:
He is (A)/ a honest and (B)/ hardworking employee (C)/ of the company. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: b) B. "Honest" begins with a vowel sound (on-est), so the article should be an, not "a". Correct: "He is an honest and hardworking employee."

Definite Article: The

"The" is used for specific, definite, or previously mentioned nouns. the exam tests these specific rules:

Rules for Using "The" in the exam

Rivers, oceans, mountain rangesthe Ganges, the Pacific, the HimalayasSingle mountains: Mount Everest (no the)

the exam 2020 — Article Omission
Identify the error:
She plays (A)/ cricket and (B)/ the guitar very (C)/ well. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: No error. Cricket (sport) takes no article. Guitar (instrument) takes "the". This is correct usage.

Omission of Articles

Articles are omitted before: proper nouns (India, Ravi), uncountable nouns used generally (water, information, advice), meals (breakfast, lunch, dinner), languages (English, Hindi), subjects (Mathematics, History), and sports (cricket, football).

the exam 2022 — Article Omission
Identify the error:
The English is (A)/ a global language (B)/ spoken by millions (C)/ of people worldwide. (D) / No error (E)
Solution: a) A. Language names do not take "the" unless referring to a specific version. Correct: "English is a global language." Exception: "the English language" is also acceptable.

11. Conditional Sentences & Subjunctive Mood

Conditional sentences follow strict grammatical patterns. the exam frequently tests the third conditional (past unreal) and the subjunctive mood (wishes, as if, as though, it is time, would rather).

Conditional Types

Conditional Sentences — Complete Reference
TypeCondition (If clause)Result (Main clause)Example
Zero (General truth)If + present simplepresent simpleIf you heat ice, it melts.
First (Real future)If + present simplewill + base verbIf it rains, I will stay home.
Second (Unreal present)If + past simplewould + base verbIf I were rich, I would travel the world.
Third (Unreal past)If + past perfectwould have + V3If I had known, I would have come.
MixedIf + past perfectwould + base verbIf I had studied, I would be a doctor now.
the exam Trick — Conditional Error Spotting
In the exam, the most common conditional error is using "would" in the if-clause. The if-clause never takes "would" or "will". Incorrect: "If I would have known" → Correct: "If I had known". Also, use "were" (not "was") in unreal conditionals for all persons.

Subjunctive Mood

The subjunctive is used after certain verbs (suggest, recommend, insist, demand, propose, order, advise) and expressions (it is time, as if, as though, would rather, wish). In the subjunctive, the verb takes the base form regardless of the subject, and "were" is used instead of "was" for all persons.

the exam 2021 — Subjunctive Error
Identify the error:

Solution: b) B. After "suggest that", use the base form of the verb (subjunctive). Correct: "I suggest that he go to the doctor." Other verbs following this pattern: recommend, insist, demand, propose, order, advise.

Common the exam Error Detection Traps

Over years of analysis, these specific traps appear repeatedly in competitive exams. Being aware of them can help you avoid mistakes:

Conditional and Subjunctive Mood Nuances in Error Detection

Conditional sentences frequently cause subtle errors because they combine tense, mood, and logical relationship in one structure. The four standard types — zero, first, second, and third — each follow a fixed tense pattern: zero uses present simple in both clauses (If water reaches 100 °C, it boils); first uses present simple in the if‑clause and will + base verb in the main clause (If it rains, we will cancel); second uses past simple in the if‑clause and would + base verb in the main clause (If I knew, I would tell); third uses past perfect in the if‑clause and would + have + past participle in the main clause (If she had studied, she would have passed). Examiners often test mixed patterns, such as “If he would have arrived earlier, he could have caught the train,” which is ungrammatical because the if‑clause must not contain “would have.” The subjunctive mood appears in mandative constructions (It is essential that he be present) and contrary‑to‑fact wishes (I wish I were there). Errors arise when the indicative “is” or “was” replaces the subjunctive “be” or “were.” In formal writing, “were” is required for all persons in the present subjunctive (If I were you), while “was” occurs only in informal registers. Questions often embed a subjunctive trigger — “recommend that,” “insist that,” “suggest that” — followed by a clause that incorrectly uses “should” or a tensed verb. In American English, the bare infinitive (without “to”) is standard after these verbs; British English often permits “should + bare infinitive” but never a finite past tense. Recognising the trigger words and the required mood eliminates a large class of error‑detection questions.

Parallelism and Ellipsis: Advanced Patterns

Parallelism requires that items in a list, comparison, or coordinated structure share the same grammatical form. The basic rule — match gerunds with gerunds, infinitives with infinitives, clauses with clauses — extends to correlative conjunctions (both…and, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also). With a correlative pair, the elements immediately following each connector must be structurally identical. For example, “She not only sings beautifully but also dances gracefully” is correct because “sings beautifully” (verb + adverb) parallels “dances gracefully.” A frequent error inserts an extra auxiliary: “She not only can sing but also can dance” — the second “can” breaks parallelism because the first element lacks an auxiliary. Ellipsis, the omission of repeated words, is permissible only when the omitted material is recoverable without ambiguity. In “He likes swimming more than running,” the verb “likes” is ellipted before “running” and the meaning stays clear. However, “He likes swimming more than she” is ambiguous: it could mean “more than she likes swimming” or “more than he likes her.” The safe form repeats the verb or adds a pronoun: “more than she does” or “more than he likes her.” Comparative structures with “as…as” and “than” also require parallel phrasing: “The project is as difficult as it is time‑consuming” (adjective + as + clause) versus the faulty “The project is as difficult as time‑consuming.” Questions may place a long noun phrase before the first correlative element and a short phrase after the second, tempting the test‑taker to overlook the mismatch. Scanning for the first connector, then checking the immediate syntactic category of the following phrase, catches most of these errors.

Technique: Correlative Parallelism Check
Identify the error in the following sentence and choose the corrected version:
"The committee decided not only to review the policy but also implementing new guidelines."
The correlative pair “not only … but also” requires parallel forms after each connector. After “not only” we have the infinitive “to review”; after “but also” we have the gerund “implementing.” To restore parallelism, change “implementing” to the infinitive “to implement” (or change “to review” to “reviewing”). The corrected sentence: “The committee decided not only to review the policy but also to implement new guidelines.”
Exam‑Day Strategy for Parallelism
When a sentence contains a correlative conjunction, underline the two connectors and the words immediately following each. Ask whether the two fragments are the same part of speech and have the same internal structure. If one is an infinitive and the other a gerund, or one a noun phrase and the other a clause, the sentence is flawed. This two‑second visual check eliminates the need to re‑read the whole sentence.

Pronoun Reference and Ambiguity in Complex Sentences

Pronoun‑antecedent agreement is straightforward in simple sentences but becomes a major error source in multi‑clause constructions. The antecedent must be a single, clear noun or noun phrase matching the pronoun in number, gender, and person. With multiple potential antecedents, the pronoun should refer to the nearest grammatically logical noun. For example, “The manager told the employee that he would receive a bonus” is ambiguous: “he” could refer to the manager or the employee. Test makers often resolve ambiguity by adding a reflexive pronoun (“himself”) or restructuring: “The manager told the employee that the employee would receive a bonus.” Relative pronouns (who, which, that) must agree with their antecedents in animacy and number: “who” for people, “which” for things, “that” for either in restrictive clauses. A frequent error uses “which” for a person (“The scientist which discovered the element”) — the correct form is “who.” Indefinite pronouns (each, every, either, neither, anyone, everyone) are singular and require singular verbs and singular pronouns (“Each of the candidates has submitted his or her application”). Collective nouns (team, committee, jury) can be singular or plural depending on whether the group acts as a unit or as individuals; the verb and subsequent pronouns must follow the chosen interpretation consistently. Questions also test pronoun case in elliptical comparisons: “She is taller than I” (subject case) versus “She is taller than me” (object case). In formal writing, the subject case after “than” is preferred because the omitted verb “am” makes “I” the subject of the implied clause. Identifying the antecedent, checking number and gender, and verifying case in elliptical contexts cover the bulk of pronoun‑reference items.

Modifier Placement: Dangling, Misplaced, and Squinting Modifiers

Modifiers — adjectives, adverbs, phrases, or clauses — must be positioned next to the word they modify. A dangling modifier occurs when the introductory phrase has no logical subject in the main clause: “Running to catch the bus, the backpack fell off.” The implied subject of “Running” is a person, but the main‑clause subject is “the backpack.” The correction supplies the correct subject: “Running to catch the bus, I dropped the backpack.” A misplaced modifier attaches to the wrong word, altering meaning: “She only gave him a book” (implies she did nothing else) versus “She gave only him a book” (implies no one else received a book). Placing “only” directly before the element it limits is essential. Squinting modifiers (two‑way modifiers) sit between two possible heads, creating ambiguity: “The teacher said on Monday she would return the papers.” It is unclear whether “on Monday” modifies “said” or “would return.” Re‑phrasing removes the squint: “On Monday the teacher said she would return the papers” or “The teacher said she would return the papers on Monday.” Participial phrases at the start of a sentence are a classic dangling‑modifier trap; the subject of the main clause must be the doer of the participle. Prepositional phrases such as “with,” “by,” “using,” and “through” often misattach: “He solved the problem using a calculator” is fine because “he” uses the calculator; “Using a calculator, the problem was solved” dangles because the problem cannot use a calculator. Questions frequently use a long introductory clause followed by a passive main clause, guaranteeing a dangling modifier. The quick fix: convert the main clause to active voice with the appropriate subject, or rewrite the introductory phrase as a subordinate clause with its own subject.

Para Jumble Logic: Thematic Chains, Transition Markers, and Inference‑Based Ordering

Beyond identifying opening and closing sentences, high‑difficulty para jumbles require tracking thematic chains — recurring nouns, synonyms, or pronouns that bind sentences together. A thematic chain often starts with a proper noun or definite description in the first sentence and continues with pronouns (he, she, it, they) or demonstratives (this, that, these, those) in subsequent sentences. The chain must be unbroken; any sentence introducing a new entity without a clear link usually belongs later or earlier, not in the middle. Transition markers provide explicit logical cues: additive (furthermore, moreover, additionally), contrastive (however, nevertheless, on the other hand), causal (therefore, consequently, as a result), and temporal (subsequently, finally). When a sentence begins with a contrastive marker, the preceding sentence must present a claim being opposed. Causal markers demand a cause in the previous sentence and an effect in the current one. Inference‑based ordering applies when no explicit marker exists; the test‑taker deduces logical flow from content. For instance, a sentence describing a problem followed by one proposing a solution indicates a problem‑solution pair, even without “therefore.” Chronological markers (dates, “first,” “later,” “eventually”) enforce a time line. A reliable method: list all sentences, note first‑sentence candidates (general statements, definitions, introductions), note last‑sentence candidates (conclusions, present‑status statements, future implications), then build mandatory pairs using pronouns, demonstratives, and transition words. Once mandatory pairs are fixed, the remaining sentences slot into the only logical gaps. Practising with passages containing multiple thematic chains helps develop the ability to track several threads, which can improve accuracy under time pressure.

  • Treating “would have” in the if‑clause of a conditional as acceptable.
  • Using “which” for a human antecedent instead of “who.”
  • Allowing a singular verb after a collective noun when the context clearly indicates plural action.
  • Placing “only” before the verb when it should modify the object or adverbial phrase.
  • Assuming a pronoun refers to the nearest noun without checking gender or number agreement.
  • Ignoring the requirement that correlative conjunctions be followed by parallel structures.
  • Overlooking a dangling participial phrase because the main clause is passive.

Mastering these patterns helps identify subtle errors that often separate high-scoring candidates from others.

Tense Consistency and Sequence of Tenses

Maintaining tense consistency across a sentence or short passage is a core requirement in error‑detection items. The principle is simple: unless a shift in time frame is explicitly signalled by a temporal marker (e.g., “yesterday”, “by the time”, “when”), verb forms must remain in the same tense family. In complex sentences the sequence‑of‑tenses rule governs the relationship between the main‑clause verb and the verb in a subordinate clause. When the main verb is past, the subordinate verb normally back‑shifts: present becomes past, present perfect becomes past perfect, and future becomes conditional (would + base). For example, “She says that she is ready” becomes “She said that she was ready.” If the main verb is present perfect, the subordinate may stay in present or present perfect: “He has explained that the system works.” A common examiner trap embeds a reported‑speech clause inside a past‑tense narrative but leaves the subordinate verb in present tense, producing an inconsistency easy to miss when the clause is long.

Conditional constructions add another layer. In a third‑conditional (counterfactual past) the if‑clause uses past perfect and the main clause uses would have + past participle: “If they had arrived earlier, they would have caught the train.” Replacing the past perfect with simple past (“If they arrived earlier…”) changes the meaning to a second‑conditional and is an error when the context demands a past counterfactual. Similarly, mixed conditionals (e.g., “If she had studied harder, she would be a doctor now”) are acceptable only when the time frames genuinely differ; otherwise they signal a logical flaw.

Time‑adverbial clauses introduced by “when”, “while”, “before”, “after”, “until”, and “as soon as” obey the same back‑shifting logic. In a past narrative, “When he arrives, we will start” must become “When he arrived, we started” or “When he had arrived, we started.” The perfect aspect in the subordinate clause often indicates completion before the main‑clause action. Examiners frequently test this by inserting a present‑perfect verb in a past‑tense matrix: “After she has finished the report, she submitted it.” The correct form is “After she had finished the report, she submitted it.”

Aspectual consistency also matters. A sentence that mixes simple and progressive aspects without justification can be flagged. “He was reading the book when the phone rings” should be “He was reading the book when the phone rang.” The progressive “was reading” sets a past reference point; the following verb must align. However, a deliberate shift to present for a universal truth is permissible: “He said that the earth revolves around the sun.” The subordinate verb stays present because the fact is timeless.

Practical tip: when scanning a sentence, first locate the main‑clause tense. Then list every subordinate verb and check whether each follows the back‑shifting rule or is justified by a timeless‑truth or future‑in‑the‑past construction. Mark any verb that violates the pattern; that is the error.

Idiomatic Preposition Collocations in Formal English

Preposition choice is governed less by strict grammatical rules than by fixed collocations standard in formal writing. Certain verbs, adjectives, and nouns obligatorily select a specific preposition; substituting another creates an error even if the phrase remains intelligible. For instance, “accustomed to”, “averse to”, “conform to”, “adhere to”, and “subscribe to” all require “to”. Replacing “to” with “with” or “for” is a frequent distractor. Likewise, “capable of”, “fond of”, “tired of”, “aware of”, and “certain of” demand “of”. The verb “insist” takes “on” (“insist on doing”), whereas “persist” takes “in” (“persist in doing”). The adjective “different” traditionally pairs with “from” in formal British‑influenced English, though “different than” appears in American usage; many formal examinations treat “different than” as an error.

Noun‑preposition pairs are equally rigid. “Reason for”, “solution to”, “answer to”, “key to”, “access to”, “attitude toward”, “preference for”, “advantage of”, “disadvantage of”, and “effect on” are canonical. A common trap is “reason of” or “solution for”. The phrase “in regard to” (or “with regard to”) is correct; “in regards to” is considered non‑standard. “Based on” is the accepted form; “based off” or “based off of” are colloquial and flagged in formal tests.

Verb‑preposition idioms often involve phrasal verbs where the particle cannot be omitted or replaced. “Account for”, “bring about”, “call off”, “carry out”, “come across”, “deal with”, “look into”, “put up with”, “run out of”, and “set up” each have a fixed particle. In error‑detection items the particle may be dropped (“The committee will look the matter”) or swapped (“look into” vs. “look over”). Only the idiomatic particle yields a correct sentence.

Adjective‑preposition collocations also appear in comparative structures. “Similar to”, “identical with”, “equal to”, “superior to”, “inferior to”, “prior to”, “subsequent to” are standard. “Similar with” or “identical to” are errors. The preposition “than” follows comparative adjectives ending in “‑er” or “more”, but “different” does not take “than” in formal style.

When a sentence contains multiple prepositional phrases, each must be checked independently. A single wrong preposition invalidates the whole item. The most efficient scanning method is to isolate each verb‑adjective‑noun head and recall its obligatory preposition. If the head is unfamiliar, test the phrase by inserting a known correct collocate (e.g., “accustomed to”) and see whether the sentence still makes sense.

Technique: Preposition‑Error Isolation
Identify the erroneous preposition in the following sentence: “The manager insisted for a thorough review of the policy before the board meeting.”
The verb “insist” requires the preposition “on” when followed by a gerund or noun phrase. The phrase “insisted for” is non‑standard. Replace “for” with “on”: “The manager insisted on a thorough review of the policy before the board meeting.” The corrected sentence now follows the idiomatic collocation “insist on”.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Preposition Items
When a sentence contains a verb, adjective, or noun you recognise as a collocation head, immediately underline the following preposition. Compare it mentally with the canonical list (e.g., “accustomed to”, “averse to”, “capable of”). If the preposition differs, mark the error without reading the rest of the sentence. This targeted scan saves time and avoids distraction by surrounding clauses.

Redundancy, Wordiness, and Conciseness

Concise expression is a hallmark of high‑scoring sentence‑improvement answers. Redundancy occurs when a word or phrase repeats meaning already conveyed elsewhere. Classic examples include “free gift” (a gift is by definition free), “past history” (history is past), “future plans” (plans are inherently future‑oriented), “basic fundamentals”, “final outcome”, “unexpected surprise”, and “close proximity”. In formal writing each pair should be reduced to the single informative word: “gift”, “history”, “plans”, “fundamentals”, “outcome”, “surprise”, “proximity”.

Wordiness often manifests as unnecessary filler phrases: “in order to” shortens to “to”; “due to the fact that” reduces to “because”; “at this point in time” becomes “now”; “for the purpose of” simplifies to “for”; “in the event that” contracts to “if”. Examiners frequently embed such phrases in long sentences to test whether the candidate can spot the more economical alternative. The improvement option that removes the filler while preserving meaning is the correct choice.

Another source of verbosity is nominalisation where a strong verb would suffice. “Make a decision” → “decide”; “conduct an investigation” → “investigate”; “provide assistance” → “assist”; “give consideration to” → “consider”. Sentence‑improvement items often present a version with a weak verb + noun phrase and a version with the direct verb. The direct‑verb version is preferred because it reduces clause weight and improves clarity.

Passive constructions are not automatically wrong, but they often add words without adding information. “The report was prepared by the committee” (seven words) versus “The committee prepared the report” (six words). When both active and passive versions are grammatically sound, the active version is usually the better answer unless context specifically requires emphasis on the receiver of the action.

Parallelism interacts with conciseness. A list such as “to collect data, analyzing the results, and the preparation of the report” mixes infinitives, gerunds, and noun phrases. The concise parallel form “to collect data, analyze the results, and prepare the report” eliminates the nominalisation “preparation of” and aligns all three elements as bare infinitives.

When evaluating answer choices, first eliminate any option containing a redundant pair or filler phrase. Then compare remaining options for grammatical correctness and parallel structure. The shortest grammatically correct, parallel option is almost always the intended answer.

Sentence Boundary Errors: Fragments, Run‑ons, and Comma Splices

A complete sentence must contain at least one independent clause (subject + finite verb) and express a complete thought. A fragment lacks either a subject, a finite verb, or both, or is a subordinate clause standing alone. Common fragment patterns include a participial phrase without a main clause (“Running through the corridor, the door slammed shut”), a prepositional phrase (“After the meeting”), an appositive phrase (“A talented musician, dedicated to his craft”), or a dependent clause (“Because the data were incomplete”). The correction either attaches the fragment to a neighbouring independent clause or supplies the missing elements.

Run‑on sentences fuse two or more independent clauses without punctuation or conjunction. Example: “The experiment failed the equipment malfunctioned.” This can be repaired by inserting a period, a semicolon, a coordinating conjunction with a comma (“The experiment failed, for the equipment malfunctioned”), or a subordinating conjunction (“Because the equipment malfunctioned, the experiment failed”).

A comma splice is a specific run‑on where a comma alone joins independent clauses: “The results were significant, they supported the hypothesis.” The comma is insufficient; the same repair options as for run‑ons apply. Examiners often disguise a comma splice by inserting a conjunctive adverb (however, therefore, moreover) after the comma: “The results were significant, however they supported the hypothesis.” The correct punctuation before a conjunctive adverb is a semicolon or a period, and a comma follows the adverb: “The results were significant; however, they supported the hypothesis.”

Complex sentences with multiple subordinate clauses can mask boundary errors. Consider: “Although the sample size was small, the findings were robust, the researchers recommended further study.” Here the first clause is dependent, the second and third are independent but only a comma separates them — a comma splice. The fix: “Although the sample size was small, the findings were robust; the researchers recommended further study.”

Detection strategy: read the sentence aloud, pausing at each punctuation mark. If a pause coincides with a complete thought and the next segment also forms a complete thought, a boundary error exists unless a proper connector (semicolon, period, coordinating conjunction with comma, subordinating conjunction) is present. Mark the error and select the answer that supplies the correct connector or punctuation.

Advanced Comparative Structures: Double Comparatives and Illogical Comparisons

Comparative forms must obey strict morphological and logical rules. A double comparative occurs when both the “‑er/more” marker and the “more/less” marker appear together: “more better”, “more faster”, “less fewer”. Such constructions are always errors. The correct form uses only one comparative marker: “better”, “faster”, “fewer”. Similarly, a double superlative (“most best”, “most fastest”) is unacceptable.

Illogical comparisons arise when the items compared are not comparable or when the comparison omits the necessary “than” clause. “The climate of Mumbai is hotter than Delhi” compares a climate with a city. The correct version: “The climate of Mumbai is hotter than that of Delhi” or “Mumbai’s climate is hotter than Delhi’s.” The pronoun “that” or the possessive form ensures the compared entities match in category.

Comparisons involving “as…as” require parallel structure on both sides. “She is as intelligent, if not more intelligent, than her sister” mixes “as…as” with “than”. The correct pattern: “She is as intelligent as her sister, if not more intelligent.” Alternatively, “She is as intelligent as, if not more intelligent than, her sister” uses both correlatives correctly.

When a comparative adjective is followed by a prepositional phrase, the preposition must be “than” for “‑er/more” forms and “as” for “as…as”. Errors such as “more superior than” (superior already implies “more”) or “as better as” are common distractors. “Superior to”, “inferior to”, “prior to”, “subsequent to” are fixed collocations that do not take “than”.

Elliptical comparisons often hide the second element. “He runs faster than anyone in the class” is acceptable because the omitted “runs” is recoverable. However, “He runs faster than anyone” can be ambiguous if the antecedent is not a runner. The safe form adds the verb: “He runs faster than anyone else runs.” In formal tests, the version with the explicit verb or “else” is preferred when the antecedent could be misread.

Finally, comparative adverbs follow the same rules: “more quickly”, not “quicklier”; “most efficiently”, not “efficientliest”. The adverb “well” has irregular comparative “better” and superlative “best”. “Good” as an adjective follows “better/best”. Mixing adjective and adverb forms (“He performed good”) is an error; the adverb “well” is required.

  • Treating a double comparative such as “more better” as acceptable.
  • Comparing unlike categories without a pronoun or possessive (e.g., “climate hotter than city”).
  • Using “than” after “as…as” or “as” after “‑er/more” forms.
  • Omitting the necessary “else” in elliptical comparisons when the antecedent includes the subject.
  • Confusing adjective and adverb comparative forms (e.g., “good” for “well”).
  • Applying “more” to absolute adjectives like “unique”, “perfect”, “complete”.
  • Failing to match the preposition in fixed comparative collocations such as “superior to”.

Inversion and Fronting for Emphasis in Formal English

Inversion — placing the auxiliary or modal verb before the subject — marks formal written English and frequently appears in error‑detection items. The most common trigger is a fronted negative or restrictive adverbial: Never have I seen such dedication; Rarely does the committee meet without a quorum; Not only did the report highlight the deficit, but it also proposed a remedy. In each case the auxiliary (have, does, did) precedes the subject (I, the committee, the report). When the main verb is a simple present or past tense, the dummy auxiliary do/does/did is inserted: Seldom do they agree on policy. Tests frequently target failure to invert after only + prepositional phrase (Only after the audit was completed did the board approve the budget) or after so + adjective (So intense was the pressure that the material fractured).

Fronting without inversion occurs when a non‑negative adverbial or complement is fronted for cohesion: In the laboratory the researchers calibrated the instruments. No auxiliary precedes the subject because the fronted element is not a restrictive negative. Errors arise when candidates incorrectly invert after neutral fronting (*In the laboratory did the researchers calibrate). Another trap is the “Not until” construction: inversion appears in the main clause, not the subordinate clause — Not until the data were verified did the team publish the results. The subordinate clause retains normal order (the data were verified).

Conditional sentences with had, were, or should also invert when if is omitted: Had the experiment failed, we would have revised the protocol; Were the results significant, the paper would be accepted; Should you need assistance, contact the coordinator. The inverted form is strictly formal; the non‑inverted If the experiment had failed… is acceptable informally but rarely appears in error‑detection sections.

Parallelism must be preserved when multiple fronted elements share an inversion: Not only did the manager approve the plan, but did she also allocate the budget. The second did is often omitted in speech but required in formal writing. Inversion after so + adjective + that (So rapid was the growth that the server crashed) and after such + noun + that (Such was the confusion that the meeting was adjourned) follows the same auxiliary‑subject rule.

Gerund–Infinitive Selection after Verbs and Adjectives

The choice between a gerund (‑ing form) and a to‑infinitive after certain verbs, adjectives, and nouns follows lexical collocation, not a single syntactic rule. Verbs of perception (see, hear, watch, notice) take a bare infinitive for completed actions (I saw him leave) and a gerund for ongoing actions (I saw him leaving). Verbs of preference (like, love, hate, prefer) accept both forms with a meaning shift: I like to swim (habitual) versus I like swimming (enjoyment). Verbs of remembering and forgetting (remember, forget, regret) change meaning: remember to do (future obligation) vs remember doing (past memory); forget to do (omitted duty) vs forget doing (past action lost); regret to inform (formal announcement) vs regret informing (remorse).

Adjectives followed by prepositions often dictate the form: interested in + gerund, capable of + gerund, responsible for + gerund, accustomed to + gerund (note: to is a preposition here). Conversely, adjectives such as eager, willing, reluctant, anxious take a to‑infinitive: eager to participate. Nouns like decision, attempt, plan, proposal combine with to‑infinitives (the decision to expand), whereas nouns such as habit, risk, chance, possibility often pair with gerunds (the habit of smoking, the risk of failing).

Complex catenative verbs — go on, stop, try, mean — illustrate meaning‑dependent selection: go on to do (next action) vs go on doing (continue); stop to do (pause for purpose) vs stop doing (cease); try to do (attempt) vs try doing (experiment); mean to do (intend) vs mean doing (imply). Error‑detection items frequently test the wrong form after these verbs, especially when the governing verb is separated from its complement by intervening clauses.

Passive constructions add another layer: He was seen to leave (infinitive) but He was seen leaving (gerund) — both grammatical, but the infinitive emphasizes the complete event, the gerund the ongoing process. In formal writing, the infinitive after passive perception verbs is preferred for completed actions. Candidates must also watch for “help,” which allows both help do and help to do (bare infinitive more common in American English, to‑infinitive in British English); tests usually accept either unless a style guide is specified.

Error‑Detection Worked Example
Identify the error, if any, in the following sentence:
“The committee regretted to announce that the project had been delayed due to unforeseen circumstances, and the members suggested postponing the launch until next quarter.”
Step 1: Locate each verb‑complement pair.
Pair 1: regretted to announce. Regret + to‑infinitive is used only for formal notifications (We regret to inform you). Here the committee feels remorse about a past decision, so the correct form is regretted announcing (gerund).
Pair 2: had been delayed — correct past perfect passive.
Pair 3: suggested postponing. Suggest takes a gerund (or a that‑clause), never an infinitive. This pair is correct.
Error: regretted to announce should be regretted announcing. The corrected sentence: “The committee regretted announcing that the project had been delayed due to unforeseen circumstances, and the members suggested postponing the launch until next quarter.”
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a sentence contains multiple verb‑complement structures, isolate each governing verb first. Write the verb in the margin and recall its collocation class (gerund‑only, infinitive‑only, both with meaning change). This prevents the “first‑error‑wins” trap where a later, subtler error is overlooked because the earlier one seems obvious.

Article Usage with Abstract Nouns, Institutions, and Unique Entities

Abstract nouns — justice, honesty, information, evidence — are generally uncountable and appear without an article in generic use: Justice delayed is justice denied. When a specific instance is identified by a following of-phrase or restrictive relative clause, the definite article appears: The justice of the Supreme Court’s ruling, The honesty displayed by the witness. Errors arise when candidates insert a/an before uncountable abstracts (*a justice) or omit the when the noun is particularized (*Justice of the ruling was swift).

Institutions such as school, college, university, hospital, prison, church, court, parliament follow a pattern: zero article for the institution’s primary purpose (go to school, be in hospital, attend church), but the for the building or a specific visit (go to the school to meet the principal, visit the hospital where she works). Exams test this distinction with sentences like “She was admitted to hospital for observation” (correct) versus “She was admitted to the hospital for observation” (incorrect unless a particular hospital is in focus).

Unique entities — the sun, the moon, the earth, the equator, the north pole, the internet, the world, the universe — always take the as they denote a single, globally recognized referent. Conversely, celestial bodies with proper names (Mars, Venus, Jupiter) take zero article. Geographic features follow set conventions: rivers, seas, mountain ranges, island groups, deserts, and canals take the (the Ganges, the Himalayas, the Sahara); individual mountains, lakes, and islands usually take zero article (Mount Everest, Lake Baikal, Madagascar). Errors often appear in para‑jumble sentences where a later sentence uses a previously introduced geographic name with the wrong article.

Proper nouns modified by a restrictive adjective phrase may acquire the: the Shakespeare of the 16th century, the India of my childhood. When the modifier is non‑restrictive (appositive), the article is omitted: Shakespeare, the greatest playwright, wrote Hamlet. Recognizing restrictive versus non‑restrictive modifiers is essential for error detection and sentence improvement.

Cohesive Devices in Para Jumbles: Lexical Chains, Referential Links, and Discourse Markers

Para‑jumble ordering relies on identifying logical flow through cohesive devices. Lexical chains — repetitions, synonyms, antonyms, and hyponym/hypernym relations — bind sentences across paragraphs. For example, a sentence introducing “urbanisation” may be followed by “city growth”, then “metropolitan expansion”, and finally “this phenomenon”. The chain signals the sequence. Candidates should underline recurring content words and map their first appearance (usually the topic sentence) to later references.

Referential links include pronouns (it, they, this, that, such), demonstratives, and definite noun phrases pointing backward. A sentence beginning with “This approach” must follow one that introduced an approach. “Such measures” requires a preceding list. Ordering errors often place a referential sentence before its antecedent, creating a dangling reference. Test‑makers may insert a cataphoric reference (“The following factors explain the trend:”) which must precede the enumeration.

Discourse markers — however, therefore, moreover, consequently, in contrast, for instance, in addition — signal rhetorical relations between adjacent sentences. However signals contrast with the preceding idea; therefore marks a conclusion from the prior statement; for instance introduces an example following a general claim. When multiple markers appear, a hierarchy of relations (contrast > addition > exemplification) helps determine the linear order. A paragraph with both “Moreover” and “However” typically places “Moreover” after a supporting point and “However” after the point it contradicts.

Temporal and causal connectors (subsequently, previously, as a result, because) impose a chronological or logical sequence. In scientific abstracts, the order “Background → Method → Results → Conclusion” is nearly invariant; sentences violating this macro‑structure are misplaced. Practising with annotated paragraphs where cohesive devices are labelled helps accelerate pattern recognition.

Punctuation with Parenthetical Elements, Dashes, and Semicolons in Complex Sentences

Parenthetical elements — words, phrases, or clauses inserted for clarification, comment, or emphasis — must be set off by paired commas, dashes, or parentheses. The choice affects tone: commas are neutral, dashes add emphasis or signal a break, parentheses downplay the insertion. Errors occur when only one delimiter is used (*The results, however were inconclusive) or when delimiters are mismatched (*The results — however, were inconclusive). The rule: the same punctuation mark must open and close the parenthetical.

Non‑restrictive relative clauses (beginning with which, who, whose and not essential to meaning) are parenthetical and require commas: The report, which was submitted late, contained errors. Restrictive clauses (essential identification) take no commas: The report that was submitted late contained errors. Confusing the two changes meaning and is a staple of error‑detection items.

Semicolons join closely related independent clauses without a coordinating conjunction. They also separate items in a complex list where items contain commas: The committee comprised Dr. Rao, cardiologist; Ms. Patel, neurologist; and Mr. Singh, oncologist. A common trap is using a semicolon before a conjunctive adverb without a following comma (*The data were incomplete; therefore the study was retracted → correct: The data were incomplete; therefore, the study was retracted).

Dashes can replace commas, semicolons, or colons for dramatic effect. A single dash may introduce a summary or afterthought: The experiment failed — the equipment malfunctioned. Two dashes enclose an emphatic insertion: The results — contrary to expectations — supported the hypothesis. Overuse of dashes in formal writing is penalised in sentence‑improvement tasks; the preferred formal alternative is paired commas or a semicolon‑colon combination.

Finally, the colon introduces an explanation, list, or quotation following an independent clause. The clause before the colon must be complete: The study examined three variables: temperature, pressure, and humidity. Using a colon after a fragment (*The variables studied were: temperature, pressure, and humidity) is incorrect because “were” requires a complement. Checking the grammatical completeness of the preceding clause is the quickest check for colon errors.

  • Treating a fronted negative adverbial as optional for inversion (e.g., “Rarely the committee meets” instead of “Rarely does the committee meet”).
  • Using a gerund after verbs requiring a to‑infinitive for future‑oriented meaning (e.g., “regret to announce” vs “regret announcing”).
  • Omitting the definite article before a particularized abstract noun or unique entity (e.g., “Justice of the ruling” instead of “The justice of the ruling”).
  • Placing a referential sentence before its antecedent in a para‑jumble, creating a dangling pronoun or demonstrative.
  • Mismatching parenthetical delimiters (e.g., opening with a comma, closing with a dash) or using a single delimiter.
  • Inserting a semicolon before a conjunctive adverb without the required following comma.
  • Using a colon after a verb or preposition that governs the following list, violating the independent‑clause rule.

Agreement with Collective Nouns, Quantifiers, and Partitive Constructions

Collective nouns such as committee, jury, fleet, staff trigger singular verb agreement when the group acts as a unit, but plural agreement when the members act individually. The decisive factor is whether the context emphasizes unity or distribution. Quantifiers like a number of, a majority of, a variety of behave similarly: a number of takes a plural verb because the focus is on the countable items, whereas the number of takes a singular verb because the phrase denotes a single statistic. Partitive constructions — half of, most of, some of, all of — follow the noun that follows the preposition. If the noun is uncountable or singular, the verb is singular (Half of the cake is gone); if the noun is plural, the verb is plural (Half of the cakes are gone). Errors arise when test‑makers embed a collective noun inside a longer subject phrase and then insert a distracting plural noun nearer the verb, tempting the examinee to match the verb to the nearest noun rather than the head of the subject.

Indefinite pronouns such as each, every, either, neither, none are traditionally singular, yet none can be plural when the sense is “not any of them” and the referent is plural (None of the candidates are qualified). The pronoun all follows the same partitive rule: All of the money is spent versus All of the books are missing. When a quantifier is modified by a prepositional phrase containing a plural noun, the verb must still agree with the quantifier’s inherent number, not with the object of the preposition. For example, A majority of the voters support the bill (plural verb with a majority of), but The majority of voters supports the bill (singular verb when the collective sense is intended). Recognising the head noun of the subject phrase and the semantic force of the quantifier helps avoid the most common agreement traps.

Practice items often combine a collective noun with a correlative conjunction (not only … but also) or an appositive that adds a plural noun. The verb must agree with the subject that precedes the correlative pair, not with the noun inserted after but also. Example: The committee, together with its advisors, has reached a decision — the verb is singular because the true subject is committee. Mastery of these patterns requires isolating the grammatical subject, ignoring intervening modifiers, and applying the semantic rule that governs each quantifier or collective noun.

Verb Complementation Patterns: Perception, Causative, and Resultative Structures

Verbs of perception (see, hear, watch, notice, observe) admit two complement types: a bare infinitive (for a complete event) and a present participle (for an ongoing action). I saw him leave implies the whole departure was witnessed; I saw him leaving stresses the process. The choice changes meaning and is a frequent error‑detection target. Causative verbs make, let, have, get follow distinct patterns: make and let take a bare infinitive (She made him apologize), have takes a bare infinitive when the agent is a person (I had him repair the roof) but a past participle when the object receives the action (I had the roof repaired), and get takes a to‑infinitive with a personal agent (I got him to repair the roof) or a past participle for a non‑personal object (I got the roof repaired). Confusing these patterns produces ungrammatical sentences such as *She made him to apologize or *I had the roof repair.

Resultative constructions pair a verb with an adjective or prepositional phrase that describes the resultant state of the object: He painted the wall red, She wiped the table clean. The resultative phrase must be compatible with the verb’s semantics; verbs that do not denote a change of state (e.g., consider, regard) cannot host resultatives. A common trap inserts a resultative after a stative verb: *He considered the problem solved is acceptable only when considered means “deemed”, but *He knew the answer correct is impossible. Examiners also test the distinction between help + bare infinitive (help him finish) and help + to‑infinitive (help him to finish); both are grammatical, but the bare form is preferred in formal writing and appears more often in correct‑answer choices.

When a perception or causative verb is passivised, the complement type shifts: the bare infinitive becomes a to‑infinitive (He was seen to leave, He was made to apologize). The participle complement remains a participle (He was seen leaving). Recognising the passive transformation rule prevents errors where a test item retains the bare infinitive after a passive perception verb. Additionally, verbs like force, compel, allow, enable follow the to‑infinitive pattern in both active and passive voices (They forced him to resign / He was forced to resign). Mastery of these complementation matrices helps eliminate answer choices that violate the verb‑specific pattern.

Technique: Complementation Matrix Check
Identify the error in the following sentence: "The manager let the employees to leave early because the project was completed ahead of schedule."
The verb let is a causative verb that requires a bare infinitive, not a to‑infinitive. The correct form is "The manager let the employees leave early because the project was completed ahead of schedule." The error is the insertion of to after let.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a sentence contains a perception, causative, or resultative verb, first label the verb type. Then recall its obligatory complement (bare infinitive, to‑infinitive, participle, or past participle). Scan the answer choices for any deviation from that complement; the choice that restores the verb‑specific pattern is almost always the correct improvement.

Nominalization, Clause Reduction, and the Grammar of Reduced Relative Clauses

Nominalization converts verbs or adjectives into nouns, often producing dense, abstract phrasing that test‑makers exploit for conciseness errors. A clause such as the fact that the committee approved the proposal can be reduced to the committee’s approval of the proposal or the approval of the proposal by the committee. While nominalizations are grammatically sound, they frequently create wordiness and obscure agency. In sentence‑improvement items, the preferred revision replaces a heavy nominalization with an active verb: The implementation of the plan was carried out by the team → The team implemented the plan. Recognising the verb hidden inside a noun (e.g., decision → decide, analysis → analyze) enables rapid condensation.

Reduced relative clauses omit the relative pronoun and the auxiliary verb, leaving a participle or a prepositional phrase. A full relative clause the report that was submitted late reduces to the report submitted late (past participle) when the clause is passive, or to the report submitting late (present participle) when the antecedent is the agent — though the latter is rare and often awkward. The reduction is permissible only when the relative clause is restrictive and the verb is either a form of be + past participle (passive) or a progressive active form. Errors appear when a non‑restrictive clause is reduced without commas, or when the reduction creates a restrictive reading. A non‑restrictive clause can be reduced to a participle phrase set off by commas (The report, submitted late, contained errors), but the relative pronoun and auxiliary must be omitted. Also, a reduced clause cannot retain a modal or perfect aspect: *The report having been submitted late is a perfect participle phrase, not a reduced relative, and must be punctuated as a separate adjunct.

Clause reduction also applies to adverbial clauses introduced by subordinators such as while, when, after, before. While he was walking home, he saw a fox reduces to While walking home, he saw a fox. The subject of the reduced clause must be identical to the subject of the main clause; otherwise a dangling modifier results (*While walking home, the fox appeared). Test items often embed a reduced adverbial clause with a mismatched subject to create a dangling modifier error. The correction either restores the full clause or changes the main‑clause subject to match the implied subject of the participle.

In para‑jumbles, nominalized sentences tend to serve as topic sentences because they package information densely, while reduced relative clauses often appear as post‑modifiers within a sentence rather than as independent sentences. Recognising the syntactic weight of a nominalized phrase helps predict its position in the logical flow: heavy noun phrases usually precede the verbs that unpack them.

Information Structure in Para Jumbles: Given‑New Ordering, Topic‑Comment Chains, and Thematic Progression

Paragraph coherence relies on the principle that each sentence typically begins with given (known) information and ends with new information. The given element often repeats a noun, pronoun, or synonym from the preceding sentence, creating a referential chain. For example, The committee reviewed the draft. It recommended several changes. The pronoun It picks up The committee as given, while recommended several changes supplies new content. When a sentence opens with a brand‑new entity without any prior anchor, it usually functions as a paragraph opener or a topic‑shifting sentence. Test‑makers exploit this by placing a sentence with a fresh proper noun in the middle of a jumbled set; the correct order must relocate it to the start or to a point where a preceding sentence introduces that entity.

Topic‑comment structure extends the given‑new principle: the topic (often the subject) announces what the sentence is about, and the comment provides the predicate information. In a well‑ordered paragraph, the topic of sentence n+1 frequently derives from the comment of sentence n. Consider: The new policy reduces emissions. This reduction improves air quality. The comment reduces emissions yields the topic This reduction for the next sentence. When a para‑jumble contains a sentence whose subject is a demonstrative (This, These, Such) or a definite noun phrase (The reduction), that sentence must follow the sentence that supplies its antecedent. Misordering creates a dangling demonstrative, a classic error.

Thematic progression patterns — constant theme, linear theme, and derived theme — provide a macro‑level map. Constant theme repeats the same subject across sentences (The algorithm… The algorithm… The algorithm…). Linear theme shifts the theme forward by making the previous comment the next theme (The algorithm processes data. The processed data feeds the model.). Derived theme splits a complex theme into sub‑themes (The system comprises three modules. Module A handles input. Module B performs analysis.). Identifying which pattern a paragraph follows allows rapid sequencing: constant‑theme paragraphs keep the same subject; linear‑theme paragraphs chain comment‑to‑topic; derived‑theme paragraphs branch from a superordinate sentence. Examiners often mix these patterns, requiring the test‑taker to detect the dominant progression and place outlier sentences accordingly.

Discourse markers reinforce the information structure. Additive markers (furthermore, moreover, in addition) signal continuation of the same theme; contrastive markers (however, on the other hand) indicate a shift to a counter‑theme; causal markers (therefore, consequently) mark a linear inference step. When a sentence begins with however, its topic must contrast with the comment of the preceding sentence. When a sentence begins with therefore, its topic must be the logical consequence of the prior comment. Using these markers as ordering clues, combined with given‑new chaining, helps determine the sequence in most high‑difficulty para‑jumbles.

Mixed Constructions and Faulty Predication: Identifying Illogical Subject‑Predicate Pairings

A mixed construction occurs when a sentence begins with one syntactic structure and switches to another mid‑way, producing a subject that cannot logically perform the predicate. Classic examples include *The reason for the delay is because the train broke down (subject reason cannot be because clause; correct: The reason for the delay is that the train broke down or The train was delayed because it broke down). Another frequent pattern: *By studying late into the night caused his fatigue (prepositional phrase By studying… cannot serve as subject of caused; correct: Studying late into the night caused his fatigue). These errors test the ability to match the grammatical subject with a semantically compatible predicate.

Faulty predication also appears with nouns that denote abstract concepts paired with action verbs: *The decision approved the budget is faulty because a decision does not perform the act of approving; correct: The decision was to approve the budget or The decision led to approval of the budget. More clear‑cut: *The book reads easily is acceptable as a middle construction, but *The book reads the student is nonsense. Examiners often embed a noun like analysis, evaluation, consideration as subject of a verb that requires an animate agent (*The analysis concluded the results → correct: The analysis led to the conclusion or The researchers concluded).

Another variant involves is when and is where definitions: *Democracy is when people vote (definition requires a noun phrase after is; correct: Democracy is a system in which people vote). Similarly, *The problem is where the pipe leaks should be The problem is the place where the pipe leaks or The pipe leaks at the problem point. Recognising that is equates two noun phrases, not a noun phrase with an adverbial clause, helps eliminate these errors.

In sentence‑improvement tasks, the correct answer typically restores a clear subject‑predicate match by either converting the subject to a suitable noun (gerund, infinitive, or noun clause) or by rephrasing the predicate to fit the existing subject. The quickest diagnostic: isolate the subject, ask “Can this subject logically do or be what the predicate says?” If the answer is no, the construction is mixed or faulty.

  • Treating a collective noun as plural when the context demands singular unity, or vice‑versa, ignoring the semantic force of the quantifier.
  • Using a to‑infinitive after causative make or let, or a bare infinitive after get with a personal agent.
  • Reducing a non‑restrictive relative clause without commas, creating a restrictive reading.
  • Placing a sentence with a fresh proper noun or demonstrative in the middle of a para‑jumble where no antecedent exists.
  • Writing reason … is because or definition … is when/where instead of a noun‑clause complement.
  • Allowing a dangling participle by reducing an adverbial clause whose implied subject differs from the main‑clause subject.
  • Pairing an abstract noun subject with an action verb that requires an animate agent, producing faulty predication.
