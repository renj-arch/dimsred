1. Active & Passive Voice

What is Voice?

Voice indicates whether the subject performs the action (Active) or receives the action (Passive). Only transitive verbs — verbs that take a direct object — can be converted from active to passive. Intransitive verbs (e.g., sleep, arrive, die) cannot be used in passive voice.

SSC CGL Shortcut
In SSC CGL Tier 2, voice questions typically appear in the "Sentence Improvement" and "Active-Passive" sections. The golden rule: identify the tense first, then apply the correct be-verb + past participle structure.

Rules for Voice Change

All Tenses — Active to Passive Conversion

The table below shows the transformation pattern for every tense. The object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive sentence; the subject of the active sentence becomes the agent introduced by by.

Tense Active Voice Passive Voice
Simple Present She writes a letter. A letter is written by her.
Present Continuous She is writing a letter. A letter is being written by her.
Present Perfect She has written a letter. A letter has been written by her.
Present Perfect Continuous She has been writing a letter. A letter has been being written by her. (Rarely used)
Simple Past She wrote a letter. A letter was written by her.
Past Continuous She was writing a letter. A letter was being written by her.
Past Perfect She had written a letter. A letter had been written by her.
Past Perfect Continuous She had been writing a letter. A letter had been being written by her. (Rarely used)
Simple Future She will write a letter. A letter will be written by her.
Future Perfect She will have written a letter. A letter will have been written by her.
SSC CGL Tip — Continuous Tenses
Present Perfect Continuous, Past Perfect Continuous, and Future Continuous are rarely used in passive voice in SSC CGL exams. If they appear, the passive form becomes cumbersome (e.g., has been being written). Most exam questions avoid these tenses in passive.
Example 1 — SSC CGL 2022 (Simple Present)
Convert to passive: The gardener waters the plants every morning.

Options:
a) The plants are watered by the gardener every morning.
b) The plants were watered by the gardener every morning.
c) The plants are being watered by the gardener every morning.
d) The plants have been watered by the gardener every morning.
Answer: a) The plants are watered by the gardener every morning.
Simple Present: waters → is/are + V3. Since "plants" is plural, use "are watered".
Example 2 — SSC CGL 2021 (Past Continuous)
Convert to passive: The children were flying kites in the park.

Options:
a) Kites were flown in the park by the children.
b) Kites were being flown in the park by the children.
c) Kites are being flown in the park by the children.
d) Kites had been flown in the park by the children.
Answer: b) Kites were being flown in the park by the children.
Rule: Past Continuous (was/were + V-ing) → was/were + being + V3.
Example 3 — SSC CGL 2020 (Future Perfect)
Convert to passive: The team will have finished the project by Monday.

Options:
a) The project will be finished by the team by Monday.
b) The project will have been finished by the team by Monday.
c) The project will have finished by the team by Monday.
d) The project has been finished by the team by Monday.
Answer: b) The project will have been finished by the team by Monday.
Future Perfect: will have + V3 → will have been + V3.

With Modal Verbs

Modal verbs (can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must, ought to, need, dare, used to) follow a fixed passive pattern: modal + be + V3. For perfect modals: modal + have been + V3.

Modal Active Voice Passive Voice
canI can solve this problem.This problem can be solved by me.
couldHe could lift the box.The box could be lifted by him.
mayShe may attend the meeting.The meeting may be attended by her.
mightThey might accept the offer.The offer might be accepted by them.
shallI shall finish the work.The work shall be finished by me.
shouldYou should obey the rules.The rules should be obeyed by you.
willShe will sign the papers.The papers will be signed by her.
wouldHe would help the poor.The poor would be helped by him.
mustWe must protect the environment.The environment must be protected by us.
ought toYou ought to respect elders.Elders ought to be respected by you.
needYou need to submit the form.The form needs to be submitted by you.
used toHe used to read novels.Novels used to be read by him.
can haveShe can have done it.It could have been done by her.
SSC CGL Trick — Modals
For modal-based passive questions in SSC CGL, always check two things: (1) the modal verb remains unchanged, (2) the main verb changes to be + V3. The only exception is ought to / used to + infinitive, which becomes ought to be / used to be + V3.
Example 4 — SSC CGL Modal Passive
Convert to passive: The government must implement these reforms immediately.

Options:
a) These reforms must be implemented by the government immediately.
b) These reforms must implemented by the government immediately.
c) These reforms must been implemented by the government immediately.
d) These reforms are implemented by the government immediately.
Answer: a) These reforms must be implemented by the government immediately.
Rule: must + V1 → must + be + V3.
Example 5 — SSC CGL Modal Perfect
Convert to passive: The manager could have approved the request.

Options:
a) The request could have approved by the manager.
b) The request could be approved by the manager.
c) The request could have been approved by the manager.
d) The request could been approved by the manager.
Answer: c) The request could have been approved by the manager.
Rule: could have + V3 → could have been + V3.

Imperative Sentences

Imperative sentences express commands, requests, advice, or suggestions. The passive construction varies depending on the type of imperative.

Type Active Passive
Command / OrderClose the door.Let the door be closed.
Request (please)Please help me.You are requested to help me.
Advice / SuggestionTake this medicine.You are advised to take this medicine.
Negative CommandDo not smoke here.You are forbidden to smoke here. / Let not smoking be done here.
Let + ObjectLet him do the work.Let the work be done by him.
SSC CGL Tip — Imperative Passive
For imperative sentences, SSC CGL usually tests the "Let + object + be + V3" pattern or "You are requested/advised/ordered to + V1" pattern. Identify the tone (command vs request vs advice) to pick the correct passive form.
Example 6 — SSC CGL Imperative Passive
Convert to passive: Please bring me a glass of water.

Options:
a) Let a glass of water be brought to me.
b) You are requested to bring me a glass of water.
c) You are ordered to bring me a glass of water.
d) A glass of water is brought by me.
Answer: b) You are requested to bring me a glass of water.
"Please" indicates a request, so use "You are requested to..."

Interrogative Sentences

For interrogative sentences, the passive conversion must preserve the question structure. There are two types: Yes/No questions and WH-questions.

Question Type Active Passive
Yes/No (Is/Are/Do/Does)Does she write poems?Are poems written by her?
Yes/No (Was/Were/Did)Did they complete the work?Was the work completed by them?
Yes/No (Has/Have/Had)Have you seen the movie?Has the movie been seen by you?
Yes/No (Modal)Can he solve this sum?Can this sum be solved by him?
WH- (What/Which)What did she buy?What was bought by her?
WH- (Who — object)Who wrote this letter?By whom was this letter written?
WH- (Whom — subject in passive)Whom did you call?Who was called by you?
SSC CGL Trick — WH-Questions
Special attention: when the active sentence begins with "Who" (subject), the passive becomes "By whom". Example: Who wrote this? → By whom was this written? This is a frequently tested pattern in SSC CGL.
Example 7 — SSC CGL Interrogative Passive
Convert to passive: Who taught you English?

Options:
a) By whom was English taught to you?
b) Whom was English taught by?
c) Who was taught English by you?
d) By who was English taught to you?
Answer: a) By whom was English taught to you?
"Who" (subject) in active → "By whom" at the start of passive sentence.

Verbs with Two Objects

Some verbs (give, send, offer, tell, teach, show, promise, buy, lend, etc.) take both a direct object (thing) and an indirect object (person). Two passive forms are possible.

Active Passive 1 (indirect → subject) Passive 2 (direct → subject)
She gave me a book.I was given a book by her.A book was given to me by her.
He taught us grammar.We were taught grammar by him.Grammar was taught to us by him.
They offered him a job.He was offered a job by them.A job was offered to him by them.
Mother bought me a dress.I was bought a dress by mother.A dress was bought for me by mother.
SSC CGL Tip — Two Objects
SSC CGL often presents both passive forms as options. In formal English, the indirect object (person) as subject is more common. E.g., "I was given a book" is preferred over "A book was given to me". Note the preposition: use to for give/send/offer/tell; use for with buy/make/get.
Example 8 — SSC CGL Two Objects
Convert to passive: The company offered Mr. Sharma a handsome salary.

Options:
a) Mr. Sharma was offered a handsome salary by the company.
b) A handsome salary offered Mr. Sharma by the company.
c) Mr. Sharma is offered a handsome salary by the company.
d) A handsome salary was offering Mr. Sharma by the company.
Answer: a) Mr. Sharma was offered a handsome salary by the company.
Indirect object (Mr. Sharma → subject). Simple Past (offered) → was offered.

Prepositional Verbs

Some verbs are followed by a fixed preposition (laugh at, look at, send for, care for, call on, deal with, rely on, etc.). In passive voice, the preposition is retained immediately after the past participle.

Active Passive
The children laughed at the clown.The clown was laughed at by the children.
She looks after her grandmother.Her grandmother is looked after by her.
We sent for the doctor.The doctor was sent for by us.
Nobody cares for such remarks.Such remarks are not cared for by anybody.
They dealt with the problem.The problem was dealt with by them.
He called on his friend.His friend was called on by him.
SSC CGL Warning — Preposition Retention
Never drop the preposition when converting to passive. A common SSC CGL trick is to present an option without the preposition (e.g., "The clown was laughed by the children"). This is always WRONG. The preposition must stay with the verb.
Example 9 — SSC CGL Prepositional Verb
Convert to passive: The principal called for an explanation.

Options:
a) An explanation was called for by the principal.
b) An explanation was called by the principal.
c) An explanation is called for by the principal.
d) An explanation was being called for by the principal.
Answer: a) An explanation was called for by the principal.
Note: "for" must be retained after "called" in the passive form. Simple Past → was + V3.

2. Direct & Indirect Speech

What is Narration?

Direct Speech quotes the exact words of the speaker (e.g., He said, "I am busy."). Indirect Speech (Reported Speech) reports the meaning without quotation marks (e.g., He said that he was busy.). SSC CGL tests both conversion directions: Direct → Indirect and Indirect → Direct.

SSC CGL Key Rule
The most important principle: when the reporting verb is in the PAST tense (said, told, asked, etc.), the verb in the reported speech must shift one tense BACK (backshift). Exception: universal truths, habits, and historical facts do NOT change tense.

Reporting Verbs

The reporting verb (he said, she told, they asked) determines the conjunction and structure in indirect speech.

Reporting Verb Sentence Type Conjunction / Change Example (Indirect)
saidStatementthatHe said that he was tired.
said to / toldStatementthat + objectHe told me that he was tired.
asked / enquiredYes/No Questionif / whetherHe asked if I was coming.
asked / enquiredWH-QuestionWH-word retainedHe asked where I lived.
ordered / commandedCommandto + V1He ordered me to stand up.
requested / beggedRequestto + V1She requested me to help her.
advised / suggestedAdvice / Suggestionto + V1 / shouldHe advised me to see a doctor.
exclaimedExclamationexclaimed with joy/sorrowHe exclaimed with joy that he had won.
proposed / saidProposal (let's)proposed that / suggested thatHe proposed that we should go for a walk.
Example 10 — SSC CGL Reporting Verb
Convert to indirect speech: The teacher said to the students, "You must submit your homework on time."

Options:
a) The teacher told the students that they must submit their homework on time.
b) The teacher said to the students that you must submit your homework on time.
c) The teacher told the students that they must submitted their homework on time.
d) The teacher told to the students that they must submit their homework on time.
Answer: a) The teacher told the students that they must submit their homework on time.
"Said to" → "told". Pronoun "you" → "they", "your" → "their". "Must" does not change in indirect speech (modal remains unchanged).

Changes in Tense

When the reporting verb is in the past tense, the tense of the reported speech shifts back according to this table:

Direct Speech Tense Indirect Speech Tense Example (Direct → Indirect)
Simple PresentSimple Past"I eat." → He said that he ate.
Present ContinuousPast Continuous"I am eating." → He said that he was eating.
Present PerfectPast Perfect"I have eaten." → He said that he had eaten.
Present Perfect ContinuousPast Perfect Continuous"I have been eating." → He said that he had been eating.
Simple PastPast Perfect"I ate." → He said that he had eaten.
Past ContinuousPast Perfect Continuous"I was eating." → He said that he had been eating.
Past PerfectPast Perfect (no change)"I had eaten." → He said that he had eaten.
Simple Future (will)would"I will eat." → He said that he would eat.
Future Continuouswould be + V-ing"I will be eating." → He said that he would be eating.
Future Perfectwould have + V3"I will have eaten." → He said that he would have eaten.
SSC CGL Exception — Universal Truths
Tense does NOT change if the reported speech expresses a universal truth, a habitual fact, or a historical fact. Example: The teacher said, "The Earth revolves around the Sun." → The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun. (No backshift)
Example 11 — SSC CGL Tense Backshift
Convert to indirect speech: Ravi said, "I was watching a movie when you called."

Options:
a) Ravi said that he was watching a movie when I called.
b) Ravi said that he had been watching a movie when I had called.
c) Ravi said that he had been watching a movie when I called.
d) Ravi said that he was watching a movie when I had called.
Answer: c) Ravi said that he had been watching a movie when I called.
Past Continuous ("was watching") → Past Perfect Continuous ("had been watching"). Past tense ("called") can remain unchanged or change to Past Perfect if needed for clarity.

Changes in Pronouns

Pronouns in the reported speech change according to the relationship between the reporter, the speaker, and the listener.

Pronoun in Direct Changes to Example
Ihe / she (matches subject of reporting verb)He said, "I am tired." → He said that he was tired.
wetheyThey said, "We are coming." → They said that they were coming.
myhis / herShe said, "My book is lost." → She said that her book was lost.
ourtheirHe said, "Our team won." → He said that their team had won.
youI / me / we / us / they / them (depends on listener)He said to me, "You are right." → He told me that I was right.
yourmy / our / his / her / theirShe said to him, "Your car is ready." → She told him that his car was ready.
mehim / herHe said, "Call me." → He said to call him.

SSC CGL Tip — Pronoun Shift
SSC CGL often tests the pronoun "you" in indirect speech. Remember: "you" changes based on who the listener is. If the speaker says "you" to me, it becomes "I/me" when I report it. If said to a third person, it becomes "he/him" or "she/her".
Example 12 — SSC CGL Pronoun Change
Convert to indirect speech: Mohan said to Sita, "I will help you with your project."

Options:
a) Mohan told Sita that he will help you with your project.
b) Mohan told Sita that I will help you with your project.
c) Mohan told Sita that he would help her with her project.
d) Mohan told Sita that I would help her with her project.
Answer: c) Mohan told Sita that he would help her with her project.
"I" (Mohan) → he; "you" (Sita) → her; "your" → her; "will" → would.

In SSC CGL, time-word changes are tested by presenting a direct sentence with "yesterday" / "tomorrow" / "today" and expecting the correct indirect equivalent. Memorize the pairs: yesterday → the previous day, tomorrow → the next day, today → that day.
Example 13 — SSC CGL Time Change
Convert to indirect speech: He said, "I met her yesterday."

Options:
a) He said that he met her yesterday.
b) He said that he had met her the previous day.
c) He said that he had met her yesterday.
d) He said that he met her the previous day.
Answer: b) He said that he had met her the previous day.
Simple Past ("met") → Past Perfect ("had met"). "Yesterday" → "the previous day".

Interrogative Sentences

For questions in indirect speech, the word order changes from question order to statement order. The question mark is removed.

Question Type Direct Speech Indirect Speech
Yes/No (with helping verb)He said, "Do you like coffee?"He asked if/whether I liked coffee.
Yes/No (present tense)She said, "Is he your brother?"She asked if/whether he was my brother.
Yes/No (past tense)He said, "Did she call you?"He asked if/whether she had called me.
WH- QuestionHe said, "Where do you live?"He asked where I lived.
WH- QuestionShe said, "What are you doing?"She asked what I was doing.
WH- Question (who)He said, "Who is she?"He asked who she was.
SSC CGL Warning — Word Order
A common SSC CGL trap: in indirect questions, the word order must be subject + verb (statement order), NOT question order. Example: "He asked where I lived" (NOT "where did I live"). Always check the word order in the options.
Example 14 — SSC CGL Interrogative
Convert to indirect speech: She said to me, "Have you finished your assignment?"

Options:
a) She asked me if you have finished your assignment.
b) She asked me if I had finished my assignment.
c) She asked me if I have finished my assignment.
d) She asked me that I had finished my assignment.
Answer: b) She asked me if I had finished my assignment.
Yes/No question → use "if/whether". Present Perfect ("have finished") → Past Perfect ("had finished"). "You" → "I"; "your" → "my".

Imperative Sentences in Narration

Imperatives (commands, requests, advice, suggestions, prohibitions) follow the pattern: reporting verb + object + to-infinitive / not + to-infinitive.

Type Direct Speech Indirect Speech
Command"Stand up!" he said.He ordered to stand up. / He ordered me to stand up.
Request"Please help me," she said.She requested to help her. / She requested me to help her.
Advice"Consult a doctor," he said.He advised to consult a doctor. / He advised me to consult a doctor.
Suggestion (let's)"Let's go for a walk," he said.He suggested that we should go for a walk. / He proposed going for a walk.
Prohibition"Don't smoke here," she said.She forbade smoking there. / She said not to smoke there.
Negative Imperative"Do not run," he said.He warned not to run.
Example 15 — SSC CGL Imperative Narration
Convert to indirect speech: The captain said to the soldiers, "Do not retreat under any circumstances."

Options:
a) The captain ordered the soldiers not to retreat under any circumstances.
b) The captain told the soldiers do not retreat under any circumstances.
c) The captain said to the soldiers not to retreat under any circumstances.
d) The captain forbade the soldiers to retreat under any circumstances.
Answer: a) The captain ordered the soldiers not to retreat under any circumstances.
Negative command: said to + object → ordered + object + not + to + V1.

Exclamatory Sentences

Exclamatory sentences express strong emotions — joy, sorrow, surprise, disgust. They use reporting verbs like exclaimed, cried out, applauded, etc., with appropriate emotion words.

Emotion Direct Speech Indirect Speech
Joy"What a beautiful dress!" she said.She exclaimed with joy that it was a very beautiful dress.
Sorrow"Alas! I failed the exam," he said.He exclaimed with sorrow that he had failed the exam.
Surprise"What! She won the lottery!" he said.He exclaimed with surprise that she had won the lottery.
Contempt / Disgust"How dare you speak to me like that!" she said.She exclaimed with contempt that I dared to speak to her like that.
Applause / Wish"How brave of you!" they said.They applauded him for his bravery. / They exclaimed that it was very brave of him.
SSC CGL Pattern — Exclamatory
SSC CGL converts "What a/an + adj + noun!" and "How + adj/adv!" to "very" in indirect speech. Example: "What a pretty girl!" → He exclaimed that she was a very pretty girl. "How beautiful!" → He exclaimed that it was very beautiful.
Example 16 — SSC CGL Exclamatory
Convert to indirect speech: She said, "How charming the sunset is!"

Options:
a) She exclaimed that the sunset is very charming.
b) She exclaimed that the sunset was very charming.
c) She exclaimed that how charming the sunset was.
d) She said that the sunset was very charming.
Answer: b) She exclaimed that the sunset was very charming.
"How charming" → "very charming". Present tense ("is") → Past tense ("was").

3. SSC CGL Error Patterns

Common Voice Errors in SSC CGL

The following error patterns appear frequently in SSC CGL Tier 2 "Sentence Improvement" and "Spot the Error" sections.

Error Type Wrong (SSC Trap) Correct
Missing auxiliary in passiveThe work completed on time.The work was completed on time.
Wrong tense auxiliaryThe letter is written yesterday.The letter was written yesterday.
Missing prepositionThe matter was looked into.The matter was looked into.
Wrong form of be-verbThey are been informed.They are being informed.
Active form in passive structureThe cake was ate by the child.The cake was eaten by the child.
Unnecessary agentThe house was built by him by himself.The house was built by him.
Let construction errorLet the work to be done.Let the work be done.
SSC CGL Error Pattern 1
Identify the error: The new bridge is building across the river.

Options:
a) The new bridge
b) is building
c) across the river
d) No error
Answer: b) is building → is being built.
A bridge cannot build itself; it must be built by someone. Use passive voice: "is being built".
SSC CGL Error Pattern 2
Identify the error: It is time that the truth is told.

Options:
a) It is time
b) that the truth
c) is told
d) No error
Answer: c) is told → was told.
After "It is time that", we use Simple Past in passive: "was told".
SSC CGL Error Pattern 3
Identify the error: He was charged for murder.

Options:
a) He was
b) charged for
c) murder
d) No error
Answer: b) charged for → charged with.
The correct preposition after "charged" (in legal context) is "with", not "for".

Error Type Wrong (SSC Trap) Correct
No tense backshiftHe said that he is tired.He said that he was tired.
Wrong conjunctionHe asked that I was coming.He asked if/whether I was coming.
Question word order retainedHe asked where did I live.He asked where I lived.
Pronoun not changedShe said that I was going.She said that she was going.
Said to → told (no object)He told that he was busy.He told me that he was busy. / He said that he was busy.
Universal truth backshiftedThe teacher said that the Earth revolved around the Sun.The teacher said that the Earth revolves around the Sun.
"that" omitted in statementsHe said he was tired.He said that he was tired. (optional but safer)
SSC CGL Error Pattern 4 (Narration)
Identify the error: The judge asked the accused why did he commit the crime.

Options:
a) The judge asked
b) the accused
c) why did he commit the crime
d) No error
Answer: c) why did he commit the crime → why he had committed the crime.
Two errors: (1) question word order → statement order, (2) Simple Past → Past Perfect.
SSC CGL Error Pattern 5 (Narration)
Identify the error: My father told that honesty is the best policy.

Options:
a) My father told
b) that honesty
c) is the best policy
d) No error
Answer: a) My father told → My father said.
"Told" requires an object (told me / told us / told him). Without an object, use "said".

Causative Verbs and Their Passive Equivalents

Causative verbs — make, have, get, let, help — introduce a cause‑effect relationship between the subject and the action performed by another agent. In active voice the pattern is subject + causative verb + object + bare infinitive / to‑infinitive / past participle. When the construction is turned into passive, the object of the causative verb becomes the subject of the passive clause, and the causative verb itself is rendered in its appropriate passive form.

Make takes a bare infinitive in the active (They made him write the report). In the passive the bare infinitive changes to a to‑infinitive: He was made to write the report. The agent (they) may be retained with by but is often omitted.

Have has two active patterns: have + object + bare infinitive (arrange for someone to do something) and have + object + past participle (cause something to be done). The passive of the first pattern follows the same rule as make: He was had to write the report (rare, formal). The second pattern yields a straightforward passive: The report was had written (more common: The report was got written or The report was written with have omitted). In modern usage get replaces have for the causative passive.

Get behaves like have but is more colloquial. Active: She got him to sign the form (to‑infinitive) or She got the form signed (past participle). Passive: He was got to sign the form (awkward) → preferred He was made to sign the form; The form was got signed → standard The form was signed.

Let takes a bare infinitive and has no passive form with let itself; the passive equivalent uses be allowed to or be permitted to. Active: They let him leave early. Passive: He was allowed to leave early.

Help accepts both bare infinitive and to‑infinitive in the active (She helped him (to) finish). In the passive the to‑infinitive is retained: He was helped to finish. The bare infinitive passive (He was helped finish) is ungrammatical.

Key points for examination: identify the causative verb, note whether the complement is bare infinitive, to‑infinitive, or past participle, then apply the appropriate passive transformation. Errors frequently arise when candidates keep the bare infinitive after make in the passive or attempt a passive with let.

Passive Constructions with Infinitive and Gerund Complements

Many transitive verbs license either a to‑infinitive or a gerund as complement. When such verbs are passivised, the complement type determines the shape of the passive clause. Verbs like want, need, like, hate, prefer, begin, start, continue, cease illustrate the contrast.

With a to‑infinitive complement, the passive retains the infinitive marker to. Active: They want to build a new bridge. Passive: A new bridge is wanted to be built (rare) → more natural: A new bridge is wanted (the infinitive is dropped) or It is wanted that a new bridge be built. In formal writing the to‑infinitive passive appears with verbs of perception or expectation: The results are expected to improve.

With a gerund complement, the passive uses the gerund after the passive verb. Active: They dislike building bridges. Passive: Building bridges is disliked. The gerund becomes the subject of the passive clause. Verbs such as enjoy, avoid, consider, risk, deny follow this pattern.

Some verbs admit both complements with a shift in meaning. Remember to lock (future obligation) vs Remember locking (past memory). In the passive: The door is remembered to be locked (obligation) vs Locking the door is remembered (memory). Candidates must recognise the semantic nuance before converting.

Verbs of perception (see, hear, watch, notice) take a bare infinitive or a gerund in the active (I saw him leave / I saw him leaving). Their passive forms differ: He was seen to leave (to‑infinitive) and He was seen leaving (gerund). Both are acceptable; the choice mirrors the active complement.

Verbs of causation like force, compel, oblige require a to‑infinitive in the active and retain it in the passive: He was forced to resign. Verbs of permission (allow, permit, enable) also keep the to‑infinitive: She was allowed to enter.

Examination traps: (1) dropping the to after passive verbs that require it; (2) converting a gerund complement into an infinitive or vice‑versa; (3) using a bare infinitive after passive perception verbs. Mastery comes from memorising the complement type for each high‑frequency verb and applying the rule consistently.

Worked Example
Convert the following active sentence into its correct passive form: The manager made the team submit the proposal by Friday.
Step 1: Identify the causative verb — made (bare infinitive complement).
Step 2: The object of the causative verb is the team; it becomes the passive subject.
Step 3: Change made to its passive form was made and convert the bare infinitive submit to a to‑infinitive to submit.
Step 4: Retain the adverbial phrase by Friday.
Result: The team was made to submit the proposal by Friday.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a voice question contains a causative verb, first underline the verb and its complement. If the complement is a bare infinitive, remember the passive must use a to‑infinitive. For let, replace with be allowed to or be permitted to. Quick mental checklist: causative → complement type → passive auxiliary + V3 → adjust infinitive/gerund. This eliminates the most common distractor options.

Reporting Verbs Governing Subjunctive and Modal Shifts

Certain reporting verbs trigger a mandative subjunctive or a modal shift in the reported clause. Verbs such as suggest, recommend, propose, demand, insist, request, order, require, ask (in the sense of demand) fall into this group. In direct speech the reported clause often uses a present simple or modal (He said, "I suggest that he go"). In indirect speech the subjunctive form (base verb without s) is retained after that: He suggested that he go. The backshift of tense does not apply; the subjunctive remains unchanged regardless of the reporting verb tense.

When the reporting verb is in the past, the modal verbs in the reported clause shift: can → could, may → might, shall → should, will → would, must → had to. However, if the reported clause expresses a mandative meaning, the subjunctive supersedes modal backshift. Example: Direct — She demanded, "He must leave." Indirect — She demanded that he leave (subjunctive) rather than She demanded that he had to leave.

Verbs of advice (advise, recommend, urge) also take the subjunctive in formal contexts: The doctor advised that she take the medicine. In less formal registers a modal should appears: The doctor advised that she should take the medicine. Both are accepted, but examinations often test the formal subjunctive form.

Verbs of permission (allow, permit, let) do not trigger subjunctive; they follow the normal backshift pattern with to‑infinitive: He allowed that she go is ungrammatical; correct: He allowed her to go.

Examination focus: identify whether the reporting verb is mandative. If yes, apply subjunctive (base form) and ignore tense backshift. If the reporting verb is non‑mandative (e.g., say, tell, think), apply standard backshift rules. Distractors often present a backshifted tense where subjunctive is required, or a subjunctive where a modal backshift is correct.

Sequence of Tenses in Embedded Indirect Speech

When indirect speech contains another layer of reporting — a report within a report — the sequence of tenses operates cumulatively. The outermost reporting verb sets the primary time reference; each embedded clause backshifts relative to its immediate reporting verb.

Consider: Direct — He said, "She told me, 'I will finish the work.'" First level: He said that she had told him that she would finish the work. The innermost will becomes would because its reporting verb told is past. The middle told becomes had told because the outermost reporting verb said is past.

If the outermost reporting verb is present (He says), backshift does not occur at the first level, but the embedded past reporting verb still triggers backshift for its own clause: He says that she told him that she would finish the work. Here told remains past (no backshift to past perfect) because the matrix verb is present, but will becomes would due to told.

When the embedded clause expresses a universal truth or a habitual fact, tense backshift is suspended at that level regardless of the reporting verb tense: He said that she said that the earth revolves around the sun. Both said verbs are past, yet revolves stays present.

Modal verbs follow the same cumulative rule: can → could → could have if two past reporting verbs stack. Example: Direct — He said, "She said, 'I can swim.'" Indirect — He said that she had said that she could swim. Only one backshift occurs because the second reporting verb said is already past; can becomes could once.

Examination items often present a three‑level quotation and ask for the correct indirect form. The candidate must track each reporting verb, apply backshift only where the reporting verb is past, and preserve universal truths. A systematic table mapping each level prevents errors.

Voice and Narration in Conditional and Hypothetical Sentences

Conditional sentences (type 1, 2, 3) and hypothetical constructions (wish, if only, as if, as though) exhibit distinct voice and narration behaviours. In active voice the condition clause uses present simple (type 1), past simple (type 2), or past perfect (type 3). The main clause uses will + V1, would + V1, or would have + V3 respectively.

When converting to passive, the verb in the main clause follows the standard passive pattern for its tense/modal: will be + V3, would be + V3, would have been + V3. The condition clause, if it contains a transitive verb, can also be passivised: If they build the road, traffic will improve → If the road is built, traffic will improve. The conditional marker if remains unchanged.

In indirect speech, conditional sentences backshift according to the reporting verb tense. Type 1 (If it rains, I will stay) reported after a past verb becomes He said that if it rained he would stay. Type 2 (If I knew, I would tell) becomes He said that if he had known he would have told (past perfect in condition, would have + V3 in result). Type 3 (If he had come, we would have seen him) remains He said that if he had come they would have seen him (no further backshift possible).

Hypothetical wish clauses follow the same backshift logic: Direct — She said, "I wish I were there." Indirect — She said that she wished she had been there (past perfect after past reporting verb). As if / as though clauses behave similarly: He acts as if he knew the answer → He acted as if he had known the answer.

Passive forms inside conditionals are tested by embedding a passive verb in either clause. Example: Direct — "If the bill is passed, the law will be enforced." Indirect (past reporting) — He said that if the bill was passed the law would be enforced. Note the condition clause backshifts from present passive is passed to past passive was passed; the main clause backshifts from future passive will be enforced to conditional passive would be enforced.

Examination traps: (1) failing to backshift the condition clause; (2) applying double backshift to type 3 conditionals; (3) mixing active and passive forms inconsistently across clauses. Mastery requires recognising the conditional type, the reporting verb tense, and then applying the appropriate passive auxiliary for each verb.

  • Treating make passive as was made submit instead of was made to submit.
  • Using a bare infinitive after passive perception verbs (was seen leave) instead of to leave or leaving.
  • Applying tense backshift to a mandative subjunctive clause (e.g., suggested that he went).
  • Backshifting a universal truth in embedded indirect speech.
  • Double backshifting a type 3 conditional (changing had come to had had come).
  • Converting let directly into passive (was let) rather than was allowed to.
  • Omitting the to after passive causative get (got him sign instead of got him to sign).

Passive Voice with Ditransitive and Prepositional Verbs

Ditransitive verbs such as give, tell, send, show, offer, promise, teach take two objects — a direct object and an indirect object. In the active voice the indirect object usually precedes the direct object (She gave him a book). When the sentence is passivised, either object can become the subject, producing two distinct passive constructions. The personal passive promotes the indirect object: He was given a book. The impersonal passive promotes the direct object while retaining the preposition to or for: A book was given to him. Both forms are grammatically correct, but examination items often test the candidate’s ability to select the form that matches the required focus or to recognise that some ditransitive verbs (e.g., ask, envy, forgive) do not allow the personal passive.

Prepositional verbs (verb + preposition) such as look at, listen to, rely on, insist on, object to behave like transitive verbs when the prepositional object is turned into the subject of a passive clause. The preposition must remain attached to the verb: The painting was looked at by everyone, The proposal was objected to by the committee. A common error is to drop the preposition (*The painting was looked by everyone) or to insert an unnecessary by before the preposition (*The painting was looked at by by everyone). Verbs that are purely intransitive (e.g., sleep, arrive) never form a passive, even when a prepositional phrase follows them.

In formal writing the impersonal passive is preferred because it avoids the awkwardness of a personal subject receiving an inanimate object.

Embedded Indirect Speech and Multiple Reporting Verbs

When direct speech contains more than one layer of quotation, each layer is governed by its own reporting verb. The tense of each reporting verb determines whether backshift applies to the clause it introduces. The outermost reporting verb sets the baseline; if it is past, the first embedded clause backshifts. The second reporting verb, if also past, triggers a further backshift for its own complement, and so on. For example: He said, “She told me, ‘I will finish the work.’” The conversion proceeds level by level: He said that she had told him that she would finish the work. The innermost will becomes would because its immediate reporting verb told is past. The middle told becomes had told because the outermost reporting verb said is past. If the outermost verb is present (He says), the first level does not backshift, but any lower past reporting verb still forces backshift for its own clause: He says that she told him that she would finish the work.

Universal truths and habitual facts suspend backshift at the level where they appear, regardless of the reporting verb tense. In He said that she said that the earth revolves around the sun, both said verbs are past, yet revolves remains present. Modal verbs follow the same cumulative rule: can → could → could have if two past reporting verbs stack. Example: Direct — He said, “She said, ‘I can swim.’” Indirect — He said that she had said that she could swim. Only one backshift occurs because the second reporting verb said is already past; can becomes could once. Examination items often present a three‑level quotation and ask for the correct indirect form. The candidate must track each reporting verb, apply backshift only where the reporting verb is past, and preserve universal truths. A systematic table mapping each level prevents errors.

Convert the following direct speech into indirect speech:

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question presents a multi‑level quotation, write down the reporting verbs in order from outermost to innermost. Mark each verb’s tense. Then work inward, applying backshift only at levels where the reporting verb is past. Keep a checklist: (1) tense backshift, (2) pronoun shift, (3) time/place shift, (4) universal‑truth preservation. This systematic approach eliminates the most common error — double backshifting a clause that already contains a past‑perfect modal.

Modal Perfects and Semi‑Modals in Reported Speech

Modal perfects (must have, should have, could have, might have, ought to have) express deduction or obligation about a past event. In indirect speech their form changes according to the reporting verb tense and the modal’s semantic category. Must used for present obligation (You must submit the report) backshifts to had to (He said that I had to submit the report). Must used for logical deduction (He must have arrived) does **not** backshift; it remains must have arrived because the deduction is anchored to the speaker’s present evidence. Should have and ought to have (unfulfilled obligation) are generally unchanged after a past reporting verb: She said that he should have consulted the manual. Could have and might have (past possibility) also stay the same: They said that the plan could have succeeded.

Semi‑modals (have to, need to, dare, used to) behave differently. Have to (external obligation) backshifts to had to: He said that we had to leave early. Need to follows the same pattern: need to → needed to. Dare as a modal (negative/interrogative) is rare in reported speech; when it appears, it usually becomes dared or is replaced by was/were brave enough to. Used to (past habit) is already past in form; it remains used to after a past reporting verb: He said that she used to walk to work. However, if the reporting verb is present, used to can be retained or shifted to would for a habitual sense: He says that she would walk to work.

A frequent trap is treating must have as a backshifting modal perfect. Because must have encodes a present‑time deduction about a past event, it resists backshift. Converting He must have finished to He had to have finished is incorrect. The correct indirect form after a past reporting verb is He said that he must have finished. Similarly, should have and ought to have do not become should have had or ought to have had. Mastery of these distinctions is essential for high‑accuracy conversion of modal‑rich passages.

Voice and Narration in Reduced and Non‑Finite Clauses

Reduced relative clauses (participial phrases) often contain passive participles that must be recognised when converting between active and passive voice. In The report prepared by the committee was submitted, the participial phrase prepared by the committee is a reduced passive relative clause equivalent to which was prepared by the committee. When the main clause is passivised, the participial phrase remains unchanged because it already carries passive morphology. However, if the original active sentence is The committee prepared the report and the task is to produce a passive version with a reduced clause, the result is The report prepared by the committee was submitted — the reduced clause is derived from the passive verb was prepared.

Infinitival clauses functioning as complements of verbs like expect, want, arrange, decide also exhibit voice alternations. Active: They expect to finish the project. Passive of the matrix verb: The project is expected to be finished. Note that the infinitival complement shifts from active to finish to passive to be finished because the logical object of finish becomes the subject of the matrix passive. When such a structure appears in direct speech, the indirect conversion must preserve the voice of the infinitival complement. Direct: She said, “We expect to finish the work.” Indirect (past reporting): She said that they expected to finish the work (no voice change in the complement because the matrix verb expect remains active). If the direct speech already contains a passive matrix: She said, “The work is expected to be finished.” Indirect: She said that the work was expected to be finished.

Gerund clauses after prepositions or verbs such as avoid, deny, admit, suggest follow the same principle. Active gerund: He denied stealing the money. Passive gerund: He denied having been stolen (rare) or more naturally He denied that the money had been stolen. In reported speech, the gerund may be backshifted to a perfect gerund when the reporting verb is past: He said that he denied having stolen the money. The perfect gerund having stolen signals anteriority relative to the reporting verb. Examination questions often test the candidate’s ability to choose between simple and perfect gerunds after a past reporting verb, and to recognise when a passive gerund (being stolen) is required.

  • Applying backshift to a modal perfect that expresses present deduction (e.g., changing must have to had to have).
  • Dropping the preposition in a prepositional‑verb passive (e.g., *The proposal was objected by the committee).
  • Promoting the indirect object of a verb that does not allow a personal passive (e.g., *He was asked a question for ask).
  • Double backshifting a type‑3 conditional inside indirect speech (changing had come to had had come).
  • Using a bare infinitive after a passive perception verb (e.g., *He was seen leave instead of He was seen to leave).
  • Failing to shift a simple gerund to a perfect gerund after a past reporting verb (e.g., He denied stealing → He denied having stolen).
  • Converting let directly into a passive form (*was let) instead of the correct was allowed to.

These traps highlight the need for precise rule application rather than reliance on intuition.

Passive Voice in Complex Nominalizations and Clausal Complements

Nominalizations turn verbs or clauses into noun phrases, and they frequently appear in formal writing where the passive voice is preferred for impersonality. When a nominalized clause contains an internal argument structure, the passive can be realized either on the matrix verb or on the embedded verb, but not both simultaneously. For example, The investigation of the fraud was completed by the agency derives from The agency completed the investigation of the fraud. The embedded prepositional phrase of the fraud remains unchanged because the nominal head investigation already licenses the internal argument. In contrast, a clausal complement such as that the fraud had been committed can be passivized at the matrix level: It was discovered that the fraud had been committed. The embedded clause retains its own voice; the matrix passive merely adds an expletive subject it. A common error is to double‑passivize: *It was discovered that the fraud had been been committed. The rule is that each finite clause may carry at most one passive morpheme. When the matrix verb is a raising verb (seem, appear, happen), the embedded subject can become the matrix subject in the passive: The fraud seems to have been committed → The fraud is seemed to have been committed is ungrammatical because seem does not passivize. Instead, the construction It seems that the fraud was committed is the only viable passive‑compatible form. Understanding the distribution of passive across nominal and clausal layers is essential for converting dense administrative sentences without altering meaning.

Free Indirect Discourse and the Boundary Between Direct and Indirect Speech

Free indirect discourse (FID) blends the deictic centre of the narrator with the voice of a character, producing a hybrid that is neither fully direct nor fully indirect. In FID, the reporting verb is often omitted, and the tense, pronouns, and modal expressions shift partially toward the narrator’s perspective while retaining the character’s lexical choices. For instance, He wondered whether he would ever see her again is indirect; Would he ever see her again? is direct; Would he ever see her again, he wondered is FID. The hallmark of FID is the retention of the character’s subjective modality (e.g., must, should, might) alongside the narrator’s past tense frame. When converting FID to strict indirect speech, the examiner expects a full backshift of tense and a replacement of the character’s deictic markers with those appropriate to the reporting context: He wondered whether he would ever see her again. Conversely, converting indirect speech to FID requires preserving the character’s modal verbs while aligning the tense with the narrative past: He thought that he must leave → He must leave, he thought. A frequent trap is to treat FID as a simple direct‑to‑indirect conversion, leading to over‑backshifting (e.g., *He wondered whether he would have ever seen her again). The correct approach is to identify the narrative tense, keep the character’s modal orientation, and adjust only the deictic elements that belong to the narrator (time adverbials, demonstratives). Mastery of this boundary enables accurate handling of literary passages that appear in comprehension and transformation items.

Transformation of Free Indirect Discourse
Convert the following free indirect discourse into standard indirect speech: She would never forgive him, she decided.
Step 1: Identify the reporting verb (decided) and its tense (past). Step 2: The modal would expresses the character’s future‑in‑the‑past intention; in indirect speech it remains would because modal perfects do not backshift. Step 3: Adjust pronouns and time references to the reporter’s perspective: She decided that she would never forgive him. The resulting sentence is fully indirect, with the reporting clause fronted and the modal unchanged.
Exam‑Day Strategy for FID Items
When a sentence lacks an explicit reporting verb but shows a past‑tense narrative frame, treat it as free indirect discourse. First, insert a plausible reporting verb (e.g., thought, decided, realized) in the past tense. Then apply the standard indirect‑speech rules: backshift tense only for non‑modal verbs, keep modal verbs unchanged, and shift pronouns and adverbials to the reporter’s viewpoint. This two‑step method prevents the common error of over‑backshifting modals.

Causative and Perception Verbs: Voice Alternations and Reporting Constraints

Causative verbs (make, have, get, let, help) and perception verbs (see, hear, watch, notice, observe) exhibit distinct voice behaviours that affect both active‑passive conversion and reported speech. In the active, the causative make takes a bare infinitive: They made him sign the document. Its passive counterpart requires a to-infinitive: He was made to sign the document. The verb let does not passivize as *was let; the accepted passive uses was allowed to: He was allowed to leave. Have in the causative sense (I had the car repaired) already carries a passive meaning in its complement; the matrix passive The car was had repaired is marginal and generally avoided in formal writing. Perception verbs follow a similar pattern: active I saw him leave (bare infinitive) versus passive He was seen to leave (to‑infinitive). When these structures appear in direct speech, the indirect conversion must preserve the infinitive type dictated by the matrix voice. Direct: She said, “I saw him leave.” Indirect (past reporting): She said that she had seen him leave (bare infinitive retained because the matrix verb see remains active). If the direct speech is already passive: She said, “He was seen to leave.” Indirect: She said that he had been seen to leave. A frequent error is to backshift the bare infinitive to a to-infinitive in indirect speech (*She said that she had seen him to leave), which violates the complementation rule of the perception verb. Similarly, converting let directly into a passive form (*was let) instead of was allowed to is a hallmark trap in transformation exercises.

Sequence of Tenses Across Multiple Embedded Reporting Verbs

When a reported utterance contains another reporting verb, the sequence‑of‑tenses rules apply recursively. Consider the direct speech: He said, “She thinks that they will arrive tomorrow.” The outermost reporting verb said is past, so the first embedded clause backshifts: He said that she thought that they would arrive the next day. Notice that thinks becomes thought (present → past) and will becomes would. If the middle reporting verb is also past in the original (He said, “She thought that they would arrive tomorrow.”), the backshift yields He said that she had thought that they would arrive the next day (past → past perfect for the middle verb, while the modal would remains unchanged because it is already a past‑form modal). When the innermost clause is a conditional, the same principle holds: He said, “She thinks that if they come, we will stay.” → He said that she thought that if they came, we would stay. The conditional antecedent backshifts from present to past, and the consequent modal will becomes would. A double backshift error occurs when a candidate converts had come to had had come inside indirect speech; the correct form retains the past perfect because the reporting verb already establishes anteriority. Mastery of recursive backshifting is tested through multi‑layered sentences where each reporting verb introduces its own temporal frame.

Ellipsis, Substitution, and Null Operators in Voice and Narration Transformations

Elliptical constructions omit material that is recoverable from context, and they interact with voice and narration in subtle ways. In passive transformations, the agent phrase by X is often ellipted when the agent is unknown or irrelevant: The report was submitted (agent omitted). When converting such a passive to active, the examiner may require the insertion of a generic subject (Someone submitted the report) or a context‑specific noun. In reported speech, ellipsis appears in responses: “Will you attend?” “I might.” The indirect form must reconstruct the elided verb: He asked whether I would attend, and I replied that I might attend. Substitution with do so or so follows similar reconstruction rules: Direct: She said, “I will finish the task, and he will do so too.” Indirect: She said that she would finish the task and that he would do so too. Null operators in relative clauses (e.g., The book Ø I read) become overt when the clause is passivized: The book that was read by me. In narration, a null complementizer in direct speech (He said Ø he was tired) must be rendered as that in formal indirect speech (He said that he was tired). Failure to restore omitted elements leads to incomplete or ungrammatical transformations, a pattern frequently exploited in error‑spotting items.

  • Double passivization of a single clause (e.g., *It was decided that the plan would be been approved).
  • Treating free indirect discourse as ordinary direct speech and over‑backshifting modal verbs.
  • Using was let instead of was allowed to for the passive of causative let.
  • Changing a bare infinitive after a perception verb to a to-infinitive in indirect speech.
  • Applying a second past‑perfect shift to an already past‑perfect verb inside multiply embedded reported speech.
  • Omitting the reconstructed verb in elliptical reported responses (e.g., *He asked if I would come and I said I might without come).
  • Leaving a null complementizer in formal indirect speech where that is required.

Passive Voice with Reflexive and Reciprocal Verbs

Reflexive verbs pair a transitive verb with a reflexive pronoun that refers back to the subject (e.g., She prepared herself). In English, a true passive of a reflexive construction is rarely grammatical because the agent and the patient coincide; the passive would require a phrase such as Herself was prepared by her, which violates the language’s case and animacy constraints. Standard grammars therefore treat reflexive clauses as inherently active. The only acceptable passive‑like alternative is to re‑lexicalise the predicate: She was prepared (with an implied external agent) or She got herself prepared (a get‑passive that retains the reflexive pronoun as an object of the light verb). Reciprocal verbs such as meet, resemble, marry behave similarly. They met each other cannot become *Each other was met by them. Instead, English uses a plural subject with an intransitive reciprocal reading: They met. When a reciprocal verb appears in a causative frame (He made them meet), the passive of the causative is possible (They were made to meet), but the reciprocal core remains active. Examiners often test the distinction by offering a spurious passive of a reflexive or reciprocal clause and expecting the candidate to recognise the ungrammaticality.

  • Reflexive pronouns block standard passivisation; only get‑passives or re‑lexicalised forms survive.
  • Reciprocal predicates resist passive because the semantic roles are symmetric.
  • Light‑verb causatives (make, let, have) can be passivised while the embedded reciprocal stays active.

Voice Alternations in Complex Predicate Constructions

Complex predicates consist of a light verb (make, take, give, have, do) plus a nominalised event (decision, walk, presentation, attempt). The voice alternation operates on the light verb, not on the noun. She made a decision → A decision was made (passive of make). The noun phrase a decision becomes the passive subject, while the original subject either disappears or appears in a by-phrase (A decision was made by her). With take, the pattern is identical: He took a walk → A walk was taken. Some light verbs admit a double‑object alternation: She gave him a presentation → He was given a presentation (indirect object promoted) or A presentation was given to him (direct object promoted). The have/get something done construction is a causative passive: She had the car repaired → The car was had repaired is marginal; the preferred passive is The car got repaired or The car was repaired with an implied causer. In formal writing, the passive of light‑verb constructions is favoured for topicalising the event noun, but examiners may trap candidates by asking for a passive of the noun itself (*A decision was decided) which is ungrammatical.

  • Passivise the light verb; the event noun becomes subject.
  • Double‑object light verbs allow two passive variants (indirect‑object or direct‑object promotion).
  • Have/get something done resists a direct passive; use get‑passive or simple passive of the main verb.

Keep negation attached to the new auxiliary.

Reported Speech with Parenthetical and Appositive Clauses

Parenthetical clauses (e.g., He said, I think, that the project would finish early) and appositive noun phrases (e.g., My brother, a doctor, said that he would help) introduce extra layers of attribution that affect backshifting and pronoun resolution. In indirect speech, the parenthetical reporting verb (I think) is usually retained as a separate clause: He said that he thought the project would finish early. The original matrix verb said still governs the primary backshift, while the parenthetical verb follows its own sequence‑of‑tenses rules. Appositives do not trigger additional backshift; they are simply integrated as noun‑phrase modifiers: My brother, who is a doctor, said that he would help. However, if the appositive contains a verb of speaking (My brother, who claimed that he was ready, said…), the embedded claim must be backshifted relative to the outer reporting verb. Examiners frequently embed a parenthetical he added or she whispered inside direct speech and ask for the indirect form; the correct answer nests the parenthetical as a subordinate reporting clause with its own tense adjustment.

  • Parenthetical reporting verbs become subordinate clauses with independent backshift.
  • Appositives without speech verbs remain unchanged; those with speech verbs require recursive backshift.
  • Pronouns inside parentheticals follow the perspective of the speaker of that clause, not the main reporter.

Negation Scope and Polarity in Passive and Indirect Speech

Negation interacts with voice and narration in ways that alter truth conditions. In a passive clause, the negative particle typically attaches to the auxiliary: The report was not submitted (scope over the entire event). When a modal is present, the position of not determines whether the modality or the proposition is negated: The report may not be submitted (permission denied) versus The report may be not submitted (rare, emphasises non‑submission as a possibility). In indirect speech, the original negation must be preserved, but the reporting verb’s polarity can invert the interpretation: He said that he would not go (direct negation) versus He denied that he would go (negative reporting verb, positive complement). Double negation errors arise when candidates backshift a negative modal and then add another not (*He said that he would not not go). Polarity items such as any, ever, anybody are licensed by negation; in passive indirect speech they must remain in the scope of the negative operator: She said that no one had seen anything (not *something). Mastery of these scope relations is essential for error‑spotting items that test subtle meaning shifts.

  • Negation attaches to the nearest auxiliary in passive; modal negation precedes the modal.
  • Indirect speech preserves the original negative polarity; reporting verbs with negative force invert complement polarity.
  • Polarity‑sensitive items must stay within the negative scope after transformation.

Pragmatic Constraints on Voice Choice in Formal Discourse

Voice selection is not purely syntactic; it reflects information structure, agency attribution, and genre conventions. In academic and legal writing, the passive is preferred when the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately backgrounded: The experiment was conducted under controlled conditions. Conversely, the active voice foregrounds responsibility: The committee approved the proposal. The “agentless passive” can become a stylistic fault if overused, leading to vague prose. Some style guides recommend a ratio of active to passive clauses (often 3:1) for readability. In reported speech, the choice between direct and indirect forms also carries pragmatic weight: direct speech preserves the speaker’s voice and evidentiality, while indirect speech integrates the proposition into the narrator’s discourse. Free indirect discourse blurs this boundary, allowing the narrator to adopt the character’s lexical choices without quotation marks. Examiners may present a paragraph and ask which voice or narration mode best satisfies a given rhetorical goal (e.g., objectivity, emphasis on result, attribution). Understanding these pragmatic parameters enables candidates to justify transformations beyond mechanical rule‑application.

  • Agentless passive signals objectivity but can obscure accountability.
  • Active voice assigns agency; preferred in policy recommendations.
  • Direct speech retains evidential force; indirect speech integrates content.
  • Free indirect discourse merges narrator and character perspectives.
  • Treating a reflexive clause as passivisable (e.g., *Himself was injured by him).
  • Passivising the noun in a light‑verb construction instead of the light verb (e.g., *A decision was decided).
  • Failing to backshift a parenthetical reporting verb independently of the main reporting verb.
  • Placing negation outside the modal scope in passive indirect speech (e.g., *The report may be not submitted for permission denial).
  • Using an agentless passive where the genre demands explicit agency (e.g., legal judgments).
  • Converting a direct‑speech polarity item (*something) to an indirect form without preserving negative licensing.
  • Over‑applying free indirect discourse markers, resulting in ambiguous attribution.

These advanced patterns complete the toolkit needed for high‑level voice and narration transformation tasks.
