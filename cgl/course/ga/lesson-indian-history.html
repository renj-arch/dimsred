1. The Indus Valley Civilization and Early Vedic Society

1.1 Urban Planning and Material Culture of the Harappan Civilization

The Harappan civilization, flourishing between 2600 BCE and 1900 BCE across the Indus‑Ghaggar‑Hakra river system, displayed a level of urban planning unprecedented in the ancient world. Cities such as Harappa, Mohenjo‑Daro, Dholavira, and Rakhigarhi were laid out on a grid pattern with streets intersecting at right angles, a sophisticated drainage network covered with baked bricks, and standardized weights and measures that facilitated long‑distance trade. The use of uniform fired bricks, the presence of granaries, and the Great Bath at Mohenjo‑Daro indicate a centralized authority capable of mobilizing labor for public works. Artifacts such as steatite seals bearing the undeciphered script, terracotta figurines, and beads of carnelian and lapis lazuli reveal a society engaged in craft specialization and maritime commerce with Mesopotamia, as attested by Mesopotamian texts referring to “Meluhha.”

The material culture also points to a relatively egalitarian urban life, with little evidence for monumental palaces or temples, suggesting that political power may have been corporate rather than monarchical. The standardization of brick sizes (ratio 4:2:1) across sites separated by hundreds of kilometres implies a shared system of measurement and possibly a common administrative framework. However, the absence of deciphered written records limits our understanding of their governance, religious beliefs, and the reasons for the eventual de‑urbanization that began around 1900 BCE.

1.2 The Decline of the Harappan Cities and the Transition to the Vedic Age

Scholars attribute the decline of the mature Harappan phase to a combination of climatic shifts, tectonic activity, and changes in river courses, particularly the drying up of the Ghaggar‑Hakra, which reduced agricultural surplus and undermined the economic base of the cities. As urban centers were abandoned, populations migrated eastward into the Ganga‑Yamuna doab and southward into the Deccan, carrying with them elements of Harappan technology such as pottery styles and metallurgy. This demographic movement set the stage for the arrival of Indo‑Aryan speaking groups, whose earliest literary expression appears in the Rigveda, composed roughly between 1500 BCE and 1200 BCE. The Vedic texts reflect a pastoral, semi‑nomadic society organized around tribal units (jana) led by chieftains (rajan), with a ritual life centered on fire sacrifices (yajna) and a pantheon of nature deities such as Indra, Agni, and Varuna.

The transition was not a sudden replacement but a gradual process of cultural synthesis. Archaeological evidence from sites like Bhagwanpura and the Painted Grey Ware culture shows a continuity of certain Harappan crafts alongside new Vedic ritual practices. The emergence of the varna system, initially a functional division of labor rather than a rigid hierarchy, and the composition of the later Samhitas, Brahmanas, and Upanishads mark the evolution from early Vedic to later Vedic society, laying the ideological foundations for the complex polities that would follow.

1.3 Society, Religion, and Polity in the Early Vedic Period

Early Vedic society was organized around the clan (kula) and the tribe (jana), with the rajan acting as a war leader and protector rather than an absolute monarch. The sabha and samiti, assemblies of elders and warriors respectively, provided a consultative framework for decision‑making, indicating a proto‑republican element in governance. Religious life revolved around the performance of yajnas, which required the memorization and precise recitation of hymns, giving rise to a specialized priestly class (brahmanas) who gradually acquired ritual authority. The concept of rta, the cosmic order maintained through sacrifice, underpinned both religious and social ethics.

Economically, the early Vedic people practiced mixed pastoralism and shifting agriculture, with cattle serving as the primary measure of wealth. The absence of urban centers meant that surplus production was limited, and redistribution occurred through gift‑giving (dana) and sacrificial feasts. As the Vedic Aryans moved into the fertile plains of the western Ganga basin, they encountered indigenous agricultural communities, leading to a gradual shift toward settled farming, the use of iron tools from around 1000 BCE, and the formation of larger territorial units that would evolve into the mahajanapadas.

2. The Age of Mahajanapadas, Mauryas, and Post‑Mauryan Polities

2.1 The Sixteen Mahajanapadas and the Rise of Magadha

By the sixth century BCE, the northern Indian subcontinent had coalesced into sixteen major territorial states known as the mahajanapadas, listed in Buddhist and Jain texts such as the Anguttara Nikaya and the Bhagavati Sutra. These included Kasi, Kosala, Anga, Magadha, Vajji, Malla, Chedi, Vatsa, Kuru, Panchala, Matsya, Surasena, Assaka, Avanti, Gandhara, and Kamboja. Among them, Magadha, situated in the fertile alluvial plains of the lower Ganga with its capital at Rajagriha (later Pataliputra), gained strategic advantage through control of iron ore deposits, riverine transport, and a powerful standing army equipped with elephants. The Haryanka dynasty, particularly Bimbisara (c. 544–492 BCE) and his son Ajatashatru (c. 492–460 BCE), expanded Magadhan territory through matrimonial alliances and military conquests, annexing Anga and defeating the Vajji confederacy.

The rise of Magadha was also facilitated by the patronage of heterodox religious movements. Both Buddhism and Jainism found royal support in Magadha, which provided them with monastic endowments and protection. This symbiosis between state power and new ascetic traditions helped legitimize Magadhan rule and contributed to the cultural integration of the region. The subsequent Shishunaga and Nanda dynasties further centralized administration, with the Nandas (c. 345–322 BCE) creating a vast imperial structure that set the template for the Mauryan empire.

2.2 Chandragupta Maurya, Chanakya, and the Foundations of the Mauryan Empire

Chandragupta Maurya (c. 322–298 BCE), guided by the Brahmin strategist Chanakya (Kautilya), overthrew the Nanda dynasty and established the first pan‑Indian empire. The Arthashastra, attributed to Chanakya, outlines a highly centralized bureaucratic apparatus with a council of ministers (mantriparishad), a network of spies (charas), and a detailed revenue system based on land measurement and taxation (bhaga, bali, kara). The empire was divided into provinces (janapadas) governed by royal princes or trusted officials (kumara, mahamatra), each supported by a hierarchy of district (sthanika) and village (gramika) officers. The capital Pataliputra, described by Megasthenes in his Indica, featured a wooden palisade, a grid of streets, and a sophisticated municipal administration.

Chandragupta’s conquests extended the empire’s frontiers to the Hindu Kush in the northwest, the Deccan plateau in the south, and the Bay of Bengal in the east. His treaty with Seleucus I Nicator around 305 BCE secured the cession of the trans‑Indus territories (Aria, Arachosia, Gedrosia) in exchange for five hundred war elephants, establishing diplomatic relations with the Hellenistic world. The Mauryan state’s ability to mobilize resources for large‑scale public works — roads, irrigation canals, and rest houses — facilitated economic integration and the spread of a common administrative culture across diverse ecological zones.

2.3 Ashoka’s Dhamma, Administration, and the Spread of Buddhism

Ashoka (c. 268–232 BCE), Chandragupta’s grandson, is renowned for his transformation from a conqueror to a proponent of non‑violence after the brutal Kalinga war (c. 261 BCE). His edicts, inscribed on pillars and rock surfaces across the empire in Prakrit using Brahmi script (and Kharosthi in the northwest), articulate a policy of Dhamma — a moral code emphasizing compassion, tolerance, respect for elders, and the welfare of all beings. The edicts also reveal administrative innovations: the appointment of Dhamma mahamatras to propagate ethical conduct, the establishment of medical facilities for humans and animals, and the planting of shade trees and digging of wells along highways.

Ashoka’s patronage of Buddhism catalyzed its expansion beyond the subcontinent. He dispatched missions to Sri Lanka (led by his son Mahinda and daughter Sanghamitta), to the Hellenistic kingdoms of Antiochus II, Ptolemy II, and others, and to Southeast Asia. The Third Buddhist Council, traditionally held at Pataliputra under his auspices, aimed at purifying the sangha and compiling the Abhidhamma Pitaka. While Ashoka’s Dhamma was not exclusively Buddhist, his support institutionalized the religion and linked it with imperial authority, creating a legacy that influenced subsequent Indian polities and the broader Asian Buddhist world.

2.4 The Post‑Mauryan Kingdoms: Shungas, Kanvas, Satavahanas, and the Indo‑Greeks

The assassination of the last Mauryan ruler Brihadratha by his commander Pushyamitra Shunga (c. 185 BCE) inaugurated the Shunga dynasty, which ruled from Pataliputra and sought to revive Brahmanical orthodoxy, as evidenced by the performance of Vedic sacrifices and the patronage of the Sanskrit grammarian Patanjali. The Shungas faced incursions from the northwest, notably the Indo‑Greek king Demetrius, but managed to retain control over the core Gangetic region. The subsequent Kanva dynasty (c. 73–28 BCE) was short‑lived, giving way to the rise of regional powers.

In the Deccan, the Satavahanas (c. 1st century BCE–3rd century CE) emerged as a major force, with their capital at Pratishthana (Paithan) and later Amaravati. They adopted the title “Lord of the Dakshinapatha,” controlled trade routes linking the western ports of Bharuch and Sopara with the interior, and issued a distinctive coinage bearing bilingual legends in Prakrit and Greek. The Satavahanas patronized both Brahmanical and Buddhist institutions, as seen in the construction of stupas at Amaravati and the excavation of chaitya halls at Karla and Nashik. Meanwhile, the Indo‑Greek kingdoms in the Punjab and Gandhara, founded by successors of Alexander’s generals, fostered a syncretic Greco‑Buddhist art style (Gandhara school) and introduced features such as the use of the Greek language on coinage and the concept of the divine ruler.

3. The Classical Age: Guptas, Vakatakas, and Regional Kingdoms

3.1 The Gupta Empire: Political Unity, Administration, and the “Golden Age”

The Gupta dynasty, founded by Sri Gupta and expanded by Chandragupta I (c. 319–335 CE) through a strategic marriage alliance with the Licchavi princess Kumaradevi, ushered in a period often termed the “Golden Age” of ancient India. Chandragupta I assumed the title Maharajadhiraja, signaling imperial sovereignty. His son Samudragupta (c. 335–375 CE), celebrated in the Allahabad Pillar inscription composed by Harishena, conducted extensive military campaigns across the subcontinent, defeating numerous kings in Aryavarta, subjugating forest tribes, and receiving tribute from frontier rulers in the northwest and the Deccan. The empire’s administration combined a central bureaucracy with a degree of local autonomy: provinces (bhuktis) were governed by uparikas, districts (vishayas) by vishayapatis, and villages by gramikas, while the crown maintained a standing army and a network of spies.

Revenue was derived primarily from land tax (bhaga), typically one‑sixth of the produce, supplemented by taxes on trade, crafts, and forest produce. The Guptas issued a high‑quality gold coinage (dinara) modeled on the Kushan standard, which facilitated long‑distance commerce and became a benchmark for subsequent Indian currencies. The period saw the codification of Hindu law in texts such as the Manusmriti and the composition of the great Sanskrit epics in their classical form, reflecting a vibrant intellectual milieu supported by royal patronage.

3.2 Cultural Achievements: Literature, Science, Art, and the Nalanda University

Gupta courts attracted luminaries such as Kalidasa, whose plays (Abhijnanashakuntalam, Vikramorvashiyam) and poems (Meghaduta, Raghuvamsha) set the standard for classical Sanskrit literature. The mathematician‑astronomer Aryabhata (born 476 CE) authored the Aryabhatiya, introducing the concept of zero as a placeholder, a sine table, and a heliocentric model of planetary motion. Varahamihira compiled the Pancha‑Siddhantika, summarizing five astronomical treatises. In medicine, the Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita were revised and expanded, systematizing Ayurvedic knowledge.

Artistically, the Gupta period produced the iconic stone sculptures of the Sarnath Buddha, the Dashavatara temple at Deogarh, and the cave paintings at Ajanta (though the latter extend into the Vakataka phase). The establishment of Nalanda as a residential university in the 5th century CE, with its curriculum covering Buddhist philosophy, logic, grammar, medicine, and astronomy, attracted scholars from China, Korea, and Southeast Asia. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang (7th century CE) left detailed accounts of Nalanda’s libraries, lecture halls, and disciplined monastic life, underscoring its role as a hub of trans‑Asian intellectual exchange.

3.3 The Vakatakas, Chalukyas, Pallavas, and the Rise of Regional Powers

While the Guptas dominated the north, the Deccan and south India witnessed the rise of powerful regional dynasties. The Vakatakas (c. 250–500 CE), contemporaries of the Guptas, ruled from Nandivardhana and later Vatsagulma, and are noted for their patronage of the Ajanta caves (caves 16, 17, 19, 26) under King Harishena. Their matrimonial alliances with the Guptas (e.g., Prabhavatigupta, daughter of Chandragupta II, married Vakataka king Rudrasena II) illustrate the diplomatic interplay between northern and Deccan polities.

In the western Deccan, the Chalukyas of Badami (c. 543–753 CE) established a kingdom that bridged the north‑south cultural divide, developing the Vesara style of temple architecture exemplified by the Virupaksha temple at Pattadakal. Further south, the Pallavas of Kanchipuram (c. 275–897 CE) pioneered the Dravidian temple form, with the shore temple at Mahabalipuram and the Kailasanatha temple at Kanchipuram serving as prototypes for later Chola architecture. These regional kingdoms maintained extensive maritime trade with Southeast Asia, as evidenced by the spread of Indian scripts, religious ideas, and the Sanskrit language to kingdoms such as Funan, Champa, and Srivijaya.

4. The Delhi Sultanate and the Vijayanagara Empire

4.1 The Five Dynasties of the Delhi Sultanate: Slave, Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid, Lodi

The establishment of the Delhi Sultanate in 1206 CE by Qutb al‑Din Aibak, a former slave (mamluk) of Muhammad Ghori, marked the beginning of sustained Muslim rule in northern India. The Mamluk (Slave) dynasty (1206–1290) consolidated control over the Gangetic plain, constructing the Qutb Minar and the Quwwat al‑Islam mosque using spolia from Hindu and Jain temples. The Khalji dynasty (1290–1320), under Alauddin Khalji, expanded the sultanate’s frontiers deep into the Deccan, reaching as far as Madurai, and introduced market reforms (price controls, grain rationing) and a standing army paid in cash (tankas) to reduce dependence on feudal levies.

The Tughlaq dynasty (1320–1413) saw Muhammad bin Tughlaq’s ambitious but disruptive projects — shifting the capital to Daulatabad, issuing token currency, and planning an invasion of Khurasan — which strained the treasury and provoked rebellions. His successor Firuz Shah Tughlaq (1351–1388) restored stability through extensive public works (canals, hospitals, madrasas) and a policy of religious orthodoxy, but the sultanate fragmented after his death. The Sayyid (1414–1451) and Lodi (1451–1526) dynasties ruled a much‑reduced territory centered on Delhi and the Punjab, with the Lodis, particularly Sikandar Lodi, promoting agriculture and founding the city of Agra. The sultanate’s end came with Babur’s victory at Panipat in 1526.

4.2 Administration, Revenue Systems, and the Iqta Mechanism

The Delhi Sultanate’s administrative framework relied on the iqta system, whereby revenue assignments (iqta) were granted to military nobles (muqtis) in lieu of salaries.

The Vijayanagara Empire: Political Structure, Economy, and Cultural Synthesis

The Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1646 CE) emerged in the southern Deccan as a response to the political vacuum left by the weakening Delhi Sultanate’s southern campaigns. Founded by Harihara I and Bukka Raya I of the Sangama dynasty, the empire expanded rapidly under Deva Raya II (r. 1424–1446) and reached its zenith under Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509–1529) of the Tuluva line. The capital, Vijayanagara (modern Hampi), was strategically placed on the Tungabhadra river, controlling the fertile Raichur Doab and the trade routes linking the western coast with the interior.

Administrative Framework

The empire retained a feudal‑style hierarchy but introduced a more centralized revenue apparatus than its contemporaries. The kingdom was divided into mandalams (provinces) governed by nayakas or mandaleshvaras, who held military and fiscal authority in return for fixed tribute. Below them, nadus (districts) and sthalas (villages) were administered by hereditary gaudas and karnams who maintained land records (kadatha) and collected the kudimai (land tax) usually assessed at one‑sixth of the produce. The kaval (police) and kavalgars ensured local order.

Economic Foundations

Agriculture formed the backbone, with extensive irrigation works — tanks (kere), canals, and the famous anicut across the Tungabhadra — enabling double cropping of rice, millets, and cotton. The empire’s coastal ports (Bhatkal, Honavar, Mangalore) facilitated a thriving maritime trade in pepper, textiles, diamonds, and horses imported from Persia and Arabia. The state minted gold varaha (≈3.4 g), silver tanka, and copper jital coins, standardising weights across the realm. Merchant guilds such as the nanadesis and chettis operated with royal charters, and the virupaksha temple complex functioned as a banking centre, issuing hundis (bills of exchange).

Cultural Synthesis

Vijayanagara’s art reflects a blend of Dravidian temple architecture (towering gopurams, pillared mandapas) with Indo‑Islamic elements (arched gateways, stucco ornamentation) visible in the Lotus Mahal and the Elephant Stables. The court patronised Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, and Tamil literature; the Ashtadiggajas (eight poets) under Krishnadevaraya produced works like Amuktamalyada (Telugu) and Jambavati Kalyanam (Sanskrit). The empire also supported the Haridasa movement (Purandara Dasa, Kanaka Dasa) which popularised devotional kirtanas in Kannada, laying foundations for Carnatic music.

The Mughal Empire: Central Administration, Mansabdari, and Revenue Reforms

From Babur’s victory at Panipat (1526) to Aurangzeb’s death (1707), the Mughal Empire created a highly centralised bureaucratic machine that integrated Persian administrative traditions with indigenous Indian practices. The emperor stood at the apex, assisted by the wazir (prime minister), mir bakhshi (military paymaster), sadr-us-sudur (chief religious officer), and khan-i-saman (lord high steward). Provincial governance was entrusted to subahdars (governors) who reported directly to the centre.

The Mansabdari System

Introduced by Akbar (r. 1556–1605) and refined by his successors, the mansabdari system assigned every noble a dual rank: zat (personal status and salary) and sawar (number of cavalrymen to be maintained). Ranks ranged from 10 to 10,000 (later 12,000 for princes). Mansabdars were paid either in cash (naqd) or through revenue assignments (jagirs) which were transferable and non‑hereditary, preventing the emergence of a feudal aristocracy. The dagh (branding of horses) and chehra (muster roll) inspections ensured compliance.

Land Revenue Reforms: Zabt and Ain‑i‑Dahsala

Akbar’s finance minister, Raja Todar Mal, codified the zabt system based on Sher Shah Suri’s earlier surveys. Cultivable land was measured using the bigha (≈0.62 acre) and classified into four categories (polaj, parauti, chachar, banjar) according to fertility and continuity of cultivation. The state demand was fixed at one‑third of the average produce (ain‑i‑dahsala) calculated over a ten‑year cycle, payable in cash after conversion using the dastur‑ul‑amal (schedule of prices). This system provided predictability for peasants and a stable revenue stream for the treasury.

Religious Policy and Institutional Integration

Akbar’s sulh‑i‑kul (universal peace) abolished the jizya (1564) and promoted a syncretic court culture, exemplified by the Ibadat Khana debates and the compilation of the Akbarnama and Ain‑i‑Akbari. Later emperors, especially Aurangzeb, reinstated the jizya (1679) and enforced a more orthodox Islamic legal framework, which strained relations with Rajput and Maratha chiefs and contributed to fiscal pressures.

Technique: Comparative Table Construction
A question asks: “Compare the revenue assessment method of the Mughal zabt system with the Vijayanagara kudimai system. Identify two structural differences.”
The Mughal zabt used a ten‑year average yield (ain‑i‑dahsala) on measured land (bigha) with a fixed one‑third state share, converted to cash via a price schedule. The Vijayanagara kudimai was a flat one‑sixth of the actual produce collected at village level, often in kind, without a multi‑year averaging mechanism. Structurally, zabt relied on centralised cadastral surveys and cash commutation, whereas kudimai operated through hereditary village accountants (karnams) and allowed payment in kind.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a comparative question appears, sketch a two‑column table in the margin: list the parameter (basis of assessment, unit of measurement, mode of payment, frequency of revision) for each system. This visual cue prevents conflating the two and saves time during answer writing.

Decline of the Mughals and the Rise of Regional Powers: Marathas, Sikhs, and Nawabs

The death of Aurangzeb in 1707 triggered a cascade of succession wars, fiscal exhaustion, and the erosion of central authority. The mansabdari system collapsed as jagirs became hereditary, and the zabt revenue records fell into disuse. Provincial governors (subahdars) asserted autonomy, founding successor states: the Nizam of Hyderabad (Asaf Jah I, 1724), the Nawab of Bengal (Murshid Quli Khan, 1717), and the Nawab of Awadh (Saadat Khan, 1722). Simultaneously, two indigenous movements reshaped the political landscape.

The Maratha Confederacy

Under the leadership of the Bhonsle family, the Marathas evolved from a guerrilla force (Shivaji, d. 1680) into a confederacy of five major houses — Peshwa (Poona), Holkar (Indore), Scindia (Gwalior), Bhonsle (Nagpur), and Gaekwad (Baroda). The Peshwa, originally the prime minister, became the de facto head after Balaji Vishwanath (1713–1720). The chauth (one‑fourth of revenue) and sardeshmukhi (additional ten percent) levies extracted from Mughal territories financed a mobile cavalry that dominated central and western India. The Third Battle of Panipat (1761) against Ahmad Shah Durrani halted northward expansion but did not destroy Maratha resilience; they recovered under Mahadji Scindia and re‑established influence in Delhi by 1771.

The Sikh Misls and the Khalsa Raj

In the Punjab, the Sikh community organised into twelve misls (confederacies) after the execution of Guru Gobind Singh (1708). The Dal Khalsa coordinated military action, and by the 1760s the misls controlled the region between the Sutlej and the Indus. Ranjit Singh (r. 1801–1839) unified the misls, captured Lahore (1799), and established the Sikh Empire, modernising the army with European officers (e.g., Jean‑François Allard) and creating a centralized revenue system (dastur‑ul‑amal adapted from Mughal practice).

Nawabs and the Carnatic Wars

The Carnatic region witnessed a proxy contest between the British and French East India Companies, each backing rival claimants to the Nawabship of Arcot. The Treaty of Paris (1763) ended French ambitions, leaving the British as the dominant European power. The Nawab of Bengal’s defeat at Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764) transferred the diwani (revenue rights) of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa to the East India Company, providing the financial base for further conquests.

European Trading Companies, Colonial Conquest, and the Establishment of British Paramountcy

Portuguese (1498), Dutch (1602), English (1600), and French (1664) companies initially sought pepper, textiles, and indigo. The English East India Company (EIC) secured a foothold at Surat (1613), Madras (1639), Bombay (1668), and Calcutta (1690). The Mughal farman of 1717 granted the EIC duty‑free trade in Bengal, a privilege that became a flashpoint after the Nawab Siraj‑ud‑Daula’s attempt to enforce customs duties led to the Battle of Plassey (1757). Robert Clive’s victory installed Mir Jafar as a puppet nawab and extracted massive indemnities.

From Diwani to Sovereignty

The Treaty of Allahabad (1765) granted the EIC the diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa, making it the de facto fiscal ruler of the richest Mughal province. The Regulating Act (1773) and Pitt’s India Act (1784) introduced parliamentary oversight, creating the post of Governor‑General of Bengal (Warren Hastings, 1773–1785) and a Supreme Court at Calcutta. The Permanent Settlement (1793) under Lord Cornwallis fixed land revenue permanently with zamindars in Bengal, creating a landed aristocracy loyal to the Company but impoverishing peasants.

Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse

Lord Wellesley (1798–1805) expanded British control through the Subsidiary Alliance: Indian rulers accepted a British resident, maintained a British‑paid contingent, and ceded external affairs. The Doctrine of Lapse (Lord Dalhousie, 1848–1856) annexed states lacking a direct male heir (Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur, Awadh). These policies, combined with the annexation of Punjab after the Anglo‑Sikh Wars (1845–1849) and the defeat of the Marathas in the Third Anglo‑Maratha War (1817–1818), brought the entire subcontinent under British paramountcy by 1857.

The Indian Freedom Struggle: Phases, Movements, and Constitutional Developments

The revolt of 1857 (Sepoy Mutiny) ended Company rule; the Government of India Act 1858 transferred sovereignty to the British Crown, establishing the Viceroy and the Secretary of State for India. The subsequent nationalist movement unfolded in overlapping phases.

Early Constitutional Agitation (1885–1905)

The Indian National Congress (INC), founded in 1885 by A.O. Hume and Indian leaders (Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta), initially petitioned for legislative councils, civil service reform, and reduction of military expenditure. The Indian Councils Act 1892 and 1909 (Morley‑Minto Reforms) introduced limited elective elements and separate electorates for Muslims, a precedent for communal representation.

Swadeshi and the Extremist Turn (1905–1918)

The Partition of Bengal (1905) sparked the Swadeshi Movement, promoting indigenous goods, national education, and boycott of British institutions. Leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, and Lala Lajpat Rai (the Lal‑Bal‑Pal trio) advocated Swaraj (self‑rule) and mass mobilisation. The Surat Split (1907) divided the Congress into Moderates and Extremists. The Home Rule Leagues (Tilak, Annie Besant, 1916) demanded dominion status on the Irish model.

Gandhian Mass Movements (1919–1942)

Mahatma Gandhi transformed the struggle through non‑violent non‑cooperation (1920–1922), the Salt March and Civil Disobedience (1930–1934), and the Quit India Movement (1942). The Government of India Act 1935 provided provincial autonomy and a federal framework (never fully implemented), expanding the electorate to about 30 million. The 1937 provincial elections brought Congress ministries in eight provinces, demonstrating administrative capacity.

Final Phase: Negotiations and Partition (1945–1947)

Post‑World War II, the British Labour government dispatched the Cabinet Mission (1946) proposing a loose federation. The failure to reconcile Congress and Muslim League demands led to the Mountbatten Plan (June 1947) and the Indian Independence Act 1947, partitioning British India into the dominions of India and Pakistan on 15 August 1947. The Constituent Assembly, elected in 1946, adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949, effective 26 January 1950, establishing a sovereign democratic republic.
