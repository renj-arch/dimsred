Historical Background and Making of the Constitution

1.1 The Demand for a Written Constitution

The demand for a written constitution in India arose from the experience of colonial rule, where the British governed through a series of statutes and executive orders that could be altered at will. Indian nationalists, especially after the Government of India Act 1935, argued that only a supreme legal document drafted by Indians themselves could guarantee civil liberties, federal distribution of powers, and a clear mechanism for amendment. The Indian National Congress formally resolved in 1934 that a constituent assembly elected on the basis of adult franchise should frame the constitution, a demand that gained momentum after the 1942 Quit India Movement and the subsequent negotiations with the British Crown.

When the Cabinet Mission arrived in 1946, it proposed a constituent assembly composed of representatives from provincial legislatures and princely states. The mission’s plan envisaged a federal union with a strong centre, but the Muslim League’s insistence on a separate constituent assembly for Pakistan led to the partition of the assembly itself. Consequently, the Indian Constituent Assembly, reduced to 299 members after partition, began its deliberations on 9 December 1946 under the temporary chairmanship of Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha, and later elected Dr. Rajendra Prasad as its permanent president.

1.2 The Constituent Assembly and Its Composition

The assembly was broadly representative, comprising members from the Congress, the Muslim League (until its withdrawal), the Scheduled Castes Federation, the Communist Party, and independents, as well as nominees from princely states. Notable figures included Dr. B. R. Ambedkar, who chaired the Drafting Committee, Jawaharlal Nehru, who moved the Objectives Resolution on 13 December 1946, and Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, who played a crucial role in integrating princely states. The assembly functioned through several committees — the Union Powers Committee, the Provincial Constitution Committee, the Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, and the Drafting Committee — each tasked with specific chapters of the constitution.

Debates in the assembly were exhaustive, covering issues such as the nature of the federation, the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles, the language question, and the provisions for emergency powers. The Drafting Committee, after studying constitutions of the United States, Canada, Australia, Ireland, and the United Kingdom, produced a draft that was circulated for public comment in 1948. The assembly met for 11 sessions over 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days, adopting the final text on 26 November 1949.

1.3 Adoption, Enforcement and the Date of Commencement

The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949, a date now celebrated as Constitution Day, but it came into force on 26 January 1950, chosen to commemorate the 1930 declaration of Purna Swaraj by the Indian National Congress. The commencement transformed the Dominion of India into the Republic of India, ending the British monarch’s role as head of state and establishing the President as the constitutional head. The original document contained 395 articles in 22 parts and 8 schedules; subsequent amendments have increased the article count to over 470 while preserving the basic structure.

The choice of 26 January also symbolised continuity with the nationalist struggle, linking the constitutional moment to the historic demand for complete independence. The Constitution’s preamble, adopted on the same day, declares India to be a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic, securing justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity for all its citizens.

Preamble and Salient Features of the Constitution

2.1 The Text and Philosophy of the Preamble

The Preamble serves as the philosophical keystone of the Constitution, encapsulating the objectives that the framers intended the polity to achieve. It begins with “We, the people of India,” asserting popular sovereignty and rejecting any external source of legitimacy. The words “sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic, republic” were deliberately chosen: “sovereign” affirms independence from foreign control; “socialist” (added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976) reflects the commitment to social and economic equity; “secular” (also added in 1976) mandates equal treatment of all religions by the state; “democratic” underscores representative government based on universal adult franchise; and “republic” indicates an elected head of state rather than a hereditary monarch.

The Preamble also enumerates the goals of justice — social, economic, and political — liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship, equality of status and opportunity, and fraternity assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the nation. The Supreme Court, in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), held that the Preamble is part of the Constitution and can be amended, but its basic structure cannot be altered, thereby elevating the Preamble to a justiciable interpretive tool.

2.2 Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic

Each adjective in the Preamble carries constitutional weight. Sovereignty means that the Indian state possesses supreme authority within its territory and is not subject to any external power; this is reflected in Article 1, which defines India as a Union of States, and in the exclusive power of Parliament to legislate on matters in the Union List. The socialist orientation is manifested in the Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV), especially Articles 38, 39, and 41, which direct the state to minimise inequalities in income and provide adequate means of livelihood. Secularism, though not defined exhaustively, has been interpreted by the judiciary to require the state to maintain neutrality among religions, as seen in the S. R. Bommai case (1994). Democracy is operationalised through regular elections under the Representation of the People Acts, and the republican character is embodied in the office of the President, elected by an electoral college under Article 54.

The interplay of these principles shapes legislative and executive action. For instance, the 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) institutionalised democratic decentralisation through Panchayats and Municipalities, giving concrete expression to the democratic and socialist ideals. The secular principle has guided the enactment of laws such as the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act, 1991, which seeks to preserve the religious character of places of worship as they existed on 15 August 1947.

2.3 Key Features Borrowed from Other Constitutions

The framers consciously borrowed institutional designs from several constitutions to suit Indian conditions. The parliamentary system, with a nominal head of state and a real executive responsible to the lower house, was modelled on the United Kingdom. The concept of fundamental rights and judicial review draws heavily from the United States Bill of Rights and the Marbury v. Madison precedent. The Directive Principles of State Policy echo the Irish Constitution’s directive principles of social policy. The federal scheme with a strong centre, residuary powers vested in the Union, and the appointment of governors by the centre follows the Canadian model. The Concurrent List, allowing both Union and State legislatures to legislate on certain subjects, is borrowed from Australia. The idea of a quasi‑federal structure with emergency provisions resembles the Weimar Constitution of Germany, while the provision for amendment by a special majority plus ratification by half the states mirrors the South African model.

These borrowings were not mechanical copies; they were adapted to India’s pluralistic society, vast geography, and developmental imperatives. For example, the Indian version of judicial review is broader than the American model because the Supreme Court can review constitutional amendments for violation of the basic structure, a doctrine absent in the United States. Similarly, the Indian parliamentary system incorporates a written constitution and a codified set of fundamental rights, unlike the unwritten British constitution.

Fundamental Rights, Duties and Directive Principles

3.1 Classification of Fundamental Rights under Part III

Part III of the Constitution (Articles 12 to 35) enumerates six groups of fundamental rights, each addressing a distinct sphere of individual liberty. The Right to Equality (Articles 14‑18) guarantees equality before law, prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, and abolishes untouchability and titles. The Right to Freedom (Articles 19‑22) protects six freedoms — speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession — subject to reasonable restrictions. The Right against Exploitation (Articles 23‑24) forbids traffic in human beings, forced labour, and employment of children in hazardous occupations. The Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25‑28) secures freedom of conscience, profession, practice, and propagation of religion, and manages religious affairs. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29‑30) protect the interests of minorities to conserve language, script, and culture and to establish educational institutions. Finally, the Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32) provides the enforcement mechanism.

The classification reflects a balance between individual liberty and social order. For instance, the reasonable restrictions under Article 19(2)‑(6) allow the state to impose limits in the interests of sovereignty, public order, decency, morality, contempt of court, defamation, and incitement to an offence. The Supreme Court has consistently held that these restrictions must be narrowly tailored and proportionate, as illustrated in the Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) judgment striking down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act.

3.2 Right to Equality and Its Judicial Interpretation

Article 14 embodies the principle of equality before law and equal protection of laws, a concept borrowed from the American Fourteenth Amendment but expanded by Indian courts to include the doctrine of reasonable classification and the prohibition of arbitrariness. In the landmark case of E. P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974), the Supreme Court declared that equality is a dynamic concept and that any arbitrary state action violates Article 14. Article 15 prohibits discrimination on specified grounds and permits special provisions for women, children, and socially and educationally backward classes, enabling affirmative action such as reservations in education and public employment.

Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity in matters of public employment, while Article 17 abolishes untouchability and makes its practice an offence punishable under the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955. Article 18 abolishes titles except military and academic distinctions, reinforcing the republican ethos. The judiciary has used these provisions to strike down discriminatory laws, for example, the Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) decision decriminalising consensual homosexual conduct by reading down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code as violative of Articles 14, 15, and 21.

3.3 Right to Freedom and Reasonable Restrictions

Article 19 guarantees six freedoms, each subject to specific grounds of restriction enumerated in clauses (2) to (6). Freedom of speech and expression may be restricted in the interests of sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the state, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, or incitement to an offence. The Supreme Court in Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950) held that freedom of speech includes freedom of propagation of ideas, and any restriction must be directly related to the permitted grounds. Freedom of assembly must be peaceful and without arms; the state may impose reasonable restrictions in the interest of public order. Freedom to form associations allows the state to regulate trade unions and political parties under laws such as the Trade Unions Act, 1926.

Freedom of movement and residence throughout the territory of India can be restricted for the protection of scheduled tribes or in the interest of the general public. Freedom to practice any profession or carry on any occupation, trade, or business is subject to professional qualifications and licensing requirements. The doctrine of proportionality, developed in cases like Modern Dental College v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2016), requires that any restriction be the least intrusive measure to achieve the legitimate aim.

3.4 Fundamental Duties and Directive Principles of State Policy

The 42nd Amendment (1976) inserted Article 51A, enumerating eleven fundamental duties, such as abiding by the Constitution, cherishing the ideals of the freedom struggle, protecting the sovereignty and integrity of India, defending the country, promoting harmony, preserving the rich heritage, protecting the environment, developing scientific temper, safeguarding public property, striving for excellence, and providing opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years. Although non‑justiciable, these duties serve as a moral compass for citizens and have been used by courts to interpret statutory obligations.

Part IV (Articles 36‑51) contains the Directive Principles of State Policy, which are guidelines for the state to achieve social and economic democracy. They are classified into socialist principles (e.g., Article 39 — equal pay for equal work), Gandhian principles (e.g., Article 40 — organisation of village panchayats), and liberal‑intellectual principles (e.g., Article 44 — uniform civil code). While not enforceable by courts, the Supreme Court in the Minerva Mills case (1980) held that the harmony between fundamental rights and directive principles is a basic feature of the Constitution, and the state must strive to implement them through legislation.

Parliament: Structure, Powers and Legislative Process

4.1 Composition and Term of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha

India’s Parliament is bicameral, consisting of the President, the Lok Sabha (House of the People), and the Rajya Sabha (Council of States). The Lok Sabha comprises a maximum of 552 members: up to 530 elected from territorial constituencies in the states, up to 20 from union territories, and not more than two nominated by the President from the Anglo‑Indian community (a provision that ceased after the 104th Amendment in 2020). Members serve a term of five years unless the House is dissolved earlier. The Rajya Sabha has a maximum strength of 250, of which 238 are elected by the legislative assemblies of states and union territories through proportional representation by means of a single transferable vote, and 12 are nominated by the President for expertise in literature, science, art, and social service. Rajya Sabha members serve staggered six‑year terms, with one‑third retiring every two years, ensuring continuity.

The composition reflects the federal principle: the Lok Sabha represents the people directly on the basis of population, while the Rajya Sabha represents the states as units, giving smaller states a voice disproportionate to their population. The Vice‑President of India serves as the ex‑officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, while the Lok Sabha elects its own Speaker and Deputy Speaker. The President summons, prorogues, and can dissolve the Lok Sabha, but cannot dissolve the Rajya Sabha.

4.2 Legislative Procedure and Money Bills

A bill may originate in either House, except a Money Bill, which can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha on the recommendation of the President. After passage by the originating House, the bill moves to the other House. For ordinary bills, the other House may reject, amend, or return the bill; if disagreement persists, the President may summon a joint sitting under Article 108, where the Lok Sabha’s numerical superiority usually prevails. Money Bills, defined under Article 110, deal exclusively with taxation, borrowing, expenditure from the Consolidated Fund, and related matters. The Rajya Sabha cannot amend a Money Bill; it may only make recommendations within fourteen days, which the Lok Sabha may accept or reject. The Speaker’s certification that a bill is a Money Bill is final and not subject to judicial review, a principle upheld in the Keshav Singh case (1965).

The legislative process also includes the President’s assent. The President may give assent, withhold assent, or return a non‑Money Bill for reconsideration. If Parliament passes the bill again, the President must give assent. This pocket veto power is rarely exercised. The Constitution also provides for the promulgation of ordinances by the President when Parliament is not in session, under Article 123, which have the same force as an Act but must be approved by Parliament within six weeks of reassembly.

4.3 Parliamentary Committees and Their Role

Parliamentary committees are essential for detailed scrutiny of legislation, budgetary proposals, and executive actions. They are of two types: standing committees, which are permanent and reconstituted annually, and ad hoc committees, formed for specific purposes. Key standing committees include the Public Accounts Committee (PAC), which examines the Comptroller and Auditor General’s reports; the Estimates Committee, which suggests economies in expenditure; the Committee on Public Undertakings, which reviews the performance of public sector enterprises; and department‑related standing committees (DRSCs) that oversee individual ministries. The PAC is traditionally chaired by a member of the opposition, ensuring accountability.

Committees operate with quasi‑judicial powers: they can summon witnesses, demand documents, and administer oaths. Their reports, though not binding, carry significant moral and political weight and often influence government policy. For example, the 2G spectrum allocation scandal was extensively examined by the Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC) in 2011, leading to policy reforms. The committee system thus compensates for the limited time available on the floor of the House and enhances the quality of legislative oversight.

Judiciary: Supreme Court, High Courts and Judicial Review

5.1 Organization and Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court of India, established under Article 124, is the apex judicial body and the final interpreter of the Constitution. It consists of the Chief Justice of India and a maximum of 33 other judges (as fixed by the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 2008). Judges are appointed by the President after consultation with the Chief Justice and such other judges as the Chief Justice may deem necessary, a process refined by the collegium system evolved in the Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993) and the Third Judges Case (1998). The Court sits in New Delhi but may hold sittings elsewhere with the Chief Justice’s approval.

The Supreme Court exercises original jurisdiction under Article 131 in disputes between the Government of India and one or more states, or between states. It has appellate jurisdiction under Articles 132‑136 over constitutional, civil, and criminal matters from High Courts, and special leave to appeal under Article 136 from any court or tribunal. Its advisory jurisdiction under Article 143 allows the President to seek its opinion on questions of law or fact of public importance. The Court’s power of judicial review, implicit in Articles 13, 32, 131‑136, and 226, enables it to strike down legislative and executive actions that violate the Constitution.

5.2 High Courts and the Doctrine of Judicial Review

Each state (or group of states) has a High Court under Article 214, with a Chief Justice and other judges appointed by the President in consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor, and the Chief Justice of the High Court. High Courts have original jurisdiction in revenue matters, admiralty, matrimonial, and contempt cases, and appellate jurisdiction over subordinate courts. Crucially, under Article 226, High Courts can issue writs — habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari — for enforcement of fundamental rights and for any other purpose, giving them a broader writ jurisdiction than the Supreme Court’s Article 32, which is limited to fundamental rights.

The doctrine of judicial review in India is more expansive than in the United States because the Supreme Court can review constitutional amendments for compliance with the basic structure, a principle established in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973). The basic structure includes supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. Subsequent cases such as Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) and Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) reinforced that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution so as to destroy its basic features.

5.3 Landmark Cases Shaping Constitutional Interpretation

Several landmark judgments have defined the contours of Indian constitutional law. In A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950), the Court initially adopted a narrow view of personal liberty under Article 21, but this was overturned in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), which held that the procedure established by law must be fair, just, and reasonable, thereby reading due process into Article 21. The S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) case laid down guidelines for the imposition of President’s Rule under Article 356, making it subject to judicial review. The Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) judgment created guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace, later codified in the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.

More recently, the Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) decision decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations, affirming dignity and equality under Articles 14, 15, 19, and 21. The Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017) judgment recognised the right to privacy as a fundamental right intrinsic to life and liberty. These cases illustrate how the judiciary, through interpretive activism, expands the protective scope of the Constitution while respecting the separation of powers.

The Indian constitutional framework thus rests on a delicate balance between a written supreme law, a parliamentary executive accountable to the legislature, and an independent judiciary empowered to safeguard fundamental rights and the basic structure, ensuring that the promise of the Preamble translates into lived reality for every citizen.

Federalism and Centre‑State Legislative Relations

The Constitution distributes legislative powers through three lists in the Seventh Schedule: the Union List (List I), the State List (List II) and the Concurrent List (List III). Parliament has exclusive authority over 97 subjects in the Union List, while state legislatures enjoy exclusive jurisdiction over 66 subjects in the State List. Both Parliament and state legislatures may legislate on the 47 subjects of the Concurrent List, but in case of conflict the central law prevails under Article 254, unless the state law has been reserved for the President’s assent and receives it.

Residuary powers belong to Parliament under Article 248, a departure from the Canadian model where residuary powers rest with the provinces. This centralising feature is balanced by the requirement that certain Union‑list matters — such as defence, foreign affairs and atomic energy — cannot be delegated to states. The Sarkaria Commission (1983‑88) recommended a cooperative federalism framework, emphasising consultation through the Inter‑State Council (Article 263) and a more restrained use of Article 356. The Punchhi Commission (2007‑10) later suggested a permanent Inter‑State Council and a clear demarcation of “national importance” subjects to reduce friction.

Financial relations are governed by the Finance Commission (Article 280) which recommends the vertical and horizontal sharing of tax revenues. The Goods and Services Tax (GST) introduced by the 101st Amendment (2016) created a dual GST structure with a GST Council (Article 279A) where the Centre and states jointly decide rates, thresholds and dispute resolution. This institutionalised fiscal federalism marks a significant shift from the earlier scheme where the Centre unilaterally determined central taxes and grants‑in‑aid.

Administrative relations allow the Centre to issue directions to states under Articles 256 and 257 for compliance with Union laws and for the maintenance of means of communication of national importance. The Centre can also deploy paramilitary forces in a state under Article 355 without invoking President’s Rule, a power upheld in the State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) judgment. However, the Supreme Court in S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) imposed strict judicial scrutiny on the use of Article 356, requiring material evidence of a breakdown of constitutional machinery.

Emergency Provisions and Judicial Review

The Constitution provides for three types of emergency: National Emergency (Article 352), State Emergency (President’s Rule, Article 356) and Financial Emergency (Article 360). A National Emergency can be proclaimed only on grounds of war, external aggression or armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment (1978) replaced “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion” and mandated that the proclamation be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month by a special majority. It also required the President to act on the written advice of the Cabinet, limiting executive discretion.

During a National Emergency, the federal structure tilts unitary: Parliament gains power to legislate on State List subjects (Article 250), the Centre can issue directions to states (Article 353), and the life of the Lok Sabha may be extended by one year at a time (Article 83). Fundamental Rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended, while Articles 20 and 21 remain enforceable. The Supreme Court in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) held that the power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 cannot be used to destroy the basic structure, and that judicial review of emergency proclamations is permissible.

President’s Rule under Article 356 is invoked when the President is satisfied that a state government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The 44th Amendment added a requirement that the proclamation be approved by Parliament within two months and that it cannot exceed three years without repeated parliamentary approval. The S. R. Bommai judgment laid down that the satisfaction of the President is subject to judicial review, that the assembly cannot be dissolved before parliamentary approval, and that secularism is a basic feature whose violation justifies central intervention.

Financial Emergency (Article 360) has never been proclaimed. It empowers the President to direct states to observe canons of financial propriety and to reduce salaries of all or any class of persons serving in the state, including judges of the High Courts. The provision remains largely dormant but underscores the Constitution’s centralising potential in fiscal crises.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Which Fundamental Rights are suspended automatically during a National Emergency?” Options include Article 14, Article 19, Article 21, Article 32. The correct answer is Article 19 only; Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended, and Article 32 (right to constitutional remedies) remains available for enforcement of Articles 20 and 21.
During a National Emergency under Article 352, Article 358 provides that the six freedoms under Article 19 are suspended. Article 359 allows the President to suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of other Fundamental Rights, but the 44th Amendment barred suspension of Articles 20 and 21. Therefore, only Article 19 is automatically suspended; the others require a separate presidential order and cannot include Articles 20 and 21.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the three emergency types, their triggering articles, the parliamentary approval timelines (one month for Art. 352, two months for Art. 356) and the specific Fundamental Rights that are immune from suspension (Art. 20, 21). A quick mental checklist — “War/Armed rebellion → Art. 352 → Art. 19 suspended; State breakdown → Art. 356 → Bommai guidelines; Fiscal crisis → Art. 360 (never used)” — helps eliminate wrong options in 10‑second MCQs.

Constitutional Amendment Procedure and Basic Structure Doctrine

Article 368 governs constitutional amendments. An amendment bill must be passed by each House of Parliament by a majority of the total membership and by a two‑thirds majority of members present and voting. Certain amendments — those affecting the federal structure, the representation of states in Parliament, the extent of executive power of the Union or states, the judiciary, or the amendment procedure itself — additionally require ratification by the legislatures of not less than one‑half of the states (Article 368 proviso). This “special majority + state ratification” category includes the 7th, 24th, 39th, 42nd, 44th, 73rd, 74th, 86th, 99th and 101st Amendments.

The basic structure doctrine, propounded in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), limits Parliament’s amending power. The Supreme Court held that while Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, it cannot alter its “basic structure”. The doctrine has been elaborated in subsequent cases: Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) added free and fair elections; Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) included judicial review and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) clarified that amendments made before 24 April 1973 are immune from basic‑structure challenge. The list of basic features is not exhaustive but includes supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, rule of law, and the unity and integrity of the nation.

The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to curtail judicial review by inserting Articles 31C, 31D, 32A, 131A, 144A, 226A, 228A and by declaring that Parliament’s constituent power was unlimited. The Supreme Court struck down several of these provisions in Minerva Mills, reaffirming that the amending power under Article 368 is not a constituent power to rewrite the Constitution’s identity. The 44th Amendment (1978) restored many pre‑42nd‑Amendment positions, including the requirement of Cabinet advice for emergency proclamations and the removal of the “internal disturbance” ground.

Recent amendments illustrate the interplay of procedure and basic structure. The 99th Amendment (2014) establishing the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) was struck down in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) for violating judicial independence, a basic feature. The 101st Amendment (2016) introducing GST passed the special‑majority‑plus‑state‑ratification test because it altered the fiscal federal balance. The 103rd Amendment (2019) providing 10 % reservation for economically weaker sections in education and public employment was upheld in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) as not violating the basic structure, the Court holding that economic criteria for reservation does not alter the Constitution’s egalitarian core.

Judicial Appointments: Collegium, NJAC, and Recent Developments

The appointment of Supreme Court and High Court judges has evolved from executive dominance to a judge‑led collegium system. The original Article 124(2) required the President to appoint judges after consultation with the Chief Justice of India (CJI). In S. P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) the Court held that “consultation” did not mean “concurrence”. This was reversed in the Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993) (Second Judges Case), which introduced the collegium of the CJI and the two senior‑most Supreme Court judges for Supreme Court appointments, and the CJI plus two senior‑most High Court judges for High Court appointments. The Third Judges Case (1998) expanded the Supreme Court collegium to the CJI and four senior‑most judges.

The 99th Constitutional Amendment (2014) and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014 created the NJAC comprising the CJI, two senior Supreme Court judges, the Union Law Minister, and two eminent persons nominated by a committee of the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition and CJI. The Supreme Court struck down both the amendment and the Act in 2015, ruling that the inclusion of the executive and politicians impaired judicial independence, a basic structure feature. The Court revived the collegium system but directed the government to finalise a Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) for transparency, eligibility criteria, and a secretariat.

Since 2015, the MoP has been under negotiation. The Supreme Court in 2017 issued guidelines for the collegium to record reasons for recommendations, consider diversity, and publish names of recommended candidates. The government has occasionally returned names for reconsideration, leading to public debates on “judicial accountability” versus “independence”. In 2023, the Court reiterated that the collegium’s primacy is non‑negotiable, while urging the executive to clear pending recommendations within a reasonable time.

High Court appointments follow a similar collegium: the Chief Justice of the High Court and two senior‑most judges recommend names to the Supreme Court collegium, which then forwards them to the President. The 2015 judgment also mandated that the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court must be consulted for transfers of High Court judges, reinforcing the principle of judicial primacy in transfers.

Anti‑Defection Law and Political Party Regulation

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment (1985), aims to curb political defections. A member of Parliament or a state legislature incurs disqualification if he voluntarily gives up membership of his party, votes or abstains contrary to party direction without prior permission, or joins another party after election. The law applies to both nominated and elected members. The 91st Amendment (2003) removed the provision allowing a split of one‑third of a legislature party to avoid disqualification, making a merger with another party the only exemption, provided at least two‑thirds of the original party’s members agree.

The Speaker (or Chairman in the Council of States) decides disqualification petitions. The Supreme Court in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) upheld the Speaker’s adjudicatory role but held that the decision is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fide, perversity, or violation of natural justice. The Court also ruled that the Speaker’s decision is not final until the period for judicial review expires.

Recent controversies include the 2019 Karnataka and 2022 Maharashtra political crises where large‑scale defections tested the anti‑defection framework. In Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v. Speaker, Karnataka Legislative Assembly (2020), the Supreme Court directed the Speaker to decide disqualification petitions within a reasonable time, emphasising that indefinite delay undermines the Tenth Schedule’s purpose. The Court also clarified that a member’s resignation does not automatically cure a pending disqualification; the Speaker must still adjudicate.

The Election Commission, under Article 324, registers political parties and enforces the Representation of the People Act, 1951. Parties must submit audited accounts, disclose funding sources, and adhere to internal democracy norms. The 2013 amendment to the Representation of the People Act mandated that parties file contribution reports above ₹20,000. The 2017 Electoral Bonds Scheme, while providing anonymity to donors, has been challenged for opacity; the Supreme Court in 2024 directed the Election Commission to disclose bond details, linking party funding transparency to the anti‑defection objective of preventing undue influence.

  • Confusing the special‑majority requirement for ordinary amendments with the additional state‑ratification requirement for federal‑structure amendments.
  • Assuming Article 19 freedoms are suspended during President’s Rule; they are suspended only under a National Emergency (Art. 352).
  • Believing the NJAC judgment abolished the collegium; it restored the collegium and mandated a Memorandum of Procedure.
  • Thinking a split of one‑third of a legislature party still avoids disqualification; the 91st Amendment removed that exception.
  • Treating the Speaker’s decision on disqualification as final and non‑reviewable; it is subject to judicial review for mala fide or perversity.

President and Vice‑President: Election, Powers, and Accountability

The President is the constitutional head of the Union executive. Election is indirect: an electoral college consisting of the elected members of both Houses of Parliament and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States (including the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union Territory of Puducherry) votes by the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote (Art. 54, 55). The President holds office for a term of five years from the date on which he enters upon his office (Art. 56) and is eligible for re‑election. Removal is possible only through impeachment for violation of the Constitution, initiated by either House of Parliament with a two‑thirds majority of the total membership of that House (Art. 61).

Executive power of the Union is vested in the President and exercised either directly or through officers subordinate to him (Art. 53). The President appoints the Prime Minister and, on the Prime Minister’s advice, the Council of Ministers (Art. 75). Other key appointments include the Chief Justice and judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts (Art. 124, 217), the Comptroller and Auditor General (Art. 148), the Chairman and members of the Union Public Service Commission (Art. 316), the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners (Art. 324), and the Governors of States (Art. 155). The President also appoints the Attorney General (Art. 76) and the Finance Commission (Art. 280).

Legislative powers include summoning and proroguing Parliament, dissolving the Lok Sabha (Art. 85), addressing both Houses (Art. 86), and sending messages (Art. 86). The President gives assent to bills passed by Parliament; a bill becomes law only after presidential assent (Art. 111). The President may return a non‑money bill for reconsideration (Art. 111). When Parliament is not in session, the President may promulgate ordinances having the same force as an Act of Parliament (Art. 123); such ordinances must be laid before Parliament within six weeks of reassembly and cease to operate if not approved.

Judicial powers comprise the granting of pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, and the power to suspend, remit or commute sentences (Art. 72). The President also has the power to refer questions of law or fact to the Supreme Court for advisory opinion (Art. 143).

The Vice‑President is elected by an electoral college consisting of members of both Houses of Parliament (Art. 66) and serves as the ex‑officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Art. 64). The Vice‑President holds office for five years (Art. 67) and may be removed by a resolution of the Rajya Sabha passed by a majority of its members and agreed to by the Lok Sabha (Art. 67(b)). In the event of a vacancy in the office of the President, the Vice‑President acts as President until a new President is elected (Art. 65).

Governor and State Executive: Appointment, Discretionary Powers, and Centre‑State Dynamics

The Governor is the constitutional head of a State, appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal (Art. 155). The Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President, but the term is normally five years (Art. 156). The Governor’s executive power extends to all matters with respect to which the State Legislature has power to make laws (Art. 154). The Governor appoints the Chief Minister and, on the Chief Minister’s advice, the Council of Ministers (Art. 164). Other appointments include the Advocate General (Art. 165), the State Election Commissioner (Art. 243K), and the Chairman and members of the State Public Service Commission (Art. 316).

The Governor’s discretionary powers are a focal point of Centre‑State relations. Under Art. 163, the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in matters where the Constitution requires the Governor to act in his discretion. Such matters include: (i) reservation of a bill for the President’s consideration (Art. 200); (ii) recommendation for President’s Rule in the State (Art. 356); (iii) appointment of a Chief Minister when no party has a clear majority; (iv) dismissal of a ministry that has lost majority support but refuses to resign; (v) seeking information from the Chief Minister on administrative matters (Art. 167). The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) held that the Governor’s report under Art. 356 is subject to judicial review and that the President’s satisfaction must be based on objective material.

Legislative functions of the Governor include summoning, proroguing and dissolving the State Legislative Assembly (Art. 174), addressing the Legislature (Art. 175), and giving assent to bills (Art. 200). The Governor may reserve a bill for the President’s assent; the President may then either assent, withhold assent, or direct the Governor to return the bill for reconsideration (Art. 201). The Governor also promulgates ordinances when the Legislature is not in session (Art. 213), subject to the same six‑week parliamentary approval requirement as Union ordinances.

The Governor’s role in the administration of Union Territories with legislatures (Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir) is distinct. In Delhi, the Lieutenant Governor (LG) exercises functions under Art. 239AA, with the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in matters of land, police and public order, where the LG acts on the President’s direction. The Supreme Court in NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018) clarified that the LG’s concurrence is not required on matters within the legislative competence of the Delhi Assembly, but the LG may refer differences to the President.

Local Self‑Government: Panchayats, Municipalities, and the 73rd/74th Amendments

The Constitution (73rd Amendment) Act, 1992 inserted Part IX (Arts. 243‑243O) providing for a three‑tier system of Panchayats in every State: Gram Panchayat at the village level, Panchayat Samiti at the intermediate (block) level, and Zilla Parishad at the district level. States with a population below 20 lakhs may omit the intermediate tier. The Amendment mandates direct elections to all seats, reservation of not less than one‑third of seats for women (including SC/ST women), and reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population. The term of each Panchayat is five years (Art. 243E); elections must be completed before the expiry of the term or within six months of dissolution.

The Constitution (74th Amendment) Act, 1992 inserted Part IXA (Arts. 243P‑243ZG) for Municipalities. Three types are envisaged: Nagar Panchayat for transitional areas, Municipal Council for smaller urban areas, and Municipal Corporation for larger urban areas. Composition mirrors the Panchayat pattern: direct elections, reservation for women (one‑third), SC/ST reservation, and a five‑year term (Art. 243U). Both Amendments provide for a State Election Commission (Art. 243K, 243ZA) to supervise elections and a State Finance Commission (Art. 243I, 243Y) to review the financial position of local bodies and recommend devolution of taxes, duties, tolls and fees.

Functional devolution is guided by the Eleventh Schedule (Panchayats) and Twelfth Schedule (Municipalities), each listing 29 and 18 subjects respectively. However, actual transfer of functions, funds and functionaries (the “3 Fs”) remains a State subject; the Constitution only obliges the State Legislature to endow Panchayats and Municipalities with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as institutions of self‑government (Arts. 243G, 243W). The Supreme Court in K. Krishnamurthy v. Union of India (2010) emphasised that the State cannot dilute the mandatory reservation provisions.

Financial resources include grants from the State Consolidated Fund on the recommendation of the State Finance Commission, a share of certain State taxes (e.g., land revenue, stamp duty), and centrally sponsored scheme funds routed through the State. The 14th and 15th Finance Commissions have recommended substantial increases in grants to local bodies, linking disbursement to performance benchmarks such as audited accounts and timely elections.

Union Territories and Special Provisions: Administration of Delhi, Puducherry, and Article 371 Variants

Union Territories (UTs) are administered by the President through an Administrator appointed under Art. 239. The Administrator may be designated as Lieutenant Governor (LG), Chief Commissioner or Administrator. For UTs with a legislative assembly (Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir), the Administrator acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in matters where the Constitution requires the Administrator to act in his discretion or on the President’s direction.

Delhi (National Capital Territory) enjoys a special status under Art. 239AA, inserted by the 69th Amendment (1991). It has a Legislative Assembly with powers to make laws on all matters in the State List and Concurrent List except three subjects: public order, police, and land. The LG is bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers on transferred subjects but may refer any matter to the President for decision. The Supreme Court in NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018) held that the LG’s concurrence is not required on matters within the Assembly’s competence, and that the LG must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in the three reserved subjects.

Puducherry has a Legislative Assembly under Art. 239A (inserted by the 14th Amendment, 1962) with powers similar to a State Legislature but subject to the President’s overriding authority. The LG of Puducherry exercises discretion in matters of finance, law and order, and services as specified by the President.

Article 371 contains special provisions for several States, often to protect cultural, economic or administrative interests. Notable variants include: Art. 371A (Nagaland) – safeguards Naga customary law and land ownership; Art. 371B (Assam) – provides for a committee of the Legislative Assembly for tribal areas; Art. 371C (Manipur) – establishes a Hill Areas Committee; Art. 371D and 371E (Andhra Pradesh) – provide for equitable opportunities in public employment and education; Art. 371F (Sikkim) – protects Sikkim’s existing laws and the Legislative Assembly’s composition; Art. 371G (Mizoram) – similar to Nagaland; Art. 371H (Arunachal Pradesh) – Governor’s special responsibility for law and order; Art. 371I (Goa) – minimum 30‑member Assembly; Art. 371J (Karnataka) – special provisions for Hyderabad‑Karnataka region. These provisions are entrenched and cannot be amended by a simple majority; they require the special majority under Art. 368 and, where they affect federal structure, ratification by half the States.

Constitutional Bodies beyond the Judiciary: CAG, Finance Commission, UPSC, Election Commission, and National Commissions

The Constitution establishes several independent bodies to safeguard fiscal accountability, fair recruitment, free elections, and the rights of marginalised communities. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) is appointed by the President under Art. 148 and holds office for six years or until age 65, whichever is earlier. The CAG audits all receipts and expenditure of the Union and States, and submits reports to the President (for Union) and Governor (for States) which are laid before Parliament and State Legislatures respectively. The CAG’s independence is secured by a removal process identical to that of a Supreme Court judge (Art. 148(1)).

The Finance Commission is constituted by the President every five years under Art. 280. It consists of a Chairman and four other members appointed by the President. Its core mandate is to recommend the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and States (vertical sharing) and the allocation among States (horizontal sharing), and to suggest measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of States to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities. The 15th Finance Commission (2020‑25) introduced performance‑based incentives for States in areas such as GST compliance, demographic performance, and forest cover.

The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) under Art. 315‑323 is the central recruiting agency for All India Services and Central Services (Group A and B). The Chairman and members are appointed by the President for a term of six years or until age 65. The UPSC conducts examinations, advises on recruitment rules, disciplinary matters, and promotions. Its recommendations are advisory but the government is bound to consult it on specified matters. State Public Service Commissions mirror the UPSC at the State level.

The Election Commission (EC) under Art. 324 consists of the Chief Election Commissioner and such number of Election Commissioners as the President may fix. The CEC and ECs have a tenure of six years or until age 65. The EC superintends, directs and controls the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice‑President. The EC also enforces the Model Code of Conduct, regulates political party registration under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, and monitors election expenditure. The Supreme Court in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2002) mandated disclosure of criminal antecedents, assets and liabilities of candidates.

National Commissions for Scheduled Castes (NCSC) and Scheduled Tribes (NCST) are established under Arts. 338 and 338A respectively. Each consists of a Chairperson, Vice‑Chairperson and three other members appointed by the President. They investigate and monitor safeguards, inquire into specific complaints, and advise on socio‑economic development. The National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) was given constitutional status by the 102nd Amendment (2018) under Art. 338B. Other statutory bodies such as the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC, 1993), Central Information Commission (CIC, 2005), and NITI Aayog (2015, executive resolution) operate outside the Constitution but are crucial for governance.
