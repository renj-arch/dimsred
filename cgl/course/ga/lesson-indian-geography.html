1. Physical Divisions of India

1.1 The Himalayan Mountain System

The Himalayas form the northern frontier of the Indian subcontinent, stretching approximately 2,400 kilometres from the Indus gorge in the west to the Brahmaputra gorge in the east. These young fold mountains originated from the collision of the Indian Plate with the Eurasian Plate, a tectonic process that continues to push the range upward by several millimetres each year. The system comprises three parallel ranges separated by longitudinal valleys. The northernmost range, the Great Himalayas or Himadri, contains the highest peaks including Kanchenjunga (8,586 metres), Nanda Devi (7,816 metres), and Kamet (7,756 metres); this range remains snowbound throughout the year and feeds the perennial glaciers that sustain the great north Indian rivers. South of the Himadri lies the Lesser Himalayas or Himachal, with altitudes between 3,700 and 4,500 metres, hosting famous hill stations such as Shimla, Mussoorie, and Darjeeling. The outermost range, the Shiwaliks, rises to 900–1,200 metres and consists of unconsolidated sediments brought down by Himalayan rivers; the longitudinal valleys between the Lesser Himalayas and the Shiwaliks are known as duns, with Dehra Dun being the most prominent example. Transversely, the Himalayas are divided into the Kashmir, Himachal, Garhwal, Kumaon, Nepal, and Assam Himalayas based on regional river basins and cultural boundaries.

1.2 The Northern Plains

South of the Himalayas lies the vast alluvial tract known as the Northern Plains, formed by the depositional work of the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra river systems and their tributaries over millions of years. The plains extend approximately 3,200 kilometres from east to west and vary in width from 150 to 300 kilometres, covering an area of about 7 lakh square kilometres. The alluvium is broadly divided into newer alluvium (khadar) found in the floodplains, which is light, sandy, and annually renewed, and older alluvium (bhangar) lying above the flood level, which contains calcareous concretions known as kankar. The plains are further subdivided into the Punjab Plains (drained by the Indus and its tributaries), the Ganga Plains (spreading across Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and West Bengal), and the Brahmaputra Plains (confined largely to Assam). The gentle gradient of these plains — often less than 20 centimetres per kilometre — makes them prone to seasonal flooding but also renders them among the most fertile and densely populated agricultural regions in the world.

1.3 The Peninsular Plateau

The Peninsular Plateau constitutes the oldest and most stable landmass of India, composed largely of Archaean gneisses and schists that have resisted tectonic upheaval since the Precambrian era. It is broadly divided by the Narmada rift valley into the Central Highlands to the north and the Deccan Plateau to the south. The Central Highlands include the Aravalli Range — one of the oldest fold mountain systems in the world — the Malwa Plateau, the Bundelkhand Upland, and the Chotanagpur Plateau, which is rich in mineral resources such as coal, iron ore, and mica. The Deccan Plateau, formed by massive basaltic lava flows during the Cretaceous period, slopes gently eastward, a tilt that directs its major rivers — Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri — toward the Bay of Bengal. The western edge of the Deccan is marked by the Western Ghats (Sahyadris), a continuous escarpment rising to 1,000–1,600 metres that intercepts the moisture-laden southwest monsoon winds, while the eastern edge is defined by the discontinuous Eastern Ghats, which average 600 metres in elevation and are dissected by river valleys.

1.4 The Indian Desert and Coastal Plains

The Thar Desert, also called the Great Indian Desert, occupies the north-western part of the country, primarily in western Rajasthan and extending into Gujarat, Punjab, and Haryana. It is an arid region of undulating sand dunes, rocky outcrops, and saline depressions, receiving less than 150 millimetres of annual rainfall; the Luni River is the only significant watercourse, and it disappears into the Rann of Kachchh. The coastal plains flank the Peninsular Plateau on both sides. The Western Coastal Plain, sandwiched between the Western Ghats and the Arabian Sea, is narrow (50–80 kilometres) and subdivided into the Konkan Coast (Maharashtra–Goa), the Kanara Coast (Karnataka), and the Malabar Coast (Kerala); it features estuaries, lagoons, and natural harbours such as Mumbai and Kochi. The Eastern Coastal Plain is wider (100–130 kilometres) and more level, comprising the Coromandel Coast (Tamil Nadu–Andhra Pradesh) and the Northern Circars (Odisha–Andhra Pradesh); it is characterised by large deltas formed by the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri rivers, which support intensive paddy cultivation.

1.5 The Island Groups

India’s territory includes two major island groups. The Andaman and Nicobar Islands, situated in the Bay of Bengal, form an arcuate chain of 572 islands (only 38 inhabited) extending over 800 kilometres; they are of volcanic origin, with Barren Island hosting India’s only active volcano, and are covered by dense tropical rainforests. The Ten Degree Channel separates the Andaman group from the Nicobar group. The Lakshadweep Islands, located in the Arabian Sea 200–440 kilometres off the Malabar Coast, consist of 36 coral atolls and reefs with a total land area of merely 32 square kilometres; they are built on the submerged Chagos-Laccadive Ridge and support coconut plantations and a unique marine ecosystem.

2. River Systems of India

2.1 Himalayan Rivers: Perennial Glacier-Fed Systems

The Himalayan rivers — Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra — are antecedent rivers, meaning they existed before the uplift of the Himalayas and maintained their courses by cutting down at the same rate as the mountains rose. The Indus rises near Lake Mansarovar in Tibet, enters India through Ladakh, and flows through Jammu & Kashmir before entering Pakistan; its major Indian tributaries are the Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Satluj, which together give Punjab its name (land of five rivers). The Ganga originates as the Bhagirathi from the Gangotri Glacier, joins the Alaknanda at Devprayag, and enters the plains at Haridwar; it is fed by left-bank tributaries such as the Ramganga, Gomti, Ghaghara, Gandak, and Kosi, and right-bank tributaries including the Yamuna, Son, and Damodar. The Brahmaputra, known as the Yarlung Tsangpo in Tibet, makes a sharp U-turn at the Namcha Barwa peak, enters India as the Dihang in Arunachal Pradesh, and flows through Assam as a braided river prone to catastrophic flooding and channel shifting; its major tributaries include the Dibang, Lohit, Subansiri, and Manas. These rivers are perennial because they receive meltwater from glaciers in summer and rainfall during the monsoon, making them the lifeline of north Indian agriculture and navigation.

2.2 Peninsular Rivers: Seasonal Rain-Fed Systems

Peninsular rivers are largely consequent rivers that follow the general slope of the plateau from west to east. The Godavari, often called the Dakshin Ganga, is the longest peninsular river (1,465 kilometres), rising at Trimbakeshwar in Maharashtra and draining a basin covering Maharashtra, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, and Odisha before forming a large delta on the east coast. The Krishna (1,400 kilometres) rises near Mahabaleshwar and flows through Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Andhra Pradesh; its major tributaries are the Bhima, Tungabhadra, and Ghataprabha. The Kaveri (800 kilometres) originates at Talakaveri in the Brahmagiri range of Karnataka and is unique among peninsular rivers for receiving rainfall from both the southwest and northeast monsoons, giving it a more reliable flow; it forms the sacred Srirangam island and a fertile delta in Tamil Nadu. The Mahanadi (858 kilometres) rises in Chhattisgarh and drains into the Bay of Bengal near Cuttack, forming a delta that supports the Hirakud Dam — one of the longest earthen dams in the world. The Narmada (1,312 kilometres) and Tapi (724 kilometres) are exceptional west-flowing rivers that occupy rift valleys formed by faulting; they form estuaries rather than deltas and are important for hydroelectric projects such as Sardar Sarovar on the Narmada.

2.3 River Regimes and Interlinking Debates

The regime of a river refers to the seasonal pattern of its discharge. Himalayan rivers exhibit a double peak — one from snowmelt in April–June and another from monsoon rainfall in July–September — resulting in a relatively sustained flow throughout the year. Peninsular rivers, by contrast, are monsoonal: they swell dramatically during June–October and dwindle to a trickle in the dry months, making them unsuitable for year-round navigation without storage. This disparity underpins the National River Linking Project, which proposes transferring surplus water from the Brahmaputra and Ganga basins to deficit peninsular basins through a network of canals; the Ken-Betwa link in the Bundelkhand region was the first to receive clearance, though ecological concerns regarding forest submergence in Panna Tiger Reserve and inter-state water disputes remain significant hurdles.

2.4 Major Lakes and Wetlands

India’s lakes are classified by origin. Tectonic lakes include Wular Lake in Kashmir (the largest freshwater lake in India) and the saltwater Pangong Tso and Tso Moriri in Ladakh. Crater lakes are represented by Lonar Lake in Maharashtra, formed by a meteorite impact. Lagoons along the coast include Chilika Lake in Odisha (Asia’s largest brackish water lagoon and a Ramsar site), Pulicat Lake on the Andhra–Tamil Nadu border, and Vembanad Lake in Kerala. Artificial reservoirs created by dams — such as Gobind Sagar (Bhakra Dam), Nagarjuna Sagar, and Indira Sagar — now dominate the inland water surface area and serve irrigation, power, and flood control. Wetlands like the Sundarbans (the world’s largest mangrove forest), Keoladeo Ghana (Bharatpur), and Loktak Lake (Manipur, with its floating phumdis) are critical for biodiversity and have been designated under the Ramsar Convention.

Solution: The river described is the Krishna. It originates near Mahabaleshwar at an elevation of about 1,337 metres, traverses approximately 1,400 kilometres, and drains a basin of 2.59 lakh square kilometres. The Bhima and Tungabhadra are its principal right-bank tributaries, while the Ghataprabha and Malaprabha join from the left. The Krishna delta, shared by Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, is a major rice-producing region.

3. Climate and Monsoon Mechanisms

3.1 Factors Influencing India’s Climate

India’s climate is governed by its latitudinal extent (8°4′ N to 37°6′ N), the Himalayan barrier, the surrounding oceans, and upper-air circulation. The Tropic of Cancer divides the country into a subtropical north and a tropical south, but the Himalayas prevent cold Central Asian winds from penetrating the subcontinent in winter, giving India a warmer climate than other regions at similar latitudes. The Indian Ocean, Bay of Bengal, and Arabian Sea act as heat reservoirs and moisture sources. In the upper troposphere, the subtropical westerly jet stream flows south of the Himalayas in winter, bringing western disturbances that cause rain in the north-west; in summer, it shifts north of the Himalayas and is replaced by the tropical easterly jet stream over the peninsula, which aids monsoon onset. The Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) migrates northward over the Gangetic plains in summer, creating a low-pressure trough that draws in moisture-laden winds.

3.2 The Southwest Monsoon: Onset, Advance, and Withdrawal

The southwest monsoon, which delivers 70–90 percent of India’s annual rainfall, is driven by the differential heating of the Tibetan Plateau and the Indian Ocean. Intense summer heating over Tibet creates a massive low-pressure cell, while the Mascarene High near Madagascar strengthens in the southern hemisphere. The resulting pressure gradient pulls the southeast trade winds across the equator, where the Coriolis force deflects them into southwesterlies. The monsoon arrives in two branches: the Arabian Sea branch strikes the Western Ghats around 1 June, giving heavy rainfall to the windward side (Mumbai, Mangalore, Thiruvananthapuram) and a rain shadow to the leeward Deccan; the Bay of Bengal branch moves up the Gangetic plains, bifurcating at the Himalayan foothills — one arm moves westward along the plains (Delhi receives rain by late June) and the other enters the Brahmaputra valley (Cherrapunji and Mawsynram receive the world’s highest rainfall). Withdrawal begins in early September from Rajasthan and proceeds southeastward, completing by mid-October.

3.3 The Retreating Monsoon and Northeast Monsoon

As the sun moves southward after the autumnal equinox, the low-pressure trough over north India weakens and shifts toward the equator. The southwest monsoon winds retreat, and clear skies return to most of the country — this is the retreating monsoon season (October–December). However, the retreating winds pick up moisture over the Bay of Bengal and bring a second rainfall spell to the Coromandel Coast (Tamil Nadu, coastal Andhra Pradesh), known as the northeast monsoon. This is the primary rainy season for Tamil Nadu, which lies in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats during the southwest monsoon. Cyclonic depressions originating in the Andaman Sea frequently cross the east coast during this period, causing heavy rain and storm surges.

3.4 Climatic Regions and Seasons

India is traditionally described as having four seasons: cold weather (December–February), hot weather (March–May), southwest monsoon (June–September), and retreating monsoon (October–November). Koeppen’s classification, however, identifies distinct climatic regions: Amw (tropical monsoon with short dry season) along the west coast and Andaman Islands; Aw (tropical savanna) over most of the peninsula; BShw (semi-arid steppe) in the rain-shadow Deccan and central India; BWhw (hot desert) in western Rajasthan; Cwg (humid subtropical) in the northern plains; Dfc (cold humid) in the higher Himalayas; and ET (tundra) above the snow line. These regions determine cropping patterns, natural vegetation, and human settlement density.

3.5 El Niño, La Niña, and the Indian Ocean Dipole

Year-to-year monsoon variability is strongly linked to ocean-atmosphere phenomena. El Niño — the anomalous warming of the central and eastern equatorial Pacific — weakens the Walker Circulation, reduces the pressure gradient driving the monsoon, and is statistically associated with below-normal rainfall in India (e.g., 2002, 2009, 2015). La Niña, the opposite phase, tends to enhance monsoon rainfall (e.g., 2010, 2020). The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), a seesaw of sea-surface temperatures between the western and eastern Indian Ocean, modulates this relationship: a positive IOD (warmer western Indian Ocean) can offset an El Niño-induced deficit, as seen in 1997 and 2019. The India Meteorological Department (IMD) uses these indices, along with the Monsoon Mission Coupled Forecasting System, to issue long-range forecasts in April and June.

4. Soils and Natural Vegetation

4.1 Major Soil Groups and Their Distribution

Indian soils are classified by the Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) into eight major groups based on genesis, colour, composition, and location. Alluvial soils, covering about 46 percent of the land area, dominate the Northern Plains and coastal deltas; they are rich in potash and lime but deficient in nitrogen and phosphorus, requiring fertiliser inputs for sustained yields. Black soils (Regur), derived from Deccan basalt, occupy the lava plateau of Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, and parts of Telangana; their high clay content (montmorillonite) gives them self-ploughing cracks in summer and excellent moisture retention, making them ideal for cotton. Red soils, formed on crystalline granites and gneisses in Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Odisha, are porous, low in humus, and require irrigation and manuring. Laterite soils, resulting from intense leaching in high-rainfall areas of the Western Ghats, Eastern Ghats, and Meghalaya, are acidic, poor in nutrients, but support plantation crops like tea, coffee, and cashew when managed. Arid soils in western Rajasthan are sandy, saline, and low in organic matter; forest soils in the Himalayas vary from acidic podzols at high altitudes to fertile loams in the valleys; peaty and marshy soils occur in waterlogged areas of Kerala (kari lands), Sundarbans, and the deltas.

4.2 Natural Vegetation Types

Natural vegetation follows climate and soil gradients. Tropical evergreen forests, with no distinct leaf-shedding season, occur where rainfall exceeds 200 centimetres — the Western Ghats (above 500 metres), Andaman Islands, and the sub-Himalayan terai; key species include rosewood, mahogany, ebony, and bamboo. Tropical moist deciduous forests (100–200 cm rainfall) cover the Shiwaliks, central India, and the eastern Ghats; sal and teak are dominant, with sal preferring the north and teak the peninsula. Tropical dry deciduous forests (70–100 cm) occupy the rain-shadow Deccan, central India, and the Aravallis; teak, tendu, and axlewood are common. Tropical thorn forests and scrub (<70 cm) characterise Rajasthan, Gujarat, and the leeward Deccan; acacia, euphorbia, and cacti prevail. Subtropical pine forests (chir pine) appear between 1,000–2,000 metres in the western Himalayas. Temperate broadleaf and coniferous forests (oak, rhododendron, deodar, fir, spruce) extend from 1,500–3,000 metres. Alpine meadows and scrub occur above the tree line (3,600 metres). Mangrove forests, dominated by sundari (Heritiera fomes) in the Sundarbans and Avicennia and Rhizophora elsewhere, fringe the tidal coasts of the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta, Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, and Kaveri deltas, and the Andamans.

4.3 Conservation Status and Protected Areas

India’s forest cover, as per the India State of Forest Report 2021, stands at 21.71 percent of the geographical area, with a target of 33 percent under the National Forest Policy 1988. The Wildlife Protection Act 1972 provides the legal framework for protected areas: 106 national parks, 573 wildlife sanctuaries, 115 conservation reserves, and 220 community reserves as of 2023. Project Tiger (1973) now encompasses 54 tiger reserves covering 75,000 square kilometres; Project Elephant (1992) covers 33 elephant reserves. Biosphere reserves, designated under UNESCO’s Man and the Biosphere Programme, number 18, of which 12 are in the World Network — Nilgiri (first, 1986), Nanda Devi, Sundarbans, Gulf of Mannar, Nokrek, Pachmarhi, Simlipal, Achanakmar-Amarkantak, Great Nicobar, Agasthyamalai, Khangchendzonga, and Panna. The Forest Rights Act 2006 recognises the rights of forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers, creating a tension between conservation and livelihood that continues to shape policy.

5. Agriculture and Cropping Patterns

5.1 Cropping Seasons and Major Crops

Indian agriculture operates on three distinct cropping seasons dictated by the monsoon. The kharif season (June–October) coincides with the southwest monsoon; rice is the principal kharif crop, grown in West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, and Odisha, requiring 100–200 cm rainfall and standing water. Other kharif crops include maize, sorghum (jowar), pearl millet (bajra), cotton, soybean, groundnut, and sugarcane (which overlaps into rabi). The rabi season (November–April) relies on winter rainfall from western disturbances and stored soil moisture; wheat is the dominant rabi crop, concentrated in the Indo-Gangetic plains (Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh), requiring cool winters and 50–75 cm water. Other rabi crops are barley, gram (chickpea), mustard, rapeseed, and linseed. The zaid season (April–June) is a short summer window for irrigated crops such as watermelon, cucumber, fodder, and moong. Cropping intensity — the ratio of gross cropped area to net sown area — has risen from 111 percent in 1950–51 to over 150 percent today, driven by irrigation expansion and short-duration varieties.

5.2 The Green Revolution and Its Aftermath

The Green Revolution, launched in the mid-1960s with the introduction of high-yielding variety (HYV) seeds (dwarf wheat varieties Lerma Rojo and Sonora 64 from CIMMYT, and IR8 rice from IRRI), assured irrigation, chemical fertilisers, and price support, transformed India from a food-deficit nation importing wheat under PL-480 to a self-sufficient and later surplus producer. Punjab, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh became the breadbasket, with wheat yields tripling from 0.8 to 2.5 tonnes per hectare by 1990. However, the revolution remained confined to wheat and rice, to irrigated tracts, and to resource-rich farmers. Ecological consequences include groundwater depletion (Punjab’s water table falling 0.5–1 metre annually), soil salinity and alkalinity from flood irrigation, micronutrient deficiencies, pesticide resistance, and loss of traditional crop diversity. The Second Green Revolution, advocated since the 2000s, emphasises eastern India (Bihar, Odisha, West Bengal, Assam), rainfed agriculture, pulses and oilseeds, conservation agriculture, and climate-resilient varieties.

5.3 Horticulture, Plantation Crops, and Livestock

India is the world’s second-largest producer of fruits and vegetables after China, with horticulture output exceeding foodgrain output since 2012–13. Mango, banana, citrus, papaya, and guava lead fruit production; potato, tomato, onion, and brinjal dominate vegetables. Plantation crops — tea (Assam, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Kerala), coffee (Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu), rubber (Kerala, Tamil Nadu), spices (pepper, cardamom, ginger, turmeric in Kerala), and coconut (Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh) — are export-oriented and labour-intensive. Livestock contributes over 30 percent of agricultural GDP; India has the world’s largest cattle and buffalo population and is the top milk producer (Operation Flood, 1970–1996, created the Amul cooperative model). Poultry and fisheries (inland and marine) have grown at 6–8 percent annually, diversifying rural incomes.

5.4 Agricultural Inputs, Credit, and Marketing

Fertiliser consumption has risen from 0.07 million tonnes (nutrient basis) in 1950–51 to over 60 million tonnes, with a skewed N:P:K ratio (often 8:3:1 against the ideal 4:2:1) due to urea subsidy. The Nutrient Based Subsidy (NBS) scheme (2010) and neem-coated urea (2015) aim to correct this. Institutional credit — through commercial banks, regional rural banks, cooperatives, and the Kisan Credit Card (1998) — covers about 70 percent of farmer households; the Interest Subvention Scheme provides short-term crop loans at 4 percent effective interest. Marketing reforms include the Agricultural Produce Market Committee (APMC) Acts, the electronic National Agriculture Market (e-NAM, 2016) linking mandis, and the Farmers’ Produce Trade and Commerce Act 2020 (repealed 2021) which sought to allow trade outside mandis. Minimum Support Prices (MSP) are announced for 23 crops based on Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) recommendations, but effective procurement remains concentrated in wheat and rice in a few states.

6. Mineral and Energy Resources

6.1 Metallic Minerals: Iron, Manganese, Chromite, Bauxite

India is endowed with large reserves of ferrous minerals. Iron ore (hematite and magnetite) reserves exceed 25 billion tonnes; the richest deposits are in the Odisha-Jharkhand belt (Badampahar, Noamundi, Gua), the Durg-Bastar-Chandrapur belt (Bailadila — high-grade hematite exported to Japan), the Ballari-Hosapete belt in Karnataka, and the Goa deposits (lower grade, now largely exhausted). India is the world’s fourth-largest iron ore producer. Manganese, essential for steel alloys, occurs in the same belts — Odisha (Sundargarh, Keonjhar), Madhya Pradesh (Balaghat), Maharashtra (Nagpur), and Karnataka. Chromite, the only ore of chromium, is concentrated in the Sukinda valley of Odisha (over 95 percent of reserves), making India a significant exporter. Bauxite, the ore of aluminium, is found in the Eastern Ghats (Odisha — Koraput, Kalahandi; Andhra Pradesh — Anantagiri), Jharkhand (Lohardaga), Gujarat (Kachchh, Jamnagar), and Maharashtra (Kolhapur); the National Aluminium Company (NALCO) and Hindalco are major producers.

6.2 Non-Metallic Minerals: Mica, Limestone, Gypsum, Salts

Mica, valued for its dielectric strength and thermal stability, is produced mainly from the Koderma-Gaya-Hazaribagh belt of Jharkhand and Bihar, and from Nellore in Andhra Pradesh; India is the world’s largest sheet mica exporter. Limestone, the raw material for cement, is widely distributed — Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Chhattisgarh, and Tamil Nadu hold major deposits; cement plants cluster near these sources. Gypsum occurs in Rajasthan (Bikaner, Jaisalmer), Gujarat (Kachchh), and Tamil Nadu; it is used in cement, plaster, and fertiliser. Rock salt and brine deposits in the Rann of Kachchh (Gujarat) and Sambhar Lake (Rajasthan) support a large salt industry; India is the third-largest salt producer globally. Other non-metallics include dolomite (for refractory bricks), phosphate (for fertiliser, mainly from Rajasthan and Madhya Pradesh), and building stones (granite, marble, sandstone).

6.3 Fossil Fuels: Coal, Petroleum, Natural Gas

Coal remains the backbone of India’s energy supply, meeting 55 percent of commercial energy demand. Gondwana coal (Permian, 250–300 million years old) constitutes 98 percent of reserves and is found in the Damodar valley (Jharia, Bokaro, Raniganj — the oldest field), the Son valley (Singrauli, Korba), the Godavari valley (Ramagundam), and the Mahanadi valley (Talcher — largest reserves). Tertiary coal (Eocene, 50–60 million years old) occurs in Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Meghalaya, and Nagaland but is high in sulphur. India is the second-largest coal producer and consumer after China, yet imports 200–250 million tonnes annually for coking coal (steel) and high-grade thermal coal. Petroleum and natural gas are produced from the Assam-Arakan basin (Digboi — Asia’s oldest refinery, Naharkatiya, Moran), the Gujarat basin (Ankleshwar, Kalol, Navagam), the Mumbai High offshore field (discovered 1974, largest producer), the Krishna-Godavari basin (KG-D6), and the Cauvery basin. The Hydrocarbon Exploration and Licensing Policy (HELP, 2016) replaced the New Exploration Licensing Policy (NELP) to attract investment through revenue-sharing contracts and marketing freedom.

6.4 Nuclear and Renewable Energy Minerals

Nuclear power requires uranium and thorium. Uranium deposits are located in the Singhbhum thrust belt of Jharkhand (Jaduguda, Bhatin, Narwapahar, Turamdih — operated by Uranium Corporation of India), the Cuddapah basin of Andhra Pradesh (Tummalapalle — one of the world’s largest reserves), and the Mahadek basin of Meghalaya (Domiasiat). Thorium, in which India holds the world’s largest reserves (about 25 percent), occurs in the monazite sands of the Kerala and Odisha coasts; it is central to India’s three-stage nuclear programme (pressurised heavy water reactors → fast breeder reactors → thorium-based reactors). Renewable energy minerals — lithium, cobalt, nickel, rare earth elements — are critical for batteries and solar panels. Lithium reserves have been identified in the Salal-Haimana area of Reasi district, Jammu & Kashmir (5.9 million tonnes inferred), and in the Mandya district of Karnataka; commercial extraction is yet to begin. The Khanij Bidesh India Ltd (KABIL) was formed in 2019 to acquire strategic mineral assets overseas.

Inter‑State River Water Disputes and Tribunal Mechanisms

The Constitution places water in the State List (Entry 17) while empowering Parliament to legislate on inter‑state rivers (Entry 56, Union List). The Inter‑State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 provides the statutory framework: a state government may request the Centre to refer a dispute to a tribunal; the Centre may also act suo motu. Once constituted, a tribunal’s award is final and binding, subject only to a Presidential reference under Article 143 for advisory opinion. Since 1956, over a dozen tribunals have been formed — notable ones include the Krishna (1969), Godavari (1969), Narmada (1969), Cauvery (1990), Ravi‑Beas (1986), Mahanadi (2018) and the recently notified Mahadayi (2022) tribunals.

Tribunal proceedings follow a quasi‑judicial process: pleadings, site inspections, hydrological modelling, and hearings of party states. The award specifies seasonal allocations, reservoir operation rules, and mechanisms for surplus/deficit years. Implementation is monitored by a Central Water Commission‑chaired committee; non‑compliance can be taken to the Supreme Court under Article 136. A recurring friction point is the “equitable apportionment” principle — tribunals balance historical use, present needs, future demands, and the doctrine of “no significant harm”. The Cauvery award (2007, modified 2018) introduced a monthly release schedule and a distress‑sharing formula, yet Karnataka and Tamil Nadu continue to litigate over real‑time reservoir data sharing.

Emerging Institutional Reforms

The 2019 amendment to the 1956 Act introduced a single standing tribunal (the Inter‑State River Water Disputes Tribunal) with benches for each dispute, aiming to reduce delays. It also mandated a transparent data‑sharing protocol through the National Water Informatics Centre. However, states retain the right to challenge tribunal composition, and the Centre’s power to “notify” a dispute remains discretionary, often leading to political deadlock before a tribunal is even constituted.

Monsoon Dynamics: Intra‑Seasonal Oscillations and Climate Change Signals

The Southwest Monsoon is not a steady flow but a succession of active and break spells governed by intra‑seasonal oscillations (ISOs). The most prominent ISO is the 30‑60 day Madden‑Julian Oscillation (MJO), an eastward‑moving envelope of enhanced convection that modulates rainfall over the Indian subcontinent. When the MJO’s convective phase aligns with the monsoon trough, the country experiences widespread active spells; its suppressed phase triggers break monsoon conditions, often lasting 7‑10 days. A secondary ISO, the 10‑20 day quasi‑biweekly oscillation, originates from the equatorial Indian Ocean and influences the timing of monsoon onset over Kerala and the subsequent northward progression.

Observational records (1901‑2023) show a statistically significant increase in the frequency of extreme daily rainfall events (>150 mm) over central India and the Western Ghats, while the number of rainy days has declined. The India Meteorological Department’s “Monsoon Mission” coupled model (CFSv2) reproduces the MJO‑monsoon linkage but underestimates break‑spell duration. Climate‑change attribution studies (IPCC AR6, 2021) indicate that warming of the Indian Ocean reduces the land‑sea thermal contrast, delaying onset and increasing the probability of late‑season cyclogenesis in the Bay of Bengal. Simultaneously, the Arabian Sea has warmed faster, contributing to a rise in very severe cyclonic storms (e.g., Tauktae 2021, Biparjoy 2023) that interact with the monsoon trough, producing anomalous rainfall over Gujarat and Rajasthan.

Operational Implications

Forecast agencies now issue extended‑range predictions (15‑30 days) based on MJO phase forecasts from the European Centre for Medium‑Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) and the National Centre for Medium Range Weather Forecasting (NCMRWF). These products guide reservoir operation in the Damodar, Bhakra‑Nangal and Sardar Sarovar systems, allowing pre‑release before anticipated active spells and conservation during predicted breaks. The “Monsoon Core Zone” concept — central India, Vidarbha, Marathwada — is used for district‑level contingency planning under the National Disaster Management Plan.

Worked Example: Interpreting MJO Phase Forecast for Reservoir Management
A reservoir operator in the Mahanadi basin receives an ECMWF MJO forecast indicating Phase 3 (enhanced convection over the Indian Ocean) for the next 20 days. Historical composites show Phase 3 raises the probability of above‑normal rainfall over the basin by 35 %. The current storage is 68 % of live capacity. The operator must decide whether to pre‑release 10 % of live capacity to create flood cushion.
Step 1: Quantify risk. A 35 % increase over the climatological 20‑day rainfall (≈120 mm) implies an expected 162 mm. The basin’s runoff coefficient (0.45) yields ~73 mm runoff, translating to ~1.2 BCM inflow. Step 2: Compare with flood‑control rule curve. The rule curve at 68 % storage permits a maximum inflow of 1.0 BCM without exceeding the flood‑control level. Step 3: Decision. Since expected inflow (1.2 BCM) exceeds the permissible limit, a pre‑release of 0.2 BCM (≈10 % live capacity) is justified. Step 4: Document the MJO phase, composite anomaly, and rule‑curve reference for audit. This structured approach converts a probabilistic forecast into an operational release decision.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question links a climate phenomenon (MJO, ENSO, IOD) to a specific hydrological decision, identify the forecast lead time, the historical composite anomaly, and the relevant rule curve or operating manual. The answer must show the logical chain: forecast → anomaly → inflow estimate → rule‑curve comparison → release quantum.

Agro‑Climatic Regionalisation and Emerging Cropping Shifts

India’s agro‑climatic classification originated with the Planning Commission’s 15 zones (1989), later refined into 127 sub‑zones by the National Agricultural Research Project (NARP) using criteria of rainfall, temperature, soil type, and growing period. The Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) further delineated 20 Agro‑Ecological Regions (AERs) for technology targeting. Each zone carries a characteristic cropping pattern: the Trans‑Gangetic Plain (Zone VI) favours wheat‑rice rotation; the Central Plateau (Zone VIII) supports soybean‑maize; the Western Dry Region (Zone XIV) is dominated by pearl millet, pulses and oilseeds.

Since the 1990s, three structural shifts have altered these patterns. First, the spread of assured irrigation (canal command expansion, groundwater tubewells) enabled double‑cropping in traditionally single‑crop zones — e.g., rice‑wheat in the Upper Ganga Plains and cotton‑wheat in the Malwa plateau. Second, market liberalisation and Minimum Support Price (MSP) signalling encouraged diversification toward horticulture (mango, banana, citrus) and high‑value spices (pepper in Kerala, turmeric in Andhra). Third, climate variability — rising temperatures, erratic monsoon onset — has pushed farmers in semi‑arid zones (Marathwada, Bundelkhand) toward short‑duration, drought‑tolerant cultivars (e.g., pigeon pea ICPL‑87, pearl millet HHB‑67) and protective cultivation (polyhouses for tomato, capsicum).

Policy Instruments Driving Change

The National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (NMSA, 2014) promotes climate‑resilient practices: contour farming, micro‑irrigation, and crop‑weather advisories via the Kisan Suvidha app. The Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (PKVY) incentivises organic clusters, altering input use patterns in the North‑Eastern Hill Region (Zone II). The recently launched “Crop Diversification Programme” (2022) targets 10 million hectares in Punjab‑Haryana to replace water‑intensive paddy with maize, pulses and oilseeds, linking subsidy to water‑saving technologies. Early adoption data (2023‑24) show a 12 % area shift in pilot districts, but procurement infrastructure for alternative crops remains a bottleneck.

Mineral Auction Regime, Critical Minerals and Strategic Reserves

The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 (MMDR Act) was comprehensively amended in 2015, 2020 and 2021 to replace first‑come‑first‑served allocations with a transparent auction regime for all major minerals except atomic minerals and minor minerals. The 2021 amendment introduced a “composite licence” (prospecting‑cum‑mining) for deep‑seated deposits, and mandated that 10 % of auction revenue flow to the District Mineral Foundation (DMF) for local development. As of March 2024, over 350 mineral blocks have been auctioned, generating cumulative revenue exceeding ₹1.2 lakh crore.

In 2023 the Ministry of Mines notified a list of 30 “critical minerals” — lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, rare‑earth elements (REEs), platinum‑group metals, and others — based on supply‑risk and economic‑importance criteria. Exploration for these minerals is prioritised under the National Mineral Exploration Trust (NMET) and the Geological Survey of India’s “Mission Critical Minerals”. The Salal‑Haimana lithium inference (5.9 Mt, Reasi, Jammu & Kashmir) and the Mandya lithium‑bearing pegmatites (Karnataka) are at advanced exploration stages; commercial mining awaits environmental clearance and processing technology finalisation.

Strategic Reserves and Overseas Acquisition

India’s three‑stage nuclear programme necessitates secure uranium and thorium supply. The Uranium Corporation of India (UCIL) operates mines in Jharkhand (Jaduguda, Turamdih) and Andhra Pradesh (Tummalapalle). For critical battery minerals, Khanij Bidesh India Ltd (KABIL), a joint venture of NALCO, HCL and MECL (formed 2019), has signed off‑take agreements with Australian and Argentine lithium projects and acquired equity in a Canadian cobalt‑nickel venture. The government also maintains a strategic petroleum reserve (5.33 Mt) at Visakhapatnam, Mangalore and Padur, and is evaluating a strategic mineral stockpile for REEs and graphite to insulate high‑tech manufacturing from supply shocks.

Geomorphological Hazards: Flood Plains, Drought‑Prone Areas and Cyclone Tracks

The Ganga‑Brahmaputra‑Meghna (GBM) floodplain, covering ~1.1 million km², experiences annual inundation driven by monsoon runoff, snowmelt, and upstream dam releases. The Central Water Commission’s Flood Forecasting Network (200+ stations) issues level‑based warnings (Yellow, Orange, Red) with lead times of 12‑48 hours. Structural measures — embankments (e.g., Kosi, Gandak), detention basins (e.g., Farakka barrage flood‑storage), and flood‑plain zoning — coexist with non‑structural tools: the National Disaster Management Authority’s (NDMA) “Flood Risk Atlas” (2021) maps 1‑in‑100‑year extents for district‑level planning.

Drought‑prone regions are identified by the India Meteorological Department using the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) and the Aridity Index. The core drought corridor stretches from western Rajasthan (Thar) through Marathwada, Vidarbha, Telangana, Rayalaseema to interior Karnataka. The “Drought Management Plan” (2016) mandates contingency cropping (short‑duration millets, fodder), water‑budgeting at gram‑panchayat level, and employment guarantee works under MGNREGA for soil‑moisture conservation (farm ponds, check dams).

Cyclone tracks in the North Indian Ocean show a bimodal pattern: pre‑monsoon (April‑June) storms originate in the Bay of Bengal and recurve toward Odisha‑West Bengal; post‑monsoon (October‑December) storms intensify over warm Bay waters and strike Andhra‑Tamil Nadu coasts. Arabian Sea cyclones, historically rare, have increased in frequency and intensity since 1998 (e.g., Gonu 2007, Kyarr 2019, Tauktae 2021). The India Meteorological Department’s “Impact‑Based Forecast” (2020) now provides district‑level wind‑speed, storm‑surge (up to 4‑6 m for super‑cyclones) and rainfall forecasts 72 hours ahead, feeding directly into the National Cyclone Risk Mitigation Project (NCRMP) evacuation protocols.
