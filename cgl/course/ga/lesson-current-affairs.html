1. National Current Affairs

1.1 Major Government Schemes and Their Objectives

India’s development agenda is reflected in a series of flagship programmes launched by successive governments. Each scheme is designed to address a specific socio‑economic challenge and is often accompanied by a dedicated budgetary allocation, implementation framework, and performance monitoring mechanism. For example, the Ayushman Bharat – Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PMJAY) seeks to provide secondary and tertiary health‑care coverage of up to Rs 5 lakh per family per year to over 10 crore poor households, thereby reducing out‑of‑pocket expenditure and preventing medical impoverishment. Similarly, the Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM‑KISAN) offers an annual cash assistance of Rs 6 000 to small and marginal farmers, aiming to augment agricultural income and stimulate rural consumption.

Other schemes such as Digital India, Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban & Rural), Make in India, and Jal Jeevan Mission target digital infrastructure, sanitation, manufacturing growth, and universal piped water supply respectively. Understanding the core purpose, target beneficiaries, and the year of launch enables a candidate to answer questions that ask for matching schemes with their objectives, or to analyse the impact of a programme on a particular sector.

1.2 Important Legislative and Policy Developments

Each fiscal year the Union Budget outlines the government’s fiscal stance, revenue targets, and expenditure priorities. Key budgetary highlights often include changes in tax rates, introduction of new welfare schemes, and allocation for defence or infrastructure. For instance, a reduction in the corporate tax rate to 22 % for domestic companies was intended to boost investment, while an increase in the excise duty on cigarettes aims to curb consumption.

Legislative developments such as the passage of the National Education Policy (NEP) 2020, the Farm Laws (repealed in 2021), or the enactment of the Data Protection Bill (under consideration) shape the regulatory environment. Candidates should note the purpose of each law, the ministries involved, and any major controversies or public responses, as exam questions frequently probe the rationale behind a law or its expected societal impact.

1.3 High‑Profile Appointments

Appointments to constitutional and statutory offices are closely watched because they influence the direction of governance and judicial interpretation. Recent appointments include the Chief Justice of India, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Chief Election Commissioner, the Chief of Defence Staff, and the heads of key regulatory bodies such as the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) and the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). Knowing the name of the incumbent, the date of appointment, and the appointing authority (usually the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers) is essential for answering direct identification questions.

1.4 National Events and Commemorations

India observes several national days that commemorate historic milestones, such as Republic Day (26 January), Independence Day (15 August), and Gandhi Jayanti (2 October). Additionally, thematic days like International Yoga Day (21 June) and World Environment Day (5 June) are celebrated with government‑led programmes. Understanding the significance of these days, the year they were first observed, and the typical activities associated with them helps in answering descriptive questions that ask for the relevance of a particular celebration.

2. International Current Affairs

2.1 Global Organisations and India’s Role

India is an active participant in multilateral forums such as the United Nations (UN), the World Trade Organization (WTO), the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the World Bank, and the G20. In the UN, India contributes troops to peace‑keeping missions and advocates for reforms in the Security Council to secure a permanent seat. Within the G20, India’s agenda often emphasizes inclusive growth, climate finance, and digital transformation. Knowing the purpose of each organisation, India’s contribution (e.g., number of peace‑keepers, voting share, or chairmanship year), and recent statements made by Indian officials is crucial for comparative questions.

2.2 Major Summits and Bilateral Agreements

Summits such as the BRICS Summit, the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) Summit, and the ASEAN‑India Summit bring together leaders to discuss trade, security, and technology cooperation. For example, the 2023 BRICS summit in Johannesburg focused on reforming global financial institutions, while the 2022 ASEAN‑India summit in New Delhi highlighted the “Act East” policy and maritime security. Bilateral agreements, such as the India‑Australia Comprehensive Strategic Partnership (2020) or the India‑France Defence Cooperation Agreement (2021), often include clauses on defence equipment transfer, joint exercises, and technology sharing. Candidates should be able to link a summit or agreement with its primary outcomes and strategic significance.

2.3 International Sports Events and India’s Performance

India’s participation in global sporting events provides a source of national pride and diplomatic soft power. The country’s medal tally at the Olympic Games, the Commonwealth Games, and the Asian Games is regularly highlighted in the media. Notable achievements include the historic bronze medal in men’s hockey at the 2020 Tokyo Olympics and the record‑breaking haul of 61 medals at the 2022 Commonwealth Games. Understanding the sport, the event year, the venue, and the athletes involved enables candidates to answer both factual and analytical questions about India’s sporting stature.

2.4 Global Economic Trends Impacting India

World economic indicators such as crude oil prices, global inflation rates, and foreign direct investment (FDI) flows directly affect India’s macro‑economic environment. A sustained rise in crude oil prices, for instance, can widen the current account deficit and trigger inflationary pressures, prompting the RBI to adjust policy rates. Conversely, an increase in FDI inflows, especially in sectors like renewable energy and electronics manufacturing, supports the “Make in India” agenda. Candidates should be able to explain the causal link between a global trend and its domestic repercussions.

3. Awards, Honours and Recognitions

3.1 Civilian Awards

India’s highest civilian honours recognize exceptional contributions in various fields. The Bharat Ratna is awarded for “exceptional service towards the advancement of art, literature, science, and public service.” Recipients include figures from diverse domains such as science (e.g., Dr C N R Rao), literature (e.g., —), and public affairs (e.g., Atal Bihari Vajpayee). The Padma Awards—Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, and Padma Shri—are conferred annually on Republic Day and cover a broader spectrum of achievements. Knowing recent awardees, their fields of work, and the year of conferment helps answer matching and short‑answer questions.

3.2 Sports Honours

The Major Dhyan Chand Khel Ratna is India’s highest sporting honour, awarded for “the most outstanding performance by a sportsperson over the period of the previous four years.” Recent recipients include athletes from athletics, badminton, and wrestling. The Arjuna Award recognises consistent performance at the international level, while the Dronacharya Award** honours coaches who have produced medal‑winning athletes. Candidates should be familiar with the criteria, the year of introduction, and notable awardees.

3.3 International Recognitions

Indian scientists, artists, and activists have been honoured with global awards such as the Nobel Prize, the Academy Awards (Oscars), and the Pulitzer Prize. For instance, the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physics was shared by Pierre Agostini, Ferenc Krausz, and Anne L’Huillier for their work on attosecond pulse generation. Understanding the field of contribution and the significance of the award enables precise answers to “who‑what‑why” type questions.

3.4 Cultural and Literary Awards

Literary accolades like the Sahitya Akademi Award, the Jnanpith Award**, and the Padma Shri for Literature & Education celebrate contributions to Indian languages and literature. The Dadasaheb Phalke Award is the highest honour in Indian cinema, recognizing lifetime achievement. Knowing the year of institution, the selection process, and recent laureates is essential for answering questions that link an award to a specific work or personality.

4. Sports – Events, Records and Administration

4.1 Major International Multi‑Sport Events

The Olympic Games, held every four years, represent the pinnacle of global sport. India’s performance trajectory has shown a steady rise, with a record medal haul of 7 at the 2020 Tokyo Olympics, including a historic gold in javelin throw. The Commonwealth Games and Asian Games follow a similar quadrennial cycle, offering opportunities for Indian athletes to compete against peers from former British colonies and Asian nations respectively. Understanding the host city, year, and India’s medal tally is vital for data‑based questions.

4.2 Notable Individual Achievements and Records

Individual milestones often capture public imagination. For example, cricketer Virat Kohli became the fastest player to reach 8,000 ODI runs in 2021, while swimmer Swati Mohan set a national record in the 200 m butterfly at the 2022 Asian Games. In athletics, Neeraj Chopra secured India’s first Olympic gold in a track‑and‑field event at Tokyo 2020. Candidates should be able to link an athlete’s name with the event, the year, and the record achieved.

4.3 Sports Administration and Policy

The Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports formulates policies such as the Khelo India Programme**, which aims to identify and nurture talent at the grassroots level through scholarships and infrastructure development. The National Sports Development Fund provides financial assistance to athletes for training and participation in international competitions. Understanding the objectives, budgetary allocation, and implementation mechanisms helps answer policy‑oriented questions.

4.4 Emerging Sports and New Disciplines

Recent years have witnessed the inclusion of new sports in major events, such as skateboarding and sport climbing at the Tokyo Olympics, and e‑sports gaining recognition from the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports. India’s participation in these emerging disciplines is modest but growing, with the government announcing dedicated training centres for e‑sports in 2023. Awareness of such trends enables candidates to discuss the evolving sports landscape.

5. Economic Current Affairs – Trends and Policy Responses

5.1 Fiscal Indicators and Their Interpretation

The Union Budget presents the government’s revenue‑expenditure outlook. Key indicators include the fiscal deficit (the gap between total expenditure and total revenue), the primary deficit (excluding interest payments), and the debt‑to‑GDP ratio. A widening fiscal deficit may signal expansionary spending but also raises concerns about fiscal sustainability, prompting the RBI to consider monetary tightening. Conversely, a reduced deficit often reflects fiscal consolidation, which can improve credit ratings and lower borrowing costs.

5.2 Monetary Policy and Inflation Management

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) uses the repo rate, reverse repo rate, and the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) to manage liquidity. When inflation, measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI), exceeds the RBI’s target band of 4 % ± 2 %, the central bank may raise the repo rate to curb demand‑pull inflation. Conversely, a slowdown in growth may lead to a rate cut to stimulate investment. Understanding the transmission mechanism helps answer cause‑and‑effect questions linking policy actions to macro‑economic outcomes.

5.3 Trade and External Sector Developments

India’s trade balance is influenced by global commodity prices, exchange rate movements, and bilateral trade agreements. A surge in crude oil prices, for instance, widens the import bill, thereby increasing the current account deficit. Conversely, the signing of a free‑trade agreement (FTA) with a regional bloc can boost export volumes by reducing tariff barriers. Recent examples include the India‑Australia Comprehensive Economic Cooperation Agreement (CECA) and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) negotiations.

5.4 Sectoral Highlights – Energy, Agriculture, and Technology

The energy sector is undergoing a transition toward renewable sources. The government’s target of 450 GW of renewable energy capacity by 2030 aims to reduce dependence on fossil fuels and meet climate commitments. In agriculture, the Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) provides crop insurance, while the e‑NAM (National Agriculture Market) platform seeks to create a unified online market for agricultural produce. In technology, the Digital India programme promotes broadband connectivity, e‑governance, and skill development, thereby fostering a knowledge‑based economy.

6. Science, Technology and Environment – Current Highlights

6.1 Space Missions and Satellite Launches

India’s space agency, ISRO, has achieved several milestones, including the successful launch of the Chandrayaan‑3 lunar mission, which demonstrated a soft landing near the Moon’s south pole. The mission’s scientific payloads aim to study lunar geology and search for water ice. Additionally, the launch of the Gaganyaan crewed mission, scheduled for the early 2020s, marks India’s entry into human spaceflight. Knowing the mission name, objectives, and launch date is essential for answering space‑related questions.

6.2 Technological Innovations and Start‑up Ecosystem

India’s start‑up ecosystem has flourished, with sectors such as fintech, health‑tech, and agritech attracting significant foreign direct investment. The government’s Startup India initiative provides tax benefits, funding support, and a simplified regulatory framework. Notable innovations include the development of indigenous 5G technology by Indian telecom firms and the creation of low‑cost ventilators during the COVID‑19 pandemic. Candidates should be able to cite examples of breakthrough technologies and the policies that facilitated them.

6.3 Environmental Policies and Climate Action

India’s commitment to the Paris Agreement includes a target of achieving 500 GW of renewable energy capacity by 2030 and reducing the emissions intensity of its GDP by 45 % from 2005 levels. The National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) outlines eight missions, ranging from the National Solar Mission to the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture. Recent policy moves, such as the introduction of a carbon tax on coal and the promotion of electric vehicles through the Faster Adoption and Manufacturing of Hybrid & Electric Vehicles (FAME) scheme, illustrate the country’s multi‑pronged approach to climate mitigation.

6.4 Health and Pandemic Preparedness

The COVID‑19 pandemic highlighted the importance of robust health infrastructure. In response, the government launched the National Digital Health Mission (NDHM), which aims to create a digital health ecosystem, and increased funding for the National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC). The development of indigenous vaccines, such as Covaxin, demonstrated self‑reliance in biotechnology. Understanding these initiatives helps answer questions on public health policy and pandemic preparedness.

India’s G20 Presidency: Priorities, Outcomes, and Institutional Legacy

India assumed the G20 presidency on 1 December 2022 and handed over the rotating chair to Brazil on 30 November 2023. The presidency’s theme was “One Earth, One Family, One Future” (Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam), with priorities in inclusive growth, climate finance, digital public infrastructure, and reform of multilateral development banks. The New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration, adopted on 9–10 September 2023, recorded consensus on key commitments including tripling global renewable energy capacity by 2030, phasing down unabated coal power, and launching the Global Biofuels Alliance. India also secured the inclusion of the African Union as a permanent G20 member, expanding the grouping’s representativeness. On the financial side, the presidency advanced the “Common Framework for Debt Treatments” and pushed for the $100 billion annual climate‑finance goal; the final text retained the existing target without a new quantified commitment. The Digital Economy Ministers’ Declaration endorsed interoperable digital identity, payments, and data‑exchange standards, citing India’s UPI and Aadhaar as reference implementations. The presidency’s institutional legacy is maintained through the G20 Secretariat in the Ministry of External Affairs, which archives working‑group reports and serves as a reference for future chairs.

Recent Constitutional Amendments and Supreme Court Interpretations (2022‑2024)

The Constitution (One Hundred and Fifth Amendment) Act, 2021, which restored state powers to identify socially and educationally backward classes, came into force in August 2021 after presidential assent. The Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act, 2019 had introduced a 10 % reservation for economically weaker sections (EWS) in central government jobs and educational institutions by inserting Articles 15(6) and 16(6). The Supreme Court, in a 3:2 majority judgment on 7 November 2022 (Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India), upheld the EWS quota, ruling that economic criteria alone do not violate the basic structure. The Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act, 2023 (Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam) provides for one‑third reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and state legislative assemblies. In 2024, the Court delivered a landmark verdict on the appointment of Election Commissioners (Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India, March 2024), mandating a selection committee comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition, and Chief Justice of India, reinforcing institutional independence. The K. S. Puttaswamy (2017) judgment established the right to privacy as a fundamental right, providing the constitutional basis for the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. These developments illustrate the dynamic tension between legislative reform and constitutional guardianship, a theme examiners frequently test through comparative‑analysis questions.

Comparative‑Analysis Trap
A question asks: “Which of the following statements correctly distinguishes the 103rd, 105th, and 106th Constitutional Amendments?” Options include references to EWS quota, state SEBC lists, and women’s reservation. Identify the correct distinction.
The 103rd Amendment (2019) inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6) for up to 10 % EWS reservation in central jobs and education. The 105th Amendment (2021) restored the power of states to prepare their own lists of socially and educationally backward classes (SEBCs) under Articles 15(4) and 16(4). The 106th Amendment (2023) inserts Article 330A, 332A, and 334A to provide one‑third reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies. The key distinctions: 103rd creates a central EWS category; 105th deals with state‑level SEBC identification; 106th mandates women’s legislative representation. Any option conflating these subjects or misattributing the amendment number is incorrect.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question presents multiple constitutional amendments, first note the amendment number and year. Then map each amendment to the specific article(s) it modifies. Use a quick mental table: Amendment → Article(s) → Subject. This eliminates confusion between EWS (103rd), SEBC lists (105th), and women’s reservation (106th).

Defence Acquisition Procedure 2020 and the Shift Toward Indigenisation

The Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020, notified on 30 September 2020, replaced the Defence Procurement Procedure (DPP) 2016 and retained a hierarchy of procurement categories — “Buy (Indian‑IDDM)”, “Buy (Indian)”, “Buy & Make (Indian)”, “Buy & Make”, and “Buy (Global)”. The “Indian‑IDDM” (Indigenously Designed, Developed and Manufactured) category receives the highest priority, mandating at least 50 % indigenous content. The DAP 2020 revised the “Make‑II” sub‑category for prototype development with government funding up to 70 % of development cost, and “Make‑III” for industry‑funded prototypes. The “Strategic Partnership (SP) Model”, introduced in DPP 2016 and continued under DAP 2020, applies to designated major platforms — fighter aircraft, helicopters, submarines, and armoured vehicles — where a select Indian private entity partners with a foreign OEM for technology transfer and domestic production. The first major SP‑model contract for naval utility helicopters is under process; the 83 LCA Tejas Mk‑1A contract (February 2021) was placed with HAL under the “Buy (Indian‑IDDM)” category. The DAP 2020 streamlined the “Offset” policy, requiring a minimum 30 % offset for contracts above ₹2,000 crore, with multipliers for MSME participation. These measures aim to reduce import dependence from roughly 60 % (2014‑15) to below 30 % by 2027‑28, a target reiterated in the “Atmanirbhar Bharat” defence roadmap.

Digital Public Infrastructure: UPI, Aadhaar, and the Open Network for Digital Commerce

India’s Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) rests on three interoperable layers — identity (Aadhaar), payments (Unified Payments Interface, UPI), and data‑exchange (Account Aggregator framework). Aadhaar, launched in 2009, reached over 1.38 billion enrolments by March 2024, providing a biometric‑based unique identifier that underpins e‑KYC, direct‑benefit transfers, and authentication for financial services. UPI, operational since April 2016, processed over 131 billion transactions worth ₹200 trillion in FY 2023‑24, making it the world’s largest real‑time payment system by volume. The National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) introduced UPI 2.0 in 2018 with features such as overdraft facility, one‑time mandates, and invoice‑in‑the‑box. The Account Aggregator (AA) framework, regulated by the Reserve Bank of India since 2021, enables consent‑based sharing of financial data across banks, insurers, and fintechs, with over 1 million active users as of December 2023. Building on these rails, the Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC) was launched in pilot mode in April 2022 across five cities; by March 2024 it had onboarded over 36,000 sellers and millions of buyers, aiming to democratise e‑commerce by unbundling discovery, ordering, and fulfilment. The government’s “Digital India” budget allocation for FY 2024‑25 earmarked substantial funds for DPI scaling, including open APIs for health, education, and agriculture (often referred to as India Stack). Examiners often test the ability to link a specific service (e.g., PM‑KISAN direct transfer) to the underlying DPI layer.

Green Finance Landscape: Sovereign Green Bonds, Carbon Markets, and Climate Finance Commitments

India issued its first sovereign green bonds (SGrBs) in January 2023, raising ₹16,000 crore through two tranches (5‑year and 10‑year) under the “Green Bond Framework” approved by the Ministry of Finance in November 2022. The framework aligns with the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) Green Bond Principles and earmarks proceeds for renewable energy, energy efficiency, pollution prevention, sustainable agriculture, and clean transportation. By March 2024, cumulative SGrB issuance reached approximately ₹36,000 crore, with the initial 10‑year bond yielding 7.18 % at issuance. Parallelly, the Ministry of Power notified the “Carbon Credit Trading Scheme” (CCTS) in June 2023, establishing a domestic compliance market for entities covered under the Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT) scheme. The CCTS provides for verified emission reductions to be traded as “Carbon Credit Certificates”; operational details including auction mechanisms are being finalised. On the international front, India reiterated its commitment to the $100 billion annual climate‑finance goal at COP28 (December 2023) and announced a “Green Credit Programme” (October 2023) to mobilise private capital for afforestation and waste‑to‑energy projects. The Reserve Bank of India’s “Framework for Acceptance of Green Deposits” (April 2023) enables banks to raise earmarked deposits for green lending, with a reporting template aligned to the Task Force on Climate‑related Financial Disclosures (TCFD). These instruments collectively support India’s strategy to meet its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) target of 45 % emission‑intensity reduction by 2030 from 2005 levels.

India's Semiconductor Mission and Supply Chain Resilience

The India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) was launched in December 2021 under the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology with a total outlay of ₹76,000 crore over six years. The mission operates through three pillars: a Production‑Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for semiconductor fabs, a Design‑Linked Incentive (DLI) scheme for chip design companies, and a Compound Semiconductor Scheme for niche materials such as gallium nitride and silicon carbide. The PLI offers fiscal support of up to 50 % of project cost for greenfield fabs and 30 % for brownfield expansions, capped at ₹12,000 crore per project. As of March 2024, in‑principle approvals have been granted for a 28 nm logic fab by the Tata Group with Powerchip Semiconductor Manufacturing Corporation (Taiwan), a 28 nm analog‑mixed‑signal fab by CG Power with Renesas and Stars Microelectronics, and an assembly‑test facility by Micron Technology in Gujarat with a committed investment of ₹22,500 crore. The DLI scheme, notified in April 2022, provides reimbursement of up to 50 % of eligible expenditure for design start‑ups, with a ceiling of ₹15 crore per applicant; by December 2023 over 30 design houses had been approved, targeting automotive, telecom, and defence segments.

Supply‑chain resilience efforts include mapping critical raw materials — high‑purity silicon wafers, photoresists, specialty gases — and identifying domestic alternatives. The Department of Commerce has entered into memoranda with Japan, the United States, and the European Union for joint research on advanced packaging and wafer‑level testing. The mission also mandates that any fab receiving incentives must source at least 25 % of its equipment and materials from domestic vendors by the fifth year of operation, a clause intended to catalyse an ancillary ecosystem of equipment manufacturers, chemical suppliers, and test‑assembly houses. Progress is monitored by a high‑level steering committee chaired by the Minister of Electronics and IT, with quarterly dashboards published on the ISM portal.

Public Sector Banking Reforms: Recapitalisation, Governance, and the Bad Bank

Between FY 2017‑18 and FY 2021‑22 the government infused ₹3.16 lakh crore into public sector banks (PSBs) through recapitalisation bonds, raising the aggregate capital adequacy ratio from 11.2 % to 14.5 %. The 2020‑21 “Atmanirbhar Bharat” package added ₹20,000 crore of front‑loaded capital to accelerate credit flow to MSMEs. In parallel, the “Indradhanush” framework (2015) was refreshed in 2022 with the “Enhanced Access and Service Excellence (EASE) 5.0” agenda, focusing on digital lending, data‑driven risk management, and board‑level accountability. The Banks Board Bureau (BBB) was replaced in 2022 by the Financial Services Institutions Bureau (FSIB) to broaden the talent pool for whole‑time directors and chairpersons.

The most structural reform is the creation of the National Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd (NARCL) and the India Debt Resolution Company Ltd (IDRCL) in 2021‑22. NARCL, capitalised at ₹6,000 crore with a government guarantee of ₹30,600 crore, acquires stressed assets above ₹500 crore at a 15 % cash‑upfront and 85 % security receipts structure. IDRCL, a private‑sector‑led asset manager, handles resolution and value maximisation. By March 2024 NARCL had acquired a substantial portfolio of stressed assets, contributing to a decline in the PSB gross NPA ratio from 9.3 % (March 2021) to 4.8 % (March 2024). The “Bad Bank” model also introduced a “Swiss Challenge” mechanism for transparent price discovery on large ticket loans. Governance upgrades include the mandatory appointment of independent directors for a minimum of three years, a cooling‑off period of two years before re‑appointment, and the adoption of a “Board‑Level Risk Appetite Statement” aligned with Basel III norms.

Technique
A question asks: “Which entity acquires stressed assets above ₹500 crore under the 2021‑22 bad‑bank framework, and what is the cash‑upfront proportion?” Identify the correct entity and proportion.
The National Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd (NARCL) acquires stressed assets above ₹500 crore. The acquisition structure stipulates 15 % cash upfront and 85 % in security receipts. This detail is explicitly stated in the NARCL operational guidelines released by the Department of Financial Services in July 2021.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question combines a newly created institution with a numeric threshold (e.g., ₹500 crore), note both the name and the exact figure. Examiners frequently swap the cash‑upfront percentage (15 % vs 20 %) or confuse NARCL with IDRCL. Write the pair “NARCL – 15 % cash” on your rough sheet before reading options.

Data Protection Evolution: From Personal Data Protection Bill to Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023

The legislative journey began with the Personal Data Protection Bill, 2019, introduced after the Justice B.N. Srikrishna Committee report (2018). The 2019 Bill proposed a Data Protection Authority, data localisation mandates for “critical personal data”, and stringent consent requirements. After extensive parliamentary debate and a Joint Committee report (2021), the government withdrew the Bill in August 2022, citing the need for a “lighter” framework. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP Act) was passed by Parliament in August 2023 and received presidential assent on 11 August 2023.

The DPDP Act defines “personal data” as any data about an identifiable individual, and “data fiduciary” as any entity determining the purpose and means of processing. Key departures from the 2019 Bill include: (1) removal of mandatory data localisation; cross‑border transfers are permitted to “trusted jurisdictions” notified by the central government; (2) a consent‑manager framework allowing users to give, manage, and withdraw consent through interoperable platforms; (3) a graded penalty regime — up to ₹250 crore for each significant violation, with additional penalties for continued non‑compliance; (4) establishment of the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) as an adjudicatory body, not a regulator, with members appointed by the central government; (5) exemptions for processing by the State for “sovereignty, security, public order” and for certain research, archival, and statistical purposes. The Act also introduces the concept of “significant data fiduciary” based on volume, sensitivity, and risk, requiring them to appoint a Data Protection Officer and conduct Data Protection Impact Assessments. Draft rules for operationalising the Act — covering trusted jurisdictions, consent‑manager standards, and DPBI procedures — have been released for consultation.

Strategic Defence Partnerships: Joint Ventures, Technology Transfer, and the iDEX Ecosystem

The Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020 institutionalised the “Strategic Partnership (SP) Model” for select high‑value platforms — fighter aircraft, helicopters, submarines, and armoured vehicles. Under this model, an Indian private entity is selected through a transparent competition to partner with a foreign Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) for joint manufacturing, technology absorption, and lifecycle support. The SP model is being applied to upcoming procurements such as the multi‑role fighter aircraft (MRFA) programme, with Indian private firms partnering foreign OEMs for joint manufacturing and technology absorption.

Parallel to the SP model, the Innovations for Defence Excellence (iDEX) framework, launched in 2018 and scaled under the Defence Innovation Organisation (DIO), funds start‑ups and MSMEs for prototype development. As of December 2023, iDEX had sanctioned over 300 projects worth ₹1,500 crore, covering areas such as AI‑based surveillance, swarm drones, and advanced materials. The “Technology Development Fund (TDF)” complements iDEX by providing grants up to ₹10 crore for projects with a Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of 3‑5. Notable joint ventures include the Tata‑Lockheed Martin facility for aerostructure production, the Dassault‑Reliance joint venture for Rafale components, and the Bharat Forge‑Rafael partnership for missile sub‑systems. The DAP 2020 also introduced a “Buy (Indian‑IDDM)” category that prioritises indigenously designed, developed, and manufactured equipment, with a minimum 50 % indigenous content for eligibility.

Climate Adaptation Finance: National Adaptation Fund, State Action Plans, and International Climate Finance Flows

The National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC) was established in 2015‑16 with a corpus of ₹350 crore, later enhanced to ₹1,000 crore in the 2021‑22 budget. The fund finances concrete adaptation projects in vulnerable sectors — water, agriculture, forestry, coastal zones — through a competitive grant mechanism administered by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC). By March 2024, NAFCC had approved 42 projects across 18 states, with a total sanctioned amount of ₹820 crore. Each state is required to prepare a State Action Plan on Climate Change (SAPCC) aligned with the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) eight missions. As of 2023, 28 states and union territories have submitted revised SAPCCs incorporating district‑level vulnerability assessments.

On the international front, India accesses the Green Climate Fund (GCF) and the Adaptation Fund (AF) through accredited entities such as NABARD, SIDBI, and the Indian Renewable Energy Development Agency (IREDA). By COP28 (December 2023), India had secured GCF approvals for multiple projects worth over US$1 billion, focusing on solar‑powered irrigation, flood‑resilient infrastructure, and mangrove restoration. The “Loss and Damage” fund agreed at COP27 remains operationalised through a transitional committee; India has advocated for a governance structure that ensures direct access for developing countries without conditionalities. Domestically, the Reserve Bank of India’s “Framework for Green Deposits” (April 2023) and the Securities and Exchange Board of India’s “Green Bond Guidelines” (2022) channel private capital toward adaptation‑linked assets, with a reporting template aligned to the Task Force on Climate‑related Financial Disclosures (TCFD).

National Green Hydrogen Mission: Objectives, Funding Architecture, and Implementation Milestones

The Union Cabinet approved the National Green Hydrogen Mission on 4 January 2023 with a total outlay of ₹19,744 crore for the period 2023‑24 to 2029‑30. The mission aims to make India a global hub for the production, utilisation, and export of green hydrogen and its derivatives, targeting an annual production capacity of 5 million metric tonnes (MMT) by 2030. The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE) is the nodal ministry, supported by a Mission Directorate that coordinates across line ministries, state governments, and industry.

The financial architecture is built around two sub‑schemes under the Strategic Interventions for Green Hydrogen Transition (SIGHT) programme:

  • Electrolyser Manufacturing Incentive – a production‑linked incentive (PLI) of ₹4,440 crore to domestic manufacturers for setting up electrolyser capacity of at least 1 GW each, with a minimum domestic value addition of 50 %.
  • Green Hydrogen Production Incentive – a direct subsidy of ₹13,050 crore for projects that produce green hydrogen at a cost not exceeding ₹150 per kg, disbursed in tranches linked to commissioned capacity and verified output.

In addition, the mission earmarks ₹1,466 crore for pilot projects in hard‑to‑abate sectors (steel, fertilizers, refineries, heavy‑duty transport), ₹400 crore for the development of hydrogen hubs and standards, and ₹388 crore for research, skill development, and international cooperation. The first round of bids for electrolyser manufacturing was opened in June 2023, with three consortia — led by Reliance Industries, Larsen & Toubro, and a joint venture of Indian Oil and ACME — receiving letters of award by December 2023.

Implementation milestones tracked by the Mission Directorate include: (1) notification of the Green Hydrogen Policy (February 2023) defining green hydrogen as hydrogen produced from renewable energy with a carbon intensity ≤ 2 kg CO₂ eq per kg H₂; (2) launch of the Hydrogen Valley Innovation Cluster in Gujarat (April 2023) to demonstrate end‑to‑end value chains; (3) signing of Memoranda of Understanding with Germany, Japan, and Australia for technology transfer and joint pilot projects (2023‑24). The mission also mandates a certification framework aligned with the International Partnership for Hydrogen and Fuel Cells in the Economy (IPHE) to enable export credibility.

India’s Labour Codes: Consolidation, Key Provisions, and Implementation Status

Parliament enacted four labour codes between 2019 and 2020 to replace 29 central labour statutes: the Code on Wages, 2019; the Industrial Relations Code, 2020; the Code on Social Security, 2020; and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020. The codes received Presidential assent in September 2020 but their operationalisation depends on the notification of central rules and concurrent framing of state rules, because labour is a concurrent subject.

Code on Wages introduces a statutory floor wage fixed by the central government (initially ₹178 per day) below which no state minimum wage can be set. It universalises minimum‑wage coverage to all employees, including the unorganised sector, and mandates timely payment (within 7 days of the wage period). Industrial Relations Code raises the threshold for standing orders from 100 to 300 workers, permits fixed‑term employment with parity of benefits, and requires a 60‑day notice for strikes in public utility services. Social Security Code extends coverage to gig and platform workers, mandates a Social Security Fund financed by a 1‑2 % cess on aggregator turnover, and provides for maternity benefit, gratuity, and employees’ compensation under a single framework. OSH Code consolidates 13 statutes, introduces a single registration for establishments with 10 or more workers, and mandates annual health examinations for workers in hazardous processes.

Central rules for the Code on Wages were notified in December 2021; rules for the other three codes were notified between February 2022 and March 2023. As of early 2024, a majority of states and union territories have pre‑published draft rules for at least two codes, but formal notification remains uneven. The staggered rollout has created a dual‑compliance environment where establishments must follow both the old Acts (where state rules are pending) and the new codes (where central rules apply). Industry associations have flagged the compliance cost of multiple registers and the ambiguity around the definition of “worker” for gig platforms as key implementation hurdles.

Technique: Identifying the Correct Code Provision
A question asks: “Which labour code introduces a statutory floor wage that binds state minimum wages?” The options list the four codes. Identify the correct code and cite the specific provision.
The Code on Wages, 2019 contains the floor‑wage mechanism. Section 9 of the Code empowers the central government to fix a floor wage taking into account living standards, and Section 10 mandates that no state minimum wage shall be lower than the floor wage. Therefore, the correct answer is the Code on Wages, with reference to Sections 9‑10.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question tests the labour codes, first map the keyword (floor wage, gig workers, fixed‑term employment, single registration) to the specific code. Keep a one‑page cheat sheet linking each code to its two hallmark provisions; this avoids confusion caused by overlapping definitions across the four statutes.

Space Sector Reforms: IN‑SPACe, NSIL, Private Participation, and Recent Missions

The Indian National Space Promotion and Authorization Centre (IN‑SPACe) was constituted in June 2020 as an autonomous single‑window agency under the Department of Space (DoS) to authorise, monitor, and promote non‑governmental space activities. Its mandate includes issuing licences for launch services, satellite operations, ground stations, and space‑based applications, and providing access to DoS infrastructure on a commercial basis. The NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), incorporated in March 2019, functions as the commercial arm of ISRO, responsible for marketing launch services (PSLV, GSLV, SSLV), satellite transponders, and technology transfer.

The Space Policy 2023, approved by the Cabinet in April 2023, formally opens the entire space value chain — design, manufacturing, launch, and downstream services — to private entities, subject to IN‑SPACe authorisation. It also defines a revenue‑sharing model for use of ISRO facilities and establishes a Space Activities Bill framework (still under legislative consideration) to provide legal certainty.

Private sector milestones since the reform include: Skyroot Aerospace launching the Vikram‑S sub‑orbital vehicle (November 2022) and the orbital Vikram‑1 (planned 2024); Agnikul Cosmos conducting the Agnibaan‑SOrTeD test flight (May 2023) from a private launchpad at Sriharikota; Pixxel deploying hyperspectral imaging satellites on ISRO’s PSLV (2022‑23); and Dhruva Space delivering satellite platforms for commercial clients. IN‑SPACe had issued over 120 authorisations by December 2023, covering launch, satellite, and ground‑segment activities.

On the flagship mission front, Chandrayaan‑3 achieved a soft landing near the lunar south pole on 23 August 2023, making India the fourth nation to land on the Moon and the first at high latitude. The Aditya‑L1 solar observatory was launched on 2 September 2023 and inserted into a halo orbit around the Sun‑Earth L1 point in January 2024. The Gaganyaan human‑spaceflight programme completed the first uncrewed test flight (TV‑D1) in October 2023, with the second uncrewed mission and the crewed flight targeted for 2025. These missions demonstrate the maturation of indigenous launch vehicle reliability (GSLV‑Mk III) and life‑support systems developed in partnership with private industry.

Electoral Reforms: Electoral Bonds Judgment, VVPAT Verification, and Model Code Enforcement

The Supreme Court’s Constitution Bench judgment of 15 February 2024 in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme, 2018 as unconstitutional. The Court held that the scheme violated the right to information under Article 19(1)(a) by anonymising political donations, and that the amendment to the Representation of the People Act, 1951, and the Income‑Tax Act, 1961, enabling the scheme were ultra vires. The judgment directed the State Bank of India to disclose details of all bonds purchased and encashed since 2019 to the Election Commission of India (ECI) within three weeks, and the ECI to publish the data on its website.

On VVPAT verification, the Supreme Court in Chandrabhan v. Election Commission of India (April 2023) mandated that the ECI conduct mandatory verification of VVPAT paper slips for 5 % of randomly selected polling stations per assembly constituency (or per parliamentary constituency segment) before finalising results. The ECI subsequently revised its counting protocol to integrate this statistical audit, and the first full‑scale implementation occurred during the 2024 general elections.

The Model Code of Conduct (MCC) continues to be enforced through the ECI’s statutory powers under Article 324. Recent amendments to the Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961 (notified March 2023) expanded the definition of “corrupt practice” to include the misuse of government machinery for social‑media campaigns and the distribution of cash‑equivalent digital vouchers. The ECI also introduced a “cVIGIL” mobile application for citizens to report MCC violations with geo‑tagged evidence, leading to a large volume of complaints during the 2024 election cycle.

Parliament is currently examining the “One Nation, One Election” proposal, which would require constitutional amendments to Articles 83, 172, and 356, and a consensus on synchronising the terms of the Lok Sabha and state legislative assemblies. A high‑level committee chaired by former President Ram Nath Kovind submitted its report in March 2024, recommending a phased approach starting with a common electoral roll and simultaneous polls for the Lok Sabha and a group of states by 2029.

Digital Health Ecosystem: Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, Interoperability, and Data Governance

The Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) was launched on 27 September 2021 by the National Health Authority (NHA) to create a unified digital health infrastructure. Its four foundational building blocks are: ABHA (Ayushman Bharat Health Account) ID — a 14‑digit unique health identifier for every citizen; Health Facility Registry (HFR) — a verified database of public and private health facilities; Health Professional Registry (HPR) — a credentialled registry of doctors, nurses, pharmacists, and allied professionals; and the Unified Health Interface (UHI) — an open‑network protocol enabling discovery, booking, and payment for health services across platforms.

Interoperability is achieved through adoption of the FHIR R4 (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) standard for clinical data exchange, DICOM for imaging, and ICD‑11 for disease coding. The NHA published the ABDM Sandbox in 2022, allowing developers to test APIs against a synthetic dataset before production onboarding. As of March 2024, over 30 crore ABHA IDs have been generated, 2.1 lakh facilities are registered on HFR, and 1.4 lakh professionals on HPR. The UHI has facilitated more than 50 lakh transactions across tele‑consultation, pharmacy, and diagnostics platforms.

Data governance rests on a consent‑manager framework modelled on the Data Empowerment and Protection Architecture (DEPA). Patients grant granular, time‑bound consent for each data request via a consent artefact signed with their ABHA‑linked digital signature. The NHA’s Health Data Management Policy (2023) mandates anonymisation for research datasets, audit trails for every access, and a grievance redressal mechanism with a 30‑day resolution window. Integration with the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) enables real‑time settlement for out‑of‑pocket payments, while the Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM‑JAY) claims engine uses ABDM identifiers for cashless verification.

Key challenges include the digital divide in rural areas (a significant share of ABHA IDs not yet linked to Aadhaar‑verified mobile numbers), the need for legacy hospital information systems to adopt FHIR, and ensuring that the consent manager ecosystem remains vendor‑neutral. The NHA has partnered with the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) to leverage the DigiLocker infrastructure for secure document storage and with the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT‑In) for cyber‑security incident response.

India's Central Bank Digital Currency Pilot: Design, Wholesale and Retail Tracks, and Monetary Policy Implications

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) launched the first phase of its Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) pilot on 1 November 2022, designated as the wholesale segment (e₹‑W). The pilot involved nine participating banks — State Bank of India, Bank of Baroda, Union Bank of India, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Kotak Mahindra Bank, Yes Bank, IDFC First Bank and HSBC — and focused on settlement of secondary market transactions in government securities. The platform uses a permissioned distributed ledger technology (DLT) built on the Hyperledger Fabric framework, with the RBI acting as the sole issuer and final settlement agent. Transactions are settled in real time on a gross‑settlement basis, eliminating the need for the Clearing Corporation of India (CCIL) as an intermediary for the pilot trades.

The retail CBDC pilot (e₹‑R) commenced on 1 February 2023 in four cities — Mumbai, New Delhi, Bengaluru and Bhubaneswar — with four banks (State Bank of India, ICICI Bank, Yes Bank and IDFC First Bank) issuing digital wallets to a closed user group of customers and merchants. The retail token is a claim on the RBI, denominated in rupees, and is transferred peer‑to‑peer without an intermediary. The wallet app incorporates a two‑factor authentication model (device binding + MPIN) and supports offline proximity payments using Near Field Communication (NFC) for feature‑phone users. As of March 2024, the retail pilot has processed over 1 million transactions valued at over ₹ 3,000 crore, with a daily average volume of several thousand transactions.

Monetary policy implications are being studied through a dedicated RBI working group. The wholesale CBDC is expected to reduce settlement risk and collateral requirements in the government securities market, potentially lowering the term premium on sovereign bonds. The retail CBDC could enhance transmission of policy rates by providing a direct interest‑bearing liability of the central bank to households, but the RBI has so far kept the e₹‑R non‑interest‑bearing to avoid disintermediation of commercial banks. The pilot also tests the feasibility of programmable money — e.g., purpose‑bound vouchers for fertiliser subsidies — which could improve fiscal targeting.

Key Design Choices

  • Token‑based, not account‑based, to preserve anonymity for low‑value transactions while enabling traceability for high‑value flows.
  • Two‑tier architecture: RBI issues to banks (wholesale) or to wallet providers (retail); banks/wallet providers handle KYC and customer onboarding.
  • Interoperability with UPI is deliberately excluded in the pilot to isolate CBDC performance metrics.

India's Cybersecurity Architecture: National Cyber Security Strategy 2020, Critical Information Infrastructure Protection, and CERT‑In Directives

The National Cyber Security Strategy (NCSS) 2020, released by the National Security Council Secretariat (NSCS), sets a 2025 horizon for achieving a “secure and resilient cyberspace”. It identifies five pillars — governance, capacity building, partnership, technology, and resilience — and proposes a National Cyber Security Coordinator (NCSC) with statutory powers, a dedicated Cyber Security Fund, and a mandatory cyber‑risk audit for all Critical Information Infrastructure (CII) entities. The strategy remains in draft form; however, several operational directives have already been issued under the Information Technology Act, 2000.

CII sectors — power, banking, telecom, transport, health, and strategic public enterprises — are notified under Section 70A of the IT Act. The National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC), established in 2014, serves as the nodal agency for threat intelligence sharing, vulnerability assessment, and incident response coordination. In April 2022, CERT‑In issued directions under Section 70B requiring all service providers, intermediaries, data centres and government organisations to report cyber incidents within six hours of detection, maintain logs for 180 days, and synchronise system clocks with the National Informatics Centre (NIC) time server. Non‑compliance attracts penalties up to ₹ 25 lakh under Section 44 of the IT Act.

The 2023 amendment to the IT Rules introduced a “Cyber Security Incident Response Framework” mandating a designated Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) for specified entities, along with quarterly penetration testing and annual red‑team exercises for CII operators. In parallel, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) launched the “Cyber Surakshit Bharat” programme to train government officials in cyber hygiene.

Operational Highlights

  • Sector‑specific Computer Emergency Response Teams (CERTs) — CERT‑Fin for banking, CERT‑Power for electricity — operate under NCIIPC coordination.
  • The “National Cyber Coordination Centre” (NCCC) provides real‑time situational awareness across government networks.
  • India’s participation in the Quad Cybersecurity Partnership (2023) and the Global Forum on Cyber Expertise (GFCE) aligns domestic standards with international norms.

India's Water Governance: Jal Jeevan Mission Progress, River Interlinking Projects, and Groundwater Management

The Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM), launched on 15 August 2019, aims to provide Functional Household Tap Connections (FHTC) to every rural household by 2024. As of 31 March 2024, the Ministry of Jal Shakti reported over 12 crore tap connections covering a majority of the targeted households. The mission follows a “Har Ghar Jal” certification process where Gram Panchayats self‑declare saturation after third‑party verification. Funding is shared 50:50 between Centre and States for non‑North‑East states and 90:10 for North‑East and Himalayan states. The mission also integrates source sustainability measures — rainwater harvesting, greywater reuse, and spring‑shed development — through the “Jal Shakti Abhiyan: Catch the Rain” campaign.

River interlinking remains a long‑term supply‑side strategy. The Ken‑Betwa Link Project (KBLP), the first under the National Perspective Plan, received cabinet approval in December 2021 with an estimated cost of ₹ 44,605 crore. The project envisages a 221 km canal transferring 1,034 million cubic metres (MCM) of water from the Ken basin to the Betwa basin, benefiting 6.2 lakh hectares in Bundelkhand. Environmental clearance was granted in August 2022; land acquisition and rehabilitation are underway. The second priority link, the Damanganga‑Pinjal link, is in the Detailed Project Report (DPR) stage.

Groundwater governance is addressed through the Atal Bhujal Yojana (ABY), a World Bank‑supported scheme launched in April 2020 covering 8,350 gram panchayats in seven water‑stressed states. ABY promotes community‑led water budgeting, demand‑side management, and convergence with MGNREGS for recharge structures. The Central Ground Water Authority (CGWA) issued revised guidelines in September 2023 mandating a “No‑Objection Certificate” for new industrial extraction in over‑exploited blocks and introducing a tiered water‑pricing framework for commercial users.

Monitoring and Data

  • JJM dashboard updates daily; each FHTC is geo‑tagged and linked to the Aadhaar‑seeded beneficiary database.
  • National Water Informatics Centre (NWIC) hosts the “India Water Resources Information System” (India‑WRIS) providing real‑time reservoir storage, groundwater levels, and water quality parameters.
  • The Composite Water Management Index (CWMI) 2023 ranked Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Andhra Pradesh as top performers.

India's Trade Policy Shift: Recent Free Trade Agreements with UAE, Australia, and the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework

India’s trade diplomacy accelerated after 2021 with the conclusion of two comprehensive economic partnership agreements and active participation in a plurilateral framework. The India‑UAE Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA) was signed on 18 February 2022 and entered into force on 1 May 2022. It eliminates tariffs on 90 % of tariff lines (covering 97 % of bilateral trade value) within five years, includes a digital trade chapter, and provides for a “Rules of Origin” threshold of 40 % value addition. Bilateral merchandise trade rose from US$ 72.9 billion in FY 2021‑22 to over US$ 84 billion in FY 2023‑24.

The India‑Australia Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement (ECTA) was signed on 2 April 2022 and became effective on 29 December 2022. It grants duty‑free access for 96 % of Indian exports (by value) to Australia, especially in textiles, leather, and pharmaceuticals, while Australia gains preferential access for coal, wine, and agricultural products. A joint committee reviews implementation annually; the first review in March 2024 noted a significant increase in Indian exports to Australia.

Simultaneously, India joined the Indo‑Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity (IPEF) launched by the United States in May 2022. IPEF comprises four pillars — trade, supply chains, clean economy, and fair economy — but does not include market‑access commitments. India participates in the supply‑chain and clean‑economy pillars, contributing to the “Critical Minerals Dialogue” and the “Clean Energy Supply Chain Initiative”. In November 2023, the IPEF partners signed the “Supply Chain Resilience Agreement”, establishing a crisis‑response network and a labour‑rights monitoring mechanism.

Strategic Implications

  • CEPA and ECTA diversify India’s export destinations away from traditional markets (US, EU) toward West Asia and Oceania.
  • Rules‑of‑origin provisions encourage domestic value addition, aligning with the “Atmanirbhar Bharat” manufacturing push.
  • IPEF engagement signals India’s willingness to shape non‑tariff norms (digital trade, labour, environment) without conceding tariff reductions.

India's Defence Export Strategy: Defence Export Promotion Council, Offset Policy Reforms, and Emerging Markets

The Defence Export Promotion Council (DEPC) was constituted in July 2020 under the Department of Defence Production to act as the single‑window facilitator for defence exports. The council’s mandate includes market identification, export‑credit facilitation, and coordination with the Ministry of External Affairs for government‑to‑government (G2G) agreements. India set an ambitious target of US$ 5 billion in defence exports by 2024‑25; actual exports reached US$ 2.1 billion in FY 2023‑24, a 32 % year‑on‑year growth.

The Defence Offset Policy was revised in 2020, raising the offset obligation from 30 % to 50 % of the contract value for deals above ₹ 2,000 crore, with a multiplier for technology transfer and MSME participation. Offsets can be discharged through direct purchase, joint ventures, or investment in Indian defence R&D. The policy also introduced a “Defence Export Clearance” fast‑track for items on the “Positive Indigenisation List” (PIL), which now contains 4,666 items across four PILs (2021‑2024).

Key export successes include the BrahMos supersonic cruise missile deal with the Philippines (US$ 375 million, signed January 2022), the Akash‑NG surface‑to‑air missile system to Armenia (US$ 250 million, 2023), and the Dornier‑228 maritime patrol aircraft to Sri Lanka and Mauritius. The Indian Navy’s “Mission‑Based Deployments” and the Indian Coast Guard’s “Capacity Building” programmes have created demand for indigenously built patrol vessels and offshore patrol vessels (OPVs) in the Indian Ocean Region.
