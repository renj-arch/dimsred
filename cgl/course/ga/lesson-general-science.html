Physics — Mechanics and Motion

1.1 Newton's Laws and Their Applications

Classical mechanics begins with the three laws formulated by Sir Isaac Newton in his 1687 work Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica. The first law, often called the law of inertia, states that a body remains at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by a net external force; this principle explains why passengers lurch forward when a bus brakes suddenly, because their bodies tend to continue moving at the original speed. The second law quantifies force as the product of mass and acceleration (F = ma), establishing that the acceleration produced in an object is directly proportional to the net force applied and inversely proportional to its mass; consequently, a heavier vehicle requires a larger engine force to achieve the same acceleration as a lighter one. The third law asserts that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction, a principle visible when a swimmer pushes water backward to propel forward, or when a rocket expels hot gases downward to gain upward thrust. These laws govern everything from the trajectory of a cricket ball to the orbital motion of satellites, and they remain the foundation for engineering calculations in bridge construction, vehicle dynamics, and space launch trajectories.

1.2 Work, Energy, and Power

Work in physics is defined as the scalar product of force and displacement in the direction of the force (W = F·s cos θ), measured in joules (J), where one joule equals one newton-metre. When a porter lifts a suitcase vertically, positive work is done against gravity; when the same porter lowers it, gravity does positive work while the porter does negative work. Energy, the capacity to do work, exists in multiple forms — kinetic energy (½mv²) due to motion, gravitational potential energy (mgh) due to position in a gravitational field, and elastic potential energy (½kx²) stored in a stretched spring. The work–energy theorem states that the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, providing a powerful alternative to force-based analysis for problems involving variable forces. The law of conservation of energy, first articulated in its modern form by Hermann von Helmholtz in 1847, declares that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed; thus, a swinging pendulum continuously exchanges kinetic and potential energy while the total mechanical energy remains constant in the absence of friction. Power, the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred (P = W/t), is measured in watts (W), where one watt equals one joule per second; a 100-watt bulb converts electrical energy into light and heat at the rate of 100 joules every second. These concepts are essential for understanding machines, engines, and the efficiency of energy conversion in everyday appliances.

1.3 Gravitation and Planetary Motion

Newton's law of universal gravitation states that every particle of matter attracts every other particle with a force directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres (F = Gm₁m₂/r²), where G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² is the universal gravitational constant determined experimentally by Henry Cavendish in 1798. This inverse-square law explains why the gravitational acceleration at Earth's surface (g ≈ 9.8 m/s²) decreases with altitude and why the Moon, though much less massive than the Sun, exerts a stronger tidal influence on Earth because tidal force depends on the gradient of the gravitational field across Earth's diameter. Johannes Kepler's three laws of planetary motion, derived from Tycho Brahe's precise observations, describe elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus (first law), equal areas swept in equal times (second law), and the square of the orbital period proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis (third law, T² ∝ a³). Newton later showed that Kepler's laws emerge mathematically from his own law of gravitation combined with the laws of motion. Artificial satellites obey the same principles: a geostationary satellite orbits at approximately 35,786 km above the equator with a period of 24 hours, matching Earth's rotation so it appears fixed in the sky, a concept first proposed by Arthur C. Clarke in 1945. Escape velocity from Earth's surface, derived by equating initial kinetic energy to gravitational potential energy at infinity, is √(2GM/R) ≈ 11.2 km/s, a value critical for space mission design.

Physics — Waves, Light, and Electricity

2.1 Wave Motion and Sound

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy through a medium or space without net transport of matter. Mechanical waves, such as sound, require a material medium and propagate via oscillations of particles about their equilibrium positions; electromagnetic waves, by contrast, consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields and can travel through vacuum. Wave parameters include wavelength (λ), the distance between successive crests; frequency (f), the number of oscillations per second measured in hertz (Hz); and wave speed (v = fλ). Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave in which particle displacement is parallel to the direction of propagation; its speed in air at 20 °C is approximately 343 m/s, increasing with temperature because warmer air molecules have higher average kinetic energy and transmit compressions more rapidly. The human ear detects frequencies from about 20 Hz to 20 kHz; frequencies above this range are ultrasound, used in medical imaging (sonography) and industrial flaw detection, while frequencies below are infrasound, produced by earthquakes and large explosions. The Doppler effect — the apparent change in frequency when source and observer move relative to each other — explains the rising pitch of an approaching siren and is exploited in radar speed guns and astronomical redshift measurements to determine the recession velocity of distant galaxies.

2.2 Light: Reflection, Refraction, and Optical Instruments

Light exhibits wave–particle duality, but for most optical phenomena at the macroscopic level the wave model suffices. Reflection follows the law that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal to the surface; plane mirrors produce virtual, upright, laterally inverted images of the same size as the object. Refraction, the bending of light at the interface between two media of different optical densities, is governed by Snell's law (n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂), where refractive index n = c/v is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum (c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s) to its speed in the medium. Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from a denser to a rarer medium at an angle exceeding the critical angle (sin θc = n₂/n₁); this principle enables optical fibres to transmit data over thousands of kilometres with minimal loss. Lenses refract light to form real or virtual images: a convex lens converges parallel rays to a focal point, while a concave lens diverges them. The lens formula (1/v − 1/u = 1/f) and magnification (m = v/u) allow calculation of image position and size. The human eye functions as a variable-focus optical system: the cornea provides most of the refractive power, while the crystalline lens adjusts its curvature (accommodation) to focus near and far objects on the retina. Defects such as myopia (near-sightedness) and hypermetropia (far-sightedness) are corrected with concave and convex lenses respectively. Dispersion, the splitting of white light into its constituent colours by a prism, occurs because refractive index varies with wavelength (violet bends most, red least); this phenomenon produces rainbows when sunlight refracts, reflects internally, and refracts again inside water droplets.

2.3 Electricity and Magnetism

Electric charge, measured in coulombs (C), is a fundamental property of matter; like charges repel, unlike charges attract, with a force described by Coulomb's law (F = kq₁q₂/r², where k = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²). Electric current (I), the rate of flow of charge (I = Q/t), is measured in amperes (A); one ampere represents one coulomb passing a point per second. In metallic conductors, current is carried by free electrons drifting at millimetres per second, yet the electric field propagates at near light speed, so a bulb lights almost instantly when a switch is closed. Ohm's law states that the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it (V = IR), provided temperature remains constant; resistance (R), measured in ohms (Ω), depends on the material's resistivity (ρ), length (l), and cross-sectional area (A) through R = ρl/A. Resistivity increases with temperature for metals but decreases for semiconductors, a property exploited in thermistors. Electrical power dissipated in a resistor is P = VI = I²R = V²/R, measured in watts; the kilowatt-hour (kWh), the commercial unit of electrical energy, equals 3.6 × 10⁶ joules. Series circuits share the same current while voltages add; parallel circuits share the same voltage while currents add. Magnetism arises from moving charges: a current-carrying wire produces a circular magnetic field (right-hand grip rule), and a coil (solenoid) behaves like a bar magnet. Electromagnetic induction, discovered by Michael Faraday in 1831, states that a changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an electromotive force (emf = −dΦ/dt); this principle underlies electric generators, transformers, and induction motors. Alternating current (AC) reverses direction periodically, typically at 50 Hz in India, allowing efficient voltage transformation for long-distance transmission.

Example 1
A convex lens of focal length 20 cm forms a real image of an object placed 30 cm from the lens. Calculate the image distance and magnification. State the nature of the image.
Solution: Using the lens formula 1/v − 1/u = 1/f with sign convention (u negative for real object, f positive for convex lens): u = −30 cm, f = +20 cm. 1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/20 + 1/(−30) = (3 − 2)/60 = 1/60. Therefore v = +60 cm. The positive sign indicates a real image formed on the opposite side of the lens. Magnification m = v/u = 60/(−30) = −2. The negative sign shows the image is inverted; magnitude 2 means it is twice the size of the object. Nature: real, inverted, magnified.
Exam Insight
In SSC CGL, numerical questions on lenses and mirrors almost always use the Cartesian sign convention (distances measured from the pole, incident light direction positive). Memorise the standard results: for a convex lens, object between f and 2f gives real, inverted, magnified image beyond 2f; object at 2f gives same-size image at 2f; object beyond 2f gives diminished image between f and 2f. These patterns appear repeatedly in previous papers.

Chemistry — Atomic Structure and Periodic Properties

3.1 Atomic Models and Subatomic Particles

The concept of the atom evolved from Dalton's indivisible spheres (1808) to J.J. Thomson's "plum pudding" model (1897) after his cathode-ray experiments discovered the electron, a negatively charged particle with a charge-to-mass ratio (e/m) of 1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg. Ernest Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1911), conducted by Geiger and Marsden, revealed that most alpha particles passed through with little deflection while a few rebounded sharply, leading to the nuclear model: a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing protons (discovered by Rutherford in 1919) and later neutrons (discovered by James Chadwick in 1932), with electrons orbiting at relatively large distances. Niels Bohr refined this in 1913 by postulating quantised electron orbits where angular momentum is an integer multiple of h/2π (h = Planck's constant = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s); electrons emit or absorb photons of specific energies (ΔE = hν) when jumping between orbits, explaining the line spectra of hydrogen. The modern quantum mechanical model, developed by Schrödinger, Heisenberg, and Dirac in the 1920s, replaces definite orbits with orbitals — regions of space where the probability of finding an electron is high — described by four quantum numbers: principal (n = 1, 2, 3…), azimuthal (l = 0 to n−1, corresponding to s, p, d, f subshells), magnetic (mₗ = −l to +l), and spin (mₛ = ±½). The Pauli exclusion principle states that no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers, limiting each orbital to two electrons of opposite spin. The Aufbau principle guides the order of filling (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p…), while Hund's rule requires maximum multiplicity — electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly before pairing.

3.2 Periodic Classification and Periodic Trends

Dmitri Mendeleev's periodic table (1869) arranged elements by increasing atomic mass and similar chemical properties, leaving gaps for undiscovered elements (eka-boron, eka-aluminium, eka-silicon) whose properties he predicted with remarkable accuracy. The modern table, based on Henry Moseley's work (1913) showing that atomic number (Z) is the fundamental property, arranges elements in 18 groups (vertical columns) and 7 periods (horizontal rows). Elements in the same group have identical valence-shell electron configurations, leading to similar chemical behaviour; for example, Group 1 alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) all have ns¹ configuration and form +1 ions. Periodic trends arise from effective nuclear charge (Zₑff) and atomic radius: across a period, Zₑff increases while radius decreases, causing ionisation energy (energy to remove an electron) and electronegativity (tendency to attract shared electrons) to increase; down a group, additional shells increase radius and shielding, so ionisation energy and electronegativity decrease. Metallic character decreases across a period and increases down a group. The noble gases (Group 18) have complete valence shells (ns²np⁶ except He 1s²), giving them very high ionisation energies and near-zero reactivity under normal conditions. Transition metals (Groups 3–12) involve progressive filling of (n−1)d orbitals, exhibiting variable oxidation states and forming coloured compounds due to d–d transitions. Lanthanides and actinides (f-block) are placed separately at the bottom; lanthanide contraction — the steady decrease in ionic radius across the series — causes post-lanthanide elements (e.g., Hf, Ta, W) to have radii similar to their upper congeners (Zr, Nb, Mo), a fact with significant chemical consequences.

Chemistry — Chemical Bonding and Common Compounds

4.1 Types of Chemical Bonds

Atoms combine to achieve stable electron configurations, typically an octet (or duet for hydrogen) in the valence shell. Ionic bonding involves complete transfer of electrons from a low-ionisation-energy metal to a high-electron-affinity non-metal, forming oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction in a giant crystal lattice; sodium chloride (NaCl) exemplifies this, with each Na⁺ surrounded by six Cl⁻ and vice versa, giving a coordination number of 6 and a high melting point (801 °C). Covalent bonding involves sharing of electron pairs between non-metals; a single bond shares one pair (H–H, Cl–Cl), a double bond two pairs (O=O, CO₂), and a triple bond three pairs (N≡N). Polar covalent bonds arise when electronegativity difference is intermediate (0.4–1.7), creating partial charges (δ⁺, δ⁻) and a dipole moment; water (H₂O) is a classic polar molecule with a bent geometry (104.5°) and a net dipole moment of 1.85 D, responsible for its high boiling point, surface tension, and solvent properties. Metallic bonding features a "sea of delocalised electrons" surrounding positive metal ions, explaining electrical conductivity, malleability, and metallic lustre. Hydrogen bonding, a special strong dipole–dipole interaction between H bonded to N, O, or F and a lone pair on another electronegative atom, is crucial for the structure of DNA, the properties of water and ice, and the secondary structure of proteins. Van der Waals forces (London dispersion forces) are weak, temporary dipoles induced in all atoms and molecules; they are the only intermolecular forces in non-polar substances like noble gases and hydrocarbons, and their strength increases with molecular size and polarisability.

4.2 Acids, Bases, and Salts

According to the Arrhenius definition (1884), acids produce H⁺ ions in aqueous solution while bases produce OH⁻ ions; the Brønsted–Lowry definition (1923) generalises acids as proton donors and bases as proton acceptors, allowing species like NH₃ (ammonia) to act as a base without containing OH⁻. The Lewis definition further extends acidity to electron-pair acceptors and basicity to electron-pair donors. Strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) dissociate completely in water; weak acids (CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, H₃PO₄) establish an equilibrium (HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻) characterised by the acid dissociation constant Ka. The pH scale (pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]) quantifies acidity: pH < 7 acidic, pH = 7 neutral, pH > 7 basic; each unit change represents a tenfold change in [H⁺]. Indicators such as phenolphthalein (colourless in acid, pink in base, transition pH 8.2–10) and methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in base, transition pH 3.1–4.4) signal the equivalence point in titrations. Salts are ionic compounds formed from acid–base neutralisation; those of strong acid + strong base (NaCl, K₂SO₄) yield neutral solutions (pH ≈ 7), while salts of weak acid + strong base (CH₃COONa, Na₂CO₃) hydrolyse to give basic solutions, and salts of strong acid + weak base (NH₄Cl, AlCl₃) give acidic solutions. Buffer solutions, containing a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base and conjugate acid), resist pH change upon addition of small amounts of acid or base; the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation (pH = pKa + log₁₀[base]/[acid]) calculates buffer pH. Blood maintains pH ≈ 7.4 via the carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer system (H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻), a vital physiological application.

4.3 Important Chemical Compounds in Daily Life

Sodium chloride (NaCl), common salt, is essential for nerve impulse transmission and fluid balance; its industrial production via the Solvay process (ammonia-soda process) yields sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, washing soda) and sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃, baking soda). Baking soda decomposes on heating (2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O), releasing CO₂ that leavens baked goods. Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), found in limestone, marble, and chalk, decomposes to calcium oxide (CaO, quicklime) and CO₂; quicklime reacts vigorously with water to form calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂, slaked lime), used in mortar and whitewash. Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), the "king of chemicals," is manufactured by the contact process (SO₂ + ½O₂ ⇌ SO₃ catalysed by V₂O₅, then SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄); its dehydrating property chars sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O) and its high boiling point allows it to displace more volatile acids from their salts. Ammonia (NH₃), produced by the Haber–Bosch process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, Fe catalyst, 450 °C, 200 atm), is the precursor to nitrogenous fertilisers (urea, ammonium nitrate) and nitric acid (Ostwald process). Bleaching powder (CaOCl₂) is made by passing chlorine over dry slaked lime; it releases chlorine slowly, acting as a disinfectant and bleaching agent. Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O), obtained by heating gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) to 120–130 °C, sets into a hard mass on mixing with water due to rehydration to gypsum, making it useful for casts, moulds, and decorative work.

Biology — Cell Biology and Human Physiology

5.1 Cell Structure and Organelle Functions

The cell, first observed by Robert Hooke in 1665 in cork tissue and later described as the basic unit of life by Schleiden and Schwann (1838–39), exists in two fundamental types: prokaryotic (bacteria, archaea) lacking a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, and eukaryotic (plants, animals, fungi, protists) possessing a nucleus and organelles. The plasma membrane, a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins (fluid mosaic model, Singer and Nicolson, 1972), regulates transport; small non-polar molecules diffuse freely, while ions and polar molecules require channels, carriers, or active transport pumps (e.g., Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase). The nucleus, enclosed by a double-membrane nuclear envelope with pores, houses chromatin (DNA + histone proteins) and the nucleolus, site of ribosomal RNA synthesis and ribosome assembly. Mitochondria, the "powerhouses," have a double membrane; the inner membrane folds into cristae where the electron transport chain generates ATP via oxidative phosphorylation (≈30–32 ATP per glucose). Chloroplasts, found in plants and algae, conduct photosynthesis in thylakoid membranes (light reactions) and stroma (Calvin cycle). The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) forms a network of tubules: rough ER, studded with ribosomes, synthesises secretory and membrane proteins; smooth ER synthesises lipids, detoxifies drugs, and stores calcium. The Golgi apparatus (Camillo Golgi, 1898) receives vesicles from ER, modifies proteins (glycosylation), sorts them, and dispatches them to lysosomes, plasma membrane, or secretion. Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes active at low pH, digesting macromolecules, worn-out organelles (autophagy), and engulfed pathogens (phagocytosis). Peroxisomes break down fatty acids and detoxify hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂ → H₂O + ½O₂ via catalase). The cytoskeleton — microtubules (tubulin), microfilaments (actin), and intermediate filaments — provides shape, enables intracellular transport, and drives cell division. Plant cells additionally possess a rigid cell wall (cellulose), a large central vacuole (turgor pressure), and plasmodesmata for intercellular communication.

5.2 Major Human Body Systems

The digestive system converts food into absorbable nutrients. Mechanical digestion begins in the mouth (mastication, salivary amylase from salivary glands), continues in the stomach (churning, pepsin activated by HCl, intrinsic factor for B₁₂ absorption), and concludes in the small intestine where pancreatic juice (trypsin, chymotrypsin, lipase, amylase) and bile (emulsifies fats) act; the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum absorb monosaccharides, amino acids, fatty acids, glycerol, vitamins, and minerals via villi and microvilli that vastly increase surface area. The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes, forms faeces, and hosts microbiota that synthesise vitamin K and some B vitamins. The respiratory system exchanges gases: air enters via nasal cavity (warming, filtering), passes through pharynx, larynx (voice box), trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, to alveoli where O₂ diffuses into pulmonary capillaries and CO₂ diffuses out; ventilation is driven by diaphragm and intercostal muscles changing thoracic volume. The circulatory system transports blood through a double circuit: pulmonary (right heart → lungs → left heart) and systemic (left heart → body → right heart). The heart has four chambers (right/left atria, right/left ventricles) with valves preventing backflow; the sinoatrial (SA) node initiates the cardiac impulse (≈72 beats/min at rest), conducted via atrioventricular (AV) node, bundle of His, and Purkinje fibres. Blood consists of plasma (55%), erythrocytes (RBCs, haemoglobin for O₂ transport), leukocytes (WBCs, immunity), and platelets (clotting). The nervous system comprises the central nervous system (brain, spinal cord) and peripheral nervous system (cranial and spinal nerves); neurons transmit signals via action potentials (Na⁺ influx, K⁺ efflux) and synapses (neurotransmitters like acetylcholine, dopamine). The excretory system centres on kidneys, each containing ~1 million nephrons that filter blood (glomerular filtration), reabsorb useful substances (proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule), and secrete wastes, producing urine concentrated by the counter-current multiplier in the loop of Henle and ADH-regulated collecting ducts.

Biology — Nutrition, Diseases, and Public Health

6.1 Vitamins, Minerals, and Deficiency Diseases

Vitamins are organic micronutrients required in small amounts for normal metabolism; they cannot be synthesised in sufficient quantities and must be obtained from diet. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) are absorbed with dietary lipids and stored in liver and adipose tissue, so excess can cause hypervitaminosis; water-soluble vitamins (B-complex, C) are not stored significantly and excess is excreted, making regular intake essential. Vitamin A (retinol) maintains vision (rhodopsin in rods), epithelial integrity, and immunity; deficiency causes night blindness, xerophthalmia, and increased infection susceptibility; sources include liver, carrots (β-carotene), and dark leafy greens. Vitamin D (cholecalciferol) is synthesised in skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol under UV-B sunlight; it promotes intestinal calcium absorption and bone mineralisation; deficiency causes rickets (children) and osteomalacia (adults); dietary sources are fatty fish, egg yolk, and fortified milk. Vitamin E (tocopherol) is a lipid-soluble antioxidant protecting cell membranes; deficiency is rare but causes haemolytic anaemia in premature infants. Vitamin K (phylloquinone, menaquinone) is essential for γ-carboxylation of clotting factors II, VII, IX, X; deficiency causes bleeding disorders; gut bacteria synthesise menaquinone. B-complex vitamins act as coenzymes: B₁ (thiamine) for pyruvate dehydrogenase (beriberi — wet/oedematous and dry/neurological); B₂ (riboflavin) for FAD (cheilosis, glossitis); B₃ (niacin) for NAD⁺ (pellagra — dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia); B₆ (pyridoxine) for transamination; B₉ (folate) and B₁₂ (cobalamin) for DNA synthesis and RBC maturation (megaloblastic anaemia); B₁₂ absorption requires intrinsic factor. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble antioxidant and cofactor for collagen hydroxylation; deficiency causes scurvy (bleeding gums, poor wound healing, anaemia); sources are citrus fruits, amla, guava. Essential minerals include calcium (bones, muscle contraction, clotting), iron (haemoglobin, cytochromes — deficiency causes microcytic anaemia), iodine (thyroid hormones — deficiency causes goitre, cretinism), zinc (enzyme cofactor, immunity), and fluorine (dental enamel — excess causes fluorosis).

6.2 Communicable and Non-Communicable Diseases

Communicable diseases are caused by pathogens — bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, helminths — and spread directly (droplet, contact, sexual) or indirectly (vectors, fomites, water, food). Bacterial diseases: tuberculosis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis, airborne, affects lungs, BCG vaccine), typhoid (Salmonella Typhi, faecal-oral, Widal test), cholera (Vibrio cholerae, waterborne, rice-water stools, oral rehydration therapy), diphtheria (Corynebacterium diphtheriae, toxin, DPT vaccine), tetanus (Clostridium tetani, spores in soil, neurotoxin, TT vaccine), pertussis (Bordetella pertussis, whooping cough).

Thermodynamics and Heat Transfer

The first law of thermodynamics states that energy cannot be created or destroyed; the change in internal energy (ΔU) of a system equals the heat added to the system (Q) minus the work done by the system (W): ΔU = Q − W. For a cyclic process ΔU = 0, so net heat input equals net work output. The second law introduces entropy (S), a measure of disorder; for any spontaneous process the total entropy of the universe increases (ΔS_universe > 0). The Kelvin‑Planck statement forbids a heat engine that converts heat completely into work without rejecting some heat to a colder reservoir; the Clausius statement forbids heat flowing spontaneously from a colder to a hotter body without external work. Carnot’s theorem shows that the maximum possible efficiency of a heat engine operating between two absolute temperatures T_hot and T_cold is η_Carnot = 1 − T_cold/T_hot, where temperatures are in kelvin. Real engines (steam turbine, internal combustion) achieve lower efficiencies because of irreversibilities such as friction, turbulence, and finite temperature differences during heat exchange.

Heat transfer occurs by three mechanisms. Conduction is the diffusion of kinetic energy through a material without bulk motion; Fourier’s law gives the heat flux q = −k ∇T, where k is thermal conductivity. Metals have high k (≈400 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ for copper) because free electrons transport energy; insulators have low k (≈0.04 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ for air). Convection involves fluid motion carrying enthalpy; the convective heat‑transfer coefficient h relates the surface heat flux to the temperature difference: q = h (T_surface − T_fluid). Natural convection arises from buoyancy forces (Grashof number), while forced convection is driven by external means (Reynolds number). Radiation transfers energy via electromagnetic waves; the Stefan‑Boltzmann law gives the emissive power of a black body E = σT⁴ (σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴). Real surfaces emit εσT⁴, where emissivity ε ≤ 1. Kirchhoff’s law states that at thermal equilibrium absorptivity equals emissivity for each wavelength.

Phase changes involve latent heat. The latent heat of fusion (L_f) for water is 334 kJ kg⁻¹; the latent heat of vaporisation (L_v) is 2260 kJ kg⁻¹ at 100 °C. During melting or boiling the temperature remains constant while energy goes into breaking intermolecular bonds. The Clausius‑Clapeyron equation dP/dT = L/(TΔV) describes the slope of the coexistence curve on a P‑T diagram. Applications include refrigeration cycles (vapour‑compression, absorption), heat pumps, and the design of thermal insulation where low‑k materials and reflective surfaces minimise conductive and radiative losses.

Modern Physics: Atomic Nucleus and Radioactivity

The nucleus contains protons (Z) and neutrons (N); the mass number A = Z + N. Nuclear radius scales as r ≈ r₀A¹ᐟ³ with r₀ ≈ 1.2 fm. The strong nuclear force, mediated by mesons, binds nucleons with a short‑range attraction that overcomes electrostatic repulsion between protons. Binding energy per nucleon (B/A) peaks near iron‑56 (≈8.8 MeV), explaining why fusion releases energy for light nuclei and fission releases energy for heavy nuclei. The semi‑empirical mass formula (Weizsäcker) accounts for volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing terms, reproducing the observed binding‑energy curve.

Radioactive decay follows first‑order kinetics: N(t) = N₀e^(−λt), where λ is the decay constant. Half‑life t₁ᐟ₂ = ln 2/λ. Common decay modes: α‑decay (emission of He²⁺ nucleus), β⁻‑decay (n → p + e⁻ + ν̅_e), β⁺‑decay (p → n + e⁺ + ν_e), electron capture, and γ‑decay (photon emission from an excited nucleus). Decay series such as the uranium‑238 series (U‑238 → Th‑234 → … → Pb‑206) illustrate sequential α and β steps. Activity A = λN is measured in becquerel (Bq) or curie (1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq). Radiocarbon dating uses the β⁻ decay of C‑14 (t₁ᐟ₂ ≈ 5730 yr) to estimate ages up to ~50 kyr.

Nuclear fission of U‑235 or Pu‑239 induced by thermal neutrons yields two medium‑mass fragments, 2–3 neutrons, and ~200 MeV energy. A self‑sustaining chain reaction requires a critical mass and a neutron multiplication factor k_eff ≥ 1. Reactors use moderators (water, graphite) to thermalise neutrons and control rods (boron, cadmium) to absorb excess neutrons. Fusion of light nuclei (e.g., D + T → He‑4 + n + 17.6 MeV) powers stars; achieving net energy on Earth requires plasma temperatures >10⁸ K and confinement (magnetic tokamak or inertial laser). Radiation protection follows the ALARA principle; dose equivalents use sievert (Sv) = gray × quality factor (Q = 1 for γ, 20 for α).

Worked Example
A sample contains 1.0 µg of pure cobalt‑60 (t₁ᐟ₂ = 5.27 yr). Calculate its initial activity in becquerel and the activity after 10 years.
Molar mass of Co‑60 ≈ 60 g mol⁻¹. Number of atoms N₀ = (1.0 × 10⁻⁶ g / 60 g mol⁻¹) × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ ≈ 1.00 × 10¹⁶ atoms. Decay constant λ = ln 2 / t₁ᐟ₂ = 0.693 / (5.27 yr × 3.156 × 10⁷ s yr⁻¹) ≈ 4.17 × 10⁻⁹ s⁻¹. Initial activity A₀ = λN₀ ≈ 4.17 × 10⁻⁹ s⁻¹ × 1.00 × 10¹⁶ ≈ 4.2 × 10⁷ Bq. After 10 yr, N = N₀e^(−λt) with t = 10 yr = 3.156 × 10⁸ s. λt ≈ 1.32, so N/N₀ = e^(−1.32) ≈ 0.267. Activity A = A₀ × 0.267 ≈ 1.1 × 10⁷ Bq.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the half‑life formula and the conversion between years and seconds; many questions ask for activity after a given time. Keep a mental table of common half‑lives (C‑14 5730 yr, U‑238 4.47 × 10⁹ yr, Co‑60 5.27 yr, I‑131 8 days) to avoid recalculating λ each time.

Organic Chemistry: Functional Groups and Reaction Mechanisms

Organic molecules are classified by functional groups that dictate reactivity. Alkanes (C–C, C–H σ bonds) undergo free‑radical halogenation via initiation (Cl₂ → 2Cl·), propagation (Cl· + RH → HCl + R·; R· + Cl₂ → RCl + Cl·), and termination steps. Alkenes possess a C=C π bond; electrophilic addition follows Markovnikov’s rule (the electrophile adds to the carbon bearing more hydrogens) because the more substituted carbocation intermediate is stabilised by hyperconjugation and inductive effects. Anti‑Markovnikov addition occurs in the presence of peroxides (radical chain) or via hydroboration‑oxidation (syn addition of H and OH). Alkynes undergo similar additions; catalytic hydrogenation with Lindlar’s catalyst stops at the cis‑alkene, while Na/NH₃ gives trans‑alkene.

Alcohols (–OH) are nucleophilic at oxygen and can be protonated to become good leaving groups (water). Primary alcohols oxidise to aldehydes (PCC) or carboxylic acids (KMnO₄, CrO₃/H₂SO₄); secondary alcohols give ketones; tertiary alcohols resist oxidation. Ether cleavage requires strong acid (HI, HBr) via SN2 at the less hindered carbon. Carbonyl compounds (aldehydes, ketones) undergo nucleophilic addition; the carbonyl carbon is electrophilic due to the polar C=O bond. Grignard reagents (RMgX) add to give alcohols after work‑up. The Wittig reaction converts carbonyls to alkenes using phosphonium ylides. Carboxylic acids and derivatives (esters, amides, acyl chlorides) react via nucleophilic acyl substitution; reactivity order: acyl chloride > anhydride > ester > amide. Hydrolysis of esters under acidic or basic conditions yields acid (or carboxylate) and alcohol; saponification is base‑catalysed and irreversible.

Aromatic electrophilic substitution (EAS) proceeds through a σ‑complex (Wheland intermediate). Activating groups (‑OH, ‑NH₂, ‑OR, ‑alkyl) donate electron density, directing ortho/para; deactivating groups (‑NO₂, ‑SO₃H, ‑COOH, ‑CHO) withdraw electron density, directing meta (except halogens, which are deactivating but ortho/para directors due to resonance donation). Nitration uses conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄ (NO₂⁺ electrophile); sulfonation uses SO₃/H₂SO₄; Friedel‑Crafts alkylation/acylation uses alkyl/acyl chloride with AlCl₃. Polynuclear aromatics (naphthalene, anthracene) show increased reactivity at the α‑positions.

Genetics: Mendelian Inheritance and Molecular Basis

Gregor Mendel’s experiments with Pisum sativum established three principles. The law of segregation: each diploid organism carries two alleles for a trait, which separate during gamete formation so each gamete receives one allele. The law of independent assortment: alleles of different genes segregate independently provided the genes are on different chromosomes or far apart on the same chromosome. The law of dominance: in a heterozygote the dominant allele masks the recessive phenotype. A monohybrid cross (Aa × Aa) yields a 3:1 phenotypic ratio; a dihybrid cross (AaBb × AaBb) yields 9:3:3:1 when genes assort independently.

At the molecular level, a gene is a DNA segment that encodes a functional product (protein or functional RNA). The central dogma: DNA → (transcription) → mRNA → (translation) → polypeptide. The genetic code is triplet, non‑overlapping, degenerate, and nearly universal; 61 codons specify 20 amino acids, three are stop signals. Transcription in eukaryotes involves RNA polymerase II, promoter elements (TATA box), and processing (5′ cap, 3′ poly‑A tail, splicing of introns). Translation occurs on ribosomes; tRNA anticodons pair with mRNA codons, and peptidyl‑transferase forms peptide bonds. Post‑translational modifications (phosphorylation, glycosylation) regulate protein activity.

Mutations alter the DNA sequence. Point mutations (substitution) can be silent, missense, or nonsense. Frameshift mutations (insertion/deletion not a multiple of three) change the reading frame. Chromosomal mutations include deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations, and aneuploidy (e.g., trisomy 21 → Down syndrome). DNA repair mechanisms (proofreading by DNA polymerase, mismatch repair, nucleotide excision repair, base excision repair, homologous recombination, non‑homologous end joining) maintain genome integrity. Epigenetic modifications (DNA methylation at CpG islands, histone acetylation/methylation) affect gene expression without altering sequence and can be heritable through mitosis.

Biotechnology and Its Applications

Recombinant DNA technology combines DNA from different sources. Restriction endonucleases (e.g., EcoRI, BamHI) cut at specific palindromic sequences, producing sticky or blunt ends. DNA ligase joins compatible ends. Vectors (plasmids, bacteriophages, cosmids, BACs, YACs) carry foreign DNA into host cells (E. coli, yeast, mammalian cells). Selection markers (antibiotic resistance, fluorescent proteins) identify transformants. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplifies a target sequence using thermostable DNA polymerase (Taq), primers, dNTPs, and cyclic denaturation (94–98 °C), annealing (50–65 °C), extension (72 °C). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) monitors amplification in real time via fluorescent dyes (SYBR Green) or probes (TaqMan) for gene‑expression analysis.

Gene cloning enables production of recombinant proteins (insulin, growth hormone, clotting factors) in bioreactors. Site‑directed mutagenesis introduces specific amino‑acid changes to study structure‑function relationships. CRISPR‑Cas9 provides targeted genome editing: a guide RNA (gRNA) directs Cas9 nuclease to a complementary DNA sequence adjacent to a PAM (NGG), creating a double‑strand break repaired by NHEJ (knockout) or HDR (precise edit). Applications include disease models, gene therapy (e.g., ex vivo correction of haematopoietic stem cells for β‑thalassaemia), and agricultural traits (herbicide resistance, biofortified crops).

Diagnostics exploit nucleic‑acid hybridisation (Southern, Northern blots), microarrays, and next‑generation sequencing (Illumina, Ion Torrent) for pathogen detection, cancer genomics, and prenatal screening. ELISA (enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay) detects antigens or antibodies using enzyme‑conjugated secondary antibodies and colourimetric substrates. Monoclonal antibodies (produced by hybridoma technology) serve as therapeutics (trastuzumab, rituximab) and research tools. Biosafety levels (BSL‑1 to BSL‑4) dictate containment for work with infectious agents; ethical guidelines (ICMR, WHO) govern human germline editing and clinical trial design.

  • Confusing the sign convention in the first law (ΔU = Q − W vs ΔU = Q + W) — always check whether work is done by or on the system.
  • Assuming all radioactive decays follow a single half‑life; decay chains involve multiple steps with different half‑lives.
  • Applying Markovnikov’s rule to radical additions or hydroboration‑oxidation, which give anti‑Markovnikov products.
  • Treating independent assortment as universal; linked genes violate it unless crossing over occurs.
  • Believing CRISPR‑Cas9 edits are always precise; off‑target effects and mosaicisms are common.
  • Mixing up the order of reactivity for carboxylic‑acid derivatives; remember acyl chloride > anhydride > ester > amide.
  • Using the Stefan‑Boltzmann law for non‑black bodies without including emissivity.

Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Current Circuits

Faraday’s law states that the induced electromotive force (emf) in a closed loop equals the negative rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop: ε = − dΦ/dt. Lenz’s law gives the direction: the induced current creates a magnetic field opposing the change in flux. For a coil of N turns, ε = −N dΦ/dt. Self‑inductance L of a coil is defined by Φ = L I, so the back emf is ε = −L dI/dt. Mutual inductance M between two coils gives ε₂ = −M dI₁/dt. Energy stored in an inductor is U = ½ L I².

In alternating current (AC) circuits, voltage and current vary sinusoidally: v(t) = Vₘ sin(ωt + φᵥ), i(t) = Iₘ sin(ωt + φᵢ). The root‑mean‑square (RMS) values are Vᵣₘₛ = Vₘ/√2 and Iᵣₘₛ = Iₘ/√2. Impedance Z combines resistance R, inductive reactance X_L = ωL, and capacitive reactance X_C = 1/(ωC) as Z = √[R² + (X_L − X_C)²]. The phase angle φ satisfies tan φ = (X_L − X_C)/R. Power factor = cos φ; real power P = Vᵣₘₛ Iᵣₘₛ cos φ, reactive power Q = Vᵣₘₛ Iᵣₘₛ sin φ, apparent power S = Vᵣₘₛ Iᵣₘₛ.

Resonance in a series RLC circuit occurs when X_L = X_C, giving ω₀ = 1/√(LC). At resonance, impedance is minimum (Z = R) and current is maximum. The quality factor Q = ω₀L/R = 1/(ω₀CR) measures sharpness. In parallel resonance, impedance is maximum. Transformers operate on mutual induction: Vₛ/Vₚ = Nₛ/Nₚ = Iₚ/Iₛ for an ideal transformer. Losses include core (hysteresis, eddy currents) and copper (I²R) losses; efficiency η = output power / input power × 100 %.

Three‑phase AC systems use three voltages 120° apart. Line voltage V_L = √3 V_ph (star) or V_L = V_ph (delta). Line current I_L = I_ph (star) or I_L = √3 I_ph (delta). Total power P = √3 V_L I_L cos φ. Power factor correction adds capacitors to reduce reactive power, improving voltage regulation and reducing transmission losses.

Wave Optics: Interference, Diffraction, and Polarisation

Interference arises when two or more coherent waves superpose. For two sources of equal amplitude A and phase difference δ, resultant intensity I = 4A² cos²(δ/2). Constructive interference occurs at δ = 2mπ (bright fringes), destructive at δ = (2m+1)π (dark fringes). In Young’s double‑slit experiment, fringe width β = λD/d, where λ is wavelength, D distance to screen, d slit separation. White light produces coloured fringes because β ∝ λ.

Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles. Single‑slit diffraction minima occur at a sin θ = mλ (m = ±1, ±2,…), where a is slit width. Central maximum width = 2λD/a. The intensity distribution is I(θ) = I₀ [sin(β)/β]² with β = (π a sin θ)/λ. A diffraction grating with N slits produces principal maxima at d sin θ = mλ; angular dispersion dθ/dλ = m/(d cos θ). Resolving power R = λ/Δλ = mN.

Polarisation restricts the vibration direction of the electric field vector. Unpolarised light becomes plane‑polarised after passing through a polaroid (Malus’s law: I = I₀ cos²θ). Brewster’s angle θ_B satisfies tan θ_B = n₂/n₁; reflected light is perfectly s‑polarised at this angle. Double refraction in birefringent crystals splits light into ordinary (o) and extraordinary (e) rays with different refractive indices. A quarter‑wave plate introduces a π/2 phase shift, converting linear to circular polarisation when the incident light is at 45° to the optic axis.

Applications: anti‑reflection coatings use thin‑film interference (optical thickness = λ/4). Holography records both amplitude and phase via interference between object and reference beams. Optical fibres rely on total internal reflection; numerical aperture NA = √(n₁² − n₂²). Polarising sunglasses reduce glare by blocking horizontally polarised reflected light.

Worked Example: Fringe Shift in Young’s Experiment
In a double‑slit setup (λ = 600 nm, d = 0.3 mm, D = 1.5 m), a thin mica sheet of thickness t = 5 µm and refractive index n = 1.58 is placed over one slit. Calculate the fringe shift on the screen.
The optical path difference introduced is Δ = (n − 1)t = (0.58)(5 µm) = 2.9 µm = 2900 nm. Number of fringes shifted = Δ/λ = 2900/600 ≈ 4.83 fringes. Linear shift = (Δ/λ) × β, where β = λD/d = (600×10⁻⁹ × 1.5)/(0.3×10⁻³) = 3 mm. Shift = 4.83 × 3 mm ≈ 14.5 mm toward the side with the mica sheet.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the three key formulas: β = λD/d (fringe width), a sin θ = mλ (single‑slit minima), and Malus’s law I = I₀ cos²θ. When a question gives a thin film or mica sheet, always compute (n−1)t first; the fringe shift is simply that path difference divided by λ multiplied by β. For polarisation, identify whether the problem asks for intensity after one polaroid (Malus) or after two crossed polaroids with a wave plate in between (phase shift adds 90°).

Chemical Kinetics, Equilibrium, and Electrochemistry

The rate law for a reaction aA + bB → products is rate = k[A]ˣ[B]ʸ, where x and y are the orders determined experimentally. Overall order = x + y. Integrated rate equations: zero‑order [A] = [A]₀ − kt; first‑order ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt; second‑order 1/[A] − 1/[A]₀ = kt. Half‑life t₁/₂: zero‑order = [A]₀/(2k); first‑order = ln2/k (independent of concentration); second‑order = 1/(k[A]₀). Arrhenius equation k = A e^(−Ea/RT); a plot of ln k vs 1/T gives slope = −Ea/R.

Catalysis lowers activation energy. Homogeneous catalysts share phase with reactants; heterogeneous catalysts provide surface adsorption (Langmuir‑Hinshelwood mechanism). Enzyme kinetics follow Michaelis‑Menten: v = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S]); Lineweaver‑Burk double reciprocal plot gives 1/v = (Km/Vmax)(1/[S]) + 1/Vmax.

Chemical equilibrium: for aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, equilibrium constant Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ/([A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ). Kp = Kc(RT)^(Δn) where Δn = (c+d)−(a+b). Le Chatelier’s principle predicts shift direction for changes in concentration, pressure, temperature. Van’t Hoff equation: d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/(RT²); integration gives ln(K₂/K₁) = −ΔH°/R (1/T₂ − 1/T₁).

Electrochemistry: Galvanic cell converts chemical energy to electrical energy. Cell notation: anode | anode electrolyte || cathode electrolyte | cathode. Standard electrode potentials (E°) measured vs SHE. Cell EMF E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. Nernst equation: E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q; at 298 K, E = E° − (0.0591/n) log₁₀ Q. Gibbs free energy ΔG = −nFE. Electrolysis: Faraday’s laws — mass deposited m = (Q M)/(nF) where Q = It. Primary cells (dry cell, mercury cell) are non‑rechargeable; secondary cells (lead‑acid, Ni‑Cd, Li‑ion) are rechargeable. Fuel cells (H₂‑O₂) combine fuel and oxidant continuously; efficiency ≈ ΔG/ΔH.

Ecology: Ecosystem Dynamics, Energy Flow, and Biogeochemical Cycles

An ecosystem comprises biotic (producers, consumers, decomposers) and abiotic components. Energy enters as solar radiation; gross primary productivity (GPP) is total photosynthetic capture. Net primary productivity (NPP) = GPP − respiration of producers. Only ~10 % of energy transfers between trophic levels (Lindeman’s 10 % rule). Ecological pyramids: pyramid of numbers (often inverted for parasites), pyramid of biomass (usually upright, inverted in phytoplankton‑zooplankton systems), pyramid of energy (always upright).

Food chains are linear; food webs show interconnected chains. Keystone species disproportionately affect community structure (e.g., sea otters controlling sea urchins). Ecological succession: primary (bare rock, sand) → pioneer species (lichens, mosses) → intermediate → climax community. Secondary succession follows disturbance (fire, logging) where soil remains. Facilitation, tolerance, and inhibition models explain species replacement.

Biogeochemical cycles recycle essential elements. Carbon cycle: photosynthesis fixes CO₂; respiration, decomposition, combustion return CO₂. Oceanic carbonate system buffers atmospheric CO₂. Nitrogen cycle: nitrogen fixation (biological by Rhizobium, Azotobacter; industrial Haber process), nitrification (NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻), assimilation, ammonification, denitrification (NO₃⁻ → N₂). Phosphorus cycle lacks a gaseous phase; weathering of rocks releases phosphate, taken up by plants, returned via decomposition. Sulphur cycle involves volcanic emissions, bacterial oxidation/reduction, and atmospheric SO₂.

Population ecology: exponential growth dN/dt = rN; logistic growth dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K) where K is carrying capacity. Life tables give survivorship curves: Type I (high late‑life survival, humans), Type II (constant mortality, birds), Type III (high early mortality, fish). r‑selected species (high r, low K) vs K‑selected (low r, high K). Metapopulation dynamics consider patch occupancy and migration.

Biodiversity indices: species richness (S), Shannon‑Wiener H′ = −Σ pᵢ ln pᵢ, Simpson’s D = Σ pᵢ². Conservation strategies: in‑situ (protected areas, biosphere reserves), ex‑situ (seed banks, captive breeding). IUCN Red List categories (Extinct, Extinct in Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern). Major drivers of biodiversity loss: habitat fragmentation, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution, climate change.

  • Confusing the sign in the Nernst equation — remember E = E° − (0.0591/n) log Q at 298 K; a positive Q (products favoured) reduces E.
  • Assuming the 10 % energy transfer rule applies to biomass pyramids; it applies only to energy flow.
  • Treating all catalysts as homogeneous; heterogeneous catalysts operate via surface adsorption and have different rate‑determining steps.
  • Using the integrated first‑order half‑life formula for a reaction that is actually second order — half‑life then depends on initial concentration.
  • Mixing up the direction of shift for an exothermic reaction when temperature is increased; Le Chatelier predicts shift toward reactants.
  • Forgetting that the phosphorus cycle has no significant atmospheric reservoir, unlike carbon and nitrogen.
  • Applying the Shannon index formula without converting raw counts to proportions pᵢ; the index requires relative abundances.

Semiconductor Physics and Electronic Devices

Semiconductors occupy the conductivity range between conductors and insulators, with a band gap (Eg) typically 0.5–3 eV. In intrinsic silicon at 300 K the gap is 1.12 eV; thermal excitation creates equal numbers of electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence band, giving an intrinsic carrier concentration ni ≈ 1.5 × 1010 cm−3. Doping introduces donor (group V) or acceptor (group III) impurities, shifting the Fermi level toward the conduction or valence band respectively. An n‑type material has electrons as majority carriers (n ≈ ND), while p‑type has holes (p ≈ NA). The mass‑action law n·p = ni2 holds under equilibrium.

A p‑n junction forms when p‑type and n‑type regions meet. Diffusion of carriers creates a depletion region devoid of free carriers, with an internal electric field that opposes further diffusion. The built‑in potential Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NAND/ni2) is typically 0.6–0.8 V for silicon. Under forward bias (p‑side positive) the barrier lowers, allowing exponential current I = I0[exp(qV/kT) − 1]; under reverse bias the current saturates at I0 until breakdown (avalanche or Zener). The diode equation is the basis for rectifiers, clippers, and voltage regulators.

Bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) consist of two p‑n junctions (npn or pnp). In active mode the emitter‑base junction is forward biased and the collector‑base junction reverse biased. The common‑emitter current gain β = IC/IB ranges 50–300; α = IC/IE = β/(β+1). The Ebers‑Moll model gives IC = IS[exp(VBE/VT) − 1] − IS/βR[exp(VBC/VT) − 1]. Field‑effect transistors (FETs) operate by voltage‑controlled channel conductivity. In an n‑channel MOSFET, a positive gate voltage induces an inversion layer; the drain current in saturation is ID = ½ μnCox(W/L)(VGS − Vth)2. Complementary MOS (CMOS) pairs n‑ and p‑channel devices for low static power logic.

Logic gates are built from transistors: NOT (inverter), NAND, NOR, and their combinations realise any Boolean function. Propagation delay tpd ≈ 0.69 ReqCL for RC‑limited switching; fan‑out and capacitive loading determine maximum clock frequency. Memory elements (SRAM, DRAM, Flash) store charge or state; DRAM requires periodic refresh because charge leaks from the storage capacitor. Power dissipation has dynamic (α C V2f) and static (leakage) components; scaling reduces V but increases leakage, motivating high‑k dielectrics and FinFET geometries.

Immunology: Innate and Adaptive Immunity, Vaccines

The immune system is organised into innate (non‑specific, rapid) and adaptive (specific, memory) arms. Innate barriers include skin, mucosal cilia, low pH, lysozyme, and the complement cascade. Pattern‑recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll‑like receptors (TLRs) detect pathogen‑associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) — lipopolysaccharide (TLR4), flagellin (TLR5), unmethylated CpG DNA (TLR9). Activation triggers phagocytosis, cytokine release (IL‑1, TNF‑α, IFN‑γ), and inflammation. Natural killer (NK) cells eliminate virus‑infected or tumour cells lacking MHC‑I.

Adaptive immunity relies on lymphocytes generated in primary lymphoid organs (bone marrow for B cells, thymus for T cells). B‑cell receptors (membrane‑bound immunoglobulin) recognise native antigens; T‑cell receptors (TCRs) recognise peptide fragments presented by major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. MHC‑I presents endogenous peptides to CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs); MHC‑II presents exogenous peptides to CD4+ helper T cells (Th1, Th2, Th17, Tfh, Treg). Activation requires two signals: antigen‑specific TCR engagement and co‑stimulation (CD28‑B7). Without co‑stimulation, anergy or apoptosis ensues.

Humoral response: activated B cells differentiate into plasma cells secreting antibodies (IgM first, then class‑switched IgG, IgA, IgE). Affinity maturation occurs in germinal centres via somatic hypermutation and selection by follicular dendritic cells. Cell‑mediated response: Th1 cells secrete IFN‑γ activating macrophages; CTLs kill target cells via perforin/granzyme or Fas‑FasL pathways. Memory B and T cells persist long‑term, enabling faster, stronger secondary responses.

Vaccines exploit immunological memory. Live‑attenuated vaccines (measles, BCG) replicate weakly, inducing robust cellular and humoral immunity. Inactivated/killed vaccines (polio‑IPV, hepatitis A) require adjuvants (alum, MF59) and multiple doses. Subunit/recombinant vaccines (hepatitis B surface antigen, HPV VLPs) present purified antigens. mRNA vaccines (COVID‑19) deliver nucleoside‑modified mRNA encoding spike protein; host cells translate antigen, triggering both MHC‑I and MHC‑II pathways. Vector vaccines (adenovirus, vesicular stomatitis virus) use replication‑deficient viruses to express antigen. Correlates of protection vary: neutralising antibody titres for many viral vaccines; Th1‑biased cellular immunity for intracellular pathogens.

Worked Example: Diode Forward Current
A silicon p‑n junction diode at 300 K has a saturation current I0 = 2 × 10−12 A. Calculate the forward current when a forward bias of 0.65 V is applied. Use the diode equation I = I0[exp(qV/kT) − 1] with q/kT ≈ 38.7 V−1 at 300 K.
Substitute values: I = 2 × 10−12[exp(38.7 × 0.65) − 1]. Exponent = 25.155. exp(25.155) ≈ 8.5 × 1010. I ≈ 2 × 10−12 × 8.5 × 1010 = 0.17 A. The −1 term is negligible. Forward current ≈ 170 mA.
Exam‑Day Strategy
For semiconductor questions, memorise the thermal voltage VT = kT/q ≈ 25.9 mV at 300 K and the diode ideality factor n (≈1 for Si). In immunology, remember the MHC restriction: CD8+ → MHC‑I, CD4+ → MHC‑II. Vaccine types are frequently matched to examples; create a two‑column table (type ↔ example) for quick recall.

Environmental Chemistry: Air, Water, and Soil Pollution and Control Technologies

Air pollutants are classified as primary (emitted directly) and secondary (formed in atmosphere). Major primary pollutants: carbon monoxide (CO) from incomplete combustion, sulfur dioxide (SO2) from fossil‑fuel burning, nitrogen oxides (NOx) from high‑temperature combustion, particulate matter (PM10, PM2.5) from dust, soot, and secondary aerosol formation, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from solvents and fuels. Secondary pollutants include ozone (O3) from NOx‑VOC photochemistry, sulfuric and nitric acids (acid rain), and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN). Control technologies: electrostatic precipitators (ESP) and baghouse filters remove particulates; flue‑gas desulfurisation (wet limestone scrubbers) captures SO2; selective catalytic reduction (SCR) with ammonia reduces NOx to N2; catalytic converters in vehicles oxidise CO and VOCs and reduce NOx.

Water pollution parameters: biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) measures organic matter oxidisable by microbes; chemical oxygen demand (COD) includes chemically oxidisable substances. Nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus) cause eutrophication; heavy metals (Hg, Pb, Cd, As) bioaccumulate. Pathogens (coliforms) indicate faecal contamination. Treatment stages: primary (screening, sedimentation), secondary (activated sludge, trickling filters, oxidation ponds) for BOD removal, tertiary (filtration, nutrient stripping, disinfection by chlorination, UV, ozonation). Advanced oxidation processes (Fenton, photocatalysis) degrade recalcitrant organics.

Soil contamination arises from industrial waste, agricultural chemicals (pesticides, fertilizers), and improper waste disposal. Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) such as DDT, PCBs, dioxins resist degradation and undergo long‑range transport. Remediation methods: excavation and landfilling (ex‑situ), soil washing with chelating agents, bioremediation (microbial degradation, phytoremediation using hyperaccumulator plants), thermal desorption, and electrokinetic removal for metals. The concept of “polluter pays” and regulatory frameworks (e.g., ambient air quality standards, effluent discharge limits) guide enforcement.

Climate‑relevant pollutants: greenhouse gases (CO2, CH4, N2O, fluorinated gases) and black carbon (a component of PM2.5) contribute to radiative forcing. Carbon capture and storage (CCS) captures CO2 from point sources (post‑combustion amine scrubbing, pre‑combustion gasification, oxy‑fuel combustion) and stores it in geological formations. Methane mitigation includes leak detection in natural‑gas infrastructure and anaerobic digestion of organic waste.

Plant Physiology: Photosynthesis, Respiration, and Hormonal Regulation

Photosynthesis occurs in chloroplasts. Light‑dependent reactions in thylakoid membranes convert photon energy to chemical energy: photosystem II (PSII) splits water (2 H2O → O2 + 4 H+ + 4 e−), generating a proton gradient for ATP synthase (photophosphorylation). Photosystem I (PSI) reduces NADP+ to NADPH. The Z‑scheme summarises electron flow: H2O → PSII → plastoquinone → cytochrome b6f → plastocyanin → PSI → ferredoxin → NADP+. Cyclic electron flow around PSI produces ATP without NADPH.

Carbon fixation (Calvin‑Benson cycle) in stroma uses ATP and NADPH. Ribulose‑1,5‑bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco) catalyses CO2 addition to RuBP, forming 3‑phosphoglycerate (3‑PGA). Three turns fix three CO2, yielding one glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate (G3P) for carbohydrate synthesis; the remaining five G3P regenerate three RuBP. Photorespiration occurs when Rubisco oxygenates RuBP, consuming O2 and releasing CO2 without ATP gain; C4 and CAM plants concentrate CO2 to suppress photorespiration.

Respiration in mitochondria mirrors glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. Glycolysis yields 2 ATP (substrate‑level) and 2 NADH per glucose. Pyruvate dehydrogenase links to acetyl‑CoA. The citric acid cycle produces 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP per acetyl‑CoA. Electron transport chain complexes I–IV pump protons; ATP synthase yields ≈2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH2. Total ≈30–32 ATP per glucose in plants (slightly lower than animals due to shuttle costs).

Plant hormones (phytohormones) coordinate growth and stress responses. Auxins (indole‑3‑acetic acid) promote cell elongation, apical dominance, root initiation; synthetic auxins (2,4‑D) are herbicides. Gibberellins (GA3) stimulate stem elongation, seed germination, flowering. Cytokinins (zeatin) promote cell division, delay senescence. Abscisic acid (ABA) induces stomatal closure, seed dormancy, stress tolerance. Ethylene (gaseous) regulates fruit ripening, leaf abscission, stress signalling. Brassinosteroids, strigolactones, and jasmonates modulate development and defence. Hormone crosstalk (e.g., auxin‑cytokinin balance determines root vs shoot formation in tissue culture) is central to plant biotechnology.

Nanotechnology: Synthesis, Properties, and Applications

Nanomaterials have at least one dimension in the 1–100 nm range, where quantum confinement and high surface‑to‑volume ratio alter physical, chemical, and biological properties. Classification: zero‑dimensional (quantum dots, nanoparticles), one‑dimensional (nanowires, nanotubes), two‑dimensional (graphene, MoS2 nanosheets), three‑dimensional (nanocomposites, bulk nanostructured metals). Synthesis routes: top‑down (ball milling, lithography, etching) and bottom‑up (chemical reduction, sol‑gel, hydrothermal, chemical vapour deposition, molecular self‑assembly). Green synthesis uses plant extracts or microorganisms as reducing/capping agents.

Optical properties: quantum dots exhibit size‑tunable photoluminescence due to quantum confinement; emission wavelength λ ∝ 1/√(size). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) in noble‑metal nanoparticles (Au, Ag) gives strong absorption/scattering in visible region; SPR shifts with size, shape, dielectric environment, enabling colorimetric sensors. Magnetic properties: superparamagnetism in iron‑oxide nanoparticles below a critical size (≈20 nm) — zero coercivity, high susceptibility — useful for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agents and hyperthermia therapy.

Mechanical reinforcement: carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and graphene impart exceptional tensile strength and electrical conductivity to polymer matrices at low loadings. Nanocomposite barrier films reduce gas permeability. Catalysis: high surface area and exposed active facets enhance activity; e.g., Pt nanoparticles on carbon for fuel‑cell electrodes, TiO2 nanoparticles for photocatalytic water splitting and pollutant degradation.

Biomedical applications: drug delivery via liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, dendrimers; targeting ligands (antibodies, folate) enable active targeting. Theranostics combine therapy and imaging (e.g., iron‑oxide core with fluorescent shell). Toxicology concerns: nanoparticle uptake, oxidative stress, inflammation; regulatory guidelines (OECD, ISO) address risk assessment. Environmental remediation: nanoscale zero‑valent iron (nZVI) reduces chlorinated organics and heavy metals in groundwater; photocatalytic TiO2 degrades dyes and pesticides under UV/visible light.
