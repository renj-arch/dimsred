Chemical Kinetics — Rate Laws, Arrhenius Equation, Order & Catalysis

Deep dive into Chemical Kinetics — the study of reaction rates and the factors that influence them. The rate of reaction r = −(1/a) d[A]/dt = +(1/c) d[C]/dt for aA → cC. Differential rate laws: rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, where k is the rate constant, m and n are the orders with respect to each reactant (overall order = m + n). The method of initial rates determines orders by measuring the initial rate at different initial concentrations. Integrated rate laws: zero order [A] = [A]₀ − kt (t_{½} = [A]₀/2k), first order ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt (t_{½} = 0.693/k, independent of [A]₀), second order 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt (t_{½} = 1/(k[A]₀)). The Arrhenius equation: k = A e^{−E_a/RT}, where E_a is the activation energy (the minimum energy required for a reaction to occur), A is the pre-exponential factor (related to collision frequency and orientation). Taking logs: ln k = ln A − E_a/(RT). The activation energy can be found from the slope of ln k vs 1/T (slope = −E_a/R). Collision theory: molecules must collide with sufficient energy (≥ E_a) and proper orientation to react. The steric factor p accounts for orientation requirements. Transition state theory: reactants form a high-energy activated complex (transition state) at the top of the potential energy barrier; the Eyring equation: k = (k_B T/h) e^{−ΔG^‡/RT} = (k_B T/h) e^{ΔS^‡/R} e^{−ΔH^‡/RT}. Molecularity: elementary reactions are classified as unimolecular (one molecule decomposes or rearranges, e.g., radioactive decay, cis-trans isomerization), bimolecular (two molecules collide, the most common type), or termolecular (three molecules, rare). Reaction mechanisms: a series of elementary steps that sum to the overall reaction. The rate-determining step is the slowest step and governs the overall rate. The steady-state approximation assumes that reactive intermediates are consumed as fast as they are formed (d[I]/dt ≈ 0). Catalysis: a substance that speeds up a reaction without being consumed. Homogeneous catalysis (catalyst in same phase, e.g., acid-catalyzed ester hydrolysis), heterogeneous catalysis (catalyst in different phase, often solid, e.g., Haber-Bosch process with iron catalyst, catalytic converters in cars). Enzyme catalysis: Michaelis-Menten kinetics E + S ⇌ ES → E + P, with rate v = V_max[S]/(K_M + [S]), where V_max = k_cat[E]_tot and K_M is the Michaelis constant (substrate concentration at half V_max). The Lineweaver-Burk plot (1/v vs 1/[S]) linearizes the equation.

"The rate of a chemical reaction is determined by the energy barrier that must be overcome, not by the thermodynamic driving force."
— Adapted from Eyring and Polanyi
Part 1

⏱️ Rate Laws and Reaction Order

Rate Laws
Differential Rate Laws, Method of Initial Rates, and Integrated Rate Laws
The rate of reaction r is defined as the change in concentration per unit time. For aA + bB → products, the differential rate law is rate = k[A]^m[B]^n. The order with respect to each reactant (m for A, n for B) must be determined experimentally — they are NOT the stoichiometric coefficients. The overall order = m + n. The method of initial rates: run several experiments varying one initial concentration while holding others constant; measure the initial rate; if doubling [A] doubles the rate → first order in A; if doubling [A] quadruples the rate → second order; if no change → zero order. Integrated rate laws: for zero order (rate = k): [A] = [A]₀ − kt; a plot of [A] vs t is linear with slope −k. For first order (rate = k[A]): ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt; plot of ln[A] vs t is linear. For second order (rate = k[A]²): 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt; plot of 1/[A] vs t is linear. The half-life t_{½} is the time for [A] to reach [A]₀/2: zero order: t_{½} = [A]₀/(2k) (depends on [A]₀); first order: t_{½} = ln 2/k = 0.693/k (independent of [A]₀); second order: t_{½} = 1/(k[A]₀) (depends on [A]₀). First-order kinetics is characteristic of radioactive decay and many unimolecular reactions. Pseudo-first-order conditions: when one reactant is in large excess, its concentration is approximately constant, and the rate law simplifies (e.g., rate = k'[A] where k' = k[B]₀). Methods for determining order: the integrated rate law method (linearity of plots), the half-life method (how t_{½} varies with [A]₀), and the initial rates method.
Rate laws describe how reaction rate depends on concentration; the order must be determined experimentally, and integrated rate laws provide linear plots that distinguish zero, first, and second order reactions.
Part 2

🌡️ The Arrhenius Equation and Temperature Dependence

Arrhenius
k = A e^{−Ea/RT}, Activation Energy, Collision Theory, and Transition State Theory
The Arrhenius equation k = A e^{−E_a/RT} describes the temperature dependence of the rate constant. The activation energy E_a is the minimum kinetic energy that colliding molecules must have to react (typically 40−400 kJ/mol). The pre-exponential factor A is related to the frequency of collisions and the orientation probability. Taking natural logs: ln k = ln A − E_a/(RT). A plot of ln k vs 1/T (called an Arrhenius plot) yields a straight line with slope = −E_a/R and intercept = ln A. The two-point form: ln(k₂/k₁) = (E_a/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). A rule of thumb: for many reactions near room temperature, doubling the temperature increases the rate by a factor of about 2−4 (a 10°C rise roughly doubles the rate for E_a ≈ 50 kJ/mol). Collision theory: for a bimolecular gas-phase reaction, the rate is Z × f × p, where Z is the collision frequency (≈ 10²⁸ m⁻³s⁻¹ for gases at 1 atm), f = e^{−E_a/RT} is the fraction of collisions with sufficient energy, and p is the steric factor (0 to 1, accounting for orientation). The collision theory predicts A = p Z. Transition state theory (Eyring, Evans-Polanyi): reactants form an activated complex (transition state) at the top of the potential energy barrier. The Eyring equation: k = (k_B T/h) e^{−ΔG^‡/RT} = (k_B T/h) e^{ΔS^‡/R} e^{−ΔH^‡/RT}, where ΔG^‡ is the Gibbs free energy of activation, ΔH^‡ is the enthalpy of activation, and ΔS^‡ is the entropy of activation. For reactions in solution, ΔH^‡ ≈ E_a − RT. A positive ΔS^‡ suggests a dissociative mechanism (the transition state is more disordered); a negative ΔS^‡ suggests an associative mechanism (more ordered). The potential energy surface (PES) maps the energy as a function of nuclear coordinates; the reaction coordinate is the path along the minimum energy pathway from reactants to products, passing through the saddle point (the transition state). Kinetic isotope effects: substituting a lighter isotope (e.g., H → D) changes the vibrational zero-point energy, affecting E_a. A primary KIE (k_H/k_D > 1) indicates bond breaking at that site in the rate-determining step.
The Arrhenius equation quantifies how reaction rates increase with temperature through the activation energy barrier; collision and transition state theories provide molecular interpretations of the pre-exponential factor and the activation barrier.
Part 3

🧩 Reaction Mechanisms, Molecularity, and the Steady-State Approximation

Mechanisms
Elementary Reactions, Molecularity, Rate-Determining Step, and Steady-State Approximation
A reaction mechanism is a sequence of elementary steps that sum to the overall balanced equation. Each elementary step has a molecularity equal to the number of molecules that collide in that step. Unimolecular (molecularity 1): a single molecule decomposes or rearranges, e.g., N₂O₄ → 2NO₂; rate = k[N₂O₄] (first order). Bimolecular (molecularity 2): two molecules collide, e.g., NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂; rate = k[NO₂][CO] (second order overall). Termolecular (molecularity 3): three molecules collide simultaneously, very rare because three-body collisions are improbable, e.g., 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂; rate = k[NO]²[O₂]. For an elementary reaction, the rate law is directly given by the stoichiometry (the order equals the molecularity). The overall reaction may consist of several elementary steps; the rate-determining step (RDS) is the slowest step — it governs the overall rate. If the first step is slow (RDS), the overall rate law equals the rate law of the first step. If a later step is RDS, the concentration of intermediates that appear in the RDS rate law must be expressed in terms of reactants using the equilibrium approximation (if preceding steps are fast equilibria) or the steady-state approximation (SSA). The SSA assumes that the concentration of a reactive intermediate I remains constant after an initial induction period: d[I]/dt ≈ 0 (rate of formation = rate of consumption). This is valid when the intermediate is highly reactive and present at very low concentration. For the mechanism A → B (k₁), B → C (k₂), applying SSA to B: d[B]/dt = k₁[A] − k₂[B] = 0, so [B] = (k₁/k₂)[A]. The overall rate = k₂[B] = k₁[A]. The pre-equilibrium approximation assumes the first step reaches equilibrium quickly before the slow step: e.g., for A + B ⇌ I (fast, K = [I]/([A][B])), I → C (slow, k₂), then rate = k₂[I] = k₂K[A][B]. The SSA is more general and reduces to the equilibrium approximation when the reverse reaction is much faster than the forward consumption.
The molecularity of an elementary reaction determines its rate law; the rate-determining step controls the overall rate, and the steady-state approximation provides a powerful method for deriving rate laws for multi-step mechanisms.
Part 4

🧪 Catalysis: Homogeneous, Heterogeneous, and Enzyme Kinetics

Catalysis
How Catalysts Work, Heterogeneous Catalysis, and Michaelis-Menten Kinetics
A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy E_a — it is regenerated unchanged at the end and does not affect the equilibrium constant (K). Homogeneous catalysis: catalyst and reactants are in the same phase (usually liquid). Examples: acid-catalyzed ester hydrolysis (H⁺ protonates the carbonyl oxygen, making the carbon more electrophilic), catalytic hydrogenation using Wilkinson's catalyst (RhCl(PPh₃)₃) in solution. Heterogeneous catalysis: catalyst is a solid (often a metal) and reactants are gases or liquids. The mechanism involves five steps: (1) diffusion of reactants to the surface, (2) adsorption of reactants onto active sites, (3) surface reaction, (4) desorption of products, (5) diffusion of products away. The Langmuir-Hinshelwood kinetics models surface reactions: rate depends on the fraction of surface covered (θ = KP/(1 + KP) for simple Langmuir adsorption). Examples: Haber-Bosch (Fe catalyst for NH₃ synthesis), Ostwald process (Pt/Rh gauze for HNO₃), catalytic converters (Pt, Pd, Rh convert CO, NOₓ, hydrocarbons to CO₂, N₂, H₂O). Enzyme catalysis is a form of homogeneous biocatalysis. The Michaelis-Menten model: E + S ⇌ ES (rate constants k₁ forward, k₋₁ reverse), ES → E + P (rate constant k_cat = k₂). Applying the steady-state approximation to [ES]: d[ES]/dt = k₁[E][S] − k₋₁[ES] − k₂[ES] = 0. The total enzyme [E]_tot = [E] + [ES]. Solving gives the Michaelis-Menten equation: v = k_cat[E]_tot[S]/(K_M + [S]) = V_max[S]/(K_M + [S]), where K_M = (k₋₁ + k₂)/k₁ (the Michaelis constant, M). When [S] = K_M, v = V_max/2. The catalytic efficiency is k_cat/K_M (diffusion-limited maximum ≈ 10⁸−10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹). The Lineweaver-Burk plot: 1/v = (K_M/V_max)(1/[S]) + 1/V_max; slope = K_M/V_max, intercept = 1/V_max. Enzyme inhibition: competitive (inhibitor binds active site, increases apparent K_M, V_max unchanged), non-competitive (inhibitor binds elsewhere, decreases V_max, K_M unchanged), uncompetitive (inhibitor binds only ES complex, decreases both).
Catalysts lower the activation energy without being consumed; enzyme kinetics follows the Michaelis-Menten model, with K_M reflecting substrate affinity and V_max reflecting the maximum catalytic rate.
