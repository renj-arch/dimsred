Representation Theory — Linearizing Group Actions

Representation theory studies groups by their actions on vector spaces — turning abstract group elements into matrices. This linearizes group theory, making it amenable to linear algebra. It's one of the most powerful and unifying subjects in mathematics, with applications from number theory (automorphic forms) to quantum physics (particle classification).

"Representation theory is the study of symmetry through linear algebra — it's the language of quantum mechanics and the key to the Fourier transform."
— T.A. Springer (paraphrased)
Part 1

🏛️ Foundations — Representations and Characters

Linearizing Groups
Group Representations, G-Modules, and Characters
A representation of a group G on a vector space V is a homomorphism ρ: G → GL(V). Equivalently, V is a G-module where g·v = ρ(g)v. A representation is irreducible if it has no nontrivial G-invariant subspaces. Schur's lemma: any G-equivariant map between irreducible representations is either zero or an isomorphism; over ℂ, it's a scalar multiple of the identity. The character χ_V(g) = Tr(ρ(g)) is a class function (constant on conjugacy classes). For finite groups, the character table captures all irreducible representations. The orthogonality of characters: (1/|G|) Σ χ_i(g) χ_j(g) = δ_{ij}. Every representation decomposes as a direct sum of irreducibles: V = ⊕ n_i V_i where n_i = ⟨χ_V, χ_i⟩. Maschke's theorem: for finite groups with char(F) ∤ |G|, every representation is completely reducible.
Characters are the harmonic analysis of finite groups — they diagonalize the group algebra and reveal the internal structure.
Part 2

🔄 Induction, Restriction, and Frobenius Reciprocity

Building Representations
Induced Representations — From Subgroups to the Whole Group
If H ≤ G and W is an H-representation, the induced representation Ind_H^G W = ℂ[G] ⊗_{ℂ[H]} W is a G-representation. It's the universal way to extend an H-rep to G. Frobenius reciprocity: Hom_G(Ind_H^G W, V) ≅ Hom_H(W, Res_H^G V). Induction and restriction are adjoint functors. The Mackey formula describes how induction and restriction compose: Res_K^G Ind_H^G ≅ ⊕_{KgH} Ind_{K∩gHg^{-1}}^K Ad_g Res_{g^{-1}Kg∩H}^H. Mackey's irreducibility criterion tells when an induced representation is irreducible. Clifford's theorem describes the structure of representations of a group restricted to a normal subgroup — they break into orbits under conjugation.
Induction builds global representations from local data — the representation theory of a group is determined by its subgroups.
Part 3

🔬 Lie Groups and Their Representations

Continuous Symmetry
Representations of Compact Lie Groups — The Highest Weight Classification
For a compact Lie group G, every continuous representation is unitarizable and completely reducible (Weyl). The Peter-Weyl theorem: L²(G) ≅ ⊕_π (V_π ⊗ V_π^*) where π runs over irreducible representations — a non-commutative Fourier transform. Irreducible representations of compact Lie groups are classified by highest weights (Cartan-Weyl theory). Pick a maximal torus T ≅ U(1)^r; the root system tells how the Lie algebra decomposes under T. The Weyl character formula gives characters of irreps as alternating sums over the Weyl group. For SU(2), irreps are V_n (spin n/2) of dimension n+1. The Borel-Weil-Bott theorem realizes irreps as cohomology groups of line bundles on the flag variety G/B — a geometric construction. Tensor products decompose via the Clebsch-Gordan (SU(2)) or Littlewood-Richardson (GL(n)) rules.
Highest weight theory classifies all symmetries of the continuous world — from SU(2) spin to the exceptional groups E₈.
Part 4

🔗 Quivers, Category O, and Geometric Representation Theory

Modern Directions
Quiver Representations, BGG Category O, and Springer Theory
A quiver is a directed graph. A quiver representation assigns a vector space to each vertex and a linear map to each arrow. Gabriel's theorem: a quiver has finite representation type iff its underlying graph is a Dynkin diagram (A_n, D_n, E₆, E₇, E₈). Representations of the preprojective algebra of a Dynkin quiver correspond to the nilpotent cone. The BGG category O (Bernstein-Gelfand-Gelfand) is the category of representations of a semisimple Lie algebra with certain finiteness conditions — it has a highest weight theory, Kazhdan-Lusztig polynomials, and Koszul duality. Geometric representation theory studies representations via varieties: the Springer resolution T*(G/B) → N gives a geometric construction of Weyl group representations on the cohomology of Springer fibers. The Lusztig conjecture (now theorem) expresses characters of simple modules in positive characteristic via Kazhdan-Lusztig polynomials, connecting to the Kac-Moody and affine Lie algebra representations.
Modern representation theory is a vast web connecting algebra, geometry, and combinatorics — from quivers to Springer theory to the geometric Langlands program.

Modular Representation Theory and Brauer Characters

When the characteristic of the ground field divides the order of a finite group, Maschke’s theorem fails and representations need not be completely reducible. Modular representation theory studies representations over a field k of characteristic p > 0 with p | |G|. The category of finitely generated kG‑modules is a Frobenius category: projective modules coincide with injective ones. The Brauer character of a kG‑module is defined on p‑regular elements (those whose order is coprime to p) by lifting eigenvalues to characteristic zero roots of unity. Brauer characters are class functions on the set of p‑regular classes and satisfy orthogonality relations analogous to the ordinary case. The decomposition matrix records the multiplicities of simple modules in the reduction modulo p of ordinary irreducible characters; its rows are indexed by ordinary irreducibles and columns by Brauer irreducibles. Blocks of the group algebra are the indecomposable two‑sided ideals; each block has a defect group, a p‑subgroup controlling the complexity of the block. Brauer’s three main theorems relate blocks of G to blocks of normalizers of p‑subgroups, providing a powerful inductive tool. The Alperin weight conjecture and the Broué abelian defect group conjecture remain central open problems guiding current research.

Representation Theory of Finite Groups of Lie Type

Finite groups of Lie type arise as fixed points of Frobenius endomorphisms on connected reductive algebraic groups over an algebraic closure of a finite field. Their representation theory blends the highest‑weight theory of the ambient algebraic group with the combinatorics of the Weyl group and the action of the Frobenius map. Deligne–Lusztig theory constructs virtual representations by taking the alternating sum of ℓ‑adic cohomology groups of certain varieties (Deligne–Lusztig varieties) associated to the Weyl group. The virtual characters RTG(θ) are parametrized by pairs consisting of a Frobenius‑stable maximal torus T and an irreducible character θ of T, up to rational conjugacy. Lusztig’s classification organizes all irreducible representations into series indexed by semisimple conjugacy classes in the dual group; unipotent characters (those appearing in the cohomology of the flag variety) form the principal series. The Jordan decomposition of characters separates the semisimple and unipotent parts, reducing the problem to the study of unipotent characters of centralizers of semisimple elements. Harish‑Chandra induction and restriction functors provide a categorical framework that mirrors parabolic induction for the algebraic group. The generic character table, expressed in terms of Green functions, is polynomial in the field size q, enabling uniform formulas for character values across the whole family.

Worked Example: Computing a Brauer Character
Let G = S₃ and p = 3. The 3‑regular classes are the identity and the transpositions. The ordinary character table of S₃ has irreducibles χ₁ (trivial), χ₂ (sign), χ₃ (standard of degree 2). Reduce modulo 3 and compute the Brauer characters of the simple kG‑modules.
Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic 3, the group algebra kS₃ has two simple modules: the trivial module and the sign module (both 1‑dimensional). The standard representation remains indecomposable but not simple; its composition factors are the two 1‑dimensional simples. The Brauer character φ₁ of the trivial module takes value 1 on both 3‑regular classes. The Brauer character φ₂ of the sign module takes value 1 on the identity and −1 on transpositions. These two Brauer characters are orthonormal with respect to the inner product (1/|G|₃′) Σ φᵢ(g) φⱼ(g⁻¹) where the sum runs over 3‑regular elements and |G|₃′ = 2. The decomposition matrix is [[1,0],[0,1],[1,1]] reflecting that the ordinary standard character reduces to the sum of the two Brauer irreducibles.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the number of simple modules in characteristic p over an algebraically closed field, first list the p‑regular conjugacy classes; their number equals the number of simple modules. Then use the decomposition matrix to relate ordinary characters to Brauer characters. Memorize the Brauer character tables for S₃, S₄, and A₅ as they appear frequently in problem sets.

Tensor Categories and Fusion Rules

A tensor category is a rigid monoidal category; when equipped with a compatible braiding (or symmetry) it becomes a braided (or symmetric) tensor category. In representation theory, the category Rep(G) of finite‑dimensional representations of a group (or quantum group) is a pivotal example. The Grothendieck ring of a semisimple tensor category is a based ring whose structure constants are the fusion rules Nijk = dim Hom(Xk, Xi ⊗ Xj). For a finite group, these are the ordinary tensor product multiplicities; for a quantum group at a root of unity, they are truncated by the level. Modular tensor categories arise from representations of affine Lie algebras at positive integral level and from quantum groups at roots of unity; they carry a modular S‑matrix and T‑matrix satisfying the Verlinde formula Nijk = Σl Sil Sjl Skl* / S0l. The Drinfeld center of a fusion category yields a modular tensor category, providing a categorical interpretation of the quantum double construction. Ocneanu rigidity states that a fusion category has only finitely many module categories up to equivalence, a deep finiteness result with applications to subfactor theory and topological quantum field theory.

Geometric Satake Correspondence

The geometric Satake equivalence, proved by Ginzburg, Mirković–Vilonen, and others, identifies the category of representations of the Langlands dual group LG of a reductive group G over ℂ with the category of G(𝒪)‑equivariant perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian GrG = G(𝒦)/G(𝒪), where 𝒦 = ℂ((t)) and 𝒪 = ℂ[[t]]. The convolution product of perverse sheaves corresponds to the tensor product of representations, and the fiber functor at the base point recovers the underlying vector space. This equivalence is a geometric incarnation of the classical Satake isomorphism for spherical Hecke algebras. It provides a powerful tool for constructing tensor functors, giving geometric insight into the Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture for affine Kac–Moody algebras, and establishing the local geometric Langlands correspondence for GLn. The affine Grassmannian stratifies into Schubert varieties Grλ indexed by dominant coweights λ; the intersection cohomology complexes ICλ correspond to the irreducible representations Vλ of LG. The weight spaces of Vλ are realized as the stalks of ICλ at the T‑fixed points, giving a geometric interpretation of the weight multiplicity formula.

Representation Theory of Affine Kac–Moody Algebras

Affine Kac–Moody algebras are central extensions of loop algebras 𝔤 ⊗ ℂ[t, t⁻¹] ⊕ ℂc ⊕ ℂd, where 𝔤 is a finite‑dimensional simple Lie algebra. Their integrable highest‑weight modules at a fixed positive integer level k are classified by dominant integral weights of the affine root system lying in the level‑k alcove. The characters of these modules are given by the Weyl–Kac character formula, which expresses them as a ratio of theta functions and the denominator identity. The modular transformation properties of these characters under SL₂(ℤ) lead to the Verlinde formula for fusion rules of the associated conformal field theory. The category of integrable highest‑weight modules at level k is a modular tensor category; its simple objects correspond to the affine weights in the alcove. The Sugawara construction embeds the Virasoro algebra into the universal enveloping algebra, giving each module a conformal weight. The Kazhdan–Lusztig equivalence relates this category to the category of representations of the quantum group Uq(𝔤) at a root of unity q = eπ i / (k+h∨). This deep connection underlies the geometric Langlands program and the theory of vertex operator algebras.

  • Confusing the number of p‑regular classes with the number of ordinary irreducible characters.
  • Assuming that every block of defect zero is a matrix algebra over the ground field; over a non-algebraically closed field, a defect-zero block is a simple algebra (a matrix algebra over a division algebra) with a unique simple module up to isomorphism.
  • Misapplying the Verlinde formula by using the wrong modular S‑matrix (e.g., using the finite group S‑matrix instead of the affine one).
  • Overlooking the role of the Frobenius morphism in Deligne–Lusztig induction; the virtual character depends on the choice of Frobenius‑stable maximal torus.
  • Confusing the abelian category of perverse sheaves with its derived category; the geometric Satake equivalence is an equivalence of abelian categories.
  • Forgetting that the level k must be a positive integer for integrable highest‑weight modules of affine Kac–Moody algebras; negative or non‑integral levels give non‑integrable or non‑unitary representations.
  • Always verify that the characteristic of the field divides the group order before invoking modular representation theory; otherwise ordinary character theory applies.

Representation Theory of Symmetric Groups and Hecke Algebras

The irreducible complex representations of the symmetric group S_n are indexed by partitions λ of n. The Specht module S^λ provides a concrete construction inside the group algebra ℂ[S_n] using Young symmetrizers. The dimension of S^λ equals the number of standard Young tableaux of shape λ, given by the hook‑length formula f^λ = n! / ∏c∈λ h(c). The character values χ^λ(μ) for a conjugacy class of cycle type μ are computed by the Murnaghan–Nakayama rule, a recursive rim‑hook removal algorithm that yields integer values and makes the character table of S_n completely explicit.

The Iwahori–Hecke algebra H_q(S_n) is a q‑deformation of ℂ[S_n] generated by T1,…,Tn-1 with relations (T_i−q)(T_i+1)=0 and the braid relations. When q is not a root of unity, H_q(S_n) is semisimple and its irreducible modules are labelled by the same partitions λ; the specialization q→1 recovers the Specht modules. At a primitive ℓ‑th root of unity the algebra ceases to be semisimple, and the representation theory is governed by the ℓ‑modular decomposition matrices, which coincide with the decomposition matrices of the symmetric group in characteristic ℓ via the Dipper–James–Murphy theory. The canonical basis of the Hecke algebra, constructed by Kazhdan and Lusztig, yields cellular structures that control the composition factors of Specht modules in the modular case.

Induction and restriction between S_n and Sn-1 are described by the branching rule: ResS_nSn-1 S^λ ≅ ⊕μ⊂λ S^μ where μ runs over partitions obtained by removing a single box from λ. The dual Pieri rule governs tensoring with the defining representation. These rules are encoded in the representation ring of the infinite symmetric group, which is isomorphic to the ring of symmetric functions Λ via the Frobenius characteristic map ch(χ^λ)=s_λ, the Schur function. Consequently, Littlewood–Richardson coefficients c^νλμ give both tensor product multiplicities for GL(∞) and induction multiplicities for symmetric groups.

The Okounkov–Vershik approach builds a Gelfand–Tsetlin basis for each irreducible S_n‑module by diagonalizing the chain of Jucys–Murphy elements J_k = (1 k)+(2 k)+⋯+(k-1 k). Their simultaneous eigenvalues are the contents of the boxes in a standard Young tableau, providing a combinatorial labelling of basis vectors. This construction extends to the Hecke algebra, where the Jucys–Murphy elements become the commuting elements Y_k = Tk-1⋯T1 T1⋯Tk-1, and their joint spectrum yields the seminormal form of H_q(S_n). The seminormal form makes the action of the generators explicit and is the starting point for the representation theory of affine Hecke algebras and for the construction of Macdonald polynomials.

Smooth Representations of p-adic Reductive Groups

Let F be a non‑archimedean local field with ring of integers 𝒪 and residue field of cardinality q. A smooth representation of a reductive group G(F) is a complex vector space V with a linear action such that every vector is fixed by some open compact subgroup. The category of smooth representations is abelian and has enough injectives; the compactly induced representation c-Ind_K^G σ from an open compact subgroup K and a smooth representation σ of K is a fundamental construction. When K is a maximal compact subgroup such as GL_n(𝒪), the Hecke algebra ℋ(G,K) = End_G(c-Ind_K^G 1) is commutative (the spherical Hecke algebra) and isomorphic to the algebra of bi‑K‑invariant compactly supported functions under convolution. The Satake isomorphism identifies ℋ(G,K) with the representation ring of the Langlands dual group ^L G, sending the characteristic function of K diag(πλ1,…,πλ_n) K to the character of the irreducible representation of ^L G with highest weight λ.

Bernstein’s decomposition splits the category of smooth representations into a direct product of blocks indexed by inertial equivalence classes of cuspidal pairs (M,σ), where M is a Levi subgroup and σ a cuspidal representation of M(F). Each block is equivalent to the module category of a certain affine Hecke algebra ℋ(G,M,σ). The center of the category, the Bernstein center 𝔷(G), acts by endomorphisms of the identity functor and is isomorphic to the algebra of regular functions on the variety of unramified Langlands parameters. This structure underlies the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n, proved by Harris–Taylor and Henniart, which matches irreducible smooth representations of GL_n(F) with n‑dimensional Frobenius‑semisimple Weil–Deligne representations of the Weil group W_F.

Supercuspidal representations, those not occurring as subquotients of any proper parabolic induction, are the building blocks of the Bernstein blocks. For GL_n, all supercuspidals are constructed by compact induction from maximal simple types (Bushnell–Kutzko theory). For classical groups, the construction uses covers and theta correspondences. The local Langlands correspondence for general reductive groups is still conjectural, but the endoscopic classification of Arthur provides a precise description of the discrete spectrum in terms of global automorphic representations and their local components.

The category of smooth representations admits a derived version where Ext groups compute extensions of representations. The derived Hecke algebra and the derived Satake equivalence (Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg) relate the derived category of constructible sheaves on the affine Grassmannian to the derived category of modules over the Langlands dual group, providing a geometric incarnation of the local Langlands correspondence for unramified representations.

Worked Example: Spherical Hecke Algebra for GL2(F)
Let F be a p‑adic field with uniformizer π. Compute the convolution product of the characteristic functions f1 = 1K diag(π,1) K and f2 = 1K diag(π,π) K in the spherical Hecke algebra ℋ(GL2(F),K) where K = GL2(𝒪). Express the result as a linear combination of the basis {1K diag(π^a,π^b) K : a≥b}.
The double coset K diag(π,1) K consists of matrices with valuation pattern (1,0). The product f1 * f2 corresponds to the convolution of the two double cosets. Using the Satake isomorphism, the basis element T(a,b) maps to the symmetric polynomial X^a Y^b + X^b Y^a in the representation ring of GL2(ℂ). For (1,0) we have X+Y; for (1,1) we have XY. Their product is (X+Y)·XY = X2Y + XY2, which corresponds to the sum of the double cosets with valuation patterns (2,1) and (1,2). Since the Hecke algebra basis is ordered with a≥b, the result is f1 * f2 = 1K diag(π2,π) K + 1K diag(π,π2) K. In the standard basis this is T(2,1) + T(1,2). Because T(1,2) = T(2,1) after ordering, the coefficient is 2·T(2,1). However the Satake isomorphism uses the symmetrized basis, so the correct expression in the usual Hecke basis is T(2,1) + q·T(1,1) where q is the residue cardinality. The precise coefficient q arises from the volume of the intersection of the double cosets; the final answer is f1 * f2 = T(2,1) + q·T(1,1).
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the structure of a spherical Hecke algebra, first identify the maximal compact subgroup and the Satake isomorphism. Write the generators as characteristic functions of double cosets indexed by dominant coweights. Use the known isomorphism to the representation ring of the dual group to translate convolution into multiplication of symmetric polynomials. This avoids messy coset counting and yields the answer in terms of the standard basis quickly.

Automorphic Forms and the Langlands Correspondence for GL_n

An automorphic representation of GL_n(𝔸_F) over a number field F is an irreducible constituent of the space of square‑integrable functions on GL_n(F)GL_n(𝔸_F) that is smooth, admissible, and of moderate growth. By Flath’s tensor product theorem, any such representation π decomposes as a restricted tensor product ⊗'_v π_v of irreducible smooth representations of GL_n(F_v) at all places v. The local components π_v are unramified for almost all finite places, meaning they contain a non‑zero vector fixed by GL_n(𝒪_v). The unramified local Langlands correspondence attaches to π_v a semisimple conjugacy class s_v in GL_n(ℂ) (the Satake parameter) such that the Hecke eigenvalues of π_v equal the eigenvalues of s_v on the standard representation.

The local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over p-adic fields, proved by Harris–Taylor and Henniart (and independently by Scholze), matches irreducible smooth representations of GL_n(F) with n-dimensional Frobenius-semisimple Weil–Deligne representations. The global Langlands correspondence for GL_n over a number field F—which would attach to each irreducible cuspidal automorphic representation π of GL_n(𝔸_F) an n-dimensional Galois representation ρ_π : Gal(𝐹̄/F) → GL_n(ℂ) with compatible local parameters—is known for n=1,2 but remains conjectural in general. When both correspondences hold, the Satake parameter s_v at an unramified place v equals the Frobenius conjugacy class ρ_π(Frob_v). The L‑function L(s,π) defined by the Euler product of local L‑factors coincides with the Artin L‑function L(s,ρ_π) where the global correspondence is known. The functional equation and analytic continuation of L(s,π) follow from the theory of Eisenstein series and the Langlands–Shahidi method.

Functoriality predicts that for any homomorphism of L‑groups ^L H → ^L G, there is a transfer of automorphic representations from H to G. For GL_n, the most important instances are the symmetric power lifts Sym^m : GL2 → GLm+1 and the Rankin–Selberg convolution GL_n × GL_m → GLnm. The existence of these lifts is known in many cases (Gelbart–Jacquet for Sym2, Kim–Shahidi for Sym3, Sym4) and is a central tool in proving cases of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture and in the proof of the Sato–Tate conjecture for modular forms.

The trace formula, both Arthur’s invariant trace formula and the simpler Selberg trace formula for GL2, provides a spectral expansion of the distribution given by integrating a test function against the kernel of the regular representation. The geometric side involves orbital integrals over conjugacy classes, while the spectral side sums over automorphic representations weighted by their characters. Stabilization of the trace formula, achieved by endoscopic transfer, isolates the contributions of representations that correspond to a given Langlands parameter, thereby giving a precise spectral decomposition of L2(G(F)G(𝔸_F)).

Categorical Representation Theory: 2-Representations and Higher Structures

Classical representation theory studies functors from a group (or algebra) to Vect. Categorical representation theory replaces Vect by a higher category such as Cat (the 2‑category of categories, functors, and natural transformations) or a monoidal category 𝒞. A 2‑representation of a monoidal category 𝒞 is a monoidal functor 𝒞 → End(𝒟) for some category 𝒟, often taken to be a module category over 𝒞. When 𝒞 is the category of representations of a Hopf algebra H, a module category over Rep(H) is equivalent to the category of modules over an algebra object in Rep(H), i.e. an H‑module algebra. This recovers the classical notion of a Hopf module.

For a finite group G, the 2‑category of G‑graded vector spaces Vec^G has simple module categories classified by pairs (H,ω) where H ≤ G and ω ∈ H3(H,ℂ^×). The corresponding module categories are the categories of H‑graded vector spaces with associator twisted by ω. This classification, due to Ostrik, is the categorical analogue of the classification of transitive G‑sets. For a fusion category 𝒞, the Morita equivalence classes of indecomposable module categories correspond to the connected étale algebras in the Drinfeld center Z(𝒞). The number of such classes is finite by Ocneanu rigidity.

In the setting of Lie algebras, the category 𝒪 for a semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤 admits a natural action of the monoidal category of projective functors (translation functors). This action categorifies the action of the Weyl group on the weight lattice and lifts the Kazhdan–Lusztig basis of the Hecke algebra to a basis of the Grothendieck group of 𝒪. The 2‑representation theory of the Kac–Moody 2‑category (Rouquier’s categorification of quantum groups) provides a categorical framework for the representation theory of quantum groups at generic q and at roots of unity. The 2‑category has objects given by weights, 1‑morphisms given by induction and restriction functors, and 2‑morphisms given by natural transformations satisfying the categorified quantum Serre relations.

Higher representation theory also appears in geometric Langlands, where the category of D‑modules on the moduli stack of G‑bundles on a curve carries an action of the monoidal category of representations of the Langlands dual group ^L G via Hecke operators. The geometric Satake equivalence identifies this monoidal category with the category of perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian, and the Hecke action categorifies the classical Satake isomorphism. The resulting 2‑representation is expected to encode the full local Langlands correspondence in a categorical form.

Representations of Rational Cherednik Algebras

The rational Cherednik algebra H_c(W,𝔥) associated to a complex reflection group W acting on a vector space 𝔥 depends on a parameter c ∈ ℂConj(W). It is generated by 𝔥, 𝔥^*, and the group algebra ℂ[W] with relations [y,x] = ⟨y,x⟩ − ∑s∈𝒮 c_s ⟨y,α_s⟩⟨α_s^∨,x⟩ s, where 𝒮 is the set of reflections, α_s a root, and α_s^∨ a coroot. For W = S_n acting on 𝔥 = ℂ^n with the standard reflection representation, H_c(S_n,𝔥) is the spherical subalgebra of the double affine Hecke algebra of type An-1 at t=1.

The category 𝒪_c of H_c‑modules that are locally nilpotent for the action of 𝔥 and finitely generated over the polynomial subalgebra ℂ[𝔥] is a highest‑weight category with standard modules Δ_c(λ) = H_c ⊗ℂ[𝔥]⋊W L_λ, where L_λ are irreducible W‑modules. The simple modules L_c(λ) are the unique simple quotients of Δ_c(λ). The parameter c is called integral if c_s ∈ ℤ for all s; in this case the category 𝒪_c is semisimple and equivalent to the category of W‑equivariant D‑modules on 𝔥reg. For non‑integral c, the blocks of 𝒪_c are controlled by the KZ functor, which sends 𝒪_c to the category of modules over the Hecke algebra H_q(W) with q = exp(2πi c). The KZ functor is exact and induces an equivalence between the quotient of 𝒪_c by the subcategory of modules supported on the reflection hyperplanes and the category of finite‑dimensional H_q(W)‑modules.

The representation theory of H_c(S_n,𝔥) is deeply connected to the combinatorics of Macdonald polynomials. The Dunkl operators y_i = ∂/∂x_i + c ∑j≠i (1−sij)/(x_i−x_j) provide a faithful representation of H_c on ℂ[x1,…,x_n]. The joint eigenfunctions of the Dunkl operators are the non‑symmetric Jack polynomials, which specialize to the non‑symmetric Macdonald polynomials when the trigonometric Cherednik algebra is considered. The symmetric Jack polynomials are the images of the non‑symmetric ones under the symmetrization operator, and they diagonalize the Calogero–Moser Hamiltonian H = ∑ ∂2/∂x_i2 + c(c−1) ∑i<j 1/(x_i−x_j)2.

The Calogero–Moser space ℳ_c = Spec Z(H_c) is a Poisson deformation of the quotient 𝔥/W. Its symplectic leaves are indexed by the partitions of n, and the fibers of the Hilbert–Chow morphism ℳ_c → 𝔥/W are the irreducible components of the nilpotent cone in the associated graded algebra. The representation theory of H_c at rational parameters c = r/m with (r,m)=1 is governed by the category 𝒪_c, whose blocks correspond to the m‑cores of partitions. The Broué–Malle–Rouquier conjecture predicts that the derived category of 𝒪_c is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on the corresponding Calogero–Moser space, a statement known as the derived McKay correspondence for rational Cherednik algebras.

  • Assuming that every smooth representation of a p‑adic group is admissible; admissibility holds for irreducible representations but not for arbitrary smooth representations.
  • Misidentifying the Satake parameter of an unramified representation with the Frobenius eigenvalue on the associated Galois representation without checking the normalization of the local Langlands correspondence.
  • Overlooking the distinction between the Bernstein center and the center of the Hecke algebra; the former acts on the whole category, the latter only on the spherical subcategory.
  • Treating the KZ functor as an equivalence of the whole category 𝒪_c rather than an equivalence after quotienting by the subcategory of modules supported on the reflection hyperplanes.
  • Confusing the parameter c of the rational Cherednik algebra with the parameter q of the Hecke algebra; they are related by q = exp(2πi c) but the categories are not equivalent for all c.
  • Assuming that the Calogero–Moser space is smooth for all parameters; it is smooth only for generic c and acquires singularities at special rational values.

These advanced topics illustrate how representation theory extends from finite groups to p‑adic groups, automorphic forms, higher categorical structures, and deformation algebras, each layer providing new tools for the Langlands program and mathematical physics.

Quantum Groups at Roots of Unity and Modular Tensor Categories

Let Uq(𝔤) be the Drinfeld–Jimbo quantum group associated to a simple Lie algebra 𝔤 over ℂ, with deformation parameter q a primitive ℓ‑th root of unity (ℓ odd and coprime to the Coxeter number). At such roots the Hopf algebra acquires a large centre generated by the ℓ‑th powers of the Chevalley generators; the finite‑dimensional representation theory splits into blocks indexed by the affine Weyl group at level ℓ−h∨. Simple modules are parametrised by dominant weights λ in the fundamental alcove Cℓ = { λ ∈ P+ | ⟨λ+ρ, θ⟩ ≤ ℓ−h∨ }, where θ is the highest root and ρ the half‑sum of positive roots. The semisimplification of the category of tilting modules yields a braided tensor category that is modular: it is semisimple, has a non‑degenerate braiding, and its simple objects correspond to the integrable highest‑weight modules of the affine Kac–Moody algebra ĝ at level k = ℓ−h∨. In this modular tensor category, the tensor product of simples decomposes according to the Verlinde formula, with fusion coefficients given by the modular S‑matrix of the associated Wess–Zumino–Witten conformal field theory. The modular data (S, T matrices) satisfy the Verlinde algebra isomorphism K0(Rep) ⊗ ℂ ≅ ℂ[Λk], where Λk is the set of level‑k dominant weights. This equivalence underlies the construction of 3‑manifold invariants (Reshetikhin–Turaev) and provides a categorical framework for topological quantum computation. The Lusztig quantum Frobenius morphism Fr : Uq(𝔤) → U(𝔤) relates the root‑of‑unity quantum group to the universal enveloping algebra of 𝔤, inducing a functor from the modular category to the category of tilting modules for the algebraic group G in characteristic ℓ. The kernel of Fr is the small quantum group uq(𝔤), whose representation category is a finite tensor category that is not semisimple but carries a pivotal structure; its projective covers give rise to the logarithmic extensions of the modular theory.

Derived Equivalences and Tilting Theory in Representation Theory

For a finite‑dimensional algebra A over an algebraically closed field, the bounded derived category Db(mod‑A) encodes the homological algebra of A‑modules. Two algebras A and B are derived equivalent if there exists an exact equivalence F : Db(mod‑A) → Db(mod‑B) commuting with the shift functor. Rickard’s theorem characterises derived equivalences by a tilting complex T in Kb(proj‑A) with endomorphism algebra B that generates the derived category. A tilting module T over A is a finitely generated module with projective dimension ≤ 1, Ext1A(T,T)=0, and an exact sequence 0 → A → T0 → T1 → 0 with Ti ∈ add(T). The Brenner–Butler theorem shows that tilting modules induce torsion pairs (𝒯, ℱ) in mod‑A and (𝒳, 𝒴) in mod‑B, yielding equivalences between the hearts of the associated t‑structures. In the context of Lie theory, the category of finite‑dimensional modules for a semisimple algebraic group G in characteristic p admits a tilting theory developed by Donkin and Ringel: indecomposable tilting modules T(λ) are indexed by dominant weights λ and have both a Weyl filtration and a good filtration. The Ringel duality functor sends the category of G‑modules with a good filtration to the category of modules for the Schur algebra S(n,d), establishing a derived equivalence between the two. For quantum groups at a root of unity, Andersen’s tilting modules provide a bridge to the modular tensor category: the semisimplification of the tilting category at level ℓ recovers the modular category of Uq(𝔤). Derived equivalences also appear in geometric representation theory via the Beilinson–Bernstein localisation: the derived category of D‑modules on the flag variety G/B is equivalent to the derived category of modules for the enveloping algebra U(𝔤) with a fixed central character. This equivalence respects t‑structures and sends Verma modules to standard D‑modules, intertwining the Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture with the geometry of Schubert varieties.

Worked Example: Tilting Module for SL2 in Characteristic 2
Let G = SL2 over an algebraically closed field k of characteristic 2. Determine the indecomposable tilting module T(2) for the dominant weight 2 (i.e. the 3‑dimensional simple module in characteristic 0).
In characteristic 2 the simple module L(2) has dimension 2 (the Steinberg module). The Weyl module Δ(2) has dimension 3 with composition factors L(2) (head) and L(0) (socle). The indecomposable tilting module T(2) must have both a Weyl filtration and a good filtration. Since Ext1(Δ(2),Δ(0)) ≠ 0, the unique non‑split extension yields a module of dimension 4 with filtration Δ(2) on top and Δ(0) at bottom. Dually, the good filtration forces the socle to be L(2) and the head L(0). Hence T(2) is the 4‑dimensional module with Loewy structure L(2) | L(0) ⊕ L(0) | L(2). Its character is ch T(2) = ch Δ(2) + ch Δ(0) = (e2+1+e−2) + 1, confirming the tilting property.
Exam Strategy: Recognising Tilting Patterns
When a question asks for the structure of a tilting module, first write down the Weyl and simple modules for the given weight and characteristic. The dimension of T(λ) is often the sum of dimensions of Δ(μ) for μ in the same linkage class; compute this sum quickly using the Jantzen sum formula.

Representation Theory of Vertex Operator Algebras and Conformal Field Theory

A vertex operator algebra (VOA) V is a ℤ‑graded vector space V = ⊕n∈ℤ Vn equipped with a vacuum vector 1 ∈ V0, a conformal vector ω ∈ V2, and a vertex operator map Y(·,z) : V → (End V)[[z,z−1]] satisfying locality, associativity, and the Virasoro relations [Lm, Ln] = (m−n)Lm+n + (c/12)(m3−m)δm+n,0. The central charge c is a key invariant. Modules over V are graded vector spaces M = ⊕ Mn with a vertex operator YM(·,z) satisfying the same axioms. The category of V‑modules carries a braided tensor structure constructed by Huang and Lepowsky using the geometry of spheres with punctures; the tensor product is defined via intertwining operators of type (M1 M2 M3). For a rational VOA (finitely many simple modules, each C2-cofinite), the module category is a modular tensor category: it is semisimple, has a non‑degenerate S‑matrix given by the characters χM(τ) = TrM qL0−c/24, and satisfies the Verlinde formula for fusion rules. The moonshine module V♮ constructed by Frenkel–Lepowsky–Meurman has central charge 24 and its automorphism group is the Monster simple group; its irreducible modules correspond to the conjugacy classes of the Monster, realising Monstrous Moonshine. In the logarithmic setting (e.g. the triplet VOA W(p) at central charge c = 1−6(p−1)2/p), the module category is not semisimple but carries a ribbon structure; projective covers of simple modules yield logarithmic conformal field theories with indecomposable Virasoro representations. The Zhu algebra A(V) = V/O(V) (where O(V) is spanned by Resz Y(a,z) b z−1) controls the classification of ordinary modules: simple A(V)-modules correspond bijectively to simple V‑modules. For affine VOAs Vk(𝔤) associated to a simple Lie algebra 𝔤 at level k, the simple modules are the integrable highest‑weight modules of the affine Kac–Moody algebra ĝ at level k, and the fusion rules coincide with those of the modular tensor category from quantum groups at a root of unity (the Kazhdan–Lusztig correspondence).

Categorical Actions of Kac–Moody Algebras via Khovanov–Lauda–Rouquier Algebras

Let 𝔤 be a symmetrisable Kac–Moody algebra with Cartan datum (I,·). The Khovanov–Lauda–Rouquier (KLR) algebra Rν (ν ∈ ℕ[I]) is a graded algebra defined by generators e(𝐢) (idempotents indexed by sequences 𝐢 of length ht(ν)), yr (degree 2), and ψr (degree −air,ir+1), subject to quadratic and braid relations encoding the Cartan matrix. The direct sum R = ⊕ν Rν categorifies the negative half Uq−(𝔤) of the quantum group: the Grothendieck group K0(R‑proj) ≅ Uq−(𝔤) as ℤ[q,q−1]-algebras, with the classes of indecomposable projective modules mapping to the canonical basis of Lusztig. The cyclotomic quotient RΛ = R / ⟨ y1⟨hi1,Λ⟩⟩ e(𝐢) ⟩ categorifies the highest‑weight module V(Λ); its graded projective modules give a categorification of the crystal basis B(Λ). The 2‑category 𝒰(𝔤) of Rouquier has objects weights λ ∈ P, 1‑morphisms given by induction/restriction functors ℰi, ℱi between module categories of cyclotomic KLR algebras, and 2‑morphisms given by the KLR diagrammatic calculus. This 2‑category provides a categorical action of 𝔤 on the direct sum of module categories ⊕λ Rλ‑mod, lifting the usual action on weight spaces. The categorified quantum group yields a braided monoidal structure on the category of finite‑dimensional graded R‑modules, which after semisimplification recovers the modular tensor category of Uq(𝔤) at a root of unity. For affine type A, the KLR algebras are isomorphic to the affine Hecke algebras at parameter q = 0, linking the theory to the representation theory of p‑adic GLn via the categorical Langlands programme. The derived equivalence between the category of modules for the cyclotomic KLR algebra and the category of perverse sheaves on the quiver variety (Varagnolo–Vasserot) realises the geometric Satake correspondence in a purely algebraic setting.

Geometric Langlands Correspondence for GLn and D-modules on the Moduli Stack of Bundles

Let X be a smooth projective curve over ℂ and Bunn the moduli stack of rank‑n vector bundles on X. The geometric Langlands conjecture predicts an equivalence between the derived category of D‑modules on Bunn (with a fixed central character) and the derived category of quasi‑coherent sheaves on the moduli stack LocSysn of rank‑n local systems (i.e. flat connections) on X. The Hecke operators Hx for x ∈ X act on D‑modules on Bunn by modifying the bundle at x; they generate a monoidal category equivalent to the category of representations of the Langlands dual group LG = GLn(ℂ). The spectral side is constructed via the Hitchin fibration h : T*Bunn → 𝒜, where 𝒜 = ⊕i=1n H0(X, Ki) is the Hitchin base. The generic fibre is an abelian variety (the Prym variety of the spectral curve), and the support of a Hecke eigensheaf is a Lagrangian subvariety of T*Bunn projecting isomorphically onto 𝒜. The fundamental result of Laumon (n=2) and Drinfeld–Gaitsgory (general n) establishes the correspondence for unramified local systems: there is a fully faithful functor from the category of D‑modules on Bunn with nilpotent singular support to the category of ind‑coherent sheaves on LocSysn. The ramified case introduces parabolic structures at marked points; the corresponding Hecke categories are categorified by the affine Grassmannian GrG and the geometric Satake equivalence identifies the spherical Hecke category with Rep(LG). The categorical Langlands programme replaces D‑modules by ind‑coherent sheaves and uses the theory of ∞‑categories to formulate a precise equivalence of symmetric monoidal ∞‑categories. Recent work of Gaitsgory–Lurie proves the conjecture for GL1 and outlines a strategy for general n via the study of the Whittaker category and the Eisenstein functor. The correspondence has profound implications for the classical Langlands programme: the trace of Frobenius on the ℓ‑adic cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas recovers the automorphic L‑functions, and the geometric Langlands equivalence provides a geometric interpretation of the functional equation.

Representation Theory of Quantum Affine Algebras and Crystal Bases

Quantum affine algebras U_q(widehat{g}) are Hopf algebra deformations of the universal enveloping algebras of affine Kac–Moody algebras. They were introduced by Drinfeld and Jimbo in the mid‑1980s as the algebraic backbone of the quantum inverse scattering method. The representation theory splits naturally into two families: highest‑weight integrable modules, classified by dominant integral weights of the underlying finite‑type Lie algebra g, and evaluation modules obtained by pulling back finite‑dimensional U_q(g)‑modules through the evaluation homomorphism operatorname{ev}_z : U_q(widehat{g}) → U_q(g) parameterised by a non‑zero complex number z. The tensor product of evaluation modules is governed by the R‑matrix, which satisfies the quantum Yang–Baxter equation and yields braid group representations.

A combinatorial breakthrough came with Kashiwara’s crystal bases (1990). For each integrable highest‑weight module V(λ) there exists a crystal graph B(λ) whose vertices are a basis at q=0 and whose edges are given by Kashiwara operators tilde{e}_i,tilde{f}_i. The crystal graph encodes the weight multiplicities and the decomposition of tensor products via the tensor product rule B(λ)⊗ B(μ) ≅ bigoplus_nu B(nu)⊕ cλμ^nu. The combinatorics of crystals for type A_n(1) reproduces the theory of Young tableaux and the Littlewood–Richardson rule; for other types it provides explicit models such as the Kyoto path model or the Littelmann path model. Crystals also admit a geometric realisation as the set of irreducible components of certain quiver varieties (Nakajima, 1998), linking the representation theory of quantum affine algebras to the geometry of moduli spaces of representations of preprojective algebras.

Finite‑dimensional representations of U_q(widehat{g}) are not all highest‑weight. The classification of simple finite‑dimensional modules was achieved by Chari and Pressley (1994) using Drinfeld polynomials. Each simple module is uniquely determined by an n-tuple of polynomials P_i(u) (one for each node of the Dynkin diagram) satisfying certain compatibility conditions. The category of finite‑dimensional modules is a braided monoidal category but not semisimple; its Grothendieck ring carries a quantum cluster algebra structure introduced by Hernandez and Leclerc (2010). This cluster structure explains the appearance of T‑systems and Y‑systems in the thermodynamic Bethe ansatz and connects to the theory of cluster algebras of Fomin and Zelevinsky.

Applications range from solvable lattice models (the six‑vertex model, the XXZ spin chain) to the geometric Langlands programme, where quantum affine algebras appear as the algebra of global sections of the sheaf of chiral differential operators on the affine Grassmannian. Recent work of Braverman, Finkelberg, and Nakajima (2016) constructs a categorical action of U_q(widehat{g}) on the derived category of coherent sheaves on quiver varieties, providing a geometric categorification of the crystal basis.

Cluster Algebras and Their Representation-Theoretic Applications

Cluster algebras were introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky in 2002 to provide an algebraic framework for total positivity and canonical bases in Lie theory. A cluster algebra A is a subalgebra of a field of rational functions generated by a set of distinguished generators called cluster variables, grouped into overlapping clusters. Mutation of clusters is an involutive transformation that replaces one cluster variable by a new one determined by an exchange relation. The combinatorics of mutations is encoded by a quiver (or a valued quiver) whose vertices correspond to cluster variables and whose arrows encode the exchange polynomials.

The representation‑theoretic incarnation of cluster algebras comes from the categorification theorem of Buan, Marsh, Reineke, Reiten, and Todorov (2006). For an acyclic quiver Q, the cluster category C_Q is the orbit category of the bounded derived category D^b(operatorname{mod}kQ) under the functor τ-1[1]. Its indecomposable objects correspond bijectively to cluster variables, and clusters correspond to maximal rigid objects. The Caldero–Chapoton formula expresses each cluster variable as the Euler characteristic of a quiver Grassmannian, giving a geometric interpretation of the Laurent phenomenon. This categorification extends to arbitrary quivers with potential via the work of Amiot (2009) and to surfaces via the tagged triangulations of Fomin, Shapiro, and Thurston (2008).

In the representation theory of quantum groups, cluster structures appear on the coordinate rings of unipotent cells N(w) and on the algebras of functions on double Bruhat cells Gu,v. Geiß, Leclerc, and Schröer (2011) proved that the quantum coordinate ring O_q(N(w)) carries a quantum cluster algebra structure whose initial seed is given by the dual canonical basis. The same authors showed that the category of finite‑dimensional modules over a quantum affine algebra U_q(widehat{g}) admits a monoidal categorification by the category of modules over a preprojective algebra, and the Grothendieck ring of this monoidal category is a cluster algebra. This result explains the appearance of T‑systems and Y‑systems as mutation sequences in the associated cluster algebra.

Cluster algebras also govern the wall‑crossing phenomena for Donaldson–Thomas invariants of 3‑dimensional Calabi–Yau categories. The Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula can be reformulated as a sequence of mutations in a cluster algebra attached to the stability conditions. In the context of the geometric Langlands programme, the cluster structure on the moduli space of local systems on a surface (the Fock–Goncharov cluster ensemble) provides a combinatorial model for the space of opers and for the spectral side of the correspondence.

Worked Example: Mutation in a Rank‑2 Cluster Algebra
Consider the cluster algebra of rank 2 with initial seed (x1,x2) and exchange matrix B = begin{pmatrix}0 & 2 -2 & 0end{pmatrix}. Perform mutation at vertex 1, then at vertex 2, and write the resulting cluster variables in terms of x1,x2.
Mutation at 1 replaces x1 by x1' = (x22+1)/(x1). The new exchange matrix is -B. Mutation at 2 replaces x2 by x2' = ((x1')2+1)/(x2) = ((x22+1)2/x12+1)/(x2) = (x24+2x22+1+x12)/(x12 x2). The final cluster is (x1',x2'). This illustrates the Laurent phenomenon: each cluster variable is a Laurent polynomial in the initial variables with integer coefficients.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the effect of a sequence of mutations, draw the quiver and track the exchange matrix sign changes. Remember that mutation is an involution: mutating twice at the same vertex returns the original seed. Use the Laurent phenomenon to check that your final expression is a Laurent polynomial in the initial cluster variables; any polynomial with negative powers signals an algebraic error.

Representation Theory of Lie Superalgebras and Supergroups

Lie superalgebras g = g0 ⊕ g1 are Z2-graded generalisations of Lie algebras where the bracket satisfies graded symmetry and the graded Jacobi identity. The basic classical simple Lie superalgebras over C were classified by Kac (1977) into the series sl(m|n), osp(m|2n), D(2,1;α), F(4), and G(3). Their representation theory diverges from the purely even case because the category of finite‑dimensional modules is not semisimple; atypical blocks appear when the highest weight satisfies a certain orthogonality condition with respect to odd isotropic roots.

Kac and Wakimoto (1994) introduced the notion of a Kac module K(λ) = operatorname{Ind}bg L0(λ), induced from a simple g0-module. Every simple finite‑dimensional g-module appears as the unique simple quotient of a Kac module. The character of a Kac module is given by a Weyl‑type denominator formula, but the characters of simple modules in atypical blocks require the solution of the Kazhdan–Lusztig problem for the corresponding Lie superalgebra. Brundan (2003) solved this for gl(m|n) by establishing an equivalence between the principal block of category O for gl(m|n) and a block of category O for a certain Lie algebra of type A, thereby transferring the known Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials.

For the orthosymplectic series osp(m|2n), the representation theory is controlled by the Brauer algebra and its quantised version. The category of finite‑dimensional representations of the quantum supergroup U_q(osp(m|2n)) is a ribbon category, and its Grothendieck ring carries a super‑version of the Verlinde formula. The super‑duality of Cheng, Wang, and Zhang (2014) relates the representation categories of gl(m|n) and gl(n|m) via a Koszul duality of the corresponding category O.

Lie supergroups such as GL(m|n) and OSp(m|2n) are representable functors on the category of supercommutative superalgebras. Their algebraic geometry is developed in the language of super‑schemes (Manin, 1988). The coordinate ring O(GL(m|n)) is a Hopf superalgebra whose finite‑dimensional comodules are precisely the finite‑dimensional representations of the Lie superalgebra gl(m|n). The cohomology of line bundles on the super‑flag variety GL(m|n)/B computes the higher Ext groups in category O and yields a super‑version of the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem.

W‑Algebras, Vertex Algebras, and the AGT Correspondence

W‑algebras are associative algebras obtained by quantum Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction from affine Kac–Moody algebras. For a simple Lie algebra g and a nilpotent element f ∈ g, the finite W‑algebra Wfin(g,f) is a filtered deformation of the Slodowy slice S_f = f + ker(operatorname{ad} f). The affine W‑algebra W^k(g,f) is a vertex algebra constructed by the BRST complex of the affine vertex algebra V^k(g) with respect to the nilpotent subalgebra generated by f. When f is principal, W^k(g,f) is the principal W‑algebra W^k(g); its representation theory is governed by the quantum Miura transformation and the Feigin–Frenkel duality.

Vertex algebras provide the algebraic framework for two‑dimensional conformal field theory. A vertex algebra V consists of a vector space equipped with a state‑field correspondence Y(a,z) = ΣninZ a(n) z-n-1 satisfying locality, associativity, and the vacuum axiom. The Zhu algebra A(V) captures the representation theory of V at genus zero; its simple modules correspond to simple V-modules. For the Virasoro vertex algebra operatorname{Vir}_c, the Zhu algebra is the universal enveloping algebra of the Virasoro Lie algebra modulo the central charge relation.

The Alday–Gaiotto–Tachikawa (AGT) correspondence (2010) relates the instanton partition function of four‑dimensional N=2 supersymmetric gauge theory on R4 (or its Ω-deformation) to conformal blocks of a two‑dimensional Liouville/Toda conformal field theory on a Riemann surface. Mathematically, it predicts an isomorphism between the equivariant cohomology of the moduli space of instantons on C2 (the Nekrasov partition function) and the space of conformal blocks of the W-algebra W(g) associated to the gauge group. The correspondence has been verified for g = sl2 by Alba, Fateev, Litvinov, and Tarnopolsky (2011) using the free‑field realisation of the Virasoro algebra, and a geometric framework for general g was developed by Braverman, Finkelberg, and Nakajima (2016) via the geometric representation theory of the affine Grassmannian and the categorical action of the quantum affine algebra.

Recent developments include the extension of AGT to Argyres–Douglas theories, where the vertex algebra is a non‑unitary W-algebra associated to a non‑principal nilpotent orbit, and the connection to the theory of irregular conformal blocks and quantum Painlevé equations. The representation theory of these W-algebras is controlled by the category O for the corresponding affine Kac–Moody algebra at admissible levels, and the fusion rules are computed by the Verlinde formula for the associated modular tensor category after semisimplification.

Real Reductive Groups: Langlands Classification and Arthur Packets

Let G be a connected real reductive algebraic group, for example GL_n(R), SL_n(R), Sp2n(R), or a real form of a complex simple group. The unitary dual of G — the set of equivalence classes of irreducible unitary representations — is a central object in harmonic analysis and the Langlands programme. Langlands (1973) classified all irreducible admissible representations of G in terms of Langlands parameters: a homomorphism φ : WR → {}^L G from the real Weil group WR to the L‑group {}^L G = widehat{G} rtimes operatorname{Gal}(C/R), modulo conjugation by widehat{G}. Each parameter determines a finite set Π_φ of irreducible admissible representations, called an L‑packet. The packets are expected to be disjoint and to exhaust the admissible dual.

The construction of the packets uses parabolic induction from tempered representations of Levi subgroups. A representation is tempered if its matrix coefficients lie in L2+ε(G) for all ε>0. The Langlands quotient theorem states that every standard module I_P^G(σ ⊗ nu) (induced from a tempered representation σ of a Levi M and a character nu in the positive Weyl chamber) has a unique irreducible quotient J_P^G(σ ⊗ nu), and every irreducible admissible representation arises uniquely in this way. The classification of tempered representations was completed by Knapp and Zuckerman (1982) using the theory of cohomological induction and the Dirac inequality.

Arthur (1989) refined the Langlands classification for the discrete spectrum of L2(G(Q)backslash G(A)) by introducing Arthur parameters psi : WR × operatorname{SL}2(C) → {}^L G. The extra operatorname{SL}2 factor encodes the unipotent part of the parameter and allows a precise description of the multiplicities in the discrete spectrum. The resulting Arthur packets Π_psi are finite sets of unitary representations that are expected to be the building blocks of the automorphic discrete spectrum. The endoscopic classification of Arthur packets for classical groups was achieved by Arthur (2013) and Mok (2015) using the stable trace formula and the fundamental lemma.

For real groups, the local Langlands correspondence is known in full generality for GL_n(R) and GL_n(C) (Langlands, 1989) and for many classical groups via the work of Vogan (1993) and Adams, Barbasch, and Vogan (1992). The correspondence matches irreducible admissible representations with Langlands parameters and predicts a bijection between the set of irreducible representations of a real group and the set of conjugacy classes of Langlands parameters, up to a finite ambiguity measured by the component group S_φ = π0(Z_{widehat{G}}(φ)). The character identities among members of an L‑packet are expressed in terms of the stable characters of endoscopic groups, providing a powerful tool for computing the Plancherel measure and the spectral side of the trace formula.

  • Treating the category of finite‑dimensional modules over a Lie superalgebra as semisimple; atypical blocks are never semisimple and require the full machinery of category O and Kazhdan–Lusztig theory.
  • Identifying the Zhu algebra of a vertex algebra with its zero‑mode algebra; the Zhu algebra is a quotient of the universal enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra of modes and is generally non‑commutative.

Representations of Infinite Discrete Groups: Amenability and Kazhdan's Property (T)

While representation theory often focuses on finite groups or Lie groups, infinite discrete groups present unique challenges. Unlike finite groups, they typically possess infinitely many non-isomorphic irreducible representations, and their unitary dual can be highly complex. Key concepts classifying these groups are amenability and Kazhdan's Property (T), defined by the behavior of their representations.

A discrete group G is amenable if it admits a left-invariant mean on the space of bounded real-valued functions. Equivalently, G satisfies the Følner condition: for every finite subset F ⊂ G and ε > 0, there exists a finite subset K ⊂ G such that |gK Δ K| / |K| < ε for all g ∈ F (Δ denotes symmetric difference). Representation-theoretically, amenability implies the trivial representation is weakly contained in the regular representation. Examples include all finite groups, abelian groups, solvable groups, and groups of subexponential growth. Free groups on two or more generators are classical non-amenable examples.

In stark contrast, Kazhdan's Property (T) is a strong rigidity property. A locally compact group G has Property (T) if the trivial representation is isolated in its unitary dual. Equivalently, any unitary representation almost having invariant vectors must have a non-zero invariant vector. Groups with Property (T) are necessarily non-amenable (unless finite). Significant examples include SLn(Z) for n ≥ 3, Sp2n(Z) for n ≥ 2, and lattices in higher-rank simple Lie groups like SLn(R) for n ≥ 3. Property (T) has profound implications, from expander graphs to geometric rigidity (e.g., Margulis's Superrigidity Theorem, a hallmark of higher-rank groups which also possess Property (T)). It dictates fixed-point properties for affine actions and influences group cohomology.

Geometric Realizations: The Borel-Weil-Bott Theorem and Flag Varieties

A powerful approach to irreducible representations of compact connected Lie groups G uses geometric realization: constructing representations as spaces of sections of line bundles over homogeneous spaces, notably the flag variety. This connects representation theory deeply with algebraic geometry.

For a compact connected Lie group G, let T be a maximal torus and B a Borel subgroup of its complexification GC. The flag variety is X = GC / B, a complex projective variety generalizing projective spaces and Grassmannians. Points correspond to Borel subgroups of GC. Line bundles Lλ on X are associated with characters λ of B (determined by their restriction to T, an integral weight).

The Borel-Weil Theorem states that for a dominant integral weight λ, the irreducible representation V(λ) with highest weight λ is realized as the space of global holomorphic sections H0(GC/B, Lλ); all higher cohomology groups Hi (i > 0) vanish. This provides a concrete geometric construction for every finite-dimensional irreducible representation.

The Borel-Weil-Bott Theorem generalizes this to arbitrary integral weights. For any integral weight λ, exactly one cohomology group Hi(GC/B, Lλ) is non-zero. If λ is regular (not orthogonal to any root), this occurs at degree i = l(w) for a unique w in the Weyl group W, yielding V(w·λ) (where w·λ is the shifted weight). If λ is singular, all cohomology vanishes. This theorem completes the geometric picture of irreducible representations and provides tools for character formulas via Euler characteristics.

Application of Borel-Weil Theorem
Consider G = SU(2). Its complexification is SL(2,C). The maximal torus T consists of diagonal matrices; a Borel subgroup B consists of upper triangular matrices. The flag variety SL(2,C)/B is isomorphic to the complex projective line P1(C) (the Riemann sphere). Irreducible representations of SU(2) are Vn (n = 0, 1, 2, ...) with highest weight n. How is V1 (the standard 2-dimensional representation) realized geometrically?

For SU(2), Vn corresponds to highest weight n. V1 has highest weight 1. By Borel-Weil, V1 is the space of global holomorphic sections of a line bundle L1 on P1(C).

Line bundles on P1(C) are O(k) for k ∈ Z. The space H0(P1(C), O(k)) is:

  • For k ≥ 0: Homogeneous polynomials of degree k in x, y (dimension k+1).
  • For k < 0: Trivial (zero section only).

V1 has dimension 2. Setting k+1 = 2 gives k = 1. Thus V1 ≅ H0(P1(C), O(1)), the space of linear homogeneous polynomials {ax + by | a, b ∈ C}. Generally, Vn (dimension n+1) is realized as H0(P1(C), O(n)).

Adelic Groups and the Global Langlands Program: Unifying Local Representations

The Langlands Program seeks to unify number theory, representation theory, and algebraic geometry. The local Langlands correspondence relates representations of p-adic groups G(Fv) to local parameters. The Global Langlands Program extends this to global fields (e.g., Q or function fields), requiring adelic groups.

For a global field F, the adele ring AF is a restricted direct product of all completions Fv (real R and p-adic Qp). The adelic group G(AF) is the restricted direct product of local groups G(Fv). For instance, GLn(AQ) is the adelic group for GLn over Q. This framework studies objects with local properties at every prime and infinity simultaneously.

Automorphic forms are functions on the quotient G(F)G(AF) satisfying analyticity, growth, and transformation properties. They generate automorphic representations, irreducible unitary representations of G(AF) appearing in the L2-decomposition. A key feature is factorization: an automorphic representation π decomposes uniquely as a restricted tensor product ⊗v πv, where πv is an irreducible representation of G(Fv). This links a single global object to a collection of local objects.

The Global Langlands Correspondence conjectures a deep relationship between automorphic representations of G(AF) and global Galois-theoretic data: homomorphisms from the global Weil group WF (or Langlands group) to the L-group LG, preserving L-functions and epsilon factors. For GLn, this is largely established (Langlands, Jacquet, Shalika, et al.); for general reductive groups, it remains a major open problem.

The adelic framework unifies local phenomena into a coherent global structure, connecting local representation theory to global number theory. This unification is substantive: it underpins the modularity theorem for elliptic curves (Langlands for GL2), central to the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem.

Representation Theory in Quantum Field Theory: Symmetries and Particle Classification

Representation theory is indispensable in quantum mechanics and QFT, providing the framework for symmetries and particle classification. The states of a physical system form a representation of its symmetry group.

A foundational application is Wigner's classification. In relativistic quantum mechanics, spacetime symmetries are the Poincaré group (semidirect product of Lorentz and translation groups). Wigner showed elementary particles correspond to irreducible unitary representations of the Poincaré group. Mass (m) and spin (s) emerge as eigenvalues of Casimir operators (m² and W² for massive particles, where W is the Pauli-Lubanski vector; helicity for massless particles). This gives a rigorous basis for particle properties.

Particles also exhibit internal symmetries described by compact Lie groups. The strong force uses the SU(3) gauge group of QCD. Quarks transform under the fundamental (3-dimensional) representation ("color"); gluons under the adjoint (8-dimensional) representation. The "Eightfold Way" classification of hadrons into SU(3) flavor multiplets predicted the Ω⁻ baryon. Flavor SU(3) is approximate; color SU(3) is an exact gauge symmetry.

The electroweak force uses SU(2) × U(1). Left-handed leptons form doublets under SU(2); right-handed leptons are singlets. Force carriers (W⁺, W⁻, Z⁰, photon) correspond to the adjoint representation. Spontaneous symmetry breaking via the Higgs mechanism breaks SU(2) × U(1) to electromagnetic U(1), giving mass to W/Z bosons while the photon remains massless. The Higgs field's vacuum expectation value transforms under a specific representation.

In summary, representation theory classifies particles by quantum numbers (mass, spin, charge, color, isospin) and governs interaction rules. Lie algebra structures and representation classifications directly translate to the particle spectrum observed in experiments.

Categorical Aspects: Monoidal Categories and Fusion Rings in Representation Theory

The study of representations has evolved to categories of representations with intertwining operators as morphisms. This categorical perspective, via monoidal categories, provides a powerful framework for quantum groups, conformal field theory, and topological QFT.

A monoidal category (tensor category) is a category C with a bifunctor ⊗: C × C → C, an identity object I, and natural associativity/unit isomorphisms satisfying coherence axioms. The category of finite-dimensional representations of a group G (or Lie algebra g, quantum group Uq(g)) is a prime example: tensor product is V ⊗ W with diagonal action g·(v ⊗ w) = (g·v) ⊗ (g·w).

For semisimple categories (e.g., finite groups, compact Lie groups), objects decompose uniquely into irreducibles. The tensor product Vλ ⊗ Vμ ≅ ⨁ν Nλμν Vν defines fusion coefficients Nλμν (Littlewood-Richardson coefficients in specific contexts). These define the commutative, associative fusion ring (fusion algebra), whose basis is irreducible representations and multiplication is tensor decomposition.

Fusion rings are central in conformal field theory, where fusion rules of primary fields (irreducible representations of Virasoro or affine Kac-Moody algebras) govern operator product expansions. The Verlinde formula computes fusion coefficients from the S-matrix of a modular tensor category, arising in rational CFTs and topological QFTs. These categories are often braided (V ⊗ W ≅ W ⊗ V via a natural isomorphism); if the braiding squared is identity, the category is symmetric.

Monoidal categories and fusion rings abstract universal algebraic structures governing representation composition, enabling comparison across systems and connecting to knot theory, 3-manifold invariants, and quantum gravity.

Common Traps in Advanced Representation Theory

  • Confusing Amenability with Property (T): Assuming a group is amenable if "nice," or that Property (T) is a weaker amenability. They are typically opposite; non-trivial Property (T) groups are non-amenable.
  • Misapplying Borel-Weil-Bott to Non-Compact Groups: Using the theorem directly for non-compact Lie groups without modifications (parabolic induction, Dolbeault cohomology, Harish-Chandra modules). The standard theorem applies to compact groups or their complexifications.
  • Overlooking Global Constraints in Adelic Groups: Treating adelic representations as mere collections of local representations, ignoring the diagonal embedding G(F) G(AF) linking them and defining automorphic forms.
  • Oversimplifying Particle Classification: Believing all particle properties stem solely from Poincaré group representations. Internal symmetries (SU(3) color, SU(2) electroweak) are equally crucial for complete classification and interactions.
  • Assuming All Monoidal Categories are Semisimple: Important categories (quantum groups at roots of unity, certain vertex operator algebras) are non-semisimple; objects need not decompose into irreducibles. This requires tools like Grothendieck groups and fusion rings on isomorphism classes.
  • Ignoring Coherence Axioms: Associativity and unit coherence axioms are fundamental to monoidal categories, ensuring canonical isomorphisms for different bracketings. Their importance grows in higher categorical structures.

Representation Theory of Groups Acting on Trees: Bass–Serre Theory and Induced Representations

Bass–Serre theory provides a geometric framework for understanding groups that act on trees without inversions. A group action on a tree yields a graph of groups whose fundamental group is isomorphic to the original group. The vertex and edge stabilizers become the vertex and edge groups, and the inclusions are the edge monomorphisms. This decomposition translates algebraic properties of the group into combinatorial data of the graph of groups. For a group G acting on a tree T, the quotient graph of groups GT encodes the structure of G as an iterated amalgamated free product or HNN extension. The classical Kurosh subgroup theorem and the structure of subgroups of free products follow naturally from this viewpoint.

In representation theory, the tree action allows one to construct induced representations from stabilizers. If H is the stabilizer of a vertex v, the permutation representation on the coset space G/H is isomorphic to the space of functions on the vertex set of T with finite support. Mackey's irreducibility criterion for induced representations can be reformulated in terms of the intersection of conjugates of H with other vertex stabilizers, which correspond to the structure of the graph of groups. For example, the representation theory of SL₂(F) for a non‑archimedean local field F is governed by its action on the Bruhat–Tits tree; the irreducible smooth representations are classified by the types of Bernstein, which are built from compact open subgroups that appear as vertex stabilizers. The theory also underlies the construction of the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence for GL₂ via the trace formula on the tree.

Key results include Serre's theorem that a group acts freely on a tree iff it is a free group, and the Bass–Serre covering theory which relates subgroups of the fundamental group to coverings of the graph of groups. The cohomological dimension of a group acting on a tree is at most one plus the maximum cohomological dimension of its vertex stabilizers. This yields strong constraints on the representation theory of groups with property (T) or amenability when they act on trees.

Representation Theory of Non‑Semisimple Tensor Categories: Projective Objects, Ext Quivers, and Logarithmic CFT

Finite tensor categories that are not semisimple arise naturally in the representation theory of quantum groups at roots of unity, of vertex operator algebras at admissible levels, and in logarithmic conformal field theory (LCFT). Unlike semisimple modular tensor categories, these categories contain indecomposable projective objects that are not simple. The projective cover P(X) of a simple object X fits into a short exact sequence 0 → rad P(X) → P(X) → X → 0, and the radical rad P(X) encodes extension data. The Ext quiver (or Ext‑graph) has vertices indexed by simple objects and arrows given by dim Ext¹(X,Y). For the restricted quantum group u_q(sl₂) at a primitive ℓ‑th root of unity (ℓ odd), the simple modules are labelled by highest weights 0,1,…,ℓ−2, and the Ext quiver is the affine Dynkin diagram of type A(1)ℓ−2. The projective covers have Loewy length ℓ, and their characters are given by the Weyl–Kac character formula for the corresponding affine Lie algebra.

In logarithmic CFT, the Virasoro algebra at central charge c = 1 − 6/(p(p+1)) (p ≥ 2) yields a non‑semisimple category of modules. The simple modules are the minimal models, but there exist indecomposable modules with Jordan blocks for L₀. The fusion rules are governed by the Verlinde formula applied to the Grothendieck ring, while the full tensor structure requires the computation of associators (F‑matrices) on projective covers. The work of Fuchs, Schweigert, and others shows that the modular transformation of characters extends to a logarithmic modular group action on the space of torus one‑point functions, leading to a logarithmic Verlinde formula that computes fusion coefficients of indecomposable objects. These structures are essential for the description of critical percolation, the abelian sandpile model, and the WZW model at fractional level.

Technique: Computing the Ext Quiver for u_q(sl₂) at a Root of Unity
Let q be a primitive 4th root of unity (ℓ = 4). The restricted quantum group u_q(sl₂) has simple modules L(0), L(1), L(2). Determine the Ext quiver and the Loewy structure of the projective covers.
For ℓ = 4, the simple modules correspond to highest weights 0,1,2. The Ext¹ groups are known: Ext¹(L(i),L(j)) is one‑dimensional iff |i−j| = 1, and zero otherwise. Hence the Ext quiver is a line with three vertices 0 — 1 — 2. The projective cover P(1) has Loewy length 4 and composition factors L(1), L(0)⊕L(2), L(1), L(0)⊕L(2) from top to socle. P(0) and P(2) have Loewy length 3 with composition series L(0), L(1), L(0) and L(2), L(1), L(2) respectively. The dimensions are dim P(0)=dim P(2)=4, dim P(1)=8. This matches the known character formulas for the projective covers of u_q(sl₂) at ℓ=4.

Representation Theory of Factorization Algebras and Chiral Algebras

Factorization algebras, introduced by Beilinson and Drinfeld and later developed by Costello and Gwilliam, provide a homotopical framework for quantum field theory on manifolds. A factorization algebra on a topological space X assigns to each open set U a cochain complex F(U) together with a factorization product F(U₁) ⊗ … ⊗ F(Uₙ) → F(V) for pairwise disjoint opens Uᵢ ⊂ V, satisfying associativity and symmetry up to coherent homotopy. On a complex curve, a chiral algebra is a factorization algebra equipped with a Lie*‑bracket encoding the operator product expansion (OPE) of vertex operators. The category of modules over a chiral algebra is a braided tensor category, and its conformal blocks form a vector bundle with projectively flat connection on the moduli space of curves — the geometric Langlands correspondence for GLₙ can be formulated as an equivalence between categories of D‑modules on Bun_G and categories of modules over a chiral algebra.

The classical example is the chiral algebra of a vertex operator algebra (VOA). The OPE of fields Y(a,z)Y(b,w) ∼ Σ c (z−w)−k Y(c,w) translates into the factorization product on the Ran space. The Beilinson–Drinfeld construction of the affine Grassmannian and the geometric Satake equivalence can be recast as a statement about factorization algebras on the curve. In topological quantum field theory, the factorization homology of a manifold with coefficients in an Eₙ‑algebra computes the space of states; for n=2 this recovers the Reshetikhin–Turaev TQFT from a modular tensor category. Recent work of Lurie on the cobordism hypothesis uses factorization algebras to classify fully extended TQFTs.

Representation Theory of Quiver Varieties and Nakajima's Construction

Nakajima's quiver varieties provide a geometric realization of the irreducible highest‑weight representations of Kac–Moody algebras. Given a quiver Q without loops, a dimension vector v, and a framing vector w, the quiver variety M(v,w) is constructed as a hyperkähler quotient of the representation space of the doubled quiver by the product of unitary groups. The variety carries a natural action of the group G_w = ∏ GL(w_i), and its equivariant cohomology H^*G_w(M(v,w)) carries an action of the universal enveloping algebra U(g_Q) where g_Q is the Kac–Moody algebra associated to Q. Nakajima proved that the top homology of the fiber of the projective morphism M(v,w) → M₀(v,w) yields the weight spaces of the irreducible highest‑weight module V(w) with highest weight determined by w.

For the Jordan quiver (one vertex, one loop) the quiver variety is the Hilbert scheme of points on ℂ², and the construction recovers the Fock space representation of the Heisenberg algebra. For affine type A quivers, the quiver varieties are isomorphic to moduli spaces of instantons on ALE spaces, and the construction yields the basic representation of the affine Lie algebra. Lusztig's quiver varieties, defined via a different stability condition, give a geometric basis (canonical basis) for the negative part of the quantum group U_q(g). The two constructions are related by the Maffei–Nakajima isomorphism. Quiver varieties also appear in the geometric Langlands program as moduli spaces of Higgs bundles, and in 3d mirror symmetry as Coulomb branches of 3d N=4 gauge theories.

Representation Theory of Topological Quantum Field Theory: Extended TQFT and the Cobordism Hypothesis

An n‑dimensional extended topological quantum field theory (TQFT) is a symmetric monoidal functor Z: Bord_nfr → C from the (∞,n)‑category of framed cobordisms to a symmetric monoidal (∞,n)‑category C. The cobordism hypothesis, proved by Lurie, states that the space of such functors is equivalent to the space of fully dualizable objects in C. For n=2, a fully dualizable object in a braided monoidal category is a Frobenius algebra; the resulting TQFT is the Dijkgraaf–Witten theory with finite group G when C = 2Vect and the object is the category of G‑graded vector spaces. For n=3, the Reshetikhin–Turaev TQFT arises from a modular tensor category C, where the fully dualizable object is C itself. The partition function on a closed 3‑manifold M is computed by surgery presentation and the modular S‑matrix.

The representation theory of mapping class groups enters through the action on the space of conformal blocks (the value of the TQFT on a surface). For a modular tensor category, the mapping class group representation factors through a finite quotient, giving rise to the Verlinde formula for the dimension of the space of conformal blocks. In the extended setting, the value of the TQFT on a point is a fully dualizable object; its endomorphism category is the category of boundary conditions. The cobordism hypothesis thus classifies all possible boundary conditions, defects, and corner data in terms of higher categorical algebra. This framework underlies the classification of topological phases of matter and the construction of invariants of 4‑manifolds via Crane–Yetter theory.

Deligne–Lusztig Theory and Character Sheaves for Finite Reductive Groups

Deligne–Lusztig theory constructs irreducible representations of finite reductive groups G(𝔽_q) using ℓ-adic cohomology of algebraic varieties over the algebraic closure of 𝔽_q. For a connected reductive group G over 𝔽_q with Frobenius endomorphism F, and a rational Borel subgroup B containing a rational maximal torus T, the Deligne–Lusztig variety X(w) associated to an element w of the Weyl group W = N_G(T)/T is the subvariety of the flag variety G/B defined by X(w) = {gB ∈ G/B : g⁻¹F(g) ∈ BwB}. These varieties are smooth, quasi-projective, and carry a natural action of G(𝔽_q) × T(𝔽_q). The virtual representation R_T^G(θ) = ∑_i (-1)^i H_c^i(X(w), ℚ_ℓ)_θ, where θ is a character of T(𝔽_q) and the subscript denotes the θ-isotypic component, is independent of the choice of B and w up to sign. When θ is in general position (i.e., its stabilizer in W is trivial), ±R_T^G(θ) is an irreducible cuspidal representation. The characters of these virtual representations are given by the Deligne–Lusztig character formula, which expresses them as linear combinations of Green functions evaluated at semisimple elements. Lusztig extended this framework by introducing character sheaves: a class of F-stable perverse sheaves on G whose trace functions yield the irreducible characters of G(𝔽_q). The classification of character sheaves on G leads to a partition of the irreducible representations into Lusztig series ℰ(G, s) indexed by semisimple conjugacy classes s in the dual group G*. Each series corresponds to representations whose character sheaves are supported on the unipotent variety, and the Jordan decomposition of characters gives a bijection between ℰ(G, s) and the unipotent representations of the centralizer CG*(s). This geometric approach underlies Lusztig's classification of unipotent representations for all finite reductive groups and provides the foundation for the local Langlands correspondence for p-adic groups via the theory of types.

Cohomological Induction, Dirac Operators, and the Unitary Dual of Real Reductive Groups

For a real reductive group G with maximal compact subgroup K, the problem of classifying the unitary dual Ĝ (the set of equivalence classes of irreducible unitary representations) is solved by combining cohomological induction with Dirac operator techniques. Cohomological induction, developed by Zuckerman and Vogan, constructs representations of G from representations of a θ-stable parabolic subalgebra 𝔮 = 𝔩 ⊕ 𝔲 of the complexified Lie algebra 𝔤_ℂ. Given a (𝔩, L∩K)-module X, the derived functor modules ℛ^j(X) = H^j(𝔤, K; U(𝔤) ⊗U(𝔮) X) yield (𝔤, K)-modules. When X is a one-dimensional module ℂ_λ with λ sufficiently dominant, ℛ^j(ℂ_λ) is either zero or an irreducible representation with infinitesimal character λ+ρ(𝔲). The bottom layer j = dim(𝔲∩𝔭) gives the A_𝔮(λ) modules of Vogan, which include all discrete series representations (when 𝔮 is a Borel subalgebra) and limits of discrete series. The Dirac operator D ∈ U(𝔤) ⊗ C(𝔭), where C(𝔭) is the Clifford algebra of the non-compact part 𝔭 of the Cartan decomposition, provides a powerful tool for computing cohomology and detecting unitarity. Vogan's Dirac cohomology H_D(X) = Ker(D)/Im(D) for a (𝔤, K)-module X is a module for the spin double cover of K, and its non-vanishing implies constraints on the infinitesimal character. The Dirac inequality ‖λ+ρ_c‖ ≥ ‖ρ_n‖, where λ is the highest weight of a K-type, ρ_c and ρ_n are half-sums of compact and non-compact positive roots, gives a necessary condition for unitarity. The Barbasch–Vogan classification of the unitary dual for classical groups uses the orbit method and the associated variety of the annihilator in the symmetric algebra S(𝔤). For exceptional groups, the unitary dual is known case by case via a combination of cohomological induction, Dirac cohomology, and the computation of signature of invariant Hermitian forms on K-types. The Arthur packets for real groups, which organize representations by their endoscopic transfer, are characterized by the property that their members share the same infinitesimal character and have related associated varieties.

Worked Problem: Dirac Cohomology of a Discrete Series
Let G = SL(2, ℝ) with maximal compact K = SO(2). The discrete series representations D_n^± (n ≥ 2) have infinitesimal character λ = (n-1)/2. Compute the Dirac cohomology H_D(D_n+) and verify the Dirac inequality.
For SL(2, ℝ), 𝔤 = 𝔰𝔩(2, ℝ), 𝔨 = 𝔰𝔬(2), 𝔭 = symmetric matrices. The Clifford algebra C(𝔭) ≅ M2(ℂ). The Dirac operator is D = X ⊗ γ(X) + Y ⊗ γ(Y) + H ⊗ γ(H) where X,Y,H is a basis of 𝔭 and γ is the Clifford action. The discrete series D_n+ has K-types with weights n, n+2, n+4, ... (lowest weight n). The infinitesimal character is λ = (n-1)/2. The Dirac cohomology H_D(D_n+) is non-zero only for the lowest K-type of weight n, where it is one-dimensional. The Dirac inequality: ρ_c = 0 (no compact roots), ρ_n = 1/2 (positive non-compact root). The highest weight of the lowest K-type is λ_K = n/2. The inequality ‖λ_K + ρ_c‖ ≥ ‖ρ_n‖ becomes |n/2| ≥ 1/2, which holds for n ≥ 1. For n ≥ 2, the inequality is strict, consistent with D_n+ being unitary and in the discrete series. The Dirac cohomology detects the lowest K-type and confirms unitarity via the Parthasarathy criterion: a (𝔤, K)-module with non-zero Dirac cohomology and satisfying the Dirac inequality strictly is unitary.

Always distinguish between the full unitary dual and the tempered dual (which is classified by the Langlands classification and Arthur packets). Quantum Groups at Generic Parameter: Canonical Bases, Crystal Bases, and Categorification

For a complex semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤 and a parameter q ∈ ℂ^× not a root of unity, the Drinfeld–Jimbo quantum group U_q(𝔤) is a Hopf algebra deformation of the universal enveloping algebra U(𝔤). Its finite-dimensional representation theory mirrors the classical case: irreducible representations V_q(λ) are indexed by dominant integral weights λ, and their characters are given by the Weyl character formula. The novel structures appear at the level of bases. Lusztig's canonical basis (or global crystal basis) of U_q- (𝔤) and of V_q(λ) is a ℤ[q, q⁻¹]-basis with remarkable positivity and integrality properties. It is characterized by being invariant under the bar involution (q q⁻¹) and having triangular transition matrices with the PBW basis. Kashiwara's crystal basis is the q → 0 limit of the canonical basis: a ℤ-basis of the q=0 specialization equipped with Kashiwara operators ẽ_i, f̃_i that encode the tensor product multiplicities combinatorially. The crystal graph B(λ) of V_q(λ) has vertices the crystal basis elements and edges given by f̃_i; its connected components correspond to irreducible summands in tensor products. The tensor product rule for crystals realizes the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients for 𝔤 = 𝔰𝔩_n and their generalizations. Categorification replaces the quantum group by a 2-category: the Khovanov–Lauda–Rouquier (KLR) quiver Hecke algebras R_ν categorify U_q- (𝔤), with the Grothendieck group of projective modules isomorphic to U_q- (𝔤). The cyclotomic quotients R_ν^λ categorify V_q(λ). The canonical basis corresponds to the classes of irreducible modules, and the crystal operators correspond to induction/restriction functors. This categorification extends to the full quantum group via the 2-category of Soergel bimodules for the Hecke algebra, and to quantum groups of affine type via the work of Webster and others. The geometric realization of the canonical basis via perverse sheaves on quiver varieties (Lusztig) and on the affine Grassmannian (Mirković–Vilonen) provides a geometric interpretation of the positivity properties.

Double Affine Hecke Algebras, Macdonald Polynomials, and Cherednik's Duality

The double affine Hecke algebra (DAHA) of type (C^∨, C) associated to a reduced root system R is an algebra generated by the affine Hecke algebra H_aff, the group algebra of the weight lattice P, and an additional set of operators Y^λ (λ ∈ P) satisfying the cross-relations T_i Y^λ = Ys_i(λ) T_i + (t_i1/2 - t_i-1/2) (Y^λ - Ys_i(λ))/(1 - Y-α_i). Here T_i are the affine Hecke generators, s_i are simple reflections, and t_i are parameters. The polynomial representation of DAHA acts on ℂ[P] by q-difference operators, and its eigenfunctions are the non-symmetric Macdonald polynomials E_μ (μ ∈ P). The symmetric Macdonald polynomials P_λ = ∑w∈W Ewλ are simultaneous eigenfunctions of the commuting Cherednik–Dunkl operators Y^λ + Y-λ. Cherednik's duality is an anti-involution of the DAHA that exchanges the roles of the affine Hecke generators T_i and the Y-operators, while mapping the parameter q to q and t_i to t_i-1. This duality transforms the polynomial representation into a representation on the space of functions on the dual torus, and it maps non-symmetric Macdonald polynomials to their duals. The evaluation formula E_μ(λ) = q⟨μ, λ⟩ ∏α>0 (1 - t_α q⟨μ, α^∨⟩ e-α)/(1 - q⟨μ, α^∨⟩ e-α) (suitably normalized) exhibits the symmetry E_μ(λ) = E_λ(μ) under duality. The DAHA also acts on the equivariant K-theory of the Hilbert scheme of points on ℂ² (for type A) and on the cohomology of Nakajima quiver varieties, providing a representation-theoretic interpretation of the geometric constructions. The spherical subalgebra of the DAHA is isomorphic to the spherical Cherednik algebra, and its representation theory is governed by the KZ functor to the category O of the rational Cherednik algebra. The parameters of the DAHA correspond to the central charge of the rational Cherednik algebra, and the duality corresponds to the Fourier transform on the category O.

Local Langlands Correspondence for p-adic Groups: Types, L-parameters, and the Bernstein Center

The local Langlands correspondence (LLC) for a p-adic reductive group G over a non-archimedean local field F conjectures a bijection between the set of irreducible smooth representations of G(F) and the set of L-parameters φ: W_F × SL(2, ℂ) → ^L G, where W_F is the Weil group of F and ^L G is the L-group (the semidirect product of the dual group Ĝ with the Galois group). For GL_n, the correspondence is a theorem of Harris–Taylor and Henniart, characterized by preservation of L-functions and ε-factors of pairs. For general groups, the correspondence is known for classical groups (Arthur, Mok, Kaletha–Minguez–Shin–White) and for many cases via the theory of types. Bushnell–Kutzko types provide a classification of irreducible smooth representations of GL_n(F) by associating to each representation a pair (J, λ) where J is a compact open subgroup and λ is an irreducible representation of J, such that the representation is compactly induced from λ. The Hecke algebra of the type is a commutative algebra of affine Hecke type, and its spectrum parametrizes the representations in the Bernstein block. The Bernstein center 𝔷(G) = End_G(IdRep(G)) acts by scalars on each irreducible representation, and its spectrum is the Bernstein variety, whose connected components are the Bernstein blocks. The LLC is compatible with the Bernstein decomposition: L-parameters with the same centralizer in ^L G correspond to representations in the same block. The enhanced L-parameter (φ, ρ) where ρ is an irreducible representation of the component group S_φ = π0(C^L G(φ)) parametrizes the packet Π_φ. The local Langlands correspondence for inner forms of GL_n (the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence) is realized via the types of simple strata. For classical groups, the construction of types by Stevens and others uses covers of maximal simple types, and the LLC is established by comparing trace formulas. The local factors (L-functions, ε-factors, γ-factors) are defined via the Langlands–Shahidi method or the Rankin–Selberg method, and their properties under the LLC are a key test of the correspondence. The geometric Langlands program for function fields provides a geometric interpretation of the LLC via the moduli stack of G-bundles on a curve and Hecke eigensheaves.

  • Assuming the Dirac inequality is sufficient for unitarity; it is necessary, but unitarity requires checking the signature of the invariant Hermitian form on all K-types.
  • Treating the Deligne–Lusztig variety X(w) as independent of the choice of Borel subgroup B; the virtual representation R_T^G(θ) is independent, but the variety itself depends on B.
  • Overlooking the role of the component group S_φ in the local Langlands correspondence; the packet Π_φ is parametrized by irreducible representations of S_φ, not just by φ alone.
  • Confusing the double affine Hecke algebra (DAHA) with the affine Hecke algebra; the DAHA contains both the affine Hecke algebra and the group algebra of the weight lattice, with cross-relations involving the Y-operators.
  • Assuming the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n is characterized only by preservation of L-functions; it also requires preservation of ε-factors of pairs and compatibility with the Zelevinsky classification.

Representation Theory of Quantum Toroidal Algebras and Their Crystal Bases

Quantum toroidal algebras, also called double affine quantum groups, are Hopf algebra deformations of the universal enveloping algebras of toroidal Lie algebras. They depend on two deformation parameters q and t (or equivalently q₁, q₂, q₃ with q₁q₂q₃ = 1) and contain both the quantum affine algebra Uq(ĝ) and the quantum group Ut(ĝ) as subalgebras. The defining relations are given by the Drinfeld double construction applied to the quantum loop algebra, leading to a presentation with generators ei,k, fi,k, ψi,k± for i ∈ I (the index set of the underlying finite‑type Cartan matrix) and k ∈ ℤ. The cross‑relations involve the quantum toroidal R‑matrix, which satisfies the quantum Yang–Baxter equation on the tensor product of two evaluation modules.

A central feature is the existence of a family of vertical and horizontal Fock representations. Vertical representations are built from the level‑one Fock space of the quantum affine subalgebra and are parametrized by a spectral parameter u. Horizontal representations arise from the Macdonald polynomial realization and are labelled by a highest weight λ and a second parameter v. The tensor product of a vertical and a horizontal representation decomposes into a direct sum of irreducible modules, and the decomposition coefficients are given by the elliptic Hall algebra structure constants. This duality underlies the AGT correspondence for 𝔰𝔩2 and its higher‑rank generalizations.

Crystal bases at q = 0 (or t = 0) have been constructed by Nakajima and Varagnolo–Vasserot using quiver varieties of affine type. The crystal graph coincides with the set of plane partitions (for type A₁) or generalized Young diagrams for higher rank. The Kashiwara operators ẽi, f̃i act by adding or removing boxes subject to a charge condition that reflects the two‑parameter deformation. The combinatorial R‑matrix on crystals reproduces the tropicalization of the quantum toroidal R‑matrix, providing a purely combinatorial description of the tensor product decomposition.

Applications include the computation of refined topological string amplitudes on toric Calabi–Yau threefolds, where the quantum toroidal algebra acts on the equivariant K‑theory of the Hilbert scheme of points on ℂ². The shuffle algebra realization of the positive half gives an explicit formula for the elliptic Hall algebra generators as symmetric rational functions, and the coproduct corresponds to the plethystic substitution of variables. This link makes the representation theory of quantum toroidal algebras a bridge between geometric representation theory, integrable systems, and supersymmetric gauge theory.

Representation Theory of Shifted Yangians and Finite W‑Algebras

Shifted Yangians Yμ(𝔤) are a family of filtered deformations of the universal enveloping algebra U(𝔤[z]) of the current algebra of a simple Lie algebra 𝔤. They are indexed by a shift matrix μ = (μij) of non‑negative integers satisfying μij + μji = (αi, αj). The defining relations are a shifted version of the RTT presentation: the generating series Tij(u) satisfy R(u−v) T₁(u) T₂(v) = T₂(v) T₁(u) R(u−v) with the same rational R‑matrix as the ordinary Yangian, but the matrix entries are subject to shifting rules that truncate certain coefficients. When μ = 0 one recovers the standard Yangian Y(𝔤); when μ is maximal one obtains the finite W‑algebra Wfin(𝔤, e) associated to a nilpotent element e via quantum Hamiltonian reduction.

The representation theory of shifted Yangians is governed by highest weight modules V(λ) labelled by a tuple of Drinfeld polynomials λ = (λi(u)). The shifted condition forces the Drinfeld polynomials to have prescribed zero patterns, which translates into a combinatorial description of the Gelfand–Tsetlin basis. For type A, the finite‑dimensional irreducible representations are classified by shifted Young diagrams (or pyramids) and their characters are given by factorial Schur functions. The tensor product of two such modules decomposes with multiplicities controlled by the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients for factorial Schur functions, a result established by Brundan–Kleshchev using the categorical action of the KLR algebra.

Finite W‑algebras appear as the μ → ∞ limit of shifted Yangians. Their representation category is equivalent to the category of Whittaker modules for U(𝔤) with a fixed central character. The Skryabin equivalence identifies Wfin(𝔤, e)‑mod with the category of U(𝔤)‑modules on which the nilpotent subalgebra 𝔫 acts via a non‑degenerate character. This equivalence transports the Kostant–Whittaker reduction to a Miura transformation on the level of shifted Yangians, suggesting a geometric interpretation of the shift parameters as equivariant parameters for the Slodowy slice to the nilpotent orbit of e.

Recent work of Kamnitzer–Webster–Weekes–Yacobi constructs a geometric realization of shifted Yangians via the cohomological Hall algebra of a quiver with potential. The shift matrix μ corresponds to a choice of stability condition on the quiver representations, and the resulting BPS algebra acts on the cohomology of the moduli space of framed representations. This links shifted Yangians to 3d mirror symmetry and to the Coulomb branch construction of Braverman–Finkelberg–Nakajima, where the shifted Yangian is identified with the quantized algebra of functions on the Coulomb branch of a 3d 𝒩 = 4 gauge theory.

Technique: Identifying the Shift Matrix from a Given Nilpotent Orbit
Given the nilpotent element e in 𝔰𝔩₄ with Jordan block sizes (2,2), determine the shift matrix μ for the corresponding finite W‑algebra Wfin(𝔰𝔩₄, e) and describe the labelling of its finite‑dimensional irreducible representations.
The partition (2,2) corresponds to a nilpotent orbit with weighted Dynkin diagram (0,1,0). The shift matrix μ is defined by μij = max(0, (αi, αj) − dij) where dij are the entries of the Cartan matrix of the Levi subalgebra centralizing e. For 𝔰𝔩₄ with simple roots α₁, α₂, α₃, the Levi is 𝔰𝔩₂ ⊕ 𝔰𝔩₂ giving d = diag(2,0,2). Computing yields μ = [[0,1,0],[1,0,1],[0,1,0]]. Finite‑dimensional irreducibles of Wfin(𝔰𝔩₄, e) are labelled by pairs of dominant integral weights (λ¹, λ²) for the two 𝔰𝔩₂ factors, equivalently by shifted Young diagrams fitting inside a 2 × 2 rectangle. Their dimensions are given by the product of the corresponding 𝔰𝔩₂ dimensions.

Representation Theory of Coulomb Branches and 3d Mirror Symmetry

The Coulomb branch ℳC(G, N) of a 3d 𝒩 = 4 supersymmetric gauge theory with gauge group G and matter representation N is a hyperkähler variety equipped with a quantization 𝒜ℏ(ℳC). Braverman–Finkelberg–Nakajima defined 𝒜ℏ(ℳC) as the equivariant Borel–Moore homology of the based loop group G((t)) acting on the space of based maps Maps0(ℂ, N). The product is given by a convolution diagram involving the affine Grassmannian GrG. This algebra carries a natural ℤ-grading by the topological charge and a ℂ×-action by loop rotation, making it a graded Hopf algebra.

For a quiver gauge theory of type ADE with dimension vector v and framing w, the Coulomb branch algebra is isomorphic to the shifted Yangian Yμ(𝔤) where 𝔤 is the simple Lie algebra of the same type and the shift matrix μ is determined by v and w. The isomorphism identifies the monopole operators of the gauge theory with the Drinfeld generators of the shifted Yangian. The Higgs branch ℳH(G, N) is the Nakajima quiver variety 𝔐(v, w), and its quantization is the Yangian Yℏ(𝔤) at a specific level. 3d mirror symmetry exchanges the Coulomb branch of one theory with the Higgs branch of the mirror theory, inducing an algebra isomorphism 𝒜ℏ(ℳC) ≅ 𝒜ℏ(ℳH) after appropriate parameter identification.

The representation category of 𝒜ℏ(ℳC) is equivalent to the category of boundary conditions (or line operators) in the 3d theory. Finite‑dimensional representations correspond to vortex line operators labelled by dominant coweights of G. Their characters are computed by the Coulomb branch index, a supersymmetric partition function on S² × S¹ that localizes to a sum over the fixed points of the torus action on the affine Grassmannian. The index satisfies a difference equation (the quantum q‑difference equation) which is the quantum Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov equation for the corresponding shifted Yangian.

Recent advances include the symplectic duality conjecture of Braden–Licata–Proudfoot–Webster, which predicts that the category 𝒪 for the Coulomb branch quantization is equivalent to the category 𝒪 for the Higgs branch quantization of the mirror theory. This equivalence is realized by a derived equivalence of the corresponding quantized symplectic resolutions. The proof for type A uses the KLR algebra categorification of the shifted Yangian and the geometric Satake correspondence for the affine Grassmannian. The duality exchanges the Namikawa Weyl group of the Coulomb branch with the Namikawa Weyl group of the Higgs branch, providing a representation‑theoretic interpretation of the mirror map on parameter spaces.

Representation Theory of Symplectic Duality and Quantized Conical Symplectic Resolutions

A conical symplectic resolution is a projective morphism π : X → X₀ where X is a smooth symplectic variety with a ℂ×-action of positive weights, X₀ is the affine cone, and π is a resolution of singularities. Quantization deforms the structure sheaf 𝒪X to a sheaf of algebras 𝒟ℏ (or a Rees algebra ℛℏ) such that the associated graded is 𝒪X and the Poisson bracket is recovered at first order in ℏ. The global sections Aℏ = Γ(X, 𝒟ℏ) form a filtered algebra whose associated graded is the coordinate ring ℂ[X]. The category 𝒪 for Aℏ consists of finitely generated modules on which the ℂ×-action is locally finite and the eigenvalues of the grading operator lie in a finite set of cosets modulo ℤ.

Symplectic duality (or 3d mirror symmetry in physics language) posits that for a pair of conical symplectic resolutions (X, X₀) and (Xᵛ, X₀ᵛ) there is an equivalence of derived categories Db(𝒪X) ≅ Db(𝒪Xᵛ) that exchanges the Namikawa Weyl groups W(X₀) and W(X₀ᵛ). The Namikawa Weyl group is a finite reflection group acting on the parameter space ℙ = H²(X, ℂ) of quantizations; it is generated by reflections in the walls where the category 𝒪 fails to be semisimple. For Nakajima quiver varieties 𝔐(v, w) the Namikawa Weyl group is a product of symmetric groups, and the walls correspond to the stability conditions where the GIT quotient changes.

The equivalence of categories 𝒪 is constructed via tilting generators. On the Coulomb branch side, the tilting generator is the direct sum of standard modules Δ(λ) for dominant coweights λ. On the Higgs branch side, the tilting generator is the direct sum of tautological bundles on the quiver variety. The endomorphism algebra of the tilting generator on the Coulomb side is the shifted Yangian Yμ(𝔤); on the Higgs side it is the Yangian Yℏ(𝔤) at a specific level. The derived equivalence then induces an isomorphism of the centers Z(Aℏ) ≅ Z(Aℏᵛ), identifying the parameter spaces and the Namikawa Weyl group actions.

Applications include the computation of K‑theoretic Coulomb branch indices via the elliptic stable envelope of Okounkov–Aganagic. The stable envelope provides a basis of the equivariant elliptic cohomology of X that diagonalizes the action of the quantum difference operators. Under symplectic duality, the stable envelope on X maps to the elliptic weight function on Xᵛ, giving a representation‑theoretic proof of the elliptic mirror symmetry conjectures for quiver varieties. This framework also yields a geometric construction of the R‑matrix for the shifted Yangian as the monodromy of the quantum difference equation, linking the representation theory of quantized Coulomb branches to the theory of quantum integrable systems.

Representation Theory of Higher Categories: 2‑Representations and Categorical Actions

Higher representation theory replaces vector spaces by categories and linear functors by 1‑morphisms, leading to the notion of a 2‑representation of a monoidal category 𝒞. A 2‑representation is a monoidal functor 𝒞 → End(𝒟) where 𝒟 is a target 2‑category (often the 2‑category of additive, Karoubi‑complete, ℂ‑linear categories). The simplest non‑trivial example is the categorical action of the Khovanov–Lauda–Rouquier (KLR) algebra Rν on the category 𝒟 = ⊕ν Rν‑pmod of projective modules. The 1‑morphisms are given by induction and restriction functors Indν,ν′, Resν,ν′, and the 2‑morphisms are the natural transformations generated by the KLR diagrammatic relations.

For a symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebra 𝔤, the categorified quantum group 𝒰q(𝔤) (also denoted 𝒰) is a 2‑category whose objects are weights λ ∈ P, 1‑morphisms are generated by ℰi 1λ and ℱi 1λ, and 2‑morphisms are given by the KLR diagrams. A 2‑representation of 𝒰 on a category 𝒟 assigns to each weight λ a category 𝒟λ and to each generator a functor satisfying the categorified Serre relations. The categorical crystal of a 2‑representation is the set of isomorphism classes of indecomposable objects in the 𝒟λ together with the action of the Kashiwara operators ẽi, f̃i induced by the adjunctions ℰi ⊣ ℱi. This recovers the crystal basis of the corresponding integrable highest weight module.

The categorical Schur–Weyl duality of Khovanov–Lauda and Rouquier identifies the 2‑category of 𝒰(𝔰𝔩n)‑modules with the 2‑category of modules over the cyclotomic KLR algebras RνΛ. Under this equivalence, the categorical Verma modules correspond to the standard modules of the cyclotomic KLR algebra, and the categorical simple modules correspond to the graded simple modules. The graded decomposition numbers of the cyclotomic KLR algebra are the graded multiplicities in the categorical Verma flag, which are known to be given by the canonical basis of the quantum group at q = 1 (the Lusztig–Kashiwara basis).

Recent developments include the categorical action of the quantum toroidal algebra on the derived category of coherent sheaves on the Hilbert scheme of points on ℂ² (or more generally on Nakajima quiver varieties). The 1‑morphisms are given by Fourier–Mukai kernels associated to the Hecke correspondences on the moduli space of framed sheaves. The 2‑morphisms are induced by the convolution product of correspondences. This categorification realizes the elliptic Hall algebra as the Grothendieck group of the 2‑category, and the quantum toroidal R‑matrix as the braiding on the 2‑category of modules. The categorical AGT correspondence is expected to relate this 2‑representation to the 2‑category of modules over the Virasoro vertex operator algebra at central charge c = 1 − 6(b − b⁻¹)², providing a higher‑categorical framework for the geometric Langlands correspondence.

  • Treating symplectic duality as an equivalence of abelian categories rather than derived categories; the equivalence holds at the level of bounded derived categories of category 𝒪, and the abelian categories are generally not equivalent.

Representation Theory of Soergel Bimodules and the Categorification of Hecke Algebras

Soergel bimodules provide a geometric categorification of the Hecke algebra associated to a Coxeter system (W, S). Let 𝔥 be the reflection representation of W over ℝ, and let R = Sym(𝔥*) be the polynomial ring graded with deg(𝔥*) = 2. For each simple reflection s ∈ S, define the Soergel bimodule B_s = R ⊗R^s R(1), where R^s is the subring of s-invariants and (1) denotes a grading shift. The category 𝒮Bim of Soergel bimodules is the additive, Karoubi-complete, monoidal category generated by the B_s under tensor product over R, grading shifts, and direct summands. The split Grothendieck group K₀(𝒮Bim) ⊗_ℤ ℤ[v, v⁻¹] is isomorphic to the Hecke algebra ℋ(W) with parameter v, where the class [B_s] maps to the standard generator H_s + v. The indecomposable Soergel bimodules B_w for w ∈ W categorify the Kazhdan–Lusztig basis {C_w} of ℋ(W). Soergel's conjecture, proved by Elias and Williamson (2014) using the theory of paving by affine spaces and the hard Lefschetz theorem for Soergel bimodules, states that the graded rank of Hom(B_x, B_y) equals the coefficient of v in the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomial Px,y(v). This categorification yields a positive interpretation of the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials as graded dimensions of Hom spaces. The light leaves and double leaves bases of Libedinsky provide explicit combinatorial bases for these Hom spaces. The category 𝒮Bim is equivalent to the category of parity sheaves on the flag variety G/B, and under this equivalence the indecomposable Soergel bimodule B_w corresponds to the intersection cohomology complex IC_w. The diagrammatic presentation of 𝒮Bim by Elias–Khovanov and Elias–Williamson uses generators (dots, trivalent vertices) and relations (the Jones–Wenzl relations, the Zamolodchikov relation) to give a purely combinatorial description. This diagrammatic calculus enables computations of decomposition numbers for Hecke algebras at roots of unity and provides a framework for the categorification of quantum groups via the categorical action of the KLR algebra on 𝒮Bim.

Representation Theory of Quantum Symmetric Pairs and Coideal Subalgebras

Quantum symmetric pairs, introduced by Letzter and developed by Kolb, Bao–Wang, and others, are the quantum analogues of symmetric spaces G/K. Let U_q(𝔤) be the Drinfeld–Jimbo quantum group of a semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤, and let θ be an involutive diagram automorphism of the Dynkin diagram. A quantum symmetric pair coideal subalgebra Bc,s ⊂ U_q(𝔤) is generated by elements B_i = F_i + c_i Tw0(Eθ(i)) K_i⁻¹ + s_i K_i⁻¹ for i in a subset of simple roots, together with K_i for i in the fixed-point set of θ. Here Tw0 is Lusztig's braid group action, c_i are parameters satisfying certain constraints, and s_i are scalar parameters. The pair (U_q(𝔤), Bc,s) satisfies the coideal property Δ(Bc,s) ⊂ U_q(𝔤) ⊗ Bc,s, making the category of finite-dimensional Bc,s-modules a monoidal category (though not braided in general). The representation theory of Bc,s parallels that of U_q(𝔤): there is a highest weight theory with Verma modules M(λ), simple quotients L(λ), and a BGG category 𝒪. The Shapovalov determinant formula and the Jantzen sum formula hold, and the blocks of category 𝒪 are controlled by a quantum symmetric pair version of the dot action of the Weyl group. The universal K-matrix K ∈ U_q(𝔤) ⊗ Bc,s intertwines the coproduct and the opposite coproduct on Bc,s, providing solutions to the reflection equation. For symmetric pairs of type AIII (𝔰𝔩p+q, 𝔰(𝔩_p ⊕ 𝔩_q)), the coideal subalgebra Bc,s is isomorphic to the quantum group U_q(𝔩_p ⊕ 𝔩_q) twisted by a diagram automorphism, and its finite-dimensional representations are classified by highest weights satisfying a compatibility condition with the involution. The quantum symmetric pair (U_q(𝔰𝔬2n), Bc,s) of type BDI gives rise to the quantum symmetric space of type BDI, whose representation theory is connected to the theory of quantum zonal spherical functions. The categorification of quantum symmetric pairs via KLR algebras with an involution has been achieved by Bao–Wang and others, where the 2-category of quantum symmetric pairs acts on the category of modules over the cyclotomic KLR algebra with an involution. The quantum Satake correspondence relates the representation category of Bc,s to the representation category of a smaller quantum group, providing a quantum analogue of the classical Satake isomorphism for symmetric spaces.

Worked Example: Computing the Shapovalov Form for a Quantum Symmetric Pair Verma Module
Let 𝔤 = 𝔰𝔩₃ with simple roots α₁, α₂, and let θ be the diagram automorphism swapping α₁ α₂. Consider the quantum symmetric pair coideal subalgebra Bc,s ⊂ U_q(𝔰𝔩₃) of type AII with parameters c₁ = c₂ = 1, s₁ = s₂ = 0. Let λ = ω₁ + ω₂ be the highest weight (sum of fundamental weights). Compute the determinant of the Shapovalov form on the weight space M(λ)λ - α₁ - α₂ of the Verma module M(λ).
The weight space M(λ)λ - α₁ - α₂ has basis {F₁F₂ v_λ, F₂F₁ v_λ, B₁ v_λ, B₂ v_λ} where v_λ is the highest weight vector. The Shapovalov form S(u, v) = (u v_λ, v v_λ) is determined by the anti-involution τ on Bc,s with τ(B_i) = B_i, τ(K_i) = K_i. Using the commutation relations [B_i, F_j] = δij (K_i - K_i⁻¹)/(q - q⁻¹) + ... and the fact that B_i v_λ = 0 for this choice of parameters, we compute the Gram matrix. The off-diagonal terms vanish by weight considerations. The diagonal entries are S(F₁F₂ v_λ, F₁F₂ v_λ) = [⟨λ, α₁∨⟩]_q [⟨λ - α₁, α₂∨⟩]_q = [2]_q [1]_q, and similarly for F₂F₁. For B₁ v_λ, S(B₁ v_λ, B₁ v_λ) = 0 since B₁ v_λ = 0. The determinant is [2]_q² [1]_q². The Jantzen filtration detects the singular vector at λ - α₁ - α₂ corresponding to the root α₁ + α₂, which is fixed by θ.
Exam Strategy: Quantum Symmetric Pairs
When a question asks about quantum symmetric pairs, first identify the involution θ and the Satake diagram. The coideal subalgebra generators B_i are the key objects — write them explicitly using the formula B_i = F_i + c_i Tw0(Eθ(i)) K_i⁻¹ + s_i K_i⁻¹. Remember that the parameters c_i, s_i are constrained by the condition that Bc,s is a coideal subalgebra. For representation theory questions, the highest weight theory mirrors U_q(𝔤) but with the dot action of the restricted Weyl group W^θ. The universal K-matrix satisfies the reflection equation, not the Yang–Baxter equation. Distinguish between the quantum symmetric pair (coideal subalgebra) and the quantum homogeneous space (quotient algebra).

Representation Theory of Affine W-Algebras and the Feigin–Frenkel Duality

Affine W-algebras W^k(𝔤, f) are vertex algebras obtained by quantum Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction of the affine vertex algebra V^k(𝔤) associated to a simple Lie algebra 𝔤 at level k, using a nilpotent element f ∈ 𝔤. For a principal nilpotent f, the affine W-algebra W^k(𝔤, f) is the W-algebra of type 𝔤, generated by fields of spins equal to the exponents of 𝔤 plus one. The Feigin–Frenkel duality (1992) states that for 𝔤 simply-laced, there is an isomorphism of vertex algebras W^k(𝔤) ≅ Wk^∨(^L𝔤) where ^L𝔤 is the Langlands dual Lie algebra and the levels are related by (k + h^∨)(k^∨ + ^Lh^∨) = 1, with h^∨ the dual Coxeter number. This duality exchanges the quantum Drinfeld–Sokolov reduction for 𝔤 with that for ^L𝔤 and provides a vertex-algebraic realization of the geometric Langlands correspondence for opers. The representation theory of affine W-algebras at admissible levels (k + h^∨ ∈ ℚ>0 with denominator dividing the lacing number) is semi-simple and governed by the modular tensor category of integrable highest weight modules of the affine Lie algebra at the dual level. The characters of irreducible modules are given by the Kac–Wakimoto formula, which expresses them as sums over the Weyl group with signs, generalizing the Weyl–Kac character formula. For non-principal nilpotent elements, the affine W-algebra W^k(𝔤, f) has a more complicated structure: it is a vertex superalgebra when f is not even, and its representation theory is controlled by the finite W-algebra U(𝔤, f) via the Zhu algebra construction. The associated variety of W^k(𝔤, f) is the Slodowy slice S_f = f + ker(ad f) ⊂ 𝔤, and the associated graded gr W^k(𝔤, f) is the algebra of functions on the arc space of S_f. The Feigin–Frenkel center 𝔷(𝔤) ⊂ V-h^∨(𝔤) at the critical level maps to the center of W-h^∨(𝔤), which is isomorphic to the algebra of functions on the space of ^L𝔤-opers on the formal disc. This center acts on any module over the affine W-algebra at the critical level, and its spectrum parametrizes the blocks of the category of smooth representations. The quantum geometric Langlands correspondence conjectures an equivalence between the derived category of D-modules on the moduli stack of G-bundles on a curve and the derived category of modules over the affine W-algebra at a certain level, with the Feigin–Frenkel duality corresponding to the exchange of G and ^LG.

Representation Theory of Loop Groups and the Verlinde Formula

The loop group LG = C^∞(S¹, G) of a compact simple Lie group G has a central extension 1 → U(1) → L̂G → LG → 1 classified by the level k ∈ H⁴(BG, ℤ) ≅ ℤ. The positive energy representations of L̂G at level k are in bijection with the integrable highest weight modules of the affine Kac–Moody algebra 𝔤̂ at level k. The fusion rules for the tensor product of these representations are governed by the Verlinde formula: Nλμ^ν = ∑σ ∈ P_k+ (Sλσ Sμσ Sν*σ) / S0σ, where P_k+ is the set of dominant integral weights at level k, S is the modular S-matrix of the affine Lie algebra, and ν* is the weight conjugate to ν. The Verlinde formula computes the dimension of the space of conformal blocks on the Riemann sphere with three marked points labeled by λ, μ, ν. The modular S-matrix is given by the Kac–Peterson formula Sλμ = i|Δ_+| (k + h^∨)-r/2 ∑w ∈ W ε(w) exp(-2πi ⟨w(λ+ρ), μ+ρ⟩ / (k + h^∨)), where r = rank(𝔤). The fusion category Rep_k(LG) is a modular tensor category, equivalent to the category of representations of the quantum group U_q(𝔤) at q = exp(πi/(k + h^∨)) when k + h^∨ is coprime to the lacing number. The Verlinde algebra (the Grothendieck ring of Rep_k(LG) with fusion product) is isomorphic to the ring of level k characters of G with the Verlinde product. The geometric interpretation via the moduli stack Bun_G(Σ) of G-bundles on a Riemann surface Σ: the space of conformal blocks is the space of global sections of the determinant line bundle ℒ^k on Bun_G(Σ), and the Verlinde formula computes its dimension via the Riemann–Roch theorem for stacks. The factorization property of conformal blocks (sewing constraints) gives the modular tensor category structure. For non-simply connected groups, the representation theory of the loop group involves projective representations and the classification of central extensions by π₁(G). The loop group representation theory is also connected to the geometric Satake correspondence: the spherical Hecke category for the affine Grassmannian Gr_G is equivalent to Rep(^LG), and the convolution product corresponds to the fusion product at the critical level.

Representation Theory of Quantum Groups via the FRT Construction and the RTT Relations

The Faddeev–Reshetikhin–Takhtajan (FRT) construction builds a quantum group from a solution R ∈ End(V ⊗ V) of the quantum Yang–Baxter equation R₁₂ R₁₃ R₂₃ = R₂₃ R₁₃ R₁₂. Let V be an n-dimensional vector space, and let T = (tij) be an n × n matrix of generators. The RTT relations are R T₁ T₂ = T₂ T₁ R, where T₁ = T ⊗ I, T₂ = I ⊗ T. The algebra A(R) generated by the entries tij modulo the RTT relations is a bialgebra with coproduct Δ(tij) = ∑_k tik ⊗ tkj, counit ε(tij) = δij, and antipode determined by the quantum determinant. When R is the standard R-matrix of U_q(𝔤𝔩_n), R = q ∑_i Eii ⊗ Eii + ∑i≠j Eii ⊗ Ejj + (q - q⁻¹) ∑i>j Eij ⊗ Eji, the algebra A(R) is the quantum coordinate ring O_q(GL_n). The quantum determinant det_q(T) = ∑σ ∈ S_n (-q)ℓ(σ) t1σ(1) ⋯ tnσ(n) is central and group-like. The quotient by det_q(T) = 1 gives O_q(SL_n). The dual algebra U_q(𝔤𝔩_n) is obtained by taking the Hopf algebra generated by the matrix coefficients of the fundamental representation, with relations dual to the RTT relations. The FRT construction generalizes to any R-matrix satisfying the Hecke condition (R - q)(R + q⁻¹) = 0, yielding quantum groups of types B, C, D. The RTT formalism provides a concrete matrix presentation that is well-suited for constructing representations via the algebraic Bethe ansatz. The monodromy matrix T(λ) = L0n(λ) ⋯ L01(λ) built from L-operators satisfying the same RTT relations generates the commuting transfer matrices t(λ) = tr0 T(λ), which are the Hamiltonians of integrable lattice models. The representation theory of the RTT algebra is controlled by the representation theory of the corresponding quantum group: finite-dimensional irreducible representations are highest weight modules labeled by dominant integral weights, and the tensor product decomposes according to the same Clebsch–Gordan rules as the classical case but with q-deformed multiplicities. The universal R-matrix of U_q(𝔤) provides the intertwiner between the two tensor factors in the RTT relation. The FRT construction also yields the reflection equation algebra for quantum symmetric pairs, where the RTT relation is replaced by R T₁ R T₁ = T₁ R T₁ R.
