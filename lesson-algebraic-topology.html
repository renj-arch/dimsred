Algebraic Topology — Attaching Algebraic Invariants to Spaces

Algebraic topology uses algebra to study topological spaces. It assigns algebraic objects — groups, rings, modules — to spaces in a way that's invariant under homeomorphism. This transforms geometric questions into algebraic ones. Two spaces are different if their algebraic invariants differ. The field began with Poincaré's 1895 Analysis Situs and has since grown into a vast web of connections between topology, algebra, and geometry.

"Algebraic topology is the art of turning topology into algebra and then solving the algebra."
— Dennis Sullivan
Part 1

🔄 Homotopy and the Fundamental Group

Loops and Deformations
The Fundamental Group — Detecting Holes via Loops
Two continuous maps f,g: X → Y are homotopic (f ≃ g) if one can be continuously deformed into the other. A homotopy is a map H: X×[0,1] → Y with H(x,0)=f(x), H(x,1)=g(x). The fundamental group π₁(X,x₀) has elements that are homotopy classes of loops (paths starting and ending at x₀). The group operation is concatenation of loops. For the circle S¹, π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ — loops are classified by winding number. For S², π₁(S²) = 0 — every loop shrinks to a point (the sphere is simply connected). The fundamental group of the figure-8 is the free group on two generators F₂. The Seifert-van Kampen theorem computes π₁ of a union: if X = U ∪ V with U∩V path-connected, then π₁(X) is the amalgamated free product of π₁(U) and π₁(V) over π₁(U∩V). This is the main computational tool for fundamental groups.
The fundamental group captures 1-dimensional holes — loops that can't be shrunk to a point.
Part 2

🏗️ Covering Spaces — Unwinding the Structure

The Universal Cover
Covering Spaces — When a Space Unrolls Into Its Universal Version
A covering space of X is a space X̃ with a map p: X̃ → X such that every point x ∈ X has a neighborhood U evenly covered by p (p^{-1}(U) is a disjoint union of copies of U). The circle S¹ is covered by ℝ via p(t) = e^{2πit}. The universal cover of the torus is ℝ². Covering spaces are intimately tied to the fundamental group: for reasonable spaces, connected coverings correspond to subgroups of π₁. The universal cover X̃ is the simply connected covering (π₁(X̃) = 0). The deck transformation group of the universal cover is π₁(X). The lifting criterion: a map f: Y → X lifts to X̃ iff f_*(π₁(Y)) ⊆ p_*(π₁(X̃)). This connects covering space theory to Galois theory — the analogy is precise and leads to the étale fundamental group in algebraic geometry.
Covering spaces unwind the holes of a space into a simply connected version — the fundamental group acts as symmetries of the unrolling.
Part 3

🧮 Homology — Counting Holes Algebraically

The Groups Hₙ
Homology — Holes of Any Dimension
While the fundamental group detects 1-dimensional holes, homology groups Hₙ(X) detect holes in every dimension. A singular n-simplex is a continuous map σ: Δⁿ → X from the standard n-simplex. The boundary map ∂ₙ: Cₙ → C_{n−1} sends an n-simplex to the alternating sum of its (n−1)-dimensional faces. The singular chain complex is ... → C₂ → C₁ → C₀ → 0 with ∂_{n−1}∘∂ₙ = 0. Define cycles Zₙ = ker ∂ₙ, boundaries Bₙ = im ∂_{n+1}. The nth homology group Hₙ(X) = Zₙ / Bₙ captures n-dimensional cycles modulo boundaries. H₀ counts path components. H₁ is the abelianization of π₁. For a surface of genus g, H₁ ≅ ℤ^{2g}. The Euler characteristic χ = Σ (−1)ⁿ rank(Hₙ). Cellular homology computes Hₙ from a CW structure — it's much more computable than singular homology. For a CW complex with cₙ n-cells, χ = Σ (−1)ⁿ cₙ.
Homology groups detect holes in every dimension — they're abelian, computable, and functorial.
Part 4

🌌 Cohomology and Poincaré Duality

The Dual Perspective
Cohomology — Cup Products and the Structure of Space
Cohomology Hⁿ(X) is the dual of homology: Hⁿ(X) = Hom(Hₙ(X), ℤ) (with more nuance). But cohomology has extra structure that homology lacks: the cup product ⌣: Hᵖ × H^q → H^{p+q}, making the cohomology of a space into a graded ring. The cup product captures how holes interact — it's the algebraic version of intersection of cycles. Poincaré duality says for a closed oriented n-manifold M, Hᵏ(M) ≅ H_{n−k}(M). For a surface of genus g, H₀ ≅ ℤ, H₁ ≅ ℤ^{2g}, H₂ ≅ ℤ, and Poincaré duality pairs H¹ with H₁. The Künneth formula computes Hₙ(X×Y) from Hₚ(X) and H_q(Y). Leray-Serre spectral sequences compute cohomology of fiber bundles. Modern algebraic topology extends these ideas to extraordinary cohomology theories like K-theory (vector bundles) and cobordism. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem connects the analysis of elliptic operators on a manifold to its topology — one of the deepest results of 20th-century mathematics.
Cohomology rings and Poincaré duality give a complete algebraic picture of a manifold's topology — the cup product tells us how cycles intersect.
