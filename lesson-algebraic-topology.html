Algebraic Topology — Attaching Algebraic Invariants to Spaces

Algebraic topology uses algebra to study topological spaces. It assigns algebraic objects — groups, rings, modules — to spaces in a way that's invariant under homeomorphism. This transforms geometric questions into algebraic ones. Two spaces are different if their algebraic invariants differ. The field began with Poincaré's 1895 Analysis Situs and has since grown into a vast web of connections between topology, algebra, and geometry.

"Algebraic topology is the art of turning topology into algebra and then solving the algebra."
— Dennis Sullivan
Part 1

🔄 Homotopy and the Fundamental Group

Loops and Deformations
The Fundamental Group — Detecting Holes via Loops
Two continuous maps f,g: X → Y are homotopic (f ≃ g) if one can be continuously deformed into the other. A homotopy is a map H: X×[0,1] → Y with H(x,0)=f(x), H(x,1)=g(x). The fundamental group π₁(X,x₀) has elements that are homotopy classes of loops (paths starting and ending at x₀). The group operation is concatenation of loops. For the circle S¹, π₁(S¹) ≅ ℤ — loops are classified by winding number. For S², π₁(S²) = 0 — every loop shrinks to a point (the sphere is simply connected). The fundamental group of the figure-8 is the free group on two generators F₂. The Seifert-van Kampen theorem computes π₁ of a union: if X = U ∪ V with U∩V path-connected, then π₁(X) is the amalgamated free product of π₁(U) and π₁(V) over π₁(U∩V). This is the main computational tool for fundamental groups.
The fundamental group captures 1-dimensional holes — loops that can't be shrunk to a point.
Part 2

🏗️ Covering Spaces — Unwinding the Structure

The Universal Cover
Covering Spaces — When a Space Unrolls Into Its Universal Version
A covering space of X is a space X̃ with a map p: X̃ → X such that every point x ∈ X has a neighborhood U evenly covered by p (p^{-1}(U) is a disjoint union of copies of U). The circle S¹ is covered by ℝ via p(t) = e^{2πit}. The universal cover of the torus is ℝ². Covering spaces are intimately tied to the fundamental group: for reasonable spaces, connected coverings correspond to subgroups of π₁. The universal cover X̃ is the simply connected covering (π₁(X̃) = 0). The deck transformation group of the universal cover is π₁(X). The lifting criterion: a map f: Y → X lifts to X̃ iff f_*(π₁(Y)) ⊆ p_*(π₁(X̃)). This connects covering space theory to Galois theory — the analogy is precise and leads to the étale fundamental group in algebraic geometry.
Covering spaces unwind the holes of a space into a simply connected version — the fundamental group acts as symmetries of the unrolling.
Part 3

🧮 Homology — Counting Holes Algebraically

The Groups Hₙ
Homology — Holes of Any Dimension
While the fundamental group detects 1-dimensional holes, homology groups Hₙ(X) detect holes in every dimension. A singular n-simplex is a continuous map σ: Δⁿ → X from the standard n-simplex. The boundary map ∂ₙ: Cₙ → C_{n−1} sends an n-simplex to the alternating sum of its (n−1)-dimensional faces. The singular chain complex is ... → C₂ → C₁ → C₀ → 0 with ∂_{n−1}∘∂ₙ = 0. Define cycles Zₙ = ker ∂ₙ, boundaries Bₙ = im ∂_{n+1}. The nth homology group Hₙ(X) = Zₙ / Bₙ captures n-dimensional cycles modulo boundaries. H₀ counts path components. H₁ is the abelianization of π₁. For a surface of genus g, H₁ ≅ ℤ^{2g}. The Euler characteristic χ = Σ (−1)ⁿ rank(Hₙ). Cellular homology computes Hₙ from a CW structure — it's much more computable than singular homology. For a CW complex with cₙ n-cells, χ = Σ (−1)ⁿ cₙ.
Homology groups detect holes in every dimension — they're abelian, computable, and functorial.
Part 4

🌌 Cohomology and Poincaré Duality

The Dual Perspective
Cohomology — Cup Products and the Structure of Space
Cohomology Hⁿ(X) is the dual of homology: Hⁿ(X) = Hom(Hₙ(X), ℤ) (with more nuance). But cohomology has extra structure that homology lacks: the cup product ⌣: Hᵖ × H^q → H^{p+q}, making the cohomology of a space into a graded ring. The cup product captures how holes interact — it's the algebraic version of intersection of cycles. Poincaré duality says for a closed oriented n-manifold M, Hᵏ(M) ≅ H_{n−k}(M). For a surface of genus g, H₀ ≅ ℤ, H₁ ≅ ℤ^{2g}, H₂ ≅ ℤ, and Poincaré duality pairs H¹ with H₁. The Künneth formula computes Hₙ(X×Y) from Hₚ(X) and H_q(Y). Leray-Serre spectral sequences compute cohomology of fiber bundles. Modern algebraic topology extends these ideas to extraordinary cohomology theories like K-theory (vector bundles) and cobordism. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem connects the analysis of elliptic operators on a manifold to its topology — one of the deepest results of 20th-century mathematics.
Cohomology rings and Poincaré duality give a complete algebraic picture of a manifold's topology — the cup product tells us how cycles intersect.

Higher Homotopy Groups and the Hurewicz Theorem

The fundamental group π₁(X, x₀) captures only 1-dimensional holes. For n ≥ 2, the higher homotopy groups πₙ(X, x₀) are defined as homotopy classes of maps (Sⁿ, s₀) → (X, x₀) where Sⁿ is the n-sphere with basepoint s₀. Unlike π₁, all higher homotopy groups are abelian — the Eckmann-Hilton argument shows that the two natural compositions (horizontal and vertical concatenation of n-cubes) coincide and are commutative. The groups πₙ(Sⁿ) ≅ ℤ for all n ≥ 1, generated by the identity map. However, πₙ(Sᵏ) for n > k are notoriously complex: π₃(S²) ≅ ℤ (the Hopf fibration), π₄(S³) ≅ ℤ/2, π₅(S³) ≅ ℤ/2, and the stable homotopy groups of spheres πₙ₊ₖ(Sⁿ) for large n form one of the central computational challenges of algebraic topology.

The Hurewicz theorem provides the fundamental bridge between homotopy and homology. For a path-connected space X, the Hurewicz homomorphism h: πₙ(X) → Hₙ(X) sends a homotopy class [f: Sⁿ → X] to the image of the fundamental class [Sⁿ] under f_*: Hₙ(Sⁿ) → Hₙ(X). The theorem states: if X is (n−1)-connected (i.e., πᵢ(X) = 0 for i < n), then h is an isomorphism for i = n and an epimorphism for i = n+1. In particular, for a simply connected space, π₂(X) ≅ H₂(X) and π₃(X) → H₃(X) is surjective. This allows computation of low-dimensional homotopy groups via homology, which is often more tractable. The relative Hurewicz theorem extends this to pairs (X, A) and is essential for the proof of the Whitehead theorem: a map between simply connected CW complexes inducing isomorphisms on all homotopy groups is a homotopy equivalence.

Postnikov towers decompose a space into its homotopy groups. A Postnikov tower for X is a sequence of spaces Xₙ with maps X → Xₙ inducing isomorphisms on πᵢ for i ≤ n and πᵢ(Xₙ) = 0 for i > n. Each stage Xₙ is obtained from Xₙ₋₁ by attaching cells of dimension ≥ n+2 to kill higher homotopy groups. The k-invariants (Postnikov invariants) are cohomology classes in Hⁿ⁺¹(Xₙ₋₁; πₙ(X)) that classify the fibration Xₙ → Xₙ₋₁. This reduces the classification of homotopy types to algebraic data: a sequence of groups πₙ and k-invariants. For simply connected spaces of finite type, this gives a complete algebraic model.

Fibrations, Cofibrations, and the Long Exact Sequence of Homotopy Groups

A fibration in the sense of Serre is a map p: E → B with the homotopy lifting property for all CW complexes: given a homotopy H: X × I → B and a lift of H₀: X → E, there exists a lift H̃: X × I → E extending the given lift. The fiber F = p⁻¹(b₀) over a basepoint b₀ ∈ B sits in a long exact sequence of homotopy groups: ... → πₙ₊₁(B) → πₙ(F) → πₙ(E) → πₙ(B) → πₙ₋₁(F) → ... This is the primary tool for computing homotopy groups of total spaces from fibers and bases, or vice versa. The connecting homomorphism πₙ₊₁(B) → πₙ(F) sends a map (Dⁿ⁺¹, ∂Dⁿ⁺¹) → (B, b₀) to the restriction of its lift to ∂Dⁿ⁺¹ ≅ Sⁿ, which lands in F.

Classic examples: the path-loop fibration ΩX → PX → X where PX is the space of paths starting at x₀ (contractible) and ΩX is the loop space. This gives πₙ(ΩX) ≅ πₙ₊₁(X). The Hopf fibrations S¹ → S³ → S², S³ → S⁷ → S⁴, S⁷ → S¹⁵ → S⁸ arise from division algebras (ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆) and yield π₃(S²) ≅ ℤ, π₇(S⁴) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/12, etc. The fibration SO(n) → SO(n+1) → Sⁿ gives πₙ(SO(n+1)) ≅ πₙ(SO(n)) ⊕ πₙ(Sⁿ) for n ≥ 3, leading to Bott periodicity: πₖ₊₈(O) ≅ πₖ(O) for the stable orthogonal group O = colim SO(n).

Cofibrations are the dual notion: a map i: A → X with the homotopy extension property. The mapping cone Cᵢ = X ∪ᵢ CA gives a cofibration sequence A → X → Cᵢ → ΣA → ΣX → ... where Σ is suspension. This induces a long exact sequence in homology and, for simply connected spaces, in homotopy. The Puppe sequence extends this to an infinite sequence of cofibrations. Fibrations and cofibrations form a model category structure on topological spaces (Quillen), where weak equivalences are weak homotopy equivalences. This abstract framework underlies modern homotopy theory and allows the construction of derived functors like Ext and Tor in topological contexts.

Worked Computation: π₃(S²) via the Hopf Fibration
Use the Hopf fibration S¹ → S³ → S² to compute π₃(S²).
The Hopf fibration is a fiber sequence S¹ → S³ → S². The long exact sequence in homotopy gives: ... → π₃(S¹) → π₃(S³) → π₃(S²) → π₂(S¹) → π₂(S³) → ... Since S¹ is a K(ℤ,1), πₙ(S¹) = 0 for n ≥ 2. Thus π₃(S¹) = 0 and π₂(S¹) = 0. Also π₂(S³) = 0 (S³ is 2-connected). The sequence reduces to 0 → π₃(S³) → π₃(S²) → 0. Since π₃(S³) ≅ ℤ (generated by the identity), the map is an isomorphism. Therefore π₃(S²) ≅ ℤ. The generator corresponds to the Hopf map η: S³ → S², which has Hopf invariant 1.
Exam Strategy: Long Exact Sequences
When a fibration F → E → B appears, immediately write the long exact sequence of homotopy groups. Identify which terms vanish (e.g., πₙ(S¹)=0 for n≥2, πₙ(Sᵏ)=0 for n<k). The connecting homomorphism often gives the only nontrivial map. For spheres, remember πₙ(Sⁿ)=ℤ and πₙ(Sᵏ)=0 for n<k. The Hopf fibrations S¹→S³→S², S³→S⁷→S⁴, S⁷→S¹⁵→S⁸ are the only sphere fibrations with sphere fibers and total spaces (Adams' theorem).</div>

Spectral Sequences — The Serre Spectral Sequence

Spectral sequences are the computational engine of modern algebraic topology. A spectral sequence is a sequence of differential graded modules (Eʳ, dʳ) with dʳ of bidegree (r, 1−r) such that Eʳ⁺¹ = H(Eʳ, dʳ). The Serre spectral sequence applies to a fibration F → E → B with B a path-connected CW complex and the action of π₁(B) on H*(F) trivial (e.g., B simply connected). It has E₂-page E₂p,q = Hᵖ(B; H^q(F)) converging to Hp+q(E). The differentials dʳ: Eʳp,q → Eʳp+r, q−r+1 measure the failure of cohomology classes on the base to lift to the total space, or the obstruction to extending classes from the fiber.

The filtration on E comes from the preimages of the skeleta of B: Fₚ = p⁻¹(B⁽ᵖ⁾). The associated graded pieces are H*(Fₚ, Fₚ₋₁) ≅ H*(B⁽ᵖ⁾, B⁽ᵖ⁻¹⁾; H*(F)) ≅ Cᵖ(B; H*(F)), giving the E₁-page. The E₂-page is the cohomology of this complex, i.e., H*(B; H*(F)). Convergence means there is a filtration on H*(E) whose associated graded is E_∞. The edge homomorphisms give the restriction H*(E) → H*(F) (q-axis) and the map H*(B) → H*(E) induced by a section (p-axis), when a section exists.

Applications: The Serre spectral sequence computes H*(ΩX) from the path-loop fibration ΩX → PX → X, yielding the loop space homology. For the Hopf fibration S¹ → S³ → S², E₂p,q = Hᵖ(S²; H^q(S¹)) has nonzero entries only at (0,0), (0,1), (2,0), (2,1). The differential d₂: E₂0,1 → E₂2,0 must be an isomorphism for convergence to H*(S³) (which is ℤ in degrees 0 and 3). This identifies the generator of H²(S²) as the transgression of the generator of H¹(S¹). The Leray-Serre spectral sequence generalizes to homology with local coefficients when π₁(B) acts nontrivially on H*(F). The Eilenberg-Moore spectral sequence computes H*(F) from H*(E) and H*(B) when E → B is a principal fibration.

Characteristic Classes of Vector Bundles

Characteristic classes measure the twisting of a vector bundle ξ: E → B. They are cohomology classes in H*(B) that are natural under pullback: f* c(ξ) = c(f*ξ). For real vector bundles, the Stiefel-Whitney classes wᵢ(ξ) ∈ Hⁱ(B; ℤ/2) are defined via the Thom isomorphism and the Wu formula. The total Stiefel-Whitney class w = 1 + w₁ + w₂ + ... satisfies w(ξ ⊕ η) = w(ξ) ⌣ w(η) (Whitney sum formula). The first class w₁ detects orientability: w₁(ξ) = 0 iff ξ is orientable. The second class w₂ detects spin structures: a manifold is spin iff w₂(TM) = 0. For the tangent bundle of ℝPⁿ, w(TℝPⁿ) = (1 + a)ⁿ⁺¹ where a generates H¹(ℝPⁿ; ℤ/2).

For complex vector bundles, Chern classes cᵢ(ξ) ∈ H²ⁱ(B; ℤ) are defined via the splitting principle: any bundle splits as a sum of line bundles after pulling back to a suitable space (the flag bundle). The total Chern class c = 1 + c₁ + c₂ + ... satisfies c(ξ ⊕ η) = c(ξ) ⌣ c(η). The top Chern class cₙ(ξ) is the Euler class of the underlying real 2n-bundle. For the tautological line bundle γ over ℂPⁿ, c₁(γ) = -x where x generates H²(ℂPⁿ; ℤ). The tangent bundle of ℂPⁿ has c(TℂPⁿ) = (1 + x)ⁿ⁺¹. Pontryagin classes pᵢ(ξ) ∈ H⁴ⁱ(B; ℤ) for real bundles are defined by pᵢ(ξ) = (-1)ⁱ c₂ᵢ(ξ ⊗ ℂ). They capture the "complexified" twisting and appear in the Hirzebruch signature theorem: the signature of a 4k-manifold is ⟨Lₖ(p₁, ..., pₖ), [M]⟩ where Lₖ is the Hirzebruch L-polynomial.

The Euler class e(ξ) ∈ Hⁿ(B; ℤ) for an oriented rank-n bundle is the obstruction to a nowhere-zero section. It satisfies e(ξ ⊕ η) = e(ξ) ⌣ e(η) and e(ξ) = 0 if ξ has a nonvanishing section. For the tangent bundle of a closed oriented manifold M, ⟨e(TM), [M]⟩ = χ(M) (Poincaré-Hopf). The Thom isomorphism H*(B) ≅ H*+n(E, E₀) (where E₀ is the complement of the zero section) gives the Thom class u ∈ Hⁿ(E, E₀) whose restriction to each fiber is the generator. The Euler class is the pullback of u by the zero section. The Gysin sequence for a sphere bundle S(ξ) → B relates H*(B) and H*(S(ξ)) via the Euler class.

Obstruction Theory and Postnikov Towers

Obstruction theory answers the question: given a map f: A → Y defined on a subcomplex A ⊂ X, can it be extended to X? Assume Y is simple (π₁(Y) acts trivially on πₙ(Y)). Build the extension skeleton by skeleton. On the (n+1)-skeleton, the obstruction to extending over an (n+1)-cell en+1 is the homotopy class of f on ∂en+1 ≅ Sⁿ, giving a cochain in Cn+1(X, A; πₙ(Y)). This cochain is a cocycle; its cohomology class on+1(f) ∈ Hn+1(X, A; πₙ(Y)) is the primary obstruction. If it vanishes, the extension exists on the (n+1)-skeleton, and one proceeds to the next dimension. The obstructions depend on choices made at lower stages; the indeterminacy is captured by the action of lower obstructions.

For lifting problems: given p: E → B a fibration with fiber F, and f: X → B, a lift f̃: X → E exists iff the obstructions in Hn+1(X; πₙ(F)) vanish. The first obstruction to a section of a fiber bundle is the primary obstruction class. For the tangent bundle of a manifold, the obstruction to a nonvanishing vector field is the Euler class. For a map f: X → Y between CW complexes, the obstruction to a homotopy equivalence is captured by the Whitehead torsion in Wh(π₁(X)).

Postnikov towers systematize this. The n-th Postnikov section Pₙ(X) has πᵢ(Pₙ(X)) = πᵢ(X) for i ≤ n and 0 for i > n. The map X → Pₙ(X) induces isomorphisms on πᵢ for i ≤ n. The fiber of Pₙ(X) → Pₙ₋₁(X) is a K(πₙ(X), n). The k-invariant kₙ ∈ Hn+1(Pₙ₋₁(X); πₙ(X)) classifies this fibration. A map f: X → Y induces maps between Postnikov towers, and the obstructions to lifting f to a map of towers are the k-invariants. For simply connected spaces of finite type, the Postnikov tower with k-invariants is a complete invariant of the homotopy type. The minimal model in rational homotopy theory (Sullivan) replaces k-invariants with a commutative differential graded algebra, making rational homotopy types computable via algebraic models.

  • Confusing the Hurewicz homomorphism direction: it goes πₙ → Hₙ, not the reverse. The isomorphism πₙ ≅ Hₙ holds only under (n−1)-connectedness.
  • Forgetting that the Serre spectral sequence requires trivial π₁(B)-action on H*(F) for the simple E₂-page H*(B; H*(F)). With nontrivial action, use local coefficients.
  • Misapplying the Whitney sum formula: w(ξ ⊕ η) = w(ξ)w(η) uses cup product, not addition. The total class is multiplicative.
  • Thinking Chern classes live in odd degrees: cᵢ ∈ H²ⁱ(B; ℤ). Pontryagin classes pᵢ ∈ H⁴ⁱ(B; ℤ). Stiefel-Whitney classes wᵢ ∈ Hⁱ(B; ℤ/2).
  • Overlooking that obstruction theory requires simple coefficients (trivial π₁-action). For non-simple spaces, obstructions live in twisted cohomology or require covering space techniques.
  • Confusing the Euler class (obstruction to a section of an oriented vector bundle) with the top Stiefel-Whitney class (mod 2 Euler class). They agree mod 2 but differ integrally.
  • Assuming all fibrations are fiber bundles: Serre fibrations have the homotopy lifting property for CW complexes, which is weaker than local triviality. The path-loop fibration is a Serre fibration but not a fiber bundle.
