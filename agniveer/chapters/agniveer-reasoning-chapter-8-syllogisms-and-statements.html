Chapter 8: Syllogisms and Statements

This chapter explains the fundamentals of logical reasoning, focusing on statements and syllogisms which are essential for the reasoning section of the Agniveer exam. It covers types of statements, rules for valid deductions, common pitfalls, and practice strategies.

Introduction

Statements are declarative sentences that can be classified as true or false, and syllogisms are logical arguments composed of two premises and a conclusion. Mastery of these concepts is essential for the reasoning section of the Agniveer exam.

Types of Statements

Categorical statements are classified into four types:

  • Universal affirmative (A) – “All A are B”
  • Universal negative (E) – “No A are B”
  • Particular affirmative (I) – “Some A are B”
  • Particular negative (O) – “Some A are not B”

Example: All dogs are mammals

Rules for Valid Syllogisms

The three fundamental rules for a valid syllogism are: (1) The middle term must be distributed at least once, (2) No term may be distributed in the conclusion unless it is distributed in the corresponding premise, (3) If a term is distributed in the conclusion, it must be distributed in the premise that contains it. Valid example: All A are B, All B are C ⇒ All A are C.

  • Distribute the middle term
  • Do not distribute a term in the conclusion without distribution in the premise
  • Follow the distribution rules for each term

Common Fallacies

Common fallacies that render a syllogism invalid include: Undistributed middle, Illicit major, Illicit minor, and Exclusive premises. Each of these violates the distribution rules outlined above.

  • Undistributed middle – middle term is not distributed in either premise
  • Illicit major – major term (predicate of conclusion) is distributed in the conclusion but not in the premise containing it
  • Illicit minor – minor term (subject of conclusion) is distributed in the conclusion but not in its premise
  • Exclusive premises – both premises are negative, which cannot yield a valid conclusion

Practice Strategies

To solve syllogism questions efficiently, first identify the premises and the conclusion, then check distribution of each term, and finally apply the valid forms such as Barbara (AAA), Darii (AII), etc. Using Venn diagrams or a quick checklist can help eliminate incorrect options.

  • Identify subject, predicate, and middle term
  • Check whether each term is distributed
  • Match the pattern with standard valid syllogistic forms
  • Eliminate options that introduce new terms or violate distribution

Quick tip: When in doubt, draw a simple Venn diagram to visualize the relationships.

Key Points to Remember

  • Statements are either true or false and form the basis of syllogistic reasoning.
  • Categorical statements are classified into A, E, I, O types based on quantity and quality.
  • Valid syllogisms follow specific distribution rules for the middle and other terms.
  • Common fallacies arise when distribution rules are violated.
  • Practice with standard forms and Venn diagrams to improve speed and accuracy.

Solved Examples

Example 1: All dogs are mammals. All mammals are animals. Therefore, all dogs are animals.

Solution: 1. Identify premises: (i) All dogs are mammals (A type). (ii) All mammals are animals (A type). 2. Check distribution: The middle term 'mammals' is distributed in the first premise (predicate of A) and in the second premise (subject of A) – thus distributed. 3. The conclusion 'all dogs are animals' has subject 'dogs' (undistributed) and predicate 'animals' (distributed in second premise). 4. Since the middle term is distributed and the conclusion follows the pattern AAA, the syllogism is valid.

Example 2: Some cats are black. All black animals are mammals. Therefore, some cats are mammals.

Solution: 1. Premises: (i) Some cats are black (I type). (ii) All black animals are mammals (A type). 2. The middle term 'black' is distributed in the second premise (predicate of A) and appears in the first premise (subject of I), so it is distributed. 3. The conclusion 'some cats are mammals' has subject 'cats' (undistributed) and predicate 'mammals' (distributed in second premise). 4. This matches the AII pattern, which is valid, so the syllogism is valid.

Example 3: All roses are flowers. Some flowers are red. Therefore, some roses are red.

Solution: 1. Premises: (i) All roses are flowers (A type). (ii) Some flowers are red (I type). 2. The middle term 'flowers' is not distributed in either premise (predicate of first is not distributed, subject of second is not distributed). 3. The conclusion 'some roses are red' has subject 'roses' (undistributed) and predicate 'red' (not distributed). 4. Because the middle term is undistributed, the syllogism commits the fallacy of undistributed middle and is invalid.

Practice Questions

Question 1Syllogisms and Statements
Which of the following is a universal affirmative statement?
A. Some dogs are black
B. All dogs are mammals
C. No dogs are mammals
D. Some dogs are not mammals
Answer: B
The universal affirmative form is 'All A are B'. Among the options, only 'All dogs are mammals' matches this pattern.
Question 2Syllogisms and Statements
In a valid syllogism, the middle term must be:
A. Undistributed
B. Distributed at least once
C. Absent from premises
D. Only in the conclusion
Answer: B
A valid syllogism requires the middle term to be distributed in at least one of the two premises.
Question 3Syllogisms and Statements
Identify the fallacy in the argument: 'All roses are flowers. Some flowers are red. Therefore, some roses are red.'
A. Undistributed middle
B. Illicit major
C. Valid
D. Exclusive premises
Answer: A
The middle term 'flowers' is not distributed in either premise, violating the rule that the middle term must be distributed. This is the undistributed middle fallacy.
Question 4Syllogisms and Statements
Which statement is particular negative?
A. All birds fly
B. No birds fly
C. Some birds do not fly
D. Some birds fly
Answer: C
'Some birds do not fly' is a particular negative (O) statement because it uses 'some' and 'not'.
Question 5Syllogisms and Statements
If the premises are 'All A are B' and 'No B are C', the conclusion 'No A are C' is:
A. Invalid
B. Valid
C. Contingent
D. Not derivable
Answer: B
From 'All A are B' and 'No B are C', we can infer 'No A are C' by transitivity of universal negative, making the conclusion valid.
Question 6Syllogisms and Statements
In the statement 'All dogs are mammals', the term 'dogs' is the:
A. Subject
B. Predicate
C. Middle term
D. Major term
Answer: A
'dogs' is the subject of the categorical proposition, which is the term about which the statement makes a claim.
Question 7Syllogisms and Statements
Which of the following syllogisms is invalid due to the fallacy of illicit major?
A. All cats are mammals. All mammals are animals. Therefore, all animals are cats.
B. All cats are mammals. Some mammals are pets. Therefore, some cats are pets.
C. No cats are mammals. All mammals are animals. Therefore, no cats are animals.
D. Some cats are mammals. All mammals are animals. Therefore, some cats are animals.
Answer: A
The major term 'animals' (predicate of the conclusion) is not distributed in any premise, violating the illicit major rule, thus the syllogism is invalid.
Question 8Syllogisms and Statements
The term that appears in both a premise and the conclusion of a categorical syllogism is called the:
A. Minor term
B. Major term
C. Middle term
D. Distributed term
Answer: C
The term common to a premise and the conclusion is the middle term, which links the subject and predicate.
Question 9Syllogisms and Statements
From the premises 'Some A are B' and 'All B are C', the valid conclusion is:
A. All A are C
B. No A are C
C. Some A are C
D. Some C are A
Answer: C
The particular affirmative premise combined with the universal affirmative yields a particular affirmative conclusion: 'Some A are C'.
