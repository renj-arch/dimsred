Chapter 5: Blood Relations and Family Trees

This chapter covers the fundamentals of blood relations, methods to construct family trees, and strategies to solve related aptitude questions frequently asked in the Agniveer exam.

Introduction

The Blood Relations and Family Tree chapter forms an integral part of the reasoning section in the Agniveer recruitment exam. Understanding kinship patterns helps in quickly decoding complex family scenarios presented in multiple‑choice questions.

Blood Relations Basics

Key familial terms include mother, father, son, daughter, brother, sister, uncle, aunt, cousin. These relationships can be categorized into direct (parent‑child) and collateral (siblings, cousins).

  • Direct line: Each generation is one step away.
  • Collateral: Relations within the same generation.
The gender‑specific terms are crucial for accurate interpretation.

Family Tree Construction

To build a family tree, start with the oldest known generation at the top and expand downward. Use a systematic approach:

  1. Identify the reference person.
  2. Mark parents above and children below.
  3. Connect siblings horizontally.

ParentsFather, Mother
ChildrenBrother, Sister

Solving Complex Relations

Complex questions often involve multiple generations and gender clues. Use the following logic flow:

  • Step 1: Identify the reference individual.
  • Step 2: Trace upward to locate parents/grandparents.
  • Step 3: Move laterally to siblings or cousins.
  • Step 4: Apply gender indicators (e.g., “brother of” vs “sister of”).

Remember the rule: Brother of father = paternal uncle and Sister of mother = maternal aunt.

Tips and Common Mistakes

Effective strategies for the Agniveer exam include:

  • Sketch quickly: Draw a mini‑tree on the answer sheet.
  • Focus on gender: Misreading “brother” as “sister” leads to wrong answers.
  • Eliminate impossible options using generational gaps.
  • Avoid assuming relationships not explicitly stated.
Regular practice with varied examples improves speed and accuracy.

Key Points to Remember

  • Direct relations are parent‑child; collateral relations are siblings, cousins, uncles, aunts.
  • Always note gender-specific terms (brother, sister, uncle, aunt).
  • Start family‑tree construction from the oldest known generation.
  • Use upward tracing (parents) then lateral movement (siblings) for complex queries.
  • Sketch a quick mini‑tree to visualise relationships and avoid errors.

Solved Examples

Example 1: If X is the brother of Y and Y is the mother of Z, how is X related to Z?

Solution: Y is the mother of Z, and X is Y's brother. A brother of one's mother is the maternal uncle. Hence, X is the maternal uncle of Z.

Example 2: In a family, P is the only child of Q. R is Q's brother. How is P related to R?

Solution: Q's brother is R, making R the uncle of P. Therefore, P is the niece/nephew of R (gender‑neutral in the question).

Example 3: A is the son of B. C is the sister of D. D is the father of B. How is A related to C?

Solution: D is the father of B, so B is D's child. C is the sister of D, making C B's aunt (paternal aunt). Since A is B's son, A is the niece/nephew of C.
