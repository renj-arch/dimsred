Mastering Percentage: The Foundation of Maths

Welcome to this comprehensive lesson on Percentage, a critical topic for the mathematics sections. This concept is not merely about calculation; it forms the bedrock for solving complex problems involving profit-loss, simple interest, and data interpretation. By mastering the logic behind percentages, you will be able to tackle questions that appear deceptively difficult in the exam hall.

Key Concepts

Basic Definition: Percentage literally means "per hundred," serving as a standardized way to compare ratios by converting them into fractions with a denominator of 100. The fundamental formula is: Percentage = (Part / Whole) × 100. Memorizing simple fraction equivalents is essential for speed; specifically, 1/2 equals 50%, 1/4 equals 25%, 1/5 equals 20%, and 1/8 equals 12.5%.

Percentage Change: This concept measures how much a value has grown or shrunk relative to its starting point, which is why the denominator must always be the original number. The formula for percentage increase is (New Value - Original Value) / Original Value × 100, while for decrease, it is (Original Value - New Value) / Original Value × 100.

Successive Percentage Change: When a value undergoes two or more percentage changes in sequence, the total change is not simply the sum of the individual percentages because the second change applies to the already modified value. The exact mathematical rule for two successive changes of x% and y% is: Total % = x + y + (xy/100). For instance, a 10% increase followed by a 10% decrease results in a net change of 10 + (-10) + (10 × -10 / 100), which equals -1%, meaning a 1% overall decrease.

Profit and Loss: In commerce, profit is the gain made over the cost price, while loss is the deficit. The standard formula for Profit % is (Profit / Cost Price) × 100, and similarly for Loss %. A crucial rule to remember is that if a trader sells an item at a certain percentage above its cost price, that percentage figure directly represents the profit percentage.

Solved Examples

Example 1: Finding a Specific Percentage
What is 15% of 300?

a) 40 b) 45 c) 50 d) 55
Solution: The correct option is b) 45.

Step-by-step reasoning:
To find a percentage of a number, we convert the percentage into a fraction and multiply it by the number.
1. Convert 15% to a fraction: 15/100.
2. Multiply by the given number (300): (15/100) × 300.
3. Simplify the calculation: The zeros cancel out, leaving 15 × 3.
4. Result: 45.

Technique to reuse: Always simplify fractions before multiplying large numbers. Recognizing that 15% is 1.5 times 10% helps too (10% of 300 is 30; 20% of 300 is 60; so 15% is exactly halfway between, which is 45).
Example 2: Working Backwards with Successive Change
A number increased by 20% becomes 240. Find the original number.

a) 180 b) 190 c) 200 d) 220
Solution: The correct option is c) 200.

Step-by-step reasoning:
This problem requires reversing the percentage change to find the original value.
1. Let the original number be x.
2. An increase of 20% means the new value is 120% of the original (100% + 20%).
3. We write this as a fraction: 120/100.
4. The equation becomes: x × (120/100) = 240.
5. To isolate x, divide 240 by the fraction (or multiply by its reciprocal): x = 240 × (100/120).
6. Simplify: 240 divided by 120 is 2. So, x = 2 × 100.
7. Result: 200.

Technique to reuse: Always convert "increased by" or "decreased by" into a multiplier fraction (e.g., +20% becomes 120/100) before solving algebraically.

Common Traps

Avoid these frequent mistakes that lead to incorrect answers in the exam:

  • Mixing up Base Values: Students often calculate percentage change using the new value as the denominator instead of the original. Always ask: "What is this compared to?" The answer is always the starting number.
  • The Successive Addition Fallacy: Adding two percentages (e.g., 10% + 10% = 20%) without accounting for the interaction term (xy/100) leads to wrong results. The actual change is always less than the sum of individual increases or more than the difference in decreases.
  • Ignoring Cost Price vs. Selling Price: In profit-loss questions, ensure you are dividing by the Cost Price (CP), not the Selling Price (SP). Dividing by SP gives the wrong percentage.

Memory Tricks

Use these shortcuts to solve problems faster without a calculator:

  • The "10-20" Rule: Remember that 10% is simply moving the decimal point one place left (e.g., 10% of 500 is 50). Therefore, 20% is double that, and 5% is half of 10%. You can build any percentage from these three building blocks.
  • Successive Change Shortcut: If you see two equal percentage changes (one up, one down), remember the net result is always a decrease. For example, +x% followed by -x% results in a net decrease of x²/100. A 20% rise and 20% fall equals a net drop of 4%
  • Population vs. Depreciation: Think of population growth as "adding" to the value (multiplying by >1) and depreciation as "subtracting" (multiplying by <1). The formula structure remains the same: P(1 ± r/100)^n.
Exam-Day Strategy
On exam day, do not waste time converting percentages to decimals unless necessary. Instead, simplify fractions immediately (e.g., change 25% to 1/4). If a question involves successive changes and the numbers are round, use the formula x + y + xy/100. However, if the numbers are complex, estimate the answer first to eliminate impossible options. Always verify your final answer by checking if it makes logical sense (e.g., a profit cannot be negative).

Successive Percentage Change with Three or More Stages

When a value is subjected to three successive percentage changes of x%, y% and z%, the overall multiplier is the product of the three individual multipliers: (1 + x/100)(1 + y/100)(1 + z/100). Expanding this product gives the exact net percentage change:

Net % = x + y + z + (xy + yz + zx)/100 + xyz/10000.

The derivation follows directly from algebraic expansion. The first‑order terms (x, y, z) represent the simple sum of the three changes. The second‑order terms (xy, yz, zx) capture the interaction between each pair of changes, and the third‑order term (xyz) accounts for the simultaneous interaction of all three. This formula reduces to the familiar two‑stage version when one of the percentages is zero.

A useful special case occurs when the three changes are equal, say each is p%. Then the net change simplifies to 3p + 3p²/100 + p³/10000. For small p (≤10) the cubic term is negligible, and the quadratic term already shows why three successive 10% increases yield a net increase of 33.1% rather than 30%.

In examinations, questions often present a scenario such as “price increased by 20%, then decreased by 10%, then increased by 5%”. Applying the formula directly avoids the error of adding the percentages (20‑10+5 = 15%) and yields the correct net change of 20‑10+5 + (20×‑10 + ‑10×5 + 5×20)/100 + (20×‑10×5)/10000 = 15 + (‑200‑50+100)/100 + (‑1000)/10000 = 15‑1.5‑0.1 = 13.4%.

When the number of stages exceeds three, the pattern continues: the net percentage equals the sum of all first‑order terms, plus the sum of all distinct pairwise products divided by 100, plus the sum of all distinct triple products divided by 10000, and so on. For practical purposes, most test items stop at three stages because the arithmetic remains manageable without a calculator.

Percentage Change in Compound Interest and Depreciation

Compound interest is the canonical example of successive percentage increase applied repeatedly over equal periods. If a principal P earns an annual rate r% compounded yearly, after n years the amount is A = P(1 + r/100)ⁿ. This is exactly the result of applying an r% increase n times in succession. The net percentage increase over the whole period is therefore (1 + r/100)ⁿ − 1, expressed as a percentage.

Depreciation follows the same mathematics with a negative rate. An asset losing d% of its value each year has a multiplier (1 − d/100) per year, and after n years its value is V = V₀(1 − d/100)ⁿ. The overall percentage decrease is 1 − (1 − d/100)ⁿ.

A frequent twist in questions is to ask for the equivalent simple interest rate that would produce the same final amount over the same period. Equating P(1 + r/100)ⁿ = P(1 + R/100)ⁿ (where R is the simple rate per period) shows that no constant simple rate can match compound growth for n>1; instead, the effective simple rate for the whole period is [(1 + r/100)ⁿ − 1] × 100.

Another common variant mixes compounding frequencies: “interest compounded half‑yearly at 12% per annum”. The per‑period rate becomes 6% and the number of periods doubles, so the amount is P(1 + 6/100)²ⁿ. Recognising that the nominal annual rate must be divided by the number of compounding periods per year is essential.

When the rate changes from year to year, the product of the yearly multipliers replaces the single‑rate power. For example, 10% in year 1, 12% in year 2, and 8% in year 3 gives a multiplier (1.10)(1.12)(1.08). The net percentage change can be obtained by expanding this product or by using the three‑stage formula with x=10, y=12, z=8.

Worked Example: Compound Interest with Varying Rates
A sum of 50,000 is invested for three years. The interest rates are 8% for the first year, 10% for the second year, and 12% for the third year, compounded annually. Find the amount at the end of three years.

Step 1: Write the yearly multipliers.

Year 1: 1 + 8/100 = 1.08

Year 2: 1 + 10/100 = 1.10

Year 3: 1 + 12/100 = 1.12

Step 2: Multiply the principal by all three multipliers.

Amount = 50,000 × 1.08 × 1.10 × 1.12

Step 3: Simplify stepwise.

50,000 × 1.08 = 54,000

54,000 × 1.10 = 59,400

59,400 × 1.12 = 66,528

Answer: 66,528.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When the number of years is small (≤3), multiply the yearly multipliers manually; it is faster than evaluating a power. For larger n with a constant rate, use the approximation (1 + r/100)ⁿ ≈ 1 + nr/100 + n(n‑1)r²/20000 to estimate the amount and eliminate distant options before exact calculation.

Profit‑Loss with Discount and Marked Price

In commercial arithmetic the marked price (MP) is the price tagged on an article before any discount. A discount of d% reduces the marked price to the selling price (SP) according to SP = MP × (1 − d/100). Profit or loss is always reckoned on the cost price (CP). Therefore profit% = (SP − CP)/CP × 100 and loss% = (CP − SP)/CP × 100.

A typical problem gives the marked price, the discount, and either the profit% or loss%, asking for the cost price. The chain of relations is CP → (profit%) → SP → (discount%) → MP. Reversing the chain: CP = SP / (1 ± profit%/100) and SP = MP × (1 − d/100). Combining gives CP = MP × (1 − d/100) / (1 ± profit%/100).

Successive discounts are another favourite. Two successive discounts of d₁% and d₂% are equivalent to a single discount of d₁ + d₂ − d₁d₂/100 %. The order does not matter because multiplication of the multipliers (1 − d₁/100)(1 − d₂/100) is commutative. However, a discount followed by a markup (or vice‑versa) is not symmetric; the net multiplier becomes (1 − d/100)(1 + m/100).

When a trader marks goods at a certain percentage above cost price and then allows a discount, the effective profit% can be found directly: if MP = CP × (1 + a/100) and discount = d%, then SP = CP × (1 + a/100)(1 − d/100). The profit% = [(1 + a/100)(1 − d/100) − 1] × 100. This formula avoids computing intermediate values and is a staple of high‑difficulty questions.

Another twist involves “cash discount” offered on the selling price for immediate payment. The cash discount is applied after the trade discount, so the final amount received is SP × (1 − cash%/100). The overall profit% must be calculated on CP using this final amount.

Percentage in Mixture and Alligation Problems

Mixture problems often present two or more components with different concentrations (expressed as percentages) that are combined to obtain a target concentration. The alligation rule provides a quick way to find the ratio in which the components must be mixed. If component A has concentration C₁% and component B has concentration C₂% (with C₁ > C₂), and the desired mixture concentration is Cₘ%, then the ratio of quantities (A : B) = (Cₘ − C₂) : (C₁ − Cₘ). This follows from the weighted‑average equation: (q₁C₁ + q₂C₂)/(q₁+q₂) = Cₘ.

The rule extends to three components by applying it pairwise, but examination questions rarely go beyond two components. When the problem gives the total quantity of the mixture and asks for the quantity of each component, first find the ratio, then split the total in that ratio.

A common variation replaces concentration with “percentage of profit” or “percentage of alcohol”. The mathematics is identical; only the wording changes. For instance, “How many litres of a 30% alcohol solution must be added to 20 litres of a 50% solution to obtain a 40% solution?” Here C₁=50, C₂=30, Cₘ=40, giving ratio (40‑30):(50‑40) = 10:10 = 1:1. Hence 20 litres of the 30% solution are needed.

When the mixture involves removal and replacement (e.g., “10 litres of milk are drawn from a 40‑litre container and replaced with water, repeated twice”), the concentration after each operation follows a geometric progression. If the initial concentration is 100% (pure milk) and each operation replaces a fraction f of the mixture with water, the milk concentration after n operations is (1 − f)ⁿ × 100%. This is a direct application of successive percentage decrease.

Alligation can also be used in “cost‑price” mixture problems: two varieties of rice costing ₹30/kg and ₹40/kg are mixed to sell at ₹36/kg with a 20% profit. First find the cost price of the mixture: SP = ₹36, profit% = 20 ⇒ CP = 36/(1.20) = ₹30/kg. Then alligate 30 and 40 to get 30 ⇒ ratio (30‑30):(40‑30) = 0:10, meaning only the cheaper variety is used – a signal that the data may be inconsistent or that the profit% is on the selling price rather than cost price. Recognising such inconsistencies is a key skill.

  • Assuming the net percentage change for three stages equals the arithmetic sum of the three percentages.
  • Treating compound interest as simple interest when the number of periods exceeds one.
  • Applying discount on cost price instead of marked price in profit‑loss questions.
  • Using the wrong base (selling price instead of cost price) for profit or loss percentage.
  • Ignoring the interaction term d₁d₂/100 when combining two successive discounts.
  • Misplacing the concentrations in the alligation diagram, leading to an inverted ratio.
  • Review these traps before the exam.

Percentage Points versus Percent Change in Comparative Statements

When two percentages are compared, the difference can be expressed either in percentage points or as a percent change. A percentage‑point difference is the simple arithmetic subtraction of the two rates (e.g., 12 % − 8 % = 4 percentage points). A percent change, however, measures the relative increase or decrease with respect to the original rate: ((new − old) / old) × 100. Confusing the two leads to gross misinterpretation of data in tables, graphs, and word problems. For instance, if a tax rate rises from 10 % to 12 %, the increase is 2 percentage points but a 20 % rise relative to the original 10 %. In examination questions, the phrase “increased by 2 %” almost always means a 2 percentage‑point rise, whereas “increased by 2 % of the original rate” signals a relative change. The distinction becomes critical when the base itself is a percentage, such as profit margins, interest rates, or concentration levels.

Consider a scenario where a company’s profit margin improves from 15 % to 18 %. The margin has risen by 3 percentage points. The percent improvement is (3 / 15) × 100 = 20 %. If a question asks “by what percent did the profit margin increase?” the answer is 20 %, not 3 %. Conversely, “by how many percentage points did the margin increase?” expects 3. Examiners often embed both phrasings in the same data‑interpretation set to test careful reading. A reliable technique is to underline the words “percentage points” or “percent” in the stem and then decide which denominator applies: the original percentage value for percent change, or none for points.

Another common twist involves successive changes expressed in points versus percent. Suppose a discount series is described as “first a 10 % discount, then an additional 5 % discount”. The second discount is usually applied on the reduced price, i.e., a 5 % relative reduction. If the wording changes to “first a 10 % discount, then an additional 5 percentage‑point discount”, the second reduction is 5 % of the original marked price, not of the already discounted price. The net multiplier in the first case is (1 − 0.10)(1 − 0.05) = 0.855, while in the second it is (1 − 0.10 − 0.05) = 0.85. The difference, though small, changes the final selling price and consequently the profit or loss percentage. Practising both interpretations side by side builds the habit of checking the exact language before setting up the equation.

In data‑interpretation tables, columns may show “Year‑1: 22 %”, “Year‑2: 25 %”. A question could ask “What is the percentage increase in the rate?” The correct computation is ((25 − 22) / 22) × 100 ≈ 13.64 %. If the question instead asks “What is the increase in percentage points?” the answer is simply 3. Misreading this distinction is a frequent source of avoidable errors, especially under time pressure. A quick mental check: if the answer choices contain both a small integer (like 3) and a decimal (like 13.6), the integer usually corresponds to points and the decimal to percent change.

Reverse Percentage Problems and Original Value Recovery

Reverse percentage problems ask for the original quantity before a known percentage increase or decrease produced a given final value. The core principle is that the final value equals the original multiplied by the appropriate multiplier (1 ± r/100). To recover the original, divide the final value by that multiplier. For a 20 % increase leading to a final amount of 120, the original is 120 / 1.20 = 100. For a 15 % decrease yielding 85, the original is 85 / 0.85 = 100. The difficulty rises when the percentage change itself is unknown and must be deduced from two successive stages, or when the final value is expressed as a percentage of another quantity.

A typical multi‑stage reverse problem: “After a 10 % increase followed by a 20 % decrease, the price becomes ₹ 792. Find the original price.” The combined multiplier is 1.10 × 0.80 = 0.88. Original = 792 / 0.88 = ₹ 900. If the order were reversed (20 % decrease then 10 % increase), the multiplier is 0.80 × 1.10 = 0.88 — identical because multiplication commutes. However, when the percentages are applied to different bases (e.g., a markup on cost price followed by a discount on marked price), the multipliers are not interchangeable. In such cases, write the chain explicitly: CP → MP = CP × (1 + a/100) → SP = MP × (1 − d/100). Then SP = CP × (1 + a/100)(1 − d/100). Given SP and one of a or d, solve for CP.

Another variant gives the final value as a percentage of the original: “The population of a town increased by 12 % in the first year and decreased by 8 % in the second year. If the population at the end of the second year is 102,960, what was the population at the beginning?” Let the initial population be P. After first year: P × 1.12. After second year: P × 1.12 × 0.92 = 102,960. Hence P = 102,960 / (1.12 × 0.92) = 100,000. The key is to multiply the successive multipliers before dividing.

When the percentage change is not given directly but hidden in a statement like “the new price is 15 % more than the old price”, the multiplier is 1.15. If the statement reads “the old price is 15 % less than the new price”, the relationship is old = new × 0.85, so new = old / 0.85. The base of the percentage matters: “A is 20 % more than B” means A = 1.20 B; “B is 20 % less than A” means B = 0.80 A. These two statements are not equivalent. A reliable habit is to translate every comparative phrase into an algebraic equation before substituting numbers.

Technique: Reverse Chain with Mixed Mark‑up and Discount
A shopkeeper marks his goods 40 % above cost price and then allows a discount of 15 % on the marked price. If the selling price is ₹ 2,380, find the cost price.
Let cost price = CP. Marked price = CP × (1 + 40/100) = CP × 1.40. Selling price = MP × (1 − 15/100) = CP × 1.40 × 0.85 = CP × 1.19. Given SP = ₹ 2,380, CP = 2,380 / 1.19 = ₹ 2,000. Answer: ₹ 2,000.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a reverse‑percentage question appears, first write the full multiplier chain (e.g., 1.40 × 0.85). Multiply the factors once to obtain a single combined multiplier. Then divide the given final value by this combined multiplier. This single‑step division avoids intermediate rounding errors and saves precious seconds.

Profit‑Loss with False Weights and Measurement Fraud

False‑weight problems introduce a deceptive element: the trader uses a scale that shows a weight different from the actual quantity delivered. The profit or loss percentage must be calculated on the basis of the actual cost incurred versus the actual revenue received. If a seller claims to sell 1 kg but actually delivers only 900 g, the effective cost price for the claimed 1 kg is the cost of 900 g, while the selling price is charged for 1 kg. The profit percentage = ((SP − effective CP) / effective CP) × 100. This framework also works when the buyer uses a false measure to receive more than paid for.

Consider a dealer who professes to sell sugar at cost price but uses a weight of 800 g for every kilogram. Let the cost price of 1 kg be ₹ 100. The dealer actually gives 800 g, costing him ₹ 80, but charges ₹ 100 (the price of 1 kg). Profit = ₹ 20 on an outlay of ₹ 80 → profit% = (20 / 80) × 100 = 25 %. The same result can be obtained by the shortcut formula: profit% = ((true weight − false weight) / false weight) × 100. Here (1000 − 800) / 800 × 100 = 25 %. This shortcut assumes the selling price equals the cost price of the claimed quantity; if a separate markup or discount is also present, combine the multipliers.

When both a markup and a false weight operate together, the chain becomes: CP (per actual gram) → marked price per claimed gram → selling price after discount → effective revenue per actual gram delivered. For example, a trader marks goods 20 % above cost price, gives a 10 % discount, and uses a 900 g weight for 1 kg. Let CP of 1 kg = ₹ 100. MP = ₹ 120. SP after discount = ₹ 108 per claimed kg. Actual quantity delivered = 900 g, so revenue per actual kg = 108 × (1000/900) = ₹ 120. Effective CP per actual kg = ₹ 100. Profit% = (120 − 100) / 100 × 100 = 20 %. The combined effect of markup, discount, and false weight can be captured by a single multiplier: (1 + markup/100)(1 − discount/100)(true weight/false weight).

A less common but examinable twist involves the buyer using a false measure. If a buyer purchases 1 kg at the seller’s price but his scale reads 1.1 kg for every actual kilogram, he receives 1.1 kg for the price of 1 kg. His gain% = ((1.1 − 1) / 1) × 100 = 10 %. When both parties cheat, the net effect multiplies: seller’s false weight factor × buyer’s false weight factor. Always identify who controls the transaction price and who controls the quantity delivered.

Successive Mark‑up and Discount Chains with Variable Bases

In many commercial scenarios, a product passes through several hands, each applying a markup or discount on its own cost base. The overall percentage change from the original manufacturer’s cost to the final consumer price is not the arithmetic sum of the individual percentages because each stage uses a different base. The correct method is to multiply the successive multipliers. For a chain of three stages: manufacturer → wholesaler (markup a %), wholesaler → retailer (markup b %), retailer → consumer (discount c %). The final price = initial cost × (1 + a/100) × (1 + b/100) × (1 − c/100). The overall percentage change = [final multiplier − 1] × 100.

Consider a product with a manufacturing cost of ₹ 500. The wholesaler adds 20 %, the retailer adds 25 %, and the retailer offers a 10 % discount to the customer. Final price = 500 × 1.20 × 1.25 × 0.90 = 500 × 1.35 = ₹ 675. Overall increase = (675 − 500) / 500 × 100 = 35 %. Notice that 20 % + 25 % − 10 % = 35 % only by coincidence; the interaction terms (20 % × 25 % etc.) are automatically handled by multiplication. If the discount were applied on the wholesaler’s price instead of the retailer’s marked price, the chain would be 500 × 1.20 × 0.90 × 1.25 = 500 × 1.35 = same result because multiplication commutes. However, if a stage involves a loss (negative markup), the multiplier becomes (1 − loss/100) and the same multiplication rule holds.

A frequent examiner twist is to give the final selling price and ask for the original cost, or to give the overall profit percentage and ask for one of the intermediate percentages. In such cases, set up the multiplier equation and solve for the unknown. For instance, “A manufacturer sells to a wholesaler at a 15 % profit. The wholesaler sells to a retailer at a 20 % profit. The retailer sells to a customer at a 10 % discount on his marked price, which was 30 % above his cost. If the customer pays ₹ 1,200, find the manufacturer’s cost.” Let manufacturer cost = C. Wholesaler cost = C × 1.15. Retailer cost = C × 1.15 × 1.20. Retailer marked price = retailer cost × 1.30. Customer price = marked price × 0.90 = C × 1.15 × 1.20 × 1.30 × 0.90 = C × 1.6146. Hence C = 1,200 / 1.6146 ≈ ₹ 743.3. Rounding to the nearest rupee is acceptable unless the question demands exact fraction.

When the chain includes a “cash discount” offered on the final selling price for immediate payment, treat it as an additional multiplier (1 − cash %/100) applied after all other stages. The overall multiplier then includes this factor. The order of cash discount relative to other discounts does not matter because it multiplies the same final amount, but if a cash discount is offered on the marked price before a trade discount, the bases differ and the order matters. Always read the wording: “cash discount on the selling price” versus “cash discount on the marked price”.

Weighted Average and Alligation with Three Components

Alligation with two components is standard, but competitive examinations occasionally extend the concept to three components. The principle remains the weighted average: (q₁C₁ + q₂C₂ + q₃C₃) / (q₁ + q₂ + q₃) = Cₘ. When three concentrations C₁ > C₂ > C₃ are mixed to obtain Cₘ, there are infinitely many ratios (q₁ : q₂ : q₃) satisfying the equation. Examination questions therefore provide an additional constraint, such as the ratio of two components, the total quantity of one component, or the cost prices of the components with a target selling price and profit percentage.

A typical three‑component problem: “Three varieties of tea costing ₹ 120/kg, ₹ 150/kg, and ₹ 180/kg are mixed in the ratio 2 : 3 : 5. The mixture is sold at ₹ 176/kg with a 10 % profit. Verify whether the given ratio is consistent.” First find the cost price of the mixture per kg: SP = ₹ 176, profit% = 10 ⇒ CP = 176 / 1.10 = ₹ 160/kg. Next compute the weighted average cost of the given ratio: (2×120 + 3×150 + 5×180) / (2+3+5) = (240 + 450 + 900) / 10 = 1590 / 10 = ₹ 159/kg. Since ₹ 159 ≠ ₹ 160, the ratio is not consistent with the stated selling price and profit; either the ratio or the profit percentage is mis‑stated. Such inconsistency detection is a valued skill.

When the ratio is unknown but two of the three quantities are given, set up the weighted‑average equation and solve for the third quantity. For example, “How many kg of tea at ₹ 120/kg must be mixed with 10 kg at ₹ 150/kg and 5 kg at ₹ 180/kg to obtain a mixture costing ₹ 155/kg?” Let x be the required kg. Equation: (120x + 150×10 + 180×5) / (x + 10 + 5) = 155. Solve: 120x + 1500 + 900 = 155(x + 15) → 120x + 2400 = 155x + 2325 → 35x = 75 → x = 75/35 = 15/7 ≈ 2.14 kg. The answer may be left as a fraction.

Alligation can also be applied pairwise to reduce a three‑component problem to two‑component steps. Choose any two components, find the ratio in which they must be mixed to reach an intermediate concentration that, when combined with the third component, yields the target. This method is algebraically equivalent but sometimes faster when the numbers are friendly. However, the direct weighted‑average equation is universally reliable and avoids the risk of choosing an inconvenient intermediate concentration.

  • Confusing percentage‑point difference with percent change when the base is itself a percentage.
  • Applying the reverse multiplier in the wrong order when mark‑up and discount bases differ.
  • Using the false‑weight shortcut without adjusting for simultaneous markup or discount.
  • Adding successive markup and discount percentages arithmetically instead of multiplying their multipliers.
  • Assuming a unique ratio exists for three‑component alligation without an extra constraint.
  • Placing the cash‑discount multiplier before a trade discount when the problem states it is on the selling price.
