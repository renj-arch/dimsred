The Number System: Your Gateway to Mathematics

The number system is the bedrock of arithmetic, providing the language and rules necessary to understand everything from basic addition to complex algebraic problems. For candidates appearing in competitive exams, mastery of this topic is not optional; it is mandatory for securing a high score in the quantitative aptitude section. This lesson will systematically break down divisibility rules, the relationship between LCM and HCF, and essential simplification techniques that you must know by heart.

Divisibility Rules

A number is divisible by another if it can be divided without leaving a remainder. Instead of performing long division for every question, you should memorize these specific patterns which apply instantly to large numbers. The rule for 2 states that any number ending in an even digit (0, 2, 4, 6, 8) is divisible by 2. For 3 and 9, the trick involves summing all digits; if the sum is divisible by 3 or 9 respectively, so is the original number. The rule for 4 depends only on the last two digits of the number being divisible by 4. For 5, the number must end in 0 or 5. A number is divisible by 6 if it satisfies both rules for 2 and 3 simultaneously. For 8, check if the number formed by the last three digits is divisible by 8. The rule for 11 requires you to find the difference between the sum of digits at odd positions and even positions; if this difference is zero or a multiple of 11, the number is divisible.

LCM & HCF

The Highest Common Factor (HCF) is the largest number that divides two or more numbers exactly, while the Least Common Multiple (LCM) is the smallest number that is exactly divisible by two or more numbers. To find these, you must first understand prime factorization, which breaks a number down into its simplest building blocks (primes). The formula for HCF involves taking only the common prime factors of the given numbers and multiplying them together with their lowest powers. Conversely, to find the LCM, you take all the prime factors present in any of the numbers, multiplying them with their highest powers. A critical relationship connects these two concepts: for any two numbers, the product of the numbers is always equal to the product of their HCF and LCM. This means if you know three out of four values (Number 1, Number 2, HCF, or LCM), you can calculate the missing one.

Simplification

Simplification refers to reducing complex mathematical expressions into manageable parts before solving them. In competitive exams, questions often involve fractions mixed with decimals or large numbers that look intimidating at first glance. The strategy is to simplify the numerator and denominator separately before dividing. For example, if you have a fraction like 40/160, do not divide immediately; instead, notice that both are divisible by 40, reducing it instantly to 1/4. Similarly, when dealing with decimals, try to convert them into fractions or multiply the entire expression by powers of 10 to remove the decimal points entirely.

Solved Examples

Example 1: Finding HCF using Prime Factors
Find the HCF of 24, 36, and 60.

a) 6 b) 8 c) 12 d) 18
Solution: c) 12. First, we break each number down into its prime factors: 24 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3; 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3; and 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5. To find the HCF, we look for the factors that appear in all three lists. The common factors are two 2s and one 3. Therefore, HCF = 2 × 2 × 3 = 12.
Example 2: Finding LCM using Prime Factors
Find the LCM of 12, 15, and 18.

a) 120 b) 150 c) 160 d) 180
Solution: d) 180. We start with prime factorization: 12 = 2² × 3¹; 15 = 2⁰ × 3¹ × 5¹; and 18 = 2¹ × 3². To find the LCM, we take the highest power of every prime number present. The highest power of 2 is 2² (from 12 or 18), the highest power of 3 is 3² (from 18), and the highest power of 5 is 5¹ (from 15). Multiplying these gives: 4 × 9 × 5 = 180.

Common Traps

Students often fall into specific traps that lead to incorrect answers during the exam. One major mistake is confusing the definitions of LCM and HCF; remember that HCF is always smaller than or equal to the smallest number in the set, while LCM is always greater than or equal to the largest number. Another common error is forgetting that 1 is a factor of every integer but is not a prime number. Students also frequently make calculation errors when applying the divisibility rule for 11 by adding digits incorrectly. Finally, many candidates ignore the relationship Product = LCM × HCF and try to solve problems from scratch rather than using this shortcut.

Memory Tricks

To retain these rules quickly, use these mnemonics and comparisons. For the cyclicity of unit digits in powers (like 2ⁿ or 7ⁿ), remember the cycle length is always 4 for most numbers; visualize a clock with 4 hours. For divisibility by 9, think "Nine" sounds like "New," implying you start fresh with the sum of digits. A helpful comparison for LCM and HCF is to think of HCF as the "Common Ground" (shared factors) and LCM as the "Meeting Point" where everyone arrives together (common multiple). When checking divisibility by 7, use the mnemonic "Double and Subtract": take the last digit, double it, and subtract from the rest.

Exam-Day Strategy
On exam day, do not spend more than 1-2 minutes on a number system question. If you get stuck on a divisibility rule, estimate the answer by checking the last digit or sum of digits to eliminate options immediately. Always verify your LCM and HCF answers using the product formula if time permits. Practice solving problems without a calculator to build speed and accuracy, as calculators are not allowed in competitive exams.

Remainder Theorem and Modular Arithmetic Applications

The remainder theorem states that for any integers a and b (with b > 0) there exist unique integers q (quotient) and r (remainder) such that a = bq + r and 0 ≤ r < b. In modular notation this is written as a ≡ r (mod b). The power of modular arithmetic lies in its compatibility with the basic operations: if a ≡ r₁ (mod m) and c ≡ r₂ (mod m) then a + c ≡ r₁ + r₂ (mod m), a − c ≡ r₁ − r₂ (mod m), and a × c ≡ r₁ × r₂ (mod m). Exponentiation follows the same rule, allowing large powers to be reduced by repeatedly applying the modulus. For a prime modulus p, Fermat’s little theorem gives a^(p−1) ≡ 1 (mod p) when a is not a multiple of p. Euler’s theorem generalises this to any modulus m using Euler’s totient function φ(m): a^φ(m) ≡ 1 (mod m) for coprime a and m. These results turn otherwise impossible calculations — such as the remainder of 2100 divided by 7 — into a few lines of work: φ(7)=6, 100 = 6×16 + 4, so 2100 ≡ (26)16 × 24 ≡ 116 × 16 ≡ 2 (mod 7). Modular arithmetic also underpins the solution of linear congruences, the Chinese Remainder Theorem, and many divisibility shortcuts used in competitive testing.

Cyclicity of Unit Digits in Powers

The unit digit of a positive integer raised to successive powers follows a repeating cycle. For bases ending in 0, 1, 5, or 6 the cycle length is 1 because the unit digit never changes. For bases ending in 4 or 9 the cycle length is 2 (4 → 6 → 4 … ; 9 → 1 → 9 …). For the remaining digits 2, 3, 7, and 8 the cycle length is 4. The complete cycles are: 2 → 4 → 8 → 6; 3 → 9 → 7 → 1; 7 → 9 → 3 → 1; 8 → 4 → 2 → 6. To find the unit digit of a^n, identify the unit digit of a, determine its cycle, compute n mod (cycle length), and pick the corresponding element (using the cycle length itself when the remainder is 0). For example, the unit digit of 72023: the cycle for 7 is length 4; 2023 mod 4 = 3, so the third element of the cycle (7 → 9 → 3 → 1) is 3. This technique eliminates the need for any large‑scale multiplication and is a staple of time‑bound arithmetic sections.

Worked Example: Unit Digit of a Large Power
Find the unit digit of 1247.
The unit digit of the base 12 is 2. The cycle for 2 is 2 → 4 → 8 → 6 (length 4). Compute 47 mod 4 = 3. The third element in the cycle is 8. Therefore the unit digit of 1247 is 8.
Exam‑Day Tip for Cyclicity
Memorise the four‑digit cycles for 2, 3, 7, 8 and the two‑digit cycles for 4, 9. When a question asks for the last digit of a huge exponent, write the base’s unit digit, its cycle length, and the exponent modulo that length. This three‑step routine takes under ten seconds and avoids any multiplication.

Properties of Consecutive Integers and Factorials

The product of any k consecutive integers is always divisible by k!. This follows because the binomial coefficient C(n, k) = n! / (k! (n−k)!) is an integer, and its numerator contains the product of k consecutive numbers. A direct consequence: among any three consecutive integers one is a multiple of 3, and at least one is even, so the product is a multiple of 6. For factorials, Legendre’s formula gives the exponent of a prime p in n! as Σ floor(n / p^i) for i = 1,2,… until p^i > n. This is the standard method to count trailing zeros in n! (use p=5). For instance, zeros in 100! = floor(100/5) + floor(100/25) = 20 + 4 = 24. Factorials also satisfy n! = n × (n−1)!, which is useful for simplifying expressions like (n+1)! / n! = n+1. In problems involving divisibility by a factorial, rewrite the given product as a binomial coefficient multiplied by the factorial to see the divisibility instantly.

Advanced LCM‑HCF Problems with Fractions and Decimals

The definitions of LCM and HCF extend naturally to rational numbers. For fractions in lowest terms, LCM( a/b , c/d ) = LCM(a, c) / HCF(b, d) and HCF( a/b , c/d ) = HCF(a, c) / LCM(b, d). The logic: a common multiple of the fractions must be an integer multiple of each, so its numerator must be a common multiple of the numerators while its denominator must divide both denominators — hence the denominator is the HCF of the denominators. Conversely, a common divisor of the fractions must divide each numerator and be a multiple of each denominator, giving the HCF of numerators over the LCM of denominators. For decimals, convert each to a fraction with a power‑of‑10 denominator, reduce to lowest terms, then apply the fraction formulas. Example: LCM of 0.6 and 0.15. Write 0.6 = 6/10 = 3/5, 0.15 = 15/100 = 3/20. LCM = LCM(3,3) / HCF(5,20) = 3 / 5 = 0.6. HCF = HCF(3,3) / LCM(5,20) = 3 / 20 = 0.15. These rules also solve “find the greatest number that divides 1.2, 2.4, and 3.6 leaving the same remainder” by converting to fractions and using HCF of the differences.

Chinese Remainder Theorem Basics for Competitive Exams

The Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) states that a system of simultaneous congruences x ≡ a₁ (mod m₁), x ≡ a₂ (mod m₂), …, x ≡ a_k (mod m_k) has a unique solution modulo M = m₁ m₂ … m_k provided the moduli are pairwise coprime. The constructive proof builds the solution as x = Σ a_i × M_i × y_i (mod M), where M_i = M / m_i and y_i is the modular inverse of M_i modulo m_i (i.e., M_i × y_i ≡ 1 (mod m_i)). For small moduli the inverse can be found by inspection or the extended Euclidean algorithm. Example: solve x ≡ 2 (mod 3), x ≡ 3 (mod 5), x ≡ 2 (mod 7). Here M = 105. M₁ = 35, inverse of 35 mod 3 is 2 (since 35×2=70≡1). M₂ = 21, inverse of 21 mod 5 is 1 (21≡1). M₃ = 15, inverse of 15 mod 7 is 1 (15≡1). Then x = 2×35×2 + 3×21×1 + 2×15×1 = 140 + 63 + 30 = 233 ≡ 23 (mod 105). The smallest positive solution is 23. CRT is invaluable for problems that ask for a number satisfying several remainder conditions, and it also underpins many cryptographic algorithms.

  • Assuming the cyclicity length is always 4; digits 0,1,5,6 have length 1 and 4,9 have length 2.
  • Forgetting to reduce the exponent modulo the cycle length and using the raw exponent instead.
  • Applying the fraction LCM/HCF formulas without first reducing fractions to lowest terms.
  • Treating decimal numbers as integers when computing LCM or HCF, ignoring the power‑of‑10 factor.
  • Using the Chinese Remainder Theorem when the moduli are not pairwise coprime; the theorem does not guarantee a solution in that case.
  • Neglecting negative remainders in modular arithmetic; always convert a negative remainder to its positive equivalent by adding the modulus.
  • Misapplying Legendre’s formula by stopping the summation too early or using the wrong prime for trailing‑zero problems.

Mastering these advanced techniques will significantly improve speed and accuracy in number system problems.

Base Conversion and Arithmetic in Non‑Decimal Bases

Any integer can be expressed in a positional numeral system with base b (b ≥ 2). The digits range from 0 to b−1, and the value of a digit string dkdk‑1…d1d0 equals Σ di·bi. Converting from base b to decimal uses this expansion directly. For the reverse direction, repeatedly divide the decimal number by b and collect remainders; the remainders read backwards give the base‑b representation. This algorithm works for any base, including binary (b=2), octal (b=8), and hexadecimal (b=16).

Arithmetic in non‑decimal bases follows the same column‑wise rules as decimal, but the carry threshold is the base. In binary addition, 1+1 = 102 (carry 1). In hexadecimal, digits A–F represent 10–15; for example, B16 + 716 = 1216 because 11+7=18 = 1·16+2. Subtraction uses borrowing of the base value. Multiplication and long division are analogous; the multiplication table for the base must be known or derived on the fly.

Base conversion is frequently tested through problems that ask for the value of a number given in an unknown base, or that require solving equations where the base itself is a variable. A typical strategy: write the polynomial equation in the unknown base, then use the fact that each digit must be less than the base to bound the base. For instance, if (32)b = (23)10, then 3b+2 = 23 → b = 7, which is valid because digits 3 and 2 are < 7.

Another common theme is the representation of fractions. A terminating expansion in base b occurs exactly when the denominator (in lowest terms) has no prime factors other than those of b. In decimal (b=10=2·5) only denominators of the form 2a5b terminate. In binary, only powers of two terminate. This property explains why 0.110 becomes a repeating binary fraction.

Conversion shortcuts

  • Binary ↔ Octal: group binary digits in threes from the radix point.
  • Binary ↔ Hexadecimal: group binary digits in fours.
  • Octal ↔ Hexadecimal: convert via binary as an intermediate.

These groupings work because 8 = 23 and 16 = 24. They reduce transcription errors and speed up calculations in competitive settings.

Digital Root, Casting Out Nines, and Divisibility by 9 and 11

The digital root of a positive integer is obtained by repeatedly summing its decimal digits until a single digit remains. Equivalently, the digital root of n is 1 + ((n−1) mod 9), with the convention that a multiple of 9 has digital root 9. This follows from the fact that 10 ≡ 1 (mod 9), so any power of 10 is congruent to 1 modulo 9, making the sum of digits congruent to the original number modulo 9.

Casting out nines is a quick verification technique for addition, subtraction, and multiplication. Compute the digital root of each operand, perform the same operation on the digital roots, and compare the result with the digital root of the claimed answer. A mismatch guarantees an error; a match does not prove correctness but catches many slips.

Divisibility by 9: a number is divisible by 9 iff its digit sum (or digital root) is a multiple of 9. Divisibility by 11 uses the alternating sum of digits: starting from the rightmost digit, add and subtract alternately. If the resulting alternating sum is a multiple of 11 (including 0), the original number is divisible by 11. This works because 10 ≡ −1 (mod 11), so 10k ≡ (−1)k (mod 11).

These rules extend to other bases. In base b, the test for divisibility by (b−1) is the sum of digits; the test for (b+1) is the alternating sum. For example, in hexadecimal, divisibility by 15 (F16) uses digit sum, and divisibility by 17 (1116) uses alternating sum.

Practical applications

  • Checking large multiplications without a calculator.
  • Finding missing digits in a number known to be a multiple of 9 or 11.
  • Simplifying remainder problems: n mod 9 equals digital root (with 9→0).

When a problem asks for the remainder of a huge number upon division by 9 or 11, replace the number by its digit sum or alternating sum respectively, then reduce modulo the divisor. This reduces the computation to a few seconds.

Worked Example: Digital Root and Missing Digit
A 7‑digit number 3 2 x 5 7 4 1 is known to be divisible by 9. Find the digit x.
The sum of the known digits is 3+2+5+7+4+1 = 22. For the whole number to be a multiple of 9, the total digit sum must be a multiple of 9. The next multiple of 9 after 22 is 27, so x = 27 − 22 = 5. (If the sum were already a multiple of 9, x would be 0 or 9, but a single digit forces x = 0.) Therefore x = 5.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Digit‑Sum Tests
When a question asks for a remainder modulo 9 or 11, write down the digit sum or alternating sum immediately. Do not attempt to divide the original large number. For modulo 9, remember that a digital root of 9 corresponds to remainder 0. For modulo 11, compute the alternating sum from the rightmost digit; if the result is negative, add 11 until it lies in 0…10. This two‑step routine eliminates arithmetic errors and fits comfortably within the time limit.

Euler’s Totient Function, Fermat’s Little Theorem, and Euler’s Theorem

Euler’s totient function φ(n) counts the positive integers ≤ n that are coprime to n. For a prime power pk, φ(pk) = pk − pk‑1 = pk‑1(p−1). Since φ is multiplicative for coprime arguments, if n = ∏ piai then φ(n) = ∏ piai‑1(pi−1). This formula allows rapid computation of φ for any integer once its prime factorisation is known.

Fermat’s Little Theorem states that if p is prime and a is not divisible by p, then ap‑1 ≡ 1 (mod p). A corollary: ap ≡ a (mod p) for all integers a. Euler’s Theorem generalises this: if gcd(a,n)=1 then aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n). The exponent φ(n) is the order of the multiplicative group modulo n. These theorems are the foundation for reducing large exponents in modular arithmetic.

To compute am mod n when gcd(a,n)=1, reduce the exponent m modulo φ(n): am ≡ am mod φ(n) (mod n). If m < φ(n) no reduction is needed. When gcd(a,n) ≠ 1, Euler’s theorem does not apply directly; one can factor n, apply the theorem to coprime parts, and recombine via the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

Carmichael function

The Carmichael function λ(n) gives the smallest positive exponent such that aλ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) for all a coprime to n. For prime powers, λ(pk) = φ(pk) except when p=2 and k≥3, where λ(2k) = 2k‑2. For general n, λ(n) = lcm(λ(p1a1), …). Using λ(n) instead of φ(n) often yields a smaller exponent and faster reduction.

Typical problem patterns

  • Find the last two digits of 7100 (mod 100). Since φ(100)=40, 740≡1, so 7100≡720. Compute 720 by repeated squaring.
  • Determine the remainder of 21000 divided by 101 (prime). By Fermat, 2100≡1, so 21000≡1.
  • Solve ax ≡ b (mod n) when a and n are coprime: reduce exponent using φ(n) or λ(n).

Mastery of these theorems turns seemingly impossible exponentiation into a handful of modular multiplications.

Number of Factors, Sum of Factors, and Product of Factors of an Integer

Let n = ∏ piai be the prime factorisation. The number of positive divisors (factors) of n is τ(n) = ∏ (ai+1). Each divisor corresponds to a choice of exponent ei with 0 ≤ ei ≤ ai for each prime.

The sum of all positive divisors is σ(n) = ∏ (piai+1−1)/(pi−1). This formula comes from the geometric series 1 + p + p2 + … + pa for each prime factor, multiplied across primes because divisors are formed by independent choices.

The product of all positive divisors of n equals nτ(n)/2. Reason: divisors can be paired as d and n/d; each pair multiplies to n. There are τ(n)/2 such pairs (if τ(n) is odd, n is a perfect square and the middle divisor is √n, which pairs with itself). Hence the product is n raised to the number of pairs.

Applications

  • Find the number of factors of 360 = 23·32·51. τ = (3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 24.
  • Sum of factors of 360: σ = (24−1)/(2−1) × (33−1)/(3−1) × (52−1)/(5−1) = 15 × 13 × 6 = 1170.
  • Product of factors of 360: 36024/2 = 36012.

Problems often ask for the number of factors of a factorial, e.g., τ(100!). Use Legendre’s formula to get exponents of each prime in 100!, then apply τ formula. Another common twist: given τ(n) and σ(n), reconstruct n or find possible n. This requires solving a system of equations in the exponents ai.

Odd and even factor counts

τ(n) is odd iff n is a perfect square (all ai even). The number of odd factors of n is obtained by ignoring the power of 2 in the factorisation. The number of even factors is τ(n) − τ(odd part). These distinctions appear in questions that separate odd/even divisors.

Solving Linear Congruences and Modular Inverses

A linear congruence a x ≡ b (mod m) has solutions iff gcd(a,m) divides b. Let d = gcd(a,m). If d ∤ b, no solution exists. If d | b, divide the congruence by d: (a/d) x ≡ (b/d) (mod m/d). Now gcd(a/d, m/d) = 1, so a/d has a modular inverse modulo m/d. The unique solution modulo m/d is x ≡ (a/d)−1·(b/d) (mod m/d). The full solution set modulo m consists of d residues spaced by m/d.

The modular inverse of a modulo m (with gcd(a,m)=1) can be found via the extended Euclidean algorithm, which yields integers u,v such that a u + m v = 1. Then u is the inverse of a modulo m. For small moduli, inspection or a pre‑computed table works. For example, the inverse of 3 modulo 11 is 4 because 3·4 = 12 ≡ 1 (mod 11).

When the modulus is prime p, every non‑zero a has an inverse, and a−1 ≡ ap‑2 (mod p) by Fermat’s Little Theorem. This provides a fast exponentiation method for inverses when p is large but exponentiation is feasible.

Systems of linear congruences

A system ai x ≡ bi (mod mi) can be solved by reducing each to x ≡ ci (mod mi) (provided gcd(ai,mi) | bi) and then applying the Chinese Remainder Theorem if the moduli are pairwise coprime. If moduli share factors, first check consistency: the congruences must agree on the shared prime‑power components. Inconsistent systems have no solution.

Typical exam questions

  • Find the smallest positive integer x such that 7x ≡ 5 (mod 12). gcd(7,12)=1, inverse of 7 mod 12 is 7 (since 7·7=49≡1). x ≡ 7·5 = 35 ≡ 11 (mod 12). Answer 11.
  • Solve 6x ≡ 9 (mod 15). gcd(6,15)=3 divides 9. Divide: 2x ≡ 3 (mod 5). Inverse of 2 mod 5 is 3. x ≡ 3·3 = 9 ≡ 4 (mod 5). Solutions modulo 15: 4, 9, 14.
  • Find the inverse of 17 modulo 101. Since 101 is prime, 1799 mod 101 gives the inverse. Compute by repeated squaring.

Proficiency in these techniques allows rapid resolution of remainder problems, cryptarithms, and many “find the number” puzzles that reduce to linear congruences.

  • Assuming φ(n) = n−1 for composite n; this holds only for primes.
  • Using Euler’s theorem when gcd(a,n) ≠ 1 without separating the common factor.
  • Forgetting that the modular inverse exists only when the base and modulus are coprime.
  • Misapplying the divisor‑count formula by adding exponents instead of multiplying (ai+1).
  • Treating the alternating‑sum test for 11 as starting from the leftmost digit; it must start from the rightmost.
  • Overlooking that the product‑of‑divisors formula nτ(n)/2 requires τ(n) to be even unless n is a perfect square, in which case the exponent is still τ(n)/2 (a half‑integer exponent yields an integer because n is a square).
  • Neglecting to reduce fractions to lowest terms before applying LCM/HCF formulas for rational numbers.

Consistent practice of these advanced concepts builds the speed and accuracy needed for high‑stakes quantitative sections.
