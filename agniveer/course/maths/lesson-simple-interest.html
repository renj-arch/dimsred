Simple & Compound Interest for the exam & the exam

Interest is the cost of borrowing money or the return on invested capital. In competitive exams, you will frequently encounter problems involving simple interest and basic compound interest calculations at a 10th standard level. To master this topic, you must understand the core concepts of principal, rate, time, and amount, as well as the specific formulas that govern these financial relationships.

Simple Interest: The Linear Approach

Simple Interest (SI) is calculated only on the original sum borrowed or invested, known as the Principal. Unlike compound interest, the interest earned does not add to the principal for future calculations; it remains constant every year. The formula is derived from the logic that interest is a fixed percentage of the principal for every unit of time.

The exact mathematical rule is: SI = (P × R × T) / 100. Here, P stands for Principal, R is the Rate of interest per annum, and T is the Time in years. The Amount you get back or owe is simply the sum of the Principal and the Simple Interest calculated.

Examiners typically ask questions where you are given three of these four variables (P, R, T, SI) and must find the fourth. Sometimes, they ask for the total Amount instead of just the interest, in which case you simply add the calculated SI to the Principal.

Compound Interest: The Exponential Growth

Compound Interest (CI) is often described as "interest on interest." In this method, the interest earned at the end of a period is added to the principal, so the next period's interest is calculated on this new, larger amount. This causes the growth to be exponential rather than linear.

The fundamental formula for the Amount in compound interest is: Amount = P(1 + R/100)^T. Consequently, Compound Interest itself is found by subtracting the Principal from this Amount: CI = Amount - P.

A crucial pattern often tested in competitive exams involves comparing Simple and Compound interest over a period of exactly two years. The difference between the two interests at the end of 2 years is mathematically equal to the simple interest earned on the first year's interest for the second year. The specific formula for this difference is: CI - SI = P(R/100)². This rule is a direct consequence of the compounding mechanism.

Solved Examples

Example 1: Mastering Simple Interest Calculation
Find the Simple Interest on ₹1000 at 8% per annum for 3 years.

a) ₹200 b) ₹220 c) ₹240 d) ₹260
Solution: The correct answer is option (c).

We use the formula SI = (P × R × T) / 100.
Substituting the values: P = 1000, R = 8, and T = 3.
SI = (1000 × 8 × 3) / 100
SI = (24000) / 100
SI = ₹240.

The total Amount would be Principal + SI = 1000 + 240 = ₹1240.
Example 2: The Two-Year Difference Trick
A sum of money amounts to ₹880 in 2 years at 5% per annum compound interest. What is the Simple Interest on this sum for the same period?

a) ₹40 b) ₹45 c) ₹50 d) ₹55
Solution: The correct answer is option (c).

First, find the Principal using the Compound Amount formula: Amount = P(1 + R/100)^T.
880 = P(1 + 5/100)²
880 = P(1.05)²
880 = P × 1.1025
P = 880 / 1.1025 ≈ ₹798 (approx).

A simpler way for exams is to calculate SI directly on the Principal derived from CI logic or use the difference formula if CI was given.

Common Traps

Students often fall into specific traps that lead to incorrect answers in competitive exams:

  • Mixing up SI and CI formulas: Applying the compound interest formula (P(1+R/100)^T) when the question explicitly asks for Simple Interest. Remember, SI is linear; CI is exponential.
  • Ignoring the "Per Annum" unit: If time is given in months, you must convert it to years by dividing by 12 before using the formula. Using months directly in the T variable will give a wrong answer.
  • The Two-Year Difference Error: Students often try to use the (CI - SI) formula for periods longer than 2 years or forget that this specific shortcut only works for exactly two years.
  • Calculation Errors with Decimals: In compound interest, squaring the rate (1+R/100)^2 introduces decimals. Always calculate the square of the decimal part first to maintain accuracy.

Memory Tricks

To recall these concepts quickly during the exam without panic:

  • The "PRT" Mantra: For Simple Interest, always remember the letters P, R, and T. If you see three of them, multiply them and divide by 100. It is a multiplication party.
  • The "Snowball Effect": Think of Compound Interest like a snowball rolling down a hill. The bigger it gets (principal increases), the faster it grows (interest increases). This visual helps remember that CI > SI over time.
  • The "Square Root" Rule: If you see a question with exactly 2 years and asks for the difference between CI and SI, immediately think of the square of the rate: P(R/100)². It's like a square shape appearing after two steps.
  • Units Check: Before multiplying, always check if Time is in months or days. If so, convert to years first. This prevents the most common calculation error.
Exam-Day Strategy
On exam day, read the question twice. First, identify if it asks for Principal, Interest, or Amount. Second, check the time unit (years vs months). If you see "2 years" and a difference between CI and SI is mentioned, use the shortcut formula immediately to save time. For Simple Interest problems, always write down P, R, T separately before multiplying to avoid confusion with the order of operations.

Variable Compounding Frequencies and Effective Annual Rate

When interest is compounded more frequently than once a year, the nominal annual rate no longer reflects the true growth of the principal. The standard formula Amount = P(1 + R/100)^T assumes annual compounding. If compounding occurs n times per year, the periodic rate becomes R/n percent and the number of periods becomes n × T. The generalized amount formula is therefore Amount = P(1 + R/(100n))^(nT). This adjustment captures the effect of interest being added to the principal multiple times within a single year, causing the effective yield to exceed the nominal rate.

The Effective Annual Rate (EAR) quantifies this difference. It is the single annual rate that would produce the same final amount if compounding were annual. Mathematically, EAR = (1 + R/(100n))^n - 1, expressed as a percentage by multiplying by 100. For example, a nominal rate of 12 % compounded monthly (n = 12) yields an EAR of (1 + 0.12/12)12 - 1 ≈ 0.1268, i.e. 12.68 %. The EAR is the figure that should be used when comparing financial products with different compounding frequencies, because it places them on a common annual basis.

In competitive examinations, questions often provide a nominal rate and a compounding frequency, then ask for the amount after a given time or the effective rate itself. A common shortcut is to compute the periodic factor (1 + R/(100n)) once, raise it to the power nT using logarithms or repeated squaring, and multiply by the principal. When the time T is not an integer number of years, convert the total number of periods directly: if the investment runs for 18 months and compounding is quarterly, the number of periods is 6 (since 18 months = 1.5 years, n = 4, periods = 4 × 1.5 = 6). Always verify that the exponent matches the total number of compounding intervals.

Another twist involves continuous compounding, where the limit as n → ∞ gives the formula Amount = P × e^(RT/100). Here e is the base of natural logarithms (≈ 2.71828). The corresponding effective annual rate becomes e^(R/100) - 1. Although continuous compounding is rare in standard banking, it appears in theoretical problems and in the valuation of certain derivatives. Recognising the continuous‑compounding formula and its derivation from the discrete formula is a useful differentiator for high‑difficulty questions.

Finally, be aware of the distinction between nominal rate (the quoted annual percentage) and periodic rate (the rate applied each compounding interval). The periodic rate is always R/n percent. When a problem states “12 % per annum compounded half‑yearly”, the periodic rate is 6 % per half‑year, not 12 % per half‑year. Misreading this detail is a frequent source of error.

Installment and Loan Amortisation Using Compound Interest

Many real‑world loans are repaid through equal periodic installments (EMIs) rather than a single lump sum. The present value of an annuity formula, derived from compound interest, gives the relationship between the loan principal P, the periodic installment X, the periodic interest rate i = R/(100n), and the total number of installments N = nT. The formula is P = X × [1 - (1 + i)^(-N)] / i. Rearranging yields the installment amount X = P × i / [1 - (1 + i)^(-N)]. This equation assumes that the first installment is paid one period after the loan is disbursed (ordinary annuity). If the first payment is immediate (annuity due), the denominator becomes [1 - (1 + i)^(-N)] / i × (1 + i).

To solve an EMI problem, first identify the compounding frequency, which usually matches the payment frequency (monthly payments → monthly compounding). Convert the annual nominal rate to the periodic rate i. Compute the total number of payments N. Then apply the formula. For example, a loan of ₹10 000 at 12 % per annum compounded monthly, to be repaid in 24 monthly installments, gives i = 0.12/12 = 0.01 and N = 24. The installment is X = 10000 × 0.01 / [1 - (1.01)^(-24)]. Evaluating (1.01)^(-24) ≈ 0.787, the denominator ≈ 0.213, so X ≈ 10000 × 0.01 / 0.213 ≈ ₹469.5. Rounding conventions vary; some exams expect the answer to the nearest rupee.

A related concept is the outstanding balance after k payments. The remaining principal equals the present value of the remaining N - k installments: Balance_k = X × [1 - (1 + i)^(-(N-k))] / i. This is useful for questions that ask for the principal repaid in a specific installment or the interest component of a particular payment. The interest portion of the k‑th installment is i × Balancek-1, and the principal repaid is X - interest.

When the installment amount is given and the principal or rate is unknown, the same formula can be solved iteratively or by using logarithms. For instance, if P, X, and N are known, the equation P = X × [1 - (1 + i)^(-N)] / i can be rearranged to isolate (1 + i)^(-N) and then solved for i using numerical methods (trial and error, interpolation, or a financial calculator). In exam settings, the rate is often a simple fraction (e.g., 10 %, 12 %) so that trial values quickly converge.

Another variation involves a balloon payment at the end of the term. The present value of the balloon payment B discounted at the periodic rate is added to the present value of the regular installments: P = X × [1 - (1 + i)^(-N)] / i + B × (1 + i)^(-N). This structure appears in certain hire‑purchase agreements and must be recognised to avoid treating the balloon as a regular installment.

Worked Example: Monthly EMI Calculation
A borrower takes a loan of ₹25 000 at 9 % per annum compounded monthly. The loan is to be repaid in 36 equal monthly installments. Find the monthly installment amount (rounded to the nearest rupee).

Step 1: Identify parameters. Principal P = 25000. Annual nominal rate R = 9 %. Compounding monthly ⇒ n = 12. Periodic rate i = R/(100n) = 9/(100×12) = 0.0075. Number of installments N = 36.

Step 2: Apply the ordinary annuity formula X = P × i / [1 - (1 + i)^(-N)].

Compute (1 + i) = 1.0075. Raise to power -N: (1.0075)^(-36). Using logarithms or a calculator, (1.0075)36 ≈ 1.309, so its reciprocal ≈ 0.764.

Denominator = 1 - 0.764 = 0.236.

Numerator = P × i = 25000 × 0.0075 = 187.5.

Installment X = 187.5 / 0.236 ≈ 794.5. Rounded to nearest rupee ⇒ ₹795.

Answer: ₹795 per month.

Exam‑Day Strategy for Installment Problems
When you see a loan repaid in equal installments, immediately write down the four variables P, i, N, X. Convert the annual rate to the periodic rate before doing any exponentiation. If the question asks for the outstanding balance after a certain number of payments, use the present‑value‑of‑remaining‑installments formula rather than recalculating the whole amortisation schedule. For balloon‑payment variants, treat the final lump sum as a separate present‑value term. Practise the shortcut (1+i)^N using the binomial approximation (1 + Ni) only when Ni is very small; otherwise use exact exponentiation to avoid cumulative error.

Growth of Principal under Mixed Simple and Compound Regimes

Some problems describe a scenario where a sum earns simple interest for an initial period and then switches to compound interest, or vice‑versa. The key is to treat each phase separately, using the amount at the end of one phase as the principal for the next. For instance, if ₹P is invested at R % simple interest for T₁ years, the amount becomes A₁ = P + (P × R × T₁)/100 = P[1 + (R T₁)/100]. This amount then serves as the principal for a compound‑interest phase of T₂ years at the same or a different rate R₂. The final amount is A₂ = A₁ × (1 + R₂/100)^T₂. The overall effective growth factor is the product of the simple‑interest factor and the compound‑interest factor.

A common twist is to ask for the equivalent single rate that would produce the same final amount if the whole period were under compound interest. Let the total time be T = T₁ + T₂. We need a rate R_eq such that P(1 + R_eq/100)^T = A₂. Solving gives R_eq = 100 × [(A₂/P)^(1/T) - 1]. This requires extracting a T‑th root, which can be done using logarithms: log(1 + R_eq/100) = (1/T) × log(A₂/P). In exam conditions, the numbers are often chosen so that the root is an integer or a simple fraction, allowing mental calculation.

Another variant involves a principal that earns compound interest for a certain period, after which the interest is withdrawn and the remaining principal continues at simple interest. Here the compound phase builds the amount A_c = P(1 + R/100)^T_c. The interest earned up to that point is I_c = A_c - P. If this interest is withdrawn, the principal reverts to P for the simple‑interest phase. The final amount becomes A_f = P + (P × R × T_s)/100. The total interest earned overall is I_c + (P × R × T_s)/100. Recognising whether the interest is reinvested or withdrawn changes the compounding base and is a frequent source of misinterpretation.

Problems may also combine different rates for the two phases. For example, a sum earns 8 % simple interest for 3 years, then 10 % compound interest for 2 years. Compute the simple‑interest amount: A₁ = P[1 + (8×3)/100] = P × 1.24. Then compound: A₂ = 1.24P × (1.10)2 = 1.24P × 1.21 = 1.4984P. The overall multiplication factor is 1.4984, i.e. a 49.84 % increase over the original principal. If the question asks for the principal given the final amount, divide the final amount by 1.4984.

When the simple‑interest phase follows the compound phase, the order of operations reverses. Suppose a sum grows at 12 % compound for 2 years, then the accumulated amount earns 6 % simple for 3 years. First compound factor: (1.12)2 = 1.2544. Then simple factor: 1 + (6×3)/100 = 1.18. Overall factor = 1.2544 × 1.18 = 1.4802. The sequence matters because the simple‑interest factor applies to a larger base when it comes after compounding.

Determining Unknown Rate or Time from Amount Data

Examiners frequently provide the principal, the final amount, and either the time or the rate, asking for the missing variable. The approach depends on whether the interest is simple or compound. For simple interest, the relationship is linear: A = P[1 + (R T)/100]. If R is unknown, rearrange to R = 100(A/P - 1)/T. If T is unknown, T = 100(A/P - 1)/R. Both formulas are direct and require only division.

For compound interest, the equation is A = P(1 + R/100)^T. Taking logarithms yields log(A/P) = T × log(1 + R/100). If R is unknown, log(1 + R/100) = log(A/P)/T, then 1 + R/100 = 10log(A/P)/T (or e... for natural logs), and finally R = 100(10log(A/P)/T - 1). If T is unknown, T = log(A/P) / log(1 + R/100). In many exam questions, the numbers are chosen so that A/P is a perfect power of (1 + R/100), making T an integer. For example, if a sum doubles in 5 years at compound interest, then 2 = (1 + R/100)5. Recognising that 2^(1/5) ≈ 1.1487 gives R ≈ 14.87 %. However, if the rate is a round number like 10 %, then (1.10)^T = 2 ⇒ T ≈ 7.27 years, which is not an integer; such a question would likely ask for the approximate time or provide options.

A useful shortcut for “doubling time” under compound interest is the Rule of 72: T ≈ 72 / R (with R in percent). This approximation works well for rates between 6 % and 10 %. For exact answers, logarithms are required. Similarly, the “tripling time” can be approximated by 114 / R. These rules are handy for quick elimination of options.

When the compounding frequency is not annual, the formula becomes A = P(1 + R/(100n))^(nT). The unknown could be the nominal rate R, the time T, or the frequency n. If n is unknown but the effective annual rate is given, use the EAR formula EAR = (1 + R/(100n))^n - 1 and solve for n by trial, since n is usually a small integer (2, 4, 12, 365). If the problem states “compounded quarterly” and asks for the nominal rate given the effective rate, rearrange R = 100n[(1 + EAR)^(1/n) - 1].

Another layer appears when the interest rate changes after a certain period. For instance, a sum amounts to ₹A₁ after T₁ years at rate R₁, then the amount is reinvested at rate R₂ for T₂ years to reach A₂. The equations are A₁ = P(1 + R₁/100)^T₁ and A₂ = A₁(1 + R₂/100)^T₂. If one of the rates is unknown, isolate it using the known amounts and times. This multi‑stage structure tests the ability to chain compound‑interest formulas correctly.

Comparative Analysis of Simple vs Compound Over Multiple Periods

Beyond the two‑year difference formula, examiners ask for the difference between simple and compound interest over three or more years, or for the ratio of the two interests. For a principal P, rate R, and time T (in years), simple interest is SI = P R T / 100. Compound interest is CI = P[(1 + R/100)^T - 1]. The difference CI - SI grows faster than the square of the rate because the compound factor includes higher‑order terms. Expanding the binomial (1 + r)^T where r = R/100 gives 1 + Tr + T(T-1)/2 r2 + T(T-1)(T-2)/6 r3 + …. Subtracting the linear term Tr leaves the difference as a polynomial in r starting with the quadratic term. For T = 3, CI - SI = P[3r2 + r3] (since 3(3-1)/2 = 3). For T = 4, the difference includes 6r2 + 4r3 + r4. Recognising these coefficients helps in quickly estimating the difference without full exponentiation.

The ratio CI / SI is another comparative metric. For small r and moderate T, the ratio is approximately 1 + (T-1)r/2. This approximation follows from the binomial expansion: CI ≈ P[Tr + T(T-1)/2 r2], so CI/SI ≈ 1 + (T-1)r/2. For example, at 10 % (r = 0.1) for 5 years, the ratio ≈ 1 + 4×0.1/2 = 1.2, meaning compound interest is about 20 % higher than simple interest. This mental shortcut is valuable for data‑interpretation sets where exact computation is time‑consuming.

Questions may also ask for the time at which compound interest exceeds simple interest by a given percentage. Set CI = k × SI where k > 1. Using the exact formulas, P[(1+r)^T - 1] = k × P r T. Cancel P and solve for T numerically. Because T appears both in the exponent and linearly, an analytical solution is rarely possible; trial with integer T values is the standard exam technique. For instance, find the smallest integer T such that CI is at least 50 % more than SI at 8 % (r = 0.08). Test T = 3: CI factor = (1.08)3 - 1 ≈ 0.2597, SI factor = 0.24, ratio ≈ 1.082. T = 4: CI factor ≈ 0.3605, SI factor = 0.32, ratio ≈ 1.126. T = 5: CI factor ≈ 0.4693, SI factor = 0.40, ratio ≈ 1.173. T = 6: CI factor ≈ 0.5869, SI factor = 0.48, ratio ≈ 1.223. T = 7: CI factor ≈ 0.7138, SI factor = 0.56, ratio ≈ 1.275. T = 8: CI factor ≈ 0.8509, SI factor = 0.64, ratio ≈ 1.329. T = 9: CI factor ≈ 0.9990, SI factor = 0.72, ratio ≈ 1.387. T = 10: CI factor ≈ 1.1589, SI factor = 0.80, ratio ≈ 1.449. T = 11: CI factor ≈ 1.3316, SI factor = 0.88, ratio ≈ 1.513. So T = 11 years is the first integer where CI exceeds SI by 50 %. This iterative method is expected in the exam.

Another comparative scenario involves two different principals invested at the same rate, one under simple interest and the other under compound interest, and the question asks when their amounts become equal. Let P₁ be the simple‑interest principal and P₂ the compound‑interest principal. Equate amounts: P₁(1 + rT) = P₂(1 + r)^T. If P₁ and P₂ are given, solve for T. If T is given, find the ratio P₁/P₂. This type of problem tests algebraic manipulation of the two growth laws simultaneously.

Finally, be aware of the “effective simple interest rate” concept: for a given compound interest rate and period, the simple interest rate that would yield the same final amount is R_eff = 100[(1 + R/100)^T - 1]/T. This is the average annual simple rate equivalent to the compound growth. It is always higher than the nominal compound rate for T > 1, and the gap widens with T. Recognising this equivalence helps in questions that mix simple‑interest language with compound‑interest data.

  • Assuming the two‑year difference formula works for any period; it is valid only for exactly two years.
  • Using the nominal annual rate directly in the periodic formula without dividing by the compounding frequency.
  • Forgetting to convert months or days into years before inserting into the simple‑interest formula.
  • Treating a balloon payment as a regular installment when calculating the present value of an annuity.
  • Applying the simple‑interest growth factor to a principal that has already been compounded, instead of using the compounded amount as the new principal.
  • Misidentifying whether the first installment is due immediately or after one period, leading to an off‑by‑one error in the annuity formula.
  • Rounding intermediate results too early, especially when raising (1 + i) to a high power, which amplifies the error.
