What is Chemistry?

Chemistry is the study of matter — its composition, properties, structure, and the changes it undergoes. In Agniveer and defence exams, chemistry questions cover atomic structure, chemical bonding, acids-bases-salts, periodic table, metals and non-metals, and everyday chemistry. Questions are at the high school level and focus on basic concepts and applications.

Key Topics in Chemistry

1. Atomic Structure: Atom = protons (positive, in nucleus) + neutrons (neutral, in nucleus) + electrons (negative, orbiting). Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons. Isotopes (same Z, different A), Isobars (same A, different Z), Isotones (same number of neutrons). Bohr's model: electrons in fixed orbits (shells K, L, M, N).

2. Chemical Bonding: Ionic bond (transfer of electrons — metal + non-metal, e.g., NaCl). Covalent bond (sharing of electrons — non-metal + non-metal, e.g., H₂O, CO₂). Metallic bond (electron sea model). Valency — combining capacity of an element.

3. Acids, Bases, and Salts: Acids (pH < 7) — sour, turn blue litmus red, examples: HCl, H₂SO₄, CH₃COOH. Bases (pH > 7) — bitter, turn red litmus blue, examples: NaOH, Ca(OH)₂, NH₄OH. Neutralization: Acid + Base → Salt + Water. pH scale (0-14), universal indicator.

4. Periodic Table: Mendeleev's periodic table (based on atomic mass). Modern periodic table (based on atomic number). 18 groups (vertical) and 7 periods (horizontal). Groups: Alkali metals (Group 1), Alkaline earth metals (Group 2), Halogens (Group 17), Noble gases (Group 18). Periodic trends: atomic size (decreases across period, increases down group), metallic character, electronegativity.

5. Metals and Non-Metals: Metals (lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductors of heat/electricity). Non-metals (dull, brittle, poor conductors). Reaction of metals with oxygen, water, acids, and displacement reactions (reactivity series: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au).

6. Everyday Chemistry: Baking soda (NaHCO₃), washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O), bleaching powder (CaOCl₂), vinegar (CH₃COOH), soap (sodium salts of fatty acids), detergents, plastics, fertilizers (urea, NPK), CFCs and ozone depletion.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Atomic Structure)
What is the atomic number of an element with 12 protons and 12 neutrons?

a) 12 b) 24 c) 6 d) 18
Solution: a) 12. Atomic number = number of protons = 12. Mass number = 12 + 12 = 24 (not asked here).
Example 2 (pH Scale)
Which of the following has the highest pH value?

a) Lemon juice b) Vinegar c) Soap solution d) Milk
Solution: c) Soap solution. Lemon juice (~2), vinegar (~3), milk (~6.5) are acidic/weakly acidic. Soap solution (~9-10) is basic, having the highest pH.
Example 3 (Reactvity Series)
Which metal is the most reactive in the reactivity series?

a) Iron b) Zinc c) Sodium d) Gold
Solution: c) Sodium is the most reactive among these. The reactivity series order: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au. Gold is the least reactive.

Shortcut Techniques

  • Remember the first 20 elements of the periodic table in order: H, He, Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F, Ne, Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar, K, Ca.
  • Valency shortcuts: Group 1 = +1, Group 2 = +2, Group 13 = +3, Group 14 = ±4, Group 15 = -3, Group 16 = -2, Group 17 = -1, Group 18 = 0.
  • For acids: Hydrochloric (HCl), Sulphuric (H₂SO₄), Nitric (HNO₃) are strong acids. Acetic (CH₃COOH), Carbonic (H₂CO₃) are weak acids.
  • For bases: NaOH, KOH are strong bases; Ca(OH)₂, NH₄OH are weak bases.

Quantum Mechanical Model and Electron Configuration Exceptions

The quantum mechanical model replaces the Bohr planetary picture with orbitals defined by four quantum numbers. The principal quantum number (n) sets the energy level, the azimuthal quantum number (l) defines the subshell (s, p, d, f), the magnetic quantum number (mₗ) specifies the orbital orientation, and the spin quantum number (mₛ) distinguishes the two electrons in an orbital. Electrons fill orbitals following the Aufbau principle (lowest n + l first), Hund’s rule (maximum multiplicity before pairing), and the Pauli exclusion principle (no two electrons share all four quantum numbers). For the first 30 elements this yields the familiar 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d¹⁰ sequence.

Two well‑known exceptions arise from the extra stability of half‑filled and completely filled d subshells. Chromium (Z = 24) adopts [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ rather than the predicted [Ar] 4s² 3d⁴, and copper (Z = 29) adopts [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰ instead of [Ar] 4s² 3d⁹. Similar anomalies appear in the 4d and 5d series (e.g., Mo, Ag, Au). The energy gap between (n + 1)s and nd orbitals narrows down a group, so the (n + 1)s electron can be promoted to achieve a half‑filled or filled d set, lowering the total energy. In the f‑block, gadolinium (Z = 64) shows [Xe] 4f⁷ 5d¹ 6s² because a half‑filled 4f⁷ subshell is especially stable.

Ionisation energies reflect these configurations. The first ionisation energy of Cr is slightly lower than that of V because the removed electron comes from the 4s orbital, leaving a stable 3d⁵ core. Conversely, Cu’s first ionisation energy is higher than Ni’s because the 4s electron is more tightly held when the 3d subshell is full. Successive ionisation energies jump dramatically when a stable noble‑gas or half‑filled configuration is breached, a pattern examiners often test by asking for the largest jump in a series.

Electron affinity trends are less regular. While halogens have the most exothermic values, the addition of an electron to a half‑filled p subshell (e.g., O → O⁻) is less exothermic than to a neighbouring element because of increased electron‑electron repulsion in the compact 2p orbital. This explains why chlorine’s electron affinity is more negative than fluorine’s, a classic textbook anomaly.

When writing configurations for ions, remove electrons from the highest principal quantum number first (the outermost shell). For transition‑metal cations, the (n + 1)s electrons are lost before the nd electrons. Thus Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, not [Ar] 4s² 3d⁴. This rule also applies to the formation of complex ions where ligand field effects may further rearrange d‑electron distribution.

VSEPR Theory, Hybridisation, and Molecular Orbital Basics

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) predicts molecular geometry by minimising repulsion between electron domains (bonding pairs and lone pairs) around a central atom. The steric number (SN) equals the number of σ‑bonds plus lone pairs. SN = 2 gives linear (180°), SN = 3 gives trigonal planar (120°) or bent if one lone pair, SN = 4 gives tetrahedral (109.5°), trigonal pyramidal (one lone pair) or bent (two lone pairs). SN = 5 yields trigonal bipyramidal; lone pairs occupy equatorial positions to reduce 90° repulsions, producing seesaw, T‑shaped, or linear shapes. SN = 6 gives octahedral; one lone pair gives square pyramidal, two give square planar.

Hybridisation provides an orbital picture consistent with VSEPR geometries. sp hybridisation (two sp orbitals) matches linear SN = 2; sp² (three sp² orbitals) matches trigonal planar SN = 3; sp³ (four sp³ orbitals) matches tetrahedral SN = 4. For SN = 5, the central atom uses sp³d (or dsp³) hybrids; for SN = 6, sp³d² (or d²sp³). In molecules with multiple bonds, only σ‑bonds count toward steric number; π‑bonds occupy unhybridised p orbitals. For example, CO₂ has SN = 2 (two double bonds) → sp hybridisation, linear. In SO₂, SN = 3 (one lone pair, two σ‑bonds) → sp², bent (~119°).

Molecular Orbital (MO) theory extends beyond localized bonds. Atomic orbitals combine to form bonding (lower energy) and antibonding (higher energy) MOs. For diatomics of the second period, the ordering up to N₂ is σ2s < σ*2s < π2pₓ = π2pᵧ < σ2p_z < π*2pₓ = π*2pᵧ < σ*2p_z. From O₂ onward, σ2p_z drops below π2p due to increased s‑p mixing. Bond order = (bonding electrons − antibonding electrons)/2. O₂ has bond order 2 and two unpaired electrons in π* orbitals, explaining its paramagnetism — a frequent multiple‑choice trap.

Exceptions to simple hybridisation appear in hypervalent molecules (e.g., SF₆, PF₅) where d‑orbital participation is now described more accurately by three‑centre‑four‑electron (3c‑4e) bonds rather than true sp³d² hybrids. In XeF₄, the square‑planar geometry arises from an octahedral electron‑domain arrangement with two lone pairs occupying axial positions; the bonding can be rationalised with sp³d² hybrids or, more rigorously, with MO descriptions involving p‑orbital donation from fluorine into xenon’s empty d orbitals.

Coordinate (dative) bonds fit naturally into VSEPR: the donor atom contributes a lone pair that becomes a bonding domain on the acceptor. In NH₄⁺, nitrogen’s steric number is 4 (four σ‑bonds, no lone pairs) → tetrahedral. In [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺, Cu²⁺ (d⁹) accepts four lone pairs, giving a square‑planar arrangement often described as dsp² hybridisation. Recognising that the geometry around the metal centre follows the same electron‑domain count helps avoid confusion when transition‑metal complexes appear in questions.

Worked Example: VSEPR and Hybridisation of SF₄
Predict the molecular shape of sulfur tetrafluoride (SF₄) and state the hybridisation of the sulfur atom.
Sulfur has six valence electrons. In SF₄ it forms four σ‑bonds to fluorine and retains one lone pair. Steric number = 4 bonds + 1 lone pair = 5. For SN = 5 the electron‑domain geometry is trigonal bipyramidal. The lone pair occupies an equatorial position to minimise 90° repulsions, giving a seesaw molecular shape. The hybridisation corresponding to five electron domains is sp³d (or dsp³). Therefore SF₄ is seesaw‑shaped with sp³d hybridisation on sulfur.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a geometry question appears, first count σ‑bonds and lone pairs on the central atom to get the steric number. Map the steric number to the standard VSEPR table (2→linear, 3→trigonal planar/bent, 4→tetrahedral/pyramidal/bent, 5→trigonal bipyramidal derivatives, 6→octahedral derivatives). Then assign hybridisation directly from the steric number (2→sp, 3→sp², 4→sp³, 5→sp³d, 6→sp³d²). This two‑step routine eliminates guesswork and works for both main‑group and many transition‑metal species.

Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis Acid–Base Concepts with Buffer Calculations

The Arrhenius definition limits acids to H⁺ producers and bases to OH⁻ producers in aqueous solution. Brønsted–Lowry broadens the scope: an acid is a proton donor, a base a proton acceptor. Every acid–base reaction involves a conjugate acid–base pair differing by one proton. For example, HCl + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻; HCl/Cl⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O are conjugate pairs. The strength of an acid is quantified by its acid dissociation constant Ka; pKa = –log₁₀Ka. Strong acids have Ka ≫ 1 (pKa < 0), weak acids have Ka < 1 (pKa > 0). The conjugate base of a strong acid is negligible as a base (Cl⁻), while the conjugate base of a weak acid (CH₃COO⁻) can accept protons appreciably.

Lewis theory removes the proton entirely: a Lewis acid is an electron‑pair acceptor, a Lewis base an electron‑pair donor. This encompasses species like BF₃ (accepts a pair from NH₃) and metal cations (Fe³⁺ accepting water ligands). Many Brønsted acids are also Lewis acids (H⁺ accepts a pair), but not all Lewis acids are Brønsted acids. Recognising the distinction is crucial when a question asks why AlCl₃ catalyses Friedel‑Crafts alkylation — it acts as a Lewis acid by accepting a chloride lone pair, generating an electrophilic carbocation.

Buffers resist pH change upon addition of small amounts of strong acid or base. A typical buffer consists of a weak acid (HA) and its conjugate base (A⁻) in comparable concentrations. The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]), derives from the Ka expression and assumes activity coefficients ≈ 1. For a buffer prepared from 0.1 M CH₃COOH (pKa = 4.76) and 0.1 M CH₃COONa, the initial pH = 4.76 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.76. Adding 0.01 mol HCl to 1 L converts 0.01 M A⁻ to HA, giving [A⁻] = 0.09 M, [HA] = 0.11 M; new pH = 4.76 + log(0.09/0.11) ≈ 4.67 — a modest shift.

Buffer capacity depends on absolute concentrations and the ratio [A⁻]/[HA]. Maximum capacity occurs when the ratio is 1 (pH = pKa). A buffer with 0.01 M each component has ten times lower capacity than one with 0.1 M each, even though both have the same pH. Examiners often test this by asking which of two buffers will show a smaller pH change after adding a fixed amount of strong acid.

Polyprotic acids (H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄) have successive Ka values (Ka₁ ≫ Ka₂ ≫ Ka₃). In a solution of NaH₂PO₄/Na₂HPO₄ the relevant equilibrium is H₂PO₄⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ with Ka₂ (pKa₂ ≈ 7.2). This system buffers near physiological pH. When calculating pH of a salt of a weak acid and weak base (e.g., NH₄CH₃COO), use the formula pH = ½(pKw + pKa(acid) – pKb(base)). For NH₄⁺ (pKa = 9.25) and CH₃COO⁻ (pKb = 9.25), pH ≈ 7.0, illustrating why ammonium acetate solutions are nearly neutral.

Effective Nuclear Charge, Shielding, and Periodic Trend Anomalies

Effective nuclear charge (Zeff) is the net positive charge felt by an electron after accounting for shielding by other electrons. Slater’s rules give a systematic way to estimate shielding constants (S): electrons in the same (n, l) group contribute 0.35 (0.30 for 1s), electrons in n‑1 shell contribute 0.85, and electrons in n‑2 or lower contribute 1.00. Zeff = Z – S. Across a period, Z increases while shielding rises only modestly, so Zeff grows, pulling the valence shell inward and decreasing atomic radius. Down a group, the principal quantum number n increases, adding a new shell; although Z also rises, the added shell’s electrons shield almost completely (≈ 1.00 each), so Zeff on the outermost electron changes little, and radius expands.

Ionisation energy (IE) generally follows Zeff: higher Zeff → higher IE. However, anomalies appear at group boundaries. The first IE of Group 13 (B, Al, Ga) is lower than that of the preceding Group 2 element because the electron removed is a p‑electron, which is less tightly held than the s‑electron of Group 2. Similarly, Group 16 (O, S) shows a dip relative to Group 15 (N, P) because the paired p‑electron experiences extra electron‑electron repulsion, making it easier to remove. These “pairing” and “subshell” anomalies are standard textbook points and appear frequently in multiple‑choice questions.

Electron affinity (EA) generally becomes more exothermic across a period, but the noble gases have positive EA (energy required) because the added electron must occupy a higher shell. Halogens have the most negative EA, yet fluorine’s EA is less negative than chlorine’s due to the compact 2p orbital causing greater repulsion. Down a group, EA becomes less exothermic as the added electron enters a more diffuse orbital, but the trend is irregular for the heavier halogens because of relativistic effects.

Metallic character decreases across a period (increasing Zeff, higher IE, lower tendency to lose electrons) and increases down a group (lower IE, larger radius). The diagonal relationship — similarity between certain pairs like Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si — arises because moving one period down increases size and decreases Zeff, while moving one group right increases Zeff and decreases size; the two effects partially cancel. For example, Li⁺ (radius ≈ 76 pm) and Mg²⁺ (radius ≈ 72 pm) have comparable charge densities, leading to similar hydrolysis behaviour and solubility patterns.

Lanthanide contraction — the steady decrease in ionic radii across the 4f series (La³⁺ ≈ 103 pm to Lu³⁺ ≈ 86 pm) — results from poor shielding of the nuclear charge by the diffuse 4f electrons. Consequently, the 5d transition metals (Hf, Ta, W) have radii almost identical to their 4d counterparts (Zr, Nb, Mo). This explains why Zr/Hf and Nb/Ta are chemically almost inseparable, a fact exploited in their industrial separation by fractional crystallisation of fluorides.

Diagonal Relationships, Lanthanide Contraction, and Oxidation State Prediction

The diagonal relationship is a practical tool for predicting chemical similarity when periodic trends oppose each other. Moving down a group increases metallic character and atomic size; moving right across a period decreases size and increases electronegativity. The combined effect often yields pairs with comparable charge density (ionic charge/ionic radius). Classic examples: Li⁺ (76 pm) vs Mg²⁺ (72 pm), Be²⁺ (45 pm) vs Al³⁺ (53 pm), B³⁺ (27 pm) vs Si⁴⁺ (40 pm). These similarities manifest in hydrolysis of cations (Li⁺ and Mg²⁺ both form weakly acidic aqua ions), solubility of carbonates (Li₂CO₃ and MgCO₃ are sparingly soluble), and formation of covalent character in otherwise ionic compounds (BeCl₂ and AlCl₃ are largely covalent).

Lanthanide contraction, as noted, compresses the 5d series to near‑identical sizes with the 4d series. This has direct consequences for oxidation‑state chemistry. For the early 5d elements (Hf, Ta, W), the +4 oxidation state (Hf⁴⁺, Ta⁴⁺, W⁴⁺) is common and stable, mirroring Zr⁴⁺, Nb⁴⁺, Mo⁴⁺. However, the higher oxidation states (+5 for Ta, +6 for W) become increasingly accessible because the contracted 5d orbitals overlap better with ligand orbitals, stabilising multiple bonds (e.g., WO₄²⁻, TaO₄⁻). In contrast, the 4d analogues show a stronger preference for lower oxidation states.

Predicting the most stable oxidation state for a transition metal involves the “18‑electron rule” for organometallics and the “maximum oxidation state = group number” for early transition metals. For groups 3–7, the highest oxidation state equals the group number (Sc +3, Ti +4, V +5, Cr +6, Mn +7). Beyond group 7, the maximum oxidation state rarely exceeds +6 (Fe +6 in FeO₄²⁻ is rare). The stability of a given oxidation state also depends on the ligand field: strong‑field ligands (CN⁻, CO) stabilise low‑spin configurations and often lower oxidation states, while weak‑field ligands (H₂O, Cl⁻) favour higher oxidation states.

In the p‑block, the inert‑pair effect explains why heavier Group 13–16 elements prefer oxidation states two units lower than the group valence. For Tl (Group 13), +1 is more stable than +3; for Pb (Group 14), +2 dominates over +4; for Bi (Group 15), +3 is favoured over +5. This arises because the ns² electrons become increasingly reluctant to participate in bonding due to relativistic stabilisation of the s orbital and poor shielding by the (n‑1)d and (n‑2)f electrons. Consequently, compounds like TlCl, PbO, and BiCl₃ are far more common than their higher‑oxidation‑state counterparts.

When a question asks for the oxidation state of a metal in a complex, assign known ligand charges (Cl⁻ = –1, CN⁻ = –1, H₂O = 0, NH₃ = 0, CO = 0, NO⁺ = +1) and solve for the metal. For example, in [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻, each CN⁻ contributes –1 (total –6). The overall charge is –4, so Fe must be +2. In [MnO₄]⁻, each O is –2 (total –8); overall –1 gives Mn = +7. This bookkeeping method works for all coordination compounds and is a staple of quantitative inorganic questions.

  • Confusing steric number with coordination number — count only σ‑bonds and lone pairs on the central atom, not π‑bonds or ligands attached through dative bonds that do not change the electron‑domain count.
  • Assuming all transition‑metal cations lose d‑electrons first — the (n + 1)s electrons are removed before nd electrons, so Fe²⁺ is 3d⁶ not 4s²3d⁴.
  • Treating electron affinity as uniformly more exothermic across a period — fluorine’s EA is less negative than chlorine’s because of 2p orbital crowding.
  • Applying the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation without checking the ratio range — it is reliable only when 0.1 ≤ [A⁻]/[HA] ≤ 10; outside this the buffer capacity is negligible.
  • Overlooking the inert‑pair effect when predicting oxidation states of heavy p‑block elements — Tl⁺, Pb²⁺, Bi³⁺ are far more stable than the group‑valence states.
  • Using Slater’s rules incorrectly for d‑ and f‑electrons — electrons in the same (n, l) group shield 0.35, but for nd or nf electrons the shielding by electrons in the same group is 0.35 only for the first electron; subsequent electrons in the same subshell shield 0.35 as well, while electrons in lower shells shield 1.00.
  • Assuming diagonal relationships hold for all properties — they are strong for charge‑density‑dependent traits (hydrolysis, solubility) but weak for redox potentials and complex‑formation constants.
