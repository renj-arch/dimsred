What is Chemistry?

Chemistry is the study of matter — its composition, properties, structure, and the changes it undergoes. In Agniveer and defence exams, chemistry questions cover atomic structure, chemical bonding, acids-bases-salts, periodic table, metals and non-metals, and everyday chemistry. Questions are at the high school level and focus on basic concepts and applications.

Key Topics in Chemistry

1. Atomic Structure: Atom = protons (positive, in nucleus) + neutrons (neutral, in nucleus) + electrons (negative, orbiting). Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons. Isotopes (same Z, different A), Isobars (same A, different Z), Isotones (same number of neutrons). Bohr's model: electrons in fixed orbits (shells K, L, M, N).

2. Chemical Bonding: Ionic bond (transfer of electrons — metal + non-metal, e.g., NaCl). Covalent bond (sharing of electrons — non-metal + non-metal, e.g., H₂O, CO₂). Metallic bond (electron sea model). Valency — combining capacity of an element.

3. Acids, Bases, and Salts: Acids (pH < 7) — sour, turn blue litmus red, examples: HCl, H₂SO₄, CH₃COOH. Bases (pH > 7) — bitter, turn red litmus blue, examples: NaOH, Ca(OH)₂, NH₄OH. Neutralization: Acid + Base → Salt + Water. pH scale (0-14), universal indicator.

4. Periodic Table: Mendeleev's periodic table (based on atomic mass). Modern periodic table (based on atomic number). 18 groups (vertical) and 7 periods (horizontal). Groups: Alkali metals (Group 1), Alkaline earth metals (Group 2), Halogens (Group 17), Noble gases (Group 18). Periodic trends: atomic size (decreases across period, increases down group), metallic character, electronegativity.

5. Metals and Non-Metals: Metals (lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductors of heat/electricity). Non-metals (dull, brittle, poor conductors). Reaction of metals with oxygen, water, acids, and displacement reactions (reactivity series: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au).

6. Everyday Chemistry: Baking soda (NaHCO₃), washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O), bleaching powder (CaOCl₂), vinegar (CH₃COOH), soap (sodium salts of fatty acids), detergents, plastics, fertilizers (urea, NPK), CFCs and ozone depletion.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Atomic Structure)
What is the atomic number of an element with 12 protons and 12 neutrons?

a) 12 b) 24 c) 6 d) 18
Solution: a) 12. Atomic number = number of protons = 12. Mass number = 12 + 12 = 24 (not asked here).
Example 2 (pH Scale)
Which of the following has the highest pH value?

a) Lemon juice b) Vinegar c) Soap solution d) Milk
Solution: c) Soap solution. Lemon juice (~2), vinegar (~3), milk (~6.5) are acidic/weakly acidic. Soap solution (~9-10) is basic, having the highest pH.
Example 3 (Reactvity Series)
Which metal is the most reactive in the reactivity series?

a) Iron b) Zinc c) Sodium d) Gold
Solution: c) Sodium is the most reactive among these. The reactivity series order: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au. Gold is the least reactive.

Shortcut Techniques

  • Remember the first 20 elements of the periodic table in order: H, He, Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F, Ne, Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar, K, Ca.
  • Valency shortcuts: Group 1 = +1, Group 2 = +2, Group 13 = +3, Group 14 = ±4, Group 15 = -3, Group 16 = -2, Group 17 = -1, Group 18 = 0.
  • For acids: Hydrochloric (HCl), Sulphuric (H₂SO₄), Nitric (HNO₃) are strong acids. Acetic (CH₃COOH), Carbonic (H₂CO₃) are weak acids.
  • For bases: NaOH, KOH are strong bases; Ca(OH)₂, NH₄OH are weak bases.

Quantum Mechanical Model and Electron Configuration Exceptions

The quantum mechanical model replaces the Bohr planetary picture with orbitals defined by four quantum numbers. The principal quantum number (n) sets the energy level, the azimuthal quantum number (l) defines the subshell (s, p, d, f), the magnetic quantum number (mₗ) specifies the orbital orientation, and the spin quantum number (mₛ) distinguishes the two electrons in an orbital. Electrons fill orbitals following the Aufbau principle (lowest n + l first), Hund’s rule (maximum multiplicity before pairing), and the Pauli exclusion principle (no two electrons share all four quantum numbers). For the first 30 elements this yields the familiar 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d¹⁰ sequence.

Two well‑known exceptions arise from the extra stability of half‑filled and completely filled d subshells. Chromium (Z = 24) adopts [Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ rather than the predicted [Ar] 4s² 3d⁴, and copper (Z = 29) adopts [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰ instead of [Ar] 4s² 3d⁹. Similar anomalies appear in the 4d and 5d series (e.g., Mo, Ag, Au). The energy gap between (n + 1)s and nd orbitals narrows down a group, so the (n + 1)s electron can be promoted to achieve a half‑filled or filled d set, lowering the total energy. In the f‑block, gadolinium (Z = 64) shows [Xe] 4f⁷ 5d¹ 6s² because a half‑filled 4f⁷ subshell is especially stable.

Ionisation energies reflect these configurations. The first ionisation energy of Cr is slightly lower than that of V because the removed electron comes from the 4s orbital, leaving a stable 3d⁵ core. Conversely, Cu’s first ionisation energy is higher than Ni’s because the 4s electron is more tightly held when the 3d subshell is full. Successive ionisation energies jump dramatically when a stable noble‑gas or half‑filled configuration is breached, a pattern examiners often test by asking for the largest jump in a series.

Electron affinity trends are less regular. While halogens have the most exothermic values, the addition of an electron to a half‑filled p subshell (e.g., O → O⁻) is less exothermic than to a neighbouring element because of increased electron‑electron repulsion in the compact 2p orbital. This explains why chlorine’s electron affinity is more negative than fluorine’s, a classic textbook anomaly.

When writing configurations for ions, remove electrons from the highest principal quantum number first (the outermost shell). For transition‑metal cations, the (n + 1)s electrons are lost before the nd electrons. Thus Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, not [Ar] 4s² 3d⁴. This rule also applies to the formation of complex ions where ligand field effects may further rearrange d‑electron distribution.

VSEPR Theory, Hybridisation, and Molecular Orbital Basics

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) predicts molecular geometry by minimising repulsion between electron domains (bonding pairs and lone pairs) around a central atom. The steric number (SN) equals the number of σ‑bonds plus lone pairs. SN = 2 gives linear (180°), SN = 3 gives trigonal planar (120°) or bent if one lone pair, SN = 4 gives tetrahedral (109.5°), trigonal pyramidal (one lone pair) or bent (two lone pairs). SN = 5 yields trigonal bipyramidal; lone pairs occupy equatorial positions to reduce 90° repulsions, producing seesaw, T‑shaped, or linear shapes. SN = 6 gives octahedral; one lone pair gives square pyramidal, two give square planar.

Hybridisation provides an orbital picture consistent with VSEPR geometries. sp hybridisation (two sp orbitals) matches linear SN = 2; sp² (three sp² orbitals) matches trigonal planar SN = 3; sp³ (four sp³ orbitals) matches tetrahedral SN = 4. For SN = 5, the central atom uses sp³d (or dsp³) hybrids; for SN = 6, sp³d² (or d²sp³). In molecules with multiple bonds, only σ‑bonds count toward steric number; π‑bonds occupy unhybridised p orbitals. For example, CO₂ has SN = 2 (two double bonds) → sp hybridisation, linear. In SO₂, SN = 3 (one lone pair, two σ‑bonds) → sp², bent (~119°).

Molecular Orbital (MO) theory extends beyond localized bonds. Atomic orbitals combine to form bonding (lower energy) and antibonding (higher energy) MOs. For diatomics of the second period, the ordering up to N₂ is σ2s < σ*2s < π2pₓ = π2pᵧ < σ2p_z < π*2pₓ = π*2pᵧ < σ*2p_z. From O₂ onward, σ2p_z drops below π2p due to increased s‑p mixing. Bond order = (bonding electrons − antibonding electrons)/2. O₂ has bond order 2 and two unpaired electrons in π* orbitals, explaining its paramagnetism — a frequent multiple‑choice trap.

Exceptions to simple hybridisation appear in hypervalent molecules (e.g., SF₆, PF₅) where d‑orbital participation is now described more accurately by three‑centre‑four‑electron (3c‑4e) bonds rather than true sp³d² hybrids. In XeF₄, the square‑planar geometry arises from an octahedral electron‑domain arrangement with two lone pairs occupying axial positions; the bonding can be rationalised with sp³d² hybrids or, more rigorously, with MO descriptions involving p‑orbital donation from fluorine into xenon’s empty d orbitals.

Coordinate (dative) bonds fit naturally into VSEPR: the donor atom contributes a lone pair that becomes a bonding domain on the acceptor. In NH₄⁺, nitrogen’s steric number is 4 (four σ‑bonds, no lone pairs) → tetrahedral. In [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺, Cu²⁺ (d⁹) accepts four lone pairs, giving a square‑planar arrangement often described as dsp² hybridisation. Recognising that the geometry around the metal centre follows the same electron‑domain count helps avoid confusion when transition‑metal complexes appear in questions.

Worked Example: VSEPR and Hybridisation of SF₄
Predict the molecular shape of sulfur tetrafluoride (SF₄) and state the hybridisation of the sulfur atom.
Sulfur has six valence electrons. In SF₄ it forms four σ‑bonds to fluorine and retains one lone pair. Steric number = 4 bonds + 1 lone pair = 5. For SN = 5 the electron‑domain geometry is trigonal bipyramidal. The lone pair occupies an equatorial position to minimise 90° repulsions, giving a seesaw molecular shape. The hybridisation corresponding to five electron domains is sp³d (or dsp³). Therefore SF₄ is seesaw‑shaped with sp³d hybridisation on sulfur.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a geometry question appears, first count σ‑bonds and lone pairs on the central atom to get the steric number. Map the steric number to the standard VSEPR table (2→linear, 3→trigonal planar/bent, 4→tetrahedral/pyramidal/bent, 5→trigonal bipyramidal derivatives, 6→octahedral derivatives). Then assign hybridisation directly from the steric number (2→sp, 3→sp², 4→sp³, 5→sp³d, 6→sp³d²). This two‑step routine eliminates guesswork and works for both main‑group and many transition‑metal species.

Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis Acid–Base Concepts with Buffer Calculations

The Arrhenius definition limits acids to H⁺ producers and bases to OH⁻ producers in aqueous solution. Brønsted–Lowry broadens the scope: an acid is a proton donor, a base a proton acceptor. Every acid–base reaction involves a conjugate acid–base pair differing by one proton. For example, HCl + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻; HCl/Cl⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O are conjugate pairs. The strength of an acid is quantified by its acid dissociation constant Ka; pKa = –log₁₀Ka. Strong acids have Ka ≫ 1 (pKa < 0), weak acids have Ka < 1 (pKa > 0). The conjugate base of a strong acid is negligible as a base (Cl⁻), while the conjugate base of a weak acid (CH₃COO⁻) can accept protons appreciably.

Lewis theory removes the proton entirely: a Lewis acid is an electron‑pair acceptor, a Lewis base an electron‑pair donor. This encompasses species like BF₃ (accepts a pair from NH₃) and metal cations (Fe³⁺ accepting water ligands). Many Brønsted acids are also Lewis acids (H⁺ accepts a pair), but not all Lewis acids are Brønsted acids. Recognising the distinction is crucial when a question asks why AlCl₃ catalyses Friedel‑Crafts alkylation — it acts as a Lewis acid by accepting a chloride lone pair, generating an electrophilic carbocation.

Buffers resist pH change upon addition of small amounts of strong acid or base. A typical buffer consists of a weak acid (HA) and its conjugate base (A⁻) in comparable concentrations. The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]), derives from the Ka expression and assumes activity coefficients ≈ 1. For a buffer prepared from 0.1 M CH₃COOH (pKa = 4.76) and 0.1 M CH₃COONa, the initial pH = 4.76 + log(0.1/0.1) = 4.76. Adding 0.01 mol HCl to 1 L converts 0.01 M A⁻ to HA, giving [A⁻] = 0.09 M, [HA] = 0.11 M; new pH = 4.76 + log(0.09/0.11) ≈ 4.67 — a modest shift.

Buffer capacity depends on absolute concentrations and the ratio [A⁻]/[HA]. Maximum capacity occurs when the ratio is 1 (pH = pKa). A buffer with 0.01 M each component has ten times lower capacity than one with 0.1 M each, even though both have the same pH. Examiners often test this by asking which of two buffers will show a smaller pH change after adding a fixed amount of strong acid.

Polyprotic acids (H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄) have successive Ka values (Ka₁ ≫ Ka₂ ≫ Ka₃). In a solution of NaH₂PO₄/Na₂HPO₄ the relevant equilibrium is H₂PO₄⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ with Ka₂ (pKa₂ ≈ 7.2). This system buffers near physiological pH. When calculating pH of a salt of a weak acid and weak base (e.g., NH₄CH₃COO), use the formula pH = ½(pKw + pKa(acid) – pKb(base)). For NH₄⁺ (pKa = 9.25) and CH₃COO⁻ (pKb = 9.25), pH ≈ 7.0, illustrating why ammonium acetate solutions are nearly neutral.

Effective Nuclear Charge, Shielding, and Periodic Trend Anomalies

Effective nuclear charge (Zeff) is the net positive charge felt by an electron after accounting for shielding by other electrons. Slater’s rules give a systematic way to estimate shielding constants (S): electrons in the same (n, l) group contribute 0.35 (0.30 for 1s), electrons in n‑1 shell contribute 0.85, and electrons in n‑2 or lower contribute 1.00. Zeff = Z – S. Across a period, Z increases while shielding rises only modestly, so Zeff grows, pulling the valence shell inward and decreasing atomic radius. Down a group, the principal quantum number n increases, adding a new shell; although Z also rises, the added shell’s electrons shield almost completely (≈ 1.00 each), so Zeff on the outermost electron changes little, and radius expands.

Ionisation energy (IE) generally follows Zeff: higher Zeff → higher IE. However, anomalies appear at group boundaries. The first IE of Group 13 (B, Al, Ga) is lower than that of the preceding Group 2 element because the electron removed is a p‑electron, which is less tightly held than the s‑electron of Group 2. Similarly, Group 16 (O, S) shows a dip relative to Group 15 (N, P) because the paired p‑electron experiences extra electron‑electron repulsion, making it easier to remove. These “pairing” and “subshell” anomalies are standard textbook points and appear frequently in multiple‑choice questions.

Electron affinity (EA) generally becomes more exothermic across a period, but the noble gases have positive EA (energy required) because the added electron must occupy a higher shell. Halogens have the most negative EA, yet fluorine’s EA is less negative than chlorine’s due to the compact 2p orbital causing greater repulsion. Down a group, EA becomes less exothermic as the added electron enters a more diffuse orbital, but the trend is irregular for the heavier halogens because of relativistic effects.

Metallic character decreases across a period (increasing Zeff, higher IE, lower tendency to lose electrons) and increases down a group (lower IE, larger radius). The diagonal relationship — similarity between certain pairs like Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si — arises because moving one period down increases size and decreases Zeff, while moving one group right increases Zeff and decreases size; the two effects partially cancel. For example, Li⁺ (radius ≈ 76 pm) and Mg²⁺ (radius ≈ 72 pm) have comparable charge densities, leading to similar hydrolysis behaviour and solubility patterns.

Lanthanide contraction — the steady decrease in ionic radii across the 4f series (La³⁺ ≈ 103 pm to Lu³⁺ ≈ 86 pm) — results from poor shielding of the nuclear charge by the diffuse 4f electrons. Consequently, the 5d transition metals (Hf, Ta, W) have radii almost identical to their 4d counterparts (Zr, Nb, Mo). This explains why Zr/Hf and Nb/Ta are chemically almost inseparable, a fact exploited in their industrial separation by fractional crystallisation of fluorides.

Diagonal Relationships, Lanthanide Contraction, and Oxidation State Prediction

The diagonal relationship is a practical tool for predicting chemical similarity when periodic trends oppose each other. Moving down a group increases metallic character and atomic size; moving right across a period decreases size and increases electronegativity. The combined effect often yields pairs with comparable charge density (ionic charge/ionic radius). Classic examples: Li⁺ (76 pm) vs Mg²⁺ (72 pm), Be²⁺ (45 pm) vs Al³⁺ (53 pm), B³⁺ (27 pm) vs Si⁴⁺ (40 pm). These similarities manifest in hydrolysis of cations (Li⁺ and Mg²⁺ both form weakly acidic aqua ions), solubility of carbonates (Li₂CO₃ and MgCO₃ are sparingly soluble), and formation of covalent character in otherwise ionic compounds (BeCl₂ and AlCl₃ are largely covalent).

Lanthanide contraction, as noted, compresses the 5d series to near‑identical sizes with the 4d series. This has direct consequences for oxidation‑state chemistry. For the early 5d elements (Hf, Ta, W), the +4 oxidation state (Hf⁴⁺, Ta⁴⁺, W⁴⁺) is common and stable, mirroring Zr⁴⁺, Nb⁴⁺, Mo⁴⁺. However, the higher oxidation states (+5 for Ta, +6 for W) become increasingly accessible because the contracted 5d orbitals overlap better with ligand orbitals, stabilising multiple bonds (e.g., WO₄²⁻, TaO₄⁻). In contrast, the 4d analogues show a stronger preference for lower oxidation states.

Predicting the most stable oxidation state for a transition metal involves the “18‑electron rule” for organometallics and the “maximum oxidation state = group number” for early transition metals. For groups 3–7, the highest oxidation state equals the group number (Sc +3, Ti +4, V +5, Cr +6, Mn +7). Beyond group 7, the maximum oxidation state rarely exceeds +6 (Fe +6 in FeO₄²⁻ is rare). The stability of a given oxidation state also depends on the ligand field: strong‑field ligands (CN⁻, CO) stabilise low‑spin configurations and often lower oxidation states, while weak‑field ligands (H₂O, Cl⁻) favour higher oxidation states.

In the p‑block, the inert‑pair effect explains why heavier Group 13–16 elements prefer oxidation states two units lower than the group valence. For Tl (Group 13), +1 is more stable than +3; for Pb (Group 14), +2 dominates over +4; for Bi (Group 15), +3 is favoured over +5. This arises because the ns² electrons become increasingly reluctant to participate in bonding due to relativistic stabilisation of the s orbital and poor shielding by the (n‑1)d and (n‑2)f electrons. Consequently, compounds like TlCl, PbO, and BiCl₃ are far more common than their higher‑oxidation‑state counterparts.

When a question asks for the oxidation state of a metal in a complex, assign known ligand charges (Cl⁻ = –1, CN⁻ = –1, H₂O = 0, NH₃ = 0, CO = 0, NO⁺ = +1) and solve for the metal. For example, in [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻, each CN⁻ contributes –1 (total –6). The overall charge is –4, so Fe must be +2. In [MnO₄]⁻, each O is –2 (total –8); overall –1 gives Mn = +7. This bookkeeping method works for all coordination compounds and is a staple of quantitative inorganic questions.

  • Confusing steric number with coordination number — count only σ‑bonds and lone pairs on the central atom, not π‑bonds or ligands attached through dative bonds that do not change the electron‑domain count.
  • Assuming all transition‑metal cations lose d‑electrons first — the (n + 1)s electrons are removed before nd electrons, so Fe²⁺ is 3d⁶ not 4s²3d⁴.
  • Treating electron affinity as uniformly more exothermic across a period — fluorine’s EA is less negative than chlorine’s because of 2p orbital crowding.
  • Applying the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation without checking the ratio range — it is reliable only when 0.1 ≤ [A⁻]/[HA] ≤ 10; outside this the buffer capacity is negligible.
  • Overlooking the inert‑pair effect when predicting oxidation states of heavy p‑block elements — Tl⁺, Pb²⁺, Bi³⁺ are far more stable than the group‑valence states.
  • Using Slater’s rules incorrectly for d‑ and f‑electrons — electrons in the same (n, l) group shield 0.35, but for nd or nf electrons the shielding by electrons in the same group is 0.35 only for the first electron; subsequent electrons in the same subshell shield 0.35 as well, while electrons in lower shells shield 1.00.
  • Assuming diagonal relationships hold for all properties — they are strong for charge‑density‑dependent traits (hydrolysis, solubility) but weak for redox potentials and complex‑formation constants.

Thermochemistry: Enthalpy, Hess’s Law, and Born–Haber Cycles

Thermochemistry quantifies heat exchanged during chemical reactions. The standard enthalpy change of reaction (ΔH°rxn) equals the sum of standard enthalpies of formation of products minus that of reactants: ΔH°rxn = Σ n ΔH°f(products) – Σ m ΔH°f(reactants). Standard enthalpies of formation (ΔH°f) refer to one mole of compound formed from its elements in their reference states at 298 K and 1 bar; by convention ΔH°f of an element in its reference state is zero. Hess’s law states that the total enthalpy change is pathway‑independent, allowing algebraic combination of known thermochemical equations. A classic application is the Born–Haber cycle for an ionic solid such as NaCl. The cycle links lattice energy (U) to measurable quantities: sublimation of Na(s) → Na(g) (ΔHsub), ionisation of Na(g) → Na⁺(g) (IE), dissociation of ½ Cl₂(g) → Cl(g) (½ D), electron affinity of Cl(g) → Cl⁻(g) (EA), and formation of NaCl(s) from gaseous ions (–U). Summing these steps gives ΔH°f(NaCl) = ΔHsub + IE + ½ D + EA – U, so U = ΔHsub + IE + ½ D + EA – ΔH°f. Using typical tabulated values (ΔHsub ≈ 108 kJ mol⁻¹, IE ≈ 496 kJ mol⁻¹, D ≈ 242 kJ mol⁻¹, EA ≈ –349 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔH°f ≈ –411 kJ mol⁻¹) yields U ≈ 787 kJ mol⁻¹, consistent with experimental lattice energies. The cycle also illustrates why compounds with high lattice energies (small, highly charged ions) are more stable. Enthalpy changes of solution, hydration, and neutralisation follow the same additive principle. In examinations, a frequent twist provides a multi‑step cycle with one missing quantity (often electron affinity or lattice energy); careful bookkeeping of signs and stoichiometric coefficients is essential.

Electrochemistry: Redox Balancing, Cell Potentials, and the Nernst Equation

Electrochemistry connects chemical change to electrical energy. A redox reaction splits into oxidation and reduction half‑reactions, each balanced for mass and charge in acidic or basic medium. In acidic solution, O atoms are balanced with H₂O, H atoms with H⁺, and charge with electrons. In basic solution, after balancing as in acid, add OH⁻ to both sides to neutralise H⁺, forming water. The standard electrode potential (E°) of a half‑cell is measured versus the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) at 298 K, 1 M ion activity, 1 bar gas pressure. The cell potential for a galvanic cell is E°cell = E°cathode – E°anode; a positive value indicates spontaneity under standard conditions. The Nernst equation extends this to non‑standard conditions: E = E° – (RT/nF) ln Q, where R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, F = 96485 C mol⁻¹, n = moles of electrons transferred, and Q is the reaction quotient. At 298 K, RT/F ≈ 0.02569 V, giving the convenient form E = E° – (0.05916/n) log₁₀ Q. For the Daniell cell Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s), E° = +0.34 V – (–0.76 V) = +1.10 V. If [Zn²⁺] = 0.01 M and [Cu²⁺] = 1.0 M, Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] = 0.01, n = 2, so E = 1.10 – (0.05916/2) log₁₀(0.01) = 1.159 V. Concentration cells, with identical electrodes but different ion concentrations, generate potential solely from the concentration gradient: E = (0.05916/n) log₁₀([cathode]/[anode]). Electrochemical series tables list E° values; a common examiner trick gives a reaction written in reverse and asks for the cell potential, requiring a sign change. Another twist involves calculating the equilibrium constant K from E° using ΔG° = –nFE° = –RT ln K, yielding log₁₀ K = nE°/0.05916 at 298 K.

Worked Example: Nernst Equation Application
A galvanic cell uses a silver electrode in 0.10 M AgNO₃ and a copper electrode in 0.010 M CuSO₄. Standard reduction potentials: Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag(s) E° = +0.80 V; Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) E° = +0.34 V. Calculate the cell potential at 298 K.
Cathode (higher E°) = Ag⁺/Ag; anode = Cu/Cu²⁺. Overall reaction: 2 Ag⁺ + Cu(s) → 2 Ag(s) + Cu²⁺. n = 2. E°cell = 0.80 – 0.34 = 0.46 V. Q = [Cu²⁺]/[Ag⁺]² = 0.010 / (0.10)² = 1.0. Since Q = 1, log₁₀ Q = 0, so E = E° = 0.46 V. The cell potential equals the standard potential because the ion activities give Q = 1.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Electrochemistry
Memorise the sign convention: E°cell = E°cathode – E°anode. When a half‑reaction is reversed, change the sign of its potential. For Nernst calculations, write the balanced overall reaction first to obtain n and Q. Use the 0.05916 V factor at 298 K; if temperature differs, compute RT/nF explicitly. Check units: concentrations in mol L⁻¹, pressures in bar. Quick sanity check: if Q < 1, log Q is negative, so E > E°; if Q > 1, E < E°.

Chemical Kinetics: Rate Laws, Activation Energy, and Catalysis

Chemical kinetics studies reaction rates and controlling factors. The rate law expresses rate as rate = k[A]ˣ[B]ʸ, where orders x, y are determined experimentally; overall order is x + y. For a first‑order reaction, the integrated rate law is ln([A]₀/[A]ₜ) = kt (linear plot of ln [A] vs. time). For second‑order (rate = k[A]²): 1/[A]ₜ – 1/[A]₀ = kt. Zero‑order: [A]ₜ = [A]₀ – kt. Half‑life (t₁/₂) depends on order: first‑order t₁/₂ = ln 2/k (independent of initial concentration), second‑order t₁/₂ = 1/(k[A]₀), zero‑order t₁/₂ = [A]₀/(2k). Temperature dependence follows the Arrhenius equation: k = A exp(–Eₐ/RT); ln k = ln A – Eₐ/(RT). A plot of ln k vs. 1/T gives slope –Eₐ/R. Catalysts provide an alternative pathway with lower Eₐ, increasing k without being consumed. Homogeneous catalysts share the reactants’ phase (e.g., enzymes); heterogeneous catalysts differ in phase (e.g., solid Pt in hydrogenation). Rate enhancement: kcat/kuncat = exp[(Eₐ,uncat – Eₐ,cat)/RT]. Common exam patterns: given concentration‑time data, determine order by testing linearity of integrated plots; given two rate constants at different temperatures, find Eₐ via ln(k₂/k₁) = –Eₐ/R (1/T₂ – 1/T₁). Careful unit conversion (kJ mol⁻¹ vs J mol⁻¹) is essential.

Coordination Chemistry: Crystal Field Theory, Spectrochemical Series, and Magnetic Properties

Crystal field theory (CFT) explains electronic structure and properties of transition‑metal complexes by treating ligands as point charges that split the degenerate d‑orbitals. In an octahedral field, the five d‑orbitals split into lower‑energy t₂g (dxy, dxz, dyz) and higher‑energy eg (dz², dx²–y²) sets, with splitting energy Δo. In tetrahedral geometry the splitting is reversed and smaller: Δt ≈ 4/9 Δo. Δ magnitude depends on metal oxidation state, period (3d < 4d < 5d), and ligand field strength. The spectrochemical series orders ligands by increasing Δ: I⁻ < Br⁻ < SCN⁻ (S) < Cl⁻ < NO₃⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ (N) < CH₃CN < py < NH₃ < en < bipy < phen < NO₂⁻ < PPh₃ < CN⁻ < CO. Strong‑field ligands (CN⁻, CO) give large Δ, favouring low‑spin configurations (electrons pair in t₂g before occupying eg). Weak‑field ligands (H₂O, halides) give high‑spin configurations. For d⁴–d⁷ octahedral ions, spin state determines unpaired electrons and magnetic moment μso = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n = unpaired electrons. Example: [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ (Fe²⁺, d⁶, strong field) is low‑spin t₂g⁶ eg⁰, n = 0, diamagnetic. [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (weak field) is high‑spin t₂g⁴ eg², n = 4, μ ≈ 4.90 BM. Tetrahedral complexes are almost always high‑spin because Δt is small. Square‑planar geometry, common for d⁸ ions (Ni²⁺, Pt²⁺, Pd²⁺), arises from large splitting placing dx²–y² highest, giving low‑spin, often diamagnetic configurations. CFT also rationalises colours: absorption of visible light promotes an electron from t₂g to eg; the wavelength absorbed corresponds to Δ. The complementary colour is observed. Typical exam questions: given a complex formula, predict geometry, spin state, unpaired electrons, and magnetic moment; given UV‑Vis absorption maxima, estimate Δ in kJ mol⁻¹ using Δ = (hcNA)/λ.

Spectroscopic Techniques: UV‑Vis, IR, and NMR Fundamentals

Spectroscopy probes electromagnetic radiation–matter interactions, yielding structural and electronic information. UV‑Vis (200–800 nm) measures electronic transitions. For transition‑metal complexes, d‑d transitions are Laporte‑forbidden in centrosymmetric environments, giving weak molar absorptivities (ε ≈ 10–100 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹). Charge‑transfer bands (ligand‑to‑metal or metal‑to‑ligand) are allowed and intense (ε > 10³). Organic molecules show π→π* and n→π* transitions; conjugated systems shift absorption to longer wavelengths (bathochromic shift). Beer–Lambert law: A = ε c l (A = absorbance, c = concentration in mol L⁻¹, l = path length in cm). Infrared (IR) spectroscopy (4000–400 cm⁻¹) detects vibrational transitions. Characteristic stretching frequencies: O–H (broad, 3400–3200 cm⁻¹), N–H (3300–3500 cm⁻¹), C=O (strong, 1750–1650 cm⁻¹), C≡C (2100–2260 cm⁻¹), C–O (1050–1150 cm⁻¹). The fingerprint region (1500–400 cm⁻¹) is unique to each molecule. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) exploits magnetic properties of spin‑½ nuclei (¹H, ¹³C). In field B₀, nuclei precess at Larmor frequency ν = γB₀/2π. Chemical shift δ (ppm) = (νsample – νref)/νref × 10⁶, referenced to tetramethylsilane (TMS) at 0 ppm. Electron‑withdrawing groups deshield nuclei, moving signals downfield (higher δ). Typical ¹H ranges: alkyl 0.9–1.5 ppm, allylic 1.6–2.2, α to carbonyl 2.1–2.5, aromatic 6.5–8.0, aldehydic 9–10. Spin‑spin coupling splits signals; the n+1 rule predicts peaks for a proton coupled to n equivalent neighbours. Integration gives relative proton counts. ¹³C NMR (broadband decoupled) shows one signal per distinct carbon; DEPT experiments distinguish CH₃, CH₂, CH, and quaternary carbons. Common exam tasks: match unknown compound’s IR and ¹H NMR data to a structure; calculate number of ¹³C NMR signals for a given symmetry.

  • Confusing the sign of ΔH in a Born–Haber cycle when reversing a step — always track the direction of each process.
  • Using the wrong n value in the Nernst equation — n is the total electrons transferred in the balanced overall cell reaction, not per half‑reaction.
  • Assuming a first‑order half‑life depends on initial concentration — it is constant for a given temperature.
  • Assigning low‑spin configuration to a tetrahedral complex — Δt is too small; tetrahedral complexes are high‑spin.
  • Misreading IR carbonyl stretches — conjugated C=O appears at lower wavenumber (≈ 1680 cm⁻¹) than non‑conjugated (≈ 1715 cm⁻¹).
  • Forgetting that ¹H NMR integration gives relative, not absolute, proton numbers — always normalise to the smallest integer ratio.

Chemical Equilibrium and the Reaction Quotient

The equilibrium constants Kc (concentration basis) and Kp (partial‑pressure basis) follow from the law of mass action: for a generic reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Kc = [C]^c[D]^d / ([A]^a[B]^b) where brackets denote equilibrium concentrations. Kp = (P_C)^c(P_D)^d / ((P_A)^a(P_B)^b) with pressures in bar. The two constants are related by Kp = Kc(RT)Δn_gas, where Δn_gas = (c+d) – (a+b) for gaseous species only. The reaction quotient Q has the same form as K but uses instantaneous concentrations or pressures. Comparing Q with K predicts the net reaction direction: if Q < K the system proceeds forward; if Q > K it proceeds in reverse; if Q = K the system is at equilibrium.

Le Chatelier’s principle: a system at equilibrium shifts to counteract a disturbance. Increasing a reactant concentration or decreasing a product concentration drives the reaction forward. For gaseous equilibria, increasing total pressure (or decreasing volume) favors the side with fewer gas moles. Temperature changes alter K itself: for an exothermic reaction (ΔH < 0) increasing temperature decreases K, shifting equilibrium toward reactants; for an endothermic reaction the opposite occurs. Catalysts accelerate forward and reverse rates equally, shortening the time to equilibrium without changing its composition.

Heterogeneous equilibria involve more than one phase. Pure solids and liquids have unit activity and are omitted from equilibrium expressions. For example, in CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g) the equilibrium constant reduces to Kp = P_CO₂. Students often err by including solid concentrations. The van't Hoff equation, ln(K₂/K₁) = –ΔH°/R (1/T₂ – 1/T₁), estimates ΔH° from K at two temperatures, assuming ΔH° is constant over the range.

Typical examination tasks: given initial concentrations and Kc, set up an ICE table and solve the resulting quadratic (or higher‑order) equation for equilibrium concentrations; calculate Kp from Kc for a gas‑phase reaction at a specified temperature; predict the effect of adding an inert gas at constant volume versus constant pressure; use the van't Hoff plot (ln K vs 1/T) to determine ΔH° and ΔS° from the slope and intercept.

Gibbs Free Energy, Entropy, and Spontaneity

The second law introduces entropy (S), the number of microscopic configurations consistent with a macroscopic state. For a reversible isothermal process, ΔS = q_rev/T. Total entropy change of the universe determines spontaneity: ΔS_univ > 0 (spontaneous), = 0 (equilibrium), < 0 (non‑spontaneous). Since evaluating ΔS_surr is often inconvenient, Gibbs free energy G = H – TS is defined for the system at constant T and P. The spontaneity criterion becomes ΔG = ΔH – TΔS: ΔG < 0 indicates spontaneity; ΔG = 0 marks equilibrium; ΔG > 0 means non‑spontaneous forward.

Standard Gibbs free energies of formation (ΔG_f°) are tabulated at 298 K and 1 bar. For any reaction, ΔG° = ΣνΔG_f°(products) – ΣνΔG_f°(reactants). ΔG° relates to K by ΔG° = –RT ln K. This links thermodynamics to equilibrium: large negative ΔG° gives large K (product‑favored); large positive ΔG° gives small K (reactant‑favored). From ΔG° = ΔH° – TΔS°, if ΔH° and ΔS° are both positive, spontaneity occurs only above T = ΔH°/ΔS°; if both are negative, spontaneity occurs only below that temperature.

Coupled reactions show how a non‑spontaneous step can be driven by a highly spontaneous one. In biology, ATP hydrolysis (ΔG° ≈ –30.5 kJ mol⁻¹) powers endergonic processes like muscle contraction and active transport. The overall ΔG is the sum of individual ΔG values; if negative, the coupled process is spontaneous. Electrochemical cells give ΔG = –nFE_cell, where n is electrons transferred and F is Faraday's constant, connecting thermodynamic driving force to measurable cell potentials.

Common pitfalls: confusing ΔG (actual free‑energy change under non‑standard conditions) with ΔG° (standard state); forgetting that ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q, so a reaction with positive ΔG° can proceed forward if Q is sufficiently small; treating ΔS° of a reaction as the simple sum of standard molar entropies without stoichiometric coefficients; assuming a negative ΔH alone guarantees spontaneity, ignoring the –TΔS term which can dominate at high temperature.

Worked Example: Determining Spontaneity from ΔH° and ΔS°
For the reaction N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) ⇌ 2 NH₃(g), ΔH° = –92.2 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = –198 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. Calculate ΔG° at 298 K and state whether the reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions. Then estimate the temperature above which the reaction becomes non‑spontaneous, assuming ΔH° and ΔS° are temperature‑independent.
First convert ΔS° to kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: –198 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = –0.198 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. ΔG° = ΔH° – TΔS° = –92.2 kJ mol⁻¹ – (298 K)(–0.198 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹) = –92.2 kJ mol⁻¹ + 59.0 kJ mol⁻¹ = –33.2 kJ mol⁻¹. Since ΔG° < 0, the reaction is spontaneous at 298 K under standard conditions. The crossover temperature T_eq = ΔH°/ΔS° = (–92.2 kJ mol⁻¹)/(–0.198 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹) ≈ 466 K. Above ≈ 466 K the –TΔS term outweighs the exothermic ΔH, making ΔG° positive and the forward reaction non‑spontaneous.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the temperature at which a reaction changes spontaneity, always compute T = ΔH°/ΔS° using consistent units (kJ with kJ, J with J). Write the unit conversion explicitly; a single unit mismatch is the most common source of lost marks. For ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q problems, first calculate ΔG° from tabulated ΔG_f° values, then evaluate Q from the given concentrations or pressures. Remember that R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; if ΔG° is in kJ, convert R to 0.008314 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ before multiplying by T.

Colligative Properties of Solutions

Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles per unit volume of solvent, not their chemical identity. The four principal effects are vapor‑pressure lowering (Raoult's law), boiling‑point elevation, freezing‑point depression, and osmotic pressure. For ideal dilute solutions, Raoult's law states P_solvent = X_solvent P°_solvent, where X_solvent is the solvent mole fraction and P°_solvent is the pure solvent vapor pressure. Relative vapor‑pressure lowering equals the solute mole fraction: (P° – P)/P° = X_solute.

Boiling‑point elevation ΔT_b = i K_b m and freezing‑point depression ΔT_f = i K_f m, where m is molality, K_b and K_f are solvent constants, and i is the van't Hoff factor (particles per formula unit). For non‑electrolytes i = 1; for NaCl i ≈ 2; for CaCl₂ i ≈ 3. Real solutions deviate due to ion‑pairing and inter‑ionic attractions; effective i is often lower than theoretical, especially at higher concentrations. The Debye–Hückel limiting law gives a first correction for activity coefficients in dilute electrolytes.

Osmotic pressure π is the pressure needed to stop net solvent flow across a semipermeable membrane. For dilute solutions, van't Hoff's equation π = i M R T applies, with M as solute molarity. This mirrors the ideal‑gas law and underpins molar mass determination of macromolecules by measuring π at several concentrations and extrapolating to zero. For non‑dissociating solutes, molar mass M_m = (mass × R T)/(π V).

Typical examination questions: calculate the boiling point of a 0.5 mol kg⁻¹ aqueous NaCl solution given K_b = 0.512 K kg mol⁻¹; determine the molar mass of an unknown polymer from osmotic pressure data at two concentrations; explain why the observed freezing‑point depression for a 0.1 m MgSO₄ solution is smaller than the theoretical value predicted with i = 2; compare the magnitude of colligative effects for equimolal solutions of glucose, NaCl, and CaCl₂.

Solid State Structures and Defects

Crystalline solids are described by a 3D Bravais lattice with a basis of atoms. 14 Bravais lattices fall into seven crystal systems: cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, hexagonal, rhombohedral, monoclinic, triclinic. The cubic system has three lattice types: simple cubic (SC), body‑centred cubic (BCC), and face‑centred cubic (FCC). Packing efficiency is 52 % for SC, 68 % for BCC, and 74 % for FCC (cubic close‑packed). Hexagonal close‑packed (HCP) also achieves 74 % efficiency with ABAB… stacking, while FCC follows ABCABC…. Coordination numbers: 6 (SC), 8 (BCC), 12 (FCC and HCP).

Real crystals contain point defects altering physical properties. Schottky defects are paired cation/anion vacancies preserving charge neutrality, common in high‑coordination, similarly sized ionic compounds like NaCl. Frenkel defects involve a cation moving to an interstitial site, leaving a vacancy; they prevail when the cation is much smaller than the anion (e.g., AgCl). Both increase with temperature per Arrhenius relations: n/N = exp(–E_f/2kT) (Schottky) and exp(–E_f/kT) (Frenkel), where E_f is formation energy. Non‑stoichiometric compounds (e.g., Fe₁₋ₓO) show metal deficiency/excess via mixed oxidation states and vacancy clusters.

Line defects (dislocations) and planar defects (grain boundaries, stacking faults) govern mechanical properties like yield strength and ductility. Edge dislocations have an extra half‑plane of atoms; screw dislocations involve helical displacement. The Burgers vector characterizes the lattice distortion magnitude and direction. In semiconductors, doping introduces impurities that donate electrons (n‑type) or accept electrons (p‑type), shifting the Fermi level and enabling p‑n junctions.

Band theory explains conductivity: in metals the valence band overlaps the conduction band or is partially filled, giving high conductivity. Insulators have a large band gap (>5 eV) between filled valence and empty conduction bands. Semiconductors have moderate gaps (≈1 eV for Si, 0.7 eV for Ge), so thermal excitation yields appreciable carriers at room temperature. Intrinsic carrier concentration follows n_i ∝ T3/2 exp(–E_g/2kT). Extrinsic doping dominates until the intrinsic regime at high temperature.

Common Traps

  • Using Kc instead of Kp (or vice‑versa) without applying the (RT)Δn_gas conversion factor.
  • Including pure solids or liquids in the equilibrium expression for heterogeneous equilibria.
  • Confusing ΔG° (standard) with ΔG (actual) and forgetting the RT ln Q term when the reaction mixture is not at standard state.
  • Assuming the van't Hoff factor i equals the theoretical number of ions for all concentrations; neglecting ion‑pairing corrections in colligative‑property calculations.
  • Treating the packing efficiency of BCC as 74 % (the FCC/HCP value) instead of 68 %.
  • Assigning a Frenkel defect to a compound with similarly sized ions (e.g., NaCl) where Schottky defects dominate.
  • Using the wrong sign for ΔS° when calculating the crossover temperature T = ΔH°/ΔS°; both ΔH° and ΔS° must carry their correct signs.

Mastering these advanced concepts and avoiding the listed traps will give you a decisive edge in any chemistry examination that tests equilibrium, thermodynamics, solution behaviour, and solid‑state fundamentals.

Phase Equilibria and the Gibbs Phase Rule

The Gibbs phase rule, F = C – P + 2, relates degrees of freedom F (independent intensive variables: temperature, pressure, composition) to components C and phases P at equilibrium. A component is the minimum number of independent species needed to define every phase’s composition. For a one‑component system (e.g., pure H₂O), F = 3 – P. In single‑phase regions (solid, liquid, vapour) F = 2; both T and P vary independently. Along a two‑phase coexistence line P = 2, F = 1; only one variable changes without altering phase count. At the triple point P = 3, F = 0; T and P are fixed. For a two‑component system (e.g., NaCl–H₂O), F = 4 – P. In a single‑phase region F = 3 (T, P, one composition variable). In a two‑phase region F = 2, permitting tie‑line construction on T–X or P–X diagrams. The lever rule follows from mass balance: phase amounts are inversely proportional to the tie‑line segments on either side of the overall composition.

Binary phase diagrams show liquidus and solidus curves meeting at the eutectic point, where a liquid of fixed composition solidifies into two solid phases of fixed compositions at constant temperature. In systems with complete solid solubility (e.g., Cu–Ni) the curves form a continuous loop; no eutectic occurs. Peritectic systems exhibit a reaction where a liquid and solid combine on cooling to form a second solid of different composition. The Clausius–Clapeyron equation, dP/dT = ΔH / (T ΔV), gives the slope of any first‑order coexistence curve. For most solid–liquid transitions ΔV > 0 (positive slope); for water ΔV < 0, so the melting curve slopes toward lower pressure as temperature rises. Integrating the Clausius–Clapeyron relation with constant ΔH and ΔV yields the logarithmic form used to estimate vapour pressures away from the normal boiling point.

Surface Chemistry: Adsorption Isotherms and Catalysis

Adsorption is the accumulation of molecules (adsorbate) at a solid or liquid surface (adsorbent). Physisorption involves weak van der Waals forces, is reversible, occurs at low temperatures, and has enthalpies of 20–40 kJ mol⁻¹. Chemisorption involves chemical bond formation, is often irreversible, requires activation energy, and shows enthalpies of 100–400 kJ mol⁻¹. Equilibrium adsorption is described by isotherms. The Langmuir model assumes a uniform surface with a fixed number of identical sites, monolayer coverage only, and no adsorbate–adsorbate interactions. Fractional coverage θ = (K P) / (1 + K P), where K is the adsorption equilibrium constant and P is gas pressure. At low pressure θ ≈ K P (Henry’s law region); at high pressure θ → 1 (saturation). The linearised form P/θ = 1/K + P yields K and monolayer capacity from a plot of P/θ versus P.

The BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) isotherm extends Langmuir to multilayer adsorption. It introduces a constant C related to the heat of adsorption in the first layer versus subsequent layers. The common linear form is:
P / [V (P₀ – P)] = 1 / (Vₘ C) + (C – 1) P / (Vₘ C P₀), where V is volume adsorbed at pressure P, P₀ is saturation vapour pressure, and Vₘ is monolayer capacity. A plot of the left‑hand side versus P/P₀ is linear for 0.05 < P/P₀ < 0.35; slope and intercept give Vₘ and C. Specific surface area S = Vₘ Nₐ σ / Vₘₒₗ, where σ is the adsorbate cross‑sectional area (0.162 nm² for N₂ at 77 K) and Vₘₒₗ is the molar volume at STP.

Surface catalysis follows the Langmuir–Hinshelwood mechanism when both reactants adsorb on the same site type, or the Eley–Rideal mechanism when one adsorbs and the other reacts from the gas phase. For a bimolecular surface reaction A* + B* → products, rate ∝ θ_A θ_B. Substituting Langmuir expressions gives rate laws showing fractional orders and high‑pressure inhibition. In heterogeneous catalysis, turnover frequency (TOF) = (moles product per unit time) / (moles active sites) is the key metric. Promoters raise TOF by modifying electronic structure; poisons block active sites. The Sabatier principle states optimal activity occurs at intermediate adsorption energy—neither too weak (no activation) nor too strong (desorption limited). This volcano‑plot relationship guides catalyst design for ammonia synthesis (Fe with K₂O, Al₂O₃ promoters) and CO oxidation (Pt, Pd).

Worked Example: Gibbs Phase Rule Application
A system contains the components H₂O and NaCl. At a certain temperature and pressure three phases coexist: ice, a saturated NaCl solution, and solid NaCl. How many degrees of freedom does this system have?
Identify C = 2 (H₂O and NaCl). Phases present: ice (solid H₂O), aqueous solution (liquid), solid NaCl → P = 3. Apply the Gibbs phase rule: F = C – P + 2 = 2 – 3 + 2 = 1. The system has one degree of freedom; either temperature or pressure can be varied independently, but not both without changing the number of phases.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Phase Rule Questions
Count components carefully: a component is not the same as a chemical species if a reaction relates them. Write the phase rule, substitute C and P, and solve for F. Remember the “+2” accounts for temperature and pressure; if the problem states “at constant pressure” replace +2 by +1. For binary diagrams, sketch a quick T–X diagram and use the lever rule to check mass‑balance calculations.

Nuclear Chemistry: Radioactive Decay Kinetics and Applications

Radioactive decay follows first‑order kinetics: disintegration rate ∝ number of radioactive nuclei N. The differential equation dN/dt = –λN integrates to N = N₀ e^(–λt), where λ is the decay constant (s⁻¹). Half‑life t₁/₂ = ln 2 / λ ≈ 0.693 / λ. Activity A = λN (becquerel, Bq) decays exponentially: A = A₀ e^(–λt). For a decay chain A → B → C, the Bateman equations give each daughter’s activity. If the parent half‑life is much longer than the daughter’s (secular equilibrium), daughter activity equals parent activity after a few daughter half‑lives. If the parent half‑life is shorter (transient equilibrium), daughter activity exceeds parent activity by a factor λ_parent / (λ_daughter – λ_parent) at equilibrium.

Common decay modes: α‑emission reduces mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2; β⁻‑emission increases atomic number by 1 (n → p + e⁻ + ν̅ₑ); β⁺‑emission or electron capture decreases atomic number by 1 (p → n + e⁺ + νₑ). γ‑emission accompanies other decays to de‑excite the nucleus without changing A or Z. The Q‑value is the mass‑energy difference: Q = [m_parent – (m_daughter + m_emitted)] c². For β⁻ decay Q = (M_parent – M_daughter) c² (atomic masses include electrons). Positron emission requires Q > 1.022 MeV to create the e⁺e⁻ pair.

Applications: Radiocarbon dating uses β⁻ decay of ¹⁴C (t₁/₂ = 5730 y) in once‑living material. The atmospheric ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio is assumed constant; age t = (1/λ) ln (A₀/A). Uranium‑lead dating exploits dual chains ²³⁸U → ²⁰⁶Pb (t₁/₂ = 4.468 Ga) and ²³⁵U → ²⁰⁷Pb (t₁/₂ = 0.704 Ga); concordia diagrams test closed‑system behaviour. In medicine, ⁹⁹ᵐTc (t₁/₂ = 6.01 h, γ‑emitter) from a ⁹⁹Mo/⁹⁹ᵐTc generator (parent t₁/₂ = 66 h) is used for SPECT imaging. Radiation therapy employs β⁻ emitters (⁹⁰Y, ¹⁷⁷Lu) or α emitters (²²⁵Ac) for targeted radionuclide therapy. Neutron activation analysis (NAA) identifies trace elements by irradiating a sample and measuring characteristic γ‑rays of induced radionuclides.

Chemical Potential, Fugacity, and Real‑Gas Behaviour

The chemical potential μ_i of species i in a mixture is the partial molar Gibbs free energy: μ_i = (∂G/∂n_i)T,P,n_j≠i. For a pure ideal gas μ = μ°(T) + RT ln(P/P°). In a real gas, pressure is replaced by fugacity f, defined so the same form holds: μ = μ°(T) + RT ln(f/P°). The fugacity coefficient φ = f/P approaches 1 as P → 0. For a van der Waals gas, ln φ = (b – a/RT) P/RT + … (virial expansion). The residual chemical potential μ^R = RT ln φ quantifies deviation from ideality. In a mixture, component fugacity f_i = y_i φ_i P, where y_i is mole fraction and φ_i is the component fugacity coefficient from an equation of state (e.g., Peng–Robinson). Equality of fugacities across phases is the phase‑equilibrium criterion: f_i^α = f_i^β for each component i.

The Gibbs–Duhem relation, Σ n_i dμ_i = –S dT + V dP, constrains chemical potentials in multicomponent systems. At constant T and P, Σ x_i dμ_i = 0, underlying the Gibbs–Duhem equation for activity coefficients in liquid solutions. Activity a_i = γ_i x_i (liquid) or a_i = f_i / f_i° (gas) gives a dimensionless effective concentration. The solute standard state in dilute solution is the hypothetical 1 mol kg⁻¹ ideal solution (Henry’s law); the solvent standard state is the pure liquid at system T and P (Raoult’s law). Excess Gibbs energy G^E = RT Σ x_i ln γ_i links activity coefficients to measurable data. Models such as Margules, van Laar, Wilson, NRTL, and UNIQUAC express G^E as a function of composition and temperature, enabling VLE prediction for non‑ideal mixtures.

For electrolyte solutions the mean ionic activity coefficient γ_± accounts for ion–ion interactions. The Debye–Hückel limiting law, log₁₀ γ_± = –A |z_+ z_-| √I, where I = ½ Σ m_i z_i² is ionic strength, is valid only at very low I (<0.01 mol kg⁻¹). The extended Debye–Hückel equation adds a linear term in I; the Pitzer equations give accurate γ_± up to high ionic strengths (6 mol kg⁻¹) by incorporating specific ion‑pair parameters. In electrochemical cells the Nernst equation uses activities: E = E° – (RT/nF) ln Q, where Q is the reaction quotient in activities. Neglecting activity corrections causes systematic errors in cell potentials, especially for concentrated or multivalent solutions.

  • Confusing components with species when a stoichiometric relation exists (e.g., CaCO₃ ⇌ CaO + CO₂ has C = 2, not 3).
  • Applying the Langmuir isotherm to multilayer data without checking the BET linear region (0.05–0.35 P/P₀).
  • Using the simple half‑life formula for a decay chain without verifying secular or transient equilibrium conditions.
  • Treating fugacity as equal to pressure for gases above 10 bar without calculating the fugacity coefficient from an appropriate equation of state.
  • Assuming activity coefficients are unity for all solutes in a mixed electrolyte; the Debye–Hückel limiting law fails above 0.01 mol kg⁻¹.
  • Neglecting the Gibbs–Duhem constraint when fitting binary VLE data, leading to thermodynamically inconsistent activity‑coefficient models.
  • Misidentifying the standard state for a solute (Henry’s law) versus the solvent (Raoult’s law) when converting between mole‑fraction and molality based activities.
