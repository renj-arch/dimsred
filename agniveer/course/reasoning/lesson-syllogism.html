What is Syllogism?

Syllogism is a logical reasoning topic where you are given two or more statements (premises) and you must determine which conclusions follow logically from them. Each statement involves relationships between categories using words like 'All', 'No', 'Some', and 'Some Not'. The exam typically asks 2-3 syllogism questions in Tier 1.

Types of Statements

1. Universal Affirmative (All A are B): Every member of category A belongs to category B. Venn diagram: circle A inside circle B.

2. Universal Negative (No A is B): No member of A belongs to B. Venn diagram: two non-overlapping circles.

3. Particular Affirmative (Some A are B): At least one member of A belongs to B. Venn diagram: overlapping circles.

4. Particular Negative (Some A are not B): At least one member of A does not belong to B. Venn diagram: part of A outside B.

The Venn Diagram Method

Draw overlapping circles for each category mentioned. For each statement, shade or mark regions that are definitely occupied or empty. A conclusion follows if it is true in ALL possible Venn diagrams that satisfy the given statements. If you can draw one valid diagram where the conclusion is false, the conclusion does not follow.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Basic)
Statements:
1. All pens are books.
2. No book is a table.

Conclusions:
I. No pen is a table.
II. Some books are pens.

Which conclusion(s) follow(s)?
Solution: Both I and II follow. From the statements: All pens are inside the set of books. No book is a table, so pens (being inside books) cannot be tables either â†’ Conclusion I follows. Conclusion II (Some books are pens) is also true since all pens are books, which implies at least some books are pens.
Example 2 (Either-Or Case)
Statements:
1. Some apples are mangoes.
2. No mango is a banana.

Conclusions:
I. Some apples are not bananas.
II. Some bananas are not apples.

Which conclusion(s) follow(s)?
Solution: Only I follows. From statements: some apples are mangoes, and no mango is banana. So those apples which are mangoes cannot be bananas â†’ Conclusion I follows. However, we cannot say anything definitive about bananas that are not apples â€” there might be some or none. So II does not follow.
Example 3 (Complementary Pair)
Statements:
1. All cats are animals.
2. Some animals are not dogs.

Conclusions:
I. Some cats are dogs.
II. No cat is a dog.

Which conclusion(s) follow(s)?
Solution: Neither I nor II follows individually. But I and II form a complementary pair (Some vs No â€” one affirmative, one negative covering all possibilities). In such cases, 'Either I or II follows' would be the correct answer if both options are available. Here, since we are just checking individually, neither is definitely true.

Shortcut Techniques

  • Use the rules of syllogism: If all A are B and all B are C, then all A are C. If no A is B and all C are A, then no C is B.
  • For 'Some' statements: at least one element of the subject belongs to the predicate.
  • Remember: 'All A are B' does NOT mean 'All B are A' â€” reverse is not always true.
  • In SSC, the either-or case occurs when two conclusions are complementary â€” one positive, one negative, covering all possibilities.
Pro Tip
Always draw at least two possible Venn diagrams if you're unsure. A conclusion follows ONLY if it is true in ALL possible diagrams. If one diagram shows it false, the conclusion does not follow.

Definite vs Possible Conclusions in Syllogism

In every syllogism the examiner distinguishes between conclusions that are definitely true in all admissible Venn diagrams and those that are merely possible in at least one diagram. A definite conclusion follows when the logical structure forces the relationship regardless of how the non‑specified elements are arranged. A possible conclusion, by contrast, holds in some arrangements but fails in others; therefore it cannot be selected as a valid inference unless the question explicitly asks for “which of the following may be true”. The key to separating the two lies in drawing the minimum and maximum overlap diagrams for each premise set. The minimum diagram places the subject and predicate circles as far apart as the premises allow; the maximum diagram merges them as much as the premises permit. If a conclusion appears in both extremes, it is definite. If it appears only in the maximum (or only in the minimum) it is merely possible. This two‑diagram test eliminates the common error of assuming that “some A are B” guarantees “some A are not B” – the latter is only possible, not definite, because the minimum diagram may have the whole of A inside B.

When a premise set contains a universal affirmative (“All A are B”) together with a particular affirmative (“Some B are C”), the definite conclusions are limited to those that can be derived by the transitive chain “All A are B” + “Some B are C” → “Some A are C” (definite) and “Some C are A” (definite by conversion). The conclusion “All A are C” is not definite because the particular premise does not distribute the middle term. Conversely, “Some A are not C” is only possible, since the minimum diagram can place all A inside C while still satisfying “Some B are C”. Practising the two‑diagram method on every new premise combination trains the mind to spot the distribution of terms and to avoid the trap of over‑generalising from a single diagram.

Examiners often frame answer options as “Only I follows”, “Only II follows”, “Either I or II follows”, “Neither I nor II follows”, and “Both I and II follow”. The “Either I or II follows” option appears exclusively when the two conclusions form a complementary pair – one affirmative, one negative – and together they exhaust all logical possibilities. In such cases each conclusion individually is only possible, but the pair as a whole is definite. Recognising this pattern requires checking whether the two conclusions are logical contradictories (e.g., “Some X are Y” vs “No X are Y”) or sub‑contraries (e.g., “Some X are Y” vs “Some X are not Y”). Only contradictory pairs generate a true either‑or situation; sub‑contrary pairs can both be true simultaneously, so “Either I or II follows” would be incorrect.

Complementary Pair Mechanics and Either-Or Logic

A complementary pair in syllogism consists of two conclusions that cannot both be false at the same time. The classic forms are the contradictory pair (A vs O, E vs I) and the contrary pair (A vs E) when the subject term is distributed in both. In the standard four‑figure mood list, the only moods that produce a genuine either‑or are those where the premises leave the relationship between the extreme terms completely undetermined – for example, “All A are B” and “Some B are not C”. Here the extreme terms A and C may be related as “Some A are C” (I) or “No A are C” (E). Both cannot be false together because if “Some A are C” is false then “No A are C” must be true, and vice‑versa. The examiner’s trick is to present the two conclusions as separate options and also include an “Either I or II follows” option. The correct choice is the either‑or option only when the two conclusions are logical contradictories covering the entire possibility space.

To verify a complementary pair, draw the two extreme Venn diagrams: one that makes the first conclusion true and the second false, and another that makes the second true and the first false. If both diagrams satisfy all premises, the pair is complementary. If a premise forces a specific relationship (e.g., “All A are B” and “All B are C” forces “All A are C”), then the contradictory conclusions cannot both be accommodated – one diagram will violate a premise – and the either‑or option is a distractor. Another frequent distractor is the sub‑contrary pair “Some A are B” and “Some A are not B”. Both can be true together (when A partially overlaps B and also has elements outside B), so they are not complementary; the correct answer in that situation is “Both I and II follow” if both are individually possible, or “Neither follows” if the premises forbid one of them.

Advanced questions sometimes embed a complementary pair inside a larger set of conclusions, asking “Which of the following conclusions follow?” with multiple statements. The test‑taker must first isolate the pair, confirm they are contradictories, and then check whether any other conclusion is definite. If a third conclusion is definite, the answer may be “Only III follows” or “Only III and either I or II follow”. The presence of a definite conclusion does not invalidate the either‑or nature of the pair; it merely adds another layer to the answer key. Mastery of this mechanic comes from systematic practice: list all conclusions, label each as definite, possible, or impossible, then apply the complementary‑pair rule only to the possible ones.

Worked Example – Complementary Pair Identification
Statements: 1. All engineers are graduates. 2. Some graduates are not managers. Conclusions: I. Some engineers are managers. II. No engineer is a manager. III. Some engineers are not managers. Determine which conclusion(s) follow.
Step 1: Translate premises. “All engineers are graduates” → Engineers ⊆ Graduates. “Some graduates are not managers” → There exists at least one graduate outside the Managers circle. Step 2: Draw minimum diagram – place Engineers entirely inside Graduates, and keep a portion of Graduates outside Managers. In this diagram Engineers may be wholly inside Managers, wholly outside, or partially overlapping; nothing forces a relation. Step 3: Draw maximum diagram – push Engineers as far into Managers as premises allow. Since only “Some graduates are not managers” is given, Engineers could still be completely inside Managers. Step 4: Evaluate conclusions. I (“Some engineers are managers”) is possible but not definite (fails in minimum diagram where Engineers are outside Managers). II (“No engineer is a manager”) is also possible but not definite (fails in maximum diagram where Engineers are inside Managers). I and II are contradictories (I vs E) and together exhaust all possibilities → they form a complementary pair. III (“Some engineers are not managers”) is the O proposition corresponding to the A “All engineers are managers”. Since “All engineers are managers” is not forced, III is merely possible, not definite. Because I and II are a complementary pair, the correct answer is “Either I or II follows”. III does not follow definitely, so it is not selected.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When you spot two conclusions that are direct contradictories (All vs Some‑not, No vs Some), pause and run the two‑diagram test. If both diagrams satisfy every premise, mark the either‑or option immediately; do not waste time checking other conclusions unless the question asks for “all that follow”. This single check often yields two marks in under thirty seconds.

Chain Syllogisms with Three or More Premises

Chain syllogisms extend the classic two‑premise format by linking three, four, or even five categorical statements. The logical engine remains the same: the middle term of each adjacent pair must be distributed at least once for a valid mediate inference. In a three‑premise chain such as “All A are B”, “All B are C”, “Some C are D”, the first two premises yield the definite intermediate “All A are C”. This new universal can then combine with the third premise. Because the third premise is particular (“Some C are D”), the middle term C is not distributed, so no universal conclusion about A and D follows. The only definite conclusion is the particular “Some A are D” obtained by aligning the universal “All A are C” with the particular “Some C are D” (the middle term C is distributed in the universal). If the third premise were “All C are D”, the chain would produce “All A are D” – a universal conclusion. The rule of thumb: count the number of universal premises that distribute the shared term; each such distribution permits one step of universal propagation.

When a chain contains a negative premise (“No B are C” or “Some B are not C”), the polarity of the conclusion flips. A single negative premise in the chain forces the final conclusion to be negative (E or O). Two negatives make the conclusion affirmative again, but the middle term must still be distributed appropriately. For example, “All A are B”, “No B are C”, “All C are D” yields “No A are C” from the first two, and then “No A are D” cannot be derived because the middle term C is not distributed in “All C are D”. The correct definite conclusion stops at “No A are C”. Examiners often add a fourth premise “Some D are not E” to test whether candidates incorrectly extend the chain beyond the last distributed middle term.

To solve chain syllogisms efficiently, write the premises in order, underline the distributed terms (subject of universal affirmatives, both terms of universal negatives, predicate of particular negatives). Then scan left‑to‑right: whenever a term appears as distributed in one premise and undistributed in the next, a mediate inference is possible. Record each intermediate conclusion; they become new premises for the next step. This systematic propagation avoids the common mistake of jumping directly from the first to the last premise, which ignores distribution requirements and produces invalid universals.

Handling Negative Premises and Particular Negatives

Negative premises – universal negative (E: “No A are B”) and particular negative (O: “Some A are not B”) – impose stricter constraints on what can be concluded. An E premise distributes both subject and predicate, allowing it to serve as a strong bridge in a chain. An O premise distributes only the predicate, which means the subject term remains undistributed and cannot act as a middle term for a universal conclusion. Consequently, any syllogism that relies on an O premise as the sole link between two extremes can yield at most a particular negative conclusion (O). For instance, “All A are B” + “Some B are not C” → the middle term B is distributed in the first premise (as predicate of a universal affirmative? Actually predicate of A is undistributed; wait: In “All A are B”, subject A is distributed, predicate B is undistributed. In “Some B are not C”, predicate C is distributed, subject B is undistributed. The middle term B is undistributed in both premises → no valid conclusion. This illustrates the classic “undistributed middle” fallacy that appears frequently in advanced questions.

When a set contains both an E and an O premise, the distribution pattern can rescue a conclusion. Example: “No A are B” (E) + “Some B are not C” (O). Here B is distributed in the E premise (both terms) and undistributed in the O premise (subject). The middle term B is distributed at least once, so a conclusion is possible. The conclusion must be negative (because at least one premise is negative) and particular (because one premise is particular). The valid conclusion is “Some A are not C” (O). Note that “No A are C” (E) would be too strong – the particular premise prevents a universal negative.

Examiners also test the conversion of negative statements. “No A are B” converts to “No B are A” (valid). “Some A are not B” does **not** convert to “Some B are not A” – that would be an illicit conversion. Many candidates mistakenly treat O as convertible, leading to false conclusions. The safe rule: only E and I convert simply; A converts by limitation (to I); O does not convert. Practising conversion drills alongside Venn diagrams eliminates this error.

Syllogism Integration in Analytical Reasoning Sets

Modern reasoning sections often embed syllogistic logic inside larger analytical reasoning puzzles – seating arrangements, grouping games, or conditional sequencing. The syllogism component usually appears as a set of categorical clues that must be combined with positional or relational clues. For example, a puzzle may state: “All doctors are engineers. Some engineers are teachers. No teacher is a lawyer.” Together with “The lawyer sits opposite the doctor” and “The teacher is not at the ends”, the solver must first resolve the categorical network to know which professions can occupy which seats. The categorical network yields definite relations: “All doctors are engineers” and “No teacher is a lawyer” give “No doctor is a lawyer” (since doctors ⊆ engineers, but engineers may include teachers; however “No teacher is a lawyer” does not directly affect doctors unless we know doctors are teachers, which we don’t). The only definite cross‑profession link is “Some engineers are teachers” (particular) and “No teacher is a lawyer” → “Some engineers are not lawyers” (O). This particular negative can be used to eliminate lawyer from the subset of engineers that are teachers, but not from all engineers.

The integration technique: first isolate all categorical statements, draw their Venn diagram, and list every definite and possible conclusion. Then treat each definite conclusion as a hard constraint in the puzzle (e.g., “No doctor is a lawyer” means those two roles cannot be assigned to the same person). Possible conclusions become conditional branches – explore them only if the puzzle permits multiple solutions. This two‑stage approach prevents the common error of mixing categorical reasoning with spatial reasoning prematurely, which leads to contradictory assignments.

Another pattern is the “syllogism‑based condition” where a rule reads: “If all managers are graduates, then the manager sits at position 3.” The test‑taker must verify whether the premise “All managers are graduates” is definitely true given the categorical clues. If the clues only make it possible, the condition is not triggered. Only when the categorical network yields a definite universal does the conditional fire. Recognising the difference between definite and possible categorical conclusions is therefore essential for solving hybrid sets accurately and quickly.

  • Assuming a particular affirmative (“Some A are B”) guarantees the corresponding particular negative (“Some A are not B”) – they are independent possibilities.
  • Treating an O premise as convertible – “Some A are not B” does not imply “Some B are not A”.
  • Ignoring distribution of the middle term in multi‑premise chains, leading to illicit universal conclusions.
  • Selecting “Either I or II follows” for sub‑contrary pairs (I vs O) instead of true contradictories (A vs O, E vs I).
  • Over‑extending a chain beyond the last distributed middle term, producing conclusions not supported by the premises.
  • Mixing definite categorical conclusions with possible ones when applying them as constraints in analytical puzzles.
  • Remember to verify each diagram against all premises before selecting an answer.

Existential Import and the Boolean Interpretation

Classical Aristotelian logic assumes that every universal affirmative (A) and universal negative (E) statement carries existential import – that is, the subject class is non‑empty. Under this reading “All A are B” implies “Some A exist” and “No A are B” also implies “Some A exist”. Modern Boolean logic, formalised by George Boole and later refined by John Venn, rejects existential import for universal statements. In the Boolean reading “All A are B” is true even when the class A is empty; it is equivalent to “There is no A that is not B”. This shift changes the validity of several syllogistic forms. For example, the mood AAI‑1 (All M are P; All S are M; therefore Some S are P) is valid in the Aristotelian system because the first premise guarantees the existence of M, which together with the second premise yields an existing S. In the Boolean system the same mood is invalid because the premises do not assert the existence of any S. Examiners frequently test this distinction by presenting a set of premises that are universally quantified and asking whether a particular conclusion follows. The correct answer hinges on recognising whether the question adopts the Aristotelian or Boolean convention. Most contemporary competitive‑exam syllabi follow the Boolean interpretation unless explicitly stated otherwise. A reliable shortcut: treat every universal premise as a conditional (“If something is A then it is B”) and only draw a particular conclusion when at least one premise is explicitly particular (I or O). When a question mentions “existential import” or “Aristotelian logic”, switch to the traditional reading and allow the universal premises to populate the Venn diagram with at least one element in the subject circle.

The Square of Opposition and Subalternation

The square of opposition is a diagram that captures the logical relations among the four categorical propositions A (All S are P), E (No S are P), I (Some S are P) and O (Some S are not P). The four corners are linked by four relations: contradiction (A ↔ O, E ↔ I), contrariety (A ↔ E), subcontrariety (I ↔ O) and subalternation (A → I, E → O). Contradictories cannot both be true and cannot both be false; one must be true and the other false. Contraries cannot both be true but can both be false. Subcontraries cannot both be false but can both be true. Subalternation means the truth of the universal (superaltern) guarantees the truth of the corresponding particular (subaltern), while the falsity of the particular guarantees the falsity of the universal. In syllogistic reasoning these relations are the backbone of either‑or questions. When a question offers two conclusions that form a contradictory pair (e.g., “All A are B” vs “Some A are not B”), exactly one follows if the premises are consistent. If the pair is contrary (e.g., “All A are B” vs “No A are B”), at most one can follow and it is possible that neither follows. Subcontrary pairs (I vs O) allow the possibility that both follow, so an “Either I or II follows” answer is incorrect for such a pair. Subalternation is useful for deriving a weaker conclusion when a stronger one is established: from a valid A conclusion you may safely infer the corresponding I conclusion, but the reverse inference is not licensed. Mastery of the square lets a candidate instantly classify any either‑or option set and avoid the common error of treating subcontraries as contradictories.

Technique: Square‑Based Either‑Or Classification
Premises: All M are P. Some S are not M. Conclusions: I. Some S are P. II. Some S are not P. Decide which conclusion(s) follow.
Draw the Boolean Venn diagram. “All M are P” shades the part of M outside P. “Some S are not M” places an X in S outside M. The X may fall inside P or outside P; both positions satisfy the premises. Therefore “Some S are P” (I) is possible but not definite. “Some S are not P” (O) is also possible but not definite. The two conclusions form a subcontrary pair (I vs O). Since subcontraries can both be true, the correct response is “Neither I nor II follows” (or “Both I and II are possible” depending on the answer format). The key step is recognising the pair as subcontrary, not contradictory.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When an either‑or set appears, first label each conclusion with its categorical form (A, E, I, O). Then locate the pair on the square of opposition. If the pair are contradictories, exactly one follows; if contraries, at most one follows; if subcontraries, both may follow; if subaltern, the universal implies the particular. This classification takes seconds and eliminates guesswork.

Obversion, Conversion, and Contraposition in Syllogistic Reasoning

Three immediate inference operations – obversion, conversion, and contraposition – allow the transformation of a single categorical proposition into an equivalent or logically related form. Obversion changes the quality (affirmative ↔ negative) and replaces the predicate with its complement; it is valid for all four forms: A → E (All S are P ⇒ No S are non‑P), E → A (No S are P ⇒ All S are non‑P), I → O (Some S are P ⇒ Some S are not non‑P), O → I (Some S are not P ⇒ Some S are non‑P). Conversion swaps subject and predicate; it is valid only for E and I (No S are P ⇔ No P are S; Some S are P ⇔ Some P are S). A converts by limitation to I (All S are P ⇒ Some P are S) but the converse does not hold. O does not convert at all. Contraposition replaces both terms with their complements and swaps them; it is valid for A and O (All S are P ⇒ All non‑P are non‑S; Some S are not P ⇒ Some non‑P are not non‑S). E and I do not contraposit validly. In syllogism questions these operations are often hidden inside premises. A premise may read “No non‑engineers are managers”, which is the obverse of “All managers are engineers”. Recognising the equivalence lets you rewrite the premise in a standard form before drawing Venn diagrams. Similarly, a statement like “Some non‑students are not teachers” is the contrapositive of “Some teachers are not students”. Practising the conversion table until it becomes automatic prevents the illicit conversion of O statements – a frequent trap where candidates infer “Some B are not A” from “Some A are not B”.

Syllogism with Multiple Middle Terms

Standard syllogisms contain exactly one middle term that appears in both premises and disappears in the conclusion. Advanced questions may present three or more premises linked by two or more middle terms, effectively forming a chain of categorical inferences. The validity of the overall chain depends on each link satisfying the distribution rules: the middle term of each individual syllogism must be distributed at least once. Consider the premises: (1) All A are B. (2) Some B are C. (3) No C are D. Here B is the middle term between (1) and (2); it is distributed in (1) (subject of A) but undistributed in (2) (subject of I). The first link therefore yields no definite conclusion. However, if we reorder to (1) All A are B. (2) All B are C. (3) No C are D, then B is distributed in (2) (subject of A) and the chain produces “All A are C” and then “No A are D”. When multiple middle terms appear, treat the set as a series of two‑premise syllogisms. Identify every possible pairing, check distribution for each middle term, and record the intermediate conclusions that are definitely valid. Only those intermediate conclusions that are universal (A or E) can serve as premises for the next link without loss of strength; particular conclusions (I or O) can only feed into a subsequent step if the next premise distributes the shared term. A practical method: list all terms, mark distribution in each premise, then run a forward‑chaining algorithm mentally – start with premises that distribute a term, derive the strongest possible conclusion, and repeat. This systematic approach avoids the common error of jumping from the first premise to the final conclusion while ignoring a weak middle link.

Syllogism in Data Sufficiency and Logical Reasoning Sets

Data sufficiency questions often embed a miniature syllogism: the stem asks whether a certain categorical conclusion can be deduced, and each statement supplies one or two premises. The candidate must decide whether statement 1 alone, statement 2 alone, both together, or neither suffices. The Boolean interpretation is the default; therefore a universal premise alone never yields a particular conclusion. When evaluating a statement, first translate it into standard form, draw the minimal Venn diagram, and check whether the target conclusion appears in every possible diagram consistent with the statement. If the conclusion is definite (appears in all diagrams), the statement is sufficient. If the conclusion is merely possible (appears in some but not all diagrams), the statement is insufficient. When two statements are combined, merge their diagrams by superimposing the shading and X‑marks; a conclusion that becomes definite only after merging indicates that both statements together are sufficient. Logical reasoning sets (e.g., grouping games with categorical clues) use the same principle: extract all categorical clues, build a combined Venn diagram, and treat every definite conclusion as a hard constraint for the puzzle. Possible conclusions become conditional branches – explore them only when the puzzle permits multiple solutions. A frequent mistake is to treat a possible conclusion as a constraint, which over‑restricts the puzzle and leads to contradictions. Always label each derived relation as “definite” or “possible” before feeding it into the larger reasoning structure.

  • Assuming existential import for universal premises under the Boolean interpretation, leading to illicit particular conclusions.
  • Treating subcontrary pairs (I vs O) as contradictory in either‑or questions, causing an incorrect “Either I or II follows” selection.
  • Illicitly converting an O statement (“Some A are not B”) to “Some B are not A”.
  • Ignoring the distribution requirement for each middle term in a multi‑premise chain, producing a conclusion that the premises do not support.
  • Using a merely possible syllogistic conclusion as a hard constraint in a data‑sufficiency or analytical‑reasoning set.
  • Misidentifying the square‑of‑opposition relation between two answer choices, especially confusing contrariety with contradiction.
  • Failing to apply obversion or contraposition to rewrite a premise into standard form before diagramming, which obscures the true logical structure.
