What Current Affairs GK Actually Tests

Current affairs questions sit on top of static GK: they ask about recent events, but the marks come from knowing the institution, scheme, treaty or judgment behind the headline. Examiners prefer questions where a 2024-2026 development connects to a permanent feature — a new scheme tested through its nodal ministry, a judgment tested through the article it interprets, a summit tested through the grouping it belongs to. Prepare the static anchor first; the headline changes every month, the anchor does not.

Reliable Sources, in Order

1. PIB (Press Information Bureau): the government's own record of schemes, appointments, defence updates and ministry achievements. Facts stated here are official; use it to confirm what a headline claims.

2. The Hindu and Indian Express: editorials and explainers for context and analysis; news pages for events. Read editorials on polity, economy and international affairs — they state the issue, the stakeholders and the debate in one place.

3. PRS Legislative Research: plain-language summaries of Bills, ordinances and parliamentary committee reports. The best single source for "what does this law actually do".

4. Ministry and regulator sites: RBI for monetary policy and banking, Finance Ministry for Budget and economic survey, Election Commission for election conduct, Supreme Court for judgments, NITI Aayog and line ministries for schemes.

5. International sources where relevant: UN bodies, WHO, IMF and World Bank reports matter when the question is about India's ranking, treaty obligations or global programmes.

What to Track: Polity and Governance

Supreme Court and High Court judgments (especially ones interpreting Fundamental Rights, federal powers and election law), constitutional amendment bills, ordinances and their validity, Governor–state and Centre–state disputes, Election Commission decisions, and major legislation in Parliament. For every item, note the article, amendment number or case name — that is what the question usually tests, not the headline.

What to Track: Economy

Union Budget and Economic Survey numbers (GDP growth, fiscal deficit, current account, inflation), RBI policy decisions (repo rate, liquidity stance), new schemes and their implementing agencies, disinvestment and public-sector moves, and international economic events that touch India (trade agreements, sanctions, rating actions). Keep one dated one-page note per Budget cycle; the numbers recur in questions for two to three years.

What to Track: International Relations and Defence

India's neighbourhood (Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Myanmar), groupings India belongs to (G20, BRICS, QUAD, SCO, ASEAN dialogue), major bilateral visits and agreements, defence deals and exercises, diaspora and consular issues, and India's positions in multilateral bodies. Map every event to the grouping or treaty it belongs to — "India hosted the G20 summit" is trivia; "G20 is a forum of 19 countries plus the EU" is the testable fact.

What to Track: Science, Technology and Environment

ISRO and Defence Research missions (mission objectives and firsts, not just launch dates), space-policy developments, health and biotechnology news, digital-economy schemes, climate agreements India has signed, renewable-energy targets, pollution and wildlife judgments, and disaster-management measures. Distinguish what a mission did from what it aims to do — questions exploit that gap.

What to Track: Awards, Sports and Appointments

Civilian awards (Bharat Ratna and the Padma series), gallantry awards and their war/peace distinction, national sports awards, Nobel and other international honours, and key appointments (heads of constitutional bodies, service chiefs, governors). For appointments, remember who appoints whom and on whose advice — that is the static GK layer underneath the news.

How to Revise Without Drowning

Make one dated note per week, not per article: headline, the static fact behind it, and one line on why it matters. Revise the last 6–12 months in the final two months before the exam; older material survives only as static GK. Practise writing 150-word answers on the five biggest issues of the month — current-affairs mains answers are judged on structure and balance, not on how many facts you cram.

Worked example: turning a headline into an answer
Headline: "Cabinet approves the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules." Write the points an examiner expects.
Answer: (1) The rules implement the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — name the parent Act. (2) Key features: consent-based processing, duties of data fiduciaries, rights of data principals, penalties for breaches. (3) Context: India's first comprehensive personal-data law; compare briefly with the EU GDPR if asked. (4) Stakeholders: citizens' privacy vs industry compliance costs. (5) Way forward: enforcement capacity of the Data Protection Board. Structure: Act → features → context → stakeholders → assessment.
Exam-day strategy
When a current-affairs question stumps you, translate it into its static anchor: which ministry, article, scheme, grouping or award is being referenced? Eliminate options that name the wrong institution first — most distractors fail at that level before any factual recall is needed.

Common Traps

  • Confusing a scheme's nodal ministry — verify on the ministry's own site, not memory.
  • Treating a draft Bill as law — check whether it received assent.
  • Recalling the year but not the amendment or article number the question actually asks.
  • Assuming India's position in a grouping (e.g., QUAD has four members: India, USA, Japan, Australia).
  • Letting the most recent headline overwrite an older, still-tested fact.

Legislative Pipeline: From Bill Introduction to Presidential Assent and the Committee Scrutiny Layer

A Bill becomes law only after it clears a defined sequence: introduction, first reading, reference to a committee (Standing, Select or Joint), second reading with clause‑by‑clause debate, third reading, passage in the other House, and finally Presidential assent under Article 111. Money Bills follow a truncated route — they originate only in Lok Sabha, cannot be amended by Rajya Sabha, and must be returned within fourteen days. The Constitution also provides for a Joint Sitting (Article 108) to resolve deadlocks on ordinary Bills, but not on Money Bills or Constitution Amendment Bills.

Parliamentary committees are the primary filter for technical scrutiny. Department‑related Standing Committees (DRSCs) examine Bills referred by the Speaker or Chairman, hear experts, and submit reports that are not binding but carry persuasive weight. Select Committees are constituted for a specific Bill; Joint Committees involve members of both Houses. The 2023 Digital Personal Data Protection Bill, for instance, was referred to a Joint Committee which recommended over ninety amendments before the Bill returned for clause‑by‑clause consideration.

Ordinances (Article 123) allow the executive to legislate when Parliament is not in session, but they must be laid before both Houses within six weeks of reassembly. If not approved, they lapse. The 2024 Ordinance on the National Medical Commission (Amendment) illustrates the trap: it was repromulgated twice, prompting Supreme Court scrutiny on the “ordinance‑raj” doctrine established in D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987).

Constitution Amendment Bills require a special majority (two‑thirds of members present and voting in each House) and, for federal provisions, ratification by at least half the State Legislatures (Article 368). The 105th Amendment (2021) restoring State power to identify socially and educationally backward classes followed this route, while the 106th Amendment (2023) providing 33 % reservation for women in Lok Sabha and State Assemblies awaited ratification at the time of writing.

Tracking the legislative pipeline means noting: (1) Bill type and originating House, (2) committee referral and report date, (3) key amendments adopted, (4) voting numbers, and (5) assent or lapse status. This static scaffold turns a fleeting headline into a testable fact pattern.

Budget Architecture: Revenue, Capital, Fiscal Markers, and Off‑Budget Financing

The Union Budget separates receipts and expenditure into Revenue and Capital accounts. Revenue receipts comprise tax revenue (direct and indirect) and non‑tax revenue (dividends, interest). Revenue expenditure covers interest payments, subsidies, salaries, and pensions. Capital receipts include market borrowings, disinvestment proceeds, and loan recoveries; capital expenditure funds asset creation — infrastructure, defence equipment, and loans to States.

Fiscal markers dominate exam questions. The fiscal deficit (total expenditure minus total receipts excluding borrowings) is the headline number; the primary deficit (fiscal deficit minus interest payments) signals the underlying stance. The 2024‑25 Budget projected a fiscal deficit of 5.1 % of GDP and a primary deficit of 0.8 %. The revenue deficit (revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts) was targeted at 2.0 %, reflecting the quality of spending.

Off‑budget financing obscures the true deficit. The National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) loans to the Food Corporation of India, and the issuance of special securities to public‑sector banks for recapitalisation, are counted as capital receipts but do not appear in the headline borrowing calendar. The 2023‑24 Economic Survey flagged that off‑budget liabilities added roughly 0.5 % of GDP to the effective deficit.

Scheme‑level tracking requires linking each announcement to its nodal ministry, funding pattern (central‑share vs state‑share), and the budget head (e.g., “Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana – Urban” under Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs, Demand No. 45). The 2024‑25 Budget introduced the “PM‑Vishwakarma” scheme with a central outlay of ₹13,000 crore over five years, funded through the Capital head.

International rating actions (Moody’s, S&P, Fitch) react to the fiscal deficit trajectory and debt‑to‑GDP ratio. India’s sovereign rating remained at “Baa3” with a stable outlook in 2024, citing the consolidation path but flagging contingent liabilities from State guarantees.

Technique: Convert a Budget Headline into a Structured Answer
Headline: “Finance Minister announces ₹10 lakh crore capital expenditure push for 2024‑25.” Write the points an examiner expects.
Answer: (1) Identify the budget document — Union Budget 2024‑25, presented on 1 February 2024. (2) Locate the figure in the Capital Expenditure section of the Expenditure Budget (Statement 12). (3) Break down the allocation: ₹3.5 lakh crore for roads and highways (Ministry of Road Transport), ₹2.2 lakh crore for railways (Ministry of Railways), ₹1.8 lakh crore for defence capital outlay, remainder for urban infrastructure and water supply. (4) Note the financing mix: ₹6 lakh crore from market borrowings, ₹2 lakh crore from disinvestment proceeds, ₹2 lakh crore from NSSF and other non‑debt capital receipts. (5) State the fiscal impact — capital expenditure rises to 3.3 % of GDP, supporting the 5.1 % fiscal deficit target. (6) Mention the policy rationale — crowding‑in private investment, employment generation, and meeting the National Infrastructure Pipeline milestones. Structure: Source → Allocation breakup → Financing → Fiscal impact → Policy rationale.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question cites a budget number, first verify the statement number (Revenue, Capital, or Expenditure) and the demand number. Eliminate options that attribute the figure to the wrong ministry or head — most distractors fail at this static‑anchor level before any arithmetic is required.

Defence Procurement Framework: DPP Categories, Indigenous Content Norms, and Offset Obligations

The Defence Acquisition Procedure (DAP) 2020, effective from 1 October 2020, classifies procurement into “Buy (Indian‑IDDM)”, “Buy (Indian)”, “Buy and Make (Indian)”, “Make I”, “Make II”, and “Strategic Partnership”. The hierarchy prioritises indigenous design, development, and manufacture (IDDM). Under “Buy (Indian‑IDDM)”, the vendor must be an Indian company with ≥ 50 % indigenous content (IC) on cost basis; “Buy (Indian)” requires ≥ 50 % IC but allows foreign OEMs with a local subsidiary.

“Make I” projects are government‑funded (up to 70 % of development cost) with a minimum 60 % IC at prototype stage; “Make II” are industry‑funded prototypes with a 50 % IC threshold. The Strategic Partnership model, introduced in 2017 and refined in DAP‑2020, selects a private Indian entity through a transparent competition to build platforms like submarines or fighter jets in collaboration with a foreign OEM.

Offset policy mandates that for contracts valued above ₹2,000 crore, the foreign vendor must discharge 30 % of the contract value through eligible Indian enterprises — either direct purchase, technology transfer, or investment in Indian defence R&D. The 2023 Rafale‑M deal for the Navy (₹58,000 crore) includes an offset clause targeting MSMEs in avionics and composites.

Indigenisation is tracked via the “Positive Indigenisation List” (PIL) issued by the Department of Defence Production. The fourth PIL (2023) added 101 items, including light‑weight torpedoes and airborne early‑warning radars, with a deadline of December 2025 for domestic procurement. The “Defence Investor Cell” monitors FDI inflows; the 2024 policy permits up to 74 % FDI under automatic route and 100 % under government route for niche technologies.

Exam questions often test the distinction between “Buy (Indian)” and “Buy (Indian‑IDDM)”, the IC percentage thresholds, and the offset trigger value. Remember that the offset obligation is calculated on the *contract value* excluding taxes and duties, and that offsets can be fulfilled through “Buy (Indian)” purchases, creating a recursive loop.

International Index Methodologies: Weighting Schemes, Data Sources, and India’s Rank Trajectories

Global indices differ in indicator selection, weighting, and data vintage. The World Bank’s “Ease of Doing Business” (discontinued 2021) used ten topics each weighted equally; the successor “Business Enabling Environment” (BEE) adopts a broader governance lens. The World Economic Forum’s Global Competitiveness Index (GCI) 4.0 (2023) aggregates twelve pillars — institutions, infrastructure, ICT adoption, macro‑stability, health, skills, product market, labour market, financial system, market size, business dynamism, innovation — with equal pillar weights but variable sub‑indicator weights.

Transparency International’s Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) aggregates thirteen expert surveys, each normalised to a 0‑100 scale, then averaged. India’s CPI score moved from 40 (2020) to 39 (2023), reflecting a marginal decline; the rank shifted from 86 to 93 among 180 countries because other nations improved faster.

The UNDP Human Development Index (HDI) combines life expectancy, expected years of schooling, mean years of schooling, and GNI per capita (PPP). India’s HDI value rose from 0.645 (2020) to 0.652 (2022), but the rank remained 132 due to population‑size effects. The Gender Inequality Index (GII) adds maternal mortality, adolescent birth rate, parliamentary representation, and labour‑force participation.

When a question asks “Why did India’s rank fall despite a score improvement?”, the answer lies in the *relative* nature of ranking: other countries’ scores changed more. Always cite the index edition year, the number of countries covered, and the specific pillar or sub‑indicator driving the movement.

Award and Appointment Mechanisms: Constitutional Provisions, Selection Committees, and Precedent Cases

Civilian awards — Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, Padma Shri — are conferred by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister. The Padma Awards Committee, chaired by the Cabinet Secretary, includes the Home Secretary, Secretary to the President, and four eminent persons. Recommendations are solicited from State Governments, Central Ministries, and the public; the final list is approved by the Prime Minister and President.

Gallantry awards follow a distinct hierarchy: Param Vir Chakra (wartime), Ashoka Chakra (peacetime), Mahavir Chakra, Kirti Chakra, Vir Chakra, Shaurya Chakra. The Defence Ministry’s Awards Committee, headed by the Defence Secretary, processes citations; the President approves on the advice of the Council of Ministers. The 2024 award of the Kirti Chakra to Major Rohit Shukla (posthumous) illustrates the peacetime‑valour distinction.

Appointments to constitutional bodies are governed by specific articles: CAG (Article 148), UPSC Chairman and Members (Article 316), Election Commissioners (Article 324), Finance Commission Chairman (Article 280). The President appoints on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but the *selection* process varies. The 2023 amendment to the Election Commission (Conditions of Service) Act introduced a three‑member Selection Committee (Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition, and a Union Cabinet Minister) for the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners, replacing the earlier executive‑only appointment.

Supreme Court judgments have shaped appointment norms. In Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) the Court upheld the collegium system for judicial appointments, while the 2023 Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India case directed transparency in the selection of the CBI Director. For statutory bodies like the NHRC, the Selection Committee comprises the Prime Minister, Speaker, Leader of Opposition, Home Minister, and a sitting Supreme Court judge.

Tracking appointments means recording: (1) constitutional or statutory provision, (2) appointing authority, (3) selection committee composition, (4) term and retirement age, (5) any recent amendment or judgment altering the process. This static layer converts a news item about a new CAG into a testable fact set.

  • Confusing the “Buy (Indian)” and “Buy (Indian‑IDDM)” categories — the former allows foreign OEMs with a local subsidiary, the latter mandates an Indian‑owned vendor.
  • Assuming the fiscal deficit target alone reflects fiscal health — off‑budget borrowings and contingent liabilities can add 0.5‑1 % of GDP to the effective deficit.
  • Treating a committee report as binding law — Standing Committee recommendations are advisory; only the enacted Bill carries legal force.
  • Quoting an index rank without the edition year and country count — ranks are relative and not comparable across years with different coverage.
  • Attributing Padma awards to the President’s personal discretion — they are awarded on the advice of the Prime Minister through a formal committee.
  • Overlooking the offset trigger threshold — offsets apply only above ₹2,000 crore contract value, not to all defence deals.

Parliamentary Committee System: Standing, Select, and Joint Committees in Legislative Scrutiny

India’s parliamentary committees operate as the engine room of legislative detail. There are three principal categories: Standing Committees (permanent, reconstituted each Lok Sabha), Select Committees (ad‑hoc for a specific Bill), and Joint Committees (comprising members of both Houses). The Rules of Procedure of Lok Sabha (Rule 331) and Rajya Sabha (Rule 267) govern their constitution, quorum (one‑third of members), and reporting timelines. Standing Committees are further divided into Department‑Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) — 24 in total — each shadowing a ministry, and other standing bodies such as the Committee on Public Accounts (PAC), Committee on Estimates, and Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU).

DRSCs examine demands for grants, Bills referred by the Speaker or Chairman, and long‑term policy issues. Their reports are recommendatory; the government is not bound to accept them, but a formal response must be laid on the Table within six months (Rule 331(4)). In the 17th Lok Sabha (2019‑2024), the DRSC on Home Affairs produced a 2022 report on the Criminal Procedure (Identification) Bill, recommending safeguards against misuse of biometric data; the government incorporated several suggestions before passage. Select Committees are constituted when a Bill is referred by either House; the 2023 Select Committee on the Digital Personal Data Protection Bill held 12 sittings, heard 45 stakeholders, and submitted a report that shaped the final clause on data localisation.

Joint Committees have been pivotal for high‑stakes legislation. The Joint Committee on the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Bill, 2021 reconciled differences between the two Houses on the definition of “financial creditor”. Its report led to the insertion of a clarification that operational creditors include statutory dues. The Committee on the Women’s Reservation Bill (2023) — a Joint Committee — recommended a 15‑year sunset clause, which was not adopted, illustrating the political limits of committee influence.

Committee membership reflects party strength via the proportional representation formula (Rule 331(2)). The Chairperson of a DRSC is usually from the opposition for PAC and COPU, while other DRSC chairs are allocated by the Speaker in consultation with party leaders. This convention ensures oversight credibility. The 2024 reconstitution saw the PAC chair shift to a senior opposition MP after the 2024 general election, a change that examiners often test through “who chairs PAC after a general election?” questions.

Reports are published on the Parliament website and become part of the public record. Aspirants should track: (1) the committee name, (2) the Bill or subject referred, (3) key recommendations, (4) government action taken (accepted, rejected, or pending), and (5) any subsequent legislative amendment. This static‑dynamic linkage converts a news item like “PAC flags irregularities in defence offsets” into a testable fact set.

Supreme Court Collegium Resolutions: Transparency, Memorandum of Procedure, and Recent Appointments

The collegium system — comprising the Chief Justice of India (CJI) and the four senior‑most Supreme Court judges for Supreme Court appointments, and the CJI plus two senior‑most judges for High Court appointments — derives its authority from the Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993) and the Third Judges Case (1998). The Memorandum of Procedure (MoP), first finalised in 1999 and revised in 2017 after the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) was struck down (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 2015), governs the consultation process, eligibility criteria, and the requirement to record reasons for each recommendation.

In 2022 the collegium began publishing its resolutions on the Supreme Court website, a practice mandated by the 2017 MoP revision. Each resolution lists the names recommended, the vacancy source, the seniority principle applied, and a brief note on merit. For example, the 6 January 2023 resolution recommended Justice Pankaj Mithal (Chief Justice of Jammu & Kashmir & Ladakh High Court) for elevation to the Supreme Court, citing his “outstanding judicial record and administrative experience”. The government cleared the appointment within three weeks, reflecting the convention that the executive does not reject a collegium recommendation unless there are “strong and cogent reasons” communicated in writing.

High Court collegiums follow a similar pattern but involve the respective High Court Chief Justice and two senior‑most judges. The 2023 resolution of the Allahabad High Court collegium recommended 12 advocates for elevation; the Supreme Court collegium cleared 9, returning 3 for reconsideration — a rare instance of “re‑consideration” that underscores the hierarchical check. The MoP also requires the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court to consult the state Governor and Chief Minister for High Court appointments, though the final say rests with the Supreme Court collegium.

Transparency debates intensified after the 2023 Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India judgment, where the Court directed the government to place the MoP in the public domain and to establish a “search‑cum‑selection committee” for the CBI Director, drawing a parallel with judicial appointments. The Court reiterated that the collegium’s deliberations are not subject to RTI, but the *outcome* (resolutions) must be accessible. Aspirants should note the distinction: the *process* remains confidential; the *output* is public.

Key data points for revision: (1) composition of each collegium tier, (2) MoP revision years (1999, 2017), (3) landmark judgments (1993, 1998, 2015, 2023), (4) recent resolutions with dates and names, (5) the “strong and cogent reasons” standard for executive rejection. These anchors turn a headline like “CJI recommends three judges” into a structured fact set.

Technique: Mapping a Collegium Resolution to Static Anchors
A news item dated 12 March 2024 states: “The Supreme Court collegium has recommended Justice A. K. Singh, Chief Justice of the Madras High Court, for appointment as a Supreme Court judge.” Identify the static provisions, the collegium composition, and the procedural step that follows the recommendation.
Static provisions: Article 124(2) (appointment of Supreme Court judges), Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993) establishing collegium, MoP 2017 (procedure). Collegium composition: CJI + four senior‑most Supreme Court judges. Next procedural step: The recommendation is sent to the Union Minister of Law & Justice; the President appoints on the advice of the Council of Ministers. The government may return the name only with “strong and cogent reasons” in writing; if the collegium reiterates, the appointment must be made.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question cites a recent judicial appointment, first recall the constitutional article (Art. 124/217), then the collegium tier, then the MoP year. Write the answer in that order — article → judgment → MoP → recent resolution — to show the full static‑dynamic chain. This sequence scores full marks because it demonstrates both legal basis and current awareness.

Finance Commission Devolution Formula: Vertical and Horizontal Sharing, Grants, and State Fiscal Space

The Finance Commission (FC), constituted under Article 280 every five years, determines the distribution of Union tax revenues between the Centre and the States (vertical devolution) and among States (horizontal devolution). The 15th Finance Commission (Chair: N. K. Singh, 2020‑2025) recommended a vertical share of 41 % of the divisible pool for the award period 2021‑26, unchanged from the 14th FC’s 42 % after adjusting for the abolition of the Planning Commission and the introduction of the GST compensation cess. The horizontal formula assigns weights: 45 % to population (2011 Census), 15 % to area, 15 % to forest & ecology, 10 % to income distance, 10 % to demographic performance, and 5 % to tax effort. The 15th FC introduced a “demographic performance” criterion to reward States that have controlled population growth, a shift from the 14th FC’s sole reliance on 1971 Census population.

Grants‑in‑aid under Article 275 complement devolution. The 15th FC earmarked ₹1.18 lakh crore for revenue deficit grants to 17 States, ₹70,000 crore for local bodies (rural and urban), and ₹45,000 crore for disaster risk management. It also recommended a “performance‑based incentive” of ₹8,000 crore for States achieving targets in health, education, and ease of doing business. The Centre accepted the vertical share and most grants but deferred the performance incentive pending a review mechanism.

The GST compensation cess, originally guaranteed for five years (2017‑22), was extended to March 2026 by the 15th FC to bridge revenue shortfalls. The Commission’s report notes that the cess collection shortfall in 2022‑23 was ₹1.1 lakh crore, met partly by market borrowings by the Centre on behalf of States. This off‑budget borrowing does not appear in the fiscal deficit but increases the effective debt burden — a nuance examiners test via “effective fiscal deficit” questions.

State fiscal space is further shaped by the FRBM Act amendments. The 15th FC recommended a fiscal deficit glide path of 4 % of GSDP for 2021‑22, 3.5 % for 2022‑23, and 3 % thereafter, with a 0.5 % additional borrowing space for States undertaking power sector reforms. Several States (e.g., Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra) availed the extra space in 2023‑24, raising their borrowing limits to 3.5 % of GSDP. The Commission also flagged the rising share of committed expenditure (salaries, pensions, interest) exceeding 60 % of revenue receipts in many States, limiting development spending.

For revision, memorise: (1) vertical share percentages for 14th and 15th FC, (2) horizontal weight matrix, (3) major grant categories and amounts, (4) GST compensation timeline, (5) FRBM glide path and reform‑linked borrowing window. These figures convert a budget speech line like “States get 41 % of divisible pool” into a precise, citable fact.

National Sports Governance Architecture: National Sports Development Code, Khelo India, and Recent Leadership Changes

Sports governance in India rests on three pillars: the National Sports Development Code of India (NSDCI), 2011 (revised 2020), the Khelo India programme (launched 2018, expanded 2021), and the Ministry of Youth Affairs & Sports (MYAS) administrative framework. The NSDCI mandates that National Sports Federations (NSFs) adopt democratic constitutions, hold elections every four years, observe age and tenure limits (president ≤ 70 years, max two terms), and comply with the Right to Information Act. Non‑compliance leads to derecognition and loss of government funding. In 2022 the MYAS derecognised the Archery Association of India for violating tenure norms, reinstating it only after a court‑supervised election in 2023.

Khelo India operates through three verticals: (1) Khelo India Youth Games (U‑17, U‑21), (2) Khelo India University Games, and (3) Khelo India Talent Identification & Development (scholarships of ₹5 lakh per annum for 8 years). The 2023‑24 budget allocated ₹3,200 crore to Khelo India, a 12 % increase over the previous year. The programme also funds the creation of 1,000 Khelo India Centres at district level, each receiving ₹25 lakh for infrastructure. The 2024 edition of the Youth Games in Chennai saw 6,500 athletes across 27 disciplines, with Maharashtra topping the medal tally.

Leadership changes at the apex bodies are frequent exam fodder. The Indian Olympic Association (IOA) elections in December 2023 resulted in P. T. Usha becoming the first woman President, with a four‑year term. The Sports Authority of India (SAI) saw the appointment of a new Director General (DG) in March 2024 — a senior IAS officer — after the previous DG completed a three‑year tenure. The National Anti‑Doping Agency (NADA) Director General was replaced in July 2023 following a WADA compliance audit that highlighted testing gaps; the new DG is a former international athlete with a sports‑law background.

The NSDCI also prescribes a Grievance Redressal Mechanism within each NSF, comprising an independent ombudsman. The 2020 revision added a mandatory Athlete Representation clause: at least 20 % of the NSF executive committee must be elected athletes. The Wrestling Federation of India (WFI) faced a probe in 2023 over allegations of sexual harassment; the Ministry appointed an ad‑hoc committee to run the federation pending elections, illustrating the Code’s enforcement teeth.

Key revision points: (1) NSDCI tenure and age caps, (2) Khelo India budget and verticals, (3) IOA/SAI/NADA leadership timeline (names, dates), (4) derecognition cases (Archery 2022, Wrestling 2023), (5) athlete representation quota. These convert a headline like “Usha elected IOA President” into a structured fact set with governance context.

Electoral Reform Mechanisms: VVPAT Verification, Electoral Bonds, NOTA, and Supreme Court Directives

Electoral integrity reforms have generated a dense cluster of statutory and judicial developments. The Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT) was introduced in 2013‑14 and made mandatory for all polling stations from the 2019 general election. The Election Commission (EC) prescribes a random verification of VVPAT slips in 5 % of polling stations per constituency (Rule 49MA of Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961). In the 2024 Lok Sabha elections, the EC increased the verification to 10 % in 20 randomly selected constituencies as a pilot. The Supreme Court, in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Election Commission of India (2023), directed the EC to publish the VVPAT verification results within 48 hours of counting, a timeline the EC complied with for the first time.

The Electoral Bonds Scheme, notified in January 2018 under the Finance Act, 2017, allows anonymous donations to political parties via bearer bonds purchased from authorised SBI branches. The scheme was challenged in the Supreme Court (Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India, 2024). In a landmark judgment (February 2024), a five‑judge Constitution Bench struck down the scheme as violative of Article 19(1)(a) (right to information) and Article 14 (equality), ordering the SBI to disclose donor details to the EC and the EC to publish them. The Court also held that the amendment to the Representation of the People Act, 1951 (Section 29C) exempting parties from disclosing bond donors was unconstitutional.

None of the Above (NOTA) was introduced in 2013 following the Supreme Court’s direction in People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2013). NOTA votes are counted but do not affect the outcome; the candidate with the highest valid votes wins even if NOTA exceeds that count. The 2024 election saw NOTA cross 1 % of total votes in 12 constituencies, a record high. The EC has proposed a rule amendment to trigger a fresh election if NOTA surpasses the winning margin, but the proposal remains pending legislative approval.

The Supreme Court’s 2023 directives on criminalisation of politics (in Public Interest Foundation v. Central Government Schemes: Design, Funding Windows, and Outcome Monitoring

Flagship programmes such as the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY), Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM), and the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes follow a three‑layer architecture: policy announcement, budgetary allocation through the Demand for Grants, and a monitoring framework anchored in the Output‑Outcome Monitoring Framework (OOMF) of the Ministry of Finance. Each scheme is assigned a unique code in the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) which enables real‑time tracking of fund flow from the Consolidated Fund of India to the implementing agency, whether a central ministry, a state government, or a public sector undertaking. The funding window is typically split into a central share (often 60 % for centrally sponsored schemes, 90 % for special category states) and a state share released in tranches linked to physical progress verified by the District Level Monitoring Committee (DLMC). The OOMF requires quarterly physical and financial progress reports, an annual outcome evaluation by the Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office (DMEO), and a mid‑term review that can trigger re‑appropriation under Rule 212 of the General Financial Rules. Recent examples include the 2023‑24 PLI for electronics manufacturing where the first tranche of ₹4,000 crore was released only after the selected firms submitted proof of capital expenditure and domestic value addition, and the 2024 JJM “Har Ghar Jal” certification that tied the final 10 % of central assistance to third‑party water quality testing. Understanding the conditionalities — utilisation certificates, geo‑tagged asset registers, and the role of the National Institute of Public Finance and Policy (NIPFP) in independent audits — converts a headline about “scheme launch” into a structured answer on fiscal federalism, accountability, and performance‑based budgeting.

State‑Level Fiscal Federalism: GST Council Dynamics, Compensation Cess, and Revenue Sharing

The Goods and Services Tax (GST) Council, constituted under Article 279A, operates on a weighted voting formula: the Centre holds one‑third of the total vote weight, while all states together hold two‑thirds, each state’s weight proportional to its share of the total GST revenue. Decisions on rate changes, exemption lists, and the compensation cess require a three‑fourths majority of the weighted votes, giving the Centre an effective veto on any proposal that does not command broad state consensus. The compensation cess, levied on sin goods and luxury items, was designed to guarantee states a 14 % year‑on‑year revenue growth for the first five years (2017‑22). When collections fell short during the pandemic, the Council approved a borrowing window in 2020‑21 and 2021‑22, allowing states to raise ₹1.1 lakh crore and ₹1.59 lakh crore respectively through special market borrowings backed by the Centre’s guarantee. The 2022‑23 fiscal year marked the end of the statutory compensation period; the Council then shifted to a “revenue neutral” approach, adjusting the cess rates on tobacco and aerated drinks to sustain the corpus for pending arrears. Meanwhile, the 15th Finance Commission’s vertical devolution of 41 % of the divisible pool and its horizontal formula — weighting population (15 %), area (15 %), forest cover (10 %), and income distance (45 %) — interacts with GST revenues because the divisible pool now includes the Centre’s share of CGST and IGST. A nuanced answer must distinguish between the Council’s political bargaining (e.g., the 2023 decision to keep the 28 % slab on cement unchanged despite industry pressure) and the Commission’s formulaic transfers, and must cite the relevant constitutional provisions (Art. 279A, Art. 280) and the GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017.

Technique: Layered Citation Mapping
A question asks: “Explain how the GST Council’s voting structure and the 15th Finance Commission’s devolution formula together shape a state’s fiscal space in 2024.”

Cite the GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017 for the cess mechanism and its expiry. Then move to the 15th Finance Commission report (2020‑25) — vertical share 41 %, horizontal weights (population 15 %, area 15 %, forest 10 %, income distance 45 %). Show the interaction: a state’s GST revenue (SGST + share of IGST) feeds the divisible pool, which the Commission redistributes. Illustrate with a hypothetical state: if its GST collection rises, its own SGST grows, but the horizontal formula may reduce its share of the Centre’s tax pool because income distance improves. Conclude that fiscal space is the net of own‑source GST revenue, compensation cess receipts (if any arrears remain), and the Commission’s devolution, all mediated by the Council’s rate decisions. Cite the 2023 Council meeting minutes (rate freeze on cement) and the 15th FC’s Chapter 4 tables.

Judicial Appointments Beyond the Collegium: High Court Transfers, Acting Chief Justices, and the Memorandum of Procedure

While the Supreme Court collegium dominates discourse on appointments, the constitutional scheme under Articles 217 and 222 also governs High Court judge transfers, acting Chief Justice appointments, and the elevation of additional judges to permanent status. The Memorandum of Procedure (MoP), last revised in 2017 after the Supreme Court’s direction in the Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association case, mandates that the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court initiates a transfer proposal, which is then vetted by the Supreme Court collegium (CJI plus two senior‑most judges). The MoP requires the collegium to record reasons for transfer in writing, a safeguard against arbitrary moves; however, the deliberative minutes remain confidential, a point contested in the 2023 petition by the Bar Association of India. Acting Chief Justices are appointed by the President under Article 223 on the recommendation of the CJI, typically when a Chief Justice retires or is elevated. The 2022 appointment of Justice S. K. Kaul as Acting Chief Justice of the Delhi High Court followed this route, and the MoP stipulates that the acting appointment cannot exceed three months unless a regular appointment is made. Additional judges, appointed for a two‑year term under Article 224, are confirmed as permanent judges only after the collegium evaluates their performance, a process that generated the 2021 “judicial vacancy” debate when several High Courts operated with 30‑40 % vacancies. The 2024 Supreme Court resolution publishing collegium recommendations for High Court appointments (including names, dates, and the “seniority‑cum‑merit” criterion) marked the first time the MoP’s transparency clause was operationalised at scale. Aspirants should be able to trace a transfer from the High Court Chief Justice’s note, through the collegium’s resolution, to the Presidential warrant, and cite the relevant MoP paragraphs (e.g., Para 4.2 for transfers, Para 6.1 for acting appointments).

International Climate Negotiations: India’s Position on Loss and Damage, Net‑Zero Targets, and COP Outcomes

India’s negotiating stance in the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) rests on the principles of Common But Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR‑RC), equity, and the right to development. At COP27 (Sharm El‑Sheikh, 2022) India joined the G77 + China bloc in securing the establishment of a Loss and Damage Fund, insisting that the fund’s governance be under the Conference of Parties (COP) rather than the World Bank, and that contributions be voluntary and based on historical emissions. The 2023 COP28 (Dubai) decision operationalised the fund with a transitional committee; India nominated a representative to the committee and pledged an initial contribution of USD 10 million, framed as a “solidarity” gesture rather than a legal obligation. On net‑zero, India announced a 2070 target at COP26 (Glasgow, 2021) and submitted its Long‑Term Low‑Emission Development Strategy (LT‑LEDS) in 2022, which projects a peak in emissions around 2030‑35 driven by renewable capacity additions (450 GW by 2030) and a gradual phase‑down of unabated coal. The strategy explicitly rejects a fixed coal‑exit date, citing energy security and the need for “just transition” financing. At COP28, India resisted language that would mandate a “phase‑out” of all fossil fuels, negotiating instead for a “phase‑down of unabated coal power” and “phase‑out of inefficient fossil fuel subsidies” — language that mirrors the 2021 Glasgow Climate Pact. The 2024 Bonn intersessional saw India push for the inclusion of “adaptation finance” as a separate agenda item, arguing that the $100 billion goal remains unmet. For exam purposes, anchor each position to the specific COP decision text (e.g., Decision 1/CP.27 para 12 on Loss and Damage, Decision 1/CP.28 para 7 on net‑zero), the relevant Indian government press release (Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, 2022 LT‑LEDS), and the CBDR‑RC principle enshrined in Article 3.1 of the UNFCCC.

Emerging Technology Governance: Data Protection Bill, Digital Personal Data Protection Act, and AI Ethics Framework

The legislative journey from the Personal Data Protection Bill, 2019 (withdrawn) to the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) illustrates the shift from a consent‑centric, localisation‑heavy model to a principle‑based, cross‑border flow framework. The DPDP Act defines “personal data” broadly, establishes the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) as an adjudicatory body, and prescribes penalties up to ₹250 crore for significant data fiduciaries. Key departures include: (1) removal of the data localisation mandate for “critical personal data”, replaced by a government‑notified “trusted geography” list; (2) introduction of “legitimate uses” (e.g., employment, public interest) that bypass explicit consent; (3) a graded consent mechanism for children’s data requiring verifiable parental consent. The Act also empowers the Central Government to exempt certain agencies (e.g., intelligence, law enforcement) via notification, a provision that sparked the 2024 Supreme Court challenge by the Internet Freedom Foundation. Parallel to data law, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) released the “National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence” (2023) and the “Responsible AI for Youth” framework (2024), which embed the OECD AI Principles — transparency, accountability, robustness — into procurement guidelines for government AI systems. The 2024 “AI Ethics Committee” under the DPBI is tasked with auditing high‑risk AI deployments (biometric identification, credit scoring) and issuing sectoral codes. Aspirants should be able to compare the DPDP Act’s “data principal rights” (access, correction, erasure, portability) with the GDPR’s Articles 15‑20, note the absence of a “right to explanation” for automated decisions, and cite the 2024 DPBI notification on “significant data fiduciary” thresholds (annual turnover ₹250 crore or processing data of >10 lakh individuals).

Constitutional Amendments Since 2020: Scope, Procedure, and Implementation

Between 2020 and 2024 the Parliament of India passed five constitutional amendment acts, each addressing a distinct policy domain. The Constitution (One Hundred and Fourth Amendment) Act, 2020 extended the reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies by ten years, pushing the expiry from 2020 to 2030. The amendment followed the standard Article 368 procedure: passage by a special majority in both Houses and ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures, which was completed within three months.

The Constitution (One Hundred and Fifth Amendment) Act, 2021 restored the power of State Governments to identify and notify Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBCs) for the purpose of reservation in education and public employment. This amendment overturned the effect of the Supreme Court’s 2021 judgment in Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister, which had held that only the President could notify SEBCs after the 102nd Amendment. The 105th Amendment added a new clause (3) to Article 342A, explicitly preserving State legislative competence.

The Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act, 2022 introduced a 10 % reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) in central government jobs and admissions to central educational institutions, excluding the creamy layer. The amendment inserted Article 15(6) and Article 16(6) and was upheld by a five‑judge Constitution Bench in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022), which ruled that the 50 % ceiling on reservations does not apply to economic criteria.

The Constitution (One Hundred and Seventh Amendment) Act, 2023 provided for the creation of a National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC) with constitutional status, replacing the earlier statutory body. It amended Article 338B and inserted Article 342A(2) to empower the President to specify SEBCs for each State and Union Territory in consultation with the Governor. The Commission’s recommendations are now binding on the Central Government for inclusion in the Central List.

The Constitution (One Hundred and Eighth Amendment) Act, 2024, popularly known as the Women’s Reservation Act, reserves one‑third of all seats in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies, and the Legislative Assembly of the National Capital Territory of Delhi for women. The amendment inserts Article 330A, 332A, and 334A, and provides for rotation of reserved constituencies after each delimitation exercise. It will come into force after the first census conducted after its enactment and the subsequent delimitation, making its operational date contingent on the 2026 census cycle.

All five amendments followed the rigid special‑majority route under Article 368(2) and, where required, State ratification under Article 368(2) proviso. The procedural discipline illustrates that even politically sensitive subjects — reservation, gender quota, federal balance — can be codified without invoking the basic‑structure doctrine, provided the amendment does not alter the Constitution’s core features.

Supreme Court Landmark Judgments 2023‑2024 on Fundamental Rights and Federalism

The Supreme Court delivered several Constitution Bench decisions between January 2023 and December 2024 that reshaped the interpretation of fundamental rights and Centre‑State relations. In K.S. Puttaswamy (II) v. Union of India (2023), a nine‑judge Bench reaffirmed the right to privacy as a facet of Article 21 and laid down a three‑prong test — legality, necessity, proportionality — for any State intrusion. The judgment directly impacted the validity of the Aadhaar‑based authentication framework and the 2023 Digital Personal Data Protection Act’s “legitimate uses” clause.

In State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2023), a five‑judge Bench held that the Governor’s power to reserve Bills for the President’s consideration under Article 200 is not absolute; the Governor must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in rare circumstances where the Bill violates constitutional provisions. The ruling curbed the practice of indefinite withholding of assent, reinforcing the principle of responsible government at the State level.

The 2024 decision in Internet Freedom Foundation v. Union of India struck down the blanket exemption granted to intelligence and law‑enforcement agencies under Section 35 of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. The Court held that such exemption must be narrowly tailored, subject to judicial oversight, and cannot override the data principal’s right to access and correction under Sections 11‑13.

On federalism, Union of India v. State of Tamil Nadu (2024) addressed the Centre’s power to legislate on “public order” under Entry 1 of the State List via the Union Territories Laws (Amendment) Act, 2023. The Court ruled that the Centre may enact laws for Union Territories on State‑list subjects only when the legislation is expressly confined to the Territory and does not encroach on State legislative fields, preserving the federal distribution of powers.

Finally, Maharashtra v. Union of India (2024) examined the constitutional validity of the 2023 amendment to the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act that expanded the definition of “terrorist act” to include “acts threatening the economic security of India”. The Court upheld the amendment but read down the phrase “economic security” to require a direct nexus with violence or threat of violence, preventing arbitrary application against peaceful protests.

Applied Scenario
A State Legislative Assembly passes a Bill providing 15 % reservation in State government jobs for a newly identified backward class. The Governor reserves the Bill for the President’s consideration, citing that the Bill exceeds the 50 % ceiling set by the Supreme Court in Indra Sawhney. The President returns the Bill with a message requesting reconsideration. The State Government re‑passes the Bill unchanged. Under the 2023 State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh judgment, what is the constitutional position of the Governor and the President?
The Governor’s reservation of the Bill under Article 200 is subject to the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers. The 2023 Constitution Bench held that the Governor cannot withhold assent indefinitely or act contrary to ministerial advice unless the Bill is unconstitutional on its face. The 50 % ceiling in Indra Sawhney applies to caste‑based reservations; economic‑criteria reservations (EWS) are exempt per the 106th Amendment. Since the Bill concerns a backward class identified under the restored State power (105th Amendment), the Governor must grant assent on re‑passage. The President’s role under Article 201 is similarly bound by the Council of Ministers’ advice at the Centre; the President cannot refuse assent on policy grounds. Therefore, after the State re‑passes the Bill, the Governor is constitutionally obliged to give assent, and the President’s prior message does not create a veto.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mixes a recent amendment with a landmark judgment, first identify the constitutional article amended, then map the judgment’s ratio to that article. Use the “amendment‑judgment‑article” triangle: (1) amendment number and year, (2) case name and year, (3) article(s) interpreted. This prevents conflating the 105th Amendment’s restoration of State power with the 102nd Amendment’s presidential notification regime.

India’s Participation in Multilateral Summits 2023‑2024: Outcomes and Commitments

India’s diplomatic calendar in 2023‑2024 featured high‑level engagement across the G20, BRICS, SCO, Quad, and the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). At the G20 New Delhi Summit (September 2023), India secured consensus on the “New Delhi Leaders’ Declaration”, which included language on reforming multilateral development banks, a voluntary commitment to triple renewable energy capacity by 2030, and a pledge to phase down unabated coal power in line with national circumstances. The declaration also endorsed the “Digital Public Infrastructure” (DPI) framework, urging members to adopt interoperable identity, payment, and data‑exchange platforms.

During the 15th BRICS Summit in Johannesburg (August 2023), India supported the admission of six new members — Argentina, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates — effective January 2024. India advocated for a BRICS‑wide local‑currency settlement mechanism and contributed to the establishment of the BRICS Contingent Reserve Arrangement’s expanded swap lines, enhancing financial resilience against dollar volatility.

At the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) Summit in New Delhi (July 2023), India chaired the Regional Anti‑Terrorist Structure (RATS) and pushed for a joint counter‑terrorism action plan that explicitly named cross‑border terror financing. The summit adopted the “New Delhi Declaration on Counter‑Terrorism”, committing members to real‑time intelligence sharing and capacity‑building exercises.

The Quad Leaders’ Summit in Tokyo (May 2023) and the subsequent Quad Foreign Ministers’ Meeting in New Delhi (March 2024) advanced the “Quad Vaccine Partnership” into a broader “Quad Health Security Initiative”, covering pandemic preparedness, antimicrobial resistance, and health‑system digitisation. The 2024 meeting also launched the “Quad Infrastructure Coordination Group” to align standards for transparent, high‑quality infrastructure financing in the Indo‑Pacific.

At COP28 in Dubai (December 2023), India announced a voluntary contribution of USD 10 million to the newly operationalised Loss and Damage Fund, emphasising that contributions remain voluntary and based on historical responsibility. India also reiterated its 2070 net‑zero target, submitted its Long‑Term Low‑Emission Development Strategy (LT‑LEDS) in 2022, and resisted language mandating a complete fossil‑fuel phase‑out, securing the “phase‑down of unabated coal” formulation.

In the United Nations General Assembly (September 2024), India co‑sponsored the “Global Digital Compact” resolution, advocating for open‑source digital public goods, data sovereignty, and inclusive AI governance. The resolution calls for a UN‑hosted multi‑stakeholder forum to develop norms for cross‑border data flows, aligning with India’s DPDP Act’s “trusted geography” approach.

Digital Public Infrastructure Expansion: UPI 2.0, DigiLocker Integration, and ONDC Rollout

The evolution of India’s Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) between 2022 and 2024 has moved from standalone platforms to an interoperable ecosystem. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI) 2.0, launched in August 2022, introduced mandate‑based recurring payments, invoice‑in‑the‑box, and signed intent/QR verification, raising the per‑transaction limit for verified merchants to ₹2 lakh. By March 2024, UPI processed over 13 billion transactions in a single month, accounting for approximately 75 % of retail digital payments by volume.

DigiLocker, the cloud‑based document wallet, was integrated with the National Academic Depository (NAD) and the National Health Authority’s Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) in 2023. This integration enables citizens to store and share verified educational certificates, vaccination records, and health‑insurance cards across government and private service providers using a single consent‑driven API. As of December 2024, DigiLocker reported over 150 million registered users and 2.3 billion document issuances.

The Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC) transitioned from pilot (April 2022) to nationwide beta (January 2023) and achieved full‑scale operational status in October 2023. ONDC’s protocol‑based architecture — built on the Beckn protocol — allows any buyer‑app, seller‑app, or logistics‑app to interoperate without a central platform. By June 2024, ONDC onboarded over 200 000 sellers across 85 cities, covering categories from grocery to mobility. The network’s transaction volume grew at a compound monthly rate of 22 % during the first year of full operation.

Interoperability is enforced through the “Unified Logistics Interface Platform” (ULIP) and the “Account Aggregator” framework, both notified under the DPDP Act’s “trusted geography” provisions. The Account Aggregator ecosystem, regulated by the Reserve Bank of India, now includes 19 licensed Account Aggregators and over 1 billion consented data‑fetch requests per quarter, enabling real‑time credit underwriting for MSMEs.

Governance of the DPI stack is coordinated by the “Digital India Corporation” (DIC) under the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, with a multi‑stakeholder steering committee comprising representatives from the RBI, NPCI, NHA, and industry bodies. The committee publishes quarterly “DPI Health Reports” that track uptime, grievance redressal turnaround, and adoption metrics, providing a transparent evidence base for policy calibration.

Space Sector Reforms and Gaganyaan Progress

India’s space sector underwent structural reform with the enactment of the Indian Space Policy 2023 and the establishment of the Indian National Space Promotion and Authorization Centre (IN‑SPACe) as a single‑window regulator for non‑governmental entities. IN‑SPACe, operational since June 2020, issued its first authorisation for a private launch service — Skyroot Aerospace’s Vikram‑S sub‑orbital flight — in November 2022. By March 2024, IN‑SPACe had granted 42 authorisations covering launch vehicles, satellite manufacturing, ground stations, and space‑based applications.

The policy delineates three categories of activity: (1) strategic, reserved for the Department of Space (DoS) and ISRO; (2) commercial, open to private entities under IN‑SPACe authorisation; and (3) collaborative, encouraging joint missions between ISRO and industry. The “Space Activities Bill” draft, circulated for public consultation in 2023, proposes a licensing regime, liability framework, and a Space Tribunal for dispute resolution.

Gaganyaan, India’s human spaceflight programme, achieved critical milestones in 2023‑2024. The first uncrewed test flight (TV‑D1) of the Crew Escape System was successfully conducted in October 2023, validating the low‑altitude escape capability. The second uncrewed orbital test flight (G1), carrying a humanoid payload “Vyommitra”, launched in February 2024 on the Human‑Rated LVM3 (HLVM3) vehicle, demonstrating end‑to‑end mission profile including orbital insertion, life‑support system checkout, and controlled re‑entry. The crewed mission (H1) is targeted for the fourth quarter of 2025, contingent on successful completion of the remaining two uncrewed flights (G2 and G3) and crew‑selection finalisation.

ISRO’s commercial arm, NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), signed 18 launch service contracts in 2023‑2024, including the launch of 36 OneWeb satellites across two LVM3 missions (October 2023 and March 2024). NSIL also entered a strategic partnership with the European launch provider Arianespace for joint marketing of the LVM3 and Ariane 6 vehicles, expanding India’s share of the global commercial launch market to an estimated 6 % by payload mass in 2024.

The “Space Situational Awareness” (SSA) capability was bolstered with the operationalisation of the “Netra” radar and optical telescope network in 2023, enabling indigenous tracking of space debris and collision avoidance for Indian assets. India also joined the “Inter‑Agency Space Debris Coordination Committee” (IADC) as a full member in 2024, committing to the “Zero Debris” charter for future missions.
