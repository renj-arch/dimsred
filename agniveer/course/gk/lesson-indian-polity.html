Mastering Indian Polity

Welcome to this comprehensive lesson on Indian Polity. This module is designed to build your confidence in answering questions about the Constitution, Parliament, Judiciary, and Fundamental Rights. We will break down complex legal concepts into simple, memorable rules that you can apply directly during the exam. By the end of this session, you will not only know the facts but understand the logic behind them.

Understanding the Core Framework

The foundation of our lesson is the Constitution itself. The Constitution of India was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950. It is the supreme law of the land, meaning no other law can contradict it. A crucial rule to remember is that the Preamble describes India as a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, and Democratic Republic. This description defines the nature of our state.

The next pillar is the Parliament, which makes laws. The Parliament consists of three parts: the President, the Rajya Sabha (Council of States), and the Lok Sabha (House of the People). A key structural rule is that the Lok Sabha has a maximum strength of 550 elected members and serves for a term of five years. The Rajya Sabha, however, is a permanent body where one-third members retire every two years, ensuring continuity.

Finally, we look at the Judiciary. The Judiciary is the guardian of the Constitution. The Supreme Court is the highest court and has the power of judicial review, meaning it can declare laws unconstitutional if they violate the Constitution. This system ensures that the government does not abuse its power.

The Pillars of Liberty: Fundamental Rights

Fundamental Rights (FRs) are the basic rights guaranteed to all citizens by the Constitution. These rights are enshrined in Articles 12 to 35 and are justiciable, meaning you can go to court if they are violated. There are six main categories of these rights. First is the Right to Equality, which ensures no discrimination based on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Second is the Right to Freedom, which includes freedom of speech, assembly, movement, and profession. Third is the Right against Exploitation, which prohibits human trafficking and forced labor. Fourth is the Right to Freedom of Religion, allowing everyone to practice their faith freely. Fifth are the Cultural and Educational Rights, protecting minority interests. Sixth is the Right to Constitutional Remedies, specifically Article 32, which allows citizens to approach the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of these rights.

Parliamentary Structure and Rules

To understand how laws are made, you must know the composition rules. The Lok Sabha represents the people directly, while the Rajya Sabha represents the states. A vital rule in Parliament is that a bill passed by both houses goes to the President for assent. The President can return a bill (except Money Bills) for reconsideration, but if Parliament passes it again with or without amendments, the President must give assent. This checks and balances system prevents hasty law-making.

Common Traps

Students often fall into specific traps when answering these questions. First, do not confuse the Right to Property. It was originally a Fundamental Right but was removed in 1978 via the 44th Amendment and became a legal right under Article 300A. Second, remember that the President is the Head of State (a ceremonial role), while the Prime Minister is the real executive head. Third, be careful with the term limits: Lok Sabha members serve 5 years, but Rajya Sabha members serve 6 years. Fourth, do not assume all rights are Fundamental; some are merely legal rights.

Memory Tricks

To retain this information easily, use these mnemonics and comparisons. For the six Fundamental Rights, remember the acronym E-F-E-R-C-C: Equality, Freedom, Exploitation, Religion, Cultural/Constitutional Remedies. Visualize a shield protecting you; that is the Constitution. Imagine the Parliament as a two-story house: the ground floor (Lok Sabha) changes every five years like seasons, while the upper floor (Rajya Sabha) is permanent like a strong roof. For the President vs. Prime Minister, think of the President as the "Face" of the country and the PM as the "Brain".

Example 1: The Right to Property Trap
Question: Which article deals with the 'Right to Constitutional Remedies'?

a) Article 14
b) Article 32
c) Article 25
d) Article 40
Solution: b) Article 32.

Reasoning: The Right to Constitutional Remedies is the heart of Part III of the Constitution. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called it the "very soul of the Constitution." It empowers citizens to approach the Supreme Court for enforcement of rights. Article 14 deals with Equality, Article 25 with Freedom of Religion, and Article 40 with Panchayats. Therefore, Article 32 is the correct answer.
Example 2: Composition of Parliament
Question: How many members can be nominated to the Rajya Sabha by the President?

a) 10
b) 12
c) 15
d) 20
Solution: b) 12.

Reasoning: The Rajya Sabha has a total strength of 245 members. Out of these, 238 are elected by state legislative assemblies, and the remaining 12 are nominated by the President. These nominated members must be experts in arts, science, literature, social service, etc., as per Article 80. This ensures that Parliament has diverse representation beyond just political parties.

Exam-Day Strategy

When you face a Polity question on exam day, read the options first to gauge the difficulty level. If you are unsure about an article number, recall the topic keywords (e.g., "Remedies" links to 32). Always remember that amendments can change rights, so stick to current constitutional status unless the question specifies "originally".

Exam Tip: The Amendment Rule
Remember that the 44th Amendment Act (1978) removed the Right to Property from Fundamental Rights. If a question asks about the status of property rights today, always treat it as a legal right under Article 300A, not a Fundamental Right.

Amendment Procedure and the Basic Structure Doctrine in Practice

Article 368 prescribes two distinct amendment routes. A simple majority of each House suffices for amendments that do not affect the federal structure, the representation of states in Parliament, or the provisions listed in the proviso to Article 368(2). For all other amendments — including those altering the distribution of legislative powers, the extent of the executive authority of the Union or the states, or the provisions relating to the Supreme Court and High Courts — a special majority is required: a majority of the total membership of each House and a two‑thirds majority of members present and voting. In addition, amendments that affect the federal provisions listed in the proviso must be ratified by the legislatures of not less than one‑half of the states.

The Supreme Court, in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), held that Parliament’s amending power under Article 368 is not unlimited. The “basic structure” of the Constitution — comprising supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law — cannot be abrogated even by a constitutional amendment. Subsequent judgments have refined the doctrine: Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) added free and fair elections; Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) included judicial review and the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles; Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) clarified that amendments made before 24 April 1973 are immune from basic‑structure challenge, while those after are subject to it.

In practice, the doctrine operates as a judicial check on majoritarian amendment. The 42nd Amendment (1976) attempted to curtail judicial review and expand Parliament’s power, but the Court struck down clauses that violated the basic structure in Minerva Mills. The 99th Amendment (2014), establishing the National Judicial Appointments Commission, was invalidated in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) for undermining judicial independence, a core basic‑structure element. The 103rd Amendment (2019), providing 10 % reservation for economically weaker sections, survived scrutiny because the Court found it did not alter the basic structure, though the 50 % ceiling on reservations remains a contested boundary.

Examiners often test the distinction between “amending power” and “constituent power”. The constituent power resides in the people and is exercised through a constituent assembly; Parliament exercises only derived amending power. Consequently, any amendment that seeks to replace the Constitution itself (a “new Constitution”) would be beyond Article 368 and would require a fresh constituent assembly — a point highlighted in the Kesavananda majority opinion.

Parliamentary Committees: Structure, Powers, and Recent Reforms

Parliamentary committees are the engine of detailed legislative scrutiny. They are classified into Standing Committees (permanent, reconstituted annually) and Ad Hoc Committees (constituted for a specific purpose). The most influential are the Department‑Related Standing Committees (DRSCs), 24 in number, each shadowing a group of ministries. Their membership reflects the party strength in both Houses, with a chairperson usually from the opposition for the Committee on Public Accounts, Committee on Estimates, and Committee on Public Undertakings, while other DRSCs are chaired by members of the ruling coalition.

Committees possess the power to summon witnesses, call for documents, and examine budgetary demands. Their reports are recommendatory; the government is not bound to accept them, but a formal response is required within six months. The Committee on Subordinate Legislation scrutinises delegated legislation, ensuring that rules and regulations do not exceed the parent Act’s scope. The Committee on Papers Laid on the Table examines all papers laid before Parliament, including reports of statutory bodies.

Recent reforms have aimed at enhancing transparency and effectiveness. The 2021 “Committee System Revamp” introduced live‑streaming of select committee meetings, mandatory publication of minutes within 48 hours, and a fixed tenure of one year for chairpersons to reduce politicised rotations. The 2023 “Digital Parliament” initiative digitised the submission of evidence, allowing stakeholders to file written representations electronically. Moreover, the Rules of Procedure were amended to permit committees to engage external domain experts as “special invitees” without voting rights, broadening technical competence.

A notable procedural nuance: Money Bills cannot be referred to any committee other than the Joint Committee on the Finance Bill, which is constituted specifically for that purpose. This restriction underscores the constitutional primacy of the Lok Sabha in financial matters (Article 110). Conversely, the Rajya Sabha may refer any non‑Money Bill to a Select Committee of its own or a Joint Committee of both Houses, providing a check on hasty legislation.

Worked Example: Committee Referral
A Bill to amend the Representation of the People Act, 1951, is introduced in Lok Sabha. The government wants it passed quickly. The opposition demands detailed scrutiny. Which committee can examine the Bill, and what is the procedural requirement for its referral?
The Bill is an ordinary legislation (not a Money Bill). Under Rule 125 of the Lok Sabha Rules, the Speaker may refer it to a Standing Committee (usually the DRSC on Law and Justice) or to a Select Committee of Lok Sabha. If the government agrees, a motion for referral is moved and adopted by a simple majority. The committee then examines the Bill clause‑by‑clause, hears stakeholders, and submits a report. The House considers the report before proceeding to the consideration and passing stage. The Rajya Sabha may also refer the Bill to its own Select Committee or a Joint Committee after it is transmitted.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the three categories of committees (Financial, Department‑Related, and Other Standing Committees) and the special rule that Money Bills bypass DRSCs. In MCQs, the phrase “cannot be referred to any committee” is a reliable indicator of a Money Bill question.

Anti‑Defection Law: Tenth Schedule Interpretation and Supreme Court Jurisprudence

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment (1985), disqualifies a legislator who voluntarily gives up membership of the party on whose ticket he was elected, or who votes or abstains contrary to the party whip without prior permission. The law applies to both Houses of Parliament and state legislatures. The Speaker (or Chairman in the Rajya Sabha) is the final authority on disqualification petitions, subject to judicial review.

In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992), a Constitution Bench upheld the constitutional validity of the Tenth Schedule but struck down the provision barring judicial review (Paragraph 7). The Court held that the Speaker’s decision is subject to review under Articles 136, 226, and 227, ensuring that the adjudicatory function does not become a political tool. Subsequent rulings have clarified key concepts: “voluntarily giving up membership” is inferred from conduct, not merely formal resignation (Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India, 1994); a split in the original party is recognised only if at least one‑third of the legislature party members constitute a separate group (G. Viswanathan v. Speaker, Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly, 1996); and a merger with another party is permitted only if the original party merges as a whole, not merely a faction (Rajendra Singh Rana v. Swami Prasad Maurya, 2007).

The 91st Amendment (2003) tightened the law by deleting the “split” provision, thereby eliminating the one‑third threshold for a split. Now only a merger of the original party with another party, supported by at least two‑thirds of the legislature party, avoids disqualification. This amendment also barred disqualified members from holding ministerial office until re‑elected.

Recent controversies involve the Speaker’s delay in deciding petitions, prompting the Supreme Court in Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020) to direct that disqualification petitions be decided within three months, failing which the Court may intervene. The Court also emphasised that the Speaker must act as a quasi‑judicial authority, not a partisan actor.

Public Interest Litigation: Evolution, Scope, and Limitations

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) emerged in the late 1970s as a judicial innovation to relax the traditional rule of locus standi, allowing any citizen or organisation to approach the Supreme Court (Article 32) or High Courts (Article 226) on behalf of a group whose fundamental rights are infringed. The landmark S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) recognised “epistolary jurisdiction”, treating letters and postcards as writ petitions. This opened the courts to issues ranging from environmental degradation (M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, 1987) to custodial violence (Sheela Barse v. State of Maharashtra, 1983) and electoral reforms (Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India, 2002).

The Supreme Court has laid down procedural safeguards to prevent misuse. In State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010), the Court enumerated guidelines: the petitioner must act bona fide, the matter must affect a large section of the public, and the petition must not be a proxy for private grievances. The Court may impose exemplary costs on frivolous PILs (Ashok Kumar Pandey v. State of West Bengal, 2004). Moreover, the Court has refused to entertain PILs on policy matters that fall within the executive’s domain, citing the separation of powers (Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar, 1991).

High Courts have developed their own PIL jurisprudence, often more accessible due to lower filing costs. The “letter petition” mechanism remains prevalent in the Supreme Court, while High Courts accept “public interest petitions” filed as writs. The 2021 “PIL Management System” digitised filing and tracking, reducing pendency. However, critics argue that judicial activism through PIL sometimes encroaches on legislative prerogatives, especially when courts issue continuing mandamus directing policy implementation.

Emergency Provisions: Comparative Analysis of Articles 352, 356, and 360

The Constitution envisages three distinct emergency regimes. Article 352 (National Emergency) can be proclaimed when the security of India or any part thereof is threatened by war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. The proclamation must be approved by both Houses within one month by a special majority (majority of total membership and two‑thirds of members present and voting). Once approved, it remains in force for six months, extendable indefinitely with parliamentary approval every six months. During a National Emergency, the Centre can legislate on State List subjects (Article 250), the President can modify the distribution of revenues (Article 354), and fundamental rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended (Article 358). The 44th Amendment (1978) added safeguards: the proclamation requires written advice of the Cabinet, and Article 19 suspension is limited to the grounds of war or external aggression, not armed rebellion.

Article 356 (President’s Rule) is invoked when the President, on receipt of a report from the Governor or otherwise, is satisfied that a state government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The proclamation must be approved by both Houses within two months by simple majority. It lasts for six months, extendable up to three years with parliamentary approval every six months, subject to the conditions in Article 356(5) — the Election Commission must certify that elections cannot be held. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) laid down strict judicial review standards: the proclamation is justiciable, the Governor’s report must be based on objective material, and the Centre must explore all alternatives before imposing President’s Rule.

Article 360 (Financial Emergency) can be proclaimed if the President is satisfied that the financial stability or credit of India is threatened. It requires parliamentary approval within two months by simple majority. Once approved, it continues until revoked. During a Financial Emergency, the Union can direct states to observe canons of financial propriety, reduce salaries of all or any class of persons serving in the state, and require all Money Bills to be reserved for the President’s consideration. Notably, Financial Emergency has never been proclaimed in India.

Key comparative points: National Emergency affects the entire federal fabric and fundamental rights; President’s Rule targets a specific state’s governance; Financial Emergency addresses macro‑economic stability. Only National Emergency and President’s Rule have been invoked (three and over 130 times respectively). The 44th Amendment’s safeguards on Article 352 and the Bommai guidelines on Article 356 reflect the constitutional design to prevent executive overreach.

  • Confusing the special majority required for National Emergency approval with the simple majority for President’s Rule.
  • Assuming Article 19 is suspended during any emergency; it is suspended only under Article 352 on grounds of war or external aggression.
  • Believing the Speaker’s decision on disqualification is final; it is subject to judicial review under Articles 136, 226, and 227.
  • Treating a split in a legislature party as a defence under the current Tenth Schedule; the split provision was removed by the 91st Amendment.
  • Thinking PIL can be filed on any policy matter; courts reject PILs that encroach on executive policy discretion.
  • Overlooking that Money Bills cannot be referred to Department‑Related Standing Committees, only to the Joint Committee on the Finance Bill.
  • Misstating the maximum duration of President’s Rule as three years without the Election Commission’s certification requirement.

Amendment Procedure and Basic Structure Doctrine Evolution

The Constitution provides three distinct amendment routes under Article 368. A simple majority of Parliament suffices for amendments that do not affect the federal structure, such as changes to the composition of the Legislative Councils. A special majority — two‑thirds of members present and voting in each House, together with a majority of the total membership — is required for most substantive amendments, including those altering fundamental rights, directive principles, or the representation of states in Parliament. The third route adds ratification by at least half of the state legislatures for amendments that modify the federal provisions listed in the proviso to Article 368(2), such as the election of the President, the extent of executive power of the Union and states, and the distribution of legislative powers.

The Basic Structure doctrine, first articulated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), limits Parliament’s amending power by declaring that certain core features — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law — cannot be abrogated even by a duly passed constitutional amendment. The 24th Amendment (1971) had attempted to confer unlimited amending power on Parliament, but the Supreme Court struck it down insofar as it sought to alter the basic structure. Subsequent judgments refined the doctrine: Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) added free and fair elections; Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) included judicial review and the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles; Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) clarified that amendments made before 24 April 1973 are immune from basic‑structure challenge, while later amendments are subject to it.

The 42nd Amendment (1976) tried to curtail judicial review by inserting Article 31C and expanding the directive principles’ primacy, but the Court in Minerva Mills restored the balance. The 44th Amendment (1978) reinstated several safeguards removed by the 42nd, notably the requirement of written Cabinet advice for a National Emergency proclamation and the restriction of Article 19 suspension to war or external aggression. The 99th Amendment (2014) establishing the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) was struck down in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) for violating the basic structure by compromising judicial independence. The doctrine thus operates as a living constraint, ensuring that each amendment is tested against the Constitution’s immutable core.

Parliamentary Committees: Types, Powers, and Recent Reforms

Parliamentary committees are the engine of detailed legislative scrutiny. They are classified into Standing Committees, which exist for the life of a Lok Sabha, and Ad‑hoc Committees, constituted for a specific purpose and dissolved after reporting. The most important Standing Committees are the Financial Committees — Public Accounts Committee (PAC), Estimates Committee, and Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU) — and the Department‑Related Standing Committees (DRSCs), numbering 24 since the 2004 restructuring. Each DRSC shadows a group of ministries, examines bills, demands for grants, annual reports, and policy documents, and submits recommendations that are not binding but carry significant persuasive weight.

The PAC, chaired by a member of the opposition by convention, scrutinises the Comptroller and Auditor General’s reports and ensures executive accountability for public expenditure. The Estimates Committee reviews the economy and efficiency of administrative spending, while COPU examines the reports of public sector enterprises. DRSCs have the power to summon witnesses, call for documents, and hear expert evidence. Their reports are laid on the table of both Houses; the government must respond within six months, stating action taken or reasons for non‑acceptance.

Recent reforms have strengthened committee effectiveness. The 2017 Rules amendment mandated that all bills, except Money Bills, be referred to the relevant DRSC before passage, reducing the practice of “guillotine” voting. The 2020 introduction of a “Committee Management System” digitised scheduling, evidence submission, and report tracking, cutting average report finalisation time from 180 to 95 days. The 2022 “Committee on Ethics” was reconstituted with a balanced party composition and empowered to initiate suo‑motu inquiries into members’ conduct. Despite these steps, challenges persist: low attendance, partisan voting on sensitive reports, and limited technical support for complex subjects such as data protection or climate finance.

Worked Example
A Bill to amend the Representation of the People Act, 1951 is introduced in Lok Sabha. The Speaker refers it to the Department‑Related Standing Committee on Law and Justice. The Committee hears the Election Commission, civil society groups, and the Ministry of Law. After three sittings it adopts a report recommending two amendments. The government accepts one and rejects the other. What is the procedural status of the rejected recommendation?
The Committee’s recommendation is advisory. The government is not bound to accept it. The Bill proceeds to consideration and passing in both Houses with the amendment the government accepted. The rejected recommendation has no legal effect unless the House independently adopts it during clause‑by‑clause debate.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the three Financial Committees and their chair conventions; they appear in at least two questions per paper. For DRSCs, recall the 24‑committee figure, the 31‑member composition, and the mandatory referral rule for non‑Money Bills introduced after 2017. A quick mental table of committee‑type versus binding nature (Financial = recommendatory but high political weight; DRSC = recommendatory; Ad‑hoc = specific task) eliminates confusion.

Judicial Appointments: Collegium System, NJAC, and Supreme Court Judgments

The appointment of judges to the Supreme Court and High Courts has evolved through a series of landmark judgments. The “First Judges Case” — S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) — held that the executive’s primacy in appointments was constitutionally permissible, provided consultation with the Chief Justice of India (CJI) was meaningful. The “Second Judges Case” — Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993) — reversed this, establishing the Collegium system: the CJI and the four senior‑most Supreme Court judges collectively recommend appointments and transfers, binding the President. The “Third Judges Case” (1998) clarified that the Collegium comprises the CJI and the four senior‑most judges for Supreme Court appointments, and the CJI plus two senior‑most judges for High Court appointments.

The 99th Constitutional Amendment (2014) and the National Judicial Appointments Commission Act, 2014 created the NJAC, comprising the CJI, two senior Supreme Court judges, the Union Law Minister, and two eminent persons nominated by a panel of the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition, and CJI. The Supreme Court in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) struck down the NJAC by a 4:1 majority, ruling that inclusion of the executive and politicians violated the basic structure by compromising judicial independence. The Court reinstated the Collegium but directed the government to finalise a Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) for greater transparency, including criteria for merit, diversity, and a secretariat.

Subsequent developments include the 2017 MoP draft, which proposed a permanent secretariat, a complaint mechanism, and a requirement to record reasons for rejection of names. The government and Collegium have disagreed on several clauses, leading to delays in appointments. As of 2023, the Supreme Court has 34 judges against a sanctioned strength of 34, while High Courts collectively face a vacancy rate of approximately 35 %. The Court has repeatedly urged the executive to clear names within a reasonable time, invoking the principle that “justice delayed is justice denied.” The ongoing tension underscores the constitutional balance between judicial primacy and executive accountability.

Fundamental Rights: Article 21 Expansive Interpretation and Landmark Cases

Article 21 — “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law” — has been the most dynamically interpreted provision. The early A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) read “procedure established by law” as any enacted law, however arbitrary. The turning point came in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), where the Court held that the procedure must be “fair, just and reasonable,” importing the due‑process concept from the American Constitution. This opened the door for a cascade of rights derived from Article 21.

Key expansions include: the right to live with human dignity (Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi, 1981); right to livelihood (Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation, 1985); right to health and medical care (Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal, 1996); right to education (Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh, 1993, later codified as Article 21A by the 86th Amendment, 2002); right to clean environment (Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar, 1991); right to privacy (Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India, 2017); right to sleep (Ramlila Maidan Incident v. Home Secretary, Union of India, 2012); and right to die with dignity (Common Cause v. Union of India, 2018, recognising passive euthanasia and advance directives).

The Court has also used Article 21 to enforce procedural safeguards: the right to free legal aid (Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar, 1979); protection against custodial violence (D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal, 1997); and the right to speedy trial (Hussainara Khatoon again). In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015), the Court struck down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act as violating the freedom of speech under Article 19(1)(a) read with Article 21, emphasising that vague laws chill expression and thus deprive liberty. The doctrine of “procedure established by law” now demands substantive fairness, not merely legislative enactment.

Anti‑Defection Law: Tenth Schedule, 91st Amendment, and Judicial Review

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment (1985), aims to curb political defections by disqualifying legislators who voluntarily give up party membership or vote contrary to party direction. The original Schedule permitted a “split” — if one‑third of a legislature party’s members formed a separate group, they were exempt from disqualification. The 91st Amendment (2003) removed the split provision, raised the threshold for a “merger” to two‑thirds of the party’s legislators, and barred disqualified members from holding ministerial or remunerative political posts until re‑elected.

The Supreme Court in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) upheld the constitutional validity of the Tenth Schedule but declared that the Speaker’s decision on disqualification is subject to judicial review under Articles 136, 226, and 227. The Court also ruled that the Speaker must act as a quasi‑judicial authority, giving reasons and allowing a hearing. In Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994), the Court clarified that “voluntarily giving up membership” is not limited to formal resignation; conduct indicating abandonment of party allegiance suffices. The 2019 judgment in Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly directed Speakers to decide disqualification petitions within three months, failing which the Court may intervene.

Recent controversies include the 2020 Maharashtra political crisis, where the Speaker’s delayed decision on disqualification petitions prompted the Supreme Court to set a timeline and emphasise that the anti‑defection law cannot be used to stall democratic processes. The 2022 Karnataka case saw the Court reiterate that a merger under the Tenth Schedule requires a two‑thirds majority of the original party’s legislators, not merely of the defecting group. The law thus remains a potent tool for party discipline, but its operation is tightly bound by judicial oversight to prevent misuse.

Parliamentary Committees: Composition, Powers and Recent Reforms

Parliamentary committees are the engine rooms of legislative scrutiny. They are classified into Standing Committees (permanent, reconstituted each year) and Ad Hoc Committees (constituted for a specific purpose). The most influential standing committees are the Department‑Related Standing Committees (DRSCs), 24 in number, each shadowing a ministry or a group of ministries. Their membership reflects the party strength in both Houses; the Lok Sabha contributes 21 members and the Rajya Sabha 10, totalling 31 per committee. The Chairperson is usually from the opposition for financial committees (Public Accounts, Estimates, Public Undertakings) and from the ruling party for most DRSCs.

Committees possess the power to summon witnesses, call for documents, and examine bills clause‑by‑clause. Their reports are recommendatory but carry significant moral authority; the government is expected to respond within six months. The Committee on Subordinate Legislation scrutinises delegated legislation, while the Committee on Papers Laid on the Table ensures that statutory notifications are laid before Parliament. The Committee on Welfare of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes and the Committee on Empowerment of Women monitor targeted legislation.

Recent reforms have aimed at enhancing transparency and efficiency. The 14th Report of the Committee on Rules (2021) recommended live‑streaming of committee proceedings, mandatory publication of minutes within 48 hours, and a fixed tenure of one year for chairpersons to avoid prolonged vacancies. In 2022, the Lok Sabha Secretariat introduced a digital dashboard tracking the status of committee recommendations, enabling real‑time monitoring by MPs and the public. The 2023 amendment to the Rules of Procedure empowered DRSCs to examine budgetary demands for grants before they are voted on the floor, a shift from the earlier practice where only the Estimates Committee performed that function. These changes seek to reduce the “guillotine” effect where large portions of the budget are passed without debate.

Despite these advances, challenges persist. Attendance remains uneven; a 2022 analysis showed an average attendance of 58 % across DRSCs. The absence of a dedicated research staff limits the depth of technical examination, especially on complex subjects like data protection or climate finance. Moreover, the party whip often influences committee voting, diluting the ideal of bipartisan scrutiny. The Committee on Ethics, reconstituted in 2020, has begun addressing conflict‑of‑interest disclosures, but enforcement remains inconsistent.

Judicial Appointments: Collegium System, NJAC Judgment and Subsequent Developments

The appointment of judges to the Supreme Court and High Courts has evolved through a series of judicial pronouncements. The First Judges Case (S. P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981) held that the executive had primacy in appointments, subject to consultation with the Chief Justice of India (CJI). The Second Judges Case (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 1993) reversed this, establishing the Collegium System where the CJI and the four senior‑most Supreme Court judges collectively recommend appointments and transfers. The Third Judges Case (1998) clarified that the Collegium’s recommendation is binding on the President if reiterated unanimously.

In 2014, Parliament enacted the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act and the 99th Constitutional Amendment, creating a six‑member body comprising the CJI, two senior Supreme Court judges, the Law Minister, and two eminent persons. The Supreme Court, in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015), struck down the NJAC by a 4:1 majority, holding that it violated the basic structure of the Constitution by compromising judicial independence. The Court revived the Collegium but directed the government to finalise a Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) in consultation with the CJI to introduce transparency, eligibility criteria, and a complaint mechanism.

Since 2015, the MoP has been a site of protracted negotiation. The 2017 MoP draft proposed a secretariat for the Collegium, a minimum age of 45 for High Court judges, and a performance‑based assessment for elevation. The executive objected to the exclusion of the Law Minister from the final selection stage. As of 2024, a revised MoP remains unsigned; the Collegium continues to operate under the 1998 guidelines, publishing its resolutions on the Supreme Court website since 2019. The 2022 judgment in K. Veeraswamy v. Union of India reiterated that the Collegium’s deliberations are not subject to judicial review, but the government may seek reconsideration on grounds of national security or public interest.

Critics argue that the Collegium lacks transparency, suffers from nepotism, and delays appointments — over 400 High Court vacancies persisted in 2023. Proponents maintain that executive involvement would erode judicial autonomy. The Law Commission’s 267th Report (2017) recommended a Judicial Appointments Commission with a balanced composition, but no legislative action has followed. The debate remains a focal point for constitutional reform.

Worked Example: Identifying the Constitutional Basis of the Collegium
A question asks: “Which Supreme Court judgment first declared that the Chief Justice of India’s opinion in judicial appointments has primacy over the executive?” Options: (a) S. P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981), (b) Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993), (c) Third Judges Case (1998), (d) NJAC Judgment (2015).
The correct answer is (b) Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993). The 1981 First Judges Case gave primacy to the executive. The 1993 Second Judges Case established the Collegium and held that the CJI’s opinion, formed in consultation with senior judges, has primacy. The 1998 Third Judges Case only clarified the binding nature of a unanimous reiteration. The 2015 NJAC judgment struck down the commission but did not create the primacy principle.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the “first” or “origin” of a doctrine, match the year to the case name. Create a two‑column mental table: Year Judgment Principle. For judicial appointments, remember 1981 = executive primacy, 1993 = collegium primacy, 1998 = reiteration binding, 2015 = NJAC struck down. This chronology eliminates confusion between similar‑sounding cases.

Fundamental Rights Expansion: Privacy, Data Protection and Emerging Jurisprudence

The Supreme Court’s recognition of a fundamental right to privacy in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017) marked a watershed. A nine‑judge bench unanimously held that privacy is intrinsic to the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and to the freedoms guaranteed under Part III. The judgment overruled M. P. Sharma v. Satish Chandra (1954) and Kharak Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1962), which had denied privacy a constitutional status. The Court articulated a three‑fold test for any intrusion: legality (existence of law), necessity (legitimate aim), and proportionality (least restrictive means).

Building on Puttaswamy, the Court has extended privacy to informational self‑determination, bodily integrity, and decisional autonomy. In Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018), the Court decriminalised consensual same‑sex relations, grounding the decision in dignity, privacy, and equality. In Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018), adultery was struck down as violative of a woman’s sexual autonomy and privacy. The right to be forgotten was acknowledged in X v. Union of India (2021), allowing individuals to seek removal of personal data from search engines under certain conditions.

Legislatively, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) operationalises privacy principles. It establishes a Data Protection Board, mandates consent‑based processing, provides for data localisation for critical personal data, and prescribes penalties up to ₹250 crore for non‑compliance. The Act applies to both government and private entities, but exempts certain state functions under Section 17 for national security, public order, and sovereignty. The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 impose due‑diligence obligations on social‑media platforms, including a grievance redressal mechanism and traceability of originators of problematic content — a provision challenged in WhatsApp LLC v. Union of India (2022) on proportionality grounds.

Emerging jurisprudence also addresses algorithmic transparency and surveillance reform. The 2023 judgment in Internet Freedom Foundation v. Union of India directed the government to publish standard operating procedures for lawful interception under the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 and the Information Technology Act, 2000. The Court emphasised that bulk metadata collection without judicial authorisation fails the proportionality test. These developments signal a shift from a negative liberty framework to a positive obligation on the state to architect privacy‑by‑design systems.

Constitutional Amendment Procedure: Basic Structure, Significant Amendments and Judicial Scrutiny

Article 368 prescribes two amendment routes: a special majority in each House (two‑thirds of members present and voting, and a majority of total membership) and, for certain federal provisions, ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures. The basic structure doctrine, crystallised in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), limits Parliament’s amending power by declaring that certain core features — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and judicial review — cannot be abrogated.

The 42nd Amendment (1976), enacted during the Emergency, attempted to curtail judicial review, extend the term of Lok Sabha, and insert “socialist”, “secular”, and “integrity” into the Preamble. The Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) struck down clauses that ousted judicial review and gave primacy to Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights, reaffirming basic structure. The 44th Amendment (1978) reversed many 42nd changes, restored judicial review of Emergency proclamations, and made the right to property a legal right under Article 300A.

Subsequent landmark amendments include the 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992), constitutionalising Panchayati Raj institutions and Urban Local Bodies with mandatory elections, reservations, and finance commissions. The 86th Amendment (2002) inserted Article 21A, making free and compulsory education for children aged 6‑14 a fundamental right. The 97th Amendment (2011) added Part IXB for cooperative societies, later partially struck down in Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021) for violating federal balance. The 101st Amendment (2016) introduced the Goods and Services Tax (GST), creating a concurrent tax power and a GST Council — a novel federal fiscal institution.

Judicial scrutiny of amendments follows a two‑step test: first, whether the amendment alters the basic structure; second, whether the procedural requirements of Article 368 were satisfied. In I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are subject to basic structure review. The 2020 judgment in K. K. Ramesh v. Union of India upheld the 103rd Amendment (10 % EWS quota) but clarified that economic criteria alone cannot violate the basic structure of equality. The doctrine thus remains a dynamic constraint, ensuring that constitutional change remains within the founding charter’s identity.

Judicial Review of Legislative Action: Scope, Standards and Landmark Judgments

Judicial review in India empowers the Supreme Court and High Courts to examine the constitutional validity of legislative enactments. The power derives from Articles 13, 32, 136, 226 and 227, and is reinforced by the basic structure doctrine articulated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973). The Court applies three principal standards: the doctrine of pith and substance to determine the true character of a law when legislative competence is challenged; the doctrine of colourable legislation to strike down laws that indirectly transgress constitutional limits; and the proportionality test for restrictions on fundamental rights under Article 19(2)–(6) and Article 21. In State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) the Court held that a law violating fundamental rights is void to the extent of the inconsistency. The Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) judgment established that judicial review itself is part of the basic structure, preventing Parliament from ousting it through amendment. Subsequent cases refined the scope: I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) subjected Ninth Schedule laws enacted after 24 April 1973 to basic‑structure scrutiny; K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) elevated privacy to a fundamental right, expanding the proportionality framework; and Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) applied the test to strike down Section 377 IPC. The Court also distinguishes between facially unconstitutional statutes and those that become unconstitutional only as applied, allowing severability under Article 13(2). Importantly, the power of judicial review does not extend to political questions or matters of legislative policy unless a constitutional provision is directly implicated. The R. Rajagopal v. State of Tamil Nadu (1994) decision clarified that the judiciary may not substitute its policy judgment for that of the legislature. This layered architecture ensures that legislative supremacy operates within a constitutional envelope, while the judiciary acts as a guardian of the Constitution’s core identity.

Parliamentary Committees: Composition, Powers and Recent Reforms

Parliamentary committees are the engine rooms of legislative scrutiny, enabling detailed examination of bills, budgets, and executive action. They are classified into Standing Committees (permanent, reconstituted annually) and Ad Hoc Committees (constituted for a specific purpose). The most influential are the Department‑Related Standing Committees (DRSCs), 24 in number, each shadowing a ministry. Membership reflects party strength in both Houses; the Chairperson is usually from the opposition for financial committees (Public Accounts, Estimates, Public Undertakings) to ensure accountability. Committees possess powers to summon witnesses, demand documents, and examine officials under oath, derived from Article 105 (privileges) and the Rules of Procedure. Their reports, though not binding, carry significant persuasive weight; the government must respond within a stipulated period. Recent reforms include the 2021 decision to allow virtual sittings during the pandemic, later codified in the Rules to permit hybrid meetings. The 2022 Committee on Ethics was reconstituted with a clearer mandate to investigate breaches of parliamentary privilege and conflict‑of‑interest allegations. The Joint Committee on the Finance Bill now receives the Finance Bill before it is passed, a procedural safeguard introduced after the 2017 controversy over the Money Bill classification of the Aadhaar Act. The Committee on Subordinate Legislation reviews delegated legislation, ensuring that executive rule‑making does not exceed statutory authority. Despite these strengths, challenges persist: limited time for deliberation, partisan voting on sensitive reports, and the absence of a mandatory debate on committee recommendations in the House. The 2023 National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution recommended granting committees the power to initiate legislation on subjects within their domain, a proposal still under consideration.

Applied Scenario
A Bill amending the Representation of the People Act, 1951 is referred to the Department‑Related Standing Committee on Law and Justice. The Committee proposes an amendment that would disqualify candidates convicted of offences carrying a minimum sentence of two years, even if the conviction is under appeal. The government accepts the recommendation but the opposition argues that the amendment violates Article 14 by creating an irrational classification. How should the Committee’s role be evaluated in this context?
The Committee’s function is advisory; it examines the Bill’s technical merits, constitutional compatibility, and stakeholder views. Its recommendation to tighten disqualification criteria reflects a policy judgment within its mandate. However, the final constitutional test — whether the classification satisfies the reasonable‑classification doctrine under Article 14 — rests with the judiciary. The Committee cannot bind Parliament or the courts; its report merely informs the legislative debate. If the Bill is passed, any challenge will be adjudicated by the Supreme Court applying the twin‑test of intelligible differentia and rational nexus. Thus, the Committee’s role is to enhance legislative quality, not to pre‑empt judicial review.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about the “binding nature” of a parliamentary committee report, answer that reports are recommendatory; the government is obliged to respond but not to implement. Remember the distinction between financial committees (chaired by opposition) and other DRSCs (chaired by ruling party members). For recent reforms, note the 2021 virtual‑sitting rule and the 2022 Ethics Committee reconstitution as key updates.

Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles: Harmonisation, Conflict and Judicial Balancing

The Constitution places Fundamental Rights (Part III) and Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV) in a dynamic relationship. Article 37 declares Directive Principles non‑justiciable but fundamental in governance, while Article 31C (inserted by the 25th Amendment, 1971) originally gave primacy to Directive Principles over Articles 14 and 19. The Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) held that the basic structure includes the balance between Parts III and IV, preventing absolute supremacy of either. In Minerva Mills (1980) the Court struck down the portion of Article 31C that gave blanket precedence to all Directive Principles, restoring judicial review of laws enacted to implement them. The doctrine of harmonious construction now guides interpretation: courts strive to give effect to both sets of provisions, reading Directive Principles as interpretive aids for Fundamental Rights. Landmark applications include Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993), where the right to education was read into Article 21 using Directive Principles (Articles 41, 45), leading to the 86th Amendment (2002) inserting Article 21A. In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985), the Court linked the right to livelihood (Article 21) with Directive Principles on adequate means of livelihood (Article 39(a)). The principle of proportionality further mediates conflicts: a law implementing a Directive Principle must not disproportionately infringe a Fundamental Right. The 2017 Puttaswamy judgment reinforced this by requiring that any restriction on privacy pursue a legitimate aim, be necessary, and be proportionate. Recent jurisprudence, such as Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018) striking down adultery law, illustrates the Court’s willingness to invalidate legislation that claims Directive Principle justification but fails the proportionality test. Thus, the constitutional scheme envisions a synergistic, not hierarchical, interaction, with the judiciary as the arbiter of the constitutional equilibrium.

Emergency Provisions: National Emergency, President’s Rule and Financial Emergency – Safeguards and Judicial Scrutiny

The Constitution provides three distinct emergency regimes under Articles 352, 356 and 360. A National Emergency (Article 352) may be proclaimed on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment (1978) introduced critical safeguards: the proclamation requires written Cabinet advice, must be approved by both Houses within one month by a special majority, and can continue only with parliamentary approval every six months. Fundamental Rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended; other rights may be suspended only by a separate Presidential order subject to judicial review. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) held that the proclamation is justiciable and that the President’s satisfaction is not immune from scrutiny. President’s Rule (Article 356) allows the Centre to assume state governance upon failure of constitutional machinery. The same judgment laid down guidelines: the Governor’s report must be based on objective material; the proclamation must be approved by Parliament within two months; and the state assembly cannot be dissolved before parliamentary approval. The Court also ruled that the imposition of President’s Rule for political reasons violates the basic structure. Financial Emergency (Article 360) has never been invoked. It empowers the President to direct states to observe fiscal propriety and to reduce salaries of constitutional authorities. Judicial review extends to the factual basis of the proclamation, as affirmed in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977). The 44th Amendment also mandated that any emergency proclamation be laid before each House, and that the President must revoke it if the Lok Sabha passes a disapproval resolution. These layered checks — parliamentary oversight, judicial review, and temporal limits — constitute the constitutional architecture preventing executive overreach while preserving the Union’s ability to respond to grave crises.

Constitutional Bodies: Election Commission, Comptroller and Auditor General, Finance Commission – Mandates and Inter‑relations

Three pivotal constitutional bodies ensure electoral integrity, fiscal accountability, and equitable resource distribution. The Election Commission of India (ECI) (Article 324) enjoys autonomy guaranteed by security of tenure and conditions of service for the Chief Election Commissioner and Election Commissioners, removable only like a Supreme Court judge. It supervises elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, and the offices of President and Vice‑President, and enforces the Model Code of Conduct. The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) (Article 148) audits all receipts and expenditure of the Union and States, submitting reports to the President or Governor for laying before Parliament or State Legislature. The CAG’s independence is protected by a six‑year term or age 65, and removal only on proven misbehaviour or incapacity. The Finance Commission (Article 280) is constituted every five years to recommend the distribution of net tax proceeds between the Centre and States (vertical sharing) and among States (horizontal sharing), and to suggest grants‑in‑aid. Its recommendations are advisory but traditionally accepted. Inter‑relations are significant: the ECI’s delimitation orders affect the territorial basis for Finance Commission calculations; the CAG audits the accounts of the ECI and the Finance Commission’s secretariat; and the Finance Commission’s devolution formula influences the fiscal space available to State Election Commissions for local body elections. The 15th Finance Commission (2021‑26) introduced performance‑based incentives for demographic management and tax effort, linking fiscal transfers to governance outcomes. Recent debates centre on the appointment process for Election Commissioners — a 2023 Supreme Court judgment in Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India directed that appointments be made by a committee comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition, and Chief Justice of India, enhancing institutional credibility. Similarly, the CAG’s expanding role in performance audits of flagship schemes (e.g., MGNREGA, Ayushman Bharat) underscores the evolving accountability landscape.
