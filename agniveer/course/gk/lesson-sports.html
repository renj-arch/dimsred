What is Sports GK?

Sports General Knowledge is an important section in Agniveer and defence exams. It covers major sports tournaments, trophies and awards, venues, famous sports personalities, sports terminology, and international sporting events. Questions are typically straightforward and test awareness of popular sports like cricket, football, hockey, tennis, badminton, and Olympics.

Key Topics in Sports GK

1. Major Sports Tournaments: Cricket: ICC Cricket World Cup (ODI), T20 World Cup, IPL, Champions Trophy, Ashes (England-Australia). Football: FIFA World Cup, UEFA Champions League, Premier League, La Liga, Indian Super League (ISL). Hockey: Hockey World Cup, FIH Pro League, Sultan Azlan Shah Cup.

2. Trophies and Cups: Cricket: World Cup, Border-Gavaskar Trophy, Duleep Trophy, Ranji Trophy, Irani Cup, Vijay Hazare Trophy. Football: World Cup trophy, European Cup, FA Cup. Hockey: World Cup, Champions Trophy, Asia Cup. Tennis: Grand Slams — Australian Open, French Open, Wimbledon, US Open. Badminton: Thomas Cup (men), Uber Cup (women), Sudirman Cup (mixed).

3. Sports Awards in India: Major Dhyan Chand Khel Ratna (highest sporting honour), Arjuna Award, Dronacharya Award (coaching), Dhyan Chand Award (lifetime achievement), Rashtriya Khel Protsahan Puruskar (promotion of sports).

4. International Events: Summer Olympics (held every 4 years), Winter Olympics, Asian Games, Commonwealth Games, Para Olympics, Youth Olympics. Latest editions: Tokyo 2020 (held in 2021), Paris 2024, Los Angeles 2028.

5. Sports Terminology: Cricket: Stump, LBW, Googly, Silly point, Powerplay, DRS. Football: Offside, Dribble, Hat-trick, Free kick, Penalty, Yellow/Red card. Hockey: Bully, Penalty corner, Stroke, Dribble. Tennis: Love, Deuce, Advantage, Break point, Grand Slam. Badminton: Smash, Drop, Lob, Net shot, Rally.

6. Stadiums and Venues: Wankhede Stadium (Mumbai), Eden Gardens (Kolkata), M.A. Chidambaram (Chennai), Lords (London), MCG (Melbourne), Salt Lake Stadium (Kolkata, football), SAI centres, JLN Stadium (Delhi).

Solved Examples

Example 1
Which country has won the most FIFA World Cup titles?

a) Germany b) Argentina c) Brazil d) Italy
Solution: c) Brazil has won the FIFA World Cup 5 times (1958, 1962, 1970, 1994, 2002), the most by any country.
Example 2
The term 'Love' is associated with which sport?

a) Cricket b) Football c) Tennis d) Hockey
Solution: c) Tennis. 'Love' means zero in tennis scoring. It originates from the French word 'l'oeuf' meaning egg, which resembles zero.
Example 3
Who was the first Indian to win an Olympic individual gold medal?

a) P.V. Sindhu b) Neeraj Chopra c) Saina Nehwal d) Abhinav Bindra
Solution: d) Abhinav Bindra won the gold medal in men's 10m air rifle shooting at the 2008 Beijing Olympics. Neeraj Chopra won gold in javelin at Tokyo 2020.

  • Group awards by category: Team sports, Individual sports, Olympic events, Asian Games events.
  • Create a year-wise timeline of recent Olympics, Asian Games, and Commonwealth Games winners.

Evolution of Cricket World Cup Formats and Qualification Pathways

The Cricket World Cup has undergone several structural changes since its inception in 1975. The inaugural tournament featured eight teams playing 60‑over matches in a round‑robin group stage followed by semi‑finals and a final. From 1987 the overs were reduced to 50 per side, a format that remained standard until the 2019 edition. The 1992 tournament introduced a single league table where all nine participants played each other once, with the top four progressing to the semi‑finals — a format revived in 2019 with ten teams.

Qualification pathways have expanded in parallel. Until 2003, full members of the International Cricket Council (ICC) gained automatic entry while associate members contested a separate trophy (the ICC Trophy) for the remaining spots. The 2007 World Cup introduced a 16‑team format with a preliminary group stage, allowing more associate nations to compete. Since 2011 the tournament has been limited to 10 or 14 teams, with the ICC World Cup Qualifier (formerly the ICC Trophy) serving as the final gateway for non‑full members. The 2023 cycle retained a 10‑team main event, but the 2027 edition is slated to return to a 14‑team format, reflecting the ICC’s strategic goal of broader global participation.

Key milestones include the 1999 introduction of the Super Six stage, the 2003 adoption of a Super Eight stage, and the 2015 decision to revert to a straight knockout after a single group phase. Each redesign aimed to balance competitive integrity, commercial viability, and development opportunities for emerging cricket nations. Understanding these shifts is essential for answering questions on tournament history, team participation records, and the impact of format on statistical milestones such as highest run‑scorer or most wickets in a single edition.

Football Governance: FIFA, Continental Confederations, and Club Licensing

Global football governance rests on a three‑tier structure. At the apex sits FIFA (Fédération Internationale de Football Association), founded in 1904, which sets the Laws of the Game, organises the World Cup, and oversees the international match calendar. Below FIFA are six continental confederations — AFC (Asia), CAF (Africa), CONCACAF (North and Central America and the Caribbean), CONMEBOL (South America), OFC (Oceania), and UEFA (Europe). Each confederation runs its own championship (e.g., UEFA European Championship, Copa América) and manages World Cup qualification slots allocated by FIFA.

Club licensing, introduced by UEFA in 2004 and later adopted by other confederations, establishes minimum criteria for clubs to participate in continental competitions. The licensing system covers sporting, infrastructure, personnel, legal, and financial dimensions. Clubs must demonstrate youth development programmes, stadium safety standards, qualified coaching staff, and transparent ownership structures. Financial fair play regulations, first enforced by UEFA in 2011, require clubs to balance spending with revenue over a rolling three‑year period, limiting excessive debt accumulation.

FIFA’s Council, composed of the president, eight vice‑presidents, and 28 members elected by the member associations, decides on major policy issues such as World Cup host selection and changes to the international calendar. The Congress, meeting annually, ratifies statutes and elects the president. Recent reforms (2016‑2022) introduced term limits, independent audit committees, and a human rights policy aligned with the United Nations Guiding Principles. These governance layers create a complex web of authority that examiners often test through scenario‑based questions on eligibility, disciplinary jurisdiction, and revenue distribution.

Technique: Mapping Governance Layers
A club from a CONCACAF member association wishes to enter the CONCACAF Champions Cup. Which governing bodies must approve its participation, and what licensing criteria are mandatory?
The club must first satisfy its national association’s domestic licensing requirements, then obtain a CONCACAF club licence, which incorporates the confederation’s sporting, infrastructure, personnel, legal, and financial criteria. FIFA’s overarching statutes apply automatically, but no separate FIFA licence is needed for continental club competition. The mandatory criteria include a certified youth academy, a stadium meeting CONCACAF safety standards, qualified coaching staff (minimum AFC/UEFA Pro Licence or equivalent), transparent ownership disclosure, and compliance with CONCACAF’s financial fair play thresholds. Only after the national association endorses the licence does CONCACAF grant entry.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about the hierarchy of approvals, list the bodies from the lowest (national association) to the highest (FIFA) and note which tier imposes licensing. This ordered approach prevents missing a mandatory step.

Olympic Games Structure: Host Selection, Sport Inclusion Criteria, and Medal Table Methodology

The International Olympic Committee (IOC) selects host cities through a two‑stage bidding process. Candidate cities submit a detailed candidature file addressing venues, accommodation, transport, legacy plans, and budget guarantees. An Evaluation Commission visits each city and publishes a report. The final decision is made by the IOC Session, where each active member votes by secret ballot; a city must secure an absolute majority. Recent reforms (Olympic Agenda 2020) introduced a “preferred host” dialogue phase, allowing the IOC to engage with interested regions before a formal bid, reducing costs and political risk.

Sport inclusion follows the Olympic Programme Commission’s recommendations, approved by the IOC Executive Board and ratified by the Session. Criteria include universality (practised in at least 75 countries on four continents for men, 40 countries on three continents for women), governance by an International Federation recognised by the IOC, gender equality, youth appeal, and compliance with the World Anti‑Doping Code. New sports may be added as “additional events” for a single edition (e.g., skateboarding, sport climbing, surfing at Tokyo 2020) or as core sports after a successful trial period.

The official medal table ranks National Olympic Committees (NOCs) by gold medals first, then silver, then bronze. This lexicographic ordering is mandated by the IOC Charter. In case of a tie on all three counts, NOCs share the same rank. The table does not weight medals by population or GDP; such alternative rankings are produced by media organisations but have no official status. Understanding the official methodology prevents confusion when questions ask for the “top‑ranked nation” versus “most total medals”.

Technology in Modern Sport: DRS, VAR, Goal‑Line Technology, and Hawk‑Eye

Decision Review System (DRS) in cricket combines ball‑tracking (Hawk‑Eye), ultra‑edge (snickometer), and hot‑spot infrared imaging to assist on‑field umpires. Introduced in Test matches in 2008 and later in ODIs and T20Is, each team receives a limited number of unsuccessful reviews per innings (two in Tests, one in white‑ball formats). The “Umpire’s Call” protocol preserves the original decision when ball‑tracking shows marginal impact on the stumps, a nuance frequently tested in rule‑application questions.

Video Assistant Referee (VAR) in football, approved by the International Football Association Board (IFAB) in 2018, reviews four match‑changing situations: goals, penalty decisions, direct red‑card incidents, and mistaken identity. The VAR team, located in a centralised operations room, communicates with the on‑field referee via a headset. The referee may conduct an on‑field review (OFR) using a pitch‑side monitor. The protocol emphasises “clear and obvious error” — a subjective threshold that generates debate and exam scenarios about intervention limits.

Goal‑Line Technology (GLT) uses either camera‑based (Hawk‑Eye) or magnetic‑field (GoalControl) systems to determine whether the whole ball has crossed the line. The system sends an encrypted signal to the referee’s watch within one second, eliminating human error on goal decisions. GLT was first used at the 2014 FIFA World Cup and is now mandatory in all FIFA tournaments and most top‑flight leagues.

Hawk‑Eye, originally developed for tennis line‑calling, employs multiple high‑speed cameras to reconstruct ball trajectory in three dimensions. In tennis it provides instant replay for challenges; in cricket it powers the ball‑tracking component of DRS; in football it underpins GLT. The cross‑sport adoption illustrates how a single technology platform can be adapted to different rule sets, a point examiners may explore through comparative questions.

Indian Sports Administration: SAI, IOA, National Sports Federations, and Funding Mechanisms

The Sports Authority of India (SAI), established in 1984 under the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports, is the principal agency for talent identification, training infrastructure, and coach development. SAI operates regional centres, National Centres of Excellence, and the Target Olympic Podium Scheme (TOPS), which provides customized financial support to elite athletes and their support teams. Funding under TOPS covers coaching, equipment, sports science, and international exposure, with periodic performance reviews determining continuation.

The Indian Olympic Association (IOA), recognised by the IOC, is the National Olympic Committee responsible for selecting and sending Indian contingents to the Olympic Games, Asian Games, Commonwealth Games, and other multi‑sport events. The IOA affiliates National Sports Federations (NSFs) — one per Olympic and recognized non‑Olympic sport — which govern domestic competition, selection policies, and athlete development pathways. NSFs must comply with the National Sports Development Code of India (2011), which mandates democratic elections, age and tenure limits for office‑bearers, and transparent accounting.

Funding flows through three main channels: (1) Central government grants to SAI and NSFs via the Ministry; (2) Corporate sponsorship and Public Sector Undertaking (PSU) contributions, often routed through the National Sports Development Fund (NSDF); (3) Private philanthropy and athlete‑personal sponsorships. The Khelo India programme, launched in 2018, adds a grassroots layer by organising annual national games for school and university athletes, with scholarship awards for top performers. Recent policy emphasis on “sports as a career” has led to the introduction of the National Sports University (Manipur) and the inclusion of sports science curricula in mainstream universities.

  • Confusing the ICC World Cup Qualifier with the ICC Trophy — they are the same tournament renamed in 2014; using the old name for recent cycles is a trap.
  • Assuming FIFA directly licences clubs for continental competitions — licensing is a confederation responsibility, not a FIFA one.
  • Believing the Olympic medal table ranks by total medals — the official order is gold, then silver, then bronze.
  • Thinking DRS allows unlimited reviews — each team has a capped number of unsuccessful reviews per innings.
  • Mixing up SAI’s operational role with IOA’s representative role — SAI builds infrastructure, IOA selects teams for multi‑sport games.
  • Overlooking the “Umpire’s Call” rule in DRS — marginal decisions revert to the on‑field call, a frequent exam twist.
  • Treating Khelo India scholarships as equivalent to TOPS funding — they target different athlete development stages.

Asian Games and Commonwealth Games: Structure, Medal Tally Rules, and Indian Performance Trends

The Asian Games, governed by the Olympic Council of Asia (OCA), were first held in New Delhi in 1951 and have since become the continent’s premier multi‑sport event, staged every four years in the same cycle as the Summer Olympics. The Commonwealth Games, organised by the Commonwealth Games Federation (CGF), trace their origins to the 1930 British Empire Games in Hamilton, Canada, and also follow a four‑year cycle offset by two years from the Olympics. Both games adopt the International Olympic Committee (IOC) medal‑table convention: nations are ranked first by gold medals, then by silver, then by bronze. This rule is frequently tested because many candidates mistakenly assume total‑medal count determines the order.

Participation criteria differ: the Asian Games require membership in the OCA (45 members) and compliance with the OCA Constitution, while the Commonwealth Games require membership in the Commonwealth of Nations (56 members) and adherence to the CGF Constitution. Both events allow a maximum of three athletes per nation per individual event and one team per nation per team event, subject to qualification standards set by the respective International Federations (IFs).

India’s medal trajectory illustrates the impact of targeted funding. At the 2010 Commonwealth Games in New Delhi, India topped the table with 101 medals (38 gold). In the 2018 Asian Games in Jakarta‑Palembang, India finished third with 69 medals (15 gold), its best ever Asian Games showing. The 2022 Commonwealth Games in Birmingham yielded 61 medals (22 gold), while the 2023 Asian Games in Hangzhou (held in 2023 due to COVID‑19 postponement) saw India climb to fourth with 107 medals (28 gold). These shifts reflect the operation of the Target Olympic Podium Scheme (TOPS) and the Khelo India programme, which channel resources toward sports where India has historic depth — wrestling, shooting, boxing, and badminton — while also investing in emerging disciplines such as athletics field events and weightlifting.

Qualification pathways for both games are managed by the IFs in coordination with the OCA or CGF. For example, World Athletics sets entry standards for track and field; the International Weightlifting Federation allocates quota places based on world rankings and continental championships. National Olympic Committees (NOCs) or Commonwealth Games Associations (CGAs) then nominate athletes who have met the standards, subject to the “one‑nation‑one‑team” rule for team sports. Understanding this layered qualification — IF standard → continental quota → NOC/CGA nomination — is essential for answering questions on athlete eligibility and team selection controversies.

Major Trophies in Team Sports: Origins, Naming, and Notable Finals

The ICC Cricket World Cup trophy, first awarded in 1975, was replaced in 1999 by the current silver‑gilt cup designed by Garrard & Co.; the original “Prudential Cup” is now displayed at the MCC Museum at Lord’s. The ICC Champions Trophy, inaugurated as the ICC KnockOut Trophy in 1998, was renamed in 2002 and discontinued after 2017, though a revival is under discussion. In football, the Jules Rimet Trophy (originally “Victory”) was awarded from 1930 to 1970; after Brazil’s third win in 1970 they retained it permanently, prompting FIFA to commission the current FIFA World Cup Trophy (designed by Silvio Gazzaniga) for 1974 onward. The UEFA Champions League trophy, known as the “European Champion Clubs’ Cup” until 2006, was redesigned by Jörg Stadelmann; the original 1955 cup is kept by Real Madrid, the most successful club with 15 titles.

Field hockey’s premier prize, the Hockey World Cup trophy, was introduced in 1971 and crafted by the Pakistan Hockey Federation; it features a silver globe atop a wooden base. The Men’s FIH Pro League, launched in 2019, awards a separate trophy but does not replace the World Cup. In rugby union, the Webb Ellis Cup — named after the sport’s mythical founder — has been contested since the inaugural 1987 Rugby World Cup; the trophy is made of sterling silver and weighs 4.5 kg. The Women’s Rugby World Cup uses the same cup since 1998. The Basketball World Cup (FIBA) trophy, the Naismith Trophy, was first awarded in 1967 and redesigned in 1998; it honours James Naismith, the sport’s inventor.

Each trophy carries a narrative that examiners exploit: the year of inception, the reason for a redesign, the nation or club that earned permanent possession, and the material composition. For instance, the Jules Rimet Trophy’s theft in 1966 and recovery by a dog named Pickles is a classic anecdote. The ICC World Cup’s 1999 redesign coincided with the tournament’s expansion to 12 teams and the introduction of the Super Six stage. The UEFA Champions League’s 2006 redesign introduced a larger base to accommodate more engraved winners. Knowing these details allows rapid elimination of distractors in multiple‑choice questions that ask “Which trophy was permanently awarded to Brazil in 1970?” or “What material is the Webb Ellis Cup made of?”

Comparative Medal Table Calculation
At the Asian Games, Country A finishes with 10 gold, 5 silver, 8 bronze. Country B finishes with 9 gold, 12 silver, 10 bronze. According to the official OCA ranking rule, which country is placed higher?
The official ranking orders nations by gold medals first. Country A has 10 gold versus Country B’s 9 gold, so Country A ranks higher regardless of silver or bronze totals. The silver and bronze counts are only used as tie‑breakers when gold counts are equal.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the gold‑first rule for every major multi‑sport event (Olympics, Asian Games, Commonwealth Games, Asian Para Games). Practice by constructing mini‑tables with three countries and varying medal spreads; the pattern will become automatic under time pressure.

Stadium Infrastructure: ICC and FIFA Standards, Iconic Venues, and Hosting Requirements

The International Cricket Council (ICC) classifies venues into three tiers for international matches. A Test‑match venue must have a minimum seating capacity of 15,000, floodlights meeting a minimum average illuminance of 1,500 lux, a playing area of at least 137 m × 150 m, and a pitch prepared to ICC specifications (22 yd length, 10 ft width). One‑Day International (ODI) venues require a minimum capacity of 20,000 and 2,000 lux floodlights, while T20 Internationals can be staged at grounds with 10,000 seats provided lighting reaches 1,500 lux. All venues must possess a media centre, a dedicated doping‑control room, and a secure players’ corridor.

FIFA’s Stadium Requirements for the World Cup are more stringent. A group‑stage stadium must hold at least 40,000 spectators; quarter‑finals 50,000; semi‑finals 60,000; and the final 80,000. The pitch dimensions must fall within 100–110 m length and 64–75 m width, with a grass surface meeting FIFA Quality Programme standards. Floodlighting must deliver a minimum of 2,500 lux horizontally and 1,500 lux vertically for HDTV broadcast. Each stadium must provide a minimum of 2,000 m² of media workspace, a mixed‑zone area, a VAR operations room, and a dedicated medical facility with an on‑site ambulance.

Iconic venues illustrate compliance and legacy. Lord’s (London) meets ICC Test criteria and hosts the MCC Museum; Melbourne Cricket Ground (capacity 100,024) satisfies both ICC and FIFA standards and staged the 1992 Cricket World Cup final and 2015 AFC Asian Cup matches. Eden Gardens (Kolkata, 68,000) underwent a 2011 renovation to meet ICC floodlight norms. Wembley Stadium (London, 90,000) was rebuilt in 2007 to FIFA final specifications and hosted the 2012 Olympic football final. Maracanã (Rio de Janeiro, 78,838) was refurbished for the 2014 FIFA World Cup and 2016 Olympics, incorporating a retractable roof and LED lighting. The Narendra Modi Stadium (Ahmedabad, 132,000) is the world’s largest cricket venue and was certified for ICC World Cup 2023 matches after upgrades to drainage, pitch technology, and spectator amenities.

Hosting a multi‑sport event adds layers: the Organising Committee must secure government guarantees for security, transport, and visa facilitation; obtain a “Host City Contract” from the IOC/OCA/CGF; and pass a series of inspection visits (CoCom for Olympics, OCA Inspection for Asian Games). Failure to meet any mandatory criterion — such as insufficient doping‑control laboratories or inadequate athlete‑village capacity — can result in the withdrawal of hosting rights, a scenario that appears in case‑study questions.

Anti‑Doping Framework: WADA Code, NADA Operations, and Landmark Indian Cases

The World Anti‑Doping Agency (WADA) was established in 1999 and its cornerstone, the World Anti‑Doping Code, came into force on 1 January 2004. The Code harmonises prohibited‑list standards, testing procedures, Therapeutic Use Exemptions (TUEs), results management, and sanctions across all signatories — International Federations, National Olympic Committees, and National Anti‑Doping Organisations (NADOs). Major revisions occurred in 2009, 2015, and 2021; the 2021 version introduced a “flexible sanctioning” regime for substances of abuse and strengthened athlete‑rights provisions.

India’s National Anti‑Doping Agency (NADA) was formed in 2005 under the Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports and became a WADA signatory in 2008. NADA conducts both in‑competition and out‑of‑competition testing, manages the Registered Testing Pool (RTP) of elite athletes, processes TUE applications, and adjudicates anti‑doping rule violations through its independent Anti‑Doping Disciplinary Panel (ADDP) and Anti‑Doping Appeal Panel (ADAP). NADA also operates the National Dope Testing Laboratory (NDTL) in New Delhi, which regained WADA accreditation in 2021 after a 2019 suspension.

Landmark Indian cases illustrate the framework’s application. In 2016, wrestler Narsingh Yadav tested positive for a banned steroid weeks before the Rio Olympics; the ADDP imposed a four‑year ban, later upheld by the Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS). In 2018, discus thrower Seema Punia received a two‑year sanction for a prohibited diuretic, with the panel noting the absence of a valid TUE. The 2022 Tokyo Olympics saw two Indian athletes — sprinter Dhanalakshmi Sekar and weightlifter Mirabai Chanu’s support staff — flagged for whereabouts failures, leading to provisional suspensions that were later lifted after successful appeals. These cases are frequently cited in questions about the burden of proof, the role of the “strict liability” principle, and the interplay between NADA and CAS.

Examiners also test knowledge of the Prohibited List categories (S0–S9, P1–P2, M1–M3), the distinction between “specified” and “non‑specified” substances, and the mandatory reporting of whereabouts for RTP athletes (quarterly filings, 60‑minute daily window). Understanding the timeline — sample collection → laboratory analysis → results management → hearing → appeal — enables accurate answers to procedural questions.

Emerging Sports in Multi‑Sport Events: Inclusion Criteria, Demonstration Sports, and Future Programme

The IOC’s Olympic Programme Commission evaluates candidate sports against seven criteria: universality (participation across continents), popularity (global fan base and media interest), governance (existence of a recognised International Federation), athlete welfare, gender equality, cost and venue complexity, and legacy value. A sport must be administered by an IF recognised by the IOC and must have held at least two World Championships before petitioning for inclusion. The final decision rests with the IOC Session, which votes on the full programme approximately seven years before the Games.

Demonstration sports allow the IOC to gauge audience response without committing to medal status. Baseball and softball appeared as demonstration sports in 1984 and 1988 before becoming official in 1992 (baseball) and 1996 (softball); both were dropped after 2008 and reinstated for Tokyo 2020. Karate, skateboarding, sport climbing, and surfing debuted as official sports at Tokyo 2020 after being showcased at the 2018 Youth Olympics. Breakdancing (breaking) will make its Olympic debut at Paris 2024, having been a demonstration event at the 2018 Youth Olympics in Buenos Aires.

The Asian Games have been more experimental. The 2018 Jakarta‑Palembang edition included esports as a demonstration sport, and the 2022 Hangzhou Games (held in 2023) awarded medals in esports for the first time, with titles such as League of Legends, Dota 2, and FIFA 23. Cricket returned to the Asian Games in 2023 after a 13‑year absence, played in the T20 format for both men and women. The Commonwealth Games introduced 3×3 basketball and mixed‑gender relays in athletics and swimming at Birmingham 2022, reflecting the CGF’s “Gender Equality Strategy”.

Future programme discussions centre on sports that satisfy the universality and youth‑appeal criteria while limiting venue costs. The IOC has provisionally approved flag football, lacrosse (sixes), and squash for the 2028 Los Angeles Games, pending final confirmation in 2025. The Asian Games 2026 (Aichi‑Nagoya) are expected to retain esports and add ju‑jutsu. For aspirants, the key is to track the “Olympic Agenda 2020+5” recommendations, which emphasise flexibility, digital engagement, and sustainability — concepts that now shape exam questions on why a sport was added or removed.

  • Confusing the medal‑table ranking rule (gold‑first) with total‑medal count — a frequent distractor in Asian Games and Commonwealth Games questions.
  • Assuming the Jules Rimet Trophy is still awarded — it was permanently given to Brazil in 1970; the current FIFA World Cup Trophy has been used since 1974.
  • Mixing ICC venue tiers — Test venues need 15,000 seats, ODI venues 20,000, T20I venues can be 10,000; floodlight lux levels differ accordingly.
  • Believing NADA can impose sanctions without a hearing — the Anti‑Doping Disciplinary Panel must conduct a hearing before any sanction.
  • Thinking demonstration sports automatically become medal sports — they require a separate IOC Session vote and must meet all inclusion criteria.
  • Overlooking the “strict liability” principle — an athlete is responsible for any prohibited substance found in their sample regardless of intent.
  • Misidentifying the governing body for a trophy — e.g., attributing the Webb Ellis Cup to World Rugby (correct) but confusing it with the Rugby League World Cup trophy (different).
