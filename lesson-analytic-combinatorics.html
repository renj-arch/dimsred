Analytic Combinatorics — Generating Functions, Singularity Analysis & the Central Limit Theorem

Analytic combinatorics is the study of combinatorial structures through the lens of complex analysis, developed systematically by Philippe Flajolet and Robert Sedgewick. The central philosophy is that combinatorial objects — trees, permutations, graphs, words — can be encoded as generating functions (ordinary or exponential), and the asymptotic growth of their coefficients is governed by the singularities of these analytic functions in the complex plane. The symbolic method provides a dictionary between combinatorial constructions (sum, product, sequence, set, cycle) and functional equations on generating functions. Singularity analysis (Flajolet-Odlyzko) then extracts coefficient asymptotics from the singular expansion near dominant singularities. The saddle point method handles entire generating functions. The theory culminates in the combinatorial central limit theorem (Hwang's quasi-powers theorem), showing that many parameters in random structures are asymptotically Gaussian. Applications range from random trees and permutations to the analysis of algorithms like quicksort, hashing, and tries.

"Analytic combinatorics merges the symbolic approach with asymptotic analysis."
— Philippe Flajolet
Part 1

📏 The Symbolic Method

OGF & EGF
Ordinary and Exponential Generating Functions, Combinatorial Constructions
Let A be a combinatorial class with size function |·| and let a_n = |{objects of size n}|. The ordinary generating function (OGF) is A(z) = Σ_{n≥0} a_n z^n. The exponential generating function (EGF) is Â(z) = Σ_{n≥0} a_n z^n/n!. The symbolic method translates set-theoretic constructions into equations on generating functions. For disjoint union: A = B ∪ C ⇒ A(z) = B(z) + C(z). For Cartesian product: A = B × C ⇒ A(z) = B(z)·C(z). For sequences: A = Seq(B) ⇒ A(z) = 1/(1−B(z)). For labelled structures, the EGF transforms products: A = B ⋆ C ⇒ Â(z) = ̂B(z)·̂C(z). The labelled set construction: A = Set(B) ⇒ Â(z) = exp(̂B(z)). The labelled cycle construction: A = Cyc(B) ⇒ Â(z) = log(1/(1−̂B(z))). Catalan numbers (binary trees): C(z) = 1 + z·C(z)² ⇒ C(z) = (1−√(1−4z))/(2z) ⇒ c_n = (1/(n+1))·(2n choose n). Permutations: P = Set(Cyc(Z)) ⇒ ̂P(z) = exp(log(1/(1−z))) = 1/(1−z) ⇒ p_n = n!.
The symbolic method gives a direct algebraic encoding of combinatorial structure — every construction on objects corresponds to an operation on generating functions.
Part 2

🌍 Singularity Analysis and the Transfer Theorem

Asymptotics
Flajolet-Odlyzko Singularity Analysis, Transfer Theorems, and the Standard Function Scale
The asymptotic growth of a_n = [z^n] F(z) is determined by the singularities of F(z) nearest the origin (dominant singularities). If F(z) has a unique dominant singularity at z = R, then a_n ∼ R^{−n}·θ(n) where θ(n) is subexponential. The standard function scale: for α ∉ −ℕ, (1−z)^{−α} ∼ Σ_{n≥0} (n+α−1 choose n) z^n and [z^n] (1−z)^{−α} ∼ n^{α−1}/Γ(α). The transfer theorem (Flajolet-Odlyzko, 1990): if f(z) ∼ c·(1−z/R)^{−α}·(log(1/(1−z/R)))^β as z → R, then [z^n] f(z) ∼ c·R^{−n}·n^{α−1}·(log n)^β/Γ(α). The square-root singularity (α = ½): for F(z) = (1−4z)^{−1/2}, we have [z^n] F(z) ∼ 4^n/√(πn). For F(z) = 1−√(1−4z) (Catalan), [z^n] F(z) ∼ 4^n/(√π·n^{3/2}). Algebraic functions have singularities at the roots of their discriminant. The critical exponents for trees: rooted trees (Cayley) have EGF T(z) = z·exp(T(z)) with T(z) ∼ 1−√(2(1−ez)) near the singularity, giving t_n ∼ n^{n−1} (Cayley's formula).
Singularities encode the exponential growth rate, and the type of singularity (pole, algebraic, logarithmic) determines the subexponential factor — this is the analytic bridge from generating functions to coefficient asymptotics.
Part 3

🧮 Random Structures and the Central Limit Theorem

CLT
The Quasi-Powers Theorem, Hwang's CLT, Drmota-Soria Theory, and Rice's Integral
Consider a bivariate generating function P(z,u) = Σ p_{n,k} z^n u^k where p_{n,k} counts objects of size n with parameter value k. The quasi-powers theorem (Hwang, 1994): if P(z,u) ∼ C(u)·(1−z/ρ(u))^{−α(u)} near z = ρ(u) with analytic α(u), ρ(u), then the distribution of X_n (the parameter) is asymptotically Gaussian: (X_n − μn)/√(σ²n) → N(0,1) where μ = −ρ'(1)/ρ(1) and σ² = μ² − ρ''(1)/ρ(1) + μ·ρ'(1)/ρ(1). The Drmota-Soria theory (1997) extends this to additive parameters in random trees: for a large class of tree parameters (e.g., number of leaves, path length), the CLT holds with linear mean and variance. Rice's integral provides a powerful tool for computing alternating sums: Σ_{k=0}^n (n choose k)(−1)^k f(k) = (1/(2πi)) ∫_C f(s) n!/s(s−1)…(s−n) ds, where C encircles {0,1,…,n}. The integral is evaluated via residue calculus by summing residues of f(s) at its poles outside the contour. Applications: the average height of trees, the average size of tries and Patricia tries, and the analysis of digital trees.
The quasi-powers theorem is the master theorem of combinatorial probability — it reduces distributional analysis to singularity analysis of bivariate generating functions, yielding Gaussian limits for a vast range of parameters.
Part 4

🔥 The Saddle Point Method and the Analysis of Algorithms

Algorithms
Saddle Point Method, Airy Asymptotics, Quicksort, Tries, and Boltzmann Sampling
The saddle point method is used when the generating function has no singularities (entire) or when the dominant singularity is too far — it applies the Cauchy integral formula a_n = (1/(2πi))∮ F(z)z^{−n−1} dz, deforming the contour to pass through the saddle point where the integrand is stationary and minimal. The Laplace method approximates the integral by a Gaussian near the saddle. Airy-type asymptotics arise in the enumeration of planar graphs: the number of labelled planar graphs on n vertices satisfies p_n ∼ c·n^{−7/2}·γ^n·n! (the Airy function appears in the refined analysis). The analysis of algorithms: Quicksort's average number of comparisons C_n satisfies C_n = n−1 + (1/n)Σ_{k=1}^n (C_{k−1}+C_{n−k}), solved via harmonic numbers to C_n = 2(n+1)H_n − 4n ∼ 2n·ln n. The Mellin transform is used for divide-and-conquer recurrences. The digital tree (trie): the expected external path length satisfies E[L_n] ∼ n·log_2 n + O(n). Boltzmann sampling (the rejection method using generating functions): to generate a random object of size near n, draw a size-biased distribution using the generating function evaluated at a tuned parameter x, then reject objects with undesired sizes. Hadamard products and functional composition allow the construction of complex generating functions from simpler ones.
The saddle point method completes the asymptotic toolkit, handling cases where the transfer theorem does not apply — together with Rice's integral and the Mellin transform, it provides a complete analytic arsenal for the analysis of algorithms.
