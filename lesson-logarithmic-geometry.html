Logarithmic Geometry — Log Structures, Degenerations & the Monster

Logarithmic geometry, developed by Fontaine, Illusie, and Kato in the 1980s–90s, is a far-reaching refinement of scheme theory designed to handle degenerations, singularities, and non-smooth phenomena as if they were smooth. The central object is a log structure on a scheme X: a sheaf of commutative monoids MX together with a homomorphism α: MX → (OX, ·) such that α−1(OX*) ≅ OX* via the natural inclusion. This extra monoidal data records combinatorial information about boundaries, divisors, and normal crossings — turning a family that degenerates along a normal crossing divisor into a log smooth morphism, the logarithmic analogue of a smooth map. The theory encompasses log étale cohomology, log de Rham cohomology (differentials with logarithmic poles), log crystalline cohomology (via the Hyodo–Kato construction), and a full log Hodge theory that extends classical Hodge-theoretic results to open and degenerate varieties via the Kato–Nakayama space. Applications range from semistable reduction and the weight–monodromy conjecture to p-adic cohomology and the arithmetic Langlands program (Fargues–Scholze). Logarithmic geometry is, in Kato's words, "the art of turning degenerations into smooth maps."

Part 1

📏 Log Structures and Monoids

Foundation
Pre-Log Structures, Log Structures, and the Category of Monoids
Let X be a scheme. A pre-log structure on X is a pair (M, α) where M is a sheaf of commutative monoids on the étale site Xét and α: M → OX is a homomorphism of monoids (OX under multiplication). A log structure is a pre-log structure such that the induced map α−1(OX*) → OX* is an isomorphism. Every pre-log structure admits an associated log structure obtained by pushing out α−1(OX*) ← α−1(OX*) ∩ M → M along the inclusion into OX*. The trivial log structure is (OX*, ι). The divisorial log structure from a divisor D ⊂ X with complement U = X \ D is MX = OX ∩ j*OU* where j: U ↪ X. The standard log point is Spec k with the log structure ℕ → k sending 1 ↦ 0 — the simplest non-trivial log scheme. A monoid in log geometry is always commutative and written multiplicatively. It is integral if the map M → Mgp (to its Grothendieck group) is injective; saturated if it is integral and for every m ∈ Mgp with mn ∈ M for some n ≥ 1, one has m ∈ M; and fine (fs) if it is integral, saturated, and finitely generated. The characteristic monoid M̅X = MX / OX* measures the "logarithmic" part of the structure — it is a sheaf of sharp monoids whose stalks encode the combinatorial data of the boundary.
A log structure enriches a scheme with a monoidal boundary; the quotient by units yields the characteristic monoid that captures the combinatorial essence of the degeneration.
Part 2

🌍 Log Smooth Morphisms and Log Étale Cohomology

Morphisms
Log Smoothness, Log Étale Maps, Log de Rham and Log Étale Cohomology
A morphism f: (X, MX) → (Y, MY) of log schemes is log smooth (resp. log étale) if it is locally of finite presentation and satisfies the logarithmic analogue of the infinitesimal lifting criterion for smooth (resp. étale) morphisms. Concretely, for a log smooth morphism, the log cotangent complex is a vector bundle concentrated in degree 0. The sheaf of logarithmic differentials Ω1X/Y(log) is defined as the OX-module generated by the usual Kähler differentials and the logarithmic derivations dlog(m) for m ∈ MX, where dlog(m) = m−1 dm. The log de Rham complex Ω•X/Y(log) = ∧• Ω1X/Y(log) gives rise to log de Rham cohomology H•dR(X/Y, log). For a smooth divisor D ⊂ X, this recovers the classical de Rham complex with logarithmic poles along D. There is a log Hodge-to-de Rham spectral sequence Ep,q1 = Hq(X, ΩpX/Y(log)) ⇒ Hp+qdR(X/Y, log). The log étale topology is the Grothendieck topology on fs log schemes where coverings are Kummer log étale — i.e., log étale and inducing finite extensions of the characteristic monoid stalks. Log étale cohomology H•log-ét(X, F) is defined with coefficients in sheaves of ℓ-adic or ℤp-modules, using the Kummer flat topology (Kummer-flat). A log geometric point is a morphism Spec k → X of log schemes where k is algebraically closed and the log structure on Spec k is fs. These provide the fiber functors for log étale fundamental groups.
Log smooth morphisms extend the notion of smoothness to degenerating families; log de Rham and log étale cohomology provide cohomological invariants that are well-behaved under degeneration.
Part 3

🧮 Semistable Reduction and Degenerations

Degenerations
Semistable Reduction, Vanishing Cycles, Weight–Monodromy, and Log Hodge Theory
The semistable reduction theorem (Grothendieck–Mumford, proved via alterations by de Jong): given a family f: X → S over a DVR with normal crossing degenerations, after a finite base change the family becomes semistable — i.e., the special fiber is a reduced normal crossing divisor. Log geometry provides the natural language: a semistable morphism is exactly a log smooth morphism for the standard log structure on the base. The vanishing cycle complex in the log setting RΨ(ℚℓ) sits in a distinguished triangle relating the log étale cohomology of the generic and special fibers. The Rapoport–Zink spectral sequence Ep,q2 = Hp(Xs, RqΨ(ℚℓ)) ⇒ Hp+q(Xη̅, ℚℓ) is the log-geometric analogue of the Leray spectral sequence for a degeneration. The Clemens–Schmid exact sequence describes the monodromy action on the cohomology of the generic fiber in terms of the special fiber's log cohomology. The weight–monodromy conjecture (proved for semistable varieties by Saito, and in general by Scholze) states that the monodromy filtration on the ℓ-adic cohomology of the generic fiber coincides with the weight filtration. The Kato–Nakayama space Xlog associated to a log analytic space (X, MX) is a topological space (with a C∞ structure in the fs case) that provides a Betti realization: log Betti cohomology H•B(Xlog, ℚ) is isomorphic to log étale cohomology with ℚ coefficients under the comparison isomorphism, giving a full logarithmic Hodge theory (Kato–Nakayama, Ogus).
Semistable reduction turns arbitrary degenerations into log smooth families; log Hodge theory extends the classical Hodge package to open and singular varieties via the Kato–Nakayama topological realization.
Part 4

🔥 Log Crystalline Cohomology and Motivic Theory

p-adic
Log Crystalline Cohomology, Hyodo–Kato Isomorphism, and Logarithmic Motives
Log crystalline cohomology (Kato, Hyodo–Kato) is the crystalline cohomology of a log scheme (X, MX) over a log base (S, MS) with divided power structures. The key object is the log de Rham–Witt complex Wω•X, a logarithmic variant of the de Rham–Witt complex, whose hypercohomology computes the log crystalline cohomology of X. For a semistable variety X over a p-adic field with special fiber Y over the residue field, the Hyodo–Kato isomorphism identifies the log crystalline cohomology of Y (with its canonical log structure) with the rigid cohomology of the generic fiber, after adjoining a period element. The monodromy operator N = log(Tu) (where T is the Frobenius action) acts nilpotently and defines the monodromy filtration. Log syntomic cohomology Sn(r)X = fiber of (Wnω•X → φpWnω•X shifted) interpolates between étale and crystalline cohomology and is central to p-adic comparison theorems (Tsuji, Faltings, Beilinson). The Kato filtration on log de Rham cohomology refines the monodromy filtration and appears in the log Cartier isomorphism C−1: ΩiXs/k(log) → ℋi(Wω•X). The weight–monodromy conjecture for semistable varieties (proved by Saito using microlocal methods, and by Scholze via perfectoid techniques) asserts that the monodromy filtration on ℓ-adic cohomology equals the weight filtration. In the arithmetic Langlands program, log geometry is indispensable: the Fargues–Scholze geometric Langlands correspondence uses log geometry on the stack of bundles on the Fargues–Fontaine curve. Finally, logarithmic motives (Binda–Park–Østvær) provide a log version of Voevodsky's triangulated category of mixed motives, complete with a log étale realization and a log motivic t-structure.
Log crystalline cohomology and the Hyodo–Kato isomorphism are the p-adic workhorses for degenerating families; logarithmic motives extend the motivic formalism to open and singular varieties.

Log Riemann–Hilbert Correspondence and Irregular Connections

The classical Riemann–Hilbert correspondence identifies regular holonomic D‑modules on a smooth complex variety with perverse sheaves on the associated analytic space. In logarithmic geometry the correspondence extends to meromorphic connections with logarithmic poles along a normal crossing divisor. Kato and Nakayama constructed a topological space Xlog that captures the monodromy of such connections; the log de Rham complex Ω•X(log) computes the cohomology of the corresponding local system on Xlog. For irregular connections the Stokes phenomenon appears, and the logarithmic enhancement of the irregular Riemann–Hilbert correspondence (Mochizuki, Kedlaya) replaces the usual Stokes filtration by a log‑Stokes filtration on the log de Rham complex. The key object is the log irregular holonomic D‑module, whose solutions form a constructible complex on the Kato–Nakayama space with a prescribed growth condition at the boundary. The equivalence of categories is proved by combining the log de Rham functor with the log Stokes functor, and it respects the six operations (pushforward, pullback, tensor, dual) in the log setting. This framework underlies the geometric Langlands program for curves with ramification, where the Hecke eigensheaves are realized as log D‑modules with prescribed irregular singularities.

Log Prismatic Cohomology and the Breuil–Kisin Classification

Prismatic cohomology, introduced by Bhatt and Scholze, provides a universal p‑adic cohomology theory for formal schemes. When a log structure is present, one works with log prisms (A, I, M) where M is a monoid mapping to A and the ideal I is equipped with a divided power structure compatible with the log structure. The log prismatic site (X, MX)△ yields a cohomology theory H•△(X, MX) that specializes to log crystalline cohomology after base change to the Breuil–Kisin prism and to log étale cohomology after inverting p. The Breuil–Kisin classification of finite flat group schemes over a p‑adic DVR extends to log group schemes: a log finite flat group scheme over (R, MR) corresponds to a log Breuil–Kisin module, i.e. a finite free module over the log Breuil–Kisin ring equipped with a Frobenius semilinear map and a log monodromy operator N satisfying the usual compatibility. The log prismatic F‑gauge (Fil•, φ) encodes the Hodge–Tate weights of the associated Galois representation, and the log Nygaard filtration gives the weight filtration predicted by the weight–monodromy conjecture. This theory has been used to prove the log version of the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture for varieties with semistable reduction, and it provides the p‑adic input for the Fargues–Scholze geometric Langlands correspondence on the log Fargues–Fontaine curve.

Worked Example: Computing Log Prismatic Cohomology of a Nodal Curve
Let X be a nodal cubic over a perfect field k of characteristic p > 0, equipped with the divisorial log structure given by the node. Compute H1△(X, MX) and identify its Hodge–Tate weights.
The log prismatic site of (X, MX) is equivalent to the prismatic site of the normalization X̃ together with a gluing datum at the two preimages of the node. The log prismatic cohomology fits into a Mayer–Vietoris sequence 0 → H0△(X) → H0△(X̃) ⊕ H0△(Spec k) → H0△(two points) → H1△(X) → H1△(X̃) → 0. Since X̃ ≅ ℙ1 has H1△(X̃) = 0, the map from the two points is surjective and H1△(X) ≅ k. The Hodge–Tate weights are obtained from the Nygaard filtration: Fil0 = H1△, Fil1 = 0, so the only weight is 0. This matches the expectation that the log H1 of a nodal curve is a Tate twist of the trivial representation.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a comparison between log crystalline and log prismatic cohomology, first state the base prisms (Breuil–Kisin vs. q‑de Rham) and then recall that both theories satisfy the same six functor formalism. The log Nygaard filtration is the bridge: its graded pieces give the log Hodge–Tate cohomology, which is canonically isomorphic to log de Rham cohomology. Write down the exact triangle relating the three cohomologies; examiners often award marks for citing the triangle rather than reproducing the full construction.

Log Perfectoid Spaces and the Fargues–Fontaine Curve

Perfectoid spaces, introduced by Scholze, admit a natural logarithmic enhancement: a log perfectoid space is a perfectoid space equipped with an fs log structure such that the tilt functor preserves the log structure. The log tilt (X♭, MX♭) is again a log perfectoid space, and the log Fontaine period map extends to a morphism of log diamonds. The Fargues–Fontaine curve XFF = (Y {∞})/φℤ carries a canonical log structure coming from the divisor at infinity; its log structure is fs and the associated Kato–Nakayama space is a circle bundle over the usual curve. Vector bundles on the log Fargues–Fontaine curve are classified by the log version of the Bhatt–Scholze theorem: they correspond to log Breuil–Kisin modules with a Frobenius and a monodromy operator satisfying the log Griffiths transversality condition. The log geometric Langlands correspondence of Fargues–Scholze uses the stack BunGlog of G‑bundles on the log curve; the Hecke operators act on the log étale cohomology of this stack, and the spectral side is given by log local systems on the log curve. The log structure is essential for handling ramified Langlands parameters, because the monodromy at the divisor encodes the Swan conductor of the corresponding Galois representation.

Log Motivic Homotopy Theory and the Logarithmic Six Functors

Voevodsky’s motivic homotopy theory has a logarithmic analogue developed by Binda, Park, and Østvær. The log motivic category DMlog(k) is built from log smooth schemes over a field k with the log Nisnevich topology; its objects are log motives Mlog(X) and the morphisms are given by log correspondences. The six functors (f!, f!, f*, f*, ⊗, Hom) exist on DMlog and satisfy the usual adjunctions and projection formulas, with the additional feature that the log purity isomorphism identifies the log motive of a log smooth pair (X, D) with a Tate twist of the log motive of the complement. The log motivic t‑structure yields a heart of log mixed motives, and the log étale realization functor lands in the derived category of log ℓ‑adic sheaves, compatible with the log weight filtration. The log motivic cohomology groups Hi,jmot(X, MX) compute higher Chow groups with log support, and they satisfy a log version of the Beilinson–Soulé vanishing conjecture. This theory provides the motivic input for the log version of the Bloch–Kato conjecture and for the construction of log motivic L‑functions.

Log Tropical Geometry, Mirror Symmetry, and Enumerative Invariants

Tropical geometry studies degenerations of algebraic varieties via piecewise‑linear objects. In the logarithmic setting, a log smooth morphism f: X → S with a standard log point base gives a tropicalization map trop: Xan → Σ(X/S) where Σ(X/S) is the cone complex of the log structure. The log Gromov–Witten invariants of a log Calabi–Yau pair (X, D) are defined by integrating against the virtual fundamental class on the moduli stack of log stable maps; they coincide with the usual Gromov–Witten invariants when D is empty. The log mirror symmetry conjecture (Gross–Siebert) asserts that the scattering diagram constructed from the log tropical data of a mirror pair reproduces the log Gromov–Witten invariants via the broken line algorithm. The log tropical vertex formalism computes these invariants combinatorially, and the log theta functions provide a basis of the log coordinate ring of the mirror. Recent work of Abramovich–Chen–Gross–Siebert extends the correspondence to higher genus and to log stable maps with arbitrary contact orders, using the log obstruction theory of the moduli space. These enumerative invariants are expected to satisfy the log version of the holomorphic anomaly equation and to be governed by a log topological string partition function.

  • Assuming the log étale fundamental group is the same as the topological fundamental group of the Kato–Nakayama space without checking the fs condition on the log structure.
  • Neglecting the monodromy operator N in the Hyodo–Kato isomorphism; it is essential for matching the weight filtration.
  • Treating log prismatic cohomology as a mere variant of crystalline cohomology; the log Nygaard filtration and the log Breuil–Kisin classification are distinct new features.
  • Overlooking the role of the log structure on the Fargues–Fontaine curve when classifying vector bundles; the divisor at infinity carries the ramification data.
  • Applying ordinary motivic homotopy theory to log schemes without using log correspondences; the log Nisnevich topology is strictly finer.
  • Misidentifying the tropicalization map for a log smooth family; it lands in the cone complex, not in the usual tropical variety of the generic fiber.
