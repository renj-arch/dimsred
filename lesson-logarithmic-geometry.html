Logarithmic Geometry — Log Structures, Degenerations & the Monster

Logarithmic geometry, developed by Fontaine, Illusie, and Kato in the 1980s–90s, is a far-reaching refinement of scheme theory designed to handle degenerations, singularities, and non-smooth phenomena as if they were smooth. The central object is a log structure on a scheme X: a sheaf of commutative monoids MX together with a homomorphism α: MX → (OX, ·) such that α−1(OX*) ≅ OX* via the natural inclusion. This extra monoidal data records combinatorial information about boundaries, divisors, and normal crossings — turning a family that degenerates along a normal crossing divisor into a log smooth morphism, the logarithmic analogue of a smooth map. The theory encompasses log étale cohomology, log de Rham cohomology (differentials with logarithmic poles), log crystalline cohomology (via the Hyodo–Kato construction), and a full log Hodge theory that extends classical Hodge-theoretic results to open and degenerate varieties via the Kato–Nakayama space. Applications range from semistable reduction and the weight–monodromy conjecture to p-adic cohomology and the arithmetic Langlands program (Fargues–Scholze). Logarithmic geometry is, in Kato's words, "the art of turning degenerations into smooth maps."

Part 1

📏 Log Structures and Monoids

Foundation
Pre-Log Structures, Log Structures, and the Category of Monoids
Let X be a scheme. A pre-log structure on X is a pair (M, α) where M is a sheaf of commutative monoids on the étale site Xét and α: M → OX is a homomorphism of monoids (OX under multiplication). A log structure is a pre-log structure such that the induced map α−1(OX*) → OX* is an isomorphism. Every pre-log structure admits an associated log structure obtained by pushing out α−1(OX*) ← α−1(OX*) ∩ M → M along the inclusion into OX*. The trivial log structure is (OX*, ι). The divisorial log structure from a divisor D ⊂ X with complement U = X \ D is MX = OX ∩ j*OU* where j: U ↪ X. The standard log point is Spec k with the log structure ℕ → k sending 1 ↦ 0 — the simplest non-trivial log scheme. A monoid in log geometry is always commutative and written multiplicatively. It is integral if the map M → Mgp (to its Grothendieck group) is injective; saturated if it is integral and for every m ∈ Mgp with mn ∈ M for some n ≥ 1, one has m ∈ M; and fine (fs) if it is integral, saturated, and finitely generated. The characteristic monoid M̅X = MX / OX* measures the "logarithmic" part of the structure — it is a sheaf of sharp monoids whose stalks encode the combinatorial data of the boundary.
A log structure enriches a scheme with a monoidal boundary; the quotient by units yields the characteristic monoid that captures the combinatorial essence of the degeneration.
Part 2

🌍 Log Smooth Morphisms and Log Étale Cohomology

Morphisms
Log Smoothness, Log Étale Maps, Log de Rham and Log Étale Cohomology
A morphism f: (X, MX) → (Y, MY) of log schemes is log smooth (resp. log étale) if it is locally of finite presentation and satisfies the logarithmic analogue of the infinitesimal lifting criterion for smooth (resp. étale) morphisms. Concretely, for a log smooth morphism, the log cotangent complex is a vector bundle concentrated in degree 0. The sheaf of logarithmic differentials Ω1X/Y(log) is defined as the OX-module generated by the usual Kähler differentials and the logarithmic derivations dlog(m) for m ∈ MX, where dlog(m) = m−1 dm. The log de Rham complex Ω•X/Y(log) = ∧• Ω1X/Y(log) gives rise to log de Rham cohomology H•dR(X/Y, log). For a smooth divisor D ⊂ X, this recovers the classical de Rham complex with logarithmic poles along D. There is a log Hodge-to-de Rham spectral sequence Ep,q1 = Hq(X, ΩpX/Y(log)) ⇒ Hp+qdR(X/Y, log). The log étale topology is the Grothendieck topology on fs log schemes where coverings are Kummer log étale — i.e., log étale and inducing finite extensions of the characteristic monoid stalks. Log étale cohomology H•log-ét(X, F) is defined with coefficients in sheaves of ℓ-adic or ℤp-modules, using the Kummer flat topology (Kummer-flat). A log geometric point is a morphism Spec k → X of log schemes where k is algebraically closed and the log structure on Spec k is fs. These provide the fiber functors for log étale fundamental groups.
Log smooth morphisms extend the notion of smoothness to degenerating families; log de Rham and log étale cohomology provide cohomological invariants that are well-behaved under degeneration.
Part 3

🧮 Semistable Reduction and Degenerations

Degenerations
Semistable Reduction, Vanishing Cycles, Weight–Monodromy, and Log Hodge Theory
The semistable reduction theorem (Grothendieck–Mumford, proved via alterations by de Jong): given a family f: X → S over a DVR with normal crossing degenerations, after a finite base change the family becomes semistable — i.e., the special fiber is a reduced normal crossing divisor. Log geometry provides the natural language: a semistable morphism is exactly a log smooth morphism for the standard log structure on the base. The vanishing cycle complex in the log setting RΨ(ℚℓ) sits in a distinguished triangle relating the log étale cohomology of the generic and special fibers. The Rapoport–Zink spectral sequence Ep,q2 = Hp(Xs, RqΨ(ℚℓ)) ⇒ Hp+q(Xη̅, ℚℓ) is the log-geometric analogue of the Leray spectral sequence for a degeneration. The Clemens–Schmid exact sequence describes the monodromy action on the cohomology of the generic fiber in terms of the special fiber's log cohomology. The weight–monodromy conjecture (proved for semistable varieties by Saito, and in general by Scholze) states that the monodromy filtration on the ℓ-adic cohomology of the generic fiber coincides with the weight filtration. The Kato–Nakayama space Xlog associated to a log analytic space (X, MX) is a topological space (with a C∞ structure in the fs case) that provides a Betti realization: log Betti cohomology H•B(Xlog, ℚ) is isomorphic to log étale cohomology with ℚ coefficients under the comparison isomorphism, giving a full logarithmic Hodge theory (Kato–Nakayama, Ogus).
Semistable reduction turns arbitrary degenerations into log smooth families; log Hodge theory extends the classical Hodge package to open and singular varieties via the Kato–Nakayama topological realization.
Part 4

🔥 Log Crystalline Cohomology and Motivic Theory

p-adic
Log Crystalline Cohomology, Hyodo–Kato Isomorphism, and Logarithmic Motives
Log crystalline cohomology (Kato, Hyodo–Kato) is the crystalline cohomology of a log scheme (X, MX) over a log base (S, MS) with divided power structures. The key object is the log de Rham–Witt complex Wω•X, a logarithmic variant of the de Rham–Witt complex, whose hypercohomology computes the log crystalline cohomology of X. For a semistable variety X over a p-adic field with special fiber Y over the residue field, the Hyodo–Kato isomorphism identifies the log crystalline cohomology of Y (with its canonical log structure) with the rigid cohomology of the generic fiber, after adjoining a period element. The monodromy operator N = log(Tu) (where T is the Frobenius action) acts nilpotently and defines the monodromy filtration. Log syntomic cohomology Sn(r)X = fiber of (Wnω•X → φpWnω•X shifted) interpolates between étale and crystalline cohomology and is central to p-adic comparison theorems (Tsuji, Faltings, Beilinson). The Kato filtration on log de Rham cohomology refines the monodromy filtration and appears in the log Cartier isomorphism C−1: ΩiXs/k(log) → ℋi(Wω•X). The weight–monodromy conjecture for semistable varieties (proved by Saito using microlocal methods, and by Scholze via perfectoid techniques) asserts that the monodromy filtration on ℓ-adic cohomology equals the weight filtration. In the arithmetic Langlands program, log geometry is indispensable: the Fargues–Scholze geometric Langlands correspondence uses log geometry on the stack of bundles on the Fargues–Fontaine curve. Finally, logarithmic motives (Binda–Park–Østvær) provide a log version of Voevodsky's triangulated category of mixed motives, complete with a log étale realization and a log motivic t-structure.
Log crystalline cohomology and the Hyodo–Kato isomorphism are the p-adic workhorses for degenerating families; logarithmic motives extend the motivic formalism to open and singular varieties.
