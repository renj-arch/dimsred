Computational Mathematics — Numerical Linear Algebra, Finite Elements & Krylov Methods

Computational mathematics is the study of numerical methods for solving mathematical problems on a computer. Finite precision arithmetic introduces roundoff errors, and backward error analysis (pioneered by Wilkinson) provides a framework: the computed solution to a problem is the exact solution to a nearby problem. Numerical linear algebra concerns matrix factorizations — the LU decomposition (A = P·L·U), the Cholesky decomposition (A = LL^T for symmetric positive definite matrices), the QR decomposition (A = QR via Householder reflections or Givens rotations), and the singular value decomposition (SVD: A = U·Σ·V^T). Direct methods (Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting) solve linear systems in O(n³) operations; iterative methods (Jacobi, Gauss-Seidel, SOR, and the conjugate gradient method) are preferred for large sparse systems. Krylov subspace methods (GMRES, MINRES, BiCG-STAB) generalize CG to non-symmetric matrices using Arnoldi and Lanczos orthogonalization. Eigenvalue computations use the QR algorithm (the Francis double shift) and the power iteration. For PDEs, finite difference methods approximate derivatives by difference quotients, and the Lax-Richtmyer equivalence theorem ensures convergence from consistency and stability. The finite element method solves the weak (variational) formulation of a PDE via the Galerkin method on a finite-dimensional subspace, with error estimates controlled by Céa's lemma.

Part 1

📏 Numerical Linear Algebra and Matrix Factorizations

Factorizations
LU, Cholesky, QR, SVD, and Backward Error Analysis
The LU decomposition factors A = P·L·U where P is a permutation matrix, L is unit lower triangular, and U is upper triangular. Partial pivoting (row swaps) ensures |ℓ_ij| ≤ 1 and avoids small pivots; complete pivoting also swaps columns but is rarely needed. The Cholesky decomposition A = LL^T exists uniquely for symmetric positive definite matrices and costs O(n³/3) flops. The QR decomposition A = QR with Q orthogonal (Q^T Q = I) and R upper triangular is computed by Householder reflections (reflectors: H = I − 2vv^T/||v||²) or Givens rotations (plane rotations to zero individual entries). The singular value decomposition A = U·Σ·V^T = Σ σ_i u_i v_i^T gives the singular values σ₁ ≥ ... ≥ σ_r > 0, the left singular vectors u_i, and the right singular vectors v_i. The condition number κ(A) = ||A||·||A⁻¹|| = σ_max/σ_min — large κ indicates ill-conditioning. Backward error: the computed solution ̃x to Ax = b satisfies (A+δA)̃x = b for some δA with ||δA|| ≤ ε·||A|| where ε is machine epsilon. The Schur complement and the Banachiewicz inversion formula give block decompositions: for a 2×2 block matrix, the Schur complement S = D − C·A⁻¹·B is fundamental to domain decomposition and multifrontal methods.
Matrix factorizations (LU, Cholesky, QR, SVD) are the workhorses of numerical linear algebra; backward error analysis justifies why computed solutions are reliable despite roundoff.
Part 2

🌍 Iterative Methods and Krylov Subspaces

Iterations
Stationary Iterations, Conjugate Gradient, GMRES, and Preconditioning
The Richardson iteration x_{k+1} = x_k + ω(b − Ax_k) converges if ρ(I − ωA) < 1. The Jacobi method: x^{k+1}_i = (b_i − Σ_{j≠i} a_{ij} x^k_j)/a_{ii}. The Gauss-Seidel method uses the most recently updated components. Successive over-relaxation (SOR) applies an extrapolation parameter ω: the optimal ω = 2/(1+√(1−ρ(J)²)) where ρ(J) is the spectral radius of the Jacobi iteration matrix. The conjugate gradient method (Hestenes-Stiefel, 1952) for symmetric positive definite A minimizes the energy norm ||x − x_k||_A = √((x−x_k)^T A (x−x_k)) over the Krylov subspace K_k(A, r₀) = span{r₀, Ar₀, ..., A^{k−1}r₀}. The algorithm generates A-orthogonal search directions p_k so that p_i^T A p_j = 0 for i ≠ j. The GMRES (Saad-Schultz, 1986) works for non-symmetric matrices: it minimizes ||b − Ax_k||₂ over the Krylov subspace using Arnoldi orthogonalization (the Arnoldi process builds an orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspace via the modified Gram-Schmidt). MINRES is for symmetric but indefinite matrices, and BiCG-STAB (van der Vorst, 1992) is a stabilized biconjugate gradient method. Preconditioning solves M⁻¹Ax = M⁻¹b where M ≈ A — the Jacobi preconditioner (M = diag(A)), incomplete LU (ILU) factorization, and SSOR preconditioner are common choices. The convergence rate of CG depends on the condition number κ(A): ||x−x_k||_A ≤ 2( (√κ−1)/(√κ+1) )^k ||x−x₀||_A.
Krylov subspace methods efficiently solve large sparse linear systems by building approximations from the subspace spanned by repeated applications of A to the residual.
Part 3

🧮 Eigenvalue Computation and the QR Algorithm

Eigenvalues
Power Iteration, QR Algorithm, Francis Double Shift, and Divide-and-Conquer
The power iteration v_{k+1} = Av_k/||Av_k|| converges linearly to the eigenvector corresponding to the dominant eigenvalue (λ₁ with |λ₁| > |λ₂|) at rate |λ₂/λ₁|^k. The Rayleigh quotient μ_k = v_k^T A v_k / v_k^T v_k gives the eigenvalue estimate. Inverse iteration solves (A−μI)w = v_k to accelerate convergence to the eigenvector closest to a shift μ. The QR algorithm (Francis, 1961): set A_0 = A; for k = 0,1,... compute A_k = Q_k R_k (QR factorization) then A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k. Without shifts, A_k converges to a triangular matrix revealing eigenvalues on the diagonal. The implicit double shift (the Francis QR step) applies two shifts simultaneously (a pair of complex conjugate shifts) to keep the computation real for real matrices — it's the foundation of the modern QR algorithm. The divide-and-conquer algorithm for symmetric tridiagonal matrices recursively partitions and solves rank-1 updates using the secular equation. The Jacobi method for symmetric matrices applies plane rotations to eliminate off-diagonal elements, converging to a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. The bidiagonal SVD algorithm (Golub-Kahan-Lanczos) reduces A to bidiagonal form via Householder reflections, then iteratively applies QR-like steps to compute the SVD. The Lanczos iteration is the symmetric reduction to tridiagonal form, while the Arnoldi iteration is the non-symmetric reduction to upper Hessenberg form — both are central to large-scale eigenvalue computation.
Eigenvalue algorithms — from the simple power iteration to the sophisticated QR algorithm with Francis shifts — form the backbone of spectral computations in science and engineering.
Part 4

🔥 Finite Differences and Finite Elements

PDEs
Finite Difference Methods, Stability, and the Finite Element Method
The finite difference method approximates derivatives by difference quotients: ∂²u/∂x² ≈ (u_{i+1}−2u_i+u_{i−1})/Δx² with truncation error O(Δx²) for central differences. For the heat equation u_t = u_{xx}, the forward Euler scheme u^{n+1}_i = u^n_i + Δt/Δx² (u^n_{i+1}−2u^n_i+u^n_{i−1}) is conditionally stable: Δt/Δx² ≤ 1/2. The Lax-Richtmyer equivalence theorem: a consistent finite difference scheme is convergent iff it is stable. For hyperbolic PDEs, the CFL condition requires Δt ≤ Δx/|c| where c is the wave speed. Von Neumann stability analysis substitutes the Fourier mode u^n_j = ξ^n e^{ijθ} and examines the amplification factor |ξ(θ)|. The finite element method (FEM) reformulates the PDE in weak (variational) form: find u ∈ H¹₀(Ω) such that ∫Ω ∇u·∇v = ∫Ω f·v for all test functions v ∈ H¹₀(Ω). The Galerkin method restricts to a finite-dimensional subspace V_h ⊂ H¹₀(Ω) spanned by piecewise linear basis functions on a triangulation. Céa's lemma: the finite element error is optimal up to the condition number — ||u−u_h||_E ≤ C·h^p·|u|_{H^{p+1}} where p is the polynomial degree and h is the mesh size. The stiffness matrix K_ij = ∫Ω ∇φ_i·∇φ_j and the mass matrix M_ij = ∫Ω φ_i·φ_j. The h-adaptivity refines the mesh locally (reducing h), while p-refinement increases the polynomial degree of the basis functions. The inf-sup (Ladyzhenskaya-Babuška-Brezzi) condition governs stability of mixed finite element methods for saddle-point problems (e.g., Stokes flow).
Finite differences provide simplicity and geometric flexibility on structured grids; finite elements excel on complex geometries with rigorous error control via Céa's lemma and adaptivity.
