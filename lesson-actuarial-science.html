Actuarial Science — Life Contingencies, Survival Models & Risk Theory

Actuarial science is the discipline that applies mathematical and statistical methods to assess risk in insurance, finance, and other industries. At its core is the survival model: a probability distribution for the random future lifetime of an individual. The life table — l_x (number alive at age x), d_x (deaths), q_x (mortality rate) — is the classical tool. The force of mortality μ(x) = −S'(x)/S(x), where S(x) = P[X > x] is the survival function, generalizes the hazard rate. The Gompertz law μ(x) = Bc^x models exponentially increasing mortality; Makeham adds a constant A. The future lifetime T_x has survival function tₚ_x = P[T_x > t] = S(x+t)/S(x). Life contingencies — whole life insurance (payable at death), term insurance, endowment insurance, and life annuities — are priced via the equivalence principle: the net single premium equals the expected present value of benefits. The Cramér-Lundberg model describes the insurer's surplus as a compound Poisson process, and ruin theory studies the probability that surplus ever becomes negative. The adjustment coefficient R > 0 yields Lundberg's inequality ψ(u) ≤ e^{−R·u} for the ultimate ruin probability. The Gerber-Shiu function provides a unified framework for analyzing ruin-related quantities.

Part 1

📏 Survival Models and the Life Table

Foundations
The Survival Function, Force of Mortality, and the Life Table
The survival function for a newborn is S(x) = P[X > x], where X is the age at death. The force of mortality (hazard rate) at age x is μ(x) = −S'(x)/S(x) = lim_{dx→0} P[x < X ≤ x+dx | X > x]/dx. The life table records l_x (number alive at exact age x from an initial cohort l_0), d_x = l_x − l_{x+1} (deaths between x and x+1), q_x = d_x/l_x (probability of death within a year), and p_x = 1−q_x (probability of survival). The Gompertz law μ(x) = Bc^x with B > 0, c > 1 captures the exponential increase in mortality with age. The Makeham law μ(x) = A + Bc^x adds a constant A representing age-independent accident hazard. The future lifetime of a life aged x is T_x with survival function tₚ_x = P[T_x > t] = S(x+t)/S(x). The complete expectation of life e̊_x = E[T_x] = ∫_0^∞ tₚ_x dt. The median future lifetime is the value m such that mₚ_x = 1/2. The Kaplan-Meier estimator provides a non-parametric estimate of the survival function from censored data.
Survival models describe the distribution of remaining lifetime; the force of mortality μ(x) and the life table are the foundational tools for quantifying and pricing mortality risk.
Part 2

🌍 Life Insurance and Annuities

Pricing
Net Single Premiums, Annuities, and the Equivalence Principle
The net single premium (NSP) is the expected present value of a policy's benefits. For a whole life insurance of $1 payable immediately at death: ̄A_x = ∫_0^∞ v^t tₚ_x μ(x+t) dt where v = 1/(1+i) is the discount factor at interest rate i. The n-year term insurance pays only if death occurs within n years: ̄A_{x:̄n|}^1 = ∫_0^n v^t tₚ_x μ(x+t) dt. The n-year pure endowment pays $1 if the insured survives n years: ̄A_{x:̄n|}^1 = v^n nₚ_x. The n-year endowment insurance combines both: ̄A_{x:̄n|} = ̄A_{x:̄n|}^1 + ̄A_{x:̄n|}^1. Life annuities are series of payments: annuity-immediate a_x = Σ_{k=1}^∞ v^k kₚ_x (payments at year end), annuity-due ä_x = Σ_{k=0}^∞ v^k kₚ_x (payments at year start). The equivalence principle sets the net premium P so that the expected present value of premiums equals the expected present value of benefits. For a whole life policy with annual premiums: P = ̄A_x / ä_x. The benefit reserve at time t (the prospective reserve) is tV = expected present value of future benefits − expected present value of future premiums. The retrospective reserve accumulates past premiums minus benefits.
Life insurance premiums are computed by equating expected present values of benefits and premiums via the equivalence principle; reserves represent the insurer's liability at any time.
Part 3

🧮 Multiple Decrements and Joint Life Models

Decrements
Multiple Decrement Tables, Associated Single Decrement Tables, and Joint Life Status
A multiple decrement model tracks exits from a population due to several competing causes (death, disability, withdrawal). The total survivors at age x are l_x^{(τ)}. The decrements due to cause j between x and x+1 are d_x^{(j)}. The associated single decrement table gives q_x^{'(j)} — the probability of decrement from cause j if no other causes operated. The relationship between dependent probabilities q_x^{(j)} (in the presence of other causes) and independent probabilities q_x^{'(j)} (in isolation) is given by the independent vs dependent formulas: q_x^{'(j)} = −ln(1−q_x^{(τ)}) · (d_x^{(j)}/d_x^{(τ)}) under the assumption of uniform distribution of decrements. Joint life status: the future lifetime of (x) and (y) both alive is T_{xy} = min(T_x, T_y) with survival function tₚ_{xy} = tₚ_x · tₚ_y assuming independence. The last survivor status T_{̄xy|} = max(T_x, T_y) has survival function tₚ_{̄xy|} = tₚ_x + tₚ_y − tₚ_{xy}. A contingent insurance pays on the death of (x) provided (y) is then alive. A reversionary annuity pays to (y) after the death of (x).
Multiple decrement models handle competing risks; joint life models extend single-life contingencies to two lives, essential for pricing products on multiple insureds.
Part 4

🔥 Risk Theory and Ruin Probability

Risk
Collective Risk Models, the Cramér-Lundberg Process, and Gerber-Shiu Functions
The collective risk model describes aggregate claims S = Σ_{i=1}^N X_i where N is the number of claims (Poisson(λ)) and X_i are i.i.d. claim amounts with distribution P. The Cramér-Lundberg model defines the surplus process U(t) = u + c·t − S(t) where u is the initial surplus, c is the premium rate per unit time, and S(t) is the compound Poisson process of aggregate claims up to time t. The probability of ultimate ruin ψ(u) = P[U(t) < 0 for some t ≥ 0] — the probability the surplus ever becomes negative. The adjustment coefficient R > 0 solves the equation λ + c·R = λ·M_X(R) where M_X(R) = E[e^{R·X}] is the moment generating function of the claim size. The Lundberg inequality gives an exponential upper bound: ψ(u) ≤ e^{−R·u}. The Gerber-Shiu expected discounted penalty function φ(u) = E[e^{−δ·T} w(U(T−), |U(T)|) I(T < ∞)] unifies analyses of the time of ruin T, the deficit at ruin |U(T)|, and the surplus before ruin U(T−). When δ = 0 and w ≡ 1, φ(u) reduces to the ruin probability ψ(u).
Risk theory models the insurer's surplus as a stochastic process; the adjustment coefficient and Lundberg's inequality provide practical bounds on ruin probabilities.
