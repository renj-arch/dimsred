Actuarial Science — Life Contingencies, Survival Models & Risk Theory

Actuarial science is the discipline that applies mathematical and statistical methods to assess risk in insurance, finance, and other industries. At its core is the survival model: a probability distribution for the random future lifetime of an individual. The life table — l_x (number alive at age x), d_x (deaths), q_x (mortality rate) — is the classical tool. The force of mortality μ(x) = −S'(x)/S(x), where S(x) = P[X > x] is the survival function, generalizes the hazard rate. The Gompertz law μ(x) = Bc^x models exponentially increasing mortality; Makeham adds a constant A. The future lifetime T_x has survival function tₚ_x = P[T_x > t] = S(x+t)/S(x). Life contingencies — whole life insurance (payable at death), term insurance, endowment insurance, and life annuities — are priced via the equivalence principle: the net single premium equals the expected present value of benefits. The Cramér-Lundberg model describes the insurer's surplus as a compound Poisson process, and ruin theory studies the probability that surplus ever becomes negative. The adjustment coefficient R > 0 yields Lundberg's inequality ψ(u) ≤ e^{−R·u} for the ultimate ruin probability. The Gerber-Shiu function provides a unified framework for analyzing ruin-related quantities.

Part 1

📏 Survival Models and the Life Table

Foundations
The Survival Function, Force of Mortality, and the Life Table
The survival function for a newborn is S(x) = P[X > x], where X is the age at death. The force of mortality (hazard rate) at age x is μ(x) = −S'(x)/S(x) = lim_{dx→0} P[x < X ≤ x+dx | X > x]/dx. The life table records l_x (number alive at exact age x from an initial cohort l_0), d_x = l_x − l_{x+1} (deaths between x and x+1), q_x = d_x/l_x (probability of death within a year), and p_x = 1−q_x (probability of survival). The Gompertz law μ(x) = Bc^x with B > 0, c > 1 captures the exponential increase in mortality with age. The Makeham law μ(x) = A + Bc^x adds a constant A representing age-independent accident hazard. The future lifetime of a life aged x is T_x with survival function tₚ_x = P[T_x > t] = S(x+t)/S(x). The complete expectation of life e̊_x = E[T_x] = ∫_0^∞ tₚ_x dt. The median future lifetime is the value m such that mₚ_x = 1/2. The Kaplan-Meier estimator provides a non-parametric estimate of the survival function from censored data.
Survival models describe the distribution of remaining lifetime; the force of mortality μ(x) and the life table are the foundational tools for quantifying and pricing mortality risk.
Part 2

🌍 Life Insurance and Annuities

Pricing
Net Single Premiums, Annuities, and the Equivalence Principle
The net single premium (NSP) is the expected present value of a policy's benefits. For a whole life insurance of $1 payable immediately at death: ̄A_x = ∫_0^∞ v^t tₚ_x μ(x+t) dt where v = 1/(1+i) is the discount factor at interest rate i. The n-year term insurance pays only if death occurs within n years: ̄A_{x:̄n|}^1 = ∫_0^n v^t tₚ_x μ(x+t) dt. The n-year pure endowment pays $1 if the insured survives n years: ̄A_{x:̄n|}^1 = v^n nₚ_x. The n-year endowment insurance combines both: ̄A_{x:̄n|} = ̄A_{x:̄n|}^1 + ̄A_{x:̄n|}^1. Life annuities are series of payments: annuity-immediate a_x = Σ_{k=1}^∞ v^k kₚ_x (payments at year end), annuity-due ä_x = Σ_{k=0}^∞ v^k kₚ_x (payments at year start). The equivalence principle sets the net premium P so that the expected present value of premiums equals the expected present value of benefits. For a whole life policy with annual premiums: P = ̄A_x / ä_x. The benefit reserve at time t (the prospective reserve) is tV = expected present value of future benefits − expected present value of future premiums. The retrospective reserve accumulates past premiums minus benefits.
Life insurance premiums are computed by equating expected present values of benefits and premiums via the equivalence principle; reserves represent the insurer's liability at any time.
Part 3

🧮 Multiple Decrements and Joint Life Models

Decrements
Multiple Decrement Tables, Associated Single Decrement Tables, and Joint Life Status
A multiple decrement model tracks exits from a population due to several competing causes (death, disability, withdrawal). The total survivors at age x are l_x^{(τ)}. The decrements due to cause j between x and x+1 are d_x^{(j)}. The associated single decrement table gives q_x^{'(j)} — the probability of decrement from cause j if no other causes operated. The relationship between dependent probabilities q_x^{(j)} (in the presence of other causes) and independent probabilities q_x^{'(j)} (in isolation) is given by the independent vs dependent formulas: q_x^{'(j)} = −ln(1−q_x^{(τ)}) · (d_x^{(j)}/d_x^{(τ)}) under the assumption of uniform distribution of decrements. Joint life status: the future lifetime of (x) and (y) both alive is T_{xy} = min(T_x, T_y) with survival function tₚ_{xy} = tₚ_x · tₚ_y assuming independence. The last survivor status T_{̄xy|} = max(T_x, T_y) has survival function tₚ_{̄xy|} = tₚ_x + tₚ_y − tₚ_{xy}. A contingent insurance pays on the death of (x) provided (y) is then alive. A reversionary annuity pays to (y) after the death of (x).
Multiple decrement models handle competing risks; joint life models extend single-life contingencies to two lives, essential for pricing products on multiple insureds.
Part 4

🔥 Risk Theory and Ruin Probability

Risk
Collective Risk Models, the Cramér-Lundberg Process, and Gerber-Shiu Functions
The collective risk model describes aggregate claims S = Σ_{i=1}^N X_i where N is the number of claims (Poisson(λ)) and X_i are i.i.d. claim amounts with distribution P. The Cramér-Lundberg model defines the surplus process U(t) = u + c·t − S(t) where u is the initial surplus, c is the premium rate per unit time, and S(t) is the compound Poisson process of aggregate claims up to time t. The probability of ultimate ruin ψ(u) = P[U(t) < 0 for some t ≥ 0] — the probability the surplus ever becomes negative. The adjustment coefficient R > 0 solves the equation λ + c·R = λ·M_X(R) where M_X(R) = E[e^{R·X}] is the moment generating function of the claim size. The Lundberg inequality gives an exponential upper bound: ψ(u) ≤ e^{−R·u}. The Gerber-Shiu expected discounted penalty function φ(u) = E[e^{−δ·T} w(U(T−), |U(T)|) I(T < ∞)] unifies analyses of the time of ruin T, the deficit at ruin |U(T)|, and the surplus before ruin U(T−). When δ = 0 and w ≡ 1, φ(u) reduces to the ruin probability ψ(u).
Risk theory models the insurer's surplus as a stochastic process; the adjustment coefficient and Lundberg's inequality provide practical bounds on ruin probabilities.

Advanced Topic 1: Select Mortality, Selectivity, and the Cornish-Fisher Expansion for Tail Risk

Standard life tables assume that mortality risk is determined solely by attained age x. However, in practice, individuals entering an insurance portfolio often have a different mortality profile than the general population at the same age. This phenomenon is known as Select Mortality. A policyholder who has just purchased life insurance (age x) typically exhibits lower mortality rates than a person of the same attained age who has held that policy for many years. The mortality experience is better described by a two-dimensional function μ(x, t), where x is the entry age and t is the duration since entry. Standard notation distinguishes between i_x(t) (select mortality at entry age x after t years) and s_x(t) (ultimate mortality at attained age x+t).

A critical advanced concept involves analyzing the tail of the distribution, specifically for extreme longevity or catastrophic failure events. While the Central Limit Theorem suggests normality for aggregate claims, individual survival times are heavily skewed. To estimate quantiles in the far right tail (e.g., the 99th percentile of future lifetime) where data is sparse, actuaries often employ the Cornish-Fisher Expansion. This technique adjusts the standard normal quantile z_p by skewness and kurtosis terms derived from the underlying survival distribution. For a random variable T_x with standardized cumulants κ1=0, κ2=1, κ3 (skewness), and κ4 (kurtosis), the adjusted quantile approximation is:

y_p ≈ z_p + (1/6)(z_p2 - 1)κ3 + (1/24)(z_p3 - 3z_p)κ4 - (1/36)(2z_p3 - 5z_p)κ32

This is vital for pricing long-term care insurance or calculating solvency capital requirements under regulatory frameworks like Solvency II, where the focus shifts from the mean to the Value-at-Risk (VaR). Textbook standard practice relies on this expansion when fitting heavy-tailed distributions like the Pareto or Log-Normal to mortality data.

Technique: Applying Select Mortality Logic
Question: A 35-year-old male purchases a whole life policy. The select mortality rate at duration 0 is q[35] = 0.001, while the ultimate rate for age 35 is q35 = 0.002. If he survives to duration 5, and selectivity fades such that i35(5) = s40, calculate the probability of survival to duration 5 given entry at 35, assuming constant ultimate rates between ages 35 and 40.
Solution: The probability of surviving from duration 0 to duration 5 is 5p_[35]. This path involves the select period. Typically, the select period is 2 or 3 years. Assuming a standard fade where after 5 years we are in the ultimate table at age 40 (since 35+5=40). The probability is calculated as: 5p_[35] = p_[35] × p_[36] × ... × p_[39]. Without specific select tables, we approximate using the given rates if uniform distribution is assumed. However, the core concept tested here is recognizing that q[x]+t < qx+t. The probability is strictly greater than what would be calculated using only ultimate rates for age 35.
Exam-Day Strategy: Reading the Select Table Notation
When encountering a question with double brackets [x] or triple brackets [[x]], immediately identify the duration t. If t is within the select period (usually 2-5 years), you must use the select table column. If t exceeds the select period, switch to the ultimate table at attained age x+t. Never mix columns without verifying the fade assumption stated in the problem.

Advanced Topic 2: Stochastic Interest Rates and The Variance of Loss at Issue

The previous sections assumed a deterministic interest rate i, leading to a constant discount factor v. In advanced risk theory, particularly for long-duration liabilities like annuities or whole life insurance, the assumption of a fixed interest rate is often relaxed. We must consider Stochastic Interest Rates. Let δ(t) be the random force of interest at time t. The present value random variable Z becomes a functional of the stochastic process r(t). For an n-year annuity-due, the present value is Y = Σk=0n-1 v_k K_x, where v_k depends on the path of interest rates.

A profound result in this domain is the decomposition of the variance of the loss at issue. Let L be the loss random variable. Under deterministic rates, Var(L) = E[Z2] - (E[Z])2. However, when interest rates are stochastic and independent of mortality, the variance increases significantly. The formula for the variance of the present value of a perpetuity under stochastic rates often involves integrating the covariance between discount factors and survival probabilities. A key landmark result is that if interest rates follow a Vasicek or Cox-Ingersoll-Ross model, the distribution of Z becomes non-normal with fat tails.

Consider the specific case where the loss variable L for a whole life policy is defined as L = vT_x - P * äT̄x. The variance of this variable under stochastic interest rates requires calculating E[(vT_x)2] which involves the second moment of the discount factor. This is distinct from the standard actuarial present value A_x which uses only the first moment. The relationship between the deterministic and stochastic variances highlights why insurers require larger capital buffers for long-tail liabilities.

Technique: Decomposing Variance with Stochastic Rates
Question: Given a whole life policy where interest rates are independent of mortality, show that the variance of the present value random variable Z is greater than the variance calculated under deterministic rates. Use the law of total variance Var(Z) = E[Var(Z|r)] + Var(E[Z|r]).
Solution: Let Z be the PV random variable. Under deterministic rates, r is constant. Under stochastic rates, r is a random variable R independent of T_x. By the Law of Total Variance: Var(Z) = E[Var(Z|R)] + Var(E[Z|R]). The term Var(Z|R) is the variance under a fixed rate R. The term Var(E[Z|R]) represents the additional uncertainty due to the randomness of the interest rate itself. Since Var(Z|R) ≥ 0 and assuming rates vary, Var(E[Z|R]) > 0. Therefore, the total stochastic variance exceeds the deterministic variance (which corresponds to E[Var(Z|R)] if we averaged over a fixed rate). This proves that ignoring interest rate volatility underestimates risk.
Exam-Day Strategy: Independence Assumptions
In almost all standard actuarial exams, unless explicitly stated otherwise, mortality and interest are assumed independent. If a question introduces dependence (e.g., "interest rates fall when longevity improves"), this is a trap for advanced topics like dynamic hedging. Always check the independence clause first before applying standard variance formulas.

Advanced Topic 3: The Cramér-Lundberg Model with Heavy-Tailed Claims and Adjustment Coefficient Properties

The Cramér-Lundberg model approximates the surplus process U(t) = u + ct - S(t). While the exponential upper bound ψ(u) ≤ e-Ru is standard, advanced analysis requires understanding the properties of the adjustment coefficient R. The equation λ + cR = λM_X(R) defines R implicitly. If the claim size distribution X has a heavy tail (e.g., Pareto or Log-Normal), the moment generating function M_X(R) may not exist for all R > 0. In such cases, the classical adjustment coefficient R might not exist, and one must rely on the Large Deviation Principle using rate functions from Cramér's Theorem.

A critical exception occurs when the premium principle is not linear (e.g., safety loading is applied differently). If the premium rate c is insufficient to satisfy the net profit condition (c > λE[X]), ruin is certain (ψ(u) = 1). However, even if the net profit condition holds, heavy-tailed claims can make the ruin probability decay much slower than exponentially. The "Lundberg Inequality" is tightest for light-tailed distributions. For heavy tails, the decay of ψ(u) is polynomial rather than exponential.

We must also consider the Gerber-Shiu function φ(u, d) = E[e-δT I(T<∞) w(U(T-), |U(T)|)]. This function generalizes ruin probability by incorporating a penalty w at the moment of ruin. If w(x,y) = 1, it reduces to the standard ruin probability. Advanced questions may ask for the sensitivity of φ with respect to the initial surplus u or the claim severity parameter α in a Pareto distribution.

Technique: Analyzing Heavy-Tailed Claims
Question: For a compound Poisson process where claims X follow a Pareto distribution with parameters α and θ, determine the condition under which the adjustment coefficient R exists. Explain why the exponential bound fails for very large u if α ≤ 1.
Solution: The MGF of a Pareto distribution exists only if we evaluate it at arguments less than a certain threshold related to α.
