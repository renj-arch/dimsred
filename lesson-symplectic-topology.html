Symplectic Topology — Symplectomorphisms, Floer Homology & Contact Geometry

Symplectic topology is the study of symplectic manifolds — even-dimensional smooth manifolds equipped with a closed non-degenerate 2-form ω. Unlike Riemannian geometry, symplectic geometry has no local invariants (Darboux's theorem), giving it a distinctly global, topological flavor. The field exploded with Gromov's introduction of pseudoholomorphic curves, leading to Floer homology (awarded the 2011 Abel Prize), Gromov-Witten invariants, and deep connections to contact geometry, mirror symmetry, and low-dimensional topology.

"Symplectic geometry is the geometry of classical phase space."
— Vladimir Arnold
Part 1

📏 Symplectic Manifolds and Darboux's Theorem

Symplectic Forms
Non-Degenerate Closed 2-Forms and Local Normal Form
A symplectic manifold (M, ω) is a 2n-dimensional smooth manifold with a closed 2-form ω (dω = 0) that is non-degenerate: ω^n ≠ 0 everywhere. This gives an isomorphism T_xM ≅ T_x^*M via v ↦ ω(v, ·). Classical phase space T^*Q (cotangent bundle) has the canonical symplectic form ω = Σ dq_i ∧ dp_i. Darboux's theorem — the central local result — states that every symplectic manifold is locally symplectomorphic to (ℝ^{2n}, Σ dx_i ∧ dy_i). Unlike Riemannian geometry, there are no local symplectic invariants; all symplectic manifolds look the same locally. Moser's stability theorem says that if ω_t is a smooth family of symplectic forms with constant cohomology class on a closed manifold, then there is an isotopy φ_t such that φ_t^*ω_t = ω_0. This implies that symplectic structures are determined up to deformation by their cohomology class. A symplectomorphism is a diffeomorphism f: M → N such that f^*ω_N = ω_M. The group Symp(M, ω) of symplectomorphisms is infinite-dimensional and plays the role of the symmetry group in Hamiltonian mechanics.
Darboux's theorem says symplectic geometry is purely global — locally every symplectic manifold is just ℝ^{2n} with the standard form. The real action happens at the global scale.
Lagrangians
Lagrangian Submanifolds and Their Neighborhoods
A Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ (M^{2n}, ω) is an n-dimensional submanifold with ω|_L = 0, i.e., ω vanishes identically on L. Examples: the zero section of T^*Q (a Lagrangian), fibers of T^*Q, and graphs of closed 1-forms. Lagrangian submanifolds are the symplectic analogue of real Lagrangians in complex geometry. Weinstein's neighborhood theorem states that a neighborhood of a Lagrangian L is symplectomorphic to a neighborhood of the zero section in T^*L equipped with its canonical symplectic form. Thus all Lagrangians look alike near the submanifold. Lagrangian intersection theory studies how Lagrangians intersect after Hamiltonian deformations — a Lagrangian L and φ_H(L) intersect in at least as many points as the topology of L dictates (when transversely intersecting). This is the foundation of Floer homology for Lagrangians. The Maslov index associates an integer to loops of Lagrangians, grading the Floer complex. A Lagrangian is monotone if its symplectic area and Maslov index are proportional — a condition ensuring well-defined Floer theory.
Lagrangian submanifolds are half-dimensional "geometric branes" on which the symplectic form vanishes. Their intersections, counted with Floer homology, reveal the global topology of the ambient symplectic manifold.
Part 2

🌍 Pseudoholomorphic Curves and Gromov's Non-squeezing

J-Holomorphic Curves
Gromov's Pseudoholomorphic Curves and Moduli Spaces
Gromov's revolutionary idea (1985): given an almost complex structure J on M that is tame (ω(·, J·) > 0), study maps u: Σ → M from a Riemann surface (Σ, j) such that du ∘ j = J ∘ du — the Cauchy-Riemann equation for pseudoholomorphic curves. These are nonlinear elliptic PDEs whose solutions are called J-holomorphic curves. The moduli space M(β, J) of all such curves representing a homology class β ∈ H₂(M; ℤ) has expected dimension given by the Fredholm index: dim = n χ(Σ) + 2 c₁(β). For generic J, the moduli space is a smooth manifold (transversality) of the expected dimension. Gromov compactness says that sequences of J-holomorphic curves have convergent subsequences in the sense of stable maps — nodal curves with bubbles. This compactness is the foundational analytic result that makes the whole theory work, analogous to convergence of sequences of holomorphic maps.
Pseudoholomorphic curves are the symplectic topologist's analytic microscope — they allow us to use PDE methods to probe the global topology of symplectic manifolds.
Non-Squeezing
Gromov's Non-Squeezing Theorem and Symplectic Capacity
Gromov's non-squeezing theorem (1985): the ball B^{2n}(r) cannot be symplectically embedded into the cylinder Z^{2n}(R) = B^2(R) × ℝ^{2n-2} unless r ≤ R. This was the first truly global symplectic invariant. It shows that symplectic embeddings are much more rigid than volume-preserving embeddings (volume gives no obstruction since vol(B^{2n}(r)) < vol(Z^{2n}(R)) is always true for small r). The proof uses a J-holomorphic sphere through the center of the ball — its area gives the obstruction. This led to the notion of symplectic capacity: a function c on symplectic manifolds that is monotone with respect to symplectic embeddings and scales as c(λM) = λ² c(M). The Gromov width (the capacity from the non-squeezing theorem) is the first of many. Gromov-Witten invariants count pseudoholomorphic curves of given genus and homology class, and are the fundamental numerical invariants of symplectic manifolds. They satisfy deformation invariance and are used for enumerative geometry — counting curves through constraints. The Kontsevich formula for rational plane curves was proved using GW invariants.
The non-squeezing theorem revealed that symplectic geometry is not just "even-dimensional volume geometry" — it has its own intrinsic rigidity measured by symplectic capacity.
Part 3

🧮 Floer Homology and Arnold Conjecture

Floer Homology
Hamiltonian Floer Homology and the Action Functional
Hamiltonian Floer homology (Floer, 1988) is an invariant of a symplectic manifold (M, ω) together with a Hamiltonian H: S¹ × M → ℝ. It is the Morse theory of the action functional 𝒜_H(γ) = ∫_{S¹} γ^*λ - ∫_{S¹} H(t, γ(t)) dt on the free loop space LM, where λ is a chosen primitive of ω (when [ω]=0 in H²). Critical points of 𝒜_H are 1-periodic orbits of the Hamiltonian vector field X_H defined by ω(X_H, ·) = dH. The Floer equation is a perturbed Cauchy-Riemann equation for maps u: ℝ×S¹ → M: ∂_s u + J(t, u)(∂_t u - X_H(t, u)) = 0. Solutions connecting critical points (s → −∞) to critical points (s → +∞) define the Floer differential. The resulting homology HF_*(M) is independent of H and J (via continuation maps) and isomorphic to the singular homology of M (the Piunikhin-Salamon-Schwarz isomorphism). Transversality for the Floer equation requires generic perturbations or virtual techniques. Compactness follows from Gromov-Floer convergence — bubbling and breaking of connecting trajectories.
Floer homology is infinite-dimensional Morse theory applied to the loop space. It turns the problem of counting periodic orbits into a robust algebraic invariant.
Arnold Conjecture
Fixed Points of Hamiltonian Symplectomorphisms and Spectral Invariants
The Arnold conjecture (1965) states that a Hamiltonian symplectomorphism φ_H (the time-1 map of a Hamiltonian flow) on a closed symplectic manifold M has at least as many fixed points as the minimal number of critical points of a Morse function on M. For M with Betti numbers b_i, the lower bound is Σ b_i (the weak Arnold conjecture). Floer proved this for monotone manifolds, and it follows in full from the isomorphism HF_*(M) ≅ H_*(M; ℤ₂), since the Floer chain complex generators are precisely the fixed points. This was one of the greatest successes of symplectic topology. Spectral invariants (Oh, Schwarz, Viterbo) assign real numbers c(α, H) to each quantum cohomology class α, derived from the filtration of the Floer complex by action. These invariants satisfy the triangle inequality c(α, H#K) ≤ c(α, H) + c(α, K) and define the Hofer norm on the group of Hamiltonian symplectomorphisms: ‖φ_H‖ = inf {∫₀¹(max H_t − min H_t) dt : φ_H = φ_H}. Spectral invariants also detect the displacement energy of subsets — the minimal Hofer norm needed to displace a subset from itself — a powerful tool in symplectic geometry.
The Arnold conjecture, proved via Floer homology, shows that the fixed-point count of a Hamiltonian symplectomorphism is bounded below by the topology of the manifold — a triumph of algebraic invariants in dynamics.
Part 4

🔥 Contact Geometry and Symplectic Fillings

Contact Structures
Contact Manifolds, Reeb Fields, and Legendrian Submanifolds
A contact manifold (Y, ξ) is an odd-dimensional manifold Y²ⁿ⁺¹ with a contact structure ξ = ker α, where α is a 1-form such that α ∧ (dα)ⁿ ≠ 0 everywhere (maximal non-degeneracy). Contact geometry is the odd-dimensional cousin of symplectic geometry. The Darboux theorem for contact structures says locally all contact manifolds are contactomorphic to (ℝ²ⁿ⁺¹, dz − Σ y_i dx_i). The Reeb vector field R_α is uniquely defined by dα(R_α, ·) = 0 and α(R_α) = 1. Its flow preserves the contact form α but not the contact structure alone. A Legendrian submanifold L ⊂ Y is an n-dimensional submanifold tangent to ξ (i.e., α|_L = 0). Legendrian knots in ℝ³ (standard contact structure dz − y dx) are studied via their front projection (x, z) and Lagrangian projection (x, y). The Thurston-Bennequin invariant tb(L) and rotation number r(L) are the basic classical invariants of Legendrian knots. Contact structures split into two fundamentally different classes: overtwisted (containing an overtwisted disc — a disc whose boundary is Legendrian with tb = 0) and tight (all others). Eliashberg's classification (1989) states that overt twisted contact structures on closed 3-manifolds are classified by homotopy classes of oriented 2-plane fields — they are flexible. Tight contact structures are rigid and much harder to classify.
Contact geometry sits at the boundary of symplectic geometry — literally: contact manifolds arise as the boundaries of symplectic domains, and their classification reveals a deep rigidity/flexibility dichotomy.
Fillings and Contact Homology
Symplectic Fillings, Liouville Domains, and Contact Homology
A symplectic filling of a contact manifold (Y, ξ) is a symplectic manifold (W, ω) with boundary ∂W = Y such that ω induces the contact structure near the boundary (e.g., ω = dα). A Liouville domain (W, λ) is a compact manifold with boundary such that ω = dλ is symplectic and the Liouville vector field V (defined by ι_V ω = λ) points outward along ∂W. The boundary of a Liouville domain is a contact manifold with α = λ|_Y. A Weinstein manifold is a Liouville domain with a Morse function whose gradient (w.r.t. the Liouville metric) is gradient-like for V. Contact homology (Eliashberg-Givental-Hofer, 2000) is an invariant of contact manifolds defined via pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectization ℝ×Y. It is part of the larger Symplectic Field Theory (SFT) framework. The Weinstein conjecture (existence of a closed Reeb orbit on any closed contact manifold) was proved by Taubes (2007) using Seiberg-Witten theory. Overtwisted contact structures are not symplectically fillable (Eliashberg-Gromov) — a tightness theorem that uses pseudoholomorphic curves in the filling. Giroux's correspondence relates contact structures on 3-manifolds to open book decompositions, enabling the use of mapping class groups in contact topology. The classification of tight contact structures on lens spaces and torus bundles has been carried out by Honda, Etnyre, and Ozbagci.
The interplay between contact manifolds and their symplectic fillings is one of the richest areas of modern topology, linking Reeb dynamics, holomorphic curves, and 3-manifold topology.

Hamiltonian Dynamics and the Hofer Metric

The group of Hamiltonian symplectomorphisms Ham(M, ω) consists of time‑1 maps of Hamiltonian flows generated by smooth, time‑dependent functions Hₜ with zero mean (on closed manifolds) or compact support (on open ones). The Hofer norm of a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism φ is defined as the infimum of the L¹,∞ norm of generating Hamiltonians: ‖φ‖ₕ = inf{∫₀¹ (max Hₜ − min Hₜ) dt : φ = φᴴ}. This norm descends to a bi‑invariant metric dₕ(φ, ψ) = ‖φ⁻¹ψ‖ₕ on Ham(M, ω). The metric is non‑degenerate (Lalonde–McDuff, 1995) and induces a topology strictly finer than the C⁰ topology. A central invariant derived from the Hofer metric is the displacement energy e(U) of a subset U ⊂ M: the minimal Hofer norm needed to move U off itself by a Hamiltonian isotopy. Gromov’s non‑squeezing theorem implies e(B²ⁿ(r)) = πr² for a standard ball, while for a symplectic cylinder Z²ⁿ(R) = B²(R) × ℝ²ⁿ⁻² the displacement energy is infinite, reflecting the rigidity of symplectic embeddings. The Hofer metric also controls the growth of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms: the asymptotic Hofer norm ‖φᵏ‖ₕ/k converges to the stable norm, which is related to spectral invariants from Floer homology. In particular, the spectral invariant c([M], H) equals the action of the maximal fixed point and satisfies the triangle inequality, making it a quasi‑morphism on the universal cover of Ham(M, ω). These tools have resolved long‑standing questions such as the C⁰‑rigidity of Hamiltonian flows (Buhovsky–Humilière–Seyfaddini, 2012) and the existence of infinitely many periodic orbits for Hamiltonians on certain symplectic manifolds.

Symplectic Capacities and Embedding Problems

A symplectic capacity is a function c that assigns to each symplectic manifold (M, ω) a number c(M, ω) ∈ [0, ∞] satisfying three axioms: monotonicity (if there exists a symplectic embedding (M₁, ω₁) → (M₂, ω₂) then c(M₁) ≤ c(M₂)), conformality (c(M, λω) = λ c(M, ω) for λ > 0), and non‑triviality (c(B²ⁿ(1)) = c(Z²ⁿ(1)) = π). The first capacity, introduced by Gromov, is the Gromov width w_G(M) = sup{πr² : B²ⁿ(r) symplectically embeds into M}. Ekeland and Hofer constructed a sequence of capacities cₖᴱᴴ for convex domains in ℝ²ⁿ using the action functional on the loop space; these capacities are symplectic invariants and satisfy cₖᴱᴴ(B²ⁿ(R)) = πR² for all k, while cₖᴱᴴ(Z²ⁿ(R)) = ∞ for k ≥ 2. For an ellipsoid E(a₁,…,aₙ) = {∑ |zᵢ|²/aᵢ < 1} the Ekeland–Hofer capacities are the numbers π aᵢ arranged in non‑decreasing order with multiplicities. This explicit computation solves many embedding problems: an ellipsoid E(a₁,…,aₙ) embeds into a ball B²ⁿ(R) iff max aᵢ ≤ R, and into a polydisk P(r₁,…,rₙ) iff the sorted aᵢ are dominated by the sorted rᵢ. The notion of symplectic packing — placing disjoint equal balls into a given manifold — leads to the packing numbers pₖ(M) = sup{ k π r² / vol(M) : k disjoint balls of radius r embed }. For ℂℙⁿ the full packing stability was proved by Biran (1997) using symplectic blow‑ups and the Seidel representation. Capacities also detect the non‑existence of exact Lagrangian embeddings: if a Lagrangian L ⊂ ℝ²ⁿ is exact, then its displacement energy is zero, contradicting the non‑zero capacity of any neighbourhood. Thus capacities provide a bridge between rigid symplectic geometry and flexible topological constructions.

Computing the Gromov width of an ellipsoid
Let E(1,4) = { |z₁|² + |z₂|²/4 < 1 } ⊂ ℝ⁴ with the standard symplectic form. Determine the Gromov width w_G(E(1,4)).
The Gromov width is the supremum of πr² such that a ball B⁴(r) symplectically embeds into E(1,4). By the monotonicity and conformality of capacities, w_G(E) equals the smallest Ekeland–Hofer capacity of E. The capacities of E(1,4) are π·1, π·4, π·4, … (the sequence of aᵢ with multiplicities). The smallest is π·1 = π. Hence w_G(E(1,4)) = π. Equivalently, the largest ball that fits has radius 1, so π·1² = π.
Exam‑day strategy
When a problem asks for a symplectic embedding obstruction, first list the relevant capacities (Gromov width, Ekeland–Hofer, Hofer–Zehnder). Check monotonicity: if the target has a smaller capacity than the source, the embedding is impossible. For ellipsoids and polydisks, remember that the Ekeland–Hofer capacities are just the defining parameters with multiplicities; this makes comparisons a simple ordering exercise.

Lagrangian Intersection Theory and the Arnold Conjecture in Higher Dimensions

Two Lagrangian submanifolds L₀, L₁ in a symplectic manifold (M, ω) intersect generically in isolated points when they are transverse. The Arnold conjecture for Lagrangian intersections asserts that the number of intersection points is at least the sum of Betti numbers of L₀ (with coefficients in a suitable field) provided L₀ and L₁ are Hamiltonian isotopic. Floer’s original construction defined a chain complex CF*(L₀, L₁) generated by intersection points, with differential counting pseudoholomorphic strips with boundary on L₀ and L₁. The homology HF*(L₀, L₁) is invariant under Hamiltonian isotopy and, when L₀ = L₁, is isomorphic to the singular cohomology H*(L; Λ) where Λ is a Novikov ring accounting for area of discs. The proof of the Arnold conjecture for general Lagrangians required overcoming transversality issues for multiply covered curves; this was achieved by Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono (2009) using Kuranishi structures and virtual fundamental cycles. Their machinery also yields the spectral sequence from the pearl complex (Morse theory on L combined with holomorphic discs) to Floer homology, which computes the quantum cohomology of M when L is a Lagrangian sphere. A striking application is the non‑displaceability of the Clifford torus in ℂℙⁿ: its Floer homology is non‑zero, so it cannot be displaced by any Hamiltonian isotopy. In the monotone setting, the minimal Maslov number N_L controls the grading; if N_L ≥ 2 the Floer differential squares to zero without Novikov coefficients. The theory extends to immersed Lagrangians and to the wrapped Fukaya category of Liouville domains, where the endomorphisms of a Lagrangian are given by its wrapped Floer cohomology, linking symplectic topology to homological mirror symmetry.

Symplectic Field Theory and the Compactness Theorem

Symplectic Field Theory (SFT), introduced by Eliashberg–Givental–Hofer (2000), is a unified framework for Gromov–Witten invariants, contact homology, and Legendrian contact homology. The basic geometric object is a symplectic cobordism (W, ω) between contact manifolds (Y₋, ξ₋) and (Y₊, ξ₊). One considers pseudoholomorphic curves in the completion Ŵ = (−∞,0]×Y₋ ∪ W ∪ [0,∞)×Y₊ with asymptotics to Reeb orbits on the ends. The SFT compactness theorem (Bourgeois–Eliashberg–Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder, 2003) states that a sequence of such curves with bounded energy and genus has a subsequence converging to a holomorphic building — a collection of curves in the symplectizations ℝ×Y₋, ℝ×Y₊ and in W, joined at Reeb orbits. This generalizes Gromov’s compactness for closed curves and the compactness for curves with Legendrian boundary. The theorem is the analytical backbone of SFT: it ensures that the differential in the contact homology chain complex squares to zero, because the boundary of the 1‑dimensional moduli space consists of broken buildings corresponding to the differential applied twice. The compactness proof uses the Hofer energy bound, the monotonicity lemma for pseudoholomorphic curves, and a delicate analysis of neck‑stretching near Reeb orbits. Applications include the computation of contact homology of Brieskorn manifolds (Ustilovsky, 1999), the proof of the Weinstein conjecture for many contact manifolds via Taubes’s isomorphism with Seiberg–Witten Floer homology, and the definition of the rational SFT invariants of symplectic fillings. The compactness theorem also underlies the functoriality of SFT under exact symplectic cobordisms, leading to the notion of an SFT algebra and its relation to the wrapped Fukaya category of the filling.

Applications to Mirror Symmetry and Topological Strings

Mirror symmetry predicts an equivalence between the symplectic geometry of a Calabi–Yau manifold (M, ω) and the complex geometry of its mirror (M̌, J). The homological mirror symmetry conjecture (Kontsevich, 1994) states that the derived Fukaya category D^πFuk(M) is equivalent to the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves D^bCoh(M̌). The Fukaya category has objects given by Lagrangian submanifolds equipped with local systems, and morphism spaces given by Floer cohomology groups HF*(L₀, L₁). The A∞‑structure on this category encodes counts of pseudoholomorphic polygons with boundary on a sequence of Lagrangians. On the B‑side, the category of coherent sheaves is algebraic and accessible via sheaf cohomology. The SYZ conjecture (Strominger–Yau–Zaslow, 1996) provides a geometric construction: M should admit a special Lagrangian torus fibration, and M̌ is obtained by dualizing the fibres. This picture explains the exchange of symplectic and complex moduli and predicts that Gromov–Witten invariants of M (counts of pseudoholomorphic spheres) equal period integrals on M̌. In topological string theory, the A‑model topological string computes Gromov–Witten invariants, while the B‑model computes variations of Hodge structure. The open‑closed string map relates the Hochschild homology of the Fukaya category to quantum cohomology, and the closed‑open map relates quantum cohomology to the endomorphisms of the unit object. These structures have been rigorously established for toric Calabi–Yau manifolds (Chan–Lau–Leung–Tseng, 2011) and for the quintic threefold (Sheridan, 2015). The interplay between symplectic topology and mirror symmetry continues to drive advances in both fields, from the classification of Lagrangian tori to the computation of higher genus Gromov–Witten invariants via the holomorphic anomaly equations.

The advanced topics above illustrate how symplectic topology connects dynamics, rigidity, and algebraic structures across dimensions.

Quantum Homology and the Seidel Representation

Quantum homology QH_*(M,ω) is a deformation of the ordinary singular homology of a closed symplectic manifold (M,ω) obtained by counting pseudoholomorphic spheres. The product, called the quantum product, is defined on the Novikov ring Λ = Z[[q^A]] where A∈ H2(M;Z) and q^A records the symplectic area ∫_Aω. For homology classes a,b∈ H_*(M) the quantum product a*b is a sum over spherical classes A of Gromov–Witten invariants ⟨ a,b,·⟩0,3^A multiplied by q^A. Associativity follows from the gluing axiom for the moduli space of stable maps overline{M}0,3(M,A). The unit for this product is the fundamental class [M].

The Seidel representation is a group homomorphism Scolon π1(Ham(M,ω))→ QH2n(M)× from the fundamental group of the Hamiltonian diffeomorphism group to the group of invertible elements of top degree in quantum homology. Given a loop {φ_t}t∈ S1 in Ham(M,ω), one constructs a Hamiltonian fibration over S2 with fibre M and a section of minimal Chern number. Counting sections of this fibration weighted by their symplectic area yields an element S({φ_t})∈ QH2n(M). Seidel proved that this map is a homomorphism and that its image lies in the group of units. The representation detects non‑trivial loops in Ham(M,ω); for example, on CP^n it recovers the generator of π1(Ham(CP^n))congZ/(n+1). The Seidel element also controls the action of π1(Ham) on Floer homology via the Piunikhin–Salamon–Schwarz isomorphism, and it appears in the computation of the quantum cohomology ring of toric manifolds through the Batyrev presentation.

Important technical points: the Novikov ring must be completed with respect to the valuation nu(q^A)=∫_Aω to ensure convergence of infinite sums. The grading on QH_*(M) is Z-graded only when the minimal Chern number N_M≥ 2; otherwise one works with a Z2 grading. The Seidel representation is compatible with the quantum product in the sense that S(γ1·γ2)=S(γ1)*S(γ2). For monotone symplectic manifolds the representation can be computed using the quantum Chevalley formula for the action of divisor classes. In the non‑monotone case one must incorporate the Novikov variable for each spherical class, and the representation takes values in the completed quantum homology QH2n(M;Λ).

Wrapped Fukaya Categories and Stop Removal

The wrapped Fukaya category W(M,Λ) of a Liouville manifold (M,λ) with a chosen stop Λ⊂∂_∞ M (a Legendrian submanifold of the contact boundary) was introduced by Abouzaid and Seidel to capture the symplectic topology of non‑compact exact symplectic manifolds. Objects are exact Lagrangian submanifolds L⊂ M that are cylindrical over Legendrians disjoint from Λ at infinity. Morphism complexes CW^*(L0,L1) are generated by Hamiltonian chords from L0 to L1 that escape to the stop; the differential counts pseudoholomorphic strips with boundary on the Lagrangians and with asymptotic markers at the stop. The A_∞‑structure is defined by counting pseudoholomorphic polygons with one output and several inputs, again with stop‑boundary conditions.

Stop removal is a fundamental functoriality result: if Λ1⊂Λ2 are stops, there is a canonical fully faithful embedding W(M,Λ2)hookrightarrowW(M,Λ1) induced by allowing chords to end on the larger stop. Sylvan proved that the wrapped Fukaya category with empty stop W(M,∅) is equivalent to the category of modules over the endomorphism algebra of a single cotangent fibre, recovering the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence with constructible sheaves. The stop encodes a “boundary condition at infinity” that makes the category invariant under Liouville homotopies fixing the stop. For a Weinstein domain W with skeleton Sk(W), the wrapped Fukaya category with stop equal to the Legendrian link of the skeleton is generated by the cocores of the critical handles; this gives a combinatorial model for W(W) via the Legendrian contact homology algebra of the attaching spheres.

Key applications include the computation of W(T^*Q) for a closed manifold Q, where the stop is the unit cotangent bundle and the category is equivalent to the derived category of constructible sheaves on Q. The stop removal theorem also underlies the proof of the generation criterion: a collection of Lagrangians generates W(M,Λ) if and only if their images generate the category after stop removal to ∅. In mirror symmetry, the wrapped Fukaya category of a punctured Riemann surface with stop at the punctures is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model, providing a categorical formulation of homological mirror symmetry for log Calabi–Yau varieties.

Technique
Explain how the Seidel representation detects a non‑trivial loop in Ham(CP2) and compute its image in quantum homology.
The generator of π1(Ham(CP2))congZ/3 is represented by the loop φ_t([z0:z1:z2])=[e2π i tz0:z1:z2]. The associated Hamiltonian fibration over S2 is the projectivisation of OoplusO(1). Sections correspond to lines in the fibres; the minimal Chern number is 3. Counting sections of area k yields the quantum homology element qk[CP2]. The Seidel element is S=q[CP1][CP2]+q2[CP1][CP2]+[CP2]. In the quantum homology ring QH_*(CP2)=Λ[H]/(H3=q) with H the hyperplane class, this element equals (1+H+H2)[CP2], which is a unit because (1+H+H2)(1-H)=1-H3=1-q. Hence the loop is non‑trivial and its Seidel image is the unit (1+H+H2)[CP2].
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for the Seidel element of a specific loop, first identify the Hamiltonian fibration and its minimal Chern number. Write the quantum homology ring presentation (e.g., Batyrev for toric manifolds). Compute the count of sections by degree using the quantum Chevalley formula or known Gromov–Witten invariants. Express the result in the Novikov ring and verify it is a unit by finding its inverse using the quantum relation. For wrapped Fukaya categories, remember that stop removal is fully faithful and that the empty‑stop category is generated by a single cotangent fibre; use this to reduce generation questions to checking whether a set of Lagrangians splits generates the category after stop removal.

Legendrian Contact Homology and the Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA

Legendrian contact homology is an invariant of Legendrian submanifolds Λ in a contact manifold (Y,xi=kerα) introduced by Chekanov and Eliashberg. The invariant takes the form of a differential graded algebra (DGA) (A(Λ),∂) over Z2 (or Z with signs after a choice of coherent orientations). The algebra A(Λ) is the free unital tensor algebra generated by the Reeb chords of Λ — integral curves of the Reeb vector field R_α that start and end on Λ. The grading of a chord c is given by the Conley–Zehnder index μCZ(c) shifted by n-3 where dim Y=2n-1. The differential ∂ counts rigid pseudoholomorphic disks in the symplectization R× Y with boundary on R×Λ and with one positive puncture asymptotic to a chord c and several negative punctures asymptotic to chords c1,…,c_k; the term contributed is ± c1⋯ c_k. The count is performed in the moduli space of such disks modulo R-translation, and the differential squares to zero because the boundary of the 1‑dimensional moduli space consists of broken configurations corresponding to ∂2.

The Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA is invariant under Legendrian isotopy up to stable tame isomorphism, which allows adding or removing pairs of generators with differential ∂ a=b. Augmentations varepsiloncolonA(Λ)toZ2 (DGA maps to the ground ring) give rise to linearized contact homology LCH_*^varepsilon(Λ), a graded vector space that is easier to compute and often distinguishes Legendrian knots with the same classical invariants (Thurston–Bennequin number and rotation number). For Legendrian knots in R3 with the standard contact structure, the DGA can be computed combinatorially from the front projection using the Ng resolution; the differential counts immersed polygons with corners at the cusps and crossings. The existence of an augmentation is equivalent to the existence of a graded normal ruling of the front diagram, a purely combinatorial condition. Higher order invariants such as the A_∞‑structure on the linearized complex and the Legendrian contact homology of satellites have been developed by Ekholm, Etnyre, Ng, and Sullivan.

Applications include the classification of Legendrian knots up to Legendrian isotopy in R3 and in the 1‑jet space J1(S1). The DGA also provides obstructions to exact Lagrangian fillings: if a Legendrian Λ bounds an exact Lagrangian L in the symplectization, then the DGA of Λ admits an augmentation induced by the Floer homology of L. Conversely, the non‑existence of augmentations proves non‑fillability. The Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA is functorial under exact Lagrangian cobordisms: a cobordism from Λ_- to Λ_+ induces a DGA map A(Λ_+)toA(Λ_-). This functoriality underlies the construction of the Legendrian contact homology category and its relation to the wrapped Fukaya category of the filling.

The Giroux Correspondence and Open Book Decompositions

The Giroux correspondence establishes a bijection between contact structures on a closed oriented 3‑manifold Y up to isotopy and open book decompositions of Y up to positive stabilization. An open book decomposition of Y is a pair (B,π) where B⊂ Y is a link (the binding) and πcolon Y∖ B→ S1 is a fibration whose fibres (the pages) are compact oriented surfaces with boundary B. The monodromy of the fibration is a diffeomorphism φ of a page Σ fixing the boundary; the open book is determined by the mapping class of φ in the mapping class group MCG(Σ,∂Σ). A positive stabilization adds a 1‑handle to the page and composes the monodromy with a right‑handed Dehn twist along a curve that runs once over the new handle. Giroux proved that two open books define isotopic contact structures if and only if they become isotopic after a sequence of positive stabilizations.

The contact structure xi associated to an open book (Σ,φ) is constructed by taking a neighbourhood of the binding modelled on ker(dθ+r2dφ) in cylindrical coordinates and extending over the pages as the kernel of a 1‑form that is dθ on the binding and dα on the pages, where α is a Liouville form on Σ with φ^*α-α=dH for a positive Hamiltonian H. The Reeb vector field is transverse to the pages and tangent to the binding. Conversely, given a contact structure xi, one can find a contact form α whose Reeb flow has a periodic orbit that serves as the binding; the pages are global surfaces of section for the Reeb flow, yielding an open book. The correspondence is natural with respect to contactomorphisms and mapping class group actions.

Key consequences: every contact 3‑manifold admits a compatible open book, so contact structures can be studied via mapping class groups. The classification of tight contact structures on a given 3‑manifold translates into the classification of open books up to positive stabilization. For example, the tight contact structure on S3 corresponds to the trivial open book with page a disk and monodromy the identity; overtwisted contact structures correspond to open books whose monodromy contains a left‑handed Dehn twist. The Giroux correspondence also underlies the proof of the Weinstein conjecture for all contact 3‑manifolds (Taubes, using Seiberg–Witten theory) because the existence of a Reeb orbit is equivalent to the existence of a binding in some open book. In higher dimensions, a generalization by Giroux and Mohsen replaces open books by “open book decompositions” with pages that are Liouville domains and monodromy a compactly supported symplectomorphism; the correspondence holds for contact manifolds of any dimension.

The Weinstein Conjecture and Reeb Dynamics

The Weinstein conjecture asserts that every closed contact manifold admits at least one closed Reeb orbit. In dimension three the conjecture was proved by Taubes using Seiberg–Witten theory, establishing a deep link between contact geometry and gauge theory. For higher dimensions the conjecture remains open in full generality, but it holds for all contact manifolds that admit a symplectic filling, a result due to Viterbo and later refined by Hofer, Wysocki, and Zehnder via pseudoholomorphic curves in symplectizations. The proof strategy typically constructs a finite‑energy pseudoholomorphic plane in the symplectization whose asymptotic limit is a closed Reeb orbit; compactness arguments then guarantee the existence of such a plane. The dynamics of the Reeb vector field are encoded in the contact form α: the Reeb vector field R satisfies ι_R dα = 0 and α(R) = 1. Closed Reeb orbits correspond to periodic solutions of the Hamiltonian system defined by the contact Hamiltonian. The conjecture has profound implications for the topology of contact manifolds: for instance, the existence of a closed Reeb orbit on a contact 3‑manifold implies the existence of a supporting open book decomposition via the Giroux correspondence. In the context of symplectic field theory, the closed Reeb orbits generate the chain complex of contact homology, so the conjecture is equivalent to the non‑vanishing of the contact homology differential in degree zero. Recent work by Cristofaro‑Gardiner, Hutchings, and others has introduced embedded contact homology (ECH) capacities, which provide effective obstructions to symplectic embeddings and give a quantitative refinement of the Weinstein conjecture in dimension three. The conjecture also motivates the study of Reeb dynamics on specific contact manifolds such as the standard contact sphere, where the Reeb flow is the Hopf fibration and all orbits are closed with the same period.

Symplectic Cohomology and Viterbo Functoriality

Symplectic cohomology SH*(W) is a Floer‑theoretic invariant of a Liouville domain W, defined as the direct limit of Hamiltonian Floer cohomology groups for Hamiltonians that grow linearly at infinity. It fits into a long exact sequence with the ordinary cohomology of W and the symplectic homology of the completion, known as the Viterbo sequence. Viterbo functoriality states that an exact symplectic embedding of Liouville domains W₁ W₂ induces a map SH*(W₂) → SH*(W₁) that respects the product structures and the Viterbo sequence. This functoriality is a cornerstone for applications to symplectic embedding problems: if SH*(W₁) vanishes while SH*(W₂) does not, then no exact embedding can exist. The construction uses continuation maps in Floer theory for a monotone homotopy of Hamiltonians that are linear on the cylindrical ends. The resulting map is independent of choices up to homotopy and commutes with the pair‑of‑pants product, making SH* a contravariant functor from the category of Liouville domains with exact embeddings to graded algebras. A key computational tool is the isomorphism between SH*(W) and the wrapped Fukaya category of W, established by Abouzaid and Seidel. This identifies the Viterbo map with the restriction functor on wrapped Fukaya categories induced by the embedding. In particular, the non‑vanishing of SH* for a Liouville domain implies that its wrapped Fukaya category is non‑trivial, which in turn yields obstructions to exact Lagrangian embeddings. The functoriality also extends to the level of Rabinowitz Floer homology, providing a bridge between symplectic cohomology and contact homology of the boundary.

Worked Example: Obstructing an Exact Embedding via Symplectic Cohomology
Let W₁ be the unit cotangent disk bundle of the 2‑sphere, and W₂ the standard 4‑ball. Show that there is no exact symplectic embedding of W₁ into W₂.
The symplectic cohomology of the 4‑ball vanishes: SH*(B⁴) = 0 because B⁴ is subcritical and its wrapped Fukaya category is trivial. For the unit cotangent disk bundle of S², which is a Liouville domain diffeomorphic to T*S² with the standard Liouville form, symplectic cohomology is isomorphic to the singular cohomology of the free loop space of S² shifted by the dimension. In particular SH*(T*S²) contains a copy of H*(LS²) which is non‑zero in infinitely many degrees (e.g., the constant loops give a copy of H*(S²)). By Viterbo functoriality, an exact embedding W₁ W₂ would induce a unital algebra map SH*(W₂) → SH*(W₁). Since SH*(W₂) = 0, the map would be the zero map, but a unital algebra map must send the unit to the unit, which is non‑zero in SH*(W₁). This contradiction shows that no such exact embedding exists.
Exam Strategy: Recognising Viterbo Functoriality in Embedding Questions
When a problem asks to rule out an exact symplectic embedding between two Liouville domains, compute or recall the symplectic cohomology of each. If the target has vanishing SH* while the source has non‑zero SH*, Viterbo functoriality gives an immediate obstruction. Remember that the map must be unital, so the unit in the source cannot map to zero. This argument appears frequently in qualifying exams and research‑level problem sets.

Contact Homology and the Bourgeois–Oancea Isomorphism

Contact homology is a Legendrian invariant of a contact manifold (Y, ξ) defined by counting pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectization ℝ × Y with asymptotic limits at Reeb orbits. The chain complex is generated by good Reeb orbits, and the differential counts rigid holomorphic cylinders. The construction requires a non‑degenerate contact form and a generic almost complex structure compatible with the symplectization. The resulting homology groups depend only on the contact structure up to isotopy. A fundamental result of Bourgeois and Oancea establishes an isomorphism between the contact homology of a contact manifold and the symplectic homology of a Liouville filling, provided the filling is exact and the contact form is induced by the Liouville form on the boundary. More precisely, if W is a Liouville domain with boundary Y and Liouville form λ, then the contact homology of (Y, ker λ|_Y) is canonically isomorphic to the positive symplectic homology SH*_+(W). This isomorphism intertwines the BV operator on contact homology with the Conley–Zehnder index shift on symplectic homology. The proof uses a stretching‑the‑neck argument: one degenerates the symplectization into a broken holomorphic building consisting of a curve in the completion of W and a curve in the symplectization, then identifies the resulting moduli spaces with those defining the symplectic homology differential. The isomorphism has several important consequences. First, it shows that contact homology is invariant under exact symplectic cobordisms, because symplectic homology is functorial under exact embeddings. Second, it provides a computational tool: for many Liouville domains (e.g., cotangent bundles) symplectic homology is known, giving explicit contact homology groups. Third, it relates the linearized contact homology (defined via an augmentation of the Chekanov–Eliashberg DGA) to the linearized symplectic homology, which is often easier to compute. The Bourgeois–Oancea isomorphism also extends to the equivariant setting, yielding an isomorphism between equivariant contact homology and the S¹‑equivariant symplectic homology, which plays a role in the proof of the Weinstein conjecture for certain classes of contact manifolds.

Stein Manifolds, Flexibility, and the h‑Principle

A Stein manifold is a complex manifold admitting a proper strictly plurisubharmonic exhaustion function. Equivalently, it is a complex manifold that can be properly holomorphically embedded into ℂ^N for some N. The foundational theorem of Eliashberg and Gromov states that the classification of Stein structures on a given smooth manifold up to deformation is governed by an h‑principle: any formal Stein structure (i.e., a homotopy class of almost complex structures together with a homotopy class of exhausting functions satisfying the convexity condition at the formal level) can be deformed to a genuine Stein structure. This flexibility result has a symplectic counterpart: a Stein manifold carries a canonical Weinstein structure, i.e., a Liouville vector field that is gradient‑like for the plurisubharmonic function. The h‑principle for Stein manifolds implies that any two Weinstein structures that are homotopic through formal Weinstein structures are isotopic through genuine Weinstein structures. A key ingredient is the existence of a flexible Weinstein structure, which contains a collection of flexible Legendrian attaching spheres in the contact boundary. The flexibility allows one to perform Weinstein handle attachments in a way that satisfies the h‑principle for Legendrian embeddings: any formal Legendrian isotopy can be realized by a genuine Legendrian isotopy. This has striking applications, such as the classification of flexible Weinstein manifolds up to symplectomorphism by their homotopy type, and the construction of exotic symplectic structures on ℝ2n that are not symplectomorphic to the standard one but are diffeomorphic. The h‑principle also underlies the proof that every open contact manifold admits a contact structure, and that any two contact structures on an open manifold are isotopic if they are homotopic as plane fields. In the closed case, the h‑principle fails, leading to the rich classification of tight versus overtwisted contact structures in dimension three and the existence of non‑fillable contact structures in higher dimensions.

Quantum Cohomology, Dubrovin Connection, and Frobenius Manifolds

Quantum cohomology QH*(X) of a symplectic manifold X is a deformation of the ordinary cup product on H*(X; Λ) where Λ is the Novikov ring. The quantum product a * b is defined by summing over genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants with three insertions: ⟨a, b, c⟩0,3,β for β ∈ H₂(X). The resulting product is associative, commutative, and has a unit given by the fundamental class. The associativity is equivalent to the Witten–Dijkgraaf–Verlinde–Verlinde (WDVV) equations, a system of quadratic relations among the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants. Dubrovin observed that the quantum cohomology, together with the Euler vector field and the intersection pairing, defines a Frobenius manifold structure on the formal neighbourhood of the origin in H*(X). The Dubrovin connection is a flat connection on the tangent bundle of this Frobenius manifold, given by ∇_X Y = X ∘ Y + z⁻¹ (X ∪ Y) where ∘ is the quantum product, ∪ is the classical cup product, and z is a formal parameter. Flatness of the Dubrovin connection is equivalent to the WDVV equations. The connection has regular singularities at z = 0 and z = ∞, and its monodromy data encode the quantum cohomology ring. In the context of mirror symmetry, the Dubrovin connection on the A‑model (quantum cohomology of X) is identified with the Gauss–Manin connection on the B‑model (variation of Hodge structure of the mirror family). This identification yields powerful computational tools: the quantum differential equation ∇_z s = 0 can be solved by period integrals on the mirror, giving explicit formulas for Gromov–Witten invariants. The Frobenius manifold structure also appears in the study of integrable systems, where the quantum cohomology of projective spaces is related to the Toda lattice, and in the classification of semisimple Frobenius manifolds via the theory of isomonodromic deformations.

Symplectic Cutting and Gluing: Constructing New Manifolds

Symplectic cutting and gluing are fundamental techniques for constructing new symplectic manifolds from existing ones, analogous to surgical procedures in topology. These methods provide powerful tools for understanding the structure and classification of symplectic manifolds, especially in the presence of group actions.

Symplectic Cutting involves taking a symplectic manifold (M, ω) equipped with a Hamiltonian action of a Lie group G, and a moment map μ: M → g* (the dual of the Lie algebra of G). If we choose a regular value c ∈ g* for the moment map, the level set μ⁻¹(c) is a smooth manifold. When G acts freely and properly on μ⁻¹(c), the quotient space μ⁻¹(c)/G inherits a natural symplectic structure. This process is known as symplectic reduction or Marsden-Weinstein reduction. Symplectic cutting is a specific instance of this, often applied when G is a circle group S¹ and the moment map has a specific form. For example, if (M, ω) is a symplectic manifold and f: M → ℝ is a Hamiltonian function for an S¹ action, then for a regular value c, the level set f⁻¹(c) can be "cut" and replaced by a boundary, leading to a new symplectic manifold. A more general formulation involves cutting along a symplectic hypersurface with a contact boundary. The key idea is to remove a neighborhood of a level set of a moment map and then "cap off" the resulting boundary with a standard piece, often a symplectic cone or a product of a contact manifold with an interval. This allows for the construction of compact symplectic manifolds with specific properties, such as those with non-trivial fundamental groups or specific Betti numbers, which might be difficult to obtain otherwise.

Symplectic Gluing is, in essence, the reverse operation or a complementary process. It involves joining two symplectic manifolds along a common symplectic boundary, or more generally, along contact boundaries. If (M₁, ω₁) and (M₂, ω₂) are two symplectic manifolds, and they both contain a common symplectic hypersurface S as a boundary component (possibly after some modification), then one can form a new symplectic manifold M = M₁ ∪_S M₂. This process requires careful matching of the symplectic forms along the common boundary. A common application is the gluing of two Liouville domains along their contact boundaries. For instance, if Σ is a contact manifold, and W₁ and W₂ are two Liouville domains whose boundaries are contactomorphic to Σ, then one can glue W₁ and W₂ along their boundaries to obtain a new symplectic manifold. This is particularly useful in constructing closed symplectic manifolds from open ones, or in understanding the structure of symplectic manifolds with boundary. For example, a symplectic sum of two symplectic manifolds X₁ and X₂ along a common symplectic submanifold S results in a new symplectic manifold X₁ #_S X₂. This technique is crucial for understanding the topology of symplectic manifolds, especially in dimension four, where it relates to the construction of exotic symplectic structures and the study of Kodaira dimension for symplectic manifolds.

Lagrangian Mean Curvature Flow and Calibrated Submanifolds

Lagrangian submanifolds play a central role in symplectic geometry, being submanifolds of half the dimension of the ambient symplectic manifold, on which the symplectic form restricts to zero. The study of their geometric properties, particularly their minimal surface characteristics, leads to the concept of Lagrangian Mean Curvature Flow (LMCF) and calibrated submanifolds.

Mean Curvature Flow (MCF) is a geometric evolution equation for submanifolds, where the normal velocity of each point on the submanifold is given by its mean curvature vector. It is the negative gradient flow of the area functional. For a general submanifold, MCF tends to shrink the submanifold to a point or to a minimal surface. However, when the submanifold is Lagrangian, the flow gains special properties due to the underlying symplectic structure.

Lagrangian Mean Curvature Flow (LMCF) is the mean curvature flow applied to Lagrangian submanifolds. A remarkable property of LMCF is that if the initial submanifold is Lagrangian, and the ambient manifold is a Calabi-Yau manifold (a Kähler manifold with trivial canonical bundle), then the flow preserves the Lagrangian condition. This means that if L₀ is a Lagrangian submanifold, then L(t) generated by LMCF will remain Lagrangian for all time t for which the flow exists. The mean curvature vector of a Lagrangian submanifold in a Calabi-Yau manifold can be identified with the gradient of a potential function, known as the phase function. The LMCF equation can then be reinterpreted as a gradient flow for this phase function, making it particularly amenable to analysis. Solutions to LMCF are often sought to converge to special Lagrangian submanifolds, which are minimal Lagrangian submanifolds that are also calibrated.

Calibrated Submanifolds are a special class of minimal submanifolds in a Riemannian manifold that arise in the context of calibrated geometry. A calibration is a closed differential form φ of degree k such that for any oriented k-dimensional subspace V of the tangent space, φ restricted to V is less than or equal to the volume form on V, with equality if and only if V is "φ-adapted". A k-dimensional submanifold M is φ-calibrated if at every point, its tangent space is φ-adapted. Calibrated submanifolds are automatically minimal. In the context of symplectic geometry, particularly in Calabi-Yau manifolds, the real part of the holomorphic volume form provides a natural calibration. Lagrangian submanifolds calibrated by this form are called special Lagrangian submanifolds. These are minimal Lagrangian submanifolds whose phase function is constant. Special Lagrangian submanifolds are crucial in homological mirror symmetry, where they correspond to holomorphic submanifolds on the mirror manifold. LMCF is a powerful tool for constructing and studying special Lagrangian submanifolds, as it provides a dynamic process that can deform arbitrary Lagrangian submanifolds into these special, minimal configurations, provided certain conditions are met, such as convexity or small initial energy.

Distinguishing Symplectic Reduction from Cutting/Gluing
When encountering terms like "symplectic cutting" or "symplectic reduction" in advanced topics, pay close attention to the context. Symplectic reduction, often associated with the Marsden-Weinstein theorem, typically results in a *quotient* manifold with a new symplectic structure. Symplectic cutting, while related, often implies a more topological surgery: removing a part of the manifold (e.g., a neighborhood of a fixed point set or a region defined by a moment map inequality) and then "capping" the resulting boundary with a standard piece (like a symplectic cone over a contact manifold). This distinction is crucial for understanding the resulting manifold's topology and geometry. Reduction often simplifies the manifold, while cutting/gluing can create more complex or exotic structures.

The Symplectic Category and Functors to Algebraic Invariants

In modern mathematics, category theory provides a powerful framework for organizing and relating different mathematical structures. Symplectic geometry is no exception, and the concept of a "symplectic category" allows for a more abstract and unified understanding of its objects and transformations, as well as the invariants derived from them.

The most basic Symplectic Category, let's call it Sympl, has as its objects symplectic manifolds (M, ω), and as its morphisms symplectomorphisms φ: (M₁, ω₁) → (M₂, ω₂). A symplectomorphism is a diffeomorphism that preserves the symplectic form, i.e., φ*ω₂ = ω₁. This category captures the fundamental notion of equivalence in symplectic geometry. However, this category is often too rigid for many purposes, as it only considers exact equivalences. More flexible notions of morphisms, such as symplectic embeddings or Lagrangian correspondences, are often considered in more advanced contexts.

A more intricate and profoundly important categorical structure is the Fukaya Category, denoted Fuk(M), associated with a symplectic manifold M. This is not a category of symplectic manifolds, but rather a category "living inside" a given symplectic manifold. Its objects are certain Lagrangian submanifolds of M (often compact, exact, or with specific boundary conditions). The morphisms between two Lagrangian submanifolds L₀ and L₁ are given by the Floer homology groups HF(L₀, L₁), which are vector spaces (or modules) generated by the intersection points of L₀ and L₁ and equipped with a differential counting pseudoholomorphic strips. Composition of morphisms is defined via a count of pseudoholomorphic triangles. The Fukaya category is a differential graded (DG) category, meaning its morphism spaces are chain complexes. It is a cornerstone of homological mirror symmetry, where it is conjectured to be equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on the mirror complex manifold. This categorical perspective allows for the translation of geometric problems about Lagrangian submanifolds into algebraic problems about chain complexes and their homologies.

Beyond the Fukaya category, various Functors to Algebraic Invariants exist, mapping objects and morphisms in Sympl (or related categories) to algebraic structures. For instance, there are functors from Sympl to:

  • Quantum Cohomology: QH*(M) is a deformation of the ordinary cohomology ring H*(M) by Gromov-Witten invariants. It is a functor in the sense that a symplectomorphism induces an isomorphism of quantum cohomology rings.
  • Symplectic Cohomology: SH*(M) is an invariant for open symplectic manifolds (Liouville domains) that captures information about periodic orbits of Hamiltonian flows. It is also functorial under exact symplectic embeddings.
  • Floer Homology Groups: While the Fukaya category uses Floer homology as morphisms, the various Floer homology theories (e.g., Hamiltonian Floer homology HF*(M, H) for a Hamiltonian H) themselves can be seen as functors from a category of Hamiltonian systems to graded vector spaces.
These functors allow mathematicians to use algebraic tools (like homological algebra, representation theory, and category theory) to study the geometric properties of symplectic manifolds. The functorial nature ensures that these algebraic invariants are indeed properties of the symplectic structure itself, independent of specific choices of coordinates or representations, and are preserved under the appropriate notion of equivalence (symplectomorphism or exact embedding). The development of these categorical frameworks has been instrumental in advancing our understanding of symplectic topology and its deep connections to other areas of mathematics and physics.

Symplectic Geometry of Integrable Hamiltonian Systems

The study of Hamiltonian systems is a classical domain where symplectic geometry finds its most direct and foundational applications. An especially rich area is that of integrable Hamiltonian systems, which exhibit a high degree of order and predictability, contrasting sharply with the chaotic behavior often seen in general dynamical systems.

A Hamiltonian System is defined by a symplectic manifold (M, ω) and a smooth function H: M → ℝ, called the Hamiltonian. The dynamics are given by the Hamiltonian vector field X_H, defined by the equation i_(X_H)ω = -dH. The integral curves of X_H represent the trajectories of the system in phase space. The Hamiltonian H is conserved along these trajectories, meaning dH/dt = 0.

A Hamiltonian system with n degrees of freedom (i.e., M is a 2n-dimensional symplectic manifold) is said to be Liouville Integrable if there exist n functionally independent, smooth functions F₁, ..., Fₙ on M that are in involution (i.e., their Poisson brackets {Fᵢ, Fⱼ} = ω(XFᵢ, XFⱼ) are zero for all i, j), and F₁ = H is one of them. The existence of these n conserved quantities in involution implies a significant simplification of the dynamics.

The cornerstone result for integrable systems is the Liouville-Arnold Theorem. This theorem states that in a neighborhood of a compact, connected regular level set of the n commuting integrals (i.e., a level set where the differentials dF₁, ..., dFₙ are linearly independent), the phase space is foliated by n-dimensional invariant tori. Furthermore, on each such torus, there exist special coordinates (q₁, ..., qₙ, p₁, ..., pₙ), known as action-angle coordinates, such that the symplectic form is given by ω = Σ dqᵢ ∧ dpᵢ, and the Hamiltonian depends only on the action variables H = H(p₁, ..., pₙ). In these coordinates, the equations of motion become remarkably simple: dqᵢ/dt = ∂H/∂pᵢ = constant, and dpᵢ/dt = -∂H/∂qᵢ = 0. This means the action variables pᵢ are conserved, and the angle variables qᵢ evolve linearly in time. The trajectories on the tori are quasi-periodic, winding around the torus with constant frequencies.

The Geometry of Phase Space for integrable systems is thus characterized by this foliation into invariant tori. These tori are Lagrangian submanifolds, as the restriction of the symplectic form to them is zero. The action variables correspond to integrals of the canonical one-form along cycles on these tori, while the angle variables parameterize the tori themselves. This geometric insight provides a deep understanding of the long-term behavior of such systems, allowing for explicit solutions and predictions. Examples of integrable systems include the Kepler problem (planetary motion), the harmonic oscillator, and the Toda lattice. The study of integrable systems bridges classical mechanics, differential geometry, and algebraic geometry, with applications ranging from celestial mechanics to quantum field theory.

Microlocal Sheaves and the Fukaya Category

The Fukaya category, as introduced previously, is a powerful invariant in symplectic geometry, encoding information about Lagrangian submanifolds and their intersections. A modern and highly abstract perspective on the Fukaya category, particularly for cotangent bundles, involves the theory of microlocal sheaves. This approach connects symplectic geometry with algebraic topology and category theory in a profound way.

A Microlocal Sheaf is a constructible sheaf on a manifold M, whose singular support is contained in a given Lagrangian submanifold of the cotangent bundle T*M. More generally, the category of microlocal sheaves on a manifold M, denoted D_c(M), is the derived category of constructible sheaves on M. The "microlocal" aspect refers to studying these sheaves not just on M, but on its cotangent bundle T*M, where the symplectic structure plays a crucial role. The singular support of a sheaf is a closed conic Lagrangian subset of T*M, which captures information about where the sheaf "changes its local system."

The deep connection to the Fukaya category arises from the work of Nadler and Zaslow, who proposed that for a smooth manifold Q, the Fukaya category of its cotangent bundle T*Q, Fuk(T*Q), is equivalent to the derived category of constructible sheaves on Q, D_c(Q). This is a precise formulation of a component of homological mirror symmetry for the pair (T*Q, Q). In this correspondence, objects of Fuk(T*Q) are exact Lagrangian submanifolds of T*Q, and objects of D_c(Q) are constructible sheaves on Q. The morphisms in Fuk(T*Q) are given by Floer homology groups, while morphisms in D_c(Q) are given by Ext groups of sheaves.

The concept of Microlocalization provides the bridge. Given a sheaf F on Q, one can associate to it a microlocal object on T*Q. This process essentially "lifts" the information about the sheaf from the base manifold Q to its cotangent bundle T*Q, where the symplectic geometry can be brought to bear. The singular support of a constructible sheaf on Q is a Lagrangian subset of T*Q. This allows for a translation between the language of sheaves (algebraic topology) and the language of Lagrangian submanifolds (symplectic geometry).

The significance of this equivalence is immense. It provides a powerful computational tool: problems in Lagrangian Floer homology, which can be notoriously difficult to compute directly using pseudoholomorphic curves, can sometimes be translated into problems about constructible sheaves, which might be more amenable to techniques from homological algebra. Conversely, geometric insights from symplectic topology can inform the study of sheaves. This framework has been extended and generalized, leading to the development of categories of "microlocal sheaves" or "wrapped Fukaya categories" for more general symplectic manifolds, and has found applications in areas such as knot theory (via the Legendrian contact homology of knots in contact manifolds) and the representation theory of quivers. It represents a pinnacle of abstraction and unification in modern geometry, linking seemingly disparate fields through categorical equivalences.

Common Traps

  • Confusing symplectic reduction with general symplectic cutting: While related, reduction typically yields a quotient manifold, whereas cutting often implies a surgical procedure involving removing a region and capping the boundary.
  • Assuming Lagrangian Mean Curvature Flow always converges to a special Lagrangian: Convergence is not guaranteed in all cases and depends on initial conditions, ambient geometry, and existence of singularities.
  • Believing the Fukaya category is a category of symplectic manifolds: The Fukaya category is associated with a *single* symplectic manifold, and its objects are Lagrangian submanifolds *within* that manifold, not entire manifolds themselves.
  • Misinterpreting the Liouville-Arnold theorem's scope: The theorem guarantees action-angle coordinates only in a neighborhood of a *compact, connected regular level set* of the integrals, not globally across the entire phase space.
  • Overlooking the "cotangent bundle" specificity in the microlocal sheaves correspondence: The equivalence between the Fukaya category and the derived category of constructible sheaves is most directly established for the cotangent bundle T*Q, not for arbitrary symplectic manifolds M.
  • Neglecting the "in involution" condition for Liouville integrability: It's not enough to have n conserved quantities; they must also commute under the Poisson bracket.
  • Assuming all Lagrangian submanifolds are calibrated: Calibrated submanifolds are a special subset of Lagrangian submanifolds that satisfy an additional geometric condition related to a calibration form.

Equivariant Symplectic Cohomology and Localization

Equivariant symplectic cohomology extends the Viterbo functoriality framework to spaces with Hamiltonian group actions. Let (M, ω) be a Liouville domain with a Hamiltonian S¹-action generated by a moment map μ: M → ℝ. The equivariant symplectic cohomology SH*S¹(M) is defined as the homology of a complex generated by S¹-equivariant Hamiltonian orbits, where the differential counts solutions to the Floer equation that are equivariant with respect to the S¹-action on the domain and target. The key computational tool is the localization theorem of Givental and Kim, which expresses SH*S¹(M) in terms of the fixed point set MS¹. For each isolated fixed point p, the contribution is a copy of the equivariant cohomology H*S¹(pt) ≅ ℚ[u], where u has degree 2. The differential in the localized complex is determined by the weights of the S¹-action on the normal bundle to the fixed point set. When M is the cotangent bundle T*Q of a closed manifold Q with the geodesic flow S¹-action, the fixed points correspond to constant loops at critical points of the energy functional, and the localization formula recovers the Morse-Bott spectral sequence converging to the loop space homology H*(LQ). For toric symplectic manifolds, the moment polytope Δ encodes the S¹-action, and the equivariant symplectic cohomology can be computed via the Givental-Lee quantum D-module associated to the mirror Landau-Ginzburg model. The localization formula takes the form SH*S¹(M) ≅ ⊕v ∈ Vertices(Δ) ℚ[u] ⊗ Λ, where Λ is the Novikov ring, and the differential is given by counting holomorphic disks with boundary on the toric divisors. This computation underlies the proof of the toric mirror symmetry conjecture for Fano toric varieties by Coates, Corti, Iritani, and Tseng. A crucial subtlety is that the S¹-action must be semifree (weights ±1) for the standard localization formula to apply; otherwise, one must use the virtual localization formalism of Graber-Pandharipande, which introduces contributions from multiple covers of holomorphic curves. The equivariant theory also provides a natural framework for the Seidel representation π₁(Ham(M, ω)) → QH*(M)×, where the S¹-action on the universal cover of Ham(M, ω) induces a map on equivariant quantum cohomology that recovers the quantum product via the quantum Kirwan map.

The Fukaya-Seidel Category and Lefschetz Fibrations

The Fukaya-Seidel category FS(π) associated to a Lefschetz fibration π: E → ℂ with exact symplectic fiber (F, ω = dλ) and m critical points provides a categorical framework for homological mirror symmetry of Landau-Ginzburg models. Objects are vanishing cycles L_i ⊂ F, which are Lagrangian spheres obtained by parallel transport of the vanishing cycle along a distinguished basis of paths γ_i from the critical values to a regular value. The morphism spaces are Floer cohomology groups HF*(L_i, L_j) computed in the fiber F, but the A_∞-structure incorporates Lefschetz thimbles — the Lagrangian disks Δ_i ⊂ E obtained by parallel transport of the vanishing disk along γ_i. The key structural result is the directed A_∞-category property: after ordering the vanishing cycles by the argument of their critical values, all higher products μ^k for k ≥ 3 vanish when evaluated on morphisms that go "backwards" in this ordering. This directedness implies that FS(π) is formal in a suitable sense, and its derived category D^π FS(π) is equivalent to the derived category of modules over the path algebra of the quiver with relations determined by the intersection pattern of vanishing cycles. The quiver is the intersection quiver of the distinguished basis, with vertices corresponding to vanishing cycles and arrows corresponding to intersection points. The relations come from the A_∞-equations and are encoded by the Seidel-Thomas braid group action on the derived category. For the A_n Milnor fiber (the Lefschetz fibration zn+1: ℂ → ℂ), the Fukaya-Seidel category is equivalent to the derived category of representations of the A_n quiver, and the braid group action recovers the classical action on exceptional collections. The wrapped Fukaya category W(F) of the fiber is recovered as the homotopy colimit of FS(π) over all possible distinguished bases, which corresponds to the stop removal procedure of Sylvan. The monodromy of the Lefschetz fibration, a symplectomorphism φ ∈ Symp_ct(F), acts on FS(π) by an autoequivalence, and the monodromy invariant Fukaya category is the category of modules over the monodromy action. This construction is central to the proof of homological mirror symmetry for toric varieties by Abouzaid, where the mirror Landau-Ginzburg model is a Lefschetz fibration whose Fukaya-Seidel category is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on the toric variety.

Worked Computation: Fukaya-Seidel Category of the A₂ Milnor Fiber
Consider the Lefschetz fibration π: E → ℂ given by π(z₁, z₂) = z₁³ + z₂² (the A₂ singularity). The fiber F = π⁻¹(1) is a punctured torus. The critical values are at 0 (with multiplicity 3). Choose a distinguished basis of vanishing paths γ₁, γ₂, γ₃ with arguments 0, 2π/3, 4π/3. The corresponding vanishing cycles L₁, L₂, L₃ are simple closed curves on the punctured torus with intersection pattern L₁ ∩ L₂ = {p₁₂}, L₂ ∩ L₃ = {p₂₃}, L₃ ∩ L₁ = {p₃₁}, all transverse with intersection number 1. Compute the quiver and the A_∞-structure on the directed Fukaya-Seidel category.
The intersection quiver has vertices 1, 2, 3 and arrows a₁₂: 1 → 2, a₂₃: 2 → 3, a₃₁: 3 → 1 corresponding to the intersection points. The directed ordering is 1 ≺ 2 ≺ 3. The morphism spaces are HF*(L_i, L_j) = ℤ⟨a_ij⟩ for i ≺ j, and HF*(L_i, L_i) = ℤ⟨e_i⟩ ⊕ ℤ⟨f_i⟩ where e_i is the unit and f_i is the degree 1 generator (the puncture). The A_∞-products: μ¹ = 0 (all cycles are exact in the fiber). μ²(a₂₃, a₁₂) = a₁₃ (the composition of paths in the quiver). μ²(a₃₁, a₂₃) = 0 because 3 ≺ 1 is false in the directed ordering. The only non-trivial μ³ is μ³(a₃₁, a₂₃, a₁₂) = e₁ (or a multiple), which corresponds to the triangle formed by the three vanishing cycles. This μ³ encodes the relation a₃₁ a₂₃ a₁₂ = 0 in the path algebra. The derived category D^π FS(π) is equivalent to D^b(Rep(A₃)), the derived category of representations of the A₃ quiver 1 → 2 → 3 with the relation that the composition of all three arrows is zero. This matches the known mirror symmetry for the A₂ singularity.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Directed A_∞-Categories
When a question asks about the Fukaya-Seidel category of a Lefschetz fibration, first identify the distinguished basis of vanishing paths and the induced ordering of vanishing cycles. The directed property means all higher products μ^k vanish on sequences of morphisms that are not strictly increasing in this ordering. To compute the quiver, draw the intersection pattern of vanishing cycles — vertices are cycles, arrows are intersection points oriented by the ordering. The A_∞-relations correspond to polygons bounded by the vanishing cycles. For A_n singularities, the quiver is the linear A_n quiver with relations from the unique (n+1)-gon. For D_n and E_n singularities, the quiver is the corresponding Dynkin quiver. Memorize the correspondence: A_n Milnor fiber A_n quiver, D_n D_n quiver, E6, E7, E8 exceptional quivers.

Embedded Contact Homology and ECH Capacities

Embedded Contact Homology (ECH), developed by Hutchings and Taubes, is a Floer-type homology theory for three-dimensional contact manifolds (Y, ξ = ker λ) that is isomorphic to Seiberg-Witten Floer cohomology and, via Taubes's isomorphism, to a version of Heegaard Floer homology. The chain complex ECC*(Y, λ, J) is generated by orbit sets α = {(α_i, m_i)}, where α_i are distinct simple Reeb orbits and m_i ∈ ℤ⁺ are multiplicities, with the constraint that m_i = 1 for hyperbolic orbits. The differential ∂ counts J-holomorphic currents in the symplectization ℝ × Y with ECH index I = 1. The ECH index is a topological invariant of a holomorphic current C given by I(C) = c_τ(C) + Q_τ(C) + CZ_τ^I(C), where c_τ is the relative first Chern class, Q_τ is the relative intersection pairing, and CZ_τ^I is a sum of Conley-Zehnder indices of the asymptotic orbits with multiplicities. The ECH capacities c_k(Y, ξ) for k ≥ 1 are a sequence of symplectic capacities for four-dimensional Liouville domains (X, ω) with contact boundary (Y, ξ), defined as the infimum of L such that there exists a class in ECH^L(Y, λ) (the filtered ECH with action ≤ L) that maps non-trivially to the contact invariant c(ξ) ∈ ECH(Y, λ). For ellipsoids E(a, b) = {π|z₁|²/a + π|z₂|²/b ≤ 1} with a ≤ b, the ECH capacities are given by the McDuff-Schlenk staircase: c_k(E(a, b)) = min{am + bn | m, n ∈ ℤ⁺, (m+1)(n+1)/2 ≥ k+1}. This formula exhibits a remarkable piecewise linear structure with accumulation points at rational multiples of the volume capacity. The ECH capacities satisfy all axioms of symplectic capacities (monotonicity, conformality, non-triviality) and are sharp for many embedding problems: for example, the embedding problem for ellipsoids into balls is completely solved by ECH capacities. The cobordism maps on ECH induced by exact symplectic cobordisms (X, ω) with ∂X = Y₊ ⊔ -Y₋ provide a functorial structure that is compatible with the Viterbo transfer map on symplectic homology. The ECH spectral sequence relates the filtered ECH of a contact manifold to its contact homology, and the U-map on ECH (counting holomorphic currents with a marked point mapping to a fixed point in Y) corresponds to the BV operator on symplectic homology under the isomorphism SH*(X) ≅ ECH*(Y). The computation of ECH for toric contact manifolds uses the combinatorial formula of Hutchings, where generators correspond to lattice paths in the moment polygon, and the differential is determined by the ECH index inequality I ≥ 1.

Lagrangian Cobordism and the Cobordism Category

Lagrangian cobordism provides a geometric relation between Lagrangian submanifolds in a symplectic manifold (M, ω) that refines Hamiltonian isotopy and Lagrangian concordance. A Lagrangian cobordism from L₋ to L₊ is an exact Lagrangian submanifold V ⊂ ℝ × M (with the symplectic form d(e^t ω)) such that V ∩ ((-∞, -R] × M) = (-∞, -R] × L₋ and V ∩ ([R, ∞) × M) = [R, ∞) × L₊ for some R > 0. The Lagrangian cobordism category Cob(M) has objects the exact Lagrangian submanifolds of M, and morphisms from L₋ to L₊ are isomorphism classes of exact Lagrangian cobordisms. Composition is given by gluing cobordisms along their ends. This category has a natural triangulated structure where exact triangles correspond to Lagrangian surgeries (the geometric counterpart of the mapping cone in the Fukaya category). The cobordism map on Floer homology Φ_V: HF*(L₋, K) → HF*(L₊, K) induced by a cobordism V is a chain map that respects the A_∞-structure. A fundamental result of Biran and Cornea is that if L₋ and L₊ are related by a Lagrangian cobordism, then their Maslov classes and minimal Maslov numbers satisfy NL₋ ≥ NL₊, and the quantum homology QH*(L₋) maps to QH*(L₊) via a unital ring homomorphism. The cobordism distance dcob(L₀, L₁) is the minimal genus of a Lagrangian cobordism between L₀ and L₁; it is a metric on the set of Hamiltonian isotopy classes of Lagrangians. For the standard symplectic ℝ²ⁿ, the nearby Lagrangian conjecture (proved by Eliashberg-Polterovich for n=2 and by Fukaya-Seidel-Smith for all n using wrapped Floer homology) states that any exact Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ T*Q is Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero section. The proof uses the fact that the wrapped Fukaya category W(T*Q) is generated by the zero section, and any exact Lagrangian L defines a module over W(T*Q) that must be representable. The Lagrangian cobordism group ΩLag_*(M) is the group of cobordism classes of closed exact Lagrangians modulo those that bound a Lagrangian cobordism to the empty set. For M = T*Q, this group is isomorphic to the singular homology H_*(Q; ℤ) via the map sending a Lagrangian to its projection to Q. The cobordism category is closely related to the Fukaya category: there is a functor Cob(M) → Fuk(M) sending a Lagrangian to itself and a cobordism to the corresponding bimodule map, which is fully faithful on the subcategory of non-displaceable Lagrangians.

The Nearby Lagrangian Conjecture and its Resolution

The nearby Lagrangian conjecture, formulated by Arnold in the 1980s, asserts that every exact Lagrangian submanifold L in the cotangent bundle T*Q of a closed manifold Q is Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero section Q ⊂ T*Q. This conjecture is a central problem in symplectic topology, as it would imply that the topology of exact Lagrangians in cotangent bundles is completely rigid. The conjecture was proved in stages: for Q = S¹ (dimension 2) by Eliashberg and Polterovich using holomorphic curves; for Q = S² (dimension 4) by Fukaya, Seidel, and Smith using the wrapped Fukaya category and the generation criterion of Abouzaid; and for general Q by Abouzaid and Kragh using the wrapped Floer homology and the Nadler-Zaslow correspondence with microlocal sheaves. The key idea in the general proof is to show that the zero section Q generates the wrapped Fukaya category W(T*Q) as a split-generator. This means that any object L ∈ W(T*Q) can be obtained from Q by a finite sequence of mapping cones, shifts, and direct summands. The generation criterion of Abouzaid states that a Lagrangian L generates W(T*Q) if and only if the wrapped Floer cohomology HW*(L, Q) ≠ 0. For an exact Lagrangian L ⊂ T*Q, the projection π: L → Q is a degree 1 map (by exactness and the fact that L is a section at infinity), which implies that the unit in HW*(Q, Q) ≅ H*(LQ) maps non-trivially to HW*(L, Q) under the map induced by the inclusion Q → L. This non-vanishing implies that L is in the split-closure of Q, hence Hamiltonian isotopic to Q. The proof uses the Viterbo isomorphism SH*(T*Q) ≅ H*(LQ) and the Abouzaid isomorphism HW*(L, Q) ≅ H*(L) for exact Lagrangians. A crucial technical ingredient is the stop removal theorem of Sylvan, which identifies the wrapped Fukaya category of T*Q with the Fukaya category of the compactified cotangent bundle with a stop at infinity. The conjecture has profound implications: it implies that the Lagrangian cobordism group of T*Q is isomorphic to H_*(Q), and that the Fukaya category of T*Q is equivalent to the derived category of constructible sheaves on Q (the Nadler-Zaslow conjecture, proved by Nadler and Zaslow for Q with a fixed metric, and by Ganatra-Pardon-Shende in general). The nearby Lagrangian conjecture fails for non-exact Lagrangians (e.g., the conormal bundle of a knot in S³ is a non-exact Lagrangian in T*S³ not isotopic to the zero section) and for non-cotangent bundle symplectic manifolds (e.g., the Clifford torus in ℂP² is not Hamiltonian isotopic to a fiber).

  • Confusing ECH capacities with Gromov width: ECH capacities c_k are a sequence of capacities, not a single number. The Gromov width is c1. For ellipsoids, c_k(E(a,b)) is not simply k·c1.
  • Assuming all Lagrangian cobordisms induce isomorphisms on Floer homology: Cobordism maps are only unital ring homomorphisms on quantum homology; they can have non-trivial kernel and cokernel on Floer homology.
  • Misapplying the nearby Lagrangian conjecture to non-exact Lagrangians: The conjecture is false for non-exact Lagrangians (e.g., conormal bundles of knots). Exactness is essential for the degree 1 projection argument.
  • Overlooking the directedness condition in Fukaya-Seidel categories: The A_∞-structure is only directed after choosing an ordering of vanishing paths by argument of critical values. Without this ordering, higher products μ^k for k ≥ 3 are generally non-zero.
  • Confusing the Fukaya-Seidel category with the wrapped Fukaya category of the fiber: FS(π) is a directed subcategory of W(F); the wrapped category is the homotopy colimit over all distinguished bases (stop removal).
  • Assuming equivariant symplectic cohomology localizes to fixed points for any group action: Localization requires the action to be semifree (weights ±1) or the use of virtual localization for non-semifree actions.
  • Neglecting the role of the stop in wrapped Fukaya categories: The stop at infinity in the compactified cotangent bundle is essential for defining the wrapped category; removing the stop recovers the compact Fukaya category.

Symplectic Reduction and the Marsden–Weinstein Quotient

Let a compact Lie group G act on a symplectic manifold (M, ω) by symplectomorphisms. If the action is Hamiltonian, there exists an equivariant moment map μ : M → 𝔤* satisfying d⟨μ, ξ⟩ = ιξ_M ω for every ξ ∈ 𝔤, where ξ_M is the vector field generated by ξ. A value α ∈ 𝔤* is regular if μ⁻¹(α) is a smooth submanifold and the G-action on it is locally free. The Marsden–Weinstein theorem asserts that the quotient M//_α G = μ⁻¹(α)/G inherits a canonical symplectic form ω_α uniquely determined by π* ω_α = i* ω, where i : μ⁻¹(α) → M is the inclusion and π : μ⁻¹(α) → M//_α G the projection. When α = 0 and 0 is a regular value, the reduced space is often denoted M//G.

If α is not regular, the quotient may be an orbifold or a stratified symplectic space. The symplectic cut, introduced by Lerman, replaces a singular reduction by a smooth manifold with a Hamiltonian circle action whose reduction at a nearby level recovers the original singular quotient. Kirwan proved that the natural map H*_G(M) → H*(M//_α G) in equivariant cohomology is surjective (Kirwan surjectivity), which computes the ordinary cohomology of the reduced space from the equivariant cohomology of M. Applications include the construction of moduli spaces of flat connections on Riemann surfaces (Atiyah–Bott), the description of coadjoint orbits as symplectic quotients of T*G, and the realization of toric symplectic manifolds as reductions of ℂ^N by a torus action.

Key technical points: the moment map must be proper for the quotient to be Hausdorff; the reduced symplectic form is independent of the choice of G-invariant metric used to define a horizontal distribution; and the dimension drops by 2 dim G. In the non‑compact setting (e.g., cotangent bundles), one often uses the shifted moment map μ - α to ensure properness. The Marsden–Weinstein quotient is functorial with respect to symplectic embeddings that intertwine the group actions, a fact used in symplectic embedding problems and in the proof of the non‑squeezing theorem via reduction by a circle action.

Toric Symplectic Manifolds and Delzant Polytopes

A toric symplectic manifold is a 2n-dimensional symplectic manifold (M, ω) equipped with an effective Hamiltonian action of an n-torus T^n. The moment map μ : M → ℝ^n has image a convex polytope Δ = μ(M) known as the Delzant polytope. Delzant’s theorem (1988) establishes a bijection between compact toric symplectic manifolds (up to equivariant symplectomorphism) and Delzant polytopes (up to translation by an element of ℤ^n and the action of GL(n, ℤ)). A Delzant polytope is a rational convex polytope that is simple (exactly n facets meet at each vertex) and smooth (the primitive inward normal vectors of the facets meeting at a vertex form a ℤ-basis of ℤ^n).

Given a Delzant polytope Δ = {x ∈ ℝ^n | ⟨x, u_i⟩ ≤ λ_i, i = 1,…,d} with primitive normals u_i ∈ ℤ^n and constants λ_i ∈ ℝ, the associated symplectic manifold is obtained by symplectic reduction of ℂ^d with its standard Kähler form by the subtorus K = ker(ℤ^d → ℤ^n) determined by the exact sequence 0 → 𝔨 → ℝ^d → ℝ^n → 0 where the map ℝ^d → ℝ^n sends the standard basis to the normals u_i. The moment map for the residual T^n-action has image exactly Δ. The symplectic form on the quotient is the unique form whose cohomology class corresponds to the vector (λ1,…,λ_d) under the identification H2(M; ℝ) ≅ ℝ^d / im(𝔨*).

Standard examples: ℂP^n corresponds to the standard simplex {x_i ≥ 0, Σ x_i ≤ 1}; the Hirzebruch surface F_k corresponds to a trapezoid with normals (1,0), (0,1), (-1,0), (0,-1) and appropriate λ_i; a symplectic blow‑up of ℂP2 at a point corresponds to chopping off a corner of the triangle. The Gromov width of a toric manifold equals the minimal lattice distance from the origin to a facet of Δ. Ekeland–Hofer capacities and ECH capacities can be read off from the lattice points in dilations of Δ. Toric degenerations provide a bridge to mirror symmetry: the Landau–Ginzburg mirror of a toric Fano manifold is a Laurent polynomial whose Newton polytope is the polar dual of Δ.

Worked Example: Computing the Gromov Width of a Hirzebruch Surface
Let F1 be the Hirzebruch surface obtained by blowing up ℂP2 at one point. Its Delzant polytope can be described by the inequalities x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0, x ≤ 1, y ≤ 1 - x after an appropriate GL(2, ℤ) transformation. Determine the Gromov width of F1.
The Gromov width of a toric symplectic manifold equals the minimum over all facets of the lattice distance from the origin to the supporting hyperplane of that facet. For the given polytope, the facets are: x = 0 (distance 0), y = 0 (distance 0), x = 1 (distance 1), and y = 1 - x i.e. x + y = 1 (distance 1). The origin lies on the first two facets, so the minimal positive distance is 1. Hence the Gromov width of F1 is 1. This matches the known fact that the blow‑up of ℂP2 at a point of capacity 1 has Gromov width 1.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the Gromov width of a toric manifold, first write down the Delzant polytope inequalities. The width is the smallest positive integer k such that the polytope contains a lattice translate of the standard simplex of size k. Equivalently, compute the minimal lattice distance from the origin to any facet not containing the origin. This avoids the need to construct explicit symplectic embeddings.

Symplectic Geometry of Coadjoint Orbits and the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau Form

Let G be a compact connected Lie group with Lie algebra 𝔤. The coadjoint action of G on the dual space 𝔤* is given by (Ad*_g ξ)(η) = ξ(Adg-1 η). Each orbit O_ξ = G·ξ carries a canonical symplectic form ω_ξ, the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau (KKS) form, defined at the point ξ by ω_ξ(ad*_X ξ, ad*_Y ξ) = ξ([X, Y]) for X, Y ∈ 𝔤. This form is G-invariant, non‑degenerate, and closed; it makes every coadjoint orbit a symplectic manifold. The moment map for the Hamiltonian G-action on O_ξ is simply the inclusion O_ξ 𝔤*.

Kirillov’s orbit method proposes a correspondence between irreducible unitary representations of G and coadjoint orbits with integral symplectic form (i.e., [ω_ξ/2π] ∈ H2(O_ξ; ℤ)). For G = SU(2), the coadjoint orbits are 2‑spheres S2_r of radius r (identified with ξ of norm r). The KKS form is r times the standard area form, so the symplectic area is 4π r. Integrality forces r ∈ ½ ℕ, reproducing the spin‑j representations with j = r. The Borel–Weil theorem realizes the representation with highest weight λ as the space of holomorphic sections of the prequantum line bundle over the orbit O_λ (which is a flag manifold).

The Duistermaat–Heckman formula computes the pushforward of the Liouville measure on a Hamiltonian T-space; for a coadjoint orbit of a torus T it reduces to the statement that the moment map image is a convex polytope (the Atiyah–Guillemin–Sternberg convexity theorem) and the symplectic volume is a polynomial in the moment map parameters. In the non‑compact case (e.g., G = SL(2, ℝ)), coadjoint orbits can be hyperboloids or cones; the KKS form remains symplectic on the regular orbits, and the orbit method extends to the unitary dual via the work of Kostant and Duflo. Coadjoint orbits also appear as symplectic leaves of the Lie–Poisson structure on 𝔤*, linking symplectic geometry to Poisson geometry and integrable systems.

Hamiltonian Group Actions, Moment Maps, and the Calabi Homomorphism

The group Ham(M, ω) of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms of a symplectic manifold (M, ω) is the identity component of the symplectomorphism group when M is closed (Banyaga’s theorem). For an open manifold, Ham_c(M, ω) denotes compactly supported Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms. A Hamiltonian isotopy {φ_t} generated by a normalized Hamiltonian H_t (i.e., ∫_M H_t ω^n = 0) defines the Calabi invariant Cal(φ) = ∫01 ∫_M H_t ω^n dt. Calabi proved that this descends to a homomorphism Cal : Ham_c(M, ω) → ℝ when M is open and exact (e.g., ℝ2n or a cotangent bundle). On a closed manifold, the Calabi invariant vanishes because the integral of a normalized Hamiltonian is zero; instead one studies the flux homomorphism Flux : Symp0(M, ω) → H1(M; ℝ)/Γ_ω, where Γ_ω is the flux group. The kernel of Flux is precisely Ham(M, ω).

The Calabi homomorphism is intimately related to the Hofer metric: for φ ∈ Ham_c(M, ω), the Hofer norm satisfies |Cal(φ)| ≤ ‖φ‖_Hofer. In dimension two, the Calabi invariant coincides with the rotation number of the boundary map for annuli, and it detects elements of infinite order in Ham_c(ℝ2). Entov–Polterovich constructed quasimorphisms on Ham(M, ω) for certain closed manifolds (e.g., ℂP^n) using spectral invariants from Floer homology; these quasimorphisms are homogeneous and extend the Calabi homomorphism in the sense that they restrict to Cal on a suitable open subset. The fragmentation property of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms (any φ ∈ Ham_c(M, ω) can be written as a product of diffeomorphisms supported in arbitrarily small balls) is essential for defining the Hofer metric and for proving that Ham(M, ω) is a simple group (Banyaga).

Symplectic Embedding Problems for Polydisks and Cubes

Beyond ellipsoids, the symplectic embedding problem asks for which a, b > 0 the polydisk P(a, b) = B2(a) × B2(b) ⊂ ℝ4 symplectically embeds into the ball B4(R). The volume constraint gives ab ≤ R2/2. Gromov’s non‑squeezing theorem implies min(a, b) ≤ R. For a ≤ b, the optimal embedding is known only in certain ranges. Schlenk (2005) determined the embedding function for P(a, 1) into B4(R) when a ≤ 1, showing a piecewise linear “staircase” similar to the McDuff–Schlenk ellipsoid embedding function but with different steps. Cristofaro‑Gardiner, Holm, and Mandini (2018) extended this to all a, b using ECH capacities: the ECH capacities of P(a, b) are c_k(P(a, b)) = min{am + bn | m, n ∈ ℕ, (m+1)(n+1) ≥ k+1}. The embedding P(a, b) B4(R) exists iff c_k(P(a, b)) ≤ c_k(B4(R)) = R·⌈(√(1+4k)-1)/2⌉ for all k.

For cubes C(a) = B2(a) × B2(a), the problem reduces to finding the smallest R such that C(a) B4(R). The ECH capacities give c_k(C(a)) = a·⌈(√(1+4k)-1)/2⌉. The optimal R is known to be R = a·√2 for a ≤ 1/√2 (by a symplectic folding construction) and R = a + 1/2 for larger a (by a result of Hind and Lisi). In higher dimensions, the embedding of B2(a) × B2n-2(b) into B2n(R) is governed by the same ECH capacities when n=2, but for n>2 the problem is largely open; only volume and Gromov width constraints are known in general. The “symplectic packing” problem — placing many disjoint copies of a polydisk into a ball — is equivalent to the embedding of a disjoint union, and its asymptotic behavior is controlled by the symplectic packing stability theorem of Biran.

  • Assuming every coadjoint orbit is simply connected: orbits of non‑simply‑connected groups (e.g., SO(3)) can have fundamental group ℤ/2; the KKS form may not be integral even when the orbit is a sphere.
  • Thinking the Delzant polytope determines the symplectic form up to scaling: the polytope determines the cohomology class of ω, but different choices of λ_i in the same chamber give non‑symplectomorphic forms.
  • Overlooking the role of the moment map in symplectic reduction: the reduced space depends on the level α; different regular values can yield non‑diffeomorphic reduced spaces (wall‑crossing).
  • Misapplying ECH capacities to polydisks in dimensions >4: ECH capacities are defined only for four‑dimensional contact manifolds; higher‑dimensional embedding problems require other tools (e.g., Gutt–Hutchings capacities).

  • Confusing the symplectic cut with the ordinary Marsden–Weinstein quotient: the cut produces a smooth manifold with a circle action whose reduction at a nearby level recovers the singular quotient; it is not the same as the quotient at the singular level.

Lagrangian Floer Theory on Non‑Exact Manifolds and the Novikov Ring

When a symplectic manifold (M, ω) is not exact, the action functional used to define Floer homology becomes multi‑valued because the symplectic area of a disc with boundary on a Lagrangian L can be non‑zero. The standard remedy is to work over the universal Novikov ring Λ = { Σ a_i Tλ_i | a_i ∈ ℤ₂, λ_i ∈ ℝ, λ_i → +∞ } where the formal variable T records symplectic area. The differential counts rigid J-holomorphic strips u : ℝ × [0,1] → M with boundary on L₀, L₁ and weights each strip by TE(u) where E(u) = ∫ u*ω. This yields a chain complex CF*(L₀, L₁; Λ) whose homology HF*(L₀, L₁; Λ) is independent of the almost complex structure J and the Hamiltonian perturbation, provided one uses a consistent system of perturbations (Floer, 1988; Oh, 1993).

A key subtlety is the monotonicity condition: there exists τ > 0 such that ω(β) = τ μ(β) for every disc class β ∈ π₂(M, L), where μ is the Maslov index. For monotone Lagrangians the Novikov ring can be replaced by a polynomial ring ℤ₂[q, q⁻¹] with deg q = 2, simplifying computations. When monotonicity fails, one must keep the full Novikov ring and also address obstruction theory (Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono). The curved A∞‑algebra associated to a single Lagrangian L has a curvature term m₀ ∈ CF⁰(L, L; Λ); m₀ = 0 is the condition for L to be unobstructed and to admit a well‑defined Floer cohomology with itself. The bulk deformation by quantum cohomology classes of M can sometimes cancel the obstruction, leading to the bulk‑deformed Floer theory used in the proof of the Arnold conjecture for general symplectic manifolds.

Another important feature is the spectral invariants defined by Oh and Schwarz. For a non‑degenerate Hamiltonian H and a class a ∈ QH*(M), the spectral invariant c(a, H) is the minimal action level at which a appears in the Floer homology of H. These invariants satisfy the triangle inequality and give rise to the Hofer metric on Ham(M, ω). In the non‑exact setting the action functional is defined on the universal cover of the loop space, and the Novikov ring ensures that the action spectrum is a well‑ordered subset of ℝ, which is essential for the continuity of spectral invariants.

Applications include the proof that the displacement energy of a Lagrangian submanifold is bounded below by the minimal positive value of the spectral invariant of the fundamental class, and the construction of quasimorphisms on Ham(M, ω) via the Entov–Polterovich method. The Novikov ring also appears naturally in the definition of wrapped Fukaya categories where one allows Hamiltonians that grow quadratically at infinity; the resulting morphism spaces are modules over Λ and the category is invariant under Liouville deformation.

Symplectic Homology of Liouville Domains and the Viterbo Transfer Map

A Liouville domain (W, λ) is a compact exact symplectic manifold with boundary such that the Liouville vector field Z defined by ι_Z dλ = λ points strictly outward along ∂W. Its completion Ŵ is obtained by attaching the positive half of the symplectization ∂W × [1, ∞). Symplectic homology SH*(W) is the Floer homology of a Hamiltonian that is C²-small on W and grows linearly with slope a ∉ Spec(∂W) on the cylindrical end. The resulting chain complex splits as a direct sum of the Morse complex of W (the “negative” part) and the Floer complex of Reeb orbits on ∂W (the “positive” part). The differential counts Floer cylinders that may travel from the interior to the boundary, encoding the interaction between the two regions.

The Viterbo transfer map V : SH*(W) → Hn-*(W, ∂W) (or equivalently to the singular homology of W) is constructed by considering a continuation map from a Hamiltonian with large positive slope to a C²-small Hamiltonian that is Morse on W. On the chain level it counts solutions of a parametrized Floer equation where the slope decreases from a to 0. The map is a ring homomorphism with respect to the pair‑of‑pants product on SH* and the intersection product on homology. Viterbo proved that if W is subcritical (i.e., admits a handle decomposition without handles of index n), then SH*(W) = 0. Conversely, non‑vanishing of SH* provides an obstruction to subcriticality and to the existence of exact Lagrangian fillings of the contact boundary.

Symplectic homology satisfies a Mayer–Vietoris property for decompositions of Liouville domains along contact hypersurfaces, and it is functorial with respect to exact symplectic embeddings. The Bourgeois–Oancea isomorphism identifies the positive part of SH*(W) with the contact homology of ∂W (when the latter is defined). This isomorphism is a key ingredient in the proof that the Weinstein conjecture holds for all contact manifolds that bound a subcritical Stein domain. Moreover, the Viterbo functoriality yields a map SH*(W₁) → SH*(W₂) for any exact embedding W₁ W₂, which is used to define symplectic capacities such as the Gutt–Hutchings capacities in dimension four.

Recent developments include the definition of equivariant symplectic homology for Liouville domains with a Hamiltonian circle action, and the computation of SH* for affine varieties using the wrapped Fukaya category of their skeleton. The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence (discussed in the next section) provides a sheaf‑theoretic model for the wrapped Fukaya category of a cotangent bundle, and the induced isomorphism on Hochschild homology recovers the Viterbo isomorphism SH*(T*Q) ≅ H-*(LQ) between symplectic homology of the cotangent bundle and the homology of the free loop space of the base.

Examiner's trap
A student claims that symplectic homology SH*(W) of a Liouville domain W is always isomorphic to the singular homology of W. Give a counterexample and explain why the claim fails.
The claim is false. For a subcritical Stein domain, e.g., the standard 4‑ball B⁴, Viterbo proved SH*(B⁴) = 0 while H_*(B⁴) ≠ 0. The Viterbo transfer map SH*(W) → Hn-*(W, ∂W) is not an isomorphism in general; it is only an isomorphism for certain classes such as cotangent bundles (where SH*(T*Q) ≅ H-*(LQ)). The failure occurs because the positive part of SH* (generated by Reeb orbits on ∂W) can cancel the negative Morse part, leading to vanishing even when the topology of W is non‑trivial.
Exam‑day strategy
When asked about symplectic homology, first state the definition via the Hamiltonian slope and the splitting into negative/positive parts. Then recall the Viterbo transfer map and its functoriality. Mention the subcritical vanishing theorem and the Bourgeois–Oancea isomorphism as two standard results that examiners often test. If a computation is required, identify whether the domain is subcritical or a cotangent bundle, because these are the two cases with complete answers.

The Fukaya Category of Cotangent Bundles and the Nadler–Zaslow Correspondence

The wrapped Fukaya category W(T*Q) of a cotangent bundle T*Q (with its canonical exact symplectic structure) is generated by the zero section Q and the cotangent fibers T*_q Q. Abouzaid proved that the wrapped Fukaya category of T*Q is quasi‑equivalent to the derived category of constructible sheaves on Q with microsupport contained in the zero section. Nadler and Zaslow gave a more geometric formulation: there is an equivalence between the (compact) Fukaya category of T*Q generated by exact Lagrangians that are Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero section and the category of locally constant sheaves on Q. The wrapped version extends this to all exact Lagrangians with Legendrian boundary at infinity, corresponding to constructible sheaves with arbitrary microsupport.

On the symplectic side, morphisms between two exact Lagrangians L₀, L₁ are given by the wrapped Floer complex CW*(L₀, L₁), which uses a Hamiltonian that grows quadratically in the fiber direction. The differential counts J-holomorphic strips with moving boundary conditions determined by the Hamiltonian flow. The A∞‑structure comes from counting polygons with one output and several inputs. On the sheaf side, morphisms are given by the derived Hom of constructible sheaves, and the A∞‑structure is induced by the convolution of microsupports.

The equivalence is constructed by sending a Lagrangian L to its microlocal sheaf μ_L, a constructible sheaf on Q whose stalk at q is the Floer cohomology HF*(L, T*_q Q). The key technical input is the generation criterion: the cotangent fibers generate W(T*Q), and any exact Lagrangian is a twisted complex of fibers. This allows one to compute wrapped Floer cohomology entirely in terms of sheaf cohomology. As a consequence, the Hochschild homology of W(T*Q) is isomorphic to the homology of the free loop space LQ, recovering Viterbo’s isomorphism SH*(T*Q) ≅ H-*(LQ).

Applications include the proof of the nearby Lagrangian conjecture for cotangent bundles (every exact Lagrangian in T*Q is Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero section) by Abouzaid–Kragh, and the computation of wrapped Fukaya categories for Weinstein manifolds that admit a Lagrangian skeleton (Ganatra–Pardon–Shende). The sheaf‑theoretic model also makes the stop removal operation transparent: removing a stop from the Legendrian boundary corresponds to allowing microsupport to cross a given hypersurface in Q.

Symplectic Reduction at Singular Levels and Stratified Symplectic Spaces

The classical Marsden–Weinstein quotient assumes a free and proper action of a Lie group G on a symplectic manifold (M, ω) with a moment map μ : M → 𝔤* such that 0 is a regular value. When the action has isotropy, the level set μ⁻¹(0) is singular and the quotient μ⁻¹(0)/G inherits a stratified symplectic structure (Sjamaar–Lerman, 1991). Each stratum corresponds to an orbit type (H) and is a symplectic manifold; the symplectic forms on different strata are compatible with the inclusions of closures.

The symplectic cut (Lerman, 1995) provides a way to replace the singular quotient by a smooth symplectic manifold with a Hamiltonian circle action. One forms the product M × ℂ with the diagonal circle action and reduces at a regular level ε > 0. The resulting cut space M_ε contains the original reduced space μ⁻¹(0)/G as a symplectic subvariety, and the exceptional divisor is a projective bundle over the fixed‑point set of the circle action. This construction is essential for defining Gromov–Witten invariants of singular quotients via localization.

In the context of geometric invariant theory (GIT), the symplectic quotient of a Kähler manifold by a complex reductive group coincides with the GIT quotient of the stable locus. The Kempf–Ness theorem identifies the symplectic quotient with the complex quotient, and the stratification by orbit types matches the Harder–Narasimhan stratification of the unstable locus. This correspondence underlies the computation of quantum cohomology of moduli spaces of bundles via the quantum Kirwan map (Woodward, 1998).

For non‑compact groups or non‑proper actions, one uses the symplectic implosion (Guillemin–Jeffrey–Sjamaar) which replaces the quotient by a stratified space with a torus action that is “as close to free as possible”. The imploded cross‑section carries a Poisson structure that restricts to symplectic forms on strata. This technique has been applied to the study of moduli spaces of flat connections on surfaces with boundary, where the moment map is the holonomy around the boundary and the imploded space parametrizes all possible holonomies.

The Contact Mapping Class Group and the Giroux Correspondence in Higher Dimensions

The contact mapping class group π₀(Cont(M, ξ)) of a contact manifold (M, ξ) is the group of isotopy classes of contactomorphisms. In dimension three, the Giroux correspondence establishes a bijection between contact structures on a closed oriented 3‑manifold up to isotopy and open book decompositions up to positive stabilization. This reduces the study of π₀(Cont(M, ξ)) to the mapping class group of the page with boundary, modulo the subgroup generated by right‑handed Dehn twists along binding curves. The correspondence also implies that every contact 3‑manifold admits a compatible open book, and that the contact mapping class group is generated by the monodromy of the open book together with the Giroux torsion elements.

In higher dimensions, a contact manifold (M2n-1, ξ) admits an open book decomposition if there exists a codimension‑2 submanifold B (the binding) with trivial normal bundle and a fibration π : M B → S¹ whose fibers are Liouville domains with contact boundary ξ|_B. Giroux proved that every contact manifold carries an open book, and that two open books define isotopic contact structures iff they are related by a sequence of positive stabilizations (adding a 1‑handle to the page and composing the monodromy with a right‑handed Dehn twist along the new vanishing cycle). The contact mapping class group then fits into an exact sequence involving the mapping class group of the page relative to the boundary and the group of contactomorphisms of the binding.

Recent work by Casals–Murphy–Presas and others has extended the Giroux correspondence to overtwisted contact structures in all dimensions, showing that the homotopy class of an almost contact structure determines the overtwisted contact structure up to isotopy. For tight contact structures, the classification remains open in general, but the flexible Weinstein domains (Cieliebak–Eliashberg) correspond to overtwisted contact boundaries, and their symplectic homology vanishes. The contact mapping class group of a flexible contact manifold is therefore closely related to the symplectic mapping class group of its flexible filling.

An important invariant is the contact homology algebra of the binding, which is functorial under positive stabilization. The Bourgeois–Oancea isomorphism identifies the linearized contact homology of the binding with the positive part of the symplectic homology of the page. This provides a bridge between the algebraic topology of the contact mapping class group and the symplectic invariants of the filling, and it is a key tool in the proof that the Weinstein conjecture holds for all contact manifolds that bound a subcritical Stein domain.

Symplectic Topology of Moduli Spaces of Flat Connections and the Atiyah–Bott Form

The moduli space M of flat G-connections on a closed oriented surface Σ of genus g ≥ 2 carries a natural symplectic structure discovered by Atiyah and Bott. The tangent space at a flat connection A is identified with the first cohomology group H¹(Σ; ad A), and the symplectic form ωAB is given by the cup product combined with the Killing form on the Lie algebra 𝔤. Explicitly, for a, b ∈ H¹(Σ; ad A), ωAB(a, b) = ∫Σ κ(a ∧ b), where κ is an invariant bilinear form. This form is non‑degenerate and closed, making M a finite‑dimensional symplectic manifold of dimension (2g−2) dim G. The mapping class group of Σ acts by symplectomorphisms on M, and the moment map for this action is the holonomy around the boundary when Σ has boundary. The symplectic quotient of the space of all connections by the gauge group yields M as a Marsden–Weinstein reduction, and the Atiyah–Bott form coincides with the reduced symplectic form. When G = SU(2), the moduli space is a compact Kähler manifold, and its symplectic volume can be computed by the Verlinde formula. The cohomology ring of M is generated by universal characteristic classes, and the symplectic form represents the first Chern class of the determinant line bundle. These structures underpin the geometric Langlands correspondence and the study of topological quantum field theories.

Symplectic Geometry of Higgs Bundles and the Hitchin System

A Higgs bundle on a Riemann surface Σ is a pair (E, φ) where E is a holomorphic vector bundle and φ ∈ H⁰(Σ, End(E) ⊗ KΣ) is the Higgs field. The moduli space MHiggs of stable Higgs bundles of fixed rank and degree carries a hyperkähler structure. In complex structure I it is the moduli of Higgs bundles; in complex structure J it is the character variety of flat connections; in complex structure K it is the moduli of solutions to Hitchin's equations. The Hitchin fibration h : MHiggs → B = ⊕i=1r H⁰(Σ, KΣi) sends a Higgs bundle to the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of φ. The generic fiber is an abelian variety (a Prym variety of the spectral curve), and the fibration is an algebraically completely integrable Hamiltonian system. The symplectic form ωI in complex structure I makes the Hitchin map a Lagrangian fibration: the fibers are Lagrangian submanifolds with respect to ωI. The action variables are given by periods of the Seiberg–Witten differential on the spectral curve. The base B is an affine space, and the discriminant locus where the spectral curve becomes singular has codimension one. The topology of MHiggs is encoded in the perverse Leray filtration, which matches the weight filtration on the character variety via non‑abelian Hodge theory. This correspondence is a cornerstone of the geometric Langlands program and mirror symmetry for moduli spaces.

Worked Example: Computing the Dimension of a Hitchin Fiber
Let Σ be a genus 3 surface and consider rank 2 Higgs bundles with trivial determinant. Determine the dimension of a generic Hitchin fiber and verify that it is Lagrangian in the symplectic manifold MHiggs.
The base B = H⁰(Σ, K²) has dimension 3g−3 = 6 by Riemann–Roch. The moduli space MHiggs has dimension 2 × (2g−2) × (rank²−1) = 2 × 4 × 3 = 24. A generic fiber is an abelian variety of dimension half the dimension of MHiggs, i.e. 12. Since the Hitchin map is a Lagrangian fibration, the fiber is a Lagrangian submanifold: its dimension equals half the dimension of the ambient symplectic manifold, and the restriction of ωI to the fiber vanishes. This matches the general fact that the fibers are Lagrangian tori in the integrable system.

Symplectic Field Theory for Higher Genus and the Gromov–Witten Invariants

Symplectic Field Theory (SFT) extends Gromov–Witten theory to punctured holomorphic curves in symplectizations of contact manifolds. For a closed symplectic manifold (W, ω), the genus‑g Gromov–Witten invariants count stable maps from genus‑g Riemann surfaces with marked points into W. In SFT, one considers the symplectization ℝ × Y of a contact manifold (Y, ξ) and counts holomorphic curves with positive and negative punctures asymptotic to Reeb orbits. The differential in the SFT algebra counts genus‑0 curves with one positive puncture; higher genus contributions appear in the master equation governing the full SFT Hamiltonian. The compactification of the moduli space of punctured curves uses the SFT compactness theorem (Bourgeois–Eliashberg–Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder), which adds broken curves and nodal curves with components in the symplectization and in the completed cobordism. The resulting algebraic structure is a differential graded Lie algebra (or an A∞‑algebra) whose homology is the contact homology. For higher genus, the Gromov–Witten potentials satisfy the topological recursion relations and the holomorphic anomaly equation. The relation between SFT and Gromov–Witten theory is made precise by the stretching‑the‑neck technique: degenerating a symplectic manifold along a contact hypersurface yields a symplectic cobordism, and the Gromov–Witten invariants of the original manifold can be expressed in terms of SFT invariants of the contact boundary. This provides a powerful tool for computing Gromov–Witten invariants of symplectic manifolds with contact type boundaries, such as projective hypersurfaces.

Symplectic Topology of Lagrangian Correspondences and the Fukaya Category of Correspondences

A Lagrangian correspondence from a symplectic manifold (M1, ω1) to (M2, ω2) is a Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ M1⁻ × M2, where M1⁻ denotes M1 with the opposite symplectic form. Composition of correspondences is defined by fiber product: given L12 ⊂ M1⁻ × M2 and L23 ⊂ M2⁻ × M3, their composite is the image of (L12 × L23) ∩ (M1⁻ × ΔM2 × M3) under projection to M1⁻ × M3, provided the intersection is transverse. This composition is associative up to Hamiltonian isotopy. The Fukaya category of correspondences, introduced by Wehrheim and Woodward, has objects symplectic manifolds and morphism spaces the Fukaya categories of the product manifolds. A Lagrangian correspondence defines a functor between Fukaya categories via the geometric composition (the “pull‑push” operation). This framework categorifies the symplectic category, where morphisms are Lagrangian correspondences modulo Hamiltonian isotopy. The main technical tool is the quilted Floer homology, which counts pseudoholomorphic quilts — maps from a surface with strip‑like ends and seams labeled by correspondences. The quilted Floer complex computes the morphism spaces in the Fukaya category of correspondences. This construction underlies the proof of the symplectic invariance of the Fukaya category under symplectic reduction and the formulation of the homological mirror symmetry conjecture for stacks.

Symplectic Duality and the Symplectic Geometry of 3d Mirror Symmetry

Symplectic duality is a relationship between two symplectic resolutions X and X! of the same affine conical symplectic singularity X0. The pair (X, X!) is expected to be related by a 3d mirror symmetry of the associated supersymmetric gauge theories. Geometrically, the Coulomb branch of one theory is the Higgs branch of the mirror, and vice versa. The symplectic duality conjecture (Braverman–Finkelberg–Nakajima) predicts an equivalence between the category of coherent sheaves on X and the category of modules over the quantized algebra of functions on X!, and a similar equivalence with roles swapped. The symplectic form on X and X! is the holomorphic symplectic form coming from the conical structure. The quantization is given by the Rees algebra of the filtration by the ℂ×‑action. The duality exchanges the Kähler parameters of X with the equivariant parameters of X!. In the case of hypertoric varieties, symplectic duality reduces to Gale duality of hyperplane arrangements. For Nakajima quiver varieties, the dual variety is the quiver variety of the transpose quiver. The symplectic duality also implies a relation between the elliptic genera of the two theories, which can be computed via localization on the fixed points of the torus action. This duality provides a geometric framework for the 3d mirror symmetry discovered by Intriligator and Seiberg, and it has been verified in many examples using the Coulomb branch construction of Braverman–Finkelberg–Nakajima.

Symplectic Groupoids and the Integration of Poisson Manifolds

A symplectic groupoid is a Lie groupoid G ⇉ M equipped with a symplectic form ω on G such that the graph of the multiplication map is a Lagrangian submanifold of G⁻ × G⁻ × G. The base M inherits a Poisson structure π uniquely determined by the condition that the source map s: G → M is Poisson and the target map t: G → M is anti‑Poisson. Conversely, a Poisson manifold (M, π) is said to be integrable if there exists a symplectic groupoid integrating it. The integrability criterion of Crainic and Fernandes (2003) states that (M, π) is integrable iff the monodromy groups of the cotangent Lie algebroid T*M are uniformly discrete. When integrable, the Weinstein groupoid — constructed as the quotient of the path space of the cotangent algebroid by the homotopy equivalence — is the unique source‑simply‑connected symplectic groupoid integrating (M, π).

The symplectic form on a symplectic groupoid is multiplicative: m*ω = pr₁*ω + pr₂*ω on the space of composable arrows. This multiplicativity implies that the unit section u: M → G is Lagrangian and the inversion map i: G → G is anti‑symplectic. The Lie algebroid of G is canonically isomorphic to the cotangent Lie algebroid T*M with anchor π♯: T*M → TM and bracket the Koszul bracket. The integration procedure therefore provides a geometric realisation of the Poisson bracket as the Hamiltonian flow of functions on the base lifted to the groupoid.

Important examples include the pair groupoid T*M ⇉ M for a symplectic manifold (M, ω) with the canonical symplectic form on T*M, the action groupoid G × M ⇉ M for a Hamiltonian G‑action with the symplectic form induced from the moment map, and the symplectic groupoid of a linear Poisson structure on the dual of a Lie algebra g*, which is the cotangent bundle T*G equipped with the canonical symplectic form twisted by the Lie–Poisson structure. The theory of symplectic groupoids underlies the deformation quantisation of Poisson manifolds via the convolution algebra of half‑densities on G, and it provides the geometric framework for the Poisson sigma model of Cattaneo and Felder.

The Duistermaat–Heckman Theorem and Exact Stationary Phase in Symplectic Geometry

The Duistermaat–Heckman theorem gives an exact formula for the push‑forward of the Liouville measure under a Hamiltonian torus action. Let (M, ω) be a compact symplectic manifold of dimension 2n with an effective Hamiltonian action of a torus T of dimension k, and let μ: M → t* be a moment map. For a regular value ξ ∈ t*, the reduced space M_ξ = μ⁻¹(ξ)/T is a symplectic orbifold. The theorem states that the symplectic volume of M_ξ is a piecewise polynomial function of ξ of degree n−k, and that the Fourier transform of the Liouville measure on M is given by a sum over the fixed point components F of the T‑action:

∫_M ei⟨μ, X⟩ ω^n/n! = ∑_F ei⟨μ(F), X⟩ (2π)dim F/2 / ∏α∈N_F (i⟨α, X⟩)

where N_F denotes the weights of the isotropy representation on the normal bundle of F. This formula is an instance of the exact stationary phase principle: the oscillatory integral localises to the critical set of the phase function ⟨μ, X⟩, which is precisely the fixed point set. The theorem has profound consequences for equivariant cohomology. The equivariant symplectic form ω − μ defines a class in the Cartan model H_T^*(M), and the Duistermaat–Heckman measure is the push‑forward of the equivariant volume form. The polynomial behaviour of the volume function reflects the fact that the equivariant cohomology class is a polynomial in the equivariant parameters.

Applications include the computation of volumes of moduli spaces of flat connections (via the symplectic reduction of the space of connections), the proof of the convexity theorem of Atiyah and Guillemin–Sternberg (the moment map image is a convex polytope), and the derivation of the Weyl character formula for compact Lie groups by applying the theorem to coadjoint orbits. In the context of geometric quantisation, the Duistermaat–Heckman formula gives the exact asymptotics of the dimension of the space of holomorphic sections of a prequantum line bundle as the tensor power tends to infinity, confirming the “quantisation commutes with reduction” principle of Guillemin and Sternberg.

Technique: Localising Oscillatory Integrals via Fixed Points
Let (M, ω) = (CP², ω_FS) with the standard Hamiltonian T²‑action given by (eiθ₁, eiθ₂)·[z₀:z₁:z₂] = [z₀:eiθ₁z₁:eiθ₂z₂]. The moment map is μ([z]) = (|z₁|², |z₂|²)/(|z₀|²+|z₁|²+|z₂|²). Compute the Duistermaat–Heckman measure on the moment polytope Δ = {(x,y) ∈ ℝ² | x≥0, y≥0, x+y≤1}.
The fixed points are the three coordinate points: p₀=[1:0:0] with μ(p₀)=(0,0), p₁=[0:1:0] with μ(p₁)=(1,0), p₂=[0:0:1] with μ(p₂)=(0,1). The isotropy weights at p₀ are (1,0) and (0,1); at p₁ they are (−1,0) and (1,−1); at p₂ they are (0,−1) and (−1,1). Applying the localisation formula for the push‑forward of the Liouville measure gives a piecewise polynomial density on Δ. On the interior of Δ the density is constant 1 (since the symplectic volume of CP² is 1/2 and the area of Δ is 1/2). The Duistermaat–Heckman measure is exactly the Lebesgue measure on Δ, confirming that the symplectic volume of the reduced space at a regular value (x,y) is 1−x−y, a linear polynomial in x,y.

Remember that the Duistermaat–Heckman theorem holds for orbifold reduced spaces; the polynomial pieces glue continuously across walls. Symplectic Topology of Hamiltonian Circle Actions and the GKM Theory

Goresky–Kottwitz–MacPherson (GKM) theory provides a combinatorial description of the equivariant cohomology of a space with a torus action whose fixed point set is isolated and whose one‑dimensional orbit closures are two‑spheres. In symplectic geometry, a Hamiltonian circle action on a compact symplectic manifold (M, ω) with isolated fixed points satisfies the GKM conditions if the weights of the isotropy representation at each fixed point are pairwise linearly independent. The GKM graph has vertices corresponding to fixed points and edges corresponding to invariant two‑spheres connecting them; each edge is labeled by the weight of the circle action on the normal bundle of the sphere.

The equivariant cohomology HS¹^*(M; ℚ) is then isomorphic to the subring of ⊕p∈MS¹ ℚ[t] consisting of tuples (f_p) such that for every edge e connecting p and q with weight α_e, the difference f_p − f_q is divisible by α_e t. This description makes the equivariant cohomology ring computable purely from the labelled graph. The ordinary cohomology H^*(M; ℚ) is obtained by setting t=0, and the GKM graph encodes the cup product structure via the moment map image: the moment map μ: M → ℝ is a Morse–Bott function whose critical points are the fixed points, and the indices are twice the number of negative weights.

Applications include the computation of the cohomology rings of Hamiltonian toric manifolds (where the GKM graph is the 1‑skeleton of the moment polytope), the proof of the Hard Lefschetz theorem for symplectic manifolds with Hamiltonian circle actions (McDuff–Tolman), and the classification of symplectic manifolds with Hamiltonian circle actions and isolated fixed points in low dimensions (Tolman–Weitsman). The GKM framework also extends to non‑abelian group actions via the Chang–Skjelbred lemma, and to the equivariant quantum cohomology where the graph is decorated with Gromov–Witten invariants of the invariant spheres.

Symplectic Cobordisms and the Symplectic Category of Cobordisms

A symplectic cobordism from a contact manifold (Y₋, ξ₋) to (Y₊, ξ₊) is a compact symplectic manifold (W, ω) with boundary ∂W = Y₋ ∪ Y₊ such that ω|ξ₋ > 0 and ω|ξ₊ > 0, and there exists a Liouville vector field V defined near the boundary pointing outward along Y₊ and inward along Y₋. The symplectic category Cobord has objects contact manifolds and morphisms symplectic cobordisms modulo symplectomorphisms relative to the boundary. Composition is given by gluing along common contact boundaries, which is well defined up to symplectic deformation equivalence because the Liouville vector fields match to give a smooth symplectic form on the glued manifold.

The symplectic category is a symmetric monoidal category under disjoint union. It receives a functor from the Weinstein category (objects Weinstein domains, morphisms exact symplectic cobordisms) and maps to the contact category (objects contact manifolds, morphisms contactomorphisms) by taking the positive boundary. The symplectic homology SH_*(W) of a Liouville domain W is functorial with respect to exact symplectic cobordisms: a cobordism W₋ → W₊ induces a map SH_*(W₋) → SH_*(W₊). This functoriality is a cornerstone of Viterbo’s functoriality and underlies the proof that symplectic homology is an invariant of the contact boundary.

Important constructions include the symplectisation ℝ × Y of a contact manifold, which is a symplectic cobordism from ∅ to Y, and the completion of a Liouville domain by attaching its symplectisation. The existence of a symplectic cobordism between two contact manifolds implies an inequality of their symplectic capacities (e.g., the Ekeland–Hofer capacities). Conversely, the non‑existence of certain cobordisms yields obstructions to symplectic embeddings, such as the non‑squeezing theorem of Gromov reinterpreted as the non‑existence of a symplectic cobordism from a ball to a cylinder of smaller radius. The symplectic category also provides the natural setting for the definition of symplectic field theory (SFT) as a functor from Cobord to the category of chain complexes.

Relative Fukaya Categories and Lagrangian Boundary Conditions

The relative Fukaya category Fuk(M, L) of a symplectic manifold M with a Lagrangian submanifold L (possibly with boundary) is an A_∞‑category whose objects are Lagrangian branes in M that are either disjoint from L or intersect L cleanly along their boundary. Morphism spaces are given by Lagrangian intersection Floer complexes, but the differential counts pseudoholomorphic strips with boundary on the two Lagrangians and with corners mapping to L. When L is a Legendrian submanifold of the contact boundary of a Liouville domain, the relative Fukaya category is quasi‑equivalent to the wrapped Fukaya category of the complement M L, as shown by Ganatra–Pardon–Shende.

The construction uses sectorial conditions on the Lagrangians to ensure compactness of the moduli spaces of pseudoholomorphic polygons. A key technical tool is the “stop removal” theorem: adding a Legendrian stop to the contact boundary corresponds to taking a quotient of the wrapped Fukaya category by the subcategory generated by the stop. The relative Fukaya category thus interpolates between the compact Fukaya category (no stops) and the fully wrapped Fukaya category (maximal stop). The A_∞‑structure maps are defined by counting pseudoholomorphic polygons with boundary on the given Lagrangians and with interior punctures asymptotic to Reeb chords on the stop.

Applications include the computation of the wrapped Fukaya category of cotangent bundles T*Q via the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence (which identifies it with the category of constructible sheaves on Q), the proof of the generation criterion for the wrapped Fukaya category (Abouzaid), and the formulation of the homological mirror symmetry conjecture for log Calabi–Yau varieties where the stop corresponds to the divisor at infinity. The relative Fukaya category also provides a categorical framework for Lagrangian cobordisms: a Lagrangian cobordism between Legendrians Λ₋ and Λ₊ in the symplectisation ℝ × Y defines a functor between the relative Fukaya categories of the corresponding stops.

The Symplectic Mapping Class Group and the Flux Homomorphism

The symplectic mapping class group of a closed symplectic manifold (M, ω) is the group of connected components π₀(Symp(M, ω)) = Symp(M, ω) / Symp₀(M, ω), where Symp₀ denotes the identity component. A central tool for studying this group is the flux homomorphism Flux : Symp₀(M, ω) → H¹(M; ℝ) / Γ_ω. Given a smooth path {φ_t} in Symp₀ with φ₀ = id, its flux is the cohomology class of the 1‑form ∫₀¹ ιX_t ω dt, where X_t is the time‑dependent vector field generating the path. The subgroup Γ_ω ⊂ H¹(M; ℝ) consists of fluxes of loops in Symp₀; it is known as the flux group. Banyaga proved that Γ_ω is a discrete lattice, so the flux homomorphism descends to a well‑defined map on the universal cover Symp₀̃ → H¹(M; ℝ) with kernel the universal cover of the Hamiltonian group Ham(M, ω). Consequently, Ham(M, ω) is exactly the kernel of Flux on Symp₀, and we have a short exact sequence 0 → Ham(M, ω) → Symp₀(M, ω) → H¹(M; ℝ) / Γ_ω → 0. The flux group is trivial for simply connected manifolds, but can be non‑trivial for tori and higher genus surfaces. For surfaces, the symplectic mapping class group coincides with the classical mapping class group, and the flux homomorphism recovers the action on homology. In higher dimensions, the structure of π₀(Symp) is far less understood; results of McDuff, Polterovich, and others show that it can be infinite even for simply connected manifolds such as certain blow‑ups of ℂP². The Calabi homomorphism on Ham for open manifolds provides a further refinement, but it vanishes on closed manifolds. Understanding the flux homomorphism is essential for distinguishing symplectomorphisms that are not Hamiltonian and for computing the symplectic mapping class group in concrete examples.

Symplectic Embedding Problems for Ellipsoids and the ECH Capacities

An ellipsoid in ℝ⁴ is defined as E(a, b) = { (z₁, z₂) | π|z₁|²/a + π|z₂|²/b < 1 } with 0 < a ≤ b. The fundamental question is: for which c does there exist a symplectic embedding E(a, b) B(c), where B(c) is the ball of capacity c? Embedded Contact Homology (ECH) provides a sequence of symplectic capacities c_k that are monotone under embeddings. For an ellipsoid E(a, b), the ECH capacities are the numbers ma + nb with m, n ∈ ℕ₀, sorted in non‑decreasing order with multiplicities. For the ball B(c), the capacities are simply kc for k ∈ ℕ₀. The embedding obstruction is c_k(E(a, b)) ≤ c_k(B(c)) for all k. This condition is not only necessary but also sufficient for four‑dimensional ellipsoids into balls, a theorem of Cristofaro‑Gardiner, Holm, Mandini, and Pires building on work of Hutchings and Taubes. The resulting embedding function f(a) = inf{ c | E(1, a) B(c) } exhibits a remarkable Fibonacci staircase: for a in certain intervals, f(a) is given by a linear function whose slopes are ratios of consecutive Fibonacci numbers. The critical values of a are the squares of ratios of Fibonacci numbers, and the function is piecewise linear with infinitely many steps accumulating at the golden ratio. This structure was first discovered by McDuff and Schlenk using ECH capacities and later proved to be sharp by the work of Frenkel and Müller. In dimensions higher than four, ECH capacities are not defined, and the embedding problem for ellipsoids remains largely open; only volume and Gromov width give general obstructions.

Technique
Compute the first three positive ECH capacities of the ellipsoid E(1, 3) and decide whether it symplectically embeds into the ball B(2).
The ECH capacities of E(a, b) are the sorted list of numbers ma + nb (m, n ≥ 0). For a = 1, b = 3 the set is {0, 1, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, …}. The first three positive capacities are 1, 2, 3. For the ball B(2) the capacities are 0, 2, 4, 6, … . The embedding condition requires c_k(E) ≤ c_k(B) for all k. Checking k = 1,2,3 gives 1 ≤ 2, 2 ≤ 4, 3 ≤ 6, all satisfied. Since the ECH capacities give a complete obstruction for ellipsoids into balls in dimension four, E(1, 3) does embed into B(2).
Exam‑day strategy
When asked about ellipsoid embeddings, first write down the lattice‑point formula for ECH capacities. Compare the two sequences term by term; if any capacity of the domain exceeds the corresponding capacity of the target, the embedding is impossible. Memorise the first few Fibonacci ratios (1, 2, 3/2, 5/3, 8/5, …) because they determine the steps of the embedding function for E(1, a) into a ball.

Weinstein Manifolds, Flexible and Non‑flexible Structures

A Weinstein manifold is an exact symplectic manifold (W, ω = dλ) equipped with a Liouville vector field Z (i.e. ℒ_Z ω = ω) that is gradient‑like for an exhausting Morse function φ : W → ℝ. The pair (Z, φ) gives a handle decomposition of W where each critical point of φ corresponds to a Weinstein handle of index equal to the Morse index. A handle of index k is called flexible if k < n (where dim W = 2n); otherwise it is non‑flexible (index n). The flexible h‑principle of Cieliebak and Eliashberg states that any formal Weinstein structure built only from flexible handles is homotopic through formal structures to a genuine Weinstein structure, and any two such structures are isotopic. Consequently, flexible Weinstein manifolds have no symplectic invariants beyond their homotopy type; their wrapped Fukaya category is generated by the core Lagrangian disks of the handles, and their symplectic homology vanishes. In contrast, non‑flexible handles carry rich symplectic invariants: the symplectic homology, the wrapped Fukaya category, and Legendrian contact homology of the attaching Legendrian spheres. Classic examples of non‑flexible Weinstein manifolds are cotangent bundles T*Q (the zero section is a non‑flexible Lagrangian) and certain Stein domains such as the Milnor fibre of an A_k singularity. The distinction is crucial for classification: up to deformation equivalence, flexible Weinstein structures are classified by their homotopy class of almost complex structures, while non‑flexible structures require subtle Legendrian isotopy data. Recent work of Eliashberg, Ganatra, and Lazarev shows that the flexible/non‑flexible dichotomy governs the behaviour of the wrapped Fukaya category under handle attachment, providing a powerful tool for computations.

Contact Surgery, Weinstein Handle Attachment, and the Flexible/Non‑flexible Dichotomy

Contact surgery along a Legendrian submanifold Λ ⊂ (Y, ξ) of dimension n‑1 in a contact manifold of dimension 2n‑1 is the contact‑geometric counterpart of attaching a Weinstein handle of index n to a symplectic filling. The surgery is performed by removing a standard neighbourhood of Λ (contactomorphic to the unit cosphere bundle S*Λ) and gluing back a contact manifold diffeomorphic to D^n × Sn‑1 with a specific contact structure determined by the Legendrian framing (the choice of a trivialisation of the contact normal bundle). When Λ is a Legendrian sphere, the resulting contact manifold is the boundary of the Weinstein domain obtained by attaching a handle to the filling. If the handle index is < n (i.e. the Legendrian has dimension < n‑1), the handle is flexible and the contact surgery does not change the contact homology or the wrapped Fukaya category of the filling; this is the content of the flexible h‑principle for contact surgery. For index n handles (Legendrian spheres of dimension n‑1), the surgery is non‑flexible: the contact homology can change, and the Legendrian contact homology differential graded algebra of Λ encodes the effect on the symplectic invariants. A fundamental example is the standard contact sphere S2n‑1, which is flexible and bounds the flexible Weinstein ball. In contrast, the boundary of a cotangent bundle T*Q is non‑flexible; contact surgery on a Legendrian sphere in ∂T*Q can produce exotic contact structures detected by Legendrian contact homology. The dichotomy also appears in the Weinstein conjecture: flexible contact manifolds always admit a Reeb orbit (by the work of Eliashberg–Kim–Polterovich), while non‑flexible ones may require additional hypotheses. Understanding the interplay between Legendrian framing, handle index, and flexibility is essential for constructing and distinguishing contact and symplectic manifolds.

The Symplectic Topology of Lagrangian Skeletons and Arboreal Singularities

A Lagrangian skeleton of a Weinstein manifold W is a Lagrangian subcomplex Λ ⊂ W that is a deformation retract of W and carries the topology of the wrapped Fukaya category. The skeleton is obtained by flowing the unstable manifolds of the Morse function φ along the Liouville vector field; it is a union of the core Lagrangian disks of the Weinstein handles. In general, Λ has singularities where handles intersect. An arboreal Lagrangian is one whose singularities are locally modeled on the Lagrangian skeleton of a Weinstein sector, i.e. on the union of coordinate planes in ℂ^n with appropriate Lagrangian boundary conditions. The arborealization theorem of Ganatra, Pardon, and Shende states that after an arbitrarily small perturbation, any Weinstein manifold admits an arboreal skeleton. The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence then identifies the wrapped Fukaya category W(W) with the category of constructible sheaves on the arboreal skeleton Λ (with microsupport conditions). This equivalence provides a completely combinatorial model for the Fukaya category: objects are constructible sheaves, morphisms are given by Ext groups, and the A_∞‑structure is encoded by the topology of the skeleton. For example, the skeleton of the cotangent bundle T*Q is the zero section Q; its arborealization yields the conormal bundle, and the wrapped Fukaya category is equivalent to constructible sheaves on Q. More generally, the skeleton of a plumbing of cotangent bundles according to a graph gives a Lagrangian skeleton whose constructible sheaf category recovers the Fukaya category of the plumbing. This approach has led to proofs of generation criteria, computations of Hochschild homology, and a geometric formulation of homological mirror symmetry for log Calabi–Yau varieties where the skeleton corresponds to the tropical limit. The theory of arboreal singularities thus bridges symplectic topology, microlocal sheaf theory, and tropical geometry.

Algebraic Torsion and the Classification of Overtwisted Contact Structures

Algebraic torsion is a contact invariant introduced by Latschev and Wendl that obstructs the existence of strong symplectic fillings. For a contact manifold (Y, ξ), algebraic torsion of order k is defined via the vanishing of certain maps in the cylindrical contact homology chain complex associated to a non-degenerate contact form. If AT(Y, ξ) = k, then any strong symplectic filling of (Y, ξ) must have vanishing symplectic homology in degrees up to k. The fundamental result is that overtwisted contact structures have algebraic torsion of order 1, while tight contact structures on closed 3-manifolds have no algebraic torsion. This provides a purely algebraic criterion to distinguish overtwisted from tight structures without analyzing the contact structure geometrically. In higher dimensions, Borman, Eliashberg, and Murphy proved that overtwisted contact structures are classified by their homotopy class of almost contact structures, and algebraic torsion detects this flexibility: a contact manifold with algebraic torsion admits a flexible Weinstein filling. The computation of algebraic torsion for Brieskorn manifolds Σ(a₀,…,aₙ) revealed that certain contact structures on these manifolds are tight but fillable, while others are overtwisted. The invariant is functorial under exact symplectic cobordisms: if (Y₋, ξ₋) embeds into (Y₊, ξ₊) via an exact cobordism, then AT(Y₋, ξ₋) ≥ AT(Y₊, ξ₊). This monotonicity property makes algebraic torsion a powerful tool for proving non-existence of exact cobordisms between contact manifolds. For example, it shows that the standard contact sphere S²ⁿ⁻¹ cannot be exactly cobordant to any contact manifold with non-zero algebraic torsion. The relationship between algebraic torsion and the contact homology algebra is subtle: vanishing of the contact homology differential on certain generators implies algebraic torsion, but the converse requires additional hypotheses on the Reeb dynamics.

The Symplectic Topology of Lagrangian Torus Fibrations and the SYZ Conjecture

The Strominger–Yau–Zaslow (SYZ) conjecture proposes that mirror symmetry for Calabi–Yau manifolds can be understood geometrically as a duality between special Lagrangian torus fibrations. A special Lagrangian submanifold L in a Calabi–Yau manifold (X, ω, Ω) satisfies ω|_L = 0 and Im(eiθΩ)|_L = 0 for some phase θ. The SYZ picture asserts that a Calabi–Yau manifold X admits a fibration π: X → B whose generic fibers are special Lagrangian tori, and the mirror X̌ is obtained by dualizing these tori fiberwise. The base B carries an affine structure with singularities, and the discriminant locus Δ ⊂ B corresponds to singular fibers. In symplectic topology, the Gross–Siebert program and the work of Kontsevich–Soibelman provide a rigorous framework using tropical geometry and wall-crossing formulas. The Fukaya category of X is expected to be equivalent to the category of coherent sheaves on X̌, and this equivalence is realized by a Fourier–Mukai transform along the torus fibration. For the quintic threefold, the SYZ fibration is not globally defined, but local models near the large complex structure limit can be constructed using toric degenerations. The symplectic topology of Lagrangian torus fibers is governed by their Floer theory: a regular fiber L_b has non-vanishing Floer cohomology HF*(L_b, L_b) ≅ H*(Tⁿ) if and only if its potential function W_b has a critical point. The potential function counts Maslov index 2 holomorphic disks with boundary on L_b, and its critical points correspond to objects in the Fukaya category that are mirror to skyscraper sheaves on X̌. The wall-crossing phenomenon occurs when the potential function changes as b crosses walls in the base B; this is encoded by the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall-crossing formula, which relates the symplectic invariants of X to the tropical geometry of the base. In the case of log Calabi–Yau surfaces, the SYZ fibration can be made completely explicit: the base is a tropical manifold, the singular fibers are focus-focus singularities, and the mirror is constructed by gluing toric charts according to the tropical data. This program has led to proofs of homological mirror symmetry for punctured Riemann surfaces, toric varieties, and certain cluster varieties.

Worked Example: Computing Algebraic Torsion for a Contact 3-Manifold
Let (Y, ξ) be the contact manifold obtained by 0-surgery on the right-handed trefoil knot in S³ with its standard contact structure. Determine whether (Y, ξ) has algebraic torsion, and explain the implications for symplectic fillability.
The right-handed trefoil is a fibered knot with monodromy a positive Dehn twist. The contact structure ξ on Y is the one supported by the open book decomposition with page a punctured torus and monodromy τ² (two positive Dehn twists). By a theorem of Honda–Kazez–Matić, this contact structure is tight but not strongly fillable. To compute algebraic torsion, consider the cylindrical contact homology chain complex for a non-degenerate contact form adapted to the open book. The Reeb orbits correspond to periodic orbits of the monodromy. The differential counts holomorphic cylinders in the symplectization. For this open book, the contact homology algebra has a generator a corresponding to the binding orbit, and the differential satisfies ∂a = 1 + higher terms. The presence of the constant term 1 implies that the contact homology vanishes, which is equivalent to algebraic torsion of order 1. More precisely, the map HC_*(Y, ξ) → HC_*(Y, ξ) induced by the identity cobordism is zero, so AT(Y, ξ) = 1. By the main theorem of Latschev–Wendl, any strong symplectic filling (W, ω) of (Y, ξ) would have symplectic homology SH_*(W) vanishing in degrees ≤ 1. But SH_*(W) always contains a copy of H*+n(W, ∂W) in non-negative degrees, leading to a contradiction unless W has no such filling. Thus (Y, ξ) is not strongly fillable, consistent with the Honda–Kazeh–Matić result. The algebraic torsion invariant provides a purely algebraic proof of non-fillability without analyzing the open book foliation directly.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Algebraic Torsion in Fillability Questions
When a problem asks whether a contact 3-manifold admits a strong symplectic filling, check if it can be presented as an open book with positive monodromy. If the monodromy is a product of positive Dehn twists, the contact structure is Stein fillable (by Eliashberg's theorem) and has no algebraic torsion. If the monodromy contains negative Dehn twists, compute the contact homology differential on the binding orbit: a constant term 1 in ∂ implies algebraic torsion and obstructs strong fillability. For higher-dimensional contact manifolds, algebraic torsion of order k obstructs fillings with vanishing symplectic homology up to degree k. Memorize the key equivalence: overtwisted ⇔ algebraic torsion = 1 ⇔ flexible filling exists.

Symplectic Homology, Growth of Reeb Orbits, and the Conley Conjecture

Symplectic homology SH_*(W) of a Liouville domain W is the homology of a chain complex generated by Reeb orbits on the contact boundary ∂W and critical points of a Morse function on W. The differential counts Floer trajectories asymptotic to Reeb orbits. A fundamental result of Viterbo and Ritter relates the growth rate of symplectic homology to the growth rate of Reeb orbits: if SH_*(W) grows exponentially (i.e., the rank of SH_*(a,b)(W) grows exponentially with the action window (a,b)), then the Reeb flow on ∂W has exponentially many periodic orbits. This connects symplectic topology to Hamiltonian dynamics. The Conley conjecture, proved by Ginzburg and Gürel for symplectically aspherical manifolds, states that every Hamiltonian diffeomorphism of a closed symplectically aspherical manifold has infinitely many periodic orbits. In the context of Liouville domains, the Conley conjecture for the Reeb flow on ∂W is equivalent to the statement that SH_*(W) is not finitely generated as a module over the Novikov ring. For cotangent bundles T*Q, symplectic homology is isomorphic to the homology of the free loop space H_*(LQ), and the growth of Reeb orbits corresponds to the growth of closed geodesics on Q. The work of Abbondandolo–Schwarz and Viterbo shows that the Chas–Sullivan loop product on H_*(LQ) corresponds to the pair-of-pants product on SH_*(T*Q). For general Liouville domains, the growth of symplectic homology is governed by the topology of the wrapped Fukaya category: if W(W) has infinitely many indecomposable objects, then SH_*(W) grows exponentially. The relationship between the Conley conjecture and symplectic homology is made precise by the Ginzburg–Gürel theorem: if SH_*(W) vanishes, then the Reeb flow on ∂W has finitely many simple periodic orbits. Conversely, if SH_*(W) is non-zero and W is subcritical, then the Reeb flow has infinitely many periodic orbits. The proof uses the fact that the symplectic homology spectral sequence degenerates at the E² page for subcritical Stein manifolds, and the non-vanishing of SH_*(W) forces the existence of infinitely many Reeb orbits to support the homology classes. This circle of ideas connects the algebraic topology of loop spaces, the dynamics of Reeb flows, and the categorical structure of the Fukaya category.

The Virtual Fundamental Class and Gromov–Witten Invariants in Symplectic Topology

The definition of Gromov–Witten invariants for general symplectic manifolds requires a virtual fundamental class because the moduli space of stable J-holomorphic maps overline{M}g,n(X, A) is not a smooth manifold of the expected dimension when J is not generic or when multiply covered curves appear. The expected dimension is virdim = (n-3)(1-g) + 2c1(A) + 2n for genus g, n marked points, and homology class A ∈ H2(X). The virtual fundamental class [overline{M}g,n(X, A)]vir ∈ Hvirdim(overline{M}g,n(X, A); Q) is constructed using the theory of Kuranishi structures (Fukaya–Ono), polyfolds (Hofer–Wysocki–Zehnder), or the algebraic geometry approach of Li–Tian and Behrend–Fantechi. In the Kuranishi approach, each point [u] in the moduli space has a Kuranishi neighborhood (V, E, Γ, s, psi) where V is a finite-dimensional manifold, E is a vector bundle, Γ is a finite group, s: V → E is a Γ-equivariant section, and psi: s-1(0)/Γ → overline{M} is a homeomorphism onto a neighborhood. The virtual fundamental class is obtained by perturbing the section s to a transverse section s' and taking the fundamental class of (s')-1(0). The Gromov–Witten invariant GWg,n^X(A; α1,…,α_n) is then defined as the integral of the pullbacks of cohomology classes α_i over the virtual fundamental class. The key properties are: (1) deformation invariance: Gromov–Witten invariants are invariant under symplectic deformations of (X, ω); (2) composition law: the invariants satisfy a gluing formula corresponding to the boundary strata of the moduli space; (3) the divisor equation and the string equation relate invariants with different numbers of marked points. For semi-positive symplectic manifolds (where c1(A) ≥ 3-n for all A with Mg,n(X, A) ≠ ∅), the moduli space is a smooth orbifold of the expected dimension for generic J, and the virtual fundamental class coincides with the ordinary fundamental class. The virtual fundamental class is essential for defining quantum cohomology QH^*(X) as a deformation of the ordinary cohomology ring, where the structure constants are genus-zero Gromov–Witten invariants with three marked points. The associativity of quantum cohomology (the WDVV equations) follows from the composition law for the virtual fundamental class on the boundary of overline{M}0,4(X, A).

Symplectic Topology of Lagrangian Submanifolds with Boundary and the Fukaya Category of Sectors

The Fukaya category of a Liouville sector extends the wrapped Fukaya category to manifolds with boundary and corners, providing a framework for gluing symplectic manifolds along contact hypersurfaces. A Liouville sector S is a manifold with corners equipped with a Liouville form λ such that the boundary is partitioned into ∂S = ∂_h S ∪ ∂_v S where ∂_h S (horizontal boundary) is a Liouville manifold and ∂_v S (vertical boundary) is a contact manifold with boundary. The Fukaya category W(S) has objects that are exact Lagrangian submanifolds L ⊂ S with boundary on ∂_h S and Legendrian boundary on ∂_v S. The morphism spaces are given by wrapped Floer cohomology HW^*(L0, L1) where the wrapping Hamiltonian is supported near ∂_h S. The key result of Ganatra–Pardon–Shende is that the Fukaya category of sectors satisfies a gluing formula: if a Liouville manifold W is decomposed as a union of sectors S1 ∪∂_v S2 along a common vertical boundary, then W(W) is equivalent to the categorical pushout of W(S1) and W(S2) over the category of the contact boundary. This provides a completely local-to-global principle for the wrapped Fukaya category. For example, the Fukaya category of a plumbing of cotangent bundles T*Q_i according to a graph Γ can be computed by gluing the Fukaya categories of the individual sectors T*Q_i along their contact boundaries. The category of a sector T*Q is equivalent to the category of constructible sheaves on Q with microsupport in the conormal bundle, and the gluing corresponds to the pushout of sheaf categories. This approach has been used to compute the wrapped Fukaya category of Weinstein manifolds by decomposing them into elementary sectors corresponding to Weinstein handles. The stop removal operation, which removes a Legendrian submanifold from the contact boundary, corresponds to a localization of the Fukaya category. The theory of sectors also provides a framework for defining relative symplectic invariants: the relative Gromov–Witten invariants of a pair (X, D) where D is a symplectic divisor can be computed using the Fukaya category of the sector obtained by removing a neighborhood of D. This connects the algebraic geometry of log Calabi–Yau pairs to the symplectic topology of their complements.

Maslov Index, Grading, and the Graded Fukaya Category

The Maslov index assigns an integer to a loop of Lagrangian subspaces in a symplectic vector space. For a Lagrangian submanifold L in a symplectic manifold (M, ω), the Maslov class μ_L ∈ H1(L; ℤ) measures the obstruction to a consistent grading of L. A grading is a lift of the Lagrangian Gauss map L → Λ(n) to the universal cover Λ̃(n), where Λ(n) is the Lagrangian Grassmannian. When μ_L = 0, such a lift exists and L is called graded. In the Fukaya category, objects are graded exact Lagrangians equipped with a spin structure; morphism spaces are Floer cochain complexes whose grading is shifted by the Maslov index of intersection paths. The differential counts rigid pseudoholomorphic strips, and the grading ensures that the differential has degree +1. The Novikov ring Λ = ℤ[[T, T-1]] (or its universal variant) records symplectic area, allowing the Fukaya category to be defined over a field of characteristic zero even when ω is not exact. The graded Fukaya category is an A_∞-category; its cohomology category is invariant under Hamiltonian isotopy. Grading also enters the definition of the phase of a Lagrangian in the context of Bridgeland stability conditions on the derived Fukaya category.

Key properties

  • The Maslov index of a loop γ: S1 → Λ(n) equals the winding number of det2 ∘ γ in U(1).
  • For a pair of graded Lagrangians (L0, α0), (L1, α1), the degree of an intersection point x ∈ L0 ∩ L1 is |x| = α1(x) - α0(x) - ½ μ(Λ0, Λ1) where μ is the Maslov index of the path of Lagrangian subspaces along a short path from T_x L0 to T_x L1.
  • If c1(M) = 0 and L is exact, the Maslov class vanishes automatically, so every exact Lagrangian admits a grading after a suitable choice of trivialization of the canonical bundle.

Hamiltonian Diffeomorphism Groups, the Fragmentation Norm, and the Calabi Homomorphism

The group Ham(M, ω) of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms of a closed symplectic manifold carries the Hofer metric d_H(φ, ψ) = infH ∫01 (max H_t - min H_t) dt, where the infimum runs over all Hamiltonians generating φ-1ψ. On open manifolds or manifolds with boundary, the fragmentation norm ‖·‖_frag is defined by covering M with displaceable open sets and summing the Hofer norms of the fragments. This norm is bi-invariant and often easier to estimate than the Hofer norm. The Calabi homomorphism Cal: Ham_c(M, ω) → ℝ is defined for Hamiltonians with compact support (or zero mean on closed manifolds) by Cal(φ_H1) = ∫01 ∫_M H_t ω^n dt. It is a group homomorphism, invariant under conjugation, and descends to the universal cover Ham̃(M, ω). On a closed manifold, the Calabi homomorphism vanishes because the integral of a Hamiltonian over a closed symplectic manifold is zero after normalizing the mean. The Calabi homomorphism detects the flux of a Hamiltonian isotopy through the boundary of a Liouville domain and plays a central role in the study of rigidity of Hamiltonian group actions.

Fragmentation versus Hofer norm

Symplectic Embedding Problems for Balls, Cylinders, and the Gromov Width

The Gromov width of a symplectic manifold (M, ω) is the supremum of π r2 such that a standard ball B2n(r) symplectically embeds into M. Gromov's non‑squeezing theorem states that the Gromov width of a cylinder Z2n(R) = B2(R) × ℝ2n-2 equals π R2. For a general domain Ω ⊂ ℝ2n, the Ekeland–Hofer capacities c_k(Ω) provide a sequence of symplectic capacities that obstruct embeddings. The first capacity c1(Ω) coincides with the Gromov width for convex domains. For a ball B2n(r), c_k = π r2 for all k. For a cylinder Z2n(R), c1 = π R2 and c_k = ∞ for k ≥ 2. The displacement energy e(Ω) is the infimum of Hofer norms of Hamiltonians that displace Ω from itself; it satisfies c1(Ω) ≤ e(Ω) ≤ 2 c1(Ω). Embedding problems for ellipsoids E(a1,…,a_n) are solved by the ECH capacities of Hutchings, which give a complete set of obstructions in four dimensions. In higher dimensions, the problem remains open in general, but the symplectic folding construction of Schlenk and others produces embeddings of polydisks into balls that are optimal for many volume ratios.

Key inequalities

  • Monotonicity: If Ω1 ⊂ Ω2 then c_k(Ω1) ≤ c_k(Ω2) for all k.
  • Conformality: c_k(λ Ω) = λ2 c_k(Ω) for λ > 0.
  • Non‑squeezing: c1(B2n(r)) = π r2 ≤ c1(Z2n(R)) = π R2 implies r ≤ R.

Symplectic Topology of Hamiltonian Floer Theory for Non‑Contractible Periodic Orbits

Classical Floer homology on a closed symplectically aspherical manifold (M, ω) uses the action functional on the space of contractible loops. When π2(M) ≠ 0 or one wishes to study orbits in a non‑trivial free homotopy class α ∈ [S1, M], the Floer complex must be built on the covering ℒ_α M of the free loop space consisting of loops in class α. The action functional 𝒜_H(γ) = -∫D2 barγ^* ω - ∫01 H_t(γ(t)) dt is well defined on the universal cover ℒ̃_α M because the symplectic area of a capping disk depends on the choice of capping. The resulting homology HF_*^α(H) is independent of the Hamiltonian and defines the Floer homology in the class α. The Seidel representation π1(Ham(M, ω)) → QH^*(M)^× arises from the continuation maps between different homotopy classes. For monotone manifolds, the quantum product can be recovered from the pair‑of‑pants product on the direct sum ⊕_α HF_*^α.

  • The Conley–Zehnder index for non‑contractible orbits uses a trivialization of TM along a reference loop in class α.
  • If c1|π2 = 0 and ω|π2 = 0 (symplectically aspherical), the Novikov ring collapses to ℤ and the theory reduces to the contractible case.
  • For a Liouville domain, the wrapped Floer homology of a Lagrangian can be interpreted as the direct limit over Hamiltonians with increasing slope, and the non‑contractible sectors correspond to different ends of the completion.

Symplectic Capacities: Hofer–Zehnder Capacity and the Displacement Energy

The Hofer–Zehnder capacity cHZ(Ω) of a domain Ω ⊂ (M, ω) is the supremum of max H over all admissible Hamiltonians H: M → [0, ∞) supported in Ω whose Hamiltonian flow has no non‑constant periodic orbits of period ≤ 1. An admissible Hamiltonian is constant near the boundary of its support and has no fast periodic orbits. The displacement energy e(Ω) is the infimum of Hofer norms of compactly supported Hamiltonians that displace Ω from itself. A fundamental inequality due to Hofer and Zehnder states cHZ(Ω) ≤ e(Ω) ≤ 2 cHZ(Ω). For a ball B2n(r), cHZ = π r2 and e = π r2. For a cylinder Z2n(R), cHZ = π R2 but e = ∞ because a cylinder cannot be displaced by a compactly supported Hamiltonian. The Hofer–Zehnder capacity is a symplectic capacity in the sense of Ekeland–Hofer: it satisfies monotonicity, conformality, and non‑triviality. It is particularly useful for detecting the existence of periodic orbits on energy surfaces: if a hypersurface Σ = ∂Ω bounds a domain with cHZ(Ω) > 0, then the Reeb flow on Σ carries a periodic orbit. This is the basis of the proof of the Weinstein conjecture for contact type hypersurfaces in ℝ2n.

Examples

  • For a polydisk P(a1,…,a_n) = B2(a1) × … × B2(a_n), cHZ = π min_i a_i2.
  • For an ellipsoid E(a1,…,a_n), cHZ = π min_i a_i (the smallest axis).
  • If Ω is a thin neighbourhood of a Lagrangian torus, cHZ(Ω) can be arbitrarily small while the volume remains bounded below.

Moduli Spaces of Pseudoholomorphic Curves with Lagrangian Boundary Conditions and Open Gromov–Witten Invariants

The compactness theorem for pseudoholomorphic curves with boundary on a Lagrangian submanifold L in a symplectic manifold (M, ω) requires a careful analysis of bubbling phenomena. When a sequence of J-holomorphic disks with boundary on L has uniformly bounded energy, Gromov compactness yields a limiting stable map whose domain is a nodal Riemann surface with boundary. The boundary components map to L and interior nodes correspond to sphere bubbles, while boundary nodes correspond to disk bubbles. The evaluation maps at boundary marked points land in L, and the resulting moduli space carries a virtual fundamental class when L is relatively spin. This virtual class defines open Gromov–Witten invariants, which count disks with prescribed boundary conditions and incidence constraints. For monotone Lagrangians, the Maslov index of a disk equals twice its Chern number, and the expected dimension of the moduli space of disks with k boundary marked points is n + μ(u) + k − 3, where n = dim L and μ(u) is the Maslov index. The open invariants satisfy a version of the WDVV equations and can be packaged into a potential function on the Fukaya category. In the case of a toric Fano manifold, the Lagrangian torus fibers are monotone and the open invariants coincide with the coefficients of the Landau–Ginzburg superpotential, providing a mirror symmetry interpretation. The construction of the virtual fundamental class uses Kuranishi structures or polyfold theory to handle transversality issues, and the resulting invariants are independent of the choice of almost complex structure and perturbation data. These invariants have been computed for many examples, including the Clifford torus in ℂPⁿ and the Chekanov torus in the unit cotangent bundle of the sphere.

Immersed Lagrangian Floer Theory and the Clean Intersection Condition

When two Lagrangian submanifolds L₀ and L₁ intersect cleanly, their intersection is a smooth submanifold C = L₀ ∩ L₁ and the tangent spaces satisfy TₚL₀ + TₚL₁ = TₚM for all p ∈ C. In this setting, the Floer complex can be defined using a Morse function on C and a perturbation of the Hamiltonian that separates the Lagrangians near C. The differential counts pseudoholomorphic strips with boundary on the perturbed Lagrangians, and the resulting homology is isomorphic to the singular homology of C with coefficients in a Novikov ring. For immersed Lagrangians, self-intersection points contribute additional generators to the Floer complex. The clean intersection condition ensures that the linearized Cauchy–Riemann operator is Fredholm and that the moduli spaces of strips are compact up to breaking. The theory extends to the case where L₀ and L₁ are immersed with only transverse double points, and the Floer differential includes contributions from polygons with corners at the intersection points. The A∞ structure on the Fukaya category of immersed Lagrangians was developed by Akaho and Joyce, and later generalized by Seidel. The clean intersection condition is also essential for the definition of the Lagrangian correspondence category, where objects are Lagrangian correspondences between symplectic manifolds and morphisms are given by Floer homology of the fiber product. In the context of mirror symmetry, immersed Lagrangians correspond to matrix factorizations on the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model, and the clean intersection condition translates to the condition that the matrix factorizations are strictly unital.

Reeb Dynamics on Contact Manifolds: The Weinstein Conjecture in Higher Dimensions

The Weinstein conjecture asserts that every closed contact manifold admits a closed Reeb orbit. In dimension three, the conjecture was proved by Taubes using embedded contact homology and Seiberg–Witten theory. In higher dimensions, the conjecture remains open in general, but significant progress has been made for contact manifolds that are boundaries of symplectically fillable domains. If (M, ξ) is the boundary of a Liouville domain (W, ω), then the symplectic homology of W provides a tool to detect Reeb orbits. The Viterbo transfer map relates the symplectic homology of W to the homology of the free loop space of M, and the existence of a non-zero element in the positive symplectic homology implies the existence of a closed Reeb orbit. For subcritically Stein fillable contact manifolds, the symplectic homology vanishes, and the conjecture is false in general; however, the existence of a closed Reeb orbit is guaranteed if the contact manifold is dynamically convex, meaning that all Reeb orbits have Conley–Zehnder index at least n+1 in dimension 2n−1. The contact homology algebra, defined using the Chekanov–Eliashberg differential graded algebra for Legendrian submanifolds, also encodes Reeb dynamics. The Bourgeois–Oancea isomorphism identifies the linearized contact homology with the symplectic homology of the filling, providing a bridge between the two theories. In the case of prequantization bundles over symplectic manifolds, the Reeb flow is the circle action, and the closed orbits correspond to the fibers. The growth rate of Reeb orbits is related to the topological entropy of the Reeb flow, and the Conley conjecture predicts infinitely many closed Reeb orbits for certain contact forms. Recent work by Ginzburg, Gürel, and others has established the existence of infinitely many closed Reeb orbits for dynamically convex contact forms on spheres and for contact forms on unit cotangent bundles of manifolds with hyperbolic geodesic flow.

The Calabi Homomorphism on the Universal Cover of the Hamiltonian Diffeomorphism Group

The Calabi homomorphism is a fundamental invariant of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms on open symplectic manifolds. For a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism φ generated by a compactly supported Hamiltonian H on a symplectic manifold (M, ω) with c₁(M) = 0, the Calabi invariant is defined as Cal(φ) = ∫₀¹ ∫_M H_t ωⁿ dt. This descends to a homomorphism from the universal cover Ham̃(M, ω) to ℝ. On closed manifolds, the Calabi homomorphism vanishes because the integral of H_t ωⁿ over M is zero for normalized Hamiltonians. However, on the universal cover of the Hamiltonian diffeomorphism group of a closed manifold, one can define a relative Calabi homomorphism using the action functional on the space of contractible loops. The Calabi homomorphism is closely related to the Hofer metric: the Hofer norm of a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism is bounded below by the absolute value of its Calabi invariant. The fragmentation norm, defined by the minimal number of fragments supported in displaceable sets, also interacts with the Calabi homomorphism. In the case of a symplectically aspherical manifold, the Calabi homomorphism on the universal cover coincides with the action functional evaluated on the constant loop, and its kernel is the commutator subgroup of Ham̃(M, ω). The Calabi homomorphism plays a crucial role in the study of the geometry of the Hamiltonian diffeomorphism group, including the existence of quasimorphisms and the structure of the fragmentation norm. For example, on the disk D²ⁿ, the Calabi homomorphism is surjective and its kernel is perfect. On the symplectization of a contact manifold, the Calabi homomorphism corresponds to the contact volume of the Reeb flow. The Calabi homomorphism also appears in the context of the Seidel representation, where the quantum homology of M acts on the Floer homology of the universal cover, and the Calabi invariant measures the deviation from being a loop of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms.

Symplectic Folding Constructions for Embedding Polydisks into Balls

The symplectic folding construction, introduced by Schlenk and refined by others, provides explicit symplectic embeddings of polydisks P(a₁,…,aₙ) = B²(a₁) × … × B²(aₙ) into balls B²ⁿ(R). The construction proceeds by iteratively folding the polydisk along coordinate hyperplanes, using a symplectic map that identifies a neighborhood of the folding hypersurface with a neighborhood of a Lagrangian disk in the ball. The optimal embedding radius R for a given polydisk is determined by the volume constraint and the Gromov width of the polydisk. For a polydisk with a₁ ≤ a₂ ≤ … ≤ aₙ, the Gromov width is π a₁², so any embedding into a ball must satisfy R ≥ a₁. The folding construction achieves R = √(a₁² + … + aₙ²) when the polydisk is sufficiently thin, i.e., when a₁² + … + aₙ₋₁² ≤ aₙ². In the general case, the optimal embedding radius is given by the solution of a combinatorial optimization problem involving the Ekeland–Hofer capacities of the polydisk and the ball. The capacities of the polydisk are c_k(P) = π ai_k² where the indices are chosen to maximize the sum of the first k capacities. The embedding exists if and only if c_k(P) ≤ c_k(B²ⁿ(R)) = π R² for all k. The folding construction realizes the embedding whenever the capacity constraints are satisfied, and it is conjectured that these constraints are also sufficient for all polydisks in dimension four. In higher dimensions, the problem is more subtle because the capacities do not form a complete set of obstructions. The folding technique has been extended to embeddings of products of ellipsoids and to the construction of symplectic embeddings of concave toric domains into convex toric domains. The method relies on the existence of a symplectic folding map that is a composition of a symplectic cut and a symplectic gluing, preserving the symplectic form globally.
