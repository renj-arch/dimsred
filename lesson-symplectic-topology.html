Symplectic Topology — Symplectomorphisms, Floer Homology & Contact Geometry

Symplectic topology is the study of symplectic manifolds — even-dimensional smooth manifolds equipped with a closed non-degenerate 2-form ω. Unlike Riemannian geometry, symplectic geometry has no local invariants (Darboux's theorem), giving it a distinctly global, topological flavor. The field exploded with Gromov's introduction of pseudoholomorphic curves, leading to Floer homology (awarded the 2011 Abel Prize), Gromov-Witten invariants, and deep connections to contact geometry, mirror symmetry, and low-dimensional topology.

"Symplectic geometry is the geometry of classical phase space."
— Vladimir Arnold
Part 1

📏 Symplectic Manifolds and Darboux's Theorem

Symplectic Forms
Non-Degenerate Closed 2-Forms and Local Normal Form
A symplectic manifold (M, ω) is a 2n-dimensional smooth manifold with a closed 2-form ω (dω = 0) that is non-degenerate: ω^n ≠ 0 everywhere. This gives an isomorphism T_xM ≅ T_x^*M via v ↦ ω(v, ·). Classical phase space T^*Q (cotangent bundle) has the canonical symplectic form ω = Σ dq_i ∧ dp_i. Darboux's theorem — the central local result — states that every symplectic manifold is locally symplectomorphic to (ℝ^{2n}, Σ dx_i ∧ dy_i). Unlike Riemannian geometry, there are no local symplectic invariants; all symplectic manifolds look the same locally. Moser's stability theorem says that if ω_t is a smooth family of symplectic forms with constant cohomology class on a closed manifold, then there is an isotopy φ_t such that φ_t^*ω_t = ω_0. This implies that symplectic structures are determined up to deformation by their cohomology class. A symplectomorphism is a diffeomorphism f: M → N such that f^*ω_N = ω_M. The group Symp(M, ω) of symplectomorphisms is infinite-dimensional and plays the role of the symmetry group in Hamiltonian mechanics.
Darboux's theorem says symplectic geometry is purely global — locally every symplectic manifold is just ℝ^{2n} with the standard form. The real action happens at the global scale.
Lagrangians
Lagrangian Submanifolds and Their Neighborhoods
A Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ (M^{2n}, ω) is an n-dimensional submanifold with ω|_L = 0, i.e., ω vanishes identically on L. Examples: the zero section of T^*Q (a Lagrangian), fibers of T^*Q, and graphs of closed 1-forms. Lagrangian submanifolds are the symplectic analogue of real Lagrangians in complex geometry. Weinstein's neighborhood theorem states that a neighborhood of a Lagrangian L is symplectomorphic to a neighborhood of the zero section in T^*L equipped with its canonical symplectic form. Thus all Lagrangians look alike near the submanifold. Lagrangian intersection theory studies how Lagrangians intersect after Hamiltonian deformations — a Lagrangian L and φ_H(L) intersect in at least as many points as the topology of L dictates (when transversely intersecting). This is the foundation of Floer homology for Lagrangians. The Maslov index associates an integer to loops of Lagrangians, grading the Floer complex. A Lagrangian is monotone if its symplectic area and Maslov index are proportional — a condition ensuring well-defined Floer theory.
Lagrangian submanifolds are half-dimensional "geometric branes" on which the symplectic form vanishes. Their intersections, counted with Floer homology, reveal the global topology of the ambient symplectic manifold.
Part 2

🌍 Pseudoholomorphic Curves and Gromov's Non-squeezing

J-Holomorphic Curves
Gromov's Pseudoholomorphic Curves and Moduli Spaces
Gromov's revolutionary idea (1985): given an almost complex structure J on M that is tame (ω(·, J·) > 0), study maps u: Σ → M from a Riemann surface (Σ, j) such that du ∘ j = J ∘ du — the Cauchy-Riemann equation for pseudoholomorphic curves. These are nonlinear elliptic PDEs whose solutions are called J-holomorphic curves. The moduli space M(β, J) of all such curves representing a homology class β ∈ H₂(M; ℤ) has expected dimension given by the Fredholm index: dim = n χ(Σ) + 2 c₁(β). For generic J, the moduli space is a smooth manifold (transversality) of the expected dimension. Gromov compactness says that sequences of J-holomorphic curves have convergent subsequences in the sense of stable maps — nodal curves with bubbles. This compactness is the foundational analytic result that makes the whole theory work, analogous to convergence of sequences of holomorphic maps.
Pseudoholomorphic curves are the symplectic topologist's analytic microscope — they allow us to use PDE methods to probe the global topology of symplectic manifolds.
Non-Squeezing
Gromov's Non-Squeezing Theorem and Symplectic Capacity
Gromov's non-squeezing theorem (1985): the ball B^{2n}(r) cannot be symplectically embedded into the cylinder Z^{2n}(R) = B^2(R) × ℝ^{2n-2} unless r ≤ R. This was the first truly global symplectic invariant. It shows that symplectic embeddings are much more rigid than volume-preserving embeddings (volume gives no obstruction since vol(B^{2n}(r)) < vol(Z^{2n}(R)) is always true for small r). The proof uses a J-holomorphic sphere through the center of the ball — its area gives the obstruction. This led to the notion of symplectic capacity: a function c on symplectic manifolds that is monotone with respect to symplectic embeddings and scales as c(λM) = λ² c(M). The Gromov width (the capacity from the non-squeezing theorem) is the first of many. Gromov-Witten invariants count pseudoholomorphic curves of given genus and homology class, and are the fundamental numerical invariants of symplectic manifolds. They satisfy deformation invariance and are used for enumerative geometry — counting curves through constraints. The Kontsevich formula for rational plane curves was proved using GW invariants.
The non-squeezing theorem revealed that symplectic geometry is not just "even-dimensional volume geometry" — it has its own intrinsic rigidity measured by symplectic capacity.
Part 3

🧮 Floer Homology and Arnold Conjecture

Floer Homology
Hamiltonian Floer Homology and the Action Functional
Hamiltonian Floer homology (Floer, 1988) is an invariant of a symplectic manifold (M, ω) together with a Hamiltonian H: S¹ × M → ℝ. It is the Morse theory of the action functional 𝒜_H(γ) = ∫_{S¹} γ^*λ - ∫_{S¹} H(t, γ(t)) dt on the free loop space LM, where λ is a chosen primitive of ω (when [ω]=0 in H²). Critical points of 𝒜_H are 1-periodic orbits of the Hamiltonian vector field X_H defined by ω(X_H, ·) = dH. The Floer equation is a perturbed Cauchy-Riemann equation for maps u: ℝ×S¹ → M: ∂_s u + J(t, u)(∂_t u - X_H(t, u)) = 0. Solutions connecting critical points (s → −∞) to critical points (s → +∞) define the Floer differential. The resulting homology HF_*(M) is independent of H and J (via continuation maps) and isomorphic to the singular homology of M (the Piunikhin-Salamon-Schwarz isomorphism). Transversality for the Floer equation requires generic perturbations or virtual techniques. Compactness follows from Gromov-Floer convergence — bubbling and breaking of connecting trajectories.
Floer homology is infinite-dimensional Morse theory applied to the loop space. It turns the problem of counting periodic orbits into a robust algebraic invariant.
Arnold Conjecture
Fixed Points of Hamiltonian Symplectomorphisms and Spectral Invariants
The Arnold conjecture (1965) states that a Hamiltonian symplectomorphism φ_H (the time-1 map of a Hamiltonian flow) on a closed symplectic manifold M has at least as many fixed points as the minimal number of critical points of a Morse function on M. For M with Betti numbers b_i, the lower bound is Σ b_i (the weak Arnold conjecture). Floer proved this for monotone manifolds, and it follows in full from the isomorphism HF_*(M) ≅ H_*(M; ℤ₂), since the Floer chain complex generators are precisely the fixed points. This was one of the greatest successes of symplectic topology. Spectral invariants (Oh, Schwarz, Viterbo) assign real numbers c(α, H) to each quantum cohomology class α, derived from the filtration of the Floer complex by action. These invariants satisfy the triangle inequality c(α, H#K) ≤ c(α, H) + c(α, K) and define the Hofer norm on the group of Hamiltonian symplectomorphisms: ‖φ_H‖ = inf {∫₀¹(max H_t − min H_t) dt : φ_H = φ_H}. Spectral invariants also detect the displacement energy of subsets — the minimal Hofer norm needed to displace a subset from itself — a powerful tool in symplectic geometry.
The Arnold conjecture, proved via Floer homology, shows that the fixed-point count of a Hamiltonian symplectomorphism is bounded below by the topology of the manifold — a triumph of algebraic invariants in dynamics.
Part 4

🔥 Contact Geometry and Symplectic Fillings

Contact Structures
Contact Manifolds, Reeb Fields, and Legendrian Submanifolds
A contact manifold (Y, ξ) is an odd-dimensional manifold Y²ⁿ⁺¹ with a contact structure ξ = ker α, where α is a 1-form such that α ∧ (dα)ⁿ ≠ 0 everywhere (maximal non-degeneracy). Contact geometry is the odd-dimensional cousin of symplectic geometry. The Darboux theorem for contact structures says locally all contact manifolds are contactomorphic to (ℝ²ⁿ⁺¹, dz − Σ y_i dx_i). The Reeb vector field R_α is uniquely defined by dα(R_α, ·) = 0 and α(R_α) = 1. Its flow preserves the contact form α but not the contact structure alone. A Legendrian submanifold L ⊂ Y is an n-dimensional submanifold tangent to ξ (i.e., α|_L = 0). Legendrian knots in ℝ³ (standard contact structure dz − y dx) are studied via their front projection (x, z) and Lagrangian projection (x, y). The Thurston-Bennequin invariant tb(L) and rotation number r(L) are the basic classical invariants of Legendrian knots. Contact structures split into two fundamentally different classes: overtwisted (containing an overtwisted disc — a disc whose boundary is Legendrian with tb = 0) and tight (all others). Eliashberg's classification (1989) states that overt twisted contact structures on closed 3-manifolds are classified by homotopy classes of oriented 2-plane fields — they are flexible. Tight contact structures are rigid and much harder to classify.
Contact geometry sits at the boundary of symplectic geometry — literally: contact manifolds arise as the boundaries of symplectic domains, and their classification reveals a deep rigidity/flexibility dichotomy.
Fillings and Contact Homology
Symplectic Fillings, Liouville Domains, and Contact Homology
A symplectic filling of a contact manifold (Y, ξ) is a symplectic manifold (W, ω) with boundary ∂W = Y such that ω induces the contact structure near the boundary (e.g., ω = dα). A Liouville domain (W, λ) is a compact manifold with boundary such that ω = dλ is symplectic and the Liouville vector field V (defined by ι_V ω = λ) points outward along ∂W. The boundary of a Liouville domain is a contact manifold with α = λ|_Y. A Weinstein manifold is a Liouville domain with a Morse function whose gradient (w.r.t. the Liouville metric) is gradient-like for V. Contact homology (Eliashberg-Givental-Hofer, 2000) is an invariant of contact manifolds defined via pseudoholomorphic curves in the symplectization ℝ×Y. It is part of the larger Symplectic Field Theory (SFT) framework. The Weinstein conjecture (existence of a closed Reeb orbit on any closed contact manifold) was proved by Taubes (2007) using Seiberg-Witten theory. Overtwisted contact structures are not symplectically fillable (Eliashberg-Gromov) — a tightness theorem that uses pseudoholomorphic curves in the filling. Giroux's correspondence relates contact structures on 3-manifolds to open book decompositions, enabling the use of mapping class groups in contact topology. The classification of tight contact structures on lens spaces and torus bundles has been carried out by Honda, Etnyre, and Ozbagci.
The interplay between contact manifolds and their symplectic fillings is one of the richest areas of modern topology, linking Reeb dynamics, holomorphic curves, and 3-manifold topology.
