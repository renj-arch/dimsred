Quantum Mechanics — Wave Functions, Schrödinger Equation, Operators, Spin & Measurement

Quantum mechanics is the fundamental theory of physics at the atomic and subatomic scale, replacing classical deterministic mechanics with a probabilistic framework based on wave functions and operators. The wave function Ψ(x,t) contains all information about a quantum system; its square modulus |Ψ(x,t)|² gives the probability density for finding a particle at position x at time t (Born interpretation). Normalization: ∫|Ψ|² dx = 1. The time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE): iħ ∂Ψ/∂t = ĤΨ, where Ĥ = −(ħ²/2m)∇² + V is the Hamiltonian operator. The time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE): Ĥψ = Eψ gives stationary states: Ψ(x,t) = ψ(x)e^{−iEt/ħ}. Solutions for the infinite square well yield quantized energies E_n = n²π²ħ²/(2mL²). The quantum harmonic oscillator has equally spaced energy levels E_n = ħω(n + ½). Operators represent observables: position x̂ = x, momentum p̂ = −iħ∂/∂x, energy Ĥ. The commutator [x̂, p̂] = iħ leads to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2. Angular momentum operators [L̂_i, L̂_j] = iħε_{ijk}L̂_k with eigenvalues L² = ℓ(ℓ+1)ħ² and L_z = mħ. Spin is intrinsic angular momentum: spin-½ particles have S_z = ±ħ/2, described by the Pauli matrices σ_x, σ_y, σ_z. The Stern-Gerlach experiment revealed spin quantization. Quantum measurement causes wave function collapse (the Copenhagen interpretation), measurement outcomes are probabilistic with expectation value ⟨A⟩ = ∫ψ*Âψ dx, and the density matrix ρ = Σ p_i |ψ_i⟩⟨ψ_i| describes mixed states.

Part 1

🌀 Wave Functions and the Schrödinger Equation

Foundations
The Wave Function, the Born Interpretation, the Time-Dependent and Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation, and the Infinite Square Well
The wave function Ψ(x,t) is a complex-valued function that provides a complete description of a quantum system. The Born rule (1926): the probability of finding a particle between x and x+dx at time t is P(x,t)dx = |Ψ(x,t)|²dx. Normalization: ∫_{-∞}^{∞} |Ψ(x,t)|² dx = 1 — the particle must be somewhere. The wave function is an element of a Hilbert space (typically L²(ℝ) for square-integrable functions). The time-dependent Schrödinger equation: iħ ∂Ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m)∂²Ψ/∂x² + VΨ. This is a linear partial differential equation — the superposition principle holds: if Ψ₁ and Ψ₂ are solutions, so is a₁Ψ₁ + a₂Ψ₂. The TDSE is first-order in time, so Ψ(x,0) determines the entire future evolution. The time-independent Schrödinger equation: Ĥψ = Eψ, where ψ(x) is a stationary wave function. The separation of variables Ψ(x,t) = ψ(x) e^{−iEt/ħ} converts the TDSE into the TISE. Stationary states have constant probability density |ψ(x)|² and definite energy E. The infinite square well (particle in a box) with V(x) = 0 for 0 < x < L and ∞ elsewhere: ψ_n(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L) for n = 1, 2, 3, ... with energies E_n = n²π²ħ²/(2mL²). The ground state (n = 1) has zero-point energy E₁ = π²ħ²/(2mL²) — a consequence of the uncertainty principle. The wave functions are orthonormal: ∫ ψ_m* ψ_n dx = δ_{mn}. Any initial state can be expanded as Ψ(x,0) = Σ c_n ψ_n(x) where c_n = ∫ ψ_n* Ψ(x,0) dx. The finite square well has a finite number of bound states (E < V₀) with exponentially decaying tails in the barrier — quantum tunneling occurs through classically forbidden regions.
The wave function is the central object of quantum mechanics; the Schrödinger equation governs its time evolution, and the Born rule provides the probabilistic interpretation of measurement outcomes.
Part 2

⚡ Operators, Observables, and the Uncertainty Principle

Mathematical Framework
Hermitian Operators, Commutators, the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle, and the Quantum Harmonic Oscillator
In quantum mechanics, every observable A is represented by a Hermitian operator Â (satisfying ⟨f|Âg⟩ = ⟨Âf|g⟩) with real eigenvalues corresponding to possible measurement outcomes. The position operator x̂: (x̂ψ)(x) = xψ(x). The momentum operator p̂: (p̂ψ)(x) = −iħ dψ/dx. The Hamiltonian (energy operator): Ĥ = p̂²/(2m) + V(x̂). The expectation value of Â in a state ψ: ⟨Â⟩ = ∫ ψ* Â ψ dx = ⟨ψ|Â|ψ⟩. The commutator [Â, B̂] = ÂB̂ − B̂Â. The canonical commutation relation: [x̂, p̂] = iħ. The generalized uncertainty principle: (ΔA)²(ΔB)² ≥ (½i⟨[Â,B̂]⟩)², where (ΔA)² = ⟨Â²⟩ − ⟨Â⟩² is the variance. From [x̂,p̂] = iħ, we get Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 — the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. This is a fundamental limit, not a limitation of measurement — it reflects the wave nature of matter. Energy-time uncertainty: ΔE Δt ≥ ħ/2. The quantum harmonic oscillator with potential V(x) = ½mω²x² has the TISE: −(ħ²/2m)d²ψ/dx² + ½mω²x²ψ = Eψ. The energy eigenvalues: E_n = ħω(n + ½) for n = 0, 1, 2, ... The ground state (n = 0) has E₀ = ½ħω. The wave functions involve Hermite polynomials: ψ_n(x) = (1/√(2ⁿ n!)) (mω/πħ)^{1/4} H_n(ξ) e^{−ξ²/2}, where ξ = √(mω/ħ) x. The ladder operator method using creation (â†) and annihilation (â) operators: â = √(mω/2ħ)(x̂ + ip̂/(mω)), â† = √(mω/2ħ)(x̂ − ip̂/(mω)). These satisfy [â, â†] = 1, Ĥ = ħω(â†â + ½), and â|n⟩ = √n|n−1⟩, â†|n⟩ = √(n+1)|n+1⟩. The number operator N̂ = â†â has eigenvalues n. This algebraic approach is powerful for solving harmonic oscillator problems without explicitly solving differential equations.
Operators and commutators form the mathematical backbone of quantum mechanics; the canonical commutation relation leads to the uncertainty principle, and the harmonic oscillator is exactly solvable via ladder operators.
Part 3

🔄 Angular Momentum and Spin

Intrinsic and Orbital
The Angular Momentum Operators, Spin-½, Pauli Matrices, and the Stern-Gerlach Experiment
The orbital angular momentum operator L = r̂ × p̂. In components: L̂_x = ŷ p̂_z − ẑ p̂_y, L̂_y = ẑ p̂_x − x̂ p̂_z, L̂_z = x̂ p̂_y − ŷ p̂_x. The commutation relations: [L̂_i, L̂_j] = iħ ε_{ijk} L̂_k (where ε_{ijk} is the Levi-Civita symbol). The squared operator L̂² = L̂_x² + L̂_y² + L̂_z² commutes with each component: [L̂², L̂_z] = 0. The simultaneous eigenstates |ℓ, m⟩ satisfy: L̂²|ℓ,m⟩ = ℓ(ℓ+1)ħ²|ℓ,m⟩ (with ℓ = 0, 1, 2, ...) and L̂_z|ℓ,m⟩ = mħ|ℓ,m⟩ (with m = −ℓ, −ℓ+1, ..., ℓ). The raising and lowering operators: L̂_± = L̂_x ± iL̂_y, satisfying L̂_±|ℓ,m⟩ = ħ√(ℓ(ℓ+1) − m(m±1))|ℓ,m±1⟩. Spin is an intrinsic form of angular momentum with no classical analog. For a spin-½ particle, the spin operators are Ŝ_i = (ħ/2)σ_i, where σ_i are the Pauli matrices: σ_x = [[0,1],[1,0]], σ_y = [[0,−i],[i,0]], σ_z = [[1,0],[0,−1]]. The Pauli matrices satisfy σ_iσ_j = δ_{ij}I + iε_{ijk}σ_k and [σ_i,σ_j] = 2iε_{ijk}σ_k. Spin eigenstates: |↑_z⟩ and |↓_z⟩ are eigenstates of S_z with eigenvalues ±ħ/2. The Stern-Gerlach experiment (1922): passing silver atoms through a non-uniform magnetic field produced two discrete spots on a detector, demonstrating spatial quantization of angular momentum. This was the first direct evidence of spin quantization. A spin-½ particle in a magnetic field B = B₀ ẑ has Hamiltonian Ĥ = −γB₀ S_z, leading to Larmor precession at frequency ω_L = γB₀. The spinor representation: a general spin state is |χ⟩ = a|↑⟩ + b|↓⟩ with |a|² + |b|² = 1. The Bloch sphere represents all pure spin states: |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|↑⟩ + e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)|↓⟩. Addition of angular momentum: the total angular momentum J = L + S satisfies |ℓ − s| ≤ j ≤ ℓ + s in integer steps. For a spin-½ particle in a p orbital (ℓ = 1), j = 3/2 or 1/2 — the fine structure of atomic spectra.
Angular momentum in quantum mechanics has both orbital and intrinsic (spin) contributions; spin-½ particles are described by Pauli matrices, and the Stern-Gerlach experiment provides direct evidence for quantization of angular momentum.
Part 4

📏 Quantum Measurement, Entanglement, and Interpretations

Measurement
The Measurement Postulate, the Density Matrix, Entanglement, Bell's Inequalities, and the Copenhagen Interpretation
The measurement postulate: upon measuring an observable Â, the result is one of its eigenvalues a_n, and the wave function collapses to the corresponding eigenstate |a_n⟩. The probability of obtaining a_n is P(a_n) = |⟨a_n|ψ⟩|². The expectation value: ⟨Â⟩ = ⟨ψ|Â|ψ⟩ = Σ a_n P(a_n). The density matrix (density operator) ρ̂ = Σ_i p_i |ψ_i⟩⟨ψ_i| describes statistical mixtures (where the system is in state |ψ_i⟩ with probability p_i). For a pure state, ρ̂ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|. The von Neumann equation: iħ dρ̂/dt = [Ĥ, ρ̂]. The entropy of entanglement: S = −Tr(ρ̂ ln ρ̂). Entanglement occurs when the state of a composite system cannot be written as a product of subsystem states: |Ψ⟩ ≠ |ψ₁⟩⊗|ψ₂⟩. The prototypical entangled state: the Bell state (EPR pair) |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. Measuring one particle instantaneously determines the state of the other — the EPR paradox (Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen, 1935) argued this implied quantum mechanics was incomplete. Bell's theorem (1964): any local hidden variable theory must satisfy Bell's inequality; quantum mechanics violates it. Bell's inequality: E(a,b) + E(a,c) + E(b,c) ≤ 1 (for certain measurement angles). The CHSH inequality: ⟨A₁B₁⟩ + ⟨A₁B₂⟩ + ⟨A₂B₁⟩ − ⟨A₂B₂⟩ ≤ 2. Experimental tests (Aspect, 1982; Zeilinger, 1997) confirmed quantum mechanical predictions and ruled out local hidden variables — nature is non-local. The Copenhagen interpretation (Bohr, Heisenberg): the wave function provides a complete description; measurement causes collapse; there is no underlying deterministic reality. Alternatives: the Many-Worlds interpretation (Everett, 1957) — all possibilities are realized in branching universes; de Broglie-Bohm pilot wave theory — deterministic particle trajectories guided by the wave function; objective collapse theories (GRW, Penrose) — spontaneous collapse mechanisms. The quantum Zeno effect: frequent measurement can slow down or freeze quantum evolution. The Kochen-Specker theorem: non-contextual hidden variable theories are impossible in Hilbert spaces of dimension ≥ 3. The quantum no-cloning theorem: an unknown quantum state cannot be cloned — the foundation of quantum cryptography.
Quantum measurement is fundamentally probabilistic and involves wave function collapse; entanglement leads to non-local correlations that violate Bell's inequalities, ruling out local hidden variable theories and confirming the intrinsically quantum nature of reality.
