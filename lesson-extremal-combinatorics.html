Extremal Combinatorics — Turán's Theorem, Ramsey Theory & Szemerédi's Regularity Lemma

Extremal combinatorics is the study of the maximum and minimum possible sizes of combinatorial objects subject to constraints. The central problem: given a family of forbidden substructures, what is the largest structure that avoids them? Turán's theorem (1941) gives the exact maximum number of edges in a Kr-free graph on n vertices: the Turán graph Tr−1(n), a complete (r−1)-partite graph with parts as equal as possible. Ramsey theory asks for the smallest n such that any red/blue edge-coloring of Kn unavoidably contains a monochromatic Kk. The probabilistic method (Erdős) shows existence without explicit construction — the random graph G(n,p) avoids a monochromatic Kk with positive probability for n ≤ 2k/2. The Lovász Local Lemma sharpens probabilistic arguments when events are only weakly dependent. Szemerédi's regularity lemma partitions large graphs into pseudorandom blocks, enabling the triangle removal lemma and Roth's theorem (3-term arithmetic progressions in dense sets). The Green-Tao theorem (2004) extends this to the primes: they contain arbitrarily long APs.

"Extremal combinatorics is the art of proving that a combinatorial structure cannot be too large without containing a specific substructure."
— Paul Erdős
Part 1

📏 Turán-type Problems

Extremal Numbers
ex(n, H): The Maximum Edges in an H-Free Graph
The extremal number ex(n, H) is the maximum number of edges in an n-vertex graph with no copy of H. Turán's theorem (1941): ex(n, Kr) = (1 − 1/(r−1)) · n²/2, uniquely achieved by the Turán graph Tr−1(n) — the complete (r−1)-partite graph on n vertices with parts differing by at most 1. For r = 3: ex(n, K3) = ⌊n²/4⌋ (Mantel's theorem, 1907). The Erdős–Stone theorem (1946) extends this to any graph H with chromatic number χ(H) ≥ 3: ex(n, H) = (1 − 1/(χ(H)−1) + o(1)) · n²/2 — the asymptotic extremal number is determined entirely by the chromatic number. The Erdős–Simonovits stability theorem: if an H-free graph G on n vertices has nearly ex(n, H) edges, then G is close to the Turán graph Tχ(H)−1(n) in edit distance. For bipartite graphs H (χ = 2), the picture is different: ex(n, H) = o(n²). The Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem (KST): ex(n, Ks,t) = O(n^{2−1/s}) for s ≤ t. For C4 = K2,2: ex(n, C4) = Θ(n^{3/2}). The Moore bound for regular graphs of degree d and girth g gives a lower bound on n: n ≥ 1 + d Σ_{i=0}^{⌊(g−3)/2⌋} (d−1)^i, connecting extremal graph theory to the girth problem. The Erdős–Gallai theorem: ex(n, Pk) ≤ (k−2)n/2 for paths. The Erdős–Sós conjecture (proved by Ajtai–Komlós–Simonovits): any n-vertex graph with more than (t−1)n/2 edges contains every tree on t+1 vertices.
The extremal number ex(n, H) of a non-bipartite graph H is asymptotically (1 − 1/(χ(H)−1)) · n²/2; for bipartite H, the growth is subquadratic and often a major open problem.
Part 2

🌍 Ramsey Theory

Ramsey
Ramsey Numbers, Van der Waerden, and Hales-Jewett
Ramsey theory embodies the principle: complete disorder is impossible. The Ramsey number R(s, t) is the smallest n such that any 2-coloring of the edges of Kn (red/blue) contains either a red Ks or a blue Kt. Known exact values: R(3,3) = 6, R(3,4) = 9, R(3,5) = 14, R(4,4) = 18, R(3,6) = 18, R(3,7) = 23. Despite decades of effort, R(5,5) is only known to lie between 43 and 48. The diagonal case R(k, k): the best known lower bound is R(k, k) > 2^{k/2} (Erdős, using random graphs — a textbook application of the probabilistic method), and the best upper bound is R(k, k) < (4 − ε)^k / √k for some ε > 0 (improved by Campos–Griffiths–Morris–Sahasrabudhe, 2023). The asymptotics: R(k,k) = 2^{(1+o(1))k/2}. The Erdős–Rado canonization theorem for ω-sequences: any coloring of the k-subsets of an infinite set contains a canonically colored subset. Van der Waerden's theorem: for any c, k ∈ ℕ, there exists W(c, k) such that any c-coloring of {1, …, W(c, k)} contains a monochromatic k-term arithmetic progression. The best bounds for W(2, k) are roughly W(2, k) ≤ 2^{2^{2^{2^{k+9}}}} (Gowers). Folkman numbers: the smallest n such that any coloring of {1,…,n} contains a monochromatic solution to x + y = z. The Hales-Jewett theorem (the combinatorial core of van der Waerden): for any c, k, there exists HJ(c, k) such that any c-coloring of the m-dimensional tic-tac-toe board [k]^m contains a combinatorial line. This implies van der Waerden, and its density version (Furstenberg–Katznelson) implies the multidimensional Szemerédi theorem.
Ramsey numbers grow exponentially; the precise asymptotics of R(k,k) remain one of the most famous open problems in combinatorics.
Part 3

🧮 The Probabilistic Method and the Local Lemma

Probabilistic
Random Graphs, the Probabilistic Method, and the Lovász Local Lemma
The probabilistic method (pioneered by Erdős): to prove the existence of a combinatorial object with a desired property, show that a random object satisfies the property with positive probability. The classic example: the lower bound R(k,k) > 2^{k/2}. Consider G(n,½) — the random graph on n vertices where each edge appears with probability ½. For n = ⌊2^{k/2}⌋, the expected number of monochromatic Kk is 2·(ⁿk)·2^{-(ᵏ₂)} < 1, so P(no monochromatic Kk) > 0. Hence R(k,k) > 2^{k/2}. The alteration method: construct a random object, then delete a small set of vertices/edges to eliminate all forbidden substructures — used to prove the existence of large independent sets and high-girth graphs with high chromatic number (Erdős, 1959). The Lovász Local Lemma (LLL, Erdős–Lovász, 1973): let A₁, …, Aₙ be events with P(Aᵢ) ≤ p. Suppose each Aᵢ is dependent on at most d other events. If ep(d+1) ≤ 1 (or p ≤ 1/(e(d+1))), then P(∩ ̄Aᵢ) > 0. The LLL is a powerful tool for avoiding rare bad events even when there are many of them, as long as each depends on few others. Applications of LLL: hypergraph 2-coloring (Property B): if each edge in a k-uniform hypergraph intersects at most 2^{k−1} other edges, the hypergraph is 2-colorable. The existence of large partial Latin squares. The algorithmic LLL (Moser–Tardos, 2010): a constructive version for events defined by independent random variables — the resampling table and the "resampling oracles" proof provide an efficient algorithm to find a configuration avoiding all bad events when the LLL conditions hold.
The probabilistic method is a cornerstone of extremal combinatorics: existence is often easier to prove than construction, and the LLL extends this to weakly dependent events.
Part 4

🔥 Szemerédi's Regularity Lemma and the Triangle Removal Lemma

Regularity
The Regularity Lemma, Removal Lemma, Roth's Theorem, and Green-Tao
The Szemerédi regularity lemma (1978) is a structural result about large graphs: for any ε > 0, every sufficiently large graph G has an ε-regular partition of its vertex set into M(ε) blocks V₁, …, Vₘ (M depends only on ε, not on the size of G). A pair (V_i, V_j) is ε-regular if for any A ⊆ V_i with |A| ≥ ε|V_i| and B ⊆ V_j with |B| ≥ ε|V_j|, the edge densities satisfy |d(A,B) − d(V_i,V_j)| ≤ ε. The regularity lemma provides a "coarse-grained" approximation of any large graph. The triangle removal lemma (Ruzsa–Szemerédi, 1978): any graph on n vertices with o(n³) triangles can be made triangle-free by removing o(n²) edges. This has a stunning consequence: Roth's theorem (1953) — any subset A ⊆ ℕ with positive upper density contains a 3-term arithmetic progression. The proof: construct a tripartite graph encoding solutions to x + y = 2z; dense sets yield many triangles, and the removal lemma forces a progression. Szemerédi's theorem (1975): for any k ≥ 3, any subset of ℕ with positive upper density contains arbitrarily long k-term arithmetic progressions. The proof uses the regularity lemma for hypergraphs and the density increment method (Szemerédi's original approach) or ergodic theory (Furstenberg) or hypergraph regularity (Gowers, Rodl–Rucinski–Schacht). The Green–Tao theorem (2004): the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. The proof uses a transference principle: embed the primes into a pseudorandom measure ν (the W-trick), apply the Szemerédi theorem relative to ν by showing that any set with positive ν-density contains long APs (relative Szemerédi theorem). The regularity method extends to many extremal problems: the Erdős–Rothschild problem (counting H-free colorings), property testing (determining whether a graph is ε-far from H-free via random sampling), the removal lemma for hypergraphs, and the combinatorial Nullstellensatz (Alon, 1999) for polynomial method proofs in combinatorics.
The regularity lemma transforms arbitrary large graphs into structured pseudorandom partitions — a unifying framework for property testing, arithmetic progressions, and extremal graph theory.
