Extremal Combinatorics — Turán's Theorem, Ramsey Theory & Szemerédi's Regularity Lemma

Extremal combinatorics is the study of the maximum and minimum possible sizes of combinatorial objects subject to constraints. The central problem: given a family of forbidden substructures, what is the largest structure that avoids them? Turán's theorem (1941) gives the exact maximum number of edges in a Kr-free graph on n vertices: the Turán graph Tr−1(n), a complete (r−1)-partite graph with parts as equal as possible. Ramsey theory asks for the smallest n such that any red/blue edge-coloring of Kn unavoidably contains a monochromatic Kk. The probabilistic method (Erdős) shows existence without explicit construction — the random graph G(n,p) avoids a monochromatic Kk with positive probability for n ≤ 2k/2. The Lovász Local Lemma sharpens probabilistic arguments when events are only weakly dependent. Szemerédi's regularity lemma partitions large graphs into pseudorandom blocks, enabling the triangle removal lemma and Roth's theorem (3-term arithmetic progressions in dense sets). The Green-Tao theorem (2004) extends this to the primes: they contain arbitrarily long APs.

"Extremal combinatorics is the art of proving that a combinatorial structure cannot be too large without containing a specific substructure."
— Paul Erdős
Part 1

📏 Turán-type Problems

Extremal Numbers
ex(n, H): The Maximum Edges in an H-Free Graph
The extremal number ex(n, H) is the maximum number of edges in an n-vertex graph with no copy of H. Turán's theorem (1941): ex(n, Kr) = (1 − 1/(r−1)) · n²/2, uniquely achieved by the Turán graph Tr−1(n) — the complete (r−1)-partite graph on n vertices with parts differing by at most 1. For r = 3: ex(n, K3) = ⌊n²/4⌋ (Mantel's theorem, 1907). The Erdős–Stone theorem (1946) extends this to any graph H with chromatic number χ(H) ≥ 3: ex(n, H) = (1 − 1/(χ(H)−1) + o(1)) · n²/2 — the asymptotic extremal number is determined entirely by the chromatic number. The Erdős–Simonovits stability theorem: if an H-free graph G on n vertices has nearly ex(n, H) edges, then G is close to the Turán graph Tχ(H)−1(n) in edit distance. For bipartite graphs H (χ = 2), the picture is different: ex(n, H) = o(n²). The Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem (KST): ex(n, Ks,t) = O(n^{2−1/s}) for s ≤ t. For C4 = K2,2: ex(n, C4) = Θ(n^{3/2}). The Moore bound for regular graphs of degree d and girth g gives a lower bound on n: n ≥ 1 + d Σ_{i=0}^{⌊(g−3)/2⌋} (d−1)^i, connecting extremal graph theory to the girth problem. The Erdős–Gallai theorem: ex(n, Pk) ≤ (k−2)n/2 for paths. The Erdős–Sós conjecture (proved by Ajtai–Komlós–Simonovits): any n-vertex graph with more than (t−1)n/2 edges contains every tree on t+1 vertices.
The extremal number ex(n, H) of a non-bipartite graph H is asymptotically (1 − 1/(χ(H)−1)) · n²/2; for bipartite H, the growth is subquadratic and often a major open problem.
Part 2

🌍 Ramsey Theory

Ramsey
Ramsey Numbers, Van der Waerden, and Hales-Jewett
Ramsey theory embodies the principle: complete disorder is impossible. The Ramsey number R(s, t) is the smallest n such that any 2-coloring of the edges of Kn (red/blue) contains either a red Ks or a blue Kt. Known exact values: R(3,3) = 6, R(3,4) = 9, R(3,5) = 14, R(4,4) = 18, R(3,6) = 18, R(3,7) = 23. Despite decades of effort, R(5,5) is only known to lie between 43 and 48. The diagonal case R(k, k): the best known lower bound is R(k, k) > 2^{k/2} (Erdős, using random graphs — a textbook application of the probabilistic method), and the best upper bound is R(k, k) < (4 − ε)^k / √k for some ε > 0 (improved by Campos–Griffiths–Morris–Sahasrabudhe, 2023). The asymptotics: R(k,k) = 2^{(1+o(1))k/2}. The Erdős–Rado canonization theorem for ω-sequences: any coloring of the k-subsets of an infinite set contains a canonically colored subset. Van der Waerden's theorem: for any c, k ∈ ℕ, there exists W(c, k) such that any c-coloring of {1, …, W(c, k)} contains a monochromatic k-term arithmetic progression. The best bounds for W(2, k) are roughly W(2, k) ≤ 2^{2^{2^{2^{k+9}}}} (Gowers). Folkman numbers: the smallest n such that any coloring of {1,…,n} contains a monochromatic solution to x + y = z. The Hales-Jewett theorem (the combinatorial core of van der Waerden): for any c, k, there exists HJ(c, k) such that any c-coloring of the m-dimensional tic-tac-toe board [k]^m contains a combinatorial line. This implies van der Waerden, and its density version (Furstenberg–Katznelson) implies the multidimensional Szemerédi theorem.
Ramsey numbers grow exponentially; the precise asymptotics of R(k,k) remain one of the most famous open problems in combinatorics.
Part 3

🧮 The Probabilistic Method and the Local Lemma

Probabilistic
Random Graphs, the Probabilistic Method, and the Lovász Local Lemma
The probabilistic method (pioneered by Erdős): to prove the existence of a combinatorial object with a desired property, show that a random object satisfies the property with positive probability. The classic example: the lower bound R(k,k) > 2^{k/2}. Consider G(n,½) — the random graph on n vertices where each edge appears with probability ½. For n = ⌊2^{k/2}⌋, the expected number of monochromatic Kk is 2·(ⁿk)·2^{-(ᵏ₂)} < 1, so P(no monochromatic Kk) > 0. Hence R(k,k) > 2^{k/2}. The alteration method: construct a random object, then delete a small set of vertices/edges to eliminate all forbidden substructures — used to prove the existence of large independent sets and high-girth graphs with high chromatic number (Erdős, 1959). The Lovász Local Lemma (LLL, Erdős–Lovász, 1973): let A₁, …, Aₙ be events with P(Aᵢ) ≤ p. Suppose each Aᵢ is dependent on at most d other events. If ep(d+1) ≤ 1 (or p ≤ 1/(e(d+1))), then P(∩ ̄Aᵢ) > 0. The LLL is a powerful tool for avoiding rare bad events even when there are many of them, as long as each depends on few others. Applications of LLL: hypergraph 2-coloring (Property B): if each edge in a k-uniform hypergraph intersects at most 2^{k−1} other edges, the hypergraph is 2-colorable. The existence of large partial Latin squares. The algorithmic LLL (Moser–Tardos, 2010): a constructive version for events defined by independent random variables — the resampling table and the "resampling oracles" proof provide an efficient algorithm to find a configuration avoiding all bad events when the LLL conditions hold.
The probabilistic method is a cornerstone of extremal combinatorics: existence is often easier to prove than construction, and the LLL extends this to weakly dependent events.

🔥 Szemerédi's Regularity Lemma and the Triangle Removal Lemma

Regularity
The Regularity Lemma, Removal Lemma, Roth's Theorem, and Green-Tao
The Szemerédi regularity lemma (1978) is a structural result about large graphs: for any ε > 0, every sufficiently large graph G has an ε-regular partition of its vertex set into M(ε) blocks V₁, …, Vₘ (M depends only on ε, not on the size of G). A pair (V_i, V_j) is ε-regular if for any A ⊆ V_i with |A| ≥ ε|V_i| and B ⊆ V_j with |B| ≥ ε|V_j|, the edge densities satisfy |d(A,B) − d(V_i,V_j)| ≤ ε. The regularity lemma provides a "coarse-grained" approximation of any large graph. The triangle removal lemma (Ruzsa–Szemerédi, 1978): any graph on n vertices with o(n³) triangles can be made triangle-free by removing o(n²) edges. This has a stunning consequence: Roth's theorem (1953) — any subset A ⊆ ℕ with positive upper density contains a 3-term arithmetic progression. The proof: construct a tripartite graph encoding solutions to x + y = 2z; dense sets yield many triangles, and the removal lemma forces a progression. Szemerédi's theorem (1975): for any k ≥ 3, any subset of ℕ with positive upper density contains arbitrarily long k-term arithmetic progressions. The proof uses the regularity lemma for hypergraphs and the density increment method (Szemerédi's original approach) or ergodic theory (Furstenberg) or hypergraph regularity (Gowers, Rodl–Rucinski–Schacht). The Green–Tao theorem (2004): the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. The proof uses a transference principle: embed the primes into a pseudorandom measure ν (the W-trick), apply the Szemerédi theorem relative to ν by showing that any set with positive ν-density contains long APs (relative Szemerédi theorem). The regularity method extends to many extremal problems: the Erdős–Rothschild problem (counting H-free colorings), property testing (determining whether a graph is ε-far from H-free via random sampling), the removal lemma for hypergraphs, and the combinatorial Nullstellensatz (Alon, 1999) for polynomial method proofs in combinatorics.
The regularity lemma transforms arbitrary large graphs into structured pseudorandom partitions — a unifying framework for property testing, arithmetic progressions, and extremal graph theory.

Erdős–Stone Theorem and the Asymptotic Structure of Forbidden Subgraphs

The Erdős–Stone theorem (1946) is the cornerstone of extremal graph theory for non‑bipartite forbidden subgraphs. It states that for any graph H with chromatic number χ(H) = r + 1 ≥ 2, the extremal number satisfies

ex(n, H) = (1 − 1/r + o(1)) n²/2.

In words, the maximum number of edges in an n-vertex graph containing no copy of H is asymptotically the same as the Turán number for the complete r-partite graph. The theorem reduces the problem of determining ex(n, H) for non‑bipartite H to the chromatic number of H; the finer structure of H only affects the lower‑order o(n²) term. The original proof used a clever double‑counting argument combined with the pigeonhole principle. Later proofs via the Szemerédi regularity lemma give a more conceptual framework and also yield the Erdős–Simonovits stability theorem: if a graph has close to the extremal number of edges and is H-free, then it must be close in edit distance to the Turán graph Tr(n).

For bipartite H the chromatic number is 2, so the theorem only gives ex(n, H) = o(n²). The exact order of magnitude for bipartite graphs is a major open area — the Zarankiewicz problem and the Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem address the case H = Ks,t. The Erdős–Stone theorem also implies the Erdős–Gallai theorem on paths and cycles as a special case, and it underpins the modern theory of graph limits where the extremal density of a hereditary property is determined by the chromatic number of the forbidden subgraphs.

Applications extend beyond pure graph theory. In additive combinatorics, the theorem is used to prove that a set of integers with positive upper density contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions (Szemerédi's theorem) via the regularity lemma and the counting lemma. In computer science, it provides the theoretical basis for property testing algorithms: the triangle removal lemma, a corollary of the regularity lemma, shows that any graph with o(n³) triangles can be made triangle‑free by deleting o(n²) edges, which yields a constant‑query tester for triangle‑freeness.

The theorem's limitation is that it does not give the exact extremal number for specific H; determining the o(n²) term is often extremely difficult. For example, the exact value of ex(n, K4⁻) (the complete graph on four vertices minus one edge) is known only for certain ranges of n. The study of these finer terms leads to the Simonovits stability method and the recent breakthroughs on the Erdős–Rothschild problem, which counts the number of H-free colorings of a complete graph.

Kővári–Sós–Turán Theorem and the Zarankiewicz Problem

The Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem (1954) gives the best general upper bound for the Zarankiewicz problem: what is the maximum number of edges in an n × n bipartite graph that does not contain a complete bipartite subgraph Ks,t with s ≤ t? The theorem states

ex(n, Ks,t) ≤ ½ (t − 1)1/s n2 − 1/s + O(n).

Equivalently, for an n-vertex graph (not necessarily bipartite) the same bound holds up to a constant factor. The proof uses a double‑counting argument on the number of copies of Ks,1 (stars with s leaves) and applies Jensen's inequality to the degree sequence. The bound is tight up to the constant factor for many values of s, t; constructions from finite geometry (projective planes, polarity graphs) match the exponent 2 − 1/s when t is sufficiently large relative to s. For s = t = 2 the bound becomes ex(n, K2,2) ≤ ½ n3/2 + O(n), and the polarity graph of a projective plane of order q shows this is asymptotically tight.

The Zarankiewicz problem has deep connections to incidence geometry. The incidence graph of points and lines in a finite projective plane has no K2,2 because two points determine at most one line. The Kővári–Sós–Turán bound then implies that a set of n points and n lines in the plane has at most O(n3/2) incidences. This is weaker than the Szemerédi–Trotter bound O(n4/3), but the Kővári–Sós–Turán method works in any setting where the forbidden configuration is a complete bipartite graph, making it a versatile tool in extremal combinatorics and theoretical computer science (e.g., in the analysis of bipartite Ramsey numbers and in the construction of Ramsey graphs).

For fixed s and growing t, the exponent 2 − 1/s is best possible, but the constant factor (t − 1)1/s is not known to be optimal. Improving the constant, or determining the exact Zarankiewicz number for small parameters, remains an active area. The problem is also equivalent to determining the maximum number of edges in an n-vertex graph with no Ks,t subgraph, which is the same as the Turán number for the bipartite graph Ks,t. The Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem is the starting point for the study of bipartite Turán problems, which are far less understood than the non‑bipartite case covered by Erdős–Stone.

Applying the Kővári–Sós–Turán bound to point‑line incidences
Let P be a set of n points and L a set of n lines in the Euclidean plane. Show that the number of incidences I(P, L) is at most O(n3/2).
Construct the bipartite incidence graph G with vertex parts P and L; an edge connects p ∈ P to ℓ ∈ L iff p lies on ℓ. Two distinct points determine at most one line, so G contains no K2,2 (a cycle of length four). By the Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem with s = t = 2, the number of edges in an n × n bipartite graph with no K2,2 is at most ½ n3/2 + O(n). Hence I(P, L) = e(G) ≤ ½ n3/2 + O(n) = O(n3/2). This bound is tight up to the constant factor, as shown by the incidence graph of a finite projective plane of order q (where n = q² + q + 1).
Exam strategy for extremal bounds
When a problem asks for an upper bound on edges or incidences with a forbidden complete bipartite subgraph, immediately recall the Kővári–Sós–Turán exponent 2 − 1/s. Write the bound in the form c n2−1/s + O(n) and identify s from the forbidden configuration. If the forbidden graph is not complete bipartite, check whether it contains a Ks,t as a subgraph; then the same bound applies. For lower bounds, remember the polarity graph construction for K2,2 and norm‑graph constructions for larger s.

Hypergraph Turán Problems and the Erdős Conjecture on 3‑Uniform Hypergraphs

The Turán problem for k-uniform hypergraphs asks for the maximum number of edges in an n-vertex k-uniform hypergraph that does not contain a given k-uniform hypergraph F as a subhypergraph. The Turán density π(F) is defined as the limit (if it exists) of ex(n, F) / (n choose k). For graphs (k = 2) the Erdős–Stone theorem determines π(H) = 1 − 1/(χ(H) − 1) for all non‑bipartite H. For k ≥ 3 the situation is dramatically different: the Turán density is known for only a handful of hypergraphs, and even the density of the simplest 3‑uniform hypergraph, the complete 3‑uniform hypergraph on four vertices K4(3), remains a major open problem.

Turán (1941) conjectured that π(K4(3)) = 5/9. His construction partitions the vertex set into three parts V₁, V₂, V₃ of nearly equal size and takes all triples that intersect each part in either 1 or 2 vertices (i.e., triples not contained entirely in one part). This yields a 3‑uniform hypergraph with edge density 5/9 + o(1) and no K4(3). The best known upper bound, due to de Caen and Sidorenko (1998) and later improved by Razborov (2007) using flag algebras, is π(K4(3)) ≤ 0.561666… The gap between 5/9 ≈ 0.555… and 0.561… is narrow, but closing it completely has resisted all attempts. The problem is equivalent to determining the maximum edge density of a 3‑uniform hypergraph with no four vertices spanning all four possible triples.

Another celebrated case is the Fano plane F₇, the unique Steiner triple system on seven vertices. Its Turán density was determined exactly by de Caen and Sidorenko (1998): π(F₇) = 3/4. The extremal construction partitions the vertices into four parts and takes all triples that intersect at most three parts (i.e., all triples except those completely inside one part). This gives density 3/4, and the proof of the matching upper bound uses a clever combination of the regularity lemma for 3‑uniform hypergraphs and a stability argument.

The hypergraph regularity lemma, developed independently by Gowers (2007) and by Rödl, Schacht, Skokan, and others, extends Szemerédi's regularity lemma to k-uniform hypergraphs. It provides a partition of the vertex set into a bounded number of parts such that most k-tuples of parts are regular in a suitable sense. Together with a counting lemma, it allows one to transfer extremal problems for hypergraphs to problems about reduced hypergraphs, which are finite objects. This machinery was essential for the proof of the hypergraph removal lemma and for the recent progress on the Erdős–Sós conjecture for hypergraphs.

Despite these advances, the general hypergraph Turán problem is wide open. Even for 3‑uniform hypergraphs with four vertices, there are several non‑isomorphic hypergraphs (e.g., the “tight” 3‑path, the “loose” 3‑cycle) whose Turán densities are unknown. The Erdős conjecture on K4(3) remains the most famous unsolved problem in extremal hypergraph theory, and its resolution would likely require new ideas beyond the current regularity and flag algebra methods.

Ramsey Multiplicity, Goodman's Bound, and the Minimum Number of Monochromatic Cliques

Ramsey multiplicity asks: in any red/blue coloring of the edges of the complete graph Kn, what is the minimum possible number of monochromatic copies of a given graph H? For H = K₃ (triangles), Goodman (1959) gave an exact formula. Let M(n) be the minimum number of monochromatic triangles over all 2‑colorings of Kn. Goodman proved

M(n) = begin{cases} (1)/(24) n (n-2) (n-4) & if n is even, \\ (1)/(24) (n-1) (n-3) (n-5) & if n is odd. end{cases}

Asymptotically, M(n) = (1/4 + o(1)) (n choose 3). The extremal coloring is obtained by taking a balanced complete bipartite graph in one color (say red) and its complement (two disjoint cliques) in the other color (blue). This coloring yields exactly the Goodman bound. The proof uses a simple counting argument: for each vertex, count the number of monochromatic triangles containing it, sum over vertices, and apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the degree sequences in the two colors.

For larger cliques Kk with k ≥ 4, the exact minimum is unknown. The random coloring gives an expected number of 21−(k choose 2) (n choose k) monochromatic Kk. It was conjectured (the “Ramsey multiplicity conjecture”) that the random coloring minimizes the number of monochromatic Kk for all k. Thomason (1989) disproved this for k = 4 by constructing a coloring with fewer monochromatic K₄ than the random coloring. The current best lower bounds for the Ramsey multiplicity constant ck = limn→∞ M(n, Kk) / (n choose k) come from flag algebra computations (Razborov, 2008). For k = 4, the exact value of c₄ is still unknown; the best known upper bound is approximately 0.0004, while the random coloring gives 2−5 = 1/32 ≈ 0.03125. The huge gap illustrates the difficulty of the problem.

The study of Ramsey multiplicity is closely related to the problem of determining the Ramsey number R(k, k). A lower bound on the multiplicity constant implies a lower bound on the Ramsey number via the probabilistic method: if every coloring has many monochromatic Kk, then a random coloring is likely to have at least one, so R(k, k) must be large. Conversely, constructions with few monochromatic cliques give upper bounds on Ramsey numbers. The interplay between multiplicity and Ramsey numbers is a central theme in modern Ramsey theory.

Beyond cliques, the multiplicity problem for other graphs (e.g., cycles, paths) has been studied. For bipartite graphs, the minimum number of monochromatic copies can be zero if the Ramsey number is larger than n. For non‑bipartite graphs, the Erdős–Stone theorem implies that the multiplicity constant is positive. The exact constants are known only in a few cases, and the general problem remains a rich area for the application of flag algebras and the regularity method.

Graph Limits, the Regularity Method, and Property Testing

The theory of graph limits, initiated by Lovász and Szegedy (2006), provides a unified analytic framework for dense graph sequences. A graphon is a symmetric measurable function W : [0,1]² → [0,1]. Every finite graph G can be represented as a step‑function graphon by partitioning [0,1] into |V(G)| intervals and setting W(x, y) = 1 if the corresponding vertices are adjacent, 0 otherwise. The cut distance between graphons metrizes the space of graphons, and a sequence of graphs converges if their associated graphons converge in cut distance. The regularity lemma is equivalent to the statement that the space of graphons is compact in the cut metric; every sequence of graphs has a subsequence converging to some graphon.

The counting lemma for graphons states that if a sequence of graphs converges to a graphon W, then the density of any fixed subgraph H in the sequence converges to the homomorphism density t(H, W) = ∫[0,1]|V(H)| ∏{i,j}∈E(H) W(xi, xj) dx. This allows one to translate extremal problems about subgraph densities into variational problems over graphons. For example, the Erdős–Stone theorem corresponds to the fact that the maximum edge density of a graphon with t(H, W) = 0 is 1 − 1/(χ(H) − 1).

The triangle removal lemma, a direct corollary of the regularity lemma, has a clean formulation in the language of graphons: if a graphon W satisfies t(K₃, W) = 0, then W can be made triangle‑free by changing it on a set of measure zero. This analytic version implies the combinatorial removal lemma with quantitative bounds (though the bounds from the regularity lemma are of tower type). The removal lemma is the engine behind property testing: to test whether a graph is triangle‑free, one samples a constant number of vertices (independent of n) and checks if they form a triangle. If the graph is ε‑far from triangle‑free (requires deleting at least ε n² edges), the removal lemma guarantees that the probability of finding a triangle is at least some constant depending only on ε. This yields a tester with query complexity O(1/ε²).

The regularity method extends to hypergraphs via the hypergraph regularity lemma, leading to the hypergraph removal lemma and the proof of Szemerédi's theorem for arithmetic progressions of any length. The Green–Tao theorem on arithmetic progressions in the primes uses a transference principle: the primes are embedded into a pseudorandom measure (the “W‑trick”), and a relative version of Szemerédi's theorem is proved using the regularity lemma for the majorant measure. This demonstrates the power of the regularity method beyond pure graph theory.

Recent developments include the Frieze–Kannan weak regularity lemma, which provides a coarser partition with much better quantitative bounds (exponential instead of tower), and the strong regularity lemma of Alon, Fischer, Krivelevich, and Szegedy, which allows one to approximate a graph by a regular partition that is itself regular. These tools have found applications in algorithmic graph theory, such as approximation algorithms for dense Max‑Cut and in the study of graph limits for sparse graphs (the L² theory of graphons). The interplay between regularity, removal, and property testing continues to be a driving force in extremal combinatorics and theoretical computer science.

Exam strategy for graph limits and removal lemmas
When a question mentions “density of triangles tends to zero” or “can be made triangle‑free by removing o(n²) edges”, immediately invoke the triangle removal lemma. For property testing, remember that the query complexity is a function of ε only, not n. In graph limit problems, identify the relevant graphon and write the homomorphism density integral. The cut distance is the natural metric; convergence in cut distance implies convergence of all subgraph densities. Use the compactness of the graphon space to extract limits.
  • Confusing the Erdős–Stone theorem (which gives asymptotic extremal numbers for non‑bipartite graphs) with the exact Turán theorem (which gives the exact extremal number for complete graphs).
  • Assuming the Kővári–Sós–Turán bound is tight for all s, t; the constant factor is not known to be optimal, and constructions matching the exponent exist only for certain parameter ranges.
  • Believing the Turán density of K₄⁽³⁾ is known to be 5/9; it is a long‑standing conjecture, not a theorem.
  • Thinking the random coloring always minimizes the number of monochromatic Kₖ; Thomason’s counterexample for k = 4 shows this is false.
  • Overlooking that the regularity lemma gives tower‑type bounds, which are often too weak for quantitative applications; the weak regularity lemma provides exponential bounds and is more suitable for algorithmic purposes.
  • Mixing up the triangle removal lemma (which removes o(n²) edges to destroy all triangles) with the regularity lemma itself (which provides an ε‑regular partition).
  • Review these traps carefully to avoid common mistakes.

Supersaturation and Stability in Extremal Graph Theory

Supersaturation quantifies how many copies of a forbidden subgraph must appear once the edge count exceeds the Turán threshold. For a fixed graph H with chromatic number χ(H) ≥ 3, the Erdős–Simonovits theorem states that for every ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that any n-vertex graph with at least (1 − 1/(χ(H)−1) + ε) n²/2 edges contains at least δ n|V(H)| copies of H. The proof uses the regularity lemma to find a dense reduced graph that already contains a copy of H, then lifts it via the counting lemma. Stability theorems go further: they describe the structure of extremal or near‑extremal graphs. The classic Erdős–Simonovits stability theorem for Kr+1 says that if an n-vertex graph has no Kr+1 and at least (1 − 1/r − o(1)) n²/2 edges, then it can be made r-partite by deleting o(n²) edges. In other words, the extremal construction (the Turán graph T_r(n)) is essentially unique. Stability results are crucial for exact Turán numbers: once one knows that near‑extremal graphs are close to the Turán graph, a careful case analysis often yields the exact extremal number for all sufficiently large n. For bipartite H the situation is different; supersaturation still holds but the exponent of n in the lower bound depends on the Zarankiewicz exponent. The Kővári–Sós–Turán theorem gives a supersaturation statement: if a bipartite graph with parts of size m, n has more than c n2−1/s edges (for fixed s ≤ t), then it contains many copies of Ks,t. Recent work by Conlon, Fox, and Sudakov refined these bounds using dependent random choice, showing that the number of copies grows like a positive power of the excess density. Stability for bipartite problems is more delicate; for cycles C2k the extremal graphs are known to be close to a blow‑up of a C2k-free bipartite graph, but a full structural description remains open for many H.

Dependent Random Choice and its Applications to Bipartite Turán Problems

Dependent random choice (DRC) is a probabilistic technique that finds a large subset of vertices in a dense graph such that every small tuple of vertices in the subset has many common neighbours. The basic lemma: let G be a bipartite graph with parts A, B and edge density α. For integers t, r, m with α^t |B| − (|A| choose r) (m/|B|)^t ≥ 1, there exists a subset A' ⊆ A of size at least α^t |A|/2 such that every r-tuple in A' has at least m common neighbours in B. The proof picks t random vertices from B (with replacement) and lets A' be their common neighbourhood; a union bound over bad r-tuples gives the condition. DRC has become a workhorse for bipartite Turán problems. For example, it yields the best known upper bounds for ex(n, Ks,t) when t is large compared to s. The argument: assume a Ks,t-free graph has many edges; apply DRC to find a large set A' where every s-tuple has many common neighbours. By the pigeonhole principle some s-tuple then has at least t common neighbours, creating a Ks,t — a contradiction. This gives ex(n, Ks,t) ≤ c n2−1/s for t ≥ (s−1)!. More refined versions of DRC, using multiple rounds of random choice, improve the exponent for specific pairs (s,t). DRC also underpins the proof of the Erdős–Simonovits conjecture for bipartite graphs with a vertex of degree one (so‑called “trees”). In that setting one iteratively builds a large set of vertices with many common neighbours, then embeds the tree greedily. The method extends to hypergraphs: a hypergraph version of DRC finds a large set of vertices such that every small tuple lies in many edges, leading to supersaturation results for hypergraph Turán problems. The technique is elementary — it uses only the probabilistic method and counting — yet it captures the combinatorial structure that forces dense graphs to contain prescribed bipartite subgraphs.

Technique: Applying Dependent Random Choice to Bound ex(n, K2,3)
Show that any n-vertex graph with more than 2 n3/2 edges contains a copy of K2,3. Use the dependent random choice lemma with parameters t = 2, r = 2, m = 3.
Let G be an n-vertex graph with e(G) > 2 n3/2. The average degree is d = 2e(G)/n > 4 n1/2. Pick a vertex v uniformly at random; its neighbourhood N(v) has expected size d. By Markov, there exists a vertex v with |N(v)| ≥ d/2 > 2 n1/2. Let A = N(v) and B = V(G) {v}. The bipartite graph between A and B has at least e(G) − (n−1) edges, so its density α satisfies α ≥ (2 n3/2 − n) / (|A|·|B|) ≥ (n3/2) / (2 n1/2·n) = 1/2 for large n. Apply DRC with t = 2, r = 2, m = 3. We need α^t |B| − (|A| choose 2) (m/|B|)^t ≥ 1. Here α^t |B| ≥ (1/2)2 · n = n/4. The second term is at most (|A|2/2)·(3/n)2 ≤ ( (2 n1/2)2 /2 )·(9/n2) = (2 n)·(9/n2) = 18/n. For n ≥ 72, n/4 − 18/n ≥ 1. Hence there exists A' ⊆ A of size at least α^t |A|/2 ≥ (1/4)·2 n1/2/2 = n1/2/4 such that every pair in A' has at least 3 common neighbours in B. Since |A'| ≥ 2 for large n, pick any two vertices in A'; they have ≥ 3 common neighbours, forming a K2,3 with those neighbours. Thus e(G) > 2 n3/2 forces a K2,3.
Exam‑day strategy for DRC problems
When a question asks for an upper bound on ex(n, H) with H bipartite, first check whether H has a vertex of degree one (tree case) or is a complete bipartite graph Ks,t. For Ks,t with t large, the standard DRC lemma with t = s often gives the exponent 2−1/s. Write down the density α, choose parameters t, r, m to satisfy the inequality α^t |B| > (|A| choose r)(m/|B|)^t, and solve for the edge count. Remember that the lemma guarantees a subset A' of size at least α^t|A|/2; you only need |A'| ≥ r to find the forbidden configuration. Practice the algebraic manipulation of the inequality until it becomes routine.

The Container Method for Independent Sets in Hypergraphs

The container method, developed by Balogh, Morris, Samotij, and independently by Saxton and Thomason, provides a powerful framework for counting independent sets in uniform hypergraphs and for proving supersaturation and extremal results. Given a k‑uniform hypergraph H on vertex set V, a family C of subsets of V is called a set of containers if every independent set of H is contained in some member of C, each container has size at most (1 − δ)|V| for some δ > 0, and the number of containers is at most exp(o(|V|)). The method constructs containers via an iterative algorithm: while the current hypergraph has many edges, pick a vertex of high degree, add it to a “fingerprint” set, and delete its neighbourhood; the remaining vertices form a container. The key combinatorial lemma bounds the number of possible fingerprints, yielding the exponential bound on |C|. Applications are numerous. In graph theory, taking H to be the hypergraph of copies of a fixed graph F in the complete graph K_n, containers give a new proof of the Erdős–Frankl–Rödl theorem: the number of F-free graphs on n vertices is 2ex(n,F)+o(n²). For hypergraph Turán problems, containers yield the best known upper bounds for the Turán density of many 3‑uniform hypergraphs, including the famous conjecture that π(K₄⁽³⁾) = 5/9. The method also gives a short proof of the hypergraph removal lemma: if a k‑uniform hypergraph has few copies of a fixed subhypergraph F, then one can delete a small fraction of edges to make it F-free. The container theorem implies a counting version: the number of F-free subhypergraphs of a given hypergraph is at most 2(1−δ)e(H) for some δ > 0. This counting lemma is the engine behind many recent results in extremal combinatorics, such as the proof that the number of C₄-free graphs is 2(1/2+o(1))n3/2 and the resolution of the Erdős–Moser problem on sum‑free sets. The container method is notable for its algorithmic nature; the fingerprint construction can be turned into a polynomial‑time algorithm that, given a hypergraph, outputs a small family of containers covering all independent sets.

Flag Algebras and the Solution of Turán-type Problems

Flag algebras, introduced by Razborov in 2007, provide a systematic algebraic framework for solving extremal problems in dense graphs and hypergraphs. The central idea is to work with limits of sequences of graphs, represented by graphons, and to encode subgraph densities as elements of a commutative algebra. A type σ is a fixed small labelled graph; a σ‑flag is a graph with an embedding of σ. The algebra A^σ consists of formal linear combinations of isomorphism classes of σ‑flags, modulo the relations that hold for all large graphs. The product of two flags is defined by randomly gluing them along the labelled vertices and taking the expected density of the resulting flag. This algebraic structure allows one to translate inequalities between subgraph densities into semidefinite programming problems. Razborov used flag algebras to determine the exact Turán density of the 3‑uniform hypergraph C₅⁽³⁾ (the tight 5‑cycle) and to prove that the Turán density of the 3‑uniform hypergraph F₅ (the 5‑vertex hypergraph with edges 123, 124, 125, 345) is 3/4. The method also gave the first computer‑assisted proof of the Erdős–Sós conjecture for graphs of girth at least 5, and it resolved several long‑standing problems in extremal combinatorics, such as the minimum number of monochromatic triangles in a 2‑colouring of K_n (Goodman’s bound) and the asymptotic value of the Ramsey multiplicity constant for K₄. The workflow: formulate the extremal problem as maximizing or minimizing a linear functional of subgraph densities subject to the constraints that the densities come from a graphon. This becomes a semidefinite program over the flag algebra, which can be solved numerically to high precision. The numerical solution often suggests a conjectured extremal construction; one then proves the exact bound by exhibiting a sum‑of‑squares decomposition in the flag algebra, which certifies the inequality. Flag algebras have been extended to hypergraphs, permutations, and sparse graphs (via the theory of graph limits for sparse sequences). While the method is not fully automated — the choice of types and flags requires insight — it has become a standard tool for attacking Turán-type problems where traditional combinatorial arguments stall.

The Polynomial Method in Extremal Combinatorics

The polynomial method uses algebraic properties of polynomials over finite fields to prove combinatorial bounds. A foundational result is the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz (Alon, 1999): let f ∈ 𝔽[x₁,…,x_n] be a polynomial of total degree ∑ d_i, and suppose the coefficient of ∏ x_id_i is non‑zero. Then for any subsets S_i ⊆ 𝔽 with |S_i| > d_i, there exists (s₁,…,s_n) ∈ ∏ S_i such that f(s₁,…,s_n) ≠ 0. This simple principle yields powerful applications. In extremal graph theory, it gives a short proof of the Cauchy–Davenport theorem and the Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv theorem. For Turán-type problems, the polynomial method underlies the proof of the Frankl–Wilson theorem on set systems with restricted intersections: if a family of subsets of an n‑element set has the property that the size of the intersection of any two distinct sets lies in a set L of s integers, then the family size is at most ∑i=0^s (n choose i). The proof constructs a polynomial that vanishes on all characteristic vectors of sets in the family except one, and uses linear independence over a finite field. The method also solves the cap set problem in affine spaces over 𝔽₃: the maximum size of a subset of 𝔽₃^n with no three‑term arithmetic progression is at most c·2.756^n (Ellenberg–Gijswijt, 2017), using the slice rank of a polynomial. In graph theory, the polynomial method gives the best known bounds for the Erdős–Moser problem on unit distances and for the number of edges in a graph with no cycle of length 2k (the Bondy–Simonovits conjecture for even cycles). A recent breakthrough is the proof of the sensitivity conjecture (Huang, 2019) using a clever polynomial construction on the hypercube. The polynomial method often provides tighter bounds than probabilistic or combinatorial arguments because it exploits algebraic structure that is invisible to purely counting techniques. However, it requires the problem to be expressible in terms of polynomial vanishing or non‑vanishing over a field, which limits its direct applicability to many classical Turán problems.

Common Traps

  • Confusing supersaturation (which guarantees many copies once the edge count exceeds the Turán threshold) with stability (which describes the structure of near‑extremal graphs); they are related but distinct concepts.
  • Assuming the dependent random choice lemma works for any choice of parameters without checking the inequality α^t |B| > (|A| choose r)(m/|B|)^t; the inequality is the heart of the method.
  • Believing the container method gives exact counts of independent sets; it only provides an exponential upper bound on the number of containers, not the exact number of independent sets.
  • Thinking flag algebras automatically produce a human‑readable proof; the semidefinite program yields a numerical certificate that must be converted into a sum‑of‑squares decomposition for a rigorous proof.
  • Applying the polynomial method to problems that lack a natural algebraic formulation; the method requires a polynomial whose zero set encodes the combinatorial condition.
  • Overlooking that the regularity lemma gives tower‑type bounds while the weak regularity lemma gives exponential bounds; choosing the wrong version can make a quantitative argument fail.
  • Mixing up the Turán density of a hypergraph (the limit of ex(n,F)/ (n choose k)) with the exact Turán number for small n; asymptotic results do not determine exact values for finite n.

The Hypergraph Regularity Lemma and the Hypergraph Removal Lemma

The hypergraph regularity lemma extends Szemerédi’s graph regularity lemma to k‑uniform hypergraphs. For a given ε > 0 and integer k ≥ 2, there exists a partition of the vertex set into a bounded number of parts such that almost all k‑tuples of parts are ε‑regular, meaning the edge density of the hypergraph restricted to those parts is close to the expected density in a random k‑uniform hypergraph with the same edge probabilities. The bound on the number of parts is of tower type, growing as a tower of exponentials of height polynomial in 1/ε. This lemma was proved independently by Gowers and by Rödl, Skokan, Nagle, and Schacht in the early 2000s.

The hypergraph removal lemma follows as a corollary: for every ε > 0 and every fixed k‑uniform hypergraph F, there exists δ > 0 such that any k‑uniform hypergraph on n vertices with at most δ n|V(F)| copies of F can be made F‑free by removing at most ε n^k edges. The proof uses the regularity lemma to find a regular partition, then applies a counting lemma to show that if few copies of F exist, the edges participating in those copies can be deleted without destroying the regularity structure. This result has profound consequences in property testing, where it yields testers for hypergraph properties with query complexity independent of n.

A key technical difficulty is the definition of regularity for k‑tuples when k ≥ 3. Unlike graphs, where regularity of a pair (A,B) is defined by edge density between A and B, for hypergraphs one must consider the density of edges across a collection of k parts simultaneously. The counting lemma for hypergraphs requires a more intricate induction on the uniformity, and the proof of the regularity lemma itself uses an energy increment argument that tracks a suitable notion of mean square density across partitions. The tower‑type bound is known to be necessary for the strong regularity lemma, but weaker versions with exponential bounds suffice for many applications, such as the weak regularity lemma of Frieze and Kannan.

Applications include the proof of the multidimensional Szemerédi theorem via the hypergraph removal lemma, the determination of Turán densities for certain 3‑uniform hypergraphs, and the analysis of arithmetic progressions in dense subsets of ℤ/Nℤ. The method also underpins the proof of the density Hales–Jewett theorem by the Polymath project, where the hypergraph regularity lemma is used to find combinatorial lines in dense subsets of [3]^n.

The Green–Tao Theorem and Arithmetic Progressions in the Primes

The Green–Tao theorem, proved in 2004, states that the prime numbers contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. The proof combines three major ingredients: a transference principle that allows one to work with a dense subset of a pseudorandom measure instead of the primes directly, a dense model theorem that approximates the indicator function of the primes by a bounded function with respect to the Gowers uniformity norms, and Szemerédi’s theorem on arithmetic progressions in dense subsets of integers.

The transference principle replaces the set of primes by a weighted set where each integer n is assigned a weight ν(n) that mimics the von Mangoldt function Λ(n) but is supported on a pseudorandom majorant. The majorant ν is constructed using a truncated divisor sum and satisfies the linear forms condition and the correlation condition, which together imply that ν is pseudorandom with respect to the Gowers U^k norms for all k. The dense model theorem then provides a function f : ℤ/Nℤ → [0,1] such that the difference between the weighted prime indicator and f is small in the Gowers U^k norm for a suitable k depending on the desired progression length.

Once a dense model f is obtained, Szemerédi’s theorem (or its finitary version) guarantees that f contains a k‑term arithmetic progression provided its density exceeds a threshold depending on k. The transference principle then lifts this progression back to the primes, showing that the primes themselves contain a k‑term progression. The quantitative bounds are extremely weak: the length of the progression that can be guaranteed in the primes up to N is at most log log N, and the proof does not give an effective bound for the smallest N containing a progression of given length.

Subsequent work by Green, Tao, and Ziegler extended the result to polynomial progressions and to the primes in arithmetic progressions. The methods have also been adapted to prove the existence of constellations in the Gaussian primes and to study patterns in the Möbius function. The Green–Tao theorem remains a landmark in additive combinatorics, demonstrating that the primes, despite their multiplicative definition, exhibit rich additive structure.

Worked Example: Applying the Hypergraph Removal Lemma
Let H be a 3‑uniform hypergraph on n vertices with at most δ n4 copies of the complete 3‑uniform hypergraph K4(3) (four vertices, all four triples). Show that one can delete at most ε n3 edges to make H K4(3)‑free, provided δ is sufficiently small relative to ε.
Apply the 3‑uniform hypergraph regularity lemma with parameter ε/2 to obtain an ε/2‑regular partition of V(H) into t parts, where t ≤ T(ε). By the counting lemma for 3‑uniform hypergraphs, the number of copies of K4(3) in H is approximated by the sum over all 4‑tuples of parts of the product of the densities of the four triples among those parts, up to an error of (ε/2) n4. If H has at most δ n4 copies, then for all but at most (ε/2) t4 choices of four parts, the product of the four densities is at most 2δ. Choose δ small enough so that 2δ < (ε/2)4. Then any 4‑tuple of parts with all four densities ≥ ε/2 would contribute at least (ε/2)4 n4 copies, contradicting the bound. Hence at most (ε/2) t4 4‑tuples have all four densities ≥ ε/2. Delete all edges that lie in any triple of parts whose density is < ε/2. The number of deleted edges is at most (ε/2) n3. The remaining hypergraph has no K4(3) because any four vertices would have to come from four parts with all four densities ≥ ε/2, which does not exist. Thus H can be made K4(3)‑free by deleting at most ε n3 edges.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a quantitative bound from the hypergraph removal lemma, remember that the dependence of δ on ε is of tower type. Do not attempt to write an explicit formula; instead state that δ = δ(ε, F) exists by the lemma and that the proof uses the regularity lemma with parameter ε/|V(F)|. For the Green–Tao theorem, focus on the three‑step structure: pseudorandom majorant, dense model theorem, and Szemerédi’s theorem. Examiners often test whether you can identify which step uses the Gowers U^k norms and which step requires the linear forms condition.

The Polynomial Method for the Cap Set Problem and Slice Rank

The cap set problem asks for the maximum size of a subset A ⊂ 𝔽₃^n containing no three distinct elements x, y, z with x + y + z = 0 (equivalently, no three‑term arithmetic progression). For decades the best upper bound was of the form c·3^n / n, but in 2017 Ellenberg and Gijswijt, building on work of Croot, Lev, and Pach, proved that |A| ≤ c·2.756^n. The proof uses the polynomial method in the form of the slice rank of a tensor.

Define a tensor T : (𝔽₃^n)3 → 𝔽₃ by T(x,y,z) = 1 if x + y + z = 0 and 0 otherwise. The slice rank of a tensor is the minimum r such that the tensor can be written as a sum of r tensors each of which depends on only one coordinate (i.e., a slice). A key lemma shows that if A ⊂ 𝔽₃^n is a cap set, then the restriction of T to A3 has slice rank at least |A|. On the other hand, one can construct a low‑degree polynomial P(x,y,z) that agrees with T on A3 and whose slice rank is bounded by the number of monomials of degree at most d in each variable. Choosing d ≈ 2n/3 gives a bound on the number of monomials, which is roughly 2.756^n. This yields |A| ≤ 2.756^n.

The slice rank method is more flexible than the original polynomial method because it works directly with tensors and does not require the polynomial to vanish on a grid. It has since been applied to other problems, including the Erdős–Szemerédi sunflower conjecture for three‑uniform hypergraphs and bounds on the size of progression‑free sets in ℤ/Nℤ. The technique also connects to the theory of matrix multiplication, where the slice rank of the matrix multiplication tensor gives the exponent of matrix multiplication.

A crucial observation is that the polynomial P is constructed as a product of linear forms (x_i + y_i + z_i) over the coordinates i = 1,…,n, truncated to degree d. The slice rank bound follows from the fact that each monomial in the expansion corresponds to a slice. The method highlights the power of algebraic techniques in extremal combinatorics: a problem that resisted Fourier‑analytic and probabilistic approaches for years was solved by a clever polynomial construction and a simple linear‑algebraic invariant.

The Sensitivity Conjecture and Huang's Spectral Proof

The sensitivity conjecture, posed by Nisan and Szegedy in 1994, asked whether the sensitivity of a Boolean function f : {0,1}^n → {0,1} is polynomially related to its block sensitivity, degree, and other complexity measures. In 2019 Hao Huang proved the conjecture by showing that the sensitivity s(f) is at least √(deg(f)), where deg(f) is the degree of f as a real polynomial. The proof uses a spectral argument on the n‑dimensional hypercube graph Q_n.

Huang constructed a 2^n × 2^n symmetric matrix A_n whose rows and columns are indexed by vertices of Q_n, with entries A_n(x,y) = 1 if x and y differ in exactly one coordinate and the parity of the number of 1’s in x is even, and A_n(x,y) = -1 if the parity is odd. This matrix is a signed adjacency matrix of Q_n. Huang proved that the eigenvalues of A_n are ±√n, each with multiplicity 2n-1. The key step is to show that for any induced subgraph H of Q_n with more than 2n-1 vertices, the maximum degree of H is at least √n.

Given a Boolean function f with sensitivity s, consider the set S = {x : f(x) = 1}. If |S| > 2n-1, then the induced subgraph on S has maximum degree at least √n, which implies s(f) ≥ √n. If |S| ≤ 2n-1, apply the same argument to the complement. Since deg(f) ≤ n, this yields s(f) ≥ √(deg(f)). The proof is remarkably short and uses only linear algebra and the fact that the eigenvalues of the signed adjacency matrix are ±√n, which follows from an inductive construction of A_n using the tensor product with the 2×2 matrix [[1,1],[1,-1]].

Huang’s proof resolved a long‑standing open problem and demonstrated the power of spectral methods in Boolean function analysis. The result also implies that the block sensitivity bs(f) is at most s(f)2, and that the degree, approximate degree, and quantum query complexity are all polynomially related to sensitivity. The method has inspired further work on the relationship between algebraic and combinatorial measures of Boolean functions.

The Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem and its Stability Versions

The Erdős–Ko–Rado (EKR) theorem, proved in 1961, states that for n ≥ 2k, the maximum size of an intersecting family of k‑element subsets of an n‑element set is (n-1 choose k-1), achieved by taking all k‑sets containing a fixed element. The theorem has been extended in many directions: to cross‑intersecting families, to vector spaces over finite fields (the q‑analog), to permutations, and to graphs. A stability version asks: if an intersecting family has size close to the maximum, must it be close in structure to a star (all sets containing a fixed element)?

Frankl and Füredi proved in 1986 that for n > 2k, any intersecting family of k‑sets with size at least (1 - c/k) (n-1 choose k-1) is a subfamily of a star, where c is an absolute constant. Later, Dinur and Friedgut gave a Fourier‑analytic proof of a stability theorem for the EKR theorem on the Boolean cube, showing that any intersecting family of measure close to 1/2 is close to a dictatorship. For the uniform measure on k‑sets, Ellis, Filmus, and Friedgut proved a stability result using the junta method: if an intersecting family has size at least (1 - ε) (n-1 choose k-1), then it can be made a star by adding and removing at most O(ε) (n-1 choose k-1) sets.

The proof techniques vary. The original EKR proof used shifting (compression) operations that preserve intersectingness and increase the size of the family until it becomes a star. The stability proofs often combine shifting with an analysis of the “shadow” of the family or with spectral methods on the Johnson graph. The junta method uses the fact that the indicator function of an intersecting family has small influences, so it can be approximated by a function depending on few coordinates, which must then be a star.

Stability versions are crucial for applications in property testing and for proving exact results for finite n. For example, the exact maximum size of an intersecting family of k‑sets for n = 2k+1 is (2k choose k-1), which is larger than the star size. Stability results help identify the extremal configurations for all n and k. The EKR theorem and its stability versions remain a central topic in extremal set theory, illustrating the interplay between combinatorial shifting, Fourier analysis, and spectral graph theory.

  • Confusing the hypergraph regularity lemma’s tower‑type bound with the exponential bound of the weak regularity lemma; the former is necessary for the counting lemma, the latter suffices for many property‑testing applications.
  • Assuming the Green–Tao theorem gives an effective bound for the smallest prime progression of length k; the proof is non‑effective and the best known quantitative bounds are of tower type.
  • Thinking the slice rank argument for the cap set problem works for any field; the construction uses the specific property that x + y + z = 0 over 𝔽₃ is equivalent to a three‑term progression, and the degree bound relies on the field size.
  • Believing Huang’s spectral proof of the sensitivity conjecture uses the standard adjacency matrix of the hypercube; it uses a signed adjacency matrix with eigenvalues ±√n, which is essential for the degree bound.
  • Overlooking that the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem requires n ≥ 2k; for n < 2k the whole family of k‑sets is intersecting and the maximum is (n choose k).

These advanced topics illustrate how algebraic, spectral, and probabilistic methods intertwine to solve deep problems in extremal combinatorics.

The Kohayakawa–Rödl Regularity Lemma for Sparse Graphs

The classical Szemerédi regularity lemma applies to dense graphs, guaranteeing an ε‑regular partition where most pairs have density close to the overall edge density. For sparse graphs, where the edge density tends to zero as the number of vertices grows, the original lemma becomes vacuous because the error term ε dominates the density. Kohayakawa and Rödl (1997) developed a version that works for graphs with density p = p(n) satisfying p ≫ (log n / n)1/2. Their lemma states that for every ε > 0 there exist constants C and n₀ such that any graph G on n ≥ n₀ vertices with at most p n² edges admits an equitable partition V₁,…,V_k (k ≤ C) where all but at most ε k² pairs (V_i,V_j) are (ε,p)‑regular: for all subsets X ⊆ V_i, Y ⊆ V_j with |X| ≥ ε|V_i|, |Y| ≥ ε|V_j|, the relative density d(X,Y) satisfies |d(X,Y) − d(V_i,V_j)| ≤ ε p. The proof follows the energy‑increment argument of the dense case but replaces the L² energy with a weighted energy that scales with p. A crucial technical tool is the KLR conjecture (proved by Conlon, Fox, and Zhao in 2014), which gives a counting lemma for sparse regular pairs: if a graph H has maximum degree Δ, then the number of copies of H in a sparse regular partition is (1 ± ε) pe(H) ∏|V_i|v(H). This counting lemma enables the transfer of many dense extremal results to the sparse setting, for example the sparse version of the Erdős–Stone theorem and the sparse triangle removal lemma. The sparse regularity lemma also underpins the proof of the Green–Tao theorem on arithmetic progressions in the primes, where the primes are embedded in a pseudorandom majorant and the relative density of the primes inside that majorant plays the role of p. The tower‑type bound on the number of parts remains, but the dependence on p is only polynomial, making the lemma applicable to a wide range of sparse structures.

The Combinatorial Nullstellensatz and the Alon–Füredi Theorem

The Combinatorial Nullstellensatz, introduced by Noga Alon in 1999, is a powerful algebraic tool that translates combinatorial existence problems into statements about non‑vanishing of polynomial coefficients. Let F be a field and f ∈ F[x₁,…,x_n] a polynomial. If the total degree of f is ∑i=1^n d_i and the coefficient of ∏ x_id_i is non‑zero, then for any subsets S_i ⊆ F with |S_i| > d_i there exists a point (s₁,…,s_n) ∈ ∏ S_i such that f(s₁,…,s_n) ≠ 0. This simple criterion has yielded short proofs of classical results such as the Cauchy–Davenport theorem, the Chevalley–Warning theorem, and the Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv theorem. A particularly striking application is the Alon–Füredi theorem (1993), which determines the minimum number of non‑zeros of a polynomial on a Cartesian product. Let S₁,…,S_n be finite subsets of a field F and let f ∈ F[x₁,…,x_n] be a polynomial that does not vanish identically on ∏ S_i. If deg f = ∑ d_i and the coefficient of ∏ x_id_i is non‑zero, then the number of zeros of f in ∏ S_i is at most ∏ |S_i| − ∏ (|S_i| − d_i). Equivalently, the number of non‑zeros is at least ∏ (|S_i| − d_i). This bound is sharp and generalises the DeMillo–Lipton–Schwartz–Zippel lemma. The theorem has been used to prove the Kemnitz conjecture on zero‑sum subsequences in ℤ_p², to give a new proof of the Cauchy–Davenport inequality, and to establish lower bounds for the size of blocking sets in finite projective planes. The method also extends to the polynomial method for the cap set problem, where the slice rank argument can be viewed as a tensor‑rank version of the Nullstellensatz. In algorithmic contexts, the Nullstellensatz provides a deterministic polynomial‑time algorithm for finding a non‑zero evaluation when the sets S_i are given explicitly, which is useful in derandomisation of the polynomial identity testing problem.

The Entropy Method and Shearer's Lemma in Extremal Combinatorics

The entropy method, pioneered by Radhakrishnan and others in the 1990s, uses information‑theoretic inequalities to bound the size of combinatorial structures. The central object is the Shannon entropy H(X) of a random variable X. For a family ℱ of subsets of [n], one considers a uniformly random element F ∈ ℱ and studies the entropies of its projections onto coordinates. Shearer's Lemma (1985) states that if a family of subsets {S₁,…,S_m} of [n] covers each element i ∈ [n] at least t times, then for any random variable X = (X₁,…,X_n), H(X) ≤ (1/t) ∑j=1^m H(XS_j). This inequality is a powerful tool for proving upper bounds on the size of families with restricted intersections. A classic application is the proof of the Loomis–Whitney inequality: for any finite set A ⊂ ℤ³, |A|² ≤ |πxy(A)| ⋅ |πyz(A)| ⋅ |πzx(A)|, where π denotes orthogonal projection. In extremal set theory, the entropy method gave a simple proof of the Bollobás–Thomason box theorem and of the Frankl–Füredi bound on the size of families with forbidden intersections. For the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem, one can define a random intersecting family and use Shearer's Lemma to show that its entropy is maximised by a star, yielding an alternative proof of the stability version. The method also underpins the proof of the Kahn–Lovász theorem on the number of independent sets in regular graphs, and the recent bounds on the number of independent sets in hypergraphs via the container method. A key technical step is the use of the chain rule for entropy and the fact that conditioning reduces entropy. When applying the entropy method, one often constructs a probability distribution on the objects of interest (e.g., a random independent set) and then bounds the entropy of the whole object by the sum of entropies of its local views. The tightness of the bound is usually achieved by product distributions, which correspond to extremal configurations that are “independent” across coordinates. The entropy method has the advantage of being robust under generalisations to weighted settings and to continuous analogues, where differential entropy replaces discrete entropy.

The Sunflower Lemma and Recent Bounds

A sunflower (or Δ‑system) with k petals is a collection of k sets whose pairwise intersections are all equal to a common core. The Erdős–Rado Sunflower Lemma (1960) states that any family of sets of size w with more than (k−1)^w w! sets contains a sunflower with k petals. The bound (k−1)^w w! is roughly (k w)^w. For fixed k, the best known upper bound was improved by Kostochka (1997) to c k w! for some constant c, and later by Alweiss, Lovett, Wu, and Zhang (2020) to (C k log w)^w for an absolute constant C. This breakthrough used a clever combination of the probabilistic method and a structural decomposition of the family into “spread” and “non‑spread” parts. A family ℱ is called δ‑spread if for every set S, the fraction of members of ℱ containing S is at most δ|S|. The key lemma shows that any large family contains a large δ‑spread subfamily. For a δ‑spread family, a random partition of the ground set into parts of appropriate size yields a sunflower with high probability. The bound (C k log w)^w is nearly tight: a construction of Naslund and Sawin (2019) shows that there exist families of size (c k log w)^w with no sunflower of size k. The sunflower lemma has applications in computational complexity, notably in the proof of the exponential lower bound for monotone circuit depth (Razborov, 1985) and in the analysis of the DNF‑SAT algorithm. It also appears in the proof of the Erdős–Szemerédi sum‑product theorem and in the recent resolution of the cap set problem via the slice rank method, where a sunflower‑free family corresponds to a set with no three‑term arithmetic progression. The current best bounds for the sunflower conjecture (which asks whether the bound can be improved to C^w for fixed k) remain open, but the logarithmic factor is now known to be necessary. The techniques developed for the sunflower problem, especially the spread approximation and the use of random partitions, have become standard tools in extremal combinatorics and theoretical computer science.

Dvir's Polynomial Method for the Finite Field Kakeya Problem

The Kakeya problem asks for the minimum size of a set K ⊂ 𝔽_q^n that contains a line in every direction. In Euclidean space, the Kakeya conjecture predicts that such sets must have full Hausdorff dimension. Over finite fields, the problem was resolved by Zeev Dvir in 2008 using the polynomial method. Dvir proved that any Kakeya set in 𝔽_q^n has size at least (q^n)/n!. The proof is remarkably short: assume |K| < (q^n)/n!. Then there exists a non‑zero polynomial P ∈ 𝔽_q[x₁,…,x_n] of degree at most d = q−1 that vanishes on K. This follows from a dimension count: the space of polynomials of degree ≤ d has dimension (d+n choose n) > |K|, so a non‑trivial polynomial vanishing on K exists. For each direction v ∈ 𝔽_q^n {0}, the line L_v = {a + t v : t ∈ 𝔽_q} is contained in K for some a. Restricting P to L_v gives a univariate polynomial of degree ≤ d in t that vanishes on all q points of the line. Since d < q, this polynomial must be identically zero, so all coefficients of P in the direction v vanish. In particular, the homogeneous part of P of degree d vanishes on all directions, which forces that homogeneous part to be the zero polynomial. But the degree of P is exactly d, contradiction. Therefore |K| ≥ (q^n)/n!. Dvir's argument also yields the stronger bound |K| ≥ (q^n)/n! + O(qn-1) and extends to sets containing a positive fraction of lines in each direction. The polynomial method has since been applied to many other problems in finite geometry, including the joints problem (Guth–Katz), the Erdős distinct distances problem in ℝ², and the cap set problem. The key idea—using the existence of a low‑degree polynomial vanishing on a small set to force algebraic constraints on the set's structure—has become a cornerstone of modern combinatorial geometry. The method also has algorithmic implications: finding a low‑degree polynomial that vanishes on a given set can be done by solving a linear system, providing a polynomial‑time algorithm for certifying that a set is not a Kakeya set.

The Kruskal-Katona Theorem and Shadows of Set Systems

The Kruskal-Katona Theorem is a cornerstone of extremal set theory, providing a fundamental bound on the size of the "shadow" of a family of sets. It generalizes Sperner's Theorem and has profound implications in combinatorics, coding theory, and graph theory. A family ℱ of k-subsets of a ground set [n] (i.e., sets of size k) has a shadow, denoted ∂ℱ, which is the family of all (k-1)-subsets that are contained in at least one set in ℱ. The theorem addresses the question: given a family ℱ of k-sets of a certain size, what is the minimum possible size of its shadow?

To state the theorem, we need the concept of a k-binomial representation of an integer. Any integer m can be uniquely written in the form: m = (xkk) + (xk-1k-1) + ... + (xjj) where xk > xk-1 > ... > xj ≥ j ≥ 1. The "shadow" of this representation is then defined as: ∂m = (xkk-1) + (xk-1k-2) + ... + (xjj-1) (with (x0) = 1 if j=1 and x≥1, and (x-1) = 0).

The Kruskal-Katona Theorem states that for any family ℱ of k-subsets of [n], |∂ℱ| ≥ ∂|ℱ|. This means that to minimize the shadow size for a given number of k-sets, one should choose the "lexicographically first" sets. For example, if we want to choose 3-sets from {1,2,3,4,5} to minimize the shadow, we would pick {1,2,3}, {1,2,4}, {1,2,5}, {1,3,4}, etc., as these sets tend to share smaller elements, thus producing a smaller shadow. The theorem provides a precise lower bound, which is tight.

A key application is to Sperner's Theorem, which states that the maximum size of an antichain in the Boolean lattice Bn is (n⌊n/2⌋). An antichain is a family of sets where no set is a subset of another. The Kruskal-Katona Theorem can be used to prove this by showing that if an antichain has sets of varying sizes, one can "compress" it to a family of sets of a single size (the middle one) without increasing the shadow, thus maximizing the size. Another application is in determining the minimum number of edges in a graph that guarantees a certain number of triangles, or more generally, the minimum number of copies of a smaller graph. It also finds use in proving bounds for error-correcting codes, particularly in understanding the structure of codes with certain distance properties.

The theorem's strength lies in its ability to provide exact bounds for extremal problems involving set systems, offering a structural understanding of how sets must be arranged to minimize or maximize certain properties. Its proof often involves a compression technique, where sets are transformed into "earlier" sets in lexicographical order, which are known to have smaller shadows. This technique is a powerful tool in combinatorial proofs.

Inverse Theorems in Additive Combinatorics: Freiman's Theorem

Inverse theorems in additive combinatorics aim to describe the structure of sets whose sumsets are "small." While direct theorems, such as the Cauchy-Davenport theorem, give lower bounds on the size of sumsets, inverse theorems address the converse: if a sumset is small, what can we say about the original set? The most celebrated result in this area is Freiman's Theorem, which characterizes sets with small doubling constants.

For a set A of integers, its sumset is A + A = {a + b : a, b ∈ A}. The doubling constant of A is defined as |A + A| / |A|. If |A + A| = 2|A| - 1, then A must be an arithmetic progression. Freiman's Theorem generalizes this observation: if the doubling constant is small (i.e., |A + A| ≤ K|A| for some constant K), then A must be "structured" in a specific way. Specifically, it must be a large subset of a generalized arithmetic progression (GAP).

A d-dimensional generalized arithmetic progression is a set of the form {a0 + x1a1 + ... + xdad : 0 ≤ xi < Li for all i}, where a0, a1, ..., ad are integers, and L1, ..., Ld are positive integers. The size of this GAP is L1 × ... × Ld. The theorem states that if |A + A| ≤ K|A|, then A is contained in a d-dimensional GAP of size at most C(K)|A|, where d and C(K) depend only on K. The original theorem by Freiman (1964) was qualitative; quantitative bounds for d and C(K) have been refined by Ruzsa, Chang, and others.

The proof of Freiman's Theorem is intricate and often involves a combination of Fourier analysis, graph theory, and geometric arguments. A key tool developed by Ruzsa is the "Ruzsa covering lemma," which states that if |A + A| is small, then A can be covered by a small number of translates of a difference set A - A. This lemma helps in reducing the problem to a more structured setting. The theorem's significance lies in providing a bridge between the size of sumsets and the algebraic structure of the underlying sets. It shows that randomness in addition implies structure in the sets themselves.

Freiman's Theorem is crucial in various areas, including the study of arithmetic progressions in dense sets (e.g., Szemerédi's Theorem, which the Green-Tao Theorem extends), the sum-product problem (which asks whether sumsets or product sets must be large), and the analysis of pseudorandom number generators. It underpins much of modern additive combinatorics, allowing researchers to infer deep structural properties from seemingly simple numerical constraints.

Application of Freiman's Theorem
Consider a set A of 10 distinct positive integers such that |A + A| = 25. Determine if A must be contained in a 1-dimensional arithmetic progression.

The doubling constant for A is |A + A| / |A| = 25 / 10 = 2.5. For a set to be an arithmetic progression, its doubling constant must be 2 - 1/|A| (for |A| ≥ 2), which for |A|=10 is 2 - 1/10 = 1.9. Since 2.5 > 1.9, A is not necessarily an arithmetic progression itself.

However, Freiman's Theorem states that if the doubling constant is small, the set must be contained in a generalized arithmetic progression of small dimension. Here, K = 2.5. Freiman's original theorem (or its quantitative versions) implies that A must be a subset of a d-dimensional GAP for some small d. For K < 3, it is known that A must be contained in a 1-dimensional arithmetic progression. Specifically, if |A + A| ≤ 2.5|A| - 3, then A is a subset of an arithmetic progression. In our case, 2.5|A| - 3 = 2.5 × 10 - 3 = 25 - 3 = 22. Since |A + A| = 25 > 22, the condition for being a subset of a 1-dimensional AP is not strictly met by this specific bound. However, more refined versions of Freiman's theorem state that for K < 3, A is indeed a subset of a 1-dimensional AP. For instance, a theorem by G. A. Freiman states that if |A + A| < 3|A|, then A is contained in an arithmetic progression of length at most |A + A| - |A| + 1. Here, length ≤ 25 - 10 + 1 = 16. So, yes, A must be contained in a 1-dimensional arithmetic progression.

Distinguishing Direct and Inverse Theorems
When tackling problems involving sumsets or other combinatorial structures, always clarify whether the question asks for a direct result (e.g., "what is the minimum size of A + B?") or an inverse result (e.g., "if A + B is small, what must A and B look like?"). Direct theorems (like Cauchy-Davenport) provide bounds on the size of the resulting structure, while inverse theorems (like Freiman's) reveal the underlying structure of the original sets. Misinterpreting the type of question can lead to applying the wrong set of theorems.

Discrepancy Theory: Balancing Partitions and Spencer's Theorem

Discrepancy theory is a branch of combinatorics that quantifies how "unbalanced" a coloring or partition of a set system can be. Given a ground set X and a family of subsets ℱ = {S1, ..., Sm} of X, a 2-coloring χ: X → {-1, +1} assigns a color to each element. The discrepancy of a set Si is defined as χ(Si) = Σx∈Si χ(x). The discrepancy of the family ℱ with respect to χ is Disc(ℱ, χ) = maxi |χ(Si)|. The central problem in discrepancy theory is to find the minimum possible discrepancy over all possible 2-colorings, i.e., Disc(ℱ) = minχ Disc(ℱ, χ).

A simple probabilistic argument shows that for any family ℱ of m sets on an n-element ground set, Disc(ℱ) ≤ c√n log m for some constant c. This is achieved by picking a random coloring. However, for many important cases, tighter bounds or even exact values are sought. One of the most significant results in this area is Spencer's Theorem (1985), which addresses the case where the number of sets m is equal to the number of elements n.

Spencer's Theorem states that for any family ℱ of n sets on an n-element ground set, there exists a 2-coloring χ such that Disc(ℱ, χ) ≤ 6√n. This result is remarkable because the bound is independent of the specific structure of the sets, depending only on the number of elements. The constant 6 has been slightly improved, but the √n dependence is known to be tight (up to a constant factor) due to constructions like the Hadamard matrix example. For a general family of m sets on n elements, the discrepancy is bounded by c√n log(2m/n) if m ≥ n, and by c√m if m < n.

The proof of Spencer's Theorem is non-trivial and often employs the "partial coloring method" or "entropy method." The partial coloring method involves iteratively coloring elements such that the discrepancy of "problematic" sets is reduced. This is a constructive approach, contrasting with purely existential probabilistic proofs. Another important result is the Beck-Fiala Theorem (1981), which states that if each element appears in at most t sets, then the discrepancy is at most 2t - 1. This theorem has algorithmic implications, as a coloring achieving this bound can be found efficiently.

Discrepancy theory has wide-ranging applications. In computer science, it is used in derandomization, where a probabilistic algorithm is converted into a deterministic one by finding a coloring with low discrepancy. It's also crucial in load balancing, fair division problems, and the design of experiments. For instance, in scheduling tasks on processors, discrepancy theory can help ensure that no processor is overloaded. In numerical analysis, it helps in constructing good quadrature rules. The study of discrepancy continues to be an active area, with connections to functional analysis, geometry, and theoretical computer science.

The Graph Container Method: Counting and Existence Results

The Graph Container Method is a powerful technique in extremal combinatorics that has revolutionized the way we count and prove the existence of combinatorial objects, especially in sparse settings. Developed independently by Balogh, Morris, and Samotij (2015) and Saxton and Thomason (2015), it provides a general framework for showing that "most" objects satisfying a certain property are contained within a small number of "containers" that are themselves "almost" satisfying the property.

The core idea is as follows: Suppose we are interested in counting the number of F-free graphs on n vertices (graphs that do not contain a specific subgraph F). Instead of directly counting these graphs, the container method identifies a relatively small family of subsets of the edge set of the complete graph Kn, called "containers." Each container C is a graph that is "almost" F-free, meaning it contains very few copies of F. The crucial property is that every F-free graph on n vertices must be a subgraph of at least one of these containers.

More formally, for a given graph F, there exists a family 𝒞 of graphs on n vertices such that:

  1. Every F-free graph G on n vertices is a subgraph of some C ∈ 𝒞.
  2. Each C ∈ 𝒞 contains "few" copies of F (e.g., o(nv(F)) copies, where v(F) is the number of vertices in F).
  3. The size of the family 𝒞 is "small" (e.g., 2o(n2) or 2o(n) depending on the problem).
The method typically constructs these containers using a randomized process. A key step involves defining a "trace" of a graph, which is a small random subset of its edges. By analyzing the properties of these traces, one can show that most F-free graphs have traces that are themselves F-free. These traces then define the containers.

The power of the container method lies in its versatility. It has been successfully applied to a wide range of problems, including:

  • Counting F-free graphs: It provides tight bounds for the number of such graphs, often matching the Erdős-Stone theorem's implications for dense graphs and extending to sparse graphs.
  • Counting independent sets: It can bound the number of independent sets in a graph, even in hypergraphs.
  • Sum-free sets: It has been used to count the number of sum-free subsets of integers.
  • Ramsey numbers: It has led to new bounds and constructions for certain Ramsey numbers.
  • Hypergraph problems: The method generalizes naturally to hypergraphs, providing solutions to many extremal problems there.
The container method often complements or even surpasses the probabilistic method and the regularity lemma in certain contexts, particularly when dealing with sparse structures or when precise counting results are required. It allows for a more refined analysis of the structure of extremal objects, moving beyond mere existence to quantitative enumeration. Its development represents a significant advancement in modern extremal combinatorics.

Extremal Problems in Geometric Settings: Helly's Theorem and its Relatives

Extremal combinatorics is not confined to graphs and set systems; it also extends to geometric configurations, where questions about intersections, coverings, and packings of geometric objects are explored. Helly's Theorem is a foundational result in this area, dealing with the intersection properties of convex sets. It provides a powerful tool for proving existence results in geometry and has numerous applications in optimization, computational geometry, and discrete geometry.

A set S in ℝd is convex if for any two points x, y ∈ S, the line segment connecting x and y is entirely contained within S. Helly's Theorem states that for a finite family of m convex sets C1, ..., Cm in ℝd, if every subcollection of d + 1 sets has a non-empty intersection, then the entire family has a non-empty intersection. That is, if ∩j∈J Cj ≠ ∅ for all J ⊆ {1, ..., m} with |J| = d + 1, then ∩i=1m Ci ≠ ∅.

The theorem is surprisingly powerful and intuitive. For example, in the plane (d = 2), if we have a collection of convex shapes (e.g., circles, squares, triangles) and every three of them intersect at a common point, then all of them must intersect at a common point. The number d + 1 is minimal; for instance, three intervals on a line (d = 1) can have pairwise intersections but no common intersection (e.g., [1,2], [1.5,2.5], [2.2,3]).

The proof of Helly's Theorem typically uses induction on the number of sets m and relies on the properties of convex hulls. For the base case m = d + 1, the condition is trivially met. For the inductive step, one assumes the theorem holds for m - 1 sets and uses the fact that the intersection of d + 1 convex sets is itself convex. A common proof strategy involves considering the intersection of all but one set, which by the inductive hypothesis is non-empty. Then, a point from each of these (m - 1) intersections is chosen, and Carathéodory's Theorem (which states that any point in the convex hull of a set of points can be expressed as a convex combination of at most d + 1 points from that set) is applied to show the existence of a common intersection point.

Helly's Theorem has numerous relatives and extensions, such as the fractional Helly theorem, the (p, q)-theorem, and topological Helly theorems. Its applications include:

  • Linear programming: It can be used to prove the existence of feasible solutions for systems of linear inequalities.
  • Computational geometry: Algorithms for finding common intersection points or regions often rely on Helly-type theorems.
  • Facility location: Determining optimal locations for facilities to serve a set of regions.
  • Geometric graph theory: Proving properties of intersection graphs of geometric objects.
The theorem highlights how local intersection properties can force global intersection properties in convex geometry, making it a fundamental tool in the study of geometric extremal problems.

Common Traps

  • Confusing Kruskal-Katona with Erdős–Ko–Rado: While both are extremal set theory theorems, Kruskal-Katona deals with the minimum size of the shadow of a family of sets, whereas Erdős–Ko–Rado deals with the maximum size of an intersecting family of sets. They address different structural questions.
  • Applying Freiman's Theorem without checking the doubling constant: Freiman's Theorem applies when the doubling constant |A + A| / |A| is "small" (i.e., bounded by a constant K). If the sumset is very large, the theorem provides no structural information.
  • Assuming Spencer's Theorem applies to any number of sets: The tight bound of O(√n) in Spencer's Theorem is specific to the case where the number of sets m is equal to the number of elements n. For m ≠ n, the bounds change, often involving logarithmic factors or dependence on m.
  • Misinterpreting "almost F-free" in the Container Method: A container is not necessarily F-free; it is only required to contain "few" copies of F. The exact definition of "few" depends on the specific problem and the parameters of the method.
  • Ignoring the convexity requirement in Helly's Theorem: Helly's Theorem strictly applies only to families of convex sets. If the sets are non-convex (e.g., star-shaped, disconnected), the theorem does not hold, and its conclusion cannot be drawn.
  • Overlooking the dimension d in Helly's Theorem: The critical number of subcollections to check for intersection is d + 1, where d is the dimension of the ambient space. Using a different number (e.g., 2 for d=2) will lead to incorrect conclusions.

The Frankl–Wilson Theorem and Modular Intersection Constraints

The Frankl–Wilson theorem, proved by Péter Frankl and Richard Wilson in 1981, is a cornerstone of the linear algebra method in extremal set theory. It gives an upper bound on the size of a family of subsets of an n‑element set when the sizes of pairwise intersections are restricted to a prescribed set of residues modulo a prime p. Formally, let p be a prime and let L be a set of s residues modulo p. Suppose ℱ is a family of subsets of [n] such that for any distinct A, B ∈ ℱ we have |A ∩ B| (mod p) ∈ L, while for every A ∈ ℱ we have |A| (mod p) ∉ L. Then |ℱ| ≤ Σi=0s C(n, i). The proof constructs, for each set A ∈ ℱ, a polynomial f_A over the finite field 𝔽_p in n variables that vanishes on the characteristic vectors of all other sets in ℱ but not on its own. Linear independence of these polynomials yields the bound.

The theorem has several striking consequences. Taking p = 2 and L = {0} gives the classical result that a family of subsets with all pairwise intersections even has size at most Σi=0⌊n/2⌋ C(n, i). More generally, Frankl and Wilson used the theorem to construct explicit graphs on N vertices with no clique or independent set of size No(1), providing lower bounds for Ramsey numbers R(k, k). The construction identifies vertices with subsets of a fixed size and connects two vertices when their intersection size lies in a carefully chosen set of residues. This yields a graph with no large homogeneous sets, showing that R(k, k) > kc log k for some constant c > 0.

Later work extended the method to non‑prime moduli using the polynomial method over rings, and to families of vectors over finite fields (the Frankl–Wilson theorem for vector spaces). The theorem also underpins the Frankl–Rödl theorem on forbidden intersections, where the condition is that no intersection size belongs to a given set of integers. In that setting the bound becomes exponential in n, illustrating how modular restrictions are much stronger than integer restrictions. The Frankl–Wilson theorem remains a paradigm for applying algebraic techniques to combinatorial extremal problems.

The Bollobás Two‑Families Theorem and its Applications

Béla Bollobás proved his Two‑Families Theorem in 1965. It states that if (A_i, B_i)i=1^m are pairs of finite sets such that A_i ∩ B_i = ∅ for all i, and A_i ∩ B_j ≠ ∅ for all i ≠ j, then Σi=1^m 1 / C(|A_i| + |B_i|, |A_i|) ≤ 1. Here C(a, b) denotes the binomial coefficient. The theorem is often called the Bollobás–Sperner inequality because it generalizes Sperner’s theorem and the LYM inequality. A standard proof uses the Lubell–Yamamoto–Meshalkin (LYM) method: consider a random permutation of the ground set and the event that all elements of A_i appear before all elements of B_i; the cross‑intersection condition guarantees these events are disjoint, and their probabilities sum to the left‑hand side.

The theorem has a remarkable range of applications. It gives a short proof of the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem for intersecting families of k‑sets when n ≥ 2k. It also yields the Hilton–Milner theorem on non‑trivial intersecting families, and it is the key tool in the proof of the Frankl–Wilson theorem via the polynomial method. In the setting of cross‑intersecting families, if ℱ and 𝒢 are families of subsets of [n] with A ∩ B ≠ ∅ for all A ∈ ℱ, B ∈ 𝒢, then one can form pairs (A, B) and apply the theorem to obtain |ℱ|·|𝒢| ≤ 22n-2 when n is even, with equality for the families of all subsets containing a fixed element. The theorem has been generalized to vector spaces (the Bollobás–Leader theorem) and to matroids (the Bollobás–Riordan theorem), showing its deep structural nature.

Applying the Bollobás Two‑Families Theorem
Let ℱ and 𝒢 be families of subsets of [n] such that A ∩ B ≠ ∅ for every A ∈ ℱ and B ∈ 𝒢. Prove that |ℱ|·|𝒢| ≤ 22n-2.
For each pair (A, B) with A ∈ ℱ, B ∈ 𝒢 define the ordered pair (A, B). The condition A ∩ B ≠ ∅ means A ∩ B ≠ ∅. However the Bollobás theorem requires A_i ∩ B_i = ∅ and cross intersections non‑empty. To fit the theorem, replace each A by its complement A^c. Then A^c ∩ B = ∅ iff A ∪ B = [n], which is not guaranteed. Instead, consider the families ℱ' = {A^c : A ∈ ℱ} and 𝒢' = 𝒢. Now for any A^c ∈ ℱ' and B ∈ 𝒢', we have A^c ∩ B = ∅ exactly when B ⊆ A. This does not hold in general. A standard trick: take a random partition of [n] into two parts X and Y of equal size (assume n even). For each A ∈ ℱ, let A_X = A ∩ X, A_Y = A ∩ Y. Define pairs (A_X, A_Y). The cross‑intersection condition A ∩ B ≠ ∅ implies that for any A ∈ ℱ, B ∈ 𝒢 we cannot have A_X ⊆ B^c and A_Y ⊆ B^c simultaneously. A more direct proof uses the LYM inequality on the product poset: the number of chains hitting both ℱ and 𝒢 is at most the total number of chains, giving |ℱ|·|𝒢| ≤ 22n-2. This bound is tight, achieved by taking all subsets containing a fixed element.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for a bound on the product of two cross‑intersecting families, first check whether the Bollobás Two‑Families Theorem can be applied by constructing suitable pairs (A_i, B_i). If the families are not naturally paired, consider taking complements or using a random partition to create disjoint pairs. The theorem’s sum of reciprocals of binomial coefficients often simplifies to a clean power‑of‑two bound.

The Ahlswede–Khachatrian Complete Intersection Theorem

The Complete Intersection Theorem, proved by Rudolf Ahlswede and Levon Khachatrian in 1997, determines the exact maximum size of a t‑intersecting family of k‑subsets of an n‑set for all parameters n, k, t. A family ℱ ⊆ C([n], k) is t‑intersecting if |A ∩ B| ≥ t for all A, B ∈ ℱ. The Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem (1961) covers the case t = 1, giving the bound C(n-1, k-1) for n ≥ 2k. For general t, Frankl (1976) conjectured that the maximum is attained by one of the families ℱ_i = {A ∈ C([n], k) : |A ∩ [t+2i]| ≥ t+i} where i ranges from 0 to ⌊(k-t)/2⌋. Ahlswede and Khachatrian proved this conjecture, showing that the maximum size is max_i |ℱ_i|. The families ℱ_i are often called the Frankl families.

The proof uses the pushing‑pulling (or compression) technique combined with the Katona circle method. One first shows that an extremal family can be assumed to be left‑compressed, then analyzes the structure of such families on a cyclic ordering of the ground set. The theorem has a clean threshold: if n > (t+1)(k-t+1) then the maximum is C(n-t, k-t), which coincides with the Erdős–Ko–Rado bound. For smaller n, the Frankl families with i ≥ 1 are larger. This phenomenon explains why the Erdős–Ko–Rado bound is not always optimal for t‑intersecting families. The theorem has applications in coding theory, where t‑intersecting families correspond to constant‑weight codes with minimum distance 2(k-t), and in cryptography for constructing access structures.

Stability versions of the theorem have been developed: if a t‑intersecting family has size close to the maximum, then it must be structurally close to one of the Frankl families. Recent work by Ellis, Filmus, and Friedgut (2012) gave a sharp stability result using Fourier analysis on the symmetric group. The Complete Intersection Theorem is a rare example of an exact extremal result valid for all parameter ranges, and its proof showcases the power of combinatorial shifting combined with algebraic methods.

The Polynomial Method for the Joints Problem in Incidence Geometry

The joints problem asks: given a set of L lines in ℝ³, what is the maximum number of joints, where a joint is a point incident to at least three non‑coplanar lines? This problem, posed by Chazelle et al. in the 1990s, was solved by Guth and Katz in 2008 using the polynomial method. They proved that the number of joints J satisfies J ≤ C L3/2 for an absolute constant C. The proof begins by assuming a set of J joints and constructing a non‑zero polynomial P of minimal degree d that vanishes at all joints. By a parameter counting argument, one can choose d ≈ (3J)1/3. If a line contains more than d joints, then P vanishes on the whole line (since a univariate polynomial of degree d with more than d roots is identically zero). Using the fact that at each joint at least three non‑coplanar lines meet, one shows that the gradient ∇P also vanishes at every joint. This forces all partial derivatives of P to vanish at the joints, which by induction on degree implies that P is identically zero — a contradiction unless J ≤ C L3/2.

The bound is tight up to the constant: a grid of L1/2 lines in the x‑direction and L1/2 lines in the y‑direction, all lying in distinct horizontal planes, yields roughly L3/2 joints. Subsequent simplifications by Kaplan, Sharir, and Shustin (2010) gave a more elementary proof using the same polynomial vanishing idea but avoiding the gradient argument by considering the polynomial’s restriction to each line. The joints theorem has been extended to higher dimensions (joints of k‑flats in ℝ^d) and to varieties (joints of algebraic curves). It also played a crucial role in Guth and Katz’s solution of the Erdős distinct distances problem, where the Elekes–Sharir framework reduces distance counting to incidence problems that are controlled by the joints theorem.

The Guth–Katz Theorem on Distinct Distances in the Plane

In 1946 Erdős asked: given n points in the plane, what is the minimum number of distinct distances determined by these points? He conjectured the minimum is n / √(log n) up to a constant factor, based on the √n × √n integer grid. For decades the best lower bound was Ω(n0.864...) via the crossing number method. In 2015 Larry Guth and Nets Hawk Katz proved a lower bound of Ω(n / log n) using a sophisticated combination of the polynomial method and the Elekes–Sharir framework. Their proof proceeds in three main steps. First, the Elekes–Sharir transformation maps each ordered pair of points (p, q) to a line in ℝ³, so that equal distances correspond to incidences between points and lines. Second, polynomial partitioning divides ℝ³ into O(r³) cells, each containing at most L / r² lines, where L is the total number of lines. By choosing r appropriately, one reduces the problem to incidences within cells and on the partitioning surface. Third, the joints theorem (or a variant for lines on a surface) controls the incidences on the surface, while induction handles the cells. The resulting bound is n / log n, which is only a logarithmic factor away from Erdős’s conjecture.

The Guth–Katz theorem was a landmark in combinatorial geometry, earning the authors the 2015 Clay Research Award. It demonstrated that the polynomial method, originally developed for finite field problems (Dvir’s Kakeya proof), could be adapted to Euclidean incidence geometry via polynomial partitioning. Subsequent work has improved the logarithmic factor: in 2019, Solymosi and Vu obtained n / (log n)1/2 using a refined partitioning scheme, and in 2021, Pach and Sharir achieved n / (log n)1/2+o(1). The exact exponent of the logarithm remains open, and closing the gap to n / √(log n) is a major unsolved problem. The theorem also spurred progress on related problems such as the number of distinct distances in higher dimensions and the incidence bounds for points and spheres.

The Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers Theorem and Additive Energy

The Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers (BSG) theorem is a cornerstone of modern additive combinatorics. It connects the additive energy of a set — the number of solutions to a + b = c + d with a,b,c,d in the set — to the existence of a large subset with small doubling. Formally, for a finite set A in an abelian group, the additive energy E(A) is the number of quadruples (a,b,c,d) ∈ A⁴ such that a + b = c + d. If E(A) ≥ |A|³ / K, then there exists a subset A' ⊆ A with |A'| ≥ |A| / (C K^C) and |A' + A'| ≤ C K^C |A'|, where C is an absolute constant. The original proof by Balog and Szemerédi (1994) used graph-theoretic methods; Gowers (1998) gave a quantitative version with polynomial dependence on K, which is essential for applications such as the proof of Szemerédi's theorem for length‑4 progressions and the Green–Tao theorem on primes. The theorem is often used in the form: if a set has large additive energy, then a positive proportion of its elements lie in a set with small doubling. This structural insight underpins many inverse theorems, including Freiman's theorem and the polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture. The BSG theorem also has a multiplicative analogue, where one replaces sums by products, and a mixed sum‑product version that is central to the sum‑product phenomenon. In examinations, a typical trap is to confuse the BSG theorem with the Plünnecke–Ruzsa inequalities, which bound the size of iterated sumsets given a small doubling constant, but do not extract a large subset from an energy hypothesis. Another common error is to assume the subset A' can be taken as the whole set A; the theorem only guarantees a subset, and the loss in size is unavoidable in general, as shown by constructions of sets with large energy but no large subset with small doubling.

The Elekes–Rónyai Problem and Polynomial Images

The Elekes–Rónyai problem asks: given a bivariate polynomial f(x,y) with real coefficients, when does the image set f(A,B) have size close to |A||B| for finite sets A,B ⊂ ℝ? Elekes and Rónyai (2000) proved that if f is not of the form g(h(x)+k(y)) or g(h(x)k(y)) for univariate polynomials g,h,k, then |f(A,B)| ≥ c |A|1/2 |B|1/2 for some absolute constant c > 0. This result was a breakthrough in the study of expanding polynomials and has deep connections to incidence geometry and the sum‑product phenomenon. The proof uses the polynomial method in the form of the Schwartz–Zippel lemma and a clever double‑counting argument on the number of solutions to f(x,y) = f(u,v). Later, Raz, Sharir, and de Zeeuw (2016) gave a stronger bound |f(A,B)| ≥ c |A|2/3 |B|2/3 for non‑degenerate polynomials, using the Guth–Katz incidence theorem for points and lines in ℝ³. The classification of exceptional polynomials — those of the form g(h(x)+k(y)) or g(h(x)k(y)) — is tight: for such polynomials one can choose A,B to be arithmetic or geometric progressions and obtain |f(A,B)| ≈ |A|+|B| or ≈ |A||B|1/2. In the finite field setting, the problem is more delicate; Bukh and Tsimerman (2013) showed that over 𝔽_p, if f is not a composition of a univariate polynomial with a group operation (addition or multiplication), then |f(A,B)| ≥ min(p, c |A|1/2 |B|1/2) for sets A,B not too large. The Elekes–Rónyai theorem is a frequent source of exam questions because it combines polynomial structure, incidence bounds, and the idea of “expansion” — a set is expanding if its image under a polynomial is significantly larger than the input sets. A common mistake is to assume that any polynomial not of the special form automatically gives |f(A,B)| ≥ |A||B| / polylog; the known bounds are polynomial in |A|,|B|, not linear. Another trap is to confuse the Elekes–Rónyai problem with the Erdős–Szemerédi sum‑product conjecture, which concerns the simultaneous expansion of A+A and A·A, whereas Elekes–Rónyai studies a single polynomial image.

Worked Example: Applying the Elekes–Rónyai Bound
Let f(x,y) = x² + y². For finite sets A,B ⊂ ℝ with |A| = |B| = n, what is the best known lower bound for |f(A,B)|? Explain why f is not of the exceptional form.
The polynomial f(x,y) = x² + y² can be written as g(h(x)+k(y)) with g(t)=t, h(x)=x², k(y)=y². This matches the additive exceptional form g(h(x)+k(y)). Therefore the Elekes–Rónyai theorem does not guarantee expansion; indeed, taking A = B = {1,2,…,n} gives f(A,B) ⊂ {2,…,2n²} so |f(A,B)| ≤ 2n², which is O(n²) = O(|A||B|). The theorem only gives a non‑trivial bound for polynomials not of this form. For a non‑exceptional polynomial such as f(x,y) = x² + xy + y², the Raz–Sharir–de Zeeuw bound yields |f(A,B)| ≥ c n4/3.
Exam Strategy: Recognising Exceptional Forms
When a question asks whether a given polynomial is expanding, first check if it can be written as g(h(x)+k(y)) or g(h(x)k(y)). Factorisations and substitutions like u = x+y, v = xy often reveal the structure. If the polynomial is exceptional, the answer is “no expansion guaranteed”; if not, cite the Raz–Sharir–de Zeeuw bound |f(A,B)| ≥ c |A|2/3|B|2/3 for real sets, or the Bukh–Tsimerman bound for finite fields.

The Regularity Lemma for Directed Graphs and Tournament Extremal Problems

Szemerédi's regularity lemma has a natural extension to directed graphs, where edges are ordered pairs and the density between two vertex sets is measured by the proportion of ordered pairs that are edges. The directed regularity lemma states that for any ε > 0 there exists M(ε) such that every directed graph on n vertices admits an ε‑regular partition of its vertex set into at most M parts, where a pair (V_i, V_j) is ε‑regular if the edge density from V_i to V_j is close to the density between any large subsets. This lemma enables the transfer of extremal results from undirected graphs to tournaments and general directed graphs. A landmark application is the solution of the directed triangle removal lemma and the proof that every tournament with no transitive subtournament of size k has at most (1 - 1/(k-1) + o(1)) n²/2 edges — a directed analogue of Turán's theorem. The proof uses the regularity lemma to find a reduced directed graph, applies the undirected Turán theorem to the underlying undirected graph of the reduced graph, and then lifts the bound back. Another important result is the directed version of the Erdős–Stone theorem: for any fixed directed graph H, the extremal number ex(n,H) = (1 - 1/(χ(H)-1) + o(1)) n²/2, where χ(H) is the chromatic number of the underlying undirected graph of H. The directed regularity lemma also underpins the counting lemma for directed cycles and the proof of the Caccetta–Häggkvist conjecture for large tournaments. In examinations, a frequent trap is to apply the undirected regularity lemma directly to a directed graph by ignoring edge directions; this loses the asymmetry of densities d(V_i,V_j) ≠ d(V_j,V_i) and can lead to incorrect reduced graphs. Another error is to assume the directed regularity lemma gives the same tower‑type bound on the number of parts as the undirected lemma; the bounds are of similar tower height but the constants differ because one must regularise both directions simultaneously.

The Density Hales–Jewett Theorem and Hypergraph Regularity

The Density Hales–Jewett (DHJ) theorem is a density version of the classical Hales–Jewett theorem on combinatorial lines in high‑dimensional cubes. It states that for any δ > 0 and integer k ≥ 2, there exists N such that any subset of [k]^N of density at least δ contains a combinatorial line — a set of k points obtained by fixing some coordinates and letting the remaining coordinates vary together over {1,…,k}. The case k = 3 is equivalent to Roth's theorem on 3‑term arithmetic progressions via a simple embedding, and the general case implies Szemerédi's theorem for progressions of length k. The first proof of DHJ for all k was given by Furstenberg and Katznelson (1991) using ergodic theory. A combinatorial proof using the hypergraph regularity lemma was achieved by the Polymath project (2009) for k = 3 and later extended to general k. The hypergraph regularity method partitions the vertex set of a k‑uniform hypergraph into a bounded number of parts such that most k‑tuples of parts are regular in a suitable sense. One then builds a reduced k‑uniform hypergraph and applies a counting lemma to find the desired combinatorial line. The proof is technically demanding because the regularity notion for hypergraphs must control not only edge densities but also the intersections of edges across different levels of the partition. The DHJ theorem has applications in theoretical computer science, particularly in property testing and the analysis of Boolean functions, where it implies that any dense subset of the hypercube contains a combinatorial subspace of dimension 1. In exams, a common mistake is to confuse the DHJ theorem with the multidimensional Szemerédi theorem, which finds constellations in ℤ^d; DHJ is specifically about combinatorial lines in [k]^N. Another trap is to assume the hypergraph regularity lemma gives a partition with a number of parts that is a tower of height polynomial in 1/ε; in fact the height is a tower of height polynomial in 1/ε, but the polynomial degree grows with the uniformity k, making the bounds extremely weak.

The Polynomial Method for the Sum‑Product Phenomenon in Finite Fields

The sum‑product phenomenon asserts that a finite set A in a field cannot have both its sumset A+A and its product set A·A small simultaneously. Over finite fields 𝔽_p, the Bourgain–Katz–Tao theorem (2004) was the first to give a quantitative bound: if A ⊂ 𝔽_p is not too large (|A| < p1-δ), then max(|A+A|, |A·A|) ≥ c |A|1+ε for some ε = ε(δ) > 0. The proof uses the polynomial method in the form of the Schwartz–Zippel lemma applied to a carefully constructed polynomial that vanishes on a large Cartesian product derived from A. Specifically, one considers the polynomial P(x,y) = ∏a∈A (x - a) and uses the fact that if both sumset and product set are small, then the set of values P(a+b) for a,b ∈ A is constrained, leading to a contradiction via degree bounds. Later, Garaev (2008) gave a simpler argument using incidence geometry and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, obtaining the explicit bound max(|A+A|, |A·A|) ≥ c min(p1/2 |A|1/2, |A|² / p1/2). The current best exponent for the sum‑product estimate in 𝔽_p is due to Rudnev, Shkredov, and others, who used the polynomial method combined with the multiplicative energy and the point‑plane incidence bound in 𝔽_p³ to achieve max(|A+A|, |A·A|) ≥ c |A|1+1/5 for |A| < p2/3. The polynomial method also yields the Elekes–Rónyai type expansion for polynomials f(x,y) over finite fields, as mentioned earlier. In examinations, a typical trap is to apply the real‑field sum‑product bounds (e.g., Erdős–Szemerédi conjecture) directly to finite fields without accounting for the wrap‑around modulo p; the finite field setting requires the set size to be less than √p or similar to avoid trivialities. Another error is to confuse the polynomial method used here (constructing a polynomial that vanishes on a product set) with the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz, which is a different tool for proving existence of non‑zeros of polynomials on grids.

  • Assuming the Elekes–Rónyai bound gives linear expansion |f(A,B)| ≥ c|A||B|; the known bounds are polynomial (e.g., |A|2/3|B|2/3) and the exceptional forms g(h(x)+k(y)), g(h(x)k(y)) are tight.
  • Applying the undirected regularity lemma to a directed graph by ignoring edge directions, thereby losing the asymmetry d(V_i,V_j) ≠ d(V_j,V_i) and producing an incorrect reduced graph.

The Szemerédi–Trotter Theorem and Incidence Bounds in the Plane

The Szemerédi–Trotter theorem (1983) gives the optimal upper bound on the number of incidences between n points and m lines in the Euclidean plane. An incidence is a pair (p, ℓ) where point p lies on line ℓ. The theorem states that the number of incidences I(n, m) satisfies I(n, m) = O(n2/3 m2/3 + n + m). This bound is tight up to the constant factor, as shown by a grid construction: take a √n × √n integer grid of points and all lines that contain at least two grid points; the number of incidences is Θ(n2/3 m2/3). The original proof used cell decomposition and crossing number arguments; later proofs by Székely (1997) derived it elegantly from the crossing number inequality. The theorem has become a cornerstone of incidence geometry and has numerous applications: it implies the Beck theorem (any set of n points in the plane determines at least cn2 lines unless most points are collinear), it yields the best known bound for the number of unit distances among n points (O(n4/3)), and it underpins the Guth–Katz proof of the Erdős distinct distances problem. In finite fields, the analogous bound fails for large sets because of the wrap‑around effect; the correct statement requires the set size to be less than p2/3 (Bourgain–Katz–Tao, 2004). A common examiner trap is to quote the Szemerédi–Trotter bound for incidences between points and arbitrary curves without the necessary algebraic restrictions; the theorem holds for lines and, with modifications, for pseudolines, but not for general algebraic curves of higher degree without additional hypotheses (e.g., the Pach–Sharir bound for curves with bounded multiplicity). Another trap is to confuse the exponent 2/3 with the exponent in the crossing number inequality (which is 3); the two are related but distinct.

The Crossing Number Inequality and its Applications to Extremal Graph Theory

The crossing number inequality, proved independently by Ajtai–Chvátal–Newborn–Szemerédi (1982) and Leighton (1983), states that any drawing of a graph with n vertices and e edges in the plane has at least cr(G) ≥ e3 / (64 n2) crossings, provided e ≥ 4n. This simple inequality is a powerful tool for translating geometric crossing information into combinatorial bounds. Its most famous application is Székely’s proof of the Szemerédi–Trotter theorem: by constructing a graph whose vertices are the points and edges are the line segments between consecutive points on each line, the number of crossings in a suitable drawing is exactly the number of incidences minus the number of edges, and the crossing number inequality yields the optimal bound. The inequality also gives a short proof of the fact that a graph with no K3,3 minor has O(n) edges (a special case of the more general minor‑excluded graph density results). In additive combinatorics, the crossing number inequality is used to prove sum‑product estimates: given a set A, one builds a bipartite graph between A+A and A·A and uses the inequality to show that at least one of the sumset or product set must be large. The constant 64 is not optimal; Pach, Radoičić, Tardos, and Tóth (2004) improved it to 31.1, and the best known constant is 4 (for e ≥ 7n) due to Ackerman (2019). However, the exact constant is rarely needed in examinations; the asymptotic form cr(G) = Ω(e3/n2) suffices. A frequent mistake is to apply the inequality when e < 4n, where the bound becomes trivial or negative; the correct hypothesis is e > 4n (or e ≥ 4n depending on the version). Another error is to assume the inequality holds for drawings on surfaces of higher genus without the appropriate genus‑dependent term; the generalisation involves the Euler characteristic and is not the same as the planar bound.

Worked Example: Deriving the Szemerédi–Trotter Bound from the Crossing Number Inequality
Let P be a set of n points and L a set of m lines in ℝ2. Construct a graph G whose vertices are the points of P. For each line ℓ ∈ L, sort the points of P ∩ ℓ along ℓ and connect consecutive points by an edge. Show that the number of edges e satisfies e = I(P, L) − m, and that any drawing of G has at most I(P, L) crossings. Use the crossing number inequality to prove I(P, L) = O(n2/3 m2/3 + n + m).
Each line ℓ containing k points contributes k−1 edges, so summing over all lines gives e = Σ(k−1) = I(P, L) − m. Draw each edge as the straight segment between its two endpoints on the corresponding line. Two edges cross only if they belong to different lines and their interiors intersect; each such crossing corresponds to a unique incidence of the four endpoints, but a standard argument shows the total number of crossings is at most I(P, L). Apply the crossing number inequality: cr(G) ≥ e3/(64 n2) provided e ≥ 4n. Since cr(G) ≤ I(P, L), we have I(P, L) ≥ (I(P, L) − m)3/(64 n2). If I(P, L) ≤ 2m, the bound I = O(m) holds. Otherwise I − m ≥ I/2, giving I3 ≤ 512 n2 I, i.e., I = O(n2/3 m2/3). Adding the trivial bounds I ≤ n m and I ≤ n + m yields the full Szemerédi–Trotter estimate.
Exam Strategy
When a problem asks for an incidence bound, first check whether the objects are lines or pseudolines; if they are general curves, cite the Pach–Sharir theorem instead. For crossing number applications, always verify the edge‑density condition e ≥ 4n before invoking the inequality. In written answers, explicitly state the construction of the auxiliary graph and the drawing used; examiners award marks for the correct translation from geometry to graph theory.

Roth's Theorem on Three-Term Arithmetic Progressions and the Fourier Analytic Proof

Roth's theorem (1953) states that any subset A ⊆ {1, …, N} of positive upper density contains a non‑trivial three‑term arithmetic progression (3‑AP). The original proof used a density increment argument combined with Fourier analysis on the cyclic group ℤ/Nℤ. The key idea: if A has density δ and contains no 3‑AP, then its Fourier transform must have a large coefficient, which allows one to pass to a long arithmetic progression where the density of A increases by a factor (1 + cδ2). Iterating this increment at most O(δ−2) times forces the density to exceed 1, a contradiction. This yields the quantitative bound that the largest 3‑AP‑free subset of {1, …, N} has size o(N), specifically N / (log log N) in Roth's original argument. Later improvements by Bourgain (1999) and Sanders (2011) refined the density increment to achieve bounds of the form N / (log N)1−o(1) and N / (log N)1+o(1) respectively. The Fourier analytic method also extends to finite fields: Meshulam (1995) proved that a subset of 𝔽3n of density δ contains a 3‑AP provided δ ≥ C/n, which is the exact analogue of Roth's theorem in the vector space setting. A common trap is to confuse the Fourier analytic proof with the combinatorial proof of Szemerédi's theorem for longer progressions; the latter uses the regularity lemma and does not give effective bounds for k ≥ 4. Another error is to assume that the density increment argument works directly in ℤ without passing to a cyclic group; the wrap‑around in ℤ/Nℤ is essential for the Fourier transform to be defined on a group. In examinations, you may be asked to sketch the density increment step: define the balanced function f = 1A − δ, compute the number of 3‑APs as Σx,d f(x)f(x+d)f(x+2d), use Parseval to relate this to the Fourier coefficients, and show that if the count is zero then some non‑zero frequency has large magnitude, yielding a density increment on a long progression.

The Erdős–Szekeres Theorem on Convex Polygons and the Happy Ending Problem

The Erdős–Szekeres theorem (1935) is a foundational result in combinatorial geometry: for every integer k ≥ 3 there exists a smallest integer ES(k) such that any set of ES(k) points in the plane in general position (no three collinear) contains k points that are the vertices of a convex k‑gon. The original paper proved ES(k) ≤ C(2k−4, k−2) + 1, which is asymptotically 4k / √(πk). The problem of determining ES(k) exactly is known as the "happy ending problem" because it led to the marriage of Esther Klein and George Szekeres. The best known upper bound, due to Suk (2017), is ES(k) ≤ 2k+o(k), improving the earlier bound of Tóth and Valtr (1998) of ES(k) ≤ C(2k−5, k−2) + 1. The lower bound, from a construction by Erdős and Szekeres themselves, is ES(k) ≥ 2k−2 + 1. The gap between 2k and 4k remained open for decades; Suk's breakthrough used a combination of the cup‑cap theorem (a Ramsey‑type result for sequences) and a careful geometric partitioning. The cup‑cap theorem states that any sequence of N distinct real numbers contains either an increasing subsequence of length a or a decreasing subsequence of length b, provided N ≥ (a−1)(b−1)+1. This is equivalent to the Erdős–Szekeres theorem on monotone subsequences (1935), which is often confused with the convex polygon theorem. In exams, a typical trap is to cite the monotone subsequence bound (a−1)(b−1)+1 as the bound for ES(k); the correct bound for convex k‑gons is much larger. Another trap is to assume that the points must be in convex position in the original set; the theorem only guarantees the existence of some subset in convex position. The problem remains open for k = 6: it is known that ES(6) = 17, but the exact value for k = 7 is unknown (between 37 and 65). The theorem has inspired numerous generalisations, including the "empty convex polygon" problem (Horton sets show that empty 7‑gons need not exist) and higher‑dimensional versions.

The Ruzsa–Szemerédi (6,3)-Theorem and the Triangle Removal Lemma

The Ruzsa–Szemerédi (6,3)-theorem (1978) addresses the following extremal problem: what is the maximum number of edges in an n‑vertex graph such that every set of 6 vertices spans at most 3 edges? Equivalently, the graph is a union of edge‑disjoint triangles that do not share edges (a linear 3‑uniform hypergraph). Ruzsa and Szemerédi proved that the maximum number of edges is o(n2), i.e., the graph has density zero. This result is intimately connected to the triangle removal lemma, which states that for every ε > 0 there exists δ > 0 such that any n‑vertex graph with at most δ n3 triangles can be made triangle‑free by removing at most ε n2 edges. The (6,3)-theorem follows by applying the triangle removal lemma to the graph formed by the edges of the triangles; if the graph had Ω(n2) edges, it would contain many triangles, contradicting the hypothesis that every 6 vertices span at most 3 edges. The triangle removal lemma itself is a corollary of Szemerédi's regularity lemma, and its proof gives a tower‑type dependence of δ on ε. The (6,3)-theorem has applications in property testing: it implies that the property of being a union of edge‑disjoint triangles is testable with one‑sided error. A related result is the (6,3)-theorem for hypergraphs, which is significantly harder and was resolved only recently via the hypergraph regularity lemma. In examinations, a common mistake is to confuse the (6,3)-theorem with the (6,4)-problem (maximum edges with no 6 vertices spanning 4 edges), which is a different extremal problem related to the Zarankiewicz problem. Another trap is to assume that the o(n2) bound is effective; the regularity lemma proof gives no explicit rate, and the best known quantitative bounds are still of tower type. When asked to prove the (6,3)-theorem from the triangle removal lemma, you must explicitly construct the auxiliary graph whose triangles correspond to the given triangles and verify that the hypothesis "every 6 vertices span at most 3 edges" implies the graph has few triangles.

  • Applying the crossing number inequality without checking the condition e ≥ 4n.
  • Citing the monotone subsequence bound (a−1)(b−1)+1 as the Erdős–Szekeres bound for convex k‑gons.

  • Mixing up the (6,3)-theorem with the (6,4)-problem or the Zarankiewicz problem.

  • Using the finite‑field Szemerédi–Trotter bound for sets larger than p2/3 without the wrap‑around restriction.

Dependent Random Choice and Its Power in Bipartite Turán Problems

Dependent Random Choice (DRC) is a probabilistic technique that extracts a large subset of vertices with many common neighbours. The method was formalized by Kostochka and Sudakov (2006) and later refined by Fox and Sudakov (2011). The core idea is to select a small random set S of vertices, examine the common neighbourhood N(S), and then prune vertices that do not have enough neighbours in N(S). By carefully choosing the size of S and the number of repetitions, one can guarantee the existence of a subset U of size m such that every t‑tuple of vertices in U shares at least ℓ common neighbours.

In bipartite extremal problems, DRC yields sharp bounds for forbidding complete bipartite subgraphs Ks,t. The classic result states that for fixed integers s≥2 and t≥2, any n‑vertex bipartite graph with edge density exceeding C n2‑1/s contains a Ks,t. The proof proceeds by taking a random set of s vertices on one side, showing that the expected size of their common neighbourhood on the other side is large, and then applying the probabilistic method to obtain a deterministic subset.

One celebrated application is the proof of the Kővári–Sós–Turán bound for Ks,t‑free graphs using DRC. The argument improves the constant factor in the exponent and, more importantly, provides a constructive algorithm: repeatedly pick random s‑tuples, keep vertices with many common neighbours, and stop when a dense bipartite subgraph emerges. This algorithmic version is useful in computer‑science exams that ask for explicit constructions.

DRC also solves problems beyond Ks,t. For example, it yields the “blow‑up” lemma for bipartite graphs with forbidden even cycles C2ℓ. By selecting a random set of ℓ vertices and analysing common neighbours, one shows that any graph with edge density ω(n1+1/ℓ) must contain C2ℓ. This bridges the gap between Turán‑type results for cliques and those for even cycles.

When writing an answer, it is essential to state the parameters clearly: choose r random vertices, let X be the set of vertices with at least d neighbours in the common neighbourhood, and then bound |X| from below using linearity of expectation. The final inequality typically looks like |X| ≥ n · (pr − ε), where p is the edge density. Concluding with “hence a Ks,t exists” completes the argument. In exams, examiners often look for the explicit choice of r and d, and for a justification that the pruning step does not destroy the required size of the set.

Stability Phenomena in Extremal Graph Theory

Stability theorems describe the structure of almost extremal graphs: if a graph G on n vertices has close to the maximum number of edges without containing a forbidden subgraph H, then G must be close (in edit distance) to the extremal configuration prescribed by Turán’s theorem. The first systematic stability result was proved by Simonovits (1968) for cliques, showing that any Kr+1‑free graph with e(G) ≥ ex(n,Kr+1) − o(n2) can be made r‑partite by deleting o(n2) edges.

The modern formulation uses the notion of ε‑closeness: for any ε>0 there exists δ>0 such that if e(G) ≥ ex(n,H) − δ n2, then G can be transformed into the Turán‑graph Tr(n) by removing at most ε n2 edges. The proof typically combines the regularity lemma with a counting argument: after applying regularity, one shows that most pairs of parts behave like dense bipartite graphs, and any deviation would create the forbidden H, contradicting the edge count.

Stability is not limited to cliques. For bipartite forbidden graphs such as Ks,t, Füredi (1991) proved a stability version: if a Ks,t‑free graph has edge count within o(n2‑1/s) of the extremal bound, then its vertex set can be partitioned into s‑1 parts such that almost all edges lie between these parts, mirroring the construction of a complete (s‑1)‑partite graph with one dense side.

Recent work by Keevash and Sudakov (2015) extended stability to hypergraphs, showing that for the 3‑uniform hypergraph Turán problem forbidding the Fano plane, any hypergraph with edge count within o(n3) of the extremal number must be close to the bipartite construction that attains the maximum. The hypergraph regularity lemma plays a crucial role, and the argument requires careful control of the “error” hyperedges.

Exam‑level implications are clear: when a question asks for the “structure of graphs with nearly maximal edges avoiding H,” the answer should mention the appropriate stability theorem, describe the extremal configuration (e.g., Turán graph, complete bipartite blow‑up), and explain that only o(n2) edges need to be altered. Providing a brief sketch—regularity → reduced graph → stability → edit distance—demonstrates depth and earns full credit.

Technique: Applying Stability to a Near‑Extremal K₄‑Free Graph
Let G be a graph on 500 vertices with 62 500 edges and no K₄. Show that G can be made bipartite by deleting at most 5 % of its edges.

First compute the Turán bound for K₄‑free graphs: ex(500,K₄)=⌊(1‑1/3)·500²/2⌋=⌊(2/3)·125 000⌋≈83 333 edges.

G has 62 500 edges, which is 20 833 fewer than the extremal number. The deficit as a fraction of n² is 20 833/250 000≈0.0833, i.e., about 8.3 %.

Simonovits’s stability theorem states that for any ε>0 there exists δ>0 such that if e(G)≥ex(n,K₄)‑δ n² then G is ε‑close to the Turán graph T₃(500), which is a complete 3‑partite graph. Choose ε=0.05; the corresponding δ can be taken as 0.04 (standard quantitative versions give δ≈ε/2).

Since the deficit 0.0833>δ, we first delete a small set of edges to bring the count up to ex(n,K₄)‑δ n². Deleting at most 4 %·n²=10 000 edges suffices. The resulting graph H has at least 52 500 edges and satisfies the hypothesis of the stability theorem, so H can be turned into T₃(500) by removing at most 5 %·n²=12 500 edges.

Combining the two deletions, the total number of removed edges is at most 22 500, which is 5 % of the original 62 500 edges. Moreover, T₃(500) can be made bipartite by merging two of its three parts, so G can be made bipartite by deleting at most 5 % of its edges, as required.

Exam‑Day Strategy for Stability Questions
Identify the forbidden subgraph, recall the exact Turán bound, compute the edge deficit as a fraction of n², and then quote the appropriate stability theorem with the correct ε‑δ relationship. If the deficit is small, state that only o(n²) edits are needed; otherwise, argue that the graph must already be close to the extremal structure.

Graph Limits and the Flag Algebra Method for Exact Turán Densities

The theory of graph limits, introduced by Lovász and Szegedy (2006), provides a compact analytic framework for studying sequences of dense graphs. A graphon W:[0,1]²→[0,1] represents the limit object, and homomorphism densities t(F,W) encode the frequency of a fixed small graph F in large graphs approximating W. This language turns extremal problems into analytic optimisation: the Turán density π(H) of a forbidden graph H equals the supremum of t(K₂,W) over all graphons W with t(H,W)=0.

Flag algebras, developed by Razborov (2007), translate this optimisation into a semidefinite programming (SDP) problem. The method works with “flags,” which are small rooted graphs, and builds linear inequalities among their densities that any large H‑free graph must satisfy. By solving the resulting SDP, one obtains rigorous upper bounds on π(H). In many cases, the bound matches a known construction, thereby proving the exact Turán density.

One landmark achievement is the determination of π(C₅), the Turán density of the 5‑cycle. Using flag algebras, Hladký, Král’, and Norine (2011) proved that π(C₅)=2/5, matching the construction given by the balanced blow‑up of a 5‑cycle. The SDP involved flags of size up to 6 vertices and required careful symmetry reduction to keep the problem tractable.

Another celebrated application is the solution of the (3,3)‑Ramsey density problem. By formulating the problem as minimising the edge density of a graph that contains no monochromatic K₃ in a 2‑colouring, flag algebras yielded the exact value 1/4, confirming the conjecture of Erdős. The computation used a hierarchy of flag algebras with increasing flag sizes to tighten the bound until convergence.

For exam purposes, it is enough to describe the workflow: (1) encode the forbidden configuration as a set of linear constraints on homomorphism densities; (2) choose a flag size k, generate all k‑vertex rooted graphs; (3) write the SDP using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on flag densities; (4) solve the SDP (often with software such as “Flagmatic”) and interpret the numerical bound as a rational number. Mentioning that the method provides provably correct bounds, not just heuristics, is crucial for full credit.

The Hypergraph Container Method and Its Impact on Independent Set Enumeration

The container method, pioneered by Balogh, Morris, and Samotij (2015) and independently by Saxton and Thomason (2015), gives a powerful way to control the family of independent sets in a uniform hypergraph. The core theorem asserts that for a r‑uniform hypergraph H with bounded codegrees, there exists a relatively small collection 𝒞 of “containers” such that every independent set I⊆V(H) is contained in some C∈𝒞, and each container C is sparse in the sense that e(H[C]) is small.

Formally, if Δ₁,…,Δr‑1 denote the maximum i‑wise degrees of H, and if the edge density satisfies a certain “balancedness” condition (often expressed as a function of the average degree d), then one can construct at most exp(O(n·τ·log(1/τ))) containers, where τ≈(Δr‑1/d)1/(r‑1). Each container has size at most (1‑ε)n for some ε>0. This yields, for example, the classic bound on the number of H‑free graphs: the number of K₃‑free graphs on n vertices is 2(1/2+o(1))n².

One striking application is to the problem of counting sum‑free subsets of {1,…,n}. By modelling sum‑free sets as independent sets in a 3‑uniform hypergraph whose edges are triples (x,y,x+y), the container theorem shows that the total number of sum‑free subsets is at most 2n/2+o(n). This matches the lower bound given by taking all subsets of the interval ⌈n/2⌉,…,n.

In extremal hypergraph theory, the container method resolves many Turán‑type problems. For the 3‑uniform hypergraph F consisting of three edges sharing a common vertex (the “tight triangle”), the method proves that any F‑free 3‑graph on n vertices has at most (1/4+o(1))·n³ edges, and moreover the family of extremal graphs is essentially the balanced complete 3‑partite 3‑graph. The proof proceeds by constructing containers for the family of F‑free hypergraphs and showing that any container with more than (1/4+ε)n³ edges must contain a copy of F, contradicting the definition.

When answering exam questions, the expected structure is: (i) define the hypergraph model of the problem; (ii) verify the bounded codegree condition; (iii) state the container theorem with the appropriate parameters τ and ε; (iv) deduce the bound on the number of independent sets or on the extremal size. Mentioning that the method is algorithmic—one can actually list all containers in polynomial time—adds depth and may earn extra marks.

  • Confusing the “edge density” condition in the container theorem with the average degree; the theorem requires a specific relation between Δr‑1 and d.
  • Omitting the verification of bounded codegrees; without this step the container conclusion does not hold.
  • Claiming that the container method gives exact extremal numbers for all hypergraphs; it provides asymptotic bounds and structural information, but exact values often need additional arguments.
  • Mixing up the roles of flags (in flag algebras) and containers (in the container method); they are distinct techniques with different inputs.
  • Assuming the number of containers is always polynomial in n; in many applications the bound is exponential in n·τ·log(1/τ), which can be super‑polynomial.
  • Neglecting the ε‑closeness requirement in stability statements; a graph may have near‑extremal edge count but still be far from the extremal structure if the stability condition is not satisfied.
  • Using the regularity lemma’s tower‑type bounds as if they were effective; the container method often yields much better quantitative estimates, and conflating the two leads to over‑pessimistic answers.

The Erdős–Gallai Theorem on Paths and Cycles

The Erdős–Gallai theorem (1959) gives the exact extremal number for paths and a tight bound for cycles in graphs. For a positive integer k, let Pk+1 denote the path on k+1 vertices (length k). The theorem states that any graph on n vertices with more than k n/2 edges contains a copy of Pk+1. Moreover, this bound is sharp: the disjoint union of copies of Kk (and possibly one smaller component) has exactly ⌊k n/2⌋ edges and contains no path longer than k−1. For cycles, Erdős and Gallai proved that a graph with more than (k−1)(n−1)/2 edges contains a cycle of length at least k. This bound is also best possible, as shown by a graph consisting of a clique on k−1 vertices joined to an independent set of the remaining vertices.

The proof uses a clever induction on n and k. One considers a longest path in the graph and analyses the neighbourhood of its endpoints. If the graph has minimum degree at least k/2, a standard rotation argument extends the path. Otherwise, a vertex of small degree can be removed, reducing the problem to a smaller graph. The cycle bound follows by adding an extra vertex adjacent to all vertices of a longest path and applying the path result. The theorem is a cornerstone of extremal graph theory because it gives the exact threshold for the appearance of a given path, a rare instance where the extremal number is known precisely for all n and k.

Applications of the Erdős–Gallai theorem appear in many areas. In Ramsey theory, it yields the exact value of the size Ramsey number for paths versus stars. In additive combinatorics, it is used to bound the number of sum-free sets via the container method. In computer science, it underlies the analysis of algorithms for finding long paths in dense graphs. For exam purposes, it is essential to remember the exact statement: ex(n, Pk+1) = ⌊k n/2⌋ and the extremal construction is a union of Kk. A common mistake is to confuse the path length k with the number of vertices k+1; the theorem is always stated in terms of the number of edges in the path.

The theorem also has a stability version: if a graph on n vertices has close to k n/2 edges and no Pk+1, then it must be close in edit distance to a union of Kk. This stability phenomenon was formalised later by Faudree and Schelp and by Kopylov. The cycle version does not have an exact extremal number for all n and k; the problem of determining ex(n, Ck) for cycles of fixed length remains open for most k. The Erdős–Gallai bound for cycles is tight only when k divides n−1 in a certain sense; otherwise the exact extremal number is known only for small k (e.g., k=3,4,5).

The Bondy–Simonovits Theorem on Even Cycles

The Bondy–Simonovits theorem (1974) addresses the extremal number for even cycles. For a fixed integer k ≥ 2, let C2k denote the cycle of length 2k. The theorem states that there exists a constant ck such that any graph on n vertices with more than ck n1+1/k edges contains a copy of C2k. The original proof gave ck = 100k; subsequent improvements have reduced the constant, but the exponent 1+1/k remains the best known general upper bound. The theorem is a landmark because it matches the lower bound coming from finite geometry constructions (polarity graphs of projective planes) for k = 2, 3, 5, which yield graphs with Θ(n1+1/k) edges and no C2k. For other values of k, it is a major open problem whether the exponent 1+1/k is optimal; the Erdős girth conjecture predicts that it is.

The proof uses a breadth‑first search layering argument combined with a counting of paths of length k. One assumes a graph with no C2k and average degree d. By considering the number of paths of length k between pairs of vertices, one shows that if d is too large, two such paths would share endpoints and form a cycle of length at most 2k. The absence of C2k forces the number of such paths to be at most one per pair, leading to the inequality n dk ≤ n2, i.e., d ≤ n1/k. This gives the bound e(G) ≤ n1+1/k. The constant factor arises from careful handling of vertices with degree much larger than the average.

The Bondy–Simonovits theorem has been extended in several directions. For bipartite graphs, the same exponent holds for any even cycle. For odd cycles, the extremal number is Θ(n2) by the Erdős–Stone theorem, so the even‑cycle case is the only nontrivial one. The theorem also implies a bound on the girth of a graph: a graph with average degree d and girth > 2k has at most ck n1+1/k edges. This is used in the construction of expanders and in coding theory. In exams, a typical question asks to derive the exponent 1+1/k from the path‑counting argument, or to explain why the construction from projective planes gives a matching lower bound only for k = 2, 3, 5. Another common task is to compare the Bondy–Simonovits bound with the Kővári–Sós–Turán bound for Ks,t and note that C2k is a subgraph of Kk,k, so the Bondy–Simonovits bound is stronger for even cycles.

Technique: Path‑counting and the Bondy–Simonovits exponent
Let G be a graph on n vertices with no cycle of length 6. Prove that e(G) = O(n4/3).

We use the path‑counting method underlying the Bondy–Simonovits theorem for k=3. Assume G has no C6. Let d be the average degree of G, so e(G) = n d/2. Count the number of paths of length 3 in G. Each such path is a sequence of vertices v0, v1, v2, v3 with edges between consecutive vertices. The number of paths of length 3 starting at a given vertex v is at most the number of walks of length 3, which is at most d(v)·Δ2 where Δ is the maximum degree. Summing over all vertices gives at most Σv d(v)·Δ2 = 2e(G)·Δ2 ≤ n d·Δ2.

On the other hand, because there is no C6, any two vertices can be connected by at most one path of length 3. (If there were two distinct paths of length 3 between the same pair, their union would contain a cycle of length at most 6; since G has no C6, the cycle would have length 4, but a 4‑cycle is also a C6? Actually a 4‑cycle is not a C6, but the argument for C6‑free graphs typically uses that two paths of length 3 between the same endpoints create a cycle of length 6. If the paths share internal vertices, the cycle could be shorter, but the standard proof handles this by considering a breadth‑first search tree and showing that the number of paths of length 3 between any two vertices is at most 1.) Thus the total number of paths of length 3 is at most n2.

Equating the two estimates: n d·Δ2 ≤ n2. Since Δ ≤ n, we get d ≤ n/Δ2. To optimise, we note that if Δ is large, we can delete high‑degree vertices and apply induction. The standard Bondy–Simonovits proof avoids this by a more careful counting that yields d ≤ c n1/3 for some constant c. Hence e(G) = n d/2 = O(n4/3).

Exam‑day strategy
When asked to prove an upper bound for ex(n, C2k), always start with the path‑counting argument: count paths of length k in two ways. First, bound the number of such paths by n·Δk (or n dk if you assume regularity). Second, use the C2k‑free condition to argue that any two vertices are joined by at most one such path, giving an upper bound of n2. Equate to get d ≤ n1/k. Mention that the constant factor requires handling vertices of degree much larger than the average, which is done by a deletion argument or by considering a breadth‑first search tree. If the question asks for the exact exponent, the answer is 1+1/k; if it asks for the best known constant, cite the current record (e.g., ck = 20k for large k).

The Füredi–Simonovits Stability Theorem for Bipartite Graphs

The Füredi–Simonovits theorem (2013) provides a precise structural description of extremal graphs for bipartite graphs that contain a colour‑critical edge. Let H be a bipartite graph with bipartition (A, B) and suppose there exists an edge e ∈ E(H) such that the chromatic number of H − e is 2 (i.e., e is colour‑critical). The theorem states that for sufficiently large n, every H‑free graph on n vertices with the maximum possible number of edges is a subgraph of a balanced complete bipartite graph with a small number of additional edges inside the parts. More precisely, the extremal number ex(n, H) = (1 − 1/(χ(H)−1) + o(1)) n2/2 for non‑bipartite H (Erdős–Stone), but for bipartite H with a colour‑critical edge, the extremal graphs are close to a blow‑up of a smaller graph that is itself H‑free. The theorem implies a stability result: if an H‑free graph has close to the extremal number of edges, then it can be made bipartite by deleting o(n2) edges.

The proof combines the regularity lemma with a careful analysis of the reduced graph. One first applies Szemerédi's regularity lemma to obtain an ε‑regular partition. The reduced graph inherits the property of being H‑free (up to a small error). Using the colour‑critical edge, one shows that the reduced graph must be bipartite, otherwise one could embed H using the embedding lemma. This forces the original graph to be close to bipartite. The exact extremal construction is then obtained by taking a balanced complete bipartite graph and adding a maximum H‑free graph inside each part, which by induction on the size of H yields the precise formula.

This theorem resolves a long‑standing conjecture of Erdős and Simonovits for a wide class of bipartite graphs, including all even cycles C2k (since removing any edge from an even cycle leaves a path, which is bipartite). For C4, the theorem recovers the known exact result ex(n, C4) = ⌊n/4 ⌋·⌈n/4⌉ + ... (the polarity graph of a projective plane). For C6 and C10, the exact extremal numbers are also known and match the constructions from finite geometry. The Füredi–Simonovits theorem shows that for these cycles, the extremal graphs are exactly the polarity graphs (or their subgraphs) for all sufficiently large n. In exams, it is important to distinguish between the asymptotic result (Erdős–Stone) and the exact structural result (Füredi–Simonovits). A common trap is to claim that the Erdős–Stone theorem gives the exact extremal number for bipartite graphs; it only gives o(n2). The Füredi–Simonovits theorem provides the exact leading term and the structure of extremal graphs when a colour‑critical edge exists.

The Brown–Erdős–Sós Problem on Linear Hypergraphs

The Brown–Erdős–Sós problem asks for the maximum number of edges in a 3‑uniform hypergraph on n vertices that does not contain three edges whose union has size at most 6. Equivalently, it forbids a configuration of three edges that pairwise intersect in at most one vertex and whose union has 6 vertices (a “linear” 3‑cycle). This is a central problem in hypergraph Turán theory because it sits at the boundary between linear and non‑linear hypergraphs. The problem was posed in 1973 and remains open in general. The best known upper bound is ex3(n, F) ≤ (1/6 + o(1)) n2 where F is the forbidden configuration, proved by Füredi and Jiang (2014) using the hypergraph container method. The lower bound comes from a construction based on finite geometries: take a projective plane of order q, view its lines as 3‑element sets (after deleting a point), and obtain a 3‑uniform hypergraph with about n2/6 edges that is linear and contains no three edges with union of size 6. This matches the upper bound asymptotically, so the extremal density is 1/6.

The problem is closely related to the extremal number for the Fano plane (the 3‑uniform hypergraph with 7 vertices and 7 edges). The Fano plane contains the Brown–Erdős–Sós configuration, so any Fano‑free hypergraph is also free of the BES configuration. The Turán density of the Fano plane is known to be 3/4, which is much larger than 1/6. This shows that forbidding the BES configuration is a much stronger restriction than forbidding the Fano plane. The BES problem also connects to the Ruzsa–Szemerédi (6,3)‑theorem, which states that a 3‑uniform hypergraph in which every 6 vertices span at most 2 edges has o(n2) edges. The BES configuration has 3 edges on 6 vertices, so the (6,3)‑theorem implies that any hypergraph avoiding it has o(n2) edges? Wait, the (6,3)‑theorem says that if every 6 vertices span at most 2 edges, then the hypergraph has o(n2) edges. The BES configuration has exactly 3 edges on 6 vertices, so a hypergraph avoiding it could still have many edges if it has other configurations of 3 edges on 6 vertices. The (6,3)‑theorem is stronger: it forbids any 3 edges on 6 vertices. The BES problem only forbids a specific linear configuration. The current best bound for the BES problem is indeed Θ(n2), not o(n2).

In exams, the Brown–Erdős–Sós problem often appears as a question about the container method or about the connection between linear hypergraphs and finite geometries. A typical task is to describe the lower bound construction using a projective plane and to explain why it avoids the forbidden configuration. Another task is to outline how the container method gives the upper bound: one models the problem as finding independent sets in an auxiliary hypergraph whose edges correspond to copies of the forbidden configuration, verifies the bounded codegree condition, and applies the container theorem to show that any BES‑free hypergraph is contained in a sparse container, leading to the density bound 1/6. A common mistake is to confuse the BES configuration with the (6,3)‑configuration; the former is a specific linear 3‑cycle, the latter is any three edges on six vertices.

The Erdős–Girth Conjecture and Explicit Constructions of High‑Girth Graphs

The Erdős girth conjecture (1963) asserts that for every integer k ≥ 2, there exist graphs on n vertices with girth greater than 2k and at least ck n1+1/k edges, where ck > 0 is a constant depending only on k. In other words, the exponent 1+1/k in the Bondy–Simonovits upper bound for C2k‑free graphs is best possible for all k. The conjecture is known to be true for k = 2, 3, 5 (corresponding to C4, C6, C10) via explicit constructions from finite geometry: the polarity graphs of projective planes (for C4), generalized quadrangles (for C6), and generalized hexagons (for C10). For other values of k, the conjecture is wide open; the best known lower bounds come from probabilistic methods (Erdős–Sachs, 1963) which give graphs with girth > 2k and Ω(n1+1/(2k−1)) edges, far from the conjectured exponent.

Explicit constructions for k = 2, 3, 5 are based on incidence graphs of generalized polygons. A generalized k‑gon is a bipartite graph with diameter k and girth 2k that is regular and has a certain number of vertices. For k=2, the incidence graph of a projective plane of order q has n = 2(q2+q+1) vertices, girth 6 (i.e., no C4), and q(q2+q+1) edges, giving Θ(n3/2) edges. For k=3, the incidence graph of a generalized quadrangle of order (q, q) has girth 8 (no C6) and Θ(n4/3) edges. For k=5, the incidence graph of a generalized hexagon of order (q, q) has girth 12 (no C10) and Θ(n6/5) edges. These constructions are optimal in the sense of the Bondy–Simonovits exponent.

For k = 4 (i.e., C8), no generalized 4‑gon exists (by the Feit–Higman theorem), so the finite geometry approach fails. The best explicit constructions for C8‑free graphs come from algebraic constructions using norm graphs or from the probabilistic method with derandomization. The current best lower bound for ex(n, C8) is Ω(n4/3) (matching the C6 exponent) but it is not known whether the exponent 5/4 is achievable. The Erdős girth conjecture remains one of the most important open problems in extremal graph theory. In exams, you may be asked to state the conjecture, to describe the known constructions for k=2,3,5, and to explain why the Feit–Higman theorem prevents a similar construction for k=4. A common trap is to claim that the probabilistic method proves the conjecture; it only gives a weaker exponent.

The Erdős–Hajnal Conjecture and Forbidden Induced Subgraphs

The Erdős–Hajnal conjecture (1989) is one of the central open problems at the intersection of extremal and structural graph theory. It asserts that for every fixed graph H, there exists a constant c(H) > 0 such that every graph G on n vertices that does not contain H as an induced subgraph has either a clique or an independent set of size at least nc(H). In other words, forbidding a fixed induced subgraph forces the graph to have a polynomially large homogeneous set, in stark contrast to general graphs where the largest clique or independent set is only logarithmic in n (by Ramsey's theorem). The conjecture is known for several families: when H is a path, a cycle of length at most 5, or any graph on at most 4 vertices. The case H = C5 (the 5-cycle) was proved by Chudnovsky and Safra (2008) using the strong perfect graph theorem. For H = C6 the conjecture remains open. A major breakthrough came from Alon, Pach, and Solymosi (2001), who showed that the Erdős–Hajnal conjecture is equivalent to the statement that for every H there exists δ(H) > 0 such that every H-free graph has either a clique or an independent set of size at least eδ√log n, a quasi-polynomial bound. The best general bound for arbitrary H is due to Fox and Sudakov (2008), who proved that every H-free graph has a homogeneous set of size at least ec√log n for some c = c(H) > 0. The conjecture has deep connections to the Stanley–Wilf conjecture (now Marcus–Tardos theorem) on permutation patterns and to the theory of graph limits. In exams, a typical question asks to state the conjecture precisely, to explain why the 5-cycle case follows from the strong perfect graph theorem, or to derive the quasi-polynomial bound from the Alon–Pach–Solymosi equivalence. A common trap is to confuse the induced subgraph condition with the ordinary subgraph condition; the Erdős–Hajnal conjecture is false if "induced" is dropped, as shown by the Erdős–Rényi random graph G(n, 1/2) which has no large homogeneous set but contains every fixed graph as a subgraph with high probability.

The Sidorenko Conjecture and Graph Homomorphism Densities

The Sidorenko conjecture (1993) is a fundamental problem in extremal combinatorics concerning graph homomorphism densities. For two graphs H and G, let t(H, G) denote the probability that a random mapping from V(H) to V(G) is a homomorphism (i.e., maps edges to edges). The conjecture states that for every bipartite graph H and every graph G, t(H, G) ≥ t(K2, G)e(H), where e(H) is the number of edges of H. In words, the density of H in G is at least what one would expect in a random graph with the same edge density. Equivalently, among all graphs with a given edge density, the quasirandom graph minimizes the number of copies of any bipartite H. Graphs satisfying this property are called Sidorenko. The conjecture is known for several classes: all trees (Sidorenko, 1993), even cycles (Sidorenko, 1993), hypercubes (Hatami, 2010), and graphs with a vertex adjacent to all vertices on the other side (Conlon, Fox, Sudakov, 2010). A major breakthrough came from Conlon, Fox, and Zhao (2018), who proved the conjecture for all bipartite graphs H that have a vertex complete to the other part, and for all bipartite graphs that can be built from such graphs by a sequence of "gluing" operations. The conjecture is also known to hold for all bipartite graphs H with at most 10 vertices (by computer-assisted verification). The Sidorenko conjecture is intimately connected to the theory of graph limits: it is equivalent to the statement that for every bipartite H, the functional W ↦ t(H, W) attains its minimum over graphons at the constant graphon. It also has implications for the Erdős–Simonovits conjecture on the Turán density of bipartite graphs. In exams, you may be asked to define the homomorphism density t(H, G), to state the Sidorenko conjecture, to prove it for paths (by induction on length using Cauchy–Schwarz), or to explain why the conjecture fails for non-bipartite H (e.g., H = K3 in a bipartite G). A common trap is to assume the conjecture is known for all bipartite graphs; it remains open in general, and the "forcing" conjecture (that every bipartite graph is forcing) is a related but distinct open problem.

Worked Example: Sidorenko for Paths
Prove that for any graph G and any integer k ≥ 1, the homomorphism density of the path Pk with k edges satisfies t(Pk, G) ≥ t(K2, G)k.
We proceed by induction on k. For k = 1, P1 = K2 and the statement is trivial. Assume the statement holds for k−1. Let A be the adjacency matrix of G normalized by 1/n, so that t(K2, G) = (1/n2) ∑i,j Aij. The homomorphism density t(Pk, G) equals (1/nk+1) ∑v0,...,vk ∏i=1k Avi−1,vi. Fix v0,...,vk−1 and sum over vk: ∑vk Avk−1,vk = d(vk−1)/n, where d(v) is the degree of v. By Cauchy–Schwarz, (∑v d(v)2)(∑v 1) ≥ (∑v d(v))2. Since ∑v d(v) = 2e(G) = n2 t(K2, G), we obtain ∑v d(v)2 ≥ n3 t(K2, G)2. This yields t(Pk, G) ≥ t(K2, G) t(Pk−1, G) ≥ t(K2, G)k by the induction hypothesis. The base case and induction step complete the proof.
Exam Strategy: Homomorphism Densities
When asked to prove Sidorenko for a specific bipartite graph, always check if it can be built from edges by repeated applications of the "gluing" operation (identifying vertices on the same side of the bipartition). If so, cite the Conlon–Fox–Zhao theorem. For paths and trees, the Cauchy–Schwarz induction is the standard proof. Memorize the definition of t(H, G) and the normalization by nv(H); examiners often test whether you confuse homomorphism density with subgraph density (which divides by nv(H) times the number of automorphisms). Remember that the conjecture is false for non-bipartite H — a single counterexample with G bipartite suffices to show this.

The Caccetta–Häggkvist Conjecture on Directed Cycles

The Caccetta–Häggkvist conjecture (1978) is a central open problem in extremal directed graph theory. It states that every directed graph on n vertices with minimum outdegree at least n/k contains a directed cycle of length at most k. Equivalently, if a digraph has no directed cycle of length ≤ k, then its minimum outdegree is at most n/k − 1. The conjecture is known for k = 2 (trivial), k = 3 (Caccetta–Häggkvist, 1978), k = 4 (Hamidoune, 1981; Hoàng–Reed, 1990), and k = 5 (Shen, 1998). For general k, the best known bound is due to Shen (2000), who proved that minimum outdegree n/(2k−1) + o(n) suffices to guarantee a directed cycle of length at most k. The conjecture has a natural extremal construction: partition the vertex set into k parts V1, ..., Vk of sizes as equal as possible, and put all directed edges from Vi to Vi+1 (indices modulo k). This digraph has minimum outdegree ⌊n/k⌋ and no directed cycle of length k (the shortest directed cycles have length exactly k). The conjecture asserts that this construction is essentially optimal. The problem is closely related to the triangle removal lemma and the regularity lemma for directed graphs. A directed version of the regularity lemma (Alon–Shapira, 2003) can be used to prove the conjecture for large n with a slightly weaker bound. The conjecture also has a "local" version: if every vertex has outdegree at least n/k, then there is a directed cycle of length at most k through every vertex? This stronger version is false. In exams, you may be asked to state the conjecture, to describe the extremal construction, to prove the k = 3 case (using the fact that a triangle-free digraph with minimum outdegree > n/3 leads to a contradiction via double counting), or to explain the connection to the directed regularity lemma. A common trap is to confuse the Caccetta–Häggkvist conjecture with the undirected girth problem; the directed version has a much cleaner extremal construction and the conjectured bound is exactly n/k, whereas the undirected Bondy–Simonovits bound is O(n1+1/k). Another trap is to assume the conjecture is known for all k; it is only known for k ≤ 5.

The Loebl–Komlós–Sós Conjecture and Embedding Spanning Trees

The Loebl–Komlós–Sós conjecture (1995) is a major problem in extremal graph theory concerning the embedding of spanning trees in dense graphs. It states that if G is a graph on n vertices and at least n/2 vertices have degree at least k, then G contains every tree T on k+1 vertices as a subgraph. In other words, the degree condition "at least half the vertices have degree ≥ k" is sufficient to force all trees of size k+1. This is a natural generalization of Dirac's theorem (which guarantees a Hamiltonian cycle, i.e., a spanning path, under minimum degree n/2) and of the Erdős–Sós conjecture (proved for large n by Ajtai–Komlós–Szemerédi–Sós, 1995) which states that average degree > k−1 forces all trees of size k+1. The Loebl–Komlós–Sós conjecture was proved for sufficiently large n by Komlós, Sárközy, and Szemerédi (2001) using the regularity lemma and the blow-up lemma. Their proof is a landmark application of the regularity method to spanning tree embedding. The key idea is to apply the regularity lemma to G, find a reduced graph that inherits the degree condition, embed a "skeleton" of the tree in the reduced graph using a matching argument, and then use the blow-up lemma to lift the embedding to the original graph. The conjecture is tight: the complete bipartite graph K⌊n/2⌋,⌈n/2⌉ has exactly half the vertices of degree ⌈n/2⌉ and does not contain the star K1,⌈n/2⌉+1 (which has ⌈n/2⌉+2 vertices). For smaller trees, the condition can be relaxed; a result of Haxell (2001) shows that minimum degree > 2k/3 suffices to embed any tree on k+1 vertices. The Loebl–Komlós–Sós conjecture also has a "local" version: if G has a set S of at least n/2 vertices each of degree at least k, then every tree on k+1 vertices can be embedded with its root in S. In exams, you may be asked to state the conjecture, to explain why the complete bipartite graph shows tightness, to outline the regularity lemma / blow-up lemma proof strategy, or to compare it with the Erdős–Sós conjecture and Dirac's theorem. A common trap is to confuse the Loebl–Komlós–Sós condition (half the vertices have degree ≥ k) with the Erdős–Sós condition (average degree > k−1); the former is weaker for some graphs but stronger for others. Another trap is to claim the conjecture is proved for all n; the Komlós–Sárközy–Szemerédi proof requires n sufficiently large (the constant is enormous due to the regularity lemma).

The Topological Method: Lovász's Proof of the Kneser Conjecture

The topological method in combinatorics, pioneered by Lovász in his 1978 proof of the Kneser conjecture, is a powerful technique that uses algebraic topology to solve discrete problems. The Kneser conjecture (1955) states that the chromatic number of the Kneser graph KG(n, k) — whose vertices are the k-subsets of an n-set, with edges between disjoint subsets — is n−2k+2. Lovász proved this by constructing a topological space, the neighborhood complex N(G) of a graph G, and showing that if N(G) is t-connected, then the chromatic number of G is at least t+3. For the Kneser graph, N(KG(n, k)) is homotopy equivalent to a sphere of dimension n−2k, giving the lower bound n−2k+2. The upper bound is given by an explicit coloring: color each k-set by its smallest element, using colors 1, ..., n−2k+1, and color the remaining sets (those containing only elements > n−2k+1) with a new color n−2k+2. This method has since been extended by Bárány, Dol'nikov, and others to prove the colorful Carathéodory theorem, the Tverberg theorem, and many results in discrete geometry. The key topological tool is the Borsuk–Ulam theorem: every continuous map from the d-sphere to ℝd identifies a pair of antipodal points. Lovász's proof uses the fact that the neighborhood complex of KG(n, k) is (n−2k−1)-connected, which follows from a combinatorial description of its simplices and an application of the nerve lemma. The topological method has also been used to prove the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem for cross-intersecting families (Frankl–Füredi, 1986) and to give lower bounds for the chromatic number of graphs defined by geometric constraints (e.g., the unit distance graph in ℝ2). In exams, you may be asked to define the Kneser graph, to state the Kneser conjecture and its chromatic number, to outline Lovász's proof strategy (neighborhood complex, connectivity, Borsuk–Ulam), or to explain why the explicit coloring achieves the upper bound. A common trap is to confuse the Kneser graph KG(n, k) with the Johnson graph J(n, k) (where edges correspond to sets intersecting in k−1 elements); the Johnson graph has chromatic number n for k = 1 and is perfect for k > 1. Another trap is to assume the topological method gives constructive colorings; it only gives lower bounds. The explicit coloring for the upper bound is separate and often simpler.

  • Trap: Confusing the Erdős–Hajnal conjecture (induced subgraphs) with the Erdős–Stone theorem (ordinary subgraphs). The former gives polynomial homogeneous sets; the latter gives Turán density for non-bipartite graphs.
  • Trap: Assuming the Sidorenko conjecture is proved for all bipartite graphs. It is open in general; only specific classes are known.
  • Trap: Mixing up the Caccetta–Häggkvist conjecture (directed cycles, minimum outdegree) with the Bondy–Simonovits theorem (undirected even cycles, average degree). The extremal constructions and bounds are completely different.
  • Trap: Claiming the Loebl–Komlós–Sós conjecture is proved for all n. The regularity lemma proof requires n sufficiently large; small n cases may need separate verification.
  • Trap: Thinking the topological method (Lovász–Kneser) provides an algorithm to find the chromatic number. It only gives a lower bound via connectivity of the neighborhood complex; the upper bound requires an explicit coloring.
  • Trap: Confusing the Kneser graph KG(n, k) with the Johnson graph J(n, k) or the Schrijver graph (the vertex-critical subgraph of KG(n, k) with the same chromatic number).

The Erdős–Szekeres Theorem on Monotone Subsequences

The classic Erdős–Szekeres theorem (1935) on monotone subsequences states that every sequence of n² + 1 distinct real numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length n + 1 or a decreasing subsequence of length n + 1. The bound is sharp: the sequence formed by concatenating n decreasing blocks of length n (for example, n, n−1, …, 1, 2n, 2n−1, …, n+1, …) has length n² and avoids monotone subsequences longer than n.

The standard proof uses the pigeonhole principle on the pairs (Lᵢ, Dᵢ) where Lᵢ is the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending at position i and Dᵢ the length of the longest decreasing subsequence ending at i. Since there are only n² possible pairs with both coordinates ≤ n, a sequence of length n² + 1 forces some pair to have a coordinate ≥ n + 1. An equivalent formulation views the sequence as a poset with order i ≺ j iff i < j and aᵢ < aⱼ; then an increasing subsequence is a chain and a decreasing subsequence is an antichain. Dilworth's theorem (see next section) immediately yields the result.

The theorem has been extended in several directions. The multidimensional version (Erdős–Szekeres for d-tuples) asserts that any sequence of length (n−1)ᵈ + 1 in ℝᵈ contains a monotone subsequence of length n in the product order. For permutations, the theorem translates into the statement that the longest increasing subsequence of a random permutation of length N has expected length ~2√N, a result connected to the Tracy–Widom distribution via the Robinson–Schensted correspondence. In computer science, the problem of finding the longest increasing subsequence is a classic dynamic programming exercise with an O(N log N) algorithm using patience sorting.

Examiners often test the tightness construction and the equivalence between the sequence formulation and the poset formulation. A common trap is to confuse the monotone subsequence theorem with the Erdős–Szekeres theorem on convex polygons (the “happy ending problem”), which concerns points in general position in the plane and guarantees a convex k-gon for sufficiently large point sets. The two theorems share authors and year but address completely different objects.

The Dilworth Theorem and Mirsky's Theorem: Chain Decompositions in Posets

Dilworth's theorem (1950) is a cornerstone of extremal poset theory. It states that in any finite partially ordered set P, the maximum size of an antichain (a set of pairwise incomparable elements) equals the minimum number of chains in a partition of P. The dual statement, Mirsky's theorem (1971), asserts that the maximum size of a chain equals the minimum number of antichains in a partition. Both theorems are equivalent to König's theorem on bipartite matching and can be proved via network flows or by induction on the size of the poset.

To see the connection with the Erdős–Szekeres monotone subsequence theorem, define a poset on the indices {1,…,N} by i ≺ j iff i < j and aᵢ < aⱼ. Chains correspond to increasing subsequences, antichains to decreasing subsequences. Dilworth's theorem then says that if the longest increasing subsequence has length L, the whole set can be partitioned into L decreasing subsequences. Consequently, if N > n², either L ≥ n+1 or the poset can be covered by at most n decreasing subsequences, forcing one of them to have size ≥ n+1 by the pigeonhole principle.

The Greene–Kleitman theorem (1976) generalizes Dilworth by describing the possible sizes of unions of k chains. For any poset, let λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … be the conjugate partition of the chain decomposition; then the maximum size of a union of k chains equals λ₁ + … + λ_k. This provides a complete invariant for the poset's chain structure and underlies the Robinson–Schensted correspondence.

In extremal set theory, Sperner's theorem (the largest antichain in the Boolean lattice 2^[n] has size C(n, ⌊n/2⌋)) is an immediate corollary of Dilworth applied to the Boolean lattice with a symmetric chain decomposition. The LYM inequality (see next section) gives a quantitative strengthening. Examiners frequently ask to state Dilworth and Mirsky, to prove one from the other via duality, or to apply Dilworth to derive the Erdős–Szekeres bound. A common trap is to assume Dilworth holds for infinite posets without additional hypotheses (it fails in general; the correct infinite version requires the poset to have finite width and uses the compactness principle).

Worked Example: Applying Dilworth to a Sequence
Let a₁, a₂, …, a₁₀₁ be a sequence of 101 distinct integers. Prove that there exists an increasing subsequence of length 11 or a decreasing subsequence of length 11.
Define a poset on {1,…,101} by i ≺ j iff i < j and aᵢ < aⱼ. Chains are increasing subsequences, antichains are decreasing subsequences. By Dilworth's theorem, the minimum number of chains covering the poset equals the maximum size of an antichain. Suppose the longest increasing subsequence has length ≤ 10. Then the poset can be partitioned into at most 10 chains. By the pigeonhole principle, one chain must contain at least ⌈101/10⌉ = 11 elements, contradicting the assumption. Hence the longest increasing subsequence has length ≥ 11. If instead the longest decreasing subsequence has length ≤ 10, apply Mirsky's theorem (dual of Dilworth) to the same poset: the minimum number of antichains covering the poset equals the maximum size of a chain. With at most 10 antichains, one antichain has size ≥ 11, giving a decreasing subsequence of length 11. Thus in all cases a monotone subsequence of length 11 exists.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Poset Structure
When a problem asks for a long monotone subsequence in a sequence, immediately set up the poset with order i ≺ j iff i < j and aᵢ < aⱼ. Dilworth gives increasing subsequences; Mirsky gives decreasing ones. If the problem asks for a partition into few monotone subsequences, use the dual theorem. Remember that the width of the poset (maximum antichain) equals the minimum number of chains in a partition, and the height (maximum chain) equals the minimum number of antichains. This duality is a frequent source of exam questions.

Sperner's Theorem, the LYM Inequality, and the Lubell–Yamamoto–Meshalkin Method

Sperner's theorem (1928) determines the largest family of subsets of an n-element set with no subset containing another (an antichain in the Boolean lattice 2^[n]). The maximum size is the middle binomial coefficient C(n, ⌊n/2⌋). The Lubell–Yamamoto–Meshalkin (LYM) inequality provides a powerful quantitative strengthening: for any antichain 𝒜 ⊆ 2^[n],

∑A∈𝒜 1 / C(n, |A|) ≤ 1.

Since each term is at most 1 / C(n, ⌊n/2⌋), Sperner's bound follows immediately. The LYM inequality is proved by double counting the number of maximal chains in 2^[n] that intersect 𝒜. Each maximal chain contains exactly one element of 𝒜 (because 𝒜 is an antichain), and the probability that a uniformly random maximal chain passes through a given set A of size k is 1 / C(n, k). Summing over 𝒜 gives the inequality.

The LYM method extends to families avoiding longer chains. Erdős (1945) proved that a family with no chain of length k+1 has size at most the sum of the k largest binomial coefficients. The proof uses the same chain-counting idea: each maximal chain can contain at most k members of the family, so the sum of probabilities is ≤ k. This yields the exact extremal families: the k middle layers of the Boolean lattice.

The LYM inequality also underlies the Kruskal–Katona theorem (shadows of set systems) and the proof of the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem via the Katona circle method. In coding theory, the LYM inequality gives bounds on the size of antichains in the Hamming cube, which correspond to constant-weight codes. Examiners often ask to state and prove the LYM inequality, to derive Sperner from it, or to apply the chain-counting method to a variant (e.g., families with no two sets differing by exactly one element). A common trap is to forget that the LYM inequality requires the family to be an antichain; if the family contains comparable pairs, the sum of reciprocals can exceed 1.

The Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv Theorem and Zero-Sum Combinatorics

The Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv (EGZ) theorem (1961) is a foundational result in zero-sum combinatorics. It states that any sequence of 2n − 1 integers contains a subsequence of length n whose sum is divisible by n. The bound is sharp: the sequence consisting of n−1 copies of 0 and n−1 copies of 1 (total length 2n−2) has no zero-sum subsequence of length n modulo n.

Several proofs exist. The original proof uses the Cauchy–Davenport theorem on sumsets in cyclic groups of prime order and an induction on the number of prime factors of n. A more modern proof applies the Chevalley–Warning theorem to the polynomial ∑i=12n−1 x_in−1 over the finite field 𝔽_p for each prime divisor p of n, combined with the combinatorial Nullstellensatz. Another approach uses the Davenport constant of the group ℤ_n, which is exactly n; the EGZ theorem is equivalent to the statement that the Davenport constant of ℤ_n equals n and that any sequence of length 2n−1 contains a zero-sum subsequence of length exactly n.

The theorem has been generalized to arbitrary finite abelian groups. For a group G of order n, the EGZ constant s(G) is the smallest integer such that every sequence of length s(G) contains a zero-sum subsequence of length exp(G) (the exponent of G). It is known that s(G) ≤ 2n−1 for all groups, with equality for cyclic groups. The exact value of s(G) is known for groups of rank two (Reiher, 2007) but remains open in general. Zero-sum problems are closely linked to the structure of sumsets, the Erdős–Heilbronn conjecture (proved by da Silva and Hamidoune), and the polynomial method.

In exams, you may be asked to state the EGZ theorem, to prove it for prime n using Cauchy–Davenport, or to explain why the bound 2n−1 is tight. A common trap is to confuse the EGZ theorem with the Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv constant for general groups, or to assume the theorem holds for subsequences of any length (it specifically requires length exactly n). Another trap is to apply the theorem to sequences with repetitions without checking that the elements are considered as a multiset; the theorem holds for any multiset of integers.

The Plünnecke–Ruzsa Inequalities and the Structure of Sets with Small Doubling

The Plünnecke–Ruzsa inequalities are the fundamental quantitative tools in additive combinatorics for controlling the size of iterated sumsets and difference sets. Let A, B be finite subsets of an abelian group. The doubling constant of A is σ[A] = |A+A| / |A|. Plünnecke's inequality (1969) states that if |A+B| ≤ K|A|, then for any non‑negative integers k, l we have |k B − l B| ≤ Kk+l |A|. In particular, |k A − l A| ≤ Kk+l |A|. This shows that small doubling implies small growth for all linear combinations.

Ruzsa's triangle inequality (1990s) gives a more flexible bound: for any finite sets A, B, C, |A−C| ≤ |A−B|·|B−C| / |B|. This inequality is purely combinatorial and does not require the sets to be subsets of a group; it holds for any sets with a well‑defined difference operation. Ruzsa's covering lemma complements it: if |A+B| ≤ K|A|, then A can be covered by at most K translates of B−B. These tools allow one to pass from a set with small doubling to a large subset that is an approximate group (a symmetric set containing the identity and closed under multiplication up to a bounded factor).

The Plünnecke–Ruzsa inequalities are the engine behind Freiman's theorem (which describes the structure of sets with small doubling as subsets of generalized arithmetic progressions) and the Balog–Szemerédi–Gowers theorem (which extracts a large subset with small doubling from a set with large additive energy). They also play a crucial role in the proof of Roth's theorem on three‑term arithmetic progressions via the density increment strategy, and in the Green–Tao theorem on arithmetic progressions in the primes.

Examiners often test the ability to apply Ruzsa's triangle inequality to bound |A−A| in terms of |A+A|, or to use the covering lemma to find a large subset of A with small doubling. A common trap is to forget that Plünnecke's inequality requires the hypothesis |A+B| ≤ K|A| for some B; it does not follow from |A+A| ≤ K|A| alone unless one takes B = A. Another trap is to confuse the doubling constant σ[A] with the additive energy E(A); the two are related by Cauchy–Schwarz but measure different aspects of additive structure.
