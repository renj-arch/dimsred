Solutions — Concentration Units, Raoult's Law, Colligative Properties & Solubility

Deep dive into Solutions — the homogeneous mixtures of two or more substances. Concentration units: molarity M = n_solute/V_solution (mol/L, temperature-dependent because volume changes with T), molality m = n_solute/kg_solvent (mol/kg, temperature-independent, preferred for colligative properties), mole fraction X_i = n_i/S n_j (dimensionless, used in Raoult's law and gas mixtures), percent compositions: weight/weight % w/w = (mass solute / mass solution) × 100%, weight/volume % w/v = (g solute / mL solution) × 100%, volume/volume % v/v = (mL solute / mL solution) × 100%, parts per million ppm = (mass solute / mass solution) × 106, parts per billion ppb = (mass solute / mass solution) × 10?. Conversion between units: molarity to molality requires solution density; mole fraction to molarity requires the molar mass of the solvent. Raoult's law: P_A = X_A P°_A, where P_A is the vapor pressure of component A above the solution, X_A is its mole fraction, and P°_A is its vapor pressure as a pure liquid. For an ideal solution (solvent-solute interactions similar to solvent-solvent interactions), Raoult's law holds for both components. For non-ideal solutions: positive deviation (P_total > X_A P°_A + X_B P°_B, e.g., ethanol + water at low ethanol concentrations, ethanol-water azeotrope) and negative deviation (P_total < ideal, e.g., acetone + chloroform due to hydrogen bonding). Henry's law: P_gas = k_H X_gas, where k_H is the Henry's law constant (increases with temperature — gas solubility decreases with heating). Colligative properties depend only on the number (not identity) of solute particles: vapor pressure lowering (?P = X_solute P°_solvent), boiling point elevation (?T_b = K_b m, where K_b is the ebullioscopic constant, e.g., 0.512 °C·kg/mol for water), freezing point depression (?T_f = K_f m, where K_f is the cryoscopic constant, e.g., 1.86 °C·kg/mol for water), osmotic pressure (? = i M R T, where i is the van't Hoff factor). The van't Hoff factor i accounts for dissociation (i > 1 for electrolytes: NaCl gives i ˜ 2, CaCl2 gives i ˜ 3) or association (i < 1). For ideal behavior, i = number of ions per formula unit; actual i is less due to ion pairing. Applications: determining molar mass from freezing point depression or osmotic pressure, desalination by reverse osmosis (applying pressure > ?). Solubility: the solubility product K_sp = ? [ion]^{coefficient} for a sparingly soluble salt (e.g., AgCl(s) ? Ag? + Cl?, K_sp = [Ag?][Cl?] = 1.8×10?¹°). The common ion effect: solubility decreases when a common ion is already present. Selective precipitation: different K_sp values allow separation of ions by controlled addition of a precipitating agent. Factors affecting solubility: temperature (most solids become more soluble at higher T, but gas solubility decreases), pressure (gas solubility follows Henry's law, P? increases solubility), common ion (decreases solubility), pH (for salts of weak acids, lower pH increases solubility by protonating the anion), complex ion formation (e.g., adding NH3 dissolves AgCl by forming [Ag(NH3)2]?).

Part 1

?? Concentration Units and Conversions

Concentration
Molarity, Molality, Mole Fraction, Percent Compositions, ppm, ppb, and Conversions
Molarity (M) = moles of solute per liter of solution. It is the most common concentration unit in the laboratory but varies with temperature (volume expands). Molality (m) = moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. It is temperature-independent and preferred for colligative property calculations. For dilute aqueous solutions, molarity and molality are numerically similar (since 1 L water ˜ 1 kg). Mole fraction (X): for component i in a mixture, X_i = n_i / (n1 + n2 + …). S X_i = 1. Used in Raoult's law and for gas-phase concentrations. Percent by mass (% w/w): (mass solute / mass solution) × 100. Percent by volume (% v/v): (volume solute / volume solution) × 100. Mass/volume percent (% w/v): (g solute / mL solution) × 100 (not a true percentage since units differ, but widely used in medicine and pharmacy). Parts per million (ppm): (mass solute / mass solution) × 106. In dilute aqueous solutions, 1 ppm ˜ 1 mg/L. Parts per billion (ppb): × 10?. Conversion between units: from molarity to molality: m = (1000 M) / (1000 ? - M M_solute) where ? is solution density (g/mL) and M_solute is molar mass (g/mol). From molality to mole fraction: X_solute = (m / m_solvent_moles) / (m / m_solvent_moles + 1/m_solvent_molar_mass) — simplified: X_solute = (m M_solvent) / (1000 + m M_solvent) for water. For very dilute solutions, X_solute ˜ m M_solvent/1000. Normality (N) = n_eq/L = M × (number of H? or e? transferred per formula unit) — used in acid-base and redox titrations but decreasing in popularity.
Different concentration units serve different purposes: molarity for volumetric work, molality for temperature-independent colligative properties, mole fraction for gas mixtures and Raoult's law, and ppm/ppb for trace analysis.
Part 2

?? Raoult's Law, Henry's Law, and Ideal Solutions

Vapor Pressure
Raoult's Law, Ideal vs Non-Ideal Solutions, Henry's Law, and Azeotropes
Raoult's law: the partial vapor pressure of a component in an ideal solution equals its mole fraction times its vapor pressure as a pure substance: P_A = X_A P°_A. For an ideal solution of A and B (both volatile), the total vapor pressure P_total = X_A P°_A + X_B P°_B = P°_B + (P°_A - P°_B) X_A — a linear function of composition. Raoult's law is obeyed when A-A, B-B, and A-B intermolecular forces are all similar (e.g., benzene-toluene, hexane-heptane). Non-ideal solutions show deviations: positive deviation occurs when A-B interactions are weaker than A-A and B-B interactions — molecules escape more easily, giving higher vapor pressure than Raoult's law predicts (e.g., ethanol + water, acetone + CS2). This can produce a maximum-boiling azeotrope (actually minimum-boiling — the azeotrope boils at a lower temperature than either pure component). Wait — positive deviation means higher vapor pressure, which means lower boiling point (minimum-boiling azeotrope). Ethanol-water forms a minimum-boiling azeotrope at 95.6% ethanol by mass (bp 78.2°C). Negative deviation occurs when A-B interactions are stronger than A-A and B-B interactions — molecules are held more tightly, giving lower vapor pressure (e.g., acetone + chloroform, HNO3 + H2O). This produces a maximum-boiling azeotrope (higher boiling point than either pure component). HNO3-water forms a maximum-boiling azeotrope at 68% HNO3 (bp 120.5°C). Henry's law: for a gas dissolved in a liquid, P_gas = k_H X_gas, where k_H is the Henry's law constant (units of pressure). Henry's law applies when the solute (gas) is dilute and its molecules are surrounded by solvent molecules (different environment from pure solute). k_H increases with temperature (gas solubility decreases at higher T). For volatile solutes at high concentration, Raoult's law applies (the solute molecules experience the same environment as in the pure liquid). Distillation separates mixtures based on vapor pressure differences; azeotropes cannot be separated by simple distillation (they distill at constant composition).
Raoult's law describes the vapor pressure of ideal solutions; deviations indicate non-ideal interactions, and Henry's law describes gas solubility at low concentrations.

?? Colligative Properties and the van't Hoff Factor

Colligative
Boiling Point Elevation, Freezing Point Depression, Osmotic Pressure, and the van't Hoff Factor
Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles, not their identity. There are four: vapor pressure lowering: ?P = X_solute P°_solvent (from Raoult's law). Boiling point elevation: a non-volatile solute raises the boiling point (because vapor pressure is lower, requiring a higher T to reach 1 atm). ?T_b = K_b m, where K_b is the ebullioscopic constant of the solvent (water: 0.512 °C·kg/mol, benzene: 2.53 °C·kg/mol). Freezing point depression: a solute lowers the freezing point (the solute disrupts the crystal lattice of the solvent). ?T_f = K_f m, where K_f is the cryoscopic constant (water: 1.86 °C·kg/mol, benzene: 5.12 °C·kg/mol). Note that K_f > K_b for the same solvent — freezing point depression is generally more sensitive. Osmotic pressure: the pressure required to prevent the net flow of solvent across a semipermeable membrane from pure solvent to a solution. ? = i M R T, where M is molarity, R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K), T in Kelvin. For dilute aqueous solutions, osmotic pressure is large even at low concentrations (? = 0.5 M × 0.08206 × 298 = 12.2 atm for 0.5 M solution). Reverse osmosis: applying pressure > ? to force solvent from the solution to pure solvent — used in desalination. The van't Hoff factor i accounts for the dissociation or association of the solute. For a non-electrolyte (sugar, urea), i = 1. For a strong electrolyte that fully dissociates, i is the number of ions per formula unit (NaCl: i = 2, CaCl2: i = 3, Na2SO4: i = 3, FeCl3: i = 4). In practice, i is slightly less than the ideal value due to ion pairing (electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions reduces the effective particle count). For weak electrolytes, i is between 1 and the full dissociation value. The colligative property equations corrected: ?T_b = i K_b m, ?T_f = i K_f m, ? = i M R T. Applications: determining the molar mass of an unknown compound from measured ?T_f or ? (the classic cryoscopic method). For example, dissolving a known mass in a known mass of solvent and measuring ?T_f gives m = ?T_f/(i K_f), from which M_solute = (mass_solute)/(m × kg_solvent).
Colligative properties — vapor pressure lowering, boiling point elevation, freezing point depression, and osmotic pressure — depend solely on the number of solute particles, with the van't Hoff factor correcting for dissociation or association.

Solubility
The Solubility Product, Common Ion Effect, Selective Precipitation, and Factors Affecting Solubility
The solubility product constant K_sp is the equilibrium constant for a sparingly soluble salt dissolving in water. For A_mB_n(s) ? mA^{n+} + nB^{m-}, K_sp = [A^{n+}]^m [B^{m-}]^n. If Q_sp < K_sp, the solution is unsaturated (more salt can dissolve). If Q_sp > K_sp, precipitation occurs until Q_sp = K_sp. HPO4²?), decreasing [anion] and increasing solubility. (4) Complex ion formation: adding NH3 to AgCl dissolves it by forming [Ag(NH3)2]? (AgCl + 2NH3 ? The solubility product K_sp quantifies the extent of dissolution of sparingly soluble salts; the common ion effect, pH, and complex formation can dramatically alter solubility by shifting the dissolution equilibrium.
