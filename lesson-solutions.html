Solutions — Concentration Units, Raoult’s Law, Colligative Properties & Solubility

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The component present in the larger amount is the solvent; the others are solutes. Many physical properties of a solution depend on the number of solute particles rather than their identity, which is why colligative properties provide a window into dissociation and association in solution.

“The properties of a solution are not merely the sum of the properties of its components; the interactions between them matter.”
The central idea behind Raoult’s and Henry’s laws
Part 1

Concentration Units and Conversions

Concentration
Molarity, molality, mole fraction and mass-based units

The choice of concentration unit depends on the measurement being made. Molarity M = nsolute/Vsolution (mol L⁻¹) is convenient for volumetric work but changes with temperature because the solution volume expands. Molality m = nsolute/masssolvent (mol kg⁻¹) is temperature-independent and is the natural unit for colligative properties. Mole fraction xi = ni/ntotal is dimensionless and convenient for vapour-pressure calculations.

Mass percent and parts per million are useful for very dilute or industrial solutions. Converting between molarity and molality requires the solution density: m = M/(ρ − M Msolute/1000), where ρ is in g cm⁻³ and M is molarity.

Part 2

Raoult’s Law, Henry’s Law and Ideal Solutions

Vapour Pressure
Ideal mixtures, deviations and azeotropes

For an ideal solution, the partial vapour pressure of each component is proportional to its mole fraction in the liquid:

pi = xi p°i

This is Raoult’s law. The total pressure is p = p₁ + p₂. An ideal solution forms when the intermolecular forces between unlike molecules are similar to those between like molecules, and the enthalpy of mixing is zero.

Real solutions deviate. Positive deviations occur when A–B interactions are weaker than A–A and B–B interactions; the molecules escape more easily, so the total pressure is higher than predicted and the mixture may form a minimum-boiling azeotrope (for example, ethanol–water). Negative deviations occur when A–B interactions are stronger, lowering the vapour pressure and potentially producing a maximum-boiling azeotrope (for example, nitric acid–water).

Henry’s law describes the solubility of a sparingly soluble gas: p = KHx. The Henry constant KH increases with temperature, so gases become less soluble in warm water. This is why dissolved oxygen levels fall in heated rivers and why carbonated drinks go flat when warm.

Part 3

Colligative Properties and the van’t Hoff Factor

Colligative Properties
Boiling-point elevation, freezing-point depression and osmotic pressure

Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles, not their chemical identity. For a non-volatile, non-electrolyte solute:

  • Relative lowering of vapour pressure: (p° − p)/p° = xsolute
  • Boiling-point elevation: ΔTb = Kb m
  • Freezing-point depression: ΔTf = Kf m
  • Osmotic pressure: π = MRT

Kb and Kf are ebullioscopic and cryoscopic constants characteristic of the solvent. For electrolytes, the number of particles is larger than the formal number of formula units, so each expression is multiplied by the van’t Hoff factor i:

ΔTb = iKbm,   ΔTf = iKfm,   π = iMRT

For complete dissociation i equals the number of ions per formula unit (2 for NaCl, 3 for CaCl₂); for weak electrolytes i lies between 1 and that limit and can be used to estimate the degree of dissociation. Association, such as dimerisation of carboxylic acids in benzene, gives i < 1. Osmotic pressure is especially useful for determining the molar mass of polymers and proteins because it is measurable at low concentrations.

Part 4

Solubility and Ksp

Solubility Equilibria
The solubility product, common ions and coupled equilibria

For a sparingly soluble salt MaXb dissolving to Ma+ and Xb−, the equilibrium constant is the solubility product:

Ksp = [Ma+]a[Xb−]b

If the salt dissociates completely and no other source of ions is present, solubility s is related to Ksp by Ksp = aabbsa+b. A common ion lowers solubility through Le Chatelier’s principle: adding NaCl to a saturated AgCl solution shifts the dissolution equilibrium to the left. pH can also matter when the anion is basic (carbonates, hydroxides, sulphides) or the cation hydrolyses. Complex formation can increase solubility by removing the free metal ion, as in the dissolution of AgCl in ammonia.

Precipitation begins when the reaction quotient Q exceeds Ksp. This criterion underlies qualitative analysis, water softening and the controlled precipitation of sparingly soluble salts.
