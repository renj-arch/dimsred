Solutions — Concentration Units, Raoult’s Law, Colligative Properties & Solubility

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The component present in the larger amount is the solvent; the others are solutes. Many physical properties of a solution depend on the number of solute particles rather than their identity, which is why colligative properties provide a window into dissociation and association in solution.

“The properties of a solution are not merely the sum of the properties of its components; the interactions between them matter.”
The central idea behind Raoult’s and Henry’s laws
Part 1

Concentration Units and Conversions

Concentration
Molarity, molality, mole fraction and mass-based units

The choice of concentration unit depends on the measurement being made. Molarity M = nsolute/Vsolution (mol L⁻¹) is convenient for volumetric work but changes with temperature because the solution volume expands. Molality m = nsolute/masssolvent (mol kg⁻¹) is temperature-independent and is the natural unit for colligative properties. Mole fraction xi = ni/ntotal is dimensionless and convenient for vapour-pressure calculations.

Mass percent and parts per million are useful for very dilute or industrial solutions. Converting between molarity and molality requires the solution density: m = M/(ρ − M Msolute/1000), where ρ is in g cm⁻³ and M is molarity.

Part 2

Raoult’s Law, Henry’s Law and Ideal Solutions

Vapour Pressure
Ideal mixtures, deviations and azeotropes

For an ideal solution, the partial vapour pressure of each component is proportional to its mole fraction in the liquid:

pi = xi p°i

This is Raoult’s law. The total pressure is p = p₁ + p₂. An ideal solution forms when the intermolecular forces between unlike molecules are similar to those between like molecules, and the enthalpy of mixing is zero.

Real solutions deviate. Positive deviations occur when A–B interactions are weaker than A–A and B–B interactions; the molecules escape more easily, so the total pressure is higher than predicted and the mixture may form a minimum-boiling azeotrope (for example, ethanol–water). Negative deviations occur when A–B interactions are stronger, lowering the vapour pressure and potentially producing a maximum-boiling azeotrope (for example, nitric acid–water).

Henry’s law describes the solubility of a sparingly soluble gas: p = KHx. The Henry constant KH increases with temperature, so gases become less soluble in warm water. This is why dissolved oxygen levels fall in heated rivers and why carbonated drinks go flat when warm.

Part 3

Colligative Properties and the van’t Hoff Factor

Colligative Properties
Boiling-point elevation, freezing-point depression and osmotic pressure

Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles, not their chemical identity. For a non-volatile, non-electrolyte solute:

  • Relative lowering of vapour pressure: (p° − p)/p° = xsolute
  • Boiling-point elevation: ΔTb = Kb m
  • Freezing-point depression: ΔTf = Kf m
  • Osmotic pressure: π = MRT

Kb and Kf are ebullioscopic and cryoscopic constants characteristic of the solvent. For electrolytes, the number of particles is larger than the formal number of formula units, so each expression is multiplied by the van’t Hoff factor i:

ΔTb = iKbm,   ΔTf = iKfm,   π = iMRT

For complete dissociation i equals the number of ions per formula unit (2 for NaCl, 3 for CaCl₂); for weak electrolytes i lies between 1 and that limit and can be used to estimate the degree of dissociation. Association, such as dimerisation of carboxylic acids in benzene, gives i < 1. Osmotic pressure is especially useful for determining the molar mass of polymers and proteins because it is measurable at low concentrations.

Part 4

Solubility and Ksp

Solubility Equilibria
The solubility product, common ions and coupled equilibria

For a sparingly soluble salt MaXb dissolving to Ma+ and Xb−, the equilibrium constant is the solubility product:

Ksp = [Ma+]a[Xb−]b

If the salt dissociates completely and no other source of ions is present, solubility s is related to Ksp by Ksp = aabbsa+b. A common ion lowers solubility through Le Chatelier’s principle: adding NaCl to a saturated AgCl solution shifts the dissolution equilibrium to the left. pH can also matter when the anion is basic (carbonates, hydroxides, sulphides) or the cation hydrolyses. Complex formation can increase solubility by removing the free metal ion, as in the dissolution of AgCl in ammonia.

Precipitation begins when the reaction quotient Q exceeds Ksp. This criterion underlies qualitative analysis, water softening and the controlled precipitation of sparingly soluble salts.

Part 5: The Thermodynamic Reality of Non-Ideality and Activity

The standard curriculum often treats Raoult's Law as a rigid rule for ideal solutions and introduces deviations merely as qualitative trends (positive vs. negative). However, at an advanced level, one must understand that all real solutions are non-ideal. The concept of activity coefficients (γ) bridges the gap between ideal behavior and reality. Raoult's Law is strictly valid only as concentration approaches zero (the solvent limit). For solutes in dilute solutions, we invoke Henry's Law, but even here, deviations occur due to specific ion-pairing or steric effects.

Landmark Case: The Azeotrope Anomaly

Textbooks state that positive deviations lead to minimum-boiling azeotropes and negative to maximum. However, the mechanism is not just "weaker" or "stronger" forces; it is about the entropy of mixing versus enthalpy of interaction. In systems like Ethanol-Water, hydrogen bonding between unlike molecules (ethanol-water) is weaker than water-water but stronger than ethanol-ethanol? No, actually, the disruption of the extensive water-water H-bond network by ethanol creates a positive deviation because the average energy of interaction in the mixture is higher (less stable) than the pure components. Conversely, Chloroform-Acetone shows negative deviation because the dipole-induced dipole interactions between the carbonyl oxygen of acetone and the hydrogen of chloroform are exceptionally strong.

Advanced Comparison: Deviation Magnitude

Consider the magnitude of deviation. A mixture showing a small positive deviation might still be treated as ideal for rough calculations, but in precise thermodynamic cycles (like distillation column design), the Gibbs-Duhem equation dictates that if component A deviates positively, component B must also deviate positively to maintain consistency. This symmetry is often ignored in basic exams but is crucial for understanding why you cannot have a "purely positive" deviation where one component obeys Raoult's law perfectly while the other does not, unless at infinite dilution.

Mechanism of Deviation:

Positive deviations (Δ Hmix > 0): Endothermic mixing. Molecules prefer their own kind (e.g., Water-Water vs Water-Ethanol). They want to escape the liquid phase more easily, increasing vapor pressure above the ideal line.

Negative deviations (Δ Hmix < 0): Exothermic mixing. Molecules form specific complexes or strong interactions (e.g., Acetone-Chloroform dipole-dipole). They hold onto each other tighter, reducing vapor pressure below the ideal line.

Technique: Calculating Activity Coefficient from Vapor Pressure Data
A mixture of Benzene (p^° = 12.8 kPa) and Toluene (p^° = 3.8 kPa) at 30°C shows a total vapor pressure of 9.5 kPa when the mole fraction of Benzene in the liquid is x_B = 0.4. Assuming Toluene follows Raoult's Law perfectly, calculate the activity coefficient (γ_B) for Benzene and determine if the deviation is positive or negative.
Solution:
First, assume Toluene (T) is ideal: p_T = x_T p^°_T.
x_T = 1 - x_B = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6.
p_T = 0.6 × 3.8 kPa = 2.28 kPa.
Total Pressure Ptotal = p_B + p_T = 9.5 kPa.
So, partial pressure of Benzene p_B = 9.5 - 2.28 = 7.22 kPa.
Ideal partial pressure for Benzene would be: pB,ideal = x_B p^°_B = 0.4 × 12.8 = 5.12 kPa.
Activity coefficient γ_B = p_B / (x_B p^°_B) = 7.22 / 5.12 ≈ 1.41.
Since γ > 1, the solution exhibits a positive deviation. Benzene molecules are escaping more readily than predicted, likely due to weaker interactions with Toluene compared to pure Benzene.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Zero Point" Trap
Examiners often ask you to compare the vapor pressure of a mixture to the average of the pure vapor pressures. Remember: For an ideal solution, Ptotal lies exactly on the straight line connecting (1, p^°1) and (0, p^°2). If the curve bows above this line, it is positive deviation. If it bows below, it is negative. Never assume a mixture behaves ideally just because the components are organic; always check for specific interactions like H-bonding or dipole moments.

Part 6: The van't Hoff Factor in Complex Electrolytes and Ion Pairs

The standard formula Δ T = i K m assumes complete dissociation for strong electrolytes (i = z_+ or z_-). However, at higher concentrations, the Debye-Hückel theory explains that ions do not exist in isolation; they form an "ionic atmosphere." This leads to the concept of mean ionic activity. The observed van't Hoff factor (iobs) is always less than the theoretical maximum (itheo) due to two reasons: incomplete dissociation (for weak electrolytes) and ion-pairing (for strong electrolytes at high concentration).

Advanced Scenario: Ion-Pairing vs. Association

In dilute solutions, NaCl gives i ≈ 1.9. In concentrated solutions (m > 1), i drops to ≈ 1.6 not because of association, but because of ion-pairing where Na+ and Cl- stick together temporarily, acting like a single particle. This is distinct from dimerization (association) seen in carboxylic acids in benzene (i < 1).
Consider CaCl2. Theoretical i = 3. In reality, due to the higher charge density of Ca2+, it attracts anions more strongly, leading to significant ion-pairing even at moderate concentrations. Thus, for CaCl2, the drop in i with concentration is steeper than for NaCl.

Mechanism: The Bjerrum Length

The extent of association/ion-pairing depends on the product of charges (z_+ z_-). A 2:2 electrolyte like MgSO4 will show a much lower observed i at low concentrations compared to a 1:1 electrolyte like KCl, because the electrostatic attraction is stronger. This is a critical distinction often missed in standard problems.

Technique: Distinguishing Association from Ion-Pairing
Acetic acid dimerizes in benzene (i < 1). However, if you dissolve NaCl in a solvent with low dielectric constant (like ethylene glycol), it may not dissociate fully at all. How do you mathematically distinguish between an electrolyte that is weakly dissociated vs. one that is strongly associated? Use the limiting law behavior: As m → 0, i approaches the stoichiometric number for strong electrolytes or 1 for non-electrolytes. If i starts below the stoichiometric limit even at infinite dilution, it implies association (dimerization). If i starts at the theoretical limit and decreases as concentration increases, it implies ion-pairing.
Solution:
Plot i vs √(m) (or just observe the trend).
Case A (Association): Curve starts at i=1 (monomer) and goes down. Slope is negative relative to stoichiometric limit.
Case B (Ion-Pairing/Strong Electrolyte): Curve starts at theoretical max (e.g., 3 for CaCl2) and goes down.
Key Differentiator: The intercept at infinite dilution (m=0). If intercept < stoichiometric number → Association. If intercept = stoichiometric number → Ion-pairing/Dissociation issues.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Weak Electrolyte" Assumption
If a question gives you the degree of dissociation (α) for a weak electrolyte, do not use i = 1+α(n-1) blindly. Always check if the solvent is non-aqueous (e.g., benzene). In non-polar solvents, even "strong" acids like HCl exist as molecular species or ion pairs with very low i. Assume α ≈ 0 unless specified otherwise for organic solvents.

Part 7: Solubility Product Nuances and Common Ion Fallacies

The standard teaching is "Add common ion, solubility decreases." This is true by Le Chatelier's principle. However, advanced problems test the pH dependence and complex formation. The Ksp expression assumes no side reactions. If the anion is basic (e.g., CO32- in CaCO3), adding acid (H+) consumes the anion, effectively removing it from equilibrium, causing massive dissolution despite no "common ion" being added. Conversely, if the cation hydrolyzes (e.g., Fe3+), the pH affects solubility differently.
