Optics — Ray Optics, Wave Optics, Interference, Diffraction & Polarization

Optics is the branch of physics that studies light — its ray-like behavior in geometrical optics and its wave nature in physical optics. Ray optics treats light as rays that travel in straight lines, reflecting (θ_i = θ_r) and refracting (n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ — Snell's law) at interfaces. Lenses and mirrors form images governed by the thin-lens equation 1/f = 1/sₒ + 1/sᵢ and the mirror formula. Wave optics (physical optics) describes light as an electromagnetic wave. The Huygens-Fresnel principle: every point on a wavefront acts as a secondary source of spherical wavelets. Interference occurs when two coherent waves superpose — Young's double-slit experiment produces bright and dark fringes at d sin θ = mλ and d sin θ = (m+½)λ. Thin-film interference arises from reflections off front and back surfaces. Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles: single-slit diffraction gives minima at a sin θ = mλ. The Rayleigh criterion for resolution: θ_min = 1.22λ/D. Diffraction gratings disperse light into spectra. Polarization describes the direction of the electric field oscillation — linear, circular, and elliptical polarization. Malus's law: I = I₀ cos²θ. Birefringence in crystals splits light into ordinary and extraordinary rays.

"Light is a wave, and the wave theory of light is essentially the theory of the propagation of vibrations in the ether."
— Christiaan Huygens
Part 1

🔦 Ray Optics: Reflection, Refraction, and Image Formation

Geometrical Optics
The Law of Reflection, Snell's Law, Total Internal Reflection, Lenses, and Mirrors
Ray optics (geometrical optics) treats light as rays that travel in straight lines, valid when the wavelength is much smaller than the objects the light encounters. The law of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection: θ_i = θ_r, with the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lying in the same plane. Snell's law of refraction: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where n = c/v is the refractive index of the medium. The index of refraction varies with wavelength (dispersion) — the basis for prisms and rainbows. Total internal reflection occurs when n₁ > n₂ and θ₁ > θ_c, where θ_c = sin⁻¹(n₂/n₁) is the critical angle. Lenses: the thin-lens equation 1/f = 1/sₒ + 1/sᵢ relates object distance sₒ, image distance sᵢ, and focal length f. The lens maker's formula: 1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) for a thin lens in air. Magnification: m = −sᵢ/sₒ = hᵢ/hₒ. Converging lenses (f > 0) can form real or virtual images; diverging lenses (f < 0) form only virtual images. Spherical mirrors: the mirror equation 1/f = 1/sₒ + 1/sᵢ with f = R/2 (R is the radius of curvature). Concave mirrors can form real or virtual images; convex mirrors form only virtual, diminished images. Optical instruments: the human eye (accommodation, near point 25 cm, far point infinity), the compound microscope (M = Mₒ × Mₑ), the refracting telescope (angular magnification M = −fₒ/fₑ), and the reflecting telescope (Newtonian, Cassegrain). Aberrations: spherical aberration (paraxial approximation fails), chromatic aberration (dispersion in lenses), coma, astigmatism, and distortion — corrected by combining lenses and using aspherical surfaces.
Ray optics provides a powerful approximate description of light propagation through lenses, mirrors, and optical instruments using only geometry and the laws of reflection and refraction.
Part 2

🌊 Wave Optics: The Huygens-Fresnel Principle and Superposition

Wave Nature
The Huygens-Fresnel Principle, Optical Path Length, Phase Difference, and Coherence
The Huygens-Fresnel principle: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of spherical secondary wavelets; the envelope of these wavelets at a later time gives the new wavefront. This principle explains both rectilinear propagation and diffraction. The optical path length (OPL) is ∫ n ds; the phase difference between two waves Δφ = (2π/λ)Δ(OPL). Two sources are coherent if they have a constant phase relationship (same frequency, fixed phase difference). Lasers produce highly coherent light; thermal sources produce incoherent light. Temporal coherence (coherence length L_c = c/Δν) and spatial coherence (from an extended source, limited by the van Cittert-Zernike theorem). The superposition principle: the net displacement at a point is the vector sum of individual wave disturbances. For light waves (scalar approximation), the intensity I = |U|² where U is the complex amplitude. Interference requires coherent sources. Young's double-slit experiment (1801) was the first direct demonstration of the wave nature of light: two slits separated by distance d produce an interference pattern with bright fringes at d sin θ = mλ and dark fringes at d sin θ = (m + ½)λ. The fringe spacing on a screen at distance L is Δy = λL/d. The intensity distribution: I(θ) = I₀ cos²(πd sin θ/λ). Thin-film interference: when light reflects off the front and back surfaces of a thin film of thickness t, the phase difference includes both the path difference 2nt and any phase shifts upon reflection (π phase shift when n_film > n_medium). Constructive: 2nt = (m + ½)λ; destructive: 2nt = mλ (for normal incidence with one phase reversal). Newton's rings demonstrate thin-film interference with a curved lens on a flat surface.
The wave nature of light is revealed through interference phenomena; Young's double-slit experiment is the definitive demonstration, and thin-film interference explains the colors of soap bubbles and oil slicks.
Part 3

🔬 Diffraction: Single-Slit, Multi-Slit, and the Rayleigh Criterion

Diffraction
Single-Slit Diffraction, Circular Apertures, the Rayleigh Criterion, and Diffraction Gratings
Single-slit diffraction: light passing through a narrow slit of width a spreads out due to the Huygens-Fresnel principle. The intensity pattern I(θ) = I₀ [sin(β)/β]², where β = (πa/λ) sin θ. Minima occur at a sin θ = mλ (m = ±1, ±2, ...). The central maximum is twice as wide as the secondary maxima; most of the energy is in the central peak. Circular apertures (the Airy pattern): a circular aperture of diameter D produces a diffraction pattern with a central bright disk (the Airy disk) surrounded by rings. The first minimum is at sin θ = 1.22λ/D. The Rayleigh criterion for resolution: two point sources are just resolved when the peak of one Airy disk coincides with the first minimum of the other — θ_min = 1.22λ/D. This is the fundamental limit on resolution for telescopes and microscopes. Diffraction gratings: a series of N parallel slits of spacing d (the grating constant) produces sharp principal maxima at d sin θ = mλ (the grating equation). The angular width of each maximum is Δθ ≈ λ/(Nd cos θ). The resolving power of a grating: R = λ/Δλ = mN. Gratings are used in spectroscopy to measure wavelengths. Blazed gratings concentrate most light into a specific order. X-ray diffraction (Bragg's law): 2d sin θ = nλ, used to determine crystal structures. The Fraunhofer vs. Fresnel diffraction: Fraunhofer (far-field) occurs when the wavefronts are planar; Fresnel (near-field) involves curved wavefronts and is more general. The Talbot effect: self-imaging of a periodic grating at certain distances.
Diffraction sets fundamental limits on image resolution; the Rayleigh criterion defines the minimum angular separation resolvable by any optical system, and diffraction gratings disperse light for spectroscopic analysis.
Part 4

🌀 Polarization and Modern Optics

Polarization
Linear, Circular, and Elliptical Polarization, Malus's Law, Birefringence, and Optical Activity
Polarization describes the orientation of the electric field vector of an EM wave. In unpolarized light, the E-field vibrates randomly in all directions perpendicular to propagation. Linear polarization: the E-field oscillates along a fixed direction. Circular polarization: the E-field vector rotates at frequency ω, tracing a circle — right-circular (clockwise) and left-circular depending on the rotation direction. Elliptical polarization: the general case where E traces an ellipse. Malus's law: when linearly polarized light of intensity I₀ passes through a polarizer at angle θ to the polarization direction, the transmitted intensity is I = I₀ cos²θ. Polarization by reflection: at Brewster's angle θ_B = tan⁻¹(n₂/n₁), the reflected light is completely polarized perpendicular to the plane of incidence. Birefringence (double refraction): in anisotropic crystals (e.g., calcite), the refractive index depends on polarization and direction. The ordinary ray (o-ray) follows Snell's law; the extraordinary ray (e-ray) does not. Wave plates: a quarter-wave plate introduces a λ/4 phase shift (converts linear to circular polarization); a half-wave plate rotates the polarization direction. Optical activity: some materials (e.g., sugar solutions) rotate the plane of polarization — the rotation angle is α = [α] L c (specific rotation times path length times concentration). Polarization in nature: the blue sky is polarized (Rayleigh scattering); bees and some birds use polarization for navigation. Liquid crystal displays (LCDs): use twisted nematic liquid crystals to control polarization and modulate light. Nonlinear optics: in intense laser fields, the polarization response is nonlinear: P = ε₀(χ^{(1)}E + χ^{(2)}E² + χ^{(3)}E³ + ...). Second-harmonic generation (SHG) doubles the frequency; the Kerr effect produces an intensity-dependent refractive index. Quantum optics: the photon is the quantum of light; the photoelectric effect (Einstein, 1905) showed that light energy is quantized as E = hν. The Fourier optics framework treats imaging as a linear system in spatial frequencies — the optical transfer function (OTF) describes how the system transmits these frequencies.
Polarization reveals the vector nature of light; combined with nonlinear and quantum optics, it enables technologies from LCDs to lasers and quantum communication.
