Algebraic Geometry — Varieties, Schemes, and the Geometry of Polynomials

Algebraic geometry studies solutions of polynomial equations. It spans affine and projective varieties, schemes (Grothendieck), sheaves, cohomology (Čech, sheaf, étale), intersection theory, and moduli spaces. It's one of the most active and abstract fields of modern mathematics, with deep connections to number theory, topology, and physics.

"Algebraic geometry seems to have acquired the reputation of being esoteric, exclusive, and very abstract, but it is actually quite concrete and relevant to many fields."
— David Mumford
Part 1

🌾 Affine and Projective Varieties

Zero Sets
From Polynomial Equations to Geometric Objects
An affine variety V(I) ⊂ 𝔸ⁿ is the set of common zeros of an ideal I ⊂ k[x₁,...,xₙ]. The Zariski topology has closed sets = algebraic sets. Hilbert's Nullstellensatz: I(V(J)) = √J — the radical of J. This is the fundamental dictionary between ideals and varieties. A variety is irreducible if it's not a union of two proper closed subsets — equivalently, its coordinate ring k[V] = k[x]/I(V) is an integral domain. The dimension is the transcendence degree of the function field k(V). Projective varieties live in ℙⁿ — defined by homogeneous polynomials. They're proper (complete) — the algebraic analogue of compactness. Morphisms are polynomial maps between varieties. The blow-up resolves singularities by replacing a point with a ℙ^{n−1}. Hironaka's theorem: every variety over ℂ admits a resolution of singularities.
The Nullstellensatz is the Rosetta Stone — it translates algebra (ideals) into geometry (varieties) and back.
Part 2

🏗️ Schemes — Grothendieck's Revolution

Schemes
Affine Schemes, Structure Sheaves, and Fiber Products
A scheme is a locally ringed space locally isomorphic to Spec R (set of prime ideals of R). This unifies varieties (R = finitely generated k-algebra) and arithmetic geometry (R = ℤ, ℚ). The structure sheaf O_X assigns to each open set the ring of regular functions. A morphism of schemes is a continuous map + a sheaf map. The fiber product X ×_S Y is the categorical product over S — it's the basis for base change. Separated and proper morphisms are the algebraic analogs of Hausdorff and compact. A scheme is Noetherian if covered by finitely many Spec of Noetherian rings. Flat morphisms are the algebraic version of continuously varying families. The Hilbert scheme parameterizes subschemes of a given scheme with fixed Hilbert polynomial — it's the moduli space of subschemes.
Schemes allow polynomial equations to be studied over any ring — including ℤ — making algebraic geometry the natural language of arithmetic geometry.
Part 3

🧮 Sheaf Cohomology and Intersection Theory

Cohomology
Čech Cohomology, Serre Duality, and the Riemann-Roch Theorem
Sheaf cohomology Hⁱ(X,ℱ) measures the obstructions to gluing local sections. Čech cohomology computes it using open covers. The Euler characteristic χ(ℱ) = Σ (−1)ⁱ dim Hⁱ is finite for coherent sheaves on proper schemes. Serre duality: Hⁱ(X, ℱ) ≅ H^{n−i}(X, ℱ^* ⊗ ω_X)^* for smooth projective varieties, where ω_X is the canonical sheaf. The Riemann-Roch theorem for curves: dim H⁰(𝒪(D)) − dim H⁰(𝒪(K−D)) = deg D + 1 − g. The Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch theorem generalizes to higher dimensions using Chern classes: χ(ℱ) = ∫_X ch(ℱ) td(T_X). The Grothendieck-Riemann-Roch applies to proper morphisms. Intersection theory (Fulton-MacPherson) defines intersection products using Chow groups A_*(X) and the moving lemma. The Mumford-Castelnuovo regularity bounds the complexity of sheaves.
Sheaf cohomology and the Riemann-Roch theorem count the number of functions with prescribed zeros and poles — the basic numerical tool of algebraic geometry.
Part 4

🔬 Moduli Spaces and Modern Developments

Parameters
Moduli Spaces, GIT Quotients, and the Minimal Model Program
Moduli spaces parameterize geometric objects — curves, vector bundles, sheaves. The moduli space of curves M_g has dimension 3g−3. The Mumford-MacDonald formula computes its cohomology. Geometric Invariant Theory (GIT) constructs quotients of group actions — stable points correspond to good orbits. The Minimal Model Program (Birkar-Cascini-Hacon-McKernan) classifies algebraic varieties up to birational equivalence — every variety is birational to a minimal model (K_X nef) or a Mori fiber space. Calabi-Yau manifolds have trivial canonical bundle (ω_X = 𝒪_X) and are central to mirror symmetry. Stable curves (nodal curves with finite automorphism group) compactify M_g. The moduli of vector bundles uses slope stability (Mumford-Takemoto) and Gieseker stability. The Langlands program in geometric form connects moduli of bundles on curves to representations of the fundamental group.
Moduli spaces turn classification into geometry — the set of all curves or bundles becomes a space with its own rich structure.

Derived Categories and Fourier–Mukai Transforms

The derived category Db(Coh X) of bounded complexes of coherent sheaves on a smooth projective variety X provides a natural home for many dualities. Objects are complexes up to quasi‑isomorphism, and morphisms are roofs of quasi‑isomorphisms. The derived tensor product ⊗L and the derived Hom RHom give a symmetric monoidal structure compatible with Serre duality: RHom(E, F ⊗ ωX[dim X]) ≅ RHom(F, E)∨. A Fourier–Mukai transform is an exact functor Φ𝒦 : Db(Coh X) → Db(Coh Y) defined by an object 𝒦 ∈ Db(Coh (X × Y)) via Φ𝒦(−) = RπY∗(πX∗(−) ⊗L 𝒦). The kernel 𝒦 encodes the integral transform; when 𝒦 is the structure sheaf of the graph of an isomorphism, Φ𝒦 recovers pull‑back. Orlov’s representability theorem states that any exact functor between bounded derived categories of smooth projective varieties that commutes with shifts is a Fourier–Mukai transform. This yields powerful criteria for derived equivalence: two varieties are derived equivalent iff there exists a kernel inducing an equivalence. Derived equivalences preserve numerical invariants such as Hochschild homology and the Euler characteristic, but they do not preserve the canonical bundle in general. Applications include the proof of the derived McKay correspondence for quotient singularities and the classification of derived categories of K3 surfaces via Mukai lattices.

Hodge Theory and Period Maps

On a smooth projective complex variety X, the Hodge decomposition Hk(X, ℂ) = ⊕p+q=k Hp,q(X) equips each cohomology group with a Hodge structure of weight k. The Hodge filtration FpHk = ⊕i≥p Hi,k−i satisfies Griffiths transversality: ∇Fp ⊂ Fp−1 ⊗ Ω1. The period domain D parametrises all Hodge structures of a given type on a fixed lattice; it is a homogeneous space Gℝ/V where G is the real algebraic group preserving the polarisation. The period map sends a point of the moduli space of polarized varieties to its Hodge structure in D. Griffiths’ infinitesimal period relation shows that the differential of the period map lands in the horizontal subspace defined by transversality. Global Torelli theorems (e.g. for curves, K3 surfaces, and certain hypersurfaces) assert that the period map is injective on the appropriate moduli space, so the Hodge structure determines the variety up to isomorphism. The mixed Hodge theory of Deligne extends these ideas to singular or non‑compact varieties, providing a functorial Hodge structure on cohomology with compact support. Variations of Hodge structure underlie the construction of the intermediate Jacobian and the Abel–Jacobi map, linking algebraic cycles to transcendental invariants.

Worked Example: Period Map for Elliptic Curves
Let Eτ = ℂ/(ℤ + τℤ) with Im τ > 0. Compute the period matrix and describe the image of the period map from the upper half‑plane to the Siegel upper half‑space of genus 1.
The Hodge decomposition of H1(Eτ, ℂ) is one‑dimensional: H1,0 is spanned by the holomorphic 1‑form dz, and H0,1 by its conjugate. Choose a symplectic basis (α, β) of H1(Eτ, ℤ) with intersection α·β = 1. The periods are ∫α dz = 1 and ∫β dz = τ. The period matrix is the 1×2 matrix (1 τ). The Siegel upper half‑space of genus 1 is exactly the set of τ with Im τ > 0, so the period map τ ↦ (1 τ) identifies the moduli space of elliptic curves with the quotient of the upper half‑plane by SL2(ℤ). This is the classical uniformisation of elliptic curves.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the period matrix of a family, first write down a basis of Hp,0 and a symplectic basis of integral homology. Compute the integrals explicitly; the matrix entries are the periods. Remember that the period map is holomorphic and horizontal — use Griffiths transversality to check whether a given deformation preserves the Hodge filtration.

Arithmetic Geometry: Étale Cohomology and the Weil Conjectures

For a smooth projective variety X over a finite field 𝔽q, étale cohomology Hiét(X, ℚℓ) (ℓ ≠ p) provides ℓ‑adic vector spaces carrying a continuous action of the absolute Galois group Gal(𝔽̄q/𝔽q). The Frobenius endomorphism Frobq acts on these spaces, and its eigenvalues are algebraic numbers independent of ℓ. The Weil conjectures — rationality of the zeta function, a functional equation, the Riemann hypothesis for eigenvalues, and the identification of Betti numbers with those of a complex lift — were established by Dwork, Grothendieck, and Deligne, with Deligne proving the final Riemann hypothesis part. The proof uses the Lefschetz trace formula for the Frobenius correspondence on X × X, the hard Lefschetz theorem and the Hodge index theorem in the ℓ‑adic setting, and the theory of weights on ℓ‑adic sheaves. Étale cohomology also realises the Galois representations attached to modular forms via the Eichler–Shimura construction, and it underlies the proof of the Sato–Tate conjecture for elliptic curves over ℚ. The formalism of six operations (f∗, f!, f∗, f!, ⊗, Hom) on derived categories of ℓ‑adic sheaves provides the technical backbone for these results.

Birational Geometry: Minimal Models and the Cone of Curves

The Minimal Model Program (MMP) aims to classify projective varieties up to birational equivalence by performing a sequence of divisorial contractions and flips. For a ℚ‑factorial projective variety X with terminal singularities, a minimal model is a birational model X′ where the canonical divisor KX′ is nef (numerically effective). If KX is not nef, the Cone Theorem of Mori describes the cone of curves NE(X) ⊂ N1(X) as the sum of the nef cone and countably many extremal rays Ri = ℝ≥0[Ci] with KX·Ci < 0. Each extremal ray admits a contraction morphism contR : X → Y. If the contraction is divisorial, it contracts a divisor to a lower‑dimensional subvariety; if it is small, it contracts curves without divisors, and a flip replaces the contracted curves by new curves with opposite normal bundle. The existence of flips in all dimensions was settled by Birkar–Cascini–Hacon–McKernan (2010). Termination of flips for varieties of general type follows from the ACC for log canonical thresholds. The abundance conjecture predicts that for a minimal model KX is semi‑ample, so some multiple defines a morphism to the canonical model. In dimension three, the MMP is complete: every variety is birational to a minimal model or a Mori fibre space (a Fano fibration). The cone of curves also controls the birational geometry of Fano varieties: the Picard number ρ(X) equals the number of extremal rays of NE(X) when X is a Mori dream space, and the Cox ring provides a universal coordinate ring for all small ℚ‑factorial modifications.

  • Confusing derived equivalence with isomorphism — derived equivalent varieties need not be isomorphic.
  • Assuming the period map is always injective — global Torelli fails for many higher‑dimensional varieties.
  • Mixing ℓ‑adic and singular cohomology weights — the Weil conjectures require the correct weight filtration on étale cohomology.
  • Treating flips as isomorphisms in codimension one — a flip changes the variety in codimension two.
  • Believing nef implies semi‑ample — abundance is a conjecture, not a theorem in general.
  • Overlooking the role of terminal singularities — the MMP only runs on ℚ‑factorial terminal models.
  • Neglecting the horizontal condition in variations of Hodge structure — Griffiths transversality is essential for period maps.

These advanced topics form the core of modern algebraic geometry examinations and research.

Deformation Theory and Obstruction Theory

Deformation theory studies how algebraic varieties, schemes, or sheaves vary in families. The infinitesimal deformation functor DefX assigns to a local Artinian algebra A with residue field k the set of isomorphism classes of flat deformations of a scheme X over Spec A. The tangent space of this functor is naturally identified with Ext1(ΩX, 𝒪X) for a smooth variety, and with Ext1(𝕃X, 𝒪X) in the general case, where 𝕃X is the cotangent complex. Obstructions to extending a deformation over a small extension 0 → I → A′ → A → 0 lie in Ext2(𝕃X, 𝒪X ⊗ I). When the obstruction class vanishes, the set of extensions is a torsor under Ext1(𝕃X, 𝒪X ⊗ I).

For a smooth projective variety X, the Kodaira–Spencer map identifies the tangent space of the moduli functor with H1(X, 𝒯X) where 𝒯X is the tangent sheaf. The obstruction space is H2(X, 𝒯X). If H2(X, 𝒯X) = 0, the deformation functor is unobstructed and the local moduli space is smooth of dimension h1(𝒯X). This vanishing holds for some Fano varieties via Kodaira-type vanishing theorems. For Calabi–Yau manifolds, the Bogomolov–Tian–Todorov theorem guarantees unobstructedness even when H2(𝒯X) ≠ 0. In contrast, varieties of general type often have non‑trivial obstructions, leading to singularities in the moduli space.

The cotangent complex 𝕃X provides a derived enhancement that works for singular schemes and for morphisms. For a closed immersion i: Z X defined by an ideal I, the normal cone CZ/X = Spec ⊕ In/In+1 controls embedded deformations. The obstruction theory for the Hilbert scheme of points on a surface, for example, is governed by Ext2(𝕀Z, 𝕀Z) where 𝕀Z is the ideal sheaf. Virtual fundamental classes in Gromov–Witten theory are constructed by choosing a perfect obstruction theory E• → 𝕃X in the derived category, yielding a cycle class of the expected dimension even when the actual moduli space has excess dimension.

In practice, exam questions often ask to compute the tangent and obstruction spaces for concrete examples: the Hilbert scheme of n points on ℙ2, the moduli of stable maps, or the deformation of a singular curve. The key steps are: write down the relevant cotangent complex or tangent sheaf, compute its cohomology using standard exact sequences (Euler sequence, normal bundle sequence), and identify the obstruction map. Remember that for a local complete intersection morphism the cotangent complex is perfect of amplitude [−1,0], so the obstruction theory is two‑term.

Stacks and Moduli Stacks

Stacks generalize schemes by allowing objects with automorphisms. A stack 𝒳 over the category of schemes is a fibered category satisfying effective descent for the étale (or fppf) topology. The most important examples in algebraic geometry are the moduli stacks of curves ℳg, of stable maps ℳg,n(X,β), and of principal G‑bundles BunG. Unlike coarse moduli spaces, stacks retain the automorphism groups of the objects they parametrize, which is essential for intersection theory and for the definition of virtual fundamental classes.

Artin's criteria give necessary and sufficient conditions for a functor to be represented by an algebraic stack: the diagonal must be representable, separated, and quasi‑compact; there must exist a smooth surjective morphism from a scheme (an atlas); and the functor must satisfy openness of versality. Deligne–Mumford stacks are those with étale diagonal, equivalently those admitting an étale atlas. The stack ℳg of smooth curves of genus g ≥ 2 is a smooth Deligne–Mumford stack of dimension 3g−3. Its compactification ℳ̄g by stable curves is proper and carries a natural perfect obstruction theory, making it a cornerstone of Gromov–Witten theory.

The inertia stack I𝒳 = 𝒳 ×𝒳×𝒳 𝒳 encodes automorphisms; its connected components correspond to twisted sectors in orbifold cohomology. For a global quotient stack [X/G] with G finite, the inertia stack is the disjoint union over conjugacy classes [g] of the fixed loci Xg. The Chen–Ruan orbifold cohomology and the associated quantum cohomology are defined using the inertia stack. In the context of the minimal model program, the stack of log canonical models and the stack of stable pairs provide modular interpretations of the steps of the program.

When working with stacks, one must distinguish between the stack itself and its coarse moduli space. The coarse space M of a Deligne–Mumford stack 𝒳 is an algebraic space with a universal map 𝒳 → M that is initial among maps to algebraic spaces. The map is proper, quasi‑finite, and induces a bijection on geometric points, but it does not preserve intersection numbers: the pushforward of the fundamental class of 𝒳 equals the fundamental class of M multiplied by the generic stabilizer order. This factor appears in the Riemann–Roch formula for stacks (the Toën–Vezzosi Riemann–Roch) and in the definition of the virtual fundamental class via the Behrend–Fantechi construction.

Worked Example: Tangent Space of the Moduli Stack of Curves
Compute the tangent space at a point corresponding to a smooth projective curve C of genus g ≥ 2 in the moduli stack ℳ_g.
The tangent space of ℳ_g at [C] is the space of first‑order deformations of C. By Kodaira–Spencer theory this is H1(C, 𝒯_C). Using Serre duality, H1(C, 𝒯_C) ≅ H0(C, Ω_C⊗2)^∨, the dual of the space of quadratic differentials. The dimension is 3g−3, which matches the dimension of ℳ_g. No obstruction appears because H2(C, 𝒯_C) = 0 for a curve. Hence the stack is smooth of dimension 3g−3 at [C].
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the tangent or obstruction space of a moduli problem, first identify the controlling sheaf or complex (tangent sheaf, cotangent complex, normal sheaf). Write the relevant exact sequence (Euler, normal bundle, conormal) and compute cohomology using standard vanishing theorems. For stacks, remember that the tangent space is the same as for the coarse space, but the obstruction theory may differ by a factor of the automorphism group; always state whether you are working on the stack or its coarse space.

Mirror Symmetry and Homological Mirror Symmetry

Mirror symmetry originated in string theory as a duality between two Calabi–Yau threefolds X and X̂ whose Hodge numbers satisfy hp,q(X) = h3−p,q(X̂). The mathematical formulation by Kontsevich (1994) states an equivalence of triangulated categories: the derived category of coherent sheaves DbCoh(X) is equivalent to the derived Fukaya category DFuk(X̂) of the mirror. The Fukaya category has objects given by Lagrangian submanifolds with local systems, and morphisms given by Floer cohomology. This equivalence exchanges the complex structure of X with the symplectic (Kähler) structure of X̂.

On the A‑model side, genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants of X̂ compute the quantum cohomology ring, which is deformed by instanton corrections. On the B‑model side, variations of Hodge structure on X encode the same data via the period map. The mirror map identifies the complexified Kähler moduli space of X̂ with the complex structure moduli space of X. For the quintic threefold in ℙ4, the mirror family is constructed by a Greene–Plesser orbifold quotient, and the Yukawa coupling on the B‑model matches the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants on the A‑model after the mirror map.

Homological mirror symmetry has been proved for many toric Calabi–Yau varieties (Abouzaid, Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono), for elliptic curves (Polishchuk–Zaslow), and for certain K3 surfaces. The Strominger–Yau–Zaslow (SYZ) conjecture provides a geometric framework: a Calabi–Yau manifold admits a special Lagrangian torus fibration, and the mirror is obtained by dualizing the fibers. Proofs in the toric setting often exploit the toric fibration structure, while other cases use different methods. In the toric setting, the mirror is often a Landau–Ginzburg model (Ŷ, W) and the equivalence is between DbCoh(X) and the category of matrix factorizations MF(Ŷ,W).

Exam questions may ask to compute the mirror map for a simple example (e.g., the quintic), to explain how the central charge Z(E) = ∫_X e−B−iω ch(E) √td(X) corresponds to the period integral on the mirror, or to describe the role of the SYZ fibration in constructing the equivalence. Be prepared to write the definition of the Fukaya category at the level of objects and morphisms, and to state the precise form of Kontsevich's conjecture: an exact equivalence of A∞ categories DbCoh(X) ≃ DFuk(X̂) intertwining the Serre functor with the shift by the dimension.

Tropical Geometry and Degenerations

Tropical geometry replaces algebraic varieties over a valued field by piecewise‑linear polyhedral complexes. Given a variety X over the field of Puiseux series ℂ{{t}}, its tropicalization Trop(X) is the image of X under the valuation map val: (ℂ{{t}})^× → ℚ applied coordinate‑wise. The result is a rational polyhedral complex of pure dimension equal to dim X, balanced at each codimension‑one face. The balancing condition encodes the tropical analogue of the zero‑locus of a polynomial: the sum of primitive normal vectors weighted by multiplicities is zero.

Degenerations provide a geometric bridge: a flat family 𝒳 → Spec ℂ[[t]] with generic fiber X and special fiber a union of toric varieties yields a tropicalization that coincides with the dual intersection complex of the special fiber. The non‑archimedean analytic space Xan in the sense of Berkovich retracts onto a skeleton homeomorphic to Trop(X). This skeleton carries a canonical piecewise‑linear structure and a weight function given by the multiplicities of the components.

Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem states that the number of complex curves of genus g and degree d in ℙ2 passing through 3d+g−1 generic points equals the number of tropical curves of the same genus and degree passing through the tropicalizations of those points, counted with multiplicities given by the lattice index of the adjacent edges. This reduces enumerative geometry to a combinatorial count. Tropical methods also compute Gromov–Witten invariants of toric varieties via the tropical vertex and the scattering diagram of Gross–Siebert.

In examinations, you may be asked to tropicalize a given hypersurface (e.g., the curve defined by x + y + 1 + t·xy = 0), to draw its Newton polygon and the associated subdivision, to verify the balancing condition at a vertex, or to explain how the tropical limit of a family of abelian varieties yields a real torus with a positive definite quadratic form (the tropical abelian variety). Remember that tropicalization commutes with taking intersections only after a suitable refinement (the tropical Bezout theorem), and that the tropical variety of an ideal is the intersection of the tropical hypersurfaces of its generators only when the generators form a tropical basis.

p‑adic Hodge Theory and Crystalline Cohomology

p‑adic Hodge theory compares the various cohomology theories of a smooth proper variety X over a p‑adic field K (finite extension of ℚp). The main comparison isomorphisms, due to Fontaine and others, relate étale cohomology Hiét(XK̄, ℚp) with de Rham cohomology HidR(X/K) and crystalline cohomology Hicrys(Xk/W(k)) where k is the residue field. The period rings BdR, Bcrys, Bst provide the coefficients for these isomorphisms.

For a variety with good reduction, the crystalline comparison isomorphism Hiét(XK̄, ℚp) ⊗ Bcrys ≅ Hicrys(Xk/W(k)) ⊗ Bcrys is compatible with the Frobenius action and the filtration. The Hodge–Tate decomposition Hiét(XK̄, ℚp) ⊗ ℂp ≅ ⊕j Hi−j(X, Ωj) ⊗ ℂp(−j) follows from the de Rham comparison after tensoring with BdR. The filtered φ‑module Dcrys(V) = (V ⊗ Bcrys)GK attached to a p‑adic Galois representation V is weakly admissible if and only if V is crystalline (Fontaine's conjecture, proved by Colmez–Fontaine).

The theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules gives an equivalence between p‑adic Galois representations of Gℚp and étale φ‑modules over the Robba ring. This equivalence underlies the proof of the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚp) by Colmez and others. In the global setting, the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture predicts that an irreducible p‑adic Galois representation of Gℚ that is unramified outside finitely many primes and de Rham at p comes from geometry (i.e., from the étale cohomology of a smooth projective variety).

Exam questions often test the definitions of the period rings, the statement of the comparison isomorphisms, and the notion of weakly admissible filtered φ‑modules. You should be able to write the Hodge–Tate weights of the Tate module of an elliptic curve with good ordinary reduction (weights 0 and 1) and to explain why the crystalline condition is equivalent to the existence of a Frobenius‑stable lattice in the de Rham cohomology. Remember that the de Rham comparison requires the variety to be defined over a field with a chosen embedding into BdR, while the crystalline comparison works for varieties with good reduction over the ring of integers.

  • Confusing the tangent space of a stack with that of its coarse moduli space — the stack tangent space includes automorphisms, the coarse space does not.
  • Assuming every deformation problem is unobstructed — obstruction spaces are often non‑zero and must be computed via Ext2.
  • Treating the Fukaya category as an ordinary category — it is an A∞ category; higher products are essential for mirror symmetry.
  • Neglecting the balancing condition when tropicalizing — a polyhedral complex that is not balanced is not a tropical variety.
  • Mixing up the period rings BdR, Bcrys, Bst — each carries different structures (filtration, Frobenius, monodromy) and gives different comparison isomorphisms.
  • Believing the mirror map is always an isomorphism of moduli spaces — it is an isomorphism only after analytic continuation and may have singularities at large complex structure limits.
  • Overlooking the role of the inertia stack in orbifold cohomology — twisted sectors contribute to the Chen–Ruan product and must be included in any computation.

Motivic Integration and the Grothendieck Ring of Varieties

Motivic integration, introduced by Kontsevich (1995) and developed by Denef and Loeser, assigns a measure to subsets of the arc space of a variety over a field k taking values in the Grothendieck ring K₀(Varₖ). The ring is generated by isomorphism classes [X] of k-varieties with the relation [X] = [Y] + [X Y] for a closed subvariety Y ⊂ X and product [X]·[Y] = [X × Y]. The class of the affine line 𝕃 = [𝔸¹] acts as a Lefschetz motive; inverting 𝕃 yields the localized ring K₀(Varₖ)[𝕃⁻¹] where the motivic measure of a cylinder set C = πₙ⁻¹(Z) (with πₙ the truncation map from the arc space to the n-jet scheme) is μ(C) = [Z]·𝕃-(n+1)dim X. This gives a finitely additive measure on the Boolean algebra of constructible subsets of the arc space. The motivic zeta function of a hypersurface f = 0 is Zₘₒₜ(f, T) = Σₙ≥1 μ({ord f = n}) Tⁿ; Denef–Loeser proved its rationality in K₀(Varₖ)[𝕃⁻¹][[T]]. Applications include the Batyrev–Kontsevich theorem that birational Calabi–Yau varieties have equal Hodge numbers, via equality of their classes in the completed Grothendieck ring. The theory also provides a motivic McKay correspondence and a framework for motivic Donaldson–Thomas invariants. Examinations may ask you to compute the class of a projective bundle ℙ(E) (which equals [X]·(1+𝕃+…+𝕃ʳ) for a rank r+1 vector bundle E on X), verify the change‑of‑variables formula for a proper birational morphism, or explain why the motivic measure of arcs with a given order of vanishing along a divisor is a polynomial in 𝕃. Convergence of infinite sums requires completion of K₀(Varₖ) with respect to the dimension filtration; the motivic integral of a function F on the arc space is defined as the limit of integrals of simple functions approximating F.

Logarithmic Geometry and Degenerations

Logarithmic geometry, systematized by Kato in the 1980s, equips a scheme X with a sheaf of monoids Mₓ and a morphism α: Mₓ → Oₓ that is an isomorphism on the complement of a divisor. A log structure encodes a divisor with normal crossings together with local equations; a morphism of log schemes is log smooth if it is locally modeled on Spec k[P] → Spec k[Q] induced by a homomorphism of fine saturated monoids Q → P that is integral and satisfies the lifting property for faces. The log de Rham complex Ω*X/k(log D) computes the log crystalline cohomology of a log smooth morphism and satisfies a Poincaré lemma. In the semistable reduction theorem, a proper variety over a discrete valuation ring acquires a log smooth model after a finite base change; the special fiber becomes a reduced simple normal crossings divisor with the canonical log structure. The Kato–Nakayama space associates to a log analytic space a topological space capturing the monodromy of the degeneration; its cohomology carries a mixed Hodge structure compatible with the weight filtration given by the log pole order. The Hyodo–Kato isomorphism identifies the log crystalline cohomology of the special fiber with the de Rham cohomology of the generic fiber after tensoring with the fraction field of the Witt vectors, and the monodromy operator N arises from the log structure. Exam questions often require writing the log structure on Spec k[x,y]/(xy) (the standard node), verifying that Spec k[t] → Spec k[x,y]/(xy) given by x ↦ t, y ↦ t is log smooth after equipping the target with the divisor xy = 0 and the source with t = 0, or describing the weight filtration on the limit mixed Hodge structure of a semistable family of curves. Distinguish the log structure from the underlying divisor: the same divisor can carry many non‑isomorphic log structures, and log smoothness depends on the chosen log structure, not merely on the divisor.

Worked Example: Log Smoothness of a Nodal Family
Let R = k[[t]] and X = Spec R[x,y]/(xy - t). Equip X with the log structure defined by the divisor xy = 0 on the special fiber and the divisor t = 0 on the base S = Spec R. Show that the projection X → S is log smooth.
The special fiber is X₀ = Spec k[x,y]/(xy), a nodal curve. The log structure on X is given by the monoid M = ℕ² ⊕ ℕ mapping to Oₓ via (a,b,c) ↦ xᵃ yᵇ tᶜ with the relation xy = t implying (1,0,0)+(0,1,0) = (0,0,1). The base log structure is ℕ → R, 1 ↦ t. Locally on X the morphism is modeled on the map of monoids ℕ → ℕ² ⊕ ℕ / ⟨(1,1,-1)⟩ sending 1 ↦ (0,0,1). This map is integral and satisfies the lifting property for faces because the target monoid is fine, saturated, and the map is injective on the quotient by the units. Hence the morphism is log smooth by Kato's criterion.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for log smoothness, first write the monoids explicitly, then check integrality and the face‑lifting condition. Do not confuse log smoothness with ordinary smoothness; a morphism can be log smooth while the underlying scheme map is not smooth (e.g., the nodal family above). Memorize the standard log structures on normal‑crossing divisors and on the base Spec k[[t]].

Algebraic Cycles and the Bloch–Beilinson Conjectures

For a smooth projective variety X over a field k, the Chow group CHⁱ(X) of codimension i cycles modulo rational equivalence carries a conjectural filtration F⁰ ⊃ F¹ ⊃ … ⊃ Fⁱ (the Bloch–Beilinson filtration) such that Gr_F⁰ CHⁱ(X) ⊗ ℚ ≅ H2i(X, ℚ(i)) (the cycle class map) and the higher graded pieces are expected to be extensions of mixed motives. Beilinson formulated precise conjectures predicting that the filtration is induced by a motivic cohomology theory and that the associated graded pieces are isomorphic to the higher Chow groups CHⁱ(X, j) (Bloch's higher Chow groups) after tensoring with ℚ. The conjectures imply the finite‑dimensionality of the Chow groups modulo homological equivalence (the Kimura–O'Sullivan conjecture) and the existence of a motivic t-structure on the derived category of motives. The regulator maps from higher Chow groups to Deligne cohomology (or absolute Hodge cohomology) provide a bridge to transcendental invariants; for example, the regulator on CH²(X, 1) for a surface X lands in H³_D(X, ℤ(2)) and detects indecomposable cycles. The Bloch–Kato conjecture (now a theorem of Voevodsky–Rost) identifies the graded pieces of the Milnor K‑theory filtration with Galois cohomology, a special case of the Bloch–Beilinson picture for X = Spec k. Examinations may ask you to define the coniveau filtration on cohomology and relate it to the Bloch–Beilinson filtration, compute the regulator of a symbol {f, g} in CH²(ℙ¹ × ℙ¹, 1), or explain why the vanishing of CH⁰(X)ₕₒₘ for a curve implies the injectivity of the Abel–Jacobi map. The filtration is only known unconditionally for curves, surfaces with p_g = 0, and certain complete intersections; for general varieties it remains a deep open problem.

Higher Chow Groups and Motivic Cohomology

Bloch's higher Chow groups CHⁱ(X, n) are defined as the homology of the complex zⁱ(X, •) of algebraic cycles on X × Δⁿ meeting the faces properly, where Δⁿ = Spec k[t₀,…,tₙ]/(Σ tⱼ - 1) is the algebraic n-simplex. They satisfy CHⁱ(X, 0) = CHⁱ(X) and admit a product CHⁱ(X, n) ⊗ CHʲ(Y, m) → CHⁱ⁺ʲ(X × Y, n+m) making them a bigraded ring. Voevodsky constructed the triangulated category of geometric motives DMₖ and proved that CHⁱ(X, n) ≅ HomDMₖ(M(X), ℤ(i)[2i-n]), identifying higher Chow groups with motivic cohomology groups H2i-n_M(X, ℤ(i)). The Bloch–Lichtenbaum spectral sequence E₂p,q = Hp-q_M(X, ℤ(-q)) ⇒ K-p-q(X) converges to the algebraic K‑theory of X; its degeneration at E₂ for smooth varieties over a field of characteristic zero is equivalent to the Beilinson–Soulé vanishing conjecture H^j_M(X, ℤ(i)) = 0 for j ≤ 0. The norm residue isomorphism theorem (Voevodsky–Rost) identifies the mod‑ℓ motivic cohomology with Galois cohomology, proving the Bloch–Kato conjecture. For a smooth projective curve C, CH¹(C, 1) ≅ k(C)ˣ and the regulator to Deligne cohomology recovers the classical Abel–Jacobi map. Examinations may ask you to compute CH¹(𝔸¹, 1) (which is k[t]ˣ = kˣ), describe the face maps in the cubical complex defining higher Chow groups, or explain how the motivic cohomology of a point H^j_M(Spec k, ℤ(i)) recovers Milnor K‑theory K^M_i(k) for j = 2i and vanishes otherwise. Note the difference between the cubical and simplicial definitions; they give isomorphic groups but the face maps differ by signs that matter in explicit calculations.

Geometric Langlands Program and Categorical Mirror Symmetry

The geometric Langlands correspondence, formulated by Beilinson and Drinfeld for a smooth projective curve X over an algebraically closed field, predicts an equivalence between the derived category of D-modules on the moduli stack Bun_G of G-bundles on X (for a reductive group G) and the derived category of quasi‑coherent sheaves on the moduli stack LocSys^L G of ^L G-local systems (where ^L G is the Langlands dual group). Hecke eigensheaves on Bun_G correspond to skyscraper sheaves on LocSys^L G. The correspondence is a categorification of the classical Langlands correspondence for function fields. Kapustin and Witten showed that the geometric Langlands equivalence arises as a mirror symmetry between two topological twists of N = 4 super Yang–Mills theory in four dimensions compactified on X × Σ; the A‑model on the Hitchin moduli space M_H(G) (the moduli of Higgs bundles) is mirror to the B‑model on M_H(^L G). The Hitchin fibration h: M_H(G) → A provides a completely integrable system; its generic fibers are abelian varieties (Prym varieties) and the mirror symmetry exchanges the fiber with its dual abelian variety, implementing a non‑abelian Fourier–Mukai transform. The categorical formulation replaces the derived category of coherent sheaves by the Fukaya category of the A‑model and the category of D-modules by the category of B‑branes; the equivalence is then an A_∞-equivalence. Recent work of Arinkin–Gaitsgory and others proves the correspondence for G = GL_n using the theory of ind‑coherent sheaves and the Whittaker normalization. Examinations may ask you to describe the Hecke operators as correspondences on Bun_G, write down the Hitchin base A = ⊕i=1^r H⁰(X, K_Xd_i) for a group of rank r with exponents d_i, or explain why the geometric Langlands correspondence for GL1 reduces to the classical Fourier–Mukai transform on the Jacobian of X. The categorical equivalence is not an equivalence of ordinary triangulated categories but of DG-categories; higher homotopies are essential and cannot be ignored.

  • Confusing the Grothendieck ring of varieties with the ring of motives — the former is a universal Euler characteristic, the latter requires a motivic t-structure not known to exist in general.
  • Assuming every log structure comes from a divisor — log structures can be non‑divisorial (e.g., the log point Spec k[ℕ] → Spec k) and log smoothness depends on the monoid map, not only on the underlying divisor.
  • Treating the Bloch–Beilinson filtration as a theorem — it is a conjecture; only the first step (cycle class map) is known unconditionally.
  • Mixing up higher Chow groups with ordinary Chow groups — CHⁱ(X, n) for n > 0 captures higher algebraic K‑theory and motivic cohomology, not just cycles modulo rational equivalence.
  • Believing the geometric Langlands equivalence holds for all reductive groups in the same form — the categorical statement requires ind‑coherent sheaves and a Whittaker model; the naive derived category equivalence fails for non‑simply‑connected groups.
  • Neglecting the A_∞ structure in mirror symmetry — the Fukaya category is an A_∞ category; an equivalence of triangulated categories loses the higher products that encode quantum corrections.
  • Overlooking the role of the dimension filtration in motivic integration — convergence of motivic integrals requires completion with respect to this filtration; working in the uncompleted ring leads to divergent series.

Derived Algebraic Geometry and Spectral Schemes

Foundations in ∞‑Categories

Derived algebraic geometry replaces the ordinary category of commutative rings with the ∞‑category of connective 𝔼∞‑ring spectra (or simplicial commutative rings). A derived scheme is a functor from connective 𝔼∞‑rings to spaces satisfying étale descent and admitting a cover by affine derived schemes Spec A with A a connective 𝔼∞‑ring. The classical scheme Spec π₀A embeds as the truncation. The cotangent complex LA/k becomes a perfect A‑module controlling derived deformation theory; its Tor‑amplitude measures the derived nature of the intersection.

Derived Intersection Theory

Given closed immersions i₁: Z₁ → X and i₂: Z₂ → X of classical schemes, their derived fibre product Z₁ ×^h_X Z₂ carries a natural derived scheme structure with structure sheaf OZ₁ ⊗^LO_X OZ₂. The virtual fundamental class of this derived intersection is the fundamental class of the derived scheme itself. This intrinsic construction underlies the Behrend–Fantechi virtual class and allows Gromov–Witten invariants to be defined without manually choosing a perfect obstruction theory.

Applications to Deformation Theory

The derived moduli stack of perfect complexes on a smooth projective variety X is locally represented by the derived spectrum of RHom(E,E). Its tangent complex at E is RHom(E,E)[1] and obstructions lie in RHom(E,E)[2]. The derived enhancement automatically encodes higher obstructions, so the Kuranishi map becomes a morphism of derived Artin stacks. This perspective informs the construction of the derived category of coherent sheaves on derived stacks and enters modern proofs of the derived Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces.

Moduli of Sheaves and Bridgeland Stability Conditions

Gieseker and Slope Stability

For a polarized projective variety (X, H) a coherent sheaf E is Gieseker‑semistable if for every proper subsheaf F ⊂ E the reduced Hilbert polynomial satisfies p_F(m) ≤ p_E(m) for m ≫ 0. Slope stability uses μ_H(E) = c₁(E)·Hdim X‑1/rank(E). The moduli space M_H(v) of S‑equivalence classes of semistable sheaves with fixed Chern character v exists as a projective scheme by the work of Maruyama, Gieseker, and Simpson.

Bridgeland Stability on Derived Categories

A Bridgeland stability condition on D^b(Coh X) consists of a heart 𝒜 of a bounded t‑structure and a group homomorphism Z: K₀(𝒜) → ℂ (the central charge) such that every object E ∈ 𝒜 has a Harder–Narasimhan filtration with respect to the phase φ(E) = (1/π) arg Z(E). The space Stab(X) of stability conditions carries a natural complex manifold structure; its connected components are acted upon by the autoequivalence group of D^b(Coh X). For K3 surfaces, Bridgeland showed that a connected component of Stab(X) covers the period domain, linking wall‑crossing to the geometry of the movable cone.

Wall‑Crossing and Donaldson–Thomas Invariants

As the stability condition varies, the set of stable objects changes only when crossing real codimension‑one walls in Stab(X). The Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula expresses the change of the Donaldson–Thomas invariant DT(γ) — defined via the Behrend‑function weighted Euler characteristic — in terms of the BPS invariants Ω(γ). For local Calabi–Yau threefolds, the DT/PT correspondence equates the generating series of stable pairs (Pandharipande–Thomas invariants) with the DT series, and wall‑crossing yields a recursive algorithm for their computation.

Worked Example: Computing a Wall in Stab(ℙ²)
Let X = ℙ² with hyperplane class H. Consider the heart 𝒜 = Coh(ℙ²) and central charge Z(E) = −∫_X e−iπH ch(E) = −(ch₀(E) − iπ ch₁(E)·H + …). For objects with Chern character v = (r, c₁, ch₂) = (2, H, 0) find the numerical wall where a subobject F with v(F) = (1, 0, −1/2) destabilizes E.
The phase of an object is φ = (1/π) arg Z. Compute Z(E) = −(2 − iπ·1) = −2 + iπ. Its argument is π − arctan(π/2). For F, Z(F) = −(1 − iπ·0) = −1, argument π. The wall occurs when φ(F) = φ(E), i.e. when Im Z(F)/Re Z(F) = Im Z(E)/Re Z(E). Since Re Z(F) = −1, Im Z(F) = 0, the equality forces Im Z(E) = 0, which never happens for this central charge. Hence no wall exists in this slice; the stability condition is generic for these numerical classes.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for a wall‑crossing computation, first write the central charge in terms of the numerical Grothendieck group, then equate phases of the candidate subobject and quotient. Remember that walls are defined by linear equations in the space of central charges; solving them reduces to a determinant condition on the charge vectors.

Virtual Fundamental Classes and Gromov–Witten Invariants

Perfect Obstruction Theories

A perfect obstruction theory on a Deligne–Mumford stack M is a morphism φ: E• → L_M in D(M) where E• is a perfect complex of amplitude [−1,0] and φ induces an isomorphism on h⁰ and a surjection on h⁻¹. The virtual fundamental class [M]vir ∈ Avdim(M) is constructed by intersecting the intrinsic normal cone C_M with the zero section of the vector bundle stack h¹/h⁰(E•^∨). This class depends on the chosen obstruction theory; different theories yield different virtual classes.

Gromov–Witten Theory

For a smooth projective variety X, the moduli stack Mg,n(X, β) of stable maps of genus g with n marked points and curve class β carries a natural perfect obstruction theory given by Rπ_* f^* T_X. The virtual dimension is ∫_β c₁(T_X) + (dim X − 3)(1 − g) + n. Gromov–Witten invariants are integrals of pullbacks of cohomology classes against [Mg,n(X, β)]vir. The genus‑zero invariants define the quantum product on H^*(X), making it a Frobenius manifold.

Mirror Symmetry for the Quintic Threefold

The quintic threefold Q ⊂ ℙ⁴ has mirror family Ǧψ defined by x₀⁵+…+x₄⁵−5ψ x₀x₁x₂x₃x₄ = 0. The genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants N_d are extracted from the Yukawa coupling of the mirror; they relate to the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants n_d by N_d = ∑k|d nd/k/k³. The Candelas–de la Ossa–Green–Parkes computation predicts n₁ = 2875, n₂ = 609250, n₃ = 317206375, matching the enumerative geometry of rational curves on Q. Higher‑genus invariants are governed by the holomorphic anomaly equations of Bershadsky–Cecotti–Ooguri–Vafa.

Non‑Archimedean Geometry and Berkovich Analytic Spaces

Berkovich Spectra

Let K be a complete non‑archimedean field with valuation |·|. The Berkovich spectrum M(A) of a strictly K‑affinoid algebra A is the set of multiplicative seminorms on A extending the valuation on K, equipped with the weakest topology making the evaluation maps f ↦ |f(x)| continuous. Unlike the rigid analytic maximal spectrum, M(A) is locally compact, Hausdorff, and path‑connected. The structure sheaf OM(A) makes M(A) a locally ringed space; the category of K‑affinoid spaces embeds fully faithfully into Berkovich spaces.

Comparison with Rigid Geometry

Huber’s adic spaces generalize both rigid analytic spaces and formal schemes. Every Berkovich space admits a canonical map to the associated adic space which is a homeomorphism on underlying topological spaces and an isomorphism on structure sheaves after sheafification. This equivalence allows one to transfer results such as the proper mapping theorem and the coherence theorem from rigid geometry to the Berkovich setting, while retaining better topological properties (local contractibility, existence of a skeleton).

Applications to p‑adic Hodge Theory

The pro‑étale site of a smooth rigid space over a p‑adic field admits a sheaf BdR+ whose global sections recover the de Rham period ring. Scholze’s perfectoid spaces provide a tilting equivalence between characteristic‑0 and characteristic‑p perfectoid algebras, enabling the construction of the p‑adic Simpson correspondence. The Berkovich skeleton of a degenerating family of Calabi–Yau varieties encodes the monodromy weight filtration, giving a geometric realization of the weight‑monodromy conjecture in the non‑archimedean setting.

Arakelov Geometry and Arithmetic Intersection Theory

Arithmetic Chow Groups

For a regular arithmetic variety X → Spec O_K (O_K the ring of integers of a number field), the arithmetic Chow group widehat{CH}^p(X) is generated by pairs (Z, g_Z) where Z is a codimension‑p cycle on the generic fibre X_K and g_Z is a Green current on the complex manifold X(ℂ) satisfying dd^c g_Z + δ_Z = ω_Z for a smooth form ω_Z. The arithmetic intersection product widehat{CH}^p(X) × widehat{CH}^q(X) → widehat{CH}p+q(X) is defined by the *-product of Green currents and extends the classical intersection product on the generic fibre.

Arithmetic Riemann–Roch Theorem

Gillet–Soulé proved an arithmetic Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem: for a proper flat morphism f: X → Y of arithmetic varieties and a hermitian vector bundle E on X, the arithmetic Chern character satisfies widehat{ch}(f_* E) = f_*(widehat{ch}(E)·widehat{Td}(T_f)) in widehat{CH}(Y)_ℚ. The analytic torsion form appears as the degree‑zero component of widehat{Td}(T_f). This theorem underlies the computation of heights of cycles via the arithmetic intersection pairing.

Faltings’ Proof of the Mordell Conjecture

Faltings used Arakelov geometry to define the stable Faltings height h_F(A) of an abelian variety A over a number field. He proved the finiteness of the set of isomorphism classes of abelian varieties with given dimension, good reduction outside a fixed finite set of places, and bounded Faltings height. The key inequality is the arithmetic Noether formula: 12 h_F(A) = widehat{deg} widehat{c}1(ωA/O_K) + ∑v|∞ log ‖Δ_v‖, where Δ_v are the discriminant modular forms. This height bound, combined with the Tate conjecture for abelian varieties over finite fields (proved by Zarhin), yields the finiteness of rational points on curves of genus ≥ 2.

  • Assuming every Bridgeland stability condition comes from a geometric heart — many stability conditions have hearts not equivalent to Coh(X) for any variety X.
  • Treating the virtual fundamental class as an ordinary fundamental class — it lives in the Chow group of the expected dimension but depends on the choice of perfect obstruction theory.
  • Neglecting the role of the skeleton in Berkovich spaces — the skeleton captures the essential homotopy type and is crucial for weight‑monodromy arguments.
  • Mixing arithmetic intersection numbers with classical intersection numbers — the Green current contribution is essential and cannot be omitted even when the cycles meet properly on the generic fibre.
  • Believing the arithmetic Riemann–Roch theorem holds without the analytic torsion term — the torsion form is the degree‑zero part of the Todd class and is mandatory for equality.

The advanced topics above provide the technical backbone for modern research problems in algebraic geometry and are frequently examined through precise computational or conceptual questions.

Equivariant Cohomology and the Atiyah–Bott Localization Formula

Equivariant cohomology extends ordinary cohomology to spaces with a group action. For a topological space X with a continuous action of a compact Lie group G, the equivariant cohomology ring HG*(X) is the ordinary cohomology of the Borel construction XG = EG ×G X, where EG → BG is a universal principal G-bundle. When G is a torus T ≅ (S1)r, the coefficient ring HT*(pt) identifies with the symmetric algebra Sym(t∨) on the dual Lie algebra, a polynomial ring in r variables of degree two.

The Atiyah–Bott localization theorem (1984) and the independent Berline–Vergne formula (1982) state that for a compact T-manifold X with isolated fixed points, the pushforward of an equivariant class α ∈ HT*(X) to a point is computed from fixed‑point data:

∫X α = Σp ∈ XT α|p / eT(NpX),

where eT(NpX) is the equivariant Euler class of the normal bundle at p, a product of the weights of the T-action on TpX. This reduces integrals over X to rational functions in the equivariant parameters, evaluated by taking the non‑equivariant limit after cancelling poles.

Applications include characteristic numbers of flag varieties, verification of the Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem for toric varieties, and derivation of the Weyl character formula via Borel–Weil–Bott. In enumerative geometry, localization expresses Gromov–Witten invariants of toric targets as sums over fixed loci in the moduli space of stable maps, a technique pioneered by Graber and Pandharipande (1999). The general formula replaces the Euler class by the equivariant top Chern class of the virtual normal bundle for non‑isolated fixed components.

Quantum Cohomology and the Dubrovin Connection

Quantum cohomology deforms the classical cup product on H*(X, ℚ) by genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants. For a smooth projective variety X, the small quantum cohomology ring QH*(X) has the same underlying vector space as H*(X) but a product ⋆ defined by

γ1 ⋆ γ2 = Σβ ∈ H2(X, ℤ) ⟨γ1, γ2, γ∨⟩0,3,β qβ,

where ⟨⋯⟩0,3,β denotes the three‑point genus‑zero invariant and qβ are formal Novikov variables. Associativity of ⋆ is equivalent to the WDVV equations, a system of quadratic relations among the invariants.

The Dubrovin connection is a flat meromorphic connection on the trivial bundle H*(X) × Spec ℚ[[q]] → Spec ℚ[[q]] given by

∇z∂z = z∂z + (c1(X) ⋆) / z + (E ⋆),

where z is the formal parameter, c1(X) the first Chern class, and E the Euler vector field encoding the grading. Flatness encodes the WDVV equations and the divisor axiom. For X = ℙn, the quantum product is H ⋆ H = H2 + q (with H the hyperplane class), and the connection reduces to z2∂q² s = q s, whose solutions are Bessel functions. Mirror symmetry predicts that the Dubrovin connection of a Calabi–Yau threefold coincides with the Gauss–Manin connection on the mirror family; this was verified for the quintic threefold by Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes (1991) and later established rigorously by Givental (1996) and Lian–Liu–Yau (1997).

Worked computation: quantum product in QH*(ℙ2)
Let H be the hyperplane class in H2(ℙ2, ℚ). Using the divisor axiom and the fact that the only non‑zero genus‑zero three‑point invariants with degree d>0 are ⟨H, H, H⟩0,3,d = 1 for d = 1, compute H ⋆ H in the small quantum cohomology ring.
The classical product gives H ∪ H = H2 (the point class). For degree d = 1, the invariant ⟨H, H, H⟩0,3,1 = 1 contributes q·pt. Higher degrees vanish because the virtual dimension of the moduli space exceeds the number of insertions. Hence H ⋆ H = H2 + q·pt. In the usual presentation QH*(ℙ2) ≅ ℚ[H, q]/(H3 = q), this matches the relation H3 = q.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for a quantum product, first write the classical cup product, then list all effective curve classes that can contribute. Use the divisor axiom to pull out powers of the hyperplane class; only the smallest degree with the correct virtual dimension survives. Remember that the Novikov variable q records the degree, not the symplectic area.

Derived Categories of Matrix Factorizations and Landau–Ginzburg Models

Given a regular function W : X → 𝔸1 on a smooth variety X (the Landau–Ginzburg potential), a matrix factorization of W is a ℤ/2‑graded free 𝒪X-module E = E0 ⊕ E1 with an odd endomorphism d such that d² = W·idE. The homotopy category of matrix factorizations, denoted MF(X, W), is triangulated. Orlov (2004) proved an equivalence between the derived category of singularities Dsg(W-1(0)) and MF(X, W) when W has isolated critical points.

Knörrer periodicity states that for a non‑degenerate quadratic form q on a vector space V, there is an equivalence MF(X × V, W + q) ≅ MF(X, W), reducing high‑dimensional matrix factorizations to lower‑dimensional ones. In homological mirror symmetry, the Fukaya–Seidel category of a Landau–Ginzburg model (Y, W) is conjecturally equivalent to MF(X, W) for the mirror X. For the quintic threefold, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model on (ℂ×)3 with potential W = x + y + z + 1/(xyz); the equivalence was established by Seidel (2003) for the A‑model and by Sheridan (2015) for the B‑model using graded matrix factorizations.

Graded matrix factorizations incorporate a ℤ‑grading compatible with a ℂ×-action scaling W. The category MFgr(X, W) carries a natural Calabi–Yau structure of dimension dim X - 1. This structure underlies the categorification of Donaldson–Thomas invariants for local Calabi–Yau threefolds via Toda (2010) and the construction of stability conditions by Bridgeland (2012).

Logarithmic Gromov–Witten Theory and Tropical Correspondence

Logarithmic geometry, developed by Kato (1989) and Illusie, equips schemes with a sheaf of monoids MX mapping to the structure sheaf, encoding degenerations and boundary divisors. A log smooth morphism f : X → Y generalizes smooth morphisms and normal‑crossings divisors. Abramovich, Chen, Gross, and Siebert (2010) defined the moduli space of log stable maps 𝔐g,n(X, β) for a log smooth target X. Its virtual fundamental class yields logarithmic Gromov–Witten invariants Ng,n,βlog.

The tropicalization of a log stable map produces a balanced weighted graph in the tropicalization of X. The Gross–Siebert correspondence theorem (2010) asserts that for a toric variety X with a maximal boundary divisor, the logarithmic Gromov–Witten invariants equal the counts of tropical curves of the same degree and genus, weighted by multiplicities given by the lattice index of the edge directions. This reduces enumerative geometry to combinatorial counting of tropical curves, computable via floor diagrams (Brugallé–Mikhalkin 2008) or the tropical vertex group (Gross–Pandharipande–Siebert 2010).

For X = ℙ2 with the toric boundary, genus‑zero logarithmic invariants coincide with classical Gromov–Witten invariants because every stable map is automatically log smooth. For higher genus, the logarithmic invariants differ by contributions from maps with components mapping into the boundary; these correspond to tropical curves with higher‑valence vertices. The correspondence has been extended to logarithmic maps to exploded manifolds (Parker 2013) and to relative Gromov–Witten theory via Li (2002) and Jun Li (2001).

Perfectoid Spaces and the Tilting Equivalence in p‑adic Hodge Theory

A perfectoid field K is a complete non‑archimedean field of mixed characteristic (0, p) whose Frobenius map on 𝒪K/p is surjective and which contains a pseudo‑uniformizer ϖ with ϖp | p. Scholze (2012) introduced perfectoid spaces as adic spaces locally modeled on Spa(K, 𝒪K). The tilt K♭ of a perfectoid field K is the fraction field of the inverse limit lim←, x↦xp 𝒪K/p; it is a perfectoid field of characteristic p. The tilting equivalence is a canonical isomorphism of categories between perfectoid K-algebras and perfectoid K♭-algebras, preserving finite étale extensions.

Fontaine's period rings BdR, Bcris, and Bst acquire natural perfectoid covers. Scholze constructed the pro‑étale site Xproét of a smooth rigid space X and a sheaf 𝔹dR+ whose global sections recover BdR+. The tilting equivalence identifies the pro‑étale cohomology of X with that of its tilt X♭, yielding a p‑adic analogue of the comparison isomorphism between étale and de Rham cohomology. This underlies the p‑adic Simpson correspondence (Liu–Zhu 2017) and the proof of the weight‑monodromy conjecture for varieties with semistable reduction over a p‑adic field (Nizioł 2016).

In the local Langlands correspondence, perfectoid Shimura varieties at infinite level (Scholze 2015) provide a geometric realization for GLn. The cohomology of the perfectoid tower carries an action of GLn(ℚp) × Wℚp and realizes the correspondence via the derived Hecke action. The tilting equivalence transports this picture to characteristic p, where the geometry of the Fargues–Fontaine curve (Fargues–Fontaine 2018) gives a classification of vector bundles and a geometric Langlands correspondence for GLn over ℚp.

  • Assuming the quantum product is commutative without checking the grading — the product is graded‑commutative, but the Novikov variables introduce a formal deformation that can obscure sign rules.
  • Treating matrix factorizations as ordinary complexes — the differential squares to the potential, not to zero, so the homotopy category is ℤ/2‑graded, not ℤ‑graded.
  • Neglecting the log structure when applying tropical correspondence — the correspondence holds for log smooth targets; dropping the log structure changes the virtual class.
  • Believing the tilting equivalence preserves all p‑adic Hodge invariants — it preserves Galois representations and étale cohomology, but de Rham and crystalline periods require the period sheaves on the pro‑étale site.
  • Using classical Gromov–Witten invariants in place of logarithmic invariants for degenerate targets — the two theories differ by boundary contributions captured by tropical curves with higher‑valence vertices.
  • Overlooking the role of the Euler vector field in the Dubrovin connection — it encodes the conformal dimension and is essential for flatness.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves and the Bondal–Orlov Reconstruction Theorem

The bounded derived category of coherent sheaves Db(Coh X) on a smooth projective variety X encodes the entire birational geometry of X. Bondal and Orlov (2001) proved that if X and Y are smooth projective varieties with equivalent derived categories as triangulated categories, then X and Y are either isomorphic or related by a sequence of flops; in particular, their canonical rings are isomorphic. The proof uses the existence of a Fourier–Mukai kernel 𝒦 ∈ Db(Coh (X × Y)) inducing the equivalence, and the fact that the Hochschild homology of the category recovers the Hodge numbers of X. A key technical tool is the notion of a spanning class of objects: a set of objects such that vanishing of Hom from all of them forces an object to be zero. For a smooth projective variety, the set of skyscraper sheaves of closed points forms a spanning class. The reconstruction theorem fails for singular varieties; counterexamples are provided by derived-equivalent non‑birational singular threefolds (Kawamata 2002). In practice, the theorem reduces many birational classification problems to the study of autoequivalences of the derived category, such as spherical twists (Seidel–Thomas 2001) and autoequivalences coming from birational maps.

Fourier–Mukai Transforms and Kernels

An exact functor Φ𝒦 : Db(Coh X) → Db(Coh Y) of the form Φ𝒦(−) = RπY∗(πX∗(−) ⊗L 𝒦) is called a Fourier–Mukai transform. Orlov’s representability theorem states that any exact functor between derived categories of smooth projective varieties admitting left and right adjoints is of this form. The kernel 𝒦 is unique up to isomorphism. The composition of two Fourier–Mukai transforms corresponds to the convolution of kernels, which is computed by a derived tensor product on the triple product X × Y × Z. This formalism underlies the proof of the reconstruction theorem and the classification of autoequivalences for K3 surfaces (Huybrechts–Stellari 2005).

Applications to Birational Geometry

If X and Y are related by a flop, the derived equivalence is induced by the universal sheaf on the fiber product X ×Z Y, where Z is the common base of the flop. The equivalence preserves the numerical Grothendieck group and the Euler pairing, hence it preserves the Mukai lattice on K3 surfaces. For Calabi–Yau threefolds, derived equivalences are expected to correspond to different phases of the same string theory, a prediction of homological mirror symmetry. The Bondal–Orlov theorem also implies that the canonical ring R(X, KX) = ⊕m≥0 H0(X, mKX) is a derived invariant, so the Kodaira dimension is a derived invariant for smooth projective varieties.

Noncommutative Algebraic Geometry and the Artin–Zhang Theory

Noncommutative algebraic geometry replaces the commutative coordinate ring of a variety by a graded (or filtered) noncommutative algebra A. The category of quasi‑coherent sheaves on the “noncommutative space” is taken to be the quotient category QGr A = Gr A / Tors A, where Gr A is the category of graded right A‑modules and Tors A is the Serre subcategory of torsion modules. Artin and Zhang (1994) developed a general framework: a noncommutative projective scheme is the pair (Projnc A, 𝒪) where Projnc A is the set of graded prime ideals of A not containing the irrelevant ideal A+, equipped with a structure sheaf defined via localization. When A is the homogeneous coordinate ring of a projective variety X, QGr A is equivalent to Coh X, recovering classical geometry.

Quantum Projective Spaces and Sklyanin Algebras

The most studied examples are the quantum projective spaces ℙqn defined by the quadratic algebra A = k⟨x0,…,xn⟩ / (xixj − q xjxi) for a parameter q ∈ k×. For generic q, QGr A behaves like the category of coherent sheaves on ℙn: it has a Serre functor, a dualizing complex, and the same Ext‑groups as the commutative case. The Sklyanin algebra S(a,b,c) is a four‑dimensional Artin–Schelter regular algebra that deforms the homogeneous coordinate ring of an elliptic curve. Its point scheme is an elliptic curve E, and the category QGr S is equivalent to the category of coherent sheaves on a noncommutative deformation of E. The Artin–Zhang theory provides a notion of ampleness for objects in QGr A, allowing one to define a noncommutative Proj and to prove a noncommutative Serre vanishing theorem.

Derived Categories and Noncommutative Resolutions

Van den Bergh introduced noncommutative crepant resolutions (NCCRs) as endomorphism algebras EndR(M) of a reflexive module M over a Gorenstein normal domain R such that the algebra has finite global dimension and is a Cohen–Macaulay R‑module. The derived category of modules over an NCCR is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on a crepant resolution of Spec R, when such a resolution exists. This construction works for quotient singularities ℂn/G, where the McKay correspondence gives an NCCR as the skew group algebra ℂ[x1,…,xn] ⋊ G. NCCRs provide a noncommutative substitute for resolutions in dimensions where crepant resolutions do not exist, and they play a central role in the derived category approach to the minimal model program.

Worked Example: Derived Equivalence of a Flop
Let X → Z ← Y be a standard flop of smooth threefolds, where Z has a single ordinary double point and the exceptional loci are ℙ1 × ℙ1. Show that the Fourier–Mukai kernel 𝒦 = 𝒪X×ZY induces an equivalence Db(Coh X) ≃ Db(Coh Y).
The fiber product W = X ×Z Y is a smooth threefold with two projections p : W → X and q : W → Y. The structure sheaf 𝒪W is a perfect complex on W, hence defines a Fourier–Mukai functor Φ(–) = Rq∗(p∗(–) ⊗L 𝒪W) = Rq∗p∗(–). By the base‑change theorem for the Cartesian square, Φ is isomorphic to the pull‑push along the flop. The inverse functor is given by the kernel 𝒪W viewed on Y ×Z X. The composition of the two functors is the identity because the convolution kernel is 𝒪ΔX, the structure sheaf of the diagonal in X × X. The same holds for the opposite composition. Therefore Φ is an exact equivalence of triangulated categories. The equivalence preserves the canonical bundle because the flop is crepant, and it matches the numerical Grothendieck groups via the natural isomorphism K0(X) ≃ K0(Y).
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for a derived equivalence between birational varieties, first identify whether the map is a flop, a blow‑up, or a more general birational transformation. For flops, the kernel is the structure sheaf of the fiber product; for blow‑ups, the kernel involves the exceptional divisor and the relative canonical bundle. Remember that Orlov’s representability theorem guarantees any exact functor with adjoints is Fourier–Mukai, so you can always look for a kernel. In multiple‑choice settings, check whether the proposed kernel is perfect and whether the projections are proper and flat.

Higher Algebraic Stacks and Derived Stacks

Classical algebraic stacks (Artin stacks) generalize schemes by allowing objects with automorphisms, such as the moduli stack of vector bundles or the quotient stack [X/G]. Higher algebraic stacks extend this by replacing the category of groupoids with ∞‑groupoids, i.e. spaces up to coherent homotopy. A derived stack is a functor from the ∞‑category of derived affine schemes (simplicial commutative rings or connective E∞‑rings) to ∞‑groupoids satisfying descent for the étale topology. The ∞‑category of derived stacks contains the ∞‑category of derived schemes as a full subcategory, and it is closed under homotopy limits and colimits. This framework, developed by Toën–Vezzosi (2005) and Lurie (2004), provides a natural home for derived moduli problems such as the moduli of perfect complexes, the moduli of local systems, and the derived mapping stack Map(X, Y).

Derived Moduli of Perfect Complexes

For a proper scheme X, the derived stack PerfX classifying perfect complexes on X is a derived Artin stack locally of finite presentation. Its truncation t0PerfX is the classical Artin stack of perfect complexes, while the higher homotopy groups at a point E ∈ PerfX are given by Ext−i(E, E) for i ≥ 1. The tangent complex at E is RHom(E, E)[1], and the obstruction theory is controlled by the Atiyah class. The derived structure remembers the higher Ext groups that are lost in the classical truncation, and it is essential for defining a virtual fundamental class when the moduli problem is not unobstructed. The derived stack PerfX carries a natural (−1)‑shifted symplectic structure (Pantev–Toën–Vaquié–Vezzosi 2013), which underlies the construction of shifted symplectic forms on derived moduli of sheaves and on derived critical loci.

Derived Mapping Stacks and the Derived Loop Space

Given derived stacks X and Y, the derived mapping stack Map(X, Y) represents the functor T ↦ Hom(T × X, Y). When X = S1 (the derived circle, i.e. the simplicial set Δ1/∂Δ1), Map(S1, X) is the derived loop space LX. Its truncation is the classical inertia stack, but the derived structure encodes the Hochschild homology of X. For a smooth scheme X, LX is equivalent to the total space of the shifted tangent bundle TX[−1], and its functions are the Hochschild homology complex HH∗(X). The derived loop space carries a natural S1‑action (rotation of loops), and the homotopy fixed points give the negative cyclic homology. These structures are central in the geometric formulation of the local Langlands correspondence and in the study of topological field theories.

Motivic Homotopy Theory and A¹‑Homotopy

Motivic homotopy theory, introduced by Morel and Voevodsky (1999), constructs a homotopy category of schemes over a base field k by inverting the A¹‑homotopy equivalence: the projection X × A¹ → X becomes a weak equivalence. The resulting ∞‑category SH(k) is the stable motivic homotopy category; its objects are motivic spectra, and its hom‑groups are the motivic cohomology groups Hp,q(X, ℤ). The theory satisfies the six functor formalism (f∗, f!, f∗, f!, ⊗, Hom) and a projective bundle formula. The motivic cohomology of a point is the Milnor K‑theory of the field: Hp,q(Spec k, ℤ) ≅ KMq(k) for p = q, and zero otherwise. The Beilinson–Soulé vanishing conjecture (proved by Voevodsky) states that Hp,q(X, ℤ) = 0 for p < 0.

A¹‑Connectedness and the A¹‑Fundamental Group

A scheme X is A¹‑connected if the map X → Spec k induces an isomorphism on π0A¹. The A¹‑fundamental group π1A¹(X) is a sheaf of groups on the Nisnevich site, constructed by Morel. For a smooth proper curve C of genus g ≥ 1, π1A¹(C) is a non‑abelian sheaf that encodes the algebraic fundamental group together with the Jacobian. The A¹‑homotopy sheaves πiA¹(X) are strictly A¹‑invariant sheaves, and they form the building blocks of the motivic stable homotopy category via the motivic Postnikov tower. The computation of π1A¹(G) for reductive groups G is a major open problem; for G = SL2 it is known to be the sheaf associated to the presheaf U ↦ SL2(U)/E2(U), where E2 is the subgroup generated by elementary matrices.

Applications to the Milnor and Bloch–Kato Conjectures

Voevodsky’s proof of the Milnor conjecture (1996) and the Bloch–Kato conjecture (2003) uses the identification of motivic cohomology with Milnor K‑theory modulo 2 and with Galois cohomology, respectively. The key input is the construction of the motivic Steenrod algebra and the computation of the motivic cohomology of the classifying space BGL. The motivic spectral sequence relating motivic cohomology to algebraic K‑theory (the motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence) converges to the higher K‑groups of the field. These results show that motivic homotopy theory provides a geometric framework for arithmetic invariants that were previously accessible only through Galois cohomology.

Hodge Modules and Saito’s Theory

Mixed Hodge modules, developed by Saito (1990), unify the theory of variations of Hodge structure, perverse sheaves, and D‑modules. A mixed Hodge module on a complex algebraic variety X is a triple (M, F, W) where M is a regular holonomic D‑module on X, F is the Hodge filtration, and W is the weight filtration, satisfying a list of axioms that generalize the properties of the intersection cohomology complex of a smooth projective variety. The category MHM(X) is an abelian category, stable under the six operations of D‑modules (pushforward, pullback, tensor product, duality). The forgetful functor to perverse sheaves (with ℚ‑coefficients) is exact and faithful, and the associated graded of the weight filtration yields pure Hodge modules of definite weight.

Decomposition Theorem and Hodge Modules

The decomposition theorem of Beilinson–Bernstein–Deligne–Gabber (1982) for the pushforward of the intersection complex under a proper map f : X → Y is refined in Saito’s theory: the pushforward of a pure Hodge module of weight w decomposes as a direct sum of pure Hodge modules of weight w + i shifted by −i. This decomposition is compatible with the weight filtration and the Hodge filtration, and it implies the hard Lefschetz theorem for the intersection cohomology of projective varieties. The theory also provides a Hodge‑theoretic proof of the Kazhdan–Lusztig conjectures for the equivariant intersection cohomology of Schubert varieties, because the category of equivariant mixed Hodge modules on the flag variety is equivalent to the category of Soergel bimodules.

Applications to Singularities and Vanishing Cycles

For a holomorphic function f : X → ℂ, the nearby and vanishing cycle functors ψf, φf lift to mixed Hodge modules. The monodromy action on the vanishing cycles is quasi‑unipotent, and the weight filtration on φfℚX[dim X] coincides with the monodromy weight filtration. This yields a mixed Hodge structure on the Milnor fiber cohomology, and the Hodge numbers can be computed from the spectrum of the singularity (Steenbrink 1977). The theory of mixed Hodge modules also gives a functorial construction of the limit mixed Hodge structure for a degeneration of Hodge structures, generalizing Schmid’s nilpotent orbit theorem.

Cotangent Complex and Derived Deformation Theory

The cotangent complex LX/Y of a morphism of schemes f : X → Y is the universal derived object controlling infinitesimal deformations and obstructions. Introduced by Illusie (1971), it lives in the derived category D−(OX) and reduces to the classical sheaf of Kähler differentials ΩX/Y when f is a local complete intersection. For a closed immersion defined by an ideal I, the truncation τ≥−1LX/Y recovers the conormal exact sequence I/I² → ΩY|X → ΩX → 0. The derived functoriality of L yields a distinguished triangle LY/Z|X → LX/Z → LX/Y → for composable morphisms, the derived analogue of the transitivity sequence for differentials.

In deformation theory, a first‑order deformation of a scheme X over a square‑zero extension 0 → M → A' → A → 0 is classified by Ext¹(LX/A, M). Obstructions to lifting across a small extension lie in Ext²(LX/A, M). When X is a local complete intersection, these groups coincide with the classical Ext groups of the cotangent sheaf; for singular or non‑lci morphisms the higher homotopy of L captures the full obstruction theory. The derived Artin approximation theorem (Artin 1974; refined by Olsson 2006) shows that formal deformations of algebraic stacks are algebraizable provided the cotangent complex is perfect of amplitude in [−1,0] — exactly the condition defining a derived Deligne–Mumford stack.

The cotangent complex also governs the derived self‑intersection of a regular embedding. If i : Z X is a regular immersion of codimension c, the derived self‑intersection Z ×LX Z has structure sheaf SymOZ(LZ/X[1]), whose homology sheaves are the exterior powers ∧iNZ/X. This identification underlies the virtual fundamental class construction of Behrend–Fantechi: a perfect obstruction theory on a Deligne–Mumford stack 𝔛 is a morphism φ : E → L𝔛 in the derived category satisfying cohomological conditions; the virtual class lives in the Chow group of the expected dimension rk(E).

Work of Bhatt–Lurie (2019) on the derived de Rham complex and the Hodge filtration on L links the cotangent complex to prismatic cohomology. For a smooth proper variety over a perfect field of characteristic p, the derived de Rham complex computes crystalline cohomology, and the conjugate filtration on L recovers the Hodge–Tate spectral sequence. These connections illustrate how the cotangent complex bridges classical deformation theory, derived algebraic geometry, and p‑adic Hodge theory.

Motivic Donaldson–Thomas Invariants and Wall‑Crossing Formulas

Motivic Donaldson–Thomas (DT) invariants refine the numerical DT invariants of Calabi–Yau threefolds by taking values in the Grothendieck ring of varieties K₀(Vark) or its localization K₀(Vark)[𝕃⁻¹]. Introduced by Kontsevich and Soibelman (2008) and independently by Joyce (2007), they are defined using the motivic Hall algebra of the category of coherent sheaves (or complexes) on a threefold X. For a fixed Chern character γ, the motivic DT invariant DTγ is the class of the moduli stack of semistable objects of class γ, weighted by the Behrend function and integrated against the motivic measure.

The wall‑crossing formula describes how DTγ changes when the stability condition varies across a wall in the space of Bridgeland stability conditions. In the motivic setting, the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing identity reads ∏γ∈Γ+ exp(∑k≥1 (1/k²) DTkγ xkγ) = ∏γ∈Γ− exp(∑k≥1 (1/k²) DT'kγ xkγ), where the ordered product runs over rays in the central charge plane. This identity holds in the completed motivic quantum torus and implies the classical numerical wall‑crossing formula after applying the Euler characteristic specialization.

For quivers with potential, motivic DT invariants can be computed explicitly using the motivic exponential and the plethystic logarithm. Efimov (2012) and Davison–Meinhardt (2015) show that the motivic DT series of the conifold quiver satisfies the quantum dilogarithm identity, providing a motivic lift of the topological vertex. For the resolved conifold, the motivic DT partition function factorizes into a product of quantum dilogarithms indexed by the BPS spectrum, confirming the physical prediction of wall‑crossing for BPS states.

The integration map from the motivic Hall algebra to the quantum torus is constructed via the motivic vanishing cycle functor φTr(W) of the trace of the potential W. The critical cohomology of the moduli stack carries a mixed Hodge structure, and its Hodge–Deligne polynomial yields the motivic DT invariant after a suitable change of variables. This construction makes the wall‑crossing formula a theorem in the category of mixed Hodge modules, as proved by Davison (2017) using cohomological Hall algebras.

Applications include the computation of refined BPS invariants for local Calabi–Yau geometries, the proof of the integrality conjecture for motivic DT invariants (Davison–Meinhardt 2020), and the derivation of Gopakumar–Vafa invariants from the motivic DT series via the M‑theory limit. The motivic framework also provides a natural setting for the categorification of DT invariants via the cohomological Hall algebra of Kontsevich–Soibelman.

Key references: Kontsevich–Soibelman, Stability Structures, Motivic Donaldson–Thomas Invariants and Cluster Transformations (2008); Joyce, Configurations in Abelian Categories (2007); Davison–Meinhardt, Motivic DT Invariants for the Conifold (2015); Davison, The Integrality Conjecture for Motivic DT Invariants (2020).

When a question asks for a wall‑crossing computation, first identify the stability chamber and the relevant central charge rays. Write the ordered product of quantum dilogarithms for the initial and final chambers; the equality of the two products is the wall‑crossing identity. Geometry of the Moduli Space of Higgs Bundles

The moduli space MHiggs(C, G) of semistable Higgs bundles on a smooth projective curve C with structure group a complex reductive group G is a hyperkähler variety of complex dimension 2(g−1)dim G + 2. A Higgs bundle is a pair (E, φ) where E is a principal G‑bundle and φ ∈ H⁰(C, ad(E) ⊗ KC) is the Higgs field. Stability is defined by the slope condition μ(F) < μ(E) for every proper φ‑invariant reduction F of E. The Hitchin map h : MHiggs → A = ⊕i=1r H⁰(C, KCdi) sends (E, φ) to the characteristic polynomial coefficients of φ, where di are the degrees of the basic invariant polynomials on the Lie algebra 𝔤.

The generic fiber of the Hitchin map is an abelian variety — the Prym variety of the spectral curve Σa ⊂ Tot(KC) defined by det(φ − λ) = 0. This makes h an algebraically completely integrable system (the Hitchin system). The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence (Corlette 1988; Donaldson 1987; Simpson 1992) identifies MHiggs with the character variety MB(C, G) = Hom(π₁(C), G)//G and with the de Rham moduli space MdR of flat connections. The diffeomorphism is not holomorphic; it intertwines the complex structures I, J, K of the hyperkähler metric, sending the Hitchin fibration on MHiggs to the projection to the space of opers on MdR.

The topology of MHiggs is encoded in its mixed Hodge polynomial, computed by Hausel–Rodriguez‑Villegas (2008) using the decomposition theorem for the Hitchin map and the support theorem for the perverse Leray filtration. The resulting formula expresses the Hodge numbers in terms of the topology of the nilpotent cone and the cohomology of the stack of Higgs bundles. For G = GLn, the Poincaré polynomial matches the Macdonald polynomial evaluation at q = t, confirming the P = W conjecture (proved by Maulik–Shen 2022) that the perverse filtration on H*(MHiggs) coincides with the weight filtration on H*(MB) under the non‑abelian Hodge isomorphism.

Boundary components of the natural compactification of MHiggs (the moduli of semistable Higgs bundles with possibly singular spectral curves) are described by Biquard–García‑Prada (2004) and Fredrickson (2018). They correspond to parabolic Higgs bundles on nodal curves obtained by degenerating the spectral curve. The virtual fundamental class of the compactified moduli space, constructed using the perfect obstruction theory of the Hitchin map, yields invariants satisfying wall‑crossing formulas analogous to those for DT invariants.

Recent developments include the categorical Hitchin system (Arinkin–Gaitsgory 2015), where the category of D‑modules on BunG is related to coherent sheaves on MHiggs via the geometric Langlands correspondence, and the computation of the cohomology of the moduli stack of Higgs bundles for non‑simply‑connected groups using endoscopic groups (Ngô 2010).

Arithmetic Intersection Theory on Shimura Varieties

Shimura varieties ShK(G, X) are higher‑dimensional analogues of modular curves, parametrizing abelian varieties with additional structure (level K, polarization, endomorphisms). They are defined over number fields (the reflex field) and admit canonical integral models over the ring of integers at primes of good reduction. Arithmetic intersection theory on these varieties, pioneered by Kudla (1997) and developed by Rapoport, Yang, Zhang, and others, studies the heights of special cycles — algebraic cycles arising from embeddings of smaller Shimura varieties — and relates their intersection numbers to derivatives of automorphic L‑functions.

A special cycle Z(T) of codimension r is defined by a positive‑definite symmetric matrix T ∈ Symr(ℚ) and a collection of special endomorphisms. Its arithmetic degree Ẑ(T) = (Z(T), ω) pairs the cycle with a metrized line bundle ω (the Hodge bundle) using the arithmetic Chow group CĤr(ShK). The Kudla program predicts that the generating series ∑T Ẑ(T) qT is a Siegel modular form of weight dim Sh + 1 valued in the arithmetic Chow group. This has been proved in several cases: for unitary Shimura varieties of signature (n−1,1) by Kudla–Rapoport–Yang (2006), for orthogonal Shimura varieties by Howard (2015), and for Hilbert modular surfaces by Bruinier–Kudla–Yang (2012).

The proof strategy uses the arithmetic Siegel–Weil formula: the arithmetic theta lift of the constant function 1 equals the Eisenstein series whose Fourier coefficients are the arithmetic intersection numbers. The local contributions at finite primes are computed via the theory of local densities of quadratic forms (Kitaoka 1993) and the geometry of the Bruhat–Tits building for the associated p‑adic group. At the archimedean place, the Green current for the special cycle is expressed in terms of the Kudla–Millson theta form, whose cohomology class represents the Poincaré dual of the cycle.

Applications include the Gross–Zagier formula for higher‑dimensional Shimura varieties (Yuan–Zhang–Zhang 2013), which expresses the Néron–Tate height of Heegner cycles on the Jacobian of a Shimura curve as the derivative of a Rankin–Selberg L‑function. The arithmetic inner product formula (Ichino–Ikeda 2010, generalized by Xue 2019) relates the arithmetic intersection of two special cycles to the central derivative of an L‑function for the tensor product of the associated automorphic representations. These results provide evidence for the Bloch–Beilinson conjectures on the relation between algebraic cycles and values of L‑functions.

Recent work of Li–Zhang (2022) extends the arithmetic Siegel–Weil formula to non‑compact Shimura varieties using toroidal compactifications and the arithmetic Hodge index theorem. The resulting modularity of the generating series of arithmetic intersections on the boundary strata opens the way to a full proof of the Kudla program for all Shimura varieties of Hodge type.

Key references: Kudla, Central Derivatives of Eisenstein Series and Height Pairings (1997); Kudla–Rapoport–Yang, Modular Forms and Special Cycles on Shimura Curves (2006); Howard, Complex Multiplication Cycles on Shimura Varieties (2015); Yuan–Zhang–Zhang, The Gross–Zagier Formula on Shimura Curves (2013); Li–Zhang, Arithmetic Siegel–Weil for Non‑Compact Shimura Varieties (2022).

Higher Direct Images and Vanishing Theorems in Positive Characteristic

Let f : X → Y be a proper morphism of smooth varieties over an algebraically closed field k of characteristic p > 0. The higher direct images Rif*ωX/Y of the relative dualizing sheaf play a central role in the classification of algebraic varieties. In characteristic zero, the Grauert–Riemenschneider vanishing theorem states that Rif*ωX = 0 for i > 0 when f is a resolution of singularities. Kollár (1986) generalized this to arbitrary proper morphisms: if f is projective and L is an f‑ample line bundle, then Rif*(ωX/Y ⊗ L) = 0 for i > 0. In positive characteristic these statements fail in general; counterexamples are due to Raynaud (1978) and Mukai (2013).

The modern approach replaces the dualizing sheaf with the derived pushforward of the structure sheaf and uses the theory of Frobenius splittings. A variety X is Frobenius split if the Frobenius map F : OX → F*OX splits as a map of OX‑modules. Mehta–Ramanathan (1985) proved that if X is Frobenius split and f is a proper morphism with Rif*OX = 0 for i > 0, then Y is also Frobenius split. This yields vanishing for higher direct images of ample line bundles on Frobenius split varieties (Kumar–Lauritzen–Thomsen 2002).

Bhatt (2012) introduced the notion of F‑singularities and proved a characteristic‑free version of the Kodaira vanishing theorem for varieties with F‑rational singularities using the derived de Rham complex and the Cartier isomorphism. In particular, if X is globally F‑regular, then Hi(X, ωX ⊗ L) = 0 for i > 0 and any ample L, recovering the Kawamata–Viehweg vanishing theorem in characteristic p for F‑regular varieties.

For higher direct images, the most general positive‑characteristic vanishing theorem is due to Patakfalvi–Zdanowicz (2018): if f : X → Y is a projective morphism of smooth varieties and L is an f‑ample line bundle, then Rif*(ωX/Y ⊗ L) = 0 for i > dim X − dim Y. The proof uses perfectoid spaces and the almost purity theorem of Scholze to reduce to characteristic zero via the tilting equivalence. This result is sharp; the bound dim X − dim Y cannot be improved without additional assumptions such as F‑injectivity of the fibers.

Applications include the minimal model program in positive characteristic (Hacon–Witaszek 2018), where vanishing of higher direct images of the relative canonical sheaf is needed to run the MMP for threefolds. The theory also underlies the construction of the moduli space of stable varieties in positive characteristic (Kollár 2020) and the proof of the abundance conjecture for surfaces in characteristic p ≥ 5 (Tanaka 2018).

The Derived Category of Coherent Sheaves on K3 Surfaces and the Global Torelli Theorem

The bounded derived category Db(Coh X) of a complex projective K3 surface X carries a rich structure reflecting the Hodge theory of X. Mukai (1987) introduced the Mukai lattice H*(X, ℤ) with the Mukai pairing, showing that the Chern character induces an isometry between the numerical Grothendieck group N(X) and a primitive sublattice of the Mukai lattice. Orlov (1997) proved that any exact equivalence Φ: Db(Coh X) → Db(Coh Y) between derived categories of K3 surfaces is of Fourier–Mukai type with kernel in Db(Coh X×Y), and that such an equivalence induces a Hodge isometry of the transcendental lattices T(X) ≅ T(Y). The Global Torelli Theorem for K3 surfaces, originally proved by Piatetski‑Shapiro and Shafarevich (1971) and later refined by Burns‑Rapoport (1975) and Looijenga‑Peters (1981), states that the period map from the moduli space of marked K3 surfaces to the period domain is surjective onto a Zariski‑open subset with fibres given by monodromy orbits. In derived terms, the derived Torelli theorem (Huybrechts‑Stellari 2005) asserts that two K3 surfaces have equivalent derived categories if and only if their transcendental lattices are Hodge‑isometric. This equivalence is realised by a Fourier–Mukai transform whose kernel is a universal sheaf on the product, and the induced action on the Mukai lattice preserves the weight‑two Hodge structure. The autoequivalence group Aut(Db(Coh X)) is described by an exact sequence 1 → Pic(X) ⋊ ℤ → Aut(Db) → OHdg(T(X)) → 1, where the first factor corresponds to tensoring by line bundles and shifts, and the second factor is the group of Hodge isometries of the transcendental lattice. Applications include the classification of Fourier–Mukai partners of a given K3 surface (Huybrechts 2006) and the result that the derived category determines the isomorphism class for K3 surfaces of sufficiently high Picard rank (Mukai 1987). The theory also underpins the construction of stability conditions on Db(Coh X) by Bridgeland (2008), where the space of stability conditions is a complex manifold of dimension 22, locally modelled on the period domain.

The Kuznetsov Component and Derived Categories of Fano Varieties

For a smooth Fano variety X of dimension n with Picard rank 1, the bounded derived category Db(Coh X) often admits a semi‑orthogonal decomposition into exceptional objects and a non‑trivial residual category, called the Kuznetsov component 𝒦u(X). Kuznetsov (2004, 2006) introduced this notion for cubic threefolds and quartic double solids, showing that 𝒦u(X) is a Calabi‑Yau category of fractional dimension. For a cubic threefold X ⊂ ℙ⁴, the decomposition Db(Coh X) = ⟨𝒪X, 𝒪X(1), 𝒦u(X)⟩ holds, and Kuznetsov (2010) conjectured that 𝒦u(X) is equivalent to the derived category of a K3 surface if and only if X is rational. The Hochschild homology of 𝒦u(X) recovers the primitive Hodge structure of X; in particular, HH0(𝒦u) ≅ H3,0(X) ⊕ H2,1(X) ⊕ H1,2(X) ⊕ H0,3(X). Kuznetsov conjectured that birational Fano varieties have equivalent Kuznetsov components; this has been verified for many families, including cubic fourfolds (Kuznetsov‑Perry 2017) and Gushel‑Mukai varieties (Kuznetsov‑Perry 2018). The component also governs the geometry of the associated moduli spaces of stable objects: for a cubic threefold, the moduli space of stable objects in 𝒦u(X) with fixed Chern character is a smooth projective hyperkähler variety of dimension 10, isomorphic to the Fano surface of lines on X (Kuznetsov‑Markushevich 2009). Recent work by Bayer‑Lahoz‑Macrì‑Nuer‑Perry‑Stellari (2021) constructs stability conditions on 𝒦u(X) for a wide class of Fano varieties, proving that the space of stability conditions is a complex manifold of dimension equal to the rank of the numerical Grothendieck group. These stability conditions enable the definition of Donaldson‑Thomas invariants for the Kuznetsov component, which are expected to match the Gromov‑Witten invariants of the original Fano variety under the categorical mirror symmetry conjecture.

Worked Example: Derived Equivalence of K3 Surfaces
Let X and Y be complex projective K3 surfaces with Picard rank 1 and transcendental lattices T(X) and T(Y) of rank 21. Suppose there exists a Hodge isometry φ: T(X) → T(Y) preserving the orientation of the positive 2‑plane. Show that Db(Coh X) and Db(Coh Y) are equivalent as triangulated categories.
By the derived Global Torelli theorem (Huybrechts‑Stellari 2005), a Hodge isometry of transcendental lattices that respects the orientation extends uniquely to a Hodge isometry of the full Mukai lattices H*(X, ℤ) → H*(Y, ℤ). Orlov's representability theorem (1997) then guarantees that any such Hodge isometry is induced by a Fourier–Mukai functor with kernel in Db(Coh X×Y). Since the isometry preserves the weight‑two Hodge structure, the Fourier–Mukai functor is an exact equivalence of triangulated categories. Hence Db(Coh X) ≅ Db(Coh Y).
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for a derived equivalence between K3 surfaces, first check whether the transcendental lattices are Hodge‑isometric and whether the orientation of the positive 2‑plane is preserved. Cite the derived Torelli theorem (Huybrechts‑Stellari) and Orlov's representability. For Kuznetsov components, recall the semi‑orthogonal decomposition and the Calabi‑Yau property; the Hochschild homology computation is a standard test for equivalence.

Equivariant Derived Categories and the McKay Correspondence

Let G be a finite subgroup of SL(2, ℂ) acting on ℂ². The quotient singularity ℂ²/G admits a crepant resolution Y → ℂ²/G given by the minimal resolution of the corresponding ADE surface singularity. The classical McKay correspondence (McKay 1980) establishes a bijection between the irreducible representations of G and the exceptional curves in Y, matching the representation graph with the Dynkin diagram of the same type. In derived language, Bridgeland‑King‑Reid (2001) proved that the bounded derived category of G-equivariant coherent sheaves on ℂ², denoted DbG(Coh ℂ²), is equivalent to Db(Coh Y) via a Fourier–Mukai transform whose kernel is the structure sheaf of the universal G-cluster. This equivalence identifies the G-equivariant skyscraper sheaves at the origin with the structure sheaves of the exceptional curves, and the tautological bundles associated to irreducible representations with line bundles on Y. The result extends to higher dimensions: for a finite subgroup G ⊂ SL(3, ℂ) acting on ℂ³, the G-Hilbert scheme G-Hilb(ℂ³) provides a crepant resolution of ℂ³/G (Ito‑Nakamura 1996), and Bridgeland‑King‑Reid (2001) conjectured (proved in many cases by Craw‑Ishii 2004) that DbG(Coh ℂ³) ≅ Db(Coh G-Hilb(ℂ³)). The equivariant derived category carries a natural action of the representation ring R(G), and the Fourier–Mukai kernel is a G-equivariant sheaf on ℂ³ × G-Hilb(ℂ³). This framework underlies the derived McKay correspondence for Gorenstein quotient singularities and has been generalised to non‑abelian groups by Kuznetsov (2008) using the theory of non‑commutative crepant resolutions. Applications include the computation of the Hochschild cohomology of the quotient stack [X/G] and the construction of stability conditions on the derived category of the resolution, which are used in the study of Donaldson‑Thomas invariants for local Calabi‑Yau threefolds.

The Derived Category of Coherent Sheaves on Orbifolds and the Theory of Stacky Curves

An orbifold (or Deligne‑Mumford stack) 𝒳 with coarse moduli space X admits a bounded derived category Db(Coh 𝒳) of coherent sheaves on the stack. When 𝒳 is a smooth proper Deligne‑Mumford stack of dimension 1 — a stacky curve — the category Db(Coh 𝒳) is generated by line bundles and torsion sheaves supported at stacky points. The inertia stack I𝒳 encodes the stabiliser groups at each point, and the Chen‑Ruan orbifold cohomology is recovered as the Hochschild homology of Db(Coh 𝒳). For a stacky curve with cyclic stabilisers of orders r1, …, rk, the numerical Grothendieck group N(𝒳) is a free abelian group of rank 2 + ∑(ri − 1). The derived category admits a semi‑orthogonal decomposition into the derived category of the coarse curve X and a collection of exceptional objects corresponding to the stacky points (Kawamata 2002). The Fourier–Mukai transform with kernel a universal sheaf on 𝒳 × 𝒳 yields autoequivalences that act on N(𝒳) by the orbifold Picard group and by spherical twists around the exceptional objects. These autoequivalences are related to the orbifold mapping class group. The theory of stability conditions on Db(Coh 𝒳) was developed by Iwanari (2009) and later by Bertram‑Coskun‑Huizenga (2018), who showed that the space of stability conditions is a complex manifold of dimension equal to the rank of N(𝒳). The wall‑crossing behaviour of stability conditions on stacky curves is governed by the combinatorics of the inertia stack and matches the wall‑crossing of Bridgeland stability on the minimal resolution of the coarse curve. This interplay provides a derived‑categorical proof of the orbifold Riemann‑Roch theorem (Toën 2006) and underlies the computation of Gromov‑Witten invariants of stacky curves via the Crepant Resolution Conjecture (Coates‑Corti‑Iritani‑Tseng 2009).

  • Neglecting the role of the inertia stack in the derived category of a stacky curve — the exceptional objects correspond precisely to the non‑trivial inertia components.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Cubic Fourfolds and the Associated K3 Category

A smooth cubic fourfold X ⊂ ℙ⁵ has a bounded derived category Dᵇ(Coh X) that admits a semi‑orthogonal decomposition

Dᵇ(Coh X) = ⟨ Ku(X), 𝒪X, 𝒪X(1), 𝒪X(2) ⟩

where the residual category Ku(X) is the right orthogonal to the exceptional collection (𝒪X, 𝒪X(1), 𝒪X(2)). Kuznetsov proved in 2010 that Ku(X) is a Calabi–Yau category of dimension 2, i.e. its Serre functor is isomorphic to the shift [2]. The primitive middle cohomology H⁴prim(X, ℤ) carries a Hodge structure of K3 type, and there is an isomorphism of Hodge structures H⁴prim(X, ℤ)(1) ≅ H²(S, ℤ) for a K3 surface S precisely when Ku(X) ≅ Dᵇ(Coh S). This equivalence is known for Pfaffian cubic fourfolds (Hassett 2000) and for cubic fourfolds containing a plane (Addington–Thomas 2014). The conjecture that X is rational if and only if Ku(X) is equivalent to the derived category of a K3 surface remains open in general. Recent work of Huybrechts, Rennemo, and others has refined the lattice‑theoretic criteria: the existence of a labelling of the cubic fourfold by a saturated rank‑2 lattice inside H⁴(X, ℤ) of discriminant d satisfying certain numerical conditions is equivalent to the existence of an associated K3 category. The period map for cubic fourfolds factors through the period domain for K3 surfaces, and the Torelli theorem for cubic fourfolds (Voisin 1986) together with the derived Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces (Huybrechts 2015) gives a categorical interpretation of the Hodge‑theoretic period map.

Categorical Enriques Surfaces and the Kuznetsov Component

Classical Enriques surfaces are quotients of K3 surfaces by a fixed‑point‑free involution; their derived categories Dᵇ(Coh S) contain a spherical object corresponding to the non‑trivial Brauer class of order 2. Kuznetsov and Perry (2019) introduced the notion of a categorical Enriques surface as a Calabi–Yau 2 category 𝒟 equipped with an autoequivalence Φ of order 2 such that the homotopy fixed‑point category 𝒟Φ is equivalent to Dᵇ(Coh S) for a K3 surface S. The Kuznetsov component Ku(X) of a cubic fourfold can carry such a structure: when X admits an involution acting symplectically on H⁴prim(X), the induced action on Ku(X) yields a categorical Enriques surface. In this setting the Brauer group of the categorical Enriques surface is identified with the 2‑torsion in the discriminant group of the lattice H⁴prim(X, ℤ). The work of Perry, Zhao, and others shows that the moduli space of categorical Enriques surfaces is a 10‑dimensional irreducible component of the moduli of Calabi–Yau 2 categories, and it maps dominantly onto the moduli of classical Enriques surfaces via the Brauer class. This categorical perspective explains why certain cubic fourfolds have a Kuznetsov component that is not equivalent to any Dᵇ(Coh S) but still behaves like an Enriques surface at the level of Hochschild homology and numerical Grothendieck groups.

Identifying the Calabi–Yau dimension of a Kuznetsov component
Let X be a smooth cubic fourfold. The Kuznetsov component Ku(X) is defined as the right orthogonal to the exceptional collection (𝒪X, 𝒪X(1), 𝒪X(2)) in Dᵇ(Coh X). Determine the Calabi–Yau dimension of Ku(X) and justify your answer.
The Serre functor on Dᵇ(Coh X) is 𝕊X = (−) ⊗ ωX[4] = (−) ⊗ 𝒪X(−3)[4] because ωX ≅ 𝒪X(−3). For any object E in the right orthogonal to the exceptional collection, the mutation functors through the exceptional objects shift the Serre functor by the length of the collection. Since the exceptional collection has length 3, the induced Serre functor on Ku(X) is 𝕊Ku ≅ [2]. Hence Ku(X) is a Calabi–Yau category of dimension 2.
Exam‑day strategy for semi‑orthogonal decompositions
When a question asks for the Calabi–Yau dimension of a residual category, first write down the Serre functor of the ambient derived category, then apply the mutation formula: each exceptional object in the collection contributes a shift of −1 to the Serre functor on the orthogonal. The resulting shift is the Calabi–Yau dimension.

Moduli of Bridgeland Stability Conditions on K3 Surfaces and the Space of Stability Conditions

For a complex projective K3 surface S, Bridgeland constructed the space Stab(S) of locally finite stability conditions on Dᵇ(Coh S). Bayer and Macrì (2014) proved that each connected component of Stab(S) is a complex manifold of dimension 22, and the forgetful map Stab(S) → Hom(N(S), ℂ) sending a stability condition to its central charge is a local homeomorphism onto an open subset of the period domain. The group of autoequivalences Aut(Dᵇ(Coh S)) acts freely on Stab(S) with quotient the moduli space of stability conditions. Wall‑crossing in Stab(S) is governed by spherical objects: crossing a wall corresponds to a spherical twist, and the wall‑and‑chamber structure matches the hyperplane arrangement defined by the (−2)‑curves in the Néron–Severi lattice. The work of Huybrechts, Stellari, and others shows that the connected component containing geometric stability conditions (those with heart Coh S) is simply connected, and its closure contains stability conditions whose hearts are tiltings of Coh S. The global description of Stab(S) for K3 surfaces of Picard rank 1 was completed by Bridgeland (2008), who identified it with the universal cover of the complement of a certain discriminant hyperplane arrangement in ℂ². For higher Picard rank, the space has multiple components related by derived autoequivalences, and the conjectural description via the period map and the monodromy group remains an active area of research.

Higher Categorical Structures in Algebraic Geometry: (∞,1)-Categories and Derived Stacks

Derived algebraic geometry replaces schemes by derived stacks, which are functors from the (∞,1)-category of simplicial commutative rings (or connective E∞-rings) to the (∞,1)-category of spaces satisfying descent for the étale topology. Lurie’s Spectral Algebraic Geometry (2018) develops this framework using ∞-categories, where the cotangent complex LX of a derived stack X is an object in the stable ∞-category of quasi‑coherent complexes QCoh(X). The deformation theory of a derived stack is controlled by the mapping space Map(LX, M) for a quasi‑coherent sheaf M, and obstruction theories arise from the truncation τ≥−1LX. Derived moduli stacks such as the derived moduli of perfect complexes Perf(−) are locally geometric derived stacks whose tangent complex at a point [E] is RHom(E, E)[1]. The (∞,1)-categorical approach also clarifies the relationship between derived categories and noncommutative geometry: a dg‑category can be regarded as a noncommutative derived stack, and Morita equivalence corresponds to equivalence in the (∞,1)-category of dg‑categories. Recent applications include the derived categorical proof of the geometric Langlands correspondence for GLn (Gaitsgory–Rozenblyum 2020) and the construction of derived loop spaces LX = X ×X×X X, whose functions compute Hochschild homology. The formalism of ∞-categories ensures that all higher homotopical information is retained, avoiding the loss of data that occurs when passing to triangulated categories.
