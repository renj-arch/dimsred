Math of QFT & Strings — S-Duality, Geometric Langlands & Topological Phases

Deep dive into the profound mathematics underlying quantum field theory and string theory — the Feynman path integral (a rigorous integration theory on the space of fields, the stationary phase approximation and perturbative expansion in Feynman diagrams), gauge theories and the Yang–Mills equations (connections on principal bundles, the moduli space of instantons, the Atiyah–Singer index theorem for the Dirac operator in the background of a gauge field), anomalies (the chiral anomaly as the index of a Dirac operator, the 't Hooft anomaly matching conditions, the Wess–Zumino–Witten term), the renormalization group (the beta function, asymptotic freedom in non-abelian gauge theories, the Callan–Symanzik equation), the string theory (the Polyakov path integral on Riemann surfaces, the BRST quantization, the Virasoro algebra and conformal field theory, the moduli space of algebraic curves, mirror symmetry and the homological mirror symmetry conjecture of Kontsevich), the S-duality (the Montonen–Olive duality for N = 4 super Yang–Mills, the electromagnetic duality exchanging electric and magnetic charges), and the geometric Langlands correspondence (the Kapustin–Witten interpretation as S-duality of a topologically twisted N = 4 super Yang–Mills theory, the Hitchin moduli space and the Langlands dual group LG).

"Quantum field theory is the language in which the laws of physics are written, but it is also a rich source of mathematics."

Part 1

⚛️ Quantum Field Theory and the Path Integral

QFT Foundations
Feynman Path Integral, Gauge Theories, and the Yang–Mills Equations
The Feynman path integral Z = ∫ Dφ exp(iS[φ]/ħ) is a heuristic integral over the infinite-dimensional space of field configurations. The partition function Z generates the correlation functions ⟨φ(x₁)⋯φ(x_n)⟩ via functional derivatives. In gauge theories, the fields are connections ∇ = d + A on a principal G-bundle P → M, where A is a g-valued 1-form (the gauge potential). The Yang–Mills action S_YM(A) = (1/2e²) ∫_M tr(F_A ∧ *F_A) where F_A = dA + A ∧ A is the curvature (the field strength). The Yang–Mills equations d_A *F_A = 0 are the Euler–Lagrange equations; solutions are gauge fields or connections. The instanton equation F_A = ±*F_A (the (anti-)self-dual Yang–Mills equations) gives absolute minima of the action on 4-manifolds. The moduli space of instantons M_k (with instanton number k) has dimension 8k − 3(1 − b₁ + b₂⁺) (for gauge group SU(2)). The Atiyah–Singer index theorem computes the index of the Dirac operator D̸ = γ^μ(∂_μ + A_μ) in a gauge field background: index(D̸) = (1/8π²) ∫_M tr(F_A ∧ F_A) — the Atiyah–Singer index theorem for the Dirac operator gives the chiral anomaly. The renormalization group flow (Wilson) integrates out high-energy degrees of freedom, producing an effective action with running coupling constants governed by the beta function β(g) = μ dg/dμ. For non-abelian gauge theories, β(g) = −(11N/3 − 2n_f/3) g³/16π² + O(g⁵) — asymptotic freedom (Gross–Politzer–Wilczek, 1973) explains why quarks are nearly free at high energies.
The path integral formalizes quantum dynamics; gauge theories describe fundamental forces via connections on principal bundles; the Yang–Mills equations govern the dynamics of gauge fields.
Part 2

🌀 Anomalies, Renormalization, and Conformal Field Theory

Symmetries
Chiral Anomalies, the Renormalization Group, and CFT
An anomaly is a classical symmetry that is broken by quantization. The most famous is the chiral anomaly (Adler–Bell–Jackiw, 1969): in a theory with massless fermions, the classically conserved axial current J⁵_μ satisfies ∂^μ J⁵_μ = (1/16π²) tr(F_μν *F^{μν}) — a non-zero divergence due to the quantum effects. The 't Hooft anomaly matching condition: if a symmetry is spontaneously broken, the low-energy theory must reproduce the same anomaly. The Wess–Zumino–Witten (WZW) term is a topological term (an integral over a 5-manifold bounding spacetime) that cancels anomalies. The renormalization group (Wilson, 1974): a QFT is described by an effective action S_Λ at scale Λ; integrating out modes between Λ and Λ' gives the Wilsonian effective action. The beta function β(g) determines how coupling constants change with scale. Fixed points of the RG flow define conformal field theories (CFTs) — quantum field theories invariant under scale and conformal transformations. In 2 dimensions, the Virasoro algebra [L_n, L_m] = (n−m)L_{n+m} + (c/12)n(n²−1)δ_{n+m,0} is the infinite-dimensional symmetry algebra; c is the central charge. The operator product expansion (OPE) encodes the singular part of the product of two local operators. The conformal bootstrap program (Belavin–Polyakov–Zamolodchikov, 1984) solves 2d CFTs entirely from symmetry constraints: the minimal models correspond to c < 1 and the unitary series c = 1 − 6/m(m+1). The modular invariance of the torus partition function imposes strong constraints on the spectrum of conformal dimensions.
Anomalies reveal the quantum breaking of classical symmetries; the renormalization group governs the flow of couplings; conformal field theory captures scale-invariant fixed points.
Part 3

🪢 String Theory, D-Branes, and Mirror Symmetry

Strings
The Polyakov Path Integral, D-Branes, and Homological Mirror Symmetry
String theory replaces point particles with 1-dimensional extended objects (strings). The Polyakov action S = (1/4πα') ∫_Σ √h h^{ab} g_{μν}(X) ∂_a X^μ ∂_b X^ν defines a 2d QFT on the worldsheet Σ coupled to the spacetime metric g. The BRST quantization (Becchi–Rouet–Stora–Tyutin) introduces ghost fields to remove unphysical degrees of freedom. The critical dimension for the bosonic string is D = 26; for the superstring, D = 10. D-branes (Dirichlet branes) are extended objects on which open strings end; they are classified by their dimension p (Dp-brane). A D-brane carries a Chan–Paton gauge group U(N) on its worldvolume. The type II string theories (IIA, IIB) have D-branes of even/odd dimensions respectively. T-duality relates type IIA on a circle of radius R to type IIB on a circle of radius α'/R. Mirror symmetry (Candelas–de la Ossa–Green–Parkes, 1991) states that Calabi–Yau 3-folds come in mirror pairs (X, X̌) such that type IIA on X is equivalent to type IIB on X̌. The homological mirror symmetry conjecture (Kontsevich, 1994) states an equivalence of derived categories: D^b(Coh(X)) ≅ D^b(Fuk(X̌)) — the derived category of coherent sheaves on X is equivalent to the Fukaya category of Lagrangian submanifolds on X̌. The enumeration of rational curves on Calabi–Yau manifolds (Gromov–Witten invariants) is computed by the mirror map via period integrals of the mirror family. Kontsevich's formality theorem and the deformation quantization program relate the Poisson geometry of the classical limit to the associative algebra structure of quantum observables.
String theory unifies gravity and gauge interactions via extended objects; mirror symmetry reveals a deep equivalence between symplectic and complex geometries on Calabi–Yau manifolds.
Part 4

🔋 S-Duality, Geometric Langlands, and Topological Phases

Duality
Montonen–Olive Duality, Kapustin–Witten, and Topological Quantum Field Theory
S-duality (Montonen–Olive, 1977) conjectures that N = 4 super Yang–Mills theory with gauge group G at coupling τ = θ/2π + 4πi/e² is equivalent to the same theory with the Langlands dual group LG at coupling −1/(n_g τ). Here n_g is the ratio of the lengths of long and short roots of G. The spectrum of 't Hooft–Polyakov monopoles (solitonic particles carrying magnetic charge) is exchanged with the perturbative spectrum of W-bosons (electric charge) under S-duality. Kapustin–Witten (2006) reinterpreted the geometric Langlands correspondence as S-duality of a topologically twisted N = 4 super Yang–Mills theory on the product of a Riemann surface C and a surface ℝ². The Hitchin moduli space M_H(G) of Higgs bundles on C appears as the moduli space of solutions to the Nahm equations. The A-branes and B-branes on M_H(G) correspond to Hecke eigensheaves and automorphic sheaves on the moduli space of G-bundles. The geometric Langlands correspondence states an equivalence of derived categories: D^b(Coh(Loc_{^LG}(C))) ≅ D^b(Mod_{D}(Bun_G(C))) — coherent sheaves on the moduli space of LG-local systems correspond to D-modules on the moduli space of G-bundles. The topological quantum field theory (TQFT) (Atiyah, Witten) assigns vector spaces to codimension-1 submanifolds and linear maps to cobordisms. The Chern–Simons theory (Witten, 1989) is a TQFT whose partition function gives the Jones polynomial and its generalizations. The triangulated category of topological phases (Kitaev, Freed–Hopkins–Teleman) classifies gapped phases of matter using unitary TQFTs, tensor categories, and the cobordism hypothesis (Lurie, Baez–Dolan).
S-duality exchanges electric and magnetic degrees of freedom; the geometric Langlands program emerges as the S-duality of topologically twisted gauge theory, bridging representation theory and quantum field theory.

Higher-Form Symmetries and Generalized Global Symmetries

Ordinary global symmetries act on local operators. In many quantum field theories, especially gauge theories, there exist symmetries whose charged objects are extended — line operators, surface operators, or higher-dimensional defects. A p-form symmetry is a symmetry whose charged objects are p-dimensional. For example, a 1-form symmetry in four dimensions acts on Wilson lines; its conserved current is a two-form Jμν satisfying ∂μJμν=0. The charged objects are the worldlines of particles (or strings) and the symmetry group can be discrete or continuous. The modern framework (Gaiotto–Kapustin–Seiberg–Willett, 2015) treats these symmetries on the same footing as ordinary 0-form symmetries, allowing for ’t Hooft anomalies that involve higher-form currents and background gauge fields for the symmetry.

In a pure U(1) gauge theory in four dimensions there is a 1-form electric symmetry (shifting the Wilson line by a phase) and a magnetic 1-form symmetry (shifting the ’t Hooft line). The two symmetries have a mixed anomaly captured by a 5-dimensional topological term ∫ B∧dC where B and C are background two-form gauge fields for the electric and magnetic symmetries. This anomaly obstructs gauging both symmetries simultaneously and underlies the self-duality of the theory under S-duality. In non-abelian gauge theories with center Z(G), the center 1-form symmetry acts on Wilson lines in representations with non-trivial center charge. Confinement can be diagnosed by the spontaneous breaking of this center symmetry: in the confined phase the symmetry is unbroken and Wilson loops obey an area law; in the deconfined phase it is broken and Wilson loops obey a perimeter law.

Higher-form symmetries also appear in topological phases of matter. In (2+1)D topological orders, the anyon excitations carry charges under a 1-form symmetry whose group is the fusion category of anyons. The braiding statistics is encoded in the mutual ’t Hooft anomaly between the electric and magnetic 1-form symmetries. The classification of symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases with higher-form symmetries uses group cohomology Hd+1(BpG, U(1)) where BpG is the p-fold classifying space. This cohomological classification matches the cobordism classification of invertible TQFTs (Freed–Hopkins, 2016).

The AdS/CFT Correspondence and Holographic Renormalization

The Anti-de Sitter/Conformal Field Theory correspondence (Maldacena, 1997) posits an exact duality between a gravitational theory on an asymptotically AdSd+1 spacetime and a conformal field theory on its d-dimensional boundary. The dictionary identifies the bulk metric gμν with the boundary stress tensor Tij, bulk scalar fields φ with boundary operators O of scaling dimension Δ, and bulk gauge fields Aμ with conserved currents Ji. The generating functional of the CFT equals the on-shell bulk action: ZCFT[J]=exp(−Sbulk[φ0=J]).

Because the bulk action diverges near the AdS boundary, a systematic renormalization procedure — holographic renormalization (de Haro–Skenderis–Solodukhin, 2001) — is required. One introduces a radial cutoff r=ε, evaluates the on-shell action as a series in ε, and adds local counterterms built from the induced metric and fields on the cutoff surface. The renormalized action yields finite one-point functions ⟨O⟩ and the trace anomaly. For a scalar of mass m in AdSd+1, the asymptotic expansion φ(r,x)=rd−Δφ(0)(x)+…+rΔφ(2Δ−d)(x)+… determines the source φ(0) and the expectation value ⟨O⟩∝φ(2Δ−d). The counterterms are universal polynomials in the boundary curvature and field strengths; for pure gravity they reproduce the Brown–York stress tensor and the holographic Weyl anomaly aEd+cId.

Holographic renormalization also provides a geometric realization of the renormalization group flow. A domain-wall solution interpolating between two AdS vacua corresponds to an RG flow between two CFTs. The holographic c-theorem (Freedman–Gubser–Pilch–Warner, 1999) states that a monotonic function constructed from the domain-wall scale factor decreases along the flow, matching the boundary c-function. Entanglement entropy in the CFT is computed by the Ryu–Takayanagi formula: SEE=Area(γA)/4GN, where γA is the minimal bulk surface anchored on the boundary region A. This formula has been generalized to higher-derivative gravities and to time-dependent backgrounds (Hubeny–Rangamani–Takayanagi, 2007).

Worked Example: Holographic One-Point Function
Consider a massive scalar φ in AdS4 with mass m2=−2 (in units where the AdS radius L=1). The asymptotic expansion near the boundary r→∞ is φ(r,x)=φ(0)(x) r−1+φ(1)(x) r−2+…. Using the standard quantization, φ(0) is the source for a boundary operator O of dimension Δ=2. Compute the renormalized one-point function ⟨O⟩ in terms of φ(1).
The on-shell bulk action for the scalar is S=½∫ d4x √g (∂φ)2+m2φ2. After introducing a cutoff r=ε and integrating by parts, the boundary term is Sbdy=−½∫ d3x √γ φ nμ∂μφ, where γ is the induced metric. Substituting the expansion gives Sbdy=−½∫ d3x (φ(0) φ(1)+…). The counterterm needed to cancel the divergence is Sct=½∫ d3x √γ φ2. The renormalized action Sren=limε→0(Sbdy+Sct) yields the one-point function ⟨O⟩=δSren/δφ(0)=−φ(1). Thus the expectation value is directly proportional to the subleading coefficient in the asymptotic expansion.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the holographic dictionary entry, write the precise scaling relation: a bulk field of mass m in AdSd+1 corresponds to a boundary operator of dimension Δ satisfying Δ(Δ−d)=m2L2. Remember the two quantizations (standard and alternative) are allowed when the Breitenlohner–Freedman bound m2L2≥−d2/4 is satisfied. For entanglement entropy, state the Ryu–Takayanagi formula and note the quantum correction (Faulkner–Lewkowycz–Maldacena, 2013) involving bulk entanglement across the minimal surface.

Categorical Structures in Topological Quantum Field Theory: Fusion and Modular Tensor Categories

Topological quantum field theories in (2+1) dimensions are fully captured by the algebraic data of a modular tensor category (MTC). An MTC is a braided fusion category with a non-degenerate S-matrix. The simple objects correspond to anyon types; the fusion rules Nijk give the decomposition of the tensor product of two anyons. The braiding is encoded in the R-symbols Rijk, and the associativity of fusion is governed by the F-symbols (6j-symbols) satisfying the pentagon equation. The modular S and T matrices are derived from the Hopf link and the twist of anyons; they furnish a projective representation of SL(2,Z) on the space of conformal blocks on the torus.

The Reshetikhin–Turaev construction (1991) builds a (2+1)D TQFT from any MTC. The partition function on a closed 3-manifold M is computed by presenting M via surgery on a framed link in S3 and evaluating the colored link invariant using the MTC data. The resulting invariant is independent of the surgery presentation because the MTC satisfies the Kirby moves. The TQFT assigns to a genus-g surface Σg the vector space of conformal blocks, whose dimension is given by the Verlinde formula dim Vg=∑i (S0i)2−2g. The mapping class group of Σg acts projectively via the modular representation.

Fusion categories without braiding describe (1+1)D topological phases and the boundaries of (2+1)D TQFTs. A fusion category C has a finite set of simple objects, a tensor product with associator, and duals. The Drinfeld center Z(C) of a fusion category is a modular tensor category; physically, Z(C) describes the bulk anyons of a (2+1)D phase whose boundary is described by C. This bulk-boundary correspondence is a categorical incarnation of the holographic principle. The classification of (2+1)D topological orders with a given symmetry G reduces to the classification of G-crossed braided extensions of the MTC (Barkeshli–Bonderson–Cheng–Wang, 2019).

Quantum Geometric Langlands and the Role of the Quantum Parameter

The classical geometric Langlands correspondence relates D-modules on the moduli stack BunG(C) of G-bundles on a curve C to coherent sheaves on the moduli stack LocL G(C) of Langlands dual local systems. The quantum geometric Langlands program (Beilinson–Drinfeld, 1991; Gaitsgory, 2004) introduces a deformation parameter ℏ (or the level k of an affine Kac–Moody algebra) and replaces D-modules by modules over the sheaf of chiral differential operators Dℏ. On the spectral side, coherent sheaves are replaced by ind-coherent sheaves on the derived stack of L G-local systems with a shifted symplectic structure.

Physically, the quantum parameter appears in the topologically twisted N=4 super Yang–Mills theory as the complexified coupling τ=θ/2π+4πi/e2. S-duality acts as τ↦−1/(ngτ). The quantum geometric Langlands correspondence is the statement that the category of A-branes (supported on Lagrangian submanifolds of the Hitchin moduli space MH(G)) at parameter τ is equivalent to the category of B-branes (coherent sheaves on MH(L G)) at parameter −1/(ngτ). The parameter ℏ is identified with the ratio of the two topological twisting parameters (t, t̄) in the Kapustin–Witten setup; the classical limit ℏ→0 recovers the usual geometric Langlands.

Recent work (Arinkin–Gaitsgory, 2015; Ben-Zvi–Sakuma, 2020) constructs the quantum Langlands functor using the Whittaker reduction and the categorical trace. The functor intertwines the action of the spherical Hecke category (convolution with Hecke eigensheaves) with the action of the quantum group Uq(L g) at q=exp(πiℏ). The quantum parameter also controls the monodromy of the quantum Hitchin connection on the space of conformal blocks of the WZW model at level k. When k is a negative integer, the correspondence relates to the representation theory of the quantum group at a root of unity, linking to the classification of modular tensor categories from quantum groups.

Classification of Topological Phases via Cobordism and Invertible TQFTs

The modern classification of gapped topological phases of matter (including symmetry-protected topological phases) uses the language of invertible topological quantum field theories (iTQFTs). An iTQFT is a symmetric monoidal functor Z: BorddG→Vectℂ from the (∞,d)-category of d-dimensional manifolds with G-structure (e.g., orientation, spin, time-reversal) to complex vector spaces, such that Z(M) is one-dimensional for every closed M. The group of deformation classes of iTQFTs in dimension d with symmetry G is isomorphic to the generalized cohomology group ΩdG(pt) (the cobordism group of G-manifolds) by the cobordism hypothesis (Lurie, 2009; Freed–Hopkins, 2016).

For bosonic SPT phases with finite group symmetry G in (d+1) dimensions, the classification is Hd+1(BG, U(1)) (group cohomology). For fermionic phases with time-reversal symmetry T2=(−1)F, the relevant G-structure is Pin− and the classification is given by the spin cobordism group ΩdPin−(pt). The first few groups are known: Ω1=0, Ω2=Z2 (the Kitaev chain), Ω3=Z2 (the 3D topological superconductor), Ω4=Z (the 4D invertible phase with chiral central charge). The Anderson dual of the cobordism spectrum yields the group of invertible TQFTs, which matches the classification of anomalies of G-symmetric QFTs in one lower dimension.

The cobordism hypothesis states that the (∞,d)-category of fully extended TQFTs with target C is equivalent to the (∞,d)-category of fully dualizable objects in C. For invertible theories, fully dualizable objects are precisely the invertible objects, and the space of such theories is the mapping space Map(BG, Pic(C)) where Pic(C) is the Picard ∞-groupoid of C. This perspective unifies the classification of anomalies, SPT phases, and topological terms in effective actions. For example, the θ-term in 4D Yang–Mills theory corresponds to an element of Ω5SO(BZ2) classifying a (4+1)D iTQFT whose boundary is the 4D theory with a discrete θ-angle.

  • Confusing the level k of the affine Kac–Moody algebra with the quantum parameter ℏ in quantum geometric Langlands; they are related by ℏ=1/(k+h∨) but not identical.
  • Assuming every fusion category is braided; only modular tensor categories have a non-degenerate braiding, while general fusion categories describe boundaries, not bulk anyons.
  • Misidentifying the holographic counterterms: the scalar counterterm is ½∫√γ φ2 only for the standard quantization; alternative quantization requires a different boundary term.
  • Overlooking the role of the Breitenlohner–Freedman bound when stating the AdS/CFT dictionary; masses below the bound lead to instabilities, not valid dual operators.
  • Treating higher-form symmetries as ordinary symmetries acting on local operators; their charged objects are extended, and their anomalies involve higher-degree background fields.
  • Confusing the cobordism classification of invertible phases with the classification of all topological orders; the latter requires non-invertible TQFTs described by modular tensor categories.
  • Remember that the quantum geometric Langlands correspondence reduces to the classical one only in the ℏ→0 limit, and the S-duality transformation on τ is the precise physical realization of the Langlands duality.
