Graph Minors — the Robertson-Seymour Theorem, Treewidth & Well-Quasi-Ordering

Graph minor theory studies the structure of graphs under the operations of vertex deletion, edge deletion, and edge contraction — collectively, taking minors. A graph H is a minor of G if H can be obtained from G by deleting vertices, deleting edges, and contracting edges. The Robertson-Seymour theorem (formerly the Wagner conjecture, proved in a monumental series of 23 papers spanning 500+ pages) states that graphs are well-quasi-ordered under the minor relation: in any infinite sequence of finite graphs, one is a minor of another. Equivalently, every minor-closed family of graphs has a finite set of forbidden minors — minimal graphs not in the family. The treewidth of a graph measures how "tree-like" it is; the Excluded Grid Theorem says graphs with large treewidth contain a large grid minor. The Graph Minors Structure Theorem describes the decomposition of H-minor-free graphs into surface-embeddable pieces with vortex and apex attachments. The theory yields a polynomial-time algorithm for any minor-closed property — one of the most sweeping algorithmic results in graph theory.

"Graph minor theory is the most profound structure theory in graph theory."
— Neil Robertson and Paul Seymour
Part 1

📏 Treewidth and Tree Decompositions

Treewidth
Tree Decompositions, Treewidth, and Brambles
A tree decomposition of a graph G = (V,E) is a pair (T, {X_t}_{t∈T}) where T is a tree and each bag X_t ⊆ V satisfies: (1) ∪_{t∈T} X_t = V; (2) for every edge uv∈E, there exists a node t with u,v∈X_t; (3) if v∈X_t ∩ X_{t'}, then v∈X_{t''} for every t'' on the unique path in T from t to t' (the interpolation property). The treewidth tw(G) = max_t |X_t| − 1, with the convention that tw(G) = 0 for edgeless graphs. Trees have treewidth 1; series-parallel graphs have treewidth 2; the k×k grid has treewidth k. The treewidth duality theorem (Seymour-Thomas): tw(G) ≥ k iff G has a bramble of order k+1 — a family of connected subgraphs that pairwise intersect. More precisely, the bramble number equals the treewidth plus 1. Bounded-treewidth graphs are well-quasi-ordered under the minor relation (a key ingredient in the full Robertson-Seymour theorem). Treewidth is also fixed-parameter tractable: for any k, there is an O(f(k)·n) algorithm to compute whether tw(G) ≤ k (Bodlaender's algorithm).
Treewidth quantifies how tree-like a graph is; the bramble characterization provides a dual certificate for large treewidth, analogous to the role of grids in planar graph separators.
Part 2

🌍 The Excluded Grid Theorem

Grid Minor
The Excluded Grid Theorem and Its Consequences
The Excluded Grid Theorem (Robertson-Seymour, 1986): for every integer k, there exists a function f(k) such that any graph G with treewidth ≥ f(k) contains the k×k grid as a minor. In other words, the grid is the "canonical witness" for large treewidth — the only obstruction to having small treewidth is containing a large grid minor. The best known bound has been improved from astronomical heights to f(k) = O(k² log k) (Chuzhoy, 2015). The wall of height h is a cubic-grid-like graph of treewidth Θ(h) — the edge version of the grid theorem. The Chekuri-Chuzhoy half-integral result: planar graphs with treewidth ≥ C·k·n^{1/2} contain a k×k grid minor. The Excluded Grid Theorem is the main structural ingredient in the Graph Minors Structure Theorem and is essential for the design of polynomial-time algorithms for minor-closed properties: to test membership in a minor-closed family, one can either use bounded-treewidth techniques (if treewidth is small) or find a large grid to extract the forbidden minor.
Large treewidth forces a large grid minor — the grid is the canonical obstruction to small treewidth, making it a universal tool for algorithmic decomposition.
Part 3

🧮 The Graph Minors Structure Theorem

Structure
H-Minor-Free Graphs: Surface Embedding, Vortices, and Apex Vertices
For any fixed graph H, every H-minor-free graph G can be decomposed into k pieces that are each almost embeddable in a surface of genus g(H), where k and g depend only on H (not on G). A graph is almost embeddable in a surface Σ if it can be obtained from a graph embedded in Σ by: (i) adding at most t vortices — disks replaced by graphs of bounded pathwidth that attach to the boundary of the disk; and (ii) adding at most a apex vertices — vertices that may be adjacent to any part of the graph arbitrarily. The decomposition is hierarchical: the pieces are glued together along cliques of bounded size (at most the size of the largest clique in H, which is bounded). The treewidth of the overall decomposition is bounded by a function of H. The structure theorem is the key to algorithmic applications: it provides a polynomial-time algorithm for testing any minor-closed property by decomposing the graph into manageable pieces. The theorem also underlies the tangle theory — a theory of high-order connectivity that captures "how a graph is organized around a large grid." A tangle of order θ is an orientation of all separations of order < θ that satisfies consistency axioms; tangles are the fundamental units of graph structure.
Any H-minor-free graph breaks into surface-embeddable chunks glued along bounded cliques, with vortices and apex vertices managing the complexity — a universal decomposition.
Part 4

🔥 Algorithmic Applications and Well-Quasi-Ordering

Algorithms
Robertson-Seymour Theorem, Kenji's Algorithm, FPT, and Courcelle's Theorem
The Robertson-Seymour theorem (2004, formerly the Wagner conjecture): graphs are well-quasi-ordered under the minor relation — every infinite sequence of finite graphs contains one that is a minor of another. This is equivalent to the statement that every minor-closed family ℱ has a finite set of forbidden minors (the obstruction set). The algorithmic consequence (Kenji's algorithm / Robertson-Seymour algorithm): for any minor-closed property P, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm to test whether G satisfies P: (1) compute the finite obstruction set O; (2) test whether G contains any H∈O as a minor (each minor test takes O(n³) for fixed H). This gives a polynomial-time algorithm for any minor-closed property — from planarity testing to knotless embeddability — without explicit case analysis. The k-disjoint paths problem (Robertson-Seymour, 1995): given k pairs of terminals in G, decide if there exist k pairwise vertex-disjoint paths connecting each pair. The algorithm runs in O(n³) for any fixed k, using the structure theorem and bounded-treewidth techniques. Courcelle's theorem: any property expressible in monadic second-order logic (MSO₂) can be decided in linear time on graphs of bounded treewidth. The fixed-parameter tractability (FPT) of minor containment: testing whether H is a minor of G is FPT parameterized by |H|. Many graph problems — vertex deletion to a minor-closed family, computing treewidth, and the k-disjoint paths problem — are FPT parameterized by treewidth. The theory of graph minors remains one of the most profound and far-reaching achievements in discrete mathematics.
The well-quasi-ordering of graphs under the minor relation implies the existence of polynomial-time algorithms for any minor-closed property — a sweeping structural-algorithmic unification.
