Scattering Theory — Wave Operators, S-Matrix & the Lax-Phillips Framework

Scattering theory studies the asymptotic behavior of waves (or quantum particles) as they interact with obstacles or potentials. The central objects are the Møller wave operators Ω_± = s-lim_{t→±∞} e^{itH} e^{-itH₀} which compare the interacting dynamics generated by H = H₀ + V to the free dynamics H₀ = −Δ. The scattering operator S = Ω_+^* Ω_- encodes the probability amplitudes for transitions between asymptotic states. In the stationary formulation, the Lippmann-Schwinger equation gives the scattering states, and the partial wave expansion reduces the problem to radial equations with phase shifts δ_ℓ(k). The Lax-Phillips theory provides a translation representation for hyperbolic PDEs, linking poles of the scattering matrix to resonances. The Birman-Krein formula connects the spectral shift function to the scattering matrix via det S(λ) = e^{−2πi ξ(λ)}. Inverse scattering (the Marchenko and Gel'fand-Levitan equations) recovers the potential from scattering data.

"Scattering theory is the study of asymptotic behavior in quantum mechanics."
— Tosio Kato
Part 1

📏 Time-Dependent Scattering Theory

Wave Operators
Møller Operators, the Scattering Operator, and the S-Matrix
The Hamiltonian is H = H₀ + V where H₀ = −Δ and V is a short-range potential satisfying |V(x)| ≤ C|x|^{−1−ε}. The Møller wave operators are defined as the strong limits Ω_± = s-lim_{t→±∞} e^{itH} e^{-itH₀}. They satisfy the intertwining property: HΩ_± = Ω_± H₀. The scattering operator S = Ω_+^* Ω_- is unitary on L²(ℝⁿ) and commutes with H₀. In the momentum representation, the S-matrix has the form ⟨k|S|k'⟩ = δ(k−k') − 2πi δ(|k|²/2−|k'|²/2) T(k,k'). The optical theorem relates the total cross-section to the forward scattering amplitude: σ_total = (4π/k) Im f(0). The Enss method provides a phase space analysis of scattering, using geometric decompositions of the Hilbert space into incoming and outgoing subspaces. The wave operators are asymptotically complete: the continuous spectral subspace of H equals the closure of the ranges of Ω_±, meaning all scattering states are describable as free states at ±∞.
The Møller wave operators Ω_± compare interacting and free dynamics at asymptotically early and late times; their product S = Ω_+^* Ω_- is the scattering operator encoding all observable transition probabilities.
Part 2

🌍 Stationary Scattering Theory and the Resolvent

Stationary
The Resolvent, Lippmann-Schwinger Equation, and Partial Wave Expansion
The resolvent R(z) = (H−z)^{−1} for Im z > 0 extends to the continuous spectrum; its boundary values R(λ±i0) as λ ∈ σ_c(H) define the incoming and outgoing solutions. The Lippmann-Schwinger equation for the outgoing scattering state: ψ^+(k,x) = e^{ik·x} − ∫ G₀^+(|x−y|,k) V(y) ψ^+(k,y) dy where G₀^+(r,k) = −e^{ikr}/(4πr) is the outgoing Green's function. The scattering amplitude f(k',k) = −(1/4π) ∫ e^{−ik'·y} V(y) ψ^+(k,y) dy. For spherically symmetric potentials, the partial wave expansion decomposes into angular momentum channels ℓ = 0,1,2,... with the radial wavefunction behaving asymptotically as sin(kr − ℓπ/2 + δ_ℓ(k))/r where δ_ℓ(k) is the phase shift. Levinson's theorem: δ_ℓ(0) − δ_ℓ(∞) = π·N_ℓ where N_ℓ is the number of bound states with angular momentum ℓ. The effective range expansion: k^{2ℓ+1} cot δ_ℓ(k) = −1/a_ℓ + (1/2) r_ℓ k² + O(k⁴) where a_ℓ is the scattering length and r_ℓ the effective range.
Stationary scattering theory reformulates the time-dependent problem via the resolvent; the partial wave expansion reduces the 3D problem to 1D radial equations with phase shifts encoding the interaction.
Part 3

🧮 Resonances and the Spectral Shift Function

Resonances
Resonance Poles, the Breit-Wigner Formula, and the Birman-Krein Theory
Resonances are poles of the analytically continued S-matrix in the lower half-plane of the complex energy plane (or momentum plane). They correspond to metastable states with finite lifetime. The Breit-Wigner formula: near a resonance at energy E_r with width Γ, the cross-section behaves as σ(E) ∼ (Γ/2)²/((E−E_r)² + (Γ/2)²). The lifetime τ = ℏ/Γ, so narrower resonances live longer. The spectral shift function ξ(λ; H, H₀) = (1/π) lim_{ε→0⁺} Im log det(I + V R₀(λ+iε)) quantifies the spectral deformation. The Birman-Krein formula: det S(λ) = e^{−2πi ξ(λ)} — a deep connection between the scattering matrix and the spectral shift. The trace formula: Tr(f(H) − f(H₀)) = ∫ f'(λ) ξ(λ) dλ for suitable functions f, known as the Krein spectral shift formula. The perturbation determinant D(z) = det(I + V R₀(z)) is an analytic function whose zeroes correspond to eigenvalues (bound states) and whose phase on the continuous spectrum gives ξ(λ).
Resonances are poles of the S-matrix in the complex plane; their widths give decay rates. The spectral shift function bridges the spectral and scattering properties via the Birman-Krein and trace formulas.
Part 4

🔥 Inverse Scattering and the Lax-Phillips Theory

Inverse
The Marchenko and Gel'fand-Levitan Equations, and the Lax-Phillips Framework
Inverse scattering recovers the potential V from scattering data (the S-matrix or reflection coefficients). The Gel'fand-Levitan equation: K(x,y) + F(x,y) + ∫_x^∞ K(x,t)F(t,y) dt = 0 for y > x, where F is constructed from the spectral function. The Marchenko equation: A(x+y) + B(x,y) + ∫_x^∞ A(y+t)B(x,t) dt = 0. Both are Fredholm integral equations that uniquely determine the potential. The Lax-Phillips scattering theory for hyperbolic PDEs studies the wave equation on exterior domains via the Lax-Phillips semigroup Z(t) = P_+ U(t) P_- acting on the outgoing translation representation. The poles of the scattering matrix correspond to resonances of the obstacle — they govern the decay of energy in the exterior domain. Applications include Selberg scattering theory for automorphic functions on hyperbolic surfaces, the Mazzeo-Melrose theory of scattering on asymptotically hyperbolic manifolds, and the theory of resonances for the Laplacian on Riemannian manifolds with cusps. The Poisson formula for resonances relates the trace of the wave group to a sum over resonances.
Inverse scattering uses integral equations (Gel'fand-Levitan, Marchenko) to reconstruct the potential. The Lax-Phillips theory provides a translation representation for wave equations, linking resonance poles to the geometry of the obstacle.
