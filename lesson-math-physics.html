Mathematical Physics — The Mathematics Underlying the Universe

Mathematical physics is the rigorous mathematical framework for theoretical physics. It includes symmetry groups (Lie groups, Lie algebras), representation theory, the mathematics of quantum mechanics (Hilbert spaces, operators, spectral theory), gauge theory (connections, curvature, fiber bundles), spinors, and the geometric formulation of classical and quantum field theory. The subject sits at the intersection of pure mathematics and fundamental physics.

"The miracle of the appropriateness of the language of mathematics for the formulation of the laws of physics is a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve."
— Eugene Wigner
Part 1

🔷 Lie Groups and Lie Algebras — The Mathematics of Symmetry

Continuous Symmetries
Lie Groups — The Language of Gauge Symmetry
A Lie group is a group that is also a smooth manifold — group operations are smooth. Examples: GL(n,ℝ), O(n), SO(n), U(n), SU(n). The Lie algebra g = T_eG is the tangent space at the identity, equipped with the Lie bracket [X,Y] = ad_X(Y). The exponential map exp: g → G maps a neighborhood of 0 onto a neighborhood of e. The classification of compact simple Lie groups yields the classical families (A_n = SU(n+1), B_n = SO(2n+1), C_n = Sp(n), D_n = SO(2n)) and five exceptional groups (G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈). Representations of Lie groups are actions on vector spaces preserving the group structure — they describe how particles transform under symmetries. The adjoint representation acts on the Lie algebra itself. The Killing form κ(X,Y) = Tr(ad_X ad_Y) gives a metric on the Lie algebra, used to classify semisimple algebras.
Lie groups encode continuous symmetries — from rotations (SO(3)) to the gauge groups of particle physics (SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1)).
Part 2

⚛️ Quantum Mechanics — Hilbert Spaces and Operators

The Mathematical Framework
Quantum Mechanics in Mathematical Language
States are unit vectors in a Hilbert space ℋ. Observables are self-adjoint operators A on ℋ. The spectral theorem decomposes A = ∫ λ dE(λ) where E is the spectral measure — eigenvalues are possible measurement outcomes. Stone's theorem says that a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group U(t) = e^{-itH} corresponds to a self-adjoint generator H (the Hamiltonian). The Heisenberg uncertainty principle is [x,p] = iℏ. Weyl's CCR (canonical commutation relations) are the exponentiated form: e^{iaP}e^{ibX} = e^{-iab}e^{ibX}e^{iaP}. The Fock space ℱ(ℋ) = ⊕_{n=0}^∞ ℋ^{⊗_s n} describes many-particle quantum systems — creation and annihilation operators satisfy CCR. Superselection sectors separate states with different charges — the Hilbert space decomposes into a direct sum of coherent subspaces.
Quantum mechanics is the spectral theory of self-adjoint operators on Hilbert space — physics becomes functional analysis.
Part 3

🔗 Gauge Theory and Fiber Bundles

Connections and Curvature
Gauge Theory — The Geometry of Fundamental Forces
A fiber bundle E → M has a base space M and fibers F attached at each point. A principal G-bundle P → M has fiber = the Lie group G — the symmetry group. A connection A on P is a g-valued 1-form that defines parallel transport (how to compare fibers at different points). The curvature F = dA + A∧A is a g-valued 2-form measuring the failure of parallel transport to be path-independent. The Yang-Mills equation d*F = 0 is the classical field equation for gauge fields — it generalizes Maxwell's equations to non-abelian groups. The Yang-Mills existence and mass gap is a Millennium Prize Problem. Instantons are self-dual connections (F = *F) on Euclidean ℝ⁴ — they minimize the Yang-Mills action and are classified by the second Chern number. Anomalies occur when a classical symmetry fails to survive quantization — they're governed by index theory.
Gauge theories are geometry on principal bundles — forces are curvature, particles are sections, and interactions are parallel transport.
Part 4

🌀 Spinors, Supersymmetry, and Quantum Field Theory

Fermions and Beyond
Spinors, SUSY, and the Algebraic Structure of QFT
Spinors are representations of the double cover Spin(n) of SO(n) — they carry half-integer spin and describe fermions. The Clifford algebra Cl_{p,q} is generated by γ^μ with {γ^μ,γ^ν} = 2g^{μν}. The Dirac equation (iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ = 0 is the relativistic wave equation for spin-½ particles. Supersymmetry (SUSY) extends the Poincaré algebra with fermionic generators Q that satisfy {Q, Q̄} = 2P — it pairs bosons with fermions. The Wess-Zumino model is the simplest SUSY field theory. Quantum field theory (QFT) is formulated via the path integral ∫ Dφ e^{iS[φ]} — a mathematically ill-defined infinite-dimensional integral that physicists compute perturbatively via Feynman diagrams. Renormalization absorbs infinities into bare parameters — the renormalization group (RG) describes how coupling constants flow with energy scale. Conformal field theory (CFT) is scale-invariant QFT — it's the basis of string theory and statistical mechanics at critical points.
Spinors, supersymmetry, and QFT push mathematics to its limits — infinite-dimensional integrals, algebraic structures, and deep geometry converge.
