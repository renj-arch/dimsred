Mathematical Physics — The Mathematics Underlying the Universe

Mathematical physics is the rigorous mathematical framework for theoretical physics. It includes symmetry groups (Lie groups, Lie algebras), representation theory, the mathematics of quantum mechanics (Hilbert spaces, operators, spectral theory), gauge theory (connections, curvature, fiber bundles), spinors, and the geometric formulation of classical and quantum field theory. The subject sits at the intersection of pure mathematics and fundamental physics.

"The miracle of the appropriateness of the language of mathematics for the formulation of the laws of physics is a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve."
— Eugene Wigner
Part 1

🔷 Lie Groups and Lie Algebras — The Mathematics of Symmetry

Continuous Symmetries
Lie Groups — The Language of Gauge Symmetry
A Lie group is a group that is also a smooth manifold — group operations are smooth. Examples: GL(n,ℝ), O(n), SO(n), U(n), SU(n). The Lie algebra g = T_eG is the tangent space at the identity, equipped with the Lie bracket [X,Y] = ad_X(Y). The exponential map exp: g → G maps a neighborhood of 0 onto a neighborhood of e. The classification of compact simple Lie groups yields the classical families (A_n = SU(n+1), B_n = SO(2n+1), C_n = Sp(n), D_n = SO(2n)) and five exceptional groups (G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈). Representations of Lie groups are actions on vector spaces preserving the group structure — they describe how particles transform under symmetries. The adjoint representation acts on the Lie algebra itself. The Killing form κ(X,Y) = Tr(ad_X ad_Y) gives a metric on the Lie algebra, used to classify semisimple algebras.
Lie groups encode continuous symmetries — from rotations (SO(3)) to the gauge groups of particle physics (SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1)).
Part 2

⚛️ Quantum Mechanics — Hilbert Spaces and Operators

The Mathematical Framework
Quantum Mechanics in Mathematical Language
States are unit vectors in a Hilbert space ℋ. Observables are self-adjoint operators A on ℋ. The spectral theorem decomposes A = ∫ λ dE(λ) where E is the spectral measure — eigenvalues are possible measurement outcomes. Stone's theorem says that a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group U(t) = e^{-itH} corresponds to a self-adjoint generator H (the Hamiltonian). The Heisenberg uncertainty principle is [x,p] = iℏ. Weyl's CCR (canonical commutation relations) are the exponentiated form: e^{iaP}e^{ibX} = e^{-iab}e^{ibX}e^{iaP}. The Fock space ℱ(ℋ) = ⊕_{n=0}^∞ ℋ^{⊗_s n} describes many-particle quantum systems — creation and annihilation operators satisfy CCR. Superselection sectors separate states with different charges — the Hilbert space decomposes into a direct sum of coherent subspaces.
Quantum mechanics is the spectral theory of self-adjoint operators on Hilbert space — physics becomes functional analysis.
Part 3

🔗 Gauge Theory and Fiber Bundles

Connections and Curvature
Gauge Theory — The Geometry of Fundamental Forces
A fiber bundle E → M has a base space M and fibers F attached at each point. A principal G-bundle P → M has fiber = the Lie group G — the symmetry group. A connection A on P is a g-valued 1-form that defines parallel transport (how to compare fibers at different points). The curvature F = dA + A∧A is a g-valued 2-form measuring the failure of parallel transport to be path-independent. The Yang-Mills equation d*F = 0 is the classical field equation for gauge fields — it generalizes Maxwell's equations to non-abelian groups. The Yang-Mills existence and mass gap is a Millennium Prize Problem. Instantons are self-dual connections (F = *F) on Euclidean ℝ⁴ — they minimize the Yang-Mills action and are classified by the second Chern number. Anomalies occur when a classical symmetry fails to survive quantization — they're governed by index theory.
Gauge theories are geometry on principal bundles — forces are curvature, particles are sections, and interactions are parallel transport.
Part 4

🌀 Spinors, Supersymmetry, and Quantum Field Theory

Fermions and Beyond
Spinors, SUSY, and the Algebraic Structure of QFT
Spinors are representations of the double cover Spin(n) of SO(n) — they carry half-integer spin and describe fermions. The Clifford algebra Cl_{p,q} is generated by γ^μ with {γ^μ,γ^ν} = 2g^{μν}. The Dirac equation (iγ^μ∂_μ − m)ψ = 0 is the relativistic wave equation for spin-½ particles. Supersymmetry (SUSY) extends the Poincaré algebra with fermionic generators Q that satisfy {Q, Q̄} = 2P — it pairs bosons with fermions. The Wess-Zumino model is the simplest SUSY field theory. Quantum field theory (QFT) is formulated via the path integral ∫ Dφ e^{iS[φ]} — a mathematically ill-defined infinite-dimensional integral that physicists compute perturbatively via Feynman diagrams. Renormalization absorbs infinities into bare parameters — the renormalization group (RG) describes how coupling constants flow with energy scale. Conformal field theory (CFT) is scale-invariant QFT — it's the basis of string theory and statistical mechanics at critical points.
Spinors, supersymmetry, and QFT push mathematics to its limits — infinite-dimensional integrals, algebraic structures, and deep geometry converge.

Topological Quantum Field Theory and Cobordism

Topological quantum field theory (TQFT) assigns algebraic data to manifolds in a way that depends only on their topology, not on any metric. The Atiyah–Segal axioms formalize this: a TQFT in dimension n is a symmetric monoidal functor Z from the cobordism category Cob_n to the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces. Objects of Cob_n are closed oriented (n−1)-manifolds; morphisms are equivalence classes of oriented n-manifolds with boundary. The functoriality Z(M ∪_N M') = Z(M') ∘ Z(M) encodes gluing of cobordisms along common boundaries. The monoidal structure Z(M ⊔ N) = Z(M) ⊗ Z(N) reflects disjoint union. For n = 2, the classification of 2D TQFTs is equivalent to commutative Frobenius algebras — the vector space Z(S¹) carries a multiplication (pair of pants), unit (disk), comultiplication, and counit satisfying the Frobenius relation. The partition function on a closed surface Σ_g of genus g is Z(Σ_g) = Tr((μ ∘ Δ)^g) where μ is multiplication and Δ is comultiplication. In 3D, Chern–Simons theory with gauge group G at level k gives a TQFT whose state spaces are spaces of conformal blocks of the Wess–Zumino–Witten model. The Reshetikhin–Turaev construction builds 3D TQFTs from modular tensor categories, which also describe anyonic statistics in topological phases of matter. The cobordism hypothesis (Baez–Dolan, proved by Lurie) states that the (∞,n)-category of fully extended TQFTs is the free symmetric monoidal (∞,n)-category with duals on one object — a TQFT is determined by its value on a point, which must be a fully dualizable object. This connects TQFT to higher category theory and the classification of invertible field theories via cobordism groups. Applications include topological quantum computation, where anyonic braiding in 2D systems realizes unitary representations of the braid group, and the classification of symmetry-protected topological phases via group cohomology and cobordism groups of manifolds with G-structure.

Algebraic Quantum Field Theory and Operator Algebras

Algebraic quantum field theory (AQFT) formulates quantum field theory in terms of nets of operator algebras on a Hilbert space, avoiding the divergences of perturbative path integrals. The Haag–Kastler axioms assign to each open bounded region O of Minkowski spacetime a von Neumann algebra A(O) of observables, satisfying isotony (O₁ ⊂ O₂ ⇒ A(O₁) ⊂ A(O₂)), locality (spacelike separated regions have commuting algebras), covariance (a unitary representation of the Poincaré group acts by automorphisms), and the spectrum condition (energy-momentum spectrum lies in the forward light cone). The vacuum state is a cyclic and separating vector for each A(O). The Reeh–Schlieder theorem states that the vacuum is cyclic for any local algebra — acting with local operators can approximate any state, implying entanglement across arbitrary distances. The Bisognano–Wichmann theorem identifies the modular group of the wedge algebra with the Lorentz boosts, linking Tomita–Takesaki modular theory to spacetime geometry. Superselection sectors correspond to inequivalent irreducible representations of the quasi-local algebra A = ∪_O A(O)'' — they describe charges (electric, color) that cannot be changed by local operations. Doplicher–Haag–Roberts (DHR) theory classifies sectors with finite statistics in terms of a symmetric tensor category of endomorphisms, reconstructing the gauge group and field algebra via Tannaka–Krein duality. In conformal field theory, the net of algebras on the circle extends to a net on the conformal compactification of Minkowski space; the modular nuclearity condition (Buchholz–Wichmann) characterizes theories with a mass gap. Type III factors appear generically — the local algebras are hyperfinite type III₁ factors, a striking difference from quantum mechanics where algebras are type I. The split property (existence of a type I factor between algebras of spacelike separated regions) is a criterion for statistical independence and is related to the nuclearity of thermal states. AQFT provides a rigorous framework for the spin-statistics theorem, the PCT theorem, and the analysis of quantum energy inequalities.

Worked Problem: Modular Theory and the KMS Condition
Let A be a von Neumann algebra with a cyclic and separating vector Ω. The Tomita operator S is defined by S A Ω = A* Ω for A ∈ A. Its polar decomposition S = J Δ1/2 gives the modular conjugation J and modular operator Δ. Show that the modular automorphism group σ_t(A) = Δit A Δ-it satisfies the Kubo–Martin–Schwinger (KMS) condition with respect to the state ω(A) = ⟨Ω, A Ω⟩ at inverse temperature β = 1.
The KMS condition states that for any A, B ∈ A, there exists a function FA,B(z) analytic in the strip 0 < Im(z) < 1, continuous on the boundary, such that FA,B(t) = ω(A σ_t(B)) and FA,B(t+i) = ω(σ_t(B) A) for real t. Define F(z) = ⟨Ω, A Δiz B Ω⟩. Since Δiz is bounded for 0 ≤ Im(z) ≤ 1, F is analytic in the strip and continuous on the boundary. For real t, F(t) = ⟨Ω, A Δit B Ω⟩ = ⟨Ω, A σ_t(B) Ω⟩ = ω(A σ_t(B)). For the upper boundary, F(t+i) = ⟨Ω, A Δi(t+i) B Ω⟩ = ⟨Ω, A Δit Δ-1 B Ω⟩. Using Δ-1 B Ω = B* Ω (since S B Ω = B* Ω and S = J Δ1/2), we have F(t+i) = ⟨Ω, A Δit B* Ω⟩ = ⟨Δ-it A* Ω, B* Ω⟩ = ⟨J Δ1/2 Δ-it A* Ω, J Δ1/2 B* Ω⟩ = ⟨σ-t(A) Ω, B Ω⟩ = ω(σ_t(B) A). The last step uses J Δit J = Δ-it and the definition of σ_t. Thus the KMS condition holds with β = 1.
Exam Strategy: Operator Algebra Classification
When a question asks about the type of a von Neumann algebra in QFT, recall: local algebras in relativistic QFT are hyperfinite type III₁ factors (Haag's theorem implies no type I factors). The hyperfinite II₁ factor appears in the group von Neumann algebra of the free group and in the infinite tensor product of matrix algebras (the CAR algebra for fermions). Type II∞ factors arise as crossed products of type II₁ by ℝ. Memorize the Connes classification: injective factors are completely classified by their type (I_n, I_∞, II₁, II∞, III_λ for λ ∈ (0,1], III₀, III₁). The Araki–Woods classification of type III factors by the modular spectrum (the S-invariant) is a standard exam topic — know that III_λ corresponds to periodic modular flow with period 2π/|ln λ|.

Geometric Quantization and Deformation Quantization

Geometric quantization constructs a quantum Hilbert space from a classical phase space (a symplectic manifold (M, ω)) in three stages. Prequantization requires the symplectic form to be integral: [ω/2π] ∈ H²(M, ℤ). Then there exists a Hermitian line bundle L → M with connection ∇ whose curvature is F_∇ = -iω. The prequantum Hilbert space is the space of square-integrable sections of L, with operators Q̂_f = -i∇X_f + f for f ∈ C^∞(M), where X_f is the Hamiltonian vector field (ιX_fω = df). This gives a representation of the Poisson algebra, but the space is too large — it contains sections varying along all directions. Polarization selects a Lagrangian subbundle P ⊂ TM ⊗ ℂ (integrable, maximal isotropic) and restricts to sections covariantly constant along P. For a real polarization (e.g., vertical polarization on T*Q), the quantum Hilbert space consists of half-densities on the leaf space, reproducing the Schrödinger representation. For a Kähler polarization (M a Kähler manifold), the quantum space is the space of holomorphic sections of L, yielding the Bargmann–Fock representation. The metaplectic correction (tensoring with the square root of the canonical bundle of the polarization) fixes the ordering ambiguities and ensures the correct semiclassical limit. Deformation quantization takes a different approach: it deforms the commutative algebra C^∞(M)[[ℏ]] into a noncommutative algebra with star product f ⋆ g = fg + (iℏ/2){f,g} + O(ℏ²), where the associativity of ⋆ is equivalent to the Jacobi identity for the Poisson bracket. The Kontsevich formality theorem proves that every Poisson manifold admits a deformation quantization, constructed via graphs and configuration space integrals. The equivalence classes of star products are classified by the second de Rham cohomology H²(M)[[ℏ]] (the characteristic class). For symplectic manifolds, the Gutt star product on the dual of a Lie algebra gives the universal enveloping algebra. The algebraic index theorem (Nest–Tsygan, Fedosov) computes the trace of a star product in terms of characteristic classes. Geometric and deformation quantization are related: the Berezin–Toeplitz quantization on a Kähler manifold produces a star product with separation of variables, and the geometric quantization Hilbert space can be recovered as the space of formal power series in ℏ modulo the ideal of sections vanishing to infinite order.

Integrable Systems and Soliton Theory

An integrable Hamiltonian system with n degrees of freedom possesses n independent Poisson-commuting first integrals (Liouville integrability). The Arnold–Liouville theorem states that the level sets of these integrals are n-tori, and action-angle variables (I, θ) exist locally with Hamiltonian H = H(I). The inverse scattering transform (IST) solves the initial value problem for nonlinear PDEs that admit a Lax pair representation ∂_t L = [L, M], where L and M are operators (often differential or matrix-valued) depending on the potential u(x,t). The spectrum of L is time-invariant (isospectral deformation), providing infinitely many conserved quantities. For the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation u_t - 6uu_x + uxxx = 0, the Lax pair is L = -∂_x² + u, M = -4∂_x³ + 6u∂_x + 3u_x. The scattering data of L (reflection coefficient, bound state eigenvalues and norming constants) evolve linearly in time, and the potential is reconstructed via the Gel'fand–Levitan–Marchenko integral equation. The n-soliton solution corresponds to pure discrete spectrum (no reflection). The Hirota bilinear method writes the solution as u = 2(ln τ)xx where τ satisfies a bilinear equation (e.g., (D_t D_x + D_x⁴)τ·τ = 0 for KdV, with Hirota derivatives D). The τ-function is a Wronskian or Pfaffian determinant, and its expansion generates multi-soliton solutions. The Kadomtsev–Petviashvili (KP) hierarchy unifies many integrable systems; its solutions are parametrized by points in the infinite Grassmannian (Sato theory). The quantum inverse scattering method (QISM) quantizes integrable systems via the Yang–Baxter equation R12(u)R13(u+v)R23(v) = R23(v)R13(u+v)R12(u). The algebraic Bethe ansatz diagonalizes the transfer matrix of the quantum monodromy matrix, yielding the spectrum of the XXZ spin chain and the nonlinear Schrödinger model. Classical r-matrices and the classical Yang–Baxter equation [r12, r13] + [r12, r23] + [r13, r23] = 0 govern the Poisson structure of integrable field theories. The bi-Hamiltonian structure (Magri) provides a recursion operator generating the hierarchy of commuting flows. Integrability in the sense of Painlevé (absence of movable critical singularities) is a necessary condition for solvability by IST.

Noncommutative Geometry and Spectral Triples

Noncommutative geometry (NCG) generalizes Riemannian geometry to spaces where the algebra of coordinates is noncommutative. A spectral triple (A, ℋ, D) consists of a *-algebra A represented on a Hilbert space ℋ, and a self-adjoint operator D (the Dirac operator) with compact resolvent such that [D, a] is bounded for all a ∈ A. The Connes distance formula d(x,y) = sup{|a(x) - a(y)| : a ∈ A, ||[D,a]|| ≤ 1} recovers the geodesic distance on a spin manifold when A = C^∞(M), ℋ = L²(M, S) (spinors), and D = iγ^μ∇_μ (the Dirac operator). The dimension spectrum is the set of poles of the zeta functions ζ_a(s) = Tr(a|D|-s) for a ∈ A. The Dixmier trace Tr_ω (a singular trace on the ideal of Dixmier-traceable operators) defines the integral ∫ a = Tr_ω(a|D|-n) for an n-dimensional spectral triple. The local index formula expresses the index of a Fredholm module in terms of residues of zeta functions, generalizing the Atiyah–Singer index theorem. The cyclic cohomology of A (Connes) plays the role of de Rham cohomology; the Chern character maps K-theory to cyclic cohomology. The Standard Model of particle physics coupled to gravity emerges from a spectral triple with A = C^∞(M) ⊗ (ℂ ⊕ ℍ ⊕ M₃(ℂ)) (the finite algebra of the Standard Model), where the inner fluctuations of the Dirac operator generate the gauge bosons (gluons, W/Z, photon) and the Higgs field as a connection on a finite noncommutative space. The spectral action Tr(f(D/Λ)) for a cutoff Λ and a test function f expands in powers of Λ, yielding the Einstein–Hilbert action, the Yang–Mills action, and the Higgs potential with specific couplings. The KO-dimension of the finite spectral triple (6 mod 8) fixes the reality and grading conditions, and the first-order condition [[D,a], JbJ-1] = 0 (where J is the real structure) encodes the gauge group. NCG also provides a framework for the quantum Hall effect (noncommutative torus), foliations (holonomy groupoids), and the Riemann zeta function (the scaling action on the adèle class space). The Baum–Connes conjecture, relating the K-theory of the reduced group C*-algebra to the equivariant K-homology of the classifying space, is a central open problem connecting NCG to representation theory.

  • Confusing the type of von Neumann algebra in AQFT: local algebras are type III₁, not type I or II — this is a direct consequence of the Reeh–Schlieder theorem and the spectrum condition.
  • Misstating the prequantization condition: the symplectic form must be integral ([ω/2π] ∈ H²(M, ℤ)), not merely closed or exact.
  • Assuming every Poisson manifold admits a strict deformation quantization (C*-algebraic) — Kontsevich gives formal star products (power series in ℏ); strict quantization is a separate, harder problem.
  • Confusing the Lax pair of KdV with that of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation — the operators L and M differ in order and structure.
  • Overlooking the metaplectic correction in geometric quantization: without the half-form bundle, the quantum Hilbert space carries the wrong Maslov index and the semiclassical limit fails.
  • Treating the spectral action as a fundamental action principle rather than an asymptotic expansion — the spectral action Tr(f(D/Λ)) is an effective action valid at energies below Λ.
  • Misapplying the cobordism hypothesis: it classifies fully extended TQFTs (down to points), not just (n−1)-dimensional boundary theories; the value on a point must be a fully dualizable object in the target category.
