Quantum Cohomology — Gromov–Witten Invariants, the WDVV Equation & Mirror Symmetry

Deep dive into Quantum Cohomology — Gromov–Witten invariants (virtual counts of pseudoholomorphic curves in a symplectic manifold X, defining intersection numbers on the moduli space of stable maps M̅_{g,n}(X, β) with Kontsevich’s virtual fundamental class), the quantum cohomology ring QH^*(X) = H^*(X) ⊗ ℂ[[q]] as a deformation of the ordinary cohomology ring with the quantum cup product α ⋆ β = Σ_{β∈H₂(X)} q^β Σ_{a,b} GW_{0,3}^{X,β}(α, β, γ_a) g^{ab} γ_b, the WDVV (Witten–Dijkgraaf–Verlinde–Verlinde) equations (the associativity of the quantum product expressed as differential equations for the prepotential), Frobenius manifolds (Dubrovin, 1992: a flat metric with a compatible associative commutative product satisfying the WDVV equations), mirror symmetry (equality of A-model GW potential and B-model period integrals), and the Mirror Theorem of Givental and Lian–Liu–Yau for counting rational curves on Calabi–Yau threefolds.

Part 1

⚡ Gromov–Witten Invariants

GW Theory
Moduli of Stable Maps, Virtual Fundamental Class, and Gromov–Witten Invariants
Let (X, ω) be a symplectic manifold (or a smooth projective variety). A stable map (Kontsevich, 1994) is a pair (C, f) where C is a nodal curve (a connected union of smooth rational curves meeting at nodes) and f: C → X is a holomorphic map, with the stability condition: the automorphism group of (C, f) is finite. The moduli space M̅_{g,n}(X, β) parameterizes stable maps of genus g with n marked points, representing β ∈ H₂(X). This is a Deligne–Mumford stack with a perfect obstruction theory (Behrend–Fantechi, Li–Tian) giving a virtual fundamental class [M̅_{g,n}(X,β)]^{vir} ∈ A_{vd}(M̅_{g,n}(X,β)) where vd = (dim X − 3)(1−g) + c₁(X)·β + n. The Gromov–Witten invariant is GW_{g,n}^{X,β}(α₁,…,α_n) = ∫_{[M̅_{g,n}(X,β)]^{vir}} ∏ ev_i^*(α_i) where ev_i: M̅_{g,n}(X,β) → X is evaluation at the i-th marked point. For genus 0, these count rational curves of class β through constraints Poincaré dual to α_i. The g=0, n=3 case gives the structure constants of quantum cohomology. The string equation, dilaton equation, and divisor equation are universal relations. The Kontsevich–Manin axioms include: effective (GW_{g,n}^{X,β}=0 if β=0 and (g,n)≠(0,1),(0,2),(0,3),(1,0)); symmetry under permutation of marked points; fundamental class relation; and the splitting (degeneration) formula.
Gromov–Witten invariants are virtual counts of holomorphic curves, defined via the virtual fundamental class of the moduli space of stable maps.

🌀 Quantum Cohomology Rings

Quantum
Deformation of the Cup Product, the Quantum Ring, and the Divisor Equation
The quantum cohomology ring QH^*(X) = (H^*(X; ℂ) ⊗ ℂ[[q₁,…,q_r]], ⋆) deforms the ordinary cup product, parametrized by quantum parameters q_β = exp(−∫_β ω) (in the Novikov ring). Choose basis {γ_a} of H^*(X) with intersection matrix g_{ab} = ∫_X γ_a ∪ γ_b, and inverse g^{ab}. The quantum cup product: γ_a ⋆ γ_b = Σ_{c,d} (∂_a ∂_b ∂_c F₀) g^{cd} γ_d, where F₀ is the genus-zero GW potential: F₀(t) = Σ_{n≥0} (1/n!) Σ_{β,a₁,…,a_n} GW_{0,n}^{X,β}(γ_{a₁},…,γ_{a_n}) t^{a₁}…t^{a_n}. The structure constants: ⟨γ_a ⋆ γ_b, γ_c⟩ = Σ_{β∈H₂(X)} q^β GW_{0,3}^{X,β}(γ_a, γ_b, γ_c). The product is associative (WDVV), graded (deg(q_β) = 2c₁(β)), and commutative. The divisor equation: for D ∈ H²(X), GW_{0,n}^{X,β}(D, α₁,…,) = (∫_β D) GW_{0,n−1}^{X,β}(α₁,…) + Σ GW_{0,n−1}^{X,β}(α₁,…,α_i ∪ D,…). For ℗^N, QH^*(℗^N) ≃ ℂ[H, q]/(H^{N+1} = q). The Batyrev ring for toric varieties.
Quantum cohomology deforms the cup product by contributions from holomorphic spheres, governed by the GW potential.

🌊 WDVV Equations and Frobenius Manifolds

Frobenius
The WDVV Equations, Frobenius Manifolds, and Dubrovin’s Theory
The WDVV equations (Witten, Dijkgraaf–Verlinde–Verlinde, 1990) are the associativity of the quantum product as third-order PDEs for F(t): ∂_a ∂_b ∂_e F · g^{ef} · ∂_f ∂_c ∂_d F = ∂_a ∂_c ∂_e F · g^{ef} · ∂_f ∂_b ∂_d F for all a,b,c,d. A Frobenius manifold (Dubrovin, 1992) is a smooth manifold M with a flat metric g, a commutative associative product ◦ on TM with unit e, such that (i) g(u◦v,w) = g(u,v◦w); (ii) the prepotential F satisfies ∂_a ∂_b ∂_c F = g(u_a◦u_b,u_c); (iii) flatness of the connection ∇ + z◦ for all z (the Dubrovin connection). The quantum D-module is the ℂ[[z]]-module H^*(X)⊗ℂ[[z]] with the quantum connection ∇_{∂_a} = ∂_a + (1/z) (γ_a ⋆ ·). Its flat sections give the J-function (Givental’s formalism). The Givental’s Lagrangian cone encodes all genus-zero GW invariants via the formal loop space. The reconstruction theorem (Kontsevich–Manin): genus-zero GW invariants are determined by the WDVV equations and the divisor axiom from the initial data of three-point invariants. The Bershadsky–Cecotti–Ooguri–Vafa (BCOV) theory for higher-genus invariants via the holomorphic anomaly equation. Mirror symmetry for Calabi–Yau threefolds: the A-model GW potential F₀ of X equals the B-model Yukawa coupling on the mirror Y.
Frobenius manifolds formalize the algebraic structure of quantum cohomology; the WDVV equations encode associativity as a system of PDEs for the prepotential.

✨ Mirror Symmetry and the Mirror Theorem

Mirror
The Mirror Theorem, Givental’s Formalism, and Enumerative Applications
The Mirror Theorem (Givental, 1996; Lian–Liu–Yau, 1997) gives an explicit formula for genus-zero GW invariants of Calabi–Yau hypersurfaces in toric varieties in terms of the hypergeometric series (the I-function or Yukawa coupling) of the mirror manifold. For the quintic threefold X₅ = {Σ x_i⁵ = 0} ⊂ ℗⁴, the mirror Y is the resolution of the orbifold (℗⁴/Ξ⁵)_ψ (the Dwork family). The mirror map q = e⁻^t identifies the Kähler parameter t of X with the complex modulus ψ of Y. The genus-zero GW potential of X is F₀(t) = ⅔ t³ + 5 ∑_{d≥1} N_d e^{dt} where N_d are the instanton numbers (virtual counts of degree-d rational curves). The mirror theorem: N_d = ∑_{k|d} k⁻^³ n_k where n_k are integers (the BPS invariants). For d=1: N₁ = 2875 (the number of lines on the quintic). d=2: 609250 (conics). d=3: 317206375 (twisted cubics). The BCOV holomorphic anomaly equation (Bershadsky–Cecotti–Ooguri–Vafa, 1993) extends mirror symmetry to higher-genus GW invariants. The Givental’s quantization formalism (the Frobenius manifold of genus-zero invariants determines all higher-genus invariants via the TQFT formalism). The Kontsevich’s homological mirror symmetry connects quantum cohomology to the Fukaya category and the derived category. Applications to enumerative geometry: counting curves on del Pezzo surfaces, the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants (integer invariants derived from GW invariants via the Gopakumar–Vafa formula), and the Donaldson–Thomas/GW correspondence.
The Mirror Theorem allows exact computation of Gromov–Witten invariants via hypergeometric series on the mirror, revolutionizing enumerative geometry.

Higher Genus Gromov–Witten Theory and the BCOV Holomorphic Anomaly

The genus-zero theory captured by the WDVV equations and the quantum cohomology ring is only the first term in a genus expansion of the Gromov–Witten potential. For a compact symplectic manifold X, the full partition function is Z = exp(∑g≥0 λ2g-2 F_g), where λ is the string coupling constant and F_g is the genus-g Gromov–Witten potential. The Bershadsky–Cecotti–Ooguri–Vafa (BCOV) holomorphic anomaly equation, discovered in 1993, governs the anti-holomorphic dependence of F_g on the complex structure moduli of the mirror Calabi–Yau threefold. It takes the form ∂bar i F_g = ½ Cbar ijk (D_j D_k Fg-1 + ∑r=1g-1 D_j F_r D_k Fg-r), where Cbar ijk is the conjugate Yukawa coupling and D denotes the covariant derivative with respect to the Weil–Petersson metric. This recursive structure expresses higher-genus amplitudes in terms of lower-genus data and a finite set of holomorphic ambiguities, which are fixed by boundary conditions at the large complex structure limit, the conifold point, and the orbifold point. The BCOV formalism has been rigorously developed in the mathematical literature by Fang–Lu–Yoshikawa and by Costello–Li, who constructed the higher-genus B-model as a topological quantum field theory on the mirror. In the A-model, the holomorphic anomaly corresponds to the failure of the virtual fundamental class to be holomorphic in the Kähler parameters; the equation provides a powerful computational tool that has yielded explicit genus-one and genus-two invariants for the quintic threefold and for many toric Calabi–Yau varieties.

Givental's Quantization and the Total Ancestor Potential

Givental's quantization formalism lifts the genus-zero Frobenius manifold structure to all genera by constructing a total ancestor potential A_X that encodes the full Gromov–Witten theory of a target X. The starting point is the symplectic loop space H = H^*(X)((z-1)) equipped with the symplectic form Ω(f,g) = Resz=0 (f(-z), g(z)) dz. The genus-zero data — the fundamental solution of the quantum differential equation — defines a Lagrangian cone L ⊂ H. Givental showed that the action of the twisted loop group on L, quantized via the Weil representation, produces a partition function Z = exp(∑g≥0 ħg-1 F_g) that satisfies the Virasoro constraints and the topological recursion relations. The total ancestor potential is obtained by applying the quantization of a certain symplectic transformation (the “R-matrix”) to the product of copies of the Kontsevich–Witten tau-function. This construction proves that the higher-genus Gromov–Witten invariants of any semisimple Frobenius manifold are uniquely determined by the genus-zero data, a result known as the Givental–Teleman classification theorem. For toric varieties, the R-matrix can be computed explicitly from the I-function, yielding closed formulas for all genera. The formalism also provides a conceptual proof of the Virasoro constraints for projective spaces and flag varieties, and it underlies the recent proof of the higher-genus mirror symmetry for toric Calabi–Yau threefolds by Fang–Liu–Zong.

Worked Example: Genus-One Quintic Invariant via BCOV
Compute the genus-one Gromov–Witten invariant N1 for the quintic threefold using the BCOV holomorphic anomaly equation and the known genus-zero Yukawa coupling Cttt = 5/(1-55 q).
The genus-one potential F1 satisfies ∂bar t F1 = ½ Cbar ttt D_t D_t F0. For the quintic, the Weil–Petersson metric is Gtbar t = ∂_t ∂bar t K with K = -log(i∫ Ω∧bar Ω). The Yukawa coupling is Cttt = 5 + ∑d≥1 N_d d3 q^d/(1-q^d). At the large complex structure limit, the holomorphic ambiguity is fixed by the constant map contribution: F1 = -1/24 log(Δ) + const, where Δ = 1-55 q is the discriminant. Expanding F1 = ∑d≥1 N1(d) q^d gives N1(1) = 2875/12? Actually the integer invariant n1 = 0 for the quintic; the genus-one Gromov–Witten invariant N1 = 0. The BCOV computation yields F1 = -1/24 log(1-55 q) + 5/12 log(q) + const, whose q-expansion has no q1 term, confirming N1 = 0.

Quantum Differential Equations and the Dubrovin Connection

The quantum differential equation (QDE) is the linear system ∇∂_a s = 0 for the Dubrovin connection ∇ = d + (1/z) (γ_a ⋆ dt^a) on the trivial bundle H^*(X) × ℂ[[z]] over the Kähler moduli space. Here γ_a is a basis of H^*(X), ⋆ is the quantum product, and z is the formal parameter. Flat sections of ∇ are of the form s(t,z) = et^a γ_a / z (1 + O(z)) and their asymptotics as z → 0 encode the Gromov–Witten invariants. The fundamental solution matrix S(t,z) satisfies S(t,z) = Ψ(t) R(t,z) eU(t)/z, where Ψ diagonalizes the quantum product, U is the diagonal matrix of canonical coordinates, and R(t,z) = 1 + R1(t) z + R2(t) z2 + … is the Givental R-matrix. The isomonodromic deformation equations for ∇ are equivalent to the WDVV equations. The Stokes phenomenon of the QDE at z = 0 and z = ∞ is governed by the central connection matrix, which in the Calabi–Yau case coincides with the matrix of BPS invariants. For Fano varieties, the QDE is regular singular at z = 0 and irregular at z = ∞; the asymptotic expansion of flat sections at z = ∞ yields the J-function J_X(t,z) = et^a γ_a / z (1 + ∑n≥1 ⟨γ_a/(z-ψ)⟩0,2 t^a / z + …). The quantum D-module is the ℂ[[z]]-module generated by the components of J_X; its holonomic rank equals dim H^*(X). The reconstruction theorem of Kontsevich–Manin states that the genus-zero Gromov–Witten potential is uniquely determined by the QDE together with the divisor and string equations.

Open Gromov–Witten Invariants and the Fukaya Category

Open Gromov–Witten invariants count holomorphic disks with boundary on a Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ X. The moduli space of stable maps from bordered Riemann surfaces carries a virtual fundamental class, and the resulting invariants depend on the relative spin structure of L. For a toric Calabi–Yau threefold, the Aganagic–Vafa brane construction places a Lagrangian torus fiber over an edge of the moment polytope; the open invariants are then computed by the topological vertex and coincide with the coefficients of the open mirror superpotential W(x) = ∑β n_β x∂β. The open mirror theorem of Chan–Lau–Leung–Tseng identifies these disk invariants with the periods of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model. In the categorical language, the Fukaya category Fuk(X) has objects given by Lagrangian branes and morphism spaces given by Floer cohomology. The open Gromov–Witten potential defines an A_∞-structure on Fuk(X), and the closed-open map sends the quantum cohomology ring QH^*(X) to the Hochschild cohomology HH^*(Fuk(X)). Homological mirror symmetry (Kontsevich, 1994) predicts an equivalence D^b Coh(Y) ≅ Fuk(X) for mirror pairs (X,Y). Under this equivalence, the open invariants correspond to Ext groups between structure sheaves of points on Y, and the disk potential becomes the generating function of open B-model invariants. Recent work by Ganatra–Pardon–Shende proves a version of homological mirror symmetry for Weinstein manifolds, providing a rigorous foundation for the open/closed correspondence.

Gopakumar–Vafa Invariants and the Donaldson–Thomas/GW Correspondence

The Gromov–Witten invariants Ng,β are rational numbers due to multiple covers and automorphisms of stable maps. Gopakumar and Vafa proposed in 1998 that there exist integer invariants ng,β (the BPS invariants) such that ∑g≥0 Ng,β λ2g-2 = ∑g≥0 ng,β (2 sin(λ/2))2g-2. The ng,β count BPS states in the topological string and are conjectured to be the Euler characteristics of the moduli spaces of D-branes. For Calabi–Yau threefolds, the genus-zero GV invariants n0,β coincide with the instanton numbers N_β. The higher-genus GV invariants have been computed for many examples using the BCOV holomorphic anomaly and the topological vertex. The Donaldson–Thomas (DT) invariants DTβ count ideal sheaves of curves in X and are integers by definition. The DT/GW correspondence, proved by Maulik–Nekrasov–Okounkov–Pandharipande for toric threefolds and by Pandharipande–Pixton for local curves, states that the generating series of DT invariants and GW invariants are related by a change of variables: ZDT(q) = ZGW(u) with q = -eiu. This equality implies the integrality of the GW invariants after the GV transformation and provides a powerful computational bridge between the two theories. The correspondence has been extended to the equivariant setting and to the relative theory, leading to the proof of the GW/PT correspondence for stable pairs by Bridgeland and by Toda.

Quantum K‑Theory and the Quantum Difference Equation

Quantum K‑theory replaces the cohomological insertion of descendant classes by K‑theoretic push‑forwards of structure sheaves on the moduli of stable maps. For a smooth projective variety X, the genus‑zero quantum K‑theory ring QK(X) is a deformation of the ordinary K‑ring K(X) whose structure constants are the K‑theoretic Gromov–Witten invariants ⟨𝒪γ₁, …, 𝒪γₙ⟩0,n,β. The deformation parameter is the Novikov variable q^β, and the product is defined by the generating series

𝒪_α ⋆ 𝒪_β = ∑γ,β q^β ⟨𝒪_α, 𝒪_β, 𝒪^γ⟩0,3,β 𝒪_γ,

where {𝒪_α} is a basis of K(X) and 𝒪^γ the dual basis under the Euler pairing. The associativity of ⋆ is encoded in the quantum difference equation (QDE) satisfied by the K‑theoretic J‑function

J_X(t,q,z) = 𝒪0 + ∑β≠0 q^β ev1*bigl((𝒪vir)/(1 - z L1)bigr) et·β,

where L1 is the cotangent line bundle at the first marked point. The QDE takes the form

(Δ_q - M(t,q)) J_X = 0,

with Δ_q the q‑difference operator (Δ_q f)(q) = f(q q^β) and M(t,q) a matrix of rational functions in z. When X is a toric variety, Givental–Lee proved that the QDE coincides with the quantum differential equation of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model after the change of variables z = e^ℏ. This identification underlies the K‑theoretic mirror theorem of Givental and of Iritani–Milanov–Tonita. For flag varieties, the quantum K‑ring is isomorphic to the equivariant K‑theory of the Peterson variety, and the QDE reduces to the quantum Toda lattice. The quantum difference equation also governs the wall‑crossing of stability conditions on the derived category D^b Coh(X), linking quantum K‑theory to Bridgeland stability.

The Crepant Resolution Conjecture and Quantum Cohomology of Orbifolds

Let 𝒳 be a smooth Deligne–Mumford stack with coarse moduli space X, and let π: Y → X be a crepant resolution. The crepant resolution conjecture (CRC) of Ruan predicts that the quantum cohomology rings QH^*(𝒳) and QH^*(Y) become isomorphic after analytic continuation in the quantum parameters and a suitable specialization of the equivariant parameters. In the toric setting, 𝒳 = [ℂ^d / G] for a finite abelian group G ⊂ SL(d,ℂ), and Y is the G‑Hilbert scheme. The quantum cohomology of 𝒳 is computed by the orbifold Gromov–Witten invariants ⟨τk₁(γ₁) … τkₙ(γₙ)⟩0,n,β𝒳, where γ_i lie in the inertia stack I𝒳. The CRC asserts that the genus‑zero potentials F0𝒳 and F0^Y satisfy

F0𝒳(t, q) = F0^Y(t', q') + quadratic terms,

after the change of variables q_i = ε_i q_i' with ε_i roots of unity determined by the age grading. For the An-1 surface singularity ℂ2/ℤ_n, the crepant resolution is the minimal resolution Y with exceptional curves E1,…,En-1. The orbifold quantum product on H^*CR(𝒳) is semisimple, and its Stokes matrix coincides with the intersection matrix of the exceptional curves. The conjecture has been proved for all toric Calabi–Yau threefolds by Coates–Corti–Iritani–Tseng using the quantum Lefschetz hyperplane theorem and the mirror theorem for toric stacks. In the non‑toric case, the CRC remains open; recent work of Gonzalez–Woodward relates it to the wall‑crossing of quasimap invariants. The CRC also implies an equivalence of the associated Frobenius manifolds, so the Dubrovin connections of 𝒳 and Y are gauge‑equivalent after analytic continuation.

Worked Example: Crepant Resolution of ℂ²/ℤ₃
Compute the orbifold quantum product of the two non‑trivial twisted sectors in 𝒳 = [ℂ²/ℤ₃] and verify that it matches the quantum product of the exceptional divisors in the minimal resolution Y after the change of variables q = -q'.
The inertia stack I𝒳 has components indexed by g ∈ ℤ₃. The non‑trivial sectors correspond to g = 1,2 with age = 1. Their classes are 1_g ∈ H2(𝒳). The orbifold three‑point invariant ⟨11, 11, 12⟩0,3,β𝒳 is non‑zero only for β = 0, giving 1/3. Hence 11 ⋆ 11 = (1/3) 12. In Y, the exceptional divisors E1, E2 satisfy E1·E1 = -2, E1·E2 = 1. The quantum product in QH^*(Y) is E1 ⋆ E1 = -2 E1 + E2 + q' E2. Setting q = -q' and identifying 11 E1, 12 E2 yields 11 ⋆ 11 = (1/3) 12 after rescaling the basis by √3. This matches the CRC prediction.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the crepant resolution conjecture, first state the precise analytic continuation in the Novikov variables and the root‑of‑unity specialization. Then recall the toric Calabi–Yau threefold case where the theorem is proved (Coates–Corti–Iritani–Tseng). For non‑toric examples, mention the role of quasimap wall‑crossing. A typical trap is to confuse the age shift with the degree shift; the age appears in the grading of the inertia stack, not in the curve class.

The Gamma Conjecture and Integral Structures in Quantum Cohomology

The Gamma conjecture, formulated by Galkin–Golyshev–Iritani, relates the asymptotic behaviour of the quantum cohomology Frobenius manifold at the large radius limit to the Gamma class of the variety. For a smooth projective variety X, the Gamma class is defined as

Γ_X = ∏i=1dim X Γ(1 + δ_i),

where δ_i are the Chern roots of the tangent bundle. The conjecture has two parts. Gamma Conjecture I states that the central charge Z_X(E) = ∫_X e-c1(X)/2 Γ_X ch(E) for an object E ∈ D^b Coh(X) coincides with the asymptotic value of the flat section of the Dubrovin connection corresponding to E under the mirror map. Gamma Conjecture II asserts that the Stokes matrix of the Dubrovin connection at the irregular singular point z = ∞ equals the Euler pairing matrix χ(E_i, E_j) of an exceptional collection {E_i} in D^b Coh(X). For Fano varieties, the quantum differential equation is regular singular at z = 0 and irregular at z = ∞; the asymptotic expansion of the J‑function at z = ∞ involves the Gamma class. The conjecture has been verified for projective spaces, Grassmannians, flag varieties, and toric Fano varieties using the mirror theorem and the theory of oscillatory integrals. In the Calabi–Yau case, the Gamma class reduces to the Todd class, and the conjecture relates the monodromy of the Picard–Fuchs equation to the derived category. Recent work of Iritani–Katzarkov–Kontsevich–Pantev extends the Gamma conjecture to the categorical setting, predicting an equivalence between the Fukaya category of the mirror and the category of matrix factorizations twisted by the Gamma class.

The Quantum Lefschetz Hyperplane Theorem and Applications

The quantum Lefschetz theorem computes the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants of a complete intersection Y ⊂ X in terms of the invariants of the ambient variety X and the Euler class of the vector bundle defining Y. Let X be a smooth projective variety and E = ⊕j=1^r L_j a convex vector bundle (i.e., H1(C, f^*E) = 0 for all stable maps f: C → X). Let Y be the zero locus of a regular section of E. Then the virtual fundamental class of the moduli space of stable maps to Y satisfies

[M0,n(Y,β)]vir = i^* [M0,n(X,β)]vir ∩ e(E0,n,β),

where i: M0,n(Y,β) → M0,n(X,β) is the inclusion and E0,n,β = π_* ev^* E is the obstruction bundle. The quantum Lefschetz hyperplane theorem states that the J‑function of Y is obtained from the J‑function of X by the hypergeometric modification

J_Y(t,z) = Bigl(∏j=1^r ∏m=1c1(L_j)·β (c1(L_j) + m z)Bigr) J_X(t,z) Big|q^β → q^β ∏j (-1)c1(L_j)·β.

This formula was proved by Coates–Givental for toric varieties and by Kim–Lho–Lee for general convex bundles using the cosection localization technique. Applications include the computation of quantum cohomology of hypersurfaces in projective spaces (the mirror theorem for quintic threefolds), the verification of the crepant resolution conjecture for toric Calabi–Yau threefolds, and the derivation of the quantum differential equation for Grassmannians via the Plücker embedding. The theorem also underlies the quantum Serre duality, which relates the J‑function of a variety to that of its anti‑canonical bundle.

The Dubrovin–Zhang Hierarchy and Integrable Systems

The genus‑zero quantum cohomology of a variety X defines a Frobenius manifold M_X. Dubrovin and Zhang constructed a hierarchy of Hamiltonian evolutionary PDEs (the Dubrovin–Zhang hierarchy) whose dispersionless limit is the principal hierarchy of M_X. The dependent variables are the flat coordinates t^α of the Frobenius manifold, and the flows are generated by the Hamiltonians

Hα,p = ∫ (δα,p)/(δ u^β) u^β dx,

where u^α = ∂2 F0 / ∂ t1 ∂ t^α are the canonical coordinates. The hierarchy is bihamiltonian, with the first Poisson bracket given by the intersection form ηαβ and the second by the Dubrovin–Novikov bracket. The tau‑function of the hierarchy coincides with the total descendant potential of the Gromov–Witten theory after the quantization of Givental. For X = ℙ1, the hierarchy reduces to the KdV hierarchy; for X = ℙ2, it yields the 2‑component Toda lattice. The Virasoro constraints for Gromov–Witten invariants are equivalent to the statement that the tau‑function is annihilated by the Virasoro operators L_n = ∑k≥0 (k+½) tk+1 ∂/∂ t^k + ¼ ∑a+b=n-1 ∂2/∂ t^a ∂ t^b. The Dubrovin–Zhang hierarchy provides a systematic way to compute higher‑genus invariants from the genus‑zero data via the topological recursion relations. Recent work of Buryak–Rossi–Shadrin relates the hierarchy to the double ramification cycle, giving a geometric interpretation of the Hamiltonian densities.

The Virasoro Constraints and Topological Recursion in Gromov–Witten Theory

The Virasoro constraints form an infinite set of linear differential equations satisfied by the total descendant potential of a target variety X. They were first conjectured by Eguchi, Hori and Xiong for ℙⁿ and later proved by Givental for all toric varieties using his quantization formalism. The constraints are expressed as Lₖ Z = 0 for k ≥ −1, where the operators Lₖ are quadratic differential operators in the formal variables tₖ^α (the couplings to gravitational descendants). Explicitly,

Lₖ = ∑m≥0 (m+½) tm+1^α ∂/∂t_m^α + ¼ ∑a+b=k−1 ηαβ ∂²/∂t_a^α ∂t_b^β + (δk,−1/2) ηαβ t0^α t0^β

with ηαβ the Poincaré pairing. The L₋₁ constraint encodes the string equation, L₀ the dilaton equation, and higher Lₖ generate the full Virasoro algebra. These constraints are equivalent to the statement that the total descendant potential is a tau‑function of the KdV hierarchy (for X = ℙ¹) or of the appropriate Dubrovin–Zhang hierarchy for general X.

Topological recursion, developed by Eynard and Orantin, provides a universal mechanism to compute all higher‑genus invariants from the genus‑zero spectral curve. In Gromov–Witten theory the spectral curve is the quantum differential equation (the Dubrovin connection) together with the genus‑zero three‑point functions. The recursion kernel is built from the Bergman kernel on the spectral curve, and the output matches the Givental quantization of the genus‑zero data. For toric Calabi–Yau threefolds the spectral curve coincides with the mirror curve, and the Eynard–Orantin invariants reproduce the B‑model topological string amplitudes, thereby giving a geometric proof of the remodeling conjecture.

The interplay between Virasoro constraints and topological recursion is subtle: the Virasoro operators annihilate the partition function, while the topological recursion constructs it order by order in the genus expansion. Recent work of Bouchard, Klemm, Mariño and Pasquetti shows that the Virasoro constraints are a consequence of the loop equations of the matrix model underlying the topological recursion. This connection explains why the Virasoro constraints hold for a wide class of theories beyond Gromov–Witten invariants, including Hurwitz numbers and intersection theory on Mg,n.

Quantum Cohomology of Flag Varieties and the Peterson Variety

The (small) quantum cohomology ring QH^*(G/P) of a generalized flag variety G/P (with G a complex semisimple Lie group and P a parabolic subgroup) admits a remarkable presentation due to Peterson and later refined by Rietsch. Let W be the Weyl group and W^P the set of minimal coset representatives. The classical cohomology has a basis of Schubert classes σ_w indexed by w ∈ W^P. The quantum product deforms the classical cup product by adding terms weighted by powers of the quantum parameters q_α corresponding to the simple roots not in the Levi of P.

Peterson’s theorem identifies QH^*(G/P) with the coordinate ring of the Peterson variety Y_P ⊂ G/B, a subvariety of the full flag variety defined by the condition that a regular nilpotent element lies in the tangent space of the opposite Schubert cell. Concretely, if e is a principal nilpotent and h the corresponding semisimple element, then Y_P = { gB ∈ G/B | Ad(g-1) e ∈ Lie(P) + Lie(U) }. The isomorphism sends a Schubert class σ_w to the restriction of the corresponding Schubert function on G/B to Y_P. The quantum parameters appear as the coordinates on the torus factor of the Peterson variety.

This description yields explicit structure constants: the quantum Littlewood–Richardson coefficients cu,vw,d are given by the intersection numbers of certain Schubert varieties inside Y_P. In type A (Grassmannians and complete flag varieties) the Peterson variety is isomorphic to the Hessenberg variety associated to the nilpotent operator, and the quantum product coincides with the quantum Schubert calculus developed by Bertram, Buch, Kresch and Tamvakis. The quantum Giambelli formula expresses any quantum Schubert class as a determinant of quantum special Schubert classes, mirroring the classical Giambelli formula but with quantum corrections encoded by the quantum Pieri rule.

Applications include the computation of the quantum cohomology of cominuscule varieties (Grassmannians, quadrics, Lagrangian Grassmannians, spinor varieties) where the Peterson variety is a toric variety, making the quantum product accessible via the mirror theorem of Givental. For non‑cominuscule flag varieties the Peterson variety is not toric, but the isomorphism still provides a powerful tool for proving associativity and for deriving the quantum differential equation (the Dubrovin connection) in terms of the Toda lattice associated to the Langlands dual group.

Technique: Using the Peterson Isomorphism to Compute a Quantum Product
Let X = Fl(ℂ³) be the complete flag variety of type A₂. Its quantum cohomology has two quantum parameters q₁, q₂ corresponding to the simple roots. Compute the quantum product σs₁ ⋆ σs₂ where s₁, s₂ are the simple reflections.
In type A₂ the Peterson variety Y is the subvariety of G/B defined by Ad(g-1) e ∈ Lie(B) + Lie(U). The Schubert classes σs₁ and σs₂ restrict to the coordinate functions x₁, x₂ on the torus factor of Y. The quantum product corresponds to multiplication in the coordinate ring: σs₁ ⋆ σs₂ = σs₁s₂ + q₁ q₂ σid. The term σs₁s₂ is the classical product (the Schubert class of the longest element), and the quantum correction q₁ q₂ σid arises because the curve class β = α₁^∨ + α₂^∨ has degree one in each quantum parameter. This matches the quantum Pieri rule for Fl(ℂ³).

The Landau–Ginzburg/Calabi–Yau Correspondence and Quantum Cohomology

The Landau–Ginzburg/Calabi–Yau (LG/CY) correspondence relates the Gromov–Witten theory of a Calabi–Yau hypersurface Y in a toric variety X to the Fan–Jarvis–Ruan–Witten (FJRW) theory of the associated Landau–Ginzburg model (W, G). Here W is a quasihomogeneous polynomial (the superpotential) and G a finite group of diagonal symmetries. The correspondence was conjectured by Hori and Vafa and proved in genus zero by Chiodo, Iritani, Ruan and others using the quantum Lefschetz theorem and the theory of I‑functions.

On the A‑model side, the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants of Y are encoded in the J‑function J_Y(t, z). On the B‑model side, the FJRW invariants produce an I‑function IW,G(t, z) which satisfies the same quantum differential equation (the Picard–Fuchs equation) after a change of variables known as the mirror map. The LG/CY correspondence asserts that the two theories are equivalent under analytic continuation in the Kähler moduli space: the large‑radius limit of Y corresponds to the orbifold point of the LG model, and the conifold point corresponds to the large‑radius limit of the LG model.

Iritani’s integral structure provides a precise match between the Gamma class Γ̂_Y of the Calabi–Yau and the Gamma class of the LG model. The Gamma class appears in the asymptotic expansion of the J‑function and in the central charge formula for D‑branes. The correspondence extends to higher genus via the BCOV holomorphic anomaly equations on the Calabi–Yau side and the Givental quantization on the LG side. Recent work of Fan, Jarvis and Ruan establishes the all‑genus correspondence for quintic threefolds, confirming that the total ancestor potentials coincide after the appropriate symplectic transformation.

Applications include the computation of Gromov–Witten invariants of Calabi–Yau complete intersections in toric varieties where the mirror theorem is not directly available, and the derivation of the Crepant Resolution Conjecture for orbifold quotients of Calabi–Yau varieties. The LG/CY correspondence also underlies the Gamma Conjecture of Gamma class integral structures, linking the asymptotics of the quantum differential equation to the topology of the derived category of coherent sheaves.

Quantum Schubert Calculus on Grassmannians and the Quantum Giambelli Formula

The quantum cohomology ring QH^*(Gr(k, n)) of the Grassmannian of k-planes in ℂⁿ is a deformation of the classical Schubert calculus. The classical ring is generated by the special Schubert classes σ₁, …, σn−k (Chern classes of the dual tautological bundle) subject to the Giambelli determinant formula σ_λ = det(σλ_i + j − i). The quantum ring introduces a single quantum parameter q (since Pic(Gr(k, n)) ≅ ℤ) and the relation σn−k+1 = q (with appropriate indexing).

The quantum Giambelli formula, proved by Bertram, Buch, Kresch and Tamvakis, states that any quantum Schubert class σ_λ can be expressed as the same determinant but with the quantum special Schubert classes σ_r (where σ_r = 0 for r > n−k and σn−k+1 = q). Explicitly, σ_λ = det(σλ_i + j − i) where the entries are interpreted in the quantum ring. This yields a completely algorithmic way to compute any quantum product: expand the determinant, replace powers of σn−k+1 by q, and reduce using the quantum Pieri rule σ_λ ⋆ σ_r = ∑ σ_μ + q ∑ σ_ν where the sums run over partitions obtained by adding r boxes with no two in the same column (classical part) and removing a full column of height k (quantum part).

The quantum Littlewood–Richardson coefficients cλ,μν,d count rational curves of degree d meeting three Schubert varieties in general position. They satisfy the symmetry cλ,μν,d = cλ^∨,μ^∨ν^∨,d where λ^∨ is the complement partition inside the k × (n−k) rectangle. The associativity of the quantum product is equivalent to the quantum Plücker relations, which are quadratic relations among the quantum minors of a k × n matrix of formal variables. These relations are precisely the exchange relations in the quantum cluster algebra structure on QH^*(Gr(k, n)) discovered by Geiss, Leclerc and Schröer.

Applications include the verification of the Peterson isomorphism for Grassmannians (the Peterson variety is the toric variety of the Gelfand–Tsetlin polytope), the computation of the quantum differential equation (the q‑difference equation for the J‑function), and the proof of the Gamma Conjecture for Grassmannians via the identification of the Gamma class with the product of Γ(1 + Chern roots). The quantum Schubert calculus also provides the enumerative geometry behind the quantum Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov equations for the GL_n quantum group.

  • Confusing the quantum parameter q in Grassmannians with the multiple parameters in partial flag varieties; Grassmannians have Picard rank one, so only one q appears.
  • Applying the classical Giambelli determinant without the quantum correction σn−k+1 = q; the quantum determinant must use the quantum special Schubert classes.
  • Neglecting the quantum Pieri rule’s column‑removal term; the quantum correction corresponds to adding a full column of height k and multiplying by q.
  • Assuming the quantum Littlewood–Richardson coefficients are always positive integers without checking the degree d; they vanish unless the degree matches the size difference of the partitions.
  • Mixing the quantum cluster algebra exchange relations with the classical Plücker relations; the quantum relations involve the parameter q and are not homogeneous in the classical grading.
  • Overlooking the role of the Gamma class in the asymptotic expansion of the J‑function; the Gamma class is not the Todd class and must be computed using the full Γ-function of Chern roots.
  • Review these traps before the exam.

Quantum Cohomology of Toric Deligne–Mumford Stacks and the Extended I‑Function

The quantum cohomology of a smooth toric Deligne–Mumford stack X is governed by an extended I‑function that incorporates both the stacky fan data and the age‑shifted cohomology classes of the inertia stack. For a stack presented as a GIT quotient [V / G] with G a product of tori and finite abelian groups, the I‑function takes the form

IX(z, q) = z ∑d ∈ Eff(X) qd ∏i=1m ∏k=0⟨Di, d⟩−1 (Di + (k + agei)z) / ∏k=1⟨Di, d⟩ (Di + k z)

where Di are the divisor classes corresponding to the stacky divisors, agei are the age shifts coming from the isotropy groups, and Eff(X) is the effective cone of curve classes. The mirror theorem for toric stacks (Coates–Corti–Iritani–Tseng) identifies this I‑function with the J‑function of X after a change of variables q = Q exp(τ) and a symplectic transformation determined by the fundamental solution of the quantum differential equation. The extended I‑function satisfies the quantum differential equation ∇z I = 0 with the Dubrovin connection ∇z = z ∂/∂τ + (τ ⋆) / z, where ⋆ denotes the quantum product. The coefficients of the I‑function expansion in powers of z⁻¹ give the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants with arbitrary insertions of divisor and age‑shifted classes. In particular, the small J‑function is recovered by restricting to the narrow sector (age = 0) and setting all non‑divisor insertions to zero. The extended I‑function also encodes the orbifold quantum product on the inertia stack IX, where the product of two twisted sectors is computed by the three‑point function with a degree‑zero map and the appropriate age grading. This structure underlies the Crepant Transformation Conjecture for toric stacks, predicting that the quantum cohomology rings of crepant resolutions are related by analytic continuation in the quantum parameters and a linear isomorphism preserving the Poincaré pairing.

Quantum Cohomology of Nakajima Quiver Varieties and the Stable Envelope Construction

Nakajima quiver varieties M arise as hyperkähler quotients of representation spaces of a quiver with a stability condition. Their equivariant quantum cohomology QHT⁎(M) carries a rich algebraic structure that can be described using Maulik–Okounkov stable envelopes. For a torus T acting on M with isolated fixed points, the stable envelope Stab𝔠 associated to a chamber 𝔠 in the Lie algebra of T provides a basis of HT⁎(M) indexed by the fixed points. The quantum product in this basis is given by the formula

Stab𝔠(p) ⋆ Stab𝔠(q) = ∑r Cpqr(q) Stab𝔠(r)

where the structure constants Cpqr(q) are rational functions in the equivariant parameters and the quantum parameters q. They are computed by counting rational curves in M with specified boundary conditions at the fixed points, which reduces to a sum over certain attracting manifolds. The stable envelope basis diagonalizes the quantum Hamiltonians coming from the quantum differential equation, and the eigenvalues are precisely the Bethe roots of the associated quantum integrable system (the XXZ spin chain for type A quivers). The quantum differential equation ∇z Ψ = 0 with ∇z = z ∂/∂τ + (τ ⋆)/z admits a fundamental solution matrix whose columns are the stable envelopes evaluated at the Bethe roots. This identifies the quantum cohomology ring with the representation ring of the quantum group Uq(𝔤) where 𝔤 is the Kac–Moody algebra of the quiver. The wall‑crossing formulas for stable envelopes as the chamber 𝔠 varies reproduce the R‑matrix of the quantum group, and the monodromy of the quantum differential equation around q = 0 and q = ∞ yields the braid group action on the category of representations. Applications include the computation of the quantum cohomology of Hilbert schemes of points on ℂ² (the Jordan quiver) and of hypertoric varieties (abelian quivers), as well as the proof of the 3‑dimensional mirror symmetry conjecture for quiver varieties via the isomorphism of quantum cohomology rings of dual varieties.

Worked Example: Small J‑Function of a Toric Stack
Let X = [ℂ³ / μ₃] where μ₃ acts with weights (1,1,1). Compute the small J‑function JX(τ, z) up to order q² using the extended I‑function.
The stacky fan has one divisor D with age shift age = 1/3 for the non‑trivial sector. The effective cone is generated by the class d with ⟨D, d⟩ = 1. The extended I‑function is
I(z, q) = z ∑d≥0 qd ∏k=0d−1 (D + (k + 1/3)z) / ∏k=1d (D + k z).
Expanding: for d = 0 the term is z. For d = 1 we get q z (D + 1/3 z) / (D + z). For d = 2 we get q² z (D + 1/3 z)(D + 4/3 z) / ((D + z)(D + 2z)). The mirror map is τ = log q + O(q); to the required order we may set q = eτ. The small J‑function is the component of I along the narrow sector (age = 0), i.e. the coefficient of 1 in the expansion in D. Since D³ = 0 in the Chen–Ruan cohomology, we keep terms up to D². After extracting the coefficient of 1 and dividing by z we obtain
JX(τ, z) = 1 + (eτ/z) (1/3) + (e2τ/z²) (1/9) + O(e3τ).
This matches the known genus‑zero invariants: ⟨τ₀(1)⟩0,1 = 1/3, ⟨τ₀(1)⟩0,2 = 1/9.

Quantum Cohomology of Hilbert Schemes of Points on Surfaces and the Lehn–Sorger Quantum Product

For a smooth projective surface S, the Hilbert scheme of n points S[n] is a smooth symplectic resolution of the symmetric product S(n). Its quantum cohomology QH⁎(S[n]) can be described using the Nakajima basis of the cohomology ring, which is indexed by partitions of n and cohomology classes of S. Lehn and Sorger gave an explicit formula for the quantum product of two divisor classes D₁, D₂ ∈ H²(S) lifted to S[n] via the tautological bundles. The quantum product is

D₁ ⋆ D₂ = D₁ ∪ D₂ + ∑d>0 qd ⟨D₁, D₂, Δd⟩0,3,d

where Δd is the class of the diagonal in S[d] under the natural identification of the exceptional divisor of the Hilbert–Chow morphism. The three‑point invariants ⟨D₁, D₂, Δd⟩0,3,d are computed by localization on the torus‑fixed loci of S[n], which are products of Hilbert schemes of points on the toric chart of S. The result is a universal polynomial in the intersection numbers D₁·D₂, D₁·KS, D₂·KS and the Chern numbers c₁(S)², c₂(S). For example, when S = ℙ² the quantum product of the hyperplane class H with itself is

H ⋆ H = H² + q (H² + 3 H·K + 3 K²) + O(q²)

with K = −3H. The full quantum ring is generated by the divisor classes and the Nakajima operators p−k(α) acting on the Fock space ⊕n H⁎(S[n]). The quantum product corresponds to the normally ordered product of the associated Heisenberg algebra generators, and the WDVV equations are equivalent to the commutation relations of the quantum Heisenberg algebra. The quantum differential equation for S[n] is a system of q‑difference equations whose solutions are given by the vertex operator realization of the J‑function. This structure underlies the Maulik–Okounkov stable envelope description for the Hilbert scheme of points on ℂ² and provides a geometric proof of the AGT correspondence for pure SU(2) gauge theory.

Quantum Cohomology of Hypertoric Varieties and the Abelian–Nonabelian Correspondence

Hypertoric varieties Y are hyperkähler analogues of toric varieties, constructed as hyperkähler quotients of a vector space by a torus action with a generic moment map. Their quantum cohomology QH⁎(Y) is governed by an abelian–nonabelian correspondence that relates the quantum product on Y to the quantum product on an associated nonabelian quotient, typically a Nakajima quiver variety. The I‑function of a hypertoric variety takes the form

IY(z, q) = z ∑d ∈ Eff(Y) qd ∏i=1N ∏k=0⟨Di, d⟩−1 (Di + k z) / ∏k=1⟨Di, d⟩ (Di + k z)

where the product runs over the hypertoric divisors Di. The mirror theorem for hypertoric varieties (Braverman–Finkelberg–Nakajima) identifies this I‑function with the J‑function after a change of variables that involves the solutions of the quantum differential equation of the associated nonabelian quotient. The quantum cohomology ring QH⁎(Y) is isomorphic to the subring of QH⁎(M) generated by the divisor classes, where M is the nonabelian quotient. This isomorphism respects the quantum product and the Poincaré pairing, and it is induced by the natural map on cohomology coming from the abelianization procedure. The quantum parameters of Y correspond to the Kähler parameters of M under the identification of the effective cones. The WDVV equations for Y follow from those for M by restriction. The Gamma class of Y appears in the asymptotic expansion of the J‑function and matches the Gamma class of M under the isomorphism, providing evidence for the Gamma conjecture in the hypertoric setting. Applications include the computation of Gromov–Witten invariants of hypertoric varieties via the known quantum cohomology of quiver varieties, and the derivation of the crepant resolution conjecture for hypertoric varieties as a special case of the abelian–nonabelian correspondence.

  • Neglecting the contribution of the diagonal class Δd in the Lehn–Sorger formula; it encodes the quantum corrections coming from curves in the exceptional divisor.
  • Treating the abelian–nonabelian correspondence as an equality of full quantum cohomology rings; it is an isomorphism only after restricting to the divisor‑generated subring.

  • Mixing the quantum parameters of the hypertoric variety with those of the associated quiver variety without applying the correct linear transformation dictated by the abelianization map.
  • Forgetting that the quantum differential equation for Hilbert schemes of points is a q‑difference equation, not a differential equation in the usual sense; the step size is determined by the equivariant parameters.

Equivariant Quantum Cohomology and the Quantum Kirwan Map

Equivariant quantum cohomology extends the ordinary quantum product by incorporating a torus action on the target variety X. Let T = (C*)^r act on X with isolated fixed points. The equivariant quantum cohomology ring QH_T^*(X) is a module over the equivariant parameter ring Λ = H_T^*(pt) ≅ C[λ1,…,λ_r]. The quantum product ⋆_q deforms the equivariant cup product by genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants with insertions of equivariant cohomology classes. The structure constants are rational functions in the equivariant parameters and the quantum parameters q^d, d ∈ Eff(X). The quantum Kirwan map κ_Q : QH_T^*(X) → QH^*(X//T) relates the equivariant quantum cohomology of X to the ordinary quantum cohomology of the symplectic (or GIT) quotient X//T. It is defined by first restricting to the fixed‑point locus X^T, then applying the non‑equivariant limit λ_i → 0 after multiplying by the appropriate Euler classes of the normal bundles. The map respects the quantum product: κ_Q(α ⋆_q β) = κ_Q(α) ⋆ κ_Q(β) in QH^*(X//T). This property was proved by Martin, Woodward, and others using the abelian/non‑abelian correspondence and the quantum Martin formula. The quantum Kirwan map is a powerful computational tool: it reduces the calculation of quantum products on non‑abelian quotients (e.g., Grassmannians, flag varieties) to the better‑understood equivariant quantum cohomology of the abelian quotient (a product of projective spaces). In practice one computes the equivariant J‑function J_X^T(t,z) via localization, applies the quantum Kirwan map to obtain JX//T(t,z), and then extracts the quantum product via the Dubrovin connection. The map also intertwines the equivariant and non‑equivariant WDVV equations, providing a conceptual proof that the quotient inherits a Frobenius manifold structure.

Key technical points: the fixed‑point contributions involve the inverse of the equivariant Euler class e_T(NF/X), which is a product of weights λ_i + …; the quantum parameters of X//T are identified with a subset of the quantum parameters of X under the map on effective cones induced by the inclusion of the moment polytope. The quantum Kirwan map is not an isomorphism in general; its kernel is generated by the quantum Stanley–Reisner relations coming from the unstable locus. The image is the subring of QH^*(X//T) generated by divisor classes pulled back from X. For Grassmannians Gr(k,n) = (C^n)^k // GL(k), the quantum Kirwan map recovers the quantum Giambelli formula and the quantum Pieri rule. For partial flag varieties, it yields the quantum Chevalley formula of Peterson and Woodward. The map also underlies the proof of the quantum Lefschetz hyperplane theorem for complete intersections in homogeneous spaces.

Hodge Integrals, the ELSV Formula, and Quantum Cohomology of a Point

The quantum cohomology of a point is trivial as a ring, but its genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants with ψ‑class insertions — the Hodge integrals ∫M̄g,n λg-1 ψ1k1 … ψ_nk_n — encode deep combinatorial structure. The celebrated ELSV formula (Ekedahl–Lando–Shapiro–Vainshtein) expresses the simple Hurwitz numbers hg,μ (covers of P1 with prescribed ramification profile μ over ∞ and simple ramification elsewhere) in terms of these Hodge integrals: hg,μ = (2g-2+|μ|+ℓ(μ))! / |Aut(μ)| ∏i=1ℓ(μ) μ_iμ_i / μ_i! ∫M̄g,ℓ(μ) λ_g / ∏i=1ℓ(μ) (1 - μ_i ψ_i). This identity bridges enumerative geometry of curves, representation theory of the symmetric group, and the topology of moduli spaces. In the language of quantum cohomology, the generating function of Hodge integrals is the total ancestor potential Apt of a point, which satisfies the Virasoro constraints and the KdV hierarchy (Witten–Kontsevich theorem). The ELSV formula can be derived by equating the localization computation of the equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants of P1 (with respect to the standard C* action) to the cut‑and‑join recursion for Hurwitz numbers. The quantum differential equation for a point is the Airy equation ∂2/∂t2 Φ = t Φ, whose solutions are the Airy functions; the J‑function is Jpt(t,z) = et/z ∑d≥0 t^d / (z^d d!). The Dubrovin connection is flat and its monodromy data reproduce the Stokes matrices of the Airy equation. The Gamma conjecture for a point reduces to the statement that the asymptotic expansion of the J‑function involves the Gamma function Γ(1 + t/z), which is exactly the content of the Stirling formula. Higher‑genus Gromov–Witten theory of a point is governed by the topological recursion of Eynard–Orantin applied to the Airy spectral curve y2 = x. The BCOV holomorphic anomaly equations in this case become the heat equation for the generating function of Hodge integrals. The ELSV formula also implies the Mariño–Vafa formula for the Gromov–Witten invariants of the resolved conifold, linking open and closed string invariants.

Worked Example: Computing a Hodge Integral via ELSV
Use the ELSV formula to compute the Hodge integral ∫M̄1,1 λ1 ψ1. The simple Hurwitz number h1,(2) counts genus‑1 covers of P1 with ramification profile (2) over ∞ and three simple branch points. The integral vanishes. This matches the known fact that λ1 = ψ1 on M̄1,1 and ∫ ψ12 = 0 for dimension reasons.

Quantum Cohomology of Moduli Spaces of Higgs Bundles and the Geometric Langlands Correspondence

The moduli space M_Higgs(G,C) of semistable Higgs bundles (E,φ) on a smooth projective curve C of genus g ≥ 2, with structure group a complex reductive group G, carries a natural hyperkähler structure. Its quantum cohomology QH^*(M_Higgs) is expected to be isomorphic, via the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence, to the quantum cohomology of the character variety M_B(G,C) = Hom(π1(C),G)//G. The geometric Langlands program predicts a deeper relationship: the category of D‑modules on Bun_G(C) (the moduli stack of G‑bundles) is equivalent to the category of coherent sheaves on the Hitchin base of M_Higgs(LG,C) for the Langlands dual group LG. In the quantum cohomology setting, this manifests as an isomorphism between the quantum connection (Dubrovin connection) on M_Higgs(G,C) and the quantum connection on M_Higgs(LG,C) after a suitable analytic continuation in the quantum parameters. The quantum parameters are the complexified Kähler classes coming from the ample line bundles on the moduli space, which correspond under the Hitchin fibration to the coordinates on the Hitchin base. The WDVV equations for M_Higgs are equivalent to the associativity of the quantum product defined by genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants with values in the cohomology of the moduli space. These invariants can be computed via localization with respect to the C* action scaling the Higgs field φ. The fixed‑point components are products of moduli spaces of bundles on C, and the virtual normal bundle contributions involve the weights of the C* action on the deformation complex. The resulting J‑function satisfies a system of quantum differential equations that coincide with the oper connection for LG‑opers on C, as conjectured by Frenkel–Gaitsgory and proved in special cases by Braverman–Finkelberg–Nakajima for G = GL_n. The Gamma class of M_Higgs appears in the asymptotic expansion of the J‑function and matches the Gamma class of the dual moduli space, providing a quantitative test of the Gamma conjecture in the non‑abelian setting. Applications include the computation of the quantum cohomology of the moduli space of rank‑2 Higgs bundles with fixed determinant, where the quantum product is expressed in terms of the Verlinde algebra and the quantum product on the corresponding character variety.

Concretely, the J‑function J_par(t,z) satisfies a q‑difference equation in the quantum parameters q_i = et_i (coming from the parabolic weights) and a differential equation in z. Quantum Cohomology of Logarithmic Gromov–Witten Theory and the Degeneration Formula

Logarithmic Gromov–Witten theory extends the usual stable‑map compactification by allowing target varieties with a normal‑crossing divisor and imposing tangency conditions along that divisor. The moduli space of logarithmic stable maps carries a virtual fundamental class constructed via the logarithmic obstruction theory of Chen and Gross–Siebert. The resulting logarithmic Gromov–Witten invariants satisfy a degeneration formula: when a smooth variety degenerates to a normal‑crossing union X₀ = X₁ ∪_D X₂, the absolute invariants of the general fiber are expressed as a sum over splittings of the curve class and insertions, weighted by relative invariants of the pairs (X₁,D) and (X₂,D) and glued by the logarithmic rubber calculus. This formula is the logarithmic analogue of the Li–Ruan and Ionel–Parker symplectic degeneration formulas, but it works entirely in the algebraic category and avoids the analytic gluing of pseudo‑holomorphic curves. The quantum product in the logarithmic quantum cohomology ring QH^*_log(X) is defined by three‑point logarithmic invariants with one insertion constrained to the divisor D; the associativity of this product follows from the logarithmic WDVV equations proved by Abramovich–Chen–Gross–Siebert. Applications include the computation of the quantum cohomology of toric varieties via the mirror theorem of Gross–Siebert, where the logarithmic structure on the toric boundary encodes the tropical counts that appear in the mirror Landau–Ginzburg potential.

The degeneration formula is implemented combinatorially by the expanded degeneration technique: one inserts chains of projective bundles over D to separate the components, and the logarithmic virtual class localizes to contributions from rubber components mapping to the rubber target P(ND/X₁ ⊕ O) ∪_D P(ND/X₂ ⊕ O). The gluing rule involves the inverse of the logarithmic Poincaré pairing on the relative insertions, which is computed by the topological recursion relations in the rubber geometry. This machinery yields a practical algorithm for computing genus‑zero logarithmic invariants of toric degenerations, and it underlies the proof of the mirror symmetry conjecture for log Calabi–Yau pairs by Bousseau, Brini, and van Garrel. In particular, the logarithmic quantum product on a toric variety X_Σ coincides with the quantum product defined by the I‑function of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model, and the logarithmic WDVV equations translate into the associativity of the Jacobian ring of the superpotential.

At higher genus, the logarithmic theory interacts with the holomorphic anomaly equation of Bershadsky–Cecotti–Ooguri–Vafa. The genus‑g logarithmic potentials satisfy a recursive anomaly equation whose boundary terms are expressed in terms of lower‑genus logarithmic invariants and the propagator on the tropical curve dual to the degeneration. This structure was used by Bousseau to prove the higher‑genus mirror symmetry for the resolved conifold and for local P², confirming that the logarithmic B‑model defined by the topological recursion on the mirror curve reproduces the logarithmic Gromov–Witten invariants. The logarithmic framework also clarifies the relationship between the relative and orbifold Gromov–Witten theories: the rubber calculus on the expanded degeneration is equivalent to the orbifold quantum cohomology of the root stack of X along D, as shown by Abramovich–Graber–Vistoli.

From an exam perspective, the key points to retain are: (1) the definition of logarithmic stable maps and their virtual class; (2) the statement of the degeneration formula as a sum over splittings with relative insertions; (3) the role of the rubber target and the logarithmic Poincaré pairing; (4) the equivalence between logarithmic quantum cohomology of toric varieties and the Jacobian ring of the mirror superpotential; (5) the higher‑genus holomorphic anomaly equation in the logarithmic setting. A typical trap is to confuse the logarithmic virtual class with the ordinary virtual class on the expanded degeneration; the former includes automorphisms of the logarithmic structure and yields the correct gluing rule without extra correction terms.

Quantum Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Curves and the Witten–Kontsevich Theorem

The moduli space of stable curves Mg,n carries a natural cohomological field theory given by the ψ‑classes ψ_i = c₁(L_i) where L_i is the cotangent line bundle at the i‑th marked point. The intersection numbers ⟨τd₁ … τd_n⟩_g = ∫[Mg,n]vir ψ₁d₁ … ψ_nd_n are the genus‑g Gromov–Witten invariants of a point. Witten conjectured, and Kontsevich proved, that the generating function of these intersection numbers satisfies the KdV hierarchy. In the language of quantum cohomology, the genus‑zero potential F₀(t) = Σn≥3 (1/n!) ⟨t … t⟩₀ defines a Frobenius manifold structure on H^*(pt) = C, which is trivial; the non‑trivial content appears at higher genus where the total potential F = Σg≥0 ħg-1 F_g satisfies the Virasoro constraints L_m Z = 0 for m ≥ -1, with Z = exp(F). These constraints are equivalent to the KdV hierarchy after the change of variables t_d = (2d+1)!! u_d.

Kontsevich’s proof uses a matrix model representation of the intersection numbers: the generating function is expressed as a formal integral over Hermitian matrices, and the asymptotic expansion of this integral yields the intersection numbers via Feynman diagrams that are ribbon graphs dual to stable curves. The matrix model satisfies the Virasoro constraints because of the invariance of the integral under changes of variables, and the KdV hierarchy follows from the string equation and the dilaton equation. An alternative proof by Okounkov and Pandharipande uses the Gromov–Witten theory of the target P¹ and the localization formula to relate the Hodge integrals on Mg,n to the Hurwitz numbers of P¹, then applies the ELSV formula to recover the KdV hierarchy. The quantum cohomology of a point is therefore the simplest example of a semi‑simple Frobenius manifold, and its higher‑genus potential is governed by the topological recursion of Eynard–Orantin on the spectral curve y² = x.

The Witten–Kontsevich theorem has several important generalizations. The r‑spin Witten conjecture (proved by Faber–Shadrin–Zvonkine) replaces the ψ‑classes by the r‑spin cohomological field theory, and the resulting hierarchy is the r‑KdV (or Gelfand–Dickey) hierarchy. The Fan–Jarvis–Ruan–Witten theory extends this to arbitrary quasihomogeneous singularities, where the quantum cohomology of the singularity (the Saito Frobenius manifold) is identified with the A‑model of the corresponding Landau–Ginzburg orbifold. In all these cases, the genus‑zero data define a Frobenius manifold, and the higher‑genus potentials are reconstructed by the Givental quantization of the symplectic loop space, which yields the total ancestor potential satisfying the Virasoro constraints. The topological recursion on the spectral curve provides a universal algorithm for computing the higher‑genus invariants once the genus‑zero data (the Frobenius manifold and the calibration) are known.

For examinations, the essential facts are: (1) the definition of ψ‑classes and the intersection numbers on Mg,n; (2) the statement of the Witten conjecture and its equivalence to the KdV hierarchy; (3) Kontsevich’s matrix model proof and the role of ribbon graphs; (4) the Virasoro constraints on the total potential and their relation to the string and dilaton equations; (5) the generalizations to r‑spin and FJRW theory. A common mistake is to claim that the KdV hierarchy governs the genus‑zero potential of a point; in fact the genus‑zero potential is a cubic polynomial and the KdV flow appears only after including higher‑genus corrections via the total potential Z.

Quantum Cohomology of the Hilbert Scheme of Points on C² and the Nekrasov Partition Function

The Hilbert scheme of n points on the complex plane, Hilbⁿ(C²), is a smooth symplectic resolution of the symmetric product Symⁿ(C²). Its equivariant quantum cohomology with respect to the torus T = (C*)² acting on C² with weights ε₁, ε₂ has been computed by Maulik–Okounkov and by Oblomkov–Okounkov–Pandharipande. The fixed points of the T‑action are indexed by partitions λ of n, and the equivariant quantum product is diagonalized by the basis of stable envelope classes constructed from the attracting manifolds of the fixed points. The quantum product by a divisor class corresponds to multiplication by the eigenvalue of the quantum Hamiltonian, which is the Calogero–Moser integrable system. The generating function of the equivariant quantum cohomology, summed over all n with a fugacity q, coincides with the Nekrasov instanton partition function of the pure N=2 supersymmetric gauge theory with gauge group U(1) in the Ω‑background. This identification is a concrete instance of the AGT correspondence for the simplest gauge group.

More precisely, the equivariant J‑function of Hilbⁿ(C²) satisfies a quantum differential equation which is the bispectral problem for the quantum Calogero–Moser system. The eigenvalues of the quantum Hamiltonians are given by the equivariant Chern characters of the tautological bundle, and the Bethe ansatz equations for the Calogero–Moser system become the quantum cohomology relations. The Nekrasov partition function Z_Nek(q, ε₁, ε₂, a) = Σλ q|λ| ∏s∈λ 1/(E(s)(E(s)+ε₁+ε₂)) where E(s) = a + ε₁ (arm(s)) - ε₂ (leg(s)+1) matches the equivariant quantum cohomology after the change of variables a = t₁ (the Kähler parameter) and the identification of the equivariant parameters with the Ω‑background. The quantum product structure constants are recovered from the expansion of log Z_Nek in the Kähler parameter.

This correspondence extends to the quantum cohomology of the Hilbert scheme of points on a general toric surface S. The equivariant quantum cohomology of Hilbⁿ(S) is described by the Maulik–Okounkov stable envelope construction, and the generating function over n yields the Nekrasov partition function of the 5d gauge theory on S × S¹, which is computed by the topological vertex. The quantum difference equation (the q‑difference version of the quantum differential equation) appears naturally because the Kähler parameters of the surface are exponentiated in the 5d theory. The Bethe/gauge correspondence of Nekrasov–Shatashvili identifies the quantum cohomology ring with the ring of functions on the Bethe variety of the corresponding quantum integrable system, and the quantum product is the convolution in the K‑theoretic stable envelope basis.

For exam preparation, focus on: (1) the fixed‑point basis of Hilbⁿ(C²) indexed by partitions; (2) the definition of the equivariant quantum product via three‑point Gromov–Witten invariants with insertions of divisor classes; (3) the statement that the generating function of equivariant quantum cohomology equals the Nekrasov partition function; (4) the role of the Calogero–Moser integrable system and the Bethe ansatz equations; (5) the extension to toric surfaces via the topological vertex. A frequent error is to confuse the equivariant quantum cohomology (a deformation of the ordinary cohomology ring) with the equivariant K‑theory ring; the latter is governed by the quantum difference equation and the stable envelope basis in K‑theory, not by the quantum differential equation.

Quantum Cohomology of Symplectic Resolutions and the Maulik–Okounkov Quantum Product

A symplectic resolution π: X → X₀ of an affine symplectic singularity X₀ carries a conical C*‑action that scales the symplectic form with weight 2. The equivariant quantum cohomology QH^*_T(X) with respect to the torus T = C* × (flavor torus) is a deformation of the ordinary cohomology ring H^*_T(X). Maulik and Okounkov constructed a geometric R‑matrix that intertwines the stable envelope bases for different chambers of the stability condition, and they proved that the quantum product is diagonalized by the stable envelope classes. The eigenvalues are given by the quantum Hamiltonians of the associated quantum integrable system, which is the quantization of the classical Hitchin system on the Coulomb branch of the 3d N=4 gauge theory whose Higgs branch is X. The quantum product by a divisor class corresponds to the action of the quantum Hamiltonian H₁, and the higher Hamiltonians H_k give the quantum products by higher Chern classes of the tautological bundles.

The construction works for a wide class of examples: the Hilbert schemes of points on C² and on ALE spaces, the Nakajima quiver varieties, the hypertoric varieties, and the moduli spaces of instantons on ALE spaces. In each case, the fixed points of the torus action are indexed by combinatorial data (partitions, tuples of partitions, lattice points in a polytope), and the stable envelopes are explicit rational functions in the equivariant parameters. The quantum differential equation ∇_z J = 0, where ∇_z = z d/dz - (1/z) A(t) - U(t), has a fundamental solution given by the J‑function, and its monodromy data (Stokes matrices) are identified with the R‑matrix of the quantum integrable system. The Bethe ansatz equations for the quantum Hamiltonians are precisely the critical point equations for the effective twisted superpotential of the 3d gauge theory, and the quantum cohomology ring is isomorphic to the Jacobian ring of that superpotential.

An important feature is the wall‑crossing behavior of the quantum product as the stability chamber varies. The R‑matrix satisfies the quantum Yang–Baxter equation, and its matrix elements are rational functions of the equivariant parameters and the quantum parameters. The wall‑crossing formula expresses the quantum product in one chamber in terms of the quantum product in another chamber conjugated by the R‑matrix. This phenomenon is the geometric counterpart of the wall‑crossing of BPS states in the associated supersymmetric gauge theory. The Maulik–Okounkov theory also provides a proof of the quantum Satake correspondence for the affine Grassmannian, where the quantum cohomology of the affine Grassmannian (which is a symplectic resolution of the nilpotent cone) is identified with the representation ring of the Langlands dual group.

Key points for examinations: (1) definition of a symplectic resolution with conical C*‑action; (2) the Maulik–Okounkov stable envelope construction and its dependence on a chamber; (3) the statement that the quantum product is diagonalized by stable envelopes and its eigenvalues are quantum Hamiltonians; (4) the wall‑crossing formula via the R‑matrix and the quantum Yang–Baxter equation; (5) examples: Hilbert schemes, Nakajima quiver varieties, hypertoric varieties. A common trap is to assume that the quantum product is independent of the chamber; in fact the quantum product changes by conjugation with the R‑matrix, and only the spectrum of the quantum Hamiltonians is chamber‑independent.

Quantum Cohomology of Relative Gromov–Witten Invariants and the Topological Vertex

Relative Gromov–Witten invariants count stable maps to a pair (X,D) where D ⊂ X is a smooth divisor, with prescribed tangency conditions along D given by a partition μ. The moduli space of relative stable maps carries a virtual fundamental class constructed by Li–Ruan (symplectic) and by Jun Li (algebraic). The relative invariants satisfy a degeneration formula that expresses the absolute invariants of a variety degenerating to a normal‑crossing union in terms of relative invariants of the components. The topological vertex, introduced by Aganagic–Klemm–Marino–Vafa, computes the generating function of relative invariants for the toric Calabi–Yau threefold C³ with three divisors D₁, D₂, D₃ meeting at a point. The vertex is a symmetric function Cλμν(q) of three partitions, and the full partition function of a toric Calabi–Yau threefold is obtained by gluing vertices along edges according to the toric diagram, summing over partitions with weights q|λ| and framing factors (-1)|λ| qκ(λ)/2 where κ(λ) = Σ_i λ_i (λ_i - 2i + 1).

The topological vertex is equivalent to the quantum cohomology of the resolved conifold and of local P². The genus‑zero relative invariants of (C³, D₁∪D₂∪D₃) determine the quantum product in the quantum cohomology of the resolved conifold, which is a non‑compact Calabi–Yau threefold. The mirror symmetry for these geometries identifies the quantum cohomology with the Jacobian ring of the mirror superpotential W = x + y + z + q/(xyz) on (C*)³. The higher‑genus relative invariants are captured by the topological string partition function, which satisfies the holomorphic anomaly equation of BCOV. The topological vertex also computes the open Gromov–Witten invariants with Lagrangian branes on the toric divisors, and these open invariants are related to the closed invariants by the open‑closed duality (the large N duality of Chern–Simons theory).

From the perspective of quantum cohomology, the relative theory provides a way to define quantum products for non‑compact or log Calabi–Yau varieties where the usual three‑point invariants diverge. One introduces a formal variable Q for the divisor class and defines the quantum product by relative three‑point invariants with one insertion constrained to the divisor. The resulting quantum cohomology ring is a module over the Novikov ring of the divisor, and the WDVV equations hold in this relative setting. The topological vertex gives an explicit combinatorial formula for all genus relative invariants of toric Calabi–Yau threefolds, and the gluing rules implement the degeneration formula at the level of the partition function. This combinatorial approach has been used to prove the higher‑genus mirror symmetry for many toric Calabi–Yau threefolds, including the resolved conifold, local P², and the local F₀ = P¹ × P¹.

Quantum Blow‑up Formula and Quantum Cohomology of Blow‑ups

The quantum cohomology ring of a blow‑up X̃ = Bl_Z X along a smooth center Z ⊂ X is determined by the quantum cohomology of X and the exceptional divisor E. Let π : X̃ → X be the blow‑up map and let i : E → X̃ be the inclusion. The cohomology H^*(X̃) splits as π^* H^*(X) ⊕ ⊕k=1c-1 i_* (ξ^k ∪ π_E^* H^*(Z)) where c = codim_X Z and ξ = c1(O_E(1)). The quantum product on X̃ deforms this additive splitting. The fundamental result, proved by Hu (1999) and later refined by Qin–Ruan and Gathmann, states that the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants of X̃ with no insertions from the exceptional divisor coincide with those of X after identifying curve classes via π_*. For invariants involving the exceptional divisor, the formula expresses them in terms of invariants of X and of the projective bundle P(NZ/X ⊕ O_Z) over Z. Concretely, if β ∈ H2(X) and d ≥ 0, the invariant ⟨τa1(γ1)…τa_n(γ_n)⟩β - d[E]X̃ with γ_i ∈ H^*(X) or H^*(E) can be computed by a sum over splittings of the curve class and distributions of insertions between the main component in X and a rubber component in the projective bundle. The rubber calculus uses the relative Gromov–Witten theory of (P(N⊕O), D0 ∪ D_∞) where D0, D_∞ are the zero and infinity sections. The resulting quantum product on H^*(X̃) is associative and satisfies the WDVV equations. For exam purposes, remember: (1) the additive basis of H^*(X̃) in terms of pull‑backs and exceptional classes; (2) the vanishing of invariants with negative powers of the exceptional divisor class; (3) the reduction to relative invariants of the projective bundle; (4) the role of the divisor equation for the exceptional divisor E, which gives a simple recursion for invariants with powers of E. Quantum Leray–Hirsch Theorem for Projective Bundles and Fibrations

Let π : P(E) → B be the projectivization of a rank r vector bundle E over a smooth projective variety B. The classical Leray–Hirsch theorem gives an additive isomorphism H^*(P(E)) ≅ H^*(B)[ξ]/(ξ^r + c1(E)ξr-1 + … + c_r(E)) where ξ = c1(OP(E)(1)). The quantum Leray–Hirsch theorem, established by Bertram, Ciocan‑Fontanine, and Kim (2004) for projective bundles and extended by Lee–Pandharipande for more general fibrations, describes the quantum cohomology ring QH^*(P(E)) as a deformation of this algebra over the Novikov ring of B. The quantum product is determined by the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants of P(E). A curve class in P(E) is of the form β + dℓ where β ∈ H2(B) and ℓ is the class of a line in the fiber. The quantum product of two classes pulled back from B is given by the quantum product in B plus corrections involving the quantum product of the fiber. The key relation is the quantum version of the projective bundle relation: ξ^r + c1(E)ξr-1 + … + c_r(E) = ∑β,d qβ+dℓ ⟨…⟩β+dℓ where the right‑hand side encodes the Gromov–Witten invariants with insertions of ξ powers. In the case where E is convex (e.g., a sum of line bundles with non‑negative Chern classes), the quantum product simplifies and the quantum cohomology of P(E) is a free module over QH^*(B) of rank r with basis 1, ξ, …, ξr-1. The structure constants are computed by the quantum Giambelli formula for the fiber Pr-1 twisted by the Chern classes of E. For a general fibration f : X → B with fiber F, the quantum Leray–Hirsch theorem holds if the fibration is relatively Fano and the quantum cohomology of F is semisimple; then QH^*(X) is a free QH^*(B)-module with basis given by a lift of a basis of QH^*(F). The proof uses the degeneration formula to split invariants of X into relative invariants of the fiber and the base. For exam purposes, remember: (1) the additive basis of QH^*(P(E)) over QH^*(B); (2) the quantum projective bundle relation and how to compute its right‑hand side using the J‑function of the fiber; (3) the condition for freeness (convexity or semisimplicity); (4) the application to computing quantum cohomology of flag varieties as iterated projective bundles. A common error is to treat the quantum parameter for the fiber class ℓ as independent of the base parameters; in fact the Novikov ring of P(E) is the Novikov ring of B adjoined with a variable Q_ℓ, and the divisor equation links Q_ℓ to the pull‑back of the Kähler class of B.

Worked Example: Quantum Product in P(O ⊕ L) over P1
Let B = P1 with hyperplane class H, and let E = O ⊕ L where L = O(1). Compute the quantum product ξ * ξ in QH^*(P(E)) where ξ = c1(OP(E)(1)). The Novikov ring has variables q for the base class H and Q for the fiber class ℓ. The classical relation is ξ2 = c1(E) ξ = H ξ. The quantum correction comes from degree (β, d) = (0,1) and (1,0) invariants. The only non‑zero genus‑zero invariant with two ξ insertions and one point insertion is ⟨ξ, ξ, pt⟩(0,1) = 1 (the fiber is P1). By the divisor equation, ⟨ξ, ξ⟩(0,1) = 1. The invariant ⟨ξ, ξ⟩(1,0) vanishes because ξ restricts to zero on a section. Hence the quantum relation is ξ2 = H ξ + Q. Therefore ξ * ξ = H ξ + Q in QH^*(P(E)).
Step 1: Identify the curve classes. The fiber class ℓ has degree 1 in the fiber and 0 in the base. The base class H has degree 1 in the base and 0 in the fiber. Step 2: Write the quantum product as ξ * ξ = ξ2 + ∑β,d ⟨ξ, ξ, γ^∨⟩β,d q^β Q^d γ where {γ} is a basis of H^*(P(E)) and γ^∨ is the dual basis. Step 3: The only classes that pair non‑trivially with ξ2 are 1 and H (since dim P(E)=3). The invariant with 1 is ⟨ξ, ξ, 1⟩ = 0 by dimension. The invariant with H is ⟨ξ, ξ, H⟩. For (β,d)=(0,1), the fiber is P1, ξ restricts to the hyperplane class on the fiber, and H restricts to 0. The integral ∫P1 ξ2 = 1. For (β,d)=(1,0), the curve is a section; ξ restricts to 0 on the zero section, so the invariant vanishes. Step 4: The dual of H is the class of a point pt (since ∫P(E) H ∪ pt = 1). Thus the correction term is Q · pt. But pt is the identity in the quantum product? Actually the quantum product with the identity is the class itself. Quantum Künneth Formula and Quantum Cohomology of Products

The quantum Künneth formula describes the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants of a product X × Y in terms of the invariants of X and Y. Let X and Y be smooth projective varieties with quantum cohomology rings QH^*(X) and QH^*(Y). The cohomology of the product is H^*(X × Y) ≅ H^*(X) ⊗ H^*(Y). A curve class in X × Y is a pair (β_X, β_Y) ∈ H2(X) ⊕ H2(Y). The genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariant with insertions γ_i ⊗ δ_i factorizes as a sum over splittings of the curve class and distributions of insertions between X and Y, but the factorization is not a simple product because the virtual class of the moduli space of stable maps to X × Y is not the product of the virtual classes of the factors. The precise formula, proved by K. Behrend and Y. Manin (1996) and later refined by A. Gathmann, states that the generating function of invariants of X × Y satisfies a system of differential equations that decouple into the quantum differential equations of X and Y after a suitable change of variables. In particular, the quantum product on QH^*(X × Y) is given by (α ⊗ β) * (γ ⊗ δ) = (α *_X γ) ⊗ (β *_Y δ) where *_X and *_Y are the quantum products in X and Y, but with the Novikov variables identified: the Novikov ring of X × Y is the tensor product of the Novikov rings of X and Y. This holds because the moduli space of stable maps to X × Y is isomorphic to the fiber product M0,n(X, β_X) ×X^n M0,n(Y, β_Y) over the evaluation maps, and the virtual class is the product of the virtual classes when the obstruction theories are compatible. The compatibility holds when X and Y are both convex (i.e., H1(C, f^* T_X) = 0 for all stable maps), which is true for homogeneous spaces and toric varieties. For non‑convex factors, the formula acquires correction terms involving higher genus invariants of the factors (the "quantum Künneth formula with corrections"). The quantum Künneth formula is essential for computing the quantum cohomology of products such as P^m × P^n, Grassmannians × flag varieties, and for proving the semisimplicity of QH^*(X × Y) from that of the factors. For exam purposes, remember: (1) the additive isomorphism H^*(X × Y) ≅ H^*(X) ⊗ H^*(Y); (2) the identification of curve classes and Novikov variables; (3) the factorization of the quantum product for convex varieties; (4) the role of the evaluation maps and the fiber product of moduli spaces; (5) the failure of naive factorization for non‑convex factors and the appearance of correction terms from the obstruction theory. A typical mistake is to assume the quantum product on a product is always the tensor product of the quantum products without checking convexity; for example, the quantum cohomology of an abelian variety times a projective space is not simply the tensor product because the abelian variety has no rational curves but its obstruction theory contributes to the virtual class of maps to the product.

Quantum Cohomology of Moduli Spaces of Stable Quotients and the Quantum Riemann–Roch Theorem

The theory of stable quotients, introduced by Marian, Oprea, and Pandharipande (2011), provides a compactification of the space of maps from a curve to a Grassmannian (or more generally to a GIT quotient) that is better behaved than the space of stable maps. For a vector bundle V on a smooth projective variety X, the moduli space of stable quotients Qg,n(X, V, β) parametrizes exact sequences 0 → S → V_C → Q → 0 on a nodal curve C of genus g with n marked points, where Q is a quotient sheaf of rank r and degree β, and S is a subsheaf satisfying a stability condition. The virtual class on this moduli space is constructed using the perfect obstruction theory derived from the deformation theory of the quotient. The genus‑zero stable quotient invariants with insertions of cohomology classes from X are related to the Gromov–Witten invariants of X by a universal change of variables (the "stable quotient / GW correspondence"). The quantum Riemann–Roch theorem, proved by Coates and Givental (2007) for toric varieties and extended by Tseng and others, computes the total descendant potential of a GIT quotient X = V//G in terms of the total descendant potential of the ambient space V and the equivariant characteristic classes of the bundle. In the language of stable quotients, the quantum Riemann–Roch theorem gives an explicit formula for the J‑function of X as a differential operator applied to the J‑function of V. The operator involves the Chern character of the virtual bundle R^•π_* f^* V on the moduli space of stable quotients. For a projective bundle P(E) → B, the quantum Riemann–Roch theorem reproduces the quantum Leray–Hirsch theorem. For a complete intersection in a toric variety, it yields the quantum Lefschetz hyperplane theorem. The stable quotient theory also provides a natural framework for the quantum K‑theory of GIT quotients, where the quantum product is defined using the K‑theoretic virtual class. For exam purposes, remember: (1) the definition of a stable quotient and the stability condition (slope stability of the subsheaf S); (2) the virtual class construction via the perfect obstruction theory; (3) the universal relation between stable quotient invariants and Gromov–Witten invariants (the "I‑function" vs "J‑function"); (4) the statement of the quantum Riemann–Roch theorem: the total ancestor potential of the quotient is obtained by quantizing a certain symplectic transformation (the "R‑matrix") acting on the total ancestor potential of the ambient space; (5) the application to computing the quantum cohomology of Grassmannians, flag varieties, and toric complete intersections. A common trap is to confuse the stable quotient moduli space with the moduli space of stable maps to the Grassmannian; they are different compactifications, but their genus‑zero invariants are related by a simple change of variables (the "mirror map" for the Grassmannian). Another error is to apply the quantum Riemann–Roch theorem without verifying that the GIT quotient is a "good" quotient (i.e., the action is linearized and the stable locus coincides with the semistable locus).

Quantum Cohomology of Moduli Spaces of Stable Pairs and the Pandharipande–Thomas Theory

The Pandharipande–Thomas (PT) theory defines invariants of a Calabi–Yau threefold X by counting stable pairs (F, s) where F is a pure one‑dimensional sheaf on X with holomorphic Euler characteristic χ(F) = n and s : O_X → F is a section with zero‑dimensional cokernel. The moduli space P_n(X, β) of stable pairs with curve class β is a proper algebraic space with a perfect obstruction theory of virtual dimension 0. The PT invariants Pn,β = ∫[P_n(X,β)]^vir 1 are conjectured to be equivalent to the Gromov–Witten invariants of X after a change of variables (the GW/PT correspondence), which is a special case of the Gopakumar–Vafa / Donaldson–Thomas correspondence. The quantum cohomology perspective enters through the generating function of PT invariants with insertions of cohomology classes via the "insertion" of relative conditions on a divisor. For a Calabi–Yau threefold X with a divisor D, one can define relative PT invariants with tangency conditions along D. The degeneration formula for PT invariants, proved by Maulik, Oblomkov, Okounkov, and Pandharipande, expresses the absolute invariants of X in terms of relative invariants of (X, D) and (D × P1, D × {0,∞}). This degeneration formula is the PT analogue of the Li–Ruan degeneration formula for GW invariants. The topological vertex computes the PT partition function for toric Calabi–Yau threefolds, and the gluing rules are identical to those for GW invariants. The quantum cohomology of X can be recovered from the genus‑zero PT invariants with one insertion of a divisor class and two insertions of point classes, via the formula ⟨D, pt, pt⟩0,βGW = ∑n Pn,β q^n (after appropriate identification of variables). The PT theory also provides a definition of the quantum product for non‑compact Calabi–Yau threefolds where the GW three‑point functions diverge; one uses the relative PT invariants with one insertion constrained to the divisor at infinity. For exam purposes, remember: (1) the definition of a stable pair and the stability condition (pure 1‑dimensional, finite cokernel); (2) the virtual dimension zero and the construction of the virtual class; (3) the GW/PT correspondence: the generating functions of GW and PT invariants are related by the change of variables q = -ei u and a universal factor M(-q)χ(X) where M is the MacMahon function; (4) the degeneration formula for PT invariants and its compatibility with the topological vertex; (5) the extraction of the quantum product from genus‑zero PT invariants with divisor insertions. A typical mistake is to treat PT invariants as curve counting invariants without the sheaf‑theoretic stability condition; the pure dimension condition is crucial for properness. Another error is to assume the GW/PT correspondence holds for all genera without the universal factor; the factor M(-q)χ(X) accounts for the contribution of constant maps in GW theory which have no analogue in PT theory.

Fan–Jarvis–Ruan–Witten Theory and Quantum Cohomology of Landau–Ginzburg Models

Fan–Jarvis–Ruan–Witten (FJRW) theory provides a mathematical foundation for the A‑model of a Landau–Ginzburg (LG) orbifold (W, G) where W : ℂN → ℂ is a non‑degenerate quasi‑homogeneous polynomial with an isolated singularity at the origin and G ⊂ SL(N, ℂ) is a finite group of diagonal symmetries containing the exponential grading element J. The state space is the G‑invariant part of the direct sum of the Milnor rings of the sectors Wg (g ∈ G). The genus‑zero FJRW invariants are defined by integrating the virtual fundamental class of the moduli space of W‑spin curves (a generalization of r‑spin curves) against a virtual cycle constructed from the cosequence exact sequence. These invariants satisfy the WDVV equations and define a Frobenius manifold structure on the state space. The quantum product is given by the three‑point genus‑zero correlators. The LG/CY correspondence, proved by Chiodo–Ruan and others for Fermat polynomials, asserts that the FJRW Frobenius manifold of (W, G) is isomorphic to the quantum cohomology Frobenius manifold of the Calabi–Yau hypersurface {W = 0} in the weighted projective stack [ℂN/G] after analytic continuation in the Kähler parameters (the mirror map). The mirror symmetry for LG models is formulated via the B‑model: the Saito primitive form of the singularity W gives a Frobenius manifold on the unfolding space, and the FJRW A‑model is identified with this B‑model via the mirror isomorphism. The higher‑genus FJRW invariants are conjectured to satisfy the Virasoro constraints and to be governed by the Givental quantization formalism. For exam purposes, remember: (1) the definition of a W‑spin curve and the cosequence construction of the virtual cycle; (2) the state space as G‑invariant Milnor ring sectors; (3) the WDVV equations and Frobenius manifold structure on the genus‑zero potential; (4) the LG/CY correspondence as an isomorphism of Frobenius manifolds after mirror map; (5) the role of the Saito primitive form on the B‑model side.

Categorical Mirror Symmetry and Quantum Cohomology of Calabi–Yau Categories

Kontsevich’s homological mirror symmetry conjecture asserts an equivalence of triangulated categories DbCoh(X) ≅ Fuk(Y) for a mirror pair of Calabi–Yau varieties X and Y. The quantum cohomology of X appears as the Hochschild cohomology HH*(DbCoh(X)) equipped with the cup product deformed by the genus‑zero open‑closed Gromov–Witten invariants of Y. On the A‑side, the Fukaya category Fuk(Y) is an A∞‑category whose objects are Lagrangian branes. The closed‑string sector is the quantum cohomology QH*(Y), and the open‑closed map OC : HH*(Fuk(Y)) → QH*(Y) is a morphism of Frobenius algebras. The genus‑zero open Gromov–Witten invariants with one boundary insertion and two interior insertions define the quantum product on HH*(Fuk(Y)) via the formula ⟨a, b, c⟩0 = ∫[M0,1+2(Y, β)]vir ev0*(a) ∪ ev1*(b) ∪ ev2*(c). The categorical mirror symmetry predicts that the Hochschild cohomology of the Fukaya category, with its deformed product, is isomorphic to the quantum cohomology of the mirror X. This has been verified for toric Calabi–Yau threefolds (Abouzaid–Auroux–Katzarkov) and for the quintic threefold (Sheridan). The Gamma conjecture relates the asymptotic behavior of the quantum differential equation (the Dubrovin connection) to the Gamma class of X, which is a characteristic class in the Hochschild cohomology of DbCoh(X). Derived Quantum Cohomology and the Moduli Space of Stable Maps to Derived Schemes

Derived algebraic geometry replaces schemes by derived schemes (or spectral schemes) whose structure sheaf is a sheaf of simplicial commutative rings (or E∞‑ring spectra). Symplectic Field Theory and Quantum Cohomology of Contact Manifolds

Symplectic Field Theory (SFT), introduced by Eliashberg–Givental–Hofer, is a topological quantum field theory associated to a contact manifold (V, ξ) with a non‑degenerate contact form α. The symplectization ℝ × V carries the symplectic form d(etα). SFT counts holomorphic curves in the symplectization with asymptotic limits at Reeb orbits. The algebraic structure is a differential graded algebra (the SFT algebra) generated by good Reeb orbits, with differential counting holomorphic curves of index 1. The linearized contact homology HC*(V) is the homology of the linearized differential. For a contact manifold that bounds a symplectic filling (W, ω), the SFT of V computes the symplectic homology SH*(W) and, via the Viterbo isomorphism, the quantum cohomology QH*(W) of the filling. The quantum product on QH*(W) can be recovered from the SFT algebra by a suitable limit (the “SFT Hamiltonian” limit). The SFT gluing formula expresses the SFT of a contact manifold obtained by contact connected sum in terms of the SFT of the summands. For a contact hypersurface in a Calabi–Yau manifold, the SFT invariants are related to the open Gromov–Witten invariants of the Lagrangian skeleton. The Bourgeois–Oancea exact sequence relates the symplectic homology of a Liouville domain to the contact homology of its boundary. Quantum Cohomology of K3 Surfaces and the Yau–Zaslow Formula

A K3 surface is a simply connected compact complex surface with trivial canonical bundle. Its genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants are governed by the Yau–Zaslow formula, which expresses the generating function of these invariants in terms of the Dedekind eta function. For a primitive curve class β with β² = 2g−2, the genus‑zero invariant N₀,β equals the coefficient of q^g in the expansion of 1/η(q)²⁴, where η(q) = q1/24 ∏n≥1 (1−q^n). This result was proved by Bryan and Leung using the crepant resolution of the symmetric product Sym^g(K3) and the identification of the virtual fundamental class with the Euler class of the obstruction bundle. The quantum cohomology ring QH*(K3) is therefore a deformation of the ordinary cohomology ring H*(K3) by the quantum product a * b = a ∪ b + ∑β≠0 N₀,β (a,b) q^β, where the sum runs over effective curve classes. Because the canonical class is trivial, the quantum parameter q^β has degree zero, so the quantum product preserves the grading. The only non‑trivial quantum corrections occur in degree two, and they are completely determined by the Yau–Zaslow numbers. Quantum Cohomology of Hyperkähler Varieties and the Beauville–Bogomolov Form

A compact hyperkähler variety X of dimension 2n carries a canonical quadratic form q on H²(X,ℚ), the Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki form, which satisfies the Fujiki relation ∫_X α2n = c_X q(α)^n for a constant c_X > 0. The quantum cohomology ring QH*(X) deforms the cup product by genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants. For hyperkähler varieties, the quantum product on H²(X) is governed by the Beauville–Bogomolov form: the quantum correction to the product of two divisor classes D₁, D₂ is proportional to q(D₁,D₂) times the fundamental class, with the proportionality constant given by a universal series in the quantum parameter. This was established by Verbitsky and later refined by Huybrechts and others using the twistor family and the hyperkähler metric. Seidel Elements and the Quantum Cohomology Action of Hamiltonian Loops

The Seidel representation provides a bridge between the symplectic mapping class group and the quantum cohomology ring. For a closed symplectic manifold (X,ω) with π1(Ham(X,ω)) non‑trivial, Seidel constructed a homomorphism

 S : π1(Ham(X,ω)) → QH* (X)×,

where QH* (X)× denotes the group of invertible elements in the quantum cohomology ring. The construction uses a family of Hamiltonian fibrations over S2 whose monodromy is the given loop; the associated Gromov–Witten invariants of sections of this fibration define the Seidel element.

Key steps for exam‑level recall:

  • Section class. For a loop γ, the associated fibration Eγ→S2 carries a distinguished section class σγ∈H2(Eγ,ℤ). The Seidel element is S(γ)=∑d⟨[pt]⟩0,1,σγ+d·q^d, where the coefficient counts genus‑zero, one‑marked‑point GW invariants of sections in class σγ+d.
  • Grading. The degree of S(γ) equals 2c1(TX)(σγ) modulo 2N, where N is the minimal Chern number. This grading explains why Seidel elements often lie in odd degree for monotone manifolds.
  • Multiplicativity. The homomorphism property S(γ1⋅γ2)=S(γ1)∗S(γ2) follows from gluing of fibrations over the pair‑of‑pants and the associativity of the quantum product.
  • Applications. In toric Fano manifolds, the Seidel elements generate the quantum cohomology as a module over the Novikov ring. For CP^n, the generator of π1(Ham) yields the element q·[pt] which recovers the well‑known relation (H)n+1=q in QH*(CP^n).

Exam‑oriented insight: when a problem asks for the quantum relation in a monotone symplectic manifold with a known Hamiltonian S1‑action, identify the fixed‑point data, compute the associated Seidel element via the localization formula, and translate it into a polynomial relation in QH* (X). Quantum Lefschetz Hyperplane Theorem for Complete Intersections

The classical Lefschetz hyperplane theorem asserts that the inclusion of a smooth ample divisor Y⊂X induces an isomorphism on cohomology up to degree dim Y−1. Its quantum analogue, proved by Givental and later refined by Kim, states that the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten theory of a smooth complete intersection can be recovered from that of the ambient space by a simple “hypergeometric modification”.

Let X be a smooth projective variety with semisimple quantum cohomology and let E→X be a convex vector bundle of rank r. For a regular section s∈H0(X,E) the zero locus Z(s)=Y is a smooth complete intersection of codimension r. The quantum Lefschetz theorem gives the following identity for the J‑function:

 JY(t,z)=JX(t,z)·∏i=1^r ∏m=1〈c1(E_i),d〉 (c1(E_i)+mz)/∏m=1〈c1(E_i),d〉 (mz),

where the product runs over the Chern roots E_i of E and d∈H2(X,ℤ) is the curve class. The right‑hand side is the ambient J‑function multiplied by a hypergeometric factor that encodes the Euler class of the obstruction bundle on the moduli space of stable maps to Y.

Important consequences for examinations:

  • Effective computation. For a hypersurface of degree d in CP^n, the quantum relation becomes (H)n+1=d·q·(H)n+1−d. This yields the well‑known “mirror map” for the quintic threefold: (H)4=5q·(H)0.
  • Convexity requirement. The theorem holds when the normal bundle NY/X is generated by global sections (convex). For non‑convex complete intersections, higher‑genus corrections appear and the simple hypergeometric factor no longer suffices.
  • Semisimplicity transfer. If QH*(X) is semisimple, then QH*(Y) is also semisimple under the Lefschetz theorem, a fact used in proving Dubrovin’s conjecture for many Fano complete intersections.
  • Mirror symmetry link. The hypergeometric modification coincides with the Picard‑Fuchs differential operator governing the period integrals of the mirror family. Thus the quantum Lefschetz theorem provides a direct route from A‑model GW invariants to B‑model periods.

Typical exam question: “Using the quantum Lefschetz theorem, derive the quantum relation for a degree‑4 hypersurface in CP3 and comment on the semisimplicity of its quantum cohomology.” The answer must display the hypergeometric factor, simplify the relation, and note that the resulting ring is isomorphic to ℂ[H]/(H4−4q).

Wall‑Crossing in GIT Quotients and the Quantum Kirwan Map

Geometric Invariant Theory (GIT) provides a systematic way to construct quotients X//G for a reductive group G acting on a smooth projective variety X. The quantum Kirwan map, introduced by Kirwan in the classical setting and extended to quantum cohomology by Ciocan‑Fontanine, Kim, and Maulik, relates the equivariant quantum cohomology QH*_G (X) to the quantum cohomology of the quotient QH* (X//G). Wall‑crossing phenomena occur when the linearisation (choice of ample line bundle) varies across a GIT chamber wall, causing the quotient to change birationally.

Key components for a rigorous answer:

  • Quantum Kirwan surjectivity. For a G‑action with semistable locus Xss, the map κ_Q : QH*_G (X)→QH* (X//G) induced by evaluation at the identity class is surjective under convexity assumptions (e.g., when X is a vector space and G acts linearly).
  • Wall‑crossing formula. Let L_± be two linearizations on opposite sides of a wall. The difference of the J‑functions satisfies

 JL_+(t,z)−JL_-(t,z)=∑β∈Eff q^β·Δ_β(t,z),

where Δ_β are explicit contributions from the unstable strata, expressed as residues of equivariant GW invariants on the fixed loci. This formula was proved by Zhou and later refined by Coates–Iritani–Jiang.

Practical implications:

  • Crepant transformation conjecture. When the wall crossing is a crepant birational transformation (e.g., a flop), the quantum cohomology rings of the two quotients are isomorphic after analytic continuation of the quantum parameters. This is a special case of the broader crepant resolution conjecture.
  • Example – Projective space vs. weighted projective space. Consider X=ℂ3 with G=ℂ^* acting with weights (1,1,−2). Varying the linearisation yields the quotient CP2 on one side and the weighted projective space ℙ(1,1,2) on the other. The wall‑crossing term accounts for the contribution of the exceptional divisor, leading to the relation H3=2q in the weighted case versus H3=q in CP2.
  • Computational technique. Use the abelian‑nonabelian correspondence to reduce the equivariant GW invariants of a non‑abelian G to those of a maximal torus T⊂G, where localization makes the wall‑crossing residues explicit.

Exam tip: if a problem asks for the quantum cohomology after a GIT variation, first decide whether the wall is crepant. If yes, state the analytic continuation principle; if not, write the wall‑crossing correction as a sum over fixed‑point contributions, citing the quantum Kirwan surjectivity theorem.

Quantum Cohomology and the Seiberg–Witten Invariants of Symplectic Four‑Manifolds

Although Seiberg–Witten (SW) theory originates in gauge theory, it has deep connections with symplectic geometry via Taubes’s “SW=GW” theorem. For a closed symplectic four‑manifold (X,ω) with b+2>1, Taubes proved that the Seiberg–Witten invariants of the canonical Spin^c structure equal the Gromov–Witten invariants counting J‑holomorphic curves representing the canonical class K_X. This identification extends to the quantum product on H2(X) when the manifold is monotone.

Important points for a thorough answer:

 α∗β = α∪β + ⟨α,β,K_X⟩·q·[pt],

where the coefficient ⟨α,β,K_X⟩ is precisely the SW invariant of the class K_X evaluated on α∧β. Thus the only non‑trivial quantum correction in degree two comes from curves in the canonical class.

  • Wall‑crossing in b+2=1. When b+2=1, the SW invariants depend on a chamber determined by the symplectic form.   • Example – Rational surfaces. For X=CP2 blown up at k≤8 points, the canonical class is K_X=−3H+∑E_i. Symplectic resolutions, such as Nakajima quiver varieties and hypertoric varieties, admit a rich equivariant quantum cohomology structure. Equivariant Localization in Gromov–Witten Theory and the Graber–Pandharipande Formula

When a torus T acts on a smooth projective variety X, the moduli space of stable maps Mg,n(X,β) inherits a T-action. The virtual fundamental class and the obstruction theory can be made T-equivariant, allowing the application of the Atiyah–Bott–Berline–Vergne localization formula. Graber and Pandharipande proved that the equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants of X localize to contributions from the fixed loci of the T-action on the moduli space. These fixed loci are described by decorated graphs whose vertices correspond to T-fixed points of X, edges to T-invariant rational curves, and legs to marked points mapping to fixed points. The contribution of each graph is a product of vertex, edge, and leg integrals involving equivariant Euler classes of the normal bundles. The formula expresses any equivariant Gromov–Witten invariant as a sum over such graphs of rational functions in the equivariant parameters. This reduces the computation of invariants on complicated varieties to combinatorial data of the torus action. In practice, one chooses a torus action with isolated fixed points and one‑dimensional orbits (e.g., the standard torus on a toric variety or on a flag variety). The localization formula then yields explicit recursive algorithms for genus‑zero invariants, and with additional work for higher genus via the virtual localization formula. The method is the backbone of many mirror symmetry computations, because the I-function and J-function of a toric variety can be derived by evaluating the localization sum. It also underlies the proof of the quantum Lefschetz hyperplane theorem for convex bundles, where the localization graph sum for the total space is compared with that of the zero locus. Mastery of the graph combinatorics, the equivariant Euler class of the obstruction bundle, and the handling of non‑compact fixed loci (via the virtual normal bundle) is essential for advanced problems.

Orbifold Quantum Cohomology and the Chen–Ruan Product

For a smooth Deligne–Mumford stack X with projective coarse moduli space, Chen and Ruan defined an orbifold cohomology ring H∗CR(X) whose underlying vector space is the direct sum of the cohomology of the inertia stack IX. The degree of a class is shifted by the age (or degree shifting number) of the corresponding twisted sector. The orbifold cup product is defined by counting degree‑zero constant maps, which yields a product that is commutative and associative but generally not graded‑commutative in the usual sense. The quantum deformation of this product, the orbifold quantum cohomology QH∗CR(X), is defined by genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants of X with insertions from twisted sectors. The three‑point invariants ⟨α,β,γ⟩0,3,β are defined via integration over the virtual fundamental class of the moduli stack of twisted stable maps. The associativity of the quantum product is equivalent to the WDVV equations in the orbifold setting, which hold because the moduli space of twisted stable maps carries a perfect obstruction theory. A central result is the crepant resolution conjecture: if Y → X is a crepant resolution, then the quantum cohomology of Y and the orbifold quantum cohomology of X become isomorphic after analytic continuation in the quantum parameters and a suitable change of variables (the Ruan conjecture). This has been verified for many toric orbifolds and for global quotients [C3/G]. The orbifold quantum product also appears in the Landau–Ginzburg/Calabi–Yau correspondence, where the FJRW theory of a Landau–Ginzburg model matches the orbifold quantum cohomology of the associated Calabi–Yau hypersurface. For exam purposes, one must be able to compute the inertia stack of a global quotient [M/G], determine the age shifts, write down the Chen–Ruan product for degree‑zero classes, and set up the localization formula for twisted stable maps on toric orbifolds.

Quantum Cohomology of Homogeneous Spaces and the Quantum Satake Correspondence

For a generalized flag variety G/P where G is a complex semisimple Lie group and P a parabolic subgroup, the (small) quantum cohomology ring QH∗(G/P) is a deformation of the classical cohomology ring, which is generated by Schubert classes. The quantum product is determined by the three‑point, genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants, which count rational curves of given degree meeting three Schubert varieties. A remarkable theorem of Peterson, and later proved by Kim, Ciocan‑Fontanine, and others, identifies the quantum cohomology of G/P with a quotient of the quantum cohomology of the affine Grassmannian GrG. This is the quantum Satake correspondence: the quantum product by divisor classes on G/P corresponds to the action of the quantum group Uq(𝔤) on the representation category of the Langlands dual group G∨. Concretely, the quantum Chevalley formula gives the product of a divisor class with a Schubert class as a sum over certain quantum Bruhat graph edges, with coefficients that are quantum parameters raised to the degree of the corresponding curve. The quantum Bruhat graph encodes the covering relations in the quantum cohomology ring. The correspondence extends to an isomorphism between the (small) quantum cohomology of G/P and the center of the quantum group at a root of unity, or equivalently to the fusion ring of the affine Lie algebra at a certain level. For the Grassmannian Gr(k,n), the quantum Satake correspondence recovers the quantum Giambelli formula and the presentation of QH∗(Gr(k,n)) as a quotient of a polynomial ring by the ideal generated by the quantum elementary symmetric functions. In the case of the full flag variety G/B, the quantum cohomology is isomorphic to the coordinate ring of the Peterson variety, a subvariety of the Langlands dual Lie algebra. These identifications allow one to compute quantum Schubert structure constants using representation‑theoretic algorithms (e.g., the quantum Littlewood–Richardson rule). Logarithmic Mirror Symmetry and the Gross–Siebert Program

The Gross–Siebert program constructs mirror pairs for Calabi–Yau varieties by degenerating them to maximally degenerate log Calabi–Yau spaces, i.e., unions of toric varieties meeting along toric divisors according to a polyhedral complex. The logarithmic Gromov–Witten invariants of the degeneration are defined using the moduli space of stable log maps, which carries a virtual fundamental class. The key insight is that the log GW invariants of the central fibre can be computed tropically: they correspond to counts of tropical curves in the associated integral affine manifold with singularities. The mirror construction then associates to this affine manifold a dual affine manifold, and the tropical curve counts on the dual side give the periods of the mirror family. The program proves that the generating functions of log GW invariants (the log I-function) satisfy the same Picard–Fuchs equations as the periods of the mirror, establishing mirror symmetry at the level of genus‑zero invariants. The logarithmic setting also provides a natural framework for the degeneration formula: the absolute GW invariants of a smooth Calabi–Yau can be expressed in terms of log GW invariants of a normal‑crossing degeneration. This has been used to prove the mirror theorem for many toric complete intersections and for hypersurfaces in toric varieties. For exam purposes, one must understand the definition of a log smooth morphism, the notion of a basic log map, the tropicalization of a log curve, and the correspondence theorem between log GW invariants and tropical curve counts. Typical questions ask to compute the tropical count for a simple affine manifold (e.g., the fan of P2 with a focus‑focus singularity) and to match it with the coefficient of the I-function of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model.

Quantum Cohomology of Symplectic Manifolds and the Piunikhin–Salamon–Schwarz Isomorphism

The Piunikhin–Salamon–Schwarz (PSS) isomorphism provides a canonical identification between the quantum cohomology ring QH*(M, ω) of a closed symplectic manifold (M, ω) and its Hamiltonian Floer homology HF*(H) for a non‑degenerate Hamiltonian H. Constructed in 1996, the map is defined by counting solutions of a Cauchy–Riemann equation on the half‑cylinder with one asymptotic condition given by a Morse critical point of a Morse function on M and the other by a 1‑periodic orbit of the Hamiltonian flow. The resulting chain map respects the product structures: the quantum cup product on QH* corresponds to the pair‑of‑pants product on Floer homology. This isomorphism is natural with respect to symplectomorphisms and plays a central role in proving the Arnold conjecture for symplectically aspherical manifolds and in the formulation of the closed‑open string map to the Fukaya category. For exam purposes, one must know the definition of the PSS map on the chain level, the transversality and compactness arguments that guarantee it is a chain isomorphism, and the statement that it intertwines the quantum product with the Floer product. A typical question asks to explain why the PSS isomorphism fails to be defined for general symplectic manifolds without the semi‑positivity or monotonicity assumptions, and how the virtual fundamental class technique extends it to all closed symplectic manifolds.

Quantum Cohomology of Toric Varieties and the Hori–Vafa Mirror

The Hori–Vafa mirror construction (2000) gives an explicit Landau–Ginzburg model mirror to a smooth projective toric variety X = X(Δ) defined by a fan Δ. The mirror is the algebraic torus (ℂ×)n equipped with a superpotential W = Σi=1m qi zvi, where the vi are the primitive generators of the one‑dimensional cones of Δ and the qi are the complexified Kähler parameters. The critical points of W correspond to the solutions of the quantum cohomology relations, and the Jacobian ring Jac(W) = ℂ[z±1]/(∂W) is canonically isomorphic to the small quantum cohomology ring QH*(X) after identifying the quantum parameters with the qi. The isomorphism is realized by the oscillatory integral representation of the I‑function, which satisfies the quantum differential equation (the Dubrovin connection). For toric Fano varieties the mirror is a Laurent polynomial, and the mirror theorem of Givental (1998) proves that the J‑function of X coincides with the I‑function of the mirror after a mirror map. Examination questions often require writing down the superpotential for a given toric variety (e.g., ℙ2 or the Hirzebruch surface F1), computing the Jacobian ring, and matching its structure constants with the three‑point genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants. One must also understand the role of the Batyrev–Borisov construction for toric complete intersections and the extension to toric Deligne–Mumford stacks via the extended I‑function.

Worked Example: Mirror of ℙ2
Write the Hori–Vafa superpotential for ℙ2, compute its Jacobian ring, and verify that the quantum product of the hyperplane class H satisfies H ⋆ H = H2 + q where q is the quantum parameter.
The fan of ℙ2 has rays v1 = (1,0), v2 = (0,1), v3 = (−1,−1). The superpotential is W = q1 z1 + q2 z2 + q3 (z1z2)−1. Setting all qi = q (the single Kähler parameter) and using the relation z1z2z3 = 1 with z3 = (z1z2)−1, we get W = q(z1 + z2 + z1−1z2−1). The Jacobian ring is ℂ[z1±1, z2±1]/(∂W/∂z1, ∂W/∂z2). The derivatives give q(1 − z1−2z2−1) = 0 and q(1 − z1−1z2−2) = 0, implying z12z2 = 1 and z1z22 = 1, so z1 = z2 = ζ where ζ3 = 1. The ring is three‑dimensional, generated by 1, z1, z12. Identifying H with z1 + z2 + z1−1z2−1 (the mirror map), one finds H ⋆ H = H2 + q, matching the known quantum cohomology of ℙ2.
Exam Strategy
When asked to construct the Hori–Vafa mirror, first write the fan data, then the superpotential as a sum over rays. Remember that the quantum parameters correspond to the Kähler moduli; for a toric variety with Picard rank r there are r independent parameters. The Jacobian ring computation reduces to solving a system of Laurent polynomial equations; use the toric relations to eliminate variables. Always check that the dimension of the Jacobian ring equals the rank of the ordinary cohomology.

Quantum Cohomology of Moduli Spaces of Sheaves on Surfaces and the Vafa–Witten Theory

Vafa–Witten theory is the topological twist of N = 4 supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory on a smooth projective surface S. Its partition function computes the Euler characteristics of the moduli spaces of semi‑stable sheaves on S with fixed Chern classes, weighted by the Behrend function. In the mathematical formulation due to Tanaka and Thomas (2017), the Vafa–Witten invariants are defined via a perfect obstruction theory on the moduli stack of Higgs sheaves, and they are expected to coincide with the generating series of Euler characteristics of the moduli spaces of stable sheaves. The connection with quantum cohomology arises through the S‑duality conjecture: the Vafa–Witten partition function on S equals the generating function of Gromov–Witten invariants of the total space of the canonical bundle KS, which is a local Calabi–Yau threefold. For surfaces with pg > 0, the Vafa–Witten invariants capture the full quantum cohomology of the Hilbert scheme of points on S via the Nakajima–Grojnowski construction. A concrete instance is the computation of the Vafa–Witten invariants for S = ℙ2 or a K3 surface, where the partition function is expressed in terms of modular forms (e.g., the Igusa cusp form for K3). Examination questions may ask to state the Vafa–Witten conjecture for a given surface, to explain how the virtual fundamental class is constructed on the moduli of Higgs sheaves, or to relate the S‑duality transformation τ ↦ −1/τ to the Fourier–Mukai transform on the derived category of S. One must also be aware of the role of the cosection localization technique in defining the invariants when the obstruction theory has a cosection.

Quantum Cohomology of Moduli Spaces of Instantons and the ADHM Construction

The ADHM construction (Atiyah–Drinfeld–Hitchin–Manin, 1978) gives a hyperkähler quotient description of the moduli space Mr,k of framed instantons of rank r and charge k on ℝ4 ≅ ℂ2. As a complex variety, Mr,k is isomorphic to the Nakajima quiver variety associated to the Jordan quiver with dimension vector (r, k). The quantum cohomology of these moduli spaces has been computed by Maulik and Okounkov (2012) using stable envelopes in equivariant cohomology with respect to the torus T = (ℂ×)2 × (ℂ×)r acting by scaling ℂ2 and rotating the framing. The quantum product is governed by the R‑matrix of the quantum affine algebra Uq(ĝ𝔩r) and coincides with the multiplication in the quantum group after a suitable specialization of the equivariant parameters. The Nekrasov partition function, which is the equivariant integral of the exponential of the equivariant first Chern class of the determinant line bundle, serves as the generating function of the quantum cohomology correlators. For exam purposes, one should be able to describe the ADHM data (matrices B1, B2, I, J satisfying the ADHM equations), explain the hyperkähler quotient, and state the Maulik–Okounkov theorem that the equivariant quantum cohomology ring is isomorphic to the spherical DAHA (double affine Hecke algebra) at a specific parameter. A typical problem asks to compute the quantum product of the divisor classes corresponding to the tautological bundles on Mr,k for small r, k (e.g., r = 1, k = 2) using the fixed‑point localization formula.

Quantum Cohomology of Moduli Spaces of Flat Connections and the Quantum Geometric Langlands Correspondence

The moduli space MB(G, C) of flat G‑bundles (or character variety) on a smooth projective curve C carries a natural symplectic structure given by the Atiyah–Bott form. Its quantum cohomology, defined via the Gromov–Witten theory of the associated Higgs bundle moduli space MDol(G, C) through the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence, is conjecturally related to the representation theory of the quantum Langlands dual group LGq. The Kapustin–Witten topological twist of N = 4 SYM on C × ℝ2 provides a physical framework: the A‑model on MDol with a complexified Kähler parameter τ is S‑dual to the B‑model on MB with parameter −1/τ. The quantum geometric Langlands correspondence predicts an equivalence between the derived category of D‑modules on BunG(C) twisted by a parameter ψ and the derived category of coherent sheaves on the moduli of LG‑local systems, where the quantum parameter q = exp(2πi/ψ) appears in the quantum cohomology of MB. Recent work of Bezrukavnikov, Finkelberg, and others has established this for G = SL2 using the geometric Satake equivalence and the quantum Coulomb branch. For examinations, one must know the definition of the quantum cohomology ring of the character variety via the Gromov–Witten invariants of the Higgs bundle moduli space, the statement of the quantum geometric Langlands conjecture, and the role of the quantum parameter in the Verlinde formula for the fusion ring of the affine Lie algebra at level k. A common question asks to explain how the quantum product on H*(MB) deforms the cup product by counting holomorphic curves in MDol, and to identify the quantum parameter with the inverse of the level in the WZW model.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the PSS isomorphism with the closed‑open string map; the PSS map lands in Hamiltonian Floer homology, while the closed‑open map goes to the Fukaya category.
  • Assuming the Hori–Vafa superpotential is a polynomial for all toric varieties; for non‑Fano toric varieties it is a Laurent polynomial with negative exponents, and the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model on a non‑compact torus.
  • Neglecting the cosection localization in Vafa–Witten theory; without it the virtual class is not well‑defined when the obstruction theory has a surjective cosection.
  • Treating the ADHM moduli space as a smooth variety for all ranks; it has singularities when the instanton charge is large, and the quantum cohomology must be defined via the virtual fundamental class on the quiver variety.
  • Identifying the quantum parameter in geometric Langlands with the Kähler parameter of the Higgs bundle moduli space; the correct identification is q = exp(2πi/ψ) where ψ is the twisting parameter for D‑modules.
