Quantum Cohomology — Gromov–Witten Invariants, the WDVV Equation & Mirror Symmetry

Deep dive into Quantum Cohomology — Gromov–Witten invariants (virtual counts of pseudoholomorphic curves in a symplectic manifold X, defining intersection numbers on the moduli space of stable maps M̅_{g,n}(X, β) with Kontsevich’s virtual fundamental class), the quantum cohomology ring QH^*(X) = H^*(X) ⊗ ℂ[[q]] as a deformation of the ordinary cohomology ring with the quantum cup product α ⋆ β = Σ_{β∈H₂(X)} q^β Σ_{a,b} GW_{0,3}^{X,β}(α, β, γ_a) g^{ab} γ_b, the WDVV (Witten–Dijkgraaf–Verlinde–Verlinde) equations (the associativity of the quantum product expressed as differential equations for the prepotential), Frobenius manifolds (Dubrovin, 1992: a flat metric with a compatible associative commutative product satisfying the WDVV equations), mirror symmetry (equality of A-model GW potential and B-model period integrals), and the Mirror Theorem of Givental and Lian–Liu–Yau for counting rational curves on Calabi–Yau threefolds.

“Quantum cohomology is the algebraic structure underlying the counting of holomorphic curves.”

Part 1

⚡ Gromov–Witten Invariants

GW Theory
Moduli of Stable Maps, Virtual Fundamental Class, and Gromov–Witten Invariants
Let (X, ω) be a symplectic manifold (or a smooth projective variety). A stable map (Kontsevich, 1994) is a pair (C, f) where C is a nodal curve (a connected union of smooth rational curves meeting at nodes) and f: C → X is a holomorphic map, with the stability condition: the automorphism group of (C, f) is finite. The moduli space M̅_{g,n}(X, β) parameterizes stable maps of genus g with n marked points, representing β ∈ H₂(X). This is a Deligne–Mumford stack with a perfect obstruction theory (Behrend–Fantechi, Li–Tian) giving a virtual fundamental class [M̅_{g,n}(X,β)]^{vir} ∈ A_{vd}(M̅_{g,n}(X,β)) where vd = (dim X − 3)(1−g) + c₁(X)·β + n. The Gromov–Witten invariant is GW_{g,n}^{X,β}(α₁,…,α_n) = ∫_{[M̅_{g,n}(X,β)]^{vir}} ∏ ev_i^*(α_i) where ev_i: M̅_{g,n}(X,β) → X is evaluation at the i-th marked point. For genus 0, these count rational curves of class β through constraints Poincaré dual to α_i. The g=0, n=3 case gives the structure constants of quantum cohomology. The string equation, dilaton equation, and divisor equation are universal relations. The Kontsevich–Manin axioms include: effective (GW_{g,n}^{X,β}=0 if β=0 and (g,n)≠(0,1),(0,2),(0,3),(1,0)); symmetry under permutation of marked points; fundamental class relation; and the splitting (degeneration) formula.
Gromov–Witten invariants are virtual counts of holomorphic curves, defined via the virtual fundamental class of the moduli space of stable maps.
Part 2

🌀 Quantum Cohomology Rings

Quantum
Deformation of the Cup Product, the Quantum Ring, and the Divisor Equation
The quantum cohomology ring QH^*(X) = (H^*(X; ℂ) ⊗ ℂ[[q₁,…,q_r]], ⋆) deforms the ordinary cup product, parametrized by quantum parameters q_β = exp(−∫_β ω) (in the Novikov ring). Choose basis {γ_a} of H^*(X) with intersection matrix g_{ab} = ∫_X γ_a ∪ γ_b, and inverse g^{ab}. The quantum cup product: γ_a ⋆ γ_b = Σ_{c,d} (∂_a ∂_b ∂_c F₀) g^{cd} γ_d, where F₀ is the genus-zero GW potential: F₀(t) = Σ_{n≥0} (1/n!) Σ_{β,a₁,…,a_n} GW_{0,n}^{X,β}(γ_{a₁},…,γ_{a_n}) t^{a₁}…t^{a_n}. The structure constants: ⟨γ_a ⋆ γ_b, γ_c⟩ = Σ_{β∈H₂(X)} q^β GW_{0,3}^{X,β}(γ_a, γ_b, γ_c). The product is associative (WDVV), graded (deg(q_β) = 2c₁(β)), and commutative. The divisor equation: for D ∈ H²(X), GW_{0,n}^{X,β}(D, α₁,…,) = (∫_β D) GW_{0,n−1}^{X,β}(α₁,…) + Σ GW_{0,n−1}^{X,β}(α₁,…,α_i ∪ D,…). For ℗^N, QH^*(℗^N) ≃ ℂ[H, q]/(H^{N+1} = q). The Batyrev ring for toric varieties.
Quantum cohomology deforms the cup product by contributions from holomorphic spheres, governed by the GW potential.
Part 3

🌊 WDVV Equations and Frobenius Manifolds

Frobenius
The WDVV Equations, Frobenius Manifolds, and Dubrovin’s Theory
The WDVV equations (Witten, Dijkgraaf–Verlinde–Verlinde, 1990) are the associativity of the quantum product as third-order PDEs for F(t): ∂_a ∂_b ∂_e F · g^{ef} · ∂_f ∂_c ∂_d F = ∂_a ∂_c ∂_e F · g^{ef} · ∂_f ∂_b ∂_d F for all a,b,c,d. A Frobenius manifold (Dubrovin, 1992) is a smooth manifold M with a flat metric g, a commutative associative product ◦ on TM with unit e, such that (i) g(u◦v,w) = g(u,v◦w); (ii) the prepotential F satisfies ∂_a ∂_b ∂_c F = g(u_a◦u_b,u_c); (iii) flatness of the connection ∇ + z◦ for all z (the Dubrovin connection). The quantum D-module is the ℂ[[z]]-module H^*(X)⊗ℂ[[z]] with the quantum connection ∇_{∂_a} = ∂_a + (1/z) (γ_a ⋆ ·). Its flat sections give the J-function (Givental’s formalism). The Givental’s Lagrangian cone encodes all genus-zero GW invariants via the formal loop space. The reconstruction theorem (Kontsevich–Manin): genus-zero GW invariants are determined by the WDVV equations and the divisor axiom from the initial data of three-point invariants. The Bershadsky–Cecotti–Ooguri–Vafa (BCOV) theory for higher-genus invariants via the holomorphic anomaly equation. Mirror symmetry for Calabi–Yau threefolds: the A-model GW potential F₀ of X equals the B-model Yukawa coupling on the mirror Y.
Frobenius manifolds formalize the algebraic structure of quantum cohomology; the WDVV equations encode associativity as a system of PDEs for the prepotential.
Part 4

✨ Mirror Symmetry and the Mirror Theorem

Mirror
The Mirror Theorem, Givental’s Formalism, and Enumerative Applications
The Mirror Theorem (Givental, 1996; Lian–Liu–Yau, 1997) gives an explicit formula for genus-zero GW invariants of Calabi–Yau hypersurfaces in toric varieties in terms of the hypergeometric series (the I-function or Yukawa coupling) of the mirror manifold. For the quintic threefold X₅ = {Σ x_i⁵ = 0} ⊂ ℗⁴, the mirror Y is the resolution of the orbifold (℗⁴/Ξ⁵)_ψ (the Dwork family). The mirror map q = e⁻^t identifies the Kähler parameter t of X with the complex modulus ψ of Y. The genus-zero GW potential of X is F₀(t) = ⅔ t³ + 5 ∑_{d≥1} N_d e^{dt} where N_d are the instanton numbers (virtual counts of degree-d rational curves). The mirror theorem: N_d = ∑_{k|d} k⁻^³ n_k where n_k are integers (the BPS invariants). For d=1: N₁ = 2875 (the number of lines on the quintic). d=2: 609250 (conics). d=3: 317206375 (twisted cubics). The BCOV holomorphic anomaly equation (Bershadsky–Cecotti–Ooguri–Vafa, 1993) extends mirror symmetry to higher-genus GW invariants. The Givental’s quantization formalism (the Frobenius manifold of genus-zero invariants determines all higher-genus invariants via the TQFT formalism). The Kontsevich’s homological mirror symmetry connects quantum cohomology to the Fukaya category and the derived category. Applications to enumerative geometry: counting curves on del Pezzo surfaces, the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants (integer invariants derived from GW invariants via the Gopakumar–Vafa formula), and the Donaldson–Thomas/GW correspondence.
The Mirror Theorem allows exact computation of Gromov–Witten invariants via hypergeometric series on the mirror, revolutionizing enumerative geometry.

Higher Genus Gromov–Witten Theory and the BCOV Holomorphic Anomaly

The genus-zero theory captured by the WDVV equations and the quantum cohomology ring is only the first term in a genus expansion of the Gromov–Witten potential. For a compact symplectic manifold X, the full partition function is Z = exp(∑g≥0 λ2g-2 F_g), where λ is the string coupling constant and F_g is the genus-g Gromov–Witten potential. The Bershadsky–Cecotti–Ooguri–Vafa (BCOV) holomorphic anomaly equation, discovered in 1993, governs the anti-holomorphic dependence of F_g on the complex structure moduli of the mirror Calabi–Yau threefold. It takes the form ∂bar i F_g = ½ Cbar ijk (D_j D_k Fg-1 + ∑r=1g-1 D_j F_r D_k Fg-r), where Cbar ijk is the conjugate Yukawa coupling and D denotes the covariant derivative with respect to the Weil–Petersson metric. This recursive structure expresses higher-genus amplitudes in terms of lower-genus data and a finite set of holomorphic ambiguities, which are fixed by boundary conditions at the large complex structure limit, the conifold point, and the orbifold point. The BCOV formalism has been rigorously developed in the mathematical literature by Fang–Lu–Yoshikawa and by Costello–Li, who constructed the higher-genus B-model as a topological quantum field theory on the mirror. In the A-model, the holomorphic anomaly corresponds to the failure of the virtual fundamental class to be holomorphic in the Kähler parameters; the equation provides a powerful computational tool that has yielded explicit genus-one and genus-two invariants for the quintic threefold and for many toric Calabi–Yau varieties.

Givental's Quantization and the Total Ancestor Potential

Givental's quantization formalism lifts the genus-zero Frobenius manifold structure to all genera by constructing a total ancestor potential A_X that encodes the full Gromov–Witten theory of a target X. The starting point is the symplectic loop space H = H^*(X)((z-1)) equipped with the symplectic form Ω(f,g) = Resz=0 (f(-z), g(z)) dz. The genus-zero data — the fundamental solution of the quantum differential equation — defines a Lagrangian cone L ⊂ H. Givental showed that the action of the twisted loop group on L, quantized via the Weil representation, produces a partition function Z = exp(∑g≥0 ħg-1 F_g) that satisfies the Virasoro constraints and the topological recursion relations. The total ancestor potential is obtained by applying the quantization of a certain symplectic transformation (the “R-matrix”) to the product of copies of the Kontsevich–Witten tau-function. This construction proves that the higher-genus Gromov–Witten invariants of any semisimple Frobenius manifold are uniquely determined by the genus-zero data, a result known as the Givental–Teleman classification theorem. For toric varieties, the R-matrix can be computed explicitly from the I-function, yielding closed formulas for all genera. The formalism also provides a conceptual proof of the Virasoro constraints for projective spaces and flag varieties, and it underlies the recent proof of the higher-genus mirror symmetry for toric Calabi–Yau threefolds by Fang–Liu–Zong.

Worked Example: Genus-One Quintic Invariant via BCOV
Compute the genus-one Gromov–Witten invariant N1 for the quintic threefold using the BCOV holomorphic anomaly equation and the known genus-zero Yukawa coupling Cttt = 5/(1-55 q).
The genus-one potential F1 satisfies ∂bar t F1 = ½ Cbar ttt D_t D_t F0. For the quintic, the Weil–Petersson metric is Gtbar t = ∂_t ∂bar t K with K = -log(i∫ Ω∧bar Ω). The Yukawa coupling is Cttt = 5 + ∑d≥1 N_d d3 q^d/(1-q^d). At the large complex structure limit, the holomorphic ambiguity is fixed by the constant map contribution: F1 = -1/24 log(Δ) + const, where Δ = 1-55 q is the discriminant. Expanding F1 = ∑d≥1 N1(d) q^d gives N1(1) = 2875/12? Actually the integer invariant n1 = 0 for the quintic; the genus-one Gromov–Witten invariant N1 = 0. The BCOV computation yields F1 = -1/24 log(1-55 q) + 5/12 log(q) + const, whose q-expansion has no q1 term, confirming N1 = 0.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the genus-one invariant of a Calabi–Yau threefold, first recall the BCOV formula F1 = -1/24 log(Δ) + (c2·J)/24 log(q) + constant. Identify the discriminant Δ from the mirror map and the second Chern class pairing. The constant term is fixed by the constant map contribution (χ/24). Write the q-expansion and read off the coefficient of q^d. This avoids re-deriving the full anomaly equation.

Quantum Differential Equations and the Dubrovin Connection

The quantum differential equation (QDE) is the linear system ∇∂_a s = 0 for the Dubrovin connection ∇ = d + (1/z) (γ_a ⋆ dt^a) on the trivial bundle H^*(X) × ℂ[[z]] over the Kähler moduli space. Here γ_a is a basis of H^*(X), ⋆ is the quantum product, and z is the formal parameter. Flat sections of ∇ are of the form s(t,z) = et^a γ_a / z (1 + O(z)) and their asymptotics as z → 0 encode the Gromov–Witten invariants. The fundamental solution matrix S(t,z) satisfies S(t,z) = Ψ(t) R(t,z) eU(t)/z, where Ψ diagonalizes the quantum product, U is the diagonal matrix of canonical coordinates, and R(t,z) = 1 + R1(t) z + R2(t) z2 + … is the Givental R-matrix. The isomonodromic deformation equations for ∇ are equivalent to the WDVV equations. The Stokes phenomenon of the QDE at z = 0 and z = ∞ is governed by the central connection matrix, which in the Calabi–Yau case coincides with the matrix of BPS invariants. For Fano varieties, the QDE is regular singular at z = 0 and irregular at z = ∞; the asymptotic expansion of flat sections at z = ∞ yields the J-function J_X(t,z) = et^a γ_a / z (1 + ∑n≥1 ⟨γ_a/(z-ψ)⟩0,2 t^a / z + …). The quantum D-module is the ℂ[[z]]-module generated by the components of J_X; its holonomic rank equals dim H^*(X). The reconstruction theorem of Kontsevich–Manin states that the genus-zero Gromov–Witten potential is uniquely determined by the QDE together with the divisor and string equations.

Open Gromov–Witten Invariants and the Fukaya Category

Open Gromov–Witten invariants count holomorphic disks with boundary on a Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ X. The moduli space of stable maps from bordered Riemann surfaces carries a virtual fundamental class, and the resulting invariants depend on the relative spin structure of L. For a toric Calabi–Yau threefold, the Aganagic–Vafa brane construction places a Lagrangian torus fiber over an edge of the moment polytope; the open invariants are then computed by the topological vertex and coincide with the coefficients of the open mirror superpotential W(x) = ∑β n_β x∂β. The open mirror theorem of Chan–Lau–Leung–Tseng identifies these disk invariants with the periods of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model. In the categorical language, the Fukaya category Fuk(X) has objects given by Lagrangian branes and morphism spaces given by Floer cohomology. The open Gromov–Witten potential defines an A_∞-structure on Fuk(X), and the closed-open map sends the quantum cohomology ring QH^*(X) to the Hochschild cohomology HH^*(Fuk(X)). Homological mirror symmetry (Kontsevich, 1994) predicts an equivalence D^b Coh(Y) ≅ Fuk(X) for mirror pairs (X,Y). Under this equivalence, the open invariants correspond to Ext groups between structure sheaves of points on Y, and the disk potential becomes the generating function of open B-model invariants. Recent work by Ganatra–Pardon–Shende proves a version of homological mirror symmetry for Weinstein manifolds, providing a rigorous foundation for the open/closed correspondence.

Gopakumar–Vafa Invariants and the Donaldson–Thomas/GW Correspondence

The Gromov–Witten invariants Ng,β are rational numbers due to multiple covers and automorphisms of stable maps. Gopakumar and Vafa proposed in 1998 that there exist integer invariants ng,β (the BPS invariants) such that ∑g≥0 Ng,β λ2g-2 = ∑g≥0 ng,β (2 sin(λ/2))2g-2. The ng,β count BPS states in the topological string and are conjectured to be the Euler characteristics of the moduli spaces of D-branes. For Calabi–Yau threefolds, the genus-zero GV invariants n0,β coincide with the instanton numbers N_β. The higher-genus GV invariants have been computed for many examples using the BCOV holomorphic anomaly and the topological vertex. The Donaldson–Thomas (DT) invariants DTβ count ideal sheaves of curves in X and are integers by definition. The DT/GW correspondence, proved by Maulik–Nekrasov–Okounkov–Pandharipande for toric threefolds and by Pandharipande–Pixton for local curves, states that the generating series of DT invariants and GW invariants are related by a change of variables: ZDT(q) = ZGW(u) with q = -eiu. This equality implies the integrality of the GW invariants after the GV transformation and provides a powerful computational bridge between the two theories. The correspondence has been extended to the equivariant setting and to the relative theory, leading to the proof of the GW/PT correspondence for stable pairs by Bridgeland and by Toda.

  • Assuming the Givental R-matrix is always the identity; it is nontrivial except for semisimple points where the quantum product is diagonalizable.
  • Neglecting the constant map contribution in F1, which fixes the holomorphic ambiguity and is essential for matching the genus-one GW invariants.

  • Misapplying the DT/GW change of variables q = -eiu without the correct framing factor for non-toric geometries.
  • Overlooking that the GV integrality conjecture is a theorem only for toric Calabi–Yau threefolds and for local curves; it remains open in general.
  • These traps highlight the most frequent errors in advanced quantum cohomology problems.
