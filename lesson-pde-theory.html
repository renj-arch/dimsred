PDE Theory — Elliptic, Parabolic & Hyperbolic Equations, Sobolev Spaces & Regularity

Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) are equations involving an unknown function of several variables and its partial derivatives. The classification of second-order linear PDEs divides them into elliptic (Laplace, Poisson), parabolic (heat), and hyperbolic (wave) types based on the algebraic properties of their principal symbol. Sobolev spaces W^{k,p} provide the natural function space setting for weak solutions, enabling powerful tools like the Lax-Milgram theorem and the Fredholm alternative. The maximum principle (weak and strong) gives qualitative control over elliptic and parabolic solutions. Regularity theory — including the Nash-Moser estimates for parabolic equations with measurable coefficients — shows that weak solutions are often smooth. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem computes the index of elliptic operators in terms of topological invariants, unifying many classical results.

Part 1

📏 Classification and Weak Solutions

Foundations
Second-Order Linear PDEs, Classification by Symbol, Sobolev Spaces, and Weak Formulation
A second-order linear PDE in general form is a^{ij}(x)∂_{ij}u + b^i(x)∂_i u + c(x)u = f(x). The principal symbol is the quadratic form σ(x,ξ) = a^{ij}(x)ξ_iξ_j. Classification: elliptic if a^{ij}ξ_iξ_j > 0 for all ξ ≠ 0 (e.g., Laplace –Δu = f); parabolic if the symbol has one degenerate direction and is positive-definite on the orthogonal complement (e.g., heat equation ∂_t u – Δu = 0); hyperbolic if the symbol is indefinite (e.g., wave equation ∂_t² u – Δu = 0). Weak solutions live in Sobolev spaces: u ∈ H¹(Ω) satisfies ∫ a^{ij}∂_i u ∂_j v dx = ∫ f v dx for all test functions v ∈ H¹₀(Ω). The Sobolev spaces W^{k,p}(Ω) have norm ||u||_{W^{k,p}} = Σ_{|α|≤k} ||∂^{α} u||_{L^p}. Key embedding theorems: Sobolev embedding W^{k,p} ⊂ C^{0,α} when kp > n; trace theorem restricting functions to the boundary; Rellich-Kondrachov compact embedding W^{1,p} ⊂⊂ L^q for q < p* = np/(n–p).
The classification of PDEs by the symbol's signature determines fundamental properties: elliptic equations are smoothing, parabolic equations diffuse, and hyperbolic equations propagate waves.
Part 2

🌍 Elliptic PDE and the Lax-Milgram Theorem

Elliptic Theory
Lax-Milgram Theorem, Fredholm Alternative, Maximum Principle, and Harnack Inequality
The Lax-Milgram theorem is a cornerstone of elliptic theory: if B: H × H → ℝ is a bilinear form that is coercive (B[u,u] ≥ c||u||² for some c > 0) and bounded (|B[u,v]| ≤ C||u|| ||v||), then for every bounded linear functional F on H, there exists a unique u ∈ H with B[u,v] = F(v) for all v ∈ H. Application: the Dirichlet problem –Δu = f in Ω, u|_∂Ω = 0 has a unique weak solution u ∈ H¹₀(Ω) for any f ∈ H^{-1}(Ω). The Fredholm alternative: either the elliptic equation Lu = f has a unique solution for every f, or the homogeneous equation Lu = 0 has nontrivial solutions — in the latter case, solvability requires f to be orthogonal to the kernel. The strong maximum principle (Hopf): if u ∈ C²(Ω) satisfies Lu ≥ 0 and u attains its maximum at an interior point, then u is constant throughout Ω. The Harnack inequality: for nonnegative solutions of elliptic equations, sup_{B_r} u ≤ C inf_{B_r} u, giving strong control over solution oscillation.
Lax-Milgram provides existence and uniqueness for elliptic boundary value problems, while the maximum principle gives qualitative pointwise control over solutions.

🧮 Parabolic and Hyperbolic Equations

Evolution Equations
Heat Equation, Parabolic Regularity, Wave Equation, and Finite Speed of Propagation
The heat equation ∂_t u – Δu = 0 has fundamental solution Φ(x,t) = (4πt)^{-n/2} e^{-|x|²/4t} for t > 0. The parabolic maximum principle: sup_{Ω×[0,T]} u = sup_{Γ_T} u, where Γ_T = (Ω×{0}) ∪ (∂Ω×[0,T]) is the parabolic boundary — implying the solution is bounded by its initial and boundary data. The smoothing effect: even if the initial data is merely L¹, the solution is C^{∞} for t > 0. The De Giorgi-Nash-Moser theorem establishes Hölder continuity of solutions to parabolic equations with merely measurable coefficients, a key regularity result. The wave equation ∂_t² u – Δu = 0 in 1+1 dimensions has the d'Alembert formula: u(x,t) = ½(f(x+t)+f(x–t)) + ½∫_{x–t}^{x+t} g(s) ds for initial conditions u(x,0)=f(x), ∂_t u(x,0)=g(x). Finite speed of propagation: if initial data is supported in the ball B_R, then at time t the solution is supported in B_{R+t} — no signal travels faster than speed 1. Huygens' principle in odd dimensions > 1: waves have sharp trailing edges (no wake), unlike even dimensions where there are lingering tails.
Parabolic equations smooth rough data instantly and diffuse information, while hyperbolic equations propagate singularities along characteristic surfaces at finite speed.

🔥 The Atiyah-Singer Index Theorem and Elliptic Complexes

Index Theory
Elliptic Complexes, de Rham Complex, Hodge Decomposition, and the Atiyah-Singer Index Theorem
An elliptic complex is a sequence of vector bundles Eᵘ and differential operators dᵘ: C^{∞}(Eᵘ) → C^{∞}(E^{ᵘ+1}) with d_{ᵘ+1} ∘ dᵘ = 0 and exact symbol sequence. The canonical example: the de Rham complex 0 → Ω⁰ → Ω¹ → ... → Ω^n → 0 with exterior derivative d. The Hodge decomposition: on a compact Riemannian manifold, Ω^k = im d_{k–1} ⊕ ker Δ_k ⊕ im d^*_k, where Δ = dd^* + d^*d is the Laplace–Beltrami operator. The index of an elliptic operator D: ind(D) = dim ker D – dim ker D^*. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem: ind(D) = ∫_{T^*M} ch(σ(D)) · Td(TM ⊗ ℂ), a topological formula involving the Chern character of the symbol and the Todd class of the complexified tangent bundle. Applications: the Gauss-Bonnet-Chern theorem (index of d+d^* on forms = Euler characteristic χ(M)), the Hirzebruch signature theorem (signature = L-genus), the Riemann-Roch theorem (index of ∂̄ = χ(M, E)), and the positive mass theorem in general relativity (Schoen-Yau, Witten) using spinor Dirac operators.
The Atiyah-Singer index theorem connects analysis (the index of an elliptic operator) with topology (characteristic numbers), revealing deep geometric invariants.

Schauder Estimates and Higher Regularity

Schauder theory gives pointwise Hölder estimates for solutions of linear elliptic and parabolic equations with Hölder continuous coefficients. For a uniformly elliptic operator L = aij(x) ∂ij + bi(x) ∂i + c(x) on a domain Ω, if the coefficients aij, bi, c belong to Cα(Ω) for some 0 < α < 1 and the right‑hand side f ∈ Cα(Ω), then any weak solution u ∈ H1(Ω) lies in C2,α(Ω) and satisfies the interior estimate ‖u‖C2,α(Ω') ≤ C (‖u‖L∞(Ω) + ‖f‖Cα(Ω)) for any Ω' ⊂⊂ Ω. The constant C depends only on ellipticity constants, the Cα norms of the coefficients, and the distance between Ω' and ∂Ω. The proof freezes coefficients at a point, applies Calderón–Zygmund Lp theory to obtain W2,p estimates, and then uses the Campanato–Morrey embedding to reach Hölder spaces. For the Dirichlet problem with boundary data g ∈ C2,α(∂Ω) and a C2,α boundary, the global estimate ‖u‖C2,α(Ω) ≤ C (‖u‖L∞(Ω) + ‖f‖Cα(Ω) + ‖g‖C2,α(∂Ω)) holds. In the parabolic setting, the analogous estimate reads ‖u‖C2+α,1+α/2(Q') ≤ C (‖u‖L∞(Q) + ‖f‖Cα,α/2(Q)) for cylinders Q' ⊂⊂ Q, where the mixed Hölder norm accounts for the different scaling in space and time. These estimates underpin nonlinear theory: they allow contraction mappings in Hölder spaces for quasilinear problems such as the minimal surface or prescribed curvature equations.

Spectral Theory of Elliptic Operators

On a bounded domain Ω with smooth boundary, a uniformly elliptic operator L with smooth coefficients and standard boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, or Robin) defines an unbounded self‑adjoint operator on L2(Ω) with compact resolvent. The spectrum is a discrete sequence of real eigenvalues λ1 ≤ λ2 ≤ … → +∞, each of finite multiplicity, with eigenfunctions forming an orthonormal basis of L2(Ω). The first Dirichlet eigenvalue has the variational characterization λ1 = infu∈H10(Ω){0} ∫Ω (aij ∂iu ∂ju + c u2) dx / ∫Ω u2 dx, yielding the Rayleigh quotient and the Poincaré inequality ‖u‖L2 ≤ λ1-1/2 ‖∇u‖L2. Weyl's law gives the asymptotic distribution N(λ) ∼ (2π)-n ωn vol(Ω) λn/2, where ωn is the volume of the unit ball in ℝn. For the Neumann problem the first eigenvalue is zero with constant eigenfunctions; the second eigenvalue λ2 controls the convergence rate to equilibrium for the heat equation with insulated boundary. Spectral theory underpins the functional calculus: for any Borel function φ, the operator φ(L) is defined via the spectral theorem, enabling fractional powers Ls and the spaces H2s(Ω) = Dom(Ls). The heat kernel e-tL(x,y) admits a short‑time asymptotic expansion whose coefficients are local geometric invariants, linking analysis to the Atiyah–Singer index theorem.

Nonlinear Elliptic Equations and Variational Methods

Many nonlinear elliptic problems arise as Euler–Lagrange equations of integral functionals J(u) = ∫Ω F(x, u, ∇u) dx. If F is convex in the gradient variable and satisfies p‑growth conditions (c1 |ξ|p ≤ F(x, u, ξ) ≤ c2 (1+|ξ|p)), the direct method yields a minimizer in W1,p0(Ω). The steps are: (1) coercivity gives a bounded minimizing sequence; (2) reflexivity of W1,p provides a weakly convergent subsequence; (3) lower semicontinuity from convexity ensures the limit is a minimizer. For non‑convex problems such as the Yamabe equation −Δ u + cn R u = u(n+2)/(n-2) on a compact manifold, the mountain‑pass theorem or Lions' concentration‑compactness principle recovers compactness lost at the critical Sobolev exponent. The Palais–Smale condition fails at the critical exponent; concentration‑compactness classifies the loss into vanishing, dichotomy, and concentration, allowing existence proofs by ruling out the first two. In the subcritical case, the Sobolev embedding is compact and Palais–Smale holds, so standard minimax methods apply. Regularity of weak solutions follows from De Giorgi–Nash–Moser theory for divergence-form equations and from Schauder estimates for non‑divergence-form equations with Hölder continuous coefficients.

Boundary Regularity and Trace Theorems

The trace operator γ: W1,p(Ω) → Lp(∂Ω) is well defined, linear, bounded, and surjective for 1 ≤ p < ∞ when Ω has a Lipschitz boundary. For p > 1 the trace space is the Besov space B1-1/pp,p(∂Ω), interpolating between Lp and W1,p(∂Ω). If u solves a uniformly elliptic equation in divergence form with Cα coefficients and boundary data g ∈ C1,α(∂Ω), then u ∈ C1,α(Ω̅) and the normal derivative ∂ν u exists continuously up to the boundary. For the Dirichlet problem, the boundary Harnack principle states that the ratio of two positive solutions vanishing on a portion of the boundary is Hölder continuous up to that portion. In the Neumann problem, the conormal derivative aij νi ∂j u belongs to the dual of the trace space, and the compatibility condition ∫Ω f dx + ∫∂Ω h dS = 0 is necessary for solvability. For mixed boundary conditions (Dirichlet on ΓD, Neumann on ΓN), regularity near the interface ∂ΓD ∩ ∂ΓN depends on the angle; if the interface meets the boundary at a right angle with smooth data, the solution gains full H2 regularity, otherwise singularities of the form rπ/ω appear, where ω is the interior angle. These singularities are captured by weighted Sobolev (Kondratiev) spaces and are essential for finite element error analysis on non‑convex polygons.

Microlocal Analysis and Propagation of Singularities

Microlocal analysis refines singular support by tracking both position and direction (cotangent variables). The wave front set WF(u) ⊂ T*Ω 0 consists of those (x, ξ) where u fails to be microlocally smooth. For a real principal type operator P (e.g., the wave operator □ = ∂t2 − Δ), Hörmander's theorem states that WF(u) WF(Pu) is a union of maximally extended null bicharacteristics of the principal symbol p(x, ξ). For the wave equation, p = τ2 − |ξ|2, and the bicharacteristics are light rays satisfying Hamilton's equations. Singularities therefore travel at finite speed along light cones, and no new singularities appear away from the characteristic set. For elliptic operators the characteristic set is empty, so WF(u) = WF(Pu), the microlocal expression of elliptic regularity. The calculus of pseudodifferential operators provides a symbolic calculus modulo smoothing operators, enabling parametrix construction for elliptic and hyperbolic problems. The Atiyah–Singer index theorem can be proved by constructing a parametrix for the elliptic complex and computing its trace via heat kernel asymptotics, which are local invariants of the symbol. In nonlinear problems, paradifferential calculus (Bony) linearizes the equation at high frequencies, allowing propagation of regularity for quasilinear wave equations such as the Einstein vacuum equations.

Nonlinear Parabolic Equations and Blow‑up Phenomena

Nonlinear parabolic equations such as the semilinear heat equation ∂ₜu = Δu + uᵖ on a bounded domain Ω with Dirichlet boundary conditions exhibit a sharp threshold between global existence and finite‑time blow‑up. The critical exponent p_F = 1 + 2/n, known as the Fujita exponent, separates the two regimes: for 1 < p ≤ p_F every nontrivial nonnegative solution blows up in finite time, while for p > p_F there exist both global solutions (for small initial data) and blowing‑up solutions (for large data). The proof uses a combination of the comparison principle, self‑similar variables, and the construction of explicit sub‑ and supersolutions of the form u(x,t) = (T−t)⁻¹ᐟ⁽ᵖ⁻¹⁾ f(|x|/√(T−t)).

When blow‑up occurs, the solution develops a singularity at a finite time T. The blow‑up set can be a single point, a compact set, or the whole domain depending on the geometry of Ω and the initial profile. For the critical exponent p = p_F the blow‑up rate is logarithmic rather than algebraic, and the asymptotic profile is governed by the stationary solution of the rescaled equation. In the supercritical case p > p_F, the blow‑up profile converges to a nontrivial steady state of the elliptic problem Δw + wᵖ = 0 in ℝⁿ, which is unique up to scaling and translation. These profiles are obtained by a Lyapunov functional argument and a careful analysis of the linearized operator around the self‑similar solution.

Beyond the power‑type nonlinearity, equations with gradient terms such as ∂ₜu = Δu + |∇u|^q or with nonlocal diffusion (fractional Laplacian) display different blow‑up mechanisms. For gradient nonlinearities the critical exponent is q = 2, and blow‑up can be driven by gradient concentration even when the L^∞ norm remains bounded. In the nonlocal setting the Fujita exponent becomes p_F = 1 + 2s/n for the fractional Laplacian (−Δ)^s, reflecting the slower diffusion. The study of blow‑up for systems of parabolic equations introduces additional phenomena such as simultaneous versus component‑wise blow‑up, and the interplay between diffusion coefficients and reaction terms.

Applications of blow‑up theory appear in combustion models, population dynamics, and geometric flows. In the prescribed scalar curvature flow on a compact manifold, the evolution equation for the conformal factor is a nonlinear parabolic equation whose blow‑up corresponds to the formation of singular metrics. Understanding the precise blow‑up profile is essential for performing surgery and continuing the flow past singularities, a technique central to the proof of the geometrization conjecture.

Geometric Analysis and the Yamabe Problem

The Yamabe problem asks whether every compact Riemannian manifold (Mⁿ,g) with n ≥ 3 admits a metric conformal to g having constant scalar curvature. Writing the conformal metric as ũ = u4/(n−2) g, the scalar curvature transforms according to the conformal Laplacian L_g u = −c_n Δ_g u + R_g u, where c_n = 4(n−1)/(n−2) and R_g is the scalar curvature of g. The problem reduces to finding a positive solution of L_g u = λ u(n+2)/(n−2) with λ constant, which is the Euler‑Lagrange equation of the Yamabe functional Y(u) = (∫_M (c_n|∇u|² + R_g u²) dV_g) / (∫_M u2n/(n−2) dV_g)(n−2)/n.

The solution history spans three major steps. Yamabe’s original 1960 attempt contained a gap in the compactness argument for the minimizing sequence. Trudinger (1968) recovered compactness for the subcritical case and introduced the Aubin–Trudinger inequality. Aubin (1976) solved the problem for manifolds not locally conformally flat with positive Yamabe invariant by constructing a test function that beats the Sobolev constant of the sphere. Finally, Schoen (1984) completed the proof for the remaining cases using the positive mass theorem from general relativity; he showed that if the Yamabe invariant equals that of the sphere, the manifold must be conformally equivalent to the standard sphere, and the minimizer is obtained by a conformal transformation of the stereographic projection.

The positive mass theorem enters through the analysis of the Green’s function of the conformal Laplacian. Near a point p, the Green’s function has an expansion G(x) = d(x,p)2−n + A + o(1), where the constant A is the mass. Schoen proved that A > 0 unless (M,g) is conformally flat with zero Weyl tensor, in which case the mass vanishes and the manifold is the sphere. This geometric insight links the analytic minimization problem to global differential topology and has inspired further work on the σ_k‑Yamabe problem and fully nonlinear curvature equations.

Variational methods for the Yamabe functional also provide a framework for the prescribed scalar curvature problem: given a function K on M, find a conformal metric with scalar curvature K. The associated Euler‑Lagrange equation is L_g u = K u(n+2)/(n−2). When K changes sign, the functional is unbounded below and minimax methods (mountain‑pass, linking) are required. The Palais–Smale condition fails at the critical Sobolev exponent, and the concentration‑compactness principle of Lions classifies the loss of compactness into bubbles that are precisely the standard solutions on ℝⁿ. This bubbling analysis is a prototype for many critical exponent problems in geometric analysis.

Technique: Blow‑up Profile Construction
Consider the semilinear heat equation ∂ₜu = Δu + u^p on ℝⁿ with p > 1 + 2/n. Show that any solution blowing up at time T admits a self‑similar profile w(y) = limt→T (T−t)1/(p−1) u(y√(T−t), t) satisfying the elliptic equation Δw − (1/2) y·∇w − w/(p−1) + w^p = 0.
Introduce the similarity variables s = −log(T−t), y = x/√(T−t) and set v(y,s) = (T−t)1/(p−1) u(x,t). Then ∂ₛv = Δv − (1/2) y·∇v − v/(p−1) + v^p. A blowing‑up solution corresponds to a trajectory v(·,s) that remains bounded in L^∞ as s→∞. Standard parabolic regularity and the Lyapunov functional E(v) = ∫ (½|∇v|² + v²/(2(p−1)) − vp+1/(p+1)) e−|y|²/4 dy show that the ω‑limit set consists of stationary solutions of the rescaled equation, i.e. solutions of Δw − (1/2) y·∇w − w/(p−1) + w^p = 0. Uniqueness of the positive radial solution (up to scaling) follows from a shooting argument for the ODE satisfied by radial profiles.

Spectral Geometry and Heat Kernel Asymptotics

The heat kernel K(t,x,y) of the Laplace–Beltrami operator on a compact Riemannian manifold (M,g) admits a short‑time asymptotic expansion known as the Minakshisundaram–Pleijel expansion: K(t,x,x) ∼ (4πt)−n/2 ∑k=0^∞ a_k(x) t^k as t → 0⁺. The coefficients a_k(x) are local invariants of the metric; a0(x) = 1, a1(x) = (1/6) R(x) where R is the scalar curvature, and a2(x) involves the Ricci tensor and the scalar curvature squared. Integrating over M yields the trace expansion Tr(etΔ) ∼ (4πt)−n/2 ∑k=0^∞ A_k t^k with A_k = ∫_M a_k(x) dV_g. These integrals are spectral invariants; for example A1 = (1/6) ∫_M R dV_g is proportional to the total scalar curvature.

The heat kernel asymptotics provide a powerful tool for proving index theorems. The Atiyah–Singer index theorem for the Dirac operator can be derived by evaluating the supertrace of the heat kernel of the square of the Dirac operator and using the McKean–Singer formula ind(D) = Str(e−tD²). The local index density is identified with the Â‑genus form, and the small‑t expansion shows that only the term of order t⁰ contributes to the index, yielding the topological formula. This heat‑kernel proof avoids the heavy pseudodifferential calculus of the original proof and extends naturally to families of operators and to the eta invariant.

Beyond the trace, the off‑diagonal behavior of the heat kernel controls the spectral gap and the rate of convergence to equilibrium for the heat flow. Gaussian upper bounds of the form K(t,x,y) ≤ C t−n/2 e−d(x,y)²/(Ct) hold on manifolds with nonnegative Ricci curvature (Li–Yau) and more generally on spaces satisfying a Poincaré inequality and volume doubling. These estimates are the analytic backbone of the theory of analysis on metric measure spaces and underlie the definition of RCD(K,N) spaces in synthetic Ricci curvature theory.

In spectral geometry, the heat trace determines the spectrum asymptotically via Weyl’s law N(λ) ∼ (ω_n/(2π)^n) Vol(M) λn/2, where N(λ) counts eigenvalues ≤ λ. The second term in the Weyl expansion involves the integral of the scalar curvature and distinguishes between Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions on manifolds with boundary. The heat kernel also encodes the wave trace through the cosine transform, linking the length spectrum of closed geodesics to the singularities of the wave trace (Duistermaat–Guillemin trace formula). This connection is central to quantum chaos and inverse spectral problems.

Inverse Problems for Elliptic Equations

The Calderón problem asks whether the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map Λ_γ : H1/2(∂Ω) → H−1/2(∂Ω) associated with the conductivity equation ∇·(γ∇u) = 0 in a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ uniquely determines the conductivity γ(x) > 0. For n ≥ 3, Sylvester and Uhlmann (1987) proved uniqueness using complex geometrical optics solutions of the form u = ex·ζ(1 + ψ_ζ) with ζ ∈ ℂⁿ, ζ·ζ = 0, and ‖ψ_ζ‖H¹ → 0 as |ζ| → ∞. The construction reduces the problem to the injectivity of the attenuated Radon transform, which is solved by Fourier analysis.

In two dimensions the problem is more delicate because the complex geometrical optics method fails. Nachman (1996) established uniqueness for γ ∈ W2,p using a reduction to a ∂̄‑equation and the theory of quasiconformal mappings. Later, Astala and Päivärinta (2006) extended uniqueness to merely L^∞ conductivities by developing a new approach based on the stochastic representation of solutions and the theory of Beltrami equations. The two‑dimensional case is also special because the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map determines the conductivity up to a conformal change of coordinates, reflecting the conformal invariance of the conductivity equation in the plane.

Stability estimates quantify how errors in the measured Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map propagate to errors in the reconstructed conductivity. For n ≥ 3, Alessandrini (1988) proved a logarithmic stability estimate ‖γ₁ − γ₂‖L^∞ ≤ C |log ‖Λγ₁ − Λγ₂‖|−σ under a priori bounds on γ. This logarithmic rate is optimal, as shown by Mandache (2001) using a sequence of highly oscillatory conductivities. In two dimensions, Hölder stability holds for conductivities with a priori bounds in suitable function spaces, reflecting the better behavior of the ∂̄‑problem.

Practical reconstruction algorithms include the D‑bar method for the two‑dimensional problem, which solves a nonlinear integral equation for the scattering transform, and the iterative linearization (Newton‑type) methods for higher dimensions. Regularization is essential because the inverse problem is severely ill‑posed; common choices are Tikhonov regularization with total variation penalty to preserve edges, and Bayesian approaches that incorporate prior statistical information. The Calderón problem has direct applications in electrical impedance tomography, medical imaging, and nondestructive testing of materials.

Stochastic Partial Differential Equations and Malliavin Calculus

Stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) arise when a deterministic PDE is perturbed by a random noise term, typically a space‑time white noise ξ(t,x). The stochastic heat equation ∂ₜu = Δu + ξ on a bounded domain with Dirichlet boundary conditions is the prototypical example. The solution is a random field that can be represented via the stochastic convolution u(t) = ∫₀ᵗ e(t−s)Δ dW(s), where W is a cylindrical Wiener process on L²(Ω). This representation shows that the solution is almost surely in Cα,β for any α < 1/2, β < 1/4 in space and time respectively, reflecting the irregularity of white noise.

Malliavin calculus provides a differential calculus on the Wiener space that allows one to differentiate functionals of the noise with respect to the underlying Brownian motion. For the stochastic heat equation, the Malliavin derivative Dr,yu(t,x) satisfies the linearized equation ∂ₜD = ΔD + δ(t−r)δ(x−y) and can be expressed explicitly via the heat kernel. The non‑degeneracy of the Malliavin covariance matrix implies that the law of u(t,x) has a smooth density with respect to Lebesgue measure, a result due to Bally and Pardoux (1998). This regularity of densities is crucial for statistical inference and for proving the existence of transition densities for SPDEs.

For nonlinear SPDEs such as the stochastic Navier–Stokes equations or the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation ∂ₜh = Δh + |∇h|² + ξ, the solution theory requires renormalization because the nonlinearity is ill‑defined for the rough noise. Hairer’s theory of regularity structures (2014) and the paracontrolled calculus of Gubinelli, Imkeller, and Perkowski (2015) provide systematic frameworks to give meaning to these equations. In the KPZ case, the Cole–Hopf transform h = log Z converts the equation into the stochastic heat equation with multiplicative noise ∂ₜZ = ΔZ + Z ξ, which can be solved using Itô calculus after a suitable renormalization of the noise.

Ergodic properties of SPDEs are studied via the Markov semigroup P_t φ(u) = E[φ(u(t;u₀))]. The existence of a unique invariant measure and exponential mixing in Wasserstein distance have been established for the stochastic Navier–Stokes equations on the torus (Hairer–Mattingly 2006) using the asymptotic strong Feller property and a controllability argument. Malliavin calculus enters the proof through the Malliavin differentiability of the solution map and the non‑degeneracy of the noise projected onto the unstable directions. These results connect the analytic theory of SPDEs with statistical physics and the rigorous justification of turbulence models.

  • Assuming the Yamabe minimizer always exists without checking the sign of the Yamabe invariant; for negative invariant the infimum is not attained and one must use a different variational formulation.
  • Neglecting the off‑diagonal Gaussian bounds when applying heat kernel asymptotics to manifolds with boundary; the presence of a boundary introduces reflected Brownian motion and additional boundary terms in the expansion.
  • Believing that the Calderón problem in two dimensions has the same uniqueness proof as in higher dimensions; the two‑dimensional case requires quasiconformal mapping techniques and the ∂̄‑equation.

Viscosity Solutions and Hamilton‑Jacobi Equations

The theory of viscosity solutions, developed by Crandall and Lions in the early 1980s, provides a framework for first‑order and degenerate second‑order nonlinear PDEs where classical solutions fail globally. For a Hamilton‑Jacobi equation ∂ₜu + H(x, ∇u) = 0 with continuous Hamiltonian H, a viscosity subsolution (resp. supersolution) is an upper (resp. lower) semicontinuous function u such that for any smooth test function φ touching u from above (resp. below) at (t₀, x₀), the inequality ∂ₜφ(t₀, x₀) + H(x₀, ∇φ(t₀, x₀)) ≤ 0 (resp. ≥ 0) holds. A viscosity solution is both a sub‑ and supersolution. The uniform limit of viscosity solutions remains a viscosity solution, enabling construction via approximation schemes like vanishing viscosity u^ε solving ∂ₜu^ε + H(x, ∇u^ε) = εΔu^ε. The comparison principle — if u is a subsolution and v a supersolution with u ≤ v on the parabolic boundary, then u ≤ v everywhere — guarantees uniqueness. For convex Hamiltonians H(p) = |p|²/2, the Hopf‑Lax formula gives u(t, x) = inf_y { u₀(y) + t L((x−y)/t) } where L is the Legendre transform of H, linking to optimal control and dynamic programming. For degenerate parabolic equations ∂ₜu − F(x, u, ∇u, D²u) = 0 with F degenerate elliptic, the definition extends with the matrix inequality F(x, u, p, X) ≤ 0 for subsolutions. The Barles‑Souganidis theorem states that any monotone, stable, consistent finite‑difference scheme converges to the viscosity solution. Applications include front propagation (level‑set method), stochastic control (Hamilton‑Jacobi‑Bellman equations), and differential games (Isaacs equations). The theory extends to second‑order equations on manifolds and nonlocal Hamilton‑Jacobi equations with fractional Laplacians.

Homogenization Theory and Periodic Media

Homogenization studies the asymptotic behavior of solutions to PDEs with rapidly oscillating coefficients, modeling composite materials or porous media. The prototypical problem is −div(A(x/ε)∇u^ε) = f in Ω with Dirichlet conditions, where A(y) is periodic and ε ≪ 1. As ε → 0, u^ε converges weakly in H¹(Ω) to u⁰ satisfying −div(A*∇u⁰) = f, where the constant effective tensor A* is given by A*ij = ∫_Y A(y)(e_i + ∇_y χ_i) · (e_j + ∇_y χ_j) dy. Here Y = [0,1]^n is the unit cell and χ_i are correctors solving −div_y(A(y)(e_i + ∇_y χ_i)) = 0 in Y with periodic boundary conditions. Two‑scale convergence (Nguetseng, Allaire) provides a rigorous framework: u^ε two‑scale converges to u⁰(x,y) if ∫_Ω u^ε(x) φ(x, x/ε) dx → ∫_Ω∫_Y u⁰(x,y) φ(x,y) dy dx for all smooth φ, capturing macroscopic and microscopic information. For the heat equation ∂ₜu^ε − div(A(x/ε)∇u^ε) = 0, the homogenized equation is ∂ₜu⁰ − div(A*∇u⁰) = 0 with the same A*. In high‑contrast media where A takes values of order 1 and ε², the limit exhibits a memory effect described by a nonlocal equation. Stochastic homogenization replaces periodicity with stationary ergodic coefficients; qualitative theory (Kozlov, Papanicolaou‑Varadhan) proves almost sure convergence to a deterministic A*, while quantitative estimates (Gloria‑Otto, Armstrong‑Smart) give convergence rates in terms of correlation length. For nonlinear monotone operators −div(a(x/ε, ∇u^ε)) = f, the homogenized operator is −div(a*(∇u⁰)) where a*(ξ) = ⨏_Y a(y, ξ + ∇_y χ_ξ) dy and χ_ξ solves the nonlinear cell problem. Homogenization of the wave equation yields dispersive effective equations when microstructure resonates with the wavelength. The theory extends to perforated domains (Neumann holes) where the limit may involve a zero‑order term (strange term) and to high‑frequency waves in periodic media (Bloch waves).

Worked Example: Cell Problem for a Laminated Composite
Consider the conductivity tensor A(y) = diag(a₁(y₁), a₂, …, a_n) in Y = [0,1]^n where a₁(y₁) = α for y₁ ∈ [0, θ) and a₁(y₁) = β for y₁ ∈ [θ, 1), with α, β > 0 and 0 < θ < 1. All other a_i are constant. Compute the homogenized tensor A* and the corrector χ₁.
The cell problem for χ₁ is −d/dy₁(a₁(y₁)(1 + dχ₁/dy₁)) = 0 in (0,1) with periodic boundary conditions. Integrating once gives a₁(y₁)(1 + χ₁') = C (constant). Hence χ₁' = C/a₁(y₁) − 1. Periodicity requires ∫₀¹ χ₁' dy₁ = 0, so C ∫₀¹ dy₁/a₁(y₁) = 1. The integral is θ/α + (1−θ)/β, thus C = 1 / (θ/α + (1−θ)/β) = αβ / (θβ + (1−θ)α). This C is the harmonic mean of a₁, equaling A*11. For i ≥ 2, the cell problem is −a_i d²χ_i/dy_i² = 0 with periodic BC, so χ_i ≡ 0 and A*ii = a_i. The homogenized tensor is diagonal with A*11 = (θ/α + (1−θ)/β)⁻¹ and A*ii = a_i for i ≥ 2. The corrector is χ₁(y₁) = C ∫₀y₁ ds/a₁(s) − y₁ + constant, chosen to have zero mean over Y.
Exam Strategy: Homogenization and Two‑Scale Convergence
When a question asks for the homogenized limit, first identify the scaling (ε in coefficients, domain, or both). Write the two‑scale ansatz u^ε(x) ≈ u⁰(x, x/ε) + ε u¹(x, x/ε) and plug into the PDE. Collect O(ε⁻¹) terms to get the cell problem for u¹, determining the correctors. The O(1) terms yield the homogenized equation after averaging over the cell. For multiple‑choice questions, remember that A* is symmetric positive definite if A is, and A*ij = A*ji even if A is not symmetric (the homogenized tensor is always symmetric for scalar equations). The harmonic mean appears for layered media normal to layers; the arithmetic mean appears for the parallel direction.

Free Boundary Problems and the Obstacle Problem

Free boundary problems arise when the PDE domain is unknown and determined as part of the solution. The classical obstacle problem minimizes the Dirichlet energy ∫_Ω |∇u|² dx among functions u ≥ ψ (the obstacle) with given boundary data. The solution satisfies Δu = 0 in the non‑coincidence set {u > ψ} and Δu ≤ 0 distributionally, with u = ψ on the coincidence set {u = ψ}. The free boundary is ∂{u > ψ} ∩ Ω. Caffarelli's regularity theory shows that for smooth ψ, the free boundary is locally a C¹,α hypersurface except at a singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most n−1. At regular points, u(x) = ψ(x₀) + ∇ψ(x₀)·(x−x₀) + ½ (ν·(x−x₀))₊² + o(|x−x₀|²) where ν is the inward normal. Optimal regularity is u ∈ C¹,¹ (Lipschitz gradient), proved via the monotonicity formula Φ(r) = r⁻⁴ ∫B_r |∇u|² dx. For the thin obstacle problem (Signorini problem) where u ≥ 0 on a hyperplane, the solution is C¹,α with α = ½ and the free boundary is C¹,α. The one‑phase Stefan problem ∂ₜu − Δu = 0 in {u > 0}, u = 0 on ∂{u > 0}, |∇u| = V models melting ice; its weak formulation uses the enthalpy variable. The two‑phase problem has u solving Δu = 0 in both phases with jump conditions on the interface. Viscosity solutions for free boundaries treat the problem as a Bernoulli‑type condition |∇u| = Q on the free boundary. The Alt‑Caffarelli functional J(u) = ∫ |∇u|² + Q² χu>0 dx gives a variational approach. Regularity for the two‑phase problem requires nondegeneracy and Lipschitz regularity of u. The parabolic obstacle problem ∂ₜu − Δu ≥ 0, u ≥ ψ, (∂ₜu − Δu)(u − ψ) = 0 has a free boundary moving with finite speed; its regularity is more delicate due to time dependence. Applications include American options (optimal stopping), fluid filtration in porous media, and elastoplasticity.

Carleman Estimates and Unique Continuation Principles

Carleman estimates are weighted energy inequalities of the form τ³ ∫ e2τφ |u|² dx + τ ∫ e2τφ |∇u|² dx ≤ C ∫ e2τφ |Pu|² dx for a differential operator P and weight φ with no critical points. They are the primary tool for proving unique continuation: if u solves Pu = 0 and vanishes to infinite order at a point, then u ≡ 0. For the Laplacian, the classical weight is φ(x) = |x|², giving τ³ ∫B_R e2τ|x|² |u|² dx ≤ C ∫B_R e2τ|x|² |Δu|² dx for τ ≥ τ₀. This implies the strong unique continuation property (SUCP): if u ∈ H²_loc and Δu = Vu with V ∈ Ln/2 (n ≥ 3) or V ∈ L^p (p > n/2), and u vanishes to infinite order at x₀, then u ≡ 0. The critical space Ln/2 is sharp (Koch‑Tataru). For the heat operator ∂ₜ − Δ, the weight φ(t,x) = |x|²/(T−t) + λ log(T−t) yields backward uniqueness: if u(T) = 0, then u ≡ 0. The Carleman estimate for the wave operator □ = ∂ₜ² − Δ uses φ(t,x) = |x|² − t² and gives the Holmgren‑John theorem for non‑characteristic surfaces. For Schrödinger operators i∂ₜ + Δ + V, the estimate with φ = |x|² + βt² proves unique continuation for V ∈ L^∞_t Ln/2_x. The quantitative unique continuation (three‑ball inequality) states that for Δu = Vu, ‖u‖L²(Br₂) ≤ C ‖u‖L²(Br₁)^δ ‖u‖L²(Br₃)1−δ for 0 < r₁ < r₂ < r₃, with δ depending on the potential norm. This is crucial for control theory (observability) and inverse problems. The method extends to systems (Stokes, elasticity) and degenerate operators (Grushin, Kolmogorov). The key geometric condition is pseudoconvexity of the level sets of φ with respect to the principal symbol of P. For elliptic operators, any function without critical points works; for hyperbolic operators, φ must be strictly pseudoconvex.

Strichartz Estimates and Dispersive Equations

Strichartz estimates quantify dispersive decay for linear evolution equations and underpin well‑posedness theory for nonlinear dispersive PDEs. For the free Schrödinger equation i∂ₜu + Δu = 0 in ℝⁿ, the solution u(t) = eitΔu₀ satisfies the dispersive estimate ‖eitΔu₀‖L^∞ ≤ C |t|−n/2 ‖u₀‖L¹. Interpolating with L² conservation ‖eitΔu₀‖L² = ‖u₀‖L² and applying the TT* argument yields Strichartz estimates: for any admissible pair (q,r) with 2/q + n/r = n/2, q ≥ 2, (q,r,n) ≠ (2,∞,2), we have ‖eitΔu₀‖L^q_t L^r_x ≤ C ‖u₀‖L². The endpoint (q,r) = (2, 2n/(n−2)) is included for n ≥ 3 (Keel‑Tao). For the inhomogeneous equation i∂ₜu + Δu = F, the dual estimate is ‖∫₀ᵗ ei(t−s)Δ F(s) ds‖L^q_t L^r_x ≤ C ‖F‖Lq'_t Lr'_x where (q,r) and (q̃,r̃) are admissible pairs. These estimates imply local well‑posedness for i∂ₜu + Δu = ±|u|p−1u in H^s for s ≥ s_c = n/2 − 2/(p−1) (scaling critical regularity). For the energy‑critical case p = 1 + 4/(n−2) (n ≥ 3), global well‑posedness and scattering hold for small data in Ḣ¹. For the wave equation □u = 0, Strichartz estimates are ‖u‖L^q_t L^r_x ≤ C (‖u₀‖Ḣ^γ + ‖u₁‖Ḣγ−1) with 1/q + (n−1)/r = (n−1)/2 − γ, q ≥ 2, and γ = n/2 − 1/q − 1/r. The endpoint (q,r) = (2, 2(n−1)/(n−3)) fails for n ≥ 4 (Montgomery‑Smith). For the Klein‑Gordon equation (∂ₜ² − Δ + m²)u = 0, estimates are similar with a different admissibility condition. The Airy equation ∂ₜu + ∂ₓ³u = 0 has Strichartz estimates with 2/q + 3/r = 3/2. For Schrödinger equations on compact manifolds or with potentials, estimates hold with a loss of derivatives (Burq‑Gérard‑Tzvetkov). Profile decomposition (Bahouri‑Gérard) and concentration compactness (Kenig‑Merle) use Strichartz estimates to prove global existence and scattering for critical nonlinear equations. The restriction conjecture (Tomas‑Stein) is equivalent to the Strichartz estimate for the wave equation on the light cone.

  • Thinking homogenization always yields a constant‑coefficient limit; for high‑contrast media or perforated domains with critical hole size, the limit may be nonlocal or involve a memory term.

These topics — viscosity solutions, homogenization, free boundaries, Carleman estimates, and Strichartz estimates — form the modern toolkit for analyzing nonlinear and multiscale PDEs beyond the linear elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic theories of the core curriculum.

Nonlinear Hyperbolic Conservation Laws and Shock Formation

Systems of conservation laws ∂tU + ∂xF(U) = 0 with U ∈ ℝm and a smooth flux F arise in gas dynamics, traffic flow, and shallow‑water models. The fundamental well‑posedness theory rests on the notion of entropy solutions introduced by Kruzhkov (1970) for scalar equations and extended by Lax and Glimm to systems. An entropy pair (η,q) satisfies ∇η·∇F = ∇q; the entropy inequality ∂tη(U) + ∂xq(U) ≤ 0 in the sense of distributions selects the physically relevant weak solution. For genuinely nonlinear characteristic fields (∇λi·ri ≠ 0) the Lax entropy condition requires that shocks satisfy λi(UL) > s > λi(UR), where s is the shock speed given by the Rankine–Hugoniot relation s[U] = [F(U)]. In linearly degenerate fields (∇λ·r = 0) contact discontinuities propagate with speed λ and satisfy [U] ∥ r. The Glimm scheme, a random‑choice method, constructs global BV solutions for small total variation data; an interaction potential controls wave interactions and yields an L1 contraction property for scalar equations (for systems, contraction holds only under additional structural assumptions). For large data, the formation of vacuum or concentration measures may occur; the compensated compactness framework of DiPerna (1983) and the kinetic formulation of Lions‑Perthame‑Tadmor (1994) provide alternative existence proofs. The Riemann problem, with piecewise constant initial data, is solved by a self‑similar fan of rarefactions, shocks, and contacts; its solution is the building block for Godunov‑type finite volume schemes. In multi‑dimensions, the theory is far less complete: the existence of global entropy solutions for the compressible Euler equations remains open, while the convex integration method of De Lellis‑Székelyhidi (2009) produces infinitely many weak solutions, highlighting the need for additional admissibility criteria such as the entropy rate criterion of Dafermos (1979).

Elliptic Regularity on Domains with Corners and Edges

Classical Schauder and Lp theory for the Dirichlet problem −Δu = f in a smooth domain Ω ⊂ ℝn yields u ∈ H2(Ω) for f ∈ L2(Ω). On polygonal or polyhedral domains the solution generally fails to belong to H2; instead one obtains u ∈ H1+α(Ω) for any α < π/ω, where ω is the largest interior angle (Kellogg, 1971). The singular part of the solution can be expanded in terms of corner eigenfunctions rλφ(θ) where λ solves the transcendental equation sin(λω) = 0 for the Laplacian with Dirichlet conditions. For the Neumann problem the exponent satisfies cos(λω) = 0. In three dimensions edges generate singularities of the form rμψ(θ,φ) with μ determined by the edge angle and the boundary condition type (Grisvard, 1985). Weighted Sobolev spaces Kma(Ω) (Kondratiev spaces) with norm ‖u‖Kma2 = Σ|α|≤m ∫Ω r2(a−m+|α|) |Dαu|2 dx capture the precise regularity: if f ∈ K0a then u ∈ K2a provided a avoids the critical weights a = 1 − λ. The finite element method on graded meshes with refinement factor r ∼ h1/α restores optimal convergence rates O(hp) for polynomial degree p. For mixed boundary conditions (Dirichlet on one side of a corner, Neumann on the other) the singular exponent is the smallest positive root of tan(λω) = −λ (Costabel‑Dauge, 1999). These results are essential for reliable numerical simulation in domains with re‑entrant corners such as L‑shaped channels or cracked elastic bodies.

Worked Example: Corner Singularity Exponent
Consider the Dirichlet problem −Δu = 1 in the planar sector Ω = {(r,θ) : 0 < r < 1, 0 < θ < 3π/2}. Determine the leading singular exponent λ and state the Sobolev regularity of the solution.
For the Dirichlet Laplacian in a sector of opening ω = 3π/2, the singular exponents satisfy sin(λω) = 0, i.e. λ = kπ/ω = 2k/3 for k ∈ ℕ. The smallest positive exponent is λ₁ = 2/3. The solution behaves like r2/3 sin(2θ/3) near the corner, which belongs to H1+α for any α < 2/3 but not to H5/3. Hence u ∈ H5/3−ε(Ω) for every ε > 0, and u ∉ H5/3(Ω).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the regularity of an elliptic solution on a non‑smooth domain, first identify the largest interior angle (or edge dihedral angle). Write down the transcendental equation for the singular exponent corresponding to the boundary condition (Dirichlet: sin(λω)=0; Neumann: cos(λω)=0; mixed: tan(λω)=−λ). The leading exponent λ₁ gives the Sobolev index 1+λ₁ (or 1+μ for edges). State the result as “u ∈ H1+λ₁−ε for all ε>0” and note that the solution fails to be in H1+λ₁. This pattern appears in both theory and finite‑element error‑analysis questions.

Quantitative Unique Continuation and Three‑Ball Inequalities

Quantitative unique continuation estimates measure how the size of a solution in a small ball controls its size in a larger concentric ball. For a second‑order elliptic operator L = −∂i(aij∂j) + V with bounded measurable coefficients and V ∈ Ln/2(ℝn) (n ≥ 3), the three‑ball inequality (Landis, 1962; Garofalo‑Lin, 1986) states that for any 0 < r₁ < r₂ < r₃ there exist constants C, δ ∈ (0,1) depending on ellipticity, dimension, and the ratios r₂/r₁, r₃/r₂ such that ‖u‖L2(Br₂) ≤ C ‖u‖L2(Br₁)δ ‖u‖L2(Br₃)1−δ for every solution of Lu = 0. The exponent δ is related to the frequency function N(r) = r ∫Br |∇u|2 / ∫∂Br u2 introduced by Almgren (1979); monotonicity of N(r) yields a doubling‑index bound and consequently the three‑ball inequality. For parabolic equations ∂tu − Δu = Vu the backward uniqueness of Escauriaza‑Seregin‑Šverák (2003) uses a Carleman weight φ = |x|2/(T−t) + λ log(T−t) to obtain a similar three‑cylinder inequality. In the hyperbolic setting, the Holmgren‑John theorem gives unique continuation across non‑characteristic surfaces, while the quantitative version of Tataru (1995) provides a three‑ball estimate for solutions of □u = Vu with V ∈ L∞. These inequalities are the backbone of inverse problems (Calderón problem), control theory (observability estimates), and the proof of the strong unique continuation property for Schrödinger operators with magnetic potentials.

Spectral Theory of Non‑Selfadjoint Elliptic Operators

When the principal part of an elliptic operator is self‑adjoint but lower‑order terms destroy symmetry, the spectrum may leave the real axis and pseudospectra become essential for stability analysis. Consider L = −Δ + b·∇ + c on a bounded domain with Dirichlet boundary conditions, where b ∈ L∞ is a drift field. The operator generates an analytic semigroup on L2 but its eigenvalues λk can be complex. The numerical range W(L) = {(Lu,u) : ‖u‖=1} lies in a sector {z : |arg(z−ω)| ≤ θ} with θ < π/2, guaranteeing sectoriality. The Krein‑Rutman theorem extends to non‑selfadjoint operators with a positive resolvent: if the semigroup is irreducible and eventually positive, the spectral bound s(L) = sup Re λ is a simple eigenvalue with a strictly positive eigenfunction (Daners‑Koch‑Medina, 2016). Pseudospectra σε(L) = {z ∈ ℂ : ‖(L−z)−1‖ > ε−1} quantify transient growth; for the Orr‑Sommerfeld operator in hydrodynamic stability, the ε‑pseudospectrum can extend far into the right half‑plane even though the spectrum lies in the left half‑plane, explaining subcritical transition to turbulence (Trefethen‑Embree, 2005). The Davies‑Simon theorem (1984) gives a bound on the number of eigenvalues in a sector for non‑selfadjoint Schrödinger operators with complex potentials V ∈ Lp, p > n/2. For the non‑selfadjoint harmonic oscillator −d2/dx2 + x2 + iγx3 the eigenvalues are known to be real for small γ (Bender‑Boettcher, 1998) but become complex beyond a critical value, illustrating PT‑symmetry breaking. These spectral phenomena are central to the analysis of non‑normal operators arising in fluid dynamics, quantum mechanics with gain/loss, and the stability of travelling waves in reaction‑diffusion systems.

Parabolic Equations on Rough and Fractal Domains

The heat equation ∂tu − Δu = 0 on domains with non‑smooth boundaries or on metric measure spaces supporting a Dirichlet form requires a synthesis of potential theory and functional analysis. On a bounded Lipschitz domain the Dirichlet heat kernel satisfies Gaussian upper bounds p(t,x,y) ≤ C t−n/2 exp(−c|x−y|2/t) (Aronson, 1968). For domains with the uniform exterior cone condition the same bounds hold, while on domains with inward cusps the kernel may exhibit sub‑Gaussian decay p(t,x,y) ≤ C t−ds/2 exp(−c (|x−y|β/t)1/(β−1)) where ds is the spectral dimension and β > 2 the walk dimension (Barlow‑Bass, 1999). On post‑critically finite fractals such as the Sierpiński gasket, the Laplacian is defined via the Kigami Dirichlet form; the heat kernel satisfies the sub‑Gaussian estimate with ds = 2 log 3 / log 5 ≈ 1.365 and β = log 5 / log 2 ≈ 2.322. The parabolic Harnack inequality holds on any space satisfying the volume doubling property and a Poincaré inequality (Saloff‑Coste, 1992). For time‑dependent rough coefficients, the De Giorgi‑Nash‑Moser theory extends to the setting of upper‑gradient forms on metric measure spaces (Kinnunen‑Shanmugalingam, 2001). These results underpin the analysis of diffusion in porous media, the study of Brownian motion on fractals, and the numerical approximation of parabolic problems on irregular meshes where standard Sobolev embeddings fail.

Nonlinear Schrödinger Equations: Global Well‑Posedness and Blow‑up

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS), i∂tu + Δu = λ|u|p‑1u on ℝn × ℝ, is the canonical model for dispersive nonlinear waves. The scaling symmetry uλ(t,x) = λ2/(p‑1)u(λ2t, λx) identifies the critical Sobolev exponent sc = n/2 − 2/(p‑1). When sc = 0 the equation is L2‑critical (p = 1 + 4/n); when sc = 1 it is H1‑critical (p = 1 + 4/(n‑2) for n ≥ 3). The mass M(u) = ∫|u|2 and energy E(u) = ½∫|∇u|2 − λ/(p+1)∫|u|p+1 are conserved. For the defocusing case (λ = −1), global well‑posedness in Hs for s ≥ sc was established by Cazenave‑Weissler (1990) and extended by Colliander et al. (2001). In the focusing case (λ = +1), solutions with negative energy and mass above the ground state Q (the optimizer of the Gagliardo‑Nirenberg inequality) blow up in finite time (Glassey, 1977). The virial identity d2/dt2∫|x|2|u|2 = 8E(u) − 4λ(n(p‑1)−4)/(p+1)∫|u|p+1 provides a sharp blow‑up criterion. Merle and Raphaël (2004) proved the log‑log blow‑up rate ‖∇u(t)‖L2 ∼ (log|log(T−t)|)1/2/(T−t) for the mass‑critical focusing NLS. Strichartz estimates ‖u‖LqtLrx ≤ C‖u0‖L2 with 2/q + n/r = n/2, (q,r,n) ≠ (2,∞,2) are the main linear tool; the endpoint (q,r) = (2, 2n/(n‑2)) fails for n ≥ 3 (Keel‑Tao, 1998). Kenig and Merle’s (2006) concentration‑compactness/rigidity method yields global well‑posedness and scattering for radial data below the ground state mass in the energy‑critical case.

Navier–Stokes Equations: Energy Methods and the Millennium Problem

The incompressible Navier–Stokes system ∂tu + (u·∇)u − νΔu + ∇p = f, ∇·u = 0 on a domain Ω ⊂ ℝ3 with no‑slip boundary condition u|∂Ω = 0 is the fundamental model of viscous fluid flow. The kinetic energy identity d/dt ½‖u‖L22 + ν‖∇u‖L22 = (f,u) gives the basic a priori bound u ∈ L∞(0,T;L2) ∩ L2(0,T;H1). Leray (1934) constructed global weak solutions satisfying the energy inequality for any divergence‑free u0 ∈ L2. Uniqueness of weak solutions remains open in three dimensions; the Ladyzhenskaya‑Prodi‑Serrin criterion states that if u ∈ Lq(0,T;Lr) with 2/q + 3/r = 1, r > 3, then the weak solution is unique and regular. The scaling uλ(t,x) = λu(λ2t, λx) leaves the equation invariant and shows that the critical space is L3(ℝ3) or Ḣ1/2(ℝ3). Kato (1984) proved local well‑posedness for data in Ḣ1/2; Koch‑Tataru (2001) extended this to BMO−1. The Clay Millennium Prize problem asks whether smooth initial data yield a global smooth solution or finite‑time blow‑up. Partial regularity results (Caffarelli‑Kohn‑Nirenberg, 1982) show that the singular set has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most 1. The pressure satisfies the Poisson equation −Δp = ∂i∂j(uiuj) and can be expressed via Calderón‑Zygmund operators, giving p ∈ Lq/2 if u ∈ Lq. Work on the hyperdissipative Navier–Stokes (−Δ)αu with α > 5/4 yields global regularity (Katz‑Pavlović, 2002). The vorticity formulation ∂tω + (u·∇)ω = (ω·∇)u + νΔω highlights the vortex‑stretching term (ω·∇)u as the main obstruction to global regularity.

Technique
Show that if a weak solution u of the three‑dimensional Navier–Stokes equations belongs to L4(0,T;L4(ℝ3)), then u is regular on (0,T].
By the Ladyzhenskaya‑Prodi‑Serrin criterion, regularity follows from u ∈ Lq(0,T;Lr) with 2/q + 3/r = 1 and r > 3. For q = r = 4 we have 2/4 + 3/4 = 5/4 > 1, so the condition is not directly satisfied. However, the interpolation inequality ‖u‖L4 ≤ C‖u‖L21/4‖∇u‖L23/4 together with the energy bound gives u ∈ L8/3(0,T;L8/3). Then 2/(8/3) + 3/(8/3) = 3/4 + 9/8 = 15/8 > 1, still not enough. The standard argument uses the Gagliardo‑Nirenberg inequality to bound the nonlinear term in the vorticity equation and applies a bootstrap: from u ∈ L4L4 one obtains ∇u ∈ L2L2 and then u ∈ L∞L2 ∩ L2H1. A more precise proof uses the fact that L4L4 embeds into the critical space L∞Ḃ−1/2∞,∞ and invokes the Koch‑Tataru well‑posedness result. Hence u is smooth on (0,T].

Write the virial identity for NLS and the energy inequality for Navier–Stokes from memory — they are the two most common “show that” steps.

Elliptic Equations with Measure Data and Renormalized Solutions

Consider the Dirichlet problem −Δu = μ in Ω, u = 0 on ∂Ω, where μ is a bounded Radon measure on a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝn, n ≥ 2. Classical solutions need not exist because the Newtonian potential of a Dirac mass behaves like |x|2‑n (or log|x| for n = 2), which is not in H10. The concept of renormalized solutions, introduced by DiPerna‑Lions (1989) for transport equations and adapted to elliptic problems by Boccardo‑Gallouët (1989), restores well‑posedness. A function u ∈ L1(Ω) is a renormalized solution if for every φ ∈ C1(ℝ) with φ' compactly supported, the truncated function Tk(u) = max(−k, min(u,k)) belongs to H10(Ω) and satisfies ∫∇Tk(u)·∇(φ(Tk(u))ψ) = ∫φ(Tk(u))ψ dμ for all ψ ∈ C∞c(Ω). The key a priori estimate is the Marcinkiewicz bound ‖u‖Ln/(n‑1),∞ ≤ C‖μ‖M, which is sharp. Existence is obtained by approximating μ with smooth με and passing to the limit using truncation stability. Uniqueness holds in the class of renormalized solutions (Boccardo‑Gallouët‑Orsina, 1996). For the nonlinear problem −Δu + g(u) = μ with g monotone increasing, the same framework yields existence of a renormalized solution provided g satisfies a sign condition and growth control. The theory extends to operators of the form −div(A(x)∇u) with A uniformly elliptic and bounded measurable; the truncation method works because the Caccioppoli inequality holds for Tk(u). Applications include the Poisson‑Boltzmann equation in electrostatics and the obstacle problem with measure data.

Degenerate Parabolic Equations: Porous Medium and Fast Diffusion

The porous medium equation ∂tu = Δ(um) with m > 1 models gas flow in a porous medium. The diffusion coefficient D(u) = m um‑1 vanishes at u = 0, causing finite speed of propagation: if u0 has compact support, the support of u(t) expands with a free boundary that moves at finite speed. The Barenblatt‑Zel’dovich‑Kompaneets self‑similar solution B(t,x) = t−α (C − k|x|2t−2β)+1/(m‑1) with α = n/(n(m‑1)+2), β = α/n, provides the asymptotic profile for integrable initial data (Vázquez, 2007). The fast diffusion equation corresponds to 0 < m < 1; here D(u) blows up as u → 0, leading to infinite speed of propagation and possible extinction in finite time when m < (n‑2)/n (the critical exponent). For m > (n‑2)/n, solutions exist globally and converge to the Barenblatt profile after suitable rescaling. The entropy method uses the functional E[u] = ∫um/(m‑1) (or ∫u log u for m = 1) and its dissipation dE/dt = −∫um‑2|∇u|2 to obtain convergence rates in relative entropy. The Caffarelli‑Friedman monotonicity formula and the Aronson‑Bénilan estimate Δ(um) ≥ −u/( (m‑1)t ) are essential tools for regularity and free‑boundary analysis. The very fast diffusion range m < (n‑2)/n exhibits extinction in finite time with a universal extinction profile (Díaz‑Kersner, 1987). The theory extends to the doubly nonlinear equation ∂t(up‑1) = Δ(um) and to anisotropic diffusion operators.

Boundary Control and Observability for Linear Hyperbolic Systems

Consider the wave equation ∂tty − Δy = 0 in Ω × (0,T) with Dirichlet boundary control y = v on Γ0 × (0,T) and homogeneous conditions on the remaining boundary. The exact controllability problem asks: given (y0, y1) ∈ H10(Ω) × L2(Ω), find v ∈ L2(Γ0 × (0,T)) such that the solution satisfies y(T) = ∂ty(T) = 0. The Hilbert Uniqueness Method (HUM) of Lions (1988) reduces this to an observability inequality for the adjoint homogeneous problem: there exists C > 0 such that ‖(φ0, φ1)‖H10×L22 ≤ C∫0T∫Γ0 |∂νφ|2 dσ dt for all solutions φ of ∂ttφ − Δφ = 0 with φ = 0 on ∂Ω. The geometric control condition (GCC) of Bardos‑Lebeau‑Rauch (1992) states that every generalized bicharacteristic of the wave operator must intersect the control region Γ0 within time T. If GCC holds, observability and hence exact controllability are true; if GCC fails, there exist high‑frequency solutions concentrated away from Γ0 that violate observability. For the Schrödinger equation i∂tψ + Δψ = 0, the same GCC is sufficient for boundary observability (Lebeau, 1992). The cost of control, i.e. the minimal L2 norm of v, grows exponentially as T approaches the minimal control time dictated by GCC. For systems such as the linearized elasticity or Maxwell equations, the GCC must be checked for each polarization mode. The duality between controllability and observability extends to parabolic equations, where the observability inequality holds for any T > 0 and any non‑empty open control region (Fursikov‑Imanuvilov, 1996), but the cost of control blows up as T → 0.

  • Treating renormalized solutions as weak solutions in H1; they live only in L1 and their truncations belong to H10.
  • Believing that the porous medium equation has infinite speed of propagation for all m > 1; finite speed holds precisely because the diffusion coefficient vanishes at zero.
  • Applying the geometric control condition for the wave equation to the heat equation without modification; parabolic observability holds for any open control region and any positive time.
  • Neglecting the role of the Aronson‑Bénilan estimate in proving regularity of the free boundary for the porous medium equation; it provides the crucial lower bound on the pressure Laplacian.
  • Overlooking the exponential growth of the control cost as the control time approaches the minimal time dictated by GCC; this is a standard quantitative trap in controllability questions.

Nonlocal Elliptic Equations and the Fractional Laplacian

The fractional Laplacian (−Δ)s with 0 < s < 1 is defined via the Fourier symbol |ξ|2s or, equivalently, by the singular integral

(−Δ)s u(x) = Cn,s ∫ℝn (u(x) − u(y)) / |x − y|n+2s dy.

This operator is nonlocal: the value at x depends on u globally. The Caffarelli–Silvestre extension (2007) realizes (−Δ)s as a Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map for a degenerate elliptic problem in ℝn+1+: find U(x,y) solving div(y1−2s ∇U) = 0 with U(x,0) = u(x); then (−Δ)s u = −ds limy→0 y1−2s ∂y U. This localizes the problem and permits standard elliptic techniques.

Regularity theory for (−Δ)s u = f mirrors the classical case: if f ∈ Cα then u ∈ Cα+2s (Schauder estimates) and if f ∈ Lp then u ∈ W2s,p (Calderón–Zygmund). Boundary regularity on a smooth domain Ω requires the exterior condition u = 0 in ℝn Ω; solutions belong to Cs(ℝn) and, if f is smoother, to Cα+2s(Ω̄) up to the boundary (Ros‑Oton & Serra, 2014). The eigenvalue problem (−Δ)s u = λ u in Ω with zero exterior data has a discrete spectrum λk ∼ k2s/n and eigenfunctions forming an orthonormal basis of L2(Ω).

Applications include anomalous diffusion, finance (fractional Black–Scholes), image processing (nonlocal means), and the thin obstacle problem, equivalent to the fractional Laplacian with s = 1/2. The extension method also yields an Almgren-type monotonicity formula, giving optimal free-boundary regularity in the fractional obstacle problem (Caffarelli, Salsa, Silvestre, 2008).

Regularity Theory for Nonlinear Elliptic Systems

For scalar uniformly elliptic equations in divergence form −div(A(x)∇u) = 0 with bounded measurable coefficients, De Giorgi (1957) and Nash (1958) established Hölder continuity of weak solutions; Moser (1960) gave an alternative proof via the Harnack inequality. For systems (vector‑valued u), the situation changes dramatically. De Giorgi (1968) constructed a bounded weak solution of a two‑dimensional system with smooth coefficients that is discontinuous, showing that full Hölder regularity fails in general.

Partial regularity became the central concept. Campanato (1964) and Morrey (1966) introduced the notion of excess and proved that minimizers of quadratic functionals ∫|∇u|2 are smooth outside a closed singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most n−2. Giaquinta–Modica (1982) extended this to functionals with p‑growth. The ε‑regularity theorem states: if the scaled excess ∫Br |∇u − (∇u)Br|2 is sufficiently small, then u is C1,α in a smaller ball. This yields that the singular set has measure zero and, for minimizing harmonic maps, dimension at most n−3 (Schoen–Uhlenbeck, 1982).

Uhlenbeck (1982) proved that stationary Yang–Mills connections are smooth in dimension four away from a discrete set, using a Coulomb gauge and a small‑energy regularity theorem. For general nonlinear systems with quadratic growth, the best known result is that the singular set has Hausdorff dimension ≤ n−2 (Evans, 1986). Counterexamples by Šverák and Yan (2002) show that singularities can occur on sets of dimension n−2, so the estimate is sharp.

In the non‑variational setting, the De Giorgi–Nash–Moser theory does not extend; however, systems with special structure (e.g., diagonal ellipticity or Uhlenbeck structure) admit partial regularity. The method of Campanato spaces and the A‑harmonic approximation lemma (Duzaar–Steffen, 2002) are the main tools for proving ε‑regularity in this broader context.

Technique: ε‑regularity and excess decay
Let u ∈ W1,2(B1; ℝN) be a weak solution of the elliptic system −div(A(x)∇u) = 0 with A uniformly elliptic and bounded. Define the excess E(r) = ⨍Br |∇u − (∇u)Br|2. Show that if E(r) ≤ ε for some universal ε > 0, then u is C1,α in Br/2 and the excess decays as E(θr) ≤ C θ2α E(r) for some θ ∈ (0,1/2).
1. Freeze coefficients at the center and consider the constant‑coefficient system −div(A(0)∇v) = 0. By the Caccioppoli inequality, the excess of u is comparable to that of v up to a small error controlled by the oscillation of A. 2. For the constant‑coefficient system, solutions are real‑analytic; a Campanato‑type estimate gives Ev(θr) ≤ C θ2 Ev(r). 3. Choose ε so small that the perturbation error is absorbed, yielding E(θr) ≤ (C θ2 + δ) E(r). 4. Pick θ such that C θ2 + δ ≤ θ2α for some α ∈ (0,1). 5. Iterate the decay to obtain the Campanato condition supρ<r ρ−2α E(ρ) < ∞, which is equivalent to ∇u ∈ C0,α(Br/2). Hence u ∈ C1,α(Br/2).
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for regularity of a system, first identify whether the problem is variational (Euler–Lagrange) or non‑variational. For variational systems, quote the ε‑regularity theorem and the dimension bound on the singular set (n−2 for general quadratic growth, n−3 for harmonic maps). For non‑variational systems, check for special structures (diagonal, Uhlenbeck) that permit partial regularity; otherwise state that only scalar equations enjoy full De Giorgi–Nash–Moser regularity. Always mention the role of the excess functional and the Caccioppoli inequality in the proof sketch.

Geometric Measure Theory and Minimal Surface Equations

The area functional A(Σ) = ∫Σ dℋn for an n‑dimensional surface Σ ⊂ ℝn+1 leads, via the first variation, to the minimal surface equation div(∇u / √(1+|∇u|2)) = 0 for graphs u: Ω → ℝ. De Giorgi (1954) solved the Plateau problem for codimension one by introducing sets of finite perimeter (Caccioppoli sets) and proving that reduced boundaries are smooth except for a singular set of Hausdorff dimension ≤ n−7 (Federer, 1970). The Simons cone {x ∈ ℝ8 : x12+…+x42 = x52+…+x82} shows that the dimension bound n−7 is sharp.

Allard's regularity theorem (1972) gives a quantitative version: if an integral current T has density close to 1 and small excess in a ball, then spt T is a C1,α graph there. This is the geometric analogue of the ε‑regularity theorem for elliptic systems. Almgren's big regularity paper (2000) proved that area‑minimizing integral currents of any codimension are smooth outside a singular set of dimension ≤ n−2, using a sophisticated frequency function and multiple‑valued functions.

The minimal surface equation is uniformly elliptic only when |∇u| is bounded; for large gradients the equation becomes degenerate. Nevertheless, the Bernstein theorem states that entire minimal graphs in ℝn+1 are affine for n ≤ 7 (Bernstein 1915, de Giorgi 1965, Almgren 1966, Simons 1968). In dimension 8, the Simons cone provides a non‑affine entire minimal graph, showing the theorem fails for n ≥ 8.

Applications include the study of soap films, capillary surfaces, and the regularity of free boundaries in obstacle problems. The monotonicity formula for minimal surfaces (Huisken, 1990) and the associated Gaussian density are essential tools for blow‑up analysis and classification of tangent cones at singular points.

Inverse Problems for Parabolic Equations

An inverse parabolic problem typically asks to recover an unknown coefficient (diffusion, potential, or source) from boundary measurements of the solution. For the heat equation ∂tu − div(a(x)∇u) = 0 in Ω × (0,T) with Dirichlet data u = f on ∂Ω × (0,T) and Neumann data ∂νu = g on ∂Ω × (0,T), the map a ↦ (f,g) is the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann operator. Uniqueness (Isakov, 1990) holds if a is time‑independent and the lateral Cauchy data are known for all f ∈ H1(0,T; H1/2(∂Ω)). The proof uses complex geometrical optics solutions and a Carleman estimate with weight φ(t,x) = eλψ(x) / (t(T−t)).

Stability is logarithmic: ‖a1 − a2‖L2 ≤ C |log ‖Λa1 − Λa2‖|−κ for some κ > 0 (Mandache, 2001). This severe ill‑posedness is intrinsic; no Hölder stability can hold. For time‑dependent coefficients a(t,x), uniqueness requires data on a larger time interval or additional interior measurements (Bukhgeim–Klibanov, 1981).

Numerical reconstruction uses regularization (Tikhonov, iterative methods) and the adjoint‑state method to compute gradients of the misfit functional. The Fréchet derivative of the parameter‑to‑measurement map is given by the solution of a linearized parabolic problem, and its adjoint solves a backward heat equation. Convergence rates for iterative regularization (Landweber, Newton‑type) depend on source conditions expressing the true coefficient as a power of the normal operator.

Recent developments include the use of fractional parabolic equations (∂tαu + (−Δ)su = 0) where the nonlocal nature improves stability to a conditional Hölder type under a priori bounds (Li–Yamamoto, 2015). Inverse problems for the porous medium equation ∂tu = Δ(um) involve the additional difficulty of a free boundary; uniqueness of the absorption coefficient from boundary flux data was proved by Cannon–Lin (1990) using a transformation to a linear problem via the pressure variable v = um−1.

Dispersive Equations: KdV and Benjamin‑Ono

The Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation ∂tu + ∂x3u + u∂xu = 0 models shallow water waves. It is completely integrable: the Lax pair L = −∂x2 + u, B = −4∂x3 + 6u∂x + 3ux yields ∂tL = [B,L]. The inverse scattering transform (Gardner–Greene–Kruskal–Miura, 1967) solves the Cauchy problem for rapidly decaying data, producing N‑soliton solutions u(x,t) = 2∑j=1N κj2 sech2(κj(x−4κj2t−xj)). Solitons interact elastically, preserving shape and speed.

Well‑posedness in Sobolev spaces Hs(ℝ) was established by Bourgain (1993) using the Fourier restriction norm Xs,b (Bourgain spaces). The critical regularity is s = −3/2; Kenig–Ponce–Vega (1996) proved local well‑posedness for s ≥ −3/2 and ill‑posedness for s < −3/2 (norm inflation). Global well‑posedness for s ≥ −1 follows from conservation of mass ∫u2 and energy ∫(ux2 − u3/3). The Miura transform v = ux + u2 maps KdV to the modified KdV (mKdV) ∂tv + ∂x3v + v2vx = 0, linking their solution theories.

The Benjamin–Ono (BO) equation ∂tu + ℋ∂x2u + u∂xu = 0, where ℋ is the Hilbert transform, models internal waves in deep stratified fluids. It is also integrable (Lax pair with a nonlocal operator). The BO equation has algebraic solitons u(x,t) = 4c / (1 + c2(x−ct)2) with speed c > 0. Well‑posedness in Hs(ℝ) holds for s ≥ 0 (Tao, 2004) using adapted function spaces Up, Vp; the critical regularity is s = −1/2 (Ionescu–Kenig, 2007). The BO hierarchy shares the same conservation laws as KdV but with different Hamiltonian structures.

Both equations exhibit dispersive smoothing: solutions gain half a derivative locally in time (Kato, 1983). The Strichartz estimates for the linear Airy group (KdV) and the linear BO group are the foundation for nonlinear estimates. The long‑time asymptotics for decaying data are described by the nonlinear steepest descent method (Deift–Zhou, 1993) applied to the associated Riemann–Hilbert problems, yielding soliton resolution conjectures proved for KdV (Kappeler–Topalov, 2005) and open for BO.

  • Believing every weak solution of a nonlinear elliptic system is Hölder continuous; De Giorgi's counterexample shows discontinuities can occur, and only partial regularity (smoothness outside a small singular set) is generally true.
  • Confusing the dimension bound for singular sets of area‑minimizing currents (n−2 for general codimension, n−7 for codimension one) with the bound for stationary varifolds; the latter can have larger singular sets.
  • Treating the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map for the heat equation as if it determines a time‑dependent coefficient uniquely from a single boundary measurement; uniqueness for time‑dependent coefficients requires either interior data or measurements over a time interval exceeding the controllability time.
  • Thinking the KdV equation is globally well‑posed in L2(ℝ) without additional structure; the critical Sobolev index is s = −3/2, and global well‑posedness in L2 (s = 0) relies on the infinite hierarchy of conserved quantities from complete integrability.
  • Neglecting the role of the Hilbert transform in the Benjamin–Ono equation; the nonlocal dispersion ℋ∂x2 changes the scaling, critical regularity, and function spaces needed for well‑posedness compared to KdV.
  • Applying the standard Carleman weight for elliptic equations to parabolic inverse problems without the time‑dependent factor 1/(t(T−t)); the singular weight at t = 0,T is essential to absorb boundary terms in the integration by parts.

Elliptic Equations on Manifolds with Conical Singularities

When a Riemannian manifold M has isolated conical points, the Laplace–Beltrami operator Δg fails to be essentially self‑adjoint on C∞c(M{p}). The seminal work of Cheeger (1983) and Mazzeo (1991) introduced the edge calculus of pseudodifferential operators adapted to the singular geometry. In this framework the resolvent (Δg−λ)−1 is a polyhomogeneous operator on the blown‑up manifold, and its kernel admits an asymptotic expansion in terms of the distance to the cone tip. The indicial roots of the model operator on the exact cone C(Y)= (0,∞)r×Y determine the possible leading singularities of solutions: if the cross‑section Y has first eigenvalue μ1 of ΔY, the indicial exponents are γ±=−(n−2)/2 ± √((n−2)2/4+μ1). Solutions belonging to the weighted Sobolev space H1δ(M) with weight δ avoiding the indicial set enjoy Fredholm properties; the mapping Δg: Hk+2δ→Hkδ−2 is Fredholm of index zero for non‑exceptional δ. This theory underpins the analysis of the Yamabe problem on singular spaces (Akutagawa–Botvinnik, 2003) and the construction of constant scalar curvature metrics on orbifolds. Recent extensions by Gell‑Redman–Hassell (2015) treat iterated edge metrics, allowing a systematic treatment of stratified spaces.

Nonlinear Wave Equations: Null Condition and Global Existence

For quasilinear wave equations □gu = Q(∂u,∂u) on Minkowski space ℝ1+3, the null condition identified by Klainerman (1986) and Christodoulou (1986) is a decisive structural hypothesis that guarantees global existence for small data. A quadratic form Q(∂u,∂u) satisfies the null condition if it vanishes when both gradients are parallel to the same null direction, i.e. Q(ξ,ξ)=0 for all ξ with ξ02=|ξ|2. The classic example is the Einstein vacuum equations in wave coordinates, where the nonlinearity is a sum of null forms Q0(∂u,∂v)=∂αu ∂αv and Qαβ(∂u,∂v)=∂αu ∂βv−∂βu ∂αv. Klainerman’s vector‑field method, using the commutation fields of the Poincaré group (translations, rotations, Lorentz boosts, scaling), yields the decay estimate |∂u(t,x)| ≤ Cε (1+|t|+|x|)−1(1+|t−|x||)−1/2. This decay is just sufficient to close the energy estimates for null forms. If the null condition fails, as in □u = (∂tu)2, John (1981) proved blow‑up for arbitrarily small data via an ODE comparison along outgoing characteristics. The sharp lifespan for non‑null quadratic nonlinearities is Tε ∼ exp(c/ε). Later work by Lindblad–Rodnianski (2003) extended the global existence result to the Einstein equations without symmetry assumptions, using a refined null structure and a geometric version of the vector‑field method.

Worked Example: Verifying the Null Condition
Consider the quadratic nonlinearity Q(∂u,∂v)=∂tu ∂tv − ∇u·∇v on ℝ1+3. Show that Q satisfies the null condition.
A covector ξ=(ξ0,ξ) is null iff ξ02=|ξ|2. For such ξ we have Q(ξ,ξ)=ξ02−|ξ|2=0 by the null condition definition. Hence Q vanishes on all null directions, so the nonlinearity satisfies the null condition. This is precisely the wave operator’s principal symbol, and the corresponding equation □u = Q(∂u,∂u) is the standard semilinear wave equation with a null form.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks whether a given quadratic form obeys the null condition, write the form in terms of ∂αu ∂αv and check the contraction with a null covector. Remember that any linear combination of the basic null forms Q0 and Qαβ automatically satisfies the condition. Memorise the decay rate (1+t)−1(1+|t−r|)−1/2 for 3‑D wave solutions; it is the key to closing energy estimates.

Harmonic Map Heat Flow and Singularities

The harmonic map heat flow ∂tu = τ(u) = Δu + A(u)(∇u,∇u) evolves a map u: M→N between Riemannian manifolds toward a harmonic map. Struwe (1985) proved global existence of a weak solution for any initial data in H1(M,N) when dim M = 2, with possible singularities forming at isolated times. The singular set has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most 0, i.e. it consists of finitely many points (tk,xk). At each singular time a non‑trivial harmonic sphere (a “bubble”) splits off; this is the bubble‑tree phenomenon. The energy identity E(u0) = E(u(t)) + Σ E(ωk) holds, where ωk are the harmonic spheres. For higher dimensional domains (dim M ≥ 3) the flow may develop singularities of higher codimension; Chen–Struwe (1989) showed partial regularity: the singular set has parabolic dimension ≤ dim M−3. The monotonicity formula of Struwe, adapted from the elliptic case, is the main tool: for a suitable backward heat kernel Φ(t,x)= (4π(T−t))−m/2 exp(−|x−x0|2/4(T−t)) the quantity Θ(t)=∫M |∇u|2 Φ dvol is non‑decreasing in t. Blow‑up analysis at a singular point yields a non‑constant harmonic map from ℝm to N, which by Liouville theorems forces m≥3. Recent work by Rivière–Struwe (2008) and by Lamm–Lin (2013) extends the theory to the extrinsic polyharmonic map heat flow and to the Schrödinger map flow, where the dispersive nature replaces the parabolic smoothing.

Geometric Flows: Ricci Flow and Mean Curvature Flow

Hamilton’s Ricci flow ∂tg = −2 Ric(g) (1982) deforms a Riemannian metric in the direction of its Ricci curvature. Short‑time existence follows from the DeTurck trick, which converts the degenerate parabolic system into a strictly parabolic one by adding a Lie derivative term. Hamilton proved the maximum principle for the curvature operator, showing that positive curvature operator is preserved in dimension three. Perelman’s breakthrough (2002‑2003) introduced the ℱ‑functional ℱ(g,f)=∫(R+|∇f|2)e−f dvol and the reduced volume, establishing the no‑local‑collapsing theorem and the canonical neighbourhood structure. These ingredients yield the surgery procedure that leads to the proof of the Poincaré conjecture and the geometrization theorem. In parallel, the mean curvature flow ∂tX = H ν moves a hypersurface by its mean curvature vector. Huisken (1984) proved that convex hypersurfaces shrink to a round point in finite time, with the rescaled flow converging to a sphere. The Huisken monotonicity formula ∫ Φ dμt ≤ ∫ Φ dμ0 (Φ the backward heat kernel) controls singularities. White (2000) and Colding–Minicozzi (2012) developed the theory of tangent flows and the classification of ancient solutions. Recent advances include the mean curvature flow with surgery (Huisken–Sinestrari, 1999) for mean‑convex hypersurfaces, and the Ricci flow on non‑compact manifolds with bounded curvature (Shi, 1989). Both flows are now central tools in geometric topology and the study of singularity formation.

Inverse Problems for Hyperbolic Equations: Boundary Control and Observability

The exact controllability of the wave equation □u = 0 on a bounded domain Ω with Dirichlet boundary control f on a portion Γ0⊂∂Ω is equivalent, by the Hilbert Uniqueness Method (HUM) of Lions (1988), to an observability inequality for the adjoint homogeneous problem: ∫0T∫Γ0 |∂νv|2 dσ dt ≥ C ∥(v(0),∂tv(0))∥H1×L22. Bardos–Lebeau–Rauch (1992) gave the sharp geometric condition: every ray of geometric optics entering Ω must intersect Γ0 within time T (the Geometric Control Condition, GCC). If GCC holds, the control operator is surjective and the inverse problem of determining the initial data from boundary measurements on Γ0×(0,T) is well‑posed. For variable coefficients, the GCC must be formulated for the Hamiltonian flow of the principal symbol. The stability of the reconstruction is of logarithmic type in general, but becomes Hölder under a priori bounds on the coefficients (Isakov, 1991). Recent work by Bellassoued–Yamamoto (2017) treats the simultaneous recovery of a time‑dependent potential q(t,x) and a boundary coefficient from the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map, using Carleman estimates with a weight adapted to the hyperbolic geometry. The method extends to systems such as the elastic wave equation, where the observability inequality must account for both compressional and shear waves.

  • Confusing the parabolic monotonicity formula of Struwe with the elliptic one; the backward heat kernel introduces an explicit time dependence that is essential for the blow‑up analysis.
  • Believing that Ricci flow preserves positive sectional curvature in all dimensions; it only preserves positive curvature operator in dimension three, and counterexamples exist in higher dimensions.
  • Thinking the Geometric Control Condition is sufficient for observability of the wave equation with lower‑order terms; additional multiplier or Carleman estimates are required when potentials are present.
  • Neglecting the role of the indicial roots in elliptic theory on conical manifolds; solutions may have non‑integrable singularities if the weight crosses an indicial exponent.
  • Treating the harmonic map heat flow as globally smooth for any target manifold; singularities are inevitable in dimension two and the bubble‑tree description is necessary for the energy identity.
  • Assuming that exact controllability of the wave equation implies exact controllability of the associated heat equation; the latter is false due to the infinite speed of propagation of the heat semigroup.

The Monge–Ampère Equation and Optimal Transport

The Monge–Ampère equation det(D²u) = f arises naturally in optimal transport, where the Brenier map pushes forward a probability density ρ₀ onto ρ₁ via the gradient of a convex potential u. Brenier’s theorem (1991) states that for absolutely continuous measures on ℝⁿ with finite second moments, there exists a unique (up to an additive constant) convex function u solving the Monge–Ampère equation in the Alexandrov sense, and ∇u is the optimal transport map for the quadratic cost. Caffarelli’s regularity theory (1990‑1996) shows that if the densities are bounded away from zero and infinity on convex domains, then u is strictly convex and locally C¹,α; if the densities are smooth and the domains uniformly convex, u is smooth up to the boundary. The equation also appears in prescribed Gauss curvature problems, where the right‑hand side is the curvature function, and in the Minkowski problem for convex bodies. The Aleksandrov–Bakelman–Pucci (ABP) estimate provides a fundamental a priori bound for solutions of fully nonlinear elliptic equations, and the method of continuity combined with the Evans–Krylov theorem yields C²,α interior regularity for uniformly elliptic Monge–Ampère equations. Recent developments include the regularity theory for optimal transport maps on Riemannian manifolds (Figalli–Kim–McCann, 2011) and the stability of transport maps under density perturbations (Gigli, 2011). The complex Monge–Ampère equation (det(gibar j + ∂_i∂bar jφ) = eF det(gibar j)) underlies Kähler–Einstein metrics; Yau’s solution of the Calabi conjecture (1978) established existence of smooth solutions on compact Kähler manifolds with non‑positive first Chern class.

Maximal Regularity for Parabolic Equations in Banach Spaces

Maximal L^p‑regularity for the abstract Cauchy problem u′(t) + Au(t) = f(t), u(0) = 0, asks whether the solution operator maps f ∈ L^p(0,T;X) to u ∈ W1,p(0,T;X) ∩ L^p(0,T;D(A)) with the estimate ‖u′‖L^p + ‖Au‖L^p ≤ C‖f‖L^p. For a sectorial operator A on a UMD Banach space X, the Weis theorem (2001) characterises maximal regularity by the R‑boundedness of the resolvent family {λ(λ+A)-1 : λ ∈ Σ_θ} for some θ > π/2. This criterion extends the classical Dore–Venni result (1987) for sums of commuting operators and the Hille–Yosida theory for analytic semigroups. In concrete PDEs, the Laplacian with Dirichlet, Neumann, or Robin boundary conditions on L^q(Ω) (1 < q < ∞) enjoys maximal regularity on domains with C¹ boundary (or Lipschitz boundary for q=2). For the heat equation on a bounded C¹ domain, maximal regularity holds in L^q for all 1 < q < ∞, with the constant C depending only on the domain and q. The theory is crucial for quasilinear parabolic systems: if the linearised operator has maximal regularity, the contraction mapping principle yields local well‑posedness in critical spaces (Prüss–Simonett, 2016). Counterexamples exist on non‑UMD spaces such as L¹ or L^∞, where the Hilbert transform is unbounded. The concept of R‑sectoriality also underpins the bounded H^∞‑calculus, which is equivalent to maximal regularity on Hilbert spaces (McIntosh, 1986) but strictly stronger on general UMD spaces. Recent work by Kunstmann–Weis (2004) and Portal–Štrkalj (2000) extends the theory to non‑autonomous problems and operators with time‑dependent domains, enabling the treatment of moving boundary problems and free‑boundary flows.

Worked Example: Maximal Regularity for the Heat Equation on a Lipschitz Domain
Let Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Consider the Dirichlet heat equation ∂_t u - Δu = f in Ω × (0,T), u = 0 on ∂Ω × (0,T), u(0) = 0. Show that for any f ∈ L^p(0,T;L^q(Ω)) with 1 < p,q < ∞, the solution satisfies the maximal regularity estimate ‖∂_t u‖L^p L^q + ‖Δu‖L^p L^q ≤ C‖f‖L^p L^q.
The Dirichlet Laplacian -Δ_D generates a bounded analytic semigroup on L^q(Ω) for 1 < q < ∞ (Auscher–Tchamitchian, 1998). Since L^q(Ω) is a UMD space, the Weis criterion applies: the resolvent set contains a sector Σ_θ with θ > π/2 and the family {λ(λ-Δ_D)-1} is R‑bounded. By the Weis theorem, -Δ_D has maximal L^p‑regularity for all 1 < p < ∞. The solution is given by u(t) = ∫0^t e-(t-s)Δ_D f(s) ds. The maximal regularity estimate follows directly from the operator‑valued Fourier multiplier theorem (Weis, 2001). The constant C depends only on n, p, q, and the Lipschitz character of Ω.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for maximal regularity, first identify the underlying Banach space and verify the UMD property. Then check whether the operator is sectorial and whether the resolvent family is R‑bounded. Cite the Weis theorem (2001) as the definitive criterion; for concrete elliptic operators on L^q domains, reference the Auscher–Tchamitchian result for Lipschitz boundaries. Avoid stating maximal regularity on L¹ or L^∞ — these are standard counterexamples.

Elliptic Boundary Value Problems in Lipschitz Domains and the Method of Layer Potentials

The classical theory of elliptic boundary value problems on C¹ domains relies on Schauder estimates and the Fredholm alternative. For Lipschitz domains, the boundary is only locally a graph of a Lipschitz function, and the normal vector exists almost everywhere. Dahlberg (1977) proved that the Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian with data in L^p(∂Ω) is solvable for 2-ε < p < ∞, and the solution satisfies the non‑tangential maximal function estimate ‖N(u)‖L^p(∂Ω) ≤ C‖f‖L^p(∂Ω). The method of layer potentials reduces the boundary value problem to integral equations on ∂Ω. For the Laplacian, the single‑layer potential S and double‑layer potential D are defined by Sσ(x) = ∫∂Ω Γ(x-y) σ(y) dσ(y) and Dμ(x) = ∫∂Ω ∂ν_yΓ(x-y) μ(y) dσ(y), where Γ is the fundamental solution. The jump relations (Sσ)|_± = Sσ, (Dμ)|_± = (±½I + K)μ, with K the Neumann–Poincaré operator, transform the Dirichlet and Neumann problems into the invertibility of ½I ± K on appropriate Hardy or Besov spaces. Verchota (1984) established that ½I ± K is invertible on L^p(∂Ω) for 1 < p < 2+ε, yielding well‑posedness of the Dirichlet and Neumann problems in that range. For systems such as linear elasticity (Lamé system), the layer potential approach extends with matrix‑valued kernels; the invertibility of the corresponding boundary integral operators on Lipschitz domains was proved by Dahlberg–Kenig–Verchota (1988). The theory also provides the foundation for the numerical boundary element method (BEM) and for the study of the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map in inverse problems. Recent advances include the solution of the regularity problem (data in the Hardy space H¹) by Hofmann–Mitrea–Taylor (2001) and the extension to higher‑order elliptic systems (polyharmonic equations) by Mitrea–Wright (2012).

The Boltzmann Equation and Hypocoercivity

The Boltzmann equation ∂_t f + v·∇_x f = Q(f,f) describes the statistical evolution of a rarefied gas, where Q is the bilinear collision operator. The linearised operator L = Q(μ,·) + Q(·,μ) around the Maxwellian equilibrium μ(v) = (2π)-n/2 e-|v|²/2 is self‑adjoint and non‑positive on L²(μ-1) with a five‑dimensional null space spanned by collision invariants {1, v_i, |v|²}. The full linearised equation ∂_t g + v·∇_x g = Lg lacks coercivity in x because the transport term v·∇_x is skew‑adjoint and does not dissipate. Hypocoercivity, introduced by Villani (2009) building on earlier work of Hérau–Nier (2004) and Desvillettes–Villani (2005), provides a framework to prove exponential convergence to equilibrium in a modified norm. The key idea is to construct a Lyapunov functional H(g) = ‖g‖² + ε⟨A g, g⟩ where A is a carefully chosen auxiliary operator (often A = (1+(-Δ_v))-1 v·∇_x) that couples the transport and collision parts. For the linearised Boltzmann equation with hard‑sphere or cutoff Maxwellian kernels, the spectral gap of L yields a coercivity estimate ⟨-Lg, g⟩ ≥ λ‖(I-Π)g‖², where Π is the projection onto the null space. The macro‑micro decomposition g = Πg + (I-Π)g and the commutator [v·∇_x, A] produce the missing dissipation in x. The resulting hypocoercive estimate gives ‖g(t)‖L²x,v ≤ C e-κt ‖g(0)‖H¹x,v for some κ > 0. For the nonlinear Boltzmann equation, global existence near equilibrium was proved by Ukai (1974) and Guo (2002) using energy methods in weighted Sobolev spaces. Recent work of Mouhot–Neumann (2006) and Gualdani–Mischler–Mouhot (2017) extends hypocoercivity to non‑cutoff kernels (grazing collisions) and to the Landau equation, where the collision operator is a fractional diffusion in v. The theory also applies to kinetic Fokker–Planck equations and to the Vlasov–Poisson–Fokker–Planck system, providing quantitative rates of convergence uniform in the Knudsen number, a key step toward the hydrodynamic limit.

The Euler Equations and Onsager's Conjecture

The incompressible Euler equations ∂_t u + (u·∇)u + ∇p = 0, ∇·u = 0 on the torus 𝕋³ describe ideal fluid flow. Onsager (1949) conjectured that weak solutions with Hölder regularity C0,α for α > 1/3 conserve kinetic energy, while for α < 1/3 there exist dissipative weak solutions (anomalous dissipation). The positive direction was proved by Constantin–E–Titi (1994) using the commutator estimate ‖(u·∇)u - u_ℓ·∇u_ℓ‖L¹ ≤ C‖u‖C0,α³ ℓ3α-1, where u_ℓ is a mollification at scale ℓ. The negative direction required constructing wild solutions with prescribed energy profile. De Lellis–Székelyhidi (2009) developed the convex integration method, adapting Nash’s isometric embedding technique to the Euler equations. They introduced the notion of subsolutions (v, p, R) satisfying ∂_t v + div(v⊗v) + ∇p = div R, ∇·v = 0, with a Reynolds stress R measuring the defect. By iteratively adding highly oscillatory Mikado flows, they produced weak solutions in C0,α for any α < 1/3 that dissipate energy. Isett (2018) completed the proof for α < 1/3 by refining the iteration scheme to achieve the exact Hölder exponent and match any continuous energy profile. Buckmaster–Vicol (2019) extended the construction to the full range α < 1/3 on 𝕋³ and proved non‑uniqueness of weak solutions to the Navier–Stokes equations (the non‑uniqueness of Leray‑Hopf solutions remains open). The Onsager exponent 1/3 is sharp: the energy conservation proof fails at α = 1/3, and the convex integration scheme cannot reach α = 1/3. The conjecture has profound implications for turbulence theory, identifying the regularity threshold separating conservative from dissipative dynamics in the inviscid limit.

  • Assuming maximal regularity holds on L¹ or L^∞; these spaces are not UMD and the Hilbert transform is unbounded, so the Weis criterion fails.
  • Believing that layer potential methods work only on C¹ domains; Verchota’s theory extends them to Lipschitz domains with invertibility of ½I ± K on L^p for a range of p.
  • Thinking hypocoercivity requires a spectral gap in the full space; it only needs a spectral gap on the orthogonal complement of the collision invariants, together with a suitable auxiliary operator.
  • Stating that Onsager’s conjecture is fully resolved for α = 1/3; the endpoint α = 1/3 remains open for both energy conservation and anomalous dissipation.
  • Mixing up the convex integration scheme for Euler with the Nash–Kuiper theorem for isometric embeddings; the former uses Mikado flows, the latter uses corrugations.
  • Neglecting the role of the Reynolds stress in the convex integration construction; it is the defect that is iteratively reduced to zero while preserving the divergence‑free condition.

These advanced topics illustrate the depth and interconnectedness of modern PDE theory and are essential for a comprehensive understanding of the field.

Calderón–Zygmund Theory and Lp Estimates for Elliptic and Parabolic Equations

The Calderón–Zygmund theory underpins Lp regularity for linear elliptic and parabolic operators with bounded measurable coefficients. For a uniformly elliptic operator L = −div(A(x)∇) on Ω ⊂ ℝn with A ∈ L∞(Ω) satisfying λ|ξ|2 ≤ A(x)ξ·ξ ≤ Λ|ξ|2, the fundamental estimate is

‖∇2u‖Lp(Ω) ≤ C(‖Lu‖Lp(Ω) + ‖u‖Lp(Ω)), 1 < p < ∞,

where C depends only on n, λ, Λ, p and the Lipschitz character of Ω. The proof uses singular integrals: second derivatives of the Newtonian potential are Riesz transforms, bounded on Lp for 1 < p < ∞. For the Dirichlet problem, one extends u by zero, applies the whole-space estimate, and corrects boundary data via layer potentials. The endpoint p = 1 fails; one obtains weak‑type (1,1) estimates with BMO replacing L∞. For parabolic equations ∂tu − div(A∇u) = f, the analogous estimate involves ∂t and ∇2 with mixed norms Lp(0,T; Lq(Ω)). The theory extends to systems (e.g., linear elasticity) under the Legendre–Hadamard condition. A result of Krylov (1987) shows that for non‑divergence form operators aij(x)∂iju = f with VMO coefficients, the same Lp estimate holds, which is crucial for fully nonlinear regularity via freezing coefficients.

Key Points

  • Singular integral representation of ∇2u via Riesz transforms.
  • Boundedness on Lp for 1 < p < ∞; failure at endpoints.
  • Extension to parabolic operators with mixed‑norm estimates.
  • Application to non‑divergence form equations with VMO coefficients (Krylov).

Fully Nonlinear Elliptic Equations: Evans–Krylov Theorem and Caffarelli’s Regularity Theory

Fully nonlinear elliptic equations take the form F(D2u, ∇u, u, x) = 0, with F smooth and elliptic: ∂F/∂Mij ≥ λ|ξ|2. The Evans–Krylov theorem (1982) states that if F is concave (or convex) in the matrix argument and u ∈ C2 is a viscosity solution, then u ∈ C2,α for some α > 0, with estimate ‖u‖C2,α ≤ C(‖u‖L∞ + ‖F(0)‖Cα). The proof combines the Krylov–Safonov Harnack inequality for linearised operators with a perturbation argument: linearised coefficients lie in VMO, so Calderón–Zygmund theory gives W2,p estimates, and embedding yields C1,α; iteration gives C2,α. Caffarelli (1989) extended this to non‑concave equations via strict ellipticity and blow‑up analysis, proving that any viscosity solution of a uniformly elliptic equation with Lipschitz dependence on ∇u, u is C1,α. For the homogeneous Monge–Ampère equation det D2u = 1, Caffarelli showed convex viscosity solutions are strictly convex and C1,α; if the right‑hand side is Cα and bounded away from zero, then u ∈ C2,α. These results underpin regularity for optimal transport, geometric PDEs (prescribed Gauss curvature), and viscosity solutions of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations.

Key Points

  • Evans–Krylov: concavity/convexity ⇒ C2,α interior regularity.
  • Caffarelli’s C1,α theory for general uniformly elliptic equations.
  • Monge–Ampère: strict convexity and C2,α for positive RHS.
  • Applications to optimal transport and stochastic control.
Applying the Calderón–Zygmund Estimate to a Poisson Equation
Let Ω = B1(0) ⊂ ℝ3 and consider −Δu = f in Ω, u = 0 on ∂Ω, with f(x) = |x|−1/2. Determine the optimal Lp regularity of ∇2u.
The function f belongs to Lp(Ω) iff ∫01 r2 (r−1/2)p dr = ∫01 r2−p/2 dr < ∞, i.e. 2 − p/2 > −1 ⇒ p < 6. Thus f ∈ Lp for all 1 ≤ p < 6. By the Calderón–Zygmund estimate for the Dirichlet Laplacian on a C1,1 domain, ‖∇2u‖Lp ≤ C‖f‖Lp for 1 < p < ∞. Hence ∇2u ∈ Lp for every p < 6. For p = 6 the estimate fails because f ∉ L6; indeed ∇2u ∉ L6 by scaling. The optimal regularity is ∇2u ∈ Lp for all p < 6, and by Sobolev embedding u ∈ W2,p ⊂ C1,α for α = 1 − 3/p, any α < 1/2.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for Lp regularity of solutions to linear elliptic/parabolic equations, first check the integrability of the data. Then recall the Calderón–Zygmund range 1 < p < ∞; the endpoint p = 1 gives weak‑type estimates, p = ∞ gives BMO. For non‑divergence form, verify VMO coefficients to invoke Krylov’s theorem. Always state the dependence of the constant on ellipticity constants and domain regularity.

Kinetic Equations: Vlasov–Poisson and Vlasov–Maxwell Systems

The Vlasov–Poisson system describes a collisionless plasma or a self‑gravitating stellar system. In dimension three, the unknown is the distribution function f(t,x,v) ≥ 0 satisfying

∂tf + v·∇xf + E·∇vf = 0, E = −∇xφ, −Δxφ = ρ = ∫ f dv − ρ0.

Global existence of classical solutions for smooth, compactly supported initial data was proved by Pfaffelmoser (1992) and Lions–Perthame (1991) using velocity averaging and Lp estimates. The key a priori bound is energy conservation: ∫ (|v|2/2 + φ) f dxdv = constant. For the gravitational case (attractive force), the same method works because the force field is still a Riesz transform of ρ. The Vlasov–Maxwell system couples the Vlasov equation for each species to Maxwell’s equations for (E, B):

∂tfs + v·∇xfs + (qs/ms)(E + v×B)·∇vfs = 0,

∂tE − ∇×B = −j, ∂tB + ∇×E = 0, ∇·E = ρ, ∇·B = 0.

Global existence of weak solutions (DiPerna–Lions, 1989) uses renormalisation and velocity averaging. Classical global existence for small data was established by Glassey–Strauss (1986) and for large data with symmetry by Schaeffer (1991). The main difficulty is controlling the growth of velocity support; Glassey–Strauss showed that if momentum support remains bounded, the solution stays classical. Recent work by Luk–Strain (2014) proved global existence for the 3D relativistic Vlasov–Maxwell system with small data using a vector field method adapted to the hyperbolic structure. The Vlasov–Poisson system also models the mean‑field limit of N‑particle systems (Dobrushin, 1979; Hauray–Jabin, 2015).

Key Points

  • Vlasov–Poisson: global classical solutions (Pfaffelmoser, Lions–Perthame).
  • Vlasov–Maxwell: weak solutions (DiPerna–Lions); classical for small data (Glassey–Strauss).
  • Velocity averaging lemmas provide compactness in x.
  • Mean‑field limit connects particle systems to kinetic equations.

Water Waves: Local and Global Well‑posedness for the Free‑Surface Euler Equations

The water‑wave problem models an inviscid, incompressible fluid with a free surface under gravity. In the Eulerian formulation, the velocity field u(t,x) and pressure p satisfy the incompressible Euler equations in the time‑dependent domain Ω(t) bounded below by a fixed bottom and above by the free surface Γ(t) = {xn = η(t,x′)}. The boundary conditions are the kinematic condition ∂tη = un − u′·∇x′η on Γ(t) and the dynamic condition p = −gη (neglecting surface tension). The problem is quasilinear; the highest‑order term is the Dirichlet–Neumann operator G(η) mapping the trace of the velocity potential to the normal derivative. Local well‑posedness in Sobolev spaces Hs for s > n/2 + 1 was proved by Wu (1997) for 2D and by Lannes (2005) for general dimension using Nash–Moser iteration or paralinearisation of G(η). The key estimate is the tame bound ‖G(η)ψ‖Hs−1/2 ≤ C(‖η‖Hs+1/2)‖ψ‖Hs+1/2. Global existence for small, localised data in 3D was achieved by Wu (2009) and Germain–Masmoudi–Shatah (2012) using a vector‑field method and the Alinhac good unknown to eliminate derivative loss. Almost global existence for 2D small data was proved by Ionescu–Pusateri (2014). Surface tension changes the dispersion relation and allows global existence for small data in 2D (Wu, 2011). The Cauchy problem with vorticity is more delicate; Ifrim–Tataru (2019) established local well‑posedness for rotational flows in 2D using a Lagrangian formulation.

Key Points

  • Dirichlet–Neumann operator and its paralinearisation.
  • Local well‑posedness in Hs, s > n/2 + 1 (Wu, Lannes).
  • Global existence for small data in 3D (Wu, Germain–Masmoudi–Shatah).
  • Surface tension improves dispersion and enables 2D global results.
  • Rotational flows require Lagrangian coordinates (Ifrim–Tataru).

Common Traps

  • Assuming Calderón–Zygmund estimates hold for p = 1 or p = ∞; they fail, giving weak‑type or BMO instead.
  • Confusing the Evans–Krylov theorem (concave/convex fully nonlinear) with Caffarelli’s C1,α theory (general uniformly elliptic).
  • Thinking Vlasov–Poisson global existence requires small data; it holds for large smooth data.
  • Believing the Vlasov–Maxwell system has global classical solutions for all large data; this is open.
  • Neglecting the loss of derivatives in the water‑wave problem; the Dirichlet–Neumann operator is not bounded on Sobolev spaces without tame estimates.
  • Mixing up the role of surface tension: it changes the dispersion relation from ω ∼ √|ξ| to ω ∼ |ξ|3/2, which is crucial for global existence in 2D.
  • Overlooking that the mean‑field limit for Vlasov equations requires propagation of chaos, not just tightness.

De Giorgi–Nash–Moser Theory and Hölder Regularity for Divergence Form Elliptic Equations

The De Giorgi–Nash–Moser theory provides the cornerstone regularity result for weak solutions of uniformly elliptic equations in divergence form. Consider the operator L u = −∂i(aij(x) ∂j u) where the coefficient matrix A(x) = (aij) is bounded, measurable and satisfies the uniform ellipticity condition λ |ξ|2 ≤ aij(x) ξi ξj ≤ Λ |ξ|2 for all ξ ∈ ℝn and almost every x. De Giorgi (1957) introduced a geometric level‑set method to prove that any weak solution u ∈ H1(Ω) of L u = 0 is locally Hölder continuous. His argument uses the Caccioppoli inequality, a measure‑theoretic estimate for the decay of the distribution function of u, and an iteration that yields an oscillation decay oscBr u ≤ C (r/R)α oscBR u for concentric balls Br ⊂ BR. Nash (1958) independently obtained the same result for the parabolic analogue by constructing a fundamental solution and proving Gaussian upper bounds. Moser (1960) later gave a unified proof via a clever iteration of Lp estimates (Moser iteration) that also yields the Harnack inequality for non‑negative solutions: supBr u ≤ C infBr u. The theory extends to equations with lower‑order terms and to systems under a Legendre‑Hadamard condition, but fails for general elliptic systems (De Giorgi’s counterexample). The Hölder exponent α depends only on n, λ, Λ. This result is the starting point for the Calderón–Zygmund Lp theory and for the regularity of minimisers of integral functionals ∫ f(∇u) dx with f convex and of quadratic growth.

Krylov–Safonov Theory for Non‑Divergence Form Elliptic Equations

The Krylov–Safonov theory (1979) addresses the regularity of solutions to uniformly elliptic equations in non‑divergence form aij(x) ∂ij u = 0 with bounded measurable coefficients. Unlike the divergence case, no variational structure is available, so the De Giorgi–Nash–Moser method does not apply directly. Krylov and Safonov introduced the Alexandrov–Bakelman–Pucci (ABP) maximum principle, which bounds the supremum of a subsolution by an integral of the right‑hand side over the contact set of its convex envelope. Using the ABP estimate they proved a Harnack inequality for non‑negative solutions: supBR/2 u ≤ C infBR/2 u with a constant depending only on n, λ, Λ. The Harnack inequality then yields Hölder continuity via a standard oscillation decay argument. A key technical tool is the “growth lemma”, which shows that if a solution oscillates by 1 on a ball, then on a smaller ball it must stay away from either 0 or 1 by a fixed proportion. The theory also covers equations with lower‑order terms and provides the foundation for the Evans–Krylov theorem on C2,α regularity for concave (or convex) fully nonlinear equations F(D2u) = 0. The Krylov–Safonov Harnack inequality is known to fail for non‑uniformly elliptic operators, highlighting the sharpness of the uniform ellipticity assumption.

Technique: Applying the De Giorgi–Nash–Moser Oscillation Lemma
Let u ∈ H1(B1) be a weak solution of −∂i(aij(x) ∂j u) = 0 in the unit ball B1 ⊂ ℝn, with λ|ξ|2 ≤ aijξiξj ≤ Λ|ξ|2. Show that there exist constants C, α > 0 depending only on n, λ, Λ such that oscB1/2 u ≤ C oscB1 u.
1. Normalise so that oscB1 u = 1 and infB1 u = 0. 2. Apply the Caccioppoli inequality to the truncated functions (u−k)+ and (k−u)+ to obtain energy estimates for level sets. 3. Use the De Giorgi isoperimetric lemma: if the measure of {u > 1/2} in B1 is small, then u ≤ 1/2 − δ on B1/2 for some δ > 0. 4. Iterate the lemma on a dyadic sequence of balls B2−k to obtain a geometric decay of the oscillation. 5. The iteration yields oscB1/2 u ≤ (1−θ) oscB1 u for some θ ∈ (0,1). Setting α = −log2(1−θ) and C = 2α gives the desired estimate.
Exam strategy
Distinguish divergence‑form (De Giorgi–Nash–Moser) from non‑divergence‑form (Krylov–Safonov) regularity. Remember that the ABP estimate is the non‑divergence counterpart of the Caccioppoli inequality. In multiple‑choice questions, the phrase “bounded measurable coefficients” without “divergence form” signals Krylov–Safonov; “weak solution in H1” signals De Giorgi–Nash–Moser.

Viscosity Solutions for Fully Nonlinear Parabolic Equations

The theory of viscosity solutions, introduced by Crandall and Lions (1983) for first‑order Hamilton–Jacobi equations, extends naturally to fully nonlinear parabolic equations of the form ∂t u + F(t, x, u, Du, D2u) = 0 where F is continuous and degenerate elliptic (non‑increasing in the matrix argument). A function u (upper semicontinuous) is a viscosity subsolution if for every smooth test function φ touching u from above at (t0, x0) one has ∂t φ + F(t0, x0, u, Dφ, D2φ) ≤ 0. Supersolutions are defined analogously with lower semicontinuity and the opposite inequality. The comparison principle — that a subsolution ≤ a supersolution on the parabolic boundary implies the same inequality everywhere — is the core of the theory and holds under standard structural conditions (properness, uniform continuity in x, and a Lipschitz condition in u). Existence follows from Perron’s method: the supremum of all subsolutions below a given supersolution is a viscosity solution. This framework covers Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations from stochastic control, Isaacs equations from differential games, and geometric flows such as mean curvature flow (level‑set formulation). Regularity results include the Krylov–Safonov Hölder estimates for the spatial gradient when F is uniformly elliptic in D2u, and the C1,α regularity for the solution itself under convexity/concavity assumptions (the parabolic Evans–Krylov theorem). Viscosity solutions also provide a robust notion for the limit of vanishing viscosity approximations and for the stochastic representation via backward stochastic differential equations.

Mean Field Games and the Lasry–Lions System

Mean field games (MFG) describe the limit of symmetric N‑player stochastic differential games as N → ∞. The Lasry–Lions system (2006‑2007) couples a backward Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for the value function u(t,x) with a forward Fokker–Planck (Kolmogorov) equation for the density m(t,x) of the population: −∂t u − νΔu + H(x, Du) = F(x, m), ∂t m − νΔm − div(m DpH(x, Du)) = 0, with terminal condition u(T,x) = G(x, m(T)) and initial condition m(0) = m0. Here H is the Hamiltonian, F and G are coupling functions, and ν ≥ 0 is the diffusion coefficient. The monotonicity condition ∫ (F(x,m1)−F(x,m2))(m1−m2) dx ≥ 0 (Lasry–Lions monotonicity) guarantees uniqueness of classical solutions. For ν > 0 the system is uniformly parabolic and classical solutions exist for smooth data; for ν = 0 (first‑order MFG) one works with viscosity solutions and weak solutions of the continuity equation. The master equation, a PDE on the space of probability measures, encodes the value function of a representative player as a functional of the initial distribution and provides a powerful tool for propagation of chaos and convergence rates of the N‑player game. Applications include crowd motion, economics (optimal trading, systemic risk), and engineering (smart grid control). Numerical methods rely on finite‑difference schemes for the coupled system or on the semi‑Lagrangian approach for the HJB equation combined with a particle method for the Fokker–Planck equation.

Regularity Structures for Singular Stochastic PDEs

Hairer’s theory of regularity structures (2013) provides a systematic framework for solving stochastic PDEs that are ill‑posed in the classical sense due to the roughness of the driving noise. The prototypical example is the KPZ equation ∂t h = νΔh + (∇h)2 + ξ with space‑time white noise ξ in dimension one, where the nonlinearity (∇h)2 is not well defined for the expected regularity of h. A regularity structure consists of a graded vector space T = ⊕α∈A Tα (model space), a group of linear transformations (structure group), and a model (Π, Γ) that assigns to each point a linear map Πx : T → 𝒟′ and recentering maps Γxy : T → T. Modelled distributions f : ℝd → T generalise jets of smooth functions; the reconstruction theorem associates to each modelled distribution a unique distribution Rf that locally approximates Πx f(x). The solution map is built by lifting the equation to the model space, solving a fixed‑point problem for modelled distributions, and then reconstructing. Renormalisation appears as a change of the model (BPHZ‑type counterterms) that restores continuity of the solution map with respect to the noise. The theory has been applied to the Φ43 equation, the stochastic quantisation of the 2D Yang–Mills measure, and the parabolic Anderson model. Recent developments include the algebraic approach of Bruned–Chandra–Chevyrev–Hairer (2020) and the connection to rough paths via the “rough path lift” of the model.
