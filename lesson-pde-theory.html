PDE Theory — Elliptic, Parabolic & Hyperbolic Equations, Sobolev Spaces & Regularity

Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) are equations involving an unknown function of several variables and its partial derivatives. The classification of second-order linear PDEs divides them into elliptic (Laplace, Poisson), parabolic (heat), and hyperbolic (wave) types based on the algebraic properties of their principal symbol. Sobolev spaces W^{k,p} provide the natural function space setting for weak solutions, enabling powerful tools like the Lax-Milgram theorem and the Fredholm alternative. The maximum principle (weak and strong) gives qualitative control over elliptic and parabolic solutions. Regularity theory — including the Nash-Moser estimates for parabolic equations with measurable coefficients — shows that weak solutions are often smooth. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem computes the index of elliptic operators in terms of topological invariants, unifying many classical results.

"PDEs are the language in which the laws of physics are written."
— Lawrence C. Evans
Part 1

📏 Classification and Weak Solutions

Foundations
Second-Order Linear PDEs, Classification by Symbol, Sobolev Spaces, and Weak Formulation
A second-order linear PDE in general form is a^{ij}(x)∂_{ij}u + b^i(x)∂_i u + c(x)u = f(x). The principal symbol is the quadratic form σ(x,ξ) = a^{ij}(x)ξ_iξ_j. Classification: elliptic if a^{ij}ξ_iξ_j > 0 for all ξ ≠ 0 (e.g., Laplace –Δu = f); parabolic if the symbol has one degenerate direction and is positive-definite on the orthogonal complement (e.g., heat equation ∂_t u – Δu = 0); hyperbolic if the symbol is indefinite (e.g., wave equation ∂_t² u – Δu = 0). Weak solutions live in Sobolev spaces: u ∈ H¹(Ω) satisfies ∫ a^{ij}∂_i u ∂_j v dx = ∫ f v dx for all test functions v ∈ H¹₀(Ω). The Sobolev spaces W^{k,p}(Ω) have norm ||u||_{W^{k,p}} = Σ_{|α|≤k} ||∂^{α} u||_{L^p}. Key embedding theorems: Sobolev embedding W^{k,p} ⊂ C^{0,α} when kp > n; trace theorem restricting functions to the boundary; Rellich-Kondrachov compact embedding W^{1,p} ⊂⊂ L^q for q < p* = np/(n–p).
The classification of PDEs by the symbol's signature determines fundamental properties: elliptic equations are smoothing, parabolic equations diffuse, and hyperbolic equations propagate waves.
Part 2

🌍 Elliptic PDE and the Lax-Milgram Theorem

Elliptic Theory
Lax-Milgram Theorem, Fredholm Alternative, Maximum Principle, and Harnack Inequality
The Lax-Milgram theorem is a cornerstone of elliptic theory: if B: H × H → ℝ is a bilinear form that is coercive (B[u,u] ≥ c||u||² for some c > 0) and bounded (|B[u,v]| ≤ C||u|| ||v||), then for every bounded linear functional F on H, there exists a unique u ∈ H with B[u,v] = F(v) for all v ∈ H. Application: the Dirichlet problem –Δu = f in Ω, u|_∂Ω = 0 has a unique weak solution u ∈ H¹₀(Ω) for any f ∈ H^{-1}(Ω). The Fredholm alternative: either the elliptic equation Lu = f has a unique solution for every f, or the homogeneous equation Lu = 0 has nontrivial solutions — in the latter case, solvability requires f to be orthogonal to the kernel. The strong maximum principle (Hopf): if u ∈ C²(Ω) satisfies Lu ≥ 0 and u attains its maximum at an interior point, then u is constant throughout Ω. The Harnack inequality: for nonnegative solutions of elliptic equations, sup_{B_r} u ≤ C inf_{B_r} u, giving strong control over solution oscillation.
Lax-Milgram provides existence and uniqueness for elliptic boundary value problems, while the maximum principle gives qualitative pointwise control over solutions.
Part 3

🧮 Parabolic and Hyperbolic Equations

Evolution Equations
Heat Equation, Parabolic Regularity, Wave Equation, and Finite Speed of Propagation
The heat equation ∂_t u – Δu = 0 has fundamental solution Φ(x,t) = (4πt)^{-n/2} e^{-|x|²/4t} for t > 0. The parabolic maximum principle: sup_{Ω×[0,T]} u = sup_{Γ_T} u, where Γ_T = (Ω×{0}) ∪ (∂Ω×[0,T]) is the parabolic boundary — implying the solution is bounded by its initial and boundary data. The smoothing effect: even if the initial data is merely L¹, the solution is C^{∞} for t > 0. The De Giorgi-Nash-Moser theorem establishes Hölder continuity of solutions to parabolic equations with merely measurable coefficients, a key regularity result. The wave equation ∂_t² u – Δu = 0 in 1+1 dimensions has the d'Alembert formula: u(x,t) = ½(f(x+t)+f(x–t)) + ½∫_{x–t}^{x+t} g(s) ds for initial conditions u(x,0)=f(x), ∂_t u(x,0)=g(x). Finite speed of propagation: if initial data is supported in the ball B_R, then at time t the solution is supported in B_{R+t} — no signal travels faster than speed 1. Huygens' principle in odd dimensions > 1: waves have sharp trailing edges (no wake), unlike even dimensions where there are lingering tails.
Parabolic equations smooth rough data instantly and diffuse information, while hyperbolic equations propagate singularities along characteristic surfaces at finite speed.
Part 4

🔥 The Atiyah-Singer Index Theorem and Elliptic Complexes

Index Theory
Elliptic Complexes, de Rham Complex, Hodge Decomposition, and the Atiyah-Singer Index Theorem
An elliptic complex is a sequence of vector bundles Eᵘ and differential operators dᵘ: C^{∞}(Eᵘ) → C^{∞}(E^{ᵘ+1}) with d_{ᵘ+1} ∘ dᵘ = 0 and exact symbol sequence. The canonical example: the de Rham complex 0 → Ω⁰ → Ω¹ → ... → Ω^n → 0 with exterior derivative d. The Hodge decomposition: on a compact Riemannian manifold, Ω^k = im d_{k–1} ⊕ ker Δ_k ⊕ im d^*_k, where Δ = dd^* + d^*d is the Laplace–Beltrami operator. The index of an elliptic operator D: ind(D) = dim ker D – dim ker D^*. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem: ind(D) = ∫_{T^*M} ch(σ(D)) · Td(TM ⊗ ℂ), a topological formula involving the Chern character of the symbol and the Todd class of the complexified tangent bundle. Applications: the Gauss-Bonnet-Chern theorem (index of d+d^* on forms = Euler characteristic χ(M)), the Hirzebruch signature theorem (signature = L-genus), the Riemann-Roch theorem (index of ∂̄ = χ(M, E)), and the positive mass theorem in general relativity (Schoen-Yau, Witten) using spinor Dirac operators.
The Atiyah-Singer index theorem connects analysis (the index of an elliptic operator) with topology (characteristic numbers), revealing deep geometric invariants.
