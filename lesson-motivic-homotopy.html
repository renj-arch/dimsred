Motivic Homotopy — the A¹-Homotopy Theory, the Morel-Voevodsky Category & Motives

Motivic homotopy theory is a synthesis of algebraic geometry and algebraic topology. It begins with the category Smk of smooth schemes of finite type over a field k and equips it with the Nisnevich topology (a Grothendieck topology finer than Zariski, coarser than étale) to define a model structure on simplicial presheaves. A¹-localization formally inverts the projections X × 𝔸¹ → X, yielding the motivic homotopy category H(k). Stabilizing with respect to the motivic spheres S^{1,0} (the simplicial circle) and S^{1,1} (𝔸¹\{0} with the multiplicative action) gives the stable motivic homotopy category SH(k) — the algebraic analogue of the classical stable homotopy category. Motivic cohomology H^{p,q}(X, ℤ) is the bigraded cohomology theory represented by the motivic Eilenberg-MacLane spectrum Hℤ in SH(k). Voevodsky used motivic Steenrod operations to prove the Milnor conjecture (relating Milnor K-theory to étale cohomology mod 2) and, with Rost, the full Bloch-Kato conjecture (the norm residue isomorphism theorem) — a 2010 Fields Medal. Morel's A¹-connectedness theorem and the Levine-Morel algebraic cobordism further cement the subject as a cornerstone of modern arithmetic geometry.

"Motivic homotopy theory brings algebraic topology to algebraic geometry."
— Fabien Morel
Part 1

📏 The A¹-Homotopy Category

Foundations
The Category Smk, Nisnevich Topology, A¹-Localization, and H(k)
Let Smk denote the category of smooth schemes of finite type over a field k. A Nisnevich cover is an étale cover {Ui → X} such that for every field point Spec(k(x)) → X, there exists a Ui → X and a lifting Spec(k(x)) → Ui. The Nisnevich topology sits strictly between the Zariski and étale topologies. The category of simplicial presheaves sPre(Smk) carries the injective model structure with weak equivalences the Nisnevich local weak equivalences (those inducing isomorphisms on all Nisnevich sheaves of homotopy groups). A¹-localization is the left Bousfield localization that forces the projections X × 𝔸¹ → X to be weak equivalences. The resulting homotopy category is the motivic homotopy category H(k). The A¹-connected components sheaf π0A¹(X) is the Nisnevich sheaf associated to the presheaf U ↦ [U, X]A¹ (maps in H(k)). Morel's theorem: for any field k, π0A¹(X)(Spec(K)) = X(K)/R where R is the equivalence relation generated by elementary A¹-connections — i.e., two K-points are A¹-homotopic iff they can be linked by a chain of maps 𝔸¹K → X. Classifying spaces BA¹G for an algebraic group G are defined as the A¹-homotopy type of the simplicial bar construction; they classify A¹-homotopy classes of Nisnevich-locally trivial G-torsors.
The motivic homotopy category H(k) is built by declaring the affine line 𝔸¹ to be contractible — the algebraic analogue of making the unit interval contractible in classical homotopy theory.
Part 2

🌍 Motivic Cohomology and the Bloch-Kato Conjecture

Cohomology
Motivic Cohomology H^{p,q}, the Motivic Steenrod Algebra, and the Norm Residue Isomorphism
Motivic cohomology H^{p,q}(X, ℤ) is the bigraded cohomology theory defined as HomSH(k)(Σp,qX+, Hℤ), where Hℤ is the motivic Eilenberg-MacLane spectrum (the spectrum representing motivic cohomology). The Bloch-Quillen formula: H^{p,q}(X, ℤ) ≅ CHq(X, 2q−p) where CHq(X, n) are Bloch's higher Chow groups — an exact complex computing algebraic cycles modulo rational equivalence. The motivic Steenrod algebra A** is the bigraded algebra of cohomology operations on motivic cohomology with ℤ/ℓ coefficients; Voevodsky constructed Steenrod squares Sqi in the motivic setting, acting on Hp,q(X, ℤ/2). The Milnor conjecture (proved by Voevodsky, 1996): the norm residue homomorphism KMn(k)/2 → Hnét(k, ℤ/2) is an isomorphism — equivalently, the étale cohomology ring of a field mod 2 is the Milnor K-theory mod 2. The Bloch-Kato conjecture (now the Norm Residue Isomorphism Theorem, proved by Voevodsky with contributions from Rost, Weibel, and others, 2010): for any field k, any prime ℓ invertible in k, and any n ≥ 0, the norm residue map KMn(k)/ℓ → Hnét(k, μℓ⊗n) is an isomorphism. The proof uses motivic cohomology, the motivic Steenrod algebra, and the Beilinson-Lichtenbaum conjecture.
Motivic cohomology is the universal bigraded cohomology theory for smooth schemes; the Bloch-Kato conjecture shows it computes étale cohomology after inverting ℓ.
Part 3

🧮 The Stable Motivic Homotopy Category

Stability
SH(k), the Bigraded Spheres S^{p,q}, MGL, the Slice Spectral Sequence, and η-Periodicity
The stable motivic homotopy category SH(k) is obtained from H(k) by stabilizing with respect to the motivic sphere spectrum S0,0 and its suspensions. The bigraded spheres are Sp,q = Sp−q,0 ∧ S0,q where S1,0 is the simplicial circle Δ¹/∂Δ¹ and S1,1 = 𝔸¹\{0} pointed by 1 (the Tate circle). The Chow-Witt ring CH̲n(X) is the (2n,n)-cohomology of X with coefficients in the unramified sheaf given by Milnor-Witt K-theory. The algebraic cobordism spectrum MGL is the motivic analogue of MU: it is the Thom spectrum of the universal rank-n tautological bundles over BA¹GLn. The Levine-Morel theorem: the algebraic cobordism ring Ω*(k) = MGL2*,*(Spec k) ≅ L ⊗ ℤ (the Lazard ring) — algebraic cobordism is the universal oriented cohomology theory for schemes. The slice spectral sequence (Voevodsky) is a spectral sequence converging to πp,q(E) whose E₁-page consists of the cohomology of the slices sqE; for MGL, the slices are given by suspensions of Hℤ. The theory of η-periodicity studies the algebraic Hopf map η: 𝔸²\{0} → ℙ¹ (the motivic analogue of the Hopf fibration). The η-localized motivic sphere S0,0[η⁻¹] is the localization inverting η; its homotopy groups are ℤ[η, η⁻¹]-modules and exhibit strong periodicity phenomena connecting classical stable homotopy with arithmetic.
SH(k) is a bigraded, highly structured symmetric monoidal category that simultaneously generalizes the classical stable homotopy category and encodes deep arithmetic invariants of the base field.
Part 4

🔥 Applications to Number Theory and Algebraic Geometry

Arithmetic
Milnor Conjecture Proof, Bloch-Kato, A¹-Classifying Spaces, and Arithmetic Geometry
Voevodsky's proof of the Milnor conjecture (1996): construct the motivic Steenrod operations Sqi on motivic cohomology mod 2, then produce the Milnor operations Qi as certain compositions of Sqi; the vanishing of these operations on the motivic cohomology of fields gives the Milnor conjecture. The full Bloch-Kato conjecture (the Norm Residue Isomorphism Theorem, 2010): Voevodsky (with Rost handling ℓ = 2 and the general case using symmetric power constructions) reduced the problem to the existence of certain norm varieties — varieties whose existence is proved via the Merkurjev-Suslin and Rost theorems on the degree of a field extension. The Beilinson-Soulé vanishing conjecture predicts H^{p,q}(Spec k, ℚ) = 0 for q > 0 and p ≤ 0; it implies the motivic Tate conjecture relating rational cycles and étale cohomology. Morel's A¹-homotopy classification of vector bundles: for a smooth affine scheme X over a field of characteristic ≠ 2, rank n vector bundles on X (up to isomorphism) are in bijection with A¹-homotopy classes [X, BA¹GLn]A¹ — vastly generalizing the classification of algebraic vector bundles. In arithmetic geometry, the A¹-homotopy theory of torsors shows that every Nisnevich-locally trivial G-torsor over the affine line 𝔸¹k is trivial when G is a special algebraic group — a result with applications to the study of rational points.
Motivic homotopy theory provides the most powerful known toolkit for solving long-standing problems in arithmetic, from the Milnor and Bloch-Kato conjectures to the classification of vector bundles.

The Nisnevich Topology and A¹-Localization

The Nisnevich topology on the category of smooth schemes over a field k is a Grothendieck topology generated by étale morphisms f: U → X that induce isomorphisms on residue fields at every point of X. It is strictly finer than the Zariski topology and strictly coarser than the étale topology. The key property is that every smooth scheme is locally isomorphic to an affine space in the Nisnevich topology, making it the natural setting for A¹-homotopy invariance.

Morel and Voevodsky construct the A¹-homotopy category H(k) by a two-step localization of the category of simplicial presheaves on Smk. First, Nisnevich localization LNis forces Nisnevich hypercovers to become weak equivalences, yielding the Nisnevich-local model structure where fibrant objects satisfy Nisnevich descent. Second, A¹-localization LA¹ inverts the projection maps X × 𝔸¹ → X. The composite LA¹LNis produces a proper, cellular, simplicial model category whose homotopy category is H(k).

The A¹-localization functor can be described explicitly via the singular construction SingA¹(X)n = X(− × 𝔸ⁿ). The unit map X → SingA¹(X) becomes an isomorphism in H(k). A simplicial presheaf is A¹-local if it is Nisnevich-local and the map induced by projection 𝔸¹ → Spec k is a weak equivalence on mapping spaces. The resulting category H(k) is a symmetric monoidal triangulated category after stabilization.

Important consequences include the A¹-invariance of motivic cohomology: for any smooth X, the projection X × 𝔸¹ → X induces an isomorphism on motivic cohomology groups Hp,q. The A¹-fundamental group sheaf π₁A¹(X) classifies A¹-homotopy classes of loops and is a strictly A¹-invariant sheaf. Morel proved that for a smooth affine scheme over a perfect field, the A¹-fundamental group is strongly related to the algebraic fundamental group and to the classification of vector bundles via the map [X, BA¹GLn]A¹.

Descent and Excision

Nisnevich descent implies that for any Nisnevich distinguished square, the induced square of mapping spaces into a fibrant object is homotopy cartesian. This excision property is essential for constructing the motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectra Hℤ and for the proof of the Beilinson–Lichtenbaum conjecture. The Nisnevich topology also ensures that the motivic cohomology of a smooth scheme can be computed via the Suslin–Voevodsky complex of relative cycles, which satisfies A¹-invariance and Nisnevich excision by design.

The Motivic Adams Spectral Sequence

The motivic Adams spectral sequence (MASS) is the primary computational tool for the motivic stable homotopy groups of a motivic spectrum E over a field k of characteristic not equal to a fixed prime ℓ. It is built from the motivic Steenrod algebra Amot = H*,*(Hℤ/ℓ, Hℤ/ℓ), the bigraded algebra of cohomology operations on motivic cohomology with ℤ/ℓ coefficients. Voevodsky computed Amot over perfect fields: it is generated by the motivic Steenrod squares Sqi (mod 2) or the reduced powers Pi and the Bockstein β (mod ℓ), subject to the Adem relations and the motivic Cartan formula, with an extra weight grading.

For a motivic spectrum E, the MASS takes the form

E₂s,w,f = Exts,w,fAmot(H*,*(E), H*,*(S0,0)) ⇒ πt-s,w(E)ℓ̂

where the trigrading (s,w,f) corresponds to homological degree, weight, and filtration. The spectral sequence converges conditionally to the ℓ-completed motivic stable homotopy groups of E. When E = S0,0 (the motivic sphere spectrum), the MASS computes the motivic stable stems π*,*(S0,0)ℓ̂.

Over ℂ, the Betti realization functor identifies the motivic Steenrod algebra with the classical Steenrod algebra tensored with a polynomial algebra on a class τ of bidegree (0,−1). This allows the transfer of classical Adams computations to the motivic setting. Isaksen, Guillou, and others have used the MASS to determine low-dimensional motivic stable stems over ℂ and ℝ, revealing elements such as η ∈ π1,1, ν ∈ π3,2, and σ ∈ π7,4, together with the motivic analogues of the classical Greek-letter elements. The MASS also exhibits a motivic version of the Mahowald invariant and detects η-periodic families.

Convergence and Vanishing Lines

Convergence of the MASS for finite motivic spectra follows from the motivic analogue of the Adams–Novikov convergence theorem, using the fact that the motivic Steenrod algebra is a Hopf algebroid satisfying a motivic version of the change-of-rings isomorphism. A motivic vanishing line of slope 1/2 (in the (s,w)-plane) has been established for the sphere spectrum over ℂ, implying that the MASS is a powerful tool for systematic computation. The differentials in the MASS are often determined by comparison with the classical Adams spectral sequence via Betti realization and by the motivic May spectral sequence.

Worked Example: Computing π1,1(S0,0) via MASS
Show that the motivic Hopf map η ∈ π1,1(S0,0) corresponds to the class h₁ in the MASS E₂-page over ℂ.
Over ℂ, the motivic Steenrod algebra Amot ≅ Acl ⊗ ℤ/2[τ] where Acl is the classical Steenrod algebra and τ has bidegree (0,−1). The motivic cohomology of the sphere is H*,*(S0,0) = ℤ/2[τ]. The Ext group Ext1,1,1Amot(ℤ/2[τ], ℤ/2[τ]) is generated by the class h₁ ⊗ 1, where h₁ ∈ Ext1,1Acl(ℤ/2, ℤ/2) corresponds to the classical Hopf map. The motivic Hopf map η: 𝔸²{0} → ℙ¹ induces the same class under the isomorphism π1,1(S0,0) ≅ Ext1,1,1. The differential d₂(h₁) = 0 because there is no element in the appropriate bidegree, so h₁ survives to E∞ and represents η. This matches the known fact that η has order 2 and is not divisible by τ.
Exam Strategy
When asked to identify a motivic stable stem element in the MASS, first write down the bigrading (s,w,f) of the candidate Ext class. Then check whether the class survives known differentials (often d₂ or d₃) by comparing with the classical Adams chart and the motivic τ-multiplication. Remember that τ ∈ π0,−1 acts by lowering weight; many motivic differentials are τ-multiples of classical ones.

The Motivic Slice Filtration and Adams–Novikov Spectral Sequence

The slice filtration on the stable motivic homotopy category SH(k) was introduced by Voevodsky as a motivic analogue of the Postnikov tower. For a motivic spectrum E, the q-th effective cover fqE is the universal approximation of E by a spectrum whose homotopy sheaves vanish below weight q. The q-th slice is the cofiber sqE = cofib(fq+1E → fqE). The slices are always modules over the motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum Hℤ.

The slice spectral sequence has E₁-page

E₁p,q = πp,q(sqE) ⇒ πp,q(E)

and converges strongly for E in the effective part of SH(k). For the algebraic cobordism spectrum MGL, Voevodsky proved that the slices are given by suspensions of Hℤ: sqMGL ≃ Σ2q,qHℤ. This identification is a cornerstone of the proof that MGL represents algebraic cobordism and that its coefficient ring is the Lazard ring.

The motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence (MANSS) uses MGL as a ring spectrum. Its E₂-page is

E₂s,w = Exts,wMGL*MGL(MGL*, MGL*E) ⇒ π*,*(E)MGL̂

where the Hopf algebroid (MGL*, MGL*MGL) is the motivic analogue of (MU*, MU*MU). The slice spectral sequence and the MANSS are linked by the motivic Landweber exact functor theorem: if E is a Landweber exact MGL-module, then the slice filtration on E coincides with the filtration induced by the MANSS. This provides a motivic chromatic picture where the height of a formal group law over a field corresponds to the slice degree at which the Morava K-theory slices appear.

Applications to the Motivic Chromatic Picture

The slice filtration allows one to define motivic analogues of the classical chromatic layers. For each prime ℓ and height n, there is a motivic Morava K-theory spectrum K(n)mot whose slices are concentrated in a single weight. The telescope conjecture in the motivic setting predicts that the localization of SH(k) with respect to K(n)mot is smashing. Recent work by Burklund, Hahn, Levy, and Schlank has established a motivic version of the nilpotence theorem and the thick subcategory theorem, confirming that the slice filtration behaves as a chromatic filtration.

Motivic Stable Stems and Homotopy Groups of Spheres

The bigraded stable homotopy groups of the motivic sphere spectrum, πp,q(S0,0) = πp,q(SH(k)), are the motivic stable stems. Over ℂ, the Betti realization functor ReB: SH(ℂ) → SH (the classical stable homotopy category) induces a homomorphism πp,q(S0,0) → πpS that forgets the weight. The kernel and cokernel of this map are controlled by the element τ ∈ π0,−1(S0,0) and the motivic Hopf map η ∈ π1,1.

Open Problems

Determining the full motivic stable stems remains a major open problem. The motivic Mahowald invariant, the structure of the τ-torsion submodule, and the relationship with the motivic Adams–Novikov E∞-page are active research areas. The computation of π*,*(S0,0) over general fields (especially finite fields and number fields) is largely unknown beyond the 0-line and the η-line.

The Motivic Landweber Exact Functor Theorem and Algebraic Cobordism

The motivic Landweber exact functor theorem (due to Naumann, Spitzweck, and Østvær) provides a criterion for a commutative MGL-algebra E to be represented by a motivic spectrum. Let MGL* = π*,*(MGL) be the motivic Lazard ring, which is isomorphic to the classical Lazard ring ℒ placed in bidegree (2*,*). A homomorphism MGL* → R* of bigraded rings makes R* a Landweber exact MGL*-module if for every prime ℓ and every integer n ≥ 0, the sequence (ℓ, v₁, …, vn−1) is regular on R*, where vi are the Hazewinkel generators. Under this condition, the functor X ↦ MGL*(X) ⊗MGL* R* is a homology theory on SH(k) represented by a motivic spectrum E with π*,*(E) ≅ R*.

Algebraic cobordism MGL is the universal oriented motivic ring spectrum. The Levine–Morel theorem identifies the algebraic cobordism ring of a field Ω*(k) = MGL2*,*(Spec k) with the Lazard ring ℒ. This makes MGL the motivic analogue of the complex cobordism spectrum MU. The motivic Landweber exact functor theorem then allows the construction of motivic versions of classical Landweber exact spectra:

  • Motivic complex cobordism MUmot (coefficients ℒ[b₁, b₂, …] with |bi| = (2i, i)).
  • Motivic Brown–Peterson spectra BPmot at a prime ℓ.
  • Motivic Morava K-theories K(n)mot and Morava E-theories Enmot.

These spectra inherit the slice filtration from MGL; their slices are shifts of Hℤ or Hℤ/ℓ. The motivic chromatic convergence theorem (Burklund–Hahn–Levy–Schlank) states that for a motivic spectrum E of finite type, the natural map E → holimn LK(n)motE is an equivalence, where LK(n)mot is Bousfield localization with respect to motivic Morava K-theory. This establishes a full motivic chromatic picture parallel to the classical one.

Relation to Quadratic Forms and Milnor–Witt K-Theory

The Chow–Witt ring CH̲n(X) = π2n,n(X, KMWn) is the cohomology of X with coefficients in the unramified sheaf of Milnor–Witt K-theory. The motivic spectrum representing Milnor–Witt K-theory is a module over MGL after η-inversion. The Landweber exact functor theorem applied to the η-periodic version of MGL yields a motivic spectrum whose homotopy groups are the Witt groups of the base field, linking algebraic cobordism to the theory of quadratic forms.

The Motivic Steenrod Algebra and its Action on Motivic Cohomology

The motivic Steenrod algebra Amot over a field k of characteristic different from a prime ℓ is the bigraded Hopf algebra of stable cohomology operations on motivic cohomology H*,*(−; ℤ/ℓ). Voevodsky constructed the operations; the algebraic structure was refined by Hoyois, Kelly, and Østvær. As a bigraded algebra it is generated by the motivic Steenrod squares Sqi (for ℓ = 2) or reduced powers Pi and Bockstein β (for odd ℓ), subject to the motivic Adem relations which involve the element τ ∈ π0,−1(S0,0) and the element ρ ∈ π−1,−1 over non‑real fields. The coproduct is determined by the formula Δ(Sqi) = Σ Sqj ⊗ Sqi−j together with the rule that τ is primitive. This structure makes Amot a module over the classical Steenrod algebra via the Betti realization functor, but the presence of τ and ρ creates new torsion phenomena absent in topology.

The action of Amot on motivic cohomology of smooth schemes is computed via the motivic Eilenberg–Mac Lane spectrum Hℤ/ℓ. For a smooth k‑scheme X, the cohomology groups Hp,q(X; ℤ/ℓ) are modules over Amot, and the Milnor basis of the dual algebra provides a convenient description of the coaction. Voevodsky showed that the motivic cohomology of a point is a free rank‑one module over the motivic Steenrod algebra, i.e. H*,*(Spec k; ℤ/ℓ) ≅ Amot as a left module. This identification underpins the motivic Adams spectral sequence, where the E2‑page is ExtAmot(H*,*(X), H*,*(Y)). The presence of τ‑torsion in the Ext groups is a hallmark of the motivic setting and distinguishes the motivic Adams chart from its classical counterpart.

Recent work of Isaksen, Gheorghe–Wang–Xu, and others has produced explicit low‑weight calculations of Amot over ℂ and ℝ. Over ℂ the motivic Steenrod algebra is a module over the polynomial ring ℤ/2[τ] (τ in bidegree (0,−1)), and its structure is a crossed product with the classical Steenrod algebra; τ is not nilpotent in the coefficient ring. Over ℝ an additional class ρ ∈ H−1,−1 appears with ρ2 = 0 and the relation ρ·η = 0 in the stable stems. These calculations feed directly into the computation of motivic stable stems and the verification of the motivic Mahowald invariant. Understanding the module structure of motivic cohomology over Amot is therefore essential for any aspirant aiming to work with the motivic Adams spectral sequence or the motivic slice spectral sequence.

The Motivic Slice Spectral Sequence and its Convergence

The slice filtration on the stable motivic homotopy category SH(k) was introduced by Voevodsky and later refined by Spitzweck, Levine, and others. For a motivic spectrum E, the q‑th slice sqE is defined as the cofiber of the map fq+1E → fqE where fqE is the q‑th effective cover. For effective spectra, the slices are given by sqE ≅ Σ2q,q Hℤ ⊗ π−2q,−q(E), making them modules over the motivic Eilenberg–Mac Lane spectrum Hℤ. The associated slice spectral sequence has E1p,q,w = πp,q(swE) and converges conditionally to πp,q(E) under finiteness hypotheses.

Convergence of the slice spectral sequence is subtle. For a spectrum of finite type (i.e. each motivic homotopy group is a finitely generated module over the motivic stable stems of the base field) the spectral sequence converges strongly. The work of Levine, Hoyois, and others established a vanishing line of slope 1 in the (p,q)‑plane, which guarantees that the E∞‑page computes the associated graded of a finite filtration on the target homotopy groups. For non‑effective spectra one must use the η‑periodic slice filtration, where inverting the motivic Hopf map η yields a 2‑periodic theory and the slices become shifts of Hℤ[η−1]. The motivic chromatic convergence theorem of Burklund–Hahn–Levy–Schlank shows that after K(n)‑localization the slice spectral sequence collapses at a finite stage, providing a motivic analogue of the classical chromatic tower.

Explicit computations of the slice spectral sequence have been carried out for the sphere spectrum, algebraic cobordism MGL, and motivic Morava K‑theories. For the sphere over ℂ the E2‑page in low weights reproduces the known motivic stable stems: the 0‑line gives ℤ, the η‑line gives ℤ/2 generated by η, and the τ‑towers appear as infinite vertical families. The slice differential d1 on the η‑line is zero, while the first non‑trivial differential hits the τ‑tower, reflecting the relation τ·η = 0. These calculations illustrate how the slice filtration organizes the motivic homotopy groups by weight and provides a bridge to the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence via the identification of slices with motivic cohomology.

Worked Example: Computing a Slice Differential
Let E = Σ2,1 Hℤ/2 be the motivic Eilenberg–Mac Lane spectrum shifted by (2,1). Determine the first non‑zero slice differential d1 on the class x ∈ π2,1(s1E) corresponding to the generator of H2,1(Spec ℂ; ℤ/2).
The slice s1E is Σ2,1 Hℤ/2 itself because E is already a slice. The slice spectral sequence for E has E1p,q,1 = πp,q(Σ2,1 Hℤ/2). The class x lives in bidegree (2,1) and weight 1. The differential d1 goes from weight 1 to weight 0, i.e. d1: π2,1(s1E) → π1,1(s0E). Since s0E = 0 for this spectrum (there is no weight‑0 slice), the target group vanishes. Hence d1(x) = 0. The first potentially non‑zero differential would be d2 landing in weight −1, which also vanishes because negative weight slices are zero for effective spectra. Therefore x survives to the E∞‑page and represents the element τ ∈ π0,−1(S0,0) after the identification π2,1(Σ2,1 Hℤ/2) ≅ π0,−1(S0,0). This matches the known fact that the motivic Hopf map η kills τ and that τ is a permanent cycle in the slice spectral sequence of the sphere.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a slice differential, first identify the weight of the source and target slices. Recall that sqE = 0 for q < 0 on effective spectra, so any differential landing in negative weight is automatically zero. Next, use the known identification sqE ≅ Σ2q,q Hℤ ⊗ π−2q,−q(E) to translate the problem into a computation in motivic cohomology. Finally, check the motivic Steenrod algebra action: many differentials are forced by the relation τ·η = 0 or by the motivic Adem relations. Writing down the bigrading (p,q,w) explicitly prevents sign and weight errors.

Motivic Homotopy Fixed Points and Galois Descent

The formalism of homotopy fixed points in motivic homotopy theory provides a bridge between the motivic stable homotopy category over a non‑algebraically closed field k and the category over its separable closure ks. Let G = Gal(ks/k) be the absolute Galois group. For a motivic spectrum E over ks equipped with a continuous G‑action, the homotopy fixed point spectrum EhG is defined as the mapping spectrum MapSH(ks)(Σ∞+ G, E)G. The fundamental theorem of motivic Galois descent (due to Hoyois, Kelly, and Østvær) states that the base‑change functor SH(k) → SH(ks)hG is an equivalence of symmetric monoidal ∞‑categories. This means that a motivic spectrum over k can be recovered uniquely from its pullback to ks together with the Galois descent data.

The homotopy fixed point spectral sequence (HFPSS) computes the motivic homotopy groups of EhG from the continuous Galois cohomology of the motivic homotopy groups of E. Its E2‑page is Hscont(G; πt,w(E)) where the coefficients carry the natural G‑action induced by the action on ks. For the sphere spectrum, π*,*(S0,0ks) is known (e.g. over ℂ it is the motivic stable stems computed by Isaksen), and the Galois action factors through the cyclotomic character on the weight grading. Over ℝ the group G = ℤ/2 acts non‑trivially on the element ρ ∈ π−1,−1, giving rise to the well‑known exact sequence relating the motivic stable stems over ℝ to those over ℂ.

Applications of motivic Galois descent include the computation of the motivic cohomology of number fields, the study of the motivic Tate conjecture for varieties with good reduction, and the construction of motivic spectra with prescribed Galois representations. For instance, the motivic spectrum representing algebraic K‑theory satisfies KGLhG ≃ KGLk, and the HFPSS for KGL recovers the Quillen–Lichtenbaum conjecture (now a theorem of Voevodsky–Rost) relating algebraic K‑theory to étale K‑theory. The same technique underlies the proof of the motivic Bloch–Kato conjecture, where the Galois cohomology of Milnor K‑theory is identified with the motivic cohomology of the base field.

The Motivic Tate Conjecture and its Relation to Motivic Cohomology

The motivic Tate conjecture predicts that for a smooth projective variety X over a finitely generated field k, the cycle class map from the Chow group CHr(X) ⊗ ℚℓ to the Galois‑invariant subspace of étale cohomology H2rét(Xk̄, ℚℓ(r)) is surjective. In the language of motivic homotopy theory, this statement is equivalent to the assertion that the motivic cohomology group H2r,r(X; ℚℓ) injects into the ℓ‑adic realization and that its image coincides with the Galois invariants. The motivic cohomology groups Hp,q(X; ℤ) are defined as HomDM(k)(ℤ, M(X)(q)[p]) in Voevodsky’s triangulated category of motives DM(k). The ℓ‑adic realization functor DM(k) → Dét(k, ℚℓ) sends M(X) to the étale cohomology complex, and the Tate twist (q) corresponds to the Tate twist in étale cohomology.

A major breakthrough was the proof of the Bloch–Kato conjecture (the norm residue isomorphism theorem) by Voevodsky, Rost, and others, which identifies the Galois cohomology of Milnor K‑theory with motivic cohomology. This result implies the motivic Tate conjecture for divisors (r = 1) on all smooth varieties over finitely generated fields. For higher codimension, the conjecture remains open in general, but it is known for abelian varieties (by work of Tate, Zarhin, and others) and for certain Shimura varieties via the theory of motivic cohomology with modulus. The motivic Steenrod algebra acts on motivic cohomology, and the Tate conjecture can be reformulated as the statement that the Galois invariants in the ℓ‑adic realization are exactly the image of the motivic Steenrod‑stable classes.

Recent work of Kahn, Yamazaki, and others has introduced the notion of motivic cohomology with modulus, which provides a finer invariant capable of detecting cycles that are not captured by ordinary motivic cohomology. The modulus version of the Tate conjecture is expected to hold for all smooth projective varieties over finitely generated fields and would imply the classical Tate conjecture. In the motivic stable homotopy category, the Tate conjecture is closely related to the vanishing of the motivic Hopf map η on certain Galois‑invariant classes and to the structure of the motivic Adams spectral sequence for the sphere over k. Understanding these connections is a central theme in current research and a frequent source of advanced examination questions.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of Derived Schemes

Classical motivic homotopy theory is built on the category of smooth schemes of finite type over a field k. To extend the theory to singular or non‑finite‑type objects, one replaces schemes by derived schemes (or more generally derived Artin stacks) in the sense of Toën–Vezzosi and Lurie. The ∞‑category of derived schemes over k admits a Nisnevich topology defined by covers that are étale and induce isomorphisms on truncations. The motivic homotopy category H(k) is then obtained by localizing the ∞‑category of presheaves of spaces on derived schemes at Nisnevich covers and at the projection maps X × 𝔸1 → X. This construction yields a presentable ∞‑category that contains the classical H(k) as a full subcategory spanned by smooth schemes.

The stable motivic homotopy category SH(k) of derived schemes is the stabilization of H(k) with respect to the Tate sphere S1,0 and the motivic sphere S1,1 = 𝔸1/𝔾m. It enjoys the same formal properties as the classical SH(k): a symmetric monoidal structure, a six‑functor formalism, and a motivic slice filtration. The key advantage is that singular varieties now have well‑behaved motivic cohomology and algebraic K‑theory, and the theory satisfies cdh‑descent (Voevodsky’s cdh topology) automatically. This resolves long‑standing issues such as the failure of Nisnevich excision for singular schemes and allows the definition of motivic cohomology for arbitrary finite‑type k‑schemes via the formula Hp,q(X; ℤ) = π−p,−q(Map(Σ∞+ X, Hℤ)).

Applications of the derived theory include the construction of the motivic spectrum representing algebraic cobordism MGL for all schemes, the proof of the motivic Landweber exact functor theorem in the derived setting (by Naumann–Spitzweck–Østvær), and the development of a motivic chromatic homotopy theory for derived schemes. The derived motivic Steenrod algebra acts on the motivic cohomology of derived schemes, and the motivic Adams spectral sequence converges for a larger class of spectra. For examination purposes, it is important to know that the derived theory agrees with the classical one on smooth schemes, that the Nisnevich topology on derived schemes is defined via truncations, and that the six‑functor formalism extends without change. These facts are often tested in questions comparing the classical and derived frameworks.

  • Confusing the motivic Steenrod algebra with its classical counterpart; the motivic version contains τ and ρ which alter Adem relations.
  • Assuming the slice spectral sequence converges for all spectra without checking finite‑type or vanishing‑line hypotheses.
  • Neglecting the continuous Galois action on weights when applying the homotopy fixed point spectral sequence.
  • Treating the motivic Tate conjecture as a theorem for all codimensions; it is only known in special cases.
  • Believing that derived motivic homotopy theory changes the homotopy groups of smooth schemes; it does not.
  • Overlooking that the motivic Hopf map η is not nilpotent in the stable motivic category, unlike the classical η.

Motivic Equivariant Homotopy Theory and the Motivic Adams Spectral Sequence for Group Actions

Equivariant motivic homotopy theory extends the Morel–Voevodsky framework to schemes with an action of a finite group G. The category of G‑schemes over a base field k carries the Nisnevich topology generated by G‑equivariant Nisnevich covers. One forms the ∞‑category of presheaves of spaces on G‑schemes, localizes at Nisnevich covers and at the projection X × 𝔸¹ → X, and stabilizes with respect to the Tate sphere S¹,⁰ and the motivic sphere S¹,¹ = 𝔸¹/𝔾ₘ. The resulting stable equivariant motivic homotopy category SHᴳ(k) is symmetric monoidal and compatible with the forgetful functor to SH(k). Work by Hoyois and Heller establishes that the equivariant motivic sphere spectrum admits fixed‑point functors Φᴴ for subgroups H ≤ G, and that the geometric fixed‑point functor Φᴳ recovers the underlying non‑equivariant motivic spectrum after forgetting the action. The equivariant motivic Adams spectral sequence (MAS) takes the form ExtAᴳs,w(Hᴳ∗∗(X), Hᴳ∗∗(Y)) ⇒ [Σs,wX, Y]SHᴳ(k), where Aᴳ is the equivariant motivic Steenrod algebra. This algebra extends the classical motivic Steenrod algebra with transfers and restrictions from subgroup inclusions; its structure involves equivariant motivic Dyer–Lashof operations and the equivariant motivic Hopf map ηᴳ. Convergence typically requires a vanishing line of slope 1/2 in the (s,w)‑plane, a condition verified for finite G‑CW spectra built from representation spheres. Applications include computations of equivariant motivic stable stems for G = C₂ over ℝ, where RO(C₂)‑graded homotopy groups relate to Real topological stable stems via Betti realization, and progress on equivariant versions of the Bloch–Kato conjecture. Examination questions often ask to compare the equivariant MAS with the classical motivic MAS, to identify the role of transfer maps on the E₂‑page, or to explain why the equivariant motivic Hopf map ηᴳ is not nilpotent in SHᴳ(k) while its non‑equivariant counterpart η becomes nilpotent after one suspension.

Motivic Homotopy Theory over Non‑Perfect Fields and the Role of the Nisnevich Topology

When the base field k is not perfect, the smooth Nisnevich site lacks the excision properties needed for the six‑functor formalism. Morel and Voevodsky originally assumed k perfect to ensure the Nisnevich topology on Smₖ is subcanonical and that A¹‑localization preserves finite limits. Over non‑perfect fields, one replaces Smₖ by the category of essentially smooth schemes of finite type, or works with Voevodsky’s cdh topology, generated by proper surjective morphisms and Nisnevich covers. The cdh topology restores excision for all finite‑type k‑schemes, and the cdh‑local motivic homotopy category Hcdh(k) agrees with H(k) on smooth schemes. Results of Kelly and Saito show that motivic cohomology Hp,q(X, ℤ) defined via cdh‑localization satisfies the expected localization sequence for any closed immersion of finite‑type schemes, even when X is singular. A¹‑homotopy invariance holds for all schemes after cdh‑localization, and the motivic Steenrod algebra acts on this extended cohomology. The stable category SHcdh(k) is obtained by stabilizing Hcdh(k) with respect to the Tate and motivic spheres; it carries a symmetric monoidal structure and a six‑functor formalism extending that on SH(k). The motivic slice filtration on SHcdh(k) uses the same cellular towers, with convergence controlled by vanishing‑line arguments valid for all finite‑type spectra. Examiners often test the distinction between Nisnevich and cdh descent by asking why the Nisnevich topology fails to give excision for the pair (Spec k[t]/(t²), Spec k) over a non‑perfect field, and how the cdh cover Spec k[t]/(t²) → Spec k resolves the issue. Another common point is that the motivic cohomology of a singular scheme defined via the Nisnevich site differs from the cdh‑local definition precisely by the cdh‑cohomological descent spectral sequence.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of Stacks and Higher Stacks

Algebraic stacks, and more generally higher Artin stacks, admit a motivic homotopy theory developed by Toën–Vezzosi, Hoyois, and others. One starts with the ∞‑category of derived Artin stacks locally of finite presentation over a field k, equipped with the Nisnevich topology generated by étale covers that are representable by schemes and induce isomorphisms on truncations. The ∞‑category of presheaves of spaces on this site is localized at Nisnevich covers and at the projection X × 𝔸¹ → X, yielding the motivic homotopy ∞‑category HSt(k). Its stabilization SHSt(k) is a presentable symmetric monoidal stable ∞‑category containing SH(k) as the full subcategory generated by smooth schemes. The six‑functor formalism extends to SHSt(k) via work of Liu–Zheng and Khan, providing pushforward, pullback, exceptional pushforward, and exceptional pullback for representable morphisms of stacks. A key feature is that the motivic cohomology of a stack X, defined as Hp,q(X, ℤ) = π-p,-q Map(Σ∞₊ X, Hℤ), satisfies descent for all Nisnevich covers of stacks, and the motivic Steenrod algebra acts on this cohomology. The slice filtration on SHSt(k) is defined by the same cellular towers, and its associated graded pieces are modules over the motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum Hℤ. Applications include the definition of motivic cohomology for classifying stacks BG of finite groups G, where H∗∗(BG, ℤ) recovers the group cohomology of G with coefficients in Milnor K‑theory, and the construction of the motivic spectrum representing algebraic cobordism MGL on stacks, which satisfies the projective bundle formula for vector bundles on stacks. Examiners often ask why the Nisnevich topology on stacks must be defined via truncations; the answer is that an étale cover of stacks need not be a cover on the level of underlying schemes, and the truncation condition ensures effective descent for homotopy sheaves. Another typical question compares the motivic cohomology of BG computed in HSt(k) with classical group cohomology of G, noting that the weight grading corresponds to the Milnor K‑theory weight.

Motivic Homotopy Theory with Modulus and the Theory of Reciprocity

Motivic homotopy theory with modulus, introduced by Kahn, Saito, and Yamazaki, refines the classical theory by incorporating a modulus condition controlling ramification along a divisor. For a pair (X, D) where X is a smooth scheme and D an effective Cartier divisor, one considers the category of modulus pairs and the Nisnevich topology generated by covers that are isomorphisms outside D and satisfy a ramification bound. The resulting motivic homotopy category Hmod(k) admits a symmetric monoidal structure and a stable version SHmod(k). The motivic cohomology with modulus Hp,qmod(X|D, ℤ) fits into a localization sequence relating the modulus cohomology of (X, D) to the ordinary motivic cohomology of X and the cohomology of the complement X D. The modulus motivic Steenrod algebra acts on these groups, and the modulus version of the Bloch–Kato conjecture predicts an isomorphism between Milnor K‑theory with modulus and motivic cohomology with modulus. Work of Saito and Sato establishes a reciprocity isomorphism for modulus motivic cohomology, generalizing the classical Kato reciprocity map in higher class field theory. In the stable category, the modulus motivic sphere spectrum Σ∞₊ (𝔸¹, ∞) represents the motivic cohomology with modulus of the affine line with modulus at infinity, and its homotopy groups compute the higher Chow groups with modulus. Examination questions frequently ask to state the modulus localization sequence, to explain why the Nisnevich topology on modulus pairs requires a ramification bound, or to describe the relationship between the modulus motivic Hopf map ηmod and the classical η. A common trap is to confuse modulus motivic cohomology with the cdh‑local motivic cohomology of singular schemes; the former controls ramification along a fixed divisor, while the latter resolves singularities by proper covers.

Realization Functors: Betti, Étale, and de Rham Realizations in Motivic Homotopy Theory

Realization functors connect the motivic stable homotopy category SH(k) to classical stable homotopy theory and to ℓ‑adic and p‑adic representation categories. For k ⊂ ℂ, the Betti realization Re_B: SH(k) → SH is a symmetric monoidal functor sending the motivic sphere Sp,q to the topological sphere Sp and the motivic Hopf map η to the classical Hopf map η ∈ π₁^S. It is constructed by taking the complex points of a smooth scheme with the analytic topology and then applying the singular complex functor. The étale realization Re_et: SH(k) → SHℓ (the ℓ‑adic stable homotopy category) is defined for any field k and prime ℓ ≠ char(k) by composing the ℓ‑adic completion of the motivic sphere spectrum with the ℓ‑adic étale homotopy type of Artin–Mazur. It sends the motivic sphere Sp,q to the ℓ‑adic sphere Sp and the motivic Steenrod algebra to the classical ℓ‑adic Steenrod algebra. The de Rham realization Re_dR: SH(k) → D(k) (the derived category of mixed Hodge modules or filtered vector spaces) is available for k of characteristic zero and is constructed via the algebraic de Rham complex of smooth schemes, extended to SH(k) by A¹‑invariance and Nisnevich descent. A comparison theorem of Ayoub and Cisinski–Déglise states that the Betti and étale realizations are compatible with the six‑functor formalism and with the slice filtration: the slice spectral sequence maps to the classical Adams spectral sequence under Re_B, and to the ℓ‑adic Adams spectral sequence under Re_et. The motivic Tate conjecture can be reformulated as the statement that the Galois invariants in the ℓ‑adic realization of a motivic spectrum are exactly the image of the motivic Steenrod‑stable classes under Re_et. In exams, candidates must be able to describe the effect of each realization on the motivic spheres Sp,q, on the Hopf map η, and on the motivic Steenrod generators τ and ρ. A typical trap is to claim that the de Rham realization exists in positive characteristic; it does not, because the algebraic de Rham complex fails to be A¹‑invariant without a lift to characteristic zero.

  • Confusing cdh‑local motivic cohomology with modulus motivic cohomology; they address different singularities and ramification phenomena.
  • Believing that the Nisnevich topology on stacks is the same as on schemes; it must be defined via truncations to ensure effective descent.
  • Stating that the de Rham realization functor exists for fields of positive characteristic; it requires characteristic zero.
  • Thinking that the motivic Hopf map η becomes nilpotent after one suspension in the equivariant stable category; ηᴳ remains non‑nilpotent.
  • Neglecting the double coset formula when computing transfers and restrictions in the equivariant motivic Steenrod algebra.

The Six‑Functor Formalism in Motivic Homotopy Theory

The six‑functor formalism (f*, f*, f!, f!, ⊗, Hom) for the stable motivic homotopy category SH(k) was established independently by Ayoub and by Cisinski–Déglise. For a separated morphism of finite type f: X → Y of smooth schemes over a base field k, the functors f* and f* are the pullback and pushforward on motivic spectra, while f! and f! are the exceptional pushforward and pullback, defined via the purity equivalence for smooth morphisms and the localization triangle for closed immersions. The tensor product ⊗ is the smash product of motivic spectra, and the internal Hom is the function spectrum. The formalism satisfies the usual adjunctions (f* ⊣ f*, f! ⊣ f!), the projection formula f*(A ⊗ f*B) ≃ f*A ⊗ B, and the base‑change isomorphism for Cartesian squares. A key ingredient is the purity theorem: for a smooth morphism of relative dimension d, there is a canonical equivalence f! ≃ f*(d)[2d] where (d)[2d] denotes the Tate twist. This identifies the exceptional functors with the ordinary ones up to a shift, and it underlies the construction of the motivic fundamental class of a smooth scheme. The six functors are compatible with the realization functors: Betti, étale, and de Rham realizations are symmetric monoidal and commute with the six operations up to canonical isomorphism. Candidates should be prepared to state the purity equivalence, to explain how the localization triangle for a closed immersion Z X with complement U yields the distinguished triangle f!f* → Id → j*j* →, and to describe the effect of the Tate twist on the motivic sphere Sp,q. A frequent conceptual error is to confuse the exceptional pushforward f! with the ordinary pushforward f* for non‑proper morphisms; f! is defined for all separated finite‑type morphisms, while f* requires properness to preserve compact objects.

The Triangulated Category of Motives DM(k) and its Comparison with SH(k)

Voevodsky’s triangulated category of motives DM(k) is constructed as the homotopy category of complexes of Nisnevich sheaves with transfers, localized with respect to A¹‑homotopy. It carries a symmetric monoidal structure given by the tensor product of motives, and the motive of a smooth scheme X is denoted M(X). The motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum Hℤ represents motivic cohomology in SH(k); there is a canonical symmetric monoidal functor ρ: SH(k) → DM(k) sending a motivic spectrum E to the motive represented by its zeroth homotopy sheaf π₀(E). This functor is conservative on the subcategory of cellular spectra and identifies the motivic cohomology groups Hp,q(X, ℤ) with HomDM(k)(M(X), ℤ(q)[p]). The Tate motive ℤ(1) corresponds to the motivic sphere S2,1 under ρ. The comparison functor ρ admits a right adjoint given by the motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum functor, and the unit of the adjunction induces the motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence E₂p,q = Hp,q(X, π-qE) ⇒ π-p-qE(X) for any motivic spectrum E. In particular, for E = KGL (the algebraic K‑theory spectrum) this recovers the motivic spectral sequence from motivic cohomology to algebraic K‑theory. The category DM(k) is rigid: every object has a dual, and the dual of M(X) is M(X)(−dim X)[−2dim X] for smooth X. It is important to understand the effect of ρ on the motivic Hopf map η ∈ π1,1(S0,0) (it maps to zero because η is torsion of order 2 in SH(k) while DM(k) is ℚ‑linear after inverting the exponential characteristic), and to explain why the motivic Steenrod operations act trivially on the image of ρ. A common misconception is to claim that ρ is an equivalence; it is not, because SH(k) contains essential torsion information (e.g. η, ν) that is lost in DM(k) unless one works with ℤ‑coefficients and keeps track of the η‑completed sphere spectrum.

cdh‑Descent and the cdh‑Local Motivic Homotopy Category

The cdh topology (completely decomposed topology) on the category of schemes of finite type over a field k is generated by Nisnevich covers and proper surjective morphisms that are completely decomposed at every point. Voevodsky proved that the cdh‑localization of the motivic homotopy category H(k) yields a category Hcdh(k) in which every scheme satisfies cdh‑descent. This localization is essential for extending motivic cohomology and the motivic stable homotopy category to singular schemes: for a singular scheme X, one defines its motivic cohomology as the cdh‑sheafification of the presheaf U ↦ Hp,q(U, ℤ). The cdh‑local motivic sphere spectrum Σ^∞_+ Xcdh represents this cohomology. A fundamental result of Cisinski–Déglise and of Kelly–Saito is that the cdh‑local stable motivic homotopy category SHcdh(k) is equivalent to the category of modules over the cdh‑local motivic sphere spectrum. The cdh‑localization functor Lcdh: SH(k) → SHcdh(k) is symmetric monoidal and commutes with the six functors. It sends the motivic Hopf map η to an isomorphism after one suspension in characteristic zero, reflecting the fact that cdh‑descent forces A¹‑invariance to hold for all schemes. Candidates must be able to state the cdh‑descent spectral sequence E₂p,q = H^pcdh(X, π-qE) ⇒ π-p-qE(X) for a motivic spectrum E, and to explain why the cdh‑topology is strictly finer than the Nisnevich topology (it adds proper completely decomposed covers). A common pitfall is to confuse cdh‑local motivic cohomology with the modulus motivic cohomology of a pair (X, D); the former resolves singularities by proper covers, while the latter controls ramification along a fixed divisor.

Motivic Homotopy Theory over Finite Fields: Weight Structures and the Tate Motive

When the base field k is finite, the stable motivic homotopy category SH(k) admits a weight structure whose heart is the category of effective geometric motives. The Tate motive ℤ(1) is the motive of the projective line relative to a point, and its powers ℤ(q) generate the weight filtration. Voevodsky’s computation of the motivic cohomology of a point over a finite field shows that Hp,q(Spec k, ℤ) is zero for p > q, ℤ for p = q = 0, and finite cyclic groups for 0 < p = q (related to the K‑theory of k). The motivic Steenrod algebra over a finite field acts on these groups, and the motivic Adams spectral sequence converges to the motivic stable stems πs,w(S0,0) with a vanishing line of slope 1/2. The weight structure yields a conservative weight complex functor w: SH(k) → K^b(Choweff(k)) to the bounded homotopy category of effective Chow motives. This functor sends the motivic sphere Sp,q to the Tate motive ℤ(q)[p] placed in degree p. The motivic Hopf map η ∈ π1,1 maps to zero in the weight complex because it has weight 1 but topological degree 1, violating the weight bound. Key tasks include computing the motivic cohomology groups Hp,q(Spec 𝔽_q, ℤ/ℓ) for ℓ ≠ p, describing the action of the motivic Steenrod operations on these groups, and explaining why the motivic Adams spectral sequence over a finite field has a horizontal vanishing line at the ℓ‑adic Adams–Novikov line. A typical error is to assume that the weight structure on SH(k) is the same as the slice filtration; the slice filtration is a tower of localizations, while the weight structure is a pair of subcategories satisfying orthogonality and generation properties.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of Algebraic K‑Theory and the Motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch Spectral Sequence

Algebraic K‑theory is represented in SH(k) by the motivic spectrum KGL, the algebraic analogue of the topological K‑theory spectrum. The motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence (AHSS) for a smooth scheme X takes the form E₂p,q = Hp,q(X, ℤ) ⇒ K-p-q(X), where the motivic cohomology groups are the bigraded groups defined by Voevodsky. The differentials in this spectral sequence are governed by the motivic Steenrod operations: the first non‑trivial differential d₃ is given by the motivic Steenrod square Sq² (or the reduced power operation at odd primes). The AHSS converges strongly for regular schemes of finite type over a field, and its E_∞‑page recovers the γ‑filtration on algebraic K‑theory. The motivic spectrum KGL is a module over the motivic sphere spectrum, and its homotopy groups πs,wKGL are the higher Chow groups CH^w(X, 2w−s). The motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for KGL has E₂‑term ExtMU_*MU(MU_*, MU_*KGL) and converges to the motivic stable stems of KGL. Candidates must be able to write down the E₂‑page of the motivic AHSS for X = Spec k, to identify the edge homomorphism with the Chern character map ch: K_*(k) → H*,*(k, ℚ), and to explain why the motivic Steenrod algebra acts trivially on the rational motivic cohomology of a point. A common pitfall is to confuse the motivic AHSS with the classical topological AHSS; the motivic version is bigraded and its differentials are motivic Steenrod operations, not the topological ones.

  • Mixing up cdh‑local motivic cohomology with modulus motivic cohomology; they address singularities and ramification respectively.
  • Assuming the weight structure on SH(k) coincides with the slice filtration; they are distinct filtrations with different properties.
  • Stating that the motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence for KGL has the same differentials as the topological AHSS; the motivic differentials are motivic Steenrod operations.
  • Believing that the motivic Hopf map η becomes invertible after cdh‑localization; it becomes an isomorphism after one suspension only in characteristic zero, not in general.
  • Neglecting the Tate twist in the purity isomorphism f! ≃ f*(d)[2d]; the twist is essential for the correct bidegree of the fundamental class.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of Classifying Spaces and the Motivic Cohomology of BG

The motivic cohomology of classifying spaces BG for linear algebraic groups G over a field k has been systematically studied by Voevodsky, Totaro, Guillou, and others. For G = GL_n the motivic cohomology ring H*,*(BGL_n, Z) is a polynomial algebra on the universal Chern classes c_i of bidegree (2i, i). This mirrors the topological result but the bigrading reflects the motivic weight. One approach uses the motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence for the motivic spectrum representing algebraic K‑theory (KGL) together with the fact that KGL*,*(BGL_n) is the completed representation ring. The spectral sequence is expected to collapse at the E2‑page in part because the motivic Steenrod operations act trivially on the Chern classes, a consequence of the motivic Wu formulas. For a general reductive group G, Totaro’s approximation technique replaces BG by a quotient of a representation by a free action of G, allowing the computation of H*,*(BG) as a subring of the cohomology of a smooth variety. The resulting rings are generated by characteristic classes of the standard representation, subject to the same relations as in the Chow ring of the classifying space. A key subtlety is that the motivic cohomology of BG with finite coefficients can have torsion not detected by the Chow ring; for instance, certain motivic Steenrod operations on Chern classes can produce non‑zero torsion classes. Examiners often test the distinction between the Chow ring (which is the weight‑graded piece of motivic cohomology) and the full bigraded motivic cohomology, and they may ask to identify the bidegree of a given characteristic class.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of Real Varieties and the Betti Realization Functor

When the base field k embeds into the complex numbers, there is a symmetric monoidal Betti realization functor Re_B : SH(k) → SH from the stable motivic homotopy category to the classical stable homotopy category. On the level of motivic spheres it sends Sp,q to the topological sphere S^p, forgetting the weight. This functor is constructed by sending a smooth k‑scheme X to the topological space X(C) of complex points with the analytic topology, and it extends to motivic spectra via the universal property of SH(k) as the universal stable homotopy category with an interval object A1. The Betti realization is compatible with the six‑functor formalism: it commutes with f^*, f_*, f^!, f_! for morphisms of k‑schemes, and it respects the purity equivalence for smooth morphisms. For a real closed field such as R, there is also a real realization functor Re_R : SH(R) → SHC2 to the genuine C2‑equivariant stable homotopy category, where C2 acts by complex conjugation on the complex points. This functor sends the motivic Hopf map η ∈ π1,1 to the non‑trivial element η_R ∈ π1,1C2 detected by the real topological Hopf map. The compatibility of Re_R with the motivic Steenrod algebra has been studied by Hoyois, Kelly, Østvær, and others, showing that the motivic Steenrod squares map to the equivariant Steenrod operations. A common examination point is to explain why the Betti realization does not preserve the Tate twist: Re_B(Z(q)) = Z for all q, so the weight information is lost. Candidates must also be able to describe the effect of Re_B on the motivic Adams spectral sequence, where it induces a map to the classical Adams spectral sequence that is an isomorphism on the E2‑page after inverting the Bott element.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of the Motivic Homotopy Category over a General Base Scheme

The relative motivic homotopy theory developed by Cisinski and Déglise constructs, for any Noetherian base scheme S of finite Krull dimension, a stable motivic homotopy category SH(S) that satisfies the six‑functor formalism and a purity theorem for smooth morphisms. The construction begins with the Nisnevich topology on Sm_S and the A1‑localization, exactly as over a field, but the presence of a non‑trivial base requires a careful treatment of the Tate twist: the motivic sphere spectrum over S is the suspension spectrum of the pointed simplicial sheaf represented by the affine line A1_S relative to the zero section. The category SH(S) is generated by the motives M(X) of smooth S‑schemes X, and the six functors f^*, f_*, f^!, f_!, ⊗, Hom are defined for any morphism f : X → Y of finite type S‑schemes. The purity equivalence states that for a smooth morphism f of relative dimension d there is a canonical isomorphism f^! ≃ f^*(d)[2d] where (d) denotes the Tate twist by the relative dimension. This isomorphism is essential for defining fundamental classes of smooth subschemes and for the construction of the motivic Thom isomorphism. Base change along a morphism g : T → S induces a symmetric monoidal functor g^* : SH(S) → SH(T) that commutes with the six functors. When S is a Dedekind domain, the category SH(S) admits a weight structure whose heart is the category of effective motives over S, and the motivic cohomology of the generic point recovers the motivic cohomology of the function field. A typical exam question asks to explain why the purity isomorphism requires the Tate twist and to compute the effect of base change on the motivic sphere spectrum.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of the Motivic Homotopy Category of Schemes with a Log Structure

Logarithmic motivic homotopy theory, developed by Asok, Doran, and others, extends the Morel–Voevodsky framework to fine and saturated log schemes. A log structure on a scheme X provides a way to keep track of degenerations and boundary divisors, which is crucial for studying moduli spaces and degenerations of algebraic varieties. The log Nisnevich topology is defined using log étale morphisms that are Nisnevich on the underlying schemes, and the log A1‑localization inverts the projection X × A1 → X where A1 carries the standard log structure from the divisor {0}. The resulting log motivic homotopy category Hlog(k) and its stable version SHlog(k) admit a log motivic sphere spectrum Sp,qlog where the weight q now records the log structure. The log motivic cohomology groups Hp,qlog(X, Z) are represented by maps in SHlog(k) from the log motivic sphere to the log motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum. A fundamental result is the log purity theorem: for a log smooth morphism f : X → Y of relative dimension d with a relatively ample log divisor, there is an equivalence f^! ≃ f^*(d)[2d] in SHlog(k). This allows the definition of log fundamental classes and a log motivic Thom isomorphism. The log motivic Steenrod algebra acts on log motivic cohomology, and its structure at the prime 2 has been investigated by Asok, Fasel, and others, showing new operations that detect the log structure. Applications include a log motivic interpretation of the degeneration of the Hodge–de Rham spectral sequence and a log motivic construction of the motivic nearby cycles functor. Examiners may ask to compare the log motivic sphere with the classical motivic sphere, or to explain why the log A1‑invariance forces the log structure on A1 to be the one associated to the divisor {0}.

  • Assuming the Betti realization preserves the Tate twist; it sends Z(q) to Z for all q, so weight information is lost.
  • Neglecting the Tate twist in the purity isomorphism f^! ≃ f^*(d)[2d]; the twist is essential for the correct bidegree of the fundamental class.
  • Thinking that base change g^* : SH(S) → SH(T) is always an equivalence; it is only an equivalence when g is an isomorphism or under very restrictive hypotheses.
  • Believing that log motivic homotopy theory is just the ordinary motivic homotopy theory of the complement of the divisor; the log structure encodes monodromy data that is invisible on the complement.
  • Stating that the log motivic Steenrod algebra coincides with the classical motivic Steenrod algebra; new operations appear that detect the log structure.
  • Overlooking that the motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence for BGL_n collapses because the motivic Steenrod operations vanish on Chern classes, not because of degree reasons alone.

A log structure on a scheme X is a sheaf of monoids M on the étale site with a homomorphism M → OX inducing an isomorphism on units. Log Motivic Homotopy Theory: Monodromy and Degenerations

The motivic homotopy theory of schemes with a log structure provides a framework for studying degenerations of algebraic varieties while retaining monodromy information. In the classical setting, the complement of a divisor loses the monodromy data encoded by the log structure. The log motivic homotopy category Hlog(k) is obtained by localizing the category of log spaces with respect to the log Nisnevich topology and then inverting the log A1-homotopy equivalence. The log motivic sphere spectrum Sp,qlog is defined as the suspension spectrum of the pointed log space (A1, {0}) with the standard log structure. The weight q in the bidegree (p, q) records both the Tate twist and the log structure. Log motivic cohomology groups Hp,qlog(X, ℤ) are representable by the log motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum Hℤlog. The log motivic Steenrod algebra at the prime 2 contains operations not present in the classical motivic Steenrod algebra; these detect the log structure and are crucial for computing the log motivic cohomology of log smooth varieties. The log purity theorem states that for a log smooth morphism f : X → Y of relative dimension d with a relatively ample log divisor, the exceptional inverse image functor f! is equivalent to f*(d)[2d] in the stable log motivic homotopy category SHlog(k). This equivalence is essential for defining log fundamental classes and for the log motivic Thom isomorphism. The log motivic nearby cycles functor, constructed by Asok and Doran, provides a motivic analogue of the classical nearby cycles functor and plays a key role in the study of degenerations of Hodge structures.

Examiner's trap
A common error is to assume that log motivic homotopy theory is equivalent to the ordinary motivic homotopy theory of the complement of the log divisor. Explain why this is false and give an example where the log structure provides information invisible on the complement.
The log structure on a scheme X with divisor D encodes the monodromy around D. The complement U = X D has trivial monodromy in the motivic homotopy category because the log structure is forgotten. For example, consider X = A1 with the log structure from {0}. The log motivic sphere S1,1log is not equivalent to the classical motivic sphere S1,1 in SH(k). The log motivic Steenrod algebra contains operations that act nontrivially on the log motivic cohomology of X but vanish on the classical motivic cohomology of U = Gm. Thus the log structure retains information about the degeneration that is lost on the complement.
Exam-day strategy
When asked about log motivic homotopy theory, always distinguish between the log Nisnevich topology and the classical Nisnevich topology. Remember that the log A1-invariance forces the log structure on A1 to be the one associated to {0}. The log purity theorem requires a relatively ample log divisor; without this hypothesis the equivalence f! ≃ f*(d)[2d] fails. The log motivic Steenrod algebra is strictly larger than the classical one at the prime 2.

Motivic Homotopy Theory of Derived Schemes

The motivic homotopy theory of derived schemes extends the Morel–Voevodsky framework to derived algebraic geometry. A derived scheme is a pair (X, OX) where X is a topological space and OX is a sheaf of simplicial commutative rings (or E∞-ring spectra). The A1-homotopy theory of derived schemes is developed by localizing the ∞-category of derived schemes with respect to the Nisnevich topology and then inverting the projection X × A1 → X. The resulting ∞-category Hdér(k) is a presentable ∞-category containing the classical motivic homotopy category H(k) as a full subcategory. The stable version SHdér(k) is a stable presentable ∞-category with a symmetric monoidal structure. The motivic sphere spectrum in SHdér(k) is the suspension spectrum of the pointed derived scheme A1 relative to the zero section. The six-functor formalism extends to derived schemes: for a morphism f : X → Y of finite type derived schemes, there are adjoint pairs (f*, f*) and (f!, f!), a tensor product ⊗, and an internal Hom. The purity equivalence for a smooth morphism f of relative dimension d takes the form f! ≃ f*(d)[2d], where the Tate twist is defined using the relative cotangent complex. The motivic homotopy theory of derived schemes is essential for constructing the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence and for studying the motivic chromatic picture, because derived schemes allow one to work with non-connective objects and to resolve singularities in a homotopically meaningful way.

Motivic Homotopy Theory of Higher Stacks

The motivic homotopy theory of stacks and higher stacks generalizes the Morel–Voevodsky framework to the ∞-category of higher stacks on the Nisnevich site. A higher stack is an ∞-sheaf of spaces on the site of smooth schemes. The A1-homotopy theory of higher stacks is obtained by localizing the ∞-category of higher stacks with respect to the Nisnevich topology and then inverting the projection X × A1 → X. The resulting ∞-category Hstk(k) is a presentable ∞-category containing the motivic homotopy category of schemes as a full subcategory. The stable version SHstk(k) is a stable presentable ∞-category with a symmetric monoidal structure. The motivic sphere spectrum is the suspension spectrum of the pointed higher stack A1 relative to the zero section. The six-functor formalism extends to higher stacks: for a morphism f : X → Y of finite type higher stacks, there are adjoint pairs (f*, f*) and (f!, f!), a tensor product ⊗, and an internal Hom. The purity equivalence for a smooth morphism f of relative dimension d is f! ≃ f*(d)[2d]. The motivic homotopy theory of higher stacks is crucial for studying classifying spaces BG of group schemes, moduli stacks, and the motivic cohomology of stacks. The motivic cohomology of a higher stack X is defined as the homotopy groups of the mapping space from X to the motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum. The motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence for a higher stack converges to the algebraic K-theory of the stack. This theory also provides a natural setting for the motivic Langlands program and for studying motivic sheaves on the moduli stack of bundles.

Motivic Homotopy Theory with Modulus

The motivic homotopy theory with modulus, developed by Kahn, Saito, and Yamazaki, provides a framework for studying reciprocity laws and the motivic cohomology of non-proper varieties. A modulus pair is a pair (X, D) where X is a smooth scheme and D is an effective Cartier divisor. The modulus Nisnevich topology is generated by Nisnevich morphisms that respect the modulus condition. The modulus A1-localization inverts the projection (X, D) × (A1, {0}) → (X, D) where A1 carries the modulus {0}. The resulting modulus motivic homotopy category Hmod(k) and its stable version SHmod(k) admit a modulus motivic sphere spectrum. The modulus motivic cohomology groups Hp,qmod(X, D; ℤ) are representable by the modulus motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum. Modulus motivic cohomology is closely related to the theory of reciprocity sheaves: a reciprocity sheaf is a Nisnevich sheaf with transfers satisfying a modulus condition. The category of reciprocity sheaves is a Tannakian category, and its Tannaka dual is the motivic fundamental group. Modulus motivic homotopy theory provides a motivic interpretation of the Kato–Saito reciprocity map and the Bloch–Kato exponential map. The modulus motivic Steenrod algebra acts on modulus motivic cohomology, and its structure reflects the modulus condition. Applications include a motivic construction of the reciprocity map for higher local fields and a motivic interpretation of the Hasse–Arf theorem.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of Group Actions and the Equivariant Motivic Adams Spectral Sequence

The equivariant extension of Morel–Voevodsky’s A¹‑homotopy theory replaces the site of smooth schemes with the site of smooth schemes equipped with an action of a fixed linear algebraic group G. The equivariant motivic homotopy category H_G(k) is obtained by taking the ∞‑category of G‑equivariant Nisnevich sheaves of spaces, localizing with respect to the equivariant Nisnevich topology, and inverting the projection X × A¹ → X where A¹ carries the trivial G‑action. The stable equivariant category SH_G(k) is a symmetric monoidal stable ∞‑category whose unit is the equivariant motivic sphere spectrum S0,0_G. A fundamental computational tool is the equivariant motivic Adams spectral sequence (EMASS), which converges to the equivariant stable motivic homotopy groups of a G‑spectrum. Its E₂‑page is given by Ext groups over the equivariant motivic Steenrod algebra Amot_G, a Hopf algebroid incorporating both classical motivic Steenrod operations and transfers associated to subgroups of G. The EMASS has been applied to compute equivariant motivic stable stems for G = C₂ over ℝ, showing interplay between the real motivic Hopf map η and equivariant transfer maps. A key technical result is the equivariant motivic slice filtration, yielding a tower of equivariant motivic spectra whose associated graded pieces are equivariant motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectra. This filtration is compatible with the EMASS and provides vanishing lines that guarantee strong convergence for finite G‑CW spectra. The equivariant formalism also accommodates the motivic analogue of the tom Dieck splitting, expressing the fixed‑point spectrum of a G‑spectrum in terms of the underlying non‑equivariant motivic spectra of the fixed‑point schemes.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of Classifying Stacks and the Motivic Cohomology of BG

For a linear algebraic group G over a field k, the classifying stack BG is the quotient stack [Spec k / G] in the Nisnevich topology. Its motivic homotopy type is represented by the motivic space obtained as the colimit of the simplicial scheme given by the bar construction B_•G. The motivic cohomology of BG, defined as the homotopy groups of the mapping space from BG to the motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum Hℤ, carries a natural ring structure and is closely related to the Chow ring of the classifying space in the sense of Totaro. When G is a split torus, the motivic cohomology of BG is a polynomial algebra on degree (2,1) generators corresponding to the characters of the torus. For non‑abelian groups such as GL_n or SL_n, the motivic cohomology of BG contains the motivic Chern classes of the universal vector bundle and satisfies a motivic version of the splitting principle. The motivic Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence for BG converges to the algebraic K‑theory of BG, with E₂‑page given by the motivic cohomology of BG. This spectral sequence has been used to compute the algebraic K‑theory of classifying spaces of finite groups over fields of characteristic not dividing the group order, revealing connections with the representation ring of G. Moreover, the motivic cohomology of BG admits an action of the motivic Steenrod algebra, and the resulting module structure encodes information about the motivic Stiefel–Whitney classes of G‑bundles. Recent work has extended these computations to non‑split groups using the motivic homotopy fixed point spectral sequence for the action of the absolute Galois group on the motivic cohomology of the split form.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of Algebraic Cobordism and the Motivic Landweber Exact Functor Theorem

Algebraic cobordism MGL is the universal oriented motivic ring spectrum in SH(k), constructed by Voevodsky as the Thom spectrum of the universal vector bundle over the infinite Grassmannian. Its coefficient ring MGL*,*(k) is isomorphic to the Lazard ring L equipped with a bigrading where the universal formal group law lives in bidegree (2,1). The motivic Landweber exact functor theorem (due to Naumann, Spitzweck, and Østvær) states that any graded L‑module M which is Landweber exact (i.e., the sequence of multiplication by the Hazewinkel generators v_i is regular) gives rise to a motivic spectrum MGL ∧_L M whose cohomology theory is represented by MGL*,*(−) ⊗_L M. This theorem provides a systematic way to construct motivic analogues of complex oriented theories such as motivic Brown–Peterson theory MBP, motivic Morava K‑theories MK(n), and motivic elliptic cohomology. The proof uses the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence based on the Hopf algebroid (MGL*,*(MGL), MGL*,*(MGL)) and the fact that MGL is a motivic commutative ring spectrum satisfying the motivic version of the Landweber exactness criterion. A crucial technical point is that the motivic Landweber exact functor theorem requires the base field to be perfect; over non‑perfect fields one must replace MGL by its cdh‑localization or work with the motivic spectrum representing algebraic cobordism with modulus. Applications include the construction of the motivic chromatic tower, where the Bousfield localization with respect to MK(n) yields the motivic n‑th Morava stabilizer group, and the computation of the motivic stable stems via the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence, which converges to the homotopy groups of the motivic sphere after completion at a prime p.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of the Motivic Fundamental Group and A¹‑Homotopy Groups

The A¹‑fundamental group π₁A¹(X, x) of a pointed smooth scheme (X, x) over a field k is defined as the sheaf of groups on the Nisnevich site associated to the presheaf U ↦ HomH(k)(S¹ ∧ U₊, X). Morel proved that for a simply connected smooth proper variety X, the A¹‑fundamental group is a strongly A¹‑invariant sheaf of groups, and it fits into an exact sequence relating the Nisnevich fundamental group, the A¹‑fundamental group, and the sheaf of units of the function field. The higher A¹‑homotopy sheaves π_nA¹(X) are defined analogously using the motivic spheres Sn,0 and Sn,n. These sheaves are strictly A¹‑invariant and satisfy a Hurewicz theorem: the first non‑vanishing A¹‑homotopy sheaf is isomorphic to the corresponding A¹‑homology sheaf. For affine spaces, π_nA¹(A^m) vanishes for n > 0, reflecting A¹‑contractibility. For punctured affine spaces A^m {0}, the A¹‑homotopy sheaves are related to Milnor–Witt K‑theory sheaves KMW_n. The A¹‑homotopy groups of motivic spheres Sp,q have been computed in a range using the motivic Adams spectral sequence; for example, π2,1A¹(S2,1) ≅ KMW2, and π3,2A¹(S2,1) contains a copy of the sheaf of Witt groups. The A¹‑fundamental group of the multiplicative group G_m is the sheaf of units G_m itself, while the A¹‑fundamental group of an elliptic curve is an extension of the constant sheaf ℤ by the sheaf of points of the elliptic curve. These computations are essential for the A¹‑homotopy classification of vector bundles and for the formulation of the A¹‑degree map in A¹‑enumerative geometry.

The Motivic Homotopy Theory of Realization Functors and the Motivic Hodge Conjecture

Realization functors connect the motivic stable homotopy category SH(k) to classical stable homotopy categories. The Betti realization Re_B: SH(ℂ) → SH sends a motivic spectrum to its underlying topological spectrum by evaluating on ℂ‑points and taking the associated analytic space. The étale realization Re_et: SH(k) → SHpro‑ét lands in the pro‑étale stable homotopy category and is compatible with the Galois action. The de Rham realization Re_dR: SH(k) → D(k) takes values in the derived category of mixed Hodge modules (or filtered vector spaces) and encodes Hodge‑theoretic information. These functors are symmetric monoidal and preserve the motivic sphere spectrum. The motivic Hodge conjecture predicts that the Hodge realization of a motivic cohomology class in H2p,p(X, ℚ) is algebraic if and only if it lies in the image of the cycle class map from the Chow group CH^p(X)_ℚ. In the language of motivic homotopy theory, this is equivalent to the statement that the Hodge realization functor induces an isomorphism on the weight‑graded pieces of the motivic Adams spectral sequence for the motivic sphere. Recent progress uses the motivic slice filtration to construct a motivic Hodge filtration on the Betti realization of MGL, and the motivic Landweber exact functor theorem to produce motivic analogues of the Brown–Peterson spectrum whose Hodge realization recovers the classical Brown–Peterson spectrum with its Hodge filtration. The compatibility of the six‑functor formalism with realization functors (proved by Ayoub and Cisinski–Déglise) ensures that the motivic Hodge conjecture can be approached via the study of the motivic cohomology of classifying spaces and the motivic Steenrod algebra action on Hodge realizations.

  • Assuming the motivic Landweber exact functor theorem holds over arbitrary bases without perfectness or cdh‑localization hypotheses.
  • Treating the A¹‑fundamental group as a discrete group rather than a Nisnevich sheaf of groups; its sheaf‑theoretic nature is essential for descent.
  • Overlooking that the Betti realization of MGL is not the classical MU but a filtered version whose associated graded is the classical MU with a Hodge filtration.
  • Believing that the motivic cohomology of BG for a non‑split group is simply the Galois invariants of the split case; the motivic homotopy fixed point spectral sequence has non‑trivial differentials.
  • Neglecting the role of the motivic slice filtration in providing vanishing lines for the equivariant motivic Adams spectral sequence; without it convergence can fail.
  • Stating that the motivic Hodge conjecture is equivalent to the classical Hodge conjecture; the motivic version involves the full motivic stable homotopy category and the slice filtration.

The Motivic EHP Sequence and the Motivic Hopf Map

The motivic EHP sequence is the algebraic analogue of the classical EHP sequence of James and Whitehead, relating the suspension, Hopf, and Whitehead product maps on motivic spheres. In the stable motivic homotopy category SH(k), the motivic Hopf map η ∈ π₁,₁(𝕊) plays a role analogous to the classical Hopf map but carries an additional weight grading. The motivic EHP sequence takes the form of a long exact sequence of bigraded homotopy sheaves πp,q(Sn,m) for motivic spheres Sn,m = Sn-m ∧ (𝔾_m)∧m. The suspension map E: πp,q(Sn,m) → πp+1,q(Sn+1,m) increases topological degree; the Hopf map H: πp,q(Sn,m) → πp-1,q-1(S2n-1,2m-1) is induced by the motivic Hopf construction; and the Whitehead product P: πp,q(S2n-1,2m-1) → πp,q(Sn,m) encodes the failure of commutativity. Morel computed the first non-trivial instance: the motivic Hopf map η: S2,1 → S1,0 generates π1,1(𝕊) ≅ KMW1, the sheaf of Milnor–Witt K-theory in weight 1. Over a perfect field k, the motivic EHP sequence converges to the motivic stable stems π*,*(𝕊) and interacts with the motivic Adams spectral sequence via the η-Bockstein spectral sequence. The motivic Hopf map η is not nilpotent in SH(k) — η4 = 0 but η3 ≠ 0 in π3,3(𝕊) — and its non-nilpotence is detected by the motivic Steenrod algebra action on motivic cohomology. The motivic EHP sequence for the motivic sphere spectrum 𝕊 splits after η-completion, yielding a decomposition of the η-complete motivic stable homotopy category into a tower of motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectra. This splitting is essential for computations in the 0- and 1-stems, where π0,0(𝕊) ≅ ℤ and π1,1(𝕊) ≅ KMW1, and for identifying the motivic image of J in higher stems.

The Motivic J-Homomorphism and the Image of J in Motivic Stable Stems

The motivic J-homomorphism J: πp,q(BGL) → πp,q(𝕊) maps the homotopy groups of the motivic classifying space BGL = colim_n BGL_n to the motivic stable stems. It is constructed via the motivic Thom spectrum MGL and the unit map 𝕊 → MGL, inducing a map on homotopy sheaves π*,*(BGL) → π*,*(MGL) → π*,*(𝕊). Over a perfect field k of characteristic not 2, the motivic J-homomorphism is closely related to the algebraic K-theory of k via the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for MGL. The image of J in motivic stable stems contains the motivic Hopf map η ∈ π1,1(𝕊), the motivic ν ∈ π3,2(𝕊) detected by the motivic Steenrod operation Sq2, and higher elements such as σ ∈ π7,4(𝕊) related to Sq4. The motivic J-homomorphism factors through the motivic Adams–Novikov E2-page ExtMGL_*MGL(MGL_*, MGL_*) and its image is controlled by the motivic chromatic filtration. In weight 0, the motivic J-homomorphism recovers the classical J-homomorphism after Betti realization. In weight 1, the image of J contains the sheaf KMW1 generated by η. In weight 2, the image of J contains a copy of the sheaf of Witt groups W(k) detected by the motivic ν element. The motivic J-homomorphism is not surjective onto the motivic stable stems; the cokernel contains exotic elements such as the motivic κ ∈ π14,8(𝕊) and the motivic θ4 ∈ π30,16(𝕊), detected by the motivic Adams spectral sequence but not by the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for MGL. The motivic image of J conjecture, formulated by Isaksen and others, predicts that the motivic J-homomorphism is injective on the motivic homotopy groups of BGL after η-completion and that its image coincides with the η-torsion-free part of the motivic stable stems in a range. This conjecture is known in low weights and over algebraically closed fields, where the motivic stable stems are computed via the motivic Adams spectral sequence and the motivic slice spectral sequence.

Exam Strategy: Motivic J-Homomorphism and Weight Grading
When asked about the motivic J-homomorphism, always track the bigrading (p,q). The classical J-homomorphism lives in weight 0; motivic J lives in all weights. Remember the key bidegrees: η ∈ π1,1, ν ∈ π3,2, σ ∈ π7,4 — for these J-image elements, the weight equals (topological degree + 1)/2. The motivic Adams spectral sequence for the sphere uses the motivic Steenrod algebra; the motivic Adams–Novikov for MGL uses the motivic Hopf algebroid MGL_*MGL. Examiners often test whether you confuse the two. The motivic image of J is detected by the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence, not the motivic Adams spectral sequence (which detects exotic elements like κ). Know the η-Bockstein spectral sequence: it computes the η-completion of motivic stable stems and splits the motivic EHP sequence.

Motivic Goodwillie Calculus and the Motivic Taylor Tower

Motivic Goodwillie calculus extends the classical Goodwillie calculus of functors from spaces to spaces to the setting of motivic spaces and motivic spectra. Given a functor F: Sm_k → SH(k) from smooth schemes to the stable motivic homotopy category, its motivic Taylor tower is a sequence of functors P_n F with natural transformations F → P_n F → Pn-1 F, where P_n F is the universal n-excisive approximation to F. The layers D_n F = hofib(P_n F → Pn-1 F) are homogeneous functors classified by spectra with Σ_n-action in SH(k). The motivic Goodwillie derivatives ∂_n F are objects in SH(k)hΣ_n, the homotopy fixed points of the symmetric group action. For the motivic suspension spectrum functor, the motivic Taylor tower converges to the motivic sphere spectrum 𝕊, and the layers are the motivic sphere spectra Sn,n with Σ_n-action. For the algebraic K-theory functor K: Sm_k → SH(k), the motivic Taylor tower recovers the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for MGL, and the motivic Goodwillie derivatives are related to the motivic homology of the symmetric groups. The motivic Goodwillie calculus interacts with the motivic slice filtration: the n-th slice s_n F of a motivic spectrum F is related to the n-th motivic Goodwillie derivative ∂_n F via the motivic slice spectral sequence. Over a perfect field, the motivic Goodwillie calculus for the functor Σ^∞_+ Ω^∞ yields a motivic analogue of the Snaith splitting, expressing the motivic sphere spectrum as a wedge of motivic Thom spectra of virtual bundles over the motivic classifying spaces BΣ_n. The motivic Goodwillie calculus also provides a framework for the motivic chromatic convergence theorem: the motivic Taylor tower of the motivic localization functor L_n (localization at the n-th motivic Morava K-theory) converges to the motivic sphere spectrum after K(n)-localization. This is the motivic analogue of the classical chromatic convergence theorem of Hopkins and Ravenel, and its proof uses the motivic slice filtration and the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for the motivic Morava K-theories K(n)mot.

The Motivic Chromatic Convergence Theorem and the Telescope Conjecture

The motivic chromatic convergence theorem states that for a perfect field k, the motivic sphere spectrum 𝕊 is the homotopy limit of its motivic chromatic localizations L_n 𝕊, where L_n is Bousfield localization with respect to the motivic Morava K-theory K(n)mot. The motivic Morava K-theories K(n)mot are motivic spectra constructed via the motivic Landweber exact functor theorem applied to MGL, using the formal group law of height n. They are bigraded analogues of the classical Morava K-theories K(n), with coefficients K(n)mot*,* ≅ 𝔽_p[v_n±1] where |v_n| = (2p^n-2, p^n-1). The motivic chromatic tower is the sequence of localizations 𝕊 → ⋯ → L_n 𝕊 → Ln-1 𝕊 → ⋯ → L0 𝕊 ≅ Hℚ, and the motivic chromatic convergence theorem asserts that the natural map 𝕊 → holim_n L_n 𝕊 is an equivalence in SH(k). The proof uses the motivic slice filtration: the motivic slice spectral sequence for L_n 𝕊 collapses at a finite page, and the motivic slices s_q L_n 𝕊 are computable in terms of motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectra. The motivic telescope conjecture predicts that the motivic chromatic localization L_n is smashing, i.e., L_n X ≅ X ∧ L_n 𝕊 for all X ∈ SH(k), and that the motivic telescope of a v_n-self-map on a motivic finite spectrum is equivalent to L_n. The motivic telescope conjecture is known for n = 0,1 over perfect fields of characteristic not p, where the motivic Morava K-theories K(0)mot ≅ Hℚ and K(1)mot ≅ KUmot/p (the motivic connective K-theory) are well-understood. For n ≥ 2, the motivic telescope conjecture is open and is closely related to the motivic chromatic splitting conjecture, which predicts a decomposition of the K(n)-local motivic sphere into a wedge of motivic suspensions of the motivic Morava E-theory E_nmot. The motivic chromatic convergence theorem has applications to the computation of motivic stable stems: the motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for L_n 𝕊 converges to the K(n)-local motivic stable stems, and the motivic chromatic spectral sequence relates the motivic stable stems to the motivic homotopy groups of the motivic Morava E-theories.

The Motivic Hurewicz Theorem and A¹-Homology Theories

The motivic Hurewicz theorem relates the A¹-homotopy sheaves π_nA¹(X) of a motivic space X to its A¹-homology sheaves H_nA¹(X), which are the Nisnevich sheaves associated to the presheaf U ↦ π_nA¹(X ∧ U_+). For a simply connected motivic space X (in the sense that π0A¹(X) = * and π1A¹(X) = 0 as Nisnevich sheaves), the motivic Hurewicz theorem states that the first non-vanishing A¹-homotopy sheaf π_nA¹(X) is isomorphic to the corresponding A¹-homology sheaf H_nA¹(X). This isomorphism is induced by the motivic Hurewicz map h: π_nA¹(X) → H_nA¹(X), which is a morphism of strictly A¹-invariant sheaves. The A¹-homology sheaves H_nA¹(X) are computable via the motivic Dold–Thom theorem: for a motivic space X, the A¹-homology sheaves are the homotopy sheaves of the motivic symmetric product SP^∞(X) = colim_n SP^n(X), where SP^n(X) is the n-th symmetric product in the motivic homotopy category. The motivic Hurewicz theorem extends to the stable motivic homotopy category: for a motivic spectrum E, the motivic Hurewicz map πp,q(E) → Hp,qA¹(E) relates the motivic stable homotopy sheaves to the motivic homology sheaves with coefficients in the motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum Hℤ. The motivic homology sheaves Hp,qA¹(E) are the homotopy sheaves of E ∧ Hℤ, where Hℤ represents motivic cohomology. The motivic Hurewicz theorem is a key input to the motivic Adams spectral sequence: the E2-page ExtAmot(H*,*(E), H*,*(F)) computes the motivic stable homotopy sheaves π*,*(F^E) when the motivic Hurewicz map is an isomorphism in a range. Over a perfect field, the motivic Hurewicz theorem for the motivic sphere spectrum 𝕊 identifies π0,0(𝕊) ≅ ℤ and π1,1(𝕊) ≅ KMW1, and the motivic Hurewicz map is an isomorphism in these bidegrees. In higher bidegrees, the motivic Hurewicz map has a non-trivial kernel and cokernel, which are detected by the motivic Steenrod algebra action on motivic cohomology. The motivic Hurewicz theorem also holds for the motivic Morava K-theories K(n)mot and the motivic Morava E-theories E_nmot, where it relates the motivic homotopy groups of these spectra to their motivic homology with coefficients in Hℤ/p.

Motivic Homotopy Theory of the Motivic Homotopy Category of Schemes with a Group Action

The equivariant extension of Morel–Voevodsky’s A¹‑homotopy theory replaces the base category of smooth schemes over a field k by the category of smooth G‑schemes for a linear algebraic group G. The resulting equivariant motivic homotopy category SHG(k) is obtained by forming the category of G‑motivic spaces, localizing with respect to the Nisnevich topology on the quotient stacks, and inverting the A¹‑line equipped with a trivial G‑action. A technical point is that the Nisnevich topology on the site of smooth G‑schemes is defined via étale slices that are G‑equivariant; this ensures that homotopy invariant sheaves with transfers become Mackey functors for G. The equivariant motivic sphere spectrum S0,0G carries a genuine G‑action, and its bigraded homotopy groups πp,qG(S) encode both the representation ring RO(G) and the motivic weight. For G = μ2 over a field of characteristic not 2, the equivariant motivic cohomology of a point is closely related to Milnor–Witt K‑theory KMW*(k) and the action of the motivic Steenrod algebra. The equivariant motivic Adams spectral sequence takes the form ExtAmotG(H*,*G(X), H*,*G(Y)) ⇒ π*,*G(YX), where AmotG is the equivariant motivic Steenrod algebra. Computations for G = μ2 over ℝ indicate that the equivariant motivic stable stems contain the classical 2‑complete stable stems as a direct summand, together with exotic classes not present in the non‑equivariant setting. The equivariant motivic slice filtration, constructed using the G‑equivariant motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for MGLG, aims to yield a convergence theorem analogous to the non‑equivariant case but requires the field to be perfect and the group to be linearly reductive to guarantee the existence of G‑equivariant resolutions.

Motivic Homotopy Theory of the Motivic Homotopy Category of Schemes with a Log Structure

Logarithmic motivic homotopy theory, developed by Kato, Nakayama, and later by Binda–Park–Østvær, incorporates a fine saturated log structure on schemes to control degenerations and boundary behaviour. The log Nisnevich topology is generated by log étale morphisms that induce isomorphisms on the associated ghost sheaves; this topology is strictly finer than the ordinary Nisnevich topology and satisfies descent for log smooth morphisms. The log motivic homotopy category SHlog(k) is obtained by localizing the category of log motivic spaces at the log Nisnevich topology and then inverting the log A¹‑line, which is the affine line equipped with the standard log structure given by the divisor at infinity. A fundamental result is the log motivic purity theorem: for a log smooth pair (X, D) with D a strict normal crossings divisor, the log motivic Thom space Thlog(ND/X) is A¹‑weak equivalent to the log motivic suspension of the complement X D. This yields a log motivic Gysin sequence relating the log motivic cohomology of X, D, and the normal bundle. The log motivic Steenrod algebra acts on log motivic cohomology, and its structure is studied via a log motivic analogue of the Milnor basis. Over a perfect field, the log motivic Adams spectral sequence converges to the log motivic stable stems, and the log motivic slice filtration provides a log motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for the log algebraic cobordism spectrum MGLlog. Applications include a log motivic interpretation of the motivic nearby cycles functor and progress toward a log motivic analogue of the Bloch–Kato conjecture for fields with discrete valuation.

Motivic Homotopy Theory of the Motivic Homotopy Category of Schemes with a Group Action and a Log Structure over Non‑Perfect Fields

Extending the previous constructions to non‑perfect base fields requires replacing the Nisnevich topology by the cdh topology, which is generated by proper surjective morphisms and Nisnevich covers. The cdh‑local equivariant log motivic homotopy category SHlog, cdhG(k) is obtained by first localizing at the equivariant log cdh topology and then inverting the equivariant log A¹‑line. The equivariant log cdh motivic Adams spectral sequence converges to the equivariant log cdh motivic stable stems, and the equivariant log cdh motivic slice filtration yields an equivariant log cdh motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for MGLlog, cdhG. Motivic Homotopy Theory of the Motivic Homotopy Category of Schemes with a Group Action and a Log Structure over Finite Fields

Over a finite field 𝔽q, the equivariant log motivic homotopy category SHlogG(𝔽q) exhibits special features due to the weight structure on SH(𝔽q) discovered by Bondarko and Østvær. The equivariant log motivic sphere spectrum S0,0G admits a weight decomposition, and its bigraded homotopy groups πp,qG, log(S) are concentrated in non‑negative weights. The behavior of the equivariant log motivic Adams spectral sequence for the equivariant log motivic Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum HℤG is a subject of active study; in some cases it collapses at the E2‑page, yielding explicit descriptions of the equivariant log motivic stable stems in terms of the equivariant log motivic Steenrod algebra. The equivariant log motivic slice filtration is expected to coincide with the weight filtration, and the equivariant log motivic Adams–Novikov spectral sequence for MGLlogG may degenerate at the E2-page in favorable situations. These results provide computational progress on the equivariant log motivic stable stems for G = μ2 over 𝔽q with q odd, and they inform the equivariant log motivic approach to the Bloch–Kato conjecture over finite fields.
