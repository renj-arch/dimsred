Geophysics — Earth's Interior, Seismology, Plate Tectonics & Geomagnetism

Deep dive into Geophysics — the layered structure of Earth's interior (the crust, mantle, liquid outer core, and solid inner core) determined by seismic velocity discontinuities (Mohorovičić, Gutenberg, Lehmann), the propagation of seismic body waves (P-waves and S-waves) and surface waves (Rayleigh and Love waves), seismic tomography as a CT scan of the Earth, the theory of plate tectonics driven by mantle convection, ridge push, and slab pull, and the geomagnetic field generated by the geodynamo in the liquid outer core including reversals recorded in paleomagnetism.

"The Earth is a dynamic planet — its interior churns, its surface drifts, and its magnetic field shields us from the solar wind."
— Inge Lehmann
Part 1

🌍 Earth's Interior Structure

Structure
Seismic Discontinuities, Compositional Layers, and the Radial Earth Model
Earth's interior is divided into concentric layers separated by seismic discontinuities where the velocity of seismic waves changes abruptly. The crust (0–35 km average, 5–70 km variation) — oceanic crust is ~6 km thick (basaltic composition, vₚ ≈ 6–7 km/s), continental crust averages 35 km (granitic, vₚ ≈ 5.5–6.5 km/s). The Mohorovičić discontinuity (Moho) at 5–70 km depth marks the crust-mantle boundary with a sharp increase in vₚ from ~7 to ~8 km/s. The mantle extends to ~2890 km depth, composed of silicate minerals (olivine, pyroxene, garnet) in the upper mantle transitioning to perovskite and post-perovskite phases in the lower mantle. The transition zone (410–660 km) features two major phase transitions: olivine → wadsleyite at 410 km and ringwoodite → bridgmanite + magnesiowüstite at 660 km. The Gutenberg discontinuity at ~2890 km separates the mantle from the liquid outer core — S-waves do not propagate through the outer core (S-wave shadow zone from 103° to 180° epicentral distance). The Lehmann discontinuity at ~5155 km separates the liquid outer core from the solid inner core (primarily iron-nickel alloy). The PREM (Preliminary Reference Earth Model) provides radial profiles of density, vₚ, vₛ, and attenuation as functions of depth. The inner core is solid with a radius of ~1220 km, temperature ~5500°C, and is slightly anisotropic (P-waves travel faster along the rotation axis).
Earth's layered interior was discovered entirely through seismology — the travel times and reflections of seismic waves from earthquakes reveal the radial structure of our planet like a natural CT scan.
Part 2

📡 Seismology and Seismic Waves

Seismology
Body Waves, Surface Waves, Travel-Time Curves, and Seismic Tomography
Seismology is the study of elastic wave propagation through the Earth. P-waves (primary or compressional waves) are longitudinal: particle motion is parallel to propagation, vₚ = √((K + 4μ/3)/ρ), where K is bulk modulus and μ is shear modulus. S-waves (secondary or shear waves) are transverse: particle motion is perpendicular to propagation, vₛ = √(μ/ρ). Since fluids have μ = 0, S-waves do not travel through liquids. The travel-time curve (time vs. epicentral distance) is the fundamental observational record — the slope of the curve at a given distance gives the apparent velocity. The travel-time equation for a flat-layered Earth: T(Δ) = ∫ 1/v(z) sec θ(z) dx. In spherical coordinates (the Earth), the ray parameter p = r sin θ/v is constant along a ray (Snell's law in spherical geometry): p = dT/dΔ. The Herglotz-Wiechert inversion uses travel-time curves to invert for velocity as a function of depth. Surface waves (Rayleigh waves: elliptical retrograde particle motion; Love waves: horizontally polarized shear waves in a low-velocity layer) are dispersive — their velocity depends on frequency, enabling depth inversion. Seismic tomography uses thousands of ray paths to construct 3D images of velocity anomalies (hot upwellings, cold subducting slabs). The receiver function method isolates conversions between P and S waves at discontinuities beneath a seismometer, mapping crustal and mantle structure.
Seismic waves are the primary tool for imaging Earth's interior — P-wave and S-wave travel times, amplitudes, and waveforms reveal everything from crustal thickness to the geometry of subducting slabs.
Part 3

🗺️ Plate Tectonics

Tectonics
Lithospheric Plates, Driving Forces, and the Wilson Cycle
Plate tectonics describes the motion of Earth's rigid outer shell (the lithosphere, ~100 km thick, comprising crust and uppermost mantle) over the weaker, ductile asthenosphere. The lithosphere is broken into ~15 major plates (Pacific, North American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, Indo-Australian, South American, Nazca, etc.) that move at rates of 1–10 cm/yr. Plate boundaries are of three types: divergent (mid-ocean ridges — new oceanic crust is created by decompression melting of upwelling mantle); convergent (subduction zones or collision zones — one plate descends into the mantle); and transform (plates slide past each other, e.g., the San Andreas Fault). The driving forces: slab pull (the negative buoyancy of cold, dense subducting lithosphere — the dominant force, ~90% of the driving force), ridge push (gravitational sliding of the lithosphere away from elevated mid-ocean ridges), and mantle drag (basal shear from asthenospheric convection). The Wilson cycle describes the opening and closing of ocean basins: continental rifting → seafloor spreading → subduction → continental collision (e.g., the formation of Pangaea and its breakup). The GPS geodesy now directly measures plate motions with millimeter precision. The slab flux (the total length of subduction zones times convergence rate) regulates the rate of mantle convection and the global carbon cycle via subduction of carbonates.
Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of geology — it explains earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building, and the evolution of Earth's surface through the slow convective motion of its rigid outer shell.
Part 4

🧲 Geomagnetism and the Geodynamo

Geomagnetism
The Dipole Field, the Geodynamo, Paleomagnetism, and Reversals
Earth's magnetic field is approximately a magnetic dipole tilted ~11° from the rotation axis, with a surface intensity of ~25–65 μT. The field originates in the liquid outer core (iron-nickel alloy) via the geodynamo: a self-sustaining dynamo driven by thermal and compositional convection in the electrically conducting fluid. The induction equation: ∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B) + η∇²B, where η = 1/μ₀σ is the magnetic diffusivity. The magnetic Reynolds number Rₘ = UL/η must exceed a critical value (~10–50) for dynamo action. In the core, Rₘ ~ 1000, ensuring self-excitation. The key force balance in the core is the mac-oscillation (Magnetic, Archimedes, Coriolis) force balance. The geomagnetic secular variation: the field changes on timescales of years to centuries (the westward drift of the non-dipole field at ~0.2°/yr). Paleomagnetism records the ancient magnetic field in rocks: when igneous rocks cool below the Curie temperature (~580°C for magnetite), they acquire a thermoremanent magnetization (TRM) aligned with the ambient field. Magnetic reversals (polarity flips) occur irregularly with intervals of 0.1–50 Myr (average ~450 kyr in the last 20 Myr). The last reversal (Brunhes-Matuyama) was 780,000 years ago. The seafloor magnetic anomalies record a symmetrical pattern of normal and reversed stripes at mid-ocean ridges — the Vine-Matthews-Morley hypothesis confirmed seafloor spreading. The paleointensity of the field can be estimated from the Thellier method. The magnetosphere deflects the solar wind, protecting Earth's atmosphere from erosion.
The geomagnetic field is generated by convective motions in the liquid core acting as a natural dynamo; paleomagnetic records of reversals provide the definitive proof of plate tectonics and seafloor spreading.

Advanced Seismology: The Anelastic Earth & Inversion Nuances

The standard model treats Earth as an elastic solid, but for competitive exams, you must master the concept of anelasticity. Real rocks dissipate energy. This attenuation is quantified by the quality factor Q. The relationship between amplitude decay and distance involves the geometric spreading (1/r) and the intrinsic attenuation term e-πfΔ/Q(f). A critical distinction often tested is the difference between body waves (P, S) which travel through volume, and surface waves (Rayleigh, Love) which are trapped near the surface. While body waves reflect off discontinuities (Moho, Core-Mantle Boundary), surface waves are dispersive; their phase velocity depends on frequency because they sample different depths. High-frequency components penetrate shallow crust, while low-frequency components probe deep mantle structure.

Landmark Case: The Gutenberg Discontinuity

Technique: Analyzing the S-wave Shadow Zone
Question: Explain why S-waves are not detected between 103° and 142° from an earthquake epicenter, while P-waves are. How does this observation constrain the physical state of Earth's core?
Solution: S-waves cannot propagate through fluids because they require a restoring shear force (shear modulus μ = 0 in fluids). The absence of direct S-waves in the angular sector from 103° to 180° implies the existence of a liquid outer core. However, P-waves are detected in this zone due to refraction at the Core-Mantle Boundary (CMB) and transmission through the liquid outer core. The sharp drop in P-wave velocity at the CMB confirms the phase change from solid mantle to liquid outer core. Furthermore, the lack of S-waves traveling *through* the core proves the outer core is liquid. This is the foundational evidence for the Earth's layered structure.

Advanced Concept: Seismic Tomography & Velocity Anomalies

Modern seismology moves beyond 1D radial models to 3D tomography. We interpret velocity anomalies relative to a reference model (like PREM). High velocities generally indicate cold, dense material (e.g., subducting slabs in the mantle transition zone or lower mantle). Low velocities indicate hot, buoyant material (e.g., Large Low Shear Velocity Provinces - LLSVPs beneath Africa and the Pacific). A common exam twist asks you to identify a "low-velocity anomaly" at 660 km depth as a potential slab stagnation zone where subducted slabs pile up before sinking into the lower mantle.

The Birch's Law Connection

For high-level questions, recall that seismic velocity correlates with density. Birch's Law states that P-wave velocity increases linearly with density in the mantle. However, this breaks down at phase transitions (like the 660 km discontinuity) where mineralogical changes cause sudden jumps in velocity independent of thermal effects.

Geomagnetism: The Non-Dipole Field & Reversal Dynamics

The textbook description of Earth's magnetic field as a simple dipole is an approximation. In reality, the field is dominated by the axial dipole (about 80-90% of the total energy), but the non-dipole component is crucial for understanding local variations and the Westward Drift.

Technique: Paleomagnetism & The Vine-Matthews-Morley Hypothesis
Question: Describe how a symmetrical pattern of magnetic stripes on the ocean floor provides proof for seafloor spreading. What is the significance of the "reversal rate" changing over geological time?
Solution: As new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges, it cools and records the ambient magnetic field direction (Thellier-Thellier method). When the Earth's field reverses polarity, subsequent crust records the opposite polarity. This creates a "barcode" of alternating normal and reversed magnetization parallel to the ridge axis. The symmetry of these stripes on either side of the ridge confirms that the seafloor is spreading symmetrically away from the center. The reversal rate (average ~300 kyr in recent history, but highly variable) indicates the stochastic nature of the geodynamo. A common exam trap is assuming reversals are periodic; they are not, making paleomagnetic dating rely on correlation with global chrons rather than simple counting.

The Geodynamo Equation Balance

You must understand the force balance in the core. The magnetic field is maintained against ohmic dissipation (diffusion) by the induction term v × B. The key dimensionless number is the Magnetic Reynolds Number, Rm. If Rm < 1, diffusion dominates and the field decays. In Earth's core, Rm ~ 1000, ensuring self-sustaining dynamo action. The Coriolis force (due to Earth's rotation) organizes the flow into columnar structures (Taylor columns), which is why the magnetic field is predominantly axial and dipolar.

Exceptions & Anomalies

The South Atlantic Anomaly (SAA) is a region where the inner Van Allen radiation belt dips closer to Earth's surface. This is caused by a localized weakness in the non-dipole component of the geomagnetic field, specifically a negative flux concentration in the core-mantle boundary beneath Africa/South America. This serves as a real-world example that the geodynamo is not perfectly symmetric.

Plate Tectonics: Driving Forces & The Wilson Cycle Mechanics

While you know plates move, competitive exams require mastery of the quantitative driving forces. Slab pull is the dominant force (~90%), driven by the negative buoyancy of cold subducting lithosphere relative to the hot asthenosphere. Ridge push contributes significantly at divergent boundaries but is secondary. Mantle drag (basal shear) is controversial in magnitude but essential for explaining plate motion directions that are not strictly perpendicular to ridges.

Technique: Calculating Slab Pull vs Ridge Push
Question: If a subducting slab has a density contrast of Δρ = -200 kg/m³ relative to the mantle, and the subduction zone length is L = 1000 km, estimate the order of magnitude of the slab pull force per unit width. How does this compare to ridge push?
Solution: Slab pull force F ≈ g × Δρ × Volume. For a unit width (1m), Volume = L × thickness. Assuming an effective subducting thickness of ~70 km, F ≈ 9.8 × (-200) × (106 m × 70,000 m). Note: The negative density contrast implies downward force (buoyancy assists sinking). The magnitude is enormous (~1014 N/m width). Ridge push relies on the elevation of the ridge crest and gravity sliding; it is generally estimated to be an order of magnitude weaker than slab pull in subduction zones. This explains why plates with active subduction (like the Pacific Plate) move faster than passive plates.

The Wilson Cycle: Opening and Closing

The Wilson cycle describes the lifecycle of an ocean basin. It begins with continental rifting (e.g., East African Rift), followed by seafloor spreading creating a new ocean (Atlantic opening). Eventually, subduction zones develop, consuming the ocean floor (Pacific closing). The final stage is continental collision (Himalayas) and the closure of the basin. A critical exam point is that continents never subduct; they are too buoyant. Only oceanic lithosphere subducts.

Mantle Plumes & Hotspots

Hotspots (like Hawaii or Yellowstone) are often attributed to mantle plumes rising from the core-mantle boundary. However, not all hotspots are plumes; some may be caused by shallow asthenospheric upwellings beneath thin lithosphere. The movement of a hotspot track relative to a stationary plate (e.g., the Hawaiian-Emperor seamount chain) allows calculation of past plate velocities and changes in direction (bends in the track).

Exam-Day Strategy: Handling Ambiguity
When a question asks about "Earth's Interior," always default to the Preliminary Reference Earth Model (PREM) layers unless specified otherwise. If asked about magnetic field generation, remember the core must be liquid and electrically conducting. If asked about plate motion, prioritize slab pull as the mechanism. Always check units: seismic velocities are in km/s, plate speeds in cm/yr, and magnetic field in microTesla (μT). Never confuse S-wave shadow zones with P-wave shadow zones; the latter is smaller due to refraction.
  • Trap 1: Assuming the Earth's core is entirely liquid. The inner core is solid due to pressure, despite high temperature.
  • Trap 2: Believing S-waves travel through the outer core. They do not; this creates the large shadow zone.
  • Trap 3: Thinking magnetic reversals are periodic like clock ticks. They are stochastic and irregular (chaotic).
  • Trap 4: Confusing the lithosphere (rigid, brittle) with the asthenosphere (ductile, plastic). The boundary is not a sharp chemical line but a rheological transition.
  • Trap 5: Ignoring the effect of attenuation (Q-factor) on amplitude calculations. Amplitude decays faster than just geometric spreading predicts due to internal friction.
