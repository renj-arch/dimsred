Nuclear Physics — Radioactivity, Nuclear Reactions, Fission & Fusion

Deep dive into Nuclear Physics — the structure of the atomic nucleus (protons and neutrons held together by the strong nuclear force), binding energy and the semi-empirical mass formula (the liquid drop model: B = a_V A − a_S A^{2/3} − a_C Z(Z−1)/A^{1/3} − a_A (N−Z)²/A + δ(A)), radioactivity and the three main decay modes (alpha decay: emission of ⁴He nucleus; beta decay: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄_e; gamma decay: de-excitation of excited nuclear states), the radioactive decay law N(t) = N₀ e^{−λt} and half-life t_{1/2} = ln 2/λ, nuclear reactions and cross sections (the probability of interaction, measured in barns), nuclear fission (the splitting of heavy nuclei, chain reactions, critical mass, nuclear reactors and the Chernobyl and Fukushima incidents), nuclear fusion (the combining of light nuclei, the proton-proton chain and CNO cycle in stars, the Tokamak and ITER), and models of nuclear structure: the liquid drop model and the nuclear shell model (magic numbers and spin-orbit coupling).

Part 1

☢️ Nuclear Structure and the Semi-Empirical Mass Formula

Binding Energy
Nuclear Forces, Binding Energy, Liquid Drop Model, and Mass Defect
The atomic nucleus consists of Z protons and N neutrons (collectively nucleons) bound by the strong nuclear force — a short-range (~1.4 fm) force that overwhelms the Coulomb repulsion between protons. The mass defect: the mass of a nucleus M(A, Z) is less than the sum of its constituent nucleons: Δm = Z m_p + N m_n − M(A, Z). The binding energy B = Δm c² is the energy required to disassemble the nucleus. The semi-empirical mass formula (Bethe-Weizsäcker formula) models the binding energy: B = a_V A − a_S A^{2/3} − a_C Z(Z−1)/A^{1/3} − a_A (N−Z)²/A + δ(A). The volume term a_V A ≈ 15.75 MeV per nucleon (each nucleon binds with its neighbors). The surface term −a_S A^{2/3} ≈ −17.8 MeV A^{2/3} corrects for nucleons at the surface with fewer neighbors. The Coulomb term −a_C Z(Z−1)/A^{1/3} ≈ −0.711 MeV Z(Z−1)/A^{1/3} accounts for proton-proton repulsion. The asymmetry term −a_A (N−Z)²/A ≈ −23.7 MeV (N−Z)²/A favors equal numbers of protons and neutrons. The pairing term δ(A) is +11.18 MeV/A^{1/2} for even-even, 0 for even-odd, −11.18 MeV/A^{1/2} for odd-odd nuclei. The valley of stability on the N-Z plot follows the minimum energy at Z ≈ A/(2 + a_C A^{2/3}/(2a_A)). Magic numbers (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) indicate particularly stable nuclei and are explained by the nuclear shell model with strong spin-orbit coupling (the Mayer-Jensen model).
The semi-empirical mass formula explains binding energy trends across the nuclear chart, balancing volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing effects.
Part 2

⏳ Radioactivity and Decay Modes

Decay
Alpha Decay, Beta Decay, Gamma Decay, and the Radioactive Decay Law
Alpha decay: emission of an α-particle (⁴He nucleus). The process is A_Z X → A−4_{Z−2} Y + ⁴₂He. The decay energy Q_α = (M_parent − M_daughter − M_α)c². Geiger-Nuttall law: log t_{1/2} ∝ 1/√Q_α — alpha decay is governed by quantum tunneling through the Coulomb barrier (Gamow's theory). Beta decay: weak interaction process. Three types: β⁻ decay (n → p + e⁻ + ν̄_e), β⁺ decay (p → n + e⁺ + ν_e), and electron capture (e⁻ + p → n + ν_e). The energy release is shared between the electron/positron and the neutrino, producing a continuous energy spectrum. The endpoint energy E_max = Q_β. Fermi's theory of beta decay: the transition rate λ = (G_F² |M|²)/(2π³ℏ⁷) ∫ p_e² (Q_β − T_e)² F(Z, p_e) dp_e, where G_F is the Fermi coupling constant. Gamma decay: de-excitation of an excited nuclear state via photon emission. Internal conversion: the nucleus transfers energy directly to an atomic electron, ejecting it. The radioactive decay law: dN/dt = −λN, giving N(t) = N₀ e^{−λt}. Half-life: t_{1/2} = ln 2/λ = 0.693/λ. Mean lifetime: τ = 1/λ. Activity: A = λN = A₀ e^{−λt}, measured in becquerels (Bq, decays/s) or curies (Ci = 3.7×10¹⁰ Bq). Decay chains: successive decays of radioactive series (uranium, thorium, actinium). Secular equilibrium: when parent half-life ≫ daughter half-life, the activities equalize.
Radioactive decay follows exponential decay law; alpha decay is quantum tunneling, beta decay is mediated by the weak interaction, and gamma decay releases nuclear excitation energy.
Part 3

⚡ Nuclear Fission and Chain Reactions

Fission
Induced Fission, Chain Reactions, Critical Mass, and Nuclear Reactors
Nuclear fission was discovered by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann in 1938 (interpreted by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch). In induced fission, a heavy nucleus (e.g., ²³⁵U) absorbs a thermal neutron, forming a compound nucleus in an excited state that splits into two fission fragments and releases 2–3 neutrons and ~200 MeV of energy. A typical fission: n + ²³⁵U → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n + energy. The energy release comes from the binding energy difference: the fission fragments are more tightly bound than the original nucleus. Chain reaction: the emitted neutrons can induce further fissions. The multiplication factor k = (# of neutrons in one generation)/(# of neutrons in the previous). k < 1: subcritical (chain dies out). k = 1: critical (sustained chain reaction). k > 1: supercritical (runaway). Critical mass: the minimum mass of fissile material needed to achieve criticality (~52 kg for pure ²³⁵U sphere, ~16 kg for ²³⁹Pu). Nuclear reactors: controlled fission with control rods (boron or cadmium, absorb neutrons) and moderator (water, graphite, heavy water) to thermalize neutrons. Types: PWR (pressurized water reactor), BWR (boiling water reactor), CANDU (heavy water), RBMK (graphite-moderated, Chernobyl type). Breeder reactors: convert fertile ²³⁸U into fissile ²³⁹Pu. Nuclear waste: fission products (³⁰Sr, ¹³⁷Cs with 30-year half-lives) and transuranic elements (²³⁹Pu, half-life 24,000 years). Nuclear accidents: the criticality of reactor design — Chernobyl (1986, RBMK design flaw), Fukushima Daiichi (2011, cooling failure after tsunami).
Nuclear fission releases energy from the Coulomb repulsion of heavy nuclei; chain reactions enable both reactors and weapons, controlled by neutron moderation and absorption.

☀️ Nuclear Fusion and Stellar Nucleosynthesis

Fusion
Thermonuclear Fusion, the Proton-Proton Chain, the CNO Cycle, and Tokamaks
Nuclear fusion combines light nuclei into heavier ones, releasing energy due to the binding energy curve (maximum at ⁵⁶Fe). The most accessible reaction: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV (the D-T reaction). The Coulomb barrier must be overcome by quantum tunneling at temperatures of ~10⁸ K. The fusion cross section σ(E) ∝ (1/E) exp(−√(E_G/E)) where E_G is the Gamow energy. The Gamow peak is the energy window where the product of the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution and the tunneling probability is maximized. In stars, hydrogen burning proceeds via two main sequences. The proton-proton (pp) chain (dominant in Sun-like stars): p + p → ²H + e⁺ + ν_e (Q = 1.44 MeV), followed by ²H + p → ³He + γ, then ³He + ³He → ⁴He + 2p (net: 4p → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν_e + 26.73 MeV). The CNO cycle (dominant in heavier stars >1.3 M_⊙): ¹²C acts as a catalyst, capturing protons in sequence to produce ⁴He while regenerating ¹²C. Helium burning: the triple-alpha process (3 ⁴He → ¹²C + γ, Q = 7.27 MeV) at T ~ 10⁸ K, followed by ¹²C + α → ¹⁶O. Stellar nucleosynthesis builds elements up to iron via successive fusion stages (C, Ne, O, Si burning) in massive stars. Beyond iron, the s-process and r-process (neutron capture) in supernovae produce heavier elements. Controlled fusion on Earth: the Tokamak (magnetic confinement, ITER in France aims for Q = 10), and inertial confinement fusion (NIF at Lawrence Livermore). The Lawson criterion: n τ T > 5 × 10²¹ m⁻³·s·keV for D-T ignition.
Nuclear fusion powers stars and offers the promise of clean energy; the competing processes of the pp-chain and CNO cycle dominate depending on stellar mass.
