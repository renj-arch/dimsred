Mathematical Methods in Physics — Special Functions, Fourier Transforms, Tensors & Group Theory

Deep dive into the mathematical methods essential for physics — special functions of mathematical physics (Bessel, Legendre, Hermite, Laguerre, Airy, hypergeometric and their differential equations, orthogonality, generating functions, recurrence relations), Fourier transforms and distributions (the Dirac delta, tempered distributions, convolution, the Paley-Wiener theorem, the sampling theorem), tensor algebra and calculus (covariant and contravariant vectors, the metric tensor, the Levi-Civita symbol, covariant differentiation, the Riemann curvature tensor, the Einstein field equations), group theory and representation theory (Lie groups SO(n), SU(n), Sp(n), the classification of compact Lie groups, Young tableaux, characters, the Peter-Weyl theorem, induced representations), and Green's functions for boundary value problems (the method of images, eigenfunction expansions, the Sturm-Liouville theory, the Helmholtz equation, retarded and advanced Green's functions in electrodynamics and quantum field theory).

Part 1

📐 Special Functions

Orthogonal Polynomials
Bessel Functions, Legendre Polynomials, Hermite and Laguerre Functions
Bessel's equation: x² y″ + x y′ + (x² − ν²) y = 0. Solutions are Bessel functions of the first kind J_ν(x) = Σ_{k=0}^∞ (−1)^k (x/2)^{ν+2k} / (k! Γ(ν+k+1)). J_ν satisfies the recurrence: J_{ν−1}(x) + J_{ν+1}(x) = (2ν/x) J_ν(x), and J_{ν−1}(x) − J_{ν+1}(x) = 2 J_ν′(x). The spherical Bessel functions j_n(x) = √(π/2x) J_{n+½}(x). The Legendre differential equation: (1−x²) y″ − 2x y′ + l(l+1) y = 0. The Legendre polynomials P_l(x) = (1/2^l l!) d^l/dx^l (x²−1)^l (Rodrigues formula). They satisfy orthogonality: ∫_{-1}^1 P_l(x) P_m(x) dx = 2δ_{lm}/(2l+1). The associated Legendre functions P_l^m(x) = (1−x²)^{m/2} d^m/dx^m P_l(x) for |m| ≤ l. Hermite's equation: y″ − 2x y′ + 2n y = 0. The Hermite polynomials H_n(x) = (−1)^n e^{x²} d^n/dx^n e^{−x²} (Rodrigues). Orthogonality: ∫_{−∞}^∞ H_n(x) H_m(x) e^{−x²} dx = 2^n n! √π δ_{nm}. They appear in the quantum harmonic oscillator wavefunctions ψ_n(x) = (1/√{2^n n!}) (mω/πħ)^{1/4} H_n(√{mω/ħ} x) e^{−mω x²/(2ħ)}. Laguerre's equation: x y″ + (1−x) y′ + n y = 0. The Laguerre polynomials L_n(x) = e^x d^n/dx^n (x^n e^{−x}) / n!. The associated Laguerre polynomials L_n^k(x) = (−1)^k d^k/dx^k L_{n+k}(x) appear in the radial wavefunctions of the hydrogen atom. The Airy equation: y″ − xy = 0, with solutions Ai(x) and Bi(x). Ai(x) ∼ exp(−⅔ x^{3/2}) / (2√π x^{1/4}) as x → +∞, and Ai(x) ∼ sin(⅔ |x|^{3/2} + π/4) / √π |x|^{1/4} as x → −∞. The hypergeometric function ₂F₁(a,b;c;z) = Σ_{k=0}^∞ (a)_k(b)_k z^k / (k! (c)_k) generalizes many special functions. The confluent hypergeometric function ₁F₁(a;c;z) is the limit of ₂F₁ as b → ∞.
Special functions arise as solutions to the differential equations that separate in various coordinate systems; their orthogonality and recurrence relations underpin spectral methods in quantum mechanics and classical field theory.
Part 2

🔁 Fourier Transforms and Distributions

Analysis
The Fourier Transform, the Dirac Delta, Convolution, and Tempered Distributions
The Fourier transform on L¹(ℝ^n): f̂(k) = (2π)^{-n/2} ∫_{ℝ^n} f(x) e^{-ik·x} d^n x. The inversion theorem: f(x) = (2π)^{-n/2} ∫_{ℝ^n} f̂(k) e^{ik·x} d^n k. The Plancherel theorem: ∫ |f|² = ∫ |f̂|² (the transform is unitary on L²). The convolution theorem: (f * g)̂ = (2π)^{n/2} f̂ ĝ. The Dirac delta δ(x) is a distribution (generalized function) satisfying ∫ δ(x) f(x) dx = f(0) for test functions f. The delta has Fourier representation δ(x) = (2π)^{-n} ∫ e^{ik·x} d^n k. The tempered distributions S′(ℝ^n) (the dual of the Schwartz space S) are the natural setting: every tempered distribution has a Fourier transform. The derivative of a distribution: ⟨∂^α T, φ⟩ = (−1)^{|α|} ⟨T, ∂^α φ⟩. The Heaviside step function H(x) has distributional derivative δ(x). The Poisson summation formula: Σ_{n∈ℤ} f(n) = Σ_{k∈ℤ} f̂(k). The Paley-Wiener theorem: a compactly supported smooth function has an entire analytic Fourier transform of exponential type. The Shannon-Nyquist sampling theorem: a bandlimited function (f̂(k) = 0 for |k| > K) is completely determined by its values at points spaced Δx = π/K (the Nyquist rate). The uncertainty principle: Δx Δk ≥ ½, where (Δx)² = ∫ (x−x₀)² |f|² / ∫ |f|². The fractional Fourier transform generalizes the transform to arbitrary angles in phase space, with applications in optics and quantum mechanics. The wavelet transform uses scaled and translated mother wavelets for time-frequency localization, with the continuous wavelet transform W_f(a,b) = (1/√a) ∫ f(t) ψ((t−b)/a) dt.
The Fourier transform diagonalizes translation-invariant linear operators; tempered distributions rigorously extend the theory to include the Dirac delta and its derivatives as needed in physics.
Part 3

🧮 Tensor Calculus and Group Theory

Symmetry
Covariant Derivatives, the Riemann Tensor, Lie Groups, and Young Tableaux
A tensor of type (r,s) is a multilinear map T: (V^*)^r × V^s → ℝ that transforms under basis change with r copies of the Jacobian (contravariant) and s copies of its inverse (covariant). The metric tensor g_{μν} defines the line element ds² = g_{μν} dx^μ dx^ν. The Christoffel symbols Γ^ρ_{μν} = ½ g^{ρσ} (∂_μ g_{νσ} + ∂_ν g_{μσ} − ∂_σ g_{μν}). The covariant derivative ∇_μ V^ν = ∂_μ V^ν + Γ^ν_{μλ} V^λ; for tensors, ∇_μ T^{ν…}_{λ…} = ∂_μ T^{ν…}_{λ…} + Γ^ν_{μρ} T^{ρ…}_{λ…} + … − Γ^ρ_{μλ} T^{ν…}_{ρ…} − …. The Riemann curvature tensor R^ρ_{σμν} = ∂_μ Γ^ρ_{νσ} − ∂_ν Γ^ρ_{μσ} + Γ^ρ_{μλ} Γ^λ_{νσ} − Γ^ρ_{νλ} Γ^λ_{μσ}. The Ricci tensor R_{μν} = R^λ_{μλν}. The Einstein tensor G_{μν} = R_{μν} − ½ g_{μν} R satisfies ∇^μ G_{μν} = 0 (the Bianchi identity). Group theory: a Lie group G is a group that is also a smooth manifold. The Lie algebra g = T_e G with bracket [X,Y] = ad_X Y. For SU(2), the generators are J_i = σ_i/2 (Pauli matrices) with [J_i,J_j] = i ε_{ijk} J_k. The representation theory of SU(2): irreducible representations are labeled by spin j = 0, ½, 1, 3/2, … with dimension 2j+1. The Clebsch-Gordan series: j₁ ⊗ j₂ = |j₁−j₂| ⊕ … ⊕ (j₁+j₂). Young tableaux for SU(n): a partition λ = (λ₁,…,λ_k) with λ₁ ≥ … ≥ λ_k labels an irreducible representation; the dimension is given by the hook-length formula. The Peter-Weyl theorem: the matrix elements of irreducible representations form an orthonormal basis for L²(G). The Schur's lemma: any intertwining operator between irreducible representations is either zero or a scalar multiple of the identity.
Tensor calculus provides the language for general relativity and differential geometry; group theory reveals the symmetry structure underlying quantum mechanics, particle physics, and condensed matter.
Part 4

🌊 Green's Functions and Boundary Value Problems

Boundary Value Problems
Green's Functions, Sturm-Liouville Theory, Eigenfunction Expansions, and the Method of Images
A Green's function G(x,x′) for a linear differential operator L satisfies L G(x,x′) = δ(x−x′) with homogeneous boundary conditions. The solution to L u = f is then u(x) = ∫ G(x,x′) f(x′) dx′. For a Sturm-Liouville operator L = −(d/dx)[p(x) d/dx] + q(x) on [a,b] with boundary conditions, the Green's function can be constructed by the method of variation of parameters. The Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem: L y_n = λ_n w(x) y_n, where y_n are orthogonal with weight w and form a complete basis. The eigenfunction expansion: G(x,x′) = Σ_n y_n(x) y_n(x′)/(λ_n (x′)) — note the complex conjugate for non-self-adjoint operators. The method of images constructs Green's functions by placing image sources outside the domain to satisfy boundary conditions. For the Laplace equation in a half-space ℝ³₊: G(x,x′) = 1/(4π|x−x′|) − 1/(4π|x−x′_I|) where x′_I is the image. The Helmholtz equation (∇² + k²) G = δ has outgoing wave Green's function G⁺(x) = −e^{ikr}/(4πr) in 3D, and G⁺(x) = −(i/4) H₀^{(1)}(kr) in 2D. The retarded Green's function in electrodynamics: G_ret(x−x′) = δ(t−t′−|x−x′|/c)/(4π|x−x′|) for t > t′, zero otherwise — it propagates causality forward in time. The advanced Green's function propagates backward. In quantum field theory, the Feynman propagator ∆_F(x−y) = ∫ d⁴p/(2π)⁴ i e^{-ip·(x−y)}/(p²−m²+iε) is a Green's function of the Klein-Gordon operator (□ + m²)∆_F = −δ⁴(x−y). The Kramers-Kronig relations relate the real and imaginary parts of the response function (the Green's function in frequency space) through causality. The Matsubara formalism uses Green's functions at finite temperature with imaginary time τ = it, where the thermal Green's function G(τ) is periodic/antiperiodic for bosons/fermions with period β = 1/(k_B T).
Green's functions are the fundamental solution operators that invert linear differential equations; they encode causality through their support properties and form the backbone of perturbative quantum field theory.
