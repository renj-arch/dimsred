Microlocal Analysis — Pseudodifferential Operators, Wavefront Sets & the Fourier Integral

Microlocal analysis studies linear partial differential equations at the level of "directions" in phase space — the cotangent bundle T*X of a manifold. By refining the concept of singularities from points in space to points in phase space, it reveals the fine structure of solutions to PDEs. Central objects include pseudodifferential operators (ΨDOs), which generalize differential operators with symbol calculus; the wavefront set of a distribution, which captures both the location and the direction of singularities; and Fourier integral operators (FIOs), which move singularities along canonical transformations. Landmark results include the Duistermaat-Hörmander propagation of singularities theorem and the Melrose b-calculus for manifolds with boundary, culminating in the Atiyah-Patodi-Singer index theorem.

"To understand a singularity, we must look in all directions."
— Lars Hörmander
Part 1

📏 Pseudodifferential Operators and Symbols

ΨDOs
Symbol Classes, Quantization, and the Calculus of Pseudodifferential Operators
A pseudodifferential operator a(x,D) acts on functions by the oscillatory integral a(x,D)f(x) = (2π)^{-n} ∫ e^{i⟨x,ξ⟩} a(x,ξ) ̂f(ξ) dξ, where a(x,ξ) is the symbol. The Hörmander symbol classes S^m_{ρ,δ} consist of a ∈ C^∞(Ω×ℝⁿ) satisfying |∂^β_x ∂^α_ξ a(x,ξ)| ≤ C_{αβ} (1+|ξ|)^{m−ρ|α|+δ|β|} for all multi-indices α,β. Here 0 ≤ δ < ρ ≤ 1, and m is the order. The principal symbol σ_m(a)(x,ξ) = lim_{t→∞} t^{-m} a(x,tξ) captures the leading-order homogenous behavior. The composition formula: the symbol of a(x,D)∘b(x,D) is given by the Leibniz product a♯b ∼ Σ_α (∂^α_ξ a)(D^α_x b)/α! (the Kohn-Nirenberg product). An operator is elliptic of order m if its principal symbol satisfies |a(x,ξ)| ≥ c|ξ|^m for large ξ. An elliptic operator admits a parametrix — a two-sided inverse modulo smoothing operators (those with symbols in S^{-∞}). This is the foundation of elliptic regularity: if P is elliptic and Pu is smooth, then u is smooth.
Pseudodifferential operators extend differential operators by allowing symbols that are not polynomials in ξ; their symbol calculus mirrors the algebra of differential operators with powerful asymptotic control.
Part 2

🌍 Wavefront Sets and Microlocal Regularity

Wavefront
The Wavefront Set, Microlocal Smoothness, and Propagation of Singularities
The wavefront set WF(u) ⊂ T^*X\0 of a distribution u refines the singular support: (x₀,ξ₀) ∉ WF(u) iff there exists a cutoff φ ∈ C^∞_c with φ(x₀)≠0 and a conic neighborhood Γ of ξ₀ such that |(φu)^(ξ)| ≤ C_N(1+|ξ|)^{-N} for all N in Γ. In other words, u is microlocally smooth at (x₀,ξ₀) if the Fourier transform decays rapidly in a cone of directions. The microlocal smoothing property: if WF(u) ∩ ell(a) = ∅ (where ell(a) = {(x,ξ) : σ(a) ≠ 0} is the elliptic set), then a(x,D)u is smooth. The propagation of singularities theorem (Duistermaat-Hörmander, 1972): let P be a real principal type operator with principal symbol p (real, homogeneous of degree m) and characteristic set Σ = {p = 0}. For Pu = f, we have WF(u)\WF(f) ⊂ Σ, and moreover WF(u) is a union of bicharacteristic strips — the integral curves of the Hamilton vector field H_p = Σ (∂p/∂ξ_i ∂/∂x_i − ∂p/∂x_i ∂/∂ξ_i) on T^*X\0. This means singularities travel along null bicharacteristics of P. The microlocal Holmgren uniqueness theorem says that if Pu = 0 and u vanishes on one side of a non-characteristic hypersurface, then u vanishes microlocally near the hypersurface.
The wavefront set records both where singularities occur and in which directions they oscillate; the propagation theorem shows that singularities flow along Hamiltonian trajectories in phase space.
Part 3

🧮 Fourier Integral Operators

FIOs
Canonical Relations, the Hörmander-Egorov Theorem, and Parametrices
A Fourier integral operator (FIO) A: C^∞(Y) → D'(X) of order m with canonical relation Λ ⊂ T^*X × T^*Y is locally given by A f(x) = ∫ e^{iφ(x,y,θ)} a(x,y,θ) f(y) dy dθ, where φ is a non-degenerate phase function (homogeneous of degree 1 in θ and with dφ non-vanishing on the critical set C_φ = {(x,y,θ): d_θ φ = 0}). The canonical relation Λ = {(x,d_x φ; y,−d_y φ) : (x,y,θ) ∈ C_φ}. The Hörmander-Egorov theorem: if F is an FIO whose canonical relation is the graph of a canonical transformation χ: T^*Y → T^*X, then F^* P F is a ΨDO on Y with principal symbol p∘χ. This conjugates pseudodifferential operators by canonical transformations, a cornerstone of microlocal reduction. The composition of FIOs corresponds to composition of canonical relations under a clean intersection condition. The solution operator to the wave equation ∂²_t u − Δ u = 0 is a FIO: the fundamental solution E(t) has WF(E(t)) = {(x,ξ; y,−η)} where (x,ξ) and (y,η) are related by geodesic flow. The L²-boundedness of FIOs holds under the graph condition: the canonical relation is a local canonical graph.
Fourier integral operators generalize ΨDOs by allowing phase functions beyond linear ones; they transport singularities along canonical transformations, making them the natural tool for solving hyperbolic equations.
Part 4

🔥 b-Calculus and Index Theory

b-Calculus
Melrose b-Calculus, the APS Index Theorem, and Analytic Torsion
The Melrose b-calculus provides a pseudodifferential framework for manifolds with boundary. A b-vector field on a manifold X with boundary ∂X is a vector field tangent to the boundary. The b-cotangent bundle ^bT^*X is the dual of the b-tangent bundle, where boundary-defining functions x introduce a degeneracy. The b-pseudodifferential operators form a calculus closed under composition, with a b-wavefront set that respects the boundary structure. The b-index theorem: for Dirac-type operators on manifolds with boundary, the index is given by the Atiyah-Patodi-Singer (APS) index theorem: ind(D) = ∫_M ̂A(Ω) + η(∂M), where η(∂M) is the eta invariant — a spectral invariant measuring the asymmetry of the spectrum of the boundary operator. The adiabatic limit relates the APS index to the b-calculus through stretching the boundary. The Cheeger-Müller theorem equates the analytic torsion (Ray-Singer torsion) with the Reidemeister torsion, a topological invariant. The Witten deformation — deforming the de Rham complex by d_t = e^{-tf} d e^{tf} for a Morse function f — provides analytic proofs of Morse inequalities via supersymmetric quantum mechanics, with instantons connecting critical points. Fukaya's Floer homology has deep connections to microlocal analysis through the study of Lagrangian intersections and the Morse theory of the action functional.
The b-calculus tames boundary singularities by adapting the pseudodifferential machinery to manifolds with boundary, leading to index theorems and connections to topological invariants.

Microlocal Obstructions, Shadowing, and the Caustic Catastrophe

The fundamental solution to the wave equation propagates singularities along null bicharacteristics, but this description breaks down at caustics. A caustic is a point where the canonical relation fails to be a local graph of a function; instead, it becomes a multi-valued map or folds over itself. When a ray hits a caustic, the standard Fourier integral operator representation using a single phase function φ(x,y,θ) ceases to be valid because the stationary phase approximation requires non-degenerate critical points (d²φ ≠ 0). At a fold catastrophe, two rays merge, and the amplitude of the wavefront undergoes a transition from oscillatory behavior to an Airy-type decay. This phenomenon is described by the Maslov index, which counts the number of times the Lagrangian submanifold intersects the zero section in the cotangent bundle. In the context of exam questions, one must recognize that while ΨDOs preserve Sobolev spaces globally, FIOs are only valid away from caustics. The remedy involves using multiple patches or "canonical charts" covering different regions of phase space, stitched together by transition functions that account for the Maslov correction factor of i^(1/2) per half-turn.

Another critical exception arises in the study of elliptic operators versus principal type operators. Elliptic operators (like the Laplacian) have empty characteristic sets, meaning they are invertible modulo smoothing operators everywhere. Their wavefront sets are empty for solutions to homogeneous equations. In contrast, hyperbolic and parabolic operators have non-empty characteristic sets where singularities propagate. A classic trap involves assuming that if a distribution u solves an elliptic equation Pu = f with smooth f, then u must be smooth. This is true globally, but microlocally, one must check the support of the symbol. If the operator is only hypoelliptic (like the heat operator ∂_t - Δ), singularities in time cannot propagate backward; a singularity at t=0 does not affect the solution for t<0, unlike hyperbolic equations where information travels along finite speed characteristics. This distinction between elliptic regularity and hypoellipticity is often tested through counter-examples involving Gevrey classes or specific weighted Sobolev spaces.

Technique: Identifying the Caustic Transition
Question: Consider the wave equation in 2D. At time t=0, a point source is located at the origin. Describe the wavefront set of the solution u(t,x) for t > 0 and explain why a single phase function cannot describe the singularity structure at the center of the expanding circle.
Solution: For t > 0, the singularities lie on the light cone |x| = t. The wavefront set is WF(u) = {(x, ξ) : |x|=t, |ξ|=1}. However, at the center of the circle (the origin), rays from all directions converge. In phase space T^*R2, this corresponds to a Lagrangian manifold that folds over itself. A single phase function φ(x,y,θ) with a non-degenerate critical point can only describe a fold or a simple wavefront locally. To describe the full solution near the caustic (the origin), one must use an Airy-type integral representation involving three variables in the phase function to capture the merging of rays. The standard FIO formula fails because the Hessian determinant of the phase vanishes at the critical point corresponding to the caustic.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Phase Space" Checklist
When solving problems involving wavefront sets, always perform a three-step check: 1) Identify the characteristic set Σ = {p=0} of the operator. 2) Determine if the singularity is propagating along Hamiltonian flow or if it is stationary (elliptic). 3) Check for caustics by verifying if the canonical relation is a global graph. If the problem mentions "focusing" or "convergence," immediately suspect a breakdown of the standard FIO calculus and consider Maslov indices or Airy integrals.

Non-Commutative Geometry and Spectral Asymptotics

Microlocal analysis provides the rigorous framework for spectral geometry, specifically Weyl's Law. The number of eigenvalues λ_k of a Laplacian Δ on a compact manifold M less than R grows like C Rn/2, where n is the dimension. The constant C depends on the volume of M and the leading coefficient of the principal symbol. Microlocally, this relates to the counting of lattice points in phase space (the unit sphere bundle) via the semiclassical trace formula. A deep result here is the connection between the heat kernel expansion and the Seeley-DeWitt coefficients. The asymptotic expansion of Tr(etΔ) as t→0 contains geometric invariants: the volume, scalar curvature, and Euler characteristic. In advanced exams, students are often asked to derive the coefficient of t(n/2)-1 in the heat trace, which involves integrating the square root of the determinant of the symbol over the phase space.

The concept extends to non-commutative geometry where the algebra of pseudodifferential operators is viewed as a deformation quantization of the classical algebra of functions on phase space. The commutator [a(x,D), b(x,D)] corresponds to the Poisson bracket {σ(a), σ(b)} plus higher order terms in the Planck constant h. This non-commutativity is central to understanding anomalies in quantum field theory and index theory. For instance, the Atiyah-Singer Index Theorem can be derived using K-theory on the Calkin algebra, which is a quotient of the operator algebra by compact operators. A specific landmark case is the index of the Dirac operator on a spin manifold, where the topological term (the Â-genus) appears naturally from the microlocal structure of the symbol.

Technique: Calculating Weyl Asymptotics
Question: Given a Laplacian -Δ on a 2D domain Ω with Dirichlet boundary conditions, state the leading order term of N(λ), the number of eigenvalues less than λ, and explain the microlocal reason for the boundary correction term.
Solution: The leading term is Weyl's Law: N(λ) ~ (Area(Ω)/(4π)) λ. The coefficient comes from integrating the symbol ξ2 over the phase space {ξ : |ξ|2 < λ}, which gives the volume of a ball in momentum space times the spatial volume. However, for Dirichlet conditions, there is a boundary correction term proportional to - (Length(∂Ω)/(4π)) λ1/2. Microlocally, this arises because the wavefront set cannot cross the boundary; the rays reflect off the boundary, effectively reducing the available phase space volume by half near the boundary compared to Neumann or free conditions. The sign depends on the boundary condition: Dirichlet gives a negative correction, while Neumann gives a positive one.

Resonances, Scattering Poles, and Geometric Optics Limits

In scattering theory, the resolvent of the Laplacian (H - z)-1 is meromorphic in the complex energy plane. The poles of this resolvent are called resonances. These are not eigenvalues but quasimodes that decay exponentially in time. Microlocally, resonances correspond to trapped rays or periodic orbits in the classical Hamiltonian flow. If the geodesic flow has no closed orbits (e.g., on a strictly convex surface), there are no resonances near the real axis (the Gevrey class result). However, if there are trapped sets (like a black hole horizon or a billiard table with concave walls), an infinite sequence of resonances accumulates at the real axis. The distribution of these poles is governed by the Selberg trace formula in negative curvature or the fractal dimension of the trapped set.

The geometric optics limit refers to the high-frequency behavior where λ → ∞ (or h → 0). In this limit, solutions behave like eiλφ times a slowly varying amplitude. The phase φ satisfies the eikonal equation |∇φ| = n(x) (refractive index), and the amplitude satisfies a transport equation along rays. A subtle point is that geometric optics fails near caustics, where the amplitude blows up if not corrected by the Van der Corput lemma or stationary phase analysis. The "twist" in exams often involves asking for the validity of geometric optics in the presence of obstacles or rough boundaries (Hölder continuous coefficients), where one must use microlocal defect measures to describe the weak limit of oscillating sequences.

Technique: Defect Measures and Weak Limits
Question: Let u_ε be a sequence of solutions to (Δ + ε-2)u = 0 on R^n with L2 bounds. As ε → 0, the sequence oscillates rapidly. Explain how to extract a microlocal defect measure and what equation it satisfies.
Solution: Since u_ε does not converge strongly, we look at its weak limit in distributions. We apply a microlocal cutoff χ(x,ξ) and consider the quadratic form |χ(D)u_ε|2. The defect measure μ is a positive Radon measure on phase space satisfying the transport equation H_p(μ) = 0, meaning it is invariant under the Hamiltonian flow of the principal symbol p(ξ). If the energy is conserved, μ is supported on the characteristic set {p=0}. This technique allows proving unique continuation principles: if u_ε vanishes in an open set microlocally, then the defect measure must vanish there, implying the solution is zero everywhere by propagation of singularities.
