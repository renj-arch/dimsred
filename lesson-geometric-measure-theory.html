Geometric Measure Theory — Hausdorff Measure, Rectifiable Sets & the Federer-Fleming Theorem

Geometric Measure Theory (GMT) studies the geometric properties of measures and sets — a powerful calculus for irregular geometric objects. The Hausdorff measure H^s generalizes the s-dimensional Lebesgue measure to non-integer dimensions, defining the Hausdorff dimension dim_H E = inf{s: H^s(E) = 0}. A set E ⊂ ℝⁿ is countably H^k-rectifiable if it is covered H^k-a.e. by Lipschitz images of ℝ^k — these sets admit an approximate tangent space at H^k-almost every point. The rectifiable currents are the oriented integration objects over rectifiable sets; the Federer-Fleming theorem (1960) establishes compactness of integral currents with bounded mass and boundary mass — the foundation for solving the Plateau problem (existence of area-minimizing surfaces with given boundary). The area and coarea formulas generalize the change-of-variables to Lipschitz maps between Euclidean spaces. Varifolds (Radon measures on U × G(k,n)) and their first variation define mean curvature in a weak sense. Allard's regularity theorem (1972) states that an integer rectifiable varifold with bounded generalized mean curvature and positive density is a smooth manifold near density-1 points. The Brakke flow is the varifold formulation of mean curvature flow — a one-parameter family of varifolds satisfying a weak inequality. White's theorem bounds the singular set of mean curvature flow: for two-convex surfaces, the singular set has dimension at most n−2.

"Geometric measure theory is the calculus of irregular geometric objects."

Part 1

📏 Hausdorff Measure and Dimension

Hausdorff
Hausdorff s-Measure, Hausdorff Dimension, and Density
The Hausdorff s-measure is defined for any s ≥ 0 by H^s(E) = lim_{δ→0} H^s_δ(E) where H^s_δ(E) = inf{ Σ_{i=1}^∞ (diam U_i)^s : E ⊂ ∪ U_i, diam U_i < δ }. Properties: H^s is an outer measure on ℝⁿ and is Borel regular. For integer s = n, H^n = c_n·L^n where L^n is the n-dimensional Lebesgue measure and c_n = ω_n/2^n (the volume of the n-dimensional ball of diameter 1). The Hausdorff dimension dim_H E = inf{s: H^s(E) = 0} = sup{s: H^s(E) = ∞}. For the Cantor set C ⊂ [0,1] (the middle-thirds Cantor set): dim_H C = log 2 / log 3. The isodiametric inequality states that for any set A ⊂ ℝⁿ, L^n(A) ≤ c_n (diam A)^n — the Euclidean ball maximizes volume for a given diameter. The upper and lower s-densities at a point x ∈ ℝⁿ are: θ^{*s}(E,x) = limsup_{r→0} H^s(E ∩ B_r(x)) / (c_s r^s) and θ^s_*(E,x) = liminf_{r→0} H^s(E ∩ B_r(x)) / (c_s r^s). For H^s-rectifiable sets, the density θ^s(E,x) exists and equals 1 at H^s-almost every point.
Hausdorff measure extends the classical notion of volume to fractional dimensions, providing the fundamental measuring tool for irregular sets in GMT.
Part 2

🌍 Rectifiable Sets and Currents

Currents
Rectifiable Sets, Approximate Tangent Spaces, and Integral Currents
A set E ⊂ ℝⁿ is countably H^k-rectifiable if there exist Lipschitz maps f_i: ℝ^k → ℝⁿ such that H^k(E \ ∪_i f_i(ℝ^k)) = 0. At H^k-almost every point x ∈ E, there exists an approximate tangent space T_x E — a k-dimensional subspace of ℝⁿ such that the scaled-up rescaled set (E − x)/r converges in measure to T_x E as r → 0. A k-dimensional rectifiable current T = τ · [[E]] is an oriented rectifiable set: E is H^k-rectifiable and τ(x) ∈ Λ_k T_xℝⁿ is a unit simple k-vector orienting the tangent space (with integer multiplicity). The mass of T is M(T) = ∫_E |τ| dH^k = H^k(E). The boundary ∂T is defined by Stokes' theorem: ∂T(ω) = T(dω). For rectifiable T, ∂T is an integer rectifiable (k−1)-current. The Federer-Fleming theorem (1960): the space I_k(U) of integer rectifiable currents with compact support in U with M(T) + M(∂T) ≤ C is compact in the flat metric F(T) = inf{M(R) + M(S): T − T' = R + ∂S}. This directly implies the existence of area-minimizing currents for any given rectifiable boundary — solving the Plateau problem. The area formula: for Lipschitz f: ℝᵐ → ℝⁿ with m ≤ n, ∫_ℝᵐ J_m f(x) dL^m = ∫_ℝⁿ N(f, y) dH^m(y) where J_m is the m-dimensional Jacobian and N(f, y) = #f^{−1}(y). The coarea formula: for Lipschitz f: ℝⁿ → ℝᵐ with m ≤ n, ∫_ℝⁿ J_m f(x) dL^n = ∫_ℝᵐ H^{n−m}(f^{−1}({y})) dL^m(y).
Rectifiable sets are measurable generalizations of smooth submanifolds; currents provide the homological integration theory that solves the Plateau problem.
Part 3

🧮 Varifolds and the Allard Regularity Theorem

Varifolds
Varifolds, First Variation, Mean Curvature, and Allard's Regularity
A k-varifold V on an open set U ⊂ ℝⁿ is a Radon measure on U × G(k,n), where G(k,n) is the Grassmannian of unoriented k-planes in ℝⁿ. The mass (or weight) of V is the Radon measure ||V|| on U defined by ||V||(A) = V(A × G(k,n)) = ∫_{G(k,n)} 1_A(x) dV(x, π). The first variation of V is the distribution δV(η) = ∫ div_π η(x) dV(x, π) for η ∈ C_c^∞(U, ℝⁿ), where div_π η(x) = Σ_{i=1}^k ⟨∇_{e_i} η(x), e_i⟩ for any orthonormal basis {e_i} of π. If δV is representable by a function: δV(η) = −∫ η(x)·H_V(x) d||V||(x), then H_V is the generalized mean curvature vector of V. An integer rectifiable varifold V is associated with an H^k-rectifiable set and an integer multiplicity. Allard's regularity theorem (1972): there exist constants ε = ε(k,n) and C = C(k,n,p) such that if V is an integer rectifiable k-varifold on ℝⁿ with 0 ∈ spt ||V||, Θ^k(||V||, 0) ≥ 1 − ε (density close to 1), and ∫ |H_V|^p d||V|| < C for some p > k, then in a neighborhood of 0, spt V is a C^{1,α} embedded smooth k-manifold (and C^∞ if H_V is smooth). This is the GMT version of the regularity of minimal surfaces: a stationary varifold (H_V = 0) with density close to 1 is smooth. The density theorem: for stationary integer rectifiable varifolds, the density Θ^k(||V||, x) is upper semicontinuous and takes values in (0, ∞). The singular set of a stationary varifold consists of points where the density exceeds 1.
Varifolds generalize currents by forgetting orientation, enabling weak formulations of mean curvature; Allard's theorem bridges the gap between weak and classical solutions.
Part 4

🔥 The Brakke Flow and the Mean Curvature Flow

Brakke
Brakke Flow, Existence, Singularities, and the Huisken-Sinestrari Theorem
The mean curvature flow (MCF) is a one-parameter family of surfaces Σ_t ⊂ ℝⁿ evolving such that the normal velocity at each point equals the mean curvature vector: V = H. The Brakke flow is the varifold formulation of MCF. A family {V_t}_{t ≥ 0} of varifolds is a Brakke flow if for all test functions f ∈ C_c^1(ℝⁿ × [0,∞), [0,∞)), the inequality holds: d/dt ∫ f(x,t) d||V_t||(x) ≤ ∫ (−|H_{V_t}|² f + ∇f·H_{V_t} + ∂_t f) d||V_t||(x) in the sense of distributions. The Brakke existence theorem: for any integer rectifiable varifold V₀ with ||V₀||(ℝⁿ) < ∞ and ||δV₀|| < ∞, there exists a Brakke flow {V_t} starting from V₀. However, Brakke flows are not unique and may develop fattening. Type I singularities satisfy |A|² ≤ C/(T−t) (the curvature blows up at the rate 1/√(T−t)) — the blow-up is a self-shrinker: a surface satisfying H = −x^⟂/2. Examples: shrinking spheres (ℝ×S^{n−1}) and shrinking cylinders (S^k × ℝ^{n−k}). Type II singularities have slower blow-up rates and produce more degenerate limits (e.g., translating solitons like the grim reaper curve). White's theorem: for MCF of surfaces with two-convexity (λ₁ + λ₂ ≥ α H for α > 0), the singular set at the first singular time has Hausdorff dimension at most n−2. The Huisken-Sinestrari theorem (2009): for two-convex MCF of hypersurfaces in ℝⁿ (n ≥ 3), the flow exists until the solution becomes convex and shrinks to a round point — the only singularities are neck pinches and round points. This gives a complete classification of singularities for two-convex MCF and provides a far-reaching generalization of the Grayson's theorem for curve shortening flow.
Brakke flow is the weak formulation of mean curvature flow, enabling the study of singularities and topological changes that smooth MCF cannot handle.
