Thermodynamics — Laws of Thermodynamics, Heat Engines, Entropy & the Carnot Cycle

Thermodynamics is the science of heat, work, and energy transformations — a macroscopic theory that emerged from the study of steam engines in the 19th century. The zeroth law establishes thermal equilibrium as an equivalence relation, defining temperature. The first law ΔU = Q − W formalizes energy conservation: heat added to a system minus work done by the system equals the change in internal energy. The second law asserts that entropy of an isolated system never decreases — the Clausius statement (heat cannot spontaneously flow from cold to hot) and the Kelvin-Planck statement (no heat engine can be 100% efficient). The third law states that absolute zero is unattainable and the entropy of a perfect crystal at T = 0 K is exactly zero. The Carnot cycle — two isothermal and two adiabatic processes — defines the maximum possible efficiency η = 1 − T_C/T_H for any heat engine operating between two reservoirs. The Clausius inequality ∮ dQ/T ≤ 0 leads to the definition of entropy as a state function dS = dQ_rev/T. The thermodynamic potentials — internal energy U, enthalpy H = U + PV, Helmholtz free energy F = U − TS, Gibbs free energy G = H − TS — and the associated Maxwell relations provide the framework for analyzing phase transitions, chemical reactions, and the thermodynamic stability of systems.

"The entropy of the universe tends to a maximum."
— Rudolf Clausius
Part 1

🌡️ The Laws of Thermodynamics

Fundamental Laws
The Zeroth, First, Second, and Third Laws: Temperature, Internal Energy, Work, and Heat
The zeroth law: if A is in thermal equilibrium with B and B with C, then A is in equilibrium with C — this defines temperature as a transitive property and allows thermometers. The first law ΔU = Q − W (or dU = đQ − đW for infinitesimal processes) expresses energy conservation. For an ideal gas, internal energy U depends only on temperature: U = nC_vT. Work done by a gas: W = ∫ P dV. Enthalpy H = U + PV is the relevant state function for constant-pressure processes; ΔH = Q_P. The second law has multiple equivalent formulations: Clausius statement — no process can transfer heat from a colder to a hotter body without external work; Kelvin-Planck statement — no cyclic process can convert heat entirely into work. The third law (Nernst-Simon statement): as T → 0 K, the entropy change ΔS for any isothermal process approaches zero; absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps. The Carnot theorem: no heat engine operating between two reservoirs can have efficiency greater than a Carnot engine η_C = 1 − T_C/T_H. All reversible engines operating between the same reservoirs have the same efficiency. The thermodynamic temperature scale is defined by the Carnot cycle: T_H/T_C = Q_H/Q_C.
The four laws of thermodynamics are among the most fundamental principles in physics — they govern all energy transformations and impose fundamental limits on efficiency and predictability.
Part 2

🔥 Heat Engines, Entropy, and the Clausius Inequality

Entropy
Heat Engines, Refrigerators, the Clausius Inequality, and Entropy as a State Function
A heat engine absorbs Q_H from a hot reservoir at T_H, does work W, and exhausts Q_C to a cold reservoir at T_C. Efficiency: η = W/Q_H = 1 − Q_C/Q_H. A refrigerator reverses the cycle: coefficient of performance K = Q_C/W = Q_C/(Q_H − Q_C). The Carnot cycle consists of four reversible processes: (1) isothermal expansion at T_H (heat absorption), (2) adiabatic expansion (temperature drops to T_C), (3) isothermal compression at T_C (heat rejection), (4) adiabatic compression (temperature rises to T_H). The area enclosed in the PV diagram equals the net work done. In the TS diagram, the Carnot cycle is a rectangle: Q_H = T_HΔS, Q_C = T_CΔS, W = (T_H − T_C)ΔS. The Clausius inequality ∮ đQ/T ≤ 0 for any cyclic process, with equality for reversible cycles. This leads to the definition of entropy S as a state function: dS = đQ_rev/T. For an irreversible process, ΔS > ∫ đQ/T — the entropy of an isolated system increases (ΔS ≥ 0). The principle of entropy increase: for any spontaneous process in an isolated system, ΔS_total ≥ 0. Entropy is a measure of microscopic disorder: S = k_B ln Ω (Boltzmann's formula, where Ω is the number of microstates). The Gibbs paradox highlights the indistinguishability of identical particles in statistical mechanics. The Loschmidt paradox asks how irreversible macroscopic behavior emerges from time-reversible microscopic dynamics — resolved by the fact that initial conditions of low entropy are extremely rare.
Entropy is the central concept of the second law — it quantifies irreversibility and the arrow of time, and its statistical interpretation connects the microscopic world to macroscopic thermodynamics.
Part 3

⚙️ Thermodynamic Potentials and Maxwell Relations

Potentials
Internal Energy, Enthalpy, Helmholtz Free Energy, Gibbs Free Energy, and the Maxwell Relations
The fundamental thermodynamic relation: dU = T dS − P dV (for a simple compressible system). The thermodynamic potentials are Legendre transforms of U: Enthalpy H = U + PV, dH = T dS + V dP (natural variables S, P). Helmholtz free energy F = U − TS, dF = −S dT − P dV (natural variables T, V). Gibbs free energy G = H − TS = U + PV − TS, dG = −S dT + V dP (natural variables T, P). The Maxwell relations follow from the equality of mixed partial derivatives of the potentials: (∂T/∂V)_S = −(∂P/∂S)_V (from dU); (∂T/∂P)_S = (∂V/∂S)_P (from dH); (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂P/∂T)_V (from dF); −(∂S/∂P)_T = (∂V/∂T)_P (from dG). These relations connect measurable quantities (heat capacities, expansion coefficients, compressibility). The heat capacities: C_V = T(∂S/∂T)_V = (∂U/∂T)_V; C_P = T(∂S/∂T)_P = (∂H/∂T)_P. For an ideal gas: C_P − C_V = nR. The Gibbs-Duhem relation: S dT − V dP + N dμ = 0, connecting intensive variables. The chemical potential μ = (∂G/∂N)_T,P is the Gibbs free energy per particle, crucial for open systems and phase equilibria. The Clausius-Clapeyron equation: dP/dT = L/(TΔV) describes the slope of phase boundaries, where L is the latent heat of the phase transition.
Thermodynamic potentials provide a complete description of equilibrium thermodynamics; the Maxwell relations are powerful tools for relating seemingly unrelated thermodynamic quantities.
Part 4

🧊 Statistical Mechanics and Advanced Topics

Statistical Foundations
The Boltzmann Distribution, Partition Functions, Ensembles, and the Statistical Interpretation of Entropy
Statistical mechanics derives macroscopic thermodynamics from the microscopic laws of mechanics and probability. The fundamental postulate: an isolated system in equilibrium is equally likely to be in any accessible microstate. The Boltzmann distribution: probability of a state with energy E_i at temperature T is P_i = (1/Z) e^{−E_i/k_B T}, where Z = Σ_i e^{−E_i/k_B T} is the partition function. All thermodynamic quantities follow from Z: F = −k_B T ln Z, U = −∂ ln Z/∂β (with β = 1/k_B T), S = k_B (ln Z + βU), P = −(∂F/∂V)_T. The three main ensembles: microcanonical (fixed U, V, N) — the most fundamental; canonical (fixed T, V, N) — the Boltzmann distribution; grand canonical (fixed T, V, μ) — variable particle number, with grand partition function Ξ = Σ_N Z_N e^{βμN}. The equipartition theorem: each quadratic degree of freedom in the Hamiltonian contributes ½k_B T to the average energy — giving U = ½Nfk_B T for a gas with f degrees of freedom per molecule. The Sackur-Tetrode equation for the entropy of an ideal monatomic gas: S = Nk_B[ln(V/Nλ³) + 5/2], where λ = h/√(2πmk_B T) is the thermal de Broglie wavelength. The Gibbs paradox is resolved by dividing by N! for indistinguishable particles. Quantum statistical mechanics: Fermi-Dirac statistics for fermions (occupation number f(E) = 1/(e^{(E−μ)/k_B T} + 1)) and Bose-Einstein statistics for bosons (f(E) = 1/(e^{(E−μ)/k_B T} − 1)). The Bose-Einstein condensate occurs when the thermal de Broglie wavelength exceeds the interparticle spacing. Planck's blackbody radiation law: u(ν, T) = (8πhν³/c³)/(e^{hν/k_B T} − 1), which resolved the ultraviolet catastrophe and ushered in quantum mechanics.
Statistical mechanics bridges the microscopic world of atoms and molecules to the macroscopic world of thermodynamics, with the partition function serving as the master key to all equilibrium properties.
