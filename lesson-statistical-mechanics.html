Statistical Mechanics — Microstates, Ensembles, Boltzmann Distribution & Partition Functions

Deep dive into Statistical Mechanics — the microscopic foundation of thermodynamics, microstates (the precise quantum state of a system) and macrostates (defined by a few thermodynamic variables), Liouville's theorem (phase space volume is conserved under Hamiltonian flow), the ergodic hypothesis (time averages equal ensemble averages), the microcanonical ensemble (fixed E, V, N — the fundamental postulate of equal a priori probabilities), the canonical ensemble (fixed T, V, N — the Boltzmann factor exp(−βE) and the canonical partition function Z = Σ exp(−βE_i)), the grand canonical ensemble (fixed T, V, μ — the grand partition function Ξ = Σ exp(−β(E_i − μN_i))), the Boltzmann distribution p_i = exp(−βE_i)/Z, the partition functions and their connection to thermodynamic potentials F = −kT ln Z, the equipartition theorem, the ideal gas, the Ising model (a lattice spin model with Hamiltonian H = −J Σ s_i s_j − h Σ s_i), Onsager's solution of the 2D Ising model (spontaneous magnetization and the critical temperature), Landau theory of phase transitions (free energy expanded in powers of the order parameter), critical phenomena (scaling laws and universality), and the renormalization group (Kadanoff's block spins and Wilson's RG flow).

Part 1

🎲 Microstates, Macrostates, and Liouville's Theorem

Phase Space
Phase Space, Liouville's Theorem, and the Ergodic Hypothesis
A microstate is a complete specification of the microscopic degrees of freedom of a system — in classical mechanics, a point (q, p) in phase space Γ of dimension 6N for N particles. A macrostate is a coarse-grained description defined by a small set of thermodynamic variables (E, V, N for the microcanonical ensemble). Liouville's theorem: the phase space density ρ(q, p, t) evolves according to ∂ρ/∂t = {H, ρ} (the Poisson bracket), and dρ/dt = 0 — phase space volume is conserved along Hamiltonian trajectories. This is the classical analogue of unitary evolution in quantum mechanics. The ergodic hypothesis asserts that for an isolated system, time averages equal ensemble averages: ⟨A⟩_time = lim_{T→∞} (1/T) ∫₀^T A(q(t), p(t)) dt = ⟨A⟩_ensemble = ∫_Γ A(q, p) ρ(q, p) dΓ, where ρ is the stationary distribution uniform over the energy shell in the microcanonical ensemble. The fundamental postulate of statistical mechanics: for an isolated system at equilibrium, all accessible microstates (with the correct total energy) are equally probable. The number of microstates Ω(E, V, N) is the central object — Boltzmann's entropy is S = k ln Ω. The principle of maximum entropy (Jaynes, 1957): the least biased probability distribution consistent with known constraints maximizes the Shannon entropy S = −k Σ p_i ln p_i.
Microstates are the full microscopic description; macrostates are coarse-grained; Liouville's theorem guarantees the conservation of phase space volume; the ergodic hypothesis connects time averages to ensemble averages, justifying the equal probability postulate.
Part 2

📊 Ensembles and the Boltzmann Distribution

Partition Functions
Microcanonical, Canonical, Grand Canonical Ensembles and the Boltzmann Factor
The microcanonical ensemble describes isolated systems with fixed energy E, volume V, and particle number N. The fundamental quantity is Ω(E, V, N), the number of microstates. Entropy: S(E, V, N) = k ln Ω(E, V, N). Temperature: 1/T = ∂S/∂E. Pressure: P = T ∂S/∂V. Chemical potential: μ = −T ∂S/∂N. The canonical ensemble describes systems in thermal contact with a heat bath at temperature T. The system energy fluctuates but temperature is fixed. The Boltzmann factor exp(−βE_i) (where β = 1/kT) gives the relative probability of microstate i with energy E_i. The canonical partition function Z = Σ_i exp(−βE_i) normalizes the distribution: p_i = exp(−βE_i)/Z. The Helmholtz free energy F = −kT ln Z, from which all thermodynamic quantities follow: S = −∂F/∂T, P = −∂F/∂V, E = Σ p_i E_i = −∂ ln Z/∂β. The grand canonical ensemble describes open systems exchanging both energy and particles with a reservoir at fixed T and μ. The grand partition function Ξ = Σ_N Σ_i exp(−β(E_i − μN_i)). The grand potential Ω = −kT ln Ξ = −PV. The average particle number ⟨N⟩ = (1/β) ∂ ln Ξ/∂μ. The equipartition theorem: each quadratic degree of freedom in the Hamiltonian contributes (1/2)kT to the average energy. For the ideal gas: Z = (V/λ³)^N / N! where λ = h/√(2πmkT) is the thermal de Broglie wavelength, yielding E = (3/2)NkT and PV = NkT.
Each ensemble corresponds to different constraints (fixed E, T, or μ). The partition function encodes all thermodynamic information; free energy connects the microscopic world to macroscopic thermodynamics.
Part 3

🔥 Applications: Ideal Gas, Paramagnetism, and Thermodynamic Potentials

Calculations
Ideal Gas, Maxwell Distribution, Paramagnetism, and the Equipartition Theorem
The ideal gas partition function: Z = (1/N!)(V/λ³)^N. Stirling's approximation ln N! ≈ N ln N − N gives F = −NkT [ln(V/Nλ³) + 1]. The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution of molecular speeds: f(v) = 4π (m/2πkT)^{3/2} v² exp(−mv²/2kT). The average speed ⟨v⟩ = √(8kT/πm), RMS speed v_rms = √(3kT/m). The equipartition yields specific heats: C_V = (f/2)Nk where f is the number of quadratic degrees of freedom (f=3 for monatomic, f=5 for diatomic at moderate temperatures). Paramagnetism: N magnetic dipoles μ in a field B. Each dipole has energy E = −μB cos θ. The partition function per dipole: z = ∫_0^{2π} dφ ∫_0^π exp(βμB cos θ) sin θ dθ = 4π sinh(βμB)/(βμB). The magnetization M = Nμ [coth(βμB) − 1/(βμB)] = Nμ L(βμB), where L(x) = coth x − 1/x is the Langevin function. For weak fields, M ≈ Nμ²B/3kT — Curie's law. Thermodynamic potentials: internal energy U, Helmholtz free energy F, Gibbs free energy G = F + PV, enthalpy H = U + PV. Maxwell relations follow from the equality of mixed partials of these potentials, providing crucial identities like (∂S/∂V)_T = (∂P/∂T)_V. The Gibbs-Helmholtz equation: ∂(G/T)/∂T = −H/T².
The partition function approach provides a systematic method to calculate macroscopic properties — magnetization, specific heat, equation of state — from microscopic Hamiltonians.
Part 4

🌌 Phase Transitions, the Ising Model, and the Renormalization Group

Criticality
Ising Model, Onsager's Solution, Landau Theory, and Wilson's Renormalization Group
The Ising model on a lattice: H = −J Σ_{⟨ij⟩} s_i s_j − h Σ_i s_i, where s_i = ±1, ⟨ij⟩ denotes nearest neighbors, J is the exchange coupling, and h is the external field. Onsager's exact solution (1944) of the 2D square-lattice Ising model with h=0 showed a second-order phase transition at T_c = 2J/[k ln(1+√2)] ≈ 2.269 J/k. The magnetization vanishes as T → T_c−: M ∼ (T_c − T)^β with β = 1/8. The specific heat diverges logarithmically: C ∼ −ln|T − T_c|. Landau theory describes phase transitions via a free energy functional expanded in powers of the order parameter φ: F(φ) = F₀ + a(T)φ² + b(T)φ⁴ + … + κ(∇φ)². For T > T_c, a > 0 and the minimum is at φ = 0 (disordered phase). For T < T_c, a < 0 and φ acquires a non-zero value (ordered phase). The critical exponent β = 1/2 in mean-field Landau theory (the order parameter grows as (T_c − T)^{1/2}). Critical exponents (α, β, γ, δ, ν, η) describe power-law singularities near T_c. The renormalization group (Wilson, 1971): coarse-grain the system by integrating out short-wavelength fluctuations, producing a flow in the space of Hamiltonians. Fixed points of the RG flow correspond to scale-invariant critical theories. Universality: systems with the same symmetry and dimensionality flow to the same fixed point, hence share the same critical exponents. The Kadanoff block-spin transformation: average spins over blocks of size b, rescale, and derive recursion relations — the foundation of the RG.
Phase transitions arise from singularities in the partition function at the thermodynamic limit; the renormalization group explains universality and provides a systematic method to compute critical exponents.
