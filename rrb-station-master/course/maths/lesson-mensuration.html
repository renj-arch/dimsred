1. Foundations of Measurement and Units

1.1 The Scope of Mensuration in Competitive Testing

Mensuration is the branch of mathematics concerned with the measurement of geometric magnitudes such as length, area, and volume, and it forms a predictable yet high-scoring segment of the quantitative aptitude syllabus. Unlike algebra or number systems where multiple solution paths may exist, mensuration problems typically rely on the direct application of standard formulae, making accuracy and speed the primary differentiators between candidates. The examiner tests not merely the memorisation of equations but the ability to identify which dimension — radius, diameter, slant height, or diagonal — is required by the specific formula, and whether the question demands a curved surface, a total surface, or a volume calculation. A thorough grasp of this module allows a candidate to secure marks quickly, leaving valuable time for more computationally intensive sections.

1.2 Standard Units and the Necessity of Conversion

Every measurement in mensuration is expressed in units, and the consistency of these units across all dimensions of a figure is the first prerequisite for a correct answer. Length is measured in millimetres, centimetres, metres, or kilometres; area, being a two-dimensional measure, is expressed in square units such as square centimetres (cm²) or square metres (m²); volume, a three-dimensional measure, uses cubic units like cubic centimetres (cm³) or cubic metres (m³). A frequent source of error arises when a problem provides the radius in centimetres and the height in metres; the candidate must convert all measurements to a single unit system before substituting values into the formula. Failure to do so produces an answer that is numerically incorrect by orders of magnitude, typically a factor of 100 or 10,000 depending on whether the mismatch involves area or volume units.

1.3 Dimensional Analysis: Length, Area, and Volume

Understanding the dimensional nature of a formula provides a powerful verification tool during the examination. The formula for the area of a rectangle, length × breadth, multiplies two lengths, yielding a dimension of length squared (L²); similarly, the area of a circle, πr², squares the radius, confirming its two-dimensional nature. Volume formulae invariably involve the product of three lengths, such as length × breadth × height for a cuboid or πr²h for a cylinder, resulting in a dimension of length cubed (L³). Surface area formulae, whether total or curved, always resolve to L² because they represent the sum of flat or curved faces. If a derived formula yields a dimension of L³ for an area question, or L² for a volume question, the candidate knows immediately that a substitution or algebraic error has occurred, allowing for rapid correction without re-reading the entire problem statement.

2. Triangles: Classification, Area, and Perimeter

2.1 Classification by Sides and Angles

Triangles are the fundamental polygons in plane geometry, classified primarily by the relative lengths of their sides and the magnitudes of their interior angles. An equilateral triangle possesses three equal sides and three equal angles of 60° each; an isosceles triangle has two equal sides and two equal base angles; a scalene triangle has no equal sides and no equal angles. By angles, a triangle may be acute (all angles < 90°), right (one angle exactly 90°), or obtuse (one angle > 90°). This classification is not merely academic; it dictates which area formula is most efficient. For a right triangle, the two legs serve as base and height directly, allowing immediate use of the half-base-height formula. For an equilateral triangle, a specialised formula derived from the Pythagorean theorem eliminates the need to calculate the altitude separately. Recognising the triangle type at a glance therefore determines the solution pathway.

2.2 The Universal Area Formula and the Role of Altitude

The most general formula for the area of any triangle is one-half multiplied by the base multiplied by the corresponding altitude (Area = ½ × b × h). The altitude is the perpendicular distance from the chosen base to the opposite vertex; it is crucial to understand that the altitude must be perpendicular to the base selected. In a right triangle, the legs are mutually perpendicular, so either leg can serve as the altitude for the other leg as base. In an obtuse triangle, the altitude from the acute angle vertex to the extended base line falls outside the triangle, yet the formula remains valid because the geometric principle — the triangle occupies exactly half the area of the enclosing parallelogram — holds regardless of the triangle's shape. Candidates must be careful not to confuse the slant height or the side length with the true perpendicular height, a confusion that frequently occurs in isosceles and scalene triangles where the altitude is not explicitly given.

2.3 Heron's Formula for Scalene Triangles

When the three sides of a triangle are known but no altitude is provided, Heron's formula becomes indispensable. It states that the area equals the square root of s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c), where a, b, and c are the side lengths and s is the semi-perimeter, defined as half the sum of the three sides (s = (a + b + c)/2). The formula works for all triangles, but it is particularly valuable for scalene triangles where the altitude is difficult to determine. The computation involves finding the semi-perimeter first, then subtracting each side from it, multiplying the four resulting numbers, and finally taking the square root. Because the numbers under the radical can become large, candidates should look for factorisation opportunities or perfect squares to simplify the arithmetic before reaching for a calculator, which is not permitted in the examination hall.

2.4 Special Cases: Equilateral, Isosceles, and Right Triangles

For an equilateral triangle of side a, the altitude splits the triangle into two 30-60-90 right triangles, yielding an altitude of (√3/2)a and a direct area formula of (√3/4)a². This formula is derived from the universal half-base-height rule and should be memorised because it bypasses the need for Heron's formula or Pythagorean calculations during the test. In an isosceles triangle with equal sides a and base b, the altitude to the base bisects the base, creating two right triangles with hypotenuse a and base b/2; the altitude is therefore √(a² − (b/2)²), and the area becomes (b/2) × √(a² − (b/2)²). For a right triangle with legs p and q and hypotenuse h, the area is simply ½pq, and the Pythagorean theorem (p² + q² = h²) allows the determination of any missing side. The inradius (r) and circumradius (R) of a right triangle also follow simple relations: r = (p + q − h)/2 and R = h/2, which occasionally appear in advanced problems combining circles and triangles.

Worked Example: Area of a Scalene Triangle Using Heron's Formula
The sides of a triangular plot are 13 metres, 14 metres, and 15 metres. Find the area of the plot in square metres.
Solution: The given sides are a = 13 m, b = 14 m, c = 15 m. Since no height is provided, Heron's formula is the appropriate method.

Step 1: Calculate the semi-perimeter s. s = (13 + 14 + 15) / 2 = 42 / 2 = 21 m.

Step 2: Compute the differences s − a, s − b, s − c. s − a = 21 − 13 = 8. s − b = 21 − 14 = 7. s − c = 21 − 15 = 6.

Step 3: Substitute into Heron's formula. Area = √[s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)] = √[21 × 8 × 7 × 6].

Step 4: Simplify the product under the square root by grouping factors into perfect squares. 21 × 7 = 147 = 3 × 49 = 3 × 7². 8 × 6 = 48 = 16 × 3 = 4² × 3. The product becomes (7² × 4² × 3²).

Step 5: Take the square root. √(7² × 4² × 3²) = 7 × 4 × 3 = 84.

Final Answer: The area of the triangular plot is 84 square metres.
Exam Insight: The 13-14-15 Triangle and Pythagorean Triplets
The triangle with sides 13, 14, 15 is a favourite in competitive exams because its semi-perimeter is an integer (21) and the differences (8, 7, 6) multiply to a perfect square (7056 = 84²), yielding an integer area. Memorise the areas of common Heronian triangles such as 3-4-5 (Area 6), 5-12-13 (Area 30), 8-15-17 (Area 60), and 13-14-15 (Area 84). Recognising these triplets allows you to solve area questions in seconds without writing the full Heron's formula expansion.

3. Quadrilaterals and Regular Polygons

3.1 Parallelograms, Rectangles, and Squares

A quadrilateral is a four-sided polygon, and its area calculation depends on the specific properties of its sub-class. A parallelogram has opposite sides parallel and equal; its area is the product of its base and the perpendicular distance between the parallel sides (Area = b × h). A rectangle is a parallelogram with four right angles; its area simplifies to length × breadth, and its diagonals are equal and bisect each other. A square is a rectangle with all sides equal; its area is side², and its diagonal is side√2, a direct consequence of the Pythagorean theorem applied to the isosceles right triangles formed by the diagonal. The perimeter of these shapes follows directly from the side lengths: 2(l + b) for a rectangle and 4a for a square. Problems often link the diagonal of a square to the diameter of an inscribed or circumscribed circle, requiring the candidate to move fluidly between linear and circular mensuration.

3.2 Rhombus and Trapezium: Diagonals and Parallel Sides

A rhombus is a parallelogram with all sides equal; its diagonals are perpendicular bisectors of each other. This orthogonality leads to a distinct area formula: Area = ½ × d₁ × d₂, where d₁ and d₂ are the lengths of the diagonals. This formula is often faster than the base-height method because the diagonals are frequently the given data. A trapezium (or trapezoid) has exactly one pair of parallel sides, called bases. Its area is the product of the average length of the bases and the perpendicular distance (height) between them: Area = ½ × (a + b) × h. If the non-parallel sides are given instead of the height, the candidate must drop perpendiculars to form right triangles and use the Pythagorean theorem to find the height, a multi-step process that tests geometric visualisation.

3.3 Area of Regular Polygons and the Apothem Concept

A regular polygon has all sides equal and all interior angles equal. It can be dissected into n congruent isosceles triangles by drawing radii from the centre to the vertices. The area of each triangle is ½ × side × apothem, where the apothem is the perpendicular distance from the centre to a side (the inradius of the polygon). The total area is therefore ½ × Perimeter × Apothem. For a regular hexagon of side a, the apothem is (√3/2)a, leading to the standard formula Area = (3√3/2)a². The interior angle of a regular n-sided polygon is (n−2)×180°/n, and the central angle is 360°/n; these angles are essential when the problem provides the radius of the circumscribed circle instead of the side length, requiring trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine) to find the side or apothem.

3.4 Cyclic Quadrilaterals and Brahmagupta's Formula

A cyclic quadrilateral is one whose four vertices lie on a circle. For such a quadrilateral with sides a, b, c, d and semi-perimeter s = (a+b+c+d)/2, the area is given by Brahmagupta's formula: Area = √[(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)(s−d)]. This is a direct generalisation of Heron's formula for triangles (which can be viewed as a degenerate quadrilateral with one side of length zero). An important property of cyclic quadrilaterals is that opposite angles are supplementary (sum to 180°). If the quadrilateral is also tangential (has an incircle), then a + c = b + d, and the area simplifies to √(abcd). These specialised formulae appear less frequently but offer a significant time advantage when the problem explicitly states the quadrilateral is cyclic.

4. The Circle: Perimeter, Area, and Circular Parts

4.1 Defining Pi and the Relationship Between Diameter and Circumference

The circle is defined as the locus of points equidistant from a fixed centre. The constant ratio of the circumference to the diameter is denoted by the Greek letter π (pi), an irrational number approximately equal to 3.14159 or, for examination convenience, the rational approximation 22/7. The circumference (perimeter) is therefore C = πd = 2πr, where d is the diameter and r is the radius. The diameter is exactly twice the radius (d = 2r). A common error is to use the diameter in the area formula where the radius is required, or vice versa. The circumference formula is linear in r, while the area formula is quadratic in r; this distinction explains why a percentage change in radius produces an equal percentage change in circumference but a squared relationship in area.

4.2 Area of a Circle and the Method of Exhaustion Logic

The area of a circle is A = πr². This formula can be understood by imagining the circle cut into many thin sectors and rearranged into a shape approximating a rectangle with length equal to half the circumference (πr) and breadth equal to the radius (r); the product gives πr². Because the radius is squared, the area is extremely sensitive to the radius value. If the radius is doubled, the area quadruples; if the radius increases by 10%, the area increases by 21% (1.1² = 1.21). Examination questions frequently test this proportional reasoning by asking for the percentage change in area given a percentage change in radius or diameter, requiring the candidate to apply the square factor correctly rather than assuming a linear relationship.

4.3 Sectors, Segments, and Arc Length Calculations

A sector is the region enclosed by two radii and an arc. Its area is a fraction of the full circle's area, determined by the central angle θ (in degrees): Area = (θ/360°) × πr². The arc length of the sector is similarly (θ/360°) × 2πr. A segment is the region between a chord and its arc. The area of a segment is found by subtracting the area of the isosceles triangle formed by the two radii and the chord from the area of the sector: Area_segment = Area_sector − Area_triangle. The triangle area is ½r²sinθ. When θ = 60°, the triangle is equilateral; when θ = 90°, it is an isosceles right triangle. These standard angles appear often because their sine values (√3/2, 1/√2) allow exact calculation without trigonometric tables.

4.4 Concentric Circles and the Area of a Ring

Concentric circles share the same centre but have different radii, R (outer) and r (inner). The region between them is called an annulus or ring. Its area is the difference of the two circle areas: πR² − πr² = π(R² − r²) = π(R − r)(R + r). This factorised form is computationally efficient because it avoids squaring large numbers separately. The width of the ring is simply R − r. Problems involving a circular path around a garden or a race track are applications of this concept. If a wire is bent from a circle into a square, the perimeter (circumference) remains constant, equating 2πr to 4a, allowing the side of the square to be found; the area then changes because the circle encloses the maximum area for a given perimeter.

5. Cubes and Cuboids: Three Dimensional Fundamentals

5.1 Volume as the Product of Base Area and Height

Volume measures the three-dimensional space occupied by a solid. For any prism or cylinder with a uniform cross-section, the volume is the area of the base multiplied by the height (V = A_base × h). A cuboid has six rectangular faces with dimensions length (l), breadth (b), and height (h). Its volume is V = l × b × h. A cube is a special cuboid where l = b = h = a, giving V = a³. The units of volume are the cube of the length units (cm³, m³). A litre is defined as 1000 cm³, and a cubic metre is 1,000,000 cm³ (10⁶ cm³); these conversions are standard in capacity problems involving water tanks or containers. When a solid is melted and recast into a new shape, the volume remains constant (conservation of volume), providing the equation that links the dimensions of the original and final solids.

5.2 Total Surface Area and the Unfolding of Nets

The total surface area (TSA) of a solid is the sum of the areas of all its faces. For a cuboid, there are three pairs of identical rectangles: 2(lb + bh + hl). For a cube, all six faces are squares of side a, so TSA = 6a². Visualising the net of the solid — the two-dimensional layout of its faces — helps ensure no face is omitted or double-counted. The lateral surface area (LSA) or curved surface area excludes the top and bottom faces; for a cuboid, LSA = 2h(l + b), representing the area of the four walls of a room. This distinction is critical in cost estimation problems: painting the walls uses LSA, while wrapping a box uses TSA. The diagonal of a cuboid, connecting opposite vertices, has length √(l² + b² + h²), derived by applying the Pythagorean theorem twice (first on the base diagonal, then on the space diagonal). For a cube, the body diagonal is a√3.

5.3 Diagonals of a Cuboid and the Longest Rod Problem

The body diagonal of a cuboid is the longest straight line that can fit inside the solid. This leads to the classic "longest rod" problem: given the dimensions of a room (a cuboid), find the length of the longest rod that can be placed inside. The answer is simply the body diagonal √(l² + b² + h²). If the rod must lie flat on the floor, the answer is the floor diagonal √(l² + b²). If the rod must stand vertically, the answer is the height h. Candidates must read the constraint carefully. The face diagonals are √(l² + b²), √(b² + h²), and √(h² + l²); these are rarely asked directly but are intermediate steps in deriving the body diagonal.

5.4 Conversion of Solids: Melting and Recasting

Problems involving the melting of a solid (sphere, cylinder, cone) and recasting it into another solid (wire, smaller spheres, cubes) rely on the principle of volume conservation. The total volume before melting equals the total volume after recasting, assuming no material loss. For example, a sphere of radius R melted into a wire of radius r (a long cylinder) gives (4/3)πR³ = πr²h, where h is the length of the wire. If melted into n identical smaller spheres of radius r, then (4/3)πR³ = n × (4/3)πr³, simplifying to R³ = nr³. The surface area, however, is not conserved; it usually increases when a large solid is divided into smaller ones because the total surface area of n small spheres is n × 4πr² = n × 4π(R/∛n)² = 4πR² × n^(1/3), which is greater than the original 4πR² for n > 1. This increase in surface area has practical implications in chemistry (reaction rates) and is occasionally tested in advanced quantitative questions.

6. Cylinders, Cones, and Spheres: Curved Surfaces and Volumes

6.1 The Right Circular Cylinder: Curved and Total Surface Area

A right circular cylinder is generated by revolving a rectangle about one of its sides. It has a curved surface and two circular flat faces (top and bottom). The curved surface area (CSA) is the area of the rectangle formed by unrolling the curved surface; its dimensions are the height h and the circumference of the base 2πr, giving CSA = 2πrh. The total surface area (TSA) adds the areas of the two circular ends: TSA = 2πrh + 2πr² = 2πr(h + r). The volume is the base area times height: V = πr²h. A hollow cylinder (pipe) has an external radius R and internal radius r. Its material volume is π(R² − r²)h, its curved surface area is 2πh(R + r) (sum of outer and inner curved surfaces), and its total surface area includes the areas of the two annular rings at the ends: 2π(R² − r²). The thickness of the pipe is R − r.

6.2 The Right Circular Cone: Slant Height and its Geometric Derivation

A right circular cone is generated by revolving a right triangle about one of its legs. The radius r and height h are the legs, and the slant height l is the hypotenuse, satisfying l² = r² + h² (l = √(r² + h²)). The curved surface area (CSA) is the area of the sector formed by unrolling the cone; the radius of this sector is l, and its arc length is the base circumference 2πr. The sector area formula gives CSA = πrl. The total surface area (TSA) adds the base area: TSA = πrl + πr² = πr(l + r). The volume of a cone is exactly one-third the volume of a cylinder with the same base and height: V = (1/3)πr²h. This 1:3 ratio is a fundamental geometric fact often tested by asking for the volume of the remaining solid when a cone is removed from a cylinder (a cylindrical hole with a conical bottom, for instance). The frustum of a cone is the portion remaining after the top is cut off by a plane parallel to the base. If the original cone has height H and radius R, and the removed top has height h and radius r, then by similarity r/R = h/H. The frustum volume is (1/3)πH(R² + Rr + r²), and its curved surface area is πl(R + r), where l = √((R − r)² + (H − h)²) is the slant height of the frustum.

6.3 The Sphere and Hemisphere: Surface Area and Volume

A sphere is the set of all points in space at a fixed distance (radius r) from a centre. Its surface area is 4πr², and its volume is (4/3)πr³. The surface area formula can be remembered as four times the area of a great circle (πr²). The volume formula shows that the sphere occupies 2/3 of the volume of its circumscribing cylinder (which has radius r and height 2r, volume 2πr³). A hemisphere is half a sphere. Its curved surface area is 2πr² (half of 4πr²). Its total surface area includes the flat circular base: TSA = 2πr² + πr² = 3πr². Its volume is half the sphere's volume: (2/3)πr³. A spherical shell (hollow sphere) with external radius R and internal radius r has material volume (4/3)π(R³ − r³). The thickness is R − r. Problems often combine a hemisphere with a cylinder or cone (e.g., a silo or a toy), requiring the candidate to sum the volumes and the relevant surface areas (curved surfaces only, excluding the joining faces).

6.4 Hollow Cylinders and Spherical Shells: Material Volume

When a solid has a cavity, the volume of the material is the difference between the outer volume and the inner volume. For a hollow cylinder (pipe), Material Volume = π(R² − r²)h. For a spherical shell, Material Volume = (4/3)π(R³ − r³). The weight of the object is then Material Volume × Density. If the object is melted and recast into a solid wire or sphere, the material volume is conserved. A common problem type gives the weight of a hollow sphere and the density of the metal, asking for the thickness. The candidate must compute the material volume from Weight/Density, equate it to (4/3)π(R³ − r³), and solve for R − r, often using the identity R³ − r³ = (R − r)(R² + Rr + r²). Since R and r are close for a thin shell, approximations may be used, but exact algebraic solution is preferred in the examination.

Composite Solids: Combination of Basic Shapes

Many examination problems present a solid that is an assembly of two or more elementary solids — a cylinder capped by hemispheres, a cone mounted on a hemisphere, a cuboid with a cylindrical hole, or a prism with a pyramidal top. The governing principle is additive: the total volume is the sum of the volumes of the constituent parts, while the total surface area is the sum of the exposed surfaces only. Faces that become interior after joining are excluded from the surface area calculation. For a capsule (a cylinder of radius r and height h with a hemisphere of the same radius at each end), the volume is V = πr²h + 2 × (2/3)πr³ = πr²h + (4/3)πr³. The curved surface area is the cylinder’s curved area 2πrh plus the curved areas of the two hemispheres 2 × 2πr² = 4πr²; the flat circular ends of the cylinder are covered, so they do not appear. A toy shaped as a cone surmounted on a hemisphere (common “top” problem) has volume (1/3)πr²hcone + (2/3)πr³ and total surface area = πrl (cone’s curved surface) + 2πr² (hemisphere’s curved surface), where l = √(r² + hcone²). When a cylindrical hole is drilled through a cuboid, the material volume is the cuboid volume minus the cylinder volume; the inner curved surface of the hole adds to the total surface area, while the two circular openings remove area from the cuboid’s faces. Cost problems (painting, polishing, metal plating) require identifying exactly which surfaces are exposed and applying the appropriate rate per unit area. A frequent twist is to give the cost of painting the outer surface of a composite solid and ask for the cost of painting the inner surface of a cavity, forcing the candidate to compute the inner curved area separately.

Frustums of Pyramids and Cones: Volume and Surface Area

A frustum is the portion of a solid (cone or pyramid) that lies between two parallel planes cutting it. For a right circular cone frustum with radii R (lower base) and r (upper base), height h, and slant height l = √((R − r)² + h²), the volume is V = (1/3)πh(R² + Rr + r²). The curved surface area is πl(R + r), and the total surface area adds the areas of the two circular ends: πR² + πr². For a frustum of a right pyramid with base areas A₁ and A₂ and height h, the volume formula generalises to V = (h/3)(A₁ + A₂ + √(A₁A₂)). This is derived by subtracting the volume of the removed top pyramid from the original pyramid, using the similarity ratio of linear dimensions. The lateral surface area of a pyramid frustum is the sum of the areas of its trapezoidal faces; for a regular pyramid frustum (square base of side a, top side b, slant height l), each trapezoid has area (1/2)(a + b)l, and there are four such faces, giving lateral area = 2(a + b)l. The total surface area includes the base and top squares. Problems often combine a frustum with a cylinder or hemisphere (e.g., a bucket with a hemispherical bottom), requiring the candidate to compute the capacity (volume) and the area of metal sheet used (total surface area excluding the open top). The slant height of a cone frustum is frequently tested via the Pythagorean triplet (3,4,5) or (5,12,13) to avoid square roots.

Worked Example: Frustum Bucket with Hemispherical Bottom
A bucket is in the form of a frustum of a cone of height 24 cm with radii of its lower and upper ends as 7 cm and 14 cm respectively. The bottom of the bucket is closed by a hemisphere of radius 7 cm. Find the capacity of the bucket and the area of the metal sheet required to make the bucket (ignore the thickness of the sheet).

Step 1: Volume of frustum. R = 14 cm, r = 7 cm, h = 24 cm.
Vfrustum = (1/3)π × 24 × (14² + 14×7 + 7²) = 8π × (196 + 98 + 49) = 8π × 343 = 2744π cm³.

Step 2: Volume of hemisphere. Radius = 7 cm.
Vhemi = (2/3)π × 7³ = (2/3)π × 343 = (686/3)π cm³ ≈ 228.67π cm³.

Step 3: Total capacity. Vtotal = 2744π + (686/3)π = (8232/3 + 686/3)π = (8918/3)π cm³ ≈ 2972.67π cm³ ≈ 9338 cm³ (using π ≈ 3.14).

Step 4: Slant height of frustum. l = √((14−7)² + 24²) = √(49 + 576) = √625 = 25 cm.

Step 5: Curved surface area of frustum. πl(R + r) = π × 25 × (14 + 7) = 525π cm².

Step 6: Curved surface area of hemisphere. 2πr² = 2π × 49 = 98π cm².

Step 7: Area of metal sheet. The bucket is open at the top, so we do not include the upper circular area. Total sheet area = CSA of frustum + CSA of hemisphere = 525π + 98π = 623π cm² ≈ 1956 cm².

Exam-Day Strategy
When a composite solid includes a frustum, always compute the slant height first; it is needed for both curved surface area and for checking whether the given dimensions form a Pythagorean triplet (which saves time). For capacity questions, add volumes of all closed parts; for sheet area, sum only the exposed curved surfaces and any base that is not open. Write the volume and area formulas in a margin before substituting numbers to avoid missing a term.

Spherical Segments, Zones, and Caps

A spherical cap is the portion of a sphere cut off by a plane. If the sphere has radius R and the cap height (distance from the plane to the top of the sphere) is h, the volume of the cap is V = (πh²/3)(3R − h). The curved surface area of the cap (excluding the base circle) is A = 2πRh. A spherical zone is the portion of a sphere between two parallel planes; its height is the distance between the planes, also denoted h. The curved surface area of a zone depends only on the sphere’s radius and the zone’s height: Azone = 2πRh. The volume of a spherical segment (the solid bounded by two parallel planes) with base radii a and b and height h is V = (πh/6)(3a² + 3b² + h²). If one of the planes passes through the centre, the segment becomes a hemisphere; if one plane is tangent, the segment becomes a cap. A spherical sector is the union of a spherical cap and the cone with its vertex at the sphere’s centre and base as the cap’s base; its volume is V = (2/3)πR²h (where h is the cap height) and its total surface area (curved cap + conical surface) is πR(2h + l) with l = √(R² + a²). These formulas appear in problems involving the volume of a spherical layer (e.g., a bowl of thickness t cut from a sphere) or the area of a zone on a globe. A common examiner trick is to give the radius of the sphere and the distance of the cutting plane from the centre, requiring the candidate to first compute the cap height h = R ± d (where d is the distance from centre to plane) before applying the cap formulas.

Optimization Problems: Maximum Volume Inscribed Solids

Competitive examinations frequently ask for the dimensions of the largest possible cylinder, cone, or sphere that can be inscribed in another solid. For a cylinder inscribed in a sphere of radius R, let the cylinder’s radius be r and half-height be x. The constraint is r² + x² = R². The cylinder volume V = 2πr²x = 2π(R² − x²)x. Maximising V with respect to x (or using AM–GM) gives x = R/√3, r = √(2/3)R, and maximum volume = (4π/3√3)R³. For a cone inscribed in a sphere of radius R, let the cone’s height be h and base radius r. The geometry yields r² = h(2R − h). Volume V = (1/3)πr²h = (1/3)πh²(2R − h). Maximising gives h = 4R/3, r = (2√2/3)R, maximum volume = (32/81)πR³. For a sphere inscribed in a cylinder (cylinder’s height = diameter of sphere), the sphere’s radius is half the cylinder’s height, and the cylinder’s radius equals the sphere’s radius. The ratio of volumes (sphere : cylinder) is 2 : 3. These results are often tested in reverse: given the dimensions of the outer solid, find the maximum volume of the inscribed solid, or given the maximum volume, find the outer solid’s dimensions. Candidates should memorise the optimal ratios: for cylinder in sphere, height : diameter = 2 : √3; for cone in sphere, height : diameter = 4 : 3.

Scaling and Similarity in Three Dimensions

When a solid is scaled by a linear factor k (all lengths multiplied by k), its surface area scales by k² and its volume scales by k³. This principle underlies model problems, weight estimation, and melting-recasting questions. If a model of a ship is built to a scale 1 : 100, the model’s surface area is 1/10⁴ of the actual ship’s surface area, and its volume (hence displacement) is 1/10⁶. If the model weighs 2 kg and is made of the same material, the actual ship would weigh 2 × 10⁶ kg = 2000 tonnes. When a solid is melted and recast into a similar solid (same shape, different size), the volume is conserved, so the linear scale factor is the cube root of the volume ratio. For example, a solid sphere of radius R melted into n identical smaller spheres: each small sphere has radius r = R/∛n. The total surface area of the n small spheres is n × 4πr² = 4πR² × n^(1/3), which is greater than the original surface area for n > 1. This increase is a classic quantitative comparison question. Percentage change problems: if each edge of a cube is increased by 20%, the new volume is (1.2)³ = 1.728 times the original, a 72.8% increase; surface area becomes (1.2)² = 1.44 times, a 44% increase. For a cylinder, if radius increases by 10% and height decreases by 10%, volume changes by factor (1.1)² × 0.9 = 1.089, an 8.9% increase. These multiplicative shortcuts avoid lengthy algebra and are essential for time management.

  • Confusing total surface area of a composite solid with the sum of total surface areas of its parts — always subtract the areas of joining faces.
  • Using the formula for volume of a cone frustum when the solid is a pyramid frustum (or vice versa) — the pyramid formula uses base areas, not radii.
  • Forgetting that the curved surface area of a spherical zone depends only on sphere radius and zone height, not on the radii of the cutting circles.
  • Applying the cylinder-in-sphere maximum volume dimensions to a cone-in-sphere problem — the optimal ratios are different.
  • Assuming surface area scales linearly with volume in melting-recasting problems — area scales as the 2/3 power of volume.
  • Neglecting to convert percentage changes into multiplicative factors before combining them for volume or area.
  • Treating the slant height of a frustum as the difference of radii instead of using the Pythagorean theorem with the vertical height.
