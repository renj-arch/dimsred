Fundamentals of Ratio

1.1 Definition and Notation of Ratio

A ratio expresses the relative size of two quantities of the same kind by showing how many times one quantity contains the other. It is written in the form a:b or as the fraction a/b, where the second term b is never zero because division by zero is undefined. The order of the terms matters; a:b is not the same as b:a unless the two quantities are equal. In practical problems the quantities may be lengths, weights, amounts of money, or any measurable attribute, provided they share the same unit of measurement.

1.2 Properties of Ratio

Ratios possess several algebraic properties that make them convenient for manipulation. Multiplying or dividing both terms of a ratio by the same non‑zero number leaves the ratio unchanged, which is the basis for simplifying ratios to their lowest terms. The sum of the terms of a ratio can be used to split a total quantity into parts that are proportional to the given ratio. If a ratio is expressed as a fraction, the usual rules of fraction arithmetic apply, allowing addition, subtraction, and comparison of ratios after bringing them to a common denominator.

1.3 Comparison of Ratios

To decide which of two ratios is larger, one can cross‑multiply the terms: for ratios a:b and c:d, the inequality a/b > c/d is equivalent to a·d > b·c, provided all terms are positive. This method avoids converting to decimals and is especially useful when the numbers are large. When ratios involve more than two quantities, they can be compared pairwise after making a common term equal, a technique that will be illustrated in the combined ratio problems later in the chapter.

1.4 Ratio in Real World Contexts

Ratios appear in everyday situations such as mixing ingredients in a recipe, scaling a map, or comparing financial metrics like debt‑to‑equity. In each case the ratio captures a relationship that remains constant even when the absolute amounts change. Understanding this invariance helps students recognise ratio problems hidden in word statements that do not explicitly use the word “ratio”.

Proportion and Its Properties

2.1 Definition of Proportion

A proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal. It is written as a:b = c:d or a/b = c/d. The four quantities a, b, c, d are called the terms of the proportion; a and d are the extremes, while b and c are the means. The equality of the two ratios implies a relationship among the four numbers that can be exploited to find an unknown term when the other three are known.

2.2 Fundamental Property of Proportion

The most important property of a proportion is that the product of the extremes equals the product of the means, expressed as a·d = b·c. This follows directly from cross‑multiplication of the equality a/b = c/d. The property provides a simple linear equation for the unknown term and is the foundation for solving all proportion problems, whether they involve direct variation, inverse variation, or continued proportion.

2.3 Types of Proportion

Proportions can be classified as direct, inverse, or continued. In direct proportion, an increase in one quantity causes a proportional increase in the other, so the ratio remains constant. In inverse proportion, an increase in one quantity causes a proportional decrease in the other, which means the product of the two quantities stays constant. Continued proportion occurs when three or more quantities are in proportion such that a:b = b:c = c:d, leading to the relationship b² = a·c, c² = b·d, and so on.

2.4 Applications of Proportion

Proportions are used to solve problems involving scaling, map reading, speed‑time‑distance relationships, and mixture calculations. For instance, if a car travels 150 kilometres in 3 hours, the distance covered in 5 hours can be found by setting up the proportion 150/3 = x/5 and solving for x. The same principle underlies the alligation rule for mixtures, which will be discussed in the next major section.

Worked Example: Finding a Missing Term in a Proportion
If 7:9 = 21:x, find the value of x.
The proportion 7:9 = 21:x means 7/9 = 21/x. By the fundamental property, the product of the extremes equals the product of the means, so 7·x = 9·21. Multiplying 9 by 21 gives 189. Dividing 189 by 7 yields 27. Therefore x = 27.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a proportion contains a variable in the denominator, cross‑multiply immediately to avoid dealing with fractions; the resulting linear equation is always easier to solve under time pressure.

Mixture and Alligation

3.1 Concept of Mixture

A mixture problem involves combining two or more components, each having a distinct value such as price per kilogram, concentration of a solution, or percentage of an alloy, to obtain a final mixture with a specified average value. The quantities of the components are usually unknown and must be determined from the given average. The underlying principle is that the total value of the mixture equals the sum of the values of the individual components, which leads to a linear equation in the unknown quantities.

3.2 Alligation Rule Derivation

The alligation rule is a shortcut derived from the weighted‑average equation. Suppose a cheaper ingredient costs C₁ per unit and a dearer ingredient costs C₂ per unit, with C₁ < C₂. If the mixture’s mean price is M, then the quantity of the cheaper ingredient multiplied by (M – C₁) equals the quantity of the dearer ingredient multiplied by (C₂ – M). Rearranging gives the ratio of quantities as (C₂ – M) : (M – C₁). This ratio is the inverse of the differences between each ingredient’s price and the mean price, which is why the rule is often remembered as “the difference opposite to the ingredient”.

3.3 Replacement Problems

Replacement problems are a special class of mixture questions where a portion of a homogeneous mixture is removed and replaced by another substance, often water, and the process may be repeated. After each operation the concentration of the original component decreases by a constant factor equal to the fraction that remains. If the initial quantity is Q, the fraction removed each time is f, and the operation is performed n times, the remaining quantity of the original component is Q·(1 – f)ⁿ. This formula follows from the fact that each operation leaves (1 – f) of the previous amount untouched.

3.4 Practical Examples

Consider a vessel containing 80 litres of a 40 % alcohol solution. If 20 litres are drawn off and replaced with water, the alcohol concentration drops because the removed portion contains alcohol in the same proportion as the whole. Applying the replacement formula with Q = 80, f = 20/80 = 1/4, and n = 1 gives a remaining alcohol volume of 80·(3/4) = 60 litres, which corresponds to a new concentration of 60/80 = 75 % of the original 40 %, i.e. 30 %. Repeating the operation once more would multiply by another factor of 3/4, yielding 45 litres of alcohol and a concentration of 22.5 %.

Partnership and Profit Sharing

4.1 Simple Partnership

In a simple partnership all partners invest their capitals for the same duration. The profit or loss is divided in the ratio of the capitals contributed. If partner A invests ₹a and partner B invests ₹b for the whole period, the profit‑sharing ratio is a:b. This direct proportionality makes simple partnership problems straightforward applications of ratio concepts.

4.2 Compound Partnership

When partners invest for different lengths of time, the partnership is called compound. The effective capital of each partner is the product of the amount invested and the time for which it remains in the business, usually measured in months. The profit‑sharing ratio then becomes (Capital₁ × Time₁) : (Capital₂ × Time₂) : … . This adjustment ensures that a partner who keeps money in the venture longer receives a proportionally larger share of the profit.

4.3 Profit Distribution with Time Factor

Consider a business where A invests ₹50,000 for 12 months, B invests ₹60,000 for 8 months, and C invests ₹70,000 for 6 months. The effective capitals are 50,000×12 = 600,000; 60,000×8 = 480,000; 70,000×6 = 420,000. The profit‑sharing ratio simplifies to 600:480:420, which reduces to 10:8:7 after dividing by 60. If the total profit is ₹1,25,000, A receives (10/25)×1,25,000 = ₹50,000, B receives ₹40,000, and C receives ₹35,000.

4.4 Special Cases

Special situations arise when a partner joins or leaves mid‑year, when additional capital is introduced, or when a partner withdraws part of the investment. In each case the time‑weighted capital must be computed for every sub‑period and summed to obtain the total effective capital for that partner. The principle remains the same: profit share is proportional to the sum of (capital × time) over all sub‑periods.

Advanced Applications and Problem Solving Strategies

5.1 Combined Ratio Problems

Many examination questions require linking several ratios that share a common term. The standard technique is to equalise the common term by multiplying each ratio by the least common multiple of the two values that the common term takes. Once the common term is identical, the ratios can be concatenated to form a single chain ratio. This method extends naturally to three or more ratios and is essential for solving partnership problems where the profit‑sharing ratio must be derived from multiple investment ratios.

5.2 Multi‑Stage Mixture Problems

Multi‑stage mixture problems involve successive operations such as adding an ingredient, removing a portion, and then adding another ingredient. The key is to track the quantity of each component after every stage using the replacement formula or the alligation rule as appropriate. Because each stage changes the total volume and the composition, it is advisable to work with absolute quantities rather than percentages to avoid cumulative rounding errors.

5.3 Partnership with Changing Capitals

When a partner increases or decreases capital during the partnership period, the timeline must be split at the points of change. For each sub‑period the effective capital is calculated separately and then added. For example, if a partner invests ₹30,000 for the first 4 months, adds ₹10,000 for the next 5 months, and withdraws ₹5,000 for the final 3 months, the total effective capital is 30,000×4 + 40,000×5 + 35,000×3. This sum is then used in the profit‑sharing ratio alongside the other partners’ effective capitals.

5.4 Strategy for Time‑Bound Exams

In a timed test, identify the type of problem quickly: ratio, proportion, mixture, or partnership. For ratio and proportion, write the given equality and cross‑multiply immediately. For mixture, decide whether alligation or the replacement formula is faster; alligation is usually quicker for two‑ingredient problems, while the replacement formula is indispensable for repeated dilution. For partnership, compute effective capitals in a table to avoid missing any time segment. Practising these decision trees reduces hesitation and improves accuracy under pressure.

Mastering these interconnected topics equips you with a versatile toolkit for quantitative reasoning in competitive examinations.

Continued Proportion and Mean Proportional

A continued proportion is a sequence of three or more quantities where the ratio of the first to the second equals the ratio of the second to the third, and so on. For three numbers a, b, c in continued proportion we write a : b = b : c. The defining property is b² = a × c. The middle term b is called the mean proportional (or geometric mean) between a and c. The first term a is the first proportional, the third term c is the third proportional to a and b. If four numbers a, b, c, d satisfy a : b = b : c = c : d, they form a continued proportion of length four; then b² = ac, c² = bd, and ad = bc.

Mean proportionals appear in geometry (the altitude of a right triangle is the mean proportional between the segments of the hypotenuse) and in algebraic manipulation of quadratic equations. To find the mean proportional between two positive numbers p and q, compute √(p×q). The third proportional to p and q is q²/p. The fourth proportional to p, q, r (with p : q = r : x) is x = q×r/p. These formulas are direct consequences of cross‑multiplication and are frequently tested in disguised forms such as “find the number which when added to each of 6, 10, 14, 22 makes them proportional”.

When a problem states that three numbers are in continued proportion and gives either the sum or the product of the extremes, set up the equations b² = ac and the given condition, then solve the resulting quadratic. For example, if the sum of the extremes is 20 and the mean proportional is 8, then a + c = 20 and ac = 64. Solving yields a = 4, c = 16 (or vice‑versa). Recognising the symmetric nature of the equations avoids unnecessary trial‑and‑error.

Key Relationships

  • Mean proportional: √(a×c)
  • Third proportional: c = b²/a
  • Fourth proportional: d = b×c/a
  • Product of extremes = product of means in any proportion

Alligation with Three or More Ingredients

The classic alligation rule handles two ingredients by placing their costs on the left and the mean price in the centre, then taking differences to obtain the mixing ratio. For three or more ingredients the same principle extends through a pairwise or matrix approach. Suppose we have ingredients priced at C₁, C₂, C₃ per unit and we desire a mixture at mean price M. Write the costs in a column, place M to the right, and compute the absolute differences |Cᵢ − M|. The ratio of the quantities is proportional to these differences, but only when the mean lies between the minimum and maximum costs. If M lies outside that range, a feasible mixture does not exist without adding a fourth component or allowing negative quantities.

A systematic method is the alligation matrix. Create a table with rows for each ingredient and two columns: cost and difference from mean. Fill the differences, then the ratio of quantities is the inverse of the differences (larger difference → smaller share). For three ingredients A (₹30), B (₹40), C (₹50) targeting ₹42, the differences are 12, 2, 8. The inverse ratio is 1/12 : 1/2 : 1/8, which simplifies to 2 : 12 : 3 after multiplying by the LCM 24. Thus the required mixing ratio is 2 : 12 : 3. Verify by weighted average: (2×30 + 12×40 + 3×50) / (2+12+3) = (60+480+150)/17 = 690/17 = ₹40.59 — not ₹42. The discrepancy shows that the simple inverse‑difference rule works only when the mean is exactly between two adjacent costs; otherwise a linear combination of two‑ingredient alligations is needed.

The reliable technique for three ingredients is to treat the problem as two successive two‑ingredient alligations. First, combine the cheapest and the dearest to obtain an intermediate price that lies between the remaining ingredient and the target. Then alligate that intermediate with the third ingredient. This yields a unique ratio provided the target price lies within the convex hull of the given prices. The method also generalises to any number of ingredients by iteratively reducing the set.

Step‑by‑Step Procedure

  1. Arrange costs in ascending order.
  2. Check whether the desired mean lies between the minimum and maximum cost.
  3. Pick the two extreme costs and alligate them to produce an intermediate mean that equals the target or lies on the same side of the third cost.
  4. Alligate the intermediate mean with the remaining cost(s) until only the target remains.
  5. Multiply the ratios obtained at each stage to get the final proportion.
Alligation Matrix for Three Ingredients
A grocer has three varieties of tea costing ₹120, ₹150, and ₹180 per kg. In what ratio must he mix them to obtain a blend worth ₹160 per kg?

Step 1: Costs in ascending order: 120, 150, 180. Target 160 lies between 150 and 180.

Step 2: Alligate the extremes 120 and 180 to get an intermediate mean. Differences: |120−160| = 40, |180−160| = 20. Ratio of 120‑tea to 180‑tea = 20 : 40 = 1 : 2. This intermediate mixture has cost (1×120 + 2×180)/3 = 480/3 = ₹160, exactly the target.

Step 3: Since the intermediate already equals the target, the third variety (₹150) is not needed. The final ratio is 1 : 0 : 2, i.e., only the cheapest and dearest in 1 : 2.

If the target were ₹155, the intermediate from 120 and 180 would be (1×120+2×180)/3 = ₹160. Then alligate 160 with 150: differences |160−155| = 5, |150−155| = 5 → ratio 1 : 1. Combine with previous 1 : 2 gives overall ratio (1×1) : (1×1) : (2×1) = 1 : 1 : 2.

Exam‑Day Strategy for Multi‑Ingredient Alligation
When you see three or more costs, first verify that the target lies within the range. If it does, use the two‑stage alligation: pair the extremes, compute the intermediate, then alligate with the middle cost. Write the intermediate ratio clearly; examiners often award partial marks for correct intermediate steps even if the final arithmetic slips.

Partnership Admission, Retirement, and Goodwill Valuation

When a new partner is admitted, the existing partners sacrifice a portion of their profit share. The sacrifice ratio is the difference between the old profit‑sharing ratio and the new ratio for each continuing partner. Conversely, when a partner retires, the remaining partners gain a share; the gaining ratio is the difference between the new and old ratios for the continuing partners. Goodwill, the value of the firm’s reputation, must be valued and adjusted. Three standard valuation methods are:

  • Average Profit Method: Goodwill = Average annual profit × Number of years’ purchase. Average profit is computed after adjusting for abnormal items and partner remuneration.
  • Super Profit Method: Super profit = Average profit − Normal profit (where normal profit = Capital employed × Normal rate of return). Goodwill = Super profit × Number of years’ purchase.
  • Capitalisation Method: Goodwill = (Average profit × 100 / Normal rate of return) − Capital employed. Alternatively, goodwill = Super profit × 100 / Normal rate of return.

On admission, the incoming partner may bring goodwill in cash or kind. If goodwill is brought in cash, it is credited to the old partners in their sacrifice ratio. If goodwill is not brought in, the new partner’s capital account is debited with his share of goodwill and the old partners are credited in the sacrifice ratio. On retirement, the retiring partner’s share of goodwill is debited to the continuing partners in their gaining ratio and credited to the retiring partner’s account. All adjustments are made through the partners’ capital accounts; the firm’s books do not show a separate goodwill account unless the partnership deed requires it.

Illustrative Adjustment

Assume A and B share profits 3 : 2. C is admitted for 1/5 share, acquiring 1/10 from A and 1/10 from B. New ratio = A: 3/5 − 1/10 = 5/10, B: 2/5 − 1/10 = 3/10, C: 2/10 → 5 : 3 : 2. Sacrifice ratio = 1 : 1. If goodwill is valued at ₹50,000 and C brings ₹20,000 cash for goodwill, the remaining ₹30,000 is adjusted by debiting C’s capital ₹30,000 and crediting A and B ₹15,000 each.

Profit Sharing with Interest on Capital and Partner Salary

Partnership deeds often provide for interest on capital at an agreed rate and salary or commission to working partners. These items are appropriations of profit, not expenses. The profit‑and‑loss appropriation account is prepared after the profit‑and‑loss account. The sequence is:

  1. Calculate net profit from the profit‑and‑loss account.
  2. Charge interest on each partner’s opening capital (and on additional capital for the period it was employed) at the agreed rate.
  3. Allocate salary/commission to entitled partners.
  4. The residual profit (or loss) is distributed in the profit‑sharing ratio.

If the residual is a loss, interest and salary are still allowed unless the deed states they are payable only out of profits. In that case, the loss is shared in the profit‑sharing ratio after reversing the appropriations. The effective capital for interest calculation must reflect the time‑weighted capital, exactly as in compound partnership. For example, if A’s capital is ₹1,00,000 for 12 months and B adds ₹50,000 after 6 months, interest for A = 1,00,000 × 12/12 × r, for B = 50,000 × 6/12 × r.

Worked Example

Partners X and Y share profits 2 : 1. Capitals: X ₹2,00,000, Y ₹1,00,000. Deed allows 10% interest on capital and ₹12,000 annual salary to X. Net profit before appropriations = ₹1,20,000. Interest: X = 20,000, Y = 10,000. Salary to X = 12,000. Total appropriations = 42,000. Residual profit = 78,000. Distributed: X = 52,000, Y = 26,000. Final shares: X = 20,000 + 12,000 + 52,000 = ₹84,000; Y = 10,000 + 26,000 = ₹36,000.

Discounted Cash Flow Approach to Partnership Valuation

In advanced partnership problems, especially those involving buy‑out or dissolution, the value of a partner’s share is the present value of expected future profit distributions. The discounted cash flow (DCF) method estimates the stream of distributable profits for each future year, discounts them at an appropriate rate (often the firm’s weighted average cost of capital or a risk‑adjusted rate), and sums the present values. The steps are:

  1. Project the firm’s maintainable profit for each forecast year, adjusting for expected growth, capital expenditure, and working‑capital changes.
  2. Determine each partner’s share of the projected profit using the agreed profit‑sharing ratio (which may change if capital contributions change).
  3. Select a discount rate reflecting the risk of the partnership’s cash flows.
  4. Compute the present value of each partner’s profit share: PV = Σ (Profit_share_t / (1 + r)ᵗ).
  5. Add the present value of any terminal value (e.g., net assets on dissolution) if applicable.

This approach is superior to simple capital‑account valuation when profits are expected to grow or when the partnership has significant intangible assets. In examinations, a simplified version may appear: “A partner’s share of profit is ₹50,000 per year for the next 5 years. If the discount rate is 10%, find the present value of his share.” The answer uses the annuity formula PV = 50,000 × [1 − (1.10)⁻⁵] / 0.10 ≈ ₹1,89,540. Understanding the annuity factor table (or the formula) is essential for quick computation.

Key Points for Exams

  • DCF valuation is only required when the problem explicitly mentions “present value”, “discount rate”, or “buy‑out value”.
  • If the profit share is constant, use the annuity factor; if it grows at a constant rate g, use the growing annuity formula PV = C × [1 − ((1+g)/(1+r))ⁿ] / (r − g).
  • Always state the assumption that the discount rate remains constant over the projection horizon.
  • Confusing sacrifice ratio with gaining ratio when a partner is admitted versus when a partner retires.
  • Applying the simple inverse‑difference alligation rule to three ingredients without checking whether the target price lies between the extremes.
  • Forgetting to time‑weight capital when computing interest on capital in a compound partnership.
  • Treating interest on capital and partner salary as expenses deducted before net profit instead of appropriations after net profit.
  • Using the wrong discount rate (e.g., cost of debt instead of weighted average cost of capital) in DCF valuation of a partnership share.
  • Neglecting to adjust goodwill for the sacrifice/gaining ratio, leading to incorrect capital account balances on admission or retirement.
  • Assuming the mean proportional is always the arithmetic mean rather than the geometric mean.

Weighted Average and Harmonic Mean in Ratio Problems

When several ratios share a common term, the combined effect is often expressed through a weighted average rather than a simple arithmetic mean. Suppose three vessels contain mixtures of milk and water in the ratios 2 : 1, 3 : 2 and 4 : 3 respectively. If equal volumes are taken from each vessel, the overall milk‑to‑water ratio is not the arithmetic mean of the three ratios; it is the ratio of the sum of milk parts to the sum of water parts, each weighted by the volume taken. In general, for quantities q₁, q₂, …, qₙ mixed in ratios a₁ : b₁, a₂ : b₂, …, aₙ : bₙ, the resultant ratio is (Σ qᵢ aᵢ) : (Σ qᵢ bᵢ). This principle extends to any situation where a composite rate is required, such as average speed over equal distances (harmonic mean) or average price per unit when quantities differ.

The harmonic mean appears naturally when the same distance is covered at different speeds. If a vehicle travels a distance d at speed v₁ and the same distance at speed v₂, the average speed for the whole journey is 2 v₁ v₂ / (v₁ + v₂), which is the harmonic mean of the two speeds. More generally, for n equal distances covered at speeds v₁, v₂, …, vₙ, the average speed is n / (Σ 1 / vᵢ). This is exactly the weighted harmonic mean with equal weights. In ratio language, speed is distance : time; equal distances mean the distance terms are identical, so the combined ratio of total distance to total time reduces to the harmonic mean of the individual speed ratios.

A frequent examination twist combines weighted arithmetic and harmonic means in a single problem. For instance, a trader mixes two varieties of rice costing ₹30 /kg and ₹40 /kg in the ratio 3 : 2, then sells the mixture at a price that yields a 20 % profit on the cost price of the mixture. The cost price per kilogram of the mixture is the weighted arithmetic mean (3 × 30 + 2 × 40) / 5 = ₹34. The selling price is 1.2 × 34 = ₹40.8. If the same trader instead mixes the rice so that the selling price per kilogram equals the harmonic mean of the two cost prices, the required mixing ratio changes. Solving 2 × 30 × 40 / (30 + 40) = ₹34.29 shows that the harmonic mean is lower than the arithmetic mean, so a higher proportion of the cheaper rice is needed to achieve that selling price. Recognising which mean applies — arithmetic for cost‑price averaging, harmonic for rate averaging — is the key to avoiding the common trap of using the wrong mean.

Another advanced scenario involves successive replacements where the quantity removed each time is not constant but follows a ratio. If a container holds 100 L of pure milk and 20 L is replaced by water, then 30 L of the new mixture is replaced by water, the concentration after each step is multiplied by a factor (1 − removed / total). The overall concentration after k steps is the product of these factors. When the removed volumes are in a fixed ratio, the product can be expressed as a single exponent, leading to a compact formula. Mastery of these patterns allows rapid solution of multi‑stage mixture problems without iterative calculation.

Alligation with Variable Concentrations and Replacement Cycles

Classical alligation assumes two ingredients of fixed concentration are mixed to obtain a target concentration. In many competitive‑exam problems the concentrations themselves vary because of repeated replacement or because the ingredients are not pure but already mixtures. Consider a 60 L cask containing 40 % alcohol. Every day 10 L of the mixture is drawn off and replaced with pure water. After the first replacement the alcohol volume becomes 60 × 0.4 × (1 − 10 / 60) = 20 L, i.e. 33.33 % alcohol. After the second replacement the same factor applies again, giving 20 × (1 − 10 / 60) = 16.67 L alcohol, or 27.78 %. After n days the alcohol volume is 24 × (5 / 6)ⁿ. The concentration after n cycles is therefore 0.4 × (5 / 6)ⁿ. This exponential decay is a direct consequence of the constant‑fraction replacement rule.

When the replacement volume changes each cycle, the factor is no longer constant. Suppose the first replacement removes 10 L, the second 15 L, the third 20 L, each time refilling with water. The concentration after three cycles is 0.4 × (1 − 10 / 60) × (1 − 15 / 60) × (1 − 20 / 60). The product of distinct factors can be evaluated quickly if the fractions are simplified: (5 / 6) × (3 / 4) × (2 / 3) = 5 / 12, giving a final concentration of 0.4 × 5 / 12 ≈ 16.67 %. Recognising that the overall factor is the product of (1 − rᵢ) where rᵢ is the fraction removed at step i turns a seemingly iterative problem into a single multiplication.

A more subtle variation mixes two solutions of different concentrations, then subjects the mixture to a replacement cycle. For example, 30 L of 20 % acid is mixed with 20 L of 50 % acid. The initial concentration is (30 × 0.2 + 20 × 0.5) / 50 = 0.32. If 10 L of this mixture is replaced by water, the new concentration becomes 0.32 × (1 − 10 / 50) = 0.256. If the problem asks for the volume of water to be added (instead of replacement) to achieve a target concentration, the alligation rule applies to the final mixture: water (0 % acid) and the 32 % mixture must be combined to give, say, 20 % acid. The ratio of mixture to water is (20 − 0) : (32 − 20) = 20 : 12 = 5 : 3. Hence 5 parts of the 32 % mixture require 3 parts water; with 50 L mixture, water needed = 50 × 3 / 5 = 30 L. Combining alligation with replacement factors is a hallmark of high‑difficulty questions.

Examiners also test the reverse process: given the final concentration after a known number of equal‑volume replacements, find the original concentration. If after 3 replacements of 10 L each from a 60 L cask the alcohol concentration is 12.5 %, the original concentration C satisfies C × (5 / 6)³ = 0.125, so C = 0.125 ÷ (125 / 216) = 0.216, i.e. 21.6 %. Solving for C requires only the exponent rule and basic algebra, not trial and error.

Technique: Combined Alligation‑Replacement
A 80 L vessel contains a mixture of milk and water in the ratio 3 : 1. First, 20 L of the mixture is removed and replaced with water. Then, 10 L of the new mixture is removed and replaced with pure milk. Find the final ratio of milk to water.
Initial milk = 80 × 3 / 4 = 60 L, water = 20 L.
After first replacement (remove 20 L, add water): fraction removed = 20 / 80 = 1 / 4. Milk becomes 60 × (1 − 1 / 4) = 45 L. Water becomes 20 × (1 − 1 / 4) + 20 = 15 + 20 = 35 L.
After second replacement (remove 10 L, add milk): fraction removed = 10 / 80 = 1 / 8. Milk before addition = 45 × (1 − 1 / 8) = 45 × 7 / 8 = 39.375 L. Add 10 L milk → 49.375 L. Water = 35 × (1 − 1 / 8) = 35 × 7 / 8 = 30.625 L.
Final ratio milk : water = 49.375 : 30.625 = multiply by 8 → 395 : 245 = divide by 5 → 79 : 49.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem mixes alligation with successive replacements, write down the concentration after each step as a product of (1 − fraction removed). Keep the total volume constant; only the component volumes change. If the replacement liquid is not water but another mixture, treat its concentration as the “added” concentration and apply the weighted‑average formula for that step. This systematic approach avoids arithmetic errors and fits neatly into the rough‑work margin.

Partnership Valuation Using Goodwill and Super Profit Method

Beyond the discounted‑cash‑flow approach, partnership valuation in examinations often relies on the goodwill‑based super‑profit method. Goodwill represents the value of a firm’s reputation, customer base, and other intangible assets that enable it to earn profits above the normal return on capital employed. The normal profit is calculated as capital employed × normal rate of return (expressed as a decimal). Super profit is the excess of average maintainable profit over normal profit. Goodwill is then valued at a certain number of years’ purchase of super profit, typically given in the problem (e.g., “goodwill is valued at 3 years’ purchase of super profit”).

Consider a firm with capital employed of ₹10,00,000 and a normal rate of return of 12 %. The average profit of the last five years, after adjusting for abnormal items, is ₹2,00,000. Normal profit = 10,00,000 × 0.12 = ₹1,20,000. Super profit = 2,00,000 − 1,20,000 = ₹80,000. If goodwill is 3 years’ purchase, goodwill = 3 × 80,000 = ₹2,40,000. The total value of the firm = capital employed + goodwill = ₹12,40,000. Each partner’s share is then allocated according to the profit‑sharing ratio, unless the partnership deed specifies a different capital‑sharing ratio for valuation purposes.

A common twist introduces a change in profit‑sharing ratio at the time of admission or retirement. Suppose partners A and B share profits 3 : 2. They admit C for a 1 / 5 share, which he acquires 1 / 10 from A and 1 / 10 from B. The new ratio becomes A = 3 / 5 − 1 / 10 = 5 / 10, B = 2 / 5 − 1 / 10 = 3 / 10, C = 2 / 10. Goodwill brought in by C is credited to A and B in their sacrificing ratio (1 : 1). If goodwill is valued at ₹2,00,000 and C brings ₹40,000 as his share of goodwill (1 / 5 of total goodwill), the journal entry debits Cash/Bank ₹40,000 and credits A’s Capital ₹20,000 and B’s Capital ₹20,000. The remaining goodwill (₹1,60,000) is not recorded unless the deed requires full goodwill to appear in the books.

Another variation uses the capitalisation method: the value of the firm = average profit × 100 / normal rate of return. Goodwill = value of firm − capital employed. Using the same numbers, value of firm = 2,00,000 × 100 / 12 = ₹16,66,667. Goodwill = 16,66,667 − 10,00,000 = ₹6,66,667. This method yields a different goodwill figure because it capitalises the entire average profit, not just the super profit. Examiners may ask to compute goodwill by both methods and comment on the difference, testing conceptual clarity.

When a partner retires, the gaining ratio (the ratio in which the remaining partners acquire the retiring partner’s share) determines how the retiring partner’s goodwill share is debited to the continuing partners’ capital accounts. If the retiring partner’s share of goodwill is ₹1,00,000 and the gaining ratio is 2 : 1, the continuing partners’ capitals are debited ₹66,667 and ₹33,333 respectively. The retiring partner’s capital account is then settled by cash payment or loan account. Mastery of sacrificing vs. gaining ratio, and the correct allocation of goodwill, is essential for partnership valuation questions.

Ratio and Proportion in Time‑Speed‑Distance and Work Problems

Time‑speed‑distance (TSD) and work‑time problems are fundamentally ratio problems. Speed is distance : time; if distance is constant, speed and time are inversely proportional. If time is constant, distance and speed are directly proportional. This duality allows the use of proportion tables to solve complex multi‑segment journeys without algebraic equations. For example, a train covers 300 km at 60 km/h, then 200 km at 80 km/h. The total time = 300 / 60 + 200 / 80 = 5 + 2.5 = 7.5 h. The average speed = total distance / total time = 500 / 7.5 = 66.67 km/h. Recognising that the harmonic mean of the two speeds weighted by distances gives the same result (2 × 60 × 80 / (60 + 80) = 68.57 km/h only when distances are equal) prevents the common mistake of averaging speeds arithmetically.

In work problems, the rate of work is work : time. If A can complete a job in 10 days and B in 15 days, their rates are 1 / 10 and 1 / 15 of the job per day. Combined rate = 1 / 10 + 1 / 15 = 1 / 6, so together they finish in 6 days. When efficiencies are given in ratios (e.g., A : B : C = 3 : 2 : 1), the time taken by each is inversely proportional to the efficiency ratio. If the total work is 60 units, A does 3 × k units per day, B 2 × k, C 1 × k. The constant k is found from the given total time or from one worker’s known time. This ratio‑based approach scales easily to problems where workers join or leave at different times.

A frequent examiner pattern combines TSD with work concepts: a pipe fills a tank in 8 h, another empties it in 12 h. If both are opened together, the net filling rate = 1 / 8 − 1 / 12 = 1 / 24, so the tank fills in 24 h. If a third pipe with a filling rate in the ratio 2 : 3 relative to the first pipe is added, its rate = 2 / 3 × 1 / 8 = 1 / 12. The new net rate = 1 / 8 + 1 / 12 − 1 / 12 = 1 / 8, so the tank fills in 8 h. The ratio of rates simplifies the arithmetic and highlights the inverse relationship between rate and time.

Advanced problems introduce variable rates: a worker’s efficiency drops by 20 % after 4 h of continuous work. If the normal rate is 1 / 10 job per hour, the first 4 h complete 4 / 10 = 0.4 of the job. The reduced rate is 0.8 × 1 / 10 = 1 / 12.5 per hour. The remaining 0.6 job takes 0.6 ÷ (1 / 12.5) = 7.5 h. Total time = 11.5 h. Treating the efficiency change as a ratio (new : old = 4 : 5) allows quick computation without decimals: time for remaining work = (remaining work) × (old time per unit) × (5 / 4). This ratio‑scaling technique is a hallmark of high‑scoring candidates.

  • Confusing arithmetic mean with harmonic mean when averaging speeds over equal distances.
  • Applying the simple alligation rule to a replacement problem without adjusting for the changing total volume.
  • Using the sacrificing ratio instead of the gaining ratio when a partner retires, or vice‑versa.
  • Treating goodwill brought in by a new partner as revenue instead of a capital adjustment.
  • Forgetting to convert efficiency ratios to time ratios (inverse proportion) in work‑time problems.
  • Assuming the normal rate of return applies to the book value of capital rather than the capital employed after revaluation.
  • Neglecting to weight the alligation differences by the actual quantities mixed when the mixture volumes are unequal.
