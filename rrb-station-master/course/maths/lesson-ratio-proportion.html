Fundamentals of Ratio

1.1 Definition and Notation of Ratio

A ratio expresses the relative size of two quantities of the same kind by showing how many times one quantity contains the other. It is written in the form a:b or as the fraction a/b, where the second term b is never zero because division by zero is undefined. The order of the terms matters; a:b is not the same as b:a unless the two quantities are equal. In practical problems the quantities may be lengths, weights, amounts of money, or any measurable attribute, provided they share the same unit of measurement.

1.2 Properties of Ratio

Ratios possess several algebraic properties that make them convenient for manipulation. Multiplying or dividing both terms of a ratio by the same non‑zero number leaves the ratio unchanged, which is the basis for simplifying ratios to their lowest terms. The sum of the terms of a ratio can be used to split a total quantity into parts that are proportional to the given ratio. If a ratio is expressed as a fraction, the usual rules of fraction arithmetic apply, allowing addition, subtraction, and comparison of ratios after bringing them to a common denominator.

1.3 Comparison of Ratios

To decide which of two ratios is larger, one can cross‑multiply the terms: for ratios a:b and c:d, the inequality a/b > c/d is equivalent to a·d > b·c, provided all terms are positive. This method avoids converting to decimals and is especially useful when the numbers are large. When ratios involve more than two quantities, they can be compared pairwise after making a common term equal, a technique that will be illustrated in the combined ratio problems later in the chapter.

1.4 Ratio in Real World Contexts

Ratios appear in everyday situations such as mixing ingredients in a recipe, scaling a map, or comparing financial metrics like debt‑to‑equity. In each case the ratio captures a relationship that remains constant even when the absolute amounts change. Understanding this invariance helps students recognise ratio problems hidden in word statements that do not explicitly use the word “ratio”.

Proportion and Its Properties

2.1 Definition of Proportion

A proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal. It is written as a:b = c:d or a/b = c/d. The four quantities a, b, c, d are called the terms of the proportion; a and d are the extremes, while b and c are the means. The equality of the two ratios implies a relationship among the four numbers that can be exploited to find an unknown term when the other three are known.

2.2 Fundamental Property of Proportion

The most important property of a proportion is that the product of the extremes equals the product of the means, expressed as a·d = b·c. This follows directly from cross‑multiplication of the equality a/b = c/d. The property provides a simple linear equation for the unknown term and is the foundation for solving all proportion problems, whether they involve direct variation, inverse variation, or continued proportion.

2.3 Types of Proportion

Proportions can be classified as direct, inverse, or continued. In direct proportion, an increase in one quantity causes a proportional increase in the other, so the ratio remains constant. In inverse proportion, an increase in one quantity causes a proportional decrease in the other, which means the product of the two quantities stays constant. Continued proportion occurs when three or more quantities are in proportion such that a:b = b:c = c:d, leading to the relationship b² = a·c, c² = b·d, and so on.

2.4 Applications of Proportion

Proportions are used to solve problems involving scaling, map reading, speed‑time‑distance relationships, and mixture calculations. For instance, if a car travels 150 kilometres in 3 hours, the distance covered in 5 hours can be found by setting up the proportion 150/3 = x/5 and solving for x. The same principle underlies the alligation rule for mixtures, which will be discussed in the next major section.

Worked Example: Finding a Missing Term in a Proportion
If 7:9 = 21:x, find the value of x.
The proportion 7:9 = 21:x means 7/9 = 21/x. By the fundamental property, the product of the extremes equals the product of the means, so 7·x = 9·21. Multiplying 9 by 21 gives 189. Dividing 189 by 7 yields 27. Therefore x = 27.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a proportion contains a variable in the denominator, cross‑multiply immediately to avoid dealing with fractions; the resulting linear equation is always easier to solve under time pressure.

Mixture and Alligation

3.1 Concept of Mixture

A mixture problem involves combining two or more components, each having a distinct value such as price per kilogram, concentration of a solution, or percentage of an alloy, to obtain a final mixture with a specified average value. The quantities of the components are usually unknown and must be determined from the given average. The underlying principle is that the total value of the mixture equals the sum of the values of the individual components, which leads to a linear equation in the unknown quantities.

3.2 Alligation Rule Derivation

The alligation rule is a shortcut derived from the weighted‑average equation. Suppose a cheaper ingredient costs C₁ per unit and a dearer ingredient costs C₂ per unit, with C₁ < C₂. If the mixture’s mean price is M, then the quantity of the cheaper ingredient multiplied by (M – C₁) equals the quantity of the dearer ingredient multiplied by (C₂ – M). Rearranging gives the ratio of quantities as (C₂ – M) : (M – C₁). This ratio is the inverse of the differences between each ingredient’s price and the mean price, which is why the rule is often remembered as “the difference opposite to the ingredient”.

3.3 Replacement Problems

Replacement problems are a special class of mixture questions where a portion of a homogeneous mixture is removed and replaced by another substance, often water, and the process may be repeated. After each operation the concentration of the original component decreases by a constant factor equal to the fraction that remains. If the initial quantity is Q, the fraction removed each time is f, and the operation is performed n times, the remaining quantity of the original component is Q·(1 – f)ⁿ. This formula follows from the fact that each operation leaves (1 – f) of the previous amount untouched.

3.4 Practical Examples

Consider a vessel containing 80 litres of a 40 % alcohol solution. If 20 litres are drawn off and replaced with water, the alcohol concentration drops because the removed portion contains alcohol in the same proportion as the whole. Applying the replacement formula with Q = 80, f = 20/80 = 1/4, and n = 1 gives a remaining alcohol volume of 80·(3/4) = 60 litres, which corresponds to a new concentration of 60/80 = 75 % of the original 40 %, i.e. 30 %. Repeating the operation once more would multiply by another factor of 3/4, yielding 45 litres of alcohol and a concentration of 22.5 %.

Partnership and Profit Sharing

4.1 Simple Partnership

In a simple partnership all partners invest their capitals for the same duration. The profit or loss is divided in the ratio of the capitals contributed. If partner A invests ₹a and partner B invests ₹b for the whole period, the profit‑sharing ratio is a:b. This direct proportionality makes simple partnership problems straightforward applications of ratio concepts.

4.2 Compound Partnership

When partners invest for different lengths of time, the partnership is called compound. The effective capital of each partner is the product of the amount invested and the time for which it remains in the business, usually measured in months. The profit‑sharing ratio then becomes (Capital₁ × Time₁) : (Capital₂ × Time₂) : … . This adjustment ensures that a partner who keeps money in the venture longer receives a proportionally larger share of the profit.

4.3 Profit Distribution with Time Factor

Consider a business where A invests ₹50,000 for 12 months, B invests ₹60,000 for 8 months, and C invests ₹70,000 for 6 months. The effective capitals are 50,000×12 = 600,000; 60,000×8 = 480,000; 70,000×6 = 420,000. The profit‑sharing ratio simplifies to 600:480:420, which reduces to 10:8:7 after dividing by 60. If the total profit is ₹1,25,000, A receives (10/25)×1,25,000 = ₹50,000, B receives ₹40,000, and C receives ₹35,000.

4.4 Special Cases

Special situations arise when a partner joins or leaves mid‑year, when additional capital is introduced, or when a partner withdraws part of the investment. In each case the time‑weighted capital must be computed for every sub‑period and summed to obtain the total effective capital for that partner. The principle remains the same: profit share is proportional to the sum of (capital × time) over all sub‑periods.

Advanced Applications and Problem Solving Strategies

5.1 Combined Ratio Problems

Many examination questions require linking several ratios that share a common term. The standard technique is to equalise the common term by multiplying each ratio by the least common multiple of the two values that the common term takes. Once the common term is identical, the ratios can be concatenated to form a single chain ratio. This method extends naturally to three or more ratios and is essential for solving partnership problems where the profit‑sharing ratio must be derived from multiple investment ratios.

5.2 Multi‑Stage Mixture Problems

Multi‑stage mixture problems involve successive operations such as adding an ingredient, removing a portion, and then adding another ingredient. The key is to track the quantity of each component after every stage using the replacement formula or the alligation rule as appropriate. Because each stage changes the total volume and the composition, it is advisable to work with absolute quantities rather than percentages to avoid cumulative rounding errors.

5.3 Partnership with Changing Capitals

When a partner increases or decreases capital during the partnership period, the timeline must be split at the points of change. For each sub‑period the effective capital is calculated separately and then added. For example, if a partner invests ₹30,000 for the first 4 months, adds ₹10,000 for the next 5 months, and withdraws ₹5,000 for the final 3 months, the total effective capital is 30,000×4 + 40,000×5 + 35,000×3. This sum is then used in the profit‑sharing ratio alongside the other partners’ effective capitals.

5.4 Strategy for Time‑Bound Exams

In a timed test, identify the type of problem quickly: ratio, proportion, mixture, or partnership. For ratio and proportion, write the given equality and cross‑multiply immediately. For mixture, decide whether alligation or the replacement formula is faster; alligation is usually quicker for two‑ingredient problems, while the replacement formula is indispensable for repeated dilution. For partnership, compute effective capitals in a table to avoid missing any time segment. Practising these decision trees reduces hesitation and improves accuracy under pressure.

Mastering these interconnected topics equips you with a versatile toolkit for quantitative reasoning in competitive examinations.

Continued Proportion and Mean Proportional

A continued proportion is a sequence of three or more quantities where the ratio of the first to the second equals the ratio of the second to the third, and so on. For three numbers a, b, c in continued proportion we write a : b = b : c. The defining property is b² = a × c. The middle term b is called the mean proportional (or geometric mean) between a and c. The first term a is the first proportional, the third term c is the third proportional to a and b. If four numbers a, b, c, d satisfy a : b = b : c = c : d, they form a continued proportion of length four; then b² = ac, c² = bd, and ad = bc.

Mean proportionals appear in geometry (the altitude of a right triangle is the mean proportional between the segments of the hypotenuse) and in algebraic manipulation of quadratic equations. To find the mean proportional between two positive numbers p and q, compute √(p×q). The third proportional to p and q is q²/p. The fourth proportional to p, q, r (with p : q = r : x) is x = q×r/p. These formulas are direct consequences of cross‑multiplication and are frequently tested in disguised forms such as “find the number which when added to each of 6, 10, 14, 22 makes them proportional”.

When a problem states that three numbers are in continued proportion and gives either the sum or the product of the extremes, set up the equations b² = ac and the given condition, then solve the resulting quadratic. For example, if the sum of the extremes is 20 and the mean proportional is 8, then a + c = 20 and ac = 64. Solving yields a = 4, c = 16 (or vice‑versa). Recognising the symmetric nature of the equations avoids unnecessary trial‑and‑error.

Key Relationships

  • Mean proportional: √(a×c)
  • Third proportional: c = b²/a
  • Fourth proportional: d = b×c/a
  • Product of extremes = product of means in any proportion

Alligation with Three or More Ingredients

The classic alligation rule handles two ingredients by placing their costs on the left and the mean price in the centre, then taking differences to obtain the mixing ratio. For three or more ingredients the same principle extends through a pairwise or matrix approach. Suppose we have ingredients priced at C₁, C₂, C₃ per unit and we desire a mixture at mean price M. Write the costs in a column, place M to the right, and compute the absolute differences |Cᵢ − M|. The ratio of the quantities is proportional to these differences, but only when the mean lies between the minimum and maximum costs. If M lies outside that range, a feasible mixture does not exist without adding a fourth component or allowing negative quantities.

A systematic method is the alligation matrix. Create a table with rows for each ingredient and two columns: cost and difference from mean. Fill the differences, then the ratio of quantities is the inverse of the differences (larger difference → smaller share). For three ingredients A (₹30), B (₹40), C (₹50) targeting ₹42, the differences are 12, 2, 8. The inverse ratio is 1/12 : 1/2 : 1/8, which simplifies to 2 : 12 : 3 after multiplying by the LCM 24. Thus the required mixing ratio is 2 : 12 : 3. Verify by weighted average: (2×30 + 12×40 + 3×50) / (2+12+3) = (60+480+150)/17 = 690/17 = ₹40.59 — not ₹42. The discrepancy shows that the simple inverse‑difference rule works only when the mean is exactly between two adjacent costs; otherwise a linear combination of two‑ingredient alligations is needed.

The reliable technique for three ingredients is to treat the problem as two successive two‑ingredient alligations. First, combine the cheapest and the dearest to obtain an intermediate price that lies between the remaining ingredient and the target. Then alligate that intermediate with the third ingredient. This yields a unique ratio provided the target price lies within the convex hull of the given prices. The method also generalises to any number of ingredients by iteratively reducing the set.

Step‑by‑Step Procedure

  1. Arrange costs in ascending order.
  2. Check whether the desired mean lies between the minimum and maximum cost.
  3. Pick the two extreme costs and alligate them to produce an intermediate mean that equals the target or lies on the same side of the third cost.
  4. Alligate the intermediate mean with the remaining cost(s) until only the target remains.
  5. Multiply the ratios obtained at each stage to get the final proportion.
Alligation Matrix for Three Ingredients
A grocer has three varieties of tea costing ₹120, ₹150, and ₹180 per kg. In what ratio must he mix them to obtain a blend worth ₹160 per kg?

Step 1: Costs in ascending order: 120, 150, 180. Target 160 lies between 150 and 180.

Step 2: Alligate the extremes 120 and 180 to get an intermediate mean. Differences: |120−160| = 40, |180−160| = 20. Ratio of 120‑tea to 180‑tea = 20 : 40 = 1 : 2. This intermediate mixture has cost (1×120 + 2×180)/3 = 480/3 = ₹160, exactly the target.

Step 3: Since the intermediate already equals the target, the third variety (₹150) is not needed. The final ratio is 1 : 0 : 2, i.e., only the cheapest and dearest in 1 : 2.

If the target were ₹155, the intermediate from 120 and 180 would be (1×120+2×180)/3 = ₹160. Then alligate 160 with 150: differences |160−155| = 5, |150−155| = 5 → ratio 1 : 1. Combine with previous 1 : 2 gives overall ratio (1×1) : (1×1) : (2×1) = 1 : 1 : 2.

Exam‑Day Strategy for Multi‑Ingredient Alligation
When you see three or more costs, first verify that the target lies within the range. If it does, use the two‑stage alligation: pair the extremes, compute the intermediate, then alligate with the middle cost. Write the intermediate ratio clearly; examiners often award partial marks for correct intermediate steps even if the final arithmetic slips.

Partnership Admission, Retirement, and Goodwill Valuation

When a new partner is admitted, the existing partners sacrifice a portion of their profit share. The sacrifice ratio is the difference between the old profit‑sharing ratio and the new ratio for each continuing partner. Conversely, when a partner retires, the remaining partners gain a share; the gaining ratio is the difference between the new and old ratios for the continuing partners. Goodwill, the value of the firm’s reputation, must be valued and adjusted. Three standard valuation methods are:

  • Average Profit Method: Goodwill = Average annual profit × Number of years’ purchase. Average profit is computed after adjusting for abnormal items and partner remuneration.
  • Super Profit Method: Super profit = Average profit − Normal profit (where normal profit = Capital employed × Normal rate of return). Goodwill = Super profit × Number of years’ purchase.
  • Capitalisation Method: Goodwill = (Average profit × 100 / Normal rate of return) − Capital employed. Alternatively, goodwill = Super profit × 100 / Normal rate of return.

On admission, the incoming partner may bring goodwill in cash or kind. If goodwill is brought in cash, it is credited to the old partners in their sacrifice ratio. If goodwill is not brought in, the new partner’s capital account is debited with his share of goodwill and the old partners are credited in the sacrifice ratio. On retirement, the retiring partner’s share of goodwill is debited to the continuing partners in their gaining ratio and credited to the retiring partner’s account. All adjustments are made through the partners’ capital accounts; the firm’s books do not show a separate goodwill account unless the partnership deed requires it.

Illustrative Adjustment

Assume A and B share profits 3 : 2. C is admitted for 1/5 share, acquiring 1/10 from A and 1/10 from B. New ratio = A: 3/5 − 1/10 = 5/10, B: 2/5 − 1/10 = 3/10, C: 2/10 → 5 : 3 : 2. Sacrifice ratio = 1 : 1. If goodwill is valued at ₹50,000 and C brings ₹20,000 cash for goodwill, the remaining ₹30,000 is adjusted by debiting C’s capital ₹30,000 and crediting A and B ₹15,000 each.

Profit Sharing with Interest on Capital and Partner Salary

Partnership deeds often provide for interest on capital at an agreed rate and salary or commission to working partners. These items are appropriations of profit, not expenses. The profit‑and‑loss appropriation account is prepared after the profit‑and‑loss account. The sequence is:

  1. Calculate net profit from the profit‑and‑loss account.
  2. Charge interest on each partner’s opening capital (and on additional capital for the period it was employed) at the agreed rate.
  3. Allocate salary/commission to entitled partners.
  4. The residual profit (or loss) is distributed in the profit‑sharing ratio.

If the residual is a loss, interest and salary are still allowed unless the deed states they are payable only out of profits. In that case, the loss is shared in the profit‑sharing ratio after reversing the appropriations. The effective capital for interest calculation must reflect the time‑weighted capital, exactly as in compound partnership. For example, if A’s capital is ₹1,00,000 for 12 months and B adds ₹50,000 after 6 months, interest for A = 1,00,000 × 12/12 × r, for B = 50,000 × 6/12 × r.

Worked Example

Partners X and Y share profits 2 : 1. Capitals: X ₹2,00,000, Y ₹1,00,000. Deed allows 10% interest on capital and ₹12,000 annual salary to X. Net profit before appropriations = ₹1,20,000. Interest: X = 20,000, Y = 10,000. Salary to X = 12,000. Total appropriations = 42,000. Residual profit = 78,000. Distributed: X = 52,000, Y = 26,000. Final shares: X = 20,000 + 12,000 + 52,000 = ₹84,000; Y = 10,000 + 26,000 = ₹36,000.

Discounted Cash Flow Approach to Partnership Valuation

In advanced partnership problems, especially those involving buy‑out or dissolution, the value of a partner’s share is the present value of expected future profit distributions. The discounted cash flow (DCF) method estimates the stream of distributable profits for each future year, discounts them at an appropriate rate (often the firm’s weighted average cost of capital or a risk‑adjusted rate), and sums the present values. The steps are:

  1. Project the firm’s maintainable profit for each forecast year, adjusting for expected growth, capital expenditure, and working‑capital changes.
  2. Determine each partner’s share of the projected profit using the agreed profit‑sharing ratio (which may change if capital contributions change).
  3. Select a discount rate reflecting the risk of the partnership’s cash flows.
  4. Compute the present value of each partner’s profit share: PV = Σ (Profit_share_t / (1 + r)ᵗ).
  5. Add the present value of any terminal value (e.g., net assets on dissolution) if applicable.

This approach is superior to simple capital‑account valuation when profits are expected to grow or when the partnership has significant intangible assets. In examinations, a simplified version may appear: “A partner’s share of profit is ₹50,000 per year for the next 5 years. If the discount rate is 10%, find the present value of his share.” The answer uses the annuity formula PV = 50,000 × [1 − (1.10)⁻⁵] / 0.10 ≈ ₹1,89,540. Understanding the annuity factor table (or the formula) is essential for quick computation.

Key Points for Exams

  • DCF valuation is only required when the problem explicitly mentions “present value”, “discount rate”, or “buy‑out value”.
  • If the profit share is constant, use the annuity factor; if it grows at a constant rate g, use the growing annuity formula PV = C × [1 − ((1+g)/(1+r))ⁿ] / (r − g).
  • Always state the assumption that the discount rate remains constant over the projection horizon.
  • Confusing sacrifice ratio with gaining ratio when a partner is admitted versus when a partner retires.
  • Applying the simple inverse‑difference alligation rule to three ingredients without checking whether the target price lies between the extremes.
  • Forgetting to time‑weight capital when computing interest on capital in a compound partnership.
  • Treating interest on capital and partner salary as expenses deducted before net profit instead of appropriations after net profit.
  • Using the wrong discount rate (e.g., cost of debt instead of weighted average cost of capital) in DCF valuation of a partnership share.
  • Neglecting to adjust goodwill for the sacrifice/gaining ratio, leading to incorrect capital account balances on admission or retirement.
  • Assuming the mean proportional is always the arithmetic mean rather than the geometric mean.

Componendo and Dividendo in Ratio Equations

Derivation and Core Identities

When two ratios are equal, a : b = c : d, the componendo rule states that (a + b) : b = (c + d) : d. The dividendo rule gives (a − b) : b = (c − d) : d. Combining both yields the componendo‑dividendo identity (a + b) : (a − b) = (c + d) : (c − d). These transformations are valid provided the denominators are non‑zero and the quantities involved are positive, which is the usual case in competitive‑exam problems.

Typical Problem Patterns

Examiners often embed a hidden proportion inside a word problem. For example, “The sum of two numbers is 45 and their difference is 15. Find the numbers.” Setting the numbers as x and y gives x + y = 45 and x − y = 15. The ratio (x + y) : (x − y) = 45 : 15 = 3 : 1. By componendo‑dividendo, x : y = (3 + 1) : (3 − 1) = 4 : 2 = 2 : 1. Hence x = 30 and y = 15. This technique bypasses simultaneous equations and saves valuable seconds.

Extension to Three‑Term Ratios

If a : b : c = p : q : r, then (a + b + c) : (a − b + c) = (p + q + r) : (p − q + r) provided the expressions are defined. This extension is useful in mixture problems where three components are combined and the overall ratio of sum to a signed combination is given.

Common Pitfalls

  • Applying componendo‑dividendo when any term could be zero or negative, which invalidates the ratio.
  • Forgetting to revert the transformed ratio back to the original variables before answering the question.
  • Using the rule on non‑proportional expressions such as a/b = c/d + e.

Ratio in Time, Speed, and Distance Problems

Fundamental Ratio Relationships

Speed, time, and distance obey the simple proportion Distance = Speed × Time. When distance is constant, speed and time are inversely proportional: S₁ : S₂ = T₂ : T₁. When time is constant, distance and speed are directly proportional: D₁ : D₂ = S₁ : S₂. When speed is constant, distance and time are directly proportional: D₁ : D₂ = T₁ : T₂. Recognising which quantity is held constant converts a word problem into a pure ratio exercise.

Average Speed for Multiple Segments

For a journey split into two parts with distances d₁ and d₂ covered at speeds v₁ and v₂, the average speed is (d₁ + d₂) / (d₁/v₁ + d₂/v₂). If the distances are equal, the harmonic mean applies: 2 v₁ v₂ / (v₁ + v₂). If the times are equal, the arithmetic mean applies: (v₁ + v₂) / 2. Expressing the given data as ratios of distances or times often reveals which mean to use without memorising formulas.

Relative Speed and Meeting Problems

Two bodies moving towards each other with speeds u and v have a relative speed u + v. The time to meet equals the initial separation divided by u + v. If they move in the same direction, the relative speed is |u − v|. The ratio of distances covered until meeting equals the ratio of their speeds because the time is common. This insight turns many “when will they meet?” questions into a single ratio step.

Circular Track Scenarios

On a circular track of length L, two runners with speeds a and b (a > b) meet every L / (a − b) time units when running in the same direction, and every L / (a + b) when running opposite. The number of distinct meeting points on the track equals the numerator of the reduced fraction (a − b) / (a + b) or (a + b) / (a − b) depending on direction. This property is a direct consequence of the ratio of speeds expressed in lowest terms.

Worked Example: Meeting on a Circular Track
Two runners start together on a 400 m circular track. Their speeds are in the ratio 5 : 3. How many distinct points on the track will they meet if they run in the same direction?
Let the speeds be 5x and 3x. Relative speed = 5x − 3x = 2x. Time for first meeting = 400 / (2x) = 200/x. In that time the faster runner covers 5x × (200/x) = 1000 m, i.e. 2.5 laps. The meeting point is therefore half a lap (200 m) from the start. After each subsequent meeting the faster runner gains another 2.5 laps, so the meeting points repeat every two meetings. The reduced ratio of relative speed to sum of speeds is (5−3) : (5+3) = 2 : 8 = 1 : 4. The numerator 1 indicates a single distinct meeting point. Hence they meet at only one point on the track.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a time‑speed‑distance problem gives a ratio of speeds, immediately write the inverse ratio for times if the distance is common. For circular tracks, reduce the speed ratio to lowest terms; the numerator of the reduced relative‑speed ratio gives the number of distinct meeting points for same‑direction motion. This shortcut eliminates the need to compute absolute speeds or times.

Ratio in Work and Efficiency Problems

Efficiency as a Ratio Concept

Work problems treat “efficiency” as the amount of work done per unit time. If worker A completes a task in a days and worker B in b days, their efficiencies are in the ratio 1/a : 1/b = b : a. When several workers collaborate, the combined efficiency is the sum of individual efficiencies. The total time taken is the reciprocal of the combined efficiency multiplied by the total work (usually taken as 1 unit).

Fractional Work and Partial Participation

If a worker joins after k days or leaves early, the work done is split into segments. For each segment, compute the effective efficiency (sum of efficiencies of workers present) and multiply by the segment duration. The sum of work across segments equals 1. Expressing each segment’s contribution as a ratio of the total work often yields a linear equation in a single unknown.

Pipes and Cisterns as Work Problems

Inlet pipes have positive efficiency, outlet pipes negative. The net efficiency is the algebraic sum. If a pipe fills a tank in p hours and another empties it in q hours, the net rate is 1/p − 1/q = (q − p) / (pq). The time to fill the tank when both are open is pq / (q − p) provided q > p. This is a direct application of the ratio of rates.

Alternate Day Working

When two workers operate on alternate days, the work done in a two‑day cycle equals the sum of their daily efficiencies. If the total work is not an exact multiple of the cycle work, the remainder is completed by the worker whose turn comes next. The ratio of their efficiencies determines how many full cycles are needed and who finishes the job.

Ratio in Geometry: Section Formula and Similar Figures

Internal and External Division

A point P dividing the line segment joining A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂) internally in the ratio m : n has coordinates ((mx₂ + nx₁)/(m + n), (my₂ + ny₁)/(m + n)). For external division the formula becomes ((mx₂ − nx₁)/(m − n), (my₂ − ny₁)/(m − n)). The ratio m : n is taken as directed; reversing the order changes the sign of the denominator.

Centroid, Incentre, and Other Centres

The centroid divides each median in the ratio 2 : 1 (vertex to centroid : centroid to midpoint). The incentre divides the angle bisectors in the ratio of the adjacent sides. These fixed ratios allow rapid coordinate computation without solving full intersection equations.

Similar Triangles and Scale Factor

If two triangles are similar, the ratio of corresponding sides equals the scale factor k. Areas are in the ratio k², and volumes (for similar solids) in the ratio k³. In coordinate geometry, a dilation centred at the origin with factor k multiplies all coordinates by k. Recognising a similarity transformation converts a seemingly complex area problem into a simple ratio calculation.

Application to Mensuration

When a solid is cut by a plane parallel to its base, the resulting smaller solid is similar to the original. The linear dimensions are in the ratio of the heights, so the volume ratio is the cube of the height ratio. This principle underlies many “frustum” questions where only the ratio of heights is given.

Ratio in Financial Mathematics: Interest and Discount Ratios

Simple Interest Ratio

For a principal P, rate r% per annum, and time t years, simple interest SI = P r t / 100. If two investments have principals P₁, P₂, rates r₁, r₂, and times t₁, t₂, the ratio of interests is P₁ r₁ t₁ : P₂ r₂ t₂. When the product P r t is constant, the interests are equal. This ratio form is frequently used in data‑interpretation sets where only proportional changes are described.

Compound Interest Ratio

Compound interest for the same principal, rate, and time follows A = P (1 + r/100)ᵗ. The ratio of amounts for two different rates r₁, r₂ over the same period is (1 + r₁/100)ᵗ : (1 + r₂/100)ᵗ. For small t (1 or 2 years) the binomial expansion gives an approximate linear ratio that examiners exploit for quick mental calculation.

True Discount and Banker’s Discount

True discount (TD) on a sum due S after time t at rate r is TD = S r t / (100 + r t). Banker’s discount (BD) is simple interest on the face value: BD = S r t / 100. The ratio TD : BD = 100 : (100 + r t). Knowing this ratio allows direct computation of one discount from the other without finding the present worth.

Installment Problems

When a loan is repaid in equal annual installments, each installment X satisfies P = X / (1 + r/100) + X / (1 + r/100)² + … + X / (1 + r/100)ⁿ. The sum of the geometric series gives X = P r/100 / [1 − (1 + r/100)⁻ⁿ]. The ratio of successive installments’ present values is constant 1 : (1 + r/100)⁻¹. This constant ratio is the key to solving “find the installment” questions in a single step.

  • Applying componendo‑dividendo to equations that are not true proportions.
  • Confusing inverse proportionality of speed‑time when distance varies.
  • Treating efficiencies as additive when workers operate sequentially rather than simultaneously.
  • Using the internal section formula for external division without changing the sign of the denominator.
  • Assuming compound‑interest ratios are linear for periods longer than two years.
  • Mixing true discount and banker’s discount formulas when only one is required.
  • Forgetting that the ratio of areas of similar figures is the square of the linear scale factor, not the scale factor itself.

Successive Discounts and Equivalent Single Discount Ratio

When a seller offers two or more discounts one after another on the marked price, the overall reduction is not the arithmetic sum of the individual percentages. If the first discount is d₁% and the second is d₂%, the price after the first discount becomes MP × (1 − d₁/100). Applying the second discount on this reduced price yields a final selling price SP = MP × (1 − d₁/100) × (1 − d₂/100). The equivalent single discount D% satisfies SP = MP × (1 − D/100). Equating the two expressions gives (1 − D/100) = (1 − d₁/100)(1 − d₂/100). Solving for D yields D = d₁ + d₂ − (d₁×d₂)/100. For three successive discounts d₁, d₂, d₃ the pattern extends to D = 100[1 − ∏(1 − dᵢ/100)]. The ratio of the equivalent single discount to the sum of the individual discounts is always less than one, reflecting the diminishing marginal impact of each additional discount. This ratio is useful in data‑interpretation sets where only the overall discount percentage is given and the examiner asks for the second discount when the first is known.

A common twist is to present the problem in terms of profit or loss after successive discounts. Suppose a trader marks goods 40% above cost price and then allows successive discounts of 10% and 5%. The effective selling price relative to cost price is 1.40 × 0.90 × 0.95 = 1.197, i.e. a 19.7% profit. The ratio of profit percent to the marked‑up percent (19.7/40 ≈ 0.4925) is a quick check for reasonableness. When the marked price is not given, assume a convenient base such as 100 or the LCM of the denominators of the discount fractions to avoid decimals. The ratio method also works in reverse: if the equivalent single discount is known and one of the successive discounts is given, the other can be found by rearranging the product equation.

In competitive examinations, successive‑discount questions often appear alongside markup‑profit‑loss scenarios. The key is to convert every percentage into a multiplicative factor (1 ± p/100) and multiply them. The final factor directly gives the overall percentage change. Because multiplication of factors is commutative, the order of discounts does not affect the final price, a fact that can be used to eliminate answer choices that depend on order. Remember that a discount followed by a markup (or vice‑versa) is handled by the same multiplicative chain; the only difference is that a markup uses a factor greater than one.

Effective Annual Rate and Continuous Compounding Ratio

The nominal annual interest rate r% compounded n times per year yields an effective annual rate (EAR) given by EAR = (1 + r/(100 n))n − 1. The ratio EAR / (r/100) measures how much more interest is earned due to intra‑year compounding. For example, a nominal 12% compounded monthly gives EAR = (1 + 0.12/12)12 − 1 ≈ 0.1268 or 12.68%; the ratio is 1.0568. As n increases, the ratio approaches the limit of continuous compounding, where the amount after one year is P er/100. The continuous‑compounding EAR is er/100 − 1, and the ratio of continuous EAR to nominal rate is (er/100 − 1) / (r/100). For small r this ratio is approximately 1 + r/200, a linear approximation examiners sometimes exploit for mental calculation.

When comparing two investment offers with different compounding frequencies, convert both to EAR before forming a ratio. If offer A is 10% compounded quarterly and offer B is 9.8% compounded monthly, compute EARA = (1 + 0.10/4)4 − 1 ≈ 0.1038 and EARB = (1 + 0.098/12)12 − 1 ≈ 0.1025. The ratio EARA : EARB ≈ 1.0127 : 1, showing A is marginally better. In installment problems where the interest is quoted as a nominal rate but payments are monthly, the monthly rate used in the present‑value formula is the nominal rate divided by 12, not the EAR divided by 12. Confusing these two rates is a frequent source of error.

The concept of ratio also appears in the derivation of the force of interest δ = ln(1 + r/100) for continuous compounding. The ratio of the force of interest to the nominal rate is δ / (r/100). For small rates this ratio is close to 1, but it diverges as rates rise. In actuarial‑type questions, the present value of a continuous annuity is expressed as an integral involving e−δt; the ratio of the present value of a continuous annuity to that of a discrete annuity with the same nominal rate is a constant that depends only on δ and the term. Recognising these constant ratios allows rapid comparison without evaluating the full integrals.

Technique: Equivalent Single Discount from Successive Discounts
A shopkeeper marks an article at ₹1,200 and offers successive discounts of 20% and 12.5%. Find the equivalent single discount percentage and the final selling price.
Convert each discount to a multiplicative factor: first factor = 1 − 20/100 = 0.80; second factor = 1 − 12.5/100 = 0.875. Overall factor = 0.80 × 0.875 = 0.70. Equivalent single discount = (1 − 0.70) × 100 = 30%. Final selling price = 1200 × 0.70 = ₹840. The ratio of equivalent discount to the sum of given discounts is 30 / (20 + 12.5) = 30 / 32.5 ≈ 0.923, confirming the diminishing effect.
Exam‑Day Strategy
For successive discounts, always multiply the (1 − d/100) factors; the order does not matter. For EAR, memorize the formula (1 + r/(100n))^n − 1 and the continuous limit e^(r/100) − 1. When a question gives a nominal rate and asks for the effective rate for a different compounding frequency, convert to EAR first, then back‑solve for the new nominal rate if required. Keep a mental table of common EAR values: 12% monthly ≈ 12.68%, 10% quarterly ≈ 10.38%, 8% half‑yearly ≈ 8.16%. This saves precious seconds.

Odds, Conditional Probability, and Bayes' Theorem as Ratio Problems

Odds in favour of an event E are defined as the ratio P(E) : P(E′), where E′ is the complement. If the probability of rain tomorrow is 0.3, the odds in favour are 0.3 : 0.7 = 3 : 7, and the odds against are 7 : 3. This ratio representation is directly usable in Bayesian updating. The conditional probability P(A|B) is the ratio of the joint probability P(A∩B) to the marginal probability P(B). Bayes' theorem rewrites the posterior odds as the product of prior odds and the likelihood ratio: Odds(A|B) = Odds(A) × [P(B|A) / P(B|A′)]. The likelihood ratio is itself a ratio of two conditional probabilities. In many examination questions, the data are given as odds or as ratios of frequencies, and the answer requires converting to probabilities, applying the ratio form of Bayes, and converting back to odds.

Consider a diagnostic test with sensitivity 90% (P(positive|disease)) and specificity 95% (P(negative|no disease)). If the disease prevalence is 2%, the prior odds of disease are 2 : 98 = 1 : 49. The likelihood ratio for a positive test is 0.90 / 0.05 = 18. Posterior odds = (1/49) × 18 = 18/49 ≈ 0.367, i.e. odds of about 18 : 49 in favour of disease given a positive result. Converting to probability gives 18/(18+49) ≈ 0.269 or 26.9%. The ratio of posterior probability to prior probability (0.269/0.02 ≈ 13.45) quantifies how much the test shifts belief. Examiners often ask for the positive predictive value directly; recognising the odds‑ratio shortcut avoids constructing a full 2×2 table.

When multiple independent tests are applied sequentially, the overall likelihood ratio is the product of individual likelihood ratios because the joint conditional probability factorises. If a second independent test has sensitivity 80% and specificity 90%, its positive likelihood ratio is 0.80/0.10 = 8. The combined likelihood ratio for two positive results is 18 × 8 = 144. Posterior odds become (1/49) × 144 = 144/49 ≈ 2.94, corresponding to a probability of 144/(144+49) ≈ 0.746. The ratio of the final probability to the initial probability (0.746/0.02 ≈ 37.3) illustrates the cumulative power of independent evidence. In data‑interpretation sets, the same multiplicative property appears when updating market‑share ratios after successive advertising campaigns.

Weighted Averages and Composite Index Ratios in Data Interpretation

A weighted average of values x₁, x₂, …, xₖ with weights w₁, w₂, …, wₖ is the ratio (∑ wᵢ xᵢ) / (∑ wᵢ). The weights themselves are often given as ratios, e.g. the expenditure shares of food, fuel, and housing in a consumer price index (CPI) might be 45 : 15 : 40. If the price relatives (current price / base price) for the three groups are 1.12, 1.08, and 1.05 respectively, the CPI is the weighted average of the relatives: (45×1.12 + 15×1.08 + 40×1.05) / (45+15+40) = (50.4 + 16.2 + 42) / 100 = 108.6 / 100 = 1.086, i.e. an 8.6% increase. The ratio of the index to 100 is the overall inflation factor. When the base‑year weights change (chain‑linking), the new index is a ratio of two weighted averages, and the link factor is the ratio of the new index to the old index for the overlapping period.

In problems involving mixture of two alloys with different metal ratios, the final composition ratio is a weighted average of the component ratios where the weights are the quantities mixed. If alloy A has copper : zinc = 3 : 2 and alloy B has copper : zinc = 5 : 3, and they are mixed in the ratio 4 : 5 by weight, the copper fraction in A is 3/5, in B is 5/8. The overall copper fraction = (4×3/5 + 5×5/8) / (4+5) = (12/5 + 25/8) / 9 = (96/40 + 125/40) / 9 = 221/360. The final copper : zinc ratio = 221 : (360−221) = 221 : 139. This method extends to any number of components and is the algebraic counterpart of the alligation rule.

Composite indices such as the Human Development Index (HDI) or the Index of Industrial Production (IIP) are geometric means of normalized sub‑indices. The geometric mean of ratios is the ratio of the geometric means, i.e. (∏ (xᵢ / yᵢ))1/k = (∏ xᵢ)1/k / (∏ yᵢ)1/k. When a question asks for the percentage change in a composite index given percentage changes in its components, convert each component change to a factor (1 + p/100), multiply the factors raised to their respective weights, and subtract one. The ratio of the new index to the old index is exactly this product. Recognising that the weighted geometric mean preserves ratios allows quick mental estimation: if two components each rise by 10% and have equal weight, the composite rises by 10% as well; if weights differ, the rise is the weighted arithmetic mean of the logarithmic changes.

  • Treating successive discounts as additive percentages instead of multiplicative factors.
  • Using the nominal rate directly in present‑value formulas when the compounding frequency differs from the payment frequency.
  • Confusing odds in favour with probability; odds are a ratio, probability is a fraction of the total.
  • Forgetting to convert likelihood ratios to posterior odds before converting back to probability in Bayes problems.
  • Applying arithmetic weighted average where a geometric weighted average is required for composite indices.
  • Mixing up the direction of the ratio in alligation (cheaper : dearer vs. dearer : cheaper) when the mean price lies outside the range of component prices.
  • Assuming the ratio of areas of similar figures equals the linear scale factor instead of its square.

Ratio in Annuities and Loan Amortization

An annuity is a series of equal payments made at regular intervals. The ratio between the present value (PV) of an annuity and the periodic payment (PMT) is the present‑value annuity factor, denoted an|i = (1 − (1+i)−n) / i, where i is the effective interest rate per period and n the number of periods. Conversely, the future‑value annuity factor sn|i = ((1+i)n − 1) / i gives the ratio of the accumulated value to the same payment. These ratios are the cornerstone of loan amortization: the loan amount L equals PMT × an|i, so PMT = L / an|i. Each instalment splits into interest Ik = outstanding balance × i and principal repayment Pk = PMT − Ik. The ratio of principal repaid in successive periods follows a geometric progression with common ratio (1+i). For a loan of ₹10,00,000 at 12 % p.a. compounded monthly over 20 years, i = 0.12/12 = 0.01, n = 240, a240|0.01 ≈ 90.819, giving PMT ≈ ₹11,011. The first month’s interest is ₹10,000, principal ₹1,011; the second month’s interest falls to ₹9,989.89, principal rises to ₹1,021.11. The ratio of principal components Pk+1/Pk = 1.01 exactly. This property allows rapid construction of an amortization schedule without iterating every period: the principal paid in period k is PMT × (1+i)k−1 / sn|i. In examinations, a common twist is to ask for the outstanding balance after m payments. The balance equals PMT × an−m|i, a direct ratio of the remaining annuity factor to the original factor. Another twist introduces a balloon payment: the loan is amortized over n periods but a final lump sum B is due at maturity. Then L = PMT × an|i + B × (1+i)−n. Solving for PMT yields PMT = (L − B vn) / an|i, where v = 1/(1+i). Recognising the ratio structure avoids setting up a full cash‑flow table.

Deferred and Growing Annuities

A deferred annuity begins after d periods. Its present value is PMT × an|i × vd. The ratio of the deferred PV to an immediate PV is simply vd. A growing annuity has payments increasing at rate g each period: PMT, PMT(1+g), PMT(1+g)2, … . The present‑value factor becomes (1 − ((1+g)/(1+i))n) / (i − g) provided i ≠ g. The ratio of the growing‑annuity factor to the level‑annuity factor quantifies the premium for growth. For i = 8 %, g = 3 %, n = 10, the level factor a10|0.08 = 6.710, the growing factor = (1 − (1.03/1.08)10) / 0.05 ≈ 8.110, ratio ≈ 1.21. Examiners often ask for the payment that equates a growing annuity to a given lump sum; the algebraic manipulation is identical to the level case once the correct factor is identified.

Ratio in Polynomial Roots and Vieta's Formulas

For a polynomial P(x) = anxn + an−1xn−1 + … + a1x + a0 with roots r1, r2, …, rn (counting multiplicities), Vieta’s formulas express elementary symmetric sums of the roots as ratios of coefficients. The sum of roots Σ ri = −an−1/an. The sum of pairwise products Σ rirj = an−2/an. In general, the k‑th elementary symmetric sum ek = (−1)k an−k/an. The product of all roots Π ri = (−1)n a0/an. These ratios are invariant under scaling of the polynomial; multiplying P(x) by a non‑zero constant leaves every ratio unchanged. This property is exploited in problems where a polynomial is given with unknown coefficients but known root relationships. For example, if a cubic x3 + px2 + qx + r has roots in arithmetic progression, let the roots be a−d, a, a+d. Then Σ r = 3a = −p ⇒ a = −p/3. The product (a−d)a(a+d) = a(a2−d2) = −r. The sum of pairwise products = 3a2−d2 = q. Eliminating d gives a relation between p, q, r: 2p3 − 9pq + 27r = 0. The ratio of coefficients encodes the progression condition without solving for the roots explicitly. A similar approach works for geometric progression: roots a/r, a, ar give product a3 = −r, sum a(1/r + 1 + r) = −p, pairwise sum a2(1/r + 1 + r) = q. The ratio q/p = a, and a3 = −r yields a = (−r)1/3. These manipulations are standard in competitive examinations and appear in disguised forms such as “find the value of k if the roots of x3 − 6x2 + kx − 8 = 0 are in geometric progression”. Using Vieta, product = 8 ⇒ a3 = 8 ⇒ a = 2. Sum = 6 ⇒ a(1/r+1+r)=6 ⇒ 1/r+1+r=3 ⇒ r=1. Then pairwise sum = k = a2(1/r+1+r)=4×3=12. The answer k = 12 follows from pure ratio reasoning.

Reciprocal and Transformed Roots

If the roots of P(x) are ri, the polynomial whose roots are 1/ri is xnP(1/x) multiplied by a suitable constant. The coefficient ratios of the transformed polynomial are the reversed ratios of the original: the new sum of roots = Σ 1/ri = en−1/en = (−a1/an) / ((−1)n a0/an) = (−1)n−1 a1/a0. This reciprocal‑root ratio is frequently asked: “If α, β, γ are roots of 2x3 − 5x2 + 3x − 7 = 0, find Σ 1/α”. Using the ratio a1/a0 = 3/(−7) = −3/7, and n=3 gives Σ 1/α = (−1)2 (−3/7) = −3/7. Another common transformation is shifting roots by a constant h: new roots ri+h. The new polynomial is P(x−h). The ratio of the new coefficient of xn−1 to the leading coefficient equals −Σ(ri+h) = −(Σ ri + nh). This yields a quick way to compute Σ (ri+h)2 or higher powers using Newton’s identities, which themselves are recursive ratio relations between power sums and elementary symmetric sums.

Worked Example: Growing Annuity Present Value
A lease requires 10 annual payments. The first payment is ₹50,000 and each subsequent payment increases by 4 %. If the discount rate is 9 % p.a., find the present value of the lease.
Identify PMT = 50,000, g = 0.04, i = 0.09, n = 10. Since i ≠ g, use the growing‑annuity factor: PV = PMT × [1 − ((1+g)/(1+i))n] / (i − g). Compute (1+g)/(1+i) = 1.04/1.09 ≈ 0.954128. Raise to the 10th power: 0.95412810 ≈ 0.6246. Numerator = 1 − 0.6246 = 0.3754. Denominator = 0.09 − 0.04 = 0.05. Factor = 0.3754 / 0.05 = 7.508. PV = 50,000 × 7.508 = ₹3,75,400. The ratio of this PV to the level‑annuity PV (factor a10|0.09 = 6.4177) is 7.508/6.4177 ≈ 1.17, confirming the 17 % premium for 4 % growth at a 9 % discount rate.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem presents a series of payments with a constant percentage change, immediately write down the growing‑annuity factor formula. Do not attempt to sum each term individually; the ratio (1+g)/(1+i) raised to n is a single exponentiation that calculators handle in seconds. For Vieta‑type questions, list the required symmetric sums (sum, pairwise sum, product) and match them to the coefficient ratios −an−1/an, an−2/an, (−1)na0/an. This converts a root‑finding problem into simple algebra.

Ratio in Index Numbers: Chain Base and Fixed Base Methods

Index numbers measure relative change in a variable over time. The fixed‑base index for period t with base period 0 is It = (Pt/P0) × 100, where P denotes price or quantity. The chain‑base index links each period to its immediate predecessor: link relative Lt = (Pt/Pt−1) × 100. The chain index Ct = Ct−1 × Lt/100, with C0 = 100. The ratio of the chain index to the fixed‑base index is the product of all link relatives divided by the direct relative: Ct/It = (∏k=1t Pk/Pk−1) / (Pt/P0) = 1. Hence, in a pure price series without weighting, chain and fixed bases coincide. Weighting introduces divergence. In a weighted aggregative index (Laspeyres, Paasche, Fisher), the fixed‑base formula uses base‑period weights w0 throughout: It = (∑ w0 Pt) / (∑ w0 P0) × 100. The chain‑base Laspeyres updates weights each period: Lt = (∑ wt−1 Pt) / (∑ wt−1 Pt−1) × 100. The ratio of successive chain indices reflects both price change and weight change. A classic examination problem supplies a table of prices and quantities for three years and asks for the Fisher ideal index for year 2 with year 0 as base. Fisher = √(Laspeyres × Paasche). Laspeyres uses base quantities q0 as weights; Paasche uses current quantities q2. Compute both ratios, multiply, take square root. The ratio of Fisher to Laspeyres indicates the degree of substitution bias. If quantities shift dramatically, the ratio deviates from 1. Another twist: given a chain index series 100, 108, 115, 124, reconstruct the fixed‑base index for year 3. Since chain relatives are 108, 106.48, 107.83, the fixed‑base index = 100 × 1.08 × 1.0648 × 1.0783 ≈ 124.0, confirming consistency when no weighting changes.

Splicing and Base Shifting

When a new base year is adopted, the old index series must be spliced. Suppose an old fixed‑base index (base 2000) runs 100, 112, 125, 138 for 2000‑2003. A new series (base 2005) starts at 100 for 2005 with values 100, 105, 110 for 2005‑2007. To splice, compute the link factor = old index for 2005 / new index for 2005. If the old index for 2005 (extrapolated) is 160, the factor = 160/100 = 1.6. Multiply all new indices by 1.6 to place them on the old base. The ratio of the spliced value to the original old value for overlapping years should be 1; any discrepancy signals a methodological break. Examiners often ask: “If the consumer price index (CPI) with base 2010 = 100 stands at 135 in 2020, and a new series with base 2020 = 100 shows 108 in 2022, what is the 2022 index on the 2010 base?” Answer: 135 × 108/100 = 145.8. The ratio 108/100 is the link relative for 2020‑2022; multiplying by the 2020 index on the old base yields the spliced value.

Ratio in Probability: Odds Ratio, Relative Risk, and Likelihood Ratios

In a 2×2 contingency table with disease status (D+, D−) and test result (T+, T−), the odds of disease given a positive test are (a/c) where a = true positives, c = false positives. The odds of disease given a negative test are (b/d) with b = false negatives, d = true negatives. The odds ratio (OR) = (a/c) / (b/d) = ad / bc. This ratio is symmetric: it equals the ratio of the odds of a positive test among diseased to the odds of a positive test among non‑diseased. Relative risk (RR) = (a/(a+b)) / (c/(c+d)) = a(c+d) / c(a+b). RR compares probabilities directly, while OR compares odds. When the disease is rare, OR ≈ RR. The positive likelihood ratio LR+ = sensitivity / (1 − specificity) = (a/(a+b)) / (c/(c+d)). The negative likelihood ratio LR− = (1 − sensitivity) / specificity = (b/(a+b)) / (d/(c+d)). Both LR+ and LR− are ratios of conditional probabilities. Bayes’ theorem in odds form: posterior odds = prior odds × LR. If prior probability of disease = 0.02, prior odds = 0.02/0.98 = 1/49. With LR+ = 18, posterior odds = 18/49, posterior probability = 18/(18+49) ≈ 0.269. The ratio of posterior to prior probability = 0.269/0.02 ≈ 13.45, quantifying the information gain. In diagnostic test evaluation, the diagnostic odds ratio DOR = LR+/LR− = (ad/bc) / (bc/ad) = (ad/bc)2? Actually DOR = (a/b) / (c/d) = ad/bc = OR. Thus the odds ratio equals the diagnostic odds ratio. Examiners may present a table and ask for the “ratio of the odds of disease in the exposed group to the odds in the unexposed group”, which is exactly the odds ratio. Recognising that OR = (a/b)/(c/d) = ad/bc avoids constructing the full 2×2 table if only marginal totals are given.

Sequential Testing and Multiplicative Likelihood Ratios

When two independent tests are applied sequentially, the overall LR+ is the product of individual LR+ values because conditional independence implies P(T1+,T2+|D+) = P(T1+|D+)P(T2+|D+). If Test A has sensitivity 0.90, specificity 0.95 → LR+A = 0.90/0.05 = 18. Test B has sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.90 → LR+B = 0.80/0.10 = 8. Combined LR+ = 144. Prior odds 1/49 become posterior odds 144/49 ≈ 2.94, posterior probability ≈ 0.746. The ratio of final to initial probability = 0.746/0.02 ≈ 37.3. This multiplicative property extends to any number of independent tests. A common trap is to add likelihood ratios instead of multiplying them. Another trap: using the same test twice does not square the LR unless the two applications are conditionally independent given disease status, which is rarely true for repeated measurements on the same specimen.

Ratio in Number Theory: GCD, LCM, and the Euclidean Algorithm

The greatest common divisor (gcd) and least common multiple (lcm) of two positive integers a and b satisfy the fundamental ratio a × b = gcd(a,b) × lcm(a,b). This identity follows from the prime‑factorisation representation: if a = ∏ piαi, b = ∏ piβi, then gcd = ∏ pimin(αi,βi), lcm = ∏ pimax(αi,βi). The product of the exponents min+max = α+β, so the product of gcd and lcm reproduces a×b. The Euclidean algorithm computes gcd by repeated division: gcd(a,b) = gcd(b, a mod b). The sequence of remainders r0=a, r1=b, rk+1 = rk−1 mod rk terminates at rn = gcd. The quotients qk = ⌊rk−1/rk⌋ form the continued‑fraction expansion of a/b. The ratio of successive remainders rk−1/rk approximates the final ratio a/b with increasing accuracy. Bézout’s identity states that there exist integers x, y such that ax + by = gcd(a,b). The extended Euclidean algorithm yields x, y by back‑substitution. The ratio x/y (when y ≠ 0) is a convergent of the continued fraction for a/b. In competitive problems, a typical question: “Find the smallest positive integer N such that N ≡ 2 (mod 3), N ≡ 3 (mod 5), N ≡ 2 (mod 7).” This is a system of congruences solved by the Chinese Remainder Theorem. The moduli are pairwise coprime, so the solution is unique modulo 105. Construct N = Σ ai Mi yi where M = 105, Mi = M/mi, yi = Mi−1 mod mi. The ratios Mi/mi are integers; the inverses yi are found via the extended Euclidean algorithm. For the given data, N = 23. The ratio of the solution to the product of moduli (23/105) is the fractional part of the unique solution in [0,1). Another classic: “If gcd(a,b)=12 and lcm(a,b)=720, find the number of ordered pairs (a,b).” Let a = 12x, b = 12y with gcd(x,y)=1. Then lcm = 12xy = 720 ⇒ xy = 60. The number of coprime factor pairs of 60 equals 2k where k is the number of distinct prime factors of 60 (2,3,5 → k=3) → 8 ordered pairs. The ratio lcm/gcd = 60 directly gives xy.

Applications to Diophantine Equations

Linear Diophantine equations ax + by = c have integer solutions iff gcd(a,b) divides c. The ratio c/gcd(a,b) scales the particular solution obtained from the extended Euclidean algorithm. If (x0, y0) solves ax + by = gcd(a,b), then (x0·c/g, y0·c/g) solves the original equation, where g = gcd(a,b). The general solution is x = x0·c/g + (b/g)t, y = y0·c/g − (a/g)t for integer t. The ratio of the step sizes (b/g) : (a/g) determines the lattice spacing of solutions. In problems asking for the number of non‑negative solutions, one finds the range of t such that both coordinates are ≥ 0. The width of this interval divided by the step ratio gives the count. For example, 6x + 9y = 120. gcd = 3, divides 120. Reduced equation 2x + 3y = 40. Particular solution (20,0). General: x = 20 + 3t, y = −2t. Non‑negative requires t ≤ 0 and t ≥ −6 (since y ≥ 0 ⇒ −2t ≥ 0 ⇒ t ≤ 0; x ≥ 0 ⇒ 20+3t ≥ 0 ⇒ t ≥ −6.66). Integer t = 0, −1, …, −6 → 7 solutions. The ratio of coefficient magnitudes (3:2) governs the step.

Ratio in Races and Games

Race problems translate speed, distance, and time into ratio language where the constant element — usually the race length — anchors the comparison. When two runners A and B cover the same distance, their times are in the inverse ratio of their speeds: tA : tB = vB : vA. If A gives B a start of x metres in a race of L metres, B effectively runs L − x while A runs L. The ratio of speeds equals the ratio of distances covered in the same time: vA : vB = L : (L − x). A dead heat occurs when both reach the finish simultaneously; the start given is then x = L(1 − vB/vA). When A beats B by t seconds, B covers the remaining distance in that extra time: vB = x/t where x is the beating distance. In games of skill (billiards, chess points), the same ratio logic applies: if A can give B 20 points in 100, the skill ratio is 100 : 80 = 5 : 4. For three players A, B, C where A gives B a points and B gives C b points in a game of P points, the transitive skill ratio is A : B : C = P : (P − a) : (P − a)(P − b)/P. Examiners frequently combine race and game concepts: “In a 1 km race, A beats B by 40 m and B beats C by 50 m. By how much does A beat C?” The trap is to add 40 + 50 = 90 m. Correct method: when A runs 1000 m, B runs 960 m. When B runs 1000 m, C runs 950 m. When B runs 960 m, C runs 950 × 960/1000 = 912 m. A beats C by 1000 − 912 = 88 m.

Ratio in Clocks and Calendars

Clock problems are ratio problems in disguise. The minute hand gains 55 minute-spaces over the hour hand every 60 minutes — a gain ratio of 55 : 60 = 11 : 12. The relative speed ratio is therefore 11/12 minute-spaces per minute. To find when the hands coincide between h and h+1 o’clock, the minute hand must gain 5h minute-spaces (the initial gap). Time required = (5h) ÷ (11/12) = 60h/11 minutes past h. For right angles, the gain needed is 5h ± 15 minute-spaces, yielding two solutions per hour except at 2–3 and 8–9 where one solution coincides with the hour boundary. For straight line (opposite), gain = 5h ± 30. The calendar uses the ratio of odd days: an ordinary year contributes 1 odd day (365 ≡ 1 mod 7), a leap year contributes 2. The 400-year Gregorian cycle contains 97 leap years and 303 ordinary years, total odd days = 97×2 + 303×1 = 497 ≡ 0 mod 7, so the calendar repeats every 400 years. To find the day of week for a given date, compute total odd days from a known anchor (e.g., 1 Jan 1900 = Monday). Century odd days: 100 years = 5, 200 = 3, 300 = 1, 400 = 0. Month odd days (cumulative): Jan 0, Feb 3, Mar 3, Apr 6, May 1, Jun 4, Jul 6, Aug 2, Sep 5, Oct 0, Nov 3, Dec 5 (leap year adds 1 after February). A frequent examiner twist: “The calendar for 2024 will repeat in which year?” 2024 is leap; next leap with same 1 Jan weekday requires total odd days ≡ 0 mod 7. 2028 adds 5 odd days (4 ordinary + 1 leap), 2032 adds 5 more → 10 ≡ 3, 2036 → 15 ≡ 1, 2040 → 20 ≡ 6, 2044 → 25 ≡ 4, 2048 → 30 ≡ 2, 2052 → 35 ≡ 0. Answer: 2052.

Worked Example: Race with Head Start and Dead Heat
In a 1200 m race, A can give B a start of 100 m and C a start of 180 m. In a 1000 m race, how much start can B give C so that the race ends in a dead heat?

Step 1: Establish speed ratios from the 1200 m race data.

A runs 1200 m while B runs 1100 m ⇒ vA : vB = 1200 : 1100 = 12 : 11.

A runs 1200 m while C runs 1020 m ⇒ vA : vC = 1200 : 1020 = 20 : 17.

Step 2: Find vB : vC by making A’s ratio term equal.

vA : vB = 12 : 11 = 60 : 55 (×5).

vA : vC = 20 : 17 = 60 : 51 (×3).

Therefore vB : vC = 55 : 51.

Step 3: In a 1000 m dead heat, B runs 1000 m. Distance C runs in same time = 1000 × (51/55) = 10200/11 = 927 3/11 m.

Step 4: Start B can give C = 1000 − 927 3/11 = 72 8/11 m.

Answer: 72 8/11 metres.

Exam-Day Strategy
For race problems, always draw a one-line diagram: mark the race length, the start given, and the distances each runner actually covers. Convert every statement into a speed ratio immediately — speeds are constant, distances vary. For clock problems, memorise the coincidence formula 60h/11 and the two right-angle offsets (±15 minute-spaces). For calendars, memorise the 12-month odd-day cumulative table and the century codes (100→5, 200→3, 300→1, 400→0). These three tables solve 90% of clock/calendar questions in under 30 seconds each.

Ratio in Algebraic Inequalities: AM–GM–HM and Cauchy–Schwarz

The classical means are ratios of sums to counts. For positive numbers a1, …, an, the arithmetic mean AM = (Σai)/n, geometric mean GM = (∏ai)1/n, harmonic mean HM = n/(Σ1/ai). The inequality chain HM ≤ GM ≤ AM follows from the ratio (AM/GM) ≥ 1 and (GM/HM) ≥ 1, with equality iff all ai are equal. In exam problems, the ratio form is often more useful: AM/GM ≥ 1 ⇒ Σai ≥ n(∏ai)1/n. For two numbers, (a+b)/2 ≥ √(ab) ⇔ (√a − √b)2 ≥ 0. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality states (Σai2)(Σbi2) ≥ (Σaibi)2. The ratio form (Σai2)/(Σaibi) ≥ (Σaibi)/(Σbi2) appears in optimisation: minimum of (x2/a + y2/b + z2/c) subject to x+y+z = constant is (constant)2/(a+b+c) by Titu’s lemma (a special case of Cauchy). The rearrangement inequality: for similarly sorted sequences, Σaibi ≥ Σaibσ(i) ≥ Σaibn+1−i. Chebyshev’s inequality: if a1 ≤ … ≤ an and b1 ≤ … ≤ bn, then (1/n)Σaibi ≥ [(1/n)Σai][(1/n)Σbi]. The ratio of the left to the right side is ≥ 1. A common problem: “Find the minimum value of (a+b+c)(1/a+1/b+1/c) for positive a,b,c.” By Cauchy, (√a·1/√a + …)2 ≤ (a+b+c)(1/a+1/b+1/c) ⇒ minimum = 9 when a=b=c. Another: “If x+y+z=1, find minimum of 1/x+1/y+1/z.” By HM ≤ AM, 3/(1/x+1/y+1/z) ≤ (x+y+z)/3 = 1/3 ⇒ 1/x+1/y+1/z ≥ 9.

Ratio in Harmonic Progression and the Harmonic Mean

A sequence is in harmonic progression (HP) iff the reciprocals are in arithmetic progression (AP). If a, b, c are in HP, then 1/a, 1/b, 1/c are in AP ⇒ 2/b = 1/a + 1/c ⇒ b = 2ac/(a+c), which is the harmonic mean of a and c. The harmonic mean of n numbers is HM = n/(Σ1/ai). For two numbers, HM = 2ab/(a+b). The ratio HM : GM : AM = 2ab/(a+b) : √(ab) : (a+b)/2. If a, b, c are in HP, then a/b = (a−b)/(b−c) — a useful ratio property. The sum of first n terms of an HP has no closed form, but approximations exist: Σk=1n 1/(a+(k−1)d) ≈ (1/d) ln((a+nd)/a). In time-speed-distance, if a journey is covered in equal distances at speeds v1, v2, …, vn, the average speed is the harmonic mean of the speeds. If covered in equal times, average speed is the arithmetic mean. A classic problem: “A car travels from A to B at 60 km/h and returns at 40 km/h. Find average speed.” Equal distances ⇒ HM = 2×60×40/(60+40) = 48 km/h. If the problem states “travels for 2 hours at 60 km/h and 3 hours at 40 km/h”, equal times ⇒ AM = (2×60+3×40)/5 = 48 km/h — coincidentally same value but different reasoning. For three equal distances at speeds a, b, c, average speed = 3abc/(ab+bc+ca). If the speeds are in HP, the average speed equals the middle speed. Examiners often ask: “Three numbers are in HP. Their sum is 37 and the sum of their reciprocals is 1/4. Find the numbers.” Let the numbers be 1/(x−d), 1/x, 1/(x+d). Sum of reciprocals = 3x = 1/4 ⇒ x = 1/12. Sum of numbers = 1/(1/12−d) + 12 + 1/(1/12+d) = 37. Solving gives d = 1/60 or −1/60. Numbers: 10, 12, 15.

Ratio in Logarithmic and Exponential Equations

Logarithms convert multiplicative ratios into additive differences: log(a/b) = log a − log b. The change-of-base formula logab = logcb/logca is a ratio of logarithms. In equations like ax = by, taking logs gives x log a = y log b ⇒ x/y = log b/log a = logab. This ratio is the key to solving exponential equations with different bases. For example, 2x = 3y = 6z. Let each equal k. Then 2 = k1/x, 3 = k1/y, 6 = k1/z. Since 2×3 = 6, k1/x+1/y = k1/z ⇒ 1/x + 1/y = 1/z. The ratio (1/x) : (1/y) : (1/z) satisfies this linear relation. Another pattern: ax + ay = az. Dividing by amin(x,y,z) reduces to a ratio equation in powers of a. If x ≤ y ≤ z, then 1 + ay−x = az−x. For integer solutions, this forces a = 2, y−x = 1, z−x = 2 (since 1+2=4). The ratio of exponents (x : y : z) = (t : t+1 : t+2). In logarithmic inequalities, the ratio logab / logba = (logab)2 ≥ 1. The function f(x) = logx(x+1) is decreasing for x > 1 because the ratio ln(x+1)/ln x decreases. A frequent question: “Compare log35 and log53.” Since log35 > 1 and log53 < 1, the ratio log35 / log53 = (log35)2 > 1. For equations like logx2 + log2x2 = 3, use change of base: 1/log2x + 1/(1+log2x) = 3. Let t = log2x. Then 1/t + 1/(1+t) = 3 ⇒ 3t2 + t − 1 = 0. The ratio of roots is −1/3.
