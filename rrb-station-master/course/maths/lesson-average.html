1. Introduction to Average

1.1 Definition and Basic Formula

The arithmetic mean, commonly called the average, is the single value that represents the central tendency of a set of numbers by distributing the total sum equally among all observations. If a data set contains n observations denoted by x₁, x₂, …, xₙ, the average A is obtained by adding all observations and dividing the sum by n, expressed as A = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) ÷ n. This definition implies that the average is sensitive to every value in the set; a change in any single observation alters the total sum and therefore the average itself. The formula is linear, which means that multiplying every observation by a constant k multiplies the average by the same constant, and adding a constant c to every observation increases the average by c. These properties make the average a convenient tool for summarising data, but they also mean that extreme values can distort the representative quality of the mean.

1.2 Properties of Simple Average

One fundamental property is that the sum of deviations of the observations from their average is always zero; mathematically Σ(xᵢ − A) = 0. This follows directly from the definition because the total excess above the mean exactly balances the total deficit below the mean. Another property is that the average of a combined group can be found from the averages and sizes of the constituent groups: if group 1 has n₁ observations with average A₁ and group 2 has n₂ observations with average A₂, the overall average A = (n₁A₁ + n₂A₂) ÷ (n₁ + n₂). This additive nature is the basis for weighted averages, which will be discussed later. The average also lies between the minimum and maximum values of the data set, guaranteeing that it never exceeds the range of the observations.

1.3 Illustrative Examples

Consider a train that stops at five stations and records the number of passengers boarding at each stop as 12, 18, 25, 30, and 15. The total number of passengers is 12 + 18 + 25 + 30 + 15 = 100, and with five stops the average boarding per stop is 100 ÷ 5 = 20 passengers. If the railway authority adds a sixth stop where 40 passengers board, the new total becomes 140 and the new average becomes 140 ÷ 6 ≈ 23.33, illustrating how a single large observation pulls the average upward. Conversely, if a stop with only 5 passengers is added, the average falls to 105 ÷ 6 = 17.5, showing the symmetric effect of low values. These simple calculations form the backbone of many quantitative aptitude questions in railway recruitment examinations.

2. Weighted Average

2.1 Concept and Formula

In many practical situations the observations do not carry equal importance; some values represent larger groups, longer durations, or higher frequencies. The weighted average accommodates this by assigning a weight wᵢ to each observation xᵢ, reflecting its relative significance. The weighted average W is computed as W = (w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ) ÷ (w₁ + w₂ + … + wₙ). When all weights are equal, the weighted average reduces to the simple arithmetic mean. The weights must be non‑negative and at least one weight must be positive; they are often whole numbers such as the number of items, the number of days, or the quantity of goods, but they can also be fractional if they represent proportions.

2.2 When to Use Weighted Average

Weighted averages appear naturally in problems involving mixtures, average speed over unequal distances, and combined groups of different sizes. For instance, if a train travels 200 km at 50 km/h and another 300 km at 60 km/h, the distances (200 and 300) serve as weights for the respective speeds because the time spent at each speed is proportional to the distance. The overall average speed is therefore (200×50 + 300×60) ÷ (200 + 300) = 56 km/h, not the simple mean of 55 km/h. Similarly, in a class where 30 students scored an average of 70 marks and 20 students scored an average of 80 marks, the class average is (30×70 + 20×80) ÷ 50 = 74 marks. Recognising the appropriate weights is the key step; once identified, the computation follows the same linear pattern as the simple average.

Example 1: Weighted Average in a Mixture Problem
A grocer mixes 10 kg of rice costing ₹40 per kg with 15 kg of rice costing ₹50 per kg. Find the average cost per kilogram of the mixture.
The quantities 10 kg and 15 kg act as weights for the respective costs. The total cost is (10 × 40) + (15 × 50) = 400 + 750 = ₹1150. The total weight is 10 + 15 = 25 kg. The weighted average cost = 1150 ÷ 25 = ₹46 per kg. This result lies between the two individual costs, closer to ₹50 because the larger quantity (15 kg) carries more weight.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a problem gives two groups with their averages and sizes, always compute the combined average using the weighted‑average formula rather than averaging the two averages; the latter is correct only when the group sizes are equal.

3. Effect of Replacement

3.1 Replacement of One Observation

Replacement problems ask how the average changes when one observation in a data set is removed and a new observation is inserted. Suppose a set of n numbers has average A. If an observation x is replaced by y, the new sum becomes nA − x + y, and the new average A′ = (nA − x + y) ÷ n = A + (y − x) ÷ n. The change in average depends only on the difference between the new and old values divided by the number of observations. This linear relationship means that replacing a value with a larger one always increases the average, and the magnitude of increase is inversely proportional to the size of the data set. For large n, a single replacement has a negligible effect, which is why averages of large populations are stable.

3.2 Replacement of Multiple Observations

When several observations are replaced simultaneously, the principle extends additively. If k observations x₁, x₂, …, x_k are replaced by y₁, y₂, …, y_k, the new average A′ = A + Σ(yᵢ − xᵢ) ÷ n. The total change is the sum of individual differences divided by n. This formula is particularly useful in problems where a group of students leaves a class and another group joins, or when a train’s passenger count at several stations is revised. The order of replacement does not matter because addition is commutative; only the net difference matters.

Consider a class of 40 students with an average weight of 55 kg. If three students weighing 50 kg, 52 kg, and 48 kg leave and three new students weighing 60 kg, 58 kg, and 62 kg join, the net change in sum is (60−50)+(58−52)+(62−48) = 10+6+14 = 30 kg. The new average becomes 55 + 30 ÷ 40 = 55.75 kg. This example shows how multiple replacements can be handled in a single step without recalculating the entire sum.

4. Average Speed

4.1 Definition and Common Misconception

Average speed is defined as the total distance travelled divided by the total time taken. A frequent error is to average the speeds of individual segments directly, which is correct only when each segment consumes the same amount of time. Because time = distance ÷ speed, segments of equal distance but different speeds take different times, so the simple mean of speeds over‑weights the faster segment. The correct approach always returns to the fundamental definition: total distance ÷ total time. This definition guarantees consistency regardless of how the journey is partitioned.

4.2 Formula for Uniform and Non‑Uniform Segments

If a journey consists of m segments with distances d₁, d₂, …, d_m and corresponding speeds v₁, v₂, …, v_m, the time for segment i is tᵢ = dᵢ ÷ vᵢ. The average speed V_avg = (d₁ + d₂ + … + d_m) ÷ (t₁ + t₂ + … + t_m) = Σdᵢ ÷ Σ(dᵢ ÷ vᵢ). When all distances are equal, say each segment is d, the formula simplifies to V_avg = m ÷ Σ(1 ÷ vᵢ), which is the harmonic mean of the speeds. When all times are equal, the average speed reduces to the arithmetic mean of the speeds. Recognising whether the problem gives equal distances or equal times allows the candidate to choose the appropriate mean instantly.

4.3 Worked Problems

A train travels the first 120 km at 60 km/h, the next 180 km at 90 km/h, and the final 200 km at 80 km/h. The total distance is 500 km. The times are 120 ÷ 60 = 2 h, 180 ÷ 90 = 2 h, and 200 ÷ 80 = 2.5 h, giving a total time of 6.5 h. The average speed is 500 ÷ 6.5 ≈ 76.92 km/h. Notice that the first two segments took equal time, so their combined average speed is the arithmetic mean (75 km/h), but the third segment’s longer time pulls the overall average toward its lower speed. Another typical question: a person covers a certain distance at 30 km/h and returns over the same distance at 20 km/h. Because distances are equal, the average speed for the round trip is the harmonic mean 2 × 30 × 20 ÷ (30 + 20) = 24 km/h. Mastery of these two patterns — equal distance and equal time — covers the vast majority of average‑speed questions in railway examinations.

5. Combined Applications and Problem‑Solving Strategies

5.1 Mixed Problems Involving Average, Weighted Average and Replacement

Examination questions often blend concepts: a data set’s average is given, a few observations are replaced, and then a weighted average with another group is required. The systematic approach is to track the total sum and total count at each stage. Start with the known average and count to obtain the initial sum. Apply each replacement by adjusting the sum using the difference of new and old values. After all replacements, compute the new average if needed. When a second group is combined, treat the first group’s sum and count as one weighted component and the second group’s sum and count as the other, then apply the weighted‑average formula. This step‑by‑step bookkeeping prevents the common mistake of averaging averages without regard to group sizes.

5.2 Shortcut Techniques and Checks

Several shortcuts accelerate calculations. If the average of n numbers is A and each number is increased by a constant c, the new average is simply A + c; similarly, multiplying each number by k multiplies the average by k. In replacement problems, the change in average equals the net change in sum divided by n, so one can often compute the answer mentally by focusing on the difference rather than recomputing the entire sum. For average speed, remember the harmonic‑mean shortcut for equal distances and the arithmetic‑mean shortcut for equal times. A quick sanity check is that the overall average speed must lie between the minimum and maximum segment speeds; if a computed value falls outside this range, an error has occurred.

5.3 Practice Approach

Consistent practice with varied data sets builds intuition for selecting the correct formula. Begin with simple average problems to internalise the sum‑count relationship, then progress to weighted averages by creating mixtures of two or three components. Next, solve replacement exercises where only the change in average is asked, which reinforces the linear nature of the average. Finally, tackle average‑speed questions that mix equal‑distance and equal‑time segments. Reviewing each solved problem by writing a one‑sentence summary of the method used creates a personal reference guide that is far more effective than memorising isolated formulas. Regular timed drills simulate examination conditions and improve both speed and accuracy.

Average of Arithmetic Progressions and Symmetric Sequences

An arithmetic progression (AP) is a sequence where each term differs from the previous one by a constant difference d. For any AP with n terms, the average (arithmetic mean) equals the average of the first and last terms, and also equals the average of any pair of terms equidistant from the ends. Mathematically, if the terms are a, a+d, …, a+(n-1)d, then the sum S = n × (first + last) ÷ 2, and the average A = S ÷ n = (first + last) ÷ 2. This property holds because the sequence is symmetric about its midpoint. When n is odd, the middle term is exactly the average; when n is even, the average is the arithmetic mean of the two central terms.

This principle extends to any symmetric data set — not only APs. If a list of numbers can be paired so that each pair sums to the same constant C, the overall average is C ÷ 2. For instance, the set {2, 5, 8, 11, 14} is an AP with first=2, last=14, average=8. The pairs (2,14), (5,11) each sum to 16, and the unpaired middle term 8 equals 16÷2. Recognising symmetry allows instant calculation of the average without summing all terms, a valuable shortcut in time‑pressured examinations.

Consider a problem: “Find the average of the first 50 even natural numbers.” The sequence is 2, 4, 6, …, 100. First=2, last=100, average = (2+100)÷2 = 51. No need to compute the sum 2550 and divide by 50. Another example: “The average of 7 consecutive integers is 20. What is the largest integer?” For consecutive integers, the average equals the middle (4th) term, so the terms are 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23; the largest is 23. In general, for any set of k consecutive integers, the average is the median, and the extremes are average ± (k-1)÷2.

When the progression is not strictly arithmetic but consists of two interleaved APs (e.g., odd and even positions follow different differences), the overall average can still be found by averaging the averages of the two sub‑progressions weighted by their counts. This technique reduces a seemingly complex series to two simple AP averages.

Reverse Engineering Averages from Partial Information

Many examination questions provide the average of a group after certain observations have been added, removed, or replaced, and ask for an unknown original value or the value of a discarded observation. The fundamental tool is the relationship Sum = Average × Count. By tracking how the sum and count change at each step, the missing value can be isolated algebraically.

Suppose the average of n numbers is A. The total sum is S = nA. If a new number x is added, the new average becomes (S+x)÷(n+1). If a number y is removed, the new average is (S-y)÷(n-1). If a number y is replaced by x, the sum changes by x-y and the new average is A + (x-y)÷n. These linear adjustments make it possible to work backwards: given the final average and the operation, solve for the unknown.

A common pattern: “The average of 12 numbers is 35. When two numbers are discarded, the average of the remaining 10 numbers becomes 32. If one discarded number is 40, find the other.” Initial sum = 12×35 = 420. Final sum = 10×32 = 320. Sum of discarded numbers = 420-320 = 100. One discarded = 40, so the other = 60. The same logic applies when numbers are added: “The average of 8 numbers is 25. After including two more numbers, the average becomes 27. If one of the new numbers is 30, find the other.” Initial sum = 200. New sum = 10×27 = 270. Sum of added numbers = 70. One added = 30, the other = 40.

More complex scenarios involve overlapping groups. For example: “In a class of 30 students, the average weight is 50 kg. The average weight of the first 18 students is 48 kg, and the average weight of the last 18 students is 52 kg. Find the weight of the 18th student (who belongs to both groups).” Sum of all 30 = 1500. Sum of first 18 = 864. Sum of last 18 = 936. The 18th student is counted twice in the sum of the two groups, so weight = (864+936) - 1500 = 300 kg. This yields 300 kg, which is unrealistic for a student's weight. Recalculating confirms the result: 864+936=1800; 1800-1500=300. Such inconsistency checks are part of reverse engineering — if the computed value is implausible, the given averages cannot coexist. In valid problems, the overlapping element will be a plausible number.

Another variant: “The average of five numbers is 20. The average of the first three is 18, and the average of the last three is 22. Find the third number.” Sum of five = 100. Sum of first three = 54. Sum of last three = 66. The third number is included in both triplets, so third = (54+66) - 100 = 20. This symmetry often yields the third number equal to the overall average.

Reverse Engineering Worked Example
The average of 10 numbers is 45. When two numbers are removed, the average of the remaining 8 numbers becomes 42. If one of the removed numbers is 50, find the other removed number.
Initial sum = 10 × 45 = 450. Final sum = 8 × 42 = 336. Sum of the two removed numbers = 450 - 336 = 114. One removed number = 50, therefore the other = 114 - 50 = 64. Answer: 64.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When reverse engineering, always write the total sum before and after each operation. This single step eliminates sign errors and makes the unknown instantly visible.

Average in Multi‑Component Mixture and Alligation Problems

Mixture problems are a direct application of weighted average where the weights are quantities (volumes, masses, counts) and the values are concentrations, prices, or other intensive properties. The alligation rule provides a visual shortcut: when two components of values v₁ and v₂ (with v₁ < v₂) are mixed to obtain a mean value M, the ratio of their quantities is (v₂ - M) : (M - v₁). This follows from the weighted‑average equation q₁v₁ + q₂v₂ = (q₁+q₂)M ⇒ q₁(M-v₁) = q₂(v₂-M).

For three or more components, the alligation can be applied successively. Mix components 1 and 2 to get an intermediate mean, then mix that result with component 3, and so on. Alternatively, set up a system of linear equations: if quantities are q₁, q₂, q₃ and values v₁, v₂, v₃, then q₁v₁+q₂v₂+q₃v₃ = (q₁+q₂+q₃)M. With two independent ratios given (e.g., q₁:q₂ and q₂:q₃), the third ratio follows. In many examination questions, only the final mean and two component values are given, and the ratio of all three quantities is asked; the solution uses the fact that the weighted average of three numbers lies between the smallest and largest, and the distances from the mean are inversely proportional to the quantities.

Example: “Tea worth ₹120/kg and ₹150/kg are mixed with a third variety in the ratio 1:1:2. If the mixture is worth ₹140/kg, find the price of the third variety.” Let the third price be x. Quantities: 1, 1, 2 kg. Weighted average: (1×120 + 1×150 + 2×x) ÷ 4 = 140 ⇒ 270 + 2x = 560 ⇒ 2x = 290 ⇒ x = ₹145/kg. Using alligation: the mean 140 lies between 120 and 150; the first two are in equal proportion, so their combined average is 135. Now mix 135 (weight 2) with x (weight 2) to get 140. Since weights are equal, 140 is the arithmetic mean of 135 and x ⇒ x = 145.

Another frequent type: “A 20 L mixture contains milk and water in the ratio 3:1. How much water must be added to make the ratio 2:1?” Milk = 15 L, water = 5 L. Let added water = w. New water = 5+w. Ratio milk:water = 15:(5+w) = 2:1 ⇒ 15 = 2(5+w) ⇒ 15 = 10+2w ⇒ w = 2.5 L. This is a replacement‑style problem where the total quantity changes but the amount of milk stays constant.

When the mixture involves cost and profit, the same weighted‑average framework applies. “A shopkeeper mixes two types of rice costing ₹30/kg and ₹40/kg and sells the mixture at ₹42/kg, making a 20% profit. Find the mixing ratio.” Cost price of mixture = 42 ÷ 1.2 = ₹35/kg. Alligation between 30 and 40 with mean 35 gives ratio (40-35):(35-30) = 5:5 = 1:1.

Average Speed in Journeys with Rest Periods and Variable Segments

The definition of average speed — total distance ÷ total time — remains valid even when the journey includes rest periods, speed changes, or acceleration phases. A rest period contributes zero distance but positive time, thereby reducing the overall average speed. If a vehicle travels a distance d at speed v, then rests for time tᵣ, the average speed for that segment plus rest is d ÷ (d/v + tᵣ). For a multi‑segment journey with rests, sum all distances and sum all times (travel + rest) before dividing.

Consider a trip: 100 km at 50 km/h, rest 30 min, then 150 km at 60 km/h. Travel times: 100÷50 = 2 h, 150÷60 = 2.5 h. Rest = 0.5 h. Total distance = 250 km. Total time = 2 + 0.5 + 2.5 = 5 h. Average speed = 250 ÷ 5 = 50 km/h. Note that the simple average of the two travel speeds (55 km/h) is higher because it ignores the rest.

When speed varies continuously (e.g., uniform acceleration), the distance covered is the area under the speed‑time graph. If the graph is a straight line from speed u to v over time T, the area (distance) = (u+v)÷2 × T, and the average speed = (u+v)÷2, which is the arithmetic mean of initial and final speeds. This matches the constant‑acceleration formula. For piecewise constant speeds with instantaneous changes, the earlier segment‑wise formula applies.

A common trap: “A car travels the first half of the distance at 40 km/h and the second half at 60 km/h. It stops for 15 minutes at the midpoint. Find the average speed.” Let total distance = 2d. Time for first half = d÷40, second half = d÷60, rest = 0.25 h. Total time = d(1/40+1/60) + 0.25 = d(5/120) + 0.25 = d/24 + 0.25. Average speed = 2d ÷ (d/24 + 0.25). If d = 120 km, average = 240 ÷ (5 + 0.25) = 240 ÷ 5.25 ≈ 45.71 km/h. Without rest, the harmonic mean would be 48 km/h; the rest lowers it further.

In problems where the journey is described in terms of time fractions (e.g., “travels at 30 km/h for the first half of the time and 50 km/h for the second half”), the average speed is the arithmetic mean of the two speeds because the times are equal. If a rest occurs during one of those time intervals, that interval’s effective speed becomes zero for the rest duration, and the average must be recomputed using total distance ÷ total time.

  • Assuming the average of an AP is the middle term when the number of terms is even — it is the mean of the two central terms.
  • Forgetting to adjust the count when reverse‑engineering after addition or removal of observations.
  • Applying the two‑component alligation rule directly to three‑component mixtures without reducing to pairwise steps.
  • Treating rest time as a segment with the same speed as the adjacent travel segment instead of speed zero.
  • Using the harmonic mean for equal‑time segments or the arithmetic mean for equal‑distance segments.
  • Neglecting to verify that a computed average lies within the range of the data set, which signals an algebraic error.

Average in Problems with Overlapping Groups and Inclusion–Exclusion

When two or more groups share members, a naïve average of group averages double‑counts the common members. The correct overall average applies the inclusion–exclusion principle to sums and counts. Let group A have n₁ members with sum S₁, group B have n₂ members with sum S₂, and their intersection have n₁₂ members with sum S₁₂. The combined sum is S₁ + S₂ – S₁₂ and the combined count is n₁ + n₂ – n₁₂. The overall average = (S₁ + S₂ – S₁₂) ÷ (n₁ + n₂ – n₁₂). This extends to three or more groups by alternately adding and subtracting intersections of increasing order.

Example: “In a class, 30 students play cricket, 25 play football, and 10 play both. The average runs scored by cricket players is 40, by football players is 30, and by those who play both is 35. Find the average runs of all students who play at least one sport.” Here n₁=30, S₁=30×40=1200; n₂=25, S₂=25×30=750; n₁₂=10, S₁₂=10×35=350. Combined sum = 1200+750–350 = 1600. Combined count = 30+25–10 = 45. Overall average = 1600 ÷ 45 ≈ 35.56.

If the intersection average is not given directly, it can be derived from additional data such as the overall average of the union or the average of the exclusive parts. For three groups A, B, C the formula becomes: total sum = ΣSᵢ – ΣSᵢⱼ + S₁₂₃; total count = Σnᵢ – Σnᵢⱼ + n₁₂₃. Intersection sums must be consistent with individual group sums; otherwise the data is contradictory.

In problems where groups are defined by overlapping criteria (e.g., students taking Mathematics, Physics, Chemistry), the same principle applies. Examiners often test whether the candidate recognises that the simple average of the three subject averages is not the average of all students unless the groups are disjoint. A quick check: the overall average must lie between the minimum and maximum of the given group averages; if it falls outside, an algebraic error has occurred.

Average of Percentages, Rates, and Growth Factors

Averages of percentages are not computed by the arithmetic mean unless the percentages refer to the same base quantity. When bases differ, a weighted average with the bases as weights is required. For example, a student scores 80 % in a test of 50 marks and 70 % in a test of 100 marks. The overall percentage = (0.80×50 + 0.70×100) ÷ (50+100) = (40+70) ÷ 150 = 73.33 %, not (80+70)÷2 = 75 %.

For successive percentage changes, the effective growth factor is the product of individual factors. The average growth rate per period (geometric mean) is the n‑th root of the product minus one. If a population grows by 10 %, then 20 %, then 5 % over three years, the overall factor = 1.10×1.20×1.05 = 1.386. The average annual growth rate = 1.386^(1/3) – 1 ≈ 0.115 = 11.5 %. Using the arithmetic mean (11.67 %) overstates the true compound rate.

Rates such as speed, work rate, or interest rate follow the same rule: the harmonic mean applies when the same distance or work is covered at different rates, while the arithmetic mean applies when the same time is spent at each rate. In finance, the average rate of return over multiple periods with varying principal amounts is the weighted arithmetic mean of the period rates, weighted by the principal at the start of each period.

A common trap: “A shopkeeper marks up goods by 20 % and then offers a discount of 10 %. What is the profit percentage?” The effective factor = 1.20×0.90 = 1.08, so profit = 8 %. The arithmetic mean of +20 % and –10 % would give +5 %, which is incorrect. Always convert percentages to multiplicative factors before averaging.

Technique: Weighted Average of Percentages with Different Bases
A candidate scores 75 % in Paper I (maximum 200 marks), 80 % in Paper II (maximum 150 marks), and 65 % in Paper III (maximum 100 marks). Find the overall percentage.
Convert each percentage to marks obtained: Paper I = 0.75×200 = 150; Paper II = 0.80×150 = 120; Paper III = 0.65×100 = 65. Total marks obtained = 150+120+65 = 335. Total maximum marks = 200+150+100 = 450. Overall percentage = (335 ÷ 450) × 100 = 74.44 %. The arithmetic mean of the three percentages (73.33 %) is wrong because the papers have different maximum marks.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem presents percentages with different denominators, immediately write down the base quantities. Multiply each percentage by its base to get the absolute contribution, sum the contributions, and divide by the sum of bases. This avoids the common mistake of averaging the percentages directly.

Average in Problems with Successive Replacements and Changing Weights

Successive replacement problems involve removing a portion of a mixture and replacing it with another substance, repeated multiple times. After each replacement the total quantity may stay constant or change, and the concentration of a component follows a geometric progression. If a vessel contains V units of pure liquid A, and each operation removes r units and replaces with liquid B, the amount of A after k operations is V×(1 – r/V)^k. The average concentration of A over the k operations (if asked) is the arithmetic mean of the concentrations after each step, but more often the final concentration is required.

When the removed quantity is a fixed fraction of the current mixture, the formula simplifies to Aₖ = A₀×(1 – f)^k where f is the fraction removed each time. If the replacement substance also contains some of the original component, the recurrence becomes Aₖ₊₁ = Aₖ×(1 – f) + C×f where C is the concentration in the added substance. This linear recurrence solves to Aₖ = (A₀ – C)×(1 – f)^k + C.

Problems may ask for the number of operations needed to reach a certain concentration. Taking logarithms gives k = log((target – C)/(A₀ – C)) ÷ log(1 – f). Ensure the argument of the log is positive; otherwise the target is unattainable.

If the total volume changes (e.g., adding pure water without removal), the weight of the component stays constant while the denominator increases. The new average = (original amount) ÷ (original volume + added volume). Combining removal and addition in a single step requires careful bookkeeping of both numerator and denominator.

Average in Problems with Errors, Corrections, and Adjusted Means

When a data entry error is discovered, the corrected mean can be found without recomputing the entire sum. If the original mean of n observations is M, and one observation x was wrongly recorded as y, the corrected sum = n×M – y + x, and the corrected mean = (n×M – y + x) ÷ n. This extends to multiple errors: subtract the sum of wrong values, add the sum of correct values, then divide by n.

If an observation is omitted and later included, treat the omission as a wrong value of 0 (or the value used in the original calculation) and apply the same correction. When a new observation is added, the new mean = (n×M + new_value) ÷ (n+1). Conversely, if an observation is removed, the new mean = (n×M – removed_value) ÷ (n–1).

In grouped frequency distributions, if the frequency of a class is corrected, adjust the total frequency and the sum of (frequency × class mark) accordingly. The corrected mean = (original_sum + Δf×class_mark) ÷ (original_N + Δf). If the class boundaries change, recompute the class marks for affected classes before adjusting.

A frequent examiner twist: “The average of 20 numbers is 35. It is found that two numbers, 42 and 28, were misread as 24 and 82. Find the correct average.” Original sum = 20×35 = 700. Wrong sum contributed = 24+82 = 106. Correct sum contributed = 42+28 = 70. Corrected sum = 700 – 106 + 70 = 664. Corrected mean = 664 ÷ 20 = 33.2. Always verify that the corrected mean lies between the minimum and maximum possible values of the data set.

Average in Problems with Time‑Weighted Averages and Moving Averages

Time‑weighted averages arise when a quantity varies over time and the average over a period is required. If a variable takes value vᵢ for duration tᵢ, the time‑weighted average = Σ(vᵢ×tᵢ) ÷ Σtᵢ. This is identical to the weighted average formula with time as weight. In a journey with speed changes, this reduces to total distance ÷ total time, which is the definition of average speed.

A moving average of order k smooths a time series by averaging the most recent k observations. For a sequence x₁, x₂, …, the simple moving average at time n (n ≥ k) is (xₙ + xₙ₋₁ + … + xₙ₋ₖ₊₁) ÷ k. The recursive update: SMAₙ = SMAₙ₋₁ + (xₙ – xₙ₋ₖ) ÷ k. This allows O(1) computation per new data point.

An exponential moving average (EMA) gives more weight to recent observations: EMAₙ = α×xₙ + (1–α)×EMAₙ₋₁, where α = 2 ÷ (k+1) for a span equivalent to a simple moving average of length k. EMA is used in financial technical analysis; exam problems may ask to compute EMA for a short series given α.

When a problem asks for the average of a quantity over a cycle (e.g., average temperature over a day with hourly readings), treat each reading as having equal time weight if intervals are equal. If intervals differ, use the time‑weighted formula. A common trap is to average the maximum and minimum values, which only equals the true average for a symmetric triangular waveform.

  • Assuming the arithmetic mean of percentages with different bases gives the correct overall percentage.
  • Using the arithmetic mean for successive growth rates instead of the geometric mean.
  • Forgetting to adjust both numerator and denominator when a replacement changes the total quantity.
  • Applying the simple average to overlapping groups without subtracting the intersection sum and count.
  • Correcting a mean by only adjusting the erroneous value and ignoring the effect on the total sum.
  • Always verify that the computed average lies within the range of the data set.

Average of Ratios and the Ratio of Averages

When a problem presents several ratios — speed‑time pairs, price‑quantity pairs, or concentration‑volume pairs — the overall average ratio is not the arithmetic mean of the individual ratios unless the denominators are equal. The correct overall ratio is the ratio of the sums: (Σ numerator) ÷ (Σ denominator). This is the weighted average of the ratios with the denominators as weights. For example, if a vehicle travels 60 km at 30 km/h and 40 km at 40 km/h, the average speed is total distance ÷ total time = (60+40) ÷ (60/30 + 40/40) = 100 ÷ (2+1) = 33.33 km/h, not (30+40)/2 = 35 km/h. The harmonic mean applies when the numerators are equal (equal distances) and the denominators vary; the arithmetic mean applies when the denominators are equal (equal times). A common examiner twist is to give a set of ratios and ask for the “average ratio” without stating whether the underlying quantities are equal. The safe procedure is to reconstruct the original quantities, sum numerators and denominators separately, and then divide.

When the Harmonic Mean Is the Correct Average

If a journey consists of several legs of equal distance d, with speeds v₁, v₂, …, vₙ, the average speed for the whole journey is the harmonic mean of the speeds: n ÷ (Σ 1/vᵢ). This follows because each leg takes time d/vᵢ, total time = d Σ 1/vᵢ, total distance = n d, so average speed = n d ÷ (d Σ 1/vᵢ) = n ÷ Σ 1/vᵢ. The same formula applies to average rates such as work rates, flow rates, or any situation where the numerator (output) is fixed and the denominator (input) varies.

When the Arithmetic Mean Is the Correct Average

If the denominators are equal — for instance, equal time intervals at different speeds — the average speed is the arithmetic mean of the speeds. More generally, if we have ratios rᵢ = aᵢ / b with a common denominator b, the average ratio = (Σ aᵢ) / (n b) = (1/n) Σ (aᵢ/b) = arithmetic mean of the ratios. Recognising which quantity is held constant decides the appropriate mean.

Weighted Average of Ratios with Unequal Weights

In most realistic data the denominators differ. The overall ratio = Σ aᵢ ÷ Σ bᵢ. This can be written as Σ (bᵢ / Σ bⱼ) × (aᵢ / bᵢ), i.e. a weighted average of the individual ratios with weights proportional to the denominators. The weight of each ratio is exactly its denominator’s share of the total denominator. This formulation is useful for quick mental checks: if one ratio has a huge denominator, it dominates the overall average.

Average in Problems with Grouped Frequency Distributions and Open‑Ended Classes

Grouped data appear when raw observations are summarised into class intervals with frequencies. The standard estimate of the mean uses class marks (mid‑points) as representatives: Mean ≈ Σ (fᵢ × xᵢ) ÷ Σ fᵢ, where xᵢ is the class mark of the i‑th class. This assumes a uniform distribution within each class. When the first or last class is open‑ended (e.g., “below 10” or “70 and above”), a class mark cannot be computed directly. The usual practical approach is to assume the open‑ended class has the same width as the adjacent closed class, or to use the median of the adjacent class as a proxy. If the distribution is roughly symmetric, the error introduced is small; for highly skewed data the estimate can be biased. Examiners often test the effect of changing the assumed width of an open‑ended class on the computed mean.

Adjustment for Class Boundary Changes

If class boundaries are altered — for instance, merging two adjacent classes or splitting a class — the frequencies must be redistributed. When merging, the new frequency is the sum of the two frequencies and the new class mark is the midpoint of the combined interval. When splitting, an assumption about the intra‑class distribution is needed; the simplest is to allocate frequencies proportionally to the sub‑interval widths. The corrected mean is then recomputed with the new Σ (fᵢ × xᵢ) and Σ fᵢ.

Effect of Sheppard’s Correction

For a continuous variable grouped into equal‑width classes, the variance computed from class marks overestimates the true variance by h²/12, where h is the class width. Sheppard’s correction subtracts this amount: corrected variance = grouped variance – h²/12. The mean itself is unbiased under the uniform‑within‑class assumption, but the correction is relevant when the problem asks for the standard deviation or coefficient of variation after grouping.

Weighted Mean of Grouped Data with Different Class Widths

When class widths differ, the class mark remains the midpoint, but the assumption of uniform density implies that the frequency density (frequency ÷ width) should be used for graphical representation. The mean formula Σ (fᵢ × xᵢ) ÷ Σ fᵢ remains valid because each observation is still represented once. However, if the problem provides frequency densities instead of frequencies, convert to frequencies first: fᵢ = densityᵢ × widthᵢ.

Technique: Reconstructing the Mean from an Open‑Ended Distribution
A frequency distribution of marks (out of 100) for 60 students is given below. The first and last classes are open‑ended. Compute the mean marks assuming the open‑ended classes have the same width as the adjacent closed classes. Exam‑Day Strategy
When you see an open‑ended class, first note the width of the neighbouring closed class. Write the assumed limits explicitly, compute the mid‑points, and then apply the standard Σf×x ÷ Σf formula. If the question asks how the mean changes when a different width is assumed, set up the mean as a function of the assumed width and differentiate or compare two numeric cases — this avoids re‑doing the whole table.

Average in Problems with Nested Groups and Hierarchical Averaging

Many real‑world data sets have a hierarchical structure: students within classes within schools, employees within departments within divisions, or products within categories within a store. The overall average can be obtained by a two‑stage (or multi‑stage) weighted average. If group j has nⱼ members with mean μⱼ, the overall mean = Σ (nⱼ μⱼ) ÷ Σ nⱼ. This is exactly the weighted average of the group means with group sizes as weights. A common error is to average the group means directly (arithmetic mean of μⱼ), which is correct only when all groups have equal size. Examiners often present a table of group means and sizes and ask for the overall mean, or give the overall mean and all but one group mean and ask for the missing group mean.

Decomposition of Total Variance

While the lesson focuses on averages, understanding the variance decomposition helps in checking consistency. Total sum of squares = Σ nⱼ (μⱼ – μ)² + Σ Σ (xᵢⱼ – μⱼ)². The first term is the between‑group variation, the second is the within‑group variation. If a problem supplies the overall mean, group means, and group sizes, you can verify whether a given within‑group variance is plausible.

Missing Group Size from Overall Mean

Suppose the overall mean of three groups is known, two group means and their sizes are given, and the third group’s size is unknown. Let the unknown size be k. Then overall mean = (n₁μ₁ + n₂μ₂ + k μ₃) ÷ (n₁ + n₂ + k). Solve for k: k = (n₁μ₁ + n₂μ₂ – (n₁+n₂) μ_overall) ÷ (μ_overall – μ₃). The denominator must be non‑zero; if μ_overall = μ₃ the overall mean equals the third group’s mean regardless of its size, and k cannot be determined uniquely.

Hierarchical Weighted Averages with Different Weighting Schemes

Sometimes the weight at a higher level is not the raw count but a derived quantity — for example, average revenue per store where each store’s weight is its floor area. The formula remains Σ (weightⱼ × meanⱼ) ÷ Σ weightⱼ. The key is to identify the correct weight at each level. A typical trap: the problem gives average marks per class and number of students per class, but then asks for the average marks per school where each school’s weight is the number of classes, not the number of students. Always read the question to see which entity is the “observation” for the final average.

Average in Problems with Simpson’s Paradox and Weighted Aggregation

Simpson’s paradox occurs when a trend appears in several groups of data but disappears or reverses when the groups are combined. The paradox is purely a consequence of weighted averaging with different group sizes. For instance, a treatment may have a higher success rate than a control in each hospital separately, but a lower overall success rate because the treatment was used more often in the hospital with a lower baseline success rate. The overall success rate = Σ (nᵢ × pᵢ) ÷ Σ nᵢ, where pᵢ is the success proportion in group i. If the treatment group sizes are skewed toward hospitals with low pᵢ, the weighted average can be lower than the control’s weighted average even though pᵢ(treatment) > pᵢ(control) for every i.

Detecting the Paradox in Tabular Data

When a table shows subgroup rates and overall rates, compute the weighted averages yourself. If the overall rate for A is lower than for B while every subgroup rate for A is higher, the paradox is present. The explanation always lies in the confounding variable (hospital, department, etc.) that affects both the grouping and the outcome. In exam questions, you may be asked to explain why the overall average contradicts the subgroup averages, or to compute the minimum sample size in a subgroup needed to reverse the overall trend.

Quantitative Condition for Reversal

Consider two groups with success rates a₁ > b₁ and a₂ > b₂. Let the sample sizes for A be n₁, n₂ and for B be m₁, m₂. The overall rates are A_overall = (n₁a₁ + n₂a₂)/(n₁+n₂) and B_overall = (m₁b₁ + m₂b₂)/(m₁+m₂). Reversal (A_overall < B_overall) occurs when the weighted average of a’s is pulled down by a large n₂ attached to a relatively low a₂, while B’s weighted average is pulled up by a large m₁ attached to a relatively high b₁. Algebraically, reversal ⇔ (n₁a₁ + n₂a₂)(m₁+m₂) < (m₁b₁ + m₂b₂)(n₁+n₂). This inequality can be rearranged to show the required ratio of sample sizes.

Application to Average Speed and Work Problems

Simpson’s paradox can appear in average speed questions: a car may have a higher average speed than a truck on each segment of a route, yet a lower overall average speed because the car spends more time on the slower segment. The same weighted‑average logic applies: overall speed = total distance ÷ total time. If the car’s distance share on the slow segment is larger, its overall speed can be lower. Recognising the paradox prevents the instinctive but wrong conclusion that “higher on every part implies higher overall”.

Average in Problems with Variable Volume Replacement and Concentration Dynamics

Replacement problems often assume the total volume stays constant. When the volume changes — for example, removing a fraction of a solution and then adding a different volume of another solution — both the amount of solute and the total volume must be tracked. Let the initial volume be V₀ with solute amount S₀ (so concentration C₀ = S₀/V₀). In one operation, a volume r is removed (taking away solute r·C_current) and a volume a of a solution with concentration C_add is added. The new solute amount S₁ = S₀ – r·C₀ + a·C_add, and the new volume V₁ = V₀ – r + a. The new concentration C₁ = S₁/V₁. If the operation repeats k times with the same r, a, and C_add, the recurrence is Sₙ₊₁ = Sₙ – r·(Sₙ/Vₙ) + a·C_add, Vₙ₊₁ = Vₙ – r + a. This is a non‑linear recurrence because the concentration Sₙ/Vₙ appears. However, if r = a (volume constant), it reduces to the linear recurrence Sₙ₊₁ = Sₙ(1 – r/V) + r·C_add, which solves to Sₙ = (S₀ – V·C_add)(1 – r/V)ⁿ + V·C_add.

Constant‑Fraction Removal with Variable Addition

A frequent variant: each step removes a fixed fraction f of the current mixture (so r = f·Vₙ) and adds a fixed volume a of pure solvent (C_add = 0). Then Vₙ₊₁ = Vₙ(1 – f) + a, and Sₙ₊₁ = Sₙ(1 – f). The solute amount decays geometrically: Sₙ = S₀(1 – f)ⁿ. The volume follows a linear recurrence: Vₙ = (V₀ – a/f)(1 – f)ⁿ + a/f (provided f ≠ 0). The concentration Cₙ = Sₙ/Vₙ = S₀(1 – f)ⁿ ÷ [(V₀ – a/f)(1 – f)ⁿ + a/f]. As n → ∞, if f > 0, (1 – f)ⁿ → 0, so Cₙ → 0. The approach to zero is slower when a is large because the denominator retains a constant term a/f.

Finding the Number of Operations to Reach a Target Concentration

When the recurrence is linear (constant volume), the closed form gives k = log((C_target – C_add)/(C₀ – C_add)) ÷ log(1 – r/V). The argument of the logarithm must be positive; otherwise the target is unattainable (e.g., trying to reach a concentration higher than both C₀ and C_add when only dilution occurs). For variable‑volume cases, no simple closed form exists in general; the exam expects either iteration for small k or an approximation using the continuous‑time differential equation dS/dt = –f S, dV/dt = –f V + a, which yields the same formulas as the discrete case with (1 – f)ⁿ replaced by e–f n.

Mixing Two Replacement Streams Simultaneously

Some problems describe a tank with an inflow of one solution and an outflow of the mixture at different rates. Let inflow rate = Q_in with concentration C_in, outflow rate = Q_out. Volume changes as dV/dt = Q_in – Q_out. Solute changes as dS/dt = Q_in C_in – Q_out (S/V). This is a first‑order linear ODE. The steady state (if Q_in = Q_out) gives C_steady = C_in. If Q_in ≠ Q_out, the volume grows or shrinks linearly and the concentration approaches C_in asymptotically. In discrete‑time exam versions, the same logic applies step by step: update V and S each minute, then compute C = S/V.

  • Assuming the arithmetic mean of ratios when denominators differ — always reconstruct total numerator and denominator.
  • Using the class mark of an open‑ended class without stating the assumed width — explicitly write the assumed limits.
  • Averaging group means directly instead of weighting by group sizes — check whether groups are equal in size.
  • Ignoring the confounding variable in Simpson’s paradox — compute weighted averages for each side before concluding.
  • Treating variable‑volume replacement as constant‑volume — track both solute amount and total volume at each step.
  • Applying the harmonic mean when the constant quantity is the denominator (equal time) instead of the numerator (equal distance).
  • Forgetting to adjust the total frequency when a class frequency is corrected in grouped data — the denominator Σf changes as well as the numerator Σf×x.

The advanced sections above equip you to handle the most subtle average‑based questions that appear in competitive examinations.

Average of Geometric and Harmonic Progressions

While the arithmetic progression (AP) average is the mean of the first and last terms, geometric progression (GP) and harmonic progression (HP) require different handling because their terms are not linearly spaced. For a GP with first term a, common ratio r (r ≠ 1) and n terms, the terms are a, ar, ar², …, arⁿ⁻¹. The sum is a(rⁿ – 1)/(r – 1) and the average is this sum divided by n. No closed‑form simplification to a single term exists unless r = 1 (degenerate AP). In an HP, the reciprocals form an AP. If the HP terms are 1/x₁, 1/x₂, …, 1/xₙ where xₖ = x₁ + (k‑1)d, the arithmetic mean of the HP terms is (1/n) Σ(1/xₖ). The harmonic mean of the HP terms is n / Σ xₖ, which equals the harmonic mean of the corresponding AP terms. This harmonic mean is always ≤ the arithmetic mean of the same set, with equality only when all terms are equal. Problems often ask for the average of a GP or HP embedded in a larger data set; the key is to compute the sum of the progression first, then combine with other observations using the weighted‑average framework.

A frequent twist is a mixed sequence where a block of terms follows a GP while the rest are arbitrary numbers. Treat the GP block as a single group: compute its sum S_GP and count n_GP, then merge with other groups via total sum ÷ total count. For an HP block, compute the sum of the actual HP terms Σ(1/xₖ) using the AP formula for the reciprocals, then the block’s contribution to the overall sum is Σ(1/xₖ). The overall arithmetic mean of the combined data is total sum ÷ total count. If the problem asks for the overall harmonic mean, compute total count ÷ total sum of reciprocals. Remember that the harmonic mean is appropriate only when the quantities represent rates with a common numerator (e.g., speed for equal distances).

Examiners also test the relationship between the three Pythagorean means for a given set: arithmetic mean (AM) ≥ geometric mean (GM) ≥ harmonic mean (HM). For a GP, the GM of the terms equals the geometric mean of the first and last term, i.e. √(a·arⁿ⁻¹) = a·r⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾/². This equals the middle term when n is odd. For an HP, the HM of the terms equals the harmonic mean of the first and last term: 2 / (1/(1/a) + 1/(1/(a+(n‑1)d))) = 2/(2a+(n‑1)d). These identities allow quick verification of answers without full summation.

Weighted Geometric Mean and Logarithmic Averages in Growth Problems

When growth rates vary across periods and the periods have different lengths or weights, the arithmetic mean of the rates misrepresents the overall growth. The correct aggregate is the weighted geometric mean of the growth factors (1 + rᵢ). If period i has weight wᵢ (often the number of years or the proportion of capital invested), the overall growth factor G = ∏(1 + rᵢ)ʷⁱ and the equivalent constant rate r_eq satisfies (1 + r_eq) = G¹/Σwᵢ. Taking logarithms converts the product into a weighted arithmetic mean: ln(1 + r_eq) = Σ wᵢ ln(1 + rᵢ) / Σ wᵢ. This logarithmic average is the natural tool for compound growth, inflation indices, and portfolio returns where contributions occur at different times.

A common exam scenario: a fund grows at 10 % for 3 years, then 5 % for 2 years. The simple arithmetic average (10+5)/2 = 7.5 % is wrong. The weighted geometric mean uses weights 3 and 2: G = (1.10)³ × (1.05)² = 1.331 × 1.1025 = 1.467. The equivalent annual rate r_eq = G¹/⁵ – 1 = 1.467⁰·² – 1 ≈ 0.0796 or 7.96 %. The logarithmic route: ln G = 3 ln 1.10 + 2 ln 1.05 = 3×0.09531 + 2×0.04879 = 0.3835; divide by 5 → 0.0767; exponentiate → e⁰·⁰⁷⁶⁷ = 1.0797, same result. The logarithmic form also handles continuous compounding: if the instantaneous rate is δ(t), the average continuous rate over [0,T] is (1/T)∫₀ᵀ δ(t) dt, which is the time‑weighted arithmetic mean of the instantaneous rates.

Be careful when weights are not integers. If an investment of ₹10,000 earns 12 % for half a year and 8 % for the next half, the weights are 0.5 each. The overall factor = (1.12)⁰·⁵ × (1.08)⁰·⁵ = √(1.12×1.08) = √1.2096 = 1.0998, giving ≈ 9.98 % annualised. The arithmetic mean (12+8)/2 = 10 % is close but not exact; the difference widens with larger rate dispersion. In problems with cash‑flows (deposits/withdrawals), the weights become the time‑weighted capital amounts, leading to the money‑weighted return (internal rate of return) which is not a simple geometric mean but requires solving Σ CFₖ (1+r)⁻ᵗᵏ = 0. The exam usually restricts to time‑weighted geometric mean unless explicitly asked for IRR.

Technique: Money‑Weighted Return via Logarithms
A portfolio returns 15 % in year 1, –5 % in year 2, and 20 % in year 3. The capital at the start of each year is ₹2 lakh, ₹3 lakh, and ₹1 lakh respectively. Find the capital‑weighted annualised return.
Step 1: Convert returns to growth factors: 1.15, 0.95, 1.20. Step 2: Weights are the capital amounts (money‑weighted). Compute weighted sum of logs: 2·ln1.15 + 3·ln0.95 + 1·ln1.20 = 2·0.13976 + 3·(–0.05129) + 1·0.18232 = 0.2795 – 0.1539 + 0.1823 = 0.3079. Total weight = 2+3+1 = 6. Average log = 0.3079/6 = 0.05132. Step 3: Exponentiate: e⁰·⁰⁵¹³² = 1.05266. Annualised return = 5.27 % (approx).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives periodic returns with different capital bases or durations, immediately write the growth factors and the corresponding weights. Use the log‑sum method: Σ wᵢ ln(1+rᵢ) / Σ wᵢ, then exponentiate. This avoids errors from multiplying many decimals and works even with fractional weights. Keep a small table: Period | Return | Factor | Weight | ln(Factor) | Weight×ln. Sum the last column, divide by total weight, then use the exponential function (or approximate eˣ ≈ 1+x for x<0.1). For true time‑weighted returns, use equal period weights and chain the factors.

Compound Annual Growth Rate versus Arithmetic Mean of Periodic Returns

The compound annual growth rate (CAGR) is the constant rate that would take the initial value to the final value over the given number of periods. If an investment grows from V₀ to Vₙ in n years, CAGR = (Vₙ/V₀)¹/ⁿ – 1. This is exactly the geometric mean of the year‑by‑year growth factors minus one. The arithmetic mean of the periodic returns, (Σ rᵢ)/n, is larger than or equal to the CAGR whenever returns vary, because of Jensen’s inequality applied to the convex exponential function. The difference is the volatility drag: for a series +50 %, –50 %, the arithmetic mean is 0 % but the CAGR is (1.5×0.5)¹/² – 1 = √0.75 – 1 ≈ –13.4 %. Exam questions often present a table of yearly returns and ask which average correctly represents the investor’s experience; the answer is always CAGR (or the geometric mean) when the question concerns the growth of a single lump‑sum investment.

A subtle variant involves periodic contributions. If an investor adds ₹10,000 each year, the arithmetic mean of the fund’s annual returns does not equal the investor’s personal return. The investor’s return is the internal rate of return (IRR) of the cash‑flow stream, which weights each year’s return by the amount of capital exposed during that year. However, if the question explicitly asks for the “average annual return of the fund” (time‑weighted), the geometric mean of the fund’s annual factors is correct. The arithmetic mean is only appropriate for averaging independent rates that apply to the same base for the same duration, such as the average interest rate offered by different banks on a fixed deposit of equal tenure.

Another trap: using the arithmetic mean of percentages when the bases differ. Example: a company’s profit margin is 10 % on ₹100 cr revenue in year 1 and 20 % on ₹200 cr revenue in year 2. The overall margin is not (10+20)/2 = 15 % but (10+40)/(100+200) = 50/300 = 16.67 %. This is a weighted arithmetic mean with weights equal to revenues. The same principle applies to average speed (weights = times) and average growth rate (weights = log‑weights). Always identify the denominator that stays constant across periods; that denominator becomes the weight for the arithmetic mean, while the geometric mean uses the product of factors raised to the time weights.

Mass‑Balance Replacement with First‑Order Reaction Kinetics

Standard replacement problems assume the solute is inert. When the solute undergoes a first‑order decay (e.g., radioactive decay, chemical degradation, bacterial die‑off) with rate constant k (units time⁻¹), the mass balance for a well‑mixed tank of constant volume V becomes dS/dt = –kS + Q_in C_in – Q_out (S/V). If inflow and outflow rates are equal (Q), the volume stays constant and the equation simplifies to dS/dt = –(k + Q/V)S + Q C_in. The solution is S(t) = S_steady + (S₀ – S_steady) e-(k+Q/V)t, where S_steady = (Q C_in) / (k + Q/V). The concentration C(t) = S(t)/V follows the same exponential approach. The effective removal rate is the sum of the hydraulic flushing rate Q/V and the reaction rate k. This additive property is a key exam shortcut: the half‑life of the solute in the tank is ln2 / (k + Q/V).

If the volume is not constant (e.g., evaporation at rate E, or inflow ≠ outflow), the equation becomes dS/dt = –kS + Q_in C_in – Q_out (S/V) and dV/dt = Q_in – Q_out – E. The system is coupled and generally requires numerical stepping unless Q_in = Q_out + E (steady volume) or k = 0 (inert case). In discrete‑time exam versions, the recurrence for each step Δt is: Sn+1 = S_n – k S_n Δt – (Q_out Δt) (S_n/V_n) + Q_in Δt C_in; Vn+1 = V_n + (Q_in – Q_out – E) Δt. The exam may ask for the concentration after a given number of steps; iterate the recurrence carefully, keeping at least four significant figures to avoid rounding drift.

A classic problem: a 1000 L tank initially contains 10 kg of a pollutant that degrades with k = 0.02 h⁻¹. Fresh water enters at 50 L/h and the mixture leaves at 50 L/h. Find the pollutant mass after 10 h. Here Q/V = 50/1000 = 0.05 h⁻¹, so total removal rate = 0.07 h⁻¹. Steady state mass = 0 (since C_in = 0). Mass after 10 h = 10 × e-0.07×10 = 10 × e-0.7 ≈ 10 × 0.4966 = 4.97 kg. If the inflow had concentration 0.01 kg/L, steady mass = (50×0.01)/0.07 = 7.14 kg, and the mass would approach that value from above or below depending on initial mass.

Cascade Replacement in Series Tanks

When a fluid passes through several well‑mixed tanks in series, each tank acts as a replacement stage. Let the tanks have volumes V₁, V₂, …, V_m and a common flow rate Q (constant volume in each). The inlet to tank 1 has concentration C_in(t); the outlet of tank i is the inlet to tank i+1. For constant C_in, the system reaches a steady state where every tank has concentration C_in. The transient behaviour is governed by a linear chain of ODEs: dC₁/dt = (Q/V₁)(C_in – C₁); dC_i/dt = (Q/V_i)(Ci-1 – C_i) for i≥2. The solution for a step change in C_in (from 0 to C₀ at t=0) is a sum of exponentials. The concentration in tank i is C_i(t) = C₀ [1 – Σj=1i Aij e-(Q/V_j)t], where coefficients Aij depend on the volume ratios. For equal volumes V, the residence time in each tank is τ = V/Q, and the outlet concentration of the m‑th tank follows the Erlang distribution: C_m(t) = C₀ [1 – e-t/τ Σk=0m-1 (t/τ)^k / k!]. This formula is a standard result for series CSTRs (continuous stirred‑tank reactors) and appears in chemical‑engineering‑type quantitative aptitude questions.

In discrete‑time replacement problems, the same logic applies stepwise. Suppose each minute a volume r is transferred from tank 1 to tank 2, from tank 2 to tank 3, …, and from tank m out of the system, while fresh solvent enters tank 1. If all tanks have equal volume V and r = V/m (so each tank’s residence time is m minutes), the fraction of original solute remaining in tank i after n steps can be computed by matrix multiplication or by iterating the recurrence: S₁(n+1) = S₁(n) (1 – r/V); S_i(n+1) = S_i(n) (1 – r/V) + (r/V) Si-1(n) for i≥2. The total solute in the system decays as (1 – r/V)^n times a polynomial in n of degree m‑1. Exam questions often ask for the number of steps to reduce the overall concentration below a threshold; the Erlang‑type closed form or the recurrence can be used. For small m (≤3) and moderate n, manual iteration is feasible and less error‑prone.

A practical variant: the tanks have different volumes. Then the hydraulic retention times τ_i = V_i/Q differ. The step response becomes a sum of exponentials with distinct rates 1/τ_i. The coefficients are obtained by partial‑fraction expansion of the transfer function ∏i=1^m 1/(1 + τ_i s). In an exam, you may be given the volumes and asked for the concentration in the last tank after a specific time. The shortcut is to compute the dimensionless time θ = t/τ_avg and use the equal‑volume Erlang formula as an approximation if the volume spread is small; otherwise, set up the linear recurrence and iterate. Always verify mass balance: total solute in all tanks plus solute that has left the system must equal initial solute plus solute entered.

  • Confusing arithmetic mean of growth rates with CAGR when returns are volatile — the arithmetic mean overstates the true compound growth.
  • Applying the geometric mean to rates that are not multiplicative (e.g., averaging simple interest rates) — geometric mean only compounds.
  • Forgetting to add the reaction rate constant k to the hydraulic flushing rate Q/V in mass‑balance replacement — the effective decay rate is the sum.
  • Treating cascade tanks as independent single‑tank replacements — the outlet of one tank is the inlet to the next, creating coupled dynamics.
  • Using the equal‑volume Erlang formula when tank volumes differ significantly — the distinct time constants change the transient shape.
  • Neglecting to convert percentage returns to decimal growth factors before taking logarithms — ln(1+r) requires r as a decimal, not a percent.
  • Assuming the weighted arithmetic mean of concentrations works for series tanks — the correct aggregate is governed by the convolution of residence‑time distributions.
