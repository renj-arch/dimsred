1. Simple Interest Fundamentals

1.1 Definition of Simple Interest

Simple interest is the interest calculated only on the original principal for the entire duration of the loan or investment, which means that the interest earned in each period remains constant because the principal does not change. In banking practice, a lender agrees to pay a fixed percentage of the principal per year, and that percentage is applied to the same principal amount irrespective of any interest that has already accrued. The simplicity of this method makes it easy to understand and to compute, especially for short‑term loans such as treasury bills or certain personal loans where the interest is not reinvested. Because the interest does not compound, the total interest after n years is directly proportional to the number of years, and the relationship can be expressed as a linear function of time. This linear growth contrasts sharply with the exponential growth seen in compound interest, and it is the reason why simple interest is rarely used for long‑term investments where the power of compounding becomes significant.

1.2 Derivation of the Simple Interest Formula

Starting from the definition, if P denotes the principal, r the annual rate of interest expressed as a percentage, and t the time in years, then the interest for one year equals P × r ÷ 100. Since the same interest accrues each year, the total simple interest I after t years is the product of the yearly interest and the number of years, giving I = P × r × t ÷ 100. The amount A, which is the sum of principal and interest, therefore becomes A = P + I = P (1 + r t ÷ 100). This derivation shows that the formula is a direct consequence of the assumption that interest is not added to the principal, and it also explains why the formula is valid for fractional years when the interest is calculated proportionally. In examinations, students are often asked to rearrange this formula to find any one of the four variables when the other three are known, so mastery of algebraic manipulation of A = P (1 + r t ÷ 100) is essential.

1.3 Applications of Simple Interest in Banking

Banks and financial institutions use simple interest for products such as short‑term fixed deposits, certain government securities, and interest calculations on overdue amounts where the contract specifies a flat rate. In these cases the interest payable is known in advance and does not depend on the reinvestment of earlier interest, which simplifies accounting and disclosure. For example, a treasury bill of ₹1,00,000 issued at a discount rate of 6 % per annum for 91 days will have its discount calculated using the simple interest formula with t = 91 ÷ 365. The predictability of the cash flow makes simple interest attractive for regulatory reporting and for borrowers who prefer a fixed cost of borrowing. However, because the interest does not grow on itself, the effective yield to the investor is lower than that of a comparable compound‑interest instrument, a fact that examiners often test by asking candidates to compare the effective annual yield of a simple‑interest product with that of a compound‑interest product of the same nominal rate.

2. Compound Interest Basics

1.4 Definition of Compound Interest

Compound interest is the interest calculated on the initial principal and also on the interest that has been added to the principal at the end of each compounding period, which means that the principal effectively grows after every period. In practice, a bank may credit interest to a savings account quarterly, and the next quarter’s interest is computed on the new balance that includes the previously credited interest. This process of earning interest on interest leads to exponential growth of the invested amount, and the frequency of compounding — whether annually, half‑yearly, quarterly, or monthly — determines how quickly the balance increases. The concept is fundamental to the time value of money, because a rupee received today can be invested to earn compound interest and therefore is worth more than a rupee received in the future. Understanding this definition is crucial for solving problems that ask for the amount after a given number of periods, the effective annual rate, or the time required for an investment to double.

1.5 Derivation of the Compound Interest Formula for Annual Compounding

Let P be the principal, r the annual nominal rate expressed as a percentage, and n the number of years. After the first year the amount becomes P (1 + r ÷ 100). This amount becomes the principal for the second year, so after the second year the amount is P (1 + r ÷ 100)². Continuing this reasoning, after n years the amount A equals P (1 + r ÷ 100)ⁿ. The compound interest earned is then CI = A − P = P [(1 + r ÷ 100)ⁿ − 1]. The derivation shows that the exponent n appears because the multiplication by the factor (1 + r ÷ 100) is repeated n times, which is the mathematical expression of “interest on interest”. In examinations, candidates are frequently required to compute (1 + r ÷ 100)ⁿ for non‑integer n when the compounding period is a fraction of a year, which is handled by using the appropriate fractional exponent or by converting the rate and period to match the compounding frequency.

1.6 Effect of Compounding Frequency on Accumulated Amount

When the nominal annual rate r is kept constant, increasing the number of compounding periods per year raises the effective annual yield because interest is credited more often and each crediting adds to the principal for the next period. For instance, a nominal rate of 12 % per annum compounded half‑yearly means the rate per half‑year is 6 % and there are two periods in a year; the amount after one year becomes P (1 + 6 ÷ 100)² = P (1.06)², which is larger than P (1 + 12 ÷ 100) = P (1.12). The general formula for a nominal rate r compounded m times per year over t years is A = P (1 + r ÷ (100 m))m t. This formula makes it clear that as m grows, the factor (1 + r ÷ (100 m))m approaches er ÷ 100, the continuous‑compounding limit. Examiners often test the ability to switch between annual, half‑yearly, quarterly, and monthly compounding by adjusting both the rate per period and the total number of periods, and they may also ask for the effective annual rate defined as (1 + r ÷ (100 m))m − 1 expressed as a percentage.

Worked Example: Difference Between Simple and Compound Interest for Two Years
A sum of ₹10,000 is invested at 8 % per annum simple interest for two years. The same sum is invested at 8 % per annum compound interest compounded annually for two years. Find the difference between the compound interest and the simple interest.
Simple interest for two years: Iₛ = P × r × t ÷ 100 = 10,000 × 8 × 2 ÷ 100 = ₹1,600. Amount under simple interest = ₹11,600. Compound interest for two years: A = P (1 + r ÷ 100)² = 10,000 (1 + 8 ÷ 100)² = 10,000 (1.08)² = 10,000 × 1.1664 = ₹11,664. Compound interest = ₹11,664 − ₹10,000 = ₹1,664. Difference = ₹1,664 − ₹1,600 = ₹64. The difference can also be obtained directly from the two‑year difference formula D = P (r ÷ 100)² = 10,000 × (8 ÷ 100)² = 10,000 × 0.0064 = ₹64.
Exam‑Day Insight
When the rate is given per annum but compounding is half‑yearly, always halve the rate and double the number of periods before applying the compound interest formula; this single step eliminates the most common calculation error.

3. Half‑Yearly Compounding

1.7 Adjustment of Rate and Period for Half‑Yearly Compounding

Half‑yearly compounding means that interest is credited twice a year, so the annual nominal rate r must be divided by two to obtain the rate per half‑year, and the total number of years t must be multiplied by two to obtain the number of half‑year periods. If the nominal rate is 10 % per annum, the half‑yearly rate becomes 5 % per half‑year. For an investment horizon of three years, the number of compounding periods is 3 × 2 = 6 half‑years. This adjustment is a direct consequence of the definition of compounding frequency and ensures that the product of the per‑period rate and the number of periods equals the nominal annual rate multiplied by the number of years. Students must remember that the formula A = P (1 + r ÷ (100 m))m t with m = 2 captures exactly this adjustment, and any mistake in halving the rate or doubling the periods will lead to an incorrect amount.

1.8 Formula for Amount with Half‑Yearly Compounding

Substituting m = 2 into the general compounding formula gives A = P (1 + r ÷ 200)2 t. Here r is the annual nominal rate in percent, t is the time in years, and the exponent 2 t is the total number of half‑year periods. The compound interest earned is CI = A − P. For example, with P = ₹5,000, r = 12 % per annum, and t = 2 years, the half‑yearly rate is 6 % and the number of periods is 4, so A = 5,000 (1 + 6 ÷ 100)⁴ = 5,000 (1.06)⁴ = 5,000 × 1.26247696 ≈ ₹6,312.38. The compound interest is therefore ₹1,312.38. This formula is frequently tested by asking candidates to compute the amount for a given principal, rate, and time when the compounding is half‑yearly, and also to find the nominal rate when the amount, principal, and time are known.

1.9 Comparison of Annual and Half‑Yearly Compounding Outcomes

Because half‑yearly compounding credits interest twice as often, the effective annual yield is higher than the nominal rate, whereas annual compounding yields exactly the nominal rate. For a nominal rate of 10 % per annum, the effective annual rate under half‑yearly compounding is (1 + 10 ÷ 200)² − 1 = (1.05)² − 1 = 0.1025 or 10.25 %. Over two years, the amount under annual compounding is P (1.10)² = P × 1.21, while under half‑yearly compounding it is P (1.05)⁴ = P × 1.21550625. The difference, though seemingly small for a single period, becomes substantial for large principals or long horizons. Examiners often present a table of amounts for the same principal at the same nominal rate but different compounding frequencies and ask the candidate to identify the correct amount or to compute the effective rate. Understanding that the effective rate increases with compounding frequency is essential for answering such questions correctly.

4. Difference Between Simple and Compound Interest Over Two Years

1.10 Derivation of the Two‑Year Difference Formula

The difference between compound interest and simple interest for a period of two years can be derived by writing the expressions for the two amounts and subtracting. Simple interest amount after two years is Aₛ = P (1 + 2r ÷ 100). Compound interest amount with annual compounding after two years is A_c = P (1 + r ÷ 100)² = P (1 + 2r ÷ 100 + r² ÷ 10000). Subtracting Aₛ from A_c gives D = A_c − Aₛ = P × r² ÷ 10000. This elegant result shows that the difference depends only on the principal and the square of the rate, and it is independent of the time beyond the two‑year horizon. The derivation also explains why the difference is zero when the rate is zero and why it grows quadratically with the rate. In examinations, this formula D = P (r ÷ 100)² is a standard shortcut that saves time compared with computing both interests separately, and candidates are expected to recognise when it can be applied.

1.11 Numerical Illustration of the Difference

Consider a principal of ₹20,000 invested at 9 % per annum for two years. Using the shortcut, the difference D = 20,000 × (9 ÷ 100)² = 20,000 × 0.0081 = ₹162. Computing the two interests separately confirms this: simple interest = 20,000 × 9 × 2 ÷ 100 = ₹3,600; compound interest = 20,000 (1.09)² − 20,000 = 20,000 × 1.1881 − 20,000 = ₹3,762; difference = ₹3,762 − ₹3,600 = ₹162. The same result is obtained if the compounding is half‑yearly, but then the difference formula changes because the effective rate per half‑year is different; the two‑year difference for half‑yearly compounding is D = P [(1 + r ÷ 200)⁴ − (1 + 2r ÷ 100)], which is larger than the annual‑compounding difference. Examiners may ask for the difference under half‑yearly compounding to test whether the candidate can adapt the formula or compute directly.

1.12 Interpretation of the Difference in Financial Planning

The two‑year difference D = P (r ÷ 100)² quantifies the extra earnings that arise solely from the reinvestment of the first year’s interest. In financial planning, this difference represents the benefit of choosing a compound‑interest product over a simple‑interest product for a two‑year horizon. For small rates the difference is modest, but for higher rates or larger principals it can be significant. For instance, at 15 % per annum on ₹1,00,000 the difference is ₹1,00,000 × (0.15)² = ₹2,250, which may influence the choice between a fixed deposit that pays simple interest and one that compounds annually. Understanding this interpretation helps candidates answer qualitative questions that ask which product yields more interest and by how much, without requiring full numerical computation.

5. Practical Problem Solving and Advanced Scenarios

1.13 Solving Mixed Interest Problems

Many examination problems combine simple and compound interest in a single scenario, such as a loan where the first year is charged at simple interest and subsequent years at compound interest, or an investment where part of the principal earns simple interest while the rest earns compound interest. The key to solving these problems is to break the time line into segments, apply the appropriate formula to each segment, and carry forward the amount as the new principal for the next segment. For example, if ₹50,000 is invested at 10 % simple interest for the first year and then at 10 % compound interest compounded annually for the next two years, the amount after the first year is ₹55,000; this becomes the principal for the compound phase, giving a final amount of ₹55,000 (1.10)² = ₹66,550. Systematic segmentation avoids confusion and ensures that each interest type is applied correctly.

1.14 Using the Difference Formula to Find Rate or Principal

The two‑year difference formula D = P (r ÷ 100)² can be rearranged to solve for the rate when the difference and principal are known, giving r = 100 √(D ÷ P). Similarly, if the difference and rate are known, the principal is P = D × 10000 ÷ r². These rearrangements are frequently tested in data‑sufficiency and quantitative‑comparison questions. For instance, if the difference between compound and simple interest on a certain sum for two years at 12 % per annum is ₹1,440, the principal is P = 1,440 × 10000 ÷ 12² = 1,440 × 10000 ÷ 144 = ₹1,00,000. Mastery of these algebraic manipulations allows candidates to answer inverse problems quickly and accurately.

1.15 Summary of Key Relationships and Checks

To consolidate the topic, remember that simple interest grows linearly as I = P r t ÷ 100, compound interest grows exponentially as A = P (1 + r ÷ 100)ⁿ for annual compounding, and the half‑yearly version replaces r by r ÷ 2 and n by 2t. The two‑year difference between compound and simple interest under annual compounding is always P (r ÷ 100)², a result that follows directly from the binomial expansion of (1 + r ÷ 100)². When checking answers, verify that the compound amount is never less than the simple amount for the same principal, rate, and time, and that the effective annual rate under half‑yearly compounding exceeds the nominal rate. These consistency checks help catch arithmetic errors before finalising a solution. Practising a variety of problems that require switching between formulas, adjusting for compounding frequency, and applying the difference shortcut will build the speed and accuracy needed for the examination.

Quarterly and Monthly Compounding Adjustments

When interest is compounded more frequently than half‑yearly, divide the nominal annual rate by the number of compounding periods per year; the total periods equal years multiplied by periods per year. For quarterly compounding the period rate is r ÷ 4 percent per quarter and the number of periods is 4t. The amount formula becomes A = P (1 + r ÷ 400)4t. For monthly compounding the period rate is r ÷ 12 percent per month and the number of periods is 12t, giving A = P (1 + r ÷ 1200)12t. These adjustments follow from the definition of compound interest: each sub‑period earns interest on the accumulated amount of the previous sub‑period. The effective annual rate (EAR) for a nominal rate r compounded m times a year is EAR = (1 + r ÷ (100m))m − 1, expressed as a decimal. Candidates should compute the EAR for quarterly (m = 4) and monthly (m = 12) compounding and compare it with the nominal rate to judge the true cost or yield of a financial product.

A common examination twist gives a nominal rate quoted for annual compounding but asks for the amount when the same nominal rate is applied with quarterly or monthly compounding. The principal remains unchanged; only the exponent and the divisor of the rate change. For example, a principal of ₹1,00,000 at 12 % per annum compounded quarterly for 3 years yields A = 1,00,000 (1 + 12 ÷ 400)12 = 1,00,000 (1.03)12. The same principal at 12 % compounded monthly for 3 years gives A = 1,00,000 (1 + 12 ÷ 1200)36 = 1,00,000 (1.01)36. The monthly amount is larger because interest is credited more often. When the problem states “compounded quarterly” but provides a rate per quarter (e.g., 3 % per quarter), the nominal annual rate is 12 % and the formula uses the quarterly rate directly: A = P (1 + 3 ÷ 100)4t. Recognising whether the given rate is nominal annual or per‑period prevents a frequent error.

Another layer appears when the compounding frequency changes during the investment horizon. For instance, an amount may be compounded quarterly for the first two years and monthly thereafter. The solution requires computing the amount after the first phase, then using that amount as the principal for the second phase with the new period rate and period count. This piecewise approach mirrors the mixed‑interest technique but with different compounding frequencies. Mastery of the quarterly and monthly formulas, together with the ability to switch frequencies mid‑stream, equips candidates to handle any compounding‑frequency variation the paper may present.

Continuous Compounding and the Limit Formula

Continuous compounding represents the theoretical limit of increasing the compounding frequency without bound. Mathematically, the amount after time t years at a nominal annual rate r (expressed as a percent) is A = P ert ÷ 100, where e ≈ 2.718281828 is the base of natural logarithms. This formula is derived by taking the limit of (1 + r ÷ (100m))mt as m → ∞. In examinations, continuous compounding usually appears in two forms: (i) direct computation of the amount or interest using the exponential function, and (ii) comparison of the effective annual rate under continuous compounding with that under discrete compounding. The effective annual rate for continuous compounding is EAR = er ÷ 100 − 1. For a nominal rate of 10 %, the continuous EAR is e0.10 − 1 ≈ 0.10517 or 10.517 %, which is higher than the EAR for monthly compounding (≈10.471 %) and quarterly compounding (≈10.381 %).

Problems may ask for the principal that will grow to a given amount under continuous compounding, requiring the rearrangement P = A e−rt ÷ 100. Others may provide the continuous compounding amount and ask for the equivalent nominal rate compounded annually, which involves solving (1 + r ÷ 100) = erc ÷ 100 for r. Because the exponential function is monotonic, a unique solution exists. Candidates should be comfortable using the approximation ex ≈ 1 + x + x²/2 for small x when a calculator is not permitted, but they must also know the exact exponential values for standard rates (e.g., e0.05 ≈ 1.05127, e0.10 ≈ 1.10517, e0.15 ≈ 1.16183).

A frequent examiner trap is to phrase a question as “interest is compounded continuously at 8 % per annum” and then ask for the interest earned in the first year. The correct approach is to compute A = P e0.08 and subtract P. Some candidates mistakenly apply the simple‑interest formula P × 8 ÷ 100 or the annual compounding formula P (1.08) − P. The continuous compounding interest for the first year is P (e0.08 − 1), which is slightly larger than the annual compounding interest. Recognising the wording “continuously” and applying the exponential formula without reverting to discrete formulas is essential for full marks.

Worked Example: Monthly vs Continuous Compounding
A sum of ₹50,000 is invested for 2 years. Option A offers 9 % per annum compounded monthly. Option B offers 9 % per annum compounded continuously. Find the difference in the final amounts (Option B − Option A).

Option A (monthly): period rate = 9 ÷ 1200 = 0.0075; periods = 24. AmountA = 50,000 × (1.0075)24. Using (1.0075)24 ≈ 1.19668, AmountA ≈ 50,000 × 1.19668 = ₹59,834.

Option B (continuous): AmountB = 50,000 × e0.09 × 2 = 50,000 × e0.18. e0.18 ≈ 1.19722. AmountB ≈ 50,000 × 1.19722 = ₹59,861.

Difference = 59,861 − 59,834 = ₹27 (approximately). The continuous compounding yields a marginally higher amount because interest is credited at every instant.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem mentions “compounded continuously”, immediately write the formula A = P e^(rt/100). Do not attempt to convert to a discrete frequency. Keep a mental table of e^x for x = 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20 to avoid calculator dependence. For monthly or quarterly compounding, note the divisor (1200 or 400) and the exponent (12t or 4t) before substituting numbers.

Variable Rate Scenarios and Piecewise Interest

Real‑world loans and investments often involve a change in the interest rate after a certain period. The mathematical treatment is to split the time line at each rate change, compute the amount up to that point using the applicable rate and compounding method, and then continue with the new rate on the accumulated amount. For example, a principal of ₹2,00,000 is invested at 8 % per annum compounded annually for the first 3 years, after which the rate rises to 10 % per annum compounded annually for the next 2 years. The amount after 3 years is A₁ = 2,00,000 (1.08)³. This amount becomes the principal for the next phase: A₂ = A₁ (1.10)². The final amount is 2,00,000 (1.08)³ (1.10)². The same principle applies when the compounding frequency changes together with the rate.

Examination questions may present the rate change in a disguised form, such as “the interest rate is increased by 2 % after the first year” or “the bank revises the rate from 7 % to 9 % at the end of the second year”. The candidate must translate the verbal description into precise numerical rates for each segment. A common error is to apply the new rate to the original principal instead of to the accumulated amount. Another error is to average the rates and apply a single compound formula, which is mathematically incorrect because compound interest is not linear in the rate.

When the rate varies every year (e.g., 5 % for year 1, 6 % for year 2, 7 % for year 3), the amount is the product of the yearly growth factors: A = P (1 + r₁/100)(1 + r₂/100)(1 + r₃/100). This multiplicative form is a direct extension of the constant‑rate formula and is often tested in data‑interpretation sets where a table of yearly rates is given. Candidates should practice constructing the product quickly, possibly using logarithms for approximation when the number of years is large.

Loan Amortisation with Simple and Compound Interest

Amortisation refers to the process of repaying a loan through regular instalments that cover both interest and principal. In competitive examinations, two simplified models appear: (i) a loan charged at simple interest on the original principal, with equal annual repayments; (ii) a loan charged at compound interest on the outstanding balance, with equal annual repayments (the standard equated‑monthly‑instalment concept). For the simple‑interest amortisation, the total interest over n years is I = P r n ÷ 100. The total repayment is P + I, and each annual instalment is (P + I) ÷ n. The principal repaid each year is the instalment minus the interest due for that year (which remains constant because simple interest is always on the original principal).

For compound‑interest amortisation, the instalment X satisfies the present‑value equation P = X ÷ (1 + r/100) + X ÷ (1 + r/100)² + … + X ÷ (1 + r/100)ⁿ. This geometric series sums to P = X [1 − (1 + r/100)−n] ÷ (r/100). Solving for X gives X = P (r/100) ÷ [1 − (1 + r/100)−n]. Examination questions often provide P, r, n and ask for X, or provide X and ask for P or r. Candidates must be able to manipulate the formula algebraically and also to compute the outstanding balance after k instalments, which is Bk = X [1 − (1 + r/100)−(n−k)] ÷ (r/100).

A typical twist is to combine the two models: a loan where the first year uses simple interest and subsequent years use compound interest on the reduced balance. The solution proceeds by calculating the first‑year interest on the full principal, subtracting the first instalment, then treating the remaining balance as the principal for a standard compound‑interest amortisation over the remaining years. Understanding the cash‑flow timeline and applying the correct interest basis at each stage is the key to solving such hybrid problems without confusion.

Effective Annual Rate and Nominal Rate Conversions

The effective annual rate (EAR) is the single annual rate that would produce the same final amount as a given nominal rate compounded m times per year. The conversion formula is EAR = (1 + r ÷ (100m))m − 1. Conversely, given an EAR, the nominal rate compounded m times is r = 100m [(1 + EAR)1/m − 1]. These conversions are essential when comparing financial products quoted on different bases. For instance, a fixed deposit advertising “9 % per annum compounded quarterly” has an EAR of (1 + 9 ÷ 400)⁴ − 1 ≈ 0.0931 or 9.31 %. Another product offering “9.2 % per annum compounded annually” has an EAR of 9.2 %. The first product is superior despite the lower nominal rate.

Examiners may ask: “If the effective annual rate is 10.25 %, find the nominal rate compounded half‑yearly.” Using the inverse formula with m = 2: r = 200 [(1.1025)1/2 − 1] = 200 (1.05 − 1) = 10 %. Another variant: “A loan charges 1 % per month. What is the effective annual rate?” Here the nominal monthly rate is 1 % per month, which corresponds to a nominal annual rate of 12 % compounded monthly. The EAR is (1 + 0.01)¹² − 1 ≈ 0.1268 or 12.68 %. Candidates must distinguish between a rate quoted per period (1 % per month) and a nominal annual rate (12 % per annum compounded monthly); both lead to the same EAR but the phrasing determines which formula to start with.

A frequent trap is to confuse the nominal rate with the periodic rate. If a problem states “interest is charged at 3 % per quarter”, the nominal annual rate is 12 % and the EAR is computed with m = 4 and r = 12. If it states “the nominal rate is 12 % per annum compounded quarterly”, the same calculation applies. However, if it states “the effective quarterly rate is 3 %”, then the EAR is (1.03)⁴ − 1 and the nominal annual rate compounded quarterly is 4 × 3 % = 12 %. The distinction between “per quarter” (periodic) and “per annum compounded quarterly” (nominal) is subtle but decisive for the correct answer.

  • Applying the simple‑interest formula to a compound‑interest segment when the rate changes mid‑term.
  • Using the original principal instead of the accumulated amount as the base for the next compounding phase.
  • Treating a nominal rate quoted per period (e.g., 1 % per month) as an annual nominal rate without multiplying by the number of periods.
  • Forgetting to adjust the exponent when the compounding frequency changes (e.g., using 12t for quarterly compounding).
  • Assuming the effective annual rate equals the nominal rate when compounding is more frequent than annually.
  • Misapplying the continuous‑compounding formula by using the discrete rate directly without converting to the continuous equivalent.
  • Calculating the instalment for a compound‑interest loan using the simple‑interest total‑repayment method.

Mastery of these advanced topics enables a candidate to dissect any interest‑related problem, select the correct formula, and execute the computation with confidence.

Real Interest Rate and Inflation Adjustment

The nominal interest rate quoted by banks or borrowers does not reflect the true purchasing power gain or loss because it ignores inflation. The real interest rate approximates the increase in real wealth and is derived from the Fisher equation: (1 + nominal rate) = (1 + real rate) × (1 + inflation rate). For small rates the linear approximation real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate is often used, but the exact multiplicative form avoids distortion when rates are high. In examination problems the inflation rate may be given as an annual percentage, and candidates must decide whether to apply the exact formula or the approximation based on the wording. If a problem states “the real rate of return is 3 % per annum and inflation is 5 % per annum, find the nominal rate”, the exact formula gives (1 + nominal) = 1.03 × 1.05 = 1.0815, so nominal = 8.15 %. Using the approximation would give 8 %, a noticeable error. Conversely, when the nominal rate and inflation are known, the real rate is obtained by rearranging: real = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. This adjustment is crucial for comparing investment alternatives across different inflation environments, such as a fixed deposit at 7 % nominal in a 4 % inflation economy versus a bond at 6 % nominal in a 1 % inflation economy. The first yields a real rate of about 2.88 %, the second about 4.95 %, so the bond is superior in real terms despite a lower nominal rate. Candidates must also recognise that tax on nominal interest further reduces the post‑tax real return. If the marginal tax rate is t, the after‑tax nominal rate is nominal × (1 − t), and the after‑tax real rate becomes (1 + nominal × (1 − t)) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. This layered adjustment appears in advanced questions that combine taxation, inflation, and compounding frequency.

Key Relationships

  • Exact Fisher equation: (1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + π) where i = nominal, r = real, π = inflation.
  • Approximation: r ≈ i − π (valid for i, π < 10 %).
  • After‑tax real rate: r_after_tax = (1 + i(1 − t)) ÷ (1 + π) − 1.

When solving, always identify whether the given rates are effective annual rates or nominal rates with a compounding frequency. Convert all to effective annual rates before applying the Fisher equation. A common twist is to provide a nominal rate compounded quarterly and an annual inflation rate; the quarterly compounding must be converted to an effective annual nominal rate first.

Sinking Fund Method and Capital Recovery Factor

A sinking fund is a systematic accumulation of periodic deposits that, together with compound interest, will amount to a predetermined future sum. It is the mirror image of loan amortisation: instead of repaying a present principal, the investor builds a future capital. The capital recovery factor (CRF) converts a present value into an equivalent uniform series of payments over n periods at interest rate i per period. The CRF is i(1 + i)ⁿ ÷ [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1]. The sinking fund factor (SFF) is the reciprocal of the future value annuity factor: i ÷ [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1]. If a company needs ₹10,00,000 in 5 years and can invest at 8 % per annum compounded annually, the annual deposit required is 10,00,000 × SFF = 10,00,000 × 0.08 ÷ [(1.08)⁵ − 1] ≈ ₹1,70,456. The same formula works for half‑yearly or quarterly deposits by adjusting i and n accordingly. In examinations, the sinking fund concept often appears disguised as “a machine costs ₹5,00,000 and will be replaced after 8 years; if the company sets aside a fund earning 10 % p.a., find the annual provision”. The answer is the annual sinking fund deposit. Another variant asks for the balance in the fund after k years, which is the future value of an annuity due or ordinary annuity depending on whether deposits are made at the beginning or end of each period. The future value of an ordinary annuity is PMT × [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1] ÷ i; for an annuity due multiply by (1 + i). Candidates must read the wording carefully: “deposited at the end of each year” implies ordinary annuity; “deposited at the beginning of each year” implies annuity due. The sinking fund factor is also used in bond valuation to determine the periodic contribution needed to redeem a bond at maturity.

Formulae Summary

  • CRF = i(1 + i)ⁿ ÷ [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1]
  • SFF = i ÷ [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1]
  • Future value of ordinary annuity = PMT × [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1] ÷ i
  • Future value of annuity due = PMT × [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1] ÷ i × (1 + i)

When the interest rate changes mid‑term, the sinking fund calculation splits into segments: compute the accumulated value up to the change date, then treat that as the new principal for the remaining periods at the new rate. This piecewise approach mirrors the variable‑rate loan amortisation already covered.

Worked Example: Sinking Fund with Rate Change
A firm wishes to accumulate ₹20,00,000 in 6 years. For the first 3 years the fund earns 9 % p.a. compounded annually; for the next 3 years the rate drops to 7 % p.a. Find the equal annual deposit made at the end of each year.
Let the annual deposit be X. After 3 years at 9 %, the accumulated value is X × [(1.09)³ − 1] ÷ 0.09 = X × 3.2781. This amount then earns 7 % for 3 more years, becoming X × 3.2781 × (1.07)³ = X × 3.2781 × 1.225043 = X × 4.016. Meanwhile, deposits made during years 4‑6 (three deposits) accumulate at 7 %: their future value is X × [(1.07)³ − 1] ÷ 0.07 = X × 3.2149. Total future value = X × (4.016 + 3.2149) = X × 7.2309. Set equal to 20,00,000 → X = 20,00,000 ÷ 7.2309 ≈ ₹2,76,600. Therefore the required annual deposit is approximately ₹2,76,600.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a sinking fund problem involves a rate change, draw a timeline. Compute the future value of the first‑segment deposits up to the change date, then compound that lump sum at the new rate for the remaining periods. Add the future value of the second‑segment deposits (calculated directly at the new rate). Equate the sum to the target amount and solve for the deposit. This stepwise method avoids the common error of applying a single average rate.

Annuities Due Versus Ordinary Annuities in Present Value Calculations

The distinction between an annuity due (payments at the beginning of each period) and an ordinary annuity (payments at the end) affects both present and future values. The present value of an ordinary annuity of 1 per period for n periods at rate i is PV_ord = [1 − (1 + i)⁻ⁿ] ÷ i. For an annuity due, each payment is discounted one period less, so PV_due = PV_ord × (1 + i). Equivalently, PV_due = 1 + [1 − (1 + i)⁻⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾] ÷ i. In loan contexts, an ordinary annuity corresponds to arrears repayment (typical for mortgages), while an annuity due corresponds to advance repayment (common for lease rentals). Examination questions may ask: “A lease requires ₹50,000 at the beginning of each year for 5 years. If the interest rate is 10 % p.a., find the present value of the lease payments.” Using the annuity due formula: PV = 50,000 × [1 − (1.10)⁻⁵] ÷ 0.10 × 1.10 = 50,000 × 3.7908 × 1.10 ≈ ₹2,08,494. If the same payments were at year‑end, the present value would be 50,000 × 3.7908 ≈ ₹1,89,540. The difference of ₹18,954 is exactly one period’s interest on the first payment. Candidates must also handle deferred annuities where the first payment occurs after a gap of m periods. The present value of a deferred ordinary annuity is PV_ord × (1 + i)⁻ᵐ. For a deferred annuity due, multiply by (1 + i)⁻ᵐ because the first payment occurs at time m (beginning of period m+1). A typical trap is to forget the extra (1 + i) factor for annuity due when the problem states “payments are made in advance”. Always sketch a timeline marking payment points relative to the valuation date.

Quick Reference

  • PV ordinary = [1 − (1 + i)⁻ⁿ] ÷ i
  • PV due = PV ordinary × (1 + i)
  • FV ordinary = [(1 + i)ⁿ − 1] ÷ i
  • FV due = FV ordinary × (1 + i)
  • Deferred ordinary (defer m periods) = PV ordinary × (1 + i)⁻ᵐ
  • Deferred due (defer m periods) = PV due × (1 + i)⁻ᵐ

When the interest rate is nominal with a compounding frequency different from the payment frequency, convert the nominal rate to an effective rate per payment period before using the annuity formulae. For example, a nominal 12 % p.a. compounded monthly with annual payments requires an effective annual rate of (1 + 0.12/12)¹² − 1 ≈ 12.68 %. Use this effective rate in the annuity formulas.

Rule of 72 and Approximate Doubling Time

The Rule of 72 is a mental shortcut to estimate the number of years required for an investment to double at a given compound interest rate. The rule states: doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ rate (where rate is expressed as a percentage). For instance, at 8 % p.a. the doubling time ≈ 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years. The exact doubling time from the compound interest formula is ln(2) ÷ ln(1 + i). For i = 0.08, exact = 0.6931 ÷ 0.07696 ≈ 9.01 years, showing the rule’s accuracy for rates between 6 % and 10 %. For rates outside this range, the rule becomes less accurate; a refined version uses 69.3 for continuous compounding or 70 for low rates. In examinations, the Rule of 72 is often tested as a quick‑check: “At what approximate rate will money double in 6 years?” Answer: 72 ÷ 6 = 12 % p.a. Candidates may also be asked to compare the rule’s estimate with the exact calculation and comment on the error. The rule can be extended to tripling (Rule of 114) and quadrupling (Rule of 144) by using the natural logarithms: tripling time ≈ 114 ÷ rate, quadrupling ≈ 144 ÷ rate. These approximations are derived from ln(3) ≈ 1.0986 and ln(4) ≈ 1.3863, multiplied by 100. While not a substitute for exact computation, they are valuable for eliminating implausible options in multiple‑choice questions. A common examiner twist is to give a nominal rate compounded quarterly and ask for the approximate doubling time using the Rule of 72. The candidate must first convert the nominal rate to an effective annual rate, then apply the rule. For example, 12 % p.a. compounded quarterly gives an effective rate of (1 + 0.03)⁴ − 1 ≈ 12.55 %. The Rule of 72 gives 72 ÷ 12.55 ≈ 5.74 years, while the exact doubling time using the quarterly compounding formula is ln(2) ÷ [4 × ln(1.03)] ≈ 5.86 years. The rule remains a reasonable approximation for MCQ elimination even with intra‑year compounding, provided the effective annual rate is used.

When to Use and When to Avoid

  • Use for quick mental estimates in MCQs where options are widely spaced.
  • Avoid when the problem demands exact answer or when rate > 15 % or < 4 %.
  • Remember the rule applies to compound interest only; simple interest doubling time is exactly 100 ÷ rate.

Interest Calculation with Irregular Compounding Intervals

Standard formulas assume a constant compounding frequency (annual, half‑yearly, quarterly, monthly). Real‑world scenarios and some examination problems introduce irregular intervals: for example, interest compounded annually for the first 2 years, then quarterly for the next 18 months, then monthly for the final 6 months. The solution requires breaking the investment horizon into segments, each with its own compounding frequency and possibly its own nominal rate. For each segment, compute the accumulation factor using the appropriate formula: (1 + r₁/100)ⁿ¹ for annual, (1 + r₂/(400))⁴ᵗ² for quarterly, (1 + r₃/(1200))¹²ᵗ³ for monthly, where t is in years. Multiply the successive factors to obtain the overall growth factor. If the principal is P, the final amount A = P × Π (segment factor). Conversely, if the final amount and the piecewise rates are known, the principal can be found by dividing by the product of factors. A typical question: “₹1,00,000 is invested at 10 % p.a. compounded annually for 2 years, then at 12 % p.a. compounded quarterly for 1 year, then at 9 % p.a. compounded monthly for 6 months. Find the amount.” Segment 1: factor = (1.10)² = 1.21. Segment 2: quarterly rate = 12/4 = 3 % per quarter, periods = 4, factor = (1.03)⁴ ≈ 1.1255. Segment 3: monthly rate = 9/12 = 0.75 % per month, periods = 6, factor = (1.0075)⁶ ≈ 1.0459. Overall factor = 1.21 × 1.1255 × 1.0459 ≈ 1.424. Amount = 1,00,000 × 1.424 = ₹1,42,400. Candidates must be careful with time units: quarterly periods = 4 × years, monthly periods = 12 × years. When a segment length is given in months, convert to years for the exponent (e.g., 18 months = 1.5 years → quarterly periods = 6). Another twist is a change in the nominal rate without a change in compounding frequency; the same segmentation applies. The key is to maintain a clear timeline and apply the correct factor for each sub‑period. Errors often arise from using the wrong exponent (e.g., using 12t for quarterly) or from mixing nominal and effective rates within a segment.

Segmented Accumulation Checklist

  • Identify each sub‑period: start date, end date, nominal rate, compounding frequency.
  • Convert each sub‑period length to the number of compounding periods.
  • Compute the accumulation factor for each sub‑period.
  • Multiply all factors to get the total growth factor.
  • Apply to principal or reverse‑engineer as required.
  • Using the simple‑interest formula for a compound‑interest segment when the rate changes mid‑term.
  • Applying the original principal instead of the accumulated amount as the base for the next compounding phase.
  • Treating a nominal rate quoted per period (e.g., 1 % per month) as an annual nominal rate without multiplying by the number of periods.
  • Forgetting to adjust the exponent when the compounding frequency changes (e.g., using 12t for quarterly compounding).
  • Assuming the effective annual rate equals the nominal rate when compounding is more frequent than annually.
  • Misapplying the continuous‑compounding formula by using the discrete rate directly without converting to the continuous equivalent.
  • Review these traps before the exam to avoid common errors.

Force of Interest and Continuous Compounding Mechanics

The force of interest, denoted by δ (delta), is the instantaneous growth rate of a unit of currency under continuous compounding. It is defined as the limit of the nominal rate divided by the compounding frequency as the frequency tends to infinity: δ = limm→∞ m × ln(1 + r/m). For a constant annual nominal rate r (decimal), this simplifies to δ = ln(1 + r). Conversely, the effective annual rate i satisfies 1 + i = eδ. This equivalence lets any discrete compounding problem be recast in continuous terms, which is especially useful when the compounding frequency changes within a single investment horizon.

When the force of interest varies with time, δ(t), the accumulation factor from time 0 to T is exp(∫0T δ(t) dt). A common examination pattern uses a piecewise constant force: for example, δ = 0.05 for the first two years and δ = 0.07 for the next three years. The total growth factor becomes e0.05×2 × e0.07×3 = e0.10+0.21 = e0.31. Candidates should recognise that the integral of a constant force over an interval is the product of the force and the interval length, turning the problem into a sum of exponents.

A frequent examiner twist gives a nominal rate compounded quarterly and asks for the equivalent constant force of interest. The steps are: convert the nominal quarterly rate to an effective annual rate i = (1 + r/4)4 − 1, then compute δ = ln(1 + i). For r = 12 % p.a. compounded quarterly, i = (1.03)4 − 1 ≈ 0.1255, and δ = ln(1.1255) ≈ 0.1182. The continuous‑compounding amount after t years is then A = P eδt. Using the continuous formula directly with the nominal rate (i.e., A = P ert) is a trap; the exponent must use the force, not the nominal rate.

Another advanced application is the valuation of a continuously paid annuity. If a payment stream of 1 per unit time is made continuously for n years under a constant force δ, the present value is (1 − e−δn)/δ. This formula appears in questions on the present value of a salary paid continuously or a pension drawdown. The derivation follows from integrating the discount factor e−δt from 0 to n. Mastery of this integral and its discrete analogue (the annuity‑due factor) distinguishes high‑scoring candidates.

Tax‑Adjusted Effective Yield on Fixed Deposits

In many jurisdictions, interest earned on fixed deposits is subject to income tax at the investor’s marginal rate. The post‑tax effective yield is therefore lower than the advertised nominal rate. If the nominal annual rate is r (decimal) and the tax rate is τ, the after‑tax rate for simple interest is r(1 − τ). For compound interest with annual compounding, the after‑tax accumulation factor after one year becomes (1 + r(1 − τ)). Over n years the factor is (1 + r(1 − τ))n. When compounding is more frequent, tax is usually deducted at each crediting period, so the periodic after‑tax rate is (r/m)(1 − τ) and the annual factor is (1 + (r/m)(1 − τ))m.

A typical examination question provides a nominal rate of 8 % p.a. compounded quarterly, a tax rate of 20 %, and asks for the effective annual yield after tax. The quarterly gross rate is 2 %; after tax it becomes 2 % × 0.8 = 1.6 % per quarter. The effective annual yield is (1.016)4 − 1 ≈ 0.0656 or 6.56 %. Candidates who simply multiply the nominal rate by (1 − τ) and quote 6.4 % lose marks because they ignore the compounding effect on the tax‑reduced periodic rate.

When tax is deducted at source only at maturity (as in some tax‑saver deposits), the entire interest is taxed once. The after‑tax amount is P[1 + rn(1 − τ)] for simple interest, or P[(1 + r)n(1 − τ) + τ] for compound interest where the principal is not taxed. The distinction between periodic tax deduction and terminal tax deduction is a favourite source of multiple‑choice distractors. Always read the wording: “interest credited quarterly and tax deducted at each credit” versus “interest paid at maturity and tax deducted at maturity”.

Inflation can be layered on top of tax to obtain a real after‑tax yield. If the inflation rate is π, the real yield ≈ (1 + after‑tax nominal yield)/(1 + π) − 1. For small rates the approximation after‑tax nominal yield − π works, but exact calculation uses the ratio. Examiners may ask: “If the nominal rate is 9 %, tax 30 %, inflation 5 %, find the real post‑tax yield.” The after‑tax nominal yield (annual compounding) is 9 % × 0.7 = 6.3 %; real yield = 1.063/1.05 − 1 ≈ 1.24 %. This three‑step chain (nominal → after‑tax → real) tests the ability to track compounding at each stage.

Worked Example: Post‑Tax Yield with Quarterly Compounding
A bank offers a fixed deposit at 10 % p.a. nominal compounded quarterly. The investor’s marginal tax rate is 25 % and tax is deducted at each quarterly crediting. Compute the effective annual yield after tax.
Quarterly gross rate = 10 %/4 = 2.5 % = 0.025. After‑tax quarterly rate = 0.025 × (1 − 0.25) = 0.01875. Effective annual factor = (1 + 0.01875)4 = 1.018754 ≈ 1.0770. Effective annual yield after tax = 1.0770 − 1 = 0.0770 = 7.70 %.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem mentions tax, first identify whether tax is applied per period or at maturity. Write the periodic after‑tax rate explicitly before raising to the power of the number of periods. Keep a small table: Period | Gross Rate | Tax Rate | Net Rate | Accumulation Factor. This prevents the common error of applying tax to the annual effective rate instead of the periodic rate.

Regular Investment Accumulation with Varying Contributions

Many savings plans involve periodic contributions that are not constant — for instance, an annual increase of 5 % in the deposit amount to match salary growth. The future value of such a growing annuity can be derived by treating each contribution as a separate lump sum compounded for the remaining periods. If the first contribution is C, the growth rate of contributions is g, the interest rate per period is i, and there are n periods, the future value is C × [(1 + i)n − (1 + g)n] / (i − g) provided i ≠ g. When i = g, the formula simplifies to n C (1 + i)n−1.

Examiners often embed this in a scenario: “An investor deposits ₹10,000 at the end of the first year and increases the deposit by 8 % each subsequent year. The account earns 10 % p.a. compounded annually. Find the amount after 10 years.” Here C = 10,000, g = 0.08, i = 0.10, n = 10. Since i ≠ g, FV = 10,000 × [(1.10)10 − (1.08)10] / (0.10 − 0.08). Compute (1.10)10 ≈ 2.5937, (1.08)10 ≈ 2.1589, difference ≈ 0.4348, divide by 0.02 = 21.74, multiply by 10,000 = ₹2,17,400. A common mistake is to use the ordinary annuity factor (1.1010 − 1)/0.10 and then multiply by an average contribution, which yields an incorrect answer.

When contributions are made at the beginning of each period (annuity‑due), each term gains one extra period of interest. The future value formula becomes C(1 + i) × [(1 + i)n − (1 + g)n] / (i − g). The present value of a growing annuity‑due is similarly C(1 + i) × [1 − ((1 + g)/(1 + i))n] / (i − g). Candidates should be comfortable switching between end‑of‑period and beginning‑of‑period conventions, as the question stem often signals the timing with phrases like “deposited at the start of each year”.

A further twist is a change in the interest rate mid‑term while contributions continue to grow. The solution requires splitting the timeline at the rate change, computing the accumulated value of contributions up to that point at the old rate, then rolling that lump sum forward at the new rate while continuing the growing contributions under the new rate. This segmented approach mirrors the irregular compounding technique but adds a growing payment stream. Practising a few such two‑segment problems builds the timeline discipline needed for the toughest quantitative questions.

Present Value of Growing Perpetuities and Annuities

A growing perpetuity pays a cash flow C at the end of the first period, then C(1 + g), C(1 + g)2, … forever, with a constant discount rate i per period (i > g). Its present value is PV = C / (i − g). This result follows from summing the infinite geometric series Σ C(1 + g)t−1 / (1 + i)t. The formula is a cornerstone of equity valuation (Gordon growth model) and appears in questions on pension liabilities or rental streams.

For a growing annuity lasting n periods, the present value is PV = C × [1 − ((1 + g)/(1 + i))n] / (i − g). When i = g, the expression becomes PV = n C / (1 + i). Examiners test the boundary condition i = g by giving a scenario where the discount rate equals the growth rate, e.g., “A lease pays ₹50,000 first year, increasing 6 % annually for 20 years. The discount rate is 6 %. Find the present value.” The correct answer is 20 × 50,000 / 1.06 ≈ ₹9,43,396, not the perpetuity formula which would be undefined.

A frequent trap is to confuse the timing of the first cash flow. If the first payment occurs immediately (annuity‑due), multiply the ordinary annuity formula by (1 + i). For a growing perpetuity‑due, PV = C(1 + i) / (i − g). Questions may phrase it as “rent payable at the beginning of each year, increasing 4 % per year, discount rate 9 %”. The candidate must recognise the due convention and apply the factor (1 + i).

Another advanced variant introduces a deferral period: the growing payments start after m periods of zero cash flow. The present value is the ordinary growing annuity/perpetuity value discounted back m periods: PV = [C / (i − g)] × (1 + i)−m for a perpetuity, or the annuity formula multiplied by (1 + i)−m. This combines the deferral concept with growth and is a favourite in papers that blend time‑value concepts.

Bond Valuation Using Spot Rate Curve

Modern fixed‑income analysis values each cash flow of a bond at the spot rate corresponding to its maturity, rather than using a single yield‑to‑maturity. The spot rate curve (zero‑coupon yield curve) gives the annualised rate zt for a zero‑coupon bond maturing at time t. The price of a coupon bond with annual coupons C and face value F maturing in n years is P = Σt=1n C / (1 + zt)t + F / (1 + zn)n. This method captures the term structure of interest rates and is essential when the curve is not flat.

Examination problems often provide a table of spot rates for 1‑, 2‑, 3‑year maturities and ask for the price of a 3‑year 8 % coupon bond with face value ₹1,000. For example, z1 = 5 %, z2 = 5.5 %, z3 = 6 %. The price = 80/1.05 + 80/(1.055)2 + 1080/(1.06)3. Compute each term: 80/1.05 ≈ 76.19; 80/1.1130 ≈ 71.88; 1080/1.1910 ≈ 906.80; sum ≈ ₹1,054.87. A candidate who uses a single YTM of 5.5 % would obtain a different price and lose marks.

The concept of forward rates follows from the spot curve: the 1‑year forward rate starting at year t, ft,t+1, satisfies (1 + zt+1)t+1 = (1 + zt)t (1 + ft,t+1). Forward rates are used to value floating‑rate notes and to construct synthetic zero‑coupon bonds. A typical question: “Given spot rates z1 = 4 %, z2 = 4.5 %, find the 1‑year forward rate one year from now.” Solve (1.045)2 = 1.04 (1 + f) → f = (1.0452/1.04) − 1 ≈ 5.00 %.”

Duration and convexity can also be computed from the spot‑rate‑discounted cash flows. Macaulay duration = Σ t × PV(CFt) / Price, where PV(CFt) uses the appropriate spot rate. Modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + yield) is an approximation; the exact interest‑rate sensitivity uses the spot curve. While full duration calculations are rare in the syllabus, understanding that each cash flow has its own discount rate is a conceptual checkpoint that examiners may test with a qualitative multiple‑choice item.

  • Applying the simple‑interest tax adjustment to a compound‑interest deposit when tax is deducted each period.
  • Using the nominal rate directly in the continuous‑compounding exponent instead of the force of interest.
  • Treating a growing annuity as an ordinary annuity with an average contribution, ignoring the growth‑rate‑versus‑interest‑rate denominator.
  • Forgetting to multiply by (1 + i) for annuity‑due present values when the first cash flow is immediate.
  • Valuing a bond with a single yield‑to‑maturity when the question supplies a spot‑rate curve.
  • Confusing the deferral period exponent sign, discounting forward instead of backward when a growing perpetuity starts after a gap.
  • Assuming the perpetuity formula C/(i−g) works when i = g, leading to division by zero.

Day Count Conventions and Their Impact on Accrued Interest

Interest accrual on fixed‑income instruments and short‑term loans depends on the day‑count convention adopted by the market or the contract. The three most common conventions are Actual/Actual, Actual/360, and 30/360. Each defines how the numerator (days elapsed) and denominator (days in the year) are computed, directly changing the accrued interest for a given principal, rate, and period.

Actual/Actual (ISMA)

Under Actual/Actual the numerator is the actual calendar days between start and end dates; the denominator is the actual days in the coupon period (365 or 366 for annual periods). This convention is commonly used for government bonds in India and for many Eurobonds. Because the denominator follows the calendar, the daily interest factor varies slightly between leap and non‑leap years, but total interest over a full year equals the stated annual rate.

Actual/360 (Money‑Market)

Money‑market instruments such as Treasury bills, commercial paper, and interbank deposits often use Actual/360. The numerator remains actual days elapsed, but the denominator is fixed at 360. This yields a slightly higher effective annual yield because the year is treated as shorter. For a 90‑day instrument at 6 % nominal, interest = Principal × 0.06 × 90 ÷ 360 = 1.5 % of principal, whereas Actual/365 would give about 1.48 %.

30/360 (Bond‑Market)

The 30/360 convention assumes each month has 30 days and the year 360 days. End‑of‑month adjustments apply: a start date of the 31st becomes the 30th; an end date of the 31st becomes the 30th when the start date is the 30th or 31st. This simplification makes coupon calculations uniform across months and is standard for corporate bonds and many agency securities. The interest for a period from 15 Jan to 15 Feb is 30 days ÷ 360 = 1/12 of the annual coupon, regardless of actual calendar length.

Effect on Accrued Interest and Settlement

When a bond trades between coupon dates, the buyer compensates the seller for accrued interest. The convention dictates the exact amount. A mismatch between the convention used and the one assumed leads to systematic errors. For example, a 10 % semi‑annual coupon bond (face value ₹1,000) traded 45 days after the last coupon under 30/360 accrues ₹12.50 (45/180 × 50), while Actual/Actual (assuming 45 actual days in a 182‑day half‑year) accrues ₹12.36. In examinations, the convention is either stated explicitly or implied by the instrument type; candidates must identify it before applying the formula.

Leap‑Year Adjustments

Under Actual/Actual, a leap year adds one day to the denominator for periods spanning 29 February. Under Actual/360 and 30/360 the denominator stays 360, so the daily rate is unchanged, but the numerator may increase by one if the period includes 29 February, slightly raising accrued interest. Awareness of this nuance prevents off‑by‑one errors in long‑dated instruments.

Compound Interest with Partial Withdrawals and Additional Deposits

Real‑world savings accounts and fixed‑deposit schemes often allow withdrawals or additional deposits before maturity. The standard compound‑interest formula assumes a single untouched principal. When cash flows occur at irregular times, the accumulation must be computed piecewise: each sub‑period uses the balance at its start as the new principal.

Piecewise Accumulation Method

Suppose a deposit of ₹1,00,000 earns 8 % per annum compounded quarterly. After 6 months (two quarters) the depositor withdraws ₹20,000. The balance after two quarters is ₹1,00,000 × (1 + 0.08/4)² = ₹1,04,040. After withdrawal the new principal is ₹84,040. For the remaining two quarters the amount becomes ₹84,040 × (1 + 0.08/4)² = ₹87,435.22 (rounded). The final amount is not simply the original principal compounded for four quarters minus the withdrawal compounded for two quarters; the withdrawal reduces the base on which subsequent interest is earned.

Additional Deposits

If the depositor adds ₹10,000 after the first quarter, the balance after quarter 1 is ₹1,00,000 × (1 + 0.02) = ₹1,02,000. Adding ₹10,000 gives ₹1,12,000. This new principal compounds for the remaining three quarters: ₹1,12,000 × (1.02)³ = ₹1,18,855.30 (rounded). The timing of the deposit matters: a deposit made earlier earns interest for more sub‑periods.

General Algorithm

  1. Sort all cash‑flow events (deposits positive, withdrawals negative) chronologically with the start and maturity dates.
  2. For each interval between consecutive events, compute the number of compounding periods (n) using the applicable frequency.
  3. Update the balance: Balance ← Balance × (1 + r/m)ⁿ + CashFlow, where r is the annual nominal rate, m the compounding frequency, and CashFlow the net deposit/withdrawal at the interval end (or beginning, per convention).
  4. Repeat until maturity.

This algorithm works for any combination of withdrawals, deposits, and rate changes if the rate is updated at each event. Examination questions often present a table of dates and amounts; the candidate must construct the timeline, apply the correct compounding frequency for each segment, and avoid the common mistake of applying a single compound‑interest formula to the net cash flow.

Interest on Overdraft and Revolving Credit

Overdraft facilities charge interest on the daily outstanding balance, usually with daily compounding (or simple interest on daily balances). The same piecewise logic applies: each day’s closing balance is the principal for that day. Because the balance can fluctuate many times a month, banks often use the average daily balance method, which is mathematically equivalent to summing daily interest. Candidates should recognise that the effective annual cost of an overdraft quoted as “12 % per annum compounded daily” exceeds 12 % simple due to daily compounding.

Accrued Interest Using 30/360 Convention
A corporate bond with face value ₹1,00,000 pays a 9 % annual coupon semi‑annually (4.5 % per half‑year). The last coupon date was 15 January. The bond is sold on 28 February. Using the 30/360 convention, calculate the accrued interest payable by the buyer.
Under 30/360 each month counts as 30 days. From 15 Jan to 28 Feb: January contributes 15 days (30‑15), February contributes 28 days (since 28 < 30). Total days = 15 + 28 = 43 days. The half‑year length is 180 days. Accrued interest = Face Value × Coupon Rate per Half‑Year × (Days Elapsed ÷ 180) = 1,00,000 × 0.045 × (43 ÷ 180) = 1,00,000 × 0.045 × 0.238888… = ₹1,075.00 (rounded to nearest rupee).
Exam‑Day Strategy for Day‑Count Questions
Identify the instrument first: government bonds → Actual/Actual; T‑bills/commercial paper → Actual/360; corporate bonds → 30/360. Write the convention name beside the data. Then compute days using the rule‑of‑thumb: 30/360 = (30 × months) + (day₂ − day₁) with end‑of‑month adjustments. For Actual/Actual, count calendar days and use the actual days in the coupon period as denominator. A quick mental check: if answer choices differ by more than 1 % of the coupon, you likely used the wrong convention.

Bond Price Sensitivity: Duration and Convexity

Duration measures the weighted average time to receive a bond’s cash flows and serves as a first‑order estimate of price change for a small yield shift. Convexity captures the curvature of the price‑yield relationship, improving the estimate for larger moves. Both are essential for managing interest‑rate risk in fixed‑income portfolios.

Macaulay Duration

For a bond paying cash flows Cₜ at times t = 1,…,n (in years) with yield y per period, Macaulay duration Dₘ = Σ (t × PVₜ) ÷ Price, where PVₜ = Cₜ ÷ (1+y)ᵗ. The weights are the present‑value proportions of each cash flow. A zero‑coupon bond’s Macaulay duration equals its maturity; for a coupon bond, duration is shorter because earlier coupons return principal sooner.

Modified Duration

Modified duration Dₘₒd = Dₘ ÷ (1 + y) (annual compounding) or Dₘ ÷ (1 + y/m) for m periods per year. It approximates the percentage price change for a 1 % (100 basis‑point) yield change: ΔP/P ≈ −Dₘₒd × Δy. For example, a bond with modified duration 5.2 will fall about 5.2 % if yields rise by 1 %.

Convexity

Convexity C = Σ [t(t+1) × PVₜ] ÷ [Price × (1+y)²] (annual compounding). The second‑order price approximation is ΔP/P ≈ −Dₘₒd × Δy + ½ × C × (Δy)². Convexity is always positive for option‑free bonds, meaning price increases more when yields fall than it decreases when yields rise by the same amount. A bond with higher convexity is more valuable in volatile rate environments.

Portfolio Duration and Convexity

For a portfolio of bonds, portfolio duration is the market‑value‑weighted average of individual durations; portfolio convexity is the weighted average of individual convexities. Immunisation strategies match portfolio duration to the investment horizon and maximise convexity subject to that constraint, reducing reinvestment risk.

Practical Calculation in Exams

Examination problems usually provide a bond’s cash‑flow schedule, yield, and ask for duration or the estimated price change for a given yield shift. Candidates should tabulate t, Cₜ, PVₜ, t×PVₜ, and t(t+1)×PVₜ, then apply the formulas. A spreadsheet‑style layout on paper reduces arithmetic errors. Remember to adjust for compounding frequency: if coupons are semi‑annual, time periods are half‑years, yield is semi‑annual, and the final modified duration must be divided by 2 to express in years.

Effective Annual Rate with Fees, Charges, and Compounding Frequency

The nominal annual rate quoted on a deposit or loan rarely reflects the true cost or return once fees, processing charges, and compounding frequency are incorporated. The effective annual rate (EAR) standardises these factors into a single annualised percentage for comparison.

EAR from Nominal Rate and Compounding

For a nominal rate r compounded m times per year, EAR = (1 + r/m)ᵐ − 1. If a bank offers 12 % nominal compounded monthly, EAR = (1 + 0.12/12)¹² − 1 ≈ 12.68 %. Continuous compounding gives the upper bound: EAR = eʳ − 1.

Incorporating Upfront Fees

Suppose a loan of ₹10,00,000 carries a 10 % nominal rate compounded annually but charges a 1 % processing fee deducted upfront. The borrower receives ₹9,90,000 but repays ₹11,00,000 after one year. The effective rate solves 9,90,000 × (1 + EAR) = 11,00,000 → EAR = 11,00,000 ÷ 9,90,000 − 1 ≈ 11.11 %. The fee raises the effective cost above the nominal 10 %.

Periodic Service Charges

Some deposits levy a quarterly maintenance charge of ₹500 on a ₹1,00,000 balance earning 8 % nominal compounded quarterly. Each quarter the gross interest is ₹1,00,000 × 0.02 = ₹2,000; after the ₹500 charge the net addition is ₹1,500. The effective quarterly growth factor is (1,00,000 + 1,500) ÷ 1,00,000 = 1.015. The EAR = (1.015)⁴ − 1 ≈ 6.14 %, noticeably lower than the 8.24 % EAR without charges.

Comparing Products

When comparing a fixed deposit at 7.5 % compounded quarterly (EAR ≈ 7.71 %) with a recurring deposit at 7.2 % compounded monthly plus a ₹200 annual fee, compute the EAR for each after adjusting for the fee. The product with the higher EAR delivers a better return for the same risk profile. Examination questions often present two or three alternatives and ask which yields the highest effective return; the candidate must calculate each EAR correctly, including all explicit charges.

Tax‑Adjusted EAR

If interest income is taxed at rate τ, a simple approximation for the post‑tax EAR is EAR × (1 − τ). For a deposit with EAR 8 % and τ = 30 %, post‑tax EAR ≈ 5.6 %. Use this version for exam speed when tax is applied annually; if tax is deducted each compounding period, the effective rate is slightly lower.

Interest Rate Parity and Covered Interest Arbitrage Basics

Interest rate parity (IRP) links the spot exchange rate, the forward exchange rate, and the interest rates of two currencies. It ensures that an investor cannot earn a risk‑free profit by borrowing in one currency, converting at the spot rate, investing in the other, and locking in the forward rate to convert back.

Covered Interest Parity Formula

Let S be the spot rate (domestic currency per unit of foreign currency), F the forward rate for the same maturity, i_d the domestic risk‑free rate, and i_f the foreign risk‑free rate, both for the same tenor. Covered interest parity states: F = S × (1 + i_d) ÷ (1 + i_f). If this equality does not hold, a covered interest arbitrage exists.

Arbitrage Steps

  1. Borrow domestic currency at i_d.
  2. Convert to foreign currency at spot S.
  3. Invest foreign currency at i_f.
  4. Enter a forward contract to sell the foreign proceeds at F.
  5. At maturity, repay the domestic loan with interest; the forward contract locks the conversion rate.

If F > S × (1 + i_d)/(1 + i_f), the forward is overpriced. The arbitrageur does the reverse: borrow foreign, convert to domestic, invest domestic, and buy foreign forward. The profit is risk‑free and disappears as market participants exploit it.

Example with Numbers

Spot USD/INR = 83.00. One‑year USD rate = 4 %, INR rate = 7 %. The IRP forward = 83 × (1.07)/(1.04) ≈ 85.39. If the market quotes a one‑year forward of 86.00, the forward is expensive. An arbitrageur borrows USD at 4 %, converts to INR at 83, invests at 7 %, and sells INR forward at 86. The locked‑in USD return exceeds 4 %, yielding a risk‑free profit.

Uncovered Interest Parity

Uncovered interest parity replaces the forward rate with the expected future spot rate E[S₁]. It states E[S₁] = S × (1 + i_d)/(1 + i_f). Unlike covered parity, it involves exchange‑rate risk and is an expectation, not an arbitrage condition. Examinations may ask to distinguish the two and to identify which one holds by arbitrage.

Application in Exam Questions

Typical problems give spot, domestic and foreign rates, and ask to compute the theoretical forward or to detect an arbitrage opportunity. Candidates must keep the currency quotation convention consistent (e.g., INR per USD) and apply the formula with the correct numerator/denominator. A common trap is swapping i_d and i_f when the quote is inverted. Writing the formula in words — “forward equals spot times one plus domestic rate over one plus foreign rate” — before plugging numbers avoids this error.

  • Using the wrong day‑count convention for the instrument type, leading to systematic accrued‑interest errors.
  • Treating a withdrawal or deposit as if it occurred at the start of the compounding period when the problem states end‑of‑period.
  • Confusing Macaulay duration with modified duration and forgetting to divide by (1+y) for the latter.
  • Neglecting convexity when estimating price changes for yield shifts larger than 50 basis points.
  • Calculating EAR from nominal rate but omitting upfront fees or periodic charges that materially alter the effective cost.
  • Swapping domestic and foreign interest rates in the covered interest parity formula when the exchange‑rate quote is inverted.
  • Applying the simple‑interest tax adjustment (EAR × (1−τ)) to a compound‑interest deposit where tax is deducted each compounding period.

Mastering these advanced concepts equips you to handle the most challenging interest‑rate problems encountered in competitive examinations.
