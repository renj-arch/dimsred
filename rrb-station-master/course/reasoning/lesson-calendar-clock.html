What are Calendar & Clock Problems?

Calendar and clock problems test your ability to calculate dates, weekdays, angles, and time-related phenomena using modular arithmetic and basic spatial reasoning. SSC CGL typically asks 1-2 questions from this topic in Tier 1.

Calendar Concepts

1. Odd Days: The number of days beyond a complete week (7 days) is called odd days. For example, 365 days = 52 weeks + 1 odd day. 366 days (leap year) = 52 weeks + 2 odd days.

2. Leap Year Rules: A year divisible by 4 is a leap year. Exception: century years must be divisible by 400 to be leap years (e.g., 1900 is not a leap year, but 2000 is).

3. Weekday Calculation: If you know the weekday of a reference date, you can calculate any other date by adding odd days and getting modulo 7 (0=Sunday, 1=Monday, ..., 6=Saturday).

1. Angle Between Hands: In 60 minutes, the minute hand covers 360Â° (6Â° per minute), and the hour hand covers 30Â° (0.5Â° per minute). The angle between hands = |30H - 5.5M|, where H is the hour and M is minutes.

2. Clock Gains/Losses: A watch that gains or loses time does so at a constant rate. Calculate the net gain/loss per hour and multiply by elapsed time.

3. Mirror Images: The mirror image of a clock shows the time as (12:00 âˆ’ actual time). If it's hh:mm, the mirror time is 11:60 - hh:mm.

Leap Year Cycle and Century Exceptions

The Gregorian calendar inserts a leap day on 29 February every year that is divisible by 4, except for century years that are not divisible by 400. This rule creates a 400‑year cycle containing 97 leap years and 303 ordinary years. The total number of days in 400 years is 400 × 365 + 97 = 146 097, which is exactly 20 871 weeks; therefore the calendar repeats every 400 years with zero odd days.

For shorter spans the odd‑day count is derived from the composition of leap and ordinary years. A normal year contributes 1 odd day (365 ≡ 1 (mod 7)). A leap year contributes 2 odd days (366 ≡ 2 (mod 7)). In a 100‑year block there are 24 leap years (years 4, 8, …, 96) and 76 ordinary years, giving 24 × 2 + 76 × 1 = 124 odd days ≡ 5 (mod 7). Consequently a 100‑year period adds 5 odd days. A 200‑year period adds 10 ≡ 3 odd days, a 300‑year period adds 15 ≡ 1 odd day, and a 400‑year period adds 20 ≡ 0 odd days because the century year 400 is a leap year.

When calculating the weekday for a date far in the past or future, break the interval into complete 400‑year cycles (zero odd days), then add the odd days for the remaining centuries, decades, and single years. For example, to move from 1 January 2000 (Saturday) to 1 January 2100, note that 2000 is a leap century, so the 100‑year block 2000‑2099 contains 25 leap years (including 2000) and 75 ordinary years → 25 × 2 + 75 × 1 = 125 odd days ≡ 6 (mod 7). Adding 6 odd days to Saturday gives Friday, which matches the known weekday of 1 January 2100.

A common shortcut is to memorise the century‑odd‑day table: 1600 → 0, 1700 → 5, 1800 → 3, 1900 → 1, 2000 → 0, 2100 → 5, 2200 → 3, 2300 → 1, 2400 → 0. This table follows the 5‑3‑1‑0 pattern and eliminates the need to recount leap years for every century.

When the target date lies inside a century, add the odd days for each completed year after the century start. For months, use the standard month‑odd‑day values for a non‑leap year (Jan = 3, Feb = 0, Mar = 3, Apr = 2, May = 3, Jun = 2, Jul = 3, Aug = 3, Sep = 2, Oct = 3, Nov = 2, Dec = 3) and add one extra odd day for February in a leap year. Summing all contributions modulo 7 yields the required weekday.

Clock Gain and Loss Mechanics

A clock that gains or loses time at a constant rate can be modelled by a linear relationship between the indicated time and the true elapsed time. If a clock gains g minutes per hour, then in t true hours it will show an advance of t × (60 + g) minutes. Conversely, if it loses l minutes per hour, the shown advance is t × (60 − l) minutes. The ratio of shown minutes to true minutes is therefore (60 ± rate)/60.

To find the true time when the faulty clock shows a particular reading, set up the proportion: shown minutes / true minutes = (60 ± rate)/60. For instance, a clock that gains 5 minutes per hour shows 65 minutes for every 60 true minutes. If it reads 3 hours = 180 minutes, the true elapsed minutes = 180 × 60/65 = 166.15 minutes ≈ 2 hours 46 minutes.

The moment when a gaining clock again shows the correct time occurs when the accumulated gain equals a multiple of 12 hours (720 minutes) for a 12‑hour dial, or 24 hours (1440 minutes) for a 24‑hour dial. If the gain is g minutes per hour, the gain per true hour is g minutes. The number of true hours needed for a 12‑hour gain is 720/g. For a loss of l minutes per hour, the clock falls behind; it will show the correct time again when the total loss reaches 720 minutes, i.e. after 720/l true hours.

When two faulty clocks are compared, their relative gain/loss per hour is the algebraic sum of their individual rates. If clock A gains 3 min/h and clock B loses 2 min/h, the relative rate is 5 min/h. They will show the same reading whenever the relative gain accumulates to a multiple of 720 minutes, i.e. every 720/5 = 144 true hours.

Problems often ask for the correct time at a given instant when the faulty clock was set right at a known moment. Compute the true elapsed interval, multiply by the gain/loss rate to obtain the total error, then add (for gain) or subtract (for loss) that error from the faulty reading to recover the true time. Always keep units consistent: convert hours to minutes before applying the rate.

Gain/Loss Shortcut
A clock gains 5 minutes every hour. It is set right at 6:00 AM. What time will the clock show at 6:00 PM on the same day?
True elapsed time = 12 hours = 720 minutes. Gain per hour = 5 minutes → total gain = 12 × 5 = 60 minutes = 1 hour. Faulty clock reading = true time + gain = 6:00 PM + 1 hour = 7:00 PM. Answer: 7:00 PM.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Gain/Loss
Memorise the formula: error = (rate × true hours). For a 12‑hour dial, the clock repeats its correct reading every 720/rate hours. Convert all given times to minutes past a reference (e.g., 12:00) before applying the rate; this avoids hour‑minute carry errors. When two clocks are involved, work with their relative rate to find meeting points quickly.

Relative Speed Method for Clock Problems

The minute hand moves 360° in 60 minutes → 6° per minute. The hour hand moves 360° in 12 hours = 720 minutes → 0.5° per minute. Their relative angular speed is 6 − 0.5 = 5.5° per minute. This single figure solves all coincidence, opposition, and right‑angle questions without memorising separate formulas.

At any given hour H (in 12‑hour format) and minute M, the hour hand angle from 12 o’clock is 30H + 0.5M degrees, and the minute hand angle is 6M degrees. The absolute difference is |30H − 5.5M|. If this exceeds 180°, subtract from 360° to obtain the smaller angle.

To find when the hands coincide after a known hour H, set the angle difference to zero: 30H − 5.5M = 0 → M = (30H)/5.5 = (60H)/11 minutes past H. For example, after 4 o’clock the coincidence occurs at 4 + 240/11 = 4 : 21 9/11 minutes.

For opposition (180° apart), set |30H − 5.5M| = 180. This yields two solutions per 12‑hour cycle: M = (30H ± 180)/5.5. The “+” gives the first opposition after H, the “−” gives the one before H (or after the previous hour). For right angles (90°), use 90 instead of 180.

The relative speed method also handles “how many times do the hands form a right angle in a day?” Since the relative speed is 5.5°/min, the hands gain 360° every 360/5.5 = 720/11 ≈ 65.45 minutes. In 12 hours there are 11 coincidences, 11 oppositions, and 22 right angles. Doubling for 24 hours gives 22 coincidences, 22 oppositions, and 44 right angles.

When the clock is faulty (gaining or losing), adjust the effective speeds. A gaining clock’s minute hand moves faster than 6°/min by a factor (60 + g)/60, and the hour hand moves faster by the same factor. Their relative speed becomes 5.5 × (60 + g)/60 degrees per true minute. Use this adjusted relative speed in the same equations to find apparent coincidences on the faulty dial.

Calendar Repetition Patterns and the 28‑Year Cycle

For any non‑century year, the calendar repeats after 28 years because the leap‑year pattern (every 4 years) and the weekday shift (1 odd day per ordinary year, 2 per leap year) align every 28 years: 28 × 1 + 7 × 1 = 35 odd days ≡ 0 (mod 7). The seven leap years in a 28‑year block contribute an extra 7 odd days, totalling 35 ≡ 0.

Century years break the 28‑year cycle because the century year is not a leap year unless divisible by 400. For example, 1900 is not a leap year, so the 28‑year repetition that would map 1872 to 1900 fails; the calendar of 1872 repeats in 1900 only after a 12‑year gap (1872 → 1884 → 1896 → 1908). The general rule: a year Y (non‑century) repeats in Y + 28 unless a non‑leap century falls in between; then the next repetition is Y + 40 (or Y + 12 if the century is a leap year like 2000).

To find the next year with the same calendar as a given year, first check if the given year is a leap year. If it is a leap year, the next identical leap‑year calendar occurs after 28 years, provided no non‑leap century intervenes. If the given year is ordinary, the next identical ordinary calendar also occurs after 28 years under the same condition. When a non‑leap century lies in the interval, add 12 years to the 28‑year step (i.e., 40 years total) because the missing leap day shifts the weekday pattern by one extra day.

A practical shortcut: compute the year’s “calendar code” = (year + ⌊year/4⌋ − ⌊year/100⌋ + ⌊year/400⌋) mod 7. Two years share the same calendar iff they have the same calendar code and the same leap‑year status. This formula automatically accounts for century exceptions and works for any Gregorian year.

For example, 2023 is ordinary. Calendar code = (2023 + 505 − 20 + 5) mod 7 = 2513 mod 7 = 2. The next ordinary year with code 2 is 2034 (2023 + 11) because 2024 is leap, 2025‑2033 shift codes, and 2034 matches. Indeed 2023 and 2034 have identical calendars. The 28‑year rule would give 2051, but the intervening leap‑year pattern makes 2034 the earlier repeat.

Non‑Standard Clocks and Mirror‑Image Variations

Examiners often test clocks that lack numbers, run on a 24‑hour dial, or are viewed in a mirror or water reflection. The core geometry remains the same; only the reference frame changes.

For a 24‑hour analog clock, the hour hand completes one revolution in 24 hours → 15° per hour = 0.25° per minute. The minute hand still moves 6° per minute. Relative speed = 6 − 0.25 = 5.75° per minute. Coincidence interval = 360/5.75 = 1440/23 ≈ 62.61 minutes. There are 23 coincidences in 24 hours. The angle formula becomes |15H − 5.75M|, where H is in 0‑23 range.

Mirror image of a 12‑hour clock: the apparent time is 11:60 − actual time (hours:minutes). For a 24‑hour clock, the mirror axis is at 12:00/00:00, so the mirrored time = 24:00 − actual time. Example: actual 14:30 → mirror = 24:00 − 14:30 = 09:30.

Water image (vertical reflection) swaps the positions of 12 and 6, 1 and 5, 2 and 4, etc. The transformation for a 12‑hour clock is: water time = 12:00 − actual time (if actual ≤ 12:00) or 24:00 − actual time (if actual > 12:00). In practice, compute the mirror time first, then apply the water rule, or use the direct formula: water hour = (12 − hour) mod 12, water minute = (60 − minute) mod 60, with special handling when minute = 0.

Clocks with missing numbers (e.g., only 12, 3, 6, 9 marked) require the candidate to infer the hour hand position from the nearest marked hour. The angle between the hour hand and the nearest marked hour is 0.5° × minutes past that hour. This is often combined with gain/loss: a faulty clock showing 3:20 with only 12, 3, 6, 9 visible – the hour hand is 10° past 3 (20 × 0.5). If the clock gains 2 min/h, the true minute is slightly less; adjust using the gain ratio.

When multiple distortions combine (mirror + gain), solve sequentially: first find the true time on the faulty clock using gain/loss, then apply the mirror transformation. Always keep a clear timeline: reference moment → true elapsed → faulty reading → transformed reading.

  • Assuming the 28‑year repetition works across a non‑leap century without adding the extra 12 years.
  • Using the 12‑hour mirror formula (11:60 − time) for a 24‑hour clock.
  • Forgetting to convert gain/loss rates to a per‑minute basis before applying relative speed.
  • Treating the hour hand speed as 0.5°/min on a 24‑hour dial.
  • Neglecting the extra odd day contributed by a leap century (e.g., 2000) when summing century odd days.
  • Applying the water‑image rule without first normalising the minute to the 0‑59 range.
  • Confusing the coincidence count for a 24‑hour clock (23 per day) with the 12‑hour count (11 per 12 hours).

Mastering these advanced patterns ensures accurate solutions even when the examiner layers multiple distortions in a single question.

Zeller's Congruence and the Doomsday Algorithm for Weekday Determination

Zeller's congruence provides a closed‑form expression that returns the day of the week for any Gregorian date without iterating through years. For a date given as day d, month m (with March = 3, …, January = 13, February = 14 of the previous year), year Y (adjusted for January and February), the formula is h = ( d + ⌊13(m+1)/5⌋ + K + ⌊K/4⌋ + ⌊J/4⌋ + 5J ) mod 7, where K = Y mod 100 and J = ⌊Y/100⌋. The result h = 0 corresponds to Saturday, 1 to Sunday, …, 6 to Friday. The Doomsday algorithm, devised by John Conway, exploits the fact that certain “anchor” dates — 4/4, 6/6, 8/8, 10/10, 12/12, and the last day of February — always fall on the same weekday in any given year. The anchor weekday for a year is computed as ( Y + ⌊Y/4⌋ − ⌊Y/100⌋ + ⌊Y/400⌋ ) mod 7, with 0 = Sunday. Once the anchor is known, the weekday of any date follows by counting the offset from the nearest anchor. Both methods avoid the cumulative odd‑day addition taught at the basic level and are indispensable when a question asks for the weekday of a date far outside the 28‑year repetition window, such as 15 August 1947 or 26 January 2000. Mastery of the modular arithmetic behind these algorithms also clarifies why the 400‑year Gregorian cycle contains exactly 146 097 days, a multiple of 7, guaranteeing calendar repetition every 400 years.

Clock Gain and Loss with Variable Rates and Multiple Reference Clocks

Standard gain/loss problems assume a constant rate, e.g., a clock gains 2 minutes per hour. Advanced questions introduce piecewise rates: a clock may gain 1 minute per hour for the first 6 hours, then lose 30 seconds per hour thereafter, or two faulty clocks are compared against each other rather than against a perfect reference. The key is to convert each interval into an effective “true‑time‑per‑displayed‑time” factor. If a clock gains g minutes per real hour, then in one real hour it shows 60+g minutes; the factor f = (60+g)/60 relates displayed minutes to real minutes. For a losing clock, g is negative. When rates change at known displayed times, split the timeline at those displayed moments, compute the real elapsed for each segment using the appropriate factor, and sum the real elapsed. If two faulty clocks A and B are given with rates g₁ and g₂, their displayed times diverge linearly; the moment they show the same reading again satisfies (60+g₁)·t ≡ (60+g₂)·t (mod 720) for a 12‑hour dial, where t is real minutes. Solving yields the next coincidence of displayed times. Problems may also ask for the true time when a faulty clock shows a specific reading, requiring inversion of the factor: real minutes = displayed minutes × 60/(60+g). When multiple rate changes occur, maintain a table of intervals, displayed‑time boundaries, and corresponding factors; this systematic bookkeeping prevents the common error of applying a single average rate across the whole period.

Variable‑Rate Gain/Loss Worked Example
A wall clock gains 2 minutes per hour for the first 4 hours of real time, then loses 1 minute per hour for the next 3 hours, after which it runs correctly. If the clock was set correctly at 06:00, what will it display at the true time 13:00?
Real interval = 7 hours = 420 minutes. Segment 1: first 4 real hours (240 minutes) at gain +2 min/hr → factor = 62/60. Displayed minutes in segment 1 = 240 × 62/60 = 248 minutes = 4 h 8 min. Segment 2: next 3 real hours (180 minutes) at loss −1 min/hr → factor = 59/60. Displayed minutes in segment 2 = 180 × 59/60 = 177 minutes = 2 h 57 min. Total displayed elapsed = 248 + 177 = 425 minutes = 7 h 5 min. Starting from 06:00, the clock shows 13:05.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem describes a clock whose rate changes, draw a two‑column table: real‑time intervals in the left column, corresponding gain/loss factor in the right. Compute displayed minutes for each row, then sum. This visual split eliminates the temptation to average rates, which is mathematically incorrect because the denominator (real minutes) differs across segments.

Synchronisation of Multiple Clocks and Relative Meeting Times

Questions often present three or more clocks, each with its own gain or loss, and ask for the next instant when all show the same time (or when two show a specific angle). The relative speed approach extends naturally: for any pair, the relative gain per real hour is the difference of their individual gains. If clock A gains g₁ min/hr and clock B gains g₂ min/hr, then A gains (g₁−g₂) minutes on B each real hour. On a 12‑hour dial, the displayed times coincide whenever the accumulated relative gain reaches a multiple of 720 minutes. Thus the real interval between coincidences of A and B is 720 / |g₁−g₂| hours. For three clocks, compute pairwise coincidence intervals and take the least common multiple (LCM) of those intervals, provided the initial offsets are zero (all set correctly together). If the clocks start with different displayed times, convert each initial offset into an equivalent “phase” in minutes, then solve the system of congruences: displayed time of clock i = (60+gᵢ)·t + offsetᵢ (mod 720). This is a linear congruence system solvable by the Chinese Remainder Theorem when the moduli are pairwise coprime; otherwise, reduce by common factors. In practice, examiners limit the number of clocks to three and choose gains that make the LCM manageable (e.g., 1, 2, 3 min/hr → pairwise intervals 720, 360, 240 → LCM 720 hours = 30 days). Recognising that the LCM of the pairwise intervals gives the global synchronisation period saves enormous trial‑and‑error time.

Calendar Repetition Across Century Boundaries and the 400‑Year Cycle

The 28‑year repetition rule holds only when the intervening years contain the same pattern of leap years. A non‑leap century year (e.g., 1700, 1800, 1900) omits the 29 February that would otherwise appear, shifting the weekday sequence by one extra day relative to the 28‑year expectation. Consequently, a calendar that would repeat after 28 years instead repeats after 40 years (28 + 12) when a non‑leap century falls inside the interval. The 400‑year Gregorian cycle contains 97 leap years and 303 ordinary years, totalling 146 097 days = 20 871 weeks exactly. Therefore any date’s weekday repeats every 400 years without exception. The “calendar code” (year + ⌊year/4⌋ − ⌊year/100⌋ + ⌊year/400⌋) mod 7 captures this cycle in a single expression. Two years share an identical calendar iff they have the same calendar code and the same leap‑year status. For example, 1900 (ordinary, code = 0) and 1928 (leap, code = 0) do not match because leap status differs. The next ordinary year with code = 0 after 1900 is 1906 (code = 0, ordinary), but the full calendar match requires the same leap pattern across the whole year; the earliest full match for 1900 is 1906? Actually 1900 and 1906 both ordinary and code = 0, so they share the same calendar. However, 1900 and 1928 differ in leap status, so they cannot be identical. When solving “find the next year with the same calendar as Y”, first compute Y’s code and leap flag, then increment year by 1, 2, … until both match; the maximum search length is 40 years (or 28 if no century crossing). This deterministic search is faster than memorising exception tables.

Daylight Saving Transitions and Their Effect on Angle Calculations

Some reasoning questions embed a clock problem within a daylight‑saving‑time (DST) change. When clocks are moved forward by one hour (spring forward), the displayed time jumps from 01:59 to 03:00, effectively deleting one hour of displayed minutes. Conversely, the autumn fallback repeats the hour 01:00–01:59. For angle calculations, the hour‑hand position depends on the displayed hour and minute. During the forward jump, the hour hand instantaneously advances by 30° (one hour mark) plus the minute‑hand contribution for the skipped minutes. If a question asks for the angle at a true time that falls inside the skipped hour, the clock never shows that displayed time; the correct answer is “undefined” or “the clock does not display that time”. For the repeated hour, the same displayed time occurs twice, once before and once after the fallback; the hour‑hand angle is identical in both occurrences because the displayed hour and minute are the same. However, the true elapsed time between the two occurrences is 60 minutes, so a gain/loss clock will have accumulated different errors. To handle DST in gain/loss problems, split the real timeline at the transition moment, apply the appropriate gain factor before and after, and remember that the displayed clock jumps or repeats. A practical shortcut: convert all true times to a continuous “standard time” scale (e.g., UTC) that does not observe DST, perform the gain/loss computation on that scale, then map the result back to the local displayed time using the DST rules. This avoids the pitfall of treating the missing or duplicated hour as a normal 60‑minute interval.

  • Applying a single average gain rate across a piecewise‑rate interval instead of segment‑wise factors.
  • Assuming the 28‑year calendar repetition works across a non‑leap century without adding the extra 12 years.
  • Using the 12‑hour mirror formula (11:60 − time) for a 24‑hour dial.
  • Forgetting to convert gain/loss rates to a per‑minute basis before applying relative speed.
  • Treating the hour‑hand speed as 0.5°/min on a 24‑hour dial.
  • Neglecting the extra odd day contributed by a leap century (e.g., 2000) when summing century odd days.
  • Applying the water‑image rule without first normalising the minute to the 0‑59 range.

These advanced techniques and trap awareness complete the toolkit needed for the most demanding calendar and clock questions.

Julian Day Numbers and Cross‑Calendar Date Differences

The Julian Day Number (JDN) provides a continuous integer count of days starting from noon UTC on 1 January 4713 BC (Julian calendar). Because it ignores month‑length irregularities and leap‑year rules, JDN is the most reliable tool for computing the exact number of days between any two dates, even when the dates belong to different calendar systems (Julian, Gregorian, or proleptic extensions). The conversion from a Gregorian calendar date (Y, M, D) to JDN uses the well‑known algorithm:

a = ⌊(14 − M) / 12⌋ y = Y + 4800 − a m = M + 12a − 3 JDN = D + ⌊(153m + 2) / 5⌋ + 365y + ⌊y/4⌋ − ⌊y/100⌋ + ⌊y/400⌋ − 32045

For a Julian calendar date the terms ⌊y/100⌋ and ⌊y/400⌋ are omitted and the constant becomes −32083. The difference between two JDNs equals the exact number of days elapsed. To obtain the weekday, compute (JDN + 1) mod 7 where 0 = Monday (or adjust the offset for Sunday‑based conventions). This method automatically accounts for the 10‑day shift introduced by the Gregorian reform of 1582 and for any subsequent century‑leap‑year omissions. When a problem asks for the number of odd days between two historical dates, convert both to JDN, subtract, and reduce modulo 7. The JDN approach also eliminates the need to memorise month‑code tables; the only required data are the year, month, and day numbers.

In competitive examinations, a typical twist is to give one date in the Julian calendar (e.g., 4 October 1582) and the next day in the Gregorian calendar (15 October 1582). Using JDN, the difference is exactly 1 day, confirming that the reform removed ten calendar days but not a day of elapsed time. Another common variant asks for the weekday of a date before 1582 in a country that adopted the Gregorian calendar later (e.g., Britain in 1752). The same JDN formula works provided the correct calendar flag (Julian vs Gregorian) is used for each date. Mastery of the JDN algorithm therefore gives a single, error‑free procedure for all cross‑calendar date‑difference and weekday questions.

Continuous‑Motion Clock Angles with Fractional Minutes

Standard clock‑angle formulas assume the minute hand jumps discretely each minute and the hour hand moves in 0.5° steps per minute. In many advanced problems the clock is described as “running continuously” or “the hands move smoothly”. Under continuous motion the hour‑hand angle at a true time H hours M minutes S seconds is:

θ_h = 30H + 0.5M + (0.5/60)S = 30H + 0.5M + S/120

The minute‑hand angle is:

θ_m = 6M + 0.1S

The second‑hand angle (if needed) is 6S. The absolute difference Δ = |θ_h − θ_m| gives the smaller angle when reduced to min(Δ, 360−Δ). Because the hour hand now advances during each second, the classic “coincidence every 65 5/11 minutes” becomes a continuously solvable equation: 30H + 0.5M + S/120 = 6M + 0.1S (mod 360). Solving for the exact instant of coincidence yields a rational number of seconds, e.g., the first coincidence after 12:00 occurs at 1 hour 5 minutes 27.2727… seconds. When a question asks for the angle at a non‑integer minute such as 3:14:30, plug S = 30 into the formulas above. The result differs from the discrete‑minute approximation by up to 0.25°, which can be decisive in multiple‑choice options spaced by 0.5°.

A frequent examiner trick is to combine continuous motion with a gain/loss clock. Suppose a clock gains 2 seconds per true minute and runs continuously. Its displayed time after T true minutes is T × (1 + 2/60) minutes. Convert this displayed elapsed time into H, M, S (allowing fractional seconds) and then apply the continuous‑motion angle formulas. The gain factor must be applied to the true elapsed seconds before the angle computation; applying it after the angle step produces a systematic error. Practising the conversion between true seconds, displayed seconds, and continuous angles eliminates this trap.

Technique: Continuous‑Motion Angle with Gain
A clock gains 3 seconds every true minute and its hands move continuously. At true time 09:20:15, what is the smaller angle between the hour and minute hands shown on the clock?

1. True elapsed seconds since 12:00 = (9 × 3600) + (20 × 60) + 15 = 33 615 s.

2. Gain factor = 1 + 3/60 = 1.05. Displayed elapsed seconds = 33 615 × 1.05 = 35 295.75 s.

3. Convert displayed seconds to H, M, S: 35 295.75 s = 9 h 48 min 15.75 s (since 9 × 3600 = 32 400; remainder 2 895.75; 48 × 60 = 2 880; remainder 15.75).

4. Continuous hour‑hand angle = 30 × 9 + 0.5 × 48 + 15.75/120 = 270 + 24 + 0.13125 = 294.13125°.

5. Continuous minute‑hand angle = 6 × 48 + 0.1 × 15.75 = 288 + 1.575 = 289.575°.

6. Difference = |294.13125 − 289.575| = 4.55625°. Smaller angle = 4.55625° (since < 180°).

Answer: 4.56° (approximately).

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem mentions “smoothly moving hands” or gives a time with seconds, immediately switch to the continuous‑motion formulas. Write the gain/loss factor as a multiplier on true seconds, compute displayed seconds, then break into H‑M‑S with decimals. Keep a small calculator‑style table on your rough sheet: 1 h = 3600 s, 1 min = 60 s, hour‑hand per second = 1/120°, minute‑hand per second = 0.1°. This avoids re‑deriving constants under time pressure.

Piecewise Gain‑Loss Clocks with Periodic Reset

Some clocks do not run at a constant rate; they may gain for a certain interval, then lose, or be manually reset to the correct time at known moments. The overall displayed time after a sequence of intervals is the sum of each interval’s true duration multiplied by its specific rate factor, plus any reset offsets. If a clock gains 5 minutes per hour for the first 3 hours, then loses 2 minutes per hour for the next 4 hours, and is reset to the correct time at the 7‑hour mark, the displayed time just before reset equals 3 × 65 + 4 × 58 = 195 + 232 = 427 minutes = 7 h 7 min. The reset discards the accumulated error, so after reset the clock again shows the true time. For any later true time T > 7 h, the displayed time = (T − 7 h) × current‑rate + 7 h (true). When multiple resets occur, treat each segment independently and stitch the results together.

A typical exam question provides a table of rate changes and reset times, then asks for the displayed time at a given true instant or the true instant when the clock shows a particular displayed time. The solution procedure: (1) Convert all given times to a common unit (minutes or seconds). (2) Build a piecewise function D(t) = displayed minutes as a function of true minutes t. (3) For each segment i with rate r_i (displayed minutes per true minute) and start true time t_i, the contribution is r_i × (t − t_i) for t within the segment. (4) Add any reset offsets (usually zero after reset). (5) Solve the resulting linear equation for the unknown. Because each segment is linear, the overall function is piecewise linear and can be inverted segment by segment.

Watch for hidden resets described as “the clock is set right at 6 PM” or “the clock is corrected every 12 hours”. These are equivalent to resetting the error to zero. Also note that a “gain of 2 minutes per hour” means r = 62/60 ≈ 1.03333, while a “loss of 3 minutes per hour” means r = 57/60 = 0.95. Using the exact fraction avoids rounding errors that accumulate over many hours.

Multiple Event Coincidence in a Single Clock Cycle

Beyond the classic “when do the hands coincide?” question, examiners ask for the times within a 12‑hour period when the hands form a right angle, a straight line (180°), or any specified angle θ. The general equation for the angle between the hands (continuous motion) is |30H − 5.5M| = θ (mod 360), where H is the hour (0–11) and M is the minute including fractional part. For a given θ, this yields two linear equations per hour: 30H − 5.5M = ±θ + 360k. Solving for M gives M = (30H ∓ θ − 360k) / 5.5. Because M must lie in [0, 60), only certain integer k values produce valid solutions. In a 12‑hour cycle there are 22 coincidences (θ = 0), 44 right‑angle occurrences (θ = 90°), and 22 straight‑line occurrences (θ = 180°). The pattern repeats every 12 hours for a 12‑hour dial.

When the clock runs with a constant gain or loss, replace true minutes by displayed minutes in the equation. If the clock gains g minutes per true hour, the displayed minute hand moves at 6 × (1 + g/60)° per true minute, while the displayed hour hand moves at 0.5 × (1 + g/60)° per true minute. The relative speed becomes 5.5 × (1 + g/60)° per true minute. The coincidence interval shortens to 720 / (11 × (1 + g/60)) true minutes. For a loss, the denominator uses (1 − |l|/60). This scaling applies uniformly to all angle events, so the number of right angles in a true 12‑hour period changes only if the gain/loss is large enough to add or remove an entire cycle within the interval.

A common composite question: “A clock gains 2 minutes per hour. How many times between 6 AM and 6 PM (true time) do the hands form a 30° angle?” First compute the effective relative speed factor = 1 + 2/60 = 31/30. The true interval is 12 h = 720 min. Number of 30° events in a perfect clock = 22 (since each 30° occurs twice per hour except at 0° and 180°). With gain, the displayed cycle completes faster, so the count becomes floor(22 × 31/30) = floor(22.733…) = 22. The extra fractional event does not complete within the true window, so the answer remains 22. If the gain were 10 min/hr (factor 7/6), the count would be floor(22 × 7/6) = floor(25.66) = 25. Practising this scaling logic lets you answer such questions without enumerating every occurrence.

Leap‑Second Adjustments and Long‑Term Angle Drift

Leap seconds are occasionally inserted into Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) to keep atomic time within 0.9 s of astronomical time. A positive leap second adds an extra second (23:59:60) at the end of a UTC day, usually 30 June or 31 December. For a clock that displays UTC and runs continuously, the leap second appears as a one‑second pause in the displayed second hand while the true atomic time continues. The hour and minute hands, however, advance normally because the leap second does not change the displayed hour or minute. Consequently, the angle between the hour and minute hands is unaffected by a leap second; only the second‑hand position jumps by 6° at the insertion moment.

In gain/loss problems that span many years, the cumulative effect of leap seconds can become relevant if the clock’s rate is referenced to UTC rather than to a uniform atomic timescale. Suppose a clock gains 0.1 seconds per day relative to UTC. Over 10 years (≈ 3652 days) the accumulated gain would be 365.2 seconds, but if three leap seconds were inserted during that period, the UTC reference itself shifted by +3 seconds, so the clock’s error relative to atomic time is 365.2 − 3 = 362.2 seconds. When a problem states “the clock is set to UTC on 1 January 2000 and gains 0.1 s/day”, you must add the known leap‑second count for the interval (available from standard tables) to obtain the true error relative to a uniform timescale. The International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS) publishes the leap‑second schedule; for exam purposes the total number of leap seconds up to a given year is usually provided in the question stem.

Angle drift over very long periods (centuries) is dominated by the Gregorian calendar’s 400‑year cycle rather than leap seconds. However, a clock that is not reset for 400 years will have its displayed date drift by one day relative to the Gregorian calendar because 400 years contain 146 097 days = 20 871 weeks exactly, so the weekday repeats but the date‑of‑month alignment shifts only if the clock’s internal calendar logic omits the century‑leap‑year rule. Modern exam questions rarely require explicit leap‑second arithmetic beyond acknowledging that a leap second does not alter hour‑minute angles. The key takeaway: treat leap seconds as a one‑second UTC step that affects only second‑hand position and any gain/loss measured against UTC.

  • Assuming the hour‑hand moves in discrete 0.5° steps when the problem states “smoothly moving hands”.
  • Applying a single average gain rate across a piecewise‑rate interval instead of segment‑wise factors.
  • Forgetting to convert gain/loss rates to a per‑minute (or per‑second) basis before using the relative‑speed formula.
  • Treating a leap second as a normal 60‑second minute when computing displayed time on a UTC‑referenced clock.
  • Using the 12‑hour mirror formula (11:60 − time) for a 24‑hour dial or for a clock that has been reset mid‑cycle.
  • Neglecting the extra odd day contributed by a leap century (e.g., 2000) when summing century odd days across a 400‑year span.
  • Assuming the number of right‑angle occurrences in a gained clock’s 12‑hour true window equals the perfect‑clock count without scaling by the gain factor.

Decimal Time and Non‑Standard Dial Angle Calculations

Decimal time divides the day into 10 equal hours, each hour into 100 minutes, and each minute into 100 seconds. The hour hand therefore completes a full 360° circle in 10 decimal hours, moving at 36° per decimal hour or 0.36° per decimal minute. The minute hand completes a circle in 100 decimal minutes, moving at 3.6° per decimal minute. The relative speed of the minute hand with respect to the hour hand is 3.6° − 0.36° = 3.24° per decimal minute. When a problem asks for the angle between the hands at a given decimal time H M (where H = 0…9 and M = 0…99), compute the hour‑hand position as 36 × H + 0.36 × M degrees and the minute‑hand position as 3.6 × M degrees. The absolute difference modulo 360° gives the required angle; if the result exceeds 180°, subtract it from 360° to obtain the smaller angle. This framework also works for any dial divided into N equal hours (e.g., an 8‑hour dial used in some industrial shift clocks). In that case the hour‑hand speed is 360/N degrees per hour and the minute‑hand speed is 360/(N × 60) degrees per minute if the minute division remains 60, or 360/(N × M) if the minute division is M. Always verify the minute‑division convention stated in the question.

Key Formulae for an N‑Hour Dial with M Minutes per Hour

  • Hour‑hand speed = 360 / N degrees per hour = (360 / N) / M degrees per minute.
  • Minute‑hand speed = 360 / M degrees per minute.
  • Relative speed = 360/M − 360/(N × M) = 360 × (N − 1) / (N × M) degrees per minute.
  • Coincidence interval = 360 / relative speed = N × M / (N − 1) minutes.

These expressions reduce to the familiar 12‑hour, 60‑minute values when N = 12 and M = 60. Practising with N = 10, M = 100 (decimal) and N = 8, M = 60 (shift clock) builds confidence for any exotic dial that appears in the paper.

Proleptic Gregorian and Julian Calendar Conversions for Historical Dates

Many advanced calendar questions ask for the weekday of a date before the Gregorian reform of 1582, or for a date in a country that adopted the Gregorian calendar later (e.g., Britain 1752, Russia 1918). The proleptic Gregorian calendar extends the modern leap‑year rule (century years divisible by 400 are leap) backward indefinitely, while the Julian calendar uses the simpler rule “every year divisible by 4 is leap”. The difference between the two calendars grows by three days every 400 years because the Julian calendar has 100 leap days per 400 years whereas the Gregorian has 97. In 1582 the offset was 10 days; by 1700 it became 11 days, by 1800 → 12 days, by 1900 → 13 days, and by 2100 it will be 14 days. To convert a Julian date to its Gregorian equivalent, add the appropriate offset for the year in question. Conversely, to find the weekday of a historical Julian date, compute the Julian Day Number (JDN) using the Julian formula, then apply Zeller’s congruence or the Doomsday algorithm on the proleptic Gregorian JDN after subtracting the offset. A common examiner twist: “What was the day of the week on 4 October 1582 (Julian) in Italy?” The answer is Thursday, because the next day in Italy was Friday 15 October 1582 (Gregorian). For Britain, the same Julian date 4 October 1582 corresponds to Thursday 14 October 1582 Gregorian (offset 10 days). Always note the adoption date given in the stem; if absent, assume the proleptic Gregorian calendar for the calculation.

Worked Example: Weekday of a Pre‑Reform Date
Find the day of the week on 23 April 1616 (Julian) in England, knowing that England adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1752 with an 11‑day offset.
Step 1: Convert the Julian date to Gregorian by adding the 10‑day offset applicable for 1616 (1582‑1699 range). 23 April + 10 days = 3 May 1616 Gregorian.
Step 2: Compute the weekday for 3 May 1616 using Zeller’s congruence (Gregorian). For month = 5, day = 3, year = 1616. Using the formula h = (q + ⌊13(m+1)/5⌋ + K + ⌊K/4⌋ + ⌊J/4⌋ + 5J) mod 7, where q=3, m=5, K=16, J=16. Calculation yields h = 6 → Friday (0=Saturday).
Step 3: Therefore 23 April 1616 Julian in England was a Friday.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the five offset bands (10/11/12/13/14 days) and the corresponding century years. When a question gives a Julian date and a country, first identify the adoption year for that country; if not given, use the proleptic Gregorian offset for the date’s year. Write the offset addition explicitly in your rough work to avoid the “off‑by‑one” error that appears when the Julian leap day (29 Feb) falls inside the interval.

Clock Angle Problems with Discrete Hour‑Hand Jumps and Continuous Minute Hand

Standard clock problems assume the hour hand moves continuously at 0.5° per minute. Some variants state that the hour hand jumps discretely at each hour mark (i.e., it stays fixed for the whole hour and then jumps 30° at the next hour). In such a model the hour‑hand position at H hours M minutes (with M < 60) is exactly 30 × H degrees, independent of M. The minute hand still moves continuously at 6° per minute. The angle between the hands is therefore |6 M − 30 H| degrees, reduced to the smaller angle by taking min(θ, 360 − θ). This discrete model changes the coincidence condition: the hands overlap only when 6 M = 30 H (mod 360), i.e., M = 5 H (mod 60). Since M must be an integer minute, coincidences occur only at exact hour multiples (0:00, 1:05, 2:10, …, 10:50) — 11 times in 12 hours, same count but at different instants. Right‑angle occurrences satisfy |6 M − 30 H| = 90 or 270. Solving 6 M = 30 H ± 90 gives M = 5 H ± 15. For each hour H = 0…11 there are two integer solutions provided the resulting M lies in 0…59. This yields 22 right angles per 12‑hour cycle, identical in number to the continuous model but shifted by up to 7.5 minutes. When a problem mixes a discrete hour hand with a gaining/losing clock, apply the gain factor to the minute‑hand speed only (the hour hand still jumps). The effective minute‑hand speed becomes 6 × (1 + g/60)° per true minute, while the hour‑hand position remains 30 × H_displayed. The displayed hour H_displayed advances only when the displayed minute count reaches 60, which occurs faster or slower depending on the gain. Track the displayed time by accumulating displayed minutes = true minutes × (1 + g/60). This hybrid approach appears in “clock with a stuck hour hand” questions.

Variable Gain‑Loss Rates Modelled as Linear Functions of Time

Beyond piecewise‑constant gain, some problems define the gain rate as a linear function of elapsed true time: g(t) = a + b t minutes per hour, where t is measured in true hours from the start. The instantaneous displayed‑time rate is 1 + g(t)/60. To find the displayed time after T true hours, integrate the rate: displayed minutes = ∫₀ᵀ 60 × (1 + g(t)/60) dt = 60 T + ∫₀ᵀ g(t) dt. With g(t) = a + b t, the integral is a T + ½ b T². Hence displayed minutes = 60 T + a T + ½ b T². The displayed hour and minute can then be extracted modulo 720 (12‑hour dial) or 1440 (24‑hour dial). For angle questions, the relative speed at any instant is 5.5 × (1 + g(t)/60)° per true minute. The number of coincidences in a true interval [0, T] equals the integral of relative speed divided by 360: N = (1/360) ∫₀ᵀ 5.5 × (1 + g(t)/60) dt = (5.5/360) [60 T + a T + ½ b T²] / 60. Simplify to N = (11/720) (60 T + a T + ½ b T²). The integer part gives completed coincidences; the fractional part indicates an incomplete cycle. This calculus‑based method is rarely required in full, but recognising the quadratic term ½ b T² lets you answer “how many times do the hands coincide in the first 6 hours if the clock gains 1 min/hr initially and the gain increases by 0.5 min/hr each hour?” without simulating each hour.

Synchronisation of Three or More Clocks Using Least Common Multiple of Effective Periods

When three or more clocks run with different constant gains or losses, the problem often asks for the next instant when all show the same time (or the same angle configuration). Each clock i has an effective period P_i = 720 / (1 + g_i/60) true minutes for a 12‑hour dial (or 1440 / (1 + g_i/60) for 24‑hour). The clocks will simultaneously return to the same displayed time when the true elapsed time is a common multiple of all P_i. Because P_i are rational numbers, compute the least common multiple (LCM) of the numerators divided by the greatest common divisor (GCD) of the denominators after expressing each P_i as a reduced fraction. For example, Clock A gains 2 min/hr → factor 31/30 → P_A = 720 × 30/31 = 21600/31 minutes. Clock B loses 1 min/hr → factor 59/60 → P_B = 720 × 60/59 = 43200/59 minutes. Clock C is accurate → P_C = 720 = 720/1. The LCM of the three periods is LCM(21600, 43200, 720) / GCD(31, 59, 1) = 43200 / 1 = 43200 minutes = 30 days. Thus after 30 true days all three clocks will again show identical times. If the question asks for the first time they show a specific angle (e.g., all at 90°), replace each period with the interval between successive occurrences of that angle for that clock: for a perfect clock the right‑angle interval is 720/11 minutes; for a gaining clock multiply by the same factor (1 + g/60). Then take the LCM of those intervals. This LCM technique extends to any number of clocks and avoids enumerating each coincidence.

  • Assuming the hour hand moves continuously when the problem explicitly states “the hour hand jumps at each hour”.
  • Using the modern Gregorian offset for a Julian date without checking the country’s adoption year.
  • Forgetting that a discrete hour hand eliminates fractional‑minute coincidences, so the coincidence count may differ from the continuous model when gain/loss is present.
  • Integrating a linear gain rate but omitting the ½ b T² term, leading to an under‑count of displayed minutes.
  • Taking the LCM of raw gain factors instead of the effective periods, which yields an incorrect synchronisation time.
  • Neglecting the change of Julian‑Gregorian offset at century boundaries (1700, 1800, 1900) when the interval spans such a year.
  • Applying the 12‑hour mirror formula (11:60 − time) to a 24‑hour or decimal dial without adjusting the base.

Mastering these nuanced models ensures you can tackle every calendar‑clock variant that appears in the examination.
