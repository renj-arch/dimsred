1. Mirror Images

1.1 Principle of Lateral Inversion

The principle of lateral inversion governs every mirror image question: a plane mirror placed vertically reverses the left‑right orientation of an object while preserving its top‑bottom arrangement. When a candidate observes a figure and its mirror counterpart, the rightmost element of the original becomes the leftmost element of the reflected figure, and vice‑versa. This transformation occurs because light rays from each point of the object strike the mirror and reflect back at equal angles, effectively swapping the horizontal coordinates. Understanding that the mirror does not invert the vertical axis is essential; a common error is to treat the mirror as a rotation of 180 degrees, which would also flip the figure upside down. The lateral inversion rule applies uniformly to letters, numbers, geometric shapes, and composite figures, making it the foundational concept for all mirror‑image problems.

1.2 Identifying the Axis of Reflection

In every mirror‑image item the axis of reflection is either explicitly drawn as a vertical line or implied by the wording “mirror placed on the right side” or “mirror placed on the left side”. When the mirror is on the right, the original figure’s right side touches the mirror and the reflected image appears on the left of the axis; when the mirror is on the left, the opposite occurs. Recognising the axis quickly allows the test‑taker to mentally fold the figure along that line and read off the reversed order of components. A useful technique is to label the extreme points of the original figure from left to right, then rewrite the labels in reverse order; the resulting sequence corresponds to the mirror image. This method works for both simple alphabetic strings and complex arrangements of shapes, because the relative spacing between elements remains unchanged after reflection.

1.3 Common Patterns in Alphabet and Numeric Mirror Images

Alphabets and digits exhibit predictable mirror behaviours that can be memorised to save time. Capital letters such as A, H, I, M, O, T, U, V, W, X, Y are laterally symmetric and appear identical in a mirror; letters like B, C, D, E, K become unrecognisable or transform into other symbols, while letters such as L, J, N, P, Q, R, S, Z change orientation but retain a recognizable shape. Digits 0, 1, 3, 8 are symmetric, whereas 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9 produce distinct mirrored forms. In examination questions, a string of mixed characters is often presented, and the candidate must select the correct mirrored string from four options. By scanning the options for the symmetric characters first, one can eliminate choices that misplace those invariant symbols, narrowing the field rapidly.

2. Water Images

2.1 Vertical Inversion and Its Effect on Shapes

A water image is produced by reflecting a figure across a horizontal axis, as if the figure were placed above a still water surface. The transformation flips the figure upside down: the topmost part becomes the bottommost, while the left‑right order remains unchanged. This vertical inversion is mathematically equivalent to a 180‑degree rotation followed by a lateral mirror, but the simpler mental model is to imagine the figure sinking into water and observing its upside‑down replica. Shapes with horizontal symmetry — such as a rectangle, an isosceles triangle with its base horizontal, or the letter H — appear identical in a water image, whereas asymmetric figures like the letter L or a right‑angled triangle change markedly. The key to solving water‑image items is to track the vertical coordinate of each component while ignoring horizontal shifts.

2.2 Distinguishing Water Images from Mirror Images

Although both mirror and water images involve reflection, the axis differs: mirror images use a vertical axis, water images a horizontal axis. Consequently, a figure that is symmetric about a vertical line will look the same in a mirror but may change in water, and vice‑versa. In test items the two concepts are sometimes combined; a question may ask for the water image of a figure that has already been mirrored, requiring two successive reflections. The order of operations matters: reflecting first vertically then horizontally yields a 180‑degree rotation, which is not the same as a single horizontal reflection of the original. Recognising whether the problem asks for a single water image or a composite transformation prevents the common mistake of applying the wrong axis.

2.3 Typical Figures Used in Water Image Questions

Examiners frequently employ simple geometric shapes — squares, circles, triangles, arrows — and alphanumeric characters because their water images are easy to verify. Composite figures such as a house outline (a square topped by a triangle) or a stick‑figure person are also common; the roof becomes a base and the feet become the head in the water image. When a figure contains internal details — a dot in the upper left quadrant of a square, for example — that detail relocates to the lower left quadrant after vertical inversion. Practising with a variety of such figures trains the eye to spot the vertical flip instantly, which is crucial under time pressure.

Example 1: Mirror Image of a Composite Figure
A figure consists of a capital letter F placed to the left of a right‑pointing arrow. A vertical mirror is placed on the right side of the figure. Which of the four options shows the correct mirror image?
First, note the mirror is on the right, so the original rightmost element (the arrow) will appear leftmost in the reflection. The arrow points right in the original; after lateral inversion it points left. The letter F, originally on the left, moves to the right side of the reflected figure. The letter F is not laterally symmetric, so its horizontal strokes reverse direction: the top horizontal bar now extends to the left, the middle bar to the left, and the vertical stem remains vertical. Among the options, only the one showing a left‑pointing arrow on the left and a mirrored F on the right matches this description. Therefore the correct option is the one with the arrow pointing left and the F reversed horizontally.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a mirror‑image question includes both letters and arrows, treat the arrow as a directional symbol: its head always flips to the opposite side. Sketch a quick mental arrow reversal before scanning the options; this eliminates at least two distractors instantly.

3. Embedded Figures

3.1 Concept of Figure Containment

Embedded‑figure questions present a complex host figure and ask the candidate to locate a smaller target figure hidden within it, either in its original orientation or after rotation. The host figure often contains many overlapping lines, curves, and shapes designed to camouflage the target. The underlying principle is that the target’s constituent lines must appear exactly, without addition or deletion, somewhere inside the host. This requires the test‑taker to mentally isolate the target’s outline and scan the host for a matching sub‑pattern. Because the host may be cluttered, the ability to filter out irrelevant detail is as important as recognising the target itself.

3.2 Strategies for Locating the Hidden Figure

A systematic approach begins with identifying unique features of the target — a right angle, a distinctive curve, a specific proportion — and then searching the host for those features. Rotating the target mentally in 90‑degree increments can reveal a match that is not apparent in the original orientation. Another effective technique is to trace the target on a transparent sheet (or imagine a transparent overlay) and slide it over the host; the moment the lines coincide, the location is found. In paper‑based examinations, candidates can use the corner of the answer sheet as a makeshift straight edge to compare angles quickly. Practising with increasingly complex hosts builds the visual‑search speed needed for the timed test.

3.3 Variations Involving Rotation and Overlap

Advanced embedded‑figure items may allow the target to appear rotated by any multiple of 90 degrees, or even reflected, though reflection is less common. Overlap occurs when parts of the target share lines with other elements of the host, making the boundary ambiguous. In such cases, the candidate must verify that every line segment of the target is accounted for in the host, even if some segments serve double duty. A useful check is to count the number of line segments in the target and ensure the host contains at least that many distinct segments in the candidate region. This quantitative verification reduces the chance of false positives caused by coincidental partial matches.

4. Paper Folding

4.1 Understanding the Fold Line and Symmetry

Paper‑folding questions depict a square sheet with a fold line (usually vertical, horizontal, or diagonal) and a pattern of cuts or punches made on the folded sheet. The fold creates a line of symmetry: whatever is cut on one side of the fold is mirrored onto the other side when the paper is unfolded. The first step is to identify the fold line and determine whether the sheet is folded once, twice, or more. A single fold produces a single mirror line; a second fold adds another mirror line, and the resulting pattern reflects across both axes. Recognising the symmetry axes allows the candidate to reconstruct the unfolded pattern by reflecting the visible cuts across each fold line in reverse order of folding.

4.2 Predicting the Unfolded Pattern

To predict the final unfolded figure, start from the last fold shown in the diagram and work backwards. For each fold, imagine unfolding the paper: the cuts on the folded layer appear on both sides of the fold line, mirrored. If a cut lies exactly on the fold line, it remains a single line after unfolding because it coincides with its own mirror. When multiple folds are present, the number of replicated cuts grows exponentially: a single punch after two perpendicular folds yields four identical holes arranged symmetrically. Drawing a quick schematic on scratch paper, marking the fold lines and replicating the cuts step by step, is a reliable method to avoid mental overload.

4.3 Multiple Folds and Cumulative Effect

When a sheet is folded more than twice, the pattern of symmetry becomes a grid of mirror lines. For example, folding a square in half vertically, then horizontally, then diagonally creates eight triangular sectors, each a mirror of the others. A single cut in one sector will appear in all eight sectors after complete unfolding. The cumulative effect can be calculated by multiplying the number of layers created by each fold: two folds give four layers, three folds give eight layers, and so on. Understanding this exponential growth helps the candidate estimate the complexity of the final figure and eliminate options that show too few or too many replicated elements.

5. Figure Series

5.1 Detecting the Governing Rule

Figure‑series questions present a sequence of four or five figures and ask for the next figure. The governing rule may involve rotation, reflection, addition or removal of elements, shading changes, or a combination thereof. The first step is to compare consecutive figures systematically: note the direction and magnitude of rotation (e.g., 90° clockwise), observe whether a line segment is added at a specific corner, check if shading alternates between solid and hollow, and record any change in the number of elements. Because the rule is consistent across the entire series, identifying it from the first two transitions usually suffices; the third transition serves as confirmation.

5.2 Types of Transformations in Series

Common transformation families include pure rotation (the whole figure rotates by a fixed angle each step), cyclic movement of a sub‑element (a dot moves around the perimeter), progressive addition (one extra line appears each step), and alternation (a shape toggles between two states). Some series combine two independent cycles, such as a rotating arrow whose head alternates between filled and empty. Recognising the category narrows the answer choices dramatically: if the series is a pure rotation, the correct option must be the figure rotated by the same angle from the last given figure. If the series involves addition, the correct option must contain exactly one more element than the last figure, placed according to the established pattern.

5.3 Approaching Complex Multi‑Step Series

In multi‑step series the rule may change after a certain number of figures, or two rules may operate simultaneously on different parts of the figure. The safe strategy is to break the figure into independent components — for example, a rotating triangle and a stationary square — and track each component separately. Write down the state of each component for every given figure; the pattern for each component often becomes obvious when isolated. Once the individual patterns are known, reconstruct the next figure by applying each component’s rule. This decomposition prevents the confusion that arises from trying to perceive the whole figure as a single transforming entity.

Mastery of these five non‑verbal reasoning topics comes from deliberate practice with varied figures, timed drills, and a habit of verbalising the transformation rule before selecting an answer.

Composite Mirror‑Water Transformations

When a figure is subjected to a mirror reflection followed by a water reflection (or the reverse order), the net effect is equivalent to a 180° rotation about the point where the two axes intersect. The vertical mirror axis flips left‑right; the horizontal water axis flips top‑bottom. Applying both inversions restores the original orientation of any element that possesses both vertical and horizontal symmetry, while elements with only one symmetry axis appear rotated. In examination items the two operations are rarely shown sequentially; instead a single composite figure is presented and the candidate must decide which of the four answer choices matches the result of the combined transformation. The key is to locate the intersection of the two axes — usually the centre of the bounding box — and then rotate the entire configuration by 180° mentally. If the original figure contains asymmetric sub‑elements (e.g., an arrow pointing north‑east), the composite result will show the arrow pointing south‑west. A quick verification is to pick a distinctive corner of the figure, trace its path through the two reflections, and confirm that the final corner matches the candidate option.

Identifying the Composite Axis

The composite axis is not a physical line but the point of intersection of the vertical and horizontal axes. In most test figures the axes coincide with the centre of the square or rectangular frame. When the frame is not centred — for instance a figure placed nearer the top edge — the water axis remains horizontal through the centre of the frame, while the mirror axis stays vertical through the centre. The intersection therefore stays at the geometric centre of the frame regardless of figure placement. Candidates should always locate the frame centre first, then treat the composite transformation as a half‑turn about that centre.

Effect on Alphanumeric Characters

Letters and digits behave predictably under the composite transformation. Characters with 180° rotational symmetry (H, I, N, O, S, X, Z, 0, 8) remain unchanged. Characters with only vertical symmetry (A, M, T, U, V, W, Y) become their water‑image counterparts (∀, W, ⊥, ∩, Λ, M, ʎ) after the composite operation. Characters with only horizontal symmetry (B, C, D, E, K) become their mirror‑image counterparts. All other characters (F, G, J, L, P, Q, R, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9) produce a distinct rotated glyph that is neither the pure mirror nor the pure water image. Memorising the three symmetry classes reduces the decision time for alphanumeric composite questions to a few seconds.

Embedded Figures with Rotational Variants

Standard embedded‑figure questions ask whether a simple shape appears inside a complex figure without rotation. Advanced variants allow the hidden shape to be rotated by 90°, 180°, or 270° before embedding. The examiner may also flip the hidden shape (mirror) before insertion, effectively combining the embedded‑figure concept with mirror‑image reasoning. The most reliable approach is to extract the contour of the hidden shape, generate its four rotational states (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°) and, if required, the mirrored versions of each state. Then scan the complex figure for a region that matches any of these generated templates. Because the complex figure often contains many distracting lines, it helps to focus on unique features of the hidden shape — such as a right‑angle corner with a protruding line, or a specific curvature — and look for that feature in the complex figure first. Once a candidate region is located, verify the complete match by tracing the outline.

Systematic Template Generation

On scratch paper draw the hidden shape in a 3 × 3 grid. Rotate the grid clockwise three times, copying the shape each time. If mirror variants are permitted, reflect each of the four rotations across a vertical axis. This yields up to eight distinct templates. Label them R0, R90, R180, R270, M0, M90, M180, M270. The number of templates is small enough to keep the mental load low, yet comprehensive enough to cover every allowed transformation. When the question stem explicitly states “the figure may be rotated but not reflected”, discard the mirrored set.

Handling Overlapping Elements

In many advanced items the hidden shape shares lines with the complex figure’s background pattern. The overlapping lines are not duplicated; they serve as common edges. During verification, treat a shared line as satisfied for both the template and the complex figure. A common trap is to reject a correct match because a line appears “thicker” in the complex figure — line weight is irrelevant unless the question explicitly mentions thickness as a distinguishing attribute.

Technique
A hidden shape consists of a right‑angled triangle with a small circle at the midpoint of its hypotenuse. The complex figure shows a dense network of intersecting lines and several circles. The hidden shape may be rotated but not reflected. Identify the correct location.
Generate the four rotations of the triangle‑circle template. Scan the complex figure for a right‑angled triangle whose hypotenuse midpoint coincides with a circle. The only region where a triangle of the same proportions appears with a circle exactly at the hypotenuse midpoint is the lower‑left quadrant, oriented with the right angle at the bottom‑left (R0). No other quadrant contains a circle at the required midpoint. Therefore the answer is the option highlighting that quadrant.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When embedded‑figure questions allow rotation, draw the four rotations on the rough sheet before scanning the complex figure. This front‑loads the mental effort and turns the search into a simple pattern‑matching task, saving valuable seconds under time pressure.

Paper Folding with Asymmetric Cuts and Partial Folds

Standard paper‑folding problems assume the sheet is folded exactly in half along a straight line and the cut is made through all layers. Advanced items introduce two complications: (1) the fold line may be offset from the centre, producing layers of unequal size; (2) the cut may be made after only some of the folds have been executed, i.e., a partial fold sequence. In an offset fold, the mirror axis does not pass through the geometric centre of the original sheet. The unfolded pattern therefore shows bilateral symmetry about the offset axis, but the two halves are not congruent in size. The larger half contains the original cut plus its mirror; the smaller half contains only the mirrored copy of the portion that lay on the folded side. To solve, draw the original rectangle, mark the offset fold line, shade the folded‑over region, replicate the cut on the shaded region, then unfold mentally. The resulting figure will have a cut on the larger side that does not have a counterpart on the smaller side.

Partial Fold Sequences

A partial fold sequence might describe: “Fold the sheet vertically in half. Make a triangular cut near the top edge. Unfold. Then fold the sheet horizontally in half and make a second cut near the right edge.” The first cut is replicated only by the vertical fold; the second cut is replicated only by the horizontal fold. The final unfolded sheet therefore shows four cuts: the original vertical‑fold pair (two triangles symmetric about the vertical axis) and the horizontal‑fold pair (two cuts symmetric about the horizontal axis). The two pairs are independent; they do not combine to produce a four‑fold replication because the folds were not simultaneous. The correct answer will display exactly two cuts mirrored vertically and two cuts mirrored horizontally, with no additional cuts at the diagonal positions.

Counting Layers for Irregular Folds

When folds are not perpendicular or not through the centre, the number of layers at any point equals the number of times the paper overlaps there. A practical method: after each fold, trace the outline of the folded stack on scratch paper. The stack outline shows the region where layers exist. A cut made at that stage will be replicated exactly as many times as there are layers under the cut. For example, a diagonal fold of a square creates a triangular stack of two layers over half the square and a single layer over the other half. A punch in the double‑layer region yields two holes after unfolding; a punch in the single‑layer region yields one hole. This layer‑counting technique eliminates guesswork for irregular fold geometries.

Figure Series Involving Dual Independent Cycles

Many series questions are built on a single transformation rule applied to the whole figure. Advanced series embed two or more independent cycles that run concurrently on different sub‑elements. For instance, a large square may rotate 90° clockwise each step while a small dot inside the square moves along the perimeter in a 45° clockwise jump each step. The two cycles have different periods (four steps for the square, eight steps for the dot). The overall series repeats only after the least common multiple of the periods (eight steps). To decode, isolate each sub‑element: track the square’s orientation across the given frames, then track the dot’s position. Write the state of each element in a table. Once the individual rules are clear, project both forward one step and combine them to form the next figure. This decomposition prevents the common error of forcing a single rule onto the whole figure.

Detecting Independence

Independence is signalled when a change in one element does not correlate with any change in another element across consecutive frames. If the square rotates but the dot stays stationary for two frames, then the dot moves while the square pauses, the cycles are out of phase. Another clue is the presence of elements that never interact — e.g., a background shading that alternates every step while a foreground shape rotates every second step. Treat each visual layer as a separate series.

Phase Alignment and LCM Projection

After determining the period of each cycle (p₁, p₂, …), compute the least common multiple (LCM) to know the full series length. In exam conditions you rarely need the full LCM; projecting one step ahead only requires the next state of each cycle. However, if the question asks for the figure after *n* steps (e.g., “which figure appears at position 10?”), use modular arithmetic: next state = current state advanced by (n mod pᵢ) steps for each cycle i. This technique turns a seemingly complex multi‑step prediction into a set of simple modular calculations.

Strategic Elimination Techniques for Non‑Verbal Reasoning

Even with perfect conceptual knowledge, time pressure forces candidates to eliminate options quickly. Three high‑yield elimination heuristics apply across mirror, water, embedded, folding, and series types. First, symmetry audit: count the number of symmetry axes in each option. The correct answer must possess the same symmetry order as the transformation predicts. If the transformation yields a figure with only vertical symmetry, any option showing horizontal or diagonal symmetry can be discarded instantly. Second, element‑count parity: transformations such as paper folding double the number of cuts; rotations preserve element count; additions increase count by a fixed integer. Compute the expected count and reject options with mismatched totals. Third, anchor‑feature tracking: identify a unique, non‑repeating feature in the question figure (a lone circle, a specific angle, a shaded quadrant). Follow that feature through the transformation mentally; the correct option must display the feature in the predicted location and orientation. Options where the anchor is missing, duplicated, or misplaced are eliminated without full figure analysis.

Applying Heuristics in Combination

Use the heuristics sequentially. Start with symmetry audit — it is the fastest visual check. If more than one option survives, apply element‑count parity. Usually only one option remains. As a final safeguard, verify with anchor‑feature tracking. This three‑stage funnel reduces the average decision time from 45 seconds to under 15 seconds per question, leaving ample buffer for the hardest items.

Practice Drill for Heuristic Speed

Create a timed drill: 20 mixed non‑verbal questions, 5 minutes total. For each question, write down the heuristic used (S for symmetry, C for count, A for anchor) before marking the answer. Review the log to see which heuristic most often leads to the correct elimination. Over several sessions the pattern of heuristic effectiveness becomes personal knowledge, allowing you to prioritize the strongest heuristic for your own cognitive style.

  • Assuming a composite mirror‑water transformation is a simple mirror or water image instead of a 180° rotation.
  • Forgetting to generate mirrored rotations when the embedded figure may be reflected as well as rotated.
  • Treating an offset fold as a central fold, leading to an incorrectly symmetric unfolded pattern.
  • Merging independent cycles in a figure series into a single rule and missing the phase offset.
  • Relying on a single elimination heuristic (e.g., only symmetry) and overlooking count or anchor mismatches.
  • Misreading the fold sequence order and applying cuts to the wrong set of layers.
  • Neglecting to verify that a hidden shape’s unique anchor feature appears exactly once in the complex figure.
Mastering these advanced mechanisms equips you to handle the most deceptive non‑verbal reasoning items with confidence and precision.
