Mirror Images and Water Images

1. Mirror Image Fundamentals

A mirror image is produced when an object is reflected across a vertical line, so that the left side of the original becomes the right side of the reflection and vice versa. The transformation preserves the vertical coordinates of every point while inverting the horizontal coordinate relative to the mirror line. Because the mirror line is usually taken as the vertical axis of the figure, any element that lies on the axis remains unchanged, while elements off the axis swap sides. This property makes it possible to predict the appearance of a reflected figure by mentally flipping the original horizontally, a skill that is tested frequently in non‑verbal reasoning sections.

When solving mirror‑image questions, the candidate should first identify the axis of reflection, which is often indicated by a dotted line or implied by the wording “mirror image”. Next, each distinct component of the figure — such as strokes, curves, or shaded regions — is examined for its distance from the axis. The component’s mirror counterpart will appear at an equal distance on the opposite side, maintaining the same orientation relative to the axis. Practising this step‑by‑step mapping builds the spatial intuition needed to answer quickly under time pressure.

2. Water Image Principles

A water image, by contrast, results from reflection across a horizontal line, as if the figure were placed on the surface of still water. The vertical coordinates of points are inverted while horizontal coordinates stay the same. Consequently, the top of the original becomes the bottom of the water image, and any element lying on the horizontal axis remains fixed. This vertical flip is the essential difference from a mirror image, and recognising it prevents the common error of applying a horizontal flip when a water image is required.

In water‑image problems the axis of reflection is usually a horizontal line drawn through the centre of the figure or implied by the phrase “water image”. The solver should trace each feature of the figure to its mirrored position below the axis, preserving left‑right orientation but reversing up‑down orientation. Because the human visual system is more accustomed to horizontal flips, deliberate practice with vertical flips improves accuracy and reduces the tendency to confuse the two types of reflection.

3. Comparative Analysis of Mirror and Water Images

Understanding the distinction between mirror and water images is crucial because examination questions often present both types in the same set, requiring the candidate to switch mental operations rapidly. A mirror image changes laterality but preserves vertical ordering, whereas a water image changes vertical ordering but preserves laterality. When a figure contains asymmetrical elements such as letters, numbers, or directional arrows, the effect of each reflection becomes immediately visible, providing a reliable check on the chosen transformation.

One effective strategy is to sketch a quick coordinate grid on the rough sheet, mark the axis, and plot a few key points of the figure. Applying the appropriate sign change to the coordinates (x → –x for mirror, y → –y for water) yields the reflected positions without ambiguity. This analytical approach complements the visual method and is especially useful for complex figures where mental rotation alone may be error‑prone.

Embedded Figures

4. Definition and Identification of Embedded Figures

An embedded figure question presents a complex host figure and asks the examinee to locate a simpler target figure hidden within it, either in its original orientation or after a rotation. The target figure is always a subset of the lines, shapes, or shaded areas that compose the host figure; no extra lines are introduced. Recognising that the embedding is exact — every line of the target must coincide with a line of the host — allows the solver to discard options that contain even a single mismatched segment.

The first step in solving such questions is to isolate the target figure and note its distinctive features: number of sides, angles, symmetry, and any unique markings such as dots or shading. Then the host figure is scanned systematically, often by dividing it into quadrants, to find a region where those features align. Because the host figure may contain many overlapping shapes, a methodical scan reduces the chance of overlooking the correct location.

5. Strategies for Locating Hidden Shapes

One reliable technique is to trace the outline of the target figure on a transparent sheet or mentally overlay it on the host figure, sliding it across the host until all edges match. In a paper‑based test, the candidate can use the corner of the answer sheet as a makeshift straight edge to compare angles. Another approach is to look for invariant properties such as the number of right angles or the presence of a unique curve that appears only once in the host figure; these act as anchors that narrow the search space dramatically.

When the target figure may be rotated, it is helpful to generate the four possible orientations (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°) mentally or by sketching them quickly. The host figure is then examined for each orientation in turn. Because rotation preserves side lengths and angles, the matching process remains the same; only the orientation of the anchor features changes. Practising this rotation‑aware search builds speed and confidence.

6. Common Variations in Embedded Figure Questions

Examinations often introduce variations such as multiple target figures, where the candidate must select the host figure that contains all given targets, or a “find the odd one out” format where four host figures are shown and only three contain the target. In the former, the solver checks each target sequentially, eliminating host figures that fail any single target. In the latter, the focus shifts to identifying the host figure that lacks a specific feature present in the other three.

Another variation involves mirrored or water‑image embeddings, where the target appears reflected within the host. The same principles apply, but the solver must first apply the appropriate reflection to the target before searching. Recognising the reflection cue early — such as a dotted line labelled “mirror” — prevents wasted effort searching for the unreflected shape.

Example Question
A host figure consists of a large square subdivided by two intersecting diagonals and a horizontal line through the centre. The target figure is a right‑angled isosceles triangle with its hypotenuse along one diagonal and its legs along the horizontal line and the other diagonal. Identify the quadrant of the host figure that contains the target figure in its original orientation.
The diagonals divide the square into four congruent right‑angled isosceles triangles. The horizontal line bisects the square, passing through the centre and parallel to the base. The target triangle has its hypotenuse on a diagonal, one leg on the horizontal line, and the other leg on the other diagonal. Only the lower‑left quadrant satisfies all three conditions: its hypotenuse lies on the descending diagonal, its vertical leg lies on the horizontal line, and its horizontal leg lies on the ascending diagonal. Therefore the target figure is embedded in the lower‑left quadrant.
Exam Insight
When a host figure contains symmetrical subdivisions, label each region mentally (e.g., upper‑left, upper‑right, lower‑left, lower‑right) before searching; this prevents re‑checking the same region and saves valuable seconds.

Paper Folding

7. Mechanics of Paper Folding and Unfolding

Paper folding questions depict a sequence of folds applied to a transparent sheet, often with a punch or cut made after the final fold. The task is to determine the pattern of holes or cuts that will appear when the sheet is completely unfolded. Each fold doubles the number of layers in the folded region, so a single punch creates multiple holes symmetrically placed about the fold lines. Understanding that every fold acts as a line of symmetry for the subsequent layers is the key to visualising the final unfolded pattern.

The direction of the fold — whether the paper is folded upward, downward, leftward, or rightward — determines which side of the fold line receives the additional layers. However, because the paper is transparent in the diagram, the final unfolded pattern is independent of the fold direction; only the position of the fold lines relative to the punch matters. This invariance simplifies the mental simulation: one can treat each fold as a mirror line that replicates the punch across it.

8. Pattern Recognition in Folded Figures

When several folds are applied successively, the pattern of holes grows exponentially. After the first fold, a single punch yields two holes; after the second fold, four holes; after the third, eight holes, and so on. The holes appear in a regular grid‑like arrangement whose spacing corresponds to the distances between successive fold lines. Recognising this geometric progression allows the solver to predict the number and relative positions of holes without drawing every intermediate step.

A useful heuristic is to work backwards from the final folded state. Starting with the punched hole in the folded diagram, reflect it across the last fold line to obtain the positions before that fold. Repeat the reflection for each preceding fold line in reverse order. This reverse‑engineering method is often faster than forward simulation because it avoids tracking multiple layers simultaneously.

9. Stepwise Approach to Solve Paper Folding Problems

Begin by identifying all fold lines in the order they are applied. Mark the punch location on the final folded figure. Then, for each fold line from last to first, replicate the existing hole pattern across that line, preserving distances. After processing all folds, the resulting set of points represents the holes on the fully unfolded sheet. Finally, compare this derived pattern with the answer options, looking for exact matches in both number and arrangement of holes.

During the comparison, pay attention to the orientation of the pattern relative to the original sheet. Some answer choices may show the correct holes but rotated or reflected; these are distractors. The correct answer must match the derived pattern in the original orientation of the sheet as shown in the question stem. Practising this stepwise method with a variety of fold sequences builds the spatial fluency required for high‑speed solving.

Figure Series

10. Types of Figure Series Patterns

Figure series questions present a sequence of frames, each containing a geometric figure that changes according to one or more logical rules. The most common pattern types include rotation (clockwise or anticlockwise by a fixed angle), addition or removal of elements (such as dots, lines, or shading), alternation between two or more distinct states, and progression of a parameter like size, number of sides, or orientation. Recognising the category of change is the first step toward extrapolating the next frame.

In many series, multiple rules operate simultaneously. For example, a figure may rotate 45° clockwise while gaining one extra dot in each step. The solver must decompose the overall transformation into its constituent elementary operations. This decomposition is facilitated by comparing consecutive frames pairwise and listing every observable difference. Once the list is complete, the solver checks whether each difference follows a consistent arithmetic or geometric progression.

11. Logical Rules Governing Series Progression

Rotation rules are typically uniform: the figure rotates by a constant angle (e.g., 90°, 45°, 30°) in a fixed direction. If the direction alternates, the series will show a back‑and‑forth motion that can be detected by observing the orientation of a distinctive marker such as an arrow or an asymmetric shape. Addition or removal rules often follow simple arithmetic sequences: +1, +2, +3 … or –1, –2, –3 … . Alternation rules produce a repeating cycle of two or three distinct figures; the period of the cycle can be found by identifying the first recurrence of a frame.

Size progression may involve scaling by a constant factor (e.g., each figure is 1.5 times larger than the previous) or by a constant increment in linear dimension. Because the figures are drawn on a discrete grid, exact scaling is rarely used; instead, the number of unit squares or the length of a side increases by a fixed integer. Shading patterns may shift, invert, or cycle through a set of hatch styles. The solver should catalogue the shading style in each frame and look for a repeating order.

12. Techniques for Extending a Given Series

After identifying the governing rules, the next frame is constructed by applying each rule once more to the last given frame. It is advisable to sketch the predicted frame lightly on the rough sheet before scanning the answer choices. This prevents the common pitfall of being swayed by a distractor that matches only a subset of the rules. When multiple answer options appear plausible, re‑examine the series for any subtle rule that was missed, such as a change in the direction of rotation after a certain number of steps.

Time management in figure series is enhanced by a two‑pass strategy. In the first pass, quickly eliminate options that violate any obvious rule (e.g., wrong rotation direction, incorrect number of added elements). In the second pass, apply the full rule set to the remaining candidates. Because the number of options is usually four or five, this approach reduces the cognitive load and increases accuracy under the strict time limits of the examination.

Integrated Problem Solving Strategies

13. Combining Multiple Non‑Verbal Concepts in a Single Question

Advanced questions often blend two or more non‑verbal reasoning types, such as a figure series where each term is a paper‑folding pattern, or an embedded‑figure task that requires a mirror‑image transformation before searching. The solver must first segment the problem into its constituent sub‑tasks, solve each sub‑task using the appropriate method, and then synthesize the results. For instance, if a series shows a folded sheet with a punch in each frame, the candidate should unfold each frame mentally, obtain the hole pattern, and then treat the sequence of unfolded patterns as a standard figure series.

Developing the habit of annotating the question diagram with symbols for folds, mirror lines, or rotation arrows helps keep track of the multiple transformations. A systematic notation — such as “F1” for first fold, “M” for mirror, “R90” for 90° rotation — turns a visually complex problem into a sequence of algebraic operations that can be executed step by step. This analytical layer reduces reliance on pure visual memory, which can falter under exam pressure.

14. Time Management and Accuracy Enhancement

Non‑verbal reasoning sections typically allocate a fixed number of minutes per question, so pacing is essential. A practical guideline is to spend no more than 30 seconds on the initial scan of a question, 60 seconds on the core solving process, and 15 seconds on verifying the answer against the options. If a question resists solution after 90 seconds, it is advisable to mark it for review and move on, returning only if time permits. This prevents a single difficult item from consuming the budget needed for several easier ones.

Accuracy is improved by a brief verification step: after selecting an answer, mentally replay the transformation in reverse to see if the original figure is recovered. For mirror and water images, reflect the chosen answer across the same axis; for paper folding, refold the unfolded pattern; for figure series, check that the chosen next term continues every identified rule. This reverse check catches many careless errors without requiring a full re‑solution.

15. Practice Routine for Sustained Improvement

Consistent practice with a varied question bank builds the pattern‑recognition database that the brain draws upon during the test. A balanced routine includes daily timed drills of 20 questions covering all five topics, followed by an untimed review of every incorrect or guessed item. During review, the solver writes a one‑sentence explanation of the rule that was missed, creating a personal error log. Over weeks, the error log reveals recurring weaknesses — such as confusion between mirror and water images or difficulty with multi‑fold paper folding — allowing targeted remediation.

Supplementing drills with spatial‑visualisation exercises, such as mentally rotating three‑dimensional objects or folding paper physically, strengthens the underlying cognitive machinery. Because the examination tests the same spatial abilities across different question formats, improvement in one area often transfers to others. Maintaining this disciplined practice schedule up to the examination date ensures that the candidate enters the test with both speed and confidence. The integrated approach described here equips aspirants to tackle any non‑verbal reasoning challenge systematically and accurately.

Mirror Image of Alphanumeric and Symbolic Sequences

When a mirror is placed vertically to the right of a string, each character undergoes lateral inversion. Capital letters such as A, H, I, M, O, T, U, V, W, X, Y retain their shape because they possess a vertical axis of symmetry; letters like B, C, D, E, K become unrecognisable unless the test provides a stylised mirror‑font where the reversed glyph is predefined. Numerals 0, 1, 8 are vertically symmetrical, while 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9 change into distinct mirror forms that must be memorised. Symbols such as +, ×, =, ÷, ≤, ≥ behave predictably: the plus sign and multiplication cross are invariant, the equality sign remains two parallel lines, but the division sign (÷) remains unchanged under vertical reflection. In competitive practice, the examiner often mixes alphanumeric strings with geometric shapes, requiring the candidate to treat each token independently and then reassemble the sequence in the correct left‑to‑right order after inversion. A reliable technique is to write the original string on a rough sheet, draw a vertical line, and sketch the mirror counterpart of each token beneath it, preserving inter‑token spacing. This manual mapping prevents the common error of reversing the entire string as a block, which would also reverse the order of characters — an error the mirror does not produce.

Horizontal mirrors (placed below the figure) generate water‑image type inversions for alphanumerics, but the standard “mirror image” question in most syllabi assumes a vertical mirror. When a question explicitly states “mirror placed on the left side”, the inversion direction reverses: the rightmost character becomes the leftmost in the reflected view, yet each character’s internal geometry is still laterally flipped. Candidates should note that the order reversal only occurs when the mirror is on the left; a mirror on the right also reverses the left‑to‑right order while flipping each glyph. Practising both orientations with a timer builds the mental flexibility needed to switch conventions instantly.

Special cases arise with ligatured scripts or stylised fonts where a character’s mirror image is not a standard glyph. In such items the answer options usually contain the exact mirrored font, so the solver must compare shapes rather than rely on memorised alphabet tables. A quick check is to identify any character that is asymmetrical (e.g., J, L, P, Q, R, S, Z) and verify that its mirrored counterpart appears in the option set. If an option shows a symmetrical version of an asymmetrical character, it can be discarded immediately.

Finally, the integration of alphanumeric mirror images with geometric figures tests the ability to maintain two independent transformation streams simultaneously. The recommended workflow: isolate the alphanumeric segment, apply the mirror rule, isolate the geometric segment, apply the appropriate geometric mirror rule (vertical axis for shapes), then merge the two transformed streams preserving their relative positions. This segmented approach reduces cognitive load and mirrors the stepwise notation advocated for combined non‑verbal problems.

Water Image Transformations with Oblique Axes

A water image is traditionally defined as a reflection across a horizontal axis placed immediately below the figure, producing a vertical inversion. Advanced questions extend this concept by positioning the reflecting line at an oblique angle — commonly 45° or 135° relative to the horizontal. In such cases each point (x, y) of the original figure maps to a new point (x', y') determined by the standard reflection formula across the line y = mx + c. For a 45° line passing through the origin (m = 1, c = 0), the transformation simplifies to swapping coordinates: (x, y) → (y, x). Consequently, a right‑angled triangle with vertices (0,0), (2,0), (0,1) becomes a triangle with vertices (0,0), (0,2), (1,0), effectively rotating the shape by 90° and mirroring it. Candidates who recognise this coordinate swap can solve the problem without drawing, simply by transposing the horizontal and vertical extents of each component.

When the oblique axis does not pass through the origin, a translation step is required: shift the figure so that the axis passes through the origin, apply the coordinate swap (or the appropriate reflection matrix for other angles), then translate back. In practice, exam figures are drawn on a grid, so the translation amounts to counting grid units. For a 135° axis (m = –1), the reflection maps (x, y) → (–y, –x) after centring. This produces a rotation of 90° in the opposite direction combined with a point inversion. Mastery of these two common oblique axes covers the vast majority of advanced water‑image items.

Shapes with internal asymmetry — such as a letter “F” or a scalene triangle — reveal the axis orientation unmistakably. Symmetrical shapes like circles, squares, or regular hexagons produce identical images for both horizontal and 45° reflections, making them poor discriminators. Therefore, examiners embed at least one clearly asymmetrical element in every oblique water‑image question. The solver should locate that element first, apply the coordinate transformation mentally, and then verify the remainder of the figure for consistency.

A practical shortcut for grid‑based figures is to trace the outline on tracing paper, fold the paper along the indicated oblique line, and observe the imprint. While physical folding is not allowed in the examination hall, rehearsing this technique during preparation trains the brain to visualise the fold line as a mirror. Over time, the mental fold becomes as rapid as the standard horizontal water‑image flip, allowing the candidate to allocate the same 30‑second scan budget to oblique variants.

Oblique Water Image Worked Example
A figure consists of a right‑angled triangle with vertices at grid points (1,1), (4,1), (1,3). The reflecting line is the 45° line passing through the origin. Which of the following sets of vertices represents the water image?
Reflect each vertex across y = x by swapping coordinates: (1,1) → (1,1); (4,1) → (1,4); (1,3) → (3,1). The transformed triangle has vertices (1,1), (1,4), (3,1). This matches option C.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Oblique Reflections
When the reflecting line is described as “45°” or “135°”, immediately write down the coordinate swap rule (x,y)→(y,x) or (x,y)→(–y,–x) on the rough sheet. Apply it to the most asymmetric vertex first; the resulting coordinate pair will usually appear in only one answer choice, letting you eliminate the other three within seconds.

Embedded Figures Under Rotation, Scaling, and Overlap

Standard embedded‑figure tasks present a simple target shape hidden within a larger complex diagram, both sharing the same orientation and scale. Advanced variants introduce three complications simultaneously: the hidden shape may be rotated by multiples of 90°, uniformly scaled (enlarged or reduced), and partially overlapped by other lines that obscure its edges. The solver must therefore treat the search as a pattern‑matching problem under the group of similarity transformations (rotation + uniform scaling) rather than a pure translation search.

A systematic method begins with extracting the invariant descriptors of the target: the sequence of interior angles, the ratio of side lengths, and the cyclic order of vertices. These descriptors are unchanged by rotation and uniform scaling. Next, scan the complex diagram for any sub‑figure that reproduces the same angle sequence. Because the diagram is usually drawn on a grid, the candidate can overlay a transparent mental grid and compare the relative vectors between successive vertices. If a match is found, verify the scale factor by measuring one side length in the target and the corresponding side in the candidate region; the factor must be constant for all sides.

Overlap adds noise: extra lines may intersect the hidden shape, creating spurious vertices. The rule is to ignore any vertex that does not conform to the target’s angle sequence. Practically, trace the target on tracing paper, then slide, rotate, and resize the tracing over the complex figure until the edges align with a subset of the diagram’s lines. The correct placement will make every target edge coincide with a line segment in the diagram, even if that segment continues beyond the target’s vertex. This mental tracing technique is faster than attempting to “see” the shape unaided.

Examiners often embed the target in a mirror‑reversed orientation as well, effectively adding a reflection to the transformation group. In such cases the angle sequence remains the same but the cyclic order reverses. The solver should therefore test both clockwise and anticlockwise traversals of the candidate region. A quick check: if the target has a distinctive concave vertex (internal angle > 180°), its mirror image will place that concavity on the opposite side. Spotting the concavity in the diagram instantly reveals whether a reflection is involved.

Multi‑Stage Paper Folding with Partial Unfolds

Classic paper‑folding questions involve a single fold followed by a punch, then a complete unfold. Advanced items chain multiple folds — sometimes alternating mountain and valley folds — and may ask for the pattern after only the first unfold, or after a selective unfold that leaves later folds intact. Each fold doubles the number of layers in the folded region; a punch through all layers creates a hole in every layer. When the paper is partially unfolded, the holes replicate according to the symmetry of the folds that have been released.

To solve, label each fold sequentially (F1, F2, …) and note its axis (vertical, horizontal, diagonal). Represent the paper as a coordinate grid; a fold reflects the coordinates of the yet‑unfolded portion across the fold line. A punch at coordinate (x, y) in the fully folded state corresponds to a set of coordinates in the original sheet obtained by applying the inverse reflections in reverse order. For a partial unfold after Fk, only the reflections of folds F(k+1) … Fn are applied; the earlier folds remain folded, so their symmetry is not yet expressed in the visible pattern.

A practical shortcut: draw the final folded shape on a small square, mark the punch, then unfold step by step on the rough sheet, copying the punch marks at each stage. After each unfold, compare the intermediate pattern with the answer choices that correspond to that stage. This stepwise drawing mirrors the “stepwise approach” taught for basic folding but extends it to multiple decision points. Candidates who practice this physical simulation develop an intuitive sense for how hole clusters migrate across the sheet.

Common pitfalls include forgetting that a valley fold mirrors the paper in the opposite sense to a mountain fold (the reflection axis is the same, but the side that moves differs). In a 2‑D representation both are simple reflections, so the distinction only matters when the problem asks which side of the paper faces up after a certain unfold. For pure hole‑pattern questions, mountain versus valley is irrelevant; only the sequence of reflection axes matters.

Figure Series Governed by Alternating and Conditional Rules

Elementary figure series follow a single recurring rule — rotation, addition, deletion, or shading change — applied uniformly at each step. Advanced series combine two or more rules that alternate, or rules that activate conditionally based on the step index (odd/even, prime/composite, etc.). For example, a series may rotate the figure 90° clockwise on odd steps and reflect it horizontally on even steps. Another may add a dot on steps that are multiples of three while deleting a line on all other steps.

The solver’s first task is to hypothesise the minimal rule set that explains every transition. Write the observed changes between consecutive frames in a table: Frame 1→2, 2→3, 3→4, … Look for periodicities in the change list. If the change list repeats every two steps, an alternating rule is likely. If the change list shows a pattern like “add, add, delete, add, add, delete”, a conditional rule based on step modulo 3 is indicated. Once the period is identified, project the next change by continuing the period.

Conditional rules often depend on a property of the figure itself, such as “if the number of shaded regions is even, rotate; otherwise reflect”. In such cases the solver must evaluate the property at each step before deciding the transformation. A reliable technique is to annotate each frame with the relevant property value (e.g., “shaded=4”) and then apply the conditional rule explicitly. This turns the visual problem into a small algorithm that can be executed on the rough sheet without mental overload.

When multiple alternating rules coexist (e.g., rotation alternates with scaling, while shading toggles every step), treat each attribute independently. Track rotation angle, scale factor, and shading state in separate columns. The next frame is assembled by updating each column according to its own schedule. This decomposition prevents the common error of conflating two independent cycles into a single spurious rule.

  • Assuming a single uniform rule when the series actually alternates between two distinct transformations.
  • Neglecting to check both clockwise and anticlockwise traversals for embedded figures that may be mirrored.
  • Applying the full unfold symmetry when the question asks for a partially unfolded stage.
  • Confusing mountain and valley folds as producing different hole patterns in 2‑D representations.
  • Reversing the order of alphanumeric characters when the mirror is on the right side.
  • Using the horizontal water‑image rule for an oblique axis without coordinate transformation.
  • Review these traps before each practice session.
