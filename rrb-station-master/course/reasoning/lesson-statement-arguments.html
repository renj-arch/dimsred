Statement, Assumption and Conclusion

Updated 20 Nov 2026
What follows, what is assumed, and what strengthens an argument.
Statement and conclusion · assumption · course of action · strengthening and weakening

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the fundamental concepts of Statement, Assumption and Conclusion
  • Apply key formulas and techniques to solve problems
  • Practice with exam-level questions to build speed and accuracy

1. Foundations of Logical Reasoning in Statements

1.1 The Nature of a Statement

A statement in logical reasoning is a declarative sentence that can be judged as true or false, and it serves as the raw material from which assumptions, conclusions, and courses of action are derived. Unlike a question or a command, a statement asserts a fact or a belief about the world, and its truth value is the anchor for every subsequent inference. In examinations, the statement is usually presented in a compact form, often containing a cause, an effect, a policy proposal, or a observation about a social phenomenon. Recognising the exact wording is crucial because a single qualifier such as “most”, “some”, or “always” changes the logical scope and determines which assumptions are justified and which conclusions follow.

1.2 Distinguishing Fact, Opinion, and Premise

A fact is a verifiable piece of information, an opinion reflects a personal judgement, and a premise is a proposition offered in support of a conclusion. In the context of these questions, the given statement may blend fact and opinion; the test‑taker must isolate the premise that the argument rests upon. For example, a statement that “the new railway line has reduced travel time by thirty percent” presents a measurable fact, whereas “the new railway line is a boon for the region” is an opinion that assumes the reduction in travel time is beneficial. The ability to separate these layers prevents the common error of treating an opinion as a proven premise.

1.3 The Logical Structure of Argumentation

Every argument implicit in a statement follows a structure of premise, assumption, and conclusion. The premise is the explicit information supplied; the assumption is the unstated bridge that makes the premise relevant to the conclusion; the conclusion is the claim that the argument seeks to establish. Understanding this triad allows a candidate to test each answer choice by asking whether it fills the gap (assumption), follows necessarily (conclusion), or proposes a practical step (course of action). The logical flow is directional: premise → assumption → conclusion, and any reversal signals a flawed option.

2. Understanding Assumptions

2.1 Definition and Role of an Assumption

An assumption is an unstated premise that must be true for the argument to hold. It is not directly asserted in the statement but is required to connect the given information to the intended inference. In testing, an assumption question asks which of the options, if false, would weaken the argument most severely. The correct assumption is therefore a necessary condition, not merely a plausible one. For instance, if a statement claims “increasing the number of ticket counters will reduce passenger waiting time”, the assumption that “the current waiting time is primarily caused by insufficient counters” is necessary; if waiting time is due to slow processing per passenger, adding counters would not help.

2.2 Types of Assumptions: Implicit vs. Explicit

Implicit assumptions are those the author takes for granted without mention, while explicit assumptions are occasionally hinted at through qualifiers such as “provided that” or “assuming”. In examination items, only implicit assumptions are tested because explicit ones are already visible. A useful technique is to negate each answer choice; if the negation destroys the logical link between statement and conclusion, the choice is the required assumption. This negation test works because a necessary assumption cannot be false without invalidating the argument.

2.3 Common Pitfalls in Identifying Assumptions

Students often confuse assumptions with inferences or with statements that merely strengthen the argument. An inference is a conclusion that can be drawn, whereas an assumption is a precondition. Strengthening statements add support but are not required for the argument’s validity. Another pitfall is selecting an assumption that is too broad; the correct assumption must be specific to the logical gap. For example, “the government has enough funds” is too general for a statement about a specific railway project; the needed assumption would be “the allocated budget for this project is sufficient”.

Example Question on Assumption
Statement: “If the railway authority introduces a mobile‑app based ticketing system, the number of passengers travelling without a ticket will decrease.” Which of the following is an assumption implicit in the statement?
1. Most passengers own a smartphone.
2. The mobile app will be free to download.
3. Ticket‑less travel is currently a major problem.
4. The railway authority has the technical capacity to develop the app.
The argument links the introduction of a mobile‑app ticketing system to a reduction in ticket‑less travel. For this link to hold, it must be assumed that the target passengers can actually use the app. Option 1 states that most passengers own a smartphone; if this were false, the app would be inaccessible to a large segment, and the predicted decrease would not occur. Option 2 (free download) is not necessary because a paid app could still be used. Option 3 describes the current situation but is not a logical bridge. Option 4 concerns the authority’s capacity, which is a practical feasibility issue, not a logical necessity for the claim. Therefore, the correct assumption is option 1.
Exam‑Day Insight
When faced with an assumption question, apply the negation test to each option mentally; the one whose negation makes the argument collapse is the answer. This method is faster than evaluating each option for plausibility.

3. Drawing Conclusions from Statements

3.1 What Constitutes a Valid Conclusion

A valid conclusion follows necessarily from the given statement together with any implicit assumptions. It must not introduce new information that is not logically entailed. In the examination, a conclusion question presents several statements and asks which of the provided conclusions can be drawn. The correct conclusion is the one that is logically compelled; any conclusion that merely could be true but is not forced is incorrect. For example, from “All trains on the northern route are electric” one can conclude “No diesel train operates on the northern route”, but one cannot conclude “Electric trains are faster” because speed is not mentioned.

3.2 Distinguishing Between Definite and Probable Conclusions

Definite conclusions are those that must be true in every possible scenario consistent with the premises. Probable conclusions are those that are likely but not guaranteed. Examination items typically require definite conclusions. Words such as “must”, “necessarily”, and “always” signal a demand for certainty, whereas “may”, “could”, and “likely” indicate probability. Recognising these qualifiers helps eliminate options that overreach the logical scope of the statement.

3.3 The Role of Quantifiers in Conclusions

Quantifiers such as “all”, “some”, “none”, and “most” shape the logical strength of a conclusion. A statement with “some” permits a conclusion only about the existence of at least one instance, not about the whole class. Misreading “some” as “all” is a frequent source of error. For instance, “Some stations have Wi‑Fi” allows the conclusion “There is at least one station with Wi‑Fi” but not “Most stations have Wi‑Fi”. Careful attention to quantifiers ensures that the conclusion matches the exact logical force of the premise.

4. Course of Action and Decision Making

4.1 Defining Course of Action

A course of action question presents a problematic situation described in a statement and asks which of the suggested actions would be appropriate, feasible, and effective. Unlike assumption or conclusion questions, these items test practical reasoning: the candidate must evaluate whether the action addresses the root cause, is implementable within the given constraints, and does not create adverse side effects. The correct course of action is usually the one that directly tackles the problem stated, rather than a peripheral or symptomatic measure.

4.2 Criteria for Evaluating Actions

Three criteria dominate the evaluation: relevance, feasibility, and sufficiency. Relevance means the action targets the specific issue raised; feasibility means it can be carried out with the resources and authority implied by the statement; sufficiency means it is likely to resolve or substantially mitigate the problem. An action that is relevant but not feasible (e.g., “build a new railway line overnight”) is rejected. An action that is feasible but irrelevant (e.g., “increase advertising for a train that is already full”) is also rejected.

4.3 Avoiding Over‑Generalised or Extreme Actions

Options that propose extreme measures such as “ban all private vehicles” or “shut down the entire network” are rarely correct because they ignore proportionality and practical constraints. Similarly, overly vague actions like “improve the system” lack specificity and cannot be judged for effectiveness. The ideal answer is concrete, proportionate, and directly linked to the problem described. For example, if the statement highlights “frequent signal failures cause delays”, a suitable course of action is “upgrade the signalling system and schedule regular maintenance”, not “replace all tracks”.

5. Strengthening and Weakening Arguments

5.1 The Logic of Strengthening

Strengthening an argument means providing additional information that makes the conclusion more likely to be true, without being a necessary assumption. A strengthening statement typically supplies evidence, eliminates alternative explanations, or confirms a causal link. In the test, the correct strengthening option will, if added to the premises, increase the probability of the conclusion. For instance, if the argument is “Introducing a mobile‑app ticketing system will reduce ticket‑less travel”, a strengthening fact could be “A pilot study in a comparable city showed a 40 % drop in ticket‑less travel after app launch”. This empirical evidence bolsters the claim.

5.2 The Logic of Weakening

Weakening an argument involves presenting information that casts doubt on the conclusion, either by attacking the causal link, introducing a plausible alternative cause, or showing that a key assumption is false. The correct weakening option, if true, would make the conclusion less credible. Using the same example, a weakening statement could be “Most passengers in the region use basic phones without internet access”, which undermines the assumption that the app will be widely usable.

5.3 Distinguishing Strengthening from Assumption

While both strengthening statements and assumptions support the argument, an assumption is indispensable; the argument collapses without it. A strengthening statement is supplementary; the argument can still stand, albeit less convincingly, without it. In practice, if negating an option destroys the argument, it is an assumption; if negating it merely reduces confidence, it is a strengthening statement. This distinction is critical because examination questions explicitly ask for one or the other, and confusing them leads to selecting the wrong option.

Complex Argument Structures: Multiple Premises and Sub‑Conclusions

In many examination passages the argument is not a simple two‑premise syllogism but a layered structure where several premises converge on an intermediate claim, which then serves as a premise for the final conclusion. Recognising these sub‑conclusions is essential because a statement that looks like a conclusion may in fact be a stepping‑stone, and the true conclusion may be implicit or appear later in the text. A typical pattern is: Premise A → Sub‑conclusion X; Premise B → Sub‑conclusion Y; X and Y together → Main conclusion Z. The test maker often asks “Which of the following can be concluded?” and the correct answer is Z, while X and Y are distractors that are technically true but not the final inference. To dissect such passages, first bracket every claim that follows a premise indicator (since, because, given that). Then look for claim‑indicators (therefore, thus, hence) that signal a move from one level to the next. If a claim is both supported by earlier statements and used to support a later one, it is a sub‑conclusion. Mapping the flow with arrows or a simple tree diagram prevents the common error of treating a sub‑conclusion as the final answer.

Another layer of complexity appears when the argument contains a concession or a counter‑consideration (e.g., “Although the new signalling system reduces failures, the cost is prohibitive”). The concession does not negate the main line; it merely qualifies it. In such cases the main conclusion is usually a qualified recommendation (“The system should be adopted in phases”) rather than an absolute endorsement. Candidates must learn to separate the concessive clause from the core inferential chain. Practice with passages that embed two or three sub‑conclusions builds the habit of tracing the logical hierarchy rather than jumping to the most salient sentence.

Conditional Reasoning: Necessary and Sufficient Conditions in Statements

Conditional statements form the backbone of many assumption and strengthening questions. A statement of the form “If the railway introduces real‑time tracking, then punctuality will improve” contains a sufficient condition (real‑time tracking) and a necessary condition (punctuality improvement) only in the direction of the arrow. The converse (“If punctuality improves, then real‑time tracking was introduced”) is not guaranteed. Examination items frequently test the confusion between necessary and sufficient conditions by offering answer choices that affirm the converse or the inverse. The correct approach is to translate every conditional into its logical form: P → Q. The only valid immediate inference is the contrapositive ¬Q → ¬P. All other transformations (Q → P, ¬P → ¬Q) are fallacious unless the passage explicitly states a biconditional (“if and only if”).

When a passage contains multiple conditionals, they may chain: P → Q, Q → R, therefore P → R. This hypothetical syllogism is valid and often underlies a conclusion that the exam asks you to identify. However, a chain broken by a missing link (e.g., P → Q, R → S) cannot support a direct inference between P and S. Candidates should practice extracting conditionals, writing them symbolically, and checking whether the conclusion follows a valid chain or commits the fallacy of affirming the consequent. A useful shortcut: underline every “if”, “only if”, “unless”, “provided that” and rewrite each as an arrow; then visually inspect whether the conclusion’s antecedent and consequent appear at the ends of a continuous arrow path.

Technique: Spotting the Hidden Sub‑Conclusion
Statement: “Because the new freight corridor reduces transit time by 30 %, logistics costs will fall. Since lower logistics costs increase profit margins, the company should invest in the corridor.” Question: Which of the following is the main conclusion of the argument? (A) The new freight corridor reduces transit time by 30 %. (B) Logistics costs will fall. (C) Lower logistics costs increase profit margins. (D) The company should invest in the corridor.
First, identify premise indicators: “Because” introduces a premise (transit‑time reduction). “Since” introduces another premise (lower costs raise margins). The claim “Logistics costs will fall” follows from the first premise and is used as a premise for the next step; it is a sub‑conclusion. The claim “Lower logistics costs increase profit margins” is a general principle, also a premise. The final sentence “The company should invest in the corridor” is supported by the chain of sub‑conclusions and is not used to support any further claim. Therefore it is the main conclusion. Answer: (D).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a passage feels dense, spend the first 30 seconds drawing a quick arrow diagram: write each distinct claim in a box, connect boxes with arrows labelled “supports” or “leads to”. The box with no outgoing arrow is the main conclusion; boxes with both incoming and outgoing arrows are sub‑conclusions. This visual map lets you answer assumption, strengthening, and course‑of‑action questions without re‑reading the text multiple times.

Common Logical Fallacies Affecting Assumption and Conclusion Tasks

Examination designers embed classic informal fallacies to test whether candidates can distinguish a genuine logical gap from a rhetorical trick. The most frequent fallacies in this module are: (1) Hasty Generalisation – drawing a broad conclusion from a single anecdote (“One train arrived on time, therefore the new schedule works”). (2) False Cause (post hoc ergo propter hoc) – assuming temporal succession implies causation (“After the app launch, complaints dropped; the app caused the drop”). (3) Slippery Slope – claiming a modest step inevitably leads to an extreme outcome (“If we allow mobile tickets, soon we will have no ticket inspectors”). (4) Appeal to Authority without expertise – citing a senior official’s opinion on a technical matter where the official lacks relevant knowledge. (5) Straw Man – misrepresenting the proposed course of action to make it easier to attack. Recognising these patterns allows you to spot the hidden assumption that the argument relies on the fallacious move. For instance, a slippery‑slope argument assumes a chain of inevitable consequences; the necessary assumption is that each step follows necessarily, which is rarely justified. When a question asks “Which assumption is implicit?”, look for the premise that would make the fallacious leap valid. If the argument commits a false‑cause fallacy, the implicit assumption is that no other factor explains the observed correlation.

Another subtle fallacy is the “Equivocation” where a key term shifts meaning between premises and conclusion (e.g., “efficiency” used first as “fuel efficiency” then as “operational efficiency”). The assumption that the term is univocal is often the hidden premise. Training yourself to flag any term that appears in both a premise and the conclusion and to verify its consistent usage dramatically improves accuracy on assumption‑identification items.

Argument Mapping and Diagramming Techniques for Exam Speed

Argument mapping is a systematic method for converting prose into a visual structure that makes logical relations explicit. The standard notation uses nodes for propositions and directed edges for inferential support. Begin by numbering each distinct claim in the passage. Next, identify indicator words: premise indicators (because, since, for, given that) point from the premise node to the claim they support; conclusion indicators (therefore, thus, hence, so) point from the supporting node to the conclusion node. When a claim functions both as a conclusion of one inference and a premise for another, it receives both incoming and outgoing edges – this is the sub‑conclusion. A completed map reveals the ultimate conclusion (node with only incoming edges), the primary premises (nodes with only outgoing edges), and any independent arguments that run parallel. In the exam, you can sketch a mini‑map on the rough sheet in under a minute. The map then serves as a reference for every question type: assumption questions target missing edges; strengthening questions look for additional nodes that would add a supportive edge; weakening questions seek nodes that would cut an existing edge or add a counter‑edge; course‑of‑action questions map onto a practical recommendation node that must be linked to the problem node by a feasible edge. Practising with past papers and deliberately drawing maps for each passage builds the muscle memory needed to execute this under time pressure.

Integrated Practice: Combining Assumption, Conclusion, Course of Action, and Strengthening in a Single Passage

High‑difficulty sets present a single scenario followed by four or five questions, each targeting a different logical skill. The passage typically describes a problem (e.g., rising passenger complaints about cleanliness), offers a proposed solution (introduce automated cleaning robots), and includes data points (pilot study showing 25 % reduction in complaints). The first question may ask for the main conclusion – the recommendation to deploy robots. The second may ask for an implicit assumption – that the pilot conditions are representative of the whole network. The third may request a strengthening statement – a report that the robots’ maintenance cost is lower than manual cleaning. The fourth may ask for a weakening statement – evidence that the robots malfunction in high‑humidity zones common on the network. The fifth may ask for a suitable course of action – phased rollout with humidity‑resistant models. To tackle such sets efficiently, read the passage once, construct the argument map, and annotate each node with its role (premise, sub‑conclusion, main conclusion, evidence, counter‑evidence). Then answer each question by consulting the map rather than re‑reading. This integrated approach prevents the common pitfall of treating each question in isolation and missing the logical dependencies that the examiner expects you to exploit.

  • Treating a sub‑conclusion as the final conclusion because it appears near the end of the passage.
  • Confusing a sufficient condition with a necessary one and selecting the converse as a valid inference.
  • Overlooking equivocation on key terms and assuming the argument uses them uniformly.
  • Accepting a slippery‑slope chain as a justified assumption without checking each link.
  • Choosing a strengthening option that merely restates a premise instead of adding new support.
  • Selecting a course of action that is feasible but does not address the root cause identified in the passage.
  • Failing to map parallel arguments and therefore missing an independent assumption required for one line of reasoning.
