Data Sufficiency

Updated 25 Nov 2026
The two-statement format: when one statement alone is enough.
Two statements · either alone · both together · not sufficient

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the fundamental concepts of Data Sufficiency
  • Apply key formulas and techniques to solve problems
  • Practice with exam-level questions to build speed and accuracy

What is Data Sufficiency?

Data Sufficiency questions test your ability to determine whether the given data is sufficient to answer a question, without actually solving it completely. Each question is followed by two statements (I and II). You must decide if statement I alone, II alone, both together, or neither is sufficient. These questions appear in mains exams.

Answer Choices

In such questions, the standard options are: (a) Only statement I alone is sufficient; (b) Only statement II alone is sufficient; (c) Both statements together are sufficient, but neither alone is sufficient; (d) Both statements together are not sufficient; (e) Either statement I or II alone is sufficient.

Types of Data Sufficiency Questions

1. Age/Relation Based: Find ages of persons based on given relationships. Statements provide ratios, sums, or differences of ages.

2. Number Based: Find a number based on its properties — factors, multiples, digits, operations.

3. Percentage/Profit Based: Find profit percentage, discount, or marked price based on given transaction details.

4. Speed-Distance Based: Find speed, time, or distance based on motion parameters.

5. Comparison Based: Determine which is larger/smaller between two quantities based on given relationships.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Age Problem)
What is the present age of A?
Statement I: The ratio of A's age to B's age 5 years ago was 3:4.
Statement II: B's present age is twice A's age 10 years ago.

a) Only I b) Only II c) Both together d) Neither e) Either
Solution: c) Both together are sufficient. Let present ages be A and B. From I: (A-5)/(B-5) = 3/4 → 4A - 20 = 3B - 15 → 4A - 3B = 5. From II: B = 2(A-10) → B = 2A - 20. Two equations, two unknowns. Solving: 4A - 3(2A-20) = 5 → 4A - 6A + 60 = 5 → -2A = -55 → A = 27.5 years. Neither statement alone gives a unique answer, but together they do.
Example 2 (Number Problem)
Is the integer N divisible by 12?
Statement I: N is divisible by 4.
Statement II: N is divisible by 3.

a) Only I b) Only II c) Both together d) Neither e) Either
Solution: c) Both together are sufficient. For a number to be divisible by 12, it must be divisible by both 3 and 4 (since 12 = 3 × 4, and 3 and 4 are coprime). Statement I alone → divisible by 4 but not necessarily by 3 (e.g., 8). Statement II alone → divisible by 3 but not necessarily by 4 (e.g., 9). Together → divisible by both 3 and 4 → divisible by 12.
Example 3 (Profit-Loss)
What is the profit percentage earned by selling an article?
Statement I: The selling price is ₹240 more than the cost price.
Statement II: The selling price is 120% of the cost price.

a) Only I b) Only II c) Both together d) Neither e) Either
Solution: b) Only II alone is sufficient. Profit % = (SP - CP)/CP × 100. Statement I: SP = CP + 240. Without knowing CP or SP, the profit % cannot be determined. Statement II: SP = 1.2 × CP → Profit = 0.2 × CP → Profit % = 20%. So statement II alone is sufficient.

Shortcut Techniques

  • Analyze each statement independently first. Do not carry over information from one statement to the other.
  • If a statement gives an equation with one unknown, it is usually sufficient. If it gives one equation with two unknowns, it is usually not sufficient.
  • For 'Yes/No' type questions, a statement is sufficient only if it gives a definite YES or a definite NO.
  • Remember: You don't need to find the exact answer — just determine whether the answer CAN be found.
Pro Tip
In competitive exams, data sufficiency questions are often tricky because statements may seem sufficient but actually aren't. Always check for hidden assumptions. For example, a statement giving a ratio of two ages is not sufficient unless there is another equation to find unique values.

Advanced Problem Types

Competitive exams feature advanced variations combining multiple reasoning concepts. Master these to score high.

Multi-Step Problems

Some questions require applying multiple reasoning techniques sequentially. Break problems into clear steps.

Common Traps

  • Assuming a pattern continues without checking all elements
  • Misinterpreting negative statements in syllogisms
  • Forgetting wrapping in alphabet coding (Z to A transition)
  • Confusing directions (East vs West)
  • Overlooking either-or cases in syllogisms
  • Not considering all possible arrangements in puzzles
Pro Tip
For puzzles, start with the most concrete clue and build around it. Draw diagrams for seating arrangements and family trees for blood relations.

Combining Algebraic and Logical Constraints

Data sufficiency questions frequently blend algebraic equations with logical conditions such as parity, divisibility, or ordering. The key is to treat each statement as a system of constraints and to ask whether the system admits a unique solution for the target quantity. When a statement supplies an equation with two unknowns, it is usually insufficient unless a second independent relation — either from the other statement or from an implicit logical rule — reduces the degrees of freedom to zero. For example, a statement that “x + y = 10” alone cannot determine x, but if the question also tells us that x and y are positive integers and that x is even, the parity condition eliminates all but a few pairs, and the combination may become sufficient. Always list the explicit equations first, then overlay any hidden logical restrictions (integer, positive, prime, distinct, etc.) before judging sufficiency.

A practical workflow: (1) Translate each statement into mathematical notation, marking known constants and variables. (2) Identify the target expression (e.g., “value of x”, “ratio of a to b”). (3) Count independent equations versus unknowns. (4) Add any domain constraints (integers, non‑negative, real). (5) Test whether the target can vary while all constraints hold. If the target is forced to a single value, the data are sufficient; if at least two admissible values exist, they are not. This method prevents the common error of assuming that “two equations, two unknowns” automatically guarantees sufficiency — the equations must be independent and the domain must not introduce extra degrees of freedom.

Consider a question asking for the value of a two‑digit number N. Statement I: “The sum of the digits of N is 9.” Statement II: “N is a multiple of 11.” Alone, each statement leaves multiple candidates (I gives 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63, 72, 81, 90; II gives 11, 22, 33, 44, 55, 66, 77, 88, 99). Together they intersect at 99 only, so the combination is sufficient. The logical constraint “two‑digit” and the algebraic constraints “digit sum = 9” and “multiple of 11” together produce a unique answer. This illustrates how logical domain restrictions can turn an otherwise under‑determined algebraic system into a sufficient one.

Data Sufficiency with Inequalities and Absolute Values

Inequalities and absolute‑value expressions introduce ranges rather than single points, which changes the sufficiency analysis dramatically. A statement such as “|x – 3| < 5” translates to –2 < x < 8, a continuous interval. If the question asks “Is x > 0?” this statement alone is insufficient because the interval contains both positive and negative values. However, if a second statement says “x is an integer”, the interval collapses to the set {–1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7}. The target “x > 0” is still not decided uniquely because both positive and non‑positive integers remain. Only when the combined constraints shrink the feasible set to a subset that lies entirely on one side of the decision boundary does sufficiency arise.

When handling absolute values, split the expression into cases based on the sign of the inner expression. Each case yields a linear inequality; the union of the case solutions forms the overall feasible region. Be careful with “or” versus “and” logic: |x| < a means –a < x < a (an “and” condition), while |x| > a means x < –a or x > a (an “or” condition). The latter creates two disjoint intervals, which often makes a statement insufficient for a yes/no question unless the other statement eliminates one interval entirely.

A typical trap is to treat an inequality like “x² > 9” as equivalent to “x > 3”. The correct translation is x < –3 or x > 3. If the question asks “Is x positive?” the statement alone is insufficient because x could be –4. Adding a second statement “x > 0” would make the combination sufficient, but adding “x is an integer” would still leave both –4 and 4 as possibilities, so insufficiency persists. Always map the inequality to its exact solution set before combining with other data.

Worked Example: Inequality Sufficiency
Question: Is the integer x positive?
Statement I: x² – 4x – 5 < 0
Statement II: x is a multiple of 3
Statement I: Factor the quadratic: (x – 5)(x + 1) < 0. The product is negative between the roots, so –1 < x < 5. Since x is an integer (implied by the question), the possible values are {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}. This set contains both non‑positive (0) and positive (1‑4) integers, so Statement I alone is insufficient.
Statement II: x = 3k for some integer k. This alone gives infinitely many positive and negative multiples, so insufficient.
Combined: Intersect {0,1,2,3,4} with multiples of 3 → {0,3}. The set still contains 0 (non‑positive) and 3 (positive). Therefore the answer cannot be determined uniquely. The correct choice is “Both statements together are not sufficient.”
Exam‑Day Strategy
When you see absolute values or quadratic inequalities, immediately sketch the number line or write the critical points. Mark the feasible intervals with open or closed circles. Then overlay the second statement’s discrete set (e.g., multiples, primes). Visual intersection is faster than algebraic manipulation and reduces sign‑error risk.

Geometric Data Sufficiency: Figures and Measurements

Geometry‑based data sufficiency questions test whether given measurements (lengths, angles, areas, perimeters) determine a unique geometric quantity. The figure may be a triangle, quadrilateral, circle, or a composite shape. A single statement often provides a partial set of constraints — for instance, two sides and a non‑included angle (SSA) — which is notoriously ambiguous because it can yield zero, one, or two possible triangles. Recognizing the congruence criteria (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS) is essential: a statement that supplies any one of these complete sets makes the triangle uniquely determined, and any derived measure (area, third side, circumradius) becomes computable.

When statements involve circles, remember that a chord length and its distance from the centre uniquely fix the radius (by the Pythagorean theorem). Conversely, knowing only the radius and a chord length does not fix the chord’s position; the chord could be anywhere at that distance from the centre. Similarly, an inscribed angle theorem tells us that the measure of an inscribed angle is half the measure of its intercepted arc. If a statement gives the arc measure, the angle is fixed; if it gives the angle, the arc is fixed. However, knowing only the radius and an inscribed angle does not determine the chord length unless the central angle is also known.

Composite figures often require adding or subtracting areas. A statement that gives the area of a rectangle and the radius of a semicircle attached to one side may be sufficient for the total area, but insufficient for the perimeter if the rectangle’s other dimension is unknown. Always identify which dimensions are locked by the given data and which remain free variables. Draw a quick schematic, label knowns, and count degrees of freedom. If the target quantity can be expressed solely in terms of locked dimensions, the data are sufficient.

Probability and Statistics in Data Sufficiency

Probability questions in data sufficiency usually ask for a specific probability value (e.g., “What is the probability that a randomly chosen ball is red?”) or a yes/no decision (“Is the probability greater than 0.5?”). A statement may provide the composition of a sample space (counts of each colour) or a relationship between probabilities (e.g., “The probability of red is twice the probability of blue”). The sample space must be fully known — either by absolute counts or by enough ratios plus a total count — to compute a numeric probability. Ratios alone are insufficient unless the total number of outcomes is also given or can be deduced from another statement.

Statistical measures such as mean, median, mode, range, and standard deviation behave similarly. Knowing the mean of a set of five numbers does not determine the median; many distributions share the same mean. However, if a statement says “The five numbers are consecutive integers”, the mean equals the median, and both become determined once the mean is known. When statements give overlapping statistical facts (e.g., “Mean = 10, Median = 10, Range = 4”), check whether the system of equations they imply has a unique solution for the target statistic. Often the combination of mean and range for a symmetric distribution is sufficient, but for an arbitrary set it is not.

Conditional probability adds another layer: a statement like “P(A|B) = 0.3” together with “P(B) = 0.5” yields P(A∩B) = 0.15. If the question asks for P(A), you still need P(A∩Bᶜ) or P(A|Bᶜ) and P(Bᶜ). Always write the probability tree or Venn diagram, list known probabilities, and see whether the unknown target can be expressed solely in terms of knowns. If any required component remains free, the data are insufficient.

Time, Speed, and Distance: Integrated Sufficiency Analysis

Time‑speed‑distance (TSD) problems are a staple of data sufficiency because they translate into linear equations of the form distance = speed × time. A single statement often provides two of the three variables for a single journey, leaving the third unknown. For example, “The train travels 300 km in 5 hours” gives speed = 60 km/h, but if the question asks for the time taken to travel 450 km, the speed is known and the answer is computable — the statement is sufficient. However, if the statement only says “The train’s speed is 20 km/h more than the bus’s speed”, we have one equation with two unknown speeds; the target (e.g., time for the train to cover a given distance) cannot be found without additional data.

Relative motion introduces the concept of effective speed: when two objects move toward each other, their relative speed is the sum; when they move in the same direction, it is the difference. A statement giving the meeting time of two vehicles starting from opposite ends provides the sum of their speeds (total distance ÷ meeting time). If a second statement gives the ratio of their speeds, the individual speeds become solvable, and any downstream question (time for one vehicle to cover the whole distance, distance covered by the other before meeting) becomes answerable. Without the ratio, the individual speeds remain a degree of freedom.

Round‑trip or multi‑segment journeys add equations for each segment. A statement like “The average speed for the whole trip is 48 km/h” together with “The speed on the first half is 40 km/h” allows solving for the speed on the second half (using harmonic mean for equal distances). If the segments are based on time rather than distance, the arithmetic mean applies. Misidentifying the averaging method is a frequent trap. Always clarify whether “half the journey” refers to distance or time before writing equations.

  • Assuming a single equation with two unknowns is sufficient because the numbers look “nice”.
  • Ignoring domain restrictions such as integer, positive, or distinct values that can collapse a solution set.
  • Treating |x| > a as a single interval instead of two disjoint intervals.
  • Confusing SSA with a valid triangle congruence criterion and declaring a triangle uniquely determined.
  • Using arithmetic mean for average speed when the problem defines equal distances, which requires harmonic mean.
  • Overlooking that a probability ratio without a total count leaves the absolute probability undetermined.
  • Always verify that the combined statements do not introduce hidden assumptions.

Data Sufficiency with Functions and Composite Operations

Function‑based data sufficiency questions test whether the given statements lock down the output of a composite expression such as f(g(x)) or g(f(x)) for a specified domain. A statement may define a function explicitly (e.g., “f(x) = 2x + 3”), give a property (“f is linear and passes through (1,5)”), or provide a functional equation (“f(x+y) = f(x) + f(y)”). The key is to determine whether the combined information yields a unique value or a definitive yes/no answer for the target expression. When a statement supplies an explicit formula, the composition can be evaluated directly. When only properties are given, check whether those properties force a single function form on the relevant domain. For instance, “f is additive and continuous” forces f(x) = cx for some constant c; if a second statement fixes c, the composition becomes determinate. If the statements only restrict the function to a family (e.g., “f is quadratic with vertex at (0,0)”), the composition may still vary with the leading coefficient, rendering the data insufficient. Always write the composition symbolically, substitute known forms, and count the remaining free parameters. If the number of free parameters exceeds the number of independent constraints, the answer is “data not sufficient”.

Data Sufficiency in Number Theory: Divisibility and Remainders

Number‑theory data sufficiency revolves around statements that give congruences, greatest common divisors, least common multiples, or explicit factorizations. A typical target asks whether an integer N is divisible by a given number, or what the remainder is when N is divided by m. A statement such as “N ≡ 3 (mod 7)” fixes the residue class modulo 7 but leaves the residue modulo 5 undetermined. If a second statement provides “N ≡ 2 (mod 5)”, the Chinese Remainder Theorem guarantees a unique solution modulo 35, making the remainder modulo 35 determinable. However, if the moduli are not coprime (e.g., modulo 6 and modulo 9), the system may have zero or multiple solutions; the data are sufficient only when the combined congruences are consistent and yield a single residue class for the asked modulus. Statements about GCD and LCM work similarly: “gcd(N, 12) = 4” restricts N to multiples of 4 that are not multiples of 12; “lcm(N, 18) = 36” forces N to be a divisor of 36 containing the prime factors needed to reach the LCM. Combining both often pins down N uniquely. Beware of statements that only give a range (“N is a two‑digit number”) without modular information; they rarely suffice alone. Always translate each statement into a set of congruences or divisor conditions, then test whether the intersection yields a single answer for the question asked.

Worked Example: Composite Function Sufficiency
Question: For all real x, is f(g(x)) > 0?
Statement 1: f(x) = x² + 1.
Statement 2: g(x) = 2x – 3.
Statement 1 alone gives f(y) = y² + 1, which is always > 0 for any real y. However, without knowing g(x), we cannot guarantee that the argument y = g(x) is real; but g(x) is a real‑valued function for real x in standard contexts, so f(g(x)) = (g(x))² + 1 > 0 holds for any real‑valued g. Yet the problem does not state that g is real‑valued; if g could be complex, the inequality might not be defined. In typical data‑sufficiency settings, functions map reals to reals unless noted. Assuming real‑valued g, Statement 1 alone is sufficient. Statement 2 alone gives g(x) = 2x – 3, but without f we cannot evaluate f(g(x)). Together they give f(g(x)) = (2x – 3)² + 1, which is clearly > 0 for all real x. Since Statement 1 alone already suffices under the usual real‑valued assumption, the correct choice is “Statement 1 alone is sufficient”.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a function question appears, first identify the target expression. Write it in terms of the innermost function. Then check each statement for an explicit formula or a property that uniquely determines that function on the required domain. If a statement gives a property (additive, monotonic, periodic), ask whether that property forces a single functional form. Count free parameters; each independent statement can eliminate at most one parameter. If after using both statements a parameter remains, the data are insufficient.

Data Sufficiency with Sequences and Series

Sequence‑based data sufficiency asks for a specific term, the sum of a finite number of terms, or a property such as convergence. Statements may provide the first few terms, a recurrence relation, a closed‑form formula, or a characteristic like “the sequence is arithmetic”. An arithmetic progression is fully determined by any two distinct terms or by the first term and common difference. A geometric progression requires the first term and common ratio, or any two terms with known indices. If a statement says “the 5th term is 17 and the 9th term is 33”, the common difference is (33‑17)/(9‑5) = 4, and the first term becomes 1; the entire sequence is locked. For a recurrence such as “aₙ₊₂ = aₙ₊₁ + aₙ” (Fibonacci‑type), two initial values are needed to generate all subsequent terms; a single initial value leaves a degree of freedom. When statements give overlapping information (e.g., “the sum of the first 10 terms is 100” and “the sequence is arithmetic with common difference 2”), set up the sum formula Sₙ = n/2 (2a + (n‑1)d) and solve for the unknown first term. If the system yields a unique value for the asked quantity, the data are sufficient. Watch for statements that only bound the sequence (“all terms are positive”) without fixing parameters; they rarely suffice alone.

Data Sufficiency in Set Theory and Venn Diagrams

Set‑theory data sufficiency typically asks for the cardinality of a union, intersection, complement, or a specific region in a Venn diagram. Statements provide cardinalities of individual sets, pairwise intersections, or the universal set size. The inclusion‑exclusion principle |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| – |A ∩ B| extends to three sets with additional triple‑intersection terms. A single statement giving |A| = 30, |B| = 25, and |A ∩ B| = 10 determines |A ∪ B| = 45 uniquely. If a statement only gives |A| = 30 and |B| = 25, the union size can range from 30 (if B ⊆ A) to 55 (if disjoint); the data are insufficient. When three sets are involved, three independent equations are needed to solve for the seven region cardinalities (including the outside region). Statements such as “|A ∩ B ∩ C| = 5” and “|A ∪ B ∪ C| = 80” together with individual set sizes may still leave degrees of freedom unless enough pairwise intersections are also known. Always draw the Venn diagram, label known regions, write the inclusion‑exclusion equations, and count unknowns versus independent equations. If the number of unknown region sizes exceeds the number of independent constraints, the answer is “data not sufficient”.

Data Sufficiency with Logical Reasoning: Conditional Statements and Quantifiers

Logical‑reasoning data sufficiency presents statements in the form of conditionals (“If P then Q”), biconditionals (“P if and only if Q”), or quantified claims (“All A are B”, “Some A are not B”). The target may be a truth value of a compound proposition or the validity of an inference. Treat each statement as a set of logical constraints. For example, “All managers are employees” translates to ∀x (Manager(x) → Employee(x)). “Some employees are not managers” translates to ∃x (Employee(x) ∧ ¬Manager(x)). Together they do not fix the exact number of managers or employees, but they may be sufficient to answer a yes/no question such as “Is it possible that no manager exists?” (Yes, the statements allow zero managers). When statements involve nested conditionals, construct a truth table or use logical equivalences (contrapositive, De Morgan) to see whether the target proposition is forced. A common trap is assuming that “If P then Q” and “Q” together imply P (affirming the consequent). In data sufficiency, check whether the combined statements eliminate all models where the target is false. If at least one consistent model makes the target false and another makes it true, the data are insufficient. Always list the atomic propositions, write each statement in symbolic form, and test satisfiability for both truth values of the target.

  • Assuming a functional property (e.g., additivity) forces a unique formula without checking continuity or domain restrictions.
  • Treating congruences with non‑coprime moduli as if they always yield a unique solution via Chinese Remainder Theorem.
  • Believing that two terms of a sequence always determine the whole sequence, ignoring the need for the sequence type (arithmetic, geometric, recursive).
  • Counting Venn‑diagram regions incorrectly by forgetting the outside region or double‑counting the triple intersection.
  • Confusing the direction of a conditional and treating “If P then Q” together with “Q” as sufficient to prove P.
  • Overlooking that a statement giving only a range (e.g., “N is a two‑digit number”) does not constrain modular residues.
  • Assuming that a single initial value of a second‑order recurrence determines all later terms.

These advanced sections equip you to dissect the most intricate data‑sufficiency items by translating each statement into precise mathematical or logical constraints and verifying whether the target becomes uniquely determined.
