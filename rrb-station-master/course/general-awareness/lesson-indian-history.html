Ancient India: From Prehistory to the Mauryan Empire

1.1 The Indus Valley Civilization

The Indus Valley Civilization, flourishing between roughly 2600 BCE and 1900 BCE, emerged in the alluvial plains of the Indus and Ghaggar‑Hakra river systems and displayed a level of urban planning that remained unmatched in the subcontinent for centuries. Its major sites — Harappa, Mohenjo‑Daro, Dholavira, Lothal and Rakhigarhi — reveal a grid‑based street layout, sophisticated drainage, standardized baked‑brick dimensions and a system of weights and measures based on a binary‑decimal progression. The civilization’s economy rested on intensive agriculture supplemented by craft production such as bead‑making, metallurgy and cotton textile weaving, while long‑distance trade links extended to Mesopotamia, the Persian Gulf and the Arabian Peninsula, as attested by seals bearing the distinctive unicorn motif found in Sumerian contexts. The script, still undeciphered, appears on thousands of seals and tablets, suggesting a bureaucratic control over commerce and possibly a form of early writing for administrative purposes. Around 1900 BCE a combination of climatic aridification, tectonic shifts altering river courses and possible over‑exploitation of resources led to the gradual abandonment of the major urban centres, after which the cultural legacy persisted in the subsequent Vedic milieu through continuity in pottery styles, religious symbols and the memory of a riverine civilization.

1.2 The Vedic Age and the Formation of Janapadas

Following the decline of the Indus urban centres, the Indo‑Aryan groups composed the Rigveda around 1500‑1200 BCE, marking the beginning of the Vedic period that lasted until about 500 BCE. The early Vedic society was pastoral, organized into tribal units called jana led by a raja who functioned more as a war‑leader than a sovereign monarch; the sabha and samiti assemblies provided a consultative framework for decision‑making. As iron technology spread in the later Vedic phase (c. 1000‑600 BCE), agriculture expanded into the Ganga‑Yamuna doab, enabling surplus production and the emergence of larger territorial entities known as janapadas. By the sixth century BCE, sixteen major mahajanapadas — such as Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa and Avanti — dominated the political landscape, each with fortified capitals, standing armies and a developing bureaucracy. The competition among these states fostered new political ideas, evident in the Arthashastra tradition attributed to Kautilya, and set the stage for the first imperial consolidation under the Mauryas.

1.3 The Mauryan Empire and Ashoka's Dhamma

Chandragupta Maurya, guided by his mentor Kautilya, overthrew the Nanda dynasty around 321 BCE and established the Mauryan Empire, which at its zenith under Ashoka (c. 268‑232 BCE) stretched from the Hindu Kush to the Bay of Bengal and deep into the Deccan. The empire’s administration was highly centralized: the mantriparishad (council of ministers) advised the emperor, a network of mahamatras oversaw provinces, and a sophisticated espionage system reported directly to the centre. Revenue was collected through a land‑tax system based on the bhaga (share) of produce, supplemented by tolls on trade routes and state monopolies on mining, salt and liquor. Ashoka’s conversion to Buddhism after the brutal Kalinga war (c. 261 BCE) prompted the propagation of Dhamma — a moral code emphasizing non‑violence, tolerance, respect for elders and welfare measures such as hospitals, veterinary clinics and roadside rest‑houses. His edicts, inscribed on pillars and rock surfaces in Prakrit using Brahmi script, constitute the earliest extensive epigraphic record in India and illustrate how imperial ideology was communicated across a linguistically diverse realm. The Mauryan decline after Ashoka’s death resulted from succession disputes, fiscal strain and the resurgence of regional powers, paving the way for the post‑Mauryan fragmentation.

Classical and Early Medieval India

2.1 The Gupta Age: Golden Age of Culture and Science

The Gupta dynasty, founded by Sri Gupta and expanded by Chandragupta I (c. 319‑335 CE), ushered in a period often described as a classical age because of remarkable achievements in literature, mathematics, astronomy, medicine and the visual arts. The imperial court at Pataliputra and later at Ujjain patronized scholars such as Kalidasa, whose plays Abhijnanasakuntalam and Meghaduta set standards for Sanskrit drama and poetry; Aryabhata, who introduced the concept of zero, the place‑value system and a heliocentric approximation of planetary motion; and Varahamihira, whose Brihat Samhita compiled knowledge of astronomy, meteorology and architecture. The Guptas maintained a decentralized feudal structure where local samantas or feudatories administered territories in return for military service and tribute, yet the centre retained control over coinage — issuing the celebrated gold dinara and silver rupaka — and over major trade routes linking the Gangetic plains with the western coast and Southeast Asia. Temple architecture evolved from the simple garbhagriha to the ornate shikhara style seen at Deogarh and Bhitargaon, while the university at Nalanda attracted students from China, Korea and Central Asia. The dynasty’s decline in the mid‑sixth century CE, precipitated by Hun invasions and internal feudatory revolts, fragmented the political unity but left a cultural template that persisted in subsequent regional kingdoms.

2.2 Post‑Gupta Regional Kingdoms and the Rise of Feudal Polities

After the Guptas, the subcontinent splintered into a mosaic of regional powers — the Maukhari of Kannauj, the Pushyabhuti of Thanesar, the Vakataka in the Deccan, the Maitraka of Valabhi and the Pratihara of Gurjara‑Pratihara in the north‑west. These kingdoms adopted a feudal‑type land grant system known as brahmadeya and devadana, whereby villages were donated to Brahmins or temples, creating a class of land‑holding intermediaries who collected revenue and provided military levies. The Pratiharas successfully resisted Arab incursions at the Battle of Rajasthan (738 CE) and later contested the Palas of Bengal and the Rashtrakutas of Manyakheta for control of Kannauj, a symbolic centre of imperial legitimacy. In the Deccan, the Rashtrakutas under Dhruva and Govinda III extended their influence as far north as Kannauj and south to the Tamil country, fostering a vibrant court culture that produced the Kailasa temple at Ellora. The constant tripartite struggle for Kannauj illustrates how the concept of a paramount sovereign persisted even in a fragmented political environment, while the proliferation of land grants entrenched a socio‑economic structure that would later be adapted by the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals.

2.3 Early Medieval South India: Cholas, Chalukyas and the Temple Economy

In the far south, the Chola dynasty rose to prominence in the ninth century CE under Vijayalaya and reached its apogee under Rajaraja I (985‑1014 CE) and his son Rajendra I (1014‑1044 CE), who transformed the Tamil littoral into a maritime empire extending to Sri Lanka, the Maldives and the Srivijaya kingdom in Sumatra. The Chola state financed its naval expeditions and massive temple building programmes through a highly organized land revenue system — the variyam (village assembly) collected the kadamai (land tax) in kind and cash, while the ur and nagar assemblies managed irrigation tanks and market regulation. The grand Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur and the Gangaikonda Cholapuram complex exemplify the integration of religious authority, royal propaganda and economic redistribution, as temple lands (devadana) supported artisans, dancers and scholars. Parallel to the Cholas, the Western Chalukyas of Kalyani and the Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi patronized the Vesara architectural style, producing the Virupaksha temple at Pattadakal and the Alampur group. The temple economy in the south thus functioned as a node of agrarian surplus extraction, craft production and long‑distance trade, linking the interior with the Indian Ocean world long before European arrival.

Example Question
Which of the following pairs correctly matches a dynasty with its characteristic land‑revenue arrangement? (a) Maurya – bhaga share of produce; (b) Gupta – zabt cash assessment; (c) Chola – kadamai collected by village assemblies; (d) Mughal – ryotwari direct settlement with cultivators.
The Mauryan state levied a share of the harvest known as bhaga, making (a) correct. The zabt system was introduced by the Mughals under Akbar, not the Guptas, so (b) is false. The Chola administration indeed relied on village assemblies (ur, sabha) to collect the kadamai, confirming (c) as correct. The ryotwari system was a British land‑revenue settlement in the Madras and Bombay presidencies, not a Mughal practice, so (d) is false. Therefore the correctly matched pairs are (a) and (c).
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks for “characteristic land‑revenue arrangement”, focus on the terminology unique to each regime — bhaga, zabt, kadamai, ryotwari, mahalwari — because examiners rarely mix them across periods.

The Sultanate and Mughal Period (Medieval India)

3.1 The Delhi Sultanate: Administration, Economy and Society

The establishment of the Delhi Sultanate in 1206 CE by Qutb‑ud‑din Aibak marked the beginning of sustained Islamic rule in northern India. The Sultanate evolved through five dynasties — Mamluk, Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid and Lodi — each contributing to a centralized administrative framework that borrowed from Persian models while adapting to Indian conditions. The iqta system assigned revenue‑yielding territories to military officers (muqtis) in lieu of salary, creating a landed military aristocracy whose loyalty depended on the sultan’s ability to enforce transfers. Alauddin Khalji (1296‑1316) reformed the revenue apparatus by fixing the land tax at half the produce (the kharaj) and introducing market controls (the shahna‑i‑mandi) to stabilize grain prices for the army. Muhammad bin Tughlaq’s ambitious but disastrous experiments — token currency, forced capital shift to Daulatabad and a massive expedition to Khurasan — strained the treasury and triggered widespread revolts. Socially, the Sultanate period saw the growth of urban centres like Delhi, Multan and Gaur, the spread of Sufi orders (Chishti, Suhrawardi) that facilitated cultural synthesis, and the emergence of a Persianised bureaucratic class that kept detailed revenue records in Persian and Arabic.

3.2 The Mughal Empire: Centralised Administration and Cultural Synthesis

Babur’s victory at Panipat (1526) laid the foundation of the Mughal Empire, but it was Akbar (1556‑1605) who forged a durable imperial structure. Akbar abolished the iqta in favour of the mansabdari system, grading officials (mansabdars) by zat (personal rank) and sawar (cavalry quota) and paying them through cash salaries or revenue assignments (jagirs) that were periodically transferred to prevent hereditary entrenchment. The land‑revenue reform known as zabt, supervised by Todar Mal, measured cultivated area with the bigha unit, classified soil fertility and fixed a cash demand (the dastur) based on a ten‑year average yield, thereby converting a share‑crop system into a predictable monetary flow. Akbar’s policy of sulh‑i‑kul (universal tolerance) manifested in the abolition of the jizya (1579), the inclusion of Rajput nobles in the mansabdari hierarchy, and the patronage of a syncretic court culture that produced the Akbarnama, the Ain‑i‑Akbari and the Mughal miniature painting tradition. Subsequent emperors — Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb — expanded the empire to its territorial zenith (covering most of the subcontinent by 1690) but also intensified fiscal extraction, re‑imposed the jizya (1679) and faced mounting resistance from Marathas, Sikhs and regional governors, setting the stage for imperial fragmentation.

3.3 Decline of the Mughals and Emergence of Regional Powers

After Aurangzeb’s death in 1707, the Mughal centre weakened rapidly due to succession wars, the erosion of the mansabdari‑jagir cycle and the loss of revenue from the Deccan campaigns. Provincial governors (subahdars) such as Nizam‑ul‑Mulk in Hyderabad, Murshid Quli Khan in Bengal and Saadat Khan in Awadh asserted de facto independence while nominally acknowledging the emperor. The Maratha confederacy, organized under the Peshwa system, extracted chauth and sardeshmukhi tributes from Mughal territories, effectively creating a parallel fiscal network. In the north‑west, the Sikh misls coalesced into the Khalsa under Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1799‑1839), establishing a sovereign state centred on Lahore. The European trading companies — especially the English East India Company — exploited this political vacuum, securing diwani rights over Bengal after the Battle of Buxar (1764) and gradually converting commercial privileges into territorial sovereignty. By the early nineteenth century the Mughal emperor had become a pensioner of the British, and the subcontinent was partitioned into a patchwork of princely states, directly administered provinces and company‑controlled territories.

Advent of Europeans and Colonial Rule (Modern India)

4.1 Portuguese, Dutch, French and British Commercial Rivalries

The arrival of Vasco da Gama at Calicut in 1498 opened a direct sea route from Europe to the Indian Ocean, initiating a century of Portuguese dominance over the spice trade through fortified enclaves at Goa, Daman, Diu and Cochin. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) challenged Portuguese control in the early seventeenth century, capturing Malacca (1641) and establishing a network of factories along the Coromandel and Malabar coasts, but their focus remained on the Indonesian archipelago. The French, entering later, set up settlements at Pondicherry, Chandernagore, Mahe and Yanam, and under Dupleix (1742‑1754) pursued a policy of political interference in Carnatic and Deccan succession disputes. The British East India Company, chartered in 1600, initially operated from Surat and later from Madras, Bombay and Calcutta, leveraging naval superiority and a sophisticated system of factory records, country‑trade and private‑trade to outmaneuver rivals. The decisive turning point came with the Battle of Plassey (1757), where Robert Clive’s alliance with Mir Jafar secured Bengal’s diwani rights, and the Battle of Buxar (1764), which confirmed British fiscal dominance over Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. By the early nineteenth century the British had eliminated French territorial ambitions (Treaty of Paris 1763) and reduced the Dutch and Portuguese to marginal enclaves, establishing a de facto monopoly over Indian trade and the beginnings of territorial empire.

4.2 British Expansion: From Battle of Plassey to the Doctrine of Lapse

Following the acquisition of diwani, the Company transformed from a commercial body into a sovereign power, employing a combination of subsidiary alliances, outright annexation and diplomatic pressure. The Subsidiary Alliance system, formalised by Lord Wellesley (1798‑1805), compelled Indian rulers to maintain British troops at their own expense, accept a British resident at court and cede control over foreign relations. The Doctrine of Lapse, articulated by Lord Dalhousie (1848‑1856), declared that any princely state without a natural male heir would revert to British control, leading to the annexation of Satara (1848), Jaitpur (1849), Sambalpur (1850), Nagpur (1854) and Jhansi (1854). Military campaigns such as the Anglo‑Maratha Wars (1775‑1818), the Anglo‑Sikh Wars (1845‑1849) and the Anglo‑Burmese Wars (1824‑1885) expanded the Company’s frontier to the Himalayas, the Punjab and the Irrawaddy. The 1857 Revolt, triggered by grievances over the new Enfield rifle cartridges, land annexations and cultural insensitivity, resulted in the dissolution of the East India Company and the proclamation of direct Crown rule through the Government of India Act 1858.

4.3 Administrative Reforms, Land Revenue Systems and Social Policies

Under Crown rule, the British introduced a uniform administrative architecture: the Viceroy as the Crown’s representative, the Executive Council and the Legislative Council (Indian Councils Act 1861, 1892, 1909). Land revenue was reorganised into three major systems — the Permanent Settlement in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa (fixed demand on zamindars), the Ryotwari system in Madras, Bombay and Assam (direct settlement with cultivators) and the Mahalwari system in the North‑Western Provinces, Punjab and Central India (village‑level collective responsibility). These systems entrenched a revenue‑maximising logic that often ignored ecological limits, contributing to famines such as the Great Bengal Famine 1770, the Deccan Famine 1876‑78 and the Bengal Famine 1943. Socially, the British enacted the Abolition of Sati (1829), the Widow Remarriage Act (1856), the Age of Consent Act (1891) and the Child Marriage Restraint Act (1929), while simultaneously promoting English education through the Macaulay Minute (1835) and establishing universities at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (1857). The introduction of the Indian Penal Code (1860), the Code of Criminal Procedure (1898) and the Indian Evidence Act (1872) created a unified legal framework that persists in modified form today. These reforms, while modernising certain institutions, also deepened colonial extraction and sowed the seeds of nationalist resistance.

Freedom Struggle and Independence

5.1 Early Resistance: Revolt of 1857 and the Rise of National Consciousness

The 1857 Revolt, often termed the First War of Independence, erupted across northern and central India as a coalition of sepoys, dispossessed zamindars, peasants and local rulers such as Nana Sahib, Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi and Bahadur Shah Zafar in Delhi. Although the uprising lacked a unified command and was brutally suppressed by 1858, it shattered the myth of British invincibility and forced the Crown to assume direct responsibility, leading to the proclamation of the Queen’s Proclamation (1858) promising respect for Indian princes, religious tolerance and non‑interference in social customs. The aftermath saw the emergence of a new educated intelligentsia — products of the colonial university system — who began articulating political demands through petitions, newspapers and associations such as the British Indian Association (1851) and the Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870). The formation of the Indian National Congress in 1885 by A.O. Hume provided a platform for moderate leaders like Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta and Gopal Krishna Gokhale to press for constitutional reforms, Indianisation of the civil services and a share in legislative councils.

5.2 Formation of the Indian National Congress and the Moderate Phase

During its first two decades the Congress operated within the constitutional framework, submitting memorials to the Viceroy and the British Parliament, and achieving incremental gains such as the Indian Councils Act 1892 (expanded legislative councils) and the Indian Universities Act 1904 (greater Indian control over higher education). The moderate strategy relied on “prayer, petition and protest”, believing that British liberalism would eventually concede self‑government. However, the partition of Bengal in 1905 by Lord Curzon, justified on administrative grounds but widely perceived as a divide‑and‑rule tactic, galvanised a more assertive nationalist response. The Swadeshi Movement (1905‑1908) promoted boycott of British goods, revival of indigenous industries and the establishment of national schools, while leaders like Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal and Lala Lajpat Rai (Lal‑Bal‑Pal) advocated “Swaraj is my birthright”. The British responded with repressive measures — the Seditious Meetings Act 1907, the Indian Press Act 1910 and the imprisonment of extremist leaders — which radicalised a generation and set the stage for mass civil disobedience.

5.3 Mass Movements, Partition and the Transfer of Power

Mahatma Gandhi’s return from South Africa in 1915 introduced the philosophy of satyagraha (non‑violent non‑cooperation) as a mass mobilisation tool. The Non‑Cooperation Movement (1920‑1922), the Salt March (1930) launching the Civil Disobedience Movement, and the Quit India Movement (1942) each drew millions of peasants, workers, students and women into the political arena, forcing the British to confront the untenability of their rule amid global war pressures. Parallel to the Congress, the All‑India Muslim League under Muhammad Ali Jinnah articulated the two‑nation theory, demanding a separate Muslim state, which gained traction after the 1937 provincial elections and the 1940 Lahore Resolution. The 1947 Mountbatten Plan partitioned British India into the Dominion of India and the Dominion of Pakistan, accompanied by massive communal violence and the displacement of an estimated ten to fifteen million people. The Indian Independence Act 1947 transferred legislative sovereignty to the Constituent Assembly, which adopted the Constitution on 26 January 1950, establishing a sovereign democratic republic. The legacy of the freedom struggle — constitutional democracy, secularism, federalism and a commitment to social justice — continues to shape the political discourse of the subcontinent.

The narrative of Indian history thus moves from the urban sophistication of the Indus Valley through the philosophical ferment of the Vedic age, the imperial experiments of the Mauryas and Guptas, the medieval synthesis under the Sultanate and Mughals, the colonial restructuring by European powers, and finally the mass‑based nationalist movement that achieved independence and laid the foundations of the modern republic.
