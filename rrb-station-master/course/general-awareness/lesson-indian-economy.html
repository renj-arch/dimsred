1. National Income Accounting and Growth Measurement

1.1 Gross Domestic Product and Its Variants

Gross domestic product measures the monetary value of all final goods and services produced within the geographic boundaries of a country during a specific period, usually a financial year running from April to March in India. It captures production by both resident and non-resident entities operating inside the territory, which means the output of a foreign-owned factory in Gujarat counts toward India's GDP while the output of an Indian-owned factory in the United Kingdom does not. The Central Statistics Office under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation releases quarterly and annual estimates, distinguishing between GDP at constant prices, which removes the effect of inflation, and GDP at current prices, which reflects prevailing market values. The production approach sums gross value added across agriculture, industry and services, the expenditure approach adds private consumption, government consumption, gross capital formation and net exports, and the income approach aggregates compensation of employees, operating surplus and mixed income; theoretically all three yield the same total, though statistical discrepancies appear in practice.

1.2 Real versus Nominal Growth and the Deflator

Nominal GDP rises when either output increases or prices rise, so policymakers separate the two effects by computing real GDP using a base year, currently 2011-12 for the official series. The implicit GDP deflator, calculated as nominal GDP divided by real GDP multiplied by one hundred, serves as the broadest measure of economy-wide price change because it covers all domestically produced goods and services, unlike consumer or wholesale indices that use fixed baskets. When the deflator rises faster than the consumer price index, it suggests that investment goods or government services are experiencing sharper price pressure than household consumption items. A falling deflator combined with rising nominal GDP signals robust volume growth with contained inflation, a combination the Monetary Policy Committee watches closely when setting the repo rate. Base year revisions, such as the shift from 2004-05 to 2011-12, incorporate new data sources like the MCA-21 corporate filings and change sectoral weights, often raising the measured size of the economy and altering the growth trajectory retrospectively.

1.3 Gross National Product and Net Factor Income from Abroad

Gross national product equals GDP plus net factor income from abroad, which is the difference between income earned by Indian residents from overseas investments and labour and income paid to foreign residents for their contributions inside India. Because India receives large remittances from its diaspora and earns income from outward foreign direct investment, while also paying profits and interest to foreign portfolio investors, the gap between GDP and GNP fluctuates with global capital flows and exchange rate movements. A widening positive NFIA implies that the nation's ownership of productive assets abroad is generating a net inflow, strengthening the country's claim on global output. The distinction matters for per capita income comparisons: GNP per capita better reflects the average purchasing power available to residents, whereas GDP per capita reflects domestic production capacity. International agencies such as the World Bank often report both metrics, and the difference can be substantial for economies with large external asset positions.

1.4 Measurement Challenges and Base Year Revisions

Measuring national income in a diverse economy involves imputations for the informal sector, which employs a majority of the workforce but maintains limited formal accounts. The CSO uses enterprise surveys, household consumption expenditure surveys conducted by the National Sample Survey Office, and administrative data from tax filings to estimate value added in unincorporated enterprises. Agricultural output relies on crop cutting experiments and market arrival data, while services like trade and transport use proxy indicators such as diesel sales and railway freight traffic. Periodic base year updates incorporate new surveys, change the commodity basket for price indices, and reclassify activities under the National Industrial Classification aligned with the International Standard Industrial Classification. The 2011-12 revision introduced financial intermediation services indirectly measured for banking and insurance, raising the services share. Users of the data must note that splicing old and new series requires caution because methodological breaks can create artificial jumps in growth rates.

2. Union Budget Framework and Fiscal Policy

2.1 Constitutional Provisions and the Budget Cycle

Article 112 of the Constitution mandates that the President shall cause to be laid before both Houses of Parliament an annual financial statement, popularly known as the Union Budget, showing estimated receipts and expenditure for the ensuing financial year. The budget cycle begins nearly a year in advance when the Budget Division of the Department of Economic Affairs issues a circular to ministries seeking preliminary estimates, followed by pre-budget consultations with industry, farmers, economists and state finance ministers. The Finance Minister presents the budget in the Lok Sabha, traditionally on the first working day of February since the 2017-18 reform that advanced the date from the last working day to allow legislative approval before the fiscal year starts. The Railway Budget, presented separately since 1924 on the recommendation of the Acworth Committee, was merged with the General Budget from 2017-19 onward following the Bibek Debroy Committee report, ending a ninety-two year tradition and integrating transport finances into the overall fiscal framework.

2.2 Revenue and Capital Account Classification

Government receipts and expenditure are split into revenue and capital accounts to distinguish recurring flows from asset-creating outlays. Revenue receipts comprise tax revenue — corporation tax, income tax, customs, central goods and services tax, and union excise duties — and non-tax revenue such as interest receipts, dividends from public sector enterprises, and spectrum auction proceeds. Revenue expenditure covers interest payments, salaries, pensions, subsidies, and grants to states; it does not create assets or reduce liabilities. Capital receipts include market borrowings, recoveries of loans, disinvestment proceeds, and small savings net collections. Capital expenditure funds asset creation like highways, railways, defence equipment, and loans to states and public enterprises. The distinction is crucial because a high revenue deficit — revenue expenditure exceeding revenue receipts — indicates that the government is borrowing to finance consumption, which is considered fiscally imprudent, whereas a capital deficit financed by borrowing for productive assets can raise future growth potential.

2.3 Fiscal Deficit, Primary Deficit and FRBM Targets

The fiscal deficit equals total expenditure minus total receipts excluding borrowings; it measures the total borrowing requirement of the government in a year. The primary deficit subtracts interest payments from the fiscal deficit, showing the borrowing needed for current operations excluding the debt service burden. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003, amended in 2018, mandates the central government to limit the fiscal deficit to three percent of GDP and eliminate the revenue deficit, with an escape clause allowing a deviation of up to 0.5 percent of GDP in cases of structural reforms or severe economic stress. The 2021-22 budget invoked this clause to set a glide path targeting 4.5 percent by 2025-26 after the pandemic pushed the deficit to 9.2 percent in 2020-21. States have their own FRBM acts, and the Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended a consolidated deficit anchor of six percent of GDP for the general government. Compliance is monitored through the Medium Term Fiscal Policy Statement presented with the budget, which projects deficits, debt, and guarantees over a rolling three-year horizon.

2.4 Tax Structure: Direct and Indirect Reforms

Direct taxes, levied on income and wealth, include corporation tax and personal income tax governed by the Income Tax Act, 1961, and the recently enacted Finance Acts that amend rates annually. The Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2019, lowered the corporate tax rate to twenty-two percent for existing domestic companies and fifteen percent for new manufacturing units, foregoing exemptions to simplify compliance. Indirect taxation underwent a historic overhaul with the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016, introducing the Goods and Services Tax from July 2017, subsuming central excise, service tax, state VAT, and other levies into a dual GST with central, state, and integrated components. The GST Council, chaired by the Union Finance Minister and comprising state finance ministers, decides rates, thresholds, and dispute resolution. Customs duties remain outside GST and are used for tariff protection and revenue. The Direct Tax Code, proposed to replace the Income Tax Act, aims to widen the base, lower rates, and reduce litigation, but has not yet been enacted. Tax buoyancy — the responsiveness of tax revenue to GDP growth — remains a key metric for assessing reform effectiveness.

Worked Example: Fiscal Deficit Calculation
In a hypothetical budget, the government estimates revenue receipts of ₹20 lakh crore, capital receipts excluding borrowings of ₹2 lakh crore, revenue expenditure of ₹26 lakh crore, and capital expenditure of ₹8 lakh crore. Calculate the fiscal deficit and the primary deficit if interest payments amount to ₹7 lakh crore.
Total receipts excluding borrowings equal revenue receipts plus non-debt capital receipts: ₹20 lakh crore + ₹2 lakh crore = ₹22 lakh crore. Total expenditure equals revenue expenditure plus capital expenditure: ₹26 lakh crore + ₹8 lakh crore = ₹34 lakh crore. Fiscal deficit = Total expenditure − Total receipts excluding borrowings = ₹34 lakh crore − ₹22 lakh crore = ₹12 lakh crore. Primary deficit = Fiscal deficit − Interest payments = ₹12 lakh crore − ₹7 lakh crore = ₹5 lakh crore. The primary deficit shows that even after meeting interest obligations, the government needs to borrow ₹5 lakh crore for its current operations and asset creation.
Exam Insight: FRBM Escape Clause Triggers
Questions often test the specific conditions permitting deviation from the fiscal deficit target under the FRBM Act. Remember the two statutory triggers: (1) structural reforms in the economy with unanticipated fiscal implications, and (2) a sharp decline in real output growth of at least three percentage points below the average of the previous four quarters. The 2020-21 pandemic deviation was justified under the second trigger, while the 2019-20 corporate tax cut deviation used the first. The Comptroller and Auditor General certifies whether the trigger conditions are met.

3. Banking System and Monetary Policy

3.1 Reserve Bank of India: Structure and Functions

The Reserve Bank of India, established on April 1, 1935, under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, and nationalised on January 1, 1949, serves as the central bank, monetary authority, regulator of the banking system, manager of foreign exchange reserves, and banker to the central and state governments. Its central board of directors, appointed by the government for four-year terms, includes the governor, up to four deputy governors, ten non-official directors representing various sectors, and two government officials. The RBI Act empowers the bank to issue currency notes under Section 22, act as banker to banks under Section 17, and regulate credit through quantitative and qualitative instruments. The Monetary Policy Committee, constituted in 2016 under Section 45ZB of the RBI Act, comprises six members — three from the RBI including the governor as ex-officio chairperson, and three external members appointed by the government — and decides the policy repo rate by majority vote, with the governor holding a casting vote in case of a tie. The committee meets at least four times a year and publishes a resolution with the reasoning behind each decision.

3.2 Monetary Policy Tools and the Inflation Targeting Framework

The RBI employs the repo rate — the rate at which it lends overnight to banks against government securities — as the primary policy signal, supported by the reverse repo rate, the marginal standing facility rate, and the bank rate. The liquidity adjustment facility, introduced in 2000, allows banks to manage daily liquidity mismatches through repo and reverse repo auctions. Open market operations, involving outright purchase or sale of government securities, and the market stabilisation scheme, used to absorb surplus liquidity from capital inflows, complement the LAF. The Cash Reserve Ratio and Statutory Liquidity Ratio are statutory pre-emptions on bank deposits; CRR balances earn no interest, while SLR investments in government securities earn market yields. In 2016, the government notified a flexible inflation targeting framework mandating the RBI to maintain consumer price index inflation at four percent with a tolerance band of two to six percent. If inflation breaches the band for three consecutive quarters, the RBI must report to the government the reasons, remedial actions, and the estimated time to return to target, enhancing accountability and transparency.

3.3 Commercial Banking Reforms and Asset Quality

Public sector banks, which account for the majority of banking assets, have undergone repeated recapitalisation and consolidation. The Indradhanush framework launched in 2015 addressed governance, capital, de-stressing, and accountability, followed by mega mergers in 2020 reducing the number of PSBs from twenty-seven to twelve. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, created a time-bound resolution process for stressed assets, shifting the paradigm from debtor-in-possession to creditor-in-control. The RBI's Prudential Framework for Resolution of Stressed Assets, 2019, replaced earlier schemes like the Strategic Debt Restructuring and Scheme for Sustainable Structuring of Stressed Assets, requiring lenders to implement resolution plans within 180 days of default. Gross non-performing assets of scheduled commercial banks peaked at 11.2 percent in March 2018 and declined to below four percent by March 2024 due to recoveries, write-offs, and improved underwriting. The Prompt Corrective Action framework restricts lending, branch expansion, and dividend distribution for banks breaching capital, asset quality, or leverage thresholds, ensuring early supervisory intervention.

3.4 Financial Inclusion and Digital Payments Infrastructure

The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana, launched in August 2014, opened over fifty crore basic savings bank deposit accounts with zero balance, RuPay debit cards, and accidental insurance cover, dramatically expanding the formal financial footprint. The JAM trinity — Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar biometric identification, and mobile connectivity — enabled direct benefit transfers for subsidies like LPG (PAHAL), fertiliser, and scholarships, plugging leakages estimated at tens of thousands of crores annually. The Unified Payments Interface, developed by the National Payments Corporation of India and launched in 2016, revolutionised retail payments by allowing instant, interoperable, mobile-first fund transfers using virtual payment addresses. UPI transaction volumes crossed ten billion per month in 2023, making India the largest real-time payments market globally. The Bharat Bill Payment System, National Electronic Toll Collection, and the upcoming Central Bank Digital Currency pilots for wholesale and retail segments extend the digital stack. The RBI's Payments Vision 2025 targets a tenfold increase in digital transactions, zero-compromise security, and universal access, reinforcing the shift toward a less-cash economy.

4. Inflation Dynamics and Price Indices

4.1 Consumer Price Index and Wholesale Price Index Construction

The Consumer Price Index measures the average change over time in the prices paid by urban and rural households for a fixed basket of consumption goods and services. The current CPI Combined series with base 2012=100 covers six groups — food and beverages, pan tobacco and intoxicants, clothing and footwear, housing, fuel and light, and miscellaneous — with weights derived from the 2011-12 Consumer Expenditure Survey. Price data are collected from 1,181 village markets and 1,114 urban markets by the Field Operations Division of the National Statistical Office. The Wholesale Price Index tracks price movements at the first point of bulk sale for 697 items classified into primary articles, fuel and power, and manufactured products. The WPI does not include services, making it less representative of the overall consumption basket but more sensitive to global commodity cycles. The divergence between CPI and WPI inflation often widens when food prices surge or when service sector costs rise, because food carries a much higher weight in CPI and services are absent from WPI. The GDP deflator, implicitly derived from national accounts, remains the broadest measure but is available only with a lag.

4.2 Causes of Inflation: Demand Pull and Cost Push Factors

Demand-pull inflation arises when aggregate demand outstrips the economy's productive capacity, often fuelled by expansionary fiscal policy, credit booms, or export surges. Cost-push inflation originates from supply-side shocks such as adverse monsoons reducing agricultural output, international crude oil price spikes raising transport and input costs, or currency depreciation increasing the rupee price of imports. India's inflation history shows a persistent role for food supply shocks — onion, tomato, and pulse cycles — which are inherently seasonal and difficult for monetary policy to address because they reflect supply constraints rather than excess demand. The RBI's inflation targeting framework focuses on headline CPI, which includes volatile food and fuel, rather than core inflation that excludes them, because households cannot avoid these essentials. However, the Monetary Policy Committee monitors core inflation to gauge the persistence of price pressures and the risk of second-round effects where food inflation spills into wage demands and broader price setting. Structural rigidities in agricultural marketing, storage, and logistics amplify supply-side volatility, making inflation management a joint responsibility of monetary and supply-side policies.

4.3 Impact on Railway Operations and Household Welfare

Rising inflation affects Indian Railways through multiple channels. Higher diesel and electricity costs increase traction expenditure, which forms a significant share of working expenses. Wage and pension bills, indexed to dearness allowance linked to the All India Consumer Price Index for Industrial Workers (CPI-IW), escalate automatically with inflation, squeezing the operating ratio — the ratio of working expenses to gross earnings. Capital expenditure on track renewal, signalling, and rolling stock faces cost overruns when steel, cement, and machinery prices rise, potentially delaying safety-critical projects. On the revenue side, freight rates are periodically rationalised to reflect input costs, but passenger fares, especially in suburban and second-class segments, are often kept below cost for social reasons, widening the cross-subsidisation burden on freight. For households, high food inflation erodes real wages, reduces discretionary spending, and forces lower-income families to cut nutritional intake, with long-term human capital consequences. The government's buffer stock operations under the Food Corporation of India and open market sales aim to stabilise cereal prices, while the Price Stabilisation Fund targets pulses and onions.

4.4 Monetary Policy Response and Supply Side Interventions

When CPI inflation breaches the upper tolerance band of six percent, the MPC typically raises the repo rate, increases the cash reserve ratio, or conducts variable rate reverse repo auctions to drain liquidity, transmitting higher rates to bank lending and deposit rates. The transmission lag varies; the external benchmark lending rate system introduced in 2019, linking retail loans to the repo rate or treasury bill yields, has accelerated pass-through compared to the earlier marginal cost of funds based lending rate regime. On the supply side, the government deploys trade policy tools — reducing import duties on edible oils, pulses, and wheat, restricting exports of wheat and broken rice, and imposing stock limits on traders under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 — to augment domestic availability. The Agriculture Marketing Infrastructure Fund and the formation of Farmer Producer Organisations aim to reduce intermediation costs. The RBI's annual report and the Economic Survey analyse the relative contributions of demand and supply factors, guiding the policy mix. Credible inflation targeting has anchored expectations, as seen in the decline of household inflation expectations in the RBI's surveys, which lowers the sacrifice ratio — the output loss required to reduce inflation.

5. Railway Finances and Economic Role

5.1 Historical Evolution and Separation of Railway Budget

Indian Railways, nationalised in 1951 by integrating forty-two separate railway systems, operated with a separate budget from 1924 until 2016-17, a practice originating from the Acworth Committee report of 1921 which recommended financial autonomy for the then commercially oriented railway companies. The separate budget allowed detailed parliamentary scrutiny of railway finances but created a siloed approach where capital allocation, tariff setting, and investment planning were disconnected from the overall macroeconomic framework. The merger, recommended by the Bibek Debroy Committee in 2015 and implemented by the NITI Aayog, integrated railway finances into the General Budget, enabling a unified view of transport sector priorities, better alignment with national development goals, and access to the central government's borrowing capacity without separate market loans. The Railway Convention Committee, a parliamentary panel, continues to examine the rate of dividend payable by railways to general revenues, a legacy mechanism now replaced by a gross budgetary support model where the central government provides capital grants and the railways retain their surplus for reinvestment.

5.2 Revenue Sources: Freight, Passenger and Sundry Earnings

Railway revenue originates primarily from freight traffic, which contributes roughly sixty-five to seventy percent of total earnings despite carrying a smaller share of traffic volume, because freight rates are priced to cross-subsidise passenger services. Coal, iron ore, cement, foodgrains, and fertiliser constitute the core commodity basket, with coal alone accounting for nearly forty percent of freight loading by weight. Passenger earnings, though lower per unit, provide essential social connectivity; the introduction of dynamic pricing for premium trains like Rajdhani, Shatabdi, and Vande Bharat, and the flexi-fare system, attempt to capture willingness to pay on high-demand routes. Sundry earnings include parcel traffic, catering, advertising, station redevelopment monetisation, and land licensing. The Railways' market share in freight transport has declined from over eighty percent at independence to around twenty-seven percent currently, lost largely to road transport due to faster transit times, door-to-door service, and competitive pricing for non-bulk commodities. Regaining share is a central objective of the National Rail Plan 2030.

5.3 Capital Expenditure, Operating Ratio and Modernisation

Capital expenditure, funded through gross budgetary support, internal resources, and extra-budgetary resources like Indian Railway Finance Corporation bonds, has risen sharply, crossing ₹2.5 lakh crore in recent budgets. Major heads include new lines, gauge conversion, doubling, rolling stock procurement, signalling and telecommunications, and safety works like track renewal and elimination of unmanned level crossings, achieved in 2019. The operating ratio, which peaked at 98.4 percent in 2017-18, improved to around 96 percent as earnings growth outpaced expenditure growth, though the pandemic caused a temporary deterioration. A lower operating ratio signals greater surplus for debt servicing and reinvestment. The Dedicated Freight Corridor Corporation of India, a special purpose vehicle, is constructing the Eastern and Western Dedicated Freight Corridors totalling 2,843 route kilometres, funded partly by Japanese and World Bank loans, to segregate freight from passenger traffic, increase average speeds from 25 kmph to 70 kmph, and enable heavier axle loads. Electrification of the broad gauge network, targeting net zero carbon emissions by 2030, has covered over ninety percent of routes, reducing diesel dependence and operating costs.

5.4 Public Private Partnerships and Dedicated Freight Corridors

Private participation in railways has evolved from the Build-Operate-Transfer model for port connectivity lines to the current framework inviting private operators to run passenger trains on selected clusters under a haulage charge and revenue share model, with the first tenders awarded in 2021. Station redevelopment under the Indian Railway Stations Development Corporation leverages commercial development of air space over stations to finance modern terminals, with Habibganj (Rani Kamlapati) and Gandhinagar Capital as early examples. The National High Speed Rail Corporation is executing the Mumbai-Ahmedabad High Speed Rail corridor with Japanese Shinkansen technology and a soft loan from JICA covering eighty percent of the project cost. Logistics parks, multi-modal cargo terminals, and the PM Gati Shakti national master plan integrate rail with road, waterways, and airports to reduce logistics costs from the current thirteen to fourteen percent of GDP toward the global benchmark of eight percent. The Railways' role in moving bulk commodities at low energy intensity per tonne-kilometre makes it indispensable for the country's climate commitments under the Paris Agreement and for the competitiveness of core sectors like steel, power, and cement.

6. External Sector and Balance of Payments

6.1 Current Account Components and Trade Balance

The current account of the balance of payments records the flow of goods, services, primary income, and secondary income between residents and non-residents. The merchandise trade balance, the difference between exports and imports of goods, has historically been in deficit for India because imports of crude oil, gold, electronic components, and machinery exceed exports of petroleum products, gems and jewellery, pharmaceuticals, and engineering goods. The services trade balance, however, runs a robust surplus driven by software exports, business process outsourcing, and travel receipts, partially offsetting the goods deficit. Primary income records investment income — interest, dividends, and reinvested earnings — on foreign assets and liabilities; India typically shows a net outflow due to larger foreign portfolio and direct investment liabilities than overseas assets. Secondary income, dominated by private remittances from the Indian diaspora, is the largest single credit item in the current account, often exceeding $100 billion annually, making India the top remittance recipient globally. A current account deficit financed by stable capital inflows like foreign direct investment is considered sustainable, whereas reliance on volatile portfolio flows creates vulnerability to sudden stops.

6.2 Capital Account Flows and Foreign Exchange Reserves

The capital account captures changes in ownership of financial assets and liabilities. Foreign direct investment, which implies lasting interest and control, is preferred for its stability and technology spillovers; the government has liberalised FDI limits in defence, insurance, telecom, and retail, allowing up to hundred percent under the automatic route in most sectors. Foreign portfolio investment in equity and debt markets is more sensitive to global risk appetite and interest rate differentials; the RBI manages volatility through the voluntary retention route for debt and by adjusting the investment limits for foreign portfolio investors in government and corporate bonds. External commercial borrowings, trade credit, and non-resident deposits under the Foreign Currency Non-Resident (FCNR) and Non-Resident External (NRE) schemes supplement capital inflows. Foreign exchange reserves, held in foreign currency assets, gold, special drawing rights, and the reserve tranche position at the IMF, crossed $600 billion in 2021 and act as a buffer against external shocks, providing import cover of over ten months. The RBI intervenes in the foreign exchange market to smooth excessive volatility without targeting a specific level, adhering to a managed float regime.

6.3 Exchange Rate Management and Rupee Convertibility

India follows a market-determined exchange rate system where the rupee's value against other currencies is shaped by demand and supply in the interbank foreign exchange market, with the RBI intervening occasionally to prevent disorderly movements. The Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System, introduced in 1992-93, replaced the fixed parity regime and established current account convertibility, allowing free conversion for trade, travel, education, and medical expenses. Capital account convertibility remains partial: residents can invest abroad up to $250,000 per financial year under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, and corporates can undertake overseas direct investment subject to prudential limits, but restrictions persist on speculative capital flows, rupee-denominated borrowing abroad (masala bonds), and non-resident access to the domestic rupee debt market beyond specified limits. The Tarapore Committee reports (1997 and 2006) laid out a roadmap for fuller capital account convertibility contingent on fiscal consolidation, inflation control, banking sector strength, and adequate reserves. The rupee's real effective exchange rate, adjusted for inflation differentials with trading partners, is monitored to assess export competitiveness; an overvalued REER hurts exports while an undervalued one fuels imported inflation.

Fiscal Federalism and Centre‑State Financial Relations

The Constitution divides financial powers between the Union and the States through three lists in the Seventh Schedule. The Union levies taxes on income (except agricultural income), customs, excise on goods manufactured or produced, and services tax (now subsumed under GST). States retain powers over land revenue, stamp duty, state excise on alcohol, and taxes on agricultural income. The Goods and Services Tax, introduced in 2017, created a concurrent tax base administered by the GST Council, where the Union holds one‑third of voting weight and States two‑thirds, requiring a three‑fourths majority for decisions. This institutional design makes the Council the primary forum for resolving rate changes, exemption lists, and compensation disputes.

Vertical devolution — the share of the divisible pool of central taxes transferred to States — is determined by the Finance Commission every five years. The Fifteenth Finance Commission (2020‑25) recommended a 41 % vertical share, down from 42 % under the Fourteenth Commission, citing the Union’s enhanced fiscal responsibilities after GST. The horizontal distribution formula assigns weights to population (15 % for 1971 census, 12.5 % for 2011 census), area, forest cover, income distance, and demographic performance. States with larger populations and lower per‑capita incomes receive higher shares, creating a redistributive bias that often triggers political debate.

States’ borrowing limits are anchored in Article 293(3), which requires Union consent for market borrowings if a State has outstanding loans from the Centre. The FRBM Act extensions to States prescribe a fiscal deficit ceiling of 3 % of GSDP, with an additional 0.5 % conditional on power sector reforms. During the COVID‑19 pandemic, the Centre allowed States to borrow up to 5 % of GSDP under the “special window” and facilitated back‑to‑back loans for GST compensation shortfalls, which were later converted into grants. These episodes illustrate how emergency provisions can temporarily relax the federal fiscal architecture.

Persistent friction points include the GST compensation cess shortfall, the treatment of cess and surcharge revenues (which are not part of the divisible pool), and the Centre’s use of centrally sponsored schemes to influence State priorities. The Supreme Court’s 2022 judgement in the State of Tamil Nadu v. Union of India case upheld the GST Council’s recommendatory nature, reinforcing that States cannot legally compel the Union to compensate beyond the statutory five‑year period. Understanding these dynamics is essential for analysing inter‑governmental fiscal negotiations in exam questions.

Public Debt Management and Debt Sustainability

India’s public debt comprises internal liabilities (market borrowings, treasury bills, small savings, provident funds, and special securities issued to the RBI) and external liabilities (multilateral and bilateral loans, sovereign bonds, and NRI deposits). The Centre’s gross debt‑to‑GDP ratio stood around 58 % in 2023‑24, while the combined Centre‑State ratio approached 90 %, a level that constrains fiscal space and raises sustainability concerns. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, as amended in 2018, sets a medium‑term target of 40 % debt‑to‑GDP for the Centre and 20 % for States, with a fiscal deficit anchor of 3 % of GDP. The Act includes an “escape clause” allowing a deviation of up to 0.5 % of GDP in cases of national security, calamity, or structural reforms; this clause was invoked in 2020‑21 to accommodate a 9.5 % fiscal deficit.

Market borrowings are conducted through dated securities (dated G‑Secs) and treasury bills (91‑day, 182‑day, 364‑day). The RBI acts as the debt manager under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934, and the Government Securities Act, 2006. The issuance calendar is announced quarterly, with a focus on lengthening the maturity profile to reduce rollover risk; the weighted average maturity of central government debt has been extended beyond 10 years in recent years. The Ways and Means Advances (WMA) facility provides temporary liquidity to the Centre and States, with separate normal and special WMA limits linked to the previous year’s revenue receipts.

Debt sustainability analysis (DSA) by the IMF and the RBI evaluates solvency (debt‑to‑GDP trajectory) and liquidity (gross financing needs). Key indicators include the interest‑growth differential (r‑g), primary deficit, and the share of short‑term debt. A favourable r‑g (nominal GDP growth exceeding the average interest rate) helps stabilise the debt ratio even with primary deficits. The 2023‑24 Budget introduced a “debt management strategy” document outlining a target to reduce the Centre’s debt‑to‑GDP to 50 % by 2030‑31 through a combination of nominal GDP growth, primary surplus generation, and asset monetisation proceeds.

State Development Loans (SDLs) are issued by State governments in the same auction platform as central securities. The RBI has encouraged States to adopt a “single borrowing window” and to lengthen maturities. The 15th Finance Commission recommended a “debt consolidation and relief” scheme for highly indebted States, linking additional borrowing space to fiscal consolidation milestones. Monitoring the composition of debt — fixed versus floating rate, rupee versus foreign currency — is critical because exchange rate depreciation can sharply increase the rupee value of external debt servicing.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Which of the following statements about the FRBM Act escape clause is correct? (a) It can be invoked only once in a five‑year period. (b) It permits a deviation of up to 1 % of GDP. (c) It was invoked in 2020‑21 due to the COVID‑19 pandemic. (d) It requires Parliamentary approval by a two‑thirds majority.”
The correct answer is (c). The escape clause allows a deviation of up to 0.5 % of GDP (not 1 %) and can be invoked in circumstances such as national security, calamity, or structural reforms. It was indeed invoked in 2020‑21 to accommodate the pandemic‑induced fiscal expansion. Parliamentary approval is by simple majority through a statutory resolution, not a two‑thirds majority. The clause does not limit the number of invocations over a five‑year cycle.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mixes FRBM targets with Finance Commission recommendations, separate the two frameworks: FRBM is a statutory rule for the Centre (and extended to States), while the Finance Commission is a constitutional body determining vertical/horizontal shares. Memorise the latest numbers — 3 % fiscal deficit, 40 %/20 % debt‑to‑GDP, 41 % vertical devolution — and the escape clause trigger events. A quick mental table of “Act vs Commission” prevents confusion.

Monetary Policy Transmission and Interest Rate Corridor

The Reserve Bank of India’s Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), constituted in 2016 under the amended RBI Act, targets consumer price inflation at 4 % with a tolerance band of ±2 %. The policy repo rate is the anchor; the reverse repo rate, marginal standing facility (MSF) rate, and standing deposit facility (SDF) rate form a symmetric corridor around the repo rate. As of the latest policy stance, the repo rate stands at 6.50 %, the SDF at 6.25 %, the reverse repo at 3.35 % (though the reverse repo is currently not the operative floor), and the MSF at 6.75 %. This corridor ensures that overnight interbank rates (the weighted average call rate) remain close to the repo rate, signalling the monetary stance to the broader financial system.

Transmission from the policy rate to bank lending rates has evolved through three regimes. Until 2010, banks used the Benchmark Prime Lending Rate (BPLR), which was opaque and slow to adjust. The Base Rate system (2010‑2016) improved transparency but still allowed banks to lend below the base rate to select borrowers. The Marginal Cost of Funds based Lending Rate (MCLR), introduced in April 2016, linked lending rates to the marginal cost of deposits and mandated monthly reset. In October 2019, the External Benchmark Lending Rate (EBLR) regime replaced MCLR for new floating‑rate loans, requiring banks to adopt an external benchmark — repo rate, 91‑day T‑bill yield, or 182‑day T‑bill yield — with a spread determined by credit risk. This shift accelerated pass‑through; empirical studies by the RBI show that the median pass‑through to fresh rupee loans rose from about 40 % under MCLR to over 70 % under EBLR within six months of a policy change.

Liquidity management tools complement the rate corridor. The Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) includes overnight repo and reverse repo auctions, variable rate repo/reverse repo (VRRR/VRRR) for longer tenors, and the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) introduced in April 2022 to absorb surplus liquidity without collateral. Open Market Operations (OMOs) — outright purchase or sale of government securities — and the Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS) are used for durable liquidity injection or absorption. The RBI’s “liquidity framework” aims to keep the system in a modest deficit (net injection) so that the overnight rate trades near the repo rate, enhancing the effectiveness of policy signalling.

Transmission to the real economy operates through four channels: interest rate, credit, asset price, and exchange rate. The interest rate channel affects investment and consumption via changes in borrowing costs. The credit channel depends on bank balance‑sheet strength; high non‑performing assets can dampen lending response. Asset price channel works through equity and real estate valuations influencing wealth and collateral. The exchange rate channel operates via capital flows; a higher policy rate attracts portfolio inflows, appreciating the rupee and affecting net exports. The RBI’s “forward guidance” — qualitative statements on the future path of rates — shapes market expectations and reduces term premia, further aiding transmission.

Financial Sector Regulation and Resolution Framework

India’s financial architecture is overseen by multiple regulators: the Reserve Bank of India (banking and payment systems), the Securities and Exchange Board of India (securities markets), the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (insurance), and the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (pension). The Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC), chaired by the Finance Minister, coordinates macro‑prudential oversight, financial literacy, and inter‑regulatory issues. The FSDC’s Sub‑Committee, headed by the RBI Governor, monitors systemic risks and recommends counter‑cyclical capital buffers, sectoral exposure limits, and stress‑testing frameworks.

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016, created a unified, time‑bound resolution process for corporate debtors, partnership firms, and individuals. The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and its appellate body (NCLAT) adjudicate cases, while Insolvency Professionals (IPs) manage the resolution process. The Code mandates a 180‑day resolution window, extendable by 90 days, with a maximum of 330 days including litigation. Since inception, the IBC has resolved over 500 cases, recovering approximately ₹3.5 lakh crore for creditors, though haircuts average 60‑70 % due to asset deterioration during delays. The 2020 amendment introduced a “pre‑packaged” resolution scheme for MSMEs, allowing a debtor‑in‑possession model with a 120‑day timeline.

To address the legacy stock of stressed assets, the government established the National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited (NARCL) in 2021, backed by a ₹30,600 crore government guarantee. NARCL acquires bad loans from banks at a discount, issues security receipts, and manages resolution through an asset management company (India Debt Resolution Company Ltd). The “bad bank” structure aims to clean bank balance sheets, improve credit flow, and reduce the gross NPA ratio, which fell from a peak of 11.2 % in 2018 to below 4 % in 2023. Asset Reconstruction Companies (ARCs) continue to operate alongside NARCL, but their recovery rates remain modest due to legal bottlenecks and valuation disputes.

Macro‑prudential tools deployed by the RBI include the Counter‑Cyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB), which can be activated when credit‑to‑GDP gaps exceed thresholds; sectoral capital requirements for real estate and capital market exposures; and the Large Exposure Framework limiting a bank’s exposure to a single counterparty to 20 % of Tier‑1 capital (25 % for interconnected entities). The RBI also mandates a Leverage Ratio of 3.5 % for domestic systemically important banks (D‑SIBs) and conducts annual stress tests covering credit, market, and liquidity shocks. These measures, combined with the prompt corrective action (PCA) framework for weak banks, form a layered defence against systemic crises.

Infrastructure Financing and Asset Monetisation

India’s infrastructure gap — estimated at over ₹100 lakh crore for the 2020‑2025 period — is being bridged through a mix of budgetary support, institutional financing, and private capital. The National Investment and Infrastructure Fund (NIIF), created in 2015 as a sovereign‑backed fund, operates three verticals: Master Fund (core infrastructure), Fund of Funds (investing in other infrastructure funds), and Strategic Opportunities Fund (greenfield projects). NIIF’s corpus exceeds ₹40,000 crore, with the government holding 49 % and domestic and international investors the remainder. It has invested in ports, logistics, renewable energy, and digital infrastructure, often co‑investing with multilateral development banks.

Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs) and Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) provide a yield‑oriented vehicle for monetising operational assets. InvITs, regulated by SEBI since 2014, hold income‑generating infrastructure assets (roads, power transmission, pipelines) and distribute at least 90 % of net distributable cash flows to unit holders. The first InvIT, IRB InvIT Fund, listed in 2017; by 2023, over a dozen InvITs had raised ₹1.2 lakh crore. REITs, introduced in 2014, focus on commercial real estate; Embassy Office Parks REIT and Mindspace Business Parks REIT are prominent listings. Both structures enjoy tax pass‑through status, making them attractive for pension and insurance funds seeking long‑dated cash flows.

The National Monetisation Pipeline (NMP), announced in 2021, targets ₹6 lakh crore of asset monetisation over four years (2022‑25) across sectors: roads (₹1.6 lakh crore), railways (₹1.5 lakh crore), power (₹1.3 lakh crore), telecom, warehousing, and mining. The pipeline uses models such as Toll‑Operate‑Transfer (TOT) for highways, Infrastructure Investment Trusts for power transmission, and long‑term leasing for railway stations and land parcels. The Railway Ministry’s “Station Redevelopment” programme leverages public‑private partnerships (PPP) on a Design‑Build‑Finance‑Operate‑Transfer (DBFOT) basis, with 125 stations identified for redevelopment. The Dedicated Freight Corridor Corporation (DFCCIL) finances the Eastern and Western DFCs through a blend of GBS, IRFC bonds, and multilateral loans, aiming to decongest the existing network and raise freight share to 45 %.

Green financing has gained prominence with the issuance of sovereign green bonds in 2022‑23 (₹16,000 crore) and 2023‑24 (₹20,000 crore), earmarked for renewable energy, energy efficiency, pollution prevention, and climate adaptation. The RBI’s “Framework for Acceptance of Green Deposits” (2023) enables banks to raise green deposits and allocate them to eligible green projects, with third‑party verification. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) has mandated Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) for the top 1,000 listed entities, aligning disclosures with global ESG standards. These initiatives collectively aim to lower the cost of capital for sustainable infrastructure and meet India’s Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement.

Goods and Services Tax Council: Decision‑Making, Dispute Resolution and Revenue Sharing

The GST Council, constituted under Article 279A of the Constitution, is the apex federal body that determines tax rates, exemptions, threshold limits and the model GST law. It comprises the Union Finance Minister as Chairperson, the Union Minister of State for Finance, and one minister nominated by each state government. Decisions are taken by a three‑fourths majority of the weighted votes: the Centre holds one‑third of the total voting weight, while the states collectively hold two‑thirds, with each state’s weight proportional to its share of the total GST revenue. This voting architecture ensures that no single jurisdiction can unilaterally impose a rate change, but it also means that a coalition of states representing more than 50 % of the weighted vote can block a proposal even if the Centre supports it.

The Council meets at least once a quarter, though extraordinary sessions are convened for urgent matters such as the COVID‑19 pandemic relief measures. Its recommendations are not legally binding on Parliament or state legislatures; however, the Constitution mandates that the Union and the states “shall” enact laws in accordance with the Council’s recommendations. In practice, the Centre and states have uniformly adopted the Council’s decisions, and any deviation would trigger a constitutional crisis. The Council also oversees the GST Compensation Cess, which was levied on sin goods and luxury items to fund the five‑year compensation guarantee to states for revenue loss arising from GST implementation. The cess collection period ended in June 2022, and the Council subsequently recommended a transition to a “revenue neutral” rate structure, leading to the rationalisation of several rate slabs in 2023.

Dispute resolution under the GST regime operates at two levels. First, the Council itself can adjudicate disagreements between the Centre and a state or among states on the interpretation of GST provisions. Second, the GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT), established under Section 109 of the Central GST Act, 2017, hears appeals against orders of the Appellate Authority. The Tribunal consists of a judicial member (a former High Court judge) and a technical member (an officer of the Indian Revenue Service). As of 2024, the Tribunal’s benches are functional in New Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai and Hyderabad, with additional benches planned. The Supreme Court retains appellate jurisdiction over GSTAT decisions under Article 136.

Revenue sharing between the Centre and states follows the dual GST model: Central GST (CGST) and State GST (SGST) are levied on intra‑state supplies, while Integrated GST (IGST) applies to inter‑state supplies and imports. IGST revenue is apportioned between the origin and destination states based on the place of supply rules. The Centre retains the CGST component, while the SGST component accrues to the destination state. The Finance Commission’s vertical devolution formula (41 % of the divisible pool to states for the 2021‑26 period) operates on the net proceeds of CGST and IGST after deducting the Centre’s share of the compensation cess. This interplay between the GST Council’s rate decisions and the Finance Commission’s devolution formula creates a feedback loop: a rate cut reduces the divisible pool, which in turn lowers the absolute transfer to states, even though the percentage share remains unchanged.

Direct Benefit Transfer Architecture: Targeting Efficiency, Aadhaar Integration and Fiscal Impact

The Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) programme, launched in 2013, aims to channel subsidies and welfare payments directly into beneficiaries’ bank accounts, eliminating intermediaries and leakages. The architecture rests on three pillars: a unique identification database (Aadhaar), a unified payments interface (UPI) and the Public Financial Management System (PFMS) for real‑time fund tracking. As of March 2024, over 1.3 billion Aadhaar numbers have been issued, covering more than 99 % of the adult population, and more than 1.1 billion bank accounts have been linked to Aadhaar, enabling seamless credit of benefits.

Targeting efficiency is achieved through a combination of inclusion and exclusion filters. Inclusion filters use socio‑economic caste census (SECC) data, the National Food Security Act (NFSA) beneficiary lists, and state‑specific eligibility criteria. Exclusion filters remove duplicate entries, deceased persons, and those exceeding income thresholds. The “JAM trinity” — Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar, and Mobile connectivity — reduces the cost of last‑mile delivery from an estimated 15‑20 % of subsidy value under the old Public Distribution System to under 2 % for DBT‑enabled schemes such as the Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM‑KISAN) and the LPG subsidy (PAHAL).

Fiscal impact assessments by the Controller General of Accounts (CGA) indicate that DBT saved the exchequer approximately ₹1.75 lakh crore between 2014‑15 and 2022‑23 by curbing diversion and ghost beneficiaries. The PM‑KISAN scheme alone transferred ₹2.2 lakh crore to 11.5 crore farmer families in FY 2023‑24. The PAHAL scheme, which moved LPG subsidy from a price‑administered mechanism to a direct cash transfer, reduced the subsidy bill from ₹30,000 crore in 2014‑15 to ₹12,000 crore in 2022‑23 while maintaining household consumption levels.

Challenges remain in the form of authentication failures, biometric mismatches, and the exclusion of marginalised groups lacking Aadhaar enrolment. The Supreme Court’s 2018 judgment in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India upheld the constitutional validity of Aadhaar but mandated that it cannot be made mandatory for services not backed by law. Consequently, the government introduced the “Aadhaar‑optional” route for certain schemes, allowing alternative identity documents. The PFMS dashboard now provides scheme‑wise, state‑wise, and district‑wise disbursement data, enabling real‑time monitoring and audit trails.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Which of the following statements about the GST Council’s voting weight is correct? (a) The Centre holds 50 % of the total voting weight. (b) States collectively hold two‑thirds of the voting weight. (c) Each state has an equal vote irrespective of revenue share. (d) Decisions require a simple majority.”
The correct answer is (b). Under Article 279A, the Centre’s voting weight is one‑third and the states together hold two‑thirds, with each state’s weight proportional to its share of GST revenue. Decisions require a three‑fourths majority of the weighted votes, not a simple majority. Option (a) overstates the Centre’s share, (c) ignores the revenue‑proportional weighting, and (d) misstates the required majority.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the exact voting fractions (1/3 Centre, 2/3 states) and the three‑fourths majority rule. When a question mentions “simple majority” or “equal state votes”, flag it as incorrect. For DBT, recall the JAM trinity components and the approximate leakage reduction (from ~15‑20 % to <2 %). Use the fiscal saving figure ₹1.75 lakh crore as a quick reference for magnitude‑based questions.

Railway Tariff Structure: Cross‑Subsidisation, Dynamic Pricing and Freight‑Passenger Revenue Balance

Indian Railways operates a dual‑revenue model where freight earnings historically cross‑subsidise passenger services. In FY 2023‑24, freight contributed roughly 65 % of total revenue (₹1.55 lakh crore) while passenger earnings accounted for about 28 % (₹0.66 lakh crore). The remaining share comes from sundry earnings such as parcel, catering and advertising. The freight tariff is determined by the Railway Board through the “Freight Tariff Schedule”, which classifies commodities into 20‑odd groups with distance‑based slab rates. Passenger fares are set under the “Passenger Fare Schedule”, with separate structures for suburban, non‑suburban, mail/express and premium trains (e.g., Rajdhani, Shatabdi, Vande Bharat).

Cross‑subsidisation is quantified by the “Operating Ratio” (OR), defined as total working expenses divided by total revenue. An OR of 100 % indicates break‑even; Indian Railways’ OR has hovered around 96‑98 % in recent years, implying that freight surplus barely covers passenger deficits. The 2022‑23 Annual Statistical Statement shows freight OR at 84 % (profitable) while passenger OR stood at 115 % (loss‑making). To reduce this imbalance, the Railways introduced “Dynamic Pricing” for premium trains in 2015, allowing fares to vary up to 1.5 times the base fare based on demand, booking window and occupancy. The scheme was extended to select mail/express services in 2021, with a cap of 1.3 times the base fare.

Freight rationalisation measures include the “Containerised Freight” initiative, the “Automobile Freight Train Operator” (AFTO) scheme, and the “General Purpose Wagon Investment Scheme” (GPWIS) which incentivises private wagon ownership. The Dedicated Freight Corridors (DFCs) — Eastern and Western — are expected to lower the average freight transit time by 30‑40 % and reduce the cost per tonne‑kilometre by 15‑20 %, thereby improving freight competitiveness against road transport. The Railways also launched the “Freight Business Development Portal” in 2022 to provide real‑time slot availability, tariff calculators and e‑booking, aiming to capture a larger share of the high‑value, time‑sensitive cargo market.

On the passenger side, the “Utsonmobile” app and the “IRCTC” platform enable dynamic fare adjustments, while the “Rail Madad” grievance redressal system monitors service quality. The 2023‑24 budget announced a “Passenger Revenue Enhancement Plan” targeting a 10 % increase in passenger earnings over five years through premium service expansion, station redevelopment (commercial exploitation of real estate), and non‑fare revenue streams such as branding and advertising. The plan also proposes a gradual rationalisation of suburban fares, which have remained largely unchanged since 2012, to reflect operational costs while protecting low‑income commuters through targeted subsidies.

Macroprudential Framework: Systemic Risk Monitoring, Countercyclical Capital Buffers and the Financial Stability and Development Council

India’s macroprudential architecture is anchored by the Financial Stability and Development Council (FSDC), chaired by the Union Finance Minister and comprising the RBI Governor, heads of SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA, and the Ministry of Finance’s Department of Economic Affairs. The FSDC’s mandate includes monitoring systemic risks, coordinating inter‑regulatory actions, and fostering financial sector development. It meets at least twice a year and can convene emergency sessions. The Council’s Sub‑Committee, chaired by the RBI Governor, operationalises macroprudential policies such as the Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB), sectoral capital requirements, and limits on loan‑to‑value (LTV) ratios for real estate exposures.

The CCyB, introduced under the Basel III framework, allows the RBI to require banks to hold additional Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital in the range of 0‑2.5 % of risk‑weighted assets when credit growth outpaces GDP growth, signalling excessive exuberance. As of March 2024, the RBI has kept the CCyB at 0 % but has signalled readiness to activate it if the credit‑to‑GDP gap exceeds the 2 % threshold for two consecutive quarters. The RBI also employs sector‑specific tools: a 75 % risk weight for residential mortgages with LTV ≤ 80 %, 100 % for LTV > 80 %, and a 125 % risk weight for commercial real estate exposures. These measures aim to curb asset‑price bubbles without tightening monetary policy broadly.

Systemic risk monitoring relies on a suite of indicators compiled in the RBI’s “Financial Stability Report” (FSR), published bi‑annually. Key metrics include the credit‑to‑GDP gap, the debt‑service ratio of households and corporates, the leverage ratio of non‑bank financial companies (NBFCs), and the interconnectedness matrix capturing exposures among banks, NBFCs, mutual funds and insurance companies. The 2023 FSR highlighted a rise in NBFC leverage to 6.8 × (from 6.2 × in 2021) and an increase in the share of unsecured personal loans in bank credit to 12 % (from 9 % in 2019), prompting the RBI to issue draft guidelines on tighter underwriting standards for unsecured lending.

The FSDC also coordinates resolution planning for systemically important financial institutions (SIFIs). The RBI’s “Prompt Corrective Action” (PCA) framework, revised in 2021, triggers restrictions on dividend distribution, branch expansion and management compensation when a bank’s CET1 ratio falls below 7 %, Tier 1 ratio below 8.5 % or leverage ratio below 3.5 %. The National Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd (NARCL) and the India Debt Resolution Company Ltd (IDRCL) constitute the “bad bank” structure, with NARCL acquiring stressed assets at a discount and IDRCL managing resolution. As of December 2023, NARCL had acquired ₹0.9 lakh crore of stressed assets, with a recovery rate of approximately 35 % on resolved accounts.

Central Bank Digital Currency Pilot: Design Choices, Wholesale vs Retail Models and Implications for Monetary Transmission

The Reserve Bank of India launched the Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) pilot in two phases: the wholesale segment (e₹‑W) in November 2022 and the retail segment (e₹‑R) in December 2022. The wholesale pilot involves select banks settling inter‑bank transactions and government securities trades on a permissioned distributed ledger, aiming to reduce settlement risk and operational costs. The retail pilot targets person‑to‑person (P2P) and person‑to‑merchant (P2M) payments in a closed user group across four cities — Mumbai, New Delhi, Bengaluru and Bhubaneswar — with plans to expand to 15 cities by end‑2024.

Design choices differentiate the two models. e₹‑W is account‑based, accessible only to regulated financial institutions, and bears interest at the prevailing repo rate, making it a direct substitute for reserve balances. e₹‑R is token‑based, held in digital wallets provided by participating banks, and does not carry interest, preserving the existing monetary policy transmission channel through bank deposits. Both forms are legal tender, denominated in rupees, and fully backed by the RBI’s balance sheet. The token architecture uses a hybrid model: the RBI issues and redeems tokens, while commercial banks handle distribution, KYC compliance and wallet management.

Implications for monetary transmission are significant. In the wholesale model, the ability to settle securities instantly on a 24×7 basis could compress the term premium on government bonds, lowering the cost of sovereign borrowing. For the retail model, widespread adoption could reduce the demand for physical cash, lowering the currency‑in‑circulation to GDP ratio (currently around 14 %). However, if a large share of household savings migrates from bank deposits to non‑interest‑bearing CBDC wallets, banks’ funding base may shrink, potentially raising the marginal cost of funds and dampening credit creation. The RBI has therefore capped individual wallet holdings at ₹1 lakh during the pilot and limited merchant acceptance to QR‑code based transactions to monitor disintermediation effects.

Cross‑border interoperability is being explored through the “Project Dunbar” collaboration with the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) and the central banks of Australia, Malaysia, Singapore and South Africa. The project tests a multi‑CBDC platform for real‑time, low‑cost foreign exchange settlement, which could reduce India’s reliance on correspondent banking networks and lower remittance costs (currently averaging 5‑6 % for inward remittances). The RBI’s 2023 “Concept Note on CBDC” outlines a phased roadmap: pilot evaluation (2022‑24), regulatory framework finalisation (2024‑25), and potential full‑scale launch contingent on financial stability assessment, cyber‑resilience testing, and public acceptance metrics.
