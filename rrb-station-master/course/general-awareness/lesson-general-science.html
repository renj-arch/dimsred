1. Foundations of Physics

1.1 Motion, Force and Newton’s Laws

Physics begins with the description of how objects move and why they change their state of motion. The concept of inertia, first quantified by Galileo and later formalised by Isaac Newton, tells us that a body at rest stays at rest and a body in uniform motion continues in a straight line unless acted upon by a net external force. Newton’s first law therefore defines the inertial frame of reference that underpins all subsequent mechanics. The second law introduces the proportionality between force, mass and acceleration, expressed as F = m a, where force is measured in newtons, mass in kilograms and acceleration in metres per second squared. This relationship makes it possible to predict the motion of a particle when the forces acting on it are known. The third law states that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction, which explains phenomena such as rocket propulsion and the recoil of a firearm. Together these three laws form a closed logical system that can be applied to everything from a falling apple to the orbital motion of planets.

1.2 Work, Energy and Power

When a force displaces an object, work is done. The scalar product of force and displacement gives the work done, measured in joules. Energy, the capacity to do work, appears in many forms: kinetic energy (½ m v²), gravitational potential energy (m g h), elastic potential energy (½ k x²) and internal energy associated with the microscopic motion of particles. The principle of conservation of energy asserts that in an isolated system the total energy remains constant, merely transforming from one form to another. Power, defined as the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred, is measured in watts (joules per second). Understanding these concepts allows us to analyse machines, electrical circuits and thermodynamic cycles, all of which are built on the conversion of energy from one form to another.

1.3 Waves, Sound and Light

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy through a medium or through space without the net transport of matter. Mechanical waves such as sound require a material medium; they propagate as longitudinal compressions and rarefactions, and their speed depends on the medium’s elasticity and density. In air at 20 °C the speed of sound is approximately 343 m s⁻¹. Electromagnetic waves, by contrast, do not need a medium; they consist of oscillating electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation. Light is a narrow band of the electromagnetic spectrum, with wavelengths from about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red). The wave nature of light explains interference, diffraction and polarisation, while the particle nature, introduced by Einstein’s photoelectric effect, explains the quantised energy packets called photons, each carrying energy E = h ν where h is Planck’s constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s) and ν is the frequency. This dual description is the cornerstone of modern optics and quantum mechanics.

2. Core Concepts in Chemistry

2.1 Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table

Chemistry is built on the understanding that matter is composed of atoms, each consisting of a dense nucleus containing protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons in quantised energy levels. The number of protons, the atomic number Z, defines the element and its position in the periodic table. The periodic law, first recognised by Dmitri Mendeleev, states that the properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers. This arrangement groups elements with similar valence electron configurations into families, allowing prediction of chemical behaviour such as reactivity, ionisation energy and atomic radius. The modern table also reflects the filling order of electron subshells (s, p, d, f) which explains the block structure and the trends observed across periods and down groups.

2.2 Chemical Bonding and Molecular Geometry

Atoms achieve stability by attaining a noble‑gas electron configuration, which they do through the formation of chemical bonds. Ionic bonding occurs when electrons are transferred from a metal to a non‑metal, creating oppositely charged ions that attract each other in a crystal lattice; typical examples are sodium chloride and magnesium oxide. Covalent bonding involves the sharing of electron pairs between non‑metals, producing discrete molecules such as water (H₂O) and carbon dioxide (CO₂). Metallic bonding is characterised by a sea of delocalised electrons surrounding a lattice of positive metal ions, giving metals their conductivity and malleability. The shape of a molecule is dictated by the repulsion between electron pairs around the central atom, as described by the Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory; for instance, methane adopts a tetrahedral geometry with bond angles of 109.5°, while water is bent at about 104.5° because of two lone pairs.

2.3 States of Matter and Thermochemistry

Matter exists primarily in three states: solid, liquid and gas, distinguished by the degree of order and the freedom of particle motion. Phase transitions such as melting, boiling and sublimation involve the absorption or release of latent heat without a change in temperature. The first law of thermodynamics, an expression of energy conservation, states that the change in internal energy of a system equals the heat added to the system minus the work done by the system (ΔU = Q – W). Enthalpy (H = U + pV) is a convenient state function for constant‑pressure processes, and the enthalpy change of a reaction (ΔH) indicates whether it is exothermic (ΔH < 0) or endothermic (ΔH > 0). Hess’s law allows the calculation of ΔH for a reaction by summing the enthalpy changes of a series of steps that lead from reactants to products, reflecting the path‑independence of state functions.

Example Question
A 2 kg block is pushed across a horizontal surface with a constant force of 10 N. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the surface is 0.2. Calculate the acceleration of the block. (Take g = 9.8 m s⁻².)
The weight of the block is mg = 2 kg × 9.8 m s⁻² = 19.6 N. The normal reaction equals the weight because the surface is horizontal, so N = 19.6 N. The frictional force is f = μ N = 0.2 × 19.6 N = 3.92 N. The net horizontal force is the applied force minus friction: F_net = 10 N – 3.92 N = 6.08 N. Using Newton’s second law, a = F_net / m = 6.08 N / 2 kg = 3.04 m s⁻². Therefore the block accelerates at 3.04 m s⁻².
Exam‑Day Insight
When a numerical problem gives a coefficient of friction, always compute the normal force first; on a horizontal surface it equals the weight, but on an incline it is mg cos θ. Forgetting the cosine factor is a common source of error.

3. Fundamentals of Biology

3.1 Cell Theory and Cell Structure

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms. The cell theory, formulated in the 1830s by Matthias Schleiden, Theodor Schwann and later refined by Rudolf Virchow, states that all living things are composed of cells, that the cell is the smallest unit of life, and that all cells arise from pre‑existing cells. Prokaryotic cells, typified by bacteria, lack a membrane‑bound nucleus and organelles; their genetic material resides in a nucleoid region. Eukaryotic cells, found in plants, animals, fungi and protists, possess a true nucleus enclosed by a double membrane and a variety of specialised organelles such as mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus and lysosomes. The plasma membrane, a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins, regulates the movement of substances in and out of the cell, maintaining homeostasis.

3.2 Genetics, DNA and Protein Synthesis

Hereditary information is stored in deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), a double‑helical polymer of nucleotides each consisting of a phosphate group, a deoxyribose sugar and one of four nitrogenous bases: adenine, thymine, cytosine and guanine. The sequence of bases encodes the instructions for building proteins. During transcription, a segment of DNA is copied into messenger RNA (mRNA) by the enzyme RNA polymerase. The mRNA then travels to the ribosome where translation occurs: transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules bring specific amino acids that correspond to three‑base codons on the mRNA, and the ribosome links them into a polypeptide chain. The genetic code is nearly universal, degenerate (most amino acids are specified by more than one codon) and non‑overlapping. Mutations, which are changes in the DNA sequence, can be silent, missense, nonsense or frameshift, each with different consequences for the resulting protein.

3.3 Evolution and Natural Selection

Evolution is the change in the heritable characteristics of biological populations over successive generations. Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace independently proposed natural selection as the mechanism: individuals with traits that confer a reproductive advantage in a given environment leave more offspring, causing those traits to become more common. The modern synthesis integrates genetics with natural selection, showing that mutation, gene flow, genetic drift and recombination provide the raw variation upon which selection acts. Evidence for evolution includes the fossil record showing transitional forms, homologous structures such as the forelimbs of mammals, molecular similarities in DNA and protein sequences across species, and observed instances of rapid adaptation such as antibiotic resistance in bacteria.

4. Human Body Systems

4.1 Circulatory System

The circulatory system transports nutrients, gases, hormones and waste products throughout the body. The heart, a muscular pump with four chambers (two atria and two ventricles), drives blood through two circuits: the pulmonary circuit carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs for oxygenation, and the systemic circuit delivers oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the rest of the body. Arteries carry blood away from the heart under high pressure; veins return blood to the heart under low pressure and contain valves to prevent backflow. Capillaries, with walls only one cell thick, are the sites of exchange between blood and tissues. The cardiac cycle is regulated by the sinoatrial node, the natural pacemaker, and modulated by the autonomic nervous system and hormones such as adrenaline.

4.2 Respiratory System

Respiration supplies oxygen for cellular metabolism and removes carbon dioxide. Air enters through the nasal cavity, passes the pharynx, larynx, trachea and bronchial tree, finally reaching the alveoli where gas exchange occurs across a thin epithelium surrounded by capillaries. Ventilation is driven by pressure changes created by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles; inhalation expands the thoracic cavity, lowering intrapulmonary pressure below atmospheric pressure, while exhalation is largely passive due to elastic recoil. The respiratory centre in the medulla oblongata monitors blood pH and CO₂ levels, adjusting breathing rate and depth to maintain acid‑base balance. Hemoglobin in red blood cells binds O₂ cooperatively, and the Bohr effect describes how increased CO₂ and decreased pH reduce hemoglobin’s affinity for oxygen, facilitating release in metabolically active tissues.

4.3 Nervous System

The nervous system provides rapid communication and coordination. It is divided into the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system (cranial and spinal nerves). Neurons are the functional units, transmitting electrical impulses along axons and communicating across synapses via neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine, dopamine and serotonin. The brain’s major regions — cerebrum, cerebellum, brainstem — control higher functions, motor coordination and vital autonomic activities respectively. Reflex arcs allow immediate responses without conscious brain involvement, protecting the organism from harm. The autonomic nervous system further splits into sympathetic (fight‑or‑flight) and parasympathetic (rest‑and‑digest) divisions, which antagonistically regulate heart rate, digestion, pupil diameter and many other visceral functions.

5. Interdisciplinary Applications and Environmental Context

5.1 Energy Resources and Sustainability

Modern society relies on the conversion of various energy resources into usable forms. Fossil fuels — coal, petroleum, natural gas — release chemical energy through combustion, but they also emit carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that contributes to global warming. Renewable sources such as solar photovoltaic cells, wind turbines, hydroelectric dams and biomass harness energy flows that are naturally replenished. Nuclear fission of uranium‑235 provides a high‑density energy source with low carbon emissions, yet it raises concerns about radioactive waste and proliferation. The concept of energy efficiency, expressed as the ratio of useful output to total input, guides the design of engines, appliances and industrial processes to minimise waste. Understanding the thermodynamic limits, such as the Carnot efficiency for heat engines, helps engineers set realistic performance targets.

5.2 Biotechnology and Genetic Engineering

Advances in molecular biology have enabled the manipulation of genetic material for medical, agricultural and industrial purposes. Recombinant DNA technology allows the insertion of a gene of interest into a host organism, producing proteins such as human insulin in bacteria. CRISPR‑Cas9, a genome‑editing tool derived from a bacterial immune system, provides precise, programmable modifications of DNA sequences. In agriculture, genetically modified crops can exhibit pest resistance, herbicide tolerance or enhanced nutritional content. Ethical, regulatory and ecological considerations accompany these technologies, prompting ongoing debate about biosafety, intellectual property and equitable access.

5.3 Public Health, Disease Prevention and Immunology

Public health integrates biology, chemistry and social science to protect populations from disease. Vaccination exploits the adaptive immune system: exposure to an attenuated or inactivated pathogen, or to a subunit such as a protein or polysaccharide, stimulates the production of memory B and T cells that confer long‑lasting immunity. The innate immune response, involving barriers, phagocytes, natural killer cells and inflammatory mediators, provides the first line of defence. Epidemiology uses statistical methods to track disease spread, identify risk factors and evaluate interventions. Antimicrobial resistance, driven by misuse of antibiotics, threatens to render many infections untreatable, underscoring the need for stewardship programmes and novel drug discovery.

The lesson concludes by reminding learners that a solid grasp of these fundamental concepts across physics, chemistry, biology and human physiology forms the backbone of the general awareness section and equips candidates to tackle applied questions with confidence.

Thermodynamic Laws, Entropy and Free Energy in Chemical Systems

The four laws of thermodynamics provide the quantitative framework for energy transformations in chemistry. The zeroth law establishes thermal equilibrium and the concept of temperature. The first law (ΔU = q + w) states that the internal energy change of a system equals heat added plus work done on the system; for a constant‑pressure process the enthalpy change ΔH = qp. The second law introduces entropy (S), a measure of energy dispersal: for any spontaneous process the total entropy change of the universe ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr > 0. The third law defines the entropy of a perfect crystal at 0 K as zero, providing an absolute reference.

Entropy changes for reactions can be estimated from standard molar entropies: ΔS°rxn = ΣνS°(products) – ΣνS°(reactants). The Gibbs free energy G = H – TS combines enthalpy and entropy; at constant temperature and pressure ΔG = ΔH – TΔS. A negative ΔG indicates spontaneity, ΔG = 0 marks equilibrium, and a positive ΔG means non‑spontaneous under the given conditions. The relationship ΔG° = –RT ln K links standard free energy to the equilibrium constant K (R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).

Real chemical processes often deviate from ideal behaviour. Non‑standard conditions are handled via ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q, where Q is the reaction quotient. Temperature dependence of ΔG° follows the Gibbs‑Helmholtz equation: (∂(ΔG°/T)/∂T)P = –ΔH°/T². Phase transitions illustrate the balance: at the normal boiling point ΔG = 0, so ΔHvap = TbΔSvap. In electrochemistry, ΔG° = –nFE° connects free energy to standard cell potential E° (n = electrons transferred, F = 96 485 C mol⁻¹).

Chemical Kinetics: Rate Laws, Arrhenius Equation and Catalysis

Chemical kinetics quantifies how fast reactions proceed. The rate law expresses the instantaneous rate as rate = k[A]ˣ[B]ʸ, where k is the rate constant, [A] and [B] are reactant concentrations, and x, y are the reaction orders (determined experimentally, not from stoichiometry). The overall order is x + y. For a first‑order reaction, the integrated rate law is ln([A]₀/[A]ₜ) = kt; for second order, 1/[A]ₜ – 1/[A]₀ = kt. Half‑life (t½) for first order is constant: t½ = ln 2 / k; for second order it depends on initial concentration: t½ = 1/(k[A]₀).

The temperature dependence of k follows the Arrhenius equation: k = A exp(–Ea/RT), where A is the pre‑exponential factor (frequency of collisions with proper orientation), Ea is the activation energy, R the gas constant, and T absolute temperature. Taking natural logs gives ln k = ln A – Ea/(RT); a plot of ln k versus 1/T yields a straight line of slope –Ea/R. The Eyring equation from transition‑state theory refines this: k = (kBT/h) exp(–ΔG‡/RT), where ΔG‡ is the Gibbs free energy of activation, kB Boltzmann’s constant, h Planck’s constant.

Catalysis lowers Ea without being consumed. Homogeneous catalysts share the same phase as reactants (e.g., acid‑catalysed esterification). Heterogeneous catalysts provide surface active sites (e.g., Fe in Haber‑Bosch, Pt in catalytic converters). Enzymes are biological macromolecular catalysts with high specificity; their kinetics often follow the Michaelis‑Menten model: v₀ = (Vmax[S])/(KM + [S]), where Vmax = kcat[E]₀ and KM is the substrate concentration at half‑maximal velocity. Inhibitors can be competitive (increase apparent KM), non‑competitive (decrease Vmax), or uncompetitive (decrease both).

Worked Example: Determining Activation Energy
A reaction has rate constants k₁ = 2.5 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at 300 K and k₂ = 1.2 × 10⁻² s⁻¹ at 320 K. Calculate the activation energy Ea in kJ mol⁻¹.
Use the two‑point Arrhenius form: ln(k₂/k₁) = –Ea/R (1/T₂ – 1/T₁).
ln(1.2 × 10⁻² / 2.5 × 10⁻³) = ln(4.8) ≈ 1.5686.
1/T₂ – 1/T₁ = 1/320 – 1/300 = (300 – 320)/(320 × 300) = –20/96 000 = –2.083 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.
Thus 1.5686 = –Ea/8.314 × (–2.083 × 10⁻⁴).
Ea = 1.5686 × 8.314 / 2.083 × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 62 600 J mol⁻¹ = 62.6 kJ mol⁻¹.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Kinetics
Memorise the integrated forms for zero, first and second order; they appear directly in multiple‑choice questions. For Arrhenius problems, always convert temperatures to Kelvin and use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. When a catalyst is mentioned, check whether the question asks for the effect on Ea (decreases) or on the pre‑exponential factor (usually unchanged). Practice sketching ln k vs 1/T plots to recognise slope‑intercept relationships quickly.

Gene Expression Regulation: Transcription Factors, Epigenetics and Non‑coding RNAs

Gene expression in eukaryotes is controlled at multiple levels. Transcription factors (TFs) are proteins that bind specific DNA sequences (promoters, enhancers, silencers) to recruit RNA polymerase II. General TFs (e.g., TFIID with TATA‑binding protein) assemble the pre‑initiation complex; regulatory TFs (activators, repressors) modulate the rate. Enhancers can act over tens of kilobases and are orientation‑independent, looping to contact the promoter via mediator complexes.

Epigenetic modifications alter chromatin structure without changing DNA sequence. DNA methylation at CpG islands (catalysed by DNMT1, DNMT3A/B) generally represses transcription by blocking TF binding and recruiting methyl‑CpG‑binding proteins that attract histone deacetylases. Histone modifications — acetylation (by HATs) neutralises lysine charge, opening chromatin; methylation can be activating (H3K4me3) or repressive (H3K27me3) depending on residue and degree. These marks are read by effector proteins (bromodomains for acetyl, chromodomains for methyl) that propagate the state through cell division.

Non‑coding RNAs provide post‑transcriptional control. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) (~22 nt) are processed from pri‑miRNA → pre‑miRNA → mature miRNA, loaded onto Argonaute in the RISC complex, and guide cleavage or translational repression of partially complementary target mRNAs. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) derive from long double‑stranded RNA, direct perfect‑match cleavage of viral or transposon RNAs. Long non‑coding RNAs (lncRNAs) scaffold chromatin‑modifying complexes (e.g., XIST recruits PRC2 for X‑chromosome inactivation). Dysregulation of these layers underlies cancers, developmental disorders, and metabolic diseases.

Endocrine Control: Hormone Classes, Feedback Loops and Clinical Correlates

The endocrine system uses hormones — chemical messengers secreted into blood — to coordinate physiology. Hormones are classified by chemical nature: peptide/protein hormones (insulin, growth hormone) bind cell‑surface receptors, triggering second‑messenger cascades (cAMP, IP₃/DAG). Steroid hormones (cortisol, estradiol, testosterone) are lipophilic, diffuse through membranes, bind intracellular nuclear receptors that act as transcription factors. Amine hormones (epinephrine, thyroid hormones) derive from tyrosine; catecholamines use membrane receptors, while thyroid hormones (T₃, T₄) enter cells and bind nuclear receptors.

Regulation relies on negative feedback loops. The hypothalamic‑pituitary‑target axis exemplifies this: hypothalamic releasing hormones stimulate anterior pituitary tropic hormones, which stimulate target glands; the target hormone then inhibits both hypothalamus and pituitary. For instance, thyrotropin‑releasing hormone (TRH) → thyroid‑stimulating hormone (TSH) → thyroxine (T₄)/triiodothyronine (T₃); elevated T₃/T₄ suppress TRH and TSH. Positive feedback is rarer but crucial: the LH surge in the menstrual cycle is driven by estradiol‑mediated positive feedback on the pituitary.

Major axes and clinical correlates:
• Hypothalamic‑pituitary‑adrenal (HPA) axis: CRH → ACTH → cortisol. Chronic stress → hypercortisolism (Cushing’s syndrome) or adrenal insufficiency (Addison’s disease).
• Hypothalamic‑pituitary‑gonadal (HPG) axis: GnRH → LH/FSH → sex steroids. Dysregulation causes hypogonadism, polycystic ovary syndrome.
• Hypothalamic‑pituitary‑thyroid (HPT) axis: TRH → TSH → T₃/T₄. Primary hypothyroidism (high TSH, low T₄) vs secondary (low TSH, low T₄).
• Pancreatic islets: insulin (β‑cells) lowers blood glucose; glucagon (α‑cells) raises it. Type 1 diabetes = autoimmune β‑cell loss; Type 2 = insulin resistance + β‑cell dysfunction.

Atmospheric Chemistry and Climate Forcing: Greenhouse Gases, Aerosols and Radiative Balance

Earth’s energy budget is governed by the balance between incoming solar radiation (≈340 W m⁻² averaged) and outgoing long‑wave radiation. Greenhouse gases (GHGs) absorb and re‑emit infrared radiation, reducing the outgoing flux. The major well‑mixed GHGs are carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O) and halogenated compounds (CFCs, HCFCs). Their radiative forcing (ΔF) quantifies the instantaneous change in net flux at the tropopause; for CO₂, ΔF ≈ 5.35 ln(C/C₀) W m⁻² (C₀ = pre‑industrial 278 ppm). CH₄ has a higher per‑molecule forcing but shorter atmospheric lifetime (~12 yr) versus CO₂ (centuries).

Aerosols — solid or liquid particles suspended in air — exert both direct and indirect effects. Direct effect: scattering (sulfate, nitrate) cools by reflecting sunlight; absorbing aerosols (black carbon) warm by absorbing solar radiation. Indirect effect: aerosols act as cloud condensation nuclei (CCN), increasing cloud droplet number, enhancing cloud albedo (Twomey effect) and potentially prolonging cloud lifetime (Albrecht effect). The net aerosol forcing is negative but uncertain (≈ –0.9 W m⁻²).

The carbon cycle couples atmosphere, oceans, and terrestrial biosphere. About 45 % of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions remain in the atmosphere; the rest is taken up by oceanic dissolution (forming carbonic acid, lowering pH) and land photosynthesis. Feedbacks include permafrost thaw releasing CH₄ and CO₂, and reduced oceanic solubility with warming.

Mitigation strategies target GHG sources: fossil‑fuel combustion (energy, transport), deforestation (land‑use change), agriculture (enteric fermentation, rice paddies, fertilizer N₂O). Technologies include carbon capture and storage (CCS), renewable energy deployment, and methane leak reduction. Adaptation addresses inevitable climate impacts: sea‑level rise, extreme heat, altered precipitation.

  • Confusing ΔG° with ΔG under non‑standard conditions; always check whether concentrations/pressures are standard.
  • Assuming reaction order equals stoichiometric coefficients; order is experimental.
  • Mixing up competitive vs non‑competitive inhibition effects on KM and Vmax.
  • Treating all epigenetic marks as uniformly repressive; H3K4me3 is activating, H3K27me3 is repressive.
  • Overlooking that steroid hormones act via intracellular receptors, not membrane receptors.
  • Attributing all aerosol forcing to cooling; black carbon produces warming.
  • Review these traps regularly to avoid losing marks on exam day.
