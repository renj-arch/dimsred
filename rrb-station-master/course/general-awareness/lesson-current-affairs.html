1 Understanding the Scope of Current Affairs for RRB Station Master

1.1 Why Current Affairs Matter in the Examination

The General Awareness segment of the RRB Station Master test allocates a substantial share of marks to events that have taken place in the recent past, because the railway administration expects its officers to be aware of the policy environment in which they operate. A candidate who can link a new railway safety directive to the broader national emphasis on infrastructure modernisation demonstrates the analytical mindset the board values. Consequently, the preparation strategy must go beyond rote memorisation of headlines and instead cultivate an understanding of cause and effect, such as how a change in the fiscal deficit target influences capital expenditure on track renewal.

1.2 Defining the Six‑Month Window

The conventional window for “last six months” in railway recruitment examinations spans the period from the beginning of March to the end of September of the examination year. This window captures the Union Budget presentation, the monsoon session of Parliament, major international summits, and the annual cycle of railway project milestones. By anchoring the study plan to these fixed calendar events, aspirants can organise their notes chronologically and avoid the common pitfall of treating every news item as equally important.

1.3 Categorising News for Efficient Retention

Effective categorisation separates national political developments, economic policy shifts, social welfare schemes, international diplomatic engagements, multilateral organisation decisions, railway‑specific announcements, key appointments, and award ceremonies. Each category follows a distinct pattern of recurrence: for instance, the Union Budget appears once a year, while the Railway Board’s safety circulars may be issued monthly. Recognising these patterns allows a student to allocate revision time proportionally, devoting more hours to high‑frequency railway updates and less to one‑off international summits.

2 National Developments: Governance, Economy, and Social Policy

2.1 Legislative Activity and Constitutional Amendments

During the March‑September 2025 window, Parliament convened for its budget and monsoon sessions, during which several bills were passed that affect the administrative framework within which the railways function. The passage of a new Public Financial Management Bill, for example, tightens the reporting requirements for capital‑intensive ministries, thereby influencing how the Railway Ministry plans its rolling‑stock procurement. When a constitutional amendment alters the distribution of legislative powers between the Centre and the States, it can redefine the jurisdiction over land acquisition for new rail corridors, making such amendments a direct concern for a station master who coordinates with state authorities.

2.2 Fiscal Policy and Budgetary Allocations

The Union Budget for the fiscal year 2025‑26, presented in February 2025, set the tone for capital expenditure across infrastructure sectors. The finance minister emphasised a higher outlay for the National Infrastructure Pipeline, with a specific earmark for the Dedicated Freight Corridor and the high‑speed rail project. An increase in the allocation for the Pradhan Mantri Gati Shakti master plan translates into faster clearances for multi‑modal logistics parks that sit adjacent to major stations. Understanding the linkage between the fiscal deficit target, market borrowing, and the resultant fund flow to the Railway Ministry enables a candidate to answer questions that ask for the “source of financing” for a new electrification project.

2.3 Social Welfare Schemes with Railway Linkages

Several flagship welfare programmes announced or expanded in this period have direct operational touchpoints for railway stations. The expansion of the Ayushman Bharat health‑cover scheme to include migrant workers who travel long distances by train means that station masters may be required to facilitate enrolment camps on platform premises. Similarly, the revised Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana guidelines now recognise railway land as a potential site for affordable housing, creating a new coordination channel between the Railway Board and the Ministry of Housing. Recognising these intersections helps the aspirant answer scenario‑based questions that test administrative awareness.

2.4 Economic Indicators and Their Railway Relevance

Key macroeconomic indicators released monthly — such as the Index of Industrial Production, the Consumer Price Index, and the Wholesale Price Index — serve as barometers for freight traffic demand. A sustained rise in the IIP for the coal and steel sectors typically precedes an uptick in bulk freight loading on the Eastern and Western Dedicated Freight Corridors. Conversely, a spike in the CPI may prompt the Railway Board to review passenger fare revision formulas to balance affordability with revenue adequacy. The ability to explain these causal chains distinguishes a well‑prepared candidate from one who merely memorises the latest GDP growth figure.

Example Question
A news report states that the Index of Industrial Production for the core sector rose by 6.2 % in July 2025 compared with the same month a year earlier. Which of the following railway‑related outcomes is most likely to follow in the subsequent quarter?
The core sector comprises coal, crude oil, natural gas, refinery products, fertilisers, steel, cement and electricity. A 6.2 % rise signals higher production of bulk commodities that are primarily transported by rail. Consequently, the Eastern and Western Dedicated Freight Corridors would experience increased loading of coal and steel, leading to higher freight revenue for the Railway Ministry in the next quarter. The correct answer is: increased freight loading on dedicated freight corridors.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question links an economic indicator to railway operations, identify the commodity group that drives the indicator and then map that commodity to the specific rail corridor or service that carries it. This two‑step mapping eliminates distractors that mention unrelated passenger services.

3 International Developments: Geopolitics, Multilateral Institutions, and Bilateral Relations

3.1 Major Multilateral Summits and Their Resolutions

The G20 Leaders’ Summit held in 2025 continued the tradition of addressing global economic governance, climate finance, and digital taxation. The communiqué emphasised a coordinated approach to mobilising private capital for green infrastructure, a theme that resonates with India’s railway electrification and solar‑powered station initiatives. Similarly, the United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP30) in 2025 produced a decision on the operationalisation of the loss‑and‑damage fund, which may unlock additional financing for climate‑resilient railway assets in vulnerable coastal zones. Understanding the language of these resolutions enables a candidate to answer questions that ask for the “international framework” supporting a domestic railway project.

3.2 Bilateral Agreements with Direct Railway Components

India’s strategic partnerships with Japan, France, and the United Arab Emirates have yielded memoranda of understanding that include technology transfer for signalling systems, joint feasibility studies for high‑speed corridors, and investment in logistics parks. The 2025 India‑Japan Joint Working Group on Railways, for instance, reviewed the progress of the Mumbai‑Ahmedabad High Speed Rail project and agreed on a revised timeline for the procurement of the E5 series Shinkansen trainsets. Such bilateral mechanisms often appear in examination questions that test knowledge of “foreign collaboration” in railway modernisation.

3.3 Geopolitical Developments Affecting Trade Routes

Shifts in the security environment of the Indian Ocean Region, including the evolving situation in the Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz, have prompted a re‑evaluation of maritime‑rail intermodal routes. The Indian Railways’ participation in the International North‑South Transport Corridor (INSTC) gained momentum in 2025 as alternative land‑based routes were sought to bypass congested sea lanes. A station master involved in freight operations at gateway stations such as Jawaharlal Nehru Port Trust must be aware of how these geopolitical currents translate into revised cargo handling schedules and customs clearance priorities.

4 Railway Sector Updates: Infrastructure, Policy, and Technology

4.1 Progress on Dedicated Freight Corridors and High‑Speed Rail

The Dedicated Freight Corridor Corporation of India Limited reported that the Eastern Corridor’s Ludhiana‑Dankuni section achieved commercial operation in the first half of 2025, while the Western Corridor’s Rewari‑Palwal stretch entered the trial‑run phase. Simultaneously, the Mumbai‑Ahmedabad High Speed Rail project saw the completion of major viaduct segments in Gujarat, with the first trainset delivery slated for late 2025. These milestones are significant because they alter the freight‑passenger mix on the existing network, freeing up path capacity on the conventional lines for additional passenger services — a direct operational concern for a station master managing platform allocation.

4.2 Safety Enhancements and the Kavach System Rollout

The indigenously developed Kavach automatic train protection system continued its phased deployment across the Golden Quadrilateral and the Diagonal routes. By September 2025, over 2,500 route kilometres had been equipped, and the Railway Board issued a directive mandating Kavach installation on all new electrified sections. The system’s ability to prevent signal passing at danger and to enforce speed restrictions automatically reduces the likelihood of collisions, thereby affecting the daily safety briefings that a station master conducts with the crew.

4.3 Digital Initiatives: Freight Business Development Portal and Passenger Service Apps

The launch of the Freight Business Development Portal in early 2025 provided a single‑window interface for cargo owners to book wagons, track consignments, and access real‑time freight rates. On the passenger side, the integration of the Rail Madad grievance redressal system with the Unified Mobile Application for New-age Governance (UMANG) streamlined complaint resolution at stations. A station master who can navigate these platforms efficiently contributes to the railway’s broader digital transformation agenda, and examination questions often test familiarity with the names and functions of these portals.

5 Key Appointments and Constitutional Posts

5.1 Appointments Affecting Railway Governance

The President of India appoints the Chairman and Members of the Railway Board on the advice of the Council of Ministers. In 2025, a new Chairman assumed office, bringing a background in public‑private partnership projects, which signalled a policy tilt towards leveraging private capital for station redevelopment. The appointment of a new Member (Infrastructure) with experience in the Dedicated Freight Corridor Corporation further aligned the Board’s leadership with the ongoing corridor execution priorities. Knowledge of these appointments helps aspirants answer questions that ask for the “current head” of a specific railway functional directorate.

5.2 Constitutional and Statutory Bodies with Railway Interface

The Comptroller and Auditor General of India, the Central Vigilance Commission, and the National Green Tribunal regularly issue observations that shape railway procurement and environmental compliance. The 2025 CAG report on the utilisation of the Rashtriya Rail Sanraksha Kosh highlighted under‑spending on track renewal, prompting the Railway Board to accelerate the tendering process for rail‑grinding machines. A station master who understands the audit‑to‑action cycle can anticipate the procedural changes that follow such reports.

5.3 State‑Level Appointments Relevant to Railway Land and Law‑Order

Chief Secretaries and Directors General of Police in states traversed by major rail corridors are pivotal for coordinating land acquisition, security during large‑scale projects, and crowd management during festivals. In 2025, the appointment of a new Chief Secretary in a key eastern state coincided with the finalisation of the land‑acquisition schedule for the third line on the Howrah‑Delhi route. Awareness of these state‑level changes equips a candidate to answer scenario‑based questions on inter‑governmental coordination.

The Padma Awards announced on Republic Day 2025 included several railway engineers and administrators recognised for contributions to indigenous train‑set design and station redevelopment. The Padma Shri conferred on a senior mechanical engineer who led the development of the Vande Bharat traction system exemplifies how technical excellence in the railway sector is celebrated at the national level. The President’s Police Medal for Gallantry and the Railway Minister’s Medal for Meritorious Service were awarded to Railway Protection Force officers who thwarted sabotage attempts on critical bridges in 2025. Such international accolades are cited in examination passages to assess a candidate’s ability to identify the awarding body and the specific criterion for which the project was honoured.

7 Effective Strategies for Retaining Current Affairs

7.1 Structured Note‑Making Aligned with the Syllabus

Adopting a template that mirrors the seven categories outlined in this lesson — national governance, economy, social policy, international summits, bilateral railway agreements, railway infrastructure, appointments, and awards — ensures that every news item finds a logical home. Writing each entry in a single sentence that captures the event, the agency involved, and the likely railway impact transforms raw information into exam‑ready knowledge. Regular weekly consolidation of these notes into a master document prevents the accumulation of fragmented clippings.

7.2 Leveraging Official Sources and Verified Summaries

The Press Information Bureau releases, the Railway Ministry’s monthly “Railway Gazette”, the Ministry of External Affairs’ “MEA Weekly”, and the Reserve Bank of India’s “Monthly Bulletin” constitute the most reliable primary sources. Supplementing these with the “Year‑End Review” publications of the respective ministries provides a consolidated view that is both authoritative and time‑efficient. Candidates should avoid unverified social‑media aggregates, as they often contain inaccuracies that can mislead answer selection.

7.3 Periodic Self‑Testing Through Thematic Quizzes

Designing short quizzes that focus on one theme at a time — for example, “Freight Corridor Milestones March‑September 2025” — reinforces retrieval pathways. Spacing these quizzes at increasing intervals (one day, three days, one week) exploits the spacing effect, a well‑established cognitive principle that improves long‑term retention. The practice of writing brief explanatory answers, rather than merely selecting options, deepens conceptual clarity and prepares the aspirant for descriptive questions that may appear in the later stages of the selection process.

Consistent application of these strategies throughout the preparation period builds a robust current‑affairs foundation that supports both the objective and descriptive components of the RRB Station Master examination.

Integration of Railway Finances with the Union Budget and the Capital Outlay Framework

The railway budget was merged with the Union Budget in 2017, ending a 92‑year tradition of a separate presentation. Since then, the Ministry of Railways receives its capital outlay as a distinct demand for grants (Demand No. 85) within the general budget. The 2024‑25 Union Budget allocated ₹2.65 lakh crore for railway capital expenditure, a 15 % increase over the revised estimate of the previous year. This outlay is split into three broad heads: (i) new lines and gauge conversion, (ii) rolling stock acquisition and maintenance facilities, and (iii) safety, signalling and technology upgrades such as Kavach and the Freight Business Development Portal.

The capital outlay is financed through a mix of gross budgetary support (GBS), internal resources (freight earnings, passenger fares, parcel revenue), and extra‑budgetary resources (EBR) raised via Indian Railway Finance Corporation (IRFC) bonds. In 2024‑25, GBS accounted for roughly 55 % of the capex, IRFC borrowings for 30 %, and the remainder from internal accruals. The Railway Board’s “Capital Investment Plan” (CIP) translates the budgetary allocation into project‑wise sanctions, each requiring a Detailed Project Report (DPR) vetted by the Finance Directorate and the Planning Commission (now NITI Aayog). The CIP also embeds a “phasing” mechanism: projects are classified as “ongoing”, “new sanctions” and “future pipeline”, allowing the Board to re‑prioritise when fiscal space contracts.

A critical nuance for examination is the treatment of “revenue surplus” versus “capital surplus”. The railway’s operating ratio (OR) — operating expenses as a percentage of revenue — has hovered around 98 % in recent years, leaving minimal revenue surplus for capital formation. Consequently, the government has increasingly relied on EBR and multilateral loans (e.g., JICA for the Mumbai‑Ahmedabad High Speed Rail) to fund large‑ticket projects without breaching the fiscal deficit target. The 2024‑25 budget also introduced a “Railway Infrastructure Fund” seeded with ₹10,000 crore from the National Investment and Infrastructure Fund (NIIF) to de‑risk private participation in station redevelopment.

From an examiner’s perspective, questions often test the candidate’s ability to trace a specific project’s funding source: for instance, identifying that the Eastern Dedicated Freight Corridor’s Phase‑II is financed 70 % by a World Bank loan and 30 % by GBS, or that the Vande Bharat train‑set procurement uses a mix of IRFC bonds and budgetary support. Understanding the flow — Budget → Demand for Grants → CIP → DPR → Sanction → Fund Release — enables accurate answers to both direct and scenario‑based items.

Public‑Private Partnership Models in Station Redevelopment and High‑Speed Corridors

Station redevelopment has moved from a purely departmental execution model to a structured PPP framework governed by the “Station Redevelopment Policy, 2018” (revised 2022). The policy defines three concession models: (i) Design‑Build‑Finance‑Operate‑Transfer (DBFOT) for greenfield stations, (ii) Operate‑Maintain‑Transfer (OMT) for brownfield upgrades, and (iii) a hybrid “Revenue Share” model where the concessionaire pays a fixed upfront premium plus a percentage of commercial revenue (retail, advertising, parking). As of March 2024, 123 stations have been awarded under these models, with a combined estimated private investment of ₹1.2 lakh crore.

The Mumbai‑Ahmedabad High Speed Rail (MAHSR) corridor exemplifies a sovereign‑guaranteed PPP. The project’s total cost of ₹1.08 lakh crore is financed 81 % by a 50‑year, 0.1 % interest loan from the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA), 10 % by the Government of India as equity, and 9 % by the participating state governments (Maharashtra and Gujarat). The concessionaire — a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) jointly owned by the Railway Ministry and the two state governments — bears construction risk, while revenue risk is mitigated through a “take‑or‑pay” agreement with Indian Railways for a guaranteed minimum number of train‑paths.

Risk allocation is the core examinable concept. In DBFOT station projects, the concessionaire assumes demand risk (footfall, retail occupancy) but the railway retains land ownership and regulatory control over safety standards. In the MAHSR SPV, the sovereign guarantee covers debt service, but cost overruns beyond a 10 % contingency are shared 50:50 between the SPV and the lending agency. The 2023 amendment to the Model Concession Agreement (MCA) introduced a “force‑majeure” clause that allows extension of concession period without penalty for pandemics or natural calamities, a provision tested in recent question papers.

Another layer is the “Land Monetisation” route: the Railway Land Development Authority (RLDA) leases surplus land adjacent to stations for commercial development, with lease rentals feeding into the station redevelopment corpus. The 2024‑25 budget earmarked ₹5,000 crore from RLDA lease proceeds to cross‑subsidise passenger amenities. Candidates should be able to differentiate between a concession agreement (long‑term operational rights) and a lease agreement (land use only), and to identify the revenue streams — fare box, non‑fare box, advertising, real‑estate — that feed each model.

Scenario‑Based Funding Identification
A new greenfield station at X is awarded under a DBFOT concession. The concessionaire invests ₹800 crore, pays an upfront premium of ₹50 crore, and agrees to share 12 % of gross commercial revenue annually. The railway provides the land on a 99‑year lease and retains safety oversight. Which of the following correctly describes the risk profile? (a) Concessionaire bears construction and demand risk; railway bears revenue risk. (b) Concessionaire bears construction risk only; railway bears demand and revenue risk. (c) Concessionaire bears construction, demand and revenue risk; railway bears only regulatory risk. (d) Both share construction risk equally; concessionaire bears demand risk.
Correct answer: (c). In a DBFOT model the concessionaire designs, builds, finances, operates and transfers the asset. The upfront premium and revenue‑share clause transfer demand and revenue risk to the concessionaire. The railway’s role is limited to land lease, safety regulation and performance monitoring, i.e., regulatory risk. Option (a) incorrectly assigns revenue risk to the railway; (b) and (d) misstate the construction risk allocation.
Exam‑Day Strategy for PPP Questions
Memorise the three concession models (DBFOT, OMT, Revenue‑Share) and the MAHSR financing split (JICA 81 %, GoI 10 %, States 9 %). When a question describes a project, first identify the model from keywords — “upfront premium + revenue share” → Revenue‑Share; “design‑build‑finance‑operate‑transfer” → DBFOT; “operate‑maintain‑transfer” → OMT. Then map the risk allocation table you prepared during revision. This two‑step filter eliminates three options in most MCQs.

Safety Audit Architecture: From the Commissioner of Railway Safety to the Kavach Rollout

The statutory safety oversight of Indian Railways rests with the Commissioner of Railway Safety (CRS), an independent authority under the Ministry of Civil Aviation. The CRS conducts mandatory inspections for every new line, gauge conversion, and major signalling alteration before commercial operation. In addition, the Railway Board’s Safety Directorate issues “Safety Circulars” that codify lessons from accident inquiries. The 2023‑24 annual report of the CRS recorded 1,127 inspections, of which 84 resulted in “conditional sanctions” requiring remedial works before opening.

Post‑accident inquiries follow a two‑tier process: a preliminary inquiry by the Divisional Railway Manager (DRM) within 30 days, and a formal statutory inquiry by the CRS if the accident involves loss of life, derailment of a passenger train, or breach of signalling rules. The inquiry report is placed before the Railway Board, which issues “Action Taken Reports” (ATRs) with timelines. The 2022‑23 ATR for the Balasore triple‑train collision mandated the accelerated deployment of Kavach on the Howrah‑Chennai main line, the replacement of all mechanical interlocking with electronic interlocking (EI) within 24 months, and the introduction of a “Track Management System” (TMS) for real‑time rail‑wear monitoring.

Kavach, the indigenously developed Automatic Train Protection (ATP) system, has been commissioned on over 1,500 route kilometres as of March 2024, covering the Delhi‑Howrah, Delhi‑Mumbai and Chennai‑Howrah corridors. The system uses ultra‑high frequency (UHF) radio communication between trackside equipment and the locomotive on‑board unit to enforce speed restrictions, signal adherence and automatic brake application. The rollout plan targets 5,000 route kilometres by March 2026, with a capital outlay of ₹12,000 crore funded through the Rashtriya Rail Sanraksha Kosh (RRSK).

Examination questions frequently test the hierarchy: CRS → Railway Board → Zonal Railway → Division. A typical twist is to ask which authority can *override* a CRS conditional sanction — the answer is the Railway Board, but only after a recorded “safety justification” and with the concurrence of the Ministry of Civil Aviation. Another common item is the distinction between “statutory inquiry” (CRS) and “departmental inquiry” (DRM), and the evidentiary value of each in subsequent disciplinary proceedings.

Green Traction Roadmap: Electrification, Hydrogen Pilots and Net‑Zero Target

Indian Railways achieved 100 % route electrification on broad‑gauge network in March 2024, eliminating diesel traction on main lines. The traction energy mix now draws 85 % from the central grid (which itself is 44 % renewable as of 2023‑24) and 15 % from dedicated solar‑wind hybrid plants commissioned on railway land. The “Net‑Zero Carbon Emission by 2030” mission, announced in the 2021‑22 budget, translates into three measurable milestones: (i) 30 GW of renewable capacity tied to railway consumption by 2027, (ii) complete phase‑out of diesel locomotives from freight service by 2028, and (iii) pilot deployment of hydrogen fuel‑cell trains on the Jind‑Sonipat section by 2025.

The hydrogen pilot, sanctioned under the “National Hydrogen Energy Mission”, involves two 1,600 HP fuel‑cell multiple units (FCMUs) built by a consortium of Indian Railways’ Research Designs and Standards Organisation (RDSO) and a private electrolyser manufacturer. The project cost of ₹180 crore is shared 60:40 between the Ministry of Railways and the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE). The pilot will test refuelling infrastructure, safety protocols for hydrogen storage, and lifecycle cost comparison with battery‑electric multiple units (BEMUs).

On the freight side, the “Green Corridor” concept integrates electrified double‑stack container trains with regenerative braking, feeding energy back to the grid. The Eastern Dedicated Freight Corridor (EDFC) has been designated the first green corridor, with a target of 30 % energy regeneration by 2026. The Railway Board’s “Energy Efficiency Circular 2023” mandates that all new electric locomotives (WAG‑9H, WAP‑7HS) meet a specific energy consumption (SEC) of ≤ 3.5 kWh/1000 GTKM, a 12 % improvement over the previous generation.

From an exam perspective, candidates must differentiate between “route electrification” (overhead equipment on tracks) and “traction decarbonisation” (source of electricity). A question may state: “Indian Railways is net‑zero because it is 100 % electrified.” The correct rebuttal: electrification shifts emissions to the grid; net‑zero requires the grid to be carbon‑free or offset through renewable procurement. The 2024‑25 budget’s allocation of ₹7,500 crore for “Railway Renewable Energy Projects” is the concrete financial commitment that examiners expect you to cite.

Freight Corridor Financing: Multilateral Loans, Sovereign Guarantees and Revenue Sharing

The Dedicated Freight Corridor Corporation of India (DFCCIL) operates as a special purpose vehicle (SPV) under the Companies Act, 2013, with 100 % equity held by the Ministry of Railways. The Eastern DFC (EDFC) — 1,856 km from Ludhiana to Dankuni — was financed through a blend of World Bank (IBRD) loans (₹12,000 crore), Asian Development Bank (ADB) loans (₹8,000 crore), and GBS (₹10,000 crore). The Western DFC (WDFC) — 1,504 km from Dadri to JNPT — uses a similar structure but adds a Japanese ODA loan (JICA) of ₹9,500 crore for the Rewari‑Palghar section.

Both corridors employ a “Revenue Sharing Agreement” (RSA) between DFCCIL and Indian Railways. Under the RSA, Indian Railways pays DFCCIL a “track access charge” (TAC) per gross tonne‑kilometre (GTKM) moved on the DFC. The TAC is indexed to the Wholesale Price Index (WPI) and revised every five years. For the EDFC, the base TAC is ₹1.85/GTKM; for the WDFC, ₹2.10/GTKM. The revenue collected services the debt, with a debt‑service coverage ratio (DSCR) target of 1.3× built into the loan covenants.

Sovereign guarantees are extended only for the multilateral loan portions; the GBS component carries no guarantee. The 2023 Cabinet approval empowered the corporation to raise “Green Bonds” up to ₹5,000 crore, earmarked for energy‑efficient rolling stock and regenerative braking infrastructure. The first tranche of ₹1,200 crore was oversubscribed 3.2× in the domestic market, signalling investor confidence in the corridor’s cash‑flow predictability.

Examination focus areas include: (a) identifying the correct multilateral partner for each corridor segment, (b) calculating the effective interest cost after WPI indexation, and (c) explaining why the RSA insulates DFCCIL from freight volume volatility — because Indian Railways guarantees a minimum TAC revenue equivalent to 70 % of projected traffic. A typical trap is to confuse the “track access charge” with the “haulage charge” levied on private container operators; the former is paid by the railway to DFCCIL, the latter by the operator to the railway.

  • Assuming the railway budget is still presented separately — it was merged in 2017.
  • Confusing DBFOT with OMT — DBFOT transfers construction and demand risk to the concessionaire; OMT transfers only operations and maintenance risk.
  • Attributing Kavach deployment to the Railway Board alone — statutory safety clearance rests with the Commissioner of Railway Safety.
  • Equating 100 % route electrification with net‑zero emissions — grid carbon intensity determines the actual carbon footprint.
  • Mixing up track access charge (paid by railway to DFCCIL) with haulage charge (paid by private operator to railway).
  • Overlooking the sovereign guarantee’s limited scope — it covers only multilateral loan tranches, not the entire project cost.
