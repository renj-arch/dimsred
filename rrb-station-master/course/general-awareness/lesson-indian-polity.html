Historical Background and Making of the Constitution

1.1 The Colonial Legacy and the Demand for a Constitution

The constitutional development of India cannot be understood without reference to the long period of British rule that shaped the administrative, legal and political institutions which the framers later inherited. The Government of India Act 1919 introduced a limited dyarchy in the provinces, while the Government of India Act 1935 provided a federal structure with a strong centre, a bicameral legislature at the centre and provincial autonomy. These Acts, however, were imposed from above and did not reflect the sovereign will of the Indian people. The demand for a constitution drafted by Indians themselves grew stronger after the 1929 Lahore session of the Indian National Congress, which declared complete independence as its goal, and the subsequent Civil Disobedience Movement demonstrated mass support for self‑governance. The failure of the Cripps Mission in 1942 and the Quit India Movement further convinced nationalist leaders that a constituent assembly elected by the people was the only legitimate body to frame a constitution.

1.2 The Constituent Assembly and Its Composition

The Constituent Assembly was constituted in November 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan. It originally comprised 389 members, of which 292 were elected by the provincial legislative assemblies and 93 were nominated by the princely states. After the partition of the country in 1947, the membership reduced to 299, with representatives from the provinces that remained in the Indian Union. The Assembly was broadly representative of the major communities, regions and political opinions, including members of the Congress, the Muslim League (until its withdrawal), the Scheduled Castes Federation, the Communist Party and independents. Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected as its President, and the Assembly functioned through a series of committees, the most important being the Drafting Committee chaired by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar. The Assembly met for 11 sessions over a period of 2 years, 11 months and 18 days, deliberating on every article in detail.

1.3 Drafting Process and Adoption

The Drafting Committee prepared the first draft in February 1948, which was circulated for public comments and debated clause by clause in the Assembly. More than 7,000 amendments were moved, of which about 2,400 were accepted. The debates reveal a careful balancing of competing visions: a strong centre versus provincial autonomy, a parliamentary system versus a presidential one, and the inclusion of justiciable fundamental rights versus non‑justiciable directive principles. The final text was adopted on 26 November 1949, and the Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950, a date chosen to commemorate the 1930 declaration of Purna Swaraj. The adoption ceremony was marked by the signing of the document by 284 members, and the Constitution became the supreme law of the land, replacing the Government of India Act 1935.

1.4 Commencement and the Role of the President

On 26 January 1950, Dr. Rajendra Prasad took oath as the first President of the Republic of India, and the Constituent Assembly transformed itself into the Provisional Parliament until the first general elections were held in 1951‑52. The President, as the constitutional head of state, was vested with executive powers that are exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. The commencement of the Constitution also marked the beginning of a new legal order in which all pre‑constitutional laws inconsistent with the fundamental rights were rendered void under Article 13. This transition laid the foundation for a democratic polity based on the rule of law, separation of powers and the protection of individual liberties.

Preamble and Salient Features of the Constitution

2.1 The Preamble: Text and Interpretation

The Preamble serves as the philosophical keystone of the Constitution. It declares India to be a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic and secures to all its citizens justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. The words “We, the people of India” emphasise popular sovereignty, indicating that the Constitution derives its authority from the people and not from any external source. The Supreme Court in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973) held that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution and can be used to interpret ambiguous provisions, but it is not itself a source of substantive power. The 42nd Amendment (1976) inserted the words “Socialist” and “Secular” and changed “unity of the Nation” to “unity and integrity of the Nation”, reflecting the political ethos of the period.

2.2 Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic

“Sovereign” means that India is free from external control and can conduct its internal and external affairs independently. “Socialist” was added to stress the commitment to social and economic justice, aiming at the reduction of inequalities in income, status and opportunities. “Secular” signifies that the State has no official religion and treats all religions equally, a principle reinforced by Articles 25 to 28 guaranteeing freedom of religion. “Democratic” indicates that the government derives its authority from the will of the people expressed through periodic elections based on universal adult franchise. “Republic” denotes that the head of state is an elected President, not a hereditary monarch. Together these adjectives define the basic character of the Indian polity and guide the interpretation of all constitutional provisions.

2.3 Fundamental Features: Federalism, Separation of Powers, Judicial Review

The Constitution establishes a quasi‑federal structure with a strong centre, often described as “federal with a unitary bias”. Legislative powers are divided between the Union and the States through the Seventh Schedule, which contains the Union List, State List and Concurrent List. The separation of powers is not absolute; the executive is drawn from the legislature, but the judiciary remains independent. Judicial review, the power of courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative and executive actions, is a basic feature affirmed in the Minerva Mills case (1980). This feature ensures that no organ of the State can exceed its constitutional limits, thereby preserving the supremacy of the Constitution.

2.4 Amendment Procedure and Basic Structure Doctrine

Article 368 provides for three categories of amendment: simple majority, special majority, and special majority with ratification by half the States. The basic structure doctrine, evolved in Kesavananda Bharati and refined in subsequent cases such as Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) and Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981), holds that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution so as to destroy its essential features, including the supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secular character, separation of powers, and federalism. This doctrine acts as a constitutional safeguard against majoritarian excesses and ensures the continuity of the constitutional identity.

Example Question
Which of the following statements about the Preamble is correct? (a) The Preamble is not a part of the Constitution. (b) The Preamble can be amended only by a simple majority. (c) The Preamble reflects the basic structure of the Constitution. (d) The Preamble grants enforceable rights to citizens.
The correct answer is (c). The Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) held that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution and embodies its basic structure. It is not merely a preface; it can be amended under Article 368 but only in a manner consistent with the basic structure doctrine. The Preamble itself does not confer justiciable rights; those are contained in Part III of the Constitution.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks about the “basic structure”, remember that the list is not exhaustive but includes supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. Any amendment that seeks to abrogate these features is liable to be struck down.

Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties

3.1 Fundamental Rights: Articles 12 to 35

Part III of the Constitution enumerates six categories of fundamental rights: right to equality (Articles 14‑18), right to freedom (Articles 19‑22), right against exploitation (Articles 23‑24), right to freedom of religion (Articles 25‑28), cultural and educational rights (Articles 29‑30), and right to constitutional remedies (Article 32). These rights are justiciable, meaning any person can approach the Supreme Court or High Courts for their enforcement. The State is prohibited from making any law that takes away or abridges these rights, and any such law is void to the extent of the inconsistency under Article 13. The Supreme Court has expanded the scope of these rights through liberal interpretation, for instance reading the right to life under Article 21 to include the right to livelihood, privacy, clean environment and speedy trial.

3.2 Directive Principles of State Policy: Articles 36 to 51

Part IV contains the Directive Principles, which are guidelines for the State to achieve social and economic democracy. They are non‑justiciable, meaning courts cannot enforce them directly, but they are fundamental in the governance of the country. Important principles include the promotion of welfare state (Article 38), equal pay for equal work (Article 39(d)), protection of workers’ health and living conditions (Article 42), uniform civil code (Article 44), and promotion of international peace (Article 51). The 42nd Amendment added several new directives, such as the duty to protect the environment (Article 48A) and to safeguard forests and wildlife (Article 48A). Although not enforceable, the courts have used them to interpret fundamental rights and to justify legislative action.

3.3 Fundamental Duties: Article 51A

Inserted by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee, Article 51A lists eleven fundamental duties of citizens, such as to abide by the Constitution, respect its ideals and institutions, cherish the noble ideals of the freedom struggle, uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India, defend the country, promote harmony, value and preserve the rich heritage of composite culture, protect the natural environment, develop scientific temper, safeguard public property, and strive towards excellence. The 86th Amendment (2002) added the duty of parents to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years. These duties are moral obligations; they are not legally enforceable but serve as a reminder that rights and duties are correlative.

3.4 Interrelationship and Judicial Balancing

The Constitution envisages a harmonious relationship between fundamental rights and directive principles. In the early years, the Supreme Court in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) held that fundamental rights prevail over directive principles in case of conflict. However, the 25th Amendment (1971) and the 42nd Amendment attempted to give primacy to directive principles. The landmark judgment in Minerva Mills (1980) restored the balance, declaring that both parts are equally important and that the basic structure includes the harmony between them. The Court has since used directive principles to expand the content of fundamental rights, for example interpreting Article 21 to include the right to health, education and shelter, thereby giving practical effect to the directive principles.

Parliament: Composition, Powers and Legislative Process

4.1 Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha: Composition and Tenure

Parliament consists of the President and two Houses: the Lok Sabha (House of the People) and the Rajya Sabha (Council of States). The Lok Sabha has a maximum strength of 552 members, of which up to 530 represent the States, up to 20 represent the Union Territories, and two may be nominated by the President from the Anglo‑Indian community (though the 104th Amendment discontinued this nomination). Members are directly elected on the basis of universal adult franchise for a term of five years, unless dissolved earlier. The Rajya Sabha has a maximum of 250 members, of which 238 are elected by the elected members of State Legislative Assemblies through proportional representation by means of a single transferable vote, and 12 are nominated by the President for their expertise in literature, science, art and social service. Rajya Sabha members serve staggered six‑year terms, with one‑third retiring every two years, making it a permanent body not subject to dissolution.

4.2 Legislative Procedure: Ordinary Bills, Money Bills, Constitutional Amendments

An ordinary bill can be introduced in either House, except a Money Bill which can only originate in the Lok Sabha. After passage by one House, it is transmitted to the other; if the second House rejects it or proposes amendments not acceptable to the first, a joint sitting may be summoned by the President under Article 108, where the decision is taken by a simple majority of the total membership of both Houses. A Money Bill, defined in Article 110, deals exclusively with taxation, borrowing, expenditure from the Consolidated Fund, and related matters. The Rajya Sabha can only make recommendations on a Money Bill, which the Lok Sabha may accept or reject; there is no joint sitting for Money Bills. Constitutional amendment bills require a special majority of two‑thirds of members present and voting in each House, and for certain federal provisions, ratification by at least half the State Legislatures.

4.3 Parliamentary Committees and Their Functions

Parliamentary committees are essential for detailed scrutiny of legislation, administration and expenditure. They are of two types: standing committees, which are permanent and reconstituted annually, and ad hoc committees, constituted for a specific purpose. Important standing committees include the Public Accounts Committee, which examines the audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General; the Estimates Committee, which suggests economies in administration; the Committee on Public Undertakings, which reviews the performance of public sector enterprises; and department‑related standing committees that examine bills, budgets and policies of specific ministries. These committees operate away from the partisan atmosphere of the House, enabling expert examination and consensus‑building.

4.4 Parliamentary Privileges and Accountability

Members of Parliament enjoy certain privileges under Article 105, such as freedom of speech in the House, immunity from legal proceedings for anything said or any vote given in Parliament, and exemption from jury service. The House also has the power to punish for contempt or breach of privilege. However, these privileges are not absolute; they are subject to the Constitution and cannot be used to obstruct the course of justice. The accountability of the executive to Parliament is exercised through questions, debates, motions, and the work of committees. The confidence of the Lok Sabha is essential for the Council of Ministers to remain in office, embodying the principle of responsible government.

Judiciary: Structure, Independence and Judicial Review

5.1 Supreme Court: Composition, Jurisdiction and Appointment

The Supreme Court is the apex judicial body of India, established under Article 124. It consists of the Chief Justice of India and a maximum of 33 other judges (as fixed by the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 2008). Judges are appointed by the President after consultation with such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as the President deems necessary. The collegium system, evolved through the Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993) and the Third Judges Case (1998), gives primacy to the opinion of the Chief Justice and the four senior‑most judges in matters of appointment and transfer. The Court exercises original jurisdiction in disputes between the Centre and States or among States (Article 131), appellate jurisdiction in civil, criminal and constitutional matters (Articles 132‑136), and advisory jurisdiction at the request of the President (Article 143). Its decisions are binding on all courts within the territory of India.

5.2 High Courts and Subordinate Judiciary

Each State has a High Court (or a common High Court for two or more States) established under Article 214. High Courts have original jurisdiction in certain matters such as writ petitions under Article 226, appellate jurisdiction over subordinate courts, and supervisory jurisdiction over all courts and tribunals within their territorial limits. Judges of High Courts are appointed by the President in consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor of the State and the Chief Justice of the High Court. The subordinate judiciary consists of district courts, sessions courts, and various specialised courts such as family courts, consumer courts and tribunals. The independence of the judiciary is secured by security of tenure (judges hold office until the age of 65 for Supreme Court and 62 for High Courts), salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund, and a removal process requiring proved misbehaviour or incapacity and a two‑thirds majority in each House of Parliament.

5.3 Judicial Review and the Basic Structure Doctrine

Judicial review is the power of the courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders. It is implicit in the Constitution and explicitly recognised in Articles 13, 32, 136, 226 and 227. The basic structure doctrine, first articulated in Kesavananda Bharati (1973), limits the amending power of Parliament by declaring that certain core features of the Constitution cannot be altered. These features include the supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secular character, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. Subsequent cases such as Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) and S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) have expanded the list to include free and fair elections, judicial review itself, and the principle of secularism as an essential feature. This doctrine ensures that the Constitution remains a living document while preserving its identity.

5.4 Public Interest Litigation and Judicial Activism

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) emerged in the late 1970s as a judicial innovation to provide access to justice for disadvantaged groups. The relaxation of the rule of locus standi allowed any public‑spirited citizen or organisation to file a petition on behalf of those whose rights are violated. Landmark PILs include Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) on undertrial prisoners, M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) on environmental protection, and Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) on sexual harassment at workplace. While PIL has expanded the reach of justice, it has also sparked debates on judicial overreach and the separation of powers. The Supreme Court has periodically laid down guidelines to prevent misuse, emphasizing that PIL should not become a substitute for legislative or executive action.

Railway Board: Constitutional Position and Administrative Framework

6.1 Evolution of Railway Administration in India

The railways in India began as private enterprises under British colonial rule, with the first passenger train running between Bombay and Thane in 1853. By the early twentieth century, the network had expanded enormously, and the need for a unified management structure became evident. The Government of India Act 1919 placed railways under a separate Railway Board, which functioned as a commercial undertaking. After independence, the Railway Board was retained as the apex body for policy formulation, planning and operation of the Indian Railways, which remained a departmental undertaking of the Central Government. The Board’s composition and powers have been modified through various executive orders and the Indian Railways Act 1989, which consolidated the legal framework governing railway administration.

6.2 Railway Board under the Ministry of Railways

Presently, the Railway Board functions as the executive arm of the Ministry of Railways, headed by a Chairman who is also the ex‑officio Principal Secretary to the Government of India in the Ministry. The Board comprises the Chairman and four other members responsible for traffic, engineering, mechanical, and electrical matters, together with a Financial Commissioner. The Board exercises powers delegated by the President under the Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, and its decisions are implemented through the zonal railways, production units and other field formations. The Ministry of Railways is a part of the Union Council of Ministers, and the Minister of Railways is accountable to Parliament for the performance and policies of the railways.

6.3 Statutory Powers and Decision‑Making Process

The Indian Railways Act 1989 empowers the Railway Board to make rules for the regulation of railway operations, safety, fares, freight, and the acquisition of land. The Board also issues executive orders, circulars and guidelines that have the force of law within the railway administration. Major policy decisions such as the introduction of new trains, revision of tariffs, investment in infrastructure, and adoption of new technologies are taken by the Board after consultation with the Ministry of Finance, the Planning Commission (now NITI Aayog) and the concerned zonal railways. The Board’s decisions are subject to parliamentary scrutiny through the Railway Budget (merged with the General Budget since 2017), questions, debates and the reports of the Committee on Railways.

6.4 Coordination with Parliament and Judiciary

Although the Railway Board is an executive body, its actions are amenable to judicial review under Articles 32 and 226. Courts have entertained petitions concerning railway safety, land acquisition, environmental impact, and service conditions of railway employees. Parliament exercises control through the annual budgetary process, the passage of railway‑specific legislation such as the Railways (Amendment) Act, and the oversight of the Committee on Railways which examines the reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General on railway finances. This triad of executive management, legislative oversight and judicial review ensures that the Railway Board operates within the constitutional framework while maintaining operational autonomy.
