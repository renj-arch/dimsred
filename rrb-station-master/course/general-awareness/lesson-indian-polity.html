Historical Background and Making of the Constitution

1.1 The Colonial Legacy and the Demand for a Constitution

The constitutional development of India cannot be understood without reference to the long period of British rule that shaped the administrative, legal and political institutions which the framers later inherited. The Government of India Act 1919 introduced a limited dyarchy in the provinces, while the Government of India Act 1935 provided a federal structure with a strong centre, a bicameral legislature at the centre and provincial autonomy. These Acts, however, were imposed from above and did not reflect the sovereign will of the Indian people. The demand for a constitution drafted by Indians themselves grew stronger after the 1929 Lahore session of the Indian National Congress, which declared complete independence as its goal, and the subsequent Civil Disobedience Movement demonstrated mass support for self‑governance. The failure of the Cripps Mission in 1942 and the Quit India Movement further convinced nationalist leaders that a constituent assembly elected by the people was the only legitimate body to frame a constitution.

1.2 The Constituent Assembly and Its Composition

The Constituent Assembly was constituted in November 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan. It originally comprised 389 members, of which 292 were elected by the provincial legislative assemblies and 93 were nominated by the princely states. After the partition of the country in 1947, the membership reduced to 299, with representatives from the provinces that remained in the Indian Union. The Assembly was broadly representative of the major communities, regions and political opinions, including members of the Congress, the Muslim League (until its withdrawal), the Scheduled Castes Federation, the Communist Party and independents. Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected as its President, and the Assembly functioned through a series of committees, the most important being the Drafting Committee chaired by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar. The Assembly met for 11 sessions over a period of 2 years, 11 months and 18 days, deliberating on every article in detail.

1.3 Drafting Process and Adoption

The Drafting Committee prepared the first draft in February 1948, which was circulated for public comments and debated clause by clause in the Assembly. More than 7,000 amendments were moved, of which about 2,400 were accepted. The debates reveal a careful balancing of competing visions: a strong centre versus provincial autonomy, a parliamentary system versus a presidential one, and the inclusion of justiciable fundamental rights versus non‑justiciable directive principles. The final text was adopted on 26 November 1949, and the Constitution came into force on 26 January 1950, a date chosen to commemorate the 1930 declaration of Purna Swaraj. The adoption ceremony was marked by the signing of the document by 284 members, and the Constitution became the supreme law of the land, replacing the Government of India Act 1935.

1.4 Commencement and the Role of the President

On 26 January 1950, Dr. Rajendra Prasad took oath as the first President of the Republic of India, and the Constituent Assembly transformed itself into the Provisional Parliament until the first general elections were held in 1951‑52. The President, as the constitutional head of state, was vested with executive powers that are exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. The commencement of the Constitution also marked the beginning of a new legal order in which all pre‑constitutional laws inconsistent with the fundamental rights were rendered void under Article 13. This transition laid the foundation for a democratic polity based on the rule of law, separation of powers and the protection of individual liberties.

Preamble and Salient Features of the Constitution

2.1 The Preamble: Text and Interpretation

The Preamble serves as the philosophical keystone of the Constitution. It declares India to be a Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic and secures to all its citizens justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. The words “We, the people of India” emphasise popular sovereignty, indicating that the Constitution derives its authority from the people and not from any external source. The Supreme Court in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973) held that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution and can be used to interpret ambiguous provisions, but it is not itself a source of substantive power. The 42nd Amendment (1976) inserted the words “Socialist” and “Secular” and changed “unity of the Nation” to “unity and integrity of the Nation”, reflecting the political ethos of the period.

2.2 Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic

“Sovereign” means that India is free from external control and can conduct its internal and external affairs independently. “Socialist” was added to stress the commitment to social and economic justice, aiming at the reduction of inequalities in income, status and opportunities. “Secular” signifies that the State has no official religion and treats all religions equally, a principle reinforced by Articles 25 to 28 guaranteeing freedom of religion. “Democratic” indicates that the government derives its authority from the will of the people expressed through periodic elections based on universal adult franchise. “Republic” denotes that the head of state is an elected President, not a hereditary monarch. Together these adjectives define the basic character of the Indian polity and guide the interpretation of all constitutional provisions.

2.3 Fundamental Features: Federalism, Separation of Powers, Judicial Review

The Constitution establishes a quasi‑federal structure with a strong centre, often described as “federal with a unitary bias”. Legislative powers are divided between the Union and the States through the Seventh Schedule, which contains the Union List, State List and Concurrent List. The separation of powers is not absolute; the executive is drawn from the legislature, but the judiciary remains independent. Judicial review, the power of courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative and executive actions, is a basic feature affirmed in the Minerva Mills case (1980). This feature ensures that no organ of the State can exceed its constitutional limits, thereby preserving the supremacy of the Constitution.

2.4 Amendment Procedure and Basic Structure Doctrine

Article 368 provides for three categories of amendment: simple majority, special majority, and special majority with ratification by half the States. The basic structure doctrine, evolved in Kesavananda Bharati and refined in subsequent cases such as Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) and Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981), holds that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution so as to destroy its essential features, including the supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secular character, separation of powers, and federalism. This doctrine acts as a constitutional safeguard against majoritarian excesses and ensures the continuity of the constitutional identity.

Example Question
Which of the following statements about the Preamble is correct? (a) The Preamble is not a part of the Constitution. (b) The Preamble can be amended only by a simple majority. (c) The Preamble reflects the basic structure of the Constitution. (d) The Preamble grants enforceable rights to citizens.
The correct answer is (c). The Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) held that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution and embodies its basic structure. It is not merely a preface; it can be amended under Article 368 but only in a manner consistent with the basic structure doctrine. The Preamble itself does not confer justiciable rights; those are contained in Part III of the Constitution.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks about the “basic structure”, remember that the list is not exhaustive but includes supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. Any amendment that seeks to abrogate these features is liable to be struck down.

Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties

3.1 Fundamental Rights: Articles 12 to 35

Part III of the Constitution enumerates six categories of fundamental rights: right to equality (Articles 14‑18), right to freedom (Articles 19‑22), right against exploitation (Articles 23‑24), right to freedom of religion (Articles 25‑28), cultural and educational rights (Articles 29‑30), and right to constitutional remedies (Article 32). These rights are justiciable, meaning any person can approach the Supreme Court or High Courts for their enforcement. The State is prohibited from making any law that takes away or abridges these rights, and any such law is void to the extent of the inconsistency under Article 13. The Supreme Court has expanded the scope of these rights through liberal interpretation, for instance reading the right to life under Article 21 to include the right to livelihood, privacy, clean environment and speedy trial.

3.2 Directive Principles of State Policy: Articles 36 to 51

Part IV contains the Directive Principles, which are guidelines for the State to achieve social and economic democracy. They are non‑justiciable, meaning courts cannot enforce them directly, but they are fundamental in the governance of the country. Important principles include the promotion of welfare state (Article 38), equal pay for equal work (Article 39(d)), protection of workers’ health and living conditions (Article 42), uniform civil code (Article 44), and promotion of international peace (Article 51). The 42nd Amendment added several new directives, such as the duty to protect the environment (Article 48A) and to safeguard forests and wildlife (Article 48A). Although not enforceable, the courts have used them to interpret fundamental rights and to justify legislative action.

3.3 Fundamental Duties: Article 51A

Inserted by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee, Article 51A lists eleven fundamental duties of citizens, such as to abide by the Constitution, respect its ideals and institutions, cherish the noble ideals of the freedom struggle, uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India, defend the country, promote harmony, value and preserve the rich heritage of composite culture, protect the natural environment, develop scientific temper, safeguard public property, and strive towards excellence. The 86th Amendment (2002) added the duty of parents to provide opportunities for education to children between six and fourteen years. These duties are moral obligations; they are not legally enforceable but serve as a reminder that rights and duties are correlative.

3.4 Interrelationship and Judicial Balancing

The Constitution envisages a harmonious relationship between fundamental rights and directive principles. In the early years, the Supreme Court in State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) held that fundamental rights prevail over directive principles in case of conflict. However, the 25th Amendment (1971) and the 42nd Amendment attempted to give primacy to directive principles. The landmark judgment in Minerva Mills (1980) restored the balance, declaring that both parts are equally important and that the basic structure includes the harmony between them. The Court has since used directive principles to expand the content of fundamental rights, for example interpreting Article 21 to include the right to health, education and shelter, thereby giving practical effect to the directive principles.

Parliament: Composition, Powers and Legislative Process

4.1 Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha: Composition and Tenure

Parliament consists of the President and two Houses: the Lok Sabha (House of the People) and the Rajya Sabha (Council of States). The Lok Sabha has a maximum strength of 552 members, of which up to 530 represent the States, up to 20 represent the Union Territories, and two may be nominated by the President from the Anglo‑Indian community (though the 104th Amendment discontinued this nomination). Members are directly elected on the basis of universal adult franchise for a term of five years, unless dissolved earlier. The Rajya Sabha has a maximum of 250 members, of which 238 are elected by the elected members of State Legislative Assemblies through proportional representation by means of a single transferable vote, and 12 are nominated by the President for their expertise in literature, science, art and social service. Rajya Sabha members serve staggered six‑year terms, with one‑third retiring every two years, making it a permanent body not subject to dissolution.

4.2 Legislative Procedure: Ordinary Bills, Money Bills, Constitutional Amendments

An ordinary bill can be introduced in either House, except a Money Bill which can only originate in the Lok Sabha. After passage by one House, it is transmitted to the other; if the second House rejects it or proposes amendments not acceptable to the first, a joint sitting may be summoned by the President under Article 108, where the decision is taken by a simple majority of the total membership of both Houses. A Money Bill, defined in Article 110, deals exclusively with taxation, borrowing, expenditure from the Consolidated Fund, and related matters. The Rajya Sabha can only make recommendations on a Money Bill, which the Lok Sabha may accept or reject; there is no joint sitting for Money Bills. Constitutional amendment bills require a special majority of two‑thirds of members present and voting in each House, and for certain federal provisions, ratification by at least half the State Legislatures.

4.3 Parliamentary Committees and Their Functions

Parliamentary committees are essential for detailed scrutiny of legislation, administration and expenditure. They are of two types: standing committees, which are permanent and reconstituted annually, and ad hoc committees, constituted for a specific purpose. Important standing committees include the Public Accounts Committee, which examines the audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General; the Estimates Committee, which suggests economies in administration; the Committee on Public Undertakings, which reviews the performance of public sector enterprises; and department‑related standing committees that examine bills, budgets and policies of specific ministries. These committees operate away from the partisan atmosphere of the House, enabling expert examination and consensus‑building.

4.4 Parliamentary Privileges and Accountability

Members of Parliament enjoy certain privileges under Article 105, such as freedom of speech in the House, immunity from legal proceedings for anything said or any vote given in Parliament, and exemption from jury service. The House also has the power to punish for contempt or breach of privilege. However, these privileges are not absolute; they are subject to the Constitution and cannot be used to obstruct the course of justice. The accountability of the executive to Parliament is exercised through questions, debates, motions, and the work of committees. The confidence of the Lok Sabha is essential for the Council of Ministers to remain in office, embodying the principle of responsible government.

Judiciary: Structure, Independence and Judicial Review

5.1 Supreme Court: Composition, Jurisdiction and Appointment

The Supreme Court is the apex judicial body of India, established under Article 124. It consists of the Chief Justice of India and a maximum of 33 other judges (as fixed by the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 2008). Judges are appointed by the President after consultation with such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as the President deems necessary. The collegium system, evolved through the Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (1993) and the Third Judges Case (1998), gives primacy to the opinion of the Chief Justice and the four senior‑most judges in matters of appointment and transfer. The Court exercises original jurisdiction in disputes between the Centre and States or among States (Article 131), appellate jurisdiction in civil, criminal and constitutional matters (Articles 132‑136), and advisory jurisdiction at the request of the President (Article 143). Its decisions are binding on all courts within the territory of India.

5.2 High Courts and Subordinate Judiciary

Each State has a High Court (or a common High Court for two or more States) established under Article 214. High Courts have original jurisdiction in certain matters such as writ petitions under Article 226, appellate jurisdiction over subordinate courts, and supervisory jurisdiction over all courts and tribunals within their territorial limits. Judges of High Courts are appointed by the President in consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor of the State and the Chief Justice of the High Court. The subordinate judiciary consists of district courts, sessions courts, and various specialised courts such as family courts, consumer courts and tribunals. The independence of the judiciary is secured by security of tenure (judges hold office until the age of 65 for Supreme Court and 62 for High Courts), salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund, and a removal process requiring proved misbehaviour or incapacity and a two‑thirds majority in each House of Parliament.

5.3 Judicial Review and the Basic Structure Doctrine

Judicial review is the power of the courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders. It is implicit in the Constitution and explicitly recognised in Articles 13, 32, 136, 226 and 227. The basic structure doctrine, first articulated in Kesavananda Bharati (1973), limits the amending power of Parliament by declaring that certain core features of the Constitution cannot be altered. These features include the supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secular character, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. Subsequent cases such as Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) and S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) have expanded the list to include free and fair elections, judicial review itself, and the principle of secularism as an essential feature. This doctrine ensures that the Constitution remains a living document while preserving its identity.

5.4 Public Interest Litigation and Judicial Activism

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) emerged in the late 1970s as a judicial innovation to provide access to justice for disadvantaged groups. The relaxation of the rule of locus standi allowed any public‑spirited citizen or organisation to file a petition on behalf of those whose rights are violated. Landmark PILs include Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) on undertrial prisoners, M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) on environmental protection, and Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) on sexual harassment at workplace. While PIL has expanded the reach of justice, it has also sparked debates on judicial overreach and the separation of powers. The Supreme Court has periodically laid down guidelines to prevent misuse, emphasizing that PIL should not become a substitute for legislative or executive action.

Railway Board: Constitutional Position and Administrative Framework

6.1 Evolution of Railway Administration in India

The railways in India began as private enterprises under British colonial rule, with the first passenger train running between Bombay and Thane in 1853. By the early twentieth century, the network had expanded enormously, and the need for a unified management structure became evident. The Government of India Act 1919 placed railways under a separate Railway Board, which functioned as a commercial undertaking. After independence, the Railway Board was retained as the apex body for policy formulation, planning and operation of the Indian Railways, which remained a departmental undertaking of the Central Government. The Board’s composition and powers have been modified through various executive orders and the Indian Railways Act 1989, which consolidated the legal framework governing railway administration.

6.2 Railway Board under the Ministry of Railways

Presently, the Railway Board functions as the executive arm of the Ministry of Railways, headed by a Chairman who is also the ex‑officio Principal Secretary to the Government of India in the Ministry. The Board comprises the Chairman and four other members responsible for traffic, engineering, mechanical, and electrical matters, together with a Financial Commissioner. The Board exercises powers delegated by the President under the Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules, and its decisions are implemented through the zonal railways, production units and other field formations. The Ministry of Railways is a part of the Union Council of Ministers, and the Minister of Railways is accountable to Parliament for the performance and policies of the railways.

6.3 Statutory Powers and Decision‑Making Process

The Indian Railways Act 1989 empowers the Railway Board to make rules for the regulation of railway operations, safety, fares, freight, and the acquisition of land. The Board also issues executive orders, circulars and guidelines that have the force of law within the railway administration. Major policy decisions such as the introduction of new trains, revision of tariffs, investment in infrastructure, and adoption of new technologies are taken by the Board after consultation with the Ministry of Finance, the Planning Commission (now NITI Aayog) and the concerned zonal railways. The Board’s decisions are subject to parliamentary scrutiny through the Railway Budget (merged with the General Budget since 2017), questions, debates and the reports of the Committee on Railways.

6.4 Coordination with Parliament and Judiciary

Although the Railway Board is an executive body, its actions are amenable to judicial review under Articles 32 and 226. Courts have entertained petitions concerning railway safety, land acquisition, environmental impact, and service conditions of railway employees. Parliament exercises control through the annual budgetary process, the passage of railway‑specific legislation such as the Railways (Amendment) Act, and the oversight of the Committee on Railways which examines the reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General on railway finances. This triad of executive management, legislative oversight and judicial review ensures that the Railway Board operates within the constitutional framework while maintaining operational autonomy.

Constitutional Amendment Process: Special Majorities, State Ratification and Judicial Review

Article 368 governs the amendment of the Constitution and distinguishes three categories of amendment based on the majority required and the need for state ratification. A simple majority of each House of Parliament suffices for amendments that do not affect the federal structure, such as changes to the salaries of judges or the creation of new states under Article 3. Most amendments, however, require a special majority: a majority of the total membership of each House and a two‑thirds majority of members present and voting. This higher threshold applies to provisions concerning fundamental rights, the directive principles, the powers of the President, the Supreme Court and High Courts, and the representation of states in Parliament.

When an amendment seeks to alter the federal character — for example, the distribution of legislative powers under the Seventh Schedule, the representation of states in Parliament, or the extent of the executive power of the Union or the states — Article 368(2) mandates ratification by the legislatures of not less than one‑half of the states. The ratification is by a simple majority in each state legislature; there is no provision for a referendum. The Constitution (Twenty‑fourth Amendment) Act, 1971, and the Constitution (Forty‑second Amendment) Act, 1976, illustrate the use of the special majority, while the Constitution (Seventy‑third Amendment) Act, 1992, and the Constitution (Seventy‑fourth Amendment) Act, 1992, required state ratification because they affected local self‑government, a state subject.

The Supreme Court has asserted the power to review constitutional amendments through the basic structure doctrine, first articulated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973). The Court held that Parliament’s amending power under Article 368 does not extend to altering the basic framework of the Constitution — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. Subsequent cases such as Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) and Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) reinforced that amendments made after 24 April 1973 are subject to judicial review if they damage the basic structure. The Court has struck down provisions of the Constitution (Thirty‑ninth Amendment) Act, 1975, which sought to insulate the election of the Prime Minister from judicial scrutiny, and the Constitution (Forty‑second Amendment) Act, 1976, insofar as it curtailed judicial review.

In practice, the amendment process involves initiation in either House, passage by the requisite majority, and, where required, transmission to state legislatures for ratification. The President’s assent is a formality; the President cannot withhold assent to a constitutional amendment bill. The procedural rigour ensures that changes to the federal balance or fundamental rights cannot be effected by a transient parliamentary majority alone, preserving constitutional stability while allowing necessary evolution.

Centre‑State Legislative Relations: Union, State and Concurrent Lists

The Seventh Schedule distributes legislative competence between the Union and the states through three lists. The Union List (List I) originally contained 97 entries, now 100 after subsequent amendments, covering subjects of national importance such as defence, atomic energy, foreign affairs, currency, banking, inter‑state trade, and railways. The State List (List II) comprises 61 entries (originally 66) including public order, police, public health, agriculture, land, and local government. The Concurrent List (List III) has 52 entries (originally 47) covering criminal law, marriage and divorce, bankruptcy, forests, education, and labour welfare.

Parliament enjoys exclusive power to legislate on Union List subjects. State legislatures have exclusive authority over State List subjects, but Parliament may legislate on a State List matter under four constitutional provisions: Article 249 (resolution by Rajya Sabha supported by two‑thirds majority), Article 250 (during a national emergency), Article 252 (consent of two or more states), and Article 253 (implementation of international treaties). The residuary power of legislation vests in Parliament under Article 248, ensuring that any subject not enumerated falls to the Union.

When both Parliament and a state legislature enact laws on a Concurrent List subject, the doctrine of repugnancy under Article 254 applies. If a state law conflicts with a central law, the central law prevails unless the state law has been reserved for the President’s assent and received it, in which case the state law operates in that state notwithstanding the central law. The Supreme Court has refined this through the “pith and substance” test (State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara, 1951) to determine the true nature of a legislation and the “occupied field” principle to assess whether Parliament has exhausted the legislative space.

Cooperative federalism is institutionalised through the Inter‑State Council (Article 263) and Zonal Councils (under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956). The Goods and Services Tax (GST) regime, introduced by the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016, created a concurrent fiscal domain with a GST Council comprising Union and state representatives, illustrating a modern shift towards shared legislative and fiscal authority. The tension between centralising tendencies — evident in the frequent use of Article 249 and the expansion of the Union List — and state autonomy remains a recurring theme in constitutional discourse and judicial adjudication.

A question asks: “Under which list does the regulation of ‘newspapers’ fall?” Options: (a) Union List, (b) State List, (c) Concurrent List, (d) Residuary.

Emergency Provisions: National Emergency, President’s Rule and Financial Emergency

The Constitution provides for three distinct emergencies, each with specific triggers, parliamentary approval requirements, and consequences for fundamental rights and federal distribution of powers. Article 352 empowers the President to proclaim a National Emergency on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. The proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month by a special majority (majority of total membership and two‑thirds of members present and voting). Once approved, the emergency continues for six months and can be extended indefinitely with repeated parliamentary approval every six months.

During a National Emergency, the executive power of the Union extends to giving directions to states on any matter (Article 353), Parliament gains power to legislate on State List subjects (Article 250), and the life of the Lok Sabha may be extended by one year at a time (Article 83(2)). Articles 358 and 359 suspend the enforcement of fundamental rights under Article 19 automatically and permit the President to suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of other fundamental rights, except Articles 20 and 21. The 44th Amendment (1978) narrowed the scope by requiring that the proclamation specify whether it is on grounds of war/external aggression or armed rebellion, and made the suspension of Article 19 conditional on the latter ground.

Article 356 provides for President’s Rule (State Emergency) when the President, on receipt of a report from the Governor or otherwise, is satisfied that a state government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. The proclamation must be approved by both Houses within two months by simple majority. It remains in force for six months, extendable up to three years with parliamentary approval every six months, subject to the condition that beyond one year, the Election Commission certifies that elections cannot be held. The Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) laid down strict guidelines: the proclamation is subject to judicial review, the Governor’s report must be based on objective material, and the assembly cannot be dissolved before parliamentary approval.

Article 360 deals with Financial Emergency, which has never been proclaimed. If the President is satisfied that the financial stability or credit of India is threatened, a proclamation may be issued, requiring parliamentary approval within two months. During its operation, the Union may direct states to observe canons of financial propriety, reduce salaries of all or any class of persons serving in the state, and reserve money bills for the President’s consideration. The provision remains a constitutional safeguard but has not been invoked, reflecting the resilience of India’s fiscal federalism.

Judicial Appointments: Collegium System, National Judicial Appointments Commission and Current Practice

The appointment of judges to the Supreme Court and High Courts has evolved from executive dominance to a judge‑led collegium system, with a brief statutory interlude. Under the original constitutional scheme (Articles 124 and 217), the President appointed judges after consultation with the Chief Justice of India (CJI) and, for High Courts, the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court. In the First Judges Case (S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981), the Supreme Court held that “consultation” did not mean “concurrence”, giving the executive primacy.

The Second Judges Case (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 1993) reversed this, ruling that the CJI’s opinion, formed in consultation with the two senior‑most Supreme Court judges, would have primacy. The Third Judges Case (1998) expanded the collegium to the CJI and four senior‑most judges for Supreme Court appointments, and for High Courts, the Chief Justice of the High Court plus two senior‑most judges. The collegium’s recommendations are binding on the President, though the government may seek reconsideration once; if the collegium reiterates, the appointment must be made.

In 2014, Parliament enacted the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act and the Constitution (Ninety‑ninth Amendment) Act, establishing a six‑member commission comprising the CJI, two senior Supreme Court judges, the Union Law Minister, and two eminent persons. The Supreme Court struck down both in 2015 (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India), holding that the NJAC violated the basic structure by compromising judicial independence and the primacy of the judiciary in appointments.

Since 2015, the collegium system operates under a Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) finalised in 2017 after negotiations between the judiciary and the government. The MoP prescribes timelines, eligibility criteria, and a transparent process for evaluating candidates, including consultation with the concerned High Court collegium and the state government. Vacancies, delays in appointment, and the lack of a formal secretariat remain contentious issues. The Law Commission’s 230th Report (2009) and the 2020 “Judicial Appointments Commission” Bill (lapsed) reflect ongoing debate on balancing independence, accountability, and diversity in the higher judiciary.

Railway Board Reforms: Restructuring, Corporate Governance and the 2020 Reorganisation

The Railway Board, the apex executive body of Indian Railways, underwent a major restructuring in 2020 aimed at improving corporate governance, decision‑making speed, and accountability. Prior to the reform, the Board consisted of a Chairman (ex‑officio Principal Secretary) and four functional members — Traffic, Engineering, Mechanical, and Electrical — plus a Financial Commissioner. The 2020 reorganisation, approved by the Cabinet, reconstituted the Board with a Chairman & Chief Executive Officer (CEO) and four members designated for Operations & Business Development, Infrastructure, Rolling Stock, and Finance. A new post of Director General (Human Resources) was created to oversee personnel matters across the railway services.

The reform also merged the eight erstwhile Group ‘A’ railway services into a single Indian Railways Management Service (IRMS) in 2021, ending the cadre‑based recruitment that had led to siloed career paths. The IRMS officers are now recruited through a common examination and undergo a unified training programme, facilitating cross‑functional mobility. The Board’s decision‑making process was streamlined: major policy proposals are now routed through the Chairman & CEO, with the four members providing domain expertise, and the Financial Commissioner ensuring fiscal discipline. The Board continues to function under the Ministry of Railways, but the delegation of powers under the Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules was updated to reflect the new structure.

Statutory powers under the Indian Railways Act, 1989, remain unchanged; the Board still makes rules for safety, fares, freight, and land acquisition. However, the 2020 changes introduced a corporate‑style governance framework: the Board now prepares a strategic business plan, monitors key performance indicators, and reports to the Ministry through a structured dashboard. The Railway Budget, merged with the General Budget since 2017, continues to be the primary instrument of parliamentary financial oversight. The Committee on Railways examines the Comptroller and Auditor General’s reports on railway finances, ensuring legislative scrutiny of the Board’s commercial decisions.

Anti‑Defection Law and the Tenth Schedule

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment Act, 1985, sets out the disqualification framework for legislators who defect from the party on whose ticket they were elected. A member incurs disqualification if he voluntarily gives up membership of his political party, votes or abstains contrary to a party whip without prior permission, or joins another party after election. The 91st Amendment Act, 2003, tightened the law by removing the “split” exception (one‑third of a legislature party) and limiting the “merger” exception to a two‑thirds majority of the legislature party agreeing to merge with another party.

The Speaker (or Chairman in the Council of States) is the initial adjudicating authority. The Supreme Court in Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) held that the Speaker’s decision is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fide, perversity, or violation of natural justice. In Rajendra Singh Rana v. Swami Prasad Maurya (2007) the Court clarified that a member’s resignation from the party does not automatically amount to “voluntarily giving up membership” unless accompanied by a public act evidencing intent. The 2018 amendment to the Rules of Procedure of Lok Sabha introduced a 30‑day timeline for the Speaker to decide disqualification petitions, aiming to curb indefinite pendency.

Key procedural safeguards: (i) the petition must be filed by any member of the House; (ii) the Speaker must give the member a hearing; (iii) the decision can be challenged in the High Court under Article 226 and subsequently in the Supreme Court under Article 136. The law does not apply to nominated members of Rajya Sabha or to members who are expelled by their party but do not join another.

Impact on Party Discipline and Floor Tests

Since 1985, the anti‑defection law has been invoked in several high‑profile floor tests — e.g., the 1998 Uttar Pradesh Assembly, the 2017 Goa Assembly, and the 2022 Maharashtra Assembly. Courts have repeatedly refused to intervene in the timing of floor tests, treating them as political questions, while insisting that the Speaker’s disqualification orders be decided before the test to avoid a “lame‑duck” legislature.

President’s Ordinance‑Making Power under Article 123

Article 123 empowers the President to promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session, provided the President is satisfied that circumstances exist which render immediate action necessary. An ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament but must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble. It ceases to operate at the expiration of six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament unless approved earlier; it may also be withdrawn by the President at any time.

The Supreme Court in R. C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) held that the President’s satisfaction is justiciable only on the ground of mala fide. In D. C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987) the Court struck down a series of re‑promulgated ordinances as a “fraud on the Constitution”, establishing that repeated re‑promulgation without legislative approval violates the constitutional scheme. The 2017 judgment in Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar reiterated that ordinance‑making is an emergency power, not a parallel legislative route, and that the President must place the ordinance before Parliament at the earliest opportunity.

Comparison with Governor’s Ordinance Power (Article 213)

The Governor enjoys an analogous power under Article 213, subject to the same six‑week ceiling and the requirement of legislative approval. However, the Governor must obtain the President’s prior assent for ordinances on matters reserved for the President’s consideration (e.g., certain financial bills). The Sarkaria Commission (1988) recommended that the Governor’s ordinance‑making be exercised sparingly and only when the state legislature is not in session.

Safeguards and Parliamentary Scrutiny

Parliamentary committees — particularly the Committee on Subordinate Legislation — examine ordinances for procedural compliance. The 44th Amendment (1978) inserted a provision that an ordinance cannot amend the Constitution. The President’s ordinance‑making power has been used for urgent economic reforms (e.g., the 1991 liberalisation ordinances) and for electoral matters (e.g., the 2013 ordinance on disqualification of convicted legislators).

Finance Commission and Fiscal Federalism

Article 280 mandates the constitution of a Finance Commission every five years to recommend the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States (vertical devolution) and the allocation among States (horizontal devolution). The Commission also recommends principles governing grants‑in‑aid to States under Article 275 and measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities.

The 15th Finance Commission (Chairman: N. K. Singh), constituted in 2017, submitted its report for the period 2021‑26. It retained the 41% vertical share for States (down from 42% recommended by the 14th Commission) and introduced a performance‑based incentive for demographic effort, tax effort, and forest cover. The Commission recommended a total devolution of ₹10.2 lakh crore for 2021‑22, with a special grant of ₹1.2 lakh crore for revenue deficit States. It also addressed the GST compensation shortfall by recommending a continuation of the compensation cess till 2026.

Horizontal Devolution Formula

The 15th Commission used a weighted formula: 45% for income distance, 15% for population (2011 Census), 15% for area, 10% for forest and ecology, 10% for demographic performance, and 5% for tax effort. This marked a shift from the 14th Commission’s heavier reliance on the 1971 Census population (17.5%) and introduced demographic performance to reward States that have controlled population growth.

Grants‑in‑Aid and Conditional Transfers

Besides tax devolution, the Commission recommends revenue deficit grants, sector‑specific grants (health, education, rural roads), and grants for local bodies. The 15th Commission earmarked ₹4.3 lakh crore for local bodies over five years, with a performance‑linked component tied to the achievement of Sustainable Development Goal targets.

Interaction with GST Council

While the Finance Commission determines the share of central taxes, the GST Council decides the rate structure and compensation mechanism. The 15th Commission’s recommendation to extend the compensation cess reflects the need to bridge the revenue gap until the GST regime stabilises. The Commission also suggested a review of the Centre’s cess and surcharge regime, which reduces the divisible pool.

Election Commission: Structure, Independence and Electoral Reforms

Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction, and control of elections in an independent Election Commission (EC). The Commission consists of a Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and such number of Election Commissioners as the President may fix — currently two. They are appointed by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but their removal requires a process akin to that of a Supreme Court judge (proved misbehaviour or incapacity, addressed by Parliament).

The Supreme Court in Election Commission of India v. State of Tamil Nadu (1993) affirmed the EC’s plenary powers under Article 324, including the authority to postpone elections, regulate campaign expenditure, and enforce the Model Code of Conduct (MCC). The MCC, though not statutorily backed, has acquired the force of law through judicial endorsement; violations can attract disqualification under the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

Electoral Reforms and Judicial Interventions

Key reforms: (i) Introduction of Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs) in 1999, with Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT) mandated from 2013; (ii) NOTA (None of the Above) option introduced in 2013 following the Supreme Court directive in People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2013); (iii) Criminalisation of politics addressed by the 2018 amendment requiring candidates to declare pending criminal cases in bold on affidavits; (iv) The 2021 amendment to the Representation of the People Act enabling remote voting for migrant workers (pilot stage).

Independence Safeguards

The CEC and ECs enjoy security of tenure (six years or up to age 65), salary and allowances charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, and cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment. The 2023 Supreme Court judgment in Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India directed that appointments be made by a committee comprising the Prime Minister, Leader of Opposition, and Chief Justice of India, enhancing institutional independence.

Railway Safety, Claims Tribunal and Regulatory Oversight

The Railways Act, 1989, establishes the statutory framework for safety, accident investigation, and compensation. Section 113 empowers the Central Government to appoint a Commissioner of Railway Safety (CRS) under the Ministry of Civil Aviation, who conducts statutory inspections, inquires into accidents, and recommends safety measures. The CRS’s reports are laid before Parliament.

The Railway Claims Tribunal (RCT), constituted under the Railway Claims Tribunal Act, 1987, adjudicates claims for compensation for loss of life, injury, or goods arising from railway accidents. It comprises a Chairman (a former High Court judge) and judicial and technical members. Appeals lie to the High Court under Article 226. The Tribunal’s jurisdiction covers both passenger and freight claims, and it applies the principle of “no fault liability” for death or injury under Section 124 of the Railways Act.

Recent Safety Initiatives

The “Kavach” indigenous Automatic Train Protection (ATP) system, approved in 2022, is being rolled out on high‑density routes to prevent signal passing at danger and collisions. The Railway Safety Fund (RSF), created in 2017, finances track renewal, bridge rehabilitation, and level‑crossing elimination. The 2023 “Mission Zero Accident” targets a 50% reduction in consequential train accidents by 2027 through technology adoption, staff training, and stricter maintenance schedules.

Judicial Oversight and Landmark Cases

In State of Haryana v. Indian Railways (2019), the Supreme Court enhanced compensation for land acquisition for a dedicated freight corridor, applying the “market value plus solatium” principle under the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013. In Union of India v. R. K. Asymmetric Federalism: Special Provisions for States

The Constitution contains a cluster of articles that depart from the uniform federal template, granting certain states distinct legislative, executive, or financial autonomy. Article 370 (now abrogated) conferred special status on Jammu & Kashmir, allowing it a separate constitution, limited Parliament’s legislative reach to defence, foreign affairs, communications, and ancillary matters, and required the state’s concurrence for extending other central laws. Article 371 provides special provisions for Maharashtra and Gujarat, while Articles 371A‑J address the unique historical, cultural, and tribal contexts of Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Andhra Pradesh, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh, and Goa. These provisions typically empower the Governor to discharge special responsibilities — such as law‑and‑order in tribal areas, administration of specified regions, or allocation of development funds — after consulting the Council of Ministers but without being bound by its advice.

The judicial interpretation of these articles has emphasised that they are not mere transitional arrangements but entrenched constitutional guarantees. In S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court held that the President’s power to impose President’s Rule under Article 356 cannot be exercised to undermine the special autonomy guaranteed under Article 371A. Similarly, the Court in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977) ruled that the Governor’s discretionary powers under Article 371 must be exercised in good faith and are subject to judicial review. The 2019 abrogation of Article 370 through a Presidential order under Article 370(3), followed by the Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, illustrates the political‑constitutional tension inherent in asymmetric federalism: the Centre invoked the “constituent power” of Parliament to alter a provision that originally required the concurrence of the state’s constituent assembly, a body that had ceased to exist.

Financial asymmetry is another dimension. The Finance Commission’s horizontal devolution formula incorporates “special category status” for states like the North‑Eastern states, Sikkim, and Himachal Pradesh, granting them a higher share of central taxes and more favourable grant‑in‑aid terms. The GST Council’s voting structure also reflects asymmetry: decisions require a three‑fourths majority of weighted votes, with the Centre holding one‑third of the total weight, but states with special provisions often negotiate specific exemptions or compensation mechanisms.

For aspirants, the key is to distinguish between provisions that are *constitutionally entrenched* (requiring a constitutional amendment under Article 368 with state ratification where applicable) and those that are *statutory* or *executive* in nature. The former cannot be altered by ordinary legislation; the latter can be modified by Parliament or the Executive, subject to the basic structure doctrine.

Money Bill Certification and Judicial Review

Article 110 defines a Money Bill exhaustively: it must contain only provisions dealing with the imposition, abolition, remission, alteration, or regulation of taxes; the borrowing of money or giving of guarantees by the Government of India; the custody of the Consolidated Fund or Contingency Fund; the appropriation of moneys out of the Consolidated Fund; the declaring of expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund; the receipt of money on account of the Consolidated Fund or the audit of accounts; or any matter incidental to these. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha certifies whether a bill is a Money Bill, and this certification is final under Article 110(3). However, the Supreme Court in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) (the Aadhaar judgment) examined the certification of the Aadhaar Act, 2016 as a Money Bill. The majority held that the Speaker’s decision is not immune from judicial review; the Court can examine whether the bill *in substance* falls within the Article 110 categories. The Court found that certain provisions — such as those relating to the establishment of the Unique Identification Authority of India and the authentication ecosystem — were not incidental to the core fiscal matters and therefore could not be passed as a Money Bill. The judgment established a *substantive* test: the pith and substance of the legislation must be confined to the enumerated fiscal heads.

Subsequent cases have refined this test. In Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank (2019), the Court struck down provisions of the Finance Act, 2017 that restructured tribunals, holding that tribunal reforms were unrelated to taxation or Consolidated Fund matters. The Court reiterated that the Speaker’s certification is subject to *limited* judicial scrutiny — limited to whether the bill *prima facie* falls within the Article 110 categories, not to the wisdom of the policy. The Rajya Sabha’s role is restricted to making recommendations within fourteen days; the Lok Sabha may accept or reject them. This procedural asymmetry underscores the constitutional design to prevent the Upper House from blocking fiscal legislation, but the Aadhaar ruling ensures that the designation cannot be used as a conduit for non‑fiscal legislation.

Practically, examiners test the distinction between a *Money Bill* and a *Financial Bill* (Article 117). A Financial Bill contains fiscal provisions *plus* other matters; it requires the President’s recommendation and can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, but the Rajya Sabha has full legislative powers over it. The Finance Bill, introduced annually to give effect to the budget, is a Money Bill. The Appropriation Bill, authorising withdrawals from the Consolidated Fund, is also a Money Bill. Misclassifying a bill that creates a new regulatory authority (e.g., the National Medical Commission Act, 2019) as a Money Bill is a common trap.

Worked Example
A Bill provides for the establishment of a National Railway Safety Authority, its funding from the Consolidated Fund, and the power to frame safety regulations. The Speaker certifies it as a Money Bill. Is the certification valid?
No. Although the Bill involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund, its primary object is the creation of a regulatory authority and the framing of safety regulations — matters not listed in Article 110. Under the *pith and substance* test (Aadhaar judgment), the Bill is a Financial Bill under Article 117, not a Money Bill. The Rajya Sabha would have full amendment powers.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks whether a Bill is a Money Bill, first list the Article 110 categories. Then identify the *dominant purpose* of the Bill. If the dominant purpose falls outside those categories, the Bill cannot be a Money Bill, regardless of incidental fiscal clauses. Remember the Aadhaar and Rojer Mathew precedents for the judicial review standard.

Presidential Pardon Power under Article 72

Article 72 empowers the President to grant pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit, or commute the sentence of any person convicted of an offence against a Union law, or by a court‑martial, or in cases where the punishment is a death sentence. The power is exercised on the advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74), but the Supreme Court has held that the President’s satisfaction is *subject to judicial review* for mala fides, arbitrariness, or non‑application of mind. In Maru Ram v. Union of India (1980), the Court laid down guidelines: the power must be exercised on the basis of relevant considerations such as the nature of the offence, the conduct of the convict, and the interests of society. The Court in Kehar Singh v. Union of India (1989) clarified that the President’s power is *not* a matter of grace alone; it is a constitutional duty that must be exercised fairly and reasonably.

The Governor enjoys a parallel power under Article 161 for offences against state laws. However, the President’s power extends to death sentences and court‑martial cases, which are outside the Governor’s jurisdiction. The 1994 Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh case affirmed that judicial review of clemency orders is limited to checking whether the decision was *arbitrary, mala fide, or based on irrelevant considerations*. The Court refused to substitute its own judgment for that of the executive.

Procedurally, the Ministry of Home Affairs processes mercy petitions, seeks the views of the concerned state government (for state offences) and the judiciary (for death sentences), and forwards recommendations to the President. The President may also seek the advice of the Attorney General. The 2014 Shatrughan Chauhan v. Union of India judgment mandated that the President must decide mercy petitions within a reasonable time, and undue delay can be a ground for commuting a death sentence to life imprisonment.

For examination purposes, distinguish between *pardon* (absolves the conviction), *commutation* (substitutes a lighter punishment), *remission* (reduces the quantum without changing the nature), *reprieve* (temporary stay), and *respite* (lesser sentence due to special circumstances). Also note that the President’s pardoning power does not extend to *legislative* disqualifications (e.g., under the Representation of the People Act) — those are governed by separate statutory provisions.

Inter‑State Council and Zonal Councils: Institutionalising Cooperative Federalism

Article 263 empowers the President to establish an Inter‑State Council (ISC) if it appears to him that the public interest would be served by such a council. The ISC was constituted in 1990 on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission. Its composition includes the Prime Minister (Chairman), Chief Ministers of all states, Chief Ministers of Union Territories with legislatures, Administrators of UTs without legislatures, and six Cabinet Ministers nominated by the Prime Minister. The Council’s mandate is to inquire into and advise upon disputes between states, investigate and discuss subjects of common interest, and make recommendations for better coordination of policy and action. The ISC meets *at least* thrice a year, though in practice meetings have been irregular.

Zonal Councils, created under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, are statutory bodies (not constitutional) divided into five zones — Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, and Southern — plus the North‑Eastern Council (established 1971). Each Zonal Council comprises the Union Home Minister (Chairman), Chief Ministers of the states in the zone, two other ministers from each state, and the Administrator of each UT in the zone. They focus on regional issues such as border disputes, water sharing, transport, and economic planning. The North‑Eastern Council has a broader mandate, including socio‑economic development and security coordination.

The functional difference is crucial: the ISC is a *constitutional* forum for *national‑level* coordination and dispute resolution, while Zonal Councils are *statutory* regional platforms. The ISC’s recommendations are *advisory*; they do not bind the Centre or states. However, the Supreme Court in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977) observed that the Centre should *generally* act on ISC recommendations to preserve federal harmony. The 2021 ISC meeting, after a gap of five years, discussed the implementation of the National Education Policy, COVID‑19 management, and the GST compensation cess.

Examiners often test the *legal basis* (Article 263 vs States Reorganisation Act), *composition* (Prime Minister vs Home Minister as Chair), and *frequency* of meetings. A common error is to conflate the ISC with the National Development Council (NDC), which was a non‑constitutional body chaired by the Prime Minister and replaced by the NITI Aayog’s Governing Council in 2015.

Legislative Councils in States: Creation, Abolition, and Role in Bicameralism

Article 169 empowers Parliament to provide for the creation or abolition of a Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad) in a state, provided the state Legislative Assembly passes a resolution by a *special majority* (a majority of the total membership and not less than two‑thirds of members present and voting). The Council’s size cannot exceed one‑third of the Assembly’s strength, with a minimum of 40 members. Members are elected through a complex electoral college: one‑third by local authorities, one‑twelfth by graduates, one‑twelfth by teachers, one‑third by members of the Legislative Assembly, and the remainder nominated by the Governor from persons with special knowledge in literature, science, art, cooperative movement, and social service.

Currently, six states have Legislative Councils: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, and Uttar Pradesh. Tamil Nadu abolished its Council in 1986; West Bengal abolished its in 1969; Punjab and Assam never had one. The 2019 resolution by the Jammu & Kashmir Legislative Assembly (before reorganisation) to create a Council lapsed due to the state’s bifurcation. The power to abolish a Council also rests with Parliament on a similar Assembly resolution.

The Council’s legislative role mirrors that of the Rajya Sabha at the state level: it can delay non‑Money Bills for up to three months (first passage) and one month (second passage), but cannot amend or reject Money Bills. Doctrine of Colourable Legislation

The doctrine of colourable legislation prevents a legislature from doing indirectly what it cannot do directly. When a law appears, on its face, to be within the legislative competence of the enacting body but in substance encroaches upon a field reserved for another legislature, courts may strike it down as a colourable exercise of power. The test is whether the legislation, though formally within the allotted list, is in reality a fraud on the Constitution — a device to bypass the distribution of powers under the Seventh Schedule.

The Supreme Court first articulated the principle in K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1954), holding that a state law imposing a tax on the sale of motor spirit, though labelled a tax on “sale”, was in effect a tax on the production of motor spirit, a Union subject. The Court emphasized that the pith and substance of the enactment must be examined; if the true character reveals an overreach, the law is void irrespective of its label.

Key elements of the doctrine:

  • Competence vs. motive: The legislature’s motive is irrelevant; only the effect of the law matters.
  • Substance over form: Courts look beyond the legislative label to the real nature and character of the measure.
  • No presumption of invalidity: The burden lies on the challenger to demonstrate that the law is a colourable exercise.

Application extends to both Union and State legislatures. In State of Bihar v. Kameshwar Singh (1952), a Bihar land‑reform law that purported to regulate “estates” but effectively abolished zamindari without compensation was held colourable because the real object was to circumvent the constitutional guarantee of compensation under Article 31 (pre‑1978). Conversely, in Union of India v. H. S. Dhillon (1972), a central law regulating inter‑state trade was upheld because its dominant purpose fell squarely within Entry 33 of the Union List.

Examiners often test the distinction between colourable legislation and legislative incompetence. The former assumes the legislature has formal competence but abuses it; the latter means the legislature never had the power at all. A typical trap question presents a state law on “public health” that incidentally regulates a Union‑list industry; the correct answer hinges on whether the dominant purpose is health (State List) or industrial regulation (Union List).

Doctrine of Pith and Substance

The doctrine of pith and substance is the principal tool for resolving conflicts between overlapping entries in the Union, State and Concurrent Lists. When a law enacted by one legislature incidentally trenches upon a subject assigned to another, the court determines the true nature and character — the “pith and substance” — of the legislation. If the dominant purpose falls within the enacting legislature’s competence, the incidental encroachment is permissible.

The classic formulation appears in State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara (1951), where a Bombay prohibition law affecting the import and possession of liquor (a Union subject under Entry 19 of the Union List) was upheld because its pith and substance was the regulation of liquor consumption, a State subject under Entry 8 of the State List. The Court held that incidental effects on import did not invalidate the law.

Steps in the analysis:

  1. Identify the dominant purpose of the statute by examining its preamble, title, scheme and operative provisions.
  2. Locate the entry in the Seventh Schedule that best matches that purpose.
  3. If the dominant purpose matches the enacting legislature’s list, the law is valid even if it incidentally touches another list.

The doctrine does not permit a legislature to colourably label a law to disguise its true aim. In K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo (cited above), the Court applied pith and substance to find the real object was taxation of production, not sale, and therefore struck the law down as colourable. The two doctrines thus operate together: pith and substance determines competence; colourable legislation checks for abuse of that competence.

Important nuances for examination:

  • The doctrine applies only when there is a genuine overlap; it cannot be invoked to justify a law that has no connection to the enacting legislature’s list.
  • For Concurrent List subjects, both Union and State may legislate; if a State law is repugnant to a Union law, Article 254 resolves the conflict — the Union law prevails unless the State law has been reserved for Presidential assent and received it.
  • The Supreme Court in G. K. Krishnan v. State of Tamil Nadu (1975) reiterated that the dominant purpose test is a question of fact, not a mechanical counting of provisions.
Worked Example: Identifying the Dominant Purpose
A State enacts the “Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation) Act” which provides for the establishment of market yards, licensing of traders, and levy of a market fee on all agricultural produce sold within the State. The Union argues the fee constitutes a tax on inter‑state trade (Union List Entry 92A). Determine the validity of the State law.
Step 1: Examine the Act’s scheme — its primary objective is to regulate the marketing of agricultural produce within the State, a subject under State List Entry 28 (markets and fairs). Step 2: The market fee is an ancillary financial measure to fund the regulatory machinery; it is not a tax on inter‑state trade per se. Step 3: Applying the pith and substance test, the dominant purpose is intra‑State market regulation, a State competence. The incidental effect on inter‑state trade does not render the law ultra vires. Therefore, the State law is valid.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question presents a law with a mixed object, first write down the two competing List entries. Then ask: “What is the *main* mischief the statute seeks to cure?” The answer points to the dominant purpose. Do not be distracted by ancillary provisions; they are relevant only for the colourable legislation check.

Doctrine of Eclipse and Severability

The doctrines of eclipse and severability address the fate of pre‑Constitutional and post‑Constitutional laws that conflict with Fundamental Rights. Both operate under Article 13, which declares that laws inconsistent with Part III are void to the extent of the inconsistency.

Doctrine of Eclipse: A pre‑Constitutional statute that violates a Fundamental Right does not die; it becomes eclipsed — dormant but not dead. The law remains on the statute book and can revive if the constitutional impediment is removed (e.g., by amendment). The Supreme Court in Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955) held that a pre‑1950 motor‑vehicles law imposing a tax on passengers was eclipsed by Article 19(1)(g) but revived when the Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act, 1955, inserted Article 19(6) permitting reasonable restrictions.

Key points:

  • Applies only to pre‑Constitutional laws (enacted before 26 January 1950).
  • The law is not void ab initio; it is merely unenforceable while the Fundamental Right exists.
  • Revival occurs automatically when the constitutional barrier is lifted; no fresh legislation is required.

Doctrine of Severability: When a statute contains both valid and invalid provisions, the court may sever the offending part and preserve the remainder, provided the valid portion is independent and capable of standing alone. The test, laid down in R. M. D. C. v. Union of India (1957), asks whether the legislature would have enacted the valid part without the invalid part. If the provisions are so intertwined that the valid part cannot survive independently, the whole statute falls.

Illustration: In State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills (1974), a labour welfare fund act contained a clause violating Article 14. The Court severed the discriminatory clause and upheld the rest because the remaining scheme was complete and workable.

Examination traps:

  • Confusing eclipse (pre‑Constitutional) with severability (any statute).
  • Assuming that a constitutional amendment automatically revives an eclipsed law — revival requires that the amendment removes the specific inconsistency.
  • Believing severability can save a statute where the invalid provision is the core of the legislative scheme.

Constitutional Provisions for Union Territories and Special Status

Union Territories (UTs) are administered directly by the President through an Administrator (Lieutenant Governor, Chief Commissioner or Administrator) under Article 239. The constitutional framework distinguishes between UTs with a legislature (Delhi, Puducherry, Jammu & Kashmir after 2019) and those without (Andaman & Nicobar, Chandigarh, Dadra & Nagar Haveli & Daman & Diu, Lakshadweep, Ladakh).

Delhi (National Capital Territory): Article 239AA, inserted by the 69th Amendment (1991), grants Delhi a Legislative Assembly and a Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister. However, Parliament retains supreme legislative power over all matters in the State List and Concurrent List for Delhi (Article 239AA(3)). The Lieutenant Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except in matters of public order, police and land, where the LG may refer differences to the President (Article 239AA(4)). The Supreme Court in Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018) held that the LG’s concurrence is not required for every decision; the LG must act on ministerial aid except in the three reserved subjects.

Puducherry: Article 239A provides for a legislature with powers similar to a State legislature but subject to Parliament’s overriding authority. The Administrator (Lieutenant Governor) exercises functions analogous to a Governor.

Jammu & Kashmir (post‑2019): The Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, bifurcated the former State into two UTs — Jammu & Kashmir (with legislature) and Ladakh (without legislature). The UT of Jammu & Kashmir now functions under Article 239A read with the Reorganisation Act; its legislative assembly has powers over State List subjects except public order and police, which remain with the Centre.

Special provisions for certain UTs: Articles 239B‑239E empower the President to make regulations for the peace, progress and good government of UTs without legislatures. These regulations have the force of law and can amend or repeal any law made by Parliament for that UT.

Key examination focus:

  • Distinguish the legislative competence of Delhi vs. Puducherry vs. other UTs.
  • Remember that Parliament can legislate on any subject for any UT (Article 246(4)).
  • The LG’s discretionary powers are limited to the three reserved subjects in Delhi; in other UTs the Administrator acts on the President’s directions.

Constitutional Provisions for Panchayats, Municipalities and Cooperative Federalism

Parts IX (Articles 243‑243O) and IXA (Articles 243P‑243ZG) of the Constitution, inserted by the 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992), institutionalise rural and urban local self‑government. They aim to deepen democratic decentralisation and operationalise cooperative federalism at the grassroots.

Panchayats (Part IX): Article 243B mandates a three‑tier structure (village, intermediate, district) in every State with a population over 20 lakhs; smaller States may have two tiers. Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and women (not less than one‑third). The State Election Commission (Article 243K) conducts elections; the State Finance Commission (Article 243I) reviews finances every five years and recommends devolution of taxes, duties, tolls and grants‑in‑aid.

Municipalities (Part IXA): Article 243Q provides for three types — Nagar Panchayat (transitional area), Municipal Council (smaller urban area) and Municipal Corporation (larger urban area). Composition, reservation, elections and finance commissions mirror the Panchayat framework (Articles 243R‑243ZG). The 74th Amendment also introduces the District Planning Committee (Article 243ZD) and Metropolitan Planning Committee (Article 243ZE) to integrate spatial planning.

Cooperative Federalism dimensions: While local bodies are State subjects (Entry 5, State List), the Constitution obliges States to endow them with powers and authority (Article 243G, 243W). The Union influences local governance through Centrally Sponsored Schemes (e.g., MGNREGA, Smart Cities Mission) and the Finance Commission’s vertical and horizontal devolution formulas, which now include grants for local bodies. The Supreme Court in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) recognised that effective decentralisation is integral to the federal structure.

Examination highlights:

  • Know the mandatory vs. discretionary provisions (e.g., three‑tier Panchayat is mandatory for States >20 lakhs; otherwise optional).
  • Reservation percentages: SC/ST proportionate to population; women not less than one‑third of total seats and offices of chairpersons.
  • State Finance Commission vs. Union Finance Commission — distinct roles, but both affect local body resources.
  • Article 243ZD (District Planning Committee) and 243ZE (Metropolitan Planning Committee) are often tested for composition and function.
  • Treating the doctrine of eclipse as applicable to post‑Constitutional laws.
  • Assuming severability can rescue a statute when the invalid provision is the legislative core.
  • Confusing the Lieutenant Governor’s discretion in Delhi (three reserved subjects) with the broader discretion of Administrators in UTs without legislatures.
  • Believing Parliament’s power over Union Territories is limited to the Union List; Article 246(4) gives Parliament plenary power for UTs.
  • Thinking the 73rd/74th Amendments make local bodies a third tier of federal government; they remain State subjects with constitutional safeguards.
  • Overlooking that the State Finance Commission’s recommendations are advisory, not binding, on the State Government.
  • Misapplying the pith and substance test to a law that has no genuine overlap between Lists.

These advanced doctrines and institutional details complete the polity coverage required for high‑level competitive examinations.

Doctrine of Harmonious Construction in Centre‑State Legislative Conflicts

The doctrine of harmonious construction requires courts to interpret seemingly contradictory constitutional provisions in a manner that gives effect to both, rather than allowing one to override the other. In the Indian federal scheme, the Union List (List I), State List (List II) and Concurrent List (List III) often contain overlapping subject‑matters. When a Union law and a State law touch the same field, the Supreme Court applies harmonious construction to ascertain whether the two statutes can operate together without conflict. The classic formulation appears in State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963), where the Court held that entries in the three Lists must be read together so that each retains its intended scope. If a genuine overlap exists, the principle of federal supremacy (Article 254) resolves the clash, but only after an attempt at harmonious reading has failed.

The technique involves three steps: first, identify the legislative competence conferred by each entry; second, examine the language of the competing statutes to see whether they can be reconciled by a narrow or purposive interpretation; third, if reconciliation is impossible, apply the repugnancy test under Article 254(1) which gives precedence to the Union law, subject to the State law having been reserved for the President’s assent under Article 254(2). The doctrine thus preserves the federal balance by preventing a mechanical “first‑in‑time” rule and by respecting the constitutional design that both Parliament and State Legislatures possess plenary powers within their respective spheres.

Landmark applications include M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India (1979), where the Court harmonised the Union’s power over “industries” (Entry 52, List I) with the State’s power over “trade and commerce within the State” (Entry 26, List II) by reading the Central statute as regulating only inter‑State aspects. In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977), the Court used harmonious construction to uphold a State law on agricultural marketing while recognising the Centre’s legislation on essential commodities. The doctrine also underpins the “pith and substance” analysis, because the true character of a law is ascertained only after the overlapping entries have been read harmoniously.

Examination focus: distinguish harmonious construction from the doctrine of pith and substance (the former is a rule of interpretation, the latter a test of legislative competence). Remember that harmonious construction is attempted before invoking Article 254; a direct jump to repugnancy without attempting reconciliation is a common error. Also note that the doctrine applies only when both laws are validly enacted under their respective entries; it cannot save a law that lacks legislative competence altogether.

Article 254 and the Repugnancy Test: Union Law Prevails Over State Law

Article 254 of the Constitution provides the definitive rule for resolving inconsistency between a Union law and a State law on a Concurrent List subject. Clause (1) states that if any provision of a State law is repugnant to a provision of a Union law, the Union law shall prevail and the State law shall, to the extent of the repugnancy, be void. Clause (2) creates an exception: a State law reserved for the President’s consideration and receiving his assent can prevail in that State, notwithstanding the repugnancy, but Parliament retains the power to override it subsequently by enacting a law on the same matter.

The Supreme Court has laid down a two‑stage test for repugnancy. First, there must be a direct conflict such that the two provisions cannot stand together — obedience to one necessarily entails disobedience to the other. Mere overlap or duplication is insufficient; the conflict must be “irreconcilable”. Second, the Union law must be a valid exercise of legislative competence under the Concurrent List. In M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India (1979), the Court held that a State law on “prevention of corruption” was repugnant to the Central Prevention of Corruption Act, 1947, because both prescribed different procedures for the same offence, making simultaneous compliance impossible. Conversely, in State of Maharashtra v. Bharat Shanti Lal Shah (2008), the Court found no repugnancy where the State law merely supplemented the Central statute by adding procedural safeguards.

An important nuance is the “occupied field” doctrine. If Parliament has legislated exhaustively on a Concurrent subject, the State legislature is deemed to have been ousted from that field even without an explicit repugnancy clause. The test for exhaustion is whether the Central law covers the entire legislative space, leaving no room for State supplementation. The Court in Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1983) applied this principle to hold that the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, occupied the field of drug regulation, rendering a State amendment void.

Procedurally, a State law that is repugnant can be saved only if it has been reserved for the President’s assent under Article 254(2). The President’s assent is a political act, but once given, the State law operates in that State until Parliament enacts a contrary law. The President may also return the Bill for reconsideration. Examination questions often test the distinction between “repugnancy” (Article 254) and “ultra vires” (lack of legislative competence). A law that exceeds the State’s entry is void ab initio, whereas a repugnant law is valid until the Union law prevails.

Worked Example: Repugnancy Analysis
A State enacts the “State Agricultural Produce Marketing Act, 2022” regulating the sale of wheat within the State. Parliament has already enacted the “Essential Commodities Act, 1955” which empowers the Centre to control the production, supply and distribution of wheat. The State Act fixes a minimum support price higher than the Central notification. A trader challenges the State Act. Decide the validity of the State Act.
Step 1 – Identify legislative entries: Wheat falls under “trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, foodstuffs” (Entry 33, List III – Concurrent). Both Parliament and the State Legislature are competent.
Step 2 – Check for direct conflict: The State Act mandates a higher minimum price than the Central notification. A trader cannot simultaneously comply with both; obedience to the State Act violates the Central order. This is an irreconcilable conflict.
Step 3 – Apply Article 254(1): The Union law (Essential Commodities Act) prevails; the State Act is void to the extent of repugnancy.
Step 4 – Consider Article 254(2) exception: The State Act was not reserved for the President’s assent. Hence the exception does not apply.
Conclusion: The State Agricultural Produce Marketing Act, 2022 is unconstitutional insofar as it fixes a minimum price contrary to the Central notification.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question presents a Centre‑State clash on a Concurrent subject, first verify that both laws derive authority from the same Concurrent entry. Then apply the two‑stage repugnancy test: (1) irreconcilable conflict, (2) Union law’s competence. Only if both are satisfied does Article 254(1) operate. Remember the President’s assent route under Article 254(2) is a narrow escape hatch — it must be expressly pleaded and proved. In MCQs, the phrase “occupied field” signals that Parliament has legislated exhaustively; the State law is then void without needing a direct conflict.

President’s Reference Power under Article 143: Scope, Procedure and Landmark References

Article 143 empowers the President to refer to the Supreme Court any question of law or fact which he considers of public importance. The Court’s opinion is advisory, not binding, but carries immense persuasive authority. The reference may be made either on the President’s own initiative or on the advice of the Council of Ministers. The Supreme Court sits in a bench of at least five judges (Article 145(3)) and delivers its opinion after hearing the Attorney General and any other parties the Court permits.

The scope of Article 143 is broad: it covers constitutional interpretation, validity of legislation, and even questions of fact that have a bearing on constitutional issues. However, the Court has declined to answer questions that are purely political, hypothetical, or relate to matters pending before a court of law. In In re: Kerala Education Bill (1958), the Court held that the reference power cannot be used to circumvent the normal judicial process. In In re: Special Courts Bill (1978), the Court opined on the constitutionality of a bill before it became law, establishing that pre‑legislative references are permissible.

Notable references include the 1998 Presidential Reference on the appointment of judges (the “Second Judges Case” advisory opinion), the 2002 reference on the Gujarat communal violence (where the Court declined to answer certain factual questions), and the 2019 reference on the constitutionality of the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 (the Court refused to entertain the reference as the matter was sub‑judice). The advisory opinions have shaped doctrine — for instance, the 1998 opinion reinforced the collegium system by affirming the primacy of the Chief Justice of India in judicial appointments.

Procedure: the President forwards the reference to the Supreme Court; the Court may ask for written submissions, hold oral hearings, and then deliver a unanimous or majority opinion. Dissenting opinions are recorded but the final advisory opinion is the majority view. The President is not bound to act on the opinion, but in practice the government respects it. Examination focus: distinguish Article 143 from Article 131 (original jurisdiction) and Article 32 (writ jurisdiction). Article 143 is advisory, non‑binding, and can be invoked only by the President; Article 131 is binding and involves disputes between the Centre and States; Article 32 is a fundamental right remedy.

Governor’s Discretionary Powers under Article 163: Constitutional Limits and Judicial Review

Article 163(1) provides that there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Chief Minister at the head to aid and advise the Governor in the exercise of his functions, except in so far as he is required by the Constitution to act in his discretion. Clause (2) states that the question whether any matter falls within the Governor’s discretion shall be decided by the Governor himself, and his decision shall be final. This textual formulation has generated extensive litigation on the scope of “discretion”.

The Supreme Court in S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) held that the Governor’s discretion is not absolute; it is subject to judicial review for mala fides, arbitrariness, or violation of constitutional provisions. The Court identified three core areas where the Governor may act without ministerial advice: (i) appointment of a Chief Minister when no party has a clear majority; (ii) dismissal of a Ministry that has lost the confidence of the Assembly but refuses to resign; (iii) reservation of a State Bill for the President’s consideration under Article 200. In Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006), the Court struck down the Governor’s decision to dissolve the Assembly on the ground that it was based on extraneous considerations, affirming that the Governor’s satisfaction must be based on objective material.

Further, in Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016), the Court ruled that the Governor’s power to summon, prorogue or dissolve the Legislative Assembly under Article 174 is not a discretionary power but must be exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. The Governor cannot act unilaterally to advance a political agenda. The Court also clarified that the Governor’s “discretion” under Article 163(2) is not a blank cheque; the finality clause does not oust judicial review of constitutional legality.

Examination traps: confusing the Governor’s discretionary powers with the President’s powers under Article 74 (where the President acts on aid and advice). Remember that the Governor’s discretion is limited to the three situations enumerated in Bommai; any other unilateral action is unconstitutional. Also, the Governor’s report under Article 356 (President’s Rule) is not a discretionary act but a constitutional duty; its adequacy is reviewable. Questions often test the distinction between “discretion” (subjective satisfaction) and “constitutional obligation” (objective criteria).

Comptroller and Auditor General: Constitutional Mandate, Independence and Audit Jurisdiction

Articles 148 to 151 establish the Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG) as the supreme audit institution. The CAG is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal (Article 148(1)) and can be removed only in the manner and on the grounds applicable to a Supreme Court judge (Article 148(2)), guaranteeing security of tenure. The salary and conditions of service are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (Article 148(3)), insulating the office from executive pressure.

The CAG’s audit mandate extends to all expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India, the Consolidated Fund of each State, and the Contingency Fund and Public Account (Article 149). The CAG also audits the accounts of any body or authority substantially financed by the Union or State governments, including government companies and corporations (Section 19 of the CAG’s (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971). The 2013 Companies Act further empowers the CAG to conduct supplementary audit of government companies.

Independence is reinforced by the requirement that the CAG’s reports be laid before each House of Parliament and each State Legislature (Article 151). The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) examines these reports, and the CAG appears before the PAC to elucidate findings. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. CAG (1995) affirmed that the CAG’s audit opinion is not subject to executive override. The CAG also performs performance audits, evaluating economy, efficiency and effectiveness of programmes — a function highlighted in the 2G spectrum allocation audit (2010) and the coal block allocation audit (2012).

Key examination points: the CAG does not audit the accounts of local bodies unless they are substantially financed by the Union or State (Article 243J and 243Z). The CAG’s role is distinct from the Finance Commission (which recommends tax devolution) and the GST Council (which decides tax rates). The CAG’s reports are not binding but carry strong persuasive weight; the executive is expected to take corrective action. Remember that the CAG’s appointment and removal procedures mirror those of a Supreme Court judge, a fact frequently tested in comparative constitutional questions.

  • Treating Article 254(2) as an automatic shield for any State law reserved for the President’s assent, ignoring Parliament’s power to override.
  • Confusing the President’s advisory jurisdiction under Article 143 with the Supreme Court’s original jurisdiction under Article 131.
  • Believing the Governor’s discretion under Article 163 is unlimited; it is confined to the three Bommai situations.
  • Thinking the CAG audits all local bodies; audit extends only to those substantially financed by the Union or State.
  • Equating the CAG’s audit opinion with a judicial order; it is advisory to the legislature, not enforceable by courts.
  • Overlooking that the Governor’s decision to reserve a Bill for the President is a discretionary act, but the President’s assent is a political act not subject to judicial review on merits.
