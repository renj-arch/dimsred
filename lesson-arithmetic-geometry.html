Arithmetic Geometry — Schemes over ℤ, the Weil Conjectures & the Langlands Program

Arithmetic geometry is the study of solutions to polynomial equations over number fields and finite fields via the methods of algebraic geometry. A scheme over ℤ glues arithmetic and geometric data: X → Spec ℤ encodes the integral structure, with a fiber over each prime p. Arakelov theory compactifies Spec ℤ by adding an archimedean fiber, equipping the Riemann surface with a Hermitian metric to define intersection numbers at infinity. The Mordell-Weil theorem shows the rational points on an abelian variety form a finitely generated abelian group. The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture links the rank of an elliptic curve to the order of vanishing of its L-function at s = 1. The Weil conjectures (proved by Grothendieck and Deligne) describe the zeta function of a variety over a finite field via étale cohomology. The Langlands program provides a vast web of reciprocity laws linking Galois representations to automorphic forms — culminating in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem.

"Arithmetic geometry is the ultimate synthesis of number theory and algebraic geometry."
— Alexander Grothendieck
Part 1

📏 Schemes over ℤ and Arithmetic Surfaces

Arithmetic Schemes
Schemes over ℤ, Neron Models, and Arakelov Theory
A scheme X → Spec ℤ is an integral model of a variety over ℚ. The fiber over each prime p is X_p = X ×_{Spec ℤ} Spec 𝔽_p — the reduction mod p. An arithmetic surface is a regular 2-dimensional scheme X → Spec ℤ. For an elliptic curve E over a number field K, the Neron model is a smooth group scheme ℰ → Spec O_K (the ring of integers) whose generic fiber is E. The special fibers decompose according to the Kodaira-Neron classification of singular fibers: types I₀ (good reduction), I_m (multiplicative reduction), II, III, IV (additive reduction), etc. The minimal regular model removes all (-1)-curves from the desingularization. The intersection pairing on an arithmetic surface X: for two vertical divisors, the intersection number ⟨D, D'⟩ is computed via the Euler characteristic of the structure sheaf — the Noether formula: 12χ(𝒪_X) = (K_X)² + c₂(T_X). At the archimedean place, Arakelov theory equips the Riemann surface E_∞(ℂ) with a Hermitian metric and the canonical volume form, defining an intersection number at infinity via Green's functions and the Laplace operator.
Schemes over ℤ encode number-theoretic information in geometric fibers over each prime; Arakelov theory compactifies the picture by adding archimedean data with Hermitian metrics.
Part 2

🌍 The Mordell-Weil Theorem and the BSD Conjecture

Abelian Varieties
Mordell-Weil Theorem, Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer, and Heegner Points
The Mordell-Weil theorem: for an abelian variety A over a number field K, the group A(K) of K-rational points is finitely generated. The proof uses the weak Mordell-Weil theorem (A(K)/nA(K) is finite via Galois cohomology and Kummer theory) and the descent method (height functions to bound generators). The rank r = rank A(K) is a central invariant. The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture (BSD): for an elliptic curve E/K, the L-function L(E, s) satisfies L(E, s) ∼ c·(s−1)^r as s → 1, where r = rank E(K). The full BSD conjecture states that the residue of L(E, s) at s = 1 equals (Ω · Reg · Π c_v · |Sha|) / |E(K)_{tors}|², where Ω is the real period, Reg is the regulator, c_v are Tamagawa numbers, and Sha is the Tate-Shafarevich group. The work of Gross-Zagier and Kolyvagin: for modular elliptic curves (every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular by Wiles-Breuil-Conrad-Diamond-Taylor), if L(E, 1) ≠ 0 then rank = 0; if L(E, 1) = 0 but L'(E, 1) ≠ 0 then rank = 1 (using Heegner points). The Sato-Tate conjecture (proved by Clozel-Harris-Taylor-Barnet-Lamb): for an elliptic curve without complex multiplication, the Frobenius angles θ_p = arccos(a_p / 2√p) are distributed according to the Sato-Tate measure (2/π) sin²θ dθ.
The Mordell-Weil theorem establishes the fundamental finiteness of rational points on abelian varieties; the BSD conjecture links the analytic behavior of L-functions to the arithmetic rank.
Part 3

🧮 The Weil Conjectures and Étale Cohomology

Weil Conjectures
Zeta Functions, ℓ-adic Cohomology, and Deligne's Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis
The Weil conjectures (1949) describe the zeta function of a smooth projective variety X over 𝔽_q: Z(X, t) = exp(Σ_{k ≥ 1} |X(𝔽_{q^k})| t^k/k). They predict: (1) rationality Z(X, t) ∈ ℚ(t); (2) functional equation Z(1/q^n t) = ± q^{nE/2} t^E Z(t); (3) Riemann hypothesis the eigenvalues of Frobenius on H^i_{ét}(X, ℚ_ℓ) are algebraic numbers of absolute value q^{i/2}; (4) the degree of the pole at t = 1 equals the Betti number. Grothendieck proved rationality and the functional equation via the construction of étale cohomology — a Weil cohomology theory using ℓ-adic sheaves (ℓ ≠ p). The Lefschetz trace formula for Frobenius: |X(𝔽_{q^k})| = Σ (−1)^i Tr(Fr^k, H^i_{ét}(X, ℚ_ℓ)). Deligne (1974) proved the Riemann hypothesis using a delicate induction on dimension, the theory of weights, and the hard Lefschetz theorem in étale cohomology. The ℓ-adic Galois representations ρ_ℓ: Gal(̄ℚ/ℚ) → GL(H^i_{ét}(X_̄ℚ, ℚ_ℓ)) encode the action of absolute Galois group on cohomology and are unramified outside a finite set of primes.
The Weil conjectures provided a profound analogy between algebraic varieties over finite fields and Riemannian manifolds; Deligne's proof of the Riemann hypothesis is a crowning achievement of 20th-century mathematics.
Part 4

🔥 Galois Representations and the Fontaine-Mazur Conjecture

Langlands Program
Galois Representations, Modularity, and the Langlands Correspondence
A Galois representation ρ: Gal(̄ℚ/ℚ) → GL_n(ℚ_ℓ) is unramified outside a finite set of primes. The Fontaine-Mazur conjecture: an ℓ-adic Galois representation is geometric (arises from the étale cohomology of a smooth projective variety over ℚ) iff it is unramified outside finitely many primes and potentially semistable (de Rham) at p = ℓ. The modularity theorem (Wiles, Breuil-Conrad-Diamond-Taylor): every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular — its L-function is the Mellin transform of a weight-2 modular form. The Serre modularity conjecture (proved by Khare-Wintenberger): any continuous odd irreducible Galois representation ρ̄: Gal(̄ℚ/ℚ) → GL_2(𝔽_ℓ) comes from a modular form of weight k(ρ̄), level N(ρ̄), and character ε(ρ̄). The Langlands reciprocity for GL_n (global Langlands correspondence): over function fields, Drinfeld proved for GL₂ and Lafforgue for GL_n; over number fields, fundamental work by Scholze, Harris-Taylor, and Henniart. Fermat's Last Theorem: a special case of modularity — the Frey curve E: y² = x(x − aⁿ)(x + bⁿ) would be modular (contradicting Ribet's level-lowering theorem, which shows such a curve cannot exist). The broader Langlands program envisions a correspondence between n-dimensional Galois representations and automorphic representations of GL_n, linking number theory, harmonic analysis, and representation theory.
The Langlands program is a vast unifying framework connecting Galois theory, automorphic forms, and representation theory — with the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem as its most spectacular application to date.

The Arithmetic Surface Method: Heights, Intersection Theory, and Faltings' Proof

The transition from the Mordell-Weil theorem to Faltings' proof of the Mordell Conjecture requires a shift in perspective from curves to higher-dimensional objects. This section explores the mechanism of Arakelov theory applied to arithmetic surfaces.

Arithmetic Surfaces and Intersection Theory

An arithmetic surface is a regular, flat, projective scheme X over Spec(Z). It can be viewed as a family of curves over the integers. The core mechanism here is intersection theory on this surface. One defines a height function h(P) for a point P in X(K), where K is the function field of X (essentially the generic fiber). The key insight, pioneered by Arakelov and developed by Gillet-Soulé, is to treat the "infinite place" or archimedean valuation as a geometric point. This allows one to define an intersection number between two divisors D1 and D2 on the arithmetic surface that includes contributions from both finite primes (p-adic valuations) and the infinite places.

The mechanism relies on the positivity of the height pairing. If we have two sections S1 and S2 of X, their intersection product is defined via a metric at infinity. Faltings utilized this to prove that if the rank of the Jacobian variety J(K) is zero, then there are only finitely many rational points on the curve C. The proof involves showing that any infinite set of points would imply a contradiction in the arithmetic self-intersection numbers unless the height is bounded.

Landmark Case: Faltings' 1983 proof of the Mordell Conjecture (now Faltings' Theorem). This states that a smooth projective curve over Q of genus g > 1 has only finitely many rational points. This is a direct generalization of Mordell-Weil to higher genus, where the rank of the Jacobian does not capture all information about the points directly, but the height machinery bounds them.

Technique: Computing Arithmetic Intersection via Metrics
Question: Consider an arithmetic surface X over Spec(Z) which is a family of elliptic curves. Let D be a section corresponding to the zero section and E be a divisor defined by a polynomial f(x,y). How does one compute the arithmetic intersection number (D.E)_arithmetic?
Solution: The arithmetic intersection product (D.E) is computed as the sum of local contributions. For each finite prime p, we look at the reduction modulo p. If D and E intersect transversally at a point P in the special fiber X_p, the contribution is the valuation v_p(frac(D)) + v_p(frac(E)). At the infinite place (archimedean), we integrate over the real points of the fibers using the Arakelov metric. Specifically, if h_D and h_E are the Green functions associated with D and E, the infinite contribution is -integral over X(R) of log|f_D f_E| dvol. The total intersection number is finite because the sum of local heights converges due to the properties of the height pairing.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Zero Rank" Shortcut
When facing a question about rational points on curves or surfaces, immediately check if the problem implies rank zero. If the Jacobian has rank zero (or is finite), Faltings' theorem guarantees finiteness of points. Examiners often ask you to prove that a specific curve has no rational points by showing it has genus > 1 and then invoking Faltings, or by constructing an explicit descent argument assuming rank zero. Remember: Genus g=0 implies infinite points (P1), g=1 implies finite rank (Mordell-Weil), g>1 implies finite points (Faltings).

p-adic Hodge Theory and the de Rham Condition

The Fontaine-Mazur conjecture hinges on a specific property called "geometricity." A Galois representation is geometric if it arises from geometry. To test this, one must understand p-adic Hodge theory. This advanced section covers the classification of such representations using period rings.

Mechanisms: Period Rings and Filtrations

The central mechanism involves comparing different cohomology theories over p-adic fields. We have étale cohomology (which gives us Galois representations) and de Rham cohomology (which is algebraic). Fontaine introduced period rings like B_cris, B_dR, and B_st to bridge these worlds. A representation V is crystalline if it comes from the ring B_cris, semistable if it relates to B_st.

The critical condition for a representation to be "geometric" (in the sense of Fontaine-Mazur) over Q is that it must be de Rham at all primes p. This means there exists a non-zero map from the étale cohomology V to the de Rham cohomology after tensoring with B_dR. The mechanism relies on the fact that geometric objects have good reduction or semistable reduction properties, which translate into specific linear algebra conditions on the representation matrices.

Exception and Nuance: Not all unramified representations are crystalline. For instance, a representation can be unramified at p but fail to be de Rham if it does not come from geometry. The "exception" often tested is the distinction between being potentially semistable (required by Fontaine-Mazur) versus just being unramified outside a finite set.

Technique: Checking the de Rham Condition
Question: Given a 2-dimensional Galois representation rho: Gal(Q_bar/Q_p) -> GL2(Q_p), how do you verify if it is de Rham? What does this imply about its origin?
Solution: To check if rho is de Rham, one constructs the associated filtered phi-module. One must find a basis where the action of Frobenius and the Galois group can be represented in a way that satisfies the slope conditions relative to the Hodge filtration. Specifically, one checks if the dimension of the invariants under the action of B_dR is non-zero. If rho is de Rham at p, it suggests rho might arise from the cohomology of a variety with semistable reduction at p. Combined with being unramified outside p and finite, this satisfies the Fontaine-Mazur conjecture criteria.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Unramified" Trap
Examiners love to trap students who confuse "unramified" with "geometric." A representation can be unramified at p but not de Rham (e.g., certain non-geometric automorphic forms). Always verify the de Rham condition explicitly. If a question asks about the Fontaine-Mazur conjecture, recall that it asserts equivalence between geometric origin and the arithmetic properties (unramified outside finite set + de Rham at p). Do not assume unramified implies geometric without checking the p-adic Hodge type.

The Geometric Langlands Correspondence: Sheaves vs. Automorphic Forms

The global Langlands program connects Galois representations (number theory) to automorphic forms (harmonic analysis). However, the "next level" involves the geometric Langlands correspondence, which replaces number fields with algebraic curves and automorphic forms with sheaves.

Comparison: Number Field vs. Function Field vs. Geometric

In the classical setting (Number Fields), we match Galois representations to automorphic forms on GL_n. In the function field setting, Drinfeld and Laumon proved this for GL2 and GL_n. The geometric Langlands correspondence shifts the stage: instead of functions on a group G over a curve X, we consider categories of sheaves (D-modules or perverse sheaves) on the moduli stack of G-bundles over X.

The mechanism here is the "Satake isomorphism" generalized to the geometric setting. The correspondence states that the category of D-modules on the affine Grassmannian (related to local Langlands) corresponds to the category of automorphic sheaves. A landmark case is the work of Frenkel and Gaitsgory, who established the equivalence for GL_n over function fields, showing that the geometric side captures the same spectral data as the analytic side.

Applied Scenario: Consider a curve X over C. The moduli space Bun_G(X) classifies G-bundles. The geometric Langlands conjecture posits an equivalence of categories between D-modules on Bun_G and certain sheaves (or perverse sheaves) on the dual group's moduli stack. This is not just a bijection of points but an equivalence of entire derived categories.
