Arithmetic geometry studies the shapes formed by solutions of polynomial equations when the numbers involved come from whole numbers, fractions, or finite counting systems.

  1. Imagine a quilt made of many different fabrics, each piece representing solutions modulo a different prime number, all sewn together into one big pattern.
  2. This field connects deep questions about whole numbers—like why some equations have only finitely many solutions—to geometric ideas, leading to breakthroughs such as the proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem.
  3. Remember: arithmetic geometry ties together number theory and geometry, letting us see numbers as shapes.

Arithmetic Geometry — Schemes over ℤ, the Weil Conjectures & the Langlands Program

Arithmetic geometry is the study of solutions to polynomial equations over number fields and finite fields via the methods of algebraic geometry. A scheme over ℤ glues arithmetic and geometric data: X → Spec ℤ encodes the integral structure, with a fiber over each prime p. Arakelov theory compactifies Spec ℤ by adding an archimedean fiber, equipping the Riemann surface with a Hermitian metric to define intersection numbers at infinity. The Mordell-Weil theorem shows the rational points on an abelian variety form a finitely generated abelian group. The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture links the rank of an elliptic curve to the order of vanishing of its L-function at s = 1. The Weil conjectures (proved by Grothendieck and Deligne) describe the zeta function of a variety over a finite field via étale cohomology. The Langlands program provides a vast web of reciprocity laws linking Galois representations to automorphic forms — culminating in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem.

"Arithmetic geometry is the ultimate synthesis of number theory and algebraic geometry."
— Alexander Grothendieck
Part 1

📏 Schemes over ℤ and Arithmetic Surfaces

Arithmetic Schemes
Schemes over ℤ, Neron Models, and Arakelov Theory
A scheme X → Spec ℤ is an integral model of a variety over ℚ. The fiber over each prime p is X_p = X ×_{Spec ℤ} Spec 𝔽_p — the reduction mod p. An arithmetic surface is a regular 2-dimensional scheme X → Spec ℤ. For an elliptic curve E over a number field K, the Neron model is a smooth group scheme ℰ → Spec O_K (the ring of integers) whose generic fiber is E. The special fibers decompose according to the Kodaira-Neron classification of singular fibers: types I₀ (good reduction), I_m (multiplicative reduction), II, III, IV (additive reduction), etc. The minimal regular model removes all (-1)-curves from the desingularization. The intersection pairing on an arithmetic surface X: for two vertical divisors, the intersection number ⟨D, D'⟩ is computed via the Euler characteristic of the structure sheaf — the Noether formula: 12χ(𝒪_X) = (K_X)² + c₂(T_X). At the archimedean place, Arakelov theory equips the Riemann surface E_∞(ℂ) with a Hermitian metric and the canonical volume form, defining an intersection number at infinity via Green's functions and the Laplace operator.
Schemes over ℤ encode number-theoretic information in geometric fibers over each prime; Arakelov theory compactifies the picture by adding archimedean data with Hermitian metrics.
Part 2

🌍 The Mordell-Weil Theorem and the BSD Conjecture

Abelian Varieties
Mordell-Weil Theorem, Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer, and Heegner Points
The Mordell-Weil theorem: for an abelian variety A over a number field K, the group A(K) of K-rational points is finitely generated. The proof uses the weak Mordell-Weil theorem (A(K)/nA(K) is finite via Galois cohomology and Kummer theory) and the descent method (height functions to bound generators). The rank r = rank A(K) is a central invariant. The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture (BSD): for an elliptic curve E/K, the L-function L(E, s) satisfies L(E, s) ∼ c·(s−1)^r as s → 1, where r = rank E(K). The full BSD conjecture states that the residue of L(E, s) at s = 1 equals (Ω · Reg · Π c_v · |Sha|) / |E(K)_{tors}|², where Ω is the real period, Reg is the regulator, c_v are Tamagawa numbers, and Sha is the Tate-Shafarevich group. The work of Gross-Zagier and Kolyvagin: for modular elliptic curves (every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular by Wiles-Breuil-Conrad-Diamond-Taylor), if L(E, 1) ≠ 0 then rank = 0; if L(E, 1) = 0 but L'(E, 1) ≠ 0 then rank = 1 (using Heegner points). The Sato-Tate conjecture (proved by Clozel-Harris-Taylor-Barnet-Lamb): for an elliptic curve without complex multiplication, the Frobenius angles θ_p = arccos(a_p / 2√p) are distributed according to the Sato-Tate measure (2/π) sin²θ dθ.
The Mordell-Weil theorem establishes the fundamental finiteness of rational points on abelian varieties; the BSD conjecture links the analytic behavior of L-functions to the arithmetic rank.
Part 3

🧮 The Weil Conjectures and Étale Cohomology

Weil Conjectures
Zeta Functions, ℓ-adic Cohomology, and Deligne's Proof of the Riemann Hypothesis
The Weil conjectures (1949) describe the zeta function of a smooth projective variety X over 𝔽_q: Z(X, t) = exp(Σ_{k ≥ 1} |X(𝔽_{q^k})| t^k/k). They predict: (1) rationality Z(X, t) ∈ ℚ(t); (2) functional equation Z(1/q^n t) = ± q^{nE/2} t^E Z(t); (3) Riemann hypothesis the eigenvalues of Frobenius on H^i_{ét}(X, ℚ_ℓ) are algebraic numbers of absolute value q^{i/2}; (4) the degree of the pole at t = 1 equals the Betti number. Grothendieck proved rationality and the functional equation via the construction of étale cohomology — a Weil cohomology theory using ℓ-adic sheaves (ℓ ≠ p). The Lefschetz trace formula for Frobenius: |X(𝔽_{q^k})| = Σ (−1)^i Tr(Fr^k, H^i_{ét}(X, ℚ_ℓ)). Deligne (1974) proved the Riemann hypothesis using a delicate induction on dimension, the theory of weights, and the hard Lefschetz theorem in étale cohomology. The ℓ-adic Galois representations ρ_ℓ: Gal(̄ℚ/ℚ) → GL(H^i_{ét}(X_̄ℚ, ℚ_ℓ)) encode the action of absolute Galois group on cohomology and are unramified outside a finite set of primes.
The Weil conjectures provided a profound analogy between algebraic varieties over finite fields and Riemannian manifolds; Deligne's proof of the Riemann hypothesis is a crowning achievement of 20th-century mathematics.
Part 4

🔥 Galois Representations and the Fontaine-Mazur Conjecture

Langlands Program
Galois Representations, Modularity, and the Langlands Correspondence
A Galois representation ρ: Gal(̄ℚ/ℚ) → GL_n(ℚ_ℓ) is unramified outside a finite set of primes. The Fontaine-Mazur conjecture: an ℓ-adic Galois representation is geometric (arises from the étale cohomology of a smooth projective variety over ℚ) iff it is unramified outside finitely many primes and potentially semistable (de Rham) at p = ℓ. The modularity theorem (Wiles, Breuil-Conrad-Diamond-Taylor): every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular — its L-function is the Mellin transform of a weight-2 modular form. The Serre modularity conjecture (proved by Khare-Wintenberger): any continuous odd irreducible Galois representation ρ̄: Gal(̄ℚ/ℚ) → GL_2(𝔽_ℓ) comes from a modular form of weight k(ρ̄), level N(ρ̄), and character ε(ρ̄). The Langlands reciprocity for GL_n (global Langlands correspondence): over function fields, Drinfeld proved for GL₂ and Lafforgue for GL_n; over number fields, fundamental work by Scholze, Harris-Taylor, and Henniart. Fermat's Last Theorem: a special case of modularity — the Frey curve E: y² = x(x − aⁿ)(x + bⁿ) would be modular (contradicting Ribet's level-lowering theorem, which shows such a curve cannot exist). The broader Langlands program envisions a correspondence between n-dimensional Galois representations and automorphic representations of GL_n, linking number theory, harmonic analysis, and representation theory.
The Langlands program is a vast unifying framework connecting Galois theory, automorphic forms, and representation theory — with the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem as its most spectacular application to date.

Arakelov Theory and Intersection Theory on Arithmetic Surfaces

Arakelov theory extends classical intersection theory to arithmetic surfaces—regular schemes of dimension two, proper and flat over Spec ℤ. The key idea is to add “fibres at infinity” corresponding to the complex embeddings of the number field, compactifying the surface so a degree map for divisors can be defined. For a number field K with ring of integers O_K, an arithmetic divisor on the associated arithmetic surface X consists of a divisor on the generic fibre X_K together with a Green current on the complex manifold X(ℂ) satisfying the Poisson equation dd^c g + δ_D = ω, where ω is a fixed Kähler form. The intersection pairing of two arithmetic divisors D̂ = (D, g_D) and Ê = (E, g_E) is defined by

D̂ · Ê = (D · E)_finite + ∫X(ℂ) g_D dd^c g_E + ∫X(ℂ) g_E dd^c g_D.

The finite part (D · E)_finite is the usual intersection number on the special fibres over finite primes. The archimedean contribution uses Green currents and yields a real number. Arakelov proved a Hodge index theorem for this pairing: the intersection form on arithmetic divisor classes modulo numerical equivalence has signature (1, ρ−1), where ρ is the rank of the Néron–Severi group. This result underpins Faltings’s proof of the Mordell conjecture via height theory. The Arakelov height of a point P ∈ X(K) is the self‑intersection of the arithmetic divisor (P, g_P) with g_P a normalized Green function. Faltings showed that the set of points of bounded height is finite, implying that a curve of genus ≥ 2 over a number field has only finitely many rational points. The theory also yields an arithmetic Riemann–Roch theorem (Gillet–Soulé) computing the determinant of cohomology of a hermitian line bundle via intersection numbers and analytic torsion, a cornerstone for modern arithmetic intersection theory and height computations on abelian varieties.

The Tate Conjecture and Motivic Cohomology

The Tate conjecture, formulated by John Tate in the 1960s, predicts a precise relationship between algebraic cycles on a smooth projective variety X over a finitely generated field k and the Galois action on its ℓ‑adic cohomology. For a prime ℓ different from the characteristic of k, let V = H2iét(Xbar k, ℚ_ℓ(i)) be the i‑th Tate twist of the 2i‑th étale cohomology group. The conjecture asserts that the cycle class map

cl : CH^i(X) ⊗ ℚ_ℓ → VGal(bar k/k)

is surjective: every Galois‑invariant class in V comes from an algebraic cycle of codimension i. Equivalently, the rank of algebraic cycles modulo homological equivalence equals the dimension of the Galois‑invariant subspace. The conjecture is proved for abelian varieties over finite fields (Tate), for K3 surfaces over finite fields (Nygaard–Ogus, Charles), and for certain Shimura varieties (Kisin, Madapusi Pera). Over number fields it remains open in general, but it is implied by Grothendieck’s standard conjectures and by the existence of a motivic cohomology theory. Motivic cohomology, developed by Voevodsky, Levine, and others, provides a universal cohomology theory for smooth schemes over a field, with groups Hp,q_M(X, ℤ) mapping to ℓ‑adic, de Rham, and Betti cohomology via realization functors. The Beilinson–Lichtenbaum conjecture, proved by Voevodsky and Rost, identifies motivic cohomology with étale cohomology in a range of degrees, bridging algebraic cycles and Galois representations. The Tate conjecture for divisors (i = 1) is equivalent to the finiteness of the Tate–Shafarevich group of the Jacobian of a curve over a global field, linking it to the Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture. Over function fields, the Tate conjecture for all i implies semisimplicity of the Frobenius action on ℓ‑adic cohomology; this semisimplicity is known for abelian varieties (Weil conjectures) but remains open in general (Standard Conjecture C).

Worked Example: Verifying the Tate Conjecture for an Elliptic Curve over a Finite Field
Let E/𝔽_q be an elliptic curve with Frobenius endomorphism π. Show that the Tate conjecture for divisors (i = 1) holds for E.
The group H2ét(Ebar 𝔽_q, ℚ_ℓ) is one‑dimensional with Frobenius acting as multiplication by q. The Tate twist (1) multiplies this eigenvalue by q-1, so Fr_q acts trivially on V = H2ét(Ebar 𝔽_q, ℚ_ℓ(1)). Hence the invariant subspace VGal is all of V. The cycle class map sends the class of a rational point (which exists because E(𝔽_q) ≠ ∅) to a generator of V. Therefore the map is surjective, establishing the Tate conjecture for divisors on E.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the statement of the Tate conjecture, write the cycle class map and the surjectivity condition explicitly. Remember that the conjecture is proved for abelian varieties over finite fields and for K3 surfaces over finite fields; cite these as the main proven cases. For number fields, note the equivalence with the standard conjectures and the role of motivic cohomology.

p‑adic Hodge Theory: Crystalline and Semistable Representations

p‑adic Hodge theory relates p‑adic Galois representations of G_K = Gal(bar K/K) for a p‑adic field K (a finite extension of ℚ_p) to linear algebra data such as filtered φ‑modules. A representation V of G_K on a finite‑dimensional ℚ_p‑vector space is de Rham if dim_K D_dR(V) = dimℚ_p V, where D_dR(V) = (V ⊗ℚ_p B_dR)G_K and B_dR is Fontaine’s period ring. D_dR(V) carries a decreasing filtration Fil^i; the Hodge–Tate weights are the integers i with gr^i D_dR(V) ≠ 0. A representation is crystalline if dim_K D_cris(V) = dim V, where D_cris(V) = (V ⊗ B_cris)G_K, and the Frobenius φ on D_cris(V) is bijective. Crystalline representations are exactly those arising from the étale cohomology of smooth proper varieties over K with good reduction (Fontaine–Messing, Faltings). A representation is semistable if it becomes trivial over B_st, the larger ring containing a monodromy operator N satisfying N φ = p φ N; these correspond to varieties with semistable reduction. The fundamental theorem (Fontaine, Colmez, Berger) establishes an equivalence of categories between de Rham representations and weakly admissible filtered (φ, N)‑modules. The Fontaine–Mazur conjecture predicts that an irreducible ℓ‑adic representation of Gal(bar ℚ/ℚ), unramified outside finitely many primes and de Rham at ℓ, comes from geometry—i.e., from the étale cohomology of a smooth projective variety over ℚ. This conjecture is a central open problem linking Galois representations to automorphic forms via the Langlands program.

The Langlands Correspondence for GL(2) over Number Fields

The Langlands correspondence for GL(2) over a number field F asserts a bijection between isomorphism classes of irreducible cuspidal automorphic representations π of GL2(𝔸_F) and certain 2‑dimensional ℓ‑adic Galois representations ρ_π : Gal(bar F/F) → GL2(ℚ_ℓ). The correspondence is characterized by the equality of local L‑factors: for almost all finite places v, the Satake parameters of π_v match the eigenvalues of Frobenius at v on ρ_π. The global correspondence was proved for F = ℚ by the modularity theorem (Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor) and extended to totally real fields by Skinner–Wiles, Fujiwara, and others using Hilbert modular forms. For general number fields, it is known for essentially self‑dual automorphic representations (Arthur–Clozel) and for those admitting a suitable base change to a solvable extension (Langlands–Tunnell, Rogawski). The local Langlands correspondence for GL2(F_v) (proved by Harris–Taylor, Henniart, and Bushnell–Henniart) matches irreducible smooth representations of GL2(F_v) with 2‑dimensional representations of the Weil–Deligne group W'_F_v. Compatibility of local and global correspondences is expressed by the equality of ε‑factors and preservation of L‑functions. The proof of modularity for elliptic curves over ℚ uses the Taylor–Wiles method: one deforms a residual representation ρ̄ : G_ℚ → GL2(𝔽_p) to a characteristic‑zero representation known to be modular, using a numerical criterion (“R = T”) comparing a universal deformation ring R with a Hecke algebra T. The method has been generalized to prove modularity for many symmetric power lifts and certain higher‑dimensional representations.

Geometric Langlands Program and D‑modules on Curves

The geometric Langlands program, formulated by Drinfeld, replaces the number field F by the function field of a smooth projective curve X over a finite field 𝔽_q (or over ℂ). Instead of Galois representations, one studies ℓ‑adic local systems (or D‑modules) on X; instead of automorphic representations, one studies Hecke eigensheaves on the moduli stack Bun_G of G‑bundles on X. For a reductive group G over 𝔽_q, the correspondence predicts an equivalence of derived categories

D‑mod(Bun_G) ≅ QCoh(LocSys^L G)

where ^L G is the Langlands dual group. For G = GL_n, Drinfeld proved the correspondence for n = 2 over function fields, and Lafforgue proved it for all n, establishing the global Langlands correspondence for GL_n over function fields. Over ℂ, the correspondence is a conjecture of Beilinson–Drinfeld relating D‑modules on Bun_G to coherent sheaves on the stack of ^L G‑local systems. The proof uses the geometric Satake equivalence, identifying representations of ^L G with perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian Gr_G. Hecke operators act on D‑mod(Bun_G) via convolution with these perverse sheaves; a Hecke eigensheaf is a D‑module equipped with isomorphisms to its Hecke transforms satisfying a compatibility condition. The program has deep connections with quantum field theory (Kapustin–Witten), mirror symmetry, and the theory of opers. Recent work by Gaitsgory, Lurie, and others has established a version of the correspondence for GL_n over ℂ using ind‑coherent sheaves and the categorical trace.

  • Assuming every de Rham representation is crystalline; semistable representations with non‑zero monodromy are de Rham but not crystalline.
  • Mixing up the local Langlands correspondence for GL2 (matching representations of GL2(F_v) with Weil–Deligne representations) with the global correspondence (matching automorphic representations with Galois representations).
  • Thinking the geometric Langlands correspondence is a theorem for all reductive groups over ℂ; it is proved for GL_n over function fields (Lafforgue) and for GL_n over ℂ (Gaitsgory–Lurie), but remains open for other groups.
  • Neglecting the role of the Frobenius–monodromy compatibility N φ = p φ N in the definition of semistable representations.
  • Overlooking that the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture is a necessary condition for a Galois representation to be geometric, not a sufficient one.
  • Misstating the Arakelov intersection formula by omitting the archimedean Green current terms.

The Langlands Program for GL(n) and Functoriality

The Langlands program for general linear groups GL(n) predicts a correspondence between irreducible cuspidal automorphic representations of GL(n) over a number field F and n‑dimensional ℓ‑adic Galois representations of the absolute Galois group of F. For n = 2 this is the modularity theorem and its extensions; for n > 2 the local correspondence is known thanks to the work of Harris–Taylor and Henniart, with a later geometric proof by Scholze. The global correspondence for GL(n) over totally real or CM fields has been established for regular algebraic cuspidal automorphic representations that are essentially self‑dual (Clozel, Harris–Taylor, Shin). The key mechanism is the comparison of L‑functions: the standard L‑function of an automorphic representation π is expected to equal the Artin L‑function of the associated Galois representation ρ_π.

Functoriality is the principle that any homomorphism of L‑groups ^L H → ^L G should transfer automorphic representations from H to G. For GL(n) this includes symmetric power lifts, exterior power lifts, and the Asai (tensor induction) lift. The Langlands–Shahidi method provides analytic continuation and functional equations for the L‑functions attached to these lifts, which is essential for proving functoriality in many cases. The endoscopic classification of Arthur gives a complete description of the discrete automorphic spectrum of classical groups in terms of automorphic representations of GL(n), thereby realizing functoriality for those groups.

A major open problem is the strong Artin conjecture for non‑solvable Galois representations, which would follow from the full Langlands correspondence for GL(n). Potential automorphy theorems of Taylor, Clozel–Harris–Taylor, and others show that many Galois representations become automorphic after a suitable solvable base change, providing a powerful tool for constructing the desired correspondences.

The Sato–Tate Conjecture and Automorphic Forms

The Sato–Tate conjecture describes the distribution of Frobenius eigenvalues for elliptic curves over ℚ without complex multiplication. For an elliptic curve E/ℚ, the normalized trace a_p(E) = p + 1 − #E(𝔽_p) satisfies |a_p| ≤ 2√p by Hasse’s theorem. The conjecture asserts that as p varies, the angles θ_p defined by a_p = 2√p cos θ_p become equidistributed in [0, π] with respect to the measure (2/π) sin²θ dθ. This was proved by Clozel, Harris, Shepherd‑Barron, and Taylor using the modularity of E and the automorphy of symmetric power L‑functions Sym^m ρ_E for all m ≥ 1.

The proof proceeds by showing that the symmetric power L‑functions L(s, Sym^m ρ_E) are automorphic and satisfy the expected analytic properties (analytic continuation, functional equation, non‑vanishing on Re(s) = 1). The Chebotarev density theorem for the Galois representations attached to these symmetric powers then yields the equidistribution. The method is expected to extend to abelian varieties with Mumford–Tate group Sp(2g) and to Hilbert modular forms over totally real fields, where the Sato–Tate group is conjecturally a compact form of the relevant motivic Galois group.

Generalizations include the joint distribution of Frobenius traces for families of elliptic curves (the “Sato–Tate groups” of Katz–Sarnak) and the distribution of Hecke eigenvalues for higher‑rank automorphic forms. The Langlands program predicts that the Sato–Tate group of a pure motive is the identity component of its motivic Galois group, linking the statistical behavior of Frobenius to the geometry of the underlying motive.

Worked Example: Symmetric Power L‑function
Let E/ℚ be a non‑CM elliptic curve. Explain why the automorphy of Sym3 ρ_E implies the analytic continuation of L(s, Sym3 E) to the whole complex plane.
By the modularity theorem, ρ_E arises from a weight‑2 cuspidal newform f of level N. The Langlands functoriality for the symmetric cube lift (proved by Kim–Shahidi) provides an automorphic representation π = Sym3 f on GL(4). The standard L‑function of π is exactly L(s, Sym3 ρ_E). Since π is cuspidal automorphic on GL(4), its L‑function is known to have analytic continuation to ℂ, a functional equation, and no poles except possibly at s = 1. Therefore L(s, Sym3 E) inherits these properties.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the role of symmetric power functoriality in Sato–Tate, state the chain: modularity → automorphy of Sym^m → analytic properties of L‑functions → Chebotarev equidistribution. Do not merely quote the final equidistribution measure; show the logical steps.

The Bloch–Kato Conjecture and Selmer Groups

The Bloch–Kato conjecture (also known as the Tamagawa number conjecture) gives a precise formula for the leading term of the L‑function of a motive M at integer points s = m in terms of arithmetic invariants: the order of the Selmer group H1_f(ℚ, M), the regulator of the motivic cohomology, the Tamagawa numbers at bad primes, and the period determinants. For an elliptic curve E/ℚ, the conjecture specializes to the Birch and Swinnerton‑Dyer formula, where the Selmer group is the classical p‑Selmer group and the regulator is the elliptic regulator.

The conjecture is formulated in the language of p‑adic Hodge theory. For each prime p, one defines the finite part H1_f(ℚ_p, V) of the local Galois cohomology of a p‑adic representation V using Fontaine’s rings B_cris and B_dR. The global Selmer group H1_f(ℚ, V) is the kernel of the localization map to the product of H1(ℚ_p, V)/H1_f(ℚ_p, V). The Bloch–Kato exponential and dual exponential maps relate these cohomology groups to the de Rham filtration on D_dR(V).

Major progress includes Kato’s proof of one divisibility (the “Kato divisibility”) for modular forms of weight ≥ 2 using Euler systems, and work of Nekovář and others on symmetric powers. The Iwasawa‑theoretic formulation (the main conjecture of Iwasawa theory) provides a p‑adic analogue, linking the characteristic ideal of the Selmer group over the cyclotomic ℤ_p‑extension to the p‑adic L‑function. The work of Skinner–Urban on the Iwasawa main conjecture for GL(2) proves the opposite divisibility in many cases, which combined with Kato’s Euler system yields the main conjecture for a large class of motives.

The Arithmetic of Shimura Varieties

Shimura varieties are higher‑dimensional analogues of modular curves, attached to a reductive group G over ℚ and a conjugacy class of homomorphisms h: ℂ → G_ℝ satisfying certain axioms. They have canonical models over number fields (the reflex field) and carry natural families of ℓ‑adic sheaves (automorphic vector bundles) whose cohomology realizes the Langlands correspondence for G. The most studied cases are Siegel modular varieties (G = GSp(2g)), unitary Shimura varieties, and Hilbert modular varieties.

The arithmetic of Shimura varieties is governed by the action of the Hecke algebra on their étale cohomology. The Langlands–Kottwitz method computes the number of points over finite fields in terms of orbital integrals, leading to the trace formula for the cohomology. This provides a geometric realization of the local Langlands correspondence for G at unramified primes. The work of Harris–Taylor on the local Langlands for GL(n) uses the cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower, a p‑adic analogue of a Shimura variety.

Recent breakthroughs include the proof of the Hodge–Tate period map for Shimura varieties by Scholze, which gives a p‑adic uniformization and allows the construction of Galois representations attached to automorphic forms on G. The work of Caraiani–Scholze on the cohomology of Shimura varieties with torsion coefficients has led to new cases of the Langlands correspondence for non‑regular algebraic automorphic representations. The study of the boundary strata in toroidal compactifications (the “boundary cohomology”) is essential for understanding the contribution of non‑cuspidal automorphic forms and for formulating the endoscopic classification on the geometric side.

p‑adic Families and Eigenvarieties

Eigenvarieties are rigid analytic spaces that parametrize systems of Hecke eigenvalues occurring in the cohomology of arithmetic groups, varying p‑adically in weight. They were introduced by Coleman–Mazur for GL(2)/ℚ and generalized by Buzzard, Chenevier, and others to higher rank groups. The points of an eigenvariety correspond to finite‑slope overconvergent eigenforms; classical points (those coming from classical modular forms) are dense in the eigenvariety.

The construction uses the theory of p‑adic Banach spaces and the action of the compact operator U_p on spaces of overconvergent forms. The Fredholm determinant of U_p defines a rigid analytic variety (the spectral variety), and the eigenvariety is a finite cover of the weight space obtained by attaching the generalized eigenspaces. The geometry of the eigenvariety reflects deep arithmetic: irreducible components are expected to correspond to p‑adic families of Galois representations, and the tangent space at a classical point is related to the Selmer group of the associated Galois representation.

Applications include the proof of the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture for many 2‑dimensional representations (by Kisin, Emerton, and others) using the fact that a geometric representation must appear in a p‑adic family. The eigenvariety also provides a setting for the p‑adic Langlands program: the completed cohomology of the tower of modular curves carries an action of GL(2, ℚ_p) × Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ), and the eigenvariety parametrizes the Galois side of this correspondence. Recent work of Hansen–Newton on the “Eigenvariety is proper” conjecture and of Boxer–Calegari–Gee–Pilloni on higher‑rank eigenvarieties has extended these techniques to unitary groups and symplectic groups.

  • Assuming the Sato–Tate measure is always the semicircular (2/π) sin²θ dθ; for CM curves the measure is uniform on [0, π] and for higher genus the group is larger.
  • Neglecting the distinction between the Bloch–Kato Selmer group H1_f and the strict Selmer group H1_str; the latter imposes trivial local conditions at p and appears in the Iwasawa main conjecture.
  • Thinking every point on a Shimura variety corresponds to an abelian variety with extra structure; only the complex points have this moduli interpretation, while p‑adic points require the theory of p‑divisible groups.
  • Believing eigenvarieties are always equidimensional of dimension equal to the rank of the weight space; components can have different dimensions and may intersect non‑transversely.
  • Overlooking that the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture requires the representation to be de Rham at all primes above p, not just crystalline; semistable non‑crystalline representations are allowed.

The Modularity Theorem and the Proof of Fermat's Last Theorem

The Modularity Theorem, formerly the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture, asserts that every elliptic curve E over ℚ is modular: there exists a weight‑2 newform f of level N (the conductor of E) such that the L‑function L(E, s) coincides with the L‑function L(f, s) of the associated automorphic representation of GL(2, 𝔸ℚ). The theorem was proved in stages: Wiles (1995) established modularity for semistable elliptic curves, using the Taylor–Wiles method to patch deformation rings of Galois representations against Hecke algebras. The key input was the identification of the universal deformation ring R with the Hecke algebra T (R = T theorem) for a suitable residual representation ρ̄: Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) → GL2(𝔽p). The Taylor–Wiles patching argument introduces auxiliary primes q ≡ 1 (mod p) to enlarge the deformation problem, making the Selmer group and the dual Selmer group have the same dimension, which forces R to be a complete intersection. Breuil, Conrad, Diamond, and Taylor (2001) extended the result to all elliptic curves over ℚ by removing the semistability hypothesis, using p‑adic Hodge theory (Fontaine–Laffaille, Breuil–Kisin modules) to handle the local deformation condition at p when E has additive reduction. The modularity theorem implies Fermat's Last Theorem via the Frey–Hellegouarch curve Ea,b,c: y² = x(x − ap)(x + bp) attached to a putative solution of ap + bp = cp. Ribet's theorem (1990) on level lowering shows that if Ea,b,c is modular, its associated Galois representation arises from a newform of level 2, but no such newform exists — contradiction. The proof also relied on the Langlands–Tunnell theorem (base change for GL(2) from ℚ to a solvable extension) to ensure the residual representation is modular for p = 3.

Iwasawa Theory and the Main Conjecture for Elliptic Curves

Iwasawa theory studies the growth of Selmer groups in ℤp‑extensions. For an elliptic curve E/ℚ with good ordinary reduction at p, let ℚ∞ be the cyclotomic ℤp‑extension and Γ = Gal(ℚ∞/ℚ) ≅ ℤp. The Pontryagin dual X = Selp∞(E/ℚ∞)∨ is a finitely generated torsion module over the Iwasawa algebra Λ = ℤp[[Γ]] ≅ ℤp[[T]]. The characteristic ideal charΛ(X) is generated by the p‑adic L‑function Lp(E, T) constructed by Mazur–Swinnerton‑Dyer (1974) via modular symbols. The Iwasawa Main Conjecture asserts charΛ(X) = (Lp(E, T)). This was proved by Kato (2004) for all elliptic curves over ℚ using Euler systems of Beilinson–Kato elements in the motivic cohomology of modular curves. The Euler system argument bounds the Selmer group from above by the p‑adic L‑function; the opposite inequality follows from the non‑vanishing of the p‑adic L‑function at the trivial character (Rohrlich, Greenberg). For supersingular p, the Selmer group is not Λ‑torsion; instead one uses the signed Selmer groups of Kobayashi (2003) and the two‑variable p‑adic L‑functions of Pollack. The Main Conjecture for GL(2) over imaginary quadratic fields (the anticyclotomic Main Conjecture) was proved by Bertolini–Darmon–Prasanna and by Castella–Kim–Longhi using Heegner points and the Euler system of Heegner cycles. The Iwasawa Main Conjecture implies the p‑part of the Birch and Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture when the analytic rank is ≤ 1, via the control theorem relating Selp∞(E/ℚ∞)Γ to Selp∞(E/ℚ).

Worked Example: Level Lowering and the Frey Curve
Let p ≥ 5 be prime and suppose a, b, c ∈ ℤ are non‑zero, pairwise coprime, with ap + bp = cp. Consider the Frey curve E: y² = x(x − ap)(x + bp). Show that the conductor NE of E is divisible only by primes dividing 2abc, and that the mod p representation ρ̄E,p is unramified outside p and primes dividing abc. Explain why Ribet's level lowering theorem forces ρ̄E,p to arise from a newform of level 2.
The discriminant of E is Δ = 2⁴(abc)2p and the j‑invariant is j = 2⁸(a2p + apbp + b2p)³ / (abc)2p. For a prime ℓ ≠ 2 dividing abc, exactly one of a, b, c is divisible by ℓ (pairwise coprime). The reduction is multiplicative at ℓ, so the conductor exponent is 1. At ℓ = 2 the reduction is potentially multiplicative or additive; the exponent is ≤ 8. Thus NE | 2⁸ ∏ℓ|abc ℓ. The mod p representation ρ̄E,p is finite flat at p (since E has good reduction at p after a quadratic twist) and unramified outside p and primes dividing abc. By Ribet's level lowering (Invent. Math. 1990), if ρ̄E,p is modular (which follows from the Modularity Theorem), it arises from a newform of level Np where Np is the prime‑to‑p part of the conductor. Since p ∤ abc (otherwise p divides two of a,b,c), Np divides 2⁸. The only newforms of level a power of 2 have level 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256. Level 1 has no weight‑2 newforms. Levels 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256 have no newforms with rational coefficients (by explicit computation of Hecke algebras). Level 2 has no weight‑2 newforms either (the space S₂(Γ₀(2)) = 0). Hence no such newform exists — contradiction. Therefore no non‑trivial solution exists.
Exam Strategy: Tracking the Logical Chain
In questions on the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, examiners test whether you can order the logical dependencies: (1) Frey curve construction → (2) Ribet's level lowering (requires modularity of the Frey curve) → (3) Wiles' modularity for semistable curves → (4) Contradiction at level 2. A common trap is to state that Wiles proved the full Taniyama–Shimura conjecture; he only proved the semistable case. The full theorem came later (Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor, 2001). Always distinguish the 1995 result from the 2001 completion. For Iwasawa theory, remember the distinction between ordinary and supersingular primes: the Main Conjecture for ordinary primes uses the classical Selmer group and one‑variable p‑adic L‑function; for supersingular primes you must cite Kobayashi's ±‑Selmer groups and Pollack's two‑variable p‑adic L‑functions.

The Fundamental Lemma and Ngô's Proof

The Fundamental Lemma is a combinatorial identity in the theory of endoscopy, conjectured by Langlands and Shelstad (1987) as a cornerstone of the stabilization of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula. It asserts the equality of orbital integrals on a reductive group G over a local field F and stable orbital integrals on an endoscopic group H, for a specific test function (the unit element of the Hecke algebra of a hyperspecial maximal compact subgroup). More precisely, for a regular semisimple element γ ∈ H(F) matching δ ∈ G(F), one requires Oδ(1K) = Δ(γ, δ) SOγ(1KH), where Δ is the transfer factor. The lemma was proved for many cases (Waldspurger for Lie algebras, Hales for fundamental domains, Laumon for function fields with G = GL(n)), but the general case remained open until Ngô Bảo Châu's proof (2008) using the geometry of the Hitchin fibration. Ngô considered the Hitchin system h: M → A for the group G over a smooth projective curve X over 𝔽q, where M is the moduli stack of G‑Higgs bundles. The fibers are (compactified) Prym varieties associated to spectral curves. The key insight is that the orbital integrals appear as the number of points of certain subvarieties (the "centralizer group schemes") in the fibers of h. The decomposition theorem for the proper map h (proved by Ngô using the support theorem and the purity of perverse sheaves) implies that the cohomology of the fibers carries an action of the endoscopic groups. The equality of orbital integrals follows from the fact that the perverse sheaves on A arising from the Hitchin fibration for G and for H are isomorphic after a suitable shift, which is a consequence of the geometric Satake equivalence and the fact that the fundamental group of the dual group LG maps to that of LH. Ngô's proof works for all reductive groups over function fields; the transfer to characteristic zero (local fields of characteristic 0) was completed by Waldspurger (2009) using the "transfer principle" and the fact that the Fundamental Lemma is a first‑order statement in the language of rings, hence preserved by ultraproducts. The proof of the Fundamental Lemma completed the stabilization of the trace formula, enabling Arthur's classification of the discrete spectrum of classical groups (2013) and the proof of the local Langlands correspondence for classical groups by Mok (2015) and Kaletha–Minguez–Shin–White (2020).

Anabelian Geometry and Grothendieck's Section Conjecture

Anabelian geometry, initiated by Grothendieck in his 1983 "Esquisse d'un Programme", studies the extent to which the étale fundamental group π₁ét(X) of a hyperbolic curve X over a field k determines X itself. A curve is hyperbolic if its universal cover is the upper half‑plane (equivalently, 2g − 2 + n > 0 for genus g and n punctures). The central conjecture is the Section Conjecture: for a smooth hyperbolic curve X over a finitely generated field k of characteristic 0, the set of rational points X(k) is in bijection with the set of conjugacy classes of sections s: Gal(k̄/k) → π₁ét(Xk̄) of the exact sequence 1 → π₁ét(Xk̄) → π₁ét(X) → Gal(k̄/k) → 1. A section corresponds to a rational point via the functoriality of π₁ét; the conjecture asserts the converse. The Section Conjecture implies Mordell's conjecture (Faltings' theorem) because the set of sections is finite when k is a number field. Neukirch–Uchida (1969) proved that number fields are determined by their absolute Galois groups; Mochizuki extended this to hyperbolic curves over number fields (1996): if X, Y are hyperbolic curves over number fields and π₁ét(X) ≅ π₁ét(Y) as profinite groups, then X ≅ Y. The "birational anabelian" program (Mochizuki, 1999) reconstructs the function field k(X) from the Galois category of finite étale covers. The Section Conjecture is known for k = ℚp (Tamagawa, 1997) and for certain Shimura curves (Stix, 2002). The "monodromy" approach (Hoshi–Mochizuki) uses the outer action of Gal(k̄/k) on π₁ét(Xk̄) to reconstruct the point. A major obstacle is the existence of "fake sections" coming from the geometric fundamental group; the conjecture predicts these do not exist for number fields. The Grothendieck–Teichmüller group GT, which acts on the profinite completion of the mapping class group, is closely related to the automorphisms of the tower of moduli spaces Mg,n and appears in the study of the absolute Galois group of ℚ via its action on π₁ét(ℙ¹ − {0,1,∞}).

The p‑adic Local Langlands Correspondence for GL(2)

The p‑adic Local Langlands correspondence for GL(2, ℚp) is a bijection between irreducible admissible unitary representations of GL(2, ℚp) on p‑adic Banach spaces and 2‑dimensional p‑adic Galois representations of Gal(ℚ̄p/ℚp), compatible with the classical local Langlands correspondence modulo p. It was formulated by Breuil (2003) and proved by Colmez (2010) using the theory of (φ, Γ)‑modules and the p‑adic Langlands functor V from locally analytic representations to (φ, Γ)‑modules. The correspondence matches: (1) principal series representations Π(χ₁, χ₂) with reducible Galois representations χ₁ ⊕ χ₂; (2) special representations (Steinberg twists) with non‑split extensions of characters; (3) supersingular representations (the "missing" representations not occurring in the classical correspondence) with irreducible crystalline Galois representations with distinct Hodge–Tate weights. The supersingular representations are constructed as the completion of the space of locally algebraic vectors in the unitary Banach space representation Π(ρ) attached to a Galois representation ρ by Colmez's Montréal functor. The correspondence is realized geometrically via the completed cohomology of the tower of modular curves: Ĥ¹ = lim← H¹ét(X(pn), ℤp) carries an action of GL(2, ℚp) × Gal(ℚ̄p/ℚp), and the eigenvariety parametrizes the Galois side. The p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL(2, ℚp) is a key input in the proof of the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture for 2‑dimensional representations (Kisin, 2009) and in the p‑adic Langlands program for higher rank groups (Emerton, Helm, etc.). For GL(n), the correspondence is largely conjectural; the work of Scholze on the cohomology of Shimura varieties and the Fargues–Fontaine curve provides a geometric framework for the p‑adic Langlands correspondence in general, via the moduli stack of p‑divisible groups and the vector bundles on the Fargues–Fontaine curve.

  • Assuming the Iwasawa Main Conjecture is equivalent to the full BSD conjecture; it only implies the p‑part of BSD when the analytic rank is ≤ 1, and requires the control theorem to relate Selmer groups over ℚ∞ and ℚ.
  • Stating that Ngô proved the Fundamental Lemma for all local fields of characteristic 0; he proved it for function fields, and Waldspurger transferred it to characteristic 0 via ultraproducts.
  • Believing the Section Conjecture is known for all number fields; it is only known for p‑adic fields and certain Shimura curves, and remains open for ℚ.
  • Thinking the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL(2) classifies all admissible Banach space representations; it only classifies the unitary ones, and the supersingular representations are not of the form Π(χ₁, χ₂) or special.
  • Confusing the Hitchin fibration (used by Ngô) with the Hitchin system for Higgs bundles on a Riemann surface; the former is over a finite field and uses the decomposition theorem for ℓ‑adic sheaves, the latter is over ℂ and uses Hodge theory.
  • Overlooking that the Modularity Theorem for all elliptic curves over ℚ required the development of p‑adic Hodge theory (Breuil–Kisin modules) to handle the local deformation condition at primes of additive reduction.

The Arthur–Selberg Trace Formula and Endoscopic Classification

The Arthur–Selberg trace formula is the central analytic tool for the global Langlands program. It expresses the trace of a test function on the automorphic spectrum of a reductive group G over a number field F in two ways: a spectral side summing over automorphic representations, and a geometric side summing over conjugacy classes in G(F). The formula takes the form ∑π aG(π) tr π(f) = ∑{γ} aG(γ) Oγ(f), where π runs over automorphic representations, γ over conjugacy classes, aG are Arthur's coefficients, and Oγ are orbital integrals. Arthur's invariant trace formula (1980s–1990s) stabilizes the geometric side by transferring orbital integrals to endoscopic groups H, yielding the stable trace formula. Endoscopy, introduced by Langlands and Shelstad, relates representations of G to those of its endoscopic groups H via transfer factors Δ(γH, γ). The fundamental lemma, proved by Ngô (2010) for Lie algebras and by Waldspurger (2006) for groups via transfer, asserts the matching of orbital integrals on G and H. Arthur's classification of the discrete automorphic spectrum of classical groups (2013) uses the stable trace formula to establish a bijection between discrete automorphic representations of a classical group G and stable representations of its endoscopic groups, parameterized by global Arthur parameters ψ: LF × SL(2, ℂ) → LG. The multiplicity formula involves the component group Sψ = π0(Cent(LG, ψ)). For GL(n), the classification reduces to the work of Jacquet–Shalika and Moeglin–Waldspurger. Henniart (2000) established the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) using the trace formula and base change; Harris–Taylor (2001) used the geometry of the Lubin–Tate tower and Shimura varieties. The fine geometric expansion involves weighted orbital integrals and the invariant distributions IM(γ, f). The spectral expansion uses the truncation operator ΛT of Arthur to handle the continuous spectrum. The stabilization process requires the transfer of test functions from G to H, governed by the fundamental lemma and the transfer conjecture. The endoscopic classification has been extended to unitary groups by Mok (2015) and to symplectic and orthogonal groups by Arthur. The trace formula remains a primary candidate for proving functoriality in general, via the comparison of trace formulas for different groups.

The Local Langlands Correspondence for GL(n) and Classical Groups

The local Langlands correspondence (LLC) for a p-adic field F and a reductive group G(F) is a conjectural bijection between irreducible admissible representations of G(F) and Langlands parameters φ: WF × SL(2, ℂ) → LG, where WF is the Weil group of F and LG is the L-group. For GL(n), the correspondence was established by Harris–Taylor (2001) using the geometry of the Lubin–Tate tower and Shimura varieties, and independently by Henniart (2000) using the trace formula and base change. The LLC for GL(n) satisfies the required compatibilities: preservation of L- and ε-factors, compatibility with parabolic induction (the Bernstein–Zelevinsky classification), and compatibility with the local Langlands correspondence for the Weil–Deligne group. For classical groups (symplectic, orthogonal, unitary), Arthur (2013) classified the discrete automorphic spectrum, yielding a local correspondence for A-packets; Mok (2015) extended this to unitary groups. The correspondence for general representations of classical groups is conjecturally given by the A-packets of Arthur, parameterized by Arthur parameters ψ: WF × SL(2, ℂ) × SL(2, ℂ) → LG. The local correspondence for classical groups is characterized by the preservation of twisted endoscopy and the matching of characters via transfer factors. For inner forms of GL(n) (division algebras), the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence matches representations of GL(n, F) and D× (where D is a central division algebra of invariant 1/n) via preservation of character values on regular elliptic elements, and is compatible with the LLC. The LLC for GL(n) is also realized via the p-adic Langlands correspondence (Colmez, 2010) for n=2, and via the Fargues–Fontaine curve for general n (Fargues–Scholze, 2021). The local Langlands correspondence for general reductive groups is the subject of ongoing work by Kaletha, Minguez, Shin, and White (KMSW), who have constructed the correspondence for many groups using the geometry of the affine Grassmannian and the categorical Langlands program. The LLC is expected to satisfy: (1) compatibility with L-functions and ε-factors (Shahidi's method), (2) compatibility with parabolic induction and the Bernstein center, (3) compatibility with the global Langlands correspondence via local-global compatibility, (4) functoriality with respect to L-homomorphisms LH → LG. The local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over function fields was established by Lafforgue (2002) using the moduli stack of shtukas.

Worked Example: Matching Orbital Integrals in Endoscopy
Let G = SL(2) over a p-adic field F, and let H = SO(2) ≅ F× be its elliptic endoscopic group. Consider the regular semisimple element γ = diag(a, a-1) in G(F) with a ≠ ±1. Its stable conjugacy class corresponds to the element γH = a + a-1 in H(F). The transfer factor is Δ(γH, γ) = η(a - a-1) where η is the quadratic character associated to the endoscopic datum. Compute the matching of orbital integrals for the characteristic function f = 1G(OF) of the maximal compact subgroup.
The orbital integral on G is Oγ(f) = ∫Gγ(F)G(F) f(g-1γg) dg. For γ regular semisimple, Gγ is the diagonal torus T ≅ F×. The integral reduces to the volume of the intersection of the conjugacy class with G(OF). By the Cartan decomposition, G(F) = ⋃n≥0 G(OF) diag(ϖn, ϖ-n) G(OF). The orbital integral is non-zero iff γ is conjugate to an element of G(OF), i.e., iff v(a) = 0 and a + a-1 ∈ OF. On H, the stable orbital integral is SOγH(fH) = ∫H(F) fH(h-1γHh) dh. The transfer fH of f is the characteristic function of OF× times the transfer factor. The fundamental lemma asserts Oγ(f) = Δ(γH, γ) SOγH(fH). For f = 1G(OF), both sides equal 1 if v(a) = 0 and a + a-1 ∈ OF, and 0 otherwise, verifying the matching in this case.
Exam Strategy: Trace Formula Structure
When a question asks about the trace formula, identify which version: the Selberg trace formula (for compact quotients), the Arthur trace formula (for non-compact quotients with truncation), the invariant trace formula (stabilized geometric side), or the stable trace formula (fully stabilized). Remember the spectral side always involves automorphic representations with Arthur's coefficients aG(π), while the geometric side involves conjugacy classes with orbital integrals. For endoscopy questions, the key is the transfer factor Δ and the fundamental lemma matching orbital integrals on G and H. The multiplicity formula m(π) = |Sψ|-1 ∑s∈Sψ ⟨s, π⟩ is a frequent target.

The Geometric Satake Equivalence and the Affine Grassmannian

The geometric Satake equivalence, established by Mirković–Vilonen (2007) building on work of Ginzburg and Lusztig, is a tensor equivalence between the category of representations of the Langlands dual group LG and the category of G(O)-equivariant perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian GrG = G(F)/G(O), where F = ℂ((t)) and O = ℂ[[t]]. The affine Grassmannian is an ind-scheme whose ℂ-points parametrize G-bundles on the formal disc D = Spec O with a trivialization on the punctured disc D× = Spec F. The convolution product on perverse sheaves, defined via the diagram Gr × Gr ← Gr ×G(O) Gr → Gr, corresponds to the tensor product of representations. The fiber functor H•: PervG(O)(Gr) → Vec, given by hypercohomology, is a tensor functor whose automorphism group is canonically LG. This provides a geometric construction of the dual group. The geometric Satake equivalence is the local geometric Langlands correspondence for the trivial local system on the curve. It extends to the derived setting (Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg) and to the categorical Langlands program (Gaitsgory–Rozenblyum). The affine Grassmannian also carries a natural action of the loop group G(F) and the affine Weyl group. The Mirković–Vilonen cycles MVλ ⊂ GrG give a basis for the weight spaces of representations. The geometric Satake equivalence is a key input in the construction of the categorical local Langlands correspondence and in the proof of the geometric Langlands conjecture for GL(n) by Gaitsgory (2015). It also underlies the Fargues–Scholze construction of the local Langlands correspondence for p-adic groups via the moduli stack of G-bundles on the Fargues–Fontaine curve. The affine Grassmannian over finite fields plays a role in the geometric Langlands program over function fields, notably in the work of Lafforgue and others. The convolution category of perverse sheaves on GrG is equivalent to the category of representations of the quantum group Uq(LG) at a root of unity, linking the geometric Satake equivalence to quantum topology. The geometric Satake equivalence for the Kac–Moody group is related to the affine quantum group and the geometric Langlands program for affine Kac–Moody algebras.

The Fargues–Fontaine Curve and the p-adic Langlands Program

The Fargues–Fontaine curve XC = XFF(C, F) is a complete regular noetherian scheme of dimension 1 associated to a perfectoid field C of characteristic p and a p-adic field F. It was introduced by Fargues and Fontaine (2011) to geometrize p-adic Hodge theory. The curve is constructed as Proj(⊕n≥0 BdR+/ξn), where BdR is Fontaine's de Rham period ring and ξ is a generator of the kernel of θ: BdR+ → C. The curve XC has a canonical point ∞; its complement is an adic space related to the perfectoid field C. The category of vector bundles on XC is equivalent to the category of (φ, Γ)-modules over the Robba ring, and hence to the category of p-adic Galois representations of Gal(ℚ̄p/ℚp) via Fontaine's equivalence. The Harder–Narasimhan filtration on vector bundles corresponds to the Hodge–Tate filtration on Galois representations. The Fargues–Fontaine curve provides a geometric framework for the p-adic local Langlands correspondence: the moduli stack of G-bundles on XC and their modifications are related to the moduli space of p-divisible groups and the Lubin–Tate tower. Fargues and Scholze (2021) constructed the local Langlands correspondence for all reductive groups over p-adic fields using the geometry of the Fargues–Fontaine curve and the moduli stack of G-bundles on XC. The key object is the Hecke stack HckG parametrizing modifications of G-bundles at ∞, whose cohomology realizes the local Langlands correspondence. The Fargues–Scholze construction uses the six-functor formalism for étale sheaves on diamonds (v-sheaves). The curve also plays a central role in the p-adic Langlands program for GL(2) (Colmez, 2010), where the completed cohomology of the modular curve tower is related to vector bundles on XC via the Fargues–Fontaine equivalence. Scholze (2013) proved the LLC for GL(n) using perfectoid spaces and the Lubin–Tate tower; the Fargues–Fontaine curve later provided a geometric framework for such correspondences. The curve also appears in the work of Caraiani–Scholze on the cohomology of Shimura varieties and the construction of Galois representations associated to automorphic forms. The Fargues–Fontaine curve is expected to be the geometric object underlying the categorical p-adic Langlands program, where the category of sheaves on BunG(XC) is equivalent to the category of representations of the L-group.

The Categorical Geometric Langlands Program

The categorical geometric Langlands program, formulated by Gaitsgory and Lurie, replaces the bijection of the classical geometric Langlands correspondence with an equivalence of categories. For a smooth projective curve X over ℂ and a reductive group G, the categorical geometric Langlands conjecture asserts an equivalence of (∞,1)-categories: D-mod(BunG(X)) ≃ QCoh(LocSysLG(X)), where BunG(X) is the moduli stack of G-bundles on X, LocSysLG(X) is the moduli stack of LG-local systems on X, D-mod denotes the category of D-modules, and QCoh denotes the category of quasi-coherent sheaves. This equivalence is expected to be compatible with Hecke eigensheaf property: the Hecke operators on D-mod(BunG) correspond to tensoring with the standard representation on QCoh(LocSysLG). Gaitsgory announced a proof for GL(n) in 2015; the full details appear in a series of papers using the theory of chiral algebras, factorization algebras, and the geometric Satake equivalence. The proof constructs the equivalence via the Whittaker model and the geometric Langlands correspondence for the affine Kac–Moody algebra at the critical level. The categorical framework allows for the formulation of the geometric Langlands correspondence for singular supports, the ramified geometric Langlands correspondence, and the geometric Langlands correspondence for boundary conditions (the Kapustin–Witten approach via S-duality of N=4 super Yang–Mills theory). The categorical geometric Langlands program is closely related to the representation theory of affine Kac–Moody algebras at the critical level, the theory of vertex algebras, and the geometric representation theory of the loop group. The equivalence is constructed using the ind-coherent sheaves on the moduli stack of local systems and the renormalized D-modules on the moduli stack of bundles. The categorical trace formula, developed by Gaitsgory and Rozenblyum, provides a categorical analogue of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula and is expected to prove functoriality in the geometric setting. The categorical geometric Langlands program for general reductive groups is the subject of ongoing work by Gaitsgory, Raskin, Rozenblyum, and others. The categorical framework also informs the Fargues–Scholze construction of the p-adic local Langlands correspondence, where categories of sheaves on moduli stacks of G-bundles on the Fargues–Fontaine curve play a role analogous to D-modules on BunG.

The Stark Conjectures and Special Values of Artin L‑functions

The Stark conjectures, formulated by Harold Stark in the 1970s, predict explicit formulas for the leading terms of Artin L‑functions at s = 0 in terms of units in abelian extensions of number fields. Let K/k be a finite abelian extension with Galois group G, and let χ be a character of G. The Artin L‑function L(s, χ, K/k) has a zero of order r_χ at s = 0, where r_χ is the dimension of the χ‑isotypic component of the unit group of K tensored with ℂ. Stark's first conjecture asserts that the leading coefficient L*(0, χ) = lims→0 s-r_χ L(s, χ) is a non‑zero algebraic number times a regulator built from S‑units of K, where S is a finite set of places containing the archimedean ones and those ramified in K/k. The refined Stark conjecture (Stark's "main conjecture") gives an explicit formula: L*(0, χ) = ε(χ) · R_χ, where ε(χ) is a root of unity and R_χ is a determinant of logarithms of S‑units forming a basis of the χ‑component. When χ is trivial, this recovers the analytic class number formula. For non‑trivial χ, the conjecture predicts that the values L*(0, χ) generate the maximal abelian extension of k unramified outside S, providing a Kronecker‑type class field theory for totally real fields. The conjectures are known in several cases: for abelian extensions of ℚ (via Kronecker–Weber and Dirichlet's class number formula), for CM fields (via Tate, Deligne–Ribet, and Cassou‑Noguès), and for function fields (via Hayes and others). The equivariant Tamagawa number conjecture (ETNC) of Burns and Flach generalizes Stark's conjectures to motives and arbitrary orders of vanishing. A key computational tool is the Rubin–Stark element, an element in the exterior power of the S‑unit group whose regulator image equals the leading term. The conjectures remain open in general but have been verified numerically for thousands of fields and are deeply connected to the Brumer–Stark conjecture on annihilators of class groups.

The Beilinson Conjectures and Higher Regulators

The Beilinson conjectures, proposed by Alexander Beilinson in the 1980s, generalize the analytic class number formula and the Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture to arbitrary smooth projective varieties over number fields. For a smooth projective variety X over a number field k, the conjectures relate the leading coefficient of the Hasse–Weil L‑function L(H^i(X), s) at integer points s = m to the rank and regulator of the motivic cohomology group H2m-i+1_M(X, ℚ(m)). Motivic cohomology, defined by Bloch and refined by Voevodsky, is expected to be the universal cohomology theory for algebraic cycles; for m = 1 it coincides with the Chow group of codimension‑1 cycles modulo rational equivalence, and for m = 2 it relates to K2 of the function field. The regulator map r_D: H2m-i+1_M(X, ℚ(m)) → H2m-i+1_D(X_ℝ, ℝ(m)) lands in Deligne cohomology, a real vector space whose dimension equals the order of vanishing of L(H^i(X), s) at s = m. Beilinson's first conjecture predicts that the regulator map is injective with image a lattice of full rank. The second conjecture gives an explicit formula for the leading coefficient: L*(H^i(X), m) = c · R · Ω, where c is a rational number, R is the determinant of the regulator matrix with respect to a ℚ‑basis of motivic cohomology, and Ω is a product of periods comparing Betti and de Rham cohomology. For curves, i = 1, m = 1 recovers the BSD conjecture. For surfaces and m = 2, the conjecture involves the group of indecomposable K2 of the function field and the Bloch–Beilinson filtration on Chow groups. The conjectures are known in very few cases: for modular forms (via Kato, Kings, and others on Euler systems), for Hilbert modular surfaces (via Harder and others), and for certain Shimura varieties (via Nekovář, Scholl, and Kings). The Tamagawa number conjecture of Bloch and Kato provides a p‑adic analogue, and the equivariant Tamagawa number conjecture of Burns and Flach unifies both. A major open problem is constructing non‑trivial elements in motivic cohomology for general varieties; the conjectures imply that the rank of motivic cohomology equals the order of vanishing of the L‑function, itself unproven in most cases.

Worked comparison: Stark vs. Beilinson regulators
Explain how the Stark regulator for an abelian extension K/k and the Beilinson regulator for a curve X/ℚ both arise as determinants of logarithmic maps, and why the Stark regulator is a special case of the Beilinson regulator when X = Spec(k) and m = 0.
The Stark regulator for a character χ of Gal(K/k) is defined as follows. Let S be a finite set of places of k containing the archimedean places and those ramified in K. The χ‑component of the S‑unit group OK,S^× ⊗ ℂ decomposes as a direct sum of χ‑eigenspaces. Choose a ℚ‑basis {u1, …, u_r} of the χ‑component of the S‑units modulo torsion. For each place v ∈ S, let log_v: K_v^× → ℝ be the normalized logarithm (log_v(x) = log|x|_v for finite v, log_v(x) = log|x| for real v, 2 log|x| for complex v). The Stark regulator R_χ is the determinant of the r × r matrix (log_v(u_i))v,i after removing one row to account for the product formula ∑_v log_v(u) = 0. This determinant is independent of the choice of basis and the removed row up to sign. For a smooth projective curve X/ℚ, the Beilinson regulator at m = 1 maps the motivic cohomology group H1_M(X, ℚ(1)) ≅ Pic(X) ⊗ ℚ to Deligne cohomology H1_D(X_ℝ, ℝ(1)) ≅ H1(X(ℂ), ℝ(1))+ (the +‑eigenspace under complex conjugation). The regulator is given by integrating differential forms of the second kind against cycles, or equivalently by the map Div0(X) → Jac(X)(ℂ) ≅ H1(X, ℂ)/(H1(X, ℤ) + F1) followed by projection to the real part. In terms of logarithms, if D = ∑ n_i (P_i) is a degree‑0 divisor, its image in the Jacobian is represented by the vector (∫P0P_i ω_j) where ω_j is a basis of holomorphic differentials. The regulator matrix is the period matrix of the Jacobian. When X = Spec(k) for a number field k, the motivic cohomology H1_M(Spec(k), ℚ(0)) is K1(k) ⊗ ℚ ≅ k^× ⊗ ℚ. The Beilinson regulator at m = 0 maps this to H1_D(Spec(k)_ℝ, ℝ(0)) ≅ (k ⊗ ℝ)^× / ℝ^×_+ ≅ ℝr1+r2-1, and the map is exactly the Dirichlet logarithm log: k^× → ℝr1+r2-1. The determinant of this map on a basis of the unit group modulo torsion is the classical regulator of k. For an abelian extension K/k, the Stark regulator for the trivial character χ = 1 is precisely this classical regulator of K. For non‑trivial χ, the Stark regulator is the χ‑component of the regulator of K, which matches the Beilinson regulator applied to the motive h0(Spec(K))(χ) with its χ‑isotypic decomposition. Thus the Stark regulator is the Beilinson regulator for the Artin motive associated to the extension K/k at the critical point s = 0.
Exam strategy: distinguishing regulator constructions
When a question asks for the regulator in a specific context, identify the cohomology theory first: Dirichlet regulator uses K1 of a number field (units); Stark regulator uses S‑units of an abelian extension and Artin L‑functions at s = 0; Beilinson regulator uses motivic cohomology H2m-i+1_M(X, ℚ(m)) and Deligne cohomology at integer points s = m; Bloch–Kato regulator uses Galois cohomology H1_f(ℚ_p, V) and p‑adic Hodge theory. The common pattern is a map from an algebraic object (units, cycles, Galois cohomology classes) to a real or p‑adic vector space whose dimension equals the order of vanishing of an L‑function. Memorize the table: (theory, source, target, L‑function point) = (Dirichlet, O_k^×, ℝr1+r2-1, ζ_k(s) at s=1), (Stark, OK,S^×(χ), ℝr_χ, L(s,χ) at s=0), (Beilinson, H_M, H_D, L(H^i,s) at s=m), (Bloch–Kato, H1_f, DdR/F0, L(V,s) at s=1).

The Taylor–Wiles Method and Modularity Lifting Theorems

The Taylor–Wiles method, introduced by Richard Taylor and Andrew Wiles in 1995, is the central technique for proving modularity lifting theorems: statements that a Galois representation ρ: G_ℚ → GL_n(𝔽_p) or GL_n(ℤ_p) arises from a modular form if its residual representation ρ̄ does. The method compares two deformation rings: the universal deformation ring R parametrizing lifts of ρ̄ with prescribed local conditions (e.g., crystalline at p, minimal ramification elsewhere), and the Hecke algebra T acting on the space of modular forms of a given level and weight that give rise to ρ̄. The goal is to prove an isomorphism R ≅ T, which implies that every deformation of ρ̄ comes from a modular form. The key innovation was to introduce auxiliary primes q ≡ 1 (mod p) at which the deformation condition is relaxed to allow "Taylor–Wiles primes" — primes where the local deformation ring is a power series ring over ℤ_p in a controlled number of variables. By adding enough such primes, one can make the dual Selmer group vanish, which by a numerical criterion (the Wiles–Lenstra criterion) forces the map R → T to be an isomorphism of complete intersections. The method requires a "minimal" case where the Selmer group is already small, and then a "patching" argument that constructs a module over a power series ring in many variables that is free of rank one. The original proof used the fact that the deformation ring for a Taylor–Wiles prime q is ℤ_p[[x1, …, x_g]] where g = dim H1(G_ℚ_q, ad0 ρ̄) = 1 for a suitable choice of q. The patching argument produces a "patched module" M_∞ over a power series ring S_∞ = ℤ_p[[y1, …, y_r]] such that M_∞ is free of rank one over S_∞, and M_∞/(y1, …, y_r) recovers the original Hecke module. This implies R ≅ T. The method has been generalized extensively: to Hilbert modular forms (Taylor, 1994; Fujiwara, 1999), to Siegel modular forms (Genestier–Tilouine, 2000; Pilloni–Stroh, 2016), to automorphic forms on unitary groups (Clozel–Harris–Taylor, 2008; Barnet‑Lamb–Gee–Geraghty–Taylor, 2014), and to the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence (Emerton, 2011). A crucial technical ingredient is "Ihara's lemma" or its variants, which control the multiplicity of modular forms in the cohomology of Shimura varieties. The Calegari–Geraghty method (2018) extends the Taylor–Wiles method to non‑minimal cases and to symmetric power liftings using the geometry of the Emerton–Gee stack. The method also underlies the proof of the Sato–Tate conjecture for elliptic curves over totally real fields (Barnet‑Lamb–Geraghty–Harris–Taylor, 2011) and the modularity of all elliptic curves over ℚ (Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor, 2001).

The Gan–Gross–Prasad Conjectures and Period Integrals

The Gan–Gross–Prasad (GGP) conjectures, formulated by Wee Teck Gan, Benedict Gross, and Dipendra Prasad between 2009 and 2012, predict a precise relationship between the non‑vanishing of certain period integrals of automorphic forms and the central values of L‑functions. Let G be a reductive group over a number field F, and let H ⊂ G be a spherical subgroup (i.e., a subgroup such that a Borel subgroup of G has an open orbit on G/H). The conjectures concern the restriction of an automorphic representation π of G(𝔸_F) to H(𝔸_F). The period integral P_H(φ) = ∫H(F)H(𝔸_F) φ(h) dh for φ ∈ π is conjectured to be non‑zero for some φ if and only if the central value L(1/2, π, r) ≠ 0, where r is a certain representation of the L‑group ^L H associated to the embedding ^L H → ^L G. In the classical case, G = SO(n+1) × SO(n) and H = SO(n) diagonally embedded; the period integral is the integral of a cusp form on SO(n+1) against a cusp form on SO(n). The conjecture then relates the non‑vanishing of this integral to L(1/2, π1 × π2) for the corresponding representations of GL(n+1) and GL(n) via the local Langlands correspondence. The local GGP conjecture predicts that for each place v, the space HomH(F_v)(π_v, ℂ) is at most one‑dimensional, and its dimension is given by an epsilon factor ε(1/2, π_v, r_v). The global conjecture states that the period integral factors as a product of local functionals, and its non‑vanishing is equivalent to the non‑vanishing of the central L‑value. The conjectures have been proved in many cases: for unitary groups (Gan–Ichino, 2014; Beuzart‑Plessis, 2016), for orthogonal groups (Waldspurger, 2010; Zhang, 2014), and for the Bessel and Fourier–Jacobi periods (Gan–Gross–Prasad, 2012; Liu, 2017). The proofs use the relative trace formula, which compares the spectral expansion (involving period integrals) with the geometric expansion (involving orbital integrals). A key input is the fundamental lemma for the relative trace formula, proved by Ngô and others. The GGP conjectures are a vast generalization of the Waldspurger formula (for toric periods on GL(2)) and the Gross–Zagier formula (for Heegner points on modular curves). They also imply the Ichino–Ikeda conjecture on the Petersson inner product of Saito–Kurokawa lifts. The conjectures are part of the broader "relative Langlands program," which seeks to understand the branching laws for representations of real and p‑adic groups and their relation to L‑functions.

Perfectoid Spaces and the Torsion Cohomology of Shimura Varieties

Perfectoid spaces, introduced by Peter Scholze in 2012, are a class of adic spaces that generalize rigid‑analytic varieties and provide a geometric framework for p‑adic Hodge theory in families. A perfectoid field is a complete non‑archimedean field K of characteristic 0 with residue characteristic p such that the Frobenius map O_K/p → O_K/p is surjective and the value group |K^×| is dense in ℝ>0. Examples include ℚ_p(p1/p^∞)^∧ and ℂ_p. A perfectoid space is an adic space locally isomorphic to Spa(R, R+) where R is a perfectoid algebra. The key feature is the tilt equivalence: a perfectoid field K of characteristic 0 has a tilt K^♭ of characteristic p, and the categories of perfectoid spaces over K and over K^♭ are equivalent. This allows one to transfer problems in p‑adic Hodge theory to characteristic p, where they become more tractable. Scholze used perfectoid spaces to construct the Hodge–Tate period map π_HT: XK^p → FlG,μ for Shimura varieties X of Hodge type at infinite level K^p, where FlG,μ is the flag variety. The map is G(ℚ_p)‑equivariant and its fibers are related to the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence. The cohomology of Shimura varieties at infinite level, H^i(XK^p, ℤ_p), carries an action of G(ℚ_p) × G(𝔸_f^p) × Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ). Scholze's theory of "diamonds" (v‑sheaves on the category of perfectoid spaces) allows one to define the cohomology of the infinite‑level Shimura variety as a solid ℤ_p‑module. The main theorem (Scholze, 2015; Caraiani–Scholze, 2017) describes the torsion cohomology H^i(XK^p, 𝔽_p) in terms of the mod‑p local Langlands correspondence for GL_n(ℚ_p). Specifically, the G(ℚ_p)‑representation on the torsion cohomology is a direct sum of representations π(ρ) associated to Galois representations ρ: G_ℚ_p → GL_n(𝔽_p) via the mod‑p local Langlands correspondence. This result is a geometric realization of the p‑adic Langlands program for GL_n. The perfectoid approach also gives a new proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over p‑adic fields (Scholze, 2013) using the cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower, which is a perfectoid space at infinite level. The Fargues–Fontaine curve, introduced earlier, can be constructed as a quotient of a perfectoid space by a Frobenius action, and the classification of vector bundles on it (the Fargues–Scholze theorem) is proved using perfectoid geometry. The theory has been extended to the cohomology of Rapoport–Zink spaces (Scholze–Weinstein, 2013) and to the mod‑p cohomology of Shimura varieties for general reductive groups (Caraiani–Emerton–Gee–Geraghty–Paskunas–Shin, 2022). A major open problem is the description of the integral cohomology H^i(XK^p, ℤ_p) and its relation to the p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL_n(ℚ_p) in the sense of Colmez and others.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Stark regulator (for Artin L‑functions at s = 0) with the Dirichlet regulator (for ζ_k(s) at s = 1); the Stark regulator uses S‑units and vanishes at s = 0, the Dirichlet regulator uses units and gives the residue at s = 1.
  • Assuming the Beilinson regulator is defined for all varieties; it is only conjecturally defined on motivic cohomology, which is not known to exist as a universal theory for all smooth projective varieties (Voevodsky's construction works for schemes over a field but the comparison with L‑functions is conjectural).
  • Thinking the Taylor–Wiles method proves modularity for all Galois representations; it requires the residual representation to be absolutely irreducible, modular, and to satisfy certain local conditions (e.g., "Taylor–Wiles primes" must exist).
  • Believing the Gan–Gross–Prasad conjecture is a theorem for all groups; it is proved for unitary, orthogonal, and symplectic groups in many cases, but remains open for exceptional groups and for non‑tempered representations.
  • Stating that perfectoid spaces are rigid‑analytic varieties; they are adic spaces, a generalization of rigid‑analytic spaces, and the tilt equivalence is a theorem about perfectoid spaces, not about all rigid‑analytic spaces.
  • Confusing the Fargues–Fontaine curve (a scheme over ℤ_p whose fibers are curves over perfectoid fields) with a perfectoid space; the curve is constructed from a perfectoid field but is not itself perfectoid.
  • Assuming the mod‑p local Langlands correspondence for GL_n(ℚ_p) is a bijection between irreducible smooth representations and Galois representations; it is a correspondence between certain classes of representations (e.g., supersingular representations and irreducible Galois representations) and is not a bijection in general.

The Hodge Conjecture and Algebraic Cycles

The Hodge conjecture, formulated by W. V. D. Hodge (1950), asserts that on a smooth projective complex variety X, every rational Hodge class of type (p,p) is a rational linear combination of classes of algebraic cycles of codimension p. A Hodge class is an element of H2p(X,ℚ) ∩ Hp,p(X) where the Hodge decomposition H^k(X,ℂ) = ⊕i+j=k Hi,j(X) holds. The conjecture is known for p = 0,1 (Lefschetz theorem on (1,1)-classes) and for abelian varieties of dimension ≤ 4. For general varieties, it remains open. The conjecture implies that the Hodge filtration on cohomology is induced by algebraic cycles, linking topology, complex geometry, and arithmetic. A counterexample to the integral version was given by Atiyah and Hirzebruch (1962) using torsion classes; the rational version is still open. The generalized Hodge conjecture extends the statement to sub‑Hodge structures. Progress includes Voisin's work on the coniveau filtration and motivic reformulations. The Hodge conjecture is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems.

The Grothendieck Standard Conjectures

In 1969 Alexander Grothendieck proposed a set of conjectures — now called the standard conjectures — that would provide a foundation for a theory of pure motives over a field k. They are labelled A, B, C, D, and sometimes E. Conjecture A (Lefschetz type) states that the Lefschetz operator Λ on cohomology is induced by an algebraic cycle; equivalently, the hard Lefschetz isomorphism is algebraic. Conjecture B (Hodge type) asserts that the Hodge filtration on the de Rham cohomology of a smooth projective variety is induced by algebraic cycles; this is a motivic analogue of the Hodge conjecture. Conjecture C (Künneth type) claims that the Künneth components of the diagonal Δ ⊂ X × X are algebraic cycles; this would give a motivic decomposition of the identity. Conjecture D (numerical equivalence = homological equivalence) predicts that numerical equivalence of algebraic cycles coincides with homological equivalence for any Weil cohomology theory. Conjecture E (semisimplicity) states that the category of motives with respect to numerical equivalence is abelian semisimple. These conjectures are known for abelian varieties (Lieberman, Kleiman) and for varieties dominated by products of curves. They imply the finite‑dimensionality of the motive of any smooth projective variety and the existence of a Weil cohomology theory with good properties. The standard conjectures remain open in general; they are stronger than the Hodge conjecture and the Tate conjecture.

Worked Example: Verifying Conjecture C for a Product of Curves
Let X = C₁ × C₂ be a product of two smooth projective curves over ℂ. Show that the Künneth components of the diagonal Δ_X ⊂ X × X are algebraic cycles.
The diagonal Δ_X ⊂ (C₁ × C₂) × (C₁ × C₂) ≅ (C₁ × C₁) × (C₂ × C₂). The Künneth decomposition of H^*(X) is ⊕i,j H^i(C₁) ⊗ H^j(C₂). The diagonal class [Δ_X] corresponds to the identity endomorphism of H^*(X). Under the Künneth isomorphism, the identity decomposes as Σi,j π_i ⊗ π_j where π_i are the projectors onto H^i(C₁) and H^j(C₂). For curves, the projectors are given by algebraic cycles: the point class for H0, the class of a point for H2, and the class of the diagonal minus the point classes for H1. Since each π_i is algebraic on C₁ and C₂, their external products are algebraic on X × X. Hence each Künneth component of Δ_X is an algebraic cycle, verifying Conjecture C for products of curves.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks about the relationship between the Hodge conjecture and the standard conjectures, recall that Conjecture B is the motivic incarnation of the Hodge conjecture. The standard conjectures imply the Hodge conjecture (via B) and the Tate conjecture (via D). Memorise the table of known cases for the Hodge conjecture; examiners often test the Lefschetz (1,1) theorem and the abelian variety results.

The Langlands Program for Function Fields and Global Shtukas

The geometric Langlands program over function fields was initiated by Drinfeld (1974) for GL(2) and extended by Lafforgue (2002) to GL(n) over global function fields. Let F = 𝔽_q(X) be the function field of a smooth projective curve X over a finite field 𝔽_q. The Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over F establishes a bijection between irreducible cuspidal automorphic representations of GL(n,𝔸_F) and irreducible n‑dimensional ℓ‑adic Galois representations of Gal(F^sep/F) that are unramified outside a finite set of places. Lafforgue's proof uses the moduli stack of global shtukas: a shtuka is a vector bundle on X with a Frobenius‑twisted isomorphism at a finite set of points. The cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas carries an action of GL(n,𝔸_F) × Gal(F^sep/F), and the correspondence is realized by the decomposition of this cohomology into Hecke eigensheaves. The geometric Langlands program for function fields replaces ℓ‑adic sheaves by D‑modules on the moduli stack of G‑bundles on X, and the correspondence becomes an equivalence of derived categories. The work of Vincent Lafforgue (2018) extends the correspondence to all reductive groups over function fields using the theory of excursion operators and the Langlands–Kottwitz method. The function field setting avoids the analytic difficulties of the number field case and has served as a proving ground for many ideas later adapted to the p‑adic Langlands program.

The p‑adic Langlands Correspondence for GL(n)

The p‑adic Langlands correspondence aims to associate to a continuous representation ρ: Gℚ_p → GL_n(ℚ_p) a unitary admissible Banach space representation Π(ρ) of GL_n(ℚ_p) over ℚ_p, compatible with the local Langlands correspondence modulo p and with the p‑adic Hodge theory of ρ. For n = 2, the correspondence was constructed by Colmez (2010) using (φ,Γ)-modules and the theory of p‑adic Banach spaces; it is a bijection between irreducible crystalline representations with distinct Hodge–Tate weights and certain unitary principal series or supersingular representations. For n ≥ 3, the correspondence is largely conjectural. Breuil, Herzig, and others have formulated a candidate using the theory of (φ,Γ)-modules over the Robba ring and the notion of trianguline representations. The Emerton–Gee stack parametrizes (φ,Γ)-modules and provides a geometric framework. The correspondence is expected to satisfy: (1) compatibility with the classical local Langlands correspondence via reduction modulo p; (2) compatibility with the p‑adic Hodge theory functors Dcris, Dst, DdR; (3) a local‑global compatibility with the completed cohomology of Shimura varieties. A recent preprint by Hansen–Kaletha–Weinstein proposes a categorical version for GL_n using the Fargues–Fontaine curve and the geometric Satake equivalence. The p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL(n) is a central open problem; its resolution would unify p‑adic Hodge theory, the representation theory of p‑adic groups, and the arithmetic of Shimura varieties.

The Theory of (φ,Γ)‑Modules and p‑adic Galois Representations

Introduced by Fontaine (1990) and developed by Cherbonnier–Colmez (1999), the theory of (φ,Γ)-modules provides an equivalence between the category of p‑adic Galois representations of Gℚ_p and the category of étale (φ,Γ)-modules over the Robba ring ℛ. Let ℚ_p^cyc be the cyclotomic extension, Γ = Gal(ℚ_p^cyc/ℚ_p) ≅ ℤ_p^×, and φ the Frobenius on the Robba ring. A (φ,Γ)-module is a finite free ℛ‑module D equipped with a semilinear φ‑action and a commuting continuous semilinear Γ‑action such that the linearized φ is an isomorphism. The functor D(V) = (V ⊗ℚ_p ℬrig^†)Gℚ_p^cyc gives the equivalence. This theory translates Galois‑theoretic problems into linear algebra over ℛ. Key applications: (i) the classification of crystalline, semistable, and de Rham representations via the associated filtered (φ,N)-modules (Fontaine's functors Dcris, Dst, DdR); (ii) the construction of the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚ_p) by Colmez; (iii) the proof of the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture for two‑dimensional representations (Kisin, 2009) via deformation theory and modularity lifting; the Emerton–Gee stack (2019) later provided a geometric framework for such results; (iv) the study of p‑adic families of Galois representations via eigenvarieties (Buzzard, Chenevier, Bellaïche). The Robba ring ℛ consists of power series convergent on an annulus 0 < r < 1; its structure allows the use of p‑adic differential equations. The theory has been extended to p‑adic fields K/ℚ_p using (φ,Γ_K)-modules where Γ_K = Gal(K^cyc/K). The (φ,Γ)-module framework is now a standard tool in p‑adic Hodge theory and the p‑adic Langlands program.

  • Assuming the standard conjectures are proved for all smooth projective varieties; they are only known for abelian varieties and varieties dominated by products of curves.
  • Thinking the geometric Langlands correspondence for function fields is a theorem for all reductive groups; Lafforgue proved it for GL(n), Vincent Lafforgue extended to all groups but with additional technical hypotheses.
  • Believing the p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL(n) is a bijection for n ≥ 3; it is only constructed for n = 2 and remains conjectural in higher rank.
  • Stating that (φ,Γ)-modules classify all p‑adic Galois representations without the étale condition; the equivalence holds for étale (φ,Γ)-modules, which correspond exactly to continuous representations.
  • Mixing up the Robba ring (convergent on an annulus) with the period ring ℬdR (a complete discrete valuation field); they serve different roles in p‑adic Hodge theory.

Prismatic Cohomology and the p‑adic Hodge Theory of Scholze

Prismatic cohomology, introduced by Bhatt and Scholze (2019), provides a unified framework for the various p‑adic cohomology theories — crystalline, de Rham, étale, and syntomic — by constructing a single cohomology theory valued in a derived prism (A, I) where A is a δ‑ring and I = (p) is a principal ideal. A prism is a pair (A, I) with A a commutative ring, I an ideal such that A is (p, I)‑complete, p ∈ I, and the Frobenius lift φ: A → A satisfies φ(x) ≡ x^p (mod p). The key example is the prism (A_inf, (ξ)) where A_inf = W(O_C^♭) for a perfectoid field C and ξ is a generator of the kernel of the Fontaine map θ: A_inf → O_C.

For a p‑adic formal scheme X over O_K, the prismatic site X_△ consists of prisms (A, I) equipped with a map X → Spf(A/I). The prismatic cohomology complex Δ_X/A is a derived functor on this site. Its fundamental properties include: (1) a natural Frobenius φ on Δ_X/A; (2) a Nygaard filtration N^≥• Δ_X/A such that gr^i_N Δ_X/A ≃ Δ_X/A {−i}[−i]; (3) comparison isomorphisms after base change: Δ_X/A ⊗^L_A A_inf ≃ RΓ_ét(X_C, Z_p) ⊗^L_Z_p A_inf (étale), Δ_X/A ⊗^L_A O_C ≃ RΓ_dR(X/O_K) ⊗^L_O_K O_C (de Rham), and Δ_X/A ⊗^L_A A_cris ≃ RΓ_cris(X/O_K) (crystalline). These isomorphisms are compatible with Frobenius and filtrations, recovering the classical p‑adic Hodge theory comparison theorems.

The theory also yields a prismatic F‑gauge, a generalization of the filtered φ‑module of Fontaine, which provides a framework for studying prismatic crystals. In particular, for a smooth proper scheme X over O_K, the prismatic cohomology Δ_X/A_inf is a perfect complex of A_inf‑modules equipped with φ and a Nygaard filtration, and its base change to A_cris recovers the crystalline cohomology with its Frobenius and Hodge filtration. This perspective has simplified proofs of the integral p‑adic Hodge theory conjectures (e.g., the integrality of the Hodge–Tate comparison) and has led to new results such as the prismatic Poincaré duality and the prismatic Künneth formula.

Applications extend to the study of p‑adic Galois representations via the prismatic F‑gauge, to the construction of the p‑adic Simpson correspondence (see later section), and to the cohomology of Shimura varieties where prismatic cohomology provides a natural integral structure on the completed cohomology groups. Recent work by Bhatt, Lurie, and others has established a six‑functor formalism for prismatic cohomology.

The Moduli of p‑divisible Groups and Rapoport–Zink Spaces

Rapoport–Zink spaces are local analogues of Shimura varieties that parametrize deformations of a fixed p‑divisible group with additional structure (polarizations, endomorphisms, level structures). Let k be a perfect field of characteristic p, and let X0 be a p‑divisible group over k of height h and dimension d. The Rapoport–Zink functor RZX0 assigns to a complete local Noetherian O_{breve{K}}‑algebra R with nilpotent ideal the set of isomorphism classes of deformations X of X0 to R together with a quasi‑isogeny ρ: X0 ×_k R/p → X ×_R R/p of height zero. When X0 carries a polarization λ0 and an action of a semisimple Q_p‑algebra B, the functor is refined to include compatible polarizations and B‑actions.

The resulting functor is representable by a formal scheme RZX0 locally formally of finite type over Spf O_{breve{K}}. Its generic fiber is a rigid analytic variety over breve{K} (the completion of the maximal unramified extension of Q_p). The geometry of these spaces is governed by the Dieudonné module of X0: the tangent space at a point corresponds to the space of deformations of the associated filtered φ‑module. The dimension of RZX0 equals the dimension of the associated local Shimura datum; for example, for the Lubin–Tate tower (height h, dimension 1) the space has dimension h−1.

Rapoport–Zink spaces play a central role in the local Langlands correspondence. The ℓ‑adic cohomology of the tower of Rapoport–Zink spaces (with varying level structures) realizes the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n(Q_p) and its inner forms, as proved by Harris–Taylor and Boyer. Moreover, the nearby cycles sheaf on the special fiber carries an action of the Weil group WQ_p × GL_n(Q_p) × D^× (where D is the division algebra invariant), and its cohomology realizes the local Jacquet–Langlands correspondence. The uniformization theorem of Rapoport–Zink identifies the p‑adic completion of a Shimura variety along its Newton stratum with a quotient of a product of a Rapoport–Zink space and a p‑adic symmetric domain, providing a crucial link between global and local Langlands.

Recent advances include the construction of the “diamond” associated to a Rapoport–Zink space in the language of Scholze’s perfectoid geometry, allowing the use of étale cohomology of diamonds to study the cohomology of the tower. The work of Caraiani–Scholze on the cohomology of Rapoport–Zink spaces for GL_n has established the local Langlands correspondence in families and proved the Kottwitz conjecture on the description of the cohomology as a virtual representation of the relevant groups.

The Relative Langlands Program and Endoscopic Transfer

The relative Langlands program, formulated by Sakellaridis and Venkatesh (2012), generalizes the classical Langlands correspondence by replacing the group G with a spherical variety X = HG (or G/H) and studying the spectral decomposition of L2(HG) or the space of automorphic forms on G that are H‑invariant. The conjectural framework associates to a spherical variety X a “relative Langlands dual” datum (^L X) consisting of a complex reductive group ^L G (the Langlands dual of G) together with a Hamiltonian action of ^L G on a symplectic variety ^L X. The functoriality principle predicts a transfer of automorphic representations from G to H (or vice versa) governed by the geometry of ^L X.

A central tool is endoscopic transfer, which originated in the work of Langlands and Shelstad on the stabilization of the trace formula. For a connected reductive group G over a local or global field, an endoscopic datum is a tuple (H, s, ^L η) where H is a quasi‑split endoscopic group of G, s ∈ ^L G is a semisimple element, and ^L η: ^L H → ^L G is an embedding of L‑groups centralizing s. The fundamental lemma (proved by Ngô for Lie algebras; the group case follows via reduction arguments of Waldspurger and work of Chaudouard–Laumon) asserts the equality of orbital integrals on G and stable orbital integrals on H for matching functions. This equality enables the transfer of distributions (characters of representations) from H to G, realizing the local Langlands correspondence for endoscopic groups.

In the relative setting, the spherical variety X = HG gives rise to a “relative trace formula” whose spectral side involves periods of automorphic forms over H. The geometric side involves orbital integrals on the symmetric space. The relative fundamental lemma, proved in many cases by Yun, Zhang, and others, establishes a matching of orbital integrals between G and its endoscopic symmetric spaces. This matching yields a relative Langlands correspondence: to an automorphic representation π of G with a non‑zero H‑period one associates a representation of the dual group ^L H (or a parameter into ^L G) satisfying certain local conditions.

Applications include the Gan–Gross–Prasad conjectures on branching laws for classical groups, the Ichino–Ikeda formula for central L‑values, and the recent proof of the global Gan–Gross–Prasad conjecture for unitary groups by Beuzart‑Plessis, Liu, Zhang, and others using the relative trace formula and endoscopic transfer. The program also connects to the theory of automorphic periods and the arithmetic of special values of L‑functions.

The p‑adic Simpson Correspondence and Nonabelian Hodge Theory

The classical Simpson correspondence (1990) establishes an equivalence between the category of semistable Higgs bundles with vanishing Chern classes on a smooth projective complex variety X and the category of semisimple local systems (representations of π1(X)). In the p‑adic setting, the correspondence relates p‑adic Galois representations of the étale fundamental group of a smooth proper rigid analytic variety over a p‑adic field to Higgs bundles (or more generally, to objects in the derived category of coherent sheaves) on the associated algebraic variety.

The p‑adic Simpson correspondence was first constructed by Faltings (2005) for abelian varieties and later generalized by Liu–Zhu (2017) and by Diao–Lan–Liu–Zhu (2020) using the theory of prismatic cohomology. The key input is the prismatic F‑gauge attached to a p‑adic Galois representation via the prismatic cohomology of the variety. For a smooth proper formal scheme X over O_K, the prismatic cohomology Δ_X/A_inf carries a Frobenius φ and a Nygaard filtration. The associated graded pieces of the Nygaard filtration, after base change to O_C, recover the Hodge–Tate cohomology groups, which are identified with the cohomology of the Higgs bundle (Ω^•_X, θ) where θ is the Higgs field.

The correspondence can be stated as an equivalence of ∞‑categories between the category of “small” p‑adic Galois representations (those with Hodge–Tate weights in [0, p−2]) and the category of “small” Higgs bundles (those with nilpotent Higgs field and weights in the same range). The functor from Galois representations to Higgs bundles is given by the prismatic F‑gauge construction; the inverse functor uses the p‑adic Riemann–Hilbert correspondence of Deninger–Scholl and the theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules. The correspondence is compatible with tensor products, duals, and the formation of cohomology groups.

Recent work of Bhatt–Lurie (2022) has placed the p‑adic Simpson correspondence in the context of the “prismatic nonabelian Hodge theory”, where the moduli stack of p‑adic Galois representations (the Emerton–Gee stack) is compared to the moduli stack of Higgs bundles via a derived version of the prismatic site. This perspective yields a conceptual proof of the correspondence and extends it to singular varieties and to families. The p‑adic Simpson correspondence provides a geometric framework for the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL_n in the crystalline case and informs the study of the cohomology of Shimura varieties via the Fargues–Fontaine curve.

Integral Models of Shimura Varieties and the Kuga–Satake Construction

Integral models of Shimura varieties over the ring of integers of the reflex field provide a geometric setting for the study of the reduction of automorphic forms and the realization of Galois representations in the cohomology of the special fiber. For a Shimura datum (G, X) with G a reductive group over Q and X a G(R)‑conjugacy class of homomorphisms S → G_R, the associated Shimura variety Sh_K(G, X) is a projective system of quasi‑projective varieties over the reflex field E. The problem of constructing smooth integral models over O_E,(p) (or over O_E) has been solved in the PEL case by Kottwitz, Rapoport, and others, and in the abelian type case by Kisin (2010) using the theory of p‑divisible groups and the Kuga–Satake construction.

The Kuga–Satake construction embeds a Shimura variety of Hodge type into a Siegel modular variety by associating to a polarized Hodge structure of weight 1 a polarized Hodge structure of weight 1 on the Clifford algebra. In the integral setting, Kisin uses the Kuga–Satake abelian scheme to extend the moduli problem to a moduli of abelian varieties with additional endomorphisms and polarizations, which admits a smooth integral model by the theory of Drinfeld level structures and the Serre–Tate theorem. The resulting integral model is canonical and independent of the choice of embedding.

The special fiber of these integral models carries a stratification by Newton polygons (or by the isomorphism class of the associated p‑divisible group). The cohomology of the special fiber, in particular the nearby cycles complex, realizes the local Langlands correspondence for the group G(Q_p) and the action of the inertia group. The work of Caraiani–Scholze on the cohomology of Shimura varieties uses the perfectoid Shimura variety at infinite level to compute the completed cohomology and to prove the local‑global compatibility of the Langlands correspondence for torsion classes.

Recent developments include the construction of integral models for Shimura varieties of non‑abelian type (by Madapusi‑Pera, Kim, and others) using the theory of canonical models and the Kuga–Satake construction for Hodge structures with non‑trivial Mumford–Tate groups. These models are essential for the study of the arithmetic of automorphic forms beyond the PEL case, for the proof of the Ramanujan conjecture for GL_n over CM fields, and for the construction of p‑adic L‑functions via the Eisenstein cohomology on the integral model.

The Colmez Functor and the p‑adic Local Langlands Correspondence for GL(2)

The p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚₚ) was established by Colmez (2010) using the theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules and the Fontaine functor Dcris. The Colmez functor attaches to a two‑dimensional p‑adic Galois representation ρ of Gℚₚ a locally analytic representation Π(ρ) of GL₂(ℚₚ) on a space of locally analytic vectors in a unitary Banach representation. When ρ is de Rham with distinct Hodge–Tate weights (0, k − 1) and k ≥ 2, Π(ρ) contains the locally algebraic representation Symk‑2(ℚₚ²) ⊗ π(ρ) where π(ρ) is the smooth irreducible representation predicted by the classical local Langlands correspondence. The construction proceeds by first forming the (φ,Γ)‑module D(ρ) over the Robba ring, then applying the (φ,Γ)‑module version of the Fontaine–Herr complex to obtain a (φ,Γ)‑module over the period ring BdR⁺. The resulting (φ,Γ)‑module is equipped with a natural action of the mirabolic subgroup, and the locally analytic induction from the mirabolic to GL₂(ℚₚ) yields Π(ρ). Colmez proved that the correspondence is compatible with the classical local Langlands correspondence modulo p, with the local‑global compatibility for modular forms, and with the p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚₚ) in the sense of Breuil and Schneider. The functor is exact on the category of trianguline representations and satisfies a compatibility with tensor products and duals. Recent work of Breuil–Hellmann–Schraen extends the construction to potentially semi‑stable representations with non‑distinct Hodge–Tate weights, using the theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules with coefficients in a finite extension of ℚₚ.

The Iwasawa Main Conjecture for Modular Forms and the Greenberg–Stevens Formula

Let f be a normalized cuspidal eigenform of weight k ≥ 2, level N prime to p, and ordinary at p. The p‑adic L‑function Lp(f, s) constructed by Mazur–Kitagawa (1986) interpolates the critical values L(f, χ, j) for Dirichlet characters χ of p‑power conductor and integers j with 1 ≤ j ≤ k − 1. The Iwasawa main conjecture for f asserts that the characteristic ideal of the Selmer group Sel(ℚ∞, Vf) over the cyclotomic ℤₚ‑extension ℚ∞ coincides with the principal ideal generated by Lp(f, s) in the Iwasawa algebra Λ = ℤₚ[[Γ]]. Greenberg and Stevens (1993) proved a formula for the derivative of Lp(f, s) at s = 1 in terms of the p‑adic height of a Heegner point on the modular Jacobian J₀(N). Their formula reads Lp′(f, 1) = (Ωf⁺/Ωf⁻) · ⟨P, P⟩p where Ωf⁺, Ωf⁻ are the real and imaginary periods of f, P is the Heegner point, and ⟨·,·⟩p is the p‑adic height pairing. This result was a crucial input for the proof of the main conjecture in the ordinary case by Skinner–Urban (2014) using the Eisenstein congruence method and the theory of p‑adic families of Galois representations. The Greenberg–Stevens formula also provides a p‑adic analogue of the Gross–Zagier formula and underlies the construction of p‑adic L‑functions for higher rank groups via the doubling method.

The Heegner point P is constructed from a CM point of discriminant −11 on X₀(11). The p‑adic height ⟨P, P⟩₅ is computed via the Coleman–Gross p‑adic height pairing and equals 0.037… in ℚ₅. The Eigencurve and p‑adic Families of Overconvergent Modular Forms

The eigencurve, introduced by Coleman and Mazur (1998), is a rigid analytic curve that parametrizes finite‑slope systems of Hecke eigenvalues for overconvergent p‑adic modular forms of tame level N. Points on the eigencurve correspond to pairs (κ, aₚ) where κ : ℤₚˣ → ℂₚˣ is a continuous weight character and aₚ is the Uₚ‑eigenvalue of an overconvergent eigenform of weight κ. The construction uses the theory of Fredholm determinants for the compact operator Uₚ acting on the Banach space of overconvergent forms of a given weight. The eigencurve is equidimensional of dimension one, and its projection to weight space is finite and flat over the locus of classical weights k ≥ 2. Classical points (those coming from classical modular forms) are dense in the eigencurve, and the Zariski closure of the set of classical points of a fixed slope is an irreducible component. The eigencurve carries a natural sheaf of p‑adic Galois representations constructed by Kisin (2003) using the theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules; this yields a p‑adic local Langlands correspondence in families. Recent work of Bellaïche–Chenevier (2009) and of Liu–Wan–Xiao (2017) has refined the geometry of the eigencurve at boundary points, proving that the eigencurve is smooth at non‑critical classical points and that the weight map is étale there. The eigencurve also plays a central role in the proof of the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture for two‑dimensional representations by Emerton (2006) via the completed cohomology of modular curves.

The Arithmetic of Drinfeld Modular Curves and the Function Field Langlands Correspondence

Drinfeld modular curves are the function‑field analogues of classical modular curves. Let 𝔽q be a finite field, A = 𝔽q[T] the polynomial ring, and K = 𝔽q(T) its fraction field. For a non‑zero ideal 𝔫 ⊂ A, the Drinfeld modular curve Y₀(𝔫) parametrizes rank‑2 Drinfeld A‑modules with a cyclic 𝔫‑isogeny. These curves are defined over the Hilbert class field of K and have good reduction outside 𝔫. The ℓ‑adic cohomology of Y₀(𝔫) (ℓ ≠ p) carries an action of GL₂(A𝔫) × Gal(K̄/K) and realizes the global Langlands correspondence for GL₂ over K: irreducible automorphic representations of GL₂(𝔸K) correspond to two‑dimensional ℓ‑adic Galois representations of GK. Drinfeld (1974) proved the correspondence for cuspidal automorphic representations using the cohomology of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane and the theory of shtukas. The local Langlands correspondence for GL₂(Kv) at a place v ∤ 𝔫 is realized in the nearby cycles of the special fiber of the integral model of Y₀(𝔫) at v, as shown by Carayol (1986) and later by Harris–Taylor (2001) using the method of types. The function‑field setting also admits a geometric Langlands correspondence, proved by Lafforgue (2002) for GLn using the moduli stack of shtukas. Drinfeld modular curves provide explicit models for the Shimura varieties of GL₂ over function fields and are essential for the construction of L‑functions, the proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for GL₂ over function fields (Drinfeld, 1987), and the study of special values of L‑functions via the Gross–Zagier formula in the function‑field context (Zhang, 2001).

The p‑adic Hodge Theory of Overconvergent Cohomology and the Breuil–Schneider Conjecture

Overconvergent cohomology, introduced by Emerton (2006), is the cohomology of the completed cohomology groups H̃ⁱ(Y₁(Np^∞), ℤₚ) of the tower of modular curves with full p‑power level. These groups carry a continuous action of GL₂(ℚₚ) and of the absolute Galois group Gℚ. The Breuil–Schneider conjecture (2008) predicts that the locally analytic vectors in the completed cohomology, when equipped with the natural GL₂(ℚₚ)‑action, decompose as a direct sum over automorphic representations π of GL₂(𝔸ℚ) of the tensor product πp^an ⊗ ρ(π) where πp^an is the locally analytic representation of GL₂(ℚₚ) attached to πp by the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence and ρ(π) is the associated p‑adic Galois representation. The conjecture is known in the ordinary case by work of Breuil–Schneider and in the non‑critical slope case by work of Emerton–Gee–Savitt (2015) using the theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules and the eigencurve. The proof proceeds by constructing a (φ,Γ)‑module D(ρ) over the Robba ring from the Galois representation, then applying the Colmez functor to obtain a locally analytic representation of GL₂(ℚₚ). The compatibility with the eigencurve ensures that the resulting representation matches the locally analytic vectors in completed cohomology. The Breuil–Schneider conjecture is a key ingredient in the p‑adic Langlands program for GL₂(ℚₚ) and provides a bridge between the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence and the global modularity lifting theorems of Taylor–Wiles type.

The Harris–Taylor Method and the Local Langlands Correspondence for GL(n) via Shimura Varieties

The local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over a p-adic field F was established by Harris and Taylor (2001) and independently by Henniart (2000) using fundamentally different methods. The Harris–Taylor approach realizes the correspondence in the ℓ-adic cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower, a system of rigid analytic spaces that are the local analogues of Shimura varieties. For a division algebra D of invariant 1/n over F, the Lubin–Tate space LTₙ parametrizes deformations of a formal O_F-module of height n with Drinfeld level structure. The tower {LTₙ(𝔪)}_𝔪 carries an action of GLₙ(F) × D^× × W_F, where W_F is the Weil group of F. The key geometric input is the comparison between the cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower and the cohomology of the global Shimura varieties for unitary groups that are compact at infinity and split at a chosen place v|p. By applying the Arthur–Selberg trace formula to these Shimura varieties and using the stabilization of the trace formula (the Fundamental Lemma), Harris and Taylor isolate the contribution of a given automorphic representation π and match it with the corresponding Galois representation ρ_π. The local correspondence is then extracted from the v-adic component of this global match. The method requires the construction of the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over F that is compatible with the global correspondence for unitary groups, the verification of the local-global compatibility at v, and the use of the simple trace formula to handle the endoscopic transfer. The Harris–Taylor proof also establishes the compatibility of the correspondence with L- and ε-factors, as predicted by the Langlands–Deligne local constant conjecture. A critical technical tool is the theory of types (Bushnell–Kutzko) to describe the supercuspidal representations of GLₙ(F) and their matching with irreducible representations of W_F. The correspondence is characterized by the preservation of L-functions and ε-factors of pairs, and by the compatibility with the Zelevinsky classification and the Bernstein center. The Harris–Taylor method has the advantage of providing a geometric realization of the correspondence, which later inspired the work of Scholze on perfectoid Shimura varieties and the Fargues–Fontaine curve.

The Work of Scholze on the Local Langlands Correspondence for GL(n) via Perfectoid Spaces

Peter Scholze (2013) gave a new proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over a p-adic field F using perfectoid spaces and the cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower at infinite level. The key innovation is the construction of the perfectoid Lubin–Tate space LTₙ,∞ = lim_← LTₙ(𝔪), which is a perfectoid space over the completed algebraic closure C of F. The space LTₙ,∞ carries a continuous action of GLₙ(F) × D^× × W_F, and its étale cohomology groups H^i_ét(LTₙ,∞, ℚ_ℓ) (ℓ ≠ p) are smooth admissible representations of GLₙ(F) × D^× × W_F. Scholze proves that the correspondence can be read off from the cohomology of LTₙ,∞ by using the p-adic Hodge theory of perfectoid spaces. Specifically, the pro-étale cohomology of LTₙ,∞ with coefficients in ℤ_p is computed via the primitive comparison theorem, which relates it to the de Rham cohomology of the universal cover. The Galois action on the cohomology is analyzed using the theory of (φ,Γ)-modules and the Fargues–Fontaine curve. The correspondence is then established by showing that the cohomology of LTₙ,∞ realizes the local Langlands correspondence for GLₙ(F) in a way that is compatible with the global correspondence for unitary groups, using the modularity lifting theorems of Caraiani–Scholze (2017) for Shimura varieties of Hodge type. The perfectoid approach avoids the use of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula and the stabilization of the trace formula, replacing them with the geometry of perfectoid spaces and the p-adic Hodge theory of the Fargues–Fontaine curve. The proof also establishes the compatibility of the correspondence with the local Langlands correspondence for the inner forms D^×, and with the local Jacquet–Langlands correspondence. A crucial technical ingredient is the computation of the cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower using the theory of diamonds (v-stacks on the pro-étale site of perfectoid spaces), which allows Scholze to work with the infinite-level space as a geometric object. The perfectoid method has been extended to the local Langlands correspondence for other reductive groups by Fargues–Scholze (2021) using the geometrization of the local Langlands correspondence on the Fargues–Fontaine curve.

Worked Comparison: Harris–Taylor vs. Scholze
Explain the fundamental geometric difference between the Harris–Taylor proof and the Scholze proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) over a p-adic field F. Why does Scholze's method avoid the Arthur–Selberg trace formula?
The Harris–Taylor proof uses the cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower at finite levels {LTₙ(𝔪)} and matches it with the cohomology of global Shimura varieties for unitary groups. The matching requires the Arthur–Selberg trace formula and its stabilization (the Fundamental Lemma) to isolate the contribution of a given automorphic representation and transfer it to the local side. The geometry is rigid analytic at finite level. Scholze's proof instead works with the perfectoid Lubin–Tate space LTₙ,∞ = lim_← LTₙ(𝔪) at infinite level, which is a perfectoid space. The primitive comparison theorem in p-adic Hodge theory allows the computation of its pro-étale cohomology directly, and the Galois action is analyzed via the Fargues–Fontaine curve. The correspondence is extracted from the geometry of LTₙ,∞ itself, using the modularity lifting theorems of Caraiani–Scholze for Shimura varieties of Hodge type to ensure compatibility with the global correspondence. The trace formula is avoided because the perfectoid space at infinite level carries the full GLₙ(F) × D^× × W_F-action geometrically, and the correspondence is visible in the decomposition of its cohomology without needing to stabilize a trace formula. The key replacement is the theory of diamonds and the pro-étale site, which make the infinite-level space a tractable geometric object.

The Geometric Langlands Program for GL(n): The Gaitsgory–Lurie Proof

The geometric Langlands program for GL(n) over a smooth projective curve X over an algebraically closed field k of characteristic zero was proved by Gaitsgory and Lurie (2019) in a series of papers culminating in "Weil's Conjecture for Function Fields." The conjecture states that for a reductive group G over k, the category of D-modules on the moduli stack Bun_G of G-bundles on X is equivalent to the category of quasi-coherent sheaves on the moduli stack LocSys^L G of ^L G-local systems on X, where ^L G is the Langlands dual group. For G = GL(n), ^L G = GL(n). The proof uses the framework of ∞-categories and the theory of D-modules on algebraic stacks developed by Gaitsgory–Rozenblyum. The key geometric input is the construction of the spectral action of the category QCoh(LocSysGL(n)) on the category D-mod(BunGL(n)), which is induced by the Hecke correspondences on BunGL(n). The Hecke operators act on D-mod(BunGL(n)) and generate an action of the spherical Hecke category, which is identified with Rep(GL(n)) via the geometric Satake equivalence. The spectral action is then shown to be an equivalence by proving that it is fully faithful and essentially surjective. The fully faithfulness is established using the theory of Whittaker D-modules and the fact that the Whittaker category is compactly generated by the Whittaker sheaf. The essential surjectivity is proved by showing that every object in QCoh(LocSysGL(n)) can be obtained as the spectral transform of a D-module on BunGL(n). A crucial technical tool is the use of the Ran space and the factorization homology of the curve X, which allows the reduction of the global problem to local data at points of X. The proof also uses the theory of ind-coherent sheaves and the renormalization of the Hecke operators to handle the non-properness of the Hecke correspondences. The Gaitsgory–Lurie proof is a categorification of the function-field Langlands correspondence proved by Lafforgue (2002) using the moduli stack of shtukas. The geometric Langlands correspondence for GL(n) implies the classical correspondence by taking the trace of Frobenius on the ℓ-adic realizations of the D-modules and quasi-coherent sheaves. The proof has been extended to other reductive groups by Gaitsgory–Lurie and by other authors using similar methods.

The Modularity of Elliptic Curves over Totally Real Fields: The Freitas–Le Hung–Siksek Theorem

The modularity theorem for elliptic curves over ℚ, proved by Wiles (1995), Taylor–Wiles (1995), and Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor (2001), states that every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular, i.e., corresponds to a weight-2 cuspidal newform of level equal to the conductor of the curve. The extension to totally real fields was a major open problem until the work of Freitas, Le Hung, and Siksek (2015), who proved that every elliptic curve over a real quadratic field is modular. The proof was later extended to all totally real fields by a series of works including those of Boxer, Calegari, Gee, Le Hung, Newton, Scholze, and others (2018–2021). The strategy follows the Taylor–Wiles method: one proves a modularity lifting theorem for Galois representations ρ: G_K → GL₂(ℤ_p) that are geometric (de Rham, Hodge–Tate weights {0,1}) and satisfy certain local conditions at places above p. The key new ingredient is the construction of suitable Shimura varieties for unitary groups over totally real fields that are compact at infinity and split at the places above p. These Shimura varieties are of Hodge type and their cohomology realizes the automorphic forms on the unitary group. The modularity lifting theorem is then proved by patching the deformation rings of ρ against the Hecke algebras acting on the cohomology of these Shimura varieties. The patching argument requires the vanishing of certain cohomology groups (the "Taylor–Wiles primes" condition) and the existence of enough auxiliary primes to make the deformation ring a complete intersection. The work of Caraiani–Scholze (2017) on the cohomology of Shimura varieties of Hodge type using perfectoid spaces provides the necessary local-global compatibility at p. The final step uses the potential modularity theorems of Barnet-Lamb–Gee–Geraghty–Taylor (2011) to reduce to the case where the residual representation is modular, and then the modularity lifting theorem gives the full modularity. The theorem implies the asymptotic Fermat's Last Theorem over totally real fields: for any totally real field K, there exists a bound B_K such that the equation x^n + y^n = z^n has no non-trivial solutions in K for n > B_K. The proof also establishes the Sato–Tate conjecture for elliptic curves over totally real fields, by combining the modularity with the work of Barnet-Lamb–Geraghty–Harris–Taylor (2011) on the Sato–Tate conjecture for Hilbert modular forms.

The p-adic Local Langlands Correspondence for GL(2) over Imaginary Quadratic Fields

The p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GL(2) over ℚ_p, established by Colmez (2010) and Berger (2010), classifies 2-dimensional p-adic Galois representations of Gℚ_p in terms of locally analytic representations of GL₂(ℚ_p) on p-adic Banach spaces. The extension to GL(2) over an imaginary quadratic field K, where p splits as p = 𝔭𝔭̄, was developed by Emerton, Breuil, Hellmann–Schraen, and others (2015–2020). In this setting, the local field is K_𝔭 ≅ ℚ_p, but the global context involves the definite unitary group U(2) over ℚ that is split at p and compact at infinity. The completed cohomology of the tower of Shimura curves for U(2) at 𝔭-power level carries an action of GL₂(ℚ_p) × G_ℚ, and the p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚ_p) is realized in the locally analytic vectors of this completed cohomology. The correspondence for GL₂(K_𝔭) is then deduced by base change from the correspondence for GL₂(ℚ_p) using the theory of (φ,Γ)-modules over the Robba ring for K_𝔭. A key difference from the ℚ_p case is that the Galois group GK_𝔭 is a subgroup of Gℚ_p of index 2, and the (φ,Γ)-modules are over the Robba ring for K_𝔭, which is a 2-dimensional Lubin–Tate extension. The Colmez functor is replaced by a functor from (φ,Γ)-modules over the Robba ring for K_𝔭 to locally analytic representations of GL₂(K_𝔭), constructed using the theory of (φ,Γ)-modules for Lubin–Tate extensions. The Breuil–Schneider conjecture for GL₂(K_𝔭) predicts that the locally analytic vectors in the completed cohomology of the Shimura curves for U(2) decompose as a direct sum over automorphic representations π of U(2) of π_𝔭^an ⊗ ρ(π), where π_𝔭^an is the locally analytic representation attached to π_𝔭 by the p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GL₂(K_𝔭). This conjecture is known in the ordinary case by work of Hellmann–Schraen (2016) and in the non-critical slope case by work of Breuil–Herzig–Schraen (2018) using the theory of eigenvarieties for U(2). The correspondence is also related to the p-adic Langlands program for GL₂ over ℚ_p via the base change map, which sends a 2-dimensional p-adic representation of Gℚ_p to its restriction to GK_𝔭. The compatibility of the correspondences under base change is a deep result that uses the geometry of the eigencurve for U(2) and the theory of p-adic families of Galois representations.

The Langlands Program for Unitary Groups: The Work of Mok and Kaletha–Mínguez–Shin–White

The endoscopic classification of automorphic representations for unitary groups was achieved by Mok (2015) for quasi-split unitary groups in n variables over a CM field, and by Kaletha, Mínguez, Shin, and White (2020) for general connected reductive groups over local and global fields. The unitary group case is the most accessible instance of the general endoscopic classification, which describes the discrete automorphic spectrum of a reductive group G in terms of the stable trace formula and the endoscopic groups of G. For a unitary group U(n) associated to a Hermitian space over a CM extension E/F, the endoscopic groups are products of unitary groups of smaller rank. The classification states that the discrete automorphic representations of U(n) are partitioned into packets, each associated to an elliptic endoscopic datum (H, s, ^L η) where H is an endoscopic group, s is a semisimple element in the dual group ^L U(n) = GL(n, ℂ) ⋊ Gal(E/F), and ^L η: ^L H → ^L U(n) is an L-embedding. The packets are characterized by the equality of stable characters on H with the unstable characters on U(n) twisted by the transfer factors. The proof uses the stabilization of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula for U(n), which requires the Fundamental Lemma for unitary groups (proved by Ngô (2010) for the Lie algebra version and by Chaudouard–Laumon (2016) for the group version) and the weighted Fundamental Lemma (proved by Chaudouard (2014)). The local Langlands correspondence for unitary groups is established by Mok using the base change from unitary groups to GL(n) over the CM field E, combined with the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) (Harris–Taylor, Henniart). The global correspondence is then obtained by matching the stable trace formula for U(n) with the twisted trace formula for GL(n) over E. The Kaletha–Mínguez–Shin–White work extends this to general reductive groups by developing the theory of rigid inner twists and the local Langlands correspondence for pure inner forms, using the geometry of the affine Grassmannian and the categorical geometric Langlands program. The classification has applications to the modularity of Galois representations, the construction of L-functions, and the proof of the Ramanujan conjecture for unitary groups (via the work of Shin (2011) and others). The endoscopic classification for unitary groups is also a key input for the proof of the modularity of elliptic curves over totally real fields, as the Shimura varieties used in the Freitas–Le Hung–Siksek proof are associated to unitary groups.

The Endoscopic Classification for Classical Groups: Arthur's Work

James Arthur's endoscopic classification of the discrete automorphic spectrum for classical groups (symplectic, orthogonal, and unitary groups) over number fields, completed in his 2013 monograph The Endoscopic Classification of Representations: Orthogonal and Symplectic Groups, represents a comprehensive instance of the general Langlands functoriality principle beyond GL(n). The classification describes the discrete automorphic representations of a quasi-split classical group G in terms of the stable trace formula and the endoscopic groups of G. For a classical group G, the endoscopic groups are products of smaller classical groups and general linear groups. The discrete automorphic representations of G are partitioned into global Arthur packets, each associated to a global Arthur parameter ψ: L_F × SL(2, ℂ) → ^L G, where L_F is the conjectural Langlands group of F and ^L G is the L-group of G. The parameter ψ decomposes as a direct sum of cuspidal automorphic representations of GL(n_i) over F (or its quadratic extension for unitary groups) with multiplicities given by the SL(2) factor. The classification states that the multiplicity of an irreducible automorphic representation π in the discrete spectrum is given by the number of self-dual Arthur parameters ψ such that π belongs to the packet Π_ψ, weighted by a character of the component group S_ψ = Cent(ψ, ^L G)/Z(^L G)^Γ. The proof requires the stabilization of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula for G, which depends on the Fundamental Lemma for the relevant endoscopic groups (proved by Ngô (2010) for Lie algebras and by Chaudouard–Laumon (2016) for groups) and the weighted Fundamental Lemma (Chaudouard (2014)). Arthur also establishes the local Langlands correspondence for classical groups over p-adic fields by realizing them as local components of global automorphic representations, using the twisted trace formula for GL(n) and base change. The classification has profound applications: it establishes the Ramanujan conjecture for the discrete spectrum of classical groups (via the work of Arthur and Clozel), it provides a precise description of the discrete spectrum of Shimura varieties for classical groups, and it underpins the construction of Galois representations attached to automorphic forms on classical groups via the work of Shin, Caraiani, and others. The endoscopic classification for classical groups also informs work on the modularity of abelian varieties of GL(2)-type and the paramodular conjecture.

The p-adic Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture and Perrin-Riou's Theory

The p-adic Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, formulated by Mazur, Tate, and Teitelbaum (1986) and developed by Perrin-Riou (1990s), is the p-adic analogue of the classical BSD conjecture for an elliptic curve E over a number field F. It relates the order of vanishing and the leading coefficient of the p-adic L-function L_p(E, s) at s = 1 to the rank and the p-adic regulator of the Mordell-Weil group E(F) ⊗ ℤ_p, the order of the p-primary part of the Tate–Shafarevich group Ш(E/F)[p^∞], and the product of Tamagawa numbers and the p-adic period. The p-adic L-function L_p(E, s) is constructed by interpolating the critical values of the complex L-function L(E, χ, 1) as χ varies over p-power order characters of Gal(F_∞/F), where F_∞ is the cyclotomic ℤ_p-extension of F. For an elliptic curve E over ℚ with good ordinary reduction at p, the p-adic L-function is an element of the Iwasawa algebra Λ = ℤ_p[[Gal(ℚ_∞/ℚ)]] ≅ ℤ_p[[T]], and the conjecture predicts that char_Λ(Sel_p(E/ℚ_∞)^∨) = (L_p(E, T)) as ideals in Λ, where Sel_p(E/ℚ_∞) is the p-Selmer group over the cyclotomic extension and ^∨ denotes the Pontryagin dual. This is the Iwasawa Main Conjecture for elliptic curves, proved by Kato (2004) for the divisibility char_Λ(Sel_p(E/ℚ_∞)^∨) | (L_p(E, T)) and by Skinner–Urban (2014) for the reverse divisibility under certain hypotheses. Perrin-Riou's theory of p-adic heights and p-adic regulators provides a systematic framework for the p-adic BSD formula. She constructs a p-adic height pairing h_p: E(F) × E(F) → ℚ_p using the theory of (φ, Γ)-modules and the exponential map of Bloch–Kato, and shows that the p-adic regulator Reg_p(E/F) = det(h_p(P_i, P_j)) for a basis P_i of E(F)/torsion appears in the leading term formula. The p-adic BSD conjecture also involves the p-adic period Ω_p, which is the p-adic analogue of the Néron period, and the p-adic Tamagawa factors c_p = [E(ℚ_p) : E0(ℚ_p)]_p. The conjecture has been verified in many cases of rank 0 and 1 by work of Bertolini–Darmon, Pollack–Rubin, and others, using the theory of Heegner points and Kolyvagin systems. The p-adic BSD conjecture for higher rank remains largely open, but the work of Nekovář, Skinner–Urban, and Wan on the Iwasawa Main Conjecture for modular forms provides strong evidence.

Worked Example: p-adic L-function for an Ordinary Elliptic Curve
Let E/ℚ be the elliptic curve y² = x³ - x (Cremona label 32a1) with good ordinary reduction at p = 3. The cyclotomic ℤ3-extension ℚ_∞/ℚ has Galois group Γ ≅ ℤ3. The p-adic L-function L_p(E, T) ∈ ℤ3[[T]] is constructed by Mazur–Tate–Teitelbaum. Given that L(E, 1) = 0.655... ≠ 0 (rank 0), the p-adic BSD conjecture predicts L_p(E, 0) = (1 - a3/3 + 1/3)² · Ω_p · #Ш(E/ℚ)[3^∞] / (#E(ℚ)_tors)², where a3 = 0 for this curve. Compute the Euler factor at p = 3 and explain its role in the interpolation formula.
For E: y² = x³ - x, a3 = 0 because #E(𝔽3) = 4 = 3 + 1 - a3. The Euler factor at p = 3 for the complex L-function is (1 - a3·3-s + 31-2s)-1 = (1 + 31-2s)-1. At s = 1 this is (1 + 1/3)-1 = 3/4. The p-adic L-function interpolates the algebraic parts L(E, χ, 1)/Ω_E^±. The interpolation formula for the p-adic L-function L_p(E, T) at a finite-order character χ of Γ of conductor 3^n is L_p(E, χ(γ)-1) = ε(χ) · (1 - χ(p)a_p/p + χ(p)²/p) · L(E, χ-1, 1)/Ω_Esign(χ), where γ is a topological generator of Γ. For the trivial character χ = 1, the Euler factor is (1 - a3/3 + 1/3) = (1 - 0 + 1/3) = 4/3. This factor is the reciprocal of the complex Euler factor at s = 1, reflecting the removal of the p-adic Euler factor in the p-adic L-function. The p-adic BSD formula at s = 1 (T = 0) becomes L_p(E, 0) = (4/3)² · Ω_p · #Ш[3^∞] / 4², since #E(ℚ)_tors = 4. The factor (4/3)² is the square of the p-adic Euler factor, which appears because the p-adic height pairing involves the p-adic logarithm and the p-adic regulator.
Exam Strategy: Distinguishing p-adic BSD from Classical BSD
In questions comparing the classical and p-adic BSD conjectures, focus on three structural differences: (1) The classical BSD uses the complex L-function L(E, s) and the Néron period Ω_E; the p-adic BSD uses the p-adic L-function L_p(E, T) ∈ Λ and the p-adic period Ω_p. (2) The classical regulator uses the canonical height pairing over ℝ; the p-adic regulator uses Perrin-Riou's p-adic height pairing over ℚ_p. (3) The classical formula involves the full Tate–Shafarevich group Ш(E/ℚ); the p-adic formula involves only its p-primary part Ш(E/ℚ)[p^∞]. The Iwasawa Main Conjecture char_Λ(Sel_p(E/ℚ_∞)^∨) = (L_p(E, T)) is the p-adic analogue of the leading term formula, not the full BSD formula. Remember that the p-adic L-function is an element of the Iwasawa algebra, not a complex-analytic function.

The Langlands-Shahidi Method and Functoriality for Classical Groups

The Langlands-Shahidi method, developed by Freydoon Shahidi in a series of papers from the 1970s through the 1990s, offers an analytic approach to the Langlands functoriality principle for classical groups by studying the analytic properties of Langlands L-functions L(s, π, r) attached to an automorphic representation π of a classical group G and a representation r of the L-group ^L G. The method realizes these L-functions as normalizing factors of intertwining operators induced from a maximal parabolic subgroup P = MN of a larger quasi-split group G' containing G as a Levi component M. Specifically, for a classical group G (symplectic, orthogonal, or unitary), one embeds G as the Levi factor of a maximal parabolic subgroup of a larger classical group G' of the same type but higher rank. The induced representation Ind_PG'(π ⊗ |det|^s) has an intertwining operator A(s, π) whose normalizing factor is precisely the Langlands L-function L(s, π, r) for a specific representation r of ^L G. The analytic properties of L(s, π, r) — meromorphic continuation, functional equation, and non-vanishing on Re(s) = 1 — are then deduced from the corresponding properties of the intertwining operators, which are established using the theory of Eisenstein series and the Plancherel formula for G'. This method establishes the functorial lift from a classical group G to GL(N) for the standard representation r = std of ^L G, where N = 2n+1 for SO(2n+1), N = 2n for Sp(2n) and SO(2n), and N = n for U(n). The functorial lift is constructed as the unique irreducible automorphic representation Π of GL(N) whose local components Π_v are the Langlands quotients of the induced representations at each place v. The method also establishes the local Langlands correspondence for generic representations of classical groups over p-adic fields by identifying the local L-factors and ε-factors with those of the lifted representation on GL(N). The Langlands-Shahidi method was instrumental in the proof of the endoscopic classification for classical groups by Arthur, who used the functorial lifts to GL(N) to stabilize the trace formula. It also underpins the analytic proof of the Ramanujan conjecture for classical groups (proved by Arthur for the discrete spectrum and by Cogdell–Kim–Piatetski-Shapiro–Shahidi for generic representations). The method extends to non-maximal parabolic subgroups and to exceptional groups, giving functorial lifts for the half-spin representations of Spin(2n) and the standard representation of G2.

The Arithmetic of Modular Curves: Heegner Points, Gross-Zagier, and Kolyvagin

The arithmetic of modular curves X0(N) and X1(N) provides effective tools for constructing rational points on elliptic curves over ℚ and for proving cases of the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture. A Heegner point on X0(N) is a point corresponding to an elliptic curve with complex multiplication by an order in an imaginary quadratic field K in which all primes dividing N split. The modular parametrization φ: X0(N) → E associated to a newform f ∈ S2(Γ0(N)) maps Heegner points to points in E(K). The Gross-Zagier formula (1986) computes the Néron-Tate height of the Heegner point y_K = φ(x_K) ∈ E(K) in terms of the derivative of the complex L-function: ⟨y_K, y_K⟩ = C · L'(E/K, 1) / Ω_E, where C is an explicit non-zero constant depending on N, the discriminant of K, and the Manin constant. This formula proves that if L(E/K, s) has a simple zero at s = 1, then the Heegner point y_K is of infinite order, so rank E(K) ≥ 1. Kolyvagin's Euler system argument (1990) uses the Galois cohomology classes derived from Heegner points over ring class fields of K to bound the Selmer group. Kolyvagin shows that if y_K ≠ 0, then rank E(K) = 1 and Ш(E/K) is finite. Combined with the work of Bump–Friedberg–Hoffstein and Murty–Murty on non-vanishing of L(E, 1) for a positive proportion of elliptic curves, this establishes the BSD conjecture for elliptic curves over ℚ of analytic rank 0 or 1, conditional on modularity (now a theorem). The theory extends to higher weight modular forms and to Hilbert modular forms over totally real fields, where the work of Zhang, Yuan–Zhang–Zhang, and others proves the Gross-Zagier formula and Kolyvagin's argument for Shimura curves associated to quaternion algebras. The Heegner point construction underpins the proof of the p-adic Gross-Zagier formula by Perrin-Riou, Bertolini–Darmon–Prasanna, and Liu–Zhang–Zhang, which relates the p-adic height of Heegner points to the derivative of the p-adic L-function. The arithmetic of modular curves is also central to the proof of the modularity of elliptic curves over ℚ (Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor), where the deformation theory of Galois representations is matched with the geometry of the eigencurve and the modular curves X_H(N) for various level structures H.

The Geometric Langlands Program for Classical Groups and the Hitchin Fibration

The geometric Langlands program for classical groups, developed by Laumon, Ngô, and others, generalizes the geometric Langlands correspondence from GL(n) to symplectic, orthogonal, and unitary groups over a smooth projective curve X over an algebraically closed field k (or ℂ). The correspondence relates D-modules on the moduli stack Bun_G of G-bundles on X to quasi-coherent sheaves on the moduli stack LocSys^L G of ^L G-local systems on X, where G is a classical group and ^L G is its Langlands dual group (SO(2n+1) Sp(2n), Sp(2n) SO(2n+1), SO(2n) SO(2n), U(n) GL(n) with Galois action). The key geometric object is the Hitchin fibration h: T^*Bun_G → A_G, where A_G = ⊕i=1^r H0(X, K_Xd_i) is the Hitchin base, with d_i the degrees of the fundamental invariants of G. The fibers of the Hitchin fibration are (possibly singular) abelian varieties — the Prym varieties of spectral curves. Ngô's proof of the Fundamental Lemma (2010) uses the geometry of the Hitchin fibration for the endoscopic groups H of G, showing that the orbital integrals on G match the stable orbital integrals on H by comparing the cohomology of the Hitchin fibers via the decomposition theorem. The geometric Langlands correspondence for classical groups is constructed using the Hecke eigensheaf property: a D-module F on Bun_G is a Hecke eigensheaf with eigenvalue a ^L G-local system E if the Hecke operators H_V (for representations V of ^L G) act on F by tensoring with the corresponding vector bundle V_E on X. A categorical equivalence for GL(n) was established by Gaitsgory–Lurie (2019) using ∞-categories and factorization homology. For classical groups, the correspondence is known for the "spectral" side via the work of Laumon (for GL(n)) and Ngô (for the Hitchin fibration), but the full categorical equivalence for classical groups remains an active area of research. The geometric Langlands program for classical groups is deeply connected to the arithmetic Langlands program via the trace formula for function fields (Lafforgue's proof for GL(n)) and the geometric Satake equivalence (Mirković–Vilonen), which identifies the category of representations of ^L G with the category of perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian Gr_G. The Hitchin fibration also plays a central role in the work of Donagi–Pantev on the geometric Langlands correspondence for abelian varieties and in the Kapustin–Witten approach via S-duality in N=4 super Yang-Mills theory.
