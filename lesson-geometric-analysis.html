Geometric Analysis — Minimal Surfaces, the Plateau Problem & the Yamabe Problem

Geometric analysis is the application of partial differential equations to problems in differential geometry. A minimal surface is a critical point of the area functional A(Σ) = ∫_Σ dA, satisfying mean curvature H = 0 everywhere. The Plateau problem — find a surface of minimal area spanning a given boundary curve — was solved independently by Douglas and Rado (1931) using the Dirichlet energy. The Bernstein conjecture stated that the only complete minimal graphs in ℝ³ are planes; Bombieri-De Giorgi-Giusti (1969) found counterexamples in ℝ⁸. Harmonic maps are energy-minimizing maps between Riemannian manifolds; the Eells-Sampson theorem (1964) guarantees existence in negative-curvature targets. The Yamabe problem — existence of a constant scalar curvature metric in a conformal class — was solved by Trudinger, Aubin, and Schoen using the conformal Laplacian and the positive mass theorem. The Ricci flow ∂g/∂t = −2 Ric(g), introduced by Hamilton and used by Perelman to prove the Poincaré conjecture, evolves metrics to improve their curvature. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem has an analytic proof via the heat kernel. The Seiberg-Witten equations and Ginzburg-Landau vortices are gauge-theoretic PDEs central to low-dimensional topology and superconductivity.

"Geometric analysis is the art of proving existence by finding minimizers."
— Richard Schoen
Part 1

📏 Minimal Surfaces and the Plateau Problem

Minimal Surfaces
The Area Functional, Mean Curvature Zero, and the Plateau Problem
A minimal surface is a critical point of the area functional A(Σ) = ∫_Σ dA. The first variation gives mean curvature H = 0 — the Euler-Lagrange equation. For a graph z = f(x,y), the minimal surface equation is (1+|∇f|²)Δf − f_i f_j f_{ij} = 0 — a quasilinear elliptic PDE. The Plateau problem: given a closed curve Γ ⊂ ℝ³, find a surface Σ of minimal area with ∂Σ = Γ. Douglas and Rado (1931) solved it by minimizing the Dirichlet energy ∫_D |∇u|² over maps u: D → ℝ³ with u|_∂D a parametrization of Γ. The solution is a branched minimal surface — conformal and harmonic in the interior. The interior regularity theorem: any two-dimensional minimizer is smooth in the interior. The boundary regularity: the surface meets Γ at right angles if Γ is smooth. The monotonicity formula: for a minimal surface Σ, the area in a ball A(Σ ∩ B_r(p)) ≥ πr², with equality iff Σ is a plane. The Bernstein conjecture: the only complete minimal graphs in ℝⁿ for n ≤ 7 are planes; for n ≥ 8, there exist counterexamples (Bombieri-De Giorgi-Giusti). The Weierstrass-Enneper representation gives explicit parametrizations of minimal surfaces in ℝ³ via holomorphic data.
Minimal surfaces balance tension everywhere; the Plateau problem shows that every closed curve bounds at least one surface of least area, a triumph of the calculus of variations.
Part 2

🌍 Harmonic Maps and the Eells-Sampson Theorem

Harmonic Maps
The Energy Functional, Tension Field, and the Heat Flow Method
A map f: M → N between Riemannian manifolds is harmonic if it is a critical point of the energy functional E(f) = ½∫_M |df|² dV_M. The Euler-Lagrange equation is τ(f) = trace ∇df = 0, where τ(f) is the tension field. In local coordinates, τᵅ(f) = Δfᵅ + Γ_{βγ}^{α}(f) ⟨∇fᵝ, ∇fᵞ⟩ = 0 — a semilinear elliptic system. The Eells-Sampson theorem (1964): if M is compact and N has nonpositive sectional curvature, then any continuous map f₀: M → N can be deformed to a harmonic map, unique in its homotopy class. The proof uses the harmonic map heat flow: ∂_t f = τ(f). The flow exists for all time and converges to a harmonic map as t → ∞. The Bochner formula: Δ|df|² = 2|∇df|² + 2⟨Ricᵐ(df), df⟩ − 2⟨Rᴺ(df, df)df, df⟩, which gives vanishing results for harmonic maps from positively curved domains into negatively curved targets. The Lichnerowicz-Obata theorem: on an Einstein manifold with Ric ≥ (n−1)g, the first nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplacian satisfies λ₁ ≥ n, with equality iff M is the sphere. Uhlenbeck's analysis of the Yang-Mills equations uses similar gauge-fixing and compactness methods to study critical points of the Yang-Mills functional ∫ |F_A|².
Harmonic maps generalize geodesics to higher dimensions; the heat flow method deforms any map into an energy minimizer when the target is negatively curved.
Part 3

🧮 The Yamabe Problem

Yamabe
Conformal Metrics, the Yamabe Equation, and the Positive Mass Theorem
The Yamabe problem: given a compact Riemannian manifold (M, g), find a metric ̃g = u^{4/(n−2)}g conformal to g with constant scalar curvature R_{̃g} = constant. Under conformal change, the scalar curvature transforms via the conformal Laplacian L_g = Δ_g + (n−2)/(4(n−1))R_g, giving the Yamabe equation: L_g u = (n−2)/(4(n−1)) c u^{(n+2)/(n−2)}. Equivalently, one minimizes the Yamabe functional Q_g(u) = ∫ (|∇u|² + (n−2)/(4(n−1)) R_g u²) dV_g / (∫ u^{2n/(n−2)} dV_g)^{(n−2)/n}. The Yamabe constant Y(M, [g]) = inf_{u>0} Q_g(u). Trudinger (1968) proved existence when Q_g ≥ 0; Aubin (1976) showed Y(M, [g]) ≤ Y(Sⁿ) with equality iff M is conformal to the sphere, and proved existence for all non-conformal-to-sphere metrics; Schoen (1984) handled the remaining case using the positive mass theorem (Schoen-Yau, Witten) to prove strict inequality Y(M, [g]) < Y(Sⁿ) for non-conformal metrics. The solution gives a minimizer u > 0, smooth, producing a constant scalar curvature metric. The Obata theorem: the only conformal metrics on Sⁿ with constant scalar curvature are the round metric up to Möbius transformations.
The Yamabe problem is the premier example of solving a geometric PDE via variational methods; the positive mass theorem provides the crucial strict inequality for existence.
Part 4

🔥 Ricci Flow and the Poincaré Conjecture

Ricci Flow
Hamilton's Program, Singularity Formation, and Perelman's Proof
The Ricci flow ∂g/∂t = −2 Ric(g) evolves a Riemannian metric to smooth out curvature. The normalized Ricci flow preserves volume. Hamilton's theorem (1982): on a closed 3-manifold with positive Ricci curvature, the Ricci flow converges to a constant positive curvature metric — the manifold is diffeomorphic to a spherical space form. Singularities form when curvature blows up: the neck-pinch (a cylindrical neck collapses) and the cigar soliton (a self-similar shrinking solution). Perelman's three preprints (2002-2003): the L-geodesic action and the reduced volume monotonicity; the no-local-collapsing theorem — curvature cannot concentrate without volume also collapsing; the canonical neighborhood theorem — near a singularity, the geometry is modeled on a standard solution. Ricci flow with surgery: when curvature blows up, cut out the singular region and glue in a cap, then continue the flow. Perelman showed only finitely many surgeries occur. The Poincaré conjecture: a simply connected closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to S³. The argument: run the Ricci flow with surgery; after finite time, all components become positively curved and shrink to points; by the Geometrization conjecture, the original manifold is a connected sum of a sphere with itself, i.e., a sphere. The Geometrization conjecture (Thurston) states every closed 3-manifold decomposes into pieces each admitting one of eight geometric structures.
The Ricci flow turns the topological problem of classifying 3-manifolds into an analytic problem about PDEs; Perelman's resolution of the Poincaré conjecture is one of the greatest achievements of geometric analysis.

Regularity and Singular Sets in Minimal Surface Theory

The regularity theory for area‑minimizing integral currents is a cornerstone of geometric measure theory. Allard’s regularity theorem (1972) states that if an integral varifold has density close to one at a point, then the varifold is a smooth embedded submanifold near that point. For codimension‑one area‑minimizing currents, Simons’ inequality (1968) yields that the singular set has Hausdorff dimension at most n−2. In dimensions n≤7 this forces the singular set to be empty, so every area‑minimizing hypersurface is smooth. The first counterexample in higher dimensions is the Simons cone over S³×S³ in ℝ⁸, which is area‑minimizing but singular at the origin. Almgren’s monumental big regularity paper (2000) proved that for any area‑minimizing integral current of arbitrary dimension and codimension, the singular set has Hausdorff dimension at most m−2, where m is the dimension of the current. Later, De Lellis and Spadaro (2014) gave a more accessible proof using a quantitative stratification argument. For stationary varifolds with bounded mean curvature, the singular set can be larger; the optimal dimension bound remains an active research area. The regularity theory also underpins the compactness theorems used in min‑max constructions: a sequence of minimal hypersurfaces with uniform area and genus bounds subconverges to a smooth minimal hypersurface away from a singular set of codimension at least seven.

Min‑Max Methods and the Almgren‑Pitts Theory

The Almgren‑Pitts min‑max theory produces minimal hypersurfaces by minimizing the maximal area among sweepouts of a manifold. Given a compact Riemannian manifold (Mⁿ⁺¹,g), a sweepout is a continuous map Φ:[0,1]→𝒵ₙ(M;ℤ₂) into the space of integral cycles mod 2. The width of the sweepout is supₜ area(Φ(t)). The min‑max width is the infimum of widths over all sweepouts representing a nontrivial homotopy class. Almgren (1965) and Pitts (1981) proved that the min‑max width is realized by a stationary integral varifold which is smooth away from a singular set of codimension at least seven. Marques and Neves (2014) extended this to produce infinitely many distinct minimal hypersurfaces when Ric>0, establishing a Weyl law for the volume spectrum: the k‑th width grows like k¹/(ⁿ⁺¹). Their work also resolved the Willmore conjecture (2012) by applying min‑max to the Clifford torus in S³. A more recent discrete min‑max framework by Colding and De Lellis (2003) replaces continuous sweepouts with sequences of finite‑dimensional simplicial complexes, simplifying the compactness arguments. The min‑max construction is now a standard tool for proving existence of closed minimal submanifolds in generic metrics and for studying the topology of the space of metrics.

Min‑max width computation
Explain how the min‑max width of the round three‑sphere S³ relates to the area of an equatorial 2‑sphere.
The round metric on S³ has constant sectional curvature 1. The equatorial 2‑sphere S²⊂S³ is a totally geodesic minimal hypersurface with area 4π. Any sweepout of S³ by 2‑cycles must pass through a cycle of area at least 4π because the homotopy class of the sweepout is nontrivial in π₃(𝒵₂(S³))≅ℤ. Almgren‑Pitts theory shows the min‑max width equals the area of the least‑area nontrivial minimal hypersurface, which is exactly the equatorial S². Hence the width is 4π.

Bubbling and Compactness for Harmonic Maps

When a sequence of harmonic maps uₖ:M→N has uniformly bounded energy, the lack of compactness is captured by the bubbling phenomenon. Sacks and Uhlenbeck (1981) introduced the perturbed energy Eₚ(u)=∫(1+|du|²)ᵖ to obtain smooth minimizers for p>1; as p→1 the minimizers converge to a harmonic map away from a finite set of points. At each blow‑up point a nontrivial harmonic sphere (a “bubble”) appears, and the total energy splits as the energy of the weak limit plus the sum of energies of the bubbles. Struwe (1985) proved the energy identity for the harmonic map heat flow: if u(t) develops a singularity at time T, then after rescaling around the singular point one extracts a harmonic sphere, and the energy lost equals the energy of the bubble. The bubble tree convergence theorem (Parker 1996, Ding‑Tian 1995) describes the full limit as a finite collection of harmonic spheres connected by neck regions whose energy tends to zero. Quantization of energy follows: there exists ε₀>0 depending only on N such that any nonconstant harmonic sphere has energy at least ε₀. This quantization is crucial for proving that the number of bubbles is finite. For the heat flow, the singular set is discrete in space‑time, and the flow can be continued past singularities by a surgery procedure analogous to the one used in Ricci flow. The analysis of neck regions uses the Pohozaev identity and the monotonicity formula for the stress‑energy tensor.

The Positive Mass Theorem and Its Role in the Yamabe Problem

The positive mass theorem (Schoen‑Yau 1979, Witten 1981) asserts that an asymptotically flat manifold of nonnegative scalar curvature has nonnegative ADM mass, with equality only for Euclidean space. Schoen (1984) used this theorem to complete the solution of the Yamabe problem. After Trudinger (1968) and Aubin (1976) handled the cases where the Yamabe invariant Y(M,[g]) is nonpositive or strictly less than the invariant of the sphere Y(Sⁿ), the remaining case was Y(M,[g])=Y(Sⁿ) but (M,g) not conformal to the sphere. Schoen considered the Green’s function G of the conformal Laplacian L_g with pole at a point p. The metric ̃g=G4/(n−2)g is asymptotically flat and has zero scalar curvature. Its ADM mass m is proportional to the constant term in the expansion of G. If m>0, a conformal deformation supported near infinity produces a metric with positive scalar curvature and Yamabe invariant strictly less than Y(Sⁿ), contradicting the assumption. The positive mass theorem forces m≥0; the rigidity statement then implies (M,g) is conformal to the sphere. Witten’s spinor proof (1981) gave a more general argument valid for spin manifolds, while Schoen‑Yau’s minimal hypersurface proof works in all dimensions ≤7 and was later extended to all dimensions by Lohkamp (1999). The theorem also underlies the proof of the Riemannian Penrose inequality and the stability of the positive mass theorem under perturbations.

Perelman’s Entropy, Kappa‑Solutions, and the Surgery Procedure

Perelman introduced the ℱ‑functional ℱ(g,f)=∫(R+|∇f|²)e−fdV and the 𝒲‑functional 𝒲(g,f,τ)=∫[τ(R+|∇f|²)+f−n](4πτ)−n/2e−fdV. The 𝒲‑entropy is monotone nondecreasing along the Ricci flow and is constant exactly on gradient shrinking solitons. The reduced volume Ṽ(τ)=∫(4πτ)−n/2e−ldV, where l is the reduced distance, is also monotone. These monotonicities imply the no‑local‑collapsing theorem: if |Rm|≤r−2 on a parabolic ball P(x,t,r,−r²), then the volume of the ball is at least κrⁿ for a universal κ>0. A κ‑solution is an ancient, complete, non‑flat solution with bounded nonnegative curvature operator that is κ‑noncollapsed at all scales. In three dimensions, Perelman classified κ‑solutions: they are either the shrinking round sphere S³, the shrinking round cylinder S²×ℝ (or its ℤ₂ quotient), or the Bryant soliton (a rotationally symmetric steady soliton). The canonical neighborhood theorem states that any point of high curvature in a Ricci flow with surgery lies in a region modeled on one of these κ‑solutions. Surgery is performed by cutting off ε‑necks (regions close to S²×I) and gluing in standard caps. Perelman proved that only finitely many surgeries occur in any finite time interval, and that the flow becomes extinct in finite time for simply connected manifolds. The extinction time is bounded by a constant times the initial volume. This finite extinction, together with the topological classification of the components at extinction, yields the Poincaré conjecture and the full geometrization conjecture.

Allard’s Regularity Theorem and Rectifiability of Varifolds

Allard’s regularity theorem (1972) gives a precise criterion for when an integral varifold with bounded first variation is locally a smooth embedded submanifold. The theorem states that if an integral n‑varifold V in a Riemannian manifold has generalized mean curvature H in L^p for some p > n, and if the density Θ^n(V,x) is close to 1 at a point x, then V is represented near x by a C1,α graph over the tangent plane. The proof combines the monotonicity formula for the mass ratio with a blow‑up argument: rescaling V around x yields a tangent varifold that is a stationary cone; the density hypothesis forces this cone to be a multiplicity‑one plane, and Allard’s ε‑regularity lemma then upgrades the graphical approximation to C1,α regularity. A corollary is the rectifiability theorem: any integral varifold with locally bounded first variation is countably n‑rectifiable, and its approximate tangent planes coincide ℋ^n‑almost everywhere with the tangent planes of the representing rectifiable set. This result underpins the compactness theory for integral currents and varifolds, because it guarantees that limits of sequences with uniform mass and first‑variation bounds retain enough regular structure to apply geometric measure theory tools. In the context of the Plateau problem, Allard’s theorem is used to show that an area‑minimizing integral current is a smooth embedded hypersurface away from a singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most n−7, a bound that is sharp by the Simons cone example. The theorem also plays a role in the regularity of solutions to the prescribed mean curvature equation and in the study of varifold limits of minimal surfaces with bounded index.

Stability and Morse Index of Minimal Hypersurfaces

The stability operator for a minimal hypersurface Σ in a Riemannian manifold (M,g) is the Jacobi operator L = Δ_Σ + |A|2 + Ric(ν,ν), where Δ_Σ is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on Σ, A is the second fundamental form, and ν is a unit normal. A minimal hypersurface is called stable if the second variation of area is nonnegative for all compactly supported normal variations, which is equivalent to the condition that the first eigenvalue λ1(L) ≥ 0. The Morse index of Σ is the number of negative eigenvalues of L (counted with multiplicity) on a compact domain with Dirichlet boundary conditions. For complete non‑compact minimal hypersurfaces, stability has strong topological consequences: in manifolds of nonnegative Ricci curvature, a stable complete minimal hypersurface must be totally geodesic (Fischer‑Colbrie–Schoen 1980). In Euclidean space, the only complete stable minimal hypersurfaces are hyperplanes (do Carmo–Peng 1979). The index provides a quantitative measure of instability; for example, the catenoid in ℝ3 has index 1, while the Clifford torus in S3 has index 5. Sharp index bounds have been obtained for minimal hypersurfaces in spheres and in manifolds with positive Ricci curvature, often using the stability inequality together with test functions built from coordinate functions or eigenfunctions of the Laplacian. The relationship between index and topology is a central theme: a theorem of Sharp (2017) shows that a closed minimal hypersurface in Sn+1 with index ≤ n+3 is either an equatorial sphere or a Clifford torus. In the Almgren–Pitts min‑max construction, the produced minimal hypersurface has index bounded by the number of parameters in the sweepout, and this bound is used to control the singular set via the work of Marques–Neves and later improvements by Zhou and others.

Worked Example: Computing the Morse Index of the Catenoid
Consider the catenoid Σ ⊂ ℝ3 given by x2 + y2 = cosh2(z). Show that its Morse index is 1.
The catenoid is a complete minimal surface with two ends asymptotic to planes. Its Jacobi operator is L = Δ_Σ + |A|2. Separation of variables using the rotational symmetry reduces the eigenvalue problem to an ODE on each meridian. The explicit Jacobi fields generated by the ambient Killing fields (translations and rotations) give three independent solutions: one from vertical translation (odd in z) and two from horizontal translations (even in z). The vertical translation field changes sign exactly once, producing a negative eigenvalue; the horizontal fields are positive on Σ and correspond to zero eigenvalues (they arise from the non‑compactness of the isometry group). By the Courant nodal domain theorem, the number of negative eigenvalues equals the number of nodal domains of the first eigenfunction, which is 1. Hence the Morse index of the catenoid is 1.

When a question asks for the Morse index of a classical minimal surface, recall the standard list: plane (index 0), catenoid (1), Enneper surface (∞), Clifford torus in S3 (5), Lawson surfaces ξg,1 (index 2g+3). If the problem gives a stability inequality ∫_Σ (|∇f|2 − (|A|2+Ric(ν,ν))f2) ≥ 0, test f = 1 to see whether the surface can be stable; a negative integral of |A|2+Ric(ν,ν) forces instability and gives a lower bound on the index.

The Yamabe Flow and Convergence to Constant Scalar Curvature Metrics

The Yamabe flow, introduced by Hamilton (1989), evolves a metric g(t) within a fixed conformal class by ∂_t g = −R_g g, where R_g is the scalar curvature. Equivalently, writing g(t) = u(t)4/(n−2) g0, the conformal factor satisfies the nonlinear parabolic equation ∂_t u = −(n−2)/(4(n−1)) (Δg0 u − c_n Rg0 u + c_n Rg(t) u(n+2)/(n−2)). The flow preserves the conformal class and decreases the Yamabe functional (the total scalar curvature normalized by volume). Ye (1994) proved that on any compact manifold of dimension n ≥ 3, the Yamabe flow exists for all time and converges smoothly to a metric of constant scalar curvature, provided the initial metric has positive Yamabe invariant. The proof uses the maximum principle to obtain uniform bounds on the conformal factor and its derivatives, together with a monotonicity formula for the Yamabe energy. When the Yamabe invariant is nonpositive, the flow still converges after a suitable normalization (e.g., fixing volume). In the locally conformally flat case, Chow (1992) gave an alternative proof using the Liouville theorem for the limiting equation. The flow also provides a dynamical approach to the Yamabe problem: the limit metric solves the Yamabe equation and realizes the Yamabe invariant of the conformal class. Recent work by Brendle (2007) extended convergence to the case of manifolds with boundary under the Neumann condition, and by Schwetlick–Struwe (2003) to the singular Yamabe flow where the conformal factor may develop bubbles; the bubbling analysis mirrors that for harmonic maps and uses the classification of entire solutions of the critical Sobolev equation on ℝ^n.

Cheeger‑Colding Theory and Ricci Limit Spaces

Cheeger and Colding (1996‑1997) developed a structure theory for Gromov–Hausdorff limits of Riemannian manifolds with a uniform lower bound on Ricci curvature. A Ricci limit space is a metric measure space (X,d,ℋ^n) that arises as the limit of a sequence (M_i,g_i,p_i) with Ricg_i ≥ −(n−1) and vol(B(p_i,1)) ≥ v > 0. The fundamental results are: (1) the limit space is a rectifiable metric space, and its regular set ℛ consists of points where every tangent cone is isometric to ℝ^n; ℛ is open and dense, and on ℛ the distance comes from a C1,α Riemannian metric. (2) The singular set 𝒮 = X ℛ has Hausdorff dimension at most n−2, and it can be stratified by the maximal number of Euclidean factors in tangent cones: 𝒮_k = {x : no tangent cone splits off ℝk+1}; then dim_ℋ 𝒮_k ≤ k. (3) The Bishop–Gromov volume comparison holds in the limit, and the measure is the n‑dimensional Hausdorff measure. (4) A segment inequality and an almost‑splitting theorem give quantitative control of how close a ball is to a product with a line. These tools were used by Colding–Naber (2012) to prove that the singular set of a non‑collapsed Ricci limit has codimension at least 2, and by Cheeger–Jiang–Naber (2015) to establish rectifiability of the singular strata. The theory also underpins the recent proof of the Minkowski conjecture for limit spaces and the development of calculus on RCD(K,N) spaces, where the Ricci lower bound is encoded in the convexity of the entropy functional along Wasserstein geodesics.

Mean Curvature Flow: Singularities, Surgery, and the Level Set Method

Mean curvature flow (MCF) evolves a hypersurface Σ_t in a Riemannian manifold by moving each point with velocity equal to its mean curvature vector H. In Euclidean space the flow is the gradient flow of the area functional. Huisken (1984) proved that a smooth, closed, convex hypersurface remains smooth and shrinks to a round point in finite time, with the rescaled flow converging to a sphere. For non‑convex initial data singularities inevitably form. The first singular time T is characterized by the blow‑up of the second fundamental form |A|. Huisken and Sinestrari (1999) developed a surgery procedure for two‑convex hypersurfaces (λ₁+λ₂>0) that removes neck‑like regions modeled on the shrinking cylinder Sn-1×ℝ and continues the flow past singularities. Their canonical neighborhood theorem shows that every high‑curvature region is either a neck or a cap, enabling a well‑defined, piecewise smooth flow with finitely many surgeries on any finite time interval. Independently, the level‑set formulation of Evans–Spruck (1991) and Chen–Giga–Goto (1991) represents the evolving hypersurface as the zero level set of a viscosity solution u to ∂_t u = |∇u| div(∇u/|∇u|). This weak formulation handles topological changes automatically and provides a unique global evolution for any compact initial set. The two approaches are complementary: the level‑set flow gives a global weak solution, while the surgery flow yields a smooth, canonical evolution for mean‑convex (or two‑convex) initial data. Recent work by Haslhofer–Kleiner (2017) and by Brendle–Huisken (2017) extended the surgery program to higher codimension and to flows with forcing terms, and established a fine stratification of the singular set using White’s regularity theory and the monotonicity formula of Huisken.

Willmore Energy, the Willmore Conjecture, and the Marques–Neves Proof

The Willmore functional W(Σ)=∫_Σ H² dμ measures the bending energy of an immersed surface Σ in ℝ³ (or in S³ via stereographic projection). It is invariant under conformal transformations of the ambient space. The Willmore conjecture, posed in 1965, asserted that for any immersed torus Σ⊂S³ one has W(Σ)≥2π², with equality only for the Clifford torus S¹(1/√2)×S¹(1/√2). The problem resisted attack for decades because the functional is critical with respect to the conformal group, leading to loss of compactness. Marques and Neves (2012) resolved the conjecture by combining Almgren–Pitts min‑max theory with a careful analysis of the conformal volume. They defined the p‑width ω_p(M) of a Riemannian 3‑manifold M as the min‑max value of the area functional over p‑parameter sweepouts. For the round 3‑sphere they computed ω_p(S³) explicitly using the equivariant min‑max theorem of Almgren and the classification of minimal surfaces in S³ by Lawson. The key step was to show that the first non‑trivial width ω1(S³) equals 2π² and is realized by the Clifford torus. The proof uses the fact that any sweepout of S³ by surfaces of genus ≤1 must contain a surface with Willmore energy at least 2π², and that the Clifford torus is the unique minimizer. Subsequent work by Ketover–Marques–Neves (2016) extended the method to prove the Freedman–He–Wang conjecture for genus g≥2, giving the optimal lower bound W(Σ)≥2π²(1+1/g) for surfaces of genus g in S³. The Willmore flow, the L²‑gradient flow of W, was studied by Kuwert–Schätzle (2001) who proved long‑time existence and convergence for small energy initial data, and by Rivière (2008) who established global weak solutions via the conservation law formulation.

Technique
Explain why the Willmore functional is conformally invariant in ℝ³ and how this invariance leads to the loss of compactness in minimizing sequences for the Willmore conjecture.
The Willmore functional W(Σ)=∫_Σ H² dμ can be written as ∫_Σ (κ₁−κ₂)² dμ + 4π χ(Σ) by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, where κ₁,κ₂ are principal curvatures and χ is the Euler characteristic. Under a conformal change of the ambient metric g̃ = e2φ g, the mean curvature transforms as H̃ = e-φ(H + 2∂_ν φ) and the area element as dμ̃ = e2φ dμ. A direct computation shows that H̃² dμ̃ = H² dμ + exact divergence terms, so the integral of H² is invariant modulo boundary contributions. For closed surfaces in ℝ³ the boundary terms vanish, giving full conformal invariance. This invariance means that a minimizing sequence can be acted upon by the non‑compact conformal group (Möbius transformations) to produce new sequences with the same energy but no convergent subsequence in the usual topology — the “bubbling” phenomenon. In the Marques–Neves proof this is circumvented by working in the conformal class of the round sphere S³, where the conformal group is compact, and by using the min‑max widths which are conformal invariants themselves.

Quantitative Stratification and ε‑Regularity for Harmonic Maps and Minimal Surfaces

Quantitative stratification, introduced by Cheeger and Naber (2013) and refined by Naber–Valtorta (2017), provides a scale‑by‑scale description of the singular set of solutions to elliptic geometric PDEs. For a stationary harmonic map u: B₁(0)⊂ℝ^m → N into a compact Riemannian manifold, the singular set Σ(u) consists of points where u fails to be C1,α. The classical result of Schoen–Uhlenbeck (1982) gives dim_ℋ Σ(u) ≤ m−3. Quantitative stratification defines, for each integer k and scale r>0, the set S^k_r,η of points where no tangent map at scale r splits off a ℝk+1 factor with η‑symmetry. The main estimate is that the r‑neighbourhood of S^k_r,η has volume at most C rm−k−η. This yields the effective Minkowski bound dim_ℳ Σ(u) ≤ m−3 and, via a covering argument, the optimal Hausdorff dimension bound. The same scheme applies to area‑minimizing integral currents (Almgren 2000) and to stationary varifolds (Allard 1972). For minimal hypersurfaces, the singular set has codimension at least 7 (Federer 1970), and quantitative stratification gives the sharp estimate that the (n−7+δ)‑dimensional Minkowski content of the singular set is zero for any δ>0. The ε‑regularity theorem is the analytic engine: if the normalized energy ∫B_r(x) |∇u|² is below a universal ε₀, then u is smooth in Br/2(x) with quantitative C1,α bounds. In the harmonic map setting, ε₀ depends only on the target geometry; for minimal surfaces it is the smallness of the excess E(x,r)=r2−n∫B_r(x) |A|². These quantitative tools have been used by Cheeger–Jiang–Naber (2015) to prove rectifiability of the singular strata of Ricci limit spaces and by Edelen–Naber (2020) to obtain quantitative differentiation for the Yang–Mills functional.

Ancient Solutions and the Classification of κ‑Solutions in Ricci Flow

An ancient solution to Ricci flow is a complete solution g(t) defined for all t∈(−∞,0] (or (−∞,T)). Perelman’s analysis of singularities relies on the classification of κ‑solutions: ancient, non‑flat, κ‑noncollapsed solutions with bounded nonnegative curvature operator. In dimension three, Perelman (2002) proved that every three‑dimensional κ‑solution is, up to scaling and isometry, either the shrinking round sphere S³, the shrinking round cylinder S²×ℝ (or its ℤ₂ quotient), or the Bryant soliton — a rotationally symmetric, steady (translation‑invariant) solution on ℝ³ with positive curvature. The proof uses the reduced volume monotonicity, the asymptotic volume ratio, and the Hamilton–Ivey pinching estimate to rule out other possibilities. In higher dimensions, the classification is incomplete, but significant progress has been made: Brendle (2013) showed that any four‑dimensional κ‑solution with positive isotropic curvature is a quotient of the shrinking sphere or cylinder; Cao–Chen–Zhu (2012) classified four‑dimensional κ‑solutions with nonnegative curvature operator. The Bryant soliton plays a special role as the unique (up to scaling) non‑compact, steady, κ‑noncollapsed ancient solution in three dimensions; it models the tip of a Type‑II singularity. Understanding ancient solutions is essential for the surgery construction: the canonical neighborhood theorem asserts that every high‑curvature region in a Ricci flow with surgery is ε‑close to a piece of a κ‑solution. Recent work by Bamler (2020) on the entropy functional and by Haslhofer–Hershkovits (2021) on the stability of the cylinder has refined the classification and provided quantitative uniqueness statements for ancient solutions arising as blow‑up limits.

Conformal Invariants: Q‑Curvature, the Paneitz Operator, and the Branson–Gover Program

In conformal geometry, the Q‑curvature Q_g is a scalar invariant of a Riemannian metric g of dimension n≥4 that transforms by the simple law Qe2wg=e-nw(Q_g+P_g w), where P_g is the Paneitz operator, a fourth‑order conformally covariant differential operator. For n=4, P_g = Δ_g² + δ( (2/3)R_g g − 2 Ric_g ) d, and the total Q‑curvature ∫_M Q_g dV_g is a conformal invariant (the Euler characteristic plus a boundary term). The Branson–Gover program seeks to generalize the Yamabe problem to higher order: given a conformal class [g], find a metric with constant Q‑curvature. This leads to the prescribed Q‑curvature equation P_g u + Q_g u = Q̃ u(n+4)/(n−4). Existence results parallel the Yamabe theory: on the sphere Sⁿ the problem is obstructed by the conformal group, and the sharp Sobolev inequality for P_g (the Beckner inequality) gives the best constant. Chang–Yang (1995) and Branson–Gover (2001) established existence for manifolds with positive Yamabe invariant and non‑positive Q‑curvature, using variational methods and the concentration‑compactness principle. The Q‑curvature flow ∂_t g = −Q_g g, introduced by Branson–Gover, preserves the conformal class and decreases the total Q‑curvature functional. Convergence results by Gursky–Malchiodi (2015) and by Hang–Yang (2016) show that, under suitable pinching conditions, the flow converges to a constant Q‑curvature metric. These higher‑order invariants also appear in the AdS/CFT correspondence, where the holographic anomaly is expressed in terms of Q‑curvature and the Paneitz operator of the boundary metric.

The Calibrated Geometry Approach to Minimal Submanifolds

Calibrated geometry, introduced by Harvey and Lawson in 1982, provides a powerful framework for constructing and recognizing volume‑minimizing submanifolds. A calibration on a Riemannian manifold (M,g) is a closed differential form φ of degree k such that φ|_ξ ≤ vol_ξ for every oriented k‑plane ξ ⊂ T_pM. A k‑dimensional submanifold Σ is calibrated by φ if φ|_TΣ = vol_Σ at every point. The fundamental theorem states that any calibrated submanifold is absolutely volume‑minimizing in its homology class: if Σ' is another submanifold with ∂Σ' = ∂Σ and [Σ'] = [Σ] in H_k(M), then vol(Σ) ≤ vol(Σ'). This gives a first‑order condition for minimality that bypasses the second variation.

Important examples include special Lagrangian submanifolds in Calabi–Yau manifolds (calibrated by Re Ω), associative and coassociative submanifolds in G₂ manifolds (calibrated by the 3‑form φ and its Hodge dual *φ), and Cayley submanifolds in Spin(7) manifolds (calibrated by a 4‑form Φ). In Kähler geometry, complex submanifolds are calibrated by powers of the Kähler form ω, and holomorphic curves are area‑minimizing. The deformation theory of calibrated submanifolds was developed by McLean, who showed that the moduli space of special Lagrangian deformations is smooth and its dimension equals the first Betti number of the submanifold. For associative submanifolds the deformation theory is obstructed, leading to a virtual fundamental class used in enumerative invariants. Calibrated geometry also underlies the SYZ conjecture in mirror symmetry, where special Lagrangian fibrations provide a geometric picture of T‑duality.

Hamilton’s Differential Harnack Inequality for Ricci Flow

In 1993 Hamilton proved a differential Harnack inequality for the Ricci flow on manifolds with nonnegative curvature operator. For a solution g(t) with Ric ≥ 0 (in the sense of curvature operator), the inequality takes the matrix form: for any vector field v,

∂_t Rij v^i v^j + 2 ∇_k Rij v^i v^j v^k + 2 Rik Rjl v^i v^j v^k v^l + (1/t) Rij v^i v^j ≥ 0.

Taking the trace yields the scalar Harnack inequality

∂_t R + 2 ⟨∇R, v⟩ + 2 Ric(v,v) + R/t ≥ 0.

This inequality implies that the quantity t(2Δf − |∇f|² + R) is nondecreasing along the flow for suitable f, and it provides a parabolic analogue of the Li–Yau Harnack estimate for the heat equation. Hamilton used it to show that ancient solutions with nonnegative curvature operator have nonnegative scalar curvature and to derive monotonicity formulas that are essential for the classification of κ‑solutions. Perelman later incorporated a similar entropy functional, but Hamilton’s Harnack remains the primary tool for proving that any blow‑up limit of a Type I singularity is a gradient shrinking soliton. The inequality fails if the curvature operator has negative eigenvalues; counterexamples exist on manifolds with mixed curvature signs.

The Classification of Gradient Ricci Solitons

A gradient Ricci soliton is a triple (M,g,f) satisfying Ric + Hess f = λ g for a constant λ. If λ > 0 the soliton is shrinking, λ = 0 steady, λ < 0 expanding. These solitons arise as self‑similar solutions of the Ricci flow and model singularities. In dimension two Hamilton classified all complete gradient solitons: the Gaussian shrinking soliton on ℝ², the cigar (Hamilton’s steady soliton) on ℝ² with positive curvature, and the expanding soliton on ℝ² with negative curvature. In dimension three Perelman proved that any compact shrinking gradient Ricci soliton is a finite quotient of the round sphere S³ or the round cylinder S²×ℝ. For noncompact shrinking solitons with positive curvature, the Bryant soliton is the unique (up to scaling) steady soliton on ℝ³ with positive sectional curvature; it is rotationally symmetric and asymptotically cylindrical. In higher dimensions the classification is far from complete. Known examples include the Gaussian soliton on ℝⁿ, Kähler–Ricci solitons on toric Fano varieties (Cao, Koiso, Wang–Zhu), and products of lower‑dimensional solitons. Brendle (2013) showed that any four‑dimensional κ‑solution with positive isotropic curvature is a quotient of the shrinking sphere or cylinder. Cao–Chen–Zhu (2012) classified four‑dimensional κ‑solutions with nonnegative curvature operator. The uniqueness of the Bryant soliton among steady κ‑noncollapsed ancient solutions in three dimensions was established by Perelman and later refined by Brendle and by Bamler using entropy methods.

The Free Boundary Minimal Surface Problem and Its Regularity Theory

Free boundary minimal surfaces are critical points of the area functional among surfaces whose boundary lies on a prescribed supporting hypersurface ∂M and meets ∂M orthogonally. The first variation formula yields the condition that the surface has zero mean curvature in the interior and that its conormal is tangent to ∂M along the boundary. Regularity theory splits into interior regularity (identical to the classical minimal surface theory) and boundary regularity. If the supporting manifold is smooth and strictly mean convex, Grisvard and Nitsche proved that the free boundary is smooth up to the boundary. For higher codimension, Allard’s boundary regularity theorem gives C1,α regularity under a density condition. Fraser and Schoen (2011) linked the first Steklov eigenvalue of a domain to the existence of free boundary minimal surfaces in the Euclidean ball, showing that the maximizer of the first Steklov eigenvalue among domains of fixed volume is a free boundary minimal surface. Min‑max methods for free boundary minimal hypersurfaces were developed by Li and Zhou (2018) and by Ketover (2016), producing existence results in manifolds with boundary. The multiplicity one conjecture for free boundary min‑max limits remains open in general, though recent work by Zhou and by Chodosh–Mantoulidis has made progress under generic metrics.

The Concentration‑Compactness Principle in the Yamabe Problem

The Yamabe problem seeks a metric of constant scalar curvature in a given conformal class. The associated Euler–Lagrange equation is the Yamabe equation Δ_g u − c_n R_g u + c_n R̃ u(n+2)/(n−2) = 0, where c_n = (n−2)/(4(n−1)). The functional lacks compactness because the Sobolev embedding H¹(M) L2n/(n−2)(M) is not compact; sequences may concentrate at points. Lions’ concentration‑compactness principle (1984) provides a systematic way to analyze such sequences. Given a bounded sequence in H¹, up to a subsequence one of three scenarios occurs: (1) compactness – the sequence converges strongly in L2n/(n−2); (2) vanishing – the sequence spreads out and converges weakly to zero; (3) dichotomy – the sequence splits into two asymptotically disjoint parts. In the Yamabe setting, dichotomy corresponds to bubbling: a subsequence concentrates at a point p, and after rescaling one obtains a nontrivial solution of the Yamabe equation on ℝⁿ, which must be the standard Aubin–Talenti bubble. This blow‑up analysis, combined with the positive mass theorem, shows that on a manifold not conformally equivalent to the sphere the Yamabe functional satisfies the Palais–Smale condition, yielding existence of a minimizer. Schoen (1984) used the positive mass theorem to rule out the loss of mass at infinity in the blow‑up, completing the solution of the Yamabe problem. Later, Li and Zhu (1999) proved a compactness theorem for Yamabe metrics: the set of unit‑volume constant scalar curvature metrics in a fixed conformal class is compact in C² unless the manifold is the sphere.

The Douglas–Rado Solution to the Plateau Problem and Parametric Minimal Surfaces

The classical Plateau problem asks for a surface of least area spanning a given Jordan curve in ℝ³. Jesse Douglas (1931) and Tibor Radó (1930) independently solved it by minimizing the Dirichlet integral among conformal parametrizations of the disk. Douglas introduced the functional D(u) = ∫_D |∇u|² for maps u from the unit disk D into ℝ³, and showed that a minimizer exists in the class of functions whose boundary values trace the given curve in a monotone fashion. Radó used a different approach based on the theory of harmonic functions and the solution of the Dirichlet problem for the Laplace equation. Both proofs rely on the fact that a conformal harmonic parametrization is a minimal surface, and that the area functional equals the Dirichlet integral for conformal maps.

The Douglas–Rado theorem states: for any rectifiable Jordan curve Γ in ℝ³ there exists a continuous map u: D̄ → ℝ³, harmonic in D, conformal almost everywhere, with u(∂D) = Γ, that minimizes area among all surfaces spanning Γ. The solution may have branch points where the differential vanishes; these are isolated and correspond to interior points where the surface fails to be an immersion. Later work by Osserman, Gulliver, and others classified the possible branch points and showed that for analytic curves the solution is analytic up to the boundary.

In the parametric setting, one considers maps from a fixed Riemann surface (usually the disk) into a Riemannian manifold. The energy functional is the Dirichlet energy, and critical points are harmonic maps. When the target is ℝⁿ, harmonic maps are precisely the coordinate functions of minimal surfaces. The Douglas–Rado method extends to higher codimension and to manifolds with nonpositive sectional curvature, where the energy functional is convex. For positively curved targets, the problem becomes more subtle because the energy functional may have multiple critical points and the direct method may fail due to lack of convexity.

Modern treatments use the language of Sobolev spaces W¹,²(D, N) and the theory of harmonic maps into metric spaces. The existence of energy‑minimizing maps with prescribed boundary data was established by Schoen and Uhlenbeck (1982) for targets with nonpositive curvature, and by Jost (1985) for general compact targets using the heat flow method. The regularity theory for minimizing harmonic maps shows that singularities can occur only in codimension at least three, and that the singular set has Hausdorff dimension at most m−3 for maps from an m‑dimensional domain.

Applications of the parametric Plateau problem include the construction of minimal surfaces in homogeneous spaces, the study of the topology of mapping spaces via min‑max methods, and the proof of the existence of closed geodesics on Riemannian manifolds through the curve shortening flow. The Douglas–Rado solution remains the foundational existence result for the Plateau problem and continues to inspire new techniques in geometric analysis.

The Harmonic Map Heat Flow: Global Existence, Blow‑up Analysis, and the Chen–Struwe Theory

The harmonic map heat flow ∂ₜu = τ(u) (where τ(u) = Δu + A(u)(∇u, ∇u) is the tension field) was introduced by Eells and Sampson (1964) to deform an arbitrary map into a harmonic map. For targets with nonpositive sectional curvature, they proved global existence and convergence to a harmonic map. In positive curvature, singularities can form in finite time. The first rigorous blow‑up analysis was carried out by Struwe (1985) for maps from a surface into a compact manifold. He showed that if a singularity occurs at time T, then after rescaling around the singular point one obtains a nontrivial harmonic map from ℝ² (or S²) into the target, called a bubble. The energy identity states that the limit of the energies equals the energy of the weak limit plus the sum of the energies of the bubbles.

Chen and Struwe (1989) extended the analysis to higher‑dimensional domains and proved that the singular set of the flow has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most m−2. They also established the no‑neck property: the region between the base map and the bubbles carries no energy in the limit. This property is crucial for understanding the topology of the space of maps and for the min‑max construction of harmonic maps. Later, Qing (1995) and Tian–Xin (1997) refined the blow‑up analysis for the heat flow of harmonic maps into spheres, showing that the bubbles are exactly the standard harmonic spheres.

The heat flow also provides a tool for proving existence of harmonic maps in non‑contractible homotopy classes. By running the flow from a map representing a given homotopy class, one either obtains global convergence to a harmonic map or a finite‑time blow‑up that produces a harmonic sphere. Iterating this procedure yields a sequence of harmonic maps and spheres whose total energy equals the initial energy. This bubble‑tree convergence was formalized by Parker (1996) and Lin–Wang (1998) for the harmonic map heat flow and for the stationary harmonic map equation.

Applications of the harmonic map heat flow include the proof of the existence of harmonic maps in any homotopy class when the target has nonpositive curvature, the construction of minimal surfaces via the heat flow for the area functional (mean curvature flow), and the study of the topology of loop spaces. The flow also plays a central role in the proof of the differentiable sphere theorem by Brendle and Schoen (2009), where the evolution of the curvature operator under Ricci flow is coupled with a harmonic map heat flow argument.

Key technical tools are the monotonicity formula for the localized energy, the ε‑regularity theorem (if the energy in a parabolic cylinder is small then the solution is smooth), and the stratification of the singular set using the dimension of the space of tangent maps. The Chen–Struwe theory remains the cornerstone of the analytic approach to harmonic maps and their heat flow.

The Yamabe Invariant (Sigma Constant) and Its Role in the Classification of Manifolds with Positive Scalar Curvature

The Yamabe invariant σ(M) (also called the sigma constant) of a smooth closed manifold M of dimension n ≥ 3 is defined as the supremum over all conformal classes [g] of the Yamabe constant Y(M, [g]), where Y(M, [g]) = infg̃∈[g] (∫_M Rg̃ dvolg̃) / (volg̃(M))(n−2)/n. Equivalently, σ(M) = sup[g] Y(M, [g]). The sign of σ(M) determines whether M admits a metric of positive scalar curvature: σ(M) > 0 if and only if M carries a metric with R > 0 somewhere; σ(M) = 0 if and only if M admits a metric with R ≥ 0 but not R > 0; σ(M) < 0 if and only if every metric on M has negative scalar curvature somewhere.

The computation of σ(M) for various classes of manifolds has been a central problem. For the sphere Sⁿ, σ(Sⁿ) = n(n−1)ωₙ2/n, where ωₙ is the volume of the unit sphere. For complex projective spaces ℂPⁿ with the Fubini–Study metric, σ(ℂPⁿ) = 4n(n+1)π2/n (vol(ℂPⁿ))(n−2)/n. For products, the invariant is not simply multiplicative; however, Petean (2000) showed that if M admits a metric of positive scalar curvature, then σ(M × N) ≥ σ(M) for any N. The invariant is also known for many 3‑manifolds: for example, σ(S³) = 6(2π²)2/3, and for hyperbolic 3‑manifolds σ(M) < 0.

The Yamabe invariant is closely related to the Gromov–Lawson–Rosenberg conjecture (now a theorem in many cases) on the existence of positive scalar curvature metrics on spin manifolds. The index theory of the Dirac operator gives an obstruction: if M is spin and the α‑invariant (the KO‑theory index of the Dirac operator) is nonzero, then σ(M) ≤ 0. Conversely, if M is simply connected of dimension ≥ 5 and not spin, the surgery theorem of Gromov and Lawson implies σ(M) > 0. For spin manifolds, the Stolz theorem (1992) states that a simply connected spin manifold of dimension ≥ 5 admits a metric of positive scalar curvature if and only if its α‑invariant vanishes.

In dimension 4, the Seiberg–Witten invariants provide obstructions to positive scalar curvature. LeBrun (1996) used the Yamabe invariant to show that for a complex surface with b₂⁺ > 1, σ(M) ≤ 4π√(2χ + 3τ) (where χ is the Euler characteristic and τ the signature), with equality only for certain Einstein metrics. This gives a sharp upper bound for the Yamabe invariant of many 4‑manifolds.

The Yamabe invariant also appears in the study of the Yamabe flow: the flow converges to a constant scalar curvature metric if the initial Yamabe constant is less than the invariant of the sphere. The invariant is a diffeomorphism invariant, making it a powerful tool for distinguishing smooth structures on topological manifolds.

The Kähler–Ricci Flow and the Existence of Kähler–Einstein Metrics on Fano Varieties

The Kähler–Ricci flow ∂ₜg = −Ric(g) + g on a Fano manifold (c₁(M) > 0) was introduced by Cao (1985) as the natural parabolic method to find Kähler–Einstein metrics. The flow preserves the Kähler class c₁(M) and can be written as a complex Monge–Ampère equation for the evolving potential φ(t): ∂ₜφ = log((ω + i∂∂̄φ)ⁿ / ωⁿ) + φ, where ω is a fixed background Kähler form. The long‑time behavior of the flow is governed by the existence of a Kähler–Einstein metric, which is equivalent to the properness of the Mabuchi K‑energy functional.

Tian and Zhu (2000) proved that if the Futaki invariant vanishes and the Mabuchi energy is bounded below, then the Kähler–Ricci flow converges exponentially fast to a Kähler–Einstein metric. The Futaki invariant is an obstruction to the existence of Kähler–Einstein metrics on Fano manifolds; it vanishes for symmetric spaces and for manifolds with a torus action satisfying certain conditions. The breakthrough by Chen, Donaldson, and Sun (2012–2015) established the equivalence between the existence of a Kähler–Einstein metric on a Fano manifold and K‑stability, an algebro‑geometric notion introduced by Tian (1997) and Donaldson (2002). Their proof uses the continuity method and the Kähler–Ricci flow to produce a weak solution, then applies a regularity theory for the complex Monge–Ampère equation.

The flow also provides a canonical way to degenerate a Fano manifold to a K‑stable limit. If the flow does not converge, it develops singularities that can be analyzed via the partial C⁰‑estimate of Chen–Wang (2015) and the work of Tian–Zhang (2016) on the formation of singularities. The singularities are modeled on Kähler–Ricci solitons, and the limit space is a Q‑Fano variety with at worst Kawamata log terminal singularities. This analytic minimal model program has led to the proof of the Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture for all Fano manifolds.

Applications include the construction of Kähler–Einstein metrics on moduli spaces of stable vector bundles, the study of the Kähler–Ricci flow on varieties with log terminal singularities, and the connection to the Kähler–Einstein problem on log Fano pairs. The flow also plays a role in the proof of the uniformization theorem for Kähler metrics on Riemann surfaces and in the study of the Calabi flow.

The Allen–Cahn Equation as a Phase‑Field Approximation to Minimal Surfaces

The Allen–Cahn equation ∂ₜu = Δu − (1/ε²)W'(u) with a double‑well potential W(u) = (1−u²)²/4 arises in materials science as a model for phase separation. As ε → 0, the zero level set of u converges to a minimal surface (or more generally a surface moving by mean curvature). This was rigorously established by Modica and Mortola (1977) for the static problem: the energy functional E_ε(u) = ∫ (ε|∇u|²/2 + W(u)/ε) Γ‑converges to the perimeter functional (up to a constant factor). The limiting interfaces are minimal hypersurfaces, and the convergence is in the sense of varifolds.

For the parabolic Allen–Cahn equation, Ilmanen (1993) proved that the level sets converge to the Brakke flow of mean curvature, provided the initial data are well‑prepared. The proof uses the monotonicity formula for the Allen–Cahn energy and the equipartition of energy property: in the limit, the gradient energy and the potential energy are equal on the interface. This equipartition is a key ingredient in the proof of the convergence to mean curvature flow.

Hutchinson and Tonegawa (2000) extended the convergence result to the case of multiple phases and to the motion of networks of surfaces meeting at prescribed angles. They showed that the Allen–Cahn equation can approximate the motion of grain boundaries in polycrystals, where triple junctions satisfy the Herring angle condition. The analysis requires a careful study of the asymptotic profile of the solution near junctions, which is given by a one‑dimensional heteroclinic solution of the ODE u'' = W'(u).

The Allen–Cahn approximation has become a powerful numerical tool for computing minimal surfaces and mean curvature flow. The diffuse interface method avoids the need for explicit front tracking and handles topological changes automatically. Rigorous error estimates for numerical schemes based on the Allen–Cahn equation have been developed by Feng and Prohl (2003) and by Barrett, Nürnberg, and Styles (2004).

In geometric analysis, the Allen–Cahn equation provides a variational approach to the existence of minimal hypersurfaces in manifolds with positive Ricci curvature. Guaraco (2018) used the min‑max theory for the Allen–Cahn energy to prove the Weyl law for the volume spectrum: the k‑th width of the Allen–Cahn energy converges to the k‑th eigenvalue of the Laplacian on the space of minimal hypersurfaces. This connects the phase‑field approximation to the Almgren–Pitts min‑max theory and gives a new proof of the existence of infinitely many minimal hypersurfaces in generic metrics.

The Bernstein Problem and Entire Minimal Graphs

The Bernstein problem asks whether an entire minimal graph in Euclidean space must be a hyperplane. In dimension two the answer is affirmative: any entire solution of the minimal surface equation div(∇u/√(1+|∇u|²)) = 0 on ℝ² is affine. Bernstein proved this in 1915–1916 using complex analysis. For higher dimensions the problem remained open until the 1960s. De Giorgi (1965) settled the case n = 3, Almgren (1966) handled n = 4, and Simons (1968) proved the result for n ≤ 7 by establishing a stability inequality for minimal hypersurfaces. The breakthrough came in 1969 when Bombieri, De Giorgi, and Giusti constructed a counterexample in ℝ⁸: a non‑planar entire minimal graph given by the zero set of a homogeneous polynomial of degree two. Their example shows that the Simons cone {x ∈ ℝ⁸ : x₁²+…+x₄² = x₅²+…+x₈²} is area‑minimizing, and a suitable perturbation yields an entire minimal graph that is not a hyperplane. The Bernstein theorem therefore holds exactly for dimensions n ≤ 7. The proof techniques involve the monotonicity formula, the Simons identity |A|² = Σ κᵢ² for the second fundamental form, and the stability operator L = Δ + |A|². Later work by Federer (1969) and Almgren (2000) placed the problem in the framework of geometric measure theory, showing that the singular set of an area‑minimizing current has codimension at least 7, which aligns with the dimension threshold in the Bernstein problem. The result has profound implications for the regularity theory of minimal hypersurfaces and for the classification of stable minimal cones.

Self‑Shrinkers and the Classification of Ancient Solutions to Mean Curvature Flow

Self‑shrinkers are hypersurfaces Σ ⊂ ℝⁿ⁺¹ satisfying H = ⟨x, ν⟩/2, where H is the mean curvature, ν the unit normal, and x the position vector. They arise as blow‑up limits of Type I singularities in mean curvature flow and correspond to critical points of the Gaussian weighted area functional F(Σ) = (4π)⁻ⁿᐟ² ∫_Σ e-|x|²/4 dμ. Huisken’s monotonicity formula (1990) shows that the Gaussian density Θ(x₀,t₀) = limt→t₀ F(Σ_t) is non‑increasing along the flow, and equality characterizes self‑shrinkers. In ℝ³ the only complete, embedded self‑shrinkers with polynomial volume growth are the plane, the cylinder S¹×ℝ, the sphere S², and the Angenent torus (Angenert 1992). Colding and Minicozzi (2012) proved that any generic mean curvature flow in ℝ³ encounters only these four models as singularities. For higher dimensions the classification is incomplete; however, Brendle (2016) classified all embedded self‑shrinkers in ℝ⁴ that are asymptotic to a cylinder, showing they are either the cylinder or a rotationally symmetric “bowl” soliton. Ancient solutions, which exist for all negative time, are closely related: any ancient solution with bounded entropy is a self‑shrinker (Huisken–Sinestrari 1999). The entropy functional λ(Σ) = supx₀,t₀ Fx₀,t₀(Σ) introduced by Colding–Minicozzi provides a Lyapunov function for the flow. Recent work by Bernstein–Wang (2020) and Hershkovits–White (2022) has extended the classification to low‑entropy ancient flows in ℝ⁴, proving that the only non‑compact ancient solutions with entropy ≤ λ(S¹×ℝ) are the cylinder and the translating bowl soliton. These results are essential for the surgery program in mean curvature flow, where one replaces high‑curvature regions by standard caps modeled on self‑shrinkers.

Technique: Identifying the Singularity Model
A mean curvature flow in ℝ³ develops a Type I singularity at (0,0). The rescaled flow converges to a complete embedded self‑shrinker with entropy strictly less than λ(S¹×ℝ). Which self‑shrinker can appear?
By the Colding–Minicozzi classification, the only complete embedded self‑shrinkers in ℝ³ with entropy ≤ λ(S¹×ℝ) are the plane (entropy 1), the sphere (entropy λ(S²) > λ(S¹×ℝ)), the cylinder (entropy λ(S¹×ℝ)), and the Angenent torus (entropy > λ(S¹×ℝ)). Since the limit has entropy strictly less than λ(S¹×ℝ), the only possibility is the plane. Therefore the singularity is a multiplicity‑one plane, which corresponds to a smooth point — contradicting the assumption of a singularity. Hence a Type I singularity with entropy < λ(S¹×ℝ) cannot occur; any genuine Type I singularity must have entropy at least λ(S¹×ℝ) and the limit is either the cylinder or the sphere.

This quick elimination often yields the answer without heavy computation.

Min‑Max Theory for Minimal Submanifolds in Higher Codimension

The Almgren–Pitts min‑max theory was originally developed for hypersurfaces (codimension one). Extending it to higher codimension requires new compactness and regularity tools because the space of integral currents of dimension k in an n‑manifold is not closed under weak convergence when k < n−1. Almgren’s monumental regularity paper (2000) proved that area‑minimizing integral currents of any codimension have singular sets of Hausdorff dimension at most k−2. Building on this, Pitts (1981) and later Schoen–Simon (1981) established a min‑max existence theory for stationary varifolds in arbitrary codimension, but the regularity of the resulting min‑max varifold remained open. In the 2010s, Zhou (2015) and De Lellis–Ramic (2018) proved that for a generic metric on a closed manifold Mⁿ, the min‑max widths produce smooth embedded minimal submanifolds of dimension k for any 2 ≤ k ≤ n−2, provided the min‑max sequence satisfies a “no‑mass‑concentration” condition. The key analytic ingredient is a replacement theorem: given a sequence of k‑currents with bounded mass and boundary, one can replace each current by a nearby integral current with the same boundary and no worse mass, while gaining higher integrability of the second fundamental form. This replacement uses the Almgren–Pitts combinatorial argument together with the Allard regularity theorem for varifolds with small excess. The resulting min‑max minimal submanifolds have Morse index bounded by the number of parameters in the sweepout. Marques–Neves (2017) applied a similar scheme to prove the Willmore conjecture, but their argument was restricted to hypersurfaces in S³. For higher codimension, the existence of infinitely many distinct minimal submanifolds in generic metrics was established by Irie–Marques–Neves (2019) using a min‑max argument on the space of cycles with coefficients in ℤ₂. Their proof also yields a Weyl law for the volume spectrum: the k‑th width grows like k1/k times a constant depending only on the dimension. These results demonstrate that the min‑max machinery, once confined to hypersurfaces, now provides a robust framework for producing minimal submanifolds of any dimension and codimension.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Boundary: The Escobar Problem

The classical Yamabe problem seeks a conformal metric of constant scalar curvature on a closed manifold. When the manifold has a non‑empty boundary, the natural analogue asks for a conformal metric with constant scalar curvature in the interior and constant mean curvature on the boundary. This is the Escobar problem, solved by Escobar (1992) for dimensions n ≥ 3. The conformal change g̃ = u4/(n−2) g transforms the scalar curvature R and the boundary mean curvature H according to R̃ = u-(n+2)/(n−2) ( -c_n Δ_g u + R u ) and H̃ = u-n/(n−2) ( 2/(n−2) ∂_ν u + H u ), where c_n = 4(n−1)/(n−2). The problem reduces to finding a positive solution of the coupled system -c_n Δ_g u + R u = R̃ u(n+2)/(n−2) in M and 2/(n−2) ∂_ν u + H u = H̃ un/(n−2) on ∂M, with prescribed constants R̃, H̃. Escobar introduced the boundary Yamabe invariant Y(M,∂M,[g]) = infu>0 ( ∫_M (c_n |∇u|² + R u²) + 2∫∂M H u² ) / ( ∫_M u2n/(n−2) )(n−2)/n. He proved that if the invariant is less than the corresponding invariant of the half‑sphere, a minimizer exists and yields the desired metric. The sharp Sobolev trace inequality on the half‑space ℝⁿ₊ = {x_n > 0} gives the threshold value. When the invariant equals the half‑sphere value, non‑existence can occur due to the lack of compactness, exactly as in the closed case. Later work by Marques (2005) and Chen–Jin (2015) extended the result to locally conformally flat manifolds with boundary and to the case of prescribed scalar curvature and boundary mean curvature functions. The Escobar problem also connects to the positive mass theorem for asymptotically flat manifolds with boundary, as shown by Escobar (1996) and later by Almaraz (2012). In the conformally flat case, the problem is equivalent to finding a conformal metric with zero scalar curvature and zero boundary mean curvature, which is a boundary value problem for the conformal Laplacian. The techniques involve blow‑up analysis, the concentration‑compactness principle, and the classification of solutions on the half‑space via the method of moving planes.

Ricci Flow on Orbifolds and Conifold Transitions

Hamilton’s Ricci flow extends naturally to Riemannian orbifolds, where the metric is smooth away from a singular set of codimension at least two modeled on finite group quotients of ℝⁿ. Hamilton (1995) proved short‑time existence and uniqueness for the Ricci flow on compact orbifolds, and showed that the flow preserves the orbifold structure. The singularities of the flow occur at the orbifold points, and the curvature blows up like the smooth case. Perelman’s surgery procedure for three‑manifolds was adapted to orbifolds by Chen–Zhu (2006) and later by Bamler (2018), who proved that the Ricci flow with surgery on a compact 3‑orbifold exists for all time and becomes extinct or converges to a geometric orbifold. In higher dimensions, the Kähler–Ricci flow on projective varieties with Kawamata log terminal singularities provides a natural setting for conifold transitions. A conifold transition replaces a nodal Calabi–Yau threefold by a smooth one via a small resolution or a deformation. The Kähler–Ricci flow on the singular variety develops a finite‑time singularity at the node; after a suitable rescaling, the flow converges to a Ricci‑flat Kähler metric on the resolved space (Song–Tian 2012). This analytic process mirrors the topological transition and provides a geometric proof of the Reid conjecture for Calabi–Yau threefolds. The flow also plays a role in the minimal model program for varieties of general type: the Kähler–Ricci flow contracts curves with negative intersection against the canonical divisor, producing a minimal model with at worst canonical singularities (Tsuji 2015). The entropy functional W(g,f,τ) = ∫_M (τ(|∇f|² + R) + f − n) (4πτ)-n/2 e-f dV introduced by Perelman is well‑defined on orbifolds and is monotone along the flow. Its monotonicity yields a no‑local‑collapsing theorem for orbifold Ricci flow, which is essential for the surgery construction. Recent work by Guo–Zhang (2021) established a pseudolocality theorem for orbifold Ricci flow, showing that if the initial metric is close to Euclidean in a neighborhood of an orbifold point, the curvature remains controlled for a short time. These results demonstrate that the Ricci flow is a robust tool for studying the geometry and topology of singular spaces, bridging the gap between smooth and algebraic geometry.

The Monotonicity Formula and Its Applications in Minimal Surface Theory

The monotonicity formula is a cornerstone of regularity theory for minimal surfaces. For a stationary integral varifold V in a Riemannian manifold M, the formula asserts that the quantity Θ(V, x, r) = r−k μV(Br(x)) is non‑decreasing in r, where k is the dimension of the varifold and μV its weight measure. In Euclidean space the proof uses the first variation formula with the vector field X(y) = (y−x) φ(|y−x|/r) and the fact that the mean curvature vanishes. The limit as r → 0 defines the density Θ(V, x), which is integer‑valued for integral varifolds by Allard’s regularity theorem. The monotonicity formula yields immediate consequences: a uniform density bound gives a local mass bound, which together with Allard’s ε‑regularity implies smoothness away from a singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most k−2. It also underlies the blow‑up analysis at singular points: rescaling a minimal surface around a singular point produces a tangent cone, which is a stationary cone with constant density. The classification of such cones in low dimensions (e.g. the Simons cone in ℝ8) is a direct application of the monotonicity formula combined with the stability inequality. In the context of the Almgren–Pitts min‑max theory, the monotonicity formula guarantees that the widths of sweepouts are realized by stationary varifolds with controlled density, enabling the construction of minimal hypersurfaces in generic metrics. Recent extensions by White and by De Lellis–Spadaro have adapted the formula to varifolds with bounded mean curvature and to the free‑boundary setting, where the boundary term contributes an additional non‑negative quantity.

The Classification of Entire Minimal Graphs and the Simons Cone

An entire minimal graph in ℝn+1 is a hypersurface given globally as the graph of a function u : ℝn → ℝ satisfying the minimal surface equation div(∇u / √(1+|∇u|2)) = 0. The classical Bernstein theorem states that for n ≤ 7 every entire minimal graph is an affine hyperplane. The proof proceeds by showing that the graph is stable, then applying the Simons identity to obtain a differential inequality for the norm of the second fundamental form, which forces it to vanish. In dimension n = 8 the theorem fails: the Simons cone C = {(x, y) ∈ ℝ4 × ℝ4 : |x| = |y|} is a minimal hypersurface that is not a hyperplane. It is a cone over the product of two spheres S3 × S3 and has singular set at the origin. The cone is stable outside the origin, and its existence shows that the Bernstein threshold is sharp. The classification of entire minimal graphs in higher dimensions remains open, but a series of results by Bombieri–De Giorgi–Giusti, by Simon, and by Ecker–Huisken have established that any entire minimal graph with at most linear growth must be a hyperplane for n ≤ 7. For n ≥ 8 there exist non‑trivial entire minimal graphs constructed via the method of calibrated geometry: the Lawlor–Osserman cones and the higher‑dimensional analogues of the Simons cone provide explicit examples. The study of these graphs is intimately linked to the regularity theory of area‑minimizing currents: the singular set of an area‑minimizing hypersurface has dimension at most n−7, and the Simons cone is the model singularity in the critical dimension n = 7. Recent work by Wickramasekera and by Chodosh–Li has extended the classification to graphs with prescribed asymptotic cones, showing that the moduli space of entire minimal graphs with a given tangent cone at infinity is finite‑dimensional and often consists of a single element.

Explain how the monotonicity formula is used to prove that the density of a stationary integral varifold at a point is an integer.
The monotonicity formula gives that Θ(V, x, r) = r−k μV(Br(x)) is non‑decreasing in r. As r → 0 the limit Θ(V, x) exists. For an integral varifold, the weight measure μV is integer‑multiplicity rectifiable. By Allard’s regularity theorem, if Θ(V, x) < 1 + ε for a small dimensional constant ε, then V is a smooth embedded disk near x with multiplicity one. In general, one blows up V at x by the rescalings ηr(y) = (y−x)/r. The monotonicity formula ensures that the masses of the rescaled varifolds are uniformly bounded on compact sets, so a subsequence converges to a stationary cone C. The density Θ(V, x) equals the mass of C in the unit ball. Since C is an integral cone, its mass is an integer multiple of the volume of the unit ball in ℝk. Hence Θ(V, x) is an integer.

The Prescribed Scalar Curvature Problem and the Kazdan–Warner Obstructions

The prescribed scalar curvature problem asks: given a smooth function f on a closed manifold M, does there exist a metric conformal to a background metric g whose scalar curvature equals f? In the conformal class [g] the scalar curvature of g̃ = u4/(n−2) g satisfies the Yamabe‑type equation −cn Δg u + Rg u = f u(n+2)/(n−2) with cn = 4(n−1)/(n−2). Kazdan and Warner discovered necessary integral conditions: if f is the scalar curvature of a metric conformal to g, then for every conformal Killing vector field X of g one must have ∫M f ⟨∇φ, X⟩ dVg = 0, where φ is the conformal factor relating the two metrics. On the standard sphere Sn these obstructions reduce to the vanishing of the first moments of f with respect to the coordinate functions. The Kazdan–Warner obstructions are not sufficient in general; there exist functions satisfying them that are not scalar curvatures of any conformal metric. The problem is variational: one seeks critical points of the Einstein–Hilbert functional restricted to the conformal class. The lack of compactness due to the critical Sobolev exponent leads to bubbling phenomena. The concentration‑compactness principle of Lions, adapted by Schoen and by Chen–Li, gives a full description of the loss of compactness: any Palais‑Smale sequence either converges or concentrates at finitely many points where the limiting profile is a standard bubble on ℝn. Using this analysis, Escobar and Schoen proved that on a locally conformally flat manifold the only obstruction is the Kazdan–Warner condition. For general manifolds, the problem remains open in dimensions n ≥ 3, though the positive mass theorem and the work of Marques on the Yamabe invariant provide topological restrictions: if the Yamabe invariant of M is positive, then any positive function can be realized as scalar curvature after a conformal change.

Perelman's Reduced Volume and the Non‑Collapsing Theorem for Ricci Flow

Perelman introduced the reduced length l(q, τ) and the reduced volume Ṽ(τ) = ∫M (4πτ)−n/2 e−l(q, τ) dVg(τ) as a monotone quantity along the Ricci flow ∂t g = −2 Ric. The reduced length is defined via ℒ‑geodesics emanating from a fixed space‑time point (p, T) backward in time; it satisfies the differential inequality ∂τ l − Δ l + |∇l|2 − R + n/(2τ) ≥ 0. Integrating this inequality yields the monotonicity d/dτ Ṽ(τ) ≤ 0. The reduced volume is scale‑invariant and equals the Gaussian volume (4π)−n/2 on the Euclidean flow. Perelman’s non‑collapsing theorem states that if the Ricci flow on a closed manifold has bounded curvature on a time interval [0, T] and the initial metric has volume bounded below, then there exists a constant κ > 0 depending only on the initial data such that every parabolic ball P(x, t, r) with r2 ≤ t and |Rm| ≤ r−2 satisfies Vol(Bg(t)(x, r)) ≥ κ rn. The proof uses the reduced volume: a hypothetical collapsing sequence would force the reduced volume to drop below the Euclidean value, contradicting monotonicity. This κ‑non‑collapsing is essential for the surgery construction because it prevents the formation of arbitrarily small necks that would be invisible at the surgery scale. The reduced volume also plays a central role in the classification of κ‑solutions: any non‑flat ancient κ‑solution has positive asymptotic reduced volume, which forces it to be a gradient shrinking soliton. In dimension three the only such solitons are the round sphere, the round cylinder S2 × ℝ, and their quotients. The reduced volume monotonicity has been extended to the Kähler‑Ricci flow and to Ricci flow on orbifolds, where the same non‑collapsing estimate holds with a modified definition of reduced length that accounts for the orbifold singularities.

The Modica–Mortola Conjecture and the Convergence of Allen–Cahn to Minimal Surfaces

The Allen–Cahn equation ε2 Δ u − W'(u) = 0 with a double‑well potential W(u) = (1−u2)2/4 arises as the Euler–Lagrange equation of the Ginzburg–Landau energy Eε(u) = ∫M (ε/2 |∇u|2 + W(u)/ε) dV. Modica and Mortola conjectured in 1977 that as ε → 0 the functionals Eε Γ‑converge to a constant multiple of the perimeter functional, and that the zero level sets of minimizers converge to minimal hypersurfaces. The conjecture was proved by Modica (1987) for the one‑dimensional case and by Sternberg (1988) for the general case using the theory of sets of finite perimeter. The Γ‑limit is c0 Per(E) where c0 = ∫−11 √(2W(s)) ds and E = {u ≈ 1}. The convergence of the diffuse interfaces to sharp interfaces is quantified by the Modica inequality ε |∇u|2 ≤ 2 W(u), which holds for any solution of the Allen–Cahn equation. This inequality implies that the energy density concentrates on a hypersurface of codimension one. The regularity of the limiting minimal hypersurface follows from the work of Hutchinson and Tonegawa (2000), who showed that the varifold limits of the measures με = (ε/2 |∇uε|2 + W(uε)/ε) dV are integral varifolds with bounded first variation, hence stationary. The second variation of the Allen–Cahn energy yields the stability inequality for the limit, which together with Allard’s regularity theorem gives smoothness away from a singular set of dimension at most n−8. Recent developments by Gaspar and Guaraco have used the Allen–Cahn min‑max construction to produce minimal hypersurfaces in generic metrics, providing an alternative to the Almgren–Pitts theory. The phase‑field approach also extends to the free‑boundary problem, where the Neumann condition ∂ν u = 0 on ∂M leads to convergence to free‑boundary minimal hypersurfaces meeting the boundary orthogonally.

The Colding–Minicozzi Theory of Minimal Surfaces in Three‑Manifolds

Tobias Colding and William Minicozzi developed a comprehensive structure theory for embedded minimal surfaces of fixed genus in a closed three‑manifold. Their work shows that a sequence of such surfaces with uniformly bounded area and genus either converges smoothly to a minimal lamination or develops a finite collection of curvature blow‑up points. At each blow‑up point the rescaled surfaces converge to a complete, properly embedded minimal surface in Euclidean space with finite total curvature. The classification of these limit surfaces — planes, catenoids, helicoids, and the Riemann examples — provides a precise description of the local models for degeneration. A key tool is the one‑sided curvature estimate, which asserts that if a minimal disk lies on one side of a plane and its boundary is far from the plane, then the curvature at the center is controlled by the distance to the boundary. This estimate yields a local graphical representation away from the blow‑up set and underpins the compactness theorem for the space of minimal surfaces with bounded genus and area. The theory also produces a foliation of the complement of the singular set by stable minimal surfaces, leading to a canonical decomposition of the ambient three‑manifold into regions foliated by minimal surfaces and regions where the minimal surfaces collapse. Applications include a proof of the Freedman–Hass–Scott conjecture on the existence of least‑area representatives for homotopy classes of surfaces, and a new approach to the topology of three‑manifolds via the min‑max construction of minimal surfaces of arbitrary genus.

The De Lellis–Spadaro Regularity Theory for Area‑Minimizing Integral Currents

Camillo De Lellis and Emanuele Spadaro gave a streamlined proof of Almgren’s partial regularity theorem for area‑minimizing integral currents of arbitrary codimension. Their approach replaces the original center‑manifold construction with a simpler “multiple‑valued function” approximation that is built directly on the current’s support. The key steps are: (1) a height bound showing that the current stays close to its tangent plane at most points; (2) a Lipschitz approximation by a multiple‑valued function whose graph approximates the current in the flat norm; (3) a harmonic approximation lemma that upgrades the Lipschitz map to a Dirichlet‑minimizing multiple‑valued function; (4) a frequency function monotonicity argument à la Almgren that controls the order of contact between the current and its tangent planes; (5) a blow‑up analysis that shows any tangent cone is a union of planes with integer multiplicities, and the singular set has Hausdorff dimension at most m−2 for an m‑dimensional current. The proof avoids the heavy combinatorial machinery of Almgren’s original center manifold and works uniformly for all codimensions. As a corollary, the regular set is open and dense in the support, and the singular set is countably (m−2)‑rectifiable. This theory has been extended to the setting of metric currents and to the regularity of minimizers of elliptic integrands with p‑growth.

The Brendle Proof of the Lawson Conjecture for Minimal Tori in the Three‑Sphere

Simon Brendle resolved the Lawson conjecture by proving that the only embedded minimal torus in the round three‑sphere S³ is the Clifford torus S¹(1/√2) × S¹(1/√2). The proof uses the min‑max theory of Marques–Neves to produce a minimal torus in each isotopy class, then applies a sharp eigenvalue estimate for the Jacobi operator on a minimal torus. Brendle shows that the first eigenvalue of the stability operator is exactly 2, and the corresponding eigenfunctions are restrictions of the coordinate functions of the ambient S³. By integrating the Simons identity and using the fact that the second fundamental form satisfies a certain elliptic inequality, he deduces that the squared norm of the second fundamental form is constant. The constancy forces the torus to be isoparametric, and the classification of isoparametric hypersurfaces in spheres yields the Clifford torus as the unique embedded example. The argument also gives a new proof of the Willmore conjecture for tori in S³, because the Willmore energy of a minimal torus in S³ equals its area, and the Clifford torus minimizes area among all minimal tori. Brendle’s method has been adapted to classify minimal tori in other homogeneous three‑manifolds, such as the Berger spheres.

The Hamilton–Shi Estimates for Ricci Flow on Noncompact Manifolds

Richard Hamilton and later Wan‑Xiong Shi derived interior derivative estimates for solutions of the Ricci flow on complete noncompact manifolds with bounded curvature. The Shi estimates state that if |Rm| ≤ K on a parabolic cylinder P(x₀, t₀, r, r²) then for any integer m ≥ 1 there exists a constant C(m, n) such that |∇^m Rm| ≤ C(m, n) K(m+2)/2 on the smaller cylinder P(x₀, t₀, r/2, r²/4). The proof proceeds by induction on m, using a localized version of the maximum principle applied to the evolution equation for |∇^m Rm|² with a carefully chosen cut‑off function in space and time. These estimates are essential for the compactness theory of Ricci flows on noncompact manifolds: they give pre‑compactness in the C^∞ topology for sequences of flows with uniform curvature bounds, enabling the construction of singularity models (κ‑solutions) in the noncompact setting. Shi’s estimates also underpin the short‑time existence theorem for Ricci flow on complete manifolds with bounded curvature, proved by Shi in 1989, and the pseudolocality theorem of Perelman, which asserts that curvature cannot become large too quickly in regions that are initially almost Euclidean.

The Gromov h‑Principle and Its Applications to Minimal Submanifolds

Mikhail Gromov’s h‑principle (homotopy principle) provides a flexible framework for solving partial differential relations that are “ample” in the sense of having many formal solutions. For minimal submanifolds, the relevant relation is the vanishing of the mean curvature vector. While the minimal surface equation is elliptic and not directly amenable to the h‑principle, Gromov showed that the space of formal minimal immersions — immersions whose differential maps the tangent bundle into the Grassmannian of minimal planes — satisfies the h‑principle in high codimension. Concretely, if the codimension is at least 3, any formal minimal immersion of an open manifold can be homotoped through formal minimal immersions to a genuine minimal immersion. This yields existence results for minimal submanifolds in prescribed homotopy classes when the target manifold has sufficient symmetry (e.g., Euclidean space or the sphere). The h‑principle also underlies the Nash–Kuiper C¹ isometric embedding theorem, which can be viewed as a minimal‑type problem for the second fundamental form. In the context of calibrated geometry, the h‑principle explains why special Lagrangian submanifolds in Calabi–Yau manifolds are abundant in high dimension: the calibration form defines a differential relation that is ample, so formal solutions can be deformed to genuine calibrated submanifolds. The Minimal Surface Equation in Lorentzian Manifolds and the Cauchy Problem

The minimal surface equation in a Lorentzian manifold (M,g) of dimension n+1 reads divg(∇u/√(1−|∇u|2)) = 0 for a spacelike graph u: Σ → ℝ, where Σ is a spacelike hypersurface. Unlike the Riemannian case, the equation is hyperbolic when |∇u|g < 1 and becomes degenerate on the light cone. The Cauchy problem asks for a solution given initial data (u, ∂tu) on a spacelike Cauchy hypersurface Σ0. In 1979 Choquet‑Bruhat and Geroch proved local existence and uniqueness for analytic data using the Cauchy‑Kovalevskaya theorem. For smooth data, the breakthrough came in 1990 when Lars Andersson and Vincent Moncrief established a well‑posedness result in Sobolev spaces Hs with s > n/2 + 1 by reducing the problem to a symmetric hyperbolic system after a suitable gauge fixing (the maximal slicing condition). Their energy estimates rely on the null structure of the nonlinearity, which yields a gain of one derivative compared to a generic quasilinear wave equation. Later, in 2003, Sergiu Klainerman and Igor Rodnianski introduced the vector‑field method to obtain global existence for small perturbations of the Minkowski plane, showing that the maximal development is future‑complete and asymptotically flat. The Lorentzian minimal surface equation also appears in the study of marginally outer trapped surfaces (MOTS) in general relativity; the stability operator coincides with the linearisation of the null expansion, linking the Cauchy problem to the Penrose inequality. Recent work by Hubert Bray and Marcus Khuri (2011) uses the Jang equation, a prescribed mean curvature equation in a Riemannian manifold, to reduce the positive mass theorem to a minimal surface problem in a Lorentzian product, illustrating the deep interplay between elliptic and hyperbolic minimal surface theories.

The Role of the Lichnerowicz Laplacian in the Yamabe Problem

The Yamabe problem seeks a conformal metric g̃ = u4/(n−2) g of constant scalar curvature on a closed Riemannian manifold (Mn, g), n ≥ 3. The Euler‑Lagrange equation is the Yamabe equation −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δg u + Rg u = λ u(n+2)/(n−2). Linearising around a constant solution u ≡ 1 yields the operator L = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δg + Rg − λ (n+2)/(n−2). When the background metric is Einstein, Rg = n(n−1)κ, the operator simplifies to a multiple of the Lichnerowicz Laplacian acting on functions, ΔL = Δg + 2κ. The spectrum of ΔL controls the stability of the Yamabe metric: if the first non‑zero eigenvalue μ1 of ΔL satisfies μ1 > 0, the constant scalar curvature metric is a local minimiser of the Yamabe functional. In 1984, Obata proved that on the standard sphere (Sn, ground) the kernel of ΔL consists exactly of the first spherical harmonics, reflecting the conformal Killing fields. This spectral gap is the analytic heart of the solution of the Yamabe problem on locally conformally flat manifolds by Schoen (1984) using the positive mass theorem. For non‑locally conformally flat manifolds, the Lichnerowicz Laplacian appears in the second variation formula for the Yamabe functional, and its positivity is equivalent to the non‑existence of non‑trivial conformal Killing vectors. In the Kähler setting, the Lichnerowicz Laplacian on (1,1)‑forms governs the stability of Kähler–Einstein metrics, linking the Yamabe problem to the Calabi–Yau theorem. Recent developments by Gursky and Viaclovsky (2007) use the fully nonlinear σk‑Yamabe problem, where the linearised operator is a generalised Lichnerowicz Laplacian involving the Schouten tensor, extending the spectral analysis to higher order curvature invariants.

The positivity of the next eigenvalue (n+2) guarantees that the round metric is a strict local minimiser of the Yamabe functional, confirming the stability of the constant scalar curvature metric on Sn.

The Convergence of the Ricci Flow on Surfaces and the Uniformization Theorem

On a closed surface Σ2 the Ricci flow ∂tg = −2K g reduces to a scalar equation for the conformal factor u(t) defined by g(t) = e2u(t) g0. The evolution becomes ∂tu = Δg0 u + K0 − e−2u K(t), where K(t) is the Gauss curvature of g(t). In 1988 Richard Hamilton proved that for any initial metric the solution exists for all time and converges exponentially fast to a constant curvature metric after normalising the area. The proof uses the maximum principle to obtain uniform bounds on u and its derivatives, and the entropy functional ∫ (|∇u|2 + 2K0u) dμ0 which is monotone decreasing. In 1991 Bennett Chow gave an alternative proof using the isoperimetric profile and the logarithmic Sobolev inequality, establishing convergence without the need for a priori curvature bounds. The limit metric has constant curvature equal to the average of the initial curvature, which by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem is 2π χ(Σ)/Area. Thus the Ricci flow provides a nonlinear parabolic proof of the Uniformization Theorem: every closed Riemann surface admits a metric of constant curvature −1, 0, or 1 according to its Euler characteristic. For non‑compact complete surfaces with bounded curvature, the flow was extended by Shi (1989) and later by Chen–Zhu (2006) who proved long‑time existence and convergence to a complete constant curvature metric under a finite topology assumption. The surface case is the only dimension where the Ricci flow is a scalar equation, making it a model for understanding singularity formation and surgery in higher dimensions. The techniques developed — maximum principle, entropy monotonicity, and the use of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem — are prototypes for Perelman’s entropy and reduced volume in three dimensions.

The Classification of Ancient Solutions to the Mean Curvature Flow in Higher Dimensions

An ancient solution to the mean curvature flow (MCF) is a family of hypersurfaces Mt ⊂ ℝn+1, t ∈ (−∞, T), satisfying ∂tx = −H ν. Classification of such solutions is central to understanding singularities. In 1995 Huisken proved that any convex ancient solution in ℝn+1 is either a shrinking sphere, a shrinking cylinder Sk × ℝn−k (1 ≤ k ≤ n−1), or a translating bowl soliton. The proof uses Huisken’s monotonicity formula and the classification of self‑similar solutions. In 2003, Colding and Minicozzi introduced the entropy functional λ(M) = supx₀,t₀ (4π t₀)−n/2 ∫M e−|x−x₀|2/4t₀ dμ, showing that low entropy forces the solution to be a sphere or cylinder. In 2015, Brendle and Choi proved that any ancient, non‑collapsed, mean‑convex solution in ℝ3 is either a sphere, a cylinder, or the translating bowl. Their argument combines the Andrews–Bryan curvature estimate with a delicate analysis of the asymptotic ratio of principal curvatures. For higher dimensions, the classification remains open beyond the convex case. In 2019, Haslhofer and Hershkovits established a local regularity theorem for ancient solutions with a cylindrical singularity, proving that the flow is smoothly modelled on the shrinking cylinder near the singular set. Recent work by Angenent, Daskalopoulos, and Sesum (2021) constructed new ancient solutions in ℝ4 that are not self‑similar, using a gluing technique between a cylinder and a translating soliton. These examples show that the landscape of ancient solutions is richer than previously thought, and the entropy functional alone does not capture all possibilities. The classification problem is directly linked to the mean curvature flow with surgery, where the canonical neighbourhood theorem requires a complete list of ancient κ‑solutions.

The Geometric Measure Theory Approach to the Plateau Problem in Metric Spaces

The classical Plateau problem asks for a surface of least area spanning a given Jordan curve in ℝ3. In the 1930s Douglas and Radó solved it using parametric methods. In the 1960s Federer and Fleming developed the theory of integral currents, providing a compactness theorem for sequences of currents with bounded mass and boundary mass. This framework was extended by Ambrosio and Kirchheim (2000) to complete metric spaces (X, d) satisfying a quadratic isoperimetric inequality. An integral current in a metric space is defined via a tuple (T, π1, …, πk) of Lipschitz functions, and its mass is the k‑dimensional Hausdorff measure of its support. The compactness theorem states that a sequence of integral k‑currents with uniformly bounded mass and boundary mass has a subsequence converging in the flat norm to an integral current. Using this, one can solve the Plateau problem for a given Lipschitz cycle in any proper metric space with a quadratic isoperimetric inequality: minimise mass among all integral currents with the prescribed boundary. The existence of a mass‑minimising current follows from the direct method of the calculus of variations, lower semicontinuity of mass, and the compactness theorem. Regularity theory in this generality is delicate; in 2011, De Lellis and Spadaro proved that an area‑minimising integral current in a smooth Riemannian manifold is regular except on a singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most k−2. Their proof adapts Almgren’s big regularity paper to the metric‑space setting by using the monotonicity formula for the density and a blow‑up analysis that yields tangent cones which are area‑minimising cones in Euclidean space. In 2018, Wenger extended the existence theory to metric spaces with a (1+ε)‑isoperimetric inequality, showing that the Plateau problem is solvable in a broad class of Carnot groups. The geometric measure theory approach thus unifies the Plateau problem across Riemannian manifolds, metric spaces, and sub‑Riemannian geometries, and provides the foundation for the Almgren–Pitts min‑max theory in non‑smooth settings.

Canonical Neighborhoods and the Thick‑Thin Decomposition in Ricci Flow Surgery

Perelman’s 2002‑2003 papers introduced a precise description of the high‑curvature regions that appear in a Ricci flow on a closed three‑manifold. The canonical neighborhood theorem states that for any point where the scalar curvature exceeds a large threshold r−2, the pointed solution is ε‑close (after parabolic rescaling) to one of a short list of model geometries: a shrinking round sphere S3, a shrinking round cylinder S2×ℝ, a Bryant steady soliton, or an ε‑neck (a region diffeomorphic to S2×(−ε−1,ε−1) with metric close to the product of a round sphere and a line). The parameters ε, r are chosen so that ε≪1 and r≪1, and the theorem holds for all times up to the first singular time.

The thick‑thin decomposition partitions the manifold at a given time into a thick part where the curvature is bounded by r−2 and a thin part consisting of a disjoint union of ε‑necks and ε‑caps. An ε‑cap is a region that is either a 3‑ball or a punctured ℝℙ3 and is bounded by an ε‑neck. The thick part has uniformly bounded geometry, so the flow can be continued smoothly there. In the thin part, each ε‑neck is surgically removed by cutting along the central S2 and gluing in standard caps (the standard solution of Hamilton). The surgery parameters δ (the curvature scale at which surgery is performed) and h (the depth of the neck) satisfy δ≪h≪r. After surgery the flow is restarted with the same Ricci flow equation, and the canonical neighborhood theorem is re‑verified at the new time slice.

This decomposition is the engine of the Ricci flow with surgery. It guarantees that the only singularities that can form are modeled on the shrinking cylinder, and that the surgery removes them without creating new high‑curvature regions. The thick part provides the compactness needed to extract a limit flow after infinitely many surgeries, leading to the geometrization of three‑manifolds. The argument also uses Perelman’s κ‑non‑collapsing theorem: any region with curvature bounded by r−2 has volume at least κr3, which prevents the formation of degenerate necks. The classification of ancient κ‑solutions (shrinking sphere, shrinking cylinder, Bryant soliton) is then used to show that every high‑curvature region is indeed an ε‑neck or a cap.

Hardt‑Simon Regularity for Area‑Minimizing Currents in Higher Codimension

In 1979 Hardt and Simon proved a landmark regularity theorem for area‑minimizing integral currents of arbitrary codimension. Let T be an m‑dimensional integral current in a smooth Riemannian manifold Nn that minimizes mass among all currents with the same boundary. Their theorem asserts that the singular set sing(T) has Hausdorff dimension at most m−2, and that the regular part reg(T) is a smooth embedded submanifold. Moreover, the singular set is countably (m−2)‑rectifiable, meaning it can be covered, up to a set of measure zero, by countably many C1 submanifolds of dimension m−2.

The proof adapts Almgren’s frequency function to the higher‑codimension setting. For a point p in the support of T, one considers the blow‑up sequence Tr = (ηp,r)#T, where ηp,r(x) = (x−p)/r. Monotonicity of the density Θ(T,p,r) = mass(T∩Br(p))/ωmrm implies that any tangent cone C = limr→0 Tr is an area‑minimizing cone in ℝn. Hardt and Simon showed that if a tangent cone is a multiplicity‑one plane, then p is a regular point. The singular set consists precisely of points where every tangent cone has multiplicity ≥2 or is a non‑flat cone. Using a dimension‑reduction argument (Federer’s reduction principle) they proved that the set of such points has dimension ≤ m−2.

Later work by Almgren (2000) and De Lellis‑Spadaro (2011) refined the estimate, showing that the singular set is actually (m−2)‑rectifiable and that the tangent cones are unique at almost every singular point. The Hardt‑Simon theorem is the foundation for the regularity theory of calibrated submanifolds (e.g., complex analytic varieties, special Lagrangian cycles) and for the compactness theory of stationary varifolds in geometric measure theory.

The Kähler‑Ricci Flow on Manifolds of General Type and the Canonical Model

Let X be a smooth projective variety of dimension n with Kodaira dimension κ(X) = n (i.e., of general type). The Kähler‑Ricci flow ∂tω = −Ric(ω) − ω, starting from a Kähler metric ω0 in the class c1(KX), exists for all t ≥ 0 (Cao 1985). As t → ∞, the metrics ω(t) develop singularities along the canonical divisor KX. The flow converges in the sense of currents to a closed positive (1,1)‑current ω∞ which is a Kähler‑Einstein metric on the canonical model Xcan = Proj ⊕m≥0 H0(X, mKX).

The convergence was established by Tsuji (2007), Tian‑Zhang (2006), and Song‑Tian (2012). They proved that ω(t) converges smoothly on the complement of the null locus Null(KX) = ∩m≥1 Bs|mKX|, and that the limiting current ω∞ has bounded local potentials. The canonical model Xcan has at worst canonical singularities, and ω∞ is a singular Kähler‑Einstein metric with Ricci curvature −ω∞ in the sense of currents. The flow thus provides an analytic realization of the Minimal Model Program: the canonical model is the unique minimal model of X, and the Kähler‑Ricci flow performs the contractions of the extremal rays of the Mori cone.

Recent work by Guo‑Song‑Weinkove (2020) gave a precise description of the metric behavior near the singularities: the flow metrics are quasi‑isometric to the reference metric with a logarithmic pole along the exceptional divisors. This analytic picture matches the algebraic prediction that the canonical bundle becomes semi‑ample after finitely many flips and divisorial contractions. The Kähler‑Ricci flow on general type varieties is therefore a bridge between complex algebraic geometry and nonlinear PDE.

Sub‑Riemannian Minimal Surfaces in the Heisenberg Group

The first Heisenberg group ℍ1 is ℝ3 with coordinates (x,y,t) and group law (x,y,t)·(x′,y′,t′) = (x+x′, y+y′, t+t′+½(xy′−yx′)). The Regularity Theory for Minimal Hypersurfaces in Manifolds with Bounded Geometry

When a minimal hypersurface is studied inside a Riemannian manifold (M,g) whose sectional curvature is bounded and whose injectivity radius is positive, the classical regularity theory of De Giorgi, Almgren, and Simons extends with only minor modifications. The key tool is the monotonicity formula for the area ratio, which holds in any ambient manifold with a uniform lower bound on the Ricci curvature. In this setting the density Θ(x) = limr→0 Area(Σ∩B_r(x))/ω_n r^n exists for every point x∈Σ and equals 1 at regular points. Allard’s ε‑regularity theorem then gives a quantitative criterion: if the excess E(Σ,x,r) = r-n∫Σ∩B_r(x)|ν-ν0|2 is smaller than a universal constant ε(n), the surface is a C1,α graph over the tangent plane. The proof proceeds by a blow‑up argument that uses the bounded geometry to compare the minimal surface equation with the Euclidean minimal surface equation, yielding uniform C1,α estimates that are independent of the point. Higher regularity follows from Schauder theory applied to the linearised minimal surface operator, which is uniformly elliptic because the second fundamental form is bounded on the graph. Consequently, the singular set of an area‑minimizing hypersurface in a manifold with bounded geometry has Hausdorff dimension at most n‑7, exactly as in the Euclidean case. This result was sharpened by Schoen and Simon (1981) who proved that the singular set is (n‑7)‑rectifiable and carries a tangent cone structure at almost every point. The bounded geometry hypothesis is essential: if the injectivity radius collapses, the monotonicity formula acquires an error term that can prevent the ε‑regularity threshold from being reached, and singularities of higher dimension may appear, as illustrated by the Simons cone in a sequence of manifolds with shrinking necks.

The Convergence of the Ricci Flow on Three‑Manifolds with Positive Scalar Curvature

Hamilton’s 1982 theorem on the Ricci flow on closed three‑manifolds with positive Ricci curvature shows that the solution exists for all time and converges, after appropriate rescaling, to a metric of constant positive sectional curvature. The proof hinges on the maximum principle applied to the evolution equation for the curvature operator, which preserves the positivity of the curvature operator in dimension three. The pinching estimate Rijkl ≥ ε R gikgjl improves as t→∞, forcing the metric to become round. When only positive scalar curvature is assumed, the flow may develop singularities in finite time. Perelman’s breakthrough (2002‑2003) introduced the reduced volume and the κ‑noncollapsing theorem, which together with the canonical neighborhood theorem allow one to perform surgery at high‑curvature regions. After each surgery the manifold splits into components, each of which either becomes extinct in finite time or continues to flow with positive scalar curvature. The thick‑thin decomposition shows that the thick part converges to a hyperbolic metric (by Thurston’s geometrization) while the thin part consists of necks that are removed by surgery. The final outcome is a finite collection of geometric pieces: spherical space forms, S2×S1 factors, and hyperbolic manifolds. The convergence on each piece is smooth away from the surgery times, and the rescaled flow on the spherical components converges to the round metric. This program completes the proof of the geometrization conjecture and, as a corollary, the Poincaré conjecture. The analytic heart is the differential Harnack inequality for the Ricci flow, which gives precise control of the scalar curvature along space‑time curves and is used to rule out non‑compact ancient κ‑solutions other than the shrinking cylinder and the Bryant soliton.

The Role of Perelman’s Entropy in the Classification of Ancient Solutions

Hamilton’s differential Harnack inequality then shows that the limit splits off a line unless it is compact, which yields the cylinder. The Bryant soliton appears as the unique non‑compact, κ‑noncollapsed ancient solution with positive curvature operator that does not split. In higher dimensions the classification is incomplete, but the entropy method still provides the only known tool to rule out exotic ancient solutions. Perelman’s entropy also controls the formation of singularities in the Ricci flow with surgery: the canonical neighborhood theorem is proved by contradiction using the fact that a sequence of blow‑up limits would have constant 𝒲, hence be a gradient shrinking soliton, and the list of such solitons in dimension three is known.

The Minimal Surface Equation in Calibrated Geometry and Special Lagrangian Submanifolds

Calibrated geometry, introduced by Harvey and Lawson (1982), gives a powerful variational framework for minimal submanifolds. A calibration on a Riemannian manifold (M,g) is a closed p‑form φ such that φ|_ξ ≤ vol_ξ for every oriented p‑plane ξ. A submanifold Σ^p is calibrated by φ if φ|_Σ = vol_Σ; calibrated submanifolds are automatically volume‑minimizing in their homology class. The most celebrated example is the special Lagrangian calibration on a Calabi‑Yau n‑fold (X,ω,Ω): the real part Re(eiθΩ) calibrates n‑dimensional submanifolds that are Lagrangian (ω|_Σ = 0) and satisfy Im(eiθΩ)|_Σ = 0. The special Lagrangian equation is a fully nonlinear elliptic PDE for a graph of a function u: Σ → ℝ^n, namely Im(eiθ det(I + i D2 u)) = 0, where D2 u is the Hessian with respect to a flat background metric. This equation is the Euler‑Lagrange equation of the volume functional restricted to the Lagrangian condition. The regularity theory for special Lagrangian submanifolds parallels that of minimal surfaces: Harvey and Lawson proved that any calibrated submanifold is smooth, and later Yuan (2001) established a Bernstein‑type theorem stating that an entire special Lagrangian graph in ℂ^n with quadratic growth must be an affine plane. The calibrated approach also yields a natural compactness theorem: a sequence of calibrated submanifolds with uniformly bounded volume has a subsequence converging in the sense of currents to a calibrated limit. This compactness is crucial in the SYZ conjecture, where special Lagrangian torus fibrations are expected to degenerate by collapsing fibers, and the limiting current is a calibrated cycle. The interplay between the calibrated form and the minimal surface equation provides a bridge between complex geometry, mirror symmetry, and geometric analysis.

  • Assuming the ε‑regularity constant is dimension‑independent; it depends on n and the curvature bounds.
  • Confusing the reduced volume monotonicity with the ℱ‑functional monotonicity; they apply to different coupled systems.
  • Thinking every ancient κ‑solution in dimension three is a shrinking cylinder; the Bryant soliton is a steady soliton and also κ‑noncollapsed.
  • Believing calibrated submanifolds are always minimal without checking the closedness of the calibration form.
  • Applying the special Lagrangian equation to non‑Lagrangian graphs; the Lagrangian condition ω|_Σ = 0 is essential.
  • Overlooking the role of the injectivity radius in the monotonicity formula for minimal hypersurfaces in bounded geometry.
  • Treating the surgery times in Ricci flow as isolated singularities without invoking the canonical neighborhood theorem.

The Minimal Surface Equation on Manifolds with Density and the Gaussian Isoperimetric Problem

A weighted Riemannian manifold is a triple (M,g,e-fdV) where f∈C∞(M) is a density function. The weighted area of a hypersurface Σ is ∫Σ e-f dA. Critical points of this functional satisfy the weighted minimal surface equation Hf=0, where Hf=H-∇f·ν is the f‑mean curvature, H the classical mean curvature and ν a unit normal. This equation appears naturally in the study of self‑shrinkers for mean curvature flow (f=|x|2/4) and in probability, where the Gaussian space (ℝn,geucl,e-|x|2/2dx) is the canonical model.

The Gaussian isoperimetric problem asks for sets of given Gaussian measure that minimise Gaussian perimeter. The solution, proved independently by Borell (1975) and Sudakov–Tsirelson (1974), states that half‑spaces are the unique minimisers. In the language of weighted minimal surfaces, the boundary of a half‑space is a hyperplane through the origin, which satisfies Hf=0 because its classical mean curvature vanishes and ∇f is orthogonal to the hyperplane. The stability operator for the weighted area functional is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator Lf=Δ-∇f·∇, whose spectrum on the Gaussian space is explicitly known: the first non‑zero eigenvalue equals 1, giving a sharp Poincaré inequality.

Morgan (2003) extended the regularity theory for weighted minimal hypersurfaces: if the Bakry–Émery Ricci curvature Ricf=Ric+Hess f is bounded below, then singular sets have codimension at least 7, exactly as in the unweighted case. The proof adapts Allard’s ε‑regularity theorem by replacing the Euclidean monotonicity formula with the weighted monotonicity formula d/dr (r1-n∫Br e-f dV)≥0, which holds when Ricf≥0. This monotonicity is the key to blow‑up analysis and to the classification of tangent cones at singular points.

Applications include the proof of the Gaussian log‑Sobolev inequality via the Bakry–Émery Γ2 calculus, and the study of eternal solutions to the mean curvature flow that are asymptotic to self‑shrinkers. In the latter context, the weighted minimal surface equation appears as the stationary condition for the Gaussian area functional, and the classification of entire weighted minimal graphs in Gaussian space (the Bernstein problem for f=|x|2/4) remains open beyond dimension 2, where the only solutions are affine hyperplanes.

Examiners often test the distinction between the classical minimal surface equation and its weighted counterpart, the role of the Bakry–Émery Ricci tensor in regularity, and the use of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator in stability analysis. A typical trap is to forget that the density weight modifies both the first variation formula and the monotonicity formula, leading to incorrect conclusions about singular set dimension.

The Minimal Surface Equation in Metric Measure Spaces and the Cheeger Constant

Metric measure spaces (X,d,μ) satisfying the Riemannian curvature‑dimension condition RCD(K,N) provide a synthetic framework where many smooth results for minimal surfaces survive. In this setting, a set of finite perimeter E⊂X has a reduced boundary ∂*E which carries a measure‑theoretic normal and a notion of mean curvature defined via the first variation of the perimeter functional P(E)=|DχE|(X). The Cheeger constant h(X)=infE P(E)/min(μ(E),μ(XE)) captures the optimal isoperimetric profile.

Ambrosio (2001) proved that on RCD(K,N) spaces the perimeter functional is lower semicontinuous with respect to L1 convergence, and that sets of finite perimeter admit a rectifiable reduced boundary in the sense of De Giorgi. The first variation formula yields a generalized mean curvature vector HE∈L2(∂*E) satisfying ∫∂*E HE·φ dμ = d/dt|t=0 P(Φt(E)) for suitable deformations Φt. When HE=0 the set E is a minimal hypersurface in the synthetic sense.

The Cheeger constant is intimately linked to the first non‑zero eigenvalue λ1 of the Laplacian on X via Cheeger’s inequality λ1≥h(X)2/4. On smooth manifolds this is classical; on RCD spaces it was extended by Gigli (2012) using the heat flow and the Bakry–Émery calculus. Moreover, the equality case in Cheeger’s inequality characterises spaces that split off a line, a result that mirrors the smooth splitting theorem.

Regularity of synthetic minimal hypersurfaces has been developed by De Philippis–Marchese–Rindler (2017): on an RCD(K,N) space the reduced boundary of a perimeter‑minimising set is (N-1)‑rectifiable and has a tangent cone at μ‑a.e. point which is a half‑space. The proof uses the monotonicity of the perimeter density Θ(r)=r1-NP(E∩Br) and a blow‑up argument that relies on the Euclidean tangent cone structure of RCD spaces.

Exam questions may ask to state the definition of a set of finite perimeter in a metric measure space, to write the first variation formula for the perimeter, or to explain how the Cheeger constant gives a lower bound for the spectral gap. A common mistake is to assume that the reduced boundary is always a smooth hypersurface; in the synthetic setting it is only rectifiable and may have singularities of codimension ≥2.

Technique: First variation of weighted perimeter
Let (M,g,e-fdV) be a weighted manifold and Σ a smooth hypersurface with unit normal ν. Derive the first variation formula for the weighted area Af(Σ)=∫Σ e-f dA under a normal variation with speed φ.
Consider the flow Φt(x)=expx(t φ(x) ν(x)). The classical first variation gives d/dt|t=0 Area(Φt(Σ)) = -∫Σ H φ dA. The density contributes d/dt|t=0 ∫Σ e-f∘Φt dA = -∫Σ (∇f·ν) φ e-f dA. Adding both parts yields d/dt|t=0 Af(Φt(Σ)) = -∫Σ (H - ∇f·ν) φ e-f dA = -∫Σ Hf φ e-f dA. Hence the Euler‑Lagrange equation is Hf=0.

When a problem asks for a first variation in a weighted or metric‑measure setting, write down the unweighted variation first, then add the contribution from the density or the metric‑measure deformation. Keep the notation Hf for the weighted mean curvature; it makes the Euler‑Lagrange equation immediate and avoids sign errors.

The Minimal Surface Equation and the Minimal Surface Equation in the Context of the Minimal Surface Equation on Manifolds with a Parallel Calibration

Calibrated geometry provides a powerful tool to produce volume‑minimising submanifolds without solving the minimal surface equation directly. A calibration on a Riemannian manifold (M,g) is a closed p‑form φ such that φ|ξ ≤ volξ for every oriented p‑plane ξ. A submanifold Σp is calibrated by φ if φ|Σ = volΣ; then Σ is absolutely volume‑minimising in its homology class. The most celebrated examples are the Kähler form ω on a Kähler manifold (calibrating complex submanifolds) and the special Lagrangian calibration Re(eiθΩ) on a Calabi–Yau n‑fold.

When a parallel calibration exists (i.e. ∇φ=0), the calibrated submanifolds are automatically minimal because the first variation of volume vanishes identically. The proof uses Stokes’ theorem: for any compactly supported variation Σt with Σ0=Σ, d/dt|t=0 Vol(Σt) = d/dt|t=0 ∫Σt φ = ∫Σ LVφ = ∫Σ d(ιVφ) = 0, since φ is closed and Σ has no boundary. This argument shows that calibrated submanifolds are not only minimal but also stable, with Morse index zero.

The regularity theory for calibrated submanifolds parallels that of area‑minimising currents. Harvey and Lawson (1982) proved that any calibrated submanifold is smooth. Later, Yuan (2001) established a Bernstein‑type theorem: an entire special Lagrangian graph in ℂn with quadratic growth must be an affine plane. The proof uses the calibrated monotonicity formula and a blow‑up analysis that reduces to the classification of tangent cones, which are calibrated cones in ℂn.

Compactness of calibrated submanifolds follows from the Federer–Fleming compactness theorem for integral currents: a sequence of calibrated submanifolds with uniformly bounded volume has a subsequence converging in the sense of currents to a calibrated limit. This compactness is crucial in the SYZ conjecture, where special Lagrangian torus fibrations degenerate by collapsing fibres, and the limiting current is a calibrated cycle representing the dual torus fibration.

Examiners frequently test the definition of a calibration, the proof that calibrated submanifolds are volume‑minimising, and the distinction between a closed form and a parallel form. A common trap is to claim that any submanifold satisfying φ|Σ=volΣ is minimal without verifying that φ is closed; the closedness is essential for the Stokes argument. Another trap is to confuse the special Lagrangian equation Im(eiθdet(I+i D2u))=0 with the minimal surface equation for a general graph; the former includes the Lagrangian condition ω|Σ=0, which is not automatic.

  • Assuming the weighted monotonicity formula holds without a lower bound on the Bakry–Émery Ricci tensor.
  • Confusing the Cheeger constant of a metric measure space with the classical isoperimetric constant of a smooth manifold.
  • Believing that every set of finite perimeter in an RCD space has a smooth reduced boundary.
  • Forgetting that the calibration form must be closed, not merely pointwise bounded by the volume form.
  • Applying the special Lagrangian equation to graphs that are not Lagrangian.
  • Overlooking the role of the density weight in the first variation of the weighted area functional.
  • Treating the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator as the standard Laplacian when analysing stability of Gaussian minimal surfaces.

These advanced topics illustrate how geometric analysis extends classical minimal surface theory to weighted, synthetic, and calibrated settings, each with its own monotonicity formulas, regularity theorems, and compactness principles.

The Yamabe Problem on Manifolds with Negative Scalar Curvature

When the Yamabe invariant Y(M,[g]) is negative, the Yamabe functional is coercive on the conformal class [g]. This coercivity eliminates the loss of compactness that plagues the positive case and guarantees the existence of a minimiser for the Yamabe quotient. Aubin (1976) and Schoen (1984) showed that on a compact manifold of dimension n ≥ 3 with Y(M,[g]) < 0 there is a unique (up to constant scaling) metric of constant negative scalar curvature in the conformal class. The proof proceeds by minimising the functional

Q(u) = (∫M (4(n−1)/(n−2) |∇u|2 + Rg u2) dvolg) / (∫M |u|2n/(n−2) dvolg)(n−2)/n

over the positive cone of H1(M). The Euler–Lagrange equation is the Yamabe equation

−4(n−1)/(n−2) Δg u + Rg u = λ u(n+2)/(n−2)

with λ = Y(M,[g]) < 0. The maximum principle forces any positive solution to be unique up to a multiplicative constant, and the constant is fixed by normalising the L2n/(n−2) norm. A standard trap is to assume that the negative case is “trivial” because the functional is bounded below; one must still verify the Palais–Smale condition, which holds automatically here due to the sign of λ. Examiners often ask to contrast the negative case with the positive case where the best constant is the Sobolev constant of the sphere and bubbling can occur. Another common twist is to ask for the behaviour of the Yamabe metric under a conformal change that preserves the sign of the scalar curvature; the answer uses the transformation law for the scalar curvature and the fact that the Yamabe invariant is a conformal invariant.

The Yamabe Problem on Metric Measure Spaces and the Bakry‑Émery Ricci Curvature

On a smooth weighted manifold (M,g,e−f dvol) the Bakry‑Émery Ricci tensor Ricf = Ric + Hess f replaces the classical Ricci tensor in many comparison theorems. The weighted Yamabe constant is defined by

Yf(M) = infu∈C∞c(M), u≠0 (∫M (4(n−1)/(n−2) |∇u|2 + Rf u2) e−f dvol) / (∫M |u|2n/(n−2) e−f dvol)(n−2)/n

where Rf = R + 2Δf − |∇f|2 is the weighted scalar curvature. Qian (1997) and later Zhang–Zhu (2010) proved that if Ricf ≥ (n−1)K g for some K ∈ ℝ and the weighted manifold satisfies a suitable Sobolev inequality, then the infimum is attained by a positive smooth function solving the weighted Yamabe equation

−4(n−1)/(n−2) Δf u + Rf u = Yf u(n+2)/(n−2)

with Δf = Δ − ∇f·∇. The proof adapts the concentration‑compactness principle to the weighted setting; the key is a weighted Trudinger‑Moser inequality that prevents loss of mass at infinity. A frequent examiner trap is to confuse the weighted Yamabe constant with the classical one when f is not constant; the two coincide only if f is harmonic and the manifold is Einstein. Another twist asks for the effect of a conformal change g̃ = u4/(n−2) g on the Bakry‑Émery tensor; the transformation law involves both u and f and is essential for verifying that the weighted Yamabe invariant is conformally invariant.

Weighted Sobolev Inequality Application
Let (M,g,e−f dvol) be a compact weighted manifold with Ricf ≥ 0. Show that the weighted Yamabe constant Yf(M) is attained by a smooth positive minimiser.
Because M is compact, the weighted Sobolev embedding H1(M,e−f dvol) L2n/(n−2)(M,e−f dvol) is compact. The functional Qf(u) is bounded below by the weighted Sobolev constant. Take a minimising sequence {uk} with ‖uk‖L2n/(n−2) = 1. By the compact embedding, a subsequence converges strongly in L2n/(n−2) and weakly in H1 to some u ≥ 0. Lower semicontinuity of the Dirichlet term gives Qf(u) ≤ liminf Qf(uk) = Yf(M). The strong convergence forces ‖u‖L2n/(n−2) = 1, so u is a minimiser. Elliptic regularity for the weighted Laplacian Δf then yields u ∈ C∞(M) and u > 0 by the strong maximum principle.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for existence of a minimiser on a weighted manifold, first state the weighted Sobolev embedding and its compactness (compact domain or suitable decay at infinity). Then write the Euler–Lagrange equation with Δf and Rf. Cite the Bakry‑Émery lower bound to invoke the maximum principle for positivity. This three‑step template works for both the classical and weighted Yamabe problems.

The Yamabe Problem on Orbifolds and Conical Singularities

An orbifold is a space locally modelled on ℝn/Γ with Γ a finite group. The Yamabe problem on orbifolds asks for a metric of constant scalar curvature in a given conformal class, where the conformal factor is allowed to have prescribed singular behaviour at the orbifold points. Akutagawa (2003) and later Botvinnik–Rosenberg (2010) developed a blow‑up analysis near a conical singularity of angle 2π/|Γ|. They introduced weighted Hölder spaces Ck,αδ with weight δ chosen to avoid indicial roots of the conformal Laplacian on the cone. The orbifold Yamabe invariant Yorb(M) is defined by the same quotient as in the smooth case but with integrals taken over the regular part. The main theorem states: if Yorb(M) < Y(Sn) then there exists a smooth orbifold metric of constant scalar curvature in the conformal class. The proof uses a minimising sequence and a concentration‑compactness argument adapted to the stratified space; bubbling can only occur at the singular strata, and the energy quantisation shows that each bubble carries at least Y(Sn) energy, which is excluded by the strict inequality. A common trap is to treat the orbifold as a smooth manifold with boundary and apply the Escobar boundary Yamabe problem; the correct approach uses the orbifold covering and the fact that the conformal Laplacian on the cone has a discrete spectrum. Examiners may ask to compute the indicial roots for the cone ℝn/ℤk and to explain why the weight δ must lie between the first two roots to obtain Fredholm properties.

The Yamabe Problem on Noncompact Manifolds with Asymptotically Conical Ends

Let (M,g) be a complete noncompact manifold that outside a compact set is diffeomorphic to (R,∞) × Σ with metric dr2 + r2 h + lower order terms, where (Σ,h) is a compact (n−1)-manifold. Such manifolds are called asymptotically conical (AC). The Yamabe problem on AC manifolds seeks a conformal factor u → 1 at infinity such that the scalar curvature of ũ = u4/(n−2) g is constant. Mazzeo (1991) and later Pacard–Xu (2008) analysed the linearised operator L = −4(n−1)/(n−2) Δg + Rg on weighted Hölder spaces Ck,αδ. The indicial roots of L on the exact cone are λ± = −(n−2)/2 ± √((n−2)2/4 + μ), where μ are eigenvalues of the spherical Laplacian on Σ. Choosing a weight δ between the first negative and first positive indicial root makes L Fredholm of index zero. The existence theorem: if the Yamabe invariant of the conformal class (defined via a suitable Sobolev quotient on the AC manifold) is less than the Yamabe invariant of the cone, then a constant scalar curvature metric exists with the prescribed asymptotics. The proof uses a continuity method combined with a priori estimates derived from the maximum principle on the cone. A typical examiner twist is to ask why the weight δ cannot be taken arbitrarily large; the answer is that the mapping properties of L fail when δ crosses an indicial root, leading to loss of surjectivity. Another trap is to confuse the AC Yamabe problem with the asymptotically hyperbolic (AH) Yamabe problem, where the conformal infinity is a compact manifold and the Fefferman‑Graham expansion governs the asymptotics.

The Yamabe Problem on Conformally Compact Manifolds and the Fefferman‑Graham Expansion

A conformally compact manifold (X,g) is the interior of a compact manifold with boundary ∂X = M such that g = ρ−2 ḡ for a defining function ρ and a metric ḡ smooth up to the boundary. The induced conformal class [ḡ|TM] on M is the conformal infinity. The Yamabe problem on X asks for a metric g̃ = u4/(n−2) g of constant scalar curvature −n(n−1) (i.e. Einstein with negative cosmological constant) that is also conformally compact with the same conformal infinity. Fefferman and Graham (1985) showed that for any representative of the conformal infinity there is a formal power series expansion of ḡ in even powers of ρ (plus a log term when n is even) that solves the Einstein equation to infinite order. The existence of a true solution was proved by Andersson–Chruściel (2005) and Mazzeo (1991) using the continuity method on weighted Hölder spaces. The key analytic ingredient is the invertibility of the linearised Einstein operator on the model hyperbolic space, which reduces to the invertibility of the conformal Laplacian on the boundary. The boundary Yamabe invariant Y(∂X) appears as an obstruction: if Y(∂X) > 0 then there is a unique solution; if Y(∂X) ≤ 0 the problem may have no solution or multiple solutions. Examiners often test the relationship between the bulk Yamabe constant of X and the boundary Yamabe constant of ∂X, and they may ask to derive the first few terms of the Fefferman‑Graham expansion for a given boundary metric. A common trap is to assume that the log term in the expansion always appears; it appears only when n is even and the obstruction tensor (the Fefferman‑Graham obstruction) does not vanish.

  • Assuming the negative Yamabe case is automatically compact without checking the Palais–Smale condition.
  • Confusing the weighted Yamabe constant with the classical one when the weight f is non‑constant.
  • Treating orbifold singularities as smooth boundary components and applying the Escobar problem.
  • Choosing a weight δ that crosses an indicial root in the AC Yamabe problem, destroying Fredholmness.
  • Believing the Fefferman‑Graham log term appears for all dimensions; it appears only in even dimensions with non‑vanishing obstruction tensor.
  • Neglecting the strict inequality Yorb(M) < Y(Sn) when invoking the orbifold existence theorem.
  • Overlooking the conformal invariance of the weighted Yamabe invariant under the combined transformation of g and f.

These advanced topics illustrate how the Yamabe problem extends to singular, weighted, noncompact, and conformally compact settings, each requiring a tailored functional‑analytic framework and a precise understanding of the underlying geometric invariants.
