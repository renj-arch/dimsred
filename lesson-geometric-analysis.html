Geometric Analysis — Minimal Surfaces, the Plateau Problem & the Yamabe Problem

Geometric analysis is the application of partial differential equations to problems in differential geometry. A minimal surface is a critical point of the area functional A(Σ) = ∫_Σ dA, satisfying mean curvature H = 0 everywhere. The Plateau problem — find a surface of minimal area spanning a given boundary curve — was solved independently by Douglas and Rado (1931) using the Dirichlet energy. The Bernstein conjecture stated that the only complete minimal graphs in ℝ³ are planes; Bombieri-De Giorgi-Giusti (1969) found counterexamples in ℝ⁸. Harmonic maps are energy-minimizing maps between Riemannian manifolds; the Eells-Sampson theorem (1964) guarantees existence in negative-curvature targets. The Yamabe problem — existence of a constant scalar curvature metric in a conformal class — was solved by Trudinger, Aubin, and Schoen using the conformal Laplacian and the positive mass theorem. The Ricci flow ∂g/∂t = −2 Ric(g), introduced by Hamilton and used by Perelman to prove the Poincaré conjecture, evolves metrics to improve their curvature. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem has an analytic proof via the heat kernel. The Seiberg-Witten equations and Ginzburg-Landau vortices are gauge-theoretic PDEs central to low-dimensional topology and superconductivity.

"Geometric analysis is the art of proving existence by finding minimizers."
— Richard Schoen
Part 1

📏 Minimal Surfaces and the Plateau Problem

Minimal Surfaces
The Area Functional, Mean Curvature Zero, and the Plateau Problem
A minimal surface is a critical point of the area functional A(Σ) = ∫_Σ dA. The first variation gives mean curvature H = 0 — the Euler-Lagrange equation. For a graph z = f(x,y), the minimal surface equation is (1+|∇f|²)Δf − f_i f_j f_{ij} = 0 — a quasilinear elliptic PDE. The Plateau problem: given a closed curve Γ ⊂ ℝ³, find a surface Σ of minimal area with ∂Σ = Γ. Douglas and Rado (1931) solved it by minimizing the Dirichlet energy ∫_D |∇u|² over maps u: D → ℝ³ with u|_∂D a parametrization of Γ. The solution is a branched minimal surface — conformal and harmonic in the interior. The interior regularity theorem: any two-dimensional minimizer is smooth in the interior. The boundary regularity: the surface meets Γ at right angles if Γ is smooth. The monotonicity formula: for a minimal surface Σ, the area in a ball A(Σ ∩ B_r(p)) ≥ πr², with equality iff Σ is a plane. The Bernstein conjecture: the only complete minimal graphs in ℝⁿ for n ≤ 7 are planes; for n ≥ 8, there exist counterexamples (Bombieri-De Giorgi-Giusti). The Weierstrass-Enneper representation gives explicit parametrizations of minimal surfaces in ℝ³ via holomorphic data.
Minimal surfaces balance tension everywhere; the Plateau problem shows that every closed curve bounds at least one surface of least area, a triumph of the calculus of variations.
Part 2

🌍 Harmonic Maps and the Eells-Sampson Theorem

Harmonic Maps
The Energy Functional, Tension Field, and the Heat Flow Method
A map f: M → N between Riemannian manifolds is harmonic if it is a critical point of the energy functional E(f) = ½∫_M |df|² dV_M. The Euler-Lagrange equation is τ(f) = trace ∇df = 0, where τ(f) is the tension field. In local coordinates, τᵅ(f) = Δfᵅ + Γ_{βγ}^{α}(f) ⟨∇fᵝ, ∇fᵞ⟩ = 0 — a semilinear elliptic system. The Eells-Sampson theorem (1964): if M is compact and N has nonpositive sectional curvature, then any continuous map f₀: M → N can be deformed to a harmonic map, unique in its homotopy class. The proof uses the harmonic map heat flow: ∂_t f = τ(f). The flow exists for all time and converges to a harmonic map as t → ∞. The Bochner formula: Δ|df|² = 2|∇df|² + 2⟨Ricᵐ(df), df⟩ − 2⟨Rᴺ(df, df)df, df⟩, which gives vanishing results for harmonic maps from positively curved domains into negatively curved targets. The Lichnerowicz-Obata theorem: on an Einstein manifold with Ric ≥ (n−1)g, the first nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplacian satisfies λ₁ ≥ n, with equality iff M is the sphere. Uhlenbeck's analysis of the Yang-Mills equations uses similar gauge-fixing and compactness methods to study critical points of the Yang-Mills functional ∫ |F_A|².
Harmonic maps generalize geodesics to higher dimensions; the heat flow method deforms any map into an energy minimizer when the target is negatively curved.
Part 3

🧮 The Yamabe Problem

Yamabe
Conformal Metrics, the Yamabe Equation, and the Positive Mass Theorem
The Yamabe problem: given a compact Riemannian manifold (M, g), find a metric ̃g = u^{4/(n−2)}g conformal to g with constant scalar curvature R_{̃g} = constant. Under conformal change, the scalar curvature transforms via the conformal Laplacian L_g = Δ_g + (n−2)/(4(n−1))R_g, giving the Yamabe equation: L_g u = (n−2)/(4(n−1)) c u^{(n+2)/(n−2)}. Equivalently, one minimizes the Yamabe functional Q_g(u) = ∫ (|∇u|² + (n−2)/(4(n−1)) R_g u²) dV_g / (∫ u^{2n/(n−2)} dV_g)^{(n−2)/n}. The Yamabe constant Y(M, [g]) = inf_{u>0} Q_g(u). Trudinger (1968) proved existence when Q_g ≥ 0; Aubin (1976) showed Y(M, [g]) ≤ Y(Sⁿ) with equality iff M is conformal to the sphere, and proved existence for all non-conformal-to-sphere metrics; Schoen (1984) handled the remaining case using the positive mass theorem (Schoen-Yau, Witten) to prove strict inequality Y(M, [g]) < Y(Sⁿ) for non-conformal metrics. The solution gives a minimizer u > 0, smooth, producing a constant scalar curvature metric. The Obata theorem: the only conformal metrics on Sⁿ with constant scalar curvature are the round metric up to Möbius transformations.
The Yamabe problem is the premier example of solving a geometric PDE via variational methods; the positive mass theorem provides the crucial strict inequality for existence.
Part 4

🔥 Ricci Flow and the Poincaré Conjecture

Ricci Flow
Hamilton's Program, Singularity Formation, and Perelman's Proof
The Ricci flow ∂g/∂t = −2 Ric(g) evolves a Riemannian metric to smooth out curvature. The normalized Ricci flow preserves volume. Hamilton's theorem (1982): on a closed 3-manifold with positive Ricci curvature, the Ricci flow converges to a constant positive curvature metric — the manifold is diffeomorphic to a spherical space form. Singularities form when curvature blows up: the neck-pinch (a cylindrical neck collapses) and the cigar soliton (a self-similar shrinking solution). Perelman's three preprints (2002-2003): the L-geodesic action and the reduced volume monotonicity; the no-local-collapsing theorem — curvature cannot concentrate without volume also collapsing; the canonical neighborhood theorem — near a singularity, the geometry is modeled on a standard solution. Ricci flow with surgery: when curvature blows up, cut out the singular region and glue in a cap, then continue the flow. Perelman showed only finitely many surgeries occur. The Poincaré conjecture: a simply connected closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to S³. The argument: run the Ricci flow with surgery; after finite time, all components become positively curved and shrink to points; by the Geometrization conjecture, the original manifold is a connected sum of a sphere with itself, i.e., a sphere. The Geometrization conjecture (Thurston) states every closed 3-manifold decomposes into pieces each admitting one of eight geometric structures.
The Ricci flow turns the topological problem of classifying 3-manifolds into an analytic problem about PDEs; Perelman's resolution of the Poincaré conjecture is one of the greatest achievements of geometric analysis.

Regularity and Singular Sets in Minimal Surface Theory

The regularity theory for area‑minimizing integral currents is a cornerstone of geometric measure theory. Allard’s regularity theorem (1972) states that if an integral varifold has density close to one at a point, then the varifold is a smooth embedded submanifold near that point. For codimension‑one area‑minimizing currents, Simons’ inequality (1968) yields that the singular set has Hausdorff dimension at most n−2. In dimensions n≤7 this forces the singular set to be empty, so every area‑minimizing hypersurface is smooth. The first counterexample in higher dimensions is the Simons cone over S³×S³ in ℝ⁸, which is area‑minimizing but singular at the origin. Almgren’s monumental big regularity paper (2000) proved that for any area‑minimizing integral current of arbitrary dimension and codimension, the singular set has Hausdorff dimension at most m−2, where m is the dimension of the current. Later, De Lellis and Spadaro (2014) gave a more accessible proof using a quantitative stratification argument. For stationary varifolds with bounded mean curvature, the singular set can be larger; the optimal dimension bound remains an active research area. The regularity theory also underpins the compactness theorems used in min‑max constructions: a sequence of minimal hypersurfaces with uniform area and genus bounds subconverges to a smooth minimal hypersurface away from a singular set of codimension at least seven.

Min‑Max Methods and the Almgren‑Pitts Theory

The Almgren‑Pitts min‑max theory produces minimal hypersurfaces by minimizing the maximal area among sweepouts of a manifold. Given a compact Riemannian manifold (Mⁿ⁺¹,g), a sweepout is a continuous map Φ:[0,1]→𝒵ₙ(M;ℤ₂) into the space of integral cycles mod 2. The width of the sweepout is supₜ area(Φ(t)). The min‑max width is the infimum of widths over all sweepouts representing a nontrivial homotopy class. Almgren (1965) and Pitts (1981) proved that the min‑max width is realized by a stationary integral varifold which is smooth away from a singular set of codimension at least seven. Marques and Neves (2014) extended this to produce infinitely many distinct minimal hypersurfaces when Ric>0, establishing a Weyl law for the volume spectrum: the k‑th width grows like k¹/(ⁿ⁺¹). Their work also resolved the Willmore conjecture (2012) by applying min‑max to the Clifford torus in S³. A more recent discrete min‑max framework by Colding and De Lellis (2003) replaces continuous sweepouts with sequences of finite‑dimensional simplicial complexes, simplifying the compactness arguments. The min‑max construction is now a standard tool for proving existence of closed minimal submanifolds in generic metrics and for studying the topology of the space of metrics.

Min‑max width computation
Explain how the min‑max width of the round three‑sphere S³ relates to the area of an equatorial 2‑sphere.
The round metric on S³ has constant sectional curvature 1. The equatorial 2‑sphere S²⊂S³ is a totally geodesic minimal hypersurface with area 4π. Any sweepout of S³ by 2‑cycles must pass through a cycle of area at least 4π because the homotopy class of the sweepout is nontrivial in π₃(𝒵₂(S³))≅ℤ. Almgren‑Pitts theory shows the min‑max width equals the area of the least‑area nontrivial minimal hypersurface, which is exactly the equatorial S². Hence the width is 4π.
Exam‑day strategy
Distinguish between the original Almgren‑Pitts continuous min‑max and the later discrete min‑max of Colding‑De Lellis. Examiners often ask which compactness theorem is used in each version: the former relies on Pitts’ compactness for integral varifolds, the latter on the compactness of discrete sweepouts and the regularity theory of De Lellis‑Tasnady.

Bubbling and Compactness for Harmonic Maps

When a sequence of harmonic maps uₖ:M→N has uniformly bounded energy, the lack of compactness is captured by the bubbling phenomenon. Sacks and Uhlenbeck (1981) introduced the perturbed energy Eₚ(u)=∫(1+|du|²)ᵖ to obtain smooth minimizers for p>1; as p→1 the minimizers converge to a harmonic map away from a finite set of points. At each blow‑up point a nontrivial harmonic sphere (a “bubble”) appears, and the total energy splits as the energy of the weak limit plus the sum of energies of the bubbles. Struwe (1985) proved the energy identity for the harmonic map heat flow: if u(t) develops a singularity at time T, then after rescaling around the singular point one extracts a harmonic sphere, and the energy lost equals the energy of the bubble. The bubble tree convergence theorem (Parker 1996, Ding‑Tian 1995) describes the full limit as a finite collection of harmonic spheres connected by neck regions whose energy tends to zero. Quantization of energy follows: there exists ε₀>0 depending only on N such that any nonconstant harmonic sphere has energy at least ε₀. This quantization is crucial for proving that the number of bubbles is finite. For the heat flow, the singular set is discrete in space‑time, and the flow can be continued past singularities by a surgery procedure analogous to the one used in Ricci flow. The analysis of neck regions uses the Pohozaev identity and the monotonicity formula for the stress‑energy tensor.

The Positive Mass Theorem and Its Role in the Yamabe Problem

The positive mass theorem (Schoen‑Yau 1979, Witten 1981) asserts that an asymptotically flat manifold of nonnegative scalar curvature has nonnegative ADM mass, with equality only for Euclidean space. Schoen (1984) used this theorem to complete the solution of the Yamabe problem. After Trudinger (1968) and Aubin (1976) handled the cases where the Yamabe invariant Y(M,[g]) is nonpositive or strictly less than the invariant of the sphere Y(Sⁿ), the remaining case was Y(M,[g])=Y(Sⁿ) but (M,g) not conformal to the sphere. Schoen considered the Green’s function G of the conformal Laplacian L_g with pole at a point p. The metric ̃g=G4/(n−2)g is asymptotically flat and has zero scalar curvature. Its ADM mass m is proportional to the constant term in the expansion of G. If m>0, a conformal deformation supported near infinity produces a metric with positive scalar curvature and Yamabe invariant strictly less than Y(Sⁿ), contradicting the assumption. The positive mass theorem forces m≥0; the rigidity statement then implies (M,g) is conformal to the sphere. Witten’s spinor proof (1981) gave a more general argument valid for spin manifolds, while Schoen‑Yau’s minimal hypersurface proof works in all dimensions ≤7 and was later extended to all dimensions by Lohkamp (1999). The theorem also underlies the proof of the Riemannian Penrose inequality and the stability of the positive mass theorem under perturbations.

Perelman’s Entropy, Kappa‑Solutions, and the Surgery Procedure

Perelman introduced the ℱ‑functional ℱ(g,f)=∫(R+|∇f|²)e−fdV and the 𝒲‑functional 𝒲(g,f,τ)=∫[τ(R+|∇f|²)+f−n](4πτ)−n/2e−fdV. The 𝒲‑entropy is monotone nondecreasing along the Ricci flow and is constant exactly on gradient shrinking solitons. The reduced volume Ṽ(τ)=∫(4πτ)−n/2e−ldV, where l is the reduced distance, is also monotone. These monotonicities imply the no‑local‑collapsing theorem: if |Rm|≤r−2 on a parabolic ball P(x,t,r,−r²), then the volume of the ball is at least κrⁿ for a universal κ>0. A κ‑solution is an ancient, complete, non‑flat solution with bounded nonnegative curvature operator that is κ‑noncollapsed at all scales. In three dimensions, Perelman classified κ‑solutions: they are either the shrinking round sphere S³, the shrinking round cylinder S²×ℝ (or its ℤ₂ quotient), or the Bryant soliton (a rotationally symmetric steady soliton). The canonical neighborhood theorem states that any point of high curvature in a Ricci flow with surgery lies in a region modeled on one of these κ‑solutions. Surgery is performed by cutting off ε‑necks (regions close to S²×I) and gluing in standard caps. Perelman proved that only finitely many surgeries occur in any finite time interval, and that the flow becomes extinct in finite time for simply connected manifolds. The extinction time is bounded by a constant times the initial volume. This finite extinction, together with the topological classification of the components at extinction, yields the Poincaré conjecture and the full geometrization conjecture.

  • Confusing the dimension bound for singular sets of area‑minimizing currents (codimension ≥7) with the bound for stationary varifolds (codimension ≥2).
  • Assuming the Almgren‑Pitts min‑max always produces a smooth hypersurface; the limit is only smooth away from a singular set of dimension at most n−7.
  • Overlooking the quantization of energy in harmonic map bubbling; the minimal energy of a nonconstant harmonic sphere is a positive constant depending on the target.
  • Misstating the positive mass theorem as requiring spin structure; Schoen‑Yau’s proof works without spin in dimensions ≤7, and Lohkamp extended it to all dimensions.
  • Believing that Ricci flow surgery can be performed infinitely many times in finite time; Perelman’s theorem guarantees only finitely many surgeries in any finite interval.
  • Thinking the Bryant soliton is a shrinking soliton; it is a steady (translation‑invariant) soliton, distinct from the shrinking cylinder and sphere.
  • Neglecting the role of the reduced volume monotonicity in proving κ‑noncollapsing; it is the key analytic tool that replaces the maximum principle in the singular setting.
