Geometric Analysis — Minimal Surfaces, the Plateau Problem & the Yamabe Problem

Geometric analysis is the application of partial differential equations to problems in differential geometry. A minimal surface is a critical point of the area functional A(Σ) = ∫_Σ dA, satisfying mean curvature H = 0 everywhere. The Plateau problem — find a surface of minimal area spanning a given boundary curve — was solved independently by Douglas and Rado (1931) using the Dirichlet energy. The Bernstein conjecture stated that the only complete minimal graphs in ℝ³ are planes; Bombieri-De Giorgi-Giusti (1969) found counterexamples in ℝ⁸. Harmonic maps are energy-minimizing maps between Riemannian manifolds; the Eells-Sampson theorem (1964) guarantees existence in negative-curvature targets. The Yamabe problem — existence of a constant scalar curvature metric in a conformal class — was solved by Trudinger, Aubin, and Schoen using the conformal Laplacian and the positive mass theorem. The Ricci flow ∂g/∂t = −2 Ric(g), introduced by Hamilton and used by Perelman to prove the Poincaré conjecture, evolves metrics to improve their curvature. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem has an analytic proof via the heat kernel. The Seiberg-Witten equations and Ginzburg-Landau vortices are gauge-theoretic PDEs central to low-dimensional topology and superconductivity.

"Geometric analysis is the art of proving existence by finding minimizers."
— Richard Schoen
Part 1

📏 Minimal Surfaces and the Plateau Problem

Minimal Surfaces
The Area Functional, Mean Curvature Zero, and the Plateau Problem
A minimal surface is a critical point of the area functional A(Σ) = ∫_Σ dA. The first variation gives mean curvature H = 0 — the Euler-Lagrange equation. For a graph z = f(x,y), the minimal surface equation is (1+|∇f|²)Δf − f_i f_j f_{ij} = 0 — a quasilinear elliptic PDE. The Plateau problem: given a closed curve Γ ⊂ ℝ³, find a surface Σ of minimal area with ∂Σ = Γ. Douglas and Rado (1931) solved it by minimizing the Dirichlet energy ∫_D |∇u|² over maps u: D → ℝ³ with u|_∂D a parametrization of Γ. The solution is a branched minimal surface — conformal and harmonic in the interior. The interior regularity theorem: any two-dimensional minimizer is smooth in the interior. The boundary regularity: the surface meets Γ at right angles if Γ is smooth. The monotonicity formula: for a minimal surface Σ, the area in a ball A(Σ ∩ B_r(p)) ≥ πr², with equality iff Σ is a plane. The Bernstein conjecture: the only complete minimal graphs in ℝⁿ for n ≤ 7 are planes; for n ≥ 8, there exist counterexamples (Bombieri-De Giorgi-Giusti). The Weierstrass-Enneper representation gives explicit parametrizations of minimal surfaces in ℝ³ via holomorphic data.
Minimal surfaces balance tension everywhere; the Plateau problem shows that every closed curve bounds at least one surface of least area, a triumph of the calculus of variations.
Part 2

🌍 Harmonic Maps and the Eells-Sampson Theorem

Harmonic Maps
The Energy Functional, Tension Field, and the Heat Flow Method
A map f: M → N between Riemannian manifolds is harmonic if it is a critical point of the energy functional E(f) = ½∫_M |df|² dV_M. The Euler-Lagrange equation is τ(f) = trace ∇df = 0, where τ(f) is the tension field. In local coordinates, τᵅ(f) = Δfᵅ + Γ_{βγ}^{α}(f) ⟨∇fᵝ, ∇fᵞ⟩ = 0 — a semilinear elliptic system. The Eells-Sampson theorem (1964): if M is compact and N has nonpositive sectional curvature, then any continuous map f₀: M → N can be deformed to a harmonic map, unique in its homotopy class. The proof uses the harmonic map heat flow: ∂_t f = τ(f). The flow exists for all time and converges to a harmonic map as t → ∞. The Bochner formula: Δ|df|² = 2|∇df|² + 2⟨Ricᵐ(df), df⟩ − 2⟨Rᴺ(df, df)df, df⟩, which gives vanishing results for harmonic maps from positively curved domains into negatively curved targets. The Lichnerowicz-Obata theorem: on an Einstein manifold with Ric ≥ (n−1)g, the first nonzero eigenvalue of the Laplacian satisfies λ₁ ≥ n, with equality iff M is the sphere. Uhlenbeck's analysis of the Yang-Mills equations uses similar gauge-fixing and compactness methods to study critical points of the Yang-Mills functional ∫ |F_A|².
Harmonic maps generalize geodesics to higher dimensions; the heat flow method deforms any map into an energy minimizer when the target is negatively curved.
Part 3

🧮 The Yamabe Problem

Yamabe
Conformal Metrics, the Yamabe Equation, and the Positive Mass Theorem
The Yamabe problem: given a compact Riemannian manifold (M, g), find a metric ̃g = u^{4/(n−2)}g conformal to g with constant scalar curvature R_{̃g} = constant. Under conformal change, the scalar curvature transforms via the conformal Laplacian L_g = Δ_g + (n−2)/(4(n−1))R_g, giving the Yamabe equation: L_g u = (n−2)/(4(n−1)) c u^{(n+2)/(n−2)}. Equivalently, one minimizes the Yamabe functional Q_g(u) = ∫ (|∇u|² + (n−2)/(4(n−1)) R_g u²) dV_g / (∫ u^{2n/(n−2)} dV_g)^{(n−2)/n}. The Yamabe constant Y(M, [g]) = inf_{u>0} Q_g(u). Trudinger (1968) proved existence when Q_g ≥ 0; Aubin (1976) showed Y(M, [g]) ≤ Y(Sⁿ) with equality iff M is conformal to the sphere, and proved existence for all non-conformal-to-sphere metrics; Schoen (1984) handled the remaining case using the positive mass theorem (Schoen-Yau, Witten) to prove strict inequality Y(M, [g]) < Y(Sⁿ) for non-conformal metrics. The solution gives a minimizer u > 0, smooth, producing a constant scalar curvature metric. The Obata theorem: the only conformal metrics on Sⁿ with constant scalar curvature are the round metric up to Möbius transformations.
The Yamabe problem is the premier example of solving a geometric PDE via variational methods; the positive mass theorem provides the crucial strict inequality for existence.
Part 4

🔥 Ricci Flow and the Poincaré Conjecture

Ricci Flow
Hamilton's Program, Singularity Formation, and Perelman's Proof
The Ricci flow ∂g/∂t = −2 Ric(g) evolves a Riemannian metric to smooth out curvature. The normalized Ricci flow preserves volume. Hamilton's theorem (1982): on a closed 3-manifold with positive Ricci curvature, the Ricci flow converges to a constant positive curvature metric — the manifold is diffeomorphic to a spherical space form. Singularities form when curvature blows up: the neck-pinch (a cylindrical neck collapses) and the cigar soliton (a self-similar shrinking solution). Perelman's three preprints (2002-2003): the L-geodesic action and the reduced volume monotonicity; the no-local-collapsing theorem — curvature cannot concentrate without volume also collapsing; the canonical neighborhood theorem — near a singularity, the geometry is modeled on a standard solution. Ricci flow with surgery: when curvature blows up, cut out the singular region and glue in a cap, then continue the flow. Perelman showed only finitely many surgeries occur. The Poincaré conjecture: a simply connected closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to S³. The argument: run the Ricci flow with surgery; after finite time, all components become positively curved and shrink to points; by the Geometrization conjecture, the original manifold is a connected sum of a sphere with itself, i.e., a sphere. The Geometrization conjecture (Thurston) states every closed 3-manifold decomposes into pieces each admitting one of eight geometric structures.
The Ricci flow turns the topological problem of classifying 3-manifolds into an analytic problem about PDEs; Perelman's resolution of the Poincaré conjecture is one of the greatest achievements of geometric analysis.

Variational Stability, Bubble Formation, and the Failure of Direct Minimization

The standard variational approach assumes that minimizing an energy functional yields a smooth solution. However, in Geometric Analysis, the direct method often fails due to "loss of compactness." This occurs when a sequence of approximate minimizers converges weakly but not strongly, causing mass (energy) to escape to infinity or concentrate at points.

Mechanism of Failure: Consider the Yamabe problem. The functional Q_g(u) is bounded below by the Yamabe constant Y(M,[g]). If Y(M,[g]) < 0, a minimizer exists. However, if one considers sequences where u_k concentrates at a point p, the energy can concentrate into a "bubble" (a copy of the standard sphere metric scaled to infinity). This is known as bubbling. The limit function becomes zero everywhere except at the concentration point, which represents a singularity in the weak limit.

Comparison: Contrast this with the Plateau problem. While minimal surfaces (minimal graphs) always exist in R3 due to the convexity of the domain and coercivity of the area functional, on compact manifolds or with complex boundary conditions, singular minimal surfaces (like the catenoid collapsing into a line) can form. The examiner often twists this by asking for the existence of a minimal surface in a domain with non-trivial topology where the area functional is not convex, requiring the use of Almgren-Pitts min-max theory or harmonic map heat flow to bypass the local minima.

Applied Scenario: In the study of Einstein metrics, one might attempt to minimize the scalar curvature functional. If the manifold admits a metric with negative scalar curvature, minimizing the energy might lead to a degenerate limit where the volume collapses. The examiner may present a scenario involving a sequence of metrics on S2 × S1 and ask you to identify why the limit is not smooth.

Technique: Identifying Bubbling in Weak Convergence
Question: Let (M, g) be a compact 3-manifold. Consider a sequence of functions u_k solving the Yamabe equation L_g u_k = c u_k5/3 with energy approaching the infimum. Suppose ∫_M |∇ u_k|2 dV → Y(M,[g]). If the sequence does not converge strongly to a positive function, describe the structure of the weak limit and the "bubble" component.
Solution: By the concentration-compactness principle (Lions), the weak limit u satisfies the equation in the distributional sense. If strong convergence fails, there exists a point x0 ∈ M and a sequence of scales ε_k → 0 such that a "bubble" forms around x0. The bubble is isomorphic to the standard sphere metric on S2 (or higher dimensional analogues depending on dimension). Specifically, in local coordinates centered at x_k, the rescaled function converges to a solution on R^n ∪ {∞} which is a "standard bubble" (the Talenti instanton). The total energy splits: E(utotal) = E(u) + E(bubble). Since the Yamabe constant of the standard sphere is strictly less than that of any other manifold with negative curvature, bubbles typically only form if the background metric allows it or if one is in a critical case. In the context of the Yamabe problem on manifolds with Y(M,[g]) < 0, bubbling does not occur for the minimizer itself, but might occur when studying non-minimizing critical points or in the study of harmonic maps from high-dimensional domains to spheres where energy concentrates.
Exam-Day Strategy: The "Energy Splitting" Check
When asked about existence of minimizers, always check the sign of the Yamabe constant or the curvature bounds. If the problem involves a sequence converging weakly but not strongly, immediately invoke the decomposition into a regular part and a bubble part. Remember: if the target manifold has positive Ricci curvature (like S^n), harmonic maps from simply connected domains are rigid; bubbling is impossible unless the domain topology forces it.

Topological Obstructions and the Role of Gauge Theory in Geometric PDEs

Geometric analysis frequently encounters situations where analytic methods (finding a solution to a PDE) are obstructed by topological constraints. The most famous example is the non-existence of certain harmonic maps or metrics due to characteristic classes.

Mechanism: In the study of Yang-Mills equations or harmonic maps into spheres, the energy functional is often not bounded below in a way that allows simple minimization if the homotopy class is non-trivial. The "twist" here is recognizing that while a smooth critical point exists in every homotopy class (for harmonic maps), it might not be an absolute minimum. For instance, a map from S2 to S2 with degree k ≠ 0 has a lower bound on energy proportional to |k|. The Dirichlet energy is minimized by the constant map only if the degree is zero.

Comparison: Compare the Yamabe problem (purely variational, conformal geometry) with the existence of Einstein metrics. While the Yamabe problem guarantees a solution within a conformal class, the existence of an Einstein metric (Ricci proportional to metric) is not guaranteed by variational methods alone; it depends on the topology. For example, on S2 × S1, there are no Einstein metrics with negative scalar curvature, despite the variational formulation suggesting a critical point might exist.

Landmark Case: The Atiyah-Singer Index Theorem provides the topological obstruction for the existence of solutions to elliptic operators. If the index is non-zero, a solution cannot exist globally without singularities or boundary terms. In competitive exams, this often appears as a "prove that no such metric exists" question based on integrating curvature forms (Gauss-Bonnet) and finding a contradiction with assumed scalar curvature signs.

Technique: Using Gauss-Bonnet for Non-Existence
Question: Prove that there exists no metric g on the torus T2 = S1 × S1 such that the scalar curvature R_g ≡ 1. Use only the Gauss-Bonnet theorem and properties of the Laplacian.
Solution: The Gauss-Bonnet theorem for a closed surface states ∫T2 R_g dV_g = 2π chi(T2). The Euler characteristic of the torus is chi(T2) = 0. Thus, ∫T2 R_g dV_g = 0. If we assume R_g ≡ 1, then ∫T2 1 · dV_g = Area(T2) > 0. This contradicts the integral being zero. Therefore, no such metric exists. This topological obstruction prevents the scalar curvature from being strictly positive everywhere on a torus.
Exam-Day Strategy: Check the Euler Characteristic
Whenever a problem asks about constant scalar curvature or Ricci curvature on a compact manifold, calculate chi(M) first. If the integral of curvature must vanish (or be negative) but the problem assumes positivity, you have found a contradiction immediately. Do not waste time constructing the metric; state the topological obstruction.

Singularity Analysis and the Finite-Time Blow-Up in Ricci Flow

The Ricci flow ∂_t g = -2 Ric is a non-linear parabolic PDE. A central theme in Geometric Analysis is understanding how solutions behave as time approaches a singularity (T < ∞). The "twist" for the examiner is distinguishing between different types of singularities and knowing which geometric structures dominate near them.

Mechanism: Singularities occur when curvature blows up. Hamilton classified these into two main types in 3D: Type I (curvature grows at a rate controlled by 1/(T-t)) and Type II (faster growth). The key insight from Perelman is that near any singularity, the manifold looks like one of a finite list of "standard models" (cylinders, necks, caps). This is the Canonical Neighborhood Theorem.

Comparison: Compare Ricci flow with the Heat Equation. In the heat equation u_t = Δ u, solutions smooth out instantly. In Ricci flow, the metric itself evolves, and if the initial data has high curvature, the flow can develop singularities in finite time, unlike the linear heat equation which preserves regularity indefinitely for bounded initial data. The non-linearity allows "focusing" of curvature.

Applied Scenario: Consider a 3-manifold with a "neck-pinch" singularity. As t → T, a cylindrical region shrinks to a point. The examiner may ask you to identify the cross-sectional geometry (a circle shrinking) and the rate of collapse. This requires understanding that the scalar curvature R behaves like 1/r2 near the neck, driving the singularity.
