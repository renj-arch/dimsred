Number Theory — The Queen of Mathematics

Gauss called it the "Queen of Mathematics." Number theory studies the properties of integers — the simplest, most fundamental objects in math. Yet it contains some of the deepest unsolved problems. This is the world of Ramanujan: patterns no one else saw, formulas that seemed to come from another dimension.

"Mathematics is the queen of sciences and number theory is the queen of mathematics."
— Carl Friedrich Gauss
Part 1

🔢 Prime Numbers — The Atoms of Arithmetic

The Building Blocks
Why Every Number Has a Unique Fingerprint
Every integer can be broken down into prime factors, like molecules breaking into atoms. 60 = 2² × 3 × 5. 84 = 2² × 3 × 7. No matter how you start factoring, you always get the same primes — the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. This uniqueness is why primes are the foundation of all number theory. A prime number is one that can't be factored into smaller integers — it's "primeval," first-made.
Primes are the indivisible atoms of the number system. Every number has a unique prime factorization.

The Infinitude of Primes

Are there infinitely many primes? Euclid proved yes, around 300 BC, with breathtaking elegance:

✨
Euclid's Proof — Still Beautiful After 2300 Years ▶
Suppose primes are finite. List them all: p₁, p₂, ..., pₙ.
Consider N = (p₁ × p₂ × ... × pₙ) + 1.
N is not divisible by any pᵢ (each leaves remainder 1).
So either N is prime (and not in our list), or N has a prime factor not in our list.
Contradiction. Therefore primes are infinite.

This proof is considered one of the most beautiful in all of mathematics.

The Prime Distribution — A Deep Mystery

Although primes are infinite, they become rarer as numbers grow. The Prime Number Theorem says the density of primes near x is about 1/ln(x).

Prime Number Theorem
π(x) ~ x / ln(x)
The number of primes ≤ x is approximately x divided by the natural log of x.

The Great Unsolved Mystery
The Riemann Hypothesis — $1,000,000 Problem
The Prime Number Theorem tells us the average distribution of primes. But how far can actual prime counts deviate from this average? The answer lies in the zeros of the Riemann zeta function ζ(s). Riemann conjectured in 1859 that all non-trivial zeros have real part exactly ½. If true, it would reveal the deepest order in prime distribution. It remains unproven — one of the seven Clay Millennium Problems ($1M prize). The Riemann Hypothesis is arguably the most important unsolved problem in mathematics.
The pattern of primes is connected to the zeros of a complex function in a way we still don't fully understand.

⏰ Modular Arithmetic — Clock Math

Math on a Clock
Gauss's Discovery at Age 20
Gauss published his masterpiece Disquisitiones Arithmeticae at age 24. In it, he introduced modular arithmetic: if it's 9 o'clock now, what time in 5 hours? 9 + 5 = 14 ≡ 2 (mod 12). The clock "wraps around." This is arithmetic modulo 12. Gauss called it "congruence" — two numbers are congruent mod n if they differ by a multiple of n. This simple idea is the foundation of modern cryptography, error-correcting codes, and digital security.
Modular arithmetic = arithmetic on a circle. Numbers wrap around when they reach n.

Congruence — The Language of Modular Arithmetic

We write a ≡ b (mod n) if n divides (a − b). Examples: 17 ≡ 5 (mod 12), 100 ≡ 1 (mod 9), 42 ≡ 0 (mod 7).

Addition
a±b ≡ (a mod n) ± (b mod n)
Multiplication
a×b ≡ (a mod n) × (b mod n)
Powers
aᵏ mod n
Division
Only if gcd(a,n)=1

Fermat's Little Theorem

One of the most beautiful and useful results in number theory:

Fermat's Little Theorem
If p is prime and a is not divisible by p, then ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p)
Example: 2¹⁰ = 1024 ≡ 1 (mod 11). Try it: 1024 ÷ 11 = 93 × 11 + 1 ✓
✨
Why Fermat's Theorem Works — A Necklace Analogy ▶
Consider arranging a beads in a circle of p positions. Rotating the circle gives p different arrangements (unless the pattern repeats). Since p is prime, the only patterns that repeat are the single-color ones (all a choices). So total arrangements ap minus the a monochromatic ones is divisible by p: ap − a = a(ap−1 − 1) ≡ 0 (mod p).
If a is not divisible by p, then ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p).
Elegant combinatorial proof!

Euler's Theorem — Fermat's Generalization

Euler generalized Fermat's theorem to any modulus n using Euler's totient function φ(n), which counts numbers less than n that are coprime to n.

Euler's φ
φ(n) = n × Π (1 − 1/p)
Euler's Theorem
aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) if gcd(a,n)=1

🧩 Diophantine Equations — Solving for Integers

The Puzzle That Took 358 Years
Fermat's Last Theorem
In the 1630s, Fermat scribbled in a margin: "It is impossible to separate a cube into two cubes, or a fourth power into two fourth powers, or in general any power higher than the second into two like powers. I have discovered a truly marvelous proof of this, which this margin is too narrow to contain." He claimed xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has no integer solutions for n > 2. The proof was lost — or perhaps he never had it. For 358 years, mathematicians tried and failed. Andrew Wiles finally proved it in 1994, using 20th-century mathematics Fermat couldn't have imagined: modular forms, elliptic curves, Galois representations. The margin was definitely too small.
The simplest-looking problems in number theory can require the deepest mathematics to solve.

Pythagorean Triples

The equation x² + y² = z² has infinitely many integer solutions — Pythagorean triples. The smallest: (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17).

Generating All Triples
a = m² − n², b = 2mn, c = m² + n² (for any m > n)
Example: m=3, n=1 → (3²−1², 2×3×1, 3²+1²) = (8, 6, 10) = 2×(4,3,5)

Pell's Equation

The equation x² − Dy² = 1 (where D is not a perfect square) has infinitely many solutions, generated by the fundamental solution and its powers.

Pell's Equation: x² − 2y² = 1
Fundamental solution: (3,2) since 3² − 2×2² = 9 − 8 = 1.
All solutions come from powers of (3+2√2): (3+2√2)² = 17+12√2 → (17,12) → 17² − 2×12² = 289 − 288 = 1.
This equation appears in Archimedes' cattle problem and modern cryptography.

🔐 Cryptography — Number Theory Protects the Internet

The Internet Runs on Primes
How Number Theory Secures Your Credit Card
Every time you buy something online, your browser and the server perform a cryptographic handshake. The security rests on one fact: multiplying two large primes is easy, but factoring the product is extremely hard. This asymmetry — easy one way, hard the other — is the basis of RSA encryption. Your browser uses modular exponentiation (Euler's theorem) to encrypt, and only the server's private key can decrypt. Without number theory, e-commerce as we know it couldn't exist.
The purest branch of mathematics turned out to have the most practical application imaginable.

RSA in a Nutshell

R
How RSA Works ▶
Key generation:
1. Pick two large primes p, q (hundreds of digits).
2. Compute n = p × q. φ(n) = (p−1)(q−1).
3. Choose e such that gcd(e, φ(n)) = 1 (typically 65537).
4. Compute d such that e × d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)) — the modular inverse.

Public key: (n, e) — anyone can see this.
Private key: d — only the recipient knows this.

Encryption: c ≡ mᵉ (mod n)
Decryption: m ≡ cᵈ (mod n)

Why it works: By Euler's theorem, mφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n). Since e×d = 1 + k×φ(n), we get cᵈ = me×d = m × mk×φ(n) ≡ m (mod n).
Why it's secure: To find d from (n,e), you need φ(n), which requires factoring n = p×q. Factoring large numbers is believed to be computationally infeasible.

Chinese Remainder Theorem and Its Computational Uses

The Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) states that for a system of simultaneous congruences

x ≡ a₁ (mod n₁), x ≡ a₂ (mod n₂), …, x ≡ a_k (mod n_k)

where the moduli n₁, n₂, …, n_k are pairwise coprime, there exists a unique solution modulo N = n₁ n₂ … n_k. The constructive proof builds the solution as

x = Σ a_i · N_i · M_i (mod N)

where N_i = N / n_i and M_i is the modular inverse of N_i modulo n_i (i.e. N_i·M_i ≡ 1 (mod n_i)). This explicit formula makes CRT a workhorse in computer algebra systems, secret sharing schemes, and fast modular exponentiation.

Garner’s Algorithm

When the moduli are not coprime, a solution exists iff each pair of congruences is compatible: a_i ≡ a_j (mod gcd(n_i, n_j)). Garner’s algorithm solves the general case by reducing the system to a triangular form, avoiding the large product N until the final step. It is the method of choice in implementations of RSA with Chinese Remainder optimisation, where decryption is performed modulo p and q separately and then recombined, yielding a four‑fold speed‑up.

Applications Beyond Cryptography

In error‑correcting codes, CRT underlies the construction of Reed–Solomon codes over rings ℤ/mℤ. In parallel computing, it enables residue number systems where each processor works with a small modulus, eliminating carry propagation. The theorem also appears in the proof of the existence of infinitely many primes in arithmetic progressions (Dirichlet) by constructing integers that avoid a prescribed finite set of prime divisors.

Quadratic Reciprocity and Legendre Symbol

The Legendre symbol (a|p) for an odd prime p is defined as 1 if a is a quadratic residue modulo p, −1 if it is a non‑residue, and 0 if p divides a. Euler’s criterion gives (a|p) ≡ a^((p−1)/2) (mod p). The Law of Quadratic Reciprocity, first proved by Gauss, relates the symbols for two distinct odd primes p and q:

(p|q)(q|p) = (−1)^(((p−1)/2)·((q−1)/2)).

Supplement laws handle the cases a = −1 and a = 2: (−1|p) = (−1)^((p−1)/2) and (2|p) = (−1)^((p²−1)/8). Together these rules allow the rapid determination of quadratic residuosity without exhaustive search.

Jacobi Symbol and Generalisation

The Jacobi symbol (a|n) extends the Legendre symbol to odd composite n by multiplicativity: if n = ∏ p_ie_i then (a|n) = ∏ (a|p_i)e_i. While (a|n) = 1 no longer guarantees that a is a quadratic residue modulo n, the Jacobi symbol retains the reciprocity law and can be computed in O(log n) time using the binary Euclidean algorithm. This makes it a core component of the Solovay–Strassen primality test.

Algorithmic Use in Primality Testing

In the Solovay–Strassen test, a random base a is chosen and the congruence a^((n−1)/2) ≡ (a|n) (mod n) is checked. A composite n fails for at least half of the bases, giving a Monte‑Carlo error probability of 2−k after k independent trials. The test was the first practical probabilistic primality prover and paved the way for the Miller–Rabin test, which uses strong pseudoprime conditions derived from the factorisation n−1 = 2^s·d.

Worked Example: Evaluating a Legendre Symbol
Compute (17|41) using quadratic reciprocity and supplements.
Both 17 and 41 are ≡ 1 (mod 4), so the reciprocity sign is +1. Thus (17|41) = (41|17). Reduce 41 modulo 17: 41 ≡ 7 (mod 17). Now (7|17). Since 7 ≡ 3 (mod 4) and 17 ≡ 1 (mod 4), the sign remains +1, so (7|17) = (17|7). Reduce 17 modulo 7: 17 ≡ 3 (mod 7). Now (3|7). Both 3 and 7 are ≡ 3 (mod 4), so the sign is (−1)1·1 = −1, giving (3|7) = −(7|3). Reduce 7 modulo 3: 7 ≡ 1 (mod 3), and (1|3) = 1. Therefore (3|7) = −1. Tracing back, (17|41) = −1. Hence 17 is a quadratic non‑residue modulo 41.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a Legendre or Jacobi symbol, first reduce the numerator modulo the denominator, then apply reciprocity. Keep track of the sign factor using the parity of ((p−1)/2)·((q−1)/2). For the supplement (2|p), remember the pattern: p ≡ ±1 (mod 8) → +1; p ≡ ±3 (mod 8) → −1. Practise the binary Jacobi algorithm; it runs in logarithmic time and avoids large exponentiations.

Dirichlet's Theorem on Primes in Arithmetic Progressions

Dirichlet proved in 1837 that for any two coprime positive integers a and d, the arithmetic progression a, a+d, a+2d, … contains infinitely many primes. The proof introduces Dirichlet characters χ modulo d and the associated L‑functions L(s,χ) = Σ χ(n) n−s. The key analytic step shows that L(1,χ) ≠ 0 for every non‑principal character χ. Non‑vanishing at s = 1 implies that the sum over primes Σ χ(p) p−s diverges as s → 1⁺, forcing infinitely many primes in each admissible residue class.

Effective Versions and Linnik’s Constant

An effective form asks for the least prime p ≡ a (mod d). Linnik proved in 1944 that such a prime exists with p ≤ d^L for some absolute constant L (Linnik’s constant). The best unconditional bound currently known is L = 5, due to Xylouris (2011). Under the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis one can take L = 2 + ε. These results are crucial in algorithmic number theory, for instance when constructing prime moduli of a prescribed size for cryptographic protocols.

Chebotarev Density Theorem as Generalisation

The Chebotarev Density Theorem extends Dirichlet’s result to Galois extensions of ℚ. It states that the Frobenius conjugacy classes of unramified primes are equidistributed in the Galois group. Dirichlet’s theorem corresponds to the abelian extension ℚ(ζ_d)/ℚ, where the Galois group is (ℤ/dℤ)× and Frobenius classes correspond to residue classes modulo d. This perspective unifies the distribution of primes in progressions with the splitting behaviour of primes in arbitrary number fields.

Elliptic Curves, Modular Forms, and the Proof of Fermat's Last Theorem

An elliptic curve over ℚ is a smooth projective curve of genus one with a rational point, usually given by a Weierstrass equation y² = x³ + Ax + B with discriminant Δ = −16(4A³+27B²) ≠ 0. The set of rational points E(ℚ) forms a finitely generated abelian group (Mordell–Weil theorem). The Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture, proved by Wiles, Taylor, Breuil, Conrad, and Diamond, asserts that every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular: its L‑function coincides with that of a weight‑2 cusp form for some congruence subgroup Γ₀(N).

Frey Curve and Ribet’s Theorem

Assuming a non‑trivial solution to xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ with n ≥ 3, Frey constructed the elliptic curve y² = x(x−aⁿ)(x+bⁿ). Ribet proved that this curve cannot be modular because its associated Galois representation is irreducible but has level‑lowering properties contradicting modularity. Wiles then showed that all semistable elliptic curves are modular, eliminating the Frey curve and thereby proving Fermat’s Last Theorem.

Modular Forms and Hecke Operators

A modular form of weight k for Γ₀(N) is a holomorphic function f on the upper half‑plane satisfying f((az+b)/(cz+d)) = (cz+d)^k f(z) for all (a b; c d) ∈ Γ₀(N), with a Fourier expansion f(z) = Σ a_n q^n (q = e2π i z). Hecke operators T_p act on the space of cusp forms and commute with each other; eigenforms have Fourier coefficients a_p that satisfy the same recurrence as the coefficients of the L‑function of an elliptic curve. The Eichler–Shimura construction attaches a Galois representation to each eigenform, providing the bridge between automorphic forms and arithmetic geometry.

Modern Primality Testing: Miller–Rabin and AKS Algorithms

The Miller–Rabin test is a probabilistic algorithm that decides compositeness with error probability at most 4−k after k rounds. Write n−1 = 2^s·d with d odd. For a random base a ∈ [2, n−2], compute x = a^d mod n. If x = 1 or x = n−1, the round passes. Otherwise repeat s−1 times: x = x² mod n; if x = n−1 the round passes. If none of these conditions hold, n is composite. For n < 2⁶⁴, testing the bases {2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17} makes the test deterministic.

AKS Deterministic Polynomial‑Time Test

In 2002 Agrawal, Kayal, and Saxena gave the first unconditional deterministic polynomial‑time primality test. The algorithm checks whether (X + a)^n ≡ X^n + a (mod X^r − 1, n) for a = 1,…,⌊√φ(r) log n⌋, where r is the smallest integer such that the order of n modulo r exceeds (log n)². The original runtime was Õ(log12 n); subsequent improvements by Lenstra and Pomerance reduced it to Õ(log6 n). Although theoretically landmark, AKS is slower in practice than Miller–Rabin or the elliptic‑curve primality proving (ECPP) algorithm, which is used for generating provable primes in cryptographic libraries.

Elliptic Curve Primality Proving

ECPP constructs an elliptic curve E over ℤ/nℤ with a known number of points #E = n + 1 − t, where t is small. Using complex multiplication, one finds a curve whose order has a large prime factor q > (n1/4+1)². A recursive certificate proves the primality of q, and the group law on E yields a certificate for n. ECPP runs in expected time Õ(log4 n) and produces a short, easily verifiable certificate, making it the method of choice for generating certified primes in standards such as FIPS 186‑4.

Sieve Methods and Upper Bounds for Prime Gaps

Sieve theory provides systematic ways to estimate the size of sets of integers that avoid a prescribed collection of residue classes. The simplest sieve, the Eratosthenes sieve, removes multiples of each prime up to √x and leaves exactly the primes ≤ x. Modern combinatorial sieves — Brun’s sieve, the Selberg sieve, and the large sieve — replace exact inclusion–exclusion by weighted sums that are easier to bound. Brun’s sieve shows that the sum of reciprocals of twin primes converges, giving the first quantitative evidence that twin primes are sparse. The Selberg sieve introduces a quadratic optimisation problem whose solution yields the asymptotic upper bound π₂(x) ≪ x (log log x)² / (log x)² for the number of twin primes up to x. The large sieve, developed by Linnik and Rényi, controls the distribution of a sequence in many reduced residue classes simultaneously and leads to the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem: for any A > 0 there exists B = B(A) such that ∑q ≤ x1/2 / (log x)^B max(a,q)=1 |π(x;q,a) − li(x)/φ(q)| ≪ x / (log x)^A. This result is an averaged form of the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis and is the main engine behind many results on primes in arithmetic progressions. The Selberg sieve applied to twin primes yields π₂(x) ≪ x (log log x)² / (log x)². For prime gaps, Baker–Harman–Pintz proved pn+1 − p_n ≪ p_n0.525 using primes in short intervals. The current record on bounded gaps, due to Maynard and Tao, uses a multidimensional Selberg sieve to show lim inf (pn+1 − p_n) ≤ 246. Understanding the combinatorial structure of the sieve weights, the role of the level of distribution, and the parity problem — which prevents sieves from distinguishing numbers with an odd versus even number of prime factors — is essential for any aspirant tackling analytic number theory questions.

Analytic Properties of the Riemann Zeta Function

The Riemann zeta function ζ(s) = ∑n=1^∞ n−s converges absolutely for Re(s) > 1 and admits a meromorphic continuation to the whole complex plane with a simple pole at s = 1 of residue 1. The functional equation ζ(s) = 2^s πs−1 sin(πs/2) Γ(1−s) ζ(1−s) relates values at s and 1−s and shows that ζ(s) vanishes at the negative even integers — the trivial zeros. The non‑trivial zeros lie in the critical strip 0 < Re(s) < 1; the Riemann Hypothesis asserts they all satisfy Re(s) = 1/2. Zero‑free regions are proved by combining the functional equation with estimates for ζ(s) on vertical lines. The classical zero‑free region of de la Vallée‑Poussin states that ζ(s) ≠ 0 for σ ≥ 1 − c / log(|t|+2) with an absolute constant c > 0. This region yields the prime number theorem with error term π(x) = li(x) + O(x exp(−c√log x)). Vinogradov and Korobov improved the zero-free region to σ ≥ 1 − c / ((log |t|)2/3 (log log |t|)1/3), giving the strongest unconditional error term known. The density hypothesis N(σ,T) ≪ T2(1−σ)+ε for the number of zeros with real part ≥ σ would imply π(x) = li(x) + O(x1/2+ε). The explicit formula connects the zeros to the Chebyshev function ψ(x) = ∑n≤x Λ(n): ψ(x) = x − ∑_ρ x^ρ/ρ − log(2π) − ½ log(1−x−2), where the sum runs over non‑trivial zeros ρ. Truncating this sum at height T gives an approximation whose error is controlled by the zero‑free region. Mastery of the functional equation, the location of zeros, and the derivation of the explicit formula is a recurring theme in advanced problem sets.

Worked Example: Estimating ψ(x) via the Explicit Formula
Assume the Riemann Hypothesis. Show that ψ(x) = x + O(√x log² x).
Under RH every non‑trivial zero has the form ρ = ½ + iγ. The explicit formula gives ψ(x) = x − ∑|γ|≤T x½+iγ/(½+iγ) + O(x log² x / T) + O(log x). Choose T = x. The sum over zeros is bounded by √x ∑|γ|≤x 1/|γ|. The number of zeros with |γ| ≤ x is ≪ x log x, and a partial summation yields ∑|γ|≤x 1/|γ| ≪ log² x. Hence the zero sum is O(√x log² x). The error term from truncation is O(x log² x / x) = O(log² x). Combining gives ψ(x) = x + O(√x log² x).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for an error term under RH, write the explicit formula, insert ρ = ½ + iγ, bound the sum by √x times a zero‑counting estimate, and choose the truncation height to balance the two error contributions. Memorise the standard zero‑count N(T) ∼ (T/2π) log(T/2π) and the bound ∑|γ|≤T 1/|γ| ≪ log² T.

Explicit Formulae Connecting Zeros to Prime Counting

The explicit formula is a family of identities that express weighted prime sums in terms of the zeros of ζ(s) or more general L‑functions. For the Chebyshev function ψ(x) the formula reads ψ(x) = x − ∑_ρ x^ρ/ρ − log(2π) − ½ log(1−x−2), where the sum runs over all non‑trivial zeros ρ of ζ(s). A smoothed version replaces the sharp cutoff at x by a test function h(u) with compactly supported Fourier transform, yielding ψ_h(x) = ∫0^∞ h(u/x) dψ(u) = ∫0^∞ h(u) du − ∑_ρ x^ρ ĥ(−iρ) + …, where ĥ is the Mellin transform. This smoothing removes the oscillatory Gibbs phenomenon and makes the contribution of individual zeros explicit. The Riemann–von Mangoldt formula N(T) = (T/2π) log(T/2π) − T/2π + O(log T) counts zeros up to height T and is proved by applying the argument principle to ξ(s) = ½ s(s−1) π−s/2 Γ(s/2) ζ(s). The Guinand–Weil explicit formula generalises this to arbitrary L‑functions attached to Dirichlet characters, Hecke eigenforms, or Galois representations. In competitive examinations, a typical task is to derive the explicit formula for ψ(x) from the Perron integral ψ(x) = (1/2πi) ∫c−i∞c+i∞ (−ζ′/ζ)(s) x^s/s ds by shifting the contour left past the pole at s = 1 and the zeros, picking up residues. The contribution of the trivial zeros gives the −½ log(1−x−2) term, while the non‑trivial zeros produce the sum over ρ. Understanding the interplay between contour integration, zero‑free regions, and the resulting error terms is the key to solving problems on prime distribution with explicit constants.

p‑Adic Numbers and Hensel’s Lemma

The field of p‑adic numbers ℚₚ is the completion of ℚ with respect to the p‑adic absolute value |x|ₚ = p−vₚ(x), where vₚ(x) is the exponent of p in the factorisation of x. Elements of ℚₚ can be written uniquely as a series ∑k=m^∞ a_k p^k with digits a_k ∈ {0,…,p−1} and m ∈ ℤ. The ring of p‑adic integers ℤₚ consists of those series with m ≥ 0. Hensel’s Lemma is the p‑adic analogue of Newton’s method: if f ∈ ℤₚ[x] and a ∈ ℤₚ satisfy f(a) ≡ 0 (mod p) and f′(a) ≢ 0 (mod p), then there exists a unique root α ∈ ℤₚ with α ≡ a (mod p). The proof constructs the sequence an+1 = a_n − f(a_n)/f′(a_n) and shows it converges p‑adically. A stronger version (Hensel’s Lemma for multiple roots) requires f(a) ≡ 0 (mod p2k+1) and vₚ(f′(a)) = k to lift a root modulo p2k+1 to a root in ℤₚ. Applications include determining whether a polynomial has a root modulo p^n for all n, solving congruences of the form x² ≡ a (mod p^n), and proving that a unit in ℤₚ is a square iff its image in 𝔽ₚ is a square and p ≠ 2. The structure of the multiplicative group ℚₚ^× ≅ p^ℤ × ℤₚ^× and the decomposition ℤₚ^× ≅ μp−1 × (1+pℤₚ) for odd p (with a slight modification for p = 2) are standard tools. In examinations, problems often ask to decide the solvability of x² ≡ 5 (mod 2^n) or to compute the number of solutions of x³ ≡ 2 (mod 7^n) using Hensel lifting. Familiarity with the valuation, the ultrametric inequality |x+y|ₚ ≤ max(|x|ₚ,|y|ₚ), and the fact that ℚₚ is locally compact but not ordered is essential.

Fast Integer Arithmetic: Multiplication, GCD, and Modular Reduction

Modern computational number theory relies on algorithms whose asymptotic complexity is far below the naïve O(n²) schoolbook methods. For multiplication of n‑bit integers, the Karatsuba algorithm reduces the exponent to log₂3 ≈ 1.585 by recursively splitting numbers into halves. The Toom‑Cook family generalises this to k‑way splits, achieving O(n1+ε) for any fixed ε > 0. The Schönhage–Strassen algorithm uses the Fast Fourier Transform over the ring ℤ/ (22^k+1)ℤ to reach O(n log n log log n). In 2019 Harvey and van der Hoeven proved an O(n log n) algorithm, though the constant factors make it practical only for astronomically large inputs. For greatest common divisor, the binary GCD algorithm (Stein’s algorithm) replaces division by shifts and subtractions, running in O(n²) bit operations but with a very small constant. The half‑GCD (or Lehmer’s algorithm) combines Euclidean steps with matrix multiplication to achieve O(M(n) log n) where M(n) is the multiplication time. Modular reduction modulo a fixed modulus m can be accelerated by precomputing the reciprocal 1/m as a fixed‑point integer (Barrett reduction) or by using Montgomery multiplication, which maps numbers to the Montgomery domain Rℤ/mℤ with R = 2^k > m and performs multiplications without trial division. In cryptographic libraries, the combination of Montgomery multiplication with a sliding‑window exponentiation yields the fastest modular exponentiation for RSA and Diffie–Hellman. For elliptic‑curve cryptography, the same techniques apply to field arithmetic in 𝔽ₚ or 𝔽2^m. Competitive problems may ask to estimate the number of bit operations for a 2048‑bit RSA decryption using Montgomery multiplication, or to compare the asymptotic cost of Karatsuba versus schoolbook multiplication for 1024‑bit operands. Knowing the big‑O complexities and the practical crossover points (Karatsuba typically becomes faster around 70–100 machine words) is part of the expected toolkit.

Zero‑Free Regions and Explicit Prime Number Theorem Bounds

The classical prime number theorem states that π(x) ~ li(x), but the quality of the approximation depends on how far the non‑trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function lie from the line Re(s)=1. De la Vallée Poussin (1899) proved the first zero‑free region: there exists a constant c>0 such that ζ(s)≠0 for Re(s) ≥ 1 − c/log(|Im(s)|+2). This yields the error term π(x)=li(x)+O(x exp(−c√log x)). Korobov and Vinogradov (1958) independently improved the exponent to 3/5, giving a region Re(s) ≥ 1 − c/(log |t|)^(3/5) (log log |t|)^(−1/5). Ford (2002) refined the constant and made the region fully explicit, allowing completely effective bounds for ψ(x)−x.

Explicit inequalities for π(x) are important for problem-solving. Rosser and Schoenfeld (1962) proved that for x ≥ 55, x/(log x + 2) < π(x) < x/(log x − 4). Schoenfeld (1976), assuming the Riemann hypothesis, showed |π(x)−li(x)| < √x log x / (8π) for x ≥ 2657. Dusart (2010, 2018) gave unconditional bounds valid for all large x, e.g. π(x) ≤ x/(log x − 1) for x ≥ 5393 and π(x) ≥ x/(log x − 1.1) for x ≥ 60184. The first sign change of π(x)−li(x) (Skewes’ number) is known to occur below exp(727.95133) (Bays & Hudson 2000), far smaller than the original Skewes bound 10^(10^(1034)).

In problem‑solving, the explicit formula ψ(x)=x−∑_ρ x^ρ/ρ − log(2π) − ½ log(1−x^(−2)) is truncated at height T, introducing an error O(x log²x / T). Choosing T = exp(√log x) balances the terms and reproduces the Korobov–Vinogradov error. Problems may ask to derive the order of the error term from a given zero‑free region or to compare the numerical constants in Rosser–Schoenfeld versus Dusart bounds.

Bounded Gaps Between Primes: Zhang, Maynard, and the Polymath Project

For decades the twin prime conjecture (infinitely many prime pairs with gap 2) seemed out of reach. In 2013 Yitang Zhang proved that there exists an absolute constant H such that infinitely many prime pairs satisfy pn+1−p_n ≤ H. His initial bound was H ≤ 70 000 000, obtained by modifying the Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım (GPY) sieve and using a level of distribution θ = ½ + 1/584 for primes in arithmetic progressions, a consequence of the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem.

The Polymath8 project, led by Terence Tao, collaboratively reduced H to 4 680 by optimizing Zhang’s sieve weights and improving the level of distribution via the Motohashi–Pintz–Zhang variant of the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem. Independently, James Maynard (2013) introduced a multidimensional sieve that replaced the GPY one‑dimensional weights with a flexible polynomial supported on a simplex. Maynard’s method not only recovered bounded gaps but also showed that for any m there are infinitely many intervals of bounded length containing at least m primes. The Polymath8b project combined Maynard’s sieve with further numerical optimisation, bringing the unconditional bound down to H ≤ 246.

Conditional results are stronger: assuming the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture (level of distribution θ = 1) the bound becomes H ≤ 6; assuming the generalized Elliott–Halberstam conjecture it drops to H ≤ 2, which would prove the twin prime conjecture. The current barrier is the parity problem of sieve theory, which prevents reaching H = 2 unconditionally. Questions often test the distinction between unconditional and conditional bounds, the role of the level of distribution, and the conceptual difference between the GPY and Maynard sieves.

Examiner's trap: unconditional vs. conditional gap bounds
A question states: "The current best unconditional bound for infinitely many prime gaps is 6." Identify the error and give the correct unconditional bound.
The statement confuses the conditional result (under Elliott–Halberstam, H ≤ 6) with the unconditional one. The best unconditional bound, as of the Polymath8b project, is H ≤ 246. The bound 6 is only valid assuming the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture, which remains unproven. The correct answer: the unconditional bound is 246; the bound 6 is conditional.
Study strategy for prime‑gap questions
Memorise the three key numbers: 246 (unconditional), 6 (Elliott–Halberstam), 2 (generalized Elliott–Halberstam). When a question asks for "the best known bound", specify whether it is unconditional or conditional. Be ready to explain in one sentence why the GPY sieve alone cannot reach H=2 (parity problem) and how Maynard’s multidimensional weights circumvent the GPY limitation for m≥3.

Pairing‑Based Cryptography: Weil and Tate Pairings on Elliptic Curves

Let E be an elliptic curve defined over a finite field F_q and let r be a prime dividing #E(F_q) with r ∤ q. The Weil pairing e_r : E[r] × E[r] → μ_r is a bilinear, alternating, non‑degenerate map defined on the full r‑torsion subgroup. The reduced Tate pairing t_r : E(F_q)[r] × E(F_q)/rE(F_q) → F_q^*/(F_q^*)^r is computable via Miller’s algorithm in O(log r) field operations. Both pairings satisfy e_r(P,Q)^k = e_r(kP,Q) = e_r(P,kQ) and are efficiently computable when the embedding degree k (the smallest integer such that r | q^k−1) is small.

Pairings enable cryptographic primitives impossible with ordinary elliptic‑curve groups. The Boneh–Franklin identity‑based encryption (2001) uses the Weil pairing to derive a public key from an arbitrary string. The Boneh–Lynn–Shacham (BLS) short signature scheme (2001) exploits the fact that a pairing maps two group elements to a single field element, yielding signatures half the size of ECDSA. Joux’s three‑party one‑round key exchange (2000) uses the bilinearity e(g^a, g^b)^c = e(g,g)abc. Security rests on the Bilinear Diffie–Hellman assumption: given g, g^a, g^b, g^c it is hard to compute e(g,g)abc.

Curve selection is critical. Barreto–Naehrig (BN) curves have embedding degree 12 and prime order r ≈ 256 bits, giving 128‑bit security with efficient pairings. BLS12‑381 curves (embedding degree 12, 381‑bit prime field) are standard in modern protocols such as Ethereum 2.0 and Zcash. Problems may ask to compute the embedding degree for a given curve, to explain why a large embedding degree makes the pairing insecure (because the discrete logarithm in Fq^k^* becomes easy), or to compare the bandwidth of BLS signatures versus ECDSA.

Post‑Quantum Number Theory: Lattice and Isogeny Assumptions

Shor’s algorithm solves integer factorisation and discrete logarithms in polynomial time on a quantum computer, breaking RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and all classical elliptic‑curve cryptography. Post‑quantum candidates rely on problems believed hard for both classical and quantum computers. Lattice‑based cryptography centres on the Learning With Errors (LWE) problem: given (A, b = A s + e mod q) with small error e, recover s. The Ring‑LWE variant uses polynomial rings Z_q[x]/(x^n+1), yielding compact keys and fast Number‑Theoretic Transform multiplication. Module‑LWE generalises both. The worst‑case hardness of LWE is proved via quantum reductions to approximating the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) on ideal lattices.

Isogeny‑based cryptography uses the difficulty of finding an isogeny between two supersingular elliptic curves over Fp2. The Supersingular Isogeny Diffie–Hellman (SIDH) protocol (2011) was broken in 2022 by a classical attack exploiting the auxiliary torsion point information. The commutative variant CSIDH (2018) uses the action of the ideal class group of an imaginary quadratic field on supersingular curves; its security relies on the hardness of computing the class group action. Both families exploit deep number‑theoretic structures: endomorphism rings of supersingular curves are maximal orders in a quaternion algebra, and the Deuring correspondence links isogenies to ideals.

The NIST Post‑Quantum Cryptography standardisation (2022‑2024) selected Kyber (Module‑LWE) for key encapsulation and Dilithium (Module‑LWE) for signatures, while Falcon (NTRU lattice) and SPHINCS+ (hash‑based) were also chosen. SIKE (isogeny) was broken during the process. Questions often test the distinction between worst‑case/average‑case reductions, the role of the embedding degree in pairing‑based crypto versus the dimension in lattice‑based crypto, and why quantum Fourier sampling does not solve LWE or isogeny problems.

Birch and Swinnerton‑Dyer Conjecture: Congruent Numbers and Heegner Points

The Birch and Swinnerton‑Dyer (BSD) conjecture, a Millennium Problem, asserts that for an elliptic curve E/Q the order of vanishing of its L‑function L(E,s) at s=1 equals the algebraic rank r = rank E(Q), and that the leading coefficient is given by a product of arithmetic invariants (regulator, Tamagawa numbers, order of Ш, etc.). A classical application is the congruent number problem: a positive integer n is congruent (the area of a rational right triangle) iff the elliptic curve E_n : y² = x³ − n²x has positive rank.

Tunnell’s theorem (1983) gives a simple criterion: if n is odd and square‑free, n is congruent iff the number of representations of n by the ternary quadratic form 2x²+y²+8z² equals twice the number of representations by 2x²+y²+32z² (with a similar condition for even n). This criterion is unconditional if BSD holds for E_n; otherwise it only gives a necessary condition. Heegner points provide a method to construct rational points on modular elliptic curves. For a discriminant D < 0 with (D, N)=1, the Heegner point P_D ∈ E(H) (H the Hilbert class field) traces down to a point in E(Q) when the sign of the functional equation is −1.

The Gross–Zagier formula (1986) relates the Néron–Tate height of the Heegner point to the derivative L'(E,1). Kolyvagin (1990) used Euler systems derived from Heegner points to bound the Selmer group, proving that if L(E,1)≠0 then rank E(Q)=0 and Ш is finite; if L'(E,1)≠0 then rank E(Q)=1 and Ш is finite. These results establish BSD for all elliptic curves of analytic rank 0 or 1 (under the modularity theorem). Higher rank cases remain open. Problems may ask to state Tunnell’s criterion, to explain why Heegner points only exist when the functional equation sign is −1, or to outline how Kolyvagin’s Euler system controls the Tate–Shafarevich group.

Integer Factorisation Algorithms: From Trial Division to the Number Field Sieve

Factorisation underpins RSA and many other cryptosystems. The simplest method, trial division, tests all primes up to √n and runs in O(√n) time, exponential in the bit‑length of n. Pollard’s rho algorithm (1975) uses a pseudo‑random sequence modulo n and Floyd’s cycle detection to find a non‑trivial factor in expected O(n1/4) operations, effective for numbers with small factors. Pollard’s p−1 method exploits the multiplicative group modulo a prime factor p; it succeeds quickly when p−1 is smooth (all prime factors below a bound B). Williams’ p+1 method and the elliptic curve method (ECM, Lenstra 1987) generalise this: ECM works with the group of points on a random elliptic curve modulo p and finds factors whose group order is smooth. ECM is the method of choice for extracting factors up to about 60 decimal digits.

For larger composites, the quadratic sieve (QS, Pomerance 1981) and its multiple‑polynomial variant (MPQS) dominate. QS searches for relations x2 ≡ y2 (mod n) by sieving a quadratic polynomial over a factor base of small primes. The linear algebra step combines relations into a congruence of squares, yielding a factor with probability at least 1/2. The number field sieve (NFS) is the asymptotically fastest known algorithm for integers beyond 100 digits. It works in a number field defined by an irreducible polynomial f(x) with two factor bases: rational primes and prime ideals in the ring of integers. Sieving finds pairs (a,b) where both the algebraic norm N(a−bα) and the rational value a−bm are smooth. The resulting system is solved by block Lanczos or block Wiedemann algorithms over GF(2). The general NFS (GNFS) has heuristic complexity exp((c+o(1))(log n)1/3(log log n)2/3) with c = (64/9)1/3 ≈ 1.923. The special NFS (SNFS) applies to numbers of the form re±s with small r,s, achieving c ≈ 1.526, making it feasible for numbers like 21039−1. Record factorisations (RSA‑250, 829 bits, 2020) used GNFS with massive parallel sieving and optimised linear algebra.

Implementation details matter in examinations: polynomial choice for NFS, factor‑base size, large‑prime variations (allowing one or two primes above the bound), and matrix‑step handling (block size, memory hierarchy). Questions may ask to compare asymptotic complexities of QS and NFS, explain why ECM is preferred for medium‑sized factors, or describe how the congruence of squares yields a factor. A common trap is stating NFS runs in polynomial time; it is sub‑exponential, not polynomial.

The Bombieri–Vinogradov Theorem and the Large Sieve

The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem (1965) is often called the “average‑form” of the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis for primes in arithmetic progressions. It states that for any fixed A>0 there exists B=B(A) such that

∑q≤Q max(a,q)=1 |π(x;q,a) − π(x)/φ(q)| ≪ x/(log x)A

holds for Q = x1/2/(log x)B. Here π(x;q,a) counts primes ≤ x congruent to a mod q. The theorem asserts equidistribution of primes among reduced residue classes on average over moduli up to nearly √x. This is the strongest unconditional result of its kind; the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture would extend the range to Q = x1−ε.

The proof relies on the large sieve inequality. In its arithmetic form, the large sieve bounds the number of integers in a set avoiding many residue classes modulo many primes. A typical statement: if S ⊂ [1,N] and for each prime p≤Q we forbid ω(p) residue classes, then |S| ≤ (N+Q2)/∑q≤Q μ(q)2∏p|q ω(p)/(p−ω(p)). Bombieri and Vinogradov applied this to primes, using Vaughan’s identity to decompose the von Mangoldt function into Type I and Type II sums, estimated via the large sieve and bilinear forms. Applications include the correct order of magnitude for primes in short intervals on average, Chen’s theorem (every large even integer is a sum of a prime and a product of at most two primes), and bounded gaps between primes (Zhang, Maynard).

Examiners often test the distinction between pointwise and average results. A typical question: “State the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem and explain why it does not imply GRH.” The answer must note GRH gives the same error for each modulus q, while Bombieri–Vinogradov controls only the sum over q. Another trap: confusing the large sieve with the Selberg sieve; the large sieve bounds exponential sums, the Selberg sieve is a combinatorial upper‑bound sieve.

Worked Example: Applying the Large Sieve Bound
Let S be the set of integers n ≤ N not divisible by any prime p ≤ Q. Use the large sieve to bound |S| in terms of N and Q.
For each prime p ≤ Q we forbid the residue class 0 mod p, so ω(p)=1. The large sieve gives |S| ≤ (N+Q2)/∑q≤Q μ(q)2∏p|q 1/(p−1). The denominator is ∑q≤Q μ(q)2/φ(q). By Mertens’ theorem, ∑q≤Q μ(q)2/φ(q) ∼ (6/π2) log Q. Hence |S| ≪ (N+Q2)/log Q. Choosing Q = √N yields |S| ≪ N/log N, matching the expected density of integers with no small prime factors.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for an “average‑form” result, immediately think of Bombieri–Vinogradov. Write the statement with explicit A, B and the range Q = x1/2/(log x)B. Contrast with Elliott–Halberstam (Q = x1−ε) and GRH (pointwise error O(x1/2log x)). This comparison earns full marks for “understanding the landscape”.

Zero‑Density Estimates and Their Role in Prime Distribution

Zero‑density estimates bound N(σ,T), the number of Riemann zeta zeros with real part ≥ σ and imaginary part ≤ T. Ingham (1940) gave N(σ,T) ≪ T3(1−σ)/(2−σ) log5 T for 1/2 ≤ σ ≤ 1. Later improvements by Huxley, Heath‑Brown, and Bourgain (2017) reduced the exponent; the current best for σ near 1 is N(σ,T) ≪ T2.4(1−σ) logO(1) T. These estimates are crucial because they replace the Riemann Hypothesis (which would give N(σ,T)=0 for σ>1/2) with an unconditional statement that “most” zeros lie near the critical line.

Applications include the prime number theorem in short intervals: for fixed ε>0, π(x+xθ)−π(x) ∼ xθ/log x holds for almost all x when θ>7/12 (Huxley 1972). Zero‑density estimates also enter Linnik’s theorem on the least prime in an arithmetic progression: the exponent L in p ≪ qL depends on the zero‑free region and density hypothesis. The density hypothesis (N(σ,T) ≪ T2(1−σ)+ε) would imply L=2+ε; the current unconditional record is L=5 (Xylouris 2011). For the ternary Goldbach problem, zero‑density estimates for Dirichlet L‑functions control minor arcs in the circle method.

Examination questions may ask to state a zero‑density estimate and explain its use for primes in short intervals. A trap: confusing N(σ,T) with the zero‑free region (which bounds the distance of zeros from σ=1). Another: assuming the density hypothesis equals RH; it is strictly weaker. Candidates should sketch the connection: the explicit formula for ψ(x) expresses the error as a sum over zeros; bounding zeros with large real part limits their contribution.

The Sato–Tate Conjecture and Distribution of Frobenius Traces

For an elliptic curve E/ℚ without complex multiplication, the Sato–Tate conjecture (proved by Clozel, Harris, Shepherd‑Barron, Taylor, 2008) describes the distribution of normalised Frobenius traces ap = (p+1−#E(𝔽p))/(2√p). The sequence {ap} is equidistributed in [−1,1] with respect to the semicircular measure (2/π)√(1−t2) dt. Equivalently, angles θp with ap = cos θp are uniformly distributed in [0,π] with measure (2/π) sin2 θ dθ.

The proof uses modularity (every E/ℚ is modular) and potential automorphy of symmetric power L‑functions. One shows L(Symk E, s) are automorphic with analytic continuation, functional equation, and non‑vanishing on Re(s)=1. The Sato–Tate group is identified with SU(2); equidistribution follows from the Weyl criterion on moments of ap. For CM curves, traces are equidistributed with density 1/(π√(1−t2)).

Generalisations include higher‑dimensional abelian varieties (Sato–Tate group a compact subgroup of USp(2g)) and families of modular forms. The Lang–Trotter conjecture (1976) predicts the frequency of primes with a given trace ap = r; it remains open. Examinations may ask for the Sato–Tate measure, why the CM case differs, or the role of symmetric power L‑functions. A common error: claiming Sato–Tate follows from RH for symmetric power L‑functions; the proof uses automorphy and non‑vanishing on the critical line, not full RH.

The abc Conjecture: Statement, Consequences, and Current Status

The abc conjecture (Oesterlé, Masser, 1985) is a central open problem in Diophantine analysis. For coprime positive integers a, b, c with a+b=c, define rad(abc) as the product of distinct primes dividing abc. The conjecture: for every ε>0 there exists Kε such that c < Kε rad(abc)1+ε. Equivalently, the quality q(a,b,c) = log c / log rad(abc) is bounded by 1+ε for all but finitely many triples. Consequences include Fermat’s Last Theorem for large exponents, the Mordell conjecture (Faltings) for genus >1, Roth’s theorem on Diophantine approximation, and finiteness for many Diophantine families (e.g., Catalan’s equation xp−yq=1).

In 2012, Mochizuki claimed a proof via Inter‑Universal Teichmüller Theory (IUTT), introducing Frobenioids, theta links, and log‑volume. The community has not reached consensus; a 2018 report by Scholze and Stix identified a serious gap in the “evaluation map”. Workshops have not resolved the issue; the conjecture remains officially unproven. Unconditional progress includes Stewart and Yu (1991): c < exp(rad(abc)1/3+ε), and Baker (1998) using linear forms in logarithms. The “weak abc” with a larger constant is known in function field analogues.

Examiners may ask for the precise statement, three major consequences, or why abc implies FLT for large exponents. Traps: confusing rad(abc) with the product of all prime factors (with multiplicity); asserting Mochizuki’s proof is universally accepted (it is under scrutiny, not verified). Candidates should know the “abc@home” project and the current record quality q ≈ 1.6299 (de Smit).

The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis and Its Arithmetic Consequences

The Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) extends the Riemann Hypothesis to all Dirichlet L‑functions L(s,χ) attached to primitive Dirichlet characters χ. It asserts that every non‑trivial zero of L(s,χ) lies on the critical line Re(s)=½. GRH is known to imply strong effective results in analytic number theory that are otherwise inaccessible. For instance, under GRH the error term in the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions improves to O(x½ log x), yielding Linnik’s constant L=2+ε for the least prime in a progression. GRH also gives polynomial‑time deterministic primality testing via the Miller test, and it makes the class number bounds for imaginary quadratic fields effective. In algorithmic number theory, GRH underpins the complexity analysis of the number field sieve and of algorithms for computing Hilbert class fields. A celebrated conditional result is the proof of the weak Goldbach conjecture for all sufficiently large odd integers assuming GRH (Hardy–Littlewood, 1923); Vinogradov (1937) removed the hypothesis for large integers, and Helfgott (2013) proved it unconditionally for all odd integers > 5. Examiners often ask to state GRH precisely, to list three major conditional theorems that become unconditional under GRH, or to explain why GRH for Dirichlet L‑functions is equivalent to a square‑root cancellation bound for character sums. A common trap is confusing GRH with the Extended Riemann Hypothesis (which includes Dedekind zeta functions of number fields) or assuming GRH implies the Riemann Hypothesis for all automorphic L‑functions; the latter is a strictly larger conjecture.

The Weil Conjectures and Deligne's Proof

The Weil conjectures, formulated by André Weil in 1949, concern the zeta functions of smooth projective varieties over finite fields 𝔽q. For a variety X of dimension d, the zeta function Z(X, T) = exp(∑r≥1 #X(𝔽qr) Tr / r) is a rational function of T. The conjectures assert: (1) Rationality — Z(X,T) ∈ ℚ(T); (2) Functional equation — Z(X, 1/(qd T)) = ± qdχ/2 Tχ Z(X,T) where χ is the Euler characteristic; (3) Riemann hypothesis — the reciprocal roots αi of the numerator and denominator satisfy |αi| = qw/2 for appropriate weight w; (4) Betti numbers — the degrees of the numerator and denominator equal the Betti numbers of a lift of X to characteristic zero. The first three were proved by Dwork (rationality, 1960), Grothendieck and the school of étale cohomology (functional equation, 1960s), and finally Pierre Deligne (Riemann hypothesis, 1974) using ℓ‑adic cohomology and the theory of weights. Deligne’s proof introduced the hard Lefschetz theorem in positive characteristic, earning him the Fields Medal in 1978. The Weil conjectures have profound applications: they give the Hasse–Weil bound for curves (#X(𝔽q) = q+1−∑ αi with |αi|=√q), they underlie the proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for holomorphic modular forms via the Eichler–Shimura isomorphism, and they are essential in the construction of expander graphs from Ramanujan graphs. In examinations, candidates may be asked to state the four conjectures, to sketch why rationality follows from the Lefschetz trace formula in étale cohomology, or to explain how the Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields implies the Sato–Tate distribution for elliptic curves with complex multiplication. A frequent error is to claim that Deligne proved the full Weil conjectures alone; in fact Dwork, Grothendieck, and Artin contributed essential pieces.

Worked Example: Applying the Weil Bound
Let C be a smooth projective curve of genus g over 𝔽q. Use the Weil conjectures to derive the Hasse–Weil bound for #C(𝔽q).
The zeta function of C is Z(C,T) = (1−α1 T)…(1−α2g T) / ((1−T)(1−qT)) where the αi are the reciprocal roots of the numerator. By the Riemann hypothesis part of the Weil conjectures, |αi| = √q for all i. The number of 𝔽q‑rational points is #C(𝔽q) = q+1−∑i=12g αi. Taking absolute values and using the triangle inequality gives |#C(𝔽q) − (q+1)| ≤ ∑ |αi| = 2g√q. Hence the Hasse–Weil bound: |#C(𝔽q) − (q+1)| ≤ 2g√q.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a consequence of the Weil conjectures, first identify whether it concerns rationality, the functional equation, or the Riemann hypothesis. The Riemann hypothesis part is the only one that gives explicit numerical bounds (e.g., Hasse–Weil, Ramanujan–Petersson). Memorise the statement of the Hasse–Weil bound for curves and the fact that the eigenvalues of Frobenius on H1 have absolute value √q. This single fact solves many multiple‑choice questions on point counts and on the Sato–Tate measure for elliptic curves with complex multiplication.

Class Field Theory: The Kronecker–Weber Theorem and Hilbert Class Fields

Class field theory describes the abelian extensions of a number field K in terms of arithmetic objects attached to K. The Kronecker–Weber theorem (1853, completed by Hilbert 1896) states that every finite abelian extension of ℚ is contained in a cyclotomic field ℚ(ζn) for some n. Equivalently, the maximal abelian extension ℚab is the union of all cyclotomic fields. Its Galois group Gal(ℚab/ℚ) is canonically isomorphic to the profinite completion of ℤ×, i.e. the idèle class group of ℚ. For a general number field K, the Hilbert class field H is the maximal unramified abelian extension of K. The Artin reciprocity isomorphism identifies Gal(H/K) with the ideal class group Cl(K). The existence of H was proved by Hilbert (1897) using the theory of Kummer extensions and the principal ideal theorem. The Takagi existence theorem (1920) generalises this: for any modulus 𝔪, there is a unique ray class field K𝔪 whose Galois group is the ray class group modulo 𝔪. The Chebotarev density theorem (1922) then shows that the Artin map is surjective and its kernel is the connected component of the idèle class group. In modern language, class field theory is the statement that the abelianised Galois group of K is the profinite completion of the idèle class group CK. This isomorphism is functorial and compatible with local‑global principles. Examiners may ask to state the Kronecker–Weber theorem, to compute the Hilbert class field of ℚ(√−5) (which is ℚ(√−5, i) of degree 2 over ℚ(√−5)), or to explain why the Hilbert class field is unramified at all finite primes. A common pitfall is confusing the Hilbert class field with the genus field (the maximal abelian extension of K that is a compositum of K with an abelian extension of ℚ). The genus field is always contained in the Hilbert class field but can be strictly smaller.

The Modularity Lifting Theorems and the Taylor–Wiles Method

The modularity lifting theorems, culminating in the work of Wiles (1995), Taylor–Wiles (1995), and Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor (2001), prove that every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular. The strategy is to show that a Galois representation ρ: Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) → GL2(ℤp) arising from an elliptic curve is isomorphic to the representation attached to a modular form. The Taylor–Wiles method introduces auxiliary primes to enlarge the deformation ring R and the Hecke algebra T, then proves an R = T isomorphism by a numerical criterion (the Wiles–Lenstra criterion). The key innovation is the “patching” argument: one constructs a system of deformation rings RQ and Hecke algebras TQ for sets Q of auxiliary primes, then takes an inverse limit to obtain a power series ring over which the patched module is free. This freeness forces R = T. The method requires the representation to be irreducible, odd, and minimally ramified at p, and it uses the fact that the local deformation condition at p is “flat” (crystalline with Hodge–Tate weights 0,1). The modularity lifting theorems have been extended to many other settings: Hilbert modular forms over totally real fields (Taylor, 2003), symmetric power lifts (Clozel–Harris–Taylor, 2008), and potential automorphy for higher‑dimensional Galois representations (Barnet‑Lamb–Gee–Geraghty–Taylor, 2014). In examinations, candidates may be asked to outline the Taylor–Wiles patching argument, to state the numerical criterion for R = T, or to explain why the “minimal” case (no auxiliary primes) fails for the Frey curve attached to a putative counterexample to Fermat’s Last Theorem. A frequent trap is to claim that the Taylor–Wiles method proves the full Langlands correspondence for GL2; it only proves modularity for representations that are known to be automorphic after a solvable base change (potential modularity).

p‑Adic Hodge Theory and the Fontaine–Mazur Conjecture

p‑Adic Hodge theory, developed by Fontaine in the 1980s, relates p‑adic Galois representations of a p‑adic field K to linear algebraic data: filtered φ‑modules (crystalline representations), (φ,Γ)‑modules (semistable representations), and de Rham representations. The fundamental period rings BdR, Bcris, Bst provide functors DdR, Dcris, Dst from the category of p‑adic Galois representations to the category of filtered vector spaces with additional structure. A representation is de Rham if dimK DdR(V) = dimℚp V; it is crystalline if the Frobenius on Dcris(V) is semisimple and the filtration is trivial after tensoring with Bcris. Fontaine’s conjecture (proved by Berger, 2002) states that every de Rham representation is potentially semistable. The Fontaine–Mazur conjecture (1995) predicts that an irreducible p‑adic Galois representation ρ: Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) → GLn(ℚp) that is unramified outside finitely many primes and de Rham at p comes from geometry, i.e. it occurs as a subquotient of the étale cohomology of a smooth projective variety over ℚ. This conjecture is a p‑adic analogue of the Hodge conjecture and is known in many cases: for n=2 it follows from modularity lifting theorems (Kisin, 2009); for symmetric powers of modular forms it follows from potential automorphy. The conjecture also implies the Tate conjecture for varieties over number fields. In examinations, students may be asked to define the period rings BdR and Bcris, to state the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture precisely, or to explain why the condition “de Rham at p” is the correct p‑adic analogue of “Hodge–Tate weights”. A common error is to confuse the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture with the Bloch–Kato conjecture (which concerns Selmer groups and special values of L‑functions) or to assume that every de Rham representation is crystalline; the latter fails for representations with non‑trivial monodromy (semistable but not crystalline).

  • Claiming Deligne proved all four Weil conjectures single‑handedly; Dwork, Grothendieck, and Artin contributed essential parts.
  • Confusing the Hilbert class field with the genus field; the genus field is the maximal abelian extension of K that is a compositum with an abelian extension of ℚ.
  • Stating that the Taylor–Wiles method proves the full Langlands correspondence for GL2; it only yields modularity for potentially modular representations.
  • Believing every de Rham representation is crystalline; semistable representations with non‑zero monodromy are de Rham but not crystalline.
  • Thinking the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture is a theorem for all dimensions; it is known only in special cases (n=2, symmetric powers).
  • Misidentifying the Kronecker–Weber theorem as the statement that every abelian extension of any number field is cyclotomic; it holds only for ℚ.

L-Functions: Analytic Continuation, Functional Equations, and Special Values

The Riemann zeta function ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 n−s (Re s > 1) is the prototype of an L-function. A general L-function L(s) = ∑ an n−s is expected to satisfy: (i) analytic continuation to a meromorphic function on ℂ with finitely many poles; (ii) a functional equation relating L(s) to L(1−s) via a gamma factor and a root number ε with |ε| = 1; (iii) an Euler product L(s) = ∏p Lp(p−s)−1 where each local factor is a polynomial in p−s of bounded degree. Dirichlet L-functions L(s, χ) = ∑ χ(n) n−s for primitive characters χ modulo q satisfy Λ(s, χ) = (π/q)−(s+a)/2 Γ((s+a)/2) L(s, χ) = ε(χ) Λ(1−s, χ̄) with a = 0 if χ(−1)=1 and a = 1 if χ(−1)=−1, and |ε(χ)| = 1. For a normalized Hecke eigenform f of weight k and level N, the completed L-function Λ(f, s) = (2π)−s Γ(s) L(f, s) satisfies Λ(f, s) = ik εf Λ(f, k−s) with εf = ±1. Special values at integers are deeply arithmetic: L(1, χ) relates to class numbers of quadratic fields via Dirichlet's class number formula; L(f, k/2) for even weight k connects to periods of f and, by the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, to the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group of the associated elliptic curve. Deligne's theorem (1979) proves that critical values L(f, m) for 1 ≤ m ≤ k−1 are algebraic multiples of explicit periods. The Selberg class formalizes these axioms; every known L-function in the Selberg class is conjectured to be automorphic (Langlands program). In examinations, candidates may be asked to write the functional equation for L(s, χ), identify the gamma factor and root number, or explain why L(1, χ) ≠ 0 for non-principal χ is equivalent to the non-vanishing of class numbers. A common trap is to confuse the completed L-function Λ(s) with the Dirichlet series L(s); the functional equation holds for Λ, not for L alone.

Iwasawa Theory: p-Adic L-Functions and the Main Conjecture

Iwasawa theory studies the growth of arithmetic objects (class groups, Selmer groups) in ℤp-extensions. Let K∞/K be the cyclotomic ℤp-extension of a number field K, with Galois group Γ ≅ ℤp. The Iwasawa algebra Λ = ℤp[[Γ]] ≅ ℤp[[T]] is a regular local ring of dimension 2. For an abelian extension L/K, the inverse limit X = lim← Cl(Ln)[p∞] of p-primary class groups is a finitely generated torsion Λ-module. The structure theorem gives X ∼ ⊕ Λ/(pμi) ⊕ Λ/(fj(T)λj) where fj are distinguished polynomials. The Iwasawa invariants μ, λ, ν describe the growth: |Cl(Kn)[p∞]| = pμ pn + λ n + ν for large n. Ferrero–Washington (1979) proved μ = 0 for abelian extensions of ℚ. The p-adic L-function Lp(s, χ) interpolates special values L(1−k, χ ωk) for integers k ≥ 1, where ω is the Teichmüller character. Kubota–Leopoldt (1964) constructed Lp(s, χ) as a measure on ℤp×; it corresponds to an element of Λ via the Amice transform. The Main Conjecture (proved by Mazur–Wiles 1984 for ℚ, Wiles 1990 for totally real fields) states that the characteristic ideal of X equals the ideal generated by the p-adic L-function: charΛ(X) = (Lp(T)). For elliptic curves E/ℚ with good ordinary reduction at p, the Selmer group Selp∞(E/K∞) is a Λ-module, and the p-adic L-function Lp(E, s) (Mazur–Swinnerton-Dyer, 1974) interpolates L(E, 1)/ΩE twisted by characters of Γ. The Main Conjecture for elliptic curves (Kato 2004, Skinner–Urban 2014) relates charΛ(Sel) to Lp(E). In examinations, students may be asked to define the Iwasawa algebra, state the structure theorem for torsion Λ-modules, or explain the interpolation property of the Kubota–Leopoldt p-adic L-function. A frequent error is to claim the Main Conjecture is proved for all elliptic curves over all number fields; it is known for ℚ and certain totally real fields, and for modular elliptic curves over ℚ by Kato and Skinner–Urban.

Worked Example: Functional Equation of a Dirichlet L-Function
Let χ be the primitive Dirichlet character modulo 5 defined by χ(1)=1, χ(2)=i, χ(3)=−i, χ(4)=−1. Write the completed L-function Λ(s, χ) and its functional equation. Determine the root number ε(χ).
The conductor is q = 5. Since χ(−1) = χ(4) = −1, the character is odd, so a = 1. The gamma factor is Γ((s+1)/2). The completed L-function is Λ(s, χ) = (π/5)−(s+1)/2 Γ((s+1)/2) L(s, χ). The functional equation is Λ(s, χ) = ε(χ) Λ(1−s, χ̄). The root number is ε(χ) = τ(χ) / (i √5) where τ(χ) = ∑a=14 χ(a) e2πi a/5 is the Gauss sum. Compute τ(χ) = χ(1)ζ + χ(2)ζ2 + χ(3)ζ3 + χ(4)ζ4 with ζ = e2πi/5. Using χ(3)=−i, χ(4)=−1 and ζ3=ζ̄2, ζ4=ζ̄, one finds τ(χ) = i√5. Thus ε(χ) = (i√5) / (i√5) = 1. The functional equation is Λ(s, χ) = Λ(1−s, χ̄).
Exam Strategy: L-Functions and Iwasawa Theory
Memorize the shape of the completed L-function Λ(s, χ) = (π/q)−(s+a)/2 Γ((s+a)/2) L(s, χ) and the parity condition a = 0 (even) or 1 (odd). For Iwasawa theory, know the definitions: Λ = ℤp[[T]], the structure theorem for torsion modules, and the statement charΛ(X) = (Lp(T)). Examiners often test whether you can distinguish between the Iwasawa invariants μ, λ, ν and their role in the growth formula pμ pn + λ n + ν. Practice computing Gauss sums for small conductors (3, 4, 5, 7, 8) to find root numbers quickly.

Serre's Modularity Conjecture and the Khare–Wintenberger Proof

Serre's conjecture (1987) predicts that every odd, irreducible, continuous Galois representation ρ: Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) → GL2(𝔽p) arises from a modular form. The conjecture specifies the optimal weight k(ρ) and level N(ρ) in terms of local invariants: at p, the weight is determined by the restriction ρ|Gal(ℚ̄p/ℚp) via Fontaine's classification of mod p representations; at primes ℓ ≠ p, the level exponent is the Artin conductor of ρ|Iℓ (tame part) plus a wild part. The conjecture has two parts: (1) modularity — ρ is modular of some weight and level; (2) optimality — the minimal weight and level are given by Serre's recipe. The proof proceeded in stages: Ribet (1990) proved level lowering, showing that if ρ is modular of level N, it is also modular of the minimal level N(ρ). Edixhoven (1992) handled weight lowering for p ≥ 3. The remaining case p = 2 was settled by Buzzard–Dickinson–Shepherd-Barron–Taylor (2001) using Hilbert modular forms. The full modularity (part 1) was proved by Khare and Wintenberger (2009) using a method of "modularity switching" between primes p and ℓ. They introduced a compatible system of Galois representations and used the Taylor–Wiles method to prove modularity for a dense set of primes, then applied a Cebotarev density argument to conclude for all primes. The proof also uses the fact that the universal deformation ring for a residual representation with "large" image is a complete intersection, which follows from the numerical criterion of Wiles. In examinations, candidates may be asked to state Serre's recipe for the optimal weight k(ρ) (e.g., k = 2 if ρ|Ip is trivial, k = p+1 if ρ|Ip is a non-trivial extension of ω by 1, etc.), to explain the role of level lowering in the proof, or to describe the modularity switching argument. A common trap is to confuse Serre's conjecture (mod p representations) with the modularity theorem (p-adic representations); the former implies the latter via lifting, but they are distinct statements. Another error is to claim the conjecture is proved for all number fields; it is known only for ℚ and certain totally real fields (Georges–Freitas–Le Hung 2015).

Faltings' Theorem and Rational Points on Curves

Faltings' theorem (1983), formerly the Mordell conjecture, states that a smooth projective curve C of genus g ≥ 2 over a number field K has only finitely many K-rational points. The proof uses three pillars: (1) the Kodaira–Parshin construction, which embeds C into its Jacobian J and covers J by abelian varieties of higher dimension to reduce to a statement about rational points on abelian varieties; (2) the Tate conjecture for abelian varieties over number fields (proved by Faltings as a lemma), which implies that HomK(A, B) ⊗ ℚℓ ≅ HomGal(K)(VℓA, VℓB) for abelian varieties A, B; (3) a height argument on the moduli space of principally polarized abelian varieties, using the fact that the set of abelian varieties with good reduction outside a fixed finite set of primes is finite up to isomorphism (Shafarevich's conjecture, proved by Faltings). The theorem is ineffective: it does not provide an algorithm to find all rational points or a bound on their number. Effective methods include Chabauty's method (when rank J(K) < g), the Mordell–Weil sieve, and the method of Coleman (p-adic integration). For genus 1 (elliptic curves), the Mordell–Weil theorem gives finite generation of E(K), but the set of rational points can be infinite. For genus 0, the Hasse principle holds: C(K) ≠ ∅ iff C has points everywhere locally. Faltings also proved the Lang conjecture for subvarieties of abelian varieties: a subvariety of an abelian variety contains only finitely many translates of abelian subvarieties. In examinations, students may be asked to state Faltings' theorem precisely, to explain why the genus condition g ≥ 2 is necessary (counterexamples: genus 0 has infinitely many points if one exists; genus 1 can have infinite Mordell–Weil group), or to outline the Kodaira–Parshin trick. A frequent error is to claim Faltings' theorem gives an algorithm to find all rational points; it is purely existential. Another is to confuse Faltings' theorem with Siegel's theorem on integral points on affine curves (which is effective for genus ≥ 1 via Baker's method).

The Circle Method: Vinogradov's Theorem and Goldbach's Problem

The Hardy–Littlewood circle method decomposes the unit interval [0, 1] into major arcs 𝔐 (neighbourhoods of rational numbers a/q with small q) and minor arcs 𝔪 (the complement). For an additive problem like representing N as a sum of primes, one writes the generating function f(α) = ∑n≤N Λ(n) e(nα) where Λ is the von Mangoldt function, and the number of representations is ∫01 f(α)k e(−Nα) dα. On major arcs, α ≈ a/q, one approximates f(α) using the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions: f(a/q + β) ≈ (μ(q)/φ(q)) ∑n≤N e(nβ) + error. The singular series 𝔖(N) = ∑q=1∞ (μ(q)/φ(q))k cq(−N) captures the local obstructions, where cq(n) is the Ramanujan sum. Vinogradov (1937) proved that every sufficiently large odd integer N is a sum of three primes (ternary Goldbach). His proof uses the estimate ∫𝔪 |f(α)|3 dα = o(N2/log3 N) via Vaughan's identity (1977) to decompose Λ into Type I and Type II sums. The binary Goldbach problem (even N as sum of two primes) remains open; the circle method gives an asymptotic formula for the number of representations assuming GRH, but unconditionally the minor arc estimate for k=2 is insufficient. Chen (1973) proved that every sufficiently large even integer is a sum of a prime and an almost-prime (P2), using a weighted sieve combined with the circle method. Helfgott (2013) completed the proof of the ternary Goldbach conjecture for all odd N ≥ 7 by refining the major/minor arc decomposition and using numerical verification for small N. The circle method also applies to Waring's problem: every sufficiently large integer is a sum of g(k) k-th powers, where g(k) = 2k + ⌊(3/2)k⌋ − 2 (Hilbert–Kamke). In examinations, candidates may be asked to define major and minor arcs, to write the singular series for the ternary Goldbach problem, or to explain why the binary Goldbach problem is harder than the ternary (the minor arc integral for k=2 is not absolutely convergent). A common trap is to state that Vinogradov's theorem proves Goldbach's conjecture; it only proves the ternary (three-prime) version for large odd integers. Another error is to claim the circle method gives an effective bound for "sufficiently large"; the original proof was ineffective due to Siegel zeros, though modern versions (Liu–Wang 2002) give explicit bounds.

  • Claiming the Iwasawa Main Conjecture is proved for all elliptic curves over all number fields; it is known for ℚ and certain totally real fields.
  • Stating that Serre's conjecture (mod p) is the same as the modularity theorem (p-adic); they are distinct, though the former implies the latter via lifting.
  • Believing Faltings' theorem provides an algorithm to find all rational points; it is purely ineffective and existential.
  • Asserting that Vinogradov's theorem proves the binary Goldbach conjecture; it proves only the ternary version for large odd integers.
  • Confusing the Iwasawa invariants μ, λ, ν with the exponents in the class group growth formula; μ is the coefficient of pn, λ of n, ν the constant.
  • Claiming the circle method gives an effective bound for "sufficiently large" in Vinogradov's theorem without mentioning the Siegel zero issue; the original proof was ineffective.

The Selberg Sieve and Its Applications

The Selberg sieve, introduced by Atle Selberg in 1947, is a combinatorial upper‑bound sieve that replaces the inclusion–exclusion weights of the Brun sieve with a quadratic form optimised by a variational principle. For a set A of integers and a set of primes P, one chooses non‑negative weights λ_d supported on square‑free d composed of primes from P, with λ1 = 1, and bounds the sifted set S(A, P, z) = #{a ∈ A : (a, P(z)) = 1} by ∑d|P(z) λ_d |A_d|, where A_d = {a ∈ A : d | a}. Selberg's key insight was to minimise the quadratic form ∑d1,d2 λd1 λd2 / [d1,d2] subject to λ1 = 1, leading to the optimal choice λ_d = μ(d) (log(z/d)/log z) for d ≤ z and 0 otherwise. This yields the fundamental inequality S(A, P, z) ≤ X / G(z) + O(∑d≤z |r_d|) where X = |A|, G(z) = ∑d≤z μ(d)2 / φ(d) and r_d = |A_d| − X/d. The sieve is particularly effective when the remainder terms r_d are small on average, a condition captured by the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem for primes in arithmetic progressions. Applications include Chen's theorem (1973) that every sufficiently large even integer is a sum of a prime and an almost‑prime P2, the proof that there are infinitely many primes p such that p+2 has at most two prime factors, and upper bounds for the number of twin primes up to x: π2(x) ≪ x (log log x)2 / (log x)2. The Selberg sieve also underpins the GPY (Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım) method that led to bounded gaps between primes; the GPY weight is a Selberg‑type weight with a carefully chosen polynomial. In examinations, candidates may be asked to derive the Selberg weights, to state the fundamental inequality, or to explain why the sieve gives an upper bound but not a lower bound. A common trap is to confuse the Selberg sieve with the large sieve; the former is a combinatorial sieve for sifting sets, the latter is an analytic inequality for exponential sums.

The Green–Tao Theorem on Arithmetic Progressions of Primes

In 2004 Ben Green and Terence Tao proved that the primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. Their proof combines three major ingredients: (1) a transference principle that allows one to work with a dense subset of a pseudorandom measure rather than the primes themselves; (2) Szemerédi's theorem (1975) stating that any subset of the integers with positive upper density contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions; (3) a construction of a pseudorandom majorant ν(n) for the von Mangoldt function Λ(n) that satisfies the linear forms condition and the correlation condition required by the transference principle. The majorant is built using the Goldston–Yıldırım sieve weights, which are Selberg‑type weights adapted to detect almost‑primes. The linear forms condition demands that for any bounded complexity system of linear forms ψ_i(n) = a_i·n + b_i, the average of ∏ ν(ψ_i(n)) over a large box is 1 + o(1). The correlation condition controls correlations of ν with nilsequences. Green and Tao verified these conditions using the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem and the Siegel–Walfisz theorem for primes in arithmetic progressions. The theorem does not give an effective bound for the length of the progression in terms of the size of the primes; effective bounds were developed in subsequent work. In examinations, candidates may be asked to state the transference principle, to define the linear forms condition, or to explain why Szemerédi's theorem alone does not apply directly to the primes (the primes have zero density). A frequent error is to claim that the Green–Tao theorem proves the twin prime conjecture; it only guarantees progressions of length k for any fixed k, not infinitely many progressions of length 2 with bounded gap.

Worked Example: Applying the Selberg Sieve to Twin Primes
Use the Selberg sieve to show that the number of twin prime pairs up to x satisfies π2(x) ≪ x (log log x)2 / (log x)2.
Let A = {n(n+2) : n ≤ x}. We sift by primes p ≤ z = x1/2. For each p, the condition p ∤ n(n+2) removes two residue classes modulo p (except p=2 where it removes one). The sifting density is κ = 2. The Selberg sieve upper bound gives S(A, P, z) ≤ X / G(z) + error, where X ≈ x and G(z) = ∑d≤z μ(d)2 / φ(d) ∼ (e^γ / 2) log z by Mertens. With z = x1/2, log z = (1/2) log x. The main term becomes x / ((e^γ/2) log z) ≪ x / log x. However, the twin prime problem requires a lower bound; the Selberg sieve only gives an upper bound. To obtain the stated bound, one uses the Selberg sieve with a weight that detects numbers with at most two prime factors, leading to π2(x) ≪ x (log log x)2 / (log x)2. The extra (log log x)2 factor arises from the optimisation of the quadratic form when the sifting dimension is 2.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for an upper bound via the Selberg sieve, write down the fundamental inequality, identify the sifting dimension κ, and quote the asymptotic G(z) ∼ (e^γ κ / Γ(κ)) (log z)^κ. For Green–Tao, remember the three pillars: transference, Szemerédi, pseudorandom majorant. Do not confuse the linear forms condition with the Gowers uniformity norms; the former is a condition on the majorant, the latter appears in the proof of Szemerédi's theorem.

The Gross–Zagier Formula and Heegner Points

The Gross–Zagier formula (1986) relates the height of Heegner points on a modular elliptic curve E/ℚ to the derivative at s = 1 of the L‑function L(E, s) twisted by a quadratic character. Let K be an imaginary quadratic field of discriminant D < 0 satisfying the Heegner hypothesis: all primes dividing the conductor N of E split in K. For each ideal class a of K, one constructs a Heegner point y_a ∈ E(K) via the modular parametrisation X0(N) → E. The Néron–Tate height pairing ⟨y_a, y_b⟩ is then expressed as a sum over ideal classes involving the derivative L'(E/K, χ, 1) where χ runs over characters of the class group. The formula reads ⟨y_a, y_b⟩ = (C / √|D|) L'(E/K, 1) + (explicit local terms), where C is a non‑zero constant depending on the modular parametrisation. This result was pivotal for the proof of the Birch and Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture in rank 1: Kolyvagin (1990) used the non‑vanishing of a single Heegner point to bound the Selmer group and prove that if L(E, 1) ≠ 0 then E(ℚ) has rank 0, and if L(E, 1) = 0 but L'(E, 1) ≠ 0 then E(ℚ) has rank 1 and the Heegner point generates a subgroup of finite index. The formula also underlies later work on the BSD conjecture for elliptic curves over totally real fields. In examinations, candidates may be asked to state the Heegner hypothesis, to define the Néron–Tate height, or to explain why the derivative of the L‑function appears (the functional equation forces L(E/K, s) to vanish at s = 1). A common trap is to confuse Heegner points with CM points on Shimura curves; Heegner points live on modular curves X0(N) and require the Heegner hypothesis, while CM points exist for any imaginary quadratic order.

The Langlands Program for GL(2) and Automorphic Representations

The Langlands program for GL(2) over ℚ predicts a bijection between irreducible admissible representations of GL2(𝔸_ℚ) (automorphic representations) and 2‑dimensional Frobenius‑semisimple representations of the absolute Galois group Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) (Galois representations). The classical case: a holomorphic newform f of weight k ≥ 2, level N, and nebentypus χ corresponds to a compatible system of ℓ‑adic Galois representations ρ_f,ℓ : Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) → GL2(ℚ_ℓ) unramified outside Nℓ, with trace of Frobenius at p ∤ Nℓ equal to the Hecke eigenvalue a_p(f). The modularity theorem (formerly Taniyama–Shimura–Weil) is the statement that every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular, i.e. its ℓ‑adic representation arises from a weight‑2 newform. The proof by Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, and Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor uses the Taylor–Wiles method: one deforms a residual mod p representation to characteristic 0 by patching together deformation rings and Hecke algebras. The local Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚ_p) classifies irreducible smooth representations of GL2(ℚ_p) in terms of 2‑dimensional representations of the Weil–Deligne group W'_ℚ_p. The global correspondence is expected to be functorial: automorphic induction, base change, and symmetric power lifts. The Sato–Tate conjecture (proved by Clozel, Harris, Shepherd‑Barron, Taylor 2008) describes the distribution of Frobenius traces for non‑CM elliptic curves and follows from the potential automorphy of symmetric powers. In examinations, candidates may be asked to define a newform, to state the modularity theorem, or to explain the role of the Taylor–Wiles patching argument. A frequent error is to claim that the Langlands correspondence is a theorem for all reductive groups; it is known in full for GL_n (by Harris–Taylor, Henniart) and a few other cases.

The Theory of Exponential Sums and Weyl's Inequality

Exponential sums S(α) = ∑n=1^N e(α n^k) with e(x) = e2π i x are central to the Hardy–Littlewood circle method and to bounds for the Riemann zeta function. Weyl's inequality (1916) gives a non‑trivial estimate for S(α) when α is close to a rational a/q with small q. Write α = a/q + β with (a,q)=1, |β| ≤ 1/q2. Then |S(α)| ≤ C_k N1+ε (q-1 + N-1 + q/N^k)1/2k-1. The proof uses repeated squaring and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to reduce the degree k to 1, at which point the sum is a geometric series. Vinogradov (1937) improved the exponent for cubic and higher sums using his mean value theorem. The modern efficient congruencing method of Wooley (2012) and the decoupling method of Bourgain–Demeter–Guth (2015) give essentially optimal bounds for the Vinogradov mean value theorem, which in turn yield the best known estimates for Weyl sums. These bounds are crucial for the minor arc estimates in the circle method: for the ternary Goldbach problem one needs ∫_𝔪 |f(α)|3 dα = o(N2 / log3 N), where f(α) = ∑n≤N Λ(n) e(α n). Vaughan's identity decomposes Λ into Type I and Type II sums, and Weyl‑type estimates for the Type II sums give the required cancellation. In examinations, candidates may be asked to state Weyl's inequality for k = 2 (quadratic Weyl sum) or to explain how the inequality is used to bound the minor arcs. A common trap is to apply Weyl's inequality directly to the von Mangoldt sum f(α); one must first decompose Λ via Vaughan's identity or Heath‑Brown's identity to separate the arithmetic structure from the oscillation.

Tate's Thesis: Adeles, Ideles, and the Functional Equation

John Tate's 1950 Princeton thesis, Fourier Analysis in Number Fields and Hecke's Zeta-Functions, unified the Riemann zeta function and Hecke L-functions on the adelic ring 𝔸_ℚ. The adele ring is the restricted product ∏'_v ℚ_v over all places v of ℚ (the real place ∞ and the p-adic places), with all but finitely many components in ℤ_p. The idele group 𝕀_ℚ = 𝔸_ℚ^× is the group of invertible adeles. Tate defined Haar measures on 𝔸_ℚ and 𝕀_ℚ, constructed the Fourier transform on 𝔸_ℚ, and proved the functional equation for the completed zeta function ξ(s) = π-s/2 Γ(s/2) ζ(s) by applying Poisson summation to the Schwartz–Bruhat function φ(x) = ∏_v φ_v(x_v), where φ_∞(x) = e-π x2 and φ_p(x) = 1ℤ_p(x). The key identity is ∫𝕀_ℚ φ(x) |x|^s d^×x = ξ(s), and Poisson summation yields ξ(s) = ξ(1-s). For a Hecke character χ : 𝕀_ℚ/ℚ^× → ℂ^×, the same method gives Λ(s, χ) = ε(χ) Λ(1-s, χ̄), where Λ(s, χ) = ∏_v L_v(s, χ_v) includes local gamma factors, and ε(χ) is the global root number. Tate's thesis also introduced the local functional equation, the classification of Hecke characters (Grössencharaktere), and the interpretation of ζ(s) as an integral over the idele class group 𝕀_ℚ/ℚ^×. This adelic framework underpins the modern theory of automorphic forms and the Langlands program. In examinations, candidates may be asked to define the adele ring, state the product formula ∏_v |x|_v = 1 for x ∈ ℚ^×, or explain how Poisson summation on 𝔸_ℚ yields the functional equation. A frequent error is confusing the additive Haar measure dx on 𝔸_ℚ with the multiplicative measure d^×x = dx/|x| on 𝕀_ℚ; the latter is used for the zeta integral. Another trap is omitting the restriction condition in the restricted product, which ensures local compactness.

The Selberg Trace Formula and Arthur's Classification

The Selberg trace formula, developed by Atle Selberg in 1956, is a non-abelian generalization of Poisson summation. For a discrete cofinite subgroup Γ of a Lie group G (typically G = SL2(ℝ) or SL2(ℂ)), it equates the spectral side — a sum over eigenvalues of the Laplacian on ΓG/K — to the geometric side — a sum over conjugacy classes in Γ. The spectral side involves the discrete spectrum (Maass cusp forms and residues of Eisenstein series) and the continuous spectrum (Eisenstein series). The geometric side splits into the identity contribution (volume term), hyperbolic contribution (primitive closed geodesics), elliptic contribution (elliptic fixed points), and parabolic contribution (cusps). For G = SL2(ℝ), the formula reads ∑_j h(r_j) + (1/4π) ∫-∞∞ h(r) (φ'/φ)(1/2+ir) dr = (vol/4π) ∫ h(r) r tanh(πr) dr + ∑γ (ℓ(γ0)/2 sinh(ℓ(γ)/2)) g(ℓ(γ)) + elliptic terms, where h is an even Paley–Wiener function, r_j are spectral parameters λ_j = 1/4 + r_j2, and g is the Fourier transform of h. James Arthur generalized this to the invariant trace formula for reductive groups over global fields (1970s–1990s), stabilizing the geometric side by transferring orbital integrals to endoscopic groups. Arthur's classification of the discrete automorphic spectrum for classical groups expresses multiplicities via Arthur parameters — homomorphisms from the conjectural Langlands group L_F to the L-group ^L G — using the stable trace formula. The proof relies on the fundamental lemma (proved by Ngô Bảo Châu in 2008) to match orbital integrals. In examinations, candidates may be asked to state the geometric and spectral sides for SL2(ℤ)SL2(ℝ), define an Arthur parameter, or explain endoscopy's role in stabilization. A common trap is conflating the Selberg trace formula (for a specific arithmetic quotient) with the Arthur–Selberg trace formula (the general invariant formula). Another error is claiming Arthur's classification proves the full Langlands correspondence; it classifies the discrete spectrum for classical groups assuming the fundamental lemma and other hypotheses, but does not establish functoriality in general.

Worked Example: Local Zeta Integral for GL(1)
Compute the local zeta integral Z(s, χ_p, φ_p) = ∫ℚ_p^× χ_p(x) φ_p(x) |x|_p^s d^×x for the unramified character χ_p(x) = |x|_pit and φ_p = 1ℤ_p, with d^×x normalized so that vol(ℤ_p^×) = 1.
The integral splits over the valuation: ∫ℚ_p^× = ∑n∈ℤ ∫p^n ℤ_p^×. For x = p^n u with u ∈ ℤ_p^×, we have |x|_p = p-n, χ_p(x) = p-int, and φ_p(x) = 1 if n ≥ 0 else 0. The measure d^×x = (1-p-1)-1 |x|_p-1 dx gives vol(p^n ℤ_p^×) = p-n(1-p-1) for n ≥ 0. Thus Z(s, χ_p, φ_p) = ∑n=0∞ p-n(s+it) p-n(1-p-1) = (1-p-1) ∑n=0∞ p-n(s+it+1) = (1-p-1) / (1 - p-(s+it+1)). The standard local L-factor is L_p(s, χ_p) = (1 - χ_p(p) p-s)-1 = (1 - p-(s+it))-1. The integral differs by the factor (1-p-1) p-(s+it) because φ_p is not the standard Schwartz–Bruhat function used in Tate's thesis. With additive Haar measure normalized so vol(ℤ_p) = 1, the multiplicative measure satisfies d^×x = (1-p-1)-1 dx/|x|_p, and the integral yields exactly L_p(s, χ_p). The key point: the local zeta integral for an unramified character and the characteristic function of ℤ_p reproduces the local L-factor up to an explicit elementary factor depending on measure normalization.
Exam Strategy: Trace Formula Components
When a question asks for the terms in the Selberg trace formula, list them in order: (1) identity contribution (volume), (2) hyperbolic contribution (sum over conjugacy classes of hyperbolic elements, i.e., closed geodesics), (3) elliptic contribution (sum over elliptic fixed points), (4) parabolic contribution (cusps, related to Eisenstein series). On the spectral side: (1) discrete spectrum (cusp forms), (2) continuous spectrum (Eisenstein series, scattering matrix). For Arthur's invariant trace formula, emphasize stabilization: the geometric side is expressed as a sum over endoscopic groups H of stable orbital integrals on H, and the fundamental lemma matches orbital integrals on G with stable orbital integrals on H. Memorize the fundamental lemma statement: for a regular semisimple γ ∈ G(F) and its transfer δ ∈ H(F), the orbital integral O_γ(1_K) equals the stable orbital integral SO_δ(1K_H) up to a transfer factor. This is the precise technical statement Ngô proved for all reductive groups in characteristic zero.

The Fundamental Lemma and Ngô Bảo Châu's Proof

The fundamental lemma, conjectured by Robert Langlands and Diana Shelstad in 1987, is a combinatorial identity between orbital integrals on a reductive group G and stable orbital integrals on its endoscopic groups H. It is the crucial local ingredient for stabilizing the Arthur–Selberg trace formula and proving the endoscopic classification. Let F be a non-archimedean local field of characteristic zero, G a connected reductive group over F, and H an endoscopic group for G. For a regular semisimple γ ∈ G(F) that is a norm from H, there exists a corresponding δ ∈ H(F). The fundamental lemma asserts that the orbital integral O_γ(1_K) = ∫G_γ(F)G(F) 1_K(g-1γg) dg for a hyperspecial maximal compact subgroup K ⊂ G(F) equals the stable orbital integral SO_δ(1K_H) = ∑δ' ∼ δ Oδ'(1K_H) on H, up to an explicit transfer factor Δ(γ, δ). Here ∼ denotes stable conjugacy. The lemma was proved for many special cases (G = SL2, GL_n, unramified unitary groups) by Langlands, Kottwitz, Shelstad, Hales, and others, but the general case remained open until Ngô Bảo Châu's 2008 proof using the Hitchin fibration. Ngô's method interprets orbital integrals as counting points on fibers of the Hitchin map h : M_G → A, where M_G is the moduli stack of G-Higgs bundles on a smooth projective curve X over a finite field 𝔽_q, and A is the Hitchin base. The fundamental lemma becomes a statement about equality of certain perverse sheaves on A, specifically the decomposition theorem for the Hitchin fibration. Ngô proved that the direct image complex R h_* ℚ_ℓ decomposes as a direct sum of intersection cohomology complexes of the supports of the endoscopic groups, and the lemma follows from equality of the stalks. The proof uses perverse sheaves, the decomposition theorem of Beilinson–Bernstein–Deligne–Gabber, and the geometry of the nilpotent cone. In examinations, candidates may be asked to state the fundamental lemma for G = SL2 and H = SO2 (a torus), define orbital and stable orbital integrals, or explain the Hitchin fibration's role in Ngô's proof. A frequent error is confusing the fundamental lemma (a local identity) with the global fundamental lemma (a trace formula statement) or the transfer conjecture (existence of matching functions). Another trap is claiming Ngô's proof uses the geometric Langlands correspondence; it uses the Hitchin fibration, which is related to geometric Langlands, but the proof is independent of the full conjecture.

Perfectoid Spaces and the p-adic Langlands Correspondence

Perfectoid spaces, introduced by Peter Scholze in 2011, are a class of adic spaces generalizing rigid-analytic varieties and providing a geometric framework for p-adic Hodge theory and the p-adic Langlands correspondence. A perfectoid field is a complete non-archimedean field K of characteristic zero with residue characteristic p such that the Frobenius map O_K/p → O_K/p is surjective and the value group |K^×| is not discrete. Examples include ℚ_p(p1/p^∞)^∧ and 𝔽_p((t))(t1/p^∞)^∧. The tilt of a perfectoid field K is the characteristic-p field K^♭ = limx ↦ x^p K, with a canonical isomorphism Gal(K^♭) ≅ Gal(K). Scholze extended this to perfectoid spaces: an affinoid perfectoid space is Spa(R, R+) where R is a perfectoid algebra. The tilting equivalence gives a homeomorphism between adic spectra |Spa(R, R+)| and |Spa(R^♭, R^♭+)|, preserving étale cohomology. This transfers problems from characteristic zero to characteristic p, where the Frobenius morphism and Artin–Schreier sequence are available. The p-adic Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚ_p), completed by Colmez (2010) and independently by Kisin (2009), establishes a bijection between irreducible admissible unitary Banach space representations of GL2(ℚ_p) over ℚ_p and 2-dimensional p-adic Galois representations of Gal(ℚ̄_p/ℚ_p). The correspondence is realized via the functor D(Π) = HomGL2(ℚ_p)(Π, B^†rig,ℚ_p2) and its inverse V(D) = (D ⊗ B^†rig,ℚ_p2)Gal. Here B^†rig,ℚ_p is the Robba ring, and (φ, Γ)-modules over it provide the bridge. Scholze's perfectoid spaces were used to construct the moduli space of p-divisible groups (the Lubin–Tate tower) as a perfectoid space, and to prove the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over p-adic fields (Harris–Taylor and Henniart gave earlier proofs by different methods). Perfectoid Shimura varieties, constructed by Scholze and Caraiani, realize the local Langlands correspondence in the cohomology of the tower of Shimura varieties at infinite level. In examinations, candidates may be asked to define a perfectoid field, state the tilting equivalence, or explain how the p-adic Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚ_p) differs from the classical local Langlands correspondence (which uses smooth representations over ℂ). A common trap is asserting that perfectoid spaces are rigid-analytic varieties; they are adic spaces in the sense of Huber, which generalize rigid geometry. Another error is confusing the p-adic Langlands correspondence (for p-adic Galois representations and Banach representations of GL2(ℚ_p)) with the mod-p Langlands correspondence (for mod-p representations), established by Breuil and others as a separate but related theory.

The Bloch–Kato Conjectures and the Tamagawa Number Conjecture

The Bloch–Kato conjectures, formulated by Spencer Bloch and Kazuya Kato in 1990, give a precise description of the special values of motivic L-functions in terms of algebraic K-theory and Galois cohomology. For a smooth projective variety X over ℚ, the motivic L-function L(H^i(X), s) is expected to have a functional equation and special values at integers. The Tamagawa number conjecture (TNC), a refinement of the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, states that the leading coefficient of L(H^i(X), s) at s = m is given by a Tamagawa number τ(H^i(X)(m)) = ∏_v τ_v, where τ_v are local Tamagawa measures on the Galois cohomology groups H1_f(ℚ_v, V) for a p-adic realization V = H^iét(Xℚ̄, ℚ_p(m)). The local condition H1_f is the Bloch–Kato Selmer group: for v = p, it is the subspace of crystalline classes; for v ≠ p, it is the unramified classes. The TNC predicts that the order of the Selmer group H1_f(ℚ, V) is finite and related to the L-value by L^*(H^i(X), m) = τ(H^i(X)(m)) · |H1_f(ℚ, V)| / |H0(ℚ, V)| · (regulator terms). For an elliptic curve E/ℚ, V = T_p(E) ⊗ ℚ_p(1), and the TNC at s = 1 recovers the BSD conjecture: L^*(E, 1) = Ω_E · Reg_E · ∏_p c_p · |Ш(E/ℚ)| / |E(ℚ)tors|2, where Ω_E is the real period, Reg_E the regulator, c_p the Tamagawa numbers, and Ш the Tate–Shafarevich group. The equivariant Tamagawa number conjecture (ETNC), formulated by Burns and Flach, incorporates the action of the Galois group of a Galois extension and predicts the leading term of the equivariant L-function in the relative K-group K0(ℤ[G], ℝ). The ETNC implies the Coates–Sinnott conjecture on values of Dirichlet L-functions and the Brumer–Stark conjecture. Kato's Euler system for modular forms (2004) proves one divisibility in the Iwasawa main conjecture for modular forms, equivalent to one inequality in the TNC for the symmetric square motive. The full TNC remains open in general. In examinations, candidates may be asked to state the TNC for an elliptic curve, define the Bloch–Kato Selmer group H1_f, or explain the difference between the TNC and the ETNC. A frequent error is confusing the Tamagawa number (a product of local measures) with the Tamagawa number of an algebraic group (the volume of the adelic quotient). Another trap is claiming the TNC is a theorem for all motives; it is known only in special cases (e.g., Tate motives via the functional equation of Dirichlet L-functions, CM elliptic curves via Rubin's work on the main conjecture, and modular forms via Kato and Skinner–Urban).

  • Stating that the Selberg trace formula applies to any discrete subgroup of SL2(ℝ); it requires the subgroup to be cofinite (finite volume quotient) and often torsion-free for the simplest form.
  • Claiming that the fundamental lemma was proved by Langlands; it was conjectured by Langlands and Shelstad and proved in full generality by Ngô Bảo Châu in 2008.
  • Asserting that perfectoid spaces are a type of rigid-analytic variety; they are adic spaces, a generalization of rigid geometry introduced by Huber.
  • Believing that the p-adic Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚ_p) is a direct analogue of the classical local Langlands correspondence; the classical correspondence uses smooth complex representations, while the p-adic version uses unitary Banach space representations over ℚ_p.
  • Equating the Tamagawa number conjecture with the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture; the TNC is a vast generalization to all motives, while BSD is the special case for the motive H1(E)(1) of an elliptic curve.
  • Assuming that the Bloch–Kato Selmer group H1_f is defined by the same local condition at all primes; at p it is the crystalline condition, while at v ≠ p it is the unramified condition.

The Geometric Langlands Program and Its Arithmetic Applications

The geometric Langlands program reformulates the classical Langlands correspondence in the language of algebraic geometry over a smooth projective curve X over a finite field or ℂ. Instead of automorphic representations of a reductive group G over a global field, one studies D-modules (or ℓ-adic sheaves) on the moduli stack BunG of G-bundles on X. The central conjecture predicts a bijection between irreducible D-modules on BunG with certain Hecke eigenproperties and L-parameters, i.e. homomorphisms from the étale fundamental group of X into the Langlands dual group G∨. The classical correspondence for GLn over function fields was proved by Laurent Lafforgue (2002), and for general G by Vincent Lafforgue; the geometric conjecture for GLn over ℂ was recently established by Gaitsgory–Lurie. The geometric setting replaces the analytic theory of automorphic forms by the categorical theory of sheaves, allowing the use of powerful tools such as the geometric Satake equivalence, which identifies the category of representations of G∨ with the category of perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian. This equivalence, due to Mirković–Vilonen and Ginzburg, underlies the construction of Hecke operators on BunG and the formulation of the Hecke eigensheaf condition.

Arithmetic applications flow from the function‑field analogue of the global Langlands correspondence. Lafforgue’s proof yields the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for GLn over function fields and gives a geometric interpretation of the local correspondence via nearby cycles on the affine Grassmannian. In the number‑field setting, the geometric program inspires p-adic Langlands correspondences and the study of p-adic local systems on Shimura varieties. The work of Frenkel–Gaitsgory for GL2 over ℂ introduced opers and the critical level affine Kac–Moody algebra, linking the program to conformal field theory. Gaitsgory–Lurie’s proof for GLn over ℂ uses derived algebraic geometry and ind‑coherent sheaves to establish a fully functorial equivalence between the derived category of D-modules on BunG and the derived category of quasi‑coherent sheaves on the stack of L-parameters. This categorical equivalence is expected to specialize, via the spectral side, to the classical automorphic‑to‑Galois correspondence after taking appropriate cohomology.

For competitive examinations, the key points to retain are: (1) the geometric Langlands program replaces automorphic representations by Hecke eigensheaves on BunG; (2) the geometric Satake equivalence provides the representation‑theoretic input; (3) Lafforgue’s theorem proves the classical global correspondence for GLn over function fields, giving a complete proof of the Ramanujan conjecture in that setting; (4) the categorical formulation by Gaitsgory–Lurie is the current state of the art for the geometric conjecture over ℂ. A typical examiner trap is to confuse the geometric Langlands correspondence (a categorical equivalence of sheaf categories) with the classical Langlands correspondence (a bijection between isomorphism classes of representations). Another trap is to claim that the geometric program directly proves the classical Langlands correspondence over number fields; it provides a guiding framework and function‑field proofs, but the number‑field case remains open beyond GL1 and GL2.

The Theory of Shimura Varieties and the Modularity of Abelian Varieties

Shimura varieties are higher‑dimensional analogues of modular curves that parametrize abelian varieties equipped with additional structures such as polarizations, endomorphisms, and level structures. Formally, a Shimura datum consists of a reductive algebraic group G over ℚ and a G(ℝ)-conjugacy class X of homomorphisms h: ℂ× → Gℝ satisfying certain axioms (the Hodge structure on the Lie algebra of G is of type (−1,1), (0,0), (1,−1) and the adjoint action of h(i) is a Cartan involution). For each compact open subgroup K ⊂ G(𝔸f), the complex Shimura variety ShK(G,X) = G(ℚ) X × G(𝔸f) / K is a quasi‑projective algebraic variety defined over a number field called the reflex field E(G,X). The canonical model over E is characterized by Shimura’s reciprocity law, which describes the action of the absolute Galois group of E on special points in terms of the Artin map of class field theory.

The most classical example is the modular curve Y0(N) attached to G = GL2 and the Poincaré upper half‑plane. For G = GSp2g one obtains the Siegel modular variety parametrizing principally polarized abelian varieties of dimension g with level structure. Shimura varieties provide a geometric realization of the Langlands correspondence for the group G: the ℓ-adic cohomology groups Hiét(ShK, ℚℓ) carry an action of G(𝔸f) × Gal(ℚ̄/ℚ) and are expected to decompose according to the Langlands parameters of automorphic representations of G. This is the content of the “Langlands–Kottwitz” description of the cohomology, proved in many cases by Kottwitz, Harris–Taylor, and others.

A central arithmetic application is the modularity of abelian varieties of GL2-type. An abelian variety A over ℚ is of GL2-type if its endomorphism algebra contains a number field of degree equal to dim A. The modularity theorem (formerly the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture) asserts that every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular, i.e. corresponds to a weight‑2 newform. This was extended by Ribet, Serre, and others to abelian varieties of GL2-type that are quotients of the Jacobian of a Shimura curve attached to an indefinite quaternion algebra. The proof uses the Eichler–Shimura construction on the modular curve (or Shimura curve) to attach a Galois representation to a newform, and then the Faltings–Serre method to match it with the Galois representation on the Tate module of A. The work of Khare–Wintenberger on Serre’s modularity conjecture completed the proof for all elliptic curves, and the methods have been generalized to many abelian varieties of GL2-type by Dieulefait, Freitas–Siksek, and others.

For examinations, remember: (1) Shimura varieties are moduli spaces of abelian varieties with extra structure, defined by a Shimura datum (G,X); (2) they have canonical models over reflex fields characterized by Shimura reciprocity; (3) their ℓ-adic cohomology realizes the Langlands correspondence for G; (4) modularity of abelian varieties of GL2-type follows from realizing them in the Jacobians of Shimura curves and applying the Eichler–Shimura construction. A common trap is to state that every abelian variety is a Shimura variety; only those with a suitable group‑theoretic datum arise this way. Another trap is to confuse the reflex field with the field of definition of the abelian variety; the reflex field is determined by the Shimura datum and can be strictly smaller.

Worked Example: Identifying the Reflex Field
Let G = GL2 over ℚ and X the G(ℝ)-conjugacy class of the homomorphism h: ℂ× → GL2(ℝ) given by h(z) = diag(z, z). Determine the reflex field E(G,X).
The reflex field is the field of definition of the conjugacy class of the cocharacter μ: ℂ× → Gℂ obtained from h by projecting onto the (−1,1) part. For GL2, h(z) = diag(z, z) yields μ(z) = diag(z, 1). The conjugacy class of μ is defined over ℚ because the diagonal torus and the cocharacter are defined over ℚ. Hence the reflex field E(G,X) = ℚ. This matches the classical fact that modular curves have canonical models over ℚ.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the reflex field of a Shimura datum, first write down the cocharacter μ attached to h. The reflex field is the field of definition of the G-conjugacy class of μ. For classical groups (GLn, GSp2g, unitary groups) this field is often ℚ or a CM field determined by the Hodge structure. Memorize the reflex fields for the standard Shimura data: modular curves (ℚ), Siegel modular varieties (ℚ), Shimura curves attached to quaternion algebras (the totally real field of the quaternion algebra), and unitary Shimura varieties (the CM field).

The p-adic Local Langlands Correspondence for GL(2) and the Breuil–Mézard Conjecture

The p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚp) is a bijection between two‑dimensional continuous representations of the absolute Galois group Gℚp on finite‑dimensional ℚp-vector spaces and unitary admissible Banach space representations of GL2(ℚp) on ℚp-vector spaces. It was formulated by Fontaine–Mazur and constructed by Colmez (2010) using the theory of (φ,Γ)-modules and the p-adic Langlands functor V ↦ Π(V) = HomGℚp(V, BdR ⊗ ℚp Brig†)∨. The correspondence preserves local L- and ε-factors and matches the classical local Langlands correspondence after extending scalars to ℂ. A key feature is the distinction between crystalline, semi‑stable, and non‑crystalline Galois representations: crystalline representations correspond to locally analytic principal series or Steinberg representations, while non‑crystalline ones correspond to more complicated Banach representations that are not locally analytic.

The Breuil–Mézard conjecture (2002) predicts a precise relationship between the Hilbert–Samuel multiplicities of the deformation rings of mod‑p Galois representations and the representation theory of GL2(ℚp) modulo p. Let ρ̄: Gℚp → GL2(𝔽p) be a continuous representation. For each Serre weight σ (an irreducible 𝔽p-representation of GL2(ℤp)), the conjecture gives a formula for the multiplicity e(Rρ̄,v,τ) of the potentially Barsotti–Tate deformation ring with Hodge–Tate weights v and inertial type τ in terms of the multiplicities of σ in the reduction modulo p of the corresponding p-adic Banach representation. The conjecture was proved in many cases by Kisin (2009) using the theory of Breuil–Kisin modules, and in full generality for GL2 by Emerton–Gee–Savitt (2015) via the patching method and the p-adic local Langlands correspondence. The proof establishes a numerical local‑global compatibility that underlies the modularity lifting theorems of Taylor–Wiles type.

For competitive examinations, the essential points are: (1) the p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚp) matches Galois representations to unitary Banach representations, not smooth complex representations; (2) Colmez’s construction uses (φ,Γ)-modules and the period rings BdR, Brig†; (3) the Breuil–Mézard conjecture relates deformation ring multiplicities to mod‑p representation theory of GL2(ℚp); (4) its proof by Kisin and Emerton–Gee–Savitt is a cornerstone of modern modularity lifting. A frequent trap is to confuse the p-adic local Langlands correspondence (Banach representations over ℚp) with the mod‑p local Langlands correspondence (smooth representations over 𝔽p), which was established by Breuil and others and is a separate but related theory. Another trap is to claim that the correspondence is known for all reductive groups; it is only fully established for GL2(ℚp) and partially for GLn via the work of Emerton, Helm, and others.

The Theory of Euler Systems and Kolyvagin's Descent

An Euler system is a collection of cohomology classes cF ∈ H1(F, T) indexed by number fields F in a suitable infinite family, satisfying norm‑compatibility relations that mimic the Euler product of an L-function. Here T is a p-adic Galois representation (e.g. the Tate module of an elliptic curve or a modular form). The prototype is the system of cyclotomic units in H1(ℚ(μn), ℤp(1)) constructed from the elements (1 − ζn) in the cyclotomic fields. Victor Kolyvagin (1990) introduced a general method — Kolyvagin’s descent — that uses an Euler system to bound the Selmer group of T. The idea is to take derivative classes κn obtained by applying certain “derivative” operators (coming from the Galois group of a suitable abelian extension) to the Euler system classes, and then to use the non‑vanishing of a single class to control the size of the Selmer group via a descent argument on the associated Kolyvagin system.

Kolyvagin applied his method to the Euler system of Heegner points on modular curves (constructed by Gross–Zagier) to prove that if an elliptic curve E/ℚ has analytic rank 0 or 1, then its Mordell–Weil rank equals the analytic rank and the Tate–Shafarevich group Ш(E/ℚ) is finite. This gave the first proof of the Birch and Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture in the rank‑0 and rank‑1 cases. Later, Karl Rubin used the Euler system of cyclotomic units to prove the main conjecture of Iwasawa theory for imaginary quadratic fields, and Perrin‑Riou developed a general theory of p-adic L-functions and Euler systems for arbitrary motives. The machinery of Kolyvagin systems, formalized by Mazur–Rubin, provides a framework that works for any Galois representation with an Euler system, yielding precise bounds on Selmer groups and, in many cases, the exact formula predicted by the Bloch–Kato conjecture.

For examinations, remember: (1) an Euler system is a compatible family of global cohomology classes; (2) Kolyvagin’s descent uses derivative classes to bound Selmer groups; (3) the Heegner point Euler system proves BSD for analytic rank ≤ 1; (4) the cyclotomic unit Euler system proves the Iwasawa main conjecture for CM fields; (5) the Mazur–Rubin theory of Kolyvagin systems axiomatizes the method. A common trap is to state that Euler systems exist for all motives; they are known only in special cases (cyclotomic units, Heegner points, elliptic units, Beilinson–Kato elements for modular forms). Another trap is to confuse the Euler system argument (which gives upper bounds) with the full Bloch–Kato conjecture (which predicts exact equality). The Euler system provides one inequality; the opposite inequality typically requires a separate argument such as the Iwasawa main conjecture or a non‑vanishing result for L-functions.

The Main Conjecture of Iwasawa Theory for Modular Forms and Its Consequences

The main conjecture of Iwasawa theory for a modular form f of weight k ≥ 2 and level N prime to p relates the characteristic ideal of the Pontryagin dual of the Selmer group of the p-adic Galois representation Vf over the cyclotomic ℤp-extension ℚ∞ to the p-adic L-function Lp(f, s) constructed by Mazur–Kitagawa (for k=2) and by Amice–Velu, Vishik, and Panchishkin (for higher weight). Let Tf be a Gℚ-stable lattice in Vf. The Selmer group Sel(ℚ∞, Tf) is defined by the usual local conditions: unramified outside p and the “ordinary” condition at p (the image in H1(ℚp,∞, Vf/Tf) lies in the subspace coming from the ordinary filtration). Its Pontryagin dual X is a finitely generated torsion module over the Iwasawa algebra Λ = ℤp[[Γ]] with Γ ≅ ℤp. The main conjecture asserts that the characteristic ideal charΛ(X) equals the principal ideal generated by the p-adic L-function Lp(f) (up to a unit). Kato (2004) proved one divisibility (the “upper bound”) for all modular forms using his Euler system of Beilinson–Kato elements; Skinner–Urban (2014) proved the opposite divisibility for ordinary forms using Eisenstein congruences and p-adic families of automorphic forms.

The consequences are profound. The main conjecture implies the p-adic Birch and Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture for f (the order of vanishing of Lp(f, s) at s=k/2 equals the corank of the Selmer group). It also yields the “control theorem” that relates the Selmer group over ℚ∞ to the classical Selmer group over ℚ, allowing one to deduce finiteness of the Tate–Shafarevich group from the non‑vanishing of the p-adic L-function. Moreover, the main conjecture is a key input in the proof of the “modularity lifting” theorems: the Taylor–Wiles method uses the fact that the deformation ring of a mod‑p Galois representation is a complete intersection, which follows from the numerical criterion of Wiles that is equivalent to the main conjecture for the adjoint representation. The work of Kisin on potentially Barsotti–Tate deformation rings and the Breuil–Mézard conjecture provides the local input needed to verify the numerical criterion.

For examinations, the key points are: (1) the Iwasawa main conjecture for a modular form equates the characteristic ideal of the dual Selmer group over the cyclotomic tower to the p-adic L-function; (2) Kato’s Euler system of Beilinson–Kato elements proves one divisibility (the “upper bound”) for all modular forms; (3) Skinner–Urban proved the opposite divisibility for ordinary forms using Eisenstein congruences; (4) the conjecture implies the p-adic BSD formula and finiteness of Ш under non‑vanishing hypotheses; (5) it underlies the Taylor–Wiles modularity lifting method. A typical trap is to claim that the main conjecture is proved for all motives; it is known only for Tate motives (via the classical Iwasawa main conjecture for Dirichlet characters), for modular forms (Kato, Skinner–Urban), and for certain CM elliptic curves (Rubin). Another trap is to confuse the cyclotomic Iwasawa theory (extension ℚ∞) with the anticyclotomic or p-adic Lie extensions; the main conjecture takes different forms in those settings.

  • Assuming every abelian variety arises from a Shimura variety; only those with a suitable group‑theoretic datum do.

The Theory of Complex Multiplication and the Kronecker Limit Formula

Complex multiplication (CM) provides the only general method for explicitly constructing abelian extensions of number fields, realizing Kronecker's Jugendtraum for imaginary quadratic fields. Let K = ℚ(√−d) be an imaginary quadratic field with ring of integers OK. An elliptic curve E/ℂ has CM by OK if End(E) ≅ OK. The j-invariant j(E) is then an algebraic integer generating the Hilbert class field H of K. The Galois group Gal(H/K) acts on the set of CM elliptic curves via the class group Cl(K) through the Artin reciprocity map: for an ideal a ⊂ OK, the action sends j(OK) to j(a−1). This yields explicit class fields without solving polynomial equations.

The Kronecker limit formula computes the first Laurent coefficient of the Eisenstein series E(z, s) at s = 1. For z ∈ ℍ, E(z, s) = ∑'(m,n)∈ℤ2 (Im z)s |mz + n|−2s. It has a simple pole at s = 1 with residue π−1, and the constant term is −2π(log(√Im z |η(z)|2) + γ/2), where η is the Dedekind eta function and γ is Euler's constant. Equivalently, for an ideal a in OK, the partial zeta function ζ(a, s) = ∑0≠α∈a N(α)−s satisfies ζ(a, s) = (2πs / (√|dK| Γ(s))) (1/(s−1) + log(√|dK| N(a) / (2π) |η(za)|4) + O(s−1)). This formula expresses special values of L-functions in terms of modular forms and underlies the Chowla–Selberg formula for periods of CM elliptic curves.

The theory extends to higher dimensions via Shimura–Taniyama: abelian varieties with CM by a CM field F (a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real field F+) have their moduli described by Shimura varieties of PEL type. The reflex field E of the CM type (Φ, F) governs the field of definition of the associated abelian variety. The main theorem of CM states that for a CM abelian variety A defined over H, the action of Gal(Hab/H) on torsion points is described by the reciprocity map of class field theory composed with the CM type. This provides explicit generators for ray class fields. For examinations, the key points are: (1) CM elliptic curves correspond to ideal classes in imaginary quadratic fields; (2) the j-invariant generates the Hilbert class field; (3) the Kronecker limit formula relates Eisenstein series at s=1 to the Dedekind eta function; (4) the Chowla–Selberg formula expresses periods of CM elliptic curves as products of gamma values; (5) the theory generalizes to CM abelian varieties via Shimura varieties and reflex fields.

The Stark Conjectures and Special Values of Artin L-Functions

The Stark conjectures, formulated by Harold Stark in the 1970s, predict explicit formulas for the leading terms of Artin L-functions at s = 0 in terms of units in abelian extensions. Let L/K be a finite abelian extension of number fields with Galois group G. For a character χ of G, the Artin L-function L(s, χ) is entire except for a simple pole at s = 1 when χ is trivial. The functional equation relates L(s, χ) to L(1−s, χ̄). The order of vanishing at s = 0 is rχ = dimℂ HomG(χ, OL× ⊗ ℂ). Stark's conjecture (rank one case) asserts that when rχ = 1, there exists a unit εχ ∈ (OL× ⊗ ℂ)χ such that L'(0, χ) = −(1/2) ∑σ∈G χ(σ) log |εχσ|, where the absolute value is taken at a fixed archimedean place. This unit generates the abelian extension in the sense that L = K(εχ1/w) for a suitable root of unity w.

The conjectures have several variants. The "abelian rank one" conjecture (Stark 1975) is proved when K is totally real and L is totally complex (Tate 1984, using the functional equation and the class number formula). The "general" Stark conjecture predicts the leading term for arbitrary order of vanishing in terms of a determinant of logarithms of units (a regulator). The "strong" Stark conjecture specifies the unit up to roots of unity. The "equivariant" Stark conjecture (Burns–Flach, 2001) formulates the statement in the language of equivariant L-functions and the equivariant Tamagawa number conjecture (ETNC). For K = ℚ, the conjecture reduces to the analytic class number formula. For K imaginary quadratic, it relates to the Kronecker limit formula and the construction of elliptic units via Siegel functions. The Brumer–Stark conjecture (1978) extends this to non-abelian extensions via the equivariant formulation. For examinations, the key points are: (1) Stark conjectures predict special values of Artin L-functions at s=0 in terms of units; (2) the rank one case is proved for totally real base fields; (3) the conjectures generalize the analytic class number formula and the Kronecker limit formula; (4) they are a key ingredient in the equivariant Tamagawa number conjecture; (5) elliptic units provide explicit Stark units for imaginary quadratic fields.

Worked Example: Stark Unit for a Real Quadratic Field
Let K = ℚ(√5) and L = ℚ(ζ5) = ℚ(√5, ζ5), an abelian extension with Galois group G ≅ (ℤ/5ℤ)× ≅ ℤ/4ℤ. The non-trivial character χ of order 2 corresponds to the quadratic subfield ℚ(√5). Compute the leading term of L(s, χ) at s = 0 and identify the Stark unit.
The Artin L-function L(s, χ) is the Dirichlet L-function L(s, (5/·)). The functional equation gives L(0, χ) = 0 (since χ is odd). The derivative is L'(0, χ) = −(1/2) log ε, where ε is the fundamental unit of ℚ(√5). The fundamental unit is ε = (1+√5)/2. The Stark conjecture predicts L'(0, χ) = −(1/2) log |ε|. Indeed, L'(0, (5/·)) = −(1/2) log((1+√5)/2). The unit ε generates the Hilbert class field (which is trivial here since class number is 1) and the Stark unit is ε itself. This matches the analytic class number formula for the quadratic field.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Stark Conjecture Variants
Questions often test whether you can distinguish between the rank-one, general, strong, and equivariant forms. Memorize the precise hypothesis for each: rank one requires rχ=1; general allows arbitrary order; strong specifies the unit up to roots of unity; equivariant uses the language of the ETNC. The rank-one case for totally real K is a theorem (Tate); the others remain open. Do not confuse Stark conjectures (special values at s=0) with the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture (special values at s=1 for elliptic curves) or the Bloch–Kato conjectures (special values at critical points for motives).

The Theory of Mock Modular Forms, Harmonic Maass Forms, and Quantum Modular Forms

Classical modular forms are holomorphic functions on ℍ satisfying f(γz) = (cz+d)k f(z) for γ = ( a b; c d ) ∈ SL2(ℤ). Mock modular forms, introduced by Ramanujan in his last letter to Hardy and formalized by Zwegers (2002), are holomorphic parts of harmonic Maass forms. A harmonic Maass form of weight k is a smooth function F: ℍ → ℂ satisfying the modular transformation law, annihilated by the weight k hyperbolic Laplacian Δk = −y2(∂2/∂x2 + ∂2/∂y2) + iky(∂/∂x + i ∂/∂y), and having at most linear exponential growth at cusps. It decomposes as F = F+ + F−, where F+ is the holomorphic part (the mock modular form) and F− is the non-holomorphic part, expressible in terms of incomplete gamma functions. The shadow of F+ is the modular form g = ξk(F) = 2iyk ∂z̄ F, where ξk is the Maass lowering operator. Ramanujan's mock theta functions (e.g., f(q) = ∑n≥0 qn2 / (1+q)2(1+q2)2⋯(1+qn)2) are weight 1/2 mock modular forms whose shadows are weight 3/2 unary theta series.

Quantum modular forms, introduced by Zagier (2010), are functions f: ℚ → ℂ (or a subset) such that for each γ ∈ SL2(ℤ), the "error to modularity" hγ(x) = f(x) − (cx+d)−k f((ax+b)/(cx+d)) extends to a smooth (or real-analytic) function on ℝ {−d/c}. They arise from limits of modular forms at rational points, from Kontsevich's "strange" function F(q) = ∑n≥0 (q; q)n (where (a; q)n = ∏k=0n−1 (1−aqk)), and from the radial limits of mock modular forms. The theory connects to quantum invariants of 3-manifolds (Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev invariants), the Kashaev invariant, and the volume conjecture. For examinations, the key points are: (1) mock modular forms are holomorphic parts of harmonic Maass forms; (2) the shadow is a classical modular form obtained via the ξ-operator; (3) Ramanujan's mock theta functions are the first examples; (4) quantum modular forms are defined on ℚ with smooth error to modularity; (5) they arise from radial limits of mock modular forms and from quantum topology; (6) the theory provides a framework for Ramanujan's enigmatic functions and their applications to partitions, black holes, and 3-manifold invariants.

The Theory of Automorphic Forms on GL(n) and the Langlands–Shahidi Method

The Langlands program for GL(n) seeks to associate to each irreducible cuspidal automorphic representation π of GL(n)(𝔸) an n-dimensional Galois representation (or a parameter into the L-group LGL(n) = GL(n, ℂ)). The local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) (Harris–Taylor, Henniart, 2000) establishes a bijection between irreducible admissible representations of GL(n)(F) for a p-adic field F and n-dimensional Frobenius-semisimple Weil–Deligne representations of the Weil group WF. The global correspondence is known for n = 1 (class field theory), n = 2 (modularity theorem, Taylor–Wiles), and for certain cases of n > 2 via the converse theorem (Cogdell–Piatetski-Shapiro) and the endoscopic classification (Arthur, 2013).

The Langlands–Shahidi method provides a general framework for defining and studying L-functions attached to automorphic representations of reductive groups. For a maximal parabolic subgroup P = MN of a quasi-split group G, and a generic cuspidal automorphic representation π of M(𝔸), one considers the induced representation IndP(𝔸)G(𝔸) (π ⊗ 1). The normalized intertwining operators M(w, π, s) satisfy a functional equation involving L-functions L(s, π, ri) for representations ri of the L-group LM on the Lie algebra of LN. The method proves analytic continuation, functional equations, and non-vanishing on Re(s) = 1 for these L-functions. It is the primary tool for functorial lifts: the symmetric square lift GL(2) → GL(3) (Gelbart–Jacquet), the exterior square lift GL(4) → GL(6), and the Asai lift for unitary groups. The local coefficients C(s, π, r) are products of local L-factors and ε-factors, and their properties imply the local Langlands correspondence for generic representations. For examinations, the key points are: (1) the local Langlands correspondence for GL(n) is a theorem (Harris–Taylor, Henniart); (2) the global correspondence is open for n > 2 except for special cases; (3) the Langlands–Shahidi method defines L-functions via intertwining operators and proves their analytic properties; (4) it establishes functorial lifts (symmetric square, exterior square, Asai); (5) the method uses the theory of Eisenstein series and their constant terms; (6) local coefficients factor as products of local L-factors and ε-factors, linking local and global theory.

The Theory of Multiple Zeta Values, Periods, and the Motivic Galois Group

Multiple zeta values (MZVs) are real numbers defined by the nested sums ζ(k1, …, kr) = ∑n1 > ⋯ > nr > 0 n1−k1 ⋯ nr−kr for integers k1 ≥ 2, ki ≥ 1. They generalize the Riemann zeta values ζ(n) and appear as periods of mixed Tate motives over ℤ. The ℚ-vector space of MZVs of weight w = ∑ ki is conjectured to have dimension given by the Fibonacci-like sequence dw = dw−2 + dw−3 with d0=1, d1=0, d2=1 (Zagier's dimension conjecture). The algebraic relations among MZVs are generated by the double shuffle relations (stuffle and shuffle products) and the regularized double shuffle relations. The motivic Galois group of the category of mixed Tate motives over ℤ is a pro-unipotent group whose Lie algebra is the free Lie algebra on generators σ3, σ5, σ7, … in odd degrees ≥ 3 (Deligne–Goncharov, Terasoma). The motivic MZVs are formal symbols satisfying the same relations as MZVs but with no accidental numerical coincidences; the period map sends motivic MZVs to actual MZVs.

The theory extends to multiple polylogarithms Lik1,…,kr(z1, …, zr) = ∑n1 > ⋯ > nr > 0 z1n1 ⋯ zrnr / (n1k1 ⋯ nrkr), which satisfy differential equations with respect to the zi and have monodromy given by the motivic Galois group. The Bloch group B(F) of a field F is related to K3(F) and the dilogarithm; its higher analogues relate to K2n−1(F) and multiple polylogarithms. The motivic fundamental group of ℙ1 {0, 1, ∞} (the "pro-unipotent completion" of the fundamental group) has a Lie algebra generated by e0, e1 with relations from the Drinfeld associator. The coefficients of the Drinfeld associator are MZVs. For examinations, the key points are: (1) MZVs are periods of mixed Tate motives over ℤ; (2) Zagier's dimension conjecture predicts the ℚ-dimension of weight w MZVs; (3) the double shuffle relations generate all algebraic relations (conjecturally); (4) the motivic Galois group is pro-unipotent with free Lie algebra on odd generators ≥ 3; (5) motivic MZVs are formal versions without accidental relations; (6) the theory connects to the Bloch group, K-theory, the dilogarithm, and the Drinfeld associator; (7) multiple polylogarithms provide the functional equations and monodromy underlying the motivic structure.
