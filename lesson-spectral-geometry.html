Part 1

🎻 The Laplace–Beltrami Operator and Its Spectrum

Spectral Theory
The Laplacian on Riemannian Manifolds, Eigenvalues, and Eigenfunctions
Let (M^n, g) be a compact Riemannian manifold without boundary. The Laplace–Beltrami operator Δ: C^∞(M) → C^∞(M) is defined by Δf = div(grad f) = g^{ij} ∇_i ∇_j f. In local coordinates, Δf = (1/√det g) ∂_i (√det g g^{ij} ∂_j f). The eigenvalue problem: Δu + λ u = 0. The spectrum Spec(M, g) is the set of eigenvalues 0 = λ_0 < λ_1 ≤ λ_2 ≤ … → ∞, each with finite multiplicity. The min-max principle (Courant–Fischer): λ_k = min_{V⊂C^∞, dim V=k} max_{f∈V\{0}} (∫|∇f|²) / (∫ f²). Eigenfunctions form an orthonormal basis of L^2(M). The nodal sets of eigenfunctions (zero sets) partition M into nodal domains — Courant's nodal domain theorem: the k-th eigenfunction has at most k nodal domains. The spectral zeta function ζ_Δ(s) = Σ λ_j^{-s} (analytic continuation via the heat kernel) and the functional determinant det(Δ) = exp(-ζ'_Δ(0)) play key roles in geometric analysis. The spectral geometry of orbifolds and graph Laplacians extends these ideas to singular spaces and discrete settings.
The spectrum of the Laplacian encodes deep geometric information: dimension, volume, and curvature integrals via asymptotic expansions.
Part 2

📐 The Weyl Law and Eigenvalue Asymptotics

Asymptotics
Weyl's Law, the Leading Term, and the Two-Term Weyl Conjecture
Let N(λ) = #{j: λ_j ≤ λ} be the eigenvalue counting function. Weyl's law (1911): as λ → ∞, N(λ) ~ ω_n Vol(M) λ^{n/2} / (2π)^n, where ω_n is the volume of the unit n-ball. The Weyl constant C_n = ω_n/(2π)^n is universal — it depends only on dimension, not on geometry. The two-term Weyl conjecture (proved by Duistermaat–Guillemin and Ivrii for manifolds without periodic geodesics): N(λ) = ω_n Vol(M) λ^{n/2}/(2π)^n + c_n Vol(∂M) λ^{(n-1)/2} + o(λ^{(n-1)/2}), where the second term involves the boundary area. The heat kernel asymptotics tr(e^{tΔ}) = Σ e^{-λ_j t} ~ (4πt)^{-n/2} Σ_{k=0}^∞ a_k t^k as t → 0^+. The heat invariants a_k are integrals of local curvature expressions: a_0 = Vol(M), a_1 = (1/6) ∫ R dV (the scalar curvature integral), a_2 involves Ricci and scalar curvature squared terms. The wave trace Tr(cos(t√Δ)) is a distribution whose singularities at t = L (lengths of closed geodesics) reveal the length spectrum of the manifold — the Duistermaat–Guillemin trace formula. The Poisson relation relates the wave trace singularities to the closed geodesics.
Weyl's law gives the leading asymptotic of eigenvalue growth; the heat kernel expansion provides spectral invariants that encode curvature integrals.
Part 3

🧩 Isospectral Manifolds: Can You Hear the Shape of a Drum?

Inverse Spectral
Sunada's Construction, Milnor's Example, and the Isospectral Problem
The isospectral problem asks whether the spectrum of the Laplacian determines the Riemannian manifold up to isometry. Milnor's 1964 example: two 16-dimensional flat tori (Z^16 quotients) with different shapes but identical spectra, disproving the naïve conjecture. Sunada's theorem (1985): if two finite covers of a common base manifold are determined by almost-conjugate subgroups of the fundamental group, then the covers are isospectral. Sunada's method produces continuous families of isospectral manifolds (e.g., isospectral Riemann surfaces with different genuses). Gordon–Webb–Wolpert (1992): two planar regions with different shapes but identical Dirichlet spectra — a concrete negative answer to Kac's question "Can one hear the shape of a drum?" The construction uses transplantation of eigenfunctions between the regions. Inverse spectral results: certain geometric properties ARE determined by the spectrum — dimension, volume, the total scalar integral (by the heat expansion), and for analytic surfaces with simple length spectrum, the metric is determined (Kac's question for analytic convex domains remains open). The spectral rigidity of simple length spectra for surfaces of negative curvature (Guillemin–Kazhdan) shows isospectral deformations of negatively curved metrics are trivial.
The isospectral problem shows that the spectrum does not uniquely determine the geometry, but it does encode many invariants — the interplay between spectral and geometric data is subtle and rich.
Part 4

🔥 Spectral Triples and Noncommutative Geometry

Noncommutative
Spectral Triples, the Connes–Lott Model, and Reconstruction
Noncommutative geometry (Connes) replaces the commutative algebra of functions C^∞(M) by a possibly noncommutative algebra A acting on a Hilbert space H with a Dirac operator D satisfying compact resolvent. The spectral triple (A, H, D) encodes geometry spectrally: the metric is recovered via Connes' formula d(p, q) = sup_{a∈A} {|a(p) − a(q)|: ||[D, a]|| ≤ 1}. The Connes–Lott model builds the Standard Model of particle physics on a spectral triple of the product geometry M × F (a finite noncommutative space F). The spectral action principle: the bosonic action is Tr(f(D/Λ)) where f is a cutoff function and Λ is a scale — this reproduces the Einstein–Hilbert action plus Yang–Mills terms from the asymptotic expansion of the heat kernel of D². The reconstruction theorem (Connes, 2008): a commutative spectral triple satisfying seven axioms (reality, orientability, Poincaré duality, etc.) reconstructs a smooth compact Riemannian spin manifold. The local index formula (Connes–Moscovici) expresses the index of a spectral triple in terms of residues of zeta functions. The even-dimensional spectral triple of (M, g) recovers the Hirzebruch signature theorem and the Gauss–Bonnet theorem as index theorems for Dirac operators.
Spectral geometry finds its most abstract formulation in noncommutative geometry, where the spectrum of the Dirac operator defines the metric, the action, and the very notion of geometric space.
