Chemical Thermodynamics — Enthalpy, Entropy, Gibbs Free Energy & Hess's Law

Deep dive into Chemical Thermodynamics — enthalpy H = U + PV: exothermic (ΔH < 0, heat released) and endothermic (ΔH > 0, heat absorbed) reactions, standard enthalpy of formation ΔH_f° (the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states), bond dissociation energies (the enthalpy required to break a specific bond averaged over similar compounds, used to estimate reaction enthalpies: ΔH_R° = Σ BDE_broken − Σ BDE_formed). Entropy S (a measure of disorder or the number of microstates W: S = k_B ln W), the second law of thermodynamics (ΔS_universe > 0 for a spontaneous process), the third law (the entropy of a perfect pure crystal at 0 K is zero), standard molar entropies S° (absolute, not relative to formation). Gibbs free energy G = H − TS: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, sign of ΔG determines spontaneity (ΔG < 0 spontaneous, ΔG = 0 at equilibrium, ΔG > 0 non-spontaneous), temperature dependence of spontaneity (ΔH and ΔS signs determine if a reaction is spontaneous at all, high, low, or no temperatures), the Gibbs-Helmholtz equation (ΔG = ΔH + T(∂ΔG/∂T)_P). Phase transitions: ΔG = 0 at equilibrium (melting point, boiling point), the Clausius-Clapeyron equation (ln P = −ΔH_vap/RT + C). Hess's law: ΔH for a reaction is the sum of ΔH for each step, regardless of the path (since enthalpy is a state function), allowing calculation of reaction enthalpies from known formation enthalpies: ΔH_R° = Σ ν ΔH_f°(products) − Σ ν ΔH_f°(reactants). Applications: formation enthalpies from combustion data, bond energy calculations, Born-Haber cycles for ionic compounds.

Part 1

🔥 Enthalpy and Calorimetry

Enthalpy
Heat of Reaction, Standard Enthalpy of Formation, Bond Energies, and Calorimetry
Enthalpy H = U + PV is a state function whose change ΔH equals the heat transferred at constant pressure (q_P). For an exothermic reaction (ΔH < 0), the system releases heat to the surroundings; for endothermic (ΔH > 0), the system absorbs heat. The standard enthalpy of formation ΔH_f° is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its constituent elements in their standard states (1 bar, specified temperature, usually 298 K). By definition, ΔH_f° = 0 for elements in their standard states (e.g., O₂(g), C(graphite), Br₂(l)). Bond dissociation energy (BDE) is the enthalpy required to break a specific bond, averaged over similar molecules. For example, the O-H bond energy in water is about 463 kJ/mol. Reaction enthalpies can be estimated: ΔH_R° ≈ Σ BDE(reactants) − Σ BDE(products) — breaking bonds costs energy (endothermic), forming bonds releases energy (exothermic). Calorimetry measures heat changes: q = m c ΔT, where m is mass, c is specific heat capacity. A bomb calorimeter (constant volume) measures ΔU = q_V; a coffee-cup calorimeter (constant pressure) measures ΔH = q_P. The heat capacity at constant pressure C_P = (∂H/∂T)_P; at constant volume C_V = (∂U/∂T)_V. For ideal gases, C_P − C_V = nR. Standard enthalpy of combustion ΔH_comb° is the enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in excess oxygen. Standard enthalpies of formation can be derived from combustion enthalpies using Hess's law. The Kirchhoff equation relates enthalpy change at different temperatures: ΔH(T₂) = ΔH(T₁) + ∫_{T₁}^{T₂} ΔC_P dT.
Enthalpy is the heat transferred at constant pressure; standard enthalpies of formation and bond energies serve as building blocks to calculate reaction enthalpies via Hess's law.
Part 2

🌀 Entropy and the Second Law

Entropy
Microstates, S = k ln W, the Second Law, and Standard Molar Entropies
Entropy S is a measure of the dispersal of energy or matter. The statistical definition, due to Boltzmann: S = k_B ln W, where W is the number of microstates (ways of arranging the energy and particles consistent with the macroscopic state). A spontaneous process is one that increases the total entropy of the universe: ΔS_univ = ΔS_sys + ΔS_surr > 0 (the second law of thermodynamics). For a reversible process at constant temperature, ΔS_sys = q_rev/T. For an irreversible process, ΔS_sys > q/T. The third law of thermodynamics: the entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero is zero (S(0 K) = 0), providing an absolute reference for entropy. Standard molar entropy S° (units J/mol·K) is the entropy of one mole of a substance in its standard state at 298 K. Unlike enthalpy, entropy has an absolute value (not a relative formation value). Gases have higher entropy than liquids, which have higher entropy than solids (gas > liquid > solid). Entropy increases with temperature, with molecular complexity, and with the number of moles of gas produced in a reaction. For a phase transition, ΔS_trans = ΔH_trans / T_trans. For example, ΔS_vap = ΔH_vap / T_b. The entropy change for a reaction: ΔS_R° = Σ ν S°(products) − Σ ν S°(reactants). The surroundings' entropy change: ΔS_surr = −ΔH_sys / T (for constant pressure). Thus the spontaneity condition ΔS_univ > 0 becomes ΔS_sys − ΔH_sys/T > 0, or ΔH_sys − T ΔS_sys < 0 — which is just −ΔG > 0.
Entropy quantifies the number of microscopic arrangements of a system; the second law dictates that the total entropy of the universe always increases for spontaneous processes.
Part 3

⚖️ Gibbs Free Energy and Spontaneity

Free Energy
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, the Criterion for Spontaneity, and the Gibbs-Helmholtz Equation
The Gibbs free energy G = H − TS is a state function that combines enthalpy and entropy into a single criterion for spontaneity at constant temperature and pressure: a process is spontaneous when ΔG < 0, at equilibrium when ΔG = 0, and non-spontaneous when ΔG > 0. The defining equation at constant T: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. The sign of ΔG depends on the signs and magnitudes of ΔH and ΔS. There are four cases: (1) ΔH < 0, ΔS > 0: spontaneous at all temperatures (e.g., 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂). (2) ΔH > 0, ΔS < 0: non-spontaneous at all temperatures (the reverse is spontaneous). (3) ΔH < 0, ΔS < 0: spontaneous at low T, non-spontaneous at high T (the entropy cost dominates at high T). (4) ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0: spontaneous at high T, non-spontaneous at low T (entropy-driven at high T, e.g., melting of ice above 0°C). The equilibrium temperature for a phase transition is T = ΔH/ΔS (where ΔG = 0). The standard Gibbs free energy of formation ΔG_f° is analogous to ΔH_f°; ΔG_f° = 0 for elements. For a reaction: ΔG_R° = Σ ν ΔG_f°(products) − Σ ν ΔG_f°(reactants). The Gibbs-Helmholtz equation relates ΔG to temperature: ΔG = ΔH + T(∂ΔG/∂T)_P, or equivalently (∂(ΔG/T)/∂T)_P = −ΔH/T². This equation allows computing ΔG at different temperatures from ΔH at a reference temperature. The van't Hoff equation (d ln K/dT = ΔH°/RT²) connects equilibrium constants with temperature and will be explored in the Equilibrium lesson. The chemical potential μ_i = (∂G/∂n_i)_{T,P,n_j} is the partial molar Gibbs free energy: at equilibrium, the chemical potential of each component is equal in all phases.
Gibbs free energy unifies enthalpy and entropy into a single criterion for spontaneity (ΔG < 0); the balance between energetic (ΔH) and entropic (TΔS) contributions determines the temperature dependence.
Part 4

🧩 Hess's Law and State Functions

Hess's Law
Hess's Law, Thermochemical Cycles, and Applications to Formation and Combustion
Hess's law states that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the path taken, depending only on the initial and final states — because enthalpy is a state function. This allows calculating ΔH for reactions that are difficult or impossible to measure directly. The general formula: ΔH_R° = Σ ν ΔH_f°(products) − Σ ν ΔH_f°(reactants), where ν are the stoichiometric coefficients. For example, to find ΔH for C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g) (which cannot be measured directly because some CO₂ always forms), one uses: (1) C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) ΔH = −393.5 kJ, and (2) CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g) ΔH = −283.0 kJ. Reversing (2) and adding to (1): ΔH = −393.5 + 283.0 = −110.5 kJ. This is Hess's law in action. Thermochemical cycles include the Born-Haber cycle (for lattice energy of ionic compounds), the combustion cycle (deriving ΔH_f° from ΔH_comb° and known ΔH_f° of CO₂ and H₂O), and the enthalpy of reaction from bond energies. Enthalpy of formation from combustion data: for a hydrocarbon C_xH_y, ΔH_f° = x ΔH_f°(CO₂) + (y/2) ΔH_f°(H₂O) − ΔH_comb°. Enthalpy of reaction from bond energies: ΔH_R° = Σ BDE(reactants) − Σ BDE(products) — this is an approximation (bond energies are averages over many compounds) but useful for estimating enthalpies when experimental data are unavailable. The same principle extends to other state functions: ΔS_R° = Σ ν S°(products) − Σ ν S°(reactants), and ΔG_R° = Σ ν ΔG_f°(products) − Σ ν ΔG_f°(reactants). The key insight: because U, H, S, and G are all state functions, their changes depend only on initial and final states, making thermochemical cycles possible.
Hess's law, a direct consequence of enthalpy being a state function, allows the calculation of reaction enthalpies via algebraic combination of known thermochemical equations — the foundation of thermochemistry.
