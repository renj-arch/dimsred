Part 1

⚛ Quantum Dots and Artificial Atoms

Artificial Atoms
Coulomb Blockade, the Constant Interaction Model, and the Kondo Effect
Quantum dots are nanoscale semiconductor structures that confine electrons in all three dimensions, creating discrete energy levels analogous to atoms. The particle-in-a-box model gives level spacing ΔE ∼ ħ²π²/(2m* L²) where L is the dot size — for L ∼ 100 nm, ΔE ∼ 1 meV. The constant interaction model: the total energy of an N-electron dot is U(N) = (Ne)²/(2C) − eN V_g C_g/C + Σ_n ε_n, where C is the total capacitance, V_g is the gate voltage, and ε_n are single-particle energies. The addition energy E_add(N) = μ(N+1) − μ(N) = e²/C + Δε (charging energy + level spacing). Coulomb blockade: when the charging energy e²/C exceeds k_B T, current is suppressed for certain gate voltages because adding an electron costs energy k_B T. The Coulomb diamond in the stability diagram dI/dV versus V_sd and V_g shows regions of stable electron number (blockaded regions). The single-electron transistor (SET) operates by tuning the dot through successive Coulomb oscillations. The Kondo effect in quantum dots: when the dot has an odd number of electrons (unpaired spin), a spin singlet forms between the dot spin and conduction electrons, producing a zero-bias conductance peak at low temperatures. The Kondo temperature T_K ∝ exp(−1/(Jν)) where J is the exchange coupling and ν is the density of states. The Anderson impurity model H = Σ_{kσ} ε_k c†_{kσ} c_{kσ} + ε_d Σ_σ d†_σ d_σ + U n_{d↑} n_{d↓} + Σ_{kσ} (V_k d†_σ c_{kσ} + h.c.) describes the dot coupled to leads. The Friedel sum rule relates the phase shift at the Fermi level to the occupation of the dot. The Fano effect in quantum dots arises from interference between a resonant tunneling path and a direct path through the dot, producing asymmetric lineshapes. The Pauli spin blockade in double quantum dots occurs when a transition is spin-forbidden, suppressing current — used to detect spin states for quantum computing.
Quantum dots are tunable artificial atoms where the interplay of charging energy, confinement, and exchange coupling gives rise to Coulomb blockade and Kondo physics, with applications in quantum computing and metrology.
Part 2

🔬 2D Materials and Graphene

Van der Waals Heterostructures
Graphene's Dirac Cone, TMDCs, Magic-Angle Twisted Bilayer Graphene, and Moiré Superlattices
Graphene is a single atomic layer of carbon in a honeycomb lattice, with two sublattices (A and B). The low-energy excitations are described by the Dirac Hamiltonian H = v_F (σ_x p_x + σ_y p_y) where v_F = 1 × 10⁶ m/s (the Fermi velocity) and σ_i are Pauli matrices acting on sublattice space, producing a linear dispersion E(k) = ± ħ v_F |k|. The density of states in graphene: ρ(E) = 2|E|/(π (ħ v_F)²) — linear, vanishing at the Dirac point. The chirality (or pseudospin) is locked to momentum: the wavefunction is an eigenstate of σ·k/|k|. The absence of backscattering in graphene: a perfect transmission through a potential barrier (Klein tunneling): T = 1 for normal incidence regardless of barrier height. The half-integer quantum Hall effect in graphene: σ_xy = ±(4e²/h)(n+½) with the factor 4 from spin and valley degeneracy. The transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) like MoS₂, WS₂, MoSe₂ have a direct bandgap in the monolayer (∼1.5–2 eV), strong spin-orbit coupling, and valley-contrasting physics where optical selection rules couple circularly polarized light to specific valleys (the valley Hall effect). Twisted bilayer graphene (TBG): two graphene layers rotated by a small angle θ form a moiré superlattice. The magic angle θ_m ≈ 1.1° produces flat bands at the Fermi level (the Bistritzer-MacDonald model), leading to correlated insulating states and superconductivity (Cao et al., 2018). The moiré period L_m = a/(2 sin(θ/2)) ≈ a/θ for small θ. The Hofstadter butterfly is the fractal energy spectrum of a 2D electron gas in a periodic potential and magnetic field — observed in moiré superlattices. hBN (hexagonal Boron Nitride) is a wide-bandgap insulator used as a substrate and dielectric layer in van der Waals heterostructures.
2D materials host Dirac fermions with unique transport properties; moiré engineering in twisted bilayers creates flat bands where electron interactions produce correlated phases including superconductivity and Mott insulators.
Part 3

🔌 Mesoscopic Physics and Quantum Transport

Quantum Transport
Landauer-Büttiker Formalism, Conductance Quantization, the Quantum Hall Effects, and the Aharonov-Bohm Effect
Mesoscopic physics describes electronic transport in systems where phase coherence is maintained across the device (L < L_φ, the phase coherence length). The Landauer formula: G = (e²/h) Σ_n T_n, where T_n is the transmission probability of the n-th conduction channel. The conductance quantum G₀ = 2e²/h ≈ 77.5 μS. In a quantum point contact, G = (2e²/h) N where N = floor(2W/λ_F) is the integer number of transverse modes — this gives the conductance staircase. The Landauer-Büttiker formalism: I_p = (e/h) Σ_q (T_{q←p} μ_p − T_{p←q} μ_q), where T_{q←p} is the transmission from lead p to lead q. The Buttiker probes model dephasing and inelastic scattering. The Aharonov-Bohm effect: the conductance of a ring oscillates with magnetic flux as G = G₀ + ΔG cos(2π Φ/Φ₀ + φ), where Φ₀ = h/e. The effect demonstrates that the vector potential A (not just B) affects the quantum phase. The weak localization correction: constructive interference of time-reversed paths reduces conductance; suppressed by a magnetic field (positive magnetoresistance). The universal conductance fluctuations (UCF): the RMS conductance fluctuations in a disordered mesoscopic sample are of order e²/h, independent of the sample size or disorder strength. The integer quantum Hall effect (IQHE): at low temperature and high magnetic field, σ_xy = ν e²/h with ν integer, and σ_xx = 0. The Landau levels E_n = ħω_c (n+½) with ω_c = eB/m*. The edge states carry the Hall current chirally. The fractional quantum Hall effect (FQHE): at ν = 1/3, 2/5, etc., the ground state is the Laughlin wavefunction Ψ_m = Π_{ianyons with fractional charge e/m and fractional statistics. The composite fermion theory (Jain): at ν = p/(2p+1), electrons bind to 2 flux quanta forming composite fermions in an effective magnetic field.
Mesoscopic transport reveals quantum coherence at the nanoscale — conductance quantization, the Aharonov-Bohm effect, weak localization, and the quantum Hall effects arise from the wave nature of electrons.
Part 4

🧲 Tunneling and Spintronics

Spin Electronics
The Josephson Effect, STM, Giant Magnetoresistance, Spin Transfer Torque, and Majorana Fermions
Single-electron tunneling (SET): when the junction capacitance is small, the charging energy e²/(2C) exceeds k_B T, suppressing tunneling at low bias — the Coulomb staircase. The Josephson effect in a superconductor-insulator-superconductor junction: the supercurrent I = I_c sin(Δφ), where Δφ is the phase difference across the junction. The AC Josephson effect: when a DC voltage V is applied, Δφ evolves as d(Δφ)/dt = 2eV/ħ, producing an AC supercurrent at frequency f = 2eV/h (the Josephson frequency, ∼483 MHz/μV). The DC Josephson effect: a DC supercurrent flows at V=0 up to the critical current I_c. Scanning tunneling microscopy (STM): the tunneling current I ∝ V exp(−2κd) where d is the tip-sample distance and κ = √(2mφ)/ħ is the decay constant (φ is the work function). The Tersoff-Hamann model: I ∝ Σ_ν |ψ_ν(r_0)|² δ(E_ν − E_F) — the STM maps the local density of states (LDOS) at the Fermi level. The giant magnetoresistance (GMR) in a ferromagnet/normal-metal/ferromagnet spin valve: R_AP/R_P = (1+P₁P₂)/(1−P₁P₂) where P = (D↑−D↓)/(D↑+D↓) is the spin polarization. The spin transfer torque (STT): a spin-polarized current transfers angular momentum to a magnetic layer, switching its magnetization direction. The Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation with Slonczewski term: dM/dt = −γ M × H_eff + (α/M_s) M × dM/dt + τ_STT. The Rashba effect: spin-orbit coupling in a 2D electron gas with inversion asymmetry: H_R = α_R (σ × k)·ẑ, where α_R is the Rashba parameter. This produces spin-momentum locking and the Dresselhaus effect in III-V semiconductors. Majorana fermions in condensed matter: particle-hole symmetric zero-energy states at the ends of a topological superconductor nanowire (the Kitaev chain model: H = Σ [−t(c†_n c_{n+1} + h.c.) − μ c†_n c_n + Δ c_n c_{n+1} + h.c.]). The Majorana zero mode γ = γ† satisfies the non-Abelian braiding statistics, with potential applications in topological quantum computing. The spin Hall effect: an electric current generates a transverse spin current without magnetic fields, described by the spin Hall conductivity σ_{SH}.
Tunneling at the nanoscale reveals single-electron and Josephson physics; spintronics exploits the electron's spin degree of freedom for memory and logic, while topological phases host Majorana anyons for fault-tolerant quantum computation.
