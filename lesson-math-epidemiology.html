Mathematical Epidemiology — the SIR Model, the Basic Reproduction Number & Herd Immunity

Mathematical epidemiology studies the spread of infectious diseases using mathematical models. The SIR model (Kermack-McKendrick, 1927) partitions a population into Susceptible (S), Infectious (I), and Recovered (R) compartments, governed by the ordinary differential equations dS/dt = −βSI, dI/dt = βSI − γI, dR/dt = γI, where β is the transmission rate and γ is the recovery rate. The basic reproduction number R₀ = βS(0)/γ gives the expected number of secondary cases from a single infected individual in a fully susceptible population. The threshold theorem states an epidemic occurs iff R₀ > 1. The final size relation R(∞) = 1 − exp(−R₀·R(∞)) determines the total attack rate. More complex models incorporate an exposed class (SEIR), waning immunity (SIRS), age structure, stochastic effects, spatial structure, and optimal control via vaccination. The next-generation matrix method generalizes R₀ computation to multi-compartment models. Network models capture the contact structure underlying transmission.

"All models are wrong, but some epidemics are useful — by giving us the mathematical R₀."
— Roy Anderson
Part 1

📏 The Deterministic SIR Model

SIR
The Compartmental SIR Model, Threshold Theorem, and Final Size Relation
The SIR model (Kermack-McKendrick, 1927) is the foundation of mathematical epidemiology. The population N = S + I + R is partitioned into three compartments: Susceptible S (those who can contract the disease), Infectious I (those who can transmit it), and Recovered R (those who have immunity). The dynamics are given by the system: dS/dt = −β·S·I, dI/dt = β·S·I − γ·I, dR/dt = γ·I, where β is the transmission rate per susceptible per infectious and γ is the recovery rate (1/γ is the mean infectious period). The basic reproduction number R₀ = β·S(0)/γ. At the disease-free equilibrium with S(0) ≈ N, R₀ = βN/γ. The Kermack-McKendrick threshold theorem: an epidemic occurs if and only if R₀ > 1. The final size relation gives the total number infected over the course of the epidemic: R(∞) = 1 − exp(−R₀·R(∞)) (when the initial susceptible fraction S(0)/N ≈ 1). The peak prevalence is I_max = 1 − (1 + ln R₀)/R₀. The SEIR model adds an exposed class E (individuals infected but not yet infectious) with latency period 1/σ: dS/dt = −βSI, dE/dt = βSI − σE, dI/dt = σE − γI, dR/dt = γI. The SIRS model allows waning immunity: recovered individuals become susceptible again at rate α, giving dR/dt = γI − αR. The general renewal equation describes the incidence i(t) (new infections at time t) as i(t) = ∫₀^∞ A(τ) i(t−τ) dτ where A(τ) is the infectiousness at time τ since infection — the force of infection depends on the entire past history of the epidemic.
The SIR model is the fundamental compartmental framework for epidemic dynamics; the threshold R₀ > 1 separates epidemic growth from extinction.
Part 2

🌍 The Basic Reproduction Number R₀

R₀
The Next-Generation Matrix Method and Computing R₀ in Complex Models
The basic reproduction number R₀ is the expected number of secondary infections caused by a single infected individual in a fully susceptible population. For complex multi-compartment models, the next-generation matrix method (Diekmann-Heesterbeek-Metz, 1990) provides a systematic way to compute R₀. Linearize the system at the disease-free equilibrium. Define the next-generation matrix K = F·V⁻¹, where F is the matrix of new infection terms (rates at which newly infected individuals enter compartments) and V is the matrix of transfer terms (rates of movement between infected compartments, including recovery and death). Then R₀ = ρ(K), the spectral radius (largest eigenvalue) of K. For the SEIR model: F = [[0, βN], [0, 0]], V = [[σ, 0], [−σ, γ]], so K = [[βN/γ, βN/γ], [0, 0]] and R₀ = βN/γ (same as SIR). For the multi-group model: R₀ = ρ(C·D) where C is the contact matrix (rates of contact between groups) and D is the diagonal matrix of infectious durations. The Ross-Macdonald model for vector-borne disease (malaria): R₀ = (m·a²·b·c·pⁿ)/(r·(−ln p)) where m is the mosquito-to-human ratio, a is the biting rate per mosquito, b is the transmission probability from infected human to mosquito, c is the transmission probability from infected mosquito to human, p is the daily survival probability of the mosquito, n is the extrinsic incubation period, and r is the human recovery rate. The basic reproduction number also captures the herd immunity threshold: the critical vaccination fraction p_c = 1 − 1/R₀.
R₀ is the single most important threshold parameter in epidemiology; the next-generation matrix method extends its computation to arbitrarily complex compartmental models.
Part 3

🧮 Stochastic and Spatial Models

Stochastic
The Stochastic SIR, Branching Processes, Metapopulations, and Network Models
Real epidemics are stochastic, especially at the start and near extinction. The stochastic SIR model treats births of new infections and removals as random events. At the start of an epidemic, when the number of susceptibles is large and the number of infectives is small, the process is approximated by a branching process. The offspring distribution (number of secondary cases per infected) is often Poisson with mean R₀ or negative binomial (allowing overdispersion). The probability of extinction q is the smallest nonnegative root of the generating function equation q = G_S(q), where G_S(z) = Σ p_k z^k is the probability generating function of the offspring distribution. For a Poisson offspring distribution with mean R₀, q = 1 if R₀ ≤ 1 and q < 1 if R₀ > 1 (specifically, q = W₀(−R₀ e^{−R₀}) / (−R₀) where W₀ is the principal branch of the Lambert W function, or simply the solution of q = e^{R₀(q−1)}). For the spatial Metapopulation model: the population is divided into patches (cities) connected by travel. The Lagrangian approach tracks individuals by their residence location and current location via a commuting matrix. The network models represent individuals as nodes and contacts as edges in a graph. The configuration model generates random graphs with a given degree distribution P(k). For an SIR epidemic on a configuration-model network, the epidemic threshold is: R₀ = (β/γ)·⟨k²⟩/⟨k⟩, where ⟨k⟩ is the mean degree and ⟨k²⟩ is the second moment. The percolation threshold corresponds to the bond percolation probability T = β/(β+γ) — an epidemic occurs if T > 1/(κ−1) where κ = ⟨k²⟩/⟨k⟩ is the degree ratio.
Stochastic effects dominate in small populations; network structure fundamentally alters the epidemic threshold through the degree distribution's second moment.
Part 4

🔥 Control Theory and Vaccination

Control
Optimal Control, Herd Immunity, and Within-Host Virus Dynamics
Optimal control theory provides a framework for designing public health interventions. Minimize the cost functional J = ∫₀^T (I(t) + c·V(t)²) dt subject to the SIR dynamics with vaccination V(t). Pontryagin's maximum principle gives necessary optimality conditions via an adjoint system of differential equations. The optimal vaccination strategy balances the cost of infection against the cost of intervention. The critical vaccination fraction for herd immunity: p_c > 1 − 1/R₀. If a proportion p of the population is vaccinated before an epidemic, the effective reproduction number is R = R₀·(1−p). When R < 1, herd immunity protects the unvaccinated. For measles (R₀ ≈ 12–18), p_c ≈ 92–95%. The ring vaccination strategy (used for smallpox eradication and Ebola) vaccinates contacts of confirmed cases. The SEIR with case isolation models quarantine (reducing contact for symptomatic individuals) and contact tracing. The within-host virus dynamics model (Nowak-May): dT/dt = λ − dT − βTV, dI/dt = βTV − aI, dV/dt = kI − μV, where T are target cells (CD4+ T cells for HIV), I are infected cells, V is the virus concentration, λ is the target cell production rate, d is the death rate of target cells, β is the infection rate, a is the death rate of infected cells, k is the virus production rate, and μ is the virus clearance rate. The HIV model includes a latently infected cell reservoir that prevents sterilizing cure. Antiretroviral therapy reduces β (entry inhibitors) or k (reverse transcriptase/protease inhibitors), leading to a characteristic viral load decay: first fast phase (free virus clearance, half-life hours), second slow phase (infected cell loss, half-life days), and third phase (latent reservoir decay, half-life years). The COVID-19 modeling used extended SEIR models incorporating asymptomatic infections (40–50% of cases), age-stratified contact patterns (social contact matrices), and non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) such as lockdowns, mask mandates, and school closures — with effectiveness measured by the time-varying reproduction number R_t.
Mathematical models guide vaccination strategies and public health policy; within-host models bridge the gap between virology and population-level epidemiology.
