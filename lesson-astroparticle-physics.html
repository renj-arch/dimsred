Part 1

☄ Cosmic Rays and High-Energy Particles

UHECRs
The Cosmic Ray Spectrum, the Knee and Ankle, the GZK Cutoff, Fermi Acceleration, and the Hillas Criterion
Cosmic rays are high-energy charged particles (mostly protons and nuclei) from space, with energies spanning from ∼10⁸ eV to >10²⁰ eV. The primary energy spectrum follows dN/dE ∝ E^{-γ} with γ ≈ 2.7 below the knee (E_k ≈ 3 × 10¹⁵ eV) and γ ≈ 3.1 above it. The knee is thought to mark the maximum energy of Galactic cosmic rays from supernova remnants (the rigidity cutoff for protons in Galactic magnetic fields). The second knee at ∼10¹⁷ eV corresponds to the cutoff for iron nuclei. The ankle at E_A ≈ 3 × 10¹⁸ eV is a hardening of the spectrum (γ ≈ 2.6), interpreted as the transition from Galactic to extragalactic cosmic rays. The GZK cutoff (Greisen-Zatsepin-Kuzmin, 1966): protons above ∼5 × 10¹⁹ eV lose energy by interacting with the CMB photons via the Δ⁺ resonance: p + γ_CMB → Δ⁺ → p + π⁰ (or n + π⁺). The suppression limits the propagation distance of UHECRs to ∼100 Mpc — the GZK horizon. The Pierre Auger Observatory in Argentina (3000 km²) detects UHECR air showers using 1600 water Cherenkov detectors (surface array) and 27 fluorescence telescopes. The fluorescence detection measures the longitudinal shower profile, giving the depth of shower maximum X_max, which constrains the primary composition (lighter primaries have deeper X_max). The Hillas criterion for particle acceleration in an astrophysical source: the maximum energy E_max ∝ Z B R, where B is the magnetic field and R is the size of the acceleration region. The Fermi first-order acceleration (diffusive shock acceleration): particles gain energy by crossing a shock front multiple times, with ΔE/E ∼ v_shock/c per crossing, producing a power-law spectrum dN/dE ∝ E^{-2}. The supernova remnant (SNR) paradigm: the expanding blast wave of a supernova accelerates cosmic rays up to the knee, with the total power ∼10⁵⁰ erg/s — matching the Galactic cosmic ray energy budget.
The cosmic ray spectrum spans 11 orders of magnitude in energy, with spectral features at the knee (Galactic maximum), ankle (extragalactic transition), and GZK cutoff (interaction with the CMB), while Fermi acceleration in shocks produces the observed power-law distribution.

🌀 Dark Matter

DM
WIMPs, Axions, Direct Detection, Indirect Detection, and the Halo Model
Dark matter evidence: galaxy rotation curves (v ∝ 1/√r in the outer parts vs the Keplerian 1/√r), the Bullet Cluster (1E 0657-558) showing the separation of baryonic and dark matter, the CMB power spectrum (Ω_DM h² = 0.120 ± 0.001 from Planck), the large-scale structure formation (the ΛCDM model matches the matter power spectrum). The WIMP miracle: a weakly-interacting massive particle (WIMP) with mass ∼100 GeV and weak-scale cross section ⟨σv⟩ ∼ 3 × 10^{-26} cm³/s naturally gives the observed relic density Ω_χ h² ∼ 0.12 via thermal freeze-out. The freeze-out calculation: the Boltzmann equation dn_χ/dt = −3H n_χ − ⟨σv⟩ (n_χ² − n_eq²). The relic density Ω_χ ∝ 1/⟨σv⟩. The direct detection of WIMPs via elastic scattering off nuclei: the differential rate dR/dE_R = (ρ_0/M_χ m_N) ∫_{v_min} f(v) v (dσ/dE_R) d³v. The spin-independent (SI) cross section σ_SI ∝ A² (coherent scattering on all nucleons). The spin-dependent (SD) cross section couples to unpaired nuclear spins. The current best limits: XENONnT sets σ_SI < 5 × 10^{-48} cm² for 50 GeV WIMPs. The DAMA/LIBRA experiment claims a detection via annual modulation (the Earth's velocity relative to the Galactic halo varies over the year), but the result is in strong tension with other experiments. The axion is a hypothetical particle from the Peccei-Quinn solution to the strong CP problem: the QCD Lagrangian has the CP-violating term θ̄ (α_s/8π) G G̃, and the axion field a(x) relaxes θ̄ to zero. The axion mass m_a ∼ 5.7 μeV (10¹² GeV/f_a), where f_a is the PQ symmetry breaking scale. The axion-photon coupling L_aγγ = −(g_{aγ}/4) a F_{μν} F̃^{μν}. The ADMX (Axion Dark Matter eXperiment) uses a resonant cavity in a strong magnetic field to convert axions to photons (the Primakoff effect) in the μeV–meV mass range. Indirect detection: WIMP annihilation produces gamma rays, neutrinos, or cosmic rays. The Fermi Bubbles and the Galactic Center excess (GCE) at ∼1–3 GeV have been interpreted as possible dark matter annihilation signals (χχ → b\bar{b} produces a spectrum peaked at ∼m_χ/20). The substructure problem: ΛCDM predicts hundreds of dwarf satellite galaxies around the Milky Way, but only ∼50 are observed — possibly due to supernova feedback, reionization suppression, or dark matter self-interactions.
Dark matter constitutes 85% of the universe's matter; the WIMP and axion are the leading particle candidates, probed by direct detection (XENONnT, LZ, ADMX), indirect detection (Fermi, CTA), and collider searches (LHC).

🌊 Neutrino Astronomy

Neutrinos
Solar Neutrinos, the MSW Effect, Supernova Neutrinos, IceCube, and High-Energy Neutrinos
Solar neutrinos are produced in the pp chain (∼85% of solar energy) and the CNO cycle (∼15%). The pp chain: p + p → d + e⁺ + ν_e (pp neutrinos, E_ν < 0.42 MeV), followed by ³He + ³He → ⁴He + 2p, or the pp-II and pp-III branches producing ⁷Be and ⁸B neutrinos (E_ν up to 15 MeV). The solar neutrino problem: the observed flux of electron neutrinos from the Sun was ∼⅓ of the standard solar model prediction (Bahcall et al.). The MSW effect (Mikheyev-Smirnov-Wolfenstein): neutrino flavor oscillations are modified by forward scattering on electrons in the solar matter. The effective mass-squared difference in matter: Δm²_M = Δm² √{(cos 2θ − √2 G_F n_e E/Δm²)² + sin² 2θ}. The solar neutrinos undergo adiabatic resonant conversion inside the Sun (the LMA-MSW solution): ν_e convert to ν_μ (or ν_τ) through the MSW resonance at density n_e = Δm² cos 2θ / (2√2 G_F E). The allowed oscillation parameters: Δm²_sol = 7.5 × 10^{-5} eV², sin² θ_{12} = 0.30 (from SNO, KamLAND, Borexino). The hep neutrino reaction: ³He + p → ⁴He + e⁺ + ν_e (the highest energy solar neutrinos, up to 18.8 MeV). Supernova neutrinos: a core-collapse supernova releases ∼99% of its gravitational binding energy (∼3 × 10⁵³ erg) as neutrinos of all flavors over ∼10 seconds. The neutronization burst (∼10 ms) produces a pulse of ν_e from p + e⁻ → n + ν_e. The accretion phase (∼0.5 s) and the Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling phase (∼10 s) produce thermal neutrino spectra with average energies ⟨E_ν_e⟩ ∼ 10 MeV, ⟨E_ν̄_e⟩ ∼ 14 MeV, ⟨E_ν_x⟩ ∼ 18 MeV. The SN 1987A neutrino detection: 19 neutrino events from the LMC were detected by Kamiokande, IMB, and Baksan — confirming core-collapse theory and giving a 3-hour early warning of the optical supernova. The diffuse supernova neutrino background (DSNB) is the integrated flux of all core-collapse supernovae in the universe. High-energy astrophysical neutrinos: IceCube at the South Pole (1 km³ of instrumented ice) detects Cherenkov light from neutrino-induced muons and cascades. The IceCube diffuse flux (2013): a flux of high-energy neutrinos (E_ν ∼ 10 TeV – 10 PeV) with E² dN/dE ≈ 1.4 × 10^{-8} GeV cm^{-2} s^{-1} sr^{-1} for an E^{-2.2} spectrum. The blazar TXS 0506+056 was the first compelling source of high-energy neutrinos (a 3σ correlation with a 290 TeV muon neutrino in 2017). The Glashow resonance: ν̄_e + e⁻ → W⁻ at E_ν = m_W²/(2 m_e) ≈ 6.3 PeV — a resonant production of the W boson. The Waxman-Bahcall bound: the maximum possible flux of high-energy neutrinos from optically thin sources is E_ν² Φ_ν ≲ 3 × 10^{-8} GeV cm^{-2} s^{-1} sr^{-1}. The cosmogenic (GZK) neutrinos: produced by the interaction of UHECR protons with CMB photons (p + γ → n + π⁺ → n + μ⁺ + ν_μ), with peak flux at E_ν ∼ 10¹⁷–10¹⁸ eV.
Neutrino astronomy spans energies from MeV (solar and supernova) to EeV (cosmogenic), using the MSW effect (solar), core-collapse bursts (SN 1987A), and the IceCube high-energy flux to probe physics from the Sun's core to distant blazars.

💥 Gamma-Ray Bursts and High-Energy Astrophysics

GRBs
The Fireball Model, the Prompt and Afterglow Emission, the Collapsar Model, and GRB 221009A (the BOAT)
Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are the most luminous electromagnetic events in the universe, with isotropic equivalent luminosities up to 10⁵⁴ erg/s. The two classes: long GRBs (T_90 > 2 s, softer spectrum, associated with type Ic supernovae, from the collapse of massive stars — the collapsar model) and short GRBs (T_90 < 2 s, harder spectrum, associated with compact object mergers — BNS or BH-NS). The fireball model: a relativistic jet with Lorentz factor Γ ∼ 100–1000 is launched from the central engine (a newly formed black hole with an accretion torus). The initial fireball is hot and optically thick. Internal shocks (collisions between shells of different Lorentz factors) produce the prompt gamma-ray emission via synchrotron radiation and inverse Compton scattering. The prompt emission spectrum is typically a broken power law (the Band function): N(E) ∝ E^{α} for E < E_break, E^{β} for E > E_break, with α ∼ −1, β ∼ −2.3, and E_break ∼ 300 keV. External shocks (the jet interacting with the circumburst medium) produce the afterglow — a power-law decay over days to years observed from radio to X-rays. The afterglow is well-described by synchrotron radiation from shock-accelerated electrons (the forward shock). A reverse shock propagates back into the ejecta, producing an early optical flash. The Blandford-Znajek mechanism: energy is extracted from a rotating black hole's spin via magnetic field lines threading the event horizon, powering the jet with power L_BZ ∝ (B² M² a²). The collapsar model (Woosley, 1993): the core of a rapidly rotating massive star collapses to a black hole, with fallback accretion powering the jet that pierces the stellar envelope, producing a long GRB coincident with a type Ic supernova (e.g., GRB 980425/SN 1998bw). GRB 221009A (October 9, 2022): the brightest of all time (BOAT) — the most energetic GRB ever detected, with E_iso ∼ 10⁵⁵ erg, a peak energy of ∼3 MeV, and detected by Fermi, Swift, and many others. The Amati relation: E_p ∝ E_iso^{0.5}. The Yonetoku relation: L_p ∝ E_p². The blazar sequence: high-luminosity blazars (FSRQs) have lower peak frequencies and higher Compton dominance; low-luminosity blazars (BL Lacs) have higher peak frequencies. The extragalactic background light (EBL) attenuates TeV gamma rays via γγ → e⁺ e⁻ pair production, limiting the horizon for very high-energy astronomy. The Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect: CMB photons are inverse-Compton scattered by hot electrons in galaxy clusters, producing a spectral distortion (the thermal SZ effect) and a kinematic SZ effect from the cluster's peculiar velocity.
Gamma-ray bursts are powered by relativistic jets from newborn black holes; the fireball model with internal/external shocks explains the prompt and afterglow emission, while multimessenger observations link short GRBs to BNS mergers and long GRBs to stellar collapse.
