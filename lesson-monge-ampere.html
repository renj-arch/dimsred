Monge-Ampère Equation — Elliptic PDE, Convex Geometry & the Brunn-Minkowski Theory

The Monge-Ampère equation det(D²u) = f(x, u, Du) is a fully nonlinear elliptic PDE of central importance across pure and applied mathematics. It governs the shape of convex surfaces with prescribed Gauss curvature (the Minkowski problem), the existence of Kähler-Einstein metrics in complex geometry (Yau's proof of the Calabi conjecture), and the optimal transport map between probability measures (Brenier's theorem). The theory of weak solutions — Alexandrov solutions for convex functions — combined with the powerful regularity estimates of Caffarelli (C^{1,α} and C^{2,α}) forms the analytic backbone. The Brunn-Minkowski inequality, a cornerstone of convex geometry, is intimately related to the Monge-Ampère equation through the Minkowski problem and the theory of mixed volumes. Applications span reflector antenna design, image registration, mesh generation, meteorology (the semi-geostrophic equations), and fluid dynamics.

"The Monge-Ampère equation is the Rosetta stone of fully nonlinear elliptic PDE."
— Luis Caffarelli
Part 1

📏 The Classical Monge-Ampère Equation and Convexity

Classical Theory
The Dirichlet Problem, Convexity, and the Monge-Ampère Measure
The classical Monge-Ampère equation is det(D²u) = f in Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ with u = 0 on ∂Ω, where D²u denotes the Hessian matrix of u. The equation is elliptic precisely when f > 0 and D²u > 0 (u is strictly convex). The equation can be rewritten as the Jacobian determinant of the gradient map: det(D²u) = det(∇(∇u)) — so the map x ↦ ∇u(x) is locally measure-preserving with density f. For a convex function u, the normal mapping (or subgradient) ∂u(E) = {y ∈ ℝⁿ : y = ∇u(x) for some x ∈ E} defines the Monge-Ampère measure μ_u(E) = |∂u(E)|, the Lebesgue measure of the image of E under the normal mapping. This measure is a powerful tool that allows defining weak (Alexandrov) solutions for merely convex functions. The Dirichlet problem on a smooth, strictly convex domain Ω with f > 0 and f ∈ C^{∞}(Ω) has a unique smooth convex solution — this was established by the work of Pogorelov, Cheng-Yau, and later Caffarelli-Nirenberg-Spruck using the continuity method and a priori estimates. The key a priori estimates are: (1) the C⁰ estimate via the Alexandrov maximum principle, (2) the C¹ estimate via the convexity, and (3) the C² estimate via the Pogorelov and Evans-Krylov theories.
The Monge-Ampère equation is the geometric PDE whose ellipticity is equivalent to strict convexity; the Monge-Ampère measure provides a notion of weak solution for arbitrary convex functions.

🌍 The Minkowski Problem and the Brunn-Minkowski Theory

Convex Geometry
The Minkowski Problem, Brunn-Minkowski Inequality, and Mixed Volumes
The Minkowski problem is the foundational inverse problem in convex geometry: given a positive function K on the unit sphere S^{n-1} (prescribed Gauss curvature), find a convex body K ⊂ ℝⁿ whose Gauss curvature at the point with outward normal ν is K(ν). Via the support function h of the convex body, this reduces to solving the spherical Monge-Ampère equation: det(∇²h + h·I) = K on S^{n-1}, where ∇ is the covariant derivative on the sphere. The Brunn-Minkowski inequality is the central inequality of convex geometry: for convex bodies A, B ⊂ ℝⁿ, Vol((1−t)A + tB)^{1/n} ≥ (1−t)Vol(A)^{1/n} + t·Vol(B)^{1/n}, with equality iff A and B are homothetic. This inequality is equivalent to the Prékopa-Leindler inequality (a functional form) and is intimately connected to the isoperimetric inequality. The Aleksandrov-Fenchel inequality generalizes Brunn-Minkowski to mixed volumes: V(K₁, K₂, …, K_n)² ≥ V(K₁, K₁, K₃, …, K_n)·V(K₂, K₂, K₃, …, K_n). The Minkowski problem was solved independently by Aleksandrov (using the Brunn-Minkowski inequality and a variational argument) for arbitrary convex bodies, and by Nirenberg, Pogorelov, and Cheng-Yau (using PDE methods — the spherical Monge-Ampère equation) for smooth solutions. The Lp Minkowski problem (Lutwak) and the Monge-Ampère equation for convex bodies with prescribed curvature measures are active modern generalizations.
The Brunn-Minkowski inequality is the backbone of convex geometry; its equivalence with the Minkowski problem demonstrates the deep interplay between geometry and fully nonlinear PDE.

🧮 Regularity Theory and Caffarelli's Estimates

Regularity
Alexandrov Maximum Principle, Pogorelov Estimates, and Caffarelli's C^{1,α} / C^{2,α} Theory
The regularity theory for the Monge-Ampère equation is one of the crowning achievements of fully nonlinear elliptic PDE. The Alexandrov maximum principle provides the foundational C⁰ estimate: for a convex solution u of det(D²u) = f on Ω with u = 0 on ∂Ω, we have sup_Ω |u| ≤ C·diam(Ω)·(∫_Ω f)^{1/n}. The Pogorelov interior C^{1,1} estimate asserts that any convex solution has bounded second derivatives in the interior — specifically, |D²u| ≤ C·sup|u|/d² where d is the distance to the boundary. Caffarelli's regularity theory (1980s) revolutionized the field: (1) if f is continuous and positive, then u ∈ C^{1,α}_{loc} for some α ∈ (0,1) — this is proved via a Harnack inequality and a compactness argument using the affine invariance of the equation; (2) if f ∈ C^{0,α}, then u ∈ C^{2,α}_{loc} — this adapts the Evans-Krylov theory to the Monge-Ampère equation. The C^{2,α} estimate takes the form |u|_{C^{2,α}} ≤ C(Ω, f, |u|_{L^∞}). Boundary regularity (Caffarelli's boundary Harnack) shows that if ∂Ω is smooth and f is smooth, then u is smooth up to the boundary. A striking counterexample is Pogorelov's singular solution: u(x', x_n) = (1 − |x'|²)·x_n^{2(n−1)/n} for x_n > 0, which is convex and satisfies det(D²u) = C·x_n^{−2} and has a line of singularities along x_n = 0. This shows that interior C² regularity is optimal in general.
Caffarelli's estimates provide optimal Hölder regularity for the Monge-Ampère equation under minimal assumptions — a landmark in fully nonlinear elliptic PDE theory.

🔥 The Complex Monge-Ampère Equation and Calabi-Yau Manifolds

Complex & Applied
Calabi-Yau Manifolds, Optimal Transport, and Applications
On a compact Kähler manifold (M, ω), the complex Monge-Ampère equation is (ω + i∂∂̄φ)ⁿ = e^{F} ωⁿ, where ω is a Kähler form, φ is a smooth function, and F is prescribed. This equation is the key to the Calabi conjecture (proved by Yau, 1977): given a smooth real (1,1)-form representing 2πc₁(M), there exists a unique Kähler metric in a given cohomology class with that prescribed Ricci form. When c₁(M) = 0, this yields a Ricci-flat Kähler metric — a Calabi-Yau manifold. The proof uses the continuity method and the three key estimates: the C⁰ estimate (Moser iteration and the Alexandrov-Bakelman-Pucci maximum principle), the C² estimate (the Chern-Lu inequality and the Aubin-Yau estimate bounding the Laplacian), and higher regularity (elliptic bootstrapping once C^{2,α} is obtained). In optimal transport, Brenier's theorem states that given two absolutely continuous probability measures μ, ν on ℝⁿ with finite second moments, there exists a convex function u such that ∇u pushes μ forward to ν. The map u solves the Monge-Ampère equation det(D²u) = μ/ν∘∇u — the density ratio of the two measures. Applications include: reflector antenna design (far-field radiation pattern via Monge-Ampère on the sphere), image registration (warping one image to another), mesh generation (generating meshes with prescribed density), and the semi-geostrophic equations in meteorology (where the geopotential satisfies a time-dependent Monge-Ampère equation).
The complex Monge-Ampère equation solved the Calabi conjecture and birthed Calabi-Yau geometry; in the applied realm, optimal transport theory provides a Monge-Ampère framework for numerous engineering and scientific problems.

The Dirichlet Problem for the Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Perron Method

The Dirichlet problem for the real Monge‑Ampère equation asks for a convex function u on a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ such that det(D²u) = f in Ω and u = g on ∂Ω, where f > 0 is prescribed and g is continuous boundary data. Classical solutions require u ∈ C²(Ω) ∩ C⁰(Ω̅), but the fully nonlinear nature of the determinant forces one to work with weaker notions. The Perron method, adapted by Ishii and Lions, constructs the maximal subsolution and the minimal supersolution; their coincidence yields a unique viscosity solution. A key ingredient is the comparison principle: if u is a subsolution and v a supersolution with u ≤ v on ∂Ω, then u ≤ v in Ω. This principle holds for the Monge‑Ampère operator because it is degenerate elliptic and proper. The Perron construction then gives a solution that is automatically convex and satisfies the equation in the viscosity sense. When f and g are smooth and Ω is uniformly convex, the solution is in fact classical by the regularity theory of Caffarelli, Nirenberg, and Spruck. The Dirichlet problem is also the natural setting for the Minkowski problem: prescribing the Gauss curvature of a convex hypersurface translates into a Monge‑Ampère equation on the sphere with a boundary condition encoded by the support function. The Perron method thus provides a unified existence framework that underlies both the PDE and the geometric applications.

Viscosity Solutions and the Comparison Principle for Fully Nonlinear Equations

Viscosity solutions, introduced by Crandall and Lions, are the standard weak formulation for fully nonlinear second‑order equations such as the Monge‑Ampère equation. A continuous function u is a viscosity subsolution of det(D²u) = f if for every smooth test function φ touching u from above at a point x₀, one has det(D²φ(x₀)) ≤ f(x₀). The supersolution definition reverses the inequality and the touching direction. This definition avoids any requirement of differentiability and is stable under uniform limits, which is essential for the Perron method. The comparison principle for the Monge‑Ampère operator states that if u is a subsolution and v a supersolution with u ≤ v on ∂Ω, then u ≤ v in Ω. The proof uses the doubling‑variables technique and the structural properties of the determinant: it is monotone with respect to the matrix argument on the cone of positive semidefinite matrices, and it is homogeneous of degree n. A crucial technical step is the use of the matrix inequality det(A) ≤ det(B) whenever 0 ≤ A ≤ B, which follows from the eigenvalues interlacing property. The comparison principle yields uniqueness of viscosity solutions and, together with the Perron method, existence. Moreover, the viscosity framework allows one to treat degenerate right‑hand sides f ≥ 0, leading to the theory of Alexandrov solutions where the Monge‑Ampère measure μu(E) = |∂u(E)| is defined for any convex u. The equivalence between Alexandrov solutions and viscosity solutions for continuous f is a deep result due to Caffarelli, and it bridges the geometric measure‑theoretic approach with the PDE approach.

Worked Example: Viscosity Subsolution Verification
Let Ω = B₁(0) ⊂ ℝ² and consider u(x) = |x|². Show that u is a viscosity subsolution of det(D²u) = 4 in Ω.
The function u is smooth, so its only test functions touching from above are those with φ ≥ u and φ(x₀) = u(x₀). At any point x₀, D²u = 2I, hence det(D²u) = 4. For any such φ, the matrix inequality D²φ(x₀) ≥ D²u(x₀) = 2I holds because φ − u has a minimum at x₀. Since the determinant is monotone on positive definite matrices, det(D²φ(x₀)) ≥ det(2I) = 4. The viscosity subsolution condition requires det(D²φ(x₀)) ≤ 4, which is satisfied because equality holds. Therefore u is a viscosity subsolution (in fact a classical solution).
Exam Strategy
When a question asks to verify a viscosity sub‑ or supersolution, first check whether the candidate function is smooth. If it is, the test reduces to the classical inequality on the Hessian determinant. For non‑smooth candidates, construct a quadratic polynomial that touches the graph and use the monotonicity of the determinant on the cone of positive semidefinite matrices. Remember that the comparison principle is the key tool for uniqueness; cite it explicitly.

The Lp Minkowski Problem and its Monge‑Ampère Formulation

The classical Minkowski problem prescribes the surface area measure of a convex body; its Lp extension, introduced by Lutwak, prescribes the Lp surface area measure Sp(K,·) = hK¹⁻ᵖ dSK, where hK is the support function and dSK the usual area measure. For p > 1 the problem asks: given a positive Borel measure μ on the sphere Sⁿ⁻¹ not concentrated on a great subsphere, find a convex body K such that Sp(K,·) = μ. This translates into the Monge‑Ampère type equation on the sphere: det(∇²h + hI) = h¹⁻ᵖ f, where h is the support function, ∇² the spherical Hessian, and f the density of μ. The equation is fully nonlinear and degenerate elliptic because the matrix ∇²h + hI must be positive semidefinite, which is exactly the convexity condition for the body. Existence for p > 1 was proved by Chou and Wang using the continuity method and a priori estimates that rely on the maximum principle and the Pogorelov interior estimate adapted to the spherical setting. Uniqueness holds up to translation when p ≠ n, while for p = n the problem is invariant under scaling and the solution is unique modulo homothety. The Lp Minkowski problem has deep connections with the Brunn‑Minkowski theory: the Lp Brunn‑Minkowski inequality, conjectured by Lutwak and proved for p > 1 by Böröczky, Lutwak, Yang, and Zhang, is equivalent to the uniqueness of solutions. The case p < 1 leads to non‑convex variational structures and remains an active research area. The PDE formulation also allows one to study regularity: if f is smooth and positive, the support function h is smooth, yielding a smooth strictly convex solution.

Monge‑Ampère Equations on Riemannian Manifolds and Prescribed Gauss Curvature

On a compact Riemannian manifold (M,g) of dimension n, the prescribed Gauss curvature problem asks for a conformal metric g̃ = e²ᵘ g whose Gauss curvature equals a given function K. In dimension two this reduces to the Liouville equation Δgu + Kg = K e²ᵘ, which is semilinear. In higher dimensions the fully nonlinear analogue involves the σk curvatures; for k = n the equation becomes the Monge‑Ampère type equation detg(∇²u + du ⊗ du − ½|∇u|²g + Ag) = K eⁿᵘ, where Ag is the Schouten tensor. This equation is elliptic precisely when the eigenvalues of the tensor lie in the positive cone Γn. The existence theory, developed by Viaclovsky, Guan, and others, uses the continuity method together with C⁰, C¹, and C² estimates. The C⁰ estimate follows from the maximum principle applied to an auxiliary function; the gradient estimate uses a clever integral identity and the Sobolev inequality; the second derivative estimate adapts the Pogorelov computation to the Riemannian setting, exploiting the concavity of log det on Γn. When the background metric has positive scalar curvature and K > 0, a solution exists and is unique up to the addition of a constant. The prescribed Gauss curvature problem on the sphere Sⁿ is exactly the Minkowski problem in geometric form, and the Monge‑Ampère equation on Sⁿ is the spherical Monge‑Ampère equation det(∇²h + hI) = f. The regularity theory on manifolds mirrors the Euclidean case: Caffarelli’s W²,ᵖ estimates and Evans‑Krylov theory extend to the Riemannian setting via local coordinate charts and the invariance of the equation under diffeomorphisms. This interplay between conformal geometry and fully nonlinear elliptic PDE has produced striking results such as the solution of the Yamabe problem for σk curvatures.

Numerical Approximation of Monge‑Ampère Equations: Wide Stencil Finite Difference Schemes

Computing solutions of the Monge‑Ampère equation is essential for applications in optimal transport, reflector design, and medical imaging. Standard finite difference schemes fail because the determinant of the discrete Hessian is not monotone, leading to non‑convergent or unstable iterations. The breakthrough came with the wide stencil monotone scheme of Oberman, Froese, and later Benamou, Froese, and Oberman. The idea is to approximate the second directional derivative D²u(x)[v,v] for a set of directions v that form a basis of ℝⁿ, using values of u at points x ± h v. The discrete Hessian is then assembled from these directional second differences, and the determinant is computed from the resulting matrix. Monotonicity is ensured by choosing the stencil directions so that the discrete operator is a non‑decreasing function of each nodal value. Consistency follows from Taylor expansion provided the stencil directions are sufficiently rich (at least n(n+1)/2 directions). The scheme converges to the viscosity solution by the Barles‑Souganidis framework: monotone, consistent, and stable schemes converge to the unique viscosity solution. For the Dirichlet problem, boundary values are imposed directly on grid points; for Neumann or periodic problems, ghost points are used. The computational cost is higher than narrow stencil methods because each grid point requires O(m) neighbours where m is the number of directions, but the guarantee of convergence to the correct weak solution outweighs the expense. Recent advances include Howard’s policy iteration (a semi‑smooth Newton method) for the discrete system, which yields superlinear convergence, and adaptive mesh refinement based on the Monge‑Ampère measure to capture singularities efficiently. These numerical methods have been successfully applied to compute optimal transport maps between densities, to design freeform lenses, and to simulate the semi‑geostrophic equations in atmospheric dynamics.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation in Optimal Transport and the Brenier Map

The quadratic optimal transport problem asks for a map T pushing a probability density f on a domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ onto a density g on Λ while minimizing the quadratic cost ∫|x−T(x)|² f(x) dx. Brenier’s theorem (1991) shows that the optimal map is the gradient of a convex function u, i.e. T = ∇u, and that u satisfies the Monge‑Ampère equation det D²u(x) = f(x) / g(∇u(x)) almost everywhere. The convexity of u is exactly the condition that the transport map be cyclically monotone, which is equivalent to optimality for the quadratic cost. When f and g are bounded away from zero and infinity on convex domains, Caffarelli’s regularity theory yields that u is strictly convex and of class C¹,α, and if the data are smooth then u is smooth up to the boundary. The same structure appears for more general costs c(x,y) satisfying the Ma‑Trudinger‑Wang (MTW) condition; the resulting equation is a generated Jacobian equation, a fully nonlinear elliptic PDE that reduces to the Monge‑Ampère form when c(x,y)=|x−y|²/2. The displacement interpolation μₜ = ((1−t)Id + t∇u)#f provides a geodesic in the Wasserstein space, and the Monge‑Ampère measure det D²u dx is precisely the push‑forward of f by ∇u. This connection underlies many applications: semi‑geostrophic equations in atmospheric dynamics, matching problems in economics, and image registration in medical imaging.

The Alexandrov‑Bakelman‑Pucci Estimate and its Role in Regularity

The Alexandrov‑Bakelman‑Pucci (ABP) estimate is a fundamental a priori bound for solutions of fully nonlinear uniformly elliptic equations. For a convex function u solving det D²u = f in a bounded domain Ω with u=0 on ∂Ω, the ABP estimate states that supΩ u ≤ C(n, diam Ω) (∫Γu fⁿ)1/n, where Γu is the contact set of the convex envelope of u. The proof uses the geometric interpretation of the Monge‑Ampère measure as the volume of the image of the subgradient mapping. The estimate is scale‑invariant and does not require any smoothness of u beyond convexity; it holds for Alexandrov solutions. Caffarelli exploited the ABP estimate to obtain a C¹,α regularity theorem: if f is bounded between positive constants λ and Λ, then any convex Alexandrov solution is locally C¹,α with an exponent α depending only on n, λ, Λ. The argument proceeds by a compactness‑contradiction method, rescaling the solution around a point where the gradient oscillation is large and using the ABP estimate to control the rescaled Monge‑Ampère measure. This C¹,α estimate is the cornerstone of the W²,p theory: once the gradient is Hölder continuous, the linearized operator has continuous coefficients, and the Evans‑Krylov theorem yields C²,α regularity for the linearized equation, which in turn gives W²,p estimates for the original Monge‑Ampère equation. The ABP estimate also plays a crucial role in the global regularity of the second boundary value problem, where the image domain ∇u(Ω) is prescribed.

Applying the ABP Estimate to Obtain a Priori Bounds
Let u be a convex Alexandrov solution of det D²u = f in the unit ball B₁ ⊂ ℝⁿ with u=0 on ∂B₁. Assume 0 < λ ≤ f(x) ≤ Λ for all x ∈ B₁. Use the ABP estimate to derive an explicit upper bound for supB₁ u in terms of λ, Λ, and n.
The contact set Γu is contained in B₁, so ∫Γu fⁿ ≤ ∫B₁ Λⁿ = Λⁿ |B₁|. The ABP estimate gives supB₁ u ≤ C(n) (Λⁿ |B₁|)1/n = C(n) Λ |B₁|1/n. Since |B₁| = πn/2/Γ(n/2+1), the bound is supB₁ u ≤ C(n) Λ (πn/2/Γ(n/2+1))1/n. The constant C(n) depends only on dimension; for the unit ball one can take C(n)=1/2 (by the classical ABP proof). Thus supB₁ u ≤ (1/2) Λ (πn/2/Γ(n/2+1))1/n. The lower bound λ does not appear in the upper estimate, but it is needed for the C¹,α regularity that follows from the ABP estimate.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for an a priori bound on a convex solution of a Monge‑Ampère equation, first check whether the right‑hand side is bounded below by a positive constant. The ABP estimate requires only an upper bound on f, but the subsequent C¹,α regularity (and any Evans‑Krylov argument) needs a positive lower bound. If the data can vanish, the ABP estimate still holds but the solution may fail to be strictly convex, and the gradient may not be Hölder continuous.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation on Domains with Oblique Boundary Conditions

The second boundary value problem for the Monge‑Ampère equation prescribes the image of the gradient mapping: ∇u(Ω) = Ω* for given convex domains Ω, Ω* ⊂ ℝⁿ. This is an oblique derivative condition because the outward normal to Ω* at ∇u(x) is not necessarily parallel to the normal of Ω at x. Caffarelli (1990) proved that if Ω and Ω* are uniformly convex with C¹,α boundaries and the right‑hand side f is bounded between positive constants, then the convex Alexandrov solution is strictly convex and belongs to C¹,α(Ω̅). The proof combines the ABP estimate with a boundary Harnack inequality for the linearized operator. The oblique condition is naturally encoded by the requirement that the subgradient mapping ∂u sends ∂Ω onto ∂Ω*. When the cost function is not quadratic, the optimal transport map satisfies a generated Jacobian equation det(D²u − A(x,∇u)) = f(x)/g(∇u) where A encodes the cost’s mixed Hessian. The same regularity theory extends provided the cost satisfies the MTW condition and the domains are c‑convex. Applications include the reflector antenna problem (where the target domain is the unit sphere) and the near‑field lens design problem, both of which lead to Monge‑Ampère type equations with oblique boundary conditions derived from Snell’s law or the law of reflection.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation in Geometric Optics: Reflector and Lens Design

In geometric optics one seeks a reflective or refractive surface that redirects a given source intensity f(x) on a domain Ω into a prescribed target intensity g(y) on a surface Σ (often the unit sphere for far‑field problems). Energy conservation yields a Monge‑Ampère type equation for the surface represented as a graph or a radial function. For a far‑field reflector, the surface is given by a radial function ρ(ξ) on the unit sphere Sⁿ⁻¹, and the mapping from incident direction ξ to reflected direction η is determined by the law of reflection. The Jacobian of this mapping leads to the equation det(D²ρ + ρ I) = f(ξ) / g(η(ξ)) · (ρ² + |∇ρ|²)(n+1)/2 / ρn+1, where D²ρ is the spherical Hessian. This equation is elliptic precisely when the reflector is convex (ρ I + D²ρ > 0). Oliker and Wang (2002) established existence and uniqueness of convex solutions for positive continuous data using the continuity method and a priori estimates that rely on the maximum principle and the ABP estimate adapted to the spherical setting. For near‑field problems the target is a bounded domain and the equation involves the Euclidean Hessian of a convex potential u with an oblique boundary condition ∇u(Ω) = Ω*. The same regularity theory applies, and numerical methods based on wide‑stencil monotone schemes have been used to compute freeform lenses for LED lighting and automotive headlamps. The key analytical difficulty is the degeneracy of the equation when the surface ceases to be strictly convex; the MTW condition for the cost generated by the optical mapping guarantees that the linearized operator remains uniformly elliptic as long as the data are positive.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Affine Plateau Problem

The affine Plateau problem asks for a hypersurface of affine maximal area spanning a given convex boundary. In the graph case, a function u defined on a convex domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ with u=0 on ∂Ω is an affine maximal hypersurface if it minimizes the affine area functional ∫Ω (det D²u)1/(n+2) dx. The Euler‑Lagrange equation is the affine maximal surface equation det D²u = (−u)-(n+2) (1+|∇u|²)(n+2)/2 for hyperbolic affine spheres, or det D²u = (1+|∇u|²)(n+2)/2 for parabolic affine spheres. Cheng and Yau (1986) proved that for any smooth uniformly convex boundary data there exists a unique smooth strictly convex solution of the hyperbolic affine sphere equation, which is asymptotic to the boundary of the convex cone over the domain. The proof uses the continuity method together with C⁰, C¹, and C² estimates; the C² estimate is a Pogorelov‑type computation adapted to the affine invariant setting. The affine maximal surface equation is a fully nonlinear elliptic Monge‑Ampère equation whose ellipticity cone is the set of positive definite matrices. The regularity theory mirrors that of the standard Monge‑Ampère equation: Caffarelli’s W²,p estimates and the Evans‑Krylov theorem apply because the operator is concave in the Hessian. The affine Plateau problem has deep connections with the Minkowski problem, the Blaschke‑Santaló inequality, and the theory of affine spheres in centro‑affine differential geometry. Recent work extends the existence theory to non‑smooth boundary data using the viscosity solution framework and the comparison principle for the affine maximal operator.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Ma‑Trudinger‑Wang Condition for General Cost Functions

In optimal transport with a general cost function c(x,y) the optimal map is characterized by a generated Jacobian equation of Monge‑Ampère type. If the cost satisfies the twist condition and the non‑degeneracy condition, the transport potential u solves

det(D²u − A(x, Du)) = B(x, Du) f(x) / g(∇u(x))

where A and B are derived from the cost. The ellipticity of this equation is not guaranteed by the convexity of u alone; it requires the Ma‑Trudinger‑Wang (MTW) condition, also called the cost‑sectional curvature condition. For a cost c ∈ C⁴, the MTW tensor is

𝔖ijkl = cij,kl − cij,r crs csk, l

and the condition (A3w) demands that for all ξ, η ∈ ℝⁿ with ξ·η = 0,

𝔖ijkl ξi ξj ηk ηl ≥ 0.

Ma, Trudinger and Wang (2005) proved that if (A3w) holds strictly (A3s) and the data f, g are positive and smooth, then the optimal potential is smooth. For the quadratic cost c(x,y) = ½|x−y|² the MTW tensor vanishes, so the condition is trivially satisfied and the equation reduces to the classical Monge‑Ampère equation det D²u = f/g(∇u). For the far‑field reflector cost c(x,y) = −log(1−x·y) on the sphere, the MTW tensor is positive definite, which explains the regularity results of Oliker and Wang (2002). Loeper (2009) showed that if (A3w) fails, one can construct smooth positive densities for which the optimal map is discontinuous. The MTW condition is therefore the sharp geometric criterion for regularity in optimal transport with non‑quadratic costs.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation on Non‑Compact Domains: Liouville Theorems and Entire Convex Solutions

Entire convex solutions of the Monge‑Ampère equation on ℝⁿ exhibit a striking rigidity. The classical Jörgens‑Calabi‑Pogorelov theorem states that any convex function u ∈ C⁴(ℝⁿ) satisfying det D²u = 1 must be a quadratic polynomial. Jörgens proved the case n = 2 in 1954, Calabi extended it to n = 3 in 1958, and Pogorelov completed the proof for all n ≥ 4 in 1964 using a clever maximum principle argument applied to the determinant of the Hessian. The theorem has been generalized in several directions. Caffarelli (1990) showed that if u is a convex Alexandrov solution of det D²u = f with f bounded between two positive constants, then u is strictly convex and its Hessian has eigenvalues bounded above and below. For the equation det D²u = eu on ℝ², Cheng and Yau (1986) proved that any entire convex solution must be of the form u(x) = log(8|x−x₀|²/(1+|x−x₀|²)²) up to an additive constant, which corresponds to the hyperbolic affine sphere. In higher dimensions, the classification of entire solutions to det D²u = eu remains open, but Liouville‑type theorems under growth conditions are known: if u is convex on ℝⁿ and det D²u ≥ 1, then u grows at least quadratically, and if u has sub‑quadratic growth it must be affine. These results are essential for the asymptotic analysis of solutions on unbounded domains and for the study of the affine Plateau problem where the domain is a convex cone.

Technique: Applying the Jörgens‑Calabi‑Pogorelov theorem
Show that any entire convex solution u ∈ C⁴(ℝ³) of det D²u = 1 is a quadratic polynomial.
Step 1. The equation det D²u = 1 implies the Hessian matrix D²u is positive definite everywhere because u is convex and the determinant is positive. Step 2. Pogorelov’s estimate for the Monge‑Ampère equation gives a bound on the third derivatives in terms of the second derivatives. In dimension three, one considers the function Φ = log det D²u + A|D²u|² and applies the maximum principle to show that the eigenvalues of D²u are constant. Step 3. Constant eigenvalues mean D²u is a constant positive definite matrix, so u is a quadratic polynomial. This completes the proof.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and Affine Spheres: Hyperbolic, Parabolic, and Elliptic Cases

Affine spheres are hypersurfaces in ℝⁿ⁺¹ whose affine normals meet at a point (elliptic), are parallel (parabolic), or diverge from a point (hyperbolic). When the hypersurface is represented as the graph of a convex function u on a convex domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ, the affine sphere condition translates into a Monge‑Ampère equation. For a hyperbolic affine sphere asymptotic to the boundary of a convex cone, Cheng and Yau (1986) proved that u satisfies

det D²u = (−u)−(n+2) (1 + |∇u|²)(n+2)/2, u < 0 in Ω, u = 0 on ∂Ω.

The parabolic affine sphere (affine maximal hypersurface) corresponds to the equation

det D²u = (1 + |∇u|²)(n+2)/2.

For elliptic affine spheres, which are compact and enclose the origin, Calabi (1972) showed that the defining equation is

det D²u = u−(n+2) (1 + |∇u|²)(n+2)/2, u > 0.

All three equations are fully nonlinear elliptic Monge‑Ampère equations whose ellipticity cone is the set of positive definite matrices. The operator F(M) = (det M)1/(n+2) is concave in M, so the Evans‑Krylov theorem yields C²,α interior regularity for any convex solution. Caffarelli’s W²,p estimates apply because the right‑hand side is a smooth function of u and ∇u. The hyperbolic case is intimately linked to the affine Plateau problem: the affine maximal surface equation is the Euler‑Lagrange equation of the affine area functional ∫Ω (det D²u)1/(n+2) dx. Cheng and Yau’s existence proof uses the continuity method together with a Pogorelov‑type C² estimate adapted to the affine invariant setting. The elliptic affine sphere equation is equivalent to the real Monge‑Ampère equation on the sphere after a Legendre transform, and its solutions correspond to centro‑affine hypersurfaces with constant affine mean curvature. Recent work extends the existence theory to non‑smooth boundary data using the viscosity solution framework and the comparison principle for the affine maximal operator.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Prescribed Jacobian Equation: Generated Jacobian Equations

Generated Jacobian equations (GJE) provide a unified framework for a wide class of Monge‑Ampère type equations arising in optimal transport, geometric optics, and semiconductor design. A GJE has the form

det(D²u − A(x, Du)) = B(x, Du) f(x) / g(Y(x, Du))

where the mapping Y(x, p) is generated by a smooth function G(x, y, z) via p = DxG(x, y, u(x)) and z = G(x, y, u(x)). The matrix A and the scalar B are derived from the second derivatives of G. The ellipticity condition for a GJE is exactly the MTW condition on the generating function G. Trudinger (2014) and subsequent works established a regularity theory: if the MTW condition (A3w) holds, the data f, g are positive and smooth, and the domains satisfy a uniform convexity condition with respect to the cost sections, then the solution u is C¹,α up to the boundary. For the near‑field reflector problem, the generating function is G(x, y, z) = −z log|x−y|, leading to the equation det(D²u + uI) = f(x)/g(∇u) · (|x|²+|∇u|²)(n+1)/2/|x|n+1. The same structure appears in freeform lens design where the target is a bounded domain and the cost is the optical path length. Numerical methods based on wide‑stencil monotone finite difference schemes have been developed to compute solutions of GJEs, and convergence follows from the Barles‑Souganidis framework once the scheme is monotone, stable, and consistent. The key analytical difficulty is the degeneracy when the generated mapping ceases to be a diffeomorphism; the MTW condition guarantees that the linearized operator remains uniformly elliptic as long as the data stay positive.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation in the Theory of Alexandrov Solutions and the Monge‑Ampère Measure

Alexandrov solutions provide a weak formulation of the Dirichlet problem for the Monge‑Ampère equation that is natural for convex functions. For a convex function u on a domain Ω, the Monge‑Ampère measure Mu is defined on Borel sets E ⊂ Ω by

Mu(E) = |∇u(E)|,

where ∇u(E) is the set of all subgradients of u at points of E and |·| denotes Lebesgue measure. A convex function u is an Alexandrov solution of det D²u = f with boundary data φ if u = φ on ∂Ω and Mu = f dx as measures. Alexandrov’s maximum principle states that for any convex u vanishing on ∂Ω,

supΩ (−u) ≤ Cn diam(Ω) (Mu(Ω))1/n.

This estimate yields existence of Alexandrov solutions for continuous boundary data and positive right‑hand side f ∈ L¹(Ω) via Perron’s method. The comparison principle holds: if u, v are convex and Mu ≥ Mv in Ω with u ≤ v on ∂Ω, then u ≤ v in Ω. Caffarelli, Nirenberg and Spruck (1984) showed that when f is continuous and positive, Alexandrov solutions coincide with viscosity solutions, and the solution is strictly convex and smooth in the interior. The measure‑theoretic formulation is essential for stability: if fk → f weakly as measures, the corresponding Alexandrov solutions converge uniformly. This framework also underpins the regularity theory for the second boundary value problem, where the image of the gradient map is prescribed.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Affine Isoperimetric Inequality

The affine isoperimetric inequality, proved by Lutwak in 1993, states that among all convex bodies of given volume the ellipsoid maximises the affine surface area. The affine surface area can be expressed as an integral of the Gaussian curvature raised to the power one half over the boundary, which in turn is the Monge‑Ampère measure of the support function. If a convex body is represented by a convex function u on the unit sphere via its support function h(u)=u, the affine surface area becomes ∫Sn-1 (det D²u)1/(n+1) dσ. The inequality then reads (∫Sn-1 (det D²u)1/(n+1) dσ)n+1 ≤ C_n Vol(K) where C_n is a dimensional constant attained only for ellipsoids. This formulation places the Monge‑Ampère operator at the centre of affine differential geometry. The proof uses the Blaschke‑Santaló inequality and the fact that the Legendre transform of u corresponds to the polar body. Equality cases are characterised by the condition that D²u is a constant multiple of the identity, i.e. u is a quadratic polynomial. The inequality has been extended to the Lp affine isoperimetric inequalities by Lutwak, Yang and Zhang (2000), where the exponent 1/(n+1) is replaced by p/(n+p). These extensions lead to Monge‑Ampère type equations of the form det D²u = f (u) |∇u|p-n which appear in the study of p‑affine surface area and the corresponding Minkowski problems. The affine isoperimetric inequality also provides a functional version of the Blaschke‑Santaló inequality, linking the Monge‑Ampère measure of a convex function to that of its Legendre transform. This duality is a key tool in the regularity theory for the Monge‑Ampère equation with right‑hand side given by a power of the solution itself.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Regularity of Optimal Transport Maps for Non‑Quadratic Costs

For a general cost function c(x,y) the optimal transport map between two densities f and g is given by y = T(x) = exp_x(∇u(x)) where u solves a generated Jacobian equation of Monge‑Ampère type. The regularity of T is governed by the Ma‑Trudinger‑Wang (MTW) tensor, which measures the cost‑sectional curvature of the cost function. Loeper (2009) showed that if the MTW condition (A3w) holds and the domains are c‑convex, then any optimal transport map is locally Hölder continuous. Figalli, Kim and McCann (2010) proved that under the stronger MTW condition (A3s) the map is actually C¹,α up to the boundary provided the densities are bounded away from zero and infinity. The equation satisfied by the potential u is det(D²u - A(x,∇u)) = B(x,∇u) f(x)/g(∇u) where A and B are derived from the second derivatives of the cost. When c(x,y)=|x-y|²/2 the matrix A vanishes and B=1, recovering the classical Monge‑Ampère equation. For the reflector antenna cost c(x,y) = -log|x-y| the MTW tensor is positive, guaranteeing regularity of the reflector map. In contrast, for the cost c(x,y)=|x-y|^p with p≠2 the MTW tensor changes sign, and regularity can fail; counterexamples by Loeper show that the transport map may develop discontinuities. The regularity theory therefore hinges on verifying the MTW condition for the specific cost, which is a fully nonlinear condition on the fourth derivatives of c. Recent work by Guillen and Kitagawa (2018) extends the theory to costs satisfying a weakened MTW condition on a subset of the domain, allowing partial regularity results. These developments illustrate how the geometry of the cost function, encoded in the MTW tensor, dictates the ellipticity and regularity of the associated Monge‑Ampère type equation.

Worked Example: Verifying the MTW Condition for the Quadratic Cost
Show that the quadratic cost c(x,y)=½|x-y|² satisfies the MTW condition (A3w) with equality.
For c(x,y)=½|x-y|² we have D_x c = x-y and D_y c = y-x. The mixed Hessian Dxyc = -I. The MTW tensor is defined as Sijkl = cij,kl - cij,r crs cs,kl where indices i,j refer to x‑derivatives and k,l to y‑derivatives. Since c is quadratic, all third and fourth derivatives vanish, so cij,kl=0. The inverse of the mixed Hessian is crs = -δrs. The term cij,r crs cs,kl also vanishes because third derivatives are zero. Hence Sijkl=0 for all indices, which means the MTW condition holds with equality (the cost‑sectional curvature is identically zero). This is the borderline case where the generated Jacobian equation reduces to the classical Monge‑Ampère equation det D²u = f/g(∇u).

Remember that A3w gives Hölder continuity of the map, while A3s yields C¹,α up to the boundary under c‑convexity. The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Viscosity Solutions on Non‑Convex Domains

The classical theory of viscosity solutions for fully nonlinear elliptic equations, developed by Crandall, Ishii and Lions (1992), assumes the domain is arbitrary but the equation is uniformly elliptic. For the Monge‑Ampère equation det D²u = f the ellipticity degenerates when the Hessian loses rank, which can happen on non‑convex domains even for convex solutions. Ishii and Lions (1990) introduced the notion of convex viscosity solutions, requiring the test functions to be convex, which restores the comparison principle on any domain provided the boundary data are convex. Later, Caffarelli, Nirenberg and Spruck (1984) proved that for the Dirichlet problem with continuous boundary data on a strictly convex domain, the unique viscosity solution is convex and coincides with the Alexandrov solution. On non‑convex domains the situation is more delicate: the solution may fail to be convex, and the Monge‑Ampère measure may not be well defined as a positive measure. However, if one imposes the additional condition that the solution is admissible (i.e. its Hessian has non‑negative eigenvalues in the viscosity sense), the comparison principle still holds. This admissibility condition is equivalent to requiring that the solution be a viscosity subsolution of the equation det D²u ≤ f and a supersolution of det D²u ≥ f simultaneously. Recent work by Figalli and Shahgholian (2015) shows that for the obstacle problem associated with the Monge‑Ampère equation, the free boundary is C¹,α provided the obstacle is strictly convex and the right‑hand side is positive. The proof uses a monotonicity formula of Almgren type adapted to the Monge‑Ampère operator. These results demonstrate that the viscosity framework can be extended beyond convex domains, but the geometry of the domain and the sign of the right‑hand side play a crucial role in maintaining ellipticity and regularity.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Geometry of Hessian Manifolds

A Hessian manifold is a manifold equipped with a flat torsion‑free connection ∇ and a Riemannian metric g such that locally g = D²u for some convex potential u. The Monge‑Ampère equation appears naturally as the condition that the volume form of g equals a prescribed function times the Lebesgue measure: det D²u = f. In affine differential geometry, the Blaschke metric of a hypersurface is a Hessian metric, and the affine normal flow leads to a parabolic Monge‑Ampère equation. Cheng and Yau (1977) used the Monge‑Ampère equation to construct complete affine hyperspheres, showing that the existence of an elliptic affine sphere is equivalent to solving det D²u = (-u)-(n+2) on a convex domain with u=0 on the boundary. The regularity of the solution is governed by the affine curvature, which is constant for affine spheres. In the context of Kähler geometry, the complex Monge‑Ampère equation (ω + i∂∂̄φ)^n = e^F ω^n on a compact Kähler manifold is the real Monge‑Ampère equation for the Kähler potential φ in local coordinates. Yau’s solution of the Calabi conjecture (1978) proves existence of a unique solution for any smooth F with ∫ e^F ω^n = ∫ ω^n. The real analogue on Hessian manifolds is the prescribed affine curvature problem: given a positive function K, find a convex function u such that det D²u = K (det D²u)(n+2)/(n+1). This reduces to det D²u = Kn+1 after a change of variables. The regularity theory for this equation on compact Hessian manifolds was developed by Trudinger and Wang (2005), who established C²,α estimates under a convexity assumption on the domain. These geometric interpretations show that the Monge‑Ampère equation is not merely a PDE but a bridge between affine differential geometry, complex geometry, and the theory of fully nonlinear elliptic equations.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Degenerate Elliptic Case: Boundary Regularity for the Dirichlet Problem

When the right‑hand side f vanishes on part of the boundary, the Monge‑Ampère equation becomes degenerate elliptic. Caffarelli (1990) proved that if f is Hölder continuous and strictly positive in the interior, the solution u is C¹,α up to the boundary provided the domain is strictly convex and the boundary data are C¹,¹. The proof uses a boundary version of the Alexandrov‑Bakelman‑Pucci estimate and a careful analysis of the sections of u near the boundary. If f vanishes like a power of the distance to the boundary, say f(x) ≈ dist(x,∂Ω)^β with β>0, the solution may lose C¹ regularity; instead one obtains a boundary Hölder exponent depending on β. Savin (2007) showed that for the model equation det D²u = x_n^β in the half‑space {x_n>0} with zero boundary data, the solution is C¹,α with α = 1/(β+2) and this exponent is optimal. In the fully degenerate case f≡0, the equation reduces to det D²u = 0, whose convex solutions are exactly the functions whose graph is a developable surface; they are not strictly convex and the Monge‑Ampère measure vanishes. The boundary regularity theory for degenerate equations is essential for applications in optimal transport where the target density may have compact support, leading to a free boundary problem. Figalli and Kim (2010) studied the regularity of the free boundary in the optimal transport problem with quadratic cost when the target density vanishes on a smooth hypersurface; they proved the free boundary is C¹,α under a non‑degeneracy condition on the source density. These results illustrate how the degeneracy of the right‑hand side influences both interior and boundary regularity, and how the geometry of the domain interacts with the degeneracy to determine the optimal Hölder exponent.

  • Applying the standard viscosity comparison principle on non‑convex domains without imposing admissibility; the principle may fail for non‑admissible functions.
  • Treating the complex Monge‑Ampère equation as identical to the real one; the complex equation involves (1,1)-forms and requires the Kähler condition, which has no analogue in the real setting.

  • Assuming the free boundary in degenerate optimal transport is always smooth; it can develop corners if the source density degenerates faster than the target density.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Weak Solutions: Alexandrov, Viscosity, and Brenier Solutions

The classical Monge‑Ampère equation det D²u = f presumes twice differentiability of u. In many geometric and transport problems the right‑hand side f is merely a measure or the solution is only convex. Three complementary weak notions have become standard: Alexandrov solutions, viscosity solutions, and Brenier (optimal transport) solutions. Alexandrov (1938) defined a solution by requiring that the Monge‑Ampère measure μ_u(E) = |∂u(E)| equals ∫_E f for every Borel set E, where ∂u is the subgradient mapping. This measure‑theoretic formulation is stable under uniform limits of convex functions and yields existence for the Dirichlet problem with continuous boundary data and non‑negative f ∈ L¹. Viscosity solutions, introduced by Crandall‑Lions (1983) for fully nonlinear equations, adapt to the Monge‑Ampère operator by testing with smooth functions touching the graph from above or below. The comparison principle holds for convex viscosity solutions on convex domains, and uniqueness follows. Brenier (1991) showed that the optimal transport map for quadratic cost is the gradient of a convex function u solving det D²u = f/g(∇u) in the sense of measures. The three notions coincide when f is continuous and strictly positive: any convex Alexandrov solution is a viscosity solution, and its gradient pushes the source density to the target density. The equivalence breaks down if f vanishes or changes sign; then one must specify which weak framework is used. Caffarelli’s regularity theory (1990‑1996) proves that if f is bounded away from zero and infinity and the domain is strictly convex, any Alexandrov solution is strictly convex and C¹,α, and if f is Hölder continuous the solution is C²,α. For merely measurable f, solutions remain Alexandrov but may fail to be C¹. In optimal transport, the Brenier map is the unique monotone transport plan; its regularity is exactly the regularity of the corresponding Monge‑Ampère solution. This triad of weak solutions underpins modern applications from geometric optics to metric geometry.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Prescribed Gauss Curvature Problem on Surfaces

On a smooth convex surface in ℝ³ the Gauss curvature K satisfies det D²u = K(1+|∇u|²)² for a graph representation u. The prescribed Gauss curvature problem asks: given a positive function K on a domain Ω ⊂ ℝ², find a convex function u such that det D²u = K(1+|∇u|²)² in Ω with u = φ on ∂Ω. This is a fully nonlinear elliptic equation of Monge‑Ampère type. The classical Minkowski problem (prescribed Gauss curvature on the sphere) corresponds to K defined on S² and u defined on the sphere via support functions. Pogorelov (1952) solved the Minkowski problem for smooth positive K using a priori estimates and the continuity method. For general domains, Caffarelli‑Nirenberg‑Spruck (1984) established existence of smooth strictly convex solutions when K is smooth, positive, and satisfies a compatibility condition ∫_Ω K⁻¹/² < ∞. The key a priori estimate is a global C² bound derived from the maximum principle applied to the quantity log λ_max + A|∇u|², where λ_max is the largest eigenvalue of D²u. If K vanishes on part of the boundary, the equation becomes degenerate elliptic; Caffarelli (1990) proved C¹,α regularity up to the boundary under a strict convexity assumption on Ω and a Hölder condition on K. The prescribed Gauss curvature equation also appears in the design of reflectors and lenses: the surface normal mapping is the gradient of u, and the intensity distribution is encoded in K. In geometric optics, the far‑field pattern leads to a Monge‑Ampère equation with a right‑hand side that may be discontinuous; viscosity solutions provide the correct framework. The problem is intimately linked to the Monge‑Ampère measure: the push‑forward of the Lebesgue measure by ∇u equals K(1+|∇u|²)² dx. This measure‑theoretic view allows one to treat weak data and to study stability under perturbations of K.

Worked Example: Prescribed Gauss Curvature on a Disk

The function K is smooth and positive in Ω, and ∫_Ω K⁻¹/² dx = ∫_Ω √(1−|x|²) dx < ∞. By the Caffarelli‑Nirenberg‑Spruck theorem, the Dirichlet problem admits a unique strictly convex solution u ∈ C²,α(Ω) for any α∈(0,1). The boundary data are zero, which is C²,α on ∂Ω. The compatibility condition holds because K⁻¹/² is integrable. The maximum principle applied to the auxiliary function w = log λ_max + A|∇u|² yields a global C² bound independent of the approximation parameter in the continuity method. Hence the solution extends continuously to the boundary with u = 0. Uniqueness follows from the comparison principle for convex viscosity solutions. Therefore a unique strictly convex solution exists in C²(Ω) ∩ C⁰(Ω̅).

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Complex Hessian Equations: k‑Hessian and (n−1)‑Hessian

The complex Monge‑Ampère equation (ω + i∂∂̄φ)ⁿ = e^F ωⁿ is the top‑degree case of a family of fully nonlinear equations involving the k‑th elementary symmetric polynomial of the eigenvalues of the complex Hessian. For 1 ≤ k ≤ n, the k‑Hessian equation σ_k(λ(∂∂̄φ)) = ψ defines a degenerate elliptic operator on the Gårding cone Γ_k = {λ : σ_j(λ) > 0 for j = 1,…,k}. When k = n this is the complex Monge‑Ampère equation; when k = 1 it reduces to the Laplace equation. The Dirichlet problem for the k‑Hessian equation on a bounded domain in ℂⁿ with smooth boundary data was solved by Caffarelli‑Nirenberg‑Spruck (1985) for k = n and by Trudinger‑Wang (1995) for general k under the assumption that the domain is strictly (k‑1)‑convex. The a priori C² estimate uses the maximum principle on the quantity log λ_max + A|∇φ|², where λ_max is the largest eigenvalue of the complex Hessian. For the (n−1)‑Hessian equation σn−1(λ) = ψ, the equation is the complex analogue of the prescribed scalar curvature problem. On compact Kähler manifolds, the existence of solutions to σ_k(λ(ω_φ)) = c σ_k(λ(ω)) with ω_φ ∈ Γ_k was established by Dinew‑Kołodziej (2014) using a continuity method and capacity estimates. The complex Hessian equations also appear in the study of special Lagrangian submanifolds and in the Donaldson‑Uhlenbeck‑Yau correspondence for Hermitian‑Yang‑Mills metrics. A crucial difference from the real case is the requirement that the form ω_φ remains in the Gårding cone, which is a nonlinear convexity condition. The viscosity theory for complex Hessian equations was developed by Eyssidieux‑Guedj‑Zeriahi (2011), showing that the comparison principle holds for bounded Γ_k‑subharmonic functions. This allows one to treat degenerate right‑hand sides ψ ∈ L^p, p > 1, and to define weak solutions via the Bedford‑Taylor Monge‑Ampère measure for the full Hessian.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Geometry of the Space of Convex Functions: Wasserstein Geometry

The set of convex functions on a convex domain Ω modulo additive constants can be identified with the space of probability measures on Ω via the Brenier map. Otto (2001) introduced a formal Riemannian structure on the Wasserstein space P₂(Ω) where the tangent space at a measure μ = ∇u#ν is the space of gradients of functions in L²(μ). The Monge‑Ampère equation appears as the geodesic equation in this geometry: a curve μ_t = (∇u_t)#ν is a constant‑speed geodesic iff u_t solves the homogeneous Monge‑Ampère equation det D²u_t = 0 in the sense of measures, with u_t linear in t. More generally, the displacement convexity of functionals such as the entropy ∫ ρ log ρ is equivalent to the convexity of the corresponding functional on the space of convex functions. McCann (1997) proved that the entropy is displacement convex, which translates into the inequality det D²u ≥ (det D²u₀)1−t (det D²u₁)^t for the interpolating convex functions. This is a nonlinear Brunn‑Minkowski inequality for the Monge‑Ampère measure. The curvature of the Wasserstein space is non‑negative in the sense of Alexandrov, and the Monge‑Ampère equation governs the Jacobi fields along geodesics. In optimal transport, the regularity of the transport map is exactly the regularity of the solution to the Monge‑Ampère equation with right‑hand side given by the ratio of densities. Caffarelli’s contraction theorem (2000) states that if the target density is log‑concave, the optimal map is a contraction in the Wasserstein distance, which follows from the convexity of the potential u. The Wasserstein geometry also provides a variational formulation: the Monge‑Ampère equation is the Euler‑Lagrange equation of the functional ∫ (½|∇u|² − u f) dx under the constraint that ∇u pushes forward the Lebesgue measure to f dx. This perspective unifies the PDE, the transport, and the geometric viewpoints.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Boundary Behavior of Solutions: Global C¹,α Estimates

Global boundary regularity for the Monge‑Ampère equation is more delicate than interior regularity because the boundary condition couples the normal derivative to the geometry of the domain. Caffarelli (1990) proved that if Ω is uniformly convex with C¹,¹ boundary, f ∈ C^α(Ω̅) with 0 < λ ≤ f ≤ Λ, and the boundary data φ ∈ C¹,¹(∂Ω), then the unique convex Alexandrov solution u belongs to C¹,α(Ω̅). The proof uses a boundary version of the Alexandrov‑Bakelman‑Pucci estimate applied to the sections S_h(x₀) = {x : u(x) < u(x₀) + ∇u(x₀)·(x−x₀) + h}. Near the boundary these sections are comparable to half‑balls after an affine normalization that flattens the boundary. The key step is a boundary Harnack inequality for the linearized Monge‑Ampère operator L_u v = trace(adj(D²u) D²v). If f vanishes on a portion of the boundary, the equation becomes degenerate and the optimal Hölder exponent drops. Savin (2007) analyzed the model det D²u = x_n^β in the half‑space {x_n > 0} with zero boundary data and showed that u ∈ C¹,α with α = 1/(β+2), and this exponent is sharp. For the fully degenerate case f ≡ 0, the solutions are developable surfaces and are not strictly convex; the Monge‑Ampère measure vanishes identically. In optimal transport with target density vanishing on a smooth hypersurface, Figalli‑Kim (2010) proved that the free boundary is C¹,α provided the source density satisfies a non‑degeneracy condition. The boundary regularity theory also extends to oblique boundary conditions arising in reflector design, where the gradient mapping is constrained to a prescribed set. The global C¹,α estimate is the foundation for higher regularity: if f ∈ C^α and the boundary is C²,α, then u ∈ C²,α(Ω̅) by the Evans‑Krylov theorem applied up to the boundary.

  • Confusing the different weak solution concepts: Alexandrov solutions are measure‑theoretic, viscosity solutions rely on test functions, and Brenier solutions come from optimal transport; they coincide only under strict positivity and continuity of the right‑hand side.
  • Assuming the prescribed Gauss curvature equation is uniformly elliptic for any positive K; ellipticity requires the gradient term (1+|∇u|²)² and the convexity of u, and degeneracy occurs if K vanishes.
  • Treating the complex k‑Hessian equation as uniformly elliptic for all k; it is only elliptic on the Gårding cone Γ_k, and the cone condition must be verified for any candidate solution.
  • Believing that displacement convexity of the entropy holds for any cost function; it is specific to the quadratic cost and the associated Monge‑Ampère geometry.
  • Overlooking the necessity of uniform convexity of the domain for global C¹,α estimates; on merely convex domains the boundary Hölder exponent may fail.
  • Applying the standard Evans‑Krylov theorem up to the boundary without a C²,α boundary; the theorem requires the boundary to be C²,α and the solution to be C¹,α up to the boundary.
  • Assuming the free boundary in degenerate optimal transport is always smooth; corners can form if the source density degenerates faster than the target density.

The Parabolic Monge‑Ampère Equation and its Viscosity Solution Theory

The parabolic Monge‑Ampère equation takes the form ∂tu = det D2u − f(x,t) in a space‑time cylinder Ω×(0,T), where Ω⊂ℝn is a bounded convex domain and f is a given non‑negative function. A function u is a viscosity subsolution if for every smooth test function φ touching u from above at (x0,t0) one has ∂tφ(x0,t0) ≤ det D2φ(x0,t0) − f(x0,t0); supersolutions are defined analogously with the opposite inequality. The comparison principle, proved by Caffarelli (1990) and refined by Wang (1992), states that if u is a subsolution and v a supersolution with u ≤ v on the parabolic boundary ∂p(Ω×(0,T)), then u ≤ v in the whole cylinder. This principle requires f to be continuous and the domain to be uniformly parabolic, i.e. the eigenvalues of D2u stay within a fixed positive cone. Existence follows from the Perron method: the supremum of all subsolutions bounded above by a given supersolution yields a viscosity solution. Regularity theory shows that if f ∈ Cα and the initial data are convex, then the solution is C1,α in space‑time (Caffarelli 1990) and, under additional convexity of the spatial domain, C2,α in space (Wang 1992). The parabolic equation appears naturally in the Kähler‑Ricci flow, where the evolving Kähler potential satisfies a complex parabolic Monge‑Ampère equation, and in the study of geometric flows of convex hypersurfaces.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation on Metric Measure Spaces and Synthetic Ricci Curvature

Lott, Villani and Sturm (2006‑2009) introduced a synthetic notion of Ricci curvature lower bounds for metric measure spaces (X,d,m) using optimal transport. A space satisfies the curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,N) if the relative entropy functional Entm(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dm is K‑displacement convex along Wasserstein geodesics, where μ = ρ m. In smooth Riemannian manifolds this condition is equivalent to Ric ≥ K and dim ≤ N. The Monge‑Ampère equation appears as the Jacobian equation for the optimal transport map T = ∇φ pushing forward the reference measure m to a target measure ν = f m: det D2φ = f / (det D T) in a suitable weak sense. On a general metric measure space the “Monge‑Ampère measure” is defined via the push‑forward of the reference measure by the optimal map, and the CD(K,N) condition translates into a Brunn‑Minkowski type inequality for this measure. Sturm (2006) proved that CD(K,N) is stable under measured Gromov‑Hausdorff convergence, and Lott‑Villani (2009) showed that it implies a sharp Bishop‑Gromov volume comparison. Recent work by Ambrosio, Gigli and Savaré (2014) constructed a heat flow on CD(K,∞) spaces and identified its gradient flow structure with the entropy, providing a fully nonlinear Monge‑Ampère framework on non‑smooth spaces.

Technique
Explain why the comparison principle for viscosity solutions of the parabolic Monge‑Ampère equation requires the right‑hand side f to be continuous and the domain to be uniformly parabolic.
The comparison principle is proved by doubling variables and using the maximum principle for semicontinuous functions. Continuity of f guarantees that the perturbed test functions converge to the same limit, so the inequality ∂tφ ≤ det D2φ − f passes to the limit without an extra error term. Uniform parabolicity (the eigenvalues of D2u staying in a fixed cone) ensures that the determinant is a uniformly elliptic operator on the set of test functions; without it the equation could degenerate and the standard Crandall‑Ishii lemma would not apply. If either hypothesis fails, counterexamples exist where a subsolution exceeds a supersolution inside the domain.

For existence recall the Perron method; for uniqueness cite the Caffarelli‑Wang comparison principle; for regularity quote the C1,α estimate (Caffarelli 1990) and the C2,α result under convex spatial domains (Wang 1992). Keep the hypotheses (continuous f, uniform parabolicity, convex initial data) explicit in your answer.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation with Obstacle: Free Boundary Regularity

The obstacle problem for the Monge‑Ampère equation seeks a convex function u ≥ ψ in a convex domain Ω such that det D2u = f in the non‑coincidence set {u > ψ} and det D2u ≥ f in Ω, with u = ψ on the contact set {u = ψ}. Here ψ is a given convex obstacle and f > 0 is a prescribed right‑hand side. Caffarelli (1998) proved that if f is bounded away from zero and infinity and ψ is C1,1, then the free boundary ∂{u > ψ} is locally a C1,α hypersurface. The proof uses a blow‑up analysis and the classification of global solutions: any global convex solution of det D2u = 1 that coincides with a quadratic polynomial on a half‑space must be a quadratic polynomial itself. Figalli (2010) extended the regularity to the case where f may vanish on a smooth hypersurface, showing that the free boundary remains C1,α provided a non‑degeneracy condition on the source density holds. This theory is directly relevant to optimal transport with density constraints, where the obstacle encodes a capacity limit on the target region.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and Nonlocal Operators: Fractional Monge‑Ampère Equations

A fractional Monge‑Ampère operator Ms (0 < s < 1) is a fully nonlinear nonlocal operator that converges to the classical determinant as s → 1. One typical definition uses the Caffarelli‑Silvestre extension: for a convex function u, Msu(x) = Cn,s ∫ℝn (u(x) − u(y) − ∇u(x)·(x−y))+ / |x−y|n+2s dy, where (·)+ denotes the positive part. Viscosity solutions for such equations were developed by Barles and Imbert (2008) and later refined by Caffarelli, Silvestre and Soria (2011). They proved a comparison principle under the structural assumption that the kernel is symmetric and comparable to |x−y|−n−2s. Interior C1,α regularity holds for bounded right‑hand sides, and C2,α regularity follows from a nonlocal Evans‑Krylov theorem when the kernel satisfies a uniform ellipticity condition. These results have applications in nonlocal optimal transport, where the cost function is a fractional power of the Euclidean distance, and in the study of free boundary problems with long‑range interactions.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Affine Maximal Hypersurface Problem

An affine maximal hypersurface in ℝn+1 is a graph of a convex function u satisfying the Euler‑Lagrange equation of the affine area functional: det D2u = (1 + |∇u|2)(n+2)/2. Calabi (1972) conjectured that the only complete affine maximal hypersurfaces are elliptic paraboloids. Cheng and Yau (1986) proved this conjecture by establishing a Pogorelov‑type estimate for entire convex solutions and showing that any such solution must be a quadratic polynomial. Later, Trudinger and Wang (2002) obtained global C2,α estimates for the Dirichlet problem on bounded uniformly convex domains with smooth boundary data. The affine maximal equation is a special case of the prescribed affine mean curvature equation, and its study has deep connections with the theory of affine spheres: the hyperbolic affine sphere equation det D2u = (−u)−n−2 and the elliptic affine sphere equation det D2u = (1+|∇u|2)(n+2)/2 share the same Monge‑Ampère structure but differ in the right‑hand side. The classification of entire solutions remains an active area, with recent progress by Jian, Wang and others on the asymptotic behavior of solutions with prescribed asymptotic cones.

  • Treating the fractional Monge‑Ampère operator as a simple power of the Laplacian; it is a fully nonlinear nonlocal operator with distinct scaling and kernel requirements.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Affine Bernstein Problem: Classification of Entire Convex Solutions

The affine Bernstein problem asks whether every entire convex solution of the Monge‑Ampère equation det D2u = 1 in ℝn must be a quadratic polynomial. Calabi conjectured in 1972 that the only complete affine maximal hypersurfaces are elliptic paraboloids, which translates into the statement that any entire convex solution with quadratic growth is a paraboloid. Cheng and Yau (1986) proved the conjecture by establishing a Pogorelov‑type interior estimate for solutions of det D2u = f with f bounded between positive constants, and then showing that any entire convex solution of det D2u = 1 has at most quadratic growth. Their argument uses the Legendre transform to convert the equation into a uniformly elliptic linear equation for the conjugate function, followed by a Liouville theorem for linear elliptic equations. Later, Trudinger and Wang (2002) obtained global C2,α estimates for the Dirichlet problem on bounded uniformly convex domains with smooth boundary data, which in turn yields a classification of entire solutions with prescribed asymptotic cones. Jian, Wang and others (2010‑2015) extended the classification to the case where the right‑hand side is a power of the solution, det D2u = u−p, showing that the only entire convex solutions are again quadratic polynomials when p lies in a certain range. The affine Bernstein problem remains open for non‑complete solutions; incomplete entire convex solutions can exhibit different asymptotic behaviour, such as the existence of non‑paraboloid solutions with prescribed asymptotic cones constructed by Bao, Li and Zhang (2018). The problem is intimately linked with the theory of affine spheres: the hyperbolic affine sphere equation det D2u = (−u)−n−2 and the elliptic affine sphere equation det D2u = (1+|∇u|2)(n+2)/2 share the same Monge‑Ampère structure but differ in the right‑hand side, and their entire solutions have been classified under various completeness assumptions.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Prescribed Gauss Curvature Problem on Compact Manifolds with Boundary

On a compact Riemannian manifold (M,g) with boundary ∂M, prescribing the Gauss curvature of a conformal metric gu = e2ug leads to a fully nonlinear elliptic equation of Monge‑Ampère type. In local coordinates the equation reads det(D2u + A(x,∇u)) = f(x)e2u, where A depends on the background metric and f is the prescribed curvature function. The boundary condition is usually taken as a Neumann condition ∂u/∂ν = φ or a Dirichlet condition u = ψ on ∂M. Guan and Li (1995) proved existence and uniqueness of smooth solutions when f > 0 and the boundary data satisfy a convexity condition that makes the domain uniformly convex with respect to the equation. Their proof relies on a priori C2,α estimates up to the boundary, obtained by adapting the Evans‑Krylov theorem to the boundary via a flattening argument and a careful analysis of the oblique derivative problem. Later, Bo Guan, Joel Spruck and others (2000‑2005) extended the regularity theory to the case where f may vanish on a subset of the boundary, provided a non‑degeneracy condition holds. The prescribed Gauss curvature problem on surfaces with boundary is a classical topic: the equation reduces to Δu + Kg = f e2u with Neumann data, and the existence theory was completed by Chang, Gursky and Yang (1995) using the method of continuity and a Moser‑Trudinger inequality. In higher dimensions the problem is more delicate because the equation is fully nonlinear; the key structural condition is the uniform ellipticity of the linearised operator, which is guaranteed when the solution is admissible (i.e. the eigenvalues of the Schouten tensor lie in the positive cone). Recent work by Chen, Li and Zhang (2021) established global C3,α estimates for the Neumann problem under the assumption that the boundary is strictly convex with respect to the admissible cone, opening the way for higher regularity and perturbation results.

Technique: Boundary C2,α Estimate for the Prescribed Gauss Curvature Problem

Apply the Evans‑Krylov theorem in the interior to obtain C2,α estimates away from the boundary. 3. Near the boundary, use a barrier function constructed from the defining function of the domain to control second derivatives in the normal direction. 4. This is the standard boundary regularity theorem of Guan‑Li (1995).

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Stability of Alexandrov Solutions under Weak Convergence of Measures

Alexandrov solutions of the Monge‑Ampère equation are defined by the requirement that the Monge‑Ampère measure Mu(E) = |∇u(E)| equals a prescribed Borel measure μ for every Borel set E. A fundamental stability theorem, due to Caffarelli (1990) and later refined by Gutierrez and Huang (1999), states that if a sequence of convex functions uk solves Muk = μk in a convex domain Ω with uniformly bounded boundary data, and the measures μk converge weakly to a measure μ that is bounded away from zero and infinity, then a subsequence of uk converges locally uniformly to a convex function u solving Mu = μ. The proof uses the Alexandrov‑Bakelman‑Pucci (ABP) estimate to obtain uniform L∞ bounds, the strict convexity of solutions (Caffarelli's strict convexity theorem) to upgrade weak convergence to locally uniform convergence, and the continuity of the Monge‑Ampère operator with respect to weak convergence of measures. Figalli, Kim and McCann (2011) extended the stability result to the case where the limiting measure may have a singular part, showing that the limit solution is still an Alexandrov solution provided the singular part does not charge the boundary. This stability theory is crucial for the existence of optimal transport maps: the Brenier map is obtained as the limit of solutions to regularised Monge‑Ampère equations, and the stability theorem guarantees that the limit satisfies the Monge‑Ampère equation in the Alexandrov sense. Recent work by De Philippis and Figalli (2014) established quantitative stability estimates in the Wasserstein distance, linking the Monge‑Ampère stability to the geometry of the space of probability measures.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Complex Monge‑Ampère Equation on Singular Kähler Spaces

The complex Monge‑Ampère equation (ω + i∂∂̄φ)n = f ωn on a compact Kähler manifold (X,ω) has been extensively studied in the smooth setting by Yau (1978) and later by Kolodziej (1998) for Lp right‑hand sides. On singular Kähler spaces, the equation must be formulated in the language of pluripotential theory. Eyssidieux, Guedj and Zeriahi (2009) developed a theory of non‑pluripolar products of positive closed currents, allowing one to define the Monge‑Ampère measure MA(φ) = (ω + i∂∂̄φ)n for bounded quasi‑plurisubharmonic functions φ. They proved existence and uniqueness of solutions with prescribed singularities when f ∈ Lp, p > 1, and the cohomology class is big. Berman, Boucksom, Guedj and Zeriahi (2010‑2015) extended the variational approach, introducing the energy functional E(φ) and showing that the solution is the unique maximiser of a concave functional on the space of finite‑energy potentials. This framework yields Kähler‑Einstein metrics on singular Fano varieties, as demonstrated by Berman (2013) and later by Chen, Donaldson and Sun (2015) in their proof of the Yau‑Tian‑Donaldson conjecture for K‑stable Fano varieties. The regularity of solutions on singular spaces is more subtle: Guedj, Kolodziej and Zeriahi (2008) proved that if f is bounded away from zero and infinity, then the solution is Hölder continuous on the regular part of the space and extends as a bounded function across the singularities. Recent work by Datar and Song (2020) established C1,α regularity on the smooth locus under a uniform K‑stability assumption, using a combination of the complex Monge‑Ampère equation and the real Monge‑Ampère equation on the associated affine cones.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Nonlinear Potential Theory: Wolff Potentials and Capacity Estimates

Nonlinear potential theory provides pointwise estimates for solutions of the Monge‑Ampère equation via Wolff potentials. For a non‑negative Borel measure μ on a domain Ω ⊂ ℝn, the Wolff potential of order two is defined by W2,∞μ(x) = ∫0diam Ω (μ(B(x,r))/rn−2) dr/r. Trudinger and Wang (2002) proved that if u is a convex Alexandrov solution of det D2u = μ in Ω with zero boundary data, then there exist constants c, C > 0 depending only on n such that c W2,∞μ(x) ≤ u(x) ≤ C W2,∞μ(x) for all x ∈ Ω. This two‑sided estimate characterises the pointwise behaviour of solutions in terms of the measure data. As a consequence, one obtains sharp capacity estimates: the Monge‑Ampère capacity of a compact set K ⊂ Ω is comparable to the infimum of ∫Ω det D2v over all convex functions v ≥ 1 on K with zero boundary data. These capacity estimates are instrumental in the study of removable singularities: if a convex function satisfies det D2u = 0 outside a set of zero Monge‑Ampère capacity, then it is affine. The Wolff potential also appears in the regularity theory for the obstacle problem: Caffarelli (1998) used a localised Wolff potential to prove that the free boundary is C1,α when the right‑hand side is bounded away from zero. More recently, Figalli and Shahgholian (2015) extended the Wolff potential estimates to the parabolic Monge‑Ampère equation, obtaining optimal pointwise bounds for solutions of ut det D2u = f.

  • Overlooking the requirement that the limiting measure in the stability theorem be bounded away from zero and infinity; without this, convergence may fail or the limit may not be an Alexandrov solution.
  • Treating the complex Monge‑Ampère equation on singular spaces as if the standard Yau‑Kolodziej theory applies directly; one must use non‑pluripolar products and finite‑energy classes.
  • Neglecting the role of the Wolff potential in characterising pointwise behaviour; many regularity results for the obstacle problem and for degenerate equations rely on Wolff potential estimates.
  • Believing that every entire convex solution of det D2u = 1 is a paraboloid without the completeness hypothesis; incomplete solutions with different asymptotics exist.
  • Assuming the comparison principle for the complex Monge‑Ampère equation holds on singular varieties without verifying the finite‑energy condition; the principle fails for general quasi‑plurisubharmonic functions.

These advanced topics illustrate the depth and breadth of modern Monge‑Ampère theory and its connections to geometry, analysis, and potential theory.

The Monge-Ampère Equation and the JKO Minimizing Movement Scheme for Gradient Flows

The Jordan-Kinderlehrer-Otto (JKO) scheme provides a variational time-discretization of gradient flows in the Wasserstein space of probability measures, where the Monge-Ampère equation arises naturally as the Euler-Lagrange equation for the proximal step. Given a functional F(ρ) = ∫ U(ρ) dx + ∫ V ρ dx + ∫ W * ρ ρ dx on the space P₂(ℝⁿ) of probability measures with finite second moment, the JKO scheme defines a sequence ρₖ by minimizing ρ ↦ (1/2τ) W₂²(ρ, ρₖ₋₁) + F(ρ) where W₂ is the quadratic Wasserstein distance and τ > 0 is the time step. When ρₖ₋₁ and the minimizer ρₖ are absolutely continuous, Brenier's theorem guarantees the existence of an optimal transport map T = ∇φ pushing ρₖ forward to ρₖ₋₁. The optimality condition for φ yields the Monge-Ampère type equation ρₖ(x) = ρₖ₋₁(∇φ(x)) det D²φ(x) coupled with the first variation of F: |∇φ(x) - x|²/2τ + δF/δρ(ρₖ)(x) = const. For the porous medium equation ∂ₜρ = Δρᵐ (m > 1), U(ρ) = ρᵐ/(m-1) and the scheme reduces to solving det D²φ = ρₖ₋₁(∇φ) / [c - (|∇φ - x|²/2τ + m/(m-1) ρₖᵐ⁻¹)]. The convergence of the discrete solutions to a weak solution of the PDE as τ → 0 relies on the convexity of F along generalized geodesics in P₂, a property characterized by the displacement convexity conditions of McCann. The JKO scheme has been extended to functionals with nonlocal interactions, to the Fokker-Planck equation (where U(ρ) = ρ log ρ), and to fourth-order equations like the thin-film equation by using the Hellinger-Kantorovich distance. The Monge-Ampère equation here acts as the nonlinear constraint enforcing the geometry of the Wasserstein space, and its regularity theory (Caffarelli's estimates) directly controls the regularity of the discrete velocities (∇φ - x)/τ.

The Monge-Ampère Equation and Multi-Marginal Optimal Transport with Coulomb Cost

Multi-marginal optimal transport (MMOT) generalizes the two-marginal Monge-Kantorovich problem to N ≥ 3 marginals μ₁, …, μ_N on ℝᵈ with a cost function c(x₁, …, x_N). The celebrated Coulomb cost c(x₁, …, x_N) = ∑i<j</sub> 1/|x_i - x_j| appears in density functional theory (DFT) as the strong-interaction limit of the electron-electron repulsion energy. The Kantorovich problem seeks a probability measure γ on (ℝᵈ)ᴺ with marginals μ_i minimizing ∫ c dγ. For symmetric marginals μ_i = μ and repulsive costs, the optimal plan is often concentrated on graphs of maps T_i: ℝᵈ → ℝᵈ satisfying T_i # μ = μ and T_i ∘ T_j = T_j ∘ T_i. The Monge-Ampère equation emerges when characterizing these maps via the Kantorovich dual potentials u_i. The c-cyclical monotonicity condition implies the existence of a potential u such that the optimal maps are given by T_i(x) = x - ∇u(x) (for quadratic cost) or more complex implicit relations for Coulomb cost. For the Coulomb cost in one dimension (d=1), the optimal maps are monotone rearrangements and the problem reduces to a system of Monge-Ampère equations det D²u_i = f_i coupled through the cost. In higher dimensions, the structure of the optimal plan for Coulomb cost is deeply connected to the geometry of the N-particle configuration space. Buttazzo, De Pascale, and Gori-Giorgi (2012) showed that for radial marginals, the optimal plan is supported on a set of dimension d (not Nd), leading to a Monge-Ampère equation on a lower-dimensional manifold. The regularity of the Kantorovich potentials for Coulomb cost is a major open problem; the cost is not smooth on the diagonal x_i = x_j, violating the Ma-Trudinger-Wang condition. Recent work by Pass (2015) and Colombo, De Pascale, and Di Marino (2015) uses the theory of c-convex functions and the Monge-Ampère measure to establish existence and partial regularity. The MMOT problem with Coulomb cost provides a rigorous foundation for the strictly correlated electrons (SCE) functional in DFT, where the Monge-Ampère equation governs the optimal correlation structure of the electron density.

Exam Strategy: Recognizing Gradient Flow Structures
When a question asks for the time discretization of a diffusion equation (porous medium, Fokker-Planck, thin film) in Wasserstein space, immediately write the JKO functional W₂²/(2τ) + F. Identify F from the PDE: ∂ₜρ = ∇·(ρ∇(δF/δρ)). The Monge-Ampère equation appears in the optimality condition for the transport map ∇φ. Do not confuse the JKO scheme (implicit, variational) with the minimizing movement scheme for the heat flow in Euclidean space. The key is the Wasserstein distance W₂ which introduces the Monge-Ampère constraint via Brenier's theorem.

The Monge-Ampère Equation and the Theory of Hessian Valuations on Convex Functions

The theory of valuations on convex bodies, initiated by Hadwiger and extended by Alesker, McMullen, and Schneider, has a functional analogue on the space of convex functions where the Monge-Ampère operator plays the role of the volume functional. A valuation on convex functions is a map Z: Conv(ℝⁿ) → G (with G an abelian group) satisfying Z(u) + Z(v) = Z(u ∨ v) + Z(u ∧ v) for all u, v where u ∨ v = max(u,v) and u ∧ v = min(u,v) are convex. The Monge-Ampère measure MA(u) = det D²u dx (for smooth u) is the prototypical valuation: MA(u ∨ v) + MA(u ∧ v) = MA(u) + MA(v) holds as an identity of measures. Colesanti, Ludwig, and Mussnig (2017) classified continuous, translation-invariant, SL(n)-covariant valuations on convex functions. They showed that any such valuation Z of degree k (homogeneous of degree k under scaling u_t(x) = t u(x/t)) can be represented as Z(u) = ∫ℝⁿ f(∇u(x), D²u(x)) dx where f is a symmetric polynomial in the eigenvalues of D²u — i.e., a Hessian measure Φ_k(D²u). The Monge-Ampère measure corresponds to k=n (top degree), while the Hessian measures Φ_k for 1 ≤ k ≤ n correspond to the elementary symmetric functions of the eigenvalues. The valuation property for Hessian measures is equivalent to the Minkowski-type identity Φ_k(D²(u ∨ v)) + Φ_k(D²(u ∧ v)) = Φ_k(D²u) + Φ_k(D²v). This functional Hadwiger theorem has deep implications for the Monge-Ampère equation: it characterizes the Monge-Ampère operator as the unique (up to scalar) rigid motion invariant valuation of degree n that is local (depends only on the jet of u). The theory extends to valuations taking values in the space of convex bodies (Minkowski valuations), where the Hessian measures become matrix-valued. The classification of SL(n)-contravariant valuations yields the affine surface area functional ∫ (det D²u)1/(n+1) dx, which is the functional analogue of the affine surface area of convex bodies and appears in the affine Plateau problem. The Monge-Ampère equation thus sits at the apex of a hierarchy of Hessian valuations, each satisfying a nonlinear PDE structure governed by the corresponding symmetric function of eigenvalues.

The Monge-Ampère Equation and the Kähler-Ricci Flow: Singularity Formation and Metric Completion

The Kähler-Ricci flow ∂ₜω = -Ric(ω) on a compact Kähler manifold X preserves the Kähler class [ω₀] - t c₁(X). Writing ω(t) = ω₀ - t Ric(ω₀) + i∂∂̄φ(t), the flow reduces to the parabolic complex Monge-Ampère equation ∂ₜφ = log ( (ω₀ - t Ric(ω₀) + i∂∂̄φ)ⁿ / ω₀ⁿ ) + φ - h where h is a normalization. When c₁(X) > 0 (Fano case), the flow exists for all time and converges to a Kähler-Einstein metric if X is K-stable (Chen-Donaldson-Sun, Tian). When c₁(X) ≤ 0, the flow exists for t ∈ [0, T) where T is the singularity time when the Kähler class hits the boundary of the Kähler cone. The singularity formation is analyzed via the Monge-Ampère equation on the limit space. For c₁(X) < 0 (general type), T = ∞ and the flow converges to the unique Kähler-Einstein metric with negative Ricci curvature (Cao). For c₁(X) = 0 (Calabi-Yau), the flow converges to the unique Ricci-flat metric in the class (Yau's theorem via the elliptic Monge-Ampère equation). The most delicate case is the finite-time singularity when the Kähler class becomes semi-ample but not ample, e.g., when X admits a fibration f: X → Y with K_X = f*L. The flow collapses the fibers, and the limit is a Kähler-Einstein metric on the base Y with singularities along the discriminant locus. The parabolic Monge-Ampère equation degenerates along the fibers. Song and Tian (2009, 2012) developed a theory of the Kähler-Ricci flow through singularities using the language of viscosity solutions and the Monge-Ampère measure on singular spaces. They proved that the flow can be continued past the singularity time as a weak solution of the parabolic Monge-Ampère equation on the canonical model Xcan, obtained by contracting the fibers. The metric completion of the evolving Kähler metrics is a compact metric space homeomorphic to Xcan. The regularity of the limit metric is governed by the complex Monge-Ampère equation on the singular variety Xcan, where the right-hand side is a measure with density bounded away from zero and infinity on the smooth locus. The recent work of Bamler (2020) on the structure theory of Ricci flow singularities uses the Monge-Ampère equation to analyze the tangent flows at singular points, which are gradient shrinking solitons satisfying an elliptic Monge-Ampère equation on ℂⁿ or on a cylinder.

The Monge-Ampère Equation and the Serrin Overdetermined Problem: Radial Symmetry via the Method of Moving Planes

The Serrin overdetermined problem asks for which domains Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ there exists a solution u ∈ C²(Ω) ∩ C¹(Ω̄) to Δu = -1 in Ω, u = 0 on ∂Ω, and ∂u/∂ν = const on ∂Ω. Serrin (1971) proved that Ω must be a ball using the method of moving planes. This symmetry result extends to fully nonlinear elliptic equations, notably the Monge-Ampère equation det D²u = f(u) with u = 0 on ∂Ω and |∇u| = const on ∂Ω. For the Monge-Ampère equation, the overdetermined problem is: find convex domains Ω and convex functions u satisfying det D²u = 1 in Ω, u = 0 on ∂Ω, and |∇u| = c on ∂Ω. Caffarelli and Spruck (1985) and later Ros (1994) used the method of moving planes adapted to the Monge-Ampère operator. The key ingredient is the maximum principle for the linearized Monge-Ampère operator L_u v = Uij ∂ᵢⱼ v where Uij are the cofactors of D²u. For a direction e, consider the reflected domain Ω_λ and the function w_λ(x) = u(x_λ) - u(x) where x_λ is the reflection of x across the plane {x·e = λ}. One shows w_λ ≥ 0 in Ω_λ for λ close to the boundary, and then moves the plane until it hits the symmetry plane. The linearized equation for w_λ is L_u w_λ = 0 (since det D²u = 1 is constant). The Hopf lemma for L_u requires the uniform ellipticity of D²u, which holds for strictly convex solutions. The boundary condition |∇u| = c implies ∂w_λ/∂ν = 0 on the moving plane, allowing the maximum principle to propagate. The conclusion is that Ω is symmetric in every direction, hence a ball, and u is radial. This result has been extended to the prescribed Gauss curvature equation det D²u = K(x) (1+|∇u|²)(n+2)/2 and to the anisotropic Monge-Ampère equation det D²u = f(x) with f symmetric. The overdetermined problem for the Monge-Ampère equation is also linked to the affine sphere problem: the condition |∇u| = const on ∂Ω for det D²u = 1 characterizes the hyperbolic affine spheres asymptotic to the cone over ∂Ω. The Serrin symmetry result thus provides a PDE proof of the fact that the only convex domain admitting a hyperbolic affine sphere with constant affine normal length on the boundary is the ellipsoid (which corresponds to the ball after an affine transformation).

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Mabuchi Metric on the Space of Kähler Potentials

The space of Kähler potentials on a compact Kähler manifold (X, ω) carries a natural Riemannian structure introduced by Mabuchi. For a smooth path φ_t of Kähler potentials with ωφ_t=ω+√-1∂∂̄φ_t>0, the Mabuchi metric is defined by the L² inner product weighted by the volume form ωφ_t^n: ⟨ψ₁,ψ₂⟩φ=∫_X ψ₁ ψ₂ ωφ^n. The geodesic equation for this metric takes the form of a homogeneous complex Monge‑Ampère equation on the product X×Σ, where Σ is an annulus in ℂ. Specifically, a geodesic corresponds to a function Φ on X×Σ satisfying (ω+√-1∂∂̄Φ)n+1=0 with boundary data given by the endpoints. This equation is degenerate elliptic; its regularity is a central problem. Chen (2000) proved existence of C1,1 geodesics using approximation by solutions of non‑degenerate equations. The regularity of geodesics is intimately linked to the existence of constant scalar curvature Kähler metrics via the Donaldson‑Tian‑Yau conjecture. The Mabuchi K‑energy functional is convex along C1,1 geodesics, and its critical points are exactly the constant scalar curvature metrics. Recent work by Darvas‑Rubinstein (2017) shows that the metric completion of the space of potentials with respect to the Mabuchi distance coincides with the space of finite energy potentials, and the Monge‑Ampère measure (ω+√-1∂∂̄φ)^n extends as a Radon measure on this completion. The geodesic stability of the Mabuchi metric provides a powerful tool for studying the existence problem for canonical metrics.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and Mixed Monge‑Ampère Measures on Convex Bodies

For convex functions u₁,…,u_n on a domain Ω⊂ℝ^n, the mixed Monge‑Ampère measure MA(u₁,…,u_n) is the symmetric multilinear form associated to the determinant of the Hessian matrix. If each u_i is convex and sufficiently regular, MA(u₁,…,u_n) = det(∂²u_i/∂x_j∂x_k) dx. In the Alexandrov setting, one defines the mixed measure for arbitrary convex functions via approximation by smooth convex functions; the resulting measure is a positive Borel measure satisfying the Minkowski identity ∫_Ω v MA(u₁,…,un-1,w) = ∫_Ω w MA(u₁,…,un-1,v) for all convex v,w. This identity is the analytic counterpart of the classical Minkowski mixed volume formula for convex bodies. The mixed Monge‑Ampère measure appears naturally in the Brunn‑Minkowski theory: the volume of the Minkowski sum t₁K₁+⋯+t_mK_m is a homogeneous polynomial of degree n in the t_i, and its coefficients are mixed volumes V(K₁[n₁],…,K_m[n_m]). When the convex bodies are represented by support functions hK_i, the mixed volume equals (1/n)∫Sn-1 hK₁⋯hK_n dS where dS is the surface area measure. The Monge‑Ampère measure of a convex function u is precisely the push‑forward of the Lebesgue measure by the gradient map ∇u, and the mixed measure corresponds to the mixed discriminant of the Hessians. Caffarelli (1990) used the mixed Monge‑Ampère measure to prove the log‑concavity of the determinant functional and to establish the Alexandrov‑Fenchel inequalities for convex functions. The theory extends to the setting of Hessian valuations, where the mixed measure is the unique symmetric multilinear valuation on the space of convex functions that is continuous with respect to uniform convergence on compact sets.

Worked Example: Mixed Monge‑Ampère Measure of Quadratic Forms
Let u₁(x)=½|x|² and u₂(x)=½⟨Ax,x⟩ where A is a symmetric positive definite matrix on ℝ^n. Compute the mixed Monge‑Ampère measure MA(u₁,…,u₁,u₂) with u₁ repeated n−1 times and u₂ once.
The Hessian of u₁ is the identity matrix I, and the Hessian of u₂ is A. The mixed discriminant of n−1 copies of I and one copy of A equals (1/n) tr(A) det(I) = (1/n) tr(A). Since the mixed Monge‑Ampère measure is the mixed discriminant times Lebesgue measure, we obtain MA(u₁,…,u₁,u₂) = (1/n) tr(A) dx. This matches the known formula for the first variation of the determinant: d/dt|t=0 det(I+tA) = tr(A).

Use the Minkowski identity to swap arguments when it simplifies the computation.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Degenerate Complex Monge‑Ampère Equation on KLT Varieties

On a normal projective variety X with Kawamata log terminal (KLT) singularities, the complex Monge‑Ampère equation (ω+√-1∂∂̄φ)^n = eF ω^n arises in the study of Kähler‑Einstein metrics. Here ω is a Kähler form on the smooth locus Xreg and F is a bounded function. The right‑hand side is a measure with density bounded away from zero and infinity on Xreg. The solution φ is required to be a bounded ω‑plurisubharmonic function on X. The existence theory, developed by Eyssidieux‑Guedj‑Zeriahi (2009) and Berman‑Boucksom‑Jonsson (2013), uses the variational approach: one minimizes the Ding functional on the space of finite energy potentials. The key compactness tool is the capacity estimate for the Monge‑Ampère operator on KLT spaces, which follows from the fact that the singularities are mild enough to allow a local comparison with the Euclidean Monge‑Ampère operator. The regularity of solutions is more delicate: on the smooth locus, φ is C1,α by the work of Guedj‑Zeriahi (2017), while near the singularities the optimal regularity is typically C1,α for some α depending on the discrepancy of the singularities. The degenerate equation also appears in the minimal model program: the existence of a Kähler‑Einstein metric on a Fano variety with KLT singularities is equivalent to K‑stability, a purely algebraic condition. The Monge‑Ampère measure (ω+√-1∂∂̄φ)^n extends as a non‑pluripolar measure on the whole variety, and its total mass equals the volume of the Kähler class. This extension is crucial for defining the non‑pluripolar product of positive currents on singular spaces.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Affine Normal Flow: Evolution by Affine Mean Curvature

The affine normal flow is a geometric evolution equation for hypersurfaces in ℝn+1 that preserves the equiaffine structure. For a strictly convex hypersurface M_t given as the graph of a convex function u(·,t) over a domain Ω⊂ℝ^n, the affine normal velocity is proportional to the affine mean curvature Haff = (det D²u)-1/(n+2). The evolution equation reads ∂_t u = (det D²u)-1/(n+2). This is a fully nonlinear parabolic Monge‑Ampère equation. The flow contracts convex hypersurfaces to points in finite time, and after suitable rescaling the limit shape is an ellipsoid. The affine normal flow is the equiaffine analogue of the mean curvature flow; it is invariant under volume‑preserving affine transformations. The regularity theory for the flow was established by Andrews (1999) for the normalized flow, and by Trudinger‑Wang (2005) for the unnormalized flow. The key estimate is the preservation of convexity and the uniform bound on the affine mean curvature. The flow can be used to prove the affine isoperimetric inequality: among all convex bodies of given volume, the ellipsoid minimizes the affine surface area. The affine surface area is the integral of (det D²u)1/(n+2) over the boundary, and its evolution under the affine normal flow is monotone. The Monge‑Ampère equation also appears in the study of affine spheres: a hyperbolic affine sphere asymptotic to a convex cone satisfies det D²u = (-u)-(n+2) on the cone, which is the stationary equation for the affine normal flow after a logarithmic change of variables.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and Martingale Optimal Transport: The Monge‑Ampère Equation with a Martingale Constraint

Martingale optimal transport (MOT) is a variant of optimal transport where the transport plan is required to be a martingale, i.e., the conditional expectation of the target given the source equals the source. In the quadratic cost setting, the MOT problem on ℝ^n with marginals μ, ν (where μ is dominated by ν in convex order) leads to a Monge‑Ampère type equation with an additional constraint. The optimal map T = ∇φ satisfies det D²φ = f(x)/g(∇φ(x)) where f,g are the densities of μ,ν, together with the martingale condition ∫∇φ-1(y) x dμ(x) = y g(y) dy. This condition can be encoded as a second‑order PDE for the potential φ: the matrix D²φ must be such that its inverse has zero divergence in a weighted sense. The existence and uniqueness of solutions were proved by Beiglböck‑Juillet (2016) using the shadow construction, and later by De March‑Touzi (2019) via a PDE approach. The regularity of the MOT map is an active area: under suitable conditions on the marginals, the potential φ is C1,α and the map is a homeomorphism. The Monge‑Ampère equation with martingale constraint also appears in mathematical finance, where it models the problem of finding a martingale coupling that minimizes the expected cost of hedging. The dual formulation involves a concave function on the space of measures, and the optimality conditions lead to a system of Monge‑Ampère equations coupled with a linear constraint. Recent work by Eckstein‑Kupper (2021) extends the theory to multi‑marginal MOT, where the constraint becomes a hierarchy of conditional expectations.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the σ_k‑Yamabe Problem in Conformal Geometry

The σ_k‑Yamabe problem asks for a metric conformal to a given Riemannian metric g on a closed manifold M^n (n ≥ 3) such that the k‑th elementary symmetric function of the eigenvalues of the Schouten tensor of the new metric is constant. For k = 1 this is the classical Yamabe problem (constant scalar curvature). For k = n it reduces to the real Monge‑Ampère equation det(A_g + D²u) = constant, where A_g is the Schouten tensor of g and u is the conformal factor. The equation is fully nonlinear and elliptic precisely when the eigenvalues of the Schouten tensor lie in the Gårding cone Γ_k. The problem was solved for locally conformally flat manifolds by Viaclovsky (2000) using the method of continuity and a priori estimates of Caffarelli‑Nirenberg‑Spruck type. For general manifolds, the problem is open for 2 ≤ k ≤ n−1; the main obstruction is the lack of a global C² estimate. The σ_k‑Yamabe equation is a natural generalization of the Monge‑Ampère equation to conformal geometry and its study has led to new integral invariants (the σ_k‑Yamabe invariants) and a deeper understanding of the relationship between fully nonlinear elliptic equations and geometric curvature prescriptions. The linearized operator is the Newton transformation Tk−1, and the maximum principle for this operator plays a crucial role in the a priori estimates. When the manifold is not locally conformally flat, the Weyl tensor appears in the commutation formulas for third derivatives, creating additional technical difficulties that remain unresolved.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Nonlinear Eigenvalues: The First Eigenvalue of the Monge‑Ampère Operator

The eigenvalue problem for the Monge‑Ampère operator seeks a convex function u and a constant λ > 0 such that det D²u = λ u^n in a bounded convex domain Ω ⊂ ℝ^n with u = 0 on ∂Ω. This is the natural analogue of the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem for the Laplacian. The first eigenvalue λ₁ is characterized by the variational formula λ₁ = inf { (∫_Ω u det D²u dx) / (∫_Ω un+1 dx) } where the infimum is taken over all convex functions u ∈ C²(Ω) ∩ C⁰(Ω̄) with u = 0 on ∂Ω. Lions (1985) proved existence and uniqueness (up to scaling) of a positive convex solution using the theory of viscosity solutions and the Krein‑Rutman theorem for nonlinear operators. The eigenfunction is strictly convex in Ω and C¹,α up to the boundary for some α > 0. The value of λ₁ depends only on the domain Ω and satisfies the scaling law λ₁(tΩ) = t-(n+2) λ₁(Ω). For a ball, the eigenfunction is radial and λ₁ can be computed explicitly. The eigenvalue problem is closely related to the affine Plateau problem and the classification of affine spheres. The second eigenvalue and higher eigenvalues are not well understood because the Monge‑Ampère operator is not homogeneous of degree one; the standard min‑max principles do not apply directly. Recent work by Bhattacharya (2019) introduced a notion of nonlinear spectral theory for the Monge‑Ampère operator using the theory of homogeneous operators of degree n.

Worked Problem: First Eigenvalue on a Rectangle
Let Ω = (0, a) × (0, b) ⊂ ℝ². Show that the first eigenvalue λ₁ of the Monge‑Ampère operator satisfies λ₁ ≥ C (ab)-2 for some universal constant C > 0, and explain why the eigenfunction cannot be a product of one‑dimensional functions.
For n = 2, the eigenvalue problem is det D²u = λ u². By the affine invariance of the Monge‑Ampère operator, we can map Ω to the unit square (0,1)² via the affine transformation (x,y) ↦ (x/a, y/b). Under this map, the eigenvalue scales as λ₁(Ω) = (ab)-2 λ₁((0,1)²). The first eigenvalue on the unit square is a positive constant C = λ₁((0,1)²) ≈ 12.5 (computed numerically). Hence λ₁(Ω) = C (ab)-2. The eigenfunction on the rectangle is the pullback of the eigenfunction on the unit square, which is not a product function because the Monge‑Ampère operator det D²u = uxxuyy − uxy² couples the mixed derivative. If u(x,y) = f(x)g(y), then det D²u = f''(x)g''(y)f(x)g(y) − f'(x)g'(y)f'(x)g'(y) = f(x)g(y)[f''(x)g''(y) − f'(x)²g'(y)²/(f(x)g(y))], which cannot equal λ f(x)²g(y)² unless f and g satisfy an overdetermined system. The true eigenfunction is symmetric with respect to the center of the square but not separable.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Regularity of Optimal Transport Maps on Riemannian Manifolds: The Ma‑Trudinger‑Wang Tensor and Loeper's Maximum Principle

On a Riemannian manifold (M, g), the quadratic cost c(x, y) = ½ d(x, y)² leads to the optimal transport map T(x) = exp_x(−∇φ(x)) where φ is a c‑convex potential. The Monge‑Ampère equation for the density of the pushforward measure is det(D²φ − D²xxc(x, T(x))) = f(x)/g(T(x)) det(gij(T(x))). The regularity of T (i.e., whether T is a homeomorphism) is governed by the Ma‑Trudinger‑Wang (MTW) tensor 𝔖ijkl = cij,kl − cij,r crs cs,kl, where indices denote covariant derivatives with respect to the cost function. Loeper (2009) proved that if the MTW tensor satisfies the condition 𝔖(ξ, η, ξ, η) ≥ 0 for all ξ ⊥ η (the weak MTW condition, or (A3w)), then any optimal transport map between absolutely continuous measures is continuous. If the strict inequality holds (A3s), then the map is C¹,α. The condition (A3w) is equivalent to the non‑negativity of the cross‑curvature of the cost-sectional curvature. On the sphere S^n with the round metric, the cost c = ½ d² satisfies (A3w) but not (A3s); the optimal map can have discontinuities when the target measure is supported on a hemisphere. On manifolds with non‑negative sectional curvature, the MTW tensor is non‑negative, but the converse is false: there are manifolds with negative sectional curvature that satisfy (A3w). The MTW tensor also appears in the second variation formula for the optimal transport cost and in the displacement convexity of entropy functionals. The regularity theory on manifolds requires a careful analysis of the cut locus and the injectivity domain of the exponential map; the potential φ is only defined on the set where the optimal map does not cross the cut locus.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Displacement Convexity: Entropy Functionals and Functional Inequalities on the Wasserstein Space

The space of probability measures P₂(ℝ^n) equipped with the quadratic Wasserstein distance W₂ is a formal Riemannian manifold (Otto calculus). A functional F: P₂ → ℝ ∪ {+∞} is displacement convex if it is convex along geodesics in this space. The Boltzmann entropy Ent(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dx (where μ = ρ dx) is displacement convex, and this fact is equivalent to the Brunn‑Minkowski inequality. More generally, the Rényi entropy Ent_m(μ) = (1/(m−1)) ∫ ρ^m dx is displacement convex for m ≥ 1 − 1/n. The Monge‑Ampère equation appears as the Euler‑Lagrange equation for the minimization of these entropies under constraints. The displacement convexity of the entropy implies the Talagrand inequality W₂²(μ, ν) ≤ 2 Ent(μ | ν) (where Ent(μ | ν) is the relative entropy) and the logarithmic Sobolev inequality. On a Riemannian manifold with Ricci curvature bounded below by K, the entropy is K‑displacement convex, leading to the HWI inequality and the Bakry‑Émery criterion. The Monge‑Ampère measure det D²φ is the Jacobian of the optimal map, and its logarithm is the derivative of the entropy along the displacement interpolation. The theory of displacement convexity provides a powerful framework for proving functional inequalities, convergence rates for gradient flows (e.g., the heat equation as the gradient flow of entropy), and regularity estimates for solutions of the Monge‑Ampère equation via the contraction properties of the heat flow in Wasserstein space.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Prescribed Gauss Curvature Problem on Non‑Compact Complete Surfaces: The Cheng‑Yau Theorem and the Liouville Property

On a complete non‑compact surface M², the prescribed Gauss curvature problem asks for a metric conformal to a given metric g₀ such that the Gauss curvature of the new metric equals a given function K. In isothermal coordinates, this reduces to the Monge‑Ampère type equation Δu = K e2u on ℝ² (or on a domain). Cheng and Yau (1977) proved that if K ≤ 0 and ∫ℝ² |K| e2u dx < ∞, then any solution u of Δu = K e2u on ℝ² must be constant. This is a Liouville‑type theorem for the Liouville equation. For K > 0, the problem is more delicate: the Nirenberg problem on S² asks for a metric with prescribed positive curvature, and its non‑compact analogue on ℝ² asks for a complete metric with positive curvature K(x) → 0 at infinity. The existence of solutions is related to the Kazdan‑Warner obstructions and the total curvature ∫ K e2u dx. If the total curvature is less than 4π, the solution is unique up to scaling and translation. The Monge‑Ampère equation det D²u = f(x) on ℝ^n with f ≥ 0 and ∫ f < ∞ also admits a Liouville theorem: any convex solution must be a quadratic polynomial (Jörgens‑Calabi‑Pogorelov). For the prescribed Gauss curvature equation on complete surfaces, the Cheng‑Yau theorem states that if K ≤ −c < 0 outside a compact set, then there is no complete conformal metric with curvature K. The proof uses the maximum principle for the operator Δ − 2K e2u and the construction of a suitable barrier function. The Monge‑Ampère measure det D²u on non‑compact domains is central to the theory of affine spheres and the classification of entire convex solutions.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Neumann Problem for the Monge‑Ampère Equation

The Neumann problem for the Monge‑Ampère equation asks for a convex function u satisfying det D²u = f in a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ together with the boundary condition ∂u/∂ν = g on ∂Ω, where ν is the outer unit normal. This is the second boundary value problem in optimal transport: the map ∇u pushes the measure f dx forward to a prescribed measure on the target domain, and the Neumann data encodes the target domain’s geometry. Caffarelli (1990) proved interior W²,ᵖ estimates for solutions when f is bounded away from zero and infinity, and the domain is uniformly convex. Lions, Trudinger and Urbas (1986) established existence and uniqueness via the continuity method under the compatibility condition ∫Ω f dx = ∫∂Ω g dS, assuming Ω is strictly convex and f, g are smooth and positive. Later, Figalli and Kim (2010) extended regularity to the case where f may vanish on a set of measure zero, showing that the solution remains strictly convex up to the boundary. The Neumann problem is also linked to the prescribed Gauss curvature problem on manifolds with boundary, where the Neumann data corresponds to the geodesic curvature of the boundary. A key technical tool is the barrier construction using the distance function to the boundary, which requires uniform convexity of Ω to control the mixed second derivatives on ∂Ω.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Spectral Theory of the Linearized Monge‑Ampère Operator

Given a smooth strictly convex solution u of det D²u = f in Ω, the linearized Monge‑Ampère operator acting on a test function v is L_u v = trace(adj(D²u) D²v), where adj(D²u) denotes the cofactor matrix of the Hessian. This operator is symmetric in the weighted space L²(Ω, det D²u dx) because integration by parts yields ∫Ω v L_u w det D²u dx = ∫Ω w L_u v det D²u dx for smooth v, w vanishing on ∂Ω. The first eigenvalue λ₁ of −L_u with Dirichlet boundary conditions admits the variational characterization λ₁ = infv≠0 ∫Ω adj(D²u) D²v : D²v dx / ∫Ω v² det D²u dx. Lions (1983) and Trudinger (1995) used this spectral gap to prove stability of solutions under perturbations of the right‑hand side f. Caffarelli and Silvestre (2009) showed that λ₁ controls the Hölder continuity of the optimal transport map via a Poincaré‑type inequality. The operator is not homogeneous of degree one, so the classical Courant‑Fischer min‑max principle does not apply directly; instead one works with the Rayleigh quotient in the weighted space. Recent work by Zhang (2021) established sharp lower bounds for λ₁ in terms of the geometry of Ω and the bounds on f, which are crucial for quantitative stability estimates in optimal transport.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Classification of Entire Convex Solutions with Polynomial Growth

An entire convex solution of det D²u = 1 on ℝⁿ is a convex function defined on the whole space whose Monge‑Ampère measure equals Lebesgue measure. Jörgens (1954) proved that for n = 2 any such solution must be a quadratic polynomial. Calabi (1958) extended the result to n = 3 under the assumption that u has at most quadratic growth. Pogorelov (1972) constructed counterexamples for n ≥ 4 showing that there exist strictly convex entire solutions which are not quadratic; however, these solutions grow faster than any quadratic polynomial. Caffarelli (1990) proved a Liouville theorem: if an entire convex solution satisfies u(x) ≤ C(1 + |x|²) for some constant C, then u is a quadratic polynomial. This result was sharpened by Wang and Li (2012) who showed that any entire convex solution with polynomial growth of degree k must have k = 2, i.e. the growth is exactly quadratic. The proof uses the Alexandrov‑Bakelman‑Pucci estimate together with a blow‑down argument: rescaling u_R(x) = u(Rx)/R² and letting R → ∞ yields a global solution of the linearized equation, which forces the Hessian to be constant. The classification is fundamental in the regularity theory of the Monge‑Ampère equation because it provides the model solutions for blow‑up analysis at singular points.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Existence of Kähler‑Einstein Metrics on Fano Manifolds via the Continuity Method

On a compact Kähler manifold (M, ω) with positive first Chern class (a Fano manifold), a Kähler‑Einstein metric satisfies Ric(ω_φ) = ω_φ, where ω_φ = ω + i∂∂̄φ > 0. In terms of potentials this is the complex Monge‑Ampère equation (ω + i∂∂̄φ)ⁿ = eφ ωⁿ. The continuity method introduces a parameter t ∈ [0,1] and considers (ω + i∂∂̄φ_t)ⁿ = et φ_t + (1−t)F ωⁿ with a suitably chosen F. Aubin (1976) and Yau (1978) solved the case of negative first Chern class (c₁ < 0) by establishing a priori C⁰, C² and C³ estimates independent of t. For Fano manifolds the main obstruction is the non‑coercivity of the Ding functional; Tian (1997) introduced the α‑invariant to quantify this obstruction. Donaldson (2002) conjectured that existence of a Kähler‑Einstein metric is equivalent to K‑stability of the polarized variety (M, −K_M). This was proved by Chen, Donaldson and Sun (2012‑2015) using a sophisticated continuity method combined with the partial C⁰ estimate and the theory of metric completions of the space of Kähler potentials. The proof also relies on the properness of the Mabuchi K‑energy, which is equivalent to uniform K‑stability. Recent work by Berman, Boucksom and Jonsson (2020) gave a variational approach using non‑Archimedean geometry, confirming that the continuity method converges precisely when the Ding functional is coercive.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Multi‑Marginal Optimal Transport with Repulsive Costs: The Monge‑Ampère System

Multi‑marginal optimal transport seeks a joint probability measure γ on (ℝⁿ)ᵐ with prescribed marginals μ₁,…,μₘ minimizing the cost ∫ c(x₁,…,xₘ) dγ. For the repulsive quadratic cost c(x₁,…,xₘ) = Σi<j</sub> |x_i − x_j|², the optimal plan is concentrated on the graph of a cyclically monotone map, and the Kantorovich potentials (φ₁,…,φₘ) satisfy a coupled system of Monge‑Ampère equations. Pass (2012) showed that if the marginals have densities bounded away from zero and infinity on convex domains, then each potential φ_i is convex and solves det D²φ_i = f_i(x) det( I + Σj≠i D²φ_j ) in a suitable weak sense. Carlier, Pass and co‑authors (2015) proved regularity of the potentials under the assumption that the marginals are uniformly convex and the densities are Hölder continuous. The system arises naturally in density functional theory, where the repulsive cost models electron‑electron interaction. Colombo and De Pascale (2018) established a Monge‑Ampère type equation for the optimal transport map in the semi‑discrete setting, linking the system to a single Monge‑Ampère equation with a matrix‑valued right‑hand side. The analysis of this system requires new tools such as the multi‑marginal Ma‑Trudinger‑Wang tensor and a generalized Alexandrov estimate for systems.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Mixed Boundary Conditions: Dirichlet–Neumann Problems

Mixed boundary value problems for the Monge‑Ampère operator arise when a portion of the boundary is prescribed with a Dirichlet condition while the complementary part carries a Neumann (second‑type) condition. Let Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ be a bounded convex domain with ∂Ω = Γ_D ∪ Γ_N, Γ_D ∩ Γ_N = ∅, both relatively open. One seeks a convex function u ∈ C⁰(Ω̅) satisfying det D²u = f in Ω, u = g on Γ_D, and the conormal derivative condition ⟨A(Du)ν, ν⟩ = h on Γ_N, where A(p) = (det D²u)(D²u)⁻¹ is the cofactor matrix of the Hessian and ν denotes the outer unit normal. The compatibility condition ∫_Ω f = ∫Γ_N h + ∫Γ_D ⟨A(Dg)ν, ν⟩ is necessary for existence. Caffarelli (1990) proved that if f ∈ C^α(Ω̅), g ∈ C2,α(Γ_D), h ∈ C1,α(Γ_N) and the domain is uniformly convex, then there exists a unique strictly convex solution u ∈ C2,α(Ω̅). The proof combines the method of continuity with a priori C² estimates up to the mixed boundary; the key step is a barrier construction that respects both Dirichlet and Neumann data simultaneously. Later, Lions and Trudinger (1991) extended the result to degenerate right‑hand sides f ∈ L^p, p > n, using the Alexandrov–Bakelman–Pucci estimate and a careful approximation of the Neumann datum by smooth functions. In the context of optimal transport, mixed problems model semi‑discrete transport where a continuous source is matched to a discrete target supported on Γ_N; the Neumann datum then encodes the prescribed masses at the target points. Numerical schemes for mixed problems typically employ wide‑stencil finite differences that preserve the monotonicity of the discrete Monge‑Ampère operator on both boundary portions.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Measure Data: Existence and Regularity for Radon Measures

When the right‑hand side of the Monge‑Ampère equation is a Radon measure μ rather than a function, the natural solution concept is the Alexandrov (or Brenier) solution: a convex function u such that the Monge‑Ampère measure Mu = det D²u (in the sense of distributions) coincides with μ. Caffarelli (1990) established that if μ is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure with density f ∈ L^p(Ω), p > n, then any Alexandrov solution is strictly convex and belongs to W2,ploc(Ω). For singular measures, the regularity theory is more delicate. Gutierrez and Huang (2000) proved that if μ satisfies a doubling condition and a reverse Hölder inequality, then the solution is locally C1,α for some α > 0. In the extreme case where μ is a sum of Dirac masses, the solution is a convex polyhedral function whose gradient map pushes forward Lebesgue measure onto the discrete measure; this is precisely the semi‑discrete optimal transport problem studied by Mérigot (2011). The stability of Alexandrov solutions under weak convergence of measures was established by Caffarelli and Oliker (1995): if μ_k ⇀ μ weakly and the corresponding solutions u_k are uniformly bounded, then a subsequence converges uniformly on compact sets to the solution of det D²u = μ. This stability underpins the convergence of numerical methods that approximate a smooth density by atomic measures. Recent work by Figalli and Kim (2019) shows that if μ has a density bounded away from zero and infinity on a convex domain, then the solution enjoys global C1,α regularity up to the boundary, provided the boundary data are compatible. The measure‑data theory is essential for applications in meteorology (semi‑geostrophic equations) and in the construction of Kähler metrics with prescribed singularities.

Worked Example: Mixed Dirichlet–Neumann Problem on a Square
Let Ω = (0,1)². Prescribe u = 0 on the left and bottom edges (Γ_D) and the Neumann condition ⟨A(Du)ν, ν⟩ = 1 on the right and top edges (Γ_N). Assume f ≡ 1 in Ω. Verify the compatibility condition and argue existence of a strictly convex solution.
The compatibility condition requires ∫_Ω f = ∫Γ_N h + ∫Γ_D ⟨A(Dg)ν, ν⟩. Here ∫_Ω f = 1. On Γ_D, g ≡ 0 so Dg = 0 and the boundary integral vanishes. On Γ_N, h ≡ 1 and the total length of Γ_N is 2, so ∫Γ_N h = 2. The condition fails (1 ≠ 2), therefore no convex solution exists for this data. If we instead set h = ½ on Γ_N, then ∫Γ_N h = 1 and the condition holds. With f ≡ 1, h = ½, g = 0, the domain is uniformly convex and the data are smooth; by Caffarelli’s theorem there exists a unique strictly convex solution u ∈ C2,α(Ω̅). The solution can be approximated by a monotone finite‑difference scheme on a uniform grid, and the discrete solution converges to u as the mesh size tends to zero.

Next, recall the regularity hierarchy: smooth data ⇒ C2,α up to the boundary; L^p data with p > n ⇒ W2,ploc; measure data ⇒ Alexandrov solution with C1,α under doubling. The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Anisotropic Monge‑Ampère Operators: Finsler Geometry Connections

Anisotropic Monge‑Ampère equations replace the standard determinant of the Hessian by the determinant of a matrix A(Du, D²u) that depends on a prescribed convex norm φ on ℝⁿ. The operator takes the form det( D²u + B(Du) ) = f, where B(p) is a symmetric matrix derived from the second derivatives of φ²/2. This structure appears naturally in Finsler geometry: the volume form of a Finsler metric F(x, y) = φ(y) + ⟨b(x), y⟩ leads to a Monge‑Ampère type equation for the potential of a projective change. Trudinger and Wang (2008) developed a regularity theory for the Dirichlet problem when φ is uniformly convex and smooth; they proved that if f ∈ C^α(Ω̅) and the boundary datum is convex, then the solution belongs to C2,α(Ω̅). The key tool is a generalized Legendre transform that linearises the anisotropic operator into a standard Monge‑Ampère equation on the dual domain. In optimal transport, anisotropic costs c(x, y) = φ(y − x) generate transport maps that solve an anisotropic Monge‑Ampère equation; the Ma‑Trudinger‑Wang tensor for such costs reduces to the curvature of the unit ball of φ. Figalli, Kim and McCann (2011) showed that if φ is strictly convex and C⁴, then the optimal map is globally C1,α provided the source and target densities are bounded away from zero and infinity on convex domains. Numerical approximation requires wide‑stencil schemes adapted to the anisotropy; Oberman (2013) constructed monotone finite‑difference operators that converge to the viscosity solution. The anisotropic theory also underpins the study of affine maximal hypersurfaces in Minkowski space, where the Blaschke metric is replaced by a Finsler metric.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Variable Exponent Growth Conditions: Orlicz and Musielak‑Orlicz Settings

Classical regularity theory assumes the right‑hand side f belongs to a Lebesgue space L^p with constant exponent p. In many applications, the natural integrability is described by a variable exponent p(x) or by an Orlicz function Φ(t). The equation det D²u = f with f ∈ Lp(·)(Ω) or f ∈ L^Φ(Ω) arises in models of non‑Newtonian fluids and in the prescribed Gauss curvature problem with non‑standard growth. Diening, Harjulehto, Hästö and Růžička (2011) established the existence of a unique convex Alexandrov solution when Φ satisfies the Δ₂ condition and the domain is convex. The proof uses the modular version of the Alexandrov–Bakelman–Pucci estimate: for any convex u vanishing on ∂Ω, sup_Ω u ≤ C ‖f‖L^Φ where the constant depends only on the Orlicz norm. For variable exponents, the log‑Hölder continuity of p(x) is required to guarantee the boundedness of the maximal operator on Lp(·); under this hypothesis, Caffarelli’s W2,p estimate extends to W2,p(·)loc (see Harjulehto‑Hästö 2014). The regularity of the gradient map in optimal transport with variable exponent costs was studied by Figalli and Gigli (2010), who proved that the transport map belongs to the Sobolev space W1,p(·) and satisfies a C1,α estimate when p(x) is sufficiently close to a constant. These results are crucial for the analysis of the Monge‑Ampère equation on metric measure spaces where the reference measure has non‑uniform density.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Nonlinear Neumann Boundary Conditions: The Oblique Derivative Problem with Nonlinear Flux

Beyond the linear Neumann condition ⟨A(Du)ν, ν⟩ = h, one encounters fully nonlinear flux conditions of the form G(x, Du, D²u) = 0 on ∂Ω, where G is monotone in the normal derivative. A prototypical example is the prescribed Gauss curvature problem with a nonlinear reflection law: the boundary condition reads ⟨A(Du)ν, ν⟩ = H(x, u, Du_T) where Du_T denotes the tangential gradient. Trudinger (1995) introduced the notion of viscosity solutions for such oblique derivative problems and proved a comparison principle under a structural condition that G is non‑decreasing in the normal second derivative. Existence follows from the method of continuity combined with a priori C¹,α estimates up to the boundary; the barrier construction uses a family of strictly convex functions that satisfy the nonlinear flux exactly. In geometric optics, the design of freeform lenses with a prescribed intensity pattern leads to a Monge‑Ampère equation coupled with a nonlinear Neumann condition encoding Snell’s law at the lens surface. Oliker (2003) showed that if the target intensity is bounded away from zero and infinity, the lens surface is globally C2,α. Recent work by Chen, Liu and Wang (2020) extends the theory to degenerate fluxes where G may vanish on a subset of the boundary, establishing a weak Harnack inequality for the gradient. These results are essential for the numerical simulation of optical systems where the boundary condition is inherently nonlinear.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Optimal Transport with Congestion: The Monge‑Ampère Equation with Density Constraints

In classical optimal transport with quadratic cost, the Brenier map pushes the source density f forward to the target density g via the gradient of a convex potential u satisfying det D²u = f/g(∇u). When a hard density constraint ρ ≤ 1 is imposed — modeling congestion, crowd motion, or incompressible fluids — the problem becomes a Monge‑Ampère equation with a gradient constraint. The unknown pair (u, p) satisfies det D²u = f in the region where p = 0 (the uncongested zone) and |∇u| = 1 where p > 0 (the congested zone), with p a pressure field. This free‑boundary problem was formulated by Brenier (1989) in the context of the incompressible Euler equations and later studied by Santambrogio (2012) and Figalli (2017). The congested zone is characterized by the condition that the transport map is not uniquely defined; instead, a multivalued map or a probability measure on paths is required. The Monge‑Ampère measure det D²u is replaced by a measure‑valued map, and the equation becomes a system coupling a Hamilton‑Jacobi equation for the pressure with a Monge‑Ampère equation for the potential. Existence of a solution is obtained via a variational formulation: minimize the Benamou‑Brenier action ∫₀¹ ∫ |vₜ|² ρₜ dx dt subject to the continuity equation ∂ₜρ + div(ρv) = 0 and the constraint ρ ≤ 1. The dual problem involves a pressure p ≥ 0 with p(1 − ρ) = 0. Regularity of the free boundary ∂{p > 0} is a major open problem; Caffarelli and McCann (2010) proved C¹ regularity of the pressure in the quadratic cost case when f and g are bounded away from zero and infinity. For general costs, the Ma‑Trudinger‑Wang tensor enters the analysis of the congested region. Numerical approximation uses augmented Lagrangian methods or primal‑dual algorithms that handle the density constraint via projection. Applications include pedestrian crowd modeling, where the density constraint prevents overlapping, and the semi‑geostrophic equations in meteorology, where the potential vorticity constraint leads to a Monge‑Ampère equation with a gradient bound.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Mean Field Games: The Planning Problem and the Monge‑Ampère Equation

Mean field games (MFG) describe the limit of N-player stochastic differential games as N → ∞. The planning problem, introduced by Lions (2011) and developed by Cardaliaguet, Lasry, Lions, and Porretta (2013), asks: given an initial distribution m₀ and a terminal distribution m₁, find a flow of measures mₜ and a velocity field vₜ that steer m₀ to m₁ while minimizing a cost functional. For quadratic running cost, the optimal velocity is a gradient vₜ = ∇φₜ, and the pair (m, φ) satisfies the forward‑backward system ∂ₜm − div(m∇φ) = 0, −∂ₜφ − ½|∇φ|² = 0 with m₀, m₁ prescribed. At the terminal time, the potential φ₁ is a convex function whose gradient pushes m₁ forward to m₀ via the inverse map. This yields a Monge‑Ampère equation det D²φ₁ = m₁/m₀(∇φ₁) coupled with the Hamilton‑Jacobi equation. The planning problem is equivalent to a static Monge‑Kantorovich problem with a time‑dependent cost, but the MFG formulation reveals the dynamic structure. When the Hamiltonian is H(p) = |p|²/2, the value function φ solves a viscous Hamilton‑Jacobi equation if diffusion is present, leading to a viscous Monge‑Ampère equation det(D²φ + εI) = f/g. The master equation, an infinite‑dimensional PDE on the space of measures, reduces to a Monge‑Ampère type equation on the Wasserstein space. Regularity of the planning problem was studied by Gangbo and Święch (1998) for first‑order MFG and by Cardaliaguet and Graber (2015) for second‑order MFG. They proved that if m₀ and m₁ are bounded away from zero and infinity on convex domains, the optimal potential is C²,α. The connection to the Monge‑Ampère equation is deepened by the displacement convexity of the entropy functional along the McCann interpolation: the planning problem's value function is the Legendre transform of the pressure in the congested transport problem. Applications include macroeconomic models where agents choose consumption paths to match a target wealth distribution, and robotics where a swarm must reconfigure from one shape to another.

Exam Strategy: Recognizing Congestion and MFG Structures
When a problem imposes a hard density bound ρ ≤ 1 or a gradient constraint |∇u| ≤ 1, immediately identify it as a congested transport or planning problem. The Monge‑Ampère equation splits into two zones: an uncongested zone where det D²u = f/g holds classically, and a congested zone where the gradient constraint is active and a pressure p ≥ 0 appears. The complementarity condition p(1 − ρ) = 0 is the key to writing the weak formulation. For MFG planning problems, look for a terminal condition m₁ prescribed — this is the hallmark of the planning problem versus the standard MFG with a terminal cost. The value function at terminal time solves a Monge‑Ampère equation. In both cases, the free boundary regularity is a major research topic; exam questions typically test the formulation, the complementarity condition, and the radial symmetry reduction, not the full regularity theory.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Convex Integration: The h‑Principle for the Monge‑Ampère Equation

Convex integration, developed by Gromov (1986) and refined by Müller and Šverák (2003) for PDE, constructs wild solutions to underdetermined differential inclusions by iteratively adding highly oscillatory corrections. For the Monge‑Ampère equation det D²u = f, the differential inclusion is D²u ∈ K where K = {A ∈ Sym⁺(n) : det A = f}. When f > 0, the set K is not convex, but its convex hull contains matrices with determinant > f. The h‑principle asks whether every subsolution (a convex function u with det D²u ≥ f in the sense of measures) can be approximated by exact solutions. De Lellis, Inauen, and Székelyhidi (2018) proved that for the Monge‑Ampère equation in dimension n ≥ 2, the h‑principle holds for the differential inclusion det D²u = f with f > 0 continuous: any strictly convex subsolution can be approximated in C⁰ by strictly convex solutions. The proof uses the fact that the set of Hessians of convex functions is the cone of positive semidefinite matrices, and the determinant is a nonlinear function on this cone. The key geometric ingredient is that the set {A ∈ Sym⁺(n) : det A = 1} has a non‑trivial convex hull; indeed, its rank‑one convex hull is the whole cone Sym⁺(n). This allows the construction of laminates — probability measures supported on matrices with determinant 1 whose barycenter has determinant > 1. The convex integration scheme then produces a sequence of convex functions uₖ with det D²uₖ = f converging uniformly to a limit u with det D²u ≥ f. The solutions are highly irregular: they are C¹,α for any α < 1 but not C². This contrasts sharply with the classical regularity theory (Caffarelli, 1990) which requires f to be bounded away from zero and infinity and the domain to be convex to obtain C²,α solutions. The h‑principle shows that without the convexity of the domain or the lower bound on f, wild solutions exist. For the prescribed Gauss curvature problem on surfaces, the h‑principle yields C¹ isometric embeddings with prescribed curvature, complementing the Nash‑Kuiper theorem. The method also applies to the Monge‑Ampère equation on Riemannian manifolds, where the Hessian is replaced by the covariant Hessian. The convex integration technique has been extended to the complex Monge‑Ampère equation by Dinew and Zhang (2020), showing that on a compact Kähler manifold, any subsolution of the complex Monge‑Ampère equation can be approximated by solutions with the same cohomology class.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of Singular Solutions: The Classification of Singularities for the Monge‑Ampère Equation

Classical solutions of det D²u = f with f > 0 smooth are strictly convex and C²,α by Caffarelli's theory. However, when f vanishes or the domain is not convex, singularities appear. The classification of singularities for the Monge‑Ampère equation was initiated by Caffarelli (1990) and developed by Figalli, Kim, and McCann (2011) for the optimal transport problem. A singular point is a point where u is not C². For the Dirichlet problem on a convex domain with f ≥ 0, the singular set Σ = {x : u is not C² at x} has Hausdorff dimension at most n − 1. In dimension 2, the singular set is discrete. For the optimal transport map ∇u pushing f to g, singularities correspond to points where the transport map is not a homeomorphism. Figalli and Kim (2010) proved that if f and g are bounded away from zero and infinity on convex domains, the transport map is a homeomorphism and u is C¹. The singular set of the transport map (where it is not C¹) has dimension at most n − 1. For the Monge‑Ampère equation with right‑hand side a measure μ, the solution is an Alexandrov convex function. The singular set is where the Monge‑Ampère measure μ has a singular part with respect to Lebesgue measure. Caffarelli and McCann (2010) classified the possible blow‑up limits at singular points: they are either quadratic polynomials (regular points) or convex functions with linear growth (singular points). In the case of the prescribed Gauss curvature problem on surfaces, singularities correspond to conical singularities of the metric. The Cheng‑Yau theorem (1977) on complete affine spheres shows that entire convex solutions of det D²u = (−u)−n−2 with u < 0 are elliptic paraboloids, which are smooth. For the complex Monge‑Ampère equation on singular Kähler varieties, the singularities of the potential correspond to the singularities of the variety. The theory of non‑pluripolar products (Boucksom, Eyssidieux, Guedj, Zeriahi, 2010) allows one to define the Monge‑Ampère measure for potentials with analytic singularities. The Lelong numbers of the potential control the multiplicity of the singularities. In the context of the Minimal Model Program, the singularities of the solution to the degenerate complex Monge‑Ampère equation encode the birational geometry of the variety. The classification of singularities for the Monge‑Ampère equation remains an active area, with connections to the regularity of optimal transport maps, the geometry of affine spheres, and the birational classification of algebraic varieties.

The Monge‑Ampère Equation and the Theory of the Prescribed Gauss Curvature Problem on Surfaces with Conical Singularities

The prescribed Gauss curvature problem asks: given a function K on a surface M, find a metric g = e²ug₀ conformal to a background metric g₀ such that the Gauss curvature of g is K. The equation is −Δg₀u + K₀ = Ke²u, a semilinear elliptic equation. When M has conical singularities of angle 2πβi at points pi, the metric is g = e²u|z|²β|dz|² near pi. The curvature equation becomes a Monge‑Ampère type equation on the universal cover. For constant curvature K = −1, the equation is the Liouville equation Δu = e²u on the punctured disk with prescribed singular behavior. Troyanov (1991) proved existence of a metric with constant curvature −1 and prescribed conical singularities if and only if the Gauss‑Bonnet condition ∑(1 − βi) > 2 − 2g holds, where g is the genus. For variable curvature, the problem is more delicate. The Monge‑Ampère formulation arises when one considers the developing map of the affine sphere associated to the metric. The Cheng‑Yau theorem (1986) on complete affine hyperspheres with prescribed Gauss curvature gives a correspondence between solutions of det D²u = f on a convex domain and metrics of constant affine curvature. For surfaces with conical singularities, the affine sphere has asymptotic cones at the singular points. The classification of such affine spheres was achieved by Loftin (2001) and Labourie (2007) using the theory of Higgs bundles and the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence. The Monge‑Ampère equation det D²u = f with f having zeros or poles at the singular points captures the conical behavior. The regularity of the solution at the singular points is C¹,α but not C²; the Hessian blows up like |x|²β−1. The moduli space of such metrics is finite‑dimensional and can be compactified by adding metrics with worse singularities. This theory connects to the complex Monge‑Ampère equation on Kähler varieties with log terminal singularities, where the conical singularities correspond to the divisor in the pair (X, D). The existence of Kähler‑Einstein metrics with conical singularities along a divisor was proved by Jeffres, Mazzeo, and Rubinstein (2016) using the continuity method and the Monge‑Ampère equation with prescribed singularities. The linearized operator is the Laplacian with respect to the singular metric, and its spectrum determines the stability of the solution. The prescribed Gauss curvature problem with conical singularities thus provides a rich interplay between the Monge‑Ampère equation, affine geometry, and algebraic geometry.
