Monge-Ampère Equation — Elliptic PDE, Convex Geometry & the Brunn-Minkowski Theory

The Monge-Ampère equation det(D²u) = f(x, u, Du) is a fully nonlinear elliptic PDE of central importance across pure and applied mathematics. It governs the shape of convex surfaces with prescribed Gauss curvature (the Minkowski problem), the existence of Kähler-Einstein metrics in complex geometry (Yau's proof of the Calabi conjecture), and the optimal transport map between probability measures (Brenier's theorem). The theory of weak solutions — Alexandrov solutions for convex functions — combined with the powerful regularity estimates of Caffarelli (C^{1,α} and C^{2,α}) forms the analytic backbone. The Brunn-Minkowski inequality, a cornerstone of convex geometry, is intimately related to the Monge-Ampère equation through the Minkowski problem and the theory of mixed volumes. Applications span reflector antenna design, image registration, mesh generation, meteorology (the semi-geostrophic equations), and fluid dynamics.

"The Monge-Ampère equation is the Rosetta stone of fully nonlinear elliptic PDE."
— Luis Caffarelli
Part 1

📏 The Classical Monge-Ampère Equation and Convexity

Classical Theory
The Dirichlet Problem, Convexity, and the Monge-Ampère Measure
The classical Monge-Ampère equation is det(D²u) = f in Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ with u = 0 on ∂Ω, where D²u denotes the Hessian matrix of u. The equation is elliptic precisely when f > 0 and D²u > 0 (u is strictly convex). The equation can be rewritten as the Jacobian determinant of the gradient map: det(D²u) = det(∇(∇u)) — so the map x ↦ ∇u(x) is locally measure-preserving with density f. For a convex function u, the normal mapping (or subgradient) ∂u(E) = {y ∈ ℝⁿ : y = ∇u(x) for some x ∈ E} defines the Monge-Ampère measure μ_u(E) = |∂u(E)|, the Lebesgue measure of the image of E under the normal mapping. This measure is a powerful tool that allows defining weak (Alexandrov) solutions for merely convex functions. The Dirichlet problem on a smooth, strictly convex domain Ω with f > 0 and f ∈ C^{∞}(Ω) has a unique smooth convex solution — this was established by the work of Pogorelov, Cheng-Yau, and later Caffarelli-Nirenberg-Spruck using the continuity method and a priori estimates. The key a priori estimates are: (1) the C⁰ estimate via the Alexandrov maximum principle, (2) the C¹ estimate via the convexity, and (3) the C² estimate via the Pogorelov and Evans-Krylov theories.
The Monge-Ampère equation is the geometric PDE whose ellipticity is equivalent to strict convexity; the Monge-Ampère measure provides a notion of weak solution for arbitrary convex functions.
Part 2

🌍 The Minkowski Problem and the Brunn-Minkowski Theory

Convex Geometry
The Minkowski Problem, Brunn-Minkowski Inequality, and Mixed Volumes
The Minkowski problem is the foundational inverse problem in convex geometry: given a positive function K on the unit sphere S^{n-1} (prescribed Gauss curvature), find a convex body K ⊂ ℝⁿ whose Gauss curvature at the point with outward normal ν is K(ν). Via the support function h of the convex body, this reduces to solving the spherical Monge-Ampère equation: det(∇²h + h·I) = K on S^{n-1}, where ∇ is the covariant derivative on the sphere. The Brunn-Minkowski inequality is the central inequality of convex geometry: for convex bodies A, B ⊂ ℝⁿ, Vol((1−t)A + tB)^{1/n} ≥ (1−t)Vol(A)^{1/n} + t·Vol(B)^{1/n}, with equality iff A and B are homothetic. This inequality is equivalent to the Prékopa-Leindler inequality (a functional form) and is intimately connected to the isoperimetric inequality. The Aleksandrov-Fenchel inequality generalizes Brunn-Minkowski to mixed volumes: V(K₁, K₂, …, K_n)² ≥ V(K₁, K₁, K₃, …, K_n)·V(K₂, K₂, K₃, …, K_n). The Minkowski problem was solved independently by Aleksandrov (using the Brunn-Minkowski inequality and a variational argument) for arbitrary convex bodies, and by Nirenberg, Pogorelov, and Cheng-Yau (using PDE methods — the spherical Monge-Ampère equation) for smooth solutions. The Lp Minkowski problem (Lutwak) and the Monge-Ampère equation for convex bodies with prescribed curvature measures are active modern generalizations.
The Brunn-Minkowski inequality is the backbone of convex geometry; its equivalence with the Minkowski problem demonstrates the deep interplay between geometry and fully nonlinear PDE.
Part 3

🧮 Regularity Theory and Caffarelli's Estimates

Regularity
Alexandrov Maximum Principle, Pogorelov Estimates, and Caffarelli's C^{1,α} / C^{2,α} Theory
The regularity theory for the Monge-Ampère equation is one of the crowning achievements of fully nonlinear elliptic PDE. The Alexandrov maximum principle provides the foundational C⁰ estimate: for a convex solution u of det(D²u) = f on Ω with u = 0 on ∂Ω, we have sup_Ω |u| ≤ C·diam(Ω)·(∫_Ω f)^{1/n}. The Pogorelov interior C^{1,1} estimate asserts that any convex solution has bounded second derivatives in the interior — specifically, |D²u| ≤ C·sup|u|/d² where d is the distance to the boundary. Caffarelli's regularity theory (1980s) revolutionized the field: (1) if f is continuous and positive, then u ∈ C^{1,α}_{loc} for some α ∈ (0,1) — this is proved via a Harnack inequality and a compactness argument using the affine invariance of the equation; (2) if f ∈ C^{0,α}, then u ∈ C^{2,α}_{loc} — this adapts the Evans-Krylov theory to the Monge-Ampère equation. The C^{2,α} estimate takes the form |u|_{C^{2,α}} ≤ C(Ω, f, |u|_{L^∞}). Boundary regularity (Caffarelli's boundary Harnack) shows that if ∂Ω is smooth and f is smooth, then u is smooth up to the boundary. A striking counterexample is Pogorelov's singular solution: u(x', x_n) = (1 − |x'|²)·x_n^{2(n−1)/n} for x_n > 0, which is convex and satisfies det(D²u) = C·x_n^{−2} and has a line of singularities along x_n = 0. This shows that interior C² regularity is optimal in general.
Caffarelli's estimates provide optimal Hölder regularity for the Monge-Ampère equation under minimal assumptions — a landmark in fully nonlinear elliptic PDE theory.
Part 4

🔥 The Complex Monge-Ampère Equation and Calabi-Yau Manifolds

Complex & Applied
Calabi-Yau Manifolds, Optimal Transport, and Applications
On a compact Kähler manifold (M, ω), the complex Monge-Ampère equation is (ω + i∂∂̄φ)ⁿ = e^{F} ωⁿ, where ω is a Kähler form, φ is a smooth function, and F is prescribed. This equation is the key to the Calabi conjecture (proved by Yau, 1977): given a smooth real (1,1)-form representing 2πc₁(M), there exists a unique Kähler metric in a given cohomology class with that prescribed Ricci form. When c₁(M) = 0, this yields a Ricci-flat Kähler metric — a Calabi-Yau manifold. The proof uses the continuity method and the three key estimates: the C⁰ estimate (Moser iteration and the Alexandrov-Bakelman-Pucci maximum principle), the C² estimate (the Chern-Lu inequality and the Aubin-Yau estimate bounding the Laplacian), and higher regularity (elliptic bootstrapping once C^{2,α} is obtained). In optimal transport, Brenier's theorem states that given two absolutely continuous probability measures μ, ν on ℝⁿ with finite second moments, there exists a convex function u such that ∇u pushes μ forward to ν. The map u solves the Monge-Ampère equation det(D²u) = μ/ν∘∇u — the density ratio of the two measures. Applications include: reflector antenna design (far-field radiation pattern via Monge-Ampère on the sphere), image registration (warping one image to another), mesh generation (generating meshes with prescribed density), and the semi-geostrophic equations in meteorology (where the geopotential satisfies a time-dependent Monge-Ampère equation).
The complex Monge-Ampère equation solved the Calabi conjecture and birthed Calabi-Yau geometry; in the applied realm, optimal transport theory provides a Monge-Ampère framework for numerous engineering and scientific problems.

The Legendre Transform as the Geometric Soul of Monge-Ampère

To truly master the Monge-Ampère equation, one must look beyond the determinant formula det(D²u) = f and understand the underlying geometric mechanism that makes it solvable. The central engine driving this theory is the Legendre transform, which acts as a bridge between the graph of a function in physical space and its dual representation in momentum (or frequency) space. For a strictly convex function u(x), the Legendre transform v(p) is defined by the relation p = ∇u(x) and v(p) = x·p - u(x). This transformation maps the graph of u to its tangent hyperplanes. The profound insight, first rigorously exploited in the context of convex bodies by Aleksandrov, is that the Monge-Ampère equation can be interpreted as a conservation law for volume forms under this mapping.

Consider the change of variables from x to p = ∇u(x). The Jacobian determinant of this transformation is precisely det(D²u). Therefore, the equation det(D²u) = f becomes a simple volume preservation statement: the volume element in the x-space (weighted by f) maps exactly to the volume element in the p-space. If we interpret the Hessian determinant as the ratio of volumes, the equation asserts that the mapping is a diffeomorphism that preserves a specific density structure. This perspective explains why boundary data is so critical: if the map ∇u fails to be injective (i.e., if the solution develops a singularity or folds), the volume conservation breaks down, and the classical equation ceases to hold in the standard sense.

This geometric view clarifies the distinction between elliptic and hyperbolic regimes. The Monge-Ampère operator is elliptic only when the Hessian matrix D²u is positive definite (or negative definite). Geometrically, this means the surface must be strictly convex everywhere; if the curvature vanishes or changes sign, the Legendre transform becomes ill-defined or multi-valued. This constraint forces solutions to live in the realm of convex geometry. The "ellipticity" condition is not merely an analytic technicality but a geometric necessity for the map ∇u to be a bijection between the domain and its image.

Historically, the reliance on the Legendre transform shifted the approach from purely PDE-based methods to convex-analytic ones. While classical calculus of variations (Euler-Lagrange) seeks critical points of functionals, the Monge-Ampère equation is often viewed as an Euler-Lagrange equation for a functional involving the determinant of the Hessian. However, unlike standard variational problems where one minimizes energy, here we are often solving an inverse problem: given the target volume distribution (the right-hand side f), what shape u produces it? The Legendre transform provides the dictionary to translate this shape into a dual space where the equation might simplify or reveal symmetries.

In modern optimal transport theory, this connection is absolute. Brenier's theorem relies entirely on the convexity of the potential function u. If u were not convex, the map ∇u would not be monotone, and the push-forward of the measure would not be unique or well-defined. Thus, the Legendre transform is not just a tool; it is the structural skeleton upon which the entire theory rests. Understanding this allows an examiner to twist questions by asking about non-convex solutions (where viscosity solutions must be introduced) or by considering degenerate cases where the determinant vanishes.

The Geometric Interpretation of the Jacobian
Question: Let u(x,y) be a strictly convex function on the unit disk such that det(D²u) = 1. Explain geometrically why the graph of u must touch every tangent plane exactly once, and how this relates to the Legendre transform.
Solution:
The condition det(D²u) = 1 implies that the mapping from the spatial coordinates (x,y) to the gradient coordinates (p,q) = (∂u/∂x, ∂u/∂y) has a Jacobian determinant of 1. Geometrically, this means that an infinitesimal area element dxdy in the domain is mapped to an equal area element dpdq in the image space. Since the function is strictly convex, the gradient map is injective (one-to-one). Therefore, every point in the range of gradients corresponds to exactly one point on the graph of u. Consequently, the tangent plane at any point on the graph is unique and does not intersect the graph again. This global injectivity is guaranteed by the strict convexity and the volume-preserving nature of the gradient map. If the determinant were zero at some point, the mapping would collapse dimensions, violating the volume preservation unless compensated elsewhere, which contradicts the strict positivity required for ellipticity in the classical sense.
Exam Strategy: The Convexity Check
Tip: Whenever you encounter a Monge-Ampère problem, immediately check the convexity of the function. If the question asks for a solution where u is not convex, remember that you are likely dealing with a "viscosity solution" or a weak formulation where the classical determinant definition fails. In competitive exams, if asked to prove uniqueness, always invoke the maximum principle or the Alexandrov-Bakelman-Pucci estimate, which rely fundamentally on the convexity structure derived from the Legendre transform logic.

The Calabi Conjecture: From Kähler Geometry to Ricci-Flat Metrics

While the real Monge-Ampère equation governs optimal transport and convex bodies, its complex counterpart revolutionized algebraic geometry through the solution of the Calabi conjecture. Proposed by Eugenio Calabi in 1954, the conjecture addressed the relationship between the Ricci curvature of a Kähler manifold and its underlying cohomology class. Specifically, Calabi asked whether a compact Kähler manifold (M, ω) admits a unique Kähler metric within a fixed cohomology class [ω] such that its Ricci form is a prescribed closed real (1,1)-form representing the first Chern class c₁(M).

The equation governing this problem is the complex Monge-Ampère equation:
(ω + i∂∂̄φ)ⁿ = eᶠ ωⁿ
where n is the complex dimension of the manifold. Here, ω is the background Kähler form, φ is the unknown real-valued potential function (the "twist" to the metric), and F encodes the prescribed Ricci curvature data. The term (ω + i∂∂̄φ)ⁿ represents the volume form of the new metric, while eᶠ ωⁿ represents the target volume form weighted by the curvature function.

The proof of this conjecture was achieved by Shing-Tung Yau in 1977, a landmark achievement that earned him the Fields Medal. The proof is a masterpiece of geometric analysis, combining deep algebraic topology with powerful analytic estimates. The strategy involves three critical steps: first, establishing a C⁰ estimate (a priori bound on the function φ) using Moser iteration and the maximum principle; second, deriving a C² estimate via the Chern-Lu inequality and the Aubin-Yau estimate, which bounds the Laplacian of φ; and third, applying elliptic bootstrapping to achieve smoothness. The uniqueness follows from the strict convexity of the operator in the space of potentials.

The significance of this result extends far beyond pure mathematics. When the first Chern class c₁(M) = 0, the solution yields a Ricci-flat Kähler metric. Such manifolds are now known as Calabi-Yau manifolds. They play a pivotal role in string theory, where they provide the compactification dimensions necessary for the theory to be consistent with observed physics. The geometry of these spaces is rigid yet rich, determined entirely by their complex structure and the cohomology class of the Kähler form.
