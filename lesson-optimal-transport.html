Optimal Transport — Wasserstein Distance & the Monge–Ampère Equation

Deep dive into Optimal Transport — the problem of moving mass from one distribution to another at minimal cost, originating with Gaspard Monge (1781) and revolutionized by Leonid Kantorovich (1942). Topics covered include the Monge problem (find a transport map T: X → Y minimizing ∫ c(x, T(x)) dμ(x)), the Kantorovich relaxation (a linear program on the space of couplings Π(μ, ν) minimizing ∫ c dπ), the Kantorovich duality (the sup over pairs (φ, ψ) of ∫ φ dμ + ∫ ψ dν with φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x, y)), the Wasserstein metric W_p(μ, ν) = (inf_{π∈Π(μ,ν)} ∫ |x − y|^p dπ)^{1/p} and its geometry on the space of probability measures, the Brenier theorem (for c(x, y) = |x − y|², the optimal transport map is the gradient of a convex function: T = ∇φ), the Monge–Ampère equation that the optimal map satisfies (det(D²φ) · ρ_ν(∇φ) = ρ_μ), the displacement convexity (interpolation along geodesics in the Wasserstein space preserves convex functionals), the Otto calculus (the Riemannian structure of the Wasserstein space where the metric tensor is the L² inner product on the space of potentials), the Benamou–Brenier formula (W₂²(μ, ν) = inf ∫ |v_t|² dμ_t dt over continuity equations ∂_t μ_t + ∇·(v_t μ_t) = 0), the connection to Ricci curvature via the Bakry–Émery criterion and the CD(K,N) condition of Lott–Sturm–Villani, and the dynamic formulation using the Hamilton–Jacobi equation.

"Optimal transport is the geometry of mass transference; it unifies analysis, geometry, and probability."

Part 1

📦 The Monge and Kantorovich Problems

Formulations
Monge's Problem, Kantorovich Relaxation, and Kantorovich Duality
The Monge problem (1781): given two probability measures μ on X and ν on Y and a cost function c: X × Y → [0, ∞], find a measurable transport map T: X → Y pushing μ forward to ν (T_# μ = ν) that minimizes the total cost ∫ c(x, T(x)) dμ(x). This is a non-convex problem — the existence of a map is not guaranteed (e.g., when μ is a Dirac mass). The Kantorovich relaxation (1942): replace maps with couplings π ∈ Π(μ, ν), which are joint probability measures on X × Y with marginals μ and ν. The problem becomes linear: minimize ∫ c dπ over π ∈ Π(μ, ν). This always has a solution when c is lower semi-continuous and bounded below. The Kantorovich duality: inf_{π∈Π(μ,ν)} ∫ c dπ = sup_{(φ,ψ) ∈ Φ_c} ∫ φ dμ + ∫ ψ dν, where Φ_c = {(φ, ψ) ∈ L¹(dμ) × L¹(dν) : φ(x) + ψ(y) ≤ c(x, y)}. The maximizing pair (φ, ψ) are the Kantorovich potentials. For c(x, y) = d(x, y) on a metric space, the dual gives the Kantorovich–Rubinstein norm: W₁(μ, ν) = sup_{‖f‖_{Lip} ≤ 1} ∫ f d(μ − ν). The cyclical monotonicity condition characterizes optimal couplings: a coupling π is optimal iff its support is c-cyclically monotone — for any finite set of points (x_i, y_i) in supp(π), Σ c(x_i, y_i) ≤ Σ c(x_{i+1}, y_i) (with x_{n+1} = x_1). The c-transform: the optimal potential ψ can be chosen as ψ(y) = inf_{x ∈ X} c(x, y) − φ(x), the c-conjugate of φ, and similarly φ = ψ^c. This gives a fixed-point characterization: φ = (φ^c)^c.
The Kantorovich relaxation turns the non-convex Monge problem into a linear program on couplings, with a dual formulation in terms of Lipschitz functions — the beginning of a rich duality theory that connects to PDEs, geometry, and probability.
Part 2

🌊 Wasserstein Geometry and the Brenier Theorem

Geometry
The Wasserstein Metric, the Brenier Theorem, and the Monge–Ampère Equation
The Wasserstein p-metric W_p(μ, ν) = (inf_{π∈Π(μ,ν)} ∫ |x − y|^p dπ)^{1/p} for p ≥ 1 metrizes weak convergence (narrow convergence) on the space P_p(X) of probability measures with finite p-th moment. The space (P₂(ℝⁿ), W₂) is a geodesic space: the geodesic μ_t interpolating μ₀ and μ₁ is given by μ_t = (T_t)_# μ₀ where T_t = (1−t)Id + tT with T = ∇φ the optimal map. The Brenier theorem (1991): for μ, ν ∈ P₂(ℝⁿ) with μ absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, there exists a unique optimal transport map T = ∇φ for the quadratic cost c(x, y) = |x − y|², where φ: ℝⁿ → ℝ is a convex function. The pushforward satisfies (∇φ)_# μ = ν. The optimal plan π = (Id, ∇φ)_# μ is uniquely determined. The Monge–Ampère equation: if μ = ρ_μ dx and ν = ρ_ν dy, then ∇φ satisfies det(D²φ(x)) · ρ_ν(∇φ(x)) = ρ_μ(x) for μ-a.e. x. This is a nonlinear second-order PDE of Monge–Ampère type. The Alexandrov theorem: a convex function φ is twice differentiable almost everywhere, so D²φ exists a.e. The Benamou–Brenier formula (2000): W₂²(μ₀, μ₁) = inf ∫₀¹ ∫ |v_t|² dμ_t dt where the infimum is over all curves μ_t of probability measures and vector fields v_t satisfying the continuity equation ∂_t μ_t + ∇·(v_t μ_t) = 0 with μ₀, μ₁ fixed. This gives the dynamic formulation of optimal transport — the optimal velocity field is a gradient: v_t = ∇φ_t for some potential φ_t solving the Hamilton–Jacobi equation ∂_t φ_t + ½|∇φ_t|² = 0.
The Brenier theorem provides an explicit construction of the optimal transport map as the gradient of a convex function, linking optimal transport to the Monge–Ampère equation and giving the Wasserstein space a rich geometric and analytic structure.
Part 3

📈 Displacement Convexity and Ricci Curvature

Curvature
Displacement Convexity, the Bakry–Émery Criterion, and the CD(K,N) Condition
Displacement convexity (McCann, 1997): a functional F on P₂(ℝⁿ) is displacement convex if it is convex along Wasserstein geodesics: F(μ_t) ≤ (1−t)F(μ₀) + tF(μ₁). The internal energy U_ν(μ) = ∫ U(dμ/dν) dν for a convex function U is displacement convex on ℝⁿ (a result of the Brenier theorem and the convexity of U). In particular, the Boltzmann–Shannon entropy H(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ dx is displacement convex on ℝⁿ. The Bakry–Émery criterion: on a Riemannian manifold M, the entropy H(μ) = ∫ ρ log ρ vol is displacement convex iff Ric ≥ 0. More precisely, for a Riemannian manifold with Ricci curvature bounded below by K ∈ ℝ, the entropy along a Wasserstein geodesic satisfies: H(μ_t) ≤ (1−t)H(μ₀) + tH(μ₁) − (K/2) t(1−t) W₂²(μ₀, μ₁) — the K-convexity of entropy on the Wasserstein space. This leads to the Lott–Sturm–Villani CD(K,N) condition: a metric measure space (X, d, m) satisfies CD(K,N) if the Rényi entropy S_N(μ) = −∫ ρ^{1−1/N} dm is K-convex along W₂-geodesics in P₂(X, m). Here K is the lower Ricci curvature bound and N is the upper dimension bound. The CD(K,∞) condition: for any two μ₀, μ₁ ∈ P₂(X) absolutely continuous w.r.t. m, there exists a geodesic μ_t such that H(μ_t) ≤ (1−t)H(μ₀) + tH(μ₁) − (K/2) t(1−t) W₂²(μ₀, μ₁). The Sturm theorem (2006): the CD(K,N) condition is stable under Gromov–Hausdorff convergence and tensorizes (a product of CD(K,N) spaces is CD(K,N₁+N₂)). The Brunn–Minkowski inequality on manifolds is a consequence of CD(K,N). The Bishop–Gromov volume comparison also follows from CD(K,N).
Displacement convexity of entropy in the Wasserstein space characterizes lower Ricci curvature bounds — this is the heart of the Lott–Sturm–Villani synthetic approach to Ricci curvature for metric measure spaces.
Part 4

🔬 The Otto Calculus and Synthetic Ricci Curvature

Calculus
The Otto Calculus on the Wasserstein Space and the RCD Condition
The Otto calculus (Otto, 2001): the Wasserstein space (P₂(M), W₂) can be viewed as a Riemannian manifold of infinite dimension. The tangent space at a smooth density ρ is T_ρ P₂ = {∇φ : φ ∈ C∞(M)} — the space of gradient vector fields. The Riemannian metric at ρ: ⟨∇φ, ∇ψ⟩_ρ = ∫ ∇φ·∇ψ dρ. The geodesic equation becomes the continuity equation ∂_t ρ_t + ∇·(ρ_t ∇φ_t) = 0 combined with the Hamilton–Jacobi equation ∂_t φ_t + ½|∇φ_t|² = 0, which together imply ∂_t² ρ_t = ∇·(ρ_t ∇(Δ φ_t + ½|∇φ_t|²)). The Ricci curvature of the Wasserstein space appears in the evolution of the Laplacian of the entropy. The Bakry–Émery operator Γ₂(f) = ½Δ|∇f|² − ⟨∇f, ∇Δf⟩ = Ric(∇f, ∇f) + ‖Hess f‖² gives the Γ₂-criterion: Ric ≥ K iff Γ₂(f) ≥ K|∇f|² for all smooth f. The RCD(K,N) condition (Gigli–Kuwada–Ohta–Ambrosio–Tresoldi): a refinement of CD(K,N) that also requires the space to be infinitesimally Hilbertian — the Sobolev space W^{1,2}(X) is a Hilbert space. This eliminates Finsler-like behavior (e.g., the CD condition on ℓ∞ spaces) and restricts to genuinely Riemannian metric measure spaces. The RCD condition is stable under tensorization, Gromov–Hausdorff convergence, and measured Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. Splitting theorem (Gigli): an RCD(0,N) space with a line splits as a product ℝ × Y. Rectifiability (Mondino–Naber): RCD(K,N) spaces are rectifiable — they have a regular set of full measure that is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to Euclidean space. The Brenier theorem on manifolds (McCann): on a compact Riemannian manifold, the optimal map for c(x, y) = d(x, y)²/2 is given by T(x) = exp_x(∇_x φ(x)) for a d²/2-concave function φ — the McCann interpolation along geodesics. The program of synthetic Ricci curvature continues to flourish: the c-convexity approach of Gozlan–Roberto–Samson–Tetali and the entropy-cost inequality framework.
The Otto calculus gives a formal Riemannian structure to the Wasserstein space, making precise the connection between optimal transport, Ricci curvature, and the synthetic geometry of metric measure spaces in the RCD framework.
