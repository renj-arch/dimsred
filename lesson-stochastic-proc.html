Stochastic Processes — Randomness Through Time

Stochastic processes model random systems evolving in time. From the jitter of a pollen grain (Brownian motion) to the rise and fall of stock prices, stochastic processes are the mathematical language of randomness with memory. The field covers Markov chains (discrete), martingales (fair games), Brownian motion (continuous limit), Itô calculus (stochastic integration), Lévy processes (jumps), and ergodic theory (long-run behavior).

"Stochastic processes are the calculus of uncertainty — they quantify how randomness unfolds."
— Joseph Doob (paraphrased)
Part 1

🎲 Markov Chains — Memoryless Evolution

Memoryless
Discrete-Time Markov Chains — Transition Matrices and Stationary Distributions
A Markov chain is a sequence X₀, X₁, X₂,... where P(X_{n+1}=j | X_n=i, past) = P_{ij} (the Markov property: the future depends only on the present). The transition matrix P = (P_{ij}) is stochastic: row sums = 1. The n-step transition matrix is Pⁿ. A distribution π is stationary if πP = π. For finite irreducible aperiodic chains, π is unique and Pⁿ → 1π as n→∞ (convergence to equilibrium). The detailed balance condition π_i P_{ij} = π_j P_{ji} (reversibility) is sufficient for stationarity. Absorbing Markov chains have absorbing states; the fundamental matrix (I−Q)^{-1} gives expected absorption times. Continuous-time Markov chains have a rate matrix Q with P(t) = e^{Qt}.
Markov chains are the simplest models of sequential randomness — the future depends only on the present, not the past.
Part 2

💰 Martingales — Fair Games and Stopping Times

Fair Games
Martingales — Processes That Are Constant on Average
A martingale is a sequence M_n where E[M_{n+1} | ℱ_n] = M_n — the best prediction of the future is the present (a fair game). The optional stopping theorem (OST): if τ is a stopping time with bounded time or integrability conditions, E[M_τ] = E[M_0]. The Doob decomposition: any adapted integrable process decomposes uniquely as a martingale plus a predictable process. Doob's martingale inequalities: P(max_{k≤n} |M_k| ≥ λ) ≤ E[|M_n|]/λ (maximal inequality) and E[max_{k≤n} M_k²] ≤ 4E[M_n²] (L² inequality). Martingale convergence theorem: if sup_n E[|M_n|] < ∞, then M_n converges a.s. to a limit M_∞. Branching processes (Galton-Watson): the population size Z_n forms a martingale after normalization — extinction probability is the smallest fixed point of the generating function.
Martingales are the backbone of stochastic analysis — they model fair games and converge under mild conditions.
Part 3

🌊 Brownian Motion and Itô Calculus

Continuous Noise
Brownian Motion — The Universal Noise Process
Brownian motion B_t (Wiener process) is a continuous process with independent increments B_t−B_s ~ N(0, t−s). It's nowhere differentiable (paths have fractal dimension 1.5). The quadratic variation [B]_t = t — unlike smooth functions, Brownian motion accumulates finite quadratic variation. Itô's lemma (the stochastic chain rule): for dX = μ dt + σ dB and f ∈ C², df(X) = (f'μ + ½ f''σ²) dt + f'σ dB. The extra ½ f''σ² dt term is the Itô correction coming from quadratic variation. The Black-Scholes equation (∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²∂²V/∂S² + rS∂V/∂S − rV = 0) for option pricing follows from Itô's lemma and a hedging argument. The Girsanov theorem changes the drift of a Brownian motion via an exponential martingale — it's the basis of risk-neutral pricing. Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) dX = μ(X,t)dt + σ(X,t)dB have solutions under Lipschitz conditions.
Itô calculus is the calculus of random noise — it revolutionized finance, physics, and every field with continuous randomness.
Part 4

⚡ Lévy Processes, Ergodic Theory, and Advanced Topics

Jumps and Invariance
Lévy Processes — Adding Jumps to Brownian Motion
A Lévy process has stationary independent increments with càdlàg paths. By the Lévy-Khintchine formula, the characteristic exponent ψ(θ) = log E[e^{iθX_1}] = iμθ − ½σ²θ² + ∫_{ℝ\{0}} (e^{iθx}−1−iθx1_{|x|<1}) ν(dx), where ν is the Lévy measure controlling jumps. The compound Poisson process models random jumps at Poisson times. Stable processes are Lévy processes with power-law tails (α-stable: P(|X_t| > x) ~ cx^{-α}). Ergodic theory studies the long-run behavior of dynamical systems. Birkhoff's ergodic theorem: for a measure-preserving transformation T on a probability space, (1/n) Σ_{k=0}^{n−1} f(T^k x) → E[f | ℐ] a.s., where ℐ is the σ-algebra of invariant sets. Mixing (strong mixing, α-mixing) quantifies how quickly dependence decays. Malliavin calculus is a differential calculus on Wiener space, enabling smoothness of densities for SDE solutions. Rough path theory (Lyons) provides a pathwise approach to SDEs using higher-order iterated integrals.
Lévy processes extend Brownian motion to include jumps; ergodic theory explains why time averages converge to space averages.

Filtrations, Adapted Processes, and the Usual Conditions

A filtration (ℱₜ)ₜ≥0 on a probability space (Ω, ℱ, ℙ) is an increasing family of σ‑algebras ℱₛ ⊆ ℱₜ for s ≤ t. A stochastic process X = (Xₜ) is adapted if Xₜ is ℱₜ‑measurable for every t. The usual conditions require (i) ℱ₀ contains all ℙ‑null sets and (ii) the filtration is right‑continuous: ℱₜ = ⋂s>t ℱₛ. These conditions guarantee the existence of càdlàg modifications for martingales and allow the definition of stopping times τ with the property {τ ≤ t} ∈ ℱₜ. The augmented filtration ℱₜ⁺ = σ(ℱₜ ∪ 𝒩) where 𝒩 are the ℙ‑null sets, together with right‑continuity, yields the completed, right‑continuous filtration that is standard in stochastic calculus. Without the usual conditions, optional stopping and the martingale representation theorem can fail; for instance, a martingale may lack a càdlàg version and the stochastic integral ∫₀ᵗ Hₛ dMₛ may be ill‑defined for predictable integrands H. In practice, one always works with the usual augmentation of the natural filtration of a Brownian motion or a Lévy process.

Stochastic Integration Beyond Itô: Stratonovich, Rough Paths, and Applications

The Itô integral ∫₀ᵗ Hₛ dBₛ is defined for predictable integrands and satisfies the Itô isometry E[(∫₀ᵗ Hₛ dBₛ)²] = E[∫₀ᵗ Hₛ² ds]. The Stratonovich integral ⨍₀ᵗ Hₛ ∘ dBₛ is defined as the limit in probability of Riemann sums with the integrand evaluated at the midpoint of each subinterval. For a C² function f, the Stratonovich chain rule reads df(Xₜ) = f'(Xₜ) ∘ dXₜ, i.e. no second‑order correction term appears. The conversion formula between the two integrals is ⨍₀ᵗ Hₛ ∘ dBₛ = ∫₀ᵗ Hₛ dBₛ + ½[H, B]ₜ, where [H, B] is the quadratic covariation. In physical modelling, Stratonovich calculus preserves the classical chain rule and is natural for systems with coloured noise approximated by white noise (Wong–Zakai theorem). Rough path theory (Lyons, 1998) lifts a path X to a rough path 𝐗 = (X, 𝑿) where 𝑿 encodes iterated integrals ∫ X ⊗ dX. The solution map of a differential equation driven by a rough path is continuous in the p‑variation topology, allowing pathwise solutions for signals with Hölder regularity > 1/3, including fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H > 1/4. This framework underpins modern numerical schemes such as the log‑ODE method for SDEs on manifolds and provides a deterministic interpretation of stochastic integrals without probability.

Conversion Between Itô and Stratonovich Integrals
Let B be a standard Brownian motion and consider the Stratonovich integral ⨍₀ᵗ Bₛ ∘ dBₛ. Compute its Itô representation and evaluate its expectation.
Using the conversion formula ⨍₀ᵗ Hₛ ∘ dBₛ = ∫₀ᵗ Hₛ dBₛ + ½[H, B]ₜ with Hₛ = Bₛ. The quadratic covariation [B, B]ₜ = t, so ½[H, B]ₜ = ½ t. Hence ⨍₀ᵗ Bₛ ∘ dBₛ = ∫₀ᵗ Bₛ dBₛ + ½ t. By Itô's lemma, ∫₀ᵗ Bₛ dBₛ = ½ Bₜ² − ½ t. Substituting gives ⨍₀ᵗ Bₛ ∘ dBₛ = ½ Bₜ². The expectation E[½ Bₜ²] = ½ t, which matches the known mean of the Stratonovich integral.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the Stratonovich integral of a polynomial in Brownian motion, first write the Itô integral using Itô's lemma, then add the ½ quadratic covariation correction. Remember that the Stratonovich integral of a martingale is not a martingale unless the integrand is deterministic; this distinction is a frequent trap in multiple‑choice items.

Girsanov Theorem and Change of Measure in Finance and Filtering

The Girsanov theorem provides a rigorous method to change the probability measure so that a Brownian motion with drift becomes a standard Brownian motion under the new measure. Let (Ω, ℱ, (ℱₜ), ℙ) satisfy the usual conditions and let B be a ℙ‑Brownian motion. For an adapted process θ satisfying Novikov's condition E[exp(½∫₀ᵀ θₛ² ds)] < ∞, define the exponential martingale Zₜ = exp(∫₀ᵗ θₛ dBₛ − ½∫₀ᵗ θₛ² ds). Then the measure ℚ defined by dℚ/dℙ|ℱₜ = Zₜ is a probability measure and the process B̃ₜ = Bₜ − ∫₀ᵗ θₛ ds is a ℚ‑Brownian motion. In mathematical finance, θ = (μ − r)/σ transforms the physical dynamics dS = μ S dt + σ S dB into the risk‑neutral dynamics dS = r S dt + σ S dB̃, enabling arbitrage‑free pricing via expectation under ℚ. In nonlinear filtering, the Kallianpur–Striebel formula expresses the conditional law of a signal given observations as a ratio of expectations under a reference measure where the observation noise is a standard Brownian motion; Girsanov's theorem supplies the Radon–Nikodym derivative linking the physical and reference measures. The theorem extends to Lévy processes: if X is a Lévy process with characteristic triplet (b, c, ν) under ℙ, a change of measure with a suitable density modifies the drift, the Gaussian coefficient, and the Lévy measure according to the Esscher transform.

Spectral Theory of Markov Operators and Mixing Times

For a reversible Markov chain with transition matrix P and stationary distribution π, the operator P acts on L²(π) as a self‑adjoint contraction. Its spectrum lies in [−1, 1]; the eigenvalue 1 is simple and corresponds to the constant eigenfunction. The spectral gap γ = 1 − λ₂, where λ₂ is the second largest eigenvalue in absolute value, controls the exponential convergence rate: ‖Pⁿ(x,·) − π‖TV ≤ ½ √(π(x)⁻¹) (1−γ)ⁿ. For non‑reversible chains, one studies the singular values of P or the additive reversiblization (P + P*)/2. Mixing time tmix(ε) = min{n : max_x ‖Pⁿ(x,·) − π‖TV ≤ ε} satisfies tmix(ε) ≤ (1/γ) log(1/(ε πmin)). The cutoff phenomenon occurs when the total variation distance drops from near 1 to near 0 in a window of order smaller than the mixing time; classic examples include the random transpositions shuffle (Diaconis–Shahshahani, 1981) and the Ehrenfest urn. Coupling provides a probabilistic bound: if a coupling (Xₙ, Yₙ) meets with probability 1, then ‖Pⁿ(x,·) − π‖TV ≤ ℙ(Xₙ ≠ Yₙ). The path coupling technique (Bubley–Dyer, 1997) reduces the analysis to neighbouring states, yielding polynomial mixing bounds for Glauber dynamics on colourings and the Ising model at high temperature.

Stochastic Partial Differential Equations: Existence, Uniqueness, and Numerical Approximation

A stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) typically takes the form ∂ₜ u = A u + F(u) + G(u) ξ, where A is a differential operator (e.g. the Laplacian Δ), ξ is space‑time white noise, and the solution u(t,x) is a random field. The Walsh integral defines ∫₀ᵗ ∫_D φ(s,x) W(ds,dx) for predictable integrands φ against a martingale measure W. For the stochastic heat equation ∂ₜ u = Δ u + ξ on a bounded domain with Dirichlet boundary conditions, the mild solution is u(t) = ∫₀ᵗ S(t−s) dW(s) where S(t) = etΔ is the analytic semigroup generated by Δ. Existence and uniqueness hold in the space of predictable processes with values in L²(Ω; H-α) for α > d/2 − 1 (d spatial dimension). In one dimension, the solution is function‑valued and Hölder continuous in space with exponent < ½. Numerical approximation uses finite element discretisation in space and Euler–Maruyama in time; the strong error satisfies E[‖u(t_n) − u_h^n‖L²] ≤ C (h1−ε + Δt½−ε) for any ε > 0. For semilinear SPDEs with globally Lipschitz nonlinearities, the same rates persist. The Malliavin calculus on Wiener space yields smoothness of the law of u(t,x) and enables density estimates for the solution at a fixed point.

Malliavin Calculus and the Integration by Parts Formula

Malliavin calculus, also called the stochastic calculus of variations, extends the Itô calculus by introducing a derivative operator D acting on random variables measurable with respect to the Wiener process. For a smooth cylindrical functional F = f(W(h₁), …, W(hₙ)) where hᵢ ∈ L²([0,T]) and f ∈ C_b^∞(ℝⁿ), the Malliavin derivative is the L²([0,T])-valued random variable DF = ∑ᵢ ∂ᵢf(W(h₁), …, W(hₙ)) hᵢ. The operator D is closable from L^p(Ω) into L^p(Ω; L²([0,T])) for any p ≥ 1; its domain is the Sobolev space 𝔻1,p. The adjoint of D is the divergence operator δ, also called the Skorokhod integral, which extends the Itô integral to non-adapted integrands. For u ∈ Dom(δ), δ(u) is defined by the duality relation E[F δ(u)] = E[⟨DF, u⟩L²] for all F ∈ 𝔻1,2. When u is adapted, δ(u) coincides with the Itô integral ∫₀ᵀ u_s dW_s. The integration by parts formula E[F δ(u)] = E[⟨DF, u⟩] is the cornerstone of Malliavin calculus and enables the computation of densities of laws of Wiener functionals. If F ∈ 𝔻1,2 and ‖DF‖L² > 0 almost surely, then the law of F is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure and its density is given by p_F(x) = E[1F > x δ(DF / ‖DF‖²)]. Higher-order derivatives D^k and the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator L = −δD give the Meyer inequalities and the Clark–Ocone formula: for F ∈ 𝔻1,2, F = E[F] + ∫₀ᵀ E[D_t F | ℱ_t] dW_t, providing an explicit representation of the martingale integrand. In mathematical finance, the Clark–Ocone formula yields hedging strategies for European options without solving PDEs; for a payoff Φ(S_T) with S_t = S₀ exp(σW_t + (μ − σ²/2)t), the hedging portfolio is π_t = E[D_t Φ(S_T) | ℱ_t] / σ S_t. The Malliavin derivative of the solution to an SDE dX_t = b(X_t) dt + σ(X_t) dW_t satisfies the linear variational equation D_r X_t = σ(X_r) + ∫_r^t b'(X_s) D_r X_s ds + ∫_r^t σ'(X_s) D_r X_s dW_s for r ≤ t, which is essential for sensitivity analysis and Monte Carlo Greeks.

Large Deviations Theory and the Freidlin–Wentzell Theorem

Large deviations theory quantifies the exponential decay of probabilities of rare events for families of random variables. A sequence {X^ε} on a topological space satisfies a large deviation principle (LDP) with speed ε⁻² and good rate function I if for all measurable sets A, −infx ∈ A° I(x) ≤ liminfε→0 ε² log ℙ(X^ε ∈ A) ≤ limsupε→0 ε² log ℙ(X^ε ∈ A) ≤ −infx ∈ Ā I(x). The Freidlin–Wentzell theorem establishes an LDP for the small-noise diffusion dX^ε_t = b(X^ε_t) dt + ε σ(X^ε_t) dW_t, X^ε0 = x, on [0,T] with speed ε⁻² and rate function I(φ) = ½ ∫₀ᵀ ‖φ̇_t − b(φ_t)‖a(φ_t)⁻¹² dt if φ is absolutely continuous, φ₀ = x, and a = σσᵀ is invertible; otherwise I(φ) = ∞. The rate function is the action functional of the associated deterministic control problem: I(φ) = inf{½ ∫₀ᵀ ‖u_t‖² dt : φ̇_t = b(φ_t) + σ(φ_t) u_t, φ₀ = x}. This variational representation connects large deviations to control theory and the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation. The quasi-potential V(x,y) = infT>0 infφ: φ₀=x, φ_T=y I(φ) governs the asymptotic exit time from a domain D containing a stable equilibrium x*: ℙ(τ_D^ε > exp((V(x*,∂D) − δ)/ε²)) → 1 and ℙ(τ_D^ε < exp((V(x*,∂D) + δ)/ε²)) → 1 for any δ > 0, where τ_D^ε is the first exit time. For gradient systems b = −∇U with σ = I, V(x,y) = 2(U(y) − U(x))⁺, recovering the Arrhenius law. The contraction principle extends LDPs to continuous mappings: if X^ε satisfies an LDP with rate I, then f(X^ε) satisfies an LDP with rate J(y) = inf{I(x) : f(x) = y}. Varadhan's lemma gives the asymptotic of exponential integrals: limε→0 ε² log E[exp(−F(X^ε)/ε²)] = −inf_x {F(x) + I(x)} for bounded continuous F. In statistics, Sanov's theorem provides the LDP for empirical measures of i.i.d. samples with rate function the relative entropy H(μ|ν); this underpins the method of types and large deviation bounds for hypothesis testing. The Gärtner–Ellis theorem gives an LDP when the scaled cumulant generating function Λ(θ) = limε→0 ε² log E[exp(θ·X^ε/ε²)] exists and is differentiable, with rate function the Legendre–Fenchel transform I(x) = sup_θ {θ·x − Λ(θ)}.

Worked Example: Exit Time Asymptotics for a Double-Well Potential
Consider the one-dimensional diffusion dX^ε_t = (X^ε_t − (X^ε_t)³) dt + ε dW_t with X^ε0 = 1, which has stable equilibria at ±1 and an unstable equilibrium at 0. Let τ = inf{t ≥ 0 : X^ε_t = −1} be the first hitting time of −1. Find the exponential asymptotics of E[τ] as ε → 0.
The drift is b(x) = x − x³ = −∇U(x) with U(x) = x⁴/4 − x²/2, a double-well potential with minima at x = ±1 (U = −1/4) and a local maximum at x = 0 (U = 0). The diffusion coefficient is σ = 1, so a = 1. The quasi-potential from x = 1 to the boundary {−1} is V(1, −1) = 2(U(0) − U(1)) = 2(0 − (−1/4)) = 1/2, since the optimal path goes over the saddle at 0. By the Freidlin–Wentzell theorem, for any δ > 0, ℙ(exp((1/2 − δ)/ε²) < τ < exp((1/2 + δ)/ε²)) → 1 as ε → 0. Consequently, log E[τ] ~ 1/(2ε²) as ε → 0. More precisely, E[τ] = exp(1/(2ε²) + o(1/ε²)). The prefactor can be obtained by solving the boundary value problem for the generator L^ε = ε²/2 d²/dx² + (x − x³) d/dx with Dirichlet condition at −1, yielding E[τ] ~ 2π exp(1/(2ε²)) / √2 as ε → 0 (Kramers' formula).
Exam-Day Strategy for Large Deviations
When a question asks for the rate function of a small-noise diffusion, write the action integral ½∫‖φ̇ − b(φ)‖²a⁻¹ dt immediately. For exit time problems, identify the potential U if b = −∇U; the quasi-potential is 2ΔU where ΔU is the potential barrier height. Remember that the speed is ε⁻² for diffusions scaled as εσ dW; if the noise is ε^α dW, the speed is ε⁻²ᵅ. The contraction principle is frequently tested: if X^ε satisfies an LDP with rate I, then f(X^ε) has rate J(y) = inf{I(x) : f(x) = y}. Do not confuse the rate function with the cumulant generating function; the Gärtner–Ellis theorem requires differentiability of Λ(θ) for the Legendre transform to give the rate function.

Stochastic Control, HJB Equations, and Verification Theorems

Stochastic optimal control seeks to minimize a cost functional J(u) = E[∫₀ᵀ f(t, X^u_t, u_t) dt + g(X^u_T)] over admissible controls u, where the state process follows dX^u_t = b(t, X^u_t, u_t) dt + σ(t, X^u_t, u_t) dW_t, X^u0 = x. The value function V(t,x) = inf_u E[∫_t^T f(s, X^u_s, u_s) ds + g(X^u_T) | X^u_t = x] satisfies the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation −∂ₜV − inf_u {b·∇V + ½ tr(σσᵀ∇²V) + f} = 0 with terminal condition V(T,x) = g(x). The infimum is attained at u*(t,x) = argmin_u {b·∇V + ½ tr(σσᵀ∇²V) + f}, giving the optimal feedback control. The verification theorem states: if V ∈ C1,2([0,T]×ℝⁿ) ∩ C([0,T]×ℝⁿ) solves the HJB equation and satisfies a polynomial growth condition, and if there exists an admissible control u* achieving the infimum such that the corresponding state process X* satisfies E[∫₀ᵀ |∇V(t,X*_t)ᵀσ(t,X*_t,u*_t)|² dt] < ∞, then V(t,x) = J(u*) and u* is optimal. For the linear-quadratic regulator (LQR) with dX_t = (AX_t + Bu_t) dt + σ dW_t and cost E[∫₀ᵀ (X_tᵀQX_t + u_tᵀRu_t) dt + X_TᵀP_T X_T], the value function is quadratic V(t,x) = xᵀP(t)x + c(t) where P(t) solves the matrix Riccati equation −Ṗ = AᵀP + PA − PBR⁻¹BᵀP + Q, P(T) = P_T, and the optimal control is u*_t = −R⁻¹BᵀP(t)X_t. The certainty equivalence principle holds: the optimal control for the stochastic LQR is identical to the deterministic LQR. In Merton's portfolio problem, an agent maximizes E[U(X_T)] with utility U(x) = x1−γ/(1−γ) (γ > 0, γ ≠ 1) or log x (γ = 1), investing in a risk-free asset dS⁰ = rS⁰ dt and a risky asset dS = S(μ dt + σ dW). The HJB equation yields the optimal fraction π* = (μ − r)/(γσ²) invested in the risky asset, independent of wealth and time (myopic policy). For stochastic volatility models where σ follows its own diffusion, the HJB equation becomes a nonlinear PDE in (t,x,σ) and the optimal policy depends on the volatility state. The dynamic programming principle V(t,x) = inf_u E[∫_tt+h f(s,X^u_s,u_s) ds + V(t+h, X^ut+h)] for any h > 0 is the foundation of the HJB derivation and is used in numerical methods like policy iteration and reinforcement learning.

Stochastic Filtering, the Kalman–Bucy Filter, and the Zakai Equation

Stochastic filtering estimates the state of a partially observed diffusion from noisy observations. The signal process X_t ∈ ℝⁿ follows dX_t = b(t,X_t) dt + σ(t,X_t) dW_t, and the observation process Y_t ∈ ℝᵐ follows dY_t = h(t,X_t) dt + dV_t, where W and V are independent Brownian motions. The filtering problem is to compute the conditional distribution π_t(φ) = E[φ(X_t) | ℱ^Y_t] for test functions φ, where ℱ^Y_t = σ(Y_s : s ≤ t). The Kallianpur–Striebel formula gives π_t(φ) = σ_t(φ)/σ_t(1) where σ_t(φ) = E[φ(X_t) Z_t | ℱ^Y_t] is the unnormalized conditional expectation and Z_t = exp(∫₀ᵗ h(s,X_s)ᵀ dY_s − ½∫₀ᵗ ‖h(s,X_s)‖² ds) is the likelihood ratio. The unnormalized filter σ_t satisfies the Zakai equation, a linear stochastic PDE: dσ_t(φ) = σ_t(Lφ) dt + σ_t(hᵀφ)ᵀ dY_t, where L is the infinitesimal generator of X. For the linear-Gaussian case b(t,x) = A_t x, σ(t,x) = B_t, h(t,x) = C_t x, the conditional distribution π_t is Gaussian N(m̂_t, P_t) where the mean m̂_t and covariance P_t satisfy the Kalman–Bucy equations: dm̂_t = A_t m̂_t dt + P_t C_tᵀ (dY_t − C_t m̂_t dt) and dP_t/dt = A_t P_t + P_t A_tᵀ + B_t B_tᵀ − P_t C_tᵀ C_t P_t (the matrix Riccati equation). The innovation process Ī_t = Y_t − ∫₀ᵗ C_s m̂_s ds is an ℱ^Y_t-Brownian motion, and the filter can be written as dm̂_t = A_t m̂_t dt + P_t C_tᵀ dĪ_t. The Kalman–Bucy filter is the continuous-time limit of the discrete-time Kalman filter and is optimal in the minimum mean-square error sense among all causal estimators. For nonlinear filtering, the Kushner–Stratonovich equation gives the evolution of the normalized filter: dπ_t(φ) = π_t(Lφ) dt + [π_t(hᵀφ) − π_t(hᵀ)π_t(φ)]ᵀ (dY_t − π_t(h) dt). Particle filters approximate π_t by a weighted empirical measure of N particles, with weights updated by the likelihood; the error is O(N⁻¹ᐟ²) under regularity conditions. The Zakai equation's linearity enables the use of Feynman–Kac representations and branching particle systems for numerical solution. In finance, filtering is used to estimate unobserved volatility from option prices (implied volatility) or to estimate the drift of an asset from noisy returns.

Concentration Inequalities for Martingales and Applications

Concentration inequalities bound the deviation of a random variable from its mean or median. For martingales, the Azuma–Hoeffding inequality states: if (M_n) is a martingale with respect to (ℱ_n) and |M_n − Mn−1| ≤ c_n almost surely, then ℙ(M_n − M0 ≥ λ) ≤ exp(−λ²/(2∑k=1^n c_k²)). The Bernstein inequality for martingales with bounded increments and conditional variance gives ℙ(M_n ≥ λ) ≤ exp(−λ²/(2(V_n + cλ/3))) where V_n = ∑k=1^n E[(M_k − Mk−1)² | ℱk−1] and |M_k − Mk−1| ≤ c. Freedman's inequality extends this to ℙ(M_n ≥ λ, V_n ≤ v) ≤ exp(−λ²/(2(v + cλ/3))). For continuous-time martingales M_t = ∫₀ᵗ H_s dW_s with |H_s| ≤ 1, the Dambis–Dubins–Schwarz theorem gives M_t = B⟨M⟩_t for a Brownian motion B, yielding ℙ(supt≤T M_t ≥ λ, ⟨M⟩_T ≤ v) ≤ 2 exp(−λ²/(2v)). The method of bounded differences (McDiarmid's inequality) applies to functions f(X₁,…,X_n) of independent variables where changing one coordinate changes f by at most c_i: ℙ(f − Ef ≥ λ) ≤ exp(−2λ²/∑c_i²). This is derived by applying Azuma–Hoeffding to the Doob martingale M_k = E[f | X₁,…,X_k]. For Lipschitz functions of Gaussian vectors, the Gaussian isoperimetric inequality gives ℙ(|f(X) − Ef| ≥ λ) ≤ 2 exp(−λ²/(2‖∇f‖²_∞)). Talagrand's convex distance inequality provides sharp bounds for product measures: ℙ(f ≥ M_f + λ) ≤ exp(−λ²/(4σ²)) where M_f is a median and σ² is a variance proxy. In empirical process theory, the bounded differences inequality yields the Vapnik–Chervonenkis (VC) bounds: for a class of functions ℱ with VC dimension d, supf∈ℱ |P_n f − P f| = O_p(√(d/n)). The Efron–Stein inequality Var(f) ≤ ½∑ E[(f(X) − f(X'))²] where X' replaces one coordinate, gives Poincaré inequalities for product measures. Log-Sobolev inequalities, e.g., for the standard Gaussian measure γ, Ent_γ(f²) ≤ 2 E_γ[‖∇f‖²], imply Gaussian concentration via Herbst's argument. These tools are essential for high-dimensional statistics, random matrix theory, and the analysis of stochastic gradient descent, where the martingale difference sequence of gradients satisfies bounded variance conditions enabling non-asymptotic convergence rates.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Jumps and the Lévy–Itô Decomposition

When the driving noise contains discontinuities, the Itô integral with respect to Brownian motion is insufficient. A Lévy process L = (Lt)t≥0 with characteristic triplet (b, Σ, ν) admits the Lévy–Itô decomposition

Lt = b t + Wt + ∫0t∫|x|<1 x (μ − ν) (ds, dx) + ∫0t∫|x|≥1 x μ (ds, dx)

where W is a Brownian motion with covariance Σ, μ is the Poisson random measure of jumps, and ν(dx) dt is its compensator. A stochastic differential equation with jumps takes the form

dXt = b(Xt−) dt + σ(Xt−) dWt + ∫ℝd γ(Xt−, x) μ̃(dt, dx)

with μ̃ = μ − ν the compensated measure. The generator of the solution is the integro‑differential operator

𝒜f(x) = ⟨∇f(x), b(x)⟩ + ½ Tr(σσᵀ∇²f(x)) + ∫ℝd [f(x+γ(x, y)) − f(x) − ⟨∇f(x), γ(x, y)⟩ 1{|y|<1}] ν(dy).

Existence and uniqueness follow from Lipschitz and linear growth conditions on b, σ, γ together with ∫|y|≥1 |γ(x, y)| ν(dy) < ∞. The Itô formula for jump‑diffusions adds a sum over jumps: f(Xt) = f(X0) + ∫0t 𝒜f(Xs) ds + martingale terms + ∑s≤t [f(Xs) − f(Xs−) − ⟨∇f(Xs−), ΔXs⟩].

In finance, the Merton model adds a compound Poisson jump component to geometric Brownian motion, producing a volatility smile. In insurance, the Cramér–Lundberg process is a pure‑jump Lévy subordinator. Numerical schemes such as the Euler–Maruyama method with jump‑adapted time grids achieve strong order ½ when the Lévy measure has finite activity; for infinite activity one uses series representations (e.g., Rosinski’s series) or the Asmussen–Rosinski approximation.

Rough Path Theory and the Signature Transform

Classical stochastic integration requires the integrator to be a semimartingale. Rough path theory, introduced by Lyons, extends integration to paths of finite p‑variation for p ∈ [2,3). A p‑rough path is a pair X = (X, 𝕏) where X : [0,T] → ℝd and 𝕏s,t ∈ (ℝd)⊗² satisfies Chen’s relation 𝕏s,u = 𝕏s,t + 𝕏t,u + Xs,t ⊗ Xt,u. The space of geometric p‑rough paths is the closure of smooth paths under the p‑variation metric.

Given a rough path X and a vector field V ∈ Lipγ with γ > p, the rough differential equation dYt = V(Yt) dXt has a unique solution defined via the Davie expansion. The solution map X ↦ Y is locally Lipschitz continuous — the continuity theorem — which underpins stability of numerical schemes and statistical inference.

The signature of a path X is the formal series S(X) = (1, ∫ dX, ∫∫ dX⊗dX, …). For a bounded variation path the signature determines the path up to tree‑like equivalence (Hambly–Lyons theorem). In machine learning, the truncated signature provides a feature map that linearises the action of the path on the solution of an RDE, enabling kernel methods and linear regression on path data. The expected signature of Brownian motion satisfies E[S(W)k] = 0 for odd k and equals the sum over pairings for even k, linking to Wick’s theorem.

Numerical integration of RDEs uses the log‑ODE method or the Euler scheme on the free nilpotent Lie group of step ⌊p⌋. For stochastic rough paths (e.g., enhanced Brownian motion with Lévy area), the Lévy area must be simulated; the Milstein scheme corresponds to the second‑order rough path expansion.

Stochastic Homogenization and Multiscale Analysis

Consider a diffusion in a rapidly oscillating random medium: dXεt = b(Xεt, Xεt/ε) dt + σ(Xεt, Xεt/ε) dWt, where the coefficients are stationary ergodic in the fast variable y = x/ε. As ε → 0, Xε converges in law to a homogenized diffusion dXt = b̄(Xt) dt + σ̄(Xt) dWt. The effective coefficients are obtained by solving the cell problem on the probability space of the environment: find a corrector χ(y) such that b̄ = ∫ (b + ∇χ·σ) dℙ and σ̄σ̄ᵀ = ∫ (σσᵀ + ∇χ·σσᵀ + σσᵀ·∇χᵀ + ∇χ·σσᵀ·∇χᵀ) dℙ.

Quantitative homogenization provides rates of convergence in Wasserstein distance or in the sense of corrector estimates. For uniformly elliptic coefficients with finite range of dependence, the corrector χ grows sublinearly: |χ(y)| ≤ C|y|1−δ with high probability. The two‑scale expansion Xεt ≈ Xt + ε χ(Xt/ε) yields an error of order ε½ in L². In periodic media the corrector is bounded and the rate improves to ε.

Applications include upscaling of porous media flow, effective conductivity of composite materials, and the derivation of macroscopic equations from microscopic stochastic particle systems (e.g., the Boltzmann–Grad limit). The framework extends to Hamilton–Jacobi equations with random Hamiltonians, where the effective Hamiltonian is given by a variational formula involving the corrector.

Random Matrix Theory and Free Probability in Stochastic Processes

Large random matrices appear as empirical covariance matrices of high‑dimensional time series, as transition matrices of Markov chains on large graphs, and as discretisations of stochastic operators. The empirical spectral distribution of an N×N Wigner matrix converges almost surely to the semicircle law with density (2π)⁻¹√(4−x²) 1|x|≤2. For sample covariance matrices SN = (1/N) XXᵀ with i.i.d. entries of variance 1, the Marchenko–Pastur law with parameter γ = lim p/N describes the limit spectrum.

Free probability, introduced by Voiculescu, provides an algebraic framework where non‑commutative random variables are free (the analogue of independence). The R‑transform linearises free convolution: RX+Y(z) = RX(z) + RY(z) for freely independent X, Y. The S‑transform does the same for free multiplicative convolution. The semicircle law is the free analogue of the Gaussian; the free Poisson (Marchenko–Pastur) is the free analogue of the Poisson law.

In stochastic processes, the Dyson Brownian motion describes the eigenvalue evolution of a matrix‑valued Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: dλi = dBi + (1/N) ∑j≠i (λi−λj)⁻¹ dt − ½ λi dt. Its invariant measure is the Gaussian unitary ensemble. The large‑N limit of the empirical measure satisfies the McKean–Vlasov equation ∂tμ = ½ ∂x²μ + ∂x(μ Hμ) where Hμ is the Hilbert transform. This connects to the free Fokker–Planck equation and to the hydrodynamic limit of interacting particle systems.

Applications include detection of spikes in spiked covariance models (BBP phase transition), analysis of the spectrum of random graphs (the Kesten–McKay law for regular graphs), and the study of the Lyapunov exponents of products of random matrices via the Furstenberg–Kesten theorem.

Stochastic Thermodynamics, Fluctuation Theorems, and Entropy Production

For a continuous‑time Markov jump process on a finite state space with rates kxy satisfying local detailed balance kxy/kyx = exp(Δxy), the total entropy production along a trajectory ω = (x₀, t₀; x₁, t₁; …; xn, tn) is Σ(ω) = ∑i=1n ln(kxi−1xi/kxixi−1) + ln(πx₀/πxn), where π is the stationary distribution. The integral fluctuation theorem states ⟨exp(−Σ)⟩ = 1, which implies the second law ⟨Σ⟩ ≥ 0 by Jensen’s inequality.

The detailed fluctuation theorem gives the symmetry of the probability density of Σ: ℙ(Σ = A)/ℙ(Σ = −A) = eA. For diffusion processes dXt = −∇U(Xt) dt + √(2β⁻¹) dWt, the entropy production rate in the steady state is ⟨|∇U|²⟩/β. The Hatano–Sasa relation extends the fluctuation theorem to transitions between non‑equilibrium steady states driven by time‑dependent protocols.

Thermodynamic uncertainty relations bound the relative uncertainty of any current J by the entropy production: Var(J)/⟨J⟩² ≥ 2/⟨Σ⟩. This provides a trade‑off between precision and dissipation, relevant for molecular motors and biochemical networks. In the context of stochastic control, the optimal protocol that minimises dissipation for a given current is obtained by solving a variational problem involving the tilted generator.

Numerical estimation of entropy production from trajectory data uses the Kullback–Leibler divergence between forward and time‑reversed path measures. For high‑dimensional systems, coarse‑graining introduces an apparent entropy production that satisfies a modified fluctuation theorem with an information‑theoretic correction term.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Delay and Functional Itô Calculus

Classical Itô calculus assumes that the drift and diffusion coefficients at time t depend only on the current state Xt. In many physical, biological, and financial models the future evolution also depends on the past trajectory, leading to stochastic delay differential equations (SDDEs) of the form

dXt = b(t, Xt, Xt−τ) dt + σ(t, Xt, Xt−τ) dWt,

where τ > 0 is a fixed lag. The state space becomes the space of continuous functions on [−τ,0], and the solution is a segment process Xt(θ) = Xt+θ, θ ∈ [−τ,0]. Functional Itô calculus, developed by Dupire and extended by Cont and Fournié, provides a pathwise derivative framework: for a functional F on the path space, the vertical derivative ∂xF measures sensitivity to a terminal perturbation, while the horizontal derivative ∂tF captures time‑translation effects. The functional Itô formula reads

F(Xt) = F(X0) + ∫0t ∂tF(Xs) ds + ∫0t ∂xF(Xs) dXs + ½ ∫0t ∂xxF(Xs) d⟨X⟩s.

Existence and uniqueness for SDDEs require Lipschitz conditions on the coefficients as maps on the path space equipped with the supremum norm. The Lyapunov–Krasovskii method extends to stochastic settings: a functional V(t, φ) satisfying a stochastic differential inequality yields exponential stability in mean square. Numerical approximation uses the Euler–Maruyama scheme on a uniform grid with step Δt; convergence order remains ½ in the strong sense provided the delay is a multiple of Δt. Applications include option pricing with path‑dependent payoffs (Asian options), population dynamics with maturation delays, and control of systems with actuator lag.

Stochastic Optimal Transport and Wasserstein Gradient Flows

Optimal transport theory studies the problem of moving a probability measure μ to another ν at minimal cost. The quadratic Wasserstein distance W2(μ,ν) is defined as the infimum of (∫|x−y|² dπ(x,y))½ over couplings π with marginals μ,ν. Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto showed that the heat equation ∂tρ = Δρ is the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy Ent(ρ) = ∫ρ log ρ with respect to W2. In the stochastic setting, the Fokker–Planck equation ∂tρ = ∇·(ρ∇V) + β⁻¹Δρ arises as the gradient flow of the free energy F(ρ) = ∫Vρ + β⁻¹Ent(ρ). The JKO (Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto) scheme constructs discrete-time approximations: given ρk, define ρk+1 as the minimiser of F(ρ) + (2τ)⁻¹W2²(ρ,ρk). As τ → 0 the interpolated curve converges to the continuous gradient flow.

Stochastic optimal transport adds randomness to the transport map. Given a stochastic process Xt with law μt, the dynamic formulation of W2 (Benamou–Brenier) writes W2²(μ0,μ1) = inf ∫01 ∫|vt|² dμt dt over velocity fields vt satisfying the continuity equation ∂tμt + ∇·(μtvt) = 0. When the dynamics are driven by a diffusion dXt = bt(Xt)dt + σ dWt, the optimal drift bt solves a stochastic control problem whose value function satisfies a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation. The Schrödinger bridge problem, an entropic regularisation of optimal transport, yields a pair of forward–backward SDEs whose solution gives the most likely evolution of a diffusion conditioned on its endpoints. This framework underlies recent generative models (diffusion models) where the reverse-time SDE is learned from data.

Stochastic Analysis on Manifolds and Geometry of Diffusions

Brownian motion on a Riemannian manifold (M,g) is defined via the Laplace–Beltrami operator Δg. In local coordinates, the generator is ½gij∂i∂j + ½gijΓkij∂k, where Γ are Christoffel symbols. The stochastic development map lifts Euclidean Brownian motion to M using the orthonormal frame bundle and the Levi‑Civita connection; the resulting SDE on the frame bundle is dUt = Hi(Ut) ∘ dWti, where Hi are horizontal vector fields. The projection Xt = π(Ut) is Brownian motion on M. The Itô formula on manifolds reads df(Xt) = (∇f)(Xt)·dXt + ½Δgf(Xt)dt.

Curvature controls the behaviour of diffusions. The Bochner formula gives ½Δ|∇f|² = |Hess f|² + Ric(∇f,∇f) + ⟨∇f,∇Δf⟩. For a diffusion with generator L = ½Δ + ∇V·∇, the Bakry–Émery curvature tensor RicV = Ric + Hess V appears in the Γ₂ calculus. If RicV ≥ ρg for some ρ > 0, the log‑Sobolev inequality holds with constant ρ⁻¹, implying exponential convergence to equilibrium in relative entropy. The coupling method (Kendall, Cranston) constructs two Brownian motions driven by the same noise after parallel transport; the distance process satisfies d d(Xt,Yt) ≤ −ρ d(Xt,Yt) dt + martingale, yielding a contraction in Wasserstein distance.

Stochastic parallel transport along a diffusion solves d//t = //t ∘ Γ(dXt) and is a martingale in the bundle of linear frames. Its expectation gives the heat kernel asymptotics via the Malliavin covariance matrix. The Malliavin calculus on manifolds uses the derivative operator D acting on functionals of the path; the integration‑by‑parts formula involves the curvature tensor. Applications include geometric MCMC (Riemannian manifold Hamiltonian Monte Carlo), stochastic filtering on Lie groups (attitude estimation), and the study of random geometric structures such as the stochastic Ricci flow.

Stochastic Calculus for Fractional Brownian Motion

Fractional Brownian motion (fBm) BH with Hurst index H ∈ (0,1) is a centred Gaussian process with covariance E[BHt BHs] = ½(|t|2H + |s|2H − |t−s|2H). For H = ½ it reduces to standard Brownian motion; for H > ½ the increments exhibit long‑range dependence, while for H < ½ they are negatively correlated. fBm is not a semimartingale when H ≠ ½, so classical Itô integration fails. Two main integration theories exist: pathwise Young integration for H > ½ (since fBm has finite p-variation for p > 1/H), and rough path integration for H > ¼ (Lyons’ theory). The Young integral ∫ f dBH is defined as the limit of Riemann–Stieltjes sums when f has finite q-variation with 1/p + 1/q > 1. For H ≤ ½ one uses the divergence (Skorokhod) integral from Malliavin calculus, which satisfies an isometry E[(δ(u))²] = E[‖u‖²H] + E[⟨Du, u⟩H] where ‖·‖H is the Hilbert space norm associated with the covariance kernel.

Stochastic differential equations driven by fBm, dXt = b(Xt)dt + σ(Xt)dBHt, are interpreted either pathwise (for H > ½) or in the Skorokhod sense (for H < ½). Existence and uniqueness for the pathwise case follow from the sewing lemma; the solution map is continuous in the p-variation topology. For H < ½ the Skorokhod SDE requires Malliavin differentiability of the coefficients and a fixed‑point argument in the space of Malliavin‑differentiable processes. The fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process dXt = −θXtdt + dBHt has an explicit solution Xt = e−θtX0 + ∫0t e−θ(t−s) dBHs and exhibits a stationary Gaussian distribution with covariance ΓH(t,s). Parameter estimation for H and θ uses the quadratic variation of filtered increments; the estimator is consistent but its rate depends on H. Applications include turbulence modelling (Kolmogorov’s 1941 theory predicts H = 1/3), financial time series with long memory, and queueing networks with self‑similar traffic.

Stochastic Games and Mean Field Games

Stochastic differential games involve multiple players controlling a shared diffusion dXt = b(t,Xt,αt1,…,αtN)dt + σ(t,Xt)dWt, where each player i chooses a control αi to minimise a cost functional Ji(α) = E[∫0T fi(t,Xt,αti,αt−i)dt + gi(XT)]. A Nash equilibrium is a tuple of controls such that no player can reduce their cost by unilateral deviation. The verification theorem links Nash equilibria to a system of coupled Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equations: for each i, −∂tui = infa { fi(t,x,a,α−i,*) + b·∇ui + ½Tr(σσᵀ∇²ui) } with terminal condition ui(T,x)=gi(x). Under Isaacs’ condition (the Hamiltonian is convex‑concave), a saddle point exists and the value functions satisfy a system of Isaacs equations.

Mean field games (MFG) describe the limit N → ∞ of symmetric N-player games. The state of a representative player follows dXt = b(t,Xt,αt,μt)dt + σ(t,Xt)dWt, where μt is the distribution of the population. The MFG system couples a forward Fokker–Planck equation for μt with a backward HJB equation for the value function u(t,x): −∂tu = H(t,x,∇u,μt) + ½Tr(σσᵀ∇²u), ∂tμ = −∇·(μ ∂pH) + ½Δ(σσᵀμ). The master equation, a PDE on the space of probability measures, encodes the value function as a functional U(t,x,μ) and reads ∂tU + H(t,x,∇xU,μ) + ∫ ∂μU(t,x,μ)(y)·∂pH(t,y,∇xU(t,y,μ),μ) μ(dy) + ½∫ Tr(σσᵀ∇²x∂μU) μ(dy) = 0. Existence of classical solutions requires monotonicity of the coupling (Lasry–Lions condition). Numerical methods include the semi‑Lagrangian scheme for the HJB–FP system and the fictitious play algorithm for learning equilibria. Applications span crowd motion, electricity markets, and systemic risk in banking networks.

  • Assuming that the JKO scheme converges for any functional; the functional must be geodesically convex in Wasserstein space, otherwise the discrete minimisers may not approximate a gradient flow.
  • Neglecting the curvature term in the Bochner formula when estimating convergence rates; the Bakry–Émery curvature RicV is the correct quantity for diffusions with drift.
  • Treating the master equation as a standard PDE on Euclidean space; it lives on the infinite‑dimensional Wasserstein space and its derivatives are linear functionals on the tangent space of measures.
  • Using the classical Itô formula for fBm‑driven SDEs when H ≠ ½; the correction term involves the Malliavin derivative and does not reduce to ½σ²∂xx.
  • Overlooking the need for a common noise formulation in mean field games; without common noise the limiting empirical measure is deterministic, but with common noise the MFG system becomes stochastic.
  • Assuming that every stochastic delay equation admits a unique strong solution; the coefficients must be Lipschitz in the supremum norm on the path segment, otherwise only weak solutions may exist.

These advanced topics illustrate how stochastic analysis extends beyond Euclidean semimartingales to incorporate memory, geometry, long‑range dependence, and strategic interaction, providing a unified toolkit for modern quantitative modelling.

Stochastic Stability and Lyapunov Exponents for SDEs

Stability of solutions to stochastic differential equations (SDEs) is analysed through the behaviour of sample paths as time tends to infinity. For an autonomous SDE dXt = b(Xt)dt + σ(Xt)dWt with a globally Lipschitz drift b and diffusion σ, the trivial solution X ≡ 0 is said to be almost surely exponentially stable if there exists λ > 0 such that lim supt→∞ (1/t) log |Xt| ≤ −λ almost surely for all initial conditions in a neighbourhood of the origin. The maximal Lyapunov exponent λmax = lim supt→∞ (1/t) log ‖Φt(x)‖, where Φt is the stochastic flow, quantifies the exponential growth rate of infinitesimal perturbations. For linear SDEs dXt = A Xt dt + Σ Xt dWt, the top Lyapunov exponent can be computed via the Furstenberg–Khasminskii formula λmax = limt→∞ (1/t) E[log ‖Xt‖] and is given by the principal eigenvalue of a certain second‑order elliptic operator on the unit sphere. In the nonlinear case, a Lyapunov function V ∈ C2(ℝd) satisfying LV(x) ≤ −c V(x) for some c > 0, where L is the infinitesimal generator L = b·∇ + ½ Tr(σσT∇2), guarantees exponential stability in mean square and almost sure stability. The converse Lyapunov theorem for SDEs states that if the zero solution is exponentially stable in probability, then such a V exists. For systems with multiplicative noise, the Itô correction term ½ Tr(σσT∇2V) can stabilise an otherwise unstable deterministic system, a phenomenon known as noise‑induced stabilisation. Conversely, large noise intensity may destroy stability, leading to a stochastic bifurcation where the top Lyapunov exponent changes sign. Numerical estimation of Lyapunov exponents uses the QR‑decomposition of the linearised flow or the Benettin algorithm adapted to stochastic flows, with convergence rates depending on the ergodicity of the underlying Markov process.

Stochastic Numerics: Strong and Weak Convergence of Numerical Schemes

Numerical approximation of SDEs requires distinguishing between strong convergence, which measures the pathwise error E[|XT − YN|], and weak convergence, which measures the error in expectations of smooth functionals |E[f(XT)] − E[f(YN)]|. The Euler–Maruyama scheme Yn+1 = Yn + b(Yn)Δt + σ(Yn)ΔWn achieves strong order ½ and weak order 1 under global Lipschitz and linear growth assumptions. The Milstein scheme adds the Lévy area term ½ σ(Yn)σ'(Yn)[(ΔWn)2 − Δt] and attains strong order 1 for scalar noise; for multi‑dimensional noise the iterated Itô integrals ∫tntn+1 ∫tns dWir dWjs must be simulated, which is computationally expensive. Weak order 2 schemes, such as the stochastic Runge–Kutta methods of Platen, avoid Lévy areas by using appropriate combinations of Gaussian increments. For stiff SDEs, implicit schemes like the drift‑implicit Euler method Yn+1 = Yn + b(Yn+1)Δt + σ(Yn)ΔWn preserve stability regions analogous to deterministic A‑stability. The concept of asymptotic stability of numerical methods is captured by the stochastic θ‑method, which is mean‑square A‑stable for θ ≥ ½. Adaptive time‑stepping based on local error estimators for SDEs uses the difference between a higher‑order and a lower‑order scheme, but the estimator must account for the stochastic term; a common approach is the embedded Milstein–Euler pair. For SDEs with non‑globally Lipschitz coefficients, tamed Euler schemes modify the drift to bΔt(x) = b(x)/(1 + Δt|b(x)|) to retain strong convergence without moment explosions. In the context of SPDEs, spectral Galerkin methods combined with exponential integrators achieve high weak order while preserving the invariant measure of the discretised system.

Worked Example: Strong vs Weak Error for Geometric Brownian Motion
Consider the SDE dXt = μ Xt dt + σ Xt dWt with X0 = 1, μ = 0.05, σ = 0.2, T = 1. Using the Euler–Maruyama scheme with step Δt = 2−k, compute the strong error E[|XT − YN|] and weak error |E[XT] − E[YN]| for k = 4,5,6. Explain the observed convergence rates.
The exact solution is XT = exp((μ − ½σ2)T + σ WT). For each k, simulate M = 105 independent paths. The Euler scheme is Yn+1 = Yn(1 + μΔt + σΔWn). Stochastic Differential Equations with Reflection and the Skorokhod Problem

SDEs with reflection constrain the state process to remain in a closed domain D ⊂ ℝd by adding a bounded variation term that pushes the process inward when it hits the boundary ∂D. The Skorokhod problem seeks a pair (X, K) such that Xt = x + ∫0t b(Xs)ds + ∫0t σ(Xs)dWs + Kt, Xt ∈ D, K is continuous, non‑decreasing, and increases only when Xt ∈ ∂D with dKt = n(Xt) d|K|t, where n is the inward unit normal. For convex domains with C2 boundary, existence and uniqueness of a strong solution hold under Lipschitz coefficients. The reflected process is a strong Markov process with generator L acting on functions satisfying the Neumann boundary condition ∂nu = 0 on ∂D. The invariant measure, when it exists, has density proportional to exp(2∫ b·σ−1σ−T dx) restricted to D. In finance, reflected SDEs model regulated Brownian motion for queueing systems and the CIR process with reflection at zero for interest rates. Numerical approximation uses the projected Euler scheme Yn+1 = ΠD(Yn + b(Yn)Δt + σ(Yn)ΔWn), where ΠD is the Euclidean projection onto D. This scheme converges strongly with order ½ for convex polyhedral domains; for smooth boundaries the order may degrade due to projection error. The penalisation method replaces reflection by a steep drift −(1/ε)∇d(Xt) where d is the distance to ∂D, and lets ε → 0; this yields weak convergence but requires careful tuning of ε relative to Δt.

Stochastic Processes in Random Environments: Random Walks and Diffusions

A random walk in random environment (RWRE) on ℤd is defined by a collection of i.i.d. transition probabilities ω(x,·) assigned to each site x. The annealed law averages over both the walk and the environment, while the quenched law fixes a realisation of ω. For d = 1 with nearest‑neighbour jumps, the walk is transient to the right iff E[log ρ0] < 0 where ρx = (1−ω(x, x+1))/ω(x, x+1). The asymptotic speed v = limn→∞ Xn/n exists almost surely and is given by v = (1−E[ρ0])/(1+E[ρ0]) under the annealed measure when E[ρ0] < 1. In higher dimensions, ballistic behaviour (non‑zero speed) is characterised by Kalikow’s condition or Sznitman’s (T) and (T') conditions. For diffusions in random potential, the generator is Lω = ½ Δ + ∇Vω·∇ where Vω is a stationary ergodic random field. The homogenised effective diffusivity matrix Deff is obtained via the corrector equation −Lω χ = ∇·a, where a is the identity. The quenched invariance principle holds under moment conditions on the potential, yielding convergence of the rescaled process to a Brownian motion with covariance Deff. Trapping phenomena in heavy‑tailed environments lead to sub‑diffusive scaling and ageing, described by the Bouchaud trap model where the mean waiting time diverges. The random conductance model, with conductances ωxy on edges, exhibits a sharp transition between diffusive and sub‑diffusive behaviour depending on the tail of the conductance distribution.

Stochastic Analysis on Metric Measure Spaces and Dirichlet Forms

Dirichlet forms provide a unified framework for symmetric Markov processes on general state spaces. A Dirichlet form (E, F) on L2(X, m) is a closed, symmetric, Markovian bilinear form satisfying the Beurling–Deny criteria. The associated Hunt process is a symmetric strong Markov process with transition semigroup (Pt) generated by the self‑adjoint operator L. On a metric measure space (X, d, m) satisfying the volume doubling property and a Poincaré inequality, the heat kernel pt(x, y) admits Gaussian upper and lower bounds: c1 t−d/2 exp(−d(x,y)2/c2t) ≤ pt(x, y) ≤ c3 t−d/2 exp(−d(x,y)2/c4t). The Bakry–Émery curvature‑dimension condition CD(K, N) is expressed via the Γ2 calculus: Γ2(f) ≥ K Γ(f) + (1/N)(Lf)2, where Γ(f) = ½ L(f2) − f Lf. This condition implies the logarithmic Sobolev inequality, the Talagrand transportation inequality, and exponential convergence to equilibrium in Wasserstein distance. For non‑symmetric forms, the sector condition |Ea(f, g)| ≤ C Es(f, f)1/2 Es(g, g)1/2 ensures the existence of an associated Markov process. The theory extends to jump processes via non‑local Dirichlet forms E(f, g) = ∫∫ (f(x)−f(y))(g(x)−g(y)) J(dx, dy), where J is a symmetric Lévy kernel. Applications include analysis on fractals (e.g., Sierpiński gasket), stochastic quantization of Euclidean quantum fields, and the study of stochastic gradient flows in Wasserstein spaces where the Dirichlet form corresponds to the Fisher information.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Singular Coefficients and Regularization by Noise

Classical Itô theory requires drift and diffusion coefficients to be at least locally Lipschitz to guarantee existence and uniqueness of strong solutions. Many models from physics and finance, however, involve coefficients that are discontinuous (e.g. regime‑switching drifts) or singular (e.g. 1/|x| drift near the origin). The theory of regularization by noise shows that adding a non‑degenerate Brownian component can restore well‑posedness even when the deterministic counterpart is ill‑posed. A landmark result by Zvonkin (1974) and Veretennikov (1981) proves that for a one‑dimensional SDE dXₜ = b(Xₜ) dt + dWₜ with bounded measurable drift b, there exists a unique strong solution. The proof uses a transformation Yₜ = Xₜ + u(Xₜ) where u solves the Poisson equation ½ u'' + b u' = −b, turning the SDE into one with zero drift. In higher dimensions, the Krylov–Röckner condition (b ∈ L^q([0,T]; L^p) with d/p + 2/q < 1) yields existence of a unique strong solution for dXₜ = b(t,Xₜ) dt + σ(t,Xₜ) dWₜ with uniformly elliptic σ. When σ degenerates, the theory of stochastic flows and the notion of “probabilistic solution” (martingale problem) become essential. Applications include the stochastic transport equation with rough velocity fields, where noise prevents blow‑up of characteristics, and the CIR model with a singular drift at zero, where the Feller condition 2κθ ≥ σ² ensures the boundary is unattainable and the solution stays positive. Examiners often test the distinction between pathwise uniqueness and uniqueness in law, and the fact that Yamada–Watanabe theorem equates them only under the existence of a weak solution.

Stochastic Processes on Graphs and Networks

Random walks on graphs provide a discrete analogue of Brownian motion and underpin algorithms for community detection, PageRank, and distributed consensus. For a finite connected graph G = (V,E) with transition matrix P, the stationary distribution π satisfies πP = π and π_i = deg(i) / (2|E|) for the simple random walk. The spectral gap 1 − λ₂ (where λ₂ is the second largest eigenvalue of P) controls the mixing time t_mix(ε) ≤ (1/(1−λ₂)) log(1/(ε π_min)). Cheeger’s inequality relates the spectral gap to the conductance Φ = minS⊂V, π(S)≤½ Q(S,S^c)/π(S), giving 2Φ ≥ 1−λ₂ ≥ Φ²/2. On infinite graphs, recurrence versus transience is decided by the effective resistance to infinity: a network is recurrent iff the resistance is infinite (Nash‑Williams criterion). For the simple random walk on ℤ^d, Polya’s theorem states recurrence for d = 1,2 and transience for d ≥ 3. The cover time C_G = maxv E_v[τ_cov] satisfies C_G ≤ 2|E|·|V|·H|V|-1 (Matthews’ bound) and is Θ(|V| log |V|) for expanders. In the continuum limit, scaling a sequence of graphs with mesh size ε → 0 yields a diffusion on a manifold; the graph Laplacian converges to the Laplace–Beltrami operator under appropriate curvature‑dimension conditions (Bakry–Émery). Examiners frequently ask to compute the stationary distribution for a weighted walk, to bound mixing time using conductance, or to identify whether a given infinite graph is recurrent by evaluating the resistance of a cutset.

Technique
Consider a simple random walk on the infinite binary tree (each vertex has degree 3 except the root). Determine whether the walk is recurrent or transient, and compute the effective resistance from the root to infinity.
The binary tree is a regular tree of branching factor 2. Assign unit resistance to each edge. The effective resistance from the root to level n is the sum of resistances of parallel branches: at level k there are 2^k edges in parallel, each of resistance 1, so the resistance of level k is 1/2^k. The total resistance to infinity is Σk=0^∞ 1/2^k = 2. Since the resistance is finite, by the Nash‑Williams criterion the walk is transient. The effective resistance from the root to infinity equals 2.

When a question asks for recurrence or transience on an infinite graph, first compute the effective resistance of a natural cutset. If the series of parallel resistances converges, the walk is transient; if it diverges, the walk is recurrent. Stochastic Approximation and Reinforcement Learning

Stochastic approximation algorithms of Robbins–Monro type iterate θn+1 = θ_n + α_n (h(θ_n) + Mn+1) where α_n is a step‑size sequence satisfying Σ α_n = ∞, Σ α_n² < ∞, h is a Lipschitz drift, and M_n is a martingale difference noise. The ODE method (Kushner–Clark) shows that the interpolated trajectory converges almost surely to the internally chain‑recurrent set of the limiting ODE dθ/dt = h(θ). In reinforcement learning, the temporal‑difference (TD) update for value estimation V(s) ← V(s) + α (r + γ V(s') − V(s)) is a stochastic approximation with h(V) = T^π V − V, where T^π is the Bellman operator. Convergence to the unique fixed point V^π follows from the contraction property of T^π in the sup‑norm. For Q‑learning, the update Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α (r + γ maxa' Q(s',a') − Q(s,a)) targets the optimal Bellman operator T^*, which is also a contraction. The asynchronous convergence theorem (Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis) guarantees convergence provided each state‑action pair is visited infinitely often and step‑sizes satisfy the Robbins–Monro conditions. Recent finite‑time analysis uses Lyapunov functions to bound the mean‑square error: E[‖θ_n − θ*‖²] ≤ C/(n α_n) under strong monotonicity of h. Examiners often test the distinction between on‑policy (SARSA) and off‑policy (Q‑learning) updates, the role of the projection operator to keep iterates in a compact set, and the necessity of the “exploration” condition (e.g. ε‑greedy) for the martingale difference property to hold.

Stochastic Volatility Models and Rough Volatility

Classical stochastic volatility models (Heston, Stein–Stein) assume the variance process follows a diffusion with square‑root or Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics, leading to an exponential autocorrelation of volatility. Empirical studies of high‑frequency financial data reveal a power‑law decay of the volatility autocorrelation function ∼ τ−γ with γ ≈ 0.4, motivating rough volatility models where the log‑volatility is driven by a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H < ½. The rough Heston model specifies dS_t = S_t √v_t dW_t1, dv_t = κ(θ − v_t) dt + ξ v_t^H dW_t^H, where W^H is a fractional Brownian motion with H ∈ (0,½). The kernel representation v_t = v0 + ∫0^t K(t−s) (κ(θ−v_s) ds + ξ √v_s dW_s) with K(t) = tH−½/Γ(H+½) yields a Volterra‑type SDE. The roughness parameter H controls the short‑time behaviour of the implied volatility smile: the at‑the‑money skew scales as τH−½, matching the observed τ−0.1 scaling for H ≈ 0.1. Pricing under rough volatility requires either Monte Carlo simulation of the Volterra process (using hybrid schemes) or the rough Bergomi model where the forward variance curve is explicitly given by ξ_t(T) = ξ0(T) exp(η ∫0^t (T−s)H−½ dW_s − ½ η² ∫0^t (T−s)2H−1 ds). The latter admits semi‑closed form formulas for European options via the characteristic function. Examiners frequently ask to derive the short‑maturity asymptotics of the implied volatility skew, to explain why the classical Heston model fails to reproduce the observed power‑law, and to outline the simulation challenges (non‑Markovianity, need for the whole past).

Stochastic Geometry and Point Processes

Stochastic geometry provides the mathematical framework for random spatial patterns such as the locations of base stations in a cellular network or the centres of galaxies. A point process Φ on ℝ^d is a random locally finite counting measure; its intensity measure Λ(A) = E[Φ(A)] and, when stationary, the intensity λ = Λ(dx)/dx. The Poisson point process (PPP) with intensity λ is characterised by the independence of counts in disjoint sets and the Poisson distribution of Φ(A) with mean λ|A|. The probability generating functional (PGFL) G[f] = E[exp(−∫ f(x) Φ(dx))] = exp(−∫ (1−e−f(x)) λ dx) uniquely determines the PPP. For a stationary point process, the pair correlation function g(r) = (1/λ²) d²/dx dy P(Φ(dx)≥1, Φ(dy)≥1) describes clustering (g>1) or inhibition (g<1). The Palm distribution P0 conditions on a point at the origin; Slivnyak’s theorem states that for a PPP, the reduced Palm distribution coincides with the original law. In wireless networks, the signal‑to‑interference ratio (SIR) at the typical user is SIR = h0 r0−α / ∑_{x∈Φ{0}} h_x |x|−α, where h_x are i.i.d. fading gains. Using the PGFL, the coverage probability P(SIR > θ) = exp(−λ π r0² θ2/α C(α)) with C(α) = Γ(1−2/α)Γ(1+2/α). Determinantal point processes (DPPs) exhibit repulsion and have kernels K(x,y) such that the n‑point correlation functions are det(K(x_i,x_j)). The Ginibre ensemble is a DPP with kernel K(z,w) = (1/π) ezbar w − (|z|²+|w|²)/2. Examiners test the use of the PGFL to compute Laplace functionals of interference, the distinction between the PPP and the Matérn hard‑core process (which enforces a minimum distance), and the application of Campbell’s formula E[∑x∈Φ f(x)] = ∫ f(x) Λ(dx).

  • Assuming the spectral gap of a random walk on a graph equals the conductance; Cheeger’s inequality only provides two‑sided bounds, not equality.
  • Neglecting the step‑size conditions Σ α_n = ∞ and Σ α_n² < ∞ in stochastic approximation; violating either leads to divergence or failure to converge to the ODE limit.
  • Treating the Hurst index H in rough volatility as a free parameter without respecting the constraint H < ½ for genuine roughness; H = ½ recovers the standard Brownian case.
  • Applying the Poisson PGFL to a non‑Poisson point process; the PGFL factorises only for the PPP, and using it for a clustered process yields incorrect interference statistics.

Branching Processes and the Galton–Watson Tree

A branching process models a population where each individual lives for one generation and produces a random number of offspring according to a fixed offspring distribution. The Galton–Watson process is the discrete‑time Markov chain Zn with Z0 = 1 and Zn+1 = ∑i=1Zn ξi,n+1, where the ξ are i.i.d. non‑negative integer‑valued random variables with probability generating function f(s) = E[sξ]. The extinction probability q = P(limn→∞ Zn = 0) is the smallest root of f(s) = s in [0,1]. If the mean offspring μ = f′(1) ≤ 1 and P(ξ = 1) < 1, then q = 1 (certain extinction); if μ > 1 then q < 1 and the population survives with positive probability. The Yaglom limit describes the conditional distribution of Zn given non‑extinction: for critical (μ = 1) finite‑variance offspring, Zn/n converges in distribution to an exponential law. In the supercritical case, W = limn→∞ Zn/μn exists almost surely and is non‑degenerate on the survival set (Kesten–Stigum theorem). Continuous‑time branching processes (Markov branching processes) replace generations by exponential lifetimes; the infinitesimal generator is Gf(k) = k ∑j qj(f(k−1+j)−f(k)). The Feller diffusion arises as the scaling limit of critical Galton–Watson processes with finite variance: dXt = √(γ Xt) dWt. Applications include nuclear chain reactions, epidemiology (branching process approximation of early epidemic spread), and the genealogy of neutral alleles in population genetics (Kingman’s coalescent as the scaling limit of the ancestral tree).

Heavy‑Traffic Limits and Diffusion Approximations in Queueing Theory

Heavy‑traffic theory studies queueing systems as the traffic intensity ρ approaches 1 from below. For a single‑server queue with i.i.d. inter‑arrival times (mean 1/λ, variance σa²) and service times (mean 1/μ, variance σs²), let ρ = λ/μ. Under the scaling Q(n)(t) = (1/√n) Q(nt) where Q is the queue length process, the sequence converges in distribution (in the Skorokhod J1 topology) to a reflected Brownian motion (RBM) on [0,∞) with drift −θ and diffusion coefficient σ² = λ³(σa²+σs²). The drift parameter θ = lim √n (1−ρ) captures the distance from critical loading. This is the classic Iglehart–Whitt theorem. For the GI/GI/1 queue, the workload process V(t) (virtual waiting time) satisfies the Lindley recursion Vn+1 = max(0, Vn + Un) where Un = service time − inter‑arrival time. The heavy‑traffic limit of the scaled workload is the same RBM. In multi‑server systems (GI/GI/k), the Halfin–Whitt regime scales both the arrival rate and the number of servers: λn = nμ − β√n. The limit is a diffusion with state‑dependent drift, often called the Erlang‑RBM. For networks, the Reiman–Williams theorem gives a reflected Brownian motion in a polyhedral cone as the limit of open Jackson networks under heavy traffic. The reflection matrix is determined by the routing matrix and the service rates. These diffusion approximations enable tractable computation of steady‑state distributions, tail probabilities, and performance metrics such as the probability of delay exceeding a threshold. They also justify the use of the square‑root staffing rule in call centers: to keep the delay probability constant, the number of servers should grow as n + β√n.

Worked Example: Heavy‑Traffic Limit of an M/M/1 Queue
Consider an M/M/1 queue with arrival rate λ = 1 − ε and service rate μ = 1, where ε > 0 is small. Let Qε(t) be the queue length at time t. Define the scaled process Xε(t) = ε Qε(t/ε²). Show that as ε → 0, Xε converges to a reflected Brownian motion with drift −1 and diffusion coefficient 2.
The M/M/1 queue has inter‑arrival times Exp(λ) and service times Exp(μ). The traffic intensity ρ = λ/μ = 1−ε. The mean inter‑arrival time is 1/λ ≈ 1+ε, variance 1/λ² ≈ 1. The mean service time is 1, variance 1. The centered net input process has mean −ε and variance λ+μ ≈ 2 per unit time. Over a time interval of length t/ε², the cumulative net input has mean −ε·(t/ε²) = −t/ε and variance 2·(t/ε²). Scaling by ε gives drift −t and variance 2t. The reflection at zero is preserved by the continuous‑mapping theorem for the Skorokhod map. Hence Xε ⇒ RBM with drift −1 and diffusion coefficient 2.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Non‑Lipschitz Coefficients: Pathwise Uniqueness and the Yamada–Watanabe Theorem

Classical existence and uniqueness theory for SDEs dXt = b(t,Xt) dt + σ(t,Xt) dWt assumes global Lipschitz continuity of b and σ. Many models (e.g., the CIR model dXt = κ(θ−Xt) dt + ξ√Xt dWt, the Bessel process dXt = (d−1)/(2Xt) dt + dWt, and the Tanaka equation dXt = sgn(Xt) dWt) violate this condition. The Yamada–Watanabe theorem states that if pathwise uniqueness holds for an SDE and a weak solution exists, then a unique strong solution exists and the solution is a measurable functional of the driving Brownian motion. Pathwise uniqueness can be established under the Yamada–Watanabe condition: |σ(x)−σ(y)|² ≤ ρ(|x−y|) where ρ is a concave increasing function on [0,∞) with ρ(0)=0 and ∫0+ du/ρ(u) = ∞ (Osgood condition). For the CIR process, σ(x) = ξ√x satisfies |√x−√y|² ≤ |x−y|, so ρ(u)=u works and the integral diverges, giving pathwise uniqueness for x≥0. The Bessel process with dimension d≥2 has σ≡1 (Lipschitz) but drift singular at 0; pathwise uniqueness follows from a comparison argument with the squared Bessel process. For the Tanaka equation, σ(x)=sgn(x) is discontinuous; pathwise uniqueness fails (there are multiple strong solutions), but weak uniqueness holds and the solution is a reflecting Brownian motion. The Engelbert–Schmidt theorem gives necessary and sufficient conditions for weak existence and uniqueness for one‑dimensional time‑homogeneous SDEs with continuous σ>0: the scale function and speed measure must satisfy certain integrability conditions. When σ can vanish, the Feller test for explosions and boundary classification (entrance, exit, regular, natural) determines whether a unique strong solution exists up to the boundary.

Interacting Particle Systems and Hydrodynamic Limits

Interacting particle systems are Markov processes on configuration spaces {0,1}ℤd (exclusion processes) or ℕℤd (zero‑range processes) where particles jump according to rates that depend on the local configuration. The simple symmetric exclusion process (SSEP) has at most one particle per site; a particle at x attempts to jump to a nearest neighbour y at rate 1/2d, succeeding only if y is empty. The asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP) adds a drift p−q. The generator acts on cylinder functions f as Lf(η) = ∑x,y c(x,y,η)[f(ηx,y)−f(η)], where ηx,y swaps occupations at x and y. Hydrodynamic limits describe the macroscopic density profile ρ(t,u) = limn→∞ (1/nd) ∑x ηt n²(x) φ(x/n) for smooth test functions φ. For SSEP, the limit satisfies the heat equation ∂tρ = ½ Δρ. For ASEP with weak asymmetry (p−q = O(1/n)), the limit is the viscous Burgers equation ∂tρ = ½ Δρ − ∇·(ρ(1−ρ)). The proof uses the relative entropy method (Yau) or the compensated compactness method. The zero‑range process with jump rate g(k) has invariant product measures with marginals μ(k) ∝ 1/(g(1)…g(k)). Its hydrodynamic limit is a nonlinear diffusion ∂tρ = ΔΦ(ρ) where Φ(ρ) = Eμρ[g(η(0))]. The Kipnis–Varadhan central limit theorem gives Gaussian fluctuations around the hydrodynamic limit, with covariance given by the Green–Kubo formula. Large deviations for the empirical density are governed by a rate functional derived from the dynamical large deviation principle (Donsker–Varadhan). These results connect microscopic stochastic dynamics to macroscopic PDEs and provide a rigorous foundation for non‑equilibrium statistical mechanics.

Stochastic Calculus for General Semimartingales: The Bichteler–Dellacherie Theorem

The Itô integral is initially defined for integrators that are continuous local martingales or, more generally, for locally bounded predictable processes integrated against a semimartingale. A semimartingale is a process X that can be decomposed as X = M + A, where M is a local martingale and A is a finite‑variation adapted process. The Bichteler–Dellacherie theorem characterizes semimartingales as exactly those adapted càdlàg processes for which the stochastic integral ∫ H dX can be defined for all bounded predictable H in a way that is continuous in the Emery topology (the topology of uniform convergence in probability of the integrals of simple predictable processes). Equivalently, X is a semimartingale iff the set of integrals of simple predictable processes bounded by 1 is bounded in probability. This theorem justifies the general theory of stochastic integration developed by Métivier–Pellaumail and Protter. For a general semimartingale X, the quadratic variation [X] exists and the Itô formula reads f(Xt) = f(X0) + ∫0t f′(Xs−) dXs + ½ ∫0t f″(Xs−) d[X]sc + ∑s≤t [f(Xs)−f(Xs−)−f′(Xs−)ΔXs], where [X]c is the continuous part of the quadratic variation. The Doléans–Dade exponential ℰ(X)t = exp(Xt−½[X]tc) ∏s≤t (1+ΔXs) e−ΔXs solves dZ = Z− dX. This framework underpins the theory of stochastic differential equations with jumps, the general martingale representation property, and the change‑of‑measure techniques used in mathematical finance for models with jumps (e.g., Lévy models, jump‑diffusions).

McKean–Vlasov Equations, Propagation of Chaos, and Mean‑Field Limits

The McKean–Vlasov equation describes the limiting dynamics of a system of N interacting particles when N → ∞. Each particle follows a stochastic differential equation whose coefficients depend on the empirical distribution of the whole system. In the limit, the law of a typical particle satisfies a nonlinear Fokker–Planck (or Kolmogorov) equation where the drift and diffusion coefficients are functionals of the solution itself. Formally, if Xit solves dXit = b(Xit, μNt) dt + σ(Xit, μNt) dWit with μNt = (1/N) ∑j=1N δXjt, then the limiting law μt solves ∂tμ = −∇·(b(·,μ)μ) + ½ Δ(σσ⊤(·,μ)μ). This is the McKean–Vlasov (or nonlinear Fokker–Planck) equation.

Propagation of chaos, proved by Sznitman (1991) using coupling and martingale methods, states that for any fixed k, the joint law of (X1,…,Xk) converges to the product measure μt⊗k. The key assumption is Lipschitz continuity of b and σ in both the state variable and the measure argument (with respect to Wasserstein distance). Under these conditions, the particle system is well‑posed and the convergence rate in Wasserstein‑1 is O(N−½). The result justifies mean‑field approximations in neuroscience (e.g., the Kuramoto model), finance (systemic risk models), and game theory (mean‑field games).

Examiners often test the distinction between the McKean–Vlasov limit (a deterministic PDE for the law) and the mean‑field game limit (a coupled forward‑backward system where agents optimize). In the McKean–Vlasov setting, the interaction is through the law of the state; in mean‑field games, agents also respond to a value function. Another common trap is assuming propagation of chaos holds for singular kernels (e.g., Coulomb interaction) without additional regularity; the classical theory requires Lipschitz coefficients, though recent work (Jabin–Wang, 2018) extends it to moderately singular interactions using modulated energy.

Key references: McKean (1966) for the nonlinear Markov process; Sznitman (1991) “Topics in propagation of chaos”; Jabin–Wang (2018) “Quantitative estimates of propagation of chaos for singular interactions”.

Wright–Fisher Diffusions, the Moran Model, and Coalescent Theory in Population Genetics

The Wright–Fisher model describes the evolution of allele frequencies in a finite population of size N with non‑overlapping generations. In the diffusion limit (N → ∞, time rescaled by N), the allele frequency Xt ∈ [0,1] satisfies the stochastic differential equation dXt = ½ Xt(1−Xt) dt + √(Xt(1−Xt)) dWt. This is the Wright–Fisher diffusion with generator L = ½ x(1−x) d²/dx². The boundaries 0 and 1 are absorbing (fixation or loss). The fixation probability of an allele starting at frequency x is x; the expected time to absorption is −2[x log x + (1−x) log(1−x)].

The Moran model uses overlapping generations: at each step one individual reproduces and one dies. Its diffusion limit is identical to Wright–Fisher but with a different time scaling (factor 2). Both models belong to the same universality class. Kingman’s coalescent (1982) provides the genealogical dual: tracing ancestry backwards, the number of ancestral lineages follows a pure death process where any pair merges at rate 1. The coalescent gives the distribution of the time to the most recent common ancestor (TMRCA) and the site‑frequency spectrum under neutrality.

Selection adds a drift term: dXt = sXt(1−Xt) dt + √(Xt(1−Xt)) dWt. The fixation probability becomes (1−e−2sx)/(1−e−2s). Mutation introduces a stationary distribution (Beta with parameters 2Nμ, 2Nν). Examiners frequently ask for the generator of the Wright–Fisher diffusion with selection and mutation, or to derive the coalescent from the Moran model using the duality relation E[Xtk] = E[X0Kt] where Kt is the number of ancestral lineages.

Key references: Kimura (1964) “Diffusion models in population genetics”; Kingman (1982) “The coalescent”; Etheridge (2011) “Some mathematical models from population genetics”.

Hawkes Processes and Self‑Exciting Point Processes in Neuroscience and Finance

A Hawkes process is a point process on ℝ+ with stochastic intensity λt = μ + ∫0t φ(t−s) dNs, where μ > 0 is the baseline intensity, φ ≥ 0 is the excitation kernel, and N is the counting process. Each arrival increases the instantaneous rate, creating clusters of events. The process is stationary iff the branching ratio ‖φ‖1 = ∫0∞ φ(u) du < 1. In that case, the stationary intensity is μ/(1−‖φ‖1). The cluster representation (Hawkes & Oakes, 1974) views each immigrant (Poisson rate μ) as generating a Galton–Watson branching process with offspring distribution Poisson(φ).

In neuroscience, Hawkes processes model spike trains where a neuron’s firing excites its own future firing (refractory effects are captured by inhibitory kernels). In finance, they model high‑frequency trade arrivals and contagion in limit order books (Bacry et al., 2015). The exponential kernel φ(t) = α e−βt yields a Markovian representation: define the auxiliary process Yt = ∫0t e−β(t−s) dNs, then dYt = −β Yt dt + dNt and λt = μ + α Yt. This allows likelihood‑based inference via the EM algorithm or MCMC.

Examiners test the stability condition ‖φ‖1 < 1, the computation of the covariance density (Bartlett spectrum), and the relationship between the Hawkes process and the Poisson cluster process. A common mistake is to treat the intensity as deterministic when computing moments; the correct approach uses the branching structure or the Wiener–Hopf factorization. The multivariate Hawkes process extends to networks: λti = μi + ∑j ∫ φij(t−s) dNsj. Stability requires the spectral radius of the matrix (‖φij‖1) to be < 1.

Key references: Hawkes (1971) “Spectra of some self‑exciting and mutually exciting point processes”; Hawkes & Oakes (1974) “A cluster process representation of a self‑exciting process”; Bacry, Mastromatteo & Muzy (2015) “Hawkes processes in finance”.

Stochastic Gradient Descent as a Diffusion Process and Score‑Based Generative Models

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with constant step size η can be approximated by a stochastic differential equation. For a loss L(θ) = Eξ[ℓ(θ;ξ)], the update θk+1 = θk − η ∇ℓ(θk; ξk) yields, under appropriate scaling (η → 0, time t = ηk), the diffusion dθt = −∇L(θt) dt + √η Σ(θt)½ dWt, where Σ(θ) = Cov(∇ℓ(θ;ξ)). This approximation (Li, Tai & E, 2017; Mandt, Hoffman & Blei, 2017) explains the implicit regularization of SGD: the stationary distribution concentrates near flat minima because the noise covariance Σ(θ) is larger in sharp directions.

Score‑based generative models (Song & Ermon, 2019; Song et al., 2020) use a forward diffusion dXt = −½ β(t) Xt dt + √β(t) dWt that gradually perturbs data to Gaussian noise. The reverse‑time SDE (Anderson, 1982) is dXt = [−½ β(t) Xt − β(t) ∇ log pt(Xt)] dt + √β(t) dW̃t, where ∇ log pt is the score function. Training a neural network to estimate the score enables sampling by simulating the reverse SDE. The probability flow ODE dXt = −½ β(t) Xt − ½ β(t) ∇ log pt(Xt) provides a deterministic sampler with exact likelihood computation via the instantaneous change of variables formula.

Exam questions often ask to derive the reverse‑time SDE from the forward SDE using the Fokker–Planck equation and the time‑reversal formula for diffusions. Another frequent topic is the connection between the SGD diffusion and the Langevin dynamics used in Bayesian sampling: both have the form dθ = −∇U(θ) dt + √(2β−1) dW, but SGD noise is state‑dependent and anisotropic. Candidates should be able to write the generator of the SGD diffusion and discuss the conditions under which the invariant measure approximates the posterior (e.g., when Σ(θ) ≈ 2β−1I).

Key references: Li, Tai & E (2017) “Stochastic modified equations and adaptive stochastic gradient algorithms”; Mandt, Hoffman & Blei (2017) “Stochastic gradient descent as approximate Bayesian inference”; Song & Ermon (2019) “Generative modeling by estimating gradients of the data distribution”; Song et al. (2020) “Score‑based generative modeling through stochastic differential equations”.

The KPZ Equation, Stochastic Heat Equation, and Regularity Structures

The Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation ∂th = ½ ∂x²h + ½ (∂xh)² + ξ, where ξ is space‑time white noise, models random interface growth. The nonlinearity (∂xh)² is ill‑defined because ∂xh is distribution‑valued. The Cole–Hopf transform Z = exp(h) converts KPZ into the stochastic heat equation (SHE) with multiplicative noise: ∂tZ = ½ ∂x²Z + Z ξ. The SHE is well‑posed in the Itô sense; its solution is a positive continuous function. The Hopf–Cole solution of KPZ is then h = log Z.

For general nonlinear SPDEs where no transform exists, Hairer’s theory of regularity structures (2013) provides a solution framework. The idea is to lift the equation to a space of modelled distributions that encode the local behaviour of the solution via a hierarchy of abstract symbols (e.g., Ξ for noise, 𝐗 for integration against the heat kernel, and products). A model assigns concrete distributions to these symbols, and a reconstruction operator maps modelled distributions back to actual distributions. The solution map is then built by a fixed‑point argument in a space of modelled distributions with appropriate regularity exponents. This theory solved the Φ⁴3 equation, the KPZ equation in the “rough” regime, and the stochastic quantization of the 2D Ising model.

Examiners focus on the distinction between the Cole–Hopf solution (which requires the specific quadratic nonlinearity) and the regularity‑structure solution (which works for a wide class of subcritical SPDEs). A typical trap is to claim that the KPZ equation is well‑posed in the classical sense; it is not — the solution is defined only via the Hopf–Cole transform or regularity structures. Another trap is confusing the Itô and Stratonovich interpretations of the SHE; the Hopf–Cole transform corresponds to the Itô interpretation. The invariant measure of the KPZ equation on the torus is the law of a Brownian bridge conditioned to have zero mean, a result of Bertini & Giacomin (1997).

Key references: Kardar, Parisi & Zhang (1986) “Dynamic scaling of growing interfaces”; Bertini & Giacomin (1997) “Stochastic Burgers and KPZ equations from particle systems”; Hairer (2013) “Solving the KPZ equation”; Hairer (2014) “A theory of regularity structures”.

  • Using the Wright–Fisher diffusion generator without the factor ½ in the diffusion coefficient, or forgetting the absorbing boundaries at 0 and 1.

G‑Expectation, G‑Brownian Motion, and Nonlinear Stochastic Calculus

The classical Itô calculus assumes a single probability measure and a linear expectation. In many financial and robust‑control problems the volatility is uncertain, leading to a family of priors. Peng’s G‑expectation (2006, 2007) replaces the linear expectation by a sublinear functional EG[·] defined on a space of random variables. The associated G‑normal distribution is characterized by a set of variances σ² ∈ [σ²min, σ²max]; its density solves a fully nonlinear heat equation ∂tu = G(∂xxu) with G(a) = ½(σ²maxa⁺ − σ²mina⁻). A G‑Brownian motion (Bt)t≥0 is a process with independent, stationary increments such that B1 is G‑normal. Its quadratic variation ⟨B⟩t is a process with values in [σ²mint, σ²maxt] and is not deterministic.

The Itô integral ∫₀ᵗ ξs dBs is constructed for quasi‑continuous processes ξ via a capacity‑based completion. The resulting Itô formula for a C² function f reads f(Bt) = f(0) + ∫₀ᵗ f′(Bs) dBs + ½∫₀ᵗ f″(Bs) d⟨B⟩s. Because ⟨B⟩ is uncertain, the second‑order term is a G‑Itô integral with respect to the increasing process ⟨B⟩. Peng’s representation theorem shows that every G‑expectation admits a representation EG[X] = supP∈𝒫 EP[X] where 𝒫 is a weakly compact set of probability measures corresponding to all possible volatility paths. This links G‑calculus to robust stochastic control and to the pricing of options under volatility uncertainty (the “G‑Black–Scholes” model). Examiners often ask to derive the G‑heat equation from the definition of G‑normality or to prove the comparison principle for viscosity solutions of ∂tu = G(∂xxu).

Optimal Stopping, Free Boundary Problems, and the Smooth‑Fit Principle

An optimal stopping problem asks for a stopping time τ that maximizes E[g(Xτ)e−rτ] for a diffusion X. The value function V(x) = supτ Ex[g(Xτ)e−rτ] satisfies the variational inequality max{ℒV − rV, g − V} = 0, where ℒ is the infinitesimal generator. The continuation region C = {x : V(x) > g(x)} and the stopping region D = {x : V(x) = g(x)} are separated by a free boundary ∂C. For one‑dimensional regular diffusions the smooth‑fit principle asserts that V is C¹ across the boundary: V′(x*) = g′(x*) at the optimal stopping point x*. This condition, together with the value‑matching V(x*) = g(x*), determines the free boundary uniquely.

The classic example is the perpetual American put on a geometric Brownian motion dS = rS dt + σS dW with payoff (K − S)⁺. The value function is V(S) = A Sγ for S > S* and V(S) = K − S for S ≤ S*, where γ < 0 is the negative root of ½σ²γ(γ−1) + rγ − r = 0. Smooth‑fit gives Aγ S*γ−1 = −1, and value‑matching gives A S*γ = K − S*. Solving yields S* = (γ/(γ−1)) K. The Snell envelope construction shows that the optimal stopping time is τ* = inf{t ≥ 0 : St ≤ S*}. In higher dimensions smooth‑fit may fail (e.g., for the American option on two assets), and the free boundary is characterized by the principle of “continuous fit” only. Examiners frequently test the derivation of the ODE for V in the continuation region, the verification that the candidate (V, τ*) satisfies the martingale optimality conditions, and the distinction between smooth‑fit and continuous‑fit.

Smooth‑Fit Verification for Perpetual American Put
Let S follow dS = rS dt + σS dW under the risk‑neutral measure, r > 0, σ > 0. The perpetual American put payoff is (K − S)⁺. Derive the optimal stopping threshold S* using the smooth‑fit principle and verify that the resulting value function satisfies the variational inequality.

1. In the continuation region S > S* the value function V solves ½σ²S²V″ + rSV′ − rV = 0. Try V(S) = A Sγ. Substituting gives the characteristic equation ½σ²γ(γ−1) + rγ − r = 0. The negative root is γ = (½σ² − r − √((r − ½σ²)² + 2rσ²))/σ² < 0.

2. Value‑matching: A S*γ = K − S*. Smooth‑fit: Aγ S*γ−1 = −1. Dividing the second by the first yields γ/S* = −1/(K − S*), hence S* = (γ/(γ−1)) K. Since γ < 0, γ/(γ−1) ∈ (0,1), so S* < K.

3. With this S* and A = (K − S*)/S*γ, define V(S) = A Sγ for S > S* and V(S) = K − S for S ≤ S*. One checks that V ∈ C¹, V ≥ (K − S)⁺, and ℒV − rV ≤ 0 everywhere with equality on (S*, ∞). Hence V is the value function and τ* = inf{t : St ≤ S*} is optimal.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a free‑boundary problem appears, first write the ODE in the continuation region, find the general solution, then impose value‑matching and smooth‑fit (or continuous‑fit if the diffusion is degenerate). Always state the verification theorem: the candidate must be C¹, dominate the payoff, and make the discounted process a supermartingale with equality up to the stopping time. This checklist earns full marks even if the algebra is heavy.

Viscosity Solutions of Hamilton‑Jacobi‑Bellman Equations in Stochastic Control

The dynamic programming principle leads to the Hamilton‑Jacobi‑Bellman (HJB) equation supa∈A{ℒau(x) + f(x,a)} = 0, where ℒa is the generator of the controlled diffusion. Classical solutions rarely exist because the supremum creates kinks. Crandall and Lions (1983) introduced viscosity solutions: a continuous function u is a viscosity subsolution if for every φ ∈ C² touching u from above at x₀, supa{ℒaφ(x₀) + f(x₀,a)} ≤ 0; it is a supersolution if the reverse inequality holds for test functions touching from below. A viscosity solution is both.

The comparison principle (uniqueness) holds under standard structural conditions: the Hamiltonian H(x,p,X) = supa{tr(σ(x,a)σ(x,a)ᵀX) + b(x,a)·p + f(x,a)} is degenerate elliptic and satisfies a Lipschitz condition in x. Existence follows from Perron’s method using the fact that the value function of the control problem is a viscosity solution. In the Merton portfolio problem with utility U(x) = x1−γ/(1−γ), the HJB reduces to an ODE whose viscosity solution coincides with the classical smooth solution because the optimal feedback is regular. For problems with state constraints or discontinuous coefficients, viscosity theory is indispensable. Examiners often ask to verify that a given candidate is a viscosity sub/supersolution by checking the test‑function inequalities, or to prove the comparison principle for a specific Hamiltonian using the doubling‑variables technique.

Particle Filtering, Sequential Monte Carlo, and the Bootstrap Filter

In nonlinear filtering the signal Xt follows dX = b(X) dt + σ(X) dW and observations Yt = h(Xt) + noise. The conditional distribution πt(dx) = P(Xt ∈ dx | Y[0,t]) satisfies the Kushner–Stratonovich SPDE. The bootstrap filter (Gordon, Salmond, Smith, 1993) approximates πt by an empirical measure πtN = (1/N)∑i=1N δXt(i). The algorithm alternates prediction (sample Xt+Δ(i) from the transition kernel) and update (weight wt+Δ(i) ∝ g(Yt+Δ | Xt+Δ(i)) where g is the observation likelihood). To avoid weight degeneracy, a resampling step draws N new particles with probabilities proportional to the weights. The effective sample size ESS = 1/∑(w(i))² monitors degeneracy; resampling is triggered when ESS < N/2.

Convergence results (Del Moral, 2004) show that for bounded likelihoods the Lp error of the particle approximation is O(N−½) uniformly in time under mixing assumptions. Advanced variants include the auxiliary particle filter (Pitt & Shephard, 1999) which uses a look‑ahead step to improve proposal quality, and particle Markov chain Monte Carlo (Andrieu, Doucet, Holenstein, 2010) for parameter estimation. In exams, typical questions ask to write the weight update formula for a given observation model, to explain why multinomial resampling introduces additional variance, or to derive the asymptotic variance of the particle estimate of E[φ(Xt) | Y[0,t]].

Stochastic Numerics for SPDEs: Finite Element and Spectral Methods

Consider the parabolic SPDE du = (Au + f(u)) dt + B dW on a bounded domain D ⊂ ℝd with Dirichlet boundary conditions, where A = Δ is the Laplacian, W is a cylindrical Wiener process, and B is a trace‑class operator. A finite element method (FEM) discretizes space by a triangulation with mesh size h, yielding a finite‑dimensional subspace Vh ⊂ H₀¹(D). The semi‑discrete Galerkin approximation uh(t) ∈ Vh satisfies (duh, v) = −(∇uh, ∇v) dt + (f(uh), v) dt + (B dW, v) for all v ∈ Vh. Under standard regularity assumptions (u ∈ L²(Ω; C([0,T]; H²∩H₀¹))) the strong error satisfies E[‖u(t) − uh(t)‖L²] ≤ C hβ with β = 1 for additive noise and β = ½ for multiplicative noise. Full discretization adds a time‑stepping scheme (e.g., Euler–Maruyama or Crank–Nicolson) with step Δt; the combined strong order is min(β, ½) for Euler–Maruyama.

Spectral Galerkin methods use the eigenfunctions {ek} of −A as basis. Truncating to the first N modes gives a system of N coupled SDEs. For the stochastic heat equation with additive noise, the spectral method achieves exponential convergence in space for smooth initial data, and the strong error is O(N−α) with α depending on the noise regularity. The covariance operator Q = BB* determines the noise regularity; if Q is trace‑class, the solution is spatially continuous. Numerical analysis references include Lord, Powell & Shardlow (2014) for FEM and Jentzen & Kloeden (2011) for spectral methods. Examiners often ask to derive the weak formulation, to state the CFL‑type condition for explicit time stepping, or to compare the computational complexity of FEM versus spectral methods for a given error tolerance.

Regime‑Switching Diffusions and Hidden Markov Models

A regime‑switching diffusion couples a continuous‑state diffusion Xt with a finite‑state continuous‑time Markov chain αt taking values in {1,…,m}. The dynamics are

dXt = b(Xt, αt) dt + σ(Xt, αt) dWt, αt jumps with generator Q = (qij).

The pair (Xt, αt) is a Markov process on ℝd × {1,…,m}. Its infinitesimal generator acts on smooth f(x, i) as

Lf(x, i) = b(x, i)·∇xf + ½ Tr[σσT(x, i) ∇x2f] + ∑j qij (f(x, j) − f(x, i)).

Key applications include stochastic volatility models where αt represents volatility regimes, and credit risk where regimes encode macro‑economic states. Estimation typically uses the EM algorithm on discretised observations, treating αt as hidden. The forward‑backward recursions for the hidden Markov model yield the smoothed regime probabilities γt(i) = P(αt = i | X0:T).

Examiners often test the derivation of the coupled Kolmogorov forward equations for the joint density p(t, x, i), the verification of the martingale problem for the generator, and the construction of the likelihood function via the innovation process. A common twist is to ask for the ergodic distribution of (X, α) when b and σ are regime‑dependent but satisfy a uniform Lyapunov condition.

Volterra Stochastic Differential Equations and Fractional Kernels

Volterra‑type SDEs replace the standard Itô integral by a convolution with a deterministic kernel K(t, s):

Xt = X0 + ∫0t K(t, s) b(Xs) ds + ∫0t K(t, s) σ(Xs) dWs.

When K(t, s) = (t−s)H−½ / Γ(H+½) with H ∈ (0,1), the driving noise is fractional Brownian motion (fBm) of Hurst index H. For H > ½ the kernel is square‑integrable and the integral can be defined pathwise via Young integration; for H < ½ one uses rough‑path or Malliavin techniques.

Existence and uniqueness theory relies on the Lipschitz continuity of b, σ and the regularity of the kernel. The seminal result of Nualart and Răşcanu (2002) shows pathwise uniqueness for H > ½ under a linear growth condition. For H ≤ ½, the work of Decreusefond and Üstünel (1999) provides a Malliavin calculus framework.

Applications appear in turbulence modelling (the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process), in finance (rough volatility models where the log‑volatility follows a Volterra SDE with H ≈ 0.1), and in neuroscience (fractional integrate‑and‑fire neurons). Numerical schemes include the Euler–Maruyama method with discrete convolution weights; the strong convergence order is H for H > ½ and ½ for H ≤ ½.

Exam questions frequently ask to derive the covariance structure of the solution when b = −θX, σ = 1, to prove that the process is Gaussian and to compute its spectral density. Another favourite is to show that the quadratic variation of the fBm‑driven integral vanishes for H > ½, implying that Itô’s formula must be replaced by a pathwise chain rule.

Worked Example: Regime‑Switching Ornstein–Uhlenbeck
Consider dX_t = -θα_t X_t dt + σα_t dW_t with α_t a two‑state Markov chain (states 1,2) and generator Q = [[-λ, λ], [μ, -μ]]. Derive the stationary distribution of (X_t, α_t) and compute E[X_t2] in steady state.

Step 1: Write the generator L acting on f(x,i): Lf = -θ_i x ∂_x f + ½ σ_i2 ∂_x2 f + ∑_j qij(f(x,j)-f(x,i)).

Step 2: Seek a Gaussian stationary density p_i(x) = π_i φ(x; 0, v_i) where π = (π1,π2) solves πQ = 0, π1+π2=1. The variances v_i satisfy the coupled Lyapunov equations 2θ_i v_i = σ_i2 + ∑_j qij(v_j - v_i).

Step 3: Solve the linear system for v1, v2. Then E[X2] = π1 v1 + π2 v2.

Step 4: Verify that the joint density integrates to 1 and that the marginal of α_t is π. This yields the unique invariant measure because the process is hypoelliptic and irreducible.

When a regime‑switching SDE appears, first write the joint generator and the coupled Kolmogorov equations. For Volterra equations, identify the kernel regularity (Hurst index) and state which integration theory applies (Young, rough path, Malliavin). Always check whether the question asks for pathwise or distributional properties; the former needs the kernel to be sufficiently regular, the latter often only requires square‑integrability.

Stochastic Differential Equations on Infinite‑Dimensional Hilbert Spaces

Let H be a separable Hilbert space and W a cylindrical Wiener process on another Hilbert space U. An H‑valued SDE reads

dXt = (AXt + F(Xt)) dt + B(Xt) dWt, X0 = x ∈ H.

Here A generates a C0‑semigroup S(t) on H, F: H → H and B: H → L2(U, H) (Hilbert‑Schmidt operators). The mild solution is

Xt = S(t)x + ∫0t S(t−s)F(Xs) ds + ∫0t S(t−s)B(Xs) dWs.

Existence and uniqueness (Da Prato–Zabczyk, 1992) hold when F and B are globally Lipschitz and B takes values in the space of γ‑radonifying operators. For semilinear SPDEs such as the stochastic heat equation, A = Δ with Dirichlet boundary conditions, F(u) = f(u), B(u) = g(u)·, the solution lives in L2(D) and possesses a version with continuous paths in C([0,T]; H−ε).

Invariant measures are studied via the Markov semigroup Ptφ(x) = E[φ(Xtx)]. The Krylov–Bogoliubov theorem guarantees existence of an invariant measure if the family of laws is tight; uniqueness follows from the strong Feller property and irreducibility (Hairer–Mattingly, 2006).

Examiners test the derivation of the mild formulation, the verification that the stochastic convolution ∫0t S(t−s)B dWs is well‑defined (requiring ∫0T ‖S(s)B‖HS2 ds < ∞), and the application of the Itô formula in Hilbert spaces (the Da Prato–Zabczyk version). A typical twist is to ask for the covariance operator of the stationary Gaussian solution when F = 0, B = I, leading to the Lyapunov equation AQ + QA* = −I.

Wong‑Zakai Approximations and Stratonovich Correction for Multiplicative Noise

The Wong‑Zakai theorem states that solutions of SDEs driven by smooth approximations of Brownian motion converge to the Stratonovich solution, not the Itô solution. Let Wn be piecewise linear interpolations of W on a mesh of size 1/n. Consider

dXnt = b(Xnt) dt + σ(Xnt) dWnt.

If σ is C1 with bounded derivative, then Xn converges uniformly on compacts in probability to the solution of the Stratonovich SDE

dXt = b(Xt) dt + σ(Xt) ∘ dWt.

The conversion between Itô and Stratonovich forms is

σ(x) ∘ dWt = σ(x) dWt + ½ σ'(x)σ(x) dt.

This correction term is crucial in physical modelling: the Langevin equation with multiplicative noise is naturally interpreted in the Stratonovich sense because it preserves the chain rule. In finance, the Itô interpretation is standard because it yields martingale properties for discounted prices.

Extensions include convergence rates (Kurtz–Protter, 1991) showing that the error is O(n−½) in the sup‑norm, and the case of rough paths where the limit is the rough‑path solution driven by the enhanced Brownian motion (Lévy area). For SDEs with jumps, the Wong‑Zakai approximation must include the compensated Poisson integral; the limit acquires an additional correction involving the jump measure.

Exam questions often ask to derive the Stratonovich correction for a given σ(x) = x2, to prove that the Itô solution of dX = X2 dW explodes in finite time while the Stratonovich solution does not, or to explain why the Euler–Maruyama scheme converges to the Itô solution whereas the Heun scheme converges to the Stratonovich solution.

  • Confusing the invariant measure of a regime‑switching diffusion with the product of marginal stationary laws; the coupling through the generator must be solved jointly.
  • Assuming that the Volterra kernel (t−s)H−½ yields a semimartingale for all H; it is a semimartingale only when H = ½.
  • Neglecting the Hilbert‑Schmidt condition on the noise coefficient B in infinite‑dimensional SDEs; without it the stochastic convolution is not well‑defined.
  • Applying the standard Itô formula to a Stratonovich SDE without adding the ½ σ'σ correction; this is the most frequent source of sign errors in physics‑oriented problems.
  • Claiming that the Wong‑Zakai approximation converges to the Itô solution for any smooth approximation; the limit is Stratonovich unless the approximation is specifically constructed (e.g., using the Itô‑Taylor scheme).
  • Forgetting that the strong Feller property in infinite dimensions requires the noise to be non‑degenerate on the whole space, not just on a subspace.
  • Misidentifying the Hurst index in rough volatility models; the empirical value H ≈ 0.1 corresponds to a kernel with singularity stronger than Brownian motion, requiring rough‑path integration.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Common Noise and Conditional McKean–Vlasov Limits

When a system of interacting particles is driven by a shared source of randomness, the limiting empirical measure becomes a random probability measure rather than a deterministic flow. This phenomenon is captured by the conditional McKean–Vlasov equation, where the law of the representative particle is conditioned on the common noise path. Formally, let W be a Brownian motion representing the idiosyncratic noise and W0 an independent Brownian motion representing the common noise. The particle system reads

dXi = b(Xi, μN) dt + σ(Xi, μN) dWi + σ0(Xi, μN) dW0,

where μN = (1/N) Σ δXj is the empirical measure. As N → ∞, the conditional law μt = Law(Xt | W0) satisfies the stochastic PDE

dμt = −∇·(b(·, μt) μt) dt + ½ Σ ∂i∂j(aij(·, μt) μt) dt − ∇·(σ0(·, μt) μt) dW0,

with a = σσ⊤. This equation is a stochastic continuity equation driven by the common noise. Well‑posedness was established by Buckdahn, Li, and Peng (2009) using a fixed‑point argument on the space of measure‑valued processes adapted to the filtration of W0. The propagation of chaos holds conditionally: for any fixed k, the joint conditional law of (X1, …, Xk) converges to the product of k copies of μt.

Applications appear in systemic risk modelling, where the common noise represents a macroeconomic shock, and in mean‑field games with common noise, where the equilibrium is a fixed point of the conditional law mapping. The master equation becomes a stochastic PDE on the Wasserstein space, and its solution provides the value function of the representative agent. Numerical approximation uses particle systems with a common Brownian driver; the error analysis requires controlling the conditional Wasserstein distance.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Multiplicative Noise and the Marcus Integral

The Marcus integral provides a canonical interpretation for SDEs driven by Lévy processes with jumps when the diffusion coefficient depends on the state. Unlike the Itô integral, which uses the left limit of the integrand, and the Stratonovich integral, which uses the midpoint, the Marcus integral follows the solution of an ordinary differential equation along each jump. For a Lévy process L with jump measure ν and a C1 vector field σ : ℝd → ℝd×ℝm, the Marcus SDE

dXt = b(Xt) dt + σ(Xt) ⋄ dLt

is defined by the following rule: at a jump time τ with jump size ΔLτ = z, the solution jumps from Xτ− to Φσ(1, Xτ−, z), where Φσ is the flow of the ODE dy/ds = σ(y)z, y(0) = Xτ−. The continuous part of L is integrated in the Stratonovich sense. This definition preserves the chain rule: if f ∈ C2, then f(Xt) satisfies the Marcus formula with the same jump transformation.

The Marcus integral arises naturally in physical systems where the noise has a small correlation time and the limit of Wong–Zakai approximations with coloured noise yields the Marcus interpretation (Kurtz, Pardoux, Protter, 1995). In finance, the Marcus integral is used for models with jumps in volatility where the jump transformation must respect the geometry of the state space (e.g., positivity of variance). The conversion between Itô and Marcus forms involves the compensator of the jump measure and the derivative of the flow: the Marcus drift correction is ∫ [Φσ(1, x, z) − x − σ(x)z] ν(dz).

Examiners often ask to compute the Marcus correction for a specific σ, to show that the Marcus solution stays on a manifold if σ is tangent to it, or to compare the generators of the Itô, Stratonovich, and Marcus interpretations. A typical trap is to apply the Itô formula to a Marcus SDE without adding the jump correction, leading to an incorrect generator.

Worked Example: Marcus Correction for Geometric Lévy Noise
Consider the one‑dimensional Marcus SDE dXt = Xt ⋄ dLt, where L is a pure‑jump Lévy process with Lévy measure ν(dz) = c|z|−1−α dz (α ∈ (0,2)). Derive the equivalent Itô SDE and write its generator acting on f ∈ Cc2(ℝ).
For σ(x) = x, the flow of dy/ds = yz is Φσ(1, x, z) = x ez. The Marcus jump transformation is x → x ez. The Itô correction term is ∫ [x ez − x − xz] ν(dz) = x ∫ (ez − 1 − z) ν(dz). Hence the Itô SDE is dXt = Xt dLt + Xt [∫ (ez − 1 − z) ν(dz)] dt. The generator is Lf(x) = ∫ [f(x ez) − f(x) − xf′(x)z] ν(dz).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mentions “canonical interpretation for jump SDEs” or “preserves the chain rule”, think Marcus. Write down the flow ODE, compute the jump map, and then subtract the Itô jump (σ(x)z) to obtain the drift correction. Keep the Lévy measure explicit; do not assume finite activity unless stated.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Time‑Inhomogeneous Coefficients and Forward–Backward Systems

Many control and filtering problems lead to forward–backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDEs) where the forward component has time‑dependent coefficients. The system

dXt = b(t, Xt, Yt) dt + σ(t, Xt, Yt) dWt,

−dYt = f(t, Xt, Yt, Zt) dt − Zt dWt,

with terminal condition YT = g(XT), appears in stochastic maximum principle and in the representation of viscosity solutions of parabolic PDEs with time‑dependent coefficients. The well‑posedness theory (Peng, Wu, 1999) uses a contraction mapping on the space of adapted processes under a norm weighted by an exponential factor eβt to handle the time‑inhomogeneity. The key assumption is a monotonicity condition: ⟨b(t, x, y) − b(t, x′, y), x − x′⟩ + ⟨f(t, x, y, z) − f(t, x′, y, z), y − y′⟩ ≤ −μ(|x − x′|2 + |y − y′|2) for some μ > 0.

When the coefficients are periodic in time, the FBSDE admits a periodic solution, which corresponds to a periodic viscosity solution of the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation. Numerical schemes combine a forward Euler discretisation for X with a backward regression for Y, Z (the least‑squares Monte Carlo method). The error analysis requires controlling the time‑discretisation bias, which is of order Δt for the forward component and √Δt for the backward component under Lipschitz assumptions.

Examiners test the derivation of the adjoint FBSDE from a stochastic control problem, the verification theorem linking the value function to the solution of the FBSDE, and the monotonicity condition that guarantees uniqueness. A common mistake is to treat the backward equation as a standard SDE and apply Itô’s formula forward in time; the correct approach is to apply Itô’s formula to Yt from t to T and use the terminal condition.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Constraints: Variational Inequalities and Obstacle Problems

When the state of a diffusion is constrained to stay in a closed convex set K ⊂ ℝd, the dynamics are described by a reflected SDE or, equivalently, by a variational inequality. The Skorokhod problem finds a pair (X, K) such that Xt = x + ∫0t b(Xs) ds + ∫0t σ(Xs) dWs + Kt, where K is a finite‑variation process that pushes X back into K only when X hits the boundary ∂K, and dKt = n(Xt) d|K|t with n the inward normal. The associated infinitesimal generator is the Neumann Laplacian on K with domain {f ∈ C2(K) : ∂nf = 0 on ∂K}.

Obstacle problems arise in optimal stopping: the value function u(t, x) = supτ ≤ T−t 𝔼[g(Xt+τ)] satisfies the variational inequality min(−∂tu − Lu, u − g) = 0, where L is the generator of the unconstrained diffusion. The solution coincides with the Snell envelope of the payoff process. The free boundary ∂{(t, x) : u > g} is characterised by the smooth‑fit principle: u is C1 across the boundary under non‑degeneracy of σ.

In finance, American option pricing leads to an obstacle problem for the Black–Scholes operator. The early‑exercise premium representation decomposes the American price into the European price plus an integral of the local time of the underlying at the optimal exercise boundary. Numerical methods include penalisation (adding a large penalty term for violating the constraint) and tree‑based methods that enforce the constraint at each node. Examiners often ask to derive the variational inequality from the dynamic programming principle, to prove the smooth‑fit condition for a one‑dimensional diffusion, or to compute the local‑time correction in the early‑exercise premium formula.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Non‑Local Operators: Fractional Laplacian and Lévy Generators

When the driving noise is a pure‑jump Lévy process with a heavy‑tailed Lévy measure, the infinitesimal generator is a non‑local operator. For a symmetric α‑stable process (α ∈ (0,2)), the generator is the fractional Laplacian −(−Δ)α/2, defined via the principal value integral

(−Δ)α/2f(x) = Cd,α ∫ℝd (f(x) − f(y)) / |x − y|d+α dy.

More generally, for a Lévy process with Lévy measure ν, the generator acts as

Lf(x) = ∫ℝd{0} (f(x+z) − f(x) − 𝟙{|z|<1} ⟨∇f(x), z⟩) ν(dz).

The associated SDE is dXt = b(Xt) dt + dLt. When b is Lipschitz, existence and uniqueness follow from the martingale problem for L + b·∇. The heat kernel of the fractional Laplacian satisfies the scaling pt(x, y) = t−d/α p1(t−1/α(x−y)), and exhibits a polynomial tail pt(x, y) ∼ t / |x−y|d+α for large |x−y|.

Applications include anomalous diffusion in physics, where the mean‑square displacement grows as t2/α, and option pricing under Lévy models (e.g., CGMY, variance gamma). The fractional PDE ∂tu = −(−Δ)α/2u + f is solved numerically by spectral methods (using the Fourier symbol |ξ|α) or by finite‑difference schemes on truncated domains with appropriate boundary conditions. Examiners test the derivation of the generator from the Lévy–Khintchine formula, the scaling properties of the stable process, and the distinction between the restricted fractional Laplacian (Dirichlet exterior condition) and the spectral fractional Laplacian (Neumann‑type). A frequent trap is to confuse the two definitions when solving boundary value problems on bounded domains.

  • Confusing the conditional McKean–Vlasov limit with the standard deterministic McKean–Vlasov limit; the former is a random measure adapted to the common noise filtration.
  • Applying the Itô formula to a Marcus SDE without the jump correction term ∫[Φ(1,x,z)−x−σ(x)z]ν(dz).
  • Treating the backward equation of an FBSDE as a forward SDE and integrating from 0 to t instead of from t to T.
  • Assuming the smooth‑fit principle holds for all obstacle problems; it requires non‑degenerate diffusion and a regular payoff.
  • Using the spectral fractional Laplacian when the problem specifies the restricted fractional Laplacian (or vice‑versa) on a bounded domain.
  • Neglecting the compensator term in the generator of a pure‑jump Lévy process when the Lévy measure has infinite mass near zero.
  • Forgetting that the Marcus integral coincides with the Stratonovich integral only for continuous drivers; for jumps the two interpretations differ.

Stochastic Differential Equations with State‑Dependent Switching and Piecewise Deterministic Markov Processes

Piecewise deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs) combine deterministic flow between random jump times with a state‑dependent switching mechanism. The dynamics are defined by a vector field F(x) governing the flow, a jump rate λ(x) ≥ 0, and a transition kernel Q(x, dy) that selects the post‑jump location. The infinitesimal generator acts on sufficiently smooth functions f as

ℒf(x) = ⟨∇f(x), F(x)⟩ + λ(x) ∫ [f(y) − f(x)] Q(x, dy).

Existence and uniqueness follow from the Davis (1984) construction under mild measurability and boundedness conditions on λ and Q. The process is strong Markov and its law solves the martingale problem for ℒ. In applications such as queueing networks, gene regulatory circuits, and stochastic hybrid systems, the switching rate λ often depends on a discrete mode component, leading to a coupled system of ODEs for each mode. The extended generator then includes a matrix of switching intensities Λ(x) = (λij(x)). The associated forward Kolmogorov equation is a system of linear hyperbolic PDEs with source terms given by Λ. Numerical simulation uses the exact flow between jumps (when available) combined with thinning or inversion for the jump times. For stability analysis, Foster–Lyapunov criteria adapted to PDMPs require a function V with ℒV ≤ −cV + d on a petite set. The ergodic theorem holds under a minorisation condition on the jump kernel and a drift condition on the flow. Examiners frequently ask to derive the generator from the dynamic programming principle, to verify the martingale problem for a given hybrid model, or to compute the invariant measure for a two‑mode linear switching diffusion.

Functional Itô Calculus for Path‑Dependent Functionals and Path‑Dependent PDEs

Functional Itô calculus, developed by Dupire (2009) and Cont & Fournié (2010), extends the classical Itô formula to non‑anticipative functionals u(t, ω) defined on the space of càdlàg paths. The vertical derivative ∂_v u(t, ω) measures the sensitivity to a perturbation of the current value ω_t, while the horizontal derivative ∂_t u(t, ω) captures the effect of extending the path by a constant segment. For a continuous semimartingale X, the functional Itô formula reads

u(t, X) = u(0, X) + ∫0^t ∂_t u(s, X) ds + ∫0^t ∂_v u(s, X) dX_s + ½ ∫0^t ∂vv u(s, X) d⟨X⟩_s.

The derivatives are defined via the Dupire–Cont–Fournié limits and coincide with classical Fréchet derivatives when u is cylindrical. Path‑dependent PDEs (PPDEs) arise naturally in optimal control and pricing of path‑dependent options (Asian, lookback). A typical PPDE takes the form ∂_t u + H(t, ω, ∂_v u, ∂vv u) = 0 with terminal condition u(T, ω) = g(ω). Viscosity solutions for PPDEs were established by Ekren, Keller, Touzi, and Zhang (2014) using the notion of semijets on the path space. The comparison principle holds under a uniform ellipticity condition on ∂vv u. Numerical approximation uses the backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE) representation combined with regression on a basis of path‑functionals (e.g., signature features). Examiners test the computation of vertical and horizontal derivatives for simple functionals such as the running maximum or the integral of the path, the verification of the functional Itô formula for a given u, and the formulation of the associated PPDE for a path‑dependent payoff.

Worked Example: Vertical Derivative of the Running Maximum
Let u(t, ω) = max0 ≤ s ≤ t ω_s for a continuous path ω. Compute the vertical derivative ∂_v u(t, ω) and the horizontal derivative ∂_t u(t, ω).
For a continuous path, the running maximum increases only when the current value equals the maximum. If ω_t < maxs<t</sub> ω_s, a small vertical perturbation does not change the maximum, so ∂_v u = 0. If ω_t = maxs≤t ω_s, a positive perturbation raises the maximum by the same amount, giving ∂_v u = 1. The horizontal derivative corresponds to extending the path by a constant segment; since the maximum over a longer interval cannot decrease, ∂_t u = 0 when ω_t < maxs<t</sub> ω_s and ∂_t u = 0 when ω_t = maxs≤t ω_s (the maximum stays the same). Thus ∂_v u(t, ω) = 1ω_t = maxs≤t ω_s and ∂_t u(t, ω) = 0.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the functional Itô formula, first identify whether the functional is cylindrical (depends on finitely many time points) or genuinely path‑dependent. For cylindrical functionals, reduce to the classical Itô formula; for genuine path‑dependence, write down the vertical and horizontal derivatives explicitly before applying the formula. Remember that the horizontal derivative vanishes for functionals that depend only on the current value and the past maximum/minimum, which is a common simplification in exam problems.

Stochastic Differential Equations Driven by Rough Paths Beyond Geometric Rough Paths: Branched Rough Paths

Rough path theory, pioneered by Lyons (1998), provides a pathwise solution theory for differential equations driven by signals of finite p‑variation with p ∈ [2,3). The geometric rough path framework encodes iterated integrals up to level ⌊p⌋ and respects the shuffle product. Branched rough paths, introduced by Gubinelli (2010) and Hairer & Kelly (2015), replace the shuffle algebra with the Connes–Kreimer Hopf algebra of rooted trees, allowing a natural description of non‑geometric noise such as the solution of stochastic PDEs with irregular forcing. A branched rough path of regularity α ∈ (1/3, 1/2) is a map X: [0,T] → H, where H is the Hopf algebra, satisfying Chen’s relation and the analytic bound |Xs,t| ≤ C|t−s|^α. The solution map for the rough differential equation dY = f(Y) dX is constructed via the Lyons–Itô map extended to the branched setting; it is locally Lipschitz in the p‑variation topology. The key advantage is that the branched signature (the group‑like element in H) separates paths modulo tree‑like equivalence, providing a universal feature set for machine learning on stochastic processes. In applications, the Marcus canonical SDE with jumps can be lifted to a branched rough path, and the Wong–Zakai approximation for non‑Gaussian noise converges in the branched rough path topology. Examiners may ask to define the Hopf algebra of rooted trees, to verify Chen’s relation for a given branched rough path, or to explain why the geometric rough path lift of a Lévy process fails to capture the area anomaly while the branched lift succeeds.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Distributional Drift and Regularization by Noise via Regularity Structures

When the drift coefficient b is a distribution (e.g., a space‑time white noise or a Dirac mass), the classical SDE dX_t = b(X_t) dt + dW_t is ill‑posed. The theory of regularity structures, developed by Hairer (2014), provides a framework to renormalise such equations. One builds a regularity structure (A, T, G) where A ⊂ ℝ contains the homogeneities of the modelled distributions, T is a graded vector space of abstract symbols, and G is a renormalisation group acting on T. The solution is lifted to a modelled distribution f: ℝ^d → T, and the abstract fixed‑point problem is solved in the space of modelled distributions. The reconstruction operator ℛ then maps the modelled distribution back to a genuine distribution, yielding a solution to the original SDE. For the one‑dimensional SDE with drift b = ξ (space‑time white noise), the regularity structure contains symbols for ξ, the stochastic convolution, and their products; renormalisation corresponds to subtracting infinite constants (Wick ordering). The resulting solution coincides with the one obtained by the Zvonkin transformation when b is a function of bounded variation, but extends to genuinely distributional drifts. In higher dimensions, the Φ4_d model and the KPZ equation are treated similarly. Examiners test the construction of the regularity structure for a given SPDE, the definition of the renormalisation group, and the proof that the reconstructed solution satisfies the original equation in the sense of distributions.

Stochastic Differential Equations on Lie Groups and the Geometry of Stochastic Flows

Stochastic differential equations on a Lie group G are written in Stratonovich form as dX_t = ∑i=1^m A_i(X_t) ∘ dW_t^i + A0(X_t) dt, where A_i are left‑invariant vector fields. The solution defines a stochastic flow of diffeomorphisms on G. The generator is the second‑order differential operator ℒ = ½ ∑ A_i2 + A0, which is hypoelliptic if the Lie algebra generated by {A_i} spans the tangent space at every point (Hörmander’s condition). The Brownian motion on a compact Lie group with a bi‑invariant metric has generator ½ Δ, where Δ is the Laplace–Beltrami operator; its heat kernel admits an explicit expansion via the Peter–Weyl theorem. For matrix Lie groups, the stochastic exponential map solves dU_t = U_t ∘ dW_t with U0 = I, yielding a stochastic process in GL(n,ℝ) whose logarithm satisfies a linear SDE in the Lie algebra. The stochastic parallel transport along the flow satisfies a linear Stratonovich SDE in the frame bundle, and its generator is the horizontal Laplacian. Applications include filtering on manifolds (the Brockett–Clark filter), stochastic mechanics (the stochastic Euler–Poincaré equations), and numerical integration using Lie group methods (Munthe‑Kaas, Crouch–Grossman). Examiners often ask to derive the generator in coordinates, to verify Hörmander’s condition for a given set of vector fields, or to compute the stochastic exponential for the Heisenberg group.

  • Confusing the vertical derivative with the classical partial derivative when the functional depends on the whole path history.
  • Assuming the functional Itô formula holds for functionals that are not vertically differentiable; the Dupire–Cont–Fournié framework requires existence of ∂_v and ∂vv.
  • Treating a branched rough path as a geometric rough path and applying the shuffle product instead of the Connes–Kreimer coproduct.
  • Neglecting the renormalisation constants in regularity structures; the reconstructed solution differs from the formal fixed point by counterterms.
  • Applying the standard Itô formula on a Lie group without converting to Stratonovich form; the correction term involves the Lie bracket of the vector fields.
  • Assuming Hörmander’s condition is sufficient for a smooth density without checking the uniform non‑degeneracy of the Malliavin covariance matrix.
  • Using the geometric signature for a process with jumps; the branched signature is required to capture the area anomaly.

These advanced topics illustrate how modern stochastic analysis blends algebraic, geometric, and analytic techniques to handle increasingly singular and path‑dependent dynamics.

Measure‑Valued Branching Processes and Superprocesses

Superprocesses are measure‑valued Markov processes that arise as scaling limits of critical branching particle systems. The canonical example is the Dawson–Watanabe superprocess, obtained by accelerating a Galton–Watson branching random walk while simultaneously shrinking particle mass. Its state at time t is a random finite measure Xt on ℝd. The evolution is characterised by the log‑Laplace functional: for any non‑negative test function f ∈ Cc+(ℝd),

𝔼[exp(−⟨Xt, f⟩)] = exp(−⟨X0, u(t,·)⟩),

where u solves the semilinear PDE ∂tu = ½Δu − ψ(u) with ψ(λ) = βλ2 (binary branching) or more general branching mechanisms ψ(λ) = αλ + βλ2 + ∫(0,∞)(e−λr−1+λr)π(dr). The measure‑valued process can be constructed via the martingale problem for the operator ℒ acting on cylinder functions F(μ)=Φ(⟨μ,f1⟩,…,⟨μ,fk⟩). The historical process, introduced by Dawson and Perkins, records the genealogical tree of the superprocess and takes values in the space of finite measures on the path space C([0,∞),ℝd). Key results include the compact support property for super‑Brownian motion in dimensions d≥2, the connection to the (1+β)‑superprocess and the Brownian snake, and the representation of superprocesses as solutions of stochastic PDEs driven by space‑time white noise: ∂tX = ½ΔX + √(γX) Ẇ. Examiners often ask for the derivation of the log‑Laplace equation from the branching particle approximation, the verification of the martingale problem for a given branching mechanism, or the proof that the total mass process ⟨Xt,1⟩ is a continuous‑state branching process (CSBP) with Laplace exponent ψ.

Random Conductance Models and Homogenization in Random Media

The random conductance model describes a reversible random walk on ℤd with edge conductances {ωe} that are i.i.d. positive random variables, possibly with heavy tails. The generator acts on functions f:ℤd→ℝ as (ℒωf)(x)=∑y∼xω{x,y}(f(y)−f(x)). Under the annealed law (averaging over ω) the walk is a Markov chain with symmetric transition probabilities. The quenched invariance principle (Kipnis–Varadhan, 1986; Biskup, 2011) states that for almost every environment satisfying a uniform ellipticity or a moment condition 𝔼[ωe]−1<∞, the rescaled process Xεt=εXt/ε2 converges in distribution to a Brownian motion with deterministic covariance matrix Σ = 𝔼[ωe]I (in the i.i.d. case). When conductances have infinite mean, the scaling limit is a fractional kinetics process or a trap model with subdiffusive behaviour. Homogenization of the associated elliptic PDE −∇·(ω∇u)=f on a bounded domain with Dirichlet boundary conditions yields an effective operator −∇·(Ahom∇u)=f where Ahom is the homogenised matrix obtained from the corrector problem. The corrector φi solves −∇·(ω(∇φi+ei))=0 in ℝd and is stationary with sublinear growth. Quantitative homogenization (Gloria–Otto, Armstrong–Smart) provides rates of convergence in terms of the spectral gap or logarithmic Sobolev inequality for the environment. Examiners test the formulation of the corrector equation, the proof of sublinearity via the ergodic theorem, and the distinction between annealed and quenched invariance principles. A typical trap is to assume uniform ellipticity when only a moment condition holds, leading to an incorrect application of the classical De Giorgi–Nash–Moser theory.

Worked Example: Quenched Invariance Principle for a Simple Conductance Model
Let {ωe} be i.i.d. conductances on edges of ℤ2 with distribution ℙ(ωe=1)=p, ℙ(ωe=κ)=1−p, where κ>0. Show that the rescaled random walk converges to a Brownian motion with covariance Σ = (p+κ(1−p))I.
The generator is (ℒωf)(x)=∑y∼xω{x,y}(f(y)−f(x)). Because the environment is i.i.d. and uniformly elliptic (0<ωe≤max(1,κ)), the Kipnis–Varadhan theorem applies. The effective diffusivity is given by the expectation of the conductance in the direction of a unit vector e1: Σ11=𝔼[ω{0,e1}]=p·1+(1−p)·κ. By symmetry Σ is a scalar matrix Σ = (p+κ(1−p))I. The corrector φ1 solves ω{0,e1}(∇φ1(e1)+1)+ω{0,−e1}(∇φ1(−e1)+1)+…=0. Stationarity and ergodicity give sublinear growth, so the quenched functional CLT holds with the stated Σ.

When a question asks for the homogenised matrix, first write the corrector equation, then argue sublinearity using the ergodic theorem or a spectral gap. Do not skip the verification that the environment satisfies the required moment condition; a missing moment assumption is a frequent source of lost marks.

Nonlinear Filtering for Jump‑Diffusion Signal Processes

Classical nonlinear filtering (the Kushner–Stratonovich and Zakai equations) assumes a diffusion signal observed in Gaussian noise. In many applications — high‑frequency finance, neuroscience, target tracking — the signal exhibits jumps. Let the signal X satisfy dXt = b(Xt)dt + σ(Xt)dWt + ∫ℝmγ(Xt−,z)Ñ(dt,dz), where Ñ is a compensated Poisson random measure with Lévy measure ν(dz). The observation process Y follows dYt = h(Xt)dt + dVt with V a Brownian motion independent of W and Ñ. The unnormalised conditional density πt(φ)=𝔼[φ(Xt)ℒt|𝒴t] (ℒ the likelihood ratio) satisfies the Zakai equation with an additional jump term:

dπt(φ) = πt(ℒφ)dt + πt(φh⊤)dYt + ∫ℝm[πt−(φ(·+γ(·,z))−φ)]Ñ(dt,dz),

where ℒ is the infinitesimal generator of X including the jump integral. The normalised filter π̂t(φ)=πt(φ)/πt(1) obeys the Kushner–Stratonovich equation with a jump correction. Existence and uniqueness of the filter density in L1 follow from the reference probability method (Girsanov for jump‑diffusions) and the stability of the Zakai equation under the total variation norm. Particle filters for jump‑diffusions (the bootstrap filter with jump proposals) require sampling from the jump component; the optimal importance density incorporates the jump intensity λ(x)=∫ν(dz). Examiners often ask to derive the Zakai equation for a given jump‑diffusion, to write the innovation process for the jump‑observation model, or to prove that the filter is robust with respect to misspecification of the Lévy measure. A common mistake is to omit the compensator term when applying Itô’s formula to the likelihood ratio, leading to an incorrect drift in the Zakai equation.

High‑Frequency Asymptotics and Microstructure Noise in Stochastic Volatility

Observed log‑prices Yt = Xt + εt consist of an efficient price X (a stochastic volatility diffusion dXt = μtdt + σtdWt) contaminated by microstructure noise ε (i.i.d. with variance η2). At sampling frequency Δn = 1/n, the realised variance RVn = ∑i=1n(YiΔ−Y(i−1)Δ)2 converges to the integrated variance IV = ∫01σt2dt plus a bias 2nη2. The two‑scale realised variance (TSRV) of Zhang, Mykland, and Aït‑Sahalia (2005) uses a slow time scale Kn≈n2/3 to cancel the noise bias: TSRV = RVn(K) − (Kn/n)RVn. Its asymptotic distribution is mixed normal with rate n1/6. The pre‑averaging estimator of Jacod et al. (2009) averages over a window kn≈θ√n, achieving the optimal rate n1/4 and a feasible central limit theorem. For multivariate assets, the realised covariance matrix suffers from the Epps effect (asynchronous trading); the Hayashi–Yoshida estimator and the multivariate pre‑averaging estimator restore consistency. Examiners test the derivation of the bias term, the choice of tuning parameters (Kn, kn) that balance variance and bias, and the proof of stable convergence in law for the pre‑averaging estimator using the martingale central limit theorem for triangular arrays. A frequent trap is to apply the standard realised variance CLT (rate √n) without accounting for the noise‑induced bias, which leads to an incorrect asymptotic variance.

Statistical Inference for Discretely Observed Diffusions

Given discrete observations Xt0, Xt1, …, Xtn of a diffusion dXt = bθ(Xt)dt + σθ(Xt)dWt, the likelihood is intractable except for linear Gaussian models. Approximate likelihood methods include the Euler–Maruyama pseudo‑likelihood (transition density approximated by a Gaussian with mean Xti+bθ(Xti)Δ and variance σθ2(Xti)Δ), the Kessler (1997) higher‑order expansion using Hermite polynomials, and the simulated likelihood via particle filters. Under high‑frequency asymptotics (Δ→0, nΔ→∞), the Euler estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal with rate √(nΔ) for the drift parameter and rate √n for the diffusion parameter. For ergodic diffusions observed at fixed Δ, the maximum likelihood estimator based on the exact transition density (when available, e.g. CIR, OU) is efficient; otherwise the Bayesian data‑augmentation MCMC (Eraker, 2001) imputes missing paths. The local asymptotic normality (LAN) property for the diffusion coefficient was established by Gobet (2002) using the Malliavin calculus. Examiners ask to write the Euler pseudo‑likelihood, to derive the score function for the diffusion parameter, to explain why the drift and diffusion parameters have different convergence rates, and to design a Metropolis‑Hastings step for the augmented path. A typical error is to treat the Euler approximation as exact for fixed Δ, ignoring the discretisation bias that does not vanish when nΔ is bounded.

  • Omitting the compensator term when deriving the Zakai equation for jump‑diffusion filtering.
  • Applying the standard realised variance CLT without correcting for microstructure noise bias.

  • Neglecting the sublinearity requirement for the corrector in quantitative homogenization.

Zvonkin–Veretennikov Theory for SDEs with Singular Drift

The classical theory of stochastic differential equations requires the drift coefficient to be at least locally Lipschitz to guarantee pathwise uniqueness via the Yamada–Watanabe theorem. In 1974 Zvonkin, and independently Veretennikov in 1979, proved that for the one‑dimensional SDE dXt = b(Xt)dt + dWt with bounded measurable drift b, a strong solution exists and pathwise uniqueness holds. The key idea is a clever change of variable: define u(x) = x + ∫0x v(y)dy where v solves the elliptic PDE ½v'' + b v' = −b with bounded solution v. Itô’s formula applied to u(Xt) cancels the singular drift, transforming the SDE into dYt = v(Yt)dWt with Lipschitz diffusion coefficient. Since u is a C1 diffeomorphism with bounded derivative, the original SDE inherits strong existence and uniqueness. The method extends to multi‑dimensional SDEs with bounded measurable drift and uniformly elliptic diffusion matrix (Veretennikov, 1981; Krylov–Röckner, 2005). A crucial technical point: the PDE solution v belongs to the Sobolev space W2,p for all p < ∞, which by Sobolev embedding gives v ∈ C1,α and ensures u is a C1 diffeomorphism. Examiners often ask to derive the PDE for v, to explain why the boundedness of b is essential for the W2,p estimate, or to contrast this transformation method with the regularization‑by‑noise approach where the noise itself restores well‑posedness (e.g. the Flandoli–Gubinelli–Priola result for the 2D Navier–Stokes equation with transport noise). A common trap is to assume the Zvonkin transform works for degenerate diffusion matrices; the uniform ellipticity condition cannot be dropped without additional structure.

Kunita Theory of Stochastic Flows and Diffeomorphisms

For an SDE dXt = b(Xt)dt + ∑k=1m σk(Xt)∘dWtk with C∞ coefficients and bounded derivatives of all orders, Kunita (1982) constructed a stochastic flow of diffeomorphisms φs,t(ω,·) : ℝd → ℝd such that for each x, t ↦ φ0,t(x) solves the SDE and φs,t ∘ φr,s = φr,t. The flow is generated by the Stratonovich SDE because the chain rule holds in its classical form. The derivative flow Dφs,t(x) satisfies the linear Stratonovich SDE dJt = Db(φt)Jtdt + ∑k Dσk(φt)Jt∘dWtk with J0 = I. Its inverse Kt = Jt−1 solves the adjoint equation. These derivative flows are essential for the Bismut–Elworthy–Li formula for the derivative of the semigroup Ptf(x) = 𝔼[f(Xtx)] and for the Malliavin calculus on the Wiener space. The flow is a Ck diffeomorphism if the coefficients are Ck+1 with bounded derivatives. Kunita also developed the theory for SDEs on manifolds, where the flow lives in the diffeomorphism group of the manifold. Examiners test the derivation of the derivative flow SDE, the proof that det Jt satisfies a linear SDE (Liouville’s formula), and the application to the support theorem (Stroock–Varadhan): the support of the law of Xt equals the closure of the set of solutions to the controlled ODE dφ = b(φ)dt + ∑ σk(φ)hk(t)dt for h ∈ L2. A frequent error is to write the derivative flow in Itô form without the correction term ½∑ D2σk(σk, J)dt.

Worked Example: Zvonkin Transform for a Bounded Drift
Consider the SDE dXt = sign(Xt)dt + dWt, X0 = 0, where sign(0) = 0. Show that a strong solution exists and is pathwise unique.
The drift b(x) = sign(x) is bounded (|b| ≤ 1) and measurable. We seek v solving ½v'' + b v' = −b. For x > 0, b = 1: ½v'' + v' = −1. The general solution is v(x) = C1 + C2e−2x − x. For x < 0, b = −1: ½v'' − v' = 1, giving v(x) = D1 + D2e2x + x. Boundedness of v as |x| → ∞ forces C2 = D2 = 0. Continuity of v and v' at 0 gives C1 = D1 and −1 = 1, which is impossible. Wait — the equation ½v'' + b v' = −b must be interpreted in the distributional sense. The correct approach: multiply by a test function and integrate by parts. The solution v is Lipschitz with v' having a jump at 0. Define u(x) = x + ∫0x v(y)dy. Then u ∈ C1 with u'(x) = 1 + v(x) > 0 (since |v| ≤ 1). Itô's formula for u(Xt) gives dYt = u'(Xt)dWt = (1+v(Xt))dWt. Since u is a C1 diffeomorphism, Yt = u(Xt) solves a Lipschitz SDE, hence Xt = u−1(Yt) is a strong solution. Pathwise uniqueness follows from the diffeomorphism property.
Exam Strategy: Singular Drift vs Regularization by Noise
Distinguish two mechanisms: (1) Zvonkin–Veretennikov uses a deterministic PDE transform to remove the drift; it requires bounded measurable drift and uniform ellipticity. (2) Regularization by noise (Flandoli et al.) uses the stochastic convolution to smooth the drift; it works for some unbounded drifts (e.g. Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin class) but requires the noise to be non‑degenerate in the right directions. In exams, if the drift is bounded and the diffusion is constant, Zvonkin is the expected method. If the drift is unbounded but the noise is multiplicative and non‑degenerate, regularization by noise may apply. Never confuse the two.

Parabolic Anderson Model and Intermittency in SPDEs

The parabolic Anderson model (PAM) is the SPDE ∂tu = Δu + u ξ on ℝd × [0,∞) with initial condition u(0,x) = 1, where ξ is space‑time white noise (or a spatially homogeneous Gaussian noise with spectral measure μ). It models the evolution of a particle density in a random potential and exhibits intermittency: the solution develops tall, sparse peaks that dominate the moments. The Feynman–Kac representation u(t,x) = 𝔼x[exp(∫0t ξ(t−s, Bs)ds)], where B is an independent Brownian motion, is the primary analytical tool. For space‑time white noise in d = 1, the solution is a well‑defined random field; in d ≥ 2 renormalization is required (the Wick product :uξ: replaces uξ). The p‑th moment Lyapunov exponent γ(p) = limt→∞ (1/t) log 𝔼[u(t,0)p] satisfies γ(p) > 0 for p > 1 and is strictly convex, a hallmark of intermittency. In d = 1 with space‑time white noise, γ(p) = p(p2−1)/24 (explicit via the replica method / Bethe ansatz). For spatially correlated noise with spectral density μ(dξ) = |ξ|−αdξ (0 < α < 2), the moment asymptotics are governed by the variational formula γ(p) = supf∈H1, ‖f‖2=1 { p∫∫ f(x)2f(y)2 |x−y|α−d dxdy − ½∫ |∇f|2 }. Examiners ask to derive the Feynman–Kac formula, to explain why renormalization is needed in d ≥ 2 (the stochastic integral ∫ u ξ diverges), to compute γ(2) for a given noise covariance, or to prove that γ(p)/p is increasing (intermittency implies higher moments grow faster). A common mistake is to treat the product uξ classically without Wick renormalization, leading to infinite moments.

Skorokhod Problem in Non‑Convex Domains and Reflected Diffusions

The classical Skorokhod problem seeks, for a given continuous path η with η(0) ∈ D̄, a pair (φ, ψ) such that φ = η + ψ, φ(t) ∈ D̄, ψ has finite variation, and ∫0∞ 1{φ(t)∈∂D} d|ψ|(t) = |ψ|([0,∞)) with dψ(t) ∈ −Nφ(t) (inward normal cone). For convex domains D, existence and uniqueness hold (Tanaka, 1979; Lions–Sznitman, 1984). For non‑convex domains, the problem is ill‑posed in general: multiple solutions or no solution may exist. The Skorokhod problem with oblique reflection replaces the normal cone by a continuous vector field γ(x) on ∂D satisfying ⟨γ(x), n(x)⟩ > 0. Even for non‑convex D, if γ is sufficiently regular and satisfies a completely‑S condition, existence and uniqueness hold (Saisho, 1987; Dupuis–Ishii, 1993). The reflected diffusion dXt = b(Xt)dt + σ(Xt)dWt + γ(Xt)dLt, where L is the boundary local time, is well‑posed under these conditions. The generator is the elliptic operator L = ½∑ aij∂i∂j + b·∇ with oblique derivative boundary condition ∂u/∂γ = 0. The invariant measure has density proportional to exp(2∫ b·a−1 dx) on D with Neumann‑type boundary condition. Examiners test the formulation of the Skorokhod problem for a given domain and reflection field, the derivation of the boundary condition for the generator, the proof of existence via penalization (adding a drift −n∇d(x)/ε where d is the distance to ∂D), and the connection to the Neumann problem for PDEs. A frequent trap is to assume the normal reflection Skorokhod problem is well‑posed for any Lipschitz domain; it fails for domains with inward cusps (e.g. {(x,y): 0 < y < xα, α > 1}).

Marcus Canonical SDEs with Lévy Noise and Stochastic Flows

For an SDE driven by a Lévy process Lt with jumps, the Itô integral ∫ σ(Xs−)dLs does not obey the classical chain rule. The Marcus (canonical) integral ∫ σ(Xs−)⋄dLs is defined so that the solution coincides with the limit of solutions to SDEs driven by piecewise‑linear approximations of L. For a jump of size z at time t, the Marcus solution flows along the ODE dφ/ds = σ(φ)z for s ∈ [0,1], i.e. Xt = Φz1(Xt−) where Φz is the flow of the vector field σ(·)z. The Marcus SDE dXt = b(Xt)dt + σ(Xt)⋄dLt preserves the chain rule: if f ∈ C1, then f(Xt) = f(X0) + ∫ f'(Xs−)b(Xs)ds + ∫ f'(Xs−)σ(Xs−)⋄dLs. The generator is Lf(x) = b(x)f'(x) + ∫ [f(Φz1(x)) − f(x) − f'(x)σ(x)z] ν(dz), where ν is the Lévy measure. Kunita’s theory extends: if σk are C∞ with bounded derivatives, the Marcus SDE generates a stochastic flow of diffeomorphisms. The derivative flow satisfies the linear Marcus SDE dJt = Db(Xt)Jtdt + ∑ Dσk(Xt)Jt⋄dLtk. This flow property is crucial for the Bismut–Elworthy–Li formula in jump‑diffusion settings and for the Malliavin calculus on the Poisson space. Examiners ask to write the Marcus SDE for a given Lévy process, to compute the jump map Φz1 for a specific σ (e.g. σ(x) = x gives Φz1(x) = xez), to derive the generator, or to explain why the Marcus integral is the correct one for physical systems with coloured noise approximated by white Lévy noise (Wong–Zakai theorem for Lévy processes). A common error is to use the Itô integral for a multiplicative jump SDE and then apply the standard Itô formula, missing the compensator correction that the Marcus integral automatically incorporates.

  • Assuming the Zvonkin transform works for degenerate diffusion matrices — uniform ellipticity is essential for the W2,p estimate on the PDE solution.
  • Writing the derivative flow in Itô form without the second‑order correction term ½∑ D2σk(σk, J)dt.
  • Treating the product uξ in the parabolic Anderson model classically without Wick renormalization in dimensions d ≥ 2.
  • Assuming the normal‑reflection Skorokhod problem is well‑posed for any Lipschitz domain — it fails for domains with inward cusps.
  • Using the Itô integral for a multiplicative jump SDE and applying the standard Itô formula, thereby omitting the compensator that the Marcus integral builds in.
  • Confusing the moment Lyapunov exponent γ(p) with the almost‑sure Lyapunov exponent lim (1/t) log u(t,0); they differ in intermittent systems.
  • Forgetting that the Marcus jump map Φz1 is the time‑1 flow of dφ/ds = σ(φ)z, not simply x + σ(x)z.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Random Time Change and Subordination

Random time change replaces the physical clock of a diffusion by an independent increasing Lévy process (a subordinator). If X is a Markov process with generator L and T is a subordinator with Laplace exponent Φ, the subordinate process Yt = XTt is again Markov with generator −Φ(−L). For Brownian motion B and an α-stable subordinator (Φ(λ)=λα/2, 0<α<2) the resulting process is a symmetric α-stable Lévy process whose generator is the fractional Laplacian −(−Δ)α/2. The subordination identity e−tΦ(−L) = ∫0∞ e−sL ℙ(Tt∈ds) links heat kernels of X and Y. In SDEs, one writes dYt = b(Yt) dTt + σ(Yt) dBTt; the drift and diffusion are stretched by the random clock. Existence and uniqueness follow from the corresponding results for X together with the independence of T. The infinitesimal generator of Y acts on smooth f as Φ(−L)f, which for α-stable subordination yields the non‑local operator −(−Δ)α/2. Applications include option pricing under stochastic volatility (the variance gamma model uses a gamma subordinator) and anomalous diffusion in physics where waiting times have heavy tails. A frequent exam twist asks to derive the generator of a subordinate diffusion from the Laplace exponent of the subordinator, or to show that the transition density of Y solves the fractional Cauchy problem ∂tu = −Φ(−L)u.

Stochastic Differential Equations Driven by Fractional Lévy Processes

A fractional Lévy process ZH of Hurst index H∈(0,1) is defined by the moving‑average integral ZHt = ∫−∞t [(t−s)H−1/2 − (−s)+H−1/2] dLs where L is a Lévy process with finite variance. For H=1/2 one recovers L itself; for H>1/2 the kernel is integrable and ZH exhibits long‑range dependence, while for H<1/2 the kernel is singular and the process has rougher paths. Stochastic integration with respect to ZH can be developed via the fractional calculus approach (Mandelbrot–Van Ness) or by using the isometry of the underlying Hilbert space 𝓗H. An SDE dXt = b(Xt)dt + σ(Xt)dZHt is interpreted in the pathwise Young sense when H>1/2 and σ is sufficiently regular, or via rough paths when H≤1/2. The generator of the solution is a non‑local pseudo‑differential operator whose symbol involves the Lévy exponent of L multiplied by the Fourier transform of the kernel. Key results: existence and uniqueness for Lipschitz coefficients when H>1/2 (Nualart–Răşcanu), and for H∈(1/3,1/2] under rough‑path assumptions (Friz–Victoir). In finance, fractional Lévy models capture volatility clustering and leverage effects; in turbulence they model intermittent energy cascades. Examiners often test the construction of the stochastic integral for a simple integrand, the computation of the covariance function Cov(ZHt, ZHs), and the derivation of the fractional Fokker–Planck equation for the density of X.

Worked Example: Covariance of a Fractional Lévy Process
Let L be a standard Brownian motion and define ZHt = ∫0t (t−s)H−1/2 dLs for H∈(0,1). Compute Cov(ZHt, ZHs) for 0≤s≤t.
Using the Itô isometry, Cov(ZHt, ZHs) = ∫0s (t−u)H−1/2(s−u)H−1/2 du. Substitute u = s v to obtain s2H ∫01 (t/s − v)H−1/2(1−v)H−1/2 dv. For t=s this reduces to s2H B(H+1/2, H+1/2) where B is the Beta function. The covariance is homogeneous of order 2H and exhibits the long‑memory property when H>1/2 because the integral diverges as t−s → ∞.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the generator of a subordinate process, first write the Laplace exponent Φ of the subordinator, then apply the functional calculus −Φ(−L) to the original generator L. For fractional Lévy SDEs, identify the Hurst index: if H>1/2 you can use Young integration and standard fixed‑point arguments; if H≤1/2 you must invoke rough‑path theory. Memorise the covariance formula for the Riemann–Liouville fractional Brownian motion — it appears in at least one problem every year.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Nonlinear Expectations and Robust Stochastic Control

Nonlinear expectations generalize linear expectation by replacing the probability measure with a set of priors 𝒫. The G-expectation 𝔼G introduced by Peng corresponds to the supremum over a family of probability measures under which the canonical process is a G-Brownian motion with volatility uncertainty σ2∈[σ̲2, σ̄2]. A G-Brownian motion B has independent, identically distributed increments under each P∈𝒫, and its quadratic variation ⟨B⟩t is a process with values in [σ̲2t, σ̄2t]. Stochastic integrals ∫ σs dBs are defined pathwise via the G-Itô formula, and the resulting integral is a G-martingale. In robust stochastic control the controller chooses a strategy u to minimize the worst‑case cost supP∈𝒫 𝔼P[∫0T f(Xs,us)ds + g(XT)]. The value function V(t,x) satisfies the fully nonlinear HJB equation ∂tV + supu { b·∇V + f + G(D2V) } = 0 where G(A) = ½ supσ∈[σ̲,σ̄] Tr(σσ⊤A). Viscosity solution theory provides existence, uniqueness, and a verification theorem: if a smooth supersolution dominates the terminal cost, it dominates the value function. The G-expectation framework also yields a robust Feynman–Kac representation for solutions of G-heat equations. Typical exam questions ask to compute G(A) for a given volatility interval, to derive the G-Itô formula for a quadratic function, or to prove the dynamic programming principle for the robust control problem.

Stochastic Differential Equations on Metric Graphs and Quantum Graphs

A metric graph Γ consists of a finite set of edges e each identified with an interval [0,ℓe] and vertices v where edges meet. Brownian motion on Γ behaves as standard Brownian motion on each edge and satisfies Kirchhoff (Neumann) conditions at vertices: the sum of outgoing derivatives equals zero. More general vertex conditions are given by a unitary scattering matrix Sv coupling incoming and outgoing derivatives (Kostrykin–Schrader). An SDE on Γ takes the form dXt = b(Xt)dt + σ(Xt)dWt where W is a Brownian motion on Γ constructed via the Dirichlet form ℰ(f,f)=½∑e∫0ℓe |f'e|2 dx with domain incorporating the vertex conditions. The generator is the Laplace operator Δ on Γ with the chosen boundary conditions, plus a drift term. For quantum graphs the Schrödinger operator −Δ + V replaces the Laplacian; stochastic representations of the semigroup e−t(−Δ+V) use Feynman–Kac formulas with a multiplicative functional exp(−∫0t V(Xs)ds). Applications include modeling diffusion in porous media, neural networks, and photonic circuits. Examiners test the formulation of the Kirchhoff conditions, the construction of the Dirichlet form, the derivation of the transition density via spectral decomposition on a star graph, and the proof that the process is a strong Markov process with continuous sample paths.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Singular Interaction and Mean‑Field Limits for Particle Systems

Consider N particles in ℝd interacting through a singular potential W(x) = |x|−α with α∈(0,d) (e.g., Coulomb α=d−2 for d≥3). The system dXit = −(1/N)∑j≠i ∇W(Xit−Xjt)dt + σ dWit is ill‑posed for point particles because the force blows up at collisions. Regularization by mollifying W at scale ε yields a well‑posed system; the mean‑field limit N→∞ with ε=ε(N) chosen appropriately leads to the McKean–Vlasov equation ∂tμ = ∇·(μ ∇(W*μ)) + ½σ2Δμ. For the Keller–Segel model (W logarithmic in d=2) the limit is the Patlak–Keller–Segel PDE. Propagation of chaos holds: the empirical measure converges in probability to the deterministic solution, and any finite marginal converges to the product measure. Quantitative rates are obtained via relative entropy methods (Jabin–Wang) or modulated energy (Serfaty). When the singularity is too strong (α≥d) the mean‑field limit may fail or require renormalization (e.g., the 2D vortex model). Recent work uses the concept of “regularity structures” to give meaning to the singular SDE directly. Exam questions often ask to write the BBGKY hierarchy for the regularized system, to identify the correct scaling ε(N) for the Coulomb case, or to explain why the logarithmic interaction in 2D is critical.

  • Confusing the generator of a subordinate process with the original generator composed with the Laplace exponent — the correct formula is −Φ(−L), not L∘Φ.
  • Assuming Young integration works for all H>0; it requires H>1/2 and sufficient regularity of the integrand.
  • Treating the G-expectation as a linear expectation and applying the classical Itô formula without the G-quadratic variation term.
  • Forgetting that Kirchhoff conditions on a metric graph involve the sum of outward derivatives, not the values of the function.
  • Using the singular potential directly in the particle system without regularization and claiming propagation of chaos — the limit may not exist or may produce a different equation.
  • Mixing up the scaling of the mollifier ε(N) for Coulomb interaction; the correct scaling is ε(N) ≈ N−1/d up to logarithmic factors.
  • Neglecting the role of the scattering matrix Sv in defining general vertex conditions on quantum graphs — Neumann is only the special case S=I.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Random Coefficients and Homogenization

When the coefficients of an SDE depend on a rapidly oscillating random field, the solution exhibits a separation of scales. Let the diffusion be dXεt = b(Xεt, ω, t/ε) dt + σ(Xεt, ω, t/ε) dWt where ε ≪ 1 and the coefficients are stationary ergodic in the fast variable. Under uniform ellipticity and Lipschitz assumptions, the family {Xε} converges in law, as ε → 0, to a homogenized diffusion dXt = b̄(Xt) dt + σ̄(Xt) dWt. The effective coefficients are given by expectations with respect to the invariant measure of the fast process: b̄(x) = ∫ b(x, y) μx(dy), σ̄σ̄⊤(x) = ∫ σσ⊤(x, y) μx(dy). The proof uses the martingale problem formulation and the Kipnis–Varadhan corrector. A key technical step is the construction of the corrector χ(x, y) solving the Poisson equation −Lyχ = b(x, y) − b̄(x) where Ly is the generator of the fast motion. The corrector yields a martingale approximation that identifies the limit. In the periodic deterministic setting the invariant measure is uniform; in the random stationary case it is the law of the environment seen from the particle. Quantitative rates of convergence are obtained via spectral gap or Poincaré inequalities for the environment process. Applications include diffusion in porous media, polymer dynamics, and financial models with fast mean‑reverting volatility. Examiners often ask to write the cell problem for a given periodic coefficient, to prove tightness of {Xε} using the Aldous criterion, or to compute the effective diffusivity for a two‑scale Ornstein–Uhlenbeck driver.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Multi‑Scale Averaging and the Khasminskii Limit

Consider a slow–fast system dXεt = f(Xεt, Yεt) dt + g(Xεt, Yεt) dWt, dYεt = (1/ε) b(Xεt, Yεt) dt + (1/√ε) σ(Xεt, Yεt) dW't where Yε evolves on a fast time scale. For each frozen x, the fast process Yx is ergodic with invariant measure μx. Khasminskii’s averaging principle states that Xε converges weakly to the averaged diffusion dXt = f̄(Xt) dt + ḡ(Xt) dWt with f̄(x) = ∫ f(x, y) μx(dy), ḡḡ⊤(x) = ∫ g g⊤(x, y) μx(dy). The proof proceeds by the martingale problem: one shows that for any smooth test function φ, the process φ(Xεt) − ∫0t Lεφ(Xεs) ds is a martingale, where Lε is the full generator. The limit generator is L̄φ = f̄·∇φ + ½ Tr(ḡḡ⊤ D2φ). Extensions cover degenerate fast dynamics (hypoelliptic case) and non‑Markovian fast noise via the martingale‑problem approach of Kurtz. Typical exam tasks: derive the Poisson equation for a given f, b, σ; prove that the corrector χ has at most polynomial growth; show that the averaged diffusion coefficient ḡ satisfies the uniform ellipticity condition if the original σ does.

Stochastic Differential Equations in Random Environments: Quenched and Annealed Laws

An SDE in a random environment takes the form dXt = b(Xt, ω) dt + σ(Xt, ω) dWt where the coefficients are measurable functions of a stationary ergodic random field ω. Two probability measures are natural: the quenched law Pω (environment fixed) and the annealed law ℙ = ∫ Pω ℙ(dω) (environment averaged). Under uniform ellipticity and boundedness, the quenched process is a diffusion with generator Lω = b(·, ω)·∇ + ½ Tr(σσ⊤(·, ω) D2). The annealed process is generally non‑Markovian because the environment introduces memory. However, the environment viewed from the particle, ωt = τXtω, is a Markov process on the space of environments with generator Lenv. Its invariant measure is the original law ℙ if the environment is stationary. The Kipnis–Varadhan theory gives a central limit theorem for additive functionals of the environment process, leading to a quenched invariance principle: under Pω, Xt/√t converges to a Brownian motion with deterministic covariance matrix Σ2 for ℙ‑a.e. ω. The annealed CLT follows by integrating over ω. The effective covariance is given by the Green–Kubo formula Σ2 = ∫ (b + ∇χ) (b + ∇χ)⊤ dℙ where χ is the corrector solving −Lenvχ = b. In dimensions d ≥ 3 the corrector exists in L2(ℙ); in d = 2 a logarithmic correction appears. Applications include transport in porous media, polymer pinning, and random conductance models. Examiners may ask to define the environment process, to write the corrector equation, or to explain why the annealed law fails the Markov property while the quenched law does not.

Stochastic Differential Equations with State‑Dependent Noise and the Stratonovich–Itô Correction

When the diffusion coefficient depends on the solution, the choice of stochastic integral matters. The Itô SDE dXt = b(Xt) dt + σ(Xt) dWt and the Stratonovich SDE dXt = b(Xt) dt + σ(Xt) ∘ dWt are related by the correction term ½ σ(Xt) σ'(Xt) dt. In vector form, if σ : ℝd → ℝd×m, the Stratonovich drift is bS(x) = b(x) − ½ ∑k=1m (∂σk/∂x) σk(x) where σk is the k‑th column. The correction arises because the Stratonovich integral obeys the chain rule, while the Itô integral adds the quadratic variation term. For numerical schemes, the Euler–Maruyama method converges strongly for Itô SDEs but only weakly for Stratonovich SDEs unless a correction is added. The Milstein scheme incorporates the Lévy area to achieve strong order 1 for Stratonovich equations. In physical modeling, Stratonovich calculus is preferred when the noise originates from a smooth approximation (Wong–Zakai theorem): if Wε are smooth processes converging to Brownian motion, the solutions of dXε = b(Xε) dt + σ(Xε) dWε converge to the Stratonovich solution. Conversely, if the noise is intrinsically white, the Itô interpretation is natural. The Marcus integral extends the Stratonovich correction to jump diffusions. Typical exam questions: derive the correction term for a given σ(x) = x2; show that the Stratonovich SDE dX = X ∘ dW has solution Xt = X0 exp(Wt); explain why the Wong–Zakai approximation fails for the Itô integral.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Anticipative Integrals and the Malliavin Calculus for Anticipative Processes

Classical Itô calculus integrates adapted processes against Brownian motion. Anticipative integrals extend this framework to integrands that depend on future values of the driving noise. The two principal constructions are the Skorokhod integral, defined as the adjoint of the Malliavin derivative, and the Russo–Vallois forward integral, defined via regularisation of the noise. For a square‑integrable random variable F in the Wiener chaos expansion, the Skorokhod integral δ(u) satisfies the duality relation E[F δ(u)] = E[⟨DF, u⟩H], where D is the Malliavin derivative and H the Cameron–Martin space. This duality yields the fundamental integration‑by‑parts formula that underpins Malliavin calculus. When the integrand u is adapted, the Skorokhod integral coincides with the Itô integral; for non‑adapted u a correction term involving the Malliavin derivative appears: δ(u) = ∫ ut dWt + ∫ Dtut dt. The Malliavin derivative DtF measures the sensitivity of F to an infinitesimal perturbation of the Brownian path at time t. The chain rule Dtφ(F) = φ'(F) DtF holds for smooth φ, and the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck operator L = -δD generates the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroup. Applications include the Clark–Ocone formula for hedging in incomplete markets, the computation of Greeks via Malliavin weights, and the regularity of densities for solutions of SDEs with non‑Lipschitz coefficients. The Meyer inequalities give Lp bounds for the Skorokhod integral, and the Nualart–Pardoux theorem characterises the domain of δ in terms of the Malliavin–Sobolev spaces Dk,p. In numerical analysis, the anticipative Riemann sums converge to the Skorokhod integral under suitable regularity, providing a basis for simulation of anticipative SDEs.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Non‑Local Operators and the Fractional Laplacian on Bounded Domains

The fractional Laplacian (-Δ)α/2 for α ∈ (0,2) is a non‑local operator defined via the singular integral (-Δ)α/2 u(x) = Cd,α ∫ℝd (u(x) - u(y)) / |x-y|d+α dy. On a bounded domain D ⊂ ℝd one must prescribe exterior conditions. The two standard realisations are the restricted fractional Laplacian, where the process is killed upon leaving D, and the spectral fractional Laplacian, defined via the eigenfunction expansion of the Dirichlet Laplacian. The restricted operator corresponds to the generator of a censored stable process, while the spectral operator generates a process that jumps inside D according to the Dirichlet heat kernel. The Dirichlet form for the restricted operator is ℰ(u,v) = Cd,α ∫D×D (u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y)) / |x-y|d+α dx dy, with domain the fractional Sobolev space Hα/20(D). For the spectral operator the form is ℰ(u,v) = ∑ λkα/2 ⟨u,φk⟩ ⟨v,φk⟩. The heat kernel of the restricted operator satisfies the boundary Harnack principle and exhibits a polynomial decay near the boundary: pt(x,y) ≍ t-d/α (1 ∧ δ(x)/t1/α) (1 ∧ δ(y)/t1/α) ∧ (t/|x-y|d+α), where δ(x) is the distance to ∂D. In SDEs driven by a symmetric α-stable Lévy process Lt, the generator of the killed process is the restricted fractional Laplacian plus a drift term. Pathwise uniqueness for dXt = b(Xt) dt + dLt with X0 ∈ D and killing at ∂D holds under a Hölder condition on b of order > 1-α/2. The associated martingale problem is well‑posed in the space of càdlàg functions with cemetery state. Numerical approximation uses the truncated Lévy measure or the finite element method for the Dirichlet form; the latter yields convergence rates of order hα/2 in the energy norm.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Singular Drift and Regularization by Noise via Paracontrolled Distributions

Consider the SDE dXt = b(Xt) dt + dWt where the drift b is a distribution of regularity β < 0. Classical theory requires b to be at least bounded measurable for strong well‑posedness (Zvonkin–Veretennikov). Regularization by noise exploits the smoothing effect of the Brownian motion to give meaning to the product b(Xt) dt. The paracontrolled distribution approach, developed by Gubinelli, Imkeller, and Perkowski, constructs a solution by postulating an ansatz X = X♯ + X♭ ≺ W, where ≺ denotes the paraproduct and X♯ is a smoother remainder. The key is to define the resonant product b(X) ∘ W via Bony's paraproduct decomposition and to show that the commutator [b, X] ≺ W is well defined. For b ∈ Cβ with β > -2/3 the equation admits a unique strong solution in the paracontrolled sense. The solution map b ↦ X is locally Lipschitz in the topology of modelled distributions. This framework extends to singular SPDEs such as the KPZ equation ∂t h = ∂xx h + (∂x h)2 + ξ where ξ is space‑time white noise. The regularity structure theory of Hairer provides an alternative algebraic formulation; both theories yield the same renormalised equation. In the SDE context, the paracontrolled ansatz reduces to a fixed‑point problem in a Besov space Bαp,q with α = 1/2 + β. The drift b may be a random distribution independent of W; the solution is then constructed pathwise for almost every realisation of b. Numerical schemes based on the paracontrolled expansion achieve strong convergence rates of order εα for the Euler scheme with a suitable mollification of the drift.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Common Noise and Conditional McKean–Vlasov Equations with Jumps

Mean‑field games with common noise lead to conditional McKean–Vlasov SDEs: dXt = b(t, Xt, 𝒫Xt|𝒢t) dt + σ(t, Xt, 𝒫Xt|𝒢t) dWt + σ0(t, Xt, 𝒫Xt|𝒢t) dWt0, where W0 is a common Brownian motion and 𝒢t = σ(W0s : s ≤ t). The conditional law 𝒫Xt|𝒢t is a random measure. When the idiosyncratic noise includes a Lévy jump component Lt with Lévy measure ν, the generator becomes a non‑local operator acting on the conditional law. The propagation of chaos holds conditionally on the common noise: the empirical measure of N particles converges in Wasserstein distance to the conditional law at rate N-1/2. The master equation for the value function U(t, x, μ) is a PDE on the Wasserstein space with a second‑order term in the measure variable arising from the common noise. For jump‑diffusions the master equation includes an integral term ∫ (U(t, x+z, μz) - U(t, x, μ) - DμU(t, x, μ)(z)) ν(dz), where μz is the push‑forward of μ by the jump map. The existence of classical solutions requires a monotonicity condition à la Lasry–Lions and a regularity assumption on the Lévy measure (e.g. ∫ |z|2 ν(dz) < ∞). The conditional McKean–Vlasov equation can be solved by a fixed‑point argument on the space of flows of conditional measures equipped with the Wasserstein‑1 metric. Numerical approximation uses a particle system with common noise; the Euler scheme with a truncated Lévy measure converges weakly with order 1 and strongly with order 1/2 under a Lipschitz condition on the coefficients with respect to the measure argument.

  • Treating the paracontrolled ansatz as a simple perturbation series — the resonant product and commutator estimates are essential for closing the fixed‑point argument.
  • Overlooking the conditional nature of the McKean–Vlasov law when common noise is present — the limiting object is a random measure, not a deterministic one.
  • Applying the standard Euler scheme to a jump‑diffusion McKean–Vlasov system without truncating the Lévy measure — the small jumps must be compensated or truncated to achieve convergence.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Rough Volatility and the Rough Heston Model

Rough volatility models replace the Brownian driver of the variance process by a fractional Brownian motion B^H with Hurst index H∈(0,½). The rough Heston model reads

dS_t = S_t √{V_t} dW_t, dV_t = λ(θ−V_t) dt + ν V_tγ dB^H_t,

where W and B^H are correlated with correlation ρ. The key feature is that the variance paths are almost surely Hölder continuous of order H−ε, reproducing the observed roughness of empirical volatility time series. Despite the non‑Markovian nature of B^H, the model retains an affine structure: the conditional characteristic function of log S_T given the filtration generated by B^H up to time t can be expressed in closed form via a Riccati system driven by the fractional noise. This allows efficient Fourier pricing and calibration. The Hurst parameter H controls the short‑time behaviour of the at‑the‑money implied volatility skew, which behaves like τH−½ as τ→0, matching market data for H≈0.1. Estimation of H from high‑frequency data uses the realised volatility signature plot or the generalised method of moments on the log‑signature of the variance process. The rough Heston model also admits a well‑posed martingale problem; existence and uniqueness follow from a Girsanov transform that shifts the fractional Brownian motion into a Brownian motion under an equivalent measure, combined with the classical Heston theory.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Reflection on Riemannian Manifolds

Reflected diffusions on a smooth Riemannian manifold M with boundary ∂M are constructed by solving the Skorokhod problem in the tangent bundle. Let Xt satisfy

dXt = b(Xt) dt + σ(Xt) dWt + dKt,

where W is a Brownian motion on the tangent space, b and σ are smooth vector fields and diffusion matrices, and K is a bounded variation process that pushes X inward along the inward unit normal n on ∂M. The reflection term satisfies

Kt = ∫0t n(Xs) d|K|s, |K|t = ∫0t 1{Xs∈∂M} d|K|s.

Existence and uniqueness hold under the uniform inward pointing condition: there exists c>0 such that ⟨b(x),n(x)⟩ + ½ Tr[σσT ∇n(x)] ≤ −c for all x∈∂M. This condition guarantees that the boundary local time does not explode. The associated Dirichlet form is

ℰ(f,f) = ½ ∫M ⟨∇f, a∇f⟩ dvol, a = σσT,

with Neumann boundary condition ∂nf = 0 on ∂M. The heat kernel pt(x,y) satisfies Gaussian upper bounds and a reflected Harnack inequality. Applications include the modelling of polymer chains confined in curved geometries and the analysis of Brownian motion on manifolds with corners where the reflection direction is a convex cone of admissible vectors. The Lions–Sznitman theorem extends the construction to domains with non‑smooth boundaries by approximating with smooth domains and passing to the limit in the Skorokhod map.

On a post‑critically finite fractal K (e.g. the Sierpiński gasket) a Laplacian ΔK is defined via Kigami’s resistance form. The fractional power (−ΔK)α/2 for α∈(0,2) is given spectrally by the eigenvalues λn and eigenfunctions φn of ΔK:

Stochastic Differential Equations with Multiplicative Noise and the Itô–Stratonovich Correction on Manifolds

On a Riemannian manifold M the Stratonovich integral preserves the chain rule, making it natural for geometric SDEs. Let Xt satisfy the Stratonovich SDE

dXt = b(Xt) dt + Σi=1m σi(Xt) ∘ dWti.

The equivalent Itô formulation acquires a correction term involving the Levi‑Civita connection ∇:

dXt = (b + ½ Σi ∇σi σi)(Xt) dt + Σi σi(Xt) dWti.

Here ∇σi σi is the covariant derivative of the vector field σi along itself. If M is embedded in ℝd and the noise is the projection of Euclidean Brownian motion onto the tangent bundle, the correction reduces to ½ times the mean curvature vector of the embedding. This geometric drift is responsible for the “Itô–Stratonovich shift” observed in stochastic development of curves. The martingale problem for the Stratonovich SDE is well posed under the usual Lipschitz and linear growth conditions on the vector fields in local charts. The associated generator is the second‑order differential operator

L = b + ½ Σi ∇σi σi + ½ Σi σi2.

Applications include the stochastic parallel transport of frames, the construction of Brownian motion on Lie groups via the Stratonovich exponential map, and the analysis of stochastic flows on manifolds where the Itô correction encodes the curvature of the underlying space. A frequent error is to omit the connection term when converting a Stratonovich SDE written in coordinates to its Itô form; the correction is not a scalar but a vector field that depends on the Christoffel symbols of the metric.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Random Coefficients and Homogenization on Manifolds

Consider an SDE on a compact Riemannian manifold M with rapidly oscillating periodic coefficients:

dXεt = b(Xεt, Xεt/ε) dt + σ(Xεt, Xεt/ε) dWt.

Here the fast variable lives on the torus 𝕋d and the coefficients are smooth and periodic in the second argument. As ε → 0, the process Xε converges in law to a homogenized diffusion X0 solving

dX0t = b̄(X0t) dt + σ̄(X0t) dWt.

The effective drift b̄ and diffusion σ̄ are obtained by solving the cell problem on the torus: for each x∈M, find the periodic corrector χ(x,·) such that

−Ly χ(x,y) = b(x,y) − b̄(x), Ly = ½ Tr[σσT ∇y∇y] + b·∇y.

The effective diffusion matrix is σ̄σ̄T = ∫𝕋d (σ + ∇yχ) (σ + ∇yχ)T dy. The convergence rate in the Wasserstein distance is of order ε under a spectral gap assumption for the fast process. This framework extends the classical Papanicolaou–Varadhan homogenization to curved state spaces and is used in the upscaling of stochastic models for diffusion in heterogeneous media such as porous rocks with curved pore geometry. A subtle point is that the corrector χ must be chosen to have zero mean with respect to the invariant measure of the fast process; otherwise the effective drift acquires an additional term involving the gradient of the invariant density.

Stochastic Differential Equations Driven by Gaussian Processes with Non‑Markovian Covariance

When the driving noise is a centered Gaussian process G with covariance kernel R(s,t) that is not of the Brownian type, the classical Itô calculus does not apply directly. The solution theory relies on the representation of G as a Volterra integral of a Brownian motion, Gt = ∫0t K(t,s) dWs, where the kernel K encodes the memory structure. Existence and uniqueness of solutions to dXt = b(Xt) dt + σ(Xt) dGt are then obtained by transforming the equation into a standard SDE with a time‑dependent diffusion coefficient σ(Xt)K(t,·) and applying the Yamada–Watanabe theorem under Lipschitz and linear growth assumptions on b and σ. The resulting process is generally non‑Markovian, and its finite‑dimensional distributions are characterised by a modified Kolmogorov forward equation involving the covariance operator of G.

For fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H ≠ ½, the kernel K is explicitly known and the transformed SDE contains a singular factor (t−s)H−½. When H > ½ the noise exhibits long‑range dependence and the solution can be defined pathwise via Young integration provided σ is sufficiently regular (β‑Hölder with β > 1−H). For H < ½ the kernel is more regular and the rough path approach yields a solution theory that extends to irregular drifts. In both regimes the Malliavin calculus for Gaussian processes provides integration‑by‑parts formulas that are essential for density estimates and for the analysis of the associated Dirichlet forms.

A key practical consequence is that numerical schemes must respect the memory kernel. Standard Euler–Maruyama discretisations fail to capture the correct covariance structure; instead one uses the Cholesky factorisation of the covariance matrix or circulant embedding methods to generate exact Gaussian increments, and then applies a deterministic integrator to the transformed Volterra equation. The strong convergence rate typically deteriorates to O(ΔtH) for H < ½ and to O(Δt1−H) for H > ½, reflecting the roughness of the driving signal.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Fractional Time Derivatives and Caputo Operators

Equations of the form ∂αt Xt = b(Xt) + σ(Xt) ξt, where ∂αt denotes the Caputo fractional derivative of order α ∈ (0,1) and ξt is white noise, arise in models of subdiffusion and viscoelastic media. The Caputo derivative is defined by ∂αt f(t) = (1/Γ(1−α)) ∫0t (t−s)−α f′(s) ds, which introduces a memory kernel of power‑law type. By applying the Laplace transform, the deterministic part of the equation is solved in terms of the Mittag‑Leffler function Eα,β(z), and the stochastic convolution takes the form Xt = ∫0t (t−s)α−1 Eα,α(−λ(t−s)α) σ(Xs) dWs for linear drift b(x)=−λx.

Well‑posedness for nonlinear coefficients is established by converting the fractional SDE into a Volterra integral equation Xt = X0 + (1/Γ(α)) ∫0t (t−s)α−1 b(Xs) ds + (1/Γ(α)) ∫0t (t−s)α−1 σ(Xs) dWs. Under global Lipschitz and linear growth conditions on b and σ, a fixed‑point argument in the space of continuous adapted processes yields a unique strong solution. The solution inherits the Hölder regularity of the stochastic convolution, which is of order α−½−ε for any ε>0, reflecting the smoothing effect of the fractional integral kernel.

Numerical approximation requires specialised schemes because the memory term couples all past time steps. The L1 scheme for the Caputo derivative combined with a stochastic convolution quadrature yields a strong convergence rate of O(Δtmin(α,½)) under sufficient regularity of the coefficients. For α close to 1 the rate approaches the classical ½, while for small α the memory dominates and the rate is essentially α. These rates are sharp and have been verified by Monte‑Carlo experiments on model problems such as the fractional Ornstein‑Uhlenbeck process.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Randomly Switching Topology and Interacting Particle Systems

In many applications the interaction graph among particles evolves randomly in time, leading to a system of SDEs dXit = b(Xit, μt) dt + σ(Xit) dWit where the empirical measure μt = (1/N) Σj δXjt is weighted by a time‑dependent adjacency matrix A(t). The matrix A(t) is a continuous‑time Markov chain on the space of graphs with generator Q, independent of the Brownian motions. This coupling produces a piecewise‑deterministic Markov process on the product space of particle positions and graph states.

Propagation of chaos holds under uniform Lipschitz assumptions on b and σ and a spectral gap condition for the graph dynamics. As N→∞, the empirical measure converges to a deterministic measure‑valued process μt that solves a nonlinear McKean–Vlasov equation with an additional averaging over the stationary distribution of the graph chain. The limiting equation reads ∂t μt = −∇·(b(·, μt) μt) + ½ Δ(σ2 μt) where the drift b is replaced by its expectation with respect to the invariant law of A(t). The rate of convergence in Wasserstein distance is O(N−½) uniformly in time provided the graph chain mixes exponentially fast.

Fluctuation theorems for the finite‑N system are obtained by linearising the McKean–Vlasov limit and adding a martingale term that captures both the particle noise and the graph switching noise. The resulting central limit theorem yields a Gaussian field whose covariance solves a Lyapunov equation involving the linearised drift and the graph generator Q. These results are used to quantify the error of mean‑field approximations in opinion dynamics on temporal networks and in epidemiological models with changing contact structures.

Stochastic Integration with Respect to Poisson Random Measures and Compensated Integrals

Let N(dt, dz) be a Poisson random measure on ℝ⁺ × E with intensity measure ν(dz) dt, where E is a measurable space. The compensated measure Ñ(dt, dz) = N(dt, dz) − ν(dz) dt is a martingale measure. If H is only integrable, the integral is defined pathwise as a compensated sum of jumps. The Itô formula for a càdlàg semimartingale X with jumps driven by N reads f(X_T) = f(X0) + ∫₀ᵀ f'(Xs-) dX_s^c + ½ ∫₀ᵀ f''(Xs-) d[X]^c_s + ∑s≤T [f(X_s) − f(Xs-) − f'(Xs-) ΔX_s], where the sum can be expressed as an integral against Ñ. This framework underlies Lévy‑driven SDEs, where the Lévy‑Itô decomposition writes the driving Lévy process as a Brownian part plus a compensated Poisson integral. Stochastic Differential Equations with Non‑Lipschitz Diffusion Coefficients and the Engelbert–Schmidt Theory

Consider the one‑dimensional SDE dX_t = σ(X_t) dW_t with σ : ℝ → ℝ measurable and σ(x) > 0 for all x. The classical Yamada–Watanabe theorem requires |σ(x) − σ(y)| ≤ ρ(|x−y|) with ∫₀⁺ ρ(u)⁻² du = ∞. Engelbert and Schmidt (1985) gave a necessary and sufficient condition for pathwise uniqueness and existence of a strong solution when σ may vanish or be irregular. Define the scale function s(x) = ∫x₀^x exp(−2∫x₀^y (b(z)/σ(z)²) dz) dy (here b ≡ 0) and the speed measure m(dx) = 2/(σ(x)²) dx. The SDE admits a unique strong solution up to an explosion time iff ∫x₀x₀+ε (s(x) − s(x₀)) m(dx) = ∞ and ∫x₀−εx₀ (s(x₀) − s(x)) m(dx) = ∞ for some ε > 0. In the driftless case this reduces to ∫x₀x₀+ε 1/σ(y)² dy = ∞ and the same on the left. If the integral is finite, the diffusion hits the boundary in finite time and pathwise uniqueness fails; one can construct multiple weak solutions by specifying different boundary behaviours (absorbing, reflecting, sticky). The Engelbert–Schmidt criterion is sharp: for σ(x) = |x|^α with α ≥ 1 the integral diverges and uniqueness holds; for α < 1 it converges and non‑uniqueness appears. Examiners frequently ask to verify the condition for a given σ and to decide whether the origin is an entrance, exit, or natural boundary.

Worked Example: Engelbert–Schmidt Condition
Let σ(x) = |x|^α with α ∈ (0,1). Determine whether the SDE dX_t = σ(X_t) dW_t has a unique strong solution starting from X0 = 0.
Compute the integral ∫₀^ε 1/σ(y)² dy = ∫₀^ε y−2α dy. This converges iff −2α > −1, i.e. α < ½. For α ∈ (0,½) the integral is finite, so the Engelbert–Schmidt condition fails and pathwise uniqueness does not hold; multiple weak solutions exist (e.g. absorbing at 0 or reflecting). For α ∈ [½,1) the integral diverges, the condition holds, and there is a unique strong solution. At α = ½ the integral diverges logarithmically, still giving uniqueness. Thus the threshold for uniqueness is α = ½.

When a question asks for the Engelbert–Schmidt condition, first write the integral ∫ 1/σ². Check convergence at the problematic point (usually 0). Remember that divergence ⇒ uniqueness, convergence ⇒ non‑uniqueness. Do not confuse this with the Yamada–Watanabe modulus condition; they are complementary but not equivalent.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Random Initial Data and the Theory of Random Dynamical Systems

A random dynamical system (RDS) on a Polish space X over a metric dynamical system (Ω, ℱ, ℙ, θ) is a measurable map φ : ℝ⁺ × Ω × X → X satisfying the cocycle property φ(t+s, ω) = φ(t, θ_s ω) ∘ φ(s, ω). For an SDE dX_t = b(X_t) dt + σ(X_t) dW_t with random initial condition X0(ω), the solution map φ(t, ω)x defines an RDS provided the coefficients are globally Lipschitz. The invariant measure of the RDS corresponds to a stationary distribution of the Markov semigroup. Oseledets’ multiplicative ergodic theorem applies to the linearised cocycle Dφ(t, ω)x, yielding Lyapunov exponents that characterise almost‑sure exponential growth rates of tangent vectors. If the top Lyapunov exponent is negative, the RDS possesses a random attractor that attracts all bounded sets. The random attractor is a compact random set A(ω) invariant under φ and pullback attracting. In contrast to deterministic attractors, random attractors depend on the noise realisation and are measurable with respect to the past σ‑field. Examiners often test the distinction between forward and pullback attraction: a set may be forward attracting but not pullback attracting when the noise is non‑stationary. Another frequent trap is to assume that a unique invariant measure implies ergodicity; for RDS one must verify that the invariant measure is ergodic for the skew‑product flow.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Multiplicative Noise on Lie Groups and the Stochastic Exponential Map

Let G be a finite‑dimensional Lie group with Lie algebra 𝔤. A left‑invariant SDE on G reads dg_t = g_t ∘ (∑i=1^m A_i ∘ dW_t^i + A0 dt), where A_i ∈ 𝔤 and ∘ denotes Stratonovich integration. The solution can be expressed via the stochastic exponential map Exp : 𝔤 → G, defined as the solution at time 1 of the Stratonovich equation dY_t = Y_t ∘ dX_t with Y0 = e and X_t = ∑ A_i W_t^i + A0 t. In the Itô formulation a correction term appears: dg_t = g_t (∑ A_i dW_t^i + (A0 + ½ ∑ adA_i2) dt), where ad_A(B) = [A, B]. The stochastic exponential satisfies the Chen–Strichartz formula Exp(X) Exp(Y) = Exp(X ⋆ Y) with the ⋆‑product given by the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series plus stochastic correction terms. For matrix groups the stochastic exponential coincides with the usual matrix exponential of the Stratonovich integral. A key result (Kunita, 1990) states that the solution flow is a diffeomorphism‑valued process, and its derivative satisfies a linear SDE on the tangent bundle. Examiners often ask to convert between Stratonovich and Itô forms on a Lie group, requiring the computation of the ad‑correction. A common mistake is to omit the ½ ∑ adA_i2 term or to apply the Euclidean Itô–Stratonovich correction instead of the Lie‑algebraic one.

The stochastic Taylor expansion of the solution X_t of dX_t = f(X_t) dt + ∑k=1^m g_k(X_t) dW_t^k around t₀ writes Xt₀+h = Xt₀ + ∑α I_α(h) f_α(Xt₀), where α runs over multi‑indices, I_α are multiple Itô integrals, and f_α are differential operators built from f, g_k and their derivatives. The stochastic Runge–Kutta methods achieve high weak order without simulating Lévy areas by matching rooted tree expansions of the weak error. Reflected Backward Stochastic Differential Equations and Optimal Stopping

A reflected backward stochastic differential equation (RBSDE) on a finite horizon [0, T] takes the form

Y_t = ξ + ∫_t^T f(s, Y_s, Z_s) ds − ∫_t^T Z_s dW_s + K_T − K_t,

where Y_t ≥ S_t for a given continuous obstacle process S_t, the increasing process K_t grows only when Y_t = S_t (the Skorokhod condition ∫0^T (Y_t − S_t) dK_t = 0), and ξ ≥ S_T is the terminal condition. The triple (Y, Z, K) is the solution. When f = 0 and S is a reward process, Y_t = ess supτ ∈ 𝒯_t 𝔼[S_τ | ℱ_t] where 𝒯_t is the set of stopping times taking values in [t, T]; this is the Snell envelope characterization of the optimal stopping problem. The driver f may depend on (y, z) with Lipschitz growth; existence and uniqueness follow from a penalization scheme Y_t^n = ξ + ∫_t^T f(s, Y_s^n, Z_s^n) ds + n ∫_t^T (Y_s^n − S_s)^− ds − ∫_t^T Z_s^n dW_s and a monotone convergence argument. The process K_t = limn→∞ n ∫0^t (Y_s^n − S_s)^− ds is the minimal increasing process enforcing the constraint. In finance, RBSDEs price American options under nonlinear expectations (e.g., with transaction costs or default risk): the value process Y_t is the superhedging cost, Z_t the hedging portfolio, and K_t the cumulative exercise premium. For Markovian obstacles S_t = h(X_t) with X_t a diffusion, the value function u(t, x) = Y_tt,x solves the variational inequality min(−∂_t u − ℒu − f(·, u, ∇u), u − h) = 0 with terminal condition u(T, x) = max(ξ(x), h(x)). The free boundary ∂{(t, x) : u(t, x) > h(x)} is the optimal exercise region. Numerical schemes combine the penalization method with regression-based Monte Carlo (Longstaff–Schwartz) or finite-difference discretization of the variational inequality. A frequent examiner trap is to confuse the RBSDE increasing process K with the local time of Y at the obstacle; K is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure only when the obstacle is regular enough, otherwise it may charge a singular set. Another trap is to assume the Snell envelope is always a supermartingale; it is a supermartingale only when the obstacle is upper semicontinuous and the filtration satisfies the usual conditions.

Brownian Motion on Fractals and Stochastic Analysis on Post‑Critically Finite Fractals

On a post‑critically finite (p.c.f.) fractal K such as the Sierpiński gasket, a Laplacian Δ_K is constructed as the limit of discrete graph Laplacians Δ_n on approximating graphs V_n. The renormalization factor r > 1 satisfies Δn+1 u = r Δ_n u on harmonic functions; for the standard gasket r = 5/3. The Dirichlet form ℰ(u, v) = limn→∞ r^n ∑x∼y in V_n (u(x)−u(y))(v(x)−v(y)) is a regular, local, conservative form on L2(K, μ) where μ is the self‑similar measure (Hausdorff measure of dimension d_H = log 3 / log 2). Neutral Stochastic Functional Differential Equations with State‑Dependent Delay

A neutral stochastic functional differential equation (NSFDE) with state‑dependent delay has the form

d[X(t) − D(t, X_t)] = f(t, X_t) dt + g(t, X_t) dW_t,

where X_t(θ) = X(t + θ) for θ ∈ [−τ, 0] is the segment process, D : [0, T] × C([−τ, 0]; ℝ^d) → ℝ^d is the neutral term, and the delay τ(t, X_t) may depend on the current state. The neutral term D(t, φ) typically involves the derivative of the past: D(t, φ) = ∫−τ0 φ(θ) d_θ μ(t, θ) or D(t, φ) = φ(−τ(t, φ)). The key difficulty is that the map φ ↦ φ − D(t, φ) must be invertible on the segment space to recover the current state from the neutral variable. A standard condition is the Lipschitz stability: |D(t, φ) − D(t, ψ)| ≤ k ‖φ − ψ‖_∞ with k < 1. Under this condition, the solution map is well‑defined and the segment process X_t is a Markov process on the space C([−τ, 0]; ℝ^d). The infinitesimal generator acts on cylindrical functions F(φ) = f(φ(θ1), …, φ(θ_n)) as ℒF(φ) = ∑_i ∂_i f(·) [f(t, φ) + D_t(t, φ)] + ½ ∑i,j ∂ij2 f(·) g g^T, where D_t is the derivative of D along the flow. Existence and uniqueness of strong solutions require the coefficients f, g to be Lipschitz in the uniform norm and the neutral term D to be Lipschitz with constant < 1. For state‑dependent delay τ(t, φ), the segment space becomes a manifold with boundary; the solution is constructed by piecing together solutions on intervals where the delay is constant, using the implicit function theorem to handle the switching times. Stability analysis uses Lyapunov‑Krasovskii functionals V(t, φ) = |φ(0) − D(t, φ)|2 + ∫−τ0 |φ(θ)|2 dθ. The derivative along solutions satisfies ℒV ≤ −c V + C if the neutral term is stable and the drift is dissipative. In control theory, the neutral term arises from systems with delays in the derivative (e.g., population models with maturation delay). A frequent examiner trap is to apply the standard Itô formula to X(t) − D(t, X_t) without verifying that the map φ ↦ φ(0) − D(t, φ) is a C1 diffeomorphism on the segment space. Another trap is to assume the segment process is Markov when the delay is state‑dependent; the Markov property holds only if the delay function τ(t, φ) depends on φ in a way that preserves the semigroup property (e.g., τ(t, φ) = τ(φ(0))). Confusing the neutral term D with the drift f leads to incorrect generator expressions; the neutral term contributes to the deterministic part of the dynamics through its time derivative.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Time‑Dependent Reflection and the Skorokhod Problem in Moving Domains

Consider a time‑dependent domain D_t ⊂ ℝ^d with C2 boundary ∂D_t moving with velocity field v(t, x). The reflected diffusion X_t in D_t satisfies

dX_t = b(t, X_t) dt + σ(t, X_t) dW_t + n(t, X_t) dL_t,

where n(t, x) is the inward unit normal to ∂D_t at x, and L_t is the boundary local time (the increasing process pushing X_t inside). The Skorokhod problem for a moving domain asks: given a continuous path η_t, find (X_t, L_t) such that X_t = η_t + ∫0^t n(s, X_s) dL_s, X_t ∈ cl(D_t), and ∫0^T 1X_s ∈ ∂D_s dL_s = L_T. The solution exists and is unique if the domain satisfies the uniform exterior sphere condition and the boundary velocity v is Lipschitz. The local time L_t is the minimal increasing process enforcing the constraint; it satisfies the Tanaka formula L_t = limε↓0 (1/ε) ∫0^t 1dist(X_s, ∂D_s) < ε |n(s, X_s) · σ(s, X_s)|2 ds. The infinitesimal generator of X_t acts on functions u ∈ C2(cl(D_t)) with ∂_n u = 0 on ∂D_t as ℒ_t u = ∂_t u + b · ∇u + ½ tr(σ σ^T ∇2 u). The transition density p(t, x; s, y) satisfies the forward Kolmogorov equation with Neumann boundary condition ∂_n p = −v · n p on ∂D_t. In finance, moving domains model barrier options with time‑dependent barriers (e.g., step‑up/step‑down barriers). The option price u(t, x) = 𝔼[e−∫_t^T r(s) ds Φ(X_T) | X_t = x] solves the parabolic variational inequality min(−∂_t u − ℒ_t u + r u, u − Φ) = 0 on D_t with Neumann condition ∂_n u = 0 on ∂D_t. Numerical methods include the penalization scheme dX_t^ε = b dt + σ dW_t − (1/ε) n(t, X_t^ε) dist(X_t^ε, ∂D_t)^− dt and the projection scheme Xt+Δt = ΠDt+Δt(X_t + b Δt + σ ΔW_t). A common examiner trap is to use the static‑domain Skorokhod condition ∫0^T 1X_s ∈ ∂D dL_s = L_T without the time‑dependent indicator; the correct condition involves the moving boundary ∂D_s. Another trap is to assume the local time L_t is the same as the boundary local time of a static domain; the moving boundary adds a drift term v · n to the local time dynamics. Forgetting the Neumann boundary condition ∂_n u = 0 when deriving the PDE for the value function leads to incorrect pricing formulas for barrier options with moving barriers.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Multiplicative Noise on Manifolds with Boundary and the Itô‑Stratonovich Correction

Let M be a smooth Riemannian manifold with boundary ∂M. A Stratonovich SDE on M with reflection at the boundary reads

dX_t = ∑i=1^m A_i(X_t) ∘ dW_t^i + A0(X_t) dt + n(X_t) dL_t,

where A_i are smooth vector fields tangent to ∂M (A_i(x) ∈ T_x ∂M for x ∈ ∂M), n is the inward unit normal, and L_t is the boundary local time. The Itô formulation acquires a correction term involving the second fundamental form II of ∂M and the covariant derivatives of A_i. In local coordinates near the boundary, the Itô SDE is

dX_t^k = ∑_i A_i^k(X_t) dW_t^i + [A0^k + ½ ∑_i (∇A_i A_i)^k + ½ ∑_i II^k(A_i, A_i)] dt + n^k dL_t,

where ∇ is the Levi‑Civita connection and II^k(u, v) = ⟨∇_u n, v⟩ n^k is the second fundamental form. The correction ½ ∑_i II(A_i, A_i) is the Itô‑Stratonovich correction due to the boundary; it vanishes if the vector fields are tangent to the boundary and the boundary is totally geodesic (II = 0). The stochastic flow φ_t : M → M generated by the SDE is a diffeomorphism of M with boundary; its derivative satisfies the linearized SDE on the tangent bundle with reflection. The generator of the reflected diffusion is ℒ = ½ ∑_i ∇A_i ∇A_i + A0 with Neumann boundary condition ∂_n u = 0. For the Brownian motion on M with reflection (A_i = orthonormal frame, A0 = 0), the generator is ½ Δ_M with Neumann condition. The heat kernel p_t(x, y) satisfies the Neumann heat equation and has the short‑time asymptotics p_t(x, y) ∼ (4πt)−d/2 e−d(x,y)2/(2t) [1 + t (R/6 + H) + …] where R is the scalar curvature and H is the mean curvature of ∂M. In filtering on manifolds with boundary, the Zakai equation for the unnormalized conditional density ρ_t is dρ_t = ℒ^* ρ_t dt + h ρ_t ∘ dY_t with Neumann condition ∂_n ρ_t = 0. A frequent examiner trap is to omit the second fundamental form correction when converting from Stratonovich to Itô on a manifold with boundary; the correction involves II(A_i, A_i), not just the connection coefficients. Another trap is to assume the reflected Brownian motion on a manifold with boundary is a semimartingale up to the boundary without verifying the non‑tangential condition; if the vector fields are not tangent to ∂M, the reflection direction must be oblique and the Skorokhod problem requires the uniform complementarity condition. Confusing the Neumann boundary condition for the generator with the Dirichlet condition for killed Brownian motion leads to incorrect eigenvalue problems.

  • Assuming the increasing process K in an RBSDE is the local time of Y at the obstacle; K is absolutely continuous only for regular obstacles, otherwise it may be singular.
  • Treating the Snell envelope as a supermartingale without verifying upper semicontinuity of the obstacle and the usual conditions on the filtration.
  • Confusing the Hausdorff dimension d_H, walk dimension d_w, and spectral dimension d_s on fractals; the relation is d_s = 2 d_H / d_w, not d_s = d_H.
  • Assuming Gaussian heat kernel bounds on fractals; the correct bounds are sub‑Gaussian with exponent d_w / (d_w − 1) > 2.
  • Applying the standard Itô formula to X(t) − D(t, X_t) in an NSFDE without verifying the diffeomorphism condition on the segment space.
  • Using the static‑domain Skorokhod condition for a moving boundary; the indicator must be 1X_s ∈ ∂D_s with the time‑dependent boundary.
  • Omitting the second fundamental form correction ½ ∑_i II(A_i, A_i) when converting Stratonovich to Itô on a manifold with boundary.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Time‑Dependent Boundary Reflection and the Moving Skorokhod Problem

When the domain of a reflected diffusion evolves in time, the classical Skorokhod problem must be reformulated. Let D_t ⊂ ℝ^d be a family of bounded domains with C2 boundaries moving with a prescribed velocity field v(t, x). The reflected SDE reads dX_t = b(t, X_t) dt + σ(t, X_t) dW_t + n(t, X_t) dL_t, where n(t, ·) is the inward unit normal to ∂D_t and L_t is the boundary local time increasing only when X_t ∈ ∂D_t. The moving Skorokhod condition is ∫0^T 1X_s ∈ ∂D_s dL_s = L_T, which replaces the static indicator 1X_s ∈ ∂D. The local time dynamics acquire an extra drift term v·n because the boundary itself moves; the Itô‑Tanaka formula applied to the distance function dist(t, X_t) yields d dist = (∂_t dist + ℒ dist) dt + ⟨∇ dist, σ dW⟩ + dL_t, and the requirement dist(t, X_t) = 0 on the boundary forces dL_t = −(∂_t dist + ℒ dist) dt − ⟨∇ dist, σ dW⟩ when X_t ∈ ∂D_t. The generator of the reflected diffusion on the moving domain is ℒ_t = ½ Tr(σσ^⊤ ∇2) + b·∇ with Neumann condition ∂_n u = 0 on ∂D_t. The associated parabolic variational inequality for the value function u(t, x) = 𝔼[∫_t^T f(s, X_s) ds + g(X_T) | X_t = x] reads min(−∂_t u − ℒ_t u + f, u − g) = 0 on D_t with ∂_n u = 0 on ∂D_t. Numerical approximation uses either a penalization scheme dX_t^ε = b dt + σ dW_t − (1/ε) n(t, X_t^ε) dist(t, X_t^ε)^− dt or a projection scheme Xt+Δt = ΠDt+Δt(X_t + b Δt + σ ΔW_t). A frequent examiner trap is to apply the static‑domain Skorokhod condition ∫0^T 1X_s ∈ ∂D dL_s = L_T without the time‑dependent indicator; the correct condition involves the moving boundary ∂D_s. Another trap is to assume the local time L_t coincides with the boundary local time of a static domain; the moving boundary adds the drift v·n to the local time dynamics. Forgetting the Neumann boundary condition ∂_n u = 0 when deriving the PDE for the value function leads to incorrect pricing formulas for barrier options with moving barriers.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Non‑Local Boundary Conditions and the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann Map

Non‑local boundary conditions arise when the reflection or absorption at the boundary depends on the global state of the process. For a diffusion X_t in a domain D with generator ℒ, the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map Λ associates to a boundary datum φ the normal derivative ∂_n u|_∂D of the solution u to ℒ u = 0 in D, u|_∂D = φ. When the boundary condition is non‑local, e.g. ∂_n u(x) = ∫_∂D K(x, y) u(y) dσ(y) for a kernel K, the map Λ becomes an integral operator on the boundary. In the stochastic representation, the process is reflected with a jump component on the boundary: when X_t hits ∂D, it either reflects instantaneously or jumps to another boundary point according to a probability kernel derived from K. The resulting boundary value problem is ℒ u = 0 in D, ∂_n u = K u on ∂D. The probabilistic construction uses a Poisson point process on the boundary local time to generate jumps; the compensator of the jump measure is the kernel K(x, y) dL_t^x dσ(y). The Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map for the fractional Laplacian (−Δ)^α/2 on a bounded domain with exterior condition u = 0 on D^c coincides with the non‑local Neumann operator ∂_n^α u = Cd,α ∫_D (u(x) − u(y))/|x−y|d+α dy, which appears in the study of stable processes killed upon exiting D. In finance, barrier options with rebates that depend on the whole path of the underlying lead to non‑local boundary conditions; the price solves a variational inequality with a non‑local Neumann term. The spectral theory of Λ is linked to the Steklov eigenvalues, which govern the rate of convergence of the reflected diffusion to its stationary distribution. A common examiner trap is to treat the non‑local Neumann condition as a standard Robin condition ∂_n u + β u = 0; the integral kernel K cannot be reduced to a multiplicative factor unless K is separable. Another trap is to assume the process remains a semimartingale after adding boundary jumps; the jump part is a pure‑jump martingale only when the kernel satisfies a symmetry condition K(x, y) = K(y, x).

Worked Example: Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann Map for a One‑Dimensional Diffusion
Consider the diffusion dX_t = dW_t on the interval (0, 1) with non‑local Neumann condition ∂_x u(0) = λ u(1), ∂_x u(1) = λ u(0), where λ > 0 is a constant. Find the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map Λ and the eigenvalues of the associated Steklov problem.
The generator is ℒ = ½ ∂_xx. For a boundary datum φ = (φ0, φ1) with φ0 = u(0), φ1 = u(1), the harmonic function solving ℒ u = 0 is linear: u(x) = φ0 + (φ1 − φ0) x. The normal derivatives are ∂_x u(0) = φ1 − φ0, ∂_x u(1) = φ1 − φ0. The non‑local condition requires φ1 − φ0 = λ φ1 at x = 0 and φ1 − φ0 = λ φ0 at x = 1. This yields the linear system (1−λ) φ1 − φ0 = 0, φ1 − (1+λ) φ0 = 0. Non‑trivial solutions exist when the determinant (1−λ)(−(1+λ)) + 1 = 0 ⇒ −(1−λ2) + 1 = 0 ⇒ λ2 = 0, so λ = 0. For λ > 0 the only harmonic function satisfying the non‑local condition is the trivial one, meaning the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map Λ is the zero operator on the subspace of admissible boundary data. The Steklov eigenvalues are the values of λ for which a non‑trivial solution exists; here the spectrum is empty for λ > 0. This illustrates that non‑local coupling can eliminate non‑trivial harmonic extensions, a fact often missed when one assumes a standard Robin parameter.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Non‑Local Boundary Conditions
When a problem presents a boundary condition involving an integral over the boundary, first write the harmonic extension explicitly (often a linear or exponential function in simple geometries). Then impose the non‑local condition to obtain a linear system for the boundary values. The Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map is the matrix that maps the boundary data to the normal derivatives; its eigenvalues are the Steklov eigenvalues. Check whether the kernel is symmetric; if not, the boundary jump process is not a martingale and the usual Feynman‑Kac representation requires a compensator correction. In multiple‑choice questions, the trap answer often replaces the integral kernel by a constant Robin coefficient; verify that the kernel is separable before simplifying.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Random Boundary Conditions and the Stochastic Neumann Problem

In many applied models the boundary of the domain is itself random, for instance a fluctuating interface in a two‑phase fluid or a stochastic volatility barrier in option pricing. Let the domain be D(ω) = {x ∈ ℝ^d : ψ(ω, x) > 0} where ψ is a random field. The diffusion X_t satisfies dX_t = b(X_t) dt + σ(X_t) dW_t + n(ω, X_t) dL_t, with the normal n and local time L_t depending on the realization ω. The stochastic Neumann problem seeks a random function u(ω, x) such that ℒ u = f in D(ω), ∂_n u = g on ∂D(ω). The solution can be represented by a Feynman‑Kac formula with expectation over both the Brownian motion and the random boundary: u(ω, x) = 𝔼[∫0^τ f(X_s) ds + g(X_τ) | X0 = x, D(ω)], where τ is the exit time from D(ω). When the random boundary is stationary and ergodic, homogenization theory shows that the effective behavior is governed by a deterministic Neumann problem on a fixed domain with an effective boundary condition ∂_n u_eff = g_eff. The corrector problem involves solving a cell problem on the probability space of the random boundary; the effective Neumann data g_eff is the average of the microscopic flux over the random boundary. In finance, a stochastic barrier option where the barrier level follows an independent diffusion leads to a random domain; the price satisfies a stochastic PDE with a random Neumann condition. A frequent examiner trap is to treat the random boundary as a deterministic perturbation and apply standard perturbation theory; the correct approach requires two‑scale convergence or the method of stochastic two‑scale convergence. Another trap is to assume the local time on the random boundary is the same as the local time on a fixed boundary; the occupation density formula must be conditioned on the random geometry, leading to an extra term involving the gradient of the random level set function ψ.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Singular Boundary Behavior and the Martin Boundary

For a transient diffusion on a domain D, the Martin boundary provides a compactification that describes all possible ways the process can escape to infinity or approach the boundary. Let X_t be a diffusion with generator ℒ = ½ Tr(a∇2) + b·∇ on D, where a is uniformly elliptic. The Green function G(x, y) satisfies ℒ_x G = −δ_y. Fix a reference point x0 ∈ D and define the Martin kernel K(x, y) = G(x, y)/G(x0, y). The Martin compactification is the closure of D in the topology generated by the functions y ↦ K(x, y). The Martin boundary ∂_M D consists of the limit points; each minimal harmonic function corresponds to a minimal Martin boundary point. The representation theorem states that any positive harmonic function h on D can be written as h(x) = ∫∂_M D K(x, ξ) dμ(ξ) for a unique measure μ on the minimal Martin boundary. For Brownian motion on a Lipschitz domain, the Martin boundary coincides with the Euclidean boundary and the Martin kernel is the Poisson kernel. For diffusions with singular drift or degenerate diffusion near the boundary, the Martin boundary can be strictly larger than the topological boundary; for example, the Bessel process of dimension δ < 2 has a Martin boundary consisting of two points {0, ∞} even though the state space is (0, ∞). In population genetics, the Wright–Fisher diffusion on the simplex has a Martin boundary that includes the vertices and edges, reflecting fixation and loss of alleles. The Martin boundary is also the key to solving the Dirichlet problem with unbounded data: the solution is the integral of the boundary data against the harmonic measure, which is the hitting distribution on the Martin boundary. A common examiner trap is to confuse the Martin boundary with the Euclidean boundary when the diffusion has singular coefficients; the minimal Martin boundary may contain points that are not in the topological closure. Another trap is to assume that every positive harmonic function is a Poisson integral of a measure on the Euclidean boundary; the correct representation uses the Martin boundary, and the harmonic measure is supported on the minimal Martin boundary.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Boundary Local Time and the Neumann Heat Kernel Asymptotics

The boundary local time L_t of a reflected diffusion measures the amount of time the process spends at the boundary. For a smooth domain D with inward normal n, the local time satisfies the occupation formula ∫0^t f(X_s) dL_s = ∫∂D f(y) ℓ_t(y) dσ(y), where ℓ_t(y) is the boundary local time density. The Neumann heat kernel p_N(t, x, y) is the transition density of the reflected Brownian motion with generator ½ Δ and Neumann condition ∂_n u = 0. Its short‑time asymptotics on a Riemannian manifold with boundary are given by the Minakshisundaram–Pleijel expansion: p_N(t, x, y) ∼ (4πt)−d/2 e−d(x,y)2/(2t) [1 + t (R/6 + H) + O(t2)], where d(x, y) is the geodesic distance, R is the scalar curvature, and H is the mean curvature of ∂D evaluated at the midpoint of the minimizing geodesic. When x or y lies on the boundary, the expansion involves the second fundamental form II of ∂D; the leading correction is ½ t ⟨II(γ̇, γ̇), n⟩ where γ is the geodesic. These asymptotics are crucial for small‑time approximations of option prices in models with reflecting barriers, for the analysis of the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann map, and for the construction of parametric approximations in statistical inference for reflected diffusions. A frequent examiner trap is to use the Dirichlet heat kernel asymptotics (which contain an extra factor of distance to the boundary) instead of the Neumann expansion; the Neumann kernel does not vanish at the boundary. Another trap is to ignore the curvature term H when the boundary is not flat; the mean curvature contributes at order t and affects the small‑time behavior of the local time. A third trap is to assume the boundary local time is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on the boundary for all times; it is only absolutely continuous for t > 0, and its density ℓ_t(y) satisfies a stochastic PDE driven by the boundary values of the Brownian motion.

  • Using the static‑domain Skorokhod condition for a moving boundary; the indicator must be time‑dependent.
  • Omitting the v·n drift term in the local time dynamics when the boundary moves.
  • Treating a non‑local Neumann kernel as a constant Robin coefficient without checking separability.
  • Assuming the boundary jump process is a martingale when the kernel is not symmetric.
  • Applying deterministic perturbation theory to a random boundary instead of stochastic two‑scale convergence.
  • Confusing the Martin boundary with the Euclidean boundary for diffusions with singular coefficients.
  • Using Dirichlet heat kernel asymptotics for a Neumann problem and ignoring the mean curvature correction.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Reflection and Oblique Derivative Boundary Conditions

Reflected diffusions arise when a process is constrained to stay inside a domain D ⊂ ℝ^d by instantaneously pushing it back along a prescribed direction whenever it attempts to exit. The classical Skorokhod problem uses the inward normal n(x) as the reflection direction, leading to the Neumann boundary condition ∂_n u = 0 for the associated generator. In many physical and financial models the reflection direction is not normal but oblique: a vector field γ(x) on ∂D that points strictly inside D, i.e. ⟨γ(x), n(x)⟩ > 0. The stochastic differential equation with oblique reflection reads

dX_t = b(X_t) dt + σ(X_t) dW_t + γ(X_t) dL_t, X_t ∈ D̅,

where L_t is the boundary local time, a non‑decreasing process that increases only when X_t ∈ ∂D. The generator of X is ℒ = ½ Tr(a∇2) + b·∇ with domain {u ∈ C2(D̅) : ⟨γ, ∇u⟩ = 0 on ∂D}. The oblique derivative condition replaces the Neumann condition and encodes a non‑symmetric conormal derivative. Existence and uniqueness for the Skorokhod problem with oblique reflection were established by Lions and Sznitman (1984) under a uniform obliqueness condition and Lipschitz regularity of γ. When γ varies with the position on the boundary, the local time L_t satisfies a Revuz correspondence with the surface measure weighted by the angle between γ and n. In finance, oblique reflection models regulated Brownian motion with state‑dependent regulation, e.g. inventory control where the replenishment rate depends on the current stock level. A frequent examiner trap is to assume that the invariant measure of an obliquely reflected diffusion is the Lebesgue measure restricted to D; the correct invariant density solves the adjoint boundary value problem ℒ*ρ = 0 with ⟨γ, ∇ρ⟩ = 0 on ∂D, which generally yields a non‑uniform density. Another trap is to apply the standard Itô–Tanaka formula for normal reflection without the correction term involving the tangential component of γ; the correct formula contains an extra integral ∫0^t ⟨∇u(X_s), γ(X_s) − n(X_s)⟩ dL_s.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Non-Local Boundary Conditions and the Regional Fractional Laplacian

Non‑local boundary conditions appear when the process can jump from the interior of a domain D directly to the boundary or when the boundary behaviour is governed by an integral operator. The regional fractional Laplacian (−Δ)^α/2|_D, α ∈ (0,2), generates a censored stable process that is killed upon leaving D. Its Dirichlet form is ℰ(u,v) = Cd,α ∫_D ∫_D (u(x)−u(y))(v(x)−v(y)) / |x−y|d+α dx dy. The associated boundary condition is non‑local: for a function u defined on D, the conormal derivative is replaced by the operator N_α u(x) = Cd,α ∫_D (u(x)−u(y)) / |x−y|d+α dy, x ∈ ∂D. The stochastic differential equation driven by the regional fractional Laplacian can be written as a jump‑diffusion with state‑dependent jump kernel that only allows jumps inside D. When the process hits the boundary, it does not reflect but rather its future evolution is determined by the non‑local operator; this leads to a “sticky” boundary behaviour where the process spends positive Lebesgue time on ∂D. The Martin boundary for the regional fractional Laplacian coincides with the Euclidean boundary for Lipschitz domains, but the Martin kernel exhibits a power‑law singularity |x−y|α−d. In applications to option pricing with barrier features, the non‑local boundary condition models a knock‑out barrier that is monitored continuously but with a probability of survival that decays as a power of the distance to the barrier. A common examiner error is to treat the regional fractional Laplacian as the spectral fractional Laplacian (defined via eigenfunction expansion) and to impose the Dirichlet condition u = 0 on ∂D; the two operators differ by a boundary term of order α/2, and their heat kernels have distinct short‑time asymptotics. Another trap is to assume that the boundary local time for the censored stable process is absolutely continuous with respect to surface measure; it is singular for α < 1 and absolutely continuous only for α > 1.

Worked Example: Oblique Reflection in a Wedge
Consider a two‑dimensional Brownian motion in the wedge D = { (r,θ) : r > 0, 0 < θ < π/4 } with oblique reflection on the two edges. On the edge θ = 0 the reflection vector is γ1 = (cos β, sin β) with β ∈ (0, π/2); on the edge θ = π/4 the reflection vector is γ2 = (−cos β, sin β). Determine the condition on β for the process to be positive recurrent and compute the invariant density up to a normalising constant.
The generator is ½ Δ with oblique derivative conditions ⟨γ_i, ∇u⟩ = 0 on each edge. In polar coordinates the boundary conditions become ∂_θ u + β ∂_r u = 0 at θ = 0 and −∂_θ u + β ∂_r u = 0 at θ = π/4. Seeking a stationary density ρ(r,θ) = rκ−1 f(θ) leads to the eigenvalue problem f'' + κ2 f = 0 with f'(0) + β κ f(0) = 0, f'(π/4) − β κ f(π/4) = 0. Positive recurrence requires κ > 0. The smallest positive κ solves tan(κ π/4) = 2β κ / (κ2 − β2). For β = 1 the equation reduces to tan(κ π/4) = 2κ/(κ2−1), whose smallest positive root is κ ≈ 1.2. The invariant density is then ρ(r,θ) ∝ rκ−1 sin(κ θ + φ) with φ determined by the boundary conditions. The process is positive recurrent because κ > 0 ensures ∫_D ρ < ∞.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the invariant measure of a reflected diffusion, first write down the adjoint boundary condition. For oblique reflection the conormal derivative is ⟨γ, ∇ρ⟩ = 0; for non‑local boundaries the adjoint involves the regional fractional Laplacian’s non‑local normal derivative. Check whether the domain is bounded — if it is, the invariant measure is always finite and can be normalised. If the domain is unbounded, verify positive recurrence via a Lyapunov function (e.g. V(x) = |x|^p) before attempting to compute the density.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Jumps and State-Dependent Intensity: Hawkes-Type Dynamics

Hawkes processes are self‑exciting point processes where the conditional intensity λ_t depends on the past jump times: λ_t = μ + ∫0^t φ(t−s) dN_s, with baseline μ > 0 and excitation kernel φ ≥ 0. When a diffusion is driven by a Hawkes‑driven jump component, the SDE takes the form

dX_t = b(X_t) dt + σ(X_t) dW_t + ∫_ℝ γ(Xt−, z) Ñ(dt, dz),

where Ñ is the compensated random measure associated with a Hawkes process whose intensity λ_t = μ + ∫0^t φ(t−s) dN_s and jump sizes are drawn from a distribution ν(dz). The state‑dependence enters through γ(x,z) and possibly through the kernel φ(t−s; X_s). This class of models captures clustering of jumps observed in high‑frequency financial data and in neuronal spike trains. The infinitesimal generator is a non‑local integro‑differential operator with a time‑inhomogeneous jump measure: ℒ_t u(x) = b(x)·∇u + ½ Tr(a∇2 u) + λ_t ∫ (u(x+γ(x,z))−u(x)−γ(x,z)·∇u) ν(dz). Because λ_t depends on the whole jump history, the process (X_t, λ_t) is Markovian in an extended state space. Existence and uniqueness of strong solutions require Lipschitz conditions on b, σ, γ and a sub‑exponential growth condition on φ to prevent explosion of the intensity. The Laplace functional of the Hawkes‑driven SDE can be computed via a Riccati‑type equation for the exponential martingale, leading to closed‑form expressions for the characteristic function of log‑returns in affine Hawkes models (e.g. the Hawkes‑CIR model). A frequent examiner trap is to treat the Hawkes intensity as a deterministic function of time and apply standard Itô formulas for Lévy‑driven SDEs; the correct approach uses the stochastic intensity λ_t as a state variable and applies the Itô formula for semimartingales with jumps. Another trap is to assume that the stationary distribution of λ_t exists for any φ; stationarity requires ∫0^∞ φ(s) ds < 1 (the branching ratio condition). If this condition fails, the intensity explodes and the SDE has no stationary solution.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Delay and Anticipative Integrals

Delay SDEs incorporate the past trajectory of the solution into the coefficients: dX_t = b(t, X_t, Xt−τ) dt + σ(t, X_t, Xt−τ) dW_t, τ > 0. The natural state space is the segment C([−τ,0]; ℝ^d). When the diffusion coefficient σ depends on the future of the driving noise (anticipative integrand), the integral ∫0^t σ(s, X_s, Xs−τ) dW_s must be interpreted in the Skorokhod sense rather than the Itô sense. The Skorokhod integral δ(u) for an anticipative process u is defined via the Malliavin derivative D and the duality relation E[δ(u) F] = E[∫0^T u_s D_s F ds] for smooth cylindrical F. For delay SDEs with anticipative noise, the solution is a process X_t that is not adapted to the Brownian filtration; its Malliavin derivative satisfies a linear delay equation. The existence and uniqueness theory (Nualart & Pardoux, 1988) requires the coefficients to be differentiable in the Malliavin sense and the delay τ to be small enough to guarantee a contraction in the appropriate Sobolev–Watanabe space. In mathematical finance, anticipative integrals model insider trading where the trader has access to future information; the resulting portfolio process is anticipative and the wealth equation involves a Skorokhod integral. The Itô formula for anticipative processes contains an extra trace term: f(X_t) = f(X0) + ∫0^t f'(X_s) δX_s + ½ ∫0^t f''(X_s) d⟨X⟩_s + ∫0^t D_s f'(X_s) σ(s, X_s, Xs−τ) ds. A common examiner error is to apply the standard Itô formula to an anticipative integral and omit the Malliavin correction term. Another trap is to assume that the solution of a delay SDE with anticipative noise is a semimartingale; it is generally not a semimartingale with respect to the original filtration, but it is a semimartingale in its own enlarged filtration.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Multiplicative Noise on Non-Geometric Rough Paths

Rough path theory extends stochastic integration to signals with Hölder regularity γ ∈ (1/3, 1/2). A geometric rough path (X, 𝕏) satisfies Chen’s relation and the shuffle product, which corresponds to the Stratonovich integral. Non‑geometric rough paths (branched rough paths) arise when the driving signal is a non‑commutative noise, e.g. the Marcus integral for Lévy‑driven SDEs or the solution of SDEs with multiplicative noise where the correction term does not satisfy the shuffle identity. The signature of a branched rough path lives in the free tensor algebra modulo the pre‑Lie relation. The integration map for a controlled rough path Y against a branched rough path X is defined via the Gubinelli derivative Y' and the branched rough integral ∫ Y dX = lim ∑ (Yt_i ΔXt_i,ti+1 + Y't_i 𝕏t_i,ti+1). For an SDE dY_t = V(Y_t) dX_t with multiplicative vector fields V, the solution is a branched rough path if the vector fields do not commute. The Itô–Stratonovich correction for non‑geometric rough paths is given by the area anomaly: the difference between the Itô and Marcus integrals equals ½ ∑i,j [V_i, V_j](Y) 𝔸i,j, where 𝔸 is the antisymmetric part of the second level of the rough path. In numerical analysis, the Euler scheme for branched rough paths converges with order γ, but the Milstein scheme requires the simulation of the Lévy area, which is not available for non‑geometric rough paths. A frequent examiner trap is to assume that the rough path lift of a semimartingale is always geometric; the Itô lift is geometric, but the Stratonovich lift of a process with jumps is non‑geometric (branched). Another trap is to apply the standard Lyons–Victoir extension theorem, which only holds for geometric rough paths; for branched rough paths one must use the Hairer–Kelly extension theorem based on the pre‑Lie structure.

  • Treating a Hawkes intensity as deterministic and omitting the branching‑ratio condition ∫ φ < 1 for stationarity.
  • Applying the standard Itô formula to an anticipative Skorokhod integral and dropping the Malliavin correction term.
  • Believing that the rough path lift of a jump‑diffusion is geometric; the Marcus lift is branched and requires the pre‑Lie area anomaly.
  • Using the Lyons–Victoir extension theorem for branched rough paths instead of the Hairer–Kelly theorem.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Randomly Varying Coefficients and Random Dynamical Systems

When the coefficients of an SDE depend on an external stochastic process that evolves on a slower time scale, the solution defines a random dynamical system (RDS) in the sense of Arnold. The driving noise is a metric dynamical system (Ω, ℱ, ℙ, (θ_t)t∈ℝ) and the solution map φ(t, ω, x) satisfies the cocycle property φ(t+s, ω) = φ(t, θ_s ω) ∘ φ(s, ω). Existence of a unique global solution requires the coefficients to be locally Lipschitz in the state variable uniformly in the random parameter and to satisfy a linear growth condition. The multiplicative ergodic theorem of Oseledets then yields Lyapunov exponents that describe the exponential growth rates of tangent vectors; these exponents are constant ℙ‑almost surely because of ergodicity of the base flow. In applications such as climate modelling or population dynamics with random carrying capacity, the random parameter may be an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, leading to a skew‑product system where the fast component is the state and the slow component is the environment. A key examiner trap is to treat the random coefficients as deterministic functions of time and to apply standard Itô calculus without accounting for the joint filtration generated by both the driving Brownian motion and the random environment. Another common error is to assume that the invariant measure of the RDS is the product of the invariant measures of the marginal processes; in general the invariant measure is supported on a random attractor that can be a fractal set. The existence of a random attractor follows from the existence of a compact absorbing set and the asymptotic compactness of the cocycle, which can be verified using the Krylov–Bogoliubov procedure adapted to the random setting. Numerical approximation of RDS requires the simulation of the base flow together with a suitable integrator for the SDE; the Euler–Maruyama scheme converges in the pullback sense under the usual Lipschitz assumptions.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Multiplicative Noise on Lie Groups and the Stochastic Exponential Map

Consider an SDE on a matrix Lie group G of the form dX_t = X_t ∘ (∑i=1^d A_i ∘ dW^i_t) + X_t B dt, where A_i, B ∈ 𝔤 (the Lie algebra) and the stochastic integral is taken in the Stratonovich sense. The Stratonovich formulation preserves the chain rule and ensures that the solution stays on G if the initial condition lies in G. The solution can be written as the stochastic exponential X_t = Exp(∫0^t ∑ A_i ∘ dW^i_s + ∫0^t B ds), where Exp is the Lie group exponential map. The Itô–Stratonovich correction on a Lie group involves the adjoint representation: the Itô drift acquires an extra term ½ ∑i=1^d adA_i^* A_i, where adA^* B = [A, B]. This correction is crucial for geometric numerical integrators such as the Munthe‑Kaas method, which lifts the SDE to the Lie algebra via the exponential map and applies a standard Runge–Kutta scheme there. In filtering on manifolds, the prediction step for a diffusion on SO(3) or SE(3) uses the stochastic exponential to propagate the mean and covariance in the Lie algebra. A frequent examiner mistake is to apply the Euclidean Itô formula directly to the matrix entries of X_t and to ignore the curvature term arising from the non‑commutativity of the group. Another trap is to assume that the stochastic exponential coincides with the ordinary exponential of the Itô integral; the two differ by the Lévy area term ½ ∑i,j [A_i, A_j] ∫0^t ∫0^s dW^i_r dW^j_s. The Magnus expansion provides a systematic way to compute the stochastic exponential as a series of iterated Stratonovich integrals, and truncation at a given order yields weak approximation schemes of arbitrarily high order.

Worked Example: Stratonovich Correction on SO(3)
Let X_t ∈ SO(3) satisfy dX_t = X_t ∘ (A ∘ dW_t) with A ∈ 𝔰𝔬(3) constant and W a scalar Brownian motion. Write the equivalent Itô SDE and identify the correction term.
The Stratonovich SDE is dX_t = X_t A ∘ dW_t. Using the conversion formula X_t A ∘ dW_t = X_t A dW_t + ½ d⟨X A, W⟩_t. Since X_t A = X_t A and dX_t = X_t A dW_t + ½ X_t A2 dt in Itô form, the quadratic covariation term is ½ X_t A2 dt. Because A ∈ 𝔰𝔬(3), A2 = -‖A‖2 I + A A^T but A^T = -A, so A2 = -‖A‖2 I. The Itô SDE becomes dX_t = X_t A dW_t - ½ ‖A‖2 X_t dt. The correction term is -½ ‖A‖2 X_t dt, which is a drift pushing the process towards the identity.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for the Itô form of a Stratonovich SDE on a Lie group, first write the Stratonovich integral as X_t ∘ A_i ∘ dW^i_t. Apply the conversion ∘ dW = dW + ½ d⟨·, W⟩ using the fact that the quadratic covariation of X_t A_i with W^i involves the derivative of the group action. Remember that the correction lives in the Lie algebra and is expressed via the adjoint representation. Practise the conversion on SO(3) and SE(3) because they appear in robotics and aerospace filtering questions.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Path-Dependent Volatility and Rough Volatility Models

Rough volatility models postulate that the log‑volatility process ν_t is driven by a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H ∈ (0, ½), leading to a volatility path that is Hölder continuous of order H - ε. The rough Heston model, for instance, specifies dS_t = S_t √{v_t} dW1_t, dv_t = λ(θ - v_t) dt + ξ v_t^α dB^H_t where B^H is a fractional Brownian motion correlated with W1. Because B^H is not a semimartingale for H ≠ ½, standard Itô calculus does not apply; one must use either the pathwise rough‑path integral (for H > ⅓) or the Malliavin calculus approach (for all H). The rough‑path lift of B^H is geometric when H > ¼ and the second level is given by the Lévy area, which can be simulated via the Cholesky factorisation of the covariance matrix. Calibration of rough volatility models to market data exploits the fact that the implied volatility skew behaves like |log K|^H for short maturities, a hallmark that distinguishes rough models from classical stochastic volatility models where the skew is linear in log‑moneyness. A common examiner trap is to treat the fractional noise as a standard Brownian motion and to write an Itô SDE for v_t; this leads to an incorrect generator and to mis‑priced options. Another error is to assume that the rough Heston model admits a closed‑form characteristic function; in fact the characteristic function involves the solution of a fractional Riccati equation that must be solved numerically. The existence and uniqueness theory for rough SDEs with path‑dependent coefficients relies on the theory of controlled rough paths: the solution Y is a controlled rough path with Gubinelli derivative Y' = σ(Y), and the fixed‑point argument is carried out in the space of controlled rough paths equipped with the γ‑Hölder norm.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Jumps and Semimartingale Characteristics

For a general semimartingale X with jumps, the triplet of predictable characteristics (B, C, ν) completely characterises its law under a given probability measure. Here B is the predictable finite‑variation part, C the predictable quadratic variation of the continuous martingale part, and ν the compensator of the jump measure μ^X. The Lévy–Itô decomposition writes X_t = X0 + B_t + X^c_t + ∫0^t ∫|x|≤1 x (μ^X - ν)(ds, dx) + ∫0^t ∫|x|>1 x μ^X(ds, dx). When the SDE is driven by a Lévy process L with characteristic triplet (b, c, ν_L), the characteristics of the solution Y of dY_t = a(Yt-) dL_t are given by B^Y_t = ∫0^t a(Ys-) b ds, C^Y_t = ∫0^t a(Ys-) c a(Ys-)^T ds, and ν^Y([0,t] × A) = ∫0^t ∫_ℝ 1_A(a(Ys-) x) ν_L(dx) ds. This representation is the basis for the martingale problem formulation and for the construction of weak solutions via the Stroock–Varadhan theory. In statistical inference for high‑frequency data, the realised power variation ∑ |Δ_i^n X|^p converges to the integral of the p‑th power of the jump size against the Lévy measure, allowing non‑parametric estimation of ν_L. A frequent examiner mistake is to ignore the truncation function in the definition of B and to write the drift as b + ∫ x ν_L(dx) without the compensator; this leads to an incorrect drift when the Lévy measure has infinite activity. Another trap is to assume that the quadratic variation [X]_t equals C_t + ∑s≤t |Δ X_s|2 for all semimartingales; the equality holds only when the jumps are square‑integrable, otherwise the sum diverges and the quadratic variation is defined via the limit of discrete approximations.

Stochastic Differential Equations with Reflection in Time-Dependent Domains and the Skorokhod Problem

The Skorokhod problem in a time‑dependent domain D(t) ⊂ ℝ^d asks for a pair (X, K) such that X_t = x0 + ∫0^t b(s, X_s) ds + ∫0^t σ(s, X_s) dW_s + K_t, X_t ∈ D(t) for all t, and K is a finite‑variation process that pushes X inward along the inward normal n(t, X_t) whenever X_t ∈ ∂D(t). The domain is assumed to satisfy a uniform exterior sphere condition and the boundary to be C1 in space and Lipschitz in time. Existence and uniqueness of a strong solution follow from a fixed‑point argument in the space of continuous functions equipped with the supremum norm, using the fact that the reflection map Γ: C([0,T]; ℝ^d) → C([0,T]; ℝ^d) is Lipschitz continuous when the domain is convex; for non‑convex time‑dependent domains one needs the additional assumption that the normal vector field is uniformly Lipschitz and that the domain does not develop cusps. The Itô–Tanaka formula for reflected diffusions contains a boundary local time term ∫0^t 1X_s ∈ ∂D(s) dL_s where L is the local time of X on the moving boundary. In finance, reflected SDEs with time‑dependent barriers model options with moving knock‑out barriers; the price satisfies a parabolic PDE with a Neumann condition on the moving boundary. A common examiner error is to treat the reflection direction as constant and to omit the time derivative of the boundary in the Skorokhod condition, which leads to an incorrect local time term. Another trap is to assume that the reflected process is a semimartingale in the original filtration; it is a semimartingale only after enlarging the filtration to include the boundary motion. Numerical schemes such as the projected Euler method converge with order ½ in the strong sense under the usual Lipschitz assumptions on the coefficients and the boundary.

Gaussian Processes, Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces, and the Karhunen–Loève Expansion

A Gaussian process is a collection of random variables {X(t) : t ∈ T} such that every finite subcollection has a multivariate normal distribution. The process is completely characterised by its mean function m(t) = E[X(t)] and covariance function C(s,t) = Cov(X(s), X(t)). For a centred Gaussian process (m ≡ 0) the covariance function is positive definite, and by the Kolmogorov extension theorem there exists a unique probability law on the space of real‑valued functions on T with these finite‑dimensional distributions. The reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) associated with C is the completion of the linear span of {C(·,t) : t ∈ T} under the inner product ⟨C(·,s), C(·,t)⟩ = C(s,t). This space, often called the Cameron–Martin space, consists of all functions f for which there exists a sequence f_n in the span converging to f in the RKHS norm; it coincides with the set of directions in which the Gaussian measure is quasi‑invariant.

Mercer's theorem (1909) states that if T is a compact metric space and C is continuous, then C admits an eigenexpansion C(s,t) = ∑k=1∞ λk ek(s) ek(t) where {ek} is an orthonormal basis of L2(T) and λk ≥ 0 are the eigenvalues of the integral operator (Tf)(s) = ∫T C(s,t) f(t) dt. The Karhunen–Loève expansion (1946–1947) then writes the process as X(t) = ∑k=1∞ √λk ξk ek(t) where ξk are independent standard normal variables. The series converges in L2(Ω×T) and, under mild conditions, uniformly on T. This representation is fundamental for simulation (truncating the series), for optimal linear filtering (the Wiener filter), and for Bayesian inference in Gaussian process regression where the posterior mean and covariance are obtained by projecting onto the RKHS.

Sample‑path regularity is governed by the behaviour of the eigenvalues. If ∑ λk < ∞ the process has a continuous version; if λk ∼ k−α with α > 1 then the paths are almost surely in the Sobolev space Hβ for β < (α−1)/2. The canonical example is the Brownian motion on [0,1] with covariance C(s,t) = min(s,t); its eigenvalues are λk = (π(k−½))−2 and eigenfunctions ek(t) = √2 sin(π(k−½)t). The RKHS of Brownian motion is the Sobolev space H10 of absolutely continuous functions with square‑integrable derivative vanishing at 0. The Cameron–Martin theorem (1944) gives the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the shifted Wiener measure: for h in the RKHS, dWh/dW = exp(∫01 h'(t) dWt − ½∫01 h'(t)2 dt). This formula underlies Girsanov’s theorem and the construction of likelihood ratios for continuously observed diffusions.

In machine learning, Gaussian process priors with kernels such as the squared‑exponential C(s,t) = exp(−|s−t|2/(2ℓ2)) or the Matérn family C(s,t) = (21−ν/Γ(ν)) (√2ν|s−t|/ℓ)ν Kν(√2ν|s−t|/ℓ) are used for non‑parametric regression. The posterior mean at a test point t* is k*T(K+σ2I)−1y where K is the Gram matrix of the training inputs and k* the vector of covariances between t* and the training points. The computational bottleneck is the O(n3) Cholesky factorisation; sparse approximations using inducing points or the Nyström method exploit the low‑rank structure of the kernel matrix, which is directly linked to the decay of the Mercer eigenvalues.

Poisson Random Measures, Compensators, and Stochastic Integration

A Poisson random measure (PRM) on ℝ+ × E, where E is a measurable space, is a random integer‑valued measure N(dt,dx) such that for disjoint sets A1,…,An the variables N(Ai) are independent Poisson random variables with means ν(Ai), where ν is a σ‑finite intensity measure on ℝ+ × E. The compensated PRM is Ñ(dt,dx) = N(dt,dx) − ν(dt,dx); it is a martingale measure in the sense that for any predictable process H(t,x) satisfying ∫0t∫E |H(s,x)|2 ν(ds,dx) < ∞ a.s., the stochastic integral ∫0t∫E H(s,x) Ñ(ds,dx) is a square‑integrable martingale with quadratic variation ∫0t∫E |H(s,x)|2 ν(ds,dx). This construction, due to Itô (1951) and later extended by Kunita and Watanabe, provides the foundation for stochastic calculus with jumps.

The Lévy–Itô decomposition writes any Lévy process Lt as Lt = bt + σWt + ∫0t∫|x|≤1 x Ñ(ds,dx) + ∫0t∫|x|>1 x N(ds,dx), where (b,σ2,ν) is the Lévy triplet. The truncation function χ(x) = x 1{|x|≤1} appears in the drift term b = γ − ∫|x|≤1 x ν(dx) to ensure convergence of the small‑jump integral. Omitting χ leads to an incorrect drift when ν has infinite activity (e.g., for a symmetric α‑stable process with α∈(1,2) the integral ∫|x|≤1 x ν(dx) diverges). The stochastic exponential of a Lévy‑driven SDE dYt = Yt− dLt is given by the Doléans–Dade formula Yt = exp(Lt − ½[L]tc) ∏s≤t (1+ΔLs) e−ΔLs, where [L]c is the continuous quadratic variation. This explicit representation is essential for change‑of‑measure techniques in finance (Esscher transform) and for the construction of martingale measures in incomplete markets.

For a general semimartingale X with jump measure μX and compensator νX, the Itô formula for a C2 function f reads f(Xt) = f(X0) + ∫0t f'(Xs−) dXsc + ½∫0t f''(Xs−) d[X]sc + ∑s≤t [f(Xs) − f(Xs−) − f'(Xs−)ΔXs]. The sum over jumps can be rewritten as ∫0t∫ℝ [f(Xs−+x) − f(Xs−) − f'(Xs−)x] μX(ds,dx). When X solves dXt = a(Xt−) dLt with L a Lévy process, the characteristics of X are BXt = ∫0t a(Xs−) b ds, CXt = ∫0t a(Xs−)2 σ2 ds, and νX([0,t]×A) = ∫0t∫ℝ 1A(a(Xs−)x) ν(dx) ds. This predictable‑characteristics representation is the basis for the martingale problem formulation of Stroock and Varadhan (1969) and for the construction of weak solutions via the method of time‑change.

In statistical inference for high‑frequency data, the realised power variation ∑i=1n |Δin X|p converges in probability to ∫0t∫ℝ |x|p ν(dx) ds when p > 2 and the sampling interval Δn → 0. This allows non‑parametric estimation of the Lévy measure ν, a key step in calibrating jump‑diffusion models to tick‑by‑tick financial data. A frequent examiner mistake is to treat the quadratic variation [X]t as Ct + ∑s≤t |ΔXs|2 without checking square‑integrability of the jumps; for infinite‑activity Lévy processes the sum diverges and the quadratic variation is defined only as the limit of discrete approximations.

Stochastic Bifurcation, Random Dynamical Systems, and Invariant Measures

A random dynamical system (RDS) on a Polish space X over a metric dynamical system (Ω,ℱ,ℙ,θ) is a measurable map φ: ℝ+ × Ω × X → X satisfying the cocycle property φ(t+s,ω) = φ(t,θsω) ∘ φ(s,ω) and φ(0,ω) = id. The driving noise is often a Wiener shift θtω(·) = ω(·+t) − ω(t) on the canonical Wiener space. For an SDE dXt = b(Xt) dt + σ(Xt) dWt with globally Lipschitz coefficients, the solution map φ(t,ω)x = Xt(ω,x) defines a continuous RDS. The existence of a random attractor A(ω) — a compact random set that is invariant (φ(t,ω)A(ω) = A(θtω)) and attracts all bounded deterministic sets — is guaranteed under dissipativity conditions (e.g., ⟨b(x),x⟩ ≤ −c|x|2 for large |x|).

Stochastic bifurcation refers to qualitative changes in the invariant measures or in the sample‑path behaviour as a parameter varies. Two main notions are P‑bifurcation (phenomenological) where the stationary density changes from unimodal to multimodal, and D‑bifurcation (dynamical) where the Lyapunov exponent of the linearised system crosses zero. For the stochastic Hopf normal form dZ = (λ + i)Z dt − |Z|2 Z dt + σ dW (complex‑valued), the trivial solution loses stability at λ = σ2/2 (D‑bifurcation) while the stationary density becomes crater‑shaped at λ = σ2 (P‑bifurcation). The Lyapunov exponent is given by the Furstenberg–Khasminskii formula λ1 = limt→∞ (1/t) log |φ(t,ω)x| = ∫ ⟨b(x),x⟩/|x|2 μ(dx) where μ is the invariant measure on the projective space. Oseledets’ multiplicative ergodic theorem (1968) provides a filtration of subspaces with distinct Lyapunov exponents for the linearised cocycle Dφ(t,ω).

Invariant measures for RDS are probability measures μ on X such that ℙ ⊗ μ is invariant under the skew‑product flow Θt(ω,x) = (θtω, φ(t,ω)x). The Krylov–Bogoliubov averaging procedure constructs invariant measures for Markovian RDS by taking weak limits of (1/T)∫0T Pt* δx dt, where Pt is the transition semigroup. Uniqueness and ergodicity follow from a strong Feller property combined with topological irreducibility (Doob’s theorem). For non‑degenerate diffusions (σσT uniformly elliptic) the transition densities are smooth and the invariant measure has a C∞ density solving the stationary Fokker–Planck equation L*ρ = 0. In the degenerate hypoelliptic case (Hörmander’s condition) the Malliavin calculus yields smooth densities and exponential convergence to equilibrium in total variation.
