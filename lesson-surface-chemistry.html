Surface Chemistry — Adsorption, Catalysis, Colloids & Emulsions

Surface chemistry studies phenomena that occur at the interface of two phases (solid-liquid, solid-gas, liquid-liquid, etc.). Adsorption is the accumulation of a substance (adsorbate) on the surface of a solid or liquid (adsorbent). Physisorption involves weak van der Waals forces (low enthalpy, reversible, multilayer) while chemisorption involves chemical bond formation (high enthalpy, irreversible, monolayer). The Freundlich isotherm x/m = k p^{1/n} and the Langmuir isotherm θ = Kp/(1+Kp) describe adsorption at equilibrium. Catalysis involves substances that increase reaction rate without being consumed — homogeneous (same phase as reactants) and heterogeneous (different phase, typically solid catalyst with gaseous reactants). Colloids are dispersions of particles (1–1000 nm) in a dispersion medium — classified as sols (solid in liquid), gels (liquid in solid), emulsions (liquid in liquid), and foams (gas in liquid or solid). The Tyndall effect (scattering of light by colloidal particles) distinguishes colloids from true solutions. Electrophoresis is the movement of colloidal particles under an electric field — used to determine charge and for separation.

"All of chemistry is surface chemistry — reactions happen at interfaces."
— Adapted from J. M. Thomas
Part 1

🧲 Adsorption: Types and Isotherms

Interface
Physisorption vs Chemisorption, Freundlich and Langmuir Isotherms, and Applications
Adsorption is a surface phenomenon where molecules of a fluid (adsorbate) adhere to the surface of a solid (adsorbent). Physisorption (physical adsorption) involves weak van der Waals forces: low enthalpy (20–40 kJ/mol), reversible, forms multilayers, decreases with temperature, and is non-specific. Chemisorption (chemical adsorption) involves chemical bond formation: high enthalpy (80–240 kJ/mol), irreversible, forms a monolayer, increases with temperature initially, and is specific. Adsorption isotherms describe the amount adsorbed as a function of pressure at constant temperature. The Freundlich isotherm: x/m = k p^{1/n} (log form: log(x/m) = log k + (1/n) log p), where n > 1 indicates favorable adsorption. The Langmuir isotherm: θ = Kp/(1+Kp), where θ is the fraction of surface covered, K is the adsorption equilibrium constant. Langmuir assumes monolayer adsorption, uniform surface, and no interaction between adsorbed molecules. At low pressure: θ ≈ Kp (linear); at high pressure: θ → 1 (saturation). BET isotherm extends Langmuir to multilayers — used to measure surface area. Applications: activated charcoal for gas masks and water purification, silica gel as a desiccant, catalysis in heterogeneous reactions, and chromatography for separation.
Adsorption arises from unbalanced forces at surfaces; the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms model equilibrium coverage and are fundamental to catalysis and separation science.
Part 2

⚗️ Catalysis: Mechanisms and Types

Acceleration
Homogeneous Catalysis, Heterogeneous Catalysis, Enzyme Catalysis, and Catalytic Promoters
A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction without being consumed, by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy. The catalyst is regenerated at the end. Homogeneous catalysis: the catalyst and reactants are in the same phase (e.g., acid-catalyzed ester hydrolysis, NO in the lead chamber process for H₂SO₄). Heterogeneous catalysis: the catalyst is in a different phase — typically a solid catalyst with gaseous or liquid reactants (e.g., Haber process: Fe for NH₃ synthesis, Ostwald process: Pt for HNO₃, catalytic converters: Pt/Pd/Rh for automobile exhaust). Adsorption theory of heterogeneous catalysis: (1) diffusion of reactants to the surface, (2) adsorption, (3) reaction on the surface, (4) desorption of products, (5) diffusion away. Active sites on the catalyst surface are where reaction occurs. Promoters increase catalyst activity (e.g., K₂O in Fe for Haber process). Poisons deactivate the catalyst (e.g., S in Fe for Haber process). Enzyme catalysis: enzymes are biological catalysts with remarkable specificity. The lock-and-key model (Emil Fischer) and induced fit model (Koshland) explain substrate binding. The Michaelis-Menten kinetics: rate = V_max[S]/(K_m+[S]). Shape-selective catalysis by zeolites (molecular sieves with pores of uniform size) — e.g., ZSM-5 for cracking and isomerization of hydrocarbons.
Catalysts accelerate reactions by lowering activation energy without being consumed; heterogeneous catalysis on solid surfaces drives most industrial chemical processes.
Part 3

🔬 Colloids: Preparation and Properties

Dispersion
Types of Colloids, Lyophilic vs Lyophobic, Preparation Methods, and Purification
Colloids are heterogeneous systems where one substance (dispersed phase, particle size 1–1000 nm) is distributed in another (dispersion medium). Types: sol (solid in liquid), gel (liquid in solid), emulsion (liquid in liquid), foam (gas in liquid), aerosol (liquid or solid in gas). Lyophilic colloids (solvent-loving): the dispersed phase has affinity for the medium — they are stable, reversible, and form spontaneously (e.g., starch in water, gelatin). Lyophobic colloids (solvent-hating): no affinity — unstable, irreversible, and require special methods to prepare (e.g., gold sol, Fe(OH)₃ sol). Preparation methods: condensation methods (chemical reactions — hydrolysis, reduction, oxidation, double decomposition) and dispersion methods (mechanical grinding, peptization, electrical disintegration — Bredig's arc method for metal colloids). Purification: dialysis (removal of electrolytes through a semipermeable membrane), electrodialysis (accelerated by electric field), and ultrafiltration (using fine-pored filters). Properties: Tyndall effect (scattering of light by colloidal particles — the path of a light beam becomes visible), Brownian movement (random zigzag motion due to bombardment by medium molecules — prevents settling), electrophoresis (movement of charged particles in an electric field), coagulation (destabilization by adding electrolytes — Hardy-Schulze rule: the ion with opposite charge and higher charge is more effective at causing coagulation).
Colloids are intermediate between true solutions and suspensions; their stability, optical properties, and electrical behavior arise from the large surface area and surface charge of the dispersed particles.
Part 4

🧪 Emulsions and Applications

Dispersions
Emulsions, Emulsifiers, Demulsification, Gels, and Industrial Applications of Surface Chemistry
Emulsions are colloidal dispersions of one liquid in another immiscible liquid (both liquids are mutually insoluble). Two types: oil-in-water (O/W) — oil dispersed in water (e.g., milk, mayonnaise) and water-in-oil (W/O) — water dispersed in oil (e.g., butter, cream). Emulsifiers (emulsifying agents) are substances that stabilize emulsions by forming a protective film around the dispersed droplets, reducing interfacial tension. Examples: soaps, detergents, proteins, gums, and surfactants. The HLB (hydrophilic-lipophilic balance) scale determines the emulsifying action of surfactants. Demulsification breaks emulsions — by heating, centrifugation, adding electrolytes, or chemical demulsifiers. Gels: colloidal systems where the dispersed phase forms a continuous network trapping the liquid — e.g., gelatin, agar-agar, silica gel. Syneresis: the spontaneous exudation of liquid from a gel. Application of colloids: purification of water (alum coagulates colloidal impurities), rubber industry (latex coagulation), paints and inks (pigment dispersion), medicines (colloidal silver as antiseptic, Argyrol), photography (silver halide emulsion on gelatin), smoke precipitation (Cottrell electrostatic precipitator removes colloidal smoke particles from industrial exhaust), food industry (mayonnaise, ice cream, whipped cream all stabilized by emulsifiers). Chromatography: separation based on differential adsorption on a stationary phase — paper chromatography, thin-layer chromatography, column chromatography.
Emulsions and gels are ubiquitous in daily life and industry; emulsifiers stabilize them by reducing interfacial tension and providing a protective barrier around dispersed droplets.
