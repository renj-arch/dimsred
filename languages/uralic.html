Hungarian · Finnish · Estonian

Three languages, one ancient family (Uralic), surrounded on all sides by Indo-European neighbors. They glue words together, sing with vowel harmony, and never met a case ending they didn't like.

🌍 The Family

The big myth to bust: Hungarian is NOT related to its neighbours' Slavic/Germanic tongues — its closest cousins are Finnish and Estonian, thousands of kilometres away. "Hungarian" even sounds like the Huns, but it isn't Turkic either; the Hun in the name comes from the Onogur tribes who once lived near the Magyars.

🎵 Vowel Harmony — the one rule that explains everything

Words sort vowels into "front" and "back" groups, and every suffix must match the word it sticks to:

GroupHungarianFinnishEstonian
Back vowelsa á o ó u úa o ua o u õ
Front vowelse é i í ö ő ü űä ö yä ö ü
Neutrali í éi ei e

Example (Hungarian dative suffix -nak/-nek): háznak "to the house" (back) but könyvnek "to the book" (front). Finnish inessive -ssa/-ssä: talossa "in the house" vs metsässä "in the forest". Once you feel harmony, you can predict almost every ending.

🧩 Agglutination — stack the bricks

Instead of separate words, Uralic languages stack suffixes onto a noun. One Hungarian word can express a whole English sentence:

LayerPieceMeaning
rootházhouse
+ pluralház-akhouses
+ caseházak-banin houses
+ possessiveházaim-banin my houses
+ plural possessiveházaimban"in my houses" — one word!

Finnish shows off too: talo (house) → taloissammekin "also in our houses" (root + in + our + also). And Finnish famously does this with verbs: juoksentelisinkohan — "I wonder if I should run around a bit" — one verb word.

🔤 Script & pronunciation quirks

  • Hungarian — s = sh, sz = s, cs = ch, gy = dy, ny = ny, ty = ty. So szia (hello) = "see-ah" and csütörtök (Thursday) starts like "chuh".
  • Finnish — y = French u, ä = English a in "hat", ö = "er" with rounded lips. Double letters are held longer — tuli (fire) vs tuuli (wind) vs tulli (customs).
  • Estonian — three-way vowel/consonant length (short, long, overlong): sada (hundred), saada (get), saada (send!) — pitch and length both matter.
  • Stress is always on the first syllable in all three — a rare mercy.

💡 Spot the family resemblance: Finnish kolme ~ Estonian kolm ~ Hungarian három; Finnish kuusi ~ Estonian kuus (6). The words for 2, 3, 6, 100 go back 6,000 years.

🧠 Quick Facts

  • No grammatical gender in any of the three — one he/she/it system (Hungarian ő, Finnish/Estonian hän/tema).
  • No verb "to have": possession is expressed as location — Finnish Minulla on literally "at-me is".
  • No future tense in Finnish/Hungarian — context + aspect carry future meaning.
  • Estonian <õ> is a unique close back unrounded vowel that exists almost nowhere else.
  • The world's longest word folklore: Finnish lentokonesuihkuturbiinimoottoriapumekaanikkoaliupseerioppilas (aeroplane jet-turbine engine auxiliary mechanic non-commissioned officer student).
  • They are all "small" languages with proud literatures — Finland's Kalevala epic even inspired Tolkien's Middle-earth mythology.
