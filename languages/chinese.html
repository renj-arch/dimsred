🎵 Tones — the heart of Chinese

In Chinese, pitch is grammar. The syllable ma in Mandarin is four different words:

ToneShapeMandarinMeaning
1flat, high (55)mā 妈mother
2rising (35)má 麻hemp
3dip, low (214)mǎ 马horse
4falling (51)mà 骂scold
0light/neutralma 吗question particle

Cantonese adds the "entering tones" — syllables that end in -p, -t, -k are extra-short and high. The classic joke-word set 唔 無 唔 (m̀h, mǒu, ...) is how beginners learn that tone changes meaning completely.

💡 Mnemonic for the four Mandarin tones: first tone = radio announcer; second tone = surprised question ("what?"); third tone = a slow "meh"; fourth tone = angry slam.

🔤 Characters — simplified vs. traditional

Each character is a syllable and often a morpheme (a meaning-unit). Two styles:

Hong Kong香港香港Xiānggǎng

Simplified (1950s mainland reform) cut strokes; Traditional kept the historical forms in Taiwan, HK, and Macau. ~3,000 characters covers 99.5% of everyday text.

Stroke rules that help you guess

  • Left-to-right, top-to-bottom; 人 = a person's two legs.
  • Many characters stack a semantic root + a phonetic hint: 妈 (mā, mother) = 女 (woman) + 马 (mǎ, gives the "ma" sound).
  • Radicals (部首) are the "categories": 氵 = water (江 river, 河 river, 海 sea).

🧩 Grammar — the beautiful part

Chinese grammar is famously light: no tenses, no plurals, no cases, no agreement. Word order + a few particles carry everything.

SVO word order, like English

Mandarin: 我 吃 饭 — Wǒ chī fàn = "I eat (rice/meal)." Subject–verb–object, but modifiers go before the noun, adverbs usually after the subject.

The four super-particles

ParticleJobExample
的 depossessive / modifier glue我的 wǒ de — "my"
了 lecompletion marker (the "past" that isn't a tense)吃了 chī le — "ate / eaten"
吗 maturns a statement into a yes/no question好吗？ hǎo ma?
在 zài"at" + also the progressive (正在 = currently)在吃饭 zài chīfàn — "eating right now"

Measure words (量词)

You can't say "three dogs" — you must say "three [animal classifier] dogs": 三只狗 sān zhī gǒu. Different things get different counters:

MeasureForExample
个 gethe general all-rounder (people, things)三个人 sān ge rén
只 zhīanimals, one of a pair一只猫 yì zhī māo
本 běnbooks & bound volumes一本书 yì běn shū
张 zhāngflat things: paper, tables, tickets一张纸 yì zhāng zhǐ
杯 bēidrinks in a cup一杯咖啡 yì bēi kāfēi
辆 liàngvehicles一辆车 yí liàng chē

Same in Cantonese — but the classic general counter is 個 go³, and you'll hear it everywhere.

Jyutping (Cantonese) uses trailing numbers for tone: 我 ngo⁵, 唔該 m̀h-gōi "thank you". It also has sounds Mandarin lost — final consonants -p, -t, -k and the ŋ- initial.

🧠 Quick Facts & Cultural Notes

  • More people learn Chinese than speak English natively — and every character is a tiny piece of calligraphic art.
  • Tone changes in context: two third tones together → first becomes second. It's automatic and natural once you're fluent.
  • Cantonese keeps final consonants (-p, -t, -k) that Mandarin lost — which is why classical poetry rhymes better read aloud in Cantonese.
  • Chinese is very "pro-drop" — speakers skip subjects they assume you know, exactly like Japanese.
  • Numbers are a language game: 8 (bā) sounds like 发 (fā, wealth) — apartments with 4 (sì, death) get renumbered.
  • Written vs. spoken register: written Chinese is compressed and literary; spoken is conversational. Learn both and you read contracts and gossip.

Tone shift rules (变调)

  • Two third tones together: the first becomes tone 2. 你好 (nǐ hǎo) → ní hǎo — you won't be expected to spell it, just say it.
  • 不 (bù, "not") before a fourth tone becomes bú: 不是 bú shì.
  • 一 (yī, "one") shifts before 4th tone (yí) and 1/2/3 (yì).
  • Neutral tone: 们, 吗, 了 lose their pitch and just whisper: 妈妈 māma.
💡 Writing order in two strokes = first the big line, then the dot. Left first, right second, top first, bottom second — inside before the frame closes.
