Korean (한국어)

The language isolate with a purpose-built alphabet — Hangul was designed in the 1440s to be learnable in a day. Add logical syllable blocks, SOV grammar, and five levels of politeness.

Languages Hub → Korean

🌍 Overview

  • Family: Koreanic — a language isolate (no demonstrable relatives, despite centuries of trying to link it to Japanese or Altaic)
  • Speakers: ~82M (South + North Korea, Yanbian in China, diaspora)
  • Script: Hangul (한글), a featural alphabet where letter shapes show how to make the sound
  • History: invented by King Sejong's scholars in 1443 and published in 1446 (Hunminjeongeum) so ordinary people could read — until then only Chinese characters were used.

🔤 Hangul in one sitting

Consonants are drawn to mirror your mouth; vowels are drawn to mirror the sky (☀) and earth (—).

Syllable blocks: letters pack into squares — consonant(s) on top, vowel in the middle, optional final consonant below. 한 = ㅎ+ㅏ+ㄴ, 글 = ㄱ+ㅡ+ㄹ. That's why Korean looks like neat little tiles.

💡 Read the word for "Korea": 한국 = ㅎ(ㅏ)ㄴ + ㅎ… wait — 한국 is han-guk. Start by sounding out 한 (han) then 국 (guk). You just read Korean.

🧩 Grammar — SOV, particles, and politeness

Word order: Subject-Object-Verb

English: "I eat rice." Korean: 저는 밥을 먹어요 — "I rice eat." The verb always comes last.

Particles

Little suffixes mark roles, like cases but looser:

The five politeness levels

You pick a verb ending based on who you're talking to. Master two to survive, know all five to be fluent:

LevelEndingUse it with
Plain-다books, diaries, news headlines
Intimate-어/-아close friends, children
Familiar-지older friends, coworkers
Polite-어요/-아요strangers, everyday life — learn this first
Formal-습니다speeches, TV news, bosses

So "I go" is 가 + level: 간다 (plain), 가 (intimate), 가요 (polite), 갑니다 (formal).

🔢 Numbers — two systems!

Korean has two number sets: native Korean (counting, age) and Sino-Korean (from Chinese; dates, money, math). You must know both.

Count people with native (두 명 = two people); pay with Sino (오천 원 = 5,000 won). 100 = 백 (Sino), 100 = 온 (native, archaic).

🧠 Quick Facts & Cultural Notes

  • Hangul Day (October 9) is a national holiday in South Korea — the only country with a holiday for its alphabet.
  • North vs South: same spoken language, but differing vocabulary and even a few spelling splits since the 1950s.
  • No gendered pronouns by default — 그 can be he, she, or it depending on context.
  • Honorifics change the verb: giving food to a respected elder uses a special form (드시다) rather than the plain 먹다 (eat).
  • 한자 (hanja): Chinese characters still appear for clarity in newspapers, names, and legal documents — about 1,800 are taught in schools.
  • 잘 자! / 잘 가! — "Sleep well!" / "Go well!" — Korean goodbyes often bless your next action rather than just say farewell.
