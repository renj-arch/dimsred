Russian & Slavic Languages

One big family — three great starting points. Russian (Cyrillic, ~260M speakers), Polish and Czech (Latin script, ~50M combined). Master the cases and consonants once, and they all open up.

Languages Hub → Russian & Slavic

🌍 The Family

Slavic languages are Indo-European, split into three branches:

BranchLanguagesScriptCases
East SlavicRussian, Ukrainian, BelarusianCyrillic6
West SlavicPolish, Czech, SlovakLatin6–7
South SlavicSerbian, Croatian, Bulgarian, SloveneCyrillic & Latin0–7

Russian uses the Cyrillic alphabet; Polish and Czech use the Latin alphabet with diacritics (Polish ł ś ź ż; Czech č ř š ž). All three are inflected: nouns change their ending based on the role they play in the sentence — that's the "six cases".

The rest are familiar: Б б = b, Г г = g, Д д = d, Ж ж = zh, З з = z, И и = ee, Л л = l, П п = p, У у = oo, Ф ф = f, Ц ц = ts, Ч ч = ch, Ш ш = sh, Щ щ = shch, Ю ю = yu, Я я = ya.

Polish — Latin + diacritics (32 letters)

The trap: Polish w sounds like English v; ł sounds like English w; c is ts; j is y. Read woda (water) as "vo-da" and kot (cat) as "kot".

Czech — Latin + diacritics (42 letters incl. digraphs)

Czech v is v (unlike Polish), c is ts, č is ch, š is sh, and the crown jewel ř is a rolled r fused with zh. č also marks long vowels: á é í ó ú ý.

🗣 Pronunciation — the two pillars

1. Stress is unpredictable. Every Russian word has one stressed syllable that you must memorize; unstressed o is pronounced like a. In Polish and Czech, stress almost always falls on the first syllable.

2. Hard vs. soft consonants. Russian consonants come in pairs — hard (та, да) and soft/palatalized (тя, дя, with a slight "y" glide). Compare мат "checkmate" vs. мять "to crumple": the т is hard in the first, soft in the second. This single distinction changes word meaning constantly.

🧩 Grammar — the six cases

Nouns, adjectives, and pronouns change endings to show their job. Learn the question that triggers each case:

CaseRoleAsking wordExample (Russian «кот» = cat)
Nominativesubjectwho/what?кот
Genitivepossession, "of"whose? from whom?кота
Dativeindirect object, "to"to whom?коту
Accusativedirect objectwhom? what? (object)кота
Instrumental"with/by means of"with whom? by what?котом
Prepositionallocation/topicabout whom? where?о коте

Polish has the same six plus Vocative (calling someone: Kot! → Kocie!). Czech has all seven. Russian has no articles — кот means both "cat" and "a/the cat".

Verbs — two aspects

Every Slavic verb comes in a pair: imperfective (ongoing/habitual) and perfective (completed). Я читал книгу = "I was reading a book"; Я прочитал книгу = "I finished reading it."

Word order

Flexible, because the cases already tell you who did what. Мальчик читает книгу and Книгу читает мальчик both mean "the boy reads the book" — word order signals emphasis, not meaning.

🧠 Quick Facts & Cultural Notes

  • Two alphabets, one family — Serbian uses BOTH Cyrillic and Latin officially.
  • Czech <ř> is one of the rarest phonemes on Earth and famously impossible for non-natives — take your time.
  • Polish consonant clusters look terrifying (szczęście "happiness") but each letter still maps to one sound — you can sound it out.
  • Cyrillic speed-win: reading "СТОП", "ТАКСИ", "КАФЕ" on a Russian street trains the alphabet for free.
  • Russian stress changes meaning — за́мок (castle) vs замо́к (lock). Always learn new words with their stress mark.
  • Feminine gender matters — Slavic languages have masculine, feminine, and neuter nouns, and adjectives/verbs agree.
