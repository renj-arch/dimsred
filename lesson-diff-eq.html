Differential Equations — The Language of Change

Differential equations describe how quantities change. They're the mathematical language of physics, biology, economics, and engineering. An ODE involves one independent variable; a PDE involves partial derivatives with respect to multiple variables. The field studies existence, uniqueness, stability, and qualitative behavior of solutions — often without solving explicitly. From Newton's laws to the Navier-Stokes equations, differential equations model virtually every continuous phenomenon.

"Differential equations are the language of nature. To understand the universe, learn to speak it."
— Freeman Dyson (paraphrased)
Part 1

📈 Ordinary Differential Equations — Flows on the Line and Plane

From Leibniz to Lyapunov
ODEs — Existence, Uniqueness, and the Geometry of Solutions
An ODE is an equation dy/dt = f(t,y). The Picard-Lindelöf theorem (Cauchy-Lipschitz) gives local existence and uniqueness when f is Lipschitz in y. The method of integrating factors solves linear first-order ODEs. For autonomous systems dy/dt = f(y), solutions are flows on the phase space. Fixed points satisfy f(y*) = 0 — they're equilibrium points. A fixed point is stable if nearby solutions stay nearby, asymptotically stable if they converge to it. Lyapunov's method proves stability without solving: find a function V(y) that decreases along trajectories. For linear systems dy/dt = Ay, stability is determined by eigenvalues: Re(λ) < 0 for all eigenvalues ⇒ asymptotic stability. Bifurcations occur when parameter changes cause qualitative changes in the phase portrait — saddle-node, transcritical, pitchfork, and Hopf are the standard examples.
ODEs are flows on phase space — you don't always need to solve them; geometry and stability analysis tell the story.
Part 2

🌊 Partial Differential Equations — Fields and Waves

Laplace, Heat, and Wave
PDEs — The Three Great Equations of Mathematical Physics
PDEs involve partial derivatives in space and time. The three classical second-order linear PDEs are: the Laplace equation ∇²u = 0 (elliptic), the heat equation u_t = α∇²u (parabolic), and the wave equation u_tt = c²∇²u (hyperbolic). The method of separation of variables assumes u(x,t) = X(x)T(t) to reduce the PDE to ODEs via eigenvalues. Fourier series solve these eigenvalue problems — the solution is expanded in eigenfunctions of the Laplacian. Duhamel's principle handles forced equations. Green's functions are the fundamental solution — the response to a point source. Characteristics for first-order PDEs (method of characteristics) reduce them to ODEs along curves. For nonlinear PDEs, shock waves can form — solutions develop discontinuities even from smooth initial data (Burgers' equation). The Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness is a Millennium Prize Problem: do 3D Navier-Stokes equations always have smooth solutions?
The three great PDEs — Laplace, heat, wave — govern potential, diffusion, and propagation, with Fourier analysis as the universal tool.
Part 3

🔄 Dynamical Systems — Long-Term Behavior and Chaos

Order to Chaos
Dynamical Systems — Attractors, Bifurcations, and the Butterfly Effect
A dynamical system is a rule for time evolution — either differential equations (continuous time) or maps (discrete time). The Poincaré map reduces a continuous flow to a discrete map by intersecting trajectories with a surface. An attractor is a set that trajectories converge to — fixed points, limit cycles, tori, or strange attractors (fractal). The Lorenz attractor from Lorenz's 1963 weather model is the iconic strange attractor — it has sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the hallmark of chaos. A system is chaotic if it has a dense orbit, sensitivity to initial conditions, and a dense set of periodic orbits. The logistic map x_{n+1} = rx_n(1−x_n) exhibits period-doubling route to chaos as r increases — the Feigenbaum constant δ = 4.669... is universal. KAM theory (Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser) says that under small perturbations, most invariant tori persist — explaining why the solar system is stable.
Chaos is deterministic but unpredictable — the butterfly effect is not an error, it's a property of nonlinear systems.
Part 4

⚡ Advanced Theory — Variational Methods, Integrability, and Modern Directions

From Euler-Lagrange to Solitons
Advanced PDE Theory — Variational Methods, Integrable Systems, and Geometric PDEs
Many PDEs arise from variational principles: solutions are critical points of a functional. The Euler-Lagrange equation gives the condition for a minimizer. The Hamilton-Jacobi equation is the PDE of classical mechanics — the action satisfies it. Solitons are localized waves of the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation that maintain their shape through nonlinear balance — they're integrable via the inverse scattering transform. Geometric PDEs include the minimal surface equation (mean curvature = 0) and the Yamabe problem (find a metric of constant scalar curvature). The Ricci flow ∂g/∂t = −2Ric(g) was used by Perelman to prove the Poincaré conjecture — it evolves metrics toward constant curvature. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem connects the index of elliptic PDEs on a manifold to topological invariants. Morse theory studies topology through critical points of functions, giving deep results in global analysis.
Modern PDE theory reaches into geometry, topology, and mathematical physics — from solitons to the Poincaré conjecture via Ricci flow.
