Differential Equations — The Language of Change

Differential equations describe how quantities change. They're the mathematical language of physics, biology, economics, and engineering. An ODE involves one independent variable; a PDE involves partial derivatives with respect to multiple variables. The field studies existence, uniqueness, stability, and qualitative behavior of solutions — often without solving explicitly. From Newton's laws to the Navier-Stokes equations, differential equations model virtually every continuous phenomenon.

"Differential equations are the language of nature. To understand the universe, learn to speak it."
— Freeman Dyson (paraphrased)
Part 1

📈 Ordinary Differential Equations — Flows on the Line and Plane

From Leibniz to Lyapunov
ODEs — Existence, Uniqueness, and the Geometry of Solutions
An ODE is an equation dy/dt = f(t,y). The Picard-Lindelöf theorem (Cauchy-Lipschitz) gives local existence and uniqueness when f is Lipschitz in y. The method of integrating factors solves linear first-order ODEs. For autonomous systems dy/dt = f(y), solutions are flows on the phase space. Fixed points satisfy f(y*) = 0 — they're equilibrium points. A fixed point is stable if nearby solutions stay nearby, asymptotically stable if they converge to it. Lyapunov's method proves stability without solving: find a function V(y) that decreases along trajectories. For linear systems dy/dt = Ay, stability is determined by eigenvalues: Re(λ) < 0 for all eigenvalues ⇒ asymptotic stability. Bifurcations occur when parameter changes cause qualitative changes in the phase portrait — saddle-node, transcritical, pitchfork, and Hopf are the standard examples.
ODEs are flows on phase space — you don't always need to solve them; geometry and stability analysis tell the story.
Part 2

🌊 Partial Differential Equations — Fields and Waves

Laplace, Heat, and Wave
PDEs — The Three Great Equations of Mathematical Physics
PDEs involve partial derivatives in space and time. The three classical second-order linear PDEs are: the Laplace equation ∇²u = 0 (elliptic), the heat equation u_t = α∇²u (parabolic), and the wave equation u_tt = c²∇²u (hyperbolic). The method of separation of variables assumes u(x,t) = X(x)T(t) to reduce the PDE to ODEs via eigenvalues. Fourier series solve these eigenvalue problems — the solution is expanded in eigenfunctions of the Laplacian. Duhamel's principle handles forced equations. Green's functions are the fundamental solution — the response to a point source. Characteristics for first-order PDEs (method of characteristics) reduce them to ODEs along curves. For nonlinear PDEs, shock waves can form — solutions develop discontinuities even from smooth initial data (Burgers' equation). The Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness is a Millennium Prize Problem: do 3D Navier-Stokes equations always have smooth solutions?
The three great PDEs — Laplace, heat, wave — govern potential, diffusion, and propagation, with Fourier analysis as the universal tool.

🔄 Dynamical Systems — Long-Term Behavior and Chaos

Order to Chaos
Dynamical Systems — Attractors, Bifurcations, and the Butterfly Effect
A dynamical system is a rule for time evolution — either differential equations (continuous time) or maps (discrete time). The Poincaré map reduces a continuous flow to a discrete map by intersecting trajectories with a surface. An attractor is a set that trajectories converge to — fixed points, limit cycles, tori, or strange attractors (fractal). The Lorenz attractor from Lorenz's 1963 weather model is the iconic strange attractor — it has sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the hallmark of chaos. A system is chaotic if it has a dense orbit, sensitivity to initial conditions, and a dense set of periodic orbits. The logistic map x_{n+1} = rx_n(1−x_n) exhibits period-doubling route to chaos as r increases — the Feigenbaum constant δ = 4.669... is universal. KAM theory (Kolmogorov-Arnold-Moser) says that under small perturbations, most invariant tori persist — explaining why the solar system is stable.
Chaos is deterministic but unpredictable — the butterfly effect is not an error, it's a property of nonlinear systems.

⚡ Advanced Theory — Variational Methods, Integrability, and Modern Directions

From Euler-Lagrange to Solitons
Advanced PDE Theory — Variational Methods, Integrable Systems, and Geometric PDEs
Many PDEs arise from variational principles: solutions are critical points of a functional. The Euler-Lagrange equation gives the condition for a minimizer. The Hamilton-Jacobi equation is the PDE of classical mechanics — the action satisfies it. Solitons are localized waves of the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation that maintain their shape through nonlinear balance — they're integrable via the inverse scattering transform. Geometric PDEs include the minimal surface equation (mean curvature = 0) and the Yamabe problem (find a metric of constant scalar curvature). The Ricci flow ∂g/∂t = −2Ric(g) was used by Perelman to prove the Poincaré conjecture — it evolves metrics toward constant curvature. The Atiyah-Singer index theorem connects the index of elliptic PDEs on a manifold to topological invariants. Morse theory studies topology through critical points of functions, giving deep results in global analysis.
Modern PDE theory reaches into geometry, topology, and mathematical physics — from solitons to the Poincaré conjecture via Ricci flow.

Sturm–Liouville Theory and Spectral Methods for Linear Operators

Sturm–Liouville theory provides the spectral foundation for a vast class of linear differential operators that appear after separation of variables in PDEs. A regular Sturm–Liouville problem on an interval a ≤ x ≤ b takes the form

−(p(x) y′)′ + q(x) y = λ w(x) y,

with boundary conditions α₁ y(a) + α₂ y′(a) = 0 and β₁ y(b) + β₂ y′(b) = 0, where p, q, w are real‑valued, p > 0, w > 0 on (a,b). The operator L y = (1/w)[−(p y′)′ + q y] is self‑adjoint in the weighted L² space with inner product ⟨u,v⟩ = ∫ₐᵇ u v w dx. Self‑adjointness guarantees a real, discrete spectrum λ₁ < λ₂ < … → ∞ and a complete orthogonal set of eigenfunctions {φₙ}. The Rayleigh quotient R[y] = ∫ₐᵇ (p y′² + q y²) dx / ∫ₐᵇ w y² dx yields the min‑max characterisation λₖ = mindim V=k max_{y∈V{0}} R[y].

Singular Sturm–Liouville problems arise when p vanishes or the interval is unbounded; classic examples are Bessel’s equation (p=x, q=ν²/x, w=x) on (0,1) and Legendre’s equation (p=1−x², q=0, w=1) on (−1,1). In both cases the spectrum may be continuous or discrete depending on boundary conditions at the singular endpoints. The completeness of eigenfunctions underlies Fourier–Bessel and Fourier–Legendre expansions, which are the spectral tools for solving the heat and wave equations in cylindrical and spherical geometries.

Spectral methods for numerical PDEs exploit this theory: the solution is expanded in the eigenbasis of the differential operator, turning the PDE into a system of ODEs for the modal coefficients. For periodic domains the Fourier basis diagonalises the Laplacian, giving exponential convergence for smooth data. For non‑periodic problems, Chebyshev or Legendre polynomials provide a similar diagonalisation after a suitable mapping. The key practical advantage is that the stiffness matrix becomes diagonal or block‑diagonal, eliminating the need for large linear solves at each time step.

Weak Solutions and Sobolev Spaces in the Modern Theory of PDEs

The classical requirement that a solution possess continuous derivatives up to the order of the equation is too restrictive for many physical problems. Distribution theory, introduced by Laurent Schwartz, extends differentiation to arbitrary locally integrable functions: the derivative of a distribution T is defined by ⟨∂ᵢT, φ⟩ = −⟨T, ∂ᵢφ⟩ for all test functions φ ∈ C_c^∞(Ω). A weak solution of −Δu = f in Ω with u = 0 on ∂Ω is a function u ∈ H₀¹(Ω) such that ∫_Ω ∇u·∇v dx = ∫_Ω f v dx for every v ∈ H₀¹(Ω). Here H₀¹(Ω) is the closure of C_c^∞(Ω) in the Sobolev norm ‖u‖H¹² = ∫_Ω (|u|² + |∇u|²) dx.

Sobolev spaces Wk,p(Ω) consist of functions whose weak derivatives up to order k belong to L^p(Ω). The Sobolev embedding theorems describe when Wk,p embeds continuously or compactly into spaces of continuous functions or other L^q spaces. For example, if kp > n then Wk,p(Ω) ⊂ Ck−[n/p]−1,γ(Ω̄) (Morrey’s inequality). The Rellich–Kondrachov theorem guarantees compact embedding of H¹(Ω) into L²(Ω) for bounded Lipschitz domains, which is essential for proving existence of eigenvalues for the Laplacian.

The Lax–Milgram lemma provides a general existence and uniqueness result for linear elliptic problems: if a bilinear form a(u,v) is bounded and coercive on a Hilbert space V, then for every continuous linear functional F there exists a unique u ∈ V with a(u,v) = F(v) for all v ∈ V. Coercivity a(v,v) ≥ α‖v‖_V² follows from Poincaré’s inequality for the Dirichlet form a(u,v)=∫∇u·∇v on H₀¹. Regularity theory then upgrades weak solutions to classical ones when the data and domain are smooth: if f ∈ L² and ∂Ω is C², then u ∈ H²(Ω) and ‖u‖H² ≤ C‖f‖L².

Weak Formulation of the Poisson Equation
Derive the weak formulation of −Δu = f in Ω with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition u = 0 on ∂Ω, and identify the function space in which the solution is sought.
Multiply the equation by a test function v ∈ C_c^∞(Ω) and integrate over Ω: ∫_Ω (−Δu) v dx = ∫_Ω f v dx. Integrate by parts using Green’s identity: ∫_Ω ∇u·∇v dx − ∫∂Ω (∂u/∂n) v ds = ∫_Ω f v dx. Since v vanishes on ∂Ω, the boundary term disappears, yielding ∫_Ω ∇u·∇v dx = ∫_Ω f v dx for all v ∈ C_c^∞(Ω). By density, this holds for all v ∈ H₀¹(Ω). The weak solution is u ∈ H₀¹(Ω) satisfying this identity. Existence and uniqueness follow from the Lax–Milgram lemma because the bilinear form a(u,v)=∫∇u·∇v is coercive on H₀¹(Ω) by the Poincaré inequality and bounded by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
Exam Strategy for Functional Analytic PDEs
When a question asks for a weak formulation, first state the test space (usually H₀¹ for Dirichlet problems). Write the integration‑by‑parts step explicitly, show the boundary term vanishes, and then cite Lax–Milgram with the two required properties: boundedness and coercivity. For eigenvalue problems, recall that compact embedding of H¹ into L² yields a discrete spectrum and an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions.

Geometric Mechanics: Hamiltonian Systems, Symplectic Structure, and Integrability

Hamiltonian mechanics reformulates Newtonian dynamics on a symplectic manifold (M, ω), where ω is a closed, non‑degenerate 2‑form. In canonical coordinates (q,p) on the cotangent bundle T*Q, ω = ∑ dp_i ∧ dq_i. A Hamiltonian function H: M → ℝ generates a vector field X_H defined by ω(X_H, ·) = dH. The flow of X_H preserves ω (Liouville’s theorem) and H itself (energy conservation). The Poisson bracket {F,G} = ω(X_F, X_G) = ∑ (∂F/∂q_i ∂G/∂p_i − ∂F/∂p_i ∂G/∂q_i) endows C^∞(M) with a Lie algebra structure; Hamilton’s equations are ẋ = {x, H}.

A system with n degrees of freedom is Liouville‑integrable if there exist n independent, Poisson‑commuting first integrals F₁=H, F₂,…,Fₙ (i.e., {F_i, F_j}=0). The Liouville–Arnold theorem states that the level set of these integrals is a union of invariant n‑tori, on which the motion is quasi‑periodic. Action‑angle variables (I,θ) provide a canonical transformation such that H = H(I) and the equations become İ = 0, θ̇ = ∂H/∂I = ω(I). The frequencies ω(I) determine the dynamics; resonance occurs when k·ω = 0 for some integer vector k ≠ 0.

KAM theory (Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser) shows that under a small Hamiltonian perturbation, most invariant tori with Diophantine frequency vectors survive, while resonant tori break up, creating chaotic layers. This explains the long‑term stability of the solar system despite perturbations. Infinite‑dimensional Hamiltonian PDEs such as the Korteweg–de Vries equation u_t + uxxx + 6u u_x = 0 admit a bi‑Hamiltonian structure and an infinite hierarchy of commuting integrals, making them completely integrable via the inverse scattering transform.

Numerical Approximation of Differential Equations: Finite Difference, Finite Element, and Spectral Methods

Numerical methods replace the continuous problem by a discrete algebraic system. For the heat equation u_t = α uxx on (0,1) with Dirichlet conditions, a uniform grid x_j = jΔx, t^n = nΔt leads to the explicit forward‑Euler scheme (u_jn+1 − u_j^n)/Δt = α (uj+1^n − 2u_j^n + uj-1^n)/Δx². Consistency requires the truncation error to vanish as Δt,Δx → 0; stability demands that errors do not amplify, which for this scheme is the CFL condition α Δt/Δx² ≤ 1/2. The Lax equivalence theorem states that for a well‑posed linear problem, consistency plus stability implies convergence.

The finite element method (FEM) starts from the weak formulation. The domain Ω is partitioned into elements (triangles, tetrahedra). On each element, the solution is approximated by piecewise polynomials (e.g., continuous P1 Lagrange basis). The global stiffness matrix Kij = ∫_Ω ∇φ_i·∇φ_j dx and load vector F_i = ∫_Ω f φ_i dx are assembled. The discrete problem K u = F is solved for the nodal values. Céa’s lemma gives the error estimate ‖u − u_h‖H¹ ≤ C infv_h∈V_h ‖u − v_h‖H¹, leading to optimal order O(h) convergence in H¹ for linear elements if u ∈ H².

Spectral methods use global basis functions (Fourier series for periodic domains, Chebyshev or Legendre polynomials for non‑periodic). The solution is represented as u_N(x) = ∑k=0^N a_k φ_k(x). Collocation (pseudospectral) evaluates the residual at Gauss–Lobatto nodes, yielding a dense but highly accurate differentiation matrix. For analytic solutions, the error decays exponentially with N (spectral accuracy). The main trade‑off is the dense linear algebra versus the sparse matrices of FEM; for time‑dependent problems, implicit‑explicit (IMEX) schemes treat the stiff linear part implicitly and the nonlinear part explicitly.

Stochastic Differential Equations: Itô Calculus, Fokker–Planck Equation, and Applications

A stochastic differential equation (SDE) driven by a Wiener process W_t reads dX_t = b(X_t) dt + σ(X_t) dW_t, where b is the drift and σ the diffusion coefficient. The Itô integral ∫0^t σ(X_s) dW_s is defined as the L² limit of sums σ(Xt_i)(Wti+1−Wt_i) with the integrand evaluated at the left endpoint. This non‑anticipative choice leads to the Itô formula: for a C² function f, df(X_t) = f′(X_t) dX_t + ½ f″(X_t) σ²(X_t) dt. The extra second‑order term distinguishes Itô calculus from ordinary calculus.

Under Lipschitz and linear growth conditions on b and σ, the SDE has a unique strong solution adapted to the filtration of W_t. The probability density p(t,x) of X_t satisfies the Fokker–Planck (forward Kolmogorov) equation ∂_t p = −∂_x (b p) + ½ ∂xx (σ² p). For time‑independent coefficients, a stationary density p_∞(x) ∝ exp(∫x 2b(y)/σ²(y) dy) / σ²(x) exists if the integral converges. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process dX_t = −θ X_t dt + σ dW_t has Gaussian stationary distribution N(0, σ²/(2θ)).

Applications abound: in finance, the Black–Scholes model assumes geometric Brownian motion dS_t = μ S_t dt + σ S_t dW_t, leading to the heat equation after a logarithmic change of variable. In physics, the Langevin equation m dV_t = −γ V_t dt + σ dW_t describes a particle in a heat bath; its position obeys a second‑order SDE. In biology, stochastic population models incorporate demographic noise via diffusion terms. Numerical simulation uses the Euler–Maruyama scheme Xn+1 = X_n + b(X_n)Δt + σ(X_n)ΔW_n, which converges with strong order ½ and weak order 1.

  • Assuming every Sturm–Liouville problem has a discrete spectrum without checking endpoint classification.
  • Neglecting the coercivity condition when applying Lax–Milgram, leading to false existence claims.
  • Mixing Itô and Stratonovich integrals in SDEs without the correction term ½ σ σ′ dt.
  • Applying the CFL condition derived for explicit finite differences to implicit or spectral schemes where it does not apply.
  • Overlooking the need for Diophantine frequency conditions in KAM theory and concluding all tori survive perturbation.
  • Treating the Fokker–Planck equation as a standard PDE without verifying the diffusion matrix is positive definite.

Bifurcation Theory and Normal Forms

Bifurcation theory studies qualitative changes in the solution structure of a dynamical system as parameters vary. The simplest codimension‑one bifurcations in flows on the plane are the saddle‑node, transcritical, pitchfork, and Hopf bifurcations. Each can be brought to a normal form by a near‑identity change of coordinates that removes non‑resonant nonlinear terms. For a scalar equation ẋ = μ + x² the saddle‑node normal form shows the creation or annihilation of two equilibria when the parameter μ passes through zero. The transcritical normal form ẋ = μx − x² exhibits an exchange of stability between two intersecting branches. The pitchfork normal form ẋ = μx − x³ (supercritical) or ẋ = μx + x³ (subcritical) captures symmetry‑breaking when the vector field is odd. The Hopf bifurcation normal form in polar coordinates ṙ = μr − r³, θ̇ = ω describes the birth of a limit cycle from an equilibrium as a pair of complex conjugate eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis. Center‑manifold reduction justifies restricting the analysis to the critical eigenspace, and the Lyapunov coefficient computed from the cubic terms determines criticality. Higher‑codimension bifurcations such as Bogdanov–Takens (double zero eigenvalue) and cusp (degenerate saddle‑node) require two unfolding parameters and lead to rich bifurcation diagrams with homoclinic loops and curves of Hopf points. In applications, the normal‑form coefficients are obtained by projecting the original vector field onto the adjoint eigenvectors, a computation that appears frequently in examination problems on coupled oscillators, chemical reactors, and population models.

Center Manifold Theory and Dimension Reduction

Center manifold theory provides a rigorous framework for reducing the dimension of a dynamical system near a non‑hyperbolic equilibrium. Consider ẋ = Ax + f(x) with f(0)=0, Df(0)=0 and the spectrum of A split into stable (Re λ < 0), unstable (Re λ > 0), and center (Re λ = 0) parts. The center manifold Wᶜ is an invariant manifold tangent to the center eigenspace Eᶜ at the origin. It is not unique, but any two center manifolds have the same Taylor expansion to any finite order. The flow on Wᶜ governs the local bifurcation behavior; the stable and unstable manifolds merely attract or repel trajectories exponentially fast. To compute an approximation, write Wᶜ as a graph y = h(x) where x ∈ Eᶜ, y ∈ Eˢ ⊕ Eᵘ. Substituting into the invariance equation Dh(x)[Aᶜx + fᶜ(x, h(x))] = Aˢh(x) + fˢ(x, h(x)) and matching coefficients yields the quadratic and cubic terms of h. The reduced system ẋ = Aᶜx + fᶜ(x, h(x)) then captures the essential dynamics. A classic example is the Lorenz system at the onset of convection: the linearization has a zero eigenvalue and a pair of stable eigenvalues; the two‑dimensional center manifold yields the well‑known Lorenz equations after a suitable projection. In examinations, candidates are often asked to compute the quadratic approximation of the center manifold for a given three‑dimensional system and to deduce the type of bifurcation from the reduced scalar equation.

Technique: Center‑Manifold Computation
Given the system ẋ = y, ẏ = −x + μy − x²y, ż = −z + x², find the quadratic approximation of the center manifold at the origin for μ = 0 and write the reduced dynamics on it.
The linear part has eigenvalues 0, ±i for the (x,y) block and −1 for z. The center eigenspace is spanned by (1,0,0) and (0,1,0). Write the center manifold as z = h(x,y) = a x² + b xy + c y² + higher order. Invariance: Dh·(ẋ,ẏ) = ż. At μ=0, ẋ = y, ẏ = −x − x²y. Compute Dh = (2a x + b y, b x + 2c y). Left side: (2a x + b y)y + (b x + 2c y)(−x − x²y). Right side: −(a x² + b xy + c y²) + x². Match quadratic terms: coefficients of x²: −b = −a + 1 → a = 1 + b. Coefficients of xy: 2a − b = −b → a = 0. Hence b = −1, a = 0. Coefficients of y²: 2c = −c → c = 0. Thus h(x,y) = −xy + O(3). Reduced system on center manifold: ẋ = y, ẏ = −x − x²y + O(4). The cubic term −x²y indicates a supercritical Hopf bifurcation when μ becomes positive.
Exam‑day strategy
When a center‑manifold question appears, first identify the center eigenspace from the Jacobian eigenvalues. Write the most general quadratic (or cubic) ansatz for the manifold graph, substitute into the invariance condition, and equate coefficients systematically. Do not attempt to solve the full nonlinear system; the reduced low‑dimensional dynamics is what the examiner expects.

Delay Differential Equations: Stability and Oscillations

Delay differential equations (DDEs) incorporate past states of the system, leading to infinite‑dimensional phase spaces even for a single scalar equation. The prototypical linear DDE ẋ(t) = −a x(t) + b x(t−τ) with τ > 0 has a characteristic equation λ + a − b e−λτ = 0. The roots λ determine stability: the trivial solution is asymptotically stable iff all roots satisfy Re λ < 0. The crossing of roots through the imaginary axis as τ varies produces Hopf bifurcations and sustained oscillations. The critical delay τ₀ for the first Hopf bifurcation satisfies ω = b sin(ω τ₀) and a = b cos(ω τ₀) with ω > 0, yielding τ₀ = (1/ω) arccos(a/b) provided |a| < |b|. For the nonlinear Mackey–Glass equation ẋ(t) = β x(t−τ) / (1 + x(t−τ)ⁿ) − γ x(t) the same linear analysis predicts the onset of periodic and chaotic dynamics as τ increases. Numerical continuation tools such as DDE‑Biftool compute bifurcation diagrams of periodic orbits and detect secondary bifurcations (period‑doubling, torus). In examinations, typical tasks include deriving the characteristic equation, applying the argument principle to count unstable roots, and using the Lambert‑W function to express roots of simple scalar DDEs. The infinite‑dimensional nature also implies that the principle of linearized stability holds under a spectral gap condition, a subtle point often tested.

Fractional Differential Equations: Memory Effects and Anomalous Diffusion

Fractional differential equations replace integer‑order derivatives by derivatives of non‑integer order α ∈ (0,1) or α ∈ (1,2), capturing power‑law memory kernels. The Caputo derivative ⁰C Dₜ^α u(t) = (1/Γ(1−α)) ∫₀ᵗ (t−s)−α u′(s) ds is preferred for initial‑value problems because it allows standard initial conditions u(0)=u₀. The linear fractional relaxation equation ⁰C Dₜ^α u(t) = −λ u(t) has the solution u(t) = u₀ E_α(−λ t^α) where E_α is the Mittag‑Leffler function, which decays like a stretched exponential for short times and a power law t−α for long times. The fractional diffusion equation ∂ₜ^α u = D ∂ₓ² u models sub‑diffusion with mean‑square displacement ⟨x²⟩ ∼ t^α. For α ∈ (1,2) the time‑fractional wave equation interpolates between diffusion and wave propagation, exhibiting a finite propagation speed only in the limit α→2. Numerical methods rely on Grünwald–Letnikov discretizations or L1 schemes for the Caputo derivative, with convergence order O(Δt2−α) for the L1 method. Stability analysis uses the discrete energy method and the fact that the fractional derivative operator is positive definite in a suitable weighted inner product. Examination questions often ask to derive the Laplace transform of the Caputo derivative, to solve a fractional relaxation problem via Laplace inversion, or to discuss the physical interpretation of the Mittag‑Leffler function as a generalization of the exponential.

Optimal Control and the Pontryagin Maximum Principle

Optimal control theory seeks a control function u(t) ∈ U ⊂ ℝᵐ that minimizes a cost functional J = φ(x(T)) + ∫₀ᵀ L(x(t), u(t), t) dt subject to the dynamics ẋ = f(x, u, t), x(0)=x₀. The Pontryagin Maximum Principle (PMP) provides necessary conditions: there exists an absolutely continuous adjoint vector p(t) ∈ ℝⁿ such that the Hamiltonian H(x, u, p, t) = p·f(x, u, t) − L(x, u, t) satisfies ṗ = −∂H/∂x, ẋ = ∂H/∂p, and the maximization condition H(x*(t), u*(t), p(t), t) = maxu∈U H(x*(t), u, p(t), t) almost everywhere. The transversality condition p(T) = ∂φ/∂x (x(T)) closes the two‑point boundary value problem. For linear‑quadratic problems (LQR) with f = Ax + Bu, L = ½(xᵀQx + uᵀRu), the optimal control is a linear state feedback u* = −R⁻¹BᵀPx where P solves the algebraic Riccati equation. Bang‑bang controls arise when the Hamiltonian is linear in u and the control set is a convex polytope; the switching function σ(t) = ∂H/∂u determines the switching times. Singular arcs occur when σ(t) ≡ 0 on an interval, requiring higher‑order conditions (generalized Legendre‑Clebsch). In competitive examinations, candidates must be able to formulate the Hamiltonian, write the adjoint equations, identify bang‑bang or singular structures, and solve the resulting boundary‑value problem for simple scalar systems such as the minimum‑time problem for a double integrator.

  • Neglecting the non‑uniqueness of the center manifold and using a particular approximation to draw global conclusions.
  • Applying the standard Hopf bifurcation formula to delay equations without checking the transversality condition for the characteristic roots.
  • Treating the Caputo derivative as a Riemann–Liouville derivative when initial conditions are given in the usual sense.
  • Forgetting the transversality condition p(T) = ∂φ/∂x in the Pontryagin Maximum Principle and leaving the adjoint terminal value unspecified.
  • Assuming every optimal control is continuous; missing bang‑bang or singular arcs when the Hamiltonian is linear in the control.
  • Using the Lambert‑W function for DDE characteristic roots without verifying the branch selection corresponds to the physical stability region.

Conservation Laws, Shock Waves, and Entropy Solutions for Hyperbolic PDEs

First‑order quasilinear systems of the form ∂ₜU + ∂ₓF(U) = 0 with U ∈ ℝᵐ and flux F : ℝᵐ → ℝᵐ arise in gas dynamics, shallow water, and traffic flow. When the Jacobian DF(U) has real eigenvalues and a complete set of eigenvectors the system is strictly hyperbolic. Smooth solutions break down in finite time because characteristics intersect, producing discontinuities called shocks. The integral form ∫(U φₜ + F(U) φₓ) dx dt = 0 for all test functions φ ∈ C_c^∞ defines a weak solution, but weak solutions are not unique. The physically relevant one is selected by an entropy condition: there exists a convex entropy function η(U) with entropy flux q(U) satisfying ∇η·DF = ∇q such that ∂ₜη(U) + ∂ₓq(U) ≤ 0 in the sense of distributions. For scalar conservation laws (m = 1) the Lax entropy condition (characteristics enter the shock) is equivalent to the Oleĭnik E‑condition and guarantees uniqueness. The Riemann problem with piecewise constant initial data U(x,0) = U_L for x < 0, U_R for x > 0 can be solved exactly by a sequence of rarefaction waves, shocks, and contact discontinuities; the solution provides the building block for Godunov‑type finite volume schemes. In systems, the Lax admissibility criteria require that the shock speed s satisfies λ_k(U_L) > s > λ_k(U_R) for a genuinely nonlinear k‑field, while for linearly degenerate fields s = λ_k(U_L) = λ_k(U_R) (contact discontinuity). The Glimm scheme and front‑tracking method construct global entropy solutions for small total variation data. Modern theory extends to systems with relaxation (e.g. Jin‑Xin model) and to multi‑dimensional conservation laws where the entropy condition must be supplemented by kinetic formulations or measure‑valued solutions. Examination questions frequently ask to derive the Rankine‑Hugoniot jump conditions [F(U)] = s[U], to verify the Lax entropy inequalities for a given shock, or to sketch the Riemann solution for the p‑system (isentropic gas dynamics) with pressure p(ρ) = ρ^γ.

Reaction–Diffusion Systems, Turing Instability, and Pattern Formation

Reaction–diffusion equations ∂ₜu = DΔu + f(u) with u ∈ ℝⁿ, D = diag(d₁,…,dₙ) positive, and smooth kinetics f model chemical reactions, morphogenesis, and ecological invasions. A spatially homogeneous steady state u* satisfies f(u*) = 0. Linearising about u* gives ∂ₜv = DΔv + J v where J = Df(u*). Expanding v in Fourier modes eik·x yields the dispersion relation det(λI + k²D − J) = 0. For a Turing instability the homogeneous state must be stable to homogeneous perturbations (all eigenvalues of J have negative real part) but unstable to some non‑zero wave number k ≠ 0. This requires that the diffusion matrix D breaks the stability: typically one species (activator) diffuses slowly while another (inhibitor) diffuses rapidly (d_inhibitor ≫ d_activator). The necessary algebraic conditions for a two‑component system are tr(J) < 0, det(J) > 0, d₁J₂₂ + d₂J₁₁ > 0, and (d₁J₂₂ + d₂J₁₁)² > 4 d₁d₂ det(J). The critical wave number k_c satisfies k_c² = (d₁J₂₂ + d₂J₁₁)/(2 d₁d₂). Near onset the pattern amplitude obeys a Ginzburg–Landau equation, and the selected pattern (stripes, spots, hexagons) depends on nonlinear mode interactions. The Schnakenberg and Gierer–Meinhardt models are classic examples. In examinations, candidates must be able to compute the Jacobian, write the dispersion relation, derive the Turing conditions, and discuss how domain size and boundary conditions (Neumann vs periodic) quantise admissible wave numbers and select the observed pattern.

Worked Example: Turing Conditions for a Two‑Species Model
Consider the activator–inhibitor kinetics f₁ = a − u + u²v, f₂ = b − u²v with a,b > 0 and diffusion coefficients d₁, d₂. Find the homogeneous steady state, compute the Jacobian, and derive the inequalities on d₁, d₂ for a Turing instability.
The steady state solves a − u + u²v = 0, b − u²v = 0 ⇒ u* = a + b, v* = b/(a+b)². The Jacobian at (u*,v*) is J = [[−1 + 2u*v*, (u*)²], [−2u*v*, −(u*)²]] = [[−1 + 2b/(a+b), (a+b)²], [−2b/(a+b), −(a+b)²]]. Trace tr(J) = −1 + 2b/(a+b) − (a+b)². For stability without diffusion we need tr(J) < 0 and det(J) > 0. det(J) = (−1 + 2b/(a+b))(−(a+b)²) + 2b(a+b) = (a+b)² − 2b(a+b) + 2b(a+b) = (a+b)² > 0. Hence det(J) > 0 automatically. The Turing conditions require d₁J₂₂ + d₂J₁₁ > 0 and (d₁J₂₂ + d₂J₁₁)² > 4 d₁d₂ det(J). Substituting J₁₁ = −1 + 2b/(a+b), J₂₂ = −(a+b)² gives d₁(−(a+b)²) + d₂(−1 + 2b/(a+b)) > 0. Since d₁,d₂ > 0 this forces −1 + 2b/(a+b) > 0 ⇒ 2b > a+b ⇒ b > a. With b > a the first inequality can be satisfied by choosing d₂ sufficiently larger than d₁. The second inequality then determines the precise ratio d₂/d₁ needed for instability. The critical wave number is k_c² = (d₁J₂₂ + d₂J₁₁)/(2 d₁d₂).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for Turing conditions, first verify homogeneous stability (trace < 0, determinant > 0). Then write the two diffusion‑driven inequalities explicitly in terms of the Jacobian entries and diffusion coefficients. Do not forget to state the physical requirement d_inhibitor ≫ d_activator; examiners often award a mark for this qualitative observation even if the algebra is incomplete.

Symmetry Reduction and Lie Group Methods for Differential Equations

Lie symmetry analysis provides a systematic way to find exact solutions, reduce order, and construct conservation laws for ODEs and PDEs. A one‑parameter Lie group of point transformations x̄ = x + ε ξ(x,u) + O(ε²), ū = u + ε η(x,u) + O(ε²) leaves a differential equation Δ(x,u,∂u,…) = 0 invariant if its infinitesimal generator X = ξ∂ₓ + η∂ᵤ satisfies the invariance condition pr^(n)X(Δ)|Δ=0 = 0, where pr^(n)X is the n‑th prolongation. Solving the resulting determining equations (linear homogeneous PDEs for ξ, η) yields the symmetry algebra. For an ODE of order n, an n‑dimensional solvable symmetry algebra allows reduction to quadratures. For PDEs, a symmetry generator yields similarity variables that reduce the number of independent variables; e.g. the scaling symmetry of the heat equation ∂ₜu = ∂ₓₓu gives the similarity variable ζ = x/√t and reduces the PDE to an ODE for the profile. Noether's theorem connects variational symmetries of a Lagrangian to conservation laws: if X is a variational symmetry, the conserved current is C = ξL + (η − ξ uₓ)∂L/∂uₓ − … . In practice, one computes the symmetry algebra using computer algebra (e.g. Maple's DEtools, Mathematica's Sym) and then constructs optimal systems of subalgebras to classify inequivalent reductions. Examination tasks often include finding the Lie point symmetries of a given second‑order ODE (e.g. y'' + f(x)y' + g(x)y = 0), determining the similarity variables for a PDE with a known scaling symmetry, or applying Noether's theorem to derive the energy conservation law for the wave equation from time‑translation invariance.

Infinite‑Dimensional Dynamical Systems: Global Attractors and Inertial Manifolds

Dissipative PDEs such as the 2D Navier–Stokes equations, reaction–diffusion systems, and the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation generate semigroups S(t) on a Hilbert space H (e.g. L² or H¹). A global attractor 𝒜 ⊂ H is a compact, invariant set that attracts all bounded sets: dist_H(S(t)B, 𝒜) → 0 as t → ∞ for every bounded B ⊂ H. Existence of a global attractor follows from the existence of a bounded absorbing set and asymptotic compactness (or the smoothing property S(t): H → V compact for t > 0). The fractal dimension of 𝒜 provides an upper bound on the number of degrees of freedom; for the 2D Navier–Stokes equations dim_f(𝒜) ≤ c Gr²/³ where Gr is the Grashof number. An inertial manifold ℳ is a finite‑dimensional, Lipschitz, positively invariant manifold that attracts all trajectories exponentially fast and contains the global attractor. Its existence requires a spectral gap condition on the linear operator A (e.g. λN+1 − λ_N > C Lip(f) where λ_k are eigenvalues of A and f is the nonlinearity). When an inertial manifold exists, the infinite‑dimensional dynamics is equivalent to an ODE on ℳ (the inertial form). Approximate inertial manifolds (AIM) are constructed by truncating the high modes and expressing them as a function of the low modes via the graph transform. In competitive examinations, candidates may be asked to prove the existence of an absorbing ball for the 2D Navier–Stokes equations using the energy inequality, to estimate the fractal dimension via the Lyapunov exponent method, or to state the spectral gap condition and explain why it fails for the 3D Navier–Stokes equations.

Inverse Problems and Parameter Identification for Differential Equations

Inverse problems seek to determine unknown coefficients, initial data, or source terms from partial observations of the solution. A typical formulation: given the forward map F: p ↦ u(p) where u solves a PDE with parameter p ∈ P (a Banach space), and noisy data y^δ = u(p†) + η with ‖η‖ ≤ δ, find p†. The problem is ill‑posed in the sense of Hadamard: solutions may not exist, may not be unique, or may not depend continuously on data. Regularisation replaces the ill‑posed problem by a family of well‑posed problems; Tikhonov regularisation minimises J_α(p) = ‖F(p) − y^δ‖²_Y + α‖p − p₀‖²_P with α > 0. Under a source condition p† − p₀ ∈ range(F'(p†)*) one obtains convergence rates ‖p_α^δ − p†‖ = O(δ2/3) for an a priori choice α ∼ δ. For nonlinear problems the iterative regularisation (Landweber, Gauss–Newton) is used. In parameter identification for ODEs (e.g. estimating kinetic rates from concentration time series) the adjoint method computes gradients efficiently: the gradient of the least‑squares cost with respect to parameters is obtained by solving the forward ODE and the adjoint ODE backward in time. For PDE‑constrained optimisation the Lagrangian L(u,p,λ) = J(u,p) + ⟨λ, e(u,p)⟩ yields the optimality system: state equation e(u,p)=0, adjoint equation e_u^*(u,p)λ = −J_u, and gradient condition J_p + ⟨λ, e_p⟩ = 0. Examination questions often ask to formulate the Tikhonov functional for a specific inverse heat conduction problem, to derive the adjoint equation for a reaction–diffusion model, or to discuss the choice of the regularisation parameter via the discrepancy principle ‖F(p_α^δ) − y^δ‖ = τδ.

Homogenization Theory for Periodic and Random Media

Homogenization addresses the effective behavior of differential equations with rapidly oscillating coefficients. Consider the elliptic problem −∇·(A(x/ε)∇uε) = f in a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝn with Dirichlet boundary data, where A(y) is Y‑periodic, uniformly elliptic and bounded. As ε → 0 the solutions uε converge weakly in H1(Ω) to a limit u0 that solves the homogenized equation −∇·(Ahom∇u0) = f with the same boundary data. The constant effective matrix Ahom is given by Ahomij = ∫Y A(y)(ei + ∇yχi(y))·(ej + ∇yχj(y)) dy, where χi are the Y‑periodic correctors solving −∇y·(A(y)(ei + ∇yχi)) = 0 in Y with zero mean. The method extends to random stationary ergodic coefficients via the subadditive ergodic theorem; the effective matrix is then deterministic almost surely. Quantitative homogenization provides rates of convergence in terms of ε, often using the spectral gap inequality or the quantitative two‑scale expansion. In competitive examinations, candidates may be asked to derive the cell problem for a given periodic conductivity, to prove the weak convergence of uε using the energy method, or to explain why the corrector χi belongs to H1per(Y) and has zero mean.

Viscosity Solutions and Hamilton–Jacobi Equations

First‑order Hamilton–Jacobi equations ut + H(x,∇u) = 0 with convex Hamiltonian H often develop discontinuities in the gradient even for smooth initial data, so classical solutions cease to exist globally. The viscosity solution framework, introduced by Crandall and Lions, selects the unique physically relevant weak solution by a comparison principle. A continuous function u is a viscosity subsolution if for every smooth test function φ touching u from above at (x0,t0) one has φt + H(x0,∇φ) ≤ 0; supersolution is defined analogously with the opposite inequality. The comparison principle states that if u is a bounded subsolution and v a bounded supersolution with u ≤ v on the parabolic boundary, then u ≤ v everywhere. Uniqueness follows immediately. Existence is obtained via Perron's method or the vanishing viscosity approximation uε solving uεt + H(x,∇uε) = εΔuε. The theory extends to second‑order degenerate elliptic equations and to Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations arising in stochastic control. In examinations, typical tasks include verifying the viscosity sub/supersolution property for a candidate function, applying the comparison principle to prove uniqueness for a given Hamiltonian, or deriving the Hopf–Lax formula for the convex Hamiltonian H(p) = ½|p|2.

Geometric Singular Perturbation Theory and Fenichel's Theorems

Systems of the form εẋ = f(x,y,ε), ẏ = g(x,y,ε) with 0 < ε ≪ 1 exhibit a separation of time scales. The critical manifold C0 = {(x,y) : f(x,y,0)=0} is normally hyperbolic if the linearization Dxf has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis. Fenichel's persistence theorem guarantees that for sufficiently small ε there exists a locally invariant slow manifold Cε that is O(ε) close to C0 and diffeomorphic to it. The flow on Cε is governed by the reduced (slow) system obtained by setting ε=0 and differentiating the constraint f(x,y,0)=0, yielding the differential‑algebraic system 0 = f(x,y,0), ẏ = g(x,y,0). Conversely, the fast subsystem is obtained by rescaling time τ = t/ε and setting ε=0: x' = f(x,y,0), y' = 0. Fenichel's theory also provides invariant foliations of stable and unstable fibers (strong stable/unstable manifolds) that are O(ε) perturbations of the linearized fibers of C0. These geometric structures underlie the analysis of relaxation oscillations, canard phenomena, and the construction of singularly perturbed heteroclinic orbits. In examinations, candidates may be asked to verify normal hyperbolicity for a given critical manifold, to write the reduced slow system and the layer (fast) system, or to explain how the exchange lemma uses the invariant foliations to track trajectories near a loss of normal hyperbolicity.

Mean Field Games and McKean–Vlasov Dynamics

Mean field games (MFG) describe the limit of N‑player stochastic differential games as N → ∞, where each agent interacts with the empirical distribution of the population. The limiting system couples a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for the value function u(t,x) with a Fokker–Planck (Kolmogorov forward) equation for the density m(t,x): −ut − νΔu + H(x,∇u) = F(x,m), mt − νΔm − ∇·(m Hp(x,∇u)) = 0, with terminal condition u(T,x)=G(x,m(T)) and initial condition m(0)=m0. Here H is the Hamiltonian, F the running cost depending on the distribution, and G the terminal cost. The McKean–Vlasov equation arises when the drift of a particle depends on the law of the particle itself: dXt = b(Xt,Law(Xt)) dt + σ dWt. Its nonlinear Fokker–Planck equation ∂tm = −∇·(b(x,m)m) + ½σ2Δm describes the evolution of the law. Existence and uniqueness for MFG systems are typically proved via fixed‑point arguments (Schauder, Banach) or monotonicity methods (Lasry–Lions). The master equation, a PDE on the space of probability measures, encodes the value function as a function of the initial distribution and provides a framework for propagation of chaos. In competitive settings, questions may ask to derive the MFG system from a discrete N‑player game with symmetric costs, to verify the monotonicity condition ∫ (F(x,m)−F(x,m'))(m−m') dx ≥ 0, or to explain the difference between the MFG system and the McKean–Vlasov equation.

Rough Paths Theory and Stochastic Differential Equations Driven by Rough Signals

Classical Itô calculus requires the driving noise to be a semimartingale; however, many natural signals (e.g., fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H ≠ ½) are not semimartingales. Rough paths theory, developed by Lyons, provides a pathwise framework for differential equations driven by Hölder continuous paths of regularity α ∈ (⅓,½]. A rough path of order p = ⌊1/α⌋ is a pair X = (X, 𝕏) where X is the path and 𝕏 is its iterated integral (the Lévy area) satisfying Chen's relation 𝕏s,t = 𝕏s,u + 𝕏u,t + Xs,u ⊗ Xu,t. The solution to the rough differential equation dY = V(Y) dX is defined as the limit of solutions to smooth approximations, and the Itô–Lyons map X ↦ Y is locally Lipschitz in the p‑variation or α‑Hölder rough path metric. This yields a deterministic well‑posedness theory that applies to fractional Brownian motion (H > ¼), Gaussian processes with sufficient regularity, and deterministic rough signals. The theory extends to branched rough paths for higher order expansions and to rough partial differential equations via the Feynman–Kac representation. In examinations, typical tasks include verifying Chen's relation for a given candidate area, computing the rough integral ∫ Y dX for a simple vector field V, or explaining why the condition α > ⅓ is necessary for the continuity of the Itô–Lyons map.

Spectral Theory of Non‑Selfadjoint Operators and Pseudospectra

Classical spectral theory for self‑adjoint operators guarantees a real spectrum and an orthogonal basis of eigenvectors. For non‑selfadjoint operators arising in fluid dynamics, non‑normal matrices, and damped wave equations, the spectrum alone can be misleading: eigenvalues may be highly sensitive to perturbations, and transient growth can occur even when all eigenvalues lie in the left half‑plane. The ε‑pseudospectrum Λε(A) = {z ∈ ℂ : ‖(zI − A)−1‖ > ε−1} captures this sensitivity. If the resolvent norm is large, tiny perturbations of size ε can move eigenvalues by O(1). In hydrodynamic stability, the Orr–Sommerfeld operator is non‑normal; its pseudospectra explain subcritical transition to turbulence at Reynolds numbers far below the linear eigenvalue threshold. Numerical computation of pseudospectra uses the singular value decomposition of (zI − A) on a grid in the complex plane. The Kreiss matrix theorem links the ε‑pseudospectral abscissa to the maximal transient amplification of the semigroup etA. For differential operators, one studies the pseudospectrum of the discretised matrix or directly the resolvent estimate ‖(λ − L)−1‖L2→L2. Applications include stability of shear flows, analysis of non‑normal quantum Hamiltonians, and design of robust feedback controllers where the H∞ norm is the supremum of the resolvent on the imaginary axis.

Nonlinear Semigroups and Maximal Monotone Operators in Evolution Equations

The theory of nonlinear semigroups extends the linear Hille–Yosida theorem to operators that are not necessarily linear but satisfy monotonicity and maximality conditions. Let X be a real Banach space. An operator A : D(A) ⊂ X → 2X is monotone if ⟨u − v, u* − v*⟩ ≥ 0 for all (u,u*), (v,v*) ∈ A. It is maximal monotone if it has no proper monotone extension. The Crandall–Liggett theorem states that for a maximal monotone operator A, the implicit Euler scheme (I + (t/n)A)−nu0 converges strongly to a limit S(t)u0 which forms a nonlinear contraction semigroup on X. This semigroup provides the unique mild solution of the abstract Cauchy problem du/dt + Au ∋ 0, u(0)=u0. Important examples include the subdifferential ∂φ of a proper, convex, lower‑semicontinuous functional φ (giving gradient flows), the p‑Laplacian Δpu = div(|∇u|p−2∇u) on L2(Ω), and the porous medium operator u ↦ Δ(um) for m>1. The theory also covers accretive operators in Banach spaces and the associated nonlinear resolvent Jλ = (I + λA)−1. Applications range from nonlinear heat equations and filtration problems to optimal transport where the Wasserstein gradient flow of the entropy functional yields the heat equation as a gradient flow in the space of probability measures.

  • Apply Crandall–Liggett to obtain the semigroup S(t).

Kinetic Equations: Boltzmann, Vlasov, and Hydrodynamic Limits

Kinetic equations describe the statistical evolution of a large number of particles. The Boltzmann equation ∂tf + v·∇xf = Q(f,f) models rarefied gases where Q is the binary collision operator satisfying conservation of mass, momentum, energy and the H‑theorem (entropy dissipation). The Vlasov equation ∂tf + v·∇xf + F·∇vf = 0 describes collisionless plasmas or gravitating systems with self‑consistent force F = −∇xφ, φ solving Poisson’s equation. Hydrodynamic limits derive macroscopic PDEs (Euler, Navier–Stokes) from kinetic models via scaling. In the incompressible Navier–Stokes limit, one introduces the Knudsen number ε, rescales time and space, and performs a Chapman–Enskog expansion f = M + εf1 + … where M is the local Maxwellian. The solvability condition (orthogonality to collision invariants) yields the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations with viscosity determined by the linearised collision operator. For the Vlasov–Poisson system, the mean‑field limit N→∞ of N‑particle Newtonian dynamics is rigorously justified using Dobrushin’s stability estimate or the BBGKY hierarchy. Landau damping, the decay of the electric field in a stable homogeneous plasma, is a celebrated phenomenon proved by Mouhot and Villani using analytic regularity and Penrose stability condition. Numerical methods include particle‑in‑cell (PIC) for Vlasov and discrete velocity models (DVM) or spectral methods for Boltzmann.

Control and Observability for Distributed Parameter Systems

Control theory for PDEs addresses steering the state of an infinite‑dimensional system by boundary or distributed actuators and reconstructing the state from partial measurements. For a linear system ∂ty = Ay + Bu, y(0)=y0 on a Hilbert space H, exact controllability in time T means the reachable set equals H. The Hilbert Uniqueness Method (HUM) of Lions reduces exact controllability to an observability inequality for the adjoint system: ∃C>0 such that ‖φ(0)‖2 ≤ C∫0T‖B*φ(t)‖2 dt for all solutions of ∂tφ = A*φ. For the wave equation on a bounded domain with Dirichlet boundary control, observability holds if the Geometric Control Condition (GCC) is satisfied: every ray of geometric optics enters the control region within time T. For the heat equation, null controllability holds in any time T>0 with distributed control, but the cost blows up as T→0 like exp(C/T). The dual notion of observability is crucial for state estimation; a Luenberger observer ∂tŷ = Aŷ + Bu + L(Cŷ − z) converges exponentially if (A,C) is detectable. For nonlinear systems, the return method and power series expansions (Coron) construct local exact controllability around a reference trajectory. Applications include flow control for drag reduction, quantum control of Schrödinger equations, and optimal placement of sensors in structural health monitoring.

Observability Inequality Checklist

  • Identify the adjoint operator A* and observation operator B*.
  • Verify the Geometric Control Condition for wave‑type equations.
  • For parabolic equations, use Carleman estimates to prove observability.
  • Compute the control cost scaling with respect to time horizon T.

Data‑Driven Discovery of Differential Equations: Sparse Identification and Neural Operators

Modern scientific machine learning aims to infer governing differential equations from noisy, high‑dimensional data. The Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) algorithm builds a library Θ(X) of candidate functions (polynomials, trigonometric, rational) and solves a sparse regression problem minΞ ‖Ẋ − Θ(X)Ξ‖F + λ‖Ξ‖1 to select a parsimonious model. Convergence guarantees require the library to contain the true dynamics and the data to satisfy a restricted isometry property. For PDEs, the library includes spatial derivatives computed via finite differences or spectral differentiation. Physics‑informed neural networks (PINNs) embed the PDE residual into the loss function: L = ‖uθ − udata‖2 + λ‖∂tuθ + N(uθ)‖2. Neural operators (DeepONet, Fourier Neural Operator) learn the solution operator G: a ↦ u mapping parameters or initial conditions to the solution field, enabling real‑time inference for parametric PDEs. The universal approximation theorem for operators states that a neural operator with sufficient width can approximate any continuous nonlinear operator between Banach spaces. Challenges include generalization to out‑of‑distribution parameters, enforcement of conservation laws, and handling multiscale phenomena. Recent work combines SINDy with weak formulations to discover PDEs from coarse, noisy measurements without requiring high‑order derivative estimates.

Microlocal Analysis and Propagation of Singularities

Microlocal analysis refines classical Fourier analysis by tracking not only frequencies but also the spatial locations where those frequencies are active. The central object is the wavefront set WF(u) of a distribution u, a closed conic subset of the cotangent bundle T*X\0 that records both the position and the direction of singularities. For a linear partial differential operator P(x,D) with principal symbol p_m(x,ξ), the fundamental theorem of propagation of singularities states that WF(u) WF(Pu) is invariant under the Hamiltonian flow of p_m on the characteristic set {p_m=0}. In concrete terms, singularities travel along bicharacteristic curves projected to the base manifold. This principle underlies the sharp regularity theory for hyperbolic equations, the construction of parametrices for elliptic and subelliptic operators, and the proof of the Hörmander condition for hypoellipticity of sums of squares of vector fields. The calculus of pseudodifferential operators provides a symbolic algebra that respects the wavefront set: if A∈Ψ^m and B∈Ψ^k then the composition AB∈Ψm+k with principal symbol the product of symbols, and the commutator [A,B] drops one order. Egorov's theorem quantifies how a pseudodifferential operator evolves under conjugation by the solution operator of a hyperbolic equation, showing that the principal symbol is transported by the Hamiltonian flow. Applications range from the proof of the Duistermaat–Guillemin trace formula for the wave group on compact manifolds to the analysis of inverse problems such as X‑ray tomography, where the canonical relation of the Radon transform is a Lagrangian manifold and microlocal inversion recovers singularities visible to the data. Modern developments include the theory of Fourier integral operators associated with canonical relations, the microlocal analysis of boundary value problems via the Boutet de Monvel calculus, and the extension to non‑smooth coefficients using paradifferential calculus.

Geometric Flows: Ricci Flow and Singularity Formation

Ricci flow ∂_t g = −2 Ric(g) deforms a Riemannian metric in the direction of its Ricci curvature, smoothing out irregularities much like the heat equation smooths a temperature distribution. Hamilton's short‑time existence theorem guarantees a unique solution for smooth initial data on a closed manifold. The maximum principle applied to the evolution equations for curvature tensors yields powerful monotonicity formulas; for instance, the scalar curvature satisfies ∂_t R = ΔR + 2|Ric|2, so the minimum of R is non‑decreasing. In three dimensions, Hamilton proved that positive Ricci curvature is preserved and that the metric becomes round as t approaches the maximal time. Perelman's breakthroughs introduced the ℱ‑functional ℱ(g,f)=∫(R+|∇f|2)e−fdV and the reduced volume, both monotone along the flow, which rule out non‑trivial collapsing and provide a canonical neighborhood structure near singularities. Singularities are classified by the blow‑up rate of curvature: Type I singularities satisfy sup|Rm| ≤ C/(T−t) and, after parabolic rescaling, converge to non‑flat gradient shrinking solitons; Type II singularities blow up faster and require more delicate analysis. The surgery procedure, pioneered by Hamilton and refined by Perelman, cuts off high‑curvature regions modeled on κ‑solutions (ancient κ‑noncollapsed solutions) and continues the flow past singular times. This program culminated in the proof of the Poincaré conjecture and the geometrization conjecture for closed 3‑manifolds. In higher dimensions, the formation of singularities is less understood; neckpinch singularities in rotationally symmetric metrics on S^n (n≥4) provide explicit Type I examples, while the stability of such singularities under perturbations remains an active research area. Recent work on the Ricci flow with surgery in four dimensions (by Bamler, Kleiner, and others) and the study of the flow on manifolds with boundary (using the DeTurck trick and boundary conditions preserving the mean curvature) extend the theory toward a full classification of singularity models.

Worked Example: Wavefront Set of a Conormal Distribution
Let u be the distribution on ℝ2 defined by u(x,y)=H(x)δ(y), where H is the Heaviside step function and δ the Dirac delta. Determine WF(u) and verify that it is invariant under the Hamiltonian flow of the principal symbol of the wave operator □=∂_t2−Δ_x.
The singular support of u is the line {x=0}. In the Fourier variables (ξ,η), the product structure gives a singularity in the ξ direction from H(x) and in the η direction from δ(y). The wavefront set is WF(u)={(0,y;ξ,0): ξ≠0}∪{(0,0;0,η): η≠0}. The principal symbol of □ is p(t,x,y;τ,ξ,η)=−τ2+ξ2+η2. Its Hamiltonian vector field is H_p=2τ∂_τ−2ξ∂_ξ−2η∂_η. On the characteristic set p=0, the flow lines are straight rays in the cotangent variables. For a point (0,y;ξ,0) with ξ≠0, the flow preserves the ξ component and leaves η=0, so the set is invariant. For (0,0;0,η) the flow preserves η and ξ=0. Hence WF(u) is a union of bicharacteristics, confirming the propagation of singularities theorem.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the wavefront set of a product or pullback, first compute the wavefront sets of the factors, then apply Hörmander's criterion for the product (the sum of the conormal directions must not contain zero). For propagation questions, identify the principal symbol, write down its Hamiltonian system, and check whether the given set is a union of integral curves. Sketching the characteristic variety and a few bicharacteristics often earns partial credit even if the full symbolic computation is omitted.

Random Dynamical Systems and Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem

Random dynamical systems (RDS) model the evolution of a state under the influence of a stochastic driving noise. Formally, an RDS over a metric dynamical system (Ω,ℱ,ℙ,θ) consists of a cocycle φ:ℕ×Ω×X→X satisfying φ(0,ω)=id and φ(n+m,ω)=φ(n,θ^m ω)∘φ(m,ω). The multiplicative ergodic theorem of Oseledets (1968) generalizes the classical Lyapunov exponent theory to linear cocycles over ergodic base transformations. For a linear cocycle A(n,ω) taking values in GL(d,ℝ) with ∫log⁺‖A(1,ω)‖ dℙ(ω)<∞, there exists a ℙ‑almost sure splitting ℝ^d=E1(ω)⊕⋯⊕E_k(ω) into Oseledets subspaces such that for any non‑zero v∈E_i(ω) the limit limn→∞ (1/n)log‖A(n,ω)v‖ equals the Lyapunov exponent λ_i. The exponents are ordered λ1>λ2>⋯>λ_k and are constant on the ergodic components. The theorem provides the foundation for the definition of random attractors, the study of stochastic bifurcations, and the analysis of stability for stochastic differential equations. In infinite dimensions, the theorem holds for compact or quasi‑compact cocycles on separable Banach spaces, enabling the treatment of stochastic PDEs such as the stochastic Navier–Stokes equations or reaction–diffusion systems with multiplicative noise. The concept of a random invariant manifold (stable, unstable, center) extends the deterministic theory via the Lyapunov–Perron method; the existence of a random inertial manifold for dissipative SPDEs reduces the long‑time dynamics to a finite‑dimensional random system. Recent advances include the development of a multiplicative ergodic theorem for non‑linear cocycles using the notion of Lyapunov metrics, and the connection between the top Lyapunov exponent and the exponential growth rate of solutions to linearized SPDEs, which is crucial for proving the existence of random exponential attractors.

Optimal Transport and Gradient Flows in Metric Spaces

Optimal transport provides a geometric framework for moving probability measures with minimal cost. Given two probability measures μ,ν on a Polish space X with cost function c(x,y), the Kantorovich problem seeks a coupling π∈Π(μ,ν) minimizing ∫c dπ. When c(x,y)=d(x,y)^p for a metric d, the minimal cost defines the p‑Wasserstein distance W_p(μ,ν). The space (𝒫_p(X),W_p) becomes a complete metric space, and absolutely continuous curves in this space can be characterized by a continuity equation ∂_t μ_t+∇·(v_t μ_t)=0 with velocity fields v_t satisfying ∫|v_t|^p dμ_t<∞. The Benamou–Brenier formula expresses W_p^p as an infimum over such curves, revealing a formal Riemannian structure on the Wasserstein space. Gradient flows of functionals ℱ:𝒫_p(X)→ℝ∪{+∞} are then defined via the minimizing movement scheme (JKO scheme): given a time step τ>0, define μk+1^τ as a minimizer of ℱ(μ)+ (1/2τ)W22(μ,μ_k^τ). As τ→0, the piecewise constant interpolation converges to a curve μ_t satisfying the evolution variational inequality (EVI) ½ d/dt W22(μ_t,ν) + ℱ(μ_t) ≤ ℱ(ν) for all ν. This abstract theory encompasses the heat equation as the gradient flow of the Boltzmann entropy ℱ(μ)=∫ρ log ρ dx on ℝ^d, the porous medium equation as the flow of the internal energy ∫U(ρ)dx, and the Fokker–Planck equation as the flow of the free energy ∫(Vρ+ρ log ρ)dx. In metric measure spaces satisfying the curvature‑dimension condition CD(K,∞) (in the sense of Lott–Sturm–Villani), the entropy is K‑convex along Wasserstein geodesics, which yields contraction estimates for the heat flow and a sharp Bakry–Émery gradient estimate. The theory extends to non‑reversible Markov processes via the concept of a generalized gradient flow in the sense of Mielke, and to discrete spaces such as graphs where the Wasserstein distance is replaced by a discrete transport metric. Recent work on entropic regularization (Sinkhorn algorithm) and on the Schrödinger problem connects optimal transport with large deviations of empirical measures, providing computational tools for high‑dimensional gradient flows.

Resurgence and Trans‑Series in Asymptotic Analysis of Differential Equations

Many differential equations admit formal power series solutions that are divergent yet encode exact information through resurgence. A trans‑series is a formal expansion of the form ∑n≥0 c_n εα_n e−β_n/ε (log ε)γ_n where the exponents α_n,β_n,γ_n belong to a well‑ordered set. The Borel transform converts a formal series ∑ a_n ε^n into a function ̂φ(ζ)=∑ a_n ζ^n/n! which often converges near the origin and can be analytically continued along rays in the complex ζ‑plane. Singularities of the Borel transform correspond to non‑perturbative contributions (instantons) in the original problem. The alien derivative Δ_ω measures the discontinuity of the Borel sum across a singularity at ζ=ω; Écalle's bridge equations relate alien derivatives of different sectors, providing a closed system that determines the full trans‑series. For the first Painlevé equation y''=6y2+x, the formal power series at infinity is divergent Gevrey‑1; its Borel transform has poles on the real axis, and the alien calculus yields the exact connection formulas for the Stokes multipliers. In quantum mechanics, the energy levels of the anharmonic oscillator x2+g x4 are given by a trans‑series in g; the large‑order behavior of perturbation coefficients is governed by the instanton action, and the resurgent structure predicts the exact non‑perturbative splitting of energy levels. The theory also applies to linear ODEs with irregular singular points (e.g., the Weber equation) where the Stokes phenomenon is described by the Voros symbols. Recent developments include the resurgent analysis of the Navier–Stokes equations in the context of turbulence, the study of resurgence in matrix models and topological strings, and the formulation of a resurgent version of the renormalization group for quantum field theories. The practical computation of trans‑series coefficients often uses the method of matched asymptotic expansions combined with the Borel–Padé summation, providing highly accurate approximations even for strongly coupled regimes.

Malliavin Calculus and Stochastic Partial Differential Equations

Malliavin calculus, also called the stochastic calculus of variations, provides an infinite‑dimensional differential calculus on the Wiener space. Its central object is the Malliavin derivative D, which acts on smooth cylindrical functionals F = f(W(h₁),…,W(hₙ)) by the chain rule DF = Σ ∂ᵢf · hᵢ. The adjoint of D with respect to the Gaussian measure is the Skorokhod integral δ, an extension of the Itô integral to non‑adapted integrands. The integration‑by‑parts formula 𝔼[⟨DF, u⟩H] = 𝔼[F δ(u)] holds for F in the Sobolev space 𝔻¹,² and u in Dom δ. This machinery yields a powerful criterion for absolute continuity of laws: if a random vector F = (F₁,…,Fₙ) belongs to 𝔻¹,² and its Malliavin covariance matrix γF = (⟨DFᵢ, DFⱼ⟩H) is almost surely invertible, then the law of F possesses a smooth density with respect to Lebesgue measure. Hörmander’s theorem on hypoellipticity of second‑order operators is a classic application: the Lie brackets of the diffusion vector fields generate the whole tangent space, which translates into the invertibility of γF for the solution of the associated SDE.

In the context of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs), Malliavin calculus is used to prove regularity of the probability laws of solutions, to establish the existence of densities for the stochastic heat equation, the stochastic Navier–Stokes equations, and the KPZ equation. For the stochastic heat equation ∂ₜu = Δu + ξ, where ξ is space‑time white noise, the solution at a fixed time t > 0 belongs to 𝔻¹,² and its Malliavin derivative satisfies a linearized equation. The non‑degeneracy of the covariance operator follows from the smoothing effect of the heat semigroup, yielding a smooth density. For the stochastic Navier–Stokes equations in two dimensions, the Malliavin covariance matrix is shown to be invertible under a suitable non‑degeneracy condition on the noise, implying that the invariant measure has a smooth density. The calculus also underpins the Bismut–Elworthy–Li formula for the gradient of the semigroup, which is essential for the study of ergodicity and the strong Feller property of Markov semigroups associated with SPDEs.

Recent developments include the extension of Malliavin calculus to rough paths and regularity structures, allowing the treatment of singular SPDEs such as the Φ⁴₃ model and the KPZ equation. In this framework, the Malliavin derivative is defined on the modelled distribution space, and the integration‑by‑parts formula is proved using the renormalisation group. These advances have opened the way to quantitative probabilistic results for a wide class of singular stochastic PDEs.

Topological Methods in Dynamical Systems: Conley Index and Morse Theory

The Conley index provides a topological invariant for isolated invariant sets of a flow, generalising the classical Morse index of non‑degenerate critical points. Given a flow φ on a locally compact metric space X, an isolating neighbourhood N is a compact set such that the maximal invariant set Inv(N, φ) is contained in the interior of N. The Conley index of Inv(N, φ) is the homotopy type of the pointed quotient space N/L, where L is an exit set for N (points that leave N in forward time). This index is independent of the choice of isolating neighbourhood and is invariant under continuation: if a family of flows φλ has a continuation of isolated invariant sets, their Conley indices are isomorphic. For a hyperbolic equilibrium with Morse index k, the Conley index is the homotopy type of a pointed k‑sphere, recovering the classical Morse index.

Morse decompositions partition an isolated invariant set into a finite collection of Morse sets ordered by the flow. The connection matrix, a boundary operator in a graded module built from the Conley indices of the Morse sets, encodes the existence of connecting orbits. The Conley index theory has been extended to infinite‑dimensional dynamical systems generated by semiflows on Banach spaces, such as reaction‑diffusion equations and the Navier–Stokes equations. In these settings, the index is defined using the homotopy type of the attractor‑repeller pairs and the associated index pairs. Applications include the proof of the existence of periodic orbits in the restricted three‑body problem, the detection of chaotic invariant sets in the Lorenz system, and the analysis of bifurcations in parameter‑dependent PDEs. The theory also provides a rigorous foundation for numerical verification of dynamical behaviour: rigorous computational algorithms construct index pairs and compute the Conley index via homology, giving computer‑assisted proofs of the existence of specific invariant sets.

Nonlinear Dispersive Equations: Strichartz Estimates and Scattering Theory

Strichartz estimates are space‑time integrability bounds for solutions of linear dispersive equations such as the Schrödinger equation i∂ₜu + Δu = 0, the wave equation ∂ₜₜu − Δu = 0, and the Airy equation ∂ₜu + ∂ₓₓₓu = 0. For the Schrödinger equation on ℝᵈ, the admissible pairs (q, r) satisfy 2/q + d/r = d/2 with q, r ≥ 2 and (q, r, d) ≠ (2, ∞, 2). The estimate ‖eitΔf‖LqtLrx ≤ C‖f‖L² holds for all admissible pairs. The endpoint (q, r) = (2, 2d/(d−2)) is true for d ≥ 3 (Keel–Tao). These estimates are proved via the dispersive decay ‖eitΔf‖L^∞ ≤ C|t|−d/2‖f‖L¹ and the TT* argument.

In nonlinear problems, Strichartz estimates are the cornerstone of the local well‑posedness theory for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS) i∂ₜu + Δu = λ|u|p−1u. The critical regularity sc = d/2 − 2/(p−1) separates the subcritical (s > sc), critical (s = sc), and supercritical (s < sc) regimes. For the energy‑critical NLS in d = 3 with p = 5 (sc = 1), global well‑posedness and scattering for large data in Ḣ¹ were proved by Colliander, Keel, Staffilani, Takaoka, and Tao using the concentration‑compactness/rigidity method. The proof combines a profile decomposition for bounded sequences in Ḣ¹, a perturbation lemma based on Strichartz estimates, and a rigidity argument that rules out the existence of a minimal‑mass blow‑up solution via the virial identity and the Morawetz estimate.

Scattering theory aims to show that the solution u(t) behaves like a free solution as t → ±∞, i.e. there exist u± ∈ Ḣ¹ such that ‖u(t) − eitΔu±‖Ḣ¹ → 0. The wave operators Ω± mapping u± to the initial data u(0) are constructed using the Duhamel formula and the Strichartz estimates. For the mass‑critical NLS in d = 2 (p = 3), global well‑posedness and scattering in L² were established by Dodson, building on the work of Bourgain and others. The key new ingredient was a long‑time Strichartz estimate for the linear propagator on frequency‑localized pieces, combined with a frequency‑localized interaction Morawetz inequality. These techniques have been extended to other dispersive models such as the generalized KdV equation, the water‑wave system, and the nonlinear wave equation.

Free Boundary Problems and Obstacle Problems in Elliptic PDEs

Free boundary problems arise when the domain of a PDE is not known a priori but is determined as part of the solution. A prototypical example is the obstacle problem: find u minimizing the Dirichlet integral ∫|∇u|² dx among functions u ≥ ψ in a bounded domain Ω, with prescribed boundary data. The solution satisfies the variational inequality ∫∇u·∇(v−u) dx ≥ 0 for all v ≥ ψ. The contact set {u = ψ} and the non‑contact set {u > ψ} are separated by the free boundary ∂{u > ψ} ∩ Ω. Caffarelli’s regularity theory shows that, for a smooth obstacle ψ, the free boundary is locally a C¹,α hypersurface except possibly at a set of singular points of Hausdorff dimension at most d−1. In the non‑degenerate case (Δψ < 0), the free boundary is analytic. The proof uses a monotonicity formula (the Almgren frequency function) and a blow‑up analysis: rescaling around a free boundary point yields a global homogeneous solution, which is classified as either a half‑space solution (regular point) or a quadratic polynomial (singular point).

Another important class is the one‑phase Stefan problem, modeling melting ice: find u ≥ 0 and a moving boundary Γ(t) such that ∂ₜu = Δu in {u > 0}, u = 0 on Γ(t), and the normal velocity Vn = −∂nu on Γ(t). The weak formulation uses the enthalpy variable and leads to a variational inequality of parabolic type. Regularity of the free boundary for the Stefan problem was established by Caffarelli and Friedman, showing that the free boundary is locally a C¹ hypersurface in space‑time under a non‑degeneracy condition. The two‑phase problem, where both phases have positive conductivity, leads to a transmission condition on the free boundary and requires more delicate analysis.

Recent advances include the study of free boundaries in non‑local equations such as the fractional obstacle problem (−Δ)^s u = f with 0 < s < 1. The free boundary regularity theory for the fractional case was developed by Caffarelli, Salsa, and Silvestre, showing that the free boundary is C¹,α for s ≥ 1/2 and has a more complex structure for s < 1/2. Numerical methods for free boundary problems, such as the level‑set method and the phase‑field approximation, are widely used in applications ranging from fluid dynamics to financial mathematics (American options).

Large Deviations for Stochastic Processes: Freidlin–Wentzell Theory and Applications

Freidlin–Wentzell theory provides a large‑deviation principle (LDP) for the family of diffusion processes X^ε satisfying dX^ε = b(X^ε) dt + √ε σ(X^ε) dW, as ε → 0. The rate function I0,T(φ) = ½ ∫₀ᵀ ‖φ̇(t) − b(φ(t))‖²a(φ(t))⁻¹ dt for absolutely continuous paths φ with φ(0) = x, and +∞ otherwise, where a = σσᵀ. The LDP states that for any Borel set A in the space of continuous paths, −infφ∈A° I(φ) ≤ liminf ε log ℙ(X^ε ∈ A) ≤ limsup ε log ℙ(X^ε ∈ A) ≤ −infφ∈Ā I(φ). This quantifies the exponential decay of probabilities of rare events.

A central application is the exit problem from a domain D containing a stable equilibrium x*. The mean exit time τ^ε satisfies lim ε log 𝔼[τ^ε] = V, where V = infφ: φ(0)=x*, φ(T)∈∂D I0,T(φ) is the quasi‑potential. The most likely exit location is the point on ∂D minimizing the quasi‑potential. This result underpins the theory of metastability: for a gradient system b = −∇U, the quasi‑potential equals 2(U(z) − U(x*)) where z is the lowest saddle on the boundary of the basin of attraction. The Eyring–Kramers formula gives the precise prefactor: 𝔼[τ^ε] ∼ (2π/|λ₁|) √(|det Hess U(z)|/det Hess U(x*)) exp(2(U(z)−U(x*))/ε), where λ₁ is the negative eigenvalue of Hess U(z).

Large‑deviation principles have been extended to stochastic PDEs, such as the stochastic reaction‑diffusion equation and the stochastic Navier–Stokes equations, using the weak convergence approach of Budhiraja and Dupuis. In these infinite‑dimensional settings, the rate function involves the Cameron–Martin space of the driving noise. Applications include the study of rare transitions in climate models, the analysis of error probabilities in communication systems, and the derivation of the Arrhenius law for chemical reaction rates. The theory also connects with the Freidlin–Wentzell random perturbations of dynamical systems, where the LDP for the empirical measure of a Markov process leads to the Donsker–Varadhan rate function, linking large deviations to entropy and the variational formula for the principal eigenvalue of the generator.

Elliptic Regularity Theory: Schauder Estimates and De Giorgi–Nash–Moser Theory

Schauder Theory

Schauder estimates provide pointwise Hölder regularity for solutions of linear elliptic equations with Hölder continuous coefficients. For a uniformly elliptic operator L u = aij(x) ∂ij u + b^i(x) ∂_i u + c(x) u on a domain Ω ⊂ ℝ^n, if the coefficients aij, b^i, c belong to Cα(Ω) and the right‑hand side f ∈ Cα(Ω), then any solution u ∈ C2(Ω) ∩ C0(Ω̅) satisfies the interior estimate ‖u‖C2,α(Ω') ≤ C (‖u‖L^∞(Ω) + ‖f‖Cα(Ω)) for any Ω' ⋐ Ω. The constant C depends only on n, α, the ellipticity constants, and the Cα norms of the coefficients. Boundary Schauder estimates require the boundary ∂Ω to be C2,α and the Dirichlet data to be C2,α(∂Ω); then u ∈ C2,α(Ω̅) with a corresponding bound. These estimates are proved by freezing coefficients, using the Newtonian potential, and applying Campanato space characterizations of Hölder spaces.

De Giorgi–Nash–Moser Theory

When coefficients are merely bounded and measurable, Schauder theory fails. De Giorgi (1957) and independently Nash (1958) and Moser (1960) established that weak solutions of divergence‑form equations ∂_i (aij(x) ∂_j u) = 0 with uniformly elliptic, bounded measurable coefficients are locally Hölder continuous. The proof uses an iterative Moser‑type Harnack inequality or De Giorgi’s level‑set method. The resulting exponent α depends only on n and the ellipticity ratio λ/Λ. This theory extends to parabolic equations and to systems under a Legendre‑Hadamard condition. It is the foundation for the regularity of minimizers of integral functionals with p‑growth and for the study of free boundary problems.

Applications

Schauder theory is the standard tool for nonlinear elliptic problems where coefficients depend on the solution, e.g., quasilinear equations of the form aij(x,u,Du) ∂ij u = f(x,u,Du). By linearizing and applying the estimates in a fixed‑point argument, one obtains classical solutions. De Giorgi–Nash–Moser regularity is essential for the existence theory of weak solutions via the direct method in the calculus of variations, because it upgrades energy solutions to continuous functions, enabling the use of maximum principles and the definition of boundary values in the trace sense.

Parabolic Maximal Regularity and Applications to Nonlinear Evolution Equations

Maximal L^p Regularity

For the abstract Cauchy problem u'(t) + A u(t) = f(t), u(0) = 0 on a Banach space X, maximal L^p regularity (1 < p < ∞) means that for every f ∈ L^p(0,T; X) the unique mild solution satisfies u', A u ∈ L^p(0,T; X) with the estimate ‖u'‖L^p + ‖A u‖L^p ≤ C ‖f‖L^p. This property is equivalent to the R‑boundedness of the resolvent family {λ(λ+A)-1 : λ ∈ Σ_θ} for some θ > π/2, or to the boundedness of the operator‑valued Fourier multiplier associated with the symbol (iξ + A)-1 on L^p(ℝ; X). On Hilbert spaces, maximal regularity coincides with A being sectorial of angle < π/2. On general Banach spaces, it is strictly stronger than analytic semigroup generation.

Operator‑Valued Fourier Multipliers

The Weis theorem characterizes maximal L^p regularity via R‑boundedness of the set {ξ (iξ + A)-1 : ξ ∈ ℝ{0}} when X is a UMD space. This criterion is verifiable for many concrete operators: the Laplacian with Dirichlet, Neumann, or Robin boundary conditions on L^q(Ω) (1 < q < ∞) enjoys maximal regularity; the Stokes operator on solenoidal L^q spaces also does. The theory extends to time‑dependent operators A(t) satisfying Acquistapace–Terreni conditions, enabling the treatment of non‑autonomous problems.

Nonlinear Applications

Maximal regularity provides a robust framework for quasilinear parabolic systems. Writing the equation as u' + A(u) u = F(u), one linearizes around a reference solution and uses the implicit function theorem in the maximal regularity space MR^p = {u ∈ W1,p(0,T; X) ∩ L^p(0,T; D(A))}. This yields local well‑posedness for a wide class of problems: the Navier–Stokes equations in critical spaces, the Cahn–Hilliard equation, and reaction‑diffusion systems with nonlinear diffusion. The approach also gives optimal regularity for the linearized problem, which is crucial for stability analysis and numerical approximation.

Critical Point Theory: Mountain Pass Theorem and Linking Structures

Palais–Smale Condition

A C1 functional Φ on a Banach space X satisfies the Palais–Smale condition at level c (PS)_c if every sequence (u_k) with Φ(u_k) → c and Φ'(u_k) → 0 in X* has a convergent subsequence. The condition is automatic for functionals that are coercive and satisfy a compactness property, e.g., when the embedding of the underlying Sobolev space into the target space is compact. In critical exponent problems the embedding fails, and (PS)_c may hold only for certain levels c; this is the starting point of the concentration‑compactness principle.

Mountain Pass Geometry

The Mountain Pass Theorem (Ambrosetti–Rabinowitz, 1973) requires Φ(0)=0, the existence of ρ>0, α>0 such that Φ(u) ≥ α for ‖u‖=ρ, and a point e with ‖e‖>ρ and Φ(e) ≤ 0. Then the minimax value c = infγ∈Γ maxt∈[0,1] Φ(γ(t)), where Γ = {γ ∈ C([0,1], X) : γ(0)=0, γ(1)=e}, is a critical value provided (PS)_c holds. The geometry captures the intuitive picture of a path crossing a mountain pass between two valleys.

Linking Theorems

Linking generalizes the mountain pass to higher‑dimensional topological structures. Let X = V ⊕ W with dim V < ∞. If Φ is bounded below on a sphere in W and Φ ≤ 0 on the boundary of a bounded set in V, then the linking value c = infh∈H supu∈Q Φ(h(u)) is critical, where Q is a suitable cylinder and H a class of homotopies preserving the linking. This framework yields multiple critical points via the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category or the genus, leading to infinitely many solutions for even functionals.

Applications to Semilinear Elliptic Equations

For −Δ u = f(x,u) on a bounded domain with Dirichlet conditions, the energy functional Φ(u) = ½∫|∇u|2 − ∫F(x,u) is C1 on H10(Ω). If f has subcritical growth, the Sobolev embedding is compact and (PS)_c holds for all c. The mountain pass gives a nontrivial solution when f is superlinear at infinity and sublinear near zero. Linking arguments produce sign‑changing solutions and, under symmetry, infinitely many solutions. For critical growth f(u) ∼ |u|2^*-2u, the lack of compactness is handled by the concentration‑compactness principle, which identifies the precise levels where (PS)_c fails.

Concentration Compactness Principle and Profile Decomposition for PDEs

Loss of Compactness

In variational problems with critical Sobolev exponent, bounded sequences in H1(ℝ^n) may fail to converge strongly due to translation invariance and scaling invariance. A bounded sequence (u_k) can exhibit three behaviors: compactness (up to subsequence, u_k → u strongly), vanishing (u_k → 0 in L2^*), or dichotomy (the sequence splits into two asymptotically orthogonal parts with disjoint supports). The concentration‑compactness lemma of Lions (1984) quantifies this trichotomy and provides a tool to recover compactness modulo translations and dilations.

Concentration Compactness Lemma

Let (u_k) be bounded in H1(ℝ^n). Define the concentration function Q_k(R) = supy∈ℝ^n ∫B_R(y) |u_k|2. Then, up to a subsequence, either (i) Q_k(R) → 0 for all R (vanishing), (ii) there exists a sequence (y_k) such that v_k(x) = u_k(x+y_k) converges weakly to a non‑zero limit (compactness up to translation), or (iii) there exists a splitting u_k = v_k + w_k + o(1) with disjoint supports asymptotically and both parts non‑trivial (dichotomy). The lemma is the basis for proving existence of minimizers for the Sobolev inequality and for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation at the critical mass.

Profile Decomposition

For a bounded sequence in a Hilbert space with a group of symmetries G (translations, dilations), a profile decomposition writes u_k = Σj=1^J gk,j φ^j + r_k^J, where φ^j are profiles, gk,j ∈ G are symmetry parameters diverging pairwise, and the remainder r_k^J vanishes in the Strichartz norm as J → ∞. This linear profile decomposition, developed by Bahouri–Gérard (1999) for the wave equation and by Keraani (2001) for the Schrödinger equation, is the nonlinear analogue of the concentration‑compactness principle. It underpins the proof of global well‑posedness and scattering for energy‑critical dispersive equations.

Applications to Critical Exponent Problems

In the Yamabe problem, the concentration‑compactness principle shows that a minimizing sequence for the Sobolev quotient either converges to a solution or concentrates at a point, leading to the classification of blow‑up profiles. For the energy‑critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation i u_t + Δ u = ±|u|4/(n-2) u, the profile decomposition yields a “critical element” that is almost periodic modulo symmetries; rigidity theorems then rule out such elements, proving global well‑posedness and scattering. The same method applies to the wave maps equation and to the harmonic map heat flow.

Blow‑up Analysis and Type I/II Singularities in Nonlinear Heat and Wave Equations

Self‑Similar Blow‑up Profiles

For the semilinear heat equation u_t = Δ u + u^p with p > 1, a solution blowing up at time T may exhibit self‑similar behavior: u(x,t) ≈ (T−t)-1/(p-1) f( (x−x0)/√(T−t) ). The profile f satisfies the elliptic equation Δ f − ½ y·∇ f − f/(p-1) + f^p = 0. Existence of positive radial solutions of this equation determines whether Type I blow‑up (rate (T−t)-1/(p-1)) occurs. For the energy‑supercritical case p > (n+2)/(n-2), there exist infinitely many self‑similar profiles, leading to a rich blow‑up dynamics.

Type I vs Type II Blow‑up

Type I blow‑up means the scaling‑invariant norm ‖u(t)‖L^∞ (T−t)1/(p-1) remains bounded. Type II blow‑up means this quantity diverges, indicating a faster or more singular collapse. For the heat equation, Type II blow‑up is associated with the existence of non‑self‑similar profiles and with the “slow” blow‑up rates (T−t)-α with α > 1/(p-1). In the wave equation utt = Δ u + u^p, the scaling is different, and the classification involves the conformal energy. The distinction is crucial for the stability of blow‑up profiles: Type I profiles are often stable under perturbations, while Type II profiles are typically unstable and require fine‑tuned initial data.

Energy Methods and Blow‑up Criteria

The classical Kaplan criterion uses the first eigenfunction of −Δ on the domain to derive a differential inequality for the spatial average, yielding a sufficient condition for blow‑up in finite time. For the heat equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions, if the initial energy E(0) = ½∫|∇u0|2 − 1/(p+1)∫ u0p+1 is negative, then the solution blows up. The concavity method of Levine gives a more general criterion: if ∫ u0 φ1 > 0 and the energy is negative, blow‑up occurs. For the wave equation, the virial identity and the Glassey exponent p_G = 1 + 2/(n-1) separate global existence from blow‑up for small data.

Recent Advances

Merle and Zaag (1997) constructed stable Type II blow‑up solutions for the heat equation in dimension n=1 with p=3, showing that the blow‑up rate is (T−t)-1/2 |log(T−t)|1/2. For the energy‑critical wave equation in 3D, Krieger–Schlag–Tataru (2009) proved the existence of a codimension‑one manifold of initial data leading to Type II blow‑up with a universal profile. In the stochastic setting, the addition of noise can either accelerate or prevent blow‑up; the analysis uses stochastic maximal regularity and comparison principles. These results illustrate the deep interplay between scaling, spectral theory, and nonlinear dynamics in singularity formation.

Backward Stochastic Differential Equations and the Nonlinear Feynman–Kac Formula

Backward stochastic differential equations (BSDEs) arose from stochastic control and mathematical finance in the early 1990s through the work of Pardoux and Peng. Unlike forward SDEs where the initial condition is prescribed, a BSDE specifies the terminal condition ξ at time T and solves backward in time: Y_t = ξ + ∫_t^T f(s, Y_s, Z_s) ds − ∫_t^T Z_s dW_s, where W is a Brownian motion. The solution is a pair of adapted processes (Y, Z) with Y representing the value process and Z the control (or martingale representation) process. The generator f(s, y, z) can be nonlinear in both arguments, making BSDEs a natural framework for recursive utilities, risk measures, and nonlinear pricing.

The nonlinear Feynman–Kac formula establishes a deep connection between BSDEs and semilinear parabolic PDEs. If u(t, x) solves ∂_t u + ½ Δ u + f(t, x, u, ∇u) = 0 with terminal condition u(T, x) = g(x), and X_t^x solves dX = dW, X_t = x, then setting Y_s = u(s, X_s), Z_s = ∇u(s, X_s) yields a solution to the BSDE with terminal condition ξ = g(X_T) and generator f(s, Y_s, Z_s). Conversely, under regularity assumptions, the BSDE solution defines a viscosity solution of the PDE. This duality extends to obstacle problems (reflected BSDEs), where Y_t ≥ S_t for a given obstacle process S, corresponding to variational inequalities and American option pricing.

Well-posedness theory for BSDEs requires Lipschitz continuity of f in (y, z) and square-integrability of ξ. For quadratic generators (|f| ≤ C(1 + |y| + |z|²)), Kobylanski proved existence via exponential transformations and BMO martingale theory. The case of superquadratic growth remains delicate; existence may fail without structural conditions. Numerical approximation uses time discretization (Euler schemes) combined with regression or neural networks for the conditional expectations defining Z. The deep BSDE method (Han, Jentzen, E) parameterizes Z by a neural network and minimizes the terminal loss, achieving high-dimensional PDE solutions beyond grid-based methods.

Applications span mathematical finance (nonlinear pricing under constraints, recursive utility), stochastic differential games (Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman–Isaacs equations), and probabilistic representation of fully nonlinear PDEs via second-order BSDEs (2BSDEs) of Cheridito, Soner, Touzi, and Victoir. The 2BSDE framework handles generators depending on the Hessian of u, linking to the stochastic Perron method for viscosity solutions. In risk theory, g-expectations defined by BSDEs generate dynamic convex risk measures satisfying time consistency, with the entropic risk measure corresponding to quadratic f.

Boundary Control and Stabilization of Partial Differential Equations

Boundary control addresses systems where the control input acts on the boundary of the spatial domain rather than distributed in the interior. For a parabolic equation ∂_t y = Δ y in Ω with Dirichlet control u on ∂Ω, the state y satisfies y|_∂Ω = u. The solution map u ↦ y is unbounded from L²(0, T; L²(∂Ω)) to L²(0, T; L²(Ω)), reflecting the ill-posedness of the Dirichlet map. The Lions transposition method defines very weak solutions by duality: for test functions φ solving the adjoint homogeneous problem, ∫_Ω y(T)φ(T) = ∫0^T ∫_∂Ω u ∂_ν φ. This yields a well-posed control-to-state map in negative Sobolev spaces.

Exact controllability asks whether for any initial state y₀ and target y_T, there exists a boundary control steering the system. For the wave equation ∂_tt y = Δ y with Dirichlet control, the Hilbert Uniqueness Method (HUM) of Lions reduces exact controllability to an observability inequality for the adjoint system: ∫0^T ∫_∂Ω |∂_ν φ|² ≥ C ‖(φ(0), ∂_t φ(0))‖²H¹₀×L². The geometric control condition (GCC) of Bardos, Lebeau, and Rauch characterizes when this holds: every ray of geometric optics must intersect the controlled boundary portion within time T. For the heat equation, null controllability holds in any time T > 0 for any nonempty open control region, but exact controllability fails due to smoothing.

Stabilization seeks feedback laws u = Ky making the closed-loop system exponentially stable. For the wave equation, boundary damping u = −∂_t y on ∂Ω yields energy decay E'(t) = −∫_∂Ω |∂_t y|². The multiplier method gives polynomial decay under GCC; exponential decay requires stronger geometric conditions. For parabolic equations, the backstepping method (Krstic, Smyshlyaev) constructs an invertible Volterra transformation mapping the original system to a target stable system, yielding explicit feedback kernels. This extends to reaction-diffusion, Navier–Stokes, and coupled PDE-ODE systems.

Boundary triplets and abstract boundary control spaces provide a functional-analytic framework. A boundary triplet (Γ₀, Γ₁, G) for a symmetric operator A* consists of a Hilbert space G and linear maps Γ₀, Γ₁: D(A*) → G such that Green's identity holds: ⟨A*f, g⟩ − ⟨f, A*g⟩ = ⟨Γ₁f, Γ₀g⟩_G − ⟨Γ₀f, Γ₁g⟩_G. Self-adjoint extensions correspond to linear relations Θ ⊂ G × G via Γ₁f = Θ Γ₀f. The Dirichlet-to-Neumann map Λ(λ) = Γ₁(Γ₀|ker(A*−λ))⁻¹ encodes spectral data. This formalism unifies control of PDEs on networks, quantum graphs, and systems with dynamic boundary conditions.

Worked Problem: Observability for the 1D Wave Equation
Consider the wave equation y_tt = y_xx on (0, 1) with Dirichlet control at x = 1: y(t, 0) = 0, y(t, 1) = u(t). The adjoint system is φ_tt = φ_xx, φ(t, 0) = φ(t, 1) = 0. Prove the observability inequality ∫0^T |φ_x(t, 1)|² dt ≥ C ‖(φ(0), φ_t(0))‖²H¹₀×L² for T > 2, and explain why it fails for T ≤ 2.

Distinguish the functional settings: distributed control uses L²(Ω) controls with bounded control-to-state maps; boundary control requires transposition or extrapolation spaces. Memorize the GCC for wave equations (rays must hit controlled boundary) and the fundamental difference: heat equation is null controllable in any time from any open boundary portion, but never exactly controllable. For stabilization, know that boundary damping for waves gives polynomial decay under GCC, exponential only with stronger geometry; backstepping gives explicit kernels for parabolic equations.

Symmetry Methods for Nonlinear Elliptic Equations: The Moving Plane Technique

The method of moving planes, introduced by Alexandrov for constant mean curvature surfaces and developed by Serrin, Gidas, Ni, and Nirenberg for semilinear elliptic equations, proves symmetry of positive solutions. Consider −Δu = f(u) in a domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ with u = 0 on ∂Ω, where f is locally Lipschitz. The method compares u(x) with its reflection u_λ(x) = u(x^λ) across a hyperplane T_λ = {x₁ = λ}, where x^λ = (2λ − x₁, x₂, …, xₙ). For λ sufficiently negative, the reflected cap Σ_λ = {x ∈ Ω : x₁ < λ} lies inside Ω, and the maximum principle gives u ≤ u_λ in Σ_λ. One then slides the plane rightward until a critical position λ₀ where either the plane hits the boundary or u ≡ u_λ₀. The strong maximum principle and Hopf's lemma then force symmetry about T_λ₀.

The key technical step is the narrow region principle: if Σ_λ is sufficiently narrow, the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of −Δ in Σ_λ exceeds the Lipschitz constant of f, so the linearized operator −Δ − f'(ξ) satisfies the maximum principle. This allows the sliding to continue past regions where u might not be monotone. For unbounded domains (e.g., ℝⁿ), one uses decay at infinity: if u(x) → 0 as |x| → ∞, then for λ ≪ 0, u < u_λ in Σ_λ by the maximum principle in unbounded domains (Berestycki, Caffarelli, Nirenberg). The method yields radial symmetry for positive solutions in balls, half-spaces, and ℝⁿ, and also proves monotonicity in the direction orthogonal to the symmetry plane.

Extensions include the moving sphere method (method of moving planes in integral form) for equations with nonlocal operators like the fractional Laplacian (−Δ)^s. For integral equations u(x) = ∫ℝⁿ |x−y|-(n−2s) f(u(y)) dy, the Kelvin transform replaces reflection. The maximum principle for anti-symmetric functions in narrow regions still holds using the integral formulation. Another extension handles systems: for cooperative systems −Δu = f(u, v), −Δv = g(u, v) with ∂f/∂v, ∂g/∂u ≥ 0, the vector-valued maximum principle applies, yielding simultaneous symmetry of components.

Applications are vast: classification of positive solutions to −Δu = u^p in ℝⁿ (Gidas–Ni–Nirenberg, 1979) shows radial symmetry and monotonicity for 1 < p < (n+2)/(n−2); the critical exponent case p = (n+2)/(n−2) corresponds to the Yamabe problem, where the method identifies all positive solutions as Aubin–Talenti bubbles. For the Lane–Emden system, symmetry holds under subcritical conditions. The method also proves nonexistence in star-shaped domains via Pohozaev identity combined with symmetry: if a positive solution exists in a star-shaped domain, moving planes forces radial symmetry, but Pohozaev gives a contradiction for supercritical exponents.

Degenerate Parabolic Equations: Porous Medium Flow and the p-Laplacian Evolution

Degenerate parabolic equations arise when the diffusion coefficient vanishes at certain solution values. The porous medium equation (PME) ∂_t u = Δ(u^m) for m > 1 models gas flow in porous media, thin fluid films, and population dynamics. The diffusion coefficient m um−1 vanishes at u = 0, causing finite speed of propagation: compactly supported initial data remain compactly supported for all time, with a free boundary ∂{u > 0} advancing at finite speed. This contrasts sharply with the heat equation (m = 1), where positivity spreads instantly. The Barenblatt–Zel'dovich–Kompaneets self-similar solution B(x, t) = t-k [C − k(m−1)|x|²/(2m n t2k/n)]_+1/(m−1) with k = n/(n(m−1)+2) describes the asymptotic profile for integrable initial data.

The p-Laplacian evolution equation ∂_t u = div(|∇u|p−2 ∇u) for p > 2 (slow diffusion) and 1 < p < 2 (fast diffusion) exhibits degeneracy in the gradient. For p > 2, the equation is degenerate where ∇u = 0; for 1 < p < 2, it is singular. Solutions are understood in the weak sense: u ∈ L^p(0, T; W1,p0(Ω)), ∂_t u ∈ Lp'(0, T; W-1,p'(Ω)). The DiBenedetto–Friedman theory establishes local Hölder continuity of weak solutions via intrinsic scaling: cylinders are scaled as Q_ρ = B_ρ × (t₀ − ρ^p, t₀) for p > 2, reflecting the degeneracy. The Harnack inequality holds with constants independent of the solution, and the free boundary for p > 2 has finite speed of propagation.

Entropy solutions and kinetic formulations handle measure-valued data. For the PME, the concept of entropy solution (Bénilan, Crandall) uses the accretivity of the operator A(u) = −Δ(u^m) in L¹. The Crandall–Liggett theorem generates the semigroup via implicit time discretization: uk+1 + τ Δ(uk+1^m) = u_k. The kinetic formulation ∂_t χ + ξ·∇_x χ = ∂_ξ m for the characteristic function χ(t, x, ξ) = 10<u(t,x)<ξ</sub> linearizes the equation and enables averaging lemmas for compactness. This approach extends to scalar conservation laws and hyperbolic-parabolic equations.

Asymptotic behavior is governed by self-similarity and entropy methods. For the PME, the rescaled solution v(y, τ) = t^k u(t^k/n y, t) with τ = log t converges to the Barenblatt profile in L¹ as τ → ∞. The relative entropy functional E[v|B] = ∫ (v^m/m − B^m/m − Bm−1(v − B)) decays exponentially along the flow, giving quantitative convergence rates. For the p-Laplacian, the asymptotic profile is the Barenblatt solution of the porous medium equation with m = p−1 (after a change of variables), and convergence holds in relative error for p > 2n/(n+2). The fast diffusion case 1 < p < 2 leads to extinction in finite time for small initial data, with extinction profiles described by very singular solutions.

Navier–Stokes Equations: Leray–Hopf Solutions, Regularity Criteria, and the Millennium Problem

The incompressible Navier–Stokes equations ∂_t u + (u·∇)u − νΔu + ∇p = f, ∇·u = 0 in ℝⁿ (n = 2, 3) or a bounded domain Ω describe viscous fluid motion. Leray (1934) constructed global weak solutions for n = 3 with finite energy initial data u₀ ∈ L²_σ (divergence-free). A Leray–Hopf solution satisfies u ∈ L^∞(0, T; L²) ∩ L²(0, T; H¹), the energy inequality ‖u(t)‖²L² + 2ν∫0^t ‖∇u‖²L² ≤ ‖u₀‖²L² + 2∫0^t ⟨f, u⟩, and the equation in the sense of distributions. Uniqueness of weak solutions in 3D remains open; it is the Clay Millennium Problem.

Regularity criteria give conditions ensuring a weak solution is smooth. The Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin criterion: if u ∈ L^r(0, T; L^s) with 2/r + 3/s = 1, s > 3, then u is smooth. The endpoint s = 3 (L^∞(0, T; L³)) was resolved by Escauriaza, Seregin, and Šverák (2003): u ∈ L^∞(0, T; L³) implies regularity. The Beale–Kato–Majda criterion for the vorticity ω = ∇×u: if ∫0^T ‖ω(t)‖L^∞ dt < ∞, then no blow-up occurs. The geometric criterion of Constantin and Fefferman: if the direction of vorticity ξ = ω/|ω| is Lipschitz in regions of high vorticity, regularity holds. These criteria exploit the vortex stretching term (ω·∇)u and the Biot–Savart law u = ∇×(−Δ)⁻¹ω.

Partial regularity theory (Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg, 1982) shows that the singular set of a suitable weak solution has parabolic Hausdorff dimension at most 1. A suitable weak solution satisfies the local energy inequality ∂_t (|u|²/2) + ν|∇u|² ≤ div((|u|²/2 + p)u) + f·u in the sense of distributions. The proof uses a blow-up argument: if a singular point exists, rescaling yields a nontrivial ancient solution with zero energy dissipation, which must be zero by Liouville theorems. The dimension bound 1 is sharp for the known examples; whether singularities actually occur is unknown.

In 2D, global well-posedness and regularity are classical: the vorticity equation ∂_t ω + u·∇ω = νΔω has a maximum principle, giving L^∞ bounds on ω and hence global H¹ bounds. The attractor is finite-dimensional (Foias–Temam). For 3D, the existence of a global attractor for weak solutions is open, but the trajectory attractor exists (Chepyzhov–Vishik). The inviscid limit ν → 0 connects to the Euler equations; Kato's criterion states that the limit holds in L^∞(0, T; L²) iff ν∫0^T ‖∇u^ν‖²L²(Γ_ν) dt → 0, where Γ_ν is a boundary layer of thickness ν. The Onsager conjecture for Euler (proved by Isett, Buckmaster–Vicol) states that Hölder continuous solutions with exponent > 1/3 conserve energy, while for exponent < 1/3 there exist dissipative solutions.

  • Confusing the functional setting for boundary control: the Dirichlet map is unbounded on L², so one must use transposition or extrapolation spaces, not standard semigroup theory.
  • Assuming the moving plane method works for all nonlinearities: the narrow region principle requires the domain width to be smaller than the first eigenvalue of −Δ, which fails for large Lipschitz constants of f.
  • Treating the porous medium equation as uniformly parabolic: the degeneracy at u = 0 changes the scaling, requires intrinsic cylinders, and invalidates standard Schauder estimates.
  • Believing Leray–Hopf solutions are unique in 3D: uniqueness is the Millennium Problem; only weak-strong uniqueness holds (a strong solution, if it exists, is unique among weak solutions).
  • Applying the Ladyzhenskaya–Prodi–Serrin criterion at the endpoint s = 3 without the Escauriaza–Seregin–Šverák theorem: the original proof required s > 3; the endpoint was a major breakthrough.
  • Neglecting the difference between slow diffusion (p > 2) and fast diffusion (1 < p < 2) in p-Laplacian evolution: the former has finite speed of propagation and waiting times; the latter has infinite speed and extinction in finite time.
  • Overlooking the role of the kinetic formulation for degenerate equations: it linearizes the problem and enables averaging lemmas, which are essential for compactness when standard energy methods fail.

Littlewood–Paley Theory and Function Spaces for Nonlinear PDEs

Littlewood–Paley theory decomposes a function into frequency-localized pieces using a dyadic partition of unity on the Fourier side. Let φ ∈ Cc∞(ℝn) be a radial bump function supported in an annulus {ξ : 3/4 ≤ |ξ| ≤ 8/3} such that ∑j∈ℤ φ(2−jξ) = 1 for ξ ≠ 0. The dyadic blocks are Δju = ℱ−1[φ(2−j·)ℱu], and the low-frequency projection is Sju = ∑k≤j−1 Δku. Hydrodynamic Limits of Kinetic Equations: From Boltzmann to Navier–Stokes

The Boltzmann equation ∂tf + v·∇xf = Q(f,f) with collision operator Q(f,f) = ∫ℝ3∫S2 B(|v−v*|, cos θ)[f'f'* − ff*] dσ dv* describes a rarefied gas at the mesoscopic scale. The hydrodynamic limit ε → 0 in the scaled equation ε∂tfε + v·∇xfε = (1/ε)Q(fε,fε) connects kinetic and fluid descriptions. Hilbert's expansion fε = M + εf1 + ε2f2 + … where M(ρ,u,T) = ρ(2πT)−3/2exp(−|v−u|2/2T) is the local Maxwellian, yields at order ε0 that f0 = M, and at order ε1 the linearized collision operator Lg = Q(M,g) + Q(g,M) satisfies Lf1 = (∂t + v·∇x)M. The solvability condition ∫ Lf1 {1,v,|v|2} dv = 0 gives the compressible Euler equations for (ρ,u,T). The Chapman–Enskog expansion goes further: f1 = L−1[(I−Π)(v·∇x)M] where Π projects onto the kernel of L (spanned by collision invariants 1,v,|v|2). This yields the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system with viscosity μ = −(1/10)∫ v⊗v L−1(v⊗v M) dv and thermal conductivity κ = −(1/3)∫ v|v|2 L−1(v|v|2M) dv. Rigorous justification requires uniform estimates in ε. DiPerna and Lions (1989) introduced renormalized solutions for the Boltzmann equation with soft potentials, proving global existence without uniqueness. The relative entropy method (Bardos–Golse–Levermore, 1991) uses the functional H(f|M) = ∫ f log(f/M) dv to control the distance to local equilibrium. For the incompressible limit (Mach number Ma → 0, Reynolds number Re fixed), the acoustic waves propagate at speed 1/Ma and disperse; the limit yields the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations (Saint-Raymond, 2003; Gallagher–Saint-Raymond–Texier, 2014). The Vlasov–Poisson–Boltzmann system couples kinetic transport with self-consistent electric fields; its hydrodynamic limit gives the Euler–Poisson or Navier–Stokes–Poisson systems. The subcharacteristic condition (Liu, 1987) for relaxation systems ∂tU + ∂xF(U) = (1/ε)R(U) requires that the equilibrium manifold's characteristic speeds interlace those of the full system, ensuring stability of the Chapman–Enskog expansion. For the BGK model Q(f) = (M[f]−f)/τ, the Prandtl number is fixed at 1; the ES-BGK model (Holway, 1966) corrects this by using an anisotropic Gaussian. The Landau equation for Coulomb interactions replaces the angular singularity with a diffusion operator in velocity space; its hydrodynamic limit yields the same Navier–Stokes equations with different transport coefficients.

Exam Strategy: Hydrodynamic Limits
When asked about hydrodynamic limits, identify the scaling (Knudsen number ε, Mach number Ma, Reynolds number Re). The compressible Euler limit is ε → 0 with Ma = O(1); the incompressible Navier–Stokes limit requires Ma → 0, Re = O(1), and ε = O(Ma). The acoustic time scale is t/ε. Key theorems: Bardos–Golse–Levermore (1991) for compressible Euler via relative entropy; Saint-Raymond (2003) for incompressible Navier–Stokes via dispersive estimates on acoustic waves. Remember the subcharacteristic condition for relaxation systems: equilibrium characteristic speeds must lie strictly between the full system's speeds. For the Boltzmann equation, the Hilbert expansion is formal; rigorous proofs use either the relative entropy method (weak solutions, large data) or energy methods in Sobolev spaces (strong solutions, small data). The DiPerna–Lions renormalized solutions are essential for global existence without smallness assumptions.

Entropy Methods and Functional Inequalities for Convergence to Equilibrium

Entropy methods quantify the rate of convergence to equilibrium for evolution equations by exploiting the dissipation of a convex entropy functional. For a Markov semigroup Pt = etL with invariant measure μ, the entropy of a density f = dν/dμ is Entμ(f) = ∫ f log f dμ. The entropy dissipation is D(f) = −d/dt Entμ(Ptf)|t=0 = −∫ Lf log f dμ. For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck semigroup L = Δ − x·∇ on ℝn with Gaussian measure dγ = (2π)−n/2e−|x|2/2dx, the logarithmic Sobolev inequality (Gross, 1975) Entγ(f2) ≤ 2∫ |∇f|2 dγ implies exponential decay Entγ(Ptf) ≤ e−2tEntγ(f). The Bakry–Émery Γ2 calculus generalizes this: for a diffusion generator L = Δ − ∇V·∇, define Γ(f,g) = (1/2)[L(fg) − fLg − gLf] and Γ2(f) = (1/2)[LΓ(f,f) − 2Γ(f,Lf)]. The curvature-dimension condition CD(ρ,∞) means Γ2(f) ≥ ρΓ(f,f) for all f. Under CD(ρ,∞) with ρ > 0, the log-Sobolev inequality holds with constant 2/ρ, and Entμ(Ptf) ≤ e−ρtEntμ(f). For the porous medium equation ∂tu = Δum (m > 1), the entropy functional is the relative entropy with respect to the Barenblatt profile B: E[u|B] = ∫ (um/(m−1) − Bm/(m−1) − Bm−1(u−B)) dx. The dissipation is D[u] = ∫ u|∇(um−1−Bm−1)|2 dx. The entropy–entropy dissipation inequality D[u] ≥ λ E[u|B] (Carrillo–Toscani, 2000) gives exponential convergence in relative entropy, which by Csiszár–Kullback–Pinsker implies L1 convergence. For the Boltzmann equation, the H-theorem states d/dt H(f) = −D(f) ≤ 0 where H(f) = ∫ f log f dv and D(f) = (1/4)∫∫∫ B(f'f'*−ff*)log(f'f'*/ff*) dσ dv* dv. Cercignani's conjecture D(f) ≥ λ H(f|M) for hard spheres was disproved (Bobylev–Cercignani–Gamba, 2000); the correct inequality involves a deficit term. For the Fokker–Planck equation ∂tf = ∇·(f∇V) + Δf, the free energy F[f] = ∫ f log f dx + ∫ fV dx satisfies d/dt F[f] = −I[f] where I[f] = ∫ f|∇log(f/f∞)|2 dx is the Fisher information. The log-Sobolev inequality I[f] ≥ 2ρ(F[f]−F[f∞]) gives exponential decay. The HWI inequality (Otto–Villani, 2000) interpolates between entropy, Fisher information, and Wasserstein distance: Entμ(f) ≤ W2(fμ,μ)√Iμ(f) − (ρ/2)W22(fμ,μ). This yields the Talagrand inequality W22 ≤ (2/ρ)Entμ and the log-Sobolev inequality. For degenerate equations like the porous medium equation, the entropy dissipation method requires weighted functional inequalities adapted to the Barenblatt profile. The Bakry–Émery theory extends to discrete settings (Markov chains on graphs) and to non-reversible dynamics where the generator is not self-adjoint; the hypocoercivity framework (Villani, 2009; Dolbeault–Mouhot–Schmeiser, 2015) handles kinetic equations like the linear Boltzmann and Fokker–Planck equations where the dissipation only controls velocity derivatives.

Inverse Scattering Transform and Soliton Theory for Integrable PDEs

The inverse scattering transform (IST) solves certain nonlinear PDEs by mapping them to a linear scattering problem. The Korteweg–de Vries equation ut − 6uux + uxxx = 0 (KdV) is the prototypical example. The Lax pair formulation Lψ = λψ, ψt = Aψ with L = −∂x2 + u(x,t) (Schrödinger operator) and A = −4∂x3 + 6u∂x + 3ux implies the compatibility condition Lt = [A,L] is exactly KdV. The spectrum of L consists of continuous spectrum [0,∞) and finitely many negative eigenvalues −κj2 (bound states). The scattering data for the continuous spectrum are the reflection coefficient r(k) and transmission coefficient a(k) for k > 0; for bound states, the norming constants cj. The time evolution of scattering data is linear: r(k,t) = r(k,0)e8ik3t, a(k,t) = a(k,0), cj(t) = cj(0)e4κj3t. The inverse problem reconstructs u(x,t) from the scattering data via the Gel'fand–Levitan–Marchenko integral equation. The N-soliton solution corresponds to pure discrete spectrum (r ≡ 0): u(x,t) = −2∂x2 log det(I + A) where Aij = cicje−(κi+κj)x + 4(κi3+κj3)t / (κi+κj). The one-soliton is u(x,t) = 2κ2sech2[κ(x−4κ2t−x0)]. The nonlinear Schrödinger equation iut + uxx + 2|u|2u = 0 (focusing NLS) has Lax pair with L = i∂x + (0 u; −ū 0) (Zakharov–Shabat system). Its solitons are u(x,t) = η sech[η(x−2ξt)]ei(ξx+(η2−ξ2)t). The sine-Gordon equation utt − uxx + sin u = 0 has solitons (kinks) u(x,t) = 4 arctan exp[±(x−vt)/√(1−v2)]. The Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation (KP-I and KP-II) extends KdV to two dimensions; its IST uses a nonlocal Riemann–Hilbert problem. The Riemann–Hilbert approach (Deift–Zhou steepest descent) provides long-time asymptotics: for KdV with step-like initial data, the solution decomposes into a modulated elliptic wave (Whitham zone) and a train of solitons. The finite-gap integration (Novikov, Dubrovin, Its, Matveev, 1970s) constructs quasi-periodic solutions on Riemann surfaces of genus g; the theta function formula u(x,t) = 2∂x2 log θ(Ux + Vt + Z0) gives all finite-gap solutions. The Hirota bilinear method writes the equation as (DtDx + Dx4)τ·τ = 0 where D is the Hirota derivative; the N-soliton solution is τ = ∑μ=0,1 exp(∑j μjηj + ∑i<j</sub> μiμjAij). The Painlevé test (Weiss–Tabor–Carnevale, 1983) checks if all movable singularities are poles; passing the test is a strong indicator of integrability. The bi-Hamiltonian structure (Magri, 1978) provides infinite hierarchies of commuting flows: KdV = ∂xδH1 = (∂x3 + 4u∂x + 2ux)δH0 with H0 = ∫ u2/2 dx, H1 = ∫ (u3 − ux2/2) dx. The quantum inverse scattering method (Faddeev–Takhtajan, 1979) quantizes the IST, leading to the Yang–Baxter equation and quantum groups.

Stochastic Homogenization and Quantitative Ergodic Theory

Stochastic homogenization studies the asymptotic behavior of solutions to PDEs with random coefficients as the correlation length ε → 0. The prototypical equation is −∇·(a(x/ε,ω)∇uε) = f in a bounded domain D ⊂ ℝd with Dirichlet boundary conditions, where a(y,ω) is a stationary ergodic random field satisfying uniform ellipticity λ|ξ|2 ≤ ξ·a(y,ω)ξ ≤ Λ|ξ|2. The homogenized matrix ahom is defined via the corrector problem: for each direction ei, find the stationary solution φi of −∇·(a(∇φi+ei)) = 0 in ℝd with ∇φi stationary and 𝔼[∇φi] = 0. Then (ahom)ij = 𝔼[a(ei+∇φi)·ej]. The existence of the corrector in d ≥ 3 was proved by Kozlov (1979) and Papanicolaou–Varadhan (1981) using the subadditive ergodic theorem. In d = 2, the corrector grows logarithmically; the gradient ∇φ is stationary but φ itself is not. Quantitative stochastic homogenization (Gloria–Otto, 2011; Armstrong–Kuusi–Mourrat, 2017) establishes rates of convergence. The key quantity is the homogenization error uε − u0 − εφ(x/ε)·∇u0. Under finite range of dependence or spectral gap assumptions on the coefficient field, one has the optimal scaling 𝔼[‖uε − u0 − εφ(x/ε)·∇u0‖H12]1/2 ≲ ε for d ≥ 3, and ≲ ε|log ε|1/2 for d = 2. The proof uses the spectral gap inequality Var[F] ≤ 𝔼[∫ |∂F/∂a|2] for functionals of the coefficient field, and the Caccioppoli inequality for the corrector. The massive term approximation adds a zero-order term T−1φT to the corrector equation, yielding an approximate corrector with exponential decay of correlations; the limit T → ∞ recovers the true corrector. The two-scale expansion uε ≈ u0 + εφ(x/ε)·∇u0 is justified by energy estimates. For the parabolic equation ∂tuε − ∇·(a(x/ε)∇uε) = f, the homogenization error satisfies similar bounds. The random conductance model on ℤd with i.i.d. conductances ae ∈ [λ,Λ] is a discrete analogue; the corrector exists for d ≥ 2 (Biskup, 2011). The quantitative central limit theorem for the rescaled corrector εφ(x/ε) (Gloria–Otto, 2012; Duerinckx–Gloria, 2020) shows convergence to a Gaussian field with covariance given by the homogenized matrix. For nonlinear equations like −∇·(a(x/ε,ω,∇uε)) = f with monotone operators, the stochastic homogenization theory (Dal Maso–Modica, 1986; Schwab, 2018) uses the subadditive ergodic theorem on the energy functional. The stochastic two-scale convergence (Bourgeat–Mikelic–Wright, 1994) provides a compactness framework. The corrector equation can be solved numerically via the representative volume element (RVE) method with periodic boundary conditions on a box of size L; the error decays as L−d/2 under spectral gap assumptions. The multiscale finite element method (MsFEM) uses local basis functions solving the heterogeneous equation on coarse blocks; the oversampling technique (Hou–Wu, 1997) reduces resonance errors. For high-contrast media where a(y,ω) takes values in {1,κ} with κ ≫ 1, the homogenized behavior depends on the percolation structure of the high-conductivity phase; the critical scaling κ ∼ ε−2 leads to nonlocal homogenized equations (Zhikov, 2000).

  • Confusing the corrector equation for stochastic homogenization with the periodic cell problem: the corrector is defined on the whole space ℝd and is stationary, not periodic; in d = 2 it grows logarithmically and does not have a stationary representative.
  • Assuming the spectral gap inequality holds for all stationary ergodic coefficient fields: it requires finite range of dependence or a Poincaré inequality for the underlying probability measure; general ergodic fields may not satisfy it.
  • Neglecting the logarithmic correction in dimension d = 2: the homogenization error scales as ε|log ε|1/2, not ε, and the corrector itself is not stationary.
  • Applying the two-scale expansion uε ≈ u0 + εφ(x/ε)·∇u0 without the corrector gradient: the term φ(x/ε) alone is not bounded in H1; only ∇φ is stationary and bounded.
  • Confusing the massive term approximation with the true corrector: the massive corrector φT solves T−1φT − ∇·(a∇φT) = ∇·(aei) and has exponential decay, but the limit T → ∞ must be taken carefully to recover the true corrector.
  • Believing that stochastic homogenization always yields a local homogenized equation: for high-contrast media with critical scaling, the limit can be nonlocal or involve memory effects.
  • Overlooking the role of the subadditive ergodic theorem in proving existence of the homogenized matrix: it applies to the energy functional on large boxes, not directly to the corrector equation.

Regularity Structures for Singular Stochastic Partial Differential Equations

Martin Hairer’s theory of regularity structures, introduced in 2014, provides a systematic framework for giving meaning to stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) whose nonlinearities involve products of distributions that are classically ill‑defined. The motivating examples include the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation ∂th = ∂x2h + (∂xh)2 + ξ, the dynamical Φ43 model ∂tu = Δu − u3 + ξ, and the stochastic quantisation of the Φ44 field. In each case the space‑time white noise ξ is a distribution of regularity −(d+2)/2−ε, so the solution is expected to have regularity just above −1, making the cubic term or the square of the gradient a product of distributions that cannot be defined by classical means.

The core of the theory is a triple (A, T, G) called a regularity structure: an index set A ⊂ ℝ bounded from below, a family of abstract Taylor‑like polynomials T = (Tα)α∈A acting on a model space, and a structure group G of linear maps on the model space that mimic the re‑expansion of Taylor polynomials under translation. A concrete model (Π, Γ) realises the abstract symbols as actual distributions Πxτ and provides recentering maps Γxy satisfying algebraic identities. The solution is then sought as a modelled distribution f ∈ Dγ,η, a map from ℝd+1 into the model space that behaves like a jet of order γ with a possible singularity of order η at the initial time.

The reconstruction theorem guarantees that any modelled distribution f with sufficient regularity admits a unique distribution Rf whose local behaviour matches the abstract jet. The fixed‑point argument for the SPDE is carried out at the level of modelled distributions, where the nonlinearities become well‑defined algebraic operations on the model space. Renormalisation enters through the action of the structure group on the model: one modifies the canonical lift of the noise by a linear map M ∈ G (the BPHZ renormalisation) so that the resulting model converges as the noise is regularised. Hairer proved that for a large class of subcritical SPDEs there exists a unique renormalised model and hence a unique solution theory. Subsequent work by Chandra, Hairer, and others extended the framework to cover boundary conditions, quasi‑linear equations, and the connection with the paracontrolled calculus of Gubinelli, Imkeller, and Perkowski.

Key milestones include Hairer’s 2014 Fields Medal lecture, the 2015 proof of convergence for the KPZ equation by Hairer and Quastel, and the 2019 construction of the Φ43 measure via regularity structures by Chandra and Hairer. The theory has also been adapted to numerical analysis, yielding convergent discretisation schemes for singular SPDEs. Understanding regularity structures requires fluency with Hölder–Besov spaces, the algebraic structure of decorated trees, and the analytic estimates that control the reconstruction operator.

Partial Differential Equations on Graphs and Networks

Differential equations on graphs arise in two complementary settings: metric (quantum) graphs, where each edge is a one‑dimensional interval equipped with a differential operator, and discrete graphs, where the unknown lives on vertices and the Laplacian is a matrix. On a metric graph Γ the standard Hamiltonian is the negative Laplacian −d2/dx2 on each edge, coupled at vertices by boundary conditions that ensure self‑adjointness. The most common are Kirchhoff (continuity of the function and sum of outgoing derivatives zero), δ‑type (continuity with a jump in the derivative proportional to the function value), and δ′‑type (continuity of the derivative with a jump in the function). The spectral theory of such operators is governed by a secular equation det(I − S(k)) = 0, where S(k) is the unitary scattering matrix built from the vertex conditions and edge lengths. This formalism, developed by Kottos and Smilansky (1999) and systematised in the monograph of Berkolaiko and Kuchment (2013), links quantum chaos, photonic crystals, and nanoscale networks.

On discrete graphs the Laplacian ΔG acts on functions f : V → ℝ by (ΔGf)(v) = Σu∼v (f(v) − f(u)). Its spectrum encodes combinatorial properties: the multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue equals the number of connected components, and the spectral gap controls mixing times for random walks. The graph Fourier transform diagonalises ΔG via its eigenvectors, enabling the definition of diffusion, wave, and Schrödinger equations on networks. Mean‑field limits on large graphs lead to graphon PDEs, where the limit object is a symmetric measurable function W : [0,1]2 → [0,1] and the evolution becomes a nonlocal integro‑differential equation. Applications range from epidemic spreading on contact networks (the SIR model on graphs) to synchronisation of coupled oscillators (Kuramoto model on complex networks) and traffic flow on road networks modelled by hyperbolic conservation laws on metric graphs with junction conditions.

Recent advances include the proof of the Berry–Keating conjecture for certain quantum graphs, the development of spectral minimal partitions on graphs, and the analysis of controllability for the wave equation on networks using the Hilbert Uniqueness Method. The interplay between the discrete and metric settings is captured by the notion of a quantum graph as a limit of thin neighbourhoods of a graph (the “fat graph” approximation), where the transverse modes are frozen and the effective dynamics is governed by the one‑dimensional Laplacian with effective vertex conditions derived from the geometry of the junction.

Identifying Self‑Adjoint Vertex Conditions
A metric graph has three edges of equal length meeting at a single vertex. Which of the following vertex conditions yields a self‑adjoint Laplacian? (a) Dirichlet on all three edges. (b) Continuity of the function and sum of outward derivatives equal to zero. (c) Continuity of the function and sum of outward derivatives equal to α times the common value, with α ∈ ℝ. (d) No condition on the function, but the sum of outward derivatives is zero.
Condition (b) is the standard Kirchhoff (or Neumann) condition; it is symmetric and makes the Laplacian self‑adjoint. Condition (c) is the δ‑type condition, also self‑adjoint for any real α. Condition (a) decouples the edges and gives a direct sum of Dirichlet Laplacians, which is self‑adjoint but does not couple the edges at the vertex. Condition (d) lacks continuity, so the domain is not a subset of H1 and the operator is not self‑adjoint. The correct answer for a coupled self‑adjoint vertex is (b) or (c).
Exam‑Day Strategy for Graph PDEs
First decide whether the problem is set on a metric graph (differential operator on edges) or a discrete graph (matrix Laplacian). For metric graphs, recall the classification of self‑adjoint vertex conditions: they correspond to Lagrangian subspaces of the boundary value space. For discrete graphs, use the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices and the variational characterisation of eigenvalues. In both cases, the key is to translate the physical junction or coupling rule into a precise algebraic condition on the boundary values or on the matrix entries.

Mean Field Games: The Master Equation and Long‑Time Behavior

The master equation, introduced by Lions in his 2007 Collège de France lectures, is a partial differential equation posed on the space of probability measures 𝒫(ℝd) that describes the value function of a representative player in a mean field game (MFG) as a function of the current distribution of all players. For a first‑order MFG with Hamiltonian H(p) and coupling F(m), the master equation reads ∂tU(t,x,m) + H(∇xU) = F(m) + ∫ℝd ∇mU(t,x,m)(y) · ∇pH(∇yU(t,y,m)) dm(y), where ∇mU denotes the Lions derivative with respect to the measure argument. For second‑order MFGs a diffusion term ΔxU and a second‑order measure derivative appear.

Well‑posedness of the master equation relies on structural monotonicity conditions. The Lasry–Lions monotonicity assumption ∫ (F(m1) − F(m2)) d(m1 − m2) ≥ 0 guarantees uniqueness of classical solutions. When the Hamiltonian is convex and the coupling is displacement convex, the master equation can be interpreted as a Hamilton–Jacobi equation on the Wasserstein space, and the method of characteristics yields a flow of measures that coincides with the MFG equilibrium. Cardaliaguet, Delarue, Lasry, and Lions (2019) established global existence and uniqueness of classical solutions for a broad class of first‑order MFGs under a monotonicity condition on the Hamiltonian and a regularity condition on the initial data.

Long‑time behavior is governed by the ergodic (or stationary) master equation, obtained by dropping the time derivative and adding an unknown constant λ (the ergodic constant). The turnpike property states that the time‑dependent solution converges exponentially fast to the stationary solution as t → ∞, provided the monotonicity is strict. This has been quantified using the Lasry–Lions monotonicity and the displacement convexity of the Hamiltonian. Applications include crowd motion models where the long‑time limit describes the steady‑state density of pedestrians, and financial mathematics where the ergodic constant represents the long‑run average cost of optimal execution in a market with many indistinguishable agents.

Geometric Analysis: The Yamabe Problem and Prescribed Scalar Curvature

The Yamabe problem asks whether, on a compact Riemannian manifold (Mn, g) of dimension n ≥ 3, one can find a conformal metric g̃ = u4/(n−2)g with constant scalar curvature. The equation for the conformal factor u > 0 is the Yamabe equation −cnΔgu + Rgu = R̃ u(n+2)/(n−2), where cn = 4(n−1)/(n−2) and Rg, R̃ are the scalar curvatures of g and g̃. Yamabe’s 1960 claim of a solution contained a gap in the use of the Sobolev embedding; Trudinger (1968) corrected it for non‑positive Yamabe invariant, Aubin (1976) solved the case of non‑locally conformally flat manifolds with positive invariant, and Schoen (1984) completed the proof using the positive mass theorem from general relativity.

The Yamabe invariant Y(M,[g]) = infg̃∈[g] (∫ Rg̃ dvolg̃) / (volg̃)(n−2)/n is a conformal invariant that determines the sign of the constant scalar curvature achievable. The prescribed scalar curvature problem asks: given a function f on M, does there exist a conformal metric with scalar curvature f? The Kazdan–Warner obstruction (1975) shows that on the sphere Sn any such f must satisfy ∫Sn ∇f · X dvol = 0 for every conformal Killing field X. For manifolds with negative Yamabe invariant, any negative function can be realised (Kazdan–Warner 1975). On the positive side, the problem is variational: one seeks critical points of the Yamabe functional. Blow‑up analysis of Palais–Smale sequences reveals that loss of compactness occurs only through bubbling of solutions concentrating at points, leading to the classification of blow‑up profiles by Schoen (1989) and Li–Zhu (1999).

Recent breakthroughs include the compactness theorem for Yamabe metrics by Brendle and Marques (2009) in dimensions 3 ≤ n ≤ 24, and the resolution of the compactness conjecture for locally conformally flat manifolds by Khuri, Marques, and Schoen (2015). The prescribed scalar curvature problem on manifolds with boundary leads to the Escobar problem, where the boundary mean curvature is also prescribed. These results illustrate the deep interplay between nonlinear elliptic PDE, geometric measure theory, and global analysis.

Inverse Problems: The Calderón Problem and Electrical Impedance Tomography

The Calderón problem, posed by Alberto Calderón in 1980, asks whether the electrical conductivity γ(x) inside a bounded domain Ω ⊂ ℝn (n ≥ 2) can be uniquely determined from the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann (DtN) map Λγ : H1/2(∂Ω) → H−1/2(∂Ω), which sends a boundary voltage f to the resulting current flux γ ∂νu|∂Ω, where u solves ∇·(γ∇u) = 0 in Ω with u|∂Ω = f. This is the mathematical model of electrical impedance tomography (EIT), a non‑invasive medical imaging technique.

In dimensions n ≥ 3, Sylvester and Uhlmann (1987) proved global uniqueness for C∞ conductivities using complex geometrical optics (CGO) solutions of the form u = ex·ζ(1 + ψζ) with ζ ∈ ℂn, ζ·ζ = 0. Their construction reduces the problem to the injectivity of the attenuated X‑ray transform. For n = 2, the problem is more delicate because CGO solutions require a different ansatz; Nachman (1996) established global uniqueness for γ ∈ W1,p, p > 2, using a ∂̄‑equation approach. Astala and Päivärinta (2006) extended uniqueness to merely bounded measurable conductivities in 2D via quasiconformal mappings.

Stability estimates are necessarily weak: Alessandrini (1988) proved a logarithmic stability estimate ‖γ1 − γ2‖L∞ ≤ C |log ‖Λγ1 − Λγ2‖|−η for some η > 0, and Mandache (2001) showed that logarithmic stability is optimal in dimensions n ≥ 3. Partial data problems, where the DtN map is known only on a subset of the boundary, have been solved using Carleman estimates (Kenig–Sjöstrand–Uhlmann 2007). The anisotropic Calderón problem, where γ is a Riemannian metric, reduces to the determination of a manifold from its boundary distance function (boundary rigidity), a problem solved in the generic case by Stefanov, Uhlmann, and Vasy (2016).

Numerical reconstruction algorithms for EIT include the D‑bar method (Knudsen–Lassas–Mueller–Siltanen 2009) which directly implements the Nachman ∂̄‑reconstruction, and iterative regularisation methods such as the Gauss–Newton scheme with total variation penalisation. The Calderón problem remains a benchmark for the interplay between microlocal analysis, geometric optics, and practical imaging.

  • Confusing regularity structures with rough paths: regularity structures handle a broader class of singular SPDEs, while rough paths focus on integration against rough signals.
  • Assuming all vertex conditions on metric graphs are self‑adjoint: only those satisfying certain symmetry and Lagrangian conditions yield self‑adjoint Laplacians.
  • Treating the master equation as a standard PDE on Euclidean space: it lives on the infinite‑dimensional Wasserstein space and requires L‑derivatives.
  • Overlooking the Kazdan–Warner obstruction when solving prescribed scalar curvature: not every function can be realised as scalar curvature of a conformal metric.
  • Believing the Calderón problem has Lipschitz stability in dimensions ≥3: the best known stability is logarithmic, and counterexamples show no better stability.

Variational Inequalities and Obstacle Problems

Variational inequalities extend the classical calculus of variations to problems where the admissible set is a closed convex subset of a Hilbert space rather than the whole space. The prototype is the obstacle problem: given a domain Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ, a boundary datum g, and an obstacle ψ ≤ g on ∂Ω, find u ∈ K = {v ∈ H¹(Ω) : v ≥ ψ a.e., v = g on ∂Ω} minimizing the Dirichlet energy ∫Ω |∇v|² dx. The Euler–Lagrange condition yields the variational inequality ∫Ω ∇u·∇(v−u) dx ≥ 0 for all v ∈ K. This formulation, introduced by Stampacchia (1964) and Lions–Stampacchia (1967), covers unilateral contact problems in elasticity, filtration through porous media, and American option pricing in finance.

Regularity theory for the obstacle problem is a cornerstone of geometric measure theory. Caffarelli (1977) proved that the free boundary ∂{u > ψ} is C¹,α except on a singular set of Hausdorff dimension at most n−2. Later, Monneau (2003) and Figalli–Serra (2019) refined the stratification of singular points. The penalization method approximates the inequality by adding a term (1/ε)(u−ψ)⁻ to the energy, leading to a semilinear elliptic equation whose solutions converge to the variational solution as ε → 0. This technique underlies many numerical schemes, including finite element methods with adaptive mesh refinement near the free boundary.

Beyond the scalar obstacle, systems of variational inequalities arise in multi‑phase flow and elastoplasticity. The theory of monotone operators (Brezis, 1973) provides existence and uniqueness for evolution variational inequalities of the form u′(t) + Au(t) ∋ f(t), where A is a maximal monotone operator. The Crandall–Liggett theorem generates a nonlinear contraction semigroup, enabling long‑time analysis. In optimal control, the state equation is often a variational inequality; the adjoint equation then involves a complementarity system, leading to M‑stationarity conditions (Mignot, 1976). These results are essential for shape optimization with unilateral constraints.

Hysteresis and Rate‑Independent Systems

Hysteresis models capture memory effects where the output depends on the history of the input, not merely its current value. The mathematical framework of rate‑independent systems, developed by Mielke, Roubíček, and others (2005‑2015), formulates evolution as an energetic solution (u(t), z(t)) satisfying a stability condition and an energy balance. The state z belongs to a set of internal variables (e.g., plastic strain, magnetization), and the dissipation potential is positively homogeneous of degree one, ensuring rate independence. The classical play and stop operators of Krasnosel’skiĭ–Pokrovskiĭ (1970) provide a representation of scalar hysteresis loops; their superposition yields the Preisach model, widely used in ferromagnetism and soil mechanics.

Mathematically, the energetic formulation replaces the differential inclusion ∂R(ż) + DₓE(t, u, z) ∋ 0 (with R the dissipation potential) by a global stability condition E(t, u(t), z(t)) ≤ E(t, u(t), z̃) + R(z̃−z(t)) for all admissible z̃, together with the energy equality E(t, u(t), z(t)) + Diss_R(z; [0,t]) = E(0, u(0), z(0)) + ∫₀ᵗ ∂ₜE(s, u(s), z(s)) ds. Existence of energetic solutions follows from a time‑discretization scheme and Helly’s selection principle, provided the energy functional is lower semicontinuous and the dissipation distance satisfies the triangle inequality. Uniqueness generally fails; instead, one studies the set of all energetic solutions and selects physically relevant ones via vanishing viscosity or viscous regularization.

Applications include damage models in concrete (Frémond, 2002), ferroelectric switching (Krejčí, 1996), and earthquake fault dynamics (Rice, 1993). In the context of PDEs, coupling a rate‑independent flow rule with a linear elastic equilibrium yields a quasi‑static evolution variational inequality. The mathematical analysis combines tools from convex analysis, BV functions, and Γ‑convergence. Recent work by Mielke–Rossi–Savaré (2013) introduced the notion of balanced viscosity solutions to capture jump behavior at discontinuities, bridging the gap between energetic and viscous solutions.

Worked Example: Energetic Solution for a Scalar Play Operator
Consider the scalar play operator with threshold r > 0: given input w ∈ W¹,¹(0,T), find output z satisfying |z(t)| ≤ r, (z(t)−w(t))(ż(t)−ẇ(t)) = 0, and z(0) = max(min(w(0), r), −r). Show that the pair (w, z) satisfies the energetic formulation with energy E(t, w, z) = ½|w−z|² and dissipation distance R(z̃−z) = r|z̃−z|.
The stability condition requires ½|w−z|² ≤ ½|w−z̃|² + r|z̃−z| for all z̃ with |z̃| ≤ r. This is equivalent to the subdifferential inclusion 0 ∈ z−w + r ∂|·|(z̃−z), which holds exactly when |z| ≤ r and (z−w)(z̃−z) ≥ −r|z̃−z|. The energy balance follows from integrating the identity d/dt ½|w−z|² = (w−z)(ẇ−ż) and using the complementarity condition (z−w)(ż−ẇ)=0, yielding Diss_R = r∫|ż| dt = ∫ r|ż| dt. Thus the play operator is an energetic solution of the rate‑independent system.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks to verify an energetic formulation, first write down the energy functional and dissipation distance explicitly. Check the stability inequality by testing against admissible perturbations; often convexity of the energy reduces this to a subdifferential condition. For the energy balance, differentiate the energy along the trajectory and use the complementarity or flow rule to identify the dissipation term. Remember that rate independence implies the dissipation distance is positively homogeneous of degree one.

Nonlocal Diffusion Equations: Fractional Laplacian and Applications

Nonlocal diffusion replaces the classical Laplacian Δ by the fractional Laplacian (−Δ)ˢ with s ∈ (0,1), defined via the singular integral (−Δ)ˢ u(x) = Cₙ,ₛ P.V. ∫ℝⁿ (u(x)−u(y))/|x−y|ⁿ⁺²ˢ dy. This operator arises as the generator of a symmetric α‑stable Lévy process and models anomalous diffusion where particles perform long jumps. The Dirichlet problem (−Δ)ˢ u = f in Ω, u = g in ℝⁿ\Ω, is well‑posed in the fractional Sobolev space Hˢ(ℝⁿ) with the Gagliardo seminorm [u]ₛ² = ∫∫ (u(x)−u(y))²/|x−y|ⁿ⁺²ˢ dx dy. Caffarelli–Silvestre (2007) introduced the extension problem: (−Δ)ˢ u = f in Ω is equivalent to a degenerate elliptic equation div(y¹⁻²ˢ ∇U) = 0 in Ω × (0,∞) with U(x,0) = u(x), enabling the use of local PDE techniques.

Regularity theory for nonlocal equations parallels the local case but with distinct scaling. Solutions to (−Δ)ˢ u = f with f ∈ L^∞ are C2s−ε for s > ½ and C1,2s−1−ε for s < ½ (Silvestre, 2007). The boundary behavior exhibits a characteristic singularity: u(x) ≈ dist(x,∂Ω)ˢ near the boundary, unlike the linear behavior for s = 1. This influences numerical methods; finite element approximations on graded meshes recover optimal convergence rates (Acosta–Borthagaray, 2017). The fractional heat equation ∂ₜu + (−Δ)ˢ u = 0 admits a fundamental solution given by the symmetric stable density, which decays like |x|⁻ⁿ⁻²ˢ at infinity, leading to heavy‑tailed distributions.

Applications span finance (option pricing under Lévy processes), image processing (nonlocal means denoising), and ecology (long‑range dispersal). In the fractional obstacle problem, the free boundary regularity was established by Caffarelli–Salsa–Silvestre (2008), showing C¹,α regularity away from a lower‑dimensional singular set. The fractional porous medium equation ∂ₜu + (−Δ)ˢ (u^m) = 0 (m > 1) exhibits finite speed of propagation for m > 1, contrasting with the infinite speed for the linear fractional heat equation. Recent work on nonlocal minimal surfaces (Caffarelli–Roquejoffre–Savin, 2010) connects the fractional perimeter functional to the fractional Laplacian, opening a geometric measure theory for nonlocal operators.

Inverse Problems for Parabolic Equations: Stability and Reconstruction

Inverse problems for parabolic equations aim to recover unknown coefficients, sources, or initial data from partial observations. The classical sideways heat equation (reconstructing initial temperature from final data) is severely ill‑posed: the solution operator is compact with exponentially decaying singular values, leading to logarithmic stability estimates. For the heat equation ∂ₜu − Δu = 0 in Ω × (0,T), the map u₀ ↦ u(·,T) is analytic, and the optimal stability is of logarithmic type: ‖u₀‖L² ≤ C |log ‖u(·,T)‖L²|−α (Mandelbaum, 1999). This contrasts with elliptic inverse problems where Lipschitz stability can hold under geometric conditions (e.g., Calderón problem with partial data).

Carleman estimates are the primary tool for proving uniqueness and stability in parabolic inverse problems. For the heat equation with unknown potential q(x), the estimate ∫ e2sφ (s³|u|² + s|∇u|²) dx dt ≤ C ∫ e2sφ |∂ₜu − Δu + qu|² dx dt, with a suitable weight φ, yields uniqueness from lateral boundary measurements (Imanuvilov–Yamamoto, 2001). For coefficient identification, the Bukhgeim–Klibanov method (1981) uses a Carleman weight depending on time to prove Lipschitz stability for the diffusion coefficient a(x) from boundary flux data, provided a is known near the boundary. Recent advances include the use of complex geometrical optics solutions for parabolic equations (Kian–Oksanen, 2019) and the development of reconstruction algorithms based on the Neumann series (Klibanov–Santosa, 1991).

Numerical reconstruction typically employs regularization: Tikhonov regularization with a Sobolev penalty, iterative methods like Landweber iteration, or Bayesian inversion with Gaussian priors. The choice of regularization parameter is critical; the discrepancy principle and L‑curve criterion are standard. For nonlinear parabolic inverse problems (e.g., identifying the reaction term in a Fisher–KPP equation), the forward map is Fréchet differentiable, allowing Gauss–Newton methods. The convergence analysis relies on the tangential cone condition and the smoothing property of the parabolic semigroup. In practice, data are often sparse in time; compressed sensing techniques combined with parabolic smoothing have been used to recover spatially varying coefficients from few temporal snapshots (Clason–Klibanov, 2020).

Data Assimilation and Model Reduction for PDEs

Data assimilation integrates noisy observations with a dynamical model to produce an optimal state estimate. In the PDE context, the model is an infinite‑dimensional evolution equation, and observations are linear functionals of the state (e.g., pointwise measurements, satellite radiances). The variational approach (4D‑Var) minimizes a cost functional J(u₀) = ½‖u₀−u_b‖B⁻¹² + ½∑k=1^N ‖H u(t_k)−y_k‖R_k⁻¹², where u_b is a background state, B the background error covariance, H the observation operator, and R_k the observation error covariance. The gradient of J is computed via the adjoint equation, requiring the solution of the forward and adjoint PDEs. The well‑posedness of 4D‑Var for nonlinear PDEs (e.g., Navier–Stokes) relies on the controllability of the linearized system and the observability inequality (Fursikov–Imanuvilov, 1996).

Sequential methods, such as the Kalman filter, propagate the error covariance forward in time. For high‑dimensional systems, the ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF) approximates the covariance by a sample covariance from an ensemble of model states. The EnKF has been rigorously analyzed for linear parabolic SPDEs (Law–Stuart–Zygalakis, 2015), showing convergence as the ensemble size increases. Localization and inflation techniques mitigate sampling errors. Particle filters provide a non‑Gaussian alternative but suffer from weight collapse in high dimensions; hybrid particle‑ensemble methods are an active research area.

Model reduction aims to replace the high‑fidelity PDE by a low‑dimensional surrogate. Proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) constructs a reduced basis from snapshots of the full solution; the Galerkin projection yields a system of ODEs for the modal coefficients. Error bounds depend on the Kolmogorov n‑width of the solution manifold. For parametric PDEs, the reduced basis method (RBM) combines a greedy algorithm with an a posteriori error estimator based on the dual norm of the residual. The empirical interpolation method (EIM) handles nonlinear terms by approximating them in a reduced space. Recent developments include physics‑informed neural networks (PINNs) that embed the PDE residual into the loss function, and operator learning frameworks (DeepONet, Fourier Neural Operator) that learn the solution map directly from data. These data‑driven surrogates are increasingly used within assimilation cycles to accelerate forecasting.
