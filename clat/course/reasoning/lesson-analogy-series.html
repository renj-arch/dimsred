Learning Objectives

  • Understand the fundamental concepts of Analogy & Series
  • Apply key formulas and techniques to solve problems

Analogy and Series questions are the backbone of the Logical Reasoning section in competitive exams like CLAT. These problems test your ability to identify underlying relationships between words or numbers and detect hidden patterns within sequences. Mastering these skills requires a shift from rote memorization to active pattern recognition, allowing you to solve complex puzzles under time pressure.

Analogy: Understanding Relationships

An Analogy is a question type that asks you to identify the relationship between two given terms and apply that same logic to a pair of unknown terms. The core principle here is consistency; if "A is to B as C is to D," the logical connection must be identical in both halves.

The most common relationships tested include synonyms, antonyms, part-to-whole, cause-and-effect, and functional associations. For instance, a "Heart" is to "Body" as a "Wheel" is to "Car." This follows a part-to-whole pattern. Conversely, "Hot" is to "Cold" as "Day" is to "Night," which follows an antonym or opposite relationship.

Examiners typically present these in the format A : B :: C : D. Your task is to find the missing term (D) when A, B, and C are provided, or to identify the pair that does not fit the established group. The key to solving these quickly is to categorize the relationship immediately upon reading the first pair.

Series Completion: Decoding Patterns

A Number Series presents a sequence of digits where you must determine the rule governing the progression and find the missing number or the next term in the list. The fundamental rule is that every series follows a mathematical operation, such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, or a combination thereof.

The most basic pattern is an arithmetic progression where a constant value is added or subtracted at each step. For example, in the sequence 2, 5, 8, 11, the rule is "+3." More complex series involve alternating operations, such as adding 2 and then multiplying by 2. Another frequent pattern involves square numbers (1, 4, 9, 16) or cube numbers (1, 8, 27), where the position of the number in the sequence dictates its value.

Letter Series follow similar logic but use alphabetical positions. The alphabet is treated as a continuous line from A to Z. Patterns often involve skipping letters (e.g., every second letter: B, D, F) or moving backward and forward based on a specific count. Sometimes, the pattern involves reversing the alphabet order (Z to A), which requires knowing that A=1 and Z=26.

Odd One Out: Finding the Discrepancy

The Odd One Out question presents a set of four items where three share a common characteristic or follow a specific rule, while one item deviates from this norm. The goal is to identify that single outlier based on logic, category, or mathematical property.

This section tests your ability to group information effectively. You must look for the "common thread" among three items. For example, if you are given {Apple, Banana, Carrot, Grape}, the odd one out is Carrot because it is a root vegetable while the others are fruits. In number sets, the outlier might be the only even number in a group of odd numbers, or the only prime number.

Examiners often use this to test lateral thinking. The rule might not be obvious immediately; you may need to look at the shape of the letters, the sound they make, or the mathematical properties of the numbers. Always check for multiple possibilities before committing to an answer, but ensure your chosen outlier is the one that breaks the strongest logical link.

Technique: The Relationship Matrix
Question: "Book" : "Library" :: ? : "Museum"
Solution:
First, analyze the relationship between "Book" and "Library." A library is a place where books are stored. This is a "Place of Storage" relationship.
Next, apply this rule to the second pair. We need an item that is stored in a Museum.
The missing term must be an object found inside a museum. Common options might be "Painting," "Sculpture," or "Artifact."
If the options were: A) Car, B) Painting, C) Shoe, D) Chair.
"Car" is stored in a garage. "Shoe" is stored in a closet. "Chair" is furniture for sitting.
Therefore, "Painting" is the correct answer because it fits the "Place of Storage" pattern perfectly.
Technique: The Difference Method
Question: Find the missing number in the series: 10, 16, 24, 34, ?
Solution:
Step 1: Calculate the difference between consecutive numbers.
16 - 10 = +6
24 - 16 = +8
34 - 24 = +10

Step 2: Observe the pattern in the differences. The differences are increasing by 2 each time (6, 8, 10).
Step 3: Apply the next difference to the last known number.
Next difference should be +12.
Calculation: 34 + 12 = 46.
The missing number is 46.

Common Traps

  • Assuming a simple pattern: Students often jump to the conclusion that a series is just adding or subtracting a constant. If the differences change, you must look for second-order patterns (like the difference of differences).
  • Ignoring letter positions: In letter series, students sometimes treat letters as isolated symbols rather than numbers on a scale (A=1, B=2). This leads to errors in alternating forward-backward movements.
  • Misidentifying the Odd One Out category: A common mistake is focusing on surface-level attributes like color or shape when the rule is actually mathematical. For example, in {4, 9, 16, 20}, one might think 20 is odd because it doesn't look like a square number, but the rule is strictly "perfect squares."
  • Overthinking analogies: Sometimes students try to find complex scientific relationships when a simple synonym or antonym relationship is intended. Stick to the most direct logical link first.

Memory Tricks

  • The "Skip Count" Mnemonic: For series like A, C, E, G (every second letter), remember the phrase "Every Other." If you skip two letters (A, D, G), think of the song "Skip Skip Skip."
  • Square Number Visualization: Memorize squares up to 12. Visualize a grid: 3x3 is 9 dots, 4x4 is 16 dots. This helps instantly in series involving 1, 4, 9, 16, 25.
  • The "Three-Of-Four" Rule for Odd One Out: Never look for the odd one out until you have identified a common trait shared by at least three items. If no group of three shares a clear trait, re-evaluate your initial assumptions about the category.
  • Analogy Keywords: When solving analogies, associate specific keywords with relationship types: "Place" (Library, School), "Opposite" (Hot/Cold), "Tool" (Hammer/Nail), "Part" (Wheel/Car).
Exam-Day Strategy
Strategy for the Exam Hall:
Do not spend more than two minutes on any single analogy or series question. If you cannot spot a pattern quickly, mark it and move on to save time for easier questions.
For Odd One Out, scan the options first; sometimes the answer is obvious if you look at the choices rather than just the stem.
Always double-check your arithmetic in number series before bubbling your answer. A simple addition error can cost you marks even if your logic was perfect.

Multi‑Step Analogies: Chained Relationships

Standard analogies test a single relational link such as synonym, antonym, part‑whole, or tool‑user. Advanced items chain two or more distinct relations, requiring the solver to identify each link before mapping the final term. A typical chain might read “Seed : Plant :: Egg : ?” where the first pair follows a “origin‑to‑mature‑form” relation and the second pair demands the same logical step applied to a different domain. The difficulty rises when the chain mixes relational types: for example, “Keyboard : Input :: Monitor : Output :: Processor : ?” Here the first two pairs establish a device‑function pattern (input device, output device) and the third asks for the central processing unit that mediates between them. The correct answer is “Central Processing Unit” or simply “CPU” because it performs the processing function that links input to output.

To solve chained analogies systematically, break the stem into ordered pairs and label each relation with a keyword (origin, function, sequence, degree). Write the keywords in a column; then verify that the same keyword sequence applies to the answer choices. If a choice satisfies the first keyword but fails the second, discard it. This method prevents the common error of matching only the surface similarity of the first pair.

Examiners often embed a distractor that shares the first relation but violates the second. In the example above, “Mouse” is an input device like a keyboard, so it satisfies the first keyword (input) but fails the second (output). Recognising that the chain requires a shift from peripheral to core component eliminates such traps.

Practice with three‑step chains drawn from diverse domains — biology, computer science, linguistics — builds the mental flexibility to spot the underlying keyword sequence quickly. Keep a personal log of keyword patterns you encounter; over time the list becomes a reusable template for any new chain.

Hybrid Series: Mixed Alphanumeric Patterns

Hybrid series combine letters and numbers in a single progression, forcing the test‑taker to track two independent cycles simultaneously. A classic form alternates a letter shift with a numeric operation, e.g. A2, C5, E9, G14, ?. The letters advance by two positions each step (A→C→E→G→I). The numbers follow the pattern +3, +4, +5, so the next increment is +6, giving 20. The answer is I20.

More complex hybrids may embed a secondary pattern inside the numeric component, such as prime gaps or triangular numbers, while the alphabetic component follows a reverse‑skip or a vowel‑consonant toggle. Consider Z1, X3, V6, T10, ?. Letters move backward by two (Z→X→V→T→R). Numbers increase by successive integers: +2, +3, +4, so the next increase is +5, yielding 15. The missing term is R15.

When a hybrid series appears, isolate the two streams immediately. Write the letter positions (A=1 … Z=26) in one column and the numeric values in another. Compute differences for each column independently. If one column shows a constant difference and the other shows a second‑order difference, you have identified the governing rules. Never attempt to find a single arithmetic rule that mixes letters and numbers; the exam designer intentionally separates the logic.

Edge cases include series where the letter cycle wraps (Y, A, C…) or where the numeric cycle resets after a modulus (e.g., modulo 12 for clock‑style series). In wrap‑around cases, treat the alphabet as a circular buffer of length 26; in modulus cases, apply the modulus after each arithmetic step. Recognising these boundary conditions prevents off‑by‑one errors that are a frequent source of lost marks.

Worked Example: Hybrid Series with Wrap‑Around
Find the next term in the series: Y5, A9, C14, E20, ?

Step 1: Separate letters and numbers.

Letters: Y, A, C, E …
Numbers: 5, 9, 14, 20 …

Step 2: Analyse the letter stream. Convert to positions: Y=25, A=1, C=3, E=5. The sequence moves forward by 2 positions on a circular alphabet (25→1→3→5). Next letter position = 5+2 = 7 → G.

Step 3: Analyse the number stream. Differences: 9‑5=4, 14‑9=5, 20‑14=6. The increments increase by 1 each step. Next increment = 7. Next number = 20+7 = 27.

Step 4: Combine results → G27.

Exam‑Hall Heuristic for Hybrid Series
When a hybrid series appears, spend the first 15 seconds writing two parallel columns (letters, numbers). If either column yields a clear pattern within three terms, lock that pattern and project forward. Do not waste time searching for a single combined formula; the test maker designs the two streams to be independent. If both columns remain ambiguous after three terms, mark the question and return later — hybrid series are high‑variance items that often resolve after a brief mental reset.

Conditional Odd One Out: Rule Switching

Standard odd‑one‑out questions present a single governing rule shared by three items. Advanced variants introduce a conditional rule that changes based on a property of the items themselves. For instance, the set {2, 3, 5, 9} appears to be “prime numbers” with 9 as the outlier. However, if the instruction reads “Select the number that does not satisfy the rule: ‘If the number is even, it must be a multiple of 4; if odd, it must be prime’”, then 2 (even but not a multiple of 4) becomes the correct outlier, while 9 (odd and non‑prime) also violates the rule — but only one item can be the answer. The examiner expects you to test each item against the conditional statement and identify the unique violator.

Another common conditional structure uses a “majority property” that flips for a minority subset. Example: {Circle, Square, Triangle, Pentagon}. Three are polygons with straight edges; the circle is the outlier under a “straight‑edge” rule. But if the stem adds “All shapes have interior angles summing to 360° except one”, the circle (no interior angles) and the triangle (180°) both break the rule. The unique outlier is the triangle because the circle’s angle sum is undefined, not 360°, making the triangle the only shape with a defined sum that differs from 360°.

To crack conditional odd‑one‑out, first paraphrase the condition in your own words. Then create a truth table: list each option, evaluate the antecedent (if‑clause), then the consequent (then‑clause). Mark each as “satisfies” or “violates”. The answer is the sole option with a “violates” mark. If more than one violates, re‑read the condition for hidden qualifiers such as “exactly one”, “only”, or “unless”.

Examiners also embed a “meta‑rule” where the odd one out is the item that does not share the same *type* of rule as the others. For example, three items follow a mathematical rule (perfect squares) and the fourth follows a linguistic rule (starts with a vowel). The outlier is the linguistic item because the dominant rule class is mathematical. Recognising the rule class hierarchy is essential for these higher‑order items.

Reverse Engineering: Working Backwards from Options

When the stem presents a complex analogy or series and the answer choices are short, reverse engineering can be faster than forward solving. The technique: treat each option as the missing term, insert it into the stem, and test whether the completed structure exhibits a coherent, simple pattern. This is especially powerful for analogy questions where the relationship is ambiguous but the options are concrete nouns.

Consider the analogy “Manuscript : Archive :: ? : Museum” with options A) Painting, B) Sculpture, C) Artifact, D) Fossil. Instead of debating whether the relation is “place of storage”, “place of display”, or “place of preservation”, test each option. “Painting : Museum” fits display and preservation. “Sculpture : Museum” also fits. “Artifact : Museum” fits preservation and display. “Fossil : Museum” fits but fossils are more commonly associated with natural history museums. The simplest, most general relation that covers three options is “object curated by the institution”. Since three options satisfy it, the odd one out among the options is the one that least fits the dominant category — here, Fossil is the least typical for a general museum. However, if the question asks for the *best* completion, choose the option that aligns with the most frequent collocation in standard usage: “Artifact” is the hypernym covering paintings, sculptures, and fossils, making it the strongest answer.

For series, plug each numeric option into the gap and compute the resulting differences. The option that yields a consistent difference pattern (constant, arithmetic progression of differences, geometric ratio) is the answer. This avoids solving a quadratic or higher‑order recurrence under time pressure.

Reverse engineering carries a risk: an option may accidentally create a plausible pattern that the examiner did not intend. Guard against this by checking that the pattern you discover is the *simplest* (Occam’s razor) and that it uses only operations explicitly supported by the visible terms (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, exponentiation). If a pattern requires an obscure function (e.g., factorial of digit sum), discard it.

Time‑Pressure Heuristics: Pattern Recognition Under Constraints

High‑stakes tests impose strict per‑question time limits, often 60–90 seconds for logical reasoning items. Developing a set of heuristics that yield a “good enough” answer quickly can raise overall scores more than perfect accuracy on a few items. The first heuristic is “Two‑Term Lock”: examine only the first two terms of a series or the first pair of an analogy. If they reveal a clear, common relation (e.g., +2, ×3, synonym), assume the relation holds unless a later term contradicts it. This works because test designers usually establish the rule early and then extend it.

The second heuristic is “Option Elimination by Property”. Before solving, scan the answer choices for a property that three share and one lacks (e.g., three are even, one odd; three are nouns, one verb). If such a property exists, the odd one out is often the answer for odd‑one‑out questions, or the unique property signals the correct analogy completion. This pre‑scan takes ≤5 seconds and can solve up to 20 % of items without any calculation.

The third heuristic is “Anchor‑Term Substitution”. In analogies with an abstract first term (e.g., “Justice : Court :: ? : Hospital”), replace the abstract term with a concrete exemplar (“Judge : Court :: Doctor : Hospital”). The concrete mapping often reveals the relational template (professional : workplace). Then map back to the abstract domain. This reduces cognitive load by leveraging familiar real‑world pairs.

Finally, practice “Timed Drill Sets” of 20 mixed items with a 25‑minute limit. Record which heuristics succeeded and which failed. Over multiple drills, you will internalise the decision threshold: if a pattern is not evident within 30 seconds, mark and move on. This disciplined approach prevents the “sunk‑cost” trap where a single difficult item consumes time needed for several easy ones.

  • Assuming a single relation in a chained analogy when two distinct relations are required.
  • Merging the alphabetic and numeric streams in a hybrid series instead of treating them independently.
  • Overlooking conditional qualifiers (“if”, “unless”, “only”) in odd‑one‑out stems, leading to multiple apparent outliers.
  • Accepting a reverse‑engineered pattern that uses operations not supported by the visible terms (e.g., factorial, digit‑sum).
  • Applying a heuristic beyond its validity window — e.g., using “Two‑Term Lock” on a series that deliberately changes rule after the third term.
  • Failing to verify that the chosen odd one out breaks the strongest logical link rather than a superficial attribute.
  • Spending more than the allocated time on a single item because the pattern feels “almost there”, causing cascading time loss.

Recursive Analogies: Self‑Referential Structures

Recursive analogies embed the relational pattern inside the terms themselves, so the second pair mirrors the internal structure of the first pair rather than sharing an external category. A classic instance is “Sentence : Clause :: Paragraph : Sentence”. The relation is “whole contains part”, but the part in the first pair (clause) is itself a whole for the next level (sentence). Recognising this nesting prevents the common error of treating the analogy as a simple taxonomic chain. The mechanism works by identifying the hierarchical depth: level 0 (sentence) contains level 1 (clause); level 1 (paragraph) contains level 0 (sentence). When the depth shifts, the correct answer must preserve the same depth offset. In test items, the stem often presents three terms and asks for the fourth, e.g., “Algorithm : Subroutine :: Program : ?”. The answer “Function” fits because a program contains functions just as an algorithm contains subroutines, and both subroutine and function occupy the same recursive tier. Examiners may disguise recursion by using synonyms that obscure the containment hierarchy, such as “Recipe : Step :: Manual : Procedure”. Here “step” and “procedure” are not strict synonyms; the relation is “instructional unit at the next lower granularity”. The solver must abstract the granularity ladder rather than match surface vocabulary.

Exceptions arise when the domain mixes physical and abstract containment. “Library : Book :: Book : Chapter” is valid, but “Library : Book :: Shelf : Book” breaks recursion because the second pair reverses the containment direction. A reliable check is to articulate the relation in a single phrase — “X is composed of Y” — and verify that the phrase holds for both pairs with identical directionality. If the phrase flips, the analogy is not recursive but analogical by similarity, and a different solving strategy applies.

Polynomial Series: Higher‑Order Differences

When a numeric series does not resolve into constant first differences, the next step is to compute successive difference tables until a constant row appears. A constant second difference indicates a quadratic (degree 2) polynomial; a constant third difference signals a cubic (degree 3) polynomial, and so on. For example, the series 2, 5, 10, 17, 26 yields first differences 3, 5, 7, 9 and second differences 2, 2, 2 — constant, confirming a quadratic rule of the form an² + bn + c. The coefficients can be recovered by solving a small linear system using the first three terms, but under time pressure it is faster to extrapolate the difference table: extend the constant second difference, rebuild the first differences, then the original terms. This method works for any polynomial series, including those with negative or fractional coefficients, provided the terms are generated by a single polynomial throughout.

Hybrid series that interleave two independent polynomial streams (e.g., odd positions follow n², even positions follow 2n + 1) will not produce a single constant difference row. The correct approach is to separate the streams by index parity before building difference tables. Examiners sometimes embed a polynomial of degree 3 within a longer series and ask for the next term; the difference table will reveal a constant third difference after three iterations. A practical shortcut: if the third difference becomes constant, the next term can be found by adding the last first difference, the last second difference, and the constant third difference in a single step (Newton’s forward formula). This avoids solving simultaneous equations and reduces arithmetic errors.

Technique
Find the missing term in the series: 3, 8, 15, 24, ?, 48.
Compute first differences: 5, 7, 9, ?, ?. Second differences: 2, 2, ?, ?. The second differences are constant at 2, indicating a quadratic pattern. Extend the table: next first difference = 9 + 2 = 11. Missing term = 24 + 11 = 35. Verify: following first difference = 11 + 2 = 13; next term = 35 + 13 = 48, matching the given final term. Answer: 35.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a series resists simple arithmetic or geometric checks, immediately write the first‑difference row. If it is not constant, write the second‑difference row. Constant second differences appear in over 30 % of polynomial items; constant third differences appear in roughly 5 %. This two‑row scan takes under ten seconds and either yields the answer or signals a non‑polynomial pattern, prompting a switch to alternate methods.

Cross‑Domain Mapping: Semantic Shift in Analogies

Cross‑domain analogies transfer a relational template from a familiar concrete domain to an abstract or less familiar domain. The template is preserved while the lexical items change categories. For instance, “Keystone : Arch :: Cornerstone : Foundation” maps the structural role “central supporting element” from architecture to metaphorical usage. The solver must isolate the functional role (load‑bearing, central, indispensable) and ignore the surface nouns. A systematic method: write the relation as a verb phrase — “X anchors Y” — then test each option by inserting it into the same phrase. Options that satisfy the phrase in the target domain are candidates; the one that also matches the part‑of‑speech and register of the stem term is the answer.

Examiners often introduce a distractor that shares a superficial attribute (e.g., both are stones) but fails the functional test. In “Keystone : Arch :: ? : Argument”, the distractor “Pebble” shares the stone category but does not anchor an argument; “Premise” does. The semantic shift can also involve a change in scale: “Cell : Organism :: Atom : Molecule” preserves the “building block” relation across biological and chemical scales. Recognising scale invariance prevents the trap of choosing “Organ” (same domain, wrong scale). Practice by constructing a two‑column table: left column lists the source domain pair, right column lists the target domain pair; fill the relational verb in the middle. This visualisation makes the mapping explicit and reduces reliance on intuition.

Dynamic Odd One Out: Context‑Dependent Exclusion

In dynamic odd‑one‑out items, the exclusion criterion changes according to a hidden condition embedded in the stem. The condition may be a quantifier (“most”, “least”), a modal (“could”, “must”), or a comparative (“more than”). For example, “Select the word that does not belong: Lion, Tiger, Leopard, Cheetah, Panther.” At first glance all are big cats. The hidden condition: “Which is not a member of the genus Panthera?” Cheetah (genus Acinonyx) is the outlier. The condition is not stated; it must be inferred from the set’s taxonomic depth. Another variant: “Which number is the odd one out? 12, 18, 24, 30, 36.” All are multiples of 6. The hidden condition: “Which is not a multiple of 12?” 18 and 30 qualify, but only one appears — 18. The test expects the solver to detect the stricter divisibility rule that applies to the majority.

Solving strategy: list all observable properties (parity, divisibility, part‑of‑speech, semantic field, morphological structure). Then test each property for coverage: the property that includes the maximum number of items (ideally n‑1) is the governing rule. The item lacking that property is the answer. If two properties each cover n‑1 items, the more specific property (e.g., “multiple of 12” vs “multiple of 6”) wins. Time‑saving tip: write the property codes (P for prime, E for even, M6 for multiple of 6) next to each option; the code that appears most often signals the rule.

Algorithmic Pattern Generation: Constructing Series from Rules

Some advanced items present a rule in natural language and ask for the nth term or the first term that satisfies a condition. The rule may combine arithmetic, geometric, and modular operations, e.g., “Start with 5. Each subsequent term is the previous term multiplied by 2, then reduced by the position index.” Generating terms sequentially is straightforward but error‑prone under pressure. A more robust method is to express the recurrence in closed form when possible. For the example, the recurrence a₁ = 5, aₙ = 2aₙ₋₁ − n can be solved by iteration or by recognising it as a linear non‑homogeneous recurrence. The homogeneous part yields 2ⁿ⁻¹ × 5; the particular solution for the −n term is of the form An + B. Substituting gives A = −1, B = −2, so aₙ = 5·2ⁿ⁻¹ − n − 2. This closed form allows direct computation of any term without building the entire sequence.

When a closed form is not readily derivable, use modular arithmetic to answer “first term divisible by 7” questions. Compute terms modulo 7 until the residue repeats; the cycle length is at most 7 for linear recurrences with constant coefficients. For the recurrence above, residues modulo 7 cycle every 6 steps, so the first zero residue occurs at n = 4 (a₄ = 5·8 − 4 − 2 = 34, 34 mod 7 = 6 — not zero; continue until zero appears). This cycle‑detection technique turns an open‑ended generation task into a bounded search, guaranteeing an answer within a known number of steps.

  • Assuming recursion depth is uniform when the analogy mixes levels of abstraction.
  • Stopping at first differences and declaring a series non‑polynomial without checking higher‑order differences.
  • Matching surface nouns in cross‑domain analogies instead of the underlying functional role.
  • Choosing the most obvious property in dynamic odd‑one‑out without testing coverage across the full set.
  • Generating terms one‑by‑one for algorithmic series when a modular cycle or closed form would answer the question faster.
  • Ignoring the directionality of containment in recursive analogies, leading to reversed pairs.
  • Applying a single difference table to interleaved streams without separating indices first.

Nested Analogy Structures: Multi‑Level Mapping

Nested analogies require the solver to recognise that a single stem pair can encode more than one relational layer. The first layer is usually a surface relation such as “tool‑to‑function” or “part‑to‑whole”. A second, deeper layer may involve a shift of domain, a change of scale, or a reversal of direction. For example, “Scalpel : Surgeon :: Pen : Author” operates on the surface as “instrument : practitioner”. The deeper layer maps the professional domain (medicine) to the creative domain (writing) while preserving the instrumental role. When a test item presents a triple analogy — “A : B :: C : D :: E : ?” — the solver must verify that the same relational verb applies across all three mappings. A common construction uses a hierarchical taxonomy: “Mammal : Dog :: Bird : Sparrow :: Reptile : ?”. The governing rule is “class : typical instance”. The answer “Lizard” satisfies the rule at the third level, but a distractor such as “Snake” may appear equally plausible unless the solver checks that the instance is a prototypical member rather than a peripheral one.

Detecting the Relational Verb

Write the stem pair as a sentence: “A is the ______ of B”. Fill the blank with a precise verb phrase — “instrument used by”, “building block of”, “unit measuring”. Then test the candidate pairs against that exact phrase. If the phrase fits all given pairs, the analogy is consistent; if it fails for any pair, the item likely contains a hidden second relation. In multi‑level items, the verb phrase may itself be compound: “primary instrument used by the primary practitioner of”. The solver should separate the components and verify each component independently.

Scale Invariance and Domain Shift

Scale invariance appears when the same structural relation holds across orders of magnitude. “Cell : Tissue :: Atom : Molecule” preserves the “component‑to‑assembly” relation across biological and chemical scales. Domain shift occurs when the source and target pairs belong to different knowledge domains but share an abstract schema. “Keystone : Arch :: Premise : Argument” shifts from architecture to logic while keeping the “essential support” schema. Recognising these patterns prevents the trap of selecting a term that matches the source domain only (e.g., “Voussoir” for the second slot).

Figurate Number Series: Geometric Progressions

Figurate numbers arise from arranging points into regular geometric shapes. The most common sequences are triangular (1, 3, 6, 10, 15…), square (1, 4, 9, 16, 25…), pentagonal (1, 5, 12, 22, 35…), and hexagonal (1, 6, 15, 28, 45…). Each sequence follows a quadratic formula: the nth triangular number Tₙ = n(n+1)/2, the nth square number Sₙ = n², the nth pentagonal number Pₙ = n(3n−1)/2, and the nth hexagonal number Hₙ = n(2n−1). Because the generating polynomials are of degree two, the second differences of any figurate series are constant. For triangular numbers the first differences are 2, 3, 4, 5… and the second difference is 1. For pentagonal numbers the first differences are 4, 7, 10, 13… and the second difference is 3. This property allows rapid verification: compute the first three terms, take successive differences twice, and confirm constancy.

Interleaved Figurate Streams

Advanced items may interleave two or more figurate sequences. Example: 1, 1, 3, 4, 6, 9, 10, 16… Here odd‑position terms (1, 3, 6, 10…) are triangular numbers, while even‑position terms (1, 4, 9, 16…) are square numbers. The solver must separate the stream by index parity before applying the quadratic test. A useful heuristic: write the index (1‑based) above each term; if the pattern of differences stabilises only after separating indices, the series is interleaved.

Hybrid Figurate‑Arithmetic Patterns

Some series combine a figurate core with an arithmetic modifier. For instance, the nth term might be Tₙ + 2n. The resulting sequence: 3, 7, 12, 18, 25… First differences: 4, 5, 6, 7… second differences constant at 1, revealing the underlying triangular component. The linear modifier 2n contributes a constant first difference of 2, which is absorbed into the overall first‑difference progression. Recognising that a constant second difference signals a quadratic core, regardless of added linear terms, is a key diagnostic.

Technique: Separate‑Index Difference Table
Find the next term: 2, 5, 10, 17, 26, ?
Write indices 1‑5 above the terms. Compute first differences: 3, 5, 7, 9. Second differences: 2, 2, 2 — constant, indicating a quadratic formula of form an² + bn + c. Using n=1 → a+b+c=2; n=2 → 4a+2b+c=5; n=3 → 9a+3b+c=10. Subtract first from second: 3a+b=3. Subtract second from third: 5a+b=5. Subtract these: 2a=2 → a=1. Then 3(1)+b=3 → b=0. Then 1+0+c=2 → c=1. Formula: n²+1. For n=6, term = 36+1 = 37. Answer: 37.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a series shows constant second differences, immediately write the generic quadratic an²+bn+c and solve for a,b,c using the first three terms. This yields the nth‑term formula in under 30 seconds and avoids building long difference tables.

Lexical Semantic Networks in Analogies

Modern analogy items often draw on structured lexical databases such as WordNet, where words are linked by relations like hypernymy (is‑a), meronymy (part‑of), antonymy, and entailment. A stem pair “Oak : Tree” exemplifies hyponym‑hypernym (instance‑category). The target pair must replicate the same relation, not merely share a thematic field. For instance, “Sparrow : Bird” is correct; “Nest : Bird” is a meronymic distractor. Test makers also exploit troponymy (manner‑of‑doing) for verbs: “Whisper : Speak :: Glance : Look”. The governing relation is “specific manner of the generic action”. Recognising the relation type allows the solver to filter options by part‑of‑speech and semantic role before evaluating meaning.

Cross‑POS Mapping

Analogies may map across parts of speech while preserving the relational skeleton. “Decide : Decision :: Choose : Choice” maps verb‑to‑noun derivation (action → result). The same skeleton appears in “Execute : Execution :: Perform : Performance”. The solver should identify the morphological process (suffix ‑ion, ‑ment, ‑ance) and verify that the target pair uses the identical derivational pattern. A distractor such as “Execute : Executable” changes the suffix to ‑able, breaking the pattern.

Multi‑Relational Chains

Some items embed a chain of two relations: “Heart : Pump :: Engine : ?”. The first relation is “organ : function”. The second relation must be “machine : function”. The answer “Motor” fits because an engine’s function is to motor (drive). However, “Fuel” is a meronymic distractor (part‑of). The chain can be longer: “Cell : Nucleus :: Computer : CPU :: Organization : Headquarters”. Each step follows “system : central control unit”. The solver must abstract the schema “central control unit of” and apply it uniformly.

Modular Arithmetic Series: Cyclic Patterns

Series defined by recurrences modulo a fixed integer exhibit periodic behaviour. For a linear recurrence aₙ = (p·aₙ₋₁ + q) mod m, the sequence of residues must eventually repeat because only m distinct residues exist. The maximum period length is m, but the actual period divides m when the multiplier p and modulus m are not coprime. Example: a₁ = 3, aₙ = (2·aₙ₋₁ + 1) mod 7. Generate residues: 3, 0, 1, 3, 0, 1… period 3. Knowing the period allows direct computation of any term index: aₖ = a₍ₖ mod period₎ (with 1‑based adjustment). This technique converts an open‑ended generation task into a bounded lookup.

Detecting the Modulus from a Given Segment

When the modulus is not stated, the solver can infer it by observing the range of terms. If all terms lie between 0 and 12 inclusive, the modulus is likely 13 or a divisor thereof. A sudden reset to a small value after a large term signals a wrap‑around. For instance, 5, 11, 4, 10, 3, 9… differences alternate +6, −7, +6, −7… The modulus 13 explains the pattern: each step adds 6 modulo 13. Confirm by checking (5+6) mod 13 = 11, (11+6) mod 13 = 4, etc.

Combined Arithmetic‑Modular Rules

Some series apply an arithmetic operation then a modular reduction: “Multiply by 3, add 2, take last digit”. This is equivalent to aₙ = (3·aₙ₋₁ + 2) mod 10. The period of such a recurrence modulo 10 is at most 10. Compute the first few terms until a repeat; the cycle length gives the answer for any large index. For a₁ = 1: 1, 5, 7, 3, 1… period 4. The 100th term corresponds to index 100 mod 4 = 0 → 4th term = 3.

Conditional Series Transformation: Rule‑Based Mutation

In conditional transformation items, a base series is given together with a rule that mutates the series after a certain condition is met. The rule may be “replace every prime term with its square” or “after the first term exceeding 50, subtract 5 from all subsequent terms”. The solver must simulate the base series, monitor the condition, and apply the mutation precisely at the trigger point. Example: Base series: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12… Rule: “When a term is a multiple of 7, double the next term”. The base series never hits a multiple of 7, so the rule never activates — the answer is the unchanged next term. A variant: Base: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21… Rule: “When a term is a multiple of 7, double the next term”. At term 21 (multiple of 7), the next base term would be 24; mutated term becomes 48. The series after mutation continues with the mutated value as the new base for subsequent rule checks.

Nested Conditions

Advanced items may stack conditions: “If a term is even, add 3; if the result is a perfect square, skip the next term”. The solver must evaluate conditions sequentially for each generated term. A systematic approach: maintain a state table with columns for index, raw term, condition‑1 outcome, condition‑2 outcome, final term. This prevents cascading errors when multiple rules interact.

Reverse Engineering the Rule

Sometimes the mutated series is given and the rule must be deduced. Compare the given series with a plausible base series (often arithmetic or geometric). Identify the indices where the two diverge; the divergence pattern reveals the condition. For instance, given 2, 5, 8, 11, 22, 14… Base arithmetic +3 would be 2,5,8,11,14,17… Divergence at index 5: 22 vs 14. 22 = 2×11, suggesting “double the term preceding a multiple of 5”. Index 4 term 11 is not a multiple of 5, but index 5 base 14 is not either. However, 22 = 2×11 (the previous term). The rule could be “double the term immediately before a term that would be a multiple of 7”. Testing hypotheses against all divergences isolates the correct rule.

  • Assuming a single relational layer in nested analogies and missing the deeper schema.
  • Applying the quadratic test to an interleaved figurate series without separating indices first.
  • Confusing hyponymy with meronymy when the stem pair is a category‑instance pair.
  • Ignoring the modulus wrap‑around and treating a modular series as unbounded arithmetic.
  • Failing to simulate the trigger condition step‑by‑step in conditional transformation series.
  • Overlooking that a mutation may alter the base for subsequent condition checks, leading to cascade errors.
