Key Concepts

Inferences & Syllogisms for CLAT

CLAT Logical Reasoning inference questions ask what must follow from given statements. Syllogisms are deductive arguments with two premises and a conclusion. Answers must follow strictly from given statements — no outside knowledge.

  • Inference rules:
    • A valid inference must be true in ALL cases where premises are true.
    • 'Some A are B' does NOT mean 'All A are B' or 'All B are A'.
    • 'All A are B' does NOT mean 'All B are A' (conversion error).
    • Negative premises → negative/no conclusion (two negative premises = no conclusion).
    • Two particular premises ('some') → no definite conclusion.
    • The middle term must be distributed at least once.
    • No term can be distributed in the conclusion if not distributed in premises.
  • Venn diagram method: Draw circles for each term; shade/exclude areas per premises; check if conclusion holds in ALL possible diagrams. If conclusion holds in every valid diagram — valid; if false in any — invalid.
  • Syllogism forms (A/E/I/O):
    • A: All A are B (universal affirmative)
    • E: No A are B (universal negative)
    • I: Some A are B (particular affirmative)
    • O: Some A are not B (particular negative)
  • Valid moods (examples): AAA-1 (Barbara: All M are P, All S are M → All S are P); AII-1 (Darii: All M are P, Some S are M → Some S are P); EAE-1 (Celarent: No M are P, All S are M → No S are P); AOO-2 (Baroco: All P are M, Some S are not M → Some S are not P).
  • Immediate inferences: Conversion (All A are B → Some B are A; No A are B → No B are A; Some A are B → Some B are A; Some A are not B → no valid converse); Obviation (All A are B → No A are non-B; Some A are B → Some A are not non-B; etc.); Contraposition (All A are B → All non-B are non-A; No A are B → No B are A).
  • Complementary pairs: If one of a pair must be true and the other false: All A are B / Some A are not B; No A are B / Some A are B. If the first is false, second true and vice versa (in either-or conclusions).
Technique: Venn diagram for syllogism
Statements: All dogs are mammals. Some mammals are pets. Conclusion: Some dogs are pets. Valid?
Answer: Invalid. Draw dogs inside mammals; pets overlapping mammals somewhere — pets may be entirely outside the dog circle. 'Some dogs are pets' is not guaranteed (drawn diagram where pets circle doesn't touch dog circle is valid). So conclusion does not follow.
Technique: Either-or (complementary)
Statements: All students are hardworking. Some students are not rich. Conclusions: (1) All hardworking are rich. (2) Some hardworking are not rich.
Answer: Conclusion (2) follows (some students who are hardworking are not rich). Conclusion (1) doesn't follow. In complementary pairs, check whether one must be true — here (2) is supported.
Technique: Conversion trap
Statement: All teachers are educated. Can we conclude 'All educated are teachers'?
Answer: No — universal affirmative (All A are B) converts only to 'Some B are A' (Some educated are teachers), not 'All B are A'.

Common Traps

  • Converting 'All A are B' to 'All B are A' — invalid.
  • Assuming 'Some A are B' means 'Some A are not B' — both can be true simultaneously.
  • Drawing conclusion from two particular premises — invalid.
  • Using outside knowledge ('All birds fly' → penguins counterexample).
  • Treating 'Some' as 'many/most' — in logic, 'some' means 'at least one' (could be all).

Memory Tricks

  • Distribution: Subject distributed in universal (All/No), predicate distributed in negative (No/...not).
  • Middle term: Must be distributed at least once, else fallacy of undistributed middle.
  • Two negatives = no conclusion; two particulars = no conclusion.
  • Complementary pairs: All/Some-not and No/Some.
CLAT inference strategy
For syllogisms, draw Venn diagrams — test whether the conclusion holds in ALL valid diagrams. For inference questions, ask 'Does this HAVE to be true?' — probable isn't enough. Eliminate options that go beyond given statements.
