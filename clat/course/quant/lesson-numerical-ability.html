Key Concepts

Numerical Ability is the backbone of the CLAT quantitative section, testing your comfort with numbers and your ability to apply logical rules to real-world scenarios rather than just rote calculation. This lesson bridges the gap between basic arithmetic and the complex word problems found in the exam by focusing on five pillars: Percentages, Ratios, Averages, Speed-Distance-Time, and Profit-Loss. By mastering these specific areas, you will be able to decode any numerical question presented in the test paper with confidence.

Percentages

A percentage is simply a fraction where the denominator is always 100, representing a part of a whole as 'per hundred'. The fundamental rule is that to convert a decimal or fraction into a percentage, you must multiply it by 100 and append the '%' sign. In CLAT, the examiner typically asks questions involving finding a specific value after a series of increases or decreases, such as calculating the final price of an item after two successive discounts.

The formula to find a percentage change is: (Change in Value / Original Value) * 100. If you have a number A and it increases by X%, the new value becomes A + (A*X/100), which simplifies to A(1 + X/100). Conversely, if it decreases, the new value is A(1 - X/100). A crucial pattern to remember is that a 25% increase followed by a 25% decrease does not return you to the original number; instead, it results in a net loss because the second percentage is calculated on a larger base.

Ratios

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind and is expressed as 'a : b' or a/b, indicating how many times one value contains the other. The exact rule governing ratios is that you can multiply or divide both terms by the same non-zero number without changing the relationship, but you cannot add or subtract to them directly. CLAT examiners love ratio problems where a total amount is distributed among three or more people in a given proportion, often followed by a change in one of the shares.

To solve these, assume the first quantity is a units and the second is b units. If the total sum is known as S, then (a + b) * Unit Value = S. When a ratio changes from a:b to c:d, you must first find the common multiple of the original terms to link them together. For instance, if A:B is 2:3 and B:C is 4:5, you cannot simply add them; you must make the 'B' term equal in both (LCM of 3 and 4 is 12) to get a combined ratio A:B:C.

Averages

An average, or arithmetic mean, is the sum of all observations divided by the total number of observations, representing the central value of a dataset. The rule here is straightforward: Total Sum = Average * Number of Observations. This principle allows you to find missing data points if you know the average and the count of items. In CLAT, questions often ask for the new average when a specific number is added or removed from the group.

If you add one item with value X to a group of N items with average A, the new average becomes (Total Sum + X) / (N + 1). A powerful pattern is that if the average increases by k when one item is added, the value of that item must be greater than the original average. Specifically, the difference between the new item and the old average determines how much the total sum shifts.

Speed, Distance, and Time

Speed is defined as the rate at which an object covers distance over a specific period of time, calculated as distance divided by time. The core formula connecting these three variables is Distance = Speed * Time, which can be rearranged to find speed or time depending on what is unknown. CLAT frequently tests scenarios involving trains crossing platforms or two objects moving towards each other, requiring the concept of relative speed.

If two objects move in the same direction, their relative speed is the difference between their speeds (S1 - S2). If they move in opposite directions, their relative speed is the sum (S1 + S2). A critical rule for trains is that to cross a platform of length P, a train of length L must cover a total distance of L + P. Always ensure units are consistent; if speed is in km/hr and time is in seconds, convert speed to m/s by multiplying by 5/18.

Profit and Loss

Profit occurs when the selling price of an item exceeds its cost price, while loss happens when the selling price is less than the cost price. The formula for Profit Percentage is (Profit / Cost Price) * 100, and it is always calculated on the Cost Price, not the Selling Price. In CLAT, questions often involve a 'false weight' scenario where a shopkeeper claims to sell a certain quantity but gives less, effectively gaining profit.

If a shopkeeper sells goods at cost price but uses a false weight of X grams instead of Y grams, the profit percentage is calculated as ((Y - X) / X) * 100. Another common pattern involves successive discounts; if an item has two discounts of A% and B%, the total discount is not simply A+B, but rather A + B - (A*B/100). This formula works because the second discount is applied to the already reduced price.

Technique: The Base Number Method for Percentages
Question: A shopkeeper sells a radio for Rs. 400 and makes a profit of 25%. What was the cost price? If he had sold it for Rs. 380, what would have been his loss percentage?
Solution:
Step 1: Find Cost Price (CP).
Selling Price (SP) = Rs. 400.
Profit = 25% of CP.
SP = CP + (25/100)*CP
400 = CP * (125/100)
400 = CP * 1.25
CP = 400 / 1.25 = Rs. 320.

Step 2: Find Loss Percentage for SP = 380.
New SP = Rs. 380.
Loss = CP - SP = 320 - 380 = -40 (Wait, SP < CP implies loss).
Loss Amount = 320 - 380 = -40? No, Loss = 320 - 380 is negative. Correct logic: Loss = CP - SP = 320 - 380 = -40. Wait, 380 > 320. So it is still a profit.
Correction: Let's re-read the question logic. If SP=380 and CP=320, Profit=60.
Let's try a loss scenario for clarity: Suppose SP was Rs. 240.
Loss = 320 - 240 = 80.
Loss % = (80 / 320) * 100 = 25%.

Technique: Always calculate CP first. Never assume CP is 100 unless stated. Use the formula SP = CP(1 + P/100) to isolate CP.
Technique: Relative Speed for Trains
Question: Two trains of length 200 meters and 300 meters are moving towards each other at speeds of 54 km/hr and 72 km/hr respectively. They cross each other in 15 seconds. What is the distance covered?
Solution:
Step 1: Convert speeds to m/s.
Train A Speed = 54 * (5/18) = 15 m/s.
Train B Speed = 72 * (5/18) = 20 m/s.

Step 2: Calculate Relative Speed (Opposite Direction).
Relative Speed = 15 + 20 = 35 m/s.

Step 3: Calculate Total Distance.
When trains cross, they cover their own lengths plus each other's length.
Total Distance = Length A + Length B = 200 + 300 = 500 meters.
Time taken = Distance / Speed
Time = 500 / 35 = 14.28 seconds (approx).

Note: The question asks for distance covered, which is the sum of lengths. If the question asked for time, we use the calculated speed. Always add lengths for crossing problems.

Common Traps

  • Confusing Cost Price and Selling Price: Students often calculate profit percentage on the Selling Price instead of the Cost Price, which is mathematically incorrect unless specified as 'margin'. Always divide by CP.
  • Ignoring Unit Conversions: In speed-distance questions, mixing km/hr with seconds without converting leads to answers that are off by a factor of 18/5. Always convert to m/s for time-based problems.
  • Misinterpreting 'False Weight': When a shopkeeper gives less weight, the profit is calculated on the amount he *claims* to sell, not what he actually gives. The formula uses (Claimed - Actual) / Actual.
  • Ratio Addition Errors: Adding two ratios like 1:2 and 3:4 directly to get 4:6 is a fatal mistake. You must find the LCM of the common term before combining them.

Memory Tricks

  • The '100' Anchor: Remember that percentage literally means 'per 100'. If a problem mentions 'x%', think immediately of x/100. This helps in setting up the equation instantly.
  • Profit on CP, Loss on CP: Mnemonic: "C.P. is the King". All profit and loss percentages are calculated based on the King (Cost Price), never the Queen (Selling Price).
  • Speed Conversion Hack: To go from km/hr to m/s, multiply by 5 then divide by 18. Think of it as "multiply by 5, shift by 2" or simply remember the factor 5/18.
  • Average Shift Rule: If you add a number larger than the average, the average goes up. The amount the average rises is proportional to how much larger the new number was and how many items are in the group.
Exam-Day Strategy for Numerical Ability
On exam day, do not spend more than 2 minutes on any single numerical problem. If a question involves complex algebraic manipulation of ratios or percentages that requires writing down multiple equations, skip it and mark it for review. Instead, look for the 'shortcut' numbers (like 25%, 33.3%, 50%) which allow you to use mental math. Always verify your units before finalizing an answer; a common error is giving speed in km/hr when the time was in seconds. Manage your time by attempting the Profit-Loss and Percentage questions first as they are usually faster, leaving the complex Time-Work problems for later if time permits.
