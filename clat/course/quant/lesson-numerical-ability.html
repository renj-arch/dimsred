1. Percentages and their Applications

1.1 The Concept of Percentage

Percentage is a way of expressing a number as a fraction of one hundred, which makes comparison across different bases straightforward. When we say that a quantity is 25 %, we mean that for every one hundred units of the reference whole, twenty‑five units belong to the part under consideration. This definition underlies every calculation that follows, because any percentage can be converted to a decimal multiplier by dividing by one hundred, and any decimal can be turned into a percentage by multiplying by one hundred. The utility of this representation becomes evident when we compare growth rates of populations, changes in prices, or marks obtained in examinations, all of which may have different absolute magnitudes but become directly comparable once expressed as percentages.

In the context of the CLAT quantitative techniques section, the ability to move fluidly between fractions, decimals, and percentages is a core skill. For instance, a question may present a data table where the share of a category is given as 3/8, and the next question asks for the same share as a percentage. Recognising that 3/8 equals 0.375 and therefore 37.5 % allows the candidate to answer without a calculator. The underlying reason is that the denominator one hundred is a universal scaling factor, so any rational number can be mapped onto it by simple multiplication.

1.2 Percentage Change and Successive Changes

When a quantity increases by a certain percentage, the new value is obtained by multiplying the original value by the factor (1 + r/100), where r is the percentage increase. Conversely, a decrease by r % uses the factor (1 − r/100). This multiplicative approach is essential because percentage changes are not additive; a 20 % increase followed by a 20 % decrease does not return the original value, since the base for the second change is already the increased amount. The net effect of successive changes is found by multiplying the individual factors, which is why the order of applying percentage changes matters only when the percentages themselves differ.

A common examination scenario involves a price that is first raised by 10 % and then discounted by 10 %. The combined factor is (1 + 0.10) × (1 − 0.10) = 1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99, indicating a net 1 % decrease. Understanding this principle prevents the trap of assuming the two changes cancel each other. The same logic extends to three or more successive changes, where the overall factor is the product of all individual factors, and the overall percentage change is derived by subtracting one from the product and multiplying by one hundred.

1.3 Applications in Data Interpretation

Data interpretation sets frequently require the candidate to compute percentage shares, growth rates, or contribution margins from tables and charts. The ability to read a bar chart showing the revenue of five companies and instantly determine that Company A’s revenue is 30 % of the total rests on the same percentage fundamentals. Moreover, questions may ask for the percentage point difference between two years’ market shares, which is a simple subtraction of the two percentages, not a percentage change calculation. Recognising the distinction between percentage points and percent change is crucial because a rise from 15 % to 18 % is a 3 percentage‑point increase but a 20 % relative increase.

In practice, candidates should develop the habit of estimating percentages mentally by using benchmark fractions such as 1/2 = 50 %, 1/4 = 25 %, 1/5 = 20 %, 1/8 = 12.5 %, and 1/10 = 10 %. These benchmarks allow rapid approximation of answers when the options are widely spaced, a typical feature of the CLAT quantitative section. The reasoning behind this strategy is that the human brain processes familiar fractions faster than arbitrary decimals, and the error introduced by approximation is usually smaller than the gap between answer choices.

2. Ratios, Proportions and Averages

2.1 Ratio Fundamentals

A ratio expresses the relative magnitude of two quantities of the same kind, written as a:b or a/b. The order matters because a:b is not the same as b:a unless a equals b. Ratios can be simplified by dividing both terms by their greatest common divisor, just as fractions are reduced. When a problem states that the ratio of boys to girls in a class is 3:2, it means that for every three boys there are two girls; the actual numbers could be 30 and 20, 15 and 10, or any multiples of the basic pair. This scalability is the defining property of ratios and is exploited when combining several ratios into a single chain.

To combine two ratios that share a common term, the common term must be made equal in both ratios. For example, if a:b = 2:3 and b:c = 4:5, the value of b in the first ratio is 3 and in the second is 4. The least common multiple of 3 and 4 is 12, so we multiply the first ratio by 4 and the second by 3, yielding a:b = 8:12 and b:c = 12:15. The combined ratio a:b:c becomes 8:12:15. This technique is frequently tested because it requires both conceptual clarity and arithmetic accuracy.

2.2 Proportion and its Properties

When two ratios are equal, they form a proportion, written as a:b = c:d or a/b = c/d. The fundamental property of a proportion is that the product of the extremes equals the product of the means, i.e., a × d = b × c. This cross‑multiplication rule is the algebraic engine behind many quantitative problems, allowing the unknown term to be isolated quickly. Proportions also obey several derived properties: invertendo (b:a = d:c), alternendo (a:c = b:d), componendo ((a+b):b = (c+d):d), and dividendo ((a−b):b = (c−d):d). These properties are not merely algebraic curiosities; they provide shortcuts for solving problems that involve sums or differences of proportional quantities.

Direct proportion occurs when two quantities increase or decrease together such that their ratio remains constant. If the cost of apples is directly proportional to the weight purchased, doubling the weight doubles the cost. Inverse proportion describes a relationship where one quantity increases while the other decreases so that their product stays constant; the classic example is speed and time for a fixed distance. Recognising whether a situation is direct or inverse determines whether we multiply or divide when scaling quantities, and misidentifying the type leads to systematic errors.

2.3 Averages and Weighted Averages

The arithmetic mean, commonly called the average, is the sum of all observations divided by the number of observations. It represents the central value that would result if the total sum were distributed equally among all items. In examinations, averages appear in contexts such as finding the mean score of a class, the average speed over a journey with varying speeds, or the mean temperature over a week. A key property is that the sum of deviations of each observation from the mean is zero, which can be used to verify calculations or to find a missing observation when the mean and the other values are known.

Weighted average extends the concept by assigning different importance, or weights, to each observation. The weighted mean is the sum of each value multiplied by its weight, divided by the sum of the weights. This arises naturally when combining groups of different sizes: the overall average height of two classes is not the simple average of the two class averages unless the classes have equal strength. Instead, each class average must be weighted by the number of students. The formula ensures that larger groups exert proportionally greater influence on the combined average, a principle that also underlies the calculation of composite indices in economics.

Worked Example: Weighted Average
Class X has 30 students with an average score of 72. Class Y has 20 students with an average score of 84. What is the combined average score of both classes?
The total score for Class X is 30 × 72 = 2160. The total score for Class Y is 20 × 84 = 1680. The combined total score is 2160 + 1680 = 3840. The total number of students is 30 + 20 = 50. The combined average is 3840 ÷ 50 = 76.8.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a weighted‑average question gives group sizes and averages, compute the total sum for each group first; this avoids the common mistake of averaging the two averages directly.

3. Profit, Loss and Discount

3.1 Cost Price, Selling Price and Profit

Profit arises when the selling price of an article exceeds its cost price. The absolute profit is the difference SP − CP, and the profit percentage is calculated on the cost price as (Profit ÷ CP) × 100. Using cost price as the base is a convention that makes profit percentages comparable across transactions of different magnitudes. If a shopkeeper buys an item for ₹200 and sells it for ₹250, the profit is ₹50 and the profit percentage is (50 ÷ 200) × 100 = 25 %. The same profit of ₹50 on a cost price of ₹500 would yield only a 10 % profit, illustrating why the base matters.

In many problems the selling price and profit percentage are given, and the cost price must be found. Rearranging the formula gives CP = SP ÷ (1 + Profit%/100). For instance, if an article is sold at ₹540 after a 20 % profit, the cost price is 540 ÷ 1.20 = ₹450. This reverse calculation is a staple of the quantitative section because it tests algebraic manipulation of the basic profit relation.

3.2 Loss, Discount and Marked Price

Loss occurs when the selling price is lower than the cost price. The loss amount is CP − SP, and the loss percentage is (Loss ÷ CP) × 100, again using cost price as the denominator. Discount, on the other hand, is a reduction offered on the marked price (also called list price). The discount amount is MP − SP, and the discount percentage is (Discount ÷ MP) × 100. It is crucial to distinguish the base: profit and loss percentages are on cost price, while discount percentage is on marked price. Confusing these bases is a frequent source of error.

A typical problem may provide the marked price, the discount percentage, and the profit percentage earned after the discount, asking for the cost price. The chain of relations is MP → (after discount) → SP → (after profit) → CP. For example, an article marked at ₹1000 is sold at a 10 % discount, so SP = ₹900. If the seller still makes a 20 % profit, then CP = 900 ÷ 1.20 = ₹750. Working stepwise through the chain ensures that each percentage is applied to the correct base.

3.3 Successive Discounts and Equivalent Single Discount

When two or more discounts are offered successively, the overall reduction is not the arithmetic sum of the individual discount percentages. Instead, each discount is applied to the price resulting from the previous discount. If a product marked at ₹1000 receives a 20 % discount followed by a 10 % discount, the first discount reduces the price to ₹800, and the second reduces it further to ₹720. The total reduction is ₹280, which corresponds to a single equivalent discount of 28 %. The formula for the equivalent single discount for two successive discounts d1 and d2 is d1 + d2 − (d1 × d2)/100. This formula derives directly from multiplying the successive factors (1 − d1/100)(1 − d2/100) and expressing the result as 1 − deq/100.

Understanding this concept prevents the common mistake of adding 20 % and 10 % to get 30 %. The same principle applies to successive percentage increases, such as a price hike followed by a tax increase. In both cases the net effect is multiplicative, not additive. Examination questions often test this by asking for the equivalent single discount or by giving the final selling price after successive discounts and asking for the original marked price.

4. Time, Work and Wages

4.1 Work Rate and Combined Work

The fundamental assumption in time‑work problems is that work is measurable and that each worker contributes at a constant rate. If a person can complete a job in n days, the fraction of work done in one day is 1/n. This daily work rate is the reciprocal of the time taken. When several workers operate together, their rates add up because they work simultaneously on the same task. For example, if A completes a job in 10 days (rate 1/10) and B in 15 days (rate 1/15), their combined rate is 1/10 + 1/15 = 5/30 = 1/6, so together they finish the job in 6 days. The additive property of rates is the cornerstone of all combined‑work calculations.

Problems may involve workers joining or leaving partway through the job. In such cases the work is divided into phases, each with a constant set of workers. The work done in each phase equals the combined rate of the active workers multiplied by the duration of that phase. Summing the work across phases must equal one whole job. This segmentation technique transforms a seemingly complex schedule into a series of simple rate‑time products.

4.2 Time and Work with Variable Rates

Not all workers have the same efficiency, and sometimes a worker’s efficiency changes (for instance, due to fatigue or learning). When efficiencies are given in ratio form, such as A is twice as efficient as B, we can assign a base rate to the less efficient worker and scale the other accordingly. If B’s rate is 1 unit per day, A’s rate is 2 units per day. The total work can then be expressed in the same units, and the time taken by each worker individually or together follows from the rate‑time‑work relationship. This approach avoids fractions until the final step, reducing arithmetic errors.

Another variation involves pipes filling or emptying a cistern. A filling pipe has a positive rate, an emptying pipe a negative rate. The net rate is the algebraic sum of all active pipes’ rates. If a cistern is filled by pipe A in 6 hours (rate 1/6) and emptied by pipe B in 9 hours (rate −1/9), the net rate when both are open is 1/6 − 1/9 = 1/18, so the cistern fills in 18 hours. The same additive principle applies, with the sign indicating direction of work.

4.3 Wages Distribution Based on Work Done

When a group of workers completes a job and the total wages are to be divided, the fair principle is to allocate wages in proportion to the amount of work each worker contributed. Since work done equals rate multiplied by time, and all workers may not work for the same duration, the share of each worker is (individual rate × time worked) divided by the sum of such products for all workers. For instance, if A works for 4 days at rate 1/10 and B works for 6 days at rate 1/15, A’s work is 4/10 = 0.4, B’s work is 6/15 = 0.4, so they share the wages equally. This proportional division is a direct application of the ratio concept to a real‑world scenario.

Examination questions sometimes give the total wages and the individual times, asking for each worker’s share. The solution path is to compute each worker’s total work units, find the ratio of these units, and then split the total wages according to that ratio. The underlying reason is that wages are a monetary representation of labour input, and labour input is measured by work done.

5. Speed, Distance and Relative Motion

5.1 Basic Speed‑Distance‑Time Relationship

The three quantities speed, distance, and time are linked by the equation Distance = Speed × Time. Any one of them can be found if the other two are known. Speed is typically expressed in kilometres per hour (km/h) or metres per second (m/s), distance in kilometres or metres, and time in hours or seconds. Converting between units is a routine requirement; for example, 1 km/h equals 5/18 m/s, and 1 m/s equals 18/5 km/h. Mastery of these conversions prevents unit‑mismatch errors that can invalidate an otherwise correct calculation.

Average speed for a journey with multiple segments is not the arithmetic mean of the segment speeds unless each segment takes the same time. The correct definition is total distance divided by total time. If a car travels 60 km at 30 km/h and another 60 km at 60 km/h, the total distance is 120 km, the total time is 2 h + 1 h = 3 h, so the average speed is 40 km/h, not 45 km/h. This distinction is a classic trap because the intuitive but wrong answer (the simple average) often appears among the options.

5.2 Relative Speed in Same and Opposite Directions

When two objects move along the same line, their relative speed depends on their directions. If they move in opposite directions, the distance between them closes at the sum of their speeds; if they move in the same direction, the faster object gains on the slower at the difference of their speeds. This principle is used to solve problems about trains crossing each other, cars overtaking, or people walking towards or away from each other. For example, two trains of lengths 200 m and 300 m travel at 72 km/h and 54 km/h respectively in opposite directions. Their relative speed is 126 km/h, which is 35 m/s. The time to completely cross each other is (200 + 300) ÷ 35 ≈ 14.3 seconds.

5.3 Boats, Streams and Effective Speed

A boat moving in a stream experiences the stream’s current as an additional speed component. When the boat goes downstream, the stream aids its motion, so the effective speed is the sum of the boat’s speed in still water (b) and the stream’s speed (s). Upstream, the stream opposes the boat, so the effective speed is the difference b − s. These two speeds are the only ones needed to solve any boat‑stream problem, because the distance travelled downstream and upstream is usually the same, and the time for each leg follows from Distance = Effective Speed × Time. If a boat takes 4 hours to go 40 km downstream and 5 hours to return the same distance upstream, the downstream speed is 10 km/h and the upstream speed is 8 km/h. Solving b + s = 10 and b − s = 8 gives b = 9 km/h and s = 1 km/h.

Problems may also ask for the average speed for a round trip in a stream. Since the distances are equal, the average speed is the harmonic mean of the downstream and upstream speeds: 2 × (b + s) × (b − s) ÷ [(b + s) + (b − s)] = (b² − s²) ÷ b. This formula shows that the average speed for a round trip is always less than the still‑water speed unless the stream speed is zero, a consequence of the longer time spent fighting the current upstream.

Mastery of these interconnected topics equips a candidate to approach the quantitative techniques section with confidence and precision.

Mixtures, Alligations and Replacement Problems

The alligation method solves problems where ingredients of different concentrations are mixed to obtain a target concentration. The rule states that the mixing ratio is inversely proportional to the differences between each ingredient’s concentration and the target. If ingredient A has concentration c₁ and ingredient B has concentration c₂, and the required mixture concentration is cₘ, then the ratio A : B = |c₂ − cₘ| : |c₁ − cₘ|. This extends to three or more components by pairwise application or a weighted‑average equation.

Replacement problems are a common variant. A vessel contains a solution; a portion is removed and replaced (often with water or a stronger solution). After each identical replacement, the concentration changes as: final concentration = initial concentration × (1 − fraction removed)n, where n is the number of cycles. If the removed fraction differs each cycle, multiply the successive factors. For example, a 40 L cask of 60 % alcohol has 8 L drawn off and replaced by water twice. The fraction removed is 8/40 = 1/5, so final alcohol % = 60 % × (4/5)² = 38.4 %.

When the replacement substance has its own concentration, the recurrence is: Ck+1 = Ck × (1 − r) + Cnew × r, where r is the fraction replaced and Cnew the added concentration. The closed form is Cn = Cnew + (C₀ − Cnew) (1 − r)n, useful for finding when concentration falls below a threshold.

Alligation also applies to cost‑price mixtures. If two rice varieties costing ₹30/kg and ₹45/kg are mixed to sell at ₹38/kg with 20 % profit, first find the cost price of the mixture: 38 ÷ 1.2 ≈ ₹31.67/kg. Alligation then gives the ratio (45 − 31.67) : (31.67 − 30) ≈ 13.33 : 1.67 ≈ 8 : 1. The same logic works for metal alloys, milk‑water mixtures, and any weighted‑average scenario.

Examiners often combine alligation with profit‑loss or successive percentage changes. A typical composite question: “A merchant mixes two teas costing ₹120/kg and ₹180/kg in the ratio 3 : 2, then sells the mixture at 25 % profit. Find the selling price per kg.” First, cost price of mixture = (3×120 + 2×180)/5 = ₹144/kg. Then selling price = 144 × 1.25 = ₹180/kg. Recognising the two‑step structure avoids the trap of applying profit directly to individual costs.

Partnership, Capital Contribution and Profit Sharing

Partnership problems rest on the principle that profit (or loss) is divided in proportion to the product of capital contributed and the time it is employed. If partner A invests ₹C₁ for t₁ months and partner B invests ₹C₂ for t₂ months, the profit‑sharing ratio is C₁t₁ : C₂t₂. When capitals change during the year — additional investment, withdrawal, or a partner joining mid‑year — split the timeline into intervals of constant capital and sum the capital‑time products for each partner.

Example: A starts with ₹50,000; B joins after 4 months with ₹30,000; after 8 months A adds ₹20,000 and B withdraws ₹10,000. The year divides into three periods: months 1‑4 (A = 50,000), months 5‑8 (A = 50,000, B = 30,000), months 9‑12 (A = 70,000, B = 20,000). A’s capital‑months = 50,000×4 + 50,000×4 + 70,000×4 = 680,000. B’s = 30,000×4 + 20,000×4 = 200,000. Profit ratio A : B = 680,000 : 200,000 = 17 : 5.

Sleeping partners (capital only) and working partners (salary/commission before profit division) add complexity. A working partner’s salary is deducted from gross profit before the remainder is shared by capital‑time ratios. If the deed stipulates a managing partner’s commission of 10 % of net profit, compute net profit after expenses, allocate the commission, then distribute the balance.

Goodwill valuation appears when a new partner is admitted. Existing partners sacrifice a share of profit; the sacrificing ratio is the difference between old and new profit‑sharing ratios. Goodwill brought in by the new partner is credited to old partners in their sacrificing ratio. For instance, if A and B share 3 : 2 and admit C for 1/5 share, the new ratio might be 3 : 2 : 1. A’s sacrifice = 3/5 − 3/6 = 3/30; B’s = 2/5 − 2/6 = 2/30; goodwill is divided 3 : 2.

Examiners test consistent conversion of months to years, handling fractional months (e.g., 3.5 months = 3.5/12 years), and applying “capital × time” when capitals are in different currencies (convert to a common base first). A common error is averaging capitals instead of weighting by time.

Worked Example: Partnership with Mid‑Year Changes
A and B start a business. A invests ₹40,000 at the beginning. After 3 months B joins with ₹60,000. After 6 months from the start, A withdraws ₹10,000 and B adds ₹20,000. At the end of the year the total profit is ₹84,000. Find each partner’s share.
Step 1: Break the year into intervals with constant capitals.
Interval 1 (months 1‑3): A = 40,000; B = 0.
Interval 2 (months 4‑6): A = 40,000; B = 60,000.
Interval 3 (months 7‑12): A = 30,000 (after withdrawal); B = 80,000 (after addition).
Step 2: Compute capital‑months.
A = 40,000×3 + 40,000×3 + 30,000×6 = 120,000 + 120,000 + 180,000 = 420,000.
B = 0×3 + 60,000×3 + 80,000×6 = 0 + 180,000 + 480,000 = 660,000.
Step 3: Profit ratio A : B = 420,000 : 660,000 = 7 : 11.
Step 4: Divide ₹84,000 in 7 : 11 → total parts = 18. A’s share = 84,000 × 7/18 = ₹32,666.67; B’s share = 84,000 × 11/18 = ₹51,333.33.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Draw a timeline for every partnership question. Mark each capital change with a vertical line, label the duration of each segment, and write the capital for each partner in that segment. This visual map prevents missing a withdrawal or addition and makes the capital‑month calculation mechanical rather than mental.

Circular Tracks, Meeting Points and Relative Motion on a Loop

When bodies move on a closed circular track, relative speed applies but the meeting distance is the track length (or a multiple). If two runners start together and run in the same direction with speeds v₁ > v₂, the faster gains one full lap on the slower every time the relative distance equals the track length L. The first meeting time is L ÷ (v₁ − v₂); subsequent meetings occur at integer multiples of this interval.

If they run in opposite directions, the closing speed is v₁ + v₂ and they meet every L ÷ (v₁ + v₂) time units. The number of distinct meeting points on the track equals the number of meetings before both return simultaneously to the start. For speeds in simplest integer ratio a : b, distinct meeting points are |a − b| (same direction) and a + b (opposite direction). This follows from the repetition of angular positions after the LCM of their lap times.

Three or more runners extend the pairwise logic. The time when all meet at the starting point is the LCM of their individual lap times. Example: Three runners A, B, C on a 400 m track at 5 m/s, 7 m/s, 9 m/s. Lap times: 80 s, 400/7 s, 400/9 s. The smallest t such that t/80, t/(400/7), t/(400/9) are integers is 400 s (since t must be a multiple of 80, and 80×7/400 = 7/5 requires t/80 to be a multiple of 5). Thus they meet at the start after 400 s.

When a runner reverses direction, the relative speed changes instantaneously. Problems asking for total meetings in a given duration require piecewise calculation: sum meetings before reversal (using one relative speed) and after reversal (using the other).

Examiners also combine circular motion with straight‑line speed‑time‑distance segments. Treat each segment independently, convert units consistently, and remember that on a circle the meeting distance is the track length, not the separation along the circle.

Installments, Compound Interest and Present Value Calculations

Installment problems ask for the equal periodic payment that repays a loan or purchases an article under compound interest. The present value (PV) of an ordinary annuity of n payments of amount X at rate r per period is PV = X × [1 − (1 + r)−n] ÷ r. The installment X = PV × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)−n]. For annuity due (payments at period start), multiply the right‑hand side by (1 + r).

When interest is compounded more frequently than the payment interval, convert the nominal rate to an effective rate per payment period. Example: 12 % p.a. compounded quarterly with monthly installments → effective monthly rate = (1 + 0.12/4)1/3 − 1 ≈ 0.99 %. Using the nominal monthly rate (1 %) introduces error.

Present value appears in “cash price vs. installment price” comparisons. An article priced ₹10,000 cash is offered at ₹2,000 down plus 4 monthly installments of ₹2,200. The implicit rate r satisfies 10,000 = 2,000 + 2,200 × [1 − (1 + r)−4] ÷ r. Solve for r by trial, interpolation, or approximation (e.g., r ≈ (2 × total interest) ÷ (principal × (n + 1)) for a rough estimate).

Compound interest with varying rates uses the product of growth factors: Amount = Principal × (1 + r₁) × (1 + r₂) × … × (1 + rₙ). If the rate changes mid‑year, split the year accordingly. Example: ₹5,000 at 8 % for the first half‑year and 10 % for the second (compounded half‑yearly) → amount = 5,000 × (1 + 0.08/2) × (1 + 0.10/2) = 5,000 × 1.04 × 1.05 = ₹5,460.

Depreciation mirrors compound interest: value after n years = initial value × (1 − d)n for constant rate d. With varying rates, multiply successive factors. Questions may ask for the year when value falls below a threshold, requiring logarithms or trial with the compound‑depreciation formula.

  • Treating the track length as the distance for every meeting on a circular track, ignoring that the first meeting may occur before a full lap is gained.
  • Using arithmetic mean of speeds instead of relative speed when runners move in opposite directions on a circle.
  • Forgetting to convert the nominal annual rate to the effective rate matching the installment frequency in present‑value calculations.
  • Applying the ordinary annuity formula when the problem states payments are made at the beginning of each period.
  • Mixing up sacrificing ratio and gaining ratio in partnership admission/retirement problems.
  • Assuming the alligation ratio applies directly to cost prices without first adjusting for profit or loss percentages.
  • Neglecting to split the timeline into constant‑capital intervals when a partner’s capital changes mid‑year.
