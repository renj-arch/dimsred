Key Concepts

Legal General Knowledge for CLAT

Legal GK covers important legal terms, landmark cases, constitutional amendments, and legal bodies. Questions test familiarity with the Indian legal system — no deep legal expertise needed, just awareness of landmark developments.

  • Important legal terms:
    • Writ: Formal written order issued by a court (habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto).
    • PIL: Public Interest Litigation — petition by public-spirited person for enforcement of others' rights.
    • Ultra vires: Beyond one's legal power/authority.
    • Habeas corpus: 'You shall have the body' — produces a detained person before court.
    • Mandamus: 'We command' — directs a public official to perform duty.
    • Prohibition: Stops a lower court from exceeding jurisdiction.
    • Certiorari: Transfers a case to a higher court for review.
    • Quo warranto: 'By what authority' — challenges a person's right to hold public office.
    • Locus standi: Right/standing to bring a case before court.
    • Bona fide: In good faith.
    • Malafide: In bad faith.
    • Audi alteram partem: Hear the other side — principle of natural justice.
    • Nemo judex in causa sua: No one should be a judge in their own case.
    • Res judicata: Matter already judged — cannot be re-litigated.
    • Stare decisis: Precedent — courts follow earlier decisions.
    • Ratio decidendi: Reason for decision (binding part of precedent).
    • Obiter dictum: Remark made in passing (not binding).
    • Mens rea: Guilty mind — mental element of crime.
    • Actus reus: Guilty act.
    • Caveat emptor: Let the buyer beware.
    • Injunction: Court order requiring someone to do/not do something.
    • Affidavit: Sworn written statement.
    • Plaintiff/Defendant: Person suing / person being sued.
    • Appellant/Respondent: Person appealing / person responding to appeal.
    • Decree/Order/Judgment: Formal adjudication / interim direction / final decision with reasons.
  • Landmark cases:
    • Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): Basic structure doctrine — Parliament cannot alter the Constitution's basic structure.
    • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Expanded Article 21 — right to life and personal liberty includes right to travel abroad; procedure must be fair, just, reasonable.
    • Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): Election dispute; part of basic structure doctrine context.
    • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): Balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; judicial review is basic structure.
    • S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): Article 356 (President's Rule) limitations; secularism is basic structure.
    • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): Sexual harassment at workplace guidelines before legislation.
    • Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985): Right to livelihood is part of Article 21.
    • MC Mehta cases: Environmental protection, oleum gas leak, polluter pays.
    • Indira Sawhney v. Union of India (1992): Mandal case — 27% OBC reservations, creamy layer exclusion.
    • Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018): Decriminalized consensual homosexual relations (Section 377 partially struck down).
    • Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018): Decriminalized adultery (Section 497 struck down).
    • Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017): Right to privacy is a fundamental right under Article 21.
    • Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017): Triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat) declared unconstitutional.
    • Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018): Sabarimala — women of all ages can enter temple.
    • K.S. Puttaswamy (2017): Privacy — see above.
    • Common Cause v. Union of India (2018): Right to die with dignity — passive euthanasia and living will.
    • Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020): Internet shutdowns must be temporary, proportionate, and published.
  • Constitutional amendments:
    • 1st Amendment (1951): Added reasonable restrictions on free speech (Articles 19(2), 31A, 31B, Ninth Schedule).
    • 24th Amendment (1971): Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights.
    • 25th Amendment (1971): Curbed right to property; gave effect to Directive Principles.
    • 42nd Amendment (1976): 'Mini Constitution' — added Socialist, Secular, Integrity to Preamble; Fundamental Duties (Art 51A); Directive Principles precedence.
    • 44th Amendment (1978): Right to Property removed from Fundamental Rights (made legal right under Art 300A); emergency safeguards.
    • 52nd Amendment (1985): Anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule).
    • 73rd & 74th Amendments (1992): Panchayats and Municipalities (Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules).
    • 86th Amendment (2002): Right to Education (Article 21A) for ages 6–14.
    • 89th Amendment (2003): National Commission for SCs/STs bifurcated.
    • 91st Amendment (2003): Anti-defection strengthening; cabinet size limit (15% of Lok Sabha).
    • 97th Amendment (2011): Cooperative societies (Article 43B, Part IXB).
    • 101st Amendment (2016): GST (Article 246A, 269A, 279A).
    • 102nd Amendment (2018): Constitutional status to National Commission for Backward Classes (Article 338B).
    • 103rd Amendment (2019): 10% EWS reservation (Articles 15(6), 16(6)).
    • 104th Amendment (2020): Extended SC/ST reservation to 2030; ended Anglo-Indian nomination.
    • 105th Amendment (2021): States can identify OBCs (restored state power after 102nd).
  • Legal bodies:
    • Supreme Court: Apex court; Articles 124–147; collegium appointments; Article 32 writ jurisdiction.
    • High Courts: Articles 214–231; Article 226 writ jurisdiction.
    • National Legal Services Authority (NALSA): Free legal aid; Lok Adalats.
    • Bar Council of India: Regulates legal profession and legal education.
    • Law Commission of India: Recommends legal reforms.
    • National Human Rights Commission (NHRC): Statutory (1993) — human rights protection.
    • National Commission for Women (NCW): Statutory (1992) — women's rights.
    • National Commission for SCs/STs/OBCs: Constitutional bodies (Articles 338, 338A, 338B).
    • Central Vigilance Commission (CVC): Statutory (2003) — anti-corruption vigilance.
    • Lokpal: Statutory anti-corruption body at Centre (Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013).
    • Election Commission of India: Article 324 — conducts elections.
    • National Green Tribunal (NGT): Statutory (2010) — environmental disputes.
    • Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT): Recovery of bank dues.
    • National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT): Company law and insolvency (IBC).
    • Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions: Consumer protection (Consumer Protection Act, 2019).
  • Important laws:
    • Constitution of India (1950)
    • Indian Penal Code, 1860 (now Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023)
    • Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (now Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023)
    • Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (now Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023)
    • Indian Contract Act, 1872
    • Right to Information Act, 2005
    • Consumer Protection Act, 2019
    • Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013
    • Whistleblowers Protection Act, 2014
    • Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988
    • Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989
    • Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005
    • Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013
    • Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009
    • National Food Security Act, 2013
    • Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016
    • Goods and Services Tax (Constitutional Amendment), 2016 / CGST Act, 2017
    • Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023
    • Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (2023 — new criminal laws replacing IPC, CrPC, Evidence Act)
Technique: Basic structure
Question: Which case established the basic structure doctrine?
Answer: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).
Technique: Habeas corpus
Question: Which writ is used to produce a detained person before court?
Answer: Habeas corpus ('you shall have the body').

Common Traps

  • Confusing writs (habeas corpus vs mandamus vs certiorari vs prohibition vs quo warranto).
  • Mixing up amendment numbers (e.g., 86th = education, 101st = GST).
  • Thinking IPC still applies — replaced by Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (2023) for new offences.
  • Missing that PIL relaxes locus standi (public-spirited person can file for others).
  • Confusing NALSA (legal aid) with NHRC (human rights).

Memory Tricks

  • Basic structure: Kesavananda Bharati (1973).
  • Privacy: Puttaswamy (2017), Article 21.
  • GST: 101st Amendment (2016).
  • Education: 86th Amendment (2002), Article 21A.
  • Anti-defection: 52nd Amendment, Tenth Schedule.
Legal GK exam strategy
Memorize landmark cases with year and one-line holding; constitutional amendments with number and subject; legal terms with one-line meaning. For passage-based questions, read the passage first — it often contains the answer context.
