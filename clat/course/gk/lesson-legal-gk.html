Legal General Knowledge for CLAT

Legal GK covers important legal terms, landmark cases, constitutional amendments, and legal bodies. Questions test familiarity with the Indian legal system — no deep legal expertise needed, just awareness of landmark developments.

  • Important legal terms:
    • Writ: Formal written order issued by a court (habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto).
    • PIL: Public Interest Litigation — petition by public-spirited person for enforcement of others' rights.
    • Ultra vires: Beyond one's legal power/authority.
    • Habeas corpus: 'You shall have the body' — produces a detained person before court.
    • Mandamus: 'We command' — directs a public official to perform duty.
    • Prohibition: Stops a lower court from exceeding jurisdiction.
    • Certiorari: Transfers a case to a higher court for review.
    • Quo warranto: 'By what authority' — challenges a person's right to hold public office.
    • Locus standi: Right/standing to bring a case before court.
    • Bona fide: In good faith.
    • Malafide: In bad faith.
    • Audi alteram partem: Hear the other side — principle of natural justice.
    • Nemo judex in causa sua: No one should be a judge in their own case.
    • Res judicata: Matter already judged — cannot be re-litigated.
    • Stare decisis: Precedent — courts follow earlier decisions.
    • Ratio decidendi: Reason for decision (binding part of precedent).
    • Obiter dictum: Remark made in passing (not binding).
    • Mens rea: Guilty mind — mental element of crime.
    • Actus reus: Guilty act.
    • Caveat emptor: Let the buyer beware.
    • Injunction: Court order requiring someone to do/not do something.
    • Affidavit: Sworn written statement.
    • Plaintiff/Defendant: Person suing / person being sued.
    • Appellant/Respondent: Person appealing / person responding to appeal.
    • Decree/Order/Judgment: Formal adjudication / interim direction / final decision with reasons.
  • Landmark cases:
    • Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): Basic structure doctrine — Parliament cannot alter the Constitution's basic structure.
    • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Expanded Article 21 — right to life and personal liberty includes right to travel abroad; procedure must be fair, just, reasonable.
    • Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): Election dispute; part of basic structure doctrine context.
    • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): Balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; judicial review is basic structure.
    • S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): Article 356 (President's Rule) limitations; secularism is basic structure.
    • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): Sexual harassment at workplace guidelines before legislation.
    • Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985): Right to livelihood is part of Article 21.
    • MC Mehta cases: Environmental protection, oleum gas leak, polluter pays.
    • Indira Sawhney v. Union of India (1992): Mandal case — 27% OBC reservations, creamy layer exclusion.
    • Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018): Decriminalized consensual homosexual relations (Section 377 partially struck down).
    • Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018): Decriminalized adultery (Section 497 struck down).
    • Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017): Right to privacy is a fundamental right under Article 21.
    • Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017): Triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat) declared unconstitutional.
    • Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018): Sabarimala — women of all ages can enter temple.
    • K.S. Puttaswamy (2017): Privacy — see above.
    • Common Cause v. Union of India (2018): Right to die with dignity — passive euthanasia and living will.
    • Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020): Internet shutdowns must be temporary, proportionate, and published.
  • Constitutional amendments:
    • 1st Amendment (1951): Added reasonable restrictions on free speech (Articles 19(2), 31A, 31B, Ninth Schedule).
    • 24th Amendment (1971): Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights.
    • 25th Amendment (1971): Curbed right to property; gave effect to Directive Principles.
    • 42nd Amendment (1976): 'Mini Constitution' — added Socialist, Secular, Integrity to Preamble; Fundamental Duties (Art 51A); Directive Principles precedence.
    • 44th Amendment (1978): Right to Property removed from Fundamental Rights (made legal right under Art 300A); emergency safeguards.
    • 52nd Amendment (1985): Anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule).
    • 73rd & 74th Amendments (1992): Panchayats and Municipalities (Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules).
    • 86th Amendment (2002): Right to Education (Article 21A) for ages 6–14.
    • 89th Amendment (2003): National Commission for SCs/STs bifurcated.
    • 91st Amendment (2003): Anti-defection strengthening; cabinet size limit (15% of Lok Sabha).
    • 97th Amendment (2011): Cooperative societies (Article 43B, Part IXB).
    • 101st Amendment (2016): GST (Article 246A, 269A, 279A).
    • 102nd Amendment (2018): Constitutional status to National Commission for Backward Classes (Article 338B).
    • 103rd Amendment (2019): 10% EWS reservation (Articles 15(6), 16(6)).
    • 104th Amendment (2020): Extended SC/ST reservation to 2030; ended Anglo-Indian nomination.
    • 105th Amendment (2021): States can identify OBCs (restored state power after 102nd).

Common Traps

  • Confusing writs (habeas corpus vs mandamus vs certiorari vs prohibition vs quo warranto).
  • Mixing up amendment numbers (e.g., 86th = education, 101st = GST).
  • Thinking IPC still applies — replaced by Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (2023) for new offences.
  • Missing that PIL relaxes locus standi (public-spirited person can file for others).
  • Confusing NALSA (legal aid) with NHRC (human rights).

Doctrine of Eclipse, Severability, and Waiver in Fundamental Rights

The doctrine of eclipse operates when a pre‑constitutional law becomes inconsistent with a fundamental right after the Constitution’s commencement. The law is not dead; it is merely eclipsed and revives if the constitutional prohibition is removed (e.g., by amendment). The Supreme Court in Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955) held that a law violating Article 19(1)(g) was eclipsed, not void ab initio. Severability allows a court to strike down only the offending portion of a statute while preserving the remainder, provided the valid part is capable of standing independently. The test, articulated in R.M.D.C. v. Union of India (1957), asks whether the legislature would have enacted the valid portion alone. Waiver of fundamental rights is generally not permitted because these rights are considered inalienable and part of the basic structure; however, a person may voluntarily forego a procedural benefit (e.g., not invoking the right to silence) without extinguishing the right itself. The Court in Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985) reiterated that fundamental rights cannot be waived by contract or conduct that defeats public policy.

Practical application: when a statute contains a clause that infringes Article 14, the court examines whether the clause is severable. If the clause is integral to the legislative scheme, the entire provision may fall. In State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat (2005), the Court severed the unconstitutional portion of a cattle‑preservation law while upholding the rest. Understanding the interplay of eclipse, severability, and waiver is essential for answering questions on the validity of legacy laws and on the limits of contractual relinquishment of constitutional protections.

Judicial Review of Constitutional Amendments: Basic Structure and Beyond

The power of Parliament to amend the Constitution under Article 368 is not unlimited. The Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) judgment introduced the basic structure doctrine, holding that amendments which alter the Constitution’s core features — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law — are void. Subsequent cases refined the doctrine: Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) struck down the 39th Amendment’s clause insulating the Prime Minister’s election from judicial scrutiny; Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) invalidated the 42nd Amendment’s attempt to curtail judicial review and to give primacy to Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights; Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) applied the doctrine prospectively to amendments after 24 April 1973. The Court also held in I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 1973 are subject to basic structure review.

Two procedural safeguards reinforce the doctrine. First, certain amendments require ratification by at least half the state legislatures (e.g., changes to the federal scheme, representation of states in Parliament). Second, the President’s assent is mandatory, but the President cannot withhold assent on a money bill; for constitutional amendment bills, the President’s role is formal. The doctrine does not freeze the Constitution; it permits evolution provided the identity of the Constitution remains intact. Examiners often test the distinction between “amending power” and “constituent power,” and whether a particular amendment (e.g., 99th Amendment establishing the National Judicial Appointments Commission) violates basic structure — the Supreme Court struck it down in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) for impairing judicial independence.

Technique: Identifying Basic Structure Violation
A 2022 amendment removes the power of High Courts to issue writs under Article 226 and transfers it exclusively to a newly created National Writ Tribunal. Does this amendment violate the basic structure?
Yes. The power of High Courts to issue writs under Article 226 is a core component of judicial review and the separation of powers, both recognized as basic structure elements in Kesavananda (1973) and reaffirmed in L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997). Removing this jurisdiction and vesting it in a tribunal not constitutionally entrenched destroys the High Court’s constitutional role and therefore violates the basic structure. The amendment would be struck down.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks whether an amendment is valid, first list the basic structure features (judicial review, federalism, secularism, democracy, rule of law, separation of powers). Then map the amendment’s effect onto that list. If the amendment removes or severely curtails any listed feature, answer “violates basic structure.” Remember the prospective application rule from Waman Rao: amendments before 24 April 1973 are immune unless they were challenged earlier.

Public Interest Litigation: Evolution, Locus Standi, and Procedural Innovations

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) transformed the Indian judiciary from a passive adjudicator into an active guardian of social justice. The relaxation of locus standi began with Ratlam Municipal Council v. Vardhichand (1980) and crystallised in S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981), where the Court held that any public‑spirited citizen could approach the Court on behalf of a disadvantaged group unable to represent itself. The “epistolary jurisdiction” — treating letters and postcards as writ petitions — was pioneered in Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) concerning undertrial prisoners. Subsequent innovations include the appointment of amicus curiae, court‑monitored committees, and continuing mandamus (e.g., Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998) on the Hawala scandal). The Supreme Court in Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) expanded PIL to bonded labour, while M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) established the “absolute liability” principle for hazardous industries.

Procedural safeguards prevent abuse: the Court may impose exemplary costs on frivolous PILs (State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2007)), and the “letter petition” route is now regulated by Supreme Court rules requiring verification. The High Courts exercise parallel PIL jurisdiction under Article 226. Examiners test knowledge of landmark PILs, the distinction between PIL and class action (the latter is statutory, e.g., under the Companies Act), and the principle that PIL cannot be used to enforce private contractual rights. Recent trends show the Court insisting on a “real and substantial” public interest, not merely a “publicity interest” (Dr. B. Singh v. Union of India (2020)).

Constitutional vs Statutory Bodies: Appointment, Tenure, and Removal Mechanisms

Constitutional bodies derive their existence, composition, and powers directly from the Constitution; statutory bodies are created by an Act of Parliament or State Legislature. This distinction governs appointment, tenure, and removal. The Election Commission (Article 324) comprises the Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and Election Commissioners appointed by the President; the CEC enjoys security of tenure akin to a Supreme Court judge — removal only by impeachment. In contrast, the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) is statutory (Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993); its Chairperson and members are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a high‑powered committee, and they hold office for five years or until age 70, removable by the President on proved misbehaviour after Supreme Court inquiry.

The Central Vigilance Commission (CVC) (Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003) consists of a Central Vigilance Commissioner and up to two Vigilance Commissioners, appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee comprising the Prime Minister, Home Minister, and Leader of Opposition. Their tenure is four years or until age 65, removable only by the President on Supreme Court advice. The Lokpal (Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013) has a Chairperson and up to eight members, appointed by the President after selection by a committee including the Prime Minister, Speaker, Leader of Opposition, Chief Justice of India (or nominee), and an eminent jurist. Tenure is five years or until age 70; removal follows the same Supreme Court inquiry route.

Key exam points: (1) Constitutional bodies (e.g., Finance Commission, Union Public Service Commission, Comptroller and Auditor General) enjoy greater independence because their removal requires impeachment or a special majority. (2) Statutory bodies can be abolished or restructured by ordinary legislation. (3) The “Collegium” system for judicial appointments (Supreme Court and High Courts) is a judge‑made convention, not a constitutional provision, and is subject to executive‑judicial tension. (4) The National Commission for Backward Classes (Article 338B) gained constitutional status via the 102nd Amendment, aligning its removal safeguards with other constitutional commissions.

Recent Criminal Law Overhaul: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam – Key Substantive and Procedural Shifts

In 2023 Parliament enacted three statutes replacing the colonial‑era criminal framework: the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) (replacing the Indian Penal Code, 1860), the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) (replacing the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973), and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) (replacing the Indian Evidence Act, 1872). The BNS reorganises offences into 20 chapters, introduces community service as a punishment for petty offences, defines “terrorist act” and “organised crime” with enhanced penalties, and removes the offence of sedition (Section 124A IPC) while inserting a new provision on “acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.” It also raises the age of criminal responsibility from 7 to 12 years, with a rebuttable presumption of maturity for 12‑18 year‑olds.

The BNSS modernises procedure: it mandates zero‑FIR registration, introduces timelines for investigation (90 days for offences punishable up to 10 years, 180 days for graver offences), provides for audio‑video recording of statements, and allows trial in absentia for proclaimed offenders. It replaces “arrest” with “detention” for certain minor offences and expands the scope of plea bargaining. The BSA updates evidence law: it recognises electronic records as primary evidence, prescribes a certificate‑based authentication mechanism (Section 63), and revises the hearsay rule to admit statements made under certain reliable circumstances. It also codifies the “doctrine of confirmation” for accomplice evidence.

Transitional provisions: offences committed before the notified date continue to be governed by the old codes; however, procedural provisions of BNSS apply to pending investigations and trials unless they prejudice the accused. The new laws came into force on 1 July 2024 (as notified). Examiners focus on the renamed sections (e.g., murder now Section 101 BNS, theft Section 303 BNS), the removal of sedition, the introduction of “community service,” and the procedural timelines. A common trap is assuming the new codes apply retrospectively to all pending cases — they do not, except for procedural aspects that are beneficial to the accused.

  • Confusing the doctrine of eclipse with the doctrine of severability — eclipse concerns pre‑constitutional laws, severability concerns statutory provisions.
  • Assuming every constitutional amendment after 1973 is automatically subject to basic structure review; the Ninth Schedule immunity was only removed prospectively from 1973 (Waman Rao).
  • Treating PIL as a substitute for class action suits; PIL is a constitutional remedy, class action is statutory and requires numerosity, commonality, and adequacy of representation.
  • Believing statutory bodies enjoy the same removal protection as constitutional bodies; only constitutional bodies require impeachment or special majority for removal.
  • Applying the new criminal codes retrospectively to substantive offences committed before 1 July 2024; substantive law changes are prospective unless expressly made retrospective.
  • Overlooking that the President’s assent to a constitutional amendment bill is a formality — the President cannot return it for reconsideration.
  • Misidentifying the “basic structure” features; the list is not exhaustive but includes judicial review, federalism, secularism, democracy, rule of law, and separation of powers.

Basic Structure Doctrine: Scope, Evolution, and Key Judgments

The basic structure doctrine limits the amending power of Parliament under Article 368 by declaring that certain core features of the Constitution cannot be altered. The doctrine originated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), where a 13‑judge bench held by a 7‑6 majority that Parliament could amend any part of the Constitution but not its basic structure. The Court did not enumerate an exhaustive list; instead it identified illustrative elements such as the supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. Subsequent judgments have expanded and clarified the doctrine. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) the Court added free and fair elections to the basic structure. Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) reinforced judicial review and the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles as part of the basic structure. Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) introduced the “doctrine of prospective overruling” for amendments made before 24 April 1973, protecting them from challenge. The S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) judgment extended the doctrine to the federal balance, holding that the dismissal of a state government under Article 356 must respect the basic structure. More recently, Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) (the NJAC case) reaffirmed judicial independence as a basic feature. The doctrine operates as a substantive limitation: any amendment that destroys or damages a basic feature is void, regardless of the procedural majority achieved. Courts apply a two‑step test — first, identify the feature claimed to be basic; second, assess whether the amendment abrogates or emasculates that feature. The burden of proof lies on the challenger. The doctrine does not freeze the Constitution; Parliament may still amend non‑basic provisions, including the addition of new fundamental rights, provided the core architecture remains intact.

Judicial Review of Ordinances and the Requirement of Parliamentary Approval

Article 123 empowers the President to promulgate ordinances when Parliament is not in session, provided the President is satisfied that circumstances exist which render immediate action necessary. An ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament but ceases to operate at the expiration of six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament unless it is approved by both Houses before that period ends. The Supreme Court has repeatedly examined the constitutional limits of this power. In R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) the Court held that the President’s satisfaction is justiciable only on the ground of mala fides, not on the adequacy of the circumstances. D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987) struck down the practice of repromulgating ordinances without legislative approval, declaring it a fraud on the Constitution. The Court ruled that repeated repromulgation bypasses parliamentary scrutiny and violates the separation of powers. In Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) a seven‑judge bench reiterated that an ordinance must be laid before Parliament at its next session and that failure to do so renders it void ab initio. The judgment also clarified that the President’s satisfaction is subject to judicial review for arbitrariness, and that the ordinance‑making power cannot be used to circumvent the legislative process for controversial or permanent legislation. The Court has also held that an ordinance cannot amend the Constitution; only a constitutional amendment under Article 368 can do so. Consequently, any ordinance that seeks to alter a basic structure feature would be ultra vires. The practical effect of these rulings is that the executive must demonstrate genuine urgency, avoid serial repromulgation, and ensure timely parliamentary ratification. Failure on any of these counts invites judicial invalidation.

Examiner's Trap
A state government repromulgates an ordinance three times in succession without placing it before the legislature. The ordinance deals with land acquisition for a public highway. A petitioner challenges the validity of the third repromulgation. Decide the outcome citing the relevant precedent.
The third repromulgation is unconstitutional. In D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987) the Supreme Court held that repeated repromulgation of an ordinance without legislative approval constitutes a fraud on the Constitution and violates the separation of powers. The Court emphasized that the ordinance‑making power is an emergency provision, not a parallel legislative route. Since the ordinance was repromulgated thrice without being laid before the legislature, it is void ab initio. The subject matter (land acquisition) does not alter this principle; the defect is procedural and fatal.
Exam Day Strategy
When a question asks about the validity of an ordinance, first check whether it was laid before Parliament within six weeks of reassembly. Then verify if there was any repromulgation without approval. Cite D.C. Wadhwa for repromulgation and Krishna Kumar Singh for the requirement of laying before Parliament. Remember that the President’s satisfaction is reviewable only for mala fides or arbitrariness, not for policy wisdom.

Constitutional Emergency Provisions: Articles 352, 356, and 360 and Judicial Scrutiny

The Constitution provides three distinct emergency powers. Article 352 deals with a Proclamation of Emergency on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. Such a proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month by a special majority and can continue for six months, extendable indefinitely with parliamentary approval every six months. During a national emergency, the federal distribution of legislative powers shifts to the Centre, and fundamental rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended; other rights may be suspended by presidential order subject to parliamentary approval. Article 356 empowers the President to assume the functions of a state government if the constitutional machinery in that state fails. Known as President’s Rule, it requires parliamentary approval within two months and can last up to three years with periodic extensions, subject to the conditions laid down in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994). The Bommai judgment established that the proclamation is subject to judicial review on grounds of mala fides, irrelevance, or violation of the basic structure, and that the dissolution of the state assembly cannot be automatic. Article 360 provides for a Financial Emergency when the financial stability or credit of India is threatened. It has never been invoked. The proclamation must be approved by Parliament within two months and empowers the Centre to direct states to observe canons of financial propriety and to reduce salaries of public servants. Judicial scrutiny of emergency proclamations focuses on the existence of the factual basis, the proportionality of the response, and compliance with procedural safeguards. In Minerva Mills the Court held that the power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 cannot be used to immunize emergency provisions from basic structure review. The 44th Amendment (1978) tightened the safeguards for Article 352 by replacing “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion” and requiring written advice of the Cabinet. For Article 356, the 44th Amendment added the requirement that the proclamation be laid before each House and that the state assembly not be dissolved before parliamentary approval. These amendments reflect the constitutional design to prevent misuse while preserving the Union’s ability to respond to genuine crises.

Law Commission of India: Major Reports and Legislative Impact

The Law Commission of India, a non‑statutory executive body reconstituted every three years, undertakes research and recommends reforms in substantive and procedural law. Its reports have frequently shaped legislation. The 14th Report (1958) on the Indian Penal Code led to the 1972 amendment introducing the concept of “criminal breach of trust” and revising offences against property. The 41st Report (1969) recommended the enactment of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, which replaced the 1898 Code and introduced anticipatory bail, plea bargaining (later added in 2005), and a revised scheme for trial of summary cases. The 84th Report (1980) on the Law of Evidence prompted the 2000 amendment to the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, incorporating provisions for electronic records and the admissibility of computer‑generated evidence. The 172nd Report (2000) on the Prevention of Corruption Act resulted in the 2018 amendment that widened the definition of “public servant”, introduced the offence of “undue advantage”, and provided for attachment of property. The 200th Report (2008) on the Arbitration and Conciliation Act recommended the 2015 and 2019 amendments that limited judicial intervention, introduced the concept of “institutional arbitration”, and clarified the grounds for setting aside arbitral awards. The 230th Report (2009) on electoral reforms suggested the introduction of “none of the above” (NOTA) on ballot papers, later implemented by the Election Commission in 2013. The 262nd Report (2015) on the death penalty recommended its abolition for all offences except terrorism‑related offences; the recommendation remains under parliamentary consideration. The Commission’s reports are not binding, but the government often introduces bills based on them, and courts frequently cite them as persuasive authority when interpreting statutes. Aspirants should be familiar with the report numbers, year, subject matter, and the consequent legislative action, as examination questions often match a report to its legislative outcome.

Alternative Dispute Resolution Framework: Arbitration, Mediation, and Conciliation under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996

The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, consolidates the law relating to domestic arbitration, international commercial arbitration, and enforcement of foreign awards, while also providing for conciliation. The Act is based on the UNCITRAL Model Law and replaces the Arbitration Act, 1940. Part I governs domestic arbitration and international commercial arbitration seated in India. It mandates that an arbitration agreement be in writing (Section 7) and that the arbitral tribunal be constituted as agreed by the parties or, failing agreement, by the Supreme Court or High Court (Section 11). The tribunal has the power to rule on its own jurisdiction (competence‑competence) under Section 16. The grounds for setting aside an arbitral award are enumerated in Section 34 and mirror Article 34 of the Model Law: incapacity, invalid agreement, lack of proper notice, award beyond scope, composition irregularity, and conflict with public policy of India. The 2015 amendment narrowed the “public policy” ground to “fundamental policy of Indian law”, “interest of justice”, and “patent illegality”. The 2019 amendment introduced the Arbitration Council of India (ACI) for grading arbitral institutions and accrediting arbitrators, and mandated time‑bound completion of proceedings (six months for domestic, twelve months for international, extendable by six months). Part II deals with enforcement of foreign awards under the New York Convention (Section 44) and the Geneva Convention (Section 53). Courts may refuse enforcement only on the limited grounds listed in Sections 48 and 57. Part III provides for conciliation, a non‑binding process where a conciliator assists parties in reaching a settlement; the settlement agreement has the status of an arbitral award (Section 74). The Act also recognises mediation through the Mediation Bill, 2021 (pending enactment), which proposes a statutory framework for court‑annexed and private mediation. Key judicial pronouncements include Bharat Aluminium Co. v. Kaiser Aluminium Technical Services (2012) (seat theory), Vidya Drolia v. Durga Trading Corp. (2020) (arbitrability of landlord‑tenant disputes), and ONGC v. Saw Pipes (2003) (public policy interpretation). Understanding the distinction between arbitration (binding), conciliation (facilitated settlement), and mediation (party‑driven negotiation) is essential for answering application‑based questions.

  • Confusing the basic structure doctrine with the doctrine of eclipse; the former limits amendment power, the latter deals with pre‑constitutional laws.
  • Assuming that every ordinance automatically lapses after six weeks; it lapses only if not approved by Parliament within that period.
  • Treating President’s Rule under Article 356 as immune from judicial review; S.R. Bommai makes it reviewable for mala fides and constitutional compliance.
  • Believing that Law Commission reports are binding on the legislature; they are advisory and only become law when enacted.
  • Thinking that an arbitral award can be set aside on mere errors of fact or law; Section 34 permits challenge only on specified jurisdictional and public‑policy grounds.
  • Overlooking the 2015 amendment’s narrowing of the “public policy” ground in arbitration; the pre‑2015 broader interpretation is no longer good law.
  • Failing to distinguish between conciliation (conciliator proposes terms) and mediation (mediator facilitates parties’ own solution); the legal consequences differ.

Doctrine of Pith and Substance and Colourable Legislation

The doctrine of pith and substance is the primary tool used by Indian courts to resolve conflicts between Union and State legislative entries in the Seventh Schedule. When a law is challenged on the ground that it encroaches upon a subject allocated to the other legislature, the court examines the true nature and character of the enactment — its “pith and substance” — rather than its incidental effects. If the dominant purpose falls within the competence of the enacting legislature, the law is upheld even if it trenches on matters assigned to the other list. The Supreme Court in State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951) and K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1954) established that the legislature’s motive is irrelevant; only the substantive content matters.

Colourable legislation is a corollary concept. It refers to a law that, while formally within the legislative competence, in reality seeks to achieve an objective that lies outside that competence. The court looks behind the façade to see whether the legislature has “coloured” its power to do indirectly what it cannot do directly. In K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo the Court held that a law imposing a tax on the sale of motor spirit, though entered under the State List entry for “taxes on the sale of goods”, was colourable because its real object was to regulate the price of motor spirit — a Union subject under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act. The test is whether the legislation, on a fair reading, is a genuine exercise of the power conferred or a fraud on the Constitution.

The interplay of the two doctrines ensures that the federal balance is maintained without paralysing legitimate legislation. A law that incidentally affects a subject in another list is saved by pith and substance; a law that masquerades as a valid exercise but actually pursues an impermissible aim is struck down as colourable. The burden of proving colourability lies on the challenger, and the presumption is in favour of constitutionality. Courts have repeatedly warned against substituting judicial policy for legislative judgment; the doctrine is not a licence to second‑guess legislative wisdom but a safeguard against constitutional fraud.

Key Judicial Illustrations

  • State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963) — upheld a Union law on coal mines regulation despite incidental impact on State land revenue.
  • Union of India v. H.S. Dhillon (1972) — struck down a State law that, under the guise of regulating agricultural produce markets, effectively controlled inter‑State trade.
  • Gujarat University v. Krishna Ranganath Mudholkar (1963) — applied pith and substance to uphold a State university act affecting Union‑list “education” entry.

Doctrine of Harmonious Construction and the Rule of Ejusdem Generis

When two or more constitutional provisions or statutory sections appear to conflict, the doctrine of harmonious construction directs courts to interpret them in a manner that gives effect to both, preserving the legislative scheme. The principle rests on the presumption that the legislature does not intend self‑contradiction. The Supreme Court in CIT v. Hindustan Bulk Carriers (2003) reiterated that a construction which renders one provision redundant must be avoided; instead, the provisions should be read together so that each operates in its own sphere.

The rule of ejusdem generis (“of the same kind”) is a specific canon of statutory interpretation applied when a general word follows an enumeration of specific items. The general word is restricted to things of the same class as those specifically listed. For example, in the phrase “houses, buildings, structures, and other premises”, “other premises” is limited to immovable property akin to houses and buildings. The rule does not apply if the specific words exhaust the genus or if the legislative context shows a contrary intention. In State of Maharashtra v. Narayan Rao (1985) the Court refused to apply ejusdem generis because the enumerated items were illustrative, not exhaustive.

Both doctrines are frequently tested in combination. A typical examination scenario presents a statute with a specific list followed by a catch‑all phrase and asks whether a particular activity falls within the catch‑all. The candidate must first decide whether the specific words form a distinct genus; if they do, the general word is confined to that genus. If the specific words are disparate, the general word retains its wide meaning, and harmonious construction may be invoked to reconcile the provision with other sections of the same Act.

Application in Constitutional Interpretation

  • Article 246 (distribution of legislative powers) read with the Seventh Schedule — harmonious construction prevents overlap between Union and State lists.
  • Article 13 (laws inconsistent with fundamental rights) and Article 31A (saving of certain laws) — the Court harmonises them by holding that Article 31A is an exception carved out by the Constitution itself.
  • Section 2(1)(a) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (“arbitration agreement”) and Section 7 (form of arbitration agreement) — ejusdem generis limits “in writing” to the modes enumerated in Section 7.
Worked Example: Ejusdem Generis in a Statutory Definition
Section 2(c) of the Environmental Protection Act defines “pollutant” as “any solid, liquid, gaseous, or other substance which causes pollution”. A factory discharges heat (thermal energy) into a river. Does heat fall within the definition?
Step 1: Identify the specific enumeration — solid, liquid, gaseous. These are states of matter. Step 2: Determine the genus — physical substances having mass and volume. Step 3: Assess the general phrase “or other substance”. By ejusdem generis, “other substance” is limited to entities of the same genus, i.e., material substances. Step 4: Heat is energy, not a material substance; it lacks mass and volume. Conclusion: Heat is not covered by the definition. The factory’s thermal discharge would have to be regulated under a separate provision dealing with “thermal pollution” if such exists.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question presents a list followed by a catch‑all phrase, first write down the specific items and ask whether they share a single, identifiable class. If they do, the general word is confined to that class; if they are heterogeneous, the general word remains broad. Always mention the presumption against redundancy — it scores easy marks for harmonious construction.

Repugnancy under Article 254: Federal Distribution of Legislative Powers

Article 254 of the Constitution provides the mechanism for resolving conflicts between Union and State laws on subjects in the Concurrent List (List III). When a State law is repugnant to a Union law on the same matter, the Union law prevails and the State law becomes void to the extent of the repugnancy. However, the State law can be saved if it has been reserved for the President’s consideration and has received his assent; in that case the State law prevails in that State, subject to Parliament’s power to override it subsequently by enacting a law “adding to, amending, varying or repealing” the State law.

The test for repugnancy was laid down in M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India (1979): (1) the two laws must contain provisions that are directly inconsistent so that they cannot stand together; (2) the inconsistency must be on the same subject matter; and (3) the Union law must be a valid exercise of legislative competence. Mere possibility of both laws operating without conflict does not amount to repugnancy. In Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1983) the Court held that a State law imposing a higher penalty for the same offence under a Central Act was not repugnant because the Central Act did not occupy the entire field.

The doctrine of occupied field is distinct from repugnancy. If Parliament has legislated exhaustively on a Concurrent subject, a State law on any aspect of that subject is invalid even without direct conflict, because the field is deemed “occupied”. The Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Bharat Shanti Lal Shah (2008) clarified that occupation of the field must be evident from the language, scheme, and purpose of the Central legislation.

Procedural Safeguards

  1. State legislature passes a law on a Concurrent subject.
  2. If the law is repugnant to an existing Central law, the State may reserve the Bill for the President’s assent under Article 254(2).
  3. President may assent, withhold assent, or return the Bill for reconsideration.
  4. If assented, the State law operates in that State notwithstanding the repugnancy.
  5. Parliament may later enact a law to override the State law, even on the same subject, by virtue of its plenary power under Article 254(2) proviso.

Landmark Applications

  • Gujarat University v. Krishna Ranganath Mudholkar (1963) — State law on university standards held not repugnant to Central UGC Act because the Central Act left room for State regulation.
  • Zameer Ahmed Latifur Rehman Sheikh v. State of Maharashtra (2010) — State amendment to the Code of Criminal Procedure (a Concurrent subject) prevailed after Presidential assent.
  • State of Tamil Nadu v. State of Kerala (2014) — Water disputes legislation: Central law on inter‑State rivers occupied the field, rendering State law inoperative.

Anti‑Defection Law: Tenth Schedule, Judicial Interpretation, and Recent Amendments

The Tenth Schedule, inserted by the 52nd Amendment Act, 1985, aims to curb political defections by disqualifying legislators who voluntarily give up membership of their party or vote contrary to party directions. The Schedule applies to both Parliament and State Legislatures. A member incurs disqualification if (a) he voluntarily gives up membership of his political party; (b) he votes or abstains from voting in the House contrary to any direction issued by his party without prior permission; or (c) an independently elected member joins a political party after election.

The phrase “voluntarily gives up membership” has been interpreted broadly. In Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994) the Supreme Court held that a member’s conduct — such as publicly opposing the party, joining another party’s rally, or resigning from party posts — can amount to voluntary relinquishment even without a formal resignation letter. The Court emphasised that the test is objective: whether a reasonable inference of intention to abandon the party can be drawn from the member’s actions.

The 91st Amendment Act, 2003 introduced two critical changes: (1) the exemption for splits (one‑third of the legislature party) was deleted, eliminating the “split” defence; (2) a merger exemption was retained — a member is not disqualified if his original party merges with another party and not less than two‑thirds of the legislature party have agreed to the merger. The amendment also barred disqualified members from holding ministerial or remunerative political posts for the remainder of their term.

Judicial review of the Speaker’s/Chairman’s decision on disqualification is available under Article 136 (special leave) and Article 226/32 (writ jurisdiction). In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) the Court upheld the finality of the Speaker’s decision subject to judicial review for mala fides, perversity, or violation of natural justice. The 2020 judgment in Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly directed Speakers to decide disqualification petitions within a reasonable time (three months) and held that inordinate delay constitutes a violation of the Constitution.

Practical Implications for Legislators

  • Party whips must be issued in writing and communicated clearly; oral directions are insufficient for disqualification proceedings.
  • Independent members who join a party after election are immediately subject to the whip of that party.
  • Merger requires a formal resolution by the original party and acceptance by the merging party; mere alignment or alliance does not constitute a merger.
  • Disqualified members lose their seat but retain the right to contest future elections unless separately barred.

Contempt of Court: Civil and Criminal Contempt, Procedure, and Defenses

The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 codifies the law of contempt in India, dividing it into civil contempt (wilful disobedience to any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ, or other process of a court, or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court) and criminal contempt (publication of any matter or doing of any act which scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of any court; prejudices or interferes with the due course of any judicial proceeding; or interferes or tends to interfere with the administration of justice in any other manner).

Civil contempt is essentially coercive — the court seeks compliance. The remedy includes imprisonment up to six months, fine up to ₹2,000, or both. The contemnor can purge the contempt by complying with the order. In Delhi Judicial Service Association v. State of Gujarat (1991) the Supreme Court held that a mere delay in compliance does not constitute wilful disobedience unless the delay is intentional and without reasonable cause.

Criminal contempt is punitive. The Act provides safeguards: (1) proceedings may be initiated only by the Supreme Court or High Court on its own motion or on a reference by the Advocate General or a law officer; (2) the contemnor has a right to be heard; (3) truth is a defence if the publication is in the public interest and made in good faith (Section 13); (4) fair and accurate reporting of judicial proceedings is protected (Section 4). In Prashant Bhushan v. Supreme Court (2020) the Court convicted a lawyer for tweets criticising the Chief Justice, holding that the statements scandalised the judiciary and were not protected as fair criticism.

The procedure for criminal contempt under Section 15 requires a notice to the contemnor, an opportunity to file a reply, and a hearing. The court may appoint an amicus curiae. The standard of proof is beyond reasonable doubt. Appeals lie to the Supreme Court under Article 136. The 2006 amendment (Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act, 2006) inserted Section 12(1A) allowing the court to impose a fine up to ₹5,000 in addition to imprisonment.

Key Defences

  • Truth and public interest — the contemnor must prove the statement is true and published for public good.
  • Fair criticism — bona fide criticism of a judicial decision after it is delivered, not targeting the judge personally.
  • Innocent publication — the publisher had no knowledge of the pending proceeding (Section 3).
  • Absence of wilfulness — for civil contempt, the disobedience must be deliberate.
  • Confusing pith and substance with colourable legislation; the former validates incidental encroachment, the latter strikes down a disguised ultra vires law.
  • Applying ejusdem generis when the specific words are not a single genus; the rule only operates if the enumeration forms a coherent class.
  • Assuming any conflict between Union and State laws on a Concurrent subject automatically triggers Article 254; direct inconsistency is required, not mere overlap.
  • Believing the anti‑defection “split” exemption still exists; it was removed by the 91st Amendment, 2003.
  • Treating criminal contempt as a strict liability offence; the Act requires mens rea (wilful scandalising) and provides statutory defences.
  • Overlooking the requirement of Presidential assent for a repugnant State law to survive; without assent the State law is void ab initio.
  • Misreading the “occupied field” doctrine as a subset of repugnancy; it operates even without direct conflict when Parliament has legislated exhaustively.
