Legal General Knowledge for CLAT

Legal GK covers important legal terms, landmark cases, constitutional amendments, and legal bodies. Questions test familiarity with the Indian legal system — no deep legal expertise needed, just awareness of landmark developments.

  • Important legal terms:
    • Writ: Formal written order issued by a court (habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto).
    • PIL: Public Interest Litigation — petition by public-spirited person for enforcement of others' rights.
    • Ultra vires: Beyond one's legal power/authority.
    • Habeas corpus: 'You shall have the body' — produces a detained person before court.
    • Mandamus: 'We command' — directs a public official to perform duty.
    • Prohibition: Stops a lower court from exceeding jurisdiction.
    • Certiorari: Transfers a case to a higher court for review.
    • Quo warranto: 'By what authority' — challenges a person's right to hold public office.
    • Locus standi: Right/standing to bring a case before court.
    • Bona fide: In good faith.
    • Malafide: In bad faith.
    • Audi alteram partem: Hear the other side — principle of natural justice.
    • Nemo judex in causa sua: No one should be a judge in their own case.
    • Res judicata: Matter already judged — cannot be re-litigated.
    • Stare decisis: Precedent — courts follow earlier decisions.
    • Ratio decidendi: Reason for decision (binding part of precedent).
    • Obiter dictum: Remark made in passing (not binding).
    • Mens rea: Guilty mind — mental element of crime.
    • Actus reus: Guilty act.
    • Caveat emptor: Let the buyer beware.
    • Injunction: Court order requiring someone to do/not do something.
    • Affidavit: Sworn written statement.
    • Plaintiff/Defendant: Person suing / person being sued.
    • Appellant/Respondent: Person appealing / person responding to appeal.
    • Decree/Order/Judgment: Formal adjudication / interim direction / final decision with reasons.
  • Landmark cases:
    • Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): Basic structure doctrine — Parliament cannot alter the Constitution's basic structure.
    • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Expanded Article 21 — right to life and personal liberty includes right to travel abroad; procedure must be fair, just, reasonable.
    • Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): Election dispute; part of basic structure doctrine context.
    • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): Balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; judicial review is basic structure.
    • S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): Article 356 (President's Rule) limitations; secularism is basic structure.
    • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): Sexual harassment at workplace guidelines before legislation.
    • Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985): Right to livelihood is part of Article 21.
    • MC Mehta cases: Environmental protection, oleum gas leak, polluter pays.
    • Indira Sawhney v. Union of India (1992): Mandal case — 27% OBC reservations, creamy layer exclusion.
    • Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018): Decriminalized consensual homosexual relations (Section 377 partially struck down).
    • Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018): Decriminalized adultery (Section 497 struck down).
    • Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017): Right to privacy is a fundamental right under Article 21.
    • Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017): Triple talaq (talaq-e-biddat) declared unconstitutional.
    • Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018): Sabarimala — women of all ages can enter temple.
    • K.S. Puttaswamy (2017): Privacy — see above.
    • Common Cause v. Union of India (2018): Right to die with dignity — passive euthanasia and living will.
    • Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020): Internet shutdowns must be temporary, proportionate, and published.
  • Constitutional amendments:
    • 1st Amendment (1951): Added reasonable restrictions on free speech (Articles 19(2), 31A, 31B, Ninth Schedule).
    • 24th Amendment (1971): Parliament can amend Fundamental Rights.
    • 25th Amendment (1971): Curbed right to property; gave effect to Directive Principles.
    • 42nd Amendment (1976): 'Mini Constitution' — added Socialist, Secular, Integrity to Preamble; Fundamental Duties (Art 51A); Directive Principles precedence.
    • 44th Amendment (1978): Right to Property removed from Fundamental Rights (made legal right under Art 300A); emergency safeguards.
    • 52nd Amendment (1985): Anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule).
    • 73rd & 74th Amendments (1992): Panchayats and Municipalities (Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules).
    • 86th Amendment (2002): Right to Education (Article 21A) for ages 6–14.
    • 89th Amendment (2003): National Commission for SCs/STs bifurcated.
    • 91st Amendment (2003): Anti-defection strengthening; cabinet size limit (15% of Lok Sabha).
    • 97th Amendment (2011): Cooperative societies (Article 43B, Part IXB).
    • 101st Amendment (2016): GST (Article 246A, 269A, 279A).
    • 102nd Amendment (2018): Constitutional status to National Commission for Backward Classes (Article 338B).
    • 103rd Amendment (2019): 10% EWS reservation (Articles 15(6), 16(6)).
    • 104th Amendment (2020): Extended SC/ST reservation to 2030; ended Anglo-Indian nomination.
    • 105th Amendment (2021): States can identify OBCs (restored state power after 102nd).
  • Legal bodies:
    • Supreme Court: Apex court; Articles 124–147; collegium appointments; Article 32 writ jurisdiction.
    • High Courts: Articles 214–231; Article 226 writ jurisdiction.
    • National Legal Services Authority (NALSA): Free legal aid; Lok Adalats.
    • Bar Council of India: Regulates legal profession and legal education.
    • Law Commission of India: Recommends legal reforms.
    • National Human Rights Commission (NHRC): Statutory (1993) — human rights protection.
    • National Commission for Women (NCW): Statutory (1992) — women's rights.
    • National Commission for SCs/STs/OBCs: Constitutional bodies (Articles 338, 338A, 338B).
    • Central Vigilance Commission (CVC): Statutory (2003) — anti-corruption vigilance.
    • Lokpal: Statutory anti-corruption body at Centre (Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013).
    • Election Commission of India: Article 324 — conducts elections.
    • National Green Tribunal (NGT): Statutory (2010) — environmental disputes.
    • Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT): Recovery of bank dues.
    • National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT): Company law and insolvency (IBC).
    • Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions: Consumer protection (Consumer Protection Act, 2019).
  • Important laws:
    • Constitution of India (1950)
    • Indian Penal Code, 1860 (now Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023)
    • Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (now Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023)
    • Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (now Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023)
    • Indian Contract Act, 1872
    • Right to Information Act, 2005
    • Consumer Protection Act, 2019
    • Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013
    • Whistleblowers Protection Act, 2014
    • Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988
    • Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989
    • Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005
    • Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act, 2013
    • Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009
    • National Food Security Act, 2013
    • Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016
    • Goods and Services Tax (Constitutional Amendment), 2016 / CGST Act, 2017
    • Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023
    • Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (2023 — new criminal laws replacing IPC, CrPC, Evidence Act)

Question: Which case established the basic structure doctrine?
Answer: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973).
Technique: Habeas corpus
Question: Which writ is used to produce a detained person before court?
Answer: Habeas corpus ('you shall have the body').

Common Traps

  • Confusing writs (habeas corpus vs mandamus vs certiorari vs prohibition vs quo warranto).
  • Mixing up amendment numbers (e.g., 86th = education, 101st = GST).
  • Thinking IPC still applies — replaced by Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (2023) for new offences.
  • Missing that PIL relaxes locus standi (public-spirited person can file for others).
  • Confusing NALSA (legal aid) with NHRC (human rights).

Memory Tricks

  • Privacy: Puttaswamy (2017), Article 21.
  • GST: 101st Amendment (2016).
  • Education: 86th Amendment (2002), Article 21A.

Legal GK exam strategy
Memorize landmark cases with year and one-line holding; constitutional amendments with number and subject; legal terms with one-line meaning. For passage-based questions, read the passage first — it often contains the answer context.

Doctrine of Eclipse, Severability, and Pith and Substance

The three doctrines form the analytical toolkit courts use when a statute conflicts with fundamental rights or exceeds legislative competence. The Doctrine of Eclipse holds that a pre‑Constitution law inconsistent with a fundamental right does not die; it becomes dormant — eclipsed — until the constitutional impediment is removed (by amendment or judicial interpretation). When the inconsistency ceases, the law revives automatically. This principle was articulated in Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955) and reaffirmed in State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills (1974).

The Doctrine of Severability (or separability) permits a court to strike down only the offending portion of a statute while preserving the remainder, provided the valid part is capable of standing independently and the legislature would have enacted it alone. The test requires (i) the unconstitutional provision to be severable without altering the statute’s nature, and (ii) the remaining provisions to be workable. R.M.D.C. v. Union of India (1957) and Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992) illustrate the application.

The Doctrine of Pith and Substance resolves conflicts between Union and State legislative entries in the Seventh Schedule. Courts examine the true character and dominant purpose of the legislation; if the pith and substance falls within the competence of the enacting legislature, incidental encroachment on another list does not invalidate the law. This doctrine underpins the validity of many central statutes that touch state subjects, such as the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951 upheld in State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951). Together, these doctrines enable a nuanced constitutional review that respects both rights and federal distribution of powers.

Judicial Review of Constitutional Amendments: From Shankari Prasad to Kesavananda and Beyond

The Supreme Court’s stance on whether Parliament can amend fundamental rights has evolved through a series of landmark judgments. In Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951) and Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965), the Court held that constitutional amendments under Article 368 are not “law” within the meaning of Article 13(2) and therefore cannot be challenged for violating fundamental rights. This position shifted in Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967), where a six‑judge bench ruled that amendments are “law” and cannot abridge fundamental rights, effectively freezing Part III.

Parliament responded with the 24th Amendment (1971), expressly declaring that Article 368 amendments are not subject to Article 13. The 25th Amendment (1971) further curtailed judicial review of laws giving effect to Directive Principles. The conflict culminated in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), where a 13‑judge bench, by a 7‑6 majority, introduced the Basic Structure Doctrine: Parliament may amend any provision, including fundamental rights, but cannot alter the Constitution’s basic structure — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law.

Subsequent cases refined the doctrine. Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) struck down the 39th Amendment’s clause insulating the Prime Minister’s election from judicial scrutiny as violating basic structure. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) held that the 42nd Amendment’s attempt to give primacy to Directive Principles over fundamental rights and to limit judicial review destroyed the basic structure. Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981) established that amendments made before 24 April 1973 (the Kesavananda date) are immune from basic‑structure challenge, while later amendments are subject to it. The 99th Amendment (2014) creating the National Judicial Appointments Commission was invalidated in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) for violating judicial independence, a core basic‑structure element. This jurisprudence makes the basic structure a living constitutional constraint on amendatory power.

Technique: Identifying the Basic‑Structure Violation
A 2022 amendment inserts a clause in Article 368 stating that “no constitutional amendment shall be questioned in any court on any ground.” A petition challenges this clause. Which principle will the Court apply, and what is the likely outcome?
The Court will apply the Basic Structure Doctrine from Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and Minerva Mills (1980). The clause seeks to oust judicial review of amendments, which the Court has held to be a basic‑structure feature (judicial review, rule of law). Therefore, the amendment will be struck down as unconstitutional because it destroys the basic structure.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks whether a constitutional amendment is valid, first locate the amendment number and year, then check if it touches any feature the Court has declared part of the basic structure (judicial review, federalism, secularism, democratic republic, separation of powers, rule of law). If it does, the amendment is vulnerable; if it merely alters procedural details without affecting those pillars, it is likely upheld.

Public Interest Litigation: Evolution, Locus Standi, and Judicial Activism

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) transformed the Indian judiciary from a passive adjudicator into an active guardian of constitutional rights. The relaxation of locus standi began with Ratlam Municipal Council v. Vardhichand (1980), where the Court allowed a citizen to enforce municipal duties. The watershed came in S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) — the “Judges’ Transfer case” — where Justice Bhagwati articulated that any public‑spirited person could approach the Court for enforcement of constitutional or legal rights of a disadvantaged group unable to do so themselves.

Subsequent PILs expanded the remedy: Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) on bonded labour; M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) on environmental pollution (the “Oleum Gas Leak” case); Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) laying down guidelines against sexual harassment at workplace; and People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (1997) on custodial deaths. The Court also devised innovative procedural tools — epistolary jurisdiction (treating letters as writ petitions), appointment of amicus curiae, and continuing mandamus (ongoing supervision of compliance).

Critics argue that PIL blurs the separation of powers, encourages judicial overreach, and burdens the docket. The Court has responded with self‑restraint guidelines: in State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010) it warned against frivolous PILs and imposed costs; in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) it reiterated that PIL cannot be a substitute for ordinary litigation where the petitioner has a direct grievance. Nonetheless, PIL remains a cornerstone of access to justice, especially for marginalised communities, and examiners frequently test the chronological evolution, the locus‑standi test (“public‑spirited person” vs “person aggrieved”), and the distinction between PIL and class action.

Appointment and Accountability of Higher Judiciary: Collegium, NJAC, and Judicial Standards

The method of appointing Supreme Court and High Court judges has been a contested constitutional issue. The original scheme under Articles 124(2) and 217(1) vested appointment power in the President acting on the advice of the Council of Ministers, with consultation of the Chief Justice of India (CJI). In S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) the Court held that “consultation” did not mean “concurrence”, giving the executive primacy. This was reversed in the Second Judges Case (Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India, 1993), where a nine‑judge bench declared that the CJI’s opinion, formed after consulting the two senior‑most Supreme Court judges, would have primacy — establishing the Collegium System.

The Third Judges Case (1998) expanded the collegium to the CJI plus four senior‑most judges for Supreme Court appointments, and the CJI plus two senior‑most judges for High Court appointments. The system operated without statutory backing until Parliament enacted the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014 and the 99th Constitutional Amendment (2014), creating a six‑member commission comprising the CJI, two senior Supreme Court judges, the Law Minister, and two eminent persons. In Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association v. Union of India (2015) (the Fourth Judges Case), a 4‑1 majority struck down NJAC, holding that judicial independence — a basic‑structure feature — requires primacy of the judiciary in appointments. The Court revived the collegium but directed the government to finalise a Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) for transparency, eligibility criteria, and complaint mechanisms.

Accountability mechanisms remain limited. The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 provides for removal of a judge on proved misbehaviour or incapacity through a parliamentary process (motion adopted by two‑thirds majority in each House). No judge has been removed under this Act; the only impeachment motion (Justice V. Ramaswami, 1993) failed for lack of majority. The In‑House Procedure (1999) allows the CJI to inquire into complaints against judges, but its outcomes are not public. The Judicial Standards and Accountability Bill (pending) proposes a National Judicial Oversight Committee, but has not become law. Examiners often ask to distinguish between “consultation”, “concurrence”, and “primacy”, to list the composition of each collegium tier, and to explain why NJAC was invalidated with reference to basic structure.

  • Confusing the doctrine of eclipse (pre‑Constitution law) with the doctrine of severability (post‑Constitution statute) — they operate at different temporal stages.
  • Assuming every amendment after 1973 is automatically subject to basic‑structure review; amendments before 24 April 1973 are immune per Waman Rao.
  • Treating PIL as a separate cause of action rather than a procedural relaxation of locus standi; the underlying right must still be a fundamental or statutory right.
  • Believing the collegium comprises only the CJI and two judges for all appointments; the Supreme Court collegium has five members, High Court collegium three.
  • Thinking the NJAC judgment abolished the collegium permanently; the Court revived the collegium and only mandated a transparent MoP.
  • Overlooking that the Judges (Inquiry) Act requires a two‑thirds majority in each House, not a simple majority, for removal.
  • Missing the distinction between continuing mandamus (ongoing supervision) and a one‑time writ of mandamus; examiners test this nuance in environmental PIL questions.
