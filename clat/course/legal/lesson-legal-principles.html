Key Concepts

Legal Principles — Torts, Contract, Criminal & Constitutional Law

CLAT Legal Reasoning gives a legal principle and a factual scenario; you must apply the principle to the facts and answer. Personal legal knowledge must NOT override the given principle.

  • Approach:
    • Read the principle carefully — identify its elements/conditions.
    • Read the factual scenario — identify parties, actions, consequences.
    • Apply the principle to facts strictly — do not bring outside law.
    • Choose the option that correctly states the legal consequence under the principle.
  • Key rule: The principle given in the question governs — even if real law differs, apply the given principle.
  • Tort law principles:
    • Negligence: Duty of care → breach → causation → damage (Donoghue v. Stevenson — neighbour principle).
    • Strict liability (Rylands v. Fletcher): Dangerous substance escapes from land → liable even without negligence.
    • Absolute liability (MC Mehta): Hazardous/industrially dangerous enterprise → absolutely liable for harm, no exceptions.
    • Vicarious liability: Master liable for servant's acts in course of employment.
    • Trespass: Direct, intentional interference with person/land/goods.
    • Defamation: Publication of false statement lowering reputation.
    • Nuisance: Unlawful interference with use/enjoyment of land.
    • Assault/Battery: Apprehension of battery / actual physical contact.
    • False imprisonment: Total restraint of liberty without lawful justification.
    • Defences: Volenti non fit injuria (voluntary consent to risk), inevitable accident, act of God, private defence, necessity.
  • Contract law principles:
    • Offer & acceptance: Proposal → acceptance → agreement.
    • Consideration: Something of value exchanged — essential for valid contract (except certain exceptions).
    • Capacity: Parties must be competent (age of majority, sound mind, not disqualified).
    • Free consent: No coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, mistake.
    • Legality of object: Object must be lawful.
    • Breach of contract: Remedies — damages, specific performance, injunction, quantum meruit.
    • Frustration of contract: Impossibility of performance discharges contract.
    • Quasi-contract: Obligations imposed by law without agreement (e.g., supply of necessaries to incapable person).
  • Criminal law principles:
    • Actus reus + mens rea: Guilty act + guilty mind generally required for criminal liability.
    • Strict liability offences: No mens rea required (e.g., certain statutory offences).
    • Right of private defence: Reasonable force to defend body/property against imminent danger.
    • Negligence (criminal): Rash/negligent act causing death (culpable homicide not amounting to murder).
    • Attempt vs preparation: Attempt = direct step towards crime; preparation alone not punishable generally.
    • Common intention vs common object: Common intention = pre-arranged plan (Section 34 IPC concept); common object = unlawful assembly (Section 149).
  • Constitutional law principles:
    • Article 14: Equality before law and equal protection of laws.
    • Article 19: Six freedoms (speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession) — subject to reasonable restrictions.
    • Article 21: Right to life and personal liberty — includes dignity, privacy, livelihood, health, education, clean environment (expansive interpretation).
    • Article 21A: Right to education (ages 6–14).
    • Article 32: Right to constitutional remedies — writ jurisdiction of Supreme Court.
    • Article 226: High Court writ jurisdiction.
    • Judicial review: Power to examine constitutionality of laws/executive actions.
    • Separation of powers: Legislature, executive, judiciary — checks and balances.
    • Rule of law: Supremacy of law, equality before law, absence of arbitrary power.
  • Legal maxims (frequently used):
    • Audi alteram partem: Hear the other side.
    • Nemo judex in causa sua: No one should judge their own case.
    • Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea: Act does not make guilty unless mind is guilty.
    • Res ipsa loquitur: The thing speaks for itself (negligence inferred from circumstances).
    • Volenti non fit injuria: To a willing person, no injury is done.
    • Ignorantia juris non excusat: Ignorance of law is no excuse.
    • Caveat emptor: Buyer beware.
    • Bona fide/Malafide: Good faith / Bad faith.
Technique: Apply principle strictly
Principle: 'Whoever causes death by doing a rash or negligent act is guilty.' Facts: A was driving carefully; a child suddenly darted onto road; A could not stop and the child died. Decide.
Answer: A was not rash/negligent (child darted suddenly, A driving carefully) → no criminal liability under the principle.
Technique: Volenti defence
Principle: 'Volenti non fit injuria — consent to risk negates liability.' Facts: Spectator at cricket match was hit by a ball. Decide.
Answer: Spectator voluntarily assumed the risk of being hit by attending → no liability (volenti applies).

Common Traps

  • Applying real law instead of the given principle — the governing mistake.
  • Ignoring elements of the principle (e.g., negligence requires all four: duty, breach, causation, damage).
  • Assuming intention where facts show accident.
  • Choosing an option that states a morally right but legally wrong outcome under the principle.

Memory Tricks

  • Negligence chain: Duty → Breach → Causation → Damage.
  • Valid contract: Offer → Acceptance → Consideration → Capacity → Free consent → Legality.
  • Criminal liability: Actus reus + Mens rea.
  • Writs: Habeas corpus (body), Mandamus (command), Prohibition (stop), Certiorari (review), Quo warranto (authority).
Legal Reasoning exam strategy
Read the principle first and underline its conditions. Apply only those conditions to the facts. Never import outside law. Eliminate options that contradict the principle or add conditions not stated.
