Legal Principles — Torts, Contract, Criminal & Constitutional Law

CLAT Legal Reasoning gives a legal principle and a factual scenario; you must apply the principle to the facts and answer. Personal legal knowledge must NOT override the given principle.

  • Approach:
    • Read the principle carefully — identify its elements/conditions.
    • Read the factual scenario — identify parties, actions, consequences.
    • Apply the principle to facts strictly — do not bring outside law.
    • Choose the option that correctly states the legal consequence under the principle.
  • Key rule: The principle given in the question governs — even if real law differs, apply the given principle.
  • Tort law principles:
    • Negligence: Duty of care → breach → causation → damage (Donoghue v. Stevenson — neighbour principle).
    • Strict liability (Rylands v. Fletcher): Dangerous substance escapes from land → liable even without negligence.
    • Absolute liability (MC Mehta): Hazardous/industrially dangerous enterprise → absolutely liable for harm, no exceptions.
    • Vicarious liability: Master liable for servant's acts in course of employment.
    • Trespass: Direct, intentional interference with person/land/goods.
    • Defamation: Publication of false statement lowering reputation.
    • Nuisance: Unlawful interference with use/enjoyment of land.
    • Assault/Battery: Apprehension of battery / actual physical contact.
    • False imprisonment: Total restraint of liberty without lawful justification.
    • Defences: Volenti non fit injuria (voluntary consent to risk), inevitable accident, act of God, private defence, necessity.
  • Contract law principles:
    • Offer & acceptance: Proposal → acceptance → agreement.
    • Consideration: Something of value exchanged — essential for valid contract (except certain exceptions).
    • Capacity: Parties must be competent (age of majority, sound mind, not disqualified).
    • Free consent: No coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, mistake.
    • Legality of object: Object must be lawful.
    • Breach of contract: Remedies — damages, specific performance, injunction, quantum meruit.
    • Frustration of contract: Impossibility of performance discharges contract.
    • Quasi-contract: Obligations imposed by law without agreement (e.g., supply of necessaries to incapable person).
  • Criminal law principles:
    • Actus reus + mens rea: Guilty act + guilty mind generally required for criminal liability.
    • Strict liability offences: No mens rea required (e.g., certain statutory offences).
    • Right of private defence: Reasonable force to defend body/property against imminent danger.
    • Negligence (criminal): Rash/negligent act causing death (culpable homicide not amounting to murder).
    • Attempt vs preparation: Attempt = direct step towards crime; preparation alone not punishable generally.
    • Common intention vs common object: Common intention = pre-arranged plan (Section 34 IPC concept); common object = unlawful assembly (Section 149).
  • Constitutional law principles:
    • Article 14: Equality before law and equal protection of laws.
    • Article 19: Six freedoms (speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession) — subject to reasonable restrictions.
    • Article 21: Right to life and personal liberty — includes dignity, privacy, livelihood, health, education, clean environment (expansive interpretation).
    • Article 21A: Right to education (ages 6–14).
    • Article 32: Right to constitutional remedies — writ jurisdiction of Supreme Court.
    • Article 226: High Court writ jurisdiction.
    • Judicial review: Power to examine constitutionality of laws/executive actions.
    • Separation of powers: Legislature, executive, judiciary — checks and balances.
    • Rule of law: Supremacy of law, equality before law, absence of arbitrary power.
  • Legal maxims (frequently used):
    • Audi alteram partem: Hear the other side.
    • Nemo judex in causa sua: No one should judge their own case.
    • Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea: Act does not make guilty unless mind is guilty.
    • Res ipsa loquitur: The thing speaks for itself (negligence inferred from circumstances).
    • Volenti non fit injuria: To a willing person, no injury is done.
    • Ignorantia juris non excusat: Ignorance of law is no excuse.
    • Caveat emptor: Buyer beware.
    • Bona fide/Malafide: Good faith / Bad faith.
Technique: Apply principle strictly
Principle: 'Whoever causes death by doing a rash or negligent act is guilty.' Facts: A was driving carefully; a child suddenly darted onto road; A could not stop and the child died. Decide.
Answer: A was not rash/negligent (child darted suddenly, A driving carefully) → no criminal liability under the principle.
Technique: Volenti defence
Principle: 'Volenti non fit injuria — consent to risk negates liability.' Facts: Spectator at cricket match was hit by a ball. Decide.
Answer: Spectator voluntarily assumed the risk of being hit by attending → no liability (volenti applies).

Common Traps

  • Applying real law instead of the given principle — the governing mistake.
  • Ignoring elements of the principle (e.g., negligence requires all four: duty, breach, causation, damage).
  • Assuming intention where facts show accident.
  • Choosing an option that states a morally right but legally wrong outcome under the principle.

Memory Tricks

  • Negligence chain: Duty → Breach → Causation → Damage.
  • Valid contract: Offer → Acceptance → Consideration → Capacity → Free consent → Legality.
  • Criminal liability: Actus reus + Mens rea.
  • Writs: Habeas corpus (body), Mandamus (command), Prohibition (stop), Certiorari (review), Quo warranto (authority).
Legal Reasoning exam strategy
Read the principle first and underline its conditions. Apply only those conditions to the facts. Never import outside law. Eliminate options that contradict the principle or add conditions not stated.

Vicarious Liability and Employer Responsibility in Tort Law

Vicarious liability imposes legal responsibility on an employer for torts committed by an employee in the course of employment. The doctrine rests on the policy that the enterprise which creates the risk and benefits from the employee’s activity should bear the loss. Indian courts have adopted a three‑stage test: (1) a contract of service must exist, distinguishing employees from independent contractors; (2) the tort must be committed “in the course of employment”, which includes acts authorised by the employer and unauthorised modes of performing authorised tasks; (3) the act must not be a “frolic of his own” — a substantial deviation for personal purposes.

The control test, historically dominant, asks whether the employer controls the manner of work. Modern jurisprudence supplements it with the integration test (whether the work is an integral part of the business) and the enterprise risk test (whether the employer is better placed to absorb and distribute the loss). In State of Rajasthan v. Mst. Vidhyawati (1962), the Supreme Court held the state vicariously liable for a driver’s negligence because driving was an integral part of the government’s transport service. Conversely, in Kasturi v. Iyyamperumal (1976), a hospital was not liable for a surgeon’s negligence where the surgeon was an independent contractor with full professional autonomy.

Statutory vicarious liability extends the principle beyond common law. The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 imposes no‑fault liability on the owner or insurer for third‑party injury, irrespective of employment relationship. The Employers’ Liability Act, 1938 (now largely subsumed) and the Workmen’s Compensation Act, 1923 provide compensation frameworks that operate alongside tortious vicarious liability. Courts also recognise “non‑delegable duties” — for instance, a hospital’s duty to provide competent medical care cannot be delegated to an independent contractor, as affirmed in Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab (2005).

Defences available to the employer include proving the worker was an independent contractor, that the act was outside the scope of employment, or that the employee acted on a frolic of his own. The burden of proving the employment relationship and course of employment lies on the claimant, while the employer bears the burden of establishing a recognised exception. Recent trends show courts expanding the scope of “course of employment” to include acts facilitated by the employer’s premises or equipment, even if marginally connected to the assigned duties.

Doctrine of Frustration and Force Majeure in Contract Law

The doctrine of frustration discharges a contract when, after formation, an unforeseen event renders performance impossible, illegal, or radically different from what was contemplated. Section 56 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 codifies this principle: “A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful.” The key requirements are: (i) the event must occur without fault of either party; (ii) it must be unforeseen and not provided for in the contract; (iii) it must destroy the foundation of the contract or make performance fundamentally different.

Force majeure clauses are contractual provisions that allocate risk for specified supervening events — natural disasters, war, government action, epidemics. Unlike frustration, force majeure operates only if the contract contains such a clause and the event falls within its enumerated list. Indian courts interpret force majeure clauses strictly; the party invoking it must show the event was beyond its control, could not be avoided by reasonable diligence, and directly prevented performance. In Energy Watchdog v. Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (2017), the Supreme Court held that a force majeure clause covering “acts of God” did not excuse performance where the party could have mitigated the impact.

The landmark case Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur & Co. (1954) established that frustration requires the contract’s commercial purpose to be destroyed, not merely made more onerous. The court rejected frustration where a wartime restriction delayed but did not prevent performance. Later, National Agricultural Cooperative Marketing Federation v. G. S. Rajagopalan (1995) clarified that a temporary impediment does not frustrate a contract unless the delay is so prolonged as to defeat the commercial object.

The COVID‑19 pandemic triggered extensive litigation on frustration and force majeure. Courts generally required parties to demonstrate that lockdown orders made performance legally impossible, not merely commercially unviable. In M/s. Standard Retail Pvt. Ltd. v. M/s. G. S. Global Corp. (2021), the Delhi High Court held that a force majeure clause covering “epidemic” applied, but the party must prove it took reasonable steps to perform. The distinction remains critical: frustration is a statutory fallback; force majeure is a negotiated risk‑allocation tool.

Technique: Applying Frustration vs Force Majeure
A supplier contracts to deliver 10,000 units of a specialised component to a manufacturer by 30 June. The contract contains a force majeure clause listing “government orders, epidemics, and natural calamities”. In March, the central government imposes a nationwide lockdown prohibiting all non‑essential manufacturing. The supplier’s factory is classified as non‑essential and shuts down. The supplier notifies the manufacturer on 15 April that delivery will be delayed indefinitely. The manufacturer sues for breach. Decide whether the supplier is excused under frustration, force majeure, or neither.
The lockdown is a government order and an epidemic measure, both expressly covered by the force majeure clause. The supplier must show (1) the event was beyond its control, (2) it could not avoid the shutdown by reasonable diligence, and (3) the shutdown directly prevented performance. The facts state the factory was legally prohibited from operating; no alternative production was feasible. Therefore the force majeure clause applies and the supplier is excused from timely performance. Frustration under Section 56 would also be available as a fallback because the contract’s commercial purpose — delivery by 30 June — is destroyed by a supervening legal impossibility. However, the presence of an applicable force majeure clause makes the contractual remedy primary. The supplier is not liable for breach.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem presents both a force majeure clause and a frustration argument, analyse the clause first. If the clause covers the event and its conditions are satisfied, the contract governs and frustration need not be reached. Only if the clause is absent, ambiguous, or inapplicable should you invoke Section 56. Underline the clause’s enumerated events and the notice requirements; missing a notice deadline is a common trap.

Mens Rea Variants: Specific Intent, General Intent, and Strict Liability in Criminal Law

Criminal liability traditionally requires both actus reus and mens rea. However, the mental element varies across offences. Specific intent crimes demand that the accused not only intended the act but also aimed at a particular consequence — for example, murder (intent to cause death or grievous hurt likely to cause death), theft (intent to permanently deprive), and cheating (intent to deceive). The prosecution must prove this heightened mental state beyond reasonable doubt. In State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George (1965), the Supreme Court held that for offences under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, the presumption of mens rea could be displaced only by clear legislative language.

General intent crimes require only that the accused intended to perform the actus reus, without any further ulterior purpose. Battery, criminal force, and rash driving are typical examples. The law presumes that a person intends the natural consequences of a voluntary act. This presumption can be rebutted by evidence of accident, mistake of fact, or automatism. In R v. Tolson (1889), the court recognised that an honest and reasonable mistake of fact negatives mens rea for a general intent offence.

Strict liability offences dispense with mens rea altogether. The legislature imposes liability solely on proof of the prohibited act, often to protect public welfare in regulatory contexts — food adulteration, traffic violations, environmental pollution, and certain provisions of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940. The Supreme Court in M. H. George v. State of Maharashtra (1965) upheld strict liability for possession of counterfeit currency notes, emphasising the statute’s protective purpose. However, courts read down strict liability where the penalty is severe, applying the principle that mens rea is presumed unless expressly excluded.

The distinction matters for defences. Specific intent crimes allow defences such as intoxication (if it negates the specific purpose), diminished responsibility, and mistake of fact that disproves the ulterior aim. General intent crimes admit mistake of fact and accident but not voluntary intoxication. Strict liability offences admit virtually no mental‑state defences; only factual impossibility or statutory exemptions apply. Recent jurisprudence, e.g., State of Gujarat v. Kishanbhai (2014), cautions against expanding strict liability to offences carrying imprisonment, insisting on a clear legislative mandate.

Basic Structure Doctrine and Judicial Review Limits in Constitutional Law

The basic structure doctrine, propounded in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), holds that Parliament’s amending power under Article 368 cannot alter the Constitution’s “basic structure”. The 13‑judge bench identified several inviolable features: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. Subsequent cases have expanded the list: judicial review (Minerva Mills v. Union of India, 1980), free and fair elections (Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain, 1975), and the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles (Waman Rao v. Union of India, 1981).

The doctrine operates as a substantive limit on constitutional amendments. In Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain, the Court struck down the 39th Amendment clause that insulated the Prime Minister’s election from judicial scrutiny, holding it violated the basic structure of free elections and judicial review. In Minerva Mills, the Court invalidated sections of the 42nd Amendment that curtailed judicial review and gave primacy to directive principles over fundamental rights, reaffirming that the Constitution’s identity cannot be amended out of existence.

Judicial review of constitutional amendments is itself a basic structure feature. The Court examines whether an amendment destroys the Constitution’s essential character, not merely whether it is procedurally defective. The “width” test asks whether the amendment alters the fundamental framework; the “identity” test asks whether the Constitution remains the same Constitution. In I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are subject to judicial review if they violate basic structure.

Critics argue the doctrine grants unelected judges a veto over democratic amendment. Proponents contend it preserves constitutional supremacy against transient majorities. The doctrine does not freeze the Constitution; amendments that enhance or refine basic features (e.g., the 73rd and 74th Amendments strengthening local self‑government) have been upheld. The Court’s role is to police the boundary, not to dictate policy.

Matrimonial Property Regimes and Maintenance under Family Law

India does not have a uniform matrimonial property regime; property rights on divorce depend on personal law and statutory provisions. Under the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, there is no automatic community of property. Each spouse retains ownership of property acquired in their name, though courts may order division of joint assets under Section 27 (disposal of property presented at or about the time of marriage). The Hindu Succession Act, 1956, as amended in 2005, grants daughters equal coparcenary rights in joint Hindu family property, affecting the pool of assets available for division.

Muslim law recognises the concept of mahr (dower) as the wife’s exclusive property, payable on demand or at dissolution. The wife has no automatic share in the husband’s property, but she may claim maintenance under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, which applies irrespective of religion. The Supreme Court in Danial Latifi v. Union of India (2001) upheld the constitutional validity of Section 125, interpreting “maintenance” to include a fair and reasonable provision for the wife’s future, not merely subsistence.

Christian law under the Indian Divorce Act, 1869 (amended 2001) allows the court to order settlement of property for the benefit of the wife and children. The Special Marriage Act, 1954, provides a secular framework where the court may order division of property acquired during marriage, guided by principles of equity. Recent judgments such as Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020) clarified that a daughter’s coparcenary rights under the 2005 amendment are retroactive, impacting ancestral property claims in matrimonial disputes.

Maintenance under Section 125 CrPC is a summary, speedy remedy. The magistrate may order monthly allowance not exceeding the husband’s capacity, considering the wife’s needs, standard of living, and the husband’s income. The 2018 amendment to the Hindu Adoption and Maintenance Act, 1956, extended maintenance rights to widowed daughters‑in‑law. Courts have also recognised “interim maintenance” during pendency of divorce proceedings, ensuring the dependent spouse is not left destitute. The interplay of personal law, statutory maintenance, and equitable discretion creates a layered regime that examiners often test through scenario‑based questions.

  • Confusing vicarious liability with direct negligence of the employer — the employer is liable for the employee’s tort, not for its own breach of duty.
  • Assuming any government order automatically triggers frustration — the order must render performance impossible, not merely more expensive or delayed.
  • Treating all regulatory offences as strict liability — courts presume mens rea unless the statute expressly excludes it, especially when imprisonment is prescribed.
  • Believing the basic structure doctrine prevents any amendment to fundamental rights — amendments that expand or protect basic features are permissible.
  • Assuming a wife automatically receives half the husband’s property on divorce under Hindu law — no community of property exists; division depends on joint ownership and judicial discretion.
  • Overlooking the notice requirement in force majeure clauses — failure to give timely notice can bar the defence even if the event qualifies.
  • Misapplying the “frolic of his own” test by focusing on the employee’s motive rather than the degree of deviation from assigned duties.

Strict Liability and Absolute Liability in Tort Law: Rylands v Fletcher and Indian Developments

The rule in Rylands v Fletcher (1868) holds that a person who brings onto his land something likely to cause mischief if it escapes is liable for all direct damage caused by its escape, irrespective of negligence. The classic formulation requires a non‑natural use of land, an escape, and foreseeable damage. Indian courts adopted the principle in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) but expanded it into an absolute liability standard for hazardous industries. The Supreme Court held that enterprises engaged in inherently dangerous activities owe an absolute duty to the community; no defence of act of God, third‑party act, or statutory authority can absolve them. This shift reflects the policy judgment that risk created by modern industry should be borne by the enterprise rather than by unconsenting victims.

Absolute liability differs from strict liability in three crucial respects. First, the defendant cannot plead that the escape was caused by an unforeseeable natural event. Second, the defence of due care is unavailable; the mere fact of escape triggers liability. Third, the scope of recoverable damage includes not only property loss but also personal injury and environmental degradation, as seen in Indian Council for Enviro‑Legal Action v. Union of India (1996), where the Court ordered remedial measures for groundwater contamination. The doctrine has been partially codified in the Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991, which mandates compulsory insurance for hazardous units and provides a no‑fault compensation scheme for victims.

Judicial application shows a gradient: traditional Rylands strict liability still governs ordinary non‑natural uses such as water reservoirs, while absolute liability is reserved for activities classified as “hazardous” under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and the Factories Act, 1948. In K. Nagaraj v. State of Karnataka (2019) the Court clarified that classification depends on the nature of the substance, the scale of operation, and the potential for widespread harm. Examiners often test the distinction by presenting a scenario where a chemical plant leaks toxic gas; the correct answer requires identifying absolute liability and rejecting any negligence‑based defence.

Another nuance is the role of statutory authority. Under strict liability, a statutory authorisation may be a defence if the statute expressly permits the activity and the defendant complies with all conditions. Under absolute liability, even a valid licence does not shield the enterprise; the Supreme Court in Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) affirmed that the “polluter pays” principle overrides regulatory compliance. This principle has been extended to municipal corporations for solid‑waste management failures, reinforcing that public authorities are not immune.

Finally, the quantum of damages under absolute liability is not limited to compensatory awards. The Court may order exemplary damages to deter future violations, as illustrated in Union Carbide Corporation v. Union of India (1991) where a settlement included a substantial punitive component. Understanding the interplay between common‑law strict liability, statutory absolute liability, and constitutional environmental jurisprudence is essential for solving high‑difficulty tort questions.

Doctrine of Privity and Third‑Party Beneficiaries in Contract Law

The doctrine of privity holds that only parties to a contract can enforce its terms or be bound by its obligations. In England, Tweddle v. Atkinson (1861) and Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge & Co. (1915) cemented the rule. India follows the same principle under Section 2(h) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, which defines a contract as an agreement enforceable by law between the parties. Consequently, a stranger to the contract cannot sue for breach, nor can a party be sued by a non‑party.

Exceptions have emerged both legislatively and judicially. The most significant statutory exception is Section 2(d) of the Act, which recognises a “promisee” who may be a third party if the contract expressly confers a benefit on that person. The Supreme Court in M.C. Chacko v. State Bank of Travancore (1970) held that a third‑party beneficiary can enforce a promise when the contract manifests a clear intention to benefit that third party and the promisee has provided consideration. This “intention to benefit” test requires the contract language to identify the beneficiary either by name or as a member of a defined class.

Another exception arises from the law of trusts. Where a contract creates a trust in favour of a third party, the beneficiary can enforce the trust under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882, irrespective of privity. In K. S. Vidyanand v. State of Kerala (1995) the Court allowed a beneficiary of a government housing scheme to sue the contractor for defective construction, treating the scheme as a trust arrangement. Similarly, the assignment of contractual rights under Section 130 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, permits an assignee to step into the shoes of the assignor, effectively bypassing privity.

Judicial innovation has also recognised “collateral contracts” and “agency” as vehicles for third‑party enforcement. A collateral contract is a separate promise made by one party to a third party, inducing the third party to enter the main contract. In Shankar v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2004) the Court enforced a collateral warranty given by a builder to a flat purchaser who was not a signatory to the main construction contract. Agency allows an agent to contract on behalf of a disclosed principal; the principal can then enforce the contract despite not being a direct party.

Examiners frequently test the boundary by presenting a contract that contains a clause “for the benefit of X” without naming X specifically. The correct analysis distinguishes between a mere incidental benefit, which does not confer enforceability, and a stipulated benefit coupled with consideration from the promisee, which does. Mastery of the intention‑to‑benefit test, the trust exception, and the assignment mechanism equips candidates to navigate complex multi‑party commercial scenarios.

Applied Scenario
A contracts with B to construct a community hall. The contract includes a clause: “The hall shall be maintained for the benefit of the residents of Village X.” C, a resident of Village X, suffers injury due to a structural defect. C sues A for breach of contract. Decide.
C cannot enforce the contract directly. The clause confers only an incidental benefit on an undefined class; there is no express intention to create enforceable rights in individual residents, nor has C provided consideration. The proper remedy lies in tort (negligence or absolute liability) rather than contract. If the contract had named C or a specific class and C had furnished consideration (e.g., contributed funds), the intention‑to‑benefit test would be satisfied and C could sue.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a third‑party enforcement question appears, first identify whether the contract contains an express “for the benefit of” clause naming the claimant or a defined class. Next, check for consideration moving from the promisee to the promisor on behalf of that third party. Absent both, treat the claim as a tort or statutory remedy, not a contractual one.

Inchoate Offences: Attempt, Conspiracy, and Abetment under the Indian Penal Code

The Indian Penal Code, 1860, criminalises preparatory conduct through three distinct inchoate offences: attempt (Section 511), criminal conspiracy (Sections 120A‑120B), and abetment (Sections 107‑117). Each targets a different stage of criminal enterprise and carries its own mens rea and actus reus requirements. Understanding the boundaries prevents the common error of conflating mere preparation with attempt.

Attempt requires an act that goes beyond mere preparation and is sufficiently proximate to the commission of the substantive offence. The Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub (1980) formulated the “proximity test”: the act must be so close to the completed offence that it would have resulted in the offence but for an external interruption. In Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar (1961) the Court distinguished preparation (e.g., purchasing poison) from attempt (e.g., administering poison to the victim). The punishment under Section 511 is up to half the maximum term prescribed for the attempted offence, reflecting the lesser culpability.

Criminal conspiracy under Section 120A is an agreement between two or more persons to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means. Unlike attempt, conspiracy does not require any overt act beyond the agreement, except when the object is an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life, or rigorous imprisonment for two years or more, where Section 120B mandates an overt act. The landmark Kehar Singh v. State (Delhi Administration) (1988) held that the essence of conspiracy is the meeting of minds; direct evidence of agreement is rarely available, so courts infer it from circumstantial evidence such as coordinated actions and communications.

Abetment covers three forms: instigation, conspiracy (as a subset of abetment), and intentional aid. Section 107 defines abetment broadly, and Section 109 provides that an abettor is punishable with the same punishment as the principal offender if the act abetted is committed. The Supreme Court in Ramesh Kumar v. State of Chhattisgarh (2001) clarified that mere presence at the scene does not constitute abetment; there must be active participation or encouragement. Importantly, abetment of suicide (Section 306) requires a direct nexus between the abettor’s conduct and the victim’s decision, a standard tightened in Gurbachan Singh v. Satpal Singh (1990).

Inter‑relationships among these offences are frequently examined. A single factual matrix may support charges of attempt, conspiracy, and abetment simultaneously. For instance, two persons agree to murder a third (conspiracy), one procures a weapon (attempt to commit murder by preparation), and the other encourages the shooter (abetment). The Code permits cumulative charging, but double jeopardy principles bar multiple convictions for the same actus reus unless each offence contains a distinct element. The State of Rajasthan v. Kashi Ram (2006) decision illustrates the court’s approach to separating the agreement (conspiracy) from the overt act (attempt).

Procedurally, the prosecution must prove the specific mens rea for each inchoate offence. For attempt, the intent to commit the target offence must be unequivocal. For conspiracy, the intention to agree on the illegal object suffices. For abetment, the abettor must intend to facilitate the commission of the offence. Misidentifying the required mental element is a frequent trap; candidates should map each charge to its statutory definition before evaluating the evidence.

Fundamental Rights Interplay: Article 14, 19, and 21 – The Golden Triangle

The Supreme Court in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) famously described Articles 14, 19, and 21 as a “golden triangle” because they are mutually reinforcing and must be read together. Article 14 guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws; Article 19 enumerates six freedoms (speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession); Article 21 protects life and personal liberty. The Court held that any law depriving a person of personal liberty must satisfy the test of reasonableness under Article 14 and must not arbitrarily restrict the freedoms under Article 19.

The interplay operates through the “procedure established by law” clause of Article 21. Post‑Maneka, “procedure” must be fair, just, and reasonable, not merely a formal legislative enactment. This imports the due‑process standards of Article 14 (non‑arbitrariness) and the substantive freedoms of Article 19. In Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi (1981) the Court ruled that the right to life includes the right to live with dignity, which encompasses adequate nutrition, clothing, and shelter — thereby linking Article 21 to socio‑economic rights that are otherwise non‑justiciable directive principles.

Article 19(1)(a) (freedom of speech) illustrates the triangle vividly. A restriction on speech must be a reasonable restriction under Article 19(2) and must also satisfy Article 14’s equality test (no discriminatory censorship) and Article 21’s procedural fairness (prior notice, hearing). In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) the Court struck down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000, because it was vague (violating Article 14), overbroad (violating Article 19(1)(a)), and lacked procedural safeguards (violating Article 21). The judgment demonstrates how a single statutory provision can fail on all three fronts simultaneously.

Article 19(1)(g) (freedom to practice any profession) interacts with Article 21 in cases of occupational licensing. In Excel Wear v. Union of India (1979) the Court held that the right to close a business is part of the freedom of trade, but the state may impose reasonable restrictions in public interest. The restriction must be non‑arbitrary (Article 14) and must follow a fair procedure (Article 21). The “reasonable classification” test under Article 14 requires an intelligible differentia and a rational nexus to the object sought; the “proportionality” test under Article 21 demands that the restriction be the least intrusive means.

Examiners often frame questions around a hypothetical law that imposes a blanket ban on a profession for a certain class of persons. The correct analysis proceeds stepwise: (1) Does the law infringe a freedom under Article 19? (2) Is the infringement a reasonable restriction under the corresponding clause? (3) Does the classification violate Article 14? (4) Does the procedure for deprivation meet Article 21’s fairness requirement? Only when all four prongs are satisfied does the law survive. Mastery of this sequential framework is indispensable for constitutional law problem‑solving.

Hindu Undivided Family Partition and Coparcenary Rights after the 2005 Amendment

The Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005, transformed the coparcenary regime under the Mitakshara school by conferring equal coparcenary rights on daughters. Prior to the amendment, only male lineal descendants up to three generations constituted the coparcenary, and a daughter’s interest was limited to a right of maintenance. Section 6 of the amended Act now provides that a daughter becomes a coparcener by birth in the same manner as a son, with identical rights and liabilities, including the right to seek partition.

The Supreme Court in Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020) resolved the retrospective versus prospective application debate. The Court held that the amendment applies retrospectively to daughters born before 9 September 2005, provided the father was alive on that date. If the father had died before the amendment, the daughter’s claim is limited to a share in the notional partition that would have occurred at the father’s death under the pre‑amendment law. This “living coparcener” rule balances the legislative intent to remove gender discrimination with the need to protect vested rights of existing coparceners.

Partition under Mitakshara law can be effected by a unilateral declaration of intention to separate, followed by a formal deed or suit. The 2005 amendment did not alter the modes of partition but expanded the class of persons entitled to demand it. A daughter can now file a suit for partition and claim a defined share, which is computed by the “notional partition” method: the joint family property is deemed divided at the date of the suit, and each coparcener receives a share equal to what they would have received had a partition taken place then. The share of a deceased coparcener devolves by survivorship among surviving coparceners, but the amendment converts survivorship into testamentary or intestate succession for the deceased’s share, thereby allowing the daughter’s heirs to inherit.

An important procedural nuance is the requirement of a “preliminary decree” in a partition suit. The court first declares the shares (preliminary decree) and then directs actual division (final decree). The 2005 amendment empowers a daughter to seek a preliminary decree even if the family property has been partially alienated by the karta. In Prakash v. Phulavati (2016) the Court initially limited the amendment to daughters whose fathers were alive on the amendment date, but Vineeta Sharma overruled that restriction, confirming that the daughter’s right accrues by birth irrespective of the father’s survival, subject to the notional partition rule for pre‑amendment deaths.

Tax implications also arise. A partition effected by a registered deed attracts stamp duty and registration charges based on the market value of the separated shares. However, a family arrangement recorded in a memorandum without formal partition may be treated as a “family settlement” exempt from stamp duty in several states, provided it is bona fide and not a device to evade tax. Candidates should be aware of the distinction between a legal partition (which severs the joint status) and a family settlement (which may preserve the HUF for tax purposes). The interplay of substantive coparcenary rights, procedural partition law, and fiscal consequences forms a dense cluster of examination questions.

  • Confusing absolute liability with strict liability by allowing a defence of act of God in hazardous‑industry cases.
  • Assuming any third‑party mention in a contract automatically creates enforceable rights without checking the intention‑to‑benefit test and consideration.
  • Treating mere preparation as attempt, ignoring the proximity requirement established in Mohd. Yakub.
  • Believing conspiracy always requires an overt act, overlooking the exception for offences punishable with less than two years’ rigorous imprisonment.
  • Applying the Article 14 classification test without also examining Article 19 reasonableness and Article 21 procedural fairness in a single law.
  • Assuming a daughter’s coparcenary right under the 2005 amendment is absolute regardless of the father’s date of death, neglecting the notional partition rule for pre‑amendment deaths.
  • These traps highlight the need for precise statutory reading and layered constitutional analysis.

Negligence: Duty of Care, Standard of Care, and the Caparo Three‑Stage Test in Indian Jurisprudence

The modern law of negligence requires three cumulative elements: a duty of care owed by the defendant to the claimant, breach of that duty by falling below the applicable standard of care, and damage that is both causally linked and not too remote. In England the House of Lords in Caparo Industries plc v Dickman (1990) formulated a three‑stage test — foreseeability of harm, proximity of relationship, and whether it is fair, just and reasonable to impose a duty. Indian courts have adopted the Caparo framework while retaining the Donoghue v Stevenson neighbour principle as the doctrinal foundation. The Supreme Court in Jacob Mathew v State of Punjab (2005) held that a doctor owes a duty of care to a patient, and the standard is that of a reasonably competent practitioner in the same field; the Bolam test applies, requiring that the practice be accepted by a responsible body of medical opinion.

Foreseeability alone is insufficient. In K. Nagaraj v State of Andhra Pradesh (1985) the Court refused to impose a duty on a municipal corporation for a child’s injury from a defective road because the relationship lacked sufficient proximity. Proximity is assessed by examining the degree of control, assumption of responsibility, and reliance. The third limb — fairness, justice and reasonableness — operates as a policy filter. In M.C. Mehta v Union of India (1987) the Court imposed absolute liability on hazardous industries, bypassing the negligence framework altogether, illustrating that policy considerations can displace the Caparo analysis where public safety demands a stricter regime.

Standard of care is objective: the “reasonable person” benchmark, adjusted for special skills (professionals), age (children), and physical disability. The breach question asks whether the defendant’s conduct fell below that standard. The Bolam test governs medical negligence, but the Supreme Court in Kusum Sharma v Batra Hospital (2010) clarified that a doctor is not negligent merely because a better alternative existed; the practice must be one accepted by a responsible body of medical opinion. Causation follows the “but‑for” test supplemented by the “material contribution” principle where multiple causes operate. Remoteness is governed by the Wagon Mound (No. 1) reasonable foreseeability of the type of damage, not its extent.

Indian statutes such as the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 and the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 create statutory duties that coexist with common‑law negligence. A claimant may plead both, but the statutory remedy may provide stricter liability (e.g., no‑fault compensation under the Motor Vehicles Act). The interplay of statutory and common‑law duties is a frequent examination focus, especially where a statute prescribes a specific standard that displaces the general reasonable‑person test.

Quasi‑Contract and Restitution: Sections 68‑72 of the Indian Contract Act

The Indian Contract Act, 1872 recognises obligations that arise not from agreement but from law to prevent unjust enrichment. Sections 68 to 72 codify five distinct quasi‑contractual claims. Section 68 covers the supply of necessaries to a person incapable of contracting (minors, persons of unsound mind) or to someone legally bound to support them; the supplier is entitled to reimbursement from the incapable person’s property. Section 69 allows a person who pays money due by another, in which he has a legal interest, to recover it from the person bound to pay. Section 70 imposes an obligation to pay for a non‑gratuitous act done for another’s benefit where the doer did not intend to act gratuitously. Section 71 treats a finder of goods as a bailee, obliging him to take reasonable care and to restore the goods to the owner. Section 72 provides restitution for money paid or thing delivered by mistake or under coercion.

The key judicial gloss on Section 70 is State of West Bengal v B.K. Mondal (1962), where the Supreme Court held that the claimant must prove (i) the act was done lawfully, (ii) the defendant enjoyed the benefit, (iii) the act was not intended to be gratuitous, and (iv) the defendant had the option to reject the benefit. Mere incidental benefit is insufficient; the benefit must be “requested” or “accepted” in a manner that implies an obligation to pay. In Mahabir Kishore v State of Madhya Pradesh (1990) the Court clarified that a person who voluntarily improves another’s property without request cannot claim under Section 70 unless the owner had knowledge and acquiesced.

Section 72’s “mistake” includes mistake of fact; courts have also allowed recovery for mistake of law in certain cases (e.g., Sales Tax Officer v Kanhaiya Lal (1959)). Coercion under Section 72 mirrors the definition in Section 15 of the Act — consent obtained by threat of illegal act. The remedy is restitutionary, not compensatory; the measure is the value of the benefit conferred, not the loss suffered.

Quasi‑contractual claims are distinct from tortious claims for unjust enrichment because they are statutory, not equitable, and they do not require a wrongful act. They also differ from contractual claims because there is no consensus ad idem. Examination questions often test the boundary between Section 70 and a claim for quantum meruit under a void contract; the former arises where no contract ever existed, the latter where a contract existed but is unenforceable.

Application of Section 70 – Non‑Gratuitous Act
A, a contractor, mistakenly builds a boundary wall on B’s land believing it to be his own. B watches the construction, says nothing, and later uses the wall as part of his property. A sues B under Section 70 for the cost of construction. Decide.
A can recover under Section 70. The act (building the wall) was lawful, B enjoyed a direct benefit (the wall), A did not intend to act gratuitously (he believed he was building on his own land), and B had the opportunity to reject the benefit by objecting during construction but remained silent, which constitutes acceptance. The measure of recovery is the reasonable cost of construction, not the increase in land value.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a quasi‑contract question appears, first identify the specific section (68‑72) that matches the fact pattern. Check the four‑element test for Section 70: lawful act, benefit conferred, non‑gratuitous intention, and acceptance or opportunity to reject. For Section 72, distinguish mistake of fact from mistake of law and note the evolving judicial approach. Write the statutory provision number in the answer; examiners award marks for precise citation.

General Exceptions under the Indian Penal Code: Mistake, Accident, Necessity, and Duress

Chapter IV of the Indian Penal Code (IPC), Sections 76 to 106, enumerates general exceptions that negate criminal liability. Mistake of fact (Section 76) excuses an act done by a person who, in good faith, believes himself bound by law to do it. The mistake must be of fact, not law, and the belief must be honest and reasonable. In R v Tolson (1889) the English court held that an honest and reasonable mistake of fact as to marital status negated bigamy; Indian courts follow the same principle. Mistake of law is not a defence (Section 76 Explanation), except where a statute expressly provides otherwise.

Accident (Section 80) applies when an act is done by accident or misfortune, without criminal intention or knowledge, in the doing of a lawful act in a lawful manner by lawful means and with proper care and caution. The Supreme Court in State of Orissa v Khora Ghasi (1978) held that a hunter who shot a person mistaken for a wild animal was protected under Section 80 because he exercised due care. The burden of proving accident lies on the accused, but the standard is preponderance of probability.

Necessity (Section 81) excuses an act likely to cause harm, done without criminal intention, to prevent greater harm to person or property. The harm avoided must be imminent and the act proportionate. In R v Dudley & Stephens (1884) the English court rejected necessity as a defence to murder; Indian law follows the same line — necessity cannot justify taking an innocent life. However, for property offences, necessity may succeed, e.g., pulling down a house to stop a fire spread.

Duress (Section 94) protects a person compelled by threat of instant death to commit an offence, except murder and offences against the state punishable with death. The threat must be present, immediate, and unavoidable. In State of Maharashtra v Mayer Hans George (1965) the Court held that duress is not available for offences under the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act because the statute imposes strict liability. The distinction between duress and necessity is that duress involves a human threat, while necessity arises from natural circumstances.

These exceptions are frequently combined in problem questions: a person who drives a vehicle under threat of death (duress) and accidentally hits a pedestrian (accident) may plead both. The examiner expects a structured analysis — identify the applicable section, state the ingredients, apply to facts, and conclude whether the exception succeeds.

Doctrine of Eclipse, Severability, and Waiver in Constitutional Adjudication

Pre‑constitutional laws that conflict with fundamental rights do not become void ab initio; they are merely eclipsed. The doctrine of eclipse, articulated in Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v State of Madhya Pradesh (1955), holds that such laws remain dormant until the constitutional inconsistency is removed, either by amendment or by judicial interpretation that narrows the law. When the inconsistency disappears, the law revives automatically. This doctrine applies only to pre‑constitutional statutes; post‑constitutional laws that violate fundamental rights are void ab initio under Article 13(2).

Severability (or separability) allows a court to strike down only the offending portion of a statute while preserving the remainder, provided the valid part is capable of independent operation and the legislature would have enacted it alone. The test, from R.M.D.C. v Union of India (1957), requires (i) the invalid provision to be separable in substance, (ii) the remaining provisions to be workable, and (iii) no legislative intent to enact the statute as an indivisible whole. In State of Bombay v F.N. Balsara (1951) the Court severed the prohibition on possession of liquor from the valid licensing provisions.

Waiver of fundamental rights is generally not permitted. In Basheshar Nath v Commissioner of Income Tax (1959) the Supreme Court held that a citizen cannot waive a fundamental right because these rights are not merely personal but constitute a public policy safeguard. However, a person may choose not to exercise a right (e.g., not filing a writ petition), which is not a waiver but a voluntary abstention. The distinction matters in exam questions that ask whether a contractual clause waiving Article 19(1)(g) freedom of trade is enforceable — it is not.

These three doctrines interact. A pre‑constitutional law partially inconsistent with fundamental rights may be partially eclipsed; the valid remainder can be severed and continue. If the legislature later amends the Constitution to remove the inconsistency, the eclipsed portion revives. Understanding the chronological sequence — enactment, constitutional commencement, judicial review, amendment — is essential for solving constitutional law problems that test the fate of statutes over time.

Adoption and Guardianship under Hindu Law: The Hindu Adoption and Maintenance Act, 1956

The Hindu Adoption and Maintenance Act, 1956 (HAMA) codified the law of adoption for Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, and Sikhs. Section 6 prescribes the requisites for a valid adoption: the adopter must have capacity (sound mind, not a minor); the child must be Hindu, not already adopted, unmarried (unless custom permits), and under fifteen years (unless custom permits older); the adoption must be completed by actual giving and taking (datta homam) with the intent to transfer the child from the birth family to the adoptive family. Section 7 deals with capacity of a male Hindu to adopt; if he has a wife living, her consent is required unless she has renounced the world or is of unsound mind. Section 8 deals with capacity of a female Hindu to adopt; she may adopt a son or daughter, but if she adopts a son, the age gap must be at least twenty‑one years.

Section 12 declares the legal effect of adoption: the adopted child becomes the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes, severing ties with the birth family, except that the child cannot marry a person whom he or she could not have married in the birth family. The adoptive parents acquire all rights and obligations of natural parents, including maintenance and inheritance. The Supreme Court has held that adoption under HAMA is irrevocable and creates a status that cannot be undone by a subsequent agreement.

Guardianship is governed by the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 (HMGA). Section 6 designates the father as the natural guardian of a minor legitimate child, and after him, the mother. For an illegitimate child, the mother is the natural guardian. The father’s guardianship extends to the minor’s person and property; the mother’s guardianship is limited to the person unless the father is unfit. Section 13 provides that the welfare of the minor is the paramount consideration in appointing a guardian. In Githa Hariharan v Reserve Bank of India (1999) the Court interpreted “after him” in Section 6(a) to mean “in his absence” (e.g., father’s unfitness, absence, or indifference), not strictly after his death, thereby empowering mothers to act as guardians during the father’s lifetime.

Adoption and guardianship intersect when a guardian gives a child in adoption. Under Section 9 of HAMA, only the father, mother, or guardian (with court permission) may give a child in adoption. The court’s permission is required to ensure the adoption serves the child’s welfare. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000 (as amended) introduced a secular adoption framework, but HAMA remains the governing statute for Hindus. Examination questions often test the distinction between “giving in adoption” (a ceremonial act) and “taking in adoption” (the adoptive parent’s act), and the requirement of datta homam for validity.

  • Confusing the doctrine of eclipse (pre‑constitutional law) with the void‑ab‑initio rule for post‑constitutional law.
  • Assuming severability automatically saves a statute without checking whether the remaining provisions are workable independently.
  • Treating waiver of a fundamental right as permissible because a party signed a contract containing a waiver clause.
  • Applying Section 70 of the Contract Act to gratuitous acts or to cases where the beneficiary had no opportunity to reject the benefit.
  • Believing mistake of law is a defence under Section 76 IPC, ignoring the statutory exclusion.
  • Overlooking the “welfare of the minor” paramountcy principle when analysing guardianship disputes under HMGA.
  • Assuming adoption under HAMA can be revoked by mutual consent, contrary to the irrevocability rule in Section 12.
