1. Foundations of Legal Reasoning in CLAT

1.1 The Role of Principle‑Fact Questions in the Examination

Principle‑fact questions form the backbone of the CLAT Legal Reasoning section because they test the ability to apply a given rule to a concrete situation without resorting to prior legal knowledge. The examination deliberately supplies a self‑contained principle so that every candidate, regardless of background, works from the same starting point. This design ensures that the assessment measures logical deduction, careful reading, and the discipline to ignore extraneous information. When a candidate treats the principle as a mere suggestion and imports external doctrines, the answer often diverges from the intended outcome, leading to loss of marks. Therefore, mastering the technique of strict application is as important as understanding the substantive law itself.

1.2 Distinguishing Legal Reasoning from General Knowledge

Legal reasoning differs from general knowledge in that it demands a structured analytical process rather than recall of facts. In a general knowledge item, the candidate retrieves a date, a name, or a statistic; in a legal reasoning item, the candidate must deconstruct the principle into its constituent elements, map each element onto the factual narrative, and then evaluate whether the principle’s conditions are satisfied. The process is sequential: first isolate the rule, then isolate the facts, then perform a logical match. This sequential discipline prevents the common error of reading the facts first and then retrofitting the principle to fit a preconceived conclusion. The examination rewards candidates who demonstrate this disciplined approach consistently across all questions.

1.3 The Importance of Strict Application of the Given Principle

Strict application means that every element stated in the principle must be present in the facts for the principle to operate; the absence of a single element renders the principle inapplicable, even if the real law would impose liability on broader grounds. For example, if a principle requires a “rash or negligent act” and the facts describe a driver who obeyed all traffic rules and reacted instantly to an unforeseeable event, the principle cannot be invoked because the element of rashness or negligence is missing. The examination deliberately includes such “near‑miss” fact patterns to test whether candidates can resist the temptation to impose liability based on moral intuition. The golden rule, therefore, is to treat the principle as a closed logical system and to decide solely on the basis of its internal criteria.

1.4 Common Structural Elements of a CLAT Legal Reasoning Item

Every legal reasoning item in CLAT follows a predictable architecture: a principle paragraph, a facts paragraph, and a question stem that asks for a decision, a liability determination, or the validity of a legal act. The principle paragraph may contain definitions, exceptions, or qualifying phrases such as “unless” or “provided that”. The facts paragraph introduces parties, actions, consequences, and sometimes background context that is irrelevant to the principle. The question stem typically offers four options, each representing a possible legal consequence. Recognising this structure allows a candidate to allocate reading time efficiently: first read the principle, underline its elements, then read the facts with those elements in mind, and finally evaluate each option against the matched elements. This systematic reading order reduces cognitive load and improves accuracy.

2. Structure and Technique of Principle‑Fact Questions

2.1 Reading the Principle: Identifying Elements and Exceptions

The first reading of the principle should be analytical rather than passive. The candidate must break the principle into discrete logical components: the actus reus (the conduct required), the mens rea or fault element (such as rashness, negligence, intention, or knowledge), any causation requirement, and any explicit exceptions or limiting clauses. For instance, a principle stating “Whoever, by doing any rash or negligent act, causes death to any person, is guilty of causing death by negligence” contains three essential elements: (1) a rash or negligent act, (2) causation of death, and (3) the absence of any statutory exception. Underlining or mentally tagging each element creates a checklist that can be applied rapidly to the facts. Exceptions, when present, are equally critical because they carve out situations where the principle does not operate even if all primary elements are satisfied.

2.2 Reading the Facts: Mapping Parties, Acts and Consequences

After the principle has been dissected, the facts are read with the checklist in mind. The candidate identifies the relevant parties, the specific conduct attributed to each party, the temporal sequence of events, and the resulting harm or legal consequence. Irrelevant background details — such as the weather, the time of day, or the personal history of a party — should be noted but not allowed to distract from the elements that matter. In a negligence scenario, the crucial facts are the speed of the vehicle, the visibility, the suddenness of the pedestrian’s movement, and the driver’s reaction. Each of these facts is then compared to the principle’s elements: does the speed constitute a rash act? Was the death caused by the driver’s act? Is there any exception such as inevitable accident? This mapping process transforms a narrative into a structured legal analysis.

2.3 Matching Facts to Principle Elements: A Step‑by‑Step Method

The matching stage is the core of legal reasoning. The candidate proceeds element by element: for each element of the principle, the candidate asks whether the facts contain a corresponding fact that satisfies it. If an element is satisfied, the candidate notes the supporting fact; if not, the principle fails at that point and the analysis can stop. This binary approach prevents the common error of “partial matching”, where a candidate assumes that because most elements are present, the principle applies. The examination often includes answer choices that exploit partial matches, so a rigorous element‑by‑element verification is essential. Once all elements are confirmed, the candidate determines the legal consequence prescribed by the principle — liability, non‑liability, validity, invalidity — and selects the option that mirrors that consequence exactly.

2.4 Selecting the Correct Option Without Importing External Law

The final step is to choose the answer option that reflects the outcome dictated solely by the given principle. The options are usually phrased as definitive statements: “X is liable”, “X is not liable”, “The contract is void”, “The fundamental right is not violated”. The candidate must resist the urge to select an option that aligns with real‑world law but contradicts the principle. For example, if the principle defines negligence narrowly and the facts show a driver acting reasonably, the correct answer is “X is not liable” even though a court applying the full law of negligence might find a breach of duty. The examination tests the ability to operate within a closed logical system; therefore, the only legitimate source of law for that question is the principle itself. Consistent adherence to this rule is the hallmark of high‑scoring candidates.

Illustrative Application: Negligence Principle
Principle: “Whoever, by doing any rash or negligent act, causes death to any person, is guilty of causing death by negligence.” Facts: A was driving his car within the prescribed speed limit on a clear, straight road. A child suddenly ran across the road from behind a parked vehicle. A applied the brakes immediately but could not stop in time and the child died. Decide whether A is guilty under the principle.
Answer: A is not guilty. The principle requires a rash or negligent act as the first element. Driving within the speed limit on a clear road and braking instantly when a child darts out unexpectedly does not constitute a rash or negligent act; the driver exercised reasonable care. Since the first element is absent, the principle does not apply, and liability cannot be imposed merely because death occurred.
Exam‑Day Insight
When the principle contains a qualifying phrase such as “rash or negligent”, treat the two alternatives as distinct elements; the facts must satisfy at least one of them. If the facts show neither rashness nor negligence, the principle fails regardless of the outcome. Mark the qualifying phrase during the first reading so you do not overlook it under time pressure.

3. Core Doctrines Frequently Tested

3.1 Negligence and the Duty of Care

Negligence remains the most frequently examined tort doctrine in CLAT because its elements — duty, breach, causation, and damage — map neatly onto the principle‑fact format. The principle usually states a duty of care in broad terms, such as “A person owes a duty to take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which could foreseeably injure another.” The facts then present a scenario where the defendant’s conduct is alleged to have fallen below the standard of a reasonable person. The candidate must identify whether a duty existed between the parties, whether the conduct breached that duty, whether the breach caused the harm, and whether the harm is of a type recognized by the principle. The foreseeability test, derived from the neighbour principle in Donoghue v Stevenson, is often embedded in the principle itself, so the candidate does not need to recall the case name but must apply the foreseeability language given.

3.2 Strict Liability and the Rule in Rylands v Fletcher

Strict liability differs from negligence in that the principle imposes liability without any requirement of fault. A typical principle reads: “A person who brings onto his land and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes is liable for all damage caused by its escape.” The elements are (1) a non‑natural use of land, (2) accumulation of a dangerous substance, (3) escape of that substance, and (4) damage resulting from the escape. The candidate must check each element against the facts; the absence of fault language means that even a perfectly careful defendant can be liable. This doctrine is frequently paired with a negligence principle in the same paper to test whether the candidate can distinguish fault‑based from non‑fault‑based liability.

3.3 Volenti Non Fit Injuria and Assumption of Risk

The maxim “volenti non fit injuria” translates to “to a willing person, no injury is done”. In CLAT, the principle is usually presented as: “A person who voluntarily consents to the risk of harm cannot claim damages for injury arising from that risk.” The elements are (1) knowledge of the specific risk, (2) free and voluntary acceptance of that risk, and (3) the injury must result from the very risk accepted. The facts often involve spectators at sporting events, participants in adventure activities, or employees in hazardous occupations. The candidate must verify that the claimant had actual knowledge of the precise danger and chose to encounter it without coercion. Mere presence at a venue does not automatically constitute consent; the principle demands a conscious, informed decision.

3.4 Offer, Acceptance and the Formation of Contract

Contract formation questions test the classical offer‑acceptance framework. A typical principle states: “A contract is formed when there is a lawful offer by one party and an unconditional acceptance of that offer by the other, communicated to the offeror.” The elements are (1) a definite offer showing intention to be bound, (2) an acceptance that mirrors the offer without variation, (3) communication of acceptance to the offeror, and (4) legality of object and consideration. The facts frequently introduce a counter‑offer, a conditional acceptance, or a revocation before communication. The candidate must trace the sequence: does the offeree’s response match the offer exactly? If the response adds a term, it is a counter‑offer, which terminates the original offer. The principle’s emphasis on “unconditional acceptance” is the decisive filter for the correct option.

4. Landmark Judicial Pronouncements and Their Application

4.1 Donoghue v Stevenson: The Neighbour Principle

Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) established the modern law of negligence by articulating the neighbour principle: “You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour.” In CLAT, the principle is often reproduced verbatim or paraphrased, and the facts involve a consumer, a manufacturer, and a latent defect. The candidate applies the foreseeability test directly from the principle: was the plaintiff a foreseeable victim of the defendant’s conduct? The case also illustrates the expansion of duty beyond contractual privity, a concept that appears in principles concerning product liability. Understanding the logical structure of the neighbour principle enables the candidate to handle any fact pattern that mirrors its reasoning, even when the case name is not mentioned.

4.2 Rylands v Fletcher: Escape of Dangerous Things

Rylands v Fletcher (1868) created the rule of strict liability for non‑natural use of land. The principle extracted from the case is: “A person who for his own purposes brings on his land and collects and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it in at his peril, and if it escapes he is prima facie liable for all damage which is the natural consequence of its escape.” The elements — non‑natural use, accumulation, escape, and damage — are directly testable. CLAT questions may present a scenario involving water reservoirs, chemical storage, or fireworks. The candidate must identify whether the use is “non‑natural” in the context of the locality and whether the escape was the direct cause of damage. The case also introduces defences such as act of God, plaintiff’s own fault, and statutory authority, which may appear as qualifying clauses in the principle.

4.3 Maneka Gandhi v Union of India: Expanding Article 21

Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978) transformed the interpretation of Article 21 of the Constitution of India, holding that “procedure established by law” must be fair, just, and reasonable, and that the right to life includes a bundle of subsidiary rights such as the right to travel abroad, the right to privacy, and the right to a clean environment. In CLAT, a principle may quote Article 21 and add the judicial gloss that the procedure must be reasonable. The facts then describe a governmental action — such as impounding a passport without a hearing — and the candidate must decide whether the procedure satisfied the fairness requirement. The case illustrates how a constitutional provision can be read expansively through judicial interpretation, and the principle‑fact format tests the candidate’s ability to apply that expanded reading without importing unrelated constitutional doctrines.

5. Integrated Practice: From Principle to Judgment

5.1 Building a Logical Chain from Principle to Conclusion

Effective legal reasoning in the examination is essentially the construction of a logical chain: principle elements → factual correspondences → satisfaction or non‑satisfaction → legal consequence. The candidate should practice writing this chain in a few sentences during preparation, even if the exam only requires selecting an option. For example, “The principle requires a rash act; the facts show the driver was within speed limits and braked instantly; therefore the rash‑act element is absent; consequently the principle does not apply and the driver is not liable.” This explicit chain clarifies thinking, reduces the chance of missing an element, and provides a mental template that can be executed rapidly under timed conditions.

5.2 Avoiding Common Logical Fallacies in Answer Choices

Answer options are often crafted to exploit logical fallacies such as affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, or conflating moral blameworthiness with legal liability under the given principle. A candidate who recognises these patterns can eliminate distractors quickly. For instance, an option stating “The driver is liable because a child died” affirms the consequent (death) without establishing the antecedent (rash act). Another option may claim “The driver is not liable because he was not speeding” which denies the antecedent (speeding) but ignores other possible rash behaviours. The disciplined element‑by‑element method neutralises these traps because each option is measured against the checklist derived from the principle, not against intuitive notions of justice.

5.3 Time Management Strategies for the Legal Reasoning Section

The Legal Reasoning section typically contains 35 to 40 questions to be completed in 35 to 40 minutes, allowing roughly one minute per question. A practical time‑management routine is: spend the first 15 seconds reading and underlining the principle’s elements; spend the next 20 seconds reading the facts while mentally ticking off the elements; spend the final 25 seconds evaluating the four options against the completed checklist. If a question appears unusually dense, mark it and move on, returning only if time permits. Consistent practice with a timer builds the rhythm needed to maintain accuracy without rushing. The goal is not merely speed but controlled speed — each step of the logical chain must be completed before the next begins.

Mastery of these techniques, combined with a thorough grasp of the core doctrines and landmark cases discussed above, equips a candidate to approach every principle‑fact question with confidence and precision.

Doctrine of Proportionality in Constitutional Adjudication

The doctrine of proportionality requires that any state action limiting a fundamental right must be suitable, necessary, and balanced in its impact. Indian courts adopted the four‑step test from the German Federal Constitutional Court and the European Court of Human Rights: (1) a legitimate aim, (2) rational connection between the measure and the aim, (3) necessity — no less restrictive alternative exists, and (4) proportionality stricto sensu — the benefit outweighs the harm to the right. The Supreme Court first articulated this framework in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) when it held that constitutional amendments cannot destroy the basic structure, implicitly invoking proportionality. In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) the Court expanded Article 21 and insisted that the procedure depriving personal liberty must be “fair, just and reasonable”, a formulation that operationalises proportionality. Later, K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) applied the test to the right to privacy, striking down the mandatory linking of Aadhaar with bank accounts because the measure was not the least restrictive means. The doctrine also governs legislative review under Article 14: a classification must have an intelligible differentia that bears a rational relation to the object, and the differential treatment must not be excessive. In State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) the Court invalidated a law that imposed a blanket ban on the sale of certain goods, finding the restriction disproportionate to the stated public health objective. Examiners frequently embed a principle that quotes the four‑step test and then present facts where a less restrictive alternative (e.g., a licensing regime instead of a total ban) is evident; the correct answer hinges on identifying the failure at the necessity or balancing stage.

Vicarious Liability and the Scope of Employer Responsibility

Vicarious liability imposes responsibility on an employer for torts committed by an employee in the course of employment. The classic test, restated in Salmond’s “course of employment” formulation, asks whether the act was authorised or a mode — however improper — of performing an authorised task. The House of Lords in Lloyd v. Grace, Smith & Co. (1912) held a solicitor’s firm liable for a clerk’s fraud because the fraud was committed while the clerk was ostensibly carrying out his duties. In India, the Supreme Court in State of Rajasthan v. Mst. Vidhyawati (1962) extended vicarious liability to the government for the negligent driving of a driver employed in the official car pool, rejecting the sovereign immunity defence for commercial activities. The “control test” and the “integration test” have been supplemented by the “enterprise risk” approach in Mohamud v. WM Morrison Supermarkets plc (2016), where the UK Supreme Court held an employer liable for an employee’s assault on a customer because the risk of such conduct was inherent in the business of serving the public. Indian courts have mirrored this in M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (1997), imposing absolute liability on enterprises engaged in hazardous activities, a principle that overlaps with vicarious liability but does not require a master‑servant relationship. Examiners often craft a principle that defines “course of employment” narrowly (e.g., “acts done within the scope of the employee’s duties and during working hours”) and then present facts where the employee deviates for a personal errand but uses the employer’s vehicle. The candidate must decide whether the deviation is a “frolic” (breaking the chain) or a “detour” (still within scope). The distinction turns on the degree of departure and whether the act serves the employer’s business even indirectly.

Application of Vicarious Liability Principle
Principle: An employer is liable for the torts of an employee committed in the course of employment. “Course of employment” includes acts authorised by the employer and acts which are unauthorised modes of doing an authorised act. Facts: A delivery driver employed by a courier company, while on his scheduled route, stops at a tea stall for a personal break. He leaves the engine running. A child playing nearby climbs into the cab, accidentally engages the gear, and the vehicle moves forward, injuring a pedestrian. The pedestrian sues the courier company.
The driver’s stop for tea is a brief personal detour but does not constitute a frolic of his own; he remains on the prescribed route, the vehicle is still under his control, and the risk of the vehicle moving unattended is inherent in the delivery business. The act of leaving the engine running is an unauthorised mode of performing the authorised act of making deliveries. Therefore the driver was acting in the course of employment, and the courier company is vicariously liable. The correct option will state that the employer is liable because the deviation was a detour, not a frolic.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a principle defines “course of employment” with a two‑part test (authorised act + unauthorised mode), first locate the authorised act in the facts (here, the delivery route). Then check whether the alleged tort arises from an unauthorised way of doing that act. If the employee’s personal motive is incidental and the risk is connected to the employer’s enterprise, treat it as a detour. Mark the option that mirrors this two‑step reasoning; discard options that focus solely on the employee’s personal motive or on the employer’s lack of direct control.

The Principle of Legitimate Expectation in Administrative Law

Legitimate expectation protects a person’s reasonable anticipation of a benefit or procedural fairness arising from a consistent administrative practice or an explicit promise. The doctrine originated in English law — Council of Civil Service Unions v. Minister for the Civil Service (1985) — and was imported into Indian jurisprudence through State of Kerala v. K. B. K. Nair (1995) and Union of India v. R. K. Jain (1999). The Supreme Court in Navjot Singh Sidhu v. State of Punjab (2005) held that a representation by a public authority, if clear, unambiguous, and relied upon, creates a legitimate expectation that the authority will act consistently. However, the expectation must be “legitimate” — not based on a mere hope — and cannot override a statutory duty or public interest. In Food Corporation of India v. M/s. Kamdhenu Cattle Feed Industries (1993) the Court refused to enforce an expectation that a tender would be awarded to the lowest bidder because the tender documents expressly reserved the right to reject all bids. The principle operates at two levels: procedural (right to be heard before a decision affecting the expectation) and substantive (entitlement to the benefit itself). Examiners often give a principle that states “a public authority must honour a clear representation unless a compelling public interest requires otherwise” and then present facts where a government department issues a circular promising a subsidy to farmers who adopt a new seed variety, but later withdraws the subsidy citing budget constraints. The candidate must assess whether the representation was sufficiently specific, whether the farmers relied on it (e.g., purchased seeds), and whether the withdrawal is justified by an overriding public interest. The correct answer typically hinges on the reliance element and the absence of a statutory power to revoke.

Judicial Review of Legislative Action: Basic Structure and Beyond

The basic structure doctrine, crystallised in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), limits Parliament’s amending power under Article 368 by declaring that certain constitutional features — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law — cannot be abrogated. Subsequent cases refined the catalogue: Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) added free and fair elections; Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) included judicial review and the balance between fundamental rights and directive principles; S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) incorporated secularism and federalism as inviolable. The doctrine also extends to ordinary legislation: in I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) the Court held that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are subject to judicial review if they violate basic structure. The “width” test (whether the amendment destroys the identity of the Constitution) and the “identity” test (whether the amendment changes the essential features) are the two analytical tools. Examiners may present a principle quoting the basic structure list and a fact scenario where a constitutional amendment removes the power of judicial review over a specific class of economic legislation. The candidate must identify that judicial review is a basic feature (per Minerva Mills) and that its removal, even for a limited domain, violates the doctrine. A common distractor argues that Parliament’s plenary power under Article 368 permits any amendment; the correct response cites the limitation imposed by the basic structure doctrine.

Comparative Analysis of Strict Liability Regimes Across Jurisdictions

Strict liability imposes responsibility without proof of fault. The English rule in Rylands v. Fletcher (1868) requires a non‑natural use of land, escape of a dangerous thing, and damage. Defences include act of God, plaintiff’s own fault, and statutory authority. India adopted the rule in M. C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) but expanded it to “absolute liability” for enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities, removing the non‑natural use requirement and most defences. The Supreme Court in Indian Council for Enviro‑Legal Action v. Union of India (1996) held that the enterprise must bear the cost of remedial measures irrespective of negligence. In the United States, the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 519‑520 imposes strict liability for abnormally dangerous activities, applying a six‑factor test (existence of high risk, likelihood of great harm, inability to eliminate risk by reasonable care, uncommon usage, inappropriateness to location, and value to community). The European Union’s Environmental Liability Directive (2004/35/EC) establishes a regime of strict liability for operators of dangerous activities, coupled with a financial security requirement. Examiners may give a principle that mirrors the Rylands rule but adds a clause “unless the activity is statutorily authorised”. The facts could describe a chemical plant operating under a licence that permits emissions within prescribed limits; a sudden leak exceeds the limits and damages nearby crops. The candidate must decide whether statutory authority is a complete defence (it is not, because the licence does not authorise the escape) and whether the Indian absolute liability principle would impose liability even if the escape were within limits. The correct answer distinguishes between the English defence of statutory authority and the Indian absolute liability standard.

  • Confusing the “detour” versus “frolic” distinction in vicarious liability by focusing only on the employee’s personal motive.
  • Treating legitimate expectation as an absolute right and ignoring the overriding public interest exception.
  • Assuming statutory authority is a blanket defence to strict liability under Rylands v. Fletcher without checking whether the statute authorises the specific escape.
  • Misapplying the basic structure doctrine to ordinary legislation that does not amend the Constitution.
  • Overlooking the necessity step in the proportionality test and selecting an option that only addresses legitimate aim.
  • Equating absolute liability under M. C. Mehta with the traditional Rylands rule and forgetting the removal of the non‑natural use requirement.
  • Failing to identify reliance as a factual prerequisite for a legitimate expectation claim.

The Doctrine of Pith and Substance in Centre‑State Legislative Relations

The doctrine of pith and substance determines whether a legislature has competence to enact a law by examining the true nature and character of the legislation, not merely its incidental effects. Originating in Canadian constitutional law (Citizens Insurance Co. v. Parsons (1881)), it was adopted by the Indian Supreme Court to interpret the distribution of powers under Article 246 read with the Seventh Schedule. The test requires the court to identify the dominant purpose of the statute — its “pith and substance” — and then match that purpose against the legislative entries in the Union, State, or Concurrent Lists. If the dominant purpose falls within the competence of the enacting legislature, the law is valid even if it incidentally trenches on a subject assigned to another legislature.

In State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara (1951) the Court upheld the Bombay Prohibition Act, holding that its pith and substance was the regulation of intoxicating liquors (Entry 8, State List) despite its incidental impact on import and export (Union List). In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977) a state tax on the entry of goods into a local area was sustained because its true character was a tax on entry of goods (Entry 52, State List), not a tax on inter‑state trade. The doctrine also underpins the distinction between legitimate incidental encroachment and colourable legislation: a law that merely appears to fall within a legislature’s competence but in reality aims at a subject outside that competence is struck down as colourable (see K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953)).

Examiners often frame a principle that quotes the pith‑and‑substance test and then present a fact scenario where a state law on “public health” also regulates the sale of a commodity that appears in the Union List. The candidate must isolate the dominant purpose — public health — and conclude that the law is valid. A common distractor argues that any overlap with a Union List entry renders the law ultra vires; the correct answer cites the principle that incidental encroachment is permissible.

The Doctrine of Eclipse and the Revival of Pre‑Constitutional Statutes

Article 13(1) of the Constitution declares that all laws in force immediately before the commencement of the Constitution, insofar as they are inconsistent with the fundamental rights, shall be void to the extent of such inconsistency. The doctrine of eclipse, first articulated in Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955), holds that a pre‑constitutional law inconsistent with a fundamental right does not cease to exist; it becomes dormant — eclipsed — by the fundamental right. The law remains on the statute book but is unenforceable against citizens so long as the inconsistency persists. If a subsequent constitutional amendment removes the inconsistency, the eclipse is lifted and the law revives automatically without the need for fresh legislation.

In Bhikaji Narain Dhakras the Madhya Pradesh Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 1947, which nationalised motor transport, was eclipsed by Article 19(1)(g) (freedom to carry on trade). The Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act, 1955 amended Article 19(6) to permit state monopolies, thereby removing the inconsistency; the 1947 Act revived and became fully operative. The Supreme Court reiterated in State of Gujarat v. Shri Ambica Mills (1974) that the doctrine applies only to citizens; non‑citizens cannot claim the benefit of fundamental rights, so the pre‑constitutional law remains enforceable against them. The doctrine is distinct from severability: severability removes the offending portion, whereas eclipse suspends the whole statute until the constitutional barrier is removed.

Examiners may give a principle stating the eclipse doctrine and a fact pattern describing a pre‑constitutional law that conflicts with Article 19(1)(g), followed by a constitutional amendment that inserts a saving clause. The candidate must recognise that the law revives and is enforceable. A frequent trap is to treat the law as permanently void or to require re‑enactment; the correct response emphasises automatic revival upon removal of the inconsistency.

Technique: Mapping Eclipse to Fact Pattern
Principle: “A pre‑constitutional law inconsistent with a fundamental right is eclipsed and revives automatically when the constitutional inconsistency is removed.” Facts: The Bombay Agricultural Produce Markets Act, 1939, empowered the state to compulsorily acquire agricultural produce. After the Constitution came into force, Article 19(1)(f) guaranteed the right to acquire, hold and dispose of property. In 1954, the Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act inserted Article 31A, protecting laws providing for acquisition of estates. A farmer challenges the 1939 Act in 1955. Decide.
The 1939 Act is a pre‑constitutional law. Its compulsory acquisition provision conflicts with Article 19(1)(f) and is therefore eclipsed. The Fourth Amendment (1954) introduced Article 31A, which validates laws providing for acquisition of estates, removing the inconsistency. Consequently, the eclipse lifts and the 1939 Act revives, becoming enforceable against the farmer. The challenge fails.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a principle mentions “eclipse” or “revival”, immediately check whether the facts describe a constitutional amendment that removes the specific fundamental‑right conflict. If the amendment addresses the exact right, the law revives; if the amendment is unrelated, the eclipse persists. Mark the amendment year and the article amended to avoid confusing it with later amendments.

The Wednesbury Principle and the Standard of Judicial Review of Administrative Discretion

The Wednesbury principle, formulated in Associated Provincial Picture Houses Ltd v. Wednesbury Corporation (1948), establishes that a decision of a public authority can be set aside if it is so unreasonable that no reasonable authority could have reached it — often described as “perversity”. Indian courts adopted this threshold in Tata Cellular v. Union of India (1994), where the Supreme Court enumerated three grounds for judicial review of administrative action: illegality, irrationality (Wednesbury unreasonableness), and procedural impropriety. The Court clarified that irrationality does not require the decision to be absurd; it suffices that the authority ignored relevant considerations, relied on irrelevant ones, or acted in a manner that no reasonable authority would.

Subsequent decisions refined the standard. In Union of India v. G. Ganayutham (1997) the Court held that a disciplinary authority’s penalty must be proportionate to the misconduct, importing a proportionality element into the Wednesbury test. Om Kumar v. Union of India (2000) explicitly recognised proportionality as a distinct ground, especially where fundamental rights are affected, but retained Wednesbury as the baseline for pure administrative discretion. The principle also demands that the decision‑maker not act mala fide or under dictation.

In principle‑fact questions, the examiner may quote the Wednesbury formulation and present a scenario where a licensing authority cancels a licence without giving the licensee an opportunity to be heard, or where a government order fixes a price that bears no rational relation to market conditions. The candidate must identify whether the authority’s action meets the “no reasonable authority” threshold. A common distractor suggests that any error of fact or law suffices; the correct answer requires the higher threshold of perversity or proportionality breach.

The Principle of Natural Justice in Administrative Proceedings

Natural justice comprises two cardinal rules: audi alteram partem (hear the other side) and nemo judex in causa sua (no one should be a judge in his own cause). While originally confined to judicial and quasi‑judicial functions, the Supreme Court in A. K. Kraipak v. Union of India (1969) extended the bias rule to administrative selections, holding that a member of a selection committee who was also a candidate created a reasonable apprehension of bias. In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) the Court linked procedural fairness to Article 21, requiring that any procedure depriving a person of life or personal liberty must be fair, just, and reasonable, thereby constitutionalising audi alteram partem for a broad range of administrative actions.

The duty to hear applies with varying intensity. For quasi‑judicial functions (e.g., disciplinary inquiries, licence revocations) a full oral hearing with cross‑examination may be required. For purely administrative functions (e.g., policy formulation, routine orders) a post‑decisional hearing or a reasoned order may suffice. Exceptions are recognised: urgency, statutory exclusion, confidentiality, and impracticability. In State of Orissa v. Dr. (Miss) Binapani Dei (1967) the Court held that a government servant dismissed without an inquiry violated natural justice because the statute mandated an inquiry.

Examiners often frame a principle stating that natural justice applies to quasi‑judicial functions and then describe a fact situation where the enquiry officer is also the complainant, or where a show‑cause notice is issued but no opportunity to respond is given. The candidate must spot the bias or the denial of hearing. A frequent trap is to argue that natural justice never applies to administrative acts; the correct response distinguishes between quasi‑judicial and purely administrative acts and applies the appropriate level of procedural protection.

Judicial Activism and Judicial Restraint in Indian Constitutional Jurisprudence

Judicial activism refers to a willingness by courts to depart from strict textual interpretation in order to advance constitutional values, often by fashioning remedies or creating new rights. Judicial restraint, by contrast, emphasises deference to the elected branches and limits judicial intervention to clear constitutional violations. The Indian trajectory illustrates a shift from early restraint to pronounced activism. In A. K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) the Court adopted a literal reading of Article 21, refusing to read “procedure established by law” as requiring fairness. The turning point came with Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), where the basic structure doctrine empowered the Court to strike down constitutional amendments. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) expanded Article 21 to include due process, and Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) laid down guidelines on sexual harassment at the workplace in the absence of legislation, a classic instance of judicial law‑making.

Public interest litigation (PIL) further broadened access; S. P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) relaxed locus standi, allowing any citizen to approach the Court for enforcement of public duties. Critics argue that such activism encroaches on legislative and executive domains, undermining separation of powers. Proponents contend that activism fills governance vacuums and protects marginalized groups. In principle‑fact questions, a principle may describe a court issuing mandatory directions to the executive to implement a policy (e.g., mid‑day meals, pollution control). The candidate must decide whether the principle characterises this as activism or restraint. The correct answer aligns with the principle’s language: if the principle defines activism as “courts directing policy implementation”, the scenario exemplifies activism; if the principle defines restraint as “courts refusing to interfere in policy matters”, the same scenario would be restraint.

  • Confusing pith and substance with colourable legislation by ignoring the dominant purpose test.
  • Treating an eclipsed law as permanently void and missing the automatic revival upon constitutional amendment.
  • Applying the Wednesbury “no reasonable authority” test as a mere error‑of‑law standard instead of the higher perversity threshold.
  • Assuming natural justice requires a full oral hearing for every administrative decision, overlooking the quasi‑judicial versus administrative distinction.
  • Equating judicial activism with any judicial intervention, without checking whether the principle defines activism by the creation of new norms or remedies.
  • Overlooking the proportionality element that Indian courts have grafted onto Wednesbury unreasonableness in fundamental‑rights cases.
  • Failing to identify the specific fundamental right whose amendment lifts the eclipse, leading to an incorrect conclusion about revival.

Doctrine of Colourable Legislation and Its Judicial Treatment

The doctrine of colourable legislation addresses situations where a legislature, lacking the constitutional competence to enact a particular law directly, attempts to achieve the same result indirectly by cloaking the measure in a different legislative form. The core inquiry is whether the true substance and effect of the enactment fall within the legislative field assigned to that legislature, irrespective of the label or preamble attached to the statute. In K. C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa (1953) the Supreme Court held that a law which in form deals with a subject within the legislative competence but in substance encroaches upon a forbidden field is colourable and therefore void. The test requires a two‑step analysis: first, identify the legislative entry under which the statute purports to be enacted; second, examine the dominant purpose and practical operation of the provision to see whether it transgresses the constitutional distribution of powers.

A frequent illustration arises in the context of taxation. If a state legislature enacts a “fee” that in reality functions as a tax on a subject reserved for the Union, the Court will strike it down as colourable. In State of Bihar v. Kameshwar Singh (1952) the Court invalidated a Bihar law that imposed a “cess” on land revenue, finding that the levy was in substance a tax on agricultural income, a Union subject. The doctrine does not invalidate every incidental effect on a forbidden field; only when the legislation’s pith and substance reveals a deliberate evasion of constitutional limits does the colourable label attach. The distinction between colourable legislation and a legitimate exercise of ancillary powers is subtle: ancillary powers permit incidental encroachment necessary for effective exercise of the primary competence, whereas colourable legislation masks a primary purpose that lies outside competence.

Judicial scrutiny also extends to the procedural guise adopted. A statute may be framed as a regulatory measure under the police power while its dominant aim is revenue generation beyond the legislature’s fiscal authority. The Court examines legislative history, the title, the preamble, and the practical operation of the law. In Union of India v. H. S. Dhillon (1971) the Court emphasized that the “real nature” of the enactment must be ascertained from its “true character and effect” rather than its formal description. This approach prevents legislatures from circumventing the federal scheme by creative drafting.

For examinees, the key is to spot a principle that defines colourable legislation as “a law which in form falls within legislative competence but in substance exceeds it” and then match the facts to the two‑step test. The facts will typically describe a statute labelled as a “regulation”, “fee”, or “licensing scheme” whose operative provisions reveal a prohibited purpose. The correct answer follows the principle’s language without importing external constitutional theory.

Doctrine of Harmonious Construction in Statutory Interpretation

Harmonious construction is the interpretive principle that statutes should be read as a coherent whole, reconciling apparently conflicting provisions so that each retains its intended effect. The maxim generalia specialibus non derogant — general provisions do not derogate from specific ones — guides the process. When two sections of the same enactment appear to clash, the court first attempts to interpret them in a manner that gives effect to both; only if reconciliation is impossible does it resort to the rule that the later or more specific provision prevails. In Sri Venkataramana Devaru v. State of Mysore (1958) the Supreme Court held that a general provision conferring power on the state to acquire property must be read subject to a specific provision protecting religious institutions, thereby preserving both.

The doctrine operates at three levels. First, intra‑statutory harmony: provisions within a single Act are construed to avoid internal inconsistency. Second, inter‑statutory harmony: where two statutes deal with the same subject, the later enactment is presumed not to repeal the earlier by implication unless there is a clear repugnancy. Third, constitutional harmony: statutory provisions are interpreted, where possible, to conform to constitutional mandates, thereby avoiding a declaration of invalidity. In M. S. M. Sharma v. Krishna Sinha (1959) the Court applied harmonious construction to reconcile the Parliamentary Privileges Act with the fundamental right to freedom of speech, holding that the privilege of freedom of speech in Parliament does not extend to publishing defamatory material outside the House.

A practical technique for candidates is to map each contested provision to its legislative purpose, then ask whether the purposes can coexist. If the facts describe a scenario where a general regulatory power is exercised in a way that nullifies a specific exemption, the principle of harmonious construction demands that the exemption be preserved. The examiner may frame a principle stating “where two provisions of the same statute are capable of being read together without conflict, they must be so read” and then present facts where an authority invokes the general provision to override the specific one. The correct option will reflect the reconciled reading.

Another nuance is the “rule of last resort”: harmonious construction yields only when the language permits a reasonable reconciliation. If the provisions are irreconcilably contradictory, the later or more specific provision prevails, and the court may declare the earlier provision impliedly repealed. This limit prevents the doctrine from becoming a tool for judicial rewriting.

Technique: Spotting Colourable Legislation in a Principle‑Fact Item
Principle: “A law which in form falls within the legislative competence of a legislature but in substance exceeds that competence is colourable and void.” Facts: The State Legislature enacts the “Urban Development Fee Act”, imposing a fee on all commercial establishments for “maintenance of municipal infrastructure”. The fee is calculated as a percentage of the establishment’s annual turnover and is collected by the State Revenue Department. The State List does not include taxation on turnover; that subject belongs to the Union List. The State argues the levy is a regulatory fee, not a tax. Question: Applying the principle, what is the correct outcome?
The principle requires a two‑step test: (1) identify the legislative entry under which the Act purports to be enacted — here, a regulatory fee under the State’s power over local government; (2) examine the dominant purpose and practical operation. The fee is measured by turnover, collected by the revenue department, and functions as a revenue‑raising measure on a subject (tax on turnover) reserved for the Union. Its substance is a tax, not a regulatory fee. Therefore the Act is colourable legislation and void. The correct answer is that the Act exceeds the State’s competence and is invalid.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Advanced Doctrines
When a principle defines a doctrine in abstract terms, first restate the definition in your own words, then isolate the factual elements that correspond to each component of the definition. For colourable legislation, tick off “form within competence” and “substance beyond competence”. For harmonious construction, note the two conflicting provisions and ask whether a reasonable reading can give effect to both. Write a one‑sentence logical chain before looking at options; this prevents the lure of plausible but extraneous reasoning.

Doctrine of Severability and Its Application to Unconstitutional Provisions

The doctrine of severability determines whether an unconstitutional portion of a statute can be excised while leaving the remainder operative. Unlike the “blue‑pencil” test used in some common‑law jurisdictions — which permits only mechanical deletion of words without altering the text — Indian courts apply a substantive severability test: the valid part must be capable of standing independently, both legally and functionally, after the offending provision is removed. In R. M. D. C. v. Union of India (1957) the Supreme Court held that a statute containing a void provision will survive if the valid portion is “complete in itself” and the legislature would have enacted it separately. The Court examines legislative intent, the structural interdependence of provisions, and whether the remaining law can achieve its purpose without the severed part.

A landmark illustration is State of Bombay v. F. N. Balsara (1951), where the Bombay Prohibition Act contained a clause prohibiting possession of liquor for medicinal purposes, which was struck down as violating the right to practice a profession. The Court severed that clause, upholding the rest of the Act because the prohibition scheme remained coherent and enforceable. Conversely, in K. T. Plantation v. State of Karnataka (2011) the Court refused severability where the unconstitutional provision was the “heart” of the legislation, and its removal would render the statute a mere shell.

The test involves three questions: (1) Is the invalid provision separable on a textual basis? (2) Does the remainder retain a complete legislative scheme? (3) Would the legislature have enacted the remainder alone? If any answer is negative, the entire statute falls. Examiners often present a principle stating “where a part of a statute is unconstitutional, the rest may survive if it is severable” and then give facts where the invalid clause is central to the regulatory objective. The correct choice reflects the negative answer to the third question.

It is crucial to distinguish severability from the doctrine of eclipse, which deals with pre‑constitutional laws that become dormant upon inconsistency with fundamental rights and revive upon amendment. Severability operates on a single enactment at the moment of judicial review; eclipse concerns temporal revival across constitutional changes.

Doctrine of Prospective Overruling and Its Limits

Prospective overruling allows a court to declare a legal rule unconstitutional or erroneous while limiting the effect of that declaration to future cases, thereby preserving the validity of past transactions and decisions that relied on the overruled rule. The doctrine originated in the United States (Great Northern Railway Co. v. Sunburst Oil & Refining Co., 1932) and was adopted in India in Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967), where the Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend fundamental rights, but applied the ruling prospectively to avoid unsettling earlier amendments. The technique was refined in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), where the basic‑structure doctrine was declared but the Court upheld the validity of past amendments that did not violate the basic structure.

The rationale is twofold: (1) to prevent injustice to parties who acted in good faith under the previous law, and (2) to maintain legal stability and avoid a flood of retrospective litigation. However, the doctrine is not a blanket licence. In I. R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007) the Court clarified that prospective overruling cannot be used to validate legislation that is void ab initio for violating the basic structure; such laws remain void from inception. The Court also held that prospective overruling is inappropriate where the overruled rule concerns a fundamental right that has been infringed — the remedy must be retrospective to vindicate the right.

Key limits include: (a) the doctrine applies only to judicial decisions, not to legislative acts; (b) it cannot be invoked to preserve an unconstitutional statute that has no severable valid portion; (c) the prospective effect must be clearly articulated in the judgment, otherwise the default is retrospective application. In principle‑fact questions, a principle may state “a court may declare a rule invalid but give its decision prospective effect to protect vested rights”. The facts will describe a scenario where a regulatory regime is struck down but the authority seeks to enforce past orders. The correct answer follows the principle’s language: if the principle permits prospective overruling, the past orders stand; if the principle requires retrospective invalidation, they fall.

Candidates should note that the Indian Supreme Court has used prospective overruling sparingly, reserving it for constitutional adjudication where massive reliance interests are at stake. It is not a tool for ordinary statutory interpretation.

Doctrine of Constitutional Morality and Its Judicial Articulation

The concept of constitutional morality was first invoked by the Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) to describe the unwritten normative framework that guides the interpretation of the Constitution beyond its textual provisions. The Court held that the basic structure of the Constitution embodies a moral order that cannot be altered by parliamentary amendment. Subsequent jurisprudence expanded this idea: in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) the Court read constitutional morality into Article 14, 15 and 21 to strike down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, emphasizing that the Constitution’s transformative promise requires the protection of dignity and equality for sexual minorities. In Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2019) (the Sabarimala case), the majority linked constitutional morality to the principle of non‑discrimination and the right to religious freedom, holding that the exclusion of women of menstruating age violated the constitutional morality of gender justice. The doctrine operates as an interpretive lens: when a statutory provision or executive action is challenged, the Court asks whether it conforms to the moral values implicit in the Preamble, the fundamental rights chapter, and the directive principles. This inquiry is not a free‑floating moral judgment; it is anchored in the text and structure of the Constitution, and the Court has repeatedly cautioned that constitutional morality cannot be used to rewrite clear legislative language. The practical effect for principle‑fact questions is that a principle may state “no law shall violate constitutional morality” and the facts will present a facially neutral statute that disproportionately impacts a protected group. The correct answer follows the Court’s two‑step test: (1) identify the constitutional value at stake (equality, dignity, secularism) and (2) assess whether the impugned measure is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim, judged against the moral consensus reflected in precedent.

Public Trust Doctrine and Environmental Jurisprudence

The public trust doctrine, rooted in Roman law and revived in Indian environmental jurisprudence, obliges the State to hold certain natural resources — rivers, forests, coastlines, and the air — in trust for the benefit of the public. The Supreme Court first articulated the doctrine in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1997) (the Oleum Gas Leak case), declaring that the State is a trustee of natural resources and cannot alienate them in a manner that defeats the public interest. In Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) the Court linked the doctrine to Articles 48A and 51A(g) of the Constitution, holding that the precautionary principle and the polluter‑pays principle are integral to the trust obligation. The doctrine was further expanded in Intellectuals Forum v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2006), where the Court prevented the conversion of a public park into a commercial complex, stating that the trust extends to urban green spaces. A critical procedural aspect is that the doctrine enables any citizen to approach the Court under Article 32 or 226 without demonstrating a private injury; the breach of the trust itself constitutes a public wrong. In principle‑fact questions, the principle often reads “the State holds natural resources in trust for the people and cannot transfer them for private gain”. The facts may describe a mining lease granted in a reserved forest without an environmental impact assessment. The correct option will reflect that the lease is void ab initio because the State failed its fiduciary duty, and the Court can issue a continuing mandamus to restore the resource. Candidates must distinguish the public trust doctrine from the doctrine of sustainable development: the former imposes a non‑delegable fiduciary duty, while the latter is a policy guideline that informs legislative balancing.

Technique: Identifying the Core Principle in a Public Trust Scenario
A State government grants a 30‑year mining lease for bauxite extraction in a declared wildlife sanctuary. The lease order states that the project will generate employment and revenue. An environmental NGO files a writ petition under Article 32, arguing that the grant violates the public trust doctrine. The principle supplied in the question reads: “The State holds natural resources in trust for the public and cannot alienate them for private commercial exploitation.” Which of the following conclusions follows from the principle and the facts?
The principle establishes a fiduciary duty that is absolute with respect to resources held in trust. The facts show that the sanctuary is a natural resource held in trust and that the lease is for private commercial exploitation (bauxite mining). The stated public benefits (employment, revenue) do not diminish the private commercial character of the exploitation. Therefore, the lease is impermissible under the principle. The correct answer is that the lease is void and the State must revoke it; any order to the contrary would breach the trust obligation.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a principle invokes the public trust doctrine, look for any language that tries to justify the alienation by citing socio‑economic benefits. The doctrine treats such justifications as irrelevant unless the principle explicitly permits a balancing test. Mark the option that treats the trust duty as non‑derogable.

Judicial Review of Ordinances: The Satisfaction Requirement and Judicial Scrutiny

Article 123 (President) and Article 213 (Governor) empower the executive to promulgate ordinances when Parliament or the State Legislature is not in session, provided the executive is satisfied that circumstances exist which render immediate action necessary. The Supreme Court in R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970) held that the satisfaction of the President is justiciable only on the ground of mala fide; the Court will not substitute its own assessment of necessity. In A.K. Roy v. Union of India (1982) the Court reiterated that the subjective satisfaction is a political question, but it may be reviewed if the ordinance is a colourable exercise of power — for instance, re‑promulgating an ordinance repeatedly without legislative approval. The landmark decision in Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) clarified that re‑promulgation of an ordinance without placing it before the legislature constitutes a fraud on the Constitution, and the Court can strike down such ordinances. The Court also held that an ordinance cannot be used to amend the Constitution or to override a judicial decision that has attained finality. In principle‑fact questions, the principle may state “an ordinance can be challenged only on the ground that the executive’s satisfaction was mala fide or that the ordinance is a colourable exercise of power”. The facts will typically describe a situation where the executive re‑promulgates an ordinance multiple times or uses an ordinance to bypass a legislative veto. The correct answer follows the narrow judicial review standard: if the facts disclose mala fide or colourable use, the ordinance is invalid; otherwise, the Court will not interfere with the executive’s assessment of urgency.

Locus Standi, Public Interest Litigation and the Expansion of Access to Justice

The traditional rule of locus standi required a petitioner to demonstrate a direct, personal legal injury. The Supreme Court relaxed this requirement in S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981), recognizing that any member of the public having sufficient interest can approach the Court for enforcement of constitutional rights. This opened the door for Public Interest Litigation (PIL). In Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) the Court entertained a letter written by a social activist as a writ petition, establishing the epistolary jurisdiction. The Court further expanded standing in Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) by allowing a group of NGOs to file a petition on behalf of women workers facing sexual harassment, leading to the formulation of the Vishaka Guidelines. The rationale is that the Constitution’s transformative vision under Articles 14, 21 and 32 demands that procedural technicalities not bar access to justice for marginalized communities. However, the Court has also set limits: in State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010) it warned against frivolous PILs and imposed costs on petitioners who misuse the jurisdiction. In principle‑fact questions, the principle may read “any person acting bona fide in public interest may file a writ petition under Article 32 for enforcement of fundamental rights”. The facts will describe a petitioner with no personal injury but a genuine concern about environmental degradation or custodial violence. The correct option will uphold the petitioner’s standing, provided the petition is not vexatious. Candidates must differentiate between bona fide public interest and “busy‑body” litigation; the latter is dismissed at the threshold.
