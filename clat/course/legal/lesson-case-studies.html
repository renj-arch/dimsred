Key Concepts

Legal Reasoning Case Studies — Application of Principles

CLAT Legal Reasoning case studies present a legal principle followed by a factual scenario. You must apply the principle strictly to the facts and choose the correct legal consequence — personal legal knowledge must not override the given principle.

  • Structure of a case-study question:
    • Principle — a rule of law stated clearly.
    • Facts — a scenario with parties, actions, and consequences.
    • Question — usually 'Decide' or 'Is X liable?' with four options.
  • Step-by-step method:
    1. Read the principle first — underline its elements/conditions and any exceptions.
    2. Read the facts — identify who did what, to whom, and with what result.
    3. Match facts to principle elements — does each element exist in the facts?
    4. Apply strictly — if an element is missing, the principle does not apply.
    5. Choose the option consistent with the principle's application, even if real law differs.
  • Golden rule: Apply ONLY the given principle. Do not bring in outside legal knowledge unless the principle itself refers to it.
  • Answer option types:
    • 'X is liable' / 'X is not liable'
    • 'X committed offence Y' / 'X did not commit offence Y'
    • 'The contract is valid/void/voidable'
    • 'The fundamental right is violated/not violated'

Illustrative Cases

Case 1 — Negligence
Principle: 'Whoever, by doing any rash or negligent act, causes death to any person, is guilty of causing death by negligence.' Facts: A was driving his car within the speed limit on a clear road. A child suddenly ran across the road from behind a parked car. A applied brakes immediately but could not stop in time and the child died. Decide.
Answer: A is NOT guilty. The principle requires a rash or negligent act. Driving within speed limit with brakes applied immediately when a child suddenly darted out is neither rash nor negligent — no breach of duty of care. Liability does not arise merely because death occurred.
Case 2 — Volenti non fit injuria
Principle: 'To a willing person, no injury is done' — a person who voluntarily consented to a risk cannot claim injury arising from that risk. Facts: P bought a ticket to watch a cricket match and sat behind the stumps. A batsman hit a six that struck P on the head, injuring him. P sued the stadium for negligence. Decide.
Answer: P is not entitled to damages. By purchasing a ticket and choosing to sit behind the stumps, P voluntarily assumed the known risk of being hit by a cricket ball — volenti non fit injuria applies.
Case 3 — Offer & Acceptance
Principle: 'A contract is formed when there is a lawful offer by one party and an unconditional acceptance of that offer by the other, communicated to the offeror.' Facts: A wrote to B: 'I will sell my car for ₹5 lakh.' B replied: 'I accept, but only if you include free insurance for one year.' Decide.
Answer: No contract is formed. B's reply is not an unconditional acceptance — it adds a new condition (free insurance), which amounts to a counter-offer, not acceptance. A's original offer was not accepted as made.
Case 4 — Article 21
Principle: 'Article 21 guarantees that no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. The right to life has been held to include the right to live with human dignity.' Facts: A state law permits detention of persons suspected of smuggling without trial for up to 12 months. X is detained under this law for 10 months without trial. X challenges the detention. Decide.
Answer: X's detention violates Article 21 — deprivation of personal liberty must be according to procedure established by law, and the right to life includes dignity; prolonged detention without trial under such a law raises serious Article 21 concerns (consistent with the expansive interpretation of Article 21).

Common Traps

  • Importing real law instead of applying the given principle — the most common error.
  • Missing an element of the principle (e.g., negligence requires duty+breach+causation+damage; all must be present).
  • Assuming intention/negligence where facts show a pure accident.
  • Treating a counter-offer as acceptance.
  • Choosing the morally desirable outcome rather than the principle-consistent outcome.

Memory Tricks

  • Principle → Facts → Match → Apply → Conclude.
  • Valid contract: Offer + unconditional acceptance + consideration + capacity + free consent + legal object.
  • Negligence: Duty → Breach → Causation → Damage (all four required).
  • Volenti: Voluntary assumption of risk = defence.
Legal Reasoning exam strategy
Read the principle twice before touching the facts. Underline every condition in the principle. For each answer option, check whether applying the principle strictly leads to that result. Eliminate options based on outside law or moral preference.
