Key Concepts

Legal Maxims & Terms for CLAT

Legal maxims are established Latin principles used in judgments and legal reasoning. CLAT legal-maxims questions test meaning and application in legal reasoning passages.

  • How maxims appear: A maxim may be given in the principle, or a question may ask the meaning of a maxim used in a passage.
  • Application: Understand the maxim's spirit, then apply it to the factual scenario just like any legal principle.

Common Legal Maxims

  • Audi alteram partem: Hear the other side — no one should be condemned unheard (principle of natural justice).
  • Nemo judex in causa sua: No one should be a judge in their own case — rule against bias.
  • Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea: An act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty — criminal liability requires actus reus + mens rea.
  • Res ipsa loquitur: The thing speaks for itself — negligence may be inferred from the circumstances (e.g., surgery sponge left in patient).
  • Volenti non fit injuria: To a willing person, no injury is done — voluntary assumption of risk is a defence.
  • Ignorantia juris non excusat: Ignorance of law is no excuse.
  • Caveat emptor: Let the buyer beware — buyer must examine goods before purchase (subject to exceptions).
  • Res judicata: Matter already judged — a matter finally decided by a competent court cannot be re-litigated between the same parties.
  • Stare decisis: To stand by things decided — courts follow binding precedents (ratio decidendi).
  • Ratio decidendi: The reason for deciding — the binding legal principle in a judgment (as opposed to obiter dictum).
  • Obiter dictum: A remark made in passing — not binding precedent.
  • Mens rea: Guilty mind — mental element of a crime.
  • Actus reus: Guilty act — the physical element of a crime.
  • Bona fide: In good faith.
  • Malafide: In bad faith.
  • Caveat venditor: Let the seller beware — modern consumer-protection counterpoint to caveat emptor.
  • Injuria sine damno: Legal injury without actual damage — actionable (e.g., trespass).
  • Damnum sine injuria: Damage without legal injury — not actionable (e.g., lawful competition causing loss).
  • Ex post facto law: Law applying retrospectively — generally prohibited for criminal offences (Article 20(1)).
  • Pro bono publico: For the public good — free legal service for public welfare.
  • Ad hoc: For a specific purpose only.
  • Status quo: Existing state of affairs — courts may order status quo to maintain position pending decision.
  • Prima facie: At first sight / on the face of it — sufficient evidence to proceed unless disproved.
  • Inter alia: Among other things.
  • Per curiam: By the court as a whole (unsigned opinion).
  • Sua sponte: Of its own accord — court acts on its own motion.
  • Nunc pro tunc: Now for then — retrospective effect.
  • Lex loci: Law of the place.
  • Pacta sunt servanda: Agreements must be kept — foundation of contract law.
  • Uberrima fides: Utmost good faith — insurance contracts require full disclosure.
  • Respondeat superior: Let the master answer — master liable for servant's acts (vicarious liability).
  • Qui facit per alium facit per se: He who acts through another acts himself — basis of vicarious liability.
  • De facto: In practice/in fact.
  • De jure: By law/legally.
Technique: Maxim in a passage
Passage mentions 'Audi alteram partem' in the context of a disciplinary hearing where the student was not heard. Question: Was the hearing valid?
Answer: No — audi alteram partem requires hearing the other side; not hearing the student violates natural justice, so the hearing is invalid.
Technique: Damnum sine injuria vs Injuria sine damno
A school opens next to an existing school, causing the existing school financial loss. Students leave. Is the existing school entitled to damages?
Answer: No — damnum sine injuria: financial loss without violation of a legal right is not actionable (lawful competition).

Common Traps

  • Confusing similar maxims (res ipsa loquitur vs res judicata; actus reus vs mens rea).
  • Applying a maxim where facts don't meet its conditions (e.g., volenti requires voluntary assumption of risk).
  • Using real law to override a maxim's clear meaning in a given principle.
  • Forgetting caveat emptor exceptions (fraud, concealed defects).

Memory Tricks

  • Natural justice twin: Audi alteram partem (hear both sides) + Nemo judex (no bias) = fair hearing.
  • Criminal twin: Actus reus (act) + Mens rea (mind).
  • Buyer/Seller beware: Caveat emptor (old rule) vs Caveat venditor (consumer protection era).
  • No damage vs no injury: Damnum sine injuria = loss without legal wrong (not actionable); Injuria sine damno = legal wrong without loss (actionable, e.g., trespass).
Legal maxims exam strategy
Learn maxims with their one-line English meaning and one example situation. In passage questions, identify which maxim is being invoked and apply it to the facts like any legal principle.
