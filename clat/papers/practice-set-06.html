A. 4% decrease
B. 4% increase
C. 10% increase
D. No change
Correct Answer: B
Shortcut: Use the successive percentage change formula: Net % = a + b + (a×b)/100. Here a = +30, b = -20. Net % = 30 - 20 + (30×-20)/100 = 10 - 6 = 4% increase. Step-by-step: Assume initial price = ₹100. After 30% increase, price = ₹130. After 20% decrease, price = 130 × 0.80 = ₹104. Net change = ₹4 on ₹100 = 4% increase.
Q3. Legal Reasoningmodel-generated · 2026-11-19
Principle: Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 embodies the doctrine of lis pendens: during the pendency of a suit relating to immovable property, the property cannot be transferred so as to affect the rights of the parties to the suit; any such transfer is subject to the decree passed in the suit. Fact: X files a suit for declaration of title over a residential house. While the suit is pending, X sells the house to Y by a registered sale deed. Y was aware of the pending litigation. The court eventually decrees the suit in favour of X. What is the effect of the sale to Y?
A. The sale deed is void ab initio and Y acquires no title.
B. The sale deed is valid and Y gets a clear title unaffected by the decree.
C. The sale deed is valid but Y’s rights are subject to the final decree in the suit; Y is bound by the outcome.
D. The sale deed is voidable at the option of X after the decree.
Correct Answer: C
Shortcut: Lis pendens does not invalidate the transfer; it merely makes the transferee’s rights subject to the court’s decision. Step‑by‑step: (1) A suit concerning title is pending. (2) X transfers the property to Y during pendency. (3) Section 52 provides that such a transfer is subject to the decree. (4) Since the decree favoured X, Y’s title is bound by that outcome; the sale is not void but Y cannot claim a title superior to the decree.

Q6. Legal Reasoningmodel-generated · 2026-11-19
Principle: Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who is, or who by reason of a mistake of fact and not by reason of a mistake of law in good faith believes himself to be, bound by law to do it. (Section 76 IPC / Section 14 BNS) Fact: A, a police officer, arrests B pursuant to a warrant issued by a Magistrate. Unknown to A, the warrant was forged by C and was not actually issued by the Court. A believed the warrant to be genuine and acted in good faith. B sues A for false imprisonment.
A. A is liable for false imprisonment because the warrant was fake and conferred no legal authority.
B. A is not liable as he acted under a mistake of fact in good faith, believing himself bound by law to execute the warrant.
C. A is liable because a mistake regarding the validity of a legal document is a mistake of law, which is not excusable.
D. A is liable for negligence in failing to verify the authenticity of the warrant with the Court before arrest.
Correct Answer: B
Shortcut: Mistake of Fact = Valid Defence (Sec 76/14); Mistake of Law = No Defence. Here, A's belief about the *existence/validity of the warrant* (a fact) was mistaken, not the law authorizing arrest. Worked Solution: The principle protects acts done under a mistake of fact in good faith. A mistakenly believed the warrant was genuine (a factual error regarding the document's authenticity), not that the law allowed arrest without a warrant (a legal error). Since he acted in good faith under this factual belief, he is protected. Option C incorrectly categorizes it as mistake of law. Option D introduces negligence, which is irrelevant to the statutory defence under Sec 76/14.

B. All chefs are botanists.
C. No artists are chefs.
D. Some botanists are not artists.
Correct Answer: C
Shortcut: Use Venn diagram elimination. From (1) botanists ∩ chefs = Ø. From (3) artists ⊆ botanists, so artists are also disjoint from chefs. Hence "No artists are chefs" follows directly. Full solution: (1) says Botanist ∩ Chef = ∅. (3) says Artist ⊆ Botanist, therefore Artist ∩ Chef ⊆ Botanist ∩ Chef = ∅, giving Artist ∩ Chef = ∅, i.e., no artist is a chef. Option C expresses this. The other options cannot be derived: (2) would require Chef ⊆ Botanist which is not given; (1) is weaker than the derived statement; (4) contradicts (3) which says all artists are botanists.
