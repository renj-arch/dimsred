Particle Physics — The Standard Model, Quarks, Leptons, Gauge Bosons & Feynman Diagrams

Particle physics asks what matter is made of and how its constituents interact at the smallest distances. The Standard Model answers most of these questions with a remarkably compact framework: twelve fermions, four gauge bosons, the Higgs boson and a set of symmetries that dictate their couplings.

“The Standard Model is the most successful scientific theory ever constructed — and we know it is not the final word.”
A summary of the modern particle-physics programme
Part 1

The Standard Model and Fundamental Particles

Matter Particles
Quarks, leptons and three generations

Fermions (spin-½ particles) are divided into quarks and leptons, each arranged in three generations. The up-type quarks are up (u), charm (c) and top (t), with electric charge +2/3; the down-type quarks are down (d), strange (s) and bottom (b), with charge −1/3. Quarks carry colour charge and are never observed in isolation because of confinement. Leptons comprise the electron (e), muon (μ) and tau (τ), each with a corresponding neutrino (νe, νμ, ντ).

Each fermion has an antiparticle with opposite quantum numbers. The Higgs boson (spin 0) completes the particle content and is responsible for the masses of the W and Z bosons and, through Yukawa couplings, the charged fermions. Neutrinos acquire mass through a mechanism that is still under investigation, most likely involving right-handed sterile states or a seesaw mechanism.

Part 2

Gauge Bosons and the Fundamental Forces

Forces
Photon, W and Z, gluons — and the missing graviton

Interactions are mediated by gauge bosons. The photon (γ) mediates electromagnetism, the W⁺, W⁻ and Z⁰ mediate the weak force, and eight gluons mediate the strong force. The gluons carry colour charge themselves, which makes QCD a non-Abelian theory with asymptotic freedom at short distances and confinement at long distances.

The electroweak theory unifies electromagnetism and the weak interaction under SU(2)L × U(1)Y. Spontaneous breaking by the Higgs field leaves the photon massless and gives the W and Z their large masses (about 80.4 GeV and 91.2 GeV, respectively). Gravity is not included in the Standard Model; a consistent quantum theory of gravity remains an open problem, although gravitational waves have been observed and are described by classical general relativity.

Part 3

Feynman Diagrams and Scattering Amplitudes

Calculations
From vertices to cross sections

Feynman diagrams are a compact bookkeeping device for perturbation theory. Each external fermion line carries a spinor, each internal fermion line a propagator, each photon or gluon line a polarisation sum, and each vertex a coupling. Momentum is conserved at every vertex. The amplitude for a process is the sum of all diagrams at a given order in the coupling.

For electron–muon scattering, the leading QED diagram is the exchange of a virtual photon. The squared amplitude, averaged over initial spins and summed over final spins, gives the differential cross section. For the related annihilation process e⁺e⁻ → μ⁺μ⁻, the massless limit gives

dσ/dΩ ≈ α²/(4s) (1 + cos²θ)

for e⁺e⁻ → μ⁺μ⁻ (with α the fine-structure constant and s the centre-of-mass energy squared). At higher orders, loops and real emissions must be included; their infrared divergences cancel between virtual and real contributions, while ultraviolet divergences are absorbed by renormalization.

Part 4

CP Violation, Neutrino Oscillations and Beyond the Standard Model

Open Questions
Where the Standard Model ends

The weak interaction violates parity maximally and also violates CP symmetry in a small but measurable way. CP violation was first observed in neutral kaon decays and is described in the Standard Model by a complex phase in the CKM quark-mixing matrix. It is one of the ingredients needed to explain the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe, although the Standard Model alone appears insufficient.

Neutrino oscillations prove that neutrinos have mass and that the three flavour states are superpositions of mass eigenstates, described by the PMNS matrix. Oscillation probabilities depend on mass-squared differences and mixing angles, and the observed pattern points to physics beyond the minimal Standard Model.

Other open problems include the nature of dark matter, the origin of dark energy, the hierarchy problem, and the absence of a quantum description of gravity. Searches at the LHC and in cosmological and astroparticle experiments test supersymmetry, extra dimensions, axions, weakly interacting massive particles and other candidate extensions. So far, the Standard Model has survived every precision test, which makes the search for its breakdown both difficult and compelling.

Part 5: The Mechanisms of Mass Generation and the Hierarchy Problem

The Standard Model treats mass generation as a consequence of spontaneous symmetry breaking via the Higgs mechanism, but the origin of the fermion masses themselves remains an empirical mystery. While the gauge boson masses (m_W ≈ 80.4 GeV, m_Z ≈ 91.2 GeV) are directly proportional to the vacuum expectation value (VEV) of the Higgs field (v ≈ 246 GeV), fermion masses arise from Yukawa couplings (y_f). The relationship is m_f = y_f v / √(2). This implies a staggering range in coupling strengths: the top quark has y_t ≈ 1, while the electron has y_e ≈ 2.9 × 10-6.

A critical advanced concept is the Hierarchy Problem. The Higgs boson mass (m_H ≈ 125 GeV) is not protected by symmetry like the photon or gluon masses are; it receives quadratic corrections from loop diagrams involving heavy particles (like the top quark). In a theory with a cutoff scale Λ (e.g., the Planck scale, MPl ≈ 1.2 × 1019 GeV), the bare mass must be fine-tuned to an extraordinary precision (10-34) to yield the observed 125 GeV. This unnatural fine-tuning suggests new physics (such as Supersymmetry or Composite Higgs models) exists near the TeV scale, a prediction that has so far evaded discovery at the LHC.

Furthermore, one must distinguish between chiral symmetry and mass generation. In the limit of zero fermion mass, the QCD Lagrangian possesses chiral symmetry (SU(2)_L × SU(2)_R). The breaking of this symmetry (both explicit via quark masses and spontaneous via the QCD condensate ⟨ qq ⟩) generates most of the proton mass. Crucially, m_p ≫ m_q, meaning over 99% of visible matter's mass comes from the strong interaction energy (E=mc2), not the Higgs mechanism.

Technique: Estimating Yukawa Couplings and Mass Scales
Question: The top quark mass is approximately 173 GeV, while the electron mass is 0.511 MeV. Assuming the Standard Model's Higgs mechanism applies to both, calculate the ratio of their Yukawa coupling constants (y_t / y_e). Explain why the top quark decays before hadronizing, whereas the electron does not.
Solution:
Using m_f = y_f v / √(2), the ratio of couplings is simply the ratio of their masses:
(y_t)/(y_e) = (m_t)/(m_e) = (173 × 109 eV)/(0.511 × 106 eV) ≈ 3.4 × 105
The top quark has a lifetime τ_t ≈ 5 × 10-25 s, determined by its weak decay width Γ_t ∝ G_F m_t3. This is shorter than the timescale for hadronization (∼ 10-24 s). Consequently, the top quark decays via t → W+ b before it can form a bound state (toponium), making its properties accessible as a free particle. The electron is stable because there are no lighter charged leptons for it to decay into while conserving charge and lepton number.
Exam-Day Strategy: Handling "Beyond the Standard Model" Questions
If a question asks about physics "beyond" the Standard Model (BSM), do not invent a theory. Instead, identify the specific experimental anomaly or theoretical tension mentioned in the prompt (e.g., neutrino mass, dark matter). State clearly that the minimal SM fails here, then describe the general class of BSM solutions (e.g., "This suggests either non-zero neutrino masses via the seesaw mechanism or new gauge bosons like Z'"). Always link back to experimental observables like cross-section deviations or missing transverse energy at colliders.

Part 6: Precision Tests, Radiative Corrections, and the Running of Couplings

The Standard Model is a renormalizable quantum field theory, meaning its predictions are finite after absorbing infinities into redefined parameters. However, precision tests at facilities like LEP and SLC rely heavily on radiative corrections. The most famous example is the W boson mass prediction. At tree level (lowest order), m_W = m_Z cos θ_W. Loop diagrams involving top quarks and Higgs bosons shift this relationship. The deviation depends on Δ r, a correction term sensitive to the top quark mass (m_t) and Higgs mass (m_H). Before the direct discovery of the top quark, precision measurements of Z-pole observables allowed physicists to predict m_t ≈ 175 GeV with remarkable accuracy.

A related advanced topic is the running of coupling constants. Unlike Newtonian gravity or classical electromagnetism where forces are constant at a given distance, in QFT, the effective strength of interactions depends on the energy scale (Q2) of the probe. In Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD), the strong coupling α_s(Q2) decreases as Q2 increases (asymptotic freedom). This allows perturbation theory to work at high energies (e.g., deep inelastic scattering). Conversely, at low energies, α_s becomes large, leading to confinement and non-perturbative phenomena like hadronization. The QED coupling α(Q2) increases logarithmically with energy, though slowly.

In contrast, the weak interaction strength is fixed by the W/Z propagators; at low energies (E ≪ m_W), it appears as a four-fermion contact interaction with Fermi constant G_F. The unification of these couplings at very high energies (Grand Unification) suggests that α1, α2, α3 might converge, but supersymmetry is usually required for exact meeting points.

Technique: Analyzing Threshold Behavior in Scattering
Question: Consider the process e+ e- → W+ W-. Why does this cross-section vanish as √(s) → 2m_W (the production threshold) if one considers only the photon and Z exchange diagrams? How does the inclusion of the Higgs boson contribution (or specific gauge cancellations in non-Abelian theories) ensure unitarity is preserved at high energies?
Solution:
At threshold, the phase space volume shrinks to zero, causing the cross-section to vanish naturally (σ ∝ β3 where β = v/c). However, the deeper issue is unitarity. At high energies (s ≫ m_W2), longitudinal W boson scattering amplitudes grow with energy (A ∼ E/m_W), violating unitarity bounds. In the SM, this is cancelled by contributions from the Higgs boson exchange and specific gauge cancellations between photon/Z diagrams. If the Higgs were absent or very heavy, the amplitude would grow indefinitely, signaling a breakdown of the theory. This cancellation mechanism is a hallmark of spontaneous symmetry breaking in non-Abelian gauge theories.
Exam-Day Strategy: Distinguishing Tree vs. Loop Level
When solving problems involving cross-sections or decay widths, explicitly state the order of approximation (Tree-level vs. Next-to-Leading Order). If a question asks about "precision," assume radiative corrections are relevant. Remember that loop diagrams introduce new kinematic variables and often require renormalization scale dependence (μ_R), which is a common source of error in advanced calculations.

Part 7: Anomalies, Global Symmetries, and the Structure of the Effective Field Theory

The Standard Model is not just a collection of particles but a specific realization of gauge symmetry SU(3)_C × SU(2)_L × U(1)_Y. A profound aspect of this structure is anomaly cancellation. Quantum loops can break classical symmetries (chiral anomalies). For the theory to be consistent, the sum of anomaly coefficients over all fermion generations must vanish. This condition uniquely dictates the number of colors (N_c=3) and the specific hypercharge assignments for quarks and leptons. If there were a fourth generation of chiral fermions with different quantum numbers, it would spoil this cancellation unless they formed complete vector-like pairs.

We must also distinguish between exact, approximate, and anomalous symmetries. Baryon number (B) and Lepton number (L) are accidental global symmetries in the renormalizable SM Lagrangian but are violated by non-perturbative sphaleron processes (instantons), which change B+L while conserving B-L. This has implications for baryogenesis. In contrast, color charge is an exact local symmetry; any violation would destroy confinement.

Finally, the concept of Effective Field Theory (EFT) is crucial for modern phenomenology. We treat the SM as a low-energy effective theory valid up to some scale Λ. Operators violating B or L (like dimension-5 Weinberg operator for neutrino mass) are suppressed by powers of 1/Λ. This framework allows physicists to systematically search for BSM physics without specifying the exact high-energy model, simply by looking for deviations in precision observables.
