Particle Physics — The Standard Model, Quarks, Leptons, Gauge Bosons & Feynman Diagrams

Particle physics asks what matter is made of and how its constituents interact at the smallest distances. The Standard Model answers most of these questions with a remarkably compact framework: twelve fermions, four gauge bosons, the Higgs boson and a set of symmetries that dictate their couplings.

“The Standard Model is the most successful scientific theory ever constructed — and we know it is not the final word.”
A summary of the modern particle-physics programme
Part 1

The Standard Model and Fundamental Particles

Matter Particles
Quarks, leptons and three generations

Fermions (spin-½ particles) are divided into quarks and leptons, each arranged in three generations. The up-type quarks are up (u), charm (c) and top (t), with electric charge +2/3; the down-type quarks are down (d), strange (s) and bottom (b), with charge −1/3. Quarks carry colour charge and are never observed in isolation because of confinement. Leptons comprise the electron (e), muon (μ) and tau (τ), each with a corresponding neutrino (νe, νμ, ντ).

Each fermion has an antiparticle with opposite quantum numbers. The Higgs boson (spin 0) completes the particle content and is responsible for the masses of the W and Z bosons and, through Yukawa couplings, the charged fermions. Neutrinos acquire mass through a mechanism that is still under investigation, most likely involving right-handed sterile states or a seesaw mechanism.

Part 2

Gauge Bosons and the Fundamental Forces

Forces
Photon, W and Z, gluons — and the missing graviton

Interactions are mediated by gauge bosons. The photon (γ) mediates electromagnetism, the W⁺, W⁻ and Z⁰ mediate the weak force, and eight gluons mediate the strong force. The gluons carry colour charge themselves, which makes QCD a non-Abelian theory with asymptotic freedom at short distances and confinement at long distances.

The electroweak theory unifies electromagnetism and the weak interaction under SU(2)L × U(1)Y. Spontaneous breaking by the Higgs field leaves the photon massless and gives the W and Z their large masses (about 80.4 GeV and 91.2 GeV, respectively). Gravity is not included in the Standard Model; a consistent quantum theory of gravity remains an open problem, although gravitational waves have been observed and are described by classical general relativity.

Part 3

Feynman Diagrams and Scattering Amplitudes

Calculations
From vertices to cross sections

Feynman diagrams are a compact bookkeeping device for perturbation theory. Each external fermion line carries a spinor, each internal fermion line a propagator, each photon or gluon line a polarisation sum, and each vertex a coupling. Momentum is conserved at every vertex. The amplitude for a process is the sum of all diagrams at a given order in the coupling.

For electron–muon scattering, the leading QED diagram is the exchange of a virtual photon. The squared amplitude, averaged over initial spins and summed over final spins, gives the differential cross section. For the related annihilation process e⁺e⁻ → μ⁺μ⁻, the massless limit gives

dσ/dΩ ≈ α²/(4s) (1 + cos²θ)

for e⁺e⁻ → μ⁺μ⁻ (with α the fine-structure constant and s the centre-of-mass energy squared). At higher orders, loops and real emissions must be included; their infrared divergences cancel between virtual and real contributions, while ultraviolet divergences are absorbed by renormalization.

Part 4

CP Violation, Neutrino Oscillations and Beyond the Standard Model

Open Questions
Where the Standard Model ends

The weak interaction violates parity maximally and also violates CP symmetry in a small but measurable way. CP violation was first observed in neutral kaon decays and is described in the Standard Model by a complex phase in the CKM quark-mixing matrix. It is one of the ingredients needed to explain the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe, although the Standard Model alone appears insufficient.

Neutrino oscillations prove that neutrinos have mass and that the three flavour states are superpositions of mass eigenstates, described by the PMNS matrix. Oscillation probabilities depend on mass-squared differences and mixing angles, and the observed pattern points to physics beyond the minimal Standard Model.

Other open problems include the nature of dark matter, the origin of dark energy, the hierarchy problem, and the absence of a quantum description of gravity. Searches at the LHC and in cosmological and astroparticle experiments test supersymmetry, extra dimensions, axions, weakly interacting massive particles and other candidate extensions. So far, the Standard Model has survived every precision test, which makes the search for its breakdown both difficult and compelling.

Electroweak Symmetry Breaking and the Higgs Mechanism

The electroweak sector of the Standard Model is based on the gauge group SU(2)L × U(1)Y. Before symmetry breaking the four gauge bosons — W1, W2, W3 of SU(2)L and B of U(1)Y — are massless. A complex scalar doublet Φ with hypercharge Y = 1/2 acquires a vacuum expectation value (vev) v ≈ 246 GeV through a potential V(Φ) = μ2Φ†Φ + λ(Φ†Φ)2 with μ2 < 0. The vev breaks SU(2)L × U(1)Y → U(1)em, leaving the photon massless while giving masses to the W and Z bosons: MW = ½ g v, MZ = ½ √(g2+g′2) v. The ratio MW/MZ = cos θW defines the weak mixing angle. Three Goldstone bosons are absorbed as the longitudinal polarizations of the massive gauge bosons; the remaining scalar degree of freedom is the physical Higgs boson with mass mh = √(2λ) v. Yukawa couplings Yf Φ ψ̄L ψR generate fermion masses mf = Yf v/√2 after Φ → (0, v/√2)T. The Higgs self‑coupling λ is directly probed by double‑Higgs production, a key target for future colliders. Precision measurements of the W mass, the effective weak mixing angle sin2θeff, and the Higgs signal strengths constrain possible deviations from the minimal Higgs sector, such as additional scalar doublets or higher‑dimensional operators.

Renormalization Group, Running Couplings, and Asymptotic Freedom

Quantum corrections modify the effective strength of each gauge interaction as a function of the momentum transfer Q. The renormalization group equation (RGE) for a gauge coupling g(μ) reads μ dg/dμ = β(g) = −(b0/16π2) g3 + …, where the one‑loop coefficient b0 = (11/3) CA − (4/3) TR nf for a non‑Abelian gauge theory with nf fermion flavours. For QCD, CA = 3, TR = ½, giving b0 = 11 − 2/3 nf. With nf ≤ 16 the coefficient is positive, so β(g) < 0 and the coupling decreases at high momentum — asymptotic freedom. Conversely, the U(1)Y hypercharge coupling has b0 = −(4/3) nf − (1/10) ns (ns scalar doublets), leading to a Landau pole at very high scales. The electroweak couplings g and g′ run logarithmically; their unification near 1013 GeV is a hint for grand unified theories. At two loops the β‑function includes gauge‑boson self‑interactions and Yukawa contributions, notably the top‑quark Yukawa yt which drives the Higgs quartic λ towards negative values at high scales, raising the stability question of the electroweak vacuum. The MS‑bar scheme is the standard subtraction scheme for collider phenomenology; matching conditions at heavy‑particle thresholds (e.g., mt, MZ) ensure continuity of the running couplings across flavour boundaries.

Dimensional Regularization and the One‑Loop QCD β‑Function
Compute the one‑loop coefficient b0 of the QCD β‑function for nf active quark flavours using dimensional regularization in the MS‑bar scheme.
In d = 4 − 2ε dimensions the gluon self‑energy receives contributions from the gluon loop, ghost loop, and quark loop. The gluon loop yields (5/3) CA, the ghost loop −(1/3) CA, and each quark flavour contributes −(4/3) TR. Summing gives b0 = (11/3) CA − (4/3) TR nf. For SU(3) with CA = 3 and TR = ½, b0 = 11 − 2/3 nf. This positive coefficient for nf ≤ 16 confirms asymptotic freedom.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the sign of the β‑function, remember that a negative β (positive b0) means the coupling decreases with energy. Write the one‑loop formula explicitly and substitute the group invariants; this avoids sign errors and earns partial credit even if the final numeric value is mis‑computed.

Precision Electroweak Observables and Global Fits

High‑precision measurements at LEP, SLC, and the LHC provide stringent tests of the Standard Model through a set of observables collectively known as the electroweak precision data. The most influential quantities include the Z‑boson mass MZ = 91.1876 GeV, the total Z width ΓZ = 2.4952 GeV, the hadronic peak cross section σhad0, the ratios Rℓ = Γhad/Γℓ, the forward‑backward asymmetries AFB0,ℓ, the τ polarization, and the left‑right asymmetry ALR measured by SLD. The W‑boson mass MW = 80.379 GeV and the effective weak mixing angle sin2θeffℓ = 0.23153 are extracted from these data. A global fit using the SM parameters {α(MZ), GF, MZ, mt, mh, αs(MZ)} yields a χ2/dof ≈ 1.0, indicating excellent consistency. The oblique parameters S, T, U (or equivalently ε1,2,3) parametrize new‑physics contributions to gauge‑boson self‑energies; current limits are |S|, |T| ≲ 0.1 at 95 % C.L. The Higgs mass prediction from the pre‑2012 fit was mh = 94 ± 25 GeV, remarkably close to the observed 125 GeV. Any deviation in future measurements of MW or sin2θeff beyond the projected experimental uncertainties would signal physics beyond the SM, such as extra Higgs doublets, supersymmetric loops, or compositeness effects.

Flavor Physics: CKM Unitarity Triangle and Rare Decays

The Cabibbo–Kobayashi–Maskawa (CKM) matrix V encodes quark flavour mixing and CP violation. Its unitarity condition VudVub* + VcdVcb* + VtdVtb* = 0 defines a triangle in the complex plane whose angles α, β, γ are measured in B‑meson decays. The sides are proportional to |VudVub*|, |VcdVcb*|, |VtdVtb*|. Precision determinations of |Vcb| and |Vub| from inclusive and exclusive semileptonic B decays currently show a ∼3σ tension, a long‑standing puzzle. The angle β is extracted from the time‑dependent CP asymmetry in B0 → J/ψ KS, giving sin 2β = 0.699 ± 0.017. Angle α comes from B → ππ, ρπ, ρρ analyses; γ is obtained from tree‑level B → DK decays using the GLW, ADS, and GGSZ methods. The unitarity triangle closure test is a powerful probe of new physics; any inconsistency would indicate non‑SM contributions to mixing or decay amplitudes. Rare flavour‑changing neutral currents such as Bs → μ+μ−, B → K* νν̄, and K+ → π+ νν̄ are loop‑suppressed in the SM and thus highly sensitive to heavy virtual particles. The measured branching ratio ℬ(Bs → μ+μ−) = (3.0 ± 0.4) × 10−9 agrees with the SM prediction (3.66 ± 0.14) × 10−9 within uncertainties. Future upgrades at LHCb and Belle II aim to reduce the experimental errors to the few‑percent level, turning these channels into precision tests of the CKM paradigm.

Neutrino Mass Generation: Seesaw Mechanisms and Majorana vs Dirac Nature

Neutrino oscillation data require at least two non‑zero mass‑squared differences, Δm212 ≈ 7.5 × 10−5 eV2 and |Δm312| ≈ 2.5 × 10−3 eV2, but they do not fix the absolute mass scale nor the Dirac/Majorana nature. The simplest extension adds three right‑handed neutrinos NR with Majorana masses MR and Yukawa couplings Yν to the Higgs doublet. After electroweak symmetry breaking the Dirac mass matrix mD = Yν v/√2 appears, and the full 6 × 6 mass matrix in the (νL, NRc) basis is (0, mD; mDT, MR). For MR ≫ mD the light neutrino masses are mν ≈ −mD MR−1 mDT — the type‑I seesaw. Typical GUT‑scale MR ∼ 1014 GeV yields sub‑eV neutrino masses for Yν ∼ 1. Variants include the type‑II seesaw (scalar triplet) and type‑III (fermion triplet). If neutrinos are Dirac particles, lepton number is conserved and tiny Yukawa couplings Yν ∼ 10−12 are required, which many theorists consider unnatural. The decisive experimental signature is neutrinoless double beta decay (0νββ), which violates lepton number by two units and occurs only if neutrinos are Majorana. The effective Majorana mass mββ = |∑ Uei2 mi| governs the 0νββ rate; current limits from KamLAND‑Zen, GERDA, and CUORE constrain mββ < 0.06–0.16 eV depending on nuclear matrix elements. A positive signal would simultaneously establish the Majorana nature and provide information on the absolute mass scale and the CP‑violating Majorana phases, which are inaccessible in oscillations.

  • Confusing the sign of the QCD β‑function: a positive b0 gives a negative β, meaning the coupling decreases at high momentum.
  • Assuming the Higgs vev v = 246 GeV is the Higgs mass; the physical Higgs mass is mh = √(2λ) v and depends on the quartic coupling λ.
  • Treating the CKM matrix as real; the single irreducible complex phase is the sole source of CP violation in the quark sector of the SM.
  • Neglecting threshold matching when running αs across quark masses; the number of active flavours nf changes at each heavy‑quark pole.
  • Equating the seesaw scale directly with the GUT scale without considering possible intermediate symmetries or low‑scale seesaw variants.
  • Overlooking that neutrinoless double beta decay probes the Majorana nature but its rate also depends on unknown nuclear matrix elements and the Majorana phases.
  • Misidentifying the oblique parameters S, T, U as direct measurements of new particles; they parametrize only universal gauge‑boson self‑energy corrections.
