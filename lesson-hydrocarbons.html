Hydrocarbons — Alkanes, Alkenes, Alkynes, Aromatic Hydrocarbons, Petroleum Refining, Cracking & Combustion

Deep dive into hydrocarbon chemistry — the structure, bonding, and reactivity of alkanes (saturated hydrocarbons with C–C single bonds and sp³ tetrahedral geometry), alkenes (unsaturated with C=C double bonds, π-bonding, and cis–trans isomerism), alkynes (C≡C triple bonds with sp hybridization and linear geometry), aromatic hydrocarbons (benzene and Hückel's (4n+2)π rule), petroleum refining (fractional distillation based on boiling points), cracking (thermal and catalytic cracking, reforming), and combustion chemistry (complete and incomplete combustion, knocking, octane number).

"Hydrocarbons are the simplest organic compounds — yet they form the backbone of the fuels that power civilization and the raw materials for synthetic chemistry."
— August Kekulé
Part 1

🔗 Alkanes and Alkenes: Sigma Bonds, Pi Bonds, and Isomerism

Hydrocarbon Families
Alkanes (CnH2n+2), Alkenes (CnH2n), Bonding, Nomenclature, and Geometric Isomerism
Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons with general formula CnH2n+2. All C–C and C–H bonds are σ-bonds formed by sp³–sp³ or sp³–s overlap. Each carbon is sp³ hybridized with tetrahedral geometry (109.5° bond angles). Free rotation about C–C single bonds allows conformational isomerism. Structural isomerism (chain and position) arises from different carbon skeletons. IUPAC nomenclature uses the -ane suffix. Alkanes are relatively inert (paraffins = "little affinity") — they undergo only free-radical substitution (halogenation) and combustion. Alkenes are unsaturated with at least one C=C double bond (general formula CnH2n). Each double-bond carbon is sp² hybridized (trigonal planar, 120°). The double bond consists of a strong σ-bond (sp²–sp² overlap) and a weaker π-bond (sideways p–p overlap). Restricted rotation around C=C gives rise to cis–trans (geometric) isomerism when each double-bond carbon has two different substituents. The C=C bond is electron-rich and undergoes electrophilic addition reactions (with HX, X₂, H₂O). Markovnikov's rule predicts the regiochemistry: H adds to the less substituted carbon. Baeyer's test (alkaline KMnO₄) detects unsaturation — the purple permanganate is decolorized. Alkenes also undergo polymerization (addition polymers like polyethylene).
Alkanes have only σ-bonds, making them relatively unreactive; the π-bond in alkenes provides a site for addition reactions and enables geometric isomerism due to restricted rotation.
Part 2

⚗️ Alkynes and Aromatic Hydrocarbons: Triple Bonds and the Benzene Ring

Unsaturation and Aromaticity
Alkynes (CnH2n−2), sp Hybridization, Benzene, Resonance, and Hückel's Rule
Alkynes contain at least one C≡C triple bond (general formula CnH2n−2). Each triple-bond carbon is sp hybridized (linear, 180°). The triple bond comprises one σ-bond (sp–sp) and two π-bonds (from two sets of perpendicular p orbitals). Terminal alkynes (R–C≡C–H) have acidic hydrogen (pKₐ ≈ 25) due to the high s-character of the C–H bond. Alkynes undergo addition reactions similarly to alkenes but can add two equivalents of H₂, X₂, or HX. Aromatic hydrocarbons contain a planar ring of atoms with a conjugated π-system. The prototype is benzene (C₆H₆), proposed by Kekulé (1865) as a six-membered ring with alternating single and double bonds. In reality, benzene is a resonance hybrid with equal C–C bond lengths (140 pm) intermediate between single (154 pm) and double (134 pm). The six p orbitals perpendicular to the ring overlap to form a delocalized π-system above and below the plane. This delocalization provides resonance stabilization energy (~150 kJ/mol), making benzene much more stable than the hypothetical "cyclohexatriene". Hückel's rule: a planar, cyclic, fully conjugated compound is aromatic if it contains (4n+2) π-electrons (n = 0,1,2...). Aromatic compounds undergo electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) — nitration, halogenation, sulfonation, Friedel–Crafts alkylation/acylation — rather than addition, preserving the aromatic stabilization.
Alkynes have linear sp-hybridized carbons and acidic terminal hydrogens; aromatic hydrocarbons achieve exceptional stability through delocalized π-electrons as described by Hückel's (4n+2)π rule.

🛢️ Petroleum Refining and Cracking: From Crude Oil to Fuels

Industrial Chemistry
Fractional Distillation, Thermal and Catalytic Cracking, Reforming, and Octane Rating
Crude oil (petroleum) is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons (alkanes, cycloalkanes, aromatics) formed from ancient organic matter under high pressure and temperature over millions of years. Fractional distillation separates crude oil into fractions based on boiling point in a fractionating column. The column has a temperature gradient (hot at bottom, cool at top). Lighter fractions with lower boiling points (refinery gas C₁–C₄, gasoline C₅–C₁₀, naphtha C₆–C₁₂) condense higher up; heavier fractions (kerosene C₁₁–C₁₃, diesel C₁₄–C₁₈, fuel oil C₁₉–C₂₅, asphalt >C₂₅) condense lower. Cracking breaks large hydrocarbon molecules into smaller, more valuable ones. Thermal cracking (high temperature ~700°C, high pressure) produces alkenes via free-radical mechanisms. Catalytic cracking (zeolite catalysts, ~500°C, moderate pressure) is more efficient and gives higher gasoline yield with better octane rating. The mechanism involves carbocation intermediates. Hydrocracking combines cracking with hydrogenation to produce saturated products. Reforming (catalytic reforming) converts straight-chain alkanes into branched alkanes, cycloalkanes, and aromatic compounds — all of which have higher octane numbers. The octane scale: iso-octane (2,2,4-trimethylpentane) = 100, n-heptane = 0. Branched and aromatic hydrocarbons resist knocking (auto-ignition) better than straight-chain alkanes.
Cracking transforms heavy, low-demand fractions into lighter, high-demand products, while reforming and isomerization improve fuel quality by producing branched and aromatic hydrocarbons with higher octane numbers.

🔥 Combustion and Environmental Impact: Energy, Emissions, and Knock

Energy and Environment
Complete and Incomplete Combustion, Greenhouse Gases, Octane Number, and Catalytic Converters
Combustion (burning) is the rapid exothermic reaction of a hydrocarbon with oxygen. Complete combustion (sufficient O₂): CxHy + (x + y/4)O₂ → xCO₂ + (y/2)H₂O + heat. This is highly exothermic (ΔH ≈ −890 kJ/mol for methane) — the energy that heats homes, powers engines, and generates electricity. Incomplete combustion (limited O₂) produces carbon monoxide (CO) (a poisonous gas that binds hemoglobin 200× more strongly than O₂) and carbon (soot). CO is a major air pollutant. Knocking (engine knock) occurs when the fuel–air mixture auto-ignites prematurely in the cylinder before the spark plug fires, producing damaging pressure waves. The octane number measures a fuel's resistance to knocking. Higher octane fuels allow higher compression ratios (more efficient engines). Lead compounds (tetraethyllead) were historically used as antiknock agents but have been phased out due to toxicity. Modern alternatives include oxygenates (ethanol, MTBE). Catalytic converters in vehicle exhaust systems use platinum, palladium, and rhodium catalysts to convert CO → CO₂, unburnt hydrocarbons → CO₂ + H₂O, and NOₓ → N₂. Environmental impact: CO₂ from hydrocarbon combustion is the primary greenhouse gas driving climate change. Sulfur impurities in crude oil produce SO₂ (acid rain). Cleaner fuels and emissions control technologies continue to evolve.
The exothermic combustion of hydrocarbons provides most of the world's energy, but incomplete combustion produces toxic CO and soot, while CO₂ emissions contribute to climate change — requiring both cleaner combustion and emissions control technologies.
