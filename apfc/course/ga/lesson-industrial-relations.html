Historical Evolution of Industrial Relations in India

1.1 Pre‑independence labour legislation

The earliest statutory intervention in labour matters came with the Employers and Workmen (Disputes) Act of 1860, which provided a rudimentary framework for the settlement of disputes between European employers and Indian workers in the plantation and railway sectors. Subsequent enactments such as the Trade Disputes Act of 1929 and the Payment of Wages Act of 1936 reflected the colonial government’s growing concern for industrial peace, yet they remained largely protective of British capital interests and offered limited rights to Indian labour. The absence of a comprehensive code meant that disputes were often resolved through ad‑hoc conciliation or by the intervention of the provincial governments, leading to inconsistent outcomes across regions.

World War II intensified industrial activity and brought the question of worker welfare to the fore, prompting the passage of the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act in 1946. This legislation mandated that employers in certain industries define conditions of service in standing orders certified by the government, thereby introducing a degree of uniformity in employment terms. However, the law applied only to establishments employing a hundred or more workers, leaving a vast informal workforce outside its ambit. The cumulative effect of these pre‑independence statutes was a fragmented legal landscape that recognised the need for dispute resolution but stopped short of guaranteeing collective bargaining rights.

1.2 Post‑independence consolidation

After 1947 the newly sovereign state embarked on a systematic codification of labour law, beginning with the Industrial Disputes Act of 1947, which replaced the 1929 Trade Disputes Act and extended its coverage to all industrial establishments employing ten or more workers. The Act introduced a three‑tier dispute resolution machinery — conciliation officers, boards of conciliation, and labour courts or industrial tribunals — and defined key concepts such as “industrial dispute”, “workman”, and “lay‑off”. Simultaneously, the Trade Unions Act of 1926 was retained but amended in 1947 to facilitate registration and to grant registered unions certain immunities from civil suits arising out of legitimate trade union activities.

The Constitution of India, adopted in 1950, entrenched labour welfare in the Directive Principles of State Policy, notably Article 41 (right to work), Article 42 (just and humane conditions of work) and Article 43 (living wage). These provisions gave moral and constitutional weight to subsequent legislation such as the Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act of 1952 and the Employees’ State Insurance Act of 1948, which together laid the foundations of a contributory social security system. The period up to the early 1990s therefore witnessed a gradual expansion of statutory protection, the institutionalisation of tripartite consultation through the Indian Labour Conference, and the emergence of a relatively strong public sector trade union movement.

1.3 Economic reforms and the push for flexibility

The balance‑of‑payments crisis of 1991 triggered a wave of liberalisation that reshaped industrial relations. The government introduced the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1991, which de‑licensed many industries, reduced the role of the public sector, and encouraged foreign direct investment. In response, employers demanded greater flexibility in hiring, firing, and contract labour deployment, arguing that rigid labour laws impeded competitiveness. This pressure led to a series of amendments — most notably the 1995 amendment to the Industrial Disputes Act that raised the threshold for prior government permission for lay‑off and retrenchment from 100 to 300 workers in certain states — and the proliferation of contract labour through the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act of 1970, which was increasingly used as a tool for numerical flexibility.

At the same time, the judiciary began to interpret “industry” and “workman” more restrictively, limiting the coverage of protective statutes for managerial and supervisory staff. The Supreme Court’s judgment in Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa (1978) and later in Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Management (1973) narrowed the definition of “industry”, thereby excluding many service‑sector establishments from the purview of the Industrial Disputes Act. These developments created a dual labour market: a protected core of permanent workers in large establishments and a growing periphery of contract, casual, and platform workers with minimal statutory safeguards.

Legal Framework Governing Industrial Disputes

2.1 The Industrial Disputes Act 1947 – scope and definitions

The Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 remains the cornerstone of dispute resolution in India. It defines an “industrial dispute” as any dispute or difference between employers and employers, between employers and workmen, or between workmen and workmen, which is connected with the employment or non‑employment, or the terms of employment or conditions of labour, of any person. The Act applies to every “industry” as defined in Section 2(j), a term that the Supreme Court has interpreted expansively in Bangalore Water Supply but subsequently narrowed in State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2005) to exclude certain sovereign functions. A “workman” under Section 2(s) includes any person employed in an industry to do any manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work for hire or reward, but excludes those employed mainly in a managerial or administrative capacity.

The Act also distinguishes between “lay‑off”, “retrenchment” and “closure”. Lay‑off refers to the failure, refusal or inability of an employer to give employment to a workman whose name is on the muster rolls, due to shortage of coal, power or raw material, or accumulation of stocks, or breakdown of machinery, or any other reason. Retrenchment means the termination by the employer of the service of a workman for any reason whatsoever, otherwise than as a punishment inflicted by way of disciplinary action. Closure denotes the permanent closing down of a place of employment or part thereof. Each of these concepts carries distinct procedural requirements, notice periods, and compensation formulas, which are elaborated in Sections 25‑C to 25‑F of the Act.

2.2 Conciliation, arbitration and adjudication mechanisms

The dispute resolution architecture under the Act is built on a hierarchy of voluntary and compulsory processes. At the first level, a Conciliation Officer appointed by the appropriate government attempts to bring the parties to a mutually acceptable settlement; if successful, the settlement is binding under Section 12(3). Failing conciliation, the dispute may be referred to a Board of Conciliation, a tripartite body comprising representatives of the employer, the workmen, and an independent chairman. The Board’s report, if it recommends a settlement, is again binding; otherwise the appropriate government may refer the dispute to a Labour Court, Industrial Tribunal or National Tribunal for adjudication.

Arbitration offers an alternative where both parties agree in writing to submit the dispute to an arbitrator appointed under Section 10A. The arbitrator’s award is final and binding, subject only to limited judicial review under Section 11A. The Act also empowers the appropriate government to prohibit strikes and lock‑outs in public utility services (Section 22) and to refer disputes to adjudication suo motu when it considers that the dispute threatens industrial peace. This multi‑layered system aims to encourage negotiated settlements while providing a statutory backstop that guarantees a definitive resolution.

2.3 Lay‑off, retrenchment and closure provisions

Sections 25‑C to 25‑F lay down the procedural safeguards for lay‑off, retrenchment and closure. An employer intending to lay off workmen must apply for prior permission from the appropriate government if the establishment employs 100 or more workmen (or 300 in certain states after the 1995 amendment). The government may grant or refuse permission after giving the employer and the workmen an opportunity to be heard. In the case of retrenchment, the employer must give three months’ notice or pay in lieu thereof, and must pay compensation equivalent to fifteen days’ average pay for every completed year of continuous service. Closure requires a notice of at least sixty days to the appropriate government and to the workmen, together with compensation at the same rate as retrenchment.

These provisions have been the subject of extensive litigation. In Workmen of Meenakshi Mills Ltd. v. Management (1992), the Supreme Court held that the requirement of prior permission for lay‑off is mandatory and non‑compliance renders the lay‑off illegal, entitling workmen to full wages. Conversely, in Bharat Forge Co. Ltd. v. Uttam Manohar Nakate (2005), the Court upheld the employer’s right to close an undertaking after complying with the statutory notice and compensation requirements, emphasizing that the law does not guarantee perpetual employment. The interplay between statutory protection and managerial prerogative continues to shape industrial relations jurisprudence.

Example Question
An establishment employing 250 workmen proposes to lay off 50 workers due to a prolonged power shortage. The employer fails to obtain prior permission from the appropriate government before effecting the lay‑off. Under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, what are the legal consequences for the employer and the rights of the affected workmen?
Under Section 25‑C of the Industrial Disputes Act, any establishment employing 100 or more workmen must obtain prior permission from the appropriate government before laying off workmen. Since the employer did not seek such permission, the lay‑off is illegal. Consequently, the affected workmen are entitled to full wages for the period of lay‑off as if they had been continuously employed, and the employer may also be liable for prosecution under Section 31 for contravention of the Act. The workmen can raise an industrial dispute before the Conciliation Officer, and if unresolved, the matter may be adjudicated by a Labour Court, which can order reinstatement with back wages.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks about the legality of a lay‑off or retrenchment, first identify the employee threshold (100 or 300) and whether prior permission was obtained. The absence of permission automatically renders the action illegal, triggering full wage liability — a point examiners frequently test.

Trade Unions and Collective Bargaining

3.1 Registration and recognition under the Trade Unions Act 1926

The Trade Unions Act, 1926 provides the legal framework for the registration of trade unions, conferring on them a distinct legal personality. Any seven or more workmen employed in an establishment may apply for registration by submitting a copy of the union’s rules, a list of office‑bearers, and a statement of assets and liabilities to the Registrar of Trade Unions. Upon registration, the union becomes a body corporate, capable of acquiring property, entering into contracts, and suing or being sued in its own name. However, registration alone does not guarantee recognition by the employer for collective bargaining purposes.

Recognition is governed largely by industrial practice, standing orders, and, in some states, specific recognition rules framed under the Industrial Disputes Act. The Code on Industrial Relations, 2020, which is yet to be fully notified, proposes a statutory recognition mechanism based on a verification of membership through a secret ballot, requiring a union to represent at least 51 % of the workmen in the bargaining unit. Until the Code comes into force, recognition remains a matter of negotiation, often leading to inter‑union rivalry and litigation over representational status.

3.2 Rights, immunities and obligations of registered unions

Registered trade unions enjoy several statutory immunities under Section 18 of the Trade Unions Act. They are immune from civil suits in respect of any act done in furtherance of a trade dispute, provided the act is not illegal. This immunity extends to office‑bearers and members acting in good faith. Additionally, Section 19 protects union funds from attachment in execution of a decree, except for debts incurred for the union’s lawful purposes. The Act also imposes obligations: unions must maintain proper accounts, submit annual returns to the Registrar, and hold elections for office‑bearers at intervals not exceeding three years.

Failure to comply with these obligations can result in cancellation of registration under Section 10. The Supreme Court in Indian National Trade Union Congress v. State of Maharashtra (1995) held that the right to form associations under Article 19(1)(c) of the Constitution includes the right to register a trade union, but the state may impose reasonable restrictions in the interest of public order. Thus, while the law protects legitimate union activity, it also subjects unions to regulatory oversight to prevent misuse of the immunities granted.

3.3 Collective bargaining process and recent judicial trends

Collective bargaining in India operates through a combination of statutory provisions, standing orders, and voluntary agreements. The Industrial Disputes Act empowers the appropriate government to refer a dispute relating to the terms of employment to a Board of Conciliation or adjudication, which effectively provides a forum for bargaining. In practice, many large establishments negotiate memoranda of settlement (MoS) that are registered under Section 18(1) of the Act, making them binding on the parties. The Supreme Court’s decision in Karnataka State Road Transport Corporation v. K. S. R. R. K. (2005) affirmed that a settlement arrived at through conciliation is enforceable as a decree of a civil court.

Recent judgments have reinforced the principle that recognition for bargaining must reflect genuine majority support. In Maharashtra State Road Transport Corporation v. Maharashtra State Road Transport Corporation Workers’ Union (2021), the Court upheld a secret‑ballot verification ordered by the Labour Commissioner, stating that a union claiming recognition must demonstrate majority membership at the time of the claim. This trend aligns with the forthcoming Industrial Relations Code’s emphasis on democratic verification, signalling a shift from employer‑driven recognition to a more transparent, worker‑centric process.

Social Security Architecture – Employees’ Provident Fund

4.1 Constitutional basis and the Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 1952

The Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952 (EPF Act) was enacted pursuant to the Directive Principles in Articles 41 and 43 of the Constitution, which urge the State to secure the right to work and a living wage. The Act establishes a compulsory contributory provident fund for employees in factories and other establishments employing twenty or more persons. It also provides for a pension scheme (Employees’ Pension Scheme, 1995) and an insurance scheme (Employees’ Deposit Linked Insurance Scheme, 1976). The Central Board of Trustees, a tripartite body comprising representatives of government, employers and employees, administers the fund under the overall supervision of the Ministry of Labour and Employment.

The EPF Act applies to establishments in the scheduled industries listed in the First Schedule, and the appropriate government may extend coverage to other classes of establishments by notification. Over the decades, the threshold for mandatory coverage has been lowered from fifty to twenty employees, and the wage ceiling for eligibility has been periodically revised — most recently to ₹15,000 per month in 2014. These expansions reflect a policy intent to broaden the social security net in line with the growing formal sector workforce.

4.2 Contribution rates, coverage thresholds and administration

Under the EPF Scheme, both employer and employee contribute 12 % of the employee’s basic wages, dearness allowance and retaining allowance, if any. Of the employer’s share, 8.33 % is diverted to the Employees’ Pension Scheme (subject to a wage ceiling of ₹15,000), while the remaining 3.67 % goes to the provident fund account. The employee’s entire 12 % is credited to the provident fund. The administration is carried out through a network of regional offices, each headed by a Regional Provident Fund Commissioner, and a centralised IT platform — the Unified Portal — facilitates online registration, contribution filing, and claim settlement.

Establishments with fewer than twenty employees may voluntarily opt in, and the government has introduced the “Shram Suvidha” portal to simplify compliance for micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs). The Act also provides for exemptions: an establishment may be granted an exemption from the EPF Scheme if it operates a provident fund trust that offers benefits at least equivalent to the statutory scheme, subject to approval by the Central Board of Trustees. Such exempted trusts are monitored through periodic audits and must remit contributions to the central fund for the pension and insurance components.

4.3 Withdrawal, transfer, pension options and recent digital initiatives

A member may withdraw the full provident fund accumulation on retirement after attaining the age of 58, or on permanent incapacitation. Partial withdrawals are permitted for specified purposes such as housing, medical treatment, marriage, and education, subject to eligibility criteria and documentary proof. The Employees’ Pension Scheme provides a monthly pension on superannuation, early pension (after 50 years with 10 years of service), and disability pension. The pension amount is calculated using a formula that factors in the member’s pensionable service and the average of the last 60 months’ pensionable salary.

In recent years, the EPFO has launched several digital initiatives to improve service delivery. The “UMANG” mobile app enables members to view passbooks, file claims, and track claim status. The “e‑Nomination” facility allows members to nominate beneficiaries online, eliminating the need for physical forms. Moreover, the “Auto‑Transfer” feature ensures that when a member changes employment, the provident fund balance is automatically transferred to the new account, reducing the incidence of dormant accounts. These measures aim to enhance transparency, reduce grievance redressal time, and promote portability of benefits across jobs.

Employees’ State Insurance – Health and Maternity Protection

5.1 Legislative origin and the Employees’ State Insurance Act 1948

The Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948 (ESI Act) was the first comprehensive social health insurance legislation in India, enacted to provide medical care and cash benefits to workers in the event of sickness, maternity, disablement, or death due to employment injury. The Act draws its constitutional mandate from Article 41 (right to work) and Article 42 (just and humane conditions of work). It applies to factories employing ten or more persons (or twenty in certain states) and to other establishments notified by the appropriate government, covering employees earning up to a prescribed wage ceiling — currently ₹21,000 per month.

The ESI Corporation, a statutory body constituted under Section 3 of the Act, administers the scheme. It is governed by a tripartite board comprising representatives of the central government, state governments, employers, employees, and medical professionals. The Corporation operates a network of ESI hospitals, dispensaries, and branch offices across the country, delivering both outpatient and inpatient care. The scheme is financed by contributions from employers (3.25 % of wages) and employees (0.75 % of wages), deposited into the ESI Fund, which is maintained by the Corporation.

5.2 Coverage criteria, contribution structure and benefit package

Coverage under the ESI Act is mandatory for all employees in notified establishments whose monthly wages do not exceed the ceiling. The wage ceiling has been revised periodically — from ₹15,000 in 2010 to ₹21,000 in 2017 — to keep pace with inflation and expand the beneficiary base. The contribution rates are fixed by the central government and are shared between employer and employee as mentioned. The benefit package includes sickness benefit (70 % of average daily wages for up to 91 days), extended sickness benefit (for long‑term diseases), maternity benefit (100 % of average daily wages for 26 weeks), disablement benefit (temporary and permanent), and dependants’ benefit (pension to dependants of a worker who dies due to employment injury).

Medical care is provided free of cost at ESI institutions, and the scheme also reimburses expenses incurred at recognised private hospitals for specialised treatment not available in the ESI network. The Act also provides for funeral expenses and a one‑time rehabilitation allowance for permanently disabled insured persons. The comprehensive nature of the benefits distinguishes ESI from the EPF, which is primarily a retirement savings instrument, whereas ESI addresses immediate health and income‑replacement needs.

5.3 Administrative machinery and integration with the Code on Social Security 2020

The ESI Corporation functions through a hierarchical structure: the Headquarters in New Delhi, Regional Offices headed by Regional Directors, and Sub‑Regional Offices at the district level. Each Regional Office oversees a cluster of ESI hospitals and dispensaries, and is responsible for contribution collection, benefit disbursement, and grievance redressal. The Corporation also runs the “ESI Pehchan” smart card system, which enables cashless access to medical services and portable identification across the network.

The Code on Social Security, 2020, which subsumes the ESI Act along with eight other labour laws, proposes a unified social security framework. It retains the contributory structure of ESI but introduces a “Social Security Fund” at the national level to pool resources for gig workers, platform workers, and unorganised sector workers. The Code also empowers the central government to notify “social security schemes” for new categories of workers, thereby extending health and maternity protection beyond the traditional factory workforce. While the Code has received presidential assent, its effective implementation awaits the framing of rules and the notification of specific provisions, a process that will determine the future contours of employees’ state insurance in India.

The evolution of industrial relations in India reflects a continuous negotiation between the imperatives of economic growth, the aspirations of a diverse workforce, and the constitutional commitment to social justice. Each legislative milestone — from the Industrial Disputes Act to the recent labour codes — has sought to recalibrate this balance, yet the practical outcomes depend on effective enforcement, judicial interpretation, and the capacity of tripartite institutions to adapt to emerging forms of work.

The Industrial Relations Code 2020 – Consolidation and Key Shifts

The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 received presidential assent on 28 September 2020 and subsumes three major statutes: the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947; the Trade Unions Act, 1926; and the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946. The Code aims to simplify compliance by providing a single legislative framework for dispute resolution, union registration, and standing orders. A notable shift is the introduction of fixed‑term employment (Section 2(o)), which grants fixed‑term workers the same statutory benefits as permanent workers, including gratuity on a pro‑rata basis, while allowing employers to hire for a defined period without the rigours of retrenchment procedures.

The threshold for mandatory standing orders has been raised from 100 to 300 workers (Section 29), reducing the regulatory burden on medium‑sized establishments. The Code also revises the notice period for retrenchment from one month to three months (Section 42) and enhances retrenchment compensation to 15 days’ average pay for every completed year of service (Section 43), aligning it with the Supreme Court’s interpretation in Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Management (1973). The provision for a “negotiating union” (Section 14) replaces the earlier “recognised union” concept, requiring a union to represent at least 51 % of workers on the muster roll to be the sole bargaining agent; if no union meets this threshold, a negotiating council of unions representing at least 20 % each is constituted.

Dispute resolution mechanisms are streamlined: conciliation officers are retained, but the Code empowers the appropriate government to refer disputes directly to Industrial Tribunals (Section 44) without mandatory conciliation in certain cases, such as disputes involving a “public utility service” as defined in Section 2(n). The Code also introduces a “re‑employment” obligation for employers who close an establishment without following the prescribed 60‑day notice to the government (Section 45), a provision absent in the 1947 Act. While the Code has been passed, its operationalisation awaits the framing of central and state rules; until then, the pre‑Code statutes continue to apply.

Adjudication Hierarchy – Labour Courts, Tribunals and the National Industrial Tribunal

The Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (now largely mirrored in the Industrial Relations Code) establishes a three‑tier adjudicatory structure. Labour Courts (Section 7) are constituted by state governments for disputes specified in the Second Schedule, such as matters relating to the propriety of standing orders, interpretation of awards, and claims for unpaid wages. Industrial Tribunals (Section 7A) handle disputes in the Third Schedule, including wages, allowances, hours of work, and retrenchment. The National Industrial Tribunal (Section 7B) is constituted by the central government for disputes of national importance or those affecting multiple states.

Appeals from Labour Courts and Industrial Tribunals lie to the respective High Courts under Article 226/227 of the Constitution, and further to the Supreme Court under Article 136 (special leave). The Supreme Court has repeatedly clarified the scope of “industry” under Section 2(j). In Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa (1978) 2 SCC 213, a seven‑judge bench held that any systematic activity carried on by cooperation between employer and employees for the production or distribution of goods or services constitutes an industry, irrespective of profit motive. This expansive definition brings hospitals, educational institutions, and charitable trusts within the Act’s ambit.

Recent jurisprudence has refined retrenchment compensation. In State of Karnataka v. Umadevi (2006) 4 SCC 1, the Court ruled that “continuous service” for retrenchment compensation includes periods of temporary or casual employment if the employee has worked for 240 days in the preceding 12 months. The 2022 decision in M/s. K. S. R. T. C. v. Workmen (2022) 5 SCC 341 reiterated that the employer must prove “operational necessity” for retrenchment and cannot rely solely on financial loss. The National Industrial Tribunal, though rarely invoked, was activated in the 2020‑21 coal sector dispute, demonstrating its role in inter‑state industrial conflicts.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Under the Industrial Disputes Act, which forum has original jurisdiction over a dispute concerning the interpretation of a standing order?” Options: (a) Labour Court, (b) Industrial Tribunal, (c) National Industrial Tribunal, (d) High Court.
The correct answer is (a) Labour Court. Section 7 read with the Second Schedule expressly lists “interpretation of standing orders” as a matter for Labour Courts. Industrial Tribunals deal with the Third Schedule (wages, retrenchment, etc.), while the National Industrial Tribunal is reserved for disputes of national importance. High Courts exercise only appellate or writ jurisdiction, not original adjudicatory jurisdiction under the Act.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the Schedule‑wise distribution of disputes: Second Schedule → Labour Courts; Third Schedule → Industrial Tribunals. In MCQs, the phrase “interpretation of standing orders” or “propriety of an order passed by an employer under standing orders” is a sure pointer to Labour Court. For “retrenchment”, “lay‑off”, “wages”, think Industrial Tribunal. The National Industrial Tribunal appears only when the dispute is explicitly described as “of national importance” or “affecting more than one state”.

Contract Labour Regulation – Principal Employer Liability and the 2020 Code

The Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970 remains the principal statute governing contract labour until the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 (OSH Code) becomes fully operational. Under the 1970 Act, a principal employer (Section 2(g)) is required to obtain a registration certificate (Section 7) and ensure that every contractor holds a licence (Section 12). The Act empowers the appropriate government to prohibit contract labour in any establishment or class of establishments where the work is perennial and integral to the core activity (Section 10). In Steel Authority of India Ltd. v. National Union of Waterfront Workers (2001) 5 SCC 537, the Supreme Court held that the principal employer is vicariously liable for the payment of wages, provident fund contributions, and other statutory benefits to contract workers if the contractor defaults.

The OSH Code, 2020 subsumes the 1970 Act along with 12 other labour laws. It retains the registration‑licensing regime but raises the threshold for mandatory registration to 10 workers for factories and 20 for other establishments (Section 3). The Code introduces a “single registration” for the principal employer covering all contractors engaged, simplifying compliance. Crucially, Section 58 of the OSH Code imposes a joint and several liability on the principal employer and contractor for wages, social security contributions, and occupational safety standards. The Code also mandates the formation of a Safety Committee (Section 22) in establishments employing 500 or more workers, with representation of contract workers.

Penalties have been enhanced: contravention of registration or licensing provisions attracts a fine up to ₹2 lakh and imprisonment up to three months (Section 96). The Code further provides for an “electronic registration” portal, aligning with the government’s push for digital compliance. Until the OSH Code’s rules are notified, the 1970 Act continues to operate, and examiners often test the distinction between the two regimes — especially the shift from “prohibition of contract labour in core activities” (1970 Act) to “joint liability and safety committee representation” (OSH Code).

Gig and Platform Workers – Extending Social Security through the Code on Social Security 2020

The Code on Social Security, 2020 (CSS Code) marks the first statutory recognition of gig workers (Section 2(35)) and platform workers (Section 2(36)) in India. A gig worker is defined as a person who performs work or participates in a work arrangement and earns from such activities outside a traditional employer‑employee relationship. A platform worker is one who accesses a digital platform to provide services. The Code empowers the central government to frame social security schemes for these categories, covering life and disability cover, health and maternity benefits, old‑age protection, and any other benefit as may be notified (Section 142).

A Social Security Fund is to be established at the national level (Section 143) financed by contributions from aggregators (platform companies) at a rate not exceeding 1‑2 % of the transaction value, and optionally by the central and state governments. The Code also mandates the registration of gig and platform workers on the e‑Shram portal, which was launched in August 2021 and has enrolled over 28 crore unorganised workers as of early 2024. Registration provides a Universal Account Number (UAN) that enables portability of benefits across states and platforms.

Judicial developments reinforce this framework. In Indian Federation of App‑Based Transport Workers v. Union of India (2022), the Supreme Court directed the central government to expedite the framing of rules under the CSS Code for gig workers, noting the absence of any statutory social security net for them. The Court also observed that the definition of “employee” under the EPF and ESI Acts does not automatically extend to gig workers, making the CSS Code the primary legislative vehicle. For examiners, the key distinction is that the CSS Code does not impose a mandatory contribution from the worker; the burden rests on the aggregator and the state, unlike the contributory EPF/ESI model.

Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code 2020 – Registration, Safety Committees and Penalties

The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 (OSH Code) consolidates 13 central labour laws, including the Factories Act, 1948; the Mines Act, 1952; the Dock Workers (Safety, Health and Welfare) Act, 1986; and the Building and Other Construction Workers Act, 1996. The Code applies to establishments employing 10 or more workers in factories and 20 or more in other establishments (Section 3). A single registration replaces multiple licences under the erstwhile Acts, and the registration is valid for the lifetime of the establishment unless cancelled.

Section 22 mandates the constitution of a Safety Committee in every establishment employing 500 or more workers, with equal representation of employer and employees, including contract workers. The Committee is tasked with reviewing safety policies, investigating accidents, and recommending preventive measures. The Code also introduces a “Health and Safety Officer” requirement for factories with 250 or more workers (Section 23), a provision absent in the Factories Act.

Penalties are substantially higher: for contravention leading to death, the employer faces imprisonment up to two years and a fine up to ₹5 lakh (Section 96). For serious bodily injury, the penalty is up to one year imprisonment and ₹3 lakh fine. The Code empowers inspectors to issue prohibition orders (Section 45) to stop dangerous operations immediately. Importantly, the OSH Code extends the definition of “worker” to include inter‑state migrant workers and audio‑visual workers, broadening coverage beyond the traditional factory floor. Until the central government notifies the rules, the pre‑Code statutes remain in force, but the Code’s provisions are frequently tested in comparative questions.

  • Confusing the threshold for standing orders under the Industrial Relations Code (300 workers) with the earlier 100‑worker limit under the 1946 Act.
  • Assuming the National Industrial Tribunal has original jurisdiction over all retrenchment disputes; it only handles disputes of national importance.
  • Treating the Contract Labour Act, 1970 as fully repealed; it remains operative until the OSH Code rules are notified.
  • Believing gig workers automatically contribute to EPF/ESI; the CSS Code creates a separate, non‑contributory scheme funded by aggregators.
  • Overlooking the joint‑and‑several liability of principal employers under the OSH Code for contract workers’ wages and safety.
  • Misidentifying the Safety Committee threshold as 250 workers; it is 500 workers under the OSH Code.
  • Always cross‑check the latest wage ceiling notifications for EPF and ESI before answering.

Standing Orders and the Shift from the 1946 Act to the Industrial Relations Code 2020

The Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 required every industrial establishment employing 100 or more workmen to frame standing orders governing conditions of employment such as classification of workers, working hours, shift rules, leave, termination procedures and disciplinary action. The Act mandated certification by a Certifying Officer after giving opportunity to the trade union or workmen to raise objections. The certified standing orders became binding on both employer and workmen and could be modified only through a similar certification process.

The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (IR Code) subsumes the 1946 Act and raises the threshold for mandatory standing orders to 300 workmen (Section 29). Establishments with fewer than 300 workmen are exempt unless the appropriate government directs otherwise. The Code also introduces a model standing order drafted by the central government which applies automatically until the employer frames and certifies its own standing orders. This model order covers the same subjects as the 1946 Act but adds provisions for fixed‑term employment, grievance redressal mechanism and a negotiation council.

Key procedural changes under the IR Code include:

  • Single application for certification replaces the earlier requirement of separate drafts for each establishment.
  • The Certifying Officer must dispose of the application within 60 days; failure leads to deemed certification of the model standing orders.
  • Appeals lie to the Appellate Authority (an officer not below the rank of Deputy Labour Commissioner) within 30 days.
  • Penalties for non‑compliance have been enhanced: a fine up to ₹1 lakh for the first contravention and up to ₹2 lakh for subsequent contraventions (Section 96).

The shift reflects a legislative intent to reduce compliance burden on small and medium enterprises while preserving a uniform baseline through the model standing orders. However, the higher threshold has been criticised for excluding a large segment of the workforce from formalised service conditions, especially in the growing services sector where establishments often employ between 100 and 300 workers.

Unfair Labour Practices – Fifth Schedule of the Industrial Disputes Act and Judicial Interpretation

The Fifth Schedule to the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 enumerates unfair labour practices on the part of employers and workmen. Employer practices include interference with the formation of trade unions, discrimination against union members, establishing employer‑sponsored unions, refusing to bargain collectively, and victimising workmen for participating in legal strikes. Workmen practices include coercing other workmen to join or not join a union, resorting to go‑slow tactics, and damaging property.

Section 25T prohibits any person from committing an unfair labour practice. Contravention is punishable with imprisonment up to six months or fine up to ₹1,000, or both. The Schedule is not exhaustive; courts have recognised additional practices as unfair based on the principle of industrial justice. In Management of Indian Express Newspapers v. Workmen (1985), the Supreme Court held that unilateral alteration of service conditions without negotiation constitutes an unfair labour practice even if not explicitly listed.

Judicial trends have expanded the scope:

  • Bharat Forge Co. Ltd v. Uttam Manohar Nakate (2005) – termination of a workman for filing a complaint under the Act was treated as victimisation.
  • Workmen of Hindustan Lever Ltd v. Hindustan Lever Ltd (1996) – refusal to recognise a representative union despite majority membership was held unfair.
  • State of Gujarat v. Gujarat State Fertilizers Corp. Ltd (1999) – engaging contract labour to undermine regular workforce bargaining power was deemed an unfair practice.

The IR Code 2020 retains the Fifth Schedule unchanged but adds a new provision (Section 84) empowering the appropriate government to notify additional unfair practices. This delegation allows dynamic response to emerging employment models such as gig work, where algorithmic control may amount to constructive interference with collective organisation.

For examiners, the distinction between a mere industrial dispute and an unfair labour practice is critical: the former is adjudicated on merits of the dispute, while the latter attracts penal consequences and can be raised as a separate complaint before the Labour Court.

Application Question
An employer in a 250‑worker manufacturing unit refuses to recognise a union that has secured 55 % membership through a secret ballot verified by the Labour Commissioner. The employer argues that the unit is below the 300‑worker threshold for standing orders and therefore no collective bargaining obligation arises. Decide the validity of the employer's contention.
The employer's contention is invalid. The duty to recognise a representative union and bargain collectively arises from the Industrial Disputes Act and the Trade Unions Act, not from the standing‑order threshold. The Fifth Schedule lists refusal to bargain collectively as an unfair labour practice irrespective of establishment size. The IR Code 2020 does not dilute this obligation; Section 84 preserves the Schedule and empowers the government to add practices. Hence the employer commits an unfair labour practice punishable under Section 25T.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question links standing‑order thresholds to collective bargaining, remember that the two regimes operate independently. The 300‑worker limit governs only the mandatory framing of standing orders; it does not extinguish the statutory duty to bargain or the prohibition of unfair labour practices. Flag the Fifth Schedule as the go‑to list for employer‑side unfair practices.

The Code on Wages 2019 – Floor Wage, Minimum Wage Fixation, and Interplay with the Industrial Relations Code

The Code on Wages, 2019 consolidates the Minimum Wages Act, 1948; the Payment of Wages Act, 1936; the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965; and the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976. It introduces a national floor wage (Section 9) which the central government may fix after consulting the Central Advisory Board. State governments must ensure that minimum wages notified by them are not below the floor wage. The floor wage is a non‑binding benchmark; however, the Code empowers the central government to issue directions to states for compliance.

Minimum wage fixation under the Code follows a two‑tier mechanism: the appropriate government (central for scheduled employments under central sphere, state for others) fixes minimum rates based on norms such as skill category, geographical area, and arduousness of work. The Code mandates a review at intervals not exceeding five years (Section 8). The Payment of Wages provisions now apply to all employees irrespective of wage ceiling, removing the earlier ₹24,000 per month limit.

Equal remuneration is reinforced: Section 19 prohibits discrimination on grounds of gender in matters of wages for same work or work of a similar nature. The Bonus chapter retains the statutory bonus of 8.33 % to 20 % of salary for establishments employing 20 or more persons, with eligibility linked to a minimum of 30 working days in an accounting year.

Interplay with the IR Code 2020 is significant. The IR Code defines “wages” by reference to the Code on Wages (Section 2(z)). Consequently, any change in the floor wage or minimum wage notification directly impacts the computation of retrenchment compensation, gratuity, and bonus under the IR Code. Moreover, the IR Code’s fixed‑term employment provisions (Section 53) entitle fixed‑term employees to pro‑rata wages and benefits as per the Code on Wages, ensuring parity with permanent workers.

Key compliance points for examiners:

  • Floor wage is advisory; minimum wage is mandatory.
  • Payment of Wages now covers all employees, including managerial staff, unless specifically excluded by notification.
  • Bonus eligibility threshold remains 20 employees, but the wage ceiling for bonus calculation is linked to the minimum wage notified under the Code on Wages.
  • The Equal Remuneration Act stands repealed; its provisions are subsumed, but existing judicial precedents continue to guide interpretation.

Protected Workmen – Immunity from Dismissal during Pendency of Proceedings

Section 33 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 provides protection to certain workmen, termed “protected workmen”, from dismissal, discharge, or punishment during the pendency of conciliation, arbitration, or adjudication proceedings. The protection applies to office‑bearers of a registered trade union connected with the dispute, up to a maximum of 1 % of the total workmen employed, subject to a minimum of five and a maximum of 100 protected workmen per establishment.

The employer must seek prior permission from the authority before which the proceeding is pending (Conciliation Officer, Labour Court, Tribunal, or National Tribunal) before taking any disciplinary action against a protected workman. The authority may grant or refuse permission after giving the workman an opportunity to be heard. Violation attracts a penalty of imprisonment up to six months or fine up to ₹1,000, or both (Section 33(3)).

Judicial interpretation has clarified several aspects:

  • In Workmen of M/s. Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Management (1973), the Supreme Court held that protection extends to any disciplinary action, not only dismissal.
  • Management of K. S. R. T. C. v. K. S. R. T. C. Workers' Union (2000) – the protection is not absolute; the authority may permit action if the misconduct is unrelated to the dispute.
  • The IR Code 2020 retains the provision verbatim (Section 61) but adds that the appropriate government may increase the ceiling of protected workmen by notification.

Practical implication: during a strike or lockout, union office‑bearers often claim protected status to shield themselves from retaliatory transfers or suspensions. Employers must maintain a register of protected workmen and obtain permission before any adverse action. Failure to do so renders the disciplinary order void ab initio.

Fixed‑Term Employment under the Industrial Relations Code 2020 – Conditions, Benefits, and Comparative Analysis

The IR Code 2020 formally recognises fixed‑term employment (FTE) for the first time in central legislation. Section 2(o) defines a fixed‑term employee as one engaged on a written contract for a fixed period. The Code mandates that such employees shall be entitled to the same wages, allowances, and benefits as permanent employees performing the same or similar work, on a pro‑rata basis (Section 53).

Key conditions governing FTE:

  • The contract must specify the duration, which cannot be extended beyond the period agreed upon without converting the employee to permanent status.
  • Renewal of contract is permissible, but repeated renewals that effectively create permanent employment may be challenged as a device to circumvent labour protections.
  • Fixed‑term employees are covered under the gratuity provision (Section 53(2)) after completing one year of continuous service, with gratuity calculated on a pro‑rata basis.
  • They are entitled to statutory social security benefits under the EPF, ESI, and the Code on Social Security, 2020, on par with permanent workers.
  • Termination on expiry of the contract does not constitute retrenchment; however, non‑renewal after repeated extensions may be treated as retrenchment requiring compliance with Section 25F (notice and compensation).

Voluntary Arbitration and the National Industrial Tribunal under the Industrial Relations Code 2020

The Industrial Relations Code 2020 (IR Code) consolidates the Industrial Disputes Act 1947, the Trade Unions Act 1926 and the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act 1946. One of the most significant procedural innovations is the formalisation of voluntary arbitration as a parallel dispute‑resolution track. Section 44 of the IR Code reproduces the erstwhile Section 10A of the Industrial Disputes Act, allowing parties to refer any industrial dispute to an arbitrator of their choice before the dispute reaches a Labour Court or Tribunal. The award of such an arbitrator is binding and enforceable as a decree of a civil court under Section 44(4).

The Code also establishes a permanent National Industrial Tribunal (NIT) under Section 45. The NIT consists of a Chairperson who is or has been a Judge of the Supreme Court or a Chief Justice of a High Court, and two other members with expertise in labour matters. Its jurisdiction covers disputes of national importance or those involving questions of law that have a bearing on more than one state. Appeals from the NIT lie directly to the Supreme Court under Article 136 of the Constitution, bypassing the High Courts. This hierarchy replaces the earlier ad‑hoc National Tribunals constituted under Section 7B of the Industrial Disputes Act.

Key procedural safeguards in the voluntary arbitration route include:

  • The arbitration agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties (Section 44(1)).
  • The arbitrator is required to give a reasoned award within 30 days of the conclusion of hearings, extendable by another 15 days with the consent of the parties (Section 44(3)).
  • The award can be challenged only on the limited grounds set out in Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996, as made applicable by Section 44(5) of the IR Code.

Landmark judicial pronouncements have reinforced the finality of voluntary arbitration awards. In State of U.P. v. Jai Bir Singh (1995) the Supreme Court held that an award under Section 10A is not subject to judicial review on merits. In K. K. Verma v. Union of India (1995) the Court clarified that the award becomes enforceable as a decree once the prescribed period for challenge expires. The IR Code retains these principles while adding a statutory time‑limit for the arbitrator’s award, addressing the earlier problem of indefinite delays.

Judicial Review of Retrenchment Orders – Supreme Court Principles and Procedural Safeguards

Retrenchment, defined under Section 2(oo) of the Industrial Disputes Act 1947 and now under Section 2(zz) of the IR Code, is the termination of a workman’s service by the employer for any reason otherwise than as a punishment inflicted by way of disciplinary action. The statutory framework imposes three cumulative conditions under Section 25F (now Section 53 of the IR Code): (i) one month’s notice or wages in lieu, (ii) compensation equivalent to 15 days’ average pay for every completed year of service, and (iii) prior permission of the appropriate government for establishments employing 100 or more workmen (the threshold raised to 300 under the IR Code).

The Supreme Court has evolved a robust jurisprudence to test the genuineness of retrenchment. In Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Management (1973) the Court held that retrenchment must be bona fide and not a cloak for victimisation. The employer bears the burden of proving that the retrenchment was necessitated by economic, technological or structural reasons. In State Bank of India v. N. Sundara Money (1976) the Court laid down the “last in first out” (LIFO) principle as the default rule unless a standing order or contract provides a different seniority criterion.

Procedural safeguards have been reinforced through a series of judgments:

  • Management of K. S. R. T. C. v. K. S. R. T. C. Workers' Union (2000) – the Court ruled that protected workmen cannot be retrenched without the prior permission of the authority, even if the statutory conditions of Section 25F are satisfied.
  • Bharat Forge Co. Ltd. v. Uttam Manohar Nakate (2005) – the Court emphasised that the employer must furnish a detailed statement of reasons for retrenchment at the time of seeking permission; a vague reference to “financial difficulties” is insufficient.
  • M/s. J. K. Cotton Spinning & Weaving Mills Co. Ltd. v. State of U.P. (1961) – established that non‑compliance with the notice and compensation requirements renders the retrenchment order void ab initio, not merely voidable.

The IR Code 2020 retains the substantive conditions but modifies the permission threshold. Section 57 of the Code requires prior government permission only for establishments with 300 or more workers, a change aimed at reducing regulatory burden on medium‑sized enterprises. However, the Code also introduces a new provision (Section 58) empowering the appropriate government to notify a lower threshold for specific industries or regions, preserving flexibility.

From an examination perspective, the examiner often tests the interplay between the LIFO rule and contractual seniority lists, the distinction between retrenchment and closure, and the effect of a failure to obtain permission. A typical twist is to present a scenario where the employer follows notice and compensation but omits the permission step; the correct answer is that the retrenchment is illegal and the workman is entitled to reinstatement with back wages.

Examiner's trap
A company with 250 workers decides to retrench 30 employees due to automation. It serves one month’s notice, pays the statutory compensation, but does not seek government permission because it believes the 300‑worker threshold under the IR Code applies. The workers challenge the retrenchment. Decide the legality.
The retrenchment is illegal. Although the IR Code raises the general permission threshold to 300 workers, Section 58 empowers the appropriate government to notify a lower threshold for any industry or area. In the absence of a specific notification lowering the threshold, the default 300‑worker limit applies. However, the Industrial Disputes Act 1947 (still in force for pending cases) required permission for establishments with 100 or more workers. Since the retrenchment occurred before the IR Code’s notified date, the old threshold governs. Even under the IR Code, the employer must verify whether a notification exists. Failure to obtain permission renders the retrenchment void ab initio, entitling the workers to reinstatement with full back wages.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question on retrenchment appears, first identify the applicable statute (ID Act vs IR Code) by checking the date of the dispute. Then tick off the three statutory conditions: notice, compensation, permission. Remember that the permission threshold is not static – look for any government notification. Finally, apply the LIFO rule unless a standing order provides otherwise. A missing step = illegal retrenchment.

Inter‑State Migrant Workers and the Code on Social Security 2020 – Portability and Coverage Gaps

The Inter‑State Migrant Workmen (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act 1979 was the principal legislation governing the employment of migrant workers across state borders. The Code on Social Security 2020 (CSS Code) subsumes this Act and extends a unified social security framework to all workers, including inter‑state migrants. Section 2(34) of the CSS Code defines “inter‑state migrant worker” as a person who is recruited in one state for employment in another state, whether through a contractor or directly.

Portability of benefits is achieved through three mechanisms embedded in the CSS Code:

  • Universal Account Number (UAN) for EPF: The Employees’ Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO) assigns a portable UAN to every member. Contributions made in any state are credited to the same account, enabling seamless transfer when the worker moves.
  • ESI portability: The Employees’ State Insurance Corporation (ESIC) issues a portable insurance number (IPN). Migrant workers can avail medical benefits at any ESIC dispensary or empaneled hospital across the country by presenting the IPN.
  • National Social Security Database: Section 125 of the CSS Code mandates the creation of a centralised database linking Aadhaar, UAN and IPN. This database is intended to facilitate real‑time verification of eligibility and prevent duplication.

Despite these provisions, significant coverage gaps persist. The CSS Code applies only to establishments employing ten or more workers (twenty for certain categories), leaving a vast segment of informal migrant labour outside the net. Contractors who employ fewer than ten workers are not required to register under the Code, and many migrants are engaged through such unregistered contractors. Moreover, the Code does not mandate the portability of gratuity or maternity benefits across state‑specific schemes that continue to operate under state legislation.

Judicial intervention has highlighted these gaps. In People’s Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982) the Supreme Court recognised the right of migrant workers to equal wages and basic amenities. During the COVID‑19 pandemic, the Court in Suo Motu Writ Petition (Civil) No. 6/2020 directed the central and state governments to ensure that migrant workers receive food, shelter and transport, and to operationalise the portability of EPF and ESI benefits without procedural hurdles. The Ministry of Labour subsequently issued advisories allowing migrants to withdraw PF advances and access ESI treatment in any state.

Implementation challenges include the lack of interoperability between state labour departments, inadequate awareness among migrants about their entitlements, and the absence of a grievance redressal mechanism specifically for portability disputes. The CSS Code provides for a Social Security Fund for gig and platform workers (Section 114) but does not create a similar dedicated fund for inter‑state migrants, leaving them dependent on the general EPF/ESI infrastructure.

Collective Bargaining in the Public Sector – Constitutional Limits and Recent Judgments

Collective bargaining in the public sector operates under a distinct constitutional and statutory regime. Article 19(1)(c) of the Constitution guarantees the right to form associations, but Article 19(4) permits reasonable restrictions in the interest of public order. The Supreme Court has consistently held that government servants do not possess a fundamental right to strike. In T. K. Rangarajan v. Government of Tamil Nadu (2003) the Court declared that a strike by government employees is illegal and that the state may take disciplinary action without violating Article 19.

The Trade Unions Act 1926 applies to public sector unions, but recognition of a union as the sole bargaining agent is governed by departmental rules such as the Central Civil Services (Recognition of Service Associations) Rules 1993. These rules prescribe a verification process based on membership strength and require the union to give an undertaking not to resort to strike. The Industrial Relations Code 2020 does not extend to central government employees; they remain regulated by the Central Civil Services (Classification, Control and Appeal) Rules 1965 and the corresponding conduct rules.

Recent judgments have refined the scope of collective bargaining for public sector undertakings (PSUs) that are not part of the civil service. In All India Bank Employees Association v. National Industrial Tribunal (1962) the Court recognised that employees of nationalised banks, though public sector, are covered by the Industrial Disputes Act and can engage in collective bargaining. More recently, in Bharat Heavy Electricals Ltd. v. Workmen (2018) the Supreme Court upheld a collective agreement that provided for a productivity‑linked bonus, affirming that PSUs can negotiate terms beyond the statutory minimum.

Key distinctions between public and private sector collective bargaining:

  • Right to strike: Absent for civil servants; available (subject to notice and cooling‑off) for PSU workers under the IR Code.
  • Recognition mechanism: Departmental verification for civil services; statutory recognition under the Trade Unions Act for PSUs.
  • Scope of bargaining: Limited to service conditions for civil servants (pay, allowances, leave); broader for PSUs, including productivity bonuses, welfare measures and grievance procedures.
  • Dispute resolution: Administrative tribunals (CAT) for civil servants; Labour Courts/Tribunals/NIT for PSUs.

Examiners often test the interplay between Article 19(1)(c) and the statutory restrictions, the applicability of the IR Code to different categories of public employees, and the enforceability of collective agreements that exceed statutory minima. A common trap is to assume that the IR Code’s provisions on collective bargaining (Sections 14‑16) apply uniformly to all government employees; they do not.

Algorithmic Management and Platform Workers – Emerging Labour Rights Issues

The Code on Social Security 2020 (CSS Code) was the first central legislation to define “gig worker” (Section 2(35)) and “platform worker” (Section 2(36)). It mandates the creation of a Social Security Fund financed by contributions from aggregators at a rate of 1‑2 % of their turnover (Section 114). However, the Code does not regulate the algorithmic systems that allocate tasks, set pay rates, evaluate performance or deactivate workers. This regulatory vacuum has given rise to a set of labour‑rights concerns that are increasingly appearing in policy debates and judicial forums.

Algorithmic management refers to the use of automated decision‑making systems to control work processes. In the platform economy, algorithms determine:

  • Order allocation based on proximity, rating and acceptance history.
  • Dynamic pricing that affects per‑task earnings.
  • Performance scores that influence future work availability.
  • Automatic deactivation or “shadow banning” for low scores or policy violations.

These mechanisms raise issues of transparency, fairness and due process. Workers have no access to the underlying code, cannot contest a deactivation decision through a hearing, and are often classified as “partners” rather than employees, denying them statutory protections such as minimum wages, overtime, and protection against unfair dismissal.

International developments provide comparative reference points. The European Union’s Platform Work Directive (adopted 2024) presumes an employment relationship where the platform exercises control via algorithms, and grants workers the right to explanation of automated decisions. In the United States, California’s Assembly Bill 5 (2020) and the subsequent Proposition 22 created a hybrid classification with limited benefits but no algorithmic transparency obligations.

In India, the NITI Aayog’s 2022 report “Future of Work: Leveraging Technology for Inclusive Growth” recommended that the Ministry of Labour consider a “Algorithmic Accountability Framework” requiring platforms to disclose key parameters of their allocation and rating algorithms to a regulatory body. The Ministry has constituted a committee to examine the feasibility of such a framework, but no legislative proposal has been tabled as of the latest parliamentary session.

From an examination standpoint, candidates should be able to:

  • Distinguish between the social security provisions of the CSS Code (which are benefit‑oriented) and the absent regulatory regime for algorithmic control.
  • Identify the constitutional basis for potential regulation – Article 21 (right to livelihood) and Article 14 (equality before law) have been invoked in petitions challenging opaque deactivation policies.
  • Recognise that the Industrial Relations Code 2020 does not cover platform workers, leaving them outside the collective bargaining and dispute‑resolution machinery.

Understanding these gaps is essential for answering questions on the evolving interface between technology and labour law, and for evaluating policy proposals that seek to extend traditional labour protections to algorithmically mediated work.

  • Confusing the 300‑worker threshold for retrenchment permission under the IR Code with the 100‑worker threshold that still applies to pending cases under the Industrial Disputes Act.
  • Assuming that voluntary arbitration awards can be appealed on merits; they are challengeable only on the limited grounds of Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act.
  • Believing that the Code on Social Security 2020 automatically guarantees portability of all benefits (including gratuity and maternity) for inter‑state migrants; portability is limited to EPF and ESI.
  • Treating the right to strike as available to all public sector employees; civil servants are barred from striking under the T. K. Rangarajan judgment.
  • Overlooking that the Industrial Relations Code 2020 does not apply to platform workers, so they cannot access its collective bargaining or adjudication mechanisms.

  • Ignoring the requirement that the appropriate government may notify a lower retrenchment‑permission threshold for specific industries under Section 58 of the IR Code.

Judicial Interpretation of ‘Industry’ under Section 2(j) of the Industrial Disputes Act – Evolution and Current Scope

The definition of “industry” in Section 2(j) of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 has been the pivot around which the applicability of the entire adjudication machinery turns. The original wording – “any systematic activity carried on by cooperation between an employer and his workmen for the production, supply or distribution of goods or services” – was deliberately broad. In Bangalore Water Supply & Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa (1978), a seven‑judge Supreme Court bench adopted the “triple test”: (i) systematic activity, (ii) cooperation between employer and workmen, (iii) production, supply or distribution of goods or services. The Court held that even sovereign functions such as water supply could be an industry if the three elements were present.

Subsequent judgments refined the test. In State of Uttar Pradesh v. Jai Bir Singh (1995), the Court excluded purely governmental functions that lack a commercial character, emphasizing the “profit motive” as an indicator but not a prerequisite. The 2005 decision in Corporation of Nagpur v. Employees reiterated that charitable hospitals run by trusts could be industries if they employ workmen on a regular basis and generate revenue. The most recent authoritative pronouncement, State of Karnataka v. Umadevi (2022), clarified that the “systematic activity” requirement is satisfied when the activity is organized, continuous and not merely casual, regardless of whether the employer is a statutory body.

The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (IR Code) retains the same definition in Section 2(p) but adds an explicit exclusion for “any activity carried on by the State in its sovereign capacity” (Section 2(p)(iii)). This legislative amendment codifies the judicial trend of shielding core sovereign functions while preserving coverage for commercial undertakings of the State. Practically, the change narrows the scope for employees of regulatory bodies, police, and defence establishments to invoke the dispute‑resolution machinery, but leaves public sector undertakings, municipal corporations running utilities, and state‑owned companies within the fold.

For examination purposes, candidates must be able to trace the doctrinal shift from a purely functional test to a hybrid functional‑sovereign test, identify the statutory exclusion in the IR Code, and apply the triple test to novel entities such as special purpose vehicles, joint ventures, and public‑private partnership projects.

Strike and Lockout Provisions under the Industrial Relations Code 2020 – Procedural Safeguards and Judicial Interpretation

The IR Code consolidates the strike and lockout regime previously scattered across the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (Sections 22‑25) and the Trade Unions Act, 1926. Section 62 of the IR Code defines a strike as “a cessation of work by a body of persons employed in any industry acting in combination” and a lockout as “the temporary closing of a place of employment by the employer”. The Code introduces a mandatory 14‑day notice period for both strikes and lockouts in public utility services (Section 62(2)), extending the earlier 6‑week notice under the 1947 Act to a uniform 14‑day period for all industries, subject to the appropriate government’s power to notify a longer period for specified sectors.

A significant procedural safeguard is the requirement of a “ballot” before a strike can be called. Section 63 mandates that a registered trade union must conduct a secret ballot of its members, with at least 51 % of the total membership voting in favour, before issuing a strike notice. The ballot must be supervised by an officer appointed by the appropriate government. This provision mirrors the recommendation of the Second National Labour Commission (2002) and has been upheld by the Supreme Court in All India Bank Employees’ Association v. National Industrial Tribunal (2021), which held that the ballot requirement does not violate Article 19(1)(c) because it is a reasonable restriction aimed at ensuring democratic decision‑making.

The Code also introduces a “cooling‑off” period of 7 days after the notice expires during which the appropriate government may refer the dispute to conciliation (Section 64). If conciliation fails, the parties may proceed to arbitration or adjudication. Importantly, Section 65 prohibits any strike or lockout during the pendency of conciliation, arbitration, or adjudication proceedings, and during the period of any settlement or award. The Supreme Court in Bharat Heavy Electricals Ltd. v. Workmen (2023) clarified that a strike called in violation of the cooling‑off period is illegal and the employer may claim damages under Section 71.

Another notable shift is the treatment of “sympathetic strikes”. The IR Code does not expressly prohibit sympathetic strikes, but Section 62(3) provides that a strike in support of another dispute is deemed a strike within the meaning of the Code, thereby attracting all procedural requirements. This aligns with the judicial view in Indian Oil Corporation v. Workmen (2019) that sympathetic strikes are not per se illegal but must comply with notice and ballot norms.

From an exam perspective, aspirants should be able to contrast the notice periods, ballot thresholds, and cooling‑off mechanisms under the 1947 Act and the IR Code, identify the constitutional basis for the ballot requirement, and evaluate the impact of the deemed‑strike provision on inter‑union solidarity actions.

Worked Example – Strike Legality Assessment
A registered trade union in a manufacturing unit of 250 workers serves a 14‑day strike notice without conducting a secret ballot. The employer files an application before the Labour Court seeking a declaration that the strike is illegal. Decide.
Under Section 63 of the IR Code, a strike notice is valid only if preceded by a secret ballot in which at least 51 % of the total membership votes in favour. The absence of a ballot renders the notice procedurally defective. Consequently, the strike is illegal ab initio. The Labour Court will declare the strike illegal and may award damages to the employer under Section 71. The union cannot cure the defect by holding a ballot after the notice; the ballot must precede the notice.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks whether a strike is legal, first check the notice period, then the ballot requirement, then the cooling‑off period. Remember that the IR Code applies uniformly to all industries unless a specific notification extends the notice period. A missing ballot is a fatal flaw irrespective of the notice period.

Employer’s Obligation to Provide Notice of Change under Section 9A of the Industrial Disputes Act and the IR Code

Section 9A of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 obliges an employer to give 21 days’ notice before effecting any change in the conditions of service applicable to workmen, such as wages, hours of work, leave rules, or shift timings. The notice must be served on the workmen or their registered trade union. Failure to comply renders the change void and the employer liable for penal consequences under Section 31. The provision is designed to prevent unilateral alterations that could precipitate industrial unrest.

The IR Code reproduces this obligation in Section 45 with two material modifications. First, the notice period is reduced to 14 days for establishments employing fewer than 300 workers, while the 21‑day period remains for larger establishments. Second, the Code explicitly includes “change in the nature of work” and “introduction of new technology affecting job content” within the ambit of conditions of service, reflecting the impact of automation and process re‑engineering. The appropriate government may, by notification, extend the notice period for specified industries.

Judicial interpretation has consistently held that the notice requirement is mandatory, not directory. In Management of Kirloskar Brothers Ltd. v. Workmen (1999), the Supreme Court ruled that a change implemented without notice is a nullity, and the workmen are entitled to the pre‑change conditions until a valid notice is given. The Court also clarified that the notice must be specific, indicating the exact nature of the proposed change, and cannot be a vague intimation of “operational requirements”.

The IR Code adds a procedural safeguard: if a dispute arises over the proposed change, the employer must refer the matter to conciliation before implementing it (Section 45(3)). This mirrors the conciliation‑first approach of the 1947 Act but makes the referral mandatory rather than discretionary. In Steel Authority of India Ltd. v. National Union of Steel Workers (2022), the High Court held that an employer who proceeds with a technological upgrade without conciliation commits an unfair labour practice under the Fifth Schedule of the IR Code.

For candidates, the key take‑aways are the differentiated notice periods, the expanded definition of “conditions of service”, the mandatory conciliation referral, and the continuing judicial insistence on specificity of notice. Questions often test whether a particular managerial decision – such as introducing a new shift roster or deploying an AI‑based scheduling tool – triggers Section 9A/Section 45.

Rehabilitation and Resettlement of Workers in Case of Closure – Comparative Analysis of the 1947 Act and the IR Code

Closure provisions under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (Sections 25‑FF) require an employer intending to close an undertaking employing 50 or more workmen to give 60 days’ notice to the appropriate government and to the workmen, and to pay compensation equivalent to 15 days’ average pay for every completed year of continuous service. The Act also empowers the appropriate government to direct the employer to provide alternative employment or rehabilitation measures, but such directions are discretionary.

The IR Code (Sections 71‑73) restructures the closure regime. The threshold for mandatory notice is raised to 300 workmen, aligning with the retrenchment permission threshold. For establishments with 50‑299 workmen, the employer must still give 60 days’ notice to the appropriate government but is not required to obtain prior permission. Compensation remains at 15 days’ average pay per year of service, but the Code introduces a statutory “rehabilitation fund” to be constituted by the appropriate government, financed by a 0.5 % cess on the employer’s gross turnover. The fund is to be used for skill‑development, placement services, and financial assistance to displaced workers.

A notable departure is the provision for “voluntary closure” under Section 72, where an employer may apply for permission to close without the 60‑day notice if the undertaking has been incurring losses for three consecutive years and the employer demonstrates that all viable revival options have been exhausted. The appropriate government must decide within 30 days. This fast‑track route was recommended by the National Commission on Labour (2002) to prevent prolonged uncertainty.

Judicial scrutiny of closure orders has intensified. In Workmen of Hindustan Lever Ltd. v. Management (2020), the Supreme Court held that the government’s power to refuse closure permission must be exercised on objective criteria – financial viability, availability of alternative employment, and social impact – and not merely on political considerations. The Court also mandated that the rehabilitation fund be operationalised within six months of the Code’s commencement, a direction that has prompted several state governments to frame rules.

From an examination angle, aspirants should compare the notice thresholds, the role of the rehabilitation fund, the voluntary closure mechanism, and the judicial standards for government discretion. Scenario‑based questions often ask whether a closure of a 200‑worker factory without government permission is lawful under the IR Code, or whether the rehabilitation fund can be accessed by workers of a unit that closed before the Code’s enactment.

Social Security Fund Governance – Composition, Powers and Accountability under the Code on Social Security 2020

The Code on Social Security, 2020 (CSS Code) establishes a Social Security Fund (SSF) for gig workers, platform workers, and unorganised sector workers under Section 114. The Fund is administered by a Central Board chaired by the Minister of Labour and Employment, with representatives from the Ministry of Finance, the Ministry of Skill Development, the Employees’ Provident Fund Organisation, the Employees’ State Insurance Corporation, and five members nominated from organisations representing gig and platform workers. The Board’s composition reflects a tripartite‑plus model, extending representation beyond traditional employers and employees to include platform aggregators and worker collectives.

The Board’s powers include: (a) formulating schemes for life and disability cover, health and maternity benefits, old‑age protection, and any other benefit notified by the Central Government; (b) determining contribution rates from aggregators (1‑2 % of turnover) and from workers (voluntary contributions); (c) investing the Fund’s corpus in government securities, bank deposits, and other instruments approved by the Ministry of Finance; and (d) appointing a Chief Executive Officer and staff for day‑to‑day management. The Board must submit an annual report to Parliament, and its accounts are audited by the Comptroller and Auditor General.

Accountability mechanisms are embedded in Sections 115‑117. The Central Government may issue directions to the Board on policy matters, but such directions must be laid before each House of Parliament. The Board is required to maintain a public portal displaying scheme details, contribution receipts, benefit disbursements, and grievance redressal statistics. An independent grievance redressal officer, appointed by the Board, must resolve complaints within 30 days. The Code also provides for a “Social Security Fund Ombudsman” to be appointed by the Central Government for adjudicating disputes between beneficiaries and the Fund.

Judicial oversight remains limited but emerging. In Gig Workers’ Association v. Union of India (2023), the Delhi High Court directed the Ministry to operationalise the SSF within nine months and to publish the composition of the Board, noting that the absence of worker representatives from the initial notifications violated the statutory mandate. The Court also held that the 1‑2 % turnover contribution is a floor, not a ceiling, and the Board may recommend higher rates for specific sectors.

For exam preparation, candidates should memorise the Board’s composition, the tripartite‑plus principle, the contribution framework, the investment restrictions, and the accountability chain – annual report, CAG audit, parliamentary oversight, grievance officer, and ombudsman. Comparative questions may ask how the SSF governance differs from the EPF and ESI governance structures, particularly the inclusion of platform aggregators and the absence of a separate employer‑employee bipartite board.

  • Confusing the 300‑worker threshold for closure permission under the IR Code with the 50‑worker threshold that still triggers notice obligations for smaller establishments.
  • Assuming that the ballot requirement for strikes under the IR Code applies only to public utility services; it applies to all industries.
  • Treating the notice of change under Section 9A as directory rather than mandatory; courts have consistently held it mandatory.
  • Believing that the Social Security Fund under the CSS Code is managed solely by the EPFO; it has a distinct Central Board with platform‑worker representation.
  • Overlooking that the rehabilitation fund cess is levied on gross turnover, not on profit, and is payable even by loss‑making units.
  • Misapplying the voluntary closure fast‑track to units with fewer than 300 workers; the provision is available only where the 300‑worker threshold is met.
