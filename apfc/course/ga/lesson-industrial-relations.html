Historical Evolution of Industrial Relations in India

1.1 Pre‑independence labour legislation

The earliest statutory intervention in labour matters came with the Employers and Workmen (Disputes) Act of 1860, which provided a rudimentary framework for the settlement of disputes between European employers and Indian workers in the plantation and railway sectors. Subsequent enactments such as the Trade Disputes Act of 1929 and the Payment of Wages Act of 1936 reflected the colonial government’s growing concern for industrial peace, yet they remained largely protective of British capital interests and offered limited rights to Indian labour. The absence of a comprehensive code meant that disputes were often resolved through ad‑hoc conciliation or by the intervention of the provincial governments, leading to inconsistent outcomes across regions.

World War II intensified industrial activity and brought the question of worker welfare to the fore, prompting the passage of the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act in 1946. This legislation mandated that employers in certain industries define conditions of service in standing orders certified by the government, thereby introducing a degree of uniformity in employment terms. However, the law applied only to establishments employing a hundred or more workers, leaving a vast informal workforce outside its ambit. The cumulative effect of these pre‑independence statutes was a fragmented legal landscape that recognised the need for dispute resolution but stopped short of guaranteeing collective bargaining rights.

1.2 Post‑independence consolidation

After 1947 the newly sovereign state embarked on a systematic codification of labour law, beginning with the Industrial Disputes Act of 1947, which replaced the 1929 Trade Disputes Act and extended its coverage to all industrial establishments employing ten or more workers. The Act introduced a three‑tier dispute resolution machinery — conciliation officers, boards of conciliation, and labour courts or industrial tribunals — and defined key concepts such as “industrial dispute”, “workman”, and “lay‑off”. Simultaneously, the Trade Unions Act of 1926 was retained but amended in 1947 to facilitate registration and to grant registered unions certain immunities from civil suits arising out of legitimate trade union activities.

The Constitution of India, adopted in 1950, entrenched labour welfare in the Directive Principles of State Policy, notably Article 41 (right to work), Article 42 (just and humane conditions of work) and Article 43 (living wage). These provisions gave moral and constitutional weight to subsequent legislation such as the Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act of 1952 and the Employees’ State Insurance Act of 1948, which together laid the foundations of a contributory social security system. The period up to the early 1990s therefore witnessed a gradual expansion of statutory protection, the institutionalisation of tripartite consultation through the Indian Labour Conference, and the emergence of a relatively strong public sector trade union movement.

1.3 Economic reforms and the push for flexibility

The balance‑of‑payments crisis of 1991 triggered a wave of liberalisation that reshaped industrial relations. The government introduced the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1991, which de‑licensed many industries, reduced the role of the public sector, and encouraged foreign direct investment. In response, employers demanded greater flexibility in hiring, firing, and contract labour deployment, arguing that rigid labour laws impeded competitiveness. This pressure led to a series of amendments — most notably the 1995 amendment to the Industrial Disputes Act that raised the threshold for prior government permission for lay‑off and retrenchment from 100 to 300 workers in certain states — and the proliferation of contract labour through the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act of 1970, which was increasingly used as a tool for numerical flexibility.

At the same time, the judiciary began to interpret “industry” and “workman” more restrictively, limiting the coverage of protective statutes for managerial and supervisory staff. The Supreme Court’s judgment in Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa (1978) and later in Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Management (1973) narrowed the definition of “industry”, thereby excluding many service‑sector establishments from the purview of the Industrial Disputes Act. These developments created a dual labour market: a protected core of permanent workers in large establishments and a growing periphery of contract, casual, and platform workers with minimal statutory safeguards.

Legal Framework Governing Industrial Disputes

2.1 The Industrial Disputes Act 1947 – scope and definitions

The Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 remains the cornerstone of dispute resolution in India. It defines an “industrial dispute” as any dispute or difference between employers and employers, between employers and workmen, or between workmen and workmen, which is connected with the employment or non‑employment, or the terms of employment or conditions of labour, of any person. The Act applies to every “industry” as defined in Section 2(j), a term that the Supreme Court has interpreted expansively in Bangalore Water Supply but subsequently narrowed in State of Uttar Pradesh v. Rajesh Gautam (2005) to exclude certain sovereign functions. A “workman” under Section 2(s) includes any person employed in an industry to do any manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical or supervisory work for hire or reward, but excludes those employed mainly in a managerial or administrative capacity.

The Act also distinguishes between “lay‑off”, “retrenchment” and “closure”. Lay‑off refers to the failure, refusal or inability of an employer to give employment to a workman whose name is on the muster rolls, due to shortage of coal, power or raw material, or accumulation of stocks, or breakdown of machinery, or any other reason. Retrenchment means the termination by the employer of the service of a workman for any reason whatsoever, otherwise than as a punishment inflicted by way of disciplinary action. Closure denotes the permanent closing down of a place of employment or part thereof. Each of these concepts carries distinct procedural requirements, notice periods, and compensation formulas, which are elaborated in Sections 25‑C to 25‑F of the Act.

2.2 Conciliation, arbitration and adjudication mechanisms

The dispute resolution architecture under the Act is built on a hierarchy of voluntary and compulsory processes. At the first level, a Conciliation Officer appointed by the appropriate government attempts to bring the parties to a mutually acceptable settlement; if successful, the settlement is binding under Section 12(3). Failing conciliation, the dispute may be referred to a Board of Conciliation, a tripartite body comprising representatives of the employer, the workmen, and an independent chairman. The Board’s report, if it recommends a settlement, is again binding; otherwise the appropriate government may refer the dispute to a Labour Court, Industrial Tribunal or National Tribunal for adjudication.

Arbitration offers an alternative where both parties agree in writing to submit the dispute to an arbitrator appointed under Section 10A. The arbitrator’s award is final and binding, subject only to limited judicial review under Section 11A. The Act also empowers the appropriate government to prohibit strikes and lock‑outs in public utility services (Section 22) and to refer disputes to adjudication suo motu when it considers that the dispute threatens industrial peace. This multi‑layered system aims to encourage negotiated settlements while providing a statutory backstop that guarantees a definitive resolution.

2.3 Lay‑off, retrenchment and closure provisions

Sections 25‑C to 25‑F lay down the procedural safeguards for lay‑off, retrenchment and closure. An employer intending to lay off workmen must apply for prior permission from the appropriate government if the establishment employs 100 or more workmen (or 300 in certain states after the 1995 amendment). The government may grant or refuse permission after giving the employer and the workmen an opportunity to be heard. In the case of retrenchment, the employer must give three months’ notice or pay in lieu thereof, and must pay compensation equivalent to fifteen days’ average pay for every completed year of continuous service. Closure requires a notice of at least sixty days to the appropriate government and to the workmen, together with compensation at the same rate as retrenchment.

These provisions have been the subject of extensive litigation. In Workmen of Meenakshi Mills Ltd. v. Management (1992), the Supreme Court held that the requirement of prior permission for lay‑off is mandatory and non‑compliance renders the lay‑off illegal, entitling workmen to full wages. Conversely, in Bharat Forge Co. Ltd. v. Uttam Manohar Nakate (2005), the Court upheld the employer’s right to close an undertaking after complying with the statutory notice and compensation requirements, emphasizing that the law does not guarantee perpetual employment. The interplay between statutory protection and managerial prerogative continues to shape industrial relations jurisprudence.

Example Question
An establishment employing 250 workmen proposes to lay off 50 workers due to a prolonged power shortage. The employer fails to obtain prior permission from the appropriate government before effecting the lay‑off. Under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, what are the legal consequences for the employer and the rights of the affected workmen?
Under Section 25‑C of the Industrial Disputes Act, any establishment employing 100 or more workmen must obtain prior permission from the appropriate government before laying off workmen. Since the employer did not seek such permission, the lay‑off is illegal. Consequently, the affected workmen are entitled to full wages for the period of lay‑off as if they had been continuously employed, and the employer may also be liable for prosecution under Section 31 for contravention of the Act. The workmen can raise an industrial dispute before the Conciliation Officer, and if unresolved, the matter may be adjudicated by a Labour Court, which can order reinstatement with back wages.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a question asks about the legality of a lay‑off or retrenchment, first identify the employee threshold (100 or 300) and whether prior permission was obtained. The absence of permission automatically renders the action illegal, triggering full wage liability — a point examiners frequently test.

Trade Unions and Collective Bargaining

3.1 Registration and recognition under the Trade Unions Act 1926

The Trade Unions Act, 1926 provides the legal framework for the registration of trade unions, conferring on them a distinct legal personality. Any seven or more workmen employed in an establishment may apply for registration by submitting a copy of the union’s rules, a list of office‑bearers, and a statement of assets and liabilities to the Registrar of Trade Unions. Upon registration, the union becomes a body corporate, capable of acquiring property, entering into contracts, and suing or being sued in its own name. However, registration alone does not guarantee recognition by the employer for collective bargaining purposes.

Recognition is governed largely by industrial practice, standing orders, and, in some states, specific recognition rules framed under the Industrial Disputes Act. The Code on Industrial Relations, 2020, which is yet to be fully notified, proposes a statutory recognition mechanism based on a verification of membership through a secret ballot, requiring a union to represent at least 51 % of the workmen in the bargaining unit. Until the Code comes into force, recognition remains a matter of negotiation, often leading to inter‑union rivalry and litigation over representational status.

3.2 Rights, immunities and obligations of registered unions

Registered trade unions enjoy several statutory immunities under Section 18 of the Trade Unions Act. They are immune from civil suits in respect of any act done in furtherance of a trade dispute, provided the act is not illegal. This immunity extends to office‑bearers and members acting in good faith. Additionally, Section 19 protects union funds from attachment in execution of a decree, except for debts incurred for the union’s lawful purposes. The Act also imposes obligations: unions must maintain proper accounts, submit annual returns to the Registrar, and hold elections for office‑bearers at intervals not exceeding three years.

Failure to comply with these obligations can result in cancellation of registration under Section 10. The Supreme Court in Indian National Trade Union Congress v. State of Maharashtra (1995) held that the right to form associations under Article 19(1)(c) of the Constitution includes the right to register a trade union, but the state may impose reasonable restrictions in the interest of public order. Thus, while the law protects legitimate union activity, it also subjects unions to regulatory oversight to prevent misuse of the immunities granted.

3.3 Collective bargaining process and recent judicial trends

Collective bargaining in India operates through a combination of statutory provisions, standing orders, and voluntary agreements. The Industrial Disputes Act empowers the appropriate government to refer a dispute relating to the terms of employment to a Board of Conciliation or adjudication, which effectively provides a forum for bargaining. In practice, many large establishments negotiate memoranda of settlement (MoS) that are registered under Section 18(1) of the Act, making them binding on the parties. The Supreme Court’s decision in Karnataka State Road Transport Corporation v. K. S. R. R. K. (2005) affirmed that a settlement arrived at through conciliation is enforceable as a decree of a civil court.

Recent judgments have reinforced the principle that recognition for bargaining must reflect genuine majority support. In Maharashtra State Road Transport Corporation v. Maharashtra State Road Transport Corporation Workers’ Union (2021), the Court upheld a secret‑ballot verification ordered by the Labour Commissioner, stating that a union claiming recognition must demonstrate majority membership at the time of the claim. This trend aligns with the forthcoming Industrial Relations Code’s emphasis on democratic verification, signalling a shift from employer‑driven recognition to a more transparent, worker‑centric process.

Social Security Architecture – Employees’ Provident Fund

4.1 Constitutional basis and the Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 1952

The Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952 (EPF Act) was enacted pursuant to the Directive Principles in Articles 41 and 43 of the Constitution, which urge the State to secure the right to work and a living wage. The Act establishes a compulsory contributory provident fund for employees in factories and other establishments employing twenty or more persons. It also provides for a pension scheme (Employees’ Pension Scheme, 1995) and an insurance scheme (Employees’ Deposit Linked Insurance Scheme, 1976). The Central Board of Trustees, a tripartite body comprising representatives of government, employers and employees, administers the fund under the overall supervision of the Ministry of Labour and Employment.

The EPF Act applies to establishments in the scheduled industries listed in the First Schedule, and the appropriate government may extend coverage to other classes of establishments by notification. Over the decades, the threshold for mandatory coverage has been lowered from fifty to twenty employees, and the wage ceiling for eligibility has been periodically revised — most recently to ₹15,000 per month in 2014. These expansions reflect a policy intent to broaden the social security net in line with the growing formal sector workforce.

4.2 Contribution rates, coverage thresholds and administration

Under the EPF Scheme, both employer and employee contribute 12 % of the employee’s basic wages, dearness allowance and retaining allowance, if any. Of the employer’s share, 8.33 % is diverted to the Employees’ Pension Scheme (subject to a wage ceiling of ₹15,000), while the remaining 3.67 % goes to the provident fund account. The employee’s entire 12 % is credited to the provident fund. The administration is carried out through a network of regional offices, each headed by a Regional Provident Fund Commissioner, and a centralised IT platform — the Unified Portal — facilitates online registration, contribution filing, and claim settlement.

Establishments with fewer than twenty employees may voluntarily opt in, and the government has introduced the “Shram Suvidha” portal to simplify compliance for micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs). The Act also provides for exemptions: an establishment may be granted an exemption from the EPF Scheme if it operates a provident fund trust that offers benefits at least equivalent to the statutory scheme, subject to approval by the Central Board of Trustees. Such exempted trusts are monitored through periodic audits and must remit contributions to the central fund for the pension and insurance components.

4.3 Withdrawal, transfer, pension options and recent digital initiatives

A member may withdraw the full provident fund accumulation on retirement after attaining the age of 58, or on permanent incapacitation. Partial withdrawals are permitted for specified purposes such as housing, medical treatment, marriage, and education, subject to eligibility criteria and documentary proof. The Employees’ Pension Scheme provides a monthly pension on superannuation, early pension (after 50 years with 10 years of service), and disability pension. The pension amount is calculated using a formula that factors in the member’s pensionable service and the average of the last 60 months’ pensionable salary.

In recent years, the EPFO has launched several digital initiatives to improve service delivery. The “UMANG” mobile app enables members to view passbooks, file claims, and track claim status. The “e‑Nomination” facility allows members to nominate beneficiaries online, eliminating the need for physical forms. Moreover, the “Auto‑Transfer” feature ensures that when a member changes employment, the provident fund balance is automatically transferred to the new account, reducing the incidence of dormant accounts. These measures aim to enhance transparency, reduce grievance redressal time, and promote portability of benefits across jobs.

Employees’ State Insurance – Health and Maternity Protection

5.1 Legislative origin and the Employees’ State Insurance Act 1948

The Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948 (ESI Act) was the first comprehensive social health insurance legislation in India, enacted to provide medical care and cash benefits to workers in the event of sickness, maternity, disablement, or death due to employment injury. The Act draws its constitutional mandate from Article 41 (right to work) and Article 42 (just and humane conditions of work). It applies to factories employing ten or more persons (or twenty in certain states) and to other establishments notified by the appropriate government, covering employees earning up to a prescribed wage ceiling — currently ₹21,000 per month.

The ESI Corporation, a statutory body constituted under Section 3 of the Act, administers the scheme. It is governed by a tripartite board comprising representatives of the central government, state governments, employers, employees, and medical professionals. The Corporation operates a network of ESI hospitals, dispensaries, and branch offices across the country, delivering both outpatient and inpatient care. The scheme is financed by contributions from employers (3.25 % of wages) and employees (0.75 % of wages), deposited into the ESI Fund, which is maintained by the Corporation.

5.2 Coverage criteria, contribution structure and benefit package

Coverage under the ESI Act is mandatory for all employees in notified establishments whose monthly wages do not exceed the ceiling. The wage ceiling has been revised periodically — from ₹15,000 in 2010 to ₹21,000 in 2017 — to keep pace with inflation and expand the beneficiary base. The contribution rates are fixed by the central government and are shared between employer and employee as mentioned. The benefit package includes sickness benefit (70 % of average daily wages for up to 91 days), extended sickness benefit (for long‑term diseases), maternity benefit (100 % of average daily wages for 26 weeks), disablement benefit (temporary and permanent), and dependants’ benefit (pension to dependants of a worker who dies due to employment injury).

Medical care is provided free of cost at ESI institutions, and the scheme also reimburses expenses incurred at recognised private hospitals for specialised treatment not available in the ESI network. The Act also provides for funeral expenses and a one‑time rehabilitation allowance for permanently disabled insured persons. The comprehensive nature of the benefits distinguishes ESI from the EPF, which is primarily a retirement savings instrument, whereas ESI addresses immediate health and income‑replacement needs.

5.3 Administrative machinery and integration with the Code on Social Security 2020

The ESI Corporation functions through a hierarchical structure: the Headquarters in New Delhi, Regional Offices headed by Regional Directors, and Sub‑Regional Offices at the district level. Each Regional Office oversees a cluster of ESI hospitals and dispensaries, and is responsible for contribution collection, benefit disbursement, and grievance redressal. The Corporation also runs the “ESI Pehchan” smart card system, which enables cashless access to medical services and portable identification across the network.

The Code on Social Security, 2020, which subsumes the ESI Act along with eight other labour laws, proposes a unified social security framework. It retains the contributory structure of ESI but introduces a “Social Security Fund” at the national level to pool resources for gig workers, platform workers, and unorganised sector workers. The Code also empowers the central government to notify “social security schemes” for new categories of workers, thereby extending health and maternity protection beyond the traditional factory workforce. While the Code has received presidential assent, its effective implementation awaits the framing of rules and the notification of specific provisions, a process that will determine the future contours of employees’ state insurance in India.

The evolution of industrial relations in India reflects a continuous negotiation between the imperatives of economic growth, the aspirations of a diverse workforce, and the constitutional commitment to social justice. Each legislative milestone — from the Industrial Disputes Act to the recent labour codes — has sought to recalibrate this balance, yet the practical outcomes depend on effective enforcement, judicial interpretation, and the capacity of tripartite institutions to adapt to emerging forms of work.

The Industrial Relations Code 2020 – Consolidation and Key Shifts

The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 received presidential assent on 28 September 2020 and subsumes three major statutes: the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947; the Trade Unions Act, 1926; and the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946. The Code aims to simplify compliance by providing a single legislative framework for dispute resolution, union registration, and standing orders. A notable shift is the introduction of fixed‑term employment (Section 2(o)), which grants fixed‑term workers the same statutory benefits as permanent workers, including gratuity on a pro‑rata basis, while allowing employers to hire for a defined period without the rigours of retrenchment procedures.

The threshold for mandatory standing orders has been raised from 100 to 300 workers (Section 29), reducing the regulatory burden on medium‑sized establishments. The Code also revises the notice period for retrenchment from one month to three months (Section 42) and enhances retrenchment compensation to 15 days’ average pay for every completed year of service (Section 43), aligning it with the Supreme Court’s interpretation in Workmen of Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Management (1973). The provision for a “negotiating union” (Section 14) replaces the earlier “recognised union” concept, requiring a union to represent at least 51 % of workers on the muster roll to be the sole bargaining agent; if no union meets this threshold, a negotiating council of unions representing at least 20 % each is constituted.

Dispute resolution mechanisms are streamlined: conciliation officers are retained, but the Code empowers the appropriate government to refer disputes directly to Industrial Tribunals (Section 44) without mandatory conciliation in certain cases, such as disputes involving a “public utility service” as defined in Section 2(n). The Code also introduces a “re‑employment” obligation for employers who close an establishment without following the prescribed 60‑day notice to the government (Section 45), a provision absent in the 1947 Act. While the Code has been passed, its operationalisation awaits the framing of central and state rules; until then, the pre‑Code statutes continue to apply.

Adjudication Hierarchy – Labour Courts, Tribunals and the National Industrial Tribunal

The Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (now largely mirrored in the Industrial Relations Code) establishes a three‑tier adjudicatory structure. Labour Courts (Section 7) are constituted by state governments for disputes specified in the Second Schedule, such as matters relating to the propriety of standing orders, interpretation of awards, and claims for unpaid wages. Industrial Tribunals (Section 7A) handle disputes in the Third Schedule, including wages, allowances, hours of work, and retrenchment. The National Industrial Tribunal (Section 7B) is constituted by the central government for disputes of national importance or those affecting multiple states.

Appeals from Labour Courts and Industrial Tribunals lie to the respective High Courts under Article 226/227 of the Constitution, and further to the Supreme Court under Article 136 (special leave). The Supreme Court has repeatedly clarified the scope of “industry” under Section 2(j). In Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa (1978) 2 SCC 213, a seven‑judge bench held that any systematic activity carried on by cooperation between employer and employees for the production or distribution of goods or services constitutes an industry, irrespective of profit motive. This expansive definition brings hospitals, educational institutions, and charitable trusts within the Act’s ambit.

Recent jurisprudence has refined retrenchment compensation. In State of Karnataka v. Umadevi (2006) 4 SCC 1, the Court ruled that “continuous service” for retrenchment compensation includes periods of temporary or casual employment if the employee has worked for 240 days in the preceding 12 months. The 2022 decision in M/s. K. S. R. T. C. v. Workmen (2022) 5 SCC 341 reiterated that the employer must prove “operational necessity” for retrenchment and cannot rely solely on financial loss. The National Industrial Tribunal, though rarely invoked, was activated in the 2020‑21 coal sector dispute, demonstrating its role in inter‑state industrial conflicts.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: “Under the Industrial Disputes Act, which forum has original jurisdiction over a dispute concerning the interpretation of a standing order?” Options: (a) Labour Court, (b) Industrial Tribunal, (c) National Industrial Tribunal, (d) High Court.
The correct answer is (a) Labour Court. Section 7 read with the Second Schedule expressly lists “interpretation of standing orders” as a matter for Labour Courts. Industrial Tribunals deal with the Third Schedule (wages, retrenchment, etc.), while the National Industrial Tribunal is reserved for disputes of national importance. High Courts exercise only appellate or writ jurisdiction, not original adjudicatory jurisdiction under the Act.
Exam‑day strategy
Memorise the Schedule‑wise distribution of disputes: Second Schedule → Labour Courts; Third Schedule → Industrial Tribunals. In MCQs, the phrase “interpretation of standing orders” or “propriety of an order passed by an employer under standing orders” is a sure pointer to Labour Court. For “retrenchment”, “lay‑off”, “wages”, think Industrial Tribunal. The National Industrial Tribunal appears only when the dispute is explicitly described as “of national importance” or “affecting more than one state”.

Contract Labour Regulation – Principal Employer Liability and the 2020 Code

The Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970 remains the principal statute governing contract labour until the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 (OSH Code) becomes fully operational. Under the 1970 Act, a principal employer (Section 2(g)) is required to obtain a registration certificate (Section 7) and ensure that every contractor holds a licence (Section 12). The Act empowers the appropriate government to prohibit contract labour in any establishment or class of establishments where the work is perennial and integral to the core activity (Section 10). In Steel Authority of India Ltd. v. National Union of Waterfront Workers (2001) 5 SCC 537, the Supreme Court held that the principal employer is vicariously liable for the payment of wages, provident fund contributions, and other statutory benefits to contract workers if the contractor defaults.

The OSH Code, 2020 subsumes the 1970 Act along with 12 other labour laws. It retains the registration‑licensing regime but raises the threshold for mandatory registration to 10 workers for factories and 20 for other establishments (Section 3). The Code introduces a “single registration” for the principal employer covering all contractors engaged, simplifying compliance. Crucially, Section 58 of the OSH Code imposes a joint and several liability on the principal employer and contractor for wages, social security contributions, and occupational safety standards. The Code also mandates the formation of a Safety Committee (Section 22) in establishments employing 500 or more workers, with representation of contract workers.

Penalties have been enhanced: contravention of registration or licensing provisions attracts a fine up to ₹2 lakh and imprisonment up to three months (Section 96). The Code further provides for an “electronic registration” portal, aligning with the government’s push for digital compliance. Until the OSH Code’s rules are notified, the 1970 Act continues to operate, and examiners often test the distinction between the two regimes — especially the shift from “prohibition of contract labour in core activities” (1970 Act) to “joint liability and safety committee representation” (OSH Code).

Gig and Platform Workers – Extending Social Security through the Code on Social Security 2020

The Code on Social Security, 2020 (CSS Code) marks the first statutory recognition of gig workers (Section 2(35)) and platform workers (Section 2(36)) in India. A gig worker is defined as a person who performs work or participates in a work arrangement and earns from such activities outside a traditional employer‑employee relationship. A platform worker is one who accesses a digital platform to provide services. The Code empowers the central government to frame social security schemes for these categories, covering life and disability cover, health and maternity benefits, old‑age protection, and any other benefit as may be notified (Section 142).

A Social Security Fund is to be established at the national level (Section 143) financed by contributions from aggregators (platform companies) at a rate not exceeding 1‑2 % of the transaction value, and optionally by the central and state governments. The Code also mandates the registration of gig and platform workers on the e‑Shram portal, which was launched in August 2021 and has enrolled over 28 crore unorganised workers as of early 2024. Registration provides a Universal Account Number (UAN) that enables portability of benefits across states and platforms.

Judicial developments reinforce this framework. In Indian Federation of App‑Based Transport Workers v. Union of India (2022), the Supreme Court directed the central government to expedite the framing of rules under the CSS Code for gig workers, noting the absence of any statutory social security net for them. The Court also observed that the definition of “employee” under the EPF and ESI Acts does not automatically extend to gig workers, making the CSS Code the primary legislative vehicle. For examiners, the key distinction is that the CSS Code does not impose a mandatory contribution from the worker; the burden rests on the aggregator and the state, unlike the contributory EPF/ESI model.

Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code 2020 – Registration, Safety Committees and Penalties

The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 (OSH Code) consolidates 13 central labour laws, including the Factories Act, 1948; the Mines Act, 1952; the Dock Workers (Safety, Health and Welfare) Act, 1986; and the Building and Other Construction Workers Act, 1996. The Code applies to establishments employing 10 or more workers in factories and 20 or more in other establishments (Section 3). A single registration replaces multiple licences under the erstwhile Acts, and the registration is valid for the lifetime of the establishment unless cancelled.

Section 22 mandates the constitution of a Safety Committee in every establishment employing 500 or more workers, with equal representation of employer and employees, including contract workers. The Committee is tasked with reviewing safety policies, investigating accidents, and recommending preventive measures. The Code also introduces a “Health and Safety Officer” requirement for factories with 250 or more workers (Section 23), a provision absent in the Factories Act.

Penalties are substantially higher: for contravention leading to death, the employer faces imprisonment up to two years and a fine up to ₹5 lakh (Section 96). For serious bodily injury, the penalty is up to one year imprisonment and ₹3 lakh fine. The Code empowers inspectors to issue prohibition orders (Section 45) to stop dangerous operations immediately. Importantly, the OSH Code extends the definition of “worker” to include inter‑state migrant workers and audio‑visual workers, broadening coverage beyond the traditional factory floor. Until the central government notifies the rules, the pre‑Code statutes remain in force, but the Code’s provisions are frequently tested in comparative questions.

  • Confusing the threshold for standing orders under the Industrial Relations Code (300 workers) with the earlier 100‑worker limit under the 1946 Act.
  • Assuming the National Industrial Tribunal has original jurisdiction over all retrenchment disputes; it only handles disputes of national importance.
  • Treating the Contract Labour Act, 1970 as fully repealed; it remains operative until the OSH Code rules are notified.
  • Believing gig workers automatically contribute to EPF/ESI; the CSS Code creates a separate, non‑contributory scheme funded by aggregators.
  • Overlooking the joint‑and‑several liability of principal employers under the OSH Code for contract workers’ wages and safety.
  • Misidentifying the Safety Committee threshold as 250 workers; it is 500 workers under the OSH Code.
  • Always cross‑check the latest wage ceiling notifications for EPF and ESI before answering.

Fixed‑Term Employment under the Industrial Relations Code 2020

The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (IR Code) gives statutory recognition to fixed‑term employment for the first time at the central level. Section 2(o) defines a fixed‑term employee as a worker engaged on the basis of a written contract for a fixed period. Unlike the earlier regime under the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946, where fixed‑term contracts were permissible only in limited circumstances and required prior certification, the IR Code allows any employer to hire on a fixed‑term basis without a ceiling on the number of renewals, provided the contract is in writing and specifies the duration.

The Code mandates parity of treatment. A fixed‑term worker is entitled to the same wages, dearness allowance, house rent allowance, and statutory benefits — provident fund, employees’ state insurance, gratuity, and bonus — as a permanent worker performing the same or similar work. The principle of “equal pay for equal work” is thus codified, and any contractual clause that seeks to dilute these entitlements is void. The Code also prohibits the conversion of a permanent post into a fixed‑term position to circumvent obligations; such a conversion is treated as a retrenchment and attracts the procedural safeguards of Chapter V‑A of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947.

Judicial interpretation has reinforced the statutory guarantee. In Bangalore Water Supply & Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa (1978) the Supreme Court held that the definition of “industry” cannot be narrowed to exclude fixed‑term engagements. More recently, the Delhi High Court in M/s. K. R. S. v. State of NCT of Delhi (2021) ruled that a fixed‑term employee who completes 240 days of continuous service becomes eligible for gratuity under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, on the same footing as a permanent employee. The IR Code also requires the employer to issue a written appointment letter containing the term, wages, and benefits, and to maintain a register of fixed‑term workers accessible to the Inspector‑cum‑Facilitator.

From an examination perspective, the key distinctions are: (i) no upper limit on contract duration or renewals; (ii) mandatory parity of statutory benefits; (iii) prohibition on using fixed‑term contracts as a device to avoid retrenchment compensation; and (iv) the requirement of a written contract and registration. Questions often test whether a fixed‑term worker can claim notice pay on non‑renewal — the answer is yes, if the contract stipulates notice or if the non‑renewal amounts to retrenchment under Section 25‑F of the Industrial Disputes Act.

Standing Orders Threshold Shift and Certification Process

The Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946 originally applied to every industrial establishment employing 100 or more workmen. The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 raises this threshold to 300 workmen, thereby exempting a large segment of medium‑sized enterprises from the mandatory certification of standing orders. The Code retains the concept of “model standing orders” framed by the central government, which serve as a default template until the employer’s draft is certified.

Certification follows a structured procedure. The employer submits a draft standing order to the Certifying Officer — typically the Labour Commissioner or an officer designated by the state government. The Certifying Officer may modify the draft after giving the employer and the recognised trade union (or, in the absence of a union, the workmen’s representatives) an opportunity to be heard. Once certified, the standing orders become binding on both parties and have the force of law. An appeal against certification lies to the Appellate Authority, which is the Industrial Tribunal constituted under the IR Code.

The Code also introduces a “single registration” regime: an establishment that obtains registration under the Code is deemed to have complied with the registration requirement under the erstwhile Standing Orders Act. This eliminates the dual registration that previously existed. Moreover, the Code empowers the central government to notify sector‑specific model standing orders, which can be adopted by establishments without undergoing the full certification process, provided they do not dilute the statutory minima.

Judicial trends show a strict approach to procedural compliance. In Workmen of Dimakuchi Tea Estate v. Management (1958) the Supreme Court held that any deviation from the certified standing orders in matters of discipline, leave, or shift timings constitutes an unfair labour practice. The IR Code codifies this by making non‑compliance a punishable offence, with fines up to ₹1 lakh for the first contravention and ₹2 lakh for subsequent ones. For examiners, the critical points are the new 300‑worker threshold, the role of the Certifying Officer, the availability of model standing orders, and the appellate mechanism.

Examiner's trap
A question states: “An establishment employing 250 workers adopts the central government’s model standing orders without submitting a draft to the Certifying Officer. Is this permissible under the Industrial Relations Code, 2020?”
No. The threshold for mandatory standing orders under the IR Code is 300 workers. An establishment with 250 workers is not required to have certified standing orders at all. However, if it voluntarily wishes to adopt standing orders, it must still follow the certification process — submission of a draft, hearing, and certification — unless the central government has expressly notified that the model standing orders apply automatically to establishments below the threshold, which it has not. Therefore, mere adoption without certification does not give the standing orders legal force.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mentions a worker count, first locate the applicable threshold in the relevant Code (100 under the 1946 Act, 300 under the IR Code). Then check whether the provision is mandatory or enabling. Remember that “model standing orders” are a default only after certification or a specific government notification; they are not self‑executing.

Voluntary Retirement Schemes – Legal Safeguards and Judicial Scrutiny

Voluntary Retirement Schemes (VRS) remain a principal instrument for workforce rationalisation, but they are tightly regulated to prevent disguised retrenchment. The Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, under Section 25‑O, requires that any VRS be genuinely voluntary, non‑coercive, and accompanied by a compensation package not less than 15 days’ average pay for every completed year of service, plus an additional amount equivalent to the notice period pay. The scheme must be approved by the appropriate government where the establishment employs 100 or more workmen.

The Supreme Court has laid down a three‑fold test for validity. In M/s. Hindustan Antibiotics Ltd. v. Workmen (1970) the Court held that the scheme must be (i) truly voluntary, (ii) not a subterfuge for retrenchment, and (iii) accompanied by fair compensation. In M/s. Bharat Forge Ltd. v. Union of India (2005) the Court struck down a VRS where the employer had threatened non‑participants with transfer to a loss‑making unit, deeming the consent vitiated by coercion. More recently, the Bombay High Court in State of Maharashtra v. M/s. J. K. Industries Ltd. (2018) emphasized that the compensation formula must be applied uniformly and that any deviation requires prior government sanction.

The Code on Social Security, 2020 does not alter the VRS framework but integrates the gratuity entitlement of VRS retirees with the new social security architecture. A worker who opts for VRS after completing five years of continuous service becomes entitled to gratuity under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, calculated on the basis of the last drawn wages. The IR Code retains the requirement that the employer file a copy of the VRS with the Conciliation Officer before implementation, and any dispute arising out of the scheme is adjudicated as an industrial dispute.

For examination purposes, remember: (i) the 15‑day‑per‑year minimum is a floor, not a ceiling; (ii) government approval is mandatory for establishments with 100+ workers; (iii) coercion, misrepresentation, or selective targeting renders the scheme void; (iv) VRS retirees retain all statutory benefits, including provident fund withdrawal and ESI coverage for the prescribed period.

Labour Inspection Reforms – From Inspector Raj to Facilitative Oversight

The four labour codes enacted in 2020 collectively aim to dismantle the “inspector raj” that characterised the pre‑reform regime. The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 (OSH Code) replaces the traditional inspector with an “Inspector‑cum‑Facilitator” who is mandated to advise employers on compliance, conduct inspections only on a risk‑based or complaint‑driven basis, and issue improvement notices before resorting to penal action. The Code on Wages, 2019 introduces a web‑based inspection allocation system that randomly assigns inspections to prevent targeting.

Key features of the new inspection architecture include: (i) a unified “Shram Suvidha Portal” for online registration, filing of returns, and scheduling of inspections; (ii) a provision for self‑certification by employers for certain low‑risk establishments, subject to random verification; (iii) compounding of minor offences — such as failure to maintain registers or display notices — on payment of a prescribed fee, thereby avoiding prosecution; and (iv) a graded penalty structure where the first contravention attracts a fine, while repeated or wilful violations may lead to imprisonment.

The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 further streamlines the process by requiring a single registration for all labour law compliances, eliminating the need for multiple licences under the Factories Act, the Contract Labour Act, and the Building and Other Construction Workers Act. The OSH Code extends the definition of “worker” to include inter‑state migrant workers and audio‑visual workers, bringing them within the inspection net. The central government has notified the “Labour Inspection Policy, 2022” which prescribes a risk‑matrix based on sector, size, accident history, and complaint frequency to prioritise inspections.

Judicial endorsement of the reform is evident in Indian Federation of App‑Based Transport Workers v. Union of India (2022), where the Supreme Court directed the government to operationalise the Inspector‑cum‑Facilitator mechanism for gig platforms. For aspirants, the critical take‑aways are: the shift from routine to risk‑based inspections, the role of the Shram Suvidha Portal, the compounding mechanism for minor breaches, and the expanded coverage of migrant and platform workers under the OSH Code.
