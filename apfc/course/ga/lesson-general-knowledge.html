Indian Polity

1.1 The Preamble and Its Significance

The Preamble to the Constitution of India declares the source of authority, the nature of the state, and the objectives that the Constitution seeks to achieve. It reads that India is a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic, assuring its citizens justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. The words “sovereign” and “republic” indicate that the ultimate power rests with the people and that the head of state is elected, not hereditary. The inclusion of “socialist” and “secular” through the Forty‑second Amendment in 1976 reflects the evolving ideological commitments of the nation. Courts have repeatedly held that the Preamble is a key to the interpretation of the Constitution, though it is not enforceable by itself; it guides the construction of fundamental rights and directive principles alike.

1.2 Fundamental Rights and Their Enforcement

Part III of the Constitution enumerates six groups of fundamental rights, ranging from the right to equality under Articles 14 to 18 to the right to constitutional remedies under Article 32. These rights are justiciable, meaning any citizen can approach the Supreme Court or High Courts for their enforcement. The doctrine of basic structure, evolved in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), limits the amending power of Parliament so that the core of fundamental rights cannot be abrogated. Judicial review, therefore, acts as a guardian of these rights, and the Supreme Court has expanded their scope through interpretations such as the right to privacy being read into Article 21 in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017).

1.3 Directive Principles of State Policy

Part IV contains the Directive Principles, which are non‑justiciable guidelines for the state to promote social and economic democracy. Articles 36 to 51 direct the state to secure a living wage, equal pay for equal work, protection of the environment, and promotion of international peace. Although not enforceable by courts, they have acquired legal weight through the principle of “harmonious construction,” whereby courts strive to interpret fundamental rights in a manner consistent with the directives. The Forty‑second Amendment added several new directives, including the duty to protect monuments and to promote scientific temper, reflecting the expanding role of the state in welfare.

1.4 Federal Structure and Centre‑State Relations

The Constitution establishes a quasi‑federal system with a strong centre. Legislative powers are divided into three lists — Union, State and Concurrent — under the Seventh Schedule. The Union List contains subjects of national importance such as defence, foreign affairs and atomic energy, while the State List covers police, public health and agriculture. In case of conflict on a Concurrent subject, the central law prevails under Article 254. The Finance Commission, constituted under Article 280, recommends the distribution of tax revenues between the centre and the states, and the Goods and Services Tax Council, created by the One Hundred and First Amendment, exemplifies cooperative federalism in fiscal matters.

Indian History

2.1 Ancient India: Indus Valley to the Mauryas

The Indus Valley Civilisation, flourishing between 2600 and 1900 BCE, displayed advanced urban planning, standardized weights and a script that remains undeciphered. The subsequent Vedic period saw the composition of the Rigveda and the emergence of varna society. The Mahajanapadas gave rise to the first large territorial states, among which Magadha became dominant. The Mauryan Empire, founded by Chandragupta Maurya in 322 BCE and expanded by Ashoka, unified most of the subcontinent and promoted Buddhism through edicts inscribed on pillars and rocks. Ashoka’s policy of dhamma emphasized moral governance, religious tolerance and welfare measures, leaving a lasting legacy on Indian political thought.

2.2 Classical Age: Guptas and Regional Kingdoms

The Gupta dynasty (c. 320–550 CE) is often called the Golden Age of India because of remarkable achievements in science, mathematics, astronomy, literature and art. Aryabhata’s concept of zero and the decimal system, Kalidasa’s plays, and the construction of the iron pillar at Delhi exemplify this flourishing. Simultaneously, southern kingdoms such as the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas developed maritime trade networks extending to Southeast Asia, while the Pallavas and later the Chalukyas patronised temple architecture, evident in the rock‑cut monuments at Mahabalipuram and the structural temples at Badami.

2.3 Medieval India: Sultanate and Mughal Periods

The Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) introduced Persianate administrative practices, a standing army and a revenue system based on iqta grants. The Mughal Empire, established by Babur in 1526, reached its zenith under Akbar, who instituted a centralized mansabdari system, a policy of Sulh‑i‑kul (universal tolerance) and a land revenue settlement known as the zabt. The empire’s decline after Aurangzeb’s death in 1707 created a power vacuum that regional powers — Marathas, Sikhs, Rajputs and the Nawabs of Bengal — filled, while European trading companies, especially the British East India Company, gradually transformed commercial privileges into territorial control.

2.4 Modern India: Colonial Rule and the Freedom Struggle

The Battle of Plassey (1757) and the Battle of Buxar (1764) laid the foundation of British political dominance. The Regulating Act of 1773 and the subsequent Charter Acts progressively increased parliamentary oversight. The Revolt of 1857, though suppressed, ended Company rule and ushered in direct Crown administration under the Government of India Act 1858. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, evolved from a petitioning body to a mass movement under leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, and later Mahatma Gandhi, whose non‑violent civil disobedience — Non‑Cooperation Movement (1920‑22), Salt March (1930) and Quit India Movement (1942) — mobilised millions. The Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) and the Mountbatten Plan (1947) culminated in the partition and independence on 15 August 1947.

Example Question
Which Article of the Constitution of India provides for the establishment of the Finance Commission?
Article 280 of the Constitution mandates the President to constitute a Finance Commission every five years or at such earlier time as the President considers necessary. The Commission recommends the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States, the principles governing grants‑in‑aid, and any other matter referred to it by the President in the interests of sound finance. Therefore, the correct answer is Article 280.
Exam‑Day Insight
In polity questions, the phrase “subject to the provisions of this Constitution” frequently signals a reference to Article 13, which declares that laws inconsistent with fundamental rights are void. Recognising this wording helps you identify the correct article without memorising every clause.

Indian Geography

3.1 Physiographic Divisions

India’s landmass is divided into six major physiographic units: the Northern Mountains, the Northern Plains, the Peninsular Plateau, the Indian Desert, the Coastal Plains and the Islands. The Himalayas, the youngest and highest mountain system, act as a climatic barrier, forcing the southwest monsoon to shed moisture over the subcontinent. The Indo‑Gangetic plain, formed by alluvial deposits of the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra, supports dense agriculture and a large share of the population. The Deccan Plateau, composed of ancient basaltic lava flows, is rich in mineral resources such as iron ore, manganese and bauxite.

3.2 Climate and Monsoon Mechanism

The Indian climate is dominated by the seasonal reversal of winds known as the monsoon. During summer, intense heating of the Tibetan Plateau creates a low‑pressure area that draws moisture‑laden winds from the Indian Ocean, resulting in the southwest monsoon from June to September. The retreat of the monsoon in October brings the northeast monsoon, which mainly affects the Coromandel coast. Variability in monsoon onset, intensity and withdrawal directly influences agricultural output, water availability and the overall economy.

3.3 Major River Systems

India’s rivers are classified into Himalayan and Peninsular systems. Himalayan rivers — Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra — are perennial, fed by snowmelt and rainfall, and form large alluvial plains. Peninsular rivers — Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi — are largely rain‑fed, seasonal and flow through hard rock terrain, creating fewer extensive plains. The interlinking of rivers has been proposed to mitigate regional water imbalances, but ecological and inter‑state concerns have limited implementation.

3.4 Soils, Natural Vegetation and Mineral Wealth

Alluvial soil, found in the northern plains and river deltas, is the most fertile and supports intensive cultivation of rice, wheat and sugarcane. Black cotton soil (regur) of the Deccan traps moisture and is suited for cotton. Red and lateritic soils, prevalent in the southern and eastern hills, are less fertile but support plantation crops like tea and coffee. India’s forest cover, classified into tropical evergreen, deciduous, thorn and montane types, harbours biodiversity hotspots such as the Western Ghats and the Eastern Himalayas. The country ranks among the top producers of coal, iron ore, manganese, mica and bauxite, with major belts in Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh and Karnataka.

Indian Economy

4.1 National Income Concepts

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) measures the market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory during a given period, irrespective of the nationality of the producers. Gross National Product (GNP) adds net factor income from abroad — wages, rent, interest and profits earned by residents overseas minus similar payments to foreign residents — to GDP. Net Domestic Product (NDP) subtracts depreciation from GDP, reflecting the sustainable output. The GDP deflator, calculated as (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100, captures the overall price level change in the economy, distinguishing real growth from inflationary effects.

4.2 Planning and Economic Reforms

After independence, India adopted a mixed economy model with five‑year plans guided by the Planning Commission, emphasizing heavy industry, import substitution and public sector dominance. The 1991 balance‑of‑payments crisis triggered structural reforms: liberalisation of industrial licensing, reduction of tariff barriers, deregulation of financial markets, and privatization of several public enterprises. The shift from a plan‑centric to a market‑oriented framework accelerated GDP growth, increased foreign direct investment, and integrated India into global value chains, though challenges of inequality, unemployment and regional disparities persist.

4.3 Banking, Monetary Policy and Financial Inclusion

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI), established in 1935 and nationalised in 1949, acts as the central bank, regulating monetary policy through instruments such as the repo rate, reverse repo rate, cash reserve ratio and statutory liquidity ratio. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) introduced a time‑bound resolution process for distressed assets, improving credit discipline. Initiatives like the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (2014) expanded banking access to over 400 million previously unbanked adults, while the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) revolutionised digital transactions, making India one of the largest real‑time payment ecosystems globally.

4.4 Fiscal Policy, Taxation and External Sector

The Union Budget, presented annually, outlines revenue and expenditure proposals, guided by the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, which sets targets for fiscal deficit and debt‑to‑GDP ratio. The Goods and Services Tax (GST), implemented in July 2017, subsumed multiple indirect taxes into a single destination‑based tax, simplifying compliance and widening the tax base. On the external front, the current account deficit is financed by capital flows — foreign portfolio investment, foreign direct investment and external commercial borrowings — while the rupee’s exchange rate is managed through a market‑determined regime with occasional RBI intervention to curb excessive volatility.

General Science

5.1 Physics: Mechanics, Thermodynamics and Modern Physics

Newton’s three laws of motion form the foundation of classical mechanics, describing the relationship between force, mass and acceleration. The law of universal gravitation explains planetary orbits and the weight of objects on Earth. Thermodynamics governs energy conversion; the first law states energy conservation, while the second law introduces entropy, dictating the direction of spontaneous processes. In modern physics, Einstein’s special relativity revises concepts of space and time at velocities approaching the speed of light, and quantum mechanics describes the probabilistic behaviour of subatomic particles, underpinning technologies such as semiconductors and lasers.

5.2 Chemistry: Atomic Structure, Periodicity and Chemical Bonding

The Bohr model and the subsequent quantum mechanical model explain electron arrangement in shells and subshells, determining the periodic properties of elements. The periodic table groups elements with similar valence electron configurations, leading to predictable trends in atomic radius, ionisation energy, electronegativity and metallic character. Chemical bonding — ionic, covalent and metallic — arises from the tendency of atoms to achieve stable electron configurations; the nature of bonding dictates physical properties such as melting point, conductivity and solubility.

5.3 Biology: Cell Theory, Genetics and Evolution

Cell theory asserts that all living organisms are composed of cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells arise from pre‑existing cells. The discovery of DNA’s double‑helix structure by Watson and Crick elucidated the molecular basis of heredity. Mendelian genetics, combined with molecular biology, explains inheritance patterns, while the modern synthesis integrates natural selection with genetic variation, providing a comprehensive framework for evolution. Biotechnology applications — recombinant DNA technology, CRISPR‑Cas9 gene editing — have transformed medicine, agriculture and environmental management.

5.4 Space, Defence and Emerging Technologies

India’s space programme, led by the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), achieved milestones such as the Aryabhata satellite (1975), the Chandrayaan‑1 lunar orbiter (2008), the Mars Orbiter Mission (2013) and the Chandrayaan‑3 soft landing near the lunar south pole (2023). The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) develops strategic systems including the Agni series of ballistic missiles, the BrahMos supersonic cruise missile and the indigenous Light Combat Aircraft Tejas. Emerging domains — artificial intelligence, quantum communication, hypersonic vehicles — are being pursued through mission‑mode projects to enhance national security and technological sovereignty.

Awards and Honours

6.1 Highest Civilian Awards

The Bharat Ratna, instituted in 1954, is the highest civilian award of the Republic of India, conferred for exceptional service or performance of the highest order in any field of human endeavour. It is awarded by the President on the recommendation of the Prime Minister, and a maximum of three recipients can be honoured in a year. The Padma awards — Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan and Padma Shri — recognise distinguished service in arts, literature, science, public affairs and other spheres, with the Padma Vibhushan being the second highest civilian honour.

6.2 Gallantry Awards

Gallantry awards are classified into wartime and peacetime categories. The Param Vir Chakra (PVC) is the highest wartime gallantry award, followed by the Maha Vir Chakra (MVC) and the Vir Chakra (VrC). In peacetime, the Ashoka Chakra (AC) is the highest, succeeded by the Kirti Chakra (KC) and the Shaurya Chakra (SC). These awards are announced on Republic Day and Independence Day, and recipients receive a medal, a monetary allowance and certain privileges such as precedence in official functions.

6.3 Literary and Cultural Awards

The Jnanpith Award, established in 1961, is India’s highest literary honour, conferred on authors writing in any of the languages listed in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution. The Sahitya Akademi Award, given annually since 1955, recognises outstanding literary works in 24 languages. The Sangeet Natak Akademi Award honours excellence in performing arts — music, dance and theatre — while the Lalit Kala Akademi Award celebrates visual arts. These institutions function under the Ministry of Culture and play a pivotal role in preserving and promoting India’s diverse cultural heritage.

6.4 Sports Awards

The Major Dhyan Chand Khel Ratna Award, renamed in 2021 from the Rajiv Gandhi Khel Ratna, is the highest sporting honour, awarded for spectacular and most outstanding performance over a period of four years. The Arjuna Award recognises consistent excellence at the international level, while the Dronacharya Award honours eminent coaches. The Dhyan Chand Award for lifetime achievement in sports and the Rashtriya Khel Protsahan Puraskar for corporate and voluntary contributions complete the spectrum of national sports recognition.
