Indian Polity

1.1 The Preamble and Its Significance

The Preamble to the Constitution of India declares the source of authority, the nature of the state, and the objectives that the Constitution seeks to achieve. It reads that India is a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic, assuring its citizens justice, liberty, equality and fraternity. The words “sovereign” and “republic” indicate that the ultimate power rests with the people and that the head of state is elected, not hereditary. The inclusion of “socialist” and “secular” through the Forty‑second Amendment in 1976 reflects the evolving ideological commitments of the nation. Courts have repeatedly held that the Preamble is a key to the interpretation of the Constitution, though it is not enforceable by itself; it guides the construction of fundamental rights and directive principles alike.

1.2 Fundamental Rights and Their Enforcement

Part III of the Constitution enumerates six groups of fundamental rights, ranging from the right to equality under Articles 14 to 18 to the right to constitutional remedies under Article 32. These rights are justiciable, meaning any citizen can approach the Supreme Court or High Courts for their enforcement. The doctrine of basic structure, evolved in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), limits the amending power of Parliament so that the core of fundamental rights cannot be abrogated. Judicial review, therefore, acts as a guardian of these rights, and the Supreme Court has expanded their scope through interpretations such as the right to privacy being read into Article 21 in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017).

1.3 Directive Principles of State Policy

Part IV contains the Directive Principles, which are non‑justiciable guidelines for the state to promote social and economic democracy. Articles 36 to 51 direct the state to secure a living wage, equal pay for equal work, protection of the environment, and promotion of international peace. Although not enforceable by courts, they have acquired legal weight through the principle of “harmonious construction,” whereby courts strive to interpret fundamental rights in a manner consistent with the directives. The Forty‑second Amendment added several new directives, including the duty to protect monuments and to promote scientific temper, reflecting the expanding role of the state in welfare.

1.4 Federal Structure and Centre‑State Relations

The Constitution establishes a quasi‑federal system with a strong centre. Legislative powers are divided into three lists — Union, State and Concurrent — under the Seventh Schedule. The Union List contains subjects of national importance such as defence, foreign affairs and atomic energy, while the State List covers police, public health and agriculture. In case of conflict on a Concurrent subject, the central law prevails under Article 254. The Finance Commission, constituted under Article 280, recommends the distribution of tax revenues between the centre and the states, and the Goods and Services Tax Council, created by the One Hundred and First Amendment, exemplifies cooperative federalism in fiscal matters.

Indian History

2.1 Ancient India: Indus Valley to the Mauryas

The Indus Valley Civilisation, flourishing between 2600 and 1900 BCE, displayed advanced urban planning, standardized weights and a script that remains undeciphered. The subsequent Vedic period saw the composition of the Rigveda and the emergence of varna society. The Mahajanapadas gave rise to the first large territorial states, among which Magadha became dominant. The Mauryan Empire, founded by Chandragupta Maurya in 322 BCE and expanded by Ashoka, unified most of the subcontinent and promoted Buddhism through edicts inscribed on pillars and rocks. Ashoka’s policy of dhamma emphasized moral governance, religious tolerance and welfare measures, leaving a lasting legacy on Indian political thought.

2.2 Classical Age: Guptas and Regional Kingdoms

The Gupta dynasty (c. 320–550 CE) is often called the Golden Age of India because of remarkable achievements in science, mathematics, astronomy, literature and art. Aryabhata’s concept of zero and the decimal system, Kalidasa’s plays, and the construction of the iron pillar at Delhi exemplify this flourishing. Simultaneously, southern kingdoms such as the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas developed maritime trade networks extending to Southeast Asia, while the Pallavas and later the Chalukyas patronised temple architecture, evident in the rock‑cut monuments at Mahabalipuram and the structural temples at Badami.

2.3 Medieval India: Sultanate and Mughal Periods

The Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) introduced Persianate administrative practices, a standing army and a revenue system based on iqta grants. The Mughal Empire, established by Babur in 1526, reached its zenith under Akbar, who instituted a centralized mansabdari system, a policy of Sulh‑i‑kul (universal tolerance) and a land revenue settlement known as the zabt. The empire’s decline after Aurangzeb’s death in 1707 created a power vacuum that regional powers — Marathas, Sikhs, Rajputs and the Nawabs of Bengal — filled, while European trading companies, especially the British East India Company, gradually transformed commercial privileges into territorial control.

2.4 Modern India: Colonial Rule and the Freedom Struggle

The Battle of Plassey (1757) and the Battle of Buxar (1764) laid the foundation of British political dominance. The Regulating Act of 1773 and the subsequent Charter Acts progressively increased parliamentary oversight. The Revolt of 1857, though suppressed, ended Company rule and ushered in direct Crown administration under the Government of India Act 1858. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, evolved from a petitioning body to a mass movement under leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, and later Mahatma Gandhi, whose non‑violent civil disobedience — Non‑Cooperation Movement (1920‑22), Salt March (1930) and Quit India Movement (1942) — mobilised millions. The Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) and the Mountbatten Plan (1947) culminated in the partition and independence on 15 August 1947.

Example Question
Which Article of the Constitution of India provides for the establishment of the Finance Commission?
Article 280 of the Constitution mandates the President to constitute a Finance Commission every five years or at such earlier time as the President considers necessary. The Commission recommends the distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States, the principles governing grants‑in‑aid, and any other matter referred to it by the President in the interests of sound finance. Therefore, the correct answer is Article 280.
Exam‑Day Insight
In polity questions, the phrase “subject to the provisions of this Constitution” frequently signals a reference to Article 13, which declares that laws inconsistent with fundamental rights are void. Recognising this wording helps you identify the correct article without memorising every clause.

Indian Geography

3.1 Physiographic Divisions

India’s landmass is divided into six major physiographic units: the Northern Mountains, the Northern Plains, the Peninsular Plateau, the Indian Desert, the Coastal Plains and the Islands. The Himalayas, the youngest and highest mountain system, act as a climatic barrier, forcing the southwest monsoon to shed moisture over the subcontinent. The Indo‑Gangetic plain, formed by alluvial deposits of the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra, supports dense agriculture and a large share of the population. The Deccan Plateau, composed of ancient basaltic lava flows, is rich in mineral resources such as iron ore, manganese and bauxite.

3.2 Climate and Monsoon Mechanism

The Indian climate is dominated by the seasonal reversal of winds known as the monsoon. During summer, intense heating of the Tibetan Plateau creates a low‑pressure area that draws moisture‑laden winds from the Indian Ocean, resulting in the southwest monsoon from June to September. The retreat of the monsoon in October brings the northeast monsoon, which mainly affects the Coromandel coast. Variability in monsoon onset, intensity and withdrawal directly influences agricultural output, water availability and the overall economy.

3.3 Major River Systems

India’s rivers are classified into Himalayan and Peninsular systems. Himalayan rivers — Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra — are perennial, fed by snowmelt and rainfall, and form large alluvial plains. Peninsular rivers — Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi — are largely rain‑fed, seasonal and flow through hard rock terrain, creating fewer extensive plains. The interlinking of rivers has been proposed to mitigate regional water imbalances, but ecological and inter‑state concerns have limited implementation.

3.4 Soils, Natural Vegetation and Mineral Wealth

Alluvial soil, found in the northern plains and river deltas, is the most fertile and supports intensive cultivation of rice, wheat and sugarcane. Black cotton soil (regur) of the Deccan traps moisture and is suited for cotton. Red and lateritic soils, prevalent in the southern and eastern hills, are less fertile but support plantation crops like tea and coffee. India’s forest cover, classified into tropical evergreen, deciduous, thorn and montane types, harbours biodiversity hotspots such as the Western Ghats and the Eastern Himalayas. The country ranks among the top producers of coal, iron ore, manganese, mica and bauxite, with major belts in Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh and Karnataka.

Indian Economy

4.1 National Income Concepts

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) measures the market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory during a given period, irrespective of the nationality of the producers. Gross National Product (GNP) adds net factor income from abroad — wages, rent, interest and profits earned by residents overseas minus similar payments to foreign residents — to GDP. Net Domestic Product (NDP) subtracts depreciation from GDP, reflecting the sustainable output. The GDP deflator, calculated as (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100, captures the overall price level change in the economy, distinguishing real growth from inflationary effects.

4.2 Planning and Economic Reforms

After independence, India adopted a mixed economy model with five‑year plans guided by the Planning Commission, emphasizing heavy industry, import substitution and public sector dominance. The 1991 balance‑of‑payments crisis triggered structural reforms: liberalisation of industrial licensing, reduction of tariff barriers, deregulation of financial markets, and privatization of several public enterprises. The shift from a plan‑centric to a market‑oriented framework accelerated GDP growth, increased foreign direct investment, and integrated India into global value chains, though challenges of inequality, unemployment and regional disparities persist.

4.3 Banking, Monetary Policy and Financial Inclusion

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI), established in 1935 and nationalised in 1949, acts as the central bank, regulating monetary policy through instruments such as the repo rate, reverse repo rate, cash reserve ratio and statutory liquidity ratio. The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) introduced a time‑bound resolution process for distressed assets, improving credit discipline. Initiatives like the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (2014) expanded banking access to over 400 million previously unbanked adults, while the Unified Payments Interface (UPI) revolutionised digital transactions, making India one of the largest real‑time payment ecosystems globally.

4.4 Fiscal Policy, Taxation and External Sector

The Union Budget, presented annually, outlines revenue and expenditure proposals, guided by the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003, which sets targets for fiscal deficit and debt‑to‑GDP ratio. The Goods and Services Tax (GST), implemented in July 2017, subsumed multiple indirect taxes into a single destination‑based tax, simplifying compliance and widening the tax base. On the external front, the current account deficit is financed by capital flows — foreign portfolio investment, foreign direct investment and external commercial borrowings — while the rupee’s exchange rate is managed through a market‑determined regime with occasional RBI intervention to curb excessive volatility.

General Science

5.1 Physics: Mechanics, Thermodynamics and Modern Physics

Newton’s three laws of motion form the foundation of classical mechanics, describing the relationship between force, mass and acceleration. The law of universal gravitation explains planetary orbits and the weight of objects on Earth. Thermodynamics governs energy conversion; the first law states energy conservation, while the second law introduces entropy, dictating the direction of spontaneous processes. In modern physics, Einstein’s special relativity revises concepts of space and time at velocities approaching the speed of light, and quantum mechanics describes the probabilistic behaviour of subatomic particles, underpinning technologies such as semiconductors and lasers.

5.2 Chemistry: Atomic Structure, Periodicity and Chemical Bonding

The Bohr model and the subsequent quantum mechanical model explain electron arrangement in shells and subshells, determining the periodic properties of elements. The periodic table groups elements with similar valence electron configurations, leading to predictable trends in atomic radius, ionisation energy, electronegativity and metallic character. Chemical bonding — ionic, covalent and metallic — arises from the tendency of atoms to achieve stable electron configurations; the nature of bonding dictates physical properties such as melting point, conductivity and solubility.

5.3 Biology: Cell Theory, Genetics and Evolution

Cell theory asserts that all living organisms are composed of cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells arise from pre‑existing cells. The discovery of DNA’s double‑helix structure by Watson and Crick elucidated the molecular basis of heredity. Mendelian genetics, combined with molecular biology, explains inheritance patterns, while the modern synthesis integrates natural selection with genetic variation, providing a comprehensive framework for evolution. Biotechnology applications — recombinant DNA technology, CRISPR‑Cas9 gene editing — have transformed medicine, agriculture and environmental management.

5.4 Space, Defence and Emerging Technologies

India’s space programme, led by the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), achieved milestones such as the Aryabhata satellite (1975), the Chandrayaan‑1 lunar orbiter (2008), the Mars Orbiter Mission (2013) and the Chandrayaan‑3 soft landing near the lunar south pole (2023). The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) develops strategic systems including the Agni series of ballistic missiles, the BrahMos supersonic cruise missile and the indigenous Light Combat Aircraft Tejas. Emerging domains — artificial intelligence, quantum communication, hypersonic vehicles — are being pursued through mission‑mode projects to enhance national security and technological sovereignty.

Awards and Honours

6.1 Highest Civilian Awards

The Bharat Ratna, instituted in 1954, is the highest civilian award of the Republic of India, conferred for exceptional service or performance of the highest order in any field of human endeavour. It is awarded by the President on the recommendation of the Prime Minister, and a maximum of three recipients can be honoured in a year. The Padma awards — Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan and Padma Shri — recognise distinguished service in arts, literature, science, public affairs and other spheres, with the Padma Vibhushan being the second highest civilian honour.

6.2 Gallantry Awards

Gallantry awards are classified into wartime and peacetime categories. The Param Vir Chakra (PVC) is the highest wartime gallantry award, followed by the Maha Vir Chakra (MVC) and the Vir Chakra (VrC). In peacetime, the Ashoka Chakra (AC) is the highest, succeeded by the Kirti Chakra (KC) and the Shaurya Chakra (SC). These awards are announced on Republic Day and Independence Day, and recipients receive a medal, a monetary allowance and certain privileges such as precedence in official functions.

6.3 Literary and Cultural Awards

The Jnanpith Award, established in 1961, is India’s highest literary honour, conferred on authors writing in any of the languages listed in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution. The Sahitya Akademi Award, given annually since 1955, recognises outstanding literary works in 24 languages. The Sangeet Natak Akademi Award honours excellence in performing arts — music, dance and theatre — while the Lalit Kala Akademi Award celebrates visual arts. These institutions function under the Ministry of Culture and play a pivotal role in preserving and promoting India’s diverse cultural heritage.

6.4 Sports Awards

The Major Dhyan Chand Khel Ratna Award, renamed in 2021 from the Rajiv Gandhi Khel Ratna, is the highest sporting honour, awarded for spectacular and most outstanding performance over a period of four years. The Arjuna Award recognises consistent excellence at the international level, while the Dronacharya Award honours eminent coaches. The Dhyan Chand Award for lifetime achievement in sports and the Rashtriya Khel Protsahan Puraskar for corporate and voluntary contributions complete the spectrum of national sports recognition.

Constitutional Amendments and the Basic Structure Doctrine

Article 368 empowers Parliament to amend the Constitution, but the procedure varies with the subject matter. Amendments affecting the federal structure, the representation of states in Parliament, or the amendment procedure itself require a special majority of two‑thirds of members present and voting in each House and ratification by at least half of the state legislatures. All other amendments need only a special majority in Parliament. Since the Constitution’s adoption, more than one hundred amendments have been enacted, ranging from the reorganisation of states (Seventh Amendment, 1956) to the introduction of the Goods and Services Tax (One Hundred and First Amendment, 2016).

The Supreme Court’s jurisprudence on the limits of amending power began with Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965), where the Court held that Parliament could amend any part, including Fundamental Rights. This position was reversed in Golaknath (1967), which declared Fundamental Rights immune from amendment. Parliament responded with the Twenty‑Fourth Amendment (1971) restoring its power, leading to the landmark Kesavananda Bharati (1973) decision. A 7‑6 majority ruled that while Parliament may amend any provision, it cannot alter the basic structure of the Constitution — supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, and the rule of law. Subsequent cases — Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), Minerva Mills (1980), Waman Rao (1981) — refined the doctrine, adding judicial review and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles to the protected core.

The basic structure doctrine functions as a constitutional “guardrail”. It does not enumerate an exhaustive list; each generation of judges identifies new elements (e.g., free and fair elections in Kihoto Hollohan 1992, judicial independence in Supreme Court Advocates‑on‑Record Association 2015). The doctrine also underpins the Court’s power to strike down constitutional amendments that violate the basic structure, a power unique among major democracies. For aspirants, the key is to remember the chronological evolution, the majority‑minority split in Kesavananda, and the distinction between “amending power” (Article 368) and “constituent power” (the power to make a new Constitution).

Judicial Review and Landmark Supreme Court Judgments

Judicial review in India derives from Articles 13, 32 and 226. Article 13 declares that any law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights is void to the extent of the inconsistency. Article 32 provides a guaranteed remedy — the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights — while Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs for any purpose, including Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court has expanded the scope of judicial review through a series of landmark judgments that shape administrative law, federal relations, and individual liberty.

Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967) held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights, treating them as “transcendental”. Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) overruled Golaknath partially, establishing the basic structure doctrine. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) broadened Article 21, ruling that “procedure established by law” must be fair, just and reasonable, thereby importing due‑process concepts. Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980) struck down clauses of the Forty‑Second Amendment that curtailed judicial review and gave primacy to Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights, reaffirming the balance as part of the basic structure. S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) laid down strict guidelines for the imposition of President’s Rule under Article 356, making the proclamation subject to judicial scrutiny. Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) formulated guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace, illustrating the Court’s power to legislate in a vacuum. Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) decriminalised consensual homosexual acts, reading down Section 377 IPC as violative of Articles 14, 15, 19 and 21. Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017) recognised the right to privacy as a fundamental right intrinsic to life and liberty.

These cases illustrate three recurring examiner themes: (1) the evolution from a literal to a purposive interpretation of Fundamental Rights; (2) the interplay between judicial review and constitutional amendments; (3) the Court’s willingness to fill legislative gaps through “judicial legislation” while insisting on constitutional morality. Aspirants should be able to match each case to the specific Article(s) invoked, the constitutional principle affirmed, and the subsequent legislative or policy response.

Five‑Year Plans, NITI Aayog and the Shift to Strategic Planning

From 1951 to 2014 India followed a series of Five‑Year Plans drafted by the Planning Commission, a body chaired by the Prime Minister. The First Plan (1951‑56) prioritised agriculture and irrigation; the Second (1956‑61) emphasised heavy industry under the Mahalanobis model; the Third (1961‑66) sought self‑reliance but was disrupted by wars and drought. The Fourth (1969‑74) introduced the “Garibi Hatao” thrust, while the Fifth (1974‑79) focused on poverty alleviation and employment. The Sixth (1980‑85) and Seventh (1985‑90) plans stressed infrastructure and human resource development. The Eighth Plan (1992‑97) coincided with the 1991 economic reforms, shifting emphasis to liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation. The Ninth (1997‑2002) and Tenth (2002‑07) plans targeted growth with social justice, introducing the “National Rural Employment Guarantee” concept. The Eleventh (2007‑12) aimed at “faster, sustainable and more inclusive growth”, and the Twelfth (2012‑17) set a 9 % growth target with a focus on health, education and skill development.

In 2015 the Planning Commission was replaced by the National Institution for Transforming India (NITI Aayog). Unlike its predecessor, NITI Aayog does not allocate financial resources; it functions as a policy think‑tank, promoting cooperative federalism through the Governing Council (Chief Ministers and Lieutenant Governors) and specialised verticals (e.g., Health, Education, Agriculture). It prepares a 15‑year Vision Document, a 7‑year Strategy Paper and a 3‑year Action Agenda, moving from rigid plan targets to outcome‑oriented monitoring. Key initiatives include the Aspirational Districts Programme, the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) localisation framework, and the “Strategy for New India @75”. The shift reflects a broader governance philosophy: decentralised planning, evidence‑based policy, and competitive federalism where states benchmark against each other.

For examination purposes, remember the chronological order of plans, the distinguishing focus of each, and the structural differences between the Planning Commission (allocation authority, top‑down) and NITI Aayog (advisory, bottom‑up). Questions often ask which plan introduced a specific scheme (e.g., Mid‑Day Meal in the Ninth Plan) or the constitutional basis of NITI Aayog (executive resolution, not a statutory body).

Monsoon Dynamics, Variability and Climate Change Implications for Agriculture

The Indian summer monsoon (June‑September) supplies roughly 75 % of the country’s annual rainfall. Its onset is driven by the differential heating of the Indian landmass and the surrounding oceans, creating a low‑pressure trough that draws moisture‑laden winds from the southwest. The monsoon progresses in two branches — the Arabian Sea branch and the Bay of Bengal branch — converging over the Indo‑Gangetic plain. Intra‑seasonal oscillations (active and break spells) are modulated by the Madden‑Julian Oscillation and the boreal summer intraseasonal oscillation.

Inter‑annual variability is strongly linked to the El Niño‑Southern Oscillation (ENSO). El Niño events (warm Pacific) tend to suppress monsoon rainfall, while La Niña (cool Pacific) enhances it. The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) provides a secondary modulator; a positive IOD can offset an El Niño‑induced deficit. The India Meteorological Department (IMD) issues long‑range forecasts using statistical and dynamical models, updated monthly. Since 2021 IMD has adopted a multi‑model ensemble (MME) approach, improving probabilistic skill.

Climate change projections (IPCC AR6) indicate a likely increase in monsoon mean rainfall by 5‑10 % by 2100 under high‑emission scenarios, but with greater intensity of extreme events — more heavy‑rain days and longer dry spells. Rising temperatures increase evapotranspiration, stressing rain‑fed crops (rice, millets, pulses). Adaptation measures promoted under the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture include drought‑tolerant varieties, micro‑irrigation, weather‑based crop insurance (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana), and agro‑meteorological advisories via the Kisan Suvidha app. Understanding the physical drivers (ENSO, IOD, MJO) and the policy response chain (forecast → advisory → insurance) is essential for questions linking geography, economy and science.

India’s Nuclear Doctrine, Strategic Forces and International Agreements

India’s nuclear doctrine, articulated in the 1999 Draft Nuclear Doctrine and reiterated in the 2003 official statement, rests on three pillars: (1) Credible Minimum Deterrence — maintaining a sufficient but not excessive arsenal; (2) No First Use (NFU) — nuclear weapons will only be used in retaliation against a nuclear attack on Indian territory or forces; (3) Civilian Control — the Nuclear Command Authority (NCA), chaired by the Prime Minister, authorises any nuclear launch. The Strategic Forces Command (SFC), established in 2003, operationalises the NCA’s directives, managing land‑based (Agni‑I to Agni‑V), sea‑based (K‑15/K‑4 SLBMs on Arihant‑class SSBNs) and air‑delivered (Mirage‑2000, Rafale, Su‑30MKI) delivery systems.

India conducted its first peaceful nuclear explosion in 1974 (Pokhran‑I) and a series of weapon tests in 1998 (Pokhran‑II), after which it declared a voluntary moratorium on further testing. The 2008 India‑US Civil Nuclear Agreement (123 Agreement) secured a waiver from the Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG), enabling civilian nuclear trade while keeping strategic programmes outside safeguards. India is not a signatory to the Nuclear Non‑Proliferation Treaty (NPT) or the Comprehensive Nuclear‑Test‑Ban Treaty (CTBT), arguing that these treaties are discriminatory. It participates in the Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR) as an adherent since 2016 and the Wassenaar Arrangement since 2017, enhancing export‑control credibility.

Recent developments include the successful test of the Agni‑V (range >5,000 km) with Multiple Independently Targetable Re‑entry Vehicle (MIRV) technology, the induction of the second Arihant‑class SSBN (INS Arighaat), and the development of the hypersonic technology demonstrator vehicle (HSTDV). The doctrine’s NFU commitment has been debated; the 2022 Defence Minister’s statement that “circumstances may warrant a review” signals strategic ambiguity without formal policy change. For exams, focus on the doctrinal triad, the command‑control architecture (NCA → SFC), the distinction between civilian and strategic programmes, and the key international agreements (123 Agreement, NSG waiver, MTCR, Wassenaar).

Parliamentary Committees: Structure, Powers and Oversight Role

Parliamentary committees are the engine rooms of legislative scrutiny. They are constituted under the Rules of Procedure of each House and derive their authority from Article 105 (privileges) and Article 118 (rules of procedure). Broadly, committees fall into two categories: Standing Committees (permanent, reconstituted each Lok Sabha) and Ad hoc Committees (formed for a specific purpose and dissolved after reporting). The most influential standing committees are the three financial committees — Public Accounts Committee (PAC), Estimates Committee, and Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU) — and the 24 Department‑Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) created in 1993 to shadow each ministry.

The PAC examines the Comptroller and Auditor General’s (CAG) audit reports on government expenditure, ensuring that money voted by Parliament has been spent for the authorised purpose. Its recommendations are not binding but carry significant moral weight; the government must submit an Action Taken Report (ATR) within six months. The Estimates Committee scrutinises the budgetary estimates of ministries, suggesting economies and improvements in administrative efficiency. COPU reviews the reports of the CAG on public sector undertakings, focusing on commercial viability and autonomy.

DRSCs examine bills, annual reports, and demand for grants of their respective ministries. They can summon officials, call for papers, and hear experts. Their reports are placed before both Houses, and the government replies through a Statement of Action Taken. Since 2004, the practice of referring all major bills (except Money Bills) to the relevant DRSC has become the norm, giving committees de facto legislative veto power through delay and amendment suggestions.

Committee membership reflects party strength in the House, but the Chairperson of PAC is conventionally from the opposition (since 1967), reinforcing accountability. Quorum is one‑third of the total membership; decisions are by majority, with the Chairperson having a casting vote. Committees meet in camera, and their proceedings are privileged — no court can question them. This confidentiality encourages candid testimony from civil servants.

Recent reforms include the introduction of e‑Committee portals for paperless functioning, live‑streaming of select committee meetings (since 2021), and the mandatory referral of all Ordinances to the concerned DRSC within 45 days of promulgation. The 2023 revision of the Lok Sabha Rules also empowered the Speaker to constitute Joint Parliamentary Committees (JPCs) on matters of national importance without a separate motion, expediting inquiries such as the 2023 JPC on the Personal Data Protection Bill.

Election Commission of India: Constitutional Mandate, Powers and Electoral Reforms

The Election Commission of India (ECI) is a permanent, independent constitutional body established under Article 324. It consists of the Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and such number of Election Commissioners as the President may fix — currently two. Appointments are made by the President on the advice of the Council of Ministers; the CEC enjoys security of tenure and can be removed only through a process akin to that of a Supreme Court judge (proved misbehaviour or incapacity, resolved by both Houses with special majority). This insulation underpins the Commission’s autonomy.

The ECI’s core functions are: (1) preparation and periodic revision of electoral rolls; (2) delimitation of constituencies (through the Delimitation Commission, whose orders are final and cannot be questioned in any court); (3) notification of election schedules; (4) recognition of political parties and allotment of symbols; (5) enforcement of the Model Code of Conduct (MCC) from the announcement of elections until the declaration of results; and (6) adjudication of disputes relating to splits/mergers of recognised parties under the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968.

The MCC, though not statutorily backed, has acquired the force of law through consistent judicial endorsement (e.g., S. Subramaniam Balaji v. State of Tamil Nadu, 2013). It restricts the ruling party from announcing new schemes, making ad‑hoc appointments, or using official machinery for campaigning. Violations attract censure, directions to stop the impugned activity, and in extreme cases, the Commission can recommend the President to disqualify a candidate under Article 324 read with the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

Key electoral reforms since 2010 include: the introduction of None of the Above (NOTA) on Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs) following the Supreme Court directive in People’s Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2013); mandatory Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail (VVPAT) for every EVM, with a statutory requirement to count VVPAT slips of five randomly selected polling stations per assembly constituency (Election Commission (Amendment) Rules, 2019); the Electoral Bonds Scheme (2018) for anonymous political funding, later struck down by the Supreme Court in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2024) as violative of the right to information under Article 19(1)(a); and the linking of Aadhaar with electoral rolls (voluntary) under the Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 to curb duplicate entries.

The Commission also oversees the Systematic Voters’ Education and Electoral Participation (SVEEP) programme, the National Voters’ Service Portal (NVSP) for online registration, and the cVIGIL mobile app for citizens to report MCC violations with geo‑tagged evidence. In 2022, the ECI piloted remote voting for migrant workers using a multi‑constituency EVM prototype, a step towards inclusive franchise.

Examiner's technique: Comparative constitutional design
Which of the following statements correctly distinguishes the removal process of the Chief Election Commissioner from that of a Supreme Court judge?
1. Both require a special majority in each House of Parliament.
2. The CEC can be removed by the President on the recommendation of the Prime Minister alone.
3. A Supreme Court judge can be removed only after an address by each House supported by a majority of the total membership and a two‑thirds majority of members present and voting; the CEC requires the same procedure.
4. The CEC’s removal does not require parliamentary approval.
Correct answer: 3. Article 324(5) provides that the CEC shall not be removed except in the manner and on the grounds prescribed for a Supreme Court judge under Article 124(4). That procedure is an address by each House of Parliament supported by a majority of the total membership of that House and by a majority of not less than two‑thirds of the members present and voting. Therefore the removal process is identical. Statement 1 is incomplete because it omits the “total membership” requirement. Statement 2 is false; the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but the constitutional procedure must be followed. Statement 4 is false.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks about the “independence” of a constitutional body, map the three pillars: (a) appointment process, (b) security of tenure/removal procedure, (c) financial autonomy (charged expenditure on Consolidated Fund). For the ECI, all three are constitutionally guaranteed — appointment by President, removal like a Supreme Court judge, and expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (Art. 324(5) read with Art. 110). Memorise this triad; it appears in polity‑economy crossover MCQs.

Union Budget Process: Constitutional Provisions, Stages and Fiscal Federalism

The Union Budget is the annual financial statement mandated by Article 112. It comprises the Annual Financial Statement (estimates of receipts and expenditure), the Demands for Grants (ministry‑wise expenditure proposals), the Appropriation Bill (authorising withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund), and the Finance Bill (giving effect to tax proposals). The budget cycle begins with the Budget Circular (September) issued by the Ministry of Finance, followed by pre‑budget consultations with states, industry, and stakeholders.

In Parliament, the budget passes through six stages: (1) Presentation by the Finance Minister (usually 1 February); (2) General Discussion (no voting, only debate on macro‑fiscal stance); (3) Departmental Consideration by the 24 DRSCs, which examine each ministry’s Demands for Grants and submit reports within a month; (4) Voting on Demands for Grants — Lok Sabha votes on each demand; Rajya Sabha only discusses; (5) Appropriation Bill — passed after voting, certifies the total amount authorised; (6) Finance Bill — must be passed within 75 days of presentation (Article 117). The Finance Bill is a Money Bill; Rajya Sabha can only recommend amendments, which Lok Sabha may accept or reject.

Fiscal federalism is operationalised through the Finance Commission (Article 280), constituted every five years to recommend the vertical share of central taxes to states and the horizontal distribution criteria (population, area, forest cover, income distance, demographic performance, tax effort). The 15th Finance Commission (2021‑26) retained the 41% vertical share but introduced a 1.5% performance‑based incentive for states meeting reform milestones. The GST Council (Article 279A) — a constitutional body with the Union Finance Minister as Chair and state finance ministers as members — decides GST rates, exemptions, and dispute resolution. Decisions require a three‑fourths weighted majority (Centre 1/3, states 2/3), ensuring consensus.

Since 2017‑18, the government has introduced Outcome Budgeting alongside the traditional line‑item budget. Each ministry presents an Outcome Budget linking financial outlays to measurable outputs (e.g., kilometres of highway constructed, number of households electrified). The Public Financial Management System (PFMS) tracks fund flow down to the last implementing agency, enabling real‑time monitoring of utilisation certificates. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003 (amended 2018) mandates a fiscal deficit target of 3% of GDP by 2020‑21 (later relaxed due to COVID‑19) and a debt‑to‑GDP ceiling of 40% for the Centre and 20% for states combined.

Environmental Governance: Legal Framework, Institutions and International Commitments

India’s environmental regime rests on a cluster of statutes enacted under Article 253 (legislation for implementing international agreements) and the Directive Principles (Article 48A, 51A(g)). The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 is the umbrella law empowering the Central Government to take all measures necessary for protecting and improving environmental quality. It authorises the setting of emission and effluent standards, regulation of hazardous substances, and the establishment of environmental laboratories.

Sector‑specific laws include the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 and the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981, which created the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs). The Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980 mandates prior Central Government approval for diversion of forest land for non‑forest purposes. The Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972 (amended 2006, 2022) schedules species, declares protected areas (national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, conservation reserves, community reserves), and establishes the National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) and the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau (WCCB).

Institutional architecture: the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC) is the nodal ministry; the National Green Tribunal (NGT), established under the NGT Act, 2010, provides specialised environmental adjudication with original and appellate jurisdiction, applying the principles of sustainable development, precautionary principle, and polluter pays. The Central Zoo Authority, Animal Welfare Board, and Coastal Zone Management Authorities operate under specific statutes.

Internationally, India is a party to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), the Kyoto Protocol (first commitment period), the Paris Agreement (ratified 2016), the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), the United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD), and the Basel, Rotterdam and Stockholm Conventions on hazardous wastes and chemicals. Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) under Paris commit India to: (i) reduce emissions intensity of GDP by 45% by 2030 from 2005 levels; (ii) achieve 50% cumulative electric power installed capacity from non‑fossil sources by 2030; (iii) create an additional carbon sink of 2.5‑3 GtCO₂e through forest and tree cover.

Recent policy instruments: the National Clean Air Programme (NCAP) (2019) targets 20‑30% reduction in PM2.5/PM10 by 2024 in 132 non‑attainment cities; the Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2016 (amended 2022) mandate Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) for plastic packaging; the Battery Waste Management Rules, 2022 introduce EPR for batteries; and the Green Credit Programme (2023) creates a market‑based mechanism for voluntary environmental actions.

Science and Technology Institutional Framework: Missions, Agencies and Innovation Metrics

The governance of scientific research in India is distributed across multiple ministries and autonomous bodies. The Department of Science and Technology (DST) under the Ministry of Science and Technology formulates policy, administers extramural research grants (SERB, INSPIRE), and coordinates national missions. The Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR), an autonomous society registered under the Societies Registration Act, runs 37 national laboratories covering aerospace, chemicals, mining, leather, and food technology. The Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO), under the Department of Space, executes launch vehicle programmes (PSLV, GSLV, LVM3), satellite constellations (NavIC, Cartosat, RISAT), and interplanetary missions (Chandrayaan, Mangalyaan, Aditya‑L1).

The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO), under the Department of Defence R&D, develops strategic systems (Agni, Prithvi, BrahMos, ASTRA, Nag) and dual‑use technologies. The Department of Biotechnology (DBT) and the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) spearhead life‑sciences research, vaccine development (COVAXIN, ZyCoV‑D), and public health surveillance. The Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) oversees nuclear power (NPCIL), fuel cycle (UCIL, NFC), and advanced reactors (PFBR, AHWR).

Since 2017, the government has launched National Missions to concentrate resources on frontier areas: (1) National Mission on Interdisciplinary Cyber‑Physical Systems (NM‑ICPS) — 25 Technology Innovation Hubs; (2) National Quantum Mission (NQM) (approved 2023, ₹6,000 crore, 2023‑31) targeting quantum computing, communication, sensing, and materials; (3) National Supercomputing Mission (NSM) — deploying 70+ petaflop systems (PARAM series) across academic institutions; (4) National AI Mission (announced 2024) focusing on foundational models, compute infrastructure, and skilling; (5) Deep Ocean Mission — manned submersible (MATSYA 6000), seabed mineral exploration, and marine biotechnology.

Innovation performance is tracked through the Global Innovation Index (GII) (India ranked 40th in 2023, up from 81st in 2015), the Nature Index (share of high‑quality publications), and the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) filings (India filed 2,500+ PCT applications in 2023). The Atal Innovation Mission (AIM) under NITI Aayog establishes Atal Tinkering Labs in schools and Atal Incubation Centres in universities. The Start‑up India initiative (2016) provides tax holidays, self‑certification, and a Fund of Funds (₹10,000 crore) managed by SIDBI.

Technology transfer is facilitated by the National Research Development Corporation (NRDC) and the Technology Development Board (TDB), which finance commercialisation of indigenous technologies. The Science, Technology and Innovation Policy (STIP) 2020 (draft) envisions a “dual‑track” approach: strengthening basic research while aligning with national priorities (energy security, health, agriculture, strategic autonomy).

  • Confusing the removal procedure of the CEC with that of a Supreme Court judge — both require the same special majority, but the CEC’s removal is governed by Article 324(5), not Article 124(4) directly.
  • Assuming the Finance Bill can be amended by Rajya Sabha — as a Money Bill, Rajya Sabha may only recommend amendments; Lok Sabha has final say.
  • Treating the National Green Tribunal as a constitutional body — it is a statutory tribunal under the NGT Act, 2010, not a constitutional court.
  • Believing the GST Council decides by simple majority — decisions need a three‑fourths weighted vote (Centre 1/3, states 2/3).
  • Mixing up the vertical share of taxes recommended by the Finance Commission with the actual devolution — the Centre may accept, modify, or reject the recommendation; the final devolution is effected through a Presidential order.
  • Attributing the launch of the National Quantum Mission to ISRO — it is steered by DST with participation from multiple agencies, not ISRO alone.
  • Overlooking that the Model Code of Conduct is not a statute — its enforceability derives from the Election Commission’s constitutional powers under Article 324 and judicial endorsement.

Inter‑State Council and Zonal Councils: Structure, Functions and Recent Meetings

The Inter‑State Council (ISC) derives its constitutional mandate from Article 263, which empowers the President to establish a body for investigating and discussing subjects of common interest between the Union and the States. The Council was first constituted in 1990 on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission. Its composition includes the Prime Minister as Chairman, the Chief Ministers of all States, the Chief Ministers of Union Territories with legislatures, and six Union Cabinet Ministers nominated by the Prime Minister. The Council meets at least thrice a year, though in practice the frequency has varied; the 12th meeting was held in July 2023 after a gap of six years, focusing on issues such as GST compensation, water disputes, and the implementation of the National Education Policy.

Zonal Councils, by contrast, are statutory bodies created under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956. Five Zonal Councils — Northern, Central, Eastern, Western and Southern — each comprise the Union Home Minister (Chairman), the Chief Ministers of the States in the zone, two other Ministers from each State, and the Administrator of each Union Territory in the zone. The North‑Eastern Council, established in 1971 under a separate Act, functions similarly for the eight north‑eastern States. Zonal Councils meet at least twice a year; the 24th meeting of the Northern Zonal Council in November 2022 deliberated on drug trafficking, border infrastructure and power sector reforms.

Both forums are advisory; their recommendations are not binding. However, they serve as institutionalised platforms for cooperative federalism, allowing States to raise grievances directly with the Centre. The ISC’s Standing Committee, chaired by the Union Home Minister, processes agenda items between plenary sessions. Zonal Councils have sector‑specific sub‑committees (e.g., on water, power, transport) that prepare detailed reports. The 2023 ISC meeting produced a consensus on a time‑bound framework for resolving inter‑State river water disputes through the proposed River Water Tribunal, illustrating how these bodies can catalyse legislative action.

Examiners often test the distinction between the constitutional origin of the ISC and the statutory origin of Zonal Councils, the chairmanship (Prime Minister vs Home Minister), and the frequency of meetings. A typical twist is to ask which body can discuss “subjects of common interest” under Article 263 — the answer is the Inter‑State Council, not the Zonal Councils.

National Human Rights Commission and State Human Rights Commissions: Powers, Limitations and Landmark Cases

The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) was established on 12 October 1993 under the Protection of Human Rights Act (PHRA), 1993, in conformity with the Paris Principles. The Chairperson must be a former Chief Justice of India or a former Supreme Court judge; the other members include one serving or retired Supreme Court judge, one serving or retired Chief Justice of a High Court, and two persons having knowledge or practical experience in human rights. The President appoints them on the recommendation of a high‑powered committee comprising the Prime Minister, the Home Minister, the Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha, the Speaker of Lok Sabha, and the Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha.

The NHRC can inquire suo motu or on a petition into violations of human rights by public servants, intervene in court proceedings involving human rights, and recommend compensation or prosecution. Its recommendations are advisory; the government must respond within one month. A critical limitation is that the NHRC cannot investigate allegations against the armed forces — Section 19 of the PHRA bars such inquiries, a provision upheld by the Supreme Court in NHRC v. State of Gujarat (2009). The Commission also lacks enforcement powers; compliance depends on political will.

State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs) mirror the NHRC at the State level, constituted under Section 21 of the PHRA. As of 2024, 26 States have functional SHRCs. Their jurisdiction is confined to matters listed in the State List and Concurrent List. The Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Madhukar Narayan (2019) clarified that SHRCs cannot entertain complaints already pending before the NHRC, preventing parallel proceedings.

Landmark interventions include the NHRC’s 2002 report on custodial deaths in Gujarat, which led to the prosecution of several police officers, and its 2020 advisory on the rights of migrant workers during the COVID‑19 lockdown. The Commission’s annual reports, tabled in Parliament, are a key source for UPSC‑type questions on human rights jurisprudence.

Worked Example: NHRC Jurisdiction Over Armed Forces
A question asks: “Can the NHRC investigate a complaint alleging human rights violations by the Indian Army in a counter‑insurgency operation?” Choose the correct statement.
Under Section 19 of the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, the NHRC is expressly barred from inquiring into any matter relating to the armed forces. The Supreme Court in NHRC v. State of Gujarat (2009) upheld this statutory exclusion. Therefore, the NHRC cannot investigate such a complaint; the appropriate forum would be a court‑martial or a writ petition under Article 32/226. The correct answer is that the NHRC has no jurisdiction over the armed forces.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the composition of the NHRC and the high‑powered selection committee; a single‑mark question often asks who recommends the Chairperson. For SHRCs, remember the 26‑State figure and the bar on parallel proceedings. When a question mentions “armed forces”, instantly recall Section 19 PHRA — it is a favourite trap.

Central Vigilance Commission, Lokpal and Lokayuktas: Institutional Design and Operational Challenges

The Central Vigilance Commission (CVC) was set up in 1964 by an executive resolution and later given statutory status through the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003. It consists of a Central Vigilance Commissioner and up to two Vigilance Commissioners, appointed by the President on the recommendation of a three‑member committee comprising the Prime Minister, the Home Minister and the Leader of Opposition in Lok Sabha. The CVC exercises superintendence over the vigilance administration of all Central Government departments, public sector undertakings and autonomous bodies. It can conduct inquiries, recommend disciplinary action, and advise on systemic improvements. However, it lacks prosecutorial powers; cases are referred to the CBI or departmental authorities.

The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, enacted after decades of debate, establishes the Lokpal at the Centre and mandates States to set up Lokayuktas. The Lokpal comprises a Chairperson (former Chief Justice of India or Supreme Court judge) and up to eight members, half of whom must be judicial. Jurisdiction covers the Prime Minister (with safeguards), Ministers, MPs, Group A‑D officers, and entities receiving foreign contributions above a threshold. The Lokpal can order preliminary inquiry, investigation (through its own wing or the CBI), and prosecution. A notable operational challenge is the requirement of a two‑thirds majority in the Lokpal bench to proceed against the Prime Minister, which has never been tested.

State Lokayuktas vary widely in strength. Karnataka’s Lokayukta, once considered a model, lost its suo motu powers after a 2015 amendment, while Maharashtra’s Lokayukta retains broad jurisdiction over the Chief Minister. The Supreme Court in Common Cause v. Union of India (2018) directed States to appoint Lokayuktas within a fixed timeline, yet several States remain non‑compliant as of 2024.

Key exam points: the CVC’s advisory nature versus the Lokpal’s quasi‑judicial powers; the composition of the selection committees (CVC vs Lokpal); the exclusion of the armed forces from both bodies; and the distinction between “vigilance” (preventive) and “corruption investigation” (punitive).

Right to Information Act, 2005: Exemptions, Appeals and Judicial Interpretation

The Right to Information Act (RTI), 2005, operationalises the fundamental right to information under Article 19(1)(a). It obliges every public authority to designate Public Information Officers (PIOs) and Appellate Authorities, and to proactively disclose certain categories of information. The Act provides a two‑tier appeal mechanism: first appeal to the departmental Appellate Authority within 30 days, and second appeal to the Central Information Commission (CIC) or State Information Commissions (SICs) within 90 days.

Section 8 enumerates ten exemption categories, including information affecting sovereignty and security (8(1)(a)), strategic scientific or economic interests (8(1)(b)), commercial confidence (8(1)(d)), and personal privacy (8(1)(j)). Section 9 bars disclosure of information that would infringe copyright. The “public interest override” in Section 8(2) allows disclosure if the public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interest. The Supreme Court in Central Board of Secondary Education v. Aditya Bandopadhyay (2011) held that the override applies only when the information sought is not covered by an absolute exemption.

Judicial interpretation has shaped the scope of “public authority”. In Namit Sharma v. Union of India (2013), the Court ruled that private bodies performing public functions (e.g., aided schools) fall under the Act. Conversely, in Board of Control for Cricket in India v. CIC (2019), the Court held that the BCCI, despite its public character, is not a public authority because it is not created by statute or Constitution.

Recent amendments (2019) empowered the Central Government to prescribe the tenure, salary and conditions of service of Information Commissioners, raising concerns about independence. The CIC’s 2022 annual report noted a pendency of over 30,000 second appeals, highlighting capacity constraints.

National Disaster Management Authority and State Disaster Management Authorities: Legal Framework, Funding and Coordination Mechanisms

The Disaster Management Act, 2005, creates a three‑tier institutional architecture. At the apex, the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) is chaired by the Prime Minister and comprises up to nine members, including the Vice‑Chairperson (a Cabinet Minister). The NDMA formulates policies, plans and guidelines, and coordinates response during “calamities of severe nature”. The National Executive Committee (NEC), headed by the Union Home Secretary, assists the NDMA in implementation.

Each State establishes a State Disaster Management Authority (SDMA) chaired by the Chief Minister, with the Chief Secretary as the ex‑officio member‑secretary. District Disaster Management Authorities (DDMAs) are chaired by the District Collector. The Act mandates the preparation of National, State and District Disaster Management Plans, updated every five years.

Funding flows through the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) and State Disaster Response Funds (SDRFs). The NDRF is financed by a cess on certain goods and services (the “National Calamity Contingent Duty”) and by budgetary allocations; the SDRFs receive contributions from the Centre (75% for general category States, 90% for special category States) and the States (25%/10%). The 15th Finance Commission recommended a revised allocation formula linking fund release to disaster risk index and preparedness metrics.

Coordination during a disaster follows the Incident Response System (IRS), adapted from the US Incident Command System. The NDMA activates the National Crisis Management Committee (NCMC) for inter‑ministerial coordination, while the SDMA activates the State Crisis Management Group. The 2021 amendment to the Act introduced the concept of “Disaster Mitigation Fund” at both national and State levels, earmarked for pre‑disaster risk reduction projects.

Exam‑relevant details: the chairmanship of NDMA and SDMA, the composition of NDRF/SDRF, the role of the NEC, the distinction between “response” (post‑event) and “mitigation” (pre‑event), and the 2021 amendment introducing mitigation funds.

  • Believing the Lokpal can directly prosecute the Prime Minister without the two‑thirds bench requirement.
  • Overlooking the public‑interest override in RTI Section 8(2) and applying it to absolute exemptions.
  • Mixing up the funding ratios of NDRF and SDRF (75:25 vs 90:10) for general and special category States.
  • Thinking the NDMA’s mitigation fund existed before the 2021 amendment; it was introduced only then.
