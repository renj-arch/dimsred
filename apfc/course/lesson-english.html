1. The English section pattern

APFC English carries 20 questions at a class-10-to-graduate level. The recurring families:

  1. Error spotting / sentence improvement (parts A–D marked) — 4–6 questions
  2. Fill in the blanks (single and double) — 4–5
  3. Para jumbles / sentence arrangement — 2–3
  4. Reading comprehension (one short passage, 3–5 questions) — 3–5
  5. Vocabulary in context: synonyms/antonyms, idioms, one-word substitution — 3–4
  6. Active-passive / narration / correction of sentences — 2–3

Key insight: almost every question type has a mechanical method. Error spotting is checklist-driven; fillers are collocation-driven; comprehension is question-driven. You are not being tested on how well you speak English — you are being tested on whether you can apply rules under time pressure.

2. Grammar rules that get asked

Subject-verb agreement

  • Subject and verb agree in number — ignore phrases in between: "The list of items is on the desk" (list is, not items).
  • "Each/every/either/neither/no one" → singular verb.
  • "Neither…nor" / "either…or" → verb agrees with the nearer subject.
  • "The number of" → singular; "A number of" → plural.
  • "Many a" + singular noun → singular verb.
  • Collective nouns: singular when acting as one unit ("The committee has decided").

Tenses and time markers

  • "Since" + point of time → present perfect: "I have lived here since 2010."
  • "For" + duration → present perfect: "for ten years".
  • "When/while" clauses: past continuous for ongoing action, simple past for the interrupting action.
  • "No sooner…than", "Hardly…when", "Scarcely…when" — fixed correlative pairs.

Prepositions and collocations

APFC loves fixed preposition pairs. Memorise the high-frequency ones:

Modifiers and parallelism

  • A modifier must sit next to what it modifies: "Running down the street, the bus hit him" is wrong — the bus wasn't running.
  • Parallel structures in lists: "reading, writing and to swim" → "swimming".
  • "Not only… but also" keeps both parts parallel.

3. Error spotting method

The sentence is split into parts A–D (sometimes E for "no error"). Apply this checklist in order — most errors are caught in the first three steps:

  1. Subject-verb agreement: find the true subject, check the verb's number.
  2. Tense consistency: does the timeline match the time markers?
  3. Preposition/collocation: does the fixed pair match?
  4. Article: a/an/the — check first mention (a), unique things (the sun), superlatives (the best).
  5. Pronoun: case (he/him), agreement, no ambiguous "it/they".
  6. Modifier placement and parallelism.
  7. Word class: adjective vs adverb ("she sings beautiful" → beautifully).
Worked example
"Neither of the two candidates (A) / have submitted (B) / their application forms (C) / before the deadline (D)."
Solution: Error in B — "Neither" is singular → "has submitted", not "have". Classic trap: the plural "candidates" sits between subject and verb.
Pro Tip
If a part reads perfectly grammatical but feels "off", check article and preposition next — those are the two most common error classes after agreement. And remember: "No error" is a legitimate answer appearing in ~10–15% of questions — don't force an error.

4. Fillers, jumbles and comprehension

Fill in the blanks

  • Read the whole sentence first — the second blank often disambiguates the first.
  • For double blanks, test each option pair in both slots; eliminate pairs that break grammar in either.
  • Collocation is king: "make a decision", "take responsibility", "heavy rain" — the wrong option is usually grammatically fine but collocationally wrong.
  • Connotation: if the passage is formal/official, prefer the formal word ("assist" over "help", "purchase" over "buy").

Para jumbles

  • Find the opening sentence: it introduces a topic without depending on prior context — usually has no pronoun pointing backward.
  • Link sentences by pronoun references ("this policy", "these findings") and connecting words (however, therefore, moreover).
  • Chronological and cause→effect logic resolves most chains.

Reading comprehension

  • Read the questions first, then scan the passage for keywords — saves time on long passages.
  • Answers are almost always paraphrases of passage lines, not verbatim — match meaning, not words.
  • "According to the passage" questions: the answer must be in the passage; outside knowledge is a trap.
  • Inference questions: choose the option that is necessarily true, not merely plausible.

5. Examiner traps

Traps that cost marks
  • "Prefer than": always wrong — prefer X to Y.
  • "More better"/"most fastest": double comparatives — never correct.
  • "One of the + plural + singular verb": "One of the boys is" — the verb follows "one", not "boys".
  • Fewer vs less: fewer = countable, less = uncountable.
  • Between vs among: between = two, among = more than two.
  • Comprehension overreach: options that add claims not in the passage are wrong even if true in real life.
  • Active-passive voice changes meaning subtly: check who does what after conversion.

6. Practice plan

Daily (30 min): 10 error-spotting (untimed, checklist aloud) + 10 fillers + 5 para jumbles + one comprehension passage.

Weekly: one full English section (20 Q, 25 min) — track accuracy per family and drill the weakest.

Vocabulary: don't memorise word lists — learn words in collocations ("heavy rain", "strong coffee", "make amends"). The exam tests usage, not dictionary knowledge.
