1. Analogy

1.1 Understanding the Core Principle of Analogy

Analogy questions test the ability to perceive a relationship between two given elements and then to apply the same relationship to a new pair. The underlying principle is that the logical link — whether semantic, functional, structural, or numerical — remains invariant across the pairs. When a candidate recognises that “pen” is to “write” as “knife” is to “cut”, the relationship identified is “instrument to its primary function”. This recognition allows the examiner to assess whether the aspirant can abstract a rule from a concrete example and transfer it without being misled by superficial similarities.

In practice, the relationship may be expressed through synonyms, antonyms, cause‑effect, part‑whole, degree of intensity, or even mathematical operations such as “square of” or “half of”. The examiner deliberately varies the domain — words, numbers, letters, or symbols — so that the test taker must first classify the type of link before searching for the answer. A strong grasp of the core principle therefore reduces the cognitive load: once the rule is isolated, the candidate merely scans the options for the pair that mirrors it.

1.2 Types of Analogy Encountered in Examinations

Word analogies dominate the paper, but number analogies, letter analogies, and mixed analogies appear with regular frequency. In a word analogy the relationship can be categorical (dog : mammal :: rose : flower), functional (key : lock :: password : account), or based on grammatical transformation (sing : sang :: run : ran). Number analogies often involve arithmetic progressions, geometric progressions, or operations like “add 3, then multiply by 2”. Letter analogies may rely on positional shifts in the alphabet (A → C, B → D) or on pattern reversals (ABC : CBA :: XYZ : ZYX). Mixed analogies combine two domains, for example “3 : 9 :: C : I” where the numeric rule “multiply by three” is mirrored by the alphabetic rule “move three places forward”.

Each type demands a slightly different analytical lens. Word analogies require a vocabulary of relational concepts; number analogies demand fluency with basic arithmetic patterns; letter analogies test familiarity with alphabetical indices; mixed analogies force the candidate to translate a rule from one symbolic system to another. Recognising the type early prevents the wasteful attempt to force a semantic explanation onto a purely numeric pattern.

1.3 Strategies for Solving Analogy Questions Efficiently

The first step is to articulate the relationship in a concise phrase such as “first is the tool used for the second” or “second is the square of the first”. Writing this phrase mentally or on rough paper anchors the rule and guards against the temptation to match surface features. The second step is to test each option against the articulated rule, discarding any pair that violates even a single condition. When two options appear to satisfy the rule, the candidate should look for a finer distinction — perhaps the degree of specificity (e.g., “car : vehicle” versus “sedan : car”) or the directionality of the relation (cause‑effect versus effect‑cause).

Time management is critical: a well‑practised aspirant can isolate the rule in under ten seconds and evaluate four options in another fifteen. Regular exposure to a wide variety of analogy families builds a mental repository of common patterns, allowing instant pattern recognition. Finally, the habit of re‑reading the question stem after selecting an answer catches occasional mis‑readings such as confusing “analogy” with “classification” instructions.

2. Classification

2.1 The Logic Behind Grouping Items

Classification tasks present a set of items — words, numbers, figures, or symbols — and ask the examinee to identify the one that does not belong to the group formed by the others. The logic rests on the existence of a common attribute shared by the majority: it could be a semantic category (all are fruits), a structural property (all are prime numbers), a geometric feature (all are closed figures with four sides), or a functional role (all are input devices). The odd item either lacks the attribute entirely or possesses a contradictory attribute that makes it an outlier.

Understanding that the grouping principle is always singular — there is one defining characteristic — helps the candidate avoid the trap of multiple plausible groupings. For instance, in the set {apple, mango, carrot, banana} the common attribute “fruit” isolates carrot as the odd one, even though carrot also shares “edible” with the others. The examiner expects the test taker to select the most specific, defining attribute rather than a generic one.

2.2 Common Bases of Classification

Examiners draw from a repertoire of bases: biological taxonomy (mammal, reptile, bird), parts of speech (noun, verb, adjective), mathematical properties (even, odd, perfect square), physical states (solid, liquid, gas), and functional categories (input, output, storage). In letter‑based classification the basis may be symmetry (A, H, I, M, O, T, U, V, W, X, Y), position in the alphabet (vowels versus consonants), or shape (curved versus straight lines). Number‑based classification frequently uses divisibility rules, digit sums, or representation in different bases (binary, octal). Figure‑based classification relies on the number of sides, presence of curves, rotational symmetry, or shading patterns.

Familiarity with these bases enables rapid scanning: the candidate mentally tags each item with its most salient property and then looks for the tag that appears only once. When the set is large, constructing a quick table of attributes on rough paper can reveal the unique tag without exhaustive pairwise comparison.

2.3 Techniques to Identify the Odd One Out

A systematic technique is to list the observable attributes of each item side by side and then count the frequency of each attribute across the set. The attribute with a frequency of one points directly to the odd item. Another technique is to form a provisional group using the first three items, deduce the rule, and then test the remaining items against it. If the rule fails for an item, that item is the outlier. In cases where two competing rules seem plausible, the rule that explains a larger subset with fewer exceptions is preferred.

Practice with timed drills sharpens the ability to spot the defining attribute instantly. It is also useful to remember that examiners sometimes embed a “double odd” scenario where two items appear odd under different rules; the instruction “choose the one that does not belong” implies a single correct answer, so the candidate must identify the rule that the setter intended, usually the most straightforward or the one that aligns with the majority of items.

Example Question
Select the word that does not belong to the group: (a) Violin (b) Flute (c) Guitar (d) Drum
The common attribute among violin, flute, and guitar is that they are string or wind instruments capable of producing sustained melodic notes through vibration of strings or air columns. A drum, however, is a percussion instrument that produces sound by striking a membrane and does not sustain pitch in the same manner. Therefore, the odd one out is (d) Drum.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a classification set contains both living and non‑living items, the examiner often uses the living‑versus‑non‑living distinction as the primary rule; check this first before exploring finer categories.

3. Series

3.1 Recognising Patterns in Number Series

Number series questions present a sequence where each term follows a deterministic rule. The most frequent rules are arithmetic progression (constant difference), geometric progression (constant ratio), alternating operations (add, then multiply), and polynomial patterns (differences of differences become constant). Recognising the pattern begins with computing first‑order differences; if they are constant, the series is arithmetic. If not, computing second‑order differences often reveals a quadratic rule. When ratios of successive terms are constant, the series is geometric. In mixed series, the operation may change at regular intervals, such as “+2, ×3, +2, ×3”.

Advanced series may embed two interleaved sub‑series, for example 2, 5, 4, 8, 6, 11 where odd positions follow +2 and even positions follow +3. The candidate must separate the terms by position, detect the independent rules, and then predict the next term of the required sub‑series. Mastery of basic algebraic identities (n², n³, n²+n) accelerates the identification of polynomial patterns.

3.2 Letter and Symbol Series

Letter series operate on the alphabetical index (A=1, B=2, … Z=26). Common patterns include constant forward or backward shifts, skip patterns (skip one, skip two), reversal, and cyclic rotation (after Z comes A). Symbol series follow analogous visual rules: rotation by 90°, reflection, addition or removal of strokes, and alternation of shading. Because the alphabet is a closed loop of 26, modular arithmetic (mod 26) underlies many shift patterns; for instance, a shift of +3 from X yields A (24+3=27 ≡ 1).

When a series mixes letters and numbers, the rule often translates a numeric operation into an alphabetic shift. For example, 2, B, 4, D, 6, F shows the numeric term indicating the position of the following letter. Recognising such cross‑domain mapping requires the candidate to keep both representations active simultaneously.

3.3 Mixed and Alternating Series

Mixed series combine two or more distinct patterns within a single sequence. A typical construction is an alternating series where odd‑position terms follow one rule and even‑position terms follow another. Another variant is a “compound” series where each term is derived from the previous two (Fibonacci‑type) or from a combination of arithmetic and geometric steps. The key to solving these is to write the terms with their position indices, then test hypotheses for each subsequence independently.

Once the sub‑patterns are isolated, the candidate can project the required term. In examination conditions, it is advisable to limit the search to the first six terms; most patterns stabilise within that window. If no clear rule emerges, the candidate should consider the possibility of a “look‑and‑say” or “self‑describing” series, though these are rare in the APFC syllabus.

4. Coding‑Decoding

4.1 Fundamental Coding Mechanisms

Coding‑decoding questions replace each element of a message — letters, digits, or symbols — with another according to a fixed scheme. The simplest scheme is a Caesar shift, where every letter moves a constant number of places forward or backward in the alphabet. Another basic mechanism is letter‑to‑number substitution based on alphabetical position (A=1, B=2). In numeric coding, each digit may be transformed by a mathematical function such as “add 3, then reverse the digits”. Understanding that the coding rule is bijective (one‑to‑one) allows the candidate to reverse‑engineer the code by applying the inverse operation.

Examiners often provide a coded word and its plain counterpart, expecting the aspirant to deduce the rule and then encode or decode a new word. The presence of multiple examples in the same question strengthens the reliability of the inferred rule; a single example may admit several plausible rules, so the candidate must test each against all given pairs before committing.

4.2 Advanced Coding Patterns

Beyond simple shifts, advanced patterns include positional coding (the code for a letter depends on its position in the word), mirror coding (first letter coded by last letter’s rule), and matrix coding (a 5×5 Polybius square where each letter maps to a pair of coordinates). In matrix coding, the keyword determines the arrangement of the alphabet, and the candidate must reconstruct the matrix before decoding. Another sophisticated pattern is “letter‑pair swapping” where adjacent letters exchange places before a shift is applied.

These patterns demand careful bookkeeping: the candidate should write the plain alphabet, the coded alphabet, and any intermediate transformations in a table. The table makes it easy to spot inconsistencies and to apply the rule to the target word without mental overload.

4.3 Decoding Strategies and Practice

A robust decoding strategy begins with listing all given plain‑coded pairs, aligning them character by character, and noting the transformation for each position. If the transformation is uniform across positions, the rule is global; if it varies, the rule is positional. The next step is to hypothesise the mathematical or logical operation (addition, subtraction, multiplication, reversal, substitution) and verify it against every pair. Once verified, the candidate applies the inverse operation to the asked word.

Regular practice with timed sets builds an intuition for common rule families — shift, reverse, alternate, matrix — and reduces the time spent on hypothesis testing. It is also beneficial to memorise the standard Polybius square and the Atbash cipher (A↔Z, B↔Y) because they appear frequently in competitive papers.

5. Direction Sense

5.1 Cardinal and Inter‑Cardinal Directions

Direction sense questions describe a person’s movement from a starting point using cardinal directions (North, South, East, West) and inter‑cardinal directions (North‑East, South‑West, etc.). The fundamental concept is that each move can be represented as a vector on a two‑dimensional grid. North corresponds to +y, South to –y, East to +x, West to –x. Inter‑cardinal moves combine equal components on both axes (e.g., North‑East is +x, +y). By summing the vectors, the final coordinates relative to the origin are obtained, and the straight‑line distance is the Euclidean norm of the resultant vector.

When the problem asks only for the direction of the final point relative to the start, the candidate can compare the signs of the resultant x and y components: positive x and positive y imply North‑East, negative x and positive y imply North‑West, and so on. Mastery of this vector addition eliminates the need for drawing diagrams in many cases, saving valuable time.

5.2 Relative Movement and Turns

Many questions phrase movements in terms of “turn left”, “turn right”, “turn about”, or “face the opposite direction”. These are relative to the current facing direction, not to the fixed cardinal frame. The standard convention: a left turn rotates the facing direction 90° anticlockwise, a right turn 90° clockwise, and an about‑turn 180°. By maintaining a variable that stores the current facing direction (initially North unless stated), each instruction updates this variable before the next linear movement is applied.

A common pitfall is to treat “turn left” as a movement toward West regardless of the current heading. The correct method is to update the heading first, then apply the distance in the new heading. Practising a few scenarios where the person makes a sequence of turns and moves builds an internal simulation that works faster than sketching each step.

5.3 Solving Complex Route Problems

Complex routes may involve multiple persons starting from different points, meeting, and then moving together, or they may ask for the shortest distance between two moving points at a given time. The systematic approach is to break the narrative into discrete legs, assign a coordinate system, and compute the position of each entity after each leg. When two entities move simultaneously, their relative position can be tracked by subtracting one coordinate pair from the other.

If the question asks for the minimum distance between two moving points, the candidate can treat the relative motion as a single vector and find the point of closest approach using the concept of perpendicular distance from a point to a line, though in the APFC context the numbers are usually chosen so that the closest approach occurs at an integer time step. Drawing a quick schematic on rough paper, even a rough one, often reveals symmetries that simplify the arithmetic.

6. Blood Relations

6.1 Basic Terminology and Generational Mapping

Blood relation questions test the ability to translate verbal descriptions of family ties into a genealogical diagram. The core terminology includes father, mother, brother, sister, son, daughter, grandfather, grandmother, uncle, aunt, nephew, niece, cousin, and the in‑law variants (father‑in‑law, mother‑in‑law, brother‑in‑law, sister‑in‑law). Each term fixes a relative position in a generational grid: the vertical axis denotes generation (0 for the reference person, +1 for parents, –1 for children), and the horizontal axis denotes sibling order or marital links.

Mapping begins by placing the reference person at the centre. Statements such as “A is the brother of B” put A and B on the same generation level with a sibling link. “C is the father of A” moves C one generation up and connects vertically. “D is the wife of C” adds a marital link on the same generation as C. By iteratively applying each statement, a complete family tree emerges, from which any queried relationship can be read directly.

6.2 Solving Direct Relationship Questions

Direct questions ask “How is X related to Y?” after providing a chain of statements. The efficient method is to construct the tree only up to the two persons involved, ignoring extraneous relatives. Using symbols (M for male, F for female) and generation numbers helps keep the diagram compact. For example, “P is the son of Q; Q is the sister of R; R is the father of S” yields: Q and R are siblings (generation 0), P is generation –1 under Q, S is generation –1 under R. Thus P and S are cousins (same generation, parents are siblings). The answer “cousin” follows without drawing the entire extended family.

When gender is not specified for a person, the candidate must consider both possibilities and see whether the final relationship changes. In most APFC questions the gender is either given or irrelevant to the asked relation (e.g., “cousin” is gender‑neutral).

6.3 Handling Extended Family and In‑Law Scenarios

Extended scenarios introduce multiple marriages, step‑relations, and in‑law terms. The key principle is that an in‑law relation always arises through marriage, not blood. “Brother‑in‑law” can be the husband of one’s sister, the brother of one’s spouse, or the husband of one’s spouse’s sister. The candidate must trace the marital link first, then apply the blood relation on the other side. A systematic way is to label each person with a unique identifier, note blood edges (parent‑child, sibling) and marriage edges (spouse), and then compute the shortest path between the two query nodes using only the allowed edge types for the target relation.

Practice with varied family puzzles builds a mental library of common patterns: “A’s mother’s brother’s son” is always A’s cousin; “A’s father’s wife’s brother” is A’s uncle (or father if the wife is the mother). Recognising these shortcuts reduces the need for full tree construction under time pressure.
