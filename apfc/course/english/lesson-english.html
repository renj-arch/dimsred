Foundations of English Grammar

1.1 The Building Blocks of Grammar

Grammar provides the structural framework that allows words to combine into meaningful sentences. The primary building blocks are the eight parts of speech: noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, and interjection. Each part performs a distinct syntactic function; for example, nouns name entities while verbs express actions or states. Understanding the role of each part enables a learner to identify the subject, predicate, and modifiers in any sentence, which is the first step toward accurate error detection and sentence correction.

Morphology, the study of word formation, shows how roots, prefixes, and suffixes alter meaning and grammatical category. A root such as “act” becomes a noun “action” with the suffix “‑ion”, a verb “activate” with “‑ate”, and an adjective “active” with “‑ive”. Recognising these patterns helps in vocabulary expansion and in spotting errors where a word form does not match its syntactic slot.

1.2 Sentence Structure and Clause Types

A sentence is a group of words that expresses a complete thought and contains at least one independent clause. An independent clause has a subject and a finite verb and can stand alone, whereas a dependent clause cannot. Complex sentences combine one independent clause with one or more dependent clauses, while compound sentences join two or more independent clauses with coordinating conjunctions. Mastery of clause boundaries is essential for punctuation decisions, especially the correct use of commas, semicolons, and colons.

Subordinate clauses are introduced by subordinating conjunctions such as “because”, “although”, “if”, and “when”. Their position—initial, medial, or final—affects the flow and emphasis of the sentence. For instance, placing a conditional clause at the beginning foregrounds the condition, while placing it at the end emphasizes the main assertion. Recognising these nuances aids in both comprehension and the construction of varied, sophisticated sentences.

1.3 Agreement, Tense, and Voice

Subject‑verb agreement requires that a singular subject take a singular verb form and a plural subject a plural verb form. Intervening phrases, collective nouns, and indefinite pronouns often create traps; for example, “The committee has decided” treats the collective noun as singular, whereas “The committee have disagreed” treats it as plural when the members act individually. Tense consistency ensures that the time frame of a narrative or argument remains clear; unwarranted shifts from past to present tense confuse the reader and are a frequent error in examination passages.

Voice indicates whether the subject performs the action (active) or receives it (passive). Active voice generally yields clearer, more direct sentences, while passive voice can be useful when the doer is unknown or irrelevant. However, excessive passivity obscures agency and can make a text monotonous. In error‑spotting tasks, a mismatch between the intended voice and the verb form—such as “The report was written by the committee” when the context demands “The committee wrote the report”—is a common target.

Vocabulary Development and Usage

2.1 Word Formation and Morphological Awareness

Expanding vocabulary systematically relies on understanding how English builds words from roots, prefixes, and suffixes. Latin and Greek roots constitute a large portion of academic and administrative terminology; for example, the root “leg” (law) appears in “legislature”, “legitimate”, and “illegal”. Prefixes such as “un‑”, “re‑”, “pre‑”, and “mis‑” modify meaning in predictable ways, while suffixes like “‑tion”, “‑ness”, “‑able”, and “‑ify” change grammatical category. A learner who internalises these patterns can deduce the meaning of unfamiliar words encountered in reading comprehension passages.

Morphological awareness also prevents errors in word‑form selection. In a sentence requiring a noun, the adjective “effective” must be replaced by “effectiveness” or “efficacy”. Similarly, the verb “to decide” becomes the noun “decision”, not “decisioning”. Recognising the correct derivational form is a skill directly tested in sentence‑correction items.

2.2 Collocation, Register, and Idiomatic Usage

Words rarely occur in isolation; they habitually combine with specific partners, a phenomenon known as collocation. Strong collocations such as “make a decision”, “take responsibility”, and “conduct an inquiry” sound natural, whereas “do a decision” or “perform responsibility” betray a lack of lexical fluency. Register—the level of formality appropriate to a context—governs word choice; “purchase” suits official reports, while “buy” fits informal correspondence. Idioms, fixed expressions whose meaning cannot be inferred from individual words, appear frequently in comprehension passages; understanding “to pull strings” as “to use influence” prevents literal misinterpretation.

Examination questions often test the ability to select the word that best fits a given collocational frame or register. A candidate who knows that “adverse” collocates with “effects” and “conditions”, while “averse” collocates with “to” (as in “averse to risk”), will avoid a common lexical error.

2.3 Synonymy, Antonymy, and Precision

Synonyms share a core meaning but differ in nuance, connotation, and usage constraints. “Slender”, “slim”, “thin”, and “skinny” all describe a lack of bulk, yet “slender” and “slim” carry a positive aesthetic connotation, whereas “skinny” may imply undernourishment. Antonyms provide opposite meanings, but gradable antonyms like “hot” and “cold” admit intermediate degrees, while complementary antonyms such as “alive” and “dead” do not. Precision in word choice eliminates ambiguity; for instance, “The policy mitigated the impact” is more exact than “The policy reduced the impact” when the intention is to lessen severity rather than merely lower quantity.

In error‑spotting, a synonym substitution that alters the intended nuance—using “cheap” instead of “economical” in a formal report—constitutes a lexical error. Developing a mental thesaurus organised by register and connotation enables rapid, accurate selection.

Example Question
Choose the word that best completes the sentence: “The committee’s ______ approach to the problem ensured that all stakeholders were consulted before a decision was taken.” Options: (a) cursory (b) comprehensive (c) perfunctory (d) superficial
The sentence praises the committee for consulting all stakeholders, indicating thoroughness. “Comprehensive” means covering all aspects, which matches the context. “Cursory”, “perfunctory”, and “superficial” all imply a lack of depth. Therefore, the correct answer is (b) comprehensive.
Exam‑Day Insight
When a sentence contains a positive evaluation followed by a blank, look for a word with a positive connotation that matches the described action; the three distractors will usually share a negative or neutral tone.

Reading Comprehension Strategies

3.1 Active Reading and Text Mapping

Effective comprehension begins with active reading: the reader annotates, predicts, and questions the text while progressing. Text mapping involves identifying the thesis statement, major supporting points, and the logical flow—whether the passage follows a cause‑effect, problem‑solution, compare‑contrast, or chronological structure. By sketching a quick outline in the margin, the examinee creates a mental scaffold that makes retrieval of specific details faster during question answering.

Skimming for the main idea and scanning for keywords are complementary techniques. Skimming the first and last paragraphs often reveals the author’s purpose, while scanning for proper nouns, dates, or technical terms locates factual answers. Practising these techniques on varied passages—editorials, scientific abstracts, policy briefs—builds adaptability across the diverse texts appearing in the examination.

3.2 Inference, Tone, and Author’s Purpose

Inference questions require the reader to derive information not explicitly stated but logically implied. The reader must combine textual evidence with reasonable assumptions, avoiding over‑generalisation. Tone reflects the author’s attitude—objective, critical, optimistic, skeptical—and is conveyed through diction, syntax, and rhetorical devices. Recognising tone helps answer questions about the author’s stance or the likely audience reaction.

Author’s purpose may be to inform, persuade, entertain, or reflect. A passage that presents balanced arguments and cites data typically aims to inform, whereas one that employs emotive language and rhetorical questions seeks to persuade. Identifying purpose guides the selection of the correct answer in “primary purpose” or “author’s attitude” items.

3.3 Question Types and Answer‑Selection Techniques

Common question categories include main‑idea, detail, vocabulary‑in‑context, inference, and application. For main‑idea questions, the correct option encapsulates the central thesis without being too narrow or too broad. Detail questions demand precise retrieval; the test‑taker should locate the relevant sentence and verify that the option matches the text verbatim or paraphrases accurately. Vocabulary‑in‑context items test the ability to deduce meaning from surrounding clues; substituting each option into the sentence checks for coherence.

Elimination is a powerful strategy: discard options that contradict the passage, introduce extraneous information, or use extreme language (“always”, “never”) unless the passage explicitly supports such absolutes. The remaining option, even if not perfect, is usually the intended answer. Time management—allocating roughly one minute per question—ensures that all items receive attention.

Sentence Correction and Error Spotting

4.1 Common Grammatical Error Categories

Error‑spotting sections typically target a limited set of recurring error types. Subject‑verb agreement errors appear when intervening phrases obscure the true subject. Pronoun‑antecedent disagreement arises when a pronoun does not match its antecedent in number, gender, or person. Faulty parallelism occurs when items in a list or comparison are not expressed in the same grammatical form, such as mixing gerunds with infinitives. Misplaced and dangling modifiers create ambiguity about which word they modify; for example, “Running to catch the bus, the rain soaked him” incorrectly suggests the rain was running.

Tense inconsistency, especially in reported speech or conditional constructions, is another frequent target. A sentence that shifts from past perfect to simple past without justification signals an error. Additionally, incorrect preposition usage—“interested on” instead of “interested in”—and article misuse—omitting “the” before a specific noun—are staples of the error‑spotting repertoire.

4.2 Sentence‑Correction Principles

Sentence‑correction items present a sentence with an underlined portion and five answer choices, the first replicating the original. The goal is to select the version that is grammatically correct, clear, concise, and idiomatic. The “original” choice is correct only if no error exists. A systematic approach: first, read the entire sentence to grasp meaning; second, isolate the underlined segment and test each option for grammaticality; third, compare the remaining options for stylistic superiority—preferring active voice, eliminating redundancy, and maintaining parallel structure.

Conciseness does not mean brevity at the expense of clarity; the best answer conveys the intended meaning with the fewest words while preserving nuance. For instance, “The reason is because” is redundant; “The reason is that” or simply “Because” is preferred. Recognising such patterns accelerates decision‑making.

4.3 Practice Methodology and Error‑Log Maintenance

Consistent practice with timed drills builds speed and accuracy. After each drill, the learner should maintain an error log categorising mistakes by type—agreement, parallelism, modifier, tense, preposition, etc. Reviewing the log weekly reveals persistent weaknesses, allowing targeted revision. Additionally, rewriting incorrect sentences in corrected form reinforces the correct pattern in memory.

Simulated full‑length tests under examination conditions develop stamina and the ability to switch between question types without loss of focus. Analysing the explanations for each answer, especially the distractors, deepens understanding of why certain constructions are unacceptable.

Integrated Practice and Exam Techniques

5.1 Combining Grammar, Vocabulary, and Comprehension

The examination does not test grammar, vocabulary, and comprehension in isolation; items often require simultaneous deployment of all three skills. A reading‑comprehension passage may contain a sentence‑correction question embedded within it, or a vocabulary‑in‑context item may hinge on recognizing a grammatical cue such as a subjunctive mood. Integrated practice—working through mixed‑type sets—trains the mind to shift fluidly between analytical modes.

One effective method is to treat each passage as a mini‑test: first skim for structure, then answer detail and inference questions, followed by any grammar or vocabulary items linked to the passage. This sequence mirrors the natural cognitive flow and reduces context‑switching overhead.

5.2 Time Allocation and Section‑Level Strategy

The English language section typically comprises 30–40 questions to be completed in 30–40 minutes. Allocating roughly one minute per question, with a buffer of five minutes for review, is a pragmatic baseline. Prioritise questions by confidence: answer straightforward grammar and vocabulary items first, then tackle comprehension questions that demand more reading time. Mark difficult items for later review rather than lingering.

During the review window, re‑read flagged sentences, verify that the chosen answer fits the grammatical and semantic context, and ensure no careless marking errors. A quick mental checklist—subject‑verb agreement, pronoun consistency, parallelism, tense logic—catches many residual mistakes.

5.3 Psychological Readiness and Test‑Day Routine

Mental composure directly influences linguistic performance. A brief breathing exercise before the section begins lowers anxiety and sharpens focus. Familiarity with the on‑screen interface—navigation, flagging, and review functions—prevents technical distractions. Adequate sleep, hydration, and a light meal sustain cognitive stamina throughout the paper.

Finally, trust the preparation. The systematic study of grammar rules, vocabulary families, comprehension techniques, and correction strategies equips the candidate with a reliable toolkit. Consistent application of these tools under timed conditions translates into the accuracy and speed the examination rewards.

Subordination, Coordination, and the Syntax of Complex Sentences

Complex sentences in formal English are built on two principal combinatory mechanisms: subordination and coordination. Subordination creates a hierarchical relation in which a dependent clause (finite, non‑finite, or verbless) functions as a constituent of a main clause — serving as subject, object, complement, adverbial, or modifier. Coordination links clauses of equal syntactic status through coordinators (and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet) or correlative pairs (either…or, neither…nor, both…and). The distinction is not merely taxonomic; it governs punctuation, information flow, and the scope of negation, quantification, and modal operators.

Finite Subordinate Clauses

Finite subordinate clauses are introduced by subordinating conjunctions (because, although, if, since, while, unless) or relative pronouns (who, which, that, whose). Their internal tense and mood follow the matrix clause’s temporal anchor, but they may exhibit back‑shifting in reported speech (e.g., “She said that she had finished the report”). A frequent examiner trap is the misuse of that as a relative pronoun after a preposition (“the report in which she referred” not “the report that she referred in”).

Non‑Finite and Verbless Clauses

Non‑finite clauses (to‑infinitives, -ing participles, -ed participles) lack an overt subject and tense, relying on control or raising structures for interpretation. In subject control (“She promised to submit the draft”), the matrix subject controls the understood subject of the infinitive. In object control (“She persuaded him to submit the draft”), the matrix object controls it. Raising verbs (seem, appear, happen) allow the subordinate subject to raise to matrix subject position (“He seems to be tired”). Verbless clauses (“When ready, the document will be forwarded”) are elliptical forms of finite adverbial clauses and must be recoverable from context.

Coordination Constraints

Coordination obeys the Coordinate Structure Constraint: extraction (wh‑movement, relativisation) cannot target a single conjunct (“*Which report did you read and the summary?”). However, Across‑the‑Board extraction is permitted when the same element is extracted from all conjuncts (“Which report did you read and summarise?”). Parallelism demands matching syntactic categories across conjuncts; mismatched categories (noun phrase + clause) produce faulty coordination (“The committee reviewed the proposal and that the budget was insufficient”).

Punctuation and Information Packaging

Subordinate clauses that precede the main clause are usually followed by a comma (“Although the data were incomplete, the analysis proceeded”). When they follow, the comma is omitted unless the clause is non‑restrictive or heavily parenthetical. Coordinated independent clauses require a comma before the coordinator unless the clauses are short and closely linked (“The draft was approved and the meeting adjourned”). Over‑use of coordination can flatten discourse hierarchy; strategic subordination signals emphasis and logical subordination.

Discourse Markers, Cohesive Devices, and Textual Flow

Coherence in extended prose depends on the systematic deployment of discourse markers (however, therefore, moreover, consequently, in contrast) and cohesive devices (reference, substitution, ellipsis, lexical repetition, collocation). Discourse markers operate at the interpersonal and textual metafunctions: they signal the speaker’s stance (evidential, concessive, inferential) and organise the rhetorical structure (addition, cause‑effect, comparison, exemplification). Their placement — initial, medial, or final — affects prosodic focus and information status.

Reference and Substitution

Anaphoric reference (personal pronouns, demonstratives, comparatives) links a current mention to an antecedent. Cataphoric reference (“This is the rule: …”) anticipates a forthcoming referent. Substitution replaces a lexical item with a pro‑form (do so, one, the same) to avoid repetition while preserving syntactic slot (“She will submit the report, and he will do so too”). Ellipsis omits recoverable material entirely (“The first draft was rejected; the second, accepted”). Mastery of these devices reduces redundancy without sacrificing clarity.

Lexical Cohesion

Lexical cohesion arises from reiteration (exact repetition, synonymy, hyponymy, meronymy) and collocation (habitual co‑occurrence such as “make a decision”, “draw a conclusion”). Examiners test the ability to recognise when a synonym disrupts collocational expectancy (“*perform a decision” is infelicitous). The lexical chain method — tracing a semantic thread across paragraphs — is a reliable comprehension strategy for locating main ideas and authorial attitude.

Marker‑Driven Rhetorical Moves

Typical rhetorical moves in argumentative texts follow a predictable marker sequence: claim → evidence → warrant → backing → qualifier → rebuttal. Markers such as “first”, “furthermore”, “in addition” signal additive moves; “however”, “on the other hand”, “nevertheless” signal concessive or contrastive moves; “therefore”, “thus”, “consequently” signal inferential moves. Recognising the move type allows the reader to anticipate the function of the next sentence and to answer inference questions with higher accuracy.

Worked Example: Discourse‑Marker Identification
Read the excerpt and select the function of the underlined marker.

“The new policy reduces processing time by 30 %. <u>Moreover</u>, it eliminates the need for manual verification.”
Answer: Additive / elaboration.
Reasoning: “Moreover” introduces a second, supporting point that strengthens the claim made in the preceding sentence. It does not express contrast, cause‑effect, or concession. In standard taxonomies of discourse markers, “moreover” belongs to the additive class. Therefore the correct function is additive elaboration.
Exam‑Day Strategy: Marker Mapping
During the first skim of a passage, underline every discourse marker and label its class (additive, adversative, causal, temporal). This produces a skeletal map of the argument’s architecture. When a question asks for the author’s purpose or the relationship between paragraphs, consult the map rather than re‑reading the full text. The technique reduces cognitive load and improves speed on inference and tone items.

Argument Structure, Logical Fallacies, and Critical Evaluation

Advanced comprehension items frequently require the test‑taker to dissect an argument’s logical architecture and to spot fallacious reasoning. An argument consists of a claim (conclusion), one or more premises (evidence), and an implicit or explicit warrant (the principle linking premises to claim). The Toulmin model (claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal) provides a systematic framework for analysis. Examiners embed common informal fallacies — hasty generalisation, false cause, slippery slope, straw man, ad hominem, appeal to authority, false dilemma — within passages to test evaluative reading.

Hasty Generalisation and Sampling Bias

A hasty generalisation draws a broad conclusion from an insufficient or unrepresentative sample (“Three employees complained about the new software; therefore the software is universally disliked”). The corrective reading strategy is to check whether the premise cites a statistically adequate, random sample or merely anecdotal instances.

False Cause (Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc)

This fallacy infers causation from temporal succession (“Revenue rose after the logo redesign; the redesign caused the rise”). Critical readers look for confounding variables, controlled studies, or mechanistic explanations before accepting causal claims.

Slippery Slope

A slippery slope argues that a modest initial step inevitably leads to a chain of extreme consequences without demonstrating the causal links (“If we allow flexible hours, soon nobody will come to the office, and productivity will collapse”). Evaluation requires demanding evidence for each transitional step.

Straw Man and Ad Hominem

A straw man misrepresents an opponent’s position to make it easier to attack (“Critics say we should ignore all safety regulations”). An ad hominem attacks the person rather than the argument (“The proposal comes from a junior analyst, so it cannot be valid”). Both divert attention from the substantive issue; the test‑taker must identify the diversion and restate the genuine argument.

Appeal to Authority and False Dilemma

Appeal to authority cites an expert outside the relevant domain (“A celebrated novelist endorses this economic policy”). False dilemma presents only two extremes (“Either we adopt the full plan or we do nothing”). Recognising these patterns enables rapid elimination of answer choices that restate the fallacy as a valid inference.

Register, Tone, and Stylistic Variation in Formal Writing

Register — the configuration of lexical, syntactic, and phonological features appropriate to a communicative situation — is a decisive factor in sentence‑correction and style‑improvement items. Formal academic and administrative registers favour nominalisation (“The implementation of the policy” over “Implementing the policy”), passive voice for agent backgrounding (“The report was submitted”), and Latinate vocabulary (“commence” vs. “start”). Conversely, excessive nominalisation creates “noun‑stack” density that impairs readability (“The policy implementation evaluation committee”).

Tone Markers

Tone reflects the writer’s attitude toward subject and audience. Objective tone avoids emotive adjectives, hedges (“appears to suggest”, “may indicate”), and first‑person pronouns. Persuasive tone employs modal verbs of obligation (“must”, “should”), rhetorical questions, and direct address. Ironic or satirical tone signals incongruity through understatement or hyperbole. Identifying tone aids answer‑selection in “author’s attitude” questions.

Stylistic Variation: Parallelism, Balance, and Rhythm

Parallel structure aligns coordinate elements in identical grammatical form (“to analyse, to evaluate, to recommend”). Faulty parallelism (“to analyse, evaluating, recommendation”) is a high‑frequency error type. Balanced sentences (antithetical or climactic) enhance rhetorical force (“Not only did the reform reduce costs, but it also improved compliance”). Rhythm — the alternation of stressed and unstressed syllables — influences perceived fluency; examiners occasionally test the ability to choose the more rhythmically felicitous option.

Register‑Shift Detection

Passages may embed a register shift (e.g., a formal report quoting an informal email). The test‑taker must recognise the shift and apply the appropriate correctness criteria to each segment. A colloquial contraction (“don’t”) is acceptable in the quoted email but not in the surrounding report. Awareness of register boundaries prevents over‑correction.

  • Treating a coordinating conjunction as a subordinating conjunction, leading to mis‑punctuation and mis‑analysis of clause hierarchy.
  • Assuming all discourse markers of the same class are interchangeable; “however” and “nevertheless” differ in formality and positional constraints.
  • Confusing a warrant with a premise; the warrant is the inferential bridge, not the evidence itself.
  • Labeling any weak argument as a “fallacy” without matching the specific fallacy pattern; examiners require precise identification.
  • Over‑applying nominalisation as a marker of formality, producing noun‑stack sentences that violate clarity criteria.
  • Ignoring register boundaries and correcting quoted informal language to formal standards.
  • Misreading a concessive marker (“although”) as a causal marker, reversing the logical relation between clauses.
