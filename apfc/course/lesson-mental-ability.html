1. The mental ability pattern

APFC Mental Ability carries 20 questions. Unlike maths, there is no formula — there are patterns and elimination rules. The recurring families:

  1. Seating / sitting arrangement (linear and circular) — usually 3–5 questions
  2. Coding-decoding (letter and number codes) — 3–4
  3. Number and alphabet series — 3–4
  4. Syllogisms — 2–3
  5. Blood relations and direction sense — 2–3
  6. Odd-one-out and classification — 2–3
  7. Analogy and series completion — the rest

The scoring rule: these are the most "free" marks in the paper — 20 questions where a trained pattern-recognition approach converts them in under a minute each. The examiners test whether you can eliminate, not whether you can think creatively.

2. Family-by-family methods

Seating arrangement — the diagram method

Never solve seating in your head. Draw 6–8 boxes (positions) immediately, label them 1–n, and place each clue as you read. Rules:

  • "A sits at the left end" → fix A at position 1. "Third to the left of B" → count positions, don't guess.
  • Circular: pick any seat as 1 and go clockwise; remember the person opposite is n/2+1 seats away.
  • "Immediate left/right" in a row facing north/south: if facing north, left = west side of the row; facing south reverses it — the classic trap.
  • Process of elimination on the answer choices: if the question asks "who sits between X and Y", check each option against the diagram — often only one survives.
Worked example
Five friends A–E sit in a row facing north. A is at the left end. B sits immediately right of A. C sits between D and E. D sits at the right end. Who sits in the middle?
Solution: Positions 1–5. A=1, B=2 (immediate right). D=5 (right end). C between D and E → with only positions 3,4 left and D=5: E=3, C=4, D=5. Middle = position 3 = E.

Coding-decoding

Write the code–letter mapping as soon as two words are given. If CAT → DBU, the rule is +1 to each letter. Test the rule on every letter of the question word before answering. For number codes (A=1…Z=26 or reverse), compute the target's positions and apply the shift. Watch for "opposite letter" codes: A↔Z, B↔Y (27 − position).

Number and alphabet series

Look at differences first: +3, +5, +7, +9 → next +11 (odd numbers). Then second differences if first differences aren't constant. For letters: AZ, CX, EV, GT → first letters A,C,E,G (+2), second Z,X,V,T (−2) → next JR. Mixed series (1, 4, 9, 16 → squares) — check squares/cubes/primes before differences.

Blood relations

Convert every statement to "X is Y's ___" chains, then collapse. "A is B's mother's brother" → A is B's maternal uncle. Generation levels: same generation = siblings/cousins/spouses; one up = parent/uncle/aunt; two up = grandparent. Gender matters: "his wife's brother" = brother-in-law.

Direction sense

Draw a compass. Track turns: right = 90° clockwise, left = 90° anticlockwise. "Turns around and walks back" = reverse direction. Distance questions: Pythagoras for diagonal shortcuts — 3-4-5 and 5-12-13 triangles appear constantly.

Odd-one-out and analogy

Find the category of four items, then the odd one breaks it. For analogies (A:B :: C:?), identify the relationship type first: part-whole, tool-use, synonym, degree, cause-effect, worker-place. If three relationships seem possible, pick the most specific.

3. Syllogism rules

Syllogisms are pure logic — no subject knowledge needed. The two statements use quantifiers: All, Some, No, Some…not. The conclusions you may draw are limited:

Syllogism conversion rules
Statement typeWhat it licensesWhat it does NOT license
All A are BSome A are B; Some B are AAll B are A
Some A are BSome B are AAll/Some definite placement
No A is BNo B is A; All A are not-BSome A are B
Some A are not BNothing definite about overlapAll A are not B

Method: draw the Venn possibility space — for each conclusion, ask "can I draw the statements true while the conclusion is false?" If yes for any arrangement, the conclusion does not follow. "Possibly true" conclusions are those compatible with at least one arrangement. Never convert "some" into "all" — that is the #1 trap.

4. Common traps

Traps that cost marks
  • Facing direction: "left of" reverses when people face south/centre — recheck every seating clue.
  • Circular vs linear: in a circle there is no "first" position — don't anchor position 1 unless the question fixes it.
  • "Immediate" vs "next": "two seats to the left" ≠ "second to the left" in some papers — count carefully.
  • Some/no confusion: "Some A are not B" does not mean "Some A are B" — they can coexist but aren't guaranteed.
  • Opposite-letter code: A=26-position, not position from Z in some conventions — verify with the given pair.
  • Series double-count: alternate series (+2,×2,+2,×2) look like one rule until you split odd/even terms.
  • Blood relation gender: "his" vs "her" changes the relation — track pronouns literally.

5. Practice plan

Daily drill (20 min): 5 seating + 5 series/coding + 5 syllogism/blood/direction + 5 odd-one-out/analogy, timed at ~50 seconds each.

Technique order: learn the family methods first (seating diagram, code table, syllogism rules), then volume. After two weeks, switch to mixed mocks — the exam never groups by family cleanly.

Error log: for every wrong answer, write the one-line rule you missed ("forgot facing-south reversal", "treated some as all"). Review the log every third day — repeat mistakes are almost always the same 2–3 traps.

Pro Tip
Mental Ability is the highest ROI section after Mathematics: 20 questions, pattern-based, and the cutoff-friendly scoring means a 16–18 here is realistic for most aspirants who drill it. If you can consistently solve seating and series, you've already banked ~8–10 questions.
