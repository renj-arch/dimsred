Perfectoid Spaces — Perfectoid Fields, Tilting & the Almost Purity Theorem

Perfectoid spaces are a class of rigid-analytic spaces introduced by Peter Scholze (2011) that revolutionized p-adic geometry. A perfectoid field K is a complete non-archimedean field with residue characteristic p such that |K^×| is not discrete and the Frobenius map O_K/p → O_K/p is surjective. Given such K, one constructs its tilt K^♭ — a perfectoid field of characteristic p — and proves an equivalence of finite étale extensions K and K^♭ (the tilting correspondence). The almost purity theorem states that a finite extension of perfectoid fields is again perfectoid, and the extension of rings of integers is almost finite étale. Perfectoid spaces are then adic spaces glued from affinoids Spa(A, A^+) where A is a perfectoid K-algebra. The v-site and the theory of diamonds provide the foundation for the geometric Satake correspondence and Scholze's proof of the local Langlands conjecture for GL_n(ℚ_p). Perfectoid geometry also gives a new proof of the Hodge-Tate decomposition and the weight-monodromy conjecture for complete intersection varieties.

"Perfectoid spaces bridge the gap between characteristic 0 and characteristic p geometry."
— Peter Scholze
Part 1

📏 Perfectoid Fields

Foundations
Perfectoid Fields, the Frobenius Condition, and the Tilt
A perfectoid field K is a complete non-archimedean field with residue characteristic p > 0 such that (i) the value group |K^×| is not discrete, and (ii) the Frobenius homomorphism Frob: O_K/p → O_K/p, x ↦ x^p, is surjective. Examples: ℚ_p(μ_{p^∞})^∧ (the completion of ℚ_p with all p-power roots of unity), ℂ_p (the completion of the algebraic closure of ℚ_p), and 𝔽_p((t^{1/p^∞})) (the completion of the perfection of 𝔽_p((t))). The tilt of K is K^♭ = lim←_{x↦x^p} O_K/p — the inverse limit of the system O_K/p ← O_K/p ← ⋯ under Frobenius. The multiplication on K^♭ is given componentwise: (x_0, x_1, …)·(y_0, y_1, …) = (x_0y_0, x_1y_1, …). The tilt is a perfectoid field of characteristic p. There is a multiplicative homeomorphism (but NOT additive) between the multiplicative monoids: (O_K, ·) ≅ (O_{K^♭}, ·). The celebrated fact: ℂ_p^♭ ≅ 𝔽_p((t^{1/p^∞})) — the t-adic completion of the perfection of 𝔽_p((t)). This identification underlies the entire tilting correspondence.
Perfectoid fields have surjective Frobenius on O_K/p and a nondiscrete value group; their tilts are perfectoid fields of characteristic p with isomorphic multiplicative monoids.
Part 2

🌍 The Tilting Correspondence

Correspondence
Equivalence of Étale Sites, the Almost Purity Theorem, and Fontaine-Wintenberger
The main theorem of Scholze (2011): for a perfectoid field K with tilt K^♭, the categories of finite étale extensions of K and of K^♭ are equivalent. Equivalently, the absolute Galois groups are isomorphic: G_K ≅ G_{K^♭}. The proof relies on the almost purity theorem: if L/K is a finite extension of perfectoid fields, then L is itself perfectoid and the corresponding extension L^♭/K^♭ is finite étale of the same degree. Moreover, O_L is "almost" a finite étale extension of O_K in the sense of Faltings' almost ring theory — the module O_L/O_K is annihilated by the maximal ideal of O_K. This equivalence of étale sites extends to perfectoid spaces: for a perfectoid space X with tilt X^♭, the étale sites X_{ét} and X^♭_{ét} are equivalent. The Fontaine-Wintenberger theorem (1979) gave the first such isomorphism: for K = ℚ_p(μ_{p^∞})^∧, the field of norms construction produces an isomorphism G_K ≅ G_{𝔽_p((t))}. Scholze's tilting correspondence vastly generalises this to all perfectoid fields.
The tilting correspondence identifies the étale topology of a perfectoid field in characteristic 0 with that of its characteristic-p tilt — a profound bridge between mixed and positive characteristic.

🧮 Perfectoid Spaces and the v-Site

Spaces
Perfectoid Algebras, Adic Spaces, the v-Topology, and Diamonds
A perfectoid K-algebra A is a Banach K-algebra that is uniform, has a pseudo-uniformizer ϖ ∈ A such that ϖ^p | p in A°, and the Frobenius A°/ϖ → A°/ϖ is surjective. A perfectoid space is an adic space locally isomorphic to Spa(A, A^+) where A is a perfectoid K-algebra. The notion of untilts: a perfectoid space over 𝔽_p that can be lifted to characteristic 0 gives rise to a perfectoid space in characteristic 0. The v-site is the largest Grothendieck topology on perfectoid spaces where coverings are "v-coverings" — universally surjective maps of schemes or preperfectoid spaces. The v-topology is the perfectoid analogue of the fpqc topology. A foundational theorem of Scholze: the structure presheaf is a sheaf on the v-site. A diamond is a v-sheaf on the category of perfectoid spaces of characteristic p — the analogue of an algebraic space in perfectoid geometry. The theory of diamonds unifies the geometry of Shimura varieties, Rapoport-Zink spaces, and the Fargues-Fontaine curve, providing the arena for the geometric Langlands program in p-adic geometry.
Perfectoid spaces are adic spaces built from perfectoid algebras; the v-site and diamonds give the sheaf-theoretic foundation for p-adic geometry in characteristic p.

The Hodge-Tate period map associates to the moduli space of shtukas a map relating p-adic Hodge theory to the geometry of the moduli space. Perfectoid geometry provides the essential p-adic analytic framework for the Hodge-Tate decomposition, the weight-monodromy conjecture, and the geometric Langlands program.

The Almost Purity Theorem: Proof Sketch and Almost Mathematics

The almost purity theorem is the technical heart of Scholze’s tilting correspondence. In classical commutative algebra a finite étale extension of a perfectoid ring would be expected to remain perfectoid, but p‑torsion in the integral structure forces one to work in Faltings’ almost mathematics. The theorem states: if A is a perfectoid Tate ring over a perfectoid field K and B is a finite étale A-algebra, then B is again perfectoid and the trace map TrB/A is almost surjective onto A. The proof reduces to the case A = K⟨X1/p∞⟩ and uses surjectivity of Frobenius on A°/ϖ. One constructs an almost isomorphism B°/ϖ ≅ A°/ϖ ⊗A° B° and checks that the almost finitely presented condition descends along the almost faithfully flat map A° → B°. The key input is the almost Cohen structure theorem for perfectoid rings, which identifies the tilt A♭ as a perfectoid algebra over K♭ with the same almost étale site. This almost purity result underlies the equivalence of étale topoi Xét ≅ X♭ét for perfectoid spaces.

Perfectoid Algebras and the Pro‑étale Site

A perfectoid K-algebra is a Banach K-algebra A that is uniform, admits a pseudo‑uniformizer ϖ with ϖp | p in A°, and such that Frobenius A°/ϖ → A°/ϖ is surjective. The category of perfectoid algebras is stable under completed tensor products, rational localisations, and finite étale extensions. Scholze introduced the pro‑étale site Xproét of a perfectoid space X to capture infinite‑level covers invisible on the ordinary étale site. Objects are pro‑finite étale morphisms Y → X with Y perfectoid; coverings are jointly surjective families. The structure presheaf 𝒪X is a sheaf on this site, and its higher cohomology computes p‑adic étale cohomology via the comparison isomorphism Hiproét(X, ℤp) ⊗ ℂp ≅ HidR(X/ℂp). The pro‑étale site also hosts the period sheaves 𝔸inf, 𝔹dR, and 𝔹cris essential for p‑adic Hodge theory. The tilting equivalence extends to pro‑étale topoi Xproét ≅ X♭proét, allowing transfer of p‑adic period sheaves to characteristic p where they are more tractable.

Worked Example: Almost Purity for a Cyclotomic Extension
Let K = ℚp(μp∞)∧ be the completed cyclotomic extension and L = K(π) where πp = p. Show that L is perfectoid and that the extension L/K is almost finite étale.
Step 1. K is perfectoid because its tilt is 𝔽p((t)) and the valuation group is p‑divisible. Step 2. The element π satisfies πp = p, so π is a pseudo‑uniformizer of L with πp | p in OL. Step 3. Frobenius on OL/π is surjective because OL/π ≅ OK/p and Frobenius on OK/p is surjective by perfectoidness of K. Step 4. By the almost purity theorem, L is perfectoid and OL is almost finite étale over OK; the trace map is almost surjective because the different ideal is killed by the maximal ideal of OK. Hence L/K is an almost finite étale extension of perfectoid fields.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks to verify perfectoidness of an explicit algebra, first check uniformity and the existence of a pseudo‑uniformizer with the divisibility condition. Then test Frobenius surjectivity modulo that pseudo‑uniformizer; this is often the quickest route because it reduces to a known perfectoid base. Remember that almost finite étale means the module of differentials is almost zero, not strictly zero.

Diamonds and the Fargues–Fontaine Curve

Diamonds are the geometric objects that replace schemes in the perfectoid world. A diamond is a v‑sheaf on the category of perfectoid spaces over 𝔽p admitting a surjective v‑cover by a representable perfectoid space. The v‑topology is generated by universally submersive maps of perfectoid spaces; it is strictly finer than the pro‑étale topology but has the advantage that the structure presheaf is a sheaf. The Fargues–Fontaine curve XFF is a fundamental diamond constructed as the quotient (Spa ℂp♭ {0}) / φℤ, where φ is the Frobenius on the tilt. Its closed points correspond to untilts of ℂp♭, i.e. perfectoid fields of characteristic zero with tilt ℂp♭. The curve is a complete, regular, one‑dimensional Noetherian scheme in the sense of diamonds, and its category of vector bundles is equivalent to the category of isocrystals over 𝔽p. This equivalence underlies the geometric Langlands correspondence for GLn(ℚp) via the Harris–Scholze local Langlands correspondence: the Hecke eigensheaf on the moduli stack of GLn-bundles on XFF matches the smooth irreducible representation of GLn(ℚp). The diamond formalism also allows one to define the moduli space of local shtukas as a diamond, providing a geometric setting for the global Langlands program over function fields.

The Hodge–Tate Period Map on Infinite‑Level Shimura Varieties

Let ShK(G, X) be a Shimura variety of Hodge type with level K ⊂ G(𝔸f). The inverse limit over all levels Sh∞ = lim← K ShK is a perfectoid space over the reflex field. Scholze constructed the Hodge–Tate period map πHT : Sh∞ → ℱℓG, μ to the flag variety attached to the Hodge cocharacter μ. This map is G(ℚp)-equivariant and its fibres are affinoid perfectoid spaces. The key property is that πHT identifies the p‑adic étale cohomology of the Shimura variety with the cohomology of local systems on the flag variety, yielding a perfectoid proof of the Hodge–Tate decomposition Hiét(ShK, ℚp) ⊗ ℂp ≅ ⊕j Hi−j(ShK, Ωj) ⊗ ℂp(−j). The map also interpolates the classical Hodge–Tate map on the generic fibre of the universal abelian variety, and its image is the adic flag variety minus the Newton strata that violate the weak admissibility condition. This geometric description of the period map is the starting point for the construction of the p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL2(ℚp) via the moduli of p‑divisible groups.

The Weight‑Monodromy Conjecture via Perfectoid Methods

The weight‑monodromy conjecture predicts that for a proper smooth variety X over a p‑adic field, the monodromy filtration on the ℓ‑adic cohomology Hiét(Xη̄, ℚℓ) coincides with the weight filtration up to a shift. Scholze and collaborators have established cases of the conjecture for complete intersection varieties of low dimension by using the perfectoid universal cover of the associated Shimura variety and the Hodge–Tate period map. The argument reduces to the case of a hypersurface in a toric variety, where cohomology can be computed via the Koszul complex on the perfectoid cover. The almost purity theorem ensures that the relevant cohomology groups are almost finitely generated modules over the perfectoid ring, and the tilting equivalence transfers the monodromy operator to characteristic p where it becomes the Frobenius action on the tilt. The weight filtration is then identified with the slope filtration of the associated isocrystal on the Fargues–Fontaine curve. This proof illustrates how perfectoid geometry converts a deep arithmetic statement into a geometric one on diamonds, where the tools of p‑adic Hodge theory become directly applicable.

  • Treating “almost finite étale” as ordinary finite étale; the almost condition means the module of differentials is killed by the maximal ideal, not that it vanishes.
  • Misidentifying the tilt of a perfectoid field: the tilt is a perfectoid field of characteristic p with the same value group, not merely the residue field.

Almost Mathematics and Almost Coherent Sheaves on Perfectoid Spaces

Almost mathematics, introduced by Faltings and developed by Scholze, replaces the category of modules over a perfectoid ring R with the quotient category where morphisms killed by the maximal ideal m are identified with zero. An almost zero module is annihilated by m; an almost isomorphism has kernel and cokernel almost zero. This framework treats finite étale extensions of perfectoid algebras as if they were finite free, since the module of differentials is almost zero. The almost purity theorem states that for a perfectoid algebra A over a perfectoid field K, any finite étale A-algebra B is almost finite étale over A; equivalently, B is almost projective of finite type and the trace map splits almost everywhere.

Almost coherent sheaves on a perfectoid space X = Spa(A, A⁺) are defined by sheafifying the almost module construction on the v‑site. The structure presheaf 𝒪X becomes a sheaf of almost rings, and the category of almost coherent 𝒪X-modules is abelian and stable under kernels, cokernels, and extensions. A key result is that higher direct images of an almost coherent sheaf under a proper morphism are again almost coherent. This almost coherence underpins approaches to the weight‑monodromy conjecture in low dimensions, where one must control the monodromy filtration on ℓ‑adic cohomology after passing to the perfectoid universal cover.

The almost setting also simplifies the cotangent complex. For a perfectoid algebra A, the cotangent complex LA/𝒪K is almost zero, reflecting that perfectoid algebras are “almost” formally smooth. Deformation theory is then governed by almost Ext groups, with obstructions vanishing almost everywhere. This almost formal smoothness underlies the existence of perfectoid covers for arbitrary rigid analytic varieties: given a rigid space Y over a perfectoid field, one constructs a pro‑étale cover Y∞ that is perfectoid with Galois group the étale fundamental group of Y. The almost purity theorem guarantees the transition maps are almost finite étale, so the limit remains in the almost category.

Examiners often test the distinction between “almost finite étale” and “finite étale”. A finite étale algebra has vanishing module of differentials; an almost finite étale algebra only has its module killed by m. Another common trap is assuming almost coherence implies ordinary coherence; the almost category is strictly larger, and statements like finiteness of cohomology hold only up to almost isomorphism. Understanding “almost zero” and “almost isomorphism” is crucial for any proof using the almost purity theorem.

The v‑Topology, Descent, and the Structure Sheaf

The v‑topology on perfectoid spaces (or analytic adic spaces) is generated by universally submersive covers: a morphism f : Y → X is a v‑cover if for every valuation ring V and morphism Spa(V, V⁺) → X, there exists a valuation ring W dominating V and a lift Spa(W, W⁺) → Y. This topology is strictly finer than the pro‑étale topology; every pro‑étale cover is a v‑cover, but some v‑covers (e.g., a perfectoid field to its tilt) are not pro‑étale. The key advantage is that the structure presheaf 𝒪X is a sheaf on the v‑site, whereas on the pro‑étale site it is a sheaf only after almost mathematics.

Descent for quasi‑coherent sheaves holds on the v‑site: given a v‑cover Y → X, the category of quasi‑coherent 𝒪X-modules is equivalent to the category of descent data on Y. This reduces to perfectoid affinoids, where a v‑cover refines to Spa(B, B⁺) → Spa(A, A⁺) induced by a faithfully flat map of perfectoid rings. The almost purity theorem shows the Amitsur complex for A → B is almost exact, and the v‑sheaf condition upgrades this to exactness without almost qualifiers. Consequently, cohomology of quasi‑coherent sheaves on perfectoid spaces is computable via Čech complexes for v‑covers, a tool unavailable in the pro‑étale topology without almost language.

The v‑topology also provides a natural home for diamonds. A diamond is a v‑sheaf locally representable by perfectoid spaces. The quotient X = (Spa ℂp♭ {0}) / φℤ defining the Fargues–Fontaine curve is a diamond because the Frobenius action is free and properly discontinuous in the v‑topology. The v‑site is essential for defining the moduli stack of local shtukas as a diamond; its v‑sheaf property ensures the stack is algebraic in the sense of diamonds. Examiners frequently ask why the v‑topology is needed instead of the étale or pro‑étale topology; the answer is that the structure sheaf fails to be a sheaf on the latter, and many geometrically meaningful quotients (e.g., by infinite discrete groups) are not representable there.

Worked Example: v‑Cover vs Pro‑étale Cover
Let K be a perfectoid field of characteristic zero with tilt K♭. Consider the morphism f : Spa(K♭, 𝒪K♭) → Spa(K, 𝒪K) induced by the untilting equivalence. Is f a pro‑étale cover? Is it a v‑cover? Justify your answer.
Solution. The map f is not pro‑étale because pro‑étale morphisms are limits of étale morphisms, and the untilting map changes the characteristic; no étale morphism exists between characteristic zero and characteristic p spaces. However, f is a v‑cover. Given any valuation ring V and a map Spa(V, V⁺) → Spa(K, 𝒪K), composition with the untilting equivalence yields a map to Spa(K♭, 𝒪K♭) after possibly extending the valuation ring (the value groups of K and K♭ are canonically identified). Thus the lifting property for v‑covers holds.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When comparing topologies on perfectoid spaces, first recall the v‑topology's defining lifting property. Check whether the morphism preserves valuation rings up to domination. If the morphism changes characteristic or involves an infinite discrete quotient, it is almost never pro‑étale but often v. Memorise the key fact: 𝒪X is a sheaf on the v‑site, not on the pro‑étale site (without almost mathematics). This single point resolves many multiple‑choice items.

Perfectoid Covers, the Pro‑étale Fundamental Group, and Galois Representations

For a connected rigid analytic variety X over a perfectoid field K, Scholze constructed a universal perfectoid cover X∞ = lim← Xi where each Xi → X is finite étale with finite étale transition maps. The inverse limit in adic spaces yields a perfectoid space because the tower is eventually almost finite étale by the almost purity theorem. The automorphism group of X∞ over X is canonically isomorphic to the étale fundamental group π₁ét(X, x̄), providing a geometric realisation of the fundamental group as deck transformations of a perfectoid space.

The perfectoid cover allows attaching p‑adic Galois representations to geometric objects. For instance, if X is a Shimura variety of Hodge type, the tower of level structures ShK has a perfectoid inverse limit Sh∞. The action of G(ℚp) on Sh∞ and the Hodge–Tate period map πHT : Sh∞ → ℱℓG,μ are defined using the perfectoid structure. The étale cohomology Hiét(ShK, ℚp) computes as the continuous group cohomology of π₁ét(ShK) with coefficients in p‑adic local systems from the perfectoid cover, recasting the Langlands–Kottwitz method geometrically.

A subtle point: the pro‑étale fundamental group of a perfectoid space is profinite, but the deck transformation group of the universal perfectoid cover is only a quotient of the full étale fundamental group; the kernel corresponds to the “almost” part of the almost purity theorem. In practice, the perfectoid cover captures the prime‑to‑p part perfectly, while the p‑part is only seen after passing to the v‑site. Examiners may ask why the perfectoid universal cover does not see the full p‑adic fundamental group; the answer lies in the almost purity theorem guaranteeing only almost finite étaleness, so p‑power torsion in the fundamental group is killed by the maximal ideal and becomes invisible in the almost category.

The Fargues–Fontaine Curve: Vector Bundles, Isocrystals, and the Geometric Langlands Correspondence

The Fargues–Fontaine curve XFF is the quotient diamond (Spa ℂp♭ {0}) / φℤ, where φ is Frobenius on the tilt ℂp♭. Its underlying topological space is the set of untilts of ℂp♭ (perfectoid fields of characteristic zero with this tilt), equipped with the quotient topology. The curve is a complete, regular, one‑dimensional Noetherian scheme in the sense of diamonds; its category of vector bundles is equivalent to the category of isocrystals over 𝔽p. This equivalence, proved by Fargues and Fontaine, sends a vector bundle ℰ to the isocrystal H⁰(XFF, ℰ) with Frobenius induced by φ.

The geometric Langlands correspondence for GLn(ℚp) is realised on XFF via the Fargues–Scholze geometrization of the local Langlands correspondence. A smooth irreducible representation π of GLn(ℚp) corresponds to a Hecke eigensheaf on the moduli stack BunGLn of rank‑n vector bundles on XFF. Hecke operators modify the vector bundle at a point, and the eigensheaf condition translates into the Langlands parameter of π. The diamond formalism is essential because BunGLn is a stack on the v‑site; its points are vector bundles on XFF defined via the v‑sheaf property.

An important computational tool is the Harder–Narasimhan filtration on vector bundles over XFF. Every vector bundle splits uniquely as a direct sum of semistable bundles of decreasing rational slopes, corresponding under the equivalence to the slope filtration of the associated isocrystal. The Newton polygon of an isocrystal is thus read off from the Harder–Narasimhan polygon. Examiners often test that the category of vector bundles on XFF is Tannakian; the fibre functor is global sections, and the Tannaka group is the Weil group of ℚp. Knowing the curve is Noetherian and regular (in the diamond sense) is also a frequent true/false item.

p‑adic Local Langlands for GL₂(ℚₚ) via Perfectoid Moduli of p‑Divisible Groups

The p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚp) was first constructed by Colmez using (φ, Γ)-modules; a geometric construction using perfectoid spaces was given by Scholze and Weinstein. They consider the moduli space M of p‑divisible groups of dimension one and height two over 𝒪ℂp with Drinfeld level structure. The infinite‑level space M∞ = lim← Mn is perfectoid, and its tilt is the moduli of p‑divisible groups over 𝒪ℂp♭. The Hodge–Tate period map πHT : M∞ → ℙ¹ℂp is a GL₂(ℚp)-equivariant map with affinoid perfectoid fibres. The pushforward of the constant sheaf ℚp along πHT decomposes as a direct sum of Banach space representations of GL₂(ℚp) realising the local Langlands correspondence.

The proof uses the fact that the cohomology of the fibres of πHT is computable via the pro‑étale site and the almost purity theorem. The fibres are isomorphic to the perfectoid universal cover of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane, and their cohomology carries a natural action of GL₂(ℚp) × D× where D is the quaternion algebra over ℚp. The Jacquet–Langlands correspondence then matches the GL₂-representations with those of D×. The perfectoid approach also yields a geometric interpretation of the Colmez functor: locally analytic vectors in the Banach representation correspond to the cohomology of the structure sheaf on the fibres.

A common examination question asks why the infinite‑level moduli space is perfectoid. The answer: the tower of Drinfeld level structures is a pro‑finite étale cover with finite étale transition maps; by the almost purity theorem, the inverse limit is perfectoid. Another trap is confusing the Hodge–Tate period map with the classical period map on the Drinfeld upper half‑plane; the former lands in the adic projective line ℙ¹ℂp with perfectoid fibres, while the latter lands in the rigid analytic projective line with merely rigid analytic fibres. The perfectoid nature of the fibres enables the use of p‑adic Hodge theory (e.g., the almost purity theorem) to compute their cohomology.

Perfectoid Shimura Varieties and the Cohomology of Local Systems

The theory of perfectoid Shimura varieties provides a geometric framework where the infinite‑level tower of a Shimura variety becomes a perfectoid space. For a Shimura datum (G, X) with G a reductive group over ℚ and X a G(ℝ)‑conjugacy class of homomorphisms 𝕊 → Gℝ, the tower of finite‑level Shimura varieties ShK(G, X) indexed by compact open subgroups K ⊂ G(𝔸f) admits a projective limit Sh∞ = lim← ShK. Scholze proved that Sh∞ is a perfectoid space over the reflex field E when the Shimura variety is of Hodge type or abelian type. The perfectoid structure allows one to apply the almost purity theorem and the pro‑étale site to compute the ℓ‑adic and p‑adic cohomology of local systems on the tower.

A key mechanism is the Hodge–Tate period map πHT : Sh∞ → Flag, where Flag is the adic flag variety associated to G. The fibres of πHT are perfectoid spaces that are (pro‑)étale covers of the basic affine space. Using the almost purity theorem, one shows that the higher direct images of ℤp-local systems are almost zero for q > 0, which yields a decomposition of the cohomology of Sh∞ into a direct sum of automorphic representations. This realises the local Langlands correspondence for G(ℚp) inside the cohomology of the perfectoid Shimura variety. The same method applies to the p‑adic cohomology via the pro‑étale comparison isomorphisms, giving a p‑adic analogue of the Matsushima formula.

Examiners often test the distinction between the classical inverse limit of rigid analytic spaces and the perfectoid inverse limit. The classical limit does not exist in the category of rigid spaces because the transition maps, though finite étale, produce rings that are not Tate algebras; the perfectoid limit exists because the almost purity theorem guarantees that the inverse limit of affinoid perfectoid algebras is again affinoid perfectoid. Another frequent trap is confusing the Hodge–Tate period map with the classical period map on the Siegel upper half‑space; the former lands in an adic flag variety with perfectoid fibres, while the latter lands in a complex flag variety with complex analytic fibres.

Applications include the construction of Galois representations attached to torsion classes in the cohomology of Shimura varieties (Caraiani–Scholze), the proof of local‑global compatibility for the Langlands correspondence, and the verification of the weight‑monodromy conjecture for certain Shimura varieties. The perfectoid approach also yields a geometric interpretation of the Kottwitz conjecture on the cohomology of Rapoport–Zink spaces.

Prismatic Cohomology and its Perfectoid Foundations

Prismatic cohomology, introduced by Bhatt and Scholze, is a universal p‑adic cohomology theory that specializes to crystalline, de Rham, and étale cohomology. A prism is a pair (A, I) where A is a δ‑ring (a ring with a Frobenius lift) and I ⊂ A is an ideal such that A is (p, I)‑complete and p ∈ I + φ(I). The prismatic site (X)△ of a p‑adic formal scheme X has objects (A, I) → X and covers given by faithfully flat maps of prisms. The structure sheaf O△ on this site yields the prismatic cohomology RΓ△(X) = RΓ((X)△, O△).

Perfectoid spaces enter the theory through the perfectoid prism (Ainf, (ξ)) where Ainf = W(𝒪C♭) is the ring of Witt vectors of the tilt of a perfectoid field C, and ξ is a generator of the kernel of the Fontaine map θ : Ainf → 𝒪C. The prismatic cohomology of a smooth formal 𝒪C‑scheme X can be computed via the perfectoid cover X∞ = X ×Spa(𝒪C) Spa(C) and the almost purity theorem: RΓ△(X) ⊗Ainf 𝒪C ≃ RΓproét(X∞, 𝒪X∞)a. This comparison identifies prismatic cohomology with the almost pro‑étale cohomology of the perfectoid universal cover.

The prismatic formalism provides a clean proof of the integral p‑adic Hodge theory comparison theorems. For a proper smooth formal scheme X over 𝒪C, the specialization maps RΓ△(X) → RΓcrys(Xk/W(k)) and RΓ△(X) → RΓdR(X/𝒪C) are obtained by base change along the canonical maps Ainf → W(k) and Ainf → 𝒪C. The Hodge–Tate comparison is the base change along Ainf → 𝒪C/ξ ≃ 𝒪C♭. The almost purity theorem ensures that these base changes are almost isomorphisms after derived completion.

Examiners may ask why the prismatic site is defined using δ‑rings rather than ordinary rings. The answer: the δ‑structure encodes the Frobenius lift needed to recover crystalline cohomology, and the condition p ∈ I + φ(I) guarantees that the prismatic cohomology is (p, I)‑complete and satisfies the expected base‑change properties. Another common question is the relationship between the prismatic cohomology of a perfectoid space and its tilt; the answer is that the prismatic cohomology of a perfectoid space over C is canonically isomorphic to the prismatic cohomology of its tilt over C♭ after base change along the Frobenius on Ainf.

The Pro‑étale Comparison Isomorphisms for Perfectoid Spaces

The pro‑étale site Xproét of a perfectoid space X was introduced by Scholze to formulate p‑adic Hodge theory geometrically. Objects are pro‑étale morphisms Y → X, i.e. inverse limits of finite étale maps with affinoid perfectoid transition maps. The structure sheaf 𝒪X+ on this site is the sheaf of integral functions, and its completed version 𝒪X = 𝒪X+[1/p] is a sheaf of complete topological rings. The key comparison isomorphisms relate the pro‑étale cohomology of 𝒪X+ to other p‑adic cohomology theories.

The first comparison is the almost étale comparison: for a smooth proper rigid space X over C, there is an almost isomorphism RΓproét(X, 𝒪X+)a ≃ RΓét(X, ℤp) ⊗ℤp Ainf. This follows from the almost purity theorem applied to the perfectoid universal cover X∞ → X. The second is the Hodge–Tate comparison: RΓproét(X, 𝒪X+)a ⊗Ainf,θ 𝒪C ≃ ⊕i ΩX/Ci[−i], where ΩX/Ci are the sheaves of differential forms. The third is the de Rham comparison: after base change along Ainf → Bdr+, one recovers the de Rham cohomology RΓdR(X/C). The fourth is the crystalline comparison: for a smooth formal 𝒪C‑model 𝔛, base change along Ainf → Acrys yields RΓcrys(𝔛k/W(k)) ⊗W(k) Acrys.

These isomorphisms are functorial in X and compatible with cup products, Frobenius, and the Galois action. The almost purity theorem is used at two stages: first to show that the perfectoid cover X∞ is affinoid perfectoid, and second to prove that the higher direct images of 𝒪X∞+ along X∞ → X are almost zero. The pro‑étale site is strictly larger than the étale site; the structure sheaf 𝒪X+ is not a sheaf on the étale site but becomes a sheaf on the pro‑étale site because the pro‑étale covers are cofinal in the v‑covers.

Examiners often test the distinction between the pro‑étale and v‑topologies. The v‑topology is generated by valuation covers, while the pro‑étale topology is generated by pro‑finite étale covers. Every pro‑étale cover is a v‑cover, but not conversely. The structure sheaf is a sheaf on the v‑site, which is essential for the definition of diamonds. Another trap is assuming that the almost isomorphism RΓproét(X, 𝒪X+)a ≃ RΓét(X, ℤp) ⊗ Ainf holds without the almost qualifier; the module of differentials ΩX/C1 is killed by the maximal ideal of 𝒪C but not zero, so the isomorphism is only almost.

The p‑adic Simpson Correspondence via Perfectoid Methods

The p‑adic Simpson correspondence, established by Liu and Zhu, relates p‑adic representations of the étale fundamental group of a smooth proper rigid space X over C to Higgs bundles on X. The perfectoid approach uses the pro‑étale site and the Hodge–Tate period map to construct a functor from the category of ℚp‑local systems on X to the category of Higgs bundles. The key input is the decomposition of the pro‑étale cohomology of the structure sheaf on the perfectoid universal cover X∞.

Let X be a smooth proper rigid space over C with a good formal model 𝔛 over 𝒪C. The perfectoid universal cover X∞ = lim← Xn is a perfectoid space with a continuous action of the étale fundamental group π1(X). The pro‑étale cohomology RΓproét(X∞, 𝒪X∞) carries a natural π1(X)‑action and a Hodge–Tate filtration. The associated graded pieces are identified with the cohomology of the sheaves of differential forms ΩX/Ci. By taking the associated graded of the Hodge–Tate filtration, one obtains a Higgs bundle (E, θ) where E = ⊕i H^i(X, ΩX/Ci) and θ is induced by the cup product with the Kodaira–Spencer class.

The correspondence is an equivalence of categories between the category of ℚp‑local systems on X that are de Rham (i.e. become trivial after tensoring with Bdr) and the category of Higgs bundles on X that are nilpotent and satisfy a certain slope condition. The perfectoid method also yields a description of the moduli space of p‑adic local systems as a diamond, and the Hodge–Tate period map identifies it with the moduli of Higgs bundles. This geometric picture is crucial for the proof of the correspondence in families and for the construction of the p‑adic non‑abelian Hodge theory.

Common examination points include the role of the almost purity theorem in ensuring that the higher direct images of 𝒪X∞ are almost zero, which guarantees that the Hodge–Tate spectral sequence degenerates almost at E2. Another point is the distinction between the p‑adic Simpson correspondence and the classical Simpson correspondence over ℂ: the former uses the pro‑étale site and perfectoid covers, while the latter uses the analytic topology and harmonic metrics. Examiners may also ask why the correspondence only works for de Rham local systems; the answer is that the Hodge–Tate period map is only defined for de Rham representations, as it requires a comparison with de Rham cohomology.

The Moduli of p‑Divisible Groups and Rapoport–Zink Spaces in the Perfectoid Setting

Rapoport–Zink spaces are formal schemes that parametrize deformations of a given p‑divisible group with additional structure (e.g. level structures, polarizations). Their generic fibres are rigid analytic spaces that admit a perfectoid uniformization at infinite level. Let (G, ι, λ) be a p‑divisible group over 𝔽p with an action of a p‑adic ring 𝒪F and a polarization. The Rapoport–Zink space M parametrizes quasi‑isogenies G → G′ of p‑divisible groups over 𝒪C‑algebras with compatible 𝒪F‑action and polarization. The tower of level‑n spaces Mn is a pro‑finite étale cover of M, and the inverse limit M∞ = lim← Mn is a perfectoid space.

The perfectoid uniformization theorem (Scholze–Weinstein) states that M∞ is isomorphic to a perfectoid open subset of a perfectoid flag variety. The Hodge–Tate period map πHT : M∞ → Flag is a GLn(ℚp)‑equivariant map with affinoid perfectoid fibres. The fibres are isomorphic to the perfectoid universal cover of the Drinfeld upper half‑space. This uniformization allows one to compute the cohomology of M∞ using the pro‑étale site and the almost purity theorem, yielding a geometric realization of the local Langlands correspondence for GLn(ℚp) and its inner forms.

The moduli of p‑divisible groups also appears in the construction of the Fargues–Fontaine curve. The curve XFF is the quotient of the perfectoid space Spa(Cp♭) {0} by the action of the Frobenius φ. Vector bundles on XFF correspond to isocrystals, and the Harder–Narasimhan filtration on vector bundles matches the slope filtration on isocrystals. The perfectoid uniformization of Rapoport–Zink spaces provides a geometric interpretation of the local Shimura varieties that appear in the Langlands program.

Examiners frequently test the statement that the infinite‑level Rapoport–Zink space is perfectoid. The proof uses the almost purity theorem: the tower Mn → M is a pro‑finite étale cover with finite étale transition maps, and the almost purity theorem implies that the inverse limit of the affinoid algebras is affinoid perfectoid. Another trap is confusing the Rapoport–Zink space with the Lubin–Tate space; the former parametrizes deformations of a p‑divisible group, while the latter parametrizes deformations of a formal group law. The Lubin–Tate space is a special case of a Rapoport–Zink space for a one-dimensional p‑divisible group with 𝒪F-action.

Log Perfectoid Spaces and the Almost Purity Theorem for Log Structures

The extension of perfectoid geometry to the logarithmic setting, developed by Scholze and refined by Bhatt–Scholze and Cesnavicius, is essential for handling semistable reduction and the weight‑monodromy conjecture in families. A log perfectoid space is a pair (X, M_X) where X is a perfectoid space and M_X is a fine, saturated log structure on the pro‑étale site X_proet such that locally on X the log structure is generated by a single element t with t^p = p in the tilt. The key technical result is the log almost purity theorem: if f : Y → X is a finite étale morphism of log perfectoid spaces that is log étale (i.e. the map of log structures is an isomorphism on the associated monoids), then the direct image f_* O_Y+ is almost finite étale over O_X+ in the almost category defined by the ideal m = (p1/p^∞). This theorem is used to show that the log pro‑étale site of a log perfectoid space has cohomological dimension zero for almost coherent sheaves, exactly as in the non‑log case. A standard proof reduces to the case of a Kummer cover Spec A⟨T1/n⟩ → Spec A where A is a perfectoid algebra, and then applies the almost purity theorem for perfectoid algebras together with the fact that the log structure is generated by p1/p^∞. A crucial application is the construction of the log prismatic site and the comparison between log prismatic cohomology and log syntomic cohomology, which provides a p‑adic cohomology theory for varieties with semistable reduction. Examiners often test the distinction between a log étale map and a merely étale map of the underlying spaces: the former requires the monoid map to be an isomorphism, which forces the ramification to be tame and compatible with the log structure. Another common point is that the log almost purity theorem fails if one works with the classical étale site instead of the pro‑étale site, because the Kummer covers needed to trivialize the log structure are only pro‑finite étale.

The Perfectoid Theory of p‑adic Period Domains for General Reductive Groups

Classical p‑adic period domains, introduced by Rapoport–Zink, are rigid analytic spaces that parametrize filtrations on a fixed isocrystal satisfying the Griffiths transversality condition. Scholze’s perfectoid approach replaces these with perfectoid period domains defined as open subsets of the adic flag variety Fl_G = G/B for a reductive group G over Q_p. Let b ∈ G(Q_p) be a basic element and μ a minuscule cocharacter. The associated local Shimura variety (or Rapoport–Zink space) at infinite level M∞ is a perfectoid space equipped with a G(Q_p)‑equivariant Hodge–Tate period map π_HT : M∞ → Fl_G whose image is the admissible locus Fl_Ga,μ (or the weakly admissible locus Fl_Gwa,μ depending on the context). For G = GL_n this recovers the Drinfeld upper half‑space and the flag variety of filtrations on an n‑dimensional vector space. For a general reductive group, the admissible locus is defined by the condition that the filtration induced by μ on the isocrystal (D, bσ) satisfies the weak admissibility condition of Fontaine–Rapoport. The perfectoid uniformization theorem states that M∞ is isomorphic to the preimage of the admissible locus under the Hodge–Tate period map on the perfectoid cover of the flag variety. The fibres of π_HT are perfectoid spaces that are affinoid perfectoid and carry a transitive action of the centralizer J_b(Q_p). This geometric picture allows one to compute the cohomology of M∞ using the pro‑étale site and to realize the local Langlands correspondence geometrically. A key technical tool is the Bhatt–Scholze projection formula for the structure sheaf on the v‑site, which identifies the higher direct images of OM∞+ along π_HT with almost coherent sheaves on the flag variety. Examiners frequently ask why the period map lands in the adic flag variety rather than the classical flag variety: the answer is that the adic flag variety parametrizes untilts of the tilt, which correspond to filtrations on the de Rham cohomology of the perfectoid cover, and its points are valued in perfectoid fields, not just in C_p. Another trap is confusing the weakly admissible locus with the admissible locus; they coincide for GL_n but differ for general groups, and the correct target for the period map in the perfectoid uniformization is the admissible locus.

Worked Problem: Log Almost Purity for Kummer Covers
Let A = C_p⟨T1/p^∞⟩ be the perfectoid Tate algebra with the log structure generated by T. Consider the Kummer cover B = A⟨U⟩/(U^p - T). Show that B is almost finite étale over A in the log almost category.
The map A → B is finite free of rank p. The log structure on A is given by the monoid N → A, 1 ↦ T. On B the log structure is the pullback, generated by U with U^p = T. The map of monoids N → N, 1 ↦ p is injective with cokernel Z/pZ, so the map is log étale after inverting p. The discriminant of B/A is p^p Tp-1. In the almost category defined by m = (p1/p^∞), the element p is invertible up to almost zero, and T is a unit in A (since T is a pseudo‑uniformizer). Hence the discriminant is almost invertible, so B is almost finite étale over A. The log almost purity theorem then implies that for any log perfectoid space X with a chart to Spa(A), the pullback of this cover is almost finite étale on X_proet.

Perfectoid Covers, the p‑adic Monodromy Theorem, and (φ, Γ)‑Modules

The p‑adic monodromy theorem of Fontaine (proved by Berger, Colmez, and Kedlaya) states that any de Rham Galois representation of GQ_p becomes crystalline over a finite extension, or equivalently, that the associated (φ, Γ)-module over the Robba ring is overconvergent. Perfectoid geometry provides a geometric proof via the perfectoid universal cover of the punctured disk. Let X = Spa(Q_p⟨T1/p^∞⟩) be the perfectoid open unit disk, and let X_∞ = Spa(Q_p⟨T1/p^∞⟩[T-1/p^∞]) be the perfectoid punctured disk. The pro‑étale fundamental group of X_∞ is isomorphic to Z_p(1), and the Galois group Gal(Q_p(μp^∞)/Q_p) acts via the cyclotomic character. A (φ, Γ)-module over the Robba ring corresponds to a vector bundle on X_∞ equipped with a Frobenius φ and a Γ-action. The perfectoid Riemann–Hilbert correspondence (due to Kedlaya–Liu and Scholze) identifies the category of (φ, Γ)-modules over the Robba ring with the category of pro‑étale local systems on X_∞ that are de Rham. The key step is the construction of the period sheaf O𝔹dR on the pro‑étale site of X_∞, which is a sheaf of complete discrete valuation rings with residue field C_p. The de Rham condition translates to the requirement that the O𝔹dR-cohomology of the local system is concentrated in degree zero and has the correct Hodge–Tate weights. The p‑adic monodromy theorem then follows from the fact that any de Rham local system on X_∞ becomes trivial on a finite étale cover of X_∞ that is itself a perfectoid space (a Kummer cover T ↦ T1/n). This geometric proof avoids the difficult analytic estimates of the original proofs and clarifies the role of the Sen operator as the derivative of the Γ-action on the pro‑étale site. Examiners often test the equivalence between de Rham (φ, Γ)-modules and de Rham Galois representations: the functor is DdR(V) = (V ⊗ BdR)GQ_p, and the perfectoid interpretation is H0proet(X_∞, V ⊗ O𝔹dR). A common trap is to confuse the Robba ring (which is a ring of functions on an open annulus) with the perfectoid algebra Q_p⟨T1/p^∞⟩[T-1/p^∞]; the latter is the completed direct limit of the former under Frobenius, and the (φ, Γ)-action extends continuously to it.

The Perfectoid Approach to Eigenvarieties and the Eigencurve

Eigenvarieties, introduced by Buzzard and Coleman–Mazur, are rigid analytic spaces that parametrize finite‑slope p‑adic families of overconvergent modular forms (or more generally, automorphic forms). The perfectoid approach, developed by Andreatta–Iovita–Pilloni and Scholze, constructs eigenvarieties as perfectoid spaces at infinite level. Let X be a compactified modular curve over Q_p, and let X_∞ = lim_← X(p^n) be the inverse limit of the modular curves with full p^n-level structure. This inverse limit is a perfectoid space (the perfectoid modular curve). The sheaf of overconvergent modular forms of weight κ on X_∞ is a perfectoid Banach sheaf ω^κ on the pro‑étale site. The Hecke operators T_p and U_p act on the cohomology H0proet(X_∞, ω^κ), and the eigenvariety is the rigid analytic space (in fact, a diamond) that parametrizes the eigenvalues of these operators on finite‑slope generalized eigenspaces. The perfectoid construction yields a perfectoid eigenvariety E_∞ which is a perfectoid space mapping to weight space W (the rigid analytic space of p‑adic characters of Z_p^×). A key theorem states that the projection E_∞ → W is partially proper and the fibres are zero‑dimensional perfectoid spaces. This allows one to prove the classicality theorem (finite‑slope overconvergent forms of classical weight are classical) by showing that the Hodge–Tate period map on X_∞ identifies the eigenvariety with a union of irreducible components of the perfectoid flag variety. The Hodge–Tate period map π_HT : X_∞ → P1 (for GL2) is a GL2(Q_p)-equivariant map whose fibres are affinoid perfectoid spaces. The pullback of the Hodge bundle ω along π_HT is trivial, which implies that the cohomology of ω^κ can be computed using the pro‑étale site and the almost purity theorem. A critical technical point is that the U_p-operator corresponds to the correspondence given by the Frobenius on the perfectoid modular curve, and its finite‑slope part is isolated using the slope filtration on the associated (φ, Γ)-modules. Examiners often ask why the eigenvariety is a perfectoid space at infinite level: the answer is that the transition maps in the tower X(p^n) → X(pn-1) are finite étale, so the almost purity theorem implies the inverse limit is perfectoid. Another trap is confusing the eigencurve (the one‑dimensional eigenvariety for GL2/Q) with the higher‑dimensional eigenvarieties for GL_n or for definite unitary groups; the perfectoid method works uniformly for all these cases.

Perfectoid Spaces and the p‑adic Riemann–Hilbert Correspondence on the Fargues–Fontaine Curve

The p‑adic Riemann–Hilbert correspondence on the Fargues–Fontaine curve XFF establishes an equivalence between the category of étale (φ, Γ)-modules (or equivalently, p‑adic Galois representations of GQ_p) and the category of regular singular connections on vector bundles over XFF. In the perfectoid setting, this correspondence is realized geometrically using the pro‑étale site of the curve. The Fargues–Fontaine curve is the quotient XFF = (Spa(C_p^♭) {0}) / φ^Z, where C_p^♭ is the tilt of C_p. Its pro‑étale site admits a period sheaf O𝔹dR which is a sheaf of complete discrete valuation rings with residue field the structure sheaf of XFF. A vector bundle E on XFF equipped with a connection ∇ : E → E ⊗ Ω1XFF that is regular singular (i.e. has logarithmic poles at the "points at infinity" corresponding to the untilts) corresponds to a (φ, Γ)-module over the Robba ring via the functor DdR(E, ∇) = H0proet(XFF, E ⊗ O𝔹dR). The perfectoid uniformization of XFF by Spa(C_p^♭) {0} allows one to pull back the connection to a Frobenius‑equivariant connection on the perfectoid space, where the φ-action is the Frobenius on the tilt. The Kedlaya–Liu theorem on the p‑adic local monodromy theorem translates into the statement that any regular singular connection on a vector bundle over XFF becomes trivial on a finite étale cover of XFF that is a perfectoid space. This geometric proof avoids the difficult analysis of the Robba ring and replaces it with the almost purity theorem on the perfectoid cover. The correspondence is compatible with the Harder–Narasimhan filtration on vector bundles: the slope filtration on the (φ, Γ)-module matches the Harder–Narasimhan filtration on the vector bundle. Examiners frequently test the identification of the points of the Fargues–Fontaine curve with untilts of C_p^♭: a point x ∈ |XFF| corresponds to an equivalence class of untilts C_p^♭ → K, and the residue field at x is the completion of K. The vector bundle O(1) on XFF corresponds to the (φ, Γ)-module Brig^† (the Robba ring), and its global sections are H0(XFF, O(1)) = BdR+. A common trap is to treat XFF as a classical curve over a field; it is a diamond (a sheaf on the category of perfectoid spaces), its structure sheaf is not a sheaf of fields, and its cohomology groups are not finite‑dimensional vector spaces but rather topological Q_p-vector spaces.

Perfectoid Spaces and the Emerton–Gee Stack for p‑adic Galois Representations

The Emerton–Gee stack Xn is a formal algebraic stack over ℤₚ that parameterizes continuous representations ρ: Gal(ℚ̄ₚ/ℚₚ) → GLn(𝔽̄ₚ) together with a choice of lattice. Its generic fiber is a diamond, and the stack plays a central role in the moduli of p‑adic Galois representations. Perfectoid methods enter through the construction of a perfectoid cover at infinite level: one considers the inverse limit of the finite étale covers Xn(pm) → Xn obtained by adding full level‑pm structures on the associated (φ, Γ)‑modules. By the almost purity theorem, this inverse limit is a perfectoid space Xn,∞. The pro‑étale site of Xn,∞ carries a sheaf of period rings 𝔹dR⁺, and the cohomology of Xn,∞ with coefficients in 𝔹dR⁺ computes the p‑adic Hodge theory of the universal representation. The irreducible components of the special fiber of Xn correspond to Serre weights, and the perfectoid uniformization allows one to relate the geometry of these components to the representation theory of GLn(ℚₚ). In particular, the Breuil–Mézard conjecture (now a theorem in many cases) is approached via an analysis of the cycles on Xn,∞ and the action of the Hecke operators on its cohomology. The perfectoid cover is also expected to provide a geometric realization of the local Langlands correspondence for GLn via the cohomology of the Emerton–Gee stack, where the Hecke action matches the action of the Bernstein center on the category of smooth representations.

Perfectoid Methods in the Geometric Satake Equivalence for p‑adic Groups

The classical geometric Satake equivalence identifies the category of representations of a complex reductive group G with the category of perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian GrG over ℂ. For p‑adic groups, the affine Grassmannian is an ind‑scheme over ℤₚ, and its geometry is more subtle. Scholze and Zhu constructed a perfectoid version of the affine Grassmannian at infinite level: one takes the inverse limit of the finite étale covers GrG(pm) → GrG given by the multiplication‑by‑pm maps on the loop group. The almost purity theorem implies that this inverse limit is a perfectoid space (in fact a diamond) GrG,∞. The loop group LG acts on GrG,∞, and the category of LG‑equivariant perverse sheaves on GrG,∞ with coefficients in ℚ̄ₗ is equivalent to the category of representations of the Langlands dual group G∨ over ℚ̄ₗ. This equivalence is the p‑adic geometric Satake correspondence. The perfectoid uniformization is essential because it provides a space on which the Frobenius acts, allowing one to define the Satake transform via the cohomology of the Hecke correspondences. The construction also yields a compatibility with the local Langlands correspondence: the Hecke eigenvalues on the cohomology of GrG,∞ match the Satake parameters of the corresponding representations of G(ℚₚ). Moreover, the perfectoid affine Grassmannian admits a stratification by Schubert cells, and the intersection cohomology complexes on these cells give the canonical basis of the representation ring of G∨.

The Emerton–Gee stack Xn parameterizes Galois representations with a choice of lattice; level structures are added to form the tower. For each m ≥ 1 there is a finite étale cover Xn(pm) → Xn obtained by trivializing the associated (φ, Γ)‑module modulo pm. The transition maps Xn(pm+1) → Xn(pm) are also finite étale. The inverse limit Xn,∞ = lim← m Xn(pm) exists as a diamond. By the almost purity theorem, any inverse limit of finite étale covers of a perfectoid space is perfectoid. Since the generic fiber of Xn is a diamond that is locally perfectoid (covered by perfectoid spaces), the tower consists of perfectoid spaces and the limit Xn,∞ is perfectoid. The pro‑étale site of Xn,∞ then carries the period sheaves 𝔹dR⁺, 𝔹cris, etc., enabling p‑adic Hodge theory computations.

Perfectoid Uniformization of Rapoport–Zink Spaces for General Reductive Groups

Rapoport–Zink spaces ℳG,μ are formal schemes over ℤₚ that classify deformations of a p‑divisible group with additional structure (a G‑structure) up to quasi‑isogeny. For GLn, Scholze proved that the infinite‑level space ℳGLn,μ,∞ = lim← m ℳGLn,μ(pm) is a perfectoid space, and the natural map ℳGLn,μ,∞ → ℳGLn,μ is a pro‑étale torsor under the group Jb(ℚₚ) of self‑quasi‑isogenies. For a general unramified reductive group G, the work of Caraiani–Scholze and of Shen extends this uniformization: the infinite‑level Rapoport–Zink space ℳG,μ,∞ is a perfectoid space (or a perfectoid diamond) and the uniformization map is a pro‑étale Jb(ℚₚ)‑torsor. The proof uses the fact that the tower ℳG,μ(pm) is obtained by adding Drinfeld level structures, which are finite étale covers. The almost purity theorem then guarantees that the limit is perfectoid. This uniformization yields a description of the ℓ‑adic cohomology of ℳG,μ in terms of representations of Jb(ℚₚ) and of G(ℚₚ), realizing the local Langlands correspondence and the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence geometrically. In the ramified case, the uniformization is more delicate; one must replace the Rapoport–Zink space by a suitable perfectoid cover of the associated local Shimura variety, and the almost purity theorem still applies after passing to a suitable log structure.

Perfectoid Spaces and the p‑adic Hodge Theory of Rigid Analytic Varieties via Prismatic Cohomology

Prismatic cohomology, introduced by Bhatt and Scholze, assigns to a p‑adic formal scheme 𝔛 a commutative algebra Δ𝔛 in the derived category of (p‑complete) ℤₚ‑modules, equipped with a Frobenius φ and a Nygaard filtration. For a rigid analytic variety X over ℚₚ, one can define its prismatic cohomology by working on the perfectoid pro‑étale site Xproet. The almost purity theorem provides a perfectoid cover X∞ → X (for instance, the inverse limit of finite étale covers obtained by taking p‑power roots of coordinates on an affinoid cover). The prismatic complex of X is then computed as the derived global sections of the structure sheaf 𝒪𝔸 on the prismatic site of X∞. This yields canonical comparison isomorphisms: ΔX ⊗𝔸 𝔸/φ ≃ RΓét(X, ℤₚ), ΔX ⊗𝔸 𝔸/(φ−1) ≃ RΓdR(X/ℚₚ), and the Nygaard filtration recovers the Hodge filtration on de Rham cohomology. The perfectoid cover simplifies the proof of the p‑adic Poincaré lemma: on X∞ the de Rham complex is quasi‑isomorphic to the constant sheaf ℚₚ because the Frobenius acts bijectively on the completed cotangent complex. Consequently, the Hodge–Tate spectral sequence degenerates at E₂, and the Hodge–Tate weights are read off from the Nygaard filtration. This approach also gives a conceptual proof of the integral p‑adic Hodge theory for proper smooth rigid analytic varieties, avoiding the use of semistable models.

Perfectoid Spaces and the Categorical p‑adic Langlands Correspondence for GLn

The categorical p‑adic Langlands program seeks an equivalence between a category of sheaves on the moduli of p‑adic Galois representations and a category of representations of the p‑adic Langlands dual group. For GLn(ℚₚ), the Emerton–Gee stack Xn and the moduli stack of (φ, Γ)‑modules provide the geometric input. Using the perfectoid uniformization Xn,∞ of the Emerton–Gee stack, one can define a category Cohla(Xn,∞) of locally analytic coherent sheaves on the perfectoid diamond. The Hecke operators act on this category via correspondences on the perfectoid cover of the Emerton–Gee stack at infinite level. On the other side, the category Repℚₚ(GLn(ℚₚ)) of smooth representations admits a spectral action of the Bernstein center. The work of Dat, Helm, Moss, and others constructs a functor from Cohla(Xn,∞) to a suitable derived category of (φ, Γ)‑modules, which is expected to be an equivalence after localization at the Eisenstein ideal. For GL₂, the categorical correspondence is fully established: the category of coherent sheaves on the perfectoid modular curve at infinite level is equivalent to the category of smooth representations of GL₂(ℚₚ) via the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence of Colmez. For GLn with n > 2, partial results exist (e.g., for generic representations), but the general equivalence remains conjectural. The perfectoid methods are indispensable because they provide a geometric space on which both the Galois side and the automorphic side act, and the almost purity theorem ensures that the relevant covers are perfectoid, allowing the use of p‑adic Hodge theory sheaves.

The Drinfeld upper half space Ωd over a p‑adic field F is a rigid analytic variety that parametrizes certain formal groups of height d. The perfectoid approach also provides a natural setting for the study of the p‑adic Banach space representations that appear in the completed cohomology of Ωd, and the almost purity theorem ensures that the relevant period rings are perfectoid, so that Fontaine’s p‑adic Hodge theory applies without modification.

Perfectoid Spaces and the Theory of (φ,Γ)-Modules over the Robba Ring via Perfectoid Covers

The Robba ring R is the ring of germs of analytic functions on an open annulus around the unit circle in the p‑adic setting. It carries a Frobenius φ and an action of the group Γ=Zp× (or a suitable open subgroup). Classical p‑adic Hodge theory attaches to a p‑adic Galois representation V a (φ,Γ)-module Drigdagger(V) over R. Perfectoid methods give a geometric construction of this equivalence: let X be a perfectoid space that is a pro‑étale cover of a rigid analytic variety Y (for instance, the perfectoid cover of the open unit disc obtained by taking all pn-th roots of the coordinate). The pro‑étale site Xproet carries a sheaf OBrigdagger whose global sections recover the Robba ring. The almost purity theorem ensures that the cover X→ Y is almost finite étale, so the pushforward of OBrigdagger is almost isomorphic to the corresponding sheaf on Yproet.

On the perfectoid cover X the Frobenius φ acts bijectively on the completed cotangent complex, which implies that the de Rham complex of OBrigdagger is quasi‑isomorphic to the constant sheaf Qp. This is the perfectoid version of the p‑adic Poincaré lemma. Consequently, the derived global sections RΓ(Xproet,OBrigdagger) compute the (φ,Γ)-module attached to the Galois representation given by the étale cohomology of Y. The equivalence of categories between p‑adic Galois representations and étale (φ,Γ)-modules over R is then realized as the statement that the functor V↦ (RΓ(Xproet,OBrigdagger)⊗QpV)Γ=1 is an equivalence, with quasi‑inverse given by the Galois cohomology of the associated (φ,Γ)-module.

The perfectoid cover also provides a natural setting for the theory of (φ,Γ)-modules with coefficients in a Banach algebra. If A is a perfectoid Qp-algebra, the relative Robba ring RA is defined as the completed tensor product Rwidehat{⊗}QpA. The same construction works on the perfectoid space Spa(A), and the almost purity theorem guarantees that the relative Robba sheaf is v‑acyclic. This allows one to define families of (φ,Γ)-modules parametrized by perfectoid spaces, which is the geometric input for the construction of eigenvarieties in the perfectoid setting (see the perfectoid approach to eigenvarieties).

A key technical point is the identification of the Galois group of the perfectoid cover with a subgroup of Γ. For the perfectoid open unit disc D∞=varprojlimx↦ xp D, the Galois group of the cover D∞→ D is canonically isomorphic to Zp(1), which embeds into Γ via the cyclotomic character. The action of this Galois group on the Robba sheaf coincides with the Γ-action, so the (φ,Γ)-module structure arises naturally from the Galois action on the perfectoid cover. This identification is a direct consequence of the almost purity theorem, which gives an almost isomorphism between the completed direct image of the structure sheaf and the structure sheaf of the base.

Finally, the perfectoid approach clarifies the relationship between the Robba ring and the period ring BdR+. The map R→ BdR+ sending a germ to its Taylor expansion at 1 is compatible with the Frobenius and Γ-actions. On the perfectoid cover, this map is realized as the map from the structure sheaf OBrigdagger to the de Rham period sheaf OBdR+, and the almost purity theorem ensures that the kernel and cokernel are almost zero. This gives a conceptual proof of the comparison isomorphism between the (φ,Γ)-module and the de Rham representation attached to a p‑adic Galois representation.

Log smooth rigid analytic varieties are rigid spaces equipped with a log structure that is locally modeled on a toric monoid. An important application is the weight‑monodromy conjecture for log smooth varieties. The log perfectoid cover provides a geometric realization of the monodromy operator as the action of the Galois group of the cover on the log étale cohomology. The degeneration of the log Hodge–Tate spectral sequence at E2 (proved using the log p‑adic Poincaré lemma) is known in many cases and provides strong evidence for the weight‑monodromy conjecture for the log étale cohomology of proper log smooth varieties over a p‑adic field.

Finally, the log perfectoid theory interacts with the theory of diamonds: the quotient of the log perfectoid cover by the relevant p‑adic group is expected to be a log diamond that represents the moduli of log p‑divisible groups. The v‑topology on this log diamond is generated by log perfectoid affinoids, and the log almost purity theorem guarantees that the log structure sheaf is v‑acyclic. This acyclicity is a key input for the construction of log Hecke operators and for progress toward a log version of the local Langlands correspondence for groups with non‑trivial log structure (e.g., unitary groups with parahoric level structure).

Perfectoid Covers of Lubin–Tate Towers and the p-adic Local Langlands Correspondence for GLn

The Lubin–Tate tower parametrizes deformations of a fixed formal group law of height n over a p-adic field. At finite level the tower consists of rigid analytic varieties that are not proper, but their inverse limit over all levels carries a natural action of GLn(ℚp) × D× × WF, where D is the central division algebra of invariant 1/n and WF is the Weil group. Scholze and Weinstein showed that the completed inverse limit is a perfectoid space. The perfectoid cover is obtained by adjoining all p-power level structures to the universal deformation; the transition maps are finite étale away from the supersingular locus and become almost finite étale after passing to the almost category. The almost purity theorem guarantees that the tilt of this perfectoid space is the analogous Lubin–Tate tower in characteristic p, where the geometry is governed by the moduli of p-divisible groups with Drinfeld level structure. The p-adic local Langlands correspondence for GLn is then realized by studying the étale cohomology of the perfectoid Lubin–Tate tower with coefficients in p-adic representations of GLn(ℚp). The action of the Weil group on the cohomology is recovered via the Fargues–Fontaine curve: the perfectoid cover maps to the curve, and vector bundles on the curve correspond to Galois representations. The resulting correspondence matches irreducible smooth representations of GLn(ℚp) with n-dimensional p-adic Galois representations, compatibly with the local Langlands correspondence modulo p. A key technical input is the computation of the pro-étale cohomology of the perfectoid cover, which is almost isomorphic to the cohomology of the tilt, allowing the use of characteristic p methods such as the Cartier isomorphism. The perfectoid approach also yields a geometric construction of the local factors of the correspondence, including the epsilon factors, via the determinant of the cohomology complex.

Perfectoid Siegel Modular Varieties and the p-adic Hodge Theory of Infinite-Level Shimura Varieties of Hodge Type

The Siegel modular variety of genus g parametrizes principally polarized abelian varieties of dimension g with level structure. At infinite prime-to-p level the inverse limit is a perfectoid space, as proved by Scholze. For Shimura varieties of Hodge type, which include Siegel varieties as a special case, the infinite-level space is also perfectoid. The perfectoid cover is constructed by taking the inverse limit over all p-power level structures at a fixed prime p, while keeping the prime-to-p level fixed. The resulting space carries an action of the p-adic group G(ℚp) where G is the reductive group associated to the Shimura datum. The Hodge–Tate period map πHT from the perfectoid cover to the flag variety is a G(ℚp)-equivariant morphism of perfectoid spaces. Its fibers are affinoid perfectoid spaces that are closely related to the Rapoport–Zink spaces for the associated p-divisible groups. The p-adic Hodge theory of the infinite-level Shimura variety is encoded in the pro-étale cohomology of the perfectoid cover. The Hodge–Tate decomposition expresses the p-adic étale cohomology as a direct sum of completed Hodge cohomology groups twisted by cyclotomic characters. This decomposition is proved by showing that the pullback of the Hodge bundle along πHT is trivialized on the perfectoid cover, which follows from the almost purity theorem and the fact that the Hodge–Tate period map is a pro-étale torsor. The weight spectral sequence degenerates at E2 for proper smooth Shimura varieties, giving a pure Hodge structure on the étale cohomology. For non-compact Shimura varieties, the boundary strata are also perfectoid, and the log perfectoid theory extends the Hodge–Tate decomposition to the log setting. The perfectoid Siegel modular variety at infinite level also provides a geometric setting for the construction of p-adic automorphic forms: the space of p-adic modular forms of infinite level is identified with the global sections of certain line bundles on the perfectoid cover, and the action of Hecke operators is realized by correspondences on the perfectoid space.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: "Explain why the perfectoid cover of the Lubin–Tate tower is not a perfectoid space in the naive sense if one only adjoins p-power level structures without completing."
The inverse limit of the finite-level Lubin–Tate spaces with only p-power level structures is not quasi-compact and its structure sheaf is not complete for the natural topology. The perfectoid cover requires taking the completion of the direct limit of the coordinate rings, which yields a perfectoid Tate ring. Without completion, the Frobenius map on the tilt is not surjective, so the tilt is not perfectoid. The almost purity theorem applies only after completion, because the transition maps become almost finite étale only in the completed setting. Thus the naive inverse limit fails the perfectoid condition that the Frobenius on the tilt be an isomorphism.

Perfectoid Spaces and the p-adic Uniformization of Shimura Varieties of Abelian Type

Shimura varieties of abelian type are those whose associated Shimura datum is of Hodge type after a suitable central extension. They include many classical modular varieties, such as Hilbert modular varieties and Siegel modular varieties with non-principal polarization. The p-adic uniformization theorem for these varieties states that the completion of the Shimura variety along its supersingular locus is isomorphic to a quotient of a perfectoid Rapoport–Zink space by a discrete subgroup of the associated p-adic group. The perfectoid Rapoport–Zink space is the infinite-level cover of the Rapoport–Zink space, which parametrizes p-divisible groups with additional structure (polarization, endomorphisms, level structure). The uniformization map is constructed by sending a point of the perfectoid Rapoport–Zink space to the associated abelian variety with its p-divisible group, using the Serre–Tate theorem in the perfectoid setting. The almost purity theorem guarantees that the map is a pro-étale cover onto its image. The quotient by the discrete subgroup is taken in the category of diamonds, yielding a perfectoid diamond that represents the moduli problem. The cohomology of the Shimura variety is then related to the cohomology of the perfectoid Rapoport–Zink space via the Leray spectral sequence for the uniformization map. The de Rham‑Witt complex WΩ•R of a perfectoid ring R provides a bridge between prismatic cohomology and classical crystalline cohomology. The perfectoid approach thus unifies all classical p-adic Hodge theory comparisons within a single geometric framework.

The Hodge‑Tate comparison isomorphism is the most accessible of the p-adic Hodge theory comparisons and serves as a prototype for the deeper de Rham and crystalline comparisons. A student claims: "The Nygaard filtration on prismatic cohomology is the prismatic analogue of the Hodge filtration, so they coincide under the de Rham comparison isomorphism." Identify the error and state the precise relationship.
The error is claiming the filtrations coincide on prismatic cohomology itself. The Nygaard filtration N≥•ΔX/A and the Hodge filtration Fil•H•dR(X/OK) live on different cohomology theories. Under the de Rham comparison isomorphism ΔX/A ⊗A BdR ≃ H•dR(X/OK) ⊗OK BdR, the Nygaard filtration on the left corresponds to the Hodge filtration on the right only after tensoring with BdR. On the prismatic side, the Nygaard filtration is defined via Frobenius: N≥r = {x | φ(x) ∈ Ir}. On the de Rham side, the Hodge filtration is defined by the stupid filtration on the de Rham complex. They are not equal on the integral prismatic cohomology; the isomorphism of filtered modules holds only after BdR-base change. The Breuil-Kisin twist O{r} on the prismatic side corresponds to the Tate twist on the Galois side, not to the Hodge filtration twist.
Exam Strategy: Filtration Comparisons
When asked about filtration comparisons in p-adic Hodge theory, always specify the coefficient ring. The Nygaard filtration on ΔX/A matches the Hodge filtration on H•dR only after ⊗ BdR. The Nygaard filtration on ΔX/A matches the slope filtration on crystalline cohomology after ⊗ Bcris. The Hodge-Tate comparison is the associated graded of the de Rham comparison with respect to the Hodge filtration, which corresponds to the Nygaard filtration on the prismatic side. Memorize the triangle: Nygaard (prismatic) → Hodge (de Rham) via BdR; Nygaard (prismatic) → Slope (crystalline) via Bcris; Hodge (de Rham) → Hodge-Tate (associated graded) via gr. Never say two filtrations "are the same" without specifying the base ring.

The Hodge-Tate Period Map on Infinite-Level Shimura Varieties: Fibers, Hecke Action, and the Construction of Galois Representations

The Hodge-Tate period map πHT: Sh∞ → Fl is a morphism from the infinite-level Shimura variety Sh∞ (a perfectoid space) to the flag variety Fl associated to the Shimura datum. For a Shimura variety of Hodge type with group G, the flag variety Fl = G/P where P is the parabolic subgroup determined by the Hodge cocharacter μ. The map πHT sends a point x ∈ Sh∞(C) to the Hodge filtration on the associated p-divisible group, giving a point in the flag variety. The fibers of πHT are affinoid perfectoid spaces that are pro-étale torsors under the unipotent radical of P. The Hecke action of G(Qp) on Sh∞ commutes with πHT up to the natural action on Fl, making πHT a G(Qp)-equivariant map. This equivariance is crucial for extracting Galois representations from the cohomology of Sh∞. The cohomology H•(Sh∞, Qp) carries an action of G(Qp) × GQ (where GQ is the absolute Galois group of the reflex field). The Leray spectral sequence for πHT degenerates because the fibers have cohomological dimension zero in the pro-étale topology. This yields a decomposition H•(Sh∞, Qp) ≃ ⊕w H•-ℓ(w)(Fl, ICw) ⊗ Vw where ICw are intersection cohomology complexes on the flag variety and Vw are Galois representations. The Galois representations Vw are constructed as the cohomology of the fibers of πHT with coefficients in local systems coming from the universal p-divisible group. The almost purity theorem shows that the map Sh∞ → ShK to a finite-level Shimura variety is almost étale, so the pro-étale cohomology of Sh∞ computes the étale cohomology of ShK in the almost category. The Hodge-Tate period map thus provides a geometric bridge between the automorphic side (cohomology of Shimura varieties with Hecke action) and the Galois side (representations of GQ). The fibers of πHT are perfectoid covers of the basic affine space G/U, and their cohomology is connected to the local Langlands correspondence for G(Qp) via the Fargues-Fontaine curve.

The p-adic Simpson Correspondence: Higgs Bundles, Local Systems, and the Non-Abelian Hodge Theory on the Fargues-Fontaine Curve

The p-adic Simpson correspondence, established by Liu-Zhu and developed further by Fargues-Scholze, is a p-adic analogue of the classical Simpson correspondence between Higgs bundles and local systems on a complex variety. In the p-adic setting, the correspondence relates two categories on a smooth proper rigid analytic variety X over a p-adic field K: the category of Higgs bundles (E, θ) where E is a vector bundle on X and θ: E → E ⊗ Ω1X is a Higgs field satisfying θ ∧ θ = 0, and the category of Qp-local systems on X (i.e., continuous representations of the pro-étale fundamental group π1(X) on finite-dimensional Qp-vector spaces). The correspondence is mediated by the Fargues-Fontaine curve XFF associated to the untilt of X. The Hodge-Tate period map πHT: X∞ → T*X[-1] from the perfectoid universal cover X∞ to the cotangent bundle of X identifies the pro-étale cohomology of X∞ with the de Rham cohomology of X twisted by the Hodge-Tate weights. A Higgs bundle (E, θ) gives rise to a vector bundle on the Fargues-Fontaine curve via the BdR-affine Grassmannian, and the condition θ ∧ θ = 0 translates to the condition that the corresponding vector bundle is semistable of slope zero. Conversely, a Qp-local system gives a vector bundle on XFF via the p-adic Riemann-Hilbert correspondence, and the semistability condition corresponds to the local system being de Rham. The correspondence is not an equivalence of categories in general; it is fully faithful on the subcategory of small Higgs bundles (those with nilpotent Higgs field of bounded nilpotence order) and small local systems (those with bounded Hodge-Tate weights). The almost purity theorem is used to descend the correspondence from the perfectoid cover X∞ to X. The pro-étale site of X is essential because the Higgs field θ is not integrable in the classical sense, but becomes integrable on the perfectoid cover where the Hodge-Tate period map trivializes the cotangent complex. The p-adic Simpson correspondence has profound applications to the p-adic Langlands program: it provides a geometric construction of the correspondence between Galois representations and automorphic forms via the cohomology of Shimura varieties, and it relates the categorical p-adic Langlands correspondence for GLn to the geometric Langlands correspondence on the Fargues-Fontaine curve.

Almost Mathematics on the v-Site: Almost Coherent Sheaves, the Almost Purity Theorem, and Almost Étale Descent

The theory of almost mathematics, developed by Faltings and systematized by Gabber-Ramero, is the technical foundation that makes perfectoid geometry computable. On the v-site of a perfectoid space X, the structure sheaf OX+ is a sheaf of almost rings with respect to the ideal m = ∪n p1/pn OX+. An OX+-module M is almost zero if m ⊗ M = 0, and a morphism is an almost isomorphism if its kernel and cokernel are almost zero. The category of almost coherent sheaves on Xv is the quotient of the category of coherent sheaves by the Serre subcategory of almost zero sheaves. The almost purity theorem states that if f: Y → X is a finite étale morphism of perfectoid spaces, then f is almost finite étale in the sense that the trace map Tr: f*OY+ → OX+ is an almost isomorphism onto its image, and f*OY+ is an almost finite projective OX+-module. This theorem is proved by reducing to the case of perfectoid algebras, where it follows from the almost purity theorem for perfectoid rings: if R is a perfectoid ring and S is a finite étale R-algebra, then S is almost finite projective over R. The almost étale descent theorem states that the category of almost coherent sheaves on Xv is equivalent to the category of almost coherent sheaves on Yv equipped with descent data for any almost finite étale cover Y → X. This allows one to compute cohomology on perfectoid covers where it is simpler, and then descend the results. For example, the pro-étale cohomology of a constant sheaf Zp on a perfectoid space X is almost zero in positive degrees, proved by covering X by affinoid perfectoid spaces where the cohomology vanishes, and then using almost étale descent. The almost purity theorem also implies that the v-topology is subcanonical for almost coherent sheaves: the sheaf condition holds up to almost isomorphism. Almost isomorphisms are not isomorphisms; they become isomorphisms only after tensoring with the almost ring OXa = OX+[m-1]. When working with period sheaves like BdR+ or Bcris, one must remember that the comparison isomorphisms hold only in the almost category, i.e., after killing m-torsion. This is why the Hodge-Tate weights of a Galois representation are determined only up to the almost category, and why the comparison isomorphisms for the Nygaard filtration on prismatic cohomology hold only in the almost category.

  • Treating the Breuil-Kisin twist O{1} as the Tate twist on the Galois side; they correspond under the crystalline comparison but live on different sites (prismatic vs étale).
  • Assuming the Hodge-Tate period map πHT is an isomorphism of rigid analytic spaces; it is a morphism of perfectoid spaces whose fibers are affinoid perfectoid, not a classical isomorphism.
  • Neglecting the almost purity hypothesis when descending period sheaves from a perfectoid cover; the descent is only almost étale, so isomorphisms hold only in the almost category.
  • Believing the p-adic Simpson correspondence is an equivalence of all Higgs bundles and all local systems; it is fully faithful only on the subcategories of small Higgs bundles and small local systems.
  • Thinking the Lubin-Tate tower at finite level is a perfectoid space; only the infinite-level limit LT∞ is perfectoid, and the almost purity theorem applies to the map LT∞ → LTm.
  • Overlooking that the Nygaard filtration is defined using the Frobenius φ and the ideal I of the prism, not by the Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex; the two filtrations are fundamentally different in nature.
