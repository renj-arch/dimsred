Inverse Problems — Regularization, Ill-Posed Problems & the Radon Transform

Inverse problems are concerned with recovering the cause of an observed effect — given measurements g^δ ≈ Kf, determine the unknown f. A problem is well-posed (Hadamard) if a solution exists, is unique, and depends continuously on the data; an ill-posed problem fails at least one. Compact operators between Hilbert spaces have no continuous inverse — the singular values σ_n → 0, and high-frequency noise is exponentially amplified. Regularization methods (Tikhonov, truncated SVD, iterative schemes) restore stability by imposing prior information. The Radon transform underpins CT scanning: line integrals of a density are inverted via the Fourier slice theorem and filtered backprojection. The Calderón problem — determine the conductivity of a body from boundary measurements — is a nonlinear, exponentially unstable inverse problem central to electrical impedance tomography. A Bayesian perspective models the unknown as a random field; the posterior distribution combines the likelihood (forward model) with a prior, quantified via MCMC or variational inference.

"An inverse problem is easy to state but hard to solve — precisely because it's unstable."
— Andrei Tikhonov
Part 1

📏 Well-Posed and Ill-Posed Problems

Foundations
Hadamard's Definition, Compact Operators, and Singular Value Decay
Hadamard defined a problem as well-posed if (1) a solution exists, (2) it is unique, and (3) it depends continuously on the data. If at least one fails, the problem is ill-posed. A canonical example: differentiation — given noisy data g^δ ≈ g, computing f = g' is ill-posed because high-frequency noise (e.g., ε sin(ωx)) is amplified by a factor ω. In operator form, we have Kf = g where K: X → Y is a compact operator between Hilbert spaces. The singular value decomposition (SVD) of a compact operator: Kf = Σ σ_n ⟨f,u_n⟩ v_n, where σ_n → 0. The ill-posedness is quantified by the decay of σ_n: severely ill-posed when σ_n decays exponentially (e.g., σ_n ~ e^{-cn}), and mildly ill-posed when σ_n decays polynomially (e.g., σ_n ~ n^{-p}). The Picard condition characterizes existence: a solution f ∈ X exists iff Σ (⟨g,v_n⟩/σ_n)² < ∞. When the data are noisy, the high-index terms ⟨g^δ,v_n⟩/σ_n blow up — regularization damps these components.
Ill-posed problems lack continuous dependence on data; the decay rate of singular values determines the severity of ill-posedness and guides the choice of regularization.
Part 2

🌍 Regularization Methods

Stabilization
Tikhonov Regularization, Morozov's Discrepancy, Truncated SVD, and Iterative Methods
Tikhonov regularization replaces the ill-posed equation Kf = g^δ with the minimization problem: minimize ||Kf − g^δ||² + α||f||², where α > 0 is the regularization parameter. The solution is f_α = (K*K + αI)^{-1}K*g^δ. The choice of α is critical: Morozov's discrepancy principle selects α so that ||Kf_α − g^δ|| = δ (the noise level). Under the source condition f† = (K*K)^ν w (with ||w|| ≤ ρ), convergence rates are ||f_α − f†|| = O(δ^{2ν/(2ν+1)}). The truncated SVD (TSVD) projects onto the first N singular components: f_N = Σ_{n=1}^N (⟨g^δ,v_n⟩/σ_n) u_n, where N acts as the regularization parameter. Iterative methods: Landweber iteration f_{k+1} = f_k + τ K*(g^δ − Kf_k) is a gradient descent for ||Kf − g^δ||²; it converges slowly but is simple. The conjugate gradient method for the normal equations (CGNR) converges much faster. The L-curve criterion plots log||Kf_α − g^δ|| vs log||f_α|| — the optimal α is near the corner of the L-shaped curve.
Regularization replaces an ill-posed problem by a nearby well-posed one; the regularization parameter balances data fit and solution stability.
Part 3

🧮 The Radon Transform and Tomography

Tomography
The Radon Transform, Fourier Slice Theorem, and Filtered Backprojection
The Radon transform Rf(s,ω) = ∫_{x·ω = s} f(x) dσ(x) for f ∈ C_c^∞(ℝ²), where ω ∈ S¹ is a direction and s ∈ ℝ is the signed distance from the origin. It maps a function to its line integrals — the raw data in X-ray CT. The Fourier slice theorem: the 1D Fourier transform of Rf in s equals the 2D Fourier transform of f evaluated along the line through the origin in direction ω: ^(Rf)(σ,ω) = ̂f(σ·ω). This is the basis for filtered backprojection (FBP): f(x) = ∫_{S¹} ∫_{ℝ} |σ| ^(Rf)_ω(σ) e^{2πiσ(s−x·ω)} dσ dω, where |σ| is the ramp filter. The limited-angle problem (incomplete angular coverage) makes the inversion more severely ill-posed. For 3D cone-beam CT, the Feldkamp-Davis-Kress (FDK) algorithm is the standard approximate reconstruction. Applications: medical imaging (CT, MRI, PET), nondestructive testing of materials, geophysical tomography (seismic traveltimes), and industrial inspection.
The Radon transform converts tomographic reconstruction into a Fourier-domain problem; filtered backprojection is the classical inversion formula.
Part 4

🔥 The Calderón Problem and Electrical Impedance Tomography

EIT
The Calderón Problem, Dirichlet-to-Neumann Map, Complex Geometrical Optics, and Bayesian Inversion
The Calderón problem (1980): determine the conductivity γ(x) > 0 of a body Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ from voltage-to-current measurements at the boundary. The forward model is div(γ∇u) = 0 in Ω with Dirichlet data u|_∂Ω = f. The data is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map Λ_γ: H^{1/2}(∂Ω) → H^{-1/2}(∂Ω) given by Λ_γ(f) = γ ∂u/∂ν|_∂Ω. Uniqueness: for Lipschitz conductivities the problem has a unique solution — Sylvester-Uhlmann (1987) for C², and Astala-Päivärinta (2006) for L^∞ in 2D. Key tool: complex geometrical optics (CGO) solutions u(x) = e^{x·(ζ+ρ)}(1 + ψ(x,ζ)) where ζ·ζ = 0 and ρ is a small perturbation. The problem is nonlinear and exhibits exponential instability — small changes in measurements cause exponentially large changes in the reconstructed conductivity. The Bayesian approach models γ as a random field with a prior distribution (e.g., Gaussian process). The posterior distribution via Bayes' theorem: π(γ|g^δ) ∝ π(g^δ|γ) π(γ). Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) samples the posterior, while variational Bayes and the ensemble Kalman filter provide scalable approximations for large-scale problems.
The Calderón problem is a nonlinear, exponentially unstable inverse problem; the Bayesian framework provides a principled way to quantify uncertainty.
