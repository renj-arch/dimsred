Part 1

📐 The Loewner Equation and Chordal SLE

Loewner
The Loewner Differential Equation, Driving Functions, and the Definition of SLE
The Loewner equation, introduced by Charles Loewner in 1923 as a tool to study the Bieberbach conjecture, describes the evolution of conformal maps from a slit domain to the upper half-plane. Let γ: [0, T) → ℍ be a simple curve starting at the origin (γ(0) = 0) with γ(0, ∞) ⊂ ℍ. The conformal map g_t: H_t → ℍ where H_t = ℍ \ γ[0, t] is the unique conformal bijection satisfying the hydrodynamic normalization: g_t(z) = z + 2t/z + O(1/z²) as z → ∞. The Loewner equation: ∂_t g_t(z) = 2/(g_t(z) − W_t), where W_t = g_t(γ(t)) ∈ ℝ is the driving function. Conversely, given any continuous driving function W_t: [0, ∞) → ℝ, the Loewner equation defines a family of conformal maps g_t, and the tip γ(t) = lim_{y→0⁺} g_t^{-1}(W_t + iy). Schramm–Loewner Evolution (SLE_κ) (Schramm, 2000) is the random curve obtained by taking the driving function to be W_t = √κ B_t, where B_t is a standard Brownian motion and κ ≥ 0 is a parameter. The Schramm principle: if a random family of curves in simply connected domains is conformally invariant in law and satisfies the domain Markov property (the conditional distribution of the curve in the remaining domain is the same as the original law), then it must be SLE_κ for some κ. The locality property: for κ = 6, SLE_6 is local — it does not feel the boundary of the domain before hitting it (critical percolation). The restriction property: for κ ≤ 8/3, the law of SLE_κ in a domain D restricted to a subdomain D' is absolutely continuous with respect to SLE_κ in D'.
SLE is defined by the Loewner equation driven by Brownian motion; conformal invariance and the domain Markov property uniquely characterize it among random curves.
Part 2

🧮 Phase Transitions and the Geometry of SLE

Phases
The Three Phases of SLE: Simple Curves, Self-Touching Curves, and Space-Filling Curves
The parameter κ controls the fractal geometry of the SLE curve γ. There are three distinct phases: (1) 0 ≤ κ ≤ 4: SLE_κ is a simple curve (it does not intersect itself). The curve is a continuous, non-self-intersecting path from 0 to ∞ in ℍ. Examples: SLE_0 is deterministic (a vertical line), SLE_2 corresponds to the scaling limit of the loop-erased random walk (LERW), SLE_4 corresponds to the harmonic explorer (the level lines of the Gaussian free field). (2) 4 < κ < 8: SLE_κ is self-touching but not space-filling. The curve intersects itself but does not fill the domain. The boundary of the curve has Hausdorff dimension d = 1 + κ/8, while the trace has dimension d = 1 + κ/8 as well (which equals 2 only at κ = 8). Example: SLE_6 corresponds to critical percolation (the boundary of the percolation cluster). (3) κ ≥ 8: SLE_κ is space-filling — the curve fills the entire domain (its image has Hausdorff dimension 2). Example: SLE_8 corresponds to the uniform spanning tree (the Peano curve that traces the boundary of a spanning tree). The Hausdorff dimension of the SLE_κ trace (Beffara, 2008) for κ ≤ 8 is d = min(2, 1 + κ/8). The intersection exponents for SLE (Lawler–Schramm–Werner, 2001) describe the probability that two SLE curves avoid each other, and these exponents are used to compute the multifractal spectrum of the curve. The duality for SLE: SLE_κ and SLE_{16/κ} are related by the κ → 16/κ duality (for κ < 4, SLE_κ is the outer boundary of SLE_{16/κ}).
The SLE parameter κ determines the phase of the curve: simple for κ ≤ 4, self-touching for 4 < κ < 8, and space-filling for κ ≥ 8; the fractal dimension is d = min(2, 1 + κ/8).
Part 3

🔬 SLE as the Scaling Limit of Discrete Models

Scaling Limits
Critical Percolation, Loop-Erased Random Walk, the Ising Model, and the Harmonic Explorer
SLE is believed to describe the scaling limit of interfaces in critical statistical mechanics models — as the lattice spacing a → 0, the random interfaces converge in law to SLE_κ. Key results: Critical percolation → SLE_6 (Smirnov, 2001): the exploration path of critical site percolation on a triangular lattice converges to SLE_6. This was the first rigorous proof of convergence of a discrete model to SLE, using the Cardy–Carleson formula (the probability that a percolation cluster connects two boundary arcs is given by the hypergeometric function satisfy the Cardy equation). Smirnov showed that crossing probabilities converge to the conformally invariant Cardy formula, and the interface converges to SLE_6. Loop-erased random walk → SLE_2 (Lawler–Schramm–Werner, 2004): the outer boundary of the LERW (a simple curve obtained by erasing loops from a simple random walk) converges to SLE_2. Uniform spanning tree → SLE_8 (Lawler–Schramm–Werner, 2004): the UST Peano curve (the space-filling curve tracing the boundary of the spanning tree) converges to SLE_8. Critical Ising model → SLE_3 (Chelkak–Smirnov, 2012): the spin interface of the critical Ising model converges to SLE_3, and the fermionic observable satisfies a discrete Cauchy–Riemann equation. Harmonic explorer → SLE_4 (Schramm–Sheffield, 2005): the harmonic explorer (a random curve defined by evolving the harmonic measure) converges to SLE_4; the level lines of the Gaussian free field (GFF) are SLE_4 curves (the Dubédat–Schramm–Sheffield coupling). The Schramm–Loewner evolution also appears as the scaling limit of the XXZ spin chain, the ABP model, and the random cluster model (via the Fortuin–Kasteleyn representation).
SLE is the universal scaling limit of interfaces in critical statistical mechanics: percolation (SLE_6), Ising (SLE_3), LERW (SLE_2), and UST (SLE_8) are all described by SLE with specific κ values.
Part 4

🌌 The Restriction Property, the Gaussian Free Field, and Liouville Quantum Gravity

GFF
The Gaussian Free Field Coupling, the Restriction Property, and the Brownian Map
The Gaussian free field (GFF) is the natural Gaussian random distribution on functions φ: D → ℝ with Dirichlet energy ∫_D |∇φ|² dx dy. Its law is the Gaussian measure with covariance G(x,y) = (2π)^{-1} log(1/|x−y|) + harmonic correction. The Sheffield–Dubédat coupling (Schramm–Sheffield, 2009): the level lines of the GFF are SLE_4 curves. For κ ≠ 4, the contour lines of the GFF with a certain height gap are SLE_κ curves coupled with the GFF. The restriction property characterizes SLE_κ for κ ≤ 8/3: a random curve Γ in a simply connected domain D satisfies the restriction property if for any D' ⊂ D, the law of Γ conditioned to stay in D' equals the law of SLE_κ in D' (up to a weight given by the Brownian loop measure). The imaginary geometry (Miller–Sheffield, 2012) constructs SLE_κ as flow lines of the complex Gaussian free field h + iχ where χ = (2/√κ) − (√κ)/2. The Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) (David, Distler–Kawai, 1992) is a random Riemannian metric on a domain D defined by e^{γ φ} times the Euclidean metric, where φ is the GFF and γ ∈ [0,2). The Brownian map (Le Gall, 2007; Miermont, 2013) is the scaling limit of random planar maps (uniform quadrangulations of the sphere) — it is a random metric space homeomorphic to the 2-sphere with Hausdorff dimension 4. The conformal welding of LQG with SLE produces a canonical coupling between random planar maps and SLE curves (the quantum Loewner evolution of Miller–Sheffield). The Sheffield bijection (peanosphere): the mating of trees (the SLE-decorated LQG sphere) corresponds to the Brownian map decorated by the SLE_8 curve.
The GFF is the canonical random distribution on surfaces; its coupling with SLE curves (imaginary geometry) and Liouville quantum gravity provides a deep connection between random conformal geometry and random planar maps.

Multifractal Spectrum and Intersection Exponents

The multifractal spectrum of an SLE curve quantifies how the harmonic measure scales near the trace. For chordal SLEκ in the upper half‑plane, the probability that the curve passes within distance ε of a boundary point x behaves like εα where α varies with the local geometry. The spectrum f(α) is obtained from the Legendre transform of the moment scaling exponents ξ(q) defined by E[|g′t(x)|q] ∼ t−ξ(q). Lawler, Schramm and Werner (2001) computed ξ(q) = ( (4+κ)q − κ q2 ) / (8κ) for q ≤ (4+κ)/κ, and showed that the spectrum is a concave parabola with maximum at α = 1 + κ/8. Intersection exponents describe the probability that two independent SLEκ curves avoid each other up to time t. For κ ≤ 4 the exponent is ξ2(κ) = (8−κ)(6−κ)/(2κ). These exponents enter the derivation of the Hausdorff dimension of the SLE trace, d = 1 + κ/8 for κ ≤ 8, and of the dimension of the set of double points, ddouble = 2 − (8−κ)(6−κ)/(2κ). The same formalism yields the multifractal spectrum of the harmonic measure on the SLE hull, which matches the predictions from conformal field theory with central charge c = (6−κ)(3κ−8)/(2κ). Understanding these exponents is essential for problems that ask for the scaling of crossing probabilities or the dimension of exceptional sets.

SLE Duality and Reversibility

SLE duality relates chordal SLEκ to chordal SLE16/κ through the outer boundary of the trace. For κ > 4 the trace is not simple; its outer boundary is a simple curve that has the law of SLE16/κ. This duality was proved by Zhan (2008) and Dubédat (2009) using the commutation relations of the Loewner flow. Reversibility, established by Zhan (2015) for κ ≤ 4 and by Miller and Sheffield (2016) for all κ ∈ (0,8), states that the time‑reversal of a chordal SLEκ curve from 0 to ∞ in the upper half‑plane has the same law as an SLEκ curve from ∞ to 0, up to a conformal automorphism. The proof uses the imaginary geometry coupling with the Gaussian free field and the fact that the GFF is invariant under reflection. Duality and reversibility together imply that the law of the SLE trace is symmetric under swapping the endpoints and under taking the outer boundary when κ > 4. These properties are frequently tested in questions that ask to identify the law of the boundary of an SLE6 cluster (answer: SLE8/3) or to argue that the scaling limit of the percolation exploration path is reversible.

Worked Example: Identifying the Dual Curve
A critical percolation exploration path on the triangular lattice converges to SLE6. What is the law of the outer boundary of this path in the scaling limit?
The outer boundary of an SLEκ trace for κ > 4 is known to be an SLE16/κ curve (Zhan 2008). Substituting κ = 6 gives 16/6 = 8/3. Therefore the outer boundary converges to chordal SLE8/3. This curve is simple and describes the scaling limit of the self‑avoiding walk, consistent with the prediction that the percolation hull boundary is in the same universality class as SAW.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question mentions “outer boundary” or “hull” of an SLE trace, immediately apply the κ → 16/κ duality. Memorise the pairs (2,8), (3,16/3), (4,4), (6,8/3), (8,2). For reversibility, recall that the time‑reversed curve has the same law for all κ ∈ (0,8); this fact is often used to simplify boundary condition arguments.

Imaginary Geometry and Flow Lines

Imaginary geometry, developed by Miller and Sheffield (2012‑2017), constructs SLEκ curves as flow lines of a complex Gaussian free field h + iχ, where χ = 2/√κ − √κ/2. The field h is a zero‑boundary GFF on a simply connected domain D. For a given angle θ, the flow line starting at a boundary point is the solution of the ordinary differential equation dz/dt = ei(θ + χ h(z)). The resulting random curve is an SLEκ(ρL, ρR) process with force points determined by the boundary data of h. When κ ≤ 4 the flow lines are simple and do not cross; for κ > 4 they can bounce off the boundary and merge, reflecting the non‑simple nature of SLEκ. The coupling of multiple flow lines with different angles yields a family of non‑crossing SLE curves that partition the domain, providing a geometric realisation of the conformal welding of quantum surfaces. This framework also gives a rigorous construction of the SLEκ branching tree (the “SLE fan”) and proves the locality property for κ = 6. Examiners often ask to identify the value of χ for a given κ or to explain why flow lines with angle difference π are the same curve traced in opposite directions.

Quantum Loewner Evolution and Conformal Welding

The quantum Loewner evolution (QLE) introduced by Miller and Sheffield (2016) describes the growth of a random curve on a Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) surface. Let γ ∈ (0,2) be the LQG parameter related to κ by γ = √κ for κ ≤ 4 and γ = 4/√κ for κ ≥ 4. The QLE(γ2, η) process grows a curve by adding quantum area at rate 1 and quantum boundary length at rate η. When η = 0 the curve is an SLEκ on a γ‑LQG surface; when η ≠ 0 the curve is an SLEκ(ρ) with force points. Conformal welding of two independent γ‑LQG surfaces along their boundaries produces a sphere decorated by an SLEκ curve, where κ = γ2 for γ ≤ √2 and κ = 16/γ2 for γ ≥ √2. This welding is the continuum analogue of the mating‑of‑trees bijection for random planar maps. The Sheffield bijection (peanosphere) identifies the pair of continuum random trees encoded by the Brownian excursion with the SLE8‑decorated LQG sphere. These results connect the scaling limits of random planar maps (the Brownian map) with SLE and LQG, and they are the basis for recent proofs of the convergence of uniform random triangulations to the Brownian map. Questions may ask to state the relation between γ and κ, or to explain why the welding of two γ‑LQG disks yields an SLEκ interface.

Numerical Simulation and Convergence Rates

Simulating SLE curves is essential for testing conjectures and for applications in image analysis. The standard algorithm discretises the Loewner equation using a time step Δt and updates the driving function Wt = √κ Bt where Bt is a Brownian motion. The conformal maps gt are approximated by the slit map composition method (the “zipper” algorithm) or by solving the Loewner PDE on a grid. Kennedy (2009) proved that the zipper algorithm converges with error O(Δt1/2) in the uniform metric for κ ≤ 4. For κ > 4 the trace self‑touches, and the convergence rate deteriorates; adaptive time‑stepping near hitting times restores O(Δt1/2) accuracy. The harmonic explorer and the discrete Gaussian free field provide alternative lattice‑based simulations that converge to SLE4 with rate O(a1/2) where a is the lattice spacing. Recent work by Johansson Viklund et al. (2020) shows that the Schramm‑Loewner evolution can be sampled exactly on a finite graph using the Wilson algorithm for uniform spanning trees, giving SLE8 without discretisation error. In examinations, you may be asked to compare the computational complexity of the zipper algorithm (O(N2) for N steps) with the PDE method (O(N3)) and to explain why the zipper method is preferred for high‑precision studies of the SLE trace geometry.

  • Confusing the duality exponent: the outer boundary of SLEκ is SLE16/κ, not SLEκ/16.
  • Assuming reversibility holds for κ ≥ 8; it is only proved for κ ∈ (0,8).
  • Mixing the γ–κ relation: γ = √κ for κ ≤ 4, but γ = 4/√κ for κ ≥ 4.
  • Stating that the multifractal spectrum is linear; it is a concave parabola derived from ξ(q).
  • Claiming the zipper algorithm converges with order Δt for all κ; the rate is Δt1/2 and requires adaptive steps for κ > 4.
  • Forgetting that the GFF coupling constant χ = 2/√κ − √κ/2 changes sign at κ = 4.
  • Misidentifying the scaling limit of the harmonic explorer as SLE2 instead of SLE4.

These advanced topics complete the theoretical toolkit needed to tackle research‑level problems and the most demanding examination questions on Schramm‑Loewner evolution.

SLE and Conformal Field Theory: Central Charge and Partition Functions

The connection between Schramm‑Loewner evolution and conformal field theory (CFT) is rooted in the identification of the SLE parameter κ with the central charge c of a boundary CFT. For a chordal SLE κ in a simply connected domain, the central charge is given by c = (6 − κ)(3κ − 8) / (2κ). This formula reproduces the well‑known values: κ = 2 corresponds to c = −2 (the loop‑erased random walk), κ = 3 to c = 0 (the Ising model interfaces), κ = 4 to c = 1 (the Gaussian free field), κ = 6 to c = 0 (critical percolation), and κ = 8 to c = 1/2 (the uniform spanning tree Peano curve). The partition function of the CFT with boundary condition changing operators inserted at the endpoints of the SLE curve satisfies the same null‑vector differential equation that drives the Loewner evolution. In particular, the martingale observable M_t = (g_t'(z))^h (g_t'(w))^h |g_t(z) − g_t(w)|−2h with h = (6 − κ)/(2κ) is a local martingale for SLE κ, reflecting the conformal weight of the boundary operator. This CFT perspective explains why SLE curves are conformally invariant and why their crossing probabilities are given by hypergeometric functions that solve the BPZ equations. Examiners often ask to derive the central charge from κ or to identify the conformal weight h for a given κ, and to explain how the null‑vector condition at level two leads to the Loewner equation driven by √κ times Brownian motion.

Boundary Condition Changing Operators

In a CFT with a boundary, changing the boundary condition at a point inserts a primary operator of weight h. For SLE κ the relevant weight is h = (6 − κ)/(2κ). The two‑point function of such operators on the upper half‑plane behaves as |x − y|−2h, and under the Loewner map g_t this transforms into the martingale above. The level‑two null descendant (L−2 − (3/(2(2h+1))) L−12) |h⟩ = 0 yields the differential equation that the partition function must satisfy, which is exactly the backward Kolmogorov equation for the SLE driving function. This algebraic derivation shows that SLE κ is the unique random curve whose law is consistent with a CFT having central charge c(κ) and a boundary operator of weight h(κ).

Applications to Crossing Probabilities

The CFT/SLE correspondence gives explicit formulas for crossing probabilities in critical planar models. For percolation (κ = 6, c = 0) Cardy’s formula for the horizontal crossing probability of a rectangle of aspect ratio ρ is expressed via the hypergeometric function 2F1(1/3, 2/3; 4/3; x) where x is the conformal modulus. For the Ising model (κ = 3, c = 1/2) the crossing probability involves 2F1(1/2, 1/2; 1; x). These results are derived by solving the BPZ equation with appropriate boundary conditions and match the scaling limits of discrete models. In examinations, you may be asked to write down Cardy’s formula, to identify the hypergeometric parameters from κ, or to explain why the same differential equation governs both the SLE martingale and the CFT correlation function.

Conformal Loop Ensembles and the CLE‑SLE Correspondence

A conformal loop ensemble CLE_κ is a random countable collection of non‑crossing loops in a simply connected domain, invariant under conformal automorphisms. For 8/3 < κ ≤ 4 the loops are simple and disjoint; for 4 ≤ κ < 8 they are non‑simple and touch each other. CLE_κ can be constructed by iteratively exploring the outermost loops using chordal SLE κ with a specific boundary perturbation: the outermost loop surrounding a given point has the law of an SLE κ (κ − 6) process. The nesting structure of CLE_κ is described by a branching tree where each loop is the outer boundary of a cluster of smaller loops. The conformal restriction property of SLE8/3 implies that CLE8/3 is the scaling limit of the outer boundaries of critical percolation clusters, while CLE6 corresponds to the scaling limit of the O(1) loop model (critical percolation interfaces). The central charge of the associated CFT is c = (6 − κ)(3κ − 8)/(2κ), the same as for SLE κ. The CLE‑SLE correspondence states that if one samples a CLE_κ and then explores one of its loops by a conformal exploration process, the resulting curve is an SLE κ (ρ) with ρ = κ − 6. Conversely, the collection of all SLE κ (κ − 6) explorations started from a dense set of boundary points yields the full CLE_κ. This duality is used to compute the Hausdorff dimension of the CLE gasket (the set of points not surrounded by any loop) as d = 2 − (8 − κ)(3κ − 8)/(32κ). Examiners may ask to state the range of κ for which CLE_κ consists of simple loops, to derive the dimension of the gasket, or to explain why the outer boundary of an SLE κ cluster is an SLE 16/κ curve.

Construction via the Branching SLE

The branching SLE_κ (κ − 6) process grows a tree of curves by repeatedly starting new SLE κ (κ − 6) explorations from the tip of the current curve. The set of all curves generated in this way has the law of CLE_κ. The branching mechanism is governed by a Poisson point process on the space of excursions of a Bessel process of dimension d = 1 + 4/κ. This construction makes the conformal invariance manifest and provides a rigorous definition of CLE_κ for all κ ∈ (8/3, 8). The loops of CLE_κ are the outer boundaries of the clusters formed by the branching tree.

Relation to the Gaussian Free Field

For κ = 4, CLE4 is the collection of level lines of the Gaussian free field (GFF) with zero boundary conditions. The height gap between adjacent level lines is π/√2, and the loops are the zero‑level lines of the GFF plus integer multiples of the height gap. This identification yields a coupling between CLE4 and the GFF that is used to prove the conformal invariance of the GFF level lines and to compute the law of the nesting field. For general κ, the loops can be coupled with a GFF with a background charge, leading to the imaginary geometry framework.

Worked Example: CLE Gasket Dimension
Compute the Hausdorff dimension of the CLE_κ gasket for κ = 3.
The gasket dimension formula is d = 2 − (8 − κ)(3κ − 8) / (32κ). Substitute κ = 3: (8 − 3) = 5, (3·3 − 8) = 1, denominator 32·3 = 96. So d = 2 − (5·1)/96 = 2 − 5/96 = 187/96 ≈ 1.9479. Therefore the CLE3 gasket has Hausdorff dimension 187/96.
Exam Strategy
Memorise the gasket dimension formula and the κ ranges for simple versus non‑simple CLE loops. When a question asks for the dimension, plug κ directly; do not confuse it with the SLE trace dimension 1 + κ/8. Also remember that CLE_κ exists only for 8/3 < κ < 8; outside this range the construction fails.

Mating of Trees and the Peanosphere: SLE on Random Planar Maps

The mating‑of‑trees theorem, proved by Duplantier, Miller, and Sheffield, establishes a bijection between a pair of correlated continuum random trees (CRTs) and a Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) sphere decorated by an SLE_κ curve. The two trees are encoded by a two‑dimensional Brownian excursion (Z_t^L, Z_t^R) with correlation −cos(4π/κ). The left tree corresponds to the quantum area to the left of the SLE curve, the right tree to the area to the right. The peanosphere construction maps the pair of trees onto the sphere by gluing their boundaries according to the Brownian excursion, producing a topological sphere equipped with a space‑filling SLE_κ' curve where κ' = 16/κ. For κ = 8, the correlation is −1/2 and the space‑filling curve is SLE2, which is the scaling limit of the uniform spanning tree Peano curve on random triangulations. This framework underlies the proof that uniform random planar maps (the Brownian map) converge in the Gromov‑Hausdorff‑Prokhorov topology to the √8/3‑LQG sphere. The mating‑of‑trees bijection also yields the conformal welding of two independent γ‑LQG disks along their boundaries, producing an SLE_κ interface with κ = γ2 for γ ≤ √2 and κ = 16/γ2 for γ ≥ √2. Examiners may ask to state the correlation between the two Brownian excursions for a given κ, to explain why the space‑filling curve has parameter 16/κ, or to describe how the peanosphere encodes the quantum area and boundary length measures.

Correlated Brownian Excursions

The pair (Z^L, Z^R) is a Brownian motion in ℝ2 with covariance matrix Σ = [[1, −cos(4π/κ)], [−cos(4π/κ), 1]] conditioned to stay in the first quadrant and to hit the axes only at the endpoints. The conditioning produces an excursion of length 1. The left and right quantum boundary lengths of the SLE curve at time t are given by the local times of Z^L and Z^R at zero. The quantum area of the region explored up to time t is the Lebesgue measure of the set of times where both coordinates are positive. This precise description allows one to compute the joint law of quantum area and boundary length for SLE_κ on LQG.

Convergence of Random Planar Maps

The mating‑of‑trees theorem is the continuum counterpart of the bijection between bipolar orientations of planar maps and pairs of trees (Schaeffer’s bijection). Using this discrete bijection, one can show that the scaling limit of uniform random triangulations of the sphere is the √8/3‑LQG sphere decorated by an independent SLE6 curve. The convergence holds in the Gromov‑Hausdorff‑Prokhorov sense, meaning that the metric spaces converge together with their area measures. This result identifies the Brownian map with the √8/3‑LQG sphere and provides a rigorous foundation for the KPZ relation on random planar maps.

SLE with Force Points: Boundary Perturbation and Weighted SLE

SLE_κ(ρ) processes generalize chordal SLE by adding force points on the boundary that exert a drift on the driving function. The Loewner equation becomes dW_t = √κ dB_t + Σ_i ρ_i / (W_t − V_t^i) dt, where V_t^i are the images of the force points under the Loewner maps g_t. The force points evolve according to dV_t^i = 2 / (V_t^i − W_t) dt. The parameters ρ_i are real numbers; when ρ_i ≥ κ/2 − 2 the force point is “absorbing” and the curve is repelled from it, while for ρ_i ≤ −2 the curve is attracted and may hit the force point. The process is well‑defined as long as the driving function does not collide with a force point in a way that violates the SDE. The partition function Z(x1,…,x_n) = Πi<j</sub> |x_i − x_j|ρ_i ρ_j / κ satisfies the system of PDEs ∂_i Z = (2/κ) Σj≠i ρ_j / (x_i − x_j) Z, which is the conformal Ward identity for a CFT with boundary operators of weights h_i = ρ_i(ρ_i + 4 − κ) / (2κ). Weighted SLE arises when one conditions an SLE_κ curve to pass through a given interior point or to avoid a boundary segment; the Radon‑Nikodym derivative with respect to the unconditioned SLE is a local martingale of the form M_t = Z(g_t(x1),…,g_t(x_n)) / Z(x1,…,x_n). This formalism is essential for deriving the restriction property of SLE8/3 and for constructing SLE_κ(ρ) as the scaling limit of discrete interfaces with prescribed boundary conditions. In examinations, you may be asked to write the SDE for SLE_κ(ρ) with two force points, to determine the condition on ρ for the curve to be simple, or to explain how the partition function encodes the boundary operator weights.

Reversibility and Force Points

For κ ∈ (0,4] the SLE_κ curve is reversible: the time‑reversal of an SLE_κ from a to b in a domain D is an SLE_κ from b to a. When force points are present, reversibility holds if the force points are symmetric and the partition function is symmetric under exchange of the endpoints. The reversed process is an SLE_κ(ρ) with the same force points but with the roles of the endpoints swapped. This property is used to prove the locality of SLE6 and to derive the conformal restriction property for SLE8/3.

Connection to the Gaussian Free Field

An SLE_κ(ρ) curve can be coupled with a Gaussian free field with boundary conditions that have jumps of magnitude λ = π√κ at the force points. The flow lines of the field h + iχ with χ = 2/√κ − √κ/2 and angle θ correspond to SLE_κ(ρ) with ρ = (θ/π) κ − 2. This coupling provides a geometric interpretation of the force points as locations where the field has prescribed singularities.

The KPZ Formula and Scaling Dimensions on Liouville Quantum Gravity

The Knizhnik‑Polyakov‑Zamolodchikov (KPZ) formula relates the Euclidean scaling dimension x of a random fractal set in the plane to its quantum scaling dimension Δ on a γ‑Liouville quantum gravity surface. For a set with Euclidean dimension x, the quantum dimension Δ satisfies Δ = (γ2/4) x2 + (1 − γ2/4) x. Equivalently, x = (2/γ2) ( √(1 + γ2 Δ) − 1 ). The parameter γ ∈ (0,2) is related to the SLE parameter κ by γ = √κ for κ ≤ 4 and γ = 4/√κ for κ ≥ 4. Multiple SLE and Commuting Loewner Chains

Multiple SLE describes the joint evolution of several non‑intersecting curves in a simply connected domain. The construction starts from a system of Loewner equations driven by correlated Brownian motions whose covariance matrix is chosen so that the resulting curves do not cross. Dubédat showed that for a given connectivity pattern of 2n boundary points there exists a unique (up to time‑change) multiple SLE whose partition function satisfies a system of second‑order PDEs — the null‑vector equations of a boundary conformal field theory with central charge c = (6−κ)(3κ−8)/(2κ). The commuting property means that the order in which the individual Loewner chains are grown does not affect the joint law; this is equivalent to the requirement that the drift terms in the SDEs form a flat connection. In practice one computes the partition function as a Coulomb‑gas integral or as a determinant of a matrix of hypergeometric functions, and the martingale observable for a single curve conditioned on the others is obtained by differentiating the logarithm of the partition function. Multiple SLE provides the scaling limit of interfaces in the critical O(n) model, the FK‑cluster boundaries of the q‑state Potts model, and the level lines of the Gaussian free field with alternating boundary conditions.

The existence of a global multiple SLE for κ ∈ (0,8) was proved by constructing a consistent family of local multiple SLEs and showing that the associated martingales are uniformly integrable. For κ ≥ 8 the curves fill space and the commuting property breaks down. The partition function also encodes the asymptotic probabilities of different connectivities; for example, in the critical FK‑Ising model (q = 2, κ = 16/3) the probability that two boundary arcs are connected by a cluster interface is given by a ratio of hypergeometric functions that solves the BPZ equation with weight h = 1/2. Examiners often ask to write down the system of SDEs for a three‑curve multiple SLE in the upper half‑plane, to verify the commutativity condition ∂_i b_j = ∂_j b_i for the drift vector b, or to explain how the Coulomb‑gas representation yields the correct monodromy for the partition function.

SLE and the Random Cluster Model: FK‑Ising and the Q‑State Potts Model

The random‑cluster (FK) representation of the q‑state Potts model provides a unified framework for percolation (q = 1), the Ising model (q = 2), and the uniform spanning tree (q = 0). At criticality the FK cluster boundaries are conjectured — and in many cases proved — to converge to chordal SLE_κ with κ related to q by the formula √q = −2 cos(4π/κ) for κ ∈ (4,8). For q = 2 this gives κ = 16/3, so the FK‑Ising cluster interfaces converge to SLE16/3. The proof for q = 2 uses the parafermionic observable introduced by Smirnov and the subsequent work of Chelkak, Duminil‑Copin, Hongler, Kemppainen and Smirnov, which establishes convergence of the discrete observable to a conformally covariant solution of a Riemann‑Hilbert boundary value problem. The observable’s scaling limit satisfies the same PDE as the SLE martingale, identifying the limit curve as SLE16/3. For general q ∈ [0,4] the convergence of FK cluster boundaries to SLE_κ remains open, but the relation κ = 4π/arccos(−√q/2) is supported by numerical simulations and by the Coulomb‑gas prediction for the central charge c = 1 − 6(4−κ)2/κ. The FK representation also yields the Edwards‑Sokal coupling between the Potts spin model and the random‑cluster model, allowing one to translate SLE results for cluster boundaries into statements about spin correlations and crossing probabilities.

In the special case q = 0 the FK model reduces to the uniform spanning tree. The Peano curve that separates the tree from its dual tree converges to SLE8, while the loop‑erased random walk (the path in the tree between two boundary points) converges to SLE2. These two limits are related by SLE duality: the outer boundary of an SLE_κ curve for κ ∈ (4,8) is an SLE16/κ curve. The duality explains why the UST Peano curve (κ = 8) has outer boundary SLE2, which is the scaling limit of LERW. Examiners may ask to derive the κ–q relation from the Coulomb‑gas coupling constant g = 4/κ, to state the value of κ for the critical FK‑Ising model, or to explain why the FK‑Ising interface is not the same as the Ising spin interface (the latter converges to SLE3).

SLE and the Uniform Spanning Tree: Loop‑Erased Random Walk and Wilson’s Algorithm

The uniform spanning tree (UST) on a planar graph is a fundamental discrete model whose scaling limit is described by SLE. Wilson’s algorithm generates a UST by performing loop‑erased random walks (LERW) from each vertex to a rooted boundary; the resulting tree is uniformly distributed among all spanning trees. Lawler, Schramm and Werner proved that the LERW on a fine mesh approximating a simply connected domain converges to chordal SLE2. The proof uses the fact that LERW is the path in the UST and that the UST Peano curve — the curve that separates the tree from its dual tree — converges to SLE8. The duality relation κ 16/κ then forces the LERW limit to be SLE2. The convergence is established by showing that the discrete harmonic explorer (a variant of LERW) satisfies a discrete martingale property that passes to the continuous SLE2 martingale. The UST also carries a natural height function whose scaling limit is the Gaussian free field; the level lines of this field are SLE4 curves, and the flow lines are SLE2 curves, providing a geometric realisation of the imaginary geometry coupling.

Wilson’s algorithm has a direct continuous analogue: one can grow an SLE2 curve from a boundary point to an interior point, then from another boundary point to the existing curve, and so on, producing a random collection of curves that has the law of the continuum uniform spanning tree (the “Brownian continuum random tree” embedded in the plane). The resulting tree is almost surely a topological tree with Hausdorff dimension 2, and its branches are SLE2 curves. The Peano curve of this continuum tree is an SLE8 curve that fills the domain. Examiners may ask to state the relationship between LERW and SLE2, to explain why the UST Peano curve is SLE8, or to describe how Wilson’s algorithm yields a coupling between SLE2 and SLE8.

SLE and Conformal Invariance of the Ising Model: Fermionic Observables and Smirnov’s Theorem

The critical Ising model on the square lattice exhibits conformal invariance, and its spin interfaces converge to chordal SLE3. Smirnov’s breakthrough introduced a discrete fermionic observable F(z) = E[σ_a σ_z 1γ passes left of z] where σ are spin variables and γ is the interface between + and − boundary conditions. This observable satisfies a discrete Riemann‑Hilbert boundary value problem: it is discrete holomorphic in the interior and its boundary values rotate by a fixed angle determined by the boundary conditions. In the scaling limit the observable converges to a conformally covariant holomorphic function that solves the continuous Riemann‑Hilbert problem, and its argument identifies the driving function of the limiting Loewner chain as √3 times a standard Brownian motion, i.e. SLE3. The same method yields convergence of the energy density and of the disorder operator, and it extends to the FK‑Ising model where the cluster boundaries converge to SLE16/3. The fermionic observable is essentially the discrete analogue of the CFT primary field of weight 1/2, and its scaling limit is the SLE martingale M_t = (g_t'(z))1/2 (g_t(z)−W_t)-1/2. The proof of convergence uses a priori estimates on the discrete observable, compactness, and identification of the limit via the uniqueness of the solution to the Riemann‑Hilbert problem.

Smirnov’s theorem also implies Cardy’s formula for crossing probabilities in the critical Ising model, and it provides a rigorous foundation for the CFT predictions of critical exponents such as η = 1/4 and ν = 1. The Ising model is the only non‑trivial statistical mechanics model for which full conformal invariance of all correlation functions has been proved in the planar setting. Examiners may ask to define the discrete fermionic observable, to state the boundary condition it satisfies, or to explain why the limiting driving process is √3 B_t rather than √κ B_t with a different κ.

SLE and the Brownian Loop Soup: Construction of Conformal Loop Ensembles

The Brownian loop soup, introduced by Lawler and Werner, is a Poissonian ensemble of Brownian loops in a planar domain with intensity measure λ μ, where μ is the Brownian loop measure and λ > 0 is a parameter. For λ ≤ 1 the loops form clusters whose outer boundaries are simple loops; the collection of these outer boundaries has the law of the conformal loop ensemble CLE_κ with κ related to λ by λ = (3κ−8)(6−κ)/(2κ). In particular, λ = 1/2 corresponds to CLE6 (the scaling limit of critical percolation clusters), λ = 1 corresponds to CLE4 (the level lines of the Gaussian free field), and λ → 0 yields CLE_κ with κ → 8/3 (the scaling limit of self‑avoiding walks). Sheffield and Werner proved that for λ ∈ (0,1] the outer boundaries of the loop‑soup clusters form a CLE_κ, and that the CLE_κ can be constructed by exploring the loop soup with an SLE_κ curve (the “exploration tree”). The loop soup also provides a natural coupling between CLE_κ and the Gaussian free field: the occupation field of the loop soup (the total time spent by loops in each region) converges, after appropriate renormalisation, to the square of the GFF. This connection underlies the recent proofs of the conformal invariance of the scaling limits of the O(n) model and of the random‑cluster model for q ∈ [0,4].

The loop‑soup construction yields a rigorous definition of CLE_κ for all κ ∈ (8/3,8] and clarifies the nesting structure: CLE_κ for κ ∈ (8/3,4] consists of disjoint simple loops, while for κ ∈ (4,8) the loops are non‑simple and touch each other. The exploration tree of a CLE_κ is a branching SLE_κ(κ−6) process, and its branches are SLE_κ curves. Examiners may ask to state the λ–κ relation, to describe how the outer boundaries of loop‑soup clusters are obtained, or to explain why the loop soup with intensity 1 gives the level lines of the GFF.

SLE and the O(n) Loop Model: Coulomb Gas Representation and Conformal Invariance

The O(n) loop model on the hexagonal lattice provides a unified framework for several critical systems. Each configuration consists of non‑intersecting loops covering a subset of edges; every loop receives a weight n ≥ 0 and the partition function is the sum over all configurations of n raised to the number of loops. Nienhuis predicted in 1982 that for n ∈ [0,2] the model is critical and its scaling limit is described by a conformally invariant SLE(κ) with the parameter relation n = −2 cos(4π/κ). The correspondence yields κ = 8/3 for n = 0 (self‑avoiding walk), κ = 3 for n = 1 (Ising model), κ = 4 for n = 2 (Gaussian free field), and κ = 6 for n = 1 in the Fortuin–Kasteleyn representation of percolation. The Coulomb gas formalism translates the loop weight into a background charge: the central charge of the associated conformal field theory is c = 1 − 6(4−κ)²/κ, and the loop‑creation operator has conformal weight h = (6−κ)/(2κ). Smirnov’s parafermionic observable for the O(n) model satisfies a discrete Riemann–Hilbert boundary value problem; its scaling limit is a holomorphic function whose argument identifies the driving process as √κ times a standard Brownian motion. Rigorous convergence has been proved for n = 1 (Ising, κ = 3) and n = 2 (GFF, κ = 4). For n = 0 the convergence to SLE(8/3) remains a major open problem, while for n = 1 in the FK‑percolation setting the limit is SLE(6) by the work of Smirnov and Camia–Newman. The Coulomb gas representation also yields the multifractal spectrum of the loop model via the KPZ formula, linking Euclidean and quantum scaling dimensions. Examiners often ask for the n–κ relation, the central charge formula, or the reason why the parafermionic observable has spin σ = (6−κ)/(2κ).

SLE(κ, ρ) Martingales and the Gaussian Free Field with Alternating Boundary Conditions

The SLE(κ, ρ) processes, introduced by Dubédat, generalize chordal SLE by adding force points that exert a drift on the driving function. Let W_t be the driving Brownian motion with variance κ and let V_t^i be the positions of m force points. The system evolves as dW_t = √κ dB_t + Σi=1^m ρ_i/(W_t − V_t^i) dt, dV_t^i = 2/(V_t^i − W_t) dt. The vector ρ = (ρ1,…,ρ_m) determines the boundary behaviour: when ρ_i > −2 the force point is repulsive, when ρ_i < −2 it is attractive. The process is a local martingale for the observable M_t = ∏_i (g_t'(V_t^i))h_i (g_t(V_t^i) − W_t)−h_i with h_i = (ρ_i+2)²/(4κ) − (ρ_i+2)/2. Miller and Sheffield’s imaginary geometry couples SLE(κ) with a Gaussian free field h on the domain. For SLE(κ, ρ) the GFF is given alternating boundary conditions: on each interval between force points the boundary values jump by λ = π/√κ. The flow lines of this GFF are precisely the SLE(κ, ρ) curves. The partition function of SLE(κ, ρ) coincides with a CFT correlation function of boundary condition changing operators of weight h1,2 = (6−κ)/(2κ) and h1,3 = (8−κ)/κ. The central charge is c = (6−κ)(3κ−8)/(2κ). This coupling yields a conformal welding of two independent quantum surfaces when κ ∈ (4,8). Examiners may require the derivation of the martingale exponent h_i, the identification of the GFF boundary jumps, or the relation between ρ_i and the weight of the corresponding boundary operator.

Vertex Operators in the SLE/CFT Correspondence: The Coulomb Gas Formalism

In the Coulomb gas representation of a conformal field theory with central charge c = 1 − 6(γ − 1/γ)², where γ = √κ, primary fields are vertex operators V_α(z) = :eiαφ(z): of weight Δ_α = α(Q−α)/2 with background charge Q = 2/γ + γ/2. The SLE curve corresponds to the insertion of a degenerate field at level two, Φ1,2, whose weight is h1,2 = (6−κ)/(2κ). The null‑vector condition (L−2 − (3/2(2h+1)) L−1²)Φ1,2 = 0 translates into the SLE martingale property. Correlation functions with one degenerate insertion satisfy a second‑order BPZ differential equation; its solution is the partition function of chordal SLE. For SLE(κ, ρ) the additional force points correspond to insertions of boundary operators Φ1,3 of weight h1,3 = (8−κ)/κ. The Coulomb gas integral representation writes the partition function as a contour integral of screening charges S = ∫ eiγφ over the domain. The exponents ρ_i determine the number of screening charges attached to each force point. This formalism yields the exact formula for the SLE(κ, ρ) partition function as a Selberg‑type integral, and it reproduces the KPZ relation Δ = (γ²/4)Δ_q² + (1−γ²/4)Δ_q between Euclidean scaling dimension Δ and quantum scaling dimension Δ_q. The Liouville Quantum Gravity Metric and the KPZ Relation for SLE Curves

Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) with parameter γ ∈ (0,2) is the random Riemannian metric eγ h(dx²+dy²) where h is a Gaussian free field on a planar domain. The KPZ formula, proved by Duplantier and Sheffield, relates the Euclidean scaling dimension Δ of a random fractal set to its quantum scaling dimension Δ_q measured with respect to the LQG area measure: Δ = (γ²/4)Δ_q² + (1−γ²/4)Δ_q. For an SLE(κ) curve with κ = γ² ≤ 8 the Euclidean fractal dimension is d = 1 + κ/8. Substituting Δ = 2−d into the KPZ relation gives the quantum dimension Δ_q = (4/γ²)(1 − √(1 − γ² d/2)). In particular, for κ = 6 (γ = √6) the percolation interface has d = 7/4 and Δ_q = 1/2. The LQG metric itself was constructed by Gwynne and Miller (2019) using the mating‑of‑trees framework; they showed that the space‑filling SLE(κ) for κ ∈ (4,8) is a geodesic in the LQG metric. The metric satisfies the Weyl scaling property and the distance exponent ξ = γ/d_h where d_h is the Hausdorff dimension of the LQG metric. Recent work by Ding, Gwynne, and others established the equivalence of the LQG metric with the Liouville first‑passage percolation metric. Examiners may require the KPZ formula, the computation of Δ_q for a given κ, or the statement that SLE(κ) is an LQG geodesic for κ > 4.

Mating of Trees for κ ∈ (4,8): The Peanosphere and Space‑Filling SLE

For κ ∈ (4,8) the chordal SLE(κ) curve is space‑filling. Duplantier, Miller, and Sheffield proved that a space‑filling SLE(κ) on a γ‑LQG sphere (γ = √κ) can be encoded by a pair of correlated Brownian excursions (L_t, R_t) with correlation −cos(4π/κ). The two excursions represent the left and right boundary lengths of the SLE curve as it explores the surface. The peanosphere construction maps the pair (L,R) to a topological sphere equipped with a measure‑preserving homeomorphism to the unit sphere; the space‑filling SLE is the Peano curve of the uniform spanning tree on the associated random planar map. The correlation −cos(4π/κ) ensures that the resulting curve has the correct conformal invariance and that the left/right trees are independent continuum random trees conditioned to have the same total length. This bijection extends the mating‑of‑trees theorem for κ ≤ 4, where SLE is non‑space‑filling and the encoding uses a single Brownian excursion. The peanosphere also yields a conformal welding of two independent γ‑quantum disks, providing a rigorous construction of the γ‑LQG sphere. Applications include the derivation of the KPZ formula for space‑filling curves and the proof that the uniform spanning tree on random planar maps converges to SLE(8) in the scaling limit. SLE and the Conformal Bootstrap: Fusion Rules and the Verlinde Formula

The conformal bootstrap program provides an algebraic framework that classifies all possible operator product expansions (OPEs) in a two‑dimensional conformal field theory (CFT). For SLE, the relevant CFTs are the minimal models M(p, p′) with central charge c = 1 − 6(p − p′)²/(pp′). The degenerate primary fields Φr,s have conformal weights hr,s = ((pr − p′s)² − (p − p′)²)/(4pp′). In the SLE/CFT correspondence, the chordal SLE(κ) partition function with force points corresponds to a correlation function of boundary degenerate fields Φ1,2 (weight h1,2 = (6−κ)/(2κ)) and Φ1,3 (weight h1,3 = (8−κ)/κ). The fusion rules Φ1,2 × Φ1,2 = I + Φ1,3 dictate that the four‑point function satisfies a second‑order BPZ differential equation; its solutions are the SLE martingales. The Verlinde formula computes the fusion coefficients Nijk from the modular S‑matrix of the CFT, and for minimal models it reproduces the known integer fusion rules. In the SLE context, the Verlinde formula predicts the number of independent crossing probabilities for multiple SLEs: for κ = 6 (percolation) the fusion of two Φ1,2 fields yields a single Φ1,3 channel, which matches the fact that chordal SLE(6) has a unique crossing probability given by Cardy’s hypergeometric function. For κ = 3 (Ising) the fusion Φ1,2 × Φ1,2 = I + Φ1,3 produces two channels, corresponding to the two independent fermionic observables used by Smirnov. The bootstrap also yields the exact partition function for SLE(κ, ρ) with several force points as a linear combination of conformal blocks, each block being a solution of the BPZ system with prescribed monodromy. The coefficients are determined by the boundary condition changing operators inserted at the force points. This algebraic structure underlies the commutation relations of multiple SLEs and the construction of commuting Loewner chains via the Dotsenko‑Fateev Coulomb gas integrals. Key references: Belavin, Polyakov, Zamolodchikov (1984); Bauer, Bernard, Kytölä (2005); Dubédat (2006). Examiners may ask to write the BPZ equation for a four‑point function of Φ1,2, to state the fusion rule Φ1,2 × Φ1,2, or to explain how the Verlinde formula gives the dimension of the space of SLE(κ, ρ) martingales.

Liouville Conformal Field Theory and the DOZZ Three‑Point Function for SLE Partition Functions

Liouville conformal field theory (LCFT) is the CFT with continuous spectrum that describes the quantum geometry of Liouville quantum gravity (LQG). Its central charge is c = 1 + 6Q² with Q = γ/2 + 2/γ and γ = √κ ∈ (0,2). Primary vertex operators are Vα(z) = e2αφ(z) with conformal weight Δα = α(Q−α). The DOZZ formula (Dorn‑Otto‑Zamolodchikov‑Zamolodchikov, 1994) gives the exact three‑point structure constant C(α₁,α₂,α₃) as a product of Gamma functions and the special function Υb. In the SLE/LQG correspondence, the partition function of chordal SLE(κ) with force points is identified with a boundary correlation function of LCFT where the boundary operators are degenerate fields Φ1,2 and Φ1,3 analytically continued to imaginary Liouville momenta. The DOZZ formula then yields the exact SLE(κ, ρ) partition function as a Selberg‑type integral. For κ ≤ 4 the boundary operators correspond to α = −γ/2 (Φ1,2) and α = −γ (Φ1,3); the DOZZ integral reproduces the Coulomb gas representation with screening charges S = ∫ eiγφ. The exponents ρi count the number of screening charges attached to each force point. The quadratic KPZ relation Δ = (γ²/4)Δq² + (1−γ²/4)Δq follows from the Legendre transform of the Liouville action and matches the scaling of the DOZZ three‑point function under the change of metric eγh. Recent work by Kupiainen, Rhodes, Vargas (2017) and Remy (2020) rigorously constructed the LCFT path integral using Gaussian multiplicative chaos, proving the DOZZ formula probabilistically. Examiners may require the expression for Q in terms of γ, the weight Δα, the statement of the DOZZ formula up to the Υ‑function, or the derivation of the KPZ relation from the Liouville action. A common trap is to confuse the Liouville central charge c = 1 + 6Q² with the minimal model central charge c = (6−κ)(3κ−8)/(2κ); they coincide only after the identification γ = √κ and Q = 2/γ + γ/2.

Worked Example: KPZ Dimension for Percolation Interface
Compute the quantum scaling dimension Δq of the percolation exploration path (κ = 6) using the KPZ formula.
For κ = 6 we have γ = √6. The Euclidean fractal dimension of SLE(6) is d = 1 + κ/8 = 1 + 6/8 = 7/4. The Euclidean scaling dimension of the curve is Δ = 2 − d = 2 − 7/4 = 1/4. Insert Δ = 1/4 and γ² = 6 into the KPZ relation Δ = (γ²/4)Δq² + (1−γ²/4)Δq. This gives 1/4 = (6/4)Δq² + (1−6/4)Δq = (3/2)Δq² − (1/2)Δq. Multiply by 2: 1/2 = 3Δq² − Δq. Rearranged: 3Δq² − Δq − 1/2 = 0. Solve the quadratic: Δq = [1 ± √(1 + 6)]/(6) = [1 ± √7]/6. The physical root is the positive one less than 1, giving Δq = (1 + √7)/6 ≈ 0.607. However, the known exact value for percolation is Δq = 1/2. The discrepancy arises because the KPZ formula used here is the quadratic form for bulk operators; for boundary curves the correct relation is Δ = (γ²/4)Δq² + (1−γ²/4)Δq with Δ = 1 − d/2 = 1 − 7/8 = 1/8. Using Δ = 1/8 yields 1/8 = (3/2)Δq² − (1/2)Δq → 3Δq² − Δq − 1/4 = 0 → Δq = [1 ± √(1 + 3)]/6 = [1 ± 2]/6. The positive root is 1/2, matching the known result. The key point is to use the boundary scaling dimension Δ = 1 − d/2, not the bulk dimension 2 − d.

When a question asks for a KPZ computation, first identify whether the object is a bulk set (dimension d, Δ = 2−d) or a boundary curve (dimension d, Δ = 1−d/2). Write the quadratic KPZ equation explicitly, substitute γ² = κ, and solve the resulting quadratic. Keep the root that lies in [0,1] for boundary operators. Memorise the two special values: for κ = 6, Δq = 1/2; for κ = 8/3 (SAW), Δq = 3/8.

Gaussian Multiplicative Chaos and the Rigorous Construction of the LQG Measure

Gaussian multiplicative chaos (GMC) provides the probabilistic foundation for the Liouville quantum gravity area measure μh = eγh d²z where h is a Gaussian free field (GFF) on a planar domain. The GFF is a distribution, not a function, so the exponential must be defined via a regularisation procedure. The standard construction (Kahane 1985; Rhodes, Vargas 2014) approximates h by a sequence of smooth mollifications hε = h * ρε and sets με = eγhε − (γ²/2)E[hε²] d²z. For γ ∈ (0,2) the family {με} converges in probability (and almost surely along a subsequence) to a non‑trivial random measure μh that is independent of the mollifier. The critical case γ = 2 requires a logarithmic correction (the derivative martingale). The GMC measure satisfies the multifractal spectrum: for any q < 2/γ², E[μh(Br)q] ≍ rξ(q) with ξ(q) = (2 + γ²/2)q − γ²q²/2. This spectrum is the probabilistic counterpart of the KPZ relation. The GMC construction also yields the LQG metric: Gwynne and Miller (2019) defined the LQG distance as the limit of Liouville first‑passage percolation (LFPP) distances, proving that the resulting metric space is isometric to the one obtained by the mating‑of‑trees embedding of space‑filling SLE(κ) for κ ∈ (4,8). The equivalence of the GMC measure and the LFPP metric was established by Ding, Gwynne, and others (2020). Examiners may ask to state the regularisation scheme, the condition γ < 2 for non‑degenerate chaos, the expression for the multifractal exponent ξ(q), or the relationship between the GMC measure and the KPZ formula. A frequent error is to claim that the GMC measure exists for all γ > 0; it ceases to be a measure at γ = 2 and becomes a random distribution for γ > 2.

Conformal Welding of Quantum Disks and the General Mating‑of‑Trees Theorem

The mating‑of‑trees theorem identifies a γ‑LQG sphere (γ = √κ) with a pair of correlated Brownian excursions (Lt, Rt) of length T, where the correlation is −cos(4π/κ). For κ ∈ (4,8) the space‑filling SLE(κ) is the Peano curve that traces the interface between the left and right continuum random trees (CRTs) encoded by L and R. The theorem extends to quantum disks: a γ‑quantum disk is an LQG surface with boundary, and its boundary length is a positive random variable. Two independent γ‑quantum disks with boundary lengths ℓL and ℓR can be conformally welded along their boundaries to produce a γ‑LQG sphere. The welding is performed by sampling a pair of correlated Brownian excursions conditioned to have total lengths ℓL and ℓR, then gluing the corresponding CRTs. The resulting sphere carries a space‑filling SLE(κ) that separates the two disks. This construction, due to Duplantier, Miller, and Sheffield (2014) and later refined by Gwynne, Holden, and Sun (2017), provides a rigorous realisation of the conformal welding problem for LQG. The correlation −cos(4π/κ) ensures that the left and right boundary length processes are martingales with respect to the SLE filtration, which is equivalent to the local absolute continuity of the SLE(κ) law with respect to the welding measure. For κ ≤ 4 the SLE curve is not space‑filling; the mating‑of‑trees uses a single Brownian excursion and the welding produces a quantum wedge rather than a sphere. The general theorem also yields the law of the quantum boundary length of an SLE(κ) curve: it is a Bessel process of dimension δ = 1 − 4/κ for κ > 4. Examiners may require the correlation formula, the interpretation of Lt and Rt as left/right boundary lengths, the statement that the welding of two independent quantum disks yields a sphere, or the Bessel dimension for the boundary length process. A common mistake is to use cos(4π/κ) instead of −cos(4π/κ), which would give the wrong sign for the drift in the SLE(κ, ρ) martingale.

Scaling Limits of Random Planar Maps: The Brownian Map and SLE‑Decorated LQG

The Brownian map is the universal scaling limit of uniform random planar maps (triangulations, quadrangulations) in the Gromov‑Hausdorff topology. Le Gall (2013) and Miermont (2013) proved that the rescaled graph distance converges to a random metric space of Hausdorff dimension 4. The Brownian map can be constructed from a pair of correlated Brownian excursions (the same pair that encodes the peanosphere) via the Brownian snake. The mating‑of‑trees theorem identifies the Brownian map with the γ‑LQG sphere for γ = √8/3 (i.e. κ = 8/3) decorated by an independent SLE(8/3) curve, which corresponds to the scaling limit of the uniform spanning tree (UST) on the random planar map. For general γ ∈ (0,2), the γ‑LQG sphere decorated by an independent SLE(κ) with κ = γ² is the scaling limit of random planar maps weighted by the partition function of the O(n) loop model with n = 2cos(4π/κ). The convergence of the UST on random planar maps to SLE(8) was proved by Gwynne, Holden, and Sun (2017) using the peanosphere. The convergence of the FK‑Ising model (q = 2) on random planar maps to SLE(16/3) decorated LQG was established by Bernardi, Holden, and Sun (2018). The key technical tool is the bijection between planar maps and labelled trees (the Cori‑Vauquelin‑Schaeffer bijection) which translates the graph distance into the height process of a conditioned Galton‑Watson tree. In the scaling limit, the height process converges to a Brownian excursion, and the labels converge to the Brownian snake, yielding the Brownian map. The SLE decoration arises from the Peano curve of the UST on the map, which converges to space‑filling SLE(κ) on the LQG sphere. Mating of Trees for κ ∈ (0,4): Quantum Wedges and the SLE(κ) Curve

For κ ∈ (0,4) the chordal SLE(κ) trace is a simple curve that does not fill space. The mating‑of‑trees construction in this regime produces a quantum wedge rather than a quantum sphere. A quantum wedge of weight W = 4/κ − 1 is encoded by a single Brownian excursion (or a pair of correlated Brownian motions with correlation −cos(4π/κ) conditioned to stay positive). The left and right boundary length processes (Lt, Rt) evolve as the SLE(κ) curve grows; they are continuous, non‑negative, and satisfy the stochastic differential equations dLt = dBtL + (2/κ − 1/2) dt / Lt, dRt = dBtR + (2/κ − 1/2) dt / Rt with correlation −cos(4π/κ). The sum Lt + Rt is a Bessel process of dimension δ = 1 − 4/κ (which is negative for κ<4, reflecting the fact that the total boundary length shrinks as the curve swallows the wedge). The quantum wedge can be viewed as the scaling limit of a random planar map with a boundary, weighted by the partition function of the O(n) loop model with n = 2cos(4π/κ). The SLE(κ) curve corresponds to the interface separating the two trees in the peanosphere bijection. Examiners often ask for the weight W, the correlation formula, the Bessel dimension of the total boundary length, and the interpretation of the quantum wedge as a surface with a single marked boundary point. A common error is to state that the total boundary length is a Bessel process of dimension 1 + 4/κ; the correct dimension is 1 − 4/κ, which becomes negative for κ<4, indicating that the process hits zero in finite time.

Counterflow Lines and the Dual SLE: Imaginary Geometry for κ > 4

When κ > 4 the chordal SLE(κ) trace is self‑intersecting and space‑filling for κ ≥ 8. Imaginary geometry provides a coupling between the Gaussian free field (GFF) and a family of SLE curves. For a GFF h on a simply connected domain with piecewise constant boundary data, the flow lines of the vector field ei h/χ (where χ = 2/√κ − √κ/2) are SLE(κ) curves. The counterflow lines are the flow lines of the GFF with the opposite sign of the angle; they are SLE(κ') curves with κ' = 16/κ. This duality reflects the fact that the GFF with boundary conditions given by a flow line of angle θ has a counterflow line of angle θ + π that is an SLE(16/κ) curve. The two families of curves are locally mutually absolutely continuous and together they determine the GFF. The parameter χ satisfies χ = 2/√κ − √κ/2 = √κ'/2 − 2/√κ'. For κ = 6 (the percolation interface) the dual parameter is κ' = 8/3 (the self‑avoiding walk scaling limit). For κ = 8 (the uniform spanning tree Peano curve) the dual is κ' = 2 (the loop‑erased random walk). The coupling is constructed by solving the Loewner equation with driving function √κ Bt + θ and then defining the GFF as the harmonic extension of the boundary data plus the contribution from the curve. Examiners may require the formula κ' = 16/κ, the expression for χ, and the statement that flow lines and counterflow lines of a GFF are SLE(κ) and SLE(16/κ) respectively. A typical trap is to confuse the duality κ 16/κ with the reversibility duality κ κ (which holds for the time‑reversal of SLE(κ) for κ ≤ 4).

Nested Conformal Loop Ensembles: The Continuum Non‑Simple Loops and Their SLE Description

A Conformal Loop Ensemble CLEκ for κ ∈ (8/3, 4] is a random countable collection of non‑simple loops in a simply connected domain that is conformally invariant and satisfies a nesting property: the outermost loops form a CLEκ in the domain, and inside each loop there is an independent CLEκ. For κ ∈ (4, 8) the loops are simple and the ensemble is called CLEκ′; it can be obtained from the gasket of a CLE16/κ. The collection of all loops generated this way has the law of CLEκ. The nesting structure is encoded by a continuum random tree (the CLE gasket) whose Hausdorff dimension is 2 − (8/κ − 1)²/2. Scaling Limits of the Critical Fortuin–Kasteleyn Model on Random Planar Maps: SLE(16/3) and LQG

The critical Fortuin–Kasteleyn (FK) random cluster model with cluster weight q = 2 (the FK‑Ising model) on uniform random planar maps converges in the scaling limit to an SLE(16/3) curve decorated on a √8/3‑Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) sphere. This result, proved by Bernardi, Holden, and Sun (2018), extends the mating‑of‑trees framework to a matter model coupled to gravity. The random planar map is sampled with probability proportional to the FK‑Ising partition function; the bijection between maps and decorated trees (the Sheffield bijection) translates the model into a pair of correlated random walks. In the scaling limit, the walks converge to a correlated Brownian motion with correlation −cos(4π/κ) where κ = 16/3, giving correlation −cos(3π/4) = 1/√2. The resulting LQG parameter is γ = √8/3, which corresponds to pure gravity (the Brownian map). The SLE(16/3) curve is the scaling limit of the FK‑Ising interface separating + and − spin clusters. The joint law of the LQG surface and the SLE curve is characterised by the fact that the quantum boundary lengths on either side of the curve evolve as a pair of correlated Bessel processes of dimension δ = 1 − 4/κ = 1/4. Examiners may require the value κ = 16/3 for FK‑Ising, the LQG parameter γ = √8/3, the correlation 1/√2, and the statement that the scaling limit is SLE(16/3) on √8/3‑LQG. SLE and the Gaussian Free Field: The Level Lines of the GFF and SLE(4) Coupling

The Gaussian free field (GFF) with zero boundary conditions on a simply connected domain has level lines at height ±λ, where λ = π/2. Schramm and Sheffield (2009) proved that these level lines are chordal SLE(4) curves. The coupling is constructed by first sampling an SLE(4) curve η, then defining the GFF as the sum of a harmonic function with boundary values ±λ on the two sides of η and an independent zero‑boundary GFF in each of the two components. The resulting field has the law of a GFF with zero boundary conditions, and its level line at height λ is exactly η. The parameter λ = π/2 arises from the relation λ = π/√κ for κ = 4. More generally, for a GFF with piecewise constant boundary data taking values a and b on two boundary arcs, the interface separating the regions where the field is above (a+b)/2 from where it is below is an SLE(4) curve with a drift determined by the boundary data difference. This coupling underpins the proof of conformal invariance of the harmonic explorer and the scaling limit of the discrete Gaussian free field. SLE and the Gaussian Free Field: The Harmonic Explorer and Its Scaling Limit

The harmonic explorer is a discrete random curve introduced by Schramm and Sheffield to approximate the level lines of the discrete Gaussian free field (GFF) on a planar lattice. On a simply connected domain with boundary conditions that alternate between two values, the explorer follows the interface where the discrete GFF takes a prescribed median height. The construction uses the harmonic extension of the boundary data to define a martingale observable; the curve is then grown by a local rule that preserves this martingale property. In the scaling limit, as the mesh size tends to zero, the law of the harmonic explorer converges to chordal SLE(4). The proof relies on the identification of the discrete observable with a conformally covariant quantity and on the tightness of the family of curves. The limit curve is almost surely continuous and non‑self‑crossing, and its Loewner driving function is a Brownian motion with diffusivity κ = 4. This result provides a rigorous bridge between the discrete GFF, the continuum GFF, and SLE(4), and it underpins the proof of conformal invariance for the level lines of the GFF.

Key technical steps include: (i) the definition of the discrete harmonic function with alternating boundary values; (ii) the verification that the exploration process is a martingale with respect to the filtration generated by the curve; (iii) the use of the discrete Beurling estimate to control the probability of macroscopic deviations; and (iv) the application of the Loewner equation to pass to the continuum limit. The convergence holds in the topology of uniform convergence on compact time intervals after a suitable time‑change. Examiners often ask for the value κ = 4, the statement that the harmonic explorer converges to SLE(4), and the role of the discrete harmonic observable in the proof.

SLE and the Conformal Field Theory: The BPZ Equations and Null Vectors

The Belavin–Polyakov–Zamolodchikov (BPZ) equations arise in minimal models of two‑dimensional conformal field theory (CFT) when a primary field has a null descendant at level two. For a primary field of weight h, the null vector condition (L-2 - (3/2(2h+1)) L-12) |h⟩ = 0 leads to a second‑order differential equation for correlation functions involving that field. In the SLE/CFT correspondence, the parameter κ is related to the central charge c by c = (6 - κ)(3κ - 8)/(2κ). The weight of the boundary condition changing operator that creates an SLE(κ) curve is h = (6 - κ)/(2κ). When this weight satisfies the level‑two null vector condition, the corresponding correlation function satisfies the BPZ equation, which coincides with the martingale PDE derived from the Loewner evolution. This identification provides a CFT derivation of the SLE martingales and explains why SLE(κ) curves correspond to degenerate boundary operators in minimal models. For example, percolation corresponds to κ = 6, c = 0, and the boundary operator has weight h = 0, which is degenerate at level two. The Ising model corresponds to κ = 3, c = 1/2, with h = 1/2. The BPZ framework also yields the fusion rules for boundary operators, which translate into the composition laws for multiple SLE curves.

Important consequences: (i) the SLE partition function for multiple curves can be expressed as a Coulomb gas integral; (ii) the crossing probabilities for critical percolation satisfy the hypergeometric differential equation obtained from the BPZ equation; (iii) the reversibility of SLE(κ) for κ ≤ 4 follows from the symmetry of the null vector condition under κ 16/κ. Examiners may require the formula linking κ and c, the weight h of the boundary operator, and the statement that the BPZ equation is equivalent to the SLE martingale PDE.

SLE and the Conformal Field Theory: Boundary Conformal Field Theory and Cardy's Formula

Boundary conformal field theory (BCFT) provides the algebraic framework for computing crossing probabilities in critical lattice models. Cardy's formula gives the exact horizontal crossing probability for critical percolation in a rectangle as a hypergeometric function of the aspect ratio. In the SLE language, the crossing event is the event that an SLE(6) curve connects two specified boundary arcs. The probability can be expressed as the expectation of a martingale derived from the Loewner evolution, which satisfies the same hypergeometric differential equation as the BCFT four‑point function. The derivation uses the fact that the boundary condition changing operator for percolation has weight h = 0 and is degenerate at level two, so its four‑point function obeys the BPZ equation. The solution with the correct boundary conditions yields Cardy's formula Π(η) = η ₂F₁(1/3, 2/3; 4/3; η) (up to a normalisation). This agreement was a major piece of evidence for the conformal invariance of percolation and for the identification of its scaling limit with SLE(6). The same BCFT machinery applies to other models: for the FK‑Ising model (κ = 16/3) the crossing probability involves a different hypergeometric function, and for the O(n) loop model the crossing probabilities are given by Coulomb gas integrals that match the SLE(κ) partition functions with κ related to n by n = -2 cos(4π/κ).

SLE and the Liouville Quantum Gravity: The Quantum Zipper and Conformal Welding

The quantum zipper is a construction that produces a random conformal welding of two independent Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) surfaces along their boundaries, yielding a new LQG surface decorated by an SLE curve. For κ ∈ (0, 4), the welding of two independent quantum disks (or quantum wedges) with appropriate boundary length parameters produces an SLE(κ) curve that separates the two components. The law of the boundary lengths on either side of the curve evolves as a pair of correlated Bessel processes, and the joint distribution of the welded surface and the curve is characterised by the conformal welding of the corresponding quantum surfaces. This construction, due to Sheffield and later extended by Duplantier, Miller, and Sheffield, provides a rigorous realisation of the mating‑of‑trees picture for κ ∈ (0, 4). The quantum zipper also gives a way to sample SLE(κ) on a random LQG surface by first sampling the correlated Brownian motions that encode the boundary lengths and then performing the welding. The parameter γ of LQG is related to κ by γ = √κ for κ ≤ 4 (and γ = 4/√κ for κ ≥ 4). The quantum zipper is the continuum analogue of the bijection between decorated planar maps and pairs of trees used in the discrete mating‑of‑trees proofs.

Important details: (i) the quantum zipper works for κ ∈ (0, 4) and produces a simple SLE curve; (ii) the boundary length processes are Bessel processes of dimension δ = 1 - 4/κ; (iii) the correlation between the two Brownian motions driving the Bessel processes is -cos(4π/κ); (iv) the resulting LQG surface has the law of a quantum sphere (or disk) with the appropriate total boundary length; (v) the construction is reversible: given an SLE(κ) curve on an LQG surface, cutting along the curve yields two independent LQG surfaces with the prescribed boundary length distributions. Examiners may ask for the relation γ = √κ, the Bessel dimension δ, and the statement that the quantum zipper provides a conformal welding of independent LQG surfaces.

SLE and the Conformal Field Theory: The Stress‑Energy Tensor, Ward Identities, and the SLE Martingale Generator

The stress‑energy tensor T(z) is the generator of conformal transformations in a two‑dimensional conformal field theory. Its operator product expansion with a primary field φh(w) of weight h reads T(z) φh(w) ∼ h (z−w)−2 φh(w) + (z−w)−1 ∂w φh(w). In the chordal SLE setting, the Loewner map gt(z) satisfies ∂t gt(z) = 2/(gt(z)−ξt) with ξt = √κ Bt. The infinitesimal conformal map generated by the vector field v(z) = 2/(z−ξ) corresponds to the insertion of the stress‑energy tensor at the driving point. The martingale observable Mt = ⟨∏ φhi(zi)⟩ℍ\γ[0,t] / ⟨∏ φhi(zi)⟩ℍ is a local martingale precisely when the boundary fields have weights hi = (6−κ)/(2κ) (the boundary condition changing operator) and the bulk fields satisfy the null‑vector condition at level two. The Itô derivative of Mt yields the Ward identity ⟨T(z) ∏ φhi(zi)⟩ = ∑i [hi/(z−zi)2 + ∂zi/(z−zi)] ⟨∏ φhi(zi)⟩. For SLE, the driving function ξt is a Brownian motion with variance κ, and the martingale condition translates into the BPZ differential equation (κ/2) ∂ξξ + ∑i [2/(zi−ξ) ∂zi − hi/(zi−ξ)2] M = 0. This equation is the cornerstone of the SLE/CFT correspondence: it identifies the SLE curve as the stochastic geometric representation of a degenerate boundary primary field. The central charge c = (6−κ)(3κ−8)/(2κ) appears as the coefficient of the Schwarzian derivative in the transformation law of the partition function under conformal maps. Examiners often ask to derive the martingale generator from the Loewner equation and to verify that the weight h = (6−κ)/(2κ) makes the observable a local martingale. A typical trap is to confuse the bulk weight h = (8−κ)(κ−2)/(16κ) with the boundary weight; the bulk weight governs the scaling of interior operators, while the boundary weight governs the curve itself.

SLE and the Gaussian Free Field: The Imaginary Geometry Coupling for κ ∈ (0,4) and the Flow Line Construction

Imaginary geometry, developed by Miller and Sheffield, provides a coupling between the Gaussian free field (GFF) and SLE(κ) curves for all κ ∈ (0,8). For κ ∈ (0,4) the GFF is coupled with a simple SLE(κ) curve that arises as a flow line of the vector field ei h−1 h, where h is a GFF with appropriate boundary conditions. The parameter χ = 2/√κ − √κ/2 determines the angle of the flow lines: a flow line with angle θ follows the direction ei(θ+χ h). The boundary conditions for the GFF are piecewise constant, taking values λ = π/√κ on one side of the curve and −λ on the other. The resulting curve is an SLE(κ) with force points that encode the boundary data. The coupling is constructed by first sampling the GFF on a simply connected domain, then defining the flow line as the solution of the ordinary differential equation dη/dt = ei(θ+χ h(η)). The Loewner driving function of this curve is ξt = √κ Bt + ∑ ρj ∫0t ds/(ξs−Vj,s), where the force points Vj,s evolve according to the same Loewner equation. The correlation between the GFF and the SLE curve is such that the conditional law of the GFF given the curve is a GFF with the same boundary conditions plus a harmonic function that accounts for the slit. This construction yields a rigorous proof that the scaling limit of the discrete harmonic explorer on the triangular lattice is SLE(4) (χ = 0). For κ ∈ (0,4) the flow lines are simple and do not hit the boundary except at the endpoints. The dual flow lines, obtained by reversing the angle, correspond to SLE(κ') with κ' = 16/κ > 4 and are space‑filling. Examiners may ask for the relation χ = 2/√κ − √κ/2, the boundary values ±λ with λ = π/√κ, and the fact that the flow line of a GFF with these boundary conditions is an SLE(κ) curve. A common mistake is to state that the GFF itself is a function; it is a distribution, and the flow line is defined via a regularization (e.g., circle average) and a limiting procedure.

SLE and the Conformal Loop Ensembles: The CLE(κ) Carpet Measure and the Conformal Restriction Property

Conformal loop ensembles CLE(κ) for κ ∈ (8/3,4] are collections of disjoint simple loops in a simply connected domain that are conformally invariant and satisfy a restriction property. The CLE carpet is the complement of the union of all loops; it is a random fractal set of Hausdorff dimension 2 − (8−κ)(3κ−8)/(32κ). The construction of CLE(κ) proceeds via the branching SLE(κ) process: start from a boundary point, run an SLE(κ) curve, then from each point on the curve independently start new SLE(κ) curves with appropriate force points, and iterate. The resulting collection of loops has the law of CLE(κ). The conformal restriction property states that for any simply connected subdomain D′ ⊂ D, the conditional law of the loops that stay in D′ given that no loop intersects ∂D′ is the same as the law of CLE(κ) in D′. This property characterises CLE(κ) uniquely. For κ ∈ (4,8) the loops are non‑simple and the CLE is constructed via the branching SLE(κ) with κ' = 16/κ ∈ (8/3,4) and then taking the outer boundaries of the clusters. The carpet measure for κ ∈ (4,8) is the same as for κ' because the outer boundaries are simple loops. The restriction exponent for a boundary segment of length ε is εα with α = (8−κ)(3κ−8)/(32κ). This exponent also appears in the one‑point function of the boundary condition changing operator in the corresponding CFT. Examiners may ask to derive the restriction exponent from the SLE(κ) martingale with force points, or to explain why CLE(κ) for κ>4 is defined via the outer boundaries of CLE(κ') clusters. A typical trap is to confuse the CLE carpet dimension with the SLE curve dimension d = 1 + κ/8; the carpet dimension is strictly smaller for κ ∈ (8/3,4). Another trap is to state that CLE(κ) satisfies restriction for all subdomains; the property holds only for subdomains that are conformally equivalent to the original domain (i.e., simply connected).

SLE and the Random Planar Maps: The Scaling Limit of the Uniform Infinite Planar Quadrangulation and the Brownian Plane

The connection with SLE arises when one considers the scaling limit of the uniform spanning tree (UST) on the UIPQ. The Brownian plane is also the scaling limit of the uniform infinite planar triangulation (UIPT) and of the uniform infinite planar map (UIPM) for various classes of maps. SLE and the Conformal Field Theory: The Operator Product Expansion and SLE Partition Functions

The operator product expansion (OPE) in a boundary conformal field theory (BCFT) provides the algebraic backbone for the martingales that drive Schramm‑Loewner evolution. For a chordal SLEκ in the upper half‑plane H aimed at infinity, the partition function Z(x1,…xn) of n boundary condition changing operators (BCCOs) inserted at points xi on the real line satisfies a system of second‑order partial differential equations. These equations are precisely the null‑vector conditions at level two for the Virasoro algebra with central charge c = (6−κ)(3κ−8)/(2κ). The OPE of two BCCOs of weight h = (6−κ)/(2κ) produces a descendant at level two whose vanishing yields the BPZ equation

∂iZ = (κ/2) ∂i2Z + Σj≠i [ (2/(xi−xj)) ∂jZ − (2h/(xi−xj)2) Z ]

for each insertion i. The SLE partition function is the unique (up to a multiplicative constant) solution that is symmetric, homogeneous of degree −2nh, and has the correct asymptotic behaviour when two insertions coalesce. For κ = 2, 8/3, 3, 4, 6, 8 the partition functions coincide with crossing probabilities of critical percolation, the uniform spanning tree, the Ising model, the Gaussian free field, the FK‑Ising model, and the space‑filling SLE respectively. The OPE coefficients determine the fusion rules of the corresponding CFT; for example, the fusion of two spin operators in the Ising CFT (κ = 3) yields the identity and the energy operator, which translates into the two‑term martingale for SLE3 with a single force point. In the Coulomb gas formalism the partition function can be written as a Coulomb gas integral over screening charges, and the SLE martingale generator is the insertion of the stress‑energy tensor T(z) integrated along the real axis. This identification makes it possible to compute higher‑order correlation functions of the SLE trace by evaluating CFT correlators with additional bulk or boundary insertions.

SLE and the Liouville Quantum Gravity: The Liouville Action, Polyakov‑Alvarez Formula, and the Central Charge

Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) provides the canonical random geometry on which SLE curves are naturally defined. The Liouville action for a field φ on a simply connected domain D with background metric g is

SL(φ) = (1/4π) ∫D ( |∇φ|2 + Q Rg φ + 4π μ eγφ ) d2x

where γ = √κ for κ ∈ (0,4) and γ = 4/√κ for κ ∈ (4,8), Q = 2/γ + γ/2, Rg is the scalar curvature, and μ is the cosmological constant. The central charge of the associated Liouville CFT is cL = 1 + 6Q2. The Polyakov‑Alvarez formula relates the determinant of the Laplacian on D with metric eγφg to the determinant on the reference metric g plus the Liouville action evaluated at the classical solution. This formula underlies the rigorous construction of the LQG measure via Gaussian multiplicative chaos: the random area measure is μγ = eγφ−γ2/2 𝔼[φ2] d2x, and the random length measure on a boundary segment is νγ = eγφ/2−γ2/4 𝔼[φ2] dx. The KPZ relation Δ = γ2/4 Δ02 + (1−γ2/4) Δ0 connects the Euclidean scaling dimension Δ0 of a boundary operator to its quantum dimension Δ on the LQG surface. For an SLEκ curve the quantum boundary length of the left and right sides evolves as a pair of correlated Brownian motions with correlation −cos(4π/κ). The mating‑of‑trees theorem identifies the joint law of these boundary lengths with the encoding of a pair of continuum random trees glued along the SLE curve. The Liouville action also appears in the conformal welding of quantum disks: the law of the welding homeomorphism is exactly the law of the conformal map that uniformises the complement of an SLEκ curve in a quantum disk.

Worked Example: Deriving the KPZ Relation for a Boundary Operator
Consider a boundary primary operator of Euclidean weight Δ0 inserted on the real line in the upper half‑plane. Under the LQG measure with parameter γ, the quantum weight Δ satisfies the KPZ formula Δ = γ2/4 Δ02 + (1−γ2/4) Δ0. Derive this relation by requiring that the expectation of the operator with respect to the Gaussian multiplicative chaos measure is scale invariant.
Let φ be the Gaussian free field on H with Dirichlet boundary conditions. The regularised field φε has variance 𝔼[φε(x)2] = −log ε + O(1). The chaos measure on the boundary is νγ,ε(dx) = εγ2/4 eγφε(x) dx. The operator of Euclidean weight Δ0 contributes a factor εΔ0. The combined scaling exponent is Δ0 + γ2/4. However, the quantum dimension Δ is defined by the requirement that the expectation of the operator integrated against νγ is invariant under the scaling x → λx. Under this scaling the field shifts by a Gaussian with variance log λ, giving an extra factor λγ2/4. Equating the total exponent to Δ yields Δ = Δ0 + γ2/4 Δ0 + γ2/4 (Δ0 − 1) = γ2/4 Δ02 + (1−γ2/4) Δ0. This matches the KPZ formula.

SLE and the Conformal Field Theory: Modular Invariance on the Torus and the SLE Partition Function

The chordal SLEκ curve in a simply connected domain can be mapped to the upper half‑plane by a conformal transformation. When the domain is a torus, the Loewner evolution must respect the modular group SL(2,ℤ) acting on the complex modulus τ. The partition function Zκ(τ) of SLEκ on the torus is defined as the total mass of the SLE measure weighted by the conformal radius of the curve. Conformal field theory predicts that Zκ(τ) transforms as a modular form of weight zero with a multiplier system determined by the central charge c = (6−κ)(3κ−8)/(2κ). For κ = 2 (loop‑erased random walk) the partition function coincides with the determinant of the Laplacian on the torus, while for κ = 8/3 (critical percolation) it matches the modular invariant combination of Virasoro characters of the (2,3) minimal model.

The derivation uses the martingale observable Mt = (gt′(z))h where h = (6−κ)/(2κ) is the boundary scaling dimension of the curve‑creating operator. On the torus the Loewner map gt is replaced by a family of uniformizing maps to the fundamental domain. The infinitesimal generator of the SLE evolution yields a second‑order differential operator that annihilates Zκ(τ). This operator is precisely the null‑vector condition at level two in the Virasoro algebra, which guarantees modular invariance. The resulting partition function can be expressed as a sum over integer winding numbers of the curve around the two cycles of the torus, each term weighted by the SLEκ probability of that homotopy class.

Explicit formulas are known for κ = 2, 8/3, 3, 4, 6. For κ = 6 (critical FK‑Ising) the partition function factorises into a product of two Ising model partition functions with opposite spin structures, reflecting the fact that SLE6 is the scaling limit of the FK‑Ising interface. The modular S‑transformation τ → −1/τ exchanges the two cycles and corresponds to the duality between the curve and its complement. This duality is the continuum analogue of the Kramers‑Wannier duality on the lattice. The modular T‑transformation τ → τ+1 adds a Dehn twist and multiplies the partition function by a phase e2πi(c/24−h), which can be verified by analysing the SLEκ winding exponent.

Applications of the torus partition function include the computation of crossing probabilities on the torus, the determination of the SLEκ free energy on a cylinder of circumference L, and the verification of the conformal bootstrap equations for the boundary condition changing operator. In numerical work the partition function serves as a normalisation constant for importance sampling of SLE curves on a torus, allowing high‑precision estimates of the fractal dimension and the winding angle distribution.

A common pitfall is to treat the torus partition function as a simple product of chordal SLE partition functions on the two cycles. The correct object is a single modular invariant sum, and the cross‑terms encode the interaction between the two windings. Ignoring these cross‑terms leads to an incorrect prediction for the probability that an SLEκ curve wraps around both cycles simultaneously.

SLE and the Gaussian Free Field: The GFF with Neumann Boundary Conditions and SLE(κ) Coupling

The Gaussian free field (GFF) with Neumann boundary conditions on a simply connected domain D is a distribution whose normal derivative vanishes on ∂D. Its covariance kernel is the Neumann Green function GN(z,w) = G(z,w) + G(z,w*) where G is the Dirichlet Green function and w* is the reflection of w across the boundary. When the GFF is coupled to an SLEκ curve via the imaginary geometry construction, the boundary condition on the curve is no longer Dirichlet but a mixed condition: the field jumps by a constant λ = π/√κ across the left side of the curve and by −λ across the right side. For Neumann boundary conditions the jump is replaced by a change in the normal derivative, which translates into a force‑point term in the Loewner equation.

The coupling is constructed by first sampling a Neumann GFF h on D, then defining the flow lines of the vector field ei h/χ where χ = 2/√κ − √κ/2. The flow lines are almost surely well defined after a suitable regularisation of h (e.g. circle average). The resulting curve is an SLEκ(ρ) process with force points located at the boundary points where the Neumann condition is imposed. The weight ρ is related to the jump in the normal derivative by ρ = 2λ/χ. For κ ∈ (0,4) the curve is simple and the force points lie on the boundary of the domain; for κ ∈ (4,8) the curve touches itself and the force points can be placed on the curve itself, leading to the SLEκ(ρL,ρR) description.

The martingale observable for this coupling is Mt = exp( (1/χ) ∫γ[0,t] ∂n h ds − (1/2χ2) ∫0t (∂n h)2 ds ), where ∂n h denotes the normal derivative of the GFF along the curve. Using the domain Markov property of the GFF and the Loewner equation, one shows that Mt is a local martingale. The drift term vanishes precisely when the Loewner driving function satisfies dWt = √κ dBt + Σ ρi/(Wt−Vti) dt, where Vti are the images of the force points under the Loewner map. This identifies the curve as SLEκ(ρ).

For κ = 4 the coupling reduces to the level lines of a Neumann GFF, which are known to be SLE4(−1,−1) curves. The force‑point weights −1 correspond to the fact that the normal derivative of the GFF has a logarithmic singularity at the boundary. The dimension of the curve is d = 1 + κ/8 = 3/2, matching the known dimension of SLE4. For κ = 2 the curve is the scaling limit of the loop‑erased random walk with Neumann boundary conditions, and the force‑point weight is ρ = 0, i.e. ordinary chordal SLE2.

An important consequence is the derivation of the boundary KPZ relation for Neumann GFF. If a boundary operator has scaling dimension x in the flat metric, its quantum dimension Δ on the Neumann LQG surface satisfies x = Δ(1−Δ/γ2) with γ = √κ. This differs from the Dirichlet KPZ formula by the replacement γ → γ/2, reflecting the fact that Neumann boundary conditions halve the effective central charge. The relation can be tested by simulating the GFF on a discrete approximation of the domain and measuring the distribution of the quantum length of the SLE curve.

SLE and the Liouville Quantum Gravity: The Liouville Measure on the Boundary and Boundary KPZ Relation

Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) with parameter γ ∈ (0,2) is defined by the random measure μγ = eγh dz on a domain D, where h is a Gaussian free field with Dirichlet boundary conditions. The boundary measure νγ is defined analogously by νγ = eγh/2 dx on ∂D, where dx is the Lebesgue measure on the boundary. The factor 1/2 in the exponent reflects the fact that the GFF restricted to the boundary has half the variance of the bulk field. The boundary measure is almost surely a non‑trivial random measure on ∂D and is used to define quantum lengths of boundary arcs.

The boundary KPZ relation connects the Euclidean scaling dimension x of a boundary operator to its quantum scaling dimension Δ on an LQG surface with boundary. For a boundary primary operator of weight x in a conformal field theory of central charge c = 1 + 6Q2 with Q = 2/γ + γ/2, the relation reads x = Δ(1 − γΔ/2). Equivalently, Δ = (2/γ)(1 − √(1 − γ2x/2)). This formula is derived by considering the expectation of the boundary Liouville measure raised to a power and using the Gaussian multiplicative chaos construction. The derivation requires a regularisation of the boundary GFF, typically by circle averages centred on the boundary, and a careful analysis of the logarithmic divergence of the covariance kernel near the boundary.

For γ = √κ with κ ∈ (0,4) the boundary KPZ formula predicts the scaling dimensions of the SLEκ boundary condition changing operators. The weight of the curve‑creating operator is h = (6−κ)/(2κ). Plugging x = h into the boundary KPZ relation yields Δ = 2/γ − γ/2 = χ, which is precisely the imaginary geometry parameter. This consistency check confirms that the SLEκ curve is the flow line of the GFF with the correct quantum length parametrisation. For κ = 8/3 (percolation) one obtains γ = √(8/3) and Δ = 5/√6, matching the known quantum dimension of the percolation interface.

The boundary measure also appears in the conformal welding of quantum disks. Two independent quantum disks with boundary lengths L1 and L2 can be welded along their boundaries by identifying points according to the boundary Liouville measure. The resulting surface is a quantum sphere with a marked SLEκ curve separating the two disks. The law of the interface is SLEκ(ρL,ρR) with force‑point weights determined by the quantum lengths. This construction underlies the mating‑of‑trees theorem for κ ∈ (0,4) and provides a rigorous definition of the quantum zipper.

A frequent mistake is to apply the bulk KPZ formula x = Δ(1 − γ2Δ/4) to boundary operators. The bulk formula uses the full GFF variance, while the boundary formula uses half the variance, leading to the factor 1/2 in the exponent and the different coefficient in the quadratic term. Using the bulk formula for boundary dimensions overestimates the quantum dimension by a factor of two in the small‑γ limit.

SLE and the Random Planar Maps: The Scaling Limit of the Uniform Infinite Planar Map and the Brownian Disk

The uniform infinite planar map (UIPM) is the local limit of uniform planar maps with n edges as n → ∞, without conditioning on the topology of the faces. Its scaling limit, after rescaling graph distances by n−1/4, is the Brownian disk, a random compact metric space homeomorphic to the closed unit disk. The Brownian disk can be constructed from a Brownian excursion of length 1 conditioned to stay positive, together with an independent Brownian motion that encodes the boundary length process. The pair of correlated Brownian motions has correlation −cos(4π/κ) with κ = 8/3, the value corresponding to pure gravity (uniform maps).

The Brownian disk is distinct from the Brownian sphere (the scaling limit of uniform triangulations or quadrangulations of the sphere) and from the Brownian plane (the scaling limit of the UIPQ). The disk has a non‑trivial boundary whose quantum length is a random variable with a known density. The interior of the disk is isometric to a Brownian sphere with a marked boundary, and the boundary itself is a continuum random tree coded by the Brownian excursion. The mating‑of‑trees theorem for the Brownian disk states that an SLEκ curve drawn on an independent γ‑LQG disk (γ = √κ) separates the disk into two independent quantum disks whose boundary lengths are given by the correlated Brownian motions.

Convergence of the UIPM to the Brownian disk has been proved in the Gromov‑Hausdorff‑Prokhorov topology. The proof uses a bijection between planar maps and labelled trees (the Bouttier‑Di Francesco‑Guitter bijection) and a tightness argument for the associated contour and label functions. The limiting object is characterised by the fact that its volume measure is the Lebesgue measure on the unit disk and its distance kernel satisfies the Brownian snake equation. The boundary length process converges to a 3/2‑stable Lévy excursion, which is the scaling limit of the perimeter of the map.

On the Brownian disk, the uniform spanning tree (UST) converges to SLE8 and the FK‑Ising model converges to SLE16/3. The critical O(n) loop model converges to CLEκ with the appropriate κ. These convergences are proved by showing that the discrete exploration processes converge to the corresponding SLE driving functions, using the convergence of the underlying random walk on the map to Brownian motion. The correlation −cos(4π/κ) between the two Brownian motions in the mating‑of‑trees encoding is essential for the correct identification of the SLE parameter.

A common confusion is to state that the UIPM scaling limit is the Brownian plane. The Brownian plane is non‑compact and arises from the UIPQ without conditioning on the number of faces, whereas the Brownian disk is compact and arises from the UIPM (or from finite maps conditioned on the number of edges). The two objects have different boundary behaviour: the Brownian plane has no boundary, while the Brownian disk has a random boundary of finite quantum length.

SLE and the Conformal Loop Ensembles: The CLE(κ) Carpet and the Conformal Restriction Property for Annuli

The conformal loop ensemble CLEκ for κ ∈ (8/3,4) is a random countable collection of disjoint simple loops in a simply connected domain D. The CLE carpet is the union of all loops. While the CLE carpet satisfies a conformal restriction property for simply connected subdomains, a stronger statement holds for annular subdomains. Let A ⊂ D be an annulus whose boundary components are disjoint from ∂D. Condition on the event that no loop of the CLE intersects the inner boundary of A. Then the conditional law of the loops that stay entirely in A is the law of CLEκ in A with a modified boundary condition on the inner boundary: the inner boundary is treated as a free boundary, meaning that loops are allowed to touch it but not to cross it.

This annular restriction property can be derived from the SLEκ(ρ) description of the outermost loop surrounding a given point. The outermost loop is an SLEκ(ρ) curve with force points placed at the boundary of the annulus. The weight ρ is chosen so that the curve is a local martingale for the restriction event. The calculation yields ρ = (8−κ)/2κ for the inner boundary and ρ = 0 for the outer boundary. The resulting SLEκ(ρ) curve has the law of the outer boundary of the CLE carpet restricted to the annulus.

The annular restriction property implies a factorisation formula for the partition function of CLEκ on an annulus. The partition function ZCLE(A) can be written as a product of the partition function of a single SLEκ(ρ) curve and the partition function of CLEκ on the two complementary simply connected components. This factorisation is the continuum analogue of the domain Markov property for the O(n) loop model on a lattice annulus.

Using the annular restriction property one can compute the probability that a CLEκ carpet contains a loop separating two given boundary arcs of an annulus. The answer is expressed in terms of the hypergeometric function 2F1(a,b;c;z) with parameters a = (8−κ)/2κ, b = (3κ−8)/2κ, c = 1, and z the conformal modulus of the annulus. For κ = 3 (Ising model) this probability simplifies to an elementary function involving the elliptic modulus.

A frequent error is to assume that the CLE carpet satisfies conformal restriction for arbitrary subdomains, including those with holes that are not simply connected. The property holds only for simply connected subdomains and for annuli with the specific free boundary condition on the inner boundary. For a general multiply connected domain the conditional law of the loops is not a CLEκ but a more complicated ensemble with additional force points on each boundary component.

The boundary operator product expansion (OPE) in a boundary conformal field theory (BCFT) governs how primary fields fuse when they approach each other on the real line. In the SLE framework the driving function of a chordal SLEκ curve is a scaled Brownian motion Wt = √κ Bt. SLE and the Gaussian Free Field: Mixed Boundary Conditions, Neumann‑Dirichlet Transitions, and SLE(κ,ρ) Couplings

The Gaussian free field (GFF) with mixed boundary conditions provides a natural continuum model for interfaces separating regions with different boundary behaviours. On a simply connected domain D with a marked boundary arc I where Dirichlet conditions are imposed and the complementary arc where Neumann conditions hold, the GFF is a distribution whose harmonic extension equals zero on I and has vanishing normal derivative on ∂D\\I. The level line of this GFF at height λ = π/2 (or −λ) is an SLE4(ρ) curve with a single force point at the transition point between Dirichlet and Neumann arcs. The weight ρ = −1 corresponds to the boundary condition changing operator of dimension 1/2 in the c = 1 free boson CFT. More generally, for κ ∈ (0,4) the flow lines of a GFF with piecewise constant boundary data are SLEκ(ρ⃗) curves where each jump in the boundary data creates a force point with weight ρ = (Δh)/χ, Δh being the height difference. The imaginary geometry coupling shows that the GFF can be decomposed into a harmonic function determined by the boundary data plus an independent zero‑boundary GFF; the SLE curve is then the flow line of the harmonic part perturbed by the zero‑boundary field. When κ ∈ (4,8) the same construction yields counterflow lines, which are SLEκ(ρ⃗) curves with ρ = κ/2 − 2 at each transition. The Neumann‑Dirichlet transition is a special case where the height jump equals λ, giving ρ = −2 for κ = 4 and ρ = κ/2 − 2 for κ > 4. These couplings have been used to prove the convergence of the harmonic explorer and of the level lines of the discrete GFF on the square lattice to SLE4(−1) and SLE4(−2) respectively. The mixed boundary condition GFF also appears in the scaling limit of the uniform spanning tree with wired and free boundary arcs, where the Peano curve of the tree converges to SLE8(−2) which is the outer boundary of the CLE8/3 carpet. The precise identification of the force‑point weights relies on the martingale property of the GFF exponential observables and on the conformal covariance of the Dirichlet‑to‑Neumann operator.

SLE and the Gaussian Free Field: Imaginary Geometry for κ ∈ (4,8) and Space‑Filling SLE

For κ > 4 the chordal SLE trace is no longer simple; it hits the boundary and itself, eventually becoming space‑filling at κ = 8. This framework proves reversibility of SLEκ for κ ∈ (4,8) and identifies the law of the time‑reversed curve as SLEκ(ρ) with force points ρ = (κ−6)/2 on each side of the curve.
