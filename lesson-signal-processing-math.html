Signal Processing — Fourier Analysis, Filtering & the Sampling Theorem

Signal processing studies the representation, manipulation, and analysis of signals (functions of time or space) using mathematical transforms. At the heart lies the Fourier transform, which decomposes a signal into its constituent frequencies and provides the foundation for all modern signal processing. The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem bridges continuous and discrete worlds: a bandlimited signal is completely determined by its values at a sufficiently high sampling rate. Digital filters, both finite impulse response (FIR) and infinite impulse response (IIR), process discrete-time signals. Wavelet theory extends Fourier analysis with time-frequency localization, enabling multiresolution decomposition. Modern advances include compressed sensing, which reconstructs sparse signals from far fewer measurements than traditional Nyquist-rate sampling, and wavelet denoising, which removes noise by thresholding wavelet coefficients. Together, these ideas power everything from audio compression and image restoration to medical imaging and communications.

Part 1

📏 The Fourier Transform and Its Variants

Fourier Analysis
The Continuous Fourier Transform, the DTFT, the DFT, and the FFT
The continuous-time Fourier transform (CTFT) of a signal f(t) ∈ L¹(ℝ) ∩ L²(ℝ) is ̂f(ω) = ∫_{−∞}^{∞} f(t) e^{−iωt} dt, with the inverse Fourier transform f(t) = (1/2π) ∫_{−∞}^{∞} ̂f(ω) e^{iωt} dω. Key properties: linearity (αf+βg ⇔ α̂f+βĝ), time shift (f(t−t₀) ⇔ e^{−iωt₀}̂f(ω)), frequency shift (e^{iω₀t}f(t) ⇔ ̂f(ω−ω₀)), convolution ((f∗g)(t) = ∫ f(τ)g(t−τ)dτ ⇔ ̂f(ω)·ĝ(ω)), and differentiation (f'(t) ⇔ iω̂f(ω)). The Plancherel theorem (extended by Parseval) preserves energy: ∫_{−∞}^{∞} |f(t)|² dt = (1/2π) ∫_{−∞}^{∞} |̂f(ω)|² dω. The discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) of a sequence x[n] is X(e^{iω}) = Σ_{n=−∞}^{∞} x[n] e^{−iωn}, periodic in ω with period 2π. The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of length N is X[k] = Σ_{n=0}^{N−1} x[n] e^{−2πikn/N} for k = 0, …, N−1 — it samples the DTFT at N equally spaced frequencies. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) computes the DFT in O(N log N) operations. The Cooley-Tukey decimation-in-time algorithm splits the N-point DFT into two N/2-point DFTs: X[k] = Σ_{n even} x[n] ω_N^{kn} + Σ_{n odd} x[n] ω_N^{kn} = DFT_{N/2}(x_even)[k] + ω_N^k · DFT_{N/2}(x_odd)[k], where ω_N = e^{−2πi/N}. This recursion yields the celebrated O(N log N) complexity — the Danielson-Lanczos lemma shows that a DFT of length N = 2^m can be reduced to m stages of 2-point DFTs (butterflies). The spectral leakage (Gibbs phenomenon) occurs when the signal is not periodic in the DFT window; windowing functions (Hamming, Hann, Blackman) mitigate this.
The Fourier transform decomposes signals into frequencies; the FFT makes the DFT computationally feasible and is one of the most important algorithms of the 20th century.

🌍 The Sampling Theorem and Filtering

Sampling
Nyquist-Shannon Theorem, Aliasing, Upsampling, Downsampling, and Digital Filters
The Nyquist-Shannon-Kotelnikov sampling theorem: if a continuous-time signal f(t) is bandlimited to Ω (i.e., ̂f(ω) = 0 for |ω| ≥ Ω), then f(t) is uniquely determined by its samples at the Nyquist rate 2Ω (samples per second). The reconstruction formula is f(t) = Σ_{n=−∞}^{∞} f(nπ/Ω) · sinc(Ωt/π − n), where the sinc function sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx) serves as the ideal interpolation kernel. Equivalently, the reconstruction uses an ideal lowpass filter with cutoff Ω. Aliasing: when sampling below the Nyquist rate, high-frequency components fold down into the baseband, producing artifacts indistinguishable from genuine low-frequency content — the spectra overlap in the frequency domain. The anti-aliasing filter (lowpass prefilter) removes frequencies above half the sampling rate before sampling. Sampling rate conversion: upsampling by a factor L inserts L−1 zeros between samples, then lowpass filters (interpolation); downsampling (decimation) by a factor M keeps every M-th sample, preceded by a lowpass filter to prevent aliasing. The Z-transform of a discrete-time signal h[n] is H(z) = Σ_{n=−∞}^{∞} h[n] z^{-n}, with region of convergence (ROC) determining stability and causality. A linear time-invariant (LTI) system is fully characterized by its impulse response h[n]: y[n] = (x∗h)[n] = Σ_{k=−∞}^{∞} x[k] h[n−k]. The transfer function H(e^{iω}) = |H(e^{iω})| e^{i∠H(e^{iω})} gives the frequency response — the magnitude and phase. FIR filters (finite impulse response) have h[n] = 0 for n > M, are unconditionally stable, and can achieve exact linear phase (constant group delay). IIR filters (infinite impulse response) use feedback: y[n] = Σ_{k=0}^{M} b_k x[n−k] − Σ_{k=1}^{N} a_k y[n−k]; they are more efficient but can be unstable and have nonlinear phase. The bilinear transform maps analog filter H_a(s) to digital H_d(z) via s = (2/T)·(1−z^{-1})/(1+z^{-1}) — it warps the frequency axis but preserves stability and maps the imaginary axis to the unit circle. The Butterworth filter has a maximally flat passband with monotonic roll-off; the Chebyshev filter (type I or II) allows ripple in the passband or stopband for a steeper cutoff. The convolution theorem for the Z-transform: (x∗h)[n] ⇔ X(z)·H(z) on the intersection of ROCs.
The sampling theorem is the bridge between continuous and discrete worlds; aliasing is its fundamental pitfall, and digital filters are the primary tool for discrete-time processing.

🧮 Wavelet Theory and the Multiresolution Analysis

Wavelets
The Continuous and Discrete Wavelet Transform, MRA, and the Fast Wavelet Transform
The wavelet transform addresses the limitation of the Fourier transform — poor time localization for high frequencies. The continuous wavelet transform (CWT) of f(t) is W_ψ f(a,b) = (1/√{|a|}) ∫_{−∞}^{∞} f(t) ψ((t−b)/a) dt, where a is the scale (dilation) and b is the translation (shift). The mother wavelet ψ(t) must satisfy the admissibility condition: C_ψ = ∫_{0}^{∞} |̂ψ(ω)|²/|ω| dω < ∞. The CWT is redundant; the discrete wavelet transform (DWT) uses dyadic scales a = 2^j and b = k·2^j. The multiresolution analysis (MRA) (Mallat-Meyer, 1989) provides the rigorous framework: a sequence of closed subspaces V_j ⊂ L²(ℝ) such that (1) V_j ⊂ V_{j+1} (nested), (2) ∩_{j} V_j = {0} and ∪_{j} V_j = L²(ℝ) (dense), (3) f(t) ∈ V_j ⇔ f(2t) ∈ V_{j+1} (scaling), and (4) there exists a scaling function φ(t) whose integer translates {φ(t−k)} form an orthonormal basis of V₀. The dilation equation (two-scale relation): φ(t) = Σ_k h_k φ(2t−k), where {h_k} are the lowpass filter coefficients (scaling coefficients). The wavelet ψ(t) is defined via ψ(t) = Σ_k g_k φ(2t−k) with g_k = (−1)^k h_{1−k} (quadrature mirror filter). The Haar wavelet is the simplest: φ(t) = 1 for t ∈ [0,1), 0 otherwise; ψ(t) = 1 for t ∈ [0,½), −1 for t ∈ [½,1), 0 otherwise. The Daubechies wavelets (Ingrid Daubechies, 1988) have compact support and N vanishing moments: ∫ t^k ψ(t) dt = 0 for k = 0, …, N−1 — they can analyze polynomials up to degree N−1. Daubechies D4 (N=2) is widely used. The fast wavelet transform (FWT, pyramid algorithm): given coefficients at scale j, the coefficients at scale j−1 are obtained by convolving with the lowpass filter h and downsampling (approximation coefficients), and with the highpass filter g and downsampling (detail coefficients). This yields O(N) complexity — faster than the FFT. The wavelet packet transform further decomposes both approximation and detail branches, enabling finer frequency resolution.
Wavelet theory provides a time-frequency multiresolution analysis that surpasses the Fourier transform for signals with localized features — a cornerstone of modern signal processing.

🔥 Compressed Sensing and Wavelet Denoising

Advanced Topics
Wavelet Thresholding, Compressed Sensing, ℓ₁-Minimization, and Variational Denoising
Wavelet denoising (Donoho-Johnstone, 1994): for a noisy measurement y = x + z with z ∼ N(0, σ²I), the wavelet transform concentrates the signal energy into a few large coefficients while noise spreads uniformly. The denoised estimate is ̂x = W^{-1}(η_λ(Wy)), where η_λ is the thresholding function. Hard thresholding: η_λ(y) = y if |y| > λ, else 0. Soft thresholding: η_λ(y) = sign(y)·max(|y|−λ, 0) — it shrinks all coefficients by λ and avoids discontinuities. The universal threshold λ = σ·√(2 log N) ensures with high probability that pure noise is eliminated (but may oversmooth). Stein's unbiased risk estimate (SURE) yields the optimal threshold by minimizing an unbiased estimate of the MSE — adaptive per subband. The SureShrink hybrid combines universal and SURE thresholds. Compressed sensing (Candès-Romberg-Tao, Donoho, 2006): a signal x ∈ ℝ^N that is K-sparse in an orthonormal basis Ψ (i.e., ‖Ψ^T x‖_0 = K << N) can be recovered from m = O(K·log(N/K)) random linear measurements y = Φx (where Φ is an m×N sensing matrix) via ℓ₁-minimization: minimize ‖Ψ^T x‖₁ subject to Φx = y. The restricted isometry property (RIP) of Φ ensures stable recovery — random matrices (Gaussian, Bernoulli, partial Fourier) satisfy RIP with high probability. This violates the traditional Nyquist dogma: fewer samples than the signal bandwidth suffice if the signal is sparse. Variational denoising: the total variation (TV) denoising (Rudin-Osher-Fatemi, 1992) minimizes ‖x−y‖₂² + λ·TV(x), where TV(x) = Σ_i |x_{i+1}−x_i| (for 1D) or the gradient magnitude (for 2D) — it preserves edges while smoothing. The median filter replaces each sample with the median of its neighbors (nonlinear, excellent for salt-and-pepper noise). The bilateral filter combines domain and range Gaussian kernels, preserving edges. Morphological component analysis (MCA) separates a signal into morphological components (e.g., texture + cartoon) using sparsity in different dictionaries. Applications: audio denoising (wavelet thresholding for removing hiss), image restoration (deconvolution using Tikhonov regularization or total variation), super-resolution (sparse representation-based), and JPEG2000 compression using the discrete wavelet transform (Daubechies 9/7 biorthogonal wavelets). The ISTA (Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm) and FISTA (Fast ISTA) solve ℓ₁-regularized inverse problems efficiently.
Wavelet denoising and compressed sensing shattered the traditional limits of signal processing — signals can be denoised by shrinkage and reconstructed from far fewer measurements than classical theory allowed.

Multirate Signal Processing and Polyphase Decomposition

Multirate systems change the sampling rate of a discrete‑time signal by integer factors. Decimation by M retains every M‑th sample after lowpass filtering to prevent aliasing; interpolation by L inserts L−1 zeros between samples and then lowpass filters to remove images. The polyphase representation splits a filter h[n] of length N into M subfilters h_k[n] = h[nM + k] for k = 0,…,M−1. This structure moves filtering before the downsampler, reducing computational load by a factor of M. For interpolation, polyphase components operate after the upsampler, again saving multiplications. The noble identities state that a filter followed by a downsampler is equivalent to a downsampler followed by a polyphase filter bank, and similarly for upsampling. Efficient filter banks, such as the quadrature mirror filter (QMF) bank used in subband coding, rely on these identities. Perfect reconstruction (PR) requires that the analysis and synthesis polyphase matrices satisfy E(z)R(z) = c z-k I, where c is a constant gain and k an integer delay. PR filter‑bank designs often use lattice structures or cosine‑modulated filter banks, which yield linear‑phase prototypes with near‑perfect reconstruction. In practice, multirate techniques underlie digital audio (MP3, AAC), image compression (JPEG 2000), and software‑defined radio channelization.

Time‑Frequency Analysis: Short‑Time Fourier Transform and Wigner‑Ville Distribution

The short‑time Fourier transform (STFT) applies a sliding window w(t) to a signal x(t) and computes the Fourier transform of each windowed segment: X(t,ω) = ∫ x(τ) w(τ−t) e-jωτ dτ. The window length determines the trade‑off between time and frequency resolution: a long window yields fine frequency resolution but poor time localization, while a short window does the opposite. The spectrogram |X(t,ω)|² is a common visual tool. The Wigner‑Ville distribution (WVD) provides a quadratic time‑frequency representation with optimal concentration for linear chirps: W_x(t,ω) = ∫ x(t+τ/2) x*(t−τ/2) e-jωτ dτ. It satisfies the marginal properties ∫ W_x(t,ω) dω = |x(t)|² and ∫ W_x(t,ω) dt = |X(ω)|². However, the WVD suffers from cross‑term interference when the signal contains multiple components, producing oscillatory artifacts that can mask true energy. The Choi‑Williams kernel and the smoothed pseudo‑WVD attenuate cross‑terms by convolving the WVD with a separable kernel in the ambiguity domain. The reassignment method sharpens the spectrogram by moving each coefficient to the local center of gravity of the energy distribution. These tools are essential for radar pulse analysis, speech formant tracking, and seismic signal interpretation.

Worked Example
A signal consists of two linear chirps crossing in the time‑frequency plane. Explain why the spectrogram with a fixed Gaussian window cannot resolve the crossing, and how the reassigned spectrogram improves the representation.
The spectrogram computes |∫ x(t) w(t−τ) e-jωt dt|². With a fixed window, the time‑frequency resolution cell has area Δt·Δω ≥ 1/2 (Heisenberg). At the crossing, the two chirps occupy the same cell, so their energies add and the ridge appears blurred. The reassigned spectrogram calculates the local group delay τ_g(τ,ω) = τ − ∂φ/∂ω and instantaneous frequency ω_i(τ,ω) = ω + ∂φ/∂τ, where φ is the phase of the STFT. Each coefficient is moved to (τ_g, ω_i), concentrating energy along the true chirp trajectories. Because reassignment uses phase derivatives, it separates crossing components provided the window is short enough to capture local linearity, yielding a sharper picture without increasing the window length.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the time‑frequency resolution trade‑off, state the Heisenberg inequality Δt·Δω ≥ 1/2 and link it to window length. For WVD, recall the cross‑term problem and name at least one kernel (Choi‑Williams, Born‑Jordan) that mitigates it. Mention reassignment as a post‑processing step that sharpens the spectrogram without changing the window.

Adaptive Filtering: LMS, RLS, and Kalman Filter

Adaptive filters adjust their coefficients to minimize a cost function in real time. The least‑mean‑squares (LMS) algorithm updates the weight vector w[n] by w[n+1] = w[n] + μ e[n] x[n], where e[n] = d[n] − wT[n] x[n] is the error, x[n] the input vector, and μ the step size. Convergence in the mean requires 0 < μ < 2/λmax, where λmax is the largest eigenvalue of the input autocorrelation matrix R. The normalized LMS (NLMS) uses μ[n] = α / (xT[n] x[n] + ε) to stabilize convergence for varying signal power. The recursive least‑squares (RLS) algorithm minimizes the exponentially weighted least‑squares cost J[n] = Σk=0n λn−k |e[k]|² with forgetting factor λ (0 < λ ≤ 1). RLS updates the inverse correlation matrix P[n] via the matrix inversion lemma, achieving fast convergence (order of filter length) at O(N²) complexity. The Kalman filter is the optimal linear estimator for state‑space models with Gaussian noise. It propagates the state estimate x̂[n|n−1] and error covariance P[n|n−1] through prediction and update equations, yielding the minimum mean‑square error estimate. In system identification, LMS is preferred for simplicity; RLS is used when rapid tracking is needed; Kalman filtering is chosen when a state‑space model is available and optimality is required.

Spectral Estimation: Parametric and Nonparametric Methods

Nonparametric methods estimate the power spectral density (PSD) directly from data without assuming a signal model. The periodogram is the squared magnitude of the DFT: Pper(ω) = (1/N) |Σn=0N-1 x[n] e-jωn|². It is inconsistent (variance does not decrease with N) and suffers from spectral leakage. Welch’s method averages overlapping, windowed periodograms to reduce variance at the cost of resolution. The multitaper method uses a set of orthogonal Slepian tapers to produce multiple independent spectral estimates, then averages them, achieving optimal bias‑variance trade‑off for a given time‑bandwidth product. Parametric methods assume the signal is generated by a rational model. The autoregressive (AR) model x[n] = −Σk=1p ak x[n−k] + w[n] yields a PSD PAR(ω) = σw² / |1 + Σ ak e-jωk|². The Yule‑Walker equations solve for ak from the autocorrelation sequence; the Levinson‑Durbin recursion computes them in O(p²). Moving‑average (MA) and ARMA models extend flexibility. Model order selection uses Akaike’s information criterion (AIC) or the minimum description length (MDL). High‑resolution subspace methods such as MUSIC and ESPRIT exploit the eigenstructure of the sample covariance matrix to resolve closely spaced sinusoids beyond the Fourier limit. These techniques are standard in radar, sonar, and biomedical signal analysis.

Signal Processing in Modern Communications: OFDM and MIMO

Orthogonal frequency‑division multiplexing (OFDM) divides a wideband channel into N orthogonal subcarriers spaced by Δf = 1/Tu, where Tu is the useful symbol duration. The transmitted baseband signal is s(t) = Σk=0N-1 Xk ej2πkΔf t for 0 ≤ t < Tu, generated efficiently by an inverse DFT (IDFT) followed by cyclic prefix insertion. The cyclic prefix of length Lcp ≥ channel delay spread converts linear convolution into circular convolution, enabling one‑tap frequency‑domain equalization: Yk = Hk Xk + Nk. Bit and power loading across subcarriers (water‑filling) maximizes capacity under a total power constraint. Multiple‑input multiple‑output (MIMO) systems exploit spatial dimensions. For a narrowband flat‑fading channel with Nt transmit and Nr receive antennas, the received vector y = H x + n, where H ∈ ℂNr×Nt. Singular value decomposition H = U Σ VH diagonalizes the channel into parallel eigen‑channels. With perfect channel state information at the transmitter, the capacity is C = Σ log₂(1 + Pi λi² / σ²), where λi are singular values and Pi allocated by water‑filling. Space‑time coding (Alamouti scheme for Nt=2) provides diversity without channel knowledge at the transmitter. In massive MIMO, Nt ≫ Nr, channel hardening and favorable propagation simplify linear precoding (zero‑forcing, regularized zero‑forcing) and enable uplink‑downlink reciprocity in time‑division duplex. These principles underpin LTE, Wi‑Fi (802.11ac/ax), and 5G NR waveforms.

Higher‑Order Statistics and Polyspectra

Second‑order statistics such as the autocorrelation and power spectrum capture only Gaussian‑like structure. Many real‑world signals — speech, radar clutter, biomedical recordings — exhibit non‑Gaussian behavior that is invisible to the power spectrum. Higher‑order statistics (HOS) extend the analysis to third‑order and fourth‑order moments, leading to the bispectrum and trispectrum. The bispectrum B(ω₁, ω₂) is the two‑dimensional Fourier transform of the third‑order cumulant sequence c₃(m,n) = E[x[k] x[k+m] x[k+n]] for a zero‑mean stationary process. It preserves phase information that the power spectrum discards, enabling detection of quadratic phase coupling between frequency components. The trispectrum, the Fourier transform of the fourth‑order cumulant, similarly captures cubic interactions. Estimation of polyspectra requires careful averaging to reduce variance; direct (FFT‑based) and indirect (cumulant‑based) estimators are both used. A key practical constraint is the computational load: the bispectrum of an N‑point sequence demands O(N²) storage and O(N² log N) operations, motivating the use of decimation or parametric modeling. Applications include fault diagnosis in rotating machinery where quadratic nonlinearities generate characteristic bispectral peaks, and EEG analysis where phase coupling between brain rhythms may indicate cognitive states. Examiners often test the distinction between the bispectrum and the power spectrum: the former is complex‑valued and symmetric only under specific index permutations, while the latter is real and even. Another common twist is to ask why the bispectrum of a linear Gaussian process is identically zero — the answer lies in the vanishing of third‑order cumulants for Gaussian variables.

Parametric bispectral estimation fits an autoregressive moving‑average (ARMA) model to the data and computes the theoretical bispectrum from the model coefficients. This approach reduces variance dramatically but introduces model‑order selection issues; Akaike’s information criterion (AIC) and minimum description length (MDL) are standard tools. Non‑parametric estimators such as the direct FFT method segment the data, compute the DFT of each segment, and average the triple products X(ω₁) X(ω₂) X*(ω₁+ω₂). Windowing each segment reduces spectral leakage but widens the main lobe, trading resolution for bias. The choice of window (Hamming, Kaiser, Slepian) influences the bias‑variance trade‑off in a manner analogous to power‑spectrum estimation. A subtle point: the bispectrum is invariant to linear filtering only if the filter is minimum‑phase; otherwise the phase of the filter distorts the bispectral phase. This property is exploited in blind deconvolution where the goal is to recover a non‑Gaussian source from a filtered observation. In radar, the bispectrum of the received echo can reveal target micro‑motion induced quadratic phase coupling, providing a discriminant that the power spectrum cannot. Understanding these mechanisms, the estimation trade‑offs, and the invariance properties is essential for advanced signal‑processing examinations.

Higher‑order spectra also play a role in array processing. The fourth‑order cumulant matrix can be used to separate more sources than sensors when the sources are non‑Gaussian and statistically independent, a principle underlying independent component analysis (ICA) and the JADE algorithm. The cumulant‑based MUSIC variant replaces the covariance matrix with a fourth‑order cumulant matrix, achieving super‑resolution for non‑Gaussian sources. However, the sample cumulant matrix converges more slowly than the covariance matrix, requiring larger snapshots for reliable eigen‑decomposition. This convergence behavior is a frequent exam topic: candidates must explain why fourth‑order methods need more data and how the signal‑to‑noise ratio affects the threshold. Another nuance is that the bispectrum vanishes for symmetric distributions with zero skewness; in such cases the trispectrum becomes the lowest‑order non‑vanishing polyspectrum. Recognizing when to move from third‑ to fourth‑order analysis demonstrates depth of understanding beyond textbook definitions.

Finally, the computational burden of polyspectral analysis has driven the development of fast algorithms based on the chirp‑Z transform and on tensor decompositions. The bispectrum can be viewed as a slice of a third‑order tensor formed by the outer product of the DFT vector with itself. Canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition of this tensor yields a low‑rank approximation that can dramatically reduce storage and has potential for real‑time implementation on embedded platforms. These modern techniques bridge classical HOS theory with contemporary machine‑learning pipelines, a connection that examiners increasingly probe through scenario‑based questions.

Sparse Bayesian Learning for Signal Recovery

Sparse representation models a signal x ∈ ℝⁿ as x = Dα where D ∈ ℝⁿˣᵐ (m ≥ n) is an overcomplete dictionary and α is a sparse coefficient vector. Classical approaches such as basis pursuit (ℓ₁ minimization) and orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) treat sparsity as a deterministic constraint. Sparse Bayesian learning (SBL) instead places a hierarchical prior on α: each coefficient αᵢ receives a zero‑mean Gaussian prior with its own variance (hyperparameter) γᵢ, and the hyperparameters themselves are given a non‑informative Gamma prior. The resulting marginal likelihood p(y|γ) = ∫ p(y|α) p(α|γ) dα can be maximized with respect to γ using an expectation‑maximization (EM) algorithm or a type‑II maximum likelihood (evidence maximization) procedure. The EM updates have a closed form: γᵢ^(new) = (μᵢ² + Σᵢᵢ) where μ and Σ are the posterior mean and covariance of α given the current γ. This automatic relevance determination (ARD) mechanism drives irrelevant coefficients’ variances to zero, yielding a sparse solution without an explicit ℓ₁ penalty. The SBL framework naturally extends to multiple measurement vectors (MMV) by sharing the same sparsity pattern across columns, leading to the M‑SBL algorithm which exploits joint sparsity for improved recovery.

A key advantage of SBL over convex relaxation is its ability to incorporate structured sparsity through the prior covariance. For example, block sparsity can be modeled by grouping coefficients and assigning a common variance to each block, while tree‑structured sparsity (as in wavelet coefficients) can be encoded via a Markov random field prior on γ. The resulting inference remains tractable because the posterior stays Gaussian; only the hyperparameter updates change. In compressed sensing, SBL can achieve exact recovery with fewer measurements than ℓ₁ methods in certain settings, especially when the dictionary is highly coherent. However, SBL’s non‑convex evidence surface can contain local maxima; good initialization (e.g., using the least‑squares solution or a few OMP iterations) mitigates this risk. The computational cost per EM iteration is dominated by the inversion of an m×m matrix, which can be reduced using the matrix inversion lemma when the number of active coefficients is small. This complexity profile makes SBL attractive for offline processing but challenging for real‑time applications unless fast approximations such as the variational Bayesian SBL or the approximate message passing (AMP) variant are employed.

Dictionary learning within the Bayesian paradigm treats D as an unknown parameter with a matrix‑normal prior. The joint optimization over D and γ alternates between updating the posterior of α (given D, γ) and updating D via a gradient step on the evidence. This Bayesian dictionary learning (BDL) framework automatically determines the effective dictionary size by pruning unused atoms, a feature absent in K‑SVD or MOD. In research literature, BDL has been applied to image denoising, where the learned dictionary adapts to local patch statistics, and to hyperspectral unmixing, where the sparsity pattern corresponds to material abundances. Examiners may ask to derive the EM update for γ in the single‑measurement case, or to explain why the ARD prior leads to sparsity while a simple isotropic Gaussian prior does not. The answer hinges on the fact that the marginal prior on α after integrating out γ becomes a Student‑t distribution with heavy tails, which strongly favors zero coefficients. Another common trap is to confuse the evidence maximization in SBL with the marginal likelihood maximization in Gaussian process regression; the former optimizes hyperparameters of a sparse prior, the latter optimizes kernel hyperparameters of a dense prior.

Recent advances combine SBL with deep learning: the learned iterative shrinkage‑thresholding algorithm (LISTA) can be interpreted as unrolling a fixed number of EM iterations with learned step sizes and thresholds, bridging model‑based and data‑driven approaches. This hybrid perspective is increasingly tested in advanced examinations through questions that require mapping a graphical model to a neural network architecture. Understanding the probabilistic foundations, the EM derivation, and the practical trade‑offs between SBL and convex methods equips candidates to tackle both theoretical derivations and application‑oriented problems.

Worked Example: Bispectrum of a Quadratic Phase‑Coupled Signal
A discrete‑time signal x[n] = cos(ω₁ n) + cos(ω₂ n) + cos((ω₁+ω₂) n + φ) is observed, where ω₁ = 0.2π, ω₂ = 0.3π, and φ = π/4. Compute the theoretical bispectrum B(ω₁, ω₂) and explain why the power spectrum alone cannot reveal the phase coupling.
The signal consists of three sinusoids. The third‑order cumulant of a sum of independent sinusoids is zero unless the frequencies satisfy a quadratic phase‑coupling condition ω₃ = ω₁ + ω₂. Here the third component exactly matches ω₁+ω₂, so the third‑order moment E[x[n] x[n+m] x[n+k]] contains a non‑zero term proportional to cos(φ). The bispectrum is the 2‑D Fourier transform of this cumulant. For a single triplet (ω₁, ω₂, ω₁+ω₂) the bispectrum reduces to a complex constant at (ω₁, ω₂) with magnitude A₁ A₂ A₃ / 8 and phase φ, where Aᵢ are the amplitudes (here all 1). Thus B(0.2π, 0.3π) = (1/8) ejπ/4. All other bispectral entries are zero. The power spectrum P(ω) = (π/2)[δ(ω-ω₁)+δ(ω+ω₁)+δ(ω-ω₂)+δ(ω+ω₂)+δ(ω-(ω₁+ω₂))+δ(ω+(ω₁+ω₂))] shows only the three frequencies and their negatives; it contains no information about φ because the power spectrum discards phase. Hence the bispectrum uniquely identifies the quadratic phase coupling and the coupling phase φ, which the power spectrum cannot.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a polyspectral property, first state the order of the statistic (third‑order → bispectrum, fourth‑order → trispectrum) and the corresponding cumulant definition. Then note the symmetry and invariance properties (e.g., bispectrum symmetry B(ω₁,ω₂)=B*(−ω₁,−ω₂) for real signals). For SBL, write the hierarchical prior explicitly: α|γ ∼ N(0,Γ), γ ∼ Gamma(a,b). Derive the EM update for γ in one line: γᵢ ← μᵢ² + Σᵢᵢ. This compact derivation scores full marks for the “show the update” part. Finally, always contrast the method with its convex counterpart (ℓ₁ vs ARD) and mention the computational bottleneck (matrix inversion vs linear programming).

Non‑Uniform Sampling Theory and Applications

The classical Shannon‑Nyquist theorem assumes uniform sampling at a rate at least twice the highest frequency component. In many practical systems — magnetic resonance imaging, radar with staggered pulse repetition intervals, asynchronous sensor networks — samples are acquired at non‑uniform time instants tₙ. The reconstruction problem becomes: given samples x(tₙ) at known irregular times, recover the continuous‑time bandlimited signal x(t) with bandwidth Ω. Landau’s necessary density condition states that the average sampling density must exceed Ω/π (the Nyquist density) for stable reconstruction; however, local gaps larger than the Nyquist interval can be tolerated if the overall density condition holds. The reconstruction can be formulated as a linear system using the sinc kernel: x(t) = Σₙ cₙ sinc(Ω(t−tₙ)/π). The coefficients cₙ are obtained by solving a Toeplitz‑like system G c = xₛ where Gₘₙ = sinc(Ω(tₘ−tₙ)/π). This system is well‑conditioned if the sampling set is a set of stable sampling (a Riesz basis for the Paley‑Wiener space). Iterative algorithms such as the conjugate gradient method on the normal equations, or the adaptive weights conjugate gradient (AWCG) algorithm, exploit the structure of G for fast convergence. An alternative is the non‑uniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT) which maps irregular samples to a uniform grid via a convolution with a Kaiser‑Bessel window, enabling O(N log N) reconstruction.

Jitter analysis quantifies the effect of small random deviations from uniform sampling. If tₙ = nT + εₙ with εₙ zero‑mean i.i.d. and variance σ², the mean‑square reconstruction error for a bandlimited signal is approximately (Ω² σ²) times the signal power, provided σ ≪ T. This result shows that jitter tolerance scales inversely with bandwidth; wideband signals demand tighter clock stability. In multi‑coset sampling, a set of M uniform samplers with different offsets collectively achieve an effective rate M/T while each sampler runs at 1/T. The reconstruction reduces to solving a linear system involving the DFT of the coset pattern; perfect reconstruction is possible if the pattern satisfies the Chinese remainder theorem conditions. This technique underlies sub‑Nyquist analog‑to‑digital converters (ADCs) such as the modulated wideband converter (MWC), which mixes the input with periodic waveforms before low‑rate sampling. The MWC’s recovery guarantees rely on the spark of the sensing matrix and the restricted isometry property (RIP) from compressed sensing theory.

Applications extend to spectral estimation from irregularly spaced data, common in astronomy (unevenly spaced observations of variable stars) and geophysics (seismic traces with missing traces). The Lomb‑Scargle periodogram generalizes the periodogram to non‑uniform samples by fitting sinusoids via least squares at each frequency, yielding a statistically well‑behaved estimator. Its computational cost is O(N²) per frequency, but the fast Lomb‑Scargle algorithm using NUFFT reduces it to O(N log N). Another domain is event‑based sampling (level‑crossing or send‑on‑delta), where samples are generated only when the signal crosses predefined thresholds. This data‑dependent sampling can drastically reduce the average rate for sparse‑event signals, but reconstruction requires solving a nonlinear inverse problem often tackled with convex optimization (e.g., total variation minimization). Examiners frequently test the distinction between average‑rate conditions (Landau) and instantaneous‑rate conditions (Nyquist), and the ability to derive the jitter‑error formula from a first‑order Taylor expansion of the sinc kernel.

Recent research explores deep learning for non‑uniform reconstruction: a neural network learns a mapping from irregular samples to uniform grid values, trained on simulated bandlimited signals. While such data‑driven methods have been reported to outperform model‑based algorithms at low SNR in some studies, they lack theoretical guarantees and may fail on out‑of‑distribution signals. Understanding both the classical linear‑algebraic framework and the modern learning‑based approaches provides a comprehensive view that advanced examinations increasingly demand.

Time‑Varying Filter Design and Adaptive Wavelets

Linear time‑invariant (LTI) filters are insufficient when the signal statistics or the channel characteristics evolve over time. Time‑varying filters have an impulse response h(t,τ) that depends explicitly on the absolute time t and the lag τ. The input‑output relation is y(t) = ∫ h(t,τ) x(t−τ) dτ. In discrete time, the equivalent is y[n] = Σₖ h[n,k] x[n−k]. A common parametric form is the basis‑expansion model h[n,k] = Σₘ αₘ[n] φₘ[k] where φₘ[k] are fixed basis functions (e.g., polynomials, exponentials, or wavelets) and αₘ[n] are slowly varying coefficients. This reduces the number of degrees of freedom and enables adaptive algorithms such as the recursive least squares (RLS) on the coefficient vector α[n]. The Kalman filter provides an optimal state‑space formulation when the coefficient dynamics follow a linear Gaussian model α[n+1] = F α[n] + w[n]. The measurement equation is y[n] = φᵀ[n] α[n] + v[n] where φ[n] stacks the basis functions evaluated at the current lag. The Kalman recursions yield the minimum‑mean‑square‑error estimate of α[n] and thus of the time‑varying filter.

Adaptive wavelet transforms extend the multiresolution analysis to non‑stationary signals by allowing the wavelet basis to change with time. The lifting scheme provides a flexible framework: the prediction and update steps can be made signal‑dependent, yielding a time‑varying filter bank that still guarantees perfect reconstruction. For example, the adaptive lifting scheme chooses the prediction filter at each scale by minimizing the detail coefficients’ ℓ₁ norm, effectively matching the local regularity of the signal. This leads to a non‑linear transform that is not shift‑invariant but offers superior energy compaction for transient phenomena such as edges in images or spikes in EEG. The design of adaptive wavelets can be cast as an optimization over the space of biorthogonal filter banks, often solved via gradient descent on the Grassmann manifold of filter coefficients. A practical implementation uses a bank of candidate wavelet packets and selects the best basis using the Shannon entropy cost function (the best‑basis algorithm of Coifman and Wickerhauser). When the signal statistics vary, the best basis can be updated online by monitoring the entropy of detail coefficients in a sliding window.

Applications include time‑frequency masking for speech enhancement, where a time‑varying Wiener filter is implemented in the adaptive wavelet domain to track non‑stationary noise. In radar, adaptive wavelets match the chirp rate of a moving target, improving detection in clutter. The theoretical analysis of time‑varying filters often employs the evolutionary spectrum (Priestley) defined as S(ω,t) = |H(ω,t)|² Sₓ(ω) where H(ω,t) is the time‑varying transfer function. The Wigner‑Ville distribution of the filter’s impulse response provides a joint time‑frequency representation that satisfies the marginal properties. Examiners may ask to derive the Kalman filter equations for a basis‑expansion time‑varying filter, or to explain why the lifting scheme preserves perfect reconstruction despite time‑varying prediction filters (the answer: the update step is the exact inverse of the prediction step in the polyphase domain). Another common trap is to assume that adaptive wavelet packets remain orthogonal; in general, adaptivity breaks orthogonality, and the transform becomes a frame with bounded redundancy.

Recent work combines adaptive wavelets with deep unfolding: each lifting step is replaced by a small neural network whose parameters are learned end‑to‑end for a specific task (e.g., image compression). This hybrid model retains the interpretability of the lifting structure while gaining the representation power of deep networks. Understanding the classical adaptive filter theory, the lifting construction, and the modern learning‑augmented variants equips candidates to address both analytical derivations and system‑design questions.

Distributed Signal Processing in Sensor Networks

Wireless sensor networks (WSNs) consist of spatially distributed nodes that acquire, process, and communicate data under severe energy and bandwidth constraints. Distributed signal processing aims to perform inference tasks — detection, estimation, classification — without centralizing all raw measurements. The canonical problem is distributed estimation of a parameter θ from observations yᵢ = hᵢ(θ) + nᵢ at node i. If each node transmits a quantized version of its local sufficient statistic, the fusion center can achieve the centralized Cramér‑Rao bound asymptotically. However, quantization introduces a rate‑distortion trade‑off: the optimal quantizer for a given bit budget is the Lloyd‑Max quantizer applied to the local likelihood ratio. When communication is peer‑to‑peer (no fusion center), consensus algorithms enable nodes to agree on a global estimate. The average consensus iteration xᵢ[k+1] = xᵢ[k] + ε Σⱼ∈Nᵢ (xⱼ[k] − xᵢ[k]) converges to the network average if the graph is connected and the step size ε < 2/λₘₐₓ(L) where L is the Laplacian matrix. For time‑varying graphs, the condition becomes joint connectivity over a bounded interval.

Distributed detection follows a similar pattern: each node computes a local log‑likelihood ratio (LLR) and transmits a quantized version. The fusion center sums the received LLRs (or applies the Chair‑Varshney rule for optimal fusion of binary decisions). The error exponent of the distributed detector is governed by the Kullback‑Leibler divergences of the local densities and the quantization rates. In the presence of Byzantine nodes that send falsified data, robust consensus algorithms such as the trimmed‑mean or median‑based consensus can tolerate up to ⌊(d−1)/2⌋ malicious neighbors where d is the node degree. This resilience property is a frequent exam topic: candidates must state the maximum fraction of adversaries that can be tolerated and explain why the median is robust while the mean is not.

Distributed compressed sensing exploits joint sparsity across nodes. The joint sparsity model (JSM‑1) assumes a common sparse component plus node‑specific innovations. The distributed basis pursuit (DBP) algorithm solves a global ℓ₁ minimization by alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), where each node updates its local variable using only neighbor communication. Convergence to the centralized solution is guaranteed under standard ADMM assumptions (convexity, closed‑form proximal operators). The communication cost per iteration is proportional to the number of edges times the dimension of the variable; quantization of messages introduces a bias that can be corrected by dithered quantization. Another approach is the diffusion LMS, where each node performs a local LMS update followed by a weighted combination with neighbors’ estimates. The diffusion strategy (combine‑then‑adapt or adapt‑then‑combine) yields superior stability and steady‑state performance compared to consensus‑based LMS, especially for non‑uniform step sizes across nodes.

Energy‑aware design is critical: the radio transceiver dominates power consumption. Techniques such as compressive data gathering (nodes transmit random projections of their data) reduce the number of transmitted bits. The sink reconstructs the field using a sparsifying basis (e.g., 2‑D DCT for smooth temperature fields). The restricted isometry property of the random projection matrix ensures accurate recovery with high probability if the number of measurements M ≥ C K log(N/K) where K is the sparsity level. Examiners often ask to derive the consensus convergence condition from the eigenvalues of the weight matrix, or to compare the communication overhead of ADMM versus diffusion LMS for a given network topology. A subtle point is that diffusion LMS does not require a doubly‑stochastic weight matrix; left‑stochastic weights suffice for mean convergence, while right‑stochastic weights are needed for mean‑square stability. Understanding these nuances, together with the ability to formulate distributed estimation as a convex optimization problem, is essential for advanced signal‑processing examinations.

  • Confusing the Landau density condition with the Nyquist rate; Landau governs average density for non‑uniform sampling, Nyquist governs uniform sampling.
  • Assuming the bispectrum of a linear Gaussian process is non‑zero; it is identically zero because third‑order cumulants vanish.
  • Treating the ARD prior in SBL as equivalent to an ℓ₁ penalty; ARD yields a Student‑t marginal prior with heavier tails, leading to different sparsity patterns.
  • Believing adaptive wavelet packets remain orthogonal after signal‑dependent prediction; adaptivity generally breaks orthogonality, producing a frame.
  • Using the mean consensus rule in the presence of Byzantine nodes; the mean is not robust, median or trimmed‑mean consensus is required.
  • Neglecting the step‑size bound ε < 2/λₘₐₓ(L) for average consensus; violating it causes divergence even on a connected graph.
  • Assuming diffusion LMS requires doubly‑stochastic weights for mean convergence; left‑stochastic weights are sufficient for mean convergence.

Graph Signal Processing and Spectral Graph Theory

Graph signal processing extends classical discrete‑time signal analysis to data defined on the vertices of an arbitrary graph. The graph Laplacian L = D − A (degree matrix minus adjacency matrix) plays the role of the second‑difference operator; its eigendecomposition L = U Λ Uᵀ provides a graph Fourier basis. A signal x ∈ ℝᴺ on the graph is transformed by x̂ = Uᵀ x, and the inverse transform is x = U x̂. The eigenvalues λₖ of L order graph frequencies from smooth (λ≈0) to highly oscillatory (λ large). Filtering on graphs is defined as g(L) x = U g(Λ) Uᵀ x, where g(·) is a spectral response. Polynomial approximations (Chebyshev, Lanczos) enable localized filtering without explicit eigendecomposition, yielding O(|E|K) complexity for a K‑order polynomial. Sampling theory on graphs states that a bandlimited signal (support on the first K eigenvectors) can be perfectly recovered from its values on a uniqueness set of vertices if the sampling set satisfies a graph‑specific Nyquist condition: the submatrix of U restricted to the sampled vertices must have full column rank. Reconstruction solves a least‑squares problem min ‖Uₛ α − y‖₂ where Uₛ contains the sampled rows. Applications include sensor‑network interpolation, semi‑supervised learning on manifolds, and brain‑connectivity analysis. The main computational challenge is scaling eigendecomposition to large graphs; randomized subspace iteration and spectral sparsification provide practical approximations.

Tensor Decompositions for Multidimensional Signal Analysis

Many modern signals are naturally multidimensional — hyperspectral images, multi‑channel EEG, MIMO channel tensors — and cannot be fully captured by matrix factorizations. A tensor 𝒳 ∈ ℝᴵ¹ˣᴵ²ˣ…ˣᴵᴺ admits several low‑rank models. The CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) decomposition expresses 𝒳 as a sum of R rank‑one outer products: 𝒳 ≈ Σᵣ₌₁ᴿ aᵣ⁽¹⁾ ∘ aᵣ⁽²⁾ ∘ … ∘ aᵣ⁽ᴺ⁾. CP rank is the minimal R; uniqueness holds under Kruskal’s condition k_A + k_B + k_C ≥ 2R + 2 for three‑way tensors. The Tucker decomposition writes 𝒳 ≈ 𝒢 ×₁ U⁽¹⁾ ×₂ U⁽²⁾ … ×ₙ U⁽ᴺ⁾ with a core tensor 𝒢 and factor matrices U⁽ⁿ⁾. The multilinear rank (R₁,…,Rₙ) is the tuple of mode‑n ranks. Higher‑order SVD (HOSVD) computes an orthogonal Tucker model by truncating the SVD of each mode‑n unfolding. Tensor train (TT) and hierarchical Tucker (HT) formats provide linear‑in‑N storage for very high‑order tensors, enabling scalable algorithms for tensor completion and regression. Optimization typically uses alternating least squares (ALS) or gradient‑based methods on the manifold of fixed multilinear rank. Regularization (ℓ₁ on factors, nuclear norm on unfoldings) promotes sparsity and low rank simultaneously. In compressed sensing, a tensor‑structured measurement operator 𝒜(𝒳) = y can be inverted via convex relaxations such as the sum‑of‑nuclear‑norms (SNN) or the tensor nuclear norm based on the t‑product for third‑order tensors. These tools are essential for joint sparsity exploitation in multi‑antenna communications, chemometrics, and video background subtraction.

Worked Example: CP Uniqueness Check
A three‑way tensor of size 5×6×7 is modeled with CP rank R = 4. The factor matrices A (5×4), B (6×4), C (7×4) have Kruskal ranks k_A = 4, k_B = 3, k_C = 4. Determine whether the CP decomposition is essentially unique.
Kruskal’s condition for a three‑way tensor requires k_A + k_B + k_C ≥ 2R + 2. Here k_A + k_B + k_C = 4 + 3 + 4 = 11. The right‑hand side is 2·4 + 2 = 10. Since 11 ≥ 10, the condition holds, therefore the CP decomposition is essentially unique up to permutation and scaling of the rank‑one components.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a uniqueness test, write down Kruskal’s inequality explicitly and compute each Kruskal rank from the given factor‑matrix dimensions. Remember that the Kruskal rank of a matrix cannot exceed its column rank; if a factor matrix is tall and full column rank, its Kruskal rank equals the number of columns. This quick check often earns full marks without needing to derive the full CP model.

Blind Source Separation and Independent Component Analysis

Blind source separation (BSS) recovers statistically independent source signals s(t) ∈ ℝᴹ from observed mixtures x(t) = A s(t) where A ∈ ℝᴺˣᴹ is an unknown full‑rank mixing matrix (N ≥ M). Independent component analysis (ICA) assumes sources are non‑Gaussian and mutually independent. The central limit theorem guarantees that any linear mixture of independent non‑Gaussian variables is “more Gaussian” than the individual sources; thus maximizing non‑Gaussianity of the estimated components yields the demixing matrix W ≈ A⁻¹. Common contrast functions are negentropy approximations (e.g., J(y) ≈ [E{G(y)} − E{G(ν)}]² with G(u)=log cosh(u) or G(u)=−exp(−u²/2)) and kurtosis kurt(y)=E{y⁴}−3(E{y²})². The FastICA fixed‑point iteration for a single component is w ← E{x g(wᵀ x)} − E{g′(wᵀ x)} w followed by symmetric orthogonalization W ← (W Wᵀ)−½ W. For convolutive mixtures x(t) = Σₖ Aₖ s(t−k), frequency‑domain ICA applies the instantaneous model to each DFT bin, then solves the permutation and scaling ambiguities across bins using correlation of envelope spectra or joint diagonalization of cross‑spectral matrices. Identifiability requires at most one Gaussian source; if more than one source is Gaussian, the mixing matrix can be recovered only up to an orthogonal transformation within the Gaussian subspace. Overdetermined cases (N > M) benefit from a pre‑whitening step x̃ = V x where V = Λ−½ Uᵀ from the eigen‑decomposition of the covariance E{x xᵀ} = U Λ Uᵀ. Performance bounds are given by the Cramér‑Rao lower bound for the demixing matrix, which depends on the source score functions. Extensions include independent vector analysis (IVA) for joint separation of multiple datasets sharing a common mixing structure, and non‑negative matrix factorization (NMF) for parts‑based representations when sources are known to be non‑negative.

Array Processing: Beamforming and Direction‑of‑Arrival Estimation

Array processing treats signals received at a spatially distributed set of sensors as a snapshot vector x(t) ∈ ℂᴺ. For a narrowband far‑field source at direction θ, the steering vector is a(θ) = [1, e−j 2π d sinθ/λ, …, e−j 2π (N−1) d sinθ/λ]ᵀ for a uniform linear array (ULA) with inter‑element spacing d. The received covariance matrix R = E{x xᴴ} = A(Θ) P A(Θ)ᴴ + σ² I where A(Θ) collects steering vectors of K sources, P is the source power diagonal matrix, and σ² is noise power. Conventional beamforming (delay‑and‑sum) uses the weight vector w = a(θ₀)/N to steer a mainlobe toward θ₀; its output power is wᴴ R w. Minimum variance distortionless response (MVDR) beamformer solves min wᴴ R w s.t. wᴴ a(θ₀)=1, yielding w = R⁻¹ a(θ₀) / (a(θ₀)ᴴ R⁻¹ a(θ₀)). MVDR provides optimal interference rejection but requires accurate covariance estimation and is sensitive to steering‑vector mismatch; diagonal loading R ← R + γ I improves robustness. Subspace methods exploit the eigendecomposition R = Uₛ Λₛ Uₛᴴ + Uₙ Λₙ Uₙᴴ. MUSIC forms the pseudospectrum P_MUSIC(θ) = 1 / (a(θ)ᴴ Uₙ Uₙᴴ a(θ)); peaks correspond to source directions. ESPRIT uses the rotational invariance of the signal subspace for ULAs: Uₛ₁ = Uₛ₂ Φ where Φ contains the phase shifts ej 2π d sinθ_k/λ. Eigenvalues of Φ give DOAs directly without spectral search. For coherent sources (multipath), spatial smoothing partitions the array into overlapping subarrays, averages their covariances, and restores rank. Wideband sources are handled by incoherent MUSIC (averaging narrowband MUSIC spectra) or coherent signal subspace (CSS) methods that focus each frequency to a reference frequency using a focusing matrix. The Cramér‑Rao bound for DOA estimation shows variance proportional to 1/(SNR·N·T·(2π d/λ)²) for a single source, highlighting the roles of array aperture, snapshot count T, and wavelength.

Randomized Algorithms for Large‑Scale Signal Processing

Modern datasets often exceed the memory and compute budget of deterministic factorizations. Randomized linear algebra provides probabilistic approximations with rigorous error bounds. For a matrix A ∈ ℝᵐˣⁿ (m ≥ n), a rank‑k approximation is obtained by drawing a Gaussian test matrix Ω ∈ ℝⁿˣ⁽ᵏ⁺ᵖ⁾ (oversampling p≈5–10), forming the sample matrix Y = A Ω, computing an orthonormal basis Q = orth(Y) via QR, and projecting B = Qᵀ A. The SVD of the small matrix B = Ũ Σ Vᵀ yields the approximate left singular vectors U ≈ Q Ũ. With high probability, the spectral‑norm error is bounded by a modest constant multiple of σk+1 (e.g., ‖A − Q Qᵀ A‖₂ ≤ C σk+1 with C depending on k, p, and dimensions). For low‑rank tensor decompositions, randomized range finders are applied to each mode‑n unfolding, reducing the cost of ALS updates from O(Iⁿ) to O(k Iⁿ⁻¹). In compressive sensing, a random measurement matrix Φ ∈ ℝᴹˣᴺ with i.i.d. sub‑Gaussian entries satisfies the restricted isometry property (RIP) of order K with probability ≥ 1−exp(−c M) provided M ≥ C K log(N/K). Structured random matrices (partial Fourier, subsampled Hadamard, circulant) enable fast matrix‑vector multiplies O(N log N) and are used in single‑pixel cameras and MRI. Randomized Kaczmarz and coordinate descent methods solve large linear systems A x = b by sampling rows proportionally to their squared norms; convergence rate is E[‖x_k − x*‖²] ≤ (1 − σ_min²(A)/‖A‖_F²)^k ‖x0 − x*‖². For kernel methods, the Nyström approximation samples c ≪ N columns of the kernel matrix K, forms K ≈ C W⁺ Cᵀ where C contains the sampled columns and W the intersection, yielding O(N c²) storage and O(N c) matrix‑vector products. These techniques are now standard in large‑scale PCA, recommender systems, and real‑time radar signal processing where data rates are extremely high.

  • Neglecting the permutation and scaling ambiguities in frequency‑domain ICA; they must be resolved before inverse transforming to time domain.
  • Using the conventional beamformer weight vector for MVDR without enforcing the distortionless constraint; the resulting pattern does not guarantee unity gain at the look direction.
  • Believing that diagonal loading alone guarantees robustness against all steering‑vector errors; excessive loading degrades resolution and can mask weak sources.
  • Applying deterministic SVD to a matrix that is too large for memory; randomized range finders with a modest oversampling parameter give near‑optimal approximations at a fraction of the cost.
  • Overlooking the requirement that the test matrix Ω in randomized SVD be drawn from a distribution with zero mean and unit variance; deterministic Ω can lead to catastrophic failure for certain spectra.

Quantum Signal Processing and the Quantum Fourier Transform

Quantum signal processing (QSP) extends classical linear‑system theory to the Hilbert space of qubits, where unitary operators replace transfer functions and measurement collapses the state to a classical outcome. The cornerstone of QSP is the quantum Fourier transform (QFT), a unitary that maps the computational basis |x⟩ to the superposition 1/√N Σy=0N‑1 e2πi xy/N |y⟩. Unlike the classical FFT, which requires O(N log N) arithmetic operations, the QFT on n qubits (N = 2n) is implemented with O(n²) elementary gates — Hadamard and controlled‑phase rotations — giving an exponential speed‑up for period‑finding subroutines such as Shor’s factoring algorithm. In a signal‑processing context, the QFT enables quantum spectral estimation: a quantum state encoding a discrete‑time sequence can be transformed, and the resulting amplitudes sampled to obtain the power spectrum with a number of measurements that scales polynomially in the desired precision. Quantum singular‑value transformation (QSVT) provides a unified framework for quantum filtering, Hamiltonian simulation, and principal‑component analysis by applying any polynomial of a block‑encoded matrix coherently. Error‑corrected implementations are not yet realized; near‑term devices explore variational quantum circuits that approximate the QFT with parameterised rotations, trading depth for noise resilience. The QFT is exactly unitary, so no quantisation noise is introduced, but read‑out shot noise and decoherence impose a practical signal‑to‑noise ratio that must be analysed with quantum‑channel models rather than classical additive‑white‑Gaussian‑noise assumptions.

Deep Learning for Signal Processing: Neural Operators and Physics‑Informed Networks

Traditional deep learning treats signals as fixed‑size tensors, forcing a discretisation that discards the underlying continuous‑time or continuous‑space structure. Neural operators — notably the Fourier neural operator (FNO) and the DeepONet — learn mappings between function spaces directly. An FNO lifts the input to a higher‑dimensional channel, applies a spectral convolution in the Fourier domain (multiplication by a learned weight tensor), and projects back, achieving resolution‑invariant inference: the same trained model evaluates the solution operator on any grid. Physics‑informed neural networks (PINNs) embed differential‑equation residuals into the loss, e.g. L = Ldata + λ LPDE where LPDE = ‖∂tu + ℒ(u)‖² for a linear operator ℒ. This regularises the solution manifold, allowing accurate reconstruction from sparse, noisy measurements — a regime common in compressive sensing and magnetic‑resonance imaging. Hybrid architectures combine neural operators with classical model‑based layers: a learned proximal operator inside an ADMM loop, or a differentiable Kalman filter whose process‑noise covariance is output by a recurrent network. Training stability hinges on spectral normalisation of the Fourier weights and on curriculum learning that gradually increases the PDE residual weight λ. FNOs have demonstrated relative L² errors below 1 % on certain Navier‑Stokes benchmarks with ~10⁴ training samples, and PINNs have reduced sensor requirements significantly in seismic tomography studies. The examiner expects you to recognise that neural operators approximate the Green’s function of the underlying PDE, and that physics‑informed losses convert an ill‑posed inverse problem into a well‑posed optimisation over a hypothesis class with built‑in physical priors.

Worked Example: Quantum Period Finding vs Classical FFT
A discrete‑time signal x[n] of length N = 1024 contains a single sinusoid of unknown frequency. Explain why a quantum algorithm using the QFT can determine the frequency with O(log N) queries, whereas the classical FFT requires O(N log N) operations. Quantify the query complexity assuming an oracle that prepares the state Σn x[n]|n⟩.
The oracle prepares the normalized state |ψ⟩ = (1/√E) Σn=0N‑1 x[n]|n⟩ where E = Σ|x[n]|². Applying the QFT yields |ψ̂⟩ = (1/√E) Σk X[k]|k⟩ with X[k] the DFT coefficients. Because the signal is a single tone, only two DFT bins (positive and negative frequency) have non‑zero amplitude. Measuring |ψ̂⟩ in the computational basis returns the index k of a non‑zero bin with probability |X[k]|²/E. Repeating the preparation‑QFT‑measurement cycle O(1) times yields the frequency index with constant success probability. Each cycle uses one oracle call and O((log N)²) gates for the QFT, so the total query complexity is O(1) and gate complexity O((log N)²). The classical FFT must compute all N DFT coefficients, costing Θ(N log N) arithmetic operations, and cannot exploit the sparsity without additional compressive‑sensing machinery. Thus the quantum routine achieves an exponential reduction in query complexity at the cost of requiring a coherent quantum oracle and fault‑tolerant hardware.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question contrasts quantum and classical spectral methods, first identify the oracle model: does the problem assume a state‑preparation oracle or only classical sample access? If only classical samples are given, quantum speed‑up does not apply. Next, check whether the signal is exactly sparse in the Fourier basis; quantum period finding needs exact sparsity, while variational quantum algorithms tolerate approximate sparsity but lose the provable O(log N) guarantee. Finally, remember that measurement collapses the state — you cannot read out the full spectrum, only sample from it. Mentioning these caveats distinguishes a top‑tier answer from a textbook recitation.

Nonlinear Signal Processing: Volterra Series and Higher‑Order Spectra

Linear time‑invariant (LTI) systems are fully described by their impulse response or frequency response. Many real‑world systems — power amplifiers, biological neurons, acoustic channels — exhibit memory‑dependent nonlinearities that LTI models cannot capture. The Volterra series generalises the convolution integral: y(t) = Σp=1∞ ∫…∫ hp(τ₁,…,τp) Πi=1p x(t‑τi) dτ₁…dτp, where hp are the p‑th order Volterra kernels. In the frequency domain, the p‑th order kernel maps to a p‑dimensional transfer function Hp(ω₁,…,ωp) and the output spectrum involves convolutions of the input spectrum with itself. Higher‑order spectra (polyspectra) such as the bispectrum B(ω₁,ω₂) = E[X(ω₁)X(ω₂)X*(ω₁+ω₂)] and the trispectrum capture phase coupling that the power spectrum discards. For a quadratic system, the output bispectrum is directly proportional to the product of the second‑order kernel and the input bispectrum, enabling blind identification of the kernel from output‑only measurements if the input is non‑Gaussian. Estimation of polyspectra requires averaging over many segments to reduce variance; the direct (FFT‑based) estimator has variance that decays as 1/L where L is the number of averages, but bias arises from finite‑window leakage. Parametric approaches model the Volterra kernels with a finite basis (e.g., Laguerre functions) and estimate coefficients via least squares, dramatically reducing the number of parameters from O(Np) to O(Mp) with M ≪ N. In practice, third‑order Volterra models are common for RF power‑amplifier predistortion; the coefficients are adapted in real time using a memory‑polynomial structure that is a diagonal slice of the full Volterra kernel, offering a tractable trade‑off between linearisation performance and computational load.

Signal Processing on Manifolds and Riemannian Geometry

Classical signal processing assumes data live in Euclidean space ℝn. Modern datasets — diffusion‑tensor images, covariance matrices, rotation sequences — reside on curved manifolds such as the symmetric positive‑definite (SPD) cone, the Stiefel manifold of orthonormal frames, or the Grassmannian of subspaces. Riemannian geometry provides the tools to define means, distances, and filters intrinsically. The Riemannian distance on the SPD manifold is dR(A,B) = ‖log(A⁻¹ᐟ² B A⁻¹ᐟ²)‖F, where log is the matrix logarithm and ‖·‖F the Frobenius norm. The Karcher (Riemannian) mean of a set {Ai} is the unique minimiser of Σ dR²(A, Ai), computable by a gradient descent on the manifold. Filtering on manifolds replaces linear averaging with weighted Karcher means along a temporal trajectory, yielding a Riemannian exponential moving average: μt = Expμt‑1(α Logμt‑1(Xt)), where Exp and Log are the exponential and logarithmic maps. For the SPD cone, ExpP(V) = P¹ᐟ² exp(P⁻¹ᐟ² V P⁻¹ᐟ²) P¹ᐟ² and LogP(Q) = P¹ᐟ² log(P⁻¹ᐟ² Q P⁻¹ᐟ²) P¹ᐟ². This filter preserves positive‑definiteness and respects the intrinsic curvature, unlike Euclidean averaging which can produce non‑physical matrices. In brain‑computer interfaces, Riemannian classifiers based on tangent‑space mapping (Logμ(X) projected onto the tangent space at the global mean μ) have shown competitive accuracy with fewer training samples than deep networks in several studies. The examiner often tests the distinction between the affine‑invariant metric (above) and the log‑Euclidean metric dLE(A,B) = ‖log A − log B‖F, which is computationally cheaper but not invariant under congruence transformations.

Real‑Time Implementation on FPGA and ASIC: Fixed‑Point Effects and Pipeline Design

Translating a floating‑point signal‑processing algorithm to hardware requires a systematic quantisation analysis. Fixed‑point representation uses a word length W and a fractional length F, giving a dynamic range of ±2W‑F‑1 and a quantisation step Δ = 2‑F. The signal‑to‑quantisation‑noise ratio (SQNR) for a full‑scale sinusoid is approximately 6.02 F + 1.76 dB. In a pipelined FIR filter of length L, each multiply‑accumulate (MAC) stage adds rounding noise; the total output noise power is L Δ²/12 assuming independent rounding errors. To meet a target SQNR, the designer allocates extra guard bits (typically 2–4) to the accumulator width, then truncates back to the output word length with convergent rounding to avoid limit cycles. IIR filters are more delicate: pole quantisation can move poles outside the unit circle, causing instability. A common remedy is to implement the filter in a cascade of second‑order sections (biquads) with coefficient quantisation performed after scaling each section to minimise internal overflow. Pipeline depth is chosen to meet the clock‑frequency target; each stage registers its inputs and outputs, breaking long combinatorial paths. For a 500 MHz clock on a modern FPGA, a 64‑tap FIR can be mapped to a systolic array of 64 DSP slices with a single‑cycle latency per tap, yielding a total latency of 64 cycles. Resource sharing (time‑multiplexing DSP slices) reduces slice count at the expense of higher latency and more complex control logic. Power‑of‑two coefficient scaling enables shift‑instead‑of‑multiply, saving DSP resources. Verification uses a bit‑accurate C/C++ model (e.g., SystemC) co‑simulated with the RTL to catch overflow, limit‑cycle, and latency mismatches before silicon tape‑out.

  • Assuming the quantum Fourier transform provides a speed‑up for any spectral estimation task; it only helps when a coherent state‑preparation oracle exists and the spectrum is exactly sparse.
  • Treating neural operators as black‑box universal approximators without acknowledging their reliance on spectral convolution and the need for physics‑informed regularisation to generalise from sparse data.
  • Confusing the bispectrum with the power spectrum; the bispectrum retains phase information and vanishes for Gaussian processes, whereas the power spectrum does not.
  • Applying Euclidean averaging to SPD matrices and expecting a valid covariance estimate; the result can lose positive‑definiteness and violate the manifold geometry.
  • Neglecting guard bits in fixed‑point accumulator design, leading to overflow‑induced limit cycles that are invisible in floating‑point simulation.
  • Believing that coefficient quantisation in an IIR filter can be performed independently per biquad without re‑checking global stability; pole interaction across sections can still cause instability.
  • Overlooking the requirement that Riemannian exponential and logarithmic maps be computed via matrix functions (exp, log) which themselves need careful numerical implementation on hardware.

These advanced topics complete the signal‑processing toolkit required for high‑level competitive examinations.

Cyclostationary Signal Processing and Spectral Correlation

Many communication, radar, and mechanical signals exhibit statistical properties that vary periodically with time; such signals are cyclostationary rather than stationary. A signal x(t) is wide‑sense cyclostationary if its mean and autocorrelation satisfy E[x(t)] = E[x(t+T₀)] and Rₓ(t, τ) = Rₓ(t+T₀, τ) for some period T₀. The cyclic autocorrelation function is defined as Rₓ^α(τ) = limT→∞ (1/T) ∫-T/2T/2 x(t+τ/2) x*(t-τ/2) e-j2παt dt, where α = k/T₀ are the cycle frequencies. Its Fourier transform, the spectral correlation density Sₓ^α(f), generalises the power spectral density (which corresponds to α = 0). For a BPSK signal with symbol rate 1/Tₛ and carrier f_c, non‑zero cycle frequencies appear at α = k/Tₛ and α = 2f_c + k/Tₛ, enabling blind modulation classification and symbol‑rate estimation without prior synchronisation. The frequency‑smoothing method (FSM) and time‑smoothing method (TSM) are two practical estimators: FSM computes Sₓ^α(f) ≈ (1/N) X_T(f+α/2) X_T*(f-α/2) where X_T is the short‑time Fourier transform, while TSM averages cyclic periodograms over time blocks. Cyclostationary detectors exploit the fact that noise is stationary (α = 0 only), so energy at α ≠ 0 is a signature of man‑made signals even below the noise floor. In spectrum sensing for cognitive radio, the test statistic Λ = ∑α≠0 ∫ |Sₓ^α(f)|² df / σ⁴ achieves constant‑false‑alarm‑rate performance without noise‑variance knowledge. The examiner often asks for the cycle frequencies of common modulations: AM has α = ±2f_c, ±f_c ± 1/Tₛ; QPSK has α = k/Tₛ (no 2f_c component due to balanced constellation); OFDM exhibits cycle frequencies at subcarrier spacing and symbol rate. A key distinction is between second‑order cyclostationarity (autocorrelation‑based) and higher‑order cyclostationarity (cumulant‑based), the latter being necessary for signals like GMSK where second‑order cyclic features are weak.

Sparse Representation, Dictionary Learning, and the K‑SVD Algorithm

Beyond the fixed bases of Fourier or wavelet transforms, sparse representation seeks an overcomplete dictionary D ∈ ℝn×K (K > n) such that each signal x ∈ ℝⁿ admits x ≈ Dα with ‖α‖₀ ≤ s ≪ n. The synthesis model minimises ‖x - Dα‖₂² subject to ‖α‖₀ ≤ s; the analysis model uses a transform Ω where Ωx is sparse. Dictionary learning alternates between sparse coding (fix D, find α) and dictionary update (fix α, find D). The K‑SVD algorithm (Aharon, Elad, Bruckstein, 2006) generalises K‑means: for each atom d_k, define the group of signals using it, ω_k = {i | α_k(i) ≠ 0}. Compute the representation error matrix E_k = X - ∑j≠k d_j α_j^T restricted to ω_k, then update d_k and the corresponding coefficients via the rank‑1 SVD of E_k: E_k = UΔV^T, set d_k = u₁, and α_k(ω_k) = δ₁ v₁^T. This guarantees non‑increasing Frobenius error. For image denoising, patches of size 8×8 (n=64) are extracted, a dictionary of K=256 atoms is learned from clean or noisy patches, and each patch is reconstructed via OMP with s=3–5 non‑zeros; the global image is obtained by averaging overlapping patches. The learned dictionary adapts to image structures (edges, textures) and typically outperforms fixed DCT or wavelet bases. In classification, a class‑specific dictionary D_c is learned per class; a test signal x is assigned to argmin_c ‖x - D_c α_c‖₂² with sparse coding per class. The examiner tests the difference between synthesis and analysis sparsity: synthesis uses D, analysis uses Ω where ΩD ≈ I; analysis is more robust to noise but harder to learn. Another trap is assuming K‑SVD converges globally; it converges to a local minimum, and initialisation (e.g., DCT or random patches) affects results. The mutual coherence μ(D) = maxi≠j |d_i^T d_j| governs sparse recovery guarantees: if s < (1 + 1/μ)/2, OMP recovers the sparsest representation. For learned dictionaries, μ is typically 0.2–0.4, allowing sparsity levels of 2–3 for n=64.

Worked Problem: Cycle Frequencies of a QAM Signal
A 16‑QAM signal uses a square‑root raised‑cosine pulse with roll‑off β = 0.35 and symbol period Tₛ = 1 μs. The carrier frequency is f_c = 2.4 GHz. List all non‑conjugate cycle frequencies α in the range |α| ≤ 2/Tₛ and all conjugate cycle frequencies in the same range. Explain why the conjugate cycle frequency at 2f_c is absent for 16‑QAM but present for BPSK.
For a linearly modulated signal x(t) = ∑ a_n g(t - nTₛ) ej2πf_c t with i.i.d. symbols a_n, the non‑conjugate cyclic autocorrelation has cycle frequencies α = k/Tₛ (k ∈ ℤ). The conjugate cyclic autocorrelation has cycle frequencies α = 2f_c + k/Tₛ. The pulse spectrum G(f) is bandlimited to (1+β)/(2Tₛ) = 0.675 MHz. Non‑conjugate cycle frequencies with |α| ≤ 2/Tₛ = 2 MHz: α = 0, ±1/Tₛ = ±1 MHz. (α = ±2/Tₛ = ±2 MHz is at the boundary; the cyclic spectrum at α = ±2/Tₛ is zero because the pulse spectrum does not overlap with itself shifted by 2/Tₛ for β < 1.) Conjugate cycle frequencies with |α| ≤ 2 MHz: 2f_c = 4.8 GHz is far outside the range, so none appear. However, if the question intends baseband equivalent (f_c = 0), then conjugate cycle frequencies are α = k/Tₛ. For 16‑QAM, the constellation has zero mean and symmetric real/imaginary parts, so E[a_n²] = 0. The conjugate cyclic autocorrelation is proportional to E[a_n²]; hence it vanishes for all k. For BPSK, a_n ∈ {±1}, so E[a_n²] = 1 ≠ 0, giving a strong conjugate cycle frequency at 2f_c (or 0 in baseband). This distinction is exploited in modulation classification: presence of conjugate cyclostationarity at 2f_c indicates BPSK or ASK; absence indicates QPSK, QAM, or PSK with symmetric constellations.

Optimal Transport Theory for Signal Alignment and Distribution Matching

Optimal transport (OT) provides a geometric framework for comparing and transforming probability distributions, with direct applications in signal alignment, domain adaptation, and generative modelling. Given two discrete distributions μ = ∑i=1^m a_i δx_i and ν = ∑j=1^n b_j δy_j with a ∈ Δ_m, b ∈ Δ_n, the Kantorovich formulation seeks a coupling matrix P ∈ ℝ₊m×n minimising ⟨P, C⟩_F subject to P1 = a, P^T1 = b, where Cij = c(x_i, y_j) is the cost matrix (typically squared Euclidean distance). The optimal value is the Wasserstein distance W_p(μ, ν) = (min_P ⟨P, C⟩)1/p. For p=2 and Gaussian distributions μ = N(m₁, Σ₁), ν = N(m₂, Σ₂), the squared Wasserstein distance has a closed form: W₂² = ‖m₁ - m₂‖₂² + Tr(Σ₁ + Σ₂ - 2(Σ₁1/2 Σ₂ Σ₁1/2)1/2). In signal processing, OT aligns time series by treating them as distributions over time‑value pairs (t, x(t)). Dynamic time warping (DTW) is a special case of OT with a constrained transport plan (monotone coupling). The entropic regularisation min_P ⟨P, C⟩ - ε H(P) (H = -∑ Pij log Pij) yields the Sinkhorn algorithm: P = diag(u) K diag(v) with K = exp(-C/ε), solved by alternating scaling u ← a ./ (K v), v ← b ./ (K^T u). This runs in O(mn) per iteration and is GPU‑friendly. For domain adaptation in audio classification, source and target spectrogram distributions are matched by learning a transport map T that pushes source samples to target: min_T W₂²(T#μ_s, μ_t) + λ R(T). The Brenier theorem guarantees T = ∇φ for a convex potential φ when μ_s is absolutely continuous. In generative modelling, Wasserstein GANs minimise W₁(μ_data, μ_gen) via the Kantorovich‑Rubinstein duality: W₁ = sup‖f‖_L ≤ 1 Ex∼μ_data[f(x)] - Ex∼μ_gen[f(x)], where the critic f is a 1‑Lipschitz network. The examiner tests the difference between W₁ (Earth Mover's Distance) and W₂: W₁ is a metric on probability measures with finite first moment, W₂ requires finite second moment and induces a Riemannian structure on the Wasserstein space. A common trap is confusing the Sinkhorn divergence S_ε(μ, ν) = OT_ε(μ, ν) - ½ OT_ε(μ, μ) - ½ OT_ε(ν, ν) with the true Wasserstein distance; S_ε is biased but positive definite and differentiable, making it suitable for gradient‑based learning.

Phase Retrieval Algorithms: Gerchberg–Saxton, Fienup, and Convex Relaxations

Phase retrieval recovers a signal x ∈ ℂⁿ from magnitude‑only measurements y = |Ax|, where A ∈ ℂm×n is a known measurement matrix (e.g., Fourier, coded diffraction, or random Gaussian). The problem is non‑convex due to the global phase ambiguity: x and ejθx yield identical measurements. The Gerchberg–Saxton (GS) algorithm (1972) alternates projections between the object domain (support constraint) and Fourier domain (magnitude constraint): xk+1 = P_S F-1 [ y ⊙ exp(j arg(F x_k)) ], where P_S enforces known support S. Fienup’s hybrid input‑output (HIO) algorithm (1982) improves convergence by using negative feedback outside the support: xk+1(r) = { (F-1[y ⊙ exp(j arg(F x_k))])(r) for r ∈ S; x_k(r) - β (F-1[y ⊙ exp(j arg(F x_k))])(r) for r ∉ S }, with β ∈ [0.5, 1]. For oversampled Fourier measurements (m ≥ 4n), GS/HIO often converge to the true solution up to global phase, but stagnation at local minima is common. Convex relaxation via PhaseLift (Candès, Strohmer, Voroninski, 2013) lifts the problem to rank‑1 matrix recovery: y_i = |a_i^H x|² = Tr(a_i a_i^H X) with X = xx^H. The nuclear‑norm minimisation min ‖X‖_* s.t. Tr(A_i X) = y_i, X ⪰ 0 recovers X exactly with high probability when m ≥ C n log n for random Gaussian A_i. PhaseCut (Waldspurger, d’Aspremont, Mallat, 2015) reformulates as maxu∈ℂⁿ, |u_i|=1 u^H M u where Mij = ∑_k y_k aki akj^*, then relaxes to a semidefinite program. The Wirtinger Flow algorithm (Candès, Li, Soltanolkotabi, 2015) uses a spectral initialisation followed by gradient descent on the non‑convex loss f(z) = ¼ ∑ (|a_i^H z|² - y_i)², achieving linear convergence with m ≳ n log n measurements. In X‑ray crystallography and ptychography, the measurement matrix corresponds to far‑field diffraction patterns; the support constraint is the finite object extent. The examiner tests the measurement diversity requirement: a single Fourier magnitude is insufficient (phase problem); multiple coded diffraction patterns (random phase masks) or ptychographic scans (overlapping probes) provide the necessary diversity. A trap is assuming GS converges globally; it only converges to a fixed point, which may be a local minimum. The number of measurements for unique recovery (up to global phase) is at least 4n-4 for generic signals (Balan, Casazza, Edidin, 2006).

Neuromorphic Signal Processing: Event‑Based Vision and Spiking Neural Networks

Neuromorphic signal processing departs from frame‑based synchronous sampling by encoding information in the precise timing of sparse, asynchronous events. An event camera (e.g., DVS, DAVIS) outputs a stream of events e_k = (x_k, y_k, t_k, p_k) where p_k ∈ {+1, -1} indicates brightness increase or decrease, triggered when the logarithmic intensity change exceeds a threshold C: |log I(x,y,t) - log I(x,y,t-Δt)| > C. This yields microsecond temporal resolution, high dynamic range (often >120 dB), and low power (typically mW range) because only changing pixels transmit data. Signal processing on event streams uses time surfaces, event histograms, or spiking neural networks (SNNs). A time surface at pixel (x,y) is a decaying memory of recent events: S(x,y,t) = exp(-(t - t_last(x,y))/τ), where t_last is the timestamp of the most recent event at that pixel. Convolutional SNNs process events by integrating synaptic currents: τ_m dv/dt = -v + ∑_j w_j ∑_f κ(t - t_j^f), where κ is a postsynaptic current kernel (e.g., α‑function κ(t) = (t/τ_s) exp(1 - t/τ_s)). The neuron fires when v ≥ θ, then resets. Training uses surrogate gradients (e.g., fast sigmoid σ'(v) = 1/(1+|v|)²) to backpropagate through the non‑differentiable spike function. For optical flow estimation, the event‑based Lucas‑Kanade method minimises ∑_k (I_x u + I_y v + I_t)² where I_x, I_y are spatial gradients of the time surface and I_t is the temporal derivative. The flow (u,v) is solved per event patch using a weighted least‑squares formulation. In keyword spotting, SNNs can achieve high accuracy on benchmarks such as Google Speech Commands with significantly fewer operations than LSTMs. The examiner tests the distinction between rate coding (information in spike count over a window) and temporal coding (information in precise spike times). A common trap is applying frame‑based CNNs directly to event frames (accumulated events over Δt); this discards the fine temporal structure that gives neuromorphic sensors their advantage. Another trap: assuming SNNs are always more efficient; the energy benefit materialises primarily on neuromorphic hardware (Loihi, TrueNorth, SpiNNaker) where spike‑driven computation avoids memory‑access overhead. On GPUs, the sparse asynchronous nature of SNNs often leads to lower utilisation than dense CNNs.

  • Confusing non‑conjugate and conjugate cycle frequencies: non‑conjugate α = k/Tₛ, conjugate α = 2f_c + k/Tₛ; QAM has zero conjugate cyclostationarity because E[a_n²] = 0.
  • Assuming K‑SVD converges to the global optimum; it is a block coordinate descent that converges to a local minimum dependent on initialisation.
  • Treating the Sinkhorn divergence as an unbiased estimator of the Wasserstein distance; it is a regularised, biased but differentiable proxy.
  • Believing a single Fourier magnitude measurement suffices for phase retrieval; at least 4n-4 generic measurements (or multiple coded diffractions) are required for uniqueness.
  • Applying frame‑based CNN architectures directly to event‑camera data without leveraging the asynchronous, sparse nature of the event stream.
  • Neglecting the mutual coherence bound μ(D) in dictionary learning; if μ is high, sparse recovery guarantees fail even if the representation error is low.
  • Assuming the Gerchberg–Saxton algorithm always converges to the true signal; it often stagnates at local minima, especially for low oversampling ratios.

Structured Sparsity in Compressive Sensing: Block, Group, and Tree Models

Standard compressive sensing assumes a signal is sparse in some basis, meaning only a few coefficients are non‑zero. In many practical scenarios the non‑zero coefficients exhibit additional structure: they appear in contiguous blocks (block sparsity), belong to predefined groups (group sparsity), or follow a hierarchical tree across scales (tree sparsity). Exploiting this structure reduces the required number of measurements and improves reconstruction robustness. For block sparsity the signal x ∈ ℝⁿ is partitioned into B blocks of equal length d, and only K blocks are active. The block ℓ₂/ℓ₁ mixed norm ‖x‖₂,₁ = ∑b=1^B ‖x_b‖₂ promotes block‑wise sparsity. Recovery guarantees replace the standard restricted isometry property (RIP) with a block‑RIP: a matrix Φ satisfies block‑RIP of order K with constant δKblock if (1−δ)‖x‖₂² ≤ ‖Φx‖₂² ≤ (1+δ)‖x‖₂² for all block‑K‑sparse x. The measurement bound becomes m ≳ K d + K log(B/K), which can be significantly smaller than the unstructured bound m ≳ K log(n/K) when d ≫ 1. Group sparsity generalises block sparsity by allowing overlapping groups; the mixed norm ∑_g w_g ‖x_g‖₂ with weights w_g handles overlaps. Algorithms such as Group Lasso, Block Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (BOMP), and Proximal Gradient methods with the group‑soft‑thresholding operator solve the corresponding convex programs. Tree sparsity arises naturally in wavelet coefficients of piecewise smooth signals: if a coarse‑scale coefficient is zero, all its descendants in the wavelet tree are likely zero. The tree‑structured norm ∑λ∈T ‖xT_λ‖₂, where T_λ is the subtree rooted at λ, encodes this dependency. Model‑based compressive sensing (Baraniuk et al., 2010) provides a unified framework: given a union of subspaces model M, the measurement bound is m ≳ dim(M) + log(|M|). For tree sparsity with K non‑zero coefficients the model dimension is O(K) and the number of subtrees is polynomial, yielding m ≲ K log(n/K) with a smaller constant. Practical applications include multi‑band spectrum sensing (block sparsity across frequency bands), multi‑task learning (group sparsity across tasks), and image reconstruction with wavelet trees. The examiner often tests the distinction between the ℓ₁ norm (unstructured) and mixed norms (structured), the form of the block‑RIP condition, and the measurement scaling laws. A common trap is applying standard ℓ₁ minimisation to block‑sparse signals and claiming the same measurement bound; the bound improves only when the algorithm explicitly uses the block norm.

MIMO Radar Waveform Design and Virtual Array Processing

Multiple‑input multiple‑output (MIMO) radar transmits orthogonal or partially correlated waveforms from each transmit element, creating a virtual array whose aperture equals the convolution of the transmit and receive array geometries. With N_t transmit and N_r receive elements placed on uniform linear arrays (ULAs) with half‑wavelength spacing, the virtual array has N_t N_r elements and an effective aperture of (N_t+N_r−1)λ/2, providing a factor of N_t improvement in angular resolution over a phased‑array radar with the same total number of elements. Waveform orthogonality is achieved in time (TDMA), frequency (FDMA), or code (CDMA) domains. In CDMA MIMO radar each transmit element emits a distinct phase‑coded sequence s_n(t) of length L, and the received signal after matched filtering yields a virtual data matrix Y = A_r(θ) diag(α) A_t^T(θ) S^H + N, where A_t and A_r are transmit and receive steering matrices, α contains target reflectivities, and S is the waveform matrix. The virtual steering vector is the Kronecker product a_v(θ) = a_r(θ) ⊗ a_t(θ). Direction‑of‑arrival (DOA) estimation on the virtual array can use subspace methods such as MUSIC or ESPRIT, achieving resolution proportional to the virtual aperture. However, waveform correlation introduces off‑diagonal terms in S^H S, degrading the virtual array model. The integrated sidelobe level (ISL) and peak sidelobe level (PSL) of the waveform set quantify this degradation. Optimisation of waveform sets for low cross‑correlation under constant‑modulus and peak‑to‑average‑power‑ratio (PAPR) constraints is a non‑convex problem; algorithms such as cyclic coordinate descent, majorisation‑minimisation, and semidefinite relaxation (SDR) are employed. For colocated MIMO radar, the Cramér‑Rao bound (CRB) for DOA estimation shows a 1/(N_t N_r) scaling with SNR, confirming the virtual array gain. In widely separated MIMO radar (statistical MIMO), the transmit elements are spaced far enough to observe independent target scattering, providing diversity gain rather than aperture gain. The examiner tests the virtual array concept, the Kronecker product steering vector, the effect of waveform cross‑correlation on the virtual array covariance, and the distinction between colocated and statistical MIMO. A frequent trap is assuming that any set of orthogonal waveforms yields a perfect virtual array; finite‑length codes have non‑zero cross‑correlation that must be accounted for in the covariance model.

Structured Sparsity Recovery via Block Orthogonal Matching Pursuit
A signal x ∈ ℝ¹⁰⁰ is partitioned into 10 blocks of length 10. Only 2 blocks are non‑zero. Measurements y = Φx are taken with Φ ∈ ℝ³⁰ˣ¹⁰⁰ satisfying block‑RIP of order 2 with δ₂block=0.1. Explain why standard OMP may fail to recover the correct support, and outline the BOMP selection rule. Compute the minimum number of measurements m required by the block‑RIP bound m ≳ K d + K log(B/K) for this configuration.
Standard OMP selects individual columns of Φ based on maximal correlation with the residual. Because the non‑zero coefficients are confined to two blocks, the energy of each active block is spread across 10 columns. The correlation of any single column from an active block with the residual is roughly 1/√10 of the block energy, while columns from inactive blocks may exhibit comparable correlations due to noise, causing OMP to pick wrong atoms. BOMP instead computes the ℓ₂ norm of the correlation vector for each block: g_b = ‖Φ_b^T r‖₂, where Φ_b contains the columns of block b. The block with the largest g_b is selected, aggregating the energy of all 10 columns and providing a stronger discriminant. For the given parameters K=2, d=10, B=10, the bound gives m ≳ 2·10 + 2·log(10/2) ≈ 20 + 2·1.61 ≈ 23.2, so at least 24 measurements are sufficient. The actual m=30 satisfies this bound, guaranteeing exact recovery with high probability under the block‑RIP condition.

When a question mentions “block sparsity”, “group sparsity”, or “tree sparsity”, immediately identify the mixed norm (‖·‖₂,₁, group‑Lasso, tree‑norm) and the corresponding model‑based measurement bound. Write the bound m ≳ dim(model) + log(|model|) and substitute the specific dimensions (K, d, B, tree depth). For MIMO radar, recall the virtual array steering vector a_v = a_r ⊗ a_t and the aperture gain N_t N_r. If waveform cross‑correlation appears, note that the virtual covariance becomes R_v = (A_r ⊙ A_t) P (A_r ⊙ A_t)^H + σ²I where ⊙ denotes Khatri‑Rao product, and the off‑diagonal terms of the waveform correlation matrix appear in P. These two patterns cover >80 % of advanced signal‑processing questions on structured sparsity and MIMO radar.

Topological Signal Processing: Persistent Homology for Time Series Analysis

Topological data analysis (TDA) provides a coordinate‑free, noise‑robust framework for characterising the shape of data. Persistent homology, the central tool of TDA, tracks the birth and death of topological features (connected components, loops, voids) across a filtration parameter, typically a distance threshold. For a scalar time series x(t), a common embedding is the sliding‑window (Takens) embedding: X_i = [x(i), x(i+τ), …, x(i+(d−1)τ)] ∈ ℝ^d, producing a point cloud in ℝ^d. The Vietoris‑Rips filtration builds simplicial complexes by connecting points whose pairwise distance ≤ ε. As ε grows, components merge (H₀), loops appear and fill (H₁), and higher‑dimensional voids form (H_k). The persistence diagram Dgm_k = {(b_i, d_i)} records birth b_i and death d_i of each k‑dimensional feature. The persistence landscape λ_k(t) = k‑th largest value of (min(t−b_i, d_i−t))_+ converts diagrams into functions in L², enabling statistical learning. For periodic signals the embedding yields a topological circle, producing a prominent H₁ point with high persistence (d−b). For chaotic attractors (e.g., Lorenz system) the embedding reveals a fractal structure with many short‑lived H₁ features. The bottleneck distance d_B(D₁, D₂) = inf_γ supp∈D₁ ‖p−γ(p)‖_∞ and the Wasserstein distance W_p(D₁, D₂) = (inf_γ ∑ ‖p−γ(p)‖_p^p)1/p provide metrics between diagrams. Stability theorems guarantee that small perturbations in the time series (in L∞ norm) induce small changes in the persistence diagram (in bottleneck distance), making TDA robust to noise. Applications include bearing fault detection (periodic impulses yield high‑persistence H₁), EEG seizure classification (changes in H₀/H₁ persistence landscapes), and financial time‑series regime detection. The examiner expects knowledge of the embedding parameters (delay τ via mutual information, dimension d via false nearest neighbours), the construction of the Vietoris‑Rips complex, the definition of persistence diagrams and landscapes, and the stability theorem. A common trap is confusing the persistence diagram (multiset of points) with the barcode (collection of intervals); they are equivalent representations but the diagram is the standard input for statistical distances.

Kernel Methods in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces for Nonlinear Filtering

Kernel methods lift signals into a high‑dimensional reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) ℋ where nonlinear relationships become linear. Given a positive‑definite kernel k: 𝒳×𝒳 → ℝ, the RKHS ℋ consists of functions f(x) = ∑_i α_i k(x_i, x) with inner product ⟨f, g⟩_ℋ = ∑i,j α_i β_j k(x_i, x_j). The kernel trick allows algorithms to operate on the Gram matrix Kij=k(x_i, x_j) without explicit feature maps. In nonlinear filtering, the state‑space model xt+1=f(x_t)+w_t, y_t=h(x_t)+v_t is addressed by embedding the state distribution into ℋ via kernel mean embeddings μ_P = 𝔼x∼P[k(x,·)]. The conditional mean embedding (CME) operator CY|X = CYX CXX-1 maps an input embedding to the output embedding, enabling Bayesian inference in ℋ. The kernel recursive least squares (KRLS) algorithm maintains a dictionary of past inputs and updates the coefficient vector α_t to minimise the regularised loss ∑i=1^t λt−i (y_i − ∑_j α_j k(x_j, x_i))² + γ‖α‖_K², where ‖α‖_K² = α^T K α. Sparsification via the approximate linear dependence (ALD) criterion keeps the dictionary size bounded. For non‑stationary environments, the kernel Kalman filter (KKF) propagates the mean and covariance in ℋ: μt|t−1 = CX_t|Xt−1 μt−1|t−1, Σt|t−1 = CX_t|Xt−1 Σt−1|t−1 CX_t|Xt−1^* + Q, with update using the CME of the observation model. The kernel choice (Gaussian RBF, Matérn, polynomial) controls the smoothness and length‑scale of the learned functions; the bandwidth σ of the RBF kernel is critical and often selected by cross‑validation or maximum likelihood on the marginal likelihood log p(y|X) = −½ y^T (K+σ_n²I)-1 y − ½ log|K+σ_n²I| − n/2 log 2π. The examiner tests the kernel mean embedding definition, the CME operator derivation, the KRLS update equations with ALD sparsification, and the connection between kernel bandwidth and the effective degrees of freedom. A frequent trap is assuming the kernel matrix K is always invertible; in practice a regularisation term γI is added, and the effective rank of K determines the model complexity.

Bayesian Nonparametric Signal Inference with Dirichlet Process Mixtures

Bayesian nonparametrics allow the model complexity to grow with data, avoiding the need to pre‑specify the number of mixture components. The Dirichlet process (DP) DP(α, H) is a distribution over probability measures; a draw G ∼ DP(α, H) is almost surely discrete: G = ∑k=1^∞ π_k δθ_k, where π_k are stick‑breaking weights π_k = β_k ∏l<k</sub> (1−β_l), β_k ∼ Beta(1, α), and θ_k ∼ H. A Dirichlet process mixture model (DPMM) for observations y_i assumes y_i | θ_i ∼ F(θ_i), θ_i ∼ G, G ∼ DP(α, H). The Chinese restaurant process (CRP) representation gives the predictive distribution for a new observation: p(θn+1 | θ1:n) = (α/(α+n)) H + ∑k=1K_n (n_k/(α+n)) δθ_k^*, where K_n is the number of occupied clusters and n_k their sizes. In signal processing, DPMMs are used for spectral density estimation (mixture of Beta or Gaussian processes), time‑varying autoregressive models (each cluster corresponds to a regime), and multi‑target tracking (each target generates a cluster of measurements). Inference algorithms include Gibbs sampling with the CRP (Neal’s Algorithm 8), blocked Gibbs with slice sampling, and variational Bayes with a truncated stick‑breaking approximation. The concentration parameter α controls the expected number of clusters: 𝔼[K_n] ≈ α log(1 + n/α). For spectral estimation, the base measure H is often a conjugate prior for the spectral parameters (e.g., inverse‑Wishart for covariance matrices). The posterior predictive density p(ynew | y1:n) integrates over the infinite mixture, automatically adapting the number of spectral peaks. The examiner expects the stick‑breaking construction, the CRP predictive rule, the role of α, and the distinction between parametric (finite mixture) and nonparametric (DP) models. A common trap is treating the DP as a prior on the number of components; it is a prior on the partition of data, and the number of components is a random variable whose posterior depends on α and the data. Another trap is ignoring the label‑switching problem in MCMC; the CRP representation avoids it by working directly with partitions.

Synthetic Aperture Radar Imaging: Range‑Doppler Processing and Backprojection Algorithms

Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) forms high‑resolution images by coherently combining echoes from a moving platform. The core signal model treats each transmitted pulse as a linear frequency‑modulated (chirp) waveform; after de‑chirping, the received baseband signal for a point target at slant range R and azimuth position x is s(t, η) = σ rect(t−2R/c) exp(−j4πR/λ) exp(−j2πfDη), where η is slow‑time, fD the Doppler frequency, and σ the reflectivity. Range compression is performed by matched filtering with the conjugated chirp, yielding a sinc‑shaped impulse response whose width determines range resolution ΔR = c/(2B) with B the chirp bandwidth. Azimuth processing exploits the Doppler history: a point target generates a quadratic phase φ(η) = −4π/λ √(R0² + (vη)²) ≈ −4πR0/λ − 2πv²η²/(λR0) for small squint. The range‑Doppler algorithm (RDA) first applies range FFT, then a range‑dependent azimuth FFT after multiplying by a residual video phase (RVP) correction term exp(jπv²η²/(λR)) to remove range‑cell migration. For wide‑beam or high‑squint geometries, the RDA approximation breaks down; the ω‑k algorithm (also called range‑migration algorithm) performs a 2‑D Stolt interpolation in the wavenumber domain to achieve exact focusing. An alternative is the time‑domain backprojection algorithm (BPA), which directly implements the synthetic aperture integral: I(x, r) = Ση s(η, t = 2√((x−xη)²+r²)/c) w(η). BPA handles arbitrary flight paths and non‑planar wavefronts at the cost of O(N³) operations; fast factorised backprojection reduces this to O(N² log N) by hierarchical sub‑aperture processing. Polarimetric SAR adds a 2×2 scattering matrix per pixel; the Sinclair matrix [SHH SHV; SVH SVV] enables decomposition (Cloude‑Pottier, Freeman‑Durden) for target classification. Interferometric SAR (InSAR) uses two apertures separated by a baseline B to measure phase difference Δφ = 4πB⊥Δh/(λR sinθ), yielding digital elevation models with centimetre‑level height accuracy. The examiner expects the derivation of the point‑target spectrum, the RVP correction factor, the Stolt mapping formula ky = √(4ω²/c² − kx²), and the computational trade‑offs between frequency‑domain and time‑domain focusing. A frequent trap is neglecting the range‑dependent azimuth FM rate in RDA, which causes defocusing at far range; another is assuming the baseline decorrelation in InSAR is purely thermal, ignoring volumetric and temporal decorrelation sources.

Physiological Signal Analysis: ECG Classification and EEG Source Imaging

Electrocardiogram (ECG) and electroencephalogram (EEG) are the two most widely analysed biomedical signals. A standard 12‑lead ECG records the heart’s electrical activity at 250–1000 Hz; the diagnostic waveform comprises P‑wave, QRS complex, and T‑wave. Automated arrhythmia detection pipelines typically follow: (1) baseline wander removal via a high‑pass filter at 0.5 Hz or a cubic spline fit to the PR segment; (2) power‑line interference suppression using a 50/60 Hz notch filter or adaptive line enhancer; (3) QRS detection with the Pan‑Tompkins algorithm — bandpass 5–15 Hz, differentiation, squaring, moving‑window integration, and adaptive thresholding; (4) beat segmentation and feature extraction (RR interval, QRS width, ST deviation, morphological coefficients from Hermite or wavelet bases); (5) classification using a support vector machine, random forest, or a 1‑D convolutional neural network. The MIT‑BIH Arrhythmia Database (48 half‑hour records, 110 k beats) remains the benchmark; state‑of‑the‑art deep models achieve >99 % F1‑score for the five AAMI classes (N, S, V, F, Q). EEG source imaging solves the inverse problem: given scalp potentials V ∈ ℝM from M electrodes, estimate the cortical current density J ∈ ℝ3N at N dipoles. The forward model is V = L J + ε, where L is the lead‑field matrix computed from a realistic head model (boundary‑element or finite‑element method). Because M ≪ 3N, the problem is ill‑posed; regularisation is mandatory. Minimum‑norm estimate (MNE) uses Ĵ = LT(L LT + λI)−1 V with Tikhonov parameter λ chosen by generalized cross‑validation. Low‑resolution electromagnetic tomography (LORETA) adds a Laplacian smoothness prior Ĵ = argmin ‖V−LJ‖² + λ‖ΔJ‖². Beamformers (LCMV) construct spatial filters w = (LTC−1L)−1LTC−1 where C is the data covariance; they suppress correlated interference but suffer from signal cancellation when sources are coherent. Dynamic statistical parametric mapping (dSPM) normalises MNE by the noise‑covariance‑derived standard deviation, yielding z‑score maps for group analysis. The examiner tests the Pan‑Tompkins threshold update equations, the lead‑field construction for a three‑shell spherical model, the resolution matrix R = L†L for MNE, and the distinction between distributed (MNE, LORETA) and focal (beamformer, MUSIC) source models. A common trap is applying a fixed QRS detection threshold across records with varying signal‑to‑noise ratio; another is ignoring the depth bias of MNE, which favours superficial sources unless depth weighting is applied.

Worked Example: Pan‑Tompkins Threshold Adaptation
A 30‑minute ECG record sampled at 360 Hz yields an initial signal peak SPKI = 1.2 mV and noise peak NPKI = 0.15 mV. The detection threshold is THRESH = NPKI + 0.25(SPKI − NPKI). After the first detected QRS, the measured peak is 1.0 mV. Update SPKI and NPKI using the standard learning rates (0.125 for signal, 0.125 for noise) and compute the new threshold.
Initial THRESH = 0.15 + 0.25(1.2 − 0.15) = 0.15 + 0.2625 = 0.4125 mV. The detected peak 1.0 mV > THRESH, so it is classified as signal. SPKInew = 0.125×1.0 + 0.875×1.2 = 0.125 + 1.05 = 1.175 mV. NPKI unchanged = 0.15 mV. New THRESH = 0.15 + 0.25(1.175 − 0.15) = 0.15 + 0.25625 = 0.40625 mV.

Massive MIMO Channel Estimation: Pilot Contamination and Bayesian Methods

Massive multiple‑input multiple‑output (MIMO) systems equip a base station with M antennas (typically 64–256) serving K single‑antenna users (K ≪ M). The uplink received pilot matrix is Yp = H ΦT + N, where H ∈ ℂM×K is the channel, Φ ∈ ℂτ×K the orthogonal pilot sequences of length τ ≥ K, and N additive white Gaussian noise. In time‑division duplex (TDD) operation, downlink channel state information is obtained via reciprocity. The fundamental impairment is pilot contamination: when neighbouring cells reuse the same pilot set, the estimated channel Ĥ = Yp Φ* (ΦT Φ*)−1 becomes Ĥ = H + Σl∈𝒞 Hl + Ñ, where Hl are contaminating channels from users in other cells. This coherent interference does not vanish as M → ∞, limiting spectral efficiency. Bayesian channel estimation exploits prior statistics: the channel vector for user k is modelled as hk ∼ 𝒞𝒩(0, Rk) with spatial correlation matrix Rk (often Toeplitz for uniform linear arrays). The minimum mean‑square error (MMSE) estimator is ĥk = Rk ΦkH (Φk Rk ΦkH + σ²I)−1 yp,k, which requires knowledge of Rk and noise variance σ². In practice, Rk is estimated from sample covariance of received data during non‑pilot symbols (covariance‑based EM) or via a parametric angular spread model. Compressed sensing approaches exploit sparsity in the angular domain: hk = A xk with A a DFT dictionary and xk having few non‑zero entries; orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) or basis pursuit denoising (BPDN) recover xk from τ < K pilots, reducing overhead. Pilot decontamination techniques include: (i) pilot assignment optimisation via graph colouring; (ii) subspace projection using the dominant eigenvectors of the contaminating covariance; (iii) coordinated multipoint (CoMP) where base stations share pilot observations to jointly estimate channels. The examiner expects the MMSE derivation, the pilot contamination SINR expression SINRk = (M ρk tr(Rk)) / (Σl≠k ρl tr(Rl) + σ²) for matched‑filter precoding, and the Cramér‑Rao bound for sparse channel estimation. A frequent trap is assuming orthogonal pilots eliminate contamination entirely — they only avoid intra‑cell interference; inter‑cell contamination persists unless pilot reuse factor >1. Another trap is applying the MMSE formula with an identity covariance (i.e., i.i.d. Rayleigh) when the true channel exhibits strong spatial correlation, leading to severe performance loss.

Audio Source Separation: Nonnegative Matrix Factorization and Deep Clustering

Single‑channel audio source separation aims to recover individual source waveforms from a mixture x(t) = Σn=1N sn(t). Time‑frequency masking operates on the short‑time Fourier transform (STFT) magnitude spectrogram X ∈ ℝ+F×T. Nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) approximates X ≈ W H where W ∈ ℝ+F×K contains spectral bases and H ∈ ℝ+K×T their activations. For supervised separation, each source n has a pre‑trained dictionary Wn; at test time, the mixture is factorised with concatenated dictionaries W = [W1 … WN] and the activation matrix is partitioned to reconstruct source spectrograms Ŝn = Wn Hn. The standard multiplicative update rules for Euclidean cost ‖X−WH‖F² are H ← H ⊙ (WTX) / (WTWH) and W ← W ⊙ (XHT) / (WHHT); for Itakura‑Saito divergence (scale‑invariant, suited to audio) the updates become H ← H ⊙ (WT(X ⊘ (WH)²)) / (WT(1 ⊘ (WH))). Deep clustering (DC) replaces the explicit dictionary with an embedding network fθ(X) = V ∈ ℝFT×D that maps each time‑frequency bin to a D‑dimensional unit vector; the loss encourages bins from the same source to have similar embeddings: L = ‖V VT − Y YT‖F², where Y ∈ {0,1}FT×N is the ideal binary mask matrix. At inference, k‑means clustering on the rows of V yields soft masks. The deep attractor network (DAN) adds attractor points an computed as the mean of embeddings weighted by a reference mask, enabling fixed‑dimensional output. Permutation invariant training (PIT) solves the label‑assignment problem for end‑to‑end waveform models (e.g., TasNet) by minimising minπ Σn ‖sn − ŝπ(n)‖² over all permutations π. The examiner tests the NMF multiplicative updates for both Euclidean and Itakura‑Saito costs, the DC loss gradient ∂L/∂V = 4(V VT − Y YT) V, and the PIT permutation search complexity (factorial in N, approximated by Hungarian algorithm for mask‑based losses). A common trap is using Euclidean NMF on magnitude spectrograms without power compression, which over‑emphasises high‑energy bins; another is assuming DC embeddings are directly usable as masks — they require a clustering step, and the number of clusters must equal the number of sources.

Power System Signal Processing: Synchrophasor‑Based Wide‑Area Monitoring

Phasor measurement units (PMUs) sample voltage and current at 30–60 frames per second (reporting rate) synchronized to UTC via GPS, producing synchrophasors V = |V| ejθ and I = |I| ejφ. The IEEE C37.118 standard defines the message format and accuracy classes (TVE ≤ 1 % for P‑class, ≤ 0.3 % for M‑class). Wide‑area monitoring systems (WAMS) aggregate data from dozens of PMUs to detect electromechanical oscillations (0.1–2 Hz), voltage instability, and line outages. The core signal model for a small‑signal oscillation mode is ΔV(t) = Σk Ak eσkt cos(ωkt + φk), where σk is the damping coefficient and ωk the angular frequency. Modal analysis uses Prony’s method, matrix pencil, or the eigensystem realization algorithm (ERA) on the multivariate PMU time series to extract (σk, ωk, Ak). For real‑time implementation, the recursive least‑squares (RLS) Prony algorithm updates the autoregressive coefficients with forgetting factor λ ≈ 0.99, tracking slowly varying modes. Voltage stability indices such as the L‑index Lj = |1 − Σi∈G Fji Vi/Vj| (where F is the reduced Jacobian) are computed from synchrophasor‑derived bus voltages. Event detection (line trip, generator loss) employs a two‑stage approach: (1) a fast change‑point detector on the rate‑of‑change‑of‑frequency (ROCOF) or voltage magnitude using cumulative sum (CUSUM) with threshold tuned to the PMU reporting latency; (2) a verification stage using the unscented Kalman filter (UKF) on the differential‑algebraic power‑system model to confirm the topology change. Data quality management addresses missing samples (linear interpolation or Kalman smoothing), bad data (Mahalanobis distance on the residual vector), and time‑alignment errors (cross‑correlation of high‑frequency components). The examiner expects the Prony characteristic polynomial derivation, the TVE formula TVE = √((Vest−Vtrue)²)/|Vtrue|, the CUSUM recursion St = max(0, St−1 + (xt−μ0)−ν), and the distinction between P‑class (protection, low latency) and M‑class (measurement, high accuracy) PMU requirements. Finite Rate of Innovation Sampling and Signal Reconstruction

The classical sampling theorem assumes bandlimited signals, yet many physical signals — streams of Dirac impulses, piecewise polynomials, non‑uniform splines, and certain solutions of linear differential equations — are not bandlimited but possess a finite number of degrees of freedom per unit time. This quantity, the rate of innovation ρ, is defined as the number of free parameters per second required to represent the signal. For a stream of K Diracs per second, x(t) = Σk ak δ(t − tk), the rate of innovation is ρ = 2K (amplitudes and locations). Vetterli, Blu, and Dragotti showed that such signals can be sampled and perfectly reconstructed at rates proportional to ρ, far below the Nyquist rate of any bandlimited approximation.

The reconstruction mechanism relies on an annihilating filter. If a sampling kernel φ(t) reproduces exponentials (e.g., a B‑spline or an exponential reproducing kernel), the sampled sequence y[n] = ⟨x(t), φ(t/T − n)⟩ satisfies a linear recurrence relation whose characteristic polynomial has roots at the signal’s innovation locations. For a stream of Diracs, the annihilating filter h[n] of length K+1 satisfies Σm=0K h[m] y[n−m] = 0. The filter coefficients are found by solving a structured linear system (Toeplitz or Hankel) built from y[n]; the roots of the polynomial H(z) = Σ h[m] z−m yield the time locations tk. Amplitudes follow from a Vandermonde system. In noise, the annihilating filter equations become inconsistent; Cadzow denoising iteratively enforces low‑rank and Toeplitz structure on the data matrix, while total least squares or matrix pencil methods improve robustness. The examiner expects the derivation of the annihilating filter for a stream of Diracs sampled with a B‑spline of degree N ≥ 2K−1, the condition that the sampling kernel must satisfy the Strang‑Fix conditions of order 2K, and the Cramér‑Rao bound for Dirac location estimation under additive white Gaussian noise. A frequent trap is assuming any low‑pass kernel works; the kernel must reproduce the exponentials e−αkt corresponding to the signal’s innovations, otherwise the annihilating filter relation does not hold.

Sub‑Nyquist Sampling for Multiband Signals: The Xampling Framework

Wideband multiband signals — cognitive radio spectra, radar returns, and ultrasound echoes — occupy a small fraction of a large bandwidth. The modulated wideband converter (MWC), introduced by Mishali and Eldar, achieves sub‑Nyquist sampling by mixing the analog input with periodic waveforms pi(t) (i = 1,…,M), low‑pass filtering each branch, and sampling at a low rate fs ≥ 2B, where B is the total occupied bandwidth, not the Nyquist bandwidth. Each mixing waveform has Fourier coefficients ci,l; the discrete‑time Fourier transform of the i‑th branch output is a linear combination of the original spectrum’s aliases. Stacking the M branches yields a continuous‑to‑finite (CTF) block that maps the continuous spectrum to a finite‑dimensional vector. If the spectrum is sparse (few active bands), the CTF matrix acts as a compressive sensing measurement matrix. Support recovery identifies the active frequency intervals via ℓ1 minimisation or orthogonal matching pursuit on the CTF output; subsequent reconstruction solves a linear system for the spectral content within each detected band. The minimal sampling rate is fs = 2B(1 + ε) where ε accounts for guard bands and transition widths. For blind spectrum sensing, the MWC requires no prior knowledge of band locations; the number of branches M must satisfy M ≥ 2Nmax where Nmax is the maximum number of active bands. The examiner tests the CTF matrix derivation, the restricted isometry property (RIP) conditions for the MWC measurement matrix, and the distinction between the MWC and the random demodulator (which uses a single branch with a random chipping sequence). A common trap is ignoring the effect of mixing waveform non‑idealities (phase noise, harmonic distortion) on the CTF matrix conditioning, which can cause support recovery failure even when the RIP holds for the ideal matrix.

A signal x(t) = 2δ(t−0.1) − 1.5δ(t−0.4) + 3δ(t−0.7) is sampled using a cubic B‑spline kernel φ(t) at T = 0.05 s. Derive the annihilating filter coefficients h[0], h[1], h[2], h[3] from the first 8 samples y[0]…y[7].

Because φ reproduces exponentials, y[n] satisfies Σm=03 h[m] y[n−m] = 0 for n ≥ 3. Space‑Time Adaptive Processing for Airborne Radar

Airborne radar must detect slow‑moving targets in the presence of strong ground clutter whose Doppler spectrum shifts with the platform’s motion. Space‑time adaptive processing (STAP) jointly filters across the antenna array (spatial dimension) and the pulse train (temporal dimension) to place nulls on the clutter ridge while preserving target response. The received data cube is rearranged into a vector x ∈ ℂNM where N is the number of antenna elements and M the number of pulses per CPI (coherent processing interval). The optimal weight vector wopt = R−1s maximises the signal‑to‑interference‑plus‑noise ratio (SINR), where R = E[xxH] is the interference covariance matrix and s is the space‑time steering vector for the target’s angle‑Doppler pair. In practice R is unknown and estimated from K training snapshots (range cells assumed target‑free) via the sample matrix inversion (SMI): R̂ = (1/K) Σk=1K xkxkH. The Reed‑Mallett‑Brennan rule states that K ≥ 2NM training samples are needed for the SMI‑STAP to achieve within 3 dB of the optimal SINR. For large NM (e.g., N=10, M=20 → NM=200), this requirement is prohibitive. Reduced‑dimension STAP methods project the data onto a lower‑dimensional subspace: Joint Domain Localised (JDL) uses beamspace and Doppler‑space transformations; Element‑Space Pre‑Doppler (ESPD) and Element‑Space Post‑Doppler (ESPD) apply spatial or temporal filtering first. Knowledge‑aided STAP (KA‑STAP) incorporates terrain maps, platform GPS/INS data, and clutter models to form a structured covariance estimate, drastically reducing training requirements. The examiner expects the clutter ridge equation fd = −(2v/λ) sin θ (for side‑looking array), the SINR loss formula L = (wHRw)/(|wHs|2 sHR−1s), and the diagonal loading technique R̂DL = R̂ + σ2I to stabilise inversion when K < NM. A frequent trap is confusing the clutter ridge slope for a forward‑looking array (where the ridge curves) with the linear ridge of a side‑looking array; another is assuming that SMI‑STAP works with K = NM samples — the 3 dB loss threshold requires K ≈ 2NM.

Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces: Channel Estimation and Beamforming

A reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) comprises M passive reflecting elements, each imposing a controllable phase shift θm ∈ [0, 2π) on the incident signal. The RIS‑aided channel from a base station (BS) to a user equipment (UE) is a cascaded channel h = GTΘf, where f ∈ ℂM is the BS‑RIS channel, G ∈ ℂM is the RIS‑UE channel, and Θ = diag(ejθ1, …, ejθM). Channel estimation is challenging because the RIS has no active RF chains; the BS must estimate the cascaded channel by transmitting pilots while cycling through RIS phase configurations. The ON/OFF or discrete Fourier transform (DFT) codebook approach uses M orthogonal phase patterns to separate the contributions of each element, requiring M pilot symbols. Compressive sensing exploits sparsity in the angular domain to reduce pilots to O(log M). Once the cascaded channel is estimated, beamforming optimisation maximises the received SNR: maxΘ, w |(GTΘf + hd)Hw|2 subject to |θm| = 1, ‖w‖2 ≤ P, where hd is the direct BS‑UE link. This non‑convex problem is typically solved by alternating optimisation (AO): fix Θ, optimise w via maximum ratio transmission (MRT); fix w, optimise Θ via manifold optimisation on the complex circle manifold or semidefinite relaxation (SDR) with Gaussian randomisation. For discrete phase shifts (b‑bit resolution), the problem becomes combinatorial; branch‑and‑bound or dynamic programming yields near‑optimal solutions. Near‑field RIS (Fresnel region) requires spherical wavefront modelling; the phase shift profile becomes quadratic in element index, enabling focusing and localisation. The examiner tests the pilot overhead for cascaded channel estimation (M vs O(log M)), the AO convergence guarantee (monotonic SNR increase, converges to a stationary point), the SDR rank‑one relaxation condition, and the distinction between far‑field (planar wave) and near‑field (spherical wave) beamforming gain scaling (M2 vs M3 for focused gain). A common trap is assuming the RIS can amplify signals — it only reflects with unit‑modulus coefficients; another is ignoring the double path loss (BS‑RIS and RIS‑UE) which makes RIS beneficial only when the direct link is blocked.

Diffusion Models for Signal Generation and Inverse Problems

Diffusion models, a class of score‑based generative models, have become the state‑of‑the‑art for signal synthesis and restoration tasks including speech enhancement, MRI reconstruction, channel estimation, and waveform generation. The forward diffusion process gradually adds Gaussian noise to a clean signal x0 over T steps: q(xt|xt−1) = N(xt; √(1−βt)xt−1, βtI), with a predefined noise schedule βt ∈ (0,1). The marginal q(xt|x0) = N(xt; √ᾱtx0, (1−ᾱt)I) where ᾱt = Πs=1t(1−βs). The reverse process is parameterised as pθ(xt−1|xt) = N(xt−1; μθ(xt, t), Σθ(xt, t)). Training minimises the variational bound, which simplifies to predicting the added noise εθ(xt, t) ≈ ε: Lsimple = Et,x0,ε[‖ε − εθ(√ᾱtx0 + √(1−ᾱt)ε, t)‖2]. This is equivalent to denoising score matching where the score function ∇x log pt(x) = −εθ(x, t)/√(1−ᾱt). For inverse problems (e.g., y = Ax + n), posterior sampling uses the measurement likelihood p(y|x) to guide the reverse diffusion: the score of the posterior is ∇x log p(x|y) = ∇x log p(x) + ∇x log p(y|x). Algorithms such as DPS (Diffusion Posterior Sampling) and MCG (Manifold Constrained Gradients) approximate the measurement gradient during the reverse process without retraining. Classifier‑free guidance enables conditional generation by training a joint model εθ(x, t, c) and an unconditional model εθ(x, t, ∅), then combining: ε̂ = εθ(x, t, ∅) + ω(εθ(x, t, c) − εθ(x, t, ∅)). Distillation (e.g., consistency models, progressive distillation) reduces sampling steps from 1000 to 1–4 by training a student model to match the multi‑step ODE trajectory of the teacher. The examiner expects the derivation of the simplified loss Lsimple, the connection between score matching and Tweedie’s formula x̂0 = (xt + (1−ᾱt)∇x log pt(xt))/√ᾱt, the DPS measurement gradient step xt−1 ← xt−1 − η∇x‖y − Ax0|t‖2, and the trade‑off between guidance scale ω and sample diversity. A frequent trap is applying unconditional diffusion to a conditional inverse problem without measurement guidance, which ignores the data consistency term; another is using a noise schedule designed for images (cosine schedule) on 1‑D signals without adjusting the time discretisation for the signal’s correlation length.

  • Assuming the annihilating filter method works with any low‑pass sampling kernel; it requires exponential reproduction (Strang‑Fix conditions).
  • Confusing the MWC’s CTF matrix with the random demodulator’s measurement matrix; the MWC uses multiple parallel branches with periodic mixing, the random demodulator uses a single branch with random chipping.
  • Believing SMI‑STAP achieves optimal SINR with K = NM training samples; the Reed‑Mallett‑Brennan rule requires K ≥ 2NM for 3 dB loss.
  • Thinking an RIS can provide power gain; it only reflects with unit‑modulus coefficients, and the double path loss often outweighs the array gain unless the direct link is blocked.
  • Using an unconditional diffusion model for an inverse problem without adding the measurement likelihood gradient, leading to samples that do not satisfy y = Ax.
  • Applying the standard cosine noise schedule to 1‑D signals without considering the signal’s autocorrelation time, causing poor reconstruction of low‑frequency components.
  • Ignoring the near‑field spherical wavefront model for RIS when the distance is less than the Fraunhofer distance 2D2/λ, resulting in incorrect phase profiles and focusing failure.

Robust Adaptive Beamforming with Diagonal Loading and Covariance Matrix Estimation

Adaptive beamforming seeks to maximize the signal‑to‑interference‑plus‑noise ratio (SINR) by adjusting the weight vector w applied to an array of M sensors. The classic Minimum Variance Distortionless Response (MVDR) beamformer solves w = R−1a / (aHR−1a) where R is the interference‑plus‑noise covariance matrix and a is the steering vector for the desired direction. In practice R is replaced by the sample covariance matrix R̂ = (1/N) Σn=1N x(n)x(n)H computed from N snapshots. When N is comparable to M, R̂ becomes ill‑conditioned, causing severe SINR degradation and the “signal self‑nulling” effect if the desired signal leaks into the training data.

Diagonal loading (DL) stabilizes the inversion by adding a scaled identity matrix: R̃ = R̂ + γI. The loading factor γ controls the trade‑off between robustness and resolution. A common rule‑of‑thumb sets γ = α · trace(R̂) / M with α ∈ [0.1, 10] chosen via cross‑validation or the “constant false alarm rate” criterion. More sophisticated approaches estimate γ analytically: the optimal loading factor that maximizes the output SINR under a spherical uncertainty model for the steering vector is γopt = (‖a‖2 / M) · (σs2 / σn2) where σs2 and σn2 are the signal and noise powers. When the steering vector mismatch is modeled as an ellipsoidal uncertainty set {a + Δ : ‖Δ‖2 ≤ ε}, the robust beamformer becomes a second‑order cone program (SOCP) that can be solved efficiently with interior‑point methods.

Covariance matrix estimation can be improved beyond the sample covariance by exploiting structural priors. The shrinkage estimator R̂shrink = ρT + (1−ρ)R̂ blends R̂ with a well‑conditioned target T (often the scaled identity or a Toeplitz matrix). The optimal shrinkage intensity ρ is derived via the Ledoit‑Wolf formula, which minimizes the mean‑squared error between the estimator and the true covariance without requiring knowledge of the true matrix. For non‑Gaussian interference, Tyler’s M‑estimator provides a robust scatter matrix that is invariant to elliptical distributions and can be computed via a fixed‑point iteration. In the presence of snapshot deficiency (N < M), the reduced‑rank beamformer projects the data onto a lower‑dimensional subspace spanned by the dominant eigenvectors of R̂ or via the multistage Wiener filter (MSWF), dramatically reducing the required training samples while preserving SINR performance.

Examiner focus: derivation of the MVDR weight vector, the effect of finite snapshots on eigenvalue spread, the diagonal loading factor selection via the “constant false alarm rate” or the “maximum SINR” criterion, the SOCP formulation for ellipsoidal uncertainty, and the Ledoit‑Wolf shrinkage intensity expression. A typical trap is to assume that increasing γ always improves robustness; excessive loading reduces the array’s ability to null interferers and effectively reverts to a conventional delay‑and‑sum beamformer. Another trap is to apply the sample covariance matrix directly when the desired signal is present in the training data, leading to signal cancellation; the correct remedy is to use a signal‑free training block or to employ a blocking matrix in the Generalized Sidelobe Canceller (GSC) structure.

Deep Unfolding Networks for Compressive Sensing Reconstruction

Compressive sensing (CS) reconstructs a sparse signal x ∈ ℝN from linear measurements y = Φx + n where Φ ∈ ℝM×N (M ≪ N) satisfies the restricted isometry property (RIP). Classical algorithms such as Basis Pursuit (BP), Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP), and Iterative Shrinkage‑Thresholding Algorithm (ISTA) solve the ℓ1‑regularized problem minx ½‖y − Φx‖22 + λ‖x‖1. ISTA iterates x(k+1) = ηλ/L(x(k) + (1/L)ΦT(y − Φx(k))) where η is the soft‑thresholding operator and L is the Lipschitz constant of the gradient. The convergence rate is O(1/k), which can be slow for high‑accuracy requirements.

Deep unfolding (also called algorithm unrolling) transforms a fixed number of ISTA iterations into a feed‑forward neural network whose layers mimic the iterative updates. Each layer k learns its own step size γk, threshold τk, and optionally a linear transform Wk that replaces ΦT. The unfolded network computes x(k+1) = ητk(Wkx(k) + γkΦT(y − Φx(k))). Training minimizes the reconstruction loss ‖x(K) − xtrue‖22 over a dataset of sparse signals and measurement matrices. Because the network depth K is typically 10–20, inference is significantly faster than running ISTA to convergence, while often achieving lower reconstruction error due to learned parameters that adapt to the statistics of the signal class and the measurement matrix.

Extensions include Learned ISTA (LISTA) where Wk = I − γkΦTΦ is constrained to preserve the ISTA structure, and the more flexible ADMM‑Net that unfolds the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers, introducing auxiliary variables and learned penalty parameters. For non‑Gaussian noise or Poisson measurements, the data‑fidelity term can be replaced by the corresponding negative log‑likelihood, and the proximal operator is learned via a small sub‑network. Theoretical analyses show that if the learned weights satisfy certain spectral conditions, the unfolded network inherits the convergence guarantees of the original algorithm while achieving a linear convergence rate in practice.

Examiner focus: the ISTA update derivation, the soft‑thresholding operator definition ητ(u) = sign(u)·max(|u|−τ, 0), the mapping from ISTA iterations to network layers, the role of learned step sizes and thresholds, the difference between LISTA and generic unfolded networks, and the training loss formulation. A common trap is to assume that any unfolded network automatically converges; without proper initialization (e.g., using the analytical ISTA parameters) the network may diverge or get stuck in poor local minima. Another trap is to ignore the measurement matrix conditioning; if Φ is highly coherent, even a learned network cannot overcome the fundamental RIP limits, and the examiner may ask for the minimum number of measurements M required for a given sparsity level s (typically M ≥ C·s·log(N/s)).

Worked Example: LISTA Parameter Count
Consider a LISTA network with K = 12 layers, measurement matrix Φ ∈ ℝ64×256, and sparse signals of length N = 256. Each layer learns a scalar step size γk and a scalar threshold τk. How many trainable parameters does the network have? If instead a generic unfolded network learns a full matrix Wk ∈ ℝ256×256 per layer in addition to γk and τk, what is the parameter count?
LISTA: 2 parameters per layer × 12 layers = 24 trainable scalars. Generic unfolded: each Wk has 256×256 = 65 536 entries, plus 2 scalars = 65 538 per layer. For 12 layers: 12 × 65 538 = 786 456 parameters. The massive increase explains why LISTA is preferred when training data is limited.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to “unfold” an iterative algorithm, first write the mathematical update for one iteration, then identify which quantities can be made learnable (step size, threshold, linear transform). Count parameters explicitly if asked; the examiner often tests whether you distinguish between structured (LISTA) and unstructured unfolding. Remember that the soft‑thresholding operator is the proximal map of the ℓ1 norm; any change to the regularizer (e.g., ℓ0 or weighted ℓ1) changes the proximal operator and must be reflected in the layer design.

Variational Bayesian Inference for Sparse Signal Recovery

Variational Bayesian (VB) inference provides a deterministic alternative to Markov chain Monte Carlo for posterior approximation in sparse linear models. The hierarchical model places a sparsity‑promoting prior on the signal coefficients x via a Gaussian scale mixture: p(x|γ) = ∏i=1N 𝒩(xi; 0, γi−1) where the precision (inverse variance) γi controls the activity of each coefficient. A Gamma hyperprior p(γi) = Ga(γi; a, b) with small shape a and rate b (e.g., a = b = 10−6) yields a heavy‑tailed marginal prior on xi that approximates the ℓ0 penalty. The noise variance σ2 also receives a Gamma prior. The joint posterior p(x, γ, σ2 | y) is intractable, so VB posits a factorized variational distribution q(x, γ, σ2) = q(x) ∏i q(γi) q(σ2) and minimizes the Kullback‑Leibler divergence KL(q‖p).

The optimal factors are obtained by taking expectations with respect to the other factors. The posterior over x is Gaussian: q(x) = 𝒩(x; μ, Σ) with Σ = (⟨σ−2⟩ΦTΦ + diag(⟨γ⟩))−1 and μ = ⟨σ−2⟩ΣΦTy. Each precision γi follows a Gamma distribution: q(γi) = Ga(γi; a + ½, b + ½⟨xi2⟩) where ⟨xi2⟩ = μi2 + Σii. The noise precision σ−2 is also Gamma: q(σ−2) = Ga(σ−2; c + M/2, d + ½⟨‖y − Φx‖22⟩) with ⟨‖y − Φx‖22⟩ = ‖y − Φμ‖22 + trace(ΦΣΦT). The algorithm iterates these updates until the variational lower bound (the evidence lower bound, ELBO) converges.

VB offers several advantages over point‑estimate methods such as the Relevance Vector Machine (RVM) or the Sparse Bayesian Learning (SBL) expectation‑maximization (EM) algorithm. The full posterior covariance Σ quantifies uncertainty in the recovered coefficients, enabling credible intervals and model selection via the ELBO. The automatic relevance determination (ARD) mechanism drives irrelevant γi to large values, effectively pruning the corresponding coefficients. Convergence is typically faster than MCMC and more stable than EM because the variational updates are guaranteed to increase the ELBO monotonically. However, the mean‑field factorization ignores posterior correlations between x and γ, which can lead to underestimation of the true posterior variance.

Examiner focus: the hierarchical prior construction, the derivation of the variational updates for μ, Σ, γi, and σ−2, the expression for the ELBO, and the ARD effect. A typical trap is to confuse the VB updates with the EM updates for SBL; in EM the hyperparameters γ are point estimates obtained by maximizing the marginal likelihood, whereas VB maintains a distribution over γ. Another trap is to assume that the variational posterior covariance Σ equals the true posterior covariance; the mean‑field approximation typically yields a smaller variance. The examiner may also ask for the computational complexity per iteration: the dominant cost is the inversion of the N×N matrix in Σ, which can be reduced to M×M via the matrix inversion lemma when M < N.

Time‑Frequency Reassignment and Synchrosqueezing for Nonstationary Analysis

The short‑time Fourier transform (STFT) provides a time‑frequency representation S(t, f) = ∫ x(τ) w(τ−t) e−j2πfτ dτ, but its resolution is limited by the Heisenberg‑Gabor uncertainty principle: the window w fixes a trade‑off between time and frequency concentration. Reassignment sharpens the STFT by moving each coefficient to the local center of gravity of the energy distribution. The reassigned coordinates are t̂(t, f) = t − Re{ST(t, f) S*(t, f)} / |S(t, f)|2 and f̂(t, f) = f + Im{SD(t, f) S*(t, f)} / |S(t, f)|2 where ST and SD are the STFTs computed with the time‑weighted window τw(τ) and the derivative window w′(τ) respectively. The reassigned spectrogram R(t, f) = Σ(t′,f′) |S(t′, f′)|2 δ(t−t̂(t′, f′)) δ(f−f̂(t′, f′)) concentrates energy along the true instantaneous frequency (IF) curves, but it is not invertible because multiple original points can map to the same reassigned bin.

Synchrosqueezing (SST) overcomes the invertibility issue by reallocating the STFT coefficients along the frequency axis only, preserving the time coordinate. The SST transform is T(t, f) = ∫|S(t, f′)|>γ S(t, f′) δ(f − f̂(t, f′)) df′ where γ is a threshold to suppress noise. The instantaneous frequency estimator f̂ is the same as in reassignment. Because the mapping is one‑to‑one in time, the original signal can be reconstructed by integrating T(t, f) over a narrow band around each ridge: x(t) ≈ (1/𝒞w) ∫f∈ℛ T(t, f) df where 𝒞w = ∫ w(τ) dτ. For multicomponent signals, ridge detection algorithms (e.g., path‑following on the synchrosqueezed magnitude) extract individual IF trajectories, enabling mode decomposition similar to empirical mode decomposition but with a solid mathematical foundation.

Both methods assume the signal consists of a sum of intrinsic mode type components x(t) = Σk Ak(t) cos(2πφk(t)) with slowly varying amplitude Ak and phase φk such that |A′k| ≪ 2π|φ′k|Ak and |φ″k| ≪ (φ′k)2. Violations (e.g., crossing IFs, rapid amplitude modulation) cause smearing in the reassigned/synchrosqueezed representation. The choice of window influences the bias‑variance trade‑off: a Gaussian window minimizes the time‑frequency area but yields larger side‑lobes; a Kaiser window with adjustable β parameter offers a practical compromise.

Examiner focus: the formulas for reassigned coordinates, the distinction between reassignment (non‑invertible) and synchrosqueezing (invertible), the ridge extraction and reconstruction formula, the admissibility conditions for the signal model, and the effect of window choice. A common trap is to apply reassignment to a signal with crossing chirps and expect clean separation; the reassigned spectrogram will show interference terms at the crossing points. Another trap is to confuse the synchrosqueezing transform with the wavelet‑based synchrosqueezing transform (WSST); the latter uses the continuous wavelet transform and reallocates along the scale axis, which is more suitable for signals with wideband components.

Federated Learning for Distributed Signal Processing in Sensor Networks

Federated learning (FL) enables collaborative model training across edge devices without sharing raw data, a critical requirement for privacy‑sensitive sensor networks (e.g., wearable health monitors, industrial IoT). Each node i holds a local dataset Di and minimizes a local loss Fi(w) = (1/|Di|) Σ(x,y)∈Di ℓ(w; x, y). The global objective is F(w) = Σi=1K (|Di|/|D|) Fi(w). The canonical Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm proceeds in rounds: the server broadcasts the global model w(t); each selected client performs E local SGD epochs to obtain wi(t+1); the server aggregates w(t+1) = Σi (|Di|/|D|) wi(t+1).

In signal processing contexts, the local loss often corresponds to a parameter estimation problem: e.g., each sensor estimates a channel vector hi from pilot observations yi = Xihi + ni. The global model may be a shared dictionary for sparse coding, a beamforming codebook, or a neural network for modulation classification. Non‑IID data across nodes (different channel statistics, varying SNR) causes client drift: local models diverge from the global optimum. Techniques to mitigate drift include FedProx, which adds a proximal term (μ/2)‖w − w(t)‖22 to the local objective; SCAFFOLD, which uses control variates to correct the client update direction; and FedDyn, which dynamically regularizes each client with a linearized global loss.

Communication efficiency is paramount. Model compression via sparsification (top‑k selection), quantization (1‑bit or 8‑bit), and low‑rank factorization reduces uplink payload. The error‑feedback mechanism accumulates compression residuals locally and adds them to the next update, preserving convergence guarantees. For wireless FL, the uplink is a multiple‑access channel; over‑the‑air computation (AirComp) exploits the superposition property of electromagnetic waves to compute the weighted sum of client updates directly in the analog domain, achieving bandwidth‑optimal aggregation. The transceiver design jointly optimizes the transmit scaling factors and the receive beamformer to minimize the mean‑squared error between the desired aggregated model and the received signal under per‑device power constraints.

Security threats include model poisoning (malicious clients send crafted updates to degrade the global model) and inference attacks (reconstructing local data from model updates). Robust aggregation rules such as Krum, median, or trimmed mean discard outliers based on pairwise distances or coordinate‑wise statistics. Differential privacy can be enforced by adding calibrated Gaussian noise to the aggregated update before the server applies it, with the privacy budget accounted via the moments accountant or Rényi DP composition.

Examiner focus: the FedAvg algorithm steps, the client drift problem and its mathematical characterization (bound on ‖∇Fi(w) − ∇F(w)‖), the proximal term in FedProx, the control variate update in SCAFFOLD, the error‑feedback mechanism for compressed communication, the AirComp signal model y = Σi hi bi wi + n, and the differential privacy noise scale σ = (Δ2√(2 log(1.25/δ)))/ε where Δ2 is the ℓ2 sensitivity of the aggregated update. A typical trap is to assume that FedAvg converges at the same rate as centralized SGD; the convergence bound contains an additional term proportional to the heterogeneity measure ζ2 = Σi (|Di|/|D|) ‖∇Fi(w*) − ∇F(w*)‖2. Another trap is to ignore the effect of local epochs E; increasing E reduces communication rounds but amplifies client drift, and the optimal E balances these effects.

  • Treating an unfolded network as a black box without verifying that the learned step sizes satisfy the spectral radius condition for convergence; unconstrained learning can produce divergent iterates.
  • Confusing variational Bayesian updates with expectation‑maximization updates for sparse Bayesian learning; VB maintains a distribution over hyperparameters while EM yields point estimates.
  • Applying reassignment to multicomponent signals with crossing instantaneous frequencies and expecting clean separation; reassignment does not resolve crossing components and introduces artifacts at intersections.
  • Believing federated averaging converges at the centralized SGD rate regardless of data heterogeneity; the convergence bound explicitly includes a heterogeneity term that can dominate if local distributions differ significantly.
  • Ignoring the need for error feedback when compressing model updates in federated learning; without residual accumulation, compression bias prevents convergence to the true optimum.
