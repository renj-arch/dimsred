Signal Processing — Fourier Analysis, Filtering & the Sampling Theorem

Signal processing studies the representation, manipulation, and analysis of signals (functions of time or space) using mathematical transforms. At the heart lies the Fourier transform, which decomposes a signal into its constituent frequencies and provides the foundation for all modern signal processing. The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem bridges continuous and discrete worlds: a bandlimited signal is completely determined by its values at a sufficiently high sampling rate. Digital filters, both finite impulse response (FIR) and infinite impulse response (IIR), process discrete-time signals. Wavelet theory extends Fourier analysis with time-frequency localization, enabling multiresolution decomposition. Modern advances include compressed sensing, which reconstructs sparse signals from far fewer measurements than traditional Nyquist-rate sampling, and wavelet denoising, which removes noise by thresholding wavelet coefficients. Together, these ideas power everything from audio compression and image restoration to medical imaging and communications.

Part 1

📏 The Fourier Transform and Its Variants

Fourier Analysis
The Continuous Fourier Transform, the DTFT, the DFT, and the FFT
The continuous-time Fourier transform (CTFT) of a signal f(t) ∈ L¹(ℝ) ∩ L²(ℝ) is ̂f(ω) = ∫_{−∞}^{∞} f(t) e^{−iωt} dt, with the inverse Fourier transform f(t) = (1/2π) ∫_{−∞}^{∞} ̂f(ω) e^{iωt} dω. Key properties: linearity (αf+βg ⇔ α̂f+βĝ), time shift (f(t−t₀) ⇔ e^{−iωt₀}̂f(ω)), frequency shift (e^{iω₀t}f(t) ⇔ ̂f(ω−ω₀)), convolution ((f∗g)(t) = ∫ f(τ)g(t−τ)dτ ⇔ ̂f(ω)·ĝ(ω)), and differentiation (f'(t) ⇔ iω̂f(ω)). The Plancherel theorem (extended by Parseval) preserves energy: ∫_{−∞}^{∞} |f(t)|² dt = (1/2π) ∫_{−∞}^{∞} |̂f(ω)|² dω. The discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) of a sequence x[n] is X(e^{iω}) = Σ_{n=−∞}^{∞} x[n] e^{−iωn}, periodic in ω with period 2π. The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of length N is X[k] = Σ_{n=0}^{N−1} x[n] e^{−2πikn/N} for k = 0, …, N−1 — it samples the DTFT at N equally spaced frequencies. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) computes the DFT in O(N log N) operations. The Cooley-Tukey decimation-in-time algorithm splits the N-point DFT into two N/2-point DFTs: X[k] = Σ_{n even} x[n] ω_N^{kn} + Σ_{n odd} x[n] ω_N^{kn} = DFT_{N/2}(x_even)[k] + ω_N^k · DFT_{N/2}(x_odd)[k], where ω_N = e^{−2πi/N}. This recursion yields the celebrated O(N log N) complexity — the Danielson-Lanczos lemma shows that a DFT of length N = 2^m can be reduced to m stages of 2-point DFTs (butterflies). The spectral leakage (Gibbs phenomenon) occurs when the signal is not periodic in the DFT window; windowing functions (Hamming, Hann, Blackman) mitigate this.
The Fourier transform decomposes signals into frequencies; the FFT makes the DFT computationally feasible and is one of the most important algorithms of the 20th century.
Part 2

🌍 The Sampling Theorem and Filtering

Sampling
Nyquist-Shannon Theorem, Aliasing, Upsampling, Downsampling, and Digital Filters
The Nyquist-Shannon-Kotelnikov sampling theorem: if a continuous-time signal f(t) is bandlimited to Ω (i.e., ̂f(ω) = 0 for |ω| ≥ Ω), then f(t) is uniquely determined by its samples at the Nyquist rate 2Ω (samples per second). The reconstruction formula is f(t) = Σ_{n=−∞}^{∞} f(nπ/Ω) · sinc(Ωt/π − n), where the sinc function sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx) serves as the ideal interpolation kernel. Equivalently, the reconstruction uses an ideal lowpass filter with cutoff Ω. Aliasing: when sampling below the Nyquist rate, high-frequency components fold down into the baseband, producing artifacts indistinguishable from genuine low-frequency content — the spectra overlap in the frequency domain. The anti-aliasing filter (lowpass prefilter) removes frequencies above half the sampling rate before sampling. Sampling rate conversion: upsampling by a factor L inserts L−1 zeros between samples, then lowpass filters (interpolation); downsampling (decimation) by a factor M keeps every M-th sample, preceded by a lowpass filter to prevent aliasing. The Z-transform of a discrete-time signal h[n] is H(z) = Σ_{n=−∞}^{∞} h[n] z^{-n}, with region of convergence (ROC) determining stability and causality. A linear time-invariant (LTI) system is fully characterized by its impulse response h[n]: y[n] = (x∗h)[n] = Σ_{k=−∞}^{∞} x[k] h[n−k]. The transfer function H(e^{iω}) = |H(e^{iω})| e^{i∠H(e^{iω})} gives the frequency response — the magnitude and phase. FIR filters (finite impulse response) have h[n] = 0 for n > M, are unconditionally stable, and can achieve exact linear phase (constant group delay). IIR filters (infinite impulse response) use feedback: y[n] = Σ_{k=0}^{M} b_k x[n−k] − Σ_{k=1}^{N} a_k y[n−k]; they are more efficient but can be unstable and have nonlinear phase. The bilinear transform maps analog filter H_a(s) to digital H_d(z) via s = (2/T)·(1−z^{-1})/(1+z^{-1}) — it warps the frequency axis but preserves stability and maps the imaginary axis to the unit circle. The Butterworth filter has a maximally flat passband with monotonic roll-off; the Chebyshev filter (type I or II) allows ripple in the passband or stopband for a steeper cutoff. The convolution theorem for the Z-transform: (x∗h)[n] ⇔ X(z)·H(z) on the intersection of ROCs.
The sampling theorem is the bridge between continuous and discrete worlds; aliasing is its fundamental pitfall, and digital filters are the primary tool for discrete-time processing.
Part 3

🧮 Wavelet Theory and the Multiresolution Analysis

Wavelets
The Continuous and Discrete Wavelet Transform, MRA, and the Fast Wavelet Transform
The wavelet transform addresses the limitation of the Fourier transform — poor time localization for high frequencies. The continuous wavelet transform (CWT) of f(t) is W_ψ f(a,b) = (1/√{|a|}) ∫_{−∞}^{∞} f(t) ψ((t−b)/a) dt, where a is the scale (dilation) and b is the translation (shift). The mother wavelet ψ(t) must satisfy the admissibility condition: C_ψ = ∫_{0}^{∞} |̂ψ(ω)|²/|ω| dω < ∞. The CWT is redundant; the discrete wavelet transform (DWT) uses dyadic scales a = 2^j and b = k·2^j. The multiresolution analysis (MRA) (Mallat-Meyer, 1989) provides the rigorous framework: a sequence of closed subspaces V_j ⊂ L²(ℝ) such that (1) V_j ⊂ V_{j+1} (nested), (2) ∩_{j} V_j = {0} and ∪_{j} V_j = L²(ℝ) (dense), (3) f(t) ∈ V_j ⇔ f(2t) ∈ V_{j+1} (scaling), and (4) there exists a scaling function φ(t) whose integer translates {φ(t−k)} form an orthonormal basis of V₀. The dilation equation (two-scale relation): φ(t) = Σ_k h_k φ(2t−k), where {h_k} are the lowpass filter coefficients (scaling coefficients). The wavelet ψ(t) is defined via ψ(t) = Σ_k g_k φ(2t−k) with g_k = (−1)^k h_{1−k} (quadrature mirror filter). The Haar wavelet is the simplest: φ(t) = 1 for t ∈ [0,1), 0 otherwise; ψ(t) = 1 for t ∈ [0,½), −1 for t ∈ [½,1), 0 otherwise. The Daubechies wavelets (Ingrid Daubechies, 1988) have compact support and N vanishing moments: ∫ t^k ψ(t) dt = 0 for k = 0, …, N−1 — they can analyze polynomials up to degree N−1. Daubechies D4 (N=2) is widely used. The fast wavelet transform (FWT, pyramid algorithm): given coefficients at scale j, the coefficients at scale j−1 are obtained by convolving with the lowpass filter h and downsampling (approximation coefficients), and with the highpass filter g and downsampling (detail coefficients). This yields O(N) complexity — faster than the FFT. The wavelet packet transform further decomposes both approximation and detail branches, enabling finer frequency resolution.
Wavelet theory provides a time-frequency multiresolution analysis that surpasses the Fourier transform for signals with localized features — a cornerstone of modern signal processing.
Part 4

🔥 Compressed Sensing and Wavelet Denoising

Advanced Topics
Wavelet Thresholding, Compressed Sensing, ℓ₁-Minimization, and Variational Denoising
Wavelet denoising (Donoho-Johnstone, 1994): for a noisy measurement y = x + z with z ∼ N(0, σ²I), the wavelet transform concentrates the signal energy into a few large coefficients while noise spreads uniformly. The denoised estimate is ̂x = W^{-1}(η_λ(Wy)), where η_λ is the thresholding function. Hard thresholding: η_λ(y) = y if |y| > λ, else 0. Soft thresholding: η_λ(y) = sign(y)·max(|y|−λ, 0) — it shrinks all coefficients by λ and avoids discontinuities. The universal threshold λ = σ·√(2 log N) ensures with high probability that pure noise is eliminated (but may oversmooth). Stein's unbiased risk estimate (SURE) yields the optimal threshold by minimizing an unbiased estimate of the MSE — adaptive per subband. The SureShrink hybrid combines universal and SURE thresholds. Compressed sensing (Candès-Romberg-Tao, Donoho, 2006): a signal x ∈ ℝ^N that is K-sparse in an orthonormal basis Ψ (i.e., ‖Ψ^T x‖_0 = K << N) can be recovered from m = O(K·log(N/K)) random linear measurements y = Φx (where Φ is an m×N sensing matrix) via ℓ₁-minimization: minimize ‖Ψ^T x‖₁ subject to Φx = y. The restricted isometry property (RIP) of Φ ensures stable recovery — random matrices (Gaussian, Bernoulli, partial Fourier) satisfy RIP with high probability. This violates the traditional Nyquist dogma: fewer samples than the signal bandwidth suffice if the signal is sparse. Variational denoising: the total variation (TV) denoising (Rudin-Osher-Fatemi, 1992) minimizes ‖x−y‖₂² + λ·TV(x), where TV(x) = Σ_i |x_{i+1}−x_i| (for 1D) or the gradient magnitude (for 2D) — it preserves edges while smoothing. The median filter replaces each sample with the median of its neighbors (nonlinear, excellent for salt-and-pepper noise). The bilateral filter combines domain and range Gaussian kernels, preserving edges. Morphological component analysis (MCA) separates a signal into morphological components (e.g., texture + cartoon) using sparsity in different dictionaries. Applications: audio denoising (wavelet thresholding for removing hiss), image restoration (deconvolution using Tikhonov regularization or total variation), super-resolution (sparse representation-based), and JPEG2000 compression using the discrete wavelet transform (Daubechies 9/7 biorthogonal wavelets). The ISTA (Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm) and FISTA (Fast ISTA) solve ℓ₁-regularized inverse problems efficiently.
Wavelet denoising and compressed sensing shattered the traditional limits of signal processing — signals can be denoised by shrinkage and reconstructed from far fewer measurements than classical theory allowed.
