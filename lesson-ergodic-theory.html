Ergodic Theory — The Long-Run Behavior of Dynamical Systems

Ergodic theory studies measure-preserving transformations and their long-term average behavior. It began with Boltzmann's ergodic hypothesis in statistical mechanics and grew into a rich field with connections to number theory (Furstenberg's correspondence), combinatorics (Szemerédi's theorem), and dynamical systems. The central result is Birkhoff's ergodic theorem: time averages converge to space averages for almost every initial condition.

"Ergodic theory is the study of the statistical behavior of deterministic dynamical systems — chaos through the lens of measure theory."
— Yakov Sinai
Part 1

📊 Measure-Preserving Systems and Birkhoff's Theorem

Averages
Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem — Time Averages Equal Space Averages
A measure-preserving transformation T on a probability space (X,μ) satisfies μ(T^{-1}A) = μ(A). A system is ergodic if the only T-invariant sets have measure 0 or 1 — the system cannot be decomposed into nontrivial invariant components. Birkhoff's pointwise ergodic theorem (1931): for any f ∈ L¹, the time average (1/n) Σ_{k=0}^{n−1} f(T^k x) converges almost everywhere to the conditional expectation E[f|ℐ], where ℐ is the σ-algebra of invariant sets. If T is ergodic, the limit is the space average ∫ f dμ a.e. von Neumann's mean ergodic theorem: the convergence holds in L² norm. The Koopman operator U_T f = f∘T is a unitary operator on L² — ergodicity is equivalent to 1 being a simple eigenvalue of U_T. Mixing: T is mixing if lim μ(A∩T^{-n}B) = μ(A)μ(B). Mixing ⇒ ergodic, but not conversely.
Birkhoff's theorem is the fundamental law of large numbers for deterministic dynamical systems — time averages converge a.e. to the ensemble average.
Part 2

🌀 Entropy — Measuring Randomness in Dynamics

Complexity
Kolmogorov-Sinai Entropy — Quantifying Chaos
The Kolmogorov-Sinai (KS) entropy h(T) measures the exponential growth rate of distinguishable orbit segments. For a finite partition α of X, the entropy of the partition is H(α) = −Σ μ(A_i) log μ(A_i). The entropy of T with respect to α is h(T,α) = lim (1/n) H(α ∨ T^{-1}α ∨ ... ∨ T^{-(n−1)}α), and h(T) = sup_α h(T,α). For a Bernoulli shift on m symbols with probabilities p_i, h = −Σ p_i log p_i. The Pesin formula (for smooth systems): h = Σ_{λ_i > 0} λ_i, the sum of positive Lyapunov exponents — a deep connection between entropy and geometry. Ornstein's theorem: Bernoulli shifts with the same entropy are isomorphic (measure-theoretically). Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem: (1/n) log μ(C_n(x)) → −h a.e. where C_n(x) is the cylinder set of length n containing x.
Kolmogorov-Sinai entropy is the exponential growth rate of the number of distinguishable orbit segments — it quantifies how chaotic a system is.
Part 3

🔗 Furstenberg's Correspondence and Applications

Number Theory
Ergodic Ramsey Theory — Furstenberg's Proof of Szemerédi's Theorem
In 1977, Furstenberg gave an ergodic-theory proof of Szemerédi's theorem: any subset of ℤ with positive upper density contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. The key idea: Furstenberg's correspondence principle associates to a set A ⊂ ℤ with positive density a measure-preserving system (X,μ,T) and a set Ã ⊂ X with μ(Ã) = d(A) such that combinatorial recurrence in ℤ corresponds to multiple recurrence in X. The multiple recurrence theorem: for a measure-preserving system with μ(A) > 0, liminf (1/N) Σ_{n=0}^{N−1} μ(A ∩ T^{-n}A ∩ T^{-2n}A ∩ ... ∩ T^{-kn}A) > 0. This implies Szemerédi for progressions of length k+1. Furstenberg-Katznelson extended this to higher dimensions (density Hales-Jewett). The Host-Kra structure theory decomposes ergodic systems into factors: the Kronecker (toral) factor, seminorm factors, and the nilspace factor.
Furstenberg's correspondence transforms problems in additive combinatorics into ergodic theory — Szemerédi's theorem becomes a statement about multiple recurrence.

⚡ Entertaining Systems and Modern Ergodic Theory

Advanced
Joinings, Disjointness, and the Pinsker Factor
A joining of two systems (X,μ,T) and (Y,ν,S) is a T×S-invariant measure on X×Y projecting to μ and ν. Furstenberg's disjointness: two systems are disjoint if their only joining is the product measure. A Kronecker system (rotation on a compact abelian group) is disjoint from any system with zero entropy (Pinsker factor). The Pinsker factor of T is the maximal factor with zero entropy. The weak Pinsker property (Thouvenot, recently resolved by Austin-Timmerman): every ergodic system decomposes into a Bernoulli factor and a zero-entropy factor. Ornstein theory classifies Bernoulli shifts: entropy is a complete invariant. Sinai's factor theorem: every ergodic system of entropy h contains a Bernoulli factor of entropy h. Modern ergodic theory classifies systems by entropy and disjointness — Bernoulli systems are the building blocks, and Ratner's theorem tames unipotent flows.

Rokhlin's Lemma and the Construction of Towers

Rokhlin's lemma (1948) is a cornerstone for approximating measure‑preserving transformations by periodic structures. Let (X,ℬ,μ) be a standard probability space and T a measure‑preserving, ergodic transformation. For any integer N ≥ 1 and any ε > 0 there exists a measurable set B (the base of a tower) such that the sets B, T⁻¹B, …, T-(N−1)B are pairwise disjoint and μ(⋃k=0N-1 T-kB) > 1 − ε. The complement of the tower has arbitrarily small measure, so the dynamics on most of the space looks like a cyclic permutation of N levels.

The proof uses the ergodic theorem applied to the indicator of a set of small measure, together with a careful selection of return times. The lemma yields a Rokhlin tower of height N; by refining the base one can obtain towers whose levels generate the σ‑algebra up to null sets. This approximation underlies many structural results: the construction of Bernoulli factors (Ornstein), the proof of the weak Pinsker property, and the approximation of entropy by finite partitions. In practice, one often builds a sequence of towers with heights N_k → ∞ and ε_k → 0 to obtain a generating partition for the system.

A quantitative version (Katok's lemma) gives explicit bounds on the measure of the error set in terms of the entropy h(T). For a system with entropy h, one can choose towers of height N such that the error measure is at most exp(−c N) for some c > 0 depending on h. This exponential control is useful in proofs of the variational principle and in the construction of symbolic models for smooth systems.

Spectral Theory of the Koopman Operator and Weak Mixing

The Koopman operator U_T : L²(X,μ) → L²(X,μ) defined by U_T f = f ∘ T is a unitary operator. Its spectral measure σ_f for f ∈ L² is the unique finite Borel measure on the unit circle 𝕋 satisfying ⟨U_T^n f, f⟩ = ∫_𝕋 z^n dσ_f(z). Ergodicity is equivalent to 1 being a simple eigenvalue of U_T; the corresponding eigenfunctions are the constants. A system is weakly mixing iff U_T has no non‑constant eigenfunctions, i.e. the spectrum of U_T on the orthogonal complement of the constants is continuous. Equivalently, for all f,g ∈ L², the Cesàro averages (1/N)∑n=0N-1 |⟨U_T^n f, g⟩ − ⟨f,1⟩⟨1,g⟩|² → 0.

Weak mixing implies ergodicity but not conversely. Classic examples: an irrational rotation on the circle has pure point spectrum (eigenvalues e2π i n α) and is ergodic but not weakly mixing. A Bernoulli shift has countable Lebesgue spectrum (the spectrum is equivalent to Lebesgue measure on 𝕋 with infinite multiplicity) and is strongly mixing, hence weakly mixing. The spectral theorem provides a complete unitary invariant: two ergodic systems are spectrally isomorphic iff their Koopman operators are unitarily equivalent on the orthogonal complement of constants.

Joinings give a powerful tool to study spectral properties. Two systems are disjoint if their only joining is the product measure. Furstenberg showed that a Kronecker system (compact group rotation) is disjoint from any system with zero entropy (the Pinsker factor). This disjointness result is a spectral statement: the Kronecker factor carries the pure point spectrum, while a zero‑entropy system has no non‑trivial eigenvalues beyond those forced by the Kronecker factor. The weak Pinsker conjecture (now a theorem of Austin and others) asserts that every ergodic system splits as a direct product of a Bernoulli system and a zero‑entropy system; the proof uses joinings and the structure of the Pinsker σ‑algebra.

Worked Example: Spectrum of an Irrational Rotation
Let T(x) = x + α (mod 1) on 𝕋 = ℝ/ℤ with Lebesgue measure, where α is irrational. Show that the Koopman operator U_T has pure point spectrum and determine its eigenvalues and eigenfunctions.
For each integer n, define f_n(x) = e2π i n x. Then U_T f_n(x) = f_n(x+α) = e2π i n α f_n(x). Hence f_n is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue λ_n = e2π i n α. The set {λ_n : n ∈ ℤ} is dense in 𝕋 because α is irrational, but each eigenvalue has multiplicity one. The linear span of {f_n} is dense in L²(𝕋) (Fourier series), so the spectral measure of any f ∈ L² is a pure point measure supported on {λ_n}. There is no continuous component; therefore the system is not weakly mixing. The eigenvalues are exactly the n‑th powers of e2π i α, and the eigenfunctions are the Fourier characters.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to distinguish ergodicity, weak mixing, and mixing, recall the hierarchy: mixing ⇒ weak mixing ⇒ ergodicity. Use spectral language: pure point spectrum ⇔ not weak mixing; continuous spectrum ⇔ weak mixing; Lebesgue spectrum ⇔ strong mixing (for many standard examples). Memorise the classic counterexamples: irrational rotation (ergodic, not weak mixing), Chacon transformation (weak mixing, not mixing), Bernoulli shift (mixing). A quick spectral check often decides the answer without lengthy computation.

Variational Principle and the Relationship Between Measure‑Theoretic and Topological Entropy

The variational principle (Goodwyn 1969, Dinaburg 1970, Goodman 1971) states that for a continuous map T on a compact metric space X, the topological entropy h_top(T) equals the supremum of measure‑theoretic entropies h_μ(T) over all T‑invariant Borel probability measures μ: h_top(T) = supμ ∈ ℳ_T(X) h_μ(T). A measure achieving the supremum is called a measure of maximal entropy. For expansive systems (e.g. subshifts of finite type, Anosov diffeomorphisms) such a measure exists and is unique under additional hypotheses (Bowen's specification property).

The proof uses the Brin‑Katok local entropy formula: for μ‑a.e. x, h_μ(T) = limε→0 limsupn→∞ −(1/n) log μ(B_n(x,ε)), where B_n(x,ε) is the Bowen ball of radius ε and length n. Topological entropy is defined via (n,ε)‑separated or spanning sets. The variational principle bridges the combinatorial definition of topological entropy with the probabilistic definition of measure‑theoretic entropy. In symbolic dynamics, the Parry measure (the measure of maximal entropy for a subshift of finite type) is a Markov measure whose transition matrix is derived from the Perron‑Frobenius eigenvector of the adjacency matrix.

Applications include the classification of invariant measures for hyperbolic systems: Sinai‑Ruelle‑Bowen (SRB) measures maximise entropy among measures with a given Lyapunov exponent sum (Pesin's formula). The variational principle also underlies the thermodynamic formalism: for a continuous potential φ, the topological pressure P(φ) = sup_μ (h_μ(T) + ∫ φ dμ). Equilibrium states are measures attaining the supremum; they generalise measures of maximal entropy (φ ≡ 0). This framework connects ergodic theory with statistical mechanics and multifractal analysis.

Polynomial and Multiple Recurrence Theorems

Furstenberg's multiple recurrence theorem (1977) states that for any measure‑preserving system (X,μ,T) and any set A with μ(A) > 0, liminfN→∞ (1/N) ∑n=0N-1 μ(A ∩ T-nA ∩ … ∩ T-knA) > 0 for every k ∈ ℕ. Bergelson and Leibman (1996) extended this to polynomial patterns: if p1,…,p_k ∈ ℤ[x] with p_i(0)=0, then liminfN→∞ (1/N) ∑n=0N-1 μ(A ∩ T-p1(n)A ∩ … ∩ T-p_k(n)A) > 0. The proof uses the structure theory of Host‑Kra characteristic factors (nilspace factors) and the Gowers‑Host‑Kra seminorms.

These recurrence results have direct combinatorial corollaries via Furstenberg's correspondence principle. Szemerédi's theorem (arithmetic progressions) corresponds to p_i(n)=in. The polynomial Szemerédi theorem (Bergelson‑Leibman) yields polynomial configurations in sets of positive upper density. Further extensions by Frantzikinakis and Host (2018) cover recurrence along sequences with zero topological entropy, such as the primes (Green‑Tao theorem on arithmetic progressions in the primes uses a relative version of the correspondence principle).

The quantitative bounds remain a major open area. For k=3, Roth's theorem gives a bound of N/(log log N) for the largest progression‑free subset of {1,…,N}. For general k, the best known bounds are of tower‑type (Gowers). In the ergodic setting, the convergence of the multiple ergodic averages (1/N)∑n=0N-1 ∏i=1^k f_i(Tp_i(n)x) was proved by Host‑Kra (2005) for linear polynomials and by Leibman (2005) for polynomials, using the machinery of nilsequences and the inverse theorem for Gowers norms.

Ratner's Theorem on Unipotent Flows and Diophantine Applications

Marina Ratner's theorems (1990‑1991) classify invariant measures and orbit closures for unipotent flows on homogeneous spaces G/Γ, where G is a Lie group and Γ a lattice. The measure classification theorem: any ergodic invariant probability measure for a unipotent one‑parameter subgroup is the Haar measure on a closed orbit of a subgroup containing the unipotent flow. The orbit closure theorem: the closure of any orbit is a finite union of closed orbits of such subgroups. These results resolved long‑standing conjectures of Raghunathan and Dani.

A celebrated application is the proof of the Oppenheim conjecture (Margulis, 1987): an indefinite quadratic form Q in ≥ 3 variables that is not proportional to a rational form takes values arbitrarily close to zero at integer points. The dynamical reformulation: the action of the orthogonal group SO(Q) on the space of lattices SL_n(ℝ)/SL_n(ℤ) contains a unipotent subgroup; measure‑classification results for unipotent flows imply that the orbit of the standard lattice under this unipotent subgroup is either closed or dense, which translates to the density of values of Q on ℤ^n.

Further applications include equidistribution of polynomial orbits on nilmanifolds (Green‑Tao‑Ziegler), quantitative non‑divergence of unipotent flows (Eskin‑Margulis‑Mozes), and the solution of the Littlewood conjecture for certain Diophantine exponents (Einsiedler‑Katok‑Lindenstrauss). Ratner's theorems also provide a measure‑rigidity framework: any measure invariant under a higher‑rank diagonalisable action with positive entropy must be algebraic (Einsiedler‑Katok‑Lindenstrauss). This rigidity underpins recent progress on the Furstenberg conjecture on ×2, ×3 invariant measures on the circle.

  • Confusing weak mixing with mixing: weak mixing only requires continuous spectrum, not decay of correlations.
  • Assuming every ergodic system has a generating partition of finite entropy; some systems (e.g. certain infinite‑entropy transformations) do not.
  • Misapplying the variational principle to non‑compact spaces without checking upper semi‑continuity of the entropy map.
  • Overlooking the requirement p_i(0)=0 in polynomial recurrence; without it the limit may be zero.
  • Treating Ratner's theorem as a statement about all flows on homogeneous spaces; it applies specifically to unipotent (or polynomial) flows.
  • Believing that the Pinsker factor is always a Bernoulli factor; it is the maximal zero‑entropy factor, which need not be Bernoulli.
  • Neglecting the distinction between measure‑theoretic and topological entropy when a problem asks for the supremum over invariant measures.

These traps highlight the subtle distinctions that examiners frequently test in advanced ergodic theory questions.

Joinings, Disjointness, and the Structure of Invariant Measures

A joining of two measure‑preserving systems (X,μ,T) and (Y,ν,S) is a T×S‑invariant probability measure λ on the product space X×Y whose marginals are μ and ν. The set of all joinings, denoted J(μ,ν), is convex and compact in the weak* topology. The extreme points of J(μ,ν) are the ergodic joinings. When the only joining is the product measure μ×ν, the systems are said to be disjoint. Furstenberg’s disjointness theorem (1967) states that a weakly mixing system is disjoint from any distal system; in particular, a weakly mixing system is disjoint from every system with discrete spectrum. This result provides a powerful tool for proving that certain invariant measures are unique: if a system is known to be weakly mixing and another candidate invariant measure yields a non‑product joining, a contradiction arises. The structure theory of joinings was further developed by Rudolph and by Host, who showed that for a system with given entropy, the set of joinings with a Bernoulli shift is either a singleton (the product) or contains a non‑trivial factor joining. Applications include the classification of joinings for horocycle flows on compact surfaces of constant negative curvature and, in higher‑rank abelian actions, the measure‑rigidity results of Einsiedler, Katok and Lindenstrauss, where the classification of joinings for diagonalizable actions on homogeneous spaces leads to rigidity theorems for measures invariant under a higher‑rank torus action with positive entropy.

Ornstein's Isomorphism Theorem and the Classification of Bernoulli Shifts

Ornstein’s isomorphism theorem (1970) is a landmark result stating that two Bernoulli shifts are isomorphic as measure‑preserving systems if and only if they have the same entropy. A Bernoulli shift is a system (A^ℤ, μ^ℤ, σ) where A is a finite or countable alphabet, μ a probability vector on A, and σ the left shift. The theorem implies that entropy is a complete invariant for the class of Bernoulli systems. The proof constructs a finitary isomorphism by a delicate matching of blocks, using the notion of very weak Bernoulli (VWB) partitions. A partition ξ is VWB if for any ε>0 there exists N such that for all n≥N the conditional distribution of ξ0 given ξ-n-1 is ε‑close in total variation to the unconditional distribution. Ornstein showed that every Bernoulli shift admits a generating VWB partition, and any two VWB partitions with the same entropy can be matched by a finitary code. The theorem extends to the class of K‑systems (Kolmogorov systems) via Sinai’s result that every K‑system is a factor of a Bernoulli shift, and the subsequent work of Ornstein and Weiss showing that a K‑system with the same entropy as a Bernoulli shift is actually isomorphic to it. The classification has profound consequences: it provides a concrete model for any system with completely positive entropy, and it underlies the proof that the time‑1 map of a geodesic flow on a compact surface of negative curvature is a Bernoulli shift. In the non‑invertible setting, one‑sided Bernoulli shifts are classified by entropy as well, and the natural extension yields the two‑sided classification. Modern refinements include the finitary isomorphism theory of Keane and Smorodinsky and the development of Ornstein theory for amenable group actions by Ornstein and Weiss.

Technique: Using Joinings to Prove Unique Ergodicity
Let (X,T) be a minimal distal system and (Y,S) a weakly mixing system. Show that the only T×S‑invariant measure on X×Y with marginals μ (the unique T‑invariant measure on X) and ν (an S‑invariant measure on Y) is the product measure μ×ν.
Since (X,T) is minimal and distal, it has discrete spectrum. By Furstenberg’s disjointness theorem, any weakly mixing system is disjoint from any system with discrete spectrum. Therefore the set of joinings J(μ,ν) consists only of the product measure μ×ν. Hence the product is the unique joining, which is exactly the statement that the only T×S‑invariant measure with the given marginals is μ×ν.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks to prove uniqueness of an invariant measure for a product system, first identify the spectral type of each factor. If one factor is weakly mixing and the other has discrete spectrum, invoke Furstenberg disjointness immediately. Remember that disjointness is symmetric and that the product measure is always a joining; the theorem tells you it is the only one.

Shannon–McMillan–Breiman Theorem and Typical Sets

The Shannon–McMillan–Breiman theorem (also called the asymptotic equipartition property) gives a pointwise description of the entropy of a measure‑preserving system. Let (X,μ,T) be an ergodic system and let ξ be a finite measurable partition. For μ‑almost every x, the information function I_ξ^n(x) = −log μ(ξ^n(x)) satisfies (1/n) I_ξ^n(x) → h_μ(T,ξ) as n→∞, where ξ^n = ⋁k=0n-1 T-kξ and h_μ(T,ξ) is the entropy of T with respect to ξ. If ξ is a generating partition, the limit equals the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy h_μ(T). The theorem implies that for large n the space X can be partitioned, up to a set of small measure, into approximately en h_μ(T) atoms of ξ^n each having measure roughly e-n h_μ(T). These atoms are called typical sequences or typical sets. The result is the measure‑theoretic analogue of the classical Shannon–McMillan theorem for stationary processes and provides the foundation for the definition of metric entropy via the limit of (1/n) H_μ(ξ^n). In the context of smooth dynamical systems, the theorem combines with the Brin–Katok local entropy formula to give a geometric interpretation: for a C1 diffeomorphism preserving an ergodic measure μ, the local entropy at μ‑a.e. point equals the metric entropy. The theorem also underlies the construction of measure‑theoretic entropy for actions of amenable groups and for sofic groups. A common pitfall is to assume the convergence holds uniformly; it is only almost sure and in L1. The rate of convergence is related to the central limit theorem for the information function, which holds for systems with sufficient mixing (e.g., Bernoulli shifts).

Effective Ergodic Theorems and Spectral Gap Estimates

Classical ergodic theorems guarantee almost sure convergence of time averages but give no quantitative rate. Effective ergodic theory seeks explicit bounds on the speed of convergence in terms of spectral properties of the Koopman operator U_T f = f∘T on L20(μ) (the subspace of zero‑mean functions). If U_T has a spectral gap, i.e., its spectrum on L20(μ) is contained in a disk of radius ρ<1, then for any f∈L20(μ) one has the exponential bound ‖(1/N)∑n=0N-1 U_T^n f‖2 ≤ C ρ^N ‖f‖2. This yields a quantitative mean ergodic theorem. For uniformly hyperbolic systems (Anosov diffeomorphisms, geodesic flows on negatively curved manifolds) the transfer operator acting on suitable anisotropic Banach spaces has a spectral gap, leading to exponential decay of correlations and effective ergodic theorems with explicit constants. In the setting of homogeneous dynamics, the spectral gap for the action of a semisimple Lie group on L20(G/Γ) (property (T) or property (τ)) gives effective equidistribution of orbits: for a unipotent flow, the rate is polynomial (typically N-δ) and can be derived from the representation theory of SL2(ℝ). Quantitative recurrence results, such as the effective Poincaré recurrence theorem of Boshernitzan, bound the measure of points that have not returned to a set A by time N in terms of the entropy and the dimension of the system. In number theory, effective ergodic theorems for horocycle flows yield explicit error terms in the equidistribution of closed horocycles, which translate into effective versions of Duke’s theorem on the distribution of Heegner points. A key technique is the use of Sobolev norms on the space of automorphic forms to control the contribution of the continuous spectrum.

Ergodic Theory of Amenable Group Actions and Sofic Entropy

While classical ergodic theory focuses on ℤ‑actions (single transformations), many natural dynamical systems arise from actions of countable amenable groups G (e.g., ℤ^d, the lamplighter group). For an amenable group, one fixes a Følner sequence (F_n) and defines the entropy of a measure‑preserving G‑action (X,μ,{T_g}) with respect to a finite partition ξ as h_μ(G,ξ) = limn→∞ (1/|F_n|) H_μ(⋁g∈F_n T_g-1ξ). The limit exists and is independent of the Følner sequence. The variational principle extends: topological entropy of a continuous G‑action on a compact metric space equals the supremum of measure‑theoretic entropies over invariant probability measures. The isomorphism theory for Bernoulli shifts over amenable groups was developed by Ornstein and Weiss, who proved that two Bernoulli shifts over the same amenable group are isomorphic iff they have the same entropy. For non‑amenable groups, classical entropy fails; Bowen introduced sofic entropy for actions of sofic groups (a class containing all amenable and all residually finite groups). Sofic entropy is defined via approximations by finite permutations (sofic approximations) and coincides with classical entropy for amenable groups. It has been used to prove rigidity results for actions of higher‑rank lattices where classical entropy is zero but sofic entropy distinguishes actions. A major open problem is whether sofic entropy satisfies a variational principle for all sofic groups. Recent work by Kerr and Li established a variational principle for sofic topological entropy defined via continuous maps into finite metric spaces. The theory of joinings and disjointness also extends to amenable group actions, with the notion of relatively independent joinings playing a central role in the structure theory of factors.

  • Confusing the definition of a joining with that of a factor map; a joining is a measure on the product, not a map between the spaces.
  • Assuming Ornstein’s theorem applies to all K‑systems without checking the very weak Bernoulli condition; some K‑systems are not Bernoulli.
  • Applying the Shannon–McMillan–Breiman theorem to non‑ergodic measures; the limit then equals the conditional entropy given the invariant σ‑algebra, not the global entropy.
  • Using a spectral gap on L2 without verifying that the observable lies in the appropriate Banach space; for hyperbolic systems the gap holds on anisotropic spaces, not on L2.
  • Treating sofic entropy as a direct generalization of Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy for all groups; it is only defined for sofic groups and may be infinite or undefined otherwise.
  • Neglecting the dependence of the Følner sequence in the definition of entropy for amenable groups; the limit is independent but the rate of convergence can vary.
  • Overlooking that Furstenberg disjointness requires one system to be weakly mixing and the other to have discrete spectrum; it does not hold for arbitrary mixing versus distal pairs.

Thermodynamic Formalism and Equilibrium States

The thermodynamic formalism transfers concepts from statistical mechanics — pressure, free energy, equilibrium states — into the setting of measure‑preserving dynamical systems. For a continuous map T on a compact metric space X and a continuous potential φ : X → ℝ, the topological pressure P(φ) is defined by the variational principle

P(φ) = sup { h_μ(T) + ∫ φ dμ : μ ∈ M_T(X) }

where M_T(X) denotes the set of T‑invariant Borel probability measures and h_μ(T) is the measure‑theoretic entropy. A measure attaining the supremum is called an equilibrium state for φ. When φ ≡ 0, pressure reduces to topological entropy and equilibrium states are measures of maximal entropy.

Existence of equilibrium states follows from upper semicontinuity of the entropy map μ ↦ h_μ(T) on expansive systems (e.g., subshifts of finite type, Axiom A diffeomorphisms). Uniqueness is more delicate: for Hölder continuous potentials on a topologically mixing subshift of finite type, the Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius theorem provides a unique equilibrium state which is a Gibbs measure. The Gibbs property means there exist constants C > 0 and P = P(φ) such that for every cylinder set [x0 … xn-1]

C-1 ≤ μ([x0 … xn-1]) exp( - nP + ∑k=0n-1 φ(T^k x) ) ≤ C

for all x in the cylinder. This exponential control yields exponential decay of correlations and a central limit theorem for Hölder observables.

Phase transitions occur when the pressure function t ↦ P(t φ) fails to be real‑analytic. For the Manneville–Pomeau map x ↦ x + x1+α (mod 1) with α ∈ (0,1), the potential φ = - log |T'| is known to exhibit a first‑order phase transition at t = 1: for t < 1 there is a unique equilibrium state absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue, while for t ≥ 1 the equilibrium state is the Dirac measure at the indifferent fixed point. Such phenomena are central in the study of multifractal spectra and dimension theory.

Applications extend to number theory: the equilibrium state for the potential φ = - s log |T'| on the Gauss map T(x) = {1/x} yields the Hausdorff dimension of sets of continued fraction expansions with prescribed growth rates. The Bowen formula dim_H J = inf { s : P(-s log |T'|) ≤ 0 } links pressure zeroes to fractal dimensions of invariant sets for conformal repellers.

Ergodic Optimization and Maximizing Measures

Ergodic optimization asks: given a continuous observable f : X → ℝ on a compact dynamical system (X,T), which invariant measures maximize the integral ∫ f dμ? The supremum β(f) = supμ ∈ M_T(X) ∫ f dμ is the maximizing ergodic average. A measure attaining this supremum is a maximizing measure. Unlike equilibrium states, maximizing measures need not be unique; the set of maximizing measures is a non‑empty, compact, convex subset of M_T(X).

For a generic continuous function (in the Baire sense) on a system with the specification property, the maximizing measure is unique and supported on a single periodic orbit. This result, due to Contreras, Lopes, and Thieullen, shows that “typical” observables are optimized by simple periodic behaviour. However, for specific observables arising from geometric problems — e.g., f(x) = - d(x, A) for a closed set A — maximizing measures can be highly complex, often supported on Cantor‑like invariant sets.

The dual problem involves sub‑actions (or calibrated sub‑actions): a continuous function u : X → ℝ satisfying u(Tx) - u(x) ≤ f(x) - β(f) for all x. The existence of a continuous sub‑action is equivalent to the existence of a maximizing measure, and any maximizing measure is supported on the set where equality holds. This is the dynamical analogue of the Lax–Oleinik formula in weak KAM theory.

In the setting of expanding maps or subshifts of finite type, the maximizing measure can be approximated by periodic orbits via the “max‑plus” spectral radius of the transfer operator L_f g(x) = maxTy=x ( ef(y) g(y) ). The max‑plus eigenvalue equals eβ(f), and eigenfunctions correspond to sub‑actions. This tropical (max‑plus) linear algebra provides effective algorithms for computing β(f) and the maximizing measure.

Applications include the study of Lyapunov exponents for matrix cocycles: the top Lyapunov exponent of a locally constant SL(2,ℝ) cocycle over a shift is the maximizing ergodic average of the logarithm of the norm. Ergodic optimization also underlies the “zero‑temperature limit” of equilibrium states: as t → ∞, equilibrium states for t f converge to maximizing measures for f, linking thermodynamic formalism to optimization.

Worked Example: Maximizing Measure for a Piecewise Affine Map
Let T : [0,1] → [0,1] be the tent map T(x) = 2x for x ≤ 1/2 and T(x) = 2(1-x) for x ≥ 1/2. Consider the observable f(x) = x. Determine the maximizing ergodic average β(f) and describe all maximizing measures.
The observable f(x)=x attains its maximum 1 at x=1, but 1 is not invariant (T(1)=0). The fixed points are 0 and 2/3. The Dirac measure δ2/3 gives ∫ f dδ2/3 = 2/3. The period‑2 orbit {2/5, 4/5} yields average (2/5+4/5)/2 = 3/5. For any invariant measure μ, the integral ∫ x dμ is the barycenter of μ. Since T is piecewise expanding, the maximizing measure for a Lipschitz observable is supported on a periodic orbit (a general result for such maps). Checking periodic orbits of low period shows that 2/3 is the highest possible average. Indeed, β(f) = 2/3 and the unique maximizing measure is δ2/3. This illustrates that for expansive systems with specification, a continuous observable always attains its supremum on the compact space of invariant measures, and the maximizer is often a simple periodic orbit measure.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the maximizing measure, first check whether the observable attains its maximum on a periodic orbit. If the system has specification, a generic observable has a unique maximizing measure supported on a single periodic orbit. For specific observables, construct a sub‑action or use the max‑plus transfer operator to bound β(f). Remember that a maximizing measure always exists for continuous observables on compact systems because the space of invariant measures is compact and the integral functional is continuous. Non‑attainment can only occur for observables that are not upper semicontinuous.

Random Dynamical Systems and the Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem

A random dynamical system (RDS) is generated by a measurable family of maps T_ω : X → X indexed by a probability space (Ω, ℱ, ℙ) with an ergodic, invertible base transformation θ : Ω → Ω. The cocycle property Tθ^n ω ∘ … ∘ T_ω = Tω(n) holds for all n ≥ 1. When each T_ω is a diffeomorphism of a compact Riemannian manifold, the derivative cocycle D T_ω(n)(x) acts on the tangent bundle. The Multiplicative Ergodic Theorem (MET) of Oseledets states that for ℙ‑a.e. ω and μ‑a.e. x (where μ is an invariant measure for the skew‑product), there exists a splitting of the tangent space

T_x X = E1(ω,x) ⊕ … ⊕ E_k(ω,x)

and real numbers λ1 > … > λ_k (the Lyapunov exponents) such that for any non‑zero v ∈ E_i

limn→±∞ (1/n) log ‖ D T_ω(n)(x) v ‖ = λ_i

The dimensions of the subspaces are constant on a set of full measure. The MET provides the foundation for the theory of non‑uniform hyperbolicity in random settings.

For linear cocycles over a shift, e.g., A_ω ∈ GL(d,ℝ) i.i.d., the top Lyapunov exponent λ1 equals the limit limn→∞ (1/n) log ‖ Aθn-1ω … A_ω ‖. Furstenberg’s formula expresses λ1 as an integral over the stationary measure on the projective space. Positivity of λ1 is equivalent to the absence of a common invariant proper subspace for the support of the distribution of A_ω (Furstenberg’s criterion).

Random dynamical systems also admit a thermodynamic formalism. For a random potential φ_ω, one defines the relative pressure P_θ(φ) and equilibrium states as invariant measures for the skew‑product maximizing h_μ + ∫ φ dμ. The variational principle holds under mild conditions, and uniqueness of equilibrium states for Hölder potentials follows from a random Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius theorem.

Applications include stochastic stability of hyperbolic attractors: if a deterministic diffeomorphism T has a Sinai–Ruelle–Bowen (SRB) measure, then small random perturbations preserve the SRB measure as the unique physical measure. The MET also underpins the theory of random matrix products, which models disordered quantum systems and wireless communication channels.

Ergodic Theory of Non‑Uniformly Hyperbolic Systems (Pesin Theory)

Pesin theory extends the uniform hyperbolicity framework to systems where Lyapunov exponents are non‑zero almost everywhere but the hyperbolic splitting may vary measurably and the expansion/contraction rates are not uniformly bounded away from 1. For a C1+α diffeomorphism T preserving an ergodic measure μ with all Lyapunov exponents non‑zero, the Oseledets splitting yields stable and unstable manifolds W^s(x), W^u(x) for μ‑a.e. x. These manifolds are immersed C1 disks whose sizes may decay to zero, but they satisfy the absolute continuity property: the holonomy map between transversals preserves the class of sets of zero Lebesgue measure.

The Katok–Strelcyn theorem guarantees that for any ε > 0 there exists a compact set Λ_ε (a Pesin block) of measure > 1‑ε on which the hyperbolic constants (angles, expansion rates, manifold sizes) are uniformly bounded. On Λ_ε the dynamics is uniformly hyperbolic, allowing the construction of Markov partitions and symbolic coding. This yields the existence of at most countably many ergodic SRB measures, each with a product structure of conditional measures on unstable manifolds absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue.

Entropy formula: for a C1+α diffeomorphism and an invariant measure μ, the Ruelle inequality h_μ(T) ≤ ∫ ∑λ_i>0 λ_i dμ holds. Equality (Pesin’s entropy formula) characterizes SRB measures. For surface diffeomorphisms, any invariant measure with positive entropy is hyperbolic and satisfies the entropy formula (Ledrappier–Young).

Non‑uniform hyperbolicity also appears in billiards with cusps (e.g., the Bunimovich stadium) and in the geodesic flow on finite‑volume negatively curved manifolds with cusps. The Chernov–Markarian theory provides polynomial decay of correlations for such systems, contrasting with exponential decay in the uniformly hyperbolic case.

Recent developments include the “Pesin theory for non‑invertible maps” (e.g., interval maps with critical points) where the unstable direction is one‑dimensional and the stable direction is replaced by a measurable partition into local stable sets. The existence of absolutely continuous invariant measures for multimodal interval maps (Lyubich, Avila–Moreira) relies on a combination of Pesin theory and complex analytic techniques.

  • Confusing the Oseledets splitting (measurable, defined almost everywhere) with a continuous dominated splitting; the latter implies uniform hyperbolicity on a compact invariant set.
  • Assuming that positive Lyapunov exponents automatically give an SRB measure; one must verify absolute continuity of conditional measures on unstable manifolds.
  • Applying the entropy formula to measures that are not invariant under a C1+α diffeomorphism; the formula fails for merely C1 maps (counterexamples by Katok).
  • Treating the random MET as a pointwise statement for every ω; it holds only for ℙ‑almost every ω and μ‑almost every x.
  • Believing that maximizing measures may fail to exist for continuous observables on compact systems; existence is guaranteed by compactness of the invariant measure space and continuity of the integral functional.
  • Misidentifying the pressure zero in Bowen’s formula as the Hausdorff dimension for all conformal repellers; the formula requires the potential -s log |T'| and the system to be conformal and expanding.
  • Overlooking that the Gibbs property for equilibrium states on subshifts of finite type requires the potential to be Hölder continuous; weaker regularity may not yield a Gibbs measure.

Infinite Measure Preserving Systems and Conservative Ergodic Theory

Classical ergodic theory assumes a finite invariant measure, often normalized to a probability. Many natural dynamical systems — geodesic flows on finite‑volume non‑compact manifolds, the Boole transformation x ↦ x − 1/x on ℝ, and the Farey map — preserve an infinite σ‑finite measure. The appropriate framework is conservative ergodic theory: a measure‑preserving transformation T on a σ‑finite measure space (X, ℬ, μ) is conservative if for every set A of positive measure, almost every point returns to A infinitely often (Poincaré recurrence holds). The Hopf decomposition splits the space into a conservative part and a dissipative part; on the conservative part one can develop an ergodic theory parallel to the finite‑measure case.

Ratio Ergodic Theorems

Because μ(X) = ∞, the Birkhoff averages (1/n)∑k=0n−1 f∘Tk converge to zero for integrable f. The meaningful limit is given by ratio ergodic theorems (Hopf 1937, Chacon–Ornstein 1960): for f, g ∈ L¹(μ) with ∫ g dμ ≠ 0, the ratio (∑k=0n−1 f∘Tk) / (∑k=0n−1 g∘Tk) converges a.e. to ∫ f dμ / ∫ g dμ. This underpins the study of wandering rates and return sequences.

Infinite Invariant Measures for Markov Shifts

For a countable‑state topological Markov shift with an irreducible transition matrix, the existence of a σ‑finite invariant measure is equivalent to the recurrence/transience classification of the associated random walk. The Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius theorem extends to this setting: a positive eigenfunction for the transfer operator yields an invariant measure, unique up to scaling, provided the Gurevich pressure is finite. The measure is finite iff the shift is positive recurrent; otherwise it is infinite and conservative.

Applications to Number Theory

The Gauss map T(x) = {1/x} on (0,1) preserves the infinite measure dμ = dx/x (and the finite Gauss measure dx/((1+x)log 2)). Its ergodic properties yield the Lévy constant for continued fractions. The geodesic flow on the modular surface SL(2,ℤ)SL(2,ℝ) preserves the finite Liouville measure; conservative ergodic theory underpins the equidistribution of long closed geodesics (Duke, 1988).

Ergodic Theory of Group Actions with Property (T) and Measure Rigidity

Kazhdan’s property (T) for a locally compact group G imposes strong spectral constraints on unitary representations: the trivial representation is isolated in the Fell topology. For measure‑preserving actions of property (T) groups, this translates into spectral gap for the Koopman representation on L²₀(μ) (the orthogonal complement of constants). Consequently, any ergodic action of a property (T) group on a probability space is strongly ergodic: there is no sequence of asymptotically invariant sets of measure bounded away from 0 and 1.

Measure Rigidity for Higher Rank Actions

The seminal work of Furstenberg (1967) on ×2, ×3 actions on the circle, and later of Katok–Spatzier (1996) and Einsiedler–Katok–Lindenstrauss (2006), shows that for actions of higher‑rank abelian groups (e.g., ℤ²) by toral automorphisms, any invariant ergodic measure with positive entropy is algebraic (Haar on a subtorus). Property (T) groups such as SL(n,ℤ) for n ≥ 3 provide a non‑amenable setting where similar rigidity holds: any ergodic invariant measure for the action on the torus 𝕋ⁿ is either Haar or supported on a finite union of rational subtori (Margulis, 1975; Katok–Spatzier). The proof uses the spectral gap to upgrade weak* limits of invariant measures to algebraic ones.

Superrigidity and Orbit Equivalence

Zimmer’s cocycle superrigidity theorem (1980) states that for an ergodic action of a higher‑rank lattice (e.g., SL(n,ℤ), n ≥ 3) on a probability space, any measurable cocycle into a Lie group with no compact factors is cohomologous to a homomorphism. This yields orbit equivalence rigidity: two such actions are orbit equivalent only if they are conjugate up to a finite index subgroup. The combination of property (T), superrigidity, and entropy methods gives a complete classification of invariant measures for many algebraic actions.

Worked Example: Spectral Gap Implies Strong Ergodicity
Let G = SL(3,ℤ) act ergodically on a probability space (X, μ) by measure‑preserving transformations. Show that the action is strongly ergodic: there is no sequence of measurable sets Aₙ with 0 < μ(Aₙ) < 1 and μ(g Aₙ Δ Aₙ) → 0 for all g ∈ G.
Because G has property (T), the Koopman representation π on L²₀(μ) has a spectral gap: there exists ε > 0 such that for any unit vector f ∈ L²₀(μ), supg∈S ‖π(g)f − f‖ ≥ ε for some finite generating set S. Suppose a sequence Aₙ contradicts strong ergodicity. Let fₙ = (1Aₙ − μ(Aₙ))/√(μ(Aₙ)(1−μ(Aₙ))) ∈ L²₀(μ). Then ‖fₙ‖ = 1 and for each g ∈ S, ‖π(g)fₙ − fₙ‖² = 2(1 − ⟨π(g)fₙ, fₙ⟩) = μ(g Aₙ Δ Aₙ)/(μ(Aₙ)(1−μ(Aₙ))) → 0. This contradicts the uniform spectral gap. Hence no such sequence exists; the action is strongly ergodic.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks to prove strong ergodicity for a property (T) group action, immediately invoke the spectral gap on L²₀. Construct normalized indicator functions of the alleged asymptotically invariant sets; their L² norms are 1 and the gap gives a uniform lower bound on displacement, contradicting asymptotic invariance. Remember that property (T) is a representation‑theoretic property; the Koopman representation is unitary and has no invariant vectors in L²₀ because the action is ergodic.

Ergodic Theory of Interval Exchange Transformations and Translation Flows

An interval exchange transformation (IET) is a piecewise isometry of an interval obtained by cutting it into finitely many subintervals and permuting them. IETs are the discrete‑time analogues of translation flows on flat surfaces (translation surfaces). The foundational results: Keane’s conjecture (proved by Masur 1982 and Veech 1982) states that for almost every choice of interval lengths (with respect to Lebesgue measure on the parameter space), the IET is uniquely ergodic. The proof uses the Teichmüller geodesic flow on the moduli space of translation surfaces and the Masur–Veech measure, which is invariant and ergodic for the SL(2,ℝ) action.

Rauzy–Veech Induction and Renormalization

The Rauzy–Veech induction is a renormalization operator on the space of IETs. It produces a sequence of IETs with combinatorial data given by a path in the Rauzy diagram. The associated Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle acts on the first cohomology of the translation surface; its Lyapunov exponents govern the deviation of ergodic averages for the translation flow. Forni (2002) proved that the top exponent is 1 and the second is strictly less than 1, implying weak mixing for almost every IET (Avila–Forni 2007).

Veech Surfaces and Lattice Surfaces

A translation surface is a Veech surface if its SL(2,ℝ) orbit is closed; equivalently, its Veech group is a lattice in SL(2,ℝ). On Veech surfaces, the translation flow is either completely periodic or uniquely ergodic in each direction (Veech dichotomy). The billiard in a regular polygon and the Ehrenfest wind‑tree model are examples. The classification of Veech surfaces in genus 2 was completed by McMullen (2003) using Teichmüller curves.

Deviation of Ergodic Averages

For a typical translation flow, the ergodic averages of a smooth observable deviate from the space average at a rate given by the Lyapunov spectrum of the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle. Zorich (1997) conjectured and Forni proved that the deviation is polynomial with exponent equal to the second Lyapunov exponent. This provides a quantitative refinement of unique ergodicity and connects to the Kochergin mixing phenomenon for flows with saddle singularities.

Ergodic Theory of Skew Products and Cocycle Rigidity

A skew product over a base transformation T : X → X with fiber a group G is a map F(x, g) = (Tx, α(x)g) where α : X → G is a measurable cocycle. The ergodic theory of F is governed by the Lyapunov exponents of the cocycle (Oseledets theorem) and by cocycle rigidity: under what conditions is a measurable cocycle cohomologous to a constant (or to a homomorphism)?

Livšic Theorem for Hyperbolic Systems

For a transitive Anosov diffeomorphism T and a Hölder continuous cocycle α : X → ℝᵈ, the Livšic theorem (1972) states that if the periodic data vanish (i.e., the product of α along every periodic orbit is the identity), then α is a coboundary: there exists a Hölder transfer function u with α(x) = u(Tx)u(x)⁻¹. This extends to matrix‑valued cocycles (Kalinin–Sadovskaya 2006) and to actions of higher‑rank abelian groups (Katok–Spatzier).

Cocycle Superrigidity for Higher Rank Actions

Zimmer’s cocycle superrigidity (1980) applies to ergodic actions of lattices in higher‑rank semisimple Lie groups (e.g., SL(n,ℤ), n ≥ 3). Any measurable cocycle into a Lie group with no compact factors is cohomologous to a homomorphism. This implies orbit equivalence rigidity and measure rigidity for such actions. Recent work by Brown–Fisher–Hurtado (2018) extends superrigidity to actions of mapping class groups and Out(Fn).

Applications to Smooth Rigidity

If a volume‑preserving Anosov diffeomorphism on the torus is smoothly conjugate to a linear automorphism, the conjugacy is forced by the vanishing of certain cocycles (the derivative cocycle). The local rigidity results of de la Llave–Marco–Moriyón (1986) and the global rigidity of Katok–Spatzier (1996) use cocycle rigidity to show that small perturbations of algebraic Anosov actions are smoothly conjugate to the algebraic model.

Quantum Ergodicity and Eigenfunction Delocalization

Quantum ergodicity studies the high‑energy limit of eigenfunctions of the Laplacian (or more general Schrödinger operators) on a compact Riemannian manifold (M, g) whose geodesic flow is ergodic on the unit cotangent bundle S*M. The Quantum Ergodicity Theorem (Shnirelman 1974, Zelditch 1987, Colin de Verdière 1985) states that there exists a density‑one subsequence of eigenfunctions whose Wigner measures converge to the Liouville measure on S*M. In other words, most high‑energy eigenfunctions equidistribute in phase space.

Quantum Unique Ergodicity (QUE)

The stronger conjecture of Quantum Unique Ergodicity (Rudnick–Sarnak 1994) asserts that all eigenfunctions equidistribute. This is known for arithmetic hyperbolic surfaces (Lindenstrauss 2006, using measure classification for higher‑rank diagonalizable actions) and for certain negatively curved manifolds (Anantharaman–Nonnenmacher 2007, using entropy methods). The proof for arithmetic surfaces combines ergodic theory of the geodesic flow with the action of Hecke operators, which provide extra symmetries yielding measure rigidity.

Entropy Bounds and Delocalization

Anantharaman (2008) proved that any semiclassical measure (limit of Wigner measures) has Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy at least half the maximal entropy of the geodesic flow. This implies that eigenfunctions cannot concentrate entirely on a single periodic orbit; they must have a minimal amount of delocalization. For the quantum cat map on the torus, the QUE property holds for all eigenfunctions (Kurlberg–Rudnick 2000) due to the explicit number‑theoretic structure.

Scarring and Counterexamples

On the Bunimovich stadium (a non‑ergodic billiard), there exist scarring eigenfunctions that concentrate on unstable periodic orbits (Heller 1984). On the flat torus, eigenfunctions are plane waves and do not equidistribute (they are periodic). The distinction between ergodic and integrable classical dynamics is sharply reflected in the quantum limit.

  • Confusing the Hopf decomposition for infinite measure systems with the ergodic decomposition for probability spaces; the conservative part need not be ergodic.
  • Assuming that property (T) implies mixing; it only gives a spectral gap, which yields strong ergodicity but not necessarily mixing (e.g., certain compact extensions).
  • Believing that every IET is uniquely ergodic; Keane’s condition (infinite distinct orbits of discontinuities) is necessary, and there are explicit counterexamples with periodic points.
  • Thinking that the Livšic theorem holds for merely continuous cocycles; Hölder regularity (or at least bounded variation) is essential for the transfer function to have the same regularity.
  • Equating quantum ergodicity (density‑one subsequence) with quantum unique ergodicity (all eigenfunctions); the latter is a much stronger statement and is false for generic ergodic systems.
  • Overlooking that the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle acts on the real Hodge bundle, not on the tangent bundle of the moduli space; its Lyapunov exponents are symplectic and come in ± pairs.
  • Misapplying Zimmer’s superrigidity to actions of amenable groups; the theorem requires higher‑rank lattices (or groups with property (T) and no compact factors).

These advanced topics illustrate how ergodic theory interacts with geometry, number theory, and mathematical physics, providing tools essential for modern research and the deepest examination questions.

Measure Classification for Diagonalizable Actions on Homogeneous Spaces

The classification of invariant measures for actions of higher‑rank diagonalizable groups on homogeneous spaces is a cornerstone of modern rigidity theory. Let G be a semisimple Lie group with finite center, Γ a lattice in G, and X = ΓG the associated homogeneous space. A subgroup A ⊂ G consisting of diagonalizable elements (a split torus) acts on X by right translations. The central problem, initiated by Furstenberg and developed by Margulis, Ratner, and later by Einsiedler, Katok, and Lindenstrauss, is to describe all A‑invariant and ergodic probability measures on X.

Einsiedler–Katok–Lindenstrauss Theorem (2006)

If A has rank at least two (i.e., contains a subgroup isomorphic to ℝ² or ℤ²), then any A‑invariant ergodic probability measure on X is either the Haar (Liouville) measure or is supported on a closed orbit of a proper algebraic subgroup containing A. In particular, for the action of the diagonal group on the space of lattices SL(n,ℤ)SL(n,ℝ) with n ≥ 3, every invariant ergodic measure is either Haar or supported on a periodic orbit of a subgroup defined over ℚ. This result yields a measure‑classification analogue of Ratner’s theorem for unipotent flows, but for diagonalizable actions it requires the higher‑rank hypothesis; rank‑one diagonal actions (e.g., geodesic flow on the modular surface) admit many exotic invariant measures.

Application to the Littlewood Conjecture

Einsiedler, Katok, and Lindenstrauss applied this classification to establish that the set of pairs (α,β) ∈ ℝ² for which the Littlewood conjecture fails has Hausdorff dimension zero. The proof embeds the Diophantine problem into the dynamics of the diagonal group on SL(3,ℤ)SL(3,ℝ) and uses the fact that any invariant measure with positive entropy must be Haar. The entropy argument, combined with the measure classification, forces the exceptional set to be small.

Positive Entropy and Rigidity

A key technical ingredient is the connection between entropy and algebraic structure. For a diagonalizable action, if an invariant measure has positive entropy with respect to some element of A, then the measure is forced to be algebraic (Haar on a closed orbit). This “entropy‑rigidity” principle underlies many recent advances, including the work of Lindenstrauss on quantum unique ergodicity for arithmetic surfaces, where the action of Hecke operators provides the higher‑rank diagonalizable group.

Limitations and Open Problems

The classification fails for rank‑one diagonal actions, where the space of invariant measures is vast (e.g., measures supported on closed geodesics, or more exotic constructions). Even in higher rank, the classification of invariant measures for actions of non‑split tori or for actions on non‑homogeneous spaces remains largely open. The “measure rigidity” program continues to explore the boundary between algebraic and non‑algebraic invariant measures.

Poisson Boundary and Furstenberg's Boundary Theory for Random Walks on Groups

Given a countable group G and a probability measure μ on G whose support generates G, the random walk driven by μ defines a Markov chain on G. The asymptotic behavior of this walk is encoded in the Poisson boundary, a measure space (B,ν) equipped with a G‑action such that every bounded μ‑harmonic function on G admits a unique representation as an integral of boundary values. Furstenberg’s seminal insight (1963) was to identify the Poisson boundary with a topological boundary equipped with a stationary measure, thereby linking random walks to the geometry of the group.

Furstenberg Boundary for Semisimple Lie Groups

For a connected semisimple Lie group G with finite center, the Furstenberg boundary is the homogeneous space G/P where P is a minimal parabolic subgroup. The unique μ‑stationary measure on G/P (for any spread‑out μ) is the Poisson boundary. This identification yields the Furstenberg formula for the drift (rate of escape) and the entropy of the random walk in terms of the stationary measure on the boundary.

Poisson Boundary of Discrete Groups

For discrete groups, the Poisson boundary can be highly non‑trivial. Kaimanovich and Vershik (1983) proved that a group has trivial Poisson boundary for some (equivalently, every) non‑degenerate measure iff the group has no non‑constant bounded harmonic functions — a property known as Liouville. Amenable groups are Liouville for symmetric measures, but many non‑amenable groups (e.g., free groups) have rich Poisson boundaries. The boundary can often be realized as the space of ends of the Cayley graph or as the Gromov boundary for hyperbolic groups.

Entropy and the Poisson Boundary

The asymptotic entropy h(μ) of the random walk equals the entropy of the stationary measure on the Poisson boundary (Kaimanovich–Vershik). This equality provides a powerful tool: if one can compute the entropy of the boundary action, one obtains the entropy of the walk. For random walks on SL(2,ℝ) or mapping class groups, the boundary entropy is related to the Lyapunov exponents of the associated cocycle (e.g., the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle for the Teichmüller flow).

Applications to Rigidity

Poisson boundary theory is a key ingredient in measure rigidity. For instance, the work of Bader–Furman (2002) on superrigidity for actions on measure spaces uses the Poisson boundary of the acting group to construct boundary maps that linearize the action. Similarly, the identification of the Poisson boundary for random walks on Out(Fₙ) (by Horbez, 2016) has led to new rigidity results for actions of outer automorphism groups.

Worked Example: Identifying the Poisson Boundary
Let G = SL(2,ℤ) and let μ be the uniform measure on the standard generators S = [[0,-1],[1,0]] and T = [[1,1],[0,1]]. Describe the Poisson boundary of the random walk driven by μ.
The group SL(2,ℤ) acts on the projective line ℙ¹(ℝ) by Möbius transformations. The random walk on SL(2,ℤ) induces a random walk on ℙ¹(ℝ). By Furstenberg's theorem, the Poisson boundary is the space ℙ¹(ℝ) equipped with the unique μ‑stationary measure ν. This measure is the harmonic measure for the random walk on the hyperbolic plane ℍ² (since SL(2,ℤ) acts on ℍ² by isometries). The boundary ∂ℍ² ≅ ℙ¹(ℝ) is the Gromov boundary of the Cayley graph of SL(2,ℤ) (which is quasi‑isometric to a tree). The stationary measure ν is singular with respect to Lebesgue measure and its Hausdorff dimension has been computed explicitly (it lies strictly between 0 and 1). Thus the Poisson boundary is (ℙ¹(ℝ), ν) with the natural SL(2,ℤ) action.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the Poisson boundary of a random walk on a semisimple Lie group, immediately recall the Furstenberg boundary G/P and the uniqueness of the stationary measure. For discrete groups, check whether the group is hyperbolic (then the boundary is the Gromov boundary) or amenable (then the boundary is trivial for symmetric measures). Memorize the Kaimanovich–Vershik entropy formula: h(μ) = H(ν) where ν is the stationary measure on the boundary. This formula often appears in problems linking entropy, drift, and Lyapunov exponents.

KAM Theory and Ergodicity of Hamiltonian Systems

Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM) theory addresses the persistence of quasi‑periodic motions in integrable Hamiltonian systems under small perturbations. Consider a real‑analytic Hamiltonian H = H₀ + εH₁ on a symplectic manifold M of dimension 2n, where H₀ is completely integrable with action‑angle variables (I,θ) ∈ U × 𝕋ⁿ. The unperturbed flow is linear on invariant tori I = const with frequency vector ω(I) = ∂H₀/∂I. KAM theory asserts that for sufficiently small ε, a large measure set of these tori survives as invariant Lagrangian tori carrying quasi‑periodic motion with Diophantine frequencies.

Diophantine Condition and Measure Estimates

A frequency vector ω ∈ ℝⁿ is Diophantine if there exist γ > 0 and τ ≥ n‑1 such that |k·ω| ≥ γ|k|-τ for all k ∈ ℤⁿ{0}. The set of Diophantine vectors has full Lebesgue measure in ℝⁿ. KAM theorems (Kolmogorov 1954, Arnold 1963, Moser 1962) prove that for each Diophantine ω there exists an invariant torus with that frequency, and the union of these tori has measure tending to full measure as ε → 0. The complement (the “chaotic sea”) can have positive measure but is typically small for small ε.

Ergodicity and the Complement of KAM Tori

The surviving KAM tori form a set of invariant tori that are barriers to transport in phase space. In two degrees of freedom (n = 2), these tori are codimension‑one and separate the energy surface, preventing global ergodicity. For n ≥ 3, the tori have codimension ≥ 2 and do not separate the energy surface; Arnold diffusion can occur, allowing orbits to drift across the chaotic region. The ergodic properties of the complement are a major open area: it is conjectured that for generic perturbations the flow on the complement is ergodic (or at least has a dense orbit), but rigorous results are limited to specific models (e.g., the standard map, or a priori unstable systems).

Quantitative KAM and Effective Estimates

Modern quantitative KAM theory (Pöschel 1982, Eliasson 1990) gives explicit bounds on the measure of the complement in terms of ε and the regularity of H₁. For analytic perturbations, the measure of the complement is typically exponentially small in a negative power of ε. These estimates are crucial for applications to celestial mechanics (e.g., stability of the solar system) and for proving the existence of positive Lyapunov exponents on the complement.

Connection to Ergodic Theory

KAM theory provides a mechanism for the coexistence of regular and chaotic dynamics. The invariant tori support purely discrete spectrum (quasi‑periodic motion), while the complement may exhibit mixing, positive entropy, and hyperbolic behavior. The decomposition of phase space into KAM tori and a chaotic component is a concrete realization of the Hopf decomposition for Hamiltonian flows. Understanding the statistical properties of the chaotic component (e.g., decay of correlations, central limit theorems) remains an active research frontier linking KAM theory to smooth ergodic theory.

Young Towers and Exponential Decay of Correlations for Non‑uniformly Hyperbolic Systems

Young towers (or Young’s inducing scheme) provide a powerful framework for proving statistical properties — such as exponential decay of correlations and the central limit theorem — for non‑uniformly hyperbolic dynamical systems. Introduced by Lai‑Sang Young (1998, 1999), the construction builds a countable Markov extension (a “tower”) over a reference set with a return time function, reducing the study of the original system to that of a Gibbs‑Markov map with good distortion control.

Construction of the Tower

Let f : M → M be a C² diffeomorphism preserving an ergodic SRB measure μ. Assume there exists a set Λ ⊂ M with hyperbolic product structure (stable and unstable manifolds) and a return time function R : Λ → ℕ such that the induced map F = f^R : Λ → Λ is a Gibbs‑Markov map: it is uniformly expanding on unstable leaves, has bounded distortion, and the partition into domains of F is countable. The tower is the space Δ = {(x,ℓ) : x ∈ Λ, 0 ≤ ℓ < R(x)} with the map f_Δ(x,ℓ) = (x,ℓ+1) for ℓ+1 < R(x) and f_Δ(x,R(x)-1) = (F(x),0). The projection π : Δ → M, π(x,ℓ) = f^ℓ(x), semi‑conjugates f_Δ to f.

Exponential Tails and Decay of Correlations

If the return time has an exponential tail, i.e., μ({R > n}) ≤ C e-γ n for some γ > 0, then the tower map f_Δ admits a spectral gap on a suitable Banach space of functions (e.g., functions with bounded variation on stable leaves). This spectral gap implies exponential decay of correlations for Hölder observables: |∫ φ·(ψ∘f^n) dμ - ∫ φ dμ ∫ ψ dμ| ≤ C‖φ‖‖ψ‖ e-β n for some β > 0. The same framework yields the central limit theorem, almost sure invariance principle, and large deviation estimates.

Applications to Specific Systems

Young towers have been successfully applied to a wide range of systems: Hénon maps (Benedicks–Young 1993), billiards with convex scatterers (Chernov 1999), the Lorenz attractor (Araújo–Pacifico 2010), and partially hyperbolic systems with mostly expanding center (Alves–Bonatti–Viana 2000). In each case, one constructs a suitable reference set Λ (often a “hyperbolic block” from Pesin theory) and verifies the Gibbs‑Markov property and exponential tail estimates using geometric arguments (e.g., growth of unstable manifolds, distortion bounds).

Polynomial Tails and Sub‑exponential Decay

When the return time has only a polynomial tail μ({R > n}) ≤ C n-β, the tower still yields polynomial decay of correlations with rate n-(β-1). This covers many intermittent maps (e.g., Pomeau–Manneville maps) and certain billiards with cusps. The polynomial case requires more delicate functional analytic tools (e.g., Banach spaces with weaker norms) but the tower structure remains the same.

Darling–Kac Theorem and Limit Laws for Infinite Measure Preserving Systems

For a conservative ergodic measure‑preserving transformation T on a σ‑finite infinite measure space (X,ℬ,μ), the classical Birkhoff averages (1/n)∑k=0n-1 f∘T^k converge to zero for any f ∈ L¹(μ). To obtain non‑trivial limit laws, one must renormalize by a sequence a_n growing slower than n. The Darling–Kac theorem (1957) provides a universal limit distribution for such renormalized sums under a mild regularity condition on the return sequence.

Return Sequences and the Darling–Kac Condition

A sequence a_n is a return sequence for T if for every set A of finite positive measure, (1/a_n)∑k=0n-1 μ(A ∩ T-kA) → 1. The Darling–Kac condition requires that the return sequence be regularly varying: a_n = n^α L(n) with 0 < α < 1 and L slowly varying. Under this condition, for any f ∈ L¹(μ), the normalized sums (1/a_n)∑k=0n-1 f∘T^k converge in distribution to a Mittag‑Leffler distribution of order α multiplied by ∫ f dμ.

Mittag‑Leffler Distribution

The Mittag‑Leffler distribution M_α (0 < α < 1) has Laplace transform E[e-s M_α] = 1/(1+s^α). It interpolates between the exponential distribution (α = 1) and a degenerate distribution at 0 (α → 0). The appearance of this distribution reflects the heavy‑tailed return times to a reference set in infinite measure systems. For the Boole transformation x ↦ x - 1/x on ℝ with Lebesgue measure, the return sequence is a_n ∼ √(2n/π) (α = 1/2) and the limit law is the Mittag‑Leffler distribution of order 1/2.

Ratio Ergodic Theorem and Applications

A closely related result is the ratio ergodic theorem (Hopf 1954): for f,g ∈ L¹(μ) with ∫ g dμ ≠ 0, (∑k=0n-1 f∘T^k) / (∑k=0n-1 g∘T^k) → (∫ f dμ)/(∫ g dμ) almost everywhere. This theorem does not require a return sequence and holds for all conservative ergodic infinite measure systems. It is a key tool in proving the Darling–Kac theorem and in applications to continued fractions (Gauss map with infinite invariant measure) and to the geodesic flow on the modular surface (where the Liouville measure is infinite).

Connection to Ergodic Theory of Markov Shifts

For countable state Markov shifts with an infinite invariant measure, the return sequence can often be computed via the spectral radius of the transition operator. The Darling–Kac theorem then yields limit laws for Birkhoff sums of observables. Recent work (Gouëzel 2004, Melbourne–Terhesiu 2012) has extended these results to non‑Markovian systems using Young towers with polynomial tails, showing that the same Mittag‑Leffler limits arise whenever the induced map has a regularly varying return time.

Ergodic Theory of Dispersing Billiards and Hyperbolic Billiard Maps

Dispersing billiards, introduced by Sinai in the early 1970s, are the paradigmatic examples of uniformly hyperbolic systems with singularities. The phase space consists of a point particle moving freely inside a planar domain with strictly convex scatterers, reflecting elastically at the boundaries. The billiard map T acts on the collision space M = ∂Q × [−π/2, π/2], where ∂Q is the boundary of the billiard table and the second coordinate records the angle of reflection. The map preserves the Liouville measure μ = cos φ dq dφ, which is finite and invariant. The key geometric feature is that the scatterers are strictly convex, which forces nearby trajectories to diverge exponentially after each collision — a mechanism known as the defocusing effect. This yields a non‑uniformly hyperbolic structure with stable and unstable cones invariant under the derivative DT. However, grazing collisions (φ = ±π/2) and corner points create singularity sets where T or its derivative are discontinuous. The modern theory, developed by Chernov, Sinai, Markarian, and others, shows that one can construct a countable Markov partition (a Young tower) with exponential tails for the return time, provided the billiard has finite horizon (the free path length between collisions is uniformly bounded). For finite‑horizon dispersing billiards, the system is a Bernoulli shift, enjoys exponential decay of correlations for Hölder observables, and satisfies a central limit theorem. In the infinite‑horizon case (e.g., the periodic Lorentz gas with unbounded corridors), the return time tail becomes polynomial of order 2, leading to a diffusion limit with a logarithmic correction and a non‑standard central limit theorem. The techniques involve careful control of the growth of derivative along unstable manifolds, distortion bounds for the holonomy maps, and the construction of a suitable Banach space of functions with bounded variation along unstable leaves. These results have been extended to higher‑dimensional dispersing billiards, to billiards with cusps, and to the Boltzmann–Sinai model of hard balls, where the same hyperbolic mechanism underlies the ergodicity of the many‑particle system.

Ergodic Theory of Piecewise Expanding Maps and Transfer Operators

Piecewise expanding maps of the interval (or of higher‑dimensional manifolds) form a broad class of non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems for which the transfer (Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius) operator provides a powerful spectral approach. Let T : [0,1] → [0,1] be a piecewise C² map with a finite partition into monotonic branches on each of which |T′| ≥ λ > 1. The transfer operator L acts on densities f by (Lf)(x) = ∑y:T(y)=x f(y) / |T′(y)|. The invariant density h (if it exists) is a fixed point of L. The classical Lasota–Yorke inequality states that for functions of bounded variation, ‖Lⁿf‖BV ≤ C λ⁻ⁿ‖f‖BV + D‖f‖L¹, where C, D are constants independent of n. This inequality implies that L is quasi‑compact on the Banach space BV, with essential spectral radius at most λ⁻¹ < 1. Consequently, the spectrum of L consists of a simple eigenvalue at 1 (corresponding to the absolutely continuous invariant measure) and the rest of the spectrum contained in a disk of radius < 1. The spectral gap yields exponential decay of correlations for observables of bounded variation against L∞ observables, a central limit theorem, and a local limit theorem. In higher dimensions, one replaces BV by spaces of functions with bounded anisotropic norms (e.g., the spaces introduced by Gouëzel–Liverani or by Baladi–Tsujii). The same strategy applies to piecewise expanding maps on manifolds with singularities, such as the Lorenz‑type maps or certain partially hyperbolic attractors. The transfer operator method also underpins the rigorous numerical approximation of invariant densities via Ulam’s method, where the convergence rate is governed by the spectral gap. Recent work has extended these techniques to random piecewise expanding maps, where the transfer operator becomes a cocycle over a base dynamics, and to open systems where one studies conditionally invariant measures and escape rates.

Spectral Gap Estimation for Transfer Operators
Let T be a piecewise C² expanding map of the interval with two full branches, each with constant slope 2. Show that the transfer operator L acting on BV has a spectral gap and compute the essential spectral radius.
For a full‑branch map with constant slope s = 2, the transfer operator is (Lf)(x) = ½[f(x/2) + f((x+1)/2)]. The Lasota–Yorke inequality on BV reads Var(Lf) ≤ ½ Var(f) + C‖f‖₁. Iterating gives Var(Lⁿf) ≤ 2⁻ⁿ Var(f) + C'‖f‖₁. Hence the essential spectral radius on BV is at most ½. Since L1 = 1, the eigenvalue 1 is simple and isolated. The spectral gap is the distance between 1 and the rest of the spectrum, which is at least ½. Therefore L has a spectral gap of size ≥ ½.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the spectral gap of a transfer operator, first write the Lasota–Yorke inequality for the relevant function space (BV, Hölder, or anisotropic). Identify the contraction factor λ⁻¹ from the expansion rate; that factor is the essential spectral radius. The gap is then 1 − λ⁻¹ (or larger if additional eigenvalues are known). Remember that the inequality must be stated with explicit constants to earn full marks.

Ergodic Theory of Non‑Amenable Group Actions: Cost, Fixed Price, and Orbit Equivalence

The study of measure‑preserving actions of non‑amenable groups has been revolutionized by Gaboriau’s theory of cost, introduced in the late 1990s. For a free, ergodic, probability‑measure‑preserving action of a countable group Γ on a standard probability space (X, μ), the cost C(Γ) is the infimum of the expected degree of a graphing that generates the orbit equivalence relation. A graphing is a countable family of partial Borel isomorphisms whose generated equivalence relation coincides with the orbit relation. The cost is an invariant of the orbit equivalence relation, not of the group itself. For amenable groups, the cost is always 1 (Ornstein–Weiss). For non‑amenable groups, the cost can be strictly larger than 1; for a free group Fr of rank r ≥ 2, the cost equals r. A group Γ has fixed price if every free ergodic action has the same cost; this cost is then an invariant of the group. Gaboriau proved that any group with Kazhdan’s property (T) has fixed price, and the cost equals 1 + β₁⁽²⁾(Γ), where β₁⁽²⁾ is the first ℓ²‑Betti number. In particular, lattices in higher‑rank semisimple Lie groups (e.g., SL(n,ℤ) for n ≥ 3) have fixed price 1. Orbit equivalence rigidity asks when two actions of possibly different groups give rise to isomorphic orbit equivalence relations. Popa’s deformation/rigidity theory and Ioana’s work show that for many groups with property (T) or with a non‑amenable normal subgroup, orbit equivalence implies conjugacy of the actions up to a compact perturbation. The cost also controls the number of generators needed for the equivalence relation: a relation of cost < 2 is treeable (generated by a free action of a free group), while cost ≥ 2 allows more complex structures. Recent developments include the computation of cost for actions on boundaries of hyperbolic groups, the connection with the first ℓ²‑Betti number of the group, and the extension of cost to measured groupoids and to actions on infinite measure spaces.

Ergodic Theory of Zimmer's Cocycle Superrigidity and Rigidity of Higher Rank Actions

Zimmer’s program, initiated in the late 1970s, aims to extend Margulis’ superrigidity theorem from linear representations to measurable cocycles arising from group actions. Let Γ be a lattice in a higher‑rank semisimple Lie group G (real rank ≥ 2). A measurable cocycle over a Γ‑action on a probability space (X, μ) with values in a Lie group H is a map α : Γ × X → H satisfying the cocycle identity α(γ₁γ₂, x) = α(γ₁, γ₂·x) α(γ₂, x). Zimmer’s cocycle superrigidity theorem states that if H has no compact factors and the Γ‑action is ergodic, then any such cocycle is cohomologous to a homomorphism ρ : Γ → H that extends continuously to G. In other words, there exists a measurable map C : X → H such that α(γ, x) = C(γ·x)⁻¹ ρ(γ) C(x) for almost every x. This result has profound consequences for the classification of actions of higher‑rank lattices. For instance, any ergodic action of Γ on a compact manifold preserving a volume form is, up to finite index and finite cover, algebraically conjugate to an action by affine transformations on a torus or to a homogeneous action on G/P. The proof combines the amenability of the Furstenberg boundary G/P, the existence of a Γ‑equivariant measurable map from the boundary to the space of probability measures on H, and the algebraic structure of H. Extensions by Furman, Bader–Furman, and others have replaced the target group H by more general algebraic groups over local fields, and have treated cocycles with values in diffeomorphism groups of manifolds, leading to rigidity results for actions on the circle and on surfaces. The cocycle superrigidity theorem also underlies the measure‑rigidity theorems for diagonalizable actions on homogeneous spaces (Einsiedler–Katok–Lindenstrauss) and the proof of the Littlewood conjecture for generic points. A key technical tool is the use of the Poisson boundary of Γ (which coincides with the Furstenberg boundary G/P) to integrate the cocycle and produce the algebraic homomorphism.

Ergodic Theory of Topological Entropy and the Variational Principle for Non‑Compact Phase Spaces

The classical variational principle asserts that for a continuous map T on a compact metric space X, the topological entropy htop(T) equals the supremum of measure‑theoretic entropies hμ(T) over all T‑invariant Borel probability measures μ. When X is non‑compact, several inequivalent definitions of topological entropy exist (Bowen’s definition via separated sets, the definition via open covers, and the definition via the growth rate of periodic orbits). Bowen’s definition, which uses (n, ε)‑separated sets without requiring compactness, yields a notion that coincides with the compact case when X is compact but can be infinite for non‑compact systems. The variational principle fails in general for Bowen entropy: there are examples where htop(T) = ∞ while all invariant probability measures have finite entropy. To recover a variational principle, one can restrict to measures with finite entropy or consider the supremum over all invariant measures (including infinite ones) after normalizing by the measure of a reference set. Another approach, due to Pesin and Pitskel, defines topological entropy for non‑compact systems via the exponential growth rate of the number of (n, ε)‑separated orbits that stay within a given compact subset; this “local” entropy satisfies a variational principle with measures supported on that compact set. For systems with a natural compactification (e.g., the geodesic flow on a finite‑volume non‑compact manifold), the entropy at infinity can be analyzed via the Bowen–Margulis measure on the unit tangent bundle of the compactified space. Recent work by Buzzi, Fisher, and others has developed a thermodynamic formalism for non‑compact systems using inducing schemes and Young towers, leading to a variational principle for the pressure function and the existence of equilibrium states for potentials with summable variations. These results are crucial for the ergodic theory of interval maps with indifferent fixed points, for the Teichmüller flow on moduli space, and for the study of billiards with cusps where the phase space has infinite volume but a natural compactification by adding the collision manifolds at infinity.

  • Believing that Zimmer’s cocycle superrigidity holds for all target Lie groups; the theorem requires the target to have no compact factors and the acting group to be a lattice in a higher‑rank semisimple Lie group.
  • Thinking that the variational principle for topological entropy holds verbatim for Bowen’s definition on non‑compact spaces; counterexamples show the supremum over probability measures can be strictly smaller than Bowen entropy.

Ergodic Theory of Partially Hyperbolic Systems and Accessibility

A partially hyperbolic diffeomorphism f on a compact manifold M admits a continuous Df-invariant splitting of the tangent bundle TM = Es ⊕ Ec ⊕ Eu where vectors in Es are uniformly contracted, vectors in Eu are uniformly expanded, and the behavior on the center bundle Ec is dominated by the hyperbolic directions: there exist constants 0 < λ < μ < 1 such that ‖Df|Es‖ ≤ λ, ‖Df-1|Eu‖ ≤ λ, and λ ≤ ‖Df|Ec‖ ≤ μ. The center direction may be neutral, weakly expanding, or weakly contracting, and its dynamics is the main source of complexity. A central concept is accessibility: any two points can be joined by a piecewise smooth path whose tangent vectors lie alternately in Es and Eu. The Pugh–Shub conjecture (proved for volume-preserving systems by Burns–Wilkinson and later extended by Avila–Crovisier–Wilkinson) asserts that stable ergodicity is equivalent to accessibility for C2 partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms. Stable ergodicity means that every C1-close volume-preserving diffeomorphism is ergodic. The proof uses the Hopf argument adapted to the center foliation and the fact that accessibility forces the center foliation to be essentially a single leaf modulo measure zero. Important subclasses include dynamically coherent systems where Ec integrates to a foliation, and center-bunched systems where the domination is strong enough to guarantee plaque expansiveness. Classic examples are the time-1 map of an Anosov flow, skew products over Anosov bases with compact fiber isometries, and frame flows on negatively curved manifolds. Open problems include the classification of accessible partially hyperbolic systems up to leaf conjugacy, the ergodicity of all volume-preserving partially hyperbolic diffeomorphisms on the 3-torus, and the existence of non-accessible examples with non-trivial center dynamics.

Ergodic Theory of Large Deviations for Dynamical Systems

Large deviation theory quantifies the exponential decay of probabilities that empirical measures deviate from their typical behavior. For a dynamical system (X, T, μ) with μ ergodic, the level-2 large deviation principle concerns the sequence of empirical measures Ln = (1/n) Σk=0n-1 δTkx. Under suitable hyperbolicity or expansion assumptions (e.g., uniformly expanding maps, Axiom A diffeomorphisms, or systems with a Young tower with exponential tails), the family {Ln} satisfies a large deviation principle with a good rate function I(ν) = supφ∈C(X) {∫ φ dν - P(φ)} where P(φ) is the topological pressure of the potential φ. This is the Legendre–Fenchel transform of the pressure function, and the Gärtner–Ellis theorem provides the general mechanism: if the scaled cumulant generating function Λ(φ) = limn→∞ (1/n) log ∫ exp(n∫ φ dLn) dμ exists and is differentiable, then the rate function is its Legendre transform. For level-1 large deviations (Birkhoff sums of a single observable), the rate function is the Legendre transform of the free energy function q ↦ P(qψ). Key results by Kifer, Orey, and Comman establish the principle for hyperbolic systems; later work by Young, Melbourne, and Nicol extends it to non-uniformly hyperbolic systems with polynomial tails, where the rate function may have flat pieces reflecting phase transitions. Examiner traps include confusing the level-1 and level-2 rate functions, assuming the Gärtner–Ellis theorem applies without checking the essential smoothness of Λ, and neglecting that the large deviation principle for empirical measures requires the underlying system to have a spectral gap on a suitable Banach space (e.g., Hölder or BV).

Worked Example: Gärtner–Ellis and Level-2 Rate Function
Let T be a topologically mixing subshift of finite type and μ its measure of maximal entropy. Define the scaled cumulant generating function Λ(φ) = limn→∞ (1/n) log ∫ exp(n∫ φ dLn) dμ for φ ∈ C(X). Show that Λ(φ) = P(φ) - P(0) and deduce the level-2 rate function I(ν).
For a subshift of finite type, the pressure function P is real-analytic on C(X). By the variational principle, P(φ) = supν invariant {hν(T) + ∫ φ dν}. The measure μ maximizes entropy, so P(0) = hμ(T). The integral ∫ exp(n∫ φ dLn) dμ equals the partition function for the potential nφ evaluated at μ, which by the Gibbs property is asymptotically exp(nP(φ)). Hence Λ(φ) = P(φ) - P(0). The Gärtner–Ellis theorem then gives the rate function I(ν) = supφ {∫ φ dν - Λ(φ)} = supφ {∫ φ dν - P(φ) + P(0)} = P(0) - infφ {P(φ) - ∫ φ dν}. By the variational principle, infφ {P(φ) - ∫ φ dν} = -hν(T) for ν invariant, and +∞ otherwise. Therefore I(ν) = hμ(T) - hν(T) for ν invariant, and ∞ otherwise. This matches the known level-2 rate function for the measure of maximal entropy.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question asks for a large deviation rate function, first identify the level (level-1 for observables, level-2 for empirical measures). Write down the pressure or free energy function, verify the hypotheses of the Gärtner–Ellis theorem (existence and differentiability of Λ), and then compute the Legendre transform. Remember that for non-uniformly hyperbolic systems the rate function may have linear segments corresponding to phase transitions; mention this nuance to gain extra credit.

Ergodic Theory of Fluctuation Theorems and Entropy Production in Non-Equilibrium Statistical Mechanics

Fluctuation theorems describe the symmetry of the large deviation rate function for the entropy production rate in non-equilibrium steady states. For a reversible Anosov diffeomorphism f preserving a smooth volume, the Gallavotti–Cohen fluctuation theorem states that the rate function ζ(σ) for the time-averaged entropy production σ satisfies ζ(-σ) - ζ(σ) = -σ. Equivalently, the probability of observing a negative entropy production over a long time interval is exponentially smaller than that of the corresponding positive value by a factor exp(-nσ). The entropy production rate is defined via the Jacobian of f with respect to the SRB measure μSRB: σ(x) = -log |det Df(x)|Eu|. For deterministic thermostatted systems (e.g., Gaussian isokinetic thermostats), the Evans–Searles transient fluctuation theorem holds for finite-time averages and implies the steady-state Gallavotti–Cohen symmetry in the limit. Ruelle extended the theorem to Axiom A attractors, showing that the SRB measure is the unique physical measure satisfying the symmetry. The proof uses the Markov partition and the fact that the SRB measure is a Gibbs state for the potential -log |det Df|Eu|. A common examiner error is to assume the fluctuation theorem holds for all invariant measures; it is specific to the SRB (or physical) measure and fails for, say, the measure of maximal entropy unless the system is volume-preserving. Another subtlety: the theorem requires time-reversibility (an involution R with R∘f = f-1∘R); without it, the symmetry is broken and only a weaker inequality holds.

Ergodic Theory of Group Actions on CAT(0) Cube Complexes and Median Spaces

A CAT(0) cube complex is a simply connected cube complex where the link of each vertex is a flag simplicial complex; it admits a natural CAT(0) metric. The Roller boundary ∂RX consists of ultrafilters on the set of halfspaces and carries a natural topology making it a compact totally disconnected space. For a group Γ acting properly and cocompactly on a CAT(0) cube complex X, the Poisson boundary of a random walk on Γ can be identified with a measurable quotient of the Roller boundary (work of Caprace–Sageev, Fernós). This identification yields powerful measure rigidity results: any stationary measure on the Roller boundary is either supported on a finite orbit or is the unique measure of maximal entropy. If Γ has property (T), then every action on a CAT(0) cube complex has a global fixed point (Niblo–Reeves), which implies that lattices in higher-rank semisimple Lie groups cannot act non-trivially on such complexes without fixing a point. The median structure of CAT(0) cube complexes (every triple of points has a unique median) allows the use of median algebras to study orbit equivalence and cost. For instance, the cost of a free action of a free group on a CAT(0) cube complex equals 1 + (rank - 1)/rank, linking geometric group theory with ergodic invariants. Examiner traps include confusing the visual boundary of a CAT(0) space with the Roller boundary of a cube complex, assuming that all groups acting on CAT(0) cube complexes are amenable (they are not; e.g., SL(3,ℤ) acts on a cube complex but has property (T)), and neglecting that the Poisson boundary identification requires the random walk to have finite first moment and the action to be essential (no fixed point at infinity).

Ergodic Theory of Thermodynamic Formalism for Non-Uniformly Hyperbolic Systems

Thermodynamic formalism extends the variational principle to non-uniformly hyperbolic systems by constructing inducing schemes (Young towers) with integrable return times. For a map T with a Young tower (Δ, F, τ) where τ: Δ → ℕ is the return time, the pressure of a potential φ is defined as P(φ) = supμ invariant {hμ(T) + ∫ φ dμ}. If φ has summable variations (Σn varn φ < ∞) and the tower has exponential tails (μ(τ > n) ≤ C e-cn), then there exists a unique equilibrium state μφ which is a Gibbs measure for the induced potential Φ = Σk=0τ-1 φ∘Tk. The work of Pesin–Senti–Zhang shows that for potentials with summable variations on towers with polynomial tails, equilibrium states may not be unique; phase transitions occur when the pressure function fails to be differentiable. Applications include interval maps with indifferent fixed points (Manneville–Pomeau maps) where the potential -t log |T'| has a phase transition at t = 1, and billiards with cusps where the geometric potential -log |det DT|Eu| yields the SRB measure as the unique equilibrium state for t = 1. A frequent examiner mistake is to assume that every Hölder continuous potential has a unique equilibrium state; this is false without the summable variations condition or when the tower has polynomial tails. Another trap is to confuse the pressure defined via separated sets (Bowen's definition) with the variational pressure; they coincide for compact systems but may differ for non-compact phase spaces, requiring the use of the Gurevich pressure for countable Markov shifts.

  • Thinking that every Hölder potential on a non-uniformly hyperbolic system has a unique equilibrium state; summable variations and tail conditions on the inducing scheme are essential.

Ergodic Theory of the Teichmüller Flow and the SL(2,ℝ) Action on Moduli Space

The Teichmüller flow acts on the moduli space of unit‑area Abelian differentials (or quadratic differentials) by stretching the horizontal direction and contracting the vertical direction. This flow is the geodesic flow for the Teichmüller metric and is invariant under the natural SL(2,ℝ) action that rotates and shears the differential. Masur (1982) and Veech (1982) independently proved that the Teichmüller flow is ergodic with respect to the Masur–Veech measure on each connected component of every stratum. The proof uses the fact that the SL(2,ℝ) action is mixing and that the flow is a one‑parameter subgroup of this action. Ratner’s measure classification for unipotent flows does not apply directly because the Teichmüller flow is not unipotent; instead, the ergodicity follows from the Hopf argument applied to the stable and unstable foliations of the flow, which are given by the horizontal and vertical measured foliations. The SL(2,ℝ) action preserves the Masur–Veech measure and is itself ergodic on each component. A key tool is the Veech dichotomy: for any SL(2,ℝ)‑invariant submanifold, either the orbit is closed (a Veech surface) or it is dense and equidistributed with respect to the Masur–Veech measure. This dichotomy has been extended by Eskin–Mirzakhani–Mohammadi (2015) to show that every SL(2,ℝ)‑orbit closure is an affine invariant submanifold, and the action on each such submanifold is ergodic with respect to a natural smooth measure. The Lyapunov spectrum of the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle over the Teichmüller flow provides quantitative information about the deviation of ergodic averages for the vertical flow on translation surfaces. The top Lyapunov exponent equals 1, and the sum of the non‑negative exponents equals the sum of the positive Lyapunov exponents of the cocycle, a result of Forni (2002) and Avila–Viana (2007). These exponents govern the rate of mixing of the Teichmüller flow and the regularity of the invariant foliations.

Ergodic Theory of the Kontsevich–Zorich Cocycle and Lyapunov Exponents

The Kontsevich–Zorich (KZ) cocycle is the linear cocycle over the Teichmüller flow obtained by the action on the first real cohomology of the underlying Riemann surface. It preserves the symplectic intersection form, so its Lyapunov spectrum is symmetric: λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … ≥ λ_g ≥ 0 ≥ -λ_g ≥ … ≥ -λ₁. The top exponent λ₁ is always 1, reflecting the expansion of the horizontal direction. The second exponent λ₂ is strictly less than 1 for all strata except the hyperelliptic components where it can equal 1. Forni (2002) proved that the KZ cocycle satisfies a uniform hyperbolicity condition on a set of full measure, which implies that the Oseledets splitting is dominated and the Lyapunov exponents are simple (no multiplicities) for almost every translation surface. Avila–Viana (2007) established simplicity of the Lyapunov spectrum for the Masur–Veech measure on every connected component of every stratum. The sum of the positive Lyapunov exponents equals the integral of the curvature of the Hodge bundle over the moduli space, a formula due to Kontsevich (1997) and later refined by Eskin–Kontsevich–Zorich (2014). This sum can be computed explicitly for each stratum using the geometry of the boundary divisors. The Lyapunov exponents control the deviation of ergodic averages for the vertical flow: for a typical translation surface, the ergodic averages of a smooth observable along the vertical flow converge with an error of order Tλ₂ (where λ₂ is the second exponent). This polynomial deviation is a hallmark of non‑uniform hyperbolicity. The KZ cocycle also appears in the study of the wind‑tree model and in the analysis of the Lyapunov spectrum of the Rauzy–Veech induction. The simplicity of the spectrum implies that the Rauzy–Veech induction is a Markov map with a spectral gap, which yields exponential mixing for the Teichmüller flow on the space of zippered rectangles.

Let H(2) be the stratum of genus‑2 Abelian differentials with a single zero of order 2. Using the Eskin–Kontsevich–Zorich formula, the sum of the positive Lyapunov exponents is (1/12) Σk=1g (1/(2k-1)) + (1/4) Σboundary divisors (contribution). Ergodic Theory of the Masur–Veech Measure and Its Ergodic Properties

The Masur–Veech measure is the natural SL(2,ℝ)‑invariant probability measure on each connected component of a stratum of Abelian or quadratic differentials. It is constructed by pulling back the Lebesgue measure on period coordinates (the integrals of the differential over a symplectic basis of relative homology) and normalizing to unit total mass on the subset of unit‑area surfaces. Masur (1982) and Veech (1982) proved that this measure is finite and ergodic for the Teichmüller flow. The ergodicity proof uses the fact that the stable and unstable manifolds of the flow are given by the horizontal and vertical foliations, and that the flow is a Bernoulli shift on a suitable symbolic coding (the Rauzy–Veech induction). The measure is also invariant under the full SL(2,ℝ) action, and the action is mixing (Avila–Gouëzel–Yoccoz, 2006). The Masur–Veech measure is the unique measure of maximal entropy for the Teichmüller flow on each component, with entropy equal to the sum of the positive Lyapunov exponents of the KZ cocycle. The measure is also the unique equilibrium state for the geometric potential -log|det DT| on the space of zippered rectangles. The support of the Masur–Veech measure is the whole component, and almost every translation surface is generic for this measure (Birkhoff’s theorem). The measure has a product structure along the stable and unstable foliations, which are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on the leaves. This absolute continuity is a key ingredient in proving the exponential mixing of the Teichmüller flow (Avila–Gouëzel–Yoccoz). The Masur–Veech measure also appears as the limiting distribution of random walks on the mapping class group (Kaimanovich–Masur, 1996) and as the stationary measure for the action of the mapping class group on the Thurston boundary of Teichmüller space.

Ergodic Theory of Random Walks on Mapping Class Groups and the Poisson Boundary of Moduli Space

Let Γ be the mapping class group of a closed surface of genus g ≥ 2, and let μ be a probability measure on Γ whose support generates a non‑elementary subgroup. The random walk driven by μ converges almost surely to a point in the Thurston boundary of Teichmüller space, which can be identified with the space of projective measured foliations. Kaimanovich–Masur (1996) proved that the Poisson boundary of (Γ, μ) is measurably isomorphic to the Thurston boundary equipped with the hitting measure ν. Moreover, ν is the unique μ‑stationary measure on the boundary and is equivalent to the Masur–Veech measure on the space of measured foliations. The entropy of the random walk h(μ) is positive and equals the integral of the logarithm of the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the action of Γ on the boundary. The work of Maher–Tiozzo (2018) shows that for any non‑elementary random walk on the mapping class group, the hitting measure is non‑atomic and gives full measure to the set of uniquely ergodic foliations. The Poisson boundary can also be realized as the space of ends of the curve complex with the harmonic measure. The identification of the Poisson boundary with the Thurston boundary yields rigidity results: any measurable Γ‑equivariant map from the Poisson boundary to a standard Borel space with a stationary measure is essentially constant (Kaimanovich). This implies that the action of Γ on the Poisson boundary is doubly ergodic. The entropy h(μ) can be computed via the formula h(μ) = ∫PMF I(ξ) dν(ξ), where I(ξ) is the intersection number of the random walk with the foliation ξ. For the simple random walk on the mapping class group, the entropy is known to be positive but its exact value is unknown. The Poisson boundary of the mapping class group is also the boundary of the Teichmüller geodesic flow, linking the random walk to the Teichmüller flow via the work of Eskin–Mirzakhani–Rafi (2018).

Ergodic Theory of Group Actions on Buildings and Affine Buildings

Buildings are simplicial complexes that generalize symmetric spaces and serve as geometric models for groups with a BN‑pair, such as Chevalley groups over local fields. An affine building is a building of type Ã_n, B̃_n, etc., and carries a natural CAT(0) metric. Groups acting properly and cocompactly on affine buildings include uniform lattices in p‑adic Lie groups and certain Kac–Moody groups. The ergodic theory of such actions parallels that of higher‑rank Lie groups. The Poisson boundary of a random walk on a group acting on an affine building can be identified with the visual boundary of the building (the space of chambers at infinity) equipped with the hitting measure. This identification, due to Kaimanovich (1994) and later refined by Caprace–Lécureux (2011), requires the random walk to have finite first moment and the action to be strongly transitive. The boundary carries a natural structure of a spherical building, and the stationary measure is the unique measure of maximal entropy for the action. If the group has property (T), then any action on an affine building has a global fixed point (Caprace–Monod, 2009), which implies that lattices in higher‑rank p‑adic groups cannot act non‑trivially without fixing a point. For groups without property (T), such as SL(n, 𝔽_q((t))), the action on the associated building is amenable and the Poisson boundary is the full visual boundary. The measure rigidity results of Bader–Furman (2012) show that any ergodic invariant measure for the action of a higher‑rank lattice on a compact space is either algebraic or supported on a finite orbit. The cost of a free action of a free group on an affine building equals 1 + (rank - 1)/rank, linking the geometric rank of the building to the ergodic invariant cost. The median structure of the building (every triple of points has a unique median) allows the use of median algebras to study orbit equivalence. A frequent examiner trap is to confuse the visual boundary of a CAT(0) space with the spherical building at infinity of an affine building; they coincide as sets but carry different topologies and measure classes.

  • Assuming the Masur–Veech measure is the only SL(2,ℝ)‑invariant measure on a stratum; there exist other invariant measures supported on affine invariant submanifolds (Eskin–Mirzakhani–Mohammadi).
  • Confusing the Lyapunov exponents of the Kontsevich–Zorich cocycle with those of the Teichmüller flow itself; the flow has only one positive exponent (the expansion rate), while the KZ cocycle has g positive exponents.
  • Believing that the Poisson boundary of the mapping class group is the same as the Thurston boundary as a topological space; they are measurably isomorphic but the hitting measure is singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure on projective measured foliations.
  • Thinking that every random walk on a group acting on an affine building has the visual boundary as its Poisson boundary; the finite first moment condition and strong transitivity are essential.
  • Neglecting that the simplicity of the KZ Lyapunov spectrum (Avila–Viana) holds for the Masur–Veech measure but may fail for other invariant measures.
  • Overlooking that the Teichmüller flow is not a unipotent flow, so Ratner’s theorems do not apply directly; ergodicity is proved via the Hopf argument and the Veech dichotomy.
  • Confusing the cost of an action on a building with the cost of the group; the cost depends on the geometry of the building and the rank of the action.

SRB Measures and Physical Measures for Non‑uniformly Hyperbolic Systems

Sinai–Ruelle–Bowen (SRB) measures are invariant probability measures that describe the statistical behavior of typical orbits in dissipative chaotic systems. For a C2 diffeomorphism f on a compact manifold M, an SRB measure μ is characterized by the property that its conditional measures on unstable manifolds are absolutely continuous with respect to the Riemannian volume on those manifolds. This absolute continuity implies that for Lebesgue‑almost every point x in a neighborhood of the attractor, the time averages of continuous observables converge to the space average with respect to μ; such measures are called physical measures.

The existence theory splits into two major settings. For uniformly hyperbolic (Axiom A) attractors, Sinai (1972) and Ruelle (1976) proved that there is a unique SRB measure supported on each basic set, and it is ergodic and mixing. Bowen (1975) extended the construction to Axiom A flows. For non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems, the breakthrough came with the work of Pesin (1977) on the existence of stable and unstable manifolds for almost every point with respect to any invariant measure with non‑zero Lyapunov exponents. However, the existence of an SRB measure required additional geometric structure. Young (1998) introduced Young towers with exponential tails, proving that any diffeomorphism admitting such a tower possesses an SRB measure. Later, Alves, Bonatti, and Viana (2000) showed that non‑uniformly expanding maps with a slow recurrence condition to the critical set also admit SRB measures.

Key properties of SRB measures include:

  • Ergodicity and mixing: For Axiom A systems the SRB measure is Bernoulli; for non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems ergodicity follows from the Hopf argument applied to the absolutely continuous conditional measures on unstable leaves.
  • Entropy formula: The metric entropy hμ(f) equals the sum of positive Lyapunov exponents (Pesin’s entropy formula), which holds for any SRB measure.
  • Statistical stability: Under small C1 perturbations, the SRB measure varies continuously in the weak* topology for Axiom A systems; for non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems statistical stability holds under stronger assumptions (e.g., uniform hyperbolicity on a large set).
  • Basin of attraction: The basin B(μ) = {x ∈ M : time averages converge to μ} has positive Lebesgue measure; in many cases it has full Lebesgue measure in a neighborhood of the attractor.

Important open problems include the classification of systems with multiple physical measures (e.g., the existence of infinitely many SRB measures for certain partially hyperbolic systems) and the relationship between SRB measures and the physical measure concept for systems with intermingled basins (Kan, 1994). The Ledrappier–Young formula (1985) gives a precise dimension formula for SRB measures in terms of Lyapunov exponents and pointwise dimensions on stable and unstable manifolds.

Thermodynamic Formalism for Countable Markov Shifts

Thermodynamic formalism extends the classical theory of equilibrium states from finite‑alphabet shifts of finite type to countable Markov shifts (also called topological Markov chains with infinitely many states). Let Σ be the set of infinite sequences over a countable alphabet S with transitions governed by an irreducible, aperiodic matrix A = (aij) of zeros and ones. The shift map σ : Σ → Σ is continuous. A potential φ : Σ → ℝ is usually assumed to be locally Hölder continuous (i.e., Hölder on each cylinder set) and summable: Σi∈S sup[i] eφ < ∞, where [i] denotes the cylinder of sequences starting with symbol i.

The central object is the Gurevich pressure PG(φ) = limn→∞ (1/n) log Σx: σnx=x eSnφ(x), where the sum runs over periodic points of period n. Sarig (1999, 2001) established a variational principle: PG(φ) = sup{hμ(σ) + ∫ φ dμ : μ σ‑invariant, ∫ φ dμ > −∞}. An equilibrium state is an invariant measure attaining the supremum. Sarig proved that if PG(φ) < ∞ and the potential has summable variations, then an equilibrium state exists and is unique; it is a Gibbs measure satisfying the Gibbs property on cylinders with a uniform constant.

Phase transitions are a hallmark of the countable setting. For the Manneville–Pomeau potential φt(x) = −t log |σ'(x)| on the renewal shift, there is a critical parameter tc = 1 such that for t < tc a unique equilibrium state exists, while for t ≥ tc no equilibrium state exists (the pressure is infinite). This corresponds to the transition from finite to infinite invariant measure for the underlying intermittent map. The Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius operator ℒφ acting on a suitable Banach space of functions (e.g., the space of functions with summable variations) has a simple maximal eigenvalue ePG(φ) and a positive eigenfunction, yielding the Gibbs measure via the eigenmeasure.

Applications include the thermodynamic formalism for interval maps with indifferent fixed points (e.g., the Farey map), for geodesic flows on non‑compact manifolds of finite volume (via symbolic coding by the Bowen–Series map), and for random walks on free groups. The theory also underpins the study of multifractal spectra for Birkhoff averages of observables on countable shifts.

Worked Example: Gurevich Pressure for a Renewal Shift
Consider the renewal shift Σ with alphabet ℕ = {1,2,3,…} and transitions ai,i+1 = 1 for all i, ai,1 = 1 for all i, all other entries 0. The potential is φ(x) = −t log (x0) where x0 is the first symbol. Compute the Gurevich pressure PG(φ) and determine for which t an equilibrium state exists.
The periodic points of period n correspond to cycles that start at some symbol k, go up to k+n−1, then return to 1. The sum of φ over the cycle is −t Σj=0n−1 log(k+j). The Gurevich pressure is PG(φ) = limn→∞ (1/n) log Σk≥1 exp(−t Σj=0n−1 log(k+j)). For large n the inner sum behaves like n log n, so the term is ≈ k−tn n−tn. Summing over k gives convergence iff tn > 1. Hence PG(φ) = 0 for t ≤ 1 and PG(φ) = ∞ for t > 1. By Sarig's theorem, an equilibrium state exists exactly when PG(φ) < ∞, i.e., for t < 1. At t = 1 the pressure is zero but the potential is not summable, so no equilibrium state exists. This matches the known phase transition for the Manneville–Pomeau map.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks about existence of equilibrium states for countable shifts, first check summability of the potential and finiteness of Gurevich pressure. Remember that summable variations plus finite pressure guarantee a unique Gibbs equilibrium state. If the pressure is infinite, no equilibrium state exists. Be ready to identify the critical parameter where the phase transition occurs; it is often the value where the series Σ sup[i] eφ diverges.

Equilibrium States for Non‑uniformly Hyperbolic Systems

The theory of equilibrium states for non‑uniformly hyperbolic diffeomorphisms and flows has been developed extensively since the 1990s. Let f : M → M be a C1+α diffeomorphism preserving an ergodic hyperbolic measure μ (all Lyapunov exponents non‑zero). For a Hölder continuous potential φ : M → ℝ, the topological pressure P(φ) is defined via separated sets or spanning sets. The variational principle holds: P(φ) = supν {hν(f) + ∫ φ dν}, where the supremum runs over all f‑invariant probability measures.

Existence and uniqueness of equilibrium states for non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems require additional geometric conditions. A major breakthrough was achieved by Climenhaga and Thompson (2012, 2016) who introduced the notion of obstructions to specification and proved that if a system has a non‑uniform specification property on a collection of “good” orbit segments that carry most of the pressure, then a unique equilibrium state exists for any Hölder potential with sufficiently small pressure gap. Their framework covers non‑uniformly hyperbolic diffeomorphisms with a Pesin set of large measure, partially hyperbolic systems with a mostly contracting center, and certain skew products over hyperbolic bases.

Another approach uses inducing schemes (Young towers). Given a Young tower with exponential tails, one can lift a potential to the tower and apply the thermodynamic formalism for countable Markov shifts (Sarig’s theory) to obtain an equilibrium state for the original system. This method yields equilibrium states for potentials with summable variations on the tower, which translates to a regularity condition on the original potential (e.g., Hölder on stable/unstable leaves). The resulting equilibrium state is the projection of the Gibbs measure on the tower and inherits statistical properties such as exponential decay of correlations and the central limit theorem for Hölder observables.

Important results include:

  • Uniqueness of the measure of maximal entropy for C1+α surface diffeomorphisms with positive entropy (Buzzi, 2010) and for certain higher‑dimensional systems with a dominated splitting.
  • Phase transitions for geometric potentials φt = −t log |det Df|Eu (the unstable Jacobian). For t = 1 the equilibrium state is the SRB measure; for t > 1 the pressure may become infinite, signalling a transition to an infinite invariant measure.
  • Equilibrium states for the geometric potential on non‑uniformly expanding maps (e.g., Viana maps) were constructed by Alves, Luzzatto, and Pinheiro (2005) using inducing schemes.

Open problems include the classification of equilibrium states for potentials with low regularity (e.g., bounded variation), the existence of multiple equilibrium states for the same potential (phase coexistence), and the extension of the theory to non‑uniformly hyperbolic flows such as the geodesic flow on manifolds with cusps.

Large Deviations for Non‑uniformly Hyperbolic Systems

Large deviation theory quantifies the exponential decay of probabilities that empirical averages deviate from their typical values. For a dynamical system (M, f, μ) with an invariant ergodic measure μ, and a continuous observable A : M → ℝ, the level‑1 large deviation principle (LDP) states that for any interval I ⊂ ℝ,

limn→∞ (1/n) log μ({x : (1/n) Σk=0n−1 A(fkx) ∈ I}) = − infa∈I I(a),

where the rate function I(a) is convex, lower semicontinuous, and satisfies I(∫ A dμ) = 0. For uniformly hyperbolic systems (Axiom A), the LDP holds for all Hölder observables with a rate function given by the Legendre transform of the pressure function q ↦ P(qA) (Ellis, 1985; Kifer, 1990).

For non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems, the LDP is more delicate. Young (1990) proved a level‑1 LDP for non‑uniformly expanding maps with a tower having exponential tails, using the spectral gap of the transfer operator on a suitable Banach space. Kifer (2001) extended this to non‑uniformly hyperbolic diffeomorphisms admitting a Young tower with exponential tails, showing that the LDP holds for Hölder observables with a rate function that is the Legendre transform of the pressure function on the tower. The key technical tool is the Gärtner–Ellis theorem applied to the logarithmic moment generating function Λ(q) = limn→∞ (1/n) log ∫ eq SnA dμ, which equals the pressure P(qA) for the lifted potential on the tower.

For systems with polynomial tails (e.g., intermittent maps of Manneville–Pomeau type), the LDP fails at the exponential scale; instead one obtains a sub‑exponential large deviation principle with a speed an = nβ (0 < β < 1) and a non‑convex rate function (Melbourne and Nicol, 2008). The level‑2 LDP (large deviations for empirical measures) has been established for non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems with exponential tails by Comman (2011) using the projective metric on the space of invariant measures.

Applications include:

  • Fluctuation of Lyapunov exponents: The LDP for the observable log |Df|Eu gives the rate of rare events where the finite‑time Lyapunov exponent deviates from its asymptotic value.
  • Escape rates from holes: For open systems, the large deviation rate function for the indicator of the hole yields the escape rate and the conditionally invariant measure.
  • Multifractal analysis: The Legendre transform of the pressure function provides the multifractal spectrum of Birkhoff averages.

Key references: Kifer (1990) “Large deviations in dynamical systems and stochastic processes”; Young (1990) “Large deviations for non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems”; Melbourne and Nicol (2008) “Large deviations for non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems with polynomial tails”.

Fluctuation Theorems for Smooth Dynamical Systems

Fluctuation theorems (FTs) are exact symmetries of the probability distribution of entropy production in non‑equilibrium steady states. In the context of smooth dynamical systems, the Gallavotti–Cohen fluctuation theorem (1995) states that for an Anosov diffeomorphism f preserving a smooth volume form and a time‑reversal involution I (i.e., I ∘ f = f−1 ∘ I), the SRB measure μ satisfies

limτ→∞ (1/τ) log ( μ({x : (1/τ) Σk=0τ−1 σ(fkx) ∈ [a,b]}) / μ({x : (1/τ) Σk=0τ−1 σ(fkx) ∈ [−b,−a]}) ) = a,

where σ(x) = −divμ(f) is the phase space contraction rate (equal to the sum of Lyapunov exponents for the SRB measure). The function σ is the instantaneous entropy production rate. The theorem implies that the large deviation rate function I(a) for the time‑averaged entropy production satisfies the symmetry I(−a) − I(a) = a.

For non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems with an SRB measure, the FT has been proved under the assumption that the system admits a Young tower with exponential tails and a time‑reversal symmetry that lifts to the tower (Bonetto, Gallavotti, and Cohen, 2000). The proof uses the thermodynamic formalism for the potential qσ and the analyticity of the pressure function P(qσ) in a strip around the real axis. The symmetry P(qσ) = P((−1−q)σ) follows from the time‑reversal invariance of the SRB measure and yields the FT via the Gärtner–Ellis theorem.

Important extensions and related results:

  • Evans–Searles FT (1994) for transient fluctuations in deterministic thermostatted systems; it holds for any initial distribution absolutely continuous with respect to volume.
  • Jarzynski equality (1997) for free‑energy differences, derived from the FT by integrating over the entropy production distribution.
  • Fluctuation theorems for stochastic dynamics (e.g., Langevin equations) where the entropy production is defined via the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the path measure with respect to its time reversal.
  • Quantum fluctuation theorems (Tasaki, 2000; Crooks, 1999) for quantum channels, with applications to quantum thermodynamics.

In smooth ergodic theory, the FT provides a rigorous bridge between microscopic reversibility and macroscopic irreversibility. It also implies the positivity of the average entropy production (the second law) and gives quantitative bounds on the probability of observing negative entropy production over finite times. For Anosov flows (e.g., geodesic flows on negatively curved manifolds), the FT holds for the SRB measure of the time‑1 map, and the entropy production rate equals the sum of positive Lyapunov exponents minus the sum of negative ones.

Open problems include the FT for systems with polynomial decay of correlations (where the pressure function may have a phase transition at q = −1/2), for partially hyperbolic systems with a non‑trivial center direction, and for random dynamical systems where the time‑reversal symmetry is only statistical.

  • Confusing the Gurevich pressure with the classical topological pressure for finite shifts; the former can be infinite even for bounded potentials.
  • Assuming that every Hölder potential on a countable Markov shift has an equilibrium state; summable variations and finite pressure are essential.
  • Thinking that SRB measures exist for all non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems; they require a geometric structure such as a Young tower with exponential tails or a non‑uniform expansion condition.
  • Believing that the large deviation rate function is always convex; for systems with polynomial tails the rate function can be non‑convex and the speed is sub‑exponential.
  • Overlooking the necessity of a time‑reversal involution for the Gallavotti–Cohen fluctuation theorem; the symmetry of the rate function fails without it.
  • Mixing up the level‑1 LDP (for observables) with the level‑2 LDP (for empirical measures); the latter requires a topology on the space of invariant measures and a projective metric argument.
  • Assuming that the measure of maximal entropy is always unique for non‑uniformly hyperbolic systems; uniqueness holds under specification‑type conditions but can fail in the presence of phase transitions.

Ergodic Theory of Circle Diffeomorphisms and Denjoy Theory

The dynamics of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms of the circle S¹ = ℝ/ℤ form a foundational chapter in ergodic theory where topological and measure-theoretic notions intertwine with exceptional clarity. Every such diffeomorphism f has a well-defined rotation number ρ(f) ∈ ℝ/ℤ, introduced by Poincaré in 1885, which captures the average angular displacement per iteration. If ρ(f) is rational, say p/q in lowest terms, then f possesses a periodic orbit of period q and every orbit converges to a periodic orbit; the dynamics is structurally stable under C¹ perturbations. If ρ(f) is irrational, Denjoy's theorem (1932) asserts that for C² diffeomorphisms (or more generally C¹ with bounded variation of the derivative), f is topologically conjugate to the rigid rotation Rρ(x) = x + ρ mod 1. Consequently, there exists a unique invariant probability measure, which is the pushforward of Lebesgue measure under the conjugacy, and the system is uniquely ergodic. The conjugacy is a homeomorphism but need not be absolutely continuous; its regularity is governed by the arithmetic properties of ρ and the smoothness of f. Herman (1979) and Yoccoz (1984) proved that for C∞ diffeomorphisms with Diophantine rotation numbers (satisfying |ρ − p/q| ≥ C/q²⁺ε for all p/q), the conjugacy is C∞, while for Liouville rotation numbers it can be arbitrarily wild. The Denjoy counterexample shows that C¹ regularity is insufficient: there exist C¹ diffeomorphisms with irrational rotation number that possess a wandering interval, hence a Cantor minimal set supporting infinitely many invariant measures. The distinction between C¹ and C¹⁺BV is sharp. In the uniquely ergodic irrational case, the unique invariant measure is either absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue (if the conjugacy is absolutely continuous) or singular. The Kotani-Last theory (1990s) relates the spectral type of the associated Schrödinger cocycle to the regularity of the conjugacy. For circle homeomorphisms with breaks (piecewise C² with jump discontinuities in the derivative), the rotation number still dictates the topological dynamics, but the invariant measure can be singular even for Diophantine rotation numbers, a phenomenon studied by Khanin, Teplinsky, and others. The renormalization operator acting on the space of circle diffeomorphisms, introduced by Lanford and developed by de Faria, de Melo, and Pinto, provides a dynamical systems approach to the classification of conjugacy classes and the universality of scaling ratios at the boundary of chaos.

Ergodic Theory of Substitution Systems and the Pisot Conjecture

Substitution dynamical systems arise from iterated application of a substitution rule σ on a finite alphabet A, such as the Fibonacci substitution 0 → 01, 1 → 0, or the Thue-Morse substitution 0 → 01, 1 → 10. The shift-invariant closure of the orbit of a fixed point of σ yields a minimal subshift (Xσ, T) when σ is primitive (some power has all entries positive). The incidence matrix Mσ records letter frequencies; its Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue λ > 1 is the inflation factor. The system is uniquely ergodic with a unique invariant measure μ whose cylinder frequencies are given by the normalized left eigenvector of Mσ. The diffraction spectrum of the associated Delone set (or the spectral measure of the Koopman operator on L²(μ)) is a central object. The Pisot conjecture, formulated in the 1990s and rooted in earlier work of Pisot (1938) and Salem (1945), posits that if λ is a Pisot number (an algebraic integer > 1 all of whose Galois conjugates lie inside the unit disk), then the dynamical system has pure discrete spectrum, i.e., it is measurably conjugate to a rotation on a compact abelian group (a torus or solenoid). This is known for substitutions of constant length (Dekking, 1978), for two-letter alphabets (Barge and Diamond, 2002; Hollander and Solomyak, 2003), and for a large class of Pisot substitutions satisfying a geometric coincidence condition (Host, 1986; Solomyak, 1997). The conjecture remains open in general. The Rauzy fractal, a compact tile in the contracting hyperplane of Mσ, provides a geometric representation of the system as a domain exchange on a torus. When the Pisot conjecture holds, the Rauzy fractal tiles the contracting space by translations, yielding a Markov partition for the toral translation. The spectral theory connects to the theory of Meyer sets and model sets: the diffraction measure of a model set is pure point if and only if the window is a Riemann measurable set (Moody, 1997). For non-Pisot substitutions, the spectrum typically contains a singular continuous component; the Rudin-Shapiro substitution (eigenvalues 2, ±√2) has a singular continuous spectrum with a known spectral measure (Queffélec, 1987). The complexity function p(n) counting words of length n grows linearly for primitive substitutions (p(n) = O(n)), and the system has zero topological entropy. The S-adic generalization, where a sequence of substitutions is applied, encompasses all minimal zero-entropy systems (Durand, 1998) and provides a framework for the Pisot conjecture in higher generality. The connection to interval exchange transformations via the suspension flow over the substitution subshift with a roof function given by the left eigenvector yields a representation as a translation flow on a translation surface, linking to the Teichmüller dynamics discussed elsewhere.

Worked Example: Rotation Number and Denjoy Conjugacy
Let f : S¹ → S¹ be a C² orientation-preserving diffeomorphism with irrational rotation number ρ. Prove that the unique f-invariant probability measure μ is the pushforward of Lebesgue measure λ under the Denjoy conjugacy h : S¹ → S¹ satisfying h ∘ f = Rρ ∘ h.
By Denjoy's theorem, h exists as a homeomorphism. The rigid rotation Rρ preserves Lebesgue measure λ uniquely. Define μ = h*λ. For any continuous φ, ∫ φ ∘ f dμ = ∫ φ ∘ f ∘ h dλ = ∫ φ ∘ h ∘ Rρ dλ = ∫ φ ∘ h dλ = ∫ φ dμ, so μ is f-invariant. Uniqueness follows from unique ergodicity of Rρ and the conjugacy. Thus μ = h*λ is the unique invariant measure.
Exam Strategy: Rotation Number Computation
For a circle diffeomorphism given by a lift F : ℝ → ℝ with F(x+1) = F(x)+1, the rotation number is ρ(f) = limn→∞ (Fn(x) − x)/n mod 1, independent of x. In exams, compute ρ by finding a periodic point if rational, or use the continued fraction expansion of ρ to determine Diophantine properties. Remember: C² + irrational ρ ⇒ unique ergodicity; C¹ + irrational ρ ⇒ possible Denjoy counterexample with wandering intervals.

Ergodic Theory of the Weil-Petersson Geodesic Flow

The Weil-Petersson (WP) metric on the moduli space Mg of Riemann surfaces of genus g ≥ 2 is a Kähler metric with negative sectional curvature but incomplete geodesics; its completion is the augmented moduli space M̄g, a stratified space where strata correspond to nodal Riemann surfaces. The WP geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle T¹Mg is a central object in the ergodic theory of moduli spaces, distinct from the Teichmüller flow (which preserves the SL(2,ℝ) action). Wolpert (1985) proved that the WP metric has negative sectional curvature bounded away from zero and infinity on compact subsets of Mg, but the curvature blows up near the boundary. The geodesic flow is Anosov on the thick part but exhibits cusp-like behavior near the boundary strata. Burns, Masur, and Wilkinson (2010) established that the WP geodesic flow is ergodic with respect to the Liouville measure on T¹Mg, using a combination of Hopf's argument and a careful analysis of the boundary behavior via the geometry of the Weil-Petersson completion. The flow is also Bernoulli (Burns, Masur, Wilkinson, 2012), implying the strongest possible chaotic behavior. The rate of mixing is polynomial, not exponential, due to the cusp excursions; the precise polynomial rate is related to the geometry of the boundary strata and the behavior of the WP metric near the Deligne-Mumford compactification. The Liouville measure is infinite on the full unit tangent bundle of the completion, but finite on the regular part; the flow is conservative and ergodic on the finite measure part. The connection to the Kontsevich-Zorich cocycle is indirect: the WP flow does not preserve a natural SL(2,ℝ) action, but its stable and unstable foliations are related to the horocycle foliations of the Teichmüller flow via the work of Mirzakhani on the volume of moduli spaces. Mirzakhani's integration formulas for WP volumes (2007) and her proof of the asymptotic growth of simple closed geodesics on hyperbolic surfaces rely on the ergodic theory of the earthquake flow, which is a different flow on T¹Mg but shares the same phase space. The WP geodesic flow also appears in the study of the renormalization of interval exchange transformations via the work of Avila, Gouëzel, and Yoccoz on the Zorich cocycle, though the precise relationship remains an active area of research. Open problems include the central limit theorem for the WP flow, the precise polynomial mixing rate, and the classification of invariant measures for the earthquake flow on the boundary strata.

Ergodic Theory of Group Actions on CAT(0) Spaces and the Visual Boundary

A CAT(0) space is a geodesic metric space where triangles are no fatter than their Euclidean comparison triangles. Important examples include Euclidean buildings, symmetric spaces of non-compact type, and the universal covers of closed non-positively curved manifolds. A group Γ acting properly discontinuously and cocompactly by isometries on a CAT(0) space X is a CAT(0) group. The visual boundary ∂X consists of equivalence classes of geodesic rays, equipped with the cone topology. The action of Γ extends to ∂X, and the dynamics on the boundary is a primary tool for studying the group. The Patterson-Sullivan construction (1976, 1979) yields a family of conformal measures {νx}x∈X on ∂X of dimension δ(Γ), the critical exponent of the Poincaré series Σγ∈Γ e−sd(x,γx). For rank-one CAT(0) spaces (those containing a rank-one geodesic, i.e., a geodesic that does not bound a flat half-plane), the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of X/Γ is ergodic with respect to the Bowen-Margulis measure (Knieper, 1998). The boundary action is a convergence group action, and if Γ is non-elementary, the action on ∂X is minimal and strongly proximal (Furstenberg, 1963; Tukia, 1994). The Poisson boundary of Γ with respect to a symmetric random walk of finite entropy is measurably isomorphic to (∂X, ν) where ν is the harmonic measure (Kaimanovich, 1994; Karlsson and Margulis, 1999). For higher-rank CAT(0) spaces (e.g., products of trees, symmetric spaces of rank ≥ 2), the boundary is a spherical building, and the dynamics is governed by the theory of higher-rank rigidity (Ballmann, Burns, Spatzier, 1990s). The Furstenberg boundary G/P for a semisimple Lie group G is a special case. The measure rigidity results of Eskin, Mirzakhani, and Mohammadi (2015) for the SL(2,ℝ) action on moduli space have analogues for actions on CAT(0) cube complexes (Fernós, 2018). The visual boundary of a CAT(0) cube complex is a median space, and the Roller boundary provides a compactification where the action is amenable. The connection to the Poisson boundary of random walks on mapping class groups (Kaimanovich and Masur, 1996) shows that the Thurston boundary of Teichmüller space is the Poisson boundary for certain random walks. The ergodic theory of the geodesic flow on CAT(0) spaces with rank-one geodesics exhibits a dichotomy: either the flow is ergodic and has a unique measure of maximal entropy (the Bowen-Margulis measure), or the space is a higher-rank symmetric space or building where the flow is not ergodic and there are many invariant measures supported on flats. The work of Caprace and Sageev (2011) on rank-one isometries of CAT(0) spaces and the structure of CAT(0) groups with rank-one elements provides a group-theoretic counterpart to the dynamical dichotomy.

Ergodic Theory of Random Walks on Hyperbolic Groups and the Martin Boundary

Let Γ be a non-elementary word-hyperbolic group in the sense of Gromov (1987), equipped with a symmetric probability measure μ of finite entropy whose support generates Γ. The random walk on Γ driven by μ converges almost surely to the Gromov boundary ∂Γ (Kaimanovich, 1994; Maher and Tiozzo, 2018). The hitting measure ν on ∂Γ is the unique μ-stationary measure, and the Poisson boundary of (Γ, μ) is measurably isomorphic to (∂Γ, ν). The Martin boundary provides a finer topological compactification of Γ using the Green function G(x, y) = Σn≥0 μn(x−1y). For hyperbolic groups, the Martin boundary is homeomorphic to the Gromov boundary (Ancona, 1987; Gouëzel, 2015), and the minimal Martin boundary coincides with the whole boundary. This identification relies on Ancona's inequalities, which state that the Green function behaves like the exponential of the Gromov product: G(x, y) ≍ e−ε(x|y)o for some ε > 0. The harmonic measure ν is the unique measure of maximal dimension for the random walk, and its Hausdorff dimension equals the asymptotic entropy h(μ) divided by the drift ℓ(μ) = lim d(o, Xn)/n (Kaimanovich and Vershik, 1983; Blachère, Haïssinsky, and Mathieu, 2008). The central limit theorem for the distance d(o, Xn) holds with variance σ² > 0 (Björklund, 2010; Mathieu and Sert, 2017). The large deviation principle for the distance and the Gromov product has been established (Gouëzel, 2017). The Martin boundary theory extends to relatively hyperbolic groups (Gouëzel and Lalley, 2019) and to random walks on the mapping class group (Maher and Tiozzo, 2018), where the boundary is the Thurston boundary of projective measured foliations. The entropy of the random walk h(μ) is related to the volume entropy of the group and the critical exponent of the Poincaré series. For a cocompact lattice in a rank-one Lie group, the harmonic measure on the Furstenberg boundary is the unique stationary measure, and the Poisson boundary is the Furstenberg boundary (Furstenberg, 1963; Kaimanovich, 1994). The connection to the ergodic theory of the geodesic flow on the associated CAT(0) space is via the Hopf parametrization: the geodesic flow on the unit tangent bundle of X/Γ is isomorphic to the action of ℝ on the space of bi-infinite geodesics, which is a quotient of ∂X × ∂X minus the diagonal. The Bowen-Margulis measure corresponds to the product of the Patterson-Sullivan measure with itself. The work of Lalley (1989) on the renewal theorem for hyperbolic groups and the distribution of closed geodesics uses the spectral theory of the transfer operator associated to the random walk.

  • Assuming that every primitive substitution with a Pisot inflation factor has pure discrete spectrum; the Pisot conjecture is open in general and requires additional conditions such as the strong coincidence condition or the geometric coincidence condition.
  • Mixing up the Weil-Petersson geodesic flow with the Teichmüller geodesic flow; the WP flow is on the moduli space with an incomplete Kähler metric, while the Teichmüller flow is on the unit tangent bundle of the moduli space of quadratic differentials and preserves the SL(2,ℝ) action.
  • Thinking that the visual boundary of a CAT(0) space is always the Poisson boundary for every random walk; this holds for symmetric random walks of finite entropy on hyperbolic groups and rank-one CAT(0) groups, but fails for higher-rank lattices where the Poisson boundary is the Furstenberg boundary G/P.
  • Believing that the Martin boundary and the Gromov boundary coincide for all hyperbolic groups without the finite entropy assumption; Ancona's inequalities require the measure to have finite exponential moment, and counterexamples exist for measures with heavy tails.
  • Overlooking the distinction between the Patterson-Sullivan measure (conformal, dimension = critical exponent) and the harmonic measure (stationary, dimension = entropy/drift); they coincide only for very special measures (e.g., the spherical measure on the boundary of hyperbolic space for the Brownian motion).
  • Assuming that ergodicity of the geodesic flow on a CAT(0) space implies the space is rank-one; there are higher-rank spaces where the flow is ergodic on a subset of full measure but not on the whole space due to the presence of flats.
