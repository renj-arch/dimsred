Family, Friends and Relationships

Environmental Studies (EVS) at the primary level helps children understand their immediate environment - starting with family and gradually expanding to community, society, and the natural world. The theme of family, friends, and relationships helps children develop social awareness, empathy, and understanding of diverse family structures and social connections.

Key Concepts

Family Structures: A family is a group of people related by blood, marriage, or adoption. Types include: Nuclear family (parents and children), Joint/Extended family (multiple generations living together - grandparents, parents, children, uncles, aunts), Single-parent family (one parent raising children), and Blended family (step-families after remarriage). In the Indian context, joint families have been traditional, though nuclear families are increasingly common in urban areas. Each family structure has its own strengths and challenges.

Relationships and Kinship: Understanding family relationships (father, mother, brother, sister, grandfather, grandmother, uncle, aunt, cousin) and kinship terms. Children learn about their family tree, family history, and how families celebrate festivals, share meals, and support each other during difficult times. Respect for elders and care for younger family members are important values taught through this theme.

Friends and Community: Friendship involves mutual affection, trust, and support. Children learn about making friends, resolving conflicts, sharing, and cooperation. The community includes neighbors, local helpers (shopkeepers, postman, police officer, doctor, teacher, gardener, domestic help), and community places (school, hospital, post office, bank, market, park). Understanding interdependence - how different people in the community help each other - is a key learning outcome.

Diversity and Sensitivity: Families differ in size, structure, language, religion, food habits, festivals, and customs. EVS aims to develop respect for diversity and eliminate stereotypes. Children learn that all family types are valid, and every family deserves respect regardless of its composition or background. This promotes inclusivity and social harmony.

Rights and Responsibilities: Introduction to basic rights (right to education, right to play, right to express opinions) and responsibilities (helping at home, respecting others, keeping the environment clean, following rules). The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) recognizes children's rights to survival, development, protection, and participation.

Solved Examples

Example 1
Raj lives with his parents, grandparents, uncle, aunt, and cousin. What type of family is this?

a) Nuclear family b) Single-parent family c) Joint family d) Blended family
Solution: c) Joint family. A joint or extended family includes multiple generations living together under one roof. In this type, grandparents, parents, children, and sometimes uncles, aunts, and cousins share household responsibilities, resources, and support each other. Joint families are common in many parts of India, especially in rural areas.
Example 2
Which of the following is NOT considered a community helper?

a) Doctor b) Teacher c) Classmate d) Postman
Solution: c) Classmate. Community helpers are people who provide services to the community and help society function smoothly - doctors (healthcare), teachers (education), postmen (communication), police (safety), etc. A classmate is a fellow learner in the same class, not a community helper. However, classmates do form an important part of a child's social world and friendships.
Example 3
The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) recognizes all of the following rights EXCEPT:

a) Right to survival b) Right to development c) Right to marriage d) Right to participation
Solution: c) Right to marriage. UNCRC recognizes four broad categories of children's rights: Survival (food, shelter, healthcare), Development (education, play, cultural activities), Protection (from abuse, exploitation, neglect), and Participation (expressing opinions, being heard). Marriage is not a children's right - child marriage is prohibited by law in India under the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act.

Shortcut Techniques

  • Family Types - My memory: Nuclear (parents+children), Joint (multi-generational), Single (one parent), Blended (step-family). Think: N-J-S-B.
  • Community Helpers - Who Helps Where: Connect helpers to places: Doctor=Hospital, Teacher=School, Postman=Post Office, Police=Police Station, Firefighter=Fire Station. This helps children understand the helper-place relationship.
  • UNCRC Rights - 4 Pillars of Childhood: Survival (live), Development (grow), Protection (safe), Participation (voice). Remember S-D-P-P as the four pillars.
  • Respect for Diversity - ABC: Accept differences, Be kind, Celebrate diversity. Simple rule: different does not mean wrong.
Pro Tip
When teaching EVS about family, avoid assuming all children come from nuclear families. Be sensitive to diverse family structures including single-parent families, children living with grandparents, and children from separated parents. Use inclusive language and examples that every child can relate to.

Integrated Approach

Evolving Family Structures in Contemporary India

Family composition in India has shifted markedly over recent decades. Census data shows a notable increase in nuclear households, with a corresponding decline in joint or extended family arrangements. Urbanisation, labour migration, and rising housing costs accelerate this trend, yet joint families persist in rural belts and among communities that value intergenerational co‑residence for economic pooling and caregiving. Single‑parent households, once statistically marginal, now constitute a measurable segment due to divorce, widowhood, and abandonment; NFHS‑5 (2019‑21) indicates that around 5 percent of children live with a single mother. Blended families, formed through remarriage, introduce step‑sibling dynamics that textbooks rarely illustrate but that affect children’s sense of belonging and inheritance rights. Child‑headed households, though numerically small, emerge in contexts of parental migration or disease, compelling policy frameworks to recognise minors as primary caregivers. Same‑sex parenting remains legally unrecognised in India, yet social science research documents its presence in urban enclaves, highlighting the need for inclusive pedagogical language. The National Curriculum Framework 2005 and the National Education Policy 2020 both stress that EVS content must reflect this diversity, avoiding a default nuclear‑family narrative that alienates learners from non‑normative backgrounds.

Economic pressures reshape intra‑family roles. In migrant families, fathers often work in distant cities while mothers manage agriculture, household finances, and children’s schooling, effectively becoming de‑facto heads of household. This shift challenges traditional gendered divisions of labour and creates new patterns of decision‑making. Technology further mediates relationships: video calls sustain emotional bonds across distances, but also introduce screen‑time concerns that affect play and study routines. Educators should incorporate illustrative case studies that map these realities — for example, a family in Bihar where the father works in Delhi, the mother runs a self‑help group, and the grandmother supervises homework — to help students analyse how structure, economy, and technology intersect. Assessment tasks can ask learners to construct a family‑type matrix for their neighbourhood, fostering data‑collection skills and empathy.

Child Protection Framework: Legislation and Implementation

India’s child protection architecture rests on constitutional guarantees and a suite of specialised statutes. Articles 15(3), 21, 24, 39(e)‑(f) and 45 of the Constitution empower the state to make special provisions for children, prohibit hazardous employment, and direct policy towards free and compulsory education. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC), ratified in 1992, informs domestic law through its four pillars — survival, development, protection, participation. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, replaced the 2000 Act and introduced a differential approach: children aged 16‑18 accused of heinous offences may be tried as adults after a preliminary assessment by the Juvenile Justice Board (JJB). The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012, defines a child as any person below 18 and mandates child‑friendly procedures, including in‑camera trials and mandatory reporting. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009, guarantees elementary education as a justiciable right, while the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016, bans employment of children below 14 in all occupations and regulates adolescent work (14‑18) in non‑hazardous sectors. The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, sets the minimum age at 18 for women and 21 for men, with penal provisions for solemnising or facilitating under‑age marriages.

Implementation relies on a decentralized machinery. At the district level, Child Welfare Committees (CWCs) function as the competent authority for children in need of care and protection, while Juvenile Justice Boards (JJBs) adjudicate matters concerning children in conflict with law. District Child Protection Units (DCPUs) coordinate services — shelter homes, foster care, sponsorship, and after‑care — and monitor compliance with the Integrated Child Protection Scheme (ICPS). The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) and its state counterparts oversee policy adherence, conduct inquiries, and recommend legislative amendments. National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) data indicates an increase in reported crimes against children, underscoring gaps between law and practice. Effective EVS teaching should therefore move beyond rote listing of Acts; learners must trace a case from reporting to rehabilitation, identifying the roles of police, CWC, JJB, and NGOs, and evaluate systemic bottlenecks such as delayed age‑determination or inadequate after‑care funding.

Scenario‑Based Application
A teacher designs an assessment for Class V students on the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015. Which method best reflects the constructivist, experiential pedagogy advocated by NCF 2005?
Correct choice: Role‑play simulation of a Child Welfare Committee hearing.
Reasoning: NCF 2005 emphasises learning through active engagement, dialogue, and real‑life contexts. A role‑play requires students to assume roles (child, social worker, CWC member), apply legal provisions (best‑interest principle, age determination), and negotiate outcomes, thereby constructing understanding collaboratively. Multiple‑choice tests and memorisation tasks assess recall only, while a written essay, though higher‑order, remains an individual, text‑bound exercise lacking the interactive, situational dimension central to constructivist EVS pedagogy.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question presents a child‑protection scenario, first identify the governing statute (e.g., POCSO for sexual offence, JJ Act for conflict‑with‑law). Next, pinpoint the specific provision (mandatory reporting, age‑determination, best‑interest). This two‑step mapping cuts recall time and prevents confusion between overlapping laws.

Pedagogical Strategies for Teaching Family and Relationships in EVS

Effective EVS instruction on family and relationships moves beyond textbook definitions toward inquiry‑driven, context‑rich experiences. The constructivist framework of NCF 2005 recommends that teachers elicit children’s prior conceptions — such as “family means mother, father, and me” — and then introduce counter‑examples through community surveys, photo‑essays, or oral histories. A practical sequence begins with a “Family Tree” activity where each learner maps three generations, noting occupations, migration, and caregiving roles. This visual tool surfaces diversity (single‑parent, grandparent‑led, foster) and becomes a data source for mathematical representation (bar graphs of family types) and language work (writing captions). Role‑play of daily routines — cooking, budgeting, conflict resolution — lets students practise empathy and observe gendered task allocation. Field visits to anganwadi centres, old‑age homes, or community kitchens connect classroom concepts to service delivery systems, reinforcing the “community helpers” strand. Assessment shifts from summative tests to portfolios: annotated family‑tree diagrams, reflective journals on a “day in my family”, and peer‑feedback sheets on group discussions. Rubrics should capture process skills — questioning, evidence‑gathering, perspective‑taking — alongside content accuracy. NEP 2020 further advocates multilingual resources; teachers can incorporate regional kinship terms (e.g., “mama”, “chacha”, “didi”) to validate linguistic diversity and deepen cultural relevance.

Gender Socialisation and Equity within Family Contexts

Gender socialisation begins at birth and is reinforced through everyday family practices — division of chores, toy selection, language, and expectations around education and mobility. Research by NCERT and others suggests that by early primary years, children associate cooking and cleaning with mothers and repair work with fathers, mirroring adult patterns. Such early scripting limits aspirations; girls may internalise lower academic confidence in STEM, while boys may suppress emotional expression. The Constitution’s Articles 15(3) and 39(a) empower the state to make special provisions for women and children and to direct policy towards equal pay and livelihood. Flagship programmes operationalise these mandates: Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (2015) targets declining child‑sex ratios through community mobilisation; Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana (2015) provides a savings instrument for girls’ education and marriage; the National Scheme of Incentive to Girls for Secondary Education (2008) offers cash transfers for enrolment. However, legal provisions alone cannot dismantle household‑level bias. EVS lessons can intervene by analysing time‑use diaries — students record hours spent on domestic work, study, play — and then discuss disparities. Comparative case studies of families that have redistributed chores (e.g., a father cooking while mother attends a night‑shift) illustrate agency and change. Teachers should also address intersecting vulnerabilities: girls from Scheduled Castes or Tribes face compounded discrimination in nutrition, schooling, and early marriage. Integrating data from the Sample Registration System (SRS) on sex‑ratio at birth and NFHS‑5 indicators on anaemia prevalence grounds discussion in evidence. Assessment tasks may ask learners to design a “gender‑fair household charter” with measurable commitments, fostering both critical thinking and civic responsibility.

Community Engagement and Experiential Learning in EVS

EVS thrives when the classroom permeates the neighbourhood. Community engagement transforms abstract concepts — “community helpers”, “local resources”, “environmental stewardship” — into lived experiences. A structured approach begins with asset mapping: students, guided by teachers, walk their village or ward, noting water sources, waste‑collection points, health sub‑centres, schools, parks, and informal workspaces (e.g., street vendors, artisans). This spatial data feeds into a community‑resource chart displayed in the classroom, linking each asset to a curriculum theme (water → science; health centre → biology; park → play). Service‑learning projects deepen ownership: a class may adopt a nearby pond, conduct a simple water‑quality test (turbidity, odour), present findings to the gram panchayat, and organise a clean‑up drive. Collaboration with local NGOs — such as those running child‑helpline services or nutrition programmes — provides expert talks and mentorship. The “Community Helpers” strand gains authenticity when a postman, ASHA worker, or mechanic visits the class, demonstrates tools, and answers student‑generated questions. Reflection journals capture emotional responses, ethical dilemmas (e.g., witnessing child labour), and proposals for action. Assessment rubrics weigh participation, documentation quality, and the feasibility of suggested interventions. NEP 2020’s emphasis on “learning by doing” and “community‑connected curriculum” aligns directly with this model, ensuring that EVS remains a bridge between knowledge, values, and civic agency.
