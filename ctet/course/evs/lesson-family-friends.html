Family, Friends and Relationships

Environmental Studies (EVS) at the primary level helps children understand their immediate environment - starting with family and gradually expanding to community, society, and the natural world. The theme of family, friends, and relationships helps children develop social awareness, empathy, and understanding of diverse family structures and social connections.

Key Concepts

Family Structures: A family is a group of people related by blood, marriage, or adoption. Types include: Nuclear family (parents and children), Joint/Extended family (multiple generations living together - grandparents, parents, children, uncles, aunts), Single-parent family (one parent raising children), and Blended family (step-families after remarriage). In the Indian context, joint families have been traditional, though nuclear families are increasingly common in urban areas. Each family structure has its own strengths and challenges.

Relationships and Kinship: Understanding family relationships (father, mother, brother, sister, grandfather, grandmother, uncle, aunt, cousin) and kinship terms. Children learn about their family tree, family history, and how families celebrate festivals, share meals, and support each other during difficult times. Respect for elders and care for younger family members are important values taught through this theme.

Friends and Community: Friendship involves mutual affection, trust, and support. Children learn about making friends, resolving conflicts, sharing, and cooperation. The community includes neighbors, local helpers (shopkeepers, postman, police officer, doctor, teacher, gardener, domestic help), and community places (school, hospital, post office, bank, market, park). Understanding interdependence - how different people in the community help each other - is a key learning outcome.

Diversity and Sensitivity: Families differ in size, structure, language, religion, food habits, festivals, and customs. EVS aims to develop respect for diversity and eliminate stereotypes. Children learn that all family types are valid, and every family deserves respect regardless of its composition or background. This promotes inclusivity and social harmony.

Rights and Responsibilities: Introduction to basic rights (right to education, right to play, right to express opinions) and responsibilities (helping at home, respecting others, keeping the environment clean, following rules). The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) recognizes children's rights to survival, development, protection, and participation.

Solved Examples

Example 1
Raj lives with his parents, grandparents, uncle, aunt, and cousin. What type of family is this?

a) Nuclear family b) Single-parent family c) Joint family d) Blended family
Solution: c) Joint family. A joint or extended family includes multiple generations living together under one roof. In this type, grandparents, parents, children, and sometimes uncles, aunts, and cousins share household responsibilities, resources, and support each other. Joint families are common in many parts of India, especially in rural areas.
Example 2
Which of the following is NOT considered a community helper?

a) Doctor b) Teacher c) Classmate d) Postman
Solution: c) Classmate. Community helpers are people who provide services to the community and help society function smoothly - doctors (healthcare), teachers (education), postmen (communication), police (safety), etc. A classmate is a fellow learner in the same class, not a community helper. However, classmates do form an important part of a child's social world and friendships.
Example 3
The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) recognizes all of the following rights EXCEPT:

a) Right to survival b) Right to development c) Right to marriage d) Right to participation
Solution: c) Right to marriage. UNCRC recognizes four broad categories of children's rights: Survival (food, shelter, healthcare), Development (education, play, cultural activities), Protection (from abuse, exploitation, neglect), and Participation (expressing opinions, being heard). Marriage is not a children's right - child marriage is prohibited by law in India under the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act.

Shortcut Techniques

  • Family Types - My memory: Nuclear (parents+children), Joint (multi-generational), Single (one parent), Blended (step-family). Think: N-J-S-B.
  • Community Helpers - Who Helps Where: Connect helpers to places: Doctor=Hospital, Teacher=School, Postman=Post Office, Police=Police Station, Firefighter=Fire Station. This helps children understand the helper-place relationship.
  • UNCRC Rights - 4 Pillars of Childhood: Survival (live), Development (grow), Protection (safe), Participation (voice). Remember S-D-P-P as the four pillars.
  • Respect for Diversity - ABC: Accept differences, Be kind, Celebrate diversity. Simple rule: different does not mean wrong.
Pro Tip
When teaching EVS about family, avoid assuming all children come from nuclear families. Be sensitive to diverse family structures including single-parent families, children living with grandparents, and children from separated parents. Use inclusive language and examples that every child can relate to.

Evolving Family Structures in Contemporary India

Family composition in India has shifted markedly over recent decades. Census data shows a notable increase in nuclear households, with a corresponding decline in joint or extended family arrangements. Urbanisation, labour migration, and rising housing costs accelerate this trend, yet joint families persist in rural belts and among communities that value intergenerational co‑residence for economic pooling and caregiving. Single‑parent households, once statistically marginal, now constitute a measurable segment due to divorce, widowhood, and abandonment; NFHS‑5 (2019‑21) indicates that around 5 percent of children live with a single mother. Blended families, formed through remarriage, introduce step‑sibling dynamics that textbooks rarely illustrate but that affect children’s sense of belonging and inheritance rights. Child‑headed households, though numerically small, emerge in contexts of parental migration or disease, compelling policy frameworks to recognise minors as primary caregivers. Same‑sex parenting remains legally unrecognised in India, yet social science research documents its presence in urban enclaves, highlighting the need for inclusive pedagogical language. The National Curriculum Framework 2005 and the National Education Policy 2020 both stress that EVS content must reflect this diversity, avoiding a default nuclear‑family narrative that alienates learners from non‑normative backgrounds.

Economic pressures reshape intra‑family roles. In migrant families, fathers often work in distant cities while mothers manage agriculture, household finances, and children’s schooling, effectively becoming de‑facto heads of household. This shift challenges traditional gendered divisions of labour and creates new patterns of decision‑making. Technology further mediates relationships: video calls sustain emotional bonds across distances, but also introduce screen‑time concerns that affect play and study routines. Educators should incorporate illustrative case studies that map these realities — for example, a family in Bihar where the father works in Delhi, the mother runs a self‑help group, and the grandmother supervises homework — to help students analyse how structure, economy, and technology intersect. Assessment tasks can ask learners to construct a family‑type matrix for their neighbourhood, fostering data‑collection skills and empathy.

Child Protection Framework: Legislation and Implementation

India’s child protection architecture rests on constitutional guarantees and a suite of specialised statutes. Articles 15(3), 21, 24, 39(e)‑(f) and 45 of the Constitution empower the state to make special provisions for children, prohibit hazardous employment, and direct policy towards free and compulsory education. The United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC), ratified in 1992, informs domestic law through its four pillars — survival, development, protection, participation. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, replaced the 2000 Act and introduced a differential approach: children aged 16‑18 accused of heinous offences may be tried as adults after a preliminary assessment by the Juvenile Justice Board (JJB). The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012, defines a child as any person below 18 and mandates child‑friendly procedures, including in‑camera trials and mandatory reporting. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009, guarantees elementary education as a justiciable right, while the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016, bans employment of children below 14 in all occupations and regulates adolescent work (14‑18) in non‑hazardous sectors. The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, sets the minimum age at 18 for women and 21 for men, with penal provisions for solemnising or facilitating under‑age marriages.

Implementation relies on a decentralized machinery. At the district level, Child Welfare Committees (CWCs) function as the competent authority for children in need of care and protection, while Juvenile Justice Boards (JJBs) adjudicate matters concerning children in conflict with law. District Child Protection Units (DCPUs) coordinate services — shelter homes, foster care, sponsorship, and after‑care — and monitor compliance with the Integrated Child Protection Scheme (ICPS). The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR) and its state counterparts oversee policy adherence, conduct inquiries, and recommend legislative amendments. National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) data indicates an increase in reported crimes against children, underscoring gaps between law and practice. Effective EVS teaching should therefore move beyond rote listing of Acts; learners must trace a case from reporting to rehabilitation, identifying the roles of police, CWC, JJB, and NGOs, and evaluate systemic bottlenecks such as delayed age‑determination or inadequate after‑care funding.

Scenario‑Based Application
A teacher designs an assessment for Class V students on the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015. Which method best reflects the constructivist, experiential pedagogy advocated by NCF 2005?
Correct choice: Role‑play simulation of a Child Welfare Committee hearing.
Reasoning: NCF 2005 emphasises learning through active engagement, dialogue, and real‑life contexts. A role‑play requires students to assume roles (child, social worker, CWC member), apply legal provisions (best‑interest principle, age determination), and negotiate outcomes, thereby constructing understanding collaboratively. Multiple‑choice tests and memorisation tasks assess recall only, while a written essay, though higher‑order, remains an individual, text‑bound exercise lacking the interactive, situational dimension central to constructivist EVS pedagogy.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question presents a child‑protection scenario, first identify the governing statute (e.g., POCSO for sexual offence, JJ Act for conflict‑with‑law). Next, pinpoint the specific provision (mandatory reporting, age‑determination, best‑interest). This two‑step mapping cuts recall time and prevents confusion between overlapping laws.

Pedagogical Strategies for Teaching Family and Relationships in EVS

Effective EVS instruction on family and relationships moves beyond textbook definitions toward inquiry‑driven, context‑rich experiences. The constructivist framework of NCF 2005 recommends that teachers elicit children’s prior conceptions — such as “family means mother, father, and me” — and then introduce counter‑examples through community surveys, photo‑essays, or oral histories. A practical sequence begins with a “Family Tree” activity where each learner maps three generations, noting occupations, migration, and caregiving roles. This visual tool surfaces diversity (single‑parent, grandparent‑led, foster) and becomes a data source for mathematical representation (bar graphs of family types) and language work (writing captions). Role‑play of daily routines — cooking, budgeting, conflict resolution — lets students practise empathy and observe gendered task allocation. Field visits to anganwadi centres, old‑age homes, or community kitchens connect classroom concepts to service delivery systems, reinforcing the “community helpers” strand. Assessment shifts from summative tests to portfolios: annotated family‑tree diagrams, reflective journals on a “day in my family”, and peer‑feedback sheets on group discussions. Rubrics should capture process skills — questioning, evidence‑gathering, perspective‑taking — alongside content accuracy. NEP 2020 further advocates multilingual resources; teachers can incorporate regional kinship terms (e.g., “mama”, “chacha”, “didi”) to validate linguistic diversity and deepen cultural relevance.

Gender Socialisation and Equity within Family Contexts

Gender socialisation begins at birth and is reinforced through everyday family practices — division of chores, toy selection, language, and expectations around education and mobility. Research by NCERT and others suggests that by early primary years, children associate cooking and cleaning with mothers and repair work with fathers, mirroring adult patterns. Such early scripting limits aspirations; girls may internalise lower academic confidence in STEM, while boys may suppress emotional expression. The Constitution’s Articles 15(3) and 39(a) empower the state to make special provisions for women and children and to direct policy towards equal pay and livelihood. Flagship programmes operationalise these mandates: Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (2015) targets declining child‑sex ratios through community mobilisation; Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana (2015) provides a savings instrument for girls’ education and marriage; the National Scheme of Incentive to Girls for Secondary Education (2008) offers cash transfers for enrolment. However, legal provisions alone cannot dismantle household‑level bias. EVS lessons can intervene by analysing time‑use diaries — students record hours spent on domestic work, study, play — and then discuss disparities. Comparative case studies of families that have redistributed chores (e.g., a father cooking while mother attends a night‑shift) illustrate agency and change. Teachers should also address intersecting vulnerabilities: girls from Scheduled Castes or Tribes face compounded discrimination in nutrition, schooling, and early marriage. Integrating data from the Sample Registration System (SRS) on sex‑ratio at birth and NFHS‑5 indicators on anaemia prevalence grounds discussion in evidence. Assessment tasks may ask learners to design a “gender‑fair household charter” with measurable commitments, fostering both critical thinking and civic responsibility.

Community Engagement and Experiential Learning in EVS

EVS thrives when the classroom permeates the neighbourhood. Community engagement transforms abstract concepts — “community helpers”, “local resources”, “environmental stewardship” — into lived experiences. A structured approach begins with asset mapping: students, guided by teachers, walk their village or ward, noting water sources, waste‑collection points, health sub‑centres, schools, parks, and informal workspaces (e.g., street vendors, artisans). This spatial data feeds into a community‑resource chart displayed in the classroom, linking each asset to a curriculum theme (water → science; health centre → biology; park → play). Service‑learning projects deepen ownership: a class may adopt a nearby pond, conduct a simple water‑quality test (turbidity, odour), present findings to the gram panchayat, and organise a clean‑up drive. Collaboration with local NGOs — such as those running child‑helpline services or nutrition programmes — provides expert talks and mentorship. The “Community Helpers” strand gains authenticity when a postman, ASHA worker, or mechanic visits the class, demonstrates tools, and answers student‑generated questions. Reflection journals capture emotional responses, ethical dilemmas (e.g., witnessing child labour), and proposals for action. Assessment rubrics weigh participation, documentation quality, and the feasibility of suggested interventions. NEP 2020’s emphasis on “learning by doing” and “community‑connected curriculum” aligns directly with this model, ensuring that EVS remains a bridge between knowledge, values, and civic agency.

Intergenerational Transmission of Occupational Skills and Family Work Practices

Families in India have historically functioned as primary sites for the transmission of occupational knowledge, especially in agrarian, artisanal, and home‑based enterprise contexts. Children observe, assist, and gradually master tasks such as weaving, pottery, dairy management, and small‑scale food processing. This informal apprenticeship is embedded in daily routines rather than formal curricula, allowing skills to be passed across generations without institutional mediation. The National Curriculum Framework (NCF) 2005 acknowledges the pedagogical value of such work‑centric learning, provided it does not compromise the child’s right to education or health.

The distinction between constructive participation and exploitative labour hinges on three criteria: age‑appropriateness, duration, and interference with schooling. The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, amended in 2016, prohibits employment of children below 14 years in any occupation and of adolescents (14‑18) in hazardous processes listed in the Schedule. However, the Act permits children to help in family enterprises after school hours or during vacations, provided the work is non‑hazardous and does not exceed three hours a day. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 (RTE) reinforces this by mandating school attendance up to age 14, making any work that displaces schooling a violation.

Research by the National Institute of Public Cooperation and Child Development (NIPCCD) suggests that in rural households engaged in handloom weaving, children often contribute limited time to preparatory tasks such as yarn winding. When such involvement remains within statutory limits, it supports skill acquisition and family income without breaching legal thresholds. Conversely, in urban informal sectors like garment stitching or waste sorting, children often work longer hours, exposing them to ergonomic hazards and school dropout. The 2011 Census recorded 4.35 million working children aged 5‑14, a decline from 12.6 million in 2001, reflecting both legislative enforcement and shifting economic structures.

Policy interventions aim to preserve beneficial intergenerational learning while eliminating hazardous exposure. The National Child Labour Project (NCLP) provides bridge schools and vocational training for rescued children, integrating them into mainstream education. Simultaneously, the Skill India Mission encourages formal certification of traditional crafts, enabling adolescents to transition from unpaid family labour to recognised apprenticeships with stipends. Teachers can illustrate these dynamics by mapping a local craft value chain, identifying tasks suitable for different age groups, and discussing how legislation shapes permissible participation.

Assessment of this concept in examinations often requires candidates to differentiate between “family‑based skill transmission” and “child labour” using statutory definitions. A typical question may present a scenario describing a 12‑year‑old assisting in a family pottery unit for four hours after school and ask whether the situation contravenes the 1986 Act. The correct response cites the three‑hour limit, the non‑hazardous nature of pottery, and the primacy of school attendance under RTE, concluding that the engagement exceeds permissible duration and therefore constitutes a violation.

Migration, Remittances, and Changing Household Dynamics

Internal and international migration has reshaped Indian family structures over the past three decades. The 2011 Census recorded 453 million internal migrants, with a significant proportion moving from rural to urban areas for employment in construction, domestic work, and manufacturing. This mobility creates “split households” where one or both parents reside in destination cities while children remain in the village under the care of grandparents or older siblings. Remittances become the primary income source, altering consumption patterns, educational investment, and gender roles within the origin household.

Remittance flows influence child well‑being in measurable ways. National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) data from the 75th round (2017‑18) indicate that households receiving remittances tend to allocate a higher share of expenditure to education and health care compared to non‑remittance households. However, the absence of parental supervision can increase children’s vulnerability to neglect, irregular school attendance, and early entry into labour. Studies by the Institute for Human Development (IHD) report that left‑behind children in states such as Bihar and Uttar Pradesh often exhibit lower learning outcomes in language and mathematics compared to peers living with both parents.

Gendered impacts are pronounced. When fathers migrate, mothers often assume sole decision‑making authority, leading to increased participation in household financial management and agricultural decisions. This shift can empower women but also intensifies their time poverty, as they juggle farm work, domestic chores, and childcare. Conversely, when mothers migrate — a growing trend in states like Kerala and Tamil Nadu — fathers or grandmothers become primary caregivers, challenging traditional gender norms around nurturing. The Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA) provides a safety net that may reduce distress migration, yet implementation gaps mean many families still rely on circular migration.

Policy responses include the “One Nation One Ration Card” scheme, enabling migrant families to access subsidised food grains at destination locations, and the “Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Maan‑Dhan” pension scheme for unorganised workers, which extends social security to migrant labourers. At the community level, Anganwadi centres and school‑based tracking systems monitor attendance of left‑behind children, triggering home visits when absenteeism exceeds thresholds. Teachers can use migration maps to visualise source‑destination corridors, discuss remittance‑driven changes in household budgets, and evaluate the effectiveness of social protection programmes.

Examination questions frequently test the ability to link migration data with child‑development indicators. A typical item may provide a table showing school enrolment rates for children in remittance‑receiving versus non‑receiving households across three states and ask candidates to infer the net effect of migration on education. The expected answer acknowledges the positive resource effect while highlighting the mediating role of parental presence, citing relevant NSSO and IHD findings.

Case‑Study Analysis
A 10‑year‑old child in a migrant household spends three hours daily helping with packaging goods for a home‑based enterprise. Using the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 and the Right to Education Act, 2009, explain whether this engagement constitutes child labour and what protective measures apply.
The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, as amended in 2016, prohibits employment of children below 14 years in any occupation. It allows children to assist in family enterprises only after school hours, for a maximum of three hours per day, provided the work is non‑hazardous. The Right to Education Act, 2009 mandates free and compulsory education for children aged 6‑14, making school attendance a legal right. In the scenario, the child is 10 years old (below 14) and spends three hours daily on packaging. Although the duration matches the statutory limit, the Act requires that such assistance occur *after* school hours. If the child’s schooling is regular and the three hours are truly after school, the engagement may be permissible. However, any interference with school attendance — such as fatigue, late arrival, or missed classes — would violate RTE. Protective measures include monitoring by the School Management Committee, referral to the District Child Protection Unit (DCPU) under the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, and enrolment in bridge education programmes under the National Child Labour Project (NCLP) if schooling is disrupted.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a scenario mentions a child’s age, hours of work, and school status, first identify the applicable age threshold (14 years under the 1986 Act). Next, check whether the work is hazardous (Schedule list) and whether the hours exceed the three‑hour post‑school limit. Finally, cross‑reference with RTE’s compulsory attendance clause. Mark keywords — “after school”, “non‑hazardous”, “regular attendance” — to quickly decide compliance or violation.

Digital Media, Screen Time, and Peer Interaction Patterns

The proliferation of affordable smartphones and data plans has transformed how children experience friendship and play. The Telecom Regulatory Authority of India (TRAI) reported over 800 million wireless internet subscribers in 2023, with a substantial share in the 10‑17 age group. Digital platforms — messaging apps, multiplayer games, and social media — now mediate peer communication, often replacing face‑to‑face interaction in neighbourhoods and schoolyards. This shift raises questions about social skill development, exposure to cyber‑risks, and the redefinition of “play” in the EVS curriculum.

Surveys by the National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT) suggest that urban primary‑school children spend more time on screens for non‑educational purposes than their rural counterparts. Excessive screen time correlates with reduced physical activity, sleep disruption, and diminished empathy scores in standardised socio‑emotional assessments. However, moderated digital engagement can support collaborative problem‑solving, language acquisition, and cultural exchange, especially for children in geographically isolated communities.

The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, impose obligations on platforms to protect child users, including age‑verification mechanisms, parental‑control tools, and grievance‑redressal officers. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012, extends to online offences, criminalising cyber‑grooming and the circulation of child sexual abuse material. Schools are encouraged to adopt “Digital Citizenship” modules within EVS, covering netiquette, privacy settings, and reporting mechanisms.

Pedagogically, teachers can design activities that juxtapose traditional games (e.g., kho‑kho, gilli‑danda) with digital equivalents, prompting learners to analyse rules, teamwork, and physical exertion. A “screen‑time diary” exercise, where students log daily digital use and reflect on mood and peer interaction quality, makes abstract data personal. Assessment may ask students to propose a balanced “play charter” that allocates time for outdoor, indoor, and digital play, justifying allocations with health guidelines from the World Health Organization (WHO) recommending limited recreational screen time for children aged 5‑17.

Examiners often test the ability to link legal provisions with classroom practice. A question might present a case where a 12‑year‑old experiences cyber‑bullying on a gaming platform and ask which statutes empower the school and the child’s guardian to seek redress. The correct answer cites the IT Rules, 2021 (platform accountability), POCSO Act (criminal offence), and the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015 (child‑friendly procedures), while also noting the school’s duty under the RTE Act to provide a safe learning environment.

Animal Husbandry, Pet Keeping, and Children's Responsibility Development

In many Indian households, animals are integral to livelihood and companionship. The 20th Livestock Census (2019) recorded 535 million livestock, with cattle, buffalo, goats, and poultry dominating rural economies. Children in pastoral and farming families routinely participate in feeding, milking, grazing, and health monitoring of livestock. This involvement cultivates responsibility, observational skills, and an understanding of biological cycles — concepts directly aligned with EVS themes of “animals” and “work”.

The distinction between beneficial participation and exploitative labour again rests on age‑appropriateness and hazard exposure. Guidelines from the Animal Welfare Board of India (AWBI) recommend that children handling large animals be supervised by adults due to injury risk. The Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960, mandates humane treatment, and any task causing animal distress — such as forced milking or beating — is illegal regardless of the performer’s age. In urban settings, pet keeping (dogs, cats, birds) offers children routine caregiving tasks: feeding, grooming, walking, and veterinary visits. Research associates pet care with higher empathy scores and lower anxiety in children aged 8‑12.

School‑based programmes like “Eco‑Clubs” and “Animal Welfare Weeks” incorporate visits to gaushalas (cow shelters), veterinary colleges, and animal‑rescue centres. These experiences link classroom learning on nutrition, reproduction, and disease prevention with real‑world practice. Teachers can assign “animal‑care logs” where students record daily observations of a household animal’s behaviour, diet, and health indicators, fostering scientific recording habits.

Policy frameworks support humane education. The National Education Policy (NEP) 2020 emphasizes values including care for living beings in the foundational stage curriculum. The Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE) has issued circulars encouraging schools to adopt “compassionate citizen” modules that include animal‑welfare topics. Assessment items may ask learners to design a “pet‑care schedule” for a hypothetical family, ensuring compliance with the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act and the child’s school timetable.

Examination traps include conflating “animal husbandry” with “child labour” without applying the hazard‑and‑hours test, or ignoring the legal requirement for adult supervision when children interact with large livestock. Candidates should remember that the 1960 Act applies to all persons, and that the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, treats cruelty to animals by a child as a behavioural indicator warranting counselling rather than punitive action.

Inclusive Classroom Practices for Children from Non‑Traditional Families

Contemporary Indian classrooms encompass children from single‑parent households, adoptive families, same‑sex parent families (though legal recognition remains limited), kinship‑care arrangements, and child‑headed households. Census data records millions of single‑parent households, and the National Family Health Survey‑5 (2019‑21) notes increasing prevalence of female‑headed households due to widowhood, separation, and migration. EVS pedagogy must address this diversity without stigmatisation, ensuring that every child’s family reality is reflected in learning materials and discussions.

The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009, guarantees non‑discriminatory access, while the National Curriculum Framework (NCF) 2005 advocates inclusive textbooks that depict varied family forms. NCERT’s “Looking Around” EVS textbooks (Classes III‑V) have progressively included illustrations of single mothers, grandparents as primary caregivers, and foster families. Teachers can supplement these with locally sourced narratives — such as a grandmother narrating her role in raising grandchildren after parental migration — to validate lived experiences.

Practical strategies include “family‑tree” activities that allow flexible representation (e.g., using circles for caregivers rather than rigid father‑mother boxes), “story circles” where children share a favourite family ritual, and “resource‑mapping” of community support systems (anganwadis, child‑helplines, NGOs). These approaches foster empathy, reduce bullying rooted in family‑type stereotypes, and align with the Sustainable Development Goal 4 (quality education) target on inclusive learning environments.

Assessment of inclusive practice may involve scenario‑based questions: “A Class IV student refuses to participate in a ‘Father’s Day’ card‑making activity because she lives with her mother and grandmother. Suggest two pedagogical responses that respect the child’s family structure while meeting the lesson’s objective of expressing gratitude.” Expected answers emphasise offering alternative prompts (“Make a card for a caregiver you appreciate”) and using the moment to discuss diverse caregiving roles, referencing the NCF’s principle of “respect for diversity”.

Examiners often test awareness of legal safeguards. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, defines “child in need of care and protection” to include those without parental care, enabling placement in kinship or foster care. The Adoption Regulations, 2017, under the Juvenile Justice Act, govern domestic and inter‑country adoption, ensuring that adoptive families receive legal recognition. Teachers should be familiar with these provisions to answer questions on the statutory basis for inclusive school policies.

  • Confusing the three‑hour post‑school limit under the Child Labour Act with a blanket permission for any family work.
  • Assuming remittance income automatically improves child outcomes without considering parental absence effects.
  • Treating all screen time as detrimental, ignoring evidence on moderated educational use.
  • Overlooking the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act when evaluating children’s livestock tasks.
  • Using rigid family‑tree templates that exclude single‑parent, adoptive, or kinship‑care structures.
  • Neglecting the Juvenile Justice Act’s definition of “child in need of care and protection” when designing inclusive classroom policies.

Climate Vulnerability, Livelihood Shocks, and Child Work Allocation in Rural Households

The National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008) identifies eight national missions, notably the National Water Mission and the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture, which directly affect rural livelihoods. The National Disaster Management Act, 2005, empowers state authorities to declare “affected areas” and mobilise relief, but lacks a specific clause protecting children from hazardous recovery work. Census 2011 data indicate that a majority of rural households rely primarily on agriculture and allied activities. When monsoon failure or flood destroys crops, household income collapses and the opportunity cost of schooling rises sharply. NFHS‑5 (2019‑21) reports that 4.3 % of children aged 5‑14 were engaged in economic activity during the reference week; the proportion rises to 7.8 % in the poorest wealth quintile and in states such as Bihar and Jharkhand where climate‑induced distress is recurrent.

The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, as amended in 2016, prohibits employment of children below 14 years in all occupations except family enterprises and the entertainment industry, provided the work is non‑hazardous and does not interfere with schooling. The amendment introduces a “hazard‑and‑hours” test: any work exceeding three hours a day or performed in hazardous processes (Schedule I) is illegal irrespective of the family‑enterprise exemption. The Supreme Court in M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996) held that employing children in hazardous industries violates constitutional protections (Articles 24, 39) and directed their rehabilitation. In practice, climate‑driven migration often displaces families to urban informal settlements where children are absorbed into construction, brick‑kiln, or domestic work — sectors explicitly listed as hazardous under the 2016 amendment.

ILO and UNICEF analyses suggest that each additional drought year increases the probability of a child entering the labour force in rain‑fed districts. The mechanism operates through three channels: (1) loss of agricultural income forces households to deploy all able‑bodied members; (2) school infrastructure is damaged or teachers are absent, reducing perceived returns to education; (3) social protection schemes such as MGNREGA provide adult wage days but do not automatically cover adolescent labour, leaving a gap that children fill. Policy responses with measurable impact include the “Climate‑Resilient Agriculture” component of PMKSY and the integration of child‑labour monitoring into District Child Protection Units (DCPUs) under ICPS. Examination questions may test the ability to link a climate shock to a statutory provision (e.g., Section 3 of the 2016 amendment) and to identify the correct enforcement authority (the Labour Commissioner, not the school principal).

Legal Provisions for Child Participation in Family and Community Decisions

India ratified the UNCRC in 1992; Article 12 obliges State Parties to assure the child capable of forming views the right to express them freely in all matters affecting the child, with due weight given to age and maturity. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 operationalises this principle. Section 2(14) defines a “child in need of care and protection” to include a child “without parental care” or “victim of abuse”, while Section 27(2) mandates that “the Committee shall, before passing any order, give the child an opportunity to be heard”. The Act establishes Child Welfare Committees (CWCs) at the district level as the competent authority to determine the child’s best interests, requiring at least one member with expertise in child psychology or social work.

At the grassroots, the Ministry of Women and Child Development issued guidelines (2013) recommending Bal Panchayats (children’s councils) in every Gram Panchayat. Maharashtra’s Bal Panchayat model, notified under the Maharashtra Village Panchayats Act, 1958, reserves two seats for children aged 12‑18 and empowers them to submit resolutions on school infrastructure, water supply, and child‑protection issues. The National Policy for Children, 2013, reiterates that “children’s participation shall be institutionalised in all policies and programmes affecting them”. The RTE Act, 2009 includes School Management Committees (SMCs) where parent representatives — often mothers — voice concerns; however, the Act does not statutorily require child members, a gap highlighted by the NCPCR in its 2017 advisory.

Judicial interpretation has reinforced participatory rights. In Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) the Supreme Court, while striking down instant triple talaq, emphasised that children’s voices in personal law matters cannot be ignored. More directly, the Delhi High Court in Bachpan Bachao Andolan v. Union of India (2011) directed that children rescued from forced labour be consulted before rehabilitation orders are passed, citing Section 27(2) of the JJ Act. Examination items often present a scenario — e.g., a 13‑year‑old girl wishes to oppose her family’s decision to marry her off — and ask which legal provision empowers her to be heard. The correct answer is Section 27(2) of the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, read with Article 12 UNCRC, not the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006, which deals with the offence but not the procedural right to participation.

Technique
A Class VIII student is asked: “Which statutory provision requires a Child Welfare Committee to hear the child before passing an order?” Options: (a) Section 12 of the RTE Act, (b) Section 27(2) of the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, (c) Section 3 of the Child Labour Amendment Act, 2016, (d) Article 21A of the Constitution.
The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, is the primary legislation governing child‑protection proceedings. Section 27(2) explicitly states that “the Committee shall, before passing any order, give the child an opportunity to be heard”. The RTE Act concerns school management, the Child Labour Amendment Act regulates employment, and Article 21A guarantees education but does not prescribe procedural hearing rights in protection proceedings. Therefore, option (b) is the correct answer.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the legal basis of child participation, first locate the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015 — specifically Section 27(2) — and the UNCRC Article 12. Eliminate any option that references education, labour, or constitutional rights without a procedural hearing component. Remember that the JJ Act creates the Child Welfare Committee as the decision‑making body; the RTE Act’s School Management Committee has no statutory role in protection orders.

Disability, Caregiving Burden, and Sibling Roles in Indian Families

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 (RPwD Act) expanded the definition of disability from seven to twenty‑one conditions and mandated 4 % reservation in government employment and 5 % in higher education. Census 2011 recorded 26.8 million persons with disabilities (2.21 % of the population); NFHS‑5 (2019‑21) estimates a prevalence of around 2.5 % among children aged 0‑17 years. The National Trust Act, 1999, provides for legal guardianship and supported decision‑making, but implementation remains uneven across states.

Caregiving in Indian households is heavily gendered. Time‑use surveys (NSSO 2019) show women spend an average of 4.5 hours per day on unpaid care work, compared with 1.2 hours for men. When a child with a disability is present, the primary caregiver is most often the mother; however, qualitative studies (UNICEF 2018; “Young Carers in India” 2020) document that older siblings — particularly girls aged 10‑14 — assume substantial caregiving tasks: feeding, medication administration, mobility assistance, and emotional support. Studies estimate that over a million children in India function as “young carers”, minors who provide regular, significant care for a family member with illness or disability. These children experience higher school‑absenteeism and report psychosocial stress levels comparable to adult caregivers.

The RPwD Act, 2016, under Section 38, obliges the appropriate government to “provide support services to families of persons with disabilities, including respite care and counselling”. The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017, further recognises the right of caregivers to access mental‑health services. Yet, the ICDS scheme lacks a dedicated component for sibling caregivers, and the Mid‑Day Meal Scheme covers only school‑going children, leaving young carers who miss school without nutritional support. Examination questions may ask: “Which Act mandates the provision of respite care for families of children with disabilities?” The answer is the RPwD Act, 2016 (Section 38), not the National Trust Act, 1999, which focuses on guardianship. Another common trap is conflating “young carer” with “child labour”; the Child Labour Amendment Act, 2016, exempts family‑based care provided it is non‑hazardous and does not exceed three hours daily, but the exemption does not apply when caregiving interferes with the child’s right to education under Article 21A.

Community Child Protection Committees: Structure, Mandate, and Operational Challenges

The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, establishes a two‑tier institutional framework: Child Welfare Committees (CWCs) at the district level and Juvenile Justice Boards (JJBs) for children in conflict with law. Complementing this, the Integrated Child Protection Scheme (ICPS), launched in 2009 and subsumed under “Mission Vatsalya” in 2021, mandates the formation of Village Level Child Protection Committees (VLCPCs) in every Gram Panchayat. A typical VLCPC comprises the Sarpanch (chairperson), an Auxiliary Nurse Midwife (ANM), the headmaster of the local school, a representative of a registered NGO working on child rights, and two child representatives (one boy, one girl) nominated by the Bal Panchayat where it exists.

The mandate of the VLCPC includes: (i) identification and referral of children in need of care and protection to the CWC within 24 hours; (ii) monitoring of child‑labour, child‑marriage, and trafficking incidents in the village; (iii) facilitating enrolment and retention of out‑of‑school children; (iv) conducting quarterly “child‑safety audits” of public spaces such as anganwadis, schools, and playgrounds. The ICPS guidelines (2011, revised 2018) allocate a recurring grant for meeting expenses, training, and awareness campaigns. However, a 2022 review by the Ministry of Women and Child Development found that less than half of Gram Panchayats had a functional VLCPC, and only a minority submitted quarterly reports to the District Child Protection Unit (DCPU).

Operational challenges are structural and resource‑based. First, the Sarpanch often lacks training in child‑protection law, leading to misclassification of cases (e.g., treating a runaway adolescent as a “missing person” rather than a “child in need of care”). Second, the ANM and school headmaster are already burdened with health and education targets, limiting their bandwidth for protection work. Third, child representatives are frequently tokenistic; many Bal Panchayats exist only on paper, and nominated children receive no orientation on reporting mechanisms. Fourth, fund flow is delayed — audits report significant disbursement lags. Fifth, political interference can result in the suppression of referrals involving locally influential families. Examination questions may present a case where a VLCPC fails to refer a child marriage to the CWC within the stipulated period and ask which statutory provision imposes a penalty. The answer lies in Section 33 of the JJ Act, 2015, which prescribes imprisonment up to six months or fine for any person who fails to report a child in need of care and protection, and the JJ Model Rules, 2016, which specify the 24‑hour referral timeline for VLCPCs.

Intra‑Household Food Distribution, Nutritional Outcomes, and Gendered Consumption Patterns

The National Food Security Act, 2013 (NFSA) entitles “priority households” — identified through the Socio‑Economic and Caste Census (SECC) — to 5 kg of foodgrains per person per month at subsidised prices (₹3/kg rice, ₹2/kg wheat, ₹1/kg coarse grains). The Act also provides for maternity benefits of ₹6,000 per pregnancy and a “take‑home ration” for children aged 6 months‑6 years under ICDS. The POSHAN Abhiyaan (National Nutrition Mission), launched in March 2018, sets a target to reduce stunting from 38.4 % (NFHS‑4) to 25 % by 2022, and to reduce anaemia among women of reproductive age by 3 percentage points per year.

NFHS‑5 (2019‑21) reports that 35.5 % of children under five are stunted, 19.3 % wasted, and 32.1 % underweight. Anaemia prevalence among women aged 15‑49 stands at 57 % (up from 53 % in NFHS‑4). Intra‑household allocation studies using IHDS (2011‑12) and NSS consumption expenditure data (2015‑16) reveal a consistent gender gap: in households with both male and female children, girls receive significantly less protein and iron‑rich food than boys of the same age. The gap widens in poorer quintiles and in regions with strong son‑preference norms (e.g., parts of Haryana, Punjab, and Rajasthan). Qualitative work by IFPRI (2020) shows that mothers often buffer daughters’ intake during lean seasons, but the buffering is insufficient to close the micronutrient gap.

The Mid‑Day Meal Scheme (MDMS), covering 11.8 crore children in government and aided schools (2022‑23), provides a cooked meal supplying 450 kcal and 12 g protein per child per day. However, the scheme does not reach children who are out of school — a group disproportionately comprising girls engaged in domestic work or sibling care. The NFSA’s “take‑home ration” for ICDS beneficiaries is meant to compensate, yet CAG audits have reported significant diversion in several states. Examination items may ask: “Which programme directly addresses the gendered intra‑household food gap for school‑age girls?” The correct response is the “Kishori Shakti Yojana” (now subsumed under the Scheme for Adolescent Girls, SAG) which provides supplementary nutrition and life‑skill education to out‑of‑school girls aged 11‑18, not the MDMS which is school‑based. Another trap is assuming the NFSA’s uniform entitlement eliminates intra‑household disparity; the Act guarantees household‑level entitlement, not individual‑level consumption, and does not mandate gender‑disaggregated monitoring.

  • Confusing the three‑hour family‑enterprise exemption under the Child Labour Amendment Act, 2016, with a blanket permission for any domestic work performed by children.
  • Attributing child‑participation rights solely to the RTE Act, 2009, while overlooking Section 27(2) of the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, and Article 12 UNCRC.
  • Treating “young carer” as a synonym for “child labour” without applying the hazard‑and‑hours test and the education‑interference criterion.
  • Assuming Village Level Child Protection Committees are universally functional; data shows less than half are active and many lack trained child representatives.
  • Believing the NFSA’s per‑capita grain entitlement automatically ensures equitable intra‑household nutrition; the Act does not regulate within‑household distribution.
  • Overlooking the RPwD Act, 2016, Section 38, which mandates respite care for families of children with disabilities, and instead citing the National Trust Act, 1999.
  • Misidentifying the Mid‑Day Meal Scheme as the primary intervention for out‑of‑school adolescent girls’ nutrition, ignoring the Scheme for Adolescent Girls (SAG) and Kishori Shakti Yojana.
