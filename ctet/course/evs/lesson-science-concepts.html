Basic Science Concepts for EVS

Science in EVS introduces children to basic concepts about the natural and physical world. It builds observation skills, curiosity, and scientific thinking. Topics cover biological sciences (plants, animals, human body), physical sciences (matter, force, energy, light, sound), and Earth sciences (weather, seasons, celestial bodies).

Key Concepts

Plants: Parts of a plant and their functions: roots (absorb water and minerals, anchor the plant), stem (transport water and nutrients, support), leaves (photosynthesis - making food using sunlight, CO2, water, and chlorophyll), flowers (reproduction - produce seeds and fruits). Types of plants: herbs (small, soft stem), shrubs (medium, woody stem), trees (tall, thick trunk), climbers (need support), creepers (grow along ground). Photosynthesis is the process by which plants make their own food using sunlight, carbon dioxide, and water, releasing oxygen as a by-product.

Animals: Classification by habitat: terrestrial (land - lion, elephant, cow), aquatic (water - fish, whale, frog), amphibious (both land and water - frog, crocodile), aerial (air - birds, bats). Classification by food habits: herbivores (eat plants - cow, deer, rabbit), carnivores (eat flesh - lion, tiger, snake), omnivores (eat both - bear, crow, human). Adaptations: camels store water in humps, polar bears have thick fur, fish have gills for breathing underwater, birds have hollow bones for flying.

Human Body: Major organ systems: Skeletal system (bones provide structure, protect organs - 206 bones in adults), Muscular system (movement), Digestive system (mouth, esophagus, stomach, intestines break down food), Respiratory system (nose, trachea, lungs breathe in oxygen, expel CO2), Circulatory system (heart pumps blood through vessels), Nervous system (brain, spinal cord, nerves control all functions), Excretory system (kidneys filter waste, produce urine). Sense organs: eyes (sight), ears (hearing), nose (smell), tongue (taste), skin (touch).

Matter and Materials: Matter exists in three states: Solid (fixed shape and volume - table, stone), Liquid (fixed volume, takes shape of container - water, milk), Gas (no fixed shape or volume, fills container - air, oxygen). Changes of state: melting (solid to liquid), freezing (liquid to solid), evaporation (liquid to gas), condensation (gas to liquid). Properties of materials: hard/soft, rough/smooth, transparent/translucent/opaque, soluble/insoluble, float/sink, magnetic/non-magnetic.

Force, Energy and Motion: Force is a push or pull that can change an object's shape, speed, direction, or state of rest. Types: gravity (Earth pulls objects down), friction (resistance when surfaces rub), magnetic force (attraction/repulsion by magnets), muscular force (generated by muscles). Simple machines: lever (seesaw), pulley (flag hoist), inclined plane (ramp), wheel and axle (bicycle), screw, wedge. Energy forms: heat (thermal), light (solar), sound (acoustic), electrical, mechanical. Light travels in straight lines; sound needs a medium to travel; shadows form when light is blocked by an opaque object.

Solved Examples

Example 1
The process by which plants make their own food using sunlight is called:

a) Respiration b) Photosynthesis c) Transpiration d) Germination
Solution: b) Photosynthesis. This process occurs in leaves (containing chlorophyll) where sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide are converted into glucose (food) and oxygen. The chemical equation is: 6CO2 + 6H2O + sunlight -> C6H12O6 + 6O2. Respiration is how organisms release energy from food; transpiration is water loss from leaves; germination is seed sprouting.
Example 2
Which of the following animals is an omnivore?

a) Cow b) Tiger c) Bear d) Deer
Solution: c) Bear. Omnivores eat both plants and animals. Bears eat berries, fruits, honey, fish, and small mammals. Cows and deer are herbivores (only plants); tigers are carnivores (only flesh). Humans are also omnivores. Understanding food habits helps children understand food chains and ecological relationships.
Example 3
Water changing into water vapor on heating is an example of:

a) Condensation b) Evaporation c) Freezing d) Melting
Solution: b) Evaporation. Evaporation is the process where a liquid changes into a gas (vapor) when heated. The reverse process, where gas changes to liquid, is condensation. Freezing is liquid to solid, and melting is solid to liquid. Evaporation is why wet clothes dry, and condensation is why water droplets form on a cold glass.

Shortcut Techniques

  • States of Matter - S-L-G: Solid (fixed shape/volume), Liquid (fixed volume, variable shape), Gas (variable shape/volume). Think of ice (solid), water (liquid), steam (gas) as a continuous sequence.
  • Plant Parts - Roots to Fruits: Remember the direction: Roots go down -> Stem goes up -> Leaves make food -> Flowers become Fruits -> Fruits contain Seeds. Think of a growing sequence from ground up.
  • Animal Diets - HCO: Herbivores eat Herbs (plants), Carnivores eat Car/flesh, Omnivores eat Overall (everything). Use the first letter of each group to recall the diet type.
  • Changes of State - F-M-E-C: Freezing (L->S), Melting (S->L), Evaporation (L->G), Condensation (G->L). Remember: going up = heat added (melting, evaporation), going down = heat removed (freezing, condensation).

Having children grow a bean plant in a cup, observe evaporation of water, or sort materials by properties makes abstract concepts concrete. Connect science to everyday observations - why do we see dew in winter? Why do iron gates rust?

Newton's Laws and Their Limitations in Everyday Contexts

Newton's first law states that a body remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by a net external force; this property is called inertia and is quantified by mass. The second law gives the quantitative relationship F = m a, where the net force vector equals mass times acceleration vector, and it also defines the unit of force, the newton. The third law asserts that for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction, meaning forces always occur in pairs acting on different bodies. Together these three statements form the foundation of classical mechanics and are sufficient for analyzing most macroscopic motions encountered in daily life.

Despite their broad applicability, the laws have well‑defined limits. They assume an inertial reference frame; in accelerating frames fictitious forces such as the Coriolis or centrifugal force must be introduced to retain the form F = m a. At speeds approaching a significant fraction of the speed of light, relativistic corrections become necessary because mass is no longer constant and the simple linear relation between force and acceleration breaks down. At atomic and sub‑atomic scales quantum effects dominate, and the deterministic trajectory concept implicit in Newtonian mechanics is replaced by probabilistic wave functions. Even in ordinary situations, non‑conservative forces like friction and air resistance mean that the net force is often a sum of many components, and the idealised “free‑body” picture must be supplemented with empirical coefficients.

Everyday examples illustrate both the power and the boundaries of the laws. When a car brakes, the friction between tyres and road provides the net force that decelerates the vehicle; the driver feels a forward push because of inertia (first law). A rocket lifts off by expelling high‑speed gases downward; the reaction force on the rocket (third law) propels it upward, while the second law governs the acceleration as mass decreases. Walking involves pushing backward on the ground; the ground pushes forward on the foot, and friction prevents slipping. In each case the net force is the vector sum of several interactions, and the simple F = m a applies only to the centre‑of‑mass motion of the system considered.

Examiners frequently test the distinction between mass and weight, the identification of the correct net force in a free‑body diagram, and the recognition that action–reaction pairs act on different bodies and therefore never cancel each other for a single object. A common twist is to present a scenario with multiple forces (gravity, normal, tension, friction) and ask which force is responsible for a given acceleration, requiring the student to isolate the net force component along the direction of motion.

Energy Transformations and Conservation in Closed Systems

Work is defined as the product of the component of force along the displacement and the magnitude of that displacement; when a constant force F acts through a distance d in the same direction, the work done is W = F d. Kinetic energy K = ½ m v² measures the energy of motion, while gravitational potential energy U = m g h (near Earth’s surface) measures the energy stored by virtue of position in a uniform field. The mechanical energy of a system is the sum E = K + U. In a closed system where only conservative forces (gravity, ideal spring force) act, the total mechanical energy remains constant: ΔK + ΔU = 0.

Real systems are rarely perfectly closed. Non‑conservative forces such as kinetic friction, air drag, and inelastic deformation convert mechanical energy into internal energy (heat, sound), so the mechanical energy decreases while the total energy of the universe is conserved. The work–energy theorem extends to include non‑conservative work: W_nc = ΔK + ΔU. Efficiency of a process is defined as the ratio of useful output energy to total input energy, often expressed as a percentage. In a simple pendulum, for example, the bob’s kinetic energy is maximum at the lowest point and zero at the turning points, while potential energy shows the opposite behaviour; a small amount of energy is lost each swing due to air resistance and pivot friction, causing the amplitude to decay gradually.

Roller‑coaster design provides a vivid illustration of energy transformation. The train is hauled to the top of the first hill, storing gravitational potential energy. As it descends, potential energy converts to kinetic energy, reaching maximum speed at the bottom. Subsequent hills are lower because some energy has been dissipated as heat and sound; the train cannot climb higher than the initial hill without an external energy input. A bouncing ball demonstrates repeated conversion between kinetic and elastic potential energy, with each bounce losing a fraction of its mechanical energy to sound and internal heating, which is why the rebound height diminishes.

Examination questions often ask for the speed of an object after falling a given height, the maximum compression of a spring when a mass is dropped onto it, or the fraction of initial mechanical energy lost in a collision. A typical twist is to provide a scenario with a non‑conservative force (e.g., a block sliding down a rough incline) and require the student to compute the work done by friction from the change in mechanical energy, then deduce the coefficient of friction. Another variant asks for the efficiency of a simple machine given input work and useful output work.

Worked Example: Pendulum Energy Loss
A simple pendulum of length 1.0 m and bob mass 0.2 kg is released from rest at an angle of 30° from the vertical. After one complete oscillation the maximum angle reduces to 25°. Assuming the only non‑conservative force is air resistance, calculate the average power dissipated by air resistance during the oscillation. (Take g = 9.8 m/s².)
The initial height of the bob relative to the lowest point is h₁ = L(1 – cos 30°) = 1.0 × (1 – 0.8660) = 0.134 m. The initial potential energy is U₁ = m g h₁ = 0.2 × 9.8 × 0.134 ≈ 0.263 J. After one oscillation the height is h₂ = L(1 – cos 25°) = 1.0 × (1 – 0.9063) = 0.0937 m, giving U₂ = 0.2 × 9.8 × 0.0937 ≈ 0.184 J. The mechanical energy lost is ΔE = U₁ – U₂ ≈ 0.079 J. The period of a simple pendulum for small angles is T ≈ 2π√(L/g) = 2π√(1.0/9.8) ≈ 2.01 s. One complete oscillation takes one period, so the average power dissipated is P = ΔE / T ≈ 0.079 J / 2.01 s ≈ 0.039 W.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem involves energy loss, first write the energy balance equation including a term for non‑conservative work. Identify the initial and final states clearly, compute the mechanical energies, and subtract to find the energy dissipated. If a time interval is given or can be derived (e.g., period of oscillation), divide the energy loss by that time to obtain average power. Keep units consistent and check that the answer’s magnitude is physically plausible.

Wave Nature of Light: Interference, Diffraction, and Polarization

Light exhibits wave behaviour characterised by wavelength λ, frequency f, and speed c in vacuum (c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s). The relationship c = λ f holds for all electromagnetic waves. Because light is a transverse wave, its electric and magnetic field vectors oscillate perpendicular to the direction of propagation. This transverse nature underlies phenomena such as polarization, which cannot be explained by a purely particle model.

Interference occurs when two or more coherent light waves superpose. Constructive interference produces bright fringes where the path difference is an integer multiple of λ (Δ = m λ), while destructive interference yields dark fringes for half‑integer multiples (Δ = (m + ½) λ). The classic double‑slit experiment demonstrates this: light passing through two narrow, closely spaced slits creates an alternating pattern of bright and dark bands on a screen. The fringe spacing on a screen at distance D from slits separated by d is given by β = λ D / d for small angles.

Diffraction is the bending of light around obstacles or through apertures comparable in size to λ. A single slit of width a produces a central maximum of angular width 2λ/a, with minima at angles satisfying a sin θ = m λ (m ≠ 0). A diffraction grating, consisting of many equally spaced slits, yields sharp principal maxima at angles where d sin θ = m λ, enabling precise wavelength measurement. The resolving power of a grating increases with the number of illuminated slits and the order of diffraction.

Polarization exploits the transverse nature of light. Unpolarized light contains electric field vectors oriented randomly in the plane perpendicular to propagation. A linear polarizer transmits only the component of the electric field along its transmission axis, reducing intensity by half for ideal unpolarized light (Malus’s law: I = I₀ cos²θ for polarized incident light). Polarization by reflection occurs at Brewster’s angle, where reflected light becomes perfectly s‑polarized. Applications include glare‑reducing sunglasses, liquid‑crystal displays, and stress analysis in transparent materials using photoelasticity.

Sound Propagation: Medium Dependence, Doppler Effect, and Resonance

Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave that requires a material medium; its particles oscillate parallel to the direction of energy transport. The speed of sound v in a medium depends on the medium’s elastic modulus and density: v = √(B/ρ) for fluids (B = bulk modulus) and v = √(Y/ρ) for solids (Y = Young’s modulus). In air at 20 °C, v ≈ 343 m/s; it increases with temperature roughly as v ∝ √T (absolute temperature). Sound travels faster in liquids (≈1500 m/s in water) and fastest in solids (≈5000 m/s in steel) because of higher stiffness.

The Doppler effect describes the change in observed frequency when there is relative motion between source and observer. For a stationary observer and a source moving toward the observer at speed vₛ, the observed frequency f' = f v / (v – vₛ). If the observer moves toward a stationary source at speed vₒ, f' = f (v + vₒ) / v. When both move, the combined formula is f' = f (v ± vₒ) / (v ∓ vₛ), with upper signs for approach. This effect is used in radar speed guns, medical ultrasound, and astronomical redshift measurements.

Resonance occurs when a system is driven at its natural frequency, leading to a large amplitude response. A simple mass–spring system has natural angular frequency ω₀ = √(k/m). An air column in a tube closed at one end supports standing waves with a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end; its fundamental frequency is f₁ = v / (4L). For a tube open at both ends, f₁ = v / (2L). Musical instruments exploit resonance: the body of a violin amplifies string vibrations, and wind instruments select specific harmonics by opening or closing tone holes.

Examination questions often combine these concepts. A typical problem gives the speed of sound in air at a certain temperature, asks for the wavelength of a 440 Hz tuning fork, then asks how the wavelength changes if the temperature rises by 10 °C. Another asks for the beat frequency heard when two slightly different frequencies interfere, or for the length of a closed pipe needed to produce a given note. The Doppler effect may be tested with a moving ambulance siren, requiring careful sign selection for source and observer velocities.

Electric Circuits: Series, Parallel, and Combined Networks with Kirchhoff's Rules

Ohm’s law states that the current I through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage V across the points, with resistance R as the constant of proportionality: V = I R. Resistance depends on material resistivity ρ, length L, and cross‑sectional area A: R = ρ L / A. Power dissipated in a resistor is P = I² R = V² / R = V I. These relations form the basis for analysing any direct‑current (DC) circuit.

In a series connection, the same current flows through all components, and the total resistance is the sum: R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + … + Rₙ. The voltage divides proportionally to each resistance (voltage divider). In a parallel connection, the voltage across each branch is identical, and the reciprocal of the equivalent resistance equals the sum of reciprocals: 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … + 1/Rₙ. The total current splits among branches inversely proportional to their resistances (current divider). Series‑parallel networks are reduced step‑by‑step by identifying simple series or parallel groups, replacing them with their equivalents, and repeating until a single equivalent resistance remains.

Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL) expresses charge conservation at a junction: the algebraic sum of currents entering a node equals the sum leaving (ΣI_in = ΣI_out). Kirchhoff’s voltage law (KVL) expresses energy conservation around any closed loop: the algebraic sum of potential differences (voltage rises and drops) equals zero (ΣV = 0). To analyse a complex circuit, assign loop currents or branch currents, write KCL equations for independent nodes, and KVL equations for independent loops. Solve the resulting simultaneous linear equations for the unknown currents and voltages.

Practical circuit analysis often involves determining the power delivered to a load, the effect of internal resistance of a source, or the behaviour of a Wheatstone bridge. A Wheatstone bridge consists of four resistors in a diamond arrangement with a galvanometer across the bridge; when the bridge is balanced (R₁/R₂ = R₃/R₄), no current flows through the galvanometer, allowing precise measurement of an unknown resistance. Examiners may ask for the condition of balance, the current through the galvanometer when unbalanced, or the equivalent resistance seen by the source. Another common scenario is a combination of series and parallel resistors with a single battery, where the student must compute the total current, the voltage across a specific resistor, and the power dissipated in that resistor.

  • Confusing mass with weight and treating weight as a force that can be added to other forces without considering direction.
  • Assuming mechanical energy is conserved in the presence of friction or air resistance without accounting for non‑conservative work.
  • Applying the double‑slit interference formula to a single‑slit diffraction pattern or vice‑versa.
  • Using the wrong sign convention in the Doppler effect formula when both source and observer are moving.
  • Neglecting to apply Kirchhoff’s current law at every independent node, leading to an under‑determined system of equations.
  • Treating the equivalent resistance of a parallel network as the arithmetic sum of the individual resistances.
  • Forgetting that the power dissipated in a resistor depends on the square of the current, so halving the current reduces power to one‑quarter.

Thermodynamics: Laws, Heat Engines, and Entropy

Thermodynamics governs energy conversion in all physical processes. The zeroth law establishes thermal equilibrium and defines temperature: if two systems are each in equilibrium with a third, they are in equilibrium with each other. The first law expresses energy conservation for a thermodynamic system: ΔU = Q − W, where ΔU is the change in internal energy, Q is heat supplied to the system, and W is work done by the system. Sign conventions vary; in physics, work done by the system is positive, while in chemistry, work done on the system is often taken as positive. The second law introduces entropy (S) and the directionality of natural processes. For any reversible process, dS = δQ_rev / T; for irreversible processes, dS > δQ / T. The Clausius statement — heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body — and the Kelvin‑Planck statement — no process can convert heat completely into work without other effects — are equivalent formulations. A heat engine operates between a hot reservoir at T_H and a cold reservoir at T_C, absorbing Q_H, rejecting Q_C, and delivering work W = Q_H − Q_C. The maximum possible efficiency is the Carnot efficiency η_C = 1 − T_C / T_H (temperatures in kelvin). Real engines have lower efficiency due to friction, non‑quasi‑static steps, and finite temperature differences. The third law states that the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as temperature approaches absolute zero, providing an absolute reference for entropy values. In examinations, typical problems ask for the efficiency of a Carnot engine given reservoir temperatures, the entropy change when ice melts at 0 °C (ΔS = mL_f / T), or the work output of a cyclic process represented on a PV diagram. Remember that internal energy is a state function, so its change around a closed cycle is zero, while heat and work are path‑dependent.

Electromagnetic Induction: Faraday's Law, Lenz's Law, and AC Fundamentals

When magnetic flux through a circuit changes, an electromotive force (emf) is induced. Magnetic flux Φ = ∫ B·dA = BA cos θ for a uniform field B crossing a planar area A at angle θ. Faraday's law states that the induced emf ε = − dΦ/dt for a single loop; for a coil of N turns, ε = − N dΦ/dt. The minus sign embodies Lenz's law: the induced current creates a magnetic field opposing the change in flux that produced it. This principle underlies electric generators, transformers, and inductors. Self‑inductance L of a coil is defined by Φ = L I, giving ε = − L dI/dt. Mutual inductance M between two coils satisfies Φ21 = M I1. In an AC generator, a coil rotates at angular speed ω in a uniform magnetic field, producing a sinusoidal emf ε = ε_max sin ωt, where ε_max = N B A ω. The root‑mean‑square (RMS) values are V_rms = V_max / √2 and I_rms = I_max / √2. For a pure resistor, voltage and current are in phase; for an inductor, current lags voltage by 90°; for a capacitor, current leads voltage by 90°. The impedance Z of a series RLC circuit is Z = √(R² + (X_L − X_C)²) with X_L = ωL and X_C = 1/(ωC). The phase angle φ satisfies tan φ = (X_L − X_C)/R. Real power dissipated is P = V_rms I_rms cos φ, where cos φ is the power factor. Resonance occurs when X_L = X_C, giving minimum impedance Z = R and maximum current. Examination questions often require calculating the induced emf in a rotating coil, the RMS current in a series RLC circuit at a given frequency, or the condition for resonance in a parallel LC tank. Pay attention to the sign of the induced emf when applying Lenz's law qualitatively, and to the distinction between peak and RMS quantities in power calculations.

Worked Example: Maximum emf in a Rotating Coil
A rectangular coil of 200 turns, area 0.01 m², rotates at 60 revolutions per second in a uniform magnetic field of 0.5 T. The axis of rotation is perpendicular to the field direction. Calculate the maximum emf induced in the coil.
The angular speed ω = 2π × frequency = 2π × 60 = 120π rad s⁻¹. Maximum emf ε_max = N B A ω = 200 × 0.5 T × 0.01 m² × 120π rad s⁻¹ = 200 × 0.5 × 0.01 × 120π = 120π V ≈ 377 V. The emf varies sinusoidally as ε = ε_max sin ωt. The RMS value would be ε_max / √2 ≈ 267 V.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Induction Problems
First identify whether the flux change is due to a changing magnetic field, a changing area, or a changing orientation. Write Φ = BA cos θ explicitly, then differentiate with respect to time. Keep the negative sign for direction only; magnitude questions usually ask for the absolute value. Convert revolutions per second to rad s⁻¹ before substituting into ε_max = N B A ω. For AC circuits, draw a phasor diagram to visualise phase relationships; it prevents sign errors when computing impedance and power factor.

Geometrical Optics: Lenses, Mirrors, and Image Formation

Geometrical optics treats light as rays that travel in straight lines and obey the laws of reflection and refraction. For a spherical mirror, the mirror formula 1/f = 1/v + 1/u relates focal length f, image distance v, and object distance u. Sign convention (Cartesian): distances measured from the pole; incident light travels from left to right; distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive. Thus, for a concave mirror f is negative, for a convex mirror f is positive; real images have negative v, virtual images positive v. Magnification m = −v/u = h_i/h_o. For thin lenses, the lens formula 1/f = 1/v − 1/u (with the same sign convention) applies. The lens maker's formula 1/f = (n−1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂) links focal length to refractive index n and radii of curvature R₁, R₂ (positive if the centre of curvature lies to the right of the surface). A combination of thin lenses in contact has equivalent power P_eq = P₁ + P₂ + …, where power P = 1/f (in metres, dioptres). For separated lenses, use the matrix method or successive application of the lens formula. Common aberrations include spherical aberration (rays far from the axis focus at different points), chromatic aberration (different wavelengths have different focal lengths), and coma. Examination problems often ask for the position and nature of the image formed by a concave mirror when the object is placed between the focal point and the centre of curvature, or the focal length of a combination of a convex lens of +10 D and a concave lens of −5 D placed 0.2 m apart. Always draw a ray diagram to verify the sign of v and the orientation of the image.

Atomic Structure and Quantum Concepts: Bohr Model, Wave‑Particle Duality, and Spectra

The Bohr model (1913) postulates that electrons occupy discrete stationary orbits with quantised angular momentum L = nħ (n = 1,2,3…). The energy of the nth level in hydrogen‑like ions is E_n = − (Z² R_H) / n², where R_H = 13.6 eV is the Rydberg constant for hydrogen and Z is the nuclear charge. Transitions between levels emit or absorb photons of energy ΔE = hν = hc/λ, giving the Rydberg formula 1/λ = R_H Z² (1/n_f² − 1/n_i²). The Lyman series (n_f = 1) lies in the ultraviolet, Balmer (n_f = 2) in the visible, Paschen (n_f = 3) in the infrared. Wave‑particle duality is expressed by de Broglie's relation λ = h/p = h/(mv) for matter waves. The Davisson‑Germer experiment confirmed electron diffraction, validating the hypothesis. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle states Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 and ΔE Δt ≥ ħ/2, limiting simultaneous precision of conjugate variables. The photoelectric effect demonstrates particle nature of light: electrons are emitted only if photon frequency exceeds a threshold ν₀ = φ/h (φ = work function). The kinetic energy of emitted electrons is K_max = hν − φ, independent of intensity. Einstein's photoelectric equation earned the 1921 Nobel Prize. In examinations, typical tasks include calculating the wavelength of the first Balmer line (n_i = 3 → n_f = 2), the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through 100 V (λ = h/√(2meV) ≈ 0.123 nm), or explaining why the photoelectric current saturates at high anode voltage. Remember that the Bohr model works only for one‑electron systems; multi‑electron atoms require quantum numbers (n, l, m_l, m_s) and the Pauli exclusion principle.

Materials Science: Mechanical Properties, Phase Changes, and Applications

Understanding the response of solids to external forces is essential for engineering and everyday phenomena. Stress σ = F/A (force per unit area) and strain ε = ΔL/L (fractional deformation) are the primary descriptors. In the linear elastic region, Hooke's law σ = E ε defines Young's modulus E. Shear modulus G relates shear stress to shear strain (τ = G γ). Bulk modulus K = −V ΔP/ΔV measures resistance to uniform compression. Poisson's ratio ν = −ε_transverse/ε_axial typically lies between 0.25 and 0.35 for metals. Beyond the elastic limit, materials exhibit plastic deformation; the yield strength marks the onset of permanent strain. Ultimate tensile strength is the maximum stress on the engineering stress‑strain curve; fracture strength is the stress at break. Ductility is quantified by percent elongation or reduction of area. Hardness tests (Brinell, Rockwell, Vickers) provide a quick comparative measure of resistance to indentation. Phase diagrams map the equilibrium phases of an alloy as a function of temperature and composition. The iron‑carbon diagram is pivotal: ferrite (α‑Fe), austenite (γ‑Fe), cementite (Fe₃C), and pearlite (lamellar ferrite + cementite) appear. The eutectoid composition (0.76 wt % C) transforms at 727 °C. Heat treatments — annealing, normalising, quenching, tempering — manipulate microstructure to achieve desired hardness, toughness, and ductility. For example, quenching steel from the austenite region forms martensite, a supersaturated solid solution that is very hard but brittle; tempering reduces brittleness. Examination questions may ask for the stress required to produce a given strain in a steel rod (σ = E ε), the phase fractions in a hypoeutectoid steel at room temperature using the lever rule, or the effect of increasing carbon content on the eutectoid temperature. Always distinguish between engineering stress/strain (based on original dimensions) and true stress/strain (based on instantaneous dimensions) when large deformations are involved.

  • Confusing sign conventions in mirror and lens formulas, especially treating virtual image distances as negative when the Cartesian convention requires positive.
  • Using peak values instead of RMS values when calculating real power in AC circuits, leading to a factor‑of‑two error.
  • Applying the Carnot efficiency formula with temperatures in Celsius instead of Kelvin.
  • Neglecting the negative sign in Faraday's law when a question asks for the direction of induced current, not just magnitude.
  • Assuming the Bohr energy formula applies to multi‑electron atoms without screening corrections.
  • Mixing engineering stress with true strain in plasticity calculations, producing inconsistent results.
  • Forgetting that the lever rule applies only to two‑phase regions on a phase diagram, not to single‑phase fields.
