Food, Nutrition and Health

Food and health form a crucial theme in EVS education. Children learn about the sources of food, its nutritional value, balanced diet, food habits, hygiene, and the relationship between food and health. This knowledge empowers children to make healthy food choices and develop good health practices from an early age.

Key Concepts

Sources of Food: Food comes from two primary sources - plants (fruits, vegetables, grains, pulses, nuts, oils, spices) and animals (milk, eggs, meat, fish, honey). Children should understand that different foods come from different parts of plants - roots (carrot, radish), stems (potato, ginger), leaves (spinach, cabbage), flowers (cauliflower, broccoli), fruits (apple, mango), and seeds (rice, wheat, pulses). Also important is understanding food chains and the difference between herbivores, carnivores, and omnivores.

Food Groups and Nutrients: Major nutrients include Carbohydrates (energy giving - rice, wheat, bread, potato, sugar), Proteins (body building - pulses, milk, eggs, meat, fish, soybeans), Fats (energy storage, insulation - butter, oil, ghee, nuts), Vitamins (protective - fruits, vegetables), and Minerals (protective - green leafy vegetables, milk, eggs). Water and dietary fiber (roughage) are also essential. Each nutrient has specific functions and deficiency causes specific diseases.

Balanced Diet and Nutritional Deficiencies: A balanced diet contains all nutrients in correct proportions. Deficiency diseases: Vitamin A deficiency causes night blindness; Vitamin B1 causes beriberi; Vitamin C causes scurvy; Vitamin D causes rickets (bone deformities); Iron deficiency causes anemia (weakness, fatigue); Iodine deficiency causes goiter (thyroid swelling); Calcium deficiency affects bone and teeth health. The Mid-Day Meal Scheme in Indian schools aims to address malnutrition among children.

Food Hygiene and Safety: Importance of clean drinking water (boiling/filtering), washing hands before eating, washing fruits and vegetables, proper food storage (refrigeration for perishables, dry storage for grains), checking expiry dates, and avoiding street food from unhygienic sources. Food spoilage due to bacteria, fungi, and insects can cause food poisoning. Preservation methods include drying, refrigeration, pickling, canning, and adding preservatives.

Health and Diseases: Health is a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being (WHO definition). Diseases are classified as Communicable (infectious - spread from person to person - cold, flu, chickenpox, COVID-19, tuberculosis, diarrhoea) and Non-communicable (non-infectious - diabetes, hypertension, asthma, allergies). Prevention through vaccination, hygiene practices, and healthy lifestyle. Personal hygiene includes regular bathing, oral care (brushing teeth), hand washing, and clean clothing.

Solved Examples

Example 1
Which nutrient is primarily responsible for providing energy to the body?

a) Proteins b) Vitamins c) Carbohydrates d) Minerals
Solution: c) Carbohydrates. Carbohydrates are the body's main source of energy. They are broken down into glucose, which is used by cells for energy. Rice, wheat, bread, and potatoes are rich sources of carbohydrates. Proteins are for growth and repair, vitamins and minerals protect the body from diseases, and fats provide stored energy and insulation.
Example 2
A child has difficulty seeing in dim light. Which vitamin deficiency is likely responsible?

a) Vitamin B1 b) Vitamin C c) Vitamin A d) Vitamin D
Solution: c) Vitamin A. Night blindness (difficulty seeing in low light) is caused by Vitamin A deficiency. Vitamin A is essential for healthy vision, especially for the retina to adjust to changes in light levels. Rich sources of Vitamin A include carrots, papaya, mango, milk, eggs, and green leafy vegetables. Vitamin D deficiency causes rickets, and Vitamin C deficiency causes scurvy.
Example 3
Which of the following is a communicable disease?

a) Diabetes b) Hypertension c) Chickenpox d) Asthma
Solution: c) Chickenpox. Chickenpox is a highly contagious viral infection that spreads through direct contact or airborne droplets. It is communicable (can spread from person to person). Diabetes, hypertension, and asthma are non-communicable diseases caused by genetic, lifestyle, or environmental factors and cannot spread from person to person.

Shortcut Techniques

  • Nutrient Functions - 4 simple rules: Carbo = Energy (fuel), Protein = Building (repair/growth), Fat = Storage (extra energy), Vitamins/Minerals = Protection (immunity). Think: Car = Fast fuel, Pro = Building blocks, Fat = Reserve tank, V&M = Bodyguards.
  • Deficiency Diseases - Vitamin ABC: A=Night blindness, B1=Beriberi, C=Scurvy, D=Rickets. For minerals: Iron=Anemia, Iodine=Goiter, Calcium=Bones. Mnemonic: 'All Boys Can Dance' - A, B, C, D.
  • Communicable vs Non-communicable: Communicable = Contagious (spreads through Contact). Non-communicable = Not spreading (internal causes). Think: C = Contact spreads, N = Not contagious.
  • Food Sources - Plant Parts: Root (under), Stem (modified), Leaf (green tops), Flower (floral), Fruit (pulpy), Seed (grains). Think: R-S-L-F-F-S - Roots, Stems, Leaves, Flowers, Fruits, Seeds.

Relate balanced diet concepts to familiar meals (dal-rice-vegetable, roti-sabzi-dal). This makes learning contextual and meaningful.

Macronutrient Metabolism and Energy Pathways

Carbohydrates, proteins and fats follow distinct catabolic routes before converging on the universal energy currency adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Dietary carbohydrates are hydrolysed to monosaccharides, primarily glucose, which enters glycolysis in the cytosol yielding a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH per molecule. Under aerobic conditions pyruvate is transported into mitochondria, decarboxylated to acetyl‑CoA and oxidised in the citric acid (Krebs) cycle, producing 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂ and 1 GTP per acetyl‑CoA. The reduced co‑enzymes donate electrons to the oxidative phosphorylation chain, generating approximately 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH₂. Complete oxidation of one glucose molecule therefore yields roughly 30–32 ATP. Fatty acids undergo β‑oxidation in the mitochondrial matrix, each round shortening the chain by two carbons and producing 1 NADH and 1 FADH₂; a typical 16‑carbon palmitate yields 106 ATP after full oxidation. Amino acids are deaminated, their carbon skeletons entering glycolysis or the Krebs cycle at various points, while the nitrogen is converted to urea in the ornithine cycle. The relative contribution of each macronutrient to total energy expenditure shifts with nutritional state: fed conditions favour carbohydrate oxidation, prolonged fasting increases fatty acid utilisation, and severe protein deficiency forces reliance on endogenous protein catabolism.

Regulation occurs through allosteric enzymes and hormonal signals. Insulin promotes glucose uptake (via GLUT4 translocation), glycogen synthesis, lipogenesis and protein synthesis, whereas glucagon and catecholamines stimulate glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, lipolysis and ketogenesis. The AMP‑activated protein kinase (AMPK) acts as a cellular energy sensor, inhibiting anabolic pathways when the AMP/ATP ratio rises. Understanding these mechanisms explains why a balanced diet supplying 50–60 % carbohydrate, 10–15 % protein and 20–30 % fat supports optimal metabolic flexibility and prevents ectopic lipid accumulation.

Micronutrient Interactions and Bioavailability

Vitamins and minerals do not function in isolation; their absorption, transport and metabolic activity are profoundly influenced by dietary context. Non‑heme iron absorption is enhanced by ascorbic acid (vitamin C) through reduction of ferric to ferrous iron and formation of a soluble chelate, while phytates, polyphenols and calcium inhibit it by forming insoluble complexes. Vitamin D increases intestinal calcium absorption by up‑regulating calbindin‑D₉k expression; inadequate vitamin D therefore limits calcium utilisation even when intake meets recommendations. Zinc and copper share intestinal transporters; high zinc supplementation can induce copper deficiency by up‑regulating metallothionein, which binds copper with higher affinity and prevents its basolateral export. Folate and vitamin B₁₂ are interdependent in the methionine synthase reaction; deficiency of either leads to functional folate deficiency and megaloblastic changes. Fat‑soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) require dietary lipid for micellar solubilisation; extremely low‑fat diets can impair their absorption despite adequate intake.

Food processing alters bioavailability. Heat treatment destroys heat‑labile vitamins such as vitamin C and certain B‑vitamins, yet can increase the bioavailability of carotenoids by disrupting plant matrices. Fermentation reduces phytate content via phytase activity, improving mineral absorption in cereals and legumes. The concept of nutrient density—micronutrient content per 100 kcal—guides dietary planning more reliably than absolute amounts, especially for vulnerable groups with high requirements but limited gastric capacity.

Applied Scenario
A 30‑year‑old vegetarian woman presents with fatigue, pallor and a haemoglobin of 9.8 g/dL. Her diet provides adequate total iron but negligible vitamin C. Which dietary modification would most effectively improve her iron status without supplementation?
Incorporate vitamin C‑rich foods (e.g., amla, guava, citrus juice) with each main meal containing plant‑based iron sources such as lentils, spinach or fortified cereals. Ascorbic acid reduces ferric iron to the absorbable ferrous form and chelates it, counteracting phytate inhibition. Adding a small amount of animal protein (if acceptable) would further enhance non‑heme iron uptake via the “meat factor,” but the single most impactful change for a strict vegetarian is consistent vitamin C co‑consumption.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question links a deficiency symptom to a nutrient, first identify the primary biochemical role of that nutrient; then check whether the stem mentions a dietary factor that modifies absorption. The correct answer often hinges on the interaction (e.g., vitamin C‑iron, vitamin D‑calcium) rather than the nutrient alone.

Nutritional Assessment Methods and Growth Monitoring

Reliable assessment combines anthropometric, biochemical, clinical and dietary tools. Anthropometry remains the cornerstone for population surveillance: weight‑for‑age, height‑for‑age, weight‑for‑height and body‑mass‑index‑for‑age Z‑scores are compared against WHO Child Growth Standards (0–5 years) and WHO Growth Reference (5–19 years). Stunting (height‑for‑age < −2 SD) reflects chronic undernutrition; wasting (weight‑for‑height < −2 SD) indicates acute loss; underweight (weight‑for‑age < −2 SD) composites both. Mid‑upper‑arm circumference (MUAC) < 115 mm in children 6–59 months flags severe acute malnutrition with high specificity. In adults, BMI < 18.5 kg/m² denotes chronic energy deficiency, while waist‑to‑height ratio > 0.5 signals central adiposity and metabolic risk.

Biochemical indices provide objective micronutrient status: serum ferritin (adjusted for inflammation), transferrin receptor, haemoglobin, serum retinol, 25‑hydroxyvitamin D, urinary iodine excretion and erythrocyte transketolase activity for thiamine. Clinical signs—Bitot’s spots, angular stomatitis, goitre, oedema—corroborate biochemical findings. Dietary assessment uses 24‑hour recall, food frequency questionnaires or weighed food records; each has trade‑offs between precision and feasibility. Growth monitoring charts plotted at regular intervals (monthly in infancy, quarterly thereafter) enable early detection of faltering trajectories, prompting timely referral to nutrition rehabilitation centres.

Public Health Nutrition Programs and Policy Frameworks

India’s nutrition landscape is shaped by centrally sponsored schemes that target the first 1 000 days. The Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) delivers supplementary nutrition, health check‑ups, immunisation and pre‑school education through Anganwadi centres. The Mid‑Day Meal Scheme provides a cooked meal meeting 450 kcal and 12 g protein per child per school day, improving enrolment and classroom attention. The National Nutrition Mission (POSHAN Abhiyaan) converges ICDS, health, sanitation and drinking‑water missions, employing real‑time monitoring via the POSHAN Tracker to reduce stunting, wasting and anaemia by 2 % annually. The National Food Security Act (2013) legally entitles 67 % of the population to subsidised food grains (5 kg/person/month) at ₹3/₹2/₹1 per kg for rice/wheat/millets. Fortification mandates—iodised salt, iron‑folic acid fortified wheat flour, vitamin A‑D fortified edible oil—address micronutrient gaps at the supply side. The recently launched Saksham Anganwadi and Mission Poshan 2.0 emphasise community‑based growth monitoring, home‑based care for severe acute malnutrition and behaviour‑change communication using local self‑help groups.

Policy evaluation relies on coverage, continuity, intensity and quality indicators. Gaps persist in last‑mile delivery, dietary diversity of supplementary foods, and convergence with water‑sanitation‑hygiene (WASH) interventions. Understanding the logical framework—inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact—helps examiners frame questions on programme design, monitoring indicators and reasons for under‑performance.

Food Safety, Contamination, and Hygiene Practices

Food‑borne hazards are classified as biological (bacteria, viruses, parasites), chemical (pesticide residues, heavy metals, mycotoxins, adulterants) and physical (foreign bodies). The Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) system identifies critical control points (CCPs) where control measures prevent, eliminate or reduce hazards to acceptable levels. In India, the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) enforces the Food Safety and Standards Act (2006) and its regulations, prescribing maximum residue limits (MRLs) for pesticides, microbiological criteria for ready‑to‑eat foods, and labelling requirements including allergen declaration. Common bacterial pathogens—Salmonella, Shigella, pathogenic Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Clostridium perfringens—thrive in the temperature danger zone (5 °C–60 °C); rapid cooling, thorough reheating to ≥ 75 °C core temperature, and strict separation of raw and cooked foods are essential controls.

Mycotoxins such as aflatoxin B₁ (produced by Aspergillus flavus on groundnuts and maize) are hepatocarcinogenic; good agricultural practices, proper drying to ≤ 13 % moisture and storage in hermetic bags reduce contamination. Heavy metals (lead, arsenic, cadmium) enter the food chain via contaminated irrigation water and industrial emissions; regular monitoring of staple crops and enforcement of Codex Alimentarius limits protect consumers. Personal hygiene—hand washing with soap at critical moments (after defecation, before food handling), use of clean water for washing produce, and safe disposal of waste—breaks the faecal‑oral transmission route. Community‑level interventions include safe water supply, sanitary latrines, and vector control to interrupt cycles of diarrhoeal diseases that exacerbate malnutrition.

  • Confusing the ATP yield of fatty acid oxidation with that of glucose oxidation.
  • Assuming all plant‑based iron is equally bioavailable without considering enhancers or inhibitors.
  • Using weight‑for‑age alone to diagnose wasting in children older than five years.
  • Attributing programme impact solely to food distribution without assessing nutrition education or WASH convergence.
  • Overlooking the temperature danger zone when evaluating food‑handling practices in case studies.
  • Neglecting the role of inflammation in interpreting serum ferritin as an iron status marker.

Life‑Cycle Nutrition: Physiological Demands from Conception to Old Age

Nutrient requirements change dramatically across the human lifespan because growth velocity, body composition, hormonal milieu, and metabolic rate are not static. During the first 1 000 days — from conception to the child’s second birthday — the foundation for lifelong health is laid. In the first trimester the embryo relies on maternal stores; additional energy is negligible, but folate demand rises to 600 µg day⁻¹ to prevent neural‑tube defects. The second trimester adds roughly 350 kcal day⁻¹ and 9.5 g protein day⁻¹, while the third trimester requires an extra 450 kcal day⁻¹ and 22 g protein day⁻¹. Iron needs double to 35 mg day⁻¹ because of expanded maternal red‑cell mass and fetal accretion; calcium absorption efficiency increases, yet the RDA remains 1 000 mg day⁻¹. Lactation imposes the highest energy cost of any physiological state: an additional 500–600 kcal day⁻¹ and 17 g protein day⁻¹ for the first six months, with sustained high demand for iodine (250 µg day⁻¹), vitamin A (1 300 µg RAE day⁻¹) and long‑chain polyunsaturated fatty acids for infant neurodevelopment.

Infancy (0–12 months) is characterised by rapid linear growth (≈25 cm year⁻¹) and brain growth (≈15 g day⁻¹). Exclusive breastfeeding supplies all nutrients in bioavailable forms up to six months; thereafter complementary foods must provide iron‑rich sources (e.g., fortified cereals, meat) because breast‑milk iron is insufficient after six months. The recommended nutrient density for complementary feeding is ≥0.8 mg iron per 100 kcal and ≥0.5 mg zinc per 100 kcal. Childhood (1–9 years) sees a gradual decline in energy per kilogram but a rise in absolute needs; calcium RDA peaks at 1 300 mg day⁻¹ during the pubertal growth spurt (10–18 years) to maximise peak bone mass. Protein requirement rises to 0.9 g kg⁻¹ day⁻¹ for adolescents, with an additional 10 g day⁻¹ for girls to support menarche‑related iron losses.

Adulthood (19–50 years) stabilises energy needs; the focus shifts to chronic‑disease prevention. The Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range (AMDR) — 45–65 % carbohydrate, 20–35 % fat, 10–35 % protein — guides meal planning. Micronutrient RDAs are gender‑specific: iron 18 mg day⁻¹ for women of reproductive age versus 8 mg day⁻¹ for men; folate 400 µg day⁻¹ for all adults. Older adults (≥60 years) experience sarcopenia, reduced gastric acid, and altered thirst sensation. Protein recommendation increases to 1.0–1.2 g kg⁻¹ day⁻¹ to preserve lean mass, while vitamin D (15 µg day⁻¹) and vitamin B₁₂ (2.4 µg day⁻¹) become critical because of diminished cutaneous synthesis and atrophic gastritis. Fibre intake (≥25 g day⁻¹) and fluid (≥30 mL kg⁻¹ day⁻¹) mitigate constipation and dehydration risk.

Understanding these stage‑specific demands allows examiners to frame questions on “additional nutrient X during trimester Y” or “why iron‑fortified complementary foods are introduced at six months”. The key is to link physiological change — expanded blood volume, bone mineral accretion, hormonal shifts — to the quantitative nutrient increments defined by the ICMR‑NIN 2020 RDAs and WHO/FAO recommendations.

Nutrition in Non‑Communicable Diseases: Metabolic Pathways and Dietary Management

Non‑communicable diseases (NCDs) — type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), hypertension, and certain cancers — share common metabolic dysregulations: chronic low‑grade inflammation, insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, and oxidative stress. Dietary management targets these pathways through macronutrient quality, micronutrient adequacy, and dietary patterns rather than single‑nutrient manipulation.

In T2DM, carbohydrate quantity and quality determine post‑prandial glucose excursions. The glycemic index (GI) and glycemic load (GL) are practical tools: low‑GI foods (legumes, whole grains, temperate fruits) produce a slower glucose rise, reducing insulin demand. Carbohydrate counting (1 g carbohydrate ≈ 4 kcal) enables insulin‑to‑carbohydrate ratios for patients on basal‑bolus regimens. Fibre ≥25 g day⁻¹ (soluble fibre ≥7 g) improves glycaemic control by delaying gastric emptying and enhancing short‑chain fatty acid production. The American Diabetes Association recommends 45–60 % energy from carbohydrate, <10 % from saturated fat, and ≥10 % from monounsaturated fat.

For CVD, the DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) and Mediterranean diets are evidence‑based patterns. Both emphasise ≥5 servings of vegetables and fruits, whole grains, nuts, legumes, low‑fat dairy, and fish (≥2 servings week⁻¹). Sodium restriction to <2 g day⁻¹ (≈5 g salt) lowers systolic BP by 5–6 mmHg. Replacing saturated fatty acids (SFA) with polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) reduces LDL‑C by ≈10 %; trans‑fat elimination is mandatory. Plant sterols/stanols (2 g day⁻¹) can lower LDL‑C an additional 5–10 %.

Hypertension management mirrors DASH but adds potassium (≥3.5 g day⁻¹) from fruits, vegetables, and legumes to counteract sodium‑induced vasoconstriction. Magnesium (≈350 mg day⁻¹) and calcium (≈1 000 mg day⁻¹) contribute to vascular tone regulation. Alcohol limitation (≤2 standard drinks day⁻¹ for men, ≤1 for women) prevents BP elevation.

Cancer prevention diets focus on maintaining healthy weight, limiting red meat (<500 g week⁻¹) and processed meat (avoid), and achieving ≥400 g day⁻¹ of non‑starchy vegetables and fruits. Phytochemicals — carotenoids, flavonoids, glucosinolates — modulate xenobiotic metabolism and apoptosis. Alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen; even low intake raises breast and oesophageal cancer risk.

Examiners often test the ability to translate a metabolic rationale into a meal plan: e.g., “Design a 1 800 kcal day for a 55‑year‑old male with T2DM and hypertension, showing sodium <2 g, fibre ≥30 g, and carbohydrate distribution across three meals and two snacks.” The answer must demonstrate carbohydrate counting, sodium sources (hidden in processed foods), and inclusion of potassium‑rich foods.

Case‑Based Application
A 48‑year‑old woman with newly diagnosed T2DM (HbA1c 8.2 %) and stage 1 hypertension (BP 145/92 mmHg) is referred for medical nutrition therapy. She consumes 2 200 kcal/day, 55 % carbohydrate, 30 % fat (12 % SFA), 15 % protein, and 3.5 g sodium. Outline the key dietary modifications, quantify the target nutrient goals, and justify each change using metabolic pathways.
1. Energy: reduce to 1 800 kcal/day to promote 0.5 kg week⁻¹ weight loss (≈500 kcal deficit). 2. Carbohydrate: 45 % of energy = 202 g/day; distribute 45 g per main meal, 15 g per snack. Choose low‑GI sources (whole oats, legumes) to blunt post‑prandial glucose and lower insulin demand. 3. Fat: total 30 % (60 g), SFA <7 % (14 g), replace SFA with MUFA (olive oil, nuts) and PUFA (fatty fish) to improve lipid profile and insulin sensitivity. 4. Protein: 1.0 g/kg (≈60 g) to preserve lean mass during weight loss. 5. Sodium: <2 g/day (≈5 g salt); eliminate processed sauces, use herbs/spices, increase potassium‑rich foods (banana, spinach) to ≥3.5 g/day for vasodilation. 6. Fibre: ≥30 g/day (soluble ≥10 g) from vegetables, fruits, whole grains to slow glucose absorption and bind bile acids. 7. Alcohol: ≤1 standard drink/day. Each modification addresses a specific pathway: reduced glucose load → lower hepatic gluconeogenesis; lower SFA → decreased LDL‑C and improved endothelial function; sodium restriction + potassium ↑ → reduced vascular resistance; weight loss → improved insulin signalling via reduced adipose‑derived inflammatory cytokines.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a case study asks for a “dietary prescription”, first list the disease‑specific nutrient targets (e.g., sodium <2 g, carbohydrate 45–60 % energy). Then allocate those targets across meals using simple arithmetic (total kcal × % ÷ 4 for carbohydrate grams). Show the calculation steps; examiners award marks for the method even if the final menu differs. Keep a mental table of common food sodium, potassium, and GI values for quick reference.

Food Processing, Fortification, and Biofortification: Technologies and Policy Implications

Food processing alters nutrient matrices through heat, mechanical shear, pH change, and packaging. Thermal treatments (pasteurisation, sterilisation, extrusion) cause predictable losses: vitamin C (15–55 %), thiamine (20–30 %), folate (25–50 %) and some polyphenols, while increasing bioavailability of lycopene in tomatoes and β‑carotene in carrots by disrupting cell walls. Milling removes bran and germ, stripping 70–80 % of iron, zinc, and B‑vitamins from wheat; refined flour therefore requires mandatory fortification in many jurisdictions. Fermentation can enhance mineral solubility (phytate degradation by phytase) and synthesise vitamin B₁₂ in certain bacterial strains, but may also produce biogenic amines if uncontrolled.

Fortification adds nutrients to foods at levels that address population‑wide gaps without exceeding tolerable upper intake levels (UL). The Codex Alimentarius sets general principles: the vehicle must be widely consumed, stable, and centrally processed. In India, the Food Safety and Standards (Fortification of Foods) Regulations, 2018 mandate: (i) iodised salt at 30 ppm iodine; (ii) wheat flour (atta) fortified with iron (30 mg kg⁻¹ as ferrous fumarate), folic acid (1.5 mg kg⁻¹), and vitamin B₁₂ (0.01 mg kg⁻¹); (iii) edible oil fortified with vitamin A (25 IU g⁻¹) and vitamin D (4.5 IU g⁻¹); (iv) milk fortified with vitamin A (500 IU L⁻¹) and vitamin D (100 IU L⁻¹). Monitoring relies on periodic market sampling and the FSSAI’s “Fortified” logo (+F) for consumer recognition.

Biofortification improves nutrient density in the crop itself through conventional breeding or agronomic practices. Notable successes: (a) High‑iron pearl millet (variety ICTP‑8203) delivering 70 mg kg⁻¹ iron; (b) Zinc‑enriched wheat (variety Zincol‑2016) with 40 mg kg⁻¹ zinc; (c) Provitamin A‑rich orange‑fleshed sweet potato (variety Beauregard) providing 10 mg β‑carotene 100 g⁻¹; (d) Golden Rice (GR2E) expressing phytoene synthase and carotene desaturase, yielding up to 35 µg β‑carotene g⁻¹ endosperm. Agronomic biofortification — foliar zinc or selenium sprays — offers a rapid, low‑cost complement but requires repeated application.

Policy integration is critical. The National Nutrition Mission (POSHAN Abhiyaan) includes fortification and biofortification as supply‑side strategies, while the Public Distribution System (PDS) distributes fortified wheat flour and oil. Challenges: (i) stability of added nutrients during storage and cooking (e.g., iron loss in acidic preparations); (ii) consumer acceptance of colour/flavour changes (e.g., yellow rice from β‑carotene); (iii) risk of excessive intake in sub‑groups consuming multiple fortified vehicles (monitoring via total diet studies). Examiners may ask to compare “fortification vs. biofortification” on criteria of cost‑effectiveness, time to scale, sustainability, and equity.

Nutrition in Emergencies: Assessment, Response, and Coordination

Humanitarian crises — conflict, natural disaster, epidemic — disrupt food systems, health services, and caring practices, precipitating acute malnutrition. The Sphere Handbook (2018) defines minimum standards: (i) Global Acute Malnutrition (GAM) prevalence <15 % triggers a “serious” classification; ≥15 % is “critical”. Mid‑Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) <115 mm (children 6–59 months) or bilateral pitting oedema identifies Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM); MUAC 115–125 mm indicates Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM). Weight‑for‑Height Z‑score (WHZ) <‑3 SD or <70 % median also defines SAM.

Initial rapid assessment uses the SMART (Standardised Monitoring and Assessment of Relief and Transitions) methodology: two‑stage cluster sampling (30 clusters × 30 children) yields prevalence estimates with 95 % confidence intervals. Data feed into the Nutrition Cluster coordination mechanism (led by UNICEF/WFP) to prioritise geographic targeting and resource allocation.

Therapeutic feeding follows the WHO/UNICEF protocol: (a) Inpatient stabilisation (Phase 1) for SAM with medical complications — F‑75 (75 kcal 100 mL⁻¹, 0.9 g protein 100 mL⁻¹) at 130 mL kg⁻¹ day⁻¹, gradual transition to F‑100 (100 kcal 100 mL⁻¹, 2.9 g protein 100 mL⁻¹) after appetite returns; (b) Outpatient therapeutic care (OTP) for uncomplicated SAM — Ready‑to‑Use Therapeutic Food (RUTF) providing 500 kcal 92 g⁻¹ sachet, 10–15 g kg⁻¹ day⁻¹ until discharge (WHZ ≥‑2 SD, no oedema for 2 weeks). MAM management uses Ready‑to‑Use Supplementary Food (RUSF) or fortified blended foods (e.g., Super Cereal Plus) at 200–250 kcal day⁻¹.

Micronutrient supplementation in emergencies: vitamin A (200 000 IU for children 12–59 months, 100 000 IU for 6–11 months) every 4–6 months; zinc (20 mg day⁻¹ for 10–14 days) during diarrhoea; multiple micronutrient powders (MNP) for children 6–23 months in food‑insecure settings. Water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) integration — safe water ≥15 L person⁻¹ day⁻¹, latrine coverage ≥1 per 20 people — reduces diarrhoeal morbidity that aggravates malnutrition.

Coordination challenges include: (i) pipeline breaks for RUTF/RUSF; (ii) security constraints limiting access; (iii) donor earmarking that fragments programming; (iv) data quality issues when SMART surveys are delayed. The Humanitarian Programme Cycle (HPC) — needs assessment, strategic planning, resource mobilisation, implementation, monitoring — provides a framework for exam questions on “steps to launch a nutrition response within 72 hours”.

Dietary Guidelines and Food‑Based Dietary Guidelines: Development, Communication, and Evaluation

Food‑Based Dietary Guidelines (FBDGs) translate nutrient recommendations into culturally acceptable food patterns. The development process follows a structured evidence‑to‑policy pathway: (1) systematic review of diet‑disease relationships; (2) nutrient profiling of local foods; (3) modelling of dietary scenarios (linear programming) to meet RDAs at minimal cost; (4) stakeholder consultation (agriculture, health, education, consumer groups); (5) field testing of messages for comprehension; (6) official endorsement and dissemination.

India’s “Dietary Guidelines for Indians” (ICMR‑NIN, 2020) comprise 15 guidelines grouped under four themes: (a) Eat a variety of foods for a balanced diet; (b) Ensure adequate intake of vegetables, fruits, whole grains, pulses, nuts, and animal‑source foods; (c) Limit salt, sugar, saturated fat, and ultra‑processed foods; (d) Practice safe food handling and active lifestyle. The accompanying “My Plate for India” visual divides a plate into ½ vegetables and fruits, ¼ cereals/millets, ¼ protein foods, with a side of dairy. Portion sizes are expressed in household measures (e.g., 1 katori cooked rice ≈ 150 g).

Communication strategies employ multiple channels: (i) mass media (TV, radio spots in regional languages); (ii) school curricula (nutrition education modules); (iii) frontline workers (Anganwadi, ASHA) using flipcharts; (iv) digital apps (POSHAN Tracker, mHealth) for personalised feedback. Behaviour‑change techniques — goal setting, self‑monitoring, social support, nudging (placement of fruits at eye level in canteens) — are embedded in the “Eat Right India” movement.

Evaluation uses a logic model: inputs (guideline development budget), activities (media campaigns, training), outputs (reach, materials distributed), outcomes (knowledge, attitude, practice scores), impact (population‑level dietary intake shifts, NCD risk factor trends). Indicators: proportion of adults consuming ≥400 g fruits/vegetables/day; mean salt intake (target <5 g/day); prevalence of overweight/obesity. Periodic National Nutrition Surveys (NNS) and the Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey (CNNS) provide trend data. Examiners may ask to “critique the 2020 Indian FBDGs using the FAO/WHO evaluation framework” — requiring identification of strengths (cultural relevance, visual plate) and gaps (quantitative sugar limit absent, limited guidance on ultra‑processed foods).
