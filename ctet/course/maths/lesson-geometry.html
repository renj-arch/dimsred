What is Geometry?

Geometry is the branch of mathematics that deals with shapes, sizes, positions, and properties of figures. In CTET, geometry questions cover basic shapes, lines and angles, triangles, quadrilaterals, circles, 3D shapes, and their properties. The focus is on conceptual understanding and pedagogical approaches for teaching geometry at the elementary level.

Key Topics in Geometry

1. Basic Geometric Shapes: Point, line, line segment, ray, angle (acute < 90°, right = 90°, obtuse > 90°, straight = 180°, reflex > 180°). Types of lines: parallel (never meet), perpendicular (meet at 90°), intersecting (cross at a point).

2. Triangles: Based on sides: equilateral (all sides equal), isosceles (2 sides equal), scalene (all sides different). Based on angles: acute (all < 90°), right (one = 90°), obtuse (one > 90°). Angle sum property: sum of three angles = 180°. Pythagoras theorem: In a right triangle, (hypotenuse)² = (base)² + (height)².

3. Quadrilaterals and Polygons: Square (4 equal sides, 4 right angles), Rectangle (opposite sides equal, 4 right angles), Parallelogram (opposite sides parallel and equal), Rhombus (all sides equal, opposite sides parallel), Trapezium (one pair of parallel sides). Polygons — pentagon (5 sides), hexagon (6 sides), octagon (8 sides). Sum of interior angles = (n-2) × 180°.

4. Circles: Centre, radius, diameter (2×radius), chord, arc, sector, segment. Circumference = 2πr. Area = πr².

5. 3D Shapes: Cube (6 square faces), Cuboid (6 rectangular faces), Sphere (no edges/vertices), Cylinder (2 circular faces + curved surface), Cone (1 circular face + curved surface). Volume formulas, surface area formulas.

6. Symmetry: Line of symmetry (divides shape into mirror images). Rotational symmetry (angle at which shape looks the same during rotation).

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Triangles)
In a right triangle, the base is 6 cm and height is 8 cm. What is the length of the hypotenuse?

a) 10 cm b) 12 cm c) 14 cm d) 100 cm
Solution: a) 10 cm. Using Pythagoras: h² = 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100 → h = √100 = 10 cm.
Example 2 (Quadrilaterals)
What is the sum of interior angles of a quadrilateral?

a) 180° b) 270° c) 360° d) 540°
Solution: c) 360°. A quadrilateral can be divided into 2 triangles. Sum = 2 × 180° = 360°. Formula: (n-2) × 180° = (4-2) × 180° = 360°.
Example 3 (Circle)
The radius of a circle is 7 cm. What is its area? (Use π = 22/7)

a) 154 cm² b) 144 cm² c) 164 cm² d) 176 cm²
Solution: a) 154 cm². Area = πr² = (22/7) × 7² = (22/7) × 49 = 22 × 7 = 154 cm².

Shortcut Techniques

  • Use visualization: Draw figures to understand properties. Geometry is best learned through diagrams.
  • For angle problems: Vertically opposite angles are equal, corresponding angles are equal (parallel lines), alternate interior angles are equal.
  • Common Pythagorean triplets: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 7-24-25, 8-15-17, 9-40-41.
  • Area shortcuts: Equilateral triangle area = (√3/4)a². Circle area = πr², circumference = 2πr.
Pro Tip
In CTET, geometry questions focus on conceptual clarity rather than complex calculations. Understand why formulas work — e.g., why area of triangle = ½ × base × height — as you may need to explain this to students using paper-cutting or grid methods.

Advanced Applications

Modern exams feature questions combining multiple quantitative concepts. Master these by practicing previous year papers.

Shortcut Methods

Percentages: Use fraction table (12.5%=1/8, 20%=1/5, 25%=1/4). SI/CI: For 2 years, CI-SI = P(r/100)^2. Time Work: Use LCM of time periods. Average Speed: 2ab/(a+b) for equal distances.

Congruence and Similarity Criteria for Triangles

Two triangles are congruent when all corresponding sides and angles are equal. The standard congruence criteria — SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, and RHS — are derived from the rigidity of a triangle: fixing three independent elements determines the shape uniquely. SSS (side‑side‑side) states that if three sides of one triangle equal three sides of another, the triangles are congruent. SAS (side‑angle‑side) requires two sides and the included angle to match. ASA (angle‑side‑angle) and AAS (angle‑angle‑side) both rely on two angles and a corresponding side; the third angle follows from the angle‑sum property (180°). RHS (right‑angle‑hypotenuse‑side) is a special case of SAS for right triangles.

Similarity, by contrast, requires only equal corresponding angles; sides are proportional. The similarity criteria are AA (angle‑angle), SAS (two sides in proportion and the included angle equal), and SSS (all three sides in proportion). AA is the most frequently used because two equal angles guarantee the third by the angle‑sum property. In classroom practice, students often confuse SAS for congruence with SAS for similarity; the distinction lies in “equal” versus “proportional”.

Proof Sketch for SAS Congruence

Given triangles ABC and DEF with AB = DE, ∠A = ∠D, and AC = DF. Place triangle ABC on DEF so that A coincides with D and AB lies along DE. Because AB = DE, point B coincides with E. Since ∠A = ∠D, ray AC aligns with ray DF. With AC = DF, point C coincides with F. Hence the triangles coincide completely.

Common Classroom Misconceptions

  • Assuming SSA (two sides and a non‑included angle) guarantees congruence — it does not, except in the ambiguous case of the Law of Sines.
  • Treating proportional sides alone as sufficient for similarity without checking the included angle (SAS similarity).
  • Believing that equal areas imply congruence; area equality is a consequence, not a criterion.

Teachers should emphasise constructing triangles with ruler and compass to visualise why each criterion works and why SSA fails. Using dynamic geometry software reinforces the concept that only three independent measurements fix a triangle.

Circle Theorems and Their Proofs

The circle theorems form a coherent set of results linking angles, chords, tangents, and arcs. The most fundamental is the theorem that the angle subtended by an arc at the centre is twice the angle subtended at any point on the remaining circumference. A second key theorem states that angles in the same segment are equal. The tangent‑radius theorem asserts that a tangent is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of contact. The alternate segment theorem connects the angle between a chord and a tangent with the angle in the opposite segment.

Proof of the Central Angle Theorem

Let O be the centre, A and B points on the circle, and C a point on the major arc AB. Draw radii OA and OB. Triangle OAB is isosceles (OA = OB = r). Let ∠AOB = 2θ. The base angles ∠OAB = ∠OBA = (180° − 2θ)/2 = 90° − θ. In triangle ACB, the exterior angle at C equals the sum of the opposite interior angles, giving ∠ACB = θ. Hence ∠AOB = 2∠ACB.

Applications in Problem Solving

  • Finding unknown angles in cyclic quadrilaterals: opposite angles sum to 180°.
  • Determining lengths using the intersecting chords theorem: if two chords intersect inside the circle, the products of their segments are equal.
  • Solving tangent‑secant problems: the square of the tangent length equals the product of the secant’s external segment and the whole secant.

These theorems are frequently combined; a single diagram may require the central angle theorem, the alternate segment theorem, and the cyclic quadrilateral property simultaneously. Mastery comes from recognising the configuration and selecting the appropriate theorem.

Worked Example: Angle Between Tangent and Chord
In the figure, PT is a tangent to the circle at point P. Chord PQ makes an angle of 35° with the tangent PT. Find the angle subtended by chord PQ at the centre O.
By the alternate segment theorem, the angle between tangent PT and chord PQ equals the angle in the alternate segment, i.e., ∠PRQ = 35° where R is any point on the major arc PQ. The central angle ∠POQ subtended by the same chord is twice the angle at the circumference: ∠POQ = 2 × 35° = 70°. Therefore the required central angle is 70°.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a circle diagram appears, first mark all given angles and radii. Identify any tangent‑radius right angles (90°) immediately; they create right triangles that unlock Pythagoras or trigonometric ratios. Next, look for cyclic quadrilaterals — opposite angles sum to 180°. Finally, apply the central‑angle theorem to convert between centre and circumference angles. Writing these steps on the rough sheet reduces careless errors.

Surface Area and Volume of Composite Solids

Composite solids are formed by joining or removing basic solids — cubes, cuboids, cylinders, cones, spheres, and hemispheres. The total surface area is the sum of the exposed faces; internal faces where solids meet are not counted. Volume is additive: the volume of the composite equals the sum of the volumes of the constituent parts (or the difference when a cavity is removed).

Typical Configurations

  • A cylinder surmounted by a hemisphere (e.g., a silo). Surface area = curved surface of cylinder + curved surface of hemisphere + base of cylinder (if closed). Volume = πr²h + (2/3)πr³.
  • A cone placed on a cylinder sharing the same base radius. Surface area = curved surface of cylinder + curved surface of cone + base of cylinder (if closed). Volume = πr²h_cyl + (1/3)πr²h_cone.
  • A cuboid with a cylindrical hole drilled through its length. Volume = volume of cuboid − volume of cylinder. Surface area = surface area of cuboid + inner curved surface of cylinder − areas of the two circular ends removed.

Step‑by‑Step Procedure

  1. Identify each basic solid and its dimensions (radius, height, side length).
  2. Sketch the net or a 3‑D view, shading the hidden faces.
  3. Write the formula for each solid’s surface area and volume.
  4. Add volumes directly. For surface area, add only the exposed areas; subtract the areas of interfaces.
  5. Simplify the expression, factorising π where possible, and substitute numerical values.

Common errors include forgetting to subtract the base area of a hemisphere when it sits on a cylinder, or double‑counting the circular interface between a cone and a cylinder. Practising with physical models or 3‑D printing helps students visualise which faces are external.

Coordinate Geometry of Lines and Circles

Placing geometric figures on the Cartesian plane translates spatial properties into algebraic equations. A line is represented by the linear equation ax + by + c = 0 or the slope‑intercept form y = mx + c. The distance from a point (x₁, y₁) to the line is |ax₁ + by₁ + c| / √(a² + b²). Two lines are parallel if their slopes are equal (a₁b₂ = a₂b₁) and perpendicular if the product of slopes is −1 (a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ = 0).

Circle Equation and Properties

A circle with centre (h, k) and radius r has equation (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r². Expanding gives x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 where centre = (−g, −f) and radius = √(g² + f² − c). The condition for a line y = mx + c to be tangent to the circle is that the perpendicular distance from the centre to the line equals the radius: |mh − k + c| / √(m² + 1) = r. Intersection of a line and a circle leads to a quadratic; discriminant > 0 gives two points, = 0 gives tangency, < 0 gives no intersection.

Applications in Problem Solving

  • Finding the equation of a circle passing through three non‑collinear points by solving the general form for g, f, c.
  • Determining the radical axis of two circles: subtract their equations to obtain a linear equation representing the line of equal power.
  • Using the chord of contact from an external point (x₁, y₁): the equation is xx₁ + yy₁ + g(x + x₁) + f(y + y₁) + c = 0.

Coordinate geometry provides a powerful algebraic check for purely synthetic results. For instance, the perpendicular bisector of a chord passes through the centre; this can be verified by showing the midpoint satisfies the line through the centre with slope negative reciprocal of the chord’s slope.

Transformational Geometry: Symmetry, Rotation, and Reflection

Transformations map a figure onto another while preserving certain properties. Isometries — translations, rotations, reflections, and glide reflections — preserve distances and angles, hence congruence. Dilations (scaling) preserve shape but not size, producing similar figures.

Line and Rotational Symmetry

A figure has line symmetry if there exists a line (axis) such that reflection across the line maps the figure onto itself. The number of distinct axes equals the order of line symmetry. Rotational symmetry of order n means the figure coincides with itself after a rotation of 360°/n about a centre. Regular polygons exhibit both: an equilateral triangle has 3 axes and rotational order 3; a square has 4 axes and order 4; a regular n‑gon has n axes and order n.

Composition of Transformations

  • Two reflections across intersecting lines produce a rotation about the intersection point by twice the angle between the lines.
  • Two reflections across parallel lines produce a translation perpendicular to the lines by twice the distance between them.
  • A rotation followed by a translation is equivalent to a single rotation about a different centre (the “rotation‑translation” theorem).

Pedagogical Emphasis

In teaching, use tracing paper or dynamic geometry software to let students physically perform reflections and rotations. Ask them to predict the image of a point before executing the transformation. This builds intuition for the algebraic representation: reflection across the x‑axis maps (x, y) → (x, −y); rotation by 90° anticlockwise about the origin maps (x, y) → (−y, x). Understanding these mappings prepares students for vector and matrix methods in higher mathematics.

  • Confusing the criteria for congruence (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, RHS) with those for similarity (AA, SAS, SSS) — remember congruence requires equality, similarity requires proportionality.
  • Assuming the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the central angle; it equals the angle in the alternate segment, which is half the central angle.
  • Forgetting to subtract the area of the common base when computing surface area of a hemisphere on a cylinder.
  • Using the distance formula for a point to a line without taking the absolute value, leading to negative distances.
  • Believing that a composition of two rotations is always a rotation; if the centres differ, the result is a rotation or a translation depending on the angles.
  • Neglecting to check the discriminant when solving line‑circle intersection, which determines the number of intersection points.
  • Always verify which faces are exposed in composite solids before adding surface areas.

Cross Sections and Nets of Three‑Dimensional Solids

Understanding the two‑dimensional shapes obtained by slicing a solid (cross sections) and the flat patterns that can be folded to form the solid (nets) is essential for visualising surface area and volume problems. A plane cut parallel to the base of a right prism or cylinder yields a cross section congruent to the base; a cut at an angle produces an ellipse in a cylinder and a parallelogram in a prism. For a right circular cone, a cut parallel to the base gives a circle, a cut through the apex gives an isosceles triangle, and an oblique cut not passing through the apex gives an ellipse. A sphere always yields a circular cross section whose radius varies with the distance of the cutting plane from the centre.

Nets are particularly useful for computing total surface area without missing or double‑counting faces. The net of a cube consists of six congruent squares arranged in a cross shape; the net of a right rectangular prism comprises three pairs of rectangles. A right circular cylinder’s net is a rectangle (the lateral surface) whose width equals the circumference 2πr and height equals the cylinder height, together with two circles for the bases. The net of a right circular cone is a sector of a circle with radius equal to the slant height ℓ and arc length equal to the base circumference 2πr; the sector angle is (2πr/ℓ)×360°. For a regular pyramid, the net consists of the base polygon and congruent isosceles triangles as lateral faces. When a solid is truncated, the net includes the top and bottom polygons plus trapezoidal lateral faces.

In examinations, candidates are often asked to identify the solid from a given net or to draw the net of a described solid. Practising with paper models or dynamic geometry software helps develop the spatial reasoning needed to recognise which edges join and which faces become adjacent after folding. A common error is to treat the lateral surface of a cone as a rectangle instead of a sector, leading to an incorrect surface‑area formula.

Geometric Constructions Using Straightedge and Compass

Classical Euclidean constructions rely on an unmarked straightedge and a compass that can be opened to any radius but cannot retain a measurement when lifted. The fundamental operations are drawing a line through two points, drawing a circle with a given centre and radius, and marking intersection points of lines and circles. From these, one can construct perpendicular bisectors, angle bisectors, parallel lines, and regular polygons such as the equilateral triangle, square, and regular hexagon. The construction of a perpendicular bisector of a segment AB proceeds by drawing circles centred at A and B with equal radius greater than half AB; the two intersection points determine the bisector line. An angle bisector is obtained by drawing an arc centred at the vertex intersecting the arms, then drawing equal‑radius arcs from those intersection points; their intersection with the vertex gives the bisector.

Constructing a line parallel to a given line through an external point uses the property that corresponding angles are equal: copy the angle formed by a transversal and the given line at the external point. A regular hexagon inscribed in a circle is built by stepping the radius around the circumference six times, because the central angle is 60°. The regular pentagon construction is more involved, requiring the golden ratio; a standard method constructs a segment of length (√5−1)/2 times the radius using a right triangle and then marks off the side length on the circle.

Construction problems in competitive tests often ask for the minimum number of steps or to prove that a constructed figure satisfies a given condition. For instance, proving that the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of a triangle’s sides is the circumcentre uses the fact that any point on a perpendicular bisector is equidistant from the segment’s endpoints. Mastery of these constructions also underpins coordinate‑geometry proofs, where the same relationships appear algebraically.

Worked Example: Constructing a Tangent from an External Point
Given a circle with centre O and radius r, and an external point P, construct the tangents from P to the circle using only straightedge and compass.
1. Draw segment OP. 2. Construct the midpoint M of OP by drawing the perpendicular bisector of OP. 3. With centre M and radius MO (= MP), draw a circle; it intersects the given circle at points T₁ and T₂. 4. Draw lines PT₁ and PT₂. These are the required tangents. Proof: ∠OT₁P is an angle in a semicircle (since T₁ lies on the circle with diameter OP), hence it is a right angle; therefore PT₁ ⟂ OT₁, so PT₁ is tangent. The same holds for PT₂.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a construction question appears, first sketch a rough diagram freehand to visualise the required elements. Then list the sequence of elementary steps (line, circle, intersection) before drawing the final figure. This reduces the chance of missing a step and saves time during the actual drawing.

Mensuration of Frustums and Truncated Solids

A frustum is the portion of a solid (usually a cone or pyramid) that lies between two parallel planes cutting the solid. For a right circular cone of height H and base radius R, if a plane parallel to the base at height h from the apex removes the top, the remaining frustum has lower radius R, upper radius r = (h/H)·R, and height H−h. The volume of the frustum equals the difference of the volumes of the original cone and the removed cone: V = (1/3)πH R² − (1/3)πh r² = (1/3)π(H−h)(R² + Rr + r²). The lateral surface area of the frustum is π(R+r)ℓ, where ℓ = √((R−r)² + (H−h)²) is the slant height of the frustum. The total surface area adds the areas of the two circular bases: πR² + πr² + π(R+r)ℓ.

For a pyramidal frustum with regular polygonal bases, the same principle applies: volume = (1/3)h (A₁ + A₂ + √(A₁A₂)), where A₁ and A₂ are the areas of the lower and upper bases and h is the perpendicular distance between them. The lateral area is the sum of the areas of the trapezoidal faces; each trapezoid has parallel sides equal to corresponding side lengths of the two bases and height equal to the slant height of the frustum. When the solid is a truncated sphere (a spherical segment), the volume is V = (πh/6)(3a² + 3b² + h²) where a and b are the radii of the two circular faces and h is the segment height; the curved surface area is 2πRh, with R the sphere radius.

Problems often combine frustums with other solids, e.g., a cone surmounted by a hemisphere, or a cylindrical hole drilled through a frustum. The key is to identify which surfaces are exposed after assembly and to apply the appropriate formulas without double‑counting the common interfaces. Drawing a net of the composite solid helps verify the exposed faces.

Similarity and Scale Factors in Practical Problems

Two figures are similar if one can be obtained from the other by a dilation (scaling) possibly combined with rigid motions. The scale factor k is the ratio of any corresponding lengths. Areas scale by k² and volumes by k³. This principle underlies many real‑world applications such as map reading, model building, and shadow problems. For example, a map with scale 1:50 000 means 1 cm on the map represents 50 000 cm (0.5 km) on the ground; an area of 4 cm² on the map corresponds to 4 × (50 000)² cm² = 10 km² in reality.

In shadow problems, the sun’s rays are effectively parallel, so the triangle formed by an object, its shadow, and the line from the top of the object to the tip of the shadow is similar to the triangle formed by a reference object of known height and its shadow. If a pole of height 3 m casts a shadow of 4 m, and a tower casts a shadow of 20 m at the same time, the tower’s height is (3/4)×20 = 15 m. The same reasoning applies to indirect measurement of inaccessible distances using a mirror or a water surface.

Scale models in engineering and architecture use the cube‑square law: if a linear dimension is doubled, surface area quadruples and volume octuples. This explains why a model bridge that holds a certain load may not support the same proportional load when built full size; material strength does not scale linearly. In examinations, candidates may be asked to compute the weight of a scaled‑up solid given the weight of the original, assuming uniform density. The weight scales with volume, i.e., by k³.

When dealing with similar three‑dimensional figures, remember that corresponding linear dimensions (height, radius, edge length) are in ratio k, surface areas in ratio k², and volumes in ratio k³. A common trap is to apply the linear scale factor directly to area or volume. Always square or cube the factor as appropriate.

  • Assuming the lateral surface of a cone unfolds into a rectangle instead of a sector, leading to an incorrect surface‑area calculation.
  • Using the linear scale factor for area or volume instead of squaring or cubing it respectively.
  • Forgetting to subtract the area of the common base when combining a hemisphere with a cylinder or a cone with a hemisphere.
  • Treating the cross section of an oblique cut through a cylinder as a circle rather than an ellipse.
  • Confusing the construction steps for a perpendicular bisector with those for an angle bisector, resulting in an incorrect locus.
  • Neglecting to verify that the discriminant of the quadratic from a line‑circle intersection is non‑negative before claiming real intersection points.
  • Applying the frustum volume formula to a truncated pyramid without adjusting for the polygonal base areas.

Euler's Formula and Polyhedral Combinatorics

For any convex polyhedron the relationship V − E + F = 2 holds, where V is the number of vertices, E the number of edges and F the number of faces. This invariant, discovered by Leonhard Euler in 1758, is the cornerstone of polyhedral combinatorics and appears in many competitive‑exam problems that ask for a missing count when the other two are known. The proof can be sketched by projecting the polyhedron onto a sphere, obtaining a planar graph, and then repeatedly removing edges of a spanning tree until only a single vertex remains; each removal reduces E and F by one while preserving V − E + F. The formula extends to non‑convex polyhedra that are topologically equivalent to a sphere, but fails for solids with holes (genus g > 0), where the Euler characteristic becomes 2 − 2g. In examinations, candidates are often given a description such as “a solid has 12 pentagonal faces and 20 hexagonal faces, each vertex is incident to three faces” and must compute V and E. Using the face‑edge incidence (each edge belongs to two faces) gives 5·12 + 6·20 = 180 edge‑incidences, so E = 90. Each vertex has degree 3, so 3V = 2E = 180 and V = 60. Substituting into Euler’s formula checks: 60 − 90 + 32 = 2. A related concept is the dual polyhedron, obtained by placing a vertex at the centre of each face and joining vertices whose faces share an edge; the dual swaps V and F while preserving E. The Platonic solids form dual pairs (tetrahedron self‑dual, cube–octahedron, dodecahedron–icosahedron). Knowing the dual relationship allows rapid derivation of one solid’s parameters from the other’s. Problems may also ask for the number of space diagonals of a polyhedron: total segments joining vertices = C(V,2); subtract edges and face diagonals. For a cube (V = 8, E = 12, each square face has 2 diagonals, 6 faces → 12 face diagonals) the space diagonals = 28 − 12 − 12 = 4. Mastery of these counting techniques turns seemingly heavy combinatorial questions into straightforward arithmetic.

Geometric Loci and Advanced Constructions

A locus is the set of all points satisfying a given geometric condition. Classical loci include the perpendicular bisector of a segment (points equidistant from the endpoints), the angle bisector (points equidistant from the sides of an angle), and the circle (points at a fixed distance from a centre). In competitive examinations, loci problems often combine several conditions, for example: “Find the locus of points P such that the sum of distances from P to two fixed points A and B is constant.” This describes an ellipse with foci A and B; the constant sum equals the major axis length 2a. Conversely, the locus where the absolute difference of distances to A and B is constant is a hyperbola. The parabola arises as the locus of points equidistant from a fixed point (focus) and a fixed line (directrix). Constructing these conics with straightedge and compass alone is impossible in general, but examiners may ask for a construction of specific points on them using intersecting circles and lines. A typical advanced construction: given a triangle ABC, construct the point P inside the triangle such that ∠PAB = ∠PBC = ∠PCA (the Brocard point). The construction proceeds by drawing circles through A and B with chord AB subtending the required angle, intersecting similarly for the other vertices; the common intersection is the Brocard point. Another frequent task is to construct a triangle given its base, the opposite angle, and the sum of the other two sides. This uses the fact that the locus of the third vertex with fixed base and given angle is an arc of a circle (the circumcircle of the base and the given angle). The condition on the sum of sides translates to an ellipse with the base endpoints as foci; the intersection of the circle and ellipse yields the required vertex. In practice, the ellipse is replaced by a circle of radius equal to the given sum centred at one endpoint, and the intersection with the circumcircle is found by standard circle‑circle intersection. Mastery of loci transforms construction problems from trial‑and‑error into a logical sequence of well‑defined steps.

Worked Example: Locus Intersection
Given segment AB = 8 cm. Construct the locus of points P such that ∠APB = 60° and PA + PB = 14 cm. Determine the number of possible positions for P.
The condition ∠APB = 60° means P lies on the arc of the circle with chord AB that subtends 60° at the circumference. The radius R of this circle satisfies chord length = 2R sin(60°) → 8 = 2R·(√3/2) → R = 8/√3 ≈ 4.62 cm. The centre O lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB at distance d = R cos(60°) = R/2 ≈ 2.31 cm from the midpoint. The second condition PA + PB = 14 cm describes an ellipse with foci A, B and major axis 2a = 14 → a = 7 cm. The focal distance c = AB/2 = 4 cm, so the semi‑minor axis b = √(a²−c²) = √(49−16) = √33 ≈ 5.74 cm. The ellipse and the circle intersect in up to four points, but symmetry about the perpendicular bisector reduces the count. Because the circle radius (≈4.62) is less than a (7) and greater than b (≈5.74) is false (4.62 < 5.74), the circle lies inside the ellipse near the centre and crosses it twice on each side of the bisector, giving four intersection points. However, the angle condition restricts P to the major arc (the arc opposite the minor arc). The major arc corresponds to the larger segment of the circle, which contains two of the four intersections. Hence there are exactly two positions for P satisfying both conditions.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a locus problem appears, first identify the geometric object (line, circle, ellipse, hyperbola, parabola) from the algebraic condition. Sketch a quick diagram marking the given elements; symmetry often halves the work. For construction‑type questions, list the required steps in order before drawing, and verify each step uses only permitted tools. Remember that the intersection of two loci is the solution set — count intersections by comparing radii, distances, and relative positions rather than solving equations fully.

Analytic Geometry of Conic Sections

The general second‑degree equation Ax² + Bxy + Cy² + Dx + Ey + F = 0 represents a conic section. The discriminant Δ = B² − 4AC classifies the conic: Δ < 0 → ellipse (circle if A = C and B = 0), Δ = 0 → parabola, Δ > 0 → hyperbola. Rotating axes to eliminate the xy term uses the angle θ satisfying cot 2θ = (A − C)/B. After rotation, the equation reduces to A'x'² + C'y'² + D'x' + E'y' + F' = 0, from which centre, axes lengths, and orientation follow by completing squares. For an ellipse centred at (h, k) with semi‑axes a ≥ b, the standard form is (x−h)²/a² + (y−k)²/b² = 1. Its foci lie on the major axis at distance c = √(a²−b²) from the centre. The eccentricity e = c/a (0 < e < 1). A parabola with vertex (h, k) and axis parallel to the x‑axis has equation (y−k)² = 4p(x−h); focus at (h+p, k), directrix x = h−p. For a hyperbola centred at (h, k) with transverse axis along x, the form is (x−h)²/a² − (y−k)²/b² = 1; foci at distance c = √(a²+b²), eccentricity e = c/a > 1, asymptotes y−k = ±(b/a)(x−h). In examinations, a common twist is to give a conic in a rotated position and ask for the length of the latus rectum, the coordinates of the foci, or the equation of the directrix. The latus rectum of an ellipse is 2b²/a, of a parabola is |4p|, of a hyperbola is 2b²/a. Another frequent task: find the equation of the tangent to a conic at a given point (x₁, y₁). For the general conic, the tangent is Axx₁ + B(xy₁ + x₁y)/2 + Cyy₁ + D(x+x₁)/2 + E(y+y₁)/2 + F = 0. For the standard ellipse, the tangent simplifies to (x₁x)/a² + (y₁y)/b² = 1. The chord of contact from an external point (x₀, y₀) to an ellipse is (x₀x)/a² + (y₀y)/b² = 1. The polar line of a point with respect to a conic follows the same pattern. Problems may also combine conics with lines: find the condition for a line y = mx + c to be tangent to an ellipse → c² = a²m² + b². For a hyperbola, c² = a²m² − b². These algebraic conditions are faster than solving the intersection quadratic and setting its discriminant to zero. Mastery of these formulas allows rapid solution of coordinate‑geometry questions that would otherwise require lengthy manipulation.

Vector Methods in Plane Geometry

Vectors provide a powerful algebraic language for Euclidean geometry. A point P with position vector **p** relative to origin O is denoted **p** = OP. The vector from A to B is **b** − **a**. The dot product **u**·**v** = |**u**||**v**|cos θ gives the angle between vectors; the cross product magnitude |**u** × **v**| = |**u**||**v**|sin θ gives the area of the parallelogram spanned by **u**, **v**. In the plane, the scalar (2‑D) cross product **u** × **v** = u₁v₂ − u₂v₁ is a signed area. The area of triangle ABC = ½|(**b**−**a**) × (**c**−**a**)|. The condition for collinearity of A, B, C is (**b**−**a**) × (**c**−**a**) = 0. The equation of a line through point **a** with direction **d** is **r** = **a** + t**d**, t ∈ ℝ. The perpendicular distance from point **p** to this line is |(**p**−**a**) × **d**| / |**d**|. The foot of the perpendicular is **a** + ((**p**−**a**)·**d** / |**d**|²) **d**. The intersection of two lines **r** = **a** + t**d** and **r** = **b** + s**e** solves **a** + t**d** = **b** + s**e**; taking cross product with **e** eliminates s: t = ((**b**−**a**) × **e**) / (**d** × **e**), provided **d** × **e** ≠ 0 (non‑parallel). The angle bisector of lines with direction vectors **d₁**, **d₂** has direction **d₁**/|**d₁**| ± **d₂**/|**d₂**|. The internal bisector uses the plus sign when the vectors point away from the vertex. The circumcentre of triangle ABC can be found as the intersection of perpendicular bisectors; using vectors, the circumcentre **o** satisfies (**o**−**a**)·(**b**−**c**) = (**o**−**b**)·(**c**−**a**) = (**o**−**c**)·(**a**−**b**). A compact formula: **o** = (|**a**|²(**b**×**c**) + |**b**|²(**c**×**a**) + |**c**|²(**a**×**b**)) / (2(**a**×**b** + **b**×**c** + **c**×**a**)), where × denotes the scalar cross product. The orthocentre **h** = **a** + **b** + **c** − 2**o** (for origin at circumcentre). The centroid **g** = (**a**+**b**+**c**)/3. Euler line: **o**, **g**, **h** are collinear with **g** dividing **oh** in ratio 1:2. In examinations, vector methods turn problems about concurrency, collinearity, and ratios into linear algebra. For instance, to prove that the medians of a triangle are concurrent, set up the median from A as **r** = **a** + t((**b**+**c**)/2 − **a**) and show that t = 2/3 gives the same point for all three medians. The same point is the centroid. Vector techniques also simplify problems involving rotating a figure: a rotation by angle θ about the origin multiplies the complex representation z → z·eiθ, which in vector terms is (x, y) → (x cos θ − y sin θ, x sin θ + y cos θ). Using this, one can find the image of a triangle after rotation and compute new side lengths or angles without coordinate geometry.

Geometric Probability and Measure

Geometric probability assigns probabilities to events by comparing measures (length, area, volume) of favourable regions to the total measure of the sample space. The classic example: a point is chosen uniformly at random inside a circle of radius R; the probability that its distance from the centre is less than r (0 ≤ r ≤ R) equals (πr²)/(πR²) = (r/R)². For a line segment of length L, the probability that a randomly chosen point divides the segment into parts whose lengths satisfy a given inequality reduces to a ratio of lengths. In two dimensions, problems often involve a random point in a rectangle, triangle, or circle, and ask for the probability that the point lies in a sub‑region defined by linear or quadratic inequalities. For instance, a point (x, y) is chosen uniformly from the square 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, 0 ≤ y ≤ 1. Find the probability that x² + y² ≤ 1. The favourable region is the quarter‑circle of radius 1 in the first quadrant, area π/4; total area = 1, so probability = π/4. When the sample space is a triangle, the uniform distribution is not uniform in Cartesian coordinates; one must use barycentric coordinates or the fact that the area measure is invariant under affine transformations. A standard result: if a point is chosen uniformly in a triangle, the probability that it lies in a sub‑triangle formed by joining points on the sides equals the ratio of the areas, which can be expressed in terms of the side‑division ratios. Buffon’s needle problem is a classic geometric probability: a needle of length ℓ ≤ d is dropped on a floor ruled with parallel lines distance d apart. The probability that the needle crosses a line is 2ℓ/(πd). The derivation integrates over the needle’s centre distance x from the nearest line (0 ≤ x ≤ d/2) and its orientation angle θ (0 ≤ θ ≤ π/2), with crossing condition x ≤ (ℓ/2) sin θ. The integral yields the stated probability. In examinations, a typical twist is to ask for the expected number of crossings when n needles are dropped, or to modify the floor to a grid of squares. The linearity of expectation makes the expected number simply n times the single‑needle probability. Another common problem: a chord of a circle is chosen “at random”. The answer depends on the interpretation of randomness (random endpoints, random midpoint, random radius and angle). The three classical models give probabilities 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 respectively for the chord length exceeding the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle. Candidates must recognise which model the question implies, usually stated as “a point is chosen uniformly on the circumference” (endpoints model) or “a point is chosen uniformly inside the circle” (midpoint model). Mastery of geometric probability requires comfort with setting up the correct measure, performing the necessary integration or area computation, and interpreting the randomisation scheme.

  • Assuming Euler’s formula applies to non‑spherical polyhedra without adjusting for genus.
  • Confusing the locus of constant sum of distances (ellipse) with constant difference (hyperbola) in construction problems.
  • Forgetting to rotate axes before identifying the conic type when B ≠ 0 in the general second‑degree equation.
  • Using the dot product to test perpendicularity in the plane but neglecting that the 2‑D cross product gives signed area, not a vector.
  • Applying the uniform‑point‑in‑square probability to a triangle without an affine transformation.
  • Treating “random chord” as a single well‑defined model instead of specifying the randomisation method.
  • Misapplying the tangent condition c² = a²m² + b² for an ellipse when the line is given in general form Ax + By + C = 0 without converting to slope‑intercept.

These advanced topics extend the core geometry syllabus into areas that frequently differentiate high‑scoring candidates in competitive examinations.
