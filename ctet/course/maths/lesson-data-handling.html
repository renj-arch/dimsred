What is Data Handling?

Data Handling is the process of collecting, organizing, representing, and interpreting data. In CTET, data handling covers tally marks, bar graphs, pictographs, pie charts, mean, median, mode, range, and basic probability. The focus is on helping young learners understand how to make sense of data in everyday life.

Key Topics in Data Handling

1. Data Collection and Organization: Data can be collected through surveys, experiments, or observations. Raw data is organized using tally marks (groups of 5: |||| for 4, |||| for 5 crossed through), frequency tables, and grouped data (class intervals).

2. Pictographs: Data represented using pictures or symbols. Each picture represents a certain number of units. Example: One apple symbol = 10 apples. Learners must interpret how many items each symbol stands for and calculate totals.

3. Bar Graphs: Data shown as rectangular bars of equal width. Height/length of bar represents frequency/value. Types: vertical bar graph, horizontal bar graph, double bar graph (for comparing two sets of data). Scale is important — choose appropriate scale for given data.

4. Pie Charts: Data represented as sectors of a circle. Each sector's angle = (component/total) × 360°. Useful for showing proportions and percentages. Best used when total of all components = 100%.

5. Measures of Central Tendency: Mean (average = sum ÷ number of items). Median (middle value when data is arranged in order — if odd number of items, middle term; if even, average of two middle terms). Mode (most frequently occurring value). Range = maximum - minimum.

6. Basic Probability: Probability = (Number of favourable outcomes) / (Total number of possible outcomes). Range: 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Experiments: coin toss (1/2 heads), dice roll (1/6 for each number), drawing cards.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Mean, Median, Mode)
Find the mean, median, and mode of: 2, 3, 3, 5, 7, 7, 7

a) Mean=4.86, Median=5, Mode=7 b) Mean=5, Median=5, Mode=3 c) Mean=4.86, Median=7, Mode=3 d) Mean=5, Median=3, Mode=7
Solution: a) Mean = (2+3+3+5+7+7+7)/7 = 34/7 = 4.86. Median = 5 (4th term when arranged). Mode = 7 (appears most frequently, 3 times).
Example 2 (Probability)
A bag contains 3 red, 5 blue, and 2 green marbles. One marble is drawn at random. What is the probability of drawing a blue marble?

a) 1/2 b) 1/5 c) 3/10 d) 1/10
Solution: a) 1/2. Total marbles = 3+5+2 = 10. Blue marbles = 5. Probability = 5/10 = 1/2.
Example 3 (Bar Graph Interpretation)
A bar graph shows the favourite fruits of 40 students: Apple=12, Banana=8, Orange=10, Mango=6, Grapes=4. How many more students like Apple than Mango?

a) 4 b) 6 c) 8 d) 10
Solution: b) 6. Apple = 12, Mango = 6. Difference = 12 - 6 = 6.

Shortcut Techniques

  • To calculate mean: Add all values and divide by count. Use mental addition techniques for speed.
  • For median: Always arrange data in ascending order first. If odd count (n), median = (n+1)/2th term. If even, average of n/2th and (n/2+1)th term.
  • For mode: Look for the value with the highest frequency — there can be more than one mode (bimodal, multimodal).
  • In pictographs, check the key carefully — one symbol may represent more than one unit.
  • Probability is always between 0 and 1. Express as fraction in simplest form.

Grouped Data and Frequency Distribution Tables

When raw data contain many observations, summarising them in a grouped frequency distribution makes patterns visible and calculations manageable. The first step is to decide the number of classes; a common rule of thumb is Sturges’ formula k = 1 + 3.322 log10 n, where n is the total frequency. Class intervals should be of equal width, mutually exclusive, and exhaustive. The lower class limit of the first interval is usually a convenient round number slightly below the minimum observation. Tally marks or a frequency column record how many observations fall in each interval. The class mark (mid‑point) is calculated as (lower limit + upper limit) ÷ 2 and is used as the representative value for all observations in that class when computing the mean of grouped data.

The mean of a grouped distribution is obtained by the formula Σ fi xi ÷ Σ fi, where fi is the frequency of the i‑th class and xi its class mark. For the median, the median class is the class where the cumulative frequency first reaches or exceeds N/2. The median is then estimated by L + [(N/2 – CF) ÷ f] × h, where L is the lower boundary of the median class, CF the cumulative frequency before the median class, f the frequency of the median class, and h the class width. The mode for grouped data uses the modal class (highest frequency) and the formula L + [(f1 – f0) ÷ (2f1 – f0 – f2)] × h, where f1 is the modal class frequency, f0 the preceding class frequency, and f2 the succeeding class frequency.

Relative frequency and percentage frequency columns are added when the distribution must be compared across data sets of different sizes. Cumulative frequency columns (less‑than and more‑than) enable quick location of percentiles and quartiles. A well‑constructed frequency table also reveals skewness: if the mean > median > mode the distribution is positively skewed; if mean < median < mode it is negatively skewed. Teachers should emphasise that grouping inevitably loses some information, so the estimated measures are approximations, not exact values.

Cumulative Frequency and Ogives

Cumulative frequency is the running total of frequencies up to the upper boundary of each class. Two types are used: the less‑than cumulative frequency (adding frequencies from the lowest class upward) and the more‑than cumulative frequency (adding from the highest class downward). Plotting the less‑than cumulative frequencies against the upper class boundaries produces a less‑than ogive; plotting the more‑than cumulative frequencies against the lower class boundaries gives a more‑than ogive. Both ogives are smooth, non‑decreasing curves that intersect at the median of the distribution.

To construct an ogive, first prepare a cumulative frequency table. Choose a suitable scale on the horizontal axis for class boundaries and on the vertical axis for cumulative frequencies. Plot points (upper boundary, less‑than CF) and join them with a freehand curve or straight line segments. The median is read directly as the horizontal coordinate corresponding to N/2 on the vertical axis. Quartiles are located similarly: the first quartile Q1 at N/4, the third quartile Q3 at 3N/4. The inter‑quartile range IQR = Q3 – Q1 measures the spread of the middle 50 % of data and is robust against outliers.

Ogives are especially useful for comparing two or more distributions on the same axes. If the less‑than ogive of distribution A lies consistently to the left of distribution B, A has generally smaller values. The steepness of an ogive indicates concentration of data: a steep rise over a short interval shows many observations clustered there. In examinations, candidates may be asked to estimate the number of observations above a given value by reading the more‑than ogive, or to determine the percentile rank of a specific score. Accuracy depends on careful scaling and precise plotting; a ruler and graph paper are recommended.

The following less‑than cumulative frequency table shows the marks of 80 students in a mathematics test. Draw the less‑than ogive and estimate the median mark.
Marks (upper boundary) | Less‑than CF
10 | 5

Exam‑Day Strategy
When an ogive question appears, first verify that the cumulative frequencies are monotonic. Sketch a quick rough ogive on the rough sheet; you only need the median or quartile reading, not a perfect curve. Use the formula L + [(N/2 – CF) ÷ f] × h as a check — if the graphical estimate differs by more than one class width, re‑read the graph.

Measures of Dispersion: Range, Mean Deviation, Variance, and Standard Deviation

Central tendency alone does not describe a data set; dispersion quantifies how spread out the observations are. The simplest measure is the range, Range = Maximum – Minimum. It is easy to compute but highly sensitive to extreme values. Mean deviation about the mean (or median) is the average of absolute deviations: MD = Σ fi |xi – A| ÷ Σ fi, where A is the chosen average. For grouped data the class mark replaces xi. Mean deviation is less affected by outliers than the range but still uses absolute values, making algebraic manipulation difficult.

Variance overcomes this by squaring deviations. For a population the variance is σ² = Σ (x – μ)² ÷ N; for a sample the unbiased estimator uses n – 1 in the denominator: s² = Σ (x – x̄)² ÷ (n – 1). In grouped form, σ² = Σ fi (xi – μ)² ÷ N. The standard deviation is the positive square root of variance, σ = √σ² or s = √s². It retains the original unit of measurement and is the most widely used dispersion measure because it is amenable to further statistical analysis (e.g., coefficient of variation, standard scores).

A shortcut for variance of grouped data uses the step‑deviation method: choose an assumed mean A, compute deviations di = (xi – A) ÷ h, then σ² = h² [ Σ fi di² ÷ N – ( Σ fi di ÷ N )² ]. This reduces arithmetic with large numbers. The coefficient of variation CV = (σ ÷ μ) × 100% allows comparison of variability across data sets with different means or units. In examinations, candidates may be asked to compute standard deviation for a small ungrouped set (direct method) and for a larger grouped set (step‑deviation method). Understanding when to use n versus n – 1 is crucial: use n for population parameters, n – 1 for sample statistics.

Probability of Compound Events and Conditional Probability

Beyond simple experiments, many questions involve compound events — the union or intersection of two or more events. For events A and B, the addition rule states P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) – P(A ∩ B). If A and B are mutually exclusive, P(A ∩ B) = 0 and the rule simplifies to P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B). The multiplication rule for independent events is P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). For dependent events, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A), where P(B|A) is the conditional probability of B given that A has occurred.

Conditional probability is defined as P(B|A) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(A), provided P(A) > 0. Bayes’ theorem extends this to reverse conditioning: P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) ÷ P(B). In a typical examination scenario, a bag contains 4 red and 6 blue balls; two balls are drawn without replacement. The probability that the second ball is blue given the first was red is P(Blue₂ | Red₁) = 6/9 = 2/3. The unconditional probability that both are blue is (6/10) × (5/9) = 1/3. Tree diagrams are an effective visual aid: each branch represents a stage, labelled with the appropriate conditional probability.

Examiners often test the distinction between independent and mutually exclusive events. Two events cannot be both independent and mutually exclusive unless one has probability zero. A common trap is to apply the multiplication rule for independent events when the draws are without replacement (dependence). Always check whether the sample space changes after the first event. Another twist involves complementary events: P(A') = 1 – P(A). Using complements can simplify calculations, e.g., finding the probability of at least one head in three coin tosses is 1 – P(no heads) = 1 – (1/2)³ = 7/8.

Data Interpretation from Mixed Graphs and Real‑World Scenarios

Modern assessments combine bar graphs, line graphs, pie charts, and tables in a single question set, requiring the candidate to switch between representations. A typical mixed‑graph problem presents a bar graph of monthly sales for two products and a pie chart of the same year’s profit distribution by region. The candidate must extract absolute values from the bar graph (using the scale), compute totals, and then apply percentages from the pie chart to answer questions such as “What is the profit from the North region in March?” This demands fluency in converting between frequencies, percentages, and angles (angle = percentage × 3.6°).

Real‑world scenarios often embed data in word problems: “A school surveyed 200 students about their preferred mode of transport. The results are shown in a double bar graph comparing boys and girls.” The candidate must read the double bar graph, compute the number of boys who cycle, the number of girls who walk, and perhaps the ratio of cyclists to walkers. Attention to the key (scale) and to whether bars are stacked or grouped is essential. Stacked bars show part‑to‑whole relationships; grouped bars show side‑by‑side comparison.

Another layer is the use of cumulative frequency polygons (ogives) alongside histograms. A histogram displays class frequencies as adjacent rectangles; the ogive overlays the cumulative curve. Questions may ask for the modal class (highest rectangle) and the median class (where the ogive crosses N/2). The ability to read both simultaneously demonstrates a deeper understanding of distribution shape. Practice with past papers that feature such composite figures builds the necessary speed and accuracy.

  • Confusing class boundaries with class limits when computing median or mode for grouped data.
  • Using the population variance formula (denominator N) for a sample data set, leading to an underestimated standard deviation.
  • Applying the multiplication rule for independent events to draws without replacement.
  • Misreading the scale of a pictograph where one symbol represents multiple units.
  • Assuming the median of a grouped distribution equals the median class mark instead of using the interpolation formula.
  • Treating mutually exclusive events as independent, or vice‑versa.
  • Forgetting to convert percentages to angles (or vice‑versa) when moving between pie charts and raw frequencies.

Mastering these advanced concepts and avoiding the listed traps will give a solid foundation for any data‑handling question encountered in competitive examinations.

Sampling Techniques and Estimation of Population Parameters

Sampling is the bridge between a manageable data set and the larger population it represents. A probability sample gives every unit a known, non‑zero chance of selection, which permits unbiased estimation of population parameters such as the mean, proportion, or variance. Simple random sampling (SRS) selects n units from N without replacement, each subset equally likely; the sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean, and its standard error equals σ/√n multiplied by the finite‑population correction √((N‑n)/(N‑1)) when sampling without replacement. Stratified sampling divides the population into homogeneous strata, draws independent SRSs from each, and combines stratum estimates using weights proportional to stratum sizes. This reduces variance when strata differ markedly in the variable of interest. Cluster sampling, by contrast, selects groups (clusters) — often geographic — then surveys all or a subsample of units within chosen clusters; it lowers cost but usually increases variance because units within a cluster tend to be similar. Systematic sampling picks every k‑th unit after a random start; it is easy to implement but can introduce bias if the list has a hidden periodicity. For each design, the estimator of the population total is the sum of sampled values multiplied by the inverse of their inclusion probabilities. Confidence intervals for the mean use the standard error and the appropriate critical value from the t‑distribution (small samples) or normal distribution (large samples). The margin of error shrinks with larger n, lower variability, and more efficient designs. Understanding the trade‑off between cost, precision, and logistical feasibility is essential for interpreting survey results in any examination context.

Correlation, Regression and the Coefficient of Determination

When two quantitative variables are observed together, the first step is to quantify the linear association. The Pearson product‑moment correlation coefficient r = Σ(xi‑x̄)(yi‑ȳ) / √[Σ(xi‑x̄)² Σ(yi‑ȳ)²] ranges from –1 to +1; values near ±1 indicate a strong linear trend, while values near 0 suggest little linear relationship. It is crucial to remember that r measures only linear dependence — a perfect quadratic pattern can yield r ≈ 0. The coefficient of determination R² = r² expresses the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the linear model. Simple linear regression fits the line ŷ = a + bx that minimises the sum of squared residuals Σ(yi‑ŷi)². The slope b = Σ(xi‑x̄)(yi‑ȳ) / Σ(xi‑x̄)² and intercept a = ȳ – b x̄. Residual analysis checks the assumptions: linearity, independence, homoscedasticity (constant variance), and normality of errors. A residual plot showing a funnel shape signals heteroscedasticity; a curved pattern signals non‑linearity. The standard error of estimate Se = √[Σ(yi‑ŷi)²/(n‑2)] quantifies typical prediction error. Confidence intervals for the mean response at a given x₀ use Se √[1/n + (x₀‑x̄)²/Σ(xi‑x̄)²]; prediction intervals for an individual observation add an extra Se term. In multiple regression, the adjusted R² penalises extra predictors, and multicollinearity (high correlation among predictors) inflates standard errors, making individual coefficients unreliable. Variance inflation factors (VIF) above 10 flag serious multicollinearity. Examiners often test the distinction between correlation and causation, the effect of outliers on r and the regression line, and the interpretation of the slope in context (e.g., “for each additional hour of study, the expected score rises by 2.3 points”).

Interpreting Regression Output
A study of 30 students yields the regression equation Score = 45.2 + 3.8 × Hours, with Se = 6.5 and Σ(Hours‑H̄)² = 210. Compute the 95 % confidence interval for the mean score of students who study 5 hours.
First find the predicted mean: ŷ = 45.2 + 3.8 × 5 = 64.2. The standard error of the mean response is Se × √[1/n + (x₀‑x̄)²/Σ(xi‑x̄)²]. Here n = 30, x₀ = 5. The sample mean of Hours (x̄) is not given directly, but we can use the fact that Σ(Hours‑H̄)² = 210. The term (x₀‑x̄)² is unknown; however, the confidence interval formula requires it. Assuming the data are centred so that x̄ = 5 (a common simplification in exam questions), the second term vanishes. Then SE_mean = 6.5 × √(1/30) ≈ 6.5 × 0.1826 ≈ 1.187. The t‑value for 28 df at 95 % is ≈ 2.048. Margin = 2.048 × 1.187 ≈ 2.43. The 95 % CI is 64.2 ± 2.43 → (61.77, 66.63). If x̄ ≠ 5, the interval widens by the extra component.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a regression question supplies Σ(x‑x̄)² but not x̄, check whether the given x₀ equals the sample mean; if it does, the (x₀‑x̄)² term drops out, saving time. Always verify the degrees of freedom (n‑2 for simple regression) before reading the t‑table.

Probability Distributions: Binomial, Poisson and Normal Approximations

Discrete probability models describe counts of successes in repeated trials. The binomial distribution B(n, p) gives the probability of exactly k successes in n independent Bernoulli trials with success probability p: P(X = k) = C(n, k) pᵏ (1‑p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. Its mean is np and variance np(1‑p). The Poisson distribution with parameter λ models rare events over a fixed interval: P(X = k) = e⁻ˡ λᵏ / k!. Mean and variance both equal λ. A binomial B(n, p) can be approximated by a Poisson with λ = np when n is large (≥ 50) and p is small (≤ 0.05), because the binomial’s variance np(1‑p) ≈ np. The normal approximation to the binomial applies when np ≥ 5 and n(1‑p) ≥ 5; then X ≈ N(np, np(1‑p)) with continuity correction: P(X ≤ k) ≈ Φ((k + 0.5 – np)/√[np(1‑p)]). Similarly, a Poisson with λ ≥ 10 can be approximated by N(λ, λ). The central limit theorem justifies using the normal distribution for sample means: for a sample of size n from any population with finite mean μ and variance σ², the sample mean X̄ ≈ N(μ, σ²/n) when n is large (typically n ≥ 30). In hypothesis testing, the test statistic Z = (X̄ – μ₀) / (σ/√n) follows the standard normal under H₀. When σ is unknown, the t‑statistic with n‑1 degrees of freedom replaces Z. Examiners frequently ask for the probability of “at most 3 defects in a batch of 200” (binomial → Poisson), or “the probability that the sample mean exceeds 52” (normal approximation). Recognising which approximation is appropriate and applying the continuity correction correctly are key scoring points.

Time Series Analysis: Trend, Seasonal Variation and Moving Averages

A time series is a sequence of observations recorded at regular intervals. Decomposition separates the series into trend (long‑term direction), seasonal (repeating short‑term pattern), cyclical (longer‑term fluctuations), and irregular (random noise) components. The additive model Yₜ = Tₜ + Sₜ + Cₜ + Iₜ assumes constant seasonal amplitude; the multiplicative model Yₜ = Tₜ × Sₜ × Cₜ × Iₜ suits data where seasonal swings grow with the level. Moving averages smooth the series to reveal the trend. A simple moving average of order m (odd) at time t is MAₜ = (Yₜ₋₍ₘ₋₁₎/₂ + … + Yₜ + … + Yₜ₊₍ₘ₋₁₎/₂) / m. For even m, a centred moving average uses two successive averages. Seasonal indices are obtained by averaging the detrended values for each season (month or quarter) and normalising so that their sum equals the number of seasons (additive) or their average equals 1 (multiplicative). Deseasonalised data = Yₜ – Sₜ (additive) or Yₜ / Sₜ (multiplicative). Forecasting extends the trend line (often linear or exponential) and reapplies seasonal indices. Exponential smoothing methods — simple, double (Holt), and triple (Holt‑Winters) — assign exponentially decreasing weights to past observations; the smoothing parameters α, β, γ are chosen to minimise forecast error (e.g., mean absolute percentage error). In examinations, candidates may be given a quarterly sales series and asked to compute a 4‑quarter centred moving average, derive seasonal indices, and produce a forecast for the next year. Mastery of the mechanics — especially the centred moving average for even periods and the normalisation step — avoids common arithmetic slips.

Index Numbers and Weighted Averages in Economic Data

Index numbers summarise changes in a group of related variables over time. The Laspeyres price index uses base‑period quantities as weights: L = Σ(p₁ᵢ q₀ᵢ) / Σ(p₀ᵢ q₀ᵢ) × 100. The Paasche index uses current‑period quantities: P = Σ(p₁ᵢ q₁ᵢ) / Σ(p₀ᵢ q₁ᵢ) × 100. The Fisher ideal index is the geometric mean √(L × P), satisfying the time‑reversal and factor‑reversal tests. Quantity indices are analogous, swapping prices and quantities. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is a Laspeyres‑type index with a fixed basket; the GDP deflator is a Paasche‑type index because it uses current‑year quantities. Weighted averages appear in composite indicators: the Human Development Index (HDI) combines life expectancy, education, and income indices using geometric mean to reflect non‑substitutability. In a weighted arithmetic mean, each component xᵢ receives weight wᵢ (Σwᵢ = 1) and the composite is Σwᵢ xᵢ. The weighted harmonic mean is appropriate for rates (e.g., average speed). The weighted geometric mean Σwᵢ ln xᵢ exponentiated gives a multiplicative average. Examiners test the ability to compute Laspeyres and Paasche indices from a price‑quantity table, to interpret the bias (Laspeyres overstates inflation if consumers substitute away from goods whose prices rise), and to choose the correct mean for a given context. Understanding the properties — base‑period bias, current‑period bias, and the ideal index — enables quick elimination of incorrect options.

  • Confusing the finite‑population correction factor in simple random sampling with the design effect in cluster sampling.
  • Treating a high Pearson r as proof of causation without checking residual patterns or confounding variables.
  • Applying the normal approximation to a binomial when np < 5 or n(1‑p) < 5, ignoring the continuity correction.
  • Using a simple moving average of even order without centring, which misaligns the trend estimate.
  • Computing a Laspeyres index with current‑period quantities instead of base‑period quantities.
  • Assuming the weighted arithmetic mean is appropriate for averaging growth rates; the geometric mean is required.
  • Forgetting to normalise seasonal indices so that they sum to the number of seasons (additive) or average to 1 (multiplicative).

Hypothesis Testing and Confidence Intervals

Hypothesis testing formalises the decision of whether an observed sample provides enough evidence to reject a null hypothesis H₀ in favour of an alternative H₁. The test statistic is computed from the sample and compared with a critical value or a p‑value. For a population mean with known variance σ², the Z‑statistic Z = (X̄ – μ₀) / (σ/√n) follows the standard normal distribution under H₀. When σ is unknown, the t‑statistic t = (X̄ – μ₀) / (s/√n) with n‑1 degrees of freedom is used. The significance level α (commonly 0.05) determines the rejection region; a two‑tailed test splits α equally between the tails.

A confidence interval (CI) gives a range of plausible values for the parameter. A (1‑α)100 % CI for the mean with known σ is X̄ ± zα/2·σ/√n; with unknown σ it becomes X̄ ± tα/2, n‑1·s/√n. The width of the interval shrinks as n grows or as variability decreases. For a proportion p, the Wald interval p̂ ± zα/2·√[p̂(1‑p̂)/n] is standard, though the Wilson score interval performs better for small n or extreme p̂.

Power analysis links sample size, effect size, α, and the probability of correctly rejecting a false H₀ (power = 1‑β). For a two‑sample mean comparison with equal variances, the required n per group is n = 2·(zα/2+zβ)²·σ²/Δ², where Δ is the minimum detectable difference. Examiners often ask to compute the minimum sample size for a desired power or to interpret a given CI in the context of a hypothesis test.

Common pitfalls include confusing the p‑value with the probability that H₀ is true, using a Z‑test when σ is estimated from the same sample, and neglecting the assumption of normality for small samples. The central limit theorem justifies normal approximations for large n (n ≥ 30), but for skewed distributions a larger n or a non‑parametric alternative may be required.

Non‑Parametric Methods for Small Samples

When the normality assumption is untenable or the sample size is too small for the central limit theorem, non‑parametric tests provide valid inference without requiring a specific distribution. The Wilcoxon signed‑rank test compares a single sample median to a hypothesised value or paired observations. Each difference dᵢ = xᵢ – μ₀ is ranked by absolute value, signs are retained, and the test statistic W⁺ is the sum of positive ranks. Under H₀, the distribution of W⁺ is known; for n > 20 a normal approximation with continuity correction is used.

The Mann‑Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank‑sum) assesses whether two independent samples come from identical distributions. All observations are pooled and ranked; U₁ = n₁n₂ + n₁(n₁+1)/2 – R₁, where R₁ is the sum of ranks for sample 1. The null hypothesis of identical distributions is rejected for extreme U values. For n₁,n₂ > 20 the normal approximation applies.

The Kruskal‑Wallis H test extends the rank‑sum idea to k ≥ 3 groups. H = (12/N(N+1)) Σ (Rⱼ²/nⱼ) – 3(N+1), where Rⱼ is the rank sum for group j and N = Σ nⱼ. Under H₀, H follows a χ² distribution with k‑1 degrees of freedom. Post‑hoc pairwise comparisons (e.g., Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction) identify which groups differ.

Spearman’s rank correlation ρₛ measures monotonic association. It is computed as Pearson’s r on the ranks of the two variables. For n ≤ 30 exact critical values are tabulated; for larger n the t‑approximation t = ρₛ√[(n‑2)/(1‑ρₛ²)] with n‑2 df is used. Kendall’s τ is an alternative based on concordant and discordant pairs, with a known variance under H₀.

Non‑parametric methods lose some efficiency relative to parametric counterparts when normality holds (asymptotic relative efficiency of the signed‑rank test vs. t‑test is 0.955), but they gain robustness against outliers and heavy tails. Examiners frequently present a small data set (n = 8‑12) and ask to choose the appropriate test, compute the statistic by hand, and state the conclusion at α = 0.05.

Worked Example: Wilcoxon Signed‑Rank Test
A teacher records the scores of 9 students before and after a remedial session: Before = {42, 55, 48, 60, 51, 44, 58, 50, 47}; After = {48, 58, 53, 65, 55, 49, 62, 54, 51}. Test at the 5 % level whether the session improved scores.

1. Compute differences d = After – Before: {6, 3, 5, 5, 4, 5, 4, 4, 4}. All are positive, so no zero differences to discard.

2. Rank absolute differences (average ranks for ties): |d| = {6,3,5,5,4,5,4,4,4}. Sorted absolute values: 3 (rank 1), 4 (four occurrences → ranks 2‑5 average = 3.5), 5 (three occurrences → ranks 6‑8 average = 7), 6 (rank 9). Assign ranks: d=6 → 9; d=3 → 1; each d=5 → 7; each d=4 → 3.5.

3. Sum of positive ranks W⁺ = 9 + 1 + 7+7+7 + 3.5+3.5+3.5+3.5 = 45.5. Since all differences are positive, W⁻ = 0.

4. For n = 9, the critical value of the smaller of W⁺ and W⁻ at α = 0.05 (two‑tailed) is 8 (from Wilcoxon tables). Here the smaller rank sum is 0, which is ≤ 8, so we reject H₀.

5. Conclusion: There is statistically significant evidence at the 5 % level that the remedial session increased scores.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question supplies a small paired data set, first check for zero differences and discard them; then rank absolute values, handling ties by assigning average ranks. Write the rank table explicitly — examiners award method marks for correct ranking even if the final arithmetic slips. Memorise the critical values for n = 5‑12 (two‑tailed α = 0.05) to avoid normal‑approximation errors on tiny samples.

Bayesian Updating and Posterior Probabilities

Bayesian inference treats parameters as random variables with prior distributions reflecting existing knowledge. After observing data, the prior is updated via Bayes’ theorem to obtain the posterior distribution: π(θ|x) ∝ L(x|θ)·π(θ), where L is the likelihood. For a binomial proportion with a Beta(α,β) prior, the posterior is Beta(α+x, β+n‑x), a conjugate pair that yields a closed‑form update.

The posterior mean E[θ|x] = (α+x)/(α+β+n) shrinks the sample proportion toward the prior mean α/(α+β). The posterior variance decreases with n, quantifying increased certainty. Credible intervals (e.g., the 2.5 % and 97.5 % quantiles of the posterior) have a direct probabilistic interpretation: there is a 95 % probability that θ lies in the interval, unlike frequentist confidence intervals.

Model comparison uses the marginal likelihood (evidence) p(x) = ∫ L(x|θ)π(θ)dθ. The Bayes factor BF₁₂ = p(x|M₁)/p(x|M₂) quantifies relative support for two models. A BF > 10 is considered strong evidence for M₁. In practice, examiners may ask to compute a posterior probability for a hypothesis given a prior odds ratio and a likelihood ratio.

Hierarchical (multilevel) models extend this framework to grouped data, allowing partial pooling of group‑level parameters. The hyper‑priors on group means and variances are estimated from the data, improving estimates for groups with few observations. This concept underlies modern meta‑analysis and small‑area estimation.

Key exam traps: confusing the prior with the sampling distribution, forgetting to normalise the posterior when using non‑conjugate priors, and interpreting a credible interval as a confidence interval. Always state the prior explicitly and show the algebraic form of the posterior before plugging numbers.

Principal Component Analysis for Dimensionality Reduction

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) transforms a set of p correlated variables into p uncorrelated principal components (PCs) ordered by the amount of variance they explain. The first PC is the linear combination a₁ᵀX that maximises Var(a₁ᵀX) subject to ‖a₁‖=1; subsequent PCs maximise remaining variance under orthogonality constraints. Mathematically, the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix Σ (or correlation matrix R) give the loadings, and eigenvalues λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … ≥ λₚ give the variances of the PCs.

If the original variables are on different scales, standardisation (using R) is essential; otherwise variables with large variances dominate. The proportion of total variance explained by the first k PCs is (Σi=1k λᵢ) / (Σi=1p λᵢ). A scree plot of eigenvalues helps choose k; the “elbow” or Kaiser’s rule (λᵢ > 1 for correlation‑based PCA) are common heuristics.

Scores for each observation are obtained by projecting the centred (and possibly scaled) data onto the eigenvectors: Z = X·A, where A contains the selected eigenvectors. These scores can be used in regression, clustering, or visualisation. However, PCs are linear combinations that may lack direct interpretability; rotation (e.g., varimax) is sometimes applied after PCA to improve interpretability, though this moves the solution away from strict variance maximisation.

In examinations, candidates may be given a covariance matrix and asked to compute the first two eigenvalues and eigenvectors, or to decide how many components to retain based on a scree plot. Numerical accuracy in solving the characteristic equation |Σ‑λI|=0 is crucial; using the trace (sum of eigenvalues = trace Σ) and determinant (product = det Σ) as checks prevents arithmetic errors.

Common mistakes: applying PCA to a correlation matrix when the variables are already comparable, retaining too many components (over‑fitting) or too few (loss of information), and interpreting loadings as correlations without standardisation. Remember that PCA is descriptive — it does not test hypotheses — and that the components are sample‑specific unless the population covariance is known.

Experimental Design: Randomised Blocks and ANOVA

Well‑designed experiments isolate the effect of treatments from nuisance variation. A completely randomised design (CRD) assigns treatments to experimental units purely by chance. When a known source of heterogeneity exists (e.g., batches, days, fields), a randomised complete block design (RCBD) groups similar units into blocks and randomises treatments within each block. The linear model for RCBD is Yij = μ + τ_i + β_j + εij, where τ_i is the fixed treatment effect, β_j is the random block effect, and εij ~ N(0,σ²).

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) partitions the total sum of squares SST = ΣΣ(Yij‑Ȳ..)² into treatment (SSTr), block (SSBl), and error (SSE) components. Degrees of freedom are a‑1 for treatments, b‑1 for blocks, and (a‑1)(b‑1) for error (a treatments, b blocks). Mean squares are obtained by dividing each SS by its df; the F‑statistic for treatments is MSTr/MS_E. Under H₀: all τ_i = 0, this F follows an Fa‑1, (a‑1)(b‑1) distribution.

Assumptions: independence, normality of errors, and homogeneity of variance across treatment‑block combinations. Residual plots (residuals vs. fitted, normal Q‑Q) diagnose violations. If assumptions fail, a non‑parametric alternative such as the Friedman test (blocks as rows, treatments as columns) can be used.

Multiple comparison procedures (Tukey’s HSD, Bonferroni, Scheffé) control the family‑wise error rate when pairwise treatment differences are examined after a significant ANOVA. Tukey’s HSD = qα, a, df_E·√(MS_E/n) where n is the number of replicates per treatment per block. The choice of procedure depends on whether all pairwise comparisons or only a subset are of interest.

Factorial designs extend RCBD to two or more treatment factors, allowing estimation of main effects and interactions. A 2×2 factorial in blocks yields four treatment combinations; the interaction sum of squares tests whether the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. Higher‑order interactions are often negligible and can be pooled into error to increase power.

Examiners frequently present an ANOVA table with missing entries and ask to complete it, compute the F‑value, and state the conclusion. Mastery of the degrees‑of‑freedom arithmetic and the relationship SST = SSTr + SSBl + SSE is essential for speed and accuracy.

  • Treating a Bayesian credible interval as a frequentist confidence interval and vice‑versa.
  • Using the normal approximation for a Wilcoxon signed‑rank test when n < 10, ignoring exact critical values.
  • Applying PCA to a covariance matrix without standardising variables measured on different scales.
  • Forgetting to include block degrees of freedom in the error term of an RCBD ANOVA, inflating the F‑statistic.
  • Interpreting a high Bayes factor as proof of a hypothesis rather than as relative evidence.
  • Assuming the first principal component always has a meaningful real‑world interpretation.
  • Neglecting to check ANOVA assumptions (normality, homoscedasticity) before reporting significance.

These advanced topics deepen the toolkit for data handling and prepare candidates for the most demanding analytical questions.

Multivariate Analysis: MANOVA and Discriminant Analysis

When several dependent variables are measured on the same experimental units, analysing each variable separately inflates the Type I error rate and ignores the correlation structure among outcomes. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) extends the univariate ANOVA framework by testing the null hypothesis that the vector of population means is identical across groups. The test statistics — Wilks’ λ, Pillai’s trace, Hotelling‑Lawley trace, and Roy’s largest root — are functions of the eigenvalues of the hypothesis‑to‑error matrix product H E⁻¹. Wilks’ λ = |E| / |H + E| is the most commonly reported; small values indicate group separation. Assumptions mirror those of ANOVA: multivariate normality, homogeneity of covariance matrices (Box’s M test), and independence of observations. Violation of covariance homogeneity can be mitigated by using Pillai’s trace, which is the most robust statistic.

Discriminant analysis (DA) complements MANOVA by constructing linear combinations of the original variables — discriminant functions — that maximise the ratio of between‑group to within‑group variance. For k groups, at most k − 1 non‑redundant functions exist. The coefficients (loadings) reveal each variable’s contribution to group separation, while the classification rule assigns new observations to the group with the highest posterior probability, assuming equal prior probabilities unless otherwise specified. Cross‑validation (leave‑one‑out or k‑fold) provides an unbiased estimate of the apparent error rate. In examinations, candidates may be given a pooled within‑group covariance matrix and group centroids and asked to compute the first discriminant function coefficients or to classify a test observation. Mastery of the relationship between MANOVA’s eigenstructure and DA’s canonical correlations is essential for interpreting output tables quickly.

Survival Analysis: Kaplan‑Meier Estimator and Cox Proportional Hazards

Survival data are characterised by a time‑to‑event outcome that may be right‑censored — the event has not occurred by the end of follow‑up. The Kaplan‑Meier (product‑limit) estimator provides a non‑parametric estimate of the survivor function S(t) = P(T > t). At each distinct event time tᵢ, the conditional probability of surviving past tᵢ is (nᵢ − dᵢ) / nᵢ, where nᵢ is the number at risk just before tᵢ and dᵢ the number of events at tᵢ. The estimator is the product of these conditional probabilities over all event times ≤ t. Confidence intervals for S(t) are commonly constructed using the Greenwood formula for the variance of the log‑negative‑log transform, which yields asymmetric intervals that respect the 0–1 bounds.

The Cox proportional hazards model introduces covariates while leaving the baseline hazard h₀(t) unspecified: h(t|X) = h₀(t) exp(βᵀX). The partial likelihood eliminates h₀(t) and yields consistent estimates of β under the proportional‑hazards assumption. Testing this assumption can be done with Schoenfeld residuals plotted against time or by including time‑dependent covariates. Hazard ratios exp(βⱼ) quantify the multiplicative effect of a one‑unit increase in covariate Xⱼ on the hazard, holding other covariates constant. In exam questions, a table of event times, numbers at risk, and numbers of events is often supplied; candidates must compute the Kaplan‑Meier estimate at a specific time point and its standard error. For the Cox model, a partial likelihood score test or Wald test may be requested for a single coefficient. Understanding the distinction between the baseline cumulative hazard (Nelson‑Aalen estimator) and the survivor function is a frequent source of confusion.

Worked Example: Kaplan‑Meier Estimate
A study follows 10 patients. Event times (months) and censoring indicator (1 = event, 0 = censored) are: (3,1), (5,0), (6,1), (8,1), (9,0), (12,1), (14,0), (15,1), (18,0), (20,1). Compute the Kaplan‑Meier survivor estimate at t = 12 months and its standard error using Greenwood’s formula.

Order the distinct event times with events: 3, 6, 8, 12, 15, 20. Censored times (5,9,14,18) reduce the risk set after their occurrence.

  1. At t=3: n₁=10, d₁=1 → conditional survival = 9/10 = 0.9. S(3)=0.9.
  2. At t=5 (censored): n reduces to 9.
  3. At t=6: n₂=9, d₂=1 → conditional = 8/9 ≈ 0.8889. S(6)=0.9×0.8889=0.8.
  4. At t=8: n₃=8 (censored at 5 removed), d₃=1 → conditional = 7/8 = 0.875. S(8)=0.8×0.875=0.7.
  5. At t=9 (censored): n reduces to 7.
  6. At t=12: n₄=7, d₄=1 → conditional = 6/7 ≈ 0.8571. S(12)=0.7×0.8571=0.6.

Greenwood variance: Σ (dᵢ / (nᵢ (nᵢ − dᵢ))) over event times ≤12.

  • t=3: 1/(10×9)=0.01111
  • t=6: 1/(9×8)=0.01389
  • t=8: 1/(8×7)=0.01786
  • t=12: 1/(7×6)=0.02381

Sum = 0.06667. Var(S) ≈ S² × sum = 0.6² × 0.06667 = 0.024. SE = √0.024 ≈ 0.155.

Thus Ŝ(12)=0.60 with SE≈0.155.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a Kaplan‑Meier table is presented, first reconstruct the risk set at each event time by subtracting both events and censorings that occurred earlier. Write the conditional survival fractions in a column; their product gives the survivor estimate. For Greenwood’s formula, keep a running sum of d/(n(n‑d)) — this avoids re‑reading the table. In Cox model questions, remember that the partial likelihood uses only the ordering of event times; you never need the baseline hazard. If asked to test proportional hazards, a quick mental check is whether the log‑minus‑log survival curves for different covariate levels are roughly parallel.

Bootstrap Resampling Methods

The bootstrap is a computer‑intensive technique for estimating the sampling distribution of any statistic by repeatedly resampling the observed data with replacement. Given an original sample of size n, a bootstrap sample is drawn by selecting n observations independently from the empirical distribution (i.e., each original observation has probability 1/n each draw). The statistic of interest — mean, median, regression coefficient, correlation — is computed for each of B bootstrap replicates (typically B ≥ 1 000). The empirical distribution of these B values approximates the true sampling distribution, allowing standard errors, bias estimates, and confidence intervals to be obtained without analytic formulas.

Three common bootstrap confidence intervals are the percentile interval (α/2 and 1‑α/2 quantiles of the bootstrap distribution), the bias‑corrected and accelerated (BCa) interval, which adjusts for bias and skewness using the jackknife influence values, and the basic (reverse‑percentile) interval. The BCa interval is preferred when the statistic’s distribution is markedly asymmetric. Bootstrap hypothesis testing proceeds by centering the bootstrap distribution at the null value (e.g., subtracting the observed statistic and adding the hypothesised value) and computing the proportion of replicates more extreme than the observed statistic. In examinations, candidates may be given a small data set (n ≤ 15) and asked to perform a manual bootstrap with B = 5 or 10 to illustrate the mechanics, or to interpret a printed bootstrap output table showing the original estimate, bootstrap mean, bias, standard error, and a 95 % percentile interval. Recognising that the bootstrap fails for statistics that are not smooth functions of the data (e.g., the maximum) or when the sample size is extremely small is a key conceptual point.

Missing Data Mechanisms and Imputation Techniques

Missing data are classified into three mechanisms: Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) — the probability of missingness is unrelated to any observed or unobserved data; Missing at Random (MAR) — missingness depends only on observed variables; and Missing Not at Random (MNAR) — missingness depends on the unobserved value itself. MCAR justifies complete‑case analysis without bias, though efficiency is lost. Under MAR, likelihood‑based methods (maximum likelihood, EM algorithm) and multiple imputation (MI) yield valid inferences. MI creates m > 1 completed data sets by drawing imputations from the posterior predictive distribution of the missing values given the observed data, analyses each data set separately, and combines results using Rubin’s rules: the overall estimate is the average of the m estimates; the total variance = within‑imputation variance + (1 + 1/m) × between‑imputation variance. Common imputation models include predictive mean matching for continuous variables, logistic regression for binary variables, and multinomial logistic regression for categorical variables. The number of imputations m should be at least the percentage of incomplete cases; modern guidance suggests m = 20–50 for high missingness. In exam scenarios, a small data matrix with a few missing entries may be provided; candidates must decide whether MCAR, MAR, or MNAR is plausible, choose an appropriate imputation method, and compute a single imputed value using predictive mean matching (identify the nearest observed donor). Understanding that single imputation underestimates variability, while MI correctly reflects uncertainty, is a frequent testing point.

Model Selection Criteria: AIC, BIC, and Cross‑Validation

Choosing among competing statistical models balances goodness‑of‑fit against complexity. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) = −2 log L + 2k, where L is the maximised likelihood and k the number of estimated parameters. AIC estimates the expected Kullback‑Leibler divergence between the fitted model and the true data‑generating process; lower AIC indicates a better trade‑off. The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) = −2 log L + k log n imposes a stronger penalty for model size, making it consistent — as n → ∞, BIC selects the true model with probability one if it is among the candidates. Both criteria assume the models are estimated by maximum likelihood and that the sample size is large enough for the asymptotic approximations to hold.

Cross‑validation (CV) provides a data‑driven estimate of predictive performance. In k‑fold CV, the data are randomly partitioned into k roughly equal folds; each fold serves once as a validation set while the remaining k‑1 folds form the training set. The prediction error (e.g., mean squared error for regression, misclassification rate for classification) is averaged over the k folds. Leave‑one‑out CV (LOOCV) is the special case k = n; it has low bias but high variance and computational cost. For time‑series data, standard random CV violates temporal dependence; instead, a rolling‑origin or blocked CV scheme respects the ordering. In examinations, a table of candidate models with their log‑likelihoods, parameter counts, and sample size is often given; candidates must compute AIC and BIC for each model and identify the preferred model under each criterion. A typical twist asks why AIC and BIC may disagree — the answer lies in the different penalty terms (2k vs. k log n) and their asymptotic properties. Knowing that AIC targets predictive accuracy while BIC targets model truth guides the interpretation of conflicting selections.

  • Confusing the bootstrap percentile interval with the BCa interval and reporting the wrong quantiles.
  • Assuming MCAR when the missingness pattern clearly depends on observed covariates (MAR).
  • Using a single imputation and reporting its standard error as if it were the final uncertainty.
  • Applying AIC/BIC to models fitted by different estimation methods (e.g., OLS vs. REML) without adjusting the likelihood.
  • Performing random k‑fold cross‑validation on time‑series data, ignoring autocorrelation.
  • Interpreting a non‑significant Box’s M test as proof of equal covariance matrices rather than as insufficient evidence against equality.
  • Treating the Kaplan‑Meier estimator as a parametric survival function and extrapolating beyond the largest observed time.

Regularization Methods for High‑Dimensional Regression

When the number of predictors p approaches or exceeds the sample size n, ordinary least squares becomes unstable or infeasible. Regularization adds a penalty term to the loss function, shrinking coefficients toward zero and performing variable selection. The two canonical penalties are the L1 norm (Lasso) and the L2 norm (Ridge). The Lasso estimator solves

minβ { Σ (yi − xiᵀβ)² + λ Σ |βj| }

while Ridge solves

minβ { Σ (yi − xiᵀβ)² + λ Σ βj² }.

The tuning parameter λ ≥ 0 controls the amount of shrinkage; λ = 0 recovers OLS, λ → ∞ forces all coefficients to zero. Lasso yields sparse solutions because the L1 ball has corners on the axes, so the optimum often lands exactly on a coordinate hyperplane, setting some βj = 0. Ridge never produces exact zeros but reduces variance substantially when predictors are highly collinear. The Elastic Net combines both penalties:

minβ { Σ (yi − xiᵀβ)² + λ₁ Σ |βj| + λ₂ Σ βj² }

with mixing parameter α = λ₁ / (λ₁ + λ₂). Elastic Net inherits sparsity from Lasso and grouping effect from Ridge, making it preferable when correlated predictors form clusters. Cross‑validation (typically 10‑fold) selects λ (and α) by minimizing prediction error. In high‑dimensional settings, the degrees of freedom of a fitted Lasso model equals the number of non‑zero coefficients, a result used for unbiased risk estimation (Stein’s unbiased risk estimate). Examiners often ask for the KKT conditions that characterize the Lasso solution: for each j, xjᵀ (y − Xβ̂) = λ sign(β̂j) if β̂j ≠ 0, and |xjᵀ (y − Xβ̂)| ≤ λ if β̂j = 0. Understanding these conditions explains why Lasso can drop a predictor even when its marginal correlation with the response is large, provided other correlated predictors absorb the signal.

Causal Inference with Potential Outcomes and Directed Acyclic Graphs

The potential‑outcome framework (Rubin Causal Model) defines a causal effect for unit i as the difference Yi(1) − Yi(0), where Yi(1) is the outcome under treatment and Yi(0) under control. Only one potential outcome is observed; the other is counterfactual. The average treatment effect (ATE) is E[Y(1) − Y(0)]. Identification requires unconfoundedness (Y(1), Y(0) ⟂ T | X) and overlap (0 < P(T=1|X) < 1). When these hold, the ATE can be estimated by regression adjustment, inverse‑probability weighting (IPW), or doubly robust estimators that combine both. Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) provide a visual language for encoding conditional independence assumptions. Nodes represent variables; directed edges represent causal mechanisms. A path is blocked if it contains a collider (→ ←) that is not conditioned on, or a non‑collider that is conditioned on. The back‑door criterion states that a set of covariates Z satisfies the back‑door condition relative to (T, Y) if no node in Z is a descendant of T and Z blocks every path between T and Y that contains an arrow into T. Adjusting for such a set yields an unbiased estimate of the causal effect. Front‑door criterion applies when all directed paths from T to Y pass through a mediator M, there is no unblocked back‑door path from T to M, and all back‑door paths from M to Y are blocked by T. In that case, the effect is identified by the product of the effect of T on M and the effect of M on Y adjusting for T. Examiners frequently test the distinction between confounding (common cause of T and Y), mediation (T → M → Y), and collider bias (conditioning on a common effect). A typical trap: adjusting for a variable that is a collider opens a spurious association, inflating type‑I error. Another trap: using a post‑treatment variable as a control, which blocks part of the causal pathway and yields a “controlled direct effect” rather than the total effect.

Worked Example: Back‑Door Adjustment
A study examines the effect of a new teaching method (T) on student scores (Y). Prior ability (A) influences both the choice of method and the score. School resources (R) affect the method but not the score directly. The DAG is A → T, A → Y, R → T. Identify a valid adjustment set for the ATE and write the IPW estimator.
The back‑door paths from T to Y are T ← A → Y. The path T ← R → T is a trivial cycle and does not reach Y. To block the only back‑door path we must condition on A. R is not required because it is not on a back‑door path to Y. Thus Z = {A} satisfies the back‑door criterion. The IPW estimator of the ATE is (1/n) Σ [ Ti Yi / ê(Ai) − (1−Ti) Yi / (1−ê(Ai)) ], where ê(A) = P(T=1|A) is estimated by logistic regression of T on A. This estimator is consistent if the propensity model is correctly specified and overlap holds.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a DAG is presented, first list all paths between treatment and outcome. Classify each as directed, back‑door, or front‑door. Then apply the back‑door or front‑door criterion mechanically: a valid adjustment set must contain no descendants of treatment and must block every back‑door path. Write the chosen set explicitly; examiners award marks for the correct set even if the final numeric answer is omitted.

Monte Carlo Integration and Importance Sampling

Monte Carlo integration approximates an integral I = ∫ f(x) dx by drawing independent samples x1, …, xN from a proposal density g(x) and computing the average (1/N) Σ f(xi) / g(xi). When g = f / I (the normalized target), the estimator has zero variance, but this requires knowing I. Importance sampling chooses a tractable g that approximates the shape of f, reducing variance relative to naïve sampling from a uniform or standard normal density. The estimator is unbiased for any g with support covering f. Its variance is Varg[ f(X)/g(X) ] / N. The effective sample size (ESS) = (Σ wi)² / Σ wi², where wi = f(xi)/g(xi), quantifies the loss of efficiency; ESS ≈ N when g ≈ f, and ESS ≪ N when the proposal is poorly matched. Self‑normalized importance sampling uses weights w̃i = wi / Σ wj to estimate ratios of integrals (e.g., posterior expectations) without knowing the normalizing constants. In high dimensions, importance sampling deteriorates because the weight distribution becomes extremely skewed; the curse of dimensionality manifests as ESS decaying exponentially with dimension. Remedies include adaptive importance sampling (iteratively fitting a mixture proposal), sequential Monte Carlo (particle filters), and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) which constructs a dependent sequence with the target as stationary distribution. Examiners often ask for the variance of the importance sampling estimator and the condition for finite variance: ∫ f(x)² / g(x) dx < ∞. A common mistake is to use a proposal with lighter tails than the target, causing infinite variance and unreliable estimates.

Robust Estimation and Influence Functions

Classical estimators (sample mean, OLS) are highly sensitive to outliers; a single extreme observation can drive the estimate arbitrarily far. Robust statistics quantifies this sensitivity via the influence function (IF). For an estimator T(F) defined as a functional of the distribution F, the IF at point x is IF(x; T, F) = limε→0 [ T((1−ε)F + εΔx) − T(F) ] / ε, where Δx is a point mass at x. The IF measures the infinitesimal effect of contaminating the data with an observation at x. For the sample mean, IF(x) = x − μ, which is unbounded. For the median, IF(x) = (1/2) sign(x − m) / f(m), bounded by 1/(2f(m)). An estimator with bounded IF is called B‑robust. The breakdown point is the smallest fraction of contamination that can drive the estimator to infinity; the median has breakdown point 0.5, the mean 0. M‑estimators generalize maximum likelihood by minimizing Σ ρ( ri ), where ri are residuals and ρ is a symmetric loss function. Huber’s ρ combines quadratic loss near zero with linear loss in the tails, yielding an IF that is linear for small residuals and constant for large ones. The corresponding ψ = ρ′ gives the estimating equation Σ ψ( ri ) = 0. Tukey’s biweight (bisquare) ρ redescends to zero for very large residuals, providing a redescending IF that completely rejects gross outliers. In regression, MM‑estimators combine a high‑breakdown initial S‑estimator with an efficient M‑step, achieving 50% breakdown and near‑Gaussian efficiency. Examiners test the ability to compute the IF for simple functionals (e.g., variance, quantiles) and to compare breakdown points of mean, median, trimmed mean (α‑trimmed has breakdown α), and M‑estimators. A frequent trap: confusing the influence function of the estimator with the influence function of the estimating equation; the latter is ψ, the former is −ψ / E[ψ′].

Meta‑Analysis: Fixed‑Effect and Random‑Effects Models

Meta‑analysis synthesizes effect sizes from k independent studies. Let θ̂i be the estimated effect (e.g., log odds ratio) with known within‑study variance σi². The fixed‑effect model assumes a common true effect θ across studies: θ̂i = θ + εi, εi ~ N(0, σi²). The inverse‑variance weighted estimator is θ̂FE = Σ wi θ̂i / Σ wi with wi = 1/σi². Its variance is 1 / Σ wi. The random‑effects model allows true effects to vary: θ̂i = θ + ui + εi, ui ~ N(0, τ²). The marginal variance of θ̂i is σi² + τ², so weights become wi* = 1/(σi² + τ²). τ² is estimated by the DerSimonian–Laird moment estimator, restricted maximum likelihood (REML), or Paule–Mandel. The random‑effects pooled estimate θ̂RE = Σ wi* θ̂i / Σ wi* has variance 1 / Σ wi*. Heterogeneity is quantified by Cochran’s Q = Σ wi (θ̂i − θ̂FE)², which under homogeneity follows χ²k−1. The I² statistic = max(0, (Q − (k−1))/Q) × 100% describes the proportion of total variation due to heterogeneity. Publication bias is assessed by funnel plots (θ̂i vs. 1/√σi²) and Egger’s regression test (intercept ≠ 0). The trim‑and‑fill method imputes missing studies to restore symmetry. Examiners often provide a table of study estimates and standard errors, ask to compute Q, I², τ² (DerSimonian–Laird), and the random‑effects pooled estimate with 95% confidence interval. A common trap: using fixed‑effect weights when heterogeneity is substantial (I² > 50%), which underestimates the variance of the pooled effect. Another trap: interpreting a non‑significant Q as evidence of homogeneity; Q has low power when k is small.

  • Confusing the Lasso KKT conditions with Ridge optimality conditions; Lasso involves sub‑gradients, Ridge involves simple linear equations.
  • Adjusting for a collider in a DAG and claiming the resulting estimate is unbiased; collider adjustment induces spurious association.
  • Using an importance sampling proposal with lighter tails than the target, leading to infinite variance and misleadingly small Monte Carlo error estimates.
  • Reporting the influence function of the estimating equation ψ instead of the estimator’s IF = −ψ / E[ψ′]; the two differ by a scaling factor.
  • Applying a fixed‑effect meta‑analysis when Cochran’s Q or I² indicates moderate to high heterogeneity, thereby understating uncertainty.
  • Interpreting a non‑significant Egger’s test as proof of no publication bias; the test has low power and asymmetry may arise from heterogeneity.
  • Assuming the median’s influence function is unbounded because the sample median can jump; the population IF of the median is bounded.
