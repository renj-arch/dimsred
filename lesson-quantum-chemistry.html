Part 1

📐 Schrödinger Equation

Wave Mechanics
Time-Independent Equation, Particle in a Box, Harmonic Oscillator, and the Hydrogen Atom
The time-independent Schrödinger equation HΨ = EΨ is the fundamental equation of quantum mechanics, where the Hamiltonian operator H = −(ℓ²/2m)∇² + V. Solutions Ψ (wavefunctions) contain all information about a system; |Ψ|² is the probability density. Particle in a one-dimensional box (infinite potential well, 0 < x < L): ψn(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L), En = n²h²/8mL² (n = 1, 2, 3, …). The ground state has non-zero energy (E₁ = h²/8mL²), the zero-point energy. Nodes in the wavefunction increase with n. Harmonic oscillator (V = 1/2 kx²): energy levels Ev = (v + 1/2)hν (v = 0, 1, 2, …), equally spaced by ΔE = hν. Wavefunctions are Hermite polynomials times a Gaussian envelope. The zero-point energy is E₀ = (1/2)hν. Rigid rotor (rotational motion): EJ = J(J+1)ℓ²/2I (J = 0, 1, 2, …), with 2J+1 degenerate MJ states. Hydrogen atom: the Coulomb potential V(r) = −e²/4πε₀r separates into radial Rnl(r) (Laguerre polynomials) and angular Ylm(θ, φ) (spherical harmonics) components. Quantum numbers: principal n = 1, 2, 3, … (energy), azimuthal l = 0, 1, …, n−1 (orbital angular momentum, s p d f), magnetic ml = −l, …, +l (orientation), and spin ms = ±1/2. Energy levels: En = −13.6 eV / n². Orbital shapes: s (spherical), p (dumbbell with nodal plane), d (cloverleaf with two nodal planes). Radial distribution functions 4πr²|Rnl|² show the probability of finding the electron at distance r from the nucleus.
The Schrödinger equation gives exact solutions for model systems that capture essential quantum behavior (quantization, zero-point energy, nodal structure) and the hydrogen atom exactly, providing the foundation for all quantum chemistry.
Part 2

🌀 Molecular Orbitals

Bonding
LCAO, Hückel Theory, Bonding/Antibonding Orbitals, and Symmetry
Molecular orbital (MO) theory describes electrons in molecules as occupying orbitals that extend over the entire molecule. The linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) approximation builds MOs as ψ = Σ ciφi. The coefficients and energies come from solving the secular equation: det(Hij − ESij) = 0, where Hij = ⟨φi|H|φj⟩ are resonance integrals and Sij = ⟨φi|φj⟩ are overlap integrals. For H₂⁺, the two 1s AOs combine to give a bonding σg (lower energy, constructive interference, electron density between nuclei) and an antibonding σu* (higher energy, destructive interference, nodal plane between nuclei). Bond order = (½)(bonding − antibonding electrons). Hückel π-electron theory treats conjugated π-systems with σ-π separation: each carbon contributes one pz AO, and the secular determinant simplifies with α (Coulomb integral) and β (resonance integral). For ethene: E = α ± β (π bonding and π* antibonding). For butadiene: four π MOs with energies α ± 1.618β and α ± 0.618β; π-electron delocalization gives stabilization energy. For benzene: the characteristic Hückel rule (4n+2 aromaticity): six π electrons fill three bonding MOs (α+2β, α+β twice degenerate) with large delocalization energy (2β relative to three isolated ethenes). Frontier molecular orbital theory (Fukui): the HOMO (highest occupied MO) and LUMO (lowest unoccupied MO) determine chemical reactivity. The HOMO-LUMO gap correlates with stability, color, and conductivity. Walsh diagrams explain molecular geometry by showing how MO energies change with bond angles (e.g., H-A-H angle in AH₂ molecules). Group theory and symmetry: molecules are classified into point groups (C2v, D∞h, Oh, Td). Character tables list irreducible representations and symmetry species. Symmetry-adapted linear combinations (SALCs) simplify MO construction. Selection rules for spectroscopy: a transition is allowed if the integral ⟨ψi|μ|ψf⟩ is non-zero, which depends on the symmetry of the states and the transition dipole moment operator.
Molecular orbital theory explains bonding through constructive and destructive interference of atomic orbitals; symmetry and group theory provide powerful tools to classify and predict molecular properties.
Part 3

💻 Computational Methods

Ab Initio
Born-Oppenheimer, Hartree-Fock, Basis Sets, and Post-HF Methods
Computational quantum chemistry solves the electronic Schrödinger equation approximately. The Born-Oppenheimer approximation fixes nuclear positions and solves for electronic motion (nuclei are much heavier and move slower). The variational principle: any trial wavefunction gives an energy that is an upper bound to the true ground-state energy. Hartree-Fock (HF) theory approximates the N-electron wavefunction as a single Slater determinant of spin-orbitals (antisymmetrized product). The Fock operator for each electron: f(1) = h(1) + Σj [Jj(1) − Kj(1)], where J is the Coulomb operator (classical repulsion) and K is the exchange operator (arising from the antisymmetry requirement, no classical analogue). The self-consistent field (SCF) procedure iteratively solves the Roothaan-Hall equations FC = SCE (in matrix form with basis functions). Basis sets are sets of atomic-like functions used to expand MOs. Minimal basis STO-3G (Slater-type orbitals approximated by 3 Gaussians). Split-valence: 6-31G (6 Gaussians for core, 3+1 for valence). Polarization functions: 6-31G(d) (or 6-31G*) adds d-functions to heavy atoms; 6-31G(d,p) adds p to H. Diffuse functions: 6-31+G for anions and excited states. Correlation-consistent: cc-pVDZ, cc-pVTZ, cc-pVQZ (Dunning). Post-Hartree-Fock methods account for electron correlation (the energy difference between HF and exact in a given basis). Møller-Plesset perturbation theory (MP2, MP3, MP4) adds correlation by perturbation theory (MP2 is the most economical). Configuration interaction (CI): full CI expands the wavefunction as a linear combination of all possible Slater determinants from excitations; CISD includes singles and doubles. Coupled cluster (CC) builds the wavefunction via an exponential excitation operator: CCSD (singles and doubles), CCSD(T) (with perturbative triples) is the “gold standard” of quantum chemistry for accurate energies. Semi-empirical methods (AM1, PM3, PM6) approximate many integrals using empirical parameters to speed up calculations. The composite methods (Gaussian G4, CBS, HEAT) combine multiple calculations to achieve high accuracy at reduced cost.
Computational methods approximate the electronic Schrödinger equation with increasing accuracy from HF through coupled cluster; basis set choice and correlation treatment determine the reliability of predictions.
Part 4

⚡ Density Functional Theory

DFT
Hohenberg-Kohn Theorems, Kohn-Sham Equations, and Applications
Density functional theory (DFT) replaces the many-electron wavefunction with the electron density ρ(r) as the central variable, reducing the 3N-dimensional problem to 3 dimensions. First Hohenberg-Kohn theorem (1964): the ground-state electron density ρ₀(r) uniquely determines the external potential vext(r) and hence all properties of the system (the density is the fundamental variable). Second Hohenberg-Kohn theorem: there exists an energy functional E[ρ] that is minimized by the true ground-state density; the variational principle holds for the density. Kohn-Sham (KS) formalism: map the interacting system onto a fictitious non-interacting system that has the same density ρ(r) = Σi |φi(r)|² (the KS orbitals). The KS equations: [−(ℓ²/2m)∇² + veff(r)]φi = εiφi. The effective potential veff(r) = vext(r) + ∫ ρ(r′)/|r−r′| dr′ + vxc(r), where the exchange-correlation potential vxc = δExc[ρ] / δρ contains all non-classical effects (exchange, correlation, self-interaction correction). The exact Exc[ρ] is unknown; approximations define different DFT methods. Local density approximation (LDA): Exc[ρ] = ∫ ρ(r)εxc(ρ(r)) dr, using the homogeneous electron gas result. Works well for solids but overbinds molecules. Generalized gradient approximation (GGA): includes density gradients ∇ρ. Popular functionals: PBE (Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof), BLYP. GGA improves atomization energies and barriers. Meta-GGA: adds kinetic energy density τ (e.g., TPSS, M06-L). Hybrid functionals: mix exact HF exchange with DFT exchange-correlation. B3LYP (Becke 3-parameter with Lee-Yang-Parr correlation): Exc = ExcLDA + a₀(ExHF − ExLDA) + ax(ExGGA − ExLDA) + EcGGA + ac(EcGGA − EcLDA). PBE0: 25% exact exchange + 75% PBE exchange + PBE correlation. Range-separated hybrids: ωB97X-D, CAM-B3LYP improve charge-transfer and Rydberg states. Applications: thermochemistry (heats of formation, reaction energies to ~1-3 kcal/mol with hybrid functionals), reaction barrier heights, molecular geometries (bond lengths to ~0.01 Å), vibrational frequencies, NMR and EPR parameters, excited states (time-dependent DFT TD-DFT), periodic systems (plane-wave DFT with pseudopotentials in VASP, Quantum ESPRESSO), and materials design (band gaps, adsorption energies, catalysis, battery materials).
DFT revolutionized quantum chemistry by using the electron density as the fundamental variable; the accuracy depends on the exchange-correlation functional, with hybrid functionals like B3LYP providing a good balance of accuracy and cost.
