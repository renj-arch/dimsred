Drinfeld Modules — Elliptic Modules, the Langlands Correspondence & the Shtuka

Drinfeld modules (also called elliptic modules) are a function-field analog of abelian varieties, introduced by Vladimir Drinfeld in his work on the Langlands program. A Drinfeld module φ of rank r over a field L of characteristic p is a ring homomorphism φ: A → L{τ} where A = 𝔽_q[T] and L{τ} is the twisted polynomial ring with τ·a = a^q·τ. The rank r is the dimension of the φ_A-module as an L-vector space. The analytic uniformization gives the exponential map exp: L_∞ → L_∞^r, the lattice analogy: a Drinfeld module of rank r corresponds to a lattice in Drinfeld's "upper half-plane" Ω^r. The reduction at finite places yields the theory of "true" and "false" eigenvalues. Drinfeld's proof of the global Langlands conjecture for GL₂ over function fields (1974) used the moduli of Drinfeld modules and the "Eichler-Shimura" relation for Hecke correspondences. The shtuka — a vector bundle with a Frobenius structure — underlies both Drinfeld modules and the Fargues-Fontaine curve. Applications extend to Goss's L-functions and Anderson t-motives.

Part 1

📏 The Definition and Basic Properties

Foundations
Twisted Polynomials, A-Modules, Rank, and Tate Modules
Let A = 𝔽_q[T] (the function-field analog of ℤ) and L a field of characteristic p. The twisted polynomial ring L{τ} is the ring of polynomials in τ with coefficients in L, subject to the commutation relation τ·a = a^q·τ for a ∈ L — this is a non-commutative ring. A Drinfeld A-module of rank r over L is a ring homomorphism φ: A → L{τ} such that φ_T = a_0 τ⁰ + a₁ τ + ⋯ + a_r τ^r where a_r ≠ 0 and a_0 = T. The rank is r = deg_τ(φ_T). The characteristic of φ at a prime p of A is derived from the "characteristic polynomial" of Frobenius at p. The exponential map exp_φ: L_∞ → L_∞^r is a ℂ_∞-valued entire function with double periodicities — its kernel is a lattice in ℂ_∞^r. The category of Drinfeld modules has isogenies (finite, surjective homomorphisms) and Tate modules T_ℓ(φ) for ℓ ≠ p — these are the ℓ-adic Tate modules analogous to those of an elliptic curve, giving ℓ-adic representations of the absolute Galois group of L.
A Drinfeld module is a homomorphism from the function-field integers (𝔽_q[T]) into twisted polynomials; its rank is the τ-degree of the image of T.
Part 2

🌍 The Analytic Uniformization

Uniformization
Drinfeld Upper Half-Plane, Lattices, and the Exponential Map
The Drinfeld upper half-plane Ω^r = ℂ_∞^r \ {all hyperplanes defined over K} is the analog of the classical upper half-plane — it parametrizes lattices of rank r in ℂ_∞. A lattice Λ ⊂ ℂ_∞^r of rank r gives rise to a Drinfeld module φ via the exponential exp_Λ(z) = z ∏_{λ∈Λ\{0}} (1 − z/λ). The Drinfeld uniformization theorem: every Drinfeld module of rank r over ℂ_∞ is isomorphic to ℂ_∞/Λ for some lattice Λ. The period lattice is ker(exp_φ). The modular interpretation: the moduli space M^r_d of Drinfeld modules of rank r with level structure (the Drinfeld modular variety) is a scheme of finite type over 𝔽_q. Points of M^r_d correspond to isomorphism classes of Drinfeld modules with a chosen level structure (a trivialization of the Tate module modulo d).
Drinfeld modules over ℂ_∞ are precisely quotients of ℂ_∞^r by lattices — a direct analog of the uniformization of elliptic curves by ℂ/Λ.
Part 3

🧮 The Global Langlands for GL₂

Langlands
Drinfeld's Proof of the Langlands Conjecture for GL₂ over Function Fields
The global Langlands correspondence for function fields (Drinfeld, 1974): for a global function field K = 𝔽_q(X) (a smooth projective curve over 𝔽_q), there is a bijection between (1) cuspidal automorphic representations of GL₂(A_K) (where A_K is the adele ring of K) and (2) ℓ-adic Galois representations ρ: Gal(K^{sep}/K) → GL₂(̄ℚ_ℓ) that are irreducible, unramified outside a finite set, and whose determinant is a power of the cyclotomic character. Drinfeld's proof constructs the Galois representation from the ℓ-adic cohomology of the moduli space of Drinfeld modules of rank 2. The key ingredient is the Hecke correspondences on the Drinfeld modular variety M^2_d and the Eichler-Shimura relation: the Frobenius correspondence at a prime p satisfies T_p = Frob_p + (deg p)·(Frob_p)^{-1} acting on the cohomology, matching the Hecke operator on the automorphic side. This was the first Langlands correspondence proved for a reductive group beyond GL₁.
Drinfeld's proof of Langlands for GL₂ over function fields used the moduli of Drinfeld modules and the Eichler-Shimura relation to match Hecke eigenvalues with Frobenius eigenvalues.
Part 4

🔥 Shtukas and the Geometric Langlands Program

Shtukas
Frobenius Structures, the Geometric Langlands, and Later Developments
A shtuka (Russian for "a thing") is a vector bundle E on a curve X × S together with a Frobenius map F: (Frob_S)^*(E) → E such that the cokernel is supported on a divisor — the "pole" and "zero" of the shtuka. Drinfeld modules of rank r give shtukas with r marked points. The geometric Langlands program over function fields: the moduli stack of G-shtukas on X replaces the adelic double quotient for the Langlands correspondence. Drinfeld's proof for GL₂ was the first step. Laurent Lafforgue (2002, Fields Medal) proved the full Langlands correspondence for GL_n over function fields using the moduli of shtukas with level structures. Roman Bezrukavnikov developed geometric Langlands for GL_n in the function field case using the affine Grassmannian and perverse sheaves. Vincent Lafforgue (2018, Breakthrough Prize) generalized to arbitrary reductive groups via the moduli of G-shtukas. The Fargues-Fontaine curve (2018) places shtukas into a unified framework in p-adic geometry, connecting Drinfeld modules, p-adic Hodge theory, and the local Langlands program.
Shtukas — vector bundles with Frobenius structure — are the fundamental geometric objects underlying Drinfeld modules and the full Langlands program over function fields.

Anderson t-Motives and the Category of Dual t-Motives

Greg Anderson introduced t-motives in 1986 as a function-field analogue of the category of motives for abelian varieties. A t-motive over a field L equipped with a homomorphism 𝔽_q[T] → L is a finitely generated module M over the non-commutative polynomial ring L{τ} (where τ acts as the q-th power Frobenius on L) together with a σ-linear map Φ: M → M satisfying certain finiteness conditions. The rank of a t-motive is the dimension of M as an L-vector space after inverting τ. Drinfeld modules of rank r correspond precisely to t-motives of rank r that are “effective” and “pure” of weight 1. The dual t-motive M^∨ = Hom_{L{τ}}(M, L{τ}) carries a natural τ-action and provides a contravariant equivalence between the category of t-motives and the category of dual t-motives. This duality mirrors the Poincaré duality for abelian varieties and is essential for constructing the rigid analytic trivialization of the associated τ-module. Anderson’s theory also yields a Tannakian category of t-motives over a finite field, with fiber functor given by the Betti realization (the ℓ-adic Tate module) and the de Rham realization (the Lie algebra of the Drinfeld module). The motivic Galois group of a t-motive controls the algebraic relations among its periods and logarithms, and the work of Papanikolas (2008) on the transcendence degree of the period matrix of a t-motive uses this Tannakian formalism to prove algebraic independence results for Drinfeld module periods. In particular, for a Drinfeld module φ of rank r over a finite field, the period lattice Λ_φ ⊂ ℂ_∞^r is the kernel of the exponential map exp_φ, and the entries of a period matrix generate a field extension of 𝔽_q(T) whose transcendence degree equals the dimension of the motivic Galois group. This deep link between t-motives, Galois representations, and transcendence is a cornerstone of modern arithmetic geometry over function fields.

Local Langlands Correspondence for GL_n over Function Fields

The local Langlands correspondence for a reductive group G over a local field F matches irreducible smooth representations of G(F) with Langlands parameters — homomorphisms from the Weil-Deligne group W'_F to the L-group ^L G. For function fields, the local fields are of the form F_q((t)) (equal characteristic) or finite extensions of Q_p (mixed characteristic). In the equal-characteristic case, the correspondence for GL_n was established by Laumon, Rapoport, and Stuhler (1993) using the geometry of Drinfeld upper half-spaces and the cohomology of the Lubin-Tate tower. Their construction realizes the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over F_q((t)) in the ℓ-adic cohomology of the Drinfeld symmetric space Ω^n = ℙn-1(ℂ_∞) {F_q((t))-rational hyperplanes}. The action of GL_n(F_q((t))) × WF_q((t)) × ℤ on the cohomology yields a bijection between irreducible supercuspidal representations of GL_n(F_q((t))) and n-dimensional irreducible representations of the Weil group WF_q((t)). For general irreducible representations, the correspondence is obtained by parabolic induction and the Zelevinsky classification. The compatibility with the global Langlands correspondence proved by Drinfeld (n=2) and Lafforgue (n≥2) is a crucial check: the local components of a global cuspidal automorphic representation of GL_n(A_K) correspond under the local Langlands map to the restrictions of the associated global Galois representation to the local Weil groups. Vincent Lafforgue (2018) extended the local correspondence to arbitrary reductive groups over function fields using the moduli of G-shtukas with one leg, thereby unifying the local and global pictures. The local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over function fields also admits a purely categorical formulation via the geometric Satake equivalence for the affine Grassmannian of GL_n over F_q((t)), where perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian correspond to representations of the dual group GL_n(ℚ_ℓ). This categorical approach, developed by Bezrukavnikov and Finkelberg, provides a geometric realization of the local Langlands parameters as Frobenius eigenvalues on the stalks of certain Hecke eigensheaves.

Identifying the correct cohomological degree
In the ℓ-adic cohomology of the Drinfeld modular curve M2_d, which degree carries the cuspidal automorphic representations corresponding to irreducible Galois representations?
The first cohomology group H1_et(M2_d, ℚ_ℓ) carries the cuspidal part; the Eichler-Shimura relation identifies the Hecke eigenvalues with Frobenius eigenvalues on this cohomology. The cuspidal automorphic representations of GL2(A_K) appear in the middle-degree cohomology of the Drinfeld modular curve, which for a curve is H1. The Hecke operators T_p act on H1, and the Eichler-Shimura relation T_p = Frob_p + (deg p)·Frob_p-1 matches the Hecke eigenvalues with the Frobenius eigenvalues of the associated 2-dimensional Galois representation. Higher cohomology groups vanish for dimension reasons, and H0 corresponds to the Eisenstein part.
Cohomological degree shortcut
For Drinfeld modular varieties of dimension d, the cuspidal automorphic representations for GLd+1 appear in H^d. Memorize that the middle degree equals the dimension of the variety; for the curve case (rank 2) it is H1. This rule avoids confusion with the classical modular curve where cusp forms live in H1 as well.

Moduli of Drinfeld Modules with Level Structure and Compactifications

The fine moduli scheme M^r_d parametrizes isomorphism classes of Drinfeld modules of rank r over 𝔽_q-schemes together with a level-d structure, i.e., an isomorphism (ℤ/dℤ)^r ≅ φ[d] of the d-torsion subgroup scheme with a constant group scheme. Drinfeld proved that M^r_d is a smooth affine scheme of finite type over 𝔽_q of dimension r-1. For r=2, M2_d is a modular curve whose compactification is a smooth projective curve over 𝔽_q. The compactification is achieved by adding cusps corresponding to degenerate Drinfeld modules (Tate uniformizations) where the lattice degenerates to a rank-1 lattice. Gekeler (1991) gave an explicit description of the cusps in terms of the Bruhat-Tits tree for PGL2(F_q((t))) and computed the genus of the compactified curve. For higher rank r≥3, the moduli space M^r_d is not proper, and several compactifications exist: the Baily-Borel (Satake) compactification, the toroidal compactifications constructed by Pink (1997) using the theory of toroidal embeddings, and the Drinfeld compactification which adds “boundary strata” corresponding to flags of sub-lattices. The toroidal compactifications are smooth and have normal crossing boundary divisors, making them suitable for Hodge-theoretic and cohomological computations. The boundary strata are themselves products of lower-rank Drinfeld modular varieties, reflecting the Levi decomposition of parabolic subgroups of GL_r. The cohomology of the compactified varieties carries a mixed Hodge structure (in the ℓ-adic sense) and the weight filtration is governed by the stratification. The Hecke correspondences extend to the compactifications, and the trace formula for the action of Hecke operators on the cohomology of the compactified moduli space is a key input in the proof of the global Langlands correspondence for GL_r by Lafforgue. The level structure d must be prime to the characteristic of the base field to ensure representability; when d is divisible by the characteristic, one uses Drinfeld level structures (full level p^n structures) which are representable by formal schemes.

Cohomology of Drinfeld Modular Varieties and the Eichler-Shimura Relation

The ℓ-adic étale cohomology groups H^i_et(M^r_d ⊗ 𝔽̄_q, ℚ_ℓ) carry commuting actions of the Hecke algebra (away from the level and the characteristic), the Galois group Gal(𝔽̄_q/𝔽_q), and the group GL_r(A_f^d) of finite adeles prime to d. Drinfeld’s proof of the Langlands correspondence for GL2 rests on the Eichler-Shimura relation for the Drinfeld modular curve M2_d: for a prime p not dividing d, the Hecke correspondence T_p acting on H1 satisfies T_p = Frob_p + (deg p)·Frob_p-1, where Frob_p is the geometric Frobenius at p. This relation identifies the eigenvalues of T_p on cuspidal Hecke eigenforms with the eigenvalues of Frobenius on the associated 2-dimensional Galois representation. For higher rank, the cohomology of M^r_d is concentrated in degrees r-1 through r(r-1)/2 (the “middle degrees”). The work of Gekeler, Reversat, and others computed the cohomology of M^r_d for small r and level, revealing that the cuspidal cohomology (the part not coming from Eisenstein cohomology or boundary contributions) appears in degree r-1 and carries irreducible representations of GL_r(A_f^d) that correspond to cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_r(A_K). The Eichler-Shimura relation generalizes to a “Shimura relation” for higher rank: the Hecke operators at unramified primes satisfy a polynomial relation whose roots are the Satake parameters of the associated automorphic representation. Lafforgue’s proof of the global Langlands correspondence for GL_n uses the full cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas with level structure, which is a compactification of the moduli of Drinfeld modules with additional data. The key geometric input is the Grothendieck-Lefschetz trace formula for the correspondence on the moduli of shtukas, which yields the equality of traces of Hecke operators and Frobenius on the cohomology. The purity of the cohomology (Weil conjectures for function fields, proved by Deligne) ensures that the eigenvalues are algebraic numbers with the expected absolute values, matching the Ramanujan-Petersson conjecture for GL_n over function fields.

Applications to Explicit Class Field Theory and Transcendence

Drinfeld modules provide an explicit class field theory for global function fields, analogous to the theory of complex multiplication for elliptic curves. Hayes (1979) showed that for a global function field K = 𝔽_q(X) and a finite place ∞, the maximal abelian extension of K unramified outside ∞ is generated by the torsion points of a sign-normalized rank-1 Drinfeld module (the Carlitz module). For higher rank, the torsion points of a Drinfeld module φ of rank r over a finite field generate abelian extensions of the field of definition of φ, and the Galois action on the Tate module T_ℓ(φ) yields a reciprocity law describing the Artin map. This explicit reciprocity is a function-field version of the Kronecker Jugendtraum. On the transcendence side, the periods of a Drinfeld module φ (the lattice Λ_φ = ker(exp_φ)) are analogues of the periods of an elliptic curve. Anderson and Thakur (1990) proved that the period of the Carlitz module is transcendental over 𝔽_q(T). Yu (1991) extended this to show that for any Drinfeld module φ over a finite field, the periods are algebraically independent over 𝔽_q(T) up to the dimension predicted by the motivic Galois group. Papanikolas (2008) developed a general criterion for algebraic independence of periods and logarithms of t-motives using the Tannakian formalism and the theory of Frobenius difference equations. His theorem implies that for a Drinfeld module φ of rank r over a finite field, the transcendence degree of the field generated by the entries of a period matrix equals the dimension of the motivic Galois group of the associated t-motive. These results have applications to special values of Goss L-functions, which are function-field analogues of the Riemann zeta function. The Goss L-function L(s, φ) attached to a Drinfeld module φ interpolates special values at positive integers, and the transcendence results imply that these special values are algebraically independent over 𝔽_q(T) in many cases. The interplay between explicit class field theory, transcendence, and the Langlands correspondence remains a vibrant area of research, with recent work of Chang, Papanikolas, and others on the algebraic relations among periods of tensor products of Drinfeld modules.

Drinfeld Modular Forms and the Drinfeld Discriminant Function

The theory of Drinfeld modular forms parallels the classical theory of modular forms for SL₂(ℤ) but lives on the Drinfeld upper half-plane Ω = ℂ_∞ K_∞, where K_∞ is the completion of a global function field K at a fixed place ∞ and ℂ_∞ is the completion of an algebraic closure of K_∞. A Drinfeld modular form of weight k and type m for a congruence subgroup Γ ⊂ GL₂(A) (where A is the ring of functions regular away from ∞) is a rigid analytic function f : Ω → ℂ_∞ satisfying f((aτ + b)/(cτ + d)) = (cτ + d)^k det(γ)^m f(τ) for all γ = [[a,b],[c,d]] ∈ Γ, and which is holomorphic at the cusps. The ring of all such forms is graded by weight and type. The Drinfeld discriminant function Δ(τ) is the analogue of the classical Δ(τ) = q ∏(1-q^n)²⁴; it is a cusp form of weight q²-1 and type 0 for GL₂(A) when A = 𝔽_q[T] and ∞ is the infinite place. Its zeros are simple and occur exactly at the points of Ω where the associated Drinfeld module has extra automorphisms, i.e., where the lattice is homothetic to a lattice with complex multiplication. The j-invariant j(τ) = g(τ)q²-1/Δ(τ), where g is the Eisenstein series of weight q-1, gives an isomorphism from the quotient Γ\Ω to the affine line 𝔸¹, and its values at CM points generate the maximal abelian extension of K unramified outside ∞, realizing the explicit class field theory of Hayes. The Fourier expansion of a Drinfeld modular form at the cusp ∞ involves coefficients in the field of definition of the form; for the discriminant, Δ(τ) = ∑a∈A_+ c_a u^a where u = 1/exp_φ(τ) is the uniformizer at the cusp and the coefficients c_a satisfy multiplicative relations reflecting the Hecke action. The Hecke operators T_𝔭 for primes 𝔭 of A act on the space of modular forms, and the eigenvalues of T_𝔭 on a normalized eigenform f are the Satake parameters of the associated automorphic representation of GL₂(A_K). The Petersson inner product ⟨f,g⟩ = ∫Γ\Ω f(τ) g(τ) |Δ(τ)|2/(q²-1) dμ(τ) (with a suitably normalized measure) makes the space of cusp forms a Hilbert space, and the Hecke operators are self-adjoint, yielding a spectral decomposition. The dimension formula for the space of cusp forms of weight k and type m is given by a Riemann-Roch calculation on the compactified moduli curve, and for large weights the dimension grows linearly with k. The theory of Drinfeld modular forms of higher level and the Atkin-Lehner involutions provide the function-field analogue of the classical newform theory of Atkin and Lehner.

Bad Reduction of Drinfeld Modules and the Tate Uniformization

Just as elliptic curves over p-adic fields can have good, multiplicative, or additive reduction, Drinfeld modules over local function fields K_𝔭 (the completion of K at a finite place 𝔭) exhibit a trichotomy of reduction types. Let φ be a Drinfeld module of rank r over K_𝔭 with ring of integers O_𝔭 and uniformizer π_𝔭. After a finite extension, φ acquires a model over O_𝔭. The reduction φ̃ is a Drinfeld module over the residue field 𝔽_𝔭 if the coefficients of φ_a for a ∈ A are integral and the leading coefficient of φ_π_𝔭 reduces to a non-zero element; this is good reduction. If the leading coefficient of φ_π_𝔭 reduces to zero but the constant term of φ_π_𝔭 is a unit, the reduction is multiplicative (or split multiplicative if the constant term is a (q^r-1)-th power in the residue field). In the multiplicative case, the Tate uniformization theorem for Drinfeld modules states that there exists a unique rank-1 Drinfeld module ψ over K_𝔭 and a lattice Λ ⊂ K_𝔭^sep of rank r such that φ is isomorphic to the quotient ψ/Λ as a Drinfeld module over K_𝔭^sep. The lattice Λ is a free A-module of rank r, discrete in K_𝔭^sep, and the Galois action on Λ is given by a character χ : Gal(K_𝔭^sep/K_𝔭) → GL_r(A) which is unramified outside the primes dividing the level. The Tate period map identifies the rigid analytic space φ(K_𝔭^sep) with the quotient K_𝔭^sep/Λ, and the reduction map φ(K_𝔭^sep) → φ̃(𝔽_𝔭) corresponds to the map K_𝔭^sep/Λ → ψ̃(𝔽_𝔭) induced by the exponential of ψ. The monodromy pairing on the Tate module T_ℓ(φ) for ℓ ≠ char(K) takes values in A_ℓ(1) and is non-degenerate precisely when the reduction is multiplicative. In the additive reduction case, the constant term of φ_π_𝔭 also reduces to zero, and the reduction φ̃ is a Frobenius module (a module over the ring of twisted polynomials where the Frobenius acts nilpotently). The conductor of φ at 𝔭, which measures the wild ramification of the Galois representation on T_ℓ(φ), is computed by the Swan conductor of the associated ℓ-adic representation; for multiplicative reduction it equals the valuation of the j-invariant, while for additive reduction it can be larger. The Néron model of a Drinfeld module over O_𝔭 exists and is unique up to unique isomorphism; its special fiber is a smooth group scheme over 𝔽_𝔭 whose identity component is a Drinfeld module in the good and multiplicative cases, and a unipotent group in the additive case. The component group of the Néron model is finite and its order is the valuation of the minimal discriminant. The theory of bad reduction is essential for the local Langlands correspondence, as the local L-factor and ε-factor of a Drinfeld module are determined by its reduction type and the monodromy operator on the associated Weil-Deligne representation.

The Geometric Satake Correspondence for Function Fields

The geometric Satake correspondence, originally formulated by Ginzburg, Lusztig, and Mirković-Vilonen for complex reductive groups, has a profound function-field analogue that plays a central role in the geometric Langlands program over curves over finite fields. Let G be a split reductive group over 𝔽_q, and let X be a smooth projective curve over 𝔽_q. The affine Grassmannian Gr_G = G(ℱ((t)))/G(ℱ[[t]]) is an ind-scheme over 𝔽_q whose ℱ_q-points parametrize G-bundles on the formal disk with a trivialization on the punctured disk. The category of spherical perverse sheaves PervG(ℱ[[t]])(Gr_G, ℚ_ℓ) on the affine Grassmannian, equipped with the convolution product, is a tensor category. The geometric Satake theorem states that this tensor category is equivalent to the category of finite-dimensional representations of the Langlands dual group ^L G over ℚ_ℓ. The equivalence is given by the fiber functor at the base point, and the convolution product corresponds to the tensor product of representations. In the function-field setting, this correspondence is used to construct Hecke eigensheaves on the moduli stack Bun_G of G-bundles on X. For a dominant coweight λ of G, the Hecke correspondence Hecke_λ → Bun_G × Bun_G has fibers isomorphic to the Schubert variety Gr_λ in the affine Grassmannian. The intersection cohomology complex IC_λ on Gr_λ corresponds under geometric Satake to the irreducible representation V_λ of ^L G. The Hecke operator T_λ acting on sheaves on Bun_G is defined by the correspondence, and a Hecke eigensheaf F is a perverse sheaf on Bun_G equipped with isomorphisms T_λ(F) ≃ V_λ ⊗ F compatible with the tensor structure. The geometric Langlands conjecture asserts a bijection between irreducible ^L G-local systems on X and Hecke eigensheaves on Bun_G. The geometric Satake correspondence provides the representation-theoretic input to define the Hecke operators and to match the eigenvalues (which are local systems) with the representations of ^L G. In the Drinfeld module context, for G = GL_r, the affine Grassmannian is the ind-scheme of lattices in K_∞^r up to homothety, and the Schubert varieties are the Drinfeld modular varieties. The geometric Satake correspondence for GL_r identifies the category of spherical perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian with the category of representations of GL_r(ℚ_ℓ). The Hecke operators on the moduli stack of shtukas are defined using the convolution on the affine Grassmannian, and the cohomology of the moduli of shtukas carries an action of the spherical Hecke algebra which, via geometric Satake, corresponds to the action of the representation ring of GL_r. This is the geometric mechanism behind the equality of Hecke eigenvalues and Frobenius eigenvalues in Lafforgue's proof of the global Langlands correspondence. The function-field geometric Satake also has a version for the loop group LG, where the category of D-modules on the affine Grassmannian (in characteristic 0) or ℓ-adic sheaves (in characteristic p) is equivalent to the category of representations of the Langlands dual group. The proof uses the Beilinson-Drinfeld Grassmannian, which is a family of affine Grassmannians over the curve X, and the factorization property of the convolution product. This factorization structure is essential for the construction of the Hecke eigensheaves via the geometric Langlands correspondence.

Special Values of Goss L-Functions and the Gross-Koblitz Formula

The Goss L-function L(s, φ) attached to a Drinfeld module φ of rank r over a global function field K is the function-field analogue of the Hasse-Weil L-function of an elliptic curve. For a place v of K where φ has good reduction, the local factor is L_v(s, φ) = det(1 - Fr_v q_v-s | V_ℓ(φ)I_v)-1, where V_ℓ(φ) is the ℓ-adic Tate module (ℓ ≠ char(K)), Fr_v is the geometric Frobenius, and I_v is the inertia group. The completed L-function Λ(s, φ) = L(s, φ) ∏v|∞ Γ_v(s) satisfies a functional equation relating s to r - s, where the gamma factors Γ_v(s) are products of the Carlitz gamma function Γ_C(z) = 1/∏i=0^∞ (1 - z/qi+1) evaluated at certain arguments. The special values of L(s, φ) at positive integers s = 1, 2, ..., r-1 are of particular arithmetic interest. The Gross-Koblitz formula for function fields, proved by Goss and Sinnott, expresses the values of the Carlitz gamma function at rational arguments in terms of the Carlitz period and the Thakur gamma function. Specifically, for a ∈ ℚ ∩ [0,1), Γ_C(a) = π_C1-a ∏i=0d-1 Γ_T(a_i) where π_C is the Carlitz period, d is the degree of the denominator of a, and Γ_T is the Thakur gamma function defined by a product over monic polynomials. The special values L(n, φ) for n = 1, ..., r-1 are conjectured to be algebraic multiples of powers of the fundamental period π_φ of φ, generalizing the classical conjecture of Deligne for motives. For the Carlitz module (rank 1), L(1, C) = π_C / (T - θ) where θ is the image of T in the constant field, and more generally L(n, C) is an algebraic multiple of π_C^n. For higher rank, the periods of φ form a lattice Λ_φ ⊂ ℂ_∞^r, and the period matrix Π_φ relates the exponential map to the lattice. The special values L(n, φ) are expected to be expressible as algebraic combinations of the entries of Π_φ and the Carlitz period. The work of Anderson, Thakur, and Yu on transcendence of periods implies that the algebraic relations among special values are governed by the motivic Galois group of the t-motive associated to φ. The Goss L-function also interpolates the values of the Goss zeta function ζ_A(s) = ∑a∈A_+ a-s at positive integers, and the special values ζ_A(n) are known to be transcendental for n ≥ 2 (Chang, Papanikolas). The functional equation for L(s, φ) involves the root number ε(φ), which is a product of local ε-factors. For a Drinfeld module with multiplicative reduction at a place v, the local ε-factor is a Gauss sum over the residue field, and the Gross-Koblitz formula evaluates this Gauss sum in terms of the Carlitz gamma function. This provides an explicit link between the analytic properties of L(s, φ) and the arithmetic of the Drinfeld module. The order of vanishing of L(s, φ) at s = r/2 (the central point) is related to the rank of the Mordell-Weil group of φ over K, by the function-field analogue of the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, which is a theorem in many cases due to the work of Tate, Milne, and others on the BSD conjecture for function fields.

The Fargues-Fontaine Curve in Equal Characteristic and the Local Langlands

The Fargues-Fontaine curve XC,𝔽_q provides a geometric framework for the local Langlands correspondence in both mixed and equal characteristic. In equal characteristic, let F be a local field of characteristic p, i.e., a finite extension of 𝔽_q((t)), and let C be the completion of an algebraic closure of F. The Fargues-Fontaine curve XC,F is a complete, regular, one-dimensional scheme over F whose closed points correspond to untilts of C, i.e., pairs (K, ι) where K is a perfectoid field of characteristic p and ι : K^♭ ≃ C is an isomorphism. The curve is constructed as Proj of the graded ring ⊕n≥0 H0(Y, O_Y(n)) where Y is the adic space associated to the untilt. The local Langlands correspondence for GL_n(F) in equal characteristic can be formulated as a bijection between irreducible smooth representations of GL_n(F) and n-dimensional Frobenius-semisimple Weil-Deligne representations of the Weil group W_F. The Fargues-Fontaine curve provides a geometric realization of this correspondence via the moduli stack of shtukas with one leg. The Fargues-Scholze theory of local Shimura varieties in equal characteristic identifies the cohomology of the moduli of shtukas with the direct sum of π ⊗ rec(π) over irreducible representations π of GL_n(F), where rec is the local Langlands correspondence. The curve XC,F also appears in the work of Genestier-Lafforgue on the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over function fields using the moduli of chtoucas (shtukas with multiple legs). The equal-characteristic Fargues-Fontaine curve is simpler than its mixed-characteristic counterpart because the untilt is unique up to isomorphism (the tilt of C is C itself), and the curve is essentially the adic space associated to the Robba ring. This gives a geometric interpretation of the local Langlands correspondence as a statement about the category of vector bundles on a curve, parallel to the geometric Satake correspondence for the global Langlands program.

The Drinfeld Modular Polynomial and Isogeny Graphs

The Drinfeld modular polynomial Φr(X, Y) ∈ 𝔽q[T][X, Y] is the analogue of the classical modular polynomial for elliptic curves. It parametrises isogenies of degree a fixed monic polynomial 𝔞 ⊂ 𝔽q[T] between Drinfeld modules of rank r. For rank two, Φ𝔞(j, j′) = 0 if and only if there exists an 𝔞‑isogeny between the Drinfeld modules with j‑invariants j and j′. The polynomial is symmetric, irreducible over the function field, and its degree in each variable equals the number of cyclic 𝔞‑submodules of a generic Drinfeld module, which is |𝔸/𝔞|r−1 ∏𝔭|𝔞 (1 − |𝔭|−r). The coefficients lie in the ring of integers of the Hilbert class field of the rational function field, reflecting the fact that the moduli problem is defined over the base field 𝔽q(T).

Construction via the Drinfeld Upper Half‑Plane

Let Ω = ℙ1(ℂ∞) ℙ1(F∞) be the Drinfeld upper half‑plane over the completion ℂ∞ of the algebraic closure of F∞. The group GL2(𝔽q[T]) acts on Ω by Möbius transformations, and the quotient Γ\Ω (with Γ a congruence subgroup) is a rigid analytic curve whose points correspond to Drinfeld modules with level structure. The modular polynomial can be obtained by eliminating the parameter τ from the equations j(τ) = X and j(γτ) = Y as γ runs over a set of representatives for ΓGL2(𝔸f)/Γ with determinant 𝔞. This description shows that the zero set of Φ𝔞 in Ω × Ω is a finite union of graphs of Hecke correspondences.

Isogeny Graphs and Supersingular Loci

Fix a prime 𝔭 of 𝔽q[T] not dividing the characteristic. The 𝔭‑isogeny graph has vertices the supersingular j‑invariants in characteristic 𝔭 and edges corresponding to 𝔭‑isogenies. This graph is a regular Ramanujan graph of degree |𝔭| + 1, a consequence of the Eichler–Shimura relation for Drinfeld modules proved by Taguchi and others. The spectral gap of this graph is exactly 2√|𝔭|, mirroring the classical result of Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak. The modular polynomial Φ𝔭(X, Y) reduced modulo 𝔭 gives the adjacency matrix of the graph, and its factorisation over the finite field 𝔽𝔭 encodes the structure of the endomorphism rings of supersingular Drinfeld modules, which are maximal orders in the quaternion algebra ramified at 𝔭 and ∞.

Applications to Cryptography and Explicit Class Field Theory

Because the isogeny graph is an expander, random walks on it mix rapidly. This property underlies the Charles–Goren–Lauter hash function adapted to Drinfeld modules, providing a post‑quantum candidate based on the difficulty of finding isogenies between supersingular Drinfeld modules. On the number‑theoretic side, the modular polynomial allows one to compute the Hilbert class field of an imaginary quadratic function field: if A = 𝔽q[T] and K = 𝔽q(T, √D) with D square‑free of odd degree, then the j‑invariant of a rank‑two Drinfeld module with complex multiplication by the integral closure of A in K generates the Hilbert class field of K. The minimal polynomial of this j‑invariant over K is a factor of Φ𝔞(X, j) for a suitably chosen 𝔞, giving an explicit class field polynomial.

The Theory of Shtukas with Multiple Legs and the Global Langlands Correspondence for GLn

A shtuka with m legs over a scheme S over 𝔽q is a tuple (ℰ, τ, x1, …, xm) where ℰ is a vector bundle of rank n on X × S, τ : σ*ℰ → ℰ is an isomorphism away from the graphs of the sections xi : S → X, and at each leg the map has a prescribed elementary modification of type (1,0,…,0). When m = 1 one recovers the local shtukas used in the Fargues–Fontaine curve; when m ≥ 2 the moduli stack ShtG,μm carries a natural action of G(𝔸f) × WFm and its ℓ‑adic cohomology realises the global Langlands correspondence for GLn over the function field F = 𝔽q(X). Lafforgue’s proof of the global Langlands correspondence for GLn proceeds by constructing a correspondence between the set of irreducible cuspidal automorphic representations π of GLn(𝔸F) and the set of continuous irreducible n‑dimensional ℓ‑adic representations ρ of the Galois group Gal(Fsep/F) that are unramified outside a finite set of places.

Moduli of Shtukas and the Hecke Action

The moduli stack Shtnm is an algebraic stack of finite type over 𝔽q, smooth of dimension (n−1)(m−2) + n(g−1) where g is the genus of X. For each place v of F, the Hecke correspondence at v modifies the vector bundle at the point xv by an elementary transformation of type μv ∈ X*(T)+. These correspondences generate an action of the spherical Hecke algebra ℋ(G(Fv)//G(𝔬v)) on the cohomology groups Hci(Shtnm, ℚℓ). The Frobenius morphism Frq acts on the legs, giving an action of the Weil group WF on the same cohomology. The key theorem of Lafforgue states that for each irreducible constituent π of the automorphic representation occurring in the cohomology, the Frobenius eigenvalues at unramified places match the Satake parameters of π, establishing the equality of L‑functions L(s, π) = L(s, ρπ).

Cohomological Realisation and the Trace Formula

The Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula applied to the correspondence (Hecke, Frobenius) on Shtnm yields an identity between a sum over automorphic representations and a sum over Galois representations. The geometric side involves the fixed points of the correspondence, which are in bijection with isomorphism classes of shtukas with a Frobenius‑invariant structure; these are precisely the points of the moduli stack of global G‑shtukas with one leg at each place of ramification. The spectral side decomposes the cohomology into a direct sum of π ⊗ ρπ ⊗ (⊗i=1m ρπ), where the multiplicity of each π is one (multiplicity one theorem for GLn over function fields). This decomposition is the function‑field analogue of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula and provides a geometric proof of the global Langlands correspondence without requiring the fundamental lemma, which in this setting is replaced by a geometric statement about the cohomology of affine Springer fibres proved by Ngô.

Relation to Drinfeld Modules and Higher Rank

For n = 2, the moduli of shtukas with two legs is closely related to the moduli of rank‑two Drinfeld modules with level structure via the uniformisation of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane. The cohomology of the Drinfeld modular curve appears as a direct summand of the cohomology of Sht22. For n > 2, there is no direct analogue of Drinfeld modules, but the shtuka formalism provides a uniform geometric framework for all ranks. The local‑global compatibility at a place v is encoded in the fact that the restriction of the global shtuka to the formal neighbourhood of the leg at v yields a local shtuka, whose associated Galois representation is the local Langlands parameter of πv. This compatibility was established by Lafforgue using the geometry of the moduli of shtukas with one leg (the local theory) and the global theory simultaneously.

The Tannakian Category of Anderson t‑Motives and the Motivic Galois Group

Anderson introduced t‑motives as a function‑field analogue of Grothendieck’s motives, providing a Tannakian framework for the study of special values of Goss L‑functions and transcendence results. A t‑motive over a field K ⊃ 𝔽q(T) is a finitely generated module M over the non‑commutative ring K[τ] (with τ a = aqτ) equipped with a σ‑linear Frobenius isomorphism τ : σ*M → M, where σ is the q‑power Frobenius on K. The category of t‑motives is rigid, abelian, and equipped with a fibre functor ω : M ↦ M ⊗K[τ] K((τ−1)) (the Betti realisation). By Tannaka duality, the automorphism group of ω is a pro‑algebraic group over 𝔽q(T), called the motivic Galois group GM. For a Drinfeld module φ of rank r, the associated t‑motive M(φ) has rank r over K[τ] and its motivic Galois group is a subgroup of GLr determined by the endomorphism ring of φ. If φ has generic characteristic (no complex multiplication), then GM = GLr; if φ has complex multiplication by a field extension L of degree r, then GM is a torus isomorphic to ResL/𝔽q(T) 𝔾m.

Dual t‑Motives and the de Rham Realisation

The dual t‑motive M∨ = HomK[τ](M, K[τ]) carries a natural left K[τ]‑module structure and a Frobenius τ∨. The de Rham realisation is given by the quotient M∨ / (τ − 1)M∨, which is a finite‑dimensional K‑vector space equipped with a connection ∇ derived from the τ‑action. The comparison isomorphism between the Betti and de Rham realisations is provided by the period matrix of the Drinfeld module, whose entries are the periods and quasi‑periods of φ. The motivic Galois group acts on the period matrix, and the dimension of GM equals the transcendence degree of the field generated by the periods over K. This is the function‑field analogue of Grothendieck’s period conjecture, proved in many cases by Papanikolas using the theory of difference Galois groups.

Applications to Transcendence and Special Values

Anderson’s log‑algebraicity theorem states that for a t‑motive M coming from a Drinfeld module, the special values of the associated Goss L‑function at positive integers are 𝔽q(T)‑linear combinations of logarithms of algebraic points on the Drinfeld module. The motivic Galois group controls the algebraic relations among these values: if GM is as large as possible (GLr), then the only relations are those forced by the functional equation of the L‑function. This yields explicit transcendence results, for example the Carlitz period πC is transcendental over 𝔽q(T) and the values ζC(n) for n ≥ 1 are algebraically independent over 𝔽q(T) modulo the known Eulerian relations. The Tannakian formalism also allows one to construct the motivic Galois group of a tensor product of t‑motives, giving a geometric interpretation of the Rankin–Selberg convolution of Goss L‑functions.

The Explicit Class Field Theory via Drinfeld Modules: The Hayes Construction

Hayes extended the classical theory of complex multiplication to the function‑field setting, showing that the torsion points of a rank‑one Drinfeld module (the Carlitz module) generate the maximal abelian extension of the rational function field 𝔽q(T) unramified outside the infinite place. For an imaginary quadratic extension K = 𝔽q(T, √D) with D square‑free of odd degree, one chooses a rank‑two Drinfeld module φ with complex multiplication by the integral closure 𝔬K of 𝔽q[T] in K. The j‑invariant j(φ) lies in the Hilbert class field H of K, and the action of the ideal class group Cl(𝔬K) on the set of isomorphism classes of such Drinfeld modules is simply transitive. Explicitly, for each ideal 𝔞 ⊂ 𝔬K there is a unique (up to isomorphism) Drinfeld module φ𝔞 with CM by 𝔬K such that the associated lattice is 𝔞−1. The map 𝔞 ↦ j(φ𝔞) induces an isomorphism Cl(𝔬K) ≅ Gal(H/K).

Construction of the Class Field Polynomial

The minimal polynomial of j(φ) over K is the class field polynomial PK(X) = ∏[𝔞]∈Cl(𝔬K) (X − j(φ𝔞)). Its coefficients lie in K and can be computed using the Drinfeld modular polynomial Φ𝔞(X, Y). For a fixed ideal 𝔞 of 𝔽q[T] coprime to the conductor of K, the polynomial Φ𝔞(X, j(φ)) factors over H, and one of its irreducible factors is exactly PK(X). This provides an algorithm to compute the Hilbert class field: choose a small 𝔞, compute Φ𝔞(X, j) modulo a prime of good reduction, factor it, and identify the factor whose roots generate an abelian extension with the correct Galois group. The degree of PK equals the class number hK, which can be computed via the function‑field analogue of the Dirichlet class number formula involving the Goss zeta function of K.

Relation to Shtukas and the Global Langlands Correspondence

The Hayes construction fits into the global Langlands correspondence for GL2 over K. The automorphic representation associated to the CM Drinfeld module is the theta lift of a Hecke character of K, and the corresponding Galois representation is the induction of that character to Gal(Ksep/K). The moduli of rank‑two Drinfeld modules with CM by 𝔬K is a zero‑dimensional substack of the Drinfeld modular curve, and its points are precisely the fixed points of the Hecke correspondences associated to ideals of 𝔬K. The action of the class group on these points realises the reciprocity law of class field theory geometrically. Moreover, the periods of the CM Drinfeld module are related to the special values of the Goss L‑function of the Hecke character, providing a function‑field analogue of the Chowla–Selberg formula.

The Perfectoid Uniformization of the Drinfeld Modular Curve at Infinite Level

The Drinfeld modular curve Mr parametrising rank‑r Drinfeld modules with full level‑N structure admits a rigid‑analytic uniformisation by the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ωr. When the level is taken to be the full infinite level, i.e. the inverse limit over all powers of a fixed prime 𝔭 of 𝔽q[T], the resulting tower of modular curves becomes a perfectoid space in the sense of Scholze. The key input is the existence of a 𝔭-adic period map from the inverse limit of the Drinfeld upper half‑planes to the perfectoid cover of the modular curve, which is equivariant for the action of GLr(𝔽q[[𝔭]]) × Dr× where Dr is the division algebra of invariant 1/r over the local field F𝔭. This perfectoid uniformisation yields a canonical Hodge–Tate period map πHT : Mr,∞ → ℙr−1 whose fibres are torsors under the Lubin–Tate group. The construction parallels the p‑adic uniformisation of Shimura varieties but uses the equal‑characteristic geometry of the Fargues–Fontaine curve for F𝔭. As a consequence, the étale cohomology of the infinite‑level modular curve carries a continuous action of the local Langlands group, and the resulting Galois representations realise the local Langlands correspondence for GLr over F𝔭 in the sense of Harris–Taylor. The perfectoid approach also provides a new proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GLr over function fields, bypassing the global methods of Lafforgue and giving a purely local geometric realisation.

The Function Field p‑adic Langlands Correspondence for GL₂

In the equal‑characteristic setting, a p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL2(F𝔭) has been developed by adapting the Colmez–Fontaine theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules to the Drinfeld upper half‑plane. The central object is the category of étale (φ,Γ)‑modules over the Robba ring ℛ𝔭 attached to the local field F𝔭. A smooth admissible representation π of GL2(F𝔭) on a ℚp-vector space corresponds to a rank‑two (φ,Γ)‑module D(π) equipped with a triangulation reflecting the Bernstein–Zelevinsky classification. The correspondence is compatible with the local Langlands correspondence of Henniart: the Weil–Deligne representation attached to π matches the (φ,Γ)‑module via the Fontaine functor. A crucial technical tool is the theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules over the period ring BdR+ constructed from the tilt of the perfectoid cover of the Drinfeld modular curve. The resulting equivalence of categories respects tensor products, duals, and the local epsilon factors. Moreover, the correspondence interpolates in families: a p‑adic family of automorphic forms on the Drinfeld modular curve gives rise to a coherent sheaf of (φ,Γ)‑modules on the weight space, providing a function‑field analogue of the eigenvariety constructed by Buzzard and Chenevier. This p‑adic Langlands programme has been used to prove cases of the function‑field Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture for Drinfeld modules of rank two by relating the order of vanishing of the Goss L‑function to the dimension of a Selmer group defined via (φ,Γ)‑modules.

Worked Example: Computing the Hodge–Tate Period Map
Let φ be a rank‑2 Drinfeld module over a finite extension L/F𝔭 with good reduction. Describe the image of the associated point x ∈ Ω2(L) under the Hodge–Tate period map πHT : Ω2 → ℙ1.
The Hodge–Tate period map is defined by the exact sequence of (φ,Γ)‑modules 0 → T𝔭(φ) ⊗ ℂp → DdR(φ) → Lie(φ) ⊗ ℂp → 0. The point x corresponds to a lattice Λ ⊂ ℂp2 stable under φT. The quotient ℂp2/Λ is the Tate module T𝔭(φ) ⊗ ℂp. The Hodge–Tate weights are (0,1); the line Lie(φ) ⊗ ℂp inside DdR(φ) is the image of the first basis vector under the period matrix. Concretely, if φT = T + gτ + Δτ2 with g,Δ ∈ L, then the period matrix is (1, ω; 0, ωq) where ω is a fundamental period. The Hodge–Tate period map sends x to the class of the line generated by (1,0) in ℙ1(L), i.e. the point 0 ∈ ℙ1. For a general Drinfeld module the image is the point in ℙ1 determined by the ratio of the two periods, which is the j‑invariant of φ.

The Geometry of Parahoric Level Structures on Drinfeld Modules

Classical Drinfeld modular varieties parametrise Drinfeld modules with full level‑N structure, i.e. an isomorphism (𝔬/N)r ≅ φ[N]. For many arithmetic applications one needs level structures at a prime 𝔭 that are not full but parahoric, corresponding to a choice of a lattice chain in the local shtuka. A parahoric level structure of type μ (a dominant cocharacter of GLr) is an isomorphism between the associated graded of the 𝔭‑adic Tate module and a fixed standard lattice chain. The moduli stack Mr,μ of rank‑r Drinfeld modules with parahoric level μ is a smooth Deligne–Mumford stack over Spec 𝔬𝔭 whose generic fibre is the usual Drinfeld modular curve and whose special fibre admits a stratification by the relative position of the lattice chain with respect to the Hodge filtration. This stratification is indexed by the affine Weyl group Wa and the Newton polygon strata coincide with the Ekedahl–Oort strata. The geometry of these strata controls the reduction of Drinfeld modules: the supersingular locus corresponds to the minimal length element in the double coset Wa\Wa/Wa. The cohomology of Mr,μ carries an action of the Hecke algebra at 𝔭, and the resulting Galois representations realise the local Langlands correspondence for the parahoric subgroup. In particular, the nearby cycles complex on the special fibre computes the local factors of the automorphic L‑function. This theory, developed by Hartl, Viehmann, and others, provides the function‑field analogue of the Rapoport–Zink spaces and is essential for the construction of the local Shimura varieties in equal characteristic.

The Function Field Analogue of the Gross–Zagier Formula and Heegner Points

Let K be an imaginary quadratic extension of the rational function field F = 𝔽q(T) in which the prime at infinity splits. A rank‑2 Drinfeld module φ with complex multiplication by the integral closure 𝔬K gives rise to a Heegner point on the Drinfeld modular curve X0(N) for a suitable level N. The height pairing of these Heegner points, computed via the Arakelov intersection theory on the modular curve, is conjecturally related to the derivative at the central point of the Goss L‑function L(φ/K, s). The function‑field Gross–Zagier formula, proved by Wei Zhang and later refined by Yun and Zhang, states that the Néron–Tate height of the Heegner divisor equals a non‑zero explicit constant times L′(φ/K, 1). The proof uses the relative trace formula for the group GL2 over K and the geometry of the moduli of shtukas with two legs. The Heegner points are constructed as fixed points of a Hecke correspondence on the moduli of rank‑2 shtukas with a leg at the CM point. The derivative of the L‑function appears as the orbital integral of a test function supported on the elliptic regular elements. This formula has immediate consequences for the Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture for Drinfeld modules: if L(φ/K, 1) ≠ 0 then the Mordell–Weil group of φ over K is finite, and if L′(φ/K, 1) ≠ 0 then the rank is exactly one. The explicit constant involves the discriminant of K, the conductor of φ, and the Tamagawa numbers at the bad primes, mirroring the classical Gross–Zagier constant.

The Iwasawa Theory for Drinfeld Modules and the Main Conjecture

Let φ be a Drinfeld module of rank r over a global function field F with good ordinary reduction at a prime 𝔭. The 𝔭‑adic Tate module T𝔭(φ) is a free 𝔬𝔭-module of rank r equipped with a continuous action of the absolute Galois group GF. The cyclotomic 𝔭‑extension F∞/F is obtained by adjoining all 𝔭‑power torsion points of the Carlitz module. The Iwasawa algebra Λ = 𝔬𝔭[[Gal(F∞/F)]] acts on the Selmer group Sel𝔭(φ/F∞) defined as the kernel of the global-to-local map in Galois cohomology. The Main Conjecture of Iwasawa theory for Drinfeld modules, formulated by Taelman and proved in the rank‑1 case by Anglès, Taelman, and others, asserts that the characteristic ideal of the Pontryagin dual of Sel𝔭(φ/F∞) coincides with the ideal generated by the 𝔭‑adic Goss L‑function L𝔭(φ, s) inside Λ. The 𝔭‑adic L‑function is constructed by interpolating the special values L(φ, χ, 1) for finite‑order characters χ of Gal(F∞/F) using the theory of Anderson t‑motives and the de Rham realisation. The proof of the Main Conjecture in higher rank uses the Euler system of Drinfeld modular units (the function‑field analogue of circular units) and the explicit reciprocity law for the Coleman map on the Lubin–Tate tower. A key ingredient is the control theorem for Selmer groups, which follows from the ordinary filtration on the (φ,Γ)‑module attached to φ. The Main Conjecture implies the 𝔭‑adic Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer formula: the order of vanishing of L𝔭(φ, s) at s = 1 equals the Λ‑rank of the Mordell–Weil group of φ over F∞, and the leading coefficient is expressed in terms of the 𝔭‑adic regulator, Tamagawa numbers, and the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group.

  • Thinking that parahoric level structures are the same as Iwahori level; parahoric corresponds to any standard parahoric subgroup, while Iwahori is the minimal one.
  • Believing the Gross–Zagier formula holds for all Drinfeld modules; it requires the CM field to be split at infinity and the Drinfeld module to have complex multiplication by the full ring of integers.
  • Overlooking that the Iwasawa Main Conjecture for Drinfeld modules is proved only in the ordinary case; the supersingular case requires a different Selmer group definition using (φ,Γ)‑modules.

Drinfeld Modular Curves and Their Compactifications via Cusp Forms

The Drinfeld modular curve X0(N) parametrises rank‑2 Drinfeld modules over a global function field F together with a cyclic N‑torsion submodule. Its construction mirrors the classical modular curve: one takes the quotient of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ω by the congruence subgroup Γ0(N) ⊂ GL2(A), where A is the ring of functions regular outside a fixed place ∞. The resulting rigid‑analytic space is not proper; its missing points correspond to cusps, which are in bijection with the double coset space Γ0(N) GL2(F) / B(F), where B is the Borel subgroup of upper‑triangular matrices. Adding these cusps yields a smooth projective curve X0(N) over F. The compactification is achieved algebraically by interpreting cusps as degenerate Drinfeld modules of rank 1 (the Carlitz module) equipped with level structure. The space of cusp forms of weight k and level N is identified with the space of global differentials on X0(N) twisted by a suitable line bundle. The Eichler–Shimura isomorphism for function fields, proved by Gekeler and Reversat, relates the cohomology of X0(N) to the space of cusp forms and their duals. The Jacobian J0(N) decomposes up to isogeny into a product of abelian varieties attached to newforms, and the Hecke algebra acts semisimply. The genus of X0(N) can be computed via the Riemann–Hurwitz formula applied to the covering Ω → X0(N), yielding explicit formulas in terms of the degree of N and the class number of A. These curves play a central role in the function‑field Langlands correspondence for GL2: the ℓ‑adic étale cohomology of X0(N) realises the automorphic representations of GL2(A) that are unramified outside N.

Hecke Operators on Drinfeld Modular Forms and the Ramanujan–Petersson Conjecture

For a Drinfeld modular form f of weight k and level N, the Hecke operator T𝔭 at a prime 𝔭 ∤ N is defined by the double coset Γ0(N) diag(1, 𝔭) Γ0(N). Its action on the Fourier expansion of f at the cusp ∞ is given by the usual recursion on coefficients: if f = Σ𝔞 c(𝔞) e𝔞, then c(𝔞𝔭) = c(𝔞) c(𝔭) − |𝔭|k−1 c(𝔞/𝔭) for 𝔭 ∤ 𝔞, where |𝔭| = qdeg 𝔭. The Hecke algebra generated by these operators is commutative and acts semisimply on the space of cusp forms. A newform is a simultaneous eigenform for all T𝔭 normalised so that c(1) = 1. The eigenvalues λ𝔭 satisfy the Ramanujan–Petersson bound |λ𝔭| ≤ 2 |𝔭|(k−1)/2. Drinfeld proved this bound for GL2 over function fields as a consequence of his proof of the Langlands correspondence for GL2: the eigenvalues are the Satake parameters of an irreducible cuspidal automorphic representation π of GL2(A), and the local Langlands correspondence identifies π𝔭 with a 2‑dimensional representation of the Weil–Deligne group of F𝔭. The bound follows from the fact that the Frobenius eigenvalues are Weil numbers of weight k−1. For higher rank GLn, Lafforgue’s global Langlands correspondence establishes the Ramanujan conjecture for all cuspidal automorphic representations. The Hecke eigenvalues also satisfy multiplicative relations reflecting the Euler product of the associated Goss L‑function L(f, s) = Π𝔭 (1 − λ𝔭 |𝔭|−s + |𝔭|k−1−2s)−1. The functional equation and analytic continuation of L(f, s) are proved via the cohomological interpretation of f as a class in H1(X0(N), ℚℓ).

Worked Example: Hecke Eigenvalue Bound
Let f be a weight‑2 newform of level 𝔭 (a prime of degree 1) over the rational function field 𝔽q(t). Suppose the Hecke eigenvalue λ𝔭 = 3. Verify whether the Ramanujan–Petersson bound holds for q = 5.
For weight 2, the bound is |λ𝔭| ≤ 2 |𝔭|(2−1)/2 = 2 √|𝔭|. Since deg 𝔭 = 1, |𝔭| = q = 5. Thus the right‑hand side is 2 √5 ≈ 4.472. The given eigenvalue is 3, and |3| = 3 ≤ 4.472, so the bound holds. If λ𝔭 were 5, the bound would be violated, contradicting the theorem of Drinfeld.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the Ramanujan–Petersson bound, recall the weight dependence: the exponent is (k−1)/2. For weight 2 the bound is 2√|𝔭|; for weight k it is 2|𝔭|(k−1)/2. Always check the normalisation of the Hecke operator (some texts use T𝔭 = |𝔭|(k−2)/2 times the double coset). The eigenvalue bound is invariant under this normalisation, but the Fourier coefficient recursion changes.

The Function Field Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer Conjecture for Drinfeld Modules

Let φ be a Drinfeld module of rank r over a global function field F with good reduction at all places. The Mordell–Weil theorem for Drinfeld modules, proved by Taguchi and Wan, states that the group φ(F) of F‑rational points is a finitely generated A‑module, where A is the ring of functions regular outside ∞. Its rank is denoted rkA φ(F). The Goss L‑function L(φ, s) is defined by an Euler product over places of good reduction, with local factors determined by the characteristic polynomial of Frobenius on the Tate module. The function‑field Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture predicts that the order of vanishing of L(φ, s) at s = 1 equals rkA φ(F), and that the leading coefficient is given by a formula involving the A‑regulator (determinant of the height pairing on a basis of the free part), the Tamagawa numbers at bad places, the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group Ш(φ/F), and the order of the torsion subgroup φ(F)tors. In the rank‑1 case (the Carlitz module), the conjecture is a theorem of Tate and later refined by Goss. For higher rank, partial results are known: Taelman proved the conjecture for Drinfeld modules of rank 2 with split multiplicative reduction at ∞ using the theory of Anderson t‑motives and the Euler system of Drinfeld modular units. The p‑adic analogue, the Main Conjecture of Iwasawa theory for Drinfeld modules, relates the characteristic ideal of the Selmer group over the cyclotomic p‑extension to the p‑adic L‑function. The proof of the full Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture remains open in general, but the function‑field setting provides a testing ground where geometric methods (shtukas, moduli spaces) give strong evidence.

Anderson's Logarithm and the Exponential Map for t‑Motives

An Anderson t‑motive M over a field K of characteristic p is a left module over the non‑commutative polynomial ring K[τ] (with τa = aqτ) that is finite free over K[t] and satisfies a nilpotence condition. The category of t‑motives is Tannakian, and its fibre functors give rise to various realisations: Betti, de Rham, and ℓ‑adic. The exponential map expM : Lie(M) → M(ℂ∞) is a K[t]‑linear surjection with kernel a discrete A‑lattice ΛM. Its inverse, the Anderson logarithm logM, is defined on a neighbourhood of 0 and extends to a homomorphism from the whole t‑motive to the Lie algebra. For the Carlitz module, logC coincides with the classical Carlitz logarithm Σi≥0 zqi / Di, where Di = (tqi − t) Di−1q. For a general t‑motive, the logarithm satisfies a functional equation logM(τ x) = Φ logM(x) where Φ is the Frobenius matrix of M. The periods of M are the values of logM on the lattice ΛM; they generate a finite‑dimensional vector space over the base field. The transcendence theory of these periods, developed by Yu and others, shows that non‑zero periods are algebraically independent over the base field under suitable hypotheses. The de Rham realisation provides a comparison isomorphism between the Betti and de Rham cohomology, and the logarithm identifies the de Rham cohomology with the Lie algebra. This formalism underlies the construction of the p‑adic L‑function in Iwasawa theory and the proof of the Gross–Koblitz formula for Goss L‑functions.

Drinfeld Modules with Complex Multiplication and Explicit Class Field Theory

A Drinfeld module φ over a global function field F has complex multiplication (CM) if its endomorphism ring EndF(φ) is larger than the base ring A. In rank 2, CM occurs precisely when EndF(φ) is an order in a quadratic imaginary extension K of F in which the place ∞ splits. The theory of CM Drinfeld modules parallels the classical theory of CM elliptic curves: the j‑invariant of a CM Drinfeld module generates the Hilbert class field of the CM order, and the torsion points generate abelian extensions of K. Hayes exploited this to give an explicit construction of the maximal abelian extension of a rational function field 𝔽q(t) using the torsion of the Carlitz module (rank 1 CM by A itself). For higher rank, the torsion of a CM Drinfeld module of rank r generates the ray class fields of the CM field K. The Galois action on torsion is described by the reciprocity map of class field theory, and the values of the Drinfeld modular function at CM points are algebraic numbers that generate class fields. The Gross–Zagier formula for Drinfeld modules relates the heights of Heegner points on the Drinfeld modular curve to derivatives of Goss L‑functions of CM Drinfeld modules. The explicit class field polynomials, whose roots are the j‑invariants of CM Drinfeld modules of a given order, can be computed using the Drinfeld modular polynomial ΦN(X, Y) and the theory of isogeny graphs. These polynomials are the function‑field analogues of the classical Hilbert class polynomials and are used in cryptographic applications to construct Drinfeld modules with prescribed endomorphism rings.

  • Mixing the Anderson logarithm with the Carlitz logarithm; the former is defined for any t‑motive, the latter is the rank‑1 special case.

The Drinfeld Modular Forms of Higher Weight and the Hecke Algebra

Drinfeld modular forms of higher weight arise as sections of line bundles on the Drinfeld modular curve X(Γ) attached to a congruence subgroup Γ ⊂ GL₂(A). For a weight k ∈ ℤ the space Mk(Γ) consists of rigid‑analytic functions f on the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ω satisfying f(γz) = (cz + d)k f(z) for all γ = (a b; c d) ∈ Γ, together with a holomorphy condition at the cusps. The Hecke operators T𝔭 for a prime 𝔭 ∤ ∞ act on Mk(Γ) by double‑coset operators, and they commute with each other. The Hecke algebra ℍ(Γ) generated by these operators is commutative and semisimple over ℂ∞. Eigenforms give rise to Galois representations ρf,ℓ : Gal(Fsep/F) → GL₂(ℚℓ) unramified outside 𝔭ℓ∞, whose Frobenius traces equal the Hecke eigenvalues. The work of Gekeler, Reversat and others shows that the Hecke algebra is isomorphic to the endomorphism algebra of the Jacobian of the Drinfeld modular curve, providing a function‑field analogue of the classical Eichler–Shimura relation. Moreover, the Ramanujan–Petersson bound |a𝔭(f)| ≤ 2 qdeg(𝔭)/2 follows from the Weil conjectures for the associated ℓ‑adic sheaf, a result proved by Lafforgue in the GL₂ case.

Fourier Expansions and the Drinfeld Discriminant

Every modular form admits a Fourier expansion at the cusp ∞ of the form f(z) = Σn≥0 an un, where u = expC(z) is the Carlitz exponential. The Drinfeld discriminant Δ(z) = uq−1 Πa∈A{0} (1 − ua)q−1 is a cusp form of weight q−1 and plays the role of the classical Δ. Its non‑vanishing on Ω implies that the graded ring of modular forms for GL₂(A) is a polynomial algebra ℂ∞[g, Δ] with g the Eisenstein series of weight q+1. This structure underlies the explicit computation of Hecke eigenvalues and the construction of Galois representations attached to eigenforms.

The Function Field Analogue of the Eichler–Shimura Isomorphism for GLn

For GL₂ over a global function field F, the Eichler–Shimura isomorphism identifies the cohomology H¹(X(Γ), ℚℓ) with the space of cusp forms of weight 2 plus its complex conjugate. In higher rank, the analogue is provided by the cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas with n legs. Lafforgue’s proof of the global Langlands correspondence for GLn constructs, for each cuspidal automorphic representation π of GLn(𝔸F), an n‑dimensional ℓ‑adic Galois representation σπ. The cohomological realisation occurs in the middle degree cohomology of the moduli space Shtn of rank‑n shtukas with prescribed level structure. The Hecke operators at unramified places act on this cohomology, and the eigenvalues match the Satake parameters of π. The isomorphism is equivariant for the action of the Hecke algebra and the Galois group, and it respects the weight filtration coming from the geometry of the compactification of Shtn. This geometric incarnation of the Langlands correspondence is the function‑field counterpart of the classical Eichler–Shimura isomorphism and of the more recent p‑adic local Langlands correspondence.

Cohomological Degree and Weight Filtration

The moduli space Shtn is a smooth Deligne–Mumford stack of dimension n−1 over F. Its ℓ‑adic cohomology carries a weight filtration whose graded pieces correspond to automorphic representations of different cuspidal support. The middle cohomology Hn−1(Shtn, ℚℓ) decomposes as a direct sum over cuspidal automorphic representations π of multiplicity one spaces σπ ⊗ π∞. The Frobenius eigenvalues on σπ are the Hecke eigenvalues of π, and the local Langlands correspondence at each place identifies the restriction of σπ to the decomposition group with the local component πv. This cohomological description also yields the functional equation and the rationality of the L‑functions attached to π.

Worked Example: Hecke Eigenvalues from Shtuka Cohomology
Let F = 𝔽q(t) and consider the moduli space of rank‑2 shtukas with one leg at a place v of degree 1. Suppose the Hecke operator Tv acts on H¹(Sht₂, ℚℓ) with characteristic polynomial X² − av X + q. Show that the associated Galois representation σ has trace av and determinant q at Frobeniusv.
The Hecke operator Tv corresponds to the double coset of the diagonal matrix diag(πv, 1) in GL₂(Fv). By the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula, the trace of Frobeniusv on the cohomology equals the trace of Tv. The characteristic polynomial of Tv is X² − av X + q, so its trace is av and its determinant is q. The Galois representation σ is the ℓ‑adic realisation of the motive attached to the shtuka; by construction the Frobenius eigenvalues on σ are exactly the eigenvalues of Tv. Hence Tr(Frobv | σ) = av and det(Frobv | σ) = q, as required.

The p‑adic Uniformization of Drinfeld Modular Varieties via Perfectoid Spaces

Recent work of Scholze and Weinstein extends the classical p‑adic uniformization of Drinfeld modular curves to higher‑dimensional Drinfeld modular varieties using perfectoid geometry. Let F be a global function field with place ∞, and let K be a finite extension of F∞. The Drinfeld modular variety XΓ parametrising Drinfeld modules of rank r with level‑Γ structure admits a perfectoid cover XΓ,∞ at infinite level. This cover is a perfectoid space over the completed algebraic closure C∞ and carries a continuous action of GLr(F∞) × Γ. The uniformization theorem identifies XΓ,∞ with the product of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ωr (a perfectoid space) and a profinite set of level structures. The Hodge–Tate period map πHT : XΓ,∞ → Flr (the flag variety) is GLr(F∞)‑equivariant and realises the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GLr over F∞. In particular, the étale cohomology of XΓ with ℚp‑coefficients decomposes as a direct sum of locally analytic representations of GLr(F∞) tensored with Galois representations, matching the predictions of the p‑adic Langlands program.

Perfectoid Cover and the Hodge–Tate Period Map

The perfectoid cover is constructed as the inverse limit of the tower of Drinfeld modular varieties with increasing level at ∞. The transition maps are finite étale, and the limit is a perfectoid space because the tilt of the generic fibre is the Drinfeld upper half‑plane in characteristic p. The Hodge–Tate period map sends a point corresponding to a Drinfeld module with trivialised Tate module to the Hodge filtration on its de Rham cohomology, viewed as a point in the flag variety. This map is surjective, open, and its fibres are the orbits of the unipotent radical of a Borel subgroup. The geometry of the fibres encodes the p‑adic Langlands correspondence: the cohomology of the fibres carries the locally analytic principal series representations, while the base carries the Galois side.

The Geometric Langlands Correspondence for Function Fields via Shtukas with Parabolic Structures

The geometric Langlands program over function fields replaces Galois representations by D‑modules on the moduli stack BunG of G‑bundles on a smooth projective curve X over 𝔽q. For G = GLn, the correspondence is realised by the Hecke eigensheaf property of certain perverse sheaves on Bunn constructed from the moduli of shtukas with parabolic level structure at a finite set of points. A parabolic shtuka is a shtuka together with a full flag in the fibre at each parabolic point, and the moduli stack Shtnpar maps to Bunn via the “forget the legs” morphism. The Hecke operators act by modifying the shtuka at a point, and the resulting complex of sheaves on Bunn satisfies the Hecke eigenproperty with eigenvalues given by the geometric Frobenius on the ℓ‑adic local systems on X. The work of Lafforgue, Varshavsky, and others proves that the category of Hecke eigensheaves is equivalent to the category of ℓ‑adic local systems on X, establishing the geometric Langlands correspondence for GLn over function fields. The parabolic structure is essential for matching the ramification data of the local system with the monodromy of the Hecke eigensheaf.

Hecke Eigensheaves and the Spectral Decomposition

For each ℓ‑adic local system E on X, the corresponding Hecke eigensheaf AutE on Bunn is obtained as the pushforward of the constant sheaf on Shtnpar along the projection to Bunn, followed by a suitable perverse truncation. The Hecke functor Hx at a point x ∈ X acts on AutE by tensoring with the fibre Ex. The spectral decomposition of the ℓ‑adic cohomology of Bunn then mirrors the decomposition of the space of automorphic forms into cuspidal and Eisenstein parts. The Eisenstein part corresponds to local systems that are induced from proper Levi subgroups, while the cuspidal part corresponds to irreducible local systems. This geometric picture provides a categorification of the classical Langlands correspondence and underlies the proof of the global Langlands conjecture for GLn over function fields.

The Theory of Drinfeld Modules over Finite Fields and the Zeta Functions

When a Drinfeld module φ is defined over a finite field 𝔽q, its Frobenius endomorphism π satisfies a characteristic polynomial Pφ(T) = Tr + a1 Tr−1 + … + ar with coefficients in A. The roots of Pφ are the Frobenius eigenvalues, and their absolute values are q1/2 by the Riemann hypothesis for Drinfeld modules proved by Drinfeld and later by Taguchi. The zeta function of φ over 𝔽q is defined as Z(φ/𝔽q, T) = exp( Σm≥1 #φ[𝔪m] Tm/m ), where 𝔪 is the maximal ideal of A. This zeta function is a rational function whose numerator is the characteristic polynomial of Frobenius acting on the Tate module. The functional equation relates Z(φ/𝔽q, T) to Z(φ/𝔽q, 1/(qT)). The special values at T = q−s give the Goss L‑function L(φ, s), which satisfies an analytic continuation and a functional equation analogous to the classical Hasse–Weil L‑function. The Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture for Drinfeld modules predicts that the order of vanishing of L(φ, s) at s = 1 equals the rank of the Mordell–Weil group φ(F), and the leading coefficient is expressed in terms of the regulator, the Tamagawa numbers, and the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group.

Explicit Formulas and the Class Number Formula

For the Carlitz module C (rank 1), the zeta function is Z(C/𝔽q, T) = 1/(1 − T)(1 − qT), and the Goss L‑function is the Carlitz zeta function ζC(s) = Σa∈A+ a−s. The class number formula for the Carlitz module relates the residue at s = 1 to the order of the class group of A. For higher rank, the leading term of L(φ, s) at s = 1 involves the determinant of the height pairing on φ(F) and the product of local Tamagawa factors. These formulas have been verified in many cases by Gekeler, Taelman, and others, providing strong evidence for the Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture in the function‑field setting.

The Drinfeld Upper Half‑Plane and Bruhat–Tits Buildings

The Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ωr over a local field F = 𝔽q((π)) is a rigid‑analytic space of dimension r−1 on which GLr(F) acts analytically. Drinfeld constructed Ωr as the complement of all F‑rational hyperplanes in the rigid projective space ℙr−1. The quotient of Ωr by a discrete torsion‑free subgroup Γ ⊂ GLr(F) yields a Drinfeld modular variety, the function‑field analogue of a Shimura variety. A fundamental insight of Drinfeld and later Mustafin is that the reduction of Ωr at the maximal ideal of the valuation ring is governed by the Bruhat–Tits building ℬr of PGLr(F). The building is a simplicial complex of dimension r−1 whose vertices correspond to homothety classes of lattices in Fr. The action of GLr(F) on Ωr extends to an action on ℬr, and the quotient graph Γ\ℬr describes the dual graph of the special fibre of a suitable formal model of Γ\Ωr.

This combinatorial model is crucial for several reasons. First, it provides a concrete description of the bad reduction of Drinfeld modular varieties, allowing the computation of their cohomology via the weight spectral sequence of Rapoport–Zink. Second, the harmonic analysis on ℬr (spherical functions, Hecke operators) translates directly into the theory of automorphic forms on GLr(F). Third, the building appears as the boundary of the perfectoid cover of Ωr constructed by Scholze and Weinstein; the Hodge–Tate period map identifies the boundary of the perfectoid space with the building, thereby linking p‑adic Hodge theory in equal characteristic to the geometry of ℬr. Finally, the local Langlands correspondence for GLr(F) is realised in the ℓ‑adic cohomology of Ωr, and the building provides the combinatorial skeleton needed to match supercuspidal representations with their Langlands parameters via the Harris–Taylor method adapted to function fields by Lafforgue and others.

In the rank‑two case the building is a regular tree of valence q+1. The Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ω2 is a rigid‑analytic curve whose quotient by Γ = GL2(A) (with A = 𝔽q[T]) is the Drinfeld modular curve. The reduction graph of this curve is precisely the quotient tree Γ\ℬ2, a finite graph whose edges correspond to supersingular Drinfeld modules. This graph is the function‑field analogue of the supersingular isogeny graph for elliptic curves, and its spectral properties are directly related to the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture proved by Drinfeld.

Harmonic Cochains and Weight‑Zero Drinfeld Modular Forms

For GL2 over a function field, the Bruhat–Tits building is a tree. A harmonic cochain is a function f on the set of oriented edges of the tree such that for every vertex v the sum of f(e) over all edges e emanating from v is zero. The space of Γ‑invariant harmonic cochains, where Γ ⊂ GL2(F) is a discrete subgroup, is naturally isomorphic to the space of cuspidal Drinfeld modular forms of weight 0 and level Γ. This isomorphism is explicit: given a harmonic cochain f, one constructs a rigid‑analytic function on Ω2 by integrating f along paths in the tree, and the harmonicity condition guarantees that the resulting function is invariant under Γ and satisfies the weight‑zero transformation rule.

The Hecke operators Tπ (for a prime π of A) act on harmonic cochains by averaging over the π+1 neighbours in the tree. Under the isomorphism, this action coincides with the classical Hecke action on Drinfeld modular forms. The eigenvalues of Tπ on harmonic cochains are therefore the Hecke eigenvalues of the corresponding automorphic forms. Drinfeld used this correspondence to prove the Ramanujan–Petersson bound |λπ| ≤ 2qdeg(π)/2 by showing that the Hecke operators on the tree are self‑adjoint with respect to the natural inner product on harmonic cochains, and their spectrum is contained in the interval [−2√q, 2√q].

The theory extends to higher rank via harmonic cochains on the building ℬr, but the combinatorics become more involved. For r>2, a harmonic cochain is a function on the set of chambers (maximal simplices) satisfying a harmonicity condition with respect to the adjacency relations of the building. The space of such cochains is expected to correspond to certain automorphic forms on GLr, but a complete dictionary is still under development. Nevertheless, the rank‑two case already provides a powerful computational tool: the dimension of the space of harmonic cochains equals the genus of the Drinfeld modular curve, and explicit bases can be obtained by solving linear equations on the finite quotient graph Γ\ℬ2. This has been used by Gekeler, Reversat, and others to compute Hecke eigenvalues and to verify the Eichler–Shimura isomorphism for GL2 in a completely explicit manner.

Worked Example: Hecke Action on Harmonic Cochains
Let Γ = GL2(𝔽q[T]) act on the Bruhat–Tits tree ℬ2 for F = 𝔽q((1/T)). Let f be a Γ‑invariant harmonic cochain. Describe the action of the Hecke operator TT (corresponding to the prime T) on f and explain why the eigenvalues satisfy the Ramanujan–Petersson bound.
The operator TT sends f to the function TTf defined by (TTf)(e) = Σe' f(e'), where the sum runs over the q+1 edges e' that share the target vertex of e and are distinct from the inverse of e. Because f is harmonic, the sum of f over all edges emanating from a vertex is zero; this implies that TT is self‑adjoint with respect to the inner product ⟨f,g⟩ = Σe f(e)g(ē) on the finite quotient graph Γ\ℬ2. The adjacency matrix of the (q+1)‑regular tree has spectrum contained in [−2√q, 2√q]; since TT is essentially this adjacency matrix restricted to the Γ‑invariant subspace, its eigenvalues λ satisfy |λ| ≤ 2√q. Translating back to Drinfeld modular forms, the Hecke eigenvalue at T is λ, and the bound |λ| ≤ 2qdeg(T)/2 = 2√q is exactly the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for GL2 over function fields.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the link between harmonic cochains and Drinfeld modular forms, state the isomorphism explicitly, mention the Hecke operator as averaging over neighbours in the tree, and cite the self‑adjointness argument that yields the Ramanujan–Petersson bound. Do not confuse weight‑zero forms with higher‑weight forms, which require iterated integrals or the Anderson logarithm.

The Jacquet–Langlands Correspondence for Function Fields

The Jacquet–Langlands correspondence in the function‑field setting relates automorphic representations of GL2 over a global function field K to automorphic representations of the multiplicative group D× of a quaternion division algebra D over K. Drinfeld’s construction of the upper half‑plane Ω2 provides a uniformisation of both the Drinfeld modular curve (for GL2) and the Shimura curve (for D×) by the same rigid‑analytic space. Concretely, if D is ramified exactly at a finite set of places S of K, then the quotient of Ω2 by an arithmetic subgroup ΓD ⊂ D× embedded in GL2(Kv) for a split place v yields a projective curve whose Jacobian carries the automorphic representations of D×. The correspondence matches Hecke eigenvalues at all places outside S, and at the ramified places the local component on the GL2 side is a special representation while on the D× side it is one‑dimensional.

The proof uses the trace formula for the two groups. The geometric side of the trace formula for GL2 involves orbital integrals on the Lie algebra, while for D× the orbital integrals are simpler because D× is anisotropic modulo centre. The matching of orbital integrals is achieved by the fundamental lemma for the unit elements of the Hecke algebras, which in the function‑field case was proved by Laumon and Ngô using geometric methods. The correspondence is compatible with the local Langlands correspondence: the local component at a ramified place corresponds to the Steinberg representation on the GL2 side and the trivial representation on the D× side. This compatibility is essential for the global Langlands correspondence for GL2 over function fields, as it allows one to transfer automorphic forms from the anisotropic group (where they are easier to construct via the cohomology of Shimura curves) to GL2.

An important application is the construction of Galois representations attached to cuspidal automorphic forms on GL2. By the work of Drinfeld, the ℓ‑adic cohomology of the Drinfeld modular curve carries an action of GL2(𝔸K,f) × Gal(Ksep/K). The Jacquet–Langlands transfer allows one to isolate the cuspidal part of this cohomology and to prove that the associated Galois representations are irreducible and satisfy the expected local‑global compatibility. This was a key step in Drinfeld’s proof of the global Langlands correspondence for GL2 over function fields.

Multiple Zeta Values and Anderson–Thakur Theory for Drinfeld Modules

In the classical setting, multiple zeta values are iterated integrals of the differential forms dz/z and dz/(1−z) on ℙ1 {0,1,∞}. Anderson and Thakur developed a function‑field analogue using the Carlitz module C, the rank‑one Drinfeld module over A = 𝔽q[T]. The Carlitz exponential expC(z) = Σi≥0 zqi/Di (with Di the Carlitz factorial) provides an analogue of the classical exponential. The Anderson–Thakur multiple star values are defined as iterated sums Σa1 > … > ar > 0 1/(a1 … ar) where the sum runs over monic polynomials in A, and the product uses the Carlitz module multiplication. These values are special values of the Goss L‑function L(C, s) at positive integers, and they satisfy shuffle and stuffle relations mirroring the classical case.

The theory is deeply connected to the Tannakian category of t‑motives. Anderson showed that the Carlitz module and its tensor powers give rise to a neutral Tannakian category whose motivic Galois group is a pro‑algebraic group over 𝔽q(T). The multiple zeta values appear as periods of certain t‑motives, and the algebraic relations among them are governed by the motivic Galois group. Chang and Mishiba proved that the dimension of the space of multiple zeta values of a given weight equals the dimension predicted by the motivic Galois group, providing strong evidence for the function‑field analogue of the Grothendieck period conjecture. Moreover, the Anderson logarithm logC(z) = Σi≥0 zqi/ℓi (with ℓi the Carlitz logarithm coefficients) gives an explicit isomorphism between the Lie algebra of the Carlitz module and the additive group, and its values at algebraic points generate the multiple zeta values.

Transcendence results are a major achievement of this theory. Yu’s sub‑t‑module theorem implies that any non‑zero multiple zeta value is transcendental over K = 𝔽q(T). More precisely, the algebraic independence of certain families of multiple zeta values has been established by Chang, Papanikolas, and others using the theory of Frobenius difference equations for t‑motives. These results are the function‑field counterparts of the classical theorems of Nesterenko and others on the algebraic independence of π and values of the Riemann zeta function. The connection to Drinfeld modular forms arises because the Anderson logarithm of the Drinfeld discriminant Δ(z) yields a modular form of weight 1, and its special values are related to the multiple zeta values via the explicit formulas of Gekeler and Taelman.

p‑adic L‑functions and the Main Conjecture for Higher Rank Drinfeld Modules

For the Carlitz module (rank 1), Taelman constructed a p‑adic L‑function Lp(C, s) as a measure on the group of units of the completion of A at a prime p, interpolating the special values L(C, n) for positive integers n. The Iwasawa main conjecture, proved by Taelman, states that the characteristic ideal of the Selmer group of the Carlitz module over the cyclotomic ℤp-extension equals the ideal generated by Lp(C, s). For higher rank Drinfeld modules φ of rank r > 1, the construction of p‑adic L‑functions is more delicate because the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ωr has dimension r−1 > 0. Anglès, Taelman, and others have defined p‑adic L‑functions by integrating certain differential forms on Ωr against measures coming from the action of the Hecke algebra. These p‑adic L‑functions interpolate the critical values of the Goss L‑function L(φ, s) at integers s = 1, …, r.

The main conjecture for higher rank predicts that the characteristic ideal of the Selmer group of φ over the cyclotomic tower is generated by the p‑adic L‑function. Partial results are known: for rank 2 Drinfeld modules with split multiplicative reduction at p, Bandini and Longhi proved one divisibility using an Euler system constructed from the Drinfeld modular units on the Drinfeld modular curve. The perfectoid uniformization of the Drinfeld modular curve at infinite level (Scholze–Weinstein) provides a new tool to construct p‑adic L‑functions for arbitrary rank by working on the perfectoid cover, where the Hodge–Tate period map gives a direct link to p‑adic Hodge theory. The function‑field Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture predicts that the order of vanishing of L(φ, s) at s = 1 equals the rank of φ(K), and the leading coefficient is expressed in terms of the p‑adic regulator, Tamagawa numbers, and the Tate–Shafarevich group. The main conjecture is the p‑adic analogue of this statement, and its proof would simultaneously establish the BSD conjecture for Drinfeld modules in many cases.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Bruhat–Tits building of GLr with the Drinfeld upper half‑plane; the building is a combinatorial skeleton, while Ωr is a rigid‑analytic space of dimension r−1.
  • Assuming harmonic cochains exist for all weights; they only give weight‑zero forms, higher weights require iterated integrals or the Anderson logarithm.
  • Thinking the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence matches all Hecke eigenvalues including at ramified places; at ramified places the local representations differ (special vs. trivial).
  • Believing multiple zeta values in function fields are defined by the same iterated integrals as in characteristic 0; they use the Carlitz exponential and sums over monic polynomials.
  • Expecting the Iwasawa main conjecture for higher rank to be proved in full generality; it is known only for rank 1 and certain rank 2 cases with split multiplicative reduction.
  • Mixing the p‑adic L‑function of a Drinfeld module with the classical p‑adic L‑function of an elliptic curve; the former lives on the Drinfeld upper half‑plane and uses the Carlitz module, the latter on the modular curve and uses the Tate curve.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Shimura Variety and Its Compactifications

Drinfeld modular varieties play the role of Shimura varieties in the function field setting. For a global function field F with ring of integers A and a fixed place ∞, the moduli space of rank‑r Drinfeld A-modules with level‑N structure is a smooth quasi‑projective variety over the completion F∞. Its analytification is isomorphic to the quotient of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ωr by the arithmetic group ΓN ⊂ GLr(A). The Baily–Borel compactification of this quotient adds boundary strata corresponding to degenerate Drinfeld modules, i.e. Drinfeld modules of lower rank equipped with a filtration. Laumon and Rapoport constructed a functorial compactification using the theory of shtukas, which identifies the boundary with moduli of shtukas with a single leg at ∞. The cohomology of these compactifications carries an action of the Hecke algebra away from ∞ and of the Galois group Gal(Fsep/F), realizing the local Langlands correspondence for GLr in the cohomology of the boundary. The Hodge–Tate period map, constructed via the perfectoid uniformization of the infinite‑level cover, identifies the generic fibre of the compactification with a flag variety, providing a function‑field analogue of the Hodge–Tate decomposition for Shimura varieties.

The Function Field Langlands Correspondence for GSp₄ via Drinfeld Modular Forms

The global Langlands correspondence for the group GSp₄ over a function field F has been established by Laumon, Rapoport and Stuhler using the cohomology of the moduli space of rank‑2 Drinfeld modules with a symplectic similitude structure. A Drinfeld module of rank 2 equipped with a principal polarization gives rise to a point on a Siegel‑type Drinfeld modular variety. The space of Drinfeld modular forms of weight (k₁,k₂) on this variety carries an action of the Hecke operators at unramified places. The eigenvalues of these Hecke operators on cuspidal eigenforms match the Satake parameters of an irreducible cuspidal automorphic representation of GSp₄(𝔸F). Via the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula applied to the ℓ‑adic cohomology of the compactified moduli space, one attaches to each eigenform a compatible system of Galois representations ρ : Gal(Fsep/F) → GSp₄(ℚℓ). The local‑global compatibility at places of good reduction follows from the comparison between the Hecke action on the moduli space and the action of the Frobenius on the Tate module of the universal Drinfeld module. At places of bad reduction, the monodromy filtration on the nearby cycles matches the local Langlands parameter predicted by the Langlands–Shelstad transfer. This construction provides the first complete case of the Langlands correspondence for a non‑general‑linear reductive group over a function field.

Worked Example: Hecke Eigenvalues on a Rank‑2 Drinfeld Modular Form
Let φ be a rank‑2 Drinfeld A-module over F = 𝔽q(T) with A = 𝔽q[T]. Suppose the Drinfeld modular form f of weight (2,0) is a Hecke eigenform for all primes 𝔭 ≠ (T). The Hecke operator T𝔭 acts on f with eigenvalue a𝔭. Show that the local L‑factor at 𝔭 attached to the automorphic representation generated by f is L𝔭(s,π) = (1 − a𝔭 q−s + q1−2s)−1.
The Hecke polynomial for GL₂ at an unramified prime 𝔭 is X² − a𝔭 X + q𝔭, where q𝔭 = |A/𝔭|. For GSp₄ the standard L‑factor is the reciprocal of the characteristic polynomial of Frobenius on the 4‑dimensional symplectic representation. Because the Drinfeld module has rank 2, its Tate module is a 2‑dimensional Galois representation; the associated automorphic representation of GSp₄ is the functorial lift from GL₂ via the embedding GL₂ → GSp₄ given by the symmetric square. The symmetric‑square L‑factor of a GL₂ representation with Hecke eigenvalue a𝔭 is (1 − a𝔭 q−s + q1−2s)−1. Hence the required L‑factor matches the stated expression.
Exam Strategy: Recognising Functorial Lifts
When a question asks for the L‑factor of a GSp₄ automorphic form coming from a rank‑2 Drinfeld module, identify the underlying GL₂ representation first. The symmetric‑square lift is the standard route; write down the GL₂ Hecke polynomial and then apply the symmetric‑square formula. This avoids memorising separate GSp₄ Hecke polynomials.

The p‑adic Hodge Theory of Drinfeld Modules and the Fontaine–Mazur Conjecture in Equal Characteristic

In equal characteristic p, the role of p‑adic Hodge theory is played by the theory of (φ,Γ)‑modules over the Robba ring of the local field F∞. For a Drinfeld A-module φ of rank r over a finite extension L/F∞, the Tate module Tℓ(φ) (ℓ ≠ p) is a Galois representation that becomes crystalline after a finite base change. Hartl and Juschka constructed a functor from the category of Drinfeld modules over L to the category of étale (φ,Γ)‑modules, which is fully faithful and compatible with tensor products. The Hodge–Tate weights of Tℓ(φ) are {0,1,…,r−1}, mirroring the Hodge–Tate weights of an abelian variety of dimension r. The Fontaine–Mazur conjecture in this setting predicts that any irreducible ℓ‑adic Galois representation of Gal(Fsep/F) which is unramified outside a finite set and de Rham at all places above ∞ arises from the cohomology of a Drinfeld modular variety. Genestier and Lafforgue proved a substantial part of this conjecture for GLr by showing that the ℓ‑adic cohomology of the compactified Drinfeld modular variety realizes all such representations. The proof uses the nearby cycles spectral sequence on the moduli space of shtukas and the compatibility of the local Langlands correspondence with the Hodge–Tate period map.

The Role of Shtukas in the Geometric Langlands Program for Reductive Groups Beyond GLₙ

Shtukas with multiple legs provide a geometric incarnation of the Langlands correspondence for an arbitrary split reductive group G over a function field. A G-shtuka over a curve X is a G-bundle on X × S together with an isomorphism outside a finite set of points (the legs) satisfying a boundedness condition. The moduli stack ShtG of such shtukas carries Hecke correspondences at each leg, and its ℓ‑adic cohomology decomposes as a direct sum over automorphic representations of G(𝔸F) of the tensor product of the automorphic representation with the associated Galois representation. For G = GLn this recovers the Drinfeld–Lafforgue correspondence. For general G, Varshavsky and later Zhu proved that the Hecke eigensheaves on the moduli stack of G-bundles are constructed from the cohomology of ShtG via the geometric Satake equivalence. The key technical input is the uniformization of ShtG by the affine Grassmannian of the Langlands dual group G˅, which identifies the stalks of the Hecke eigensheaf with the cohomology of the corresponding affine Schubert variety. This geometric construction yields the local Langlands correspondence for G in families and provides a categorification of the trace formula.

The Drinfeld Module Approach to Explicit Class Field Theory for Imaginary Quadratic Function Fields

When the base field F is an imaginary quadratic extension of 𝔽q(T) and the ring A is the integral closure of 𝔽q[T] in F, Drinfeld modules of rank 1 (i.e. Carlitz‑type modules) generate the maximal abelian extension of F unramified outside ∞. Hayes showed that the torsion points of a sign‑normalized rank‑1 Drinfeld module produce explicit class fields, and the class field polynomial can be written in terms of the Drinfeld modular function j(z). For higher rank, the theory of complex multiplication (CM) Drinfeld modules provides explicit generators of ray class fields. A CM Drinfeld module of rank r is one whose endomorphism ring is an order in a CM field of degree 2r over F. The values of the Drinfeld modular invariant at CM points are algebraic and generate abelian extensions of the CM field. The Shimura reciprocity law for Drinfeld modules, proved by Gekeler and Reversat, describes the Galois action on these singular moduli in terms of the idele class group of the CM field. This yields an effective algorithm for computing class polynomials, which are the function‑field analogues of the Hilbert class polynomials for elliptic curves with CM.

The Function Field Sato–Tate Conjecture for Drinfeld Modules

The Sato–Tate conjecture predicts the equidistribution of Frobenius conjugacy classes attached to a Drinfeld module of rank two over a global function field. For a Drinfeld module φ of rank two over a finite field 𝔽_q(T) with generic characteristic, the characteristic polynomial of Frobenius at a place v of good reduction is X² − a_v X + q_v, where a_v ∈ ℤ and q_v is the size of the residue field. The normalized trace a_v / √q_v lies in the interval [−2, 2]. The conjecture asserts that as v varies, the angles θ_v defined by a_v = 2√q_v cos θ_v become equidistributed with respect to the Sato–Tate measure (2/π) sin²θ dθ on [0, π]. Taguchi proved the conjecture for Drinfeld modules without complex multiplication in 1995 by relating the Frobenius eigenvalues to the eigenvalues of Hecke operators on the space of harmonic cochains on the Drinfeld upper half‑plane. Pink later gave a more geometric proof using the ℓ‑adic cohomology of the Drinfeld modular curve and the Weil conjectures for the associated Galois representations. For Drinfeld modules with complex multiplication, the distribution is discrete and supported on a finite set of angles determined by the CM field. The proof uses the fact that the Galois representation attached to φ is abelian in the CM case, so the Frobenius eigenvalues are roots of unity. The Sato–Tate conjecture for higher rank Drinfeld modules remains open; the expected limiting measure is the Haar measure on the maximal compact subgroup of the Langlands dual group, but the necessary automorphy and potential automorphy results are not yet available in full generality.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Modularity Theorem for Elliptic Curves over Function Fields

Drinfeld’s global Langlands correspondence for GL₂ over a global function field F implies that every elliptic curve E over F is modular in the sense that its L‑function L(E, s) coincides with the L‑function of a Drinfeld modular form of weight two and level determined by the conductor of E. Drinfeld proved this in 1974 by constructing a surjective morphism from the Drinfeld modular curve X₀(𝔫) to the Jacobian of the modular curve, and then showing that the Galois representation on the Tate module of E arises from the cohomology of X₀(𝔫). The modular parametrization sends a point on the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ω² to the corresponding Drinfeld module with level structure, and the pullback of the invariant differential on E yields a Drinfeld modular form f_E of weight two. The Fourier expansion of f_E at the cusp ∞ has coefficients a_v that match the traces of Frobenius on the Tate module of E at places v of good reduction. Lafforgue’s proof of the global Langlands correspondence for GL_n extended this modularity to higher dimensional abelian varieties with GL_n‑type Galois representations. The function field modularity theorem has been used to prove the function field analogue of the Birch and Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture for elliptic curves of analytic rank zero, and to construct explicit Heegner points on elliptic curves over imaginary quadratic function fields via the theory of complex multiplication Drinfeld modules.

Examiner's trap
A student claims that the modularity theorem for elliptic curves over function fields follows directly from the classical modularity theorem over ℚ by base change. Explain why this reasoning is incorrect and identify the key input that makes the function field case accessible.
The classical modularity theorem over ℚ relies on the Taylor–Wiles method and the deformation theory of Galois representations in characteristic zero, which does not transfer to positive characteristic because the relevant deformation rings are not formally smooth. In the function field setting, Drinfeld used the geometry of the Drinfeld modular curve and the fact that the ℓ‑adic cohomology of this curve carries an action of the Hecke algebra that matches the Galois side via the Eichler–Shimura relation. The crucial input is the existence of a moduli space of Drinfeld modules with level structure whose cohomology realizes the required Galois representations, a geometric object that has no direct analogue over number fields. Therefore the function field modularity theorem is proved by geometric methods specific to positive characteristic, not by base change from ℚ.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for the proof strategy of the function field modularity theorem, mention the Drinfeld modular curve, the Eichler–Shimura relation for Drinfeld modules, and the comparison of Galois representations on Tate modules with Hecke eigenvalues on weight‑two Drinfeld modular forms. Do not confuse this with the Taylor–Wiles method used over ℚ.

Half‑Integral Weight Drinfeld Modular Forms and the Shimura Correspondence

The theory of Drinfeld modular forms of half‑integral weight was developed by Gekeler, Reversat, and later by Basson and Breuer. A Drinfeld modular form of weight k/2 (k odd) on a congruence subgroup Γ ⊂ GL₂(A) is a rigid‑analytic function on the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ω² satisfying a transformation law with the automorphy factor (c z + d)k/2, where the square root is defined using a fixed choice of a theta characteristic. The space of such forms is finite‑dimensional and carries an action of Hecke operators T_v for places v not dividing the level. The Shimura correspondence lifts a half‑integral weight form f to an integral weight form Sh(f) of weight k−1 on a possibly larger level, preserving Hecke eigenvalues at almost all places. The construction uses the theta series attached to a rank‑one Drinfeld module (the Carlitz module) and the Rankin–Selberg method adapted to the function field setting. Gekeler and Reversat proved that the Shimura map is injective and its image is characterized by the vanishing of certain Fourier coefficients at places where the quadratic character attached to the theta characteristic is non‑trivial. This correspondence has been used to relate special values of L‑functions of Drinfeld modular forms of half‑integral weight to periods of the associated integral weight forms, providing a function field analogue of Waldspurger’s formula. Recent work by Wei and Zhang extends the Shimura correspondence to higher rank Drinfeld modular forms using the theory of metaplectic covers of GL_n over function fields.

The Local Langlands Correspondence for Inner Forms of GLₙ over Function Fields

The local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over a non‑archimedean local field F of positive characteristic was established by Lafforgue using the geometry of the moduli space of shtukas with one leg. For inner forms of GL_n, such as the multiplicative group of a central division algebra D of dimension n² over F, the correspondence matches irreducible smooth representations of D^× with n‑dimensional Frobenius‑semisimple representations of the Weil group W_F. The proof proceeds by realizing the representation of D^× in the ℓ‑adic cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower for D, which is a rigid‑analytic space uniformizing the moduli of formal D‑modules. The local Langlands correspondence for inner forms is compatible with the Jacquet–Langlands transfer: the character of a representation of D^× matches the character of its transfer to GL_n(F) up to a sign determined by the reduced norm. Genestier and Lafforgue extended this to the case of parahoric level structures, showing that the correspondence respects the filtration by depth and the action of the Hecke algebra. The function field case is simpler than the p‑adic case because the Lubin–Tate tower is a Drinfeld modular variety and its cohomology can be computed using the theory of shtukas with multiple legs. The correspondence has been used to prove the local Langlands correspondence for all inner forms of GL_n and to establish the compatibility with the global Langlands correspondence via the trace formula.

The Categorical Geometric Langlands Correspondence for Function Fields via Shtukas

The categorical geometric Langlands program seeks to replace the numerical correspondence between automorphic representations and Galois representations by an equivalence of categories. For a smooth projective curve X over a finite field 𝔽_q and a reductive group G, the category of D‑modules on the moduli stack Bun_G of G‑bundles on X is conjectured to be equivalent to the category of quasi‑coherent sheaves on the stack LocSysG^∨ of G^∨‑local systems on X. In the function field setting, this equivalence is constructed using the moduli stack Sht_G of G‑shtukas with multiple legs. The Hecke correspondences on Sht_G induce functors on the category of sheaves on Bun_G, and the geometric Satake equivalence identifies the spherical Hecke category with the representation category of the Langlands dual group G^∨. Varshavsky proved that the cohomology of Sht_G yields Hecke eigensheaves on Bun_G, and Zhu extended this to a full categorical equivalence for GL_n using the theory of factorization algebras on the Ran space of X. The key technical input is the uniformization of Sht_G by the affine Grassmannian GrG^∨, which identifies the stalks of the Hecke eigensheaf with the cohomology of affine Schubert varieties. This construction provides a categorification of the Arthur–Selberg trace formula and yields the local Langlands correspondence for G in families. Recent work by Gaitsgory and Lurie on the categorical geometric Langlands over ℂ has inspired parallel developments in the function field case, where the use of ℓ‑adic sheaves and the Weil conjectures makes the theory unconditional.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the Sato–Tate measure for Drinfeld modules with the classical Sato–Tate measure for elliptic curves over ℚ; the function field measure is (2/π) sin²θ dθ, while the classical measure is (2/π) sin²θ dθ for non‑CM curves but the underlying group is SU(2) in both cases, so the measures coincide, but the proof uses harmonic cochains on the Drinfeld upper half‑plane, not modular curves over ℂ.
  • Assuming the function field modularity theorem follows from the classical modularity theorem by base change; the proofs are fundamentally different because the deformation theory in characteristic p is not formally smooth.
  • Believing that half‑integral weight Drinfeld modular forms exist for all levels; they require a choice of theta characteristic and only exist on congruence subgroups where the quadratic character is trivial.
  • Thinking the local Langlands correspondence for inner forms of GL_n is identical to that for GL_n; the correspondence for D^× involves a sign factor in the character identity and uses the Lubin–Tate tower for D, not the Drinfeld upper half‑plane.
  • Expecting the categorical geometric Langlands equivalence to hold for all reductive groups without additional hypotheses; the current unconditional results are for GL_n and certain inner forms, while the general case requires the fundamental lemma and the geometric Satake equivalence for the dual group.
  • Mixing the Shimura correspondence for Drinfeld modular forms with the classical Shimura correspondence; the function field version uses the Carlitz module as the theta kernel and the Rankin–Selberg method on the Drinfeld upper half‑plane.
  • Overlooking the role of the Hodge–Tate period map in identifying the generic fibre of the perfectoid cover of the Drinfeld modular curve with a flag variety; this identification is essential for the p‑adic Hodge theory of Drinfeld modules and for the construction of the categorical equivalence.

The Function Field Analogue of the Bloch–Kato Conjecture for Drinfeld Modules

The Bloch–Kato conjecture in classical number theory relates the order of vanishing of the complex L‑function of a motive at an integer point to the dimension of a Selmer group defined via p‑adic Hodge theory. In the function field setting, Goss L‑functions of Drinfeld modules and their associated t‑motives play the role of complex L‑functions, while the ℓ‑adic Tate modules and the de Rham cohomology of Anderson t‑motives provide the p‑adic Hodge‑theoretic input. For a Drinfeld module φ of rank r over a global function field K with fraction field F_q(θ), the Goss L‑function L(φ, s) converges on a right half‑plane and satisfies a functional equation relating s to 1−s. The special values at positive integers s = n are known to be periods of the t‑motive M(φ) in the sense of Anderson–Thakur. The function field Bloch–Kato conjecture predicts that the order of vanishing of L(φ, s) at s = n equals the dimension of the Selmer group H1_f(K, M(φ)(n)), where M(φ)(n) denotes the n‑th Tate twist of the t‑motive and the subscript f indicates the finite part defined by local conditions at places of bad reduction and at the infinite place. These local conditions are formulated using the de Rham realisation of M(φ) and the Hodge–Tate decomposition of the associated ℓ‑adic Galois representation. For rank‑one Drinfeld modules (Carlitz module), the conjecture reduces to the class number formula for the cyclotomic function field extension, which was proved by Hayes. For higher rank, the conjecture is open in general, but significant progress has been made by Böckle, Pink, and others using the theory of shtukas. The cohomology of the moduli stack of shtukas with multiple legs provides a geometric realisation of the Selmer groups, and the trace formula for the Hecke action on this cohomology yields the required relation between L‑values and Selmer ranks. A key technical tool is the weight filtration on the cohomology of shtukas, which corresponds to the monodromy filtration on the de Rham side. The conjecture also implies a function field analogue of the Tamagawa number conjecture, relating the leading coefficient of L(φ, s) at s = n to a product of local Tamagawa factors, the regulator of the Mordell–Weil group of φ, and the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group. Recent work by Taguchi and Wan on p‑adic L‑functions for Drinfeld modules has provided a p‑adic interpolation of these special values, opening the way for a p‑adic Bloch–Kato conjecture in equal characteristic.

Drinfeld Shtukas with Level Structure and Local‑Global Compatibility for GL_n

The moduli stack Sht_G^μ of G‑shtukas with level structure at a finite set of places S is a fundamental object in the geometric Langlands program over function fields. For G = GL_n, a level structure at a place v ∉ S is given by a trivialisation of the shtuka's legs at v compatible with the Frobenius action, which corresponds to fixing a basis of the fibre of the associated vector bundle at v. The stack ShtGL_n^μ parametrises tuples (E, φ, α) where E is a vector bundle of rank n on a curve X over 𝔽_q, φ: σ^*E → E(∑x∈S μ_x x) is a Frobenius‑twisted isomorphism with prescribed modifications μ_x at the legs, and α is a level structure at places outside S. The Hecke correspondences at places v ∉ S act on ShtGL_n^μ by modifying the level structure, and their cohomology realises the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n(F_v) in families. Local‑global compatibility means that for an automorphic representation π of GL_n(𝔸_F) occurring in the cohomology of ShtGL_n^μ, the local component π_v matches the Weil–Deligne representation attached to the Galois representation ρ_π|Gal(F_v^s/F_v) under the local Langlands correspondence of Harris–Taylor and Henniart, transported to the function field setting via the geometric Satake equivalence. The proof of local‑global compatibility for GL_n over function fields was achieved by Lafforgue for n = 2 and by Varshavsky, Zhu, and others for general n using the uniformisation of ShtGL_n^μ by the affine Grassmannian GrGL_n. The key step is the identification of the stalks of the Hecke eigensheaf on BunGL_n with the cohomology of affine Schubert varieties in GrGL_n, which carries an action of the spherical Hecke algebra isomorphic to the representation ring of GL_n(ℂ). The level structure ensures that the Hecke operators at v act through the spherical Hecke algebra of GL_n(F_v), and the compatibility with the local Langlands correspondence follows from the fact that the geometric Satake equivalence intertwines the Hecke action with the tensor product of representations of the dual group. For parahoric level structures, the moduli stack is uniformised by the affine flag variety, and the local‑global compatibility involves the Iwahori–Hecke algebra and the local Langlands correspondence for representations with non‑trivial conductor. This theory has been extended to the case of multiple legs by Genestier and Lafforgue, who showed that the cohomology of ShtGL_n^μ with multiple legs realises the global Langlands correspondence for GL_n with prescribed local components at the legs, and that the local‑global compatibility holds for all places, including those with wild ramification.

Worked Example: Computing the Hecke Action on a Rank‑2 Drinfeld Shtuka
Let X = ℙ1𝔽_q with coordinate t, and consider a rank‑2 Drinfeld shtuka (E, φ) over X with legs at 0 and ∞, where E = O ⊕ O(1) and φ: σ^*E → E(0 + ∞) is given by the matrix [[t, 1], [0, t]] in the standard basis. Compute the action of the Hecke operator T_v at a place v ≠ 0, ∞ corresponding to the characteristic function of GL2(O_v) diag(π_v, 1) GL2(O_v) on the fibre of the associated Hecke eigensheaf at this shtuka.
The Hecke correspondence at v modifies the shtuka by replacing the vector bundle E at v with a subbundle E' ⊂ E such that E/E' is a skyscraper sheaf of length 1 supported at v. For GL2, there are exactly two such modifications: E' = E(−v) (the upper modification) and E' = ker(E → O_v) where the map is projection onto the second factor (the lower modification). The Hecke operator T_v acts on the fibre of the eigensheaf by summing the contributions from these two modifications, weighted by the eigenvalues of the Frobenius at v. In the geometric Satake equivalence, the spherical Hecke algebra H(GL2(F_v)//GL2(O_v)) is isomorphic to ℤ[X1±1, X2±1]S2, and the characteristic function of GL2(O_v) diag(π_v, 1) GL2(O_v) corresponds to the symmetric polynomial X1 + X2. The eigenvalue of T_v on the Hecke eigensheaf associated to an automorphic representation π is the trace of the Satake parameter diag(α_v, β_v) of π_v, i.e., α_v + β_v. For the given shtuka, the Frobenius action on the fibre at v is given by the matrix φ_v = [[t(v), 1], [0, t(v)]] where t(v) is the value of the coordinate t at v. The Satake parameters are the eigenvalues of φ_v, which are both equal to t(v) (since the matrix is upper triangular with equal diagonal entries). Thus the Hecke eigenvalue is 2 t(v). The action of T_v on the fibre is therefore multiplication by 2 t(v). This matches the local Langlands correspondence: the Weil–Deligne representation attached to the Galois representation ρ_π|Gal(F_v^s/F_v) has Frobenius eigenvalues α_v = β_v = t(v), and the local L‑factor is (1 − t(v) q_v-s)-2.
Exam Strategy: Local‑Global Compatibility Checklist
When a question asks about local‑global compatibility for Drinfeld shtukas, verify three points: (1) the moduli stack has level structure at the place in question, (2) the Hecke operator at that place acts via the spherical Hecke algebra, and (3) the eigenvalue matches the trace of the Satake parameter of the automorphic representation. Remember that for parahoric level, the Iwahori–Hecke algebra replaces the spherical one, and the local Langlands correspondence involves the depth of the representation. The geometric Satake equivalence is the bridge that translates Hecke eigenvalues into representation‑theoretic data of the dual group.

The Moduli of Drinfeld Shtukas for General Reductive Groups and the Geometric Satake in Families

While the theory of Drinfeld shtukas for GL_n is highly developed, the extension to general reductive groups G over a global function field F is essential for the full geometric Langlands program. A G‑shtuka over a curve X/𝔽_q is a pair (E, φ) where E is a G‑bundle on X and φ: σ^*E → E is an isomorphism outside a finite set of legs, with prescribed modifications at the legs given by a cocharacter μ of G. The moduli stack Sht_G^μ parametrising such objects is an algebraic stack locally of finite type over 𝔽_q. For G = GL_n, the uniformisation by the affine Grassmannian GrGL_n identifies ShtGL_n^μ with a quotient of a product of affine Grassmannians by a loop group, but for general G the uniformisation involves the affine Grassmannian Gr_G of the Langlands dual group G^∨. The geometric Satake equivalence, proved by Mirković–Vilonen for ℓ‑adic sheaves and by Zhu for the function field setting, establishes a tensor equivalence between the category of spherical perverse sheaves on Gr_G and the category of finite‑dimensional representations of G^∨. In families, this equivalence is upgraded to a functor from the category of sheaves on the moduli stack of G‑bundles Bun_G to the category of sheaves on the stack of G^∨‑local systems LocSysG^∨, constructed via the Hecke correspondences on Sht_G^μ. The key result of Zhu and later of Gaitsgory–Lurie (in the complex setting) is that the cohomology of Sht_G^μ with coefficients in a spherical sheaf on Gr_G yields a Hecke eigensheaf on Bun_G whose eigenvalue is the local system on X corresponding to the representation of G^∨. For G = GSp4, the moduli of shtukas was studied by Laumon, Rapoport, and Stuhler, who constructed the local Langlands correspondence for GSp4 using the cohomology of the associated Shimura variety (the Drinfeld modular variety for GSp4). For general G, the work of Varshavsky and of Chen–Zhu–Zhang has established the geometric Satake equivalence in families over the Ran space of X, allowing the construction of Hecke eigensheaves for any reductive G. The main technical obstacle is the singularities of the moduli stack Sht_G^μ, which are resolved by the theory of factorisation algebras on the Ran space. The categorical geometric Langlands equivalence for G is then conjectured to be an equivalence between the derived category of D‑modules on Bun_G and the derived category of quasi‑coherent sheaves on LocSysG^∨, compatible with the Hecke action and the geometric Satake equivalence. This equivalence has been proved unconditionally for GL_n by Zhu using the theory of shtukas with multiple legs, and for certain inner forms of GL_n by Genestier–Lafforgue. For other groups, the fundamental lemma of Ngô and the weighted fundamental lemma of Chaudouard–Laumon are required to stabilise the trace formula and prove the required spectral decomposition.

The Function Field p‑adic Local Langlands Correspondence for GL_n and the Colmez Functor

The p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over a p‑adic field relates continuous representations of GL_n(F) on p‑adic Banach spaces to continuous representations of the Weil–Deligne group of F on p‑adic vector spaces. In the function field setting, the role of the p‑adic field is played by a local field F_v of characteristic p, and the correspondence relates representations of GL_n(F_v) on vector spaces over a complete algebraically closed field C of characteristic p (or over a p‑adic field in the mixed characteristic analogue) to representations of the local Langlands group of F_v. The function field p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL2 was constructed by the author and others using the perfectoid uniformisation of the Drinfeld modular curve at infinite level. For GL_n, the correspondence is built using the cohomology of the Lubin–Tate tower for GL_n, which in equal characteristic is the Drinfeld modular variety of level p^∞. The Colmez functor, which in the classical setting attaches a (φ, Γ)‑module to a p‑adic representation of GL2(ℚ_p), has a function field analogue due to Hartl and others: it associates to a continuous representation of GL_n(F_v) on a C‑vector space a finite‑dimensional vector space over F_v with a Frobenius‑semilinear operator (an étale φ‑module). The key theorem states that this functor induces an equivalence between the category of admissible locally analytic representations of GL_n(F_v) on C‑vector spaces and the category of étale φ‑modules over the Robba ring of F_v. The proof uses the perfectoid cover of the Drinfeld modular variety at infinite level, whose generic fibre is a perfectoid space uniformised by the Drinfeld upper half‑plane. The Hodge–Tate period map identifies this perfectoid space with a flag variety for GL_n, and the cohomology of the flag variety with coefficients in automorphic vector bundles realises the Colmez functor. The correspondence is compatible with the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n in the sense that the φ‑module attached to a representation π corresponds to the Weil–Deligne representation attached to π under the classical local Langlands correspondence. For inner forms of GL_n, the correspondence is modified by a sign factor as in the Jacquet–Langlands transfer. The function field p‑adic local Langlands correspondence has been used to prove the compatibility of the global Langlands correspondence with the p‑adic Hodge theory of Drinfeld modules, and to construct p‑adic L‑functions for automorphic forms on GL_n over function fields via the method of Euler systems.

Drinfeld Modular Forms for Higher Rank Groups and the Langlands–Shahidi Method

The theory of Drinfeld modular forms for GL2 over a function field F is classical, but the extension to higher rank groups G is necessary for the Langlands–Shahidi method, which constructs L‑functions for automorphic representations of G by integrating Eisenstein series against cusp forms. A Drinfeld modular form of weight k and level Γ for a reductive group G over F is a section of a line bundle on the Drinfeld modular variety Sh_G(Γ) = G(F)G(𝔸_F)/K_∞Γ, where K_∞ is a maximal compact subgroup of G(F_∞) and Γ is a congruence subgroup of G(𝔸_F^f). For G = GL_n, the Drinfeld modular variety is the moduli space of rank‑n Drinfeld modules with level structure, and modular forms are functions on the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ω^n = ℙ^n(ℂ_∞) ∪H H, where H runs over F_∞‑rational hyperplanes. The Langlands–Shahidi method for GL_n over function fields was developed by Lafforgue and others: one considers the Eisenstein series E(g, s, f) induced from a cuspidal automorphic representation π of a Levi subgroup M of a parabolic P = MN, and the constant term of E along the opposite parabolic gives an intertwining operator M(s, π) whose normalising factor is a product of L‑functions L(s, π, r_i) where r_i are the representations of the L‑group ^L M on the Lie algebra of the unipotent radical of P. The poles of these L‑functions correspond to the poles of the Eisenstein series, and the residues yield automorphic representations of G. For G = GSp4, the Drinfeld modular forms are sections of the determinant line bundle on the Siegel modular variety of genus 2 over F, and the Langlands–Shahidi method produces the spinor L‑function and the standard L‑function of a cuspidal representation of GSp4. The Fourier expansion of a Drinfeld modular form for GSp4 involves Whittaker functions on the unipotent radical of the Siegel parabolic, and the coefficients are given by sums over ideal classes in the function field. The Hecke operators act on the space of Drinfeld modular forms via double cosets Γ g Γ, and the Hecke algebra is commutative for GL_n but non‑commutative for GSp4. The Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for Drinfeld modular forms of higher rank predicts that the Satake parameters of a cuspidal automorphic representation have absolute value q(k−1)/2 at unramified places, where k is the weight. This was proved for GL_n by Lafforgue using the cohomology of shtukas, and for GSp4 by Weissauer using the geometry of the Siegel modular variety. The Langlands–Shahidi method also provides a functional equation for the L‑functions, and the local coefficients are related to the γ‑factors of the local Langlands correspondence. Recent work by Hundley and others has extended the method to cover the exterior square L‑function for GL_n and the symmetric square L‑function for GL2, using the theory of Drinfeld modular forms on the metaplectic cover of GL2.

Unitary Drinfeld Modular Forms and the Base Change Lifting

Let F be a global function field of characteristic p and let E/F be a separable quadratic extension. The unitary group U(n) attached to a Hermitian space of dimension n over E is a reductive group over F. Drinfeld modular forms for U(n) are defined as sections of automorphic line bundles on the Shimura‑type variety ShU(Γ) = U(F)U(𝔸F)/K∞Γ, where K∞ is a maximal compact subgroup of U(F∞) and Γ is a congruence subgroup. The Drinfeld upper half‑plane for U(n) is the complement of F∞‑rational Hermitian hyperplanes in ℙn−1(ℂ∞). Fourier expansions involve Whittaker functions on the unipotent radical of the standard Borel, and the coefficients are sums over ideal classes in the quadratic extension E.

The base change lifting for unitary groups was established by Lafforgue and later refined by Shin and others. It provides a functorial transfer of automorphic representations π of U(𝔸F) to automorphic representations BC(π) of GLn(𝔸E) that are conjugate‑self‑dual. At unramified places the Satake parameters of BC(π) are the union of the Satake parameters of π and their Galois conjugates. The lifting is compatible with the local Langlands correspondence for U(n) and GLn over the local fields Fv and Ew. The proof uses the comparison of trace formulas for U(n) and GLn together with the fundamental lemma for the unit elements of the Hecke algebras, which was proved by Ngô for the Lie algebras of unitary groups.

Applications include the construction of Galois representations attached to cuspidal automorphic representations of U(n) via the known correspondence for GLn over function fields (Lafforgue). The base change also yields a function field analogue of the Arthur‑Clozel endoscopic classification for unitary groups, and it plays a crucial role in the proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for U(n) by reducing to the known case for GLn.

Drinfeld Modules for Orthogonal Groups and the Theta Correspondence

Let V be a non‑degenerate quadratic space of dimension m over a global function field F. The split orthogonal group SO(m) and its double cover, the metaplectic group Mp(m), admit Drinfeld modular varieties defined as moduli spaces of Drinfeld modules equipped with an orthogonal level structure. A Drinfeld module of rank r with orthogonal structure is a Drinfeld module φ together with a symmetric bilinear form on its Tate module compatible with the Frobenius endomorphism. The associated symmetric space is the Drinfeld upper half‑plane Ωr with the action of SO(m)(F∞) via the natural embedding into GLr(F∞).

The theta correspondence for function fields, developed by Gan, Gross, and Prasad, relates automorphic representations of SO(m) and Sp(2n) inside the Weil representation of the metaplectic group. In the Drinfeld setting, the theta lift of a cuspidal automorphic representation π of SO(m) is realized in the cohomology of the moduli space of Drinfeld modules with orthogonal level structure. The local theta correspondence at a place v is governed by the local Langlands parameters: the L‑parameter of the theta lift is the tensor product of the L‑parameter of π with the standard representation of the dual group of Sp(2n). Global compatibility follows from the comparison of the relative trace formula for the dual pair (SO(m), Sp(2n)) with the geometric trace formula for the moduli of shtukas with orthogonal and symplectic structures.

Explicit formulas for the Fourier coefficients of theta lifts involve sums over isotropic vectors in the quadratic space, and the non‑vanishing of the lift is controlled by the central value of the standard L‑function L(½, π, Std). The function field analogue of the Rallis inner product formula expresses the Petersson norm of the theta lift as a product of local zeta integrals, providing a precise link between periods of Drinfeld modular forms and special values of L‑functions.

The L‑group of the quasi‑split unitary group U(3) is the semi‑direct product GL3(ℂ) ⋊ Gal(E/F), where the non‑trivial element of Gal(E/F) acts on GL3(ℂ) by transpose‑inverse composed with complex conjugation. At a split place v, the local Langlands parameter of π is a homomorphism WFv → GL3(ℂ) given by the unramified character sending geometric Frobenius to diag(αv, βv, γv). The base change lifting BC(π) is the automorphic representation of GL3(𝔸E) whose local component at w is the unramified principal series with Satake parameters {αv, βv, γv} (each viewed as a character of Ew× via the norm map). Because v splits, the two places w, w̄ above v give the same parameters, and the conjugate‑self‑duality condition reads αvγv = βv² = qvk−1 for the appropriate weight k.
Exam‑day strategy for unitary and orthogonal Drinfeld modular forms
When a question asks for the L‑group of a unitary or orthogonal group over a function field, write the dual group (GLn(ℂ) or SOm(ℂ), Sp2n(ℂ)) and then describe the Galois action explicitly. For base change, remember that at split places the Satake parameters are simply copied, while at inert places they are paired with their Galois conjugates. For theta lifts, the key point is the tensor product of L‑parameters; keep the dimension match (m = 2n+1 or 2n) in mind to decide whether the lift lands in a symplectic or orthogonal group.

Exceptional Group Drinfeld Modular Forms and the Langlands–Shahidi Method

Let G be a split simply‑connected exceptional group of type G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, or E₈ over a global function field F. Drinfeld modular forms for G are defined as sections of line bundles on the moduli stack of G‑shtukas with level structure, which is a higher‑dimensional analogue of the Drinfeld modular variety. The minimal parabolic subgroup B = TU gives a decomposition of the Lie algebra of the unipotent radical u into root spaces. The Langlands–Shahidi method attaches to each maximal parabolic P = MN a set of representations ri of the L‑group LM on the graded pieces of the Lie algebra of N. For exceptional groups these representations are the fundamental representations of the dual group (e.g., the 7‑dimensional representation for G₂, the 26‑dimensional for F₄, the 27‑dimensional for E₆, the 56‑dimensional for E₇, and the adjoint 248‑dimensional for E₈).

The Eisenstein series E(g, s, f) induced from a cuspidal automorphic representation π of M(𝔸F) has a constant term along the opposite parabolic given by an intertwining operator M(s, π). The normalising factor of M(s, π) is a product of L‑functions L(s, π, ri) where ri run over the representations of LM on the root spaces of N. The poles of these L‑functions correspond to residues of the Eisenstein series, which generate automorphic representations of G. The functional equation follows from the functional equations of the individual L‑functions, and the local coefficients are identified with the γ‑factors of the local Langlands correspondence for G established by L. Lafforgue for GLn and extended to exceptional groups by work of Braverman, Kazhdan, and others using the geometric Langlands correspondence for the dual group.

Explicit Fourier expansions of Drinfeld modular forms on exceptional groups involve Whittaker functions on the unipotent radical of the Borel subgroup. The coefficients are expressed as sums over the set of F‑rational points of the affine Grassmannian for the dual group, weighted by the values of the spherical function. This description is essential for the proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for exceptional groups, which predicts that the Satake parameters of a cuspidal representation have absolute value q(k−1)/2 at unramified places. The conjecture is known for G₂ and F₄ via the work of Kim and Shahidi on functorial lifts to GL7 and GL26, and for the simply‑laced types E₆, E₇, E₈ it follows from the geometric Langlands correspondence and the purity of the cohomology of the moduli of G‑shtukas.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Modularity Lifting Theorems for Galois Representations

Let ρ : Gal(𝔽q(t)sep/𝔽q(t)) → GLn(ℤℓ) be a continuous ℓ‑adic Galois representation unramified outside a finite set of places, with ℓ ≠ p. The function field modularity lifting problem asks whether ρ arises from a cuspidal automorphic representation of GLn over the function field. The Taylor–Wiles method was adapted to the function field setting by Böckle, Khare, and others. The key input is the existence of a suitable deformation ring R for ρ and a Hecke algebra 𝕋 acting on the space of Drinfeld modular forms of appropriate level and weight. One proves an isomorphism R ≅ 𝕋 after localising at a maximal ideal corresponding to a residual representation ρ̄ that is absolutely irreducible and satisfies a Taylor–Wiles hypothesis (the “numerical coincidence” that the Selmer group and the dual Selmer group have the same dimension).

The proof uses the cohomology of the moduli space of Drinfeld modules with level structure at the auxiliary primes introduced by the Taylor–Wiles trick. The auxiliary primes are chosen so that the local deformation condition at those primes is “minimal” and the corresponding local Hecke algebra is a power series ring. The patching argument then produces a module over a power series ring in several variables that is both finite free over the deformation ring and faithful over the Hecke algebra, forcing the isomorphism. The function field setting simplifies several technical points: the local deformation rings at places not dividing ℓ are formally smooth because the wild inertia group is a pro‑p group and ℓ ≠ p, and the global Euler characteristic formula for Galois cohomology over function fields is exact, eliminating the need for the “Wiles formula” correction term.

As a consequence, any irreducible ℓ‑adic representation of the Galois group of a global function field that is geometric (i.e., de Rham at places above ℓ) and satisfies the usual local‑global compatibility conditions is automorphic. This result provides the function field analogue of the modularity theorem for elliptic curves over ℚ and underlies the proof of the Sato–Tate conjecture for Drinfeld modules, the construction of p‑adic L‑functions, and the verification of the Bloch–Kato conjecture for the associated t‑motives.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Arthur–Selberg Trace Formula for GLₙ over Function Fields

The Arthur–Selberg trace formula in the number field setting expresses the spectral side of the automorphic spectrum in terms of geometric orbital integrals. Over a global function field F = 𝔽_q(t) the same philosophy holds, but the geometry of the moduli of Drinfeld shtukas replaces the symmetric space. The trace formula for GLₙ is obtained by integrating the kernel of a Hecke operator on the space of cuspidal automorphic forms against the characteristic function of a compact open subgroup of GLₙ(𝔸_F). The geometric side expands as a sum over conjugacy classes in GLₙ(F) of orbital integrals on the adelic group, while the spectral side is a sum over automorphic representations weighted by their Hecke eigenvalues. The crucial input is the Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula applied to the ℓ‑adic cohomology of the moduli stack of Drinfeld shtukas with one leg, which carries a natural action of the Hecke algebra and of the Galois group. The cohomology groups decompose as a direct sum of tensor products of automorphic representations and Galois representations, and the trace of Frobenius on the cohomology yields the desired identity. The function field setting simplifies several analytic difficulties: the orbital integrals are finite sums because the group is totally disconnected, and the fundamental lemma for the unit elements of the Hecke algebra was proved by Lafforgue using the geometry of the moduli of shtukas. This trace formula is the engine behind the global Langlands correspondence for GLₙ over function fields and underlies the proof of the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for Drinfeld modular forms.

In practice, the trace formula is used to compare the trace of Frobenius on the cohomology of the moduli of shtukas with the trace of Hecke operators on spaces of Drinfeld modular forms. The comparison yields an equality of L‑functions, which in turn gives the local‑global compatibility of the Langlands correspondence. The formula also provides a tool for proving multiplicity one results: the spectral side shows that each cuspidal automorphic representation occurs with multiplicity one in the cohomology. The geometric side involves orbital integrals that can be computed explicitly for regular semisimple elements using the Bruhat–Tits building of GLₙ over the local fields F_v. For non‑regular elements, the orbital integrals are expressed in terms of weighted orbital integrals, a technique introduced by Arthur and adapted to the function field case by Lafforgue and later by Ngô. The trace formula also admits a stable version, where the geometric side is summed over stable conjugacy classes; this stable trace formula is the starting point for the endoscopic classification of automorphic representations for classical groups over function fields.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Endoscopic Classification for Classical Groups over Function Fields

The endoscopic classification, pioneered by Arthur for real groups, describes the discrete automorphic spectrum of a classical group in terms of stable distributions on its endoscopic groups. In the function field setting the same picture emerges, with the moduli of G‑shtukas playing the role of Shimura varieties. For a split classical group G such as SO(2n+1), Sp(2n), or SO(2n) over F, the stable trace formula for G is compared with the (unstable) trace formula for its endoscopic groups H, which are products of general linear groups. The comparison is mediated by the fundamental lemma for the unit elements of the Hecke algebras of G and H, which was proved by Ngô using the Hitchin fibration on the moduli of Higgs bundles and the geometry of the affine Grassmannian. The result is a decomposition of the discrete automorphic spectrum of G into packets, each packet being associated to a global Langlands parameter φ : Gal(F^sep/F) → ^L G. The parameter φ factors through the L‑group of an endoscopic group H, and the packet consists of the automorphic representations of G that are transfers of a single cuspidal automorphic representation of H. The multiplicity formula for each representation in the packet is given by the size of the centralizer of the image of φ in the dual group, exactly as in Arthur’s multiplicity formula. This classification has been carried out for symplectic and odd orthogonal groups by Lafforgue and for even orthogonal groups by recent work of Chen, Liao, and others using the theory of G‑shtukas with parabolic level structure.

The endoscopic classification has several concrete consequences for Drinfeld modules and shtukas. First, it provides a precise description of the automorphic representations that arise in the cohomology of the moduli of G‑shtukas, which in turn yields the local‑global compatibility of the Langlands correspondence for G. Second, it allows one to transfer functorial lifts from GLₙ to classical groups, giving a function field analogue of the theta correspondence and of the descent construction of Ginzburg–Rallis–Soudry. Third, the classification implies the Ramanujan–Petersson bounds for automorphic forms on classical groups, because the bounds are known for GLₙ via the purity of the cohomology of Drinfeld shtukas. Finally, the endoscopic classification is the key input for the proof of the function field analogue of the Gan–Gross–Prasad conjecture, which relates periods of automorphic forms on G to special values of L‑functions attached to the relevant Langlands parameters.

Technique: Using the Stable Trace Formula to Identify Endoscopic Packets
Let G = Sp(4) over 𝔽_q(t) and let H = GL(2) × GL(2) be an elliptic endoscopic group. Suppose a cuspidal automorphic representation π_H of H has Satake parameters {α_v, β_v} at an unramified place v. Describe the Satake parameters of the transferred representation π_G of G at v, and explain how the stable trace formula guarantees that the L‑function L(s, π_G, std) equals L(s, π_H, std ⊗ std).
At an unramified place v the Satake parameter of π_H is a pair of semisimple conjugacy classes (diag(α_v, β_v), diag(γ_v, δ_v)) in GL(2) × GL(2). The L‑embedding ^L H → ^L G sends (g₁, g₂) to the block‑diagonal matrix diag(g₁, g₂) inside Sp(4, ℂ) after identifying the dual group of Sp(4) with SO(5, ℂ). Consequently the Satake parameter of the transfer π_G is the conjugacy class in SO(5, ℂ) with eigenvalues {α_v, β_v, γ_v, δ_v, (α_v β_v γ_v δ_v)-1}. The standard L‑function of π_G is the product over v of det(1 - Satake(π_G,v) q_v-s)-1, which expands exactly to the product of the standard L‑functions of the two GL(2) factors, i.e. L(s, π_H, std ⊗ std). The stable trace formula matches the stable orbital integrals on G with the unstable orbital integrals on H; the fundamental lemma ensures the equality of the transfer factors, so the spectral side of the stable trace formula for G contains the character of π_G with the same multiplicity as the character of π_H on the spectral side for H. Hence the L‑functions coincide.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Fontaine–Laffaille Theory in Equal Characteristic

Fontaine–Laffaille theory classifies finite flat group schemes over the ring of integers of a p‑adic field with p > 2 in terms of filtered Dieudonné modules with a nilpotent operator. In equal characteristic p the role of the p‑adic field is played by a local field F_v = 𝔽_q((π)) of characteristic p, and the analogue of a finite flat group scheme is a finite flat Drinfeld module (or more generally a finite flat Anderson t‑motive) over the valuation ring O_v. The classification was developed by Hartl and Juschka, who introduced the category of (φ, ∇)‑modules over the Robba ring in equal characteristic. A Drinfeld module φ of rank r over O_v gives rise to a φ‑module M = lim_← φ[π^n] over the period ring Ainfeq, equipped with a Frobenius φ and a connection ∇ satisfying the Griffiths transversality condition. The Fontaine–Laffaille condition translates into the requirement that the Hodge filtration on M has length < p and that the Frobenius on the graded pieces is nilpotent of order ≤ 1. Under these hypotheses the functor from finite flat Drinfeld modules to filtered φ‑modules is an equivalence of categories. This equivalence is the key input for the p‑adic Hodge theory of Drinfeld modules: it identifies the étale cohomology of a Drinfeld module with its de Rham cohomology via the period isomorphism, and it provides the crystalline comparison isomorphism for t‑motives with good reduction.

The theory has several important applications. First, it yields a classification of the possible reduction types of a Drinfeld module at a place v of bad reduction: the filtered φ‑module encodes the monodromy operator N, and the weight filtration on the associated (φ, N)‑module recovers the Tate uniformization in the multiplicative reduction case. Second, it gives a concrete description of the Galois representation attached to a Drinfeld module of rank r: the representation is crystalline in the sense of Fontaine, and its Hodge–Tate weights are {0, 1, …, r‑1}. Third, the Fontaine–Laffaille equivalence is used in the proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GL_r over function fields via the Colmez functor: the functor sends a crystalline Galois representation to a (φ, ∇)‑module, which by the equivalence corresponds to a finite flat Drinfeld module, and the latter determines a smooth representation of GL_r(F_v) through the cohomology of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane. Finally, the theory extends to higher rank Anderson t‑motives, providing a Tannakian category of crystalline t‑motives that is equivalent to the category of crystalline Galois representations, thus establishing the function field analogue of the Fontaine–Mazur conjecture for t‑motives.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Breuil–Kisin Classification of Finite Flat Group Schemes

The Breuil–Kisin classification describes finite flat group schemes over the ring of integers of a p‑adic field in terms of Kisin modules, i.e. modules over the ring 𝔖 = W(k)[[u]] equipped with a Frobenius semilinear map φ satisfying a certain divisibility condition. In equal characteristic the ring 𝔖 is replaced by the ring A = 𝔽_q[[u]] and the Frobenius is the q‑power map on coefficients. A finite flat Drinfeld module of rank r over O_v gives a Kisin module M which is a finite free A‑module of rank r together with a φ‑semilinear map φ_M : M → M such that the cokernel of 1 ⊗ φ_M : φ^*M → M is killed by the Eisenstein polynomial E(u) = u^e - π, where e is the ramification index of F_v/𝔽_q((π)). The category of such Kisin modules is equivalent to the category of finite flat Drinfeld modules over O_v. The proof follows the same pattern as in mixed characteristic: one constructs a period ring Ainfeq = Weq(O_v♭) and shows that the functor M ↦ (M ⊗_A Ainfeq)φ=1 is an equivalence onto the category of finite free ℤ_ℓ‑modules with a continuous Galois action that are crystalline with Hodge–Tate weights in [0, r‑1]. The Breuil–Kisin classification is more flexible than Fontaine–Laffaille because it does not require the Hodge filtration length to be < p; it works for all ranks and all ramification indices.

Applications of the Breuil–Kisin classification in the function field setting are parallel to those in mixed characteristic. It provides a concrete description of the moduli stack of Drinfeld modules with parahoric level structure at v, which is represented by a formal scheme whose generic fiber is the Drinfeld upper half‑plane. The special fiber of this moduli space is stratified by the isomorphism class of the associated Kisin module, and the strata are affine Deligne–Lusztig varieties in the affine Grassmannian of GL_r. This stratification is the key geometric input for the proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GL_r via the cohomology of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane (the function field analogue of the Harris–Taylor method). Moreover, the classification allows one to compute the nearby cycles of the universal Drinfeld module at v, which yields the monodromy operator N on the associated (φ, N)‑module and gives a precise description of the weight‑monodromy conjecture for t‑motives. Finally, the Breuil–Kisin modules are the natural objects on which the Colmez functor acts, providing a fully faithful embedding of the category of crystalline Galois representations into the category of smooth representations of GL_r(F_v).

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the p‑adic Local Langlands Correspondence for GLₙ via the Emerton–Gee Stack

The p‑adic local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over a p‑adic field was formulated by Emerton and Gee using the moduli stack of (φ, Γ)‑modules, now called the Emerton–Gee stack. In equal characteristic the role of (φ, Γ)‑modules is played by (φ, ∇)‑modules over the Robba ring in characteristic p, and the Emerton–Gee stack is replaced by the stack of (φ, ∇)‑modules with a prescribed Hodge filtration. This stack was constructed by Hellmann and Schraen, and its irreducible components are indexed by Serre weights, i.e. irreducible representations of GL_n(𝔽_q) over 𝔽_ℓ. The p‑adic local Langlands correspondence in this setting is a bijection between the set of irreducible smooth representations of GL_n(F_v) over 𝔽_ℓ that are generic and the set of continuous Galois representations ρ : Gal(F_v^sep/F_v) → GL_n(𝔽_ℓ) that are semisimple and satisfy a certain “generic” condition. The correspondence is realized geometrically by taking the mod ℓ cohomology of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane with coefficients in a local system attached to ρ; the resulting representation of GL_n(F_v) is the desired automorphic representation. The Emerton–Gee stack provides a deformation-theoretic framework: a point of the stack corresponds to a (φ, ∇)‑module, and its tangent space computes the Ext groups in the category of (φ, ∇)‑modules, which by the p‑adic Langlands correspondence match the Ext groups of the corresponding Galois representations.

The function field version has several distinctive features. Because the residue characteristic equals the characteristic of the base field, the Robba ring is a ring of power series in one variable with coefficients in 𝔽_q((π)), and the Frobenius is the q‑power map. The connection ∇ is a derivation with respect to the variable, and the Griffiths transversality condition is automatic for rank one objects but imposes non‑trivial constraints for higher rank. The stack of (φ, ∇)‑modules is an ind‑scheme of infinite type, but its reduced substack is a finite union of projective varieties, each isomorphic to a product of flag varieties. The irreducible components are in bijection with the Serre weights, and the multiplicity of a Serre weight in the mod ℓ reduction of a crystalline representation is given by the length of the corresponding component in the special fiber of the Breuil–Kisin deformation ring. This geometric picture yields a proof of the Breuil–Mézard conjecture for GL_n over function fields, which relates the Hilbert–Samuel multiplicities of the deformation rings to the multiplicities of Serre weights in the mod ℓ local Langlands correspondence. The conjecture was proved by Hu and Wang using the geometry of the moduli of (φ, ∇)‑modules and the cohomology of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Taylor–Wiles Method for Function Fields

The Taylor–Wiles method, originally devised to prove modularity lifting theorems for Galois representations over number fields, has been transplanted to the function field setting by Böckle, Khare, and Thorne. In this context one works with continuous ℓ‑adic representations ρ : Gal(F^sep/F) → GL_n(ℚ_ℓ) that are unramified outside a finite set of places and satisfy a suitable “Taylor–Wiles” hypothesis at a set of auxiliary primes. The key input is the existence of a suitable deformation ring R^univ parametrising lifts of the residual representation ρ̄ with prescribed local conditions, and a Hecke algebra 𝕋 acting on the space of automorphic forms on GL_n over the function field F. The method proceeds by patching together the completed local deformation rings at the auxiliary primes to produce a “patched” module M_∞ over a power series ring S_∞ = ℤ_ℓ[[x1,…,x_g]]. The numerical coincidence dim M_∞ = dim S_∞ forces R^univ ≅ 𝕋, yielding the desired modularity lifting theorem. In the function field case the role of the Shimura variety is played by the moduli stack of Drinfeld shtukas with level structure, whose ℓ‑adic cohomology carries the Hecke action. The work of Lafforgue provides the global Langlands correspondence for GL_n, and the Taylor–Wiles patching argument shows that any sufficiently generic residual representation lifts to a characteristic zero representation that occurs in the cohomology of these shtuka spaces. The auxiliary primes are chosen so that the local deformation rings are formally smooth of the expected dimension; this is achieved by imposing “Taylor–Wiles” level structures that kill the dual Selmer group. The resulting modularity lifting theorem has been used to prove automorphy for many Galois representations arising from Drinfeld modules of higher rank, and to establish the compatibility of the local Langlands correspondence with the global one.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Crystalline Comparison Isomorphism for Anderson t‑Motives

For an Anderson t‑motive M over a finite field 𝔽_q, there are two natural ℓ‑adic realisations: the Betti realisation given by the étale cohomology of the associated shtuka, and the de Rham realisation obtained from the rigid analytic uniformisation of the corresponding Drinfeld module. The crystalline comparison isomorphism identifies the de Rham cohomology H1_dR(M) ⊗F Bcris with the étale cohomology H1_et(M, ℚ_ℓ) ⊗ℚ_ℓ Bcris, where Bcris is Fontaine’s crystalline period ring in equal characteristic, constructed by Hartl and Juschka using the Robba ring with a Frobenius and a connection (φ,∇). The isomorphism is functorial, respects the Frobenius and the filtration, and is compatible with the weight‑monodromy conjecture proved by Lafforgue for shtukas. In practice one computes the Hodge–Tate weights of M from the Newton polygon of the associated (φ,∇)‑module; these weights coincide with the slopes of the Frobenius on the crystalline Dieudonné module of the reduction of the Drinfeld module at a place of good reduction. The comparison theorem was first established by G. Anderson for rank one t‑motives (the Carlitz module) and later extended by Hartl–Juschka to arbitrary rank using the theory of (φ,∇)‑modules over the Robba ring. A crucial technical step is the construction of a (φ,∇)‑module Dcris(M) that is canonically isomorphic to the de Rham realisation equipped with its Hodge filtration. The isomorphism then follows from the fully faithful embedding of the category of Anderson t‑motives into the category of (φ,∇)‑modules, proved by Papanikolas using the theory of difference equations in positive characteristic. This crystalline comparison underpins the p‑adic Hodge theory of Drinfeld modules and is the equal‑characteristic analogue of the Fontaine–Messing theorem for abelian varieties.

Worked Example: Computing Hodge–Tate Weights
Let M be the rank‑2 Anderson t‑motive attached to a Drinfeld module φ of rank 2 over 𝔽_q[t] with characteristic polynomial of Frobenius X2 − aX + q, where a ∈ 𝔽_q[t] has degree 1. Determine the Hodge–Tate weights of M.
The Newton polygon of the Frobenius on the crystalline Dieudonné module has slopes 0 and 1 because the characteristic polynomial is ordinary (the constant term q has valuation 1). By the crystalline comparison isomorphism the Hodge–Tate weights are exactly these slopes. Hence the Hodge–Tate weights of M are {0,1}.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Explicit Reciprocity Law for Drinfeld Modules

Classical explicit reciprocity laws (e.g. the Kronecker–Weber theorem and the explicit formulas of Coates–Wiles) have a precise counterpart for Drinfeld modules, developed by Hayes, Gekeler, and Taelman. Let φ be a Drinfeld module of rank r over the rational function field K = 𝔽_q(t) with sign‑normalised rank r. The explicit reciprocity law describes the action of the Galois group Gal(K^ab/K) on the torsion points φ[𝔞] for an ideal 𝔞 ⊂ 𝔽_q[t] in terms of the Hayes module structure on the Carlitz module and the Drinfeld discriminant Δ(φ). Concretely, for a prime 𝔭 of good reduction the Frobenius element Frob_𝔭 acts on φ[𝔞] by the rule Frob_𝔭(x) = φπ_𝔭(x), where π_𝔭 is a uniformiser at 𝔭 and φπ_𝔭 denotes the endomorphism of φ corresponding to π_𝔭 under the Drinfeld module structure. Gekeler proved that the values of the Drinfeld modular form Δ(φ) at CM points generate the maximal abelian extension of the imaginary quadratic function field, and that the reciprocity map sends a prime 𝔭 to the class of the Drinfeld module φ modulo 𝔭 in the ideal class group of the endomorphism ring. Taelman extended this to higher rank by constructing a “Drinfeld–Hayes” pairing ⟨·,·⟩ : φ[𝔞] × φ[𝔞] → μ𝔞 (the group of 𝔞‑torsion roots of unity) which is Galois‑equivariant and satisfies ⟨Frob_𝔭(x), y⟩ = ⟨x, φπ_𝔭(y)⟩. This pairing yields an explicit description of the Artin map for the ray class field modulo 𝔞, and it is the function field analogue of the explicit reciprocity law for elliptic curves with complex multiplication. The law is instrumental in the construction of Stark units for function fields and in the proof of the Gross–Koblitz formula for Goss L‑functions.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Stark Conjectures in the Function Field Setting

Stark’s conjectures predict that the leading term at s = 0 of the Artin L‑function L(s, χ) for a Galois character χ of a number field can be expressed as a regulator of certain “Stark units”. In the function field world the role of the Artin L‑function is played by the Goss L‑function L(s, χ) attached to a character of the Galois group of a finite abelian extension of a global function field F. Hayes proved the function field Stark conjecture for the Carlitz module: the special value L(0, χ) equals (up to an explicit factor) the logarithm of a unit in the ray class field generated by torsion points of the Carlitz module. For higher rank Drinfeld modules, the conjecture was formulated by Goss and later refined by Taelman. The statement involves the Anderson logarithm log_φ : φ(ℂ_∞) → Lie(φ) and the special values of the Goss L‑function at negative integers. The leading term L*(0, χ) is conjectured to be a product of a rational factor, a power of the Drinfeld discriminant Δ(φ), and the determinant of a matrix of Anderson logarithms of certain “Stark points” coming from the torsion of φ. The proof for rank one is due to Hayes (1979) and uses the explicit class field theory of the Carlitz module. For rank r ≥ 2, partial results have been obtained by Anglès, Taelman, and others using the theory of t‑motives and the crystalline comparison isomorphism. In particular, Taelman proved the conjecture for characters that factor through the Galois group of the maximal abelian extension of an imaginary quadratic function field, by identifying the Stark units with special values of the Drinfeld modular form Δ(φ) at CM points. The remaining cases are open and constitute a major research direction linking Drinfeld modules, shtukas, and special values of L‑functions.

The Drinfeld Module Analogue of the Modularity Lifting Theorems for Galois Representations via the Emerton–Gee Stack

Although the Emerton–Gee stack for GL_n over function fields was mentioned in the context of the p‑adic local Langlands correspondence, its role in modularity lifting theorems deserves a separate treatment. The stack 𝔛EG parametrises (φ,∇)‑modules over the Robba ring with a fixed Hodge filtration. Its irreducible components are indexed by Serre weights, i.e. irreducible representations of GL_n(𝔽_q) over 𝔽_ℓ. A modularity lifting theorem in this setting asserts that a continuous Galois representation ρ : Gal(F_v^sep/F_v) → GL_n(𝔽_ℓ) which is “generic” (its restriction to inertia is a direct sum of distinct fundamental characters) lifts to a characteristic zero representation ρ̃ : Gal(F_v^sep/F_v) → GL_n(ℤ_ℓ) that is automorphic, i.e. arises from the ℓ‑adic cohomology of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane with appropriate level structure. The proof follows the Taylor–Wiles–Kisin patching method, but the deformation rings are replaced by the completed local rings of 𝔛EG at points corresponding to ρ. The key geometric input is that the special fibre of the versal deformation ring is a union of components each isomorphic to a product of flag varieties, and the multiplicities of Serre weights in the mod ℓ reduction of a crystalline lift are given by the lengths of these components. This geometric picture was established by Hellmann–Schraen and later used by Hu–Wang to prove the Breuil–Mézard conjecture for GL_n over function fields. The modularity lifting theorem then follows by patching the cohomology of the Drinfeld upper half‑plane (which realises the local Langlands correspondence) against the deformation theory on 𝔛EG. The result provides a complete parallel to the modularity lifting theorems of Kisin, Emerton–Gee, and others in mixed characteristic, while exploiting the simpler structure of the Robba ring in equal characteristic.
