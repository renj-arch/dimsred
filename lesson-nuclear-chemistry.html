Part 1

☢️ Radioactivity

Decay Modes
Alpha, Beta, Gamma Decay, Decay Series, and Types of Radiation
Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of particles or radiation from unstable atomic nuclei. Alpha (α) decay: the nucleus emits a helium-4 nucleus (⁰He²⁺), reducing atomic number by 2 and mass number by 4. Example: ⁶³⁵U → ⁶₀⁵Th + α. Alpha particles are heavy, slow, have low penetration (stopped by paper), but high linear energy transfer and strong ionizing power. Beta (β&supmin;) decay: a neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino: ₀¹n → ₁¹p + e&supmin; + ν̄e. Atomic number increases by 1, mass number unchanged. Example: ₄¹₄C → ₅¹₄N + β&supmin; + ν̄e. Beta particles are electrons, lighter, faster, penetrate a few mm into tissue. Beta-plus (β⁺) decay (positron emission): a proton converts to a neutron, emitting a positron and an electron neutrino: ₁¹p → ₀¹n + e⁺ + νe. Example: ₃⁶F → ₃⁴O + β⁺ + νe. Electron capture (EC): an inner atomic electron is captured by the nucleus, converting a proton to a neutron with neutrino emission: ₁¹p + e&supmin; → ₀¹n + νe. Gamma (γ) decay: emission of high-energy photons from excited nuclear states (often following α or β decay). Gamma rays have high penetration (cm of lead, meters of concrete), low ionizing power, but high health hazard. Decay series: ⁶³⁵U decays through 14 steps (including α and β decays) to stable ₀₂₀⁴Pb (half-life 4.47 × 10⁶ years). ⁶³⁵Th series ends at ₀₂₀⁶Pb. Specific activity: decays per second per gram (Bq/g). Secular equilibrium occurs when parent and daughter half-lives differ greatly.
Radioactive decay transforms unstable nuclei via characteristic α, β, and γ emissions, each with distinct penetration and ionizing power determined by the decay mode and energy.
Part 2

⏳ Half-Life & Kinetics

Decay Law
Decay Constant, Half-Life Calculations, and Radiometric Dating
Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics: the rate of decay is proportional to the number of radioactive atoms present. The decay law: N(t) = N₀e−λt, where λ is the decay constant (probability of decay per unit time, units s&supmin;¹), N₀ is initial number of atoms, N(t) is remaining at time t. The half-life t1/2 is the time for half the nuclei to decay: t1/2 = ln 2 / λ = 0.693 / λ. The mean lifetime τ = 1/λ = t1/2 / ln 2. The activity A = λN = −dN/dt, measured in becquerels (Bq, 1 decay/s) or curies (Ci, 3.7 × 10₁₀ Bq). Radiocarbon dating (Libby, Nobel 1960): ₄¹₄C is produced in the upper atmosphere by cosmic neutron capture on ₅¹₄N (¹₄N + n → ¹₄C + p). Living organisms maintain equilibrium ¹₄C/¹₂C ratio (~1.3 × 10&supmin;¹²); after death, ¹₄C decays (t1/2 = 5730 years) and the ratio decreases. The age t = (1/λ) ln(A₀/A). Uranium-lead dating: ⁶³⁵U decays to ₀₂₀⁴Pb (t1/2 = 4.47 Ga) and ⁶³₂U to ₀₂₀₂Pb (t1/2 = 704 Ma); the ratio gives ages for rocks and meteorites. Potassium-argon dating: ₁⁶K (t1/2 = 1.25 Ga) decays to ₁⁶Ar (by EC) and ₂₀Ca (by β&supmin;). Dating limits: ¹₄C useful to ~50,000 years; U-Pb to billions of years.
The exponential decay law and half-life provide the foundation for measuring time across scales from seconds to billions of years through radiometric dating techniques.
Part 3

⚿ Nuclear Reactions

Energy
Fission, Fusion, Chain Reactions, Critical Mass, and Nuclear Reactors
Nuclear reactions involve changes in atomic nuclei, releasing enormous energy compared to chemical reactions. Nuclear fission: a heavy nucleus splits into two lighter nuclei, releasing neutrons and ~200 MeV per fission. ⁶³⁵U + n → ⁶³⁶U* → fission fragments (e.g., ⁶₀⁶Kr + ⁶⁶⁶Ba) + 2-3 neutrons + ~200 MeV. The neutrons are fast (~1 MeV); they must be slowed (moderated) to thermal energies (~0.025 eV) to sustain the chain reaction in thermal reactors. Chain reaction: each fission produces neutrons that cause further fissions. Critical mass: the minimum mass of fissile material (⁶³⁵U, ⁶⁶⁵Pu) needed to sustain a chain reaction, depending on geometry, enrichment, and neutron reflectors. Nuclear reactors: PWR (pressurized water reactor) uses enriched UO₂ fuel, pressurized water as moderator and coolant (~155 bar, ~320°C), steam generator to produce power. BWR: water boils in the reactor core, steam drives turbine directly. CANDU: uses natural uranium fuel, heavy water D₂O moderator. Control rods (B₄C, Ag-In-Cd) absorb neutrons to regulate reaction rate. Nuclear fusion: light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. The D-T fusion: ₁²H + ₁³H → ₂₀He + ₀¹n + 17.6 MeV. The Coulomb barrier (electrostatic repulsion) requires temperatures >10⁶ K for fusion. Magnetic confinement (tokamak, stellarator) uses strong magnetic fields to contain plasma (ITER project aims for Q ≥ 10). Inertial confinement (NIF) uses lasers to compress and heat a D-T pellet. Proton-proton chain: in stars, 4 protons fuse to ₀He + 2e⁺ + 2νe + 26.7 MeV.
Fission releases energy by splitting heavy nuclei and powers current nuclear reactors; fusion promises abundant clean energy by combining light nuclei but requires extreme conditions to overcome the Coulomb barrier.

🏥 Applications

Nuclear Technology
Nuclear Medicine, Radiotherapy, Power Generation, and Industrial Uses
Nuclear medicine uses radioisotopes for diagnosis and therapy. Technetium-99m (⁶⁶Tcm, t1/2 = 6 hours, gamma 140 keV) is the most widely used medical isotope, obtained from ⁶⁶Mo/⁶⁶Tcm generators; used for SPECT imaging of heart, brain, bone, and tumors. Fluorine-18 (t1/2 = 110 min) in FDG (fluorodeoxyglucose) is used in PET (positron emission tomography) to image metabolic activity in cancer, neurology (Alzheimer’s), and cardiology. Iodine-131 (t1/2 = 8 days, β and γ) for thyroid cancer therapy and hyperthyroidism. Radiotherapy: Co-60 (t1/2 = 5.27 years, γ 1.17 and 1.33 MeV) in teletherapy units; linear accelerators (linacs) deliver high-energy X-rays or electrons. Brachytherapy: I-125 or Pd-103 seeds implanted into tumors for localized radiation. Nuclear power: ~440 reactors worldwide, ~10% of global electricity. Radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs): Pu-238 decays (t1/2 = 87.7 years, α) producing heat converted to electricity via thermocouples — used in Voyager, Cassini, Mars rovers (Curiosity, Perseverance) where solar power is insufficient. Industrial applications: neutron activation analysis (NAA) for trace element detection (sensitivity ppb); industrial radiography (Ir-192, Co-60) for weld inspection; thickness gauges (Kr-85, Sr-90) in manufacturing; sterilization of medical equipment (γ from Co-60). Nuclear weapons: fission bombs (gun-type ⁶³⁵U, implosion ⁶⁶⁵Pu) and thermonuclear (hydrogen) bombs using fission trigger for fusion. The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT) and Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty regulate nuclear arms.
Nuclear technology has diverse societal applications from life-saving medical imaging and cancer therapy to carbon-free electricity generation and deep-space exploration.
