Electrochemistry — Galvanic Cells, Nernst Equation, Electrolysis & Conductance

Electrochemistry is the study of charge transfer at the interface between an electronic conductor and an ionic conductor. It explains how a spontaneous redox reaction can deliver electrical work in a galvanic cell, and how an external voltage can force the reverse reaction in an electrolytic cell. The same framework links cell potential to Gibbs energy and equilibrium, and connects ionic motion in solution to measurable conductivity.

“Electrochemistry is the bridge between chemistry and electricity — the study of the interplay between chemical change and electrical energy.”
Adapted from Arrhenius and Nernst
Part 1

Galvanic Cells and Standard Electrode Potentials

Galvanic Cells
Cell construction, standard reduction potentials and the electrochemical series

A galvanic (voltaic) cell converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy. The reaction is split into two half-reactions at separate electrodes. Oxidation occurs at the anode; in a galvanic cell the anode is the negative electrode because electrons are released there. Reduction occurs at the cathode, which is positive. A salt bridge or porous frit completes the circuit by allowing spectator ions to migrate and maintain electrical neutrality.

Cell notation lists the anode half-cell on the left and the cathode half-cell on the right: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s). A single vertical bar marks a phase boundary; the double bar represents the salt bridge.

The standard cell potential is the difference between the standard reduction potentials of the two half-cells:

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Reduction potentials are measured relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), Pt | H₂(g, 1 bar) | H⁺(aq, 1 M), whose potential is defined as 0.000 V. The electrochemical series orders half-reactions by decreasing E°. Species with more positive reduction potentials are stronger oxidising agents; species with more negative reduction potentials are stronger reducing agents. A positive E°cell corresponds to a spontaneous reaction under standard conditions, with ΔG° = −nFE°cell.

Part 2

The Nernst Equation and Thermodynamic Connections

Nernst Equation
Concentration dependence, concentration cells and equilibrium constants

Real cells rarely operate under standard conditions. The Nernst equation relates the cell potential to the reaction quotient Q:

E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q

At 298 K, RT/F = 0.025693 V, so converting to base-10 logarithms gives:

E = E° − (0.05916 V/n) log Q

For the Daniell cell, Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺], and the potential falls as the product concentration rises. A concentration cell has identical electrodes but different electrolyte concentrations; its potential is E = −(RT/nF) ln([dilute]/[concentrated]), and it continues to work until the two concentrations equalise.

The thermodynamic link is ΔG = −nFE. At equilibrium E = 0 and Q = K, so ΔG° = −RT ln K = −nFE°cell, giving:

ln K = nFE°cell/(RT)   or   log K = nE°cell/0.05916 at 298 K.

This is the basis of potentiometric measurements, including the glass-electrode pH meter, in which the measured potential varies linearly with pH over the electrode’s working range.

Part 3

Electrolysis and Faraday’s Laws

Electrolysis
Driving non-spontaneous reactions and counting electrons

An electrolytic cell uses an external power supply to drive a reaction with a negative E°cell. The cathode remains the site of reduction and the anode the site of oxidation, but the signs are reversed relative to a galvanic cell: the cathode is connected to the negative terminal of the supply.

Faraday’s laws quantify the amount of product. The mass deposited or liberated is proportional to the charge passed, and

m = (Q/F)(M/z), where Q = I t.

Here F = 96,485 C mol⁻¹ is the Faraday constant, M is molar mass and z is the number of electrons transferred per formula unit. The same relation gives the volume of gas at an electrode through the ideal-gas equation.

In practice the applied voltage must exceed the reversible cell potential because of overpotential (especially for gas evolution) and ohmic losses. Electroplating, electrowinning, chlorine production and water electrolysis all depend on these principles. For water, the overall reaction is 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂, with a thermodynamic minimum of 1.23 V, although commercial cells run at higher voltages to achieve useful current densities.

Part 4

Conductance and Ionic Mobility

Conductance
Conductivity, molar conductivity and Kohlrausch’s law

The conductance of an electrolyte solution is described by its conductivity κ, the reciprocal of resistivity, usually quoted in S cm⁻¹. Molar conductivity normalises κ by concentration:

Λm = κ/c, with units S cm² mol⁻¹ when c is in mol cm⁻³.

For strong electrolytes, Λm falls slowly and linearly with the square root of concentration at low concentration, as described by Kohlrausch’s empirical law:

Λm = Λ°m − K√c

where Λ°m is the limiting molar conductivity at infinite dilution. For weak electrolytes, Λm rises steeply as concentration falls because the degree of dissociation increases. Kohlrausch’s law of independent migration states that each ionic species contributes independently at infinite dilution:

Λ°m = ν₊λ°₊ + ν₋λ°₋

Conductometric titrations exploit the sharp change in conductivity at the equivalence point. Conductivity data also yield the degree of dissociation α = Λm/Λ°m and, for a weak acid, the acid dissociation constant through Ostwald’s dilution law.
