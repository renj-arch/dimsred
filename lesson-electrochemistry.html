Electrochemistry — Galvanic Cells, Nernst Equation, Electrolysis & Conductance

Electrochemistry is the study of charge transfer at the interface between an electronic conductor and an ionic conductor. It explains how a spontaneous redox reaction can deliver electrical work in a galvanic cell, and how an external voltage can force the reverse reaction in an electrolytic cell. The same framework links cell potential to Gibbs energy and equilibrium, and connects ionic motion in solution to measurable conductivity.

“Electrochemistry is the bridge between chemistry and electricity — the study of the interplay between chemical change and electrical energy.”
Adapted from Arrhenius and Nernst
Part 1

Galvanic Cells and Standard Electrode Potentials

Galvanic Cells
Cell construction, standard reduction potentials and the electrochemical series

A galvanic (voltaic) cell converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy. The reaction is split into two half-reactions at separate electrodes. Oxidation occurs at the anode; in a galvanic cell the anode is the negative electrode because electrons are released there. Reduction occurs at the cathode, which is positive. A salt bridge or porous frit completes the circuit by allowing spectator ions to migrate and maintain electrical neutrality.

Cell notation lists the anode half-cell on the left and the cathode half-cell on the right: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s). A single vertical bar marks a phase boundary; the double bar represents the salt bridge.

The standard cell potential is the difference between the standard reduction potentials of the two half-cells:

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

Reduction potentials are measured relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), Pt | H₂(g, 1 bar) | H⁺(aq, 1 M), whose potential is defined as 0.000 V. The electrochemical series orders half-reactions by decreasing E°. Species with more positive reduction potentials are stronger oxidising agents; species with more negative reduction potentials are stronger reducing agents. A positive E°cell corresponds to a spontaneous reaction under standard conditions, with ΔG° = −nFE°cell.

Part 2

The Nernst Equation and Thermodynamic Connections

Nernst Equation
Concentration dependence, concentration cells and equilibrium constants

Real cells rarely operate under standard conditions. The Nernst equation relates the cell potential to the reaction quotient Q:

E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q

At 298 K, RT/F = 0.025693 V, so converting to base-10 logarithms gives:

E = E° − (0.05916 V/n) log Q

For the Daniell cell, Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺], and the potential falls as the product concentration rises. A concentration cell has identical electrodes but different electrolyte concentrations; its potential is E = −(RT/nF) ln([dilute]/[concentrated]), and it continues to work until the two concentrations equalise.

The thermodynamic link is ΔG = −nFE. At equilibrium E = 0 and Q = K, so ΔG° = −RT ln K = −nFE°cell, giving:

ln K = nFE°cell/(RT)   or   log K = nE°cell/0.05916 at 298 K.

This is the basis of potentiometric measurements, including the glass-electrode pH meter, in which the measured potential varies linearly with pH over the electrode’s working range.

Part 3

Electrolysis and Faraday’s Laws

Electrolysis
Driving non-spontaneous reactions and counting electrons

An electrolytic cell uses an external power supply to drive a reaction with a negative E°cell. The cathode remains the site of reduction and the anode the site of oxidation, but the signs are reversed relative to a galvanic cell: the cathode is connected to the negative terminal of the supply.

Faraday’s laws quantify the amount of product. The mass deposited or liberated is proportional to the charge passed, and

m = (Q/F)(M/z), where Q = I t.

Here F = 96,485 C mol⁻¹ is the Faraday constant, M is molar mass and z is the number of electrons transferred per formula unit. The same relation gives the volume of gas at an electrode through the ideal-gas equation.

In practice the applied voltage must exceed the reversible cell potential because of overpotential (especially for gas evolution) and ohmic losses. Electroplating, electrowinning, chlorine production and water electrolysis all depend on these principles. For water, the overall reaction is 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂, with a thermodynamic minimum of 1.23 V, although commercial cells run at higher voltages to achieve useful current densities.

Part 4

Conductance and Ionic Mobility

Conductance
Conductivity, molar conductivity and Kohlrausch’s law

The conductance of an electrolyte solution is described by its conductivity κ, the reciprocal of resistivity, usually quoted in S cm⁻¹. Molar conductivity normalises κ by concentration:

Λm = κ/c, with units S cm² mol⁻¹ when c is in mol cm⁻³.

For strong electrolytes, Λm falls slowly and linearly with the square root of concentration at low concentration, as described by Kohlrausch’s empirical law:

Λm = Λ°m − K√c

where Λ°m is the limiting molar conductivity at infinite dilution. For weak electrolytes, Λm rises steeply as concentration falls because the degree of dissociation increases. Kohlrausch’s law of independent migration states that each ionic species contributes independently at infinite dilution:

Λ°m = ν₊λ°₊ + ν₋λ°₋

Conductometric titrations exploit the sharp change in conductivity at the equivalence point. Conductivity data also yield the degree of dissociation α = Λm/Λ°m and, for a weak acid, the acid dissociation constant through Ostwald’s dilution law.

Liquid Junction Potentials and Their Correction in Cell Measurements

When two electrolyte solutions of different composition or concentration meet, a diffusion potential arises because ions migrate at different speeds. This liquid‑junction potential (LJP) adds an uncontrolled voltage to the measured cell EMF and can be as large as several tens of millivolts. In a conventional cell diagram the double vertical bar (||) represents a salt bridge that is intended to minimise the LJP by using a concentrated, equitransferent electrolyte such as KCl or KNO₃. Even with a salt bridge a residual junction potential remains; its magnitude depends on the mobility difference between the cation and anion of the bridge electrolyte and on the concentration gradients at the two interfaces.

The Henderson equation provides a quantitative estimate for a single liquid junction:

Ej = (RT/F) × ( (u₊ − u₋) / (u₊ + u₋) ) × ln (a₁ / a₂)

where u₊ and u₋ are the ionic mobilities of the cation and anion, and a₁, a₂ are the activities of the electrolyte on the two sides. For a KCl bridge u₊ ≈ u₋, so the prefactor (u₊−u₋)/(u₊+u₋) is close to zero, which is why KCl is the standard choice. In cells where a salt bridge cannot be used — for example, solid‑state electrochemical cells or measurements in non‑aqueous media — the LJP must be either calculated from known mobilities or eliminated experimentally by the “zero‑current” method: the cell is short‑circuited through a high‑impedance voltmeter until the current falls to zero, at which point the measured voltage equals the true thermodynamic EMF plus the LJP. The LJP can then be subtracted if the bridge composition is known.

In potentiometric titrations the LJP is often the dominant source of systematic error near the equivalence point, where the analyte concentration changes rapidly. Modern practice uses a double‑junction reference electrode (an inner KCl filling solution separated from the sample by a second junction filled with a solution of similar ionic strength to the sample) to keep the LJP constant throughout the titration. When reporting standard potentials, the IUPAC convention requires that the LJP be either negligible (<0.1 mV) or explicitly corrected and reported.

Temperature Dependence of Electrode Potentials and the Thermodynamic Derivation

The standard electrode potential E° is defined at 298.15 K, but real measurements are frequently made at other temperatures. The temperature coefficient (∂E/∂T)p is directly related to the entropy change of the half‑reaction through the Gibbs‑Helmholtz relation. Starting from ΔG = −nFE and ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, differentiation with respect to temperature at constant pressure gives:

(∂E/∂T)p = ΔS / (nF)

Thus a positive temperature coefficient indicates a positive reaction entropy (increase in disorder), while a negative coefficient signals a decrease in entropy. For the hydrogen electrode the temperature coefficient is essentially zero because the reaction H⁺ + e⁻ → ½ H₂ involves no net change in the number of gas molecules. In contrast, the Cu²⁺/Cu couple shows a small negative coefficient (≈ −0.0005 V K⁻¹) reflecting the ordering of water molecules around the highly charged Cu²⁺ ion.

Integrating the temperature coefficient between two temperatures yields the potential at the second temperature, provided ΔS is assumed constant over the interval:

E(T₂) = E(T₁) + (ΔS / nF) × (T₂ − T₁)

When ΔS is not known, the full thermodynamic cycle can be used: ΔG°(T) = ΔH°(T) − TΔS°(T). Standard enthalpies and entropies of formation for aqueous ions are tabulated (e.g., in the NIST Chemistry WebBook), allowing calculation of E° at any temperature. For the Daniell cell, using ΔH° = −218 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS° = −210 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ gives E°(298 K) = 1.10 V and predicts a decrease of about 0.7 mV per kelvin, in good agreement with experimental data.

In practice, the Nernst equation is often written with the temperature‑dependent factor (RT/nF) ln Q. At 310 K (physiological temperature) the factor becomes 0.0267 V, and the base‑10 form is E = E° − (0.05916 V × T/298) / n × log Q. This temperature scaling is essential for bio‑electrochemical sensors and for high‑temperature electrolysis (e.g., solid‑oxide electrolysis cells operating at 1073 K).

Worked Example: Temperature Correction of a Cell Potential
A galvanic cell based on the reaction Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) has E° = 1.103 V at 298 K. The standard entropy change for the reaction is ΔS° = −210 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Calculate the standard cell potential at 310 K assuming ΔS° is temperature‑independent.
Use E°(T₂) = E°(T₁) + (ΔS° / nF) × (T₂ − T₁). Here n = 2, F = 96485 C mol⁻¹, ΔS° = −210 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T₁ = 298 K, T₂ = 310 K. ΔS°/(nF) = −210 / (2 × 96485) = −1.088 × 10⁻³ V K⁻¹. ΔT = 12 K. Correction = −1.088 × 10⁻³ × 12 = −0.0131 V. E°(310 K) = 1.103 V − 0.0131 V = 1.090 V (rounded to three decimal places).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the temperature dependence of a cell potential, first identify whether ΔS° or (∂E/∂T) is given. If only E° at 298 K is supplied, you cannot compute the potential at another temperature without thermodynamic data. Memorise the sign convention: a negative ΔS° makes the potential decrease with temperature. For Nernst‑equation problems, always replace 0.05916 V by (0.05916 V × T/298) when T ≠ 298 K.

Overpotential, Tafel Kinetics and Practical Electrolysis Voltage

The thermodynamic decomposition voltage of an electrolytic reaction is the reversible cell potential Erev obtained from the Nernst equation at the operating concentrations. In any real electrolyser the applied voltage Vapp must exceed Erev because of three distinct overpotentials: activation overpotential (ηact), ohmic overpotential (ηohm), and concentration (mass‑transport) overpotential (ηconc). The total cell voltage is Vapp = Erev + ηact,anode + ηact,cathode + ηohm + ηconc,anode + ηconc,cathode.

Activation overpotential arises from the finite rate of the electron‑transfer step at the electrode surface. For a single‑step, one‑electron transfer the Butler‑Volmer equation relates current density j to ηact:

j = j₀ [ exp(αₐ nF ηact / RT) − exp(−α_c nF ηact / RT) ]

where j₀ is the exchange current density, αₐ and α_c are the anodic and cathodic transfer coefficients (typically ≈0.5). At high overpotentials (|η| > 0.1 V) one exponential dominates, giving the Tafel equation:

ηact = a + b log|j| with b = 2.303 RT / (α nF)

The Tafel slope b is a diagnostic tool: a slope of ≈120 mV dec⁻¹ at 298 K indicates α = 0.5 for a one‑electron rate‑determining step; a slope of ≈60 mV dec⁻¹ suggests a preceding chemical step or a two‑electron transfer with α = 1. In industrial chlor‑alkali cells the anode reaction (2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻) exhibits a Tafel slope near 120 mV dec⁻¹, while the cathode hydrogen evolution on nickel shows ≈110 mV dec⁻¹.

Ohmic overpotential is simply ηohm = j × ρ × l, where ρ is the electrolyte resistivity and l the inter‑electrode distance. Modern zero‑gap designs minimise l and use highly conductive membranes (e.g., Nafion) to keep ηohm below 0.1 V at current densities of 1 A cm⁻².

Concentration overpotential appears when the reactant supply to the electrode surface cannot match the consumption rate. For a planar electrode under diffusion control, the limiting current density jlim = nF D Cb / δ, where D is the diffusion coefficient, Cb the bulk concentration, and δ the diffusion layer thickness. The concentration overpotential follows ηconc = (RT/nF) ln(1 − j/jlim). In water electrolysis at high current densities, bubble coverage further increases the effective δ, raising ηconc dramatically.

Practical electrolyser design therefore targets high exchange current densities (by catalyst selection), low resistivity electrolytes, and turbulent flow or porous electrodes to shrink δ. The overall energy efficiency is ηenergy = (ΔG°/nF) / Vapp × 100 %. For alkaline water electrolysis at 1 A cm⁻², typical Vapp ≈ 1.85 V, giving ηenergy ≈ 66 % (ΔG°/nF = 1.23 V).

Debye–Hückel–Onsager Theory and Conductivity of Strong Electrolytes

Kohlrausch’s empirical law Λm = Λ°m − K√c works well up to about 0.01 M for 1:1 electrolytes, but deviations appear at higher concentrations because the simple √c term does not capture ion‑ion correlation and solvent‑structure effects. The Debye–Hückel–Onsager (DHO) equation provides a theoretical foundation for the concentration dependence of molar conductivity of strong electrolytes in the dilute regime:

Λm = Λ°m − (A + B Λ°m) √c

where A = (F² / (3π η)) (2 / (εRT))1/2 and B = (F / (6π η)) (2 / (εRT))1/2. Here η is the solvent viscosity, ε the dielectric constant, and the other symbols have their usual meanings. The term A√c represents the electrophoretic effect (drag of the ionic atmosphere on the moving ion), while B Λ°m √c represents the relaxation effect (asymmetry of the ionic atmosphere caused by the ion’s motion). Both effects reduce conductivity relative to the infinite‑dilution limit.

For aqueous NaCl at 298 K, using η = 0.89 cP, ε = 78.5, Λ°m = 126.5 S cm² mol⁻¹, the DHO equation predicts K ≈ 94 S cm² mol⁻¹ M⁻¹ᐟ², close to the experimental Kohlrausch constant. The theory also predicts that the limiting molar conductivity of an ion λ°i is proportional to its mobility ui via λ°i = F ui. Mobilities can be estimated from Stokes’ law ui = |z|e / (6π η ri) where ri is the hydrodynamic radius. This link allows estimation of Λ°m for ions lacking experimental data.

At concentrations above ~0.1 M the DHO equation fails because the linearised Poisson–Boltzmann approximation breaks down and ion pairing becomes significant. Extended models (e.g., the Pitzer equations or the Mean Spherical Approximation) incorporate specific ion‑pair formation constants and three‑body interactions. In practice, conductivity measurements in the 0.1–1 M range are fitted to empirical polynomials or to the Fuoss‑Onsager equation, which adds a term proportional to c log c to account for ion‑pair relaxation.

Conductometric titrations exploit the sharp change in Λm at the equivalence point. For a strong acid–strong base titration, the conductivity initially falls as H⁺ (λ° = 349.8 S cm² mol⁻¹) is replaced by Na⁺ (λ° = 50.1 S cm² mol⁻¹), reaches a minimum at equivalence, then rises as excess OH⁻ (λ° = 199.1 S cm² mol⁻¹) appears. The method is especially valuable for coloured or turbid solutions where visual indicators fail.

Mixed Potential Theory and Corrosion Electrochemistry

When a metal is immersed in an electrolyte, several redox reactions can occur simultaneously on its surface: the metal dissolution (anodic) reaction M → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻ and one or more cathodic reactions such as oxygen reduction O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ or hydrogen evolution 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂. Because the metal is a single electronic conductor, the net current must be zero at open circuit; the electrode adopts a mixed potential Ecorr where the sum of anodic currents equals the sum of cathodic currents. This is the corrosion potential. The corresponding current density (the corrosion current icorr) is the rate of metal loss.

Evans diagrams (log i vs. E) provide a graphical solution. The anodic Tafel line (slope βa) and the cathodic Tafel line (slope βc) intersect at (Ecorr, icorr). If the cathodic reaction is oxygen reduction, its exchange current density is often low, so the cathodic Tafel line lies far below the anodic line, giving a low icorr and a relatively noble Ecorr. In deaerated acid, hydrogen evolution dominates; its exchange current on many metals is higher, shifting Ecorr to more active values and increasing icorr.

Polarisation resistance Rp measured by a small AC perturbation around Ecorr is related to icorr by the Stern‑Geary equation:

icorr = (βa βc) / (2.303 (βa + βc) Rp)

This linear‑polarisation technique is the basis of many corrosion‑monitoring probes. For iron in neutral water with βa = 0.12 V dec⁻¹, βc = 0.10 V dec⁻¹, and Rp = 2 kΩ cm², icorr ≈ 1.2 µA cm⁻², corresponding to a penetration rate of ~0.015 mm yr⁻¹.

Passivation occurs when a protective oxide film forms, drastically reducing the anodic exchange current density and shifting the anodic Tafel line downward. The mixed potential then moves into the passive region, and icorr drops by orders of magnitude. Breakdown of passivity (pitting) is triggered when the local chloride concentration exceeds a critical value, causing a sudden increase in anodic current at a fixed potential. The pitting potential Epit is a key parameter for stainless‑steel selection in chloride environments.

Galvanic coupling of two dissimilar metals creates a mixed‑potential cell where the more active metal becomes the anode and corrodes faster, while the noble metal acts as a cathode. The galvanic current can be predicted from the individual polarisation curves of the two metals; the intersection of the anodic curve of the active metal with the cathodic curve of the noble metal gives the galvanic corrosion current. This principle underlies sacrificial anode cathodic protection (Zn or Mg anodes on steel pipelines) and the design of corrosion‑resistant alloys.

  • Assuming the salt bridge eliminates the liquid‑junction potential completely; a residual LJP of a few millivolts is typical and must be corrected for high‑precision work.
  • Using the 298 K Nernst factor (0.05916 V) at other temperatures without scaling by T/298.
  • Confusing activation overpotential with ohmic drop; the former follows a Tafel law, the latter is linear with current density.
  • Applying Kohlrausch’s √c law beyond its dilute‑solution limit (≈0.01 M for 1:1 electrolytes) where ion pairing invalidates the linear relation.
  • Treating the corrosion potential as a thermodynamic equilibrium potential; it is a kinetic mixed potential where net current is zero but individual half‑reactions are far from equilibrium.
  • Neglecting the concentration overpotential in high‑current‑density electrolysis; bubble coverage can increase the effective diffusion layer thickness by an order of magnitude.
  • Using the Stern‑Geary equation without verifying that the polarisation resistance is measured in the linear region (typically ±10 mV around Ecorr).
