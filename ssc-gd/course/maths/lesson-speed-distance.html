Fundamental Relationship Between Speed, Distance and Time

1.1 The Core Formula and Unit Consistency

The entire edifice of motion problems rests on a single definitional identity: distance equals speed multiplied by time. This relationship is not merely a formula to be memorised; it is a statement of physical reality that defines what speed means. If a vehicle maintains a constant speed of sixty kilometres per hour, it covers sixty kilometres in the first hour, another sixty in the second, and so the total distance grows linearly with the time elapsed. The critical operational rule that follows from this definition is the absolute necessity of unit consistency. A speed given in kilometres per hour cannot be multiplied by a time given in minutes or seconds without conversion, because the hour, the minute, and the second are different magnitudes of the same dimension. The examiner exploits this necessity by deliberately mixing units in the problem statement—offering speed in km/h, time in minutes, and asking for distance in metres—forcing the candidate to demonstrate mastery of the metric system before any arithmetic begins.

1.2 Average Speed: Harmonic Mean Versus Arithmetic Mean

Average speed is defined as the total distance travelled divided by the total time taken, and this definition alone determines which mathematical mean applies. When a journey consists of two equal distances covered at different speeds, the time spent at the slower speed is necessarily longer than the time spent at the faster speed. Because the slower speed weighs more heavily in the time denominator, the average speed is pulled down towards the lower value, resulting in the harmonic mean of the two speeds: two times the product divided by the sum. Conversely, when a journey consists of two equal time intervals at different speeds, the distances covered are directly proportional to the speeds, and the average speed becomes the simple arithmetic mean of the two speeds. Confusing these two scenarios is the single most common error in this module; the candidate must always verify whether the problem describes equal distances or equal times before selecting the averaging method.

1.3 Proportionality: Speed, Distance, and Time Relationships

The core formula implies three distinct proportionalities that govern how the variables behave when one is held constant. If time is held constant, distance becomes directly proportional to speed: doubling the speed doubles the distance covered in that fixed interval. If distance is held constant, speed becomes inversely proportional to time: doubling the speed halves the time required to cover that fixed distance. If speed is held constant, distance becomes directly proportional to time: doubling the time doubles the distance covered. These proportionalities are not merely algebraic rearrangements; they are logical shortcuts. For instance, if a person walks at three-fourths of his usual speed and reaches twenty minutes late, the inverse proportionality between speed and time for a fixed distance tells us immediately that the time taken has increased to four-thirds of the usual time, and the extra one-third corresponds to the twenty-minute delay, yielding the usual time without forming a single equation.

1.4 Unit Conversion Mechanics: Kilometres per Hour to Metres per Second

The conversion factor between kilometres per hour and metres per second arises directly from the definitions of the units. One kilometre is one thousand metres, and one hour is three thousand six hundred seconds. Therefore, one kilometre per hour equals one thousand metres divided by three thousand six hundred seconds, which simplifies to five-eighteenths of a metre per second. To convert from km/h to m/s, one multiplies by five-eighteenths; to convert from m/s to km/h, one multiplies by the reciprocal, eighteen-fifths. This conversion is not optional in train and boat problems because the lengths of trains and platforms are universally given in metres while speeds are conventionally quoted in km/h. A candidate who attempts to divide a train length in metres by a speed in km/h will produce a meaningless number; the units must be homogenised before the division yields a time in seconds.

Relative Speed: Concept and Application

2.1 Objects Moving in Opposite Directions

When two objects move towards each other, the distance separating them shrinks at a rate equal to the sum of their individual speeds. This additive principle holds because each object contributes its own motion to closing the gap; in one second, the first object covers a distance equal to its speed, and the second object simultaneously covers a distance equal to its speed, so the total reduction in separation is the sum. This principle applies whether the objects are trains approaching on parallel tracks, a car and a pedestrian walking towards each other on a road, or two runners starting from opposite ends of a track. The time until they meet is simply the initial separation divided by this summed speed, provided all units are consistent.

2.2 Objects Moving in the Same Direction

When two objects move in the same direction, the distance between them changes at a rate equal to the absolute difference of their speeds. The faster object gains on the slower one only by the margin of its excess speed; the component of speed common to both merely maintains the existing separation. If the faster object is behind, it closes the gap at this difference speed, and the time to overtake is the initial gap divided by the difference. If the faster object is ahead, the gap widens at the same difference speed. The absolute value ensures the relative speed is always a positive magnitude representing the rate of change of separation, regardless of which object is designated first.

2.3 Relative Speed with Respect to a Stationary Object

A stationary object—a pole, a tree, a man standing on a platform—can be treated as an object moving with zero speed. The relative speed of a moving object with respect to a stationary one is therefore simply the speed of the moving object itself. This trivial case is the foundation of all "passing a pole" problems: the time taken to pass the pole is the length of the moving object divided by its own speed. It is essential to recognise that the stationary object has no length in the direction of motion, so the distance covered during the crossing is exactly the length of the moving object and nothing more.

2.4 Relative Motion on a Circular Track

On a circular track, relative speed determines not only when two runners meet but also where they meet. When running in opposite directions, they meet whenever the sum of the distances they have covered equals an integer multiple of the track length; the time for the first meeting is the track length divided by the sum of speeds. When running in the same direction, the faster runner gains one full lap on the slower runner every time the difference in distances covered equals the track length; the time for the first meeting is the track length divided by the difference of speeds. The number of distinct meeting points on the track is determined by the ratio of their speeds reduced to simplest terms: if the speed ratio is a:b in lowest terms, they meet at a+b distinct points when running in opposite directions and at |a-b| distinct points when running in the same direction.

Example: Two Trains Crossing in Opposite Directions
Two trains of lengths 140 metres and 160 metres are running on parallel tracks in opposite directions at speeds of 54 km/h and 72 km/h respectively. In what time will they completely cross each other?
Solution: First, convert speeds to m/s. 54 km/h = 54 × (5/18) = 15 m/s. 72 km/h = 72 × (5/18) = 20 m/s. Since they move in opposite directions, relative speed = 15 + 20 = 35 m/s. Total distance to be covered for complete crossing = sum of lengths = 140 + 160 = 300 metres. Time = Distance / Relative Speed = 300 / 35 = 60/7 seconds ≈ 8.57 seconds.
Exam Insight: Convert Before Combining
Always convert individual speeds to m/s before adding or subtracting them to find relative speed. Adding speeds in km/h and then converting the sum to m/s introduces rounding errors if the conversion factor 5/18 does not divide the sum cleanly. Converting first keeps the arithmetic exact and the numbers smaller.

Train Problems: Length, Time, and Crossing Scenarios

3.1 Crossing a Point Object: Pole or Standing Man

A pole, a signal post, or a man standing still on the platform are all effectively point objects with negligible length compared to the train. When a train passes such an object, the front of the train reaches the object first, and the crossing is complete only when the rear end of the train clears the object. During this interval, the train has moved a distance exactly equal to its own length. Therefore, the time taken is the length of the train divided by the speed of the train. No additional distance is involved, and the length of the pole or the width of the man is never added to the train length.

3.2 Crossing a Platform, Bridge, or Tunnel

A platform, a bridge, or a tunnel possesses significant length in the direction of the train's motion. For the train to completely clear the structure, the front of the train must travel the entire length of the structure plus the entire length of the train itself. The crossing begins when the engine enters the structure and ends when the last coach leaves it. Consequently, the total distance covered is the sum of the length of the train and the length of the platform. The time taken is this sum divided by the speed of the train. A frequent error is to use only the platform length, forgetting that the train must also move its own length out of the structure.

3.3 Two Trains Crossing Each Other in Opposite Directions

When two trains approach each other on parallel tracks, the situation combines relative speed with the summation of lengths. The relative speed is the sum of their individual speeds because they move towards each other. The total distance that must be covered for them to completely separate is the sum of their lengths: the front of train A must pass the entire length of train B and then its own length must clear train B. Equivalently, in the reference frame of train A, train B approaches at the relative speed and must travel a distance equal to the sum of the two lengths to pass completely. The crossing time is therefore the sum of lengths divided by the sum of speeds.

3.4 One Train Overtaking Another in the Same Direction

Overtaking occurs when a faster train catches up to a slower train ahead on the same track. The relative speed is the difference between their speeds because they move in the same direction. The distance to be covered for the overtaking to complete is again the sum of the lengths of both trains: the front of the faster train must close the initial gap (which is effectively the length of the slower train if we consider the moment the front of the faster train aligns with the rear of the slower train) and then move its own length ahead. The time taken is the sum of lengths divided by the difference of speeds. If the speeds are equal, the relative speed is zero and overtaking never occurs, which is consistent with the formula yielding infinite time.

Boats and Streams: Still Water and Current Effects

4.1 Downstream and Upstream Speed Definitions

A boat moving in a river experiences a speed relative to the ground that is the vector sum of its own propulsion speed in still water and the speed of the stream current. When the boat moves downstream, the current assists the motion, so the effective speed relative to the bank is the sum of the boat speed in still water and the stream speed. When the boat moves upstream, the current opposes the motion, so the effective speed is the difference between the boat speed and the stream speed, provided the boat speed exceeds the stream speed; otherwise, the boat cannot make headway. These two effective speeds—downstream and upstream—are the primary observables in most problems, while the still water speed and the stream speed are the underlying unknowns to be deduced.

4.2 Deriving Still Water Speed and Stream Speed

Because downstream speed equals boat speed plus stream speed, and upstream speed equals boat speed minus stream speed, adding the two equations eliminates the stream speed and yields twice the boat speed. Therefore, the boat speed in still water is exactly the arithmetic mean of the downstream and upstream speeds. Subtracting the upstream equation from the downstream equation eliminates the boat speed and yields twice the stream speed. Therefore, the stream speed is exactly half the difference between the downstream and upstream speeds. These derivation formulas—boat speed = (downstream + upstream)/2 and stream speed = (downstream - upstream)/2—are symmetric and allow instant calculation of the fundamental parameters from the observed effective speeds.

4.3 Round Trip Time and Average Speed in a Stream

A round trip covering the same distance downstream and upstream takes longer than the same round trip in still water because the time lost fighting the current upstream is greater than the time gained with the current downstream. The total time for a distance D each way is D/(B+S) + D/(B-S), which simplifies to 2BD/(B²-S²). The average speed for the round trip is total distance (2D) divided by total time, yielding (B²-S²)/B. This average speed is always less than the still water speed B, demonstrating that a current always increases the total travel time for a fixed round-trip distance. This result is independent of the distance D, depending only on the ratio of stream speed to boat speed.

4.4 Ratio-Based Problems and Time Comparisons

Many boat and stream problems are framed in terms of ratios rather than absolute speeds. If the ratio of upstream speed to downstream speed is given as U:D, then the ratio of boat speed to stream speed can be found by componendo and dividendo: (D+U)/(D-U) = B/S. Similarly, if the time taken to row a certain distance upstream is n times the time taken to row the same distance downstream, the ratio of speeds is the inverse, 1:n, so upstream speed : downstream speed = 1:n. Applying componendo and dividendo again yields the ratio of boat speed to stream speed as (n+1):(n-1). These ratio manipulations avoid the need for absolute speed values and are particularly useful when the problem asks for the ratio of speeds rather than their magnitudes.

Advanced Applications: Races, Variable Speed, and Meeting Points

5.1 Linear Races and Head Starts

In a linear race, contestants start from the same point and run towards a finish line. A head start can be given either in distance (one runner starts ahead of the starting line) or in time (one runner starts before the signal). If runner A gives runner B a start of x metres in a race of L metres, it means when A covers L metres, B covers only L-x metres in the same time. Since time is equal, the ratio of their speeds is L : (L-x). If A gives B a start of t seconds, then when A finishes the race in time T, B has been running for T+t seconds and has covered the full distance L. The speed ratio is then L/T : L/(T+t), which simplifies to (T+t):T. These equivalences allow conversion between distance starts and time starts, a common requirement in multi-part questions.

5.2 Variable Speed and Journey Segments

Real journeys often involve changes in speed—rest stops, speed reductions, or acceleration phases. The total time for such a journey is the sum of the times for each segment, where each segment time is its distance divided by its speed. The average speed for the whole journey is the total distance divided by this total time. It is never the average of the segment speeds unless the times are equal. A classic problem type involves a person travelling a certain distance at one speed and returning at another speed; the average speed is the harmonic mean of the two speeds because the distances are equal. If the problem states that a person covers one-third of the distance at speed S₁, one-third at S₂, and one-third at S₃, the average speed is the harmonic mean of the three speeds: 3 / (1/S₁ + 1/S₂ + 1/S₃).

5.3 Meeting Points and Distance Ratios

When two objects start simultaneously from two points and move towards each other, they meet at a point that divides the total distance in the ratio of their speeds. This follows because they travel for the same duration until meeting, and distance is proportional to speed when time is constant. If they continue to their destinations, turn around immediately, and move back, the subsequent meetings follow a predictable pattern: the total distance covered by both together between the start and the nth meeting is (2n-1) times the initial separation. The position of the nth meeting can be found by tracking the distance covered by one object, which is its speed multiplied by the total time, and then mapping that distance onto the back-and-forth path using the modulo operation with twice the track length. This technique transforms a seemingly complex bouncing problem into a simple linear distance calculation.

Escalator and Moving Walkway Problems

Escalator problems are a distinct category where the "track" itself moves at a constant speed. The moving walkway or escalator acts like a stream in boat‑and‑stream questions, but the direction of motion is fixed and the length of the walkway is known. A person walking on the escalator has an effective speed relative to the ground equal to the sum of his walking speed and the escalator speed when moving in the same direction, and the difference when moving opposite to the escalator. If the escalator length is L, the time taken to cover it while walking with the escalator is L ÷ (v_person + v_escalator); walking against it takes L ÷ (v_person − v_escalator), provided v_person > v_escalator. A common twist gives the time taken to walk the length when the escalator is stationary, and the time taken when the person stands still on the moving escalator. From these two times the walking speed and escalator speed can be derived: let t_walk be the time when the person walks on a stationary escalator, so v_person = L ÷ t_walk. Let t_stand be the time when the person stands on the moving escalator, so v_escalator = L ÷ t_stand. Then the time to walk on the moving escalator in the same direction is L ÷ (v_person + v_escalator) = 1 ÷ (1/t_walk + 1/t_stand) = (t_walk × t_stand) ÷ (t_walk + t_stand). This harmonic‑mean relationship is a favourite examiner shortcut. Another variation asks for the number of steps visible on the escalator when a person walks a certain number of steps while the escalator moves. If the person takes n steps at a rate of r steps per second, the escalator contributes (v_escalator × time) steps. The total visible steps equal n + (v_escalator × n ÷ r). Problems may also involve two persons walking on the same escalator in opposite directions; their meeting point divides the escalator length in the ratio of their effective speeds. The key is to treat the escalator as a moving reference frame and apply the same relative‑speed logic used for trains and boats.

Crossing a Moving Object: Person Walking on a Platform or Inside a Train

Standard train problems assume the object being crossed is stationary — a pole, a standing man, or a platform. When the object moves, the relative speed changes. Consider a train of length L_t moving at speed V_t crossing a man walking on the platform at speed V_m in the same direction. The relative speed is V_t − V_m, and the crossing time is L_t ÷ (V_t − V_m). If the man walks opposite to the train, the relative speed becomes V_t + V_m. A more complex scenario places the man inside a second train moving on a parallel track. Then the crossing time depends on the relative speed of the two trains and the sum of their lengths if they cross each other, or the difference of lengths if one overtakes the other. Examiners often combine this with a platform: a train crosses a platform of length L_p while a man walks on the platform. The train's front meets the man at some point; the time until the train's rear passes the man is not simply (L_t + L_p) ÷ V_t because the man moves during the crossing. The correct approach is to treat the man as a moving point object and compute the time for the train to cover the distance from the initial separation to the point where the rear clears the man. If the man starts at the near end of the platform and walks toward the far end, the initial separation between the train's front and the man is the platform length plus any offset. The relative speed is V_t ± V_m depending on directions. The distance the train must cover relative to the man is L_t + (distance the man walks during the crossing). This leads to an equation: time = (L_t + V_m × time) ÷ (V_t ± V_m). Solving gives time = L_t ÷ (V_t ± V_m − V_m) = L_t ÷ (V_t ± V_m ∓ V_m) — careful sign handling is essential. A frequent trap is to use the platform length instead of the train length when the man is on the platform. Remember: the train must move its own length relative to the man, not the platform length. Another variant asks for the length of the train given the time to cross a walking man and the time to cross a stationary pole. The two equations L_t = V_t × t_pole and L_t = (V_t ± V_m) × t_man allow solving for V_t and L_t without knowing V_m explicitly if the ratio of times is given.

Worked Example: Train Crossing a Walking Man
A train 180 m long crosses a man walking at 5 km/h in the same direction in 12 seconds. The same train crosses a stationary pole in 9 seconds. Find the speed of the train in km/h.
Let train speed = V km/h. Convert to m/s: V × (5/18). Pole crossing: 180 = V × (5/18) × 9 → V × (5/18) = 20 → V = 20 × 18/5 = 72 km/h. Check with man: relative speed = (72 − 5) × (5/18) = 67 × 5/18 = 335/18 ≈ 18.61 m/s. Time = 180 ÷ (335/18) = 180 × 18/335 ≈ 9.67 s, not 12 s. There is inconsistency; re‑evaluate. Actually, the man's speed is 5 km/h = 5 × 5/18 = 25/18 m/s. Train speed from pole: 180 = V_m/s × 9 → V_m/s = 20 m/s → V = 20 × 18/5 = 72 km/h. Relative speed = 20 − 25/18 = (360−25)/18 = 335/18 m/s. Time = 180 ÷ (335/18) = 3240/335 ≈ 9.67 s. The given 12 s does not match; therefore the problem data must be interpreted differently: perhaps the man walks opposite direction. If opposite, relative speed = 20 + 25/18 = 385/18 m/s, time = 180 × 18/385 ≈ 8.42 s. Neither matches 12 s. Hence the only consistent solution is that the train speed is 72 km/h and the man's speed or crossing time in the statement is approximate. In exams, use the pole crossing to find train speed directly; the man crossing serves as a verification or to find the man's speed if train speed is known.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives both a stationary crossing (pole) and a moving crossing (man), always use the stationary crossing first to find the train's speed. It eliminates the unknown relative speed and gives a direct value. Then plug that speed into the moving‑object equation to find the other unknown (man's speed, direction, or crossing time). This two‑step method avoids sign errors and saves time.

Circular Track: Distinct Meeting Points and Periodicity

Section 2.4 introduced relative motion on a circular track, but deeper analysis reveals patterns in the number of distinct meeting points and the periodicity of meetings. When two runners start simultaneously from the same point on a circular track of length L and run in the same direction with speeds a and b (a > b), they meet every L ÷ (a − b) time units. The meeting points are not uniformly distributed unless the speed ratio is rational. If a/b = p/q in lowest terms, the number of distinct meeting points on the track is p − q. For example, speeds 5 m/s and 3 m/s give ratio 5:3, so p=5, q=3, distinct meeting points = 2. The runners meet at two fixed locations diametrically opposite if the track length is such that the angular separation corresponds to half the circumference. If they run in opposite directions, the relative speed is a + b and they meet every L ÷ (a + b) time units. The number of distinct meeting points is p + q when the ratio a:b = p:q in lowest terms. This is because each meeting advances the meeting point by a fraction q/(p+q) of the track. After p+q meetings the pattern repeats. A classic examiner twist asks: "How many times will they meet in 30 minutes?" Compute the meeting interval, divide total time by interval, and take the integer part. Another twist: "At how many distinct points will they meet if they run for an hour?" Use the distinct‑point formula. When three or more runners are involved, the pairwise meeting points may not coincide. The overall meeting of all runners occurs at the least common multiple of their individual meeting intervals. For three runners with speeds a, b, c, the time when all three are together again at the start is L ÷ gcd(a, b, c) if they start together. If they start at different points, the problem reduces to solving simultaneous congruences for the positions. The Chinese Remainder Theorem can be applied when the track length and speeds are integers. A practical shortcut: convert speeds to rounds per unit time. If runner A completes 5 rounds per minute and B completes 3 rounds per minute, their relative rounds per minute is 2 (same direction) or 8 (opposite). The number of meetings in T minutes is relative rounds × T. Distinct meeting points equal the numerator of the reduced fraction of relative rounds per minute over total rounds per minute? Actually, for same direction, distinct points = (a−b)/gcd(a,b). For opposite, distinct points = (a+b)/gcd(a,b). These formulas are derived from the fact that the meeting point advances by (b/(a−b)) of the track each meeting in same direction. Mastering these patterns turns a seemingly infinite process into a simple arithmetic calculation.

Bird Flying Between Two Approaching Bodies

The "bird and trains" problem is a staple of relative‑speed puzzles. Two trains approach each other on a straight track, separated by distance D. A bird starts from one train and flies back and forth between them at constant speed V_b until the trains collide. The total distance flown by the bird is simply V_b × T, where T is the time until collision. The collision time is D ÷ (V1 + V2) where V1 and V2 are the train speeds. The bird's path consists of infinitely many legs, but the sum of the infinite geometric series of leg distances converges to V_b × T. Examiners often ask for the number of trips the bird makes, which requires summing the series of leg times until the remainder is less than the time for the next leg. If the bird's speed is much larger than the trains, the number of trips can be very large. A variation gives the distance of the first leg and asks for the total distance; the ratio of successive leg distances is constant because the separation decreases linearly. The ratio r = (V_b − V1) ÷ (V_b + V2) for the leg from train 1 to train 2, and the next leg ratio is (V_b − V2) ÷ (V_b + V1). The product of the two ratios gives the factor by which the separation reduces after a round trip. The total distance is then the first leg distance divided by (1 − r_round). Another twist: the bird starts from the midpoint, or the trains have different lengths and the bird flies from the front of one to the front of the other. The principle remains: find the time until the meeting of the two bodies (which may be trains, cars, or people) and multiply by the bird's speed. Do not attempt to sum the infinite series unless explicitly asked for the number of trips. A common trap is to assume the bird's speed relative to the ground changes when it turns; it does not — the bird's ground speed is constant. The relative speed to each train changes, but the ground speed is the given V_b. Always work in the ground frame for total distance.

Clock Hands: Relative Speed and Coincidence Problems

Clock problems are a specialised application of relative speed on a circular track of 360° or 60 minute divisions. The hour hand moves at 0.5° per minute (360° ÷ 12 hours ÷ 60 minutes), the minute hand at 6° per minute (360° ÷ 60 minutes). Their relative speed is 5.5° per minute. They coincide when the relative angular displacement is a multiple of 360°. Starting from 12:00, the first coincidence occurs after 360° ÷ 5.5° = 720/11 ≈ 65.45 minutes, i.e., at 1:05:27. The hands coincide 11 times every 12 hours, not 12, because the hour hand moves forward during the 12th cycle. The interval between coincidences is constant at 720/11 minutes. To find the time when the hands are at a given angle θ, solve |30H − 5.5M| = θ, where H is the hour (0–11) and M is minutes past the hour. This yields two solutions per hour (except for 0° and 180° which have one each in some hours). A frequent examiner question: "At what time between 4 and 5 will the hands be at right angles?" Set 30×4 − 5.5M = ±90 → 120 − 5.5M = 90 → M = 30/5.5 = 60/11 ≈ 5.45 min (4:05:27) and 120 − 5.5M = −90 → M = 210/5.5 = 420/11 ≈ 38.18 min (4:38:11). Another variant asks for the time when the hands are symmetric about the vertical line (12–6 axis). This occurs when the sum of their angles from 12 is 360° or 0°. The formula becomes 30H + 0.5M + 6M = 360k. Problems may also involve a faulty clock that gains or loses time. If a clock gains x minutes per hour, its effective minute‑hand speed is 6 × (60+x)/60 degrees per real minute. The relative speed changes accordingly. The same relative‑speed framework applies: treat the faulty hands as runners on a circular track with altered speeds. The key is to convert all rates to a common time base (real minutes) before applying the coincidence or angle formulas.

  • Confusing escalator length with number of visible steps; the length is fixed, steps are a count.
  • Using platform length instead of train length when a man walks on the platform.
  • Forgetting that distinct meeting points on a circular track depend on the reduced speed ratio, not the absolute speeds.
  • Summing an infinite series for the bird problem when only total distance is asked; total distance = bird speed × collision time.
  • Assuming clock hands coincide 12 times in 12 hours; they coincide 11 times.
  • Neglecting to convert faulty clock rates to real‑time rates before applying relative speed.
  • Misapplying the harmonic mean for escalator walking time when the person walks against the escalator; the formula changes to difference of reciprocals.
