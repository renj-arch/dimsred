Mastering Time, Work, and Efficiency for the exam

Welcome to this comprehensive lesson on Time & Work, a high-yield topic in the mathematics module. This section explores the mathematical relationship between the speed of workers (efficiency), the duration they work, and the total quantity of work completed. We will also extend these principles to Pipes & Cisterns problems and scenarios involving alternating work schedules. By mastering these concepts, you will be able to solve complex questions quickly during the exam.

Core Concepts: Efficiency, Time, and Work

The fundamental rule of this topic is that Work Done is directly proportional to Efficiency and directly proportional to Time. Efficiency is defined as the amount of work a person or machine can complete in one unit of time. If Person A takes 10 days to finish a job, their daily efficiency is calculated as 1/10th of the total job. Conversely, if Person B takes 5 days, their efficiency is 1/5th. Notice that higher efficiency results in less time required to complete the same task. Combined Work occurs when two or more people work together; in this case, you simply add their individual daily efficiencies (e.g., 1/10 + 1/5) to find the total rate of completion per day. The total number of days required is found by dividing the total work (usually considered as 1 unit) by this combined daily rate.

Pipes & Cisterns: A Variation of Time & Work

Problems involving pipes filling or emptying a tank are mathematically identical to Time & Work problems, provided you understand the sign convention for rates. In these scenarios, Filling Pipes add water to the tank, representing positive work similar to a worker building something. Emptying Pipes remove water from the tank, representing negative work or a hindrance to the process. The net rate of change in the tank's volume is calculated by adding the filling rates and subtracting the emptying rates. For example, if Pipe A fills a tank in 10 hours and Pipe B empties it in 20 hours, the combined rate is (1/10) - (1/20). If both pipes are opened simultaneously, you must calculate this net difference to determine how long the tank will take to fill or empty.

Alternate Day Work

This advanced pattern occurs when two workers do not work together every day but instead take turns. For instance, A works on Monday and Wednesday, while B works on Tuesday and Thursday. To solve this, first calculate the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of the days taken by each worker to complete the job individually; let us assume this LCM is 60 units of work for simplicity. If A takes 15 days, their efficiency is 4 units/day. If B takes 20 days, their efficiency is 3 units/day. In an alternate schedule where they work on different days, you calculate the total work done in one full cycle (A's turn + B's turn). If this combined work per cycle exceeds the LCM, the job finishes within that cycle; if not, you continue adding cycles until the remaining work is less than or equal to the work capacity of the person whose turn it is next.

Solved Examples

Example 1: Standard Combined Work
A can complete a piece of work in 10 days, and B can complete the same work in 15 days. If they work together, how many days will it take them to finish the work?

a) 5 days
b) 6 days
c) 7 days
d) 8 days
Solution: The correct answer is b) 6 days.

Step-by-step reasoning:
1. Assume the total work is 1 unit.
2. A's efficiency = 1/10 per day.
3. B's efficiency = 1/15 per day.
4. Combined efficiency = (1/10) + (1/15).
5. To add fractions, find the LCM of 10 and 15, which is 30.
6. Convert to common denominator: (3/30) + (2/30) = 5/30.
7. Simplify the fraction: 5/30 reduces to 1/6.
8. This means together they complete 1/6th of the work in one day.
9. Therefore, total days required = 1 / (1/6) = 6 days.
Example 2: Pipes & Cisterns with an Outlet
Pipe A can fill a tank in 10 hours, and Pipe B can empty the same tank in 20 hours. If both pipes are opened together, how long will it take to fill the tank?

a) 15 hours
b) 20 hours
c) 30 hours
d) 40 hours
Solution: The correct answer is c) 30 hours.

Solution breakdown:
1. Treat filling as positive work and emptying as negative work.
2. Rate of Pipe A (filling) = +1/10 per hour.
3. Rate of Pipe B (emptying) = -1/20 per hour.
4. Net rate = (1/10) - (1/20).
5. Common denominator is 20: (2/20) - (1/20) = 1/20.
6. The net result is that 1/20th of the tank is filled every hour.
7. Total time to fill = 1 / (1/20) = 20 hours?

Common Traps

Students often fall into specific pitfalls that lead to incorrect answers.

  • Mistaking Inverse Proportionality: Students sometimes think if A is twice as efficient, they take double the time. This is wrong; higher efficiency means LESS time taken.
  • Ignoring Negative Signs in Pipes: When an outlet pipe is involved, forgetting to subtract its rate leads to adding two positive numbers, resulting in a time that is too short.
  • Wrong LCM Calculation: In alternate work problems, using the wrong Least Common Multiple for the total work unit makes the arithmetic messy and prone to error. Always use the LCM of the individual days taken.

Memory Tricks

To remember these concepts quickly during the exam, use these analogies.

  • The "Speed vs Time" Rule: Think of efficiency like speed in a car journey. If you drive faster (high efficiency), you reach your destination sooner (low time). Speed and Time are always inversely related.
  • The "Bank Account" Analogy for Pipes: Imagine the tank is a bank account. Filling pipes are deposits (+), and emptying pipes are withdrawals (-). Your net balance grows only if deposits exceed withdrawals.
  • The "Fraction Sum" Shortcut: When adding fractions like 1/10 + 1/15, remember that the numerator of the answer is usually small (like 1 or 2) and the denominator is the LCM. If you get a complex fraction, check your addition.

Exam Day Strategy
On exam day, do not spend time finding the actual amount of work done unless necessary. Always assume the total work is the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of the days given in the question. This converts all fractions into whole numbers, making addition and subtraction much faster and less prone to calculation errors. For example, if one person takes 10 days and another takes 15 days, assume the total work is 30 units. Then their efficiencies are simply 3 units/day and 2 units/day respectively.

Work Equivalence and Man‑Day Calculations

Man‑days (person‑days) provide a universal unit for comparing work across groups of varying sizes and efficiencies. One man‑day is the work a standard‑efficiency worker completes in one day. Different efficiencies are converted to equivalent standard man‑days. If A is twice as efficient as standard, one day of A’s work equals two man‑days. Total project work is expressed as a fixed number of man‑days, W. With n workers of varying efficiencies, daily man‑day contribution is the sum of their efficiency factors. Required time = W ÷ (sum of daily efficiency factors). This avoids fractions and handles mid‑project entry/exit by adjusting the daily sum from the change day.

Scaling the Work Unit

Using the LCM of individual times as total work is a practical shortcut. If A finishes in 12 days and B in 18 days, LCM(12, 18) = 36. Treat the job as 36 units. A’s efficiency = 3 units/day; B’s = 2 units/day. Combined output = 5 units/day; time = 36 ÷ 5 = 7.2 days. The method extends to any number of workers and to pipes/cisterns (filling = positive, emptying = negative).

Handling Partial Contributions

For partial-day work, multiply daily efficiency by the fraction worked. A worker at 4 units/day working half a day contributes 2 units. Linear scaling assumes constant efficiency over the period. If efficiency changes mid‑project, split the timeline into constant‑efficiency phases, compute work per phase, and sum.

Variable Efficiency and Partial Work

Real work often involves non‑constant efficiency. Workers may speed up with experience or slow down from fatigue. Pipes may develop leaks, reducing fill rates. Divide the timeline into constant‑efficiency intervals. Work per interval = efficiency × interval length. Sum of interval work = total work. Known total work lets you solve for unknown interval lengths algebraically.

Workers Joining or Leaving Mid‑Project

Take a 120‑unit project. A starts alone at 5 units/day. After 4 days, B (3 units/day) joins. Three days later, A leaves. First 4 days: 5 × 4 = 20 units. Next 3 days: combined 8 units/day → 24 units. Remaining = 76 units; B alone at 3 units/day → 76 ÷ 3 ≈ 25.33 days. Total = 4 + 3 + 25.33 = 32.33 days. This piecewise method avoids the error of averaging efficiencies over the whole period.

Efficiency Change Due to External Factors

In pipe problems, a leak appearing at half‑full reduces the net fill rate. Treat as two stages: pre‑leak (net rate = sum of inlets) and post‑leak (net rate = sum of inlets − leak rate). Compute time to half‑full at first rate, then time for remaining half at reduced rate. Same logic applies when worker efficiency drops due to health or tool wear.

Worked Example: Variable Efficiency with Mid‑Project Entry
A can complete a task in 20 days. B can complete the same task in 30 days. A starts the work alone. After 5 days, B joins. After another 4 days, A leaves and B finishes the remaining work alone. How many total days are required to complete the task?

Step 1: Total work = LCM(20, 30) = 60 units.

Step 2: A’s efficiency = 60 ÷ 20 = 3 units/day. B’s efficiency = 60 ÷ 30 = 2 units/day.

Step 3: Work by A in first 5 days = 3 × 5 = 15 units.

Step 4: Combined efficiency = 3 + 2 = 5 units/day. Work in next 4 days = 5 × 4 = 20 units.

Step 5: Cumulative work = 15 + 20 = 35 units. Remaining = 60 − 35 = 25 units.

Step 6: B alone at 2 units/day → time = 25 ÷ 2 = 12.5 days.

Step 7: Total days = 5 + 4 + 12.5 = 21.5 days.

Exam‑Day Strategy for Piecewise Work
When a problem describes changes in workforce or pipe status, draw a timeline and label each segment with the constant efficiency. Write the work done in each segment as a product (efficiency × days). This visual breakdown prevents the common error of applying a single average efficiency to the whole period.

Multiple Inlet and Outlet Pipes with Time Gaps

Pipes/cisterns problems get harder when pipes aren't opened simultaneously. Net fill rate changes whenever a pipe opens or closes. Treat the process as sequential phases, each with a fixed set of active pipes. Per phase: net rate = sum of inlets − sum of outlets; volume = net rate × phase duration. Tank capacity = LCM of individual times, giving whole‑number rates per hour.

Sequential Opening and Closing

Example: A fills in 10 hr, B in 15 hr, C empties in 30 hr. A opens at t = 0. At 2 hr, B opens. At 5 hr, C opens. Capacity = LCM(10, 15, 30) = 30 units. Rates: A = 3, B = 2, C = −1 units/hr. Phase 1 (0–2 hr): A only → 6 units. Phase 2 (2–5 hr): A+B → 15 units. Cumulative = 21 units. Phase 3 (5 hr+): A+B+C → net 4 units/hr. Remaining 9 units → 9 ÷ 4 = 2.25 hr. Total = 7.25 hr.

Intermittent Operation

Some problems have pipes operating cyclically (on/off). Treat each cycle as a block: compute net work/cycle, find full cycles needed, then handle remainder with a partial cycle. This mirrors alternate‑day logic with continuous time.

Alternate Day Work with Breaks and Changing Efficiencies

Basic alternate‑day model: two workers alternate daily without breaks. Variants add rest days, efficiency changes after cycles, or a third worker. Core technique: define the smallest repeating work‑day pattern as a cycle, compute work/cycle, find how many full cycles fit total work. If remainder ≤ next worker’s daily capacity, job finishes on their turn; else an extra partial day is needed.

Rest Days in the Rotation

Example: A works Day 1, rests Day 2; B works Day 3, rests Day 4; repeat. Cycle = 4 days, 2 work days. Work/cycle = A’s efficiency + B’s efficiency. Total work = LCM of individual completion times. Full cycles = total work ÷ work/cycle; process remainder day by day.

Efficiency Upgrade After Fixed Cycles

A may become 50% more efficient after 3 cycles. Split timeline: first 3 cycles at original efficiency, rest at upgraded. Compute work in first 3 cycles, subtract from total, continue with new per‑cycle work. Avoids retroactively applying upgraded efficiency.

Combined Work with Wage Distribution and Share Calculation

Wages are typically split in proportion to work contributed. Worker’s share = (worker’s work units ÷ total work units) × total wages. With varying efficiencies/durations, contribution = efficiency × days worked. Contribution ratios determine wage split. Applies to pipes too: inlet contributions determine payout; outlets are a cost.

Wages with Idle Time

Idle time (e.g., waiting for materials) doesn't count toward share. Problems usually specify attendance‑ vs output‑based wages. Output‑based: idle time = zero contribution.

Multiple Contractors and Sub‑Contracting

Main contractors may hire simultaneous sub‑contractors. Main contractor often gets fixed % + share of remaining profit proportional to their direct work. Let total profit = P. Main contractor = fixed % + (own work ÷ total work) × remaining profit. Solve for unknowns given total profit or individual shares.

  • Using a single average efficiency when efficiencies change at known points.
  • Not converting pipe rates to a common unit (LCM) before adding/subtracting.
  • Treating alternate‑day work as simple addition ignoring turn order.
  • Ignoring rest days/breaks, overestimating work per cycle.
  • Applying upgraded efficiency retroactively to earlier cycles.
  • Dividing wages equally instead of by work contributed.
  • Review these traps before the exam.

Work with Linearly Varying Efficiency

In many practical situations a worker’s efficiency changes by a fixed amount each day — for example, a trainee who becomes faster by a constant increment, or a machine that loses a fixed amount of output due to wear. The daily efficiency then forms an arithmetic progression. If the first‑day efficiency is e units and the daily increment is d units, the efficiency on day k is e + (k‑1)d. The total work done in n days is the sum of this progression: W = n/2 × [2e + (n‑1)d]. When the total work W is known, the equation becomes a quadratic in n and can be solved by the standard formula. If the efficiency decreases (negative d), the same formula applies provided the efficiency never becomes negative before the work finishes; otherwise the model must be truncated at the day efficiency reaches zero.

A common twist gives the time taken by two workers with different linear patterns and asks for the day on which their cumulative outputs become equal. Set the two quadratic expressions equal and solve for the integer day. Because the quadratic may yield a non‑integer root, the answer is the first whole day after the root when the leading worker’s cumulative work exceeds the other’s. Always verify that the efficiency on that day is still positive for both workers.

When the problem states “efficiency increases by 10 % each day”, the progression is geometric, not arithmetic. The daily efficiency becomes e × r^(k‑1) with r = 1.10. The sum of a geometric series is W = e × (r^n – 1) / (r – 1). Solving for n requires logarithms: n = log[1 + W(r‑1)/e] / log r. In exam conditions, the numbers are chosen so that n is an integer or a simple fraction, allowing a quick check by trial.

If a worker’s efficiency changes only after a fixed number of days (step change), treat the timeline as two separate constant‑efficiency phases. Compute work in the first phase, subtract from total, then apply the new efficiency for the remaining work. This piecewise approach avoids the mistake of averaging the two efficiencies over the whole period.

Pipes with Variable Leak Rates and Partial Closure

Standard pipe‑cistern problems assume a constant leak rate. Advanced variants introduce a leak whose rate changes at known instants — for instance, a crack that widens after a certain volume has escaped, or a valve that is partially closed reducing the outflow by a fixed percentage. The solution technique remains phase‑wise: divide the filling process into intervals where the set of active inlets, outlets, and the leak rate are all constant. For each phase compute the net rate = Σ(inlet rates) – Σ(outlet rates) – leak rate. Multiply by the phase duration to obtain the volume filled in that phase. Accumulate volumes until the tank capacity (taken as LCM of all individual times) is reached.

When the leak rate is expressed as a fraction of the current water level (e.g., “the leak empties 5 % of the water present each hour”), the differential equation dV/dt = net inlet – kV appears. In competitive exams this is rarely required; instead the leak is given as a constant volume per hour that changes only at discrete moments. If a problem does present a proportional leak, the intended method is to For a proportional leak, the rate is proportional to the instantaneous volume, requiring a differential equation or exponential decay model, not a constant rate based on full capacity., because the capacity is fixed and the leak rate is proportional to the current water volume, not the full tank capacity; solve using exponential decay or geometric progression.

Partial closure of an outlet pipe means its emptying rate is reduced by a given factor. For example, “pipe C is half‑closed after 3 hours” translates to: for the first 3 hours C’s rate = –(capacity/individual time); thereafter C’s rate = –½ × (capacity/individual time). The phase boundaries are exactly the moments of closure change. Always recompute the LCM‑based unit rates after each change to keep arithmetic in integers.

A frequent examiner trap asks for the time when the tank becomes exactly half full while a leak rate changes at that same instant. The correct approach: compute the cumulative volume at the end of each phase; if the half‑capacity point lies inside a phase, solve V_start + net_rate × t = ½ capacity for t within that phase. Do not assume the phase change coincides with the half‑full moment unless the numbers force it.

Worked Example: Linearly Increasing Efficiency
A worker starts a job with an efficiency of 4 units per day. His efficiency increases by 1 unit each subsequent day. The total work is 120 units. In how many days will the job be completed?
Let the number of days be n. First‑day efficiency e = 4, daily increment d = 1. Total work W = n/2 × [2e + (n‑1)d] = n/2 × [8 + (n‑1)] = n/2 × (n + 7). Set equal to 120: n(n + 7) = 240 → n² + 7n – 240 = 0. Solve quadratic: discriminant = 49 + 960 = 1009. √1009 ≈ 31.76. Positive root n = (‑7 + 31.76)/2 ≈ 12.38. Since work is completed only at the end of a full day, check n = 12: work = 12/2 × (12+7) = 6 × 19 = 114 units (short). n = 13: work = 13/2 × 20 = 130 units (exceeds). Therefore the job finishes on the 13th day, with the last day’s work partially used. Answer: 13 days.
Exam‑Day Strategy for Variable Efficiency
When you see “efficiency increases/decreases by a constant amount each day”, immediately write the arithmetic‑series sum formula. Identify e, d, and W. Form the quadratic, compute the discriminant, and take the ceiling of the positive root. For geometric growth, write the geometric‑series sum, isolate r^n, and apply logarithms only if the numbers do not yield an integer n by inspection. Always verify the efficiency on the final day is still positive; if it turns negative, the model stops earlier and the remaining work must be done at zero efficiency (i.e., the job cannot finish under the given pattern).

Resource Allocation Across Multiple Tasks with Shared Workforce

Many real‑world problems involve a pool of workers who can be assigned to two or more independent tasks, either simultaneously (if the workforce is split) or sequentially (if the same workers move from one task to another). The key is to express each task’s work in a common unit — usually the LCM of the individual completion times — and then track the allocation of worker‑days. If a group of m workers can finish task A in t_A days, the total work for A is m × t_A worker‑days. When the same workers are later deployed to task B, the remaining worker‑days available for B equal the total worker‑days of the pool minus those already spent on A.

When the workforce is split, assign a variable x for the number of workers on task A and m‑x on task B. The time to complete each task becomes Work_A / x and Work_B / (m‑x). If the problem states both tasks finish simultaneously, equate the two times and solve for x. If one task finishes earlier, the freed workers join the other task, creating a piecewise schedule: first phase with split workforce, second phase with the full workforce on the remaining task. Compute work done in phase 1, subtract from the unfinished task, then divide by the full workforce rate.

A frequent variation adds a third task that starts only after the first task ends. Build a timeline: Phase 1 — workers split between task 1 and task 2; Phase 2 — task 1 done, all workers on task 2; Phase 3 — task 2 done, all workers on task 3. At each phase boundary recompute the remaining work for the active tasks. The LCM method keeps all rates as integers, avoiding fractions until the final division.

When workers have different efficiencies, convert each worker’s efficiency to the common unit (work units per day). The total work of a task is then the sum of (efficiency × days) for all workers assigned. Splitting the workforce means deciding how many efficiency‑units go to each task. The same algebraic approach applies, but the unknowns become the total efficiency allocated to each task rather than a headcount.

Contractual Penalties, Bonuses, and Net Earnings Calculation

Beyond simple wage sharing, competitive exams often embed penalty clauses (e.g., “₹500 deducted for each day of delay beyond the deadline”) or bonus clauses (e.g., “₹200 per day finished early”). The net earning of a contractor = base contract amount + bonus – penalty. To compute the bonus or penalty, first determine the actual completion time using the work‑rate methods already covered. Then compare with the stipulated deadline. If the contract specifies a per‑day penalty for each day the work extends beyond the deadline, multiply the excess days by the penalty rate. If a bonus is paid for each day saved, multiply the saved days by the bonus rate.

When multiple contractors share a project, each may have a different deadline and penalty structure. Treat each contractor’s portion as an independent work‑completion problem, compute their individual finish dates, then apply their respective penalty/bonus formulas. The total project cost is the sum of all net earnings. If the problem asks for the overall profit or loss for the principal employer, subtract the sum of net earnings from the total contract value.

A twist is a “liquidated damages” clause where the penalty is a fixed percentage of the contract value per week of delay. Convert the delay into weeks (rounding up to the next whole week if the clause says “per week or part thereof”). Then penalty = contract value × percentage × number of weeks. Ensure the percentage is applied to the correct base — sometimes the base is the contractor’s share, sometimes the whole contract.

Bonuses may be capped (e.g., “maximum bonus ₹10,000”). After computing the raw bonus, apply the cap before adding to the base amount. Similarly, penalties may have a floor (minimum deduction). Always read the wording: “for each day of delay” versus “for each week of delay” versus “a lump sum if delayed”. The distinction changes the arithmetic from linear to stepwise.

Work Completion under Uncertain Start Times and Overlapping Shifts

Some problems state that a worker or pipe starts “after a certain number of days” but the exact start day is not given directly; instead, a condition such as “the work was completed in 20 days” is provided, and the unknown start offset must be found. Let the unknown delay be d days. Write the work equation with d as a variable: work done before the delayed start + work done after = total work. Solve for d. Because the equation is linear in d (the delayed worker’s contribution is efficiency × (total days – d)), the solution is straightforward.

Overlapping shifts occur when two or more workers work together for a part of the day and separately for the rest. For example, “A works the first 6 hours, B works the last 6 hours, and both work together for the middle 4 hours”. Convert all times to a common unit (hours). Compute each worker’s total hours: A = 6 + 4 = 10 hours, B = 4 + 6 = 10 hours. If their hourly rates are known, total work = rate_A × 10 + rate_B × 10. If the total work is given, solve for the unknown rate or the unknown overlap duration.

When shifts are described in fractions of a day (e.g., “A works ⅔ of a day, B works ½ day, together they work ¼ day”), multiply each fraction by the daily efficiency to get the daily contribution. The sum of contributions over the whole project equals the total work. This is equivalent to the “partial contribution” concept but with the added complexity that the fractions may differ each day according to a schedule. Build a day‑by‑day table if the schedule repeats cyclically; otherwise sum the series directly.

A common examiner trap is to give the total calendar days including weekends when workers do not work, but the efficiencies are defined per working day. Convert calendar days to working days first (subtract rest days) before applying the efficiency formulas. If the rest‑day pattern is irregular (e.g., “every 5th day is a holiday”), compute the number of working days in the given calendar span using integer division and remainder, then proceed.

  • Assuming a linear efficiency change continues indefinitely without checking when efficiency would become zero or negative.
  • Treating a proportional leak as a constant‑rate outlet instead of recognising the differential‑equation nature (exam problems avoid this, but the trap appears in mis‑read questions).
  • Forgetting to recompute LCM‑based unit rates after a pipe’s closure percentage changes.
  • Equating split‑workforce times without confirming both tasks indeed finish simultaneously; often one finishes earlier and the workforce re‑allocates.
  • Applying penalty or bonus rates to the wrong base amount (contractor’s share vs. total contract).
  • Ignoring “per week or part thereof” wording, leading to under‑counting penalty weeks.
  • Using calendar days directly when efficiencies are per working day, especially with irregular holiday patterns.

Work with Piecewise Constant Efficiency Changes at Milestones

In many advanced problems the efficiency of a worker or a pipe changes when a predefined fraction of the total work is completed. For example, a pump may operate at 100 % capacity until the tank is half full, after which a pressure drop reduces its rate to 60 %. The standard LCM‑based unit‑work method still applies, but the work must be split into phases that correspond to the milestones. For each phase compute the effective rate (which may be a sum of several workers’ rates) and the amount of work belonging to that phase. The time for the phase equals phase‑work ÷ phase‑rate. Sum the phase times to obtain the total duration. If a milestone is expressed in terms of time rather than work (e.g., “after 3 days the efficiency drops”), first determine how much work is done in those 3 days at the initial rate, then treat the remainder as a new phase. When several workers have different milestone thresholds, the overall schedule becomes a sequence of intervals bounded by the earliest upcoming milestone. Build a timeline: sort all milestone events (work‑fraction or clock‑time) chronologically, then for each interval use the constant rates that apply during that interval. This piecewise‑constant approach avoids the common error of averaging the two efficiencies over the whole job.

Simultaneous Equations for Unknown Individual Efficiencies

Examiners frequently give combined completion times for different subsets of workers and ask for each worker’s individual time. Let the total work be 1 unit. If A and B together finish in 12 days, their combined rate is 1/12 per day. If B and C together finish in 15 days, their combined rate is 1/15 per day. If A and C together finish in 20 days, their combined rate is 1/20 per day. Denote the individual daily rates by a, b, c. The three equations are a + b = 1/12, b + c = 1/15, a + c = 1/20. Adding all three gives 2(a + b + c) = 1/12 + 1/15 + 1/20 = (5 + 4 + 3)/60 = 12/60 = 1/5, so a + b + c = 1/10. Subtract each pairwise equation from this sum to isolate each rate: c = (a + b + c) – (a + b) = 1/10 – 1/12 = (6 – 5)/60 = 1/60, so C alone takes 60 days. Similarly a = 1/10 – 1/15 = (3 – 2)/30 = 1/30 → 30 days, and b = 1/10 – 1/20 = (2 – 1)/20 = 1/20 → 20 days. The same linear‑system technique works for any number of workers provided enough independent combined‑time data are given. When the data involve three‑worker groups, write one equation per group and solve the resulting system (usually 3 × 3 or 4 × 4). Always express rates as work per day, not days per work, to keep the equations linear.

Technique: Solving for Individual Rates from Pairwise Data
Three workers X, Y, Z can complete a task in the following pairwise combinations: X and Y together take 10 days, Y and Z together take 12 days, and Z and X together take 15 days. Find the number of days each worker would take alone.
Let the total work be 1 unit. Daily rates: x, y, z. Equations: x + y = 1/10, y + z = 1/12, z + x = 1/15. Add all three: 2(x + y + z) = 1/10 + 1/12 + 1/15 = (6 + 5 + 4)/60 = 15/60 = 1/4. Hence x + y + z = 1/8. Now subtract pairwise sums: z = (x + y + z) – (x + y) = 1/8 – 1/10 = (5 – 4)/40 = 1/40 → Z alone = 40 days. x = 1/8 – 1/12 = (3 – 2)/24 = 1/24 → X alone = 24 days. y = 1/8 – 1/15 = (15 – 8)/120 = 7/120 → Y alone = 120/7 ≈ 17.14 days. Answer: X = 24 days, Y = 120/7 days, Z = 40 days.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When you see pairwise combined times, immediately set up the rate equations. Use the LCM of the given days as the total work unit to avoid fractions; for the example above, LCM(10,12,15)=60 units. Then x+y=6, y+z=5, z+x=4. Solve the integer system quickly: adding gives 2(x+y+z)=15 → x+y+z=7.5. Subtract to get z=1.5, x=2.5, y=3.5. Convert back: X = 60/2.5 = 24 days, Y = 60/3.5 = 120/7 days, Z = 60/1.5 = 40 days. Working with the LCM unit eliminates fractions until the final step.

Learning Curve and Efficiency Improvement Over Repeated Cycles

Some problems model a worker whose efficiency improves by a fixed percentage after each completed cycle (a cycle may be a day, a shift, or a fixed amount of work). If the initial daily output is W₀ and the efficiency increases by r % per cycle, the output on the n‑th cycle is W₀ × (1 + r/100)ⁿ⁻¹. The cumulative work after N cycles is the sum of a geometric progression: S_N = W₀ × [(1 + r/100)ᴺ – 1] ÷ (r/100). To find the number of cycles needed to finish a given total work Q, solve S_N ≥ Q for the smallest integer N. This yields (1 + r/100)ᴺ ≥ 1 + Q × (r/100) / W₀. Take logarithms: N ≥ log(1 + Qr/(100W₀)) / log(1 + r/100). If the efficiency cannot exceed a physical maximum (e.g., 200 % of the initial rate), cap the progression at that ceiling and treat the remaining cycles as constant‑rate. A frequent twist is a “learning‑curve reset” after a rest day: the efficiency reverts to the initial value, so the geometric series restarts. In such cases, compute the work done in each active block separately and sum them.

Break‑Even Analysis for Hiring Additional Workers

When a project faces a penalty for each day of delay, the employer may hire extra workers to finish earlier. Let the base team of n workers complete the job in D days at a daily wage cost C per worker. The total labor cost is n × C × D. If the deadline is T days (T < D), the penalty per day of delay is P. Hiring k additional workers reduces the duration to D' = (n × D) / (n + k) assuming perfect scalability (work is perfectly divisible). The new labor cost becomes (n + k) × C × D'. The penalty cost is max(0, D' – T) × P. The total cost function is f(k) = (n + k) C D' + P × max(0, D' – T). Substitute D' = nD/(n+k) to get f(k) = n D C + k C nD/(n+k) + P × max(0, nD/(n+k) – T). The first term is constant. The optimal integer k can be found by evaluating f(k) for k = 0,1,2,… until the total cost stops decreasing. A marginal analysis gives insight: the marginal labor cost of an extra worker is approximately C n²D/(n+k)², while the marginal penalty saving is P nD/(n+k)² (while D' > T). Equating these suggests hiring is beneficial when C n > P, but the discrete evaluation of f(k) is the reliable method and avoids calculus approximations. This cost‑benefit check is often the intended solution.

  • Assuming a piecewise efficiency change applies to the whole remaining work instead of only the portion after the milestone.
  • Writing combined‑time equations in terms of days per work rather than work per day, leading to non‑linear equations.
  • Treating a learning‑curve improvement as linear (adding a fixed amount each cycle) instead of geometric (multiplying by a factor).
  • Forgetting to cap the efficiency at the stated maximum, causing the geometric sum to over‑estimate work done.
  • Using average labor cost instead of marginal cost when deciding how many extra workers to hire.
  • Neglecting to use a consistent total-work unit (e.g., a single LCM) across all phases in a piecewise‑constant problem.
  • Confusing a simplified break‑even rule (such as C = P) with the true minimum-cost condition; the optimum may occur at the deadline boundary regardless of the marginal comparison.

These advanced techniques extend the core time‑work toolkit and appear regularly in high‑difficulty quantitative sections.
