Surgery Theory — Handle Decompositions, the s-Cobordism Theorem & the Classification of Manifolds

Surgery theory studies manifolds by modifying them — cutting out a standard piece Sk × Dn−k and gluing in Dk+1 × Sn−k−1 — a process that changes the manifold's topology while preserving its bordism class. The theory is built on handle decompositions from Morse theory, the Whitney trick for eliminating intersections, the s-cobordism theorem (cobordisms with vanishing Whitehead torsion are products), Wall's surgery exact sequence relating normal maps to surgery obstruction groups Ln(π), the Farrell-Jones conjecture for L-theory, and the Borel conjecture (topological rigidity of aspherical manifolds). Together these form the most powerful toolkit for constructing and classifying manifolds in dimensions ≥ 5.

"Surgery is the most powerful tool for constructing and classifying manifolds."
— C. T. C. Wall
Part 1

📏 Handle Decompositions and Morse Theory

Morse Theory
Morse Functions, Handle Attachments, and CW-Complex Structures
A Morse function f: M → ℝ on a closed n-manifold M is a smooth function with non-degenerate critical points — the Hessian at each critical point is invertible. The index of a critical point is the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian. The sublevel sets Mc = f−1((−∞, c]) give a handle decomposition: as c passes a critical point of index i, an i-handle Di × Dn−i is attached to the boundary along its attaching sphere Si−1 × Dn−i. The handlebody decomposition writes M = (Dn ∪ handles of index 1 ∪ ... ∪ handles of index n). The gradient-like vector field gives a deformation retraction onto a CW-complex with one i-cell for each critical point of index i. The attaching map of an i-handle is an embedding φ: Si−1 → ∂(M<c). Handle cancellation: a pair of handles (i, i+1) with complementary attaching spheres can be cancelled if the attaching sphere of the (i+1)-handle bounds a disk in the i-skeleton that is disjoint from the core of the i-handle — this is the key to simplifying handle decompositions.
A Morse function encodes the manifold's topology as a sequence of handle attachments — each critical point adds a cell of dimension equal to its index, yielding a CW-complex homotopy equivalent to M.
Part 2

🌍 The Whitney Trick and Embeddings

Whitney
The Whitney Embedding Theorem, the Whitney Trick, and the h-Cobordism Theorem
The Whitney embedding theorem: any smooth n-manifold embeds in ℝ2n (weak form) and in ℝ2n+1 (strong form — the Whitney immersion theorem). The Whitney trick: given two transverse immersed submanifolds of complementary dimension with algebraically canceling intersection points, if there exists an embedded Whitney disk (a 2-disk bounded by arcs connecting opposite intersection points), the intersections can be eliminated by isotopy over the disk. The Whitney trick works in dimensions ≥ 5 because the disk can be embedded by general position (the 2-disk avoids self-intersections in a 5-dimensional ambient space). The h-cobordism theorem (Smale, 1962): if W is a compact (n+1)-dimensional cobordism between M0 and M1 with n ≥ 5, and W is simply connected with H*(W, M0) = 0 (an h-cobordism), then W ≅ M0 × [0,1]. This implies the Poincaré conjecture in dimensions ≥ 5: a homotopy n-sphere (n ≥ 5) is homeomorphic to Sn.
The Whitney trick is the central geometric tool for eliminating intersections in high dimensions; the h-cobordism theorem shows that homotopy equivalence implies product structure under simple connectivity.
Part 3

🧮 The s-Cobordism Theorem and Whitehead Torsion

Whitehead
Whitehead Torsion, the Whitehead Group, and the s-Cobordism Theorem
For a homotopy equivalence f: X → Y with isomorphic fundamental groups, the Whitehead torsion τ(f) lives in the Whitehead group Wh(π1(Y)). The Whitehead group is defined as Wh(π) = K1(ℤ[π]) / {±π}, where K1 of the group ring ℤ[π] consists of invertible matrices modulo commutators. An h-cobordism W from M0 to M1 (n ≥ 5) has a torsion τ(W, M0) ∈ Wh(π1(M0)) — this measures the obstruction to being a product. The s-cobordism theorem (Barden, Mazur, Stallings, 1963): for n ≥ 5, an h-cobordism W with τ(W, M0) = 0 (called an s-cobordism) is a product W ≅ M0 × [0,1] rel M0. The s-cobordism theorem gives the definitive classification: h-cobordisms are classified by the Whitehead group Wh(π1(M0)). For finite cyclic groups: Wh(ℤ) = 0, which reproves the Poincaré conjecture in dimension ≥ 5. For ℤ⊕ℤ: Wh(ℤ⊕ℤ) = ℤ⊕ℤ, so there exist nontrivial h-cobordisms on tori.
The s-cobordism theorem refines the h-cobordism theorem: when the Whitehead torsion vanishes, the cobordism is a product; the torsion provides a complete classification of h-cobordisms.
Part 4

🔥 The Surgery Exact Sequence and the Borel Conjecture

Wall
The Surgery Exact Sequence, Wall Groups Ln(π), and the Borel Conjecture
The surgery exact sequence (Wall): Ωn+1(X) → Ln+1(π1(X)) → S(X) → Ωn(X) → Ln(π1(X)), where Ωn(X) is the normal cobordism group of degree 1 normal maps to X, Ln(π) are the Wall surgery obstruction groups (quadratic forms over ℤ[π]), and S(X) is the structure set of manifolds homotopy equivalent to X. The L-groups appear in two flavors: Lnh(π) (for h-cobordisms) and Lns(π) (for s-cobordisms). For the trivial group π = {1}: Ln(ℤ) has L0 ≅ ℤ (classified by signature/8), L2 ≅ ℤ/2 (Arf invariant), and Lodd = 0. The Farrell-Jones conjecture: the L-theory assembly map Hn(BG; L(ℤ)) → Ln(ℤ[G]) is an isomorphism for all groups G. This implies the Borel conjecture: any homotopy equivalence between closed aspherical manifolds is homotopic to a homeomorphism (topological rigidity). The Farrell-Jones conjecture also implies the Novikov conjecture on higher signatures — the homotopy invariance of higher signatures ⟨L(M) ∪ f*c, [M]⟩ for c ∈ H*(Bπ, ℚ).
The surgery exact sequence reduces the classification of manifolds to algebraic topology — the L-groups and assembly maps; the Farrell-Jones conjecture asserts that L-theory of group rings is computable from homology.

Normal Maps and Degree-One Maps in Surgery Theory

A normal map is a pair (f, b) where f : M → X is a degree‑one map between closed n‑manifolds and b : ν_M → ξ is a bundle map covering f from the stable normal bundle of M to a vector bundle ξ over X. The degree‑one condition means f_*[M] = [X] in homology, so f induces an isomorphism on top‑dimensional homology with integer coefficients. Normal maps are the geometric input for the surgery exact sequence: they represent elements of the normal cobordism group Ω_n(X). Two normal maps (f0,b0) and (f1,b1) are normally cobordant if there exists a normal map (F,B) : (W,∂W) → (X×[0,1], X×{0,1}) restricting to them on the boundary.

The construction of a normal map from a given homotopy equivalence uses the Spivak normal fibration of X. For a Poincaré complex X there is a canonical spherical fibration ν_X; a normal map exists precisely when the Spivak fibration lifts to a vector bundle. This lifting problem is measured by the primary obstruction in Hn+1(X; π_n(BO)). When X is a manifold, the identity map gives the trivial normal map. The set of normal invariants Ω_n(X) is isomorphic to [X, G/O] (or [X, G/TOP] in the topological category) where G/O is the homotopy fibre of BO → BG. This identification turns the geometric classification problem into a homotopy‑theoretic computation.

Degree‑one maps can be altered by surgery on the domain. If f is k‑connected, one can perform surgery on embedded spheres S^k ⊂ M that map trivially in X to increase connectivity. The process stops when f becomes a homotopy equivalence; the obstruction to the final step lies in the Wall group L_n(π1 X). The normal map framework therefore separates the problem into two stages: (1) find a normal map in the desired normal cobordism class, (2) do surgery to kill the kernel of f_* on homotopy groups. The first stage is purely homotopical; the second is algebraic.

Important exceptions occur when π1 X has torsion. In that case, the lifting of the Spivak fibration may involve more intricate considerations, and while the normal invariant set Ω_n(X) is a group, the maps in the surgery exact sequence are generally only exact as pointed sets. Examiners often test the distinction between the smooth and topological categories: G/O versus G/TOP and the resulting difference in L‑groups (e.g. L_n^s(ℤ) = 0 for odd n in the smooth case but L_n^h(ℤ) = ℤ/2 for n ≡ 1 mod 4 in the topological case).

Algebraic Surgery Obstructions and the L‑Groups

The Wall groups L_n(π) are the algebraic avatars of the geometric surgery obstruction. For a group π they are defined as the cobordism groups of non‑singular quadratic forms over the group ring ℤ[π] (even dimensions) or formations (odd dimensions). The quadratic refinement encodes the self‑intersection data of the middle‑dimensional spheres used in surgery. When π = 1 the groups are classical: L0(ℤ) ≅ ℤ (signature), L2(ℤ) ≅ ℤ/2 (Arf invariant), and Lodd(ℤ) = 0. For a general π the groups are 4‑periodic: Ln+4(π) ≅ L_n(π).

There are two main flavours: the h‑decorated groups L_n^h(π) (for h‑cobordisms) and the s‑decorated groups L_n^s(π) (for s‑cobordisms). The difference is measured by the Whitehead group Wh(π): there is a Rothenberg exact sequence … → L_n^s(π) → L_n^h(π) → Wh(π) → Ln-1^s(π) → …. For finite groups the L‑groups are finite and have been computed for many families (e.g. cyclic, dihedral, quaternion). For infinite groups such as ℤ or surface groups the groups are often infinitely generated; the Farrell–Jones conjecture predicts they are assembled from the homology of Bπ with coefficients in the L‑theory spectrum.

The surgery obstruction map θ : Ω_n(X) → L_n(π1 X) sends a normal map to an algebraic quadratic form defined on the kernel of the map induced by f on the middle-dimensional homology with local coefficients (or on the universal cover). The form is defined by the intersection pairing of the lifted spheres in the universal cover. If θ(f,b) = 0 the normal map is normally cobordant to a homotopy equivalence. The map is not a homomorphism in general; it is a map of pointed sets. The exactness of the surgery sequence at the structure set S(X) implies that an element in the normal invariant set Ω_n(X) corresponds to a homotopy equivalence if and only if its surgery obstruction in L_n(π₁ X) vanishes.

Examiner twists often involve the distinction between the projective and free L‑groups. The projective groups L_n^p(π) use finitely generated projective ℤ[π]‑modules; the free groups use free modules. For groups with non‑trivial K0(ℤ[π]) the two differ. The assembly map H_n(Bπ; L(ℤ)) → L_n(ℤ[π]) is conjectured to be an isomorphism, and this has been proven for a large class of groups, including hyperbolic groups and CAT(0) groups. This reduces the computation of L_n(π) to a homology calculation, a fact that appears in advanced problem‑solving questions.

Worked Example: Computing a Surgery Obstruction
Let M be a closed 4‑manifold with π1 M = ℤ and suppose a degree‑one normal map (f,b) : M → S1 × S3 has kernel K2(M) ≅ ℤ[ℤ] as a module over ℤ[ℤ]. The intersection form on K2 is given by the matrix (1) over ℤ[ℤ]. Determine the surgery obstruction θ(f,b) ∈ L4(ℤ[ℤ]).
The group L4(ℤ) ≅ ℤ is detected by the signature. The quadratic form over ℤ[ℤ] is a rank‑one free module with form (1). Applying the augmentation ε : ℤ[ℤ] → ℤ (sending the generator t to 1) yields the ordinary integer form (1) on ℤ. Its signature is 1, so the image in L4(ℤ) is the generator 1. The surgery obstruction lives in L4(ℤ[ℤ]). The assembly map for π = ℤ is an isomorphism H4(Bℤ; L(ℤ)) ⊕ H0(Bℤ; L4(ℤ)) → L4(ℤ[ℤ]). Since Bℤ = S1, H4(S1; L(ℤ)) = 0 and H0(S1; L4(ℤ)) ≅ ℤ. Thus L4(ℤ[ℤ]) ≅ L4(ℤ) ≅ ℤ. The obstruction is the element corresponding to the signature of the augmented form, i.e. the generator of ℤ. Hence θ(f,b) = 1 ∈ ℤ ≅ L4(ℤ[ℤ]).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the surgery obstruction of a normal map, first identify the fundamental group and the relevant L‑group decoration (h, s, or p). Then compute the kernel module and its intersection form over the group ring. Use the augmentation map to reduce to the classical L‑groups of ℤ when the assembly map is known to be an isomorphism (e.g. for π = ℤ, ℤ^n or hyperbolic groups). Remember that odd‑dimensional L‑groups of ℤ vanish, so any obstruction in odd dimensions must come from the π‑part. Write down the Rothenberg sequence if the decoration matters.

Geometric Surgery on Manifolds with Boundary

Surgery theory extends to manifolds with boundary by working relative to a fixed boundary manifold. Let (W, ∂W) be a compact (n+1)‑manifold with boundary ∂W = M0 ∪ M1. A relative normal map (f,b) : (W, ∂W) → (X × [0,1], X × {0,1}) between manifolds with boundary, where f is degree-one on the boundary components, gives an element in the relative normal invariant group Ωn+1(X, ∂X). The surgery obstruction lies in the relative L‑group Ln+1(π1 X → π1 ∂X), which fits into a long exact sequence of L‑groups associated to the map of group rings induced by inclusion.

The key geometric step is to perform surgery on the interior of W while keeping the boundary fixed. If the map f is k‑connected on the boundary, surgery can be performed in the interior of W, relative to the boundary. The obstruction to extending a boundary homotopy equivalence across the interior is the relative surgery obstruction. When the boundary is already a homotopy equivalence, the relative obstruction vanishes and the interior can be surgered to a product M0 × [0,1] rel boundary. This is the relative version of the s‑cobordism theorem: an h‑cobordism with trivial relative Whitehead torsion is a product.

An important exception occurs when the boundary has non‑trivial fundamental group and the inclusion π1 ∂W → π1 W is not injective. Then the relative L‑group may contain contributions from the kernel of the map on fundamental groups. The Wall realization theorem says that any element of the relative L‑group can be realized by a relative normal map, but the geometric construction may require handles of index up to n. In dimensions ≥ 6 the Whitney trick works for the interior handles, but in dimension 5 one must use the Casson handle technique (Freedman) to achieve the same result in the topological category.

Examiners often test the distinction between the absolute and relative surgery exact sequences. The absolute sequence classifies manifolds homotopy equivalent to a given X; the relative sequence classifies cobordisms between two such manifolds. The map S(X) → Ω_n(X) in the absolute sequence forgets the homotopy equivalence, while the relative sequence has a map S(X, ∂X) → Ωn+1(X, ∂X) that forgets the relative homotopy equivalence. Knowing which sequence to apply is a common trap.

The Role of the Assembly Map in the Farrell–Jones Conjecture

The assembly map A : H_n(Bπ; L(ℤ)) → L_n(ℤ[π]) is the central homomorphism in the Farrell–Jones conjecture. Here L(ℤ) denotes the L‑theory spectrum of the integers, and H_n(Bπ; L(ℤ)) is the generalized homology of the classifying space Bπ with coefficients in that spectrum. The conjecture asserts that A is an isomorphism for all groups π. If true, it implies that the algebraic L‑groups of the group ring are completely determined by the homology of Bπ and the known L‑groups of the trivial group.

The map is constructed by a geometric procedure that associates elements of the generalized homology of Bπ to elements of the algebraic L-groups, often using controlled surgery techniques. The conjecture has been proved for a large class of groups: hyperbolic groups (Bartels–Lück–Reich), CAT(0) groups, solvable groups, and groups acting properly on finite‑dimensional CAT(0) complexes. The proof uses controlled topology and the theory of flow spaces.

Consequences for manifold classification are profound. The Borel conjecture (topological rigidity of aspherical manifolds) follows from the isomorphism conjecture in L‑theory together with the vanishing of the structure set S(X) for aspherical X. The Novikov conjecture on higher signatures follows from the rational injectivity of the assembly map in K‑theory, which is a corollary of the L‑theory version via the Rothenberg sequence. In practice, to compute L_n(ℤ[π]) for a specific group one often computes the homology groups H_*(Bπ; L_*(ℤ)) using the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence. The E2 page is H_p(Bπ; L_q(ℤ)) and the differentials are determined by the action of π on the L‑groups.

A typical exam question asks to compute L5(ℤ[ℤ2]). Since Bℤ2 = T2, the homology groups are H0 = ℤ, H1 = ℤ2, H2 = ℤ. The L‑groups of ℤ are L0 = ℤ, L1 = 0, L2 = ℤ/2, L3 = 0, L4 = ℤ. The spectral sequence collapses at E2 because all differentials vanish for degree reasons, giving L5(ℤ[ℤ2]) ≅ H1(T2; L4) ⊕ H3(T2; L2) = ℤ2 ⊕ 0 = ℤ2. This illustrates how the assembly map reduces an algebraic problem to a topological one.

Applications to High‑Dimensional Knot Theory and Exotic Spheres

Surgery theory provides the classification of high‑dimensional knots. A knot is an embedding S^n ⊂ Sn+2. The complement X = Sn+2 - ν(S^n) is an (n+2)‑manifold with boundary S^n × S1. The knot group π1 X and the Alexander module H1(Ẋ; ℤ) (where Ẋ is the infinite cyclic cover) are the primary invariants. For n ≥ 3, the concordance class of a simple knot is closely related to invariants derived from the Seifert form, which is a non‑singular quadratic form over ℤ[t, t-1]. The surgery obstruction for making the complement a product S^n × S1 × [0,1] lies in Ln+2(ℤ[t, t-1]). The Levine–Tristram signatures provide invariants of knot concordance. For simple knots, the algebraic concordance group of Seifert forms is isomorphic to the geometric concordance group for odd n, and has a kernel related to the Arf invariant for even n.

Exotic spheres are another major application. The group Θ_n of homotopy n‑spheres up to h‑cobordism fits into the exact sequence 0 → bPn+1 → Θ_n → Coker(J) → 0, where bPn+1 is the subgroup of spheres bounding parallelizable manifolds and Coker(J) is the cokernel of the stable J‑homomorphism. Surgery theory provides a formula for the order of bP₄k, which is related to Bernoulli numbers. For example bP8 ≅ ℤ/28, bP12 ≅ ℤ/992. The Kervaire invariant problem asks whether bP4k+2 contains an element of order 2; this is equivalent to the existence of a framed manifold with Kervaire invariant 1. The solution (Hill–Hopkins–Ravenel, 2016) shows such elements exist only in dimensions 2, 6, 14, 30, 62, and possibly 126.

The connection between knot theory and exotic spheres appears via the plumbing construction. A plumbing of disk bundles over spheres according to a graph yields a manifold whose boundary is a homotopy sphere. The intersection form of certain plumbings is related to the Seifert form of the associated boundary knot. Milnor’s exotic 7‑spheres arise as boundaries of plumbings of the E8 diagram. The surgery exact sequence for the sphere S^n reduces to 0 → Θ_n → Ω_n(S^n) → L_n(ℤ) → 0, where Ω_n(S^n) ≅ π_n(G/O). The map to L_n(ℤ) is the surgery obstruction; its kernel is the group of exotic spheres.

Examiner traps often involve confusing the smooth and topological categories. In the topological category Θ_nTOP = 0 for all n (by the topological Poincaré conjecture, proved by Freedman for n=4 and by Smale for n≥5). In the smooth category Θ_n is non‑trivial for many n. Also, the concordance group of knots is isomorphic to the algebraic concordance group of Seifert forms only for n ≥ 3; for classical knots (n=1) the algebraic classification fails because the Whitney trick does not work in dimension 3.

  • Confusing the absolute surgery exact sequence with the relative one when the problem asks for a cobordism classification.
  • Assuming the assembly map is always an isomorphism; it is only proven to be an isomorphism for groups for which the Farrell–Jones conjecture has been established.
  • Forgetting that the Whitehead torsion lives in Wh(π) and that Wh(ℤ) = 0 but Wh(ℤ ⊕ ℤ) ≠ 0.
  • Mixing up the decorations h, s, and p on L‑groups; the Rothenberg sequence relates them but they are not equal in general.
  • Thinking the Whitney trick works in dimension 4; it requires ambient dimension ≥ 5, so surgery in dimension 4 needs Freedman’s topological techniques.
  • Neglecting the distinction between smooth and topological L‑groups (e.g. L1^s(ℤ) = 0 smoothly but L1^h(ℤ) = ℤ/2 topologically).
  • Overlooking that the surgery obstruction map is a map of pointed sets, not a homomorphism, so exactness at S(X) is only exactness of pointed sets.

These advanced topics complete the core toolkit for applying surgery theory to the classification of high‑dimensional manifolds.

The Novikov Conjecture and Higher Signatures

The Novikov conjecture asserts that the higher signatures of a closed oriented manifold are homotopy invariants. For a manifold M with fundamental group π and a class x ∈ H*(Bπ; ℚ), the higher signature is the rational number ⟨L(M) ∪ f*(x), [M]⟩ where f : M → Bπ classifies the universal cover and L(M) is the Hirzebruch L-class. The conjecture states that if h : N → M is a homotopy equivalence, then the higher signatures of N and M agree for all x. This is equivalent to the rational injectivity of the assembly map A : H*(Bπ; L*(ℚ)) → L*(ℚ[π]) ⊗ ℚ. The conjecture has been proved for many classes of groups, including word-hyperbolic groups, CAT(0) groups, and amenable groups, via work of Kasparov, Skandalis, Yu, and others using controlled topology and operator algebras. A key tool is the Mishchenko-Fomenko higher index theorem: the higher signature equals the index of the signature operator twisted by the Mishchenko line bundle over Bπ. The conjecture remains open for general groups; a counterexample would be a group with non-injective assembly map. The rational version is equivalent to injectivity of the assembly map in L-theory with rational coefficients. The integral Novikov conjecture (injectivity over ℤ) is false in general due to torsion phenomena in L-groups. The connection to surgery theory is direct: the surgery obstruction map θ : S(M) → L*(ℤ[π]) factors through the assembly map, so Novikov injectivity implies that the surgery obstruction detects the rational homotopy type. Problems may ask to compute higher signatures for specific manifolds (e.g., T2 × S2) or to explain why the conjecture implies the Borel conjecture for groups with torsion-free L-groups.

Equivariant Surgery Theory and Group Actions on Manifolds

Equivariant surgery theory classifies manifolds with a smooth or topological action of a finite group G. The basic problem: given a G-manifold M and a G-normal map (f, b) : N → M, when can we do G-surgery to make it a G-homotopy equivalence? The fixed-point sets MH for subgroups H ≤ G are submanifolds, and the normal bundle of MH in M carries a representation of the Weyl group WG(H) = NG(H)/H. The equivariant surgery obstruction lives in the G-equivariant L-group L*G(ℤ[π]) which splits as a product over conjugacy classes of subgroups of G of relative L-groups of the Weyl groups. The Rothenberg sequence relates the projective, free, and h-decorated equivariant L-groups. A fundamental result is the G-surgery exact sequence: ⋯ → Ln+1G(ℤ[π]) → SG(M) → NG(M) → LnG(ℤ[π]). The structure set SG(M) classifies G-manifolds G-homotopy equivalent to M. Smith theory gives constraints on fixed-point sets: if G = ℤ/p acts on a homology sphere, the fixed set is a mod p homology sphere. The Atiyah-Bott-Segal localization formula expresses equivariant indices in terms of fixed-point data. For G = ℤ/2, the equivariant L-groups split as L*ℤ/2(ℤ) ≅ L*(ℤ) ⊕ L*−(ℤ) where the second summand is the L-group of quadratic forms with an involution. The Cappell-Shaneson splitting theorem for free actions reduces the classification to the non-equivariant case. A typical problem: compute the equivariant surgery obstruction for a ℤ/2-action on S4 with fixed set S2, using the fact that the obstruction lies in L4ℤ/2(ℤ) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2 with components given by the ordinary signature and the Arf invariant of the fixed-point set.

Worked Example: Equivariant Surgery Obstruction for ℤ/2 on S⁴
Let ℤ/2 act smoothly on S⁴ with fixed-point set S². A ℤ/2-normal map (f, b) : N → S⁴ has degree 1 on the fixed sets. Compute the equivariant surgery obstruction θ^ℤ/2(f, b) ∈ L₄^ℤ/2(ℤ) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2.
The splitting L₄^ℤ/2(ℤ) ≅ L₄(ℤ) ⊕ L₄⁻(ℤ) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2 corresponds to the ordinary signature and the Kervaire-Arf invariant of the fixed-point set. The map f restricts to f^fix : N^ℤ/2 → S², a degree-1 normal map of 2-manifolds. The ordinary surgery obstruction θ(f^fix) ∈ L₂(ℤ) = ℤ/2 is the Arf invariant of the kernel quadratic form on H₁(N^ℤ/2; ℤ/2). The free part of the obstruction is the signature difference: sign(N) − sign(S⁴) = sign(N). By the G-signature theorem, sign(N) = sign(N^ℤ/2) = sign(S²) = 0. Thus the ℤ-component is 0. The ℤ/2-component is the Arf invariant of the fixed-point surgery problem. If the fixed-point surgery can be done (i.e., N^ℤ/2 is h-cobordant to S²), the Arf invariant vanishes. In general, θ^ℤ/2 = (0, Arf(f^fix)). The obstruction vanishes iff the fixed-point surgery obstruction vanishes, which for 2-manifolds is always true (since L₂(ℤ) = ℤ/2 but the structure set of S² is trivial). Hence θ^ℤ/2 = 0 and the ℤ/2-normal map is normally cobordant to a ℤ/2-homotopy equivalence.
Exam Strategy: Equivariant L-Groups
Memorize the splitting L_*^G(ℤ) ≅ ∏_(H) L_*(ℤ[W_G H]) for G finite. For G = ℤ/2, L_*^ℤ/2(ℤ) ≅ L_*(ℤ) ⊕ L_*⁻(ℤ). The negative L-groups L_*⁻(ℤ) are 4-periodic: L₀⁻ = ℤ, L₁⁻ = 0, L₂⁻ = ℤ/2, L₃⁻ = 0. The map to L_*(ℤ) is the forgetful map; the map to L_*⁻(ℤ) is the fixed-point obstruction. In questions, identify the fixed-point set dimension and use the G-signature theorem to compute the free part.

The Cappell-Shaneson UNil Groups and Splitting Obstructions

When a manifold M splits along a submanifold N with fundamental group π1(N) → π1(M) injective, the fundamental group of M is an amalgamated free product π1(M) ≅ G1 ∗H G2 or an HNN extension. The surgery obstruction groups for such splittings are the Cappell-Shaneson UNil groups. For an amalgamated free product G = G1 ∗H G2 with H → Gi injective, the L-group L*(ℤ[G]) splits as L*(ℤ[G1]) ⊕ L*(ℤ[G2]) ⊕ UNil*(H; G1, G2). The UNil groups measure the failure of the Mayer-Vietoris sequence in L-theory to be exact; they appear as the cokernel of the map L*(ℤ[G1]) ⊕ L*(ℤ[G2]) → L*(ℤ[G]). For H = {1}, UNil*({1}; G1, G2) is the classical UNil group of the free product. These groups are notoriously difficult to compute; they are often infinitely generated and not finitely presented. A key result: if H has finite index in both G1 and G2, then UNil* = 0. The Farrell-Hsiang method uses controlled topology to show UNil vanishes for many groups (e.g., word-hyperbolic groups). The splitting obstruction for a manifold M = M1 ∪N M2 lies in UNiln+1(π1(N); π1(M1), π1(M2)). If this obstruction vanishes, the manifold splits geometrically. The UNil groups also appear in the classification of manifolds with boundary: the relative surgery exact sequence for a pair (M, ∂M) involves UNil when the inclusion ∂M → M induces an amalgamated product structure. Problems may ask to explain why UNil groups vanish when the amalgamating subgroup has finite index in both factors, or to compute the splitting obstruction for a product manifold like S2 × S1 split along S2 × {pt}.

Controlled Surgery and the Topological Rigidity of Aspherical Manifolds

Controlled surgery theory, developed by Quinn, Ferry, Pedersen, and others, replaces the algebraic L-groups with controlled L-groups L*(X; ℤ[π]) where X is a metric space (often the universal cover Ẽ of a manifold). A controlled quadratic Poincaré complex is a chain complex over ℤ[π] with a quadratic structure where the chain homotopies are required to have small propagation (controlled by a parameter ε > 0). The controlled assembly map Aε : H*(Bπ; L*(ℤ)) → L*(Ẽ; ℤ[π])ε is studied as ε → 0. The fundamental theorem of controlled surgery: a degree-1 normal map f : N → M of n-manifolds with n ≥ 5 is normally cobordant to a homotopy equivalence iff its controlled surgery obstruction in Ln(Ẽ; ℤ[π])ε vanishes for all sufficiently small ε. This is the technical engine behind the proof of the Borel conjecture for many groups. For a closed aspherical manifold M with fundamental group π, the Borel conjecture states that any homotopy equivalence h : N → M is homotopic to a homeomorphism. The strategy: the surgery obstruction θ(h) ∈ Ln(ℤ[π]) maps to the controlled obstruction. If the controlled assembly map is an isomorphism (the controlled L-theory isomorphism conjecture), then θ(h) = 0 implies the controlled obstruction vanishes, so h is normally cobordant to a homeomorphism. The Farrell-Jones conjecture in L-theory implies the controlled isomorphism conjecture. Groups for which this is known include: hyperbolic groups (Bartels-Lück-Reich), CAT(0) groups (Bartels-Lück), solvable groups (Farrell-Jones), and more generally groups acting properly on finite-dimensional CAT(0) spaces. The controlled approach also gives the structure set S(M) a topology (the controlled topology) making it a homology manifold. A typical problem: explain why the controlled surgery obstruction of a homotopy equivalence of aspherical manifolds vanishes if the Farrell-Jones assembly map is injective.

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Rho-Invariant

The rho-invariant ρ(M, φ) of an odd-dimensional manifold M with a representation φ : π1(M) → U(k) is a secondary invariant defined by Atiyah-Patodi-Singer using the η-invariant of the signature operator twisted by the flat bundle associated to φ. For a 4k−1 dimensional manifold, ρ(M, φ) = ηφ(M) − η0(M) where ηφ is the η-invariant of the twisted signature operator and η0 is the η-invariant of the untwisted operator. The rho-invariant is a homotopy invariant of the pair (M, φ) and defines a homomorphism ρ : L4k−1s(ℤ[π]) → ℝ/ℤ (or ℚ/ℤ for finite π). The surgery obstruction map θ : S(M) → Ln(ℤ[π]) can be detected by rho-invariants: for a normal map (f, b) : N → M representing an element of S(M), the rho-invariant of the kernel quadratic form equals the difference of rho-invariants of N and M. This gives a commutative diagram: S(M) → Ln(ℤ[π]) → ℝ/ℤ where the composition is ρ(N) − ρ(M). For π finite, the rho-invariant takes values in ℚ/ℤ and the map L4k−1s(ℤ[π]) → ℚ/ℤ is injective on the torsion subgroup. This is the basis of the rho-invariant surgery obstruction used by Chang-Weinberger and others to prove topological rigidity for manifolds with finite fundamental group. The connection to the η-invariant of the Dirac operator gives the α-invariant in KO-theory, linking surgery to index theory. A standard computation: for M = L(p, q) a 3-dimensional lens space, ρ(M, φ) = (2/p) ∑j=1p−1 cot(πj/p) cot(πqj/p) for the 1-dimensional representation φ(g) = e2πi/p. The surgery obstruction for a homotopy equivalence of lens spaces is detected by the difference of these rho-invariants. Problems may ask to compute the rho-invariant for a specific lens space or to explain why the rho-invariant map factors through the surgery obstruction group.

  • Confusing the Novikov conjecture with the Borel conjecture. The Novikov conjecture concerns homotopy invariance of higher signatures (rational injectivity of assembly); the Borel conjecture concerns topological rigidity of aspherical manifolds (structure set is a point). Novikov does not imply Borel; they are independent.
  • Thinking equivariant L-groups are just L-groups of the fixed-point set. The splitting L_*^G(ℤ) ≅ ∏ L_*(ℤ[W_G H]) involves Weyl groups, not just fixed-point fundamental groups. The fixed-point set M^H has fundamental group π1(M^H) which maps to W_G H, but they are not equal in general.
  • Assuming UNil groups always vanish. UNil groups are often non-zero and infinitely generated. They vanish when the amalgamating subgroup has finite index in both factors, but this is a special case. The free product UNil({1}; G₁, G₂) is rarely zero.
  • Mixing up controlled surgery with ordinary surgery. Controlled surgery uses metric control (ε-chains) on the universal cover; ordinary surgery uses algebraic L-groups. The controlled assembly map goes from homology of Bπ to controlled L-groups of the universal cover, not to ordinary L-groups.
  • Believing the rho-invariant detects all surgery obstructions. The rho-invariant detects only the torsion part of L4k−1^s(ℤ[π]) for finite π, and only the image in ℝ/ℤ. For infinite π, the rho-invariant takes values in ℝ and the map is not injective. The full surgery obstruction requires the L-group, not just rho.
  • Forgetting that the surgery exact sequence is a sequence of pointed sets, not groups. Exactness at S(M) means the preimage of the basepoint in L_n(ℤ[π]) is the image of the structure set map. It does not imply a group structure on S(M) unless the action of Ln+1 is free and transitive, which is not true in general.
  • Overlooking the distinction between smooth and topological categories in equivariant surgery. The equivariant L-groups L_*^G(ℤ) differ in the smooth and topological categories. For G = ℤ/2, the topological L-groups contain 2-torsion not present in the smooth groups (e.g., the Kervaire invariant appears in the smooth category but vanishes topologically). This affects the classification of G-manifolds.

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Topological Manifolds and the Kirby–Siebenmann Invariant

A key feature in the topological setting is the Kirby–Siebenmann invariant ks(M) ∈ H4(M; ℤ/2), which obstructs the existence of a PL structure on M. For a topological manifold M the normal invariant map factors through the forgetful map G/TOP → G/PL, whose kernel is the Eilenberg–MacLane space K(ℤ/2, 3). Consequently, the surgery obstruction for a normal map (f, b) : N → M contains an extra term: the evaluation of ks(M) on the fundamental class. If ks(M) ≠ 0, M admits no PL structure, and STOP(M) may be empty despite a vanishing algebraic surgery obstruction. The exact sequence remains exact as a sequence of pointed sets; exactness at STOP(M) means a normal invariant lies in the image of the structure set precisely when its surgery obstruction is zero in L_n(ℤ[π]). In practice, ks(M) is computed as the obstruction to lifting the stable topological normal bundle to a PL bundle. For example, Freedman's E8 manifold has ks ≠ 0 and admits no smooth structure; in dimensions ≥ 5 the same invariant distinguishes homotopy equivalent but non-homeomorphic topological manifolds. Typical problems: show a degree‑one normal map between topological manifolds with vanishing KS invariant is normally cobordant to a homeomorphism, or compute the effect of connected sum with an exotic sphere on the KS invariant.

Relative Surgery Theory and the Surgery Exact Sequence of a Pair

For manifolds with boundary or maps of pairs (M, ∂M) → (X, Y), the surgery exact sequence extends to a relative version. Let (M, ∂M) be a compact n‑manifold with boundary and (f, b) : (M, ∂M) → (X, Y) a degree‑one normal map of pairs that is a homotopy equivalence on the boundary. The relative structure set S(M, ∂M) consists of such normal maps up to cobordism relative to the boundary. The relative surgery exact sequence reads

⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[π1(X)]) → S(M, ∂M) → [ (M, ∂M), (G/TOP, *) ] → L_n(ℤ[π1(X)]) → ⋯

The normal invariant lies in the relative homotopy classes of maps to G/TOP constant on the boundary. The surgery obstruction map θ sends a relative normal invariant to the Wall group of π1(X). Relative L‑groups L_n(ℤ[π1(X)] → ℤ[π1(Y)]) appear when π1(∂M) → π1(M) is not injective; if injective, the relative L‑group coincides with the absolute one. The relative theory is essential for the s‑cobordism theorem via surgery: one constructs a relative normal map on the trace of a handle decomposition; vanishing of the relative surgery obstruction (the Whitehead torsion) allows surgery relative to the boundary to yield a product cobordism. In knot theory, the relative surgery exact sequence for a knot complement (S3 νK, ∂) with target (D4, S3) classifies knot concordances. The relative normal invariant is the Seifert form; the surgery obstruction recovers invariants like the Levine–Tristram signatures. Examiners often ask: write the relative surgery exact sequence for a pair; identify the relative L‑group (e.g., for a knot complement with π1 = ℤ); explain why the relative structure set is a pointed set, not a group.

Worked Example: Relative Surgery Obstruction for a Knot Complement
Let K ⊂ S3 be a knot with complement M = S3 νK. Consider the inclusion (M, ∂M) → (D4, S3) as a degree‑one normal map of pairs. Compute the relative surgery obstruction in L4(ℤ[ℤ]) and explain its relation to the Arf invariant of K.
π1(M) = ℤ, generated by a meridian. The relative normal invariant, represented by a map (M, ∂M) → (G/TOP, *), corresponds via Pontryagin–Thom to the Seifert form V of K. θ maps this to L4(ℤ[ℤ]) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2. The ℤ summand is the signature of a 4‑manifold bounded by M (zero for a knot complement); the ℤ/2 summand is the Arf invariant. Concretely, the obstruction is the quadratic refinement of the linking form on H1(M; ℤ/2) induced by V. For a knot, this refinement is the Arf invariant Arf(K) ∈ ℤ/2. Thus the obstruction vanishes iff Arf(K) = 0, the condition for topological sliceness (Freedman). Smooth sliceness has further obstructions (e.g., Rochlin invariant), but the relative sequence fully captures topological sliceness.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the relative surgery exact sequence, write down the three terms explicitly: the relative L‑group, the relative structure set, and the relative normal invariants. Identify the fundamental groups of the source and target, and state whether the boundary inclusion induces an injection on π1. If it does, the relative L‑group equals the absolute L‑group of the target; if not, mention the relative L‑group notation L_*(ℤ[π1(X)] → ℤ[π1(Y)]). This precision earns full marks.

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Eta Invariant in Odd Dimensions

For a closed (4k−1)-manifold M, the surgery obstruction map θ : S(M) → L4k−1(ℤ[π]) is detected analytically by the APS η-invariant of the twisted signature operator for a unitary representation φ : π1(M) → U(r). For a normal map (f, b) : N → M, η_φ(N) − η_φ(M) equals the image of the surgery obstruction under ρ_φ : L4k−1(ℤ[π]) → ℝ/ℤ. This yields a commutative diagram S(M) → L4k−1(ℤ[π]) → ℝ/ℤ composing to ρ_φ(N) − ρ_φ(M). The η-invariant is the analytic continuation at s=0 of ∑λ≠0 sign(λ) |λ|-s for eigenvalues λ of the twisted signature operator. It is a spectral invariant of the flat bundle associated to φ. The ρ-invariant is the reduced η-invariant ρ_φ(M) = η_φ(M) − η0(M), with η0 untwisted. For finite π, ρ takes values in ℚ/ℤ and L4k−1s(ℤ[π]) → ℚ/ℤ is injective on torsion. Chang and Weinberger used this to prove topological rigidity for manifolds with finite fundamental group. In the smooth category, the Dirac operator's η-invariant gives the α-invariant in KO-theory, linking surgery to index theory. A standard computation: ρ(L(p,q), φ) = (2/p) ∑j=1p-1 cot(πj/p) cot(πqj/p) for φ(g)=e2πi/p. Problems may ask to derive this via the Hirzebruch signature theorem for manifolds with boundary, or explain why ρ detects only torsion in L4k−1(ℤ[π]).

Surgery Theory for Manifolds with Corners and Stratified Spaces

Classical surgery assumes manifolds (possibly with boundary). Geometric situations often yield spaces with corners or stratifications, e.g., compactifications of locally symmetric spaces or quotients by non-free actions. Quinn, Weinberger, and others extended surgery to these settings using stratified systems of sets and controlled topology. A manifold with corners of codimension c is locally [0, ∞)c × ℝn-c; its boundary strata are manifolds with corners of lower codimension. One defines a stratified structure set Sstrat(X) of normally nonsingular stratified homotopy equivalences (f, b) : N → X with stratified normal invariants in [X, G/TOP]strat. The surgery exact sequence becomes a long exact sequence of stratified L-groups L*strat(ℤ[π1(X)]), built from the L-groups of strata fundamental groups with connecting maps encoding corner gluing data. The key tool is the stratified transversality theorem, allowing a stratified map to be made transverse to strata via controlled perturbation. The surgery obstruction lies in a direct sum of strata L-groups; its vanishing is equivalent to a stratified normal cobordism to a stratified homeomorphism. An application is topological rigidity of locally symmetric spaces with boundary (e.g., Borel–Serre compactification), where the stratified sequence reduces to the ordinary one because strata are aspherical and the assembly map is an isomorphism. Examiners may ask: describe the local model of a codimension-2 corner; write the stratified sequence for a manifold with faces; explain why L*strat(Y × [0, ∞)) splits as L*(ℤ[π1(Y)]) ⊕ L*-1(ℤ[π1(Y)]).

The Role of the Assembly Map in the Baum–Connes Conjecture and Surgery

The Baum–Connes conjecture predicts the assembly map μ : K*top(EΓ) → K*(C*r(Γ)) is an isomorphism for every discrete group Γ, where EΓ is the classifying space for proper actions. This has profound implications for surgery: the analytic structure group San(BΓ) from the Higson–Roe analytic surgery sequence fits into a commutative diagram with the topological sequence. The analytic surgery sequence is ⋯ → Kn+1(C*r(Γ)) → Sann(BΓ) → Kntop(EΓ) → Kn(C*r(Γ)) → ⋯; μ maps topological normal invariants Kntop(EΓ) to the analytic L-group Kn(C*r(Γ)). If μ is injective (Novikov conjecture), the analytic surgery obstruction detects the same information as the topological one, and San injects into the topological structure set. If μ is an isomorphism (Baum–Connes), the analytic and topological sequences are equivalent, allowing classification of manifolds over BΓ entirely analytically. This proved the Novikov conjecture for a-T-menable groups and hyperbolic groups. Problems may ask: explain how assembly map injectivity implies homotopy invariance of higher signatures; describe the relation between San(BΓ) and S(M) for π1(M)=Γ. The key point: the analytic surgery obstruction uses the Mishchenko–Fomenko index theorem, replacing algebraic L-groups with K-theory of the group C*-algebra, with the assembly map bridging the two.

  • Confusing the Kirby–Siebenmann invariant with the smooth obstruction; ks lives in H4(M; ℤ/2) and obstructs PL structures, not smooth structures directly.
  • Treating the relative surgery exact sequence as a sequence of groups; it is a sequence of pointed sets, and exactness means the preimage of the basepoint equals the image of the preceding map.
  • Assuming the η‑invariant detects the entire surgery obstruction group; it only detects the torsion part of L4k−1s(ℤ[π]) for finite π and takes values in ℝ/ℤ (or ℚ/ℤ).
  • Overlooking the distinction between stratified L‑groups and ordinary L‑groups; stratified groups involve direct sums over strata and connecting homomorphisms that encode corner gluing data.
  • Believing the Baum–Connes assembly map is the same as the Farrell–Jones assembly map; Baum–Connes targets K‑theory of the reduced C*-algebra, while Farrell–Jones targets algebraic K‑ and L‑theory of the group ring.
  • Forgetting that the relative surgery obstruction for a knot complement lives in L4(ℤ[ℤ]) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2, and that the ℤ/2 summand is the Arf invariant, not the signature.
  • Mixing up the ρ‑invariant with the ordinary η‑invariant; the ρ‑invariant is the reduced η‑invariant η_φ − η0 and is a homotopy invariant of the pair (M, φ).

The Surgery Structure Set and the Classification of Manifolds up to Homotopy Equivalence

The surgery structure set S(X) of a closed manifold X of dimension n ≥ 5 is the set of equivalence classes of homotopy equivalences f : M → X from closed manifolds M. Two equivalences f₁ : M₁ → X and f₂ : M₂ → X are equivalent if a diffeomorphism (or PL homeomorphism, or homeomorphism, depending on category) h : M₁ → M₂ exists such that f₂ ∘ h ≃ f₁. The structure set classifies manifolds within a given homotopy type: f : M → X represents the trivial element of S(X) precisely when f is homotopic to a homeomorphism. The surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[π₁(X)]) → S(X) → [X, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[π₁(X)]) (smooth category) computes S(X) as the fiber of the surgery obstruction map θ : [X, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[π₁(X)]). The normal invariant η(f) ∈ [X, G/O] of a homotopy equivalence f measures the difference between the stable normal bundle of M and the pullback of X's; if η(f) = 0, then f is normally cobordant to a normal map with trivial normal invariant, and f is homotopic to a homeomorphism exactly when its surgery obstruction vanishes. The action of Ln+1(ℤ[π₁(X)]) on S(X) corresponds to performing surgery on the normal map; the orbit space is the set of manifolds homotopy equivalent to X up to homeomorphism. For simply connected X, S(X) is in bijection with ker θ. In the non-simply connected case, Whitehead torsion τ(f) ∈ Wh(π₁(X)) gives an additional invariant; the simple structure set S^s(X) classifies simple homotopy equivalences, and the forgetful map S^s(X) → S(X) has fibers given by the action of Wh(π₁(X)). The s-cobordism theorem implies two simple homotopy equivalences represent the same element of S^s(X) iff they are concordant (cobordant through an h-cobordism). S(X) is a pointed set with basepoint id_X; exactness means the image of Ln+1(ℤ[π₁(X)]) in S(X) is the preimage of the basepoint under S(X) → [X, G/O]. For aspherical manifolds, the Borel conjecture asserts S(X) is a single element (every homotopy equivalence is homotopic to a homeomorphism), equivalent to the assembly map in algebraic K- and L-theory being an isomorphism. In the topological category, STOP(X) fits into ⋯ → Ln+1^s(ℤ[π₁(X)]) → STOP(X) → [X, G/TOP] → L_n^s(ℤ[π₁(X)]), where L-groups are Wall's simple/projective quadratic L-groups. The map SDIFF(X) → STOP(X) relates to Kirby–Siebenmann smoothing theory; its kernel and cokernel are controlled by H^i(X; Γn-i) where Γ_k are homotopy sphere groups. Examiners often ask: define S(X) and explain how the surgery exact sequence classifies manifolds homotopy equivalent to X; describe the action of Ln+1(ℤ[π₁(X)]) on S(X); explain the difference between S(X) and S^s(X) and the role of Whitehead torsion.

The Surgery Obstruction Map for Non-Simply Connected Manifolds and the Wall Realization Problem

For a non-simply connected manifold X with fundamental group π = π₁(X), the surgery obstruction map θ : [X, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[π]) takes values in Wall's quadratic L-group L_n(ℤ[π]), classifying non-singular quadratic forms over ℤ[π] up to stable isomorphism. A degree-one normal map (f, b) : M → X determines a quadratic Poincaré complex (C_*(M̃), ψ) over ℤ[π], where M̃ is the universal cover and ψ refines the intersection pairing on Hn/2(M̃; ℤ[π]) (n even) or the kernel of Hn/2(M̃) → Hn/2(X̃) (n odd). The surgery obstruction θ(f, b) is the class of this complex in L_n(ℤ[π]). The Wall realization problem asks: given x ∈ L_n(ℤ[π]), does a normal map (f, b) : M → X exist with θ(f, b) = x? Wall proved that for n ≥ 5 and all finitely presented groups π, the map θ is surjective, by constructing explicit normal maps realizing any quadratic form. The construction realizes any quadratic form over ℤ[π] as the intersection form of a normal map obtained by surgery on X × D^k. For manifolds with boundary, a relative surgery obstruction θ(f, b) ∈ L_n(ℤ[π]) for (M, ∂M) → (X, ∂X) is realizable iff its image under ∂ : L_n(ℤ[π]) → Ln-1(ℤ[π]) matches the boundary obstruction. Ranicki's algebraic surgery exact sequence ⋯ → H_n(X; ℙ) → L_n(ℤ[π₁(X)]) → S_n(X) → Hn-1(X; ℙ) → ⋯ provides an algebraic framework, where ℙ is the quadratic Poincaré complex spectrum. The map H_n(X; ℙ) → L_n(ℤ[π]) is the assembly map in quadratic L-theory; its image consists of surgery obstructions of normal maps. The Wall realization theorem is equivalent to the surjectivity of the assembly map for geometric Poincaré complexes. For groups with torsion, L_n(ℤ[π]) contains torsion summands detected by the ρ-invariant (odd dimensions) and the Arf invariant (even dimensions over ℤ/2). Realizing torsion elements requires handling the Kervaire invariant and Browder–Livesay invariant. Examiners may ask: state the Wall realization theorem; explain how a quadratic form over ℤ[π] is realized as an intersection form; describe the assembly map's role in the algebraic surgery exact sequence; explain why the boundary map in L-theory governs realization for manifolds with boundary.

Worked Example: Realizing a Surgery Obstruction
Let X = S¹ × S³ (n = 4). The fundamental group is π = ℤ. The quadratic L-group L₄(ℤ[ℤ]) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2, where the ℤ summand is the signature and the ℤ/2 summand is the Arf invariant. Construct a normal map (f, b) : M → X with surgery obstruction (σ, Arf) = (8, 1).
Step 1: The normal invariant set [X, G/O] ≅ H⁴(X; ℤ) ⊕ H²(X; ℤ/2) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2. The signature map σ : [X, G/O] → ℤ is an isomorphism on the first summand; the Arf invariant map Arf : [X, G/O] → ℤ/2 is an isomorphism on the second. Step 2: Choose η ∈ [X, G/O] corresponding to (8, 1). This is represented by a degree-one normal map (f, b) : M → X where M is obtained from X by surgery on a framed embedding S¹ × D³ X with framing giving the desired Arf invariant. Step 3: The surgery obstruction θ(η) = (σ(η), Arf(η)) = (8, 1) by construction. Since n = 4, the Whitney trick fails and the surgery exact sequence does not apply directly; this illustrates algebraic realization in dimension 4 where Freedman's topological category gives a homeomorphism classification but the smooth category does not. For n ≥ 5, the same algebraic construction yields a genuine normal map with the prescribed obstruction.
Exam Strategy: Surgery Obstruction Computations
When asked to compute or realize a surgery obstruction, first identify the relevant L-group L_n(ℤ[π]) and its decomposition (signature, Arf, ρ-invariant, etc.). The normal invariant set [X, G/O] is computable via the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence: a direct sum of cohomology groups Hn-i(X; π_i(G/O)). The surgery obstruction map θ is a natural transformation of homology theories; on each summand it is the signature, Arf invariant, or a higher ρ-invariant. For simply connected X, θ is essentially the signature map. For π = ℤ, L2k(ℤ[ℤ]) ≅ L2k(ℤ) ⊕ L2k-1(ℤ) by the Shaneson splitting; the obstruction splits as (signature, ρ-invariant). Memorize low-dimensional L-groups: L₀(ℤ) = ℤ (signature), L₁(ℤ) = 0, L₂(ℤ) = ℤ/2 (Arf), L₃(ℤ) = 0, L₄(ℤ) = ℤ (signature). For finite groups, L-groups contain torsion detected by ρ-invariants. Check dimension parity: even dimensions involve quadratic forms (signature, Arf); odd dimensions involve formations (ρ-invariant).

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Poincaré Complexes and the Browder-Novikov Theorem

The classical surgery exact sequence applies to manifolds; the Browder–Novikov theorem extends it to geometric Poincaré complexes. A finite n-dimensional Poincaré complex X is a finite CW complex with a fundamental class [X] ∈ H_n(X; ℤ) such that cap product with [X] induces isomorphisms H^k(X; ℤ[π₁(X)]) → Hn-k(X; ℤ[π₁(X)]) for all k. Every closed manifold is a Poincaré complex, but not conversely (e.g., a Poincaré homology sphere with a cell attached). The surgery structure set S(X) for a Poincaré complex X is the set of equivalence classes of degree-one normal maps (f, b) : M → X from manifolds M, where equivalence is normal cobordism. The surgery exact sequence for Poincaré complexes is ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[π₁(X)]) → S(X) → [X, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[π₁(X)]), where [X, G/O] is the set of normal invariants. The map S(X) → [X, G/O] forgets the manifold structure; [X, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[π₁(X)]) is the surgery obstruction map. The Browder–Novikov theorem states that X is homotopy equivalent to a manifold iff S(X) is non-empty, i.e., the surgery obstruction map θ : [X, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[π₁(X)]) has 0 in its image. The Spivak normal fibration ν_X : X → BG replaces the stable normal bundle; a normal map (f, b) : M → X lifts ν_X to BO (or BTOP, BPL). The obstruction to a normal map is the Kervaire–Milnor obstruction in H^n(X; πn-1(G/O)); if it vanishes, normal invariants form a torsor over H^n(X; ℤ) ⊕ Hn-2(X; ℤ/2) ⊕ ⋯. This sequence underpins Ranicki's total surgery obstruction theory, defining s(X) ∈ S_n(X) in the algebraic structure group such that s(X) = 0 iff X is homotopy equivalent to a manifold. The algebraic structure group S_n(X) fits into ⋯ → H_n(X; ℙ) → L_n(ℤ[π₁(X)]) → S_n(X) → Hn-1(X; ℙ) → ⋯. The total surgery obstruction s(X) is the image of [X] under H_n(X; ℙ) → S_n(X); for manifolds, s(X) = 0 automatically. The Browder–Novikov theorem reduces "Is X a manifold?" to a computation in algebraic L-theory. Examiners may ask: define a geometric Poincaré complex and its Spivak normal fibration; state the Browder–Novikov theorem; explain the difference between the surgery exact sequences for manifolds and Poincaré complexes; describe the total surgery obstruction and its role in the algebraic surgery exact sequence.

The Surgery Obstruction in the PL and DIFF Categories: Smoothing Theory and the Kervaire-Milnor Obstruction

Surgery theory operates in three categories: TOP (topological), PL (piecewise-linear), and DIFF (smooth). The surgery exact sequences are ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[π]) → SCAT(X) → [X, G/CAT] → L_n(ℤ[π]) for CAT = TOP, PL, DIFF. The spaces G/TOP, G/PL, G/O are homotopy fibers of BTOP → BG, BPL → BG, BO → BG, where BG classifies spherical fibrations. The Kirby–Siebenmann invariant ks(X) ∈ H⁴(X; ℤ/2) obstructs lifting the Spivak fibration from BTOP to BPL; it vanishes iff X admits a PL structure. The smoothing obstruction for a PL manifold X lies in H³(X; Γ₄); since Γ₄ = 0, every PL manifold of dimension ≠ 4 is smoothable. In dimension 4, the Kirby–Siebenmann invariant obstructs PL structures, while PL manifolds are smoothable (Γ₄=0). The groups Γ_k of homotopy spheres (Kervaire–Milnor) measure category differences: Γ_k = π_k(G/O) for k ≥ 5, and ⋯ → Γn+1 → SDIFF(X) → SPL(X) → H^n(X; Γ_n) → ⋯ relates structure sets. The Kervaire invariant Φ ∈ L4k+2(ℤ) ≅ ℤ/2 obstructs a framed manifold from bounding a framed manifold with a quadratic refinement; it equals the Arf invariant of the middle intersection form. The Kervaire invariant one problem (which dimensions n = 2^k - 2 admit a framed manifold with Kervaire invariant 1) was solved by Hill, Hopkins, and Ravenel (2016): such manifolds exist only for n = 2, 6, 14, 30, 62 and possibly 126. Milnor exotic spheres are elements of Γ_n bounding parallelizable manifolds, detected by the α-invariant (surgery obstruction in KO-theory) and ρ-invariant. The smooth surgery obstruction map θ : [X, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[π]) factors through [X, G/PL] and L_n(ℤ[π]) → L_n^s(ℤ[π]) (decoration change h → s). The PL sequence uses simple L-groups L_n^s(ℤ[π]) (PL manifolds have canonical simple structure); the TOP sequence uses projective L-groups L_n^p(ℤ[π]) for non-simply connected manifolds (decorations coincide for simply connected). Kirby–Siebenmann smoothing theory shows SDIFF(X) → SPL(X) is a bijection for dim X ≠ 4, and SPL(X) → STOP(X) has kernel/cokernel controlled by H^i(X; Γn-i). Examiners may ask: describe the three surgery exact sequences and maps between them; define the Kirby–Siebenmann invariant and its role in PL smoothing theory; state the Kervaire invariant one theorem; explain how Γ_k relates structure sets across categories.

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Signature Defect: Novikov Additivity and the Atiyah-Patodi-Singer Index Theorem

The signature defect of a manifold with boundary is the difference between the manifold's signature and its boundary's signature (suitably defined). For a compact oriented 4k-manifold M with boundary ∂M = N, the signature σ(M) is not a homotopy invariant of (M, N); the Novikov additivity theorem states that if M = M₁ ∪_N M₂, then σ(M) = σ(M₁) + σ(M₂) iff the ρ-invariant of N vanishes. The ρ-invariant ρ(N, φ) ∈ ℝ/ℤ (or ℚ/ℤ for finite π₁) of a closed oriented (4k-1)-manifold N with representation φ : π₁(N) → U(r) is ρ(N, φ) = η_φ(N) - η0(N), where η_φ is the Atiyah–Patodi–Singer eta invariant of the signature operator twisted by φ, and η0 is the untwisted eta invariant. The Atiyah–Patodi–Singer index theorem for a Dirac-type operator D on a 4k-manifold M with boundary N states the index with APS boundary conditions is ∫_M Â(M) ch(E) - (η(N) + h)/2, where h is the kernel dimension of the boundary operator. Applied to the signature operator, this gives σ(M) = ∫_M L(M) - ρ(∂M), where L(M) is the Hirzebruch L-class. The surgery obstruction map θ : [X, G/O] → L4k(ℤ[π]) for a closed 4k-manifold X relates to the signature defect: for a normal map (f, b) : M → X, θ(f, b) is the class of the quadratic form on K2k(M̃) = ker(H2k(M̃) → H2k(X̃)); its signature is the defect σ(M) - σ(X). For odd-dimensional obstructions L4k-1(ℤ[π]), the ρ-invariant gives a homomorphism ρ : L4k-1^s(ℤ[π]) → Hom(R_π, ℝ/ℤ) where R_π is the representation ring. The Novikov conjecture (homotopy invariance of higher signatures) is equivalent to injectivity of the assembly map H_*(Bπ; ℙ) → L_*(ℤ[π]); the ρ-invariant detects the assembly map's image in the torsion part of L4k-1(ℤ[π]). The surgery exact sequence for a manifold with boundary (X, ∂X) involves relative L-groups L_n(ℤ[π₁(X)], ℤ[π₁(∂X)]) and the boundary map ∂ : L_n(ℤ[π₁(X)]) → Ln-1(ℤ[π₁(∂X)]) corresponding to the ρ-invariant. Cappell–Shaneson UNil groups appear in splitting obstructions when the fundamental group splits as a free product with amalgamation. Examiners may ask: state the Novikov additivity theorem; define the ρ-invariant and its relation to the η-invariant; state the APS index theorem for the signature operator; explain the surgery obstruction's relation to the signature defect; describe the ρ-invariant's role in the surgery exact sequence for manifolds with boundary.

  • Assuming the surgery obstruction map θ is always surjective; it is surjective for n ≥ 5 by the Wall realization theorem, but the image may be a proper subgroup for manifolds with boundary or Poincaré complexes without a normal map.
  • Mixing up decorations h, s, p in L-theory; the smooth sequence uses L^h (or L^s for simple homotopy equivalences), the PL sequence uses L^s, and the TOP sequence uses L^p for non-simply connected manifolds.
  • Treating the Kirby–Siebenmann invariant ks ∈ H⁴(X; ℤ/2) as an obstruction to smooth structures; it obstructs PL structures, while the smoothing obstruction for PL manifolds lies in H³(X; Γ₄) (vanishing for dim ≠ 4).
  • Believing the ρ-invariant detects the entire group L4k-1(ℤ[π]); it detects only the torsion part for finite π (values in ℝ/ℤ or ℚ/ℤ), while the free part is detected by the signature in even dimensions.

These advanced topics complete the core framework of surgery theory for the rigorous classification of high-dimensional manifolds.

Ranicki's Total Surgery Obstruction and the Algebraic Surgery Exact Sequence

Andrew Ranicki's algebraic surgery theory provides a purely algebraic formulation of the geometric surgery exact sequence, replacing the geometric normal invariant set [X, G/O] with the algebraic normal invariant set Hn(X; 𝕃•) (homology with coefficients in the quadratic L-spectrum 𝕃•) and the geometric structure set S(X) with the algebraic structure set Salg(X). The central object is the total surgery obstruction s(X) ∈ Sn(X) of an n-dimensional geometric Poincaré complex X, which vanishes if and only if X is homotopy equivalent to a topological manifold. The algebraic surgery exact sequence takes the form

⋯ → Hn+1(X; 𝕃•) → Salg(X) → [X, G/TOP] → Hn(X; 𝕃•) → ⋯

where the map [X, G/TOP] → Hn(X; 𝕃•) is the assembly map in L-theory. For a manifold X, the total surgery obstruction s(X) = 0, and the algebraic structure set Salg(X) is isomorphic to the geometric structure set S(X). Ranicki's construction uses quadratic Poincaré complexes (C, ψ) where C is a chain complex of ℤ[π1(X)]-modules and ψ is a chain homotopy class of quadratic structures. The algebraic surgery obstruction of a normal map (f, b) : M → X is the cobordism class of the quadratic Poincaré kernel σ*(f, b) ∈ Ln(ℤ[π1(X)]). The total surgery obstruction s(X) is built from the symmetric signature σ*(X) ∈ Hn(X; 𝕃•) (using symmetric L-theory) by a canonical map Hn(X; 𝕃•) → Sn(X). This framework unifies the smooth, PL, and TOP categories through the maps of spectra G/O → G/PL → G/TOP and the corresponding L-theory spectra 𝕃h → 𝕃s → 𝕃p. The algebraic surgery exact sequence is natural with respect to maps of spaces and provides a homotopy-theoretic foundation for surgery theory that avoids the technicalities of handlebody decompositions and the Whitney trick. Examiners may ask: define the total surgery obstruction and state its vanishing criterion; describe the algebraic surgery exact sequence and its relation to the geometric sequence; explain the role of the symmetric signature in constructing s(X); contrast the quadratic and symmetric L-spectra 𝕃• and 𝕃•.

The Topological s-Cobordism Theorem and Freedman's Disk Embedding Theorem

The smooth s-cobordism theorem fails in dimension 4, but Michael Freedman's work established the topological s-cobordism theorem for dimension ≥ 5 and, remarkably, for dimension 4 under the fundamental group condition π1 = 1. A topological s-cobordism (W; M, N) is a cobordism where the inclusions M W and N W are homotopy equivalences and the Whitehead torsion τ(W, M) ∈ Wh(π1(W)) vanishes. The theorem states that for dim W ≥ 5, or dim W = 4 with π1(W) = 1, such an s-cobordism is homeomorphic to M × [0, 1]. The proof relies on Freedman's disk embedding theorem: a map of a 2-disk D2 into a 4-manifold with trivial normal bundle and vanishing self-intersection number can be approximated by a locally flat embedding if the fundamental group of the complement is "good" (e.g., trivial or subexponential growth). This replaces the Whitney trick, which fails in dimension 4 for smooth embeddings but works for topological (locally flat) embeddings when the fundamental group is good. The topological s-cobordism theorem implies the topological Poincaré conjecture in dimension 4: a homotopy 4-sphere is homeomorphic to S4. It also implies the classification of simply connected topological 4-manifolds by their intersection form (Freedman's classification theorem). For non-simply connected 4-manifolds, the s-cobordism theorem holds if π1 is good; counterexamples exist for "bad" groups (e.g., groups containing a non-abelian free subgroup) due to the failure of the disk embedding theorem. The topological s-cobordism theorem is the foundation for the surgery exact sequence in the TOP category in dimension 4. Examiners may ask: state the topological s-cobordism theorem and its dimension/fundamental group hypotheses; describe characteristics of fundamental groups for which Freedman's disk embedding theorem applies; explain why the Whitney trick fails smoothly in dimension 4 but works topologically for good groups; state the classification of simply connected topological 4-manifolds.

Worked Example: Total Surgery Obstruction of a Poincaré Complex
Let X be a 5-dimensional geometric Poincaré complex with π1(X) = ℤ. Suppose the symmetric signature σ*(X) ∈ H5(X; 𝕃•) maps to a non-zero element in the algebraic structure group S5(X) under the canonical map H5(X; 𝕃•) → S5(X). Determine whether X is homotopy equivalent to a topological manifold and describe the obstruction.
The total surgery obstruction s(X) ∈ S5(X) is precisely the image of the symmetric signature σ*(X) under the map H5(X; 𝕃•) → S5(X). By hypothesis, this image is non-zero, so s(X) ≠ 0. Ranicki's theorem states that a geometric Poincaré complex X is homotopy equivalent to a topological manifold if and only if s(X) = 0. Since s(X) ≠ 0, X is not homotopy equivalent to any topological manifold. The non-vanishing of s(X) is the complete obstruction; there is no further geometric obstruction. In the algebraic surgery exact sequence ⋯ → H6(X; 𝕃•) → Salg(X) → [X, G/TOP] → H5(X; 𝕃•) → ⋯, the element s(X) corresponds to the obstruction to lifting the Spivak normal fibration of X to a topological normal map. For π1 = ℤ, the L-groups L5(ℤ[ℤ]) ≅ L5(ℤ) ⊕ L4(ℤ) ≅ 0 ⊕ ℤ = ℤ (by the Bass-Heller-Swan decomposition), and the assembly map H5(Bℤ; 𝕃•) → L5(ℤ[ℤ]) is an isomorphism. The non-zero image of the symmetric signature in S5(X) detects the failure of X to be a manifold.
Exam Strategy: Distinguishing Geometric vs Algebraic Surgery
When a question asks about the "surgery exact sequence", clarify which version: the geometric sequence (normal maps, structure sets, L-groups) or Ranicki's algebraic sequence (homology with L-spectrum coefficients, algebraic structure sets, total surgery obstruction). The algebraic sequence works for all Poincaré complexes without assuming a normal map exists. The total surgery obstruction s(X) is the single invariant that decides if a Poincaré complex is a manifold. For simply connected X, s(X) = 0 automatically for dim ≥ 5 (Browder-Novikov), but for non-simply connected X, s(X) can be non-zero. Memorise: s(X) = 0 ⇔ X ≃ M (topological manifold). The symmetric signature σ*(X) ∈ Hn(X; 𝕃•) is the input; the map to Sn(X) is the output.

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Topological Manifolds and the Quinn Obstruction

Frank Quinn developed a surgery theory for topological manifolds that does not require the fundamental group to be "good" in Freedman's sense. The Quinn obstruction (or Quinn index) is an invariant ι(M) ∈ 1 + 8ℤ for a topological 4-manifold M that refines the Kirby-Siebenmann invariant ks(M) ∈ H4(M; ℤ/2). For a closed topological 4-manifold, ks(M) = 0 if and only if M admits a PL structure, but Quinn showed that even when ks(M) = 0, there is a secondary obstruction to smoothing the PL structure, captured by the Quinn index. The Quinn obstruction lives in the topological surgery exact sequence for a 4-manifold M:

⋯ → L5(ℤ[π1(M)]) → STOP(M) → [M, G/TOP] → L4(ℤ[π1(M)]) → ⋯

where the map [M, G/TOP] → L4(ℤ[π1(M)]) is the surgery obstruction map θ. For simply connected M, L4(ℤ) ≅ ℤ (signature) and L5(ℤ) = 0. The normal invariant set [M, G/TOP] classifies topological normal maps. The Kirby-Siebenmann invariant ks(M) is the image of the Spivak normal fibration under [M, G/TOP] → H4(M; ℤ/2). Quinn's work extends the surgery exact sequence to topological Poincaré complexes and defines the Quinn resolution obstruction for a map f : X → Y of topological manifolds to be homotopic to a homeomorphism. The Quinn index ι(M) is defined using controlled surgery on the universal cover M̃ with control map to the end space of π1(M). For simply connected manifolds, the Quinn index is related to the signature and can detect the existence of exotic smooth structures on certain topological manifolds. The Quinn obstruction is the key to the topological s-cobordism theorem for all fundamental groups in dimension ≥ 5 and to the classification of topological manifolds up to homeomorphism. Examiners may ask: define the Quinn obstruction and its relation to the Kirby-Siebenmann invariant; explain how controlled surgery is used to define the Quinn index; describe the role of the Quinn obstruction in the topological s-cobordism theorem.

The Surgery Obstruction Map for Manifolds with Fundamental Group ℤ and the Classification of Fake Tori

The classification of manifolds homotopy equivalent to the n-torus Tn = (S1)n is a classical application of surgery theory. For n ≥ 5, the structure set S(Tn) fits into the surgery exact sequence

⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[ℤn]) → S(Tn) → [Tn, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[ℤn]) → ⋯

The L-groups of ℤ[ℤn] are computed via the Bass-Heller-Swan decomposition and the Shaneson splitting: Lk(ℤ[ℤn]) ≅ ⊕i=0n (Lk-i(ℤ) ⊗ Hi(Tn; ℤ)) ⊕ (UNil-terms). For n = 1, Lk(ℤ[ℤ]) ≅ Lk(ℤ) ⊕ Lk-1(ℤ). The normal invariant set [Tn, G/O] ≅ ⊕i=0n Hi(Tn; πn-i(G/O)) is computed using the homotopy groups of G/O. The surgery obstruction map θ : [Tn, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[ℤn]) is a homomorphism whose kernel is the image of the structure set. A fake torus is a manifold M homotopy equivalent to Tn but not homeomorphic (or diffeomorphic) to it. The Wall realization theorem implies that elements of L-groups can be realized as surgery obstructions of normal maps to Poincaré complexes. For n = 5, L5(ℤ[ℤ]) ≅ L5(ℤ) ⊕ L4(ℤ) ≅ 0 ⊕ ℤ = ℤ, so there are infinitely many distinct fake 5-tori up to h-cobordism. The s-cobordism theorem (smooth or topological) then classifies them up to homeomorphism/diffeomorphism. The Borel conjecture for ℤn (proved by Farrell-Jones for n ≤ 3 and in general by Bartels-Lück-Reich) implies that the assembly map H*(Bℤn; 𝕃•) → L*(ℤ[ℤn]) is an isomorphism, so the structure set S(Tn) is in bijection with the kernel of θ. Examiners may ask: compute Ln(ℤ[ℤ]) for n = 4, 5, 6; describe the surgery exact sequence for Tn; define a fake torus and explain how surgery theory classifies them; state the Borel conjecture for ℤn and its implication for S(Tn).

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Failure of Smooth Surgery in Dimension 4: Exotic ℝ⁴ and the h-Cobordism Theorem

Dimension 4 is exceptional in surgery theory: the Whitney trick fails for smooth embeddings of 2-disks in 4-manifolds, the smooth h-cobordism theorem is false, and there exist uncountably many exotic ℝ4 (smooth manifolds homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to standard ℝ4). The smooth surgery exact sequence breaks down because it is not exact at the L-group term, meaning the surgery obstruction map θ : [X, G/O] → L4(ℤ[π1(X)]) is not generally surjective. Freedman's topological surgery theory works for π1 = 1 (and good groups), giving a topological h-cobordism theorem and classification of simply connected topological 4-manifolds by intersection form. However, the smooth h-cobordism theorem fails: there exist smooth h-cobordisms (W; M, N) between simply connected 4-manifolds that are not products. The obstruction is measured by the Seiberg-Witten invariants or Donaldson's polynomial invariants, which are gauge-theoretic and have no analogue in high-dimensional surgery. The exotic ℝ4 are constructed by attaching a Casson handle (a topological 2-handle that is not smooth) to a 4-manifold and using Freedman's theorem to show the result is homeomorphic to ℝ4, while Donaldson's theorem shows it is not diffeomorphic to standard ℝ4. The smooth surgery exact sequence for a 4-manifold X would be

⋯ → L5(ℤ[π1(X)]) → SDIFF(X) → [X, G/O] → L4(ℤ[π1(X)]) → ⋯

but the map θ is not surjective, and SDIFF(X) does not classify smooth structures up to diffeomorphism in the same way. The K3 surface is a simply connected smooth 4-manifold with intersection form 2(-E8) ⊕ 3H, serving as a significant example in the study of smooth 4-manifolds. The 11/8 conjecture relates the signature and Euler characteristic of smooth spin 4-manifolds. Examiners may ask: explain why the Whitney trick fails in smooth dimension 4; state Freedman's topological h-cobordism theorem for π1 = 1; define exotic ℝ4 and sketch their construction using Casson handles; contrast the topological and smooth surgery exact sequences in dimension 4; describe the role of Donaldson's and Seiberg-Witten invariants as obstructions to smooth surgery.

  • Confusing Ranicki's total surgery obstruction s(X) with the surgery obstruction θ(f, b) of a normal map; s(X) is an invariant of a Poincaré complex X (vanishing ⇔ X ≃ manifold), while θ(f, b) is the obstruction to doing surgery on a given normal map (f, b) to make it a homotopy equivalence.
  • Assuming the topological s-cobordism theorem holds for all fundamental groups in dimension 4; it holds only for good groups (Freedman), and fails for bad groups (e.g., free groups of rank ≥ 2) due to the failure of the disk embedding theorem.
  • Treating the Quinn obstruction as a primary obstruction like the Kirby-Siebenmann invariant; the Quinn index is a secondary obstruction that refines ks(M) and detects exotic smooth structures on topological manifolds with ks = 0.
  • Believing the surgery exact sequence for Tn classifies fake tori up to homeomorphism without invoking the s-cobordism theorem; the sequence gives h-cobordism classes, and the s-cobordism theorem (smooth or topological) is needed to pass to homeomorphism/diffeomorphism classes.
  • Overlooking that the smooth surgery exact sequence in dimension 4 is not exact at [X, G/O] → L4(ℤ[π1]) because the Whitney trick fails; the map θ is not surjective, and the structure set SDIFF(X) does not have the same classification meaning as in high dimensions.
  • Confusing Casson handles (topological 2-handles) with smooth 2-handles; Casson handles are built from infinitely iterated immersed disks and are homeomorphic to D2 × D2 but generally not diffeomorphic to it; they are the topological substitute for smooth handles in Freedman's theory.

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Classification of Fake Lens Spaces

Fake lens spaces are manifolds homotopy equivalent but not homeomorphic to a standard lens space L(m, q) = S2k−1/ℤm. Their classification is a cornerstone of non-simply connected surgery theory. For a lens space L = L(m, q) of dimension n = 2k − 1 ≥ 5, the structure set S(L) fits into the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[ℤm]) → S(L) → [L, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[ℤm]). The normal invariants [L, G/O] are computed via the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence; for n odd they are isomorphic to a direct sum of cohomology groups Hn(L; ℤ) ⊕ Hn−4(L; ℤ) ⊕ ⋯. The surgery obstruction map θ : [L, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[ℤm]) is determined by the ρ-invariant of Atiyah–Patodi–Singer. For n = 4k − 1, the L-group Ln(ℤ[ℤm]) contains a ℤ summand (signature) and torsion summands detected by the ρ-invariant. Wall showed that two lens spaces L(m, q) and L(m, q′) are simple homotopy equivalent iff q ≡ ±q′ r2 (mod m) for some r coprime to m. They are homeomorphic iff in addition the ρ-invariants agree. The classification reduces to computing the image of θ and the action of the Whitehead group Wh(ℤm) on S(L). For m odd, Wh(ℤm) = 0, so simple homotopy equivalence implies homeomorphism. For m even, Wh(ℤm) ≠ 0 and there exist fake lens spaces that are homotopy equivalent but not simple homotopy equivalent. The complete classification for all m was achieved by Wall, Milgram, and others using the surgery exact sequence and the computation of L-groups of finite cyclic groups.

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Surface Group

Let Σg be a closed orientable surface of genus g ≥ 2, and let π = π1(Σg) be its fundamental group. The surgery exact sequence for a manifold M with π1(M) ≅ π takes the form ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[π]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[π]). The group π is a Poincaré duality group of dimension 2, and its classifying space Bπ ≃ Σg. The assembly map A : Hn(Bπ; L•) → Ln(ℤ[π]) is central to the Farrell–Jones conjecture, which is known for surface groups by work of Bartels, Lück, and Reich. The L-groups Ln(ℤ[π]) decompose via the Rothenberg sequence into the projective L-groups Lnp(ℤ[π]) and the Tate cohomology Ĥn(ℤ/2; Wh(π)). For surface groups, Wh(π) = 0 by a theorem of Waldhausen, so the Rothenberg sequence collapses and Ln(ℤ[π]) ≅ Lnp(ℤ[π]). The projective L-groups are computed by the assembly map from the homology of Bπ with coefficients in the L-theory spectrum. The normal invariants [M, G/O] are given by Hn(M; ℤ) ⊕ Hn−4(M; ℤ) ⊕ ⋯ for n odd, and by Hn(M; ℤ/2) ⊕ Hn−2(M; ℤ/2) ⊕ ⋯ for n even, together with K-theory invariants. The surgery obstruction map θ is determined by the higher signatures and the ρ-invariant. For aspherical manifolds with π1 = π, the Borel conjecture (proved for surface groups by Farrell and Jones) implies that the structure set S(M) consists of a single element, so any homotopy equivalence is homotopic to a homeomorphism. The surgery exact sequence then identifies the kernel of θ with the image of the assembly map. Examiners may ask: state the Farrell–Jones conjecture for surface groups; explain why Wh(π) = 0 for surface groups; compute L5(ℤ[π]) for π = π1(Σ2); describe the surgery exact sequence for M = Σg × Sn−2.

Worked Example: Surgery Obstruction for a Fake Lens Space
Let L = L(5,1) be the 3-dimensional lens space with fundamental group ℤ/5. Suppose a normal map (f, b) : M → L represents an element x ∈ [L, G/O] corresponding to the generator of H3(L; ℤ) ≅ ℤ/5. Compute the surgery obstruction θ(x) ∈ L3(ℤ[ℤ/5]).
For n = 3, L3(ℤ[ℤ/5]) ≅ ℤ/2 ⊕ ℤ/2 (Wall). The normal invariant x ∈ H3(L; ℤ) ≅ ℤ/5 maps under θ to the ρ-invariant of the associated flat bundle. The ρ-invariant for ℤ/5 takes values in ℚ/ℤ, and the surgery obstruction lands in the 2-torsion subgroup. The generator of H3(L; ℤ) corresponds to a degree-1 normal map with ρ-invariant 1/5 mod ℤ. The map θ : ℤ/5 → ℤ/2 ⊕ ℤ/2 sends the generator to the non-zero element in the first ℤ/2 summand (the Arf invariant of the associated quadratic form over 𝔽2). Thus θ(x) = (1, 0) ∈ ℤ/2 ⊕ ℤ/2. This element is non-zero, so the normal map cannot be surgered to a homotopy equivalence. The structure set S(L) is the preimage of 0 under θ in the exact sequence of pointed sets; since θ is a homomorphism on the ℤ/5 summand, its kernel has order 5/|im θ| = 5/2, which is not integral — this indicates that the normal invariant group has additional summands beyond H3(L; ℤ). The full calculation shows there are exactly two simple homotopy equivalence classes of manifolds homotopy equivalent to L(5,1), one being L itself and the other a fake lens space detected by the non-trivial element of ker(θ).
Exam Strategy: Lens Space Classification
When asked to classify fake lens spaces, first determine the dimension parity. For n = 4k−1, the ρ-invariant takes values in ℚ/ℤ and the surgery obstruction map is computed via the Atiyah–Patodi–Singer index theorem. For n = 4k+1, the obstruction is the Arf invariant of a quadratic form over 𝔽2. The L-groups for cyclic groups have the form L4k(ℤ[ℤ/m]) ≅ ℤ ⊕ (ℤ/2)r ⊕ (ℤ/m)s, L4k+2 ≅ (ℤ/2)t, L4k+1 ≅ (ℤ/2)u, L4k−1 ≅ ℤ ⊕ (ℤ/2)v ⊕ (ℤ/m)w, where the exponents depend on the prime factorization of m. For odd m, Wh(ℤ/m) = 0, so simple homotopy equivalence = homeomorphism. For even m, Wh(ℤ/m) ≠ 0 and you must account for the action of the Whitehead group on the structure set.

The Surgery Obstruction and the Classification of Fake Projective Spaces

Fake projective spaces are manifolds homotopy equivalent but not homeomorphic to ℝℙn, ℂℙn, or ℍℙn. Their classification uses the surgery exact sequence for the fundamental group ℤ/2 (real case) or the trivial group (complex and quaternionic cases). For ℝℙn with n ≥ 5, the structure set S(ℝℙn) fits into ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[ℤ/2]) → S(ℝℙn) → [ℝℙn, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[ℤ/2]). The normal invariants are computed via the cohomology of ℝℙn with coefficients in the homotopy groups of G/O. For n odd, [ℝℙn, G/O] is a direct sum of groups Hi(ℝℙn; πi(G/O)). The L-groups of ℤ[ℤ/2] are known: L4k(ℤ[ℤ/2]) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2 ⊕ ℤ/2, L4k+2 ≅ ℤ/2 ⊕ ℤ/2, L4k+1 ≅ ℤ/2, L4k−1 ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ/2 ⊕ ℤ/2. The surgery obstruction map θ is determined by the ρ-invariant and the Arf invariant. For ℝℙ2k, the classification of fake projective spaces was completed by Wall, Cappell, and Shaneson. Fake ℝℙn exist for many dimensions n ≥ 5; the exceptional dimensions are related to the Kervaire invariant problem. For ℂℙn and ℍℙn, the fundamental group is trivial, so the surgery exact sequence reduces to the simply connected case. The structure set S(ℂℙn) is trivial for most n (by work of Browder, Novikov, and Sullivan), meaning there are no fake complex projective spaces in those dimensions. Non-trivial structure sets occur in dimensions related to the Kervaire invariant. For ℍℙn, the classification is similar. The surgery obstruction map for simply connected manifolds is determined by the signature and the Kervaire invariant. Examiners may ask: state the surgery exact sequence for ℝℙn; compute L5(ℤ[ℤ/2]); explain why fake ℂℙn are rare; describe the role of the ρ-invariant in detecting fake ℝℙn.

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Assembly Map for Hyperbolic Groups

A group Γ is hyperbolic (in the sense of Gromov) if its Cayley graph is δ-hyperbolic. The Farrell–Jones conjecture for hyperbolic groups was proved by Bartels and Lück (2012) using controlled surgery theory. The assembly map A : Hn(EVCΓ; L•) → Ln(ℤ[Γ]) is an isomorphism, where EVCΓ is the classifying space for virtually cyclic subgroups. For a closed aspherical manifold M with π1(M) = Γ hyperbolic, the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[Γ]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[Γ]) simplifies because the assembly map is an isomorphism. The normal invariants [M, G/O] are isomorphic to Hn(M; ℤ) ⊕ Hn−4(M; ℤ) ⊕ ⋯ for n odd. The surgery obstruction map θ corresponds under the assembly isomorphism to the map induced by the L-theory orientation of M. The Borel conjecture holds for hyperbolic groups: any homotopy equivalence f : N → M is homotopic to a homeomorphism. Thus S(M) is a single point, and the surgery exact sequence identifies the kernel of θ with the image of the assembly map. The L-groups Ln(ℤ[Γ]) for hyperbolic groups are computed by the homology of the Rips complex. The Whitehead group Wh(Γ) vanishes for torsion-free hyperbolic groups (by a theorem of Bartels, Lück, and Reich), so simple homotopy equivalence coincides with homotopy equivalence. For hyperbolic groups with torsion, Wh(Γ) may be non-zero, and the structure set S(M) classifies manifolds up to s-cobordism. The controlled surgery theory developed by Ferry, Quinn, and others provides the technical framework for proving the assembly map isomorphism. Examiners may ask: state the Farrell–Jones conjecture for L-theory; explain why the assembly map is an isomorphism for hyperbolic groups; describe the structure set S(M) for an aspherical manifold with hyperbolic fundamental group; compute L5(ℤ[Γ]) for Γ a surface group (which is hyperbolic).

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Free Product

Let Γ = Γ1 * Γ2 be a free product of groups. The L-groups of ℤ[Γ] are computed by the Mayer–Vietoris sequence in L-theory (Cappell, 1974): ⋯ → Ln(ℤ) → Ln(ℤ[Γ1]) ⊕ Ln(ℤ[Γ2]) → Ln(ℤ[Γ]) → Ln−1(ℤ) → ⋯. The UNil groups of Cappell and Shaneson appear as the cokernel of the map Ln(ℤ[Γ1]) ⊕ Ln(ℤ[Γ2]) → Ln(ℤ[Γ]). For a manifold M with π1(M) = Γ, the surgery exact sequence involves these L-groups. The normal invariants [M, G/O] are computed by the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence. The surgery obstruction map θ : [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[Γ]) factors through the Mayer–Vietoris sequence. The structure set S(M) classifies manifolds homotopy equivalent to M up to s-cobordism. The Cappell–Shaneson UNil groups measure the failure of the surgery exact sequence to split. For Γ = ℤ * ℤ (free group of rank 2), the L-groups are known: Ln(ℤ[F2]) ≅ Ln(ℤ) ⊕ Ln(ℤ) ⊕ UNiln(ℤ; ℤ, ℤ). The UNil groups are 2-torsion and are non-zero in dimensions ≡ 0, 1 mod 4. The surgery exact sequence for a manifold M with π1 = F2 takes the form ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[F2]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[F2]). The map θ is not surjective in general; its cokernel is related to the UNil groups. The topological s-cobordism theorem fails for F2 in dimension 4 (Freedman), but holds in high dimensions by the work of Farrell and Jones. The classification of manifolds with free product fundamental groups is a major application of the UNil theory. Examiners may ask: write the Mayer–Vietoris sequence for L-groups of a free product; define the Cappell–Shaneson UNil groups; explain why the topological s-cobordism theorem fails for F2 in dimension 4; compute L5(ℤ[F2]).

  • Assuming the surgery obstruction map θ for lens spaces is always surjective; it is not, and its image determines the structure set.
  • Overlooking that Wh(ℤ/m) = 0 for odd m but non-zero for even m; this affects whether simple homotopy equivalence implies homeomorphism.

  • Confusing the classification of fake ℝℙn (fundamental group ℤ/2) with fake ℂℙn (simply connected); the surgery exact sequences and L-groups are completely different.

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Polycyclic Group

Let Γ be a polycyclic group, i.e., a group admitting a subnormal series with cyclic factors. The Farrell–Jones conjecture in L‑theory holds for all polycyclic groups (Farrell and Jones, 1993). Consequently, the assembly map A : Hₙ(BΓ; L(ℤ)) → Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) is an isomorphism for all n. For a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ Γ, the surgery exact sequence

⋯ → Lₙ₊₁(ℤ[Γ]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Lₙ(ℤ[Γ])

simplifies because the assembly map identifies the L‑groups with the homology of BΓ with coefficients in the L‑theory spectrum. The normal invariants [M, G/O] are computed by the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence and are isomorphic to ⊕ᵢ Hⁿ⁻ⁱ(M; Lᵢ(ℤ)). Since Lᵢ(ℤ) is ℤ for i ≡ 0 (mod 4), ℤ/2 for i ≡ 2 (mod 4), and 0 otherwise, the normal invariants are a direct sum of ordinary cohomology groups of M. The surgery obstruction map θ : [M, G/O] → Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) corresponds under the assembly isomorphism to the map induced by the L‑theory orientation of M. The structure set S(M) classifies manifolds homotopy equivalent to M up to s‑cobordism. For polycyclic groups, the Whitehead group Wh(Γ) is finitely generated (Bass, Heller, and Swan), and the s‑cobordism theorem holds in the topological category (Freedman and Quinn in dimension 4; Kirby–Siebenmann and Quinn in high dimensions). The Borel conjecture is established for polycyclic groups: any homotopy equivalence f : N → M is homotopic to a homeomorphism. Thus S(M) is a single point, and the surgery exact sequence identifies the kernel of θ with the image of the assembly map. Examiners may ask: state the Farrell–Jones conjecture for polycyclic groups; explain why the assembly map is an isomorphism; describe the structure set S(M) for an aspherical manifold with polycyclic fundamental group; compute L₅(ℤ[Γ]) for Γ a crystallographic group.

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Classification of Fake Nilmanifolds and Solvmanifolds

A nilmanifold is a compact manifold of the form N = ΓG where G is a simply connected nilpotent Lie group and Γ ⊂ G is a discrete cocompact subgroup (a lattice). A solvmanifold is defined analogously with G solvable. By rigidity of nilmanifolds (Malcev), any homotopy equivalence between nilmanifolds is homotopic to a homeomorphism (in fact, an affine diffeomorphism). The fundamental group Γ is a torsion‑free nilpotent group, hence polycyclic. The surgery exact sequence for a nilmanifold N of dimension n ≥ 5 takes the form

⋯ → Lₙ₊₁(ℤ[Γ]) → S(N) → [N, G/O] → Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]).

Because the assembly map is an isomorphism for nilpotent groups (Farrell–Jones), Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) ≅ Hₙ(BΓ; L(ℤ)). The normal invariants [N, G/O] are isomorphic to ⊕ᵢ Hⁿ⁻ⁱ(N; Lᵢ(ℤ)). The surgery obstruction map θ corresponds to the map induced by the L‑theory orientation. The Borel conjecture holds for nilmanifolds, so S(N) is a single point. For solvmanifolds, the situation is more subtle: the fundamental group is polycyclic but not necessarily nilpotent. The Farrell–Jones conjecture holds for all polycyclic groups, so the assembly map is again an isomorphism. However, the Borel conjecture for solvmanifolds is known only in certain cases (e.g., for infrasolvmanifolds by work of Lee and Raymond). The structure set S(M) may be non‑trivial if the Borel conjecture fails. The classification of fake nilmanifolds and solvmanifolds up to homeomorphism is therefore equivalent to the computation of the kernel and cokernel of θ. The L‑groups Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) for nilpotent Γ are computed by the homology of the classifying space BΓ, which is itself a nilmanifold. The Whitehead group Wh(Γ) vanishes for torsion‑free nilpotent groups (Bass, Heller, and Swan), so simple homotopy equivalence coincides with homotopy equivalence. Examiners may ask: define a nilmanifold and a solvmanifold; state the rigidity theorem for nilmanifolds; explain why the surgery exact sequence for a nilmanifold collapses to a point; compute L₅(ℤ[Γ]) for Γ the integer Heisenberg group.

Worked Example: Surgery Obstruction for a 3‑Dimensional Nilmanifold
Let N be a closed 3‑dimensional nilmanifold with fundamental group Γ the integer Heisenberg group (upper triangular 3×3 integer matrices with 1 on the diagonal). Compute the structure set S(N) and the surgery obstruction map θ : [N, G/O] → L₃(ℤ[Γ]).
Step 1: Γ is torsion‑free nilpotent, hence polycyclic. The Farrell–Jones conjecture holds, so the assembly map A : H₃(BΓ; L(ℤ)) → L₃(ℤ[Γ]) is an isomorphism. Step 2: BΓ is a 3‑dimensional nilmanifold (Γ has Hirsch length 3). The L‑theory spectrum L(ℤ) has homotopy groups L₀(ℤ)=ℤ, L₁(ℤ)=0, L₂(ℤ)=ℤ/2, L₃(ℤ)=0. By the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence, H₃(BΓ; L(ℤ)) ≅ H₃(BΓ; ℤ) ⊕ H₁(BΓ; ℤ/2). For the Heisenberg group, H₁(Γ; ℤ) ≅ ℤ², H₃(Γ; ℤ) ≅ ℤ. Thus L₃(ℤ[Γ]) ≅ ℤ ⊕ (ℤ/2)². Step 3: The normal invariants [N, G/O] ≅ H³(N; ℤ) ⊕ H¹(N; ℤ/2) ≅ ℤ ⊕ (ℤ/2)² by Poincaré duality. Step 4: The surgery obstruction map θ corresponds under the assembly isomorphism to the map induced by the L‑theory orientation. For a nilmanifold, the L‑theory orientation is the fundamental class in H₃(BΓ; L(ℤ)). The map θ is an isomorphism (by the Borel conjecture for nilmanifolds). Step 5: Therefore S(N) = 0 (a single point). Any homotopy equivalence N' → N is homotopic to a homeomorphism.
Exam Strategy: Recognising When the Assembly Map Is an Isomorphism
The Farrell–Jones conjecture (proved for polycyclic, hyperbolic, CAT(0), and many other groups) implies the assembly map in L‑theory is an isomorphism. When a question gives a fundamental group from one of these classes, immediately replace Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) with Hₙ(BΓ; L(ℤ)) and compute using the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence. This reduces algebraic L‑theory to ordinary homology of the classifying space, which is often computable from a known presentation of Γ.

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a CAT(0) Group

A group Γ is a CAT(0) group if it acts properly and cocompactly by isometries on a CAT(0) metric space. The Farrell–Jones conjecture in L‑theory holds for all CAT(0) groups (Bartels and Lück, 2012). Hence the assembly map A : Hₙ(BΓ; L(ℤ)) → Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) is an isomorphism. For a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ Γ, the surgery exact sequence

⋯ → Lₙ₊₁(ℤ[Γ]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Lₙ(ℤ[Γ])

simplifies as before. The normal invariants [M, G/O] are computed by the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence. The surgery obstruction map θ corresponds under the assembly isomorphism to the map induced by the L‑theory orientation. The structure set S(M) classifies manifolds homotopy equivalent to M up to s‑cobordism. For CAT(0) groups, the Whitehead group Wh(Γ) vanishes for torsion‑free CAT(0) groups (Bartels, Lück, and Reich), so simple homotopy equivalence coincides with homotopy equivalence. The Borel conjecture holds for torsion‑free CAT(0) groups: any homotopy equivalence f : N → M is homotopic to a homeomorphism. Thus S(M) is a single point. For CAT(0) groups with torsion, Wh(Γ) may be non‑zero, and S(M) classifies up to s‑cobordism. Controlled surgery theory (Ferry and Quinn) provides the technical framework for proving the assembly map isomorphism. Important examples of CAT(0) groups include: fundamental groups of compact non‑positively curved manifolds; right‑angled Artin groups; mapping class groups of surfaces (Bestvina, Bromberg, and Fujiwara); and many Coxeter groups. Examiners may ask: define a CAT(0) group; state the Bartels–Lück theorem on the Farrell–Jones conjecture for CAT(0) groups; explain why the assembly map is an isomorphism; describe the structure set S(M) for an aspherical manifold with CAT(0) fundamental group; compute L₅(ℤ[Γ]) for Γ a right‑angled Artin group associated to a pentagon.

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Classification of Fake Manifolds with Fundamental Group an Arithmetic Group

Let G be a semisimple algebraic group over ℚ, and let Γ ⊂ G(ℚ) be an arithmetic subgroup (commensurable with G(ℤ)). Arithmetic groups are fundamental examples of groups satisfying the Baum–Connes conjecture (established for higher rank by Lafforgue and for rank one by Chabert–Echterhoff–Nest, among others). The Farrell–Jones conjecture in L‑theory has been established for broad classes of arithmetic groups, particularly those of type (F) (admitting a finite classifying space). For a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ Γ, the surgery exact sequence involves the L‑groups Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]). The assembly map A : Hₙ(BΓ; L(ℤ)) → Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) is conjectured to be an isomorphism (Farrell–Jones). When it is an isomorphism, the L‑groups are computed by the homology of BΓ with coefficients in the L‑theory spectrum. The normal invariants [M, G/O] are computed by the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence. The surgery obstruction map θ corresponds to the map induced by the L‑theory orientation. The structure set S(M) classifies manifolds homotopy equivalent to M up to s‑cobordism. For arithmetic groups, the Borel conjecture is known in many cases (e.g., for locally symmetric spaces of non‑positive curvature by work of Farrell–Jones and Bartels–Lück). The classification of fake arithmetic manifolds (manifolds homotopy equivalent to a locally symmetric space ΓX) is a central problem. The L‑groups Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) for arithmetic groups are related to the cohomology of the symmetric space X and the boundary strata in the Borel–Serre compactification. The Whitehead group Wh(Γ) vanishes for many arithmetic groups (Bass, Milnor, and Serre for SLₙ(ℤ), n ≥ 3; Carter and others more generally). Examiners may ask: define an arithmetic group; state the Baum–Connes conjecture for arithmetic groups; explain the relationship between the Farrell–Jones and Baum–Connes conjectures; describe the surgery exact sequence for a locally symmetric space; compute L₅(ℤ[SL₃(ℤ)]).

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Relatively Hyperbolic Group

A group Γ is relatively hyperbolic with respect to a collection of peripheral subgroups {Hᵢ} if it acts properly and cocompactly on a hyperbolic space with parabolic subgroups conjugate to the Hᵢ. The Farrell–Jones conjecture in L‑theory holds for relatively hyperbolic groups provided it holds for the peripheral subgroups (Bartels, Lück, and Reich, 2008). Hence the assembly map A : Hₙ(BΓ; L(ℤ)) → Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) is an isomorphism if the peripheral subgroups satisfy the conjecture. For a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ Γ, the surgery exact sequence

⋯ → Lₙ₊₁(ℤ[Γ]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Lₙ(ℤ[Γ])

simplifies when the assembly map is an isomorphism. The L‑groups Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) are computed by a Mayer–Vietoris type sequence involving the L‑groups of the peripheral subgroups and the L‑groups of the vertex groups in a graph of groups decomposition. The normal invariants [M, G/O] are computed by the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence. The surgery obstruction map θ corresponds to the map induced by the L‑theory orientation. The structure set S(M) classifies manifolds up to s‑cobordism. For relatively hyperbolic groups, the Whitehead group Wh(Γ) may be non‑zero even if the peripheral subgroups have vanishing Wh (Bartels and Lück). The Borel conjecture holds for torsion‑free relatively hyperbolic groups with peripheral subgroups satisfying the Borel conjecture. Important examples include: fundamental groups of finite‑volume hyperbolic manifolds with cusps (peripheral subgroups ≅ ℤⁿ); mapping class groups of punctured surfaces; and free products with amalgamation over finite subgroups. Examiners may ask: define a relatively hyperbolic group; state the Bartels–Lück–Reich theorem on the Farrell–Jones conjecture for relatively hyperbolic groups; write the Mayer–Vietoris sequence for L‑groups of a relatively hyperbolic group; explain why the assembly map is an isomorphism when the peripheral subgroups satisfy the conjecture; compute L₅(ℤ[Γ]) for Γ the fundamental group of a once‑punctured torus bundle over the circle.

  • Confusing the Borel conjecture (homotopy equivalence implies homeomorphism) with the s‑cobordism theorem (s‑cobordism implies product); the former implies the latter but not conversely.
  • Overlooking that Wh(Γ) may be non‑zero for groups with torsion even when the Farrell–Jones conjecture holds; this affects whether S(M) classifies up to homeomorphism or s‑cobordism.
  • Treating the L‑groups Lₙ(ℤ[Γ]) as purely algebraic invariants without using the assembly map to compute them via homology of BΓ; the assembly isomorphism is the primary computational tool.
  • Neglecting the distinction between the topological, PL, and smooth surgery exact sequences; the normal invariants [M, G/O], [M, G/PL], [M, G/O] differ, and the Kirby–Siebenmann invariant obstructs lifting from topological to PL.
  • Assuming the surgery obstruction map θ is always surjective; its image determines the structure set, and non‑surjectivity is the source of exotic manifolds.
  • Confusing the classification of fake nilmanifolds (where rigidity forces S(M) = 0) with fake solvmanifolds (where the Borel conjecture may fail and S(M) may be non‑trivial).

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Mapping Class Group

The mapping class group Mod(Σg) of a closed oriented surface Σg of genus g ≥ 1 is the group of isotopy classes of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms. For g ≥ 3, these groups are not relatively hyperbolic and do not act properly on CAT(0) spaces, yet they satisfy the Farrell–Jones conjecture in both K- and L-theory (Bartels and Lück, 2012). The proof uses a hierarchical hyperbolic space structure on the curve complex and a flow space construction. For a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ Mod(Σg), the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[Mod(Σg)]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[Mod(Σg)]) simplifies because the assembly map A : Hn(BMod(Σg); L(ℤ)) → Ln(ℤ[Mod(Σg)]) is an isomorphism. The L-groups are computed via the homology of the classifying space BMod(Σg) with coefficients in the L-theory spectrum. Harer's stability theorem implies Hk(Mod(Σg); ℤ) stabilizes for g ≫ k, and the stable homology is known (Madsen–Weiss theorem). The Whitehead group Wh(Mod(Σg)) vanishes for all g (Farrell–Jones implies the K-theoretic assembly map is injective, and Wh is a direct summand of K₁). The structure set S(M) therefore classifies manifolds up to homeomorphism (topological category) or diffeomorphism (smooth category, modulo the Kervaire–Milnor obstruction). Examiners may ask: state the Bartels–Lück theorem on Farrell–Jones for mapping class groups; explain why Mod(Σg) is not relatively hyperbolic for g ≥ 3; describe the role of the curve complex in the proof; compute L₅(ℤ[Mod(Σ₂)]) using Harer stability and the Madsen–Weiss theorem.

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Classification of Fake Manifolds with Fundamental Group an Outer Automorphism Group of a Free Group

The outer automorphism group Out(Fn) of a free group of rank n ≥ 2 is a central object in geometric group theory. Like mapping class groups, Out(Fn) satisfies the Farrell–Jones conjecture in K- and L-theory (Bartels and Lück, 2012), proved using the action on Outer space (Culler–Vogtmann) and a flow space construction. For a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ Out(Fn), the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[Out(Fn)]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[Out(Fn)]) has assembly isomorphism A : Hn(BOut(Fn); L(ℤ)) → Ln(ℤ[Out(Fn)]). The L-groups are computed from the homology of BOut(Fn). Hatcher and Vogtmann showed Out(Fn) is a virtual duality group of dimension 2n−3, and the rational cohomology ring is known in a stable range (Galatius, 2011). The Whitehead group Wh(Out(Fn)) vanishes. The Borel conjecture holds for torsion-free subgroups of Out(Fn) (e.g., the Torelli subgroup). Fake manifolds with fundamental group Out(Fn) are classified by the structure set S(M), which is isomorphic to the normal invariants [M, G/O] modulo the image of the surgery obstruction map θ. The map θ corresponds to the L-theory orientation of M. Examiners may ask: state the Bartels–Lück theorem for Out(Fn); explain the role of Outer space in the proof; describe the virtual duality dimension of Out(Fn); compute L₆(ℤ[Out(F₃)]) using the known homology of BOut(F₃); explain why the Borel conjecture for Out(Fn) remains open for the full group (torsion elements).

Worked Computation: L₅(ℤ[Mod(Σ₂)]) via Assembly Isomorphism
Compute the L-group L₅(ℤ[Mod(Σ₂)]) using the assembly isomorphism and Harer stability.
By the Bartels–Lück theorem, the assembly map A : H₅(BMod(Σ₂); L(ℤ)) → L₅(ℤ[Mod(Σ₂)]) is an isomorphism. The L-theory spectrum L(ℤ) has homotopy groups Lₖ(ℤ) = ℤ, 0, ℤ/2, 0 for k ≡ 0,1,2,3 mod 4 (with 4-periodicity). The Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence E²p,q = Hp(BMod(Σ₂); Lq(ℤ)) ⇒ Lp+q(ℤ[Mod(Σ₂)]) computes the target. For genus 2, Harer stability gives Hₖ(Mod(Σ₂); ℤ) for k ≤ 2 (stable range). The stable homology Hₖ(Mod(Σ∞); ℤ) is known: H₀ = ℤ, H₁ = 0, H₂ = ℤ (Mumford–Morita–Miller class κ₁). For k=5, we need E²p,q with p+q=5. Non-zero Lq(ℤ) occur at q ≡ 0,2 mod 4. So (p,q) = (5,0), (3,2), (1,4≡0). H₅(Mod(Σ₂); ℤ) is unstable; H₃(Mod(Σ₂); ℤ/2) and H₁(Mod(Σ₂); ℤ) = 0. The differential d² : E²3,2 = H₃(Mod(Σ₂); ℤ/2) → E²1,3 = 0 vanishes. The group L₅(ℤ[Mod(Σ₂)]) is an extension of the E∞ terms. In practice, for low genera, explicit computations (e.g., by Boldsen–Doll) show L₅(ℤ[Mod(Σ₂)]) ≅ ℤ/2 ⊕ (unstable part). The key step is recognizing the assembly isomorphism reduces the algebraic problem to group homology.
Exam Strategy: Assembly Map as Computational Bridge
When asked to compute Ln(ℤ[Γ]) for a group Γ satisfying Farrell–Jones, always write the assembly isomorphism A : Hn(BΓ; L(ℤ)) → Ln(ℤ[Γ]) first. Then invoke the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence with E²p,q = Hp(BΓ; Lq(ℤ)). The Lq(ℤ) coefficients are 4-periodic: ℤ, 0, ℤ/2, 0. This reduces the problem to computing group homology Hp(Γ; ℤ) and Hp(Γ; ℤ/2). For mapping class groups and Out(Fn), cite Harer stability and the Madsen–Weiss / Galatius theorems for stable homology. Examiners award marks for correctly setting up the spectral sequence even if the final extension problem is not fully resolved.

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Right-Angled Artin Group

A right-angled Artin group (RAAG) AΓ is defined by a finite simplicial graph Γ: generators correspond to vertices, with commutation relations for each edge. RAAGs interpolate between free groups (no edges) and free abelian groups (complete graph). Every RAAG acts properly and cocompactly on a CAT(0) cube complex (the Salvetti complex), so the Farrell–Jones conjecture holds in K- and L-theory (Bartels–Lück for CAT(0) groups). For a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ AΓ, the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[AΓ]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[AΓ]) has assembly isomorphism A : Hn(BAΓ; L(ℤ)) → Ln(ℤ[AΓ]). The classifying space BAΓ is the Salvetti complex, a non-positively curved cube complex of dimension equal to the clique number of Γ. Its homology is computed by the flag complex of Γ: Hk(AΓ; ℤ) is the direct sum of reduced homology groups of links of (k−1)-cliques. The L-groups Ln(ℤ[AΓ]) are therefore computable via the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence with known E² page. The Whitehead group Wh(AΓ) vanishes (Farrell–Jones + Bass–Heller–Swan for free abelian subgroups). The Borel conjecture holds for all RAAGs (Bartels–Lück–Reich). Fake manifolds with fundamental group AΓ are classified by S(M) ≅ [M, G/O] / im(θ). The surgery obstruction map θ is induced by the L-theory orientation of M. Examiners may ask: define a RAAG and its Salvetti complex; state the Farrell–Jones status for RAAGs; describe the homology of AΓ in terms of the flag complex of Γ; compute L₄(ℤ[AΓ]) for Γ a 5-cycle; explain why Wh(AΓ) = 0 implies S(M) classifies up to homeomorphism.

The Surgery Obstruction Map and the Classification of Fake Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Coxeter Group

A Coxeter group W is defined by a presentation with generators s ∈ S and relations (sisj)mij = 1, where mii = 1 and mij ∈ {2,3,…,∞} for i ≠ j. The Davis complex ΣW is a CAT(0) cell complex on which W acts properly and cocompactly. Hence the Farrell–Jones conjecture holds for all Coxeter groups (Bartels–Lück for CAT(0) groups). For a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ W, the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[W]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[W]) has assembly isomorphism A : Hn(BW; L(ℤ)) → Ln(ℤ[W]). The classifying space BW is the Davis complex modulo the W-action, an orbifold with fundamental group W. The homology of W is computed via the nerve of the Coxeter system: Hk(W; ℤ) is related to the reduced homology of spherical subcomplexes of the nerve. The L-groups Ln(ℤ[W]) are accessible via the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence. The Whitehead group Wh(W) vanishes for all Coxeter groups (Davis–Leary, using Farrell–Jones). The Borel conjecture holds for all torsion-free Coxeter groups (which are precisely the right-angled Coxeter groups with no triangles in the nerve). For Coxeter groups with torsion, the Borel conjecture is open in general, but the s-cobordism theorem holds (Wh = 0). Fake manifolds with fundamental group W are classified by S(M) up to s-cobordism. The surgery obstruction map θ corresponds to the L-theory orientation. Examiners may ask: define the Davis complex of a Coxeter group; state the Farrell–Jones status for Coxeter groups; describe the nerve and its relation to group homology; compute L₅(ℤ[W]) for W the right-angled Coxeter group of a 4-cycle; explain why Wh(W) = 0 but the Borel conjecture may fail for Coxeter groups with torsion.

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Thompson Group

Thompson's groups F, T, V are groups of piecewise-linear homeomorphisms of the unit interval (F), the circle (T), and the Cantor set (V). They are finitely presented, infinite, and have exotic properties: F is torsion-free and contains no free subgroups, T and V are simple. The Farrell–Jones conjecture for these groups is a major open problem. It is known that F satisfies the K-theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture (Wegner, 2015) using the action on the Stein complex, but the L-theoretic version remains open. For T and V, even the K-theoretic version is open. Consequently, for a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ F, T, or V, the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[Γ]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[Γ]) cannot be simplified via an assembly isomorphism. The L-groups Ln(ℤ[Γ]) are not computable by current methods. The Whitehead group Wh(F) is unknown (vanishing would follow from Farrell–Jones in K-theory). The Borel conjecture for F is open; for T and V it is open. Fake manifolds with these fundamental groups are not classified. The surgery obstruction map θ : [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[Γ]) cannot be analyzed algebraically. This represents a frontier where geometric topology meets deep open problems in group theory.   • Confusing the stable homology of Mod(Σg) (Madsen–Weiss) with the unstable homology for a fixed genus; examiners often ask for Ln(ℤ[Mod(Σ₂)]) where stability does not apply in high degrees.
  • Treating RAAGs and Coxeter groups as identical because both are CAT(0); their classifying spaces (Salvetti vs. Davis complex) and homology computations differ fundamentally.
  • Stating that Wh(F) = 0 for Thompson's group F; this is an open problem equivalent to the K-theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture for F.
  • Claiming the Borel conjecture holds for all Coxeter groups; it is only known for torsion-free (right-angled, triangle-free) Coxeter groups.
  • Neglecting that the surgery obstruction map θ for groups with unknown L-groups (e.g., Thompson groups) cannot be analyzed, so the structure set S(M) is not computable.

These advanced topics illustrate the boundary between computable surgery theory (groups satisfying Farrell–Jones) and the frontier where fundamental group properties remain mysterious.

Surgery Theory for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Nilpotent Group and the Malcev Completion

Nilpotent groups form a cornerstone in high‑dimensional surgery because they are the simplest non‑abelian groups for which the Farrell–Jones conjecture is known in full generality. A theorem of Farrell and Jones (later refined by Bartels, Lück, and Reich) establishes that the assembly map

A : Hn(BG; L(ℤ)) → Ln(ℤ[G])

is an isomorphism for every virtually nilpotent group G. Consequently, for a closed topological manifold M of dimension n ≥ 5 with π₁(M) ≅ G virtually nilpotent, the surgery exact sequence

⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[G]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[G])

can be rewritten entirely in terms of the homology of BG with coefficients in the L‑theory spectrum. The classifying space BG for a torsion‑free nilpotent group is a compact nilmanifold; its homology is computable via the Malcev completion, which embeds G as a uniform lattice in a simply connected nilpotent Lie group N. The rational homotopy type of BG is captured by the Chevalley–Eilenberg complex of the Lie algebra of N, and this algebraic model feeds directly into the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence for L‑theory.

The Whitehead group Wh(G) vanishes for all torsion‑free nilpotent groups (a consequence of the isomorphism Wh(G) ≅ K₁(ℤ[G])/±G and the fact that the assembly map in K‑theory is an isomorphism). Hence the s‑cobordism theorem holds: any h‑cobordism between manifolds with fundamental group G is a product. The Borel conjecture is also known for torsion‑free nilpotent groups; any homotopy equivalence between closed manifolds with such fundamental groups is homotopic to a homeomorphism. For virtually nilpotent groups with torsion, the Borel conjecture remains open in general, and Wh(G) may be non‑zero because the Whitehead group of a finite quotient need not vanish; consequently the s‑cobordism theorem is not guaranteed in this case.

The Malcev completion provides a bridge between the algebraic surgery obstruction groups Ln(ℤ[G]) and the representation theory of the associated Lie group. In particular, the surgery obstruction map θ : [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[G]) can be analyzed via the rho‑invariant of the associated flat bundle over M, which in the nilpotent case can be expressed in terms of eta‑invariants on the nilmanifold BG. The structure set S(M) fits into the exact sequence ⋯ → Hn+1(BG; L) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Hn(BG; L) → ⋯, so S(M) is the kernel of the surgery obstruction map [M, G/O] → Hn(BG; L). For nilpotent groups this kernel is often a finite abelian group computable from Lie algebra cohomology.

Examiners frequently test the following points: the role of the Malcev completion in computing L‑groups; the vanishing of Wh(G) for torsion‑free nilpotent groups; the distinction between the Borel conjecture (known) and the s‑cobordism theorem (also known) for these groups; and the use of the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence to compute Ln(ℤ[G]) from H*(BG; L(ℤ)).

Surgery Theory for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Group of Polynomial Growth and Gromov's Theorem

Gromov's celebrated theorem states that a finitely generated group has polynomial growth if and only if it is virtually nilpotent. This equivalence collapses the surgery theory for groups of polynomial growth onto the virtually nilpotent case discussed in the previous section. For a closed manifold Mⁿ (n ≥ 5) with π₁(M) of polynomial growth, the fundamental group is virtually nilpotent, so the full Farrell–Jones isomorphism in both K‑ and L‑theory is available. The surgery exact sequence therefore simplifies to

⋯ → Hn+1(BG; L(ℤ)) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Hn(BG; L(ℤ)) → ⋯

where the assembly map is an isomorphism. The classifying space BG is homotopy equivalent to a finite complex (a compact nilmanifold modulo a finite group action), and its homology can be computed from the rational Lie algebra of the Malcev completion of a nilpotent subgroup of finite index.

A subtle point arises when the finite index nilpotent subgroup has torsion. The virtually nilpotent group G fits into an exact sequence 1 → N → G → F → 1 with N torsion‑free nilpotent and F finite. The L‑groups Ln(ℤ[G]) are then accessible via the spectral sequence of the group extension, and the assembly map for G factors through the assembly map for N together with the transfer maps induced by F. For torsion‑free virtually nilpotent groups, Wh(G) = 0 and the s‑cobordism theorem holds. For groups with torsion, Wh(G) may be non‑zero because Wh(F) for a finite group F is not always zero (the Bass–Heller–Swan theorem gives a formula but not universal vanishing), so the s‑cobordism theorem can fail.

The Borel conjecture for groups of polynomial growth is known in the torsion‑free case (i.e., for nilpotent groups) and remains open for virtually nilpotent groups with torsion, although the s‑cobordism theorem provides a strong rigidity statement up to s‑cobordism when it applies. The structure set S(M) classifies manifolds homotopy equivalent to M up to s‑cobordism; when the Borel conjecture holds, this classification is up to homeomorphism.

Examiners may ask: state Gromov's theorem and explain its impact on surgery theory; describe how the surgery exact sequence for a virtually nilpotent group reduces to homology of BG; compute L₅(ℤ[G]) for G the discrete Heisenberg group (a torsion‑free nilpotent group of rank 3); discuss why Wh(G) = 0 for torsion‑free groups of polynomial growth but may be non‑zero with torsion; and outline the role of the finite quotient F in the spectral sequence for L‑theory.

Worked Example: Surgery Obstruction for a Nilmanifold
Let M be a closed smooth 7‑manifold with π₁(M) ≅ H₃(ℤ), the integer Heisenberg group. The normal invariant of a degree‑one normal map f : N → M corresponds to an element x ∈ [M, G/O] ≅ H⁷(M; ℤ/2) ⊕ H³(M; ℤ) (using the known homotopy groups of G/O in this range). Compute the surgery obstruction θ(x) ∈ L₇(ℤ[H₃(ℤ)]) and determine whether f is normally cobordant to a homotopy equivalence.
The group H₃(ℤ) is torsion‑free nilpotent of class 2. By the Farrell–Jones isomorphism, L₇(ℤ[H₃(ℤ)]) ≅ H₇(BH₃(ℤ); L(ℤ)). The classifying space BH₃(ℤ) is a compact nilmanifold of dimension 3, so its homology vanishes above degree 3. The L‑theory spectrum L(ℤ) has homotopy groups Lₖ(ℤ) = ℤ, ℤ/2, 0, ℤ for k ≡ 0,1,2,3 (mod 4). The Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence E²p,q = Hp(BH₃(ℤ); Lq(ℤ)) converges to Lp+q(ℤ[H₃(ℤ)]). Since BH₃(ℤ) has dimension 3, only p ≤ 3 contribute. For total degree 7, the only possible (p,q) pairs are (3,4), (2,5), (1,6), (0,7). But L₄(ℤ)=ℤ, L₅(ℤ)=0, L₆(ℤ)=0, L₇(ℤ)=ℤ. Thus E²3,4 = H₃(BH₃(ℤ); ℤ) ≅ ℤ, and E²0,7 = H₀(BH₃(ℤ); ℤ) ≅ ℤ. All differentials vanish for degree reasons, so L₇(ℤ[H₃(ℤ)]) ≅ ℤ ⊕ ℤ. The surgery obstruction map θ factors through the assembly isomorphism, so θ(x) corresponds to the image of x under the Pontryagin–Thom construction followed by the assembly map. In practice, the ℤ summand from H₃ detects the Kervaire invariant, while the ℤ summand from H₀ detects the signature defect. For a degree‑one normal map, the signature defect vanishes, so θ(x) lies in the H₃ summand. If the Kervaire invariant of x is zero, then θ(x)=0 and f is normally cobordant to a homotopy equivalence; otherwise the obstruction is non‑zero and no such cobordism exists.
Exam Strategy
When a question mentions a group of polynomial growth, immediately replace it with "virtually nilpotent" and invoke the Farrell–Jones isomorphism. Write down the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence for L‑theory with E² page Hp(BG; Lq(ℤ)). Remember that BG has finite dimension equal to the Hirsch length of the nilpotent subgroup. This reduces L‑group computations to group homology, which is often tractable via the Malcev completion. Always check whether the group has torsion; if it does, the Borel conjecture may be open and Wh(G) may be non‑zero, so the s‑cobordism theorem is not automatic.

Surgery Theory for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Group Acting Properly on a CAT(0) Cube Complex

Groups that act properly and cocompactly on finite‑dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes form a broad class containing right‑angled Artin groups, right‑angled Coxeter groups, and many other groups arising in geometric group theory. A deep theorem of Bartels, Lück, and Reich (building on work of Haglund–Wise and others) proves that the Farrell–Jones conjecture in both K‑ and L‑theory holds for all such groups. Consequently, for a closed topological manifold Mⁿ (n ≥ 5) with π₁(M) = G acting properly on a CAT(0) cube complex, the assembly map

A : Hn(BG; L(ℤ)) → Ln(ℤ[G])

is an isomorphism. The surgery exact sequence therefore becomes a long exact sequence of homology groups of BG with coefficients in the L‑theory spectrum.

The classifying space BG for these groups can often be taken to be the quotient of the CAT(0) cube complex by the G‑action, which is a compact non‑positively curved cube complex. Its homology is computable via the combinatorics of the cube complex: the chain complex of the cube complex gives a free ℤ[G]‑resolution of ℤ, and the associated spectral sequence for L‑theory has E² page Hp(BG; Lq(ℤ)). Because the cube complex is finite‑dimensional, the spectral sequence collapses at a finite stage, making L‑group computations feasible in principle.

For torsion‑free groups in this class, the Whitehead group Wh(G) vanishes (a consequence of the K‑theoretic Farrell–Jones isomorphism), so the s‑cobordism theorem holds: any h‑cobordism between manifolds with such fundamental groups is a product. For groups with torsion, the vanishing of Wh(G) is not guaranteed in general. The Borel conjecture is known for torsion‑free groups in this class (by work of Bartels–Lück using controlled topology), but remains open for groups with torsion.

A key technical tool is the "controlled assembly map" which compares the L‑theory of the group ring with the L‑theory of the category of controlled modules over the cube complex. The vanishing of the relative assembly map implies the isomorphism. Examiners may ask: define a CAT(0) cube complex and a proper action; state the Farrell–Jones status for this class; explain why the assembly map is an isomorphism; describe how the cube complex structure yields a finite model for BG; and discuss the relationship between the vanishing of Wh(G) and the s‑cobordism theorem.

Surgery Theory for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Group with Finite Decomposition Complexity

Finite decomposition complexity (FDC) is a coarse geometric notion introduced by Guentner, Tessera, and Yu that generalizes finite asymptotic dimension. A metric space has FDC if it can be decomposed into uniformly bounded pieces after finitely many steps of a decomposition process. Groups with FDC include all groups with finite asymptotic dimension, all relatively hyperbolic groups with peripheral subgroups of FDC, and many others. A landmark theorem of Bartels, Lück, and Reich establishes that the Farrell–Jones conjecture in both K‑ and L‑theory holds for all groups with finite decomposition complexity.

For a closed manifold Mⁿ (n ≥ 5) with π₁(M) = G having FDC, the assembly map A : Hn(BG; L(ℤ)) → Ln(ℤ[G]) is an isomorphism. The surgery exact sequence therefore reduces to a homology exact sequence. The classifying space BG need not be a finite complex, but the FDC property allows one to construct a "controlled" model for the assembly map using the decomposition of the group. This controlled approach is essential because BG may have infinite geometric dimension.

For torsion‑free groups with FDC, the Whitehead group Wh(G) vanishes (by the K‑theoretic Farrell–Jones isomorphism), so the s‑cobordism theorem holds. For groups with torsion, Wh(G) may not vanish, and the s‑cobordism theorem is not universally guaranteed. The Borel conjecture is known for torsion‑free groups with FDC (by work of Bartels–Lück–Weinberger using controlled surgery), but remains open for groups with torsion.

A practical consequence is that the structure set S(M) for manifolds with FDC fundamental groups is computable up to the homology of BG. In many cases, BG can be approximated by a sequence of finite complexes whose homology stabilizes, allowing explicit calculations of L‑groups via the Atiyah–Hirzebruch spectral sequence. Examiners may test: definition of finite decomposition complexity; statement of the Farrell–Jones theorem for FDC groups; why the assembly map is an isomorphism despite BG possibly being infinite‑dimensional; the role of controlled algebra in the proof; and the distinction between the s‑cobordism theorem (true for torsion‑free groups) and the Borel conjecture (open with torsion).

Surgery Theory for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Group with the Haagerup Property and the Baum–Connes Conjecture

The Haagerup property (also known as a‑T‑menability) is a weak form of amenability that holds for many groups, including free groups, Coxeter groups, and groups acting properly on Hilbert spaces. The Baum–Connes conjecture predicts that the assembly map in K‑theory for the reduced group C*‑algebra is an isomorphism. Higson and Kasparov proved that the Baum–Connes conjecture holds for all groups with the Haagerup property. This has profound implications for surgery theory because the Novikov conjecture (homotopy invariance of higher signatures) follows from the Baum–Connes conjecture via the Mishchenko–Fomenko assembly map.

However, the Farrell–Jones conjecture in L‑theory (which is the relevant assembly map for the surgery exact sequence) is not known to follow from the Baum–Connes conjecture. For a closed manifold Mⁿ (n ≥ 5) with π₁(M) = G having the Haagerup property, we cannot in general replace Ln(ℤ[G]) by Hn(BG; L(ℤ)). The surgery exact sequence remains

⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[G]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[G]) → ⋯

with L‑groups that are often inaccessible. The vanishing of Wh(G) is also not guaranteed by the Haagerup property alone; it is known for some classes (e.g., free groups, Coxeter groups) but open in general. Consequently, the s‑cobordism theorem may fail for some Haagerup groups if Wh(G) ≠ 0.

The interplay between the Baum–Connes assembly map (in K‑theory of C*‑algebras) and the Farrell–Jones assembly map (in L‑theory of group rings) is a central theme. The former gives information about the Novikov conjecture and the structure of the reduced C*‑algebra, while the latter controls the surgery exact sequence. For groups where both conjectures hold (e.g., hyperbolic groups, CAT(0) groups), the surgery theory is completely computable. For Haagerup groups where only Baum–Connes is known, the surgery obstruction map θ : [M, G/O] → Ln(ℤ[G]) cannot be analyzed algebraically, and the structure set S(M) remains mysterious.

Examiners may ask: state the Higson–Kasparov theorem; explain why the Baum–Connes conjecture does not imply the Farrell–Jones conjecture in L‑theory; give examples of Haagerup groups for which Wh(G) is known to vanish and for which it is unknown; discuss the implications for the classification of manifolds with such fundamental groups; and describe the role of the Mishchenko–Fomenko assembly map in connecting higher signatures to the surgery exact sequence.

  • Confusing the Farrell–Jones isomorphism (L‑theory of group rings) with the Baum–Connes isomorphism (K‑theory of reduced C*‑algebras); they are distinct assembly maps with different geometric consequences.
  • Assuming that the Haagerup property implies vanishing of the Whitehead group; this is false in general and is a common trap.
  • Stating that the Borel conjecture holds for all groups with finite decomposition complexity; it is only known for torsion‑free such groups.
  • Neglecting that the assembly map for groups acting on CAT(0) cube complexes requires a proper, cocompact action; a merely proper action is insufficient for the known proofs.
  • Treating the Malcev completion as a tool only for nilpotent groups; it also applies to virtually nilpotent groups after passing to a finite index subgroup.
  • Claiming that Gromov's theorem on polynomial growth gives a direct computation of L‑groups without invoking the virtually nilpotent structure; the reduction to a nilpotent subgroup of finite index is essential.
  • Overlooking that the s‑cobordism theorem for groups with FDC follows from the K‑theoretic Farrell–Jones isomorphism, not from the L‑theoretic one; the two are independent statements.

Surgery Theory for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Group with Property (T)

Groups with Kazhdan's property (T) — such as higher rank lattices SL(n,ℤ) for n ≥ 3, Sp(2n,ℤ), and uniform lattices in simple Lie groups of real rank at least two — present a distinctive challenge in surgery theory. The Baum–Connes conjecture in K‑theory of the reduced group C*‑algebra is known for many property (T) groups through the work of Lafforgue, Kasparov, and others, which yields the Novikov conjecture on homotopy invariance of higher signatures. However, the Farrell–Jones conjecture in L‑theory of the group ring ℤ[G], which controls the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[G]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[G]) → ⋯, remains open for most infinite property (T) groups. Consequently, the assembly map A: H_*(BG; L(ℤ)) → L_*(ℤ[G]) cannot be replaced by an isomorphism, and the L‑groups L_n(ℤ[G]) are largely inaccessible by algebraic topology alone.

The surgery obstruction map θ: [M, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[G]) therefore cannot be analyzed via the homology of BG. The structure set S(M) of a closed manifold M^n (n ≥ 5) with π₁(M) = G stays mysterious; even the question of whether S(M) is finite or infinite is generally unanswered. The s‑cobordism theorem for such G requires the vanishing of the Whitehead group Wh(G). For property (T) groups, Wh(G) is often unknown — it vanishes for some arithmetic lattices by work of Farrell–Jones but is open in general. If Wh(G) ≠ 0, there exist non‑trivial h‑cobordisms that are not products, so the classification of manifolds up to s‑cobordism fails.

A common examiner trap is to conflate the Baum–Connes isomorphism (K‑theory of C*_r(G)) with the Farrell–Jones isomorphism (L‑theory of ℤ[G]). They are distinct assembly maps with different geometric consequences: the former gives the Novikov conjecture, the latter gives the surgery exact sequence. Another trap is assuming that property (T) implies Wh(G) = 0; this is false — there are property (T) groups with non‑trivial Whitehead group (e.g., certain finite extensions of ℤ^n). A third trap is stating that the Borel conjecture holds for all property (T) groups; it is only known for those that are torsion‑free and satisfy the L‑theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture, which is a much smaller class.

In practice, when a problem asks about the classification of manifolds with fundamental group a higher rank lattice, the correct approach is to state that the surgery exact sequence cannot be simplified algebraically, that the Novikov conjecture holds via Baum–Connes, but that the s‑cobordism theorem and the Borel conjecture are open. If the group is known to satisfy the L‑theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture (e.g., certain uniform lattices in Sp(n,1) by work of Bartels–Lück–Reich), then the assembly map is an isomorphism and the structure set becomes computable up to the homology of BG.

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Braid Group

The braid groups B_n (n ≥ 2) are torsion‑free, have finite asymptotic dimension, and satisfy the Farrell–Jones conjecture in both K‑ and L‑theory (proved by Wegner, Bartels–Lück, and others). Their classifying space BB_n is the configuration space of n unordered points in ℝ², whose homology is well understood via the Cohen–Taylor spectral sequence. For a closed manifold M^n (n ≥ 5) with π₁(M) = B_n, the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[B_n]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[B_n]) → ⋯ can be analyzed through the assembly map A: H_*(BB_n; L(ℤ)) → L_*(ℤ[B_n]), which is an isomorphism. This reduces the computation of the L‑groups to the homology of BB_n with coefficients in the quadratic L‑spectrum L(ℤ).

The structure set S(M) therefore fits into a long exact sequence of abelian groups whose terms are the homology groups H_*(BB_n; L(ℤ)). In particular, the surgery obstruction map θ: [M, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[B_n]) corresponds under the assembly isomorphism to a map from the normal invariants to a direct sum of homology groups. The Whitehead group Wh(B_n) is known to vanish (Farrell–Jones in K‑theory plus the fact that B_n is torsion‑free), so the s‑cobordism theorem holds: any h‑cobordism between manifolds with fundamental group B_n is a product. However, the Borel conjecture — that every homotopy equivalence of closed aspherical manifolds with fundamental group B_n is homotopic to a homeomorphism — is not a formal consequence of the Farrell–Jones isomorphism alone; it requires the additional vanishing of the structure set for all such manifolds, which is still open for braid groups in dimensions ≥ 5.

Examiners often test the distinction between the configuration space model for BB_n and the classifying space of a hyperbolic group. Braid groups are not hyperbolic for n > 3 (they contain ℤ² subgroups), so techniques relying on hyperbolicity (e.g., the Rips complex) do not apply. Another trap is assuming that the assembly map isomorphism immediately implies the Borel conjecture; one must also verify that the structure set S(M) is trivial, which involves analyzing the map [M, G/O] → H_n(BB_n; L(ℤ)) and showing it is injective with image equal to the kernel of the next map. A third trap is confusing the braid group B_n with the pure braid group P_n; the latter is a finite index subgroup, so the Farrell–Jones conjecture for B_n implies it for P_n, but the L‑groups differ by induction/restriction.

Identifying the correct assembly map
A closed 6‑manifold M has fundamental group B4. The problem asks: which assembly map must be an isomorphism to compute the surgery obstruction group L6(ℤ[B4]) from the homology of BB4? Explain why the Baum–Connes assembly map is insufficient.
The relevant assembly map is the Farrell–Jones assembly map in L‑theory: A_L: H_*(BB4; L(ℤ)) → L_*(ℤ[B4]). The Baum–Connes assembly map A_K: K_*top(BB4) → K_*(C*_r(B4)) computes the K‑theory of the reduced C*‑algebra and yields the Novikov conjecture, but it does not give information about the quadratic L‑groups that appear in the surgery exact sequence. Since the surgery obstruction map θ lands in L6(ℤ[B4]), one needs the L‑theoretic isomorphism to replace L6(ℤ[B4]) by H6(BB4; L(ℤ)) ⊕ lower degree terms. The Baum–Connes map cannot achieve this because its target is K‑theory of a C*‑algebra, not L‑theory of a group ring.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mentions a specific group (braid group, mapping class group, etc.), first recall whether the group is known to satisfy the Farrell–Jones conjecture in L‑theory. If yes, write down the assembly map isomorphism and the resulting identification of L‑groups with homology of the classifying space. Then state explicitly what remains open (Borel conjecture, computation of the structure set, vanishing of Wh(G)). This two‑step answer — what is known, what is not — captures the examiner's expectation for advanced surgery theory questions.

Surgery Obstruction Map and the Classification of Fake Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Limit Group

Limit groups (also called fully residually free groups) are finitely generated groups that arise as limits of sequences of homomorphisms into a free group. They are torsion‑free, have finite asymptotic dimension, and act properly on CAT(0) cube complexes (by work of Guirardel). The Farrell–Jones conjecture in L‑theory is known for limit groups through the combination of the Bartels–Lück–Reich theorem for groups with finite decomposition complexity and the fact that limit groups have finite decomposition complexity (proved by Guentner–Tessera–Yu). Consequently, the assembly map A: H_*(BG; L(ℤ)) → L_*(ℤ[G]) is an isomorphism for any limit group G.

For a closed manifold M^n (n ≥ 5) with π₁(M) = G a limit group, the surgery exact sequence becomes ⋯ → Hn+1(BG; L(ℤ)) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → H_n(BG; L(ℤ)) → ⋯. The structure set S(M) is therefore an extension of a subgroup of the normal invariants by a quotient of the homology of BG. The surgery obstruction map θ: [M, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[G]) corresponds under the assembly isomorphism to a map from the normal invariants to H_n(BG; L(ℤ)). The Whitehead group Wh(G) vanishes for limit groups (they are torsion‑free and satisfy the K‑theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture), so the s‑cobordism theorem holds: any h‑cobordism is a product.

The Borel conjecture for limit groups — that every homotopy equivalence of closed aspherical manifolds with fundamental group a limit group is homotopic to a homeomorphism — is equivalent to the vanishing of the structure set S(M) for all such M. This is not known in general. A key difficulty is that the classifying space BG for a limit group is not a finite complex; its homology can be computed via the action on a CAT(0) cube complex, but the map [M, G/O] → H_n(BG; L(ℤ)) is hard to analyze. Examiners may ask for the definition of a limit group, the status of the Farrell–Jones conjecture, and why the Borel conjecture does not follow automatically from the assembly isomorphism. A common trap is to assume that all limit groups are hyperbolic; they are not (e.g., ℤ² is a limit group). Another trap is to confuse the L‑theoretic assembly map with the K‑theoretic one; the former controls surgery, the latter controls the Novikov conjecture.

The Surgery Exact Sequence for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Graph Manifold Group

Graph manifold groups are fundamental groups of compact 3‑manifolds that are unions of Seifert fibered pieces along tori. By the work of Liu, Przytycki, and Wise, these groups are virtually compact special, hence they act properly and cocompactly on CAT(0) cube complexes. They have finite asymptotic dimension and satisfy the Farrell–Jones conjecture in both K‑ and L‑theory (Bartels–Lück–Reich for FDC groups). For a closed manifold M^n (n ≥ 5) with π₁(M) = G a graph manifold group, the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[G]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[G]) → ⋯ can be rewritten using the assembly isomorphism A: H_*(BG; L(ℤ)) → L_*(ℤ[G]). The structure set S(M) fits into a long exact sequence of homology groups of BG with coefficients in L(ℤ).

The Whitehead group Wh(G) vanishes because G is torsion‑free and satisfies the K‑theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture, so the s‑cobordism theorem holds. The Borel conjecture for aspherical manifolds with fundamental group a graph manifold group is known in dimension 3 by Perelman's geometrization (any homotopy equivalence of closed graph manifolds is homotopic to a homeomorphism). In higher dimensions, however, the Borel conjecture is open: one must show that the structure set S(M) is trivial for every closed aspherical M with π₁(M) = G. This requires analyzing the map [M, G/O] → H_n(BG; L(ℤ)) and proving it is injective with image equal to the kernel of the next map, which is a difficult problem in controlled topology.

Examiners often test the distinction between graph manifold groups and hyperbolic 3‑manifold groups. Graph manifold groups contain ℤ² subgroups (the torus fibers), so they are not hyperbolic. Another trap is assuming that the Borel conjecture for graph manifolds in dimension 3 implies the Borel conjecture for all manifolds with the same fundamental group; the dimension of the manifold matters because the surgery exact sequence depends on n. A third trap is confusing the virtually compact special property with the group being a right‑angled Artin group; graph manifold groups are not RAAGs but are virtually special, which is sufficient for the Farrell–Jones conjecture via the cube complex action.

Surgery Theory for Manifolds with Fundamental Group a Group with Finite Asymptotic Dimension

Finite asymptotic dimension (FAD) is a coarse geometric invariant introduced by Gromov. Groups with FAD include hyperbolic groups, CAT(0) groups, mapping class groups (conjecturally), and many others. A fundamental result of Guentner, Tessera, and Yu shows that finite asymptotic dimension implies finite decomposition complexity (FDC). By the Bartels–Lück–Reich theorem, the K‑theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture holds for all groups with FDC, hence for all groups with FAD. The L‑theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture is also known for many classes of groups with FAD (e.g., groups acting properly on CAT(0) spaces with finite asymptotic dimension), but it is not known in full generality for all FAD groups.

For a closed manifold M^n (n ≥ 5) with π₁(M) = G a group of finite asymptotic dimension, the surgery exact sequence ⋯ → Ln+1(ℤ[G]) → S(M) → [M, G/O] → L_n(ℤ[G]) → ⋯ can be approached via controlled surgery. If the L‑theoretic assembly map A: H_*(BG; L(ℤ)) → L_*(ℤ[G]) is an isomorphism (which holds for many FAD groups), then the structure set S(M) is computable up to the homology of BG. The Whitehead group Wh(G) vanishes for torsion‑free groups satisfying the K‑theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture, so the s‑cobordism theorem holds for torsion‑free FAD groups. The Borel conjecture for such groups is equivalent to the vanishing of S(M) for all aspherical M with that fundamental group, which remains open in general.

A frequent examiner trap is to conflate finite asymptotic dimension with finite decomposition complexity; FAD implies FDC, but the converse is false (there are groups with FDC but infinite asymptotic dimension). Another trap is assuming that the L‑theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture holds for all FAD groups; it is only known for subclasses (e.g., groups with a finite dimensional classifying space for proper actions). A third trap is stating that the Borel conjecture follows from the assembly map isomorphism; one must also prove that the structure set is trivial, which requires additional geometric input such as the existence of a fundamental domain for the action on a contractible space.

  • Assuming property (T) implies vanishing of the Whitehead group; Wh(G) can be non‑trivial for property (T) groups.
  • Believing that the assembly map isomorphism automatically yields the Borel conjecture; the structure set must also vanish.
  • Treating braid groups as hyperbolic for n > 3; they contain ℤ² subgroups and are not hyperbolic.
  • Equating finite asymptotic dimension with finite decomposition complexity; FAD ⇒ FDC but not conversely.
  • Assuming all limit groups are hyperbolic; ℤ² is a limit group and is not hyperbolic.
  • Thinking the Borel conjecture for graph manifolds in dimension 3 implies it in all dimensions; the surgery exact sequence depends on the manifold dimension.
