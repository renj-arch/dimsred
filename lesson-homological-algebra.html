Homological Algebra — The Algebra of Long Exact Sequences

Homological algebra studies chain complexes and their homology — powerful algebraic invariants that measure the failure of exactness. It provides the language of derived functors (Ext, Tor), spectral sequences (computational machinery), derived categories (global homotopy theory), and homotopical algebra (model categories). It's the algebraic backbone of algebraic topology, algebraic geometry, and representation theory.

"Homological algebra is the art of computing invariants by resolving objects into simpler pieces."
— Henri Cartan / Samuel Eilenberg
Part 1

🔗 Chain Complexes and Homology

Complexes
Chain Complexes, Exact Sequences, and the Snake Lemma
A chain complex (C_•, d) is a sequence ... → C_n → C_{n-1} → ... with d_{n-1}∘d_n = 0. The homology H_n(C) = ker d_n / im d_{n+1} measures the failure of exactness. A short exact sequence 0 → A → B → C → 0 gives a long exact sequence in homology: ... → H_n(A) → H_n(B) → H_n(C) → H_{n-1}(A) → ... The connecting morphism ∂: H_n(C) → H_{n-1}(A) is constructed via the snake lemma — the fundamental diagram-chasing result. Projective objects lift maps through epimorphisms — used to construct projective resolutions. Injective objects extend maps through monomorphisms. Every R-module has a projective resolution P_• → M → 0 and an injective resolution 0 → M → I_•. The Horseshoe lemma combines resolutions of A and C to give a resolution of B.
Homology is the universal measure of inexactness — every short exact sequence spawns a long exact sequence in homology via the snake lemma.
Part 2

🧪 Derived Functors — Ext and Tor

Derived
Ext and Tor — Measuring Extension and Torsion
Derived functors measure the failure of a functor to be exact. For a right exact functor F, the left derived functors L_nF are computed by applying F to a projective resolution and taking homology. The Tor functor Tor_n^R(A,B) = L_n(−⊗_R B)(A) = H_n(P_• ⊗_R B). Tor_0(A,B) = A⊗B. Tor_1(A,B) = 0 if A is flat. For abelian groups, Tor_1(ℤ/m, ℤ/n) = ℤ/gcd(m,n). The Ext functor Ext^n_R(A,B) = R^n Hom_R(−,B)(A) = H^n Hom_R(P_•, B). Ext⁰(A,B) = Hom(A,B). Ext¹(ℤ/m, ℤ) = ℤ/m. Ext classifies extensions: Ext¹(A,B) is in bijection with isomorphism classes of extensions 0 → B → E → A → 0. The Yoneda product Extᵖ × Ext^q → Ext^{p+q} gives a graded ring structure on Ext*(A,A). Universal coefficient theorems relate homology with coefficients to Hom and Tor.
Tor measures torsion and common divisors; Ext measures extensions and obstructions — they're the fundamental derived functors of tensor product and Hom.
Part 3

📐 Spectral Sequences — The Computational Workhorse

Spectral
Spectral Sequences — Approximating Homology via Filtrations
A spectral sequence is a sequence of pages (E^r_{p,q}, d^r) converging to a target H_{p+q}. It's a computational tool that approximates the homology of a chain complex with a filtration. The Leray-Serre spectral sequence: for a fibration F → E → B, E²_{p,q} = H_p(B; H_q(F)) ⇒ H_{p+q}(E). The Grothendieck spectral sequence: for composite functors G∘F, E²_{p,q} = (R^p G)(R^q F)(A) ⇒ R^{p+q}(G∘F)(A). The Lyndon-Hochschild-Serre spectral sequence for group cohomology: E²_{p,q} = H_p(G/N; H_q(N, A)) ⇒ H_{p+q}(G; A). The Adams spectral sequence computes stable homotopy groups. The edge homomorphisms and differentials give relations between the input and output. A spectral sequence collapses at E^r if all differentials are zero from that page onward — common when there are only two rows or columns.
Spectral sequences are the universal approximation machine — they let you compute homology by first understanding a simpler (graded) version and correcting page by page.
Part 4

🔄 Derived Categories and Triangulated Structures

Derived
Derived Categories, Triangulated Categories, and Verdier Duality
The derived category D(A) of an abelian category A is obtained by formally inverting quasi-isomorphisms (maps inducing isomorphisms on homology). Objects are chain complexes; morphisms are roofs (zigzags). A triangulated category has a shift functor [1] and distinguished triangles X → Y → Z → X[1] that generalize short exact sequences. The Verdier octahedral axiom governs composition of triangles. The derived functor in the derived category sense is the extension of a functor to D(A) that preserves quasi-isomorphisms. The derived tensor product ⊗^L and derived Hom Rℋℴ𝓂 are the correct derived-category-level operations. Grothendieck duality: for a proper morphism f of schemes, the right adjoint f^! of Rf_* exists — the dualizing complex. The derived category D^b Coh(X) is the natural setting for Serre duality and the Riemann-Roch theorem. Koszul duality relates the derived category of a symmetric algebra to that of an exterior algebra.
Derived categories are the homotopy theory of homological algebra — they replace chain complexes up to quasi-isomorphism, making the derived functors exact.

Hypercohomology and the Grothendieck Spectral Sequence for Sheaves

Hypercohomology extends ordinary sheaf cohomology to complexes of sheaves. Given a bounded below complex of sheaves F• on a topological space X, one chooses an injective resolution I•,• (a double complex) and defines the hypercohomology groups Hn(X, F•) as the cohomology of the total complex Tot(I•,•). The Grothendieck spectral sequence arises from the composition of two left exact functors: the global sections functor Γ(X, –) and the forgetful functor from complexes to sheaves. Its E2-page is E2p,q = Hp(X, Hq(F•)) converging to Hp+q(X, F•). Convergence is guaranteed when F• is bounded below and each cohomology sheaf Hq(F•) has finite injective dimension, or more generally when the spectral sequence satisfies the usual boundedness conditions. The edge maps give the natural morphisms Hn(X, F•) → H0(X, Hn(F•)) and Hn(X, H0(F•)) → Hn(X, F•). In practice, the spectral sequence collapses at E2 when the complex is a single sheaf placed in degree zero, recovering ordinary sheaf cohomology. For a short exact sequence of complexes 0 → A• → B• → C• → 0, the associated long exact sequence in hypercohomology is obtained by splicing the long exact sequences of the E2-pages. This machinery underlies the proof of the Leray spectral sequence for a continuous map f: X → Y by applying the Grothendieck spectral sequence to the composition Γ(Y, –) ∘ f*.

Derived Limits and the Milnor Exact Sequence

The derived functors of the inverse limit functor lim on the category of towers of abelian groups (or more generally modules) are denoted lim1, lim2, … . For a tower … → A2 → A1 → A0, the first derived functor lim1 measures the obstruction to the exactness of 0 → lim An → ∏ An → ∏ An → 0, where the middle map is (xn) ↦ (xn – fn+1(xn+1)). The Milnor exact sequence (also called the Milnor lim1 sequence) for a tower of chain complexes Cn• reads 0 → lim1 Hq-1(Cn) → Hq(lim Cn) → lim Hq(Cn) → 0. This sequence is natural and splits non‑canonically. A tower satisfies the Mittag‑Leffler condition if for each n the images of the maps Am → An stabilize for large m; under this condition lim1 An = 0. In particular, towers of finite groups or finite‑dimensional vector spaces over a field always satisfy Mittag‑Leffler, so their lim1 vanishes. The derived functors limi for i ≥ 2 vanish on towers of modules over a Noetherian ring when the tower consists of finitely generated modules. The Milnor sequence is the primary tool for computing the cohomology of a homotopy limit of spectra or of a derived inverse limit in a derived category.

Diagram chase in the Milnor sequence
Let An = ℤ/2nℤ with the natural surjections An+1 → An. Compute lim1 An and describe the group lim An.
The inverse limit lim An is the 2‑adic integers ℤ2. The tower does not satisfy Mittag‑Leffler because the images are the whole groups, which do not stabilize to a proper subgroup. The standard description of lim1 for a tower of surjective maps between finite groups gives lim1 An = 0. Indeed, for a tower of surjections of finite groups the Mittag‑Leffler condition holds trivially, so lim1 vanishes. Hence the Milnor sequence reduces to 0 → Hq(lim Cn) → lim Hq(Cn) → 0, an isomorphism.
Exam‑day strategy
When a problem asks for lim1 of a tower, first check whether the transition maps are surjective or whether the tower satisfies Mittag‑Leffler. If either holds, lim1 = 0 immediately. Only when the maps fail surjectivity and the images do not stabilize do you need to compute the cokernel of the map ∏ An → ∏ An explicitly.

t‑Structures, Hearts, and the Construction of Abelian Categories Inside Derived Categories

A t‑structure on a triangulated category D consists of two full subcategories D≤0 and D≥0 satisfying: (1) D≤0[1] ⊂ D≤0, D≥0[−1] ⊂ D≥0; (2) Hom(X, Y) = 0 for X ∈ D≤0, Y ∈ D≥1; (3) for every object E there is a distinguished triangle X → E → Y → X[1] with X ∈ D≤0, Y ∈ D≥1. The heart H = D≤0 ∩ D≥0 is an abelian category. The standard t‑structure on the derived category D(A) of an abelian category A has D≤0 = {complexes with vanishing cohomology in positive degrees} and D≥0 = {complexes with vanishing cohomology in negative degrees}; its heart is canonically equivalent to A. Non‑standard t‑structures arise from tilting theory: given a torsion pair (𝒯, ℱ) in A, the Happel‑Reiten‑Smalø construction yields a new t‑structure on Db(A) whose heart is the category of extensions of objects of ℱ[1] by objects of 𝒯. This heart is again abelian but generally not equivalent to A. The cohomology functors Hn associated to a t‑structure take values in the heart and satisfy the long exact sequence property. In the derived category of coherent sheaves on a smooth projective variety, Bridgeland stability conditions are built from slicings, which are ℝ‑indexed families of t‑structures satisfying a compatibility condition. The existence of a bounded t‑structure with a Noetherian heart implies that the triangulated category is algebraic and often allows one to recover the original abelian category up to derived equivalence.

Compact Generation, Brown Representability, and the Neeman–Ravenel Theorem

An object C in a triangulated category T with coproducts is compact if the functor Hom(C, –) commutes with arbitrary coproducts. A set of compact objects S generates T if the only object orthogonal to all shifts of objects in S is zero. The Brown representability theorem states that any cohomological functor F: Top → Ab that sends coproducts to products is representable, provided T is compactly generated. Dually, any homological functor sending products to coproducts is corepresentable. Neeman proved that the derived category D(R) of a ring R is compactly generated by the single compact object R (viewed as a complex concentrated in degree zero). More generally, for a quasi‑compact and quasi‑separated scheme X, the derived category Dqc(X) of quasi‑coherent sheaves is compactly generated by perfect complexes. The Neeman–Ravenel theorem extends this to the stable homotopy category: the compact objects are precisely the finite spectra, and the category is compactly generated by the sphere spectrum. Compact generation is the key hypothesis for the existence of Bousfield localizations and for the construction of the telescope conjecture in chromatic homotopy theory. In practice, to verify that a functor between compactly generated triangulated categories admits a right adjoint, it suffices to check that it preserves coproducts; the left adjoint then automatically preserves compact objects.

Duality Theorems: Grothendieck Duality, Verdier Duality, and Serre Duality in Derived Categories

Grothendieck duality for a proper morphism f: X → Y of Noetherian schemes states that the derived pushforward Rf*: Dbcoh(X) → Dbcoh(Y) admits a right adjoint f!. The functor f! is characterized by the isomorphism Hom(Rf*A, B) ≅ Hom(A, f!B) for A ∈ Dbcoh(X), B ∈ Dbcoh(Y). When f is finite type and separated, f! can be constructed via compactifications and the Verdier dualizing complex. Verdier duality on a locally compact space X of finite cohomological dimension provides a dualizing complex ωX such that the functor DX(–) = Rℋℴ𝓂(–, ωX) is an anti‑equivalence of Db(X) with itself, exchanging Rf* and Rf!. For a smooth projective variety X of dimension n over a field, Serre duality takes the form Hi(X, F)∨ ≅ Hn−i(X, F∨ ⊗ ωX) where ωX is the canonical sheaf. In the derived category this is the statement that the functor Rℋℴ𝓂(–, ωX[n]) is a Serre functor, i.e. a bi‑exact auto‑equivalence satisfying Hom(A, B)∨ ≅ Hom(B, SA). The existence of a Serre functor characterises Calabi–Yau categories of dimension n (where S ≅ [n]). These dualities are the backbone of the derived category approach to the Riemann–Roch theorem, the Fourier–Mukai transform, and the construction of Bridgeland stability conditions.

  • Treating the heart of a t‑structure as the original abelian category without checking that the t‑structure is the standard one.
  • Applying Brown representability without verifying compact generation of the source triangulated category.
  • Using Grothendieck duality for non‑proper morphisms; the right adjoint f! exists only under properness or after passing to a suitable compactification.
  • Neglecting the shift in Serre duality: the dualizing object is ωX[n], not merely ωX.

Fourier–Mukai Transforms and Derived Equivalences

The Fourier–Mukai transform is a functor between derived categories of coherent sheaves on two varieties, defined by an integral kernel. Given a morphism p : X × Y → X and q : X × Y → Y, and an object K ∈ Db(Coh(X × Y)), the transform ΦK : Db(Coh(X)) → Db(Coh(Y)) sends E to Rq*(p*E ⊗L K). When ΦK is an equivalence, K is called a Fourier–Mukai kernel and the varieties are derived equivalent. Orlov's representability theorem states that any exact equivalence between bounded derived categories of smooth projective varieties is of Fourier–Mukai type. This result underpins the derived Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces and the classification of derived equivalences for abelian varieties via the Mukai lattice. The kernel for the classical Fourier–Mukai transform on an abelian variety A and its dual Â is the Poincaré line bundle on A × Â. Derived equivalences preserve many invariants: Hochschild homology, the Grothendieck group, and the Serre functor. They also induce isomorphisms on Hochschild cohomology, which controls deformations. In mirror symmetry, the homological mirror symmetry conjecture predicts that the derived Fukaya category of a Calabi–Yau manifold is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on its mirror; Fourier–Mukai kernels provide the mathematical framework for such equivalences.

Key Properties

  • ΦK is exact and commutes with shifts.
  • If K is a sheaf flat over X, ΦK sends sheaves to complexes with cohomology sheaves supported on the image of the support.
  • The composition of two Fourier–Mukai transforms is again a Fourier–Mukai transform; the kernel is given by a convolution formula.
  • Autoequivalences of Db(Coh(X)) form a group; for K3 surfaces this group is generated by shifts, tensoring with line bundles, and spherical twists.

Perverse Sheaves and the Decomposition Theorem

Perverse sheaves are objects in the derived category of constructible sheaves on a stratified space that satisfy certain cohomological amplitude conditions relative to a middle perversity. For a complex algebraic variety X of dimension n, a complex K ∈ Dbc(X, ℚ) is perverse if for every stratum S of codimension c, the cohomology sheaves satisfy Hi(iS*K) = 0 for i > −c and Hi(iS!K) = 0 for i < −c. The category of perverse sheaves Perv(X) is the heart of a t‑structure on Dbc(X) and is abelian. Simple perverse sheaves are intersection cohomology complexes IC(Z, L) associated to a local system L on a smooth locally closed subvariety Z. The Decomposition Theorem of Beilinson–Bernstein–Deligne–Gabber states that for a proper map f : X → Y with X smooth, the derived pushforward Rf*ℚX[dim X] splits in Dbc(Y) as a direct sum of shifted simple perverse sheaves. This theorem implies the hard Lefschetz theorem and the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations for intersection cohomology. It also yields the semisimplicity of the monodromy action on the cohomology of the fibres of a projective morphism. Applications include the proof of the Kazhdan–Lusztig conjecture on characters of highest weight modules and the geometric Satake equivalence relating representations of a reductive group to perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian.

Standard and Costandard Objects

  • The standard perverse sheaf Δ(Z, L) = iZ!L[dim Z] and the costandard ∇(Z, L) = iZ*L[dim Z] have simple quotient and subobject IC(Z, L).
  • Every perverse sheaf admits a finite filtration with subquotients isomorphic to simple perverse sheaves.
  • The nearby and vanishing cycles functors Ψf, Φf preserve perversity up to shift and are essential for studying degenerations.
Worked Example: Decomposition Theorem for a Blow‑up
Let π : X̃ → X be the blow‑up of a smooth projective variety X along a smooth centre Z of codimension c ≥ 2. Describe the decomposition of Rπ*ℚX̃[dim X] into simple perverse sheaves on X.
The exceptional divisor E ≅ ℙ(NZ/X) is a ℙc−1‑bundle over Z. The map π is an isomorphism outside E and contracts E to Z. By the Decomposition Theorem, Rπ*ℚX̃[dim X] ≅ ICX ⊕ ⨁i=1c−1 ICZ(−i)[−2i]. Here ICX = ℚX[dim X] is the intersection complex of X, and ICZ(−i) are the intersection complexes of Z twisted by the Tate motive ℚ(−i). The summands correspond to the primitive cohomology of the fibres ℙc−1. The shifts reflect the fact that the fibres have dimension c−1 and the perverse t‑structure centers at middle degree. This decomposition is canonical and compatible with the action of the relative hard Lefschetz operator.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the perverse cohomology sheaves of a pushforward, first identify the stratification and the relevant intersection complexes. Use the support and cosupport conditions to rule out impossible summands. Remember that the Decomposition Theorem only guarantees a non‑canonical splitting; the isomorphism in the derived category is not unique, but the multiplicities of simple perverse sheaves are invariants.

Hochschild Homology, Cyclic Homology, and the HKR Theorem

Hochschild homology HH*(A) of an associative algebra A over a field k is the homology of the Hochschild chain complex C*(A) = A ⊗ A⊗n with the Hochschild differential. For a smooth commutative algebra A, the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg (HKR) theorem gives an isomorphism HHn(A) ≅ ΩnA/k, the module of algebraic differential n‑forms. This identifies Hochschild homology with the exterior algebra of the module of Kähler differentials. Cyclic homology HC*(A) is the homology of the cyclic complex, obtained from the Hochschild complex by imposing the action of the cyclic group. There is a Connes long exact sequence relating HH, HC, and the periodicity operator S : HCn → HCn−2. For smooth algebras, HCn(A) ≅ ⨁i≥0 Hn−2idR(A) where H*dR is algebraic de Rham cohomology. In the derived setting, Hochschild homology of a dg‑category or a derived scheme is computed by the derived tensor product over the enveloping algebra. The HKR isomorphism extends to derived schemes and stacks, where it becomes a quasi‑isomorphism between the Hochschild chain complex and the de Rham complex. This underpins the construction of the categorical Chern character from K‑theory to cyclic homology and the proof of the Riemann–Roch theorem in derived algebraic geometry. The cyclic homology of a Calabi–Yau category carries a non‑degenerate pairing, which is the categorical analogue of the Mukai pairing on K3 surfaces.

Computational Tools

  • The Hochschild complex of a tensor product satisfies a Künneth formula: HH*(A ⊗ B) ≅ HH*(A) ⊗ HH*(B).
  • For a smooth projective variety X, HH*(X) ≅ ⨁p−q=* Hq(X, Ωp).
  • The cyclic homology of a matrix algebra Mn(A) is isomorphic to HC*(A) (Morita invariance).
  • The negative cyclic homology HC*− is the homotopy fixed points of the circle action on the Hochschild complex.

Derived Categories of Singularities and Matrix Factorizations

The derived category of singularities Dsg(X) of a scheme X is the Verdier quotient Db(Coh(X)) / Perf(X), where Perf(X) is the thick subcategory of perfect complexes. For an affine scheme Spec R with an isolated singularity, Dsg(R) is equivalent to the stable category of maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules. Orlov's theorem establishes a triangle equivalence between Dsg(R) and the homotopy category of matrix factorizations of a potential W when R = S/(W) for a regular ring S. A matrix factorization of W is a pair of free S‑modules F0, F1 with maps d0 : F0 → F1, d1 : F1 → F0 such that d1d0 = W·id and d0d1 = W·id. The category of matrix factorizations MF(W) is a triangulated category with shift given by swapping the two modules and changing signs. The equivalence Dsg(S/(W)) ≅ MF(W) is a cornerstone of homological mirror symmetry: for a Landau–Ginzburg model (X, W), the B‑model category is MF(W) and the A‑model is the Fukaya–Seidel category. Knörrer periodicity states that MF(W + xy) ≅ MF(W), which allows reduction of the dimension of the singularity. The singularity category of a Gorenstein ring is Calabi–Yau of dimension equal to the Krull dimension minus one. This structure appears in the study of cluster categories and the categorification of cluster algebras.

Key Equivalences

  • For a hypersurface singularity f : (ℂn,0) → (ℂ,0), MF(f) is equivalent to the category of vanishing cycles of f.
  • The derived category of singularities of a projective variety X is equivalent to the graded singularity category of its affine cone.
  • If X has rational singularities, Dsg(X) = 0.
  • The Hochschild homology of MF(W) computes the Jacobian ring of W.

Koszul Duality and Operads

Koszul duality is a correspondence between quadratic algebras and their quadratic duals. For a quadratic algebra A = T(V)/(R) with R ⊂ V ⊗ V, the Koszul dual A! = T(V*)/(R⊥) where R⊥ ⊂ V* ⊗ V* is the annihilator of R. If A is Koszul, the Ext‑algebra ExtA(k,k) is isomorphic to A! as a graded algebra. This duality extends to operads: the Koszul dual of the associative operad is the associative operad itself (self‑dual), the Lie operad is dual to the commutative operad, and the Poisson operad is self‑dual up to a shift. In the derived setting, Koszul duality becomes an equivalence between the derived category of modules over a Koszul operad and the derived category of comodules over its Koszul dual cooperad. This is the basis of the bar–cobar adjunction and the theory of ∞‑operads. For a Koszul algebra A, the minimal model of A is given by the cobar construction on A!. The Koszul complex K(A) = A ⊗ A! provides a free resolution of the trivial module. Applications include the formality of the little disks operad (Kontsevich), the computation of the homology of configuration spaces, and the construction of deformation quantizations via the formality of the Hochschild cochain complex. In representation theory, Koszul duality relates category O for a semisimple Lie algebra to the category of modules over the corresponding Koszul dual algebra, yielding the Beilinson–Ginzburg–Soergel equivalence.

Operadic Koszul Duality

  • The bar construction B(P) of an operad P is a cooperad; the cobar construction Ω(C) of a cooperad C is an operad.
  • For a Koszul operad P, the natural map Ω(B(P)) → P is a quasi‑isomorphism of operads.
  • The Koszul dual of the A∞ operad is the A∞ operad with a shifted grading.
  • Quadratic data (E, R) for an operad consists of an 𝕊‑module E and a sub‑𝕊‑module R ⊂ ℱ(E)(2).
  • Confusing the derived category of singularities with the derived category of coherent sheaves on a resolution; they are related by a semi‑orthogonal decomposition but not equivalent.
  • Assuming the HKR isomorphism holds for singular algebras; it fails in general and requires derived de Rham cohomology for a correct statement.
  • Treating the Fourier–Mukai kernel as unique; different kernels can give the same equivalence, and the kernel is only unique up to isomorphism in the derived category of the product.
  • Neglecting the shift in the perverse t‑structure when applying the Decomposition Theorem; the middle perversity places the intersection complex in degree −dim X.
  • Applying Koszul duality to non‑quadratic algebras without passing to a quadratic approximation or using the theory of curved Koszul duality.
  • Forgetting that the cyclic homology of a smooth algebra computes de Rham cohomology only in characteristic zero; in positive characteristic one must use the derived de Rham–Witt complex.
  • Misidentifying the Serre functor on the derived category of singularities; it is given by the canonical module shifted by the dimension, not by the dualizing complex of the ambient regular ring.

Derived Deformation Theory and the Cotangent Complex

The cotangent complex LX/Y of a morphism of schemes (or derived schemes) X → Y is the universal object controlling infinitesimal deformations of X over Y. In the classical setting the sheaf of Kähler differentials ΩX/Y suffices for smooth morphisms, but for arbitrary morphisms it fails to capture higher obstruction information. The derived functor of Kähler differentials, constructed via a simplicial resolution or a cofibrant replacement in the model category of commutative differential graded algebras, yields a complex LX/Y placed in non‑positive degrees whose cohomology sheaves H-i(LX/Y) encode obstructions to lifting deformations across square‑zero extensions. The fundamental exact triangle LY/Z ⊗L O_X → LX/Z → LX/Y → expresses functoriality and allows computation by transitivity. For a closed immersion defined by an ideal I, the cotangent complex is quasi‑isomorphic to I/I2[1], reflecting the fact that first‑order deformations are governed by the normal bundle. In derived deformation theory one studies the functor Def_X : Art_k → ∞‑Grpd sending a local Artin k‑algebra A to the space of derived deformations of X over Spec A. Its tangent complex is RHom(LX/k, O_X) and the obstruction theory lives in Ext2(LX/k, O_X). When X is a local complete intersection the cotangent complex is perfect of Tor‑amplitude [-1,0], and the deformation functor is pro‑representable by a complete local Noetherian ring. The derived moduli stack of objects in a dg‑category carries a natural (−1)‑shifted symplectic structure, and its tangent complex at a point is the shifted Ext complex of the object with itself. This framework underlies the construction of virtual fundamental classes in enumerative geometry and the definition of derived critical loci for potentials in topological string theory.

Derived Azumaya Algebras and the Brauer Group

An Azumaya algebra over a scheme X is a sheaf of algebras A which is locally isomorphic to a matrix algebra End(E) for a vector bundle E; its class in the cohomological Brauer group Br(X) = H2et(X, G_m)tors measures the obstruction to being globally a matrix algebra. In derived algebraic geometry one replaces sheaves of algebras by dg‑algebras or spectral algebras and considers derived Azumaya algebras: dg‑algebras A which are locally quasi‑equivalent to End(E) for a perfect complex E. The derived Brauer group Brder(X) classifies derived Morita equivalence classes of such algebras. A theorem of Toën shows that for a quasi‑compact quasi‑separated derived scheme X the natural map Br(X) → Brder(X) is an isomorphism, so derived Azumaya algebras do not produce new Brauer classes. However, the derived perspective clarifies the relationship with twisted derived categories: a derived Azumaya algebra A determines a sheaf of dg‑categories Mod(A) which is locally equivalent to Perf(X). The global sections of this sheaf form the derived category of A‑modules, and derived Morita equivalence corresponds to equivalence of these dg‑categories. The derived Brauer group also appears in the study of derived equivalences of K3 surfaces: a Fourier–Mukai transform with kernel a derived Azumaya algebra on the product induces an autoequivalence of the derived category, and the associated class in Brder(X × X) controls the deformation of the equivalence. In positive characteristic the derived Brauer group interacts with the crystalline Brauer group via the de Rham–Witt complex, and the derived Azumaya condition ensures that the associated Brauer class lifts to a class in H2crys(X/W) compatible with the Frobenius action.

Worked Example: Obstruction Class in Derived Deformation Theory
Let X = Spec k[x]/(x2) be the dual numbers over a field k of characteristic zero. Compute the cotangent complex LX/k and determine the obstruction space for deformations of X over k[ε]/(ε3).
The ring k[x]/(x2) is a complete intersection, so its cotangent complex is given by the two‑term complex I/I2 → Ωk[x]/k ⊗ O_X where I = (x2). Here I/I2 ≅ k·x2 and Ωk[x]/k ⊗ O_X ≅ O_X·dx. The differential sends x2 to 2x·dx = 0 in O_X because x2 = 0. Hence LX/k ≅ k[1] ⊕ O_X·dx placed in degrees −1 and 0. The tangent complex is RHom(LX/k, O_X) ≅ Ext1(k, O_X) ⊕ Hom(O_X·dx, O_X) ≅ k ⊕ k. The obstruction space is Ext2(LX/k, O_X) ≅ Ext1(k, O_X) ≅ k. For a deformation over k[ε]/(ε3) the obstruction class lives in this one‑dimensional k‑vector space; it vanishes precisely when the deformation extends to the next order. In this example the obstruction class is zero, so X admits a deformation to k[ε]/(ε3) given by k[x,ε]/(x2, ε3, xε).
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the obstruction space of a deformation problem, identify the cotangent complex of the object first. For a local complete intersection the cotangent complex is perfect of amplitude [−1,0]; the obstruction space is then Ext2(L, O) which often reduces to Ext1 of the conormal sheaf. Write down the relevant Ext groups explicitly rather than quoting general theorems.

Derived Categories of D‑modules and the Riemann–Hilbert Correspondence

The derived category D^bhol(D_X) of holonomic D‑modules on a smooth complex algebraic variety X is the natural home for systems of linear differential equations with regular singularities. The Riemann–Hilbert correspondence, proved by Kashiwara and Mebkhout, establishes an equivalence of triangulated categories between D^bhol(D_X) and the derived category D^b_c(ℂ_X) of constructible complexes of sheaves of ℂ‑vector spaces. Under this equivalence the de Rham functor DR(M) = Ω_X^• ⊗O_X^L M sends a holonomic D‑module to a constructible complex whose cohomology sheaves are the solution sheaves of the differential system. The inverse functor is the solution functor Sol(M) = RHomD_X(M, O_X)[dim X]. The correspondence respects the six operations: pushforward of D‑modules corresponds to pushforward of constructible sheaves, and the duality functor on D‑modules (the Verdier dual twisted by the canonical bundle) matches the Verdier dual on constructible sheaves. In the derived setting one works with the dg‑enhancement of these categories; the equivalence lifts to a quasi‑equivalence of dg‑categories, which is essential for functoriality of the six operations at the level of ∞‑categories. The irregular Riemann–Hilbert correspondence extends the equivalence to meromorphic connections with irregular singularities by replacing constructible sheaves with enhanced ind‑sheaves on the real blow‑up of X along the divisor of poles. This theory underlies the geometric Langlands program, where Hecke eigensheaves on the moduli stack of G‑bundles correspond to D‑modules on the moduli stack of local systems, and the derived category of D‑modules on the affine Grassmannian carries a natural action of the loop group whose categorical representation theory is governed by the derived Satake equivalence.

Derived Categories of Quiver Representations and Stability Conditions

For a finite quiver Q without oriented cycles, the category Rep_k(Q) of finite‑dimensional representations over a field k is equivalent to the module category of the path algebra kQ. Its bounded derived category D^b(Rep_k(Q)) is triangle equivalent to the perfect derived category Perf(kQ). When Q is an ADE quiver, the path algebra is of finite global dimension and the derived category admits a full exceptional collection given by the simple representations at each vertex. For general quivers, the derived category can be described via the Ginzburg dg‑algebra associated to a potential, and the cluster category construction yields a 2‑Calabi–Yau triangulated category whose cluster‑tilting objects correspond to seeds in the cluster algebra of Q. Bridgeland stability conditions on D^b(Rep_k(Q)) are given by a pair (Z, 𝒫) where Z : K0(D^b) → ℂ is a group homomorphism (the central charge) and 𝒫(φ) are full additive subcategories of semistable objects of phase φ. For acyclic quivers the space of stability conditions Stab(D^b) is a complex manifold of dimension equal to the number of vertices; its connected components are classified by the action of the braid group on exceptional collections. The wall‑crossing formula for Donaldson–Thomas invariants of quivers with potential expresses the change of the generating series of stable objects across walls of marginal stability in terms of the Kontsevich–Soibelman product of quantum dilogarithms. In the context of 3‑Calabi–Yau categories arising from quivers with potential, the derived category of the Ginzburg algebra carries a natural stability condition whose heart is the category of finite‑dimensional modules over the Jacobian algebra; the associated DT invariants compute the BPS state counts in the corresponding supersymmetric gauge theory.

Derived Algebraic Geometry: Derived Stacks and the Moduli of Objects

A derived stack is a functor from the ∞‑category of derived affine schemes (or simplicial commutative rings) to ∞‑groupoids satisfying descent for the étale topology. The moduli stack of perfect complexes on a proper derived scheme X, denoted Perf(X), is a derived Artin stack locally of finite presentation. Its tangent complex at a point corresponding to a perfect complex E is RHom(E, E)[1], and the obstruction theory is governed by RHom(E, E)[2]. The derived structure encodes higher automorphisms and obstructions that are invisible at the classical truncation; for example, the classical moduli of vector bundles on a K3 surface is smooth of dimension 2r2c12 − 2(r2−1)χ(O_X) but the derived moduli stack carries a (−1)‑shifted symplectic structure whose associated Lagrangian intersections produce virtual fundamental classes used in the definition of Donaldson–Thomas invariants. The derived stack of coherent sheaves Coh(X) is not geometric in general because the automorphism groups are not of finite type, but its open substack of perfect complexes is a derived Artin stack. The derived mapping stack Map(Y, X) for derived schemes Y, X represents the functor of derived morphisms; when Y is a point this recovers the derived loop space LX whose functions are the Hochschild homology of X. The derived critical locus of a function f : X → 𝔸1 is the derived fiber product X ×df, T^*X, 0 X; its structure sheaf is the Koszul complex of the differential df, and its (−1)‑shifted symplectic structure is the starting point for the AKSZ construction of topological quantum field theories. These tools are central to the modern formulation of the geometric Langlands correspondence, where the derived stack of G‑local systems on a curve carries a natural (2−dim)‑shifted symplectic structure and the category of D‑modules on the moduli of G‑bundles is identified with quasi‑coherent sheaves on the derived stack of local systems via the derived geometric Satake equivalence.

Model Structures on Chain Complexes and the Projective‑Injective Model

The category Ch(R) of unbounded chain complexes of modules over a ring R admits several Quillen model structures that make homological algebra a branch of homotopical algebra. The projective model structure has weak equivalences the quasi‑isomorphisms, fibrations the degreewise surjections, and cofibrations the maps with the left lifting property against trivial fibrations; its cofibrant objects are precisely the complexes of projective modules that are degreewise split. Dually, the injective model structure has cofibrations the degreewise injections, fibrations the maps with the right lifting property against trivial cofibrations, and fibrant objects the complexes of injective modules. Both model structures are cofibrantly generated: the sets of generating cofibrations are the inclusions Sn-1P → D^nP for all projective (resp. injective) modules P, where Sn-1P is the sphere complex concentrated in degree n‑1 and D^nP is the disk complex concentrated in degrees n and n‑1. The existence of these model structures, proved by Hovey (1999) and earlier by Quillen for bounded below complexes, allows one to compute derived functors by taking cofibrant or fibrant replacements inside Ch(R) rather than passing to the derived category. In particular, the total left derived functor of ⊗_R is obtained by replacing the left argument with a cofibrant complex of projectives, which recovers the classical Tor groups; the total right derived functor of Hom_R is obtained by replacing the right argument with a fibrant complex of injectives, giving Ext groups. The two model structures are Quillen equivalent via the identity functor, and the homotopy category of either model structure is canonically equivalent to the unbounded derived category D(R). This equivalence respects the triangulated structure because the suspension functor corresponds to the shift of complexes, and distinguished triangles arise from mapping cone sequences that are homotopy cofiber sequences in the model category. A subtle point is that the projective model structure exists for any ring, while the injective model structure requires R to be a Grothendieck category (e.g. modules over a ring) to guarantee enough injectives; for general abelian categories one often uses the “projective” model structure on the category of complexes of projectives, which exists under mild hypotheses.

Bousfield Localization and the Construction of Stable Homotopy Categories

Bousfield localization is a systematic method for modifying a model category so that a chosen class of morphisms becomes weak equivalences while preserving the underlying category. Given a model category M and a set S of maps, the left Bousfield localization L_S M has the same cofibrations as M, and its weak equivalences are the S‑local equivalences: a map f is an S‑local equivalence iff for every S‑local object X (i.e. an object such that Map(s,X) is a weak equivalence for all s∈S) the induced map Map(f,X) is a weak equivalence of simplicial sets. The fibrant objects in L_S M are precisely the S‑local objects that are fibrant in M. When M is the category of spectra with the stable model structure, localizing with respect to a homology theory E_* yields the E‑local stable homotopy category, whose objects are E‑local spectra and whose morphisms are given by E‑local homotopy classes of maps. The classic example is localization at a prime p, producing the p‑local stable homotopy category, and further localization at the Morava K‑theory K(n) gives the K(n)‑local category central to chromatic homotopy theory. Bousfield proved that the localization functor L_S exists whenever M is left proper and cellular, and that the localized model structure is again left proper and cellular. In algebraic contexts, one localizes the derived category D(R) with respect to a set of maps that become isomorphisms after applying a functor such as completion or torsion; the resulting localized derived category is equivalent to the derived category of the corresponding localized ring or of the category of torsion modules. The construction also underlies the theory of derived categories of sheaves on a site: the local model structure on simplicial presheaves is a left Bousfield localization of the global projective model structure at the covering sieves, and its homotopy category is the ∞‑topos of sheaves of spaces. A key technical tool is the existence of a set of generating acyclic cofibrations for the localized model structure, which follows from the small object argument applied to the S‑local equivalences. Understanding Bousfield localization is essential for working with Adams‑type spectral sequences, because the E2‑page of the Adams spectral sequence for a spectrum X is ExtE_*E(E_*,E_*X), and convergence is controlled by the E‑nilpotent completion, which is precisely the Bousfield localization at E.

Technique
Let R be a commutative Noetherian ring and let E = R/p be the residue field at a prime ideal p. Describe the Bousfield localization of the derived category D(R) with respect to the homology theory H_*(-;E) and identify the resulting localized category.
The homology theory H_*(-;E) is represented by the complex E concentrated in degree 0. A map f in D(R) is an H_*(-;E)-equivalence iff the induced map on homology with coefficients in E is an isomorphism, i.e. iff f ⊗^L_R E is a quasi‑isomorphism. The Bousfield localization L_E D(R) therefore has as weak equivalences the maps that become isomorphisms after derived tensoring with E. The E‑local objects are those complexes X such that RHom_R(E,X) ≃ 0 implies X ≃ 0; equivalently, X is derived p‑complete. By the derived version of Matlis duality, the full subcategory of E‑local objects is equivalent to the derived category of the p‑adic completion R_p, i.e. L_E D(R) ≃ D(R_p). The localization functor is given by derived completion X ↦ RHom_R(R/p^∞, X) ≃ X ⊗^L_R R_p. Thus the localized category is the derived category of the p‑adically complete ring R_p.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a Bousfield localization of a derived category, first identify the representing object of the homology theory (often a residue field or a Morava K‑theory). Then recall that localization at a field E = R/p yields derived p‑completion, and the localized category is the derived category of the corresponding complete ring. Write down the universal property: the localization functor is the left adjoint to the inclusion of E‑local objects. This pattern appears repeatedly in both algebraic and topological settings.

The Adams Spectral Sequence and Its Applications to Stable Homotopy

The Adams spectral sequence is a first‑quadrant spectral sequence that computes the p‑local stable homotopy groups of spheres (and more generally of any spectrum) from Ext groups over the Steenrod algebra. For a prime p, let A be the mod p Steenrod algebra and let X be a connective spectrum. The E2‑page is E2s,t = Ext_As,t(H^*(X;F_p), F_p), where the internal degree t‑s gives the homotopy degree. The differentials d_r have bidegree (r, r‑1). The spectral sequence converges conditionally to the p‑completion of the stable homotopy groups πt-s(X)^∧_p. Adams originally constructed it using the Adams resolution of X by Eilenberg–MacLane spectra, which is a tower of fibrations whose fibers are wedges of copies of H F_p. The associated exact couple yields the spectral sequence. A crucial feature is the vanishing line: for the sphere spectrum, there is a line of slope 1/(p‑1) beyond which all Ext groups vanish, guaranteeing that only finitely many differentials can affect a given stem. This vanishing line is proved using the nilpotence theorem of Devinatz–Hopkins–Smith and the thick subcategory theorem. The Adams spectral sequence has been used to compute the first several stable stems at p=2 (e.g. π0^S = Z, π1^S = Z/2, π2^S = Z/2, π3^S = Z/8, π4^S = 0, π5^S = 0, π6^S = Z/2, π7^S = Z/16, π8^S = Z/2 ⊕ Z/2, π9^S = Z/2 ⊕ Z/2 ⊕ Z/2) and at odd primes. The Adams–Novikov spectral sequence replaces the Steenrod algebra with the Hopf algebroid (MU_*, MU_*MU) of complex cobordism, giving a more powerful tool that sees chromatic information. The E2‑page is ExtMU_*MUs,t(MU_*, MU_*X), and convergence is to the p‑local homotopy groups after suitable completion. The chromatic filtration organizes the Adams–Novikov E2‑page by height of formal groups, and the Morava stabilizer group actions appear as Ext groups over the Morava stabilizer algebra. Modern computations use the algebraic duality resolution and the tmf (topological modular forms) spectrum to access higher chromatic heights. Understanding the Adams spectral sequence requires fluency with homological algebra over Hopf algebroids, the theory of comodules, and the geometry of formal groups.

Derived Categories of Noncommutative Schemes and the Bondal–Orlov Reconstruction

Noncommutative algebraic geometry studies abelian categories that behave like categories of quasi‑coherent sheaves on a scheme but arise from noncommutative algebras. A central result is the Bondal–Orlov reconstruction theorem (2001): if X is a smooth projective variety over a field k with ample canonical or anti‑canonical bundle, then the bounded derived category D^b(Coh X) determines X up to isomorphism. The proof uses the fact that the autoequivalence group of D^b(Coh X) contains the group of Fourier–Mukai transforms, and that the Serre functor S_X = - ⊗ ω_X[dim X] is a canonical enhancement of the duality. For noncommutative “spaces” such as the derived category of modules over a finite‑dimensional algebra A, one can sometimes recover A from D^b(mod‑A) as the endomorphism algebra of a tilting object. A tilting object T in a triangulated category T is an object such that Hom(T,T[i]) = 0 for i ≠ 0 and the smallest thick subcategory containing T is T itself. If T is a tilting object in D^b(mod‑A), then End(T) is derived equivalent to A. The derived Morita theorem states that two rings A and B are derived equivalent iff there exists a tilting complex in D^b(mod‑A) whose endomorphism ring is B. For noncommutative projective schemes in the sense of Artin–Zhang, one considers the quotient category qgr‑A = gr‑A / tors‑A of graded modules modulo torsion; the derived category D^b(qgr‑A) plays the role of D^b(Coh X). Van den Bergh introduced noncommutative crepant resolutions: a noncommutative algebra Λ that is a finitely generated module over its center R, such that D^b(mod‑Λ) ≃ D^bsg(R) (the singularity category). These provide categorical resolutions of singularities where a commutative resolution does not exist. The reconstruction philosophy extends to derived categories of coherent sheaves on stacks: if X is a smooth proper Deligne–Mumford stack with generically trivial stabilizers, then D^b(Coh X) determines X up to isomorphism (Lunts–Orlov). In the context of derived categories of singularities, Orlov’s theorem identifies the singularity category Dsg(X) with the graded singularity category of the corresponding graded algebra, linking noncommutative geometry to matrix factorizations.

Motivic Derived Categories and the Bloch–Beilinson Filtration

The theory of motives seeks a universal cohomology theory for algebraic varieties. Voevodsky’s triangulated category of geometric motives DMgm(k) over a field k is constructed as the homotopy category of complexes of Nisnevich sheaves with transfers, localized at the Tate object. The derived category of mixed motives DM(k) is obtained by further localizing at the Tate twist. The Bloch–Beilinson conjectures predict a descending filtration F^ν on the Chow groups CH^i(X) ⊗ Q such that the graded pieces Gr_F^ν CH^i(X) ⊗ Q are isomorphic to Ext2i-νMM(Q(0), Q(i)) in the category of mixed motives. This filtration would be functorial, compatible with the cycle class map, and would satisfy F1 = CH^ihom (homologically trivial cycles) and Fi+1 = 0. The existence of such a filtration is equivalent to the existence of a weight structure on DM(k) whose heart is the category of pure motives. The weight structure (also called a co‑t‑structure) gives a way to decompose any motive into a tower of pure motives of increasing weight. The conjectural abelian category of mixed motives MM(k) would be the heart of a motivic t‑structure on DM(k), and the Bloch–Beilinson filtration would be the filtration induced by the truncation functors of this t‑structure. Recent work by Ayoub, Cisinski–Déglise, and others constructs a motivic t‑structure on DM(k) for fields of characteristic zero under the assumption of the standard conjectures (or using the theory of Nori motives). The Nori motive category is defined as the category of representations of a certain affine group scheme, and it provides an unconditional abelian category of motives for varieties over a field of characteristic zero. The derived category of Nori motives is equivalent to the derived category of mixed motives in the sense of Voevodsky after suitable completion. The Bloch–Beilinson filtration has been constructed unconditionally for certain classes of varieties (e.g. curves, surfaces with p_g = 0, and abelian varieties) using the theory of relative Chow–Künneth decompositions. The filtration is deeply connected to the Hodge conjecture, the Tate conjecture, and the standard conjectures on algebraic cycles. Understanding the motivic derived categories and the Bloch–Beilinson filtration requires mastery of triangulated categories, t‑structures, weight structures, and the interplay between algebraic cycles and homological algebra.

  • Misidentifying the E2‑page of the Adams spectral sequence as Ext over the Steenrod algebra without specifying the coefficient module H^*(X;F_p).
  • Believing the Bondal–Orlov reconstruction holds for all smooth projective varieties; it requires ample canonical or anti‑canonical bundle.
  • Treating the Bloch–Beilinson filtration as a theorem rather than a conjecture; it is known only in special cases and under standard conjectures.
  • Overlooking the role of the Serre functor in reconstruction; the Serre functor encodes the canonical bundle and is essential for recovering the variety.
  • Neglecting the distinction between derived equivalence and Morita equivalence; derived equivalence is strictly weaker and classified by tilting complexes.

These advanced topics illustrate how homological algebra, model categories, and derived categories intertwine to solve deep problems in algebra, topology, and geometry.

Derived Categories of Quasi‑Coherent Sheaves on Schemes and the Six Operations

The derived category D(QCoh X) of quasi‑coherent sheaves on a scheme X is the natural habitat for the six Grothendieck operations: f*, f*, f!, f!, ⊗L, and RHom. For a morphism f : X → Y of finite type between Noetherian schemes, the derived pull‑back Lf* : D(QCoh Y) → D(QCoh X) is left adjoint to the derived push‑forward Rf*. When f is proper, Rf* preserves coherence and admits a right adjoint f! (the twisted inverse image) by Grothendieck duality. The exceptional push‑forward f! is defined for separated finite‑type morphisms and coincides with Rf* when f is proper. The derived tensor product ⊗L and derived Hom RHom make D(QCoh X) a closed symmetric monoidal category. The projection formula Lf*(−) ⊗L Lg*(−) ≃ L(g∘f)*(−) and base‑change isomorphisms hold under Tor‑independence hypotheses. For a closed immersion i : Z X with open complement j : U X, the recollement sequence i*i! → Id → j*j* → i*i![1] expresses the gluing of derived categories along a closed‑open decomposition. The compact objects in D(QCoh X) are precisely the perfect complexes; when X is regular, every bounded complex of quasi‑coherent sheaves is perfect, so Db(Coh X) ≃ Db(QCoh X)c. The Neeman–Ravenel theorem identifies the Brown representability conditions for D(QCoh X) and shows that the category is compactly generated by a single perfect complex when X is quasi‑compact and separated.

Tilting Theory and Derived Equivalences for Finite‑Dimensional Algebras

Let A be a finite‑dimensional algebra over a field k. A tilting complex T in Kb(proj‑A) is a bounded complex of finitely generated projective A‑modules such that HomK(A)(T, T[i]) = 0 for all i ≠ 0 and the smallest thick subcategory containing T is Kb(proj‑A). The endomorphism algebra B = EndK(A)(T) is then derived equivalent to A: the functor RHomA(T, −) : D(A) → D(B) is a triangle equivalence with quasi‑inverse − ⊗LB T. Rickard’s theorem states that two algebras are derived equivalent iff they are related by a sequence of tilting complexes. The Auslander–Reiten quiver of Db(mod‑A) is invariant under derived equivalence, and the number of isomorphism classes of simple modules equals the rank of the Grothendieck group K0(A). Silting objects generalize tilting complexes by dropping the projectivity requirement; a silting object in Db(mod‑A) induces a t‑structure whose heart is a finite‑length abelian category. The mutation of silting objects corresponds to left/right approximations and yields a combinatorial model for the space of t‑structures. For gentle algebras, the derived equivalence classification reduces to the combinatorics of surface triangulations, linking homological algebra to cluster categories and the Caldero–Chapoton map.

Worked Example: Constructing a Tilting Complex
Let A = kQ/I where Q is the quiver 1 → 2 → 3 and I is generated by the path of length two. Exhibit a tilting complex T in Kb(proj‑A) and compute its endomorphism algebra B.
The indecomposable projectives are P₁ = (1 → 2 → 3), P₂ = (0 → 2 → 3), P₃ = (0 → 0 → 3). Consider the complex T = (P₁ → P₂) concentrated in degrees 0 and 1 with the natural inclusion. One checks Hom(T, T[1]) = 0 and that T generates Kb(proj‑A). The endomorphism algebra B = End(T) has quiver 1 ⇉ 2 with relations making it the path algebra of the Kronecker quiver modulo the commutativity relation. Thus A and B are derived equivalent but not Morita equivalent.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks whether two algebras are derived equivalent, first look for a tilting complex or a silting object. Compute Ext‑vanishing and generation conditions; a single counterexample in Hom(T, T[i]) for i ≠ 0 disproves tilting. Remember that derived equivalence preserves the Grothendieck group and the Auslander–Reiten quiver, so mismatched ranks or AR‑quiver shapes give quick negative answers.

Derived Categories of Differential Graded Algebras and the Homotopy Theory of DG‑Modules

A differential graded (DG) algebra A over a commutative ring k is a graded algebra equipped with a differential d of degree +1 satisfying the graded Leibniz rule. The derived category D(A) of DG‑modules is obtained from the category of DG‑modules by inverting quasi‑isomorphisms. Every DG‑algebra is quasi‑isomorphic to a cofibrant replacement in the projective model structure on DG‑algebras, and D(A) depends only on the quasi‑isomorphism class of A. The derived tensor product ⊗LA and derived Hom RHomA make D(A) a closed symmetric monoidal triangulated category. When A is formal (quasi‑isomorphic to its cohomology algebra H*(A) with zero differential), D(A) ≃ D(H*(A)). The Morita theory for DG‑algebras states that two DG‑algebras are derived equivalent iff they are connected by a zig‑zag of quasi‑isomorphisms of DG‑bimodules that are compact generators on each side. Keller’s theorem identifies the homotopy category of DG‑modules over a DG‑algebra with the derived category of a certain ordinary algebra (the dg‑endomorphism algebra of a compact generator). This reduces many questions about DG‑categories to classical derived categories. The bar‑cobar construction provides a cofibrant resolution of a DG‑algebra and computes its Hochschild cohomology as ExtA⊗Aop(A, A). In rational homotopy theory, the Sullivan minimal model of a simply connected space X is a commutative DG‑algebra whose derived category of modules is equivalent to the rational derived category of X.

Derived Categories of Stacks and the Derived Category of a Stack

For an algebraic stack 𝒳 locally of finite type over a field, the derived category Dqc(𝒳) of quasi‑coherent complexes is defined as the homotopy limit of the derived categories of quasi‑coherent sheaves on a smooth hypercover by schemes. When 𝒳 has affine diagonal, Dqc(𝒳) is compactly generated by perfect complexes, and the compact objects are precisely the perfect complexes. The six operations extend to stacks: for a representable morphism f : 𝒳 → 𝒴, the functors Lf*, Rf*, f!, f! satisfy the same adjunctions and base‑change formulas as for schemes, provided one works with the lisse‑étale or flat‑fppf site. The derived category of coherent sheaves Db(Coh 𝒳) embeds fully faithfully into Dqc(𝒳) when 𝒳 is proper and has finite stabilizers. The Bondal–Orlov reconstruction theorem extends to smooth proper Deligne–Mumford stacks with generically trivial stabilizers: Db(Coh 𝒳) determines 𝒳 up to isomorphism. For a global quotient stack [X/G] with G a finite group, Dqc([X/G]) is equivalent to the G‑equivariant derived category DG(QCoh X). The inertia stack I𝒳 controls the decomposition of Dqc(𝒳) into components indexed by conjugacy classes of stabilizers, a key input for the McKay correspondence and for the study of derived categories of orbifolds.

Derived Categories of Modules over Ring Spectra and Stable Homotopy Theory

Let R be a ring spectrum (an E∞‑ring in the stable homotopy category). The ∞‑category ModR of R‑module spectra is a stable presentable ∞‑category whose homotopy category Ho(ModR) is the derived category D(R) of R‑modules. The smash product ⊗R and function spectrum FR(−,−) give D(R) a closed symmetric monoidal structure. When R = Hℤ (the Eilenberg–Mac Lane spectrum of ℤ), D(R) recovers the classical derived category of abelian groups. For R = MU (complex cobordism), D(R) encodes the Adams–Novikov spectral sequence. The Morita theory of ring spectra (Schwede–Shipley) states that two ring spectra are Morita equivalent iff their ∞‑categories of modules are equivalent as symmetric monoidal ∞‑categories. The Picard group Pic(R) of invertible R‑modules classifies the auto‑equivalences of D(R) given by tensoring with an invertible module. The chromatic filtration organizes ring spectra by height of formal groups; at height n, the Morava E‑theory spectrum En has a derived category that controls the K(n)‑local stable homotopy category. The nilpotence and periodicity theorems of Devinatz–Hopkins–Smith imply that the thick subcategory theorem holds in D(R) for any finite ring spectrum R, giving a classification of thick subcategories by support varieties in the Balmer spectrum.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Formal Schemes and Formal GAGA

Let X be a Noetherian formal scheme obtained as the completion of a scheme Y along a closed subscheme Z. The category Coh(𝑋) of coherent sheaves on X is an abelian category, and its bounded derived category Db(Coh 𝑋) carries a natural t‑structure whose heart is Coh(𝑋). The formal GAGA theorem (Grothendieck, EGA III 1, 5.1.4) asserts that the completion functor ̂ : Db(Coh 𝑌) → Db(Coh 𝑋) is an equivalence when Y is proper over a complete local ring and Z is defined by an ideal of definition. The proof uses the theorem on formal functions to identify cohomology of a coherent sheaf on Y with the inverse limit of its cohomology on the infinitesimal thickenings Yn. Consequently, Ext‑groups in Db(Coh 𝑋) compute the same groups as in Db(Coh 𝑌), and the Grothendieck group K0 is preserved. In practice, this equivalence allows one to replace a formal neighbourhood problem by an algebraic one on a proper scheme, where tools such as the derived category of perfect complexes and the six operations are available. A subtle point is that the equivalence fails if Y is not proper; then the natural map on Ext‑groups need not be an isomorphism, and the derived category of the formal scheme can be strictly larger. The formal GAGA theorem also extends to the setting of derived algebraic geometry: for a derived formal scheme 𝔛 the ∞‑category Dqc(𝔛) of quasi‑coherent complexes is equivalent to the limit of Dqc(𝑌n), and the compact objects correspond to perfect complexes on the algebraic approximation.

Derived Categories of Constructible Sheaves and Verdier Duality for Stratified Maps

Let X be a real or complex algebraic variety equipped with a Whitney stratification S. The category Shc(X, S) of S‑constructible sheaves of k‑vector spaces (or more generally of modules over a Noetherian ring) is an abelian subcategory of all sheaves on X. Its bounded derived category Dbc(X, S) inherits a t‑structure whose heart is Shc(X, S). Verdier duality provides a contravariant auto‑equivalence 𝔻X : Dbc(X, S) → Dbc(X, S) characterized by the isomorphism RHom(ℱ, 𝔻X𝒢) ≅ RΓc(X, ℱ ⊗ 𝒢) for all ℱ, 𝒢. When f : X → Y is a stratified map (i.e. the preimage of each stratum of Y is a union of strata of X), the six operations Rf*, Rf!, f*, f! preserve constructibility and satisfy the usual adjunctions. The key computational tool is the nearby/vanishing cycles functors ψf, φf for a function f on a complex analytic space; they relate the constructible derived category of the total space to that of the fibre. The monodromy action on ψf yields a mixed Hodge structure on the cohomology of the Milnor fibre, and the weight filtration coincides with the perverse t‑structure filtration. In the real setting, the Verdier dual of a constructible sheaf is again constructible, and the duality interchanges the costandard and standard objects of the perverse t‑structure. A common mistake is to apply the classical Verdier duality formula 𝔻Xℱ = RHom(ℱ, ωX) with the dualizing complex ωX of a smooth variety to a singular stratified space; the correct dualizing complex is the intersection cohomology complex ICX, which is the intermediate extension of the constant sheaf on the smooth locus.

Worked Example: Verdier Dual of a Constant Sheaf on a Stratified Curve
Let X be the complex affine curve defined by y² = x³ with the stratification S = {0} ∪ (X {0}). Compute the Verdier dual 𝔻X kX of the constant sheaf kX in Dbc(X, S).
The curve X has an isolated singularity at the origin. The constant sheaf kX is constructible for S. The dualizing complex on a complex algebraic variety of dimension 1 is ωX = ICX[1], where ICX is the intersection cohomology complex. For a curve with an isolated singularity, ICX is the intermediate extension of the constant sheaf on the smooth locus U = X {0}. Explicitly, ICX fits into a distinguished triangle kU[1] → ICX → k{0}. Applying Verdier duality 𝔻Xℱ = RHom(ℱ, ωX) to ℱ = kX gives 𝔻XkX = RHom(kX, ICX[1]). Since kX is the pushforward of kU with no higher direct images, RHom(kX, ICX) computes the derived global sections of ICX on X. The cohomology sheaves are ℋ⁰ = kX and ℋ¹ = k{0}. Shifting by 1 yields 𝔻XkX ≅ kX[1] ⊕ k{0}[0]. In the perverse t‑structure this object is the direct sum of the standard object kX[1] and the costandard object k{0}, illustrating how Verdier duality exchanges standard and costandard objects on a singular stratified space.
Exam‑Day Strategy

Do not automatically use the constant sheaf shifted by the dimension; that is valid only for smooth varieties. Write down the distinguished triangle defining the IC complex, then apply RHom termwise. Remember that the perverse t‑structure makes the duality exchange standard and costandard objects, a fact that often appears as a multiple‑choice distractor.

Derived Categories of Modules over Hopf Algebras and the Nichols–Zoeller Theorem

Let H be a finite‑dimensional Hopf algebra over a field k. The category H‑Mod of left H‑modules is a finite tensor category; its bounded derived category Db(H‑Mod) inherits a monoidal structure from the tensor product over k equipped with the comultiplication of H. The Nichols–Zoeller theorem (1989) states that H is a free module over any Hopf subalgebra K ⊆ H. In derived language this means the restriction functor ResKH : Db(H‑Mod) → Db(K‑Mod) is exact and preserves compact objects, while its left adjoint IndKH = H ⊗K − is also exact because H is K‑free. Consequently the pair (Ind, Res) is a Frobenius adjunction, and the induced monad on Db(K‑Mod) is given by tensoring with the Hopf algebra H viewed as a K‑bimodule. The Drinfeld double D(H) acts naturally on Db(H‑Mod) via the quantum double construction; its representation category is braided equivalent to the centre of H‑Mod. For a semisimple Hopf algebra (e.g. a group algebra of a finite group in characteristic not dividing the order), Db(H‑Mod) is equivalent to the derived category of finite‑dimensional modules over the dual Hopf algebra H* by Tannaka–Krein duality. In the non‑semisimple case, the stable module category stmod‑H embeds as the Verdier quotient of Db(H‑Mod) by the perfect complexes, and the Tate cohomology functors Ĥn become the Hom‑sets in this triangulated category. The Nichols–Zoeller freeness also implies that the Hochschild cohomology HH•(H) is a module over the cohomology ring H•(K, k) for any Hopf subalgebra K, a fact used in the detection of support varieties for Hopf algebras.

Derived Categories of Representations of Lie Algebroids and the Derived Category of D‑modules on Lie Groupoids

A Lie algebroid A over a smooth manifold M is a vector bundle equipped with a Lie bracket on its sections and an anchor map ρ : A → TM satisfying the Leibniz rule. The universal enveloping algebra U(A) is a sheaf of filtered algebras on M; its category of quasi‑coherent modules is equivalent to the category of A‑modules (representations of the Lie algebroid). The bounded derived category Db(A‑Mod) carries a natural t‑structure whose heart is the abelian category of A‑modules. When A = TM, U(A) is the sheaf of differential operators DM, and Db(A‑Mod) recovers the derived category of D‑modules on M. For a general Lie algebroid, the anchor induces a morphism of filtered algebras U(A) → DM, giving a forgetful functor to D‑modules. If G ⇒ M is a Lie groupoid integrating A, the convolution algebra of compactly supported smooth functions on G (or its completion) yields a Morita equivalence between the category of A‑modules and the category of D‑modules on the differentiable stack [M/G]. In derived terms, the ∞‑category D(A‑Mod) is equivalent to the ∞‑category of quasi‑coherent complexes on the derived stack [M/G]. The six operations for D‑modules on stacks (Kashiwara–Schapira) transport to D(A‑Mod), providing functors f!, f! for morphisms of Lie algebroids. The characteristic variety of an A‑module lives in the cotangent bundle T*M and is coisotropic with respect to the Poisson structure induced by the anchor; this is the algebroid analogue of the involutivity theorem for D‑modules. A frequent error is to assume that every Lie algebroid integrates to a Lie groupoid; integrability requires the monodromy groups to be uniformly discrete (Crainic–Fernandes). When integration fails, the derived category of A‑modules still exists but lacks a geometric description via groupoid convolution.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Projective Schemes and the Artin–Zhang Theory

Let A be a connected graded Noetherian algebra over a field k generated in degree 1. The noncommutative projective scheme Projnc A is defined by the quotient category QGr A = Gr A / Tors A, where Gr A is the category of graded right A‑modules and Tors A the Serre subcategory of torsion modules. Artin and Zhang (1994) proved that QGr A is a Grothendieck category with enough injectives, and its derived category D(QGr A) is compactly generated by the images of the shifted free modules A(n). The functor π : Gr A → QGr A has a right adjoint ω (the section functor) and a left adjoint given by the direct sum of graded pieces. The derived functor Rπ : D(Gr A) → D(QGr A) identifies D(QGr A) with the Verdier quotient of D(Gr A) by the subcategory of complexes with torsion cohomology. When A satisfies the Artin–Schelter regularity and the Cohen–Macaulay property (i.e. A is an AS‑regular algebra of global dimension d), the category QGr A behaves like the category of coherent sheaves on a smooth projective variety of dimension d‑1. In particular, Serre duality holds: there exists a dualizing object ωA such that RHom(ℱ, ωA) computes the Verdier dual. The point scheme of A (the scheme parametrizing point modules) controls the geometry of QGr A; for a generic Sklyanin algebra the point scheme is an elliptic curve, and D(QGr A) is derived equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on that elliptic curve. A common pitfall is to confuse the graded module category Gr A with the quasi‑coherent category on the noncommutative scheme; the former is too large and contains torsion objects that become zero in QGr A. The derived equivalence with a commutative variety holds only under the AS‑regular and χ conditions; without them the noncommutative projective scheme may have infinite global dimension and no Serre duality.

Derived Categories of Modules over Structured Ring Spectra and the Chromatic Filtration

Structured ring spectra — notably E∞‑ring spectra — provide a homotopical refinement of commutative rings. Their module categories admit a symmetric monoidal smash product, and the associated derived category is the stable ∞‑category of modules ModR. The chromatic filtration organizes these categories by the height of formal groups detected by Morava K‑theories K(n) and Morava E‑theories En. At height zero one recovers rational homotopy theory; at height one the p‑complete K‑theory spectrum KUp governs the v1‑periodic layer; higher heights correspond to increasingly intricate vn‑periodic phenomena. The nilpotence theorem of Devinatz–Hopkins–Smith asserts that a map of finite spectra is nilpotent iff it is nilpotent after applying every Morava K‑theory. Consequently, the Bousfield localization LK(n) isolates the n‑th chromatic layer, and the chromatic convergence theorem reconstructs the p‑local stable homotopy category from the tower of localizations. In the derived category ModR, the same filtration appears via the K(n)‑localizations of R‑modules. A key computational tool is the Adams–Novikov spectral sequence, whose E2‑page is Ext over the Hopf algebroid (MU*, MU*MU) and which converges to the K(n)‑local homotopy groups of R. The chromatic spectral sequence, built from the tower of K(n)‑localizations, provides a finer filtration on the homotopy groups of the sphere spectrum. Examiners often test the distinction between K(n)‑local and En‑local categories: the former is a smashing localization, the latter is not, and the En‑local category retains more torsion information. Another frequent trap is to assume that the chromatic tower splits; it does not, and the failure of splitting is measured by the chromatic fracture square, which relates LK(n) and LEn via the fiber of the map LEn → LK(n). Mastery of these structures is essential for any problem involving periodicity, nilpotence, or the computation of homotopy groups of structured ring spectra.

Derived Categories of Sheaves on Higher Stacks and the ∞‑Topos Perspective

Higher stacks are ∞‑sheaves of ∞‑groupoids on a site equipped with a Grothendieck topology; they form an ∞‑topos X. The ∞‑category of sheaves of spectra on X, denoted Shv(X, Sp), is the natural home for derived categories of quasi‑coherent complexes on derived stacks. When X is a derived Artin stack locally of finite presentation, the ∞‑category QCoh(X) of quasi‑coherent complexes is a stable presentable ∞‑category equipped with a symmetric monoidal tensor product. The six‑functor formalism (f*, f*, f!, f!, ⊗, Hom) lifts to this ∞‑categorical setting, and the proper base change and projection formulas hold under the usual hypotheses. A central result is Lurie’s representability theorem: for a morphism f: X → Y of derived stacks that is quasi‑compact and quasi‑separated, the pushforward f* preserves small colimits and admits a right adjoint f!. The ∞‑topos perspective also clarifies the relationship between étale and Nisnevich descent: the étale ∞‑topos of a derived scheme is hypercomplete, while the Nisnevich ∞‑topos is not, leading to different behaviors for the cotangent complex. In practice, one often works with the ∞‑category of ind‑coherent sheaves IndCoh(X), which is better behaved for singular derived stacks because it admits a dualizing object even when QCoh(X) does not. The Verdier duality functor on IndCoh(X) is given by D(ℱ) = RHom(ℱ, ωX) where ωX is the relative dualizing complex. A common examiner twist is to ask for the difference between the derived category of quasi‑coherent sheaves on a classical stack and on its derived enhancement: the latter retains derived intersection data encoded in the cotangent complex, and the natural functor QCoh(Xcl) → QCoh(X) is fully faithful but not essentially surjective unless X is discrete. Another subtle point is that the ∞‑category of sheaves of spaces on a higher stack satisfies descent for all higher covers, whereas the derived category of complexes only satisfies descent for 1‑covers; this distinction matters when computing cohomology of moduli stacks with non‑trivial automorphism groups.

Examiner's trap
Let R be an E∞‑ring spectrum and let K(n) be the n‑th Morava K‑theory at a fixed prime p. True or false: The Bousfield localization LK(n) on Mod_R is smashing, and the natural map LE_n → LK(n) admits a section in the ∞‑category of localizations.
False. While LK(n) is indeed a smashing localization on Mod_R, the map LE_n → LK(n) does not split. The chromatic fracture square exhibits the fiber of this map as the E_n‑localization of the K(n)‑acyclic objects, and the non‑splitting is precisely the obstruction measured by the chromatic spectral sequence. A splitting would imply that the E_n‑local category decomposes as a product of the K(n)‑local category and the K(n)‑acyclic category, which contradicts the known non‑trivial extensions between chromatic layers (e.g. the Greek letter elements in the stable homotopy groups of spheres).
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks about the relationship between K(n)‑local and E_n‑local categories, first recall the nilpotence theorem and the definition of smashing localization. Write down the chromatic fracture square explicitly; it is the universal diagram that relates the two localizations. If the problem asks whether a map of localizations splits, check whether the fiber is zero — in the chromatic setting it is never zero for n ≥ 1. Memorize the statement: “LK(n) is smashing; LE_n is not; the map LE_n → LK(n) does not split.” This three‑point checklist resolves most multiple‑choice and short‑answer items on chromatic homotopy theory.

Derived Categories of Factorization Algebras and the Beilinson–Drinfeld Construction

Factorization algebras on a topological space (or a complex curve) encode the algebraic structure of local observables in quantum field theory. A factorization algebra F assigns to each open set U a chain complex F(U) together with compatible factorization maps F(U1) ⊗ … ⊗ F(Uk) → F(U) for pairwise disjoint Ui ⊂ U. The ∞‑category Fact(X) of factorization algebras on a manifold X carries a symmetric monoidal structure given by the factorization product. Beilinson and Drinfeld constructed a chiral algebra on a smooth curve C as a factorization algebra on the Ran space Ran(C) with a specific singular support condition. The derived category of modules over a chiral algebra is equivalent to the derived category of D‑modules on the moduli space of bundles with a certain level structure — this is the geometric Langlands correspondence at the categorical level. In the derived setting, one works with the ∞‑category Fact(X)dg of dg‑factorization algebras; its homotopy category is the derived category of factorization algebras. A key technical result is the Koszul duality between factorization algebras and factorization coalgebras, which identifies the derived category of a factorization algebra with the derived category of comodules over its Koszul dual factorization coalgebra. This duality underpins the proof that the category of chiral differential operators on a curve is Koszul self‑dual. Examiners often test the distinction between the Ran space and the symmetric product: the Ran space parametrizes finite non‑empty subsets of C with a topology that remembers collisions, while the symmetric product forgets collision data. The factorization property is defined on the Ran space, and the failure of a prefactorization algebra to satisfy the factorization axiom on the symmetric product is measured by the factorization homology of the complement of the diagonal. Another common pitfall is to confuse the chiral algebra (which lives on the Ran space with a Lie*‑bialgebra structure) with a vertex algebra (which is a chiral algebra on the formal disk). The two are related by the localization functor from the Ran space to the formal disk, but they are not equivalent categories.

Derived Categories of Modules over ∞‑Categories and the ∞‑Categorical Yoneda Lemma

Let C be a small ∞‑category. The ∞‑category of presheaves of spaces PSh(C) = Fun(Cop, S) is the free cocompletion of C under colimits. The ∞‑categorical Yoneda embedding y: C → PSh(C) is fully faithful and exhibits PSh(C) as the ∞‑category of modules over the ∞‑category C in the sense of Lurie’s “Higher Algebra”. When C is stable, the ∞‑category of additive presheaves of spectra Funadd(Cop, Sp) is the derived category of C‑modules. The universal property states that for any presentable stable ∞‑category D, the restriction functor FunL(PShadd(C), D) → Fun(C, D) is an equivalence, where FunL denotes colimit‑preserving functors. This is the ∞‑categorical analogue of the classical fact that the derived category of a ring is the free cocompletion of the category of finitely generated projective modules. A crucial technical point is that the Yoneda embedding preserves finite limits if and only if C has finite limits; otherwise the essential image consists of those presheaves that are left exact. In the context of derived algebraic geometry, the ∞‑category of quasi‑coherent complexes on a derived stack X is equivalent to the ∞‑category of modules over the ∞‑category of perfect complexes Perf(X)op. The ∞‑categorical Yoneda lemma then yields a fully faithful embedding Perf(X) → QCoh(X) whose essential image is the subcategory of compact objects. Examiners frequently ask to identify the compact objects in a given presentable stable ∞‑category; the answer is always the essential image of the Yoneda embedding of the generating ∞‑category. Another typical question concerns the relationship between the ∞‑categorical Yoneda lemma and the classical derived Morita theory: if A and B are dg‑algebras, an equivalence D(A) ≃ D(B) induces an equivalence of the underlying ∞‑categories of perfect complexes, and the Yoneda embedding transports the compact generator A to a compact generator of D(B). The converse — that any equivalence of the ∞‑categories of perfect complexes lifts to an equivalence of the derived categories — holds because the Ind‑completion of Perf(A) recovers D(A). This reconstruction principle is a direct consequence of the ∞‑categorical Yoneda lemma.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Derived Schemes and the Derived Picard Group

For a derived scheme X (a locally ringed ∞‑topos whose structure sheaf takes values in connective E∞‑rings), the ∞‑category Coh(X) of coherent complexes is the full subcategory of QCoh(X) spanned by those objects whose cohomology sheaves are coherent on the classical truncation Xcl and are almost perfect. The derived Picard group Pic(X) is the group of equivalence classes of invertible objects in Coh(X) under derived tensor product. When X is a classical scheme, Pic(X) recovers the usual Picard group of line bundles. For a derived scheme, however, Pic(X) receives a natural map from the classical Picard group and also contains classes coming from the cotangent complex: a square‑zero extension of X by a coherent sheaf F corresponds to an element of Ext1(LX, F), and the associated derived line bundle is the pushforward of the structure sheaf along the extension. The derived Picard group fits into an exact sequence 0 → H1(Xcl, OX×) → Pic(X) → Ext1(LX, OX) → 0, where the rightmost term classifies derived deformations of the structure sheaf. This sequence is a derived analogue of the exponential sequence in complex geometry. A frequent exam question asks to compute Pic(X) for a derived scheme given by a derived intersection: if X = Spec A ×RSpec R Spec B, then the cotangent complex LX is the fiber of LA/R ⊕ LB/R → LR/R, and the Ext group can be computed via the Tor spectral sequence. Another trap is to assume that every invertible object in QCoh(X) is coherent; this fails for non‑Noetherian derived schemes, where there exist invertible quasi‑coherent complexes that are not almost perfect. The derived Picard group also controls the autoequivalences of Coh(X) given by tensoring with a derived line bundle; these are the only autoequivalences that preserve the t‑structure when X is smooth and proper. Finally, the derived Picard group appears in the statement of derived Torelli theorems: for a derived K3 surface, the derived Picard group together with the Mukai pairing determines the derived equivalence class.

Derived Categories of Equivariant Sheaves and the Bernstein–Lunts Formalism

Let G be a linear algebraic group acting on a scheme X over a field k. The equivariant derived category DG(X) is defined as the derived category of G-equivariant sheaves of k-vector spaces on X. Bernstein and Lunts (1994) constructed this category by replacing X with a simplicial scheme EG ×G X built from a contractible free G-space EG. The resulting category is independent of the choice of EG up to canonical equivalence and carries a natural monoidal structure given by derived tensor product over the representation ring R(G).

The formalism yields a localization theorem: for a closed G-invariant subscheme Z ⊂ X with complement U, there is a distinguished triangle RΓZ(F) → F → Rj*j*F → in DG(X) for any F. When G is a torus, the equivariant cohomology H*G(X) is a module over the symmetric algebra Sym(𝔤*) and the localization theorem implies that after inverting the equivariant parameters the restriction map H*G(X) → H*G(XG) becomes an isomorphism. This is the algebraic counterpart of the Atiyah–Bott–Berline–Vergne localization formula.

The equivariant derived category is equivalent to the derived category of the quotient stack [X/G]. Under this equivalence, the six operations (pullback, pushforward, tensor, Hom, duality) on DG(X) correspond to the stacky six operations. The equivariant t‑structure is defined by requiring that the underlying complex lies in the usual t‑structure on D(X); its heart is the abelian category of G-equivariant perverse sheaves. The equivariant decomposition theorem (Ginzburg, 1995) states that for a proper G-equivariant map f: X → Y with X smooth, the complex Rf*ℚX[dim X] splits into a direct sum of shifted equivariant intersection cohomology complexes.

Applications include the equivariant McKay correspondence: for a finite subgroup G ⊂ SL2(ℂ), the derived category DG(ℂ2) is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on the minimal resolution of ℂ2/G. The equivalence is realized by a Fourier–Mukai kernel given by the structure sheaf of the universal G-cluster. Examiners often test the distinction between the equivariant derived category and the derived category of the quotient variety; the former retains the G-action on cohomology, while the latter loses it unless one works with the stack quotient.

Derived Categories of Logarithmic Schemes and the Log Derived Category

A logarithmic scheme (or log scheme) is a scheme X equipped with a sheaf of monoids MX and a homomorphism α: MX → OX that induces an isomorphism on the open subset where α maps into the units. Kato (1989) introduced the log étale site and the log crystalline site, which allow one to define cohomology theories that behave well on degenerations. The log derived category D(X, M) is the derived category of sheaves of k-vector spaces on the log étale topos of (X, M).

The log de Rham complex Ω•(X,M)/S computes log crystalline cohomology and carries a Hodge filtration whose graded pieces are the log differential forms. For a log smooth morphism f: (X, M) → (Y, N), the relative log de Rham complex is locally free and satisfies a log version of the Poincaré lemma. The six operations extend to the log setting: the log pullback f* and log pushforward Rf* are defined using the log étale morphism of topoi, and the log duality functor Dlog is given by RHom(−, f!ℚℓ) where f! is the extraordinary inverse image for the structural morphism to a point.

A central result is the log decomposition theorem (Kato–Nakayama, 1999): for a proper log smooth map f: (X, M) → (Y, N) with X log smooth over a field, the complex Rf*ℚX[dim X] splits into a direct sum of shifted log intersection cohomology complexes. This theorem underlies the log version of the hard Lefschetz theorem and the log Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations. In mirror symmetry, the log derived category of a log Calabi–Yau pair (X, D) (where D is a divisor with normal crossings) is expected to be equivalent to the Fukaya category of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model; the log structure encodes the boundary divisor and the associated monodromy data.

Examiners frequently ask to compare the log derived category with the ordinary derived category of the complement U = X D. The log derived category retains information about the singularities along D via the monodromy weight filtration, whereas the ordinary derived category on U loses this data. A typical trap is to assume that log smoothness implies ordinary smoothness; the log structure precisely records the controlled singularities that are allowed.

Worked Example: Equivariant Localization on ℙ¹
Let G = ℂ× act on X = ℙ¹ with weights (1, 0) on homogeneous coordinates [x:y]. Compute the equivariant cohomology ring H*G(ℙ¹) and verify the localization theorem.
The fixed points are p = [1:0] and q = [0:1]. The equivariant cohomology of a point is H*G(pt) = ℚ[t] where t has degree 2. By the Borel construction, H*G(ℙ¹) ≅ ℚ[t, h]/(h(h−t)), where h is the hyperplane class. The restriction to p sends h ↦ 0, to q sends h ↦ t. After inverting t, the map H*G(ℙ¹)[t−1] → H*G({p,q})[t−1] ≅ ℚ[t, t−1] ⊕ ℚ[t, t−1] is an isomorphism: any class a + bh maps to (a, a+bt), which can be solved uniquely for a, b after inverting t. This confirms the localization theorem.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for an equivariant or log derived category computation, first identify the underlying group or log structure, then write down the relevant fixed‑point locus or boundary divisor. Use the localization theorem or the log smoothness criterion to reduce the problem to a computation on a simpler space. Always check whether the six operations are being applied in the equivariant/log sense or on the naive quotient; the distinction is a frequent source of lost marks.

Derived Categories of Mixed Hodge Modules and the Saito Theory

M. Saito (1990) constructed the abelian category MHM(X) of mixed Hodge modules on a complex algebraic variety X. Its derived category DbMHM(X) carries a canonical t-structure whose heart is MHM(X) and a pair of filtrations: the Hodge filtration F on the underlying filtered D-module and the weight filtration W on the underlying perverse sheaf. The forgetful functor rat: DbMHM(X) → Dbc(X, ℚ) to the derived category of constructible sheaves is exact and faithful, and it intertwines the weight filtration on MHM with the perverse t-structure on constructible sheaves.

The six operations (pullback, pushforward, tensor, Hom, duality, nearby/vanishing cycles) lift to DbMHM and are compatible with rat. For a morphism f: X → Y, the direct image Rf* preserves mixed Hodge modules and the Hodge filtration satisfies the Griffiths transversality condition. The Saito decomposition theorem asserts that for a projective morphism f: X → Y with X smooth, the complex Rf*ℚX[dim X] splits in DbMHM(Y) into a direct sum of shifted pure Hodge modules of weight dim X + i. This is a Hodge‑theoretic refinement of the Beilinson–Bernstein–Deligne–Gabber decomposition theorem.

The nearby cycles ψf and vanishing cycles φf functors for a holomorphic function f: X → ℂ are defined on DbMHM and carry a nilpotent monodromy operator N. The limit mixed Hodge structure on the nearby cycles satisfies the N-filtration property: the weight filtration is the monodromy weight filtration shifted by the Hodge weight. This machinery yields a Hodge‑theoretic proof of the hard Lefschetz theorem for intersection cohomology of projective varieties and underlies the theory of polarizable Hodge modules.

In examinations, a common question is to describe the weight filtration on the intersection cohomology of a singular variety. The answer uses the fact that ICX is a pure Hodge module of weight dim X, so its cohomology groups carry a pure Hodge structure of weight dim X + i. Another trap is to confuse the Hodge filtration on the de Rham complex of a smooth variety with the Hodge filtration on the underlying filtered D-module of a mixed Hodge module; the latter is defined only up to a shift and satisfies Griffiths transversality, while the former is the naive filtration by form degree.

Derived Categories of Bridgeland Stability Conditions and Wall‑Crossing

A Bridgeland stability condition on a triangulated category D consists of a group homomorphism Z: K(D) → ℂ (the central charge) and a slicing P(ϕ) for ϕ ∈ ℝ satisfying the Harder–Narasimhan property and the support property. The space Stab(D) of all such stability conditions carries a natural topology making it a complex manifold (possibly infinite‑dimensional). For the derived category DbCoh(X) of a smooth projective variety, the existence of stability conditions is known for surfaces (Bridgeland, 2002), for threefolds with certain numerical conditions (Bayer–Macrì–Toda, 2014), and for K3 surfaces (Bridgeland, 2008).

The wall‑and‑chamber structure on Stab(D) is defined by the loci where a given object becomes strictly semistable. Crossing a wall changes the set of stable objects, and the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula expresses the transformation of the Donaldson–Thomas invariants (or the motivic Hall algebra) in terms of the product of quantum dilogarithms associated to the stable objects that decay. For the derived category of a quiver with potential, the wall‑crossing formula computes the BPS spectrum of the associated 4d N=2 theory.

On a Calabi–Yau threefold X, the numerical Grothendieck group is equipped with the Euler pairing, and the central charge is often taken as Z(E) = −∫X e−B+iω ch(E) for a complexified Kähler class B+iω. The large volume limit corresponds to the chamber where the stable objects are Gieseker‑stable sheaves. As one moves towards the conifold point, walls are crossed and the stable objects include complexes supported on curves. The wall‑crossing formula then reproduces the topological string partition function.

Examiners test the ability to verify the support property for a given heart of a t‑structure, and to compute the central charge on a set of generators of K(D). A typical trap is to assume that every heart of a bounded t‑structure admits a stability condition; the support property is a non‑trivial constraint that fails for many hearts. Another trap is to confuse the wall‑crossing formula for numerical DT invariants with the motivic wall‑crossing formula; the latter lives in the quantum torus and requires the integration map from the motivic Hall algebra.

Derived Categories of Categorical Hall Algebras and Cohomological Hall Algebras

Let Q be a quiver with potential W. The cohomological Hall algebra (CoHA) of Kontsevich and Soibelman (2011) is the graded vector space H = ⊕d∈ℕQ₀ HBM•(Md, φTr W) where Md is the moduli stack of representations of dimension d and φTr W is the vanishing cycle functor of the trace of the potential. The multiplication is defined by the correspondence Md × Me ← Md,e → Md+e using the push‑pull formalism in the derived category of constructible sheaves on the moduli stack.

The CoHA carries a natural bialgebra structure with coproduct given by the reverse correspondence. After applying the integration map to the quantum torus, the CoHA yields the BPS Lie algebra whose elements are the Donaldson–Thomas invariants. The categorification of the Hall algebra is achieved by working directly in the derived category DbCoh(Md) or in the category of mixed Hodge modules on Md; the perverse t‑structure ensures that the vanishing cycles are perverse and the multiplication is compatible with the weight filtration.

For the Jordan quiver (one vertex, one loop) with zero potential, the CoHA is isomorphic to the positive part of the quantum affine algebra Uq(𝔤𝔩1) and the wall‑crossing formula reproduces the pentagon identity for the quantum dilogarithm. For the Kronecker quiver (two vertices, two arrows) with a generic potential, the CoHA is related to the spherical Hall algebra of the derived category of the resolved conifold. The motivic Hall algebra of Joyce (2007) provides a motivic lift of the CoHA, taking values in the Grothendieck ring of varieties; the integration map then recovers the numerical DT invariants.

In examinations, a frequent question asks to describe the multiplication in the CoHA for a quiver without potential. The answer uses the fact that the vanishing cycle functor is replaced by the constant sheaf, and the multiplication is the usual Hall algebra multiplication on the stack of representations. A trap is to forget that the CoHA is graded by the dimension vector and that the product respects this grading; another is to confuse the CoHA with the (non‑cohomological) Ringel–Hall algebra, which counts isomorphism classes of representations over finite fields rather than cohomology of the moduli stack.

  • Assuming that the equivariant derived category of a G-scheme coincides with the derived category of the quotient variety; the stack quotient retains the group action on cohomology.
  • Confusing log smoothness with ordinary smoothness; log structures encode controlled singularities along a divisor.
  • Neglecting the support property when constructing Bridgeland stability conditions; many hearts of t‑structures fail this condition.
  • Mixing the numerical wall‑crossing formula with the motivic wall‑crossing formula; the latter lives in the quantum torus and requires the integration map.
  • Treating the cohomological Hall algebra as an ordinary algebra without the bialgebra coproduct; the coproduct is essential for the BPS Lie algebra structure.
  • Overlooking the role of the perverse t‑structure in the categorification of Hall algebras; vanishing cycles must be perverse for the multiplication to be compatible with weights.
  • Believing that every mixed Hodge module underlies a variation of Hodge structure; singular support can be non‑empty, and the Hodge filtration satisfies Griffiths transversality only on the smooth locus.

Derived Categories of Fukaya Categories and Homological Mirror Symmetry

The Fukaya category Fuk(X) of a symplectic manifold (X, ω) is an A∞-category whose objects are Lagrangian submanifolds equipped with local systems and whose morphism spaces are Lagrangian intersection Floer complexes. When X is a Calabi–Yau manifold, Kontsevich's homological mirror symmetry conjecture asserts an equivalence of triangulated categories DπFuk(X) ≃ DbCoh(X∨) where X∨ is the mirror Calabi–Yau and Dπ denotes the split-closed derived category. The A∞-structure on Fuk(X) encodes higher Massey products in Floer cohomology; the A∞-functor to DbCoh(X∨) must respect these higher compositions. For the quintic threefold, the equivalence has been verified at the level of numerical invariants via the closed string mirror map and open string disk invariants. The wrapped Fukaya category W(X) of a Liouville domain X is defined using Hamiltonian orbits at infinity; its derived category is equivalent to the derived category of singularities of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model. The closed-open string map SH•(X) → HH•(W(X)) from symplectic cohomology to Hochschild cohomology is a key tool for proving generation criteria. Abouzaid's generation criterion states that a collection of Lagrangians split-generates W(X) if and only if the induced map on symplectic cohomology is surjective. In examinations, a typical question asks to explain why the Fukaya category is only an A∞-category and not a dg-category; the answer is that the associativity of Floer products holds only up to coherent homotopy because the moduli spaces of pseudoholomorphic polygons have boundary strata corresponding to broken configurations. Another trap is to confuse the compact Fukaya category with the wrapped Fukaya category; the former uses exact Lagrangians with Legendrian boundary, the latter uses non-compact Lagrangians asymptotic to Legendrians at infinity.

Derived Categories of Microlocal Sheaves and the Nadler–Zaslow Correspondence

The microlocal theory of sheaves, developed by Kashiwara and Schapira, associates to a conic Lagrangian subvariety Λ ⊂ T*X a category of microlocal sheaves μsh(Λ) whose objects are constructible sheaves on X with singular support contained in Λ. The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence (2009) establishes an equivalence between the derived category of constructible sheaves on a real analytic manifold M with singular support in a conic Lagrangian Λ and the wrapped Fukaya category of the cotangent bundle T*M with stops at Λ. Concretely, for Λ = T*MM (the zero section), the equivalence reads DbCohΛ(T*M) ≃ W(T*M, Λ). The singular support SS(F) of a sheaf F is a conic coisotropic subset of T*X; the microsupport condition SS(F) ⊂ Λ ensures that F is locally constant along the leaves of the characteristic foliation of Λ. The quantization of a Lagrangian correspondence L ⊂ T*X × T*Y is a kernel KL ∈ Db(X × Y) whose singular support is contained in L; the associated integral transform implements the pull-push on microlocal sheaves. The Guillermou–Kashiwara–Schapira sheaf quantization of a contact transformation gives a functor between categories of microlocal sheaves. In the setting of the geometric Langlands program, the category of D-modules on the moduli stack of G-bundles is equivalent to the category of microlocal sheaves on the Hitchin base with singular support in the global nilpotent cone. Examiners often test the definition of singular support: SS(F) is the complement of the set of covectors (x, ξ) such that RΓ{φ≥0}(F)x = 0 for some function φ with dφ(x) = ξ. A common error is to assume that SS(F) is always Lagrangian; it is only coisotropic in general, and Lagrangian precisely when F is constructible with respect to a stratification satisfying the Whitney conditions.

Worked Example: Microlocal Sheaf Quantization of a Lagrangian Correspondence
Let X = Y = ℝ, and let L ⊂ T*ℝ × T*ℝ be the Lagrangian correspondence given by the graph of the symplectomorphism (x, ξ) ↦ (x, ξ + 1). Construct the quantization kernel KL ∈ Db(ℝ × ℝ) and describe its action on a constructible sheaf F ∈ DbCohΛ(ℝ) with Λ = {ξ = 0}.
The symplectomorphism is generated by the function S(x, y) = x − y on ℝ × ℝ. The quantization kernel is KL = ℂΔ[1] ⊗ eiS where Δ ⊂ ℝ × ℝ is the diagonal. Explicitly, KL is the rank one local system on Δ with monodromy ei around the diagonal. For F = ℂℝ (the constant sheaf), the integral transform ΦK(F) = Rπ2!(KL ⊗ π1*F) computes to the constant sheaf ℂℝ shifted by [1] with the same monodromy. The singular support of the result is {ξ = 1}, which is the image of Λ under the symplectomorphism. This matches the microlocal prediction that quantization of a Lagrangian correspondence acts by pushforward of singular support.
Exam Strategy: Microlocal Sheaves and Fukaya Categories
When a question asks for the relationship between constructible sheaves and Fukaya categories, always mention the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence by name and state the precise equivalence: DbCohΛ(T*M) ≃ W(T*M, Λ). For quantization questions, recall that the kernel is supported on the Lagrangian correspondence and its singular support is contained in that correspondence. The monodromy of the local system on the kernel is determined by the generating function of the symplectomorphism. If asked to distinguish SS(F) from the wavefront set WF(F), note that SS is conic and coisotropic while WF is the projection to the base of the microsupport in the analytic category.

Derived Categories of En-Algebras and Higher Algebraic Structures

An En-algebra in a symmetric monoidal ∞-category C is an algebra over the little n-disks operad En. For n = 1 this recovers associative algebras; for n = ∞ it gives commutative algebras. The derived category of modules over an En-algebra A carries a natural En-monoidal structure given by the relative tensor product ⊗A. The Dunn additivity theorem states that En ⊗ Em ≃ En+m, so an En-algebra in Em-algebras is an En+m-algebra. The center of an En-algebra is an En+1-algebra; this is the higher Deligne conjecture proved by Lurie and Francis. For an E2-algebra A, the category of A-modules is braided monoidal, and the Drinfeld center construction recovers the E3-structure on the Hochschild cochains. The factorization homology ∫MA of an En-algebra A over a framed n-manifold M is an invariant that satisfies excision; for M = Sn it computes the n-fold bar construction. The cobordism hypothesis of Baez–Dolan–Lurie classifies fully extended topological field theories with values in a symmetric monoidal (∞,n)-category; the value on a point is a fully dualizable object, which for n = 2 corresponds to a Calabi–Yau E2-algebra. In the context of derived algebraic geometry, the derived stack of En-algebras is represented by the moduli of n-shifted symplectic structures. The derived category of coherent sheaves on a derived Artin stack carries a natural E2-monoidal structure when the stack is 1-shifted symplectic. Examiners test the distinction between En-algebras and Pn-algebras (Poisson n-algebras); the latter are the homology of the former, and the formality of the little disks operad over ℚ (Kontsevich, Tamarkin) gives a quasi-isomorphism En ≃ Pn in characteristic zero. A trap is to assume that every braided monoidal category comes from an E2-algebra; the Drinfeld center of a monoidal category is always braided, but not every braided category is a center.

Derived Categories of Motives and the Motivic t-Structure

Voevodsky's triangulated category of geometric motives DMgm(k) over a perfect field k is constructed as the homotopy category of the Nisnevich-localized model structure on complexes of presheaves with transfers. The category of effective motives DMeff(k) admits a motivic t-structure whose heart is the category of mixed motives MM(k), conjecturally abelian and Tannakian. The weight filtration on motives corresponds to the filtration by codimension of support; the graded pieces are pure motives. The realization functors to Betti, de Rham, and ℓ-adic cohomology are exact with respect to the motivic t-structure. The motivic spectral sequence E2p,q = ExtpMM(ℚ(0), ℚ(q)) ⇒ K2q−p(k) ⊗ ℚ relates motivic Ext-groups to algebraic K-theory. The Beilinson–Soulé vanishing conjecture predicts that Hp(M, ℚ(q)) = 0 for p < 0, which is equivalent to the motivic t-structure being non-degenerate. The category of noncommutative motives NMot(k) of Tabuada is the idempotent completion of the category of dg-categories up to Morita equivalence; it admits a universal additive invariant U : dgCatk → NMot(k) and a universal localizing invariant. The motivic Galois group acts on the fiber functors of the Tannakian category of mixed motives; its Lie algebra is the motivic Lie algebra, which is conjecturally free on generators in odd degrees. In examinations, a standard question asks to define the motivic t-structure on DMeff(k): the heart consists of motives M such that Hom(M(n)[m], M) = 0 for m > 0 and all n ∈ ℤ. A trap is to confuse the motivic t-structure with the weight filtration; the weight filtration exists unconditionally, while the motivic t-structure is conjectural in general (known for k = ℚ, number fields, and finite fields by work of Bondarko, Hébert, and others). Another error is to assume that the category of mixed motives is equivalent to the category of realizations; the realization functors are faithful but not full, and the motivic Galois group measures the difference.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Algebraic Stacks and the Derived Satake Equivalence

For an algebraic stack X locally of finite type over a field k, the derived category DbCoh(X) of coherent sheaves is defined as the full subcategory of DbQCoh(X) spanned by complexes with coherent cohomology sheaves. When X = BG for a reductive group G, DbCoh(BG) is equivalent to the derived category of finite-dimensional G-representations. The derived Satake equivalence of Bezrukavnikov–Finkelberg (2010) states that for the affine Grassmannian GrG of a reductive group G over ℂ, there is an equivalence of monoidal triangulated categories DbG(O)(GrG) ≃ DbCoh(G∨) where G∨ is the Langlands dual group. The left side is the derived category of G(O)-equivariant constructible sheaves on the affine Grassmannian, and the monoidal structure is given by convolution. The right side is the derived category of coherent sheaves on the dual group, with monoidal structure given by tensor product. The equivalence intertwines the geometric Satake correspondence with the Tannakian formalism. For the loop group LG, the derived category of G(O)-equivariant D-modules on GrG is equivalent to the derived category of quasi-coherent sheaves on the derived stack LocSysG∨(D×) of G∨-local systems on the punctured disk. The categorical geometric Langlands conjecture predicts an equivalence DbCoh(LocSysG∨(X)) ≃ DbG(BunG(X)) for a smooth projective curve X. The derived category of coherent sheaves on the moduli stack of G-bundles BunG carries a natural action of the spherical Hecke category via Hecke correspondences. Examiners test the definition of the convolution product on DbG(O)(GrG): for sheaves F, G on GrG, their convolution is m!(F ⊠ G) where m : GrG ×G(O) GrG → GrG is the multiplication map. A common trap is to confuse the affine Grassmannian GrG = G(K)/G(O) with the affine flag variety FlG = G(K)/I where I is the Iwahori subgroup; the derived Satake equivalence uses the former, while the affine Hecke category uses the latter. Another error is to assume the equivalence holds for non-reductive groups; the geometric Satake correspondence fails for non-reductive groups because the affine Grassmannian is not ind-proper.

  • Assuming the singular support SS(F) of a constructible sheaf is always Lagrangian; it is coisotropic in general and Lagrangian only when the stratification satisfies Whitney conditions.
  • Treating every braided monoidal category as the Drinfeld center of a monoidal category; the center construction always yields a braided category, but not every braided category arises this way.
  • Confusing En-algebras with Pn-algebras; the latter are the homology of the former, and formality holds only in characteristic zero.
  • Assuming the motivic t-structure on DMeff(k) is known unconditionally; it is conjectural in general and known only for specific fields (ℚ, number fields, finite fields).
  • Confusing the affine Grassmannian GrG with the affine flag variety FlG in the derived Satake equivalence; the former is G(K)/G(O), the latter is G(K)/I.
  • Believing the derived Satake equivalence holds for non-reductive groups; the geometric Satake correspondence requires reductivity for the ind-properness of the affine Grassmannian.

Derived Categories of Modules over Differential Graded Categories and Morita Theory

The derived category of a differential graded (dg) category 𝒞 is constructed by first forming the category of dg‑modules Mod‑𝒞, then localising at the class of quasi‑isomorphisms. This yields a triangulated category D(𝒞) which inherits a natural enhancement by a dg‑category of cofibrant‑fibrant objects. Morita theory for dg‑categories states that a dg‑functor F: 𝒞 → 𝒟 induces an equivalence D(𝒞) ≃ D(𝒟) if and only if F is a quasi‑equivalence, i.e. it induces isomorphisms on all morphism cohomology groups and its image generates 𝒟 under shifts, cones and retracts. In practice one often replaces a dg‑category by a quasi‑equivalent cofibrant one, for instance by taking a cofibrant replacement in the projective model structure on dg‑categories. The derived Morita theorem then reduces the classification of derived equivalences to the computation of Hochschild cohomology of the dg‑category, because the obstruction to lifting a triangulated equivalence to a dg‑equivalence lives in HH³. Examiners frequently ask to verify that the derived category of perfect complexes on a smooth proper scheme X is equivalent to the derived category of a dg‑enhancement of the category of vector bundles, and to explain why the dg‑structure is essential for the uniqueness of the enhancement.

A concrete instance is the derived category of a finite‑dimensional algebra A. The dg‑category of projective A‑modules is quasi‑equivalent to A itself regarded as a dg‑category concentrated in degree zero. Its derived category D(A) coincides with the usual derived category of A‑modules. If A is derived‑equivalent to another algebra B, then there exists a tilting complex T in D(A) such that End(T) ≃ B and the functor RHom(T, –) gives the equivalence. The dg‑Morita perspective clarifies that the tilting complex must be a compact generator of D(A) and that the endomorphism dg‑algebra of T is formal if and only if the equivalence lifts to a dg‑equivalence. This distinction is a common source of exam traps: students may assert that any derived equivalence comes from a tilting complex without checking compactness or generation.

When the dg‑category is not proper, the derived category may fail to be compactly generated. In such cases one works with the subcategory of compact objects D_c(𝒞) and uses the fact that the inclusion D_c(𝒞) → D(𝒞) preserves coproducts. The Brown representability theorem for triangulated categories then guarantees that any cohomological functor sending coproducts to products is representable by an object of D(𝒞). This machinery underlies the proof that the derived category of a Grothendieck abelian category is well generated, a result used heavily in the construction of the six operations for derived categories of sheaves.

Finally, the dg‑Morita theory extends to ∞‑categories via the Dwyer–Kan localisation. The ∞‑categorical version states that the ∞‑category of modules over a small stable ∞‑category 𝒞 is equivalent to the ∞‑category of modules over any other stable ∞‑category 𝒟 if and only if 𝒞 and 𝒟 are Morita equivalent in the ∞‑sense. This viewpoint is essential for modern approaches to derived algebraic geometry where one works with ∞‑categories of quasi‑coherent sheaves on derived stacks.

Derived Categories of Sheaves on Non‑Archimedean Analytic Spaces and Étale Cohomology

Let K be a complete non‑archimedean field with valuation ring O_K and residue field k. The category of rigid analytic spaces over K, in the sense of Tate, admits a Grothendieck topology generated by admissible open coverings. The derived category of étale sheaves of ℓ‑adic coefficients on a rigid space X, denoted D_et(X, ℚ_ℓ), is defined as the derived category of the abelian category of étale sheaves. A fundamental result of Huber and later Scholze shows that for smooth proper rigid spaces the étale cohomology groups satisfy Poincaré duality and the weight formalism analogous to the classical case over ℂ. The comparison theorem with singular cohomology of the associated complex analytic space (via the analytification functor) is a deep theorem of Berkovich and Huber, and it is frequently tested by asking to state the precise hypotheses: X must be smooth, proper, and the coefficient field must have characteristic different from the residue characteristic.

The six operations formalism for rigid analytic spaces was developed by Laszlo and Olsson, extending the work of Deligne on ℓ‑adic sheaves. The derived pushforward Rf_* and the exceptional pushforward Rf_! coincide for proper morphisms, while for open immersions the exceptional inverse image f^! is given by the nearby cycles functor. The nearby cycles functor Ψ_f for a morphism f: X → Spa(K) captures the degeneration of the special fibre in a formal model of X. Its monodromy filtration yields the weight spectral sequence, which computes the étale cohomology of the generic fibre in terms of the cohomology of the special fibre. In examinations, a typical question asks to describe the weight spectral sequence for a semistable formal model and to identify the E₁‑page as the cohomology of the strata of the special fibre with appropriate Tate twists.

Another important tool is the pro‑étale site introduced by Bhatt and Scholze. On the pro‑étale site the higher direct images of the constant sheaf ℚ_ℓ become easier to compute because the site has enough weakly contractible objects. The derived category of pro‑étale sheaves is equivalent to the derived category of étale sheaves, but the pro‑étale topology allows one to define the period sheaves B_dR and B_cris, which are essential for p‑adic Hodge theory. A common exam trap is to confuse the pro‑étale site with the usual étale site when stating the comparison isomorphism between de Rham cohomology and étale cohomology; the period sheaves live naturally on the pro‑étale site, not on the classical étale site.

Finally, the theory of diamonds and v‑stacks, developed by Scholze, provides a geometric framework where the étale cohomology of rigid spaces can be studied via the cohomology of perfectoid spaces. The derived category of sheaves on a diamond is defined using the v‑topology, and the resulting theory satisfies a version of the six operations. Questions may ask to explain why the v‑topology is strictly finer than the étale topology and how this fineness resolves the failure of proper base change for the étale topology on non‑proper rigid spaces.

Worked Example: Weight Spectral Sequence for a Semistable Model
Let X be a smooth proper rigid analytic space over K admitting a semistable formal model 𝔛 over O_K with special fibre 𝔛_k = ∪i∈I D_i a simple normal crossings divisor. Write D_J = ∩j∈J D_j for non‑empty subsets J ⊂ I. Describe the E₁‑page of the weight spectral sequence converging to H^*_et(X, ℚ_ℓ) and identify the differentials.
The weight spectral sequence has E₁p,q = ⊕|J|=p+1 Hq-p_et(D_J, ℚ_ℓ)(-p). The differential d₁: E₁p,q → E₁p+1,q is the alternating sum of the Gysin maps induced by the inclusions D_{J∪{i}} D_J. The sequence degenerates at E₂ and yields a filtration on H^n_et(X, ℚ_ℓ) whose graded pieces are the weight‑graded pieces of the mixed Hodge structure (or ℓ‑adic weight filtration). The Tate twist (-p) reflects the fact that each stratum D_J has codimension p in the special fibre. This description is exactly the one proved by Rapoport–Zink and later generalised by Saito.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a spectral sequence, first write down the indexing convention (cohomological vs homological) and the expected abutment. Then state the E₁ or E₂ page explicitly, including all Tate twists. Examiners award marks for the correct identification of the terms and the differentials, even if you do not compute the full limit. Memorise the standard weight spectral sequence for semistable reduction and the Leray spectral sequence for the structure morphism to Spa(K).

Derived Categories of Representations of Quantum Groups and the Kazhdan–Lusztig Equivalence

Let 𝔤 be a complex semisimple Lie algebra and U_q(𝔤) its Drinfeld–Jimbo quantum group at a generic parameter q (not a root of unity). The category Rep(U_q(𝔤)) of finite‑dimensional type‑1 representations is a braided tensor category equivalent, as a braided category, to the category of representations of the corresponding affine Lie algebra at a suitable level. The Kazhdan–Lusztig equivalence, proved by Kazhdan and Lusztig in the early 1990s, constructs a tensor equivalence between the category of integrable highest‑weight modules of the affine Kac–Moody algebra 𝔤̂ at level k and the category of finite‑dimensional representations of the quantum group U_q(𝔤) with q = exp(π i / (k+h^∨)). On the derived level, this lifts to an equivalence of derived categories D^b(Rep(U_q(𝔤))) ≃ D^b(𝒪_k) where 𝒪_k is the category 𝒪 for the affine Lie algebra at level k. The equivalence intertwines the braiding on the quantum side with the monodromy of the Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov connection on the affine side.

A key technical ingredient is the use of the Harish‑Chandra localisation functor, which sends a module over the affine Lie algebra to a D‑module on the affine flag variety. The derived localisation theorem of Beilinson–Bernstein identifies the derived category of 𝒪_k with the derived category of D‑modules on the affine flag variety with a fixed central character. On the quantum side, the geometric Satake correspondence identifies Rep(U_q(𝔤)) with the category of perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian Gr_G. The Kazhdan–Lusztig equivalence then becomes a compatibility between the convolution product on perverse sheaves and the tensor product of quantum group representations. Examiners often ask to explain why the parameter q must be a root of unity for the equivalence to be an equivalence of braided tensor categories, and to describe the role of the modular tensor category structure that appears at roots of unity.

When q is a root of unity, the representation category of the restricted quantum group u_q(𝔤) is not semisimple. Its derived category carries a natural action of the modular group SL(2,ℤ) via the Lyubashenko construction, and the centre of this derived category is equivalent to the derived category of representations of the corresponding logarithmic conformal field theory. This deep connection is a frequent source of advanced questions: one may be asked to state the Verlinde formula for the fusion rules in terms of the S‑matrix of the modular tensor category, and to explain how the derived equivalence transports the S‑matrix to the monodromy of the KZ connection.

Finally, the derived Kazhdan–Lusztig equivalence extends to the setting of quantum groups at roots of unity via the work of Bezrukavnikov and Finkelberg, who constructed an equivalence between the derived category of coherent sheaves on the Steinberg variety and the derived category of representations of the quantum group at a root of unity. This equivalence is a cornerstone of geometric representation theory and is often tested by asking to describe the convolution algebra on the Steinberg variety and its relation to the affine Hecke algebra.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Resolutions and Van den Bergh Duality

A noncommutative crepant resolution (NCCR) of a normal Gorenstein domain R is an endomorphism algebra A = End_R(M) where M is a reflexive R‑module such that A has finite global dimension and is a Cohen–Macaulay R‑module. Van den Bergh proved that if R admits an NCCR, then the derived category D^b(mod‑A) is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on a crepant resolution of Spec R, when such a resolution exists. Moreover, the functor RHom_R(M, –) gives a derived equivalence between D^b(Coh(Spec R)) and D^b(mod‑A). This equivalence is a noncommutative analogue of the Bondal–Orlov theorem for commutative crepant resolutions.

The duality theory for NCCRs is governed by Van den Bergh duality, which states that for a Calabi–Yau algebra A of dimension d there is a functorial isomorphism Ext^i_A(X, Y) ≅ Extd-i_A(Y, X)^* for all perfect complexes X, Y. In the context of an NCCR, the algebra A is typically a noncommutative Calabi–Yau algebra of dimension equal to the Krull dimension of R. The duality implies that the Serre functor on D^b(mod‑A) is given by the shift [d], which is a hallmark of a Calabi–Yau category. Exam questions often ask to verify the Calabi–Yau property for the path algebra of a quiver with potential arising from a dimer model, and to compute the Serre functor explicitly.

Another important aspect is the mutation of NCCRs. Given an NCCR A = End_R(M), one can replace a summand of M by its mutation to obtain a new NCCR A'. The derived categories D^b(mod‑A) and D^b(mod‑A') are equivalent, and the equivalence is given by a tilting complex constructed from the mutation. This process is the noncommutative counterpart of flops in the minimal model program. A typical exam problem presents a quiver with potential and asks to perform a mutation at a vertex, write down the new quiver with potential, and describe the induced derived equivalence on the level of tilting complexes.

Infinity‑operads, introduced by Lurie and independently by Cisinski–Moerdijk, encode higher homotopy coherence for algebraic structures with multiple inputs. The category of dendroidal sets, equipped with the Cisinski–Moerdijk model structure, presents the ∞‑category of ∞‑operads. The Derived Category of Perfect Complexes and its Role in Algebraic K-Theory

The derived category of perfect complexes, denoted Dperf(X) for a scheme X, plays a pivotal role in modern algebraic geometry and K-theory. A complex of OX-modules is called perfect if, locally on X, it is quasi-isomorphic to a bounded complex of finite-rank free OX-modules. Equivalently, a complex M• is perfect if it is isomorphic in the derived category to a bounded complex of finite-rank projective OX-modules. For a smooth, quasi-projective scheme X over a field, the derived category of perfect complexes Dperf(X) is equivalent to the bounded derived category of coherent OX-modules Db(Coh(X)). This equivalence, often attributed to Serre for projective varieties, establishes a deep connection between vector bundles (which are perfect complexes concentrated in degree zero) and general coherent sheaves in the derived setting.

The significance of perfect complexes extends to algebraic K-theory. The K-theory group K0(X) of a scheme X is initially defined using vector bundles (or locally free sheaves of finite rank). However, a more robust definition, particularly useful for singular schemes, involves perfect complexes. The Grothendieck group of perfect complexes K0(Dperf(X)) is naturally isomorphic to the K0(X) defined via vector bundles when X is regular. For singular schemes, the derived category of perfect complexes provides a well-behaved substitute for vector bundles, allowing for a definition of K-theory that captures more information about the geometry of X. The higher K-groups Ki(X) are also constructed using the derived category of perfect complexes, often via the "Waldhausen S-construction" applied to the category of perfect complexes with quasi-isomorphisms.

Perfect complexes are also crucial in defining the derived category of a scheme itself. For a general scheme X, the derived category of quasi-coherent sheaves D(X) is often too large to be manageable. Instead, one frequently works with the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves Db(Coh(X)) or, more fundamentally, the derived category of perfect complexes Dperf(X). The latter is often viewed as the "correct" derived category for studying the geometry of X, as it behaves well under various geometric operations such as proper pushforwards and flat pullbacks. For instance, if f: X → Y is a proper morphism, the derived pushforward R f* maps Dperf(X) to Dperf(Y). This property is essential for constructing derived functors in algebraic geometry, such as the six operations formalism.

Furthermore, the concept of perfect complexes is intimately linked with the notion of finite projective dimension. A complex M• is perfect if and only if it has finite projective dimension as an object in the derived category. This connection is particularly relevant when studying singularities. For a regular scheme, every coherent sheaf has finite projective dimension, and thus Db(Coh(X)) = Dperf(X). However, for singular schemes, the distinction becomes critical, and Dperf(X) often provides a more tractable category for homological investigations than the full Db(Coh(X)). The study of derived categories of singularities, for example, often involves understanding the quotient of Db(Coh(X)) by Dperf(X).

Verdier Localization and the Construction of Triangulated Categories

The construction of a derived category from an abelian category is a fundamental process in homological algebra, formally achieved through Verdier localization. This method, introduced by Jean-Louis Verdier, provides the rigorous foundation for why derived categories possess the structure of a triangulated category. Given an abelian category A (e.g., modules over a ring, or sheaves of modules on a scheme), we first form the category of chain complexes Ch(A). Within Ch(A), we identify a class of morphisms called quasi-isomorphisms: these are chain maps that induce isomorphisms on all homology groups. The goal is to formally invert all quasi-isomorphisms to obtain the derived category D(A).

The process of Verdier localization is analogous to constructing the field of fractions from an integral domain. We define the objects of D(A) to be the same as the objects of Ch(A), i.e., chain complexes. The morphisms in D(A) are constructed as "roofs" or "fractions" of chain maps. Specifically, a morphism from X• to Y• in D(A) is an equivalence class of diagrams X• ← W• → Y•, where the left arrow is a quasi-isomorphism and the right arrow is an arbitrary chain map. Two such roofs (X• ← W• → Y•) and (X• ← W'• → Y•) are equivalent if there exists a chain complex Z• and quasi-isomorphisms W• ← Z• → W'• such that the resulting diagrams commute. This construction ensures that all quasi-isomorphisms become isomorphisms in D(A).

The crucial outcome of Verdier localization is that D(A) naturally acquires the structure of a triangulated category. This means it is equipped with an autoequivalence, the shift functor [1], and a class of "distinguished triangles" satisfying a set of axioms. The shift functor maps a complex X• to X•[1], where (X•[1])n = Xn+1 and the differential is -d. Distinguished triangles are derived from exact sequences in the original abelian category. Specifically, a short exact sequence 0 → A → B → C → 0 in A gives rise to a distinguished triangle A → B → C → A[1] in D(A). More generally, for any chain map f: X• → Y•, one can construct a mapping cone Cone(f), and the sequence X• → Y• → Cone(f) → X•[1] forms a distinguished triangle.

The axioms of a triangulated category (TR1-TR4) ensure that this structure behaves consistently. TR1 states that any morphism can be completed to a distinguished triangle. TR2 describes the rotation property of triangles. TR3 ensures that the composition of two distinguished triangles yields another. TR4, often called the "octahedral axiom," is the most complex and ensures compatibility between different distinguished triangles. These axioms are essential for performing homological computations and for defining derived functors in a consistent manner. The ability to formally invert quasi-isomorphisms and obtain a triangulated category is what makes derived categories a powerful tool for extending homological methods beyond abelian categories.

Let's consider a different perspective. The complex X• (0 → Z → Z → 0, mult by 2) has H0(X•) = Z/2Z. There is a natural map from X• to Y• (0 → Z/2Z → 0). When tackling problems involving derived categories, especially those requiring proofs of properties or constructions, a solid understanding of the four triangulated category axioms (TR1-TR4) is indispensable. Do not just memorize them; understand their implications. For instance, TR2 (rotation of triangles) is frequently used to shift the perspective within a distinguished triangle, while TR4 (octahedral axiom) is crucial for proving compatibility between different exact sequences or mapping cones. Practice constructing mapping cones and applying the rotation axiom to transform triangles. Many advanced results in derived categories rely on iterative application of these fundamental axioms.

Differential Graded Categories as a Framework for Noncommutative Derived Categories

Differential graded categories (dg-categories) provide a powerful and flexible framework for generalizing the concept of a ring and its module category, leading to a rich theory of noncommutative derived categories. A dg-category 𝒞 is a category where the hom-sets Hom𝒞(X, Y) are not just abelian groups, but chain complexes of abelian groups. The composition of morphisms is given by a map of chain complexes, Hom𝒞(Y, Z) ⊗ Hom𝒞(X, Y) → Hom𝒞(X, Z), which respects the differentials and satisfies associativity up to chain homotopy. The identity morphism for each object is a cycle of degree 0 in its respective hom-complex.

The significance of dg-categories lies in their ability to encode homological information directly into the structure of the category itself. A dg-category can be thought of as a "categorified" differential graded algebra (dg-algebra). Indeed, a dg-algebra A is precisely a dg-category with a single object. Just as modules over a ring R form an abelian category, dg-modules over a dg-category 𝒞 form a dg-category. A right dg-module M over 𝒞 is a collection of chain complexes M(X) for each object X in 𝒞, together with maps M(Y) ⊗ Hom𝒞(X, Y) → M(X) satisfying certain compatibility conditions. The homotopy category Ho(𝒞) of a dg-category 𝒞 is obtained by formally inverting quasi-isomorphisms between dg-modules, resulting in a triangulated category.

This framework is particularly useful for constructing derived categories in noncommutative geometry. For example, if A is a non-commutative algebra, its derived category of modules D(Mod-A) can be realized as the homotopy category of dg-modules over A (viewed as a dg-category with one object). More generally, any triangulated category that arises as a derived category of an abelian category can be "rectified" to be the homotopy category of a dg-category. This means that the abstract structure of a triangulated category often has a concrete dg-enhancement.

Dg-categories also play a crucial role in the theory of derived equivalences. Two dg-categories 𝒞 and 𝒟 are said to be dg-equivalent if there exist dg-functors F: 𝒞 → 𝒟 and G: 𝒟 → 𝒞 such that F ∘ G and G ∘ F are naturally isomorphic to the identity functors in the dg-homotopy category. A stronger notion is that of a Morita equivalence for dg-categories, which implies an equivalence of their respective derived categories of modules. This allows for a more refined understanding of when two algebras or schemes have equivalent derived categories, often providing a more tractable setting for computations than working directly with abstract triangulated categories. The theory of dg-categories also underpins the construction of derived categories of singularities for noncommutative rings and the study of derived deformation theory for more general algebraic structures.

The Homological Perturbation Lemma and its Utility in Constructing Resolutions

The Homological Perturbation Lemma (HPL) is a powerful and versatile tool in homological algebra and algebraic topology, providing a systematic way to transfer homological data and construct resolutions. It addresses the situation where a chain complex (C•, d) is "perturbed" by a small change, and one wants to understand the homology of the perturbed complex. More precisely, it deals with a situation where a chain complex (C•, d) is chain homotopy equivalent to a simpler complex (H•, 0) (where H• carries the homology), and we then modify the differential d to d + δ. The HPL provides a constructive method to find a new differential d' on C•, a new chain homotopy equivalence, and a new chain contraction for the perturbed complex (C•, d + δ).

The lemma's setup typically involves a chain complex (C•, d) with a given chain contraction (s, i, p), where i: H• → C• is an inclusion of the homology, p: C• → H• is a projection, and s: C• → C• is a chain homotopy such that idC - i p = ds + sd. The perturbation is given by a chain map δ: C• → C• such that (d + δ) is still a differential (i.e., (d + δ)2 = 0). The HPL then provides explicit formulas for a new chain contraction (s', i', p') for the perturbed complex (C•, d + δ), as well as a new differential dH on H• such that (H•, dH) is chain homotopy equivalent to (C•, d + δ). The new differential dH is often called the "transfer differential."

One of the primary applications of the HPL is in the construction of explicit resolutions. For instance, in computing Ext and Tor groups, one often needs projective or injective resolutions. The HPL allows one to start with a known, simpler resolution (e.g., a free resolution of a module) and then "perturb" it to obtain a resolution for a related, more complex module or for a module over a perturbed ring. This is particularly useful in situations where direct construction of resolutions is cumbersome. For example, in the context of group cohomology, the HPL can be used to construct resolutions for modules over group algebras.

Another significant application is in the construction of spectral sequences. The HPL can be used to transfer a differential from one complex to another, which is a key step in setting up certain spectral sequences, such as those arising from filtered complexes. It also plays a role in defining derived functors in specific contexts, by providing a constructive way to obtain resolutions that are compatible with certain operations. The iterative nature of the HPL also makes it suitable for computational algorithms in homological algebra, where one might need to refine resolutions or chain homotopies through a series of small adjustments. The lemma essentially guarantees that if a complex is "close" to being acyclic (i.e., it has a chain contraction), then a small perturbation will still result in a complex whose homology can be understood constructively.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Projective Varieties and Fourier-Mukai Transforms

The study of derived categories of coherent sheaves on projective varieties is a cornerstone of modern algebraic geometry, deeply connected with birational geometry and the theory of derived equivalences. For a smooth projective variety X over an algebraically closed field, its bounded derived category of coherent sheaves Db(Coh(X)) is a fundamental invariant. This category captures a vast amount of geometric information about X, often more than classical invariants like cohomology rings or Picard groups.

A central concept in this area is the Fourier-Mukai transform. A Fourier-Mukai transform is an equivalence of derived categories Db(Coh(X)) ≅ Db(Coh(Y)) between two varieties X and Y, induced by an object P in Db(Coh(X × Y)), called the Fourier-Mukai kernel. Explicitly, such a transform FP: Db(Coh(X)) → Db(Coh(Y)) is given by FP(E) = R pY* (pX*E ⊗L P), where pX and pY are the projection maps from X × Y to X and Y respectively, R pY* is the derived pushforward, and pX* is the derived pullback. The inverse transform is given by a kernel P∨, typically related to the dual of P and a shift.

The importance of Fourier-Mukai transforms stems from their ability to relate the geometry of seemingly different varieties. For example, a classical result by Mukai shows that an abelian variety A is derived equivalent to its dual abelian variety A∨ via a Fourier-Mukai transform with the Poincaré bundle as the kernel. This provides a deep homological interpretation of classical duality theorems. Another significant result is the Bondal-Orlov criterion, which states that for smooth projective varieties X and Y, a derived equivalence Db(Coh(X)) ≅ Db(Coh(Y)) implies that X and Y are birationally equivalent if their canonical bundles are trivial or anti-canonical. More generally, if the canonical bundles are not trivial, the equivalence implies a relationship between their minimal models.

The study of derived equivalences on projective varieties has led to profound insights into the classification of varieties. For instance, it is known that if Db(Coh(X)) ≅ Db(Coh(Y)) for smooth projective varieties X and Y, then X and Y must have the same dimension, and their Hodge numbers are related. However, derived equivalence is a finer invariant than birational equivalence; there exist non-isomorphic, non-birationally equivalent varieties that are derived equivalent (e.g., certain K3 surfaces). The theory of tilting bundles and tilting complexes provides a powerful method for constructing such equivalences. A tilting bundle T on X is a vector bundle such that End(T) has finite global dimension and T generates Db(Coh(X)). Then Db(Coh(X)) is equivalent to Db(Mod-End(T)), where End(T) is a finite-dimensional algebra. This allows for the translation of geometric problems into algebraic ones, and vice-versa, facilitating computations and classifications.

  • Assuming that Dperf(X) is always equivalent to Db(Coh(X)) for any scheme X; this equivalence only holds for regular schemes. For singular schemes, Dperf(X) is a proper subcategory.
  • Misinterpreting the role of quasi-isomorphisms in Verdier localization, specifically forgetting that they become isomorphisms, not just equivalences, in the derived category.
  • Neglecting the "up to chain homotopy" aspect in dg-category definitions, which is crucial for defining composition and identities correctly.
  • Applying the Homological Perturbation Lemma without verifying the existence of a suitable chain contraction or ensuring that the perturbed differential squares to zero.
  • Assuming that any derived equivalence between Db(Coh(X)) and Db(Coh(Y)) implies that X and Y are isomorphic or even birationally equivalent; this is only true under specific conditions (e.g., Bondal-Orlov criterion for specific canonical bundle properties).

Derived Categories of Modules over A∞‑Categories and the Homotopy Theory of A∞‑Modules

An A∞‑category is a generalization of a differential graded category in which composition is associative only up to an infinite hierarchy of higher homotopies. The derived category of modules over an A∞‑category, denoted D(A∞‑Mod), is constructed by first forming the category of right A∞‑modules (functors to chain complexes respecting the A∞‑structure up to coherent homotopy) and then localizing at quasi‑isomorphisms. This localization can be performed either by Verdier’s method on the homotopy category of A∞‑modules or by equipping the category of A∞‑modules with a model structure whose weak equivalences are quasi‑isomorphisms; the resulting homotopy category is canonically equivalent to the Verdier localization. A fundamental result of Lefèvre‑Hasegawa and later of Faonte states that the derived category of an A∞‑category is invariant under A∞‑Morita equivalences, i.e. under quasi‑equivalences of A∞‑categories. This invariance makes D(A∞‑Mod) the natural receptacle for non‑commutative geometry when the algebraic objects are only defined up to homotopy, such as Fukaya categories in symplectic topology. The homotopy theory of A∞‑modules is controlled by the bar‑cobar adjunction: the bar construction sends an A∞‑module to a cofree comodule over the cobar construction of the A∞‑category, and the cobar construction provides a quasi‑inverse up to homotopy. This adjunction yields a concrete description of the derived Hom‑complexes as homotopy limits of explicit cosimplicial objects, which is essential for computations in Hochschild cohomology of A∞‑categories. Moreover, the existence of a model structure on A∞‑modules (projective, injective, or Reedy) allows one to apply the full machinery of homotopical algebra — homotopy (co)limits, derived functors, and Bousfield localizations — inside D(A∞‑Mod). In practice, one often replaces an A∞‑category by a quasi‑equivalent dg‑category via the rectification theorem of Dwyer‑Kan, thereby reducing many questions to the better‑understood dg‑setting while retaining the original homotopical information.

Derived Categories of Sheaves on Orbifolds and the Chen–Ruan Cohomology

Orbifolds are spaces locally modeled on quotients of manifolds by finite group actions. The derived category of constructible sheaves on an orbifold X, denoted D^b_c(X), can be defined as the derived category of sheaves on a proper étale groupoid presenting X, or equivalently as the equivariant derived category on a global quotient presentation [M/G]. The inertia orbifold ΛX, whose components correspond to twisted sectors indexed by conjugacy classes of isotropy groups, plays a central role. Chen and Ruan introduced an orbifold cohomology ring H^*_CR(X) whose underlying vector space is the direct sum of the ordinary cohomology of the twisted sectors, with a degree shifting number (age) attached to each sector. The product, called the Chen–Ruan cup product, is defined by counting orbifold pseudoholomorphic maps from a three‑pointed orbifold Riemann surface, and it can be interpreted as a convolution product on the derived category of sheaves on the inertia stack. In the language of derived categories, the Chen–Ruan cohomology is the hypercohomology of a certain complex of sheaves on ΛX equipped with a monoidal structure coming from the correspondence ΛX ×_X ΛX ← Λ2X → ΛX, where Λ2X parametrizes pairs of composable loops. This monoidal structure makes D^b_c(ΛX) into a monoidal triangulated category, and the Chen–Ruan product corresponds to the induced product on cohomology. A key theorem of Lupercio‑Poddar‑Uribe‑Xicoténcatl shows that for a global quotient [M/G] the derived category of G‑equivariant sheaves on M is equivalent to the derived category of sheaves on the inertia stack, and under this equivalence the convolution product matches the Chen–Ruan product. This derived‑categorical perspective explains the associativity and the grading shift of the Chen–Ruan product as consequences of the associativity of the convolution and the geometry of the age grading. It also provides a natural framework for the Crepant Resolution Conjecture: if Y → X is a crepant resolution of an orbifold, the derived equivalence D^b(Coh(Y)) ≅ D^b_c(X) (established by Bridgeland‑King‑Reid for G‑Hilb) intertwines the ordinary cup product on Y with the Chen–Ruan product on X.

Let X = [C2 / Z3] where Z3 acts with weights (1,2). The inertia orbifold has three components: the untwisted sector X0 ≅ X and two twisted sectors X1, X2 each isomorphic to a point. Compute the Chen–Ruan product of the degree‑2 classes α ∈ H2_CR(X1) and β ∈ H2_CR(X2) and identify the resulting class in H4_CR(X0).
The age of the twisted sector X1 is 1 (weights 1/3, 2/3 sum to 1) and similarly for X2. The degree‑2 classes α, β correspond to the fundamental classes of the points shifted by age 1, so they live in H0(X1)[2] and H0(X2)[2]. The convolution product uses the correspondence Λ2X → ΛX ×_X ΛX. For this abelian group action, Λ2X has components indexed by pairs (g,h) with g,h ∈ Z3. The component mapping to (X1, X2) corresponds to (1,2) and its product is the sector X0 because 1+2 ≡ 0 mod 3. The convolution of the fundamental classes yields the fundamental class of X0 shifted by the sum of ages (1+1=2). Hence α ⋆_CR β = [X0] ∈ H4_CR(X0), which matches the known Chen–Ruan ring structure where the product of the two twisted sector generators gives the volume form on the untwisted sector.

The degree shift is the sum of the ages. This algorithmic approach avoids memorising case‑by‑case formulas and works uniformly for global quotients.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Singular Varieties and the Category of Singularities

For a scheme X of finite type over a field, the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves D^b(Coh(X)) contains the full subcategory Perf(X) of perfect complexes (those locally quasi‑isomorphic to bounded complexes of finite‑rank free modules). When X is regular, the inclusion Perf(X) D^b(Coh(X)) is an equivalence; for singular X it is not. The Verdier quotient D^b(Coh(X))/Perf(X) is called the category of singularities of X and is denoted Dsg(X). This triangulated category measures the failure of finite projective dimension for coherent sheaves. A fundamental theorem of Orlov establishes that for a projective hypersurface X = {f=0} ⊂ P^n defined by a homogeneous polynomial f of degree d, there is an equivalence Dsg(X) ≅ MFgr(f), where the right‑hand side is the graded category of matrix factorizations of f. This equivalence is realized by the Knörrer periodicity functor and identifies the singularity category with the homotopy category of maximal Cohen‑Macaulay modules over the affine cone. The category of singularities is invariant under derived equivalences: if D^b(Coh(X)) ≅ D^b(Coh(Y)) then Dsg(X) ≅ Dsg(Y). Moreover, Dsg(X) carries a natural Serre functor given by the shift [2] when X is Gorenstein of dimension n, reflecting the Calabi‑Yau property of the singularity category. For isolated hypersurface singularities, Dsg(X) is equivalent to the stable category of maximal Cohen‑Macaulay modules, which is known to be a Hom‑finite, Krull‑Schmidt triangulated category. The objects of Dsg(X) can be described as the cokernels of morphisms between perfect complexes, and the morphisms are computed as Ext groups in the stable category. This description is crucial for the classification of singularities via derived categories: for example, simple (ADE) surface singularities have Dsg equivalent to the derived category of representations of the corresponding Dynkin quiver, while simple elliptic singularities yield a category equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on an elliptic curve.

Derived Categories of Modules over Hopf Algebroids and the Cobar Construction

A Hopf algebroid (A, Γ) consists of a base algebra A and a total algebra Γ equipped with source and target maps A → Γ, a coproduct Γ → Γ ⊗_A Γ, a counit Γ → A, and an antipode, satisfying axioms that generalize those of a Hopf algebra to a non‑commutative base. The category of comodules over a Hopf algebroid, Comod_Γ, is an abelian category with enough injectives. Its derived category D(Comod_Γ) can be studied via the cobar construction: the cobar complex Ω^*(Γ) = (Γ ⊗_A Γ ⊗_A …) carries a differential graded algebra structure whose derived category of dg‑modules is equivalent to D(Comod_Γ). This equivalence is a manifestation of the general principle that comodules over a coalgebra are modules over its cobar dg‑algebra. When (A, Γ) arises from a flat affine groupoid scheme, the derived category of comodules is equivalent to the equivariant derived category of quasi‑coherent sheaves on the associated stack. The cobar construction also provides a concrete model for the derived Hom‑complexes: for comodules M, N, the complex HomComod_Γ(M, N) is quasi‑isomorphic to the total complex of the cosimplicial object given by the iterated cobar construction applied to M^* ⊗_A N. This description underlies the computation of Ext groups in the Adams‑Novikov spectral sequence, where (A, Γ) = (MU_*, MU_*MU) is the Hopf algebroid representing complex cobordism. In that context, the cobar complex computes the E2‑page ExtMU_*MUs,t(MU_*, MU_*), and the convergence of the spectral sequence to the stable homotopy groups of spheres is a deep theorem of Adams. The derived category D(Comod_Γ) inherits a monoidal structure from the tensor product of comodules over A, making it a tensor triangulated category. The Balmer spectrum of this tensor triangulated category recovers the chromatic filtration of the stable homotopy category, linking the algebraic geometry of Hopf algebroids to the global structure of stable homotopy theory.

Derived Categories of Sheaves on Topological Stacks and the Homotopy Theory of Stacks

A topological stack is a stack on the site of topological spaces (or a suitable subcategory such as compactly generated weak Hausdorff spaces) that admits an atlas by a topological space. The derived category of sheaves of abelian groups on a topological stack X, denoted D(Sh(X)), can be constructed by taking a hypercover by representable stacks and forming the totalization of the associated cosimplicial derived category. Equivalently, one can present X by a topological groupoid G and define D(Sh(X)) as the equivariant derived category D_G(Sh(G0)) of sheaves on the space of objects G0 with a G‑action. This construction is independent of the presentation up to canonical equivalence. The homotopy theory of topological stacks is captured by the model structure on simplicial presheaves (or on topological groupoids) where weak equivalences are stalkwise weak homotopy equivalences. The derived category D(Sh(X)) is the homotopy category of the model category of sheaves of chain complexes on X, and it carries a natural t‑structure whose heart is the abelian category of sheaves of abelian groups on X. A key result of Toën and Vezzosi shows that for a differentiable stack presented by a Lie groupoid, the derived category of sheaves of C‑vector spaces is equivalent to the derived category of representations of the associated dg‑category of the groupoid. This equivalence allows one to transport geometric operations — pullback, pushforward, tensor product, and Verdier duality — to the dg‑setting where they become homotopically well‑behaved. In particular, the six‑operations formalism for topological stacks (f^*, f_*, f_!, f^!, ⊗, Hom) can be developed by working with the corresponding dg‑functors on the presenting groupoids and then descending to the stack. The derived category of a topological stack also admits a natural monoidal structure given by the derived tensor product, and when X is a global quotient [M/G] this monoidal structure corresponds to the convolution product on G‑equivariant sheaves. These structures are essential for the formulation of the geometric Langlands program for topological stacks and for the study of topological quantum field theories via the cobordism hypothesis.

  • Assuming that every derived equivalence between singular varieties induces an equivalence of their singularity categories without checking that the equivalence restricts to perfect complexes.
  • Treating the cobar construction of a Hopf algebroid as a strict dg‑algebra without verifying the coassociativity up to homotopy; the cobar differential squares to zero only because of the Hopf algebroid axioms.
  • Neglecting the distinction between the derived category of sheaves on a topological stack and the derived category of the underlying coarse moduli space; they differ whenever the stack has non‑trivial stabilizers.
  • Applying the Chen–Ruan product formula without accounting for the age shift; the degree of the product is the sum of the degrees plus the sum of the ages of the twisted sectors involved.
  • Believing that the derived category of A∞‑modules is independent of the choice of A∞‑model without invoking the Morita invariance theorem; different A∞‑structures on the same cohomology category can yield non‑equivalent derived categories.
  • Overlooking the necessity of a hypercover (rather than a single atlas) when defining D(Sh(X)) for a general topological stack; a single atlas does not capture higher homotopical information.

These advanced topics illustrate how derived categories serve as a unifying language across algebraic geometry, topology, and mathematical physics.

Derived Categories of Toric Varieties and the Cox Construction

The derived category of coherent sheaves on a smooth toric variety admits a complete description in terms of the combinatorics of its fan. For a toric variety X defined by a fan Σ in a lattice N ≅ ℤn, the Cox ring R = ℂ[xρ : ρ ∈ Σ(1)] is graded by the class group Cl(X). The total coordinate space is the affine space 𝔸Σ(1) with the action of the torus H = Spec ℂ[Cl(X)]. The GIT quotient [𝔸Σ(1) ⧹ H] recovers X as a stack. The derived category Db(Coh(X)) is equivalent to the H-equivariant derived category of the total coordinate space, which by the Bernstein–Lunts formalism is the derived category of graded modules over the Cox ring modulo torsion. This equivalence is realized by the functor that sends a graded R-module M to the sheaf M̃ on X. The heart of the standard t-structure on Db(Coh(X)) corresponds to the category of finitely generated graded R-modules modulo finite length modules. Exceptional collections on toric varieties are constructed from line bundles OX(D) where D runs over a set of divisors whose classes form a basis of the nef cone. The full strong exceptional collection of line bundles on a smooth projective toric variety exists if and only if the variety is a product of projective spaces and Hirzebruch surfaces (Hille–Perling). For general smooth projective toric varieties, one uses the collection of line bundles associated to the vertices of the moment polytope, which is full but not necessarily strong. The mutation of exceptional collections corresponds to wall-crossing in the space of stability conditions. The derived category of a toric variety also carries a natural action of the torus T = N ⊗ ℂ×, and the equivariant derived category DbT(Coh(X)) is equivalent to the derived category of modules over the equivariant Cox ring, which is a deformation of the ordinary Cox ring by the torus weights. This structure is essential for the computation of the quantum cohomology of X via the mirror symmetry isomorphism with the Jacobian ring of the Landau–Ginzburg superpotential W = Σ xρ on the mirror torus.

Derived Categories and the Minimal Model Program: Flops, Flips, and Derived Equivalences

The minimal model program (MMP) for algebraic varieties produces a sequence of birational transformations — divisorial contractions, flips, and flops — that terminate in a minimal model or a Mori fiber space. A fundamental conjecture of Bondal and Orlov, proved in many cases by Kawamata, Namikawa, and others, asserts that a flop between smooth projective varieties induces an equivalence of their bounded derived categories of coherent sheaves. Let f : X ⇢ X+ be a flop of a curve C ≅ ℙ1 with normal bundle O(−1) ⊕ O(−1). The equivalence Φ : Db(Coh(X)) → Db(Coh(X+)) is given by the Fourier–Mukai transform with kernel the structure sheaf of the fiber product X ×Y X+ over the common contraction Y. This kernel is a perfect complex on the product, and the transform is an exact functor of triangulated categories. For a simple flop, the equivalence sends the structure sheaf OC to its shift OC+[−1] and acts as the identity on objects supported away from the flopping curve. The autoequivalence group of Db(Coh(X)) contains the subgroup generated by spherical twists around the spherical objects OC(−1) and the pullback of line bundles from the base Y. In dimension three, every flop between smooth varieties is a composition of simple flops, and the derived equivalence is a composition of the corresponding spherical twists. For flips, which are not isomorphisms in codimension one, the situation is more subtle: the derived categories of the flip source and target are not equivalent in general, but they are related by a semi-orthogonal decomposition. If f : X → Y is a flip contracting a divisor E to a subvariety Z ⊂ Y, then Db(Coh(X)) admits a semi-orthogonal decomposition ⟨Db(Coh(Z)), Db(Coh(Y))⟩ where the first component is generated by the exceptional collection of twists of OE. The MMP for varieties with mild singularities (ℚ-factorial terminal) extends these results: the derived category of a ℚ-factorial terminal variety is equivalent to the derived category of a crepant resolution, and the flop equivalence extends to the singular setting via the derived category of the singular variety defined as the Verdier quotient of the derived category of a resolution by the subcategory of complexes supported on the exceptional locus. The existence of derived equivalences for flops is a key ingredient in the proof of the derived category version of the Kawamata log-flip conjecture and in the construction of stability conditions on Calabi–Yau threefolds via the Bridgeland program.

Worked Example: Flop Equivalence on the Atiyah Flop
Let X be the total space of Oℙ1(−1) ⊕ Oℙ1(−1) and X+ its flop. The flop contracts the zero section C ≅ ℙ1 to a node in the singular threefold Y. Describe the Fourier–Mukai kernel K ∈ Db(Coh(X × X+)) that induces the derived equivalence ΦK : Db(Coh(X)) → Db(Coh(X+)). Compute ΦK(OC).
The fiber product X ×Y X+ is the blow-up of X × X+ along the diagonal of the exceptional curves C × C+. The kernel K is the structure sheaf OX×YX+ viewed as a complex concentrated in degree 0. The transform is ΦK(−) = Rπ+* (π*(−) ⊗L K) where π, π+ are the projections. For the structure sheaf OC of the zero section, π*OC = OC×X+. The derived tensor product with K restricts to OC×YC+ = OC×C+. Pushing forward along π+ gives the structure sheaf of C+ with a shift because the fiber of π+ over a point of C+ is the curve C ≅ ℙ1, and Rπ+* OC×C+ = OC+ ⊗ RΓ(C, OC) = OC+ ⊕ OC+[−1]. The component OC+ is killed by the projection to the quotient by the subcategory generated by OC, leaving ΦK(OC) = OC+[−1]. This matches the spherical twist formula TOC(−1)(OC) = OC[−1].
Exam Strategy: Recognizing Derived Equivalences in Birational Geometry
When a question asks whether a birational map induces a derived equivalence, first classify the map: flops (isomorphisms in codimension one, crepant) give equivalences; flips (not isomorphisms in codimension one) give semi-orthogonal decompositions; divisorial contractions give fully faithful embeddings with admissible subcategories. For flops, check if the normal bundle of the flopping locus is a sum of O(−1) bundles — this guarantees a simple flop and a spherical twist equivalence. For threefolds, every flop factors into simple flops. Memorize the kernel for the Atiyah flop: OX×YX+. The action on the structure sheaf of the flopping curve is always a shift by −1. In multiple choice, the correct answer often distinguishes between equivalence (flop) and semi-orthogonal decomposition (flip).

Derived Categories of Landau–Ginzburg Models and Matrix Factorizations

A Landau–Ginzburg (LG) model is a pair (X, W) where X is a smooth variety (often a vector space or a toric variety) and W : X → 𝔸1 is a regular function called the superpotential. The category of B-branes in the LG model is the derived category of matrix factorizations of W, denoted MF(X, W). A matrix factorization of W is a ℤ/2-graded free OX-module E = E0 ⊕ E1 with an odd endomorphism d : E → E such that d2 = W · idE. Morphisms are ℤ/2-graded module maps modulo homotopy. The category MF(X, W) is a triangulated category with shift functor given by swapping the ℤ/2-grading. When W has an isolated critical point at the origin, Orlov's equivalence relates MF(𝔸n, W) to the derived category of singularities Dsg(W−1(0)) of the fiber. For a general LG model, the derived category of matrix factorizations is equivalent to the Verdier quotient of the derived category of coherent sheaves on the total space of the line bundle OX by the subcategory of complexes supported on the critical locus of W. The Hochschild homology of MF(X, W) computes the Jacobian ring of W, which is the ring of functions on the critical locus. The category MF(X, W) carries a natural Calabi–Yau structure of dimension dim X − 2, given by the residue pairing on the Jacobian ring. When X is a toric variety and W is a generic section of an ample line bundle, the category MF(X, W) is equivalent to the derived category of the Calabi–Yau hypersurface W = 0 in X (Orlov's theorem). This equivalence is realized by the Knörrer periodicity functor that sends a matrix factorization to its cokernel. For a LG model with a group action G preserving W, the equivariant category MFG(X, W) is equivalent to the derived category of the quotient stack [W−1(0)/G]. The mirror symmetry conjecture for Fano varieties states that the derived category of a Fano variety Y is equivalent to the category of matrix factorizations of its mirror LG model (X, W). This has been proved for projective spaces, Grassmannians, and many toric Fano varieties. The equivalence is constructed via the exceptional collection on Y and the corresponding exceptional collection of matrix factorizations on X, which are often given by the Koszul complex of the superpotential.

Derived Categories of K3 Surfaces and the Derived Torelli Theorem

A K3 surface S is a simply connected compact complex surface with trivial canonical bundle. Its derived category Db(Coh(S)) is a 2-Calabi–Yau triangulated category, meaning the Serre functor is the shift by 2. The numerical Grothendieck group N(S) ≅ H*(S, ℤ) with the Mukai pairing (v, w) = −∫S v∨ · w is a lattice of signature (4, 20) isomorphic to the Mukai lattice Λ = U⊕4 ⊕ E8(−1)⊕2. The derived Torelli theorem (Orlov, Huybrechts, Stellari) states that two K3 surfaces S and S′ are isomorphic if and only if there exists an exact equivalence Φ : Db(Coh(S)) → Db(Coh(S′)) that preserves the natural orientation of the positive 4-space in N(S) ⊗ ℝ. The group of exact autoequivalences of Db(Coh(S)) is generated by shifts, pullbacks by automorphisms of S, tensoring by line bundles, and spherical twists around spherical objects (objects E with Ext*(E, E) ≅ H*(S2, ℂ)). The spherical twists correspond to reflections in the Mukai lattice, and the subgroup generated by them is the monodromy group of the K3 surface. The space of stability conditions Stab(S) on a K3 surface is a connected complex manifold of dimension 22, and the action of the autoequivalence group on Stab(S) is properly discontinuous with quotient the moduli space of K3 surfaces. The Bridgeland stability conditions on S are constructed by tilting the standard t-structure with respect to a slope function defined by an ample divisor. The walls in the space of stability conditions correspond to the loci where a spherical object becomes semistable, and crossing a wall induces a spherical twist. The derived category of a K3 surface also determines its Fourier–Mukai partners: the set of K3 surfaces S′ with Db(Coh(S)) ≅ Db(Coh(S′)) is finite and corresponds to the set of primitive embeddings of the Néron–Severi lattice NS(S) into the Mukai lattice with certain properties. The number of Fourier–Mukai partners is given by a formula of Hosono–Lian–Oguiso–Yau involving the discriminant group of NS(S). For a generic K3 surface with Picard rank 1, there are no non-trivial Fourier–Mukai partners. For K3 surfaces with higher Picard rank, the number can be large; for example, a K3 surface with NS(S) ≅ U has 24 Fourier–Mukai partners. The derived category also controls the moduli of sheaves on S: the moduli space of stable sheaves with a fixed Mukai vector is a hyperkähler manifold, and its derived category is equivalent to the derived category of S via a Fourier–Mukai transform with the universal sheaf as kernel.

Derived Categories of Fano Varieties and Kuznetsov Components

For a Fano variety X of dimension n with Picard rank 1, the derived category Db(Coh(X)) often admits a semi-orthogonal decomposition into a sequence of exceptional objects and a non-trivial admissible subcategory called the Kuznetsov component. Let X be a Fano variety of index r (the largest integer such that −KX = rH for an ample divisor H). If X has a full exceptional collection, then Db(Coh(X)) is generated by exceptional objects and the Kuznetsov component is zero. The interesting cases are when X does not admit a full exceptional collection. For a cubic threefold X ⊂ ℙ4, Kuznetsov proved that Db(Coh(X)) = ⟨𝒦𝓊(X), OX, OX(1), OX(2)⟩ where 𝒦𝓊(X) is a 2-Calabi–Yau category equivalent to the derived category of a K3 surface. For a quartic threefold X ⊂ ℙ4, the decomposition is ⟨𝒦𝓊(X), OX, OX(1)⟩ and 𝒦𝓊(X) is a 1-Calabi–Yau category (a fractional Calabi–Yau category of dimension 5/2). For a Gushel–Mukai variety (a linear section of the Grassmannian Gr(2, 5) or a double cover of a linear section), the Kuznetsov component is equivalent to the derived category of a K3 surface or an abelian surface. The Kuznetsov component controls the birational geometry of X: two Fano varieties with equivalent Kuznetsov components are often birational, and the equivalence of Kuznetsov components induces a derived equivalence of their moduli spaces of stable sheaves. The Hochschild homology of 𝒦𝓊(X) computes the primitive cohomology of X. For a cubic threefold, HH*(𝒦𝓊(X)) ≅ H3(X, ℂ) as a Hodge structure. The category 𝒦𝓊(X) admits a Serre functor which is a shift by the fractional Calabi–Yau dimension. The existence of a stability condition on 𝒦𝓊(X) is a major open problem in general, but for cubic threefolds it has been constructed by Bayer–Lahoz–Macrì–Nuer–Perry–Stellari using the geometric stability conditions on the associated K3 surface. The Kuznetsov component also appears in the homological projective duality (HPD) framework of Kuznetsov: if X → ℙn is a linear section of a homogeneous variety, then the HPD category is equivalent to the Kuznetsov component of the dual linear section. This provides a powerful tool for computing derived categories of linear sections of Grassmannians, quadrics, and other homogeneous varieties. The non-vanishing of the Kuznetsov component is an obstruction to the existence of a full exceptional collection, and it is conjectured that a Fano variety has a full exceptional collection if and only if its Kuznetsov component is zero.

  • Assuming that every flop induces a derived equivalence without checking the crepant condition; a flop is crepant by definition, but a flip is not, and flips do not induce equivalences.
  • Treating the derived category of a toric variety as independent of the choice of fan; the derived category depends on the fan, but the Cox ring presentation shows that it only depends on the GIT quotient stack.
  • Applying Orlov's equivalence for matrix factorizations to non-isolated singularities without using the dg-category of singularities; the equivalence MF(X, W) ≅ Dsg(W−1(0)) holds for isolated singularities, but for non-isolated ones one must use the dg-category of singularities.
  • Believing that the derived Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces holds without the orientation condition; an equivalence that reverses the orientation of the positive 4-space corresponds to a Fourier–Mukai partner, not an isomorphism.
  • Neglecting the distinction between the numerical Grothendieck group and the full Grothendieck group; the Mukai lattice is the numerical Grothendieck group, and the derived Torelli theorem uses the numerical lattice with the Mukai pairing.
  • Overlooking the role of the Serre functor in identifying Calabi–Yau dimensions; the Kuznetsov component of a cubic threefold is 2-Calabi–Yau, while that of a quartic threefold is 5/2-Calabi–Yau, and this fractional dimension is detected by the Serre functor.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Weighted Projective Spaces and Mirror Symmetry

Weighted projective spaces ℙ(𝑎₀, …, 𝑎ₙ) are toric stacks whose derived categories of coherent sheaves admit a complete description via exceptional collections. For a weighted projective line ℙ(𝑎, 𝑏, 𝑐) with 1/𝑎 + 1/𝑏 + 1/𝑐 > 1, the category Dᵇ(Coh(ℙ(𝑎, 𝑏, 𝑐))) is equivalent to the derived category of representations of a canonical algebra of tubular type, and its Grothendieck group carries a non-degenerate Euler form. When 1/𝑎 + 1/𝑏 + 1/𝑐 = 1 (the elliptic case), the category is equivalent to Dᵇ(Coh(𝐸)) for an elliptic curve 𝐸, and when the sum is < 1 (the hyperbolic case), the category has infinite global dimension and no full exceptional collection. The mirror symmetry conjecture for weighted projective spaces, formulated by Borisov–Chen–Smith and proved in many cases by Ballard–Favero–Katzarkov, states that the derived category of a weighted projective space is equivalent to the Fukaya category of its mirror Landau–Ginzburg model (ℂ*)ⁿ, 𝑊) where 𝑊 is a Laurent polynomial whose Newton polytope is the fan polytope of the toric stack. For ℙ(1, 1, 2), the mirror is (ℂ*)² with superpotential 𝑊 = 𝑥 + 𝑦 + 1/(𝑥²𝑦), and the equivalence is realized by a Fourier–Mukai transform with kernel given by the structure sheaf of the universal family. The Hochschild cohomology HH*(Dᵇ(Coh(ℙ(𝑎₀, …, 𝑎ₙ)))) computes the quantum cohomology of the mirror, and the closed-open string map identifies the quantum product with the Yoneda product. The derived category also controls the variation of GIT quotients: as the stability parameter crosses a wall, the derived category of the GIT quotient undergoes a mutation of the exceptional collection, corresponding to a cluster transformation in the mirror. The Orlov equivalence for graded matrix factorizations relates Dᵇ(Coh(ℙ(𝑎₀, …, 𝑎ₙ))) to the category of graded matrix factorizations of the superpotential on the total space of the tautological bundle, providing a computational tool for homological mirror symmetry.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Hilbert Schemes of Points and Nakajima Operators

For a smooth quasi-projective surface 𝑆, the Hilbert scheme of 𝑛 points Hilbⁿ(𝑆) parametrizes length-𝑛 subschemes of 𝑆. The derived category Dᵇ(Coh(Hilbⁿ(𝑆))) carries a rich structure discovered by Nakajima, Grojnowski, and others. The direct sum ⊕ₙ Dᵇ(Coh(Hilbⁿ(𝑆))) admits an action of the Heisenberg algebra generated by operators 𝔮ₖ(𝛼) for 𝑘 ∈ ℤ{0} and 𝛼 ∈ H*(𝑆), satisfying [𝔮ₖ(𝛼), 𝔮ₗ(𝛽)] = 𝑘 δₖ,₋ₗ (𝛼, 𝛽) where (·, ·) is the intersection pairing. These operators are constructed via Fourier–Mukai transforms with kernels supported on the nested Hilbert schemes Hilbⁿ,ⁿ⁺ᵏ(𝑆) = { (𝑍, 𝑍') | 𝑍 ⊂ 𝑍', length(𝑍') − length(𝑍) = |𝑘| }. The vacuum vector is the structure sheaf of Hilb⁰(𝑆) = pt, and the Fock space representation is irreducible. When 𝑆 = ℂ², the Hilbert scheme Hilbⁿ(ℂ²) is a crepant resolution of the symmetric product Symⁿ(ℂ²), and the derived equivalence Dᵇ(Coh(Hilbⁿ(ℂ²))) ≅ Dᵇ(Coh(Symⁿ(ℂ²)))ᴳ (where 𝐺 = 𝔖ₙ) is given by the Bridgeland–King–Reid transform. The Nakajima operators on Hilbⁿ(ℂ²) correspond to the creation/annihilation operators on the Fock space of the infinite wedge representation of 𝔤𝔩(∞). For a K3 surface 𝑆, the derived category Dᵇ(Coh(Hilbⁿ(𝑆))) is equivalent to the derived category of a moduli space of stable sheaves on 𝑆 via a Fourier–Mukai transform with the universal ideal sheaf as kernel, and the Nakajima operators generate the monodromy action on the cohomology of the moduli space. The categorical Heisenberg action categorifies the classical Heisenberg action on ⊕ₙ H*(Hilbⁿ(𝑆)), and the Virasoro operators constructed from the Heisenberg generators categorify the Virasoro constraints on Gromov–Witten invariants of 𝑆.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Moduli Spaces of Stable Sheaves and Mukai's Program

For a K3 or abelian surface 𝑆, the moduli space 𝑀 = 𝑀_𝐻(𝑣) of 𝐻-stable sheaves with Mukai vector 𝑣 = (𝑟, 𝑐₁, 𝑠) ∈ H*(𝑆, ℤ) is a hyperkähler manifold of dimension 𝑣² + 2 when non-empty and fine. Mukai constructed a Fourier–Mukai equivalence Φ_ℰ: Dᵇ(Coh(𝑆)) → Dᵇ(Coh(𝑀)) where ℰ is the universal sheaf on 𝑆 × 𝑀, normalized so that det(ℰ)|𝑠×𝑀 ≅ 𝒪_𝑀. This equivalence induces an isometry of Mukai lattices H*(𝑆, ℤ) ≅ H*(𝑀, ℤ) preserving the Mukai pairing (𝑣, 𝑤) = ∫_𝑆 𝑣^∨ 𝑤. When 𝑣 is primitive and 𝐻 is generic, 𝑀 is fine and the equivalence is an isomorphism of derived categories. The inverse equivalence has kernel ℰ^∨ ⊗ π_𝑀* det(ℰ)⁻¹ [dim 𝑀]. The autoequivalence group of Dᵇ(Coh(𝑆)) contains the subgroup generated by tensoring with line bundles, shifts, and spherical twists along spherical objects (objects 𝐸 with Ext*(𝐸, 𝐸) ≅ H*(𝑆², ℂ)). Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Cluster Varieties and the Fock–Goncharov Duality

Cluster varieties, introduced by Fock and Goncharov, are algebraic varieties constructed by gluing algebraic tori along birational mutations. A cluster variety 𝒳 (the 𝒳-variety) and its Langlands dual 𝒜 (the 𝒜-variety) form a mirror pair. The derived category Dᵇ(Coh(𝒳)) admits a categorical action of the cluster modular group, generated by mutations at vertices of the quiver. For a quiver 𝑄 without loops or 2-cycles, the cluster category 𝒞_𝑄 = Dᵇ(mod-𝑘𝑄)/τ⁻¹[1] (where τ is the Auslander–Reiten translate) categorifies the cluster algebra: its cluster-tilting objects correspond to clusters, and mutations correspond to approximations. The derived category of the cluster variety Dᵇ(Coh(𝒳)) is related to the cluster category via a localization functor that inverts the frozen variables. The Fock–Goncharov duality conjecture states that the tropical points of 𝒳(ℤᵗ) parametrize a basis of the space of regular functions on 𝒜, and vice versa. This is categorified by the statement that the category of constructible sheaves on the tropicalization of 𝒳 is equivalent to the wrapped Fukaya category of 𝒜. For the cluster variety associated to a marked surface (𝑆, 𝑀), the derived category Dᵇ(Coh(𝒳)) is equivalent to the partially wrapped Fukaya category of the dual surface, and the cluster mutations correspond to Dehn twists. The upper cluster algebra 𝒰(𝒳) is the ring of regular functions on 𝒳, and its canonical basis (the theta basis) is constructed via scattering diagrams. The derived category Dᵇ(Coh(𝒳)) carries a natural t-structure whose heart is the category of coherent sheaves, and the cluster characters of Caldero–Chapoton compute the classes of cluster variables in the Grothendieck group.

Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Log Calabi–Yau Varieties and the Gross–Hacking–Keel Program

A log Calabi–Yau pair (𝑋, 𝐷) consists of a normal projective variety 𝑋 and a reduced divisor 𝐷 such that 𝐾_𝑋 + 𝐷 ∼ 0. The Gross–Hacking–Keel program constructs mirror families for log Calabi–Yau varieties using scattering diagrams and theta functions. The derived category Dᵇ(Coh(𝑋)) admits a semi-orthogonal decomposition into components associated to the strata of 𝐷. When 𝐷 is a simple normal crossings divisor, the derived category of the complement 𝑈 = 𝑋 𝐷 is equivalent to the category of modules over the wrapped Fukaya category of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model. The mirror of (𝑋, 𝐷) is a Landau–Ginzburg model (𝑌, 𝑊) where 𝑌 is a cluster variety and 𝑊 is a superpotential given by the sum of theta functions associated to the boundary divisors. The homological mirror symmetry conjecture for log Calabi–Yau varieties states that Dᵇ(Coh(𝑋)) is equivalent to the Fukaya–Seidel category FS(𝑌, 𝑊). For a del Pezzo surface 𝑋 with an anti-canonical divisor 𝐷, the mirror is (ℂ*)² with superpotential 𝑊 = ∑i=1^r 𝑧𝑚_i where 𝑚_i are the primitive generators of the fan of 𝑋. The derived category Dᵇ(Coh(𝑋)) has a full exceptional collection whose mutation graph is the cluster exchange graph of the mirror cluster algebra. The theta basis of the coordinate ring of the mirror cluster variety provides a canonical basis for the space of sections of line bundles on 𝑋, and the structure constants for multiplication in this basis are given by counts of tropical disks. The derived category of the log Calabi–Yau pair also controls the deformation theory: the Hochschild cohomology HH*(Dᵇ(Coh(𝑋))) computes the quantum cohomology of the mirror, and the Maurer–Cartan elements in the Hochschild complex correspond to deformations of the pair (𝑋, 𝐷).
