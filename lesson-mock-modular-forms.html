Mock Modular Forms — Ramanujan's Theta Functions, Harmonic Maass Forms & Quantum Modularity

Deep dive into Mock Modular Forms — classical modular forms for SL₂(ℤ) (the Eisenstein series E_k, the discriminant Δ = η(τ)²⁴, the j-invariant), Ramanujan's mock theta functions (the 17 original functions from his last letter to Hardy, 1920, with asymptotic expansions that mock modular behavior), harmonic Maass forms (Bruinier–Funke, 2004: non-holomorphic modular forms of weight k with eigenvalue (1−k/2) under the hyperbolic Laplacian Δ_k, whose holomorphic part is a mock modular form), Zwegers's completion (the breakthrough 2002 thesis showing that mock theta functions become harmonic Maass forms when corrected by a non-holomorphic Eichler integral), indefinite theta series, quantum modular forms (Zagier, 2010: functions defined on ℚ that transform modularly with a periodic error), Ramanujan's lost notebook identities, and connections to partitions (the Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic formula p(n) ∼ e^{π√(2n/3)}/(4√3 n)), moonshine (the Mathieu and Umbral moonshine conjectures relating mock modular forms to finite groups), and knot invariants (the colored Jones polynomial and the volume conjecture).

Part 1

🌙 Classical Modular Forms and Theta Series

Modular Forms
The Modular Group SL₂(ℤ), Eisenstein Series, and Classical Theta Functions
The modular group Γ = SL₂(ℤ) acts on the upper half-plane ℍ = {τ ∈ ℂ | Im(τ) > 0} by τ ↦ (aτ + b)/(cτ + d). A modular form of weight k ∈ ℤ for Γ is a holomorphic function f: ℍ → ℂ satisfying the transformation f((aτ + b)/(cτ + d)) = (cτ + d)^k f(τ) and holomorphicity at the cusp (the Fourier expansion f(τ) = Σ_{n≥0} a(n) q^n, q = e^{2πiτ}). The simplest examples: the Eisenstein series E_k(τ) = ½ Σ_{(m,n)≠(0,0)} (mτ + n)^{-k} for even k ≥ 4. Their Fourier expansions: E_k(τ) = 1 − (2k/B_k) Σ_{n≥1} σ_{k−1}(n) q^n where σ_r(n) = Σ_{d|n} d^r and B_k are Bernoulli numbers. The discriminant Δ(τ) = (E_4(τ)³ − E_6(τ)²)/1728 = q ∏_{n≥1} (1 − q^n)²⁴ = η(τ)²⁴ (Dedekind eta function). The j-invariant j(τ) = E_4(τ)³/Δ(τ) is a modular function (weight 0) giving a bijection between ℍ/Γ and ℂ. Classical theta functions: the Jacobi theta function θ(τ, z) = Σ_{n∈ℤ} (−1)^n q^{n²/2} e^{2πinz} transforms as a modular form in τ (weight ½) and a quasi-periodic function in z. The Hecke operators T_n act on the space M_k(Γ) of modular forms, commuting and being self-adjoint with respect to the Petersson inner product. The Eichler–Shimura theory relates modular forms to elliptic curves via the L-function L(f, s) = Σ a(n) n^{-s} — the modularity theorem (Taniyama–Shimura–Weil, proved by Wiles et al.: every elliptic curve over ℚ is modular).
Classical modular forms are automorphic functions on the upper half-plane whose Fourier coefficients encode deep arithmetic information, via Hecke operators and L-functions.

📝 Ramanujan's Mock Theta Functions

Mock Theta
Ramanujan's Last Letter, Asymptotic Expansions, and the Failure of Modularity
In his last letter to Hardy (1920), Ramanujan introduced 17 mock theta functions: series like f(q) = 1 + Σ_{n≥1} q^{n²}/((1+q)²(1+q²)²…(1+q^n)²), φ(q) = 1 + Σ_{n≥1} q^{n²}/((1+q²)(1+q⁴)…(1+q^{2n})), and ψ(q) = Σ_{n≥1} q^{n²}/((1−q)(1−q³)…(1−q^{2n−1})). These series have exponential singularities at roots of unity: as q → e^{2πir/s} radially, f(q) has asymptotics of the form (−1)^{s−1} e^{πi(1−2r)/24s} (4s/3)^{1/2} (πi/s)^{1/2} e^{π²/s(1−2r)} + O(1), involving a modular transformation of the asymptotic series — but the sum differs from any modular form. For decades they defied understanding: they are q-series with q-expansions like modular forms, but their theta-like behavior near roots of unity suggested a hidden modular completion. The Watson–Whipple transformation formulas gave identities relating mock theta functions to basic hypergeometric series ₂φ₁ and ₈φ₇. Andrews's combinatorial interpretations of mock theta functions in terms of partitions (e.g., the rank of a partition and the Andrews–Garvan crank). The mock theta conjectures (Andrews–Dyson–Hickerson, 1988) expressed certain mock theta functions as indefinite theta series, providing the first structural understanding.
Ramanujan's mock theta functions exhibit modular asymptotic behavior at roots of unity despite not being modular forms — they mock modularity.

✨ Harmonic Maass Forms and the Zwegers Completion

Completion
Zwegers's Thesis, Harmonic Maass Forms, and the Bruinier–Funke Theory
The key breakthrough came from Sander Zwegers's PhD thesis (Utrecht, 2002). A harmonic Maass form (Bruinier–Funke, 2004) of weight k ∈ ½ℤ for Γ is a smooth function F: ℍ → ℂ that transforms like a modular form of weight k, is annihilated by the weight-k hyperbolic Laplacian Δ_k = −y²(∂_x² + ∂_y²) + iky(∂_x + i∂_y), and has at most linear exponential growth at the cusps. A harmonic Maass form F decomposes as F = F⁺ + F⁻ where F⁺ is the holomorphic part (a mock modular form) and F⁻ is the non-holomorphic part (an Eichler integral of a cusp form). Specifically, F⁻(τ) = Σ_{n<0} b(n) Γ(1−k, 4π|n|y) q^n + constant, where Γ(s, x) is the incomplete Gamma function. Zwegers's completion: for Ramanujan's mock theta function f(q), define the shadow g(τ) = q^{-1/24} η(τ) (the Dedekind eta function). Then the completion ̂f(τ) = q^{-1/24}f(q) + i√3 ∫_{−τ̄}^{i∞} g(z) (−i(z+τ))^{-1/2} dz is a harmonic Maass form of weight ½. The shadow operator ξ_k = 2i y^k ∂/∂τ̄ maps harmonic Maass forms of weight k to cusp forms of weight 2−k. The kernel is the space of mock modular forms. The Borcherds lift (Borcherds, 1995) relates harmonic Maass forms to infinite products and the Griffiths–Zagier theory. Indefinite theta series (Zwegers): Σ_{n∈ℤ^r} (−1)^{Q(n)} q^{Q(n)} where Q is an indefinite quadratic form — these give mock modular forms whose shadows are unary theta series.
Mock modular forms are the holomorphic parts of harmonic Maass forms; the non-holomorphic correction restores modularity, exactly as Zwegers discovered.

🔗 Quantum Modular Forms and Moonshine

Quantum
Zagier's Quantum Modular Forms, Moonshine, and Partition Functions
Quantum modular forms (Zagier, 2010) are functions f: ℚ → ℂ (defined only at rational points) satisfying the modular transformation f(τ) − (cτ + d)^{-k} f((aτ + b)/(cτ + d)) = g(τ), where for each γ ∈ SL₂(ℤ), the error g(τ) extends to a real-analytic function on ℂ\ℙ¹(ℝ) with some continuity property. Ramanujan's mock theta functions, when restricted to roots of unity, give quantum modular forms. The Eichler integral ∫_τ^{i∞} f(z) (z−τ)^{-k} dz of a cusp form produces a quantum modular form. The Mathieu moonshine (Eguchi–Ooguri–Tachikawa, 2010): the elliptic genus of the K3 surface decomposes into characters of the Mathieu group M₂₄, with mock modular forms appearing as McKay–Thompson series. The Umbral moonshine (Cheng–Duncan–Harvey, 2012): for each of the 23 Niemeier lattices, there is a finite group and a vector-valued mock modular form whose coefficients encode group characters — a profound generalization of monstrous moonshine. The partition function p(n): the generating function Σ p(n) q^n = 1/∏_{n≥1} (1−q^n) = q^{1/24}/η(τ) is a modular form of weight −½ (essentially). The Hardy–Ramanujan circle method uses the modular transformation of η to derive p(n) ∼ e^{π√(2n/3)}/(4√3 n). Dyson's rank and the Andrews–Garvan crank give combinatorial interpretations of Ramanujan's congruences p(5k+4) ≡ 0 mod 5, p(7k+5) ≡ 0 mod 7, p(11k+6) ≡ 0 mod 11. The Kontsevich–Zagier period conjectures and WRT invariants of 3-manifolds (the volume conjecture relates the colored Jones polynomial of a knot to the hyperbolic volume of its complement via quantum modular forms).
Mock and quantum modular forms connect to moonshine (finite group theory), partition congruences, and invariants of 3-manifolds and knots.

Higher Weight Mock Modular Forms and Their Shadows

The theory of mock modular forms extends naturally beyond weight ½. Bruinier and Funke defined harmonic Maass forms of weight k ∈ ½ℤ for congruence subgroups, and the shadow operator ξ_k = 2i y^k ∂/∂τ̄ maps a harmonic Maass form of weight k to a cusp form of weight 2−k. For weight 3/2 the shadow is a weight ½ cusp form, often a unary theta series attached to a quadratic form of signature (1,1). The holomorphic part of a weight 3/2 harmonic Maass form is a mock modular form whose Fourier coefficients frequently encode arithmetic data such as Hurwitz class numbers or traces of singular moduli. Zagier’s work on the generating function of Hurwitz class numbers H(N) shows that ΣN≥0 H(N) q^N is the holomorphic part of a weight 3/2 harmonic Maass form whose shadow is the weight ½ theta series θ(τ) = Σn∈ℤ n qn². The completion adds a non‑holomorphic integral of the shadow, restoring full modular transformation under SL₂(ℤ). Similar phenomena appear for weight 5/2, where the shadow is a weight −½ cusp form, and for vector‑valued mock modular forms arising from Weil representations of the metaplectic group Mp₂(ℤ). The Borcherds lift takes a weight ½ harmonic Maass form with integral principal part and produces a meromorphic modular form whose divisor is prescribed by the coefficients of the mock part. This lift has been used to construct infinite product expansions for mock modular forms of higher weight, linking them to automorphic products on orthogonal groups of signature (2, n). In the context of moonshine, the McKay–Thompson series for elements of the Mathieu group M₂₄ are weight ½ mock modular forms whose shadows are eta‑quotients; their higher‑weight counterparts appear as twining genera of K3 sigma models. The classification of shadows for weights up to 5/2 is largely understood for level 1, and the space of mock modular forms of a given weight and level is finite‑dimensional, with dimension given by the dimension of the corresponding space of cusp forms plus the dimension of the space of weakly holomorphic modular forms of the dual weight.

Mock Modular Forms in Topological String Theory

Topological string theory on Calabi–Yau threefolds produces generating functions of BPS invariants that are mock modular forms. The topological string partition function Z_top(τ, z) depends on the Kähler modulus τ and an elliptic parameter z; its Fourier expansion in q = e2πiτ and y = e2πiz yields coefficients that count BPS states. For local Calabi–Yau geometries such as the resolved conifold, the partition function factorises into a product of a holomorphic mock modular form and a non‑holomorphic theta series, mirroring the Zwegers completion. The holomorphic part is a mock Jacobi form of weight 0 and index 1, whose shadow is a unary theta series of weight 1. The wall‑crossing formula of Kontsevich and Soibelman describes how BPS invariants change across walls of marginal stability; the discontinuity is captured by the non‑holomorphic completion of the mock modular form. In the work of Alexandrov, Pioline, and others, the Fourier coefficients of the mock part are identified with the integer invariants Ω(γ; z) that satisfy the wall‑crossing identity. The mock modular form thus encodes the “protected” part of the BPS spectrum, while the shadow records the contribution of multi‑centered black hole solutions that decay across walls. This physical interpretation provides a concrete mechanism for the appearance of mock modularity: the holomorphic anomaly equation of Bershadsky, Cecotti, Ooguri, and Vafa (BCOV) states that the τ̄‑derivative of the topological string amplitude is proportional to the shadow. The anomaly equation can be integrated to produce the non‑holomorphic completion, yielding a fully modular object. For compact Calabi–Yau threefolds, the genus‑zero free energy is a mock modular form of weight −2 whose shadow is the Yukawa coupling, a weight 2 modular form. Higher‑genus amplitudes are quasi‑modular forms expressible as polynomials in the Eisenstein series E₂ with coefficients that are mock modular forms. The interplay between mock modularity, wall‑crossing, and the holomorphic anomaly has become a central tool in the exact computation of topological string amplitudes and the microscopic entropy of supersymmetric black holes in string theory.

Technique: Identifying the Shadow of a Mock Modular Form
Given the weight 3/2 mock modular form M(τ) = Σn≥0 H(n) q^n where H(n) are Hurwitz class numbers, determine its shadow S(τ) and describe the completed harmonic Maass form ̂M(τ).
The shadow is obtained by applying the ξ3/2 operator: ξ3/2(M) = 2i y3/2 ∂M/∂τ̄. Since M is holomorphic, the derivative acts on the non‑holomorphic completion term. The standard result (Zagier) is that the shadow is the weight ½ unary theta series S(τ) = θ(τ) = Σn∈ℤ n qn². The completed harmonic Maass form is ̂M(τ) = M(τ) + (non‑holomorphic integral of θ). This integral converges and transforms as a weight 3/2 modular form. The holomorphic part M(τ) is the mock modular form; the integral term is the non‑holomorphic correction that restores modularity.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the shadow of a mock modular form, first identify the weight k of the mock part. The shadow always has weight 2−k and is a cusp form. Recall the standard shadows: weight ½ → weight 3/2 shadow is a unary theta series; weight 3/2 → weight ½ shadow is a theta series of a quadratic form; weight 5/2 → weight −½ shadow is an eta‑quotient. Memorise the ξ_k formula and the general shape of the integral completion; they appear frequently in problem sets.

Ramanujan's Lost Notebook and the Mock Theta Conjectures

The discovery of Ramanujan’s lost notebook in 1976 by George Andrews at Trinity College, Cambridge, revealed over one hundred pages of previously unknown q‑series identities. Among them were the final entries on mock theta functions of orders 3, 5, and 7, together with statements that these functions could be expressed as indefinite theta series. Andrews and Dragonette (1977) began the systematic study, and Hickerson (1988) proved the mock theta conjectures by constructing explicit indefinite theta series representations for all seventeen of Ramanujan’s mock theta functions. The conjectures asserted that each mock theta function f(q) can be written as a sum over a lattice of signature (1,1) with a sign factor (−1)Q(n) where Q is an indefinite quadratic form. For example, the order‑3 mock theta function admits an indefinite theta series representation after a suitable change of variables. These representations made the modular completion transparent: the indefinite theta series is the holomorphic part of a harmonic Maass form whose shadow is a unary theta series attached to the quadratic form Q. The lost notebook also contained transformation formulas for the mock theta functions under the modular group, anticipating Zwegers’s completion by more than two decades. The work of Andrews, Garvan, and others on the rank and crank of partitions provided combinatorial interpretations of the coefficients, linking the mock theta functions to the generating functions of partition ranks. The rank generating function R(z; q) = Σn,m N(m,n) z^m q^n is a mock Jacobi form of weight 1 and positive index, and its specialization at roots of unity yields the mock theta functions. The resolution of the mock theta conjectures thus unified three strands: q‑hypergeometric identities, indefinite theta series, and the theory of harmonic Maass forms.

The Role of Indefinite Theta Series in Mock Modular Forms

Indefinite theta series provide a concrete construction of mock modular forms. Zwegers (2002) showed that for any non‑degenerate quadratic form Q of signature (1, r−1) on ℤ^r, the series Θ_Q(τ) = Σn∈ℤ^r (−1)B(n,n₀) qQ(n)/2 where B is the associated bilinear form and n₀ a fixed vector, is a mock modular form of weight r/2. The sign factor (−1)B(n,n₀) implements a “partial” summation that renders the series conditionally convergent; the non‑holomorphic completion replaces the sign by the error function erf(√(π y) B(n,n₀)/√Q(n)) and yields a harmonic Maass form. The shadow of Θ_Q is the unary theta series attached to the negative definite subspace of Q. This construction recovers all of Ramanujan’s mock theta functions (r = 2) and produces higher‑rank examples such as the Appell–Lerch sums, which are mock Jacobi forms of weight 1 and index m. The indefinite theta series also appear in the work of Bringmann, Folsom, and Ono on the generating functions of ranks and cranks for partitions with restricted differences. In the context of quantum modular forms, the radial limits of indefinite theta series at rational points give quantum modular forms in the sense of Zagier, with the error term expressed as an Eichler integral of the shadow. The theory of indefinite theta series has been extended to vector‑valued forms by using the Weil representation of the metaplectic group; the resulting vector‑valued mock modular forms are the holomorphic parts of vector‑valued harmonic Maass forms whose shadows are vector‑valued unary theta series. This framework underlies the umbral moonshine correspondence, where for each Niemeier lattice the mock modular forms are built from indefinite theta series of signature (1, 23) attached to the root system of the lattice.

Applications to Black Hole Entropy and Wall‑Crossing

The microscopic entropy of supersymmetric black holes in string theory is computed by counting BPS states, and the generating functions of these degeneracies are often mock modular forms. The seminal work of Dijkgraaf, Verlinde, and Verlinde (1997) on the elliptic genus of the symmetric product orbifold of K3 showed that the generating function of 1/4‑BPS dyons in N=4 string theory is the inverse of the Igusa cusp form Φ₁₀, a Siegel modular form of weight 10. The Fourier coefficients of 1/Φ₁₀ are given by a Rademacher‑type expansion whose leading term is a mock modular form of weight −10. Later, Sen (2007) identified the mock modular form as the holomorphic part of a harmonic Maass form whose shadow is a weight 12 cusp form built from the discriminant Δ(τ). The wall‑crossing formula for 1/4‑BPS dyons across lines of marginal stability is precisely the statement that the mock modular form jumps by the addition of a non‑holomorphic Eichler integral of the shadow. This jump matches the physical expectation that multi‑centered black hole solutions appear or disappear as the moduli cross a wall. The Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula, expressed in terms of the quantum dilogarithm, can be rewritten as a transformation property of the completed harmonic Maass form. In the work of Cheng, Duncan, and Harvey on umbral moonshine, the mock modular forms attached to the 23 Niemeier lattices are conjectured to compute the BPS degeneracies of certain string compactifications on K3 × T², and their shadows encode the contributions of polar states that control the wall‑crossing behaviour. The Mathieu moonshine observation that the elliptic genus of K3 decomposes into characters of M₂₄ with mock modular coefficients further ties the representation theory of finite groups to the mock modular structure of black hole partition functions. These connections have turned mock modular forms into a central tool for exact microstate counting in quantum gravity.

Borcherds Products and Mock Modular Forms

The Borcherds lift constructs meromorphic modular forms on orthogonal groups of signature (2, n) from weakly holomorphic modular forms of weight 1−n/2. When the input form is the holomorphic part of a harmonic Maass form, the resulting Borcherds product acquires a denominator whose zeros and poles are supported on Heegner divisors determined by the shadow. The exponent of each divisor equals the Fourier coefficient of the mock modular form at the corresponding negative discriminant. This mechanism is closely related to generating functions of BPS invariants in string theory, such as the denominator formula of the fake monster Lie algebra, which exhibit mock modular properties. The lift also yields explicit infinite product expansions for the elliptic genus of K3 and for partition functions of 1/4‑BPS dyons in N=4 string theory. In these cases the shadow is a weight 12 cusp form built from the discriminant Δ(τ), and the wall‑crossing behaviour of the dyon spectrum is encoded in the non‑holomorphic completion of the mock modular input. The Borcherds product thus provides a geometric realisation of the Zwegers completion: the non‑holomorphic error term corresponds to the automorphic Green function attached to the Heegner divisor.

Mock Modular Forms for Non‑Congruence Subgroups

Classical modular forms are usually studied on congruence subgroups of SL₂(ℤ), but mock modular forms arise naturally on non‑congruence groups such as the Atkin–Swinnerton‑Dyer groups Γ₀(N)⁺ or the groups generated by τ ↦ τ+1 and τ ↦ −1/(Nτ). The theory of harmonic Maass forms extends to these groups because the ξₖ operator and the Laplacian are defined on any Fuchsian group of the first kind. However, the space of mock modular forms on a non‑congruence group can be infinite‑dimensional, unlike the finite‑dimensional space on congruence subgroups. This phenomenon is reflected in the existence of “mock Eisenstein series” whose shadows are weight 2−k cusp forms that do not satisfy the usual Atkin–Lehner relations. The Fourier coefficients of such forms often encode arithmetic data of non‑congruence modular curves, for example the dimensions of spaces of cusp forms on the corresponding Shimura curves. Recent work of Bringmann, Kane, and Kohnen indicates that the radial limits of these mock modular forms at rational points give quantum modular forms whose error terms are Eichler integrals of the non‑congruence shadows, providing a new class of quantum modular forms beyond Zagier’s original examples.

Worked Example: Shadow of a Weight 1/2 Mock Modular Form on Γ₀(4)
Let f(τ) = q⁻¹/²⁴ + 2 + 3q + … be the holomorphic part of a harmonic Maass form of weight 1/2 on Γ₀(4) with multiplier system χ. Compute the shadow g = ξ₁/₂(f) and verify that g is a weight 3/2 cusp form on the same group.
The ξₖ operator is ξₖ = 2i yᵏ ∂/∂τ̄. For weight 1/2, ξ₁/₂(f) = 2i y¹/² ∂f̄/∂τ̄. Since f = h + g* where h is holomorphic and g* is the non‑holomorphic completion, ∂f̄/∂τ̄ = ∂g*/∂τ̄. By construction g* = (−4π)⁻¹/² ∫−τ̄i∞ (τ̄+w)⁻¹/² g(w) dw, so ∂g*/∂τ̄ = (−4π)⁻¹/² (−τ̄+τ)⁻¹/² g(τ) = (−4π)⁻¹/² (2iy)⁻¹/² g(τ). Multiplying by 2i y¹/² gives ξ₁/₂(f) = g(τ). Hence the shadow is exactly the weight 3/2 cusp form g appearing in the completion. One checks that g transforms with the conjugate multiplier χ̄ and satisfies the cusp condition because the integral defining g* converges at all cusps of Γ₀(4).

Remember to keep track of the multiplier system: the shadow transforms with the complex conjugate multiplier.

p‑adic Mock Modular Forms and Iwasawa Theory

Just as classical modular forms admit p‑adic families via Hida theory, mock modular forms can be interpolated p‑adically when their shadows are ordinary at p. The construction uses the p‑adic ξₖ operator acting on overconvergent harmonic Maass forms, which are sections of the sheaf of p‑adic distributions on the modular curve. The resulting p‑adic mock modular forms satisfy a p‑adic modular transformation law with an error term given by a p‑adic Eichler integral of the shadow. These objects appear naturally in the study of p‑adic L‑functions of weight 1 modular forms: the derivative of a p‑adic L‑function at a critical point can be expressed as the value of a p‑adic mock modular form at a CM point. In Iwasawa theory, the characteristic ideal of the Selmer group of a weight 1 motive is conjecturally generated by the p‑adic mock modular form attached to the corresponding harmonic Maass form. Recent work of Guerzhoy, Kent, and Ono constructs p‑adic families of mock theta functions by varying the parameter r in Ramanujan’s original definitions, and shows that the p‑adic valuations of their Fourier coefficients satisfy congruences predicted by the Bloch–Kato conjecture.

Mock Jacobi Forms and Enumerative Geometry of Calabi–Yau Threefolds

Mock Jacobi forms are the Jacobi‑form analogues of mock modular forms: they are holomorphic in the elliptic variable z and transform like a Jacobi form of weight k and index m up to a non‑holomorphic correction involving the error function. Zwegers showed that the Appell–Lerch sums μ(u, v; τ) are mock Jacobi forms of weight 1 and index 1. In enumerative geometry, generating functions of refined BPS invariants of Calabi–Yau threefolds often take the form of mock Jacobi forms. For example, the refined Donaldson–Thomas invariants of the resolved conifold are encoded in a mock Jacobi form whose shadow is a weight 2 Jacobi cusp form built from the Weierstrass ℘‑function. The wall‑crossing formula for these invariants, derived from the Kontsevich–Soibelman product of quantum dilogarithms, translates precisely into the statement that the mock Jacobi form jumps by the addition of a non‑holomorphic Eichler integral of its shadow when the stability condition crosses a wall. This provides a rigorous mathematical framework for the physical expectation that the BPS spectrum changes discontinuously while the completed harmonic Maass form remains invariant.

Vector‑Valued Mock Modular Forms and the Weil Representation

Many problems in moonshine and string theory require mock modular forms that transform under a vector‑valued representation of the metaplectic double cover Mp₂(ℤ). The Weil representation attached to a finite quadratic module (discriminant form) provides such a representation. A vector‑valued harmonic Maass form is a function F: ℍ → ℂ[D] (where D is the discriminant group) satisfying Δₖ F = 0 and transforming with the Weil representation ρ_D. Its holomorphic part is a vector‑valued mock modular form, and its shadow is a vector‑valued cusp form of weight 2−k obtained by applying the component‑wise ξₖ operator. The theory of indefinite theta series of signature (1, n) yields explicit constructions of these objects: the completion replaces the sign function by the error function evaluated on the Lorentzian lattice, and the resulting vector‑valued mock modular forms are precisely the holomorphic parts of the completed forms. This construction underlies the umbral moonshine correspondence, where for each of the 23 Niemeier lattices the mock modular forms are built from indefinite theta series of signature (1, 23) attached to the root system. The shadows encode the contributions of polar states that control wall‑crossing in the associated string compactifications on K3 × T².

Higher Depth Mock Modular Forms and Iterated Integrals

The classical theory of mock modular forms deals with functions whose non‑holomorphic completion involves a single Eichler integral of a cusp form, called the shadow. Zagier introduced the notion of depth to capture mock modular forms whose completions require iterated integrals of several cusp forms. A mock modular form of depth d has a completion that is a real‑analytic modular form whose Laplacian image is a linear combination of products of d cusp forms. The holomorphic part then satisfies a system of differential equations involving the operators ξₖ and the raising operator. For depth two, the shadow is a pair of cusp forms (g₁, g₂) and the completion contains a double integral of their product. Work by Bringmann, Ono, and Rhoades gave explicit examples arising from generating functions of ranks and cranks of partitions, indicating that the rank generating function is a depth‑two mock modular form whose shadows are weight 3/2 cusp forms related to Cohen–Eisenstein series. The algebraic structure of higher depth forms is governed by multiple modular values and elliptic multiple zeta values that appear as periods of the iterated integrals. Work by Gangl, Kaneko, and Zagier connects depth‑two mock modular forms to double Eisenstein series and the theory of multiple modular forms for SL₂(ℤ). In practice, verifying modularity of a depth‑d candidate requires checking that the vector of its iterated derivatives transforms as a vector‑valued modular form under the Weil representation of the metaplectic group Mp₂(ℤ) of rank d.

Mock Hilbert Modular Forms over Real Quadratic Fields

Let F be a real quadratic field with discriminant D and ring of integers 𝒪_F. A Hilbert modular form of parallel weight k for the group SL₂(𝒪_F) is a holomorphic function on the product of two upper half‑planes ℍ² satisfying the usual transformation law under the action of SL₂(𝒪_F). The theory of harmonic Maass forms extends to this setting: a harmonic Hilbert–Maass form is a real‑analytic function on ℍ² annihilated by the product of the weight‑k hyperbolic Laplacians Δₖ,₁ Δₖ,₂ and transforming with a multiplier system. Its holomorphic part is a mock Hilbert modular form. The completion replaces the sign function in the Fourier expansion over totally positive elements of the different ideal 𝔡⁻¹ with an error function depending on the product of the two imaginary parts. The shadow is a Hilbert cusp form of parallel weight 2−k obtained by applying the component‑wise ξₖ operator. Work by Bruinier and Funke constructed a theta lift from harmonic Maass forms on SL₂(ℝ) to Hilbert modular forms on the orthogonal group O(2,2) associated to the quadratic space 𝒪_F ⊕ 𝒪_F with the norm form. This lift sends a mock modular form of weight k to a mock Hilbert modular form of parallel weight k, and the shadow corresponds under the Shimura correspondence. Explicit examples include mock Hilbert modular forms arising from generating functions of Heegner divisors on Hilbert modular surfaces, whose shadows are the weight 2 Hilbert cusp forms representing the cohomology classes of those divisors. The Fourier coefficients of mock Hilbert modular forms encode intersection numbers of Heegner cycles, providing a geometric interpretation analogous to the Borcherds product formula in the elliptic case.

Worked Example: Completion of a Mock Hilbert Modular Form
Let F = ℚ(√5) with discriminant 5. Suppose f(τ₁, τ₂) = ∑n₁,n₂ ≫ 0 a(n₁,n₂) q₁n₁ q₂n₂ is a mock Hilbert modular form of parallel weight 1/2 whose shadow is the Hilbert cusp form g(τ₁, τ₂) = ∑n₁,n₂ ≫ 0 b(n₁,n₂) q₁n₁ q₂n₂ of parallel weight 3/2. Write down the non‑holomorphic completion hat f and verify that ξ1/2,1 ξ1/2,2 hat f = g.
The completion takes the form hat f(τ₁, τ₂) = f(τ₁, τ₂) + C ∑n₁,n₂ ≫ 0 b(n₁,n₂) Γ(1/2, 4π y₁ n₁) Γ(1/2, 4π y₂ n₂) q₁-n₁ q₂-n₂, where Γ(1/2, t) = √π erfc(√t) and C is a normalizing constant. Applying ξ1/2,1 = 2i y₁1/2 ∂/∂τ̄₁ eliminates the holomorphic part and differentiates the incomplete gamma factors. A second application of ξ1/2,2 recovers the shadow g(τ₁, τ₂). Thus the completed form is a harmonic Hilbert–Maass form with the prescribed shadow.

When a question asks for the completion of a mock Hilbert modular form, remember that the error function depends on the product of the two imaginary parts. The shadow always has parallel weight 2−k, and the ξ‑operator acts component‑wise. Write the completion as a sum over totally positive integers in the different ideal, and use the derivative of the complementary error function to check the ξ‑image quickly.

Mock Modular Forms for Orthogonal Groups of Signature (2, n) and the Theta Correspondence

The Borcherds lift constructs meromorphic modular forms on orthogonal groups O(2, n) from vector‑valued mock modular forms of weight 1−n/2 for the Weil representation of Mp₂(ℤ) attached to an even lattice of signature (1, n−1). The input is a mock modular form whose shadow is a cusp form of weight 1+n/2. The theta kernel for the dual pair (SL₂, O(2, n)) is the Siegel theta series Θ_L(τ, z) = ∑λ∈L eπ i (λ,λ) τ + 2π i (λ, z) where L is an even lattice of signature (2, n) and z belongs to the associated symmetric domain. Integrating a harmonic Maass form F(τ) against Θ_L(τ, z) over the fundamental domain of SL₂(ℤ) yields a function Φ(z) on the orthogonal symmetric domain. The holomorphic part of F produces the Borcherds product expansion of Φ(z), while the non‑holomorphic part contributes a Green’s function that cancels the singularities along the Heegner divisors. Work by Harvey and Moore showed that the wall‑crossing behaviour of BPS states in string theory compactifications is governed by the jump of the mock modular form across walls of marginal stability, which corresponds to the change in the Borcherds product when the vector z crosses a Heegner divisor. The shadow of the mock modular form encodes the polar part of the Borcherds product, i.e. the divisor of the resulting meromorphic modular form. In the case n = 2, the orthogonal group is O(2,2) ≅ SL₂(ℝ) × SL₂(ℝ) and the construction reduces to the classical Shimura correspondence. For n > 2, vector‑valued mock modular forms are built from indefinite theta series of signature (1, n) as described by Zwegers and later in work on wall‑crossing in N=2 supergravity.

Mock Modular Forms in the Langlands Program: Automorphic Representations and the Theta Lift

The Langlands program predicts a correspondence between automorphic representations of reductive algebraic groups and Galois representations. Classical modular forms correspond to automorphic representations of GL₂(𝔸). Mock modular forms are not automorphic in this strict sense because they are not eigenfunctions of the Hecke algebra on the space of cusp forms; however, their completions are harmonic Maass forms, which are eigenfunctions of the hyperbolic Laplacian and transform under the metaplectic double cover Mp₂(𝔸). Work by Kudla and Millson constructed a theta lift from harmonic Maass forms on Mp₂(𝔸) to automorphic forms on orthogonal groups O(p, q) over totally real fields. This lift realizes the Shimura correspondence at the level of automorphic representations and provides a geometric interpretation of the shadow as a period integral of a cusp form on the orthogonal group. In the local Langlands correspondence for Mp₂(ℚ_p), the Weil representation decomposes into a direct sum of genuine representations, and mock modular forms correspond to certain non‑tempered representations whose Langlands parameters are induced from characters of the quadratic extension. Work by Gan, Gross, and Prasad on the theta correspondence for metaplectic groups shows that the space of harmonic Maass forms of weight k matches the space of automorphic forms on the orthogonal group O(2, n) with a prescribed infinitesimal character. This embedding allows one to transfer analytic properties of mock modular forms (e.g., the asymptotic behaviour of Fourier coefficients) to the spectral theory of automorphic forms on higher rank groups, and conversely to use Langlands functoriality to produce new mock modular forms from automorphic forms on orthogonal groups via the inverse theta lift.

Quantum Modular Forms on the Projective Line and Their Mock Counterparts

A quantum modular form in the sense of Zagier (2010) is a complex‑valued function f defined on ℚ ∪ {i∞} such that for every γ = (a b; c d) ∈ SL₂(ℤ) the error function h_γ(x) = f(x) − (cx+d)−k f(γ x) extends to an analytic function on an open subset of ℝ containing the orbit of x. The prototype is the Kontsevich–Zagier series F(q) = ∑n≥0 (q;q)_n, which converges for |q|<1 and defines a quantum modular form of weight 1/2 on the set of roots of unity. Work by Folsom, Ono, and Rhoades proved that the radial limits of the mock theta functions at rational points are quantum modular forms. Specifically, for Ramanujan’s mock theta function f(q) = ∑n≥0 qn²/(−q;q)_n², the function x ↦ limr→1⁻ f(e2π i x r) defined on ℚ is a quantum modular form of weight 1/2 whose error terms are given by Eichler integrals of the weight 3/2 shadow. The quantum modularity of mock theta functions is equivalent to the statement that their completions are harmonic Maass forms: the non‑holomorphic part provides the analytic continuation of the error function across the real line. In the framework of quantum modular forms on the projective line ℙ¹(ℚ), the group SL₂(ℤ) acts by Möbius transformations, and the space of quantum modular forms of weight k carries a representation of the group algebra of SL₂(ℤ). The map sending a harmonic Maass form to its boundary values on ℚ is a homomorphism of SL₂(ℤ)‑modules, and its kernel consists of the cusp forms. This perspective unifies the theory of mock modular forms, quantum modular forms, and the theory of periods of modular forms, and it underlies work on the asymptotics of quantum invariants of knots.

Mock Modular Forms and Modular Graph Functions

Modular graph functions arise in the low‑energy expansion of genus‑one string amplitudes and are defined as sums over lattice momenta of products of scalar Green functions on a torus. Their Fourier expansions involve non‑holomorphic Eisenstein series and, crucially, the non‑holomorphic completions of certain mock modular forms. A key example is the two‑loop modular graph function Ca,b,c(τ) which satisfies a Laplace equation on the upper half‑plane whose source term is a product of holomorphic Eisenstein series. When the source term is a mock modular form, the solution is a harmonic Maass form whose holomorphic part is a mock modular form and whose shadow is a cusp form of complementary weight. This mechanism was first exhibited for the D4 function, and later generalized to arbitrary weight. The key structural fact is that the space of modular graph functions is closed under the action of the hyperbolic Laplacian, and the mock modular sector appears precisely when the graph contains a sub‑graph whose momentum sum vanishes, producing a holomorphic anomaly. Examiners often test the ability to identify the shadow of a given mock modular form by writing the associated Laplace equation and reading off the source term. A common twist is to present a modular graph function with a non‑trivial automorphy factor and ask for the weight and multiplier system of its mock modular component.

The interplay extends to the theory of multiple modular values. The coefficients of the q‑expansion of a mock modular form that appears in a modular graph function are periods of mixed Hodge structures attached to the graph. These periods can be expressed as rational linear combinations of multiple zeta values (MZVs) and their elliptic generalizations. Recent work shows that the single‑valued projection of the elliptic multiple zeta values yields the Fourier coefficients of the mock modular part. Consequently, any exam question that asks for the transcendental weight of a coefficient can be answered by counting the loop order of the graph and the depth of the associated MZV. The depth of the mock modular form (in the sense of iterated integrals) is not the same as the weight of its shadow; this distinction is a frequent source of error.

From a computational perspective, the method of symbols and coproducts on the space of modular graph functions provides an algorithm to extract the mock modular component. One writes the symbol of the function, isolates the letters corresponding to the holomorphic Eisenstein series, and integrates them to obtain the mock modular form. The non‑holomorphic completion is then reconstructed by solving the inhomogeneous Laplace equation with the known shadow. This algorithm has been implemented in software packages such as ModularGraphFunctions for Mathematica. Candidates should be familiar with the steps: (1) compute the symbol, (2) identify the holomorphic letters, (3) integrate to obtain the mock modular form, (4) verify the shadow by applying the ξ‑operator. The examiner may provide a truncated symbol and ask to reconstruct the first few Fourier coefficients of the mock modular form.

Finally, the physical interpretation ties mock modular forms to the spectrum of BPS states in certain string compactifications. The generating function of BPS indices for D‑brane bound states on a Calabi–Yau threefold is a mock modular form whose shadow encodes the wall‑crossing formula of Kontsevich and Soibelman. The modular graph function computes the one‑loop correction to the effective action, and its mock modular part captures the discontinuity across walls of marginal stability. This connection explains why the mock modular forms appearing in string theory have integral Fourier coefficients and why their shadows are cusp forms with algebraic coefficients. A typical advanced question asks to derive the wall‑crossing discontinuity from the non‑holomorphic completion of a given mock modular form.

Mock Modular Forms and Resurgence Theory

Resurgence theory, developed by Écalle in the 1980s, provides a framework for relating the asymptotic expansion of a function to its non‑perturbative completions. Mock modular forms furnish a natural laboratory for resurgence because their Fourier coefficients exhibit factorial growth and their Borel transforms have singularities on the positive real axis. Seminal work in resurgence theory showed that the asymptotic expansion of the mock theta function f(q) = ∑n≥0 qn²/(−q;q)n² as q → 1⁻ is a divergent series whose Borel sum reproduces the non‑holomorphic completion of the mock modular form. The singularities of the Borel transform correspond precisely to the poles of the shadow, and the Stokes automorphism across each singularity is given by the period integral of the shadow. This establishes a one‑to‑one correspondence between the alien derivatives of the asymptotic series and the Fourier coefficients of the shadow.

The mechanism can be described concretely. Let h(τ) be a harmonic Maass form of weight k with holomorphic part M(τ) (the mock modular form) and shadow g(τ) (a cusp form of weight 2−k). The asymptotic expansion of M(τ) as Im τ → 0⁺ is a formal power series in q = e2πiτ. Its Borel transform B[M](ζ) converges for |ζ| < 2π and admits analytic continuation to the complex ζ‑plane with simple poles at ζ = 2πn for n ∈ ℕ. The residue at ζ = 2πn is proportional to the n‑th Fourier coefficient of the shadow g. The alien derivative Δ2πn acting on the asymptotic series yields the same coefficient, confirming the resurgence relation. This structure is not limited to weight 1/2; it holds for any weight where the ξ‑operator maps harmonic Maass forms to cusp forms.

An important subtlety is the role of the depth of the mock modular form. Higher‑depth mock modular forms (those obtained by iterated integrals of modular forms) have asymptotic expansions whose Borel transforms exhibit higher‑order poles and branch cuts. The alien calculus then involves iterated alien derivatives, and the Stokes data are organized by the motivic Galois group of mixed elliptic motives. Recent extensions of the resurgence analysis to depth‑two mock modular forms arising from the Rankin–Cohen brackets of weight‑1/2 mock theta functions showed that the double alien derivative corresponds to the convolution of two shadows, a fact that can be tested by asking the candidate to compute the first few terms of the double alien derivative from the known Fourier expansions.

From an examination perspective, the typical trap is to confuse the Borel plane singularities of the mock modular form with those of its shadow. The shadow is a genuine modular form, its asymptotic expansion is convergent, and its Borel transform has no singularities on the positive real axis. Only the mock modular part produces the divergent series. Another common error is to assume that the Stokes constants are always rational; in fact they are periods of the underlying mixed Hodge structure and can be transcendental (e.g., values of the Riemann zeta function at odd integers). A well‑prepared candidate should be able to write the Borel transform of a given mock theta function, locate its first three singularities, and express the corresponding alien derivatives in terms of the shadow’s Fourier coefficients.

Worked Example: Alien Derivative of a Mock Theta Function
Let f(q) = ∑n≥0 qn²/(−q;q)n² be the mock theta function of order three. Its shadow is the weight‑3/2 cusp form g(τ) = η(τ)³ where η is the Dedekind eta function. Compute the first alien derivative Δ2π of the asymptotic expansion of f(e−t) as t → 0⁺.
The asymptotic expansion of f(e−t) is a divergent series ∑k≥0 ak tk−1/2. Its Borel transform has a simple pole at ζ = 2π with residue equal to the first Fourier coefficient of the shadow g. Since g(τ) = η(τ)³ = q1/8 ∏n≥1 (1−qn)³, the coefficient of q1/8 is 1. Therefore the residue is 1/(2π). The alien derivative Δ2π extracts this residue, giving Δ2π f = 1/(2π) times the formal monomial t−1/2. In terms of the original variable q, this corresponds to adding the term (1/(2π)) q1/8 to the non‑holomorphic completion, which matches the known completion of the mock theta function.

When a question asks for the alien derivative or Borel singularities of a mock modular form, first identify the shadow using the ξ‑operator. Write down the first few Fourier coefficients of the shadow; these are the residues at the Borel poles. The alien derivative is then a linear combination of these residues multiplied by the appropriate power of the asymptotic variable. Familiarise yourself with standard shadows (e.g., η³ for the order‑three mock theta function) to avoid recomputing the ξ‑operator under time pressure.

Mock Modular Forms and Vertex Operator Algebras

Vertex operator algebras (VOAs) provide an algebraic framework for two‑dimensional conformal field theories. The graded dimension (or character) of a module over a VOA is a function on the upper half‑plane that often transforms as a (vector‑valued) modular form. For many VOAs of CFT type, the characters of irreducible modules are not modular but mock modular; their completions are harmonic Maass forms whose shadows are characters of a related VOA. The first rigorous instance is the singlet VOA M(1) at central charge c = −2, studied by Adamović and Milas. Its irreducible characters are mock modular forms of weight 1/2, and their shadows are the characters of the triplet VOA W(1) which is a logarithmic extension. The ξ‑operator maps the completed characters of M(1) to the characters of W(1), realizing the shadow as a genuine modular form.

The mechanism generalises to the family of N = 2 superconformal VOAs at central charge c = 3(1−2/k) for integer k ≥ 2. The characters of the massive irreducible modules are mock modular forms of weight 0, and their shadows are the characters of the massless modules, which are modular forms of weight 2. This was proved by Eguchi, Hikami, and collaborators using the spectral flow isomorphism and the modular properties of the Appell–Lerch sums that appear in the characters. The Appell–Lerch sum is a classical example of a mock modular form; its completion involves the error function and its shadow is a unary theta series. In the VOA context, the Appell–Lerch sum arises from the sum over spectral flow sectors, and the non‑holomorphic completion corresponds to the contribution of the continuous spectrum in the logarithmic theory.

An important structural result is the correspondence between the category of modules of a VOA and the category of representations of the associated quantum group at a root of unity. For the singlet VOA, the quantum group is Uq(sl₂) at q = i. The mock modular characters correspond to the non‑semisimple projective covers, while the shadows correspond to the simple modules. This categorical equivalence explains why the space of mock modular forms is closed under the action of the modular group only after completion: the non‑holomorphic part encodes the extension data between projective and simple objects. Examiners may ask to identify the shadow of a given VOA character by recognising the underlying quantum group and its representation theory.

From a computational angle, the characters are often expressed as q‑series with product expansions. For example, the vacuum character of the singlet VOA is χ0(τ) = q−1/24 ∏n≥1 (1−qn)−1 ∑n∈ℤ (−1)n qn(3n−1)/2. The sum is a false theta series, which is a mock modular form of weight 1/2. Its completion adds an integral of a unary theta series, and the shadow is the weight‑3/2 unary theta series ∑n∈ℤ n qn². A typical exam problem provides the product‑sum expression and asks to compute the first three Fourier coefficients of the shadow by applying the ξ‑operator termwise. The key step is to differentiate the error function factor and use the identity ξk(Ek) = 0 for non‑holomorphic Eisenstein series.

Mock Modular Forms and Quantum Invariants of Three‑Manifolds

The Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev (WRT) invariant Zk(M) of a closed oriented three‑manifold M at level k is a quantum invariant defined via the representation theory of the quantum group Uq(sl₂) at q = e2πi/(k+2). For manifolds that are mapping tori of surface homeomorphisms, the WRT invariant can be expressed as a sum over SU(2) flat connections of a product of a mock modular form and a phase factor. This was discovered by Hikami for the figure‑eight knot complement and later extended by Gukov, Pei, Putrov, and Vafa to a large class of plumbed three‑manifolds. The mock modular form that appears is the homological block Ẑa(q), a q‑series with integer coefficients that converges for |q| < 1 and whose radial limits at roots of unity reproduce the WRT invariants. The homological blocks are mock modular forms of weight 1/2 (or vector‑valued of weight 3/2) whose shadows are given by the linking form on the first homology of the manifold.

The construction proceeds by writing the partition function of a three‑dimensional N = 2 supersymmetric theory on M × S¹ as a sum over boundary conditions labelled by a ∈ H¹(M; ℤ). Each block Ẑa(q) is the generating function of BPS states in the theory, and its mock modularity follows from the wall‑crossing formula for the BPS indices. The shadow is a unary theta series associated to the quadratic refinement of the linking form. Concretely, if the linking form is represented by a symmetric integer matrix Q, the shadow is ∑x∈ℤb (x·Qx) qx·Qx/2 where b = rank H₁(M; ℤ). The non‑holomorphic completion of Ẑa involves the error function of the square root of the quadratic form, exactly as in the classical Appell–Lerch sum.

A crucial feature is the quantum modularity of the homological blocks. The function Ẑa(q) defined on the unit disk extends to a quantum modular form on ℚ ∪ {i∞} in the sense of Zagier. The error term for the modular transformation τ ↦ −1/τ is an Eichler integral of the shadow, which is a period of the associated cusp form. This quantum modular property is equivalent to the statement that the radial limits of Ẑa at roots of unity give the WRT invariants, and it provides a bridge between the analytic theory of mock modular forms and the topological quantum field theory of three‑manifolds. Exam questions often present a plumbing graph and ask to write down the linking matrix, construct the associated quadratic form, and identify the first few terms of the homological block and its shadow.

Another direction is the relation to the 3d‑3d correspondence, which equates the partition function of a three‑dimensional theory on M with the partition function of a five‑dimensional theory on M × ℝ². The mock modular forms appear as the vortex partition functions on ℝ² × S¹, and their shadows encode the contribution of the monopole operators. The work of Dimofte, Gaiotto, and Gukov shows that the mock modular forms satisfy a set of difference equations (the quantum A‑polynomial) whose classical limit is the A‑polynomial of the knot. This difference equation can be used to compute the homological blocks recursively, a technique that is frequently tested in advanced problem sets.

Mock Modular Forms and the Theory of Umbral Moonshine

Umbral moonshine extends monstrous moonshine by attaching a family of mock modular forms to each of the 23 Niemeier lattices. For a Niemeier lattice with root system X, the associated mock modular form H_X(τ) has weight 1/2; its shadow is a unary theta series of weight 3/2 determined by the discriminant form. The coefficients of H_X encode dimensions of modules for the umbral group G_X, a finite subgroup of the lattice automorphism group. The construction uses the theta lift of a vector‑valued mock modular form for the Weil representation of SL₂(ℤ) attached to the discriminant form. A key computational tool is the Rademacher‑type series for the mock modular form, which converges rapidly and allows extraction of Fourier coefficients to high precision. Problems may ask for the first few Fourier coefficients of H_X given the root system X, or to verify that the ξ‑operator applied to the completion yields the expected shadow. The proof of the umbral moonshine conjectures by Cheng, Duncan, and Harvey uses Borcherds products for the relevant orthogonal groups and the classification of Niemeier lattices. Understanding the interplay between the lattice discriminant form, the Weil representation, and the mock modular completion is essential for computing the explicit shadow or verifying the mock modular transformation law under the generators S and T of SL₂(ℤ).

Mock Modular Forms and the Theory of Quantum Black Hole Microstates

The microscopic degeneracy of supersymmetric black holes in string theory is captured by Fourier coefficients of certain mock modular forms. For a class of N=4, d=4 black holes with electric and magnetic charges (Q,P), the generating function of BPS indices is a meromorphic Jacobi form whose polar part yields a mock modular form after applying the ξ‑operator. The completion involves an integral of a Siegel theta series of weight 3/2, and the shadow is a unary theta series built from the quadratic refinement of the charge lattice. The wall‑crossing formula of Kontsevich and Soibelman translates into the statement that the mock modular form jumps by a sum of products of theta series when moduli cross a wall of marginal stability. A typical problem may present a charge lattice with intersection matrix Q and ask to compute the first five Fourier coefficients of the mock modular form by expanding the Appell‑Lerch sum and applying the error‑function completion. Key steps: (i) write the indefinite theta series as a sum over a cone in the lattice, (ii) replace the sign function by the error function to obtain the non‑holomorphic completion, (iii) differentiate with respect to the non‑holomorphic variable to extract the shadow, and (iv) verify that the shadow matches the expected unary theta series. A further step may ask for the change in the mock modular form across a wall, which requires evaluating the Kontsevich‑Soibelman product of BPS indices and expressing the result as a sum of theta series with coefficients given by the Donaldson‑Thomas invariants of the quiver associated to the charge lattice.

Differentiate termwise: ∂bar τ E((m+1/2)√{2 Im τ}) = - (m+1/2) √{2/ Im τ} e-π (m+1/2)2 2 Im τ. Multiplying by 2i Im(τ)1/2 gives -2i (m+1/2) √2 e-2π (m+1/2)2 Im τ. Mock Modular Forms and the Theory of p‑adic Mock Modular Forms and Iwasawa Theory

p‑adic mock modular forms arise by interpolating the Fourier coefficients of a classical mock modular form along a Hida family. Let f(τ) = ∑n≥0 a(n) q^n be a mock modular form of weight k with shadow g(τ) = ∑n≥1 b(n) q^n. For a prime p where the coefficients are p‑integral, one constructs a p‑adic measure μ_f on ℤ_p^× such that ∫ℤ_p^× χ(x) xj dμ_f(x) = L(f, χ, j+1) for Dirichlet characters χ of p‑power conductor and integers j in the critical range. The shadow is a cusp form of weight 2−k on the orthogonal group, obtained by applying the ξ‑operator to the lifted form. The global theta lift of a mock modular form from SL₂ to a higher rank group G produces an automorphic representation whose local components are described by the local theta correspondence. Mock Modular Forms in Partition Theory: Unveiling Hidden Structures

Ramanujan's discovery of mock theta functions was deeply intertwined with partition theory, the study of representing integers as sums of positive integers. While classical modular forms yield exact formulas and congruences for the partition function p(n), mock theta functions arise naturally when studying finer partition statistics. The generating function for p(n) is essentially the reciprocal of the Dedekind eta function, a modular form of weight -1/2. However, generating functions for statistics like the rank or crank of a partition lead to mock modular forms.

The rank of a partition is its largest part minus the number of parts; its generating function is closely related to mock theta functions. The crank, conjectured by Dyson and constructed by Andrews and Garvan, provides combinatorial explanations for Ramanujan's congruences (e.g., p(5n+4) ≡ 0 (mod 5)). The generating functions for crank statistics are mock modular forms. Specifically, the generating function for the number of partitions of n with crank m, denoted M(m,n), can be expressed using mock theta functions. These functions "complete" certain q-series identities, supplying the modularity properties necessary for deeper number-theoretic analysis.

For instance, Ramanujan's third-order mock theta function f(q) = ∑n=0∞ qn² / (-q;q)n² is central to understanding these statistics. Their completion to harmonic Maass forms reveals shadows that are unary theta series of weight 3/2. The non-holomorphic part of this completion provides the "correction term" restoring modularity. This interplay allows exact formulas for partition ranks and cranks, crucial for proving congruences and analyzing the distribution of these statistics. Mock modular forms thus extend the reach of classical modular forms to capture phenomena that are "almost" modular but require non-holomorphic completion.

Constructing Harmonic Maass Forms: Beyond Zwegers' Completion

Zwegers' work established how to complete Ramanujan's mock theta functions into harmonic Maass forms. For general weights and characters, systematic constructions often rely on Poincaré series. These series, fundamental in modular form theory, build forms with prescribed Fourier coefficients; for harmonic Maass forms, the construction accommodates a non-holomorphic component.

A harmonic Maass form F(τ) of weight k for a congruence subgroup Γ is a real-analytic function on the upper half-plane satisfying: (i) annihilation by the weight-k hyperbolic Laplacian Δk = -y²(∂²/∂x² + ∂²/∂y²) + iky(∂/∂x + i∂/∂y), and (ii) at most polynomial growth at cusps. The key to construction via Poincaré series is specifying a "shadow" g(τ), a classical cusp form of weight 2-k. The non-holomorphic part of F(τ) is then an integral transform of g(τ).

One defines a Poincaré series by summing over cosets of Γ. For a shadow g(τ) = ∑n=1∞ c(n)qn, the non-holomorphic part involves the incomplete Gamma function Γ(1-k, 4πny). The holomorphic part is determined by the condition ΔkF = 0 and growth conditions. This construction proves the existence of harmonic Maass forms with prescribed shadows and facilitates the study of their Fourier coefficients, which encode deep arithmetic information.

Many errors arise from misapplying integral transforms or series expansions specific to certain weights. Hecke Operators on Mock Modular Forms: Unraveling Arithmetic Properties

Hecke operators are linear operators on spaces of modular forms, fundamental for understanding arithmetic properties like the multiplicativity of Fourier coefficients. Extending their action to mock modular forms (harmonic Maass forms) is crucial for developing their arithmetic theory. For a harmonic Maass form F(τ) of weight k, the Hecke operator Tp (p prime) acts on F(τ) compatibly with the classical action.

A harmonic Maass form decomposes as F(τ) = F+(τ) + F-(τ). The action of Tp on F(τ) yields a new harmonic Maass form (TpF)(τ) whose Fourier coefficients follow standard Hecke relations. Crucially, Hecke operators commute with the hyperbolic Laplacian Δk, preserving the space of harmonic Maass forms.

The action of Tp on the shadow g(τ) is the classical action on a cusp form. If g(τ) is a Hecke eigenform, its coefficients are multiplicative. The challenge lies in translating this to the holomorphic and non-holomorphic parts of F(τ). Hecke operators preserve the decomposition up to a specific transformation: if F has shadow g, then TpF has shadow Tpg. This compatibility allows the application of classical modular form techniques to study congruences and coefficient distributions of mock modular forms.

Mock Modular Forms and the Theory of Special L-values

The theory of L-functions is central to modern number theory, encoding deep arithmetic information. Special values at integer points often relate to fundamental invariants like class numbers or periods. Mock modular forms provide a framework for understanding certain special L-values, particularly those arising from "anomalous" or non-holomorphic settings.

For a classical modular form f(τ) of weight k, its L-function L(f, s) has critical values proportional to periods. For a mock modular form F(τ) of weight k with shadow g(τ) of weight 2-k, the associated L-function is typically defined as L(g, s). The non-holomorphic part of F(τ) acts as a regularization, enabling the study of these L-functions in a broader context.

Connections arise through the Rankin-Selberg method. Adaptations involving mock modular forms yield L-functions with interesting properties. For instance, special values of L-functions of half-integer weight forms (often shadows of mock modular forms) relate to coefficients of mock theta functions. This is relevant to the Bloch-Kato conjecture, where special L-values are linked to arithmetic invariants of motives. Mock modular forms, as "completed" objects, help interpret these values, especially when regularization is needed.

Furthermore, quantum modular forms—closely related to mock modular forms—provide a framework where the modularity "error term" is a specific function, often an Eichler integral of the shadow. This error term can be interpreted as a special value or period integral, linking mock modularity to the arithmetic of L-functions. This area actively bridges analytic number theory, algebraic geometry, and automorphic forms.

Foundations: Mock Modular Forms and Harmonic Maass Forms

The theory of mock modular forms centers on the decomposition of a harmonic Maass form into a holomorphic part (the mock modular form) and a non‑holomorphic completion. The completion is constructed via an Eichler integral of the shadow, a cusp form of complementary weight 2−k. This completion is not unique; different choices of the non‑holomorphic kernel yield distinct harmonic Maass forms with the same holomorphic part. The ambiguity is measured by period integrals of the shadow, which relate to special values of the associated L‑function. The space of completions carries an action of the Hecke algebra, and the resulting Hecke‑equivariant structure governs congruences between Fourier coefficients of the mock modular form and its shadow. The framework extends to vector‑valued mock modular forms transforming under the Weil representation of a lattice of signature (1, n), where the non‑holomorphic completion involves the incomplete gamma function and the resulting forms satisfy differential equations generalizing the classical heat equation. These equations play a role in establishing modularity of certain generating functions in topological string theory.

Mock Jacobi Forms, Wall‑Crossing, and the Holomorphic Anomaly

A significant development connects mock modular forms to meromorphic Jacobi forms. The Zwegers completion of a mock Jacobi form yields a real‑analytic Jacobi form whose non‑holomorphic part integrates a theta series against the complementary error function. The resulting object transforms with a multiplier system that encodes wall‑crossing behavior of BPS invariants in N=2 supersymmetric gauge theories. The mock modular form attached to a charge vector captures the discontinuity of indexed degeneracies across walls of marginal stability. The Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula can be reformulated in terms of the modularity of a generating function built from mock modular forms. This perspective has informed new approaches to the wall‑crossing formula for quivers with superpotential and to computing refined BPS invariants via the holomorphic anomaly equation. The holomorphic anomaly equation relates the antiholomorphic derivative of the mock modular form to products of lower‑weight mock modular forms; solving it recursively determines the non‑holomorphic completion uniquely up to the addition of a classical modular form.

Worked Example: Hecke Action on a Weight 1/2 Mock Modular Form (Illustrative)
Let F(τ) be the holomorphic part of a harmonic Maass form of weight 1/2 on Γ₀(4) with shadow g(τ) = η(τ)³. Compute the effect of the Hecke operator T₃ on F, given that g is a Hecke eigenform with T₃ g = 4 g.
The Hecke operator T₃ acts on the completed harmonic Maass form F = F⁺ + F⁻ componentwise. The shadow of T₃ F is T₃ g = 4 g. The holomorphic part (T₃ F)⁺ is obtained by applying the classical Hecke operator to the Fourier expansion of F⁺ and adding the contribution from the non‑holomorphic completion. For weight k=1/2, the classical Hecke operator involves a factor 3k-1 = 3-1/2 and a sum over matrices with determinant 3. The non‑holomorphic part adjusts the constant term and principal part to ensure the completed form is an eigenfunction of the hyperbolic Laplacian with eigenvalue λ = k(1−k)/4 = 1/16. The key principle: the shadow transforms classically, while the holomorphic part receives a correction from the completion.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the effect of a Hecke operator on a mock modular form, first identify the weight and the shadow. Write down the classical Hecke action on the shadow; the shadow of the Hecke‑transformed mock form is exactly the Hecke transform of the original shadow. Then apply the classical Hecke operator to the holomorphic part, remembering the weight‑dependent factor pk-1. Finally, note that the non‑holomorphic completion contributes only to the constant term and to the coefficients of the principal part; it does not alter the shadow. This stepwise approach avoids the common mistake of treating the Hecke operator as acting only on the holomorphic part.

Mock Modular Forms on Non‑Congruence Subgroups

The theory extends to non‑congruence subgroups, where the space of mock modular forms is infinite‑dimensional and the classical Hecke theory does not apply directly. One approach defines mock modular forms of level N by requiring the shadow to be a cusp form on the congruence closure of the subgroup. The completion then involves an integral over the fundamental domain of the non‑congruence subgroup, yielding a non‑holomorphic part expressed as a sum over coset representatives rather than a simple Eichler integral. This construction has produced mock modular forms whose Fourier coefficients encode arithmetic invariants of associated modular curves, such as dimensions of spaces of modular forms of fractional weight. Generating functions of these dimensions are themselves mock modular forms of weight 1/2, with shadows given by certain eta‑quotients. The analysis uses harmonic Maass forms on the universal covering of the modular curve and the Selberg trace formula for the hyperbolic Laplacian.

p‑Adic Mock Modular Forms and L‑Value Interpolation

p‑Adic mock modular forms are p‑adic analytic functions on the upper half‑plane satisfying a mock modular transformation law with respect to a p‑adic analytic family of matrices. The p‑adic completion replaces the incomplete gamma function with the p‑adic gamma function and integrates against a p‑adic measure on the boundary. The resulting p‑adic harmonic Maass forms satisfy a p‑adic differential equation analogous to the classical heat equation. Their Fourier coefficients are p‑adic numbers that interpolate special values of the complex L‑function of the shadow. This interpolation underlies the p‑adic Birch–Swinnerton‑Dyer conjecture for modular abelian varieties. The p‑adic mock modular form attached to a Hida family of ordinary modular forms provides a p‑adic analytic continuation of the algebraic part of the L‑values; its shadow is the p‑adic modular form obtained via the p‑adic theta operator. The construction relies on overconvergent modular forms and the Coleman–Mazur eigencurve.

The advanced theory of mock modular forms unifies several directions: the Hecke theory of harmonic Maass forms, the wall‑crossing structure of BPS invariants, the geometry of non‑congruence subgroups, and the p‑adic interpolation of L‑values. Mastery requires fluency with the incomplete gamma function, the Weil representation, the theory of Jacobi forms, and basics of p‑adic analysis. Examiners often test the ability to translate between the combinatorial definition of a mock theta function and its analytic completion, to compute the action of Hecke operators on both parts, and to recognize the shadow from transformation properties.

Mock Modular Forms and Real Analytic Eisenstein Series

The theory of mock modular forms whose shadows are real analytic Eisenstein series was developed by Bringmann, Ono, and others. This work showed that the non‑holomorphic completion of certain mock modular forms can be expressed as an integral of a real analytic Eisenstein series against the incomplete gamma function. The construction yields harmonic Maass forms of weight k whose shadows are the classical Eisenstein series of weight 2−k, providing a bridge between the combinatorial world of mock theta functions and the spectral theory of the hyperbolic Laplacian.

In this setting the holomorphic part of the mock modular form is a q‑series with coefficients that often count partitions with prescribed congruence conditions. The real analytic Eisenstein series contributes a non‑holomorphic term involving the incomplete gamma function Γ(1−k, 4πny). The modular transformation of the completed form follows from the well‑known transformation of the Eisenstein series together with the functional equation of the gamma function. This mechanism explains why the shadow of a mock modular form of weight k is a modular form of weight 2−k, a fact that is sometimes obscured in the classical theta‑series approach.

Applications of this theory include the derivation of exact formulas for the Fourier coefficients of mock modular forms via the circle method, and the proof of congruences for partition functions that were previously inaccessible. The method also extends to higher level Eisenstein series, where the shadow becomes a linear combination of Eisenstein series attached to Dirichlet characters. In each case the completion remains a harmonic Maass form, and the Hecke operators act compatibly on both the holomorphic part and the Eisenstein shadow.

Mock Modular Forms in the Geometric Langlands Program

The geometric Langlands program predicts a correspondence between D‑modules on the moduli stack of G‑bundles on a curve and coherent sheaves on the moduli stack of Langlands dual local systems. In the case G = GL(2) this correspondence can be made explicit using the theory of mock modular forms. Work by Ben‑Zvi, Sakuma, and others identifies certain mock modular forms as sections of natural line bundles on the Hitchin moduli space, where the shadow corresponds to the spectral data of a Hecke eigensheaf.

Concretely, a mock modular form of weight 1/2 can be realized as a generating function of dimensions of spaces of conformal blocks for the affine Lie algebra sl̂₂ at a suitable level. The non‑holomorphic completion then encodes the wall‑crossing behaviour of these conformal blocks as the stability condition varies. This geometric interpretation suggests the appearance of the incomplete gamma function: it arises from the integration over the non‑compact directions in the Hitchin fibration. The resulting harmonic Maass form satisfies a system of differential equations that match the quantum differential equations of the associated quantum curve.

The geometric Langlands perspective also illuminates the role of the multiplier system. In the classical theory the multiplier is a character of the metaplectic cover of SL₂(ℤ). In the geometric setting the multiplier emerges from the monodromy of the flat connection on the moduli space, linking the arithmetic of mock modular forms to the topology of the underlying curve. This connection has been applied to prove new instances of the mock modular conjectures for higher rank groups by translating them into statements about the cohomology of moduli stacks.

Mock Modular Forms for Higher Rank Groups

Beyond SL₂, mock modular forms have been constructed for symplectic groups Sp(2n) and exceptional groups such as G₂. Foundational work by Raum and subsequent work by Westerholt‑Raum and others introduced vector‑valued mock modular forms transforming under the Weil representation of the metaplectic cover of Sp(2n, ℤ). The shadows in this setting are vector‑valued cusp forms of weight 2−k, and the completion involves the incomplete gamma function attached to each component of the vector.

A key technical tool is the theta lift from orthogonal groups of signature (2, n) to Sp(2n). The lift of a harmonic Maass form on the orthogonal group produces a mock modular form on the symplectic group with shadow given by the theta lift of the original shadow. This construction generalizes the classical Saito‑Kurokawa lift and explains many of the arithmetic properties of the Fourier coefficients, such as their relation to central L‑values of standard L‑functions.

For exceptional groups the theory is less developed; progress by Pollack and others uses modular forms on the exceptional domain of type E₇. The mock modular forms that appear are attached to certain nilpotent orbits, and their shadows are residues of Eisenstein series on the exceptional group. These objects are expected to play a role in the geometric Langlands program for exceptional groups and in the study of BPS invariants in string theory compactifications on Calabi–Yau threefolds with exceptional holonomy.

Mock Modular Forms and Quantum Modular Forms on Knot Complements

Quantum modular forms, introduced by Zagier, are functions on the rational projective line that exhibit modular transformation properties up to an error term which is a smooth function. For knot complements, the colored Jones polynomial evaluated at roots of unity gives rise to quantum modular forms. Gukov, Pei, Putrov, and Vafa showed that the radial limits of these quantum modular forms are mock modular forms, with the shadow encoding the asymptotic expansion of the Chern‑Simons partition function.

Concretely, let J_N(q) be the N‑colored Jones polynomial of a knot K. The function φ_K(τ) = limN→∞ J_N(e2πiτ) (after suitable normalization) gives a quantum modular form on ℚ. Its radial limit as τ approaches a rational number from the upper half‑plane yields a mock modular form M_K(τ) of weight 1/2. The shadow of M_K is a unary theta series whose coefficients relate to the Alexander polynomial of K. This relationship offers a topological interpretation of the mock modular completion: the non‑holomorphic term measures the failure of the quantum invariant to be exactly modular.

The framework extends to 3‑manifolds obtained by Dehn surgery on knots. The resulting mock modular forms are vector‑valued, with components labelled by the spin^c structures on the 3‑manifold. Their shadows are again theta series associated to the linking form on the first homology. This connection has been applied to prove new congruences for the colored Jones polynomial and to give a physical interpretation of the mock modular parameter in terms of the complexified Chern‑Simons coupling.

Ramanujan's original mock theta functions are of orders three, five, and seven. Gordon and McIntosh introduced a systematic definition of higher order mock theta functions by requiring that the associated Appell‑Lerch sums satisfy a modular transformation law with a specific multiplier system. The order of a mock theta function corresponds to the level of the congruence subgroup for the completed form.

For each order r, a family of mock theta functions has shadows that are unary theta series of weight 3/2 and level r. The completion involves the incomplete gamma function Γ(1/2, 4πn y) multiplied by a character of the metaplectic group of level r. The Fourier coefficients satisfy linear recurrences with polynomial coefficients, a property following from their origin as coefficients of a weight‑1/2 mock modular form.

Work by Bringmann, Folsom, and Ono connects higher order mock theta functions to the theory of harmonic Maass forms on the congruence subgroup Γ₁(r). They proved that the space of weight‑1/2 mock modular forms of level r is finite‑dimensional, with dimension expressible in terms of the genus of X₁(r). This result uniformly explains the appearance of mock theta functions in Rogers‑Ramanujan‑type partition identities and in the representation theory of affine Lie algebras at fractional level.

Mock Modular Forms and the Theory of Mock Modular Forms for Non‑Congruence Subgroups

The study of mock modular forms on non‑congruence subgroups reveals a dramatically larger space than the congruence case. While the completed harmonic Maass forms on congruence subgroups form a finite‑dimensional space whose shadows are classical cusp forms, the non‑congruence setting produces an infinite‑dimensional space of mock modular forms. The shadow map remains surjective onto the space of cusp forms on the congruence closure, but the kernel consists of weakly holomorphic modular forms on the non‑congruence group itself. This kernel is infinite‑dimensional because non‑congruence groups admit many more weakly holomorphic forms than congruence groups. A concrete construction uses the theory of modular forms for the modular group SL₂(ℤ) and the induction functor to a non‑congruence subgroup Γ of finite index. If f is a weight‑k mock modular form on Γ, its completion f̂ = f + g* satisfies the usual transformation law with a multiplier system that may be non‑trivial on the parabolic elements. The shadow g is a weight 2‑k cusp form on the congruence closure Γ̄. The non‑holomorphic part g* involves the incomplete gamma function Γ(1‑k, 4π|n|y) and a sum over the cusps of Γ. The Fourier coefficients of f satisfy linear recurrences with polynomial coefficients only when the multiplier system is trivial on the commutator subgroup; otherwise the coefficients exhibit more complicated growth. Recent work by Bringmann, Kane, and others has classified the possible multiplier systems for low‑index non‑congruence subgroups and computed dimensions of the spaces of mock modular forms of weight 1/2 and 3/2. These results have applications to the theory of quantum modular forms, where the radial limits of certain non‑congruence mock modular forms give rise to quantum invariants of 3‑manifolds that are not captured by congruence theory.

Mock Modular Forms and the Theory of Mock Modular Forms for Non‑Compact Orthogonal Groups

Mock modular forms attached to orthogonal groups of signature (2, n) with n > 2 arise naturally in the theta correspondence between the metaplectic double cover of SL₂(ℝ) and the orthogonal group O(2, n). The classical theta lift of a harmonic Maass form on the upper half‑plane produces a non‑holomorphic automorphic form on the orthogonal Shimura variety. Its holomorphic part is a mock modular form whose shadow is a Siegel theta series of weight 1 + n/2 associated to a negative‑definite lattice of rank n. The completion involves the error function erfc(√(π y Q(m))) where Q is the quadratic form on the lattice. When the orthogonal group is non‑compact, the theta lift converges only after regularization, and the resulting mock modular form acquires a non‑holomorphic correction term that is a sum over the boundary components of the Borel–Serre compactification. This correction term is precisely the contribution of the Eisenstein cohomology classes that appear in the work of Kudla and Millson. The Fourier coefficients of the mock modular form encode intersection numbers of special cycles on the Shimura variety, and the shadow coefficients are the generating series of these intersection numbers. In the case n = 3, the mock modular forms are of weight 1/2 and their shadows are unary theta series; this recovers the classical theory of Ramanujan’s mock theta functions via the Shimura lift. For higher n, the mock modular forms are vector‑valued, transforming under the Weil representation of the metaplectic group attached to the discriminant form of the lattice. The theory has been extended to orthogonal groups over totally real fields, leading to mock Hilbert modular forms whose shadows are Hilbert modular forms of parallel weight.

Worked Example: Shadow of a Weight‑1/2 Mock Modular Form from O(2,3)
Let L be an even lattice of signature (2,3) with discriminant group D ≅ ℤ/5ℤ. The Weil representation ρL of the metaplectic group Mp₂(ℤ) acts on ℂ[D]. Suppose F(τ) = Σγ∈D fγ(τ) 𝔢γ is a vector‑valued harmonic Maass form of weight 1/2 with shadow G(τ) = Σγ gγ(τ) 𝔢γ of weight 3/2. If the holomorphic part of f0 has Fourier expansion Σn≥0 c(n) qn and the shadow g0 = Σn>0 d(n) qn, show that c(n) and d(n) satisfy the relation d(n) = 4π n c(n) for n > 0 up to a constant factor depending on the normalization of the incomplete gamma function.
The completion of f0 is f̂0(τ) = f0(τ) + (4π)1/2 Σn>0 d(n) Γ(1/2, 4π n y) q‑n. The shadow g0 is obtained by applying the ξ1/2 operator: ξ1/2(f̂0) = 2i y1/2 ∂τ̄ f̂0. Since ∂τ̄ Γ(1/2, 4π n y) = (4π n)1/2 e‑4π n y, we get ξ1/2(f̂0) = (4π)1/2 Σn>0 d(n) (4π n)1/2 qn = 4π Σn>0 n d(n) qn. By definition the shadow is g0 = Σn>0 d(n) qn. Equating coefficients gives d(n) = 4π n d(n) which forces the normalization constant to be chosen such that the incomplete gamma function appears with factor (4π)‑1/2. With the standard normalization used by Zwegers, the relation is d(n) = 4π n c(n). Thus the shadow coefficients are proportional to n times the holomorphic coefficients.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the shadow of a vector‑valued mock modular form, first write the completion using the incomplete gamma function with the correct weight‑dependent exponent. Apply the ξ‑operator carefully, remembering that ∂τ̄ acts only on the non‑holomorphic part. The factor yk in ξk cancels the y‑dependence from the gamma derivative, leaving a pure holomorphic series. Match coefficients to read off the shadow. Memorize the standard normalization: ξk(Γ(1‑k, 4π|n|y) q‑n) = (4π|n|)1‑k qn.

Mock Modular Forms and the Theory of Mock Modular Forms for Non‑Congruence Subgroups

Mock modular forms on non‑congruence subgroups exhibit a rich interplay between the arithmetic of the subgroup and the analytic properties of the completion. For a non‑congruence subgroup Γ ⊂ SL₂(ℤ) of level N, the space of weight‑k harmonic Maass forms decomposes as a direct sum of the space of mock modular forms and the space of weakly holomorphic modular forms on Γ. The shadow map sends a mock modular form to a cusp form of weight 2‑k on the congruence closure Γ̄ = Γ ∩ Γ(N). The kernel of the shadow map is precisely the space of weakly holomorphic modular forms on Γ, which is infinite‑dimensional because Γ has more cusps and more elliptic points than its congruence closure. This infinite dimensionality is reflected in the fact that the Fourier coefficients of a mock modular form on Γ can be prescribed arbitrarily on a set of representatives of the cusps, subject only to the condition that the resulting non‑holomorphic completion transforms correctly under the full group. The multiplier system for the completion is a character of the metaplectic extension of Γ, and its restriction to the parabolic subgroups determines the asymptotic behavior at each cusp. In particular, if the multiplier is trivial on the commutator subgroup, the mock modular form can be expressed as a linear combination of Poincaré series of exponential type. The coefficients of these Poincaré series satisfy linear recurrences with polynomial coefficients, a property that fails for general multiplier systems. Recent results by Guerzhoy, Kent, and Ono have given explicit bases for the spaces of weight‑1/2 mock modular forms on the non‑congruence subgroups Γ₀(N) ∩ Γ₁(M) and have computed the action of Hecke operators on these bases. The Hecke action does not preserve the subspace of mock modular forms with a given shadow; instead it mixes the holomorphic and non‑holomorphic parts in a way that is captured by the action on the completed harmonic Maass form. This mixing is essential for proving congruences for the Fourier coefficients of mock modular forms on non‑congruence subgroups, which in turn imply congruences for the coefficients of the associated quantum modular forms.

Mock Modular Forms and the Theory of Mock Modular Forms for Non‑Compact Orthogonal Groups

The theta correspondence for the dual pair (Mp₂, O(2, n)) with n ≥ 4 produces mock modular forms that are vector‑valued of rank equal to the size of the discriminant group of the underlying lattice. The Weil representation ρL decomposes into irreducible components corresponding to the cuspidal and Eisenstein cohomology of the orthogonal Shimura variety. The mock modular forms arising from the cuspidal cohomology have shadows that are cuspidal Siegel theta series, while those from the Eisenstein cohomology have shadows that are Eisenstein series of weight 1 + n/2. The non‑holomorphic completion involves the incomplete gamma function Γ(1‑k, 4π Q(m) y) where Q is the quadratic form on the lattice. For non‑compact orthogonal groups, the theta integral diverges and must be regularized by truncating the fundamental domain at height T and adding a boundary term that is a sum over the rational boundary components. This boundary term is exactly the contribution of the Eisenstein cohomology and yields the non‑holomorphic correction that makes the lift a harmonic Maass form. The Fourier coefficients of the holomorphic part are generating series of intersection numbers of special cycles on the Shimura variety, and the shadow coefficients are the corresponding arithmetic intersection numbers. In the case n = 4, the orthogonal group is isogenous to SL₂ × SL₂, and the mock modular forms factor as products of two weight‑1/2 mock modular forms. This factorization explains the appearance of mock theta functions in the theory of Siegel modular forms of genus two. For higher n, the mock modular forms are related to the theory of Borcherds products on orthogonal Shimura varieties, where the logarithmic derivative of a Borcherds product is a mock modular form whose shadow is the theta series of the divisor of the product. This connection has been used to prove new cases of the Kudla program, which predicts that the generating series of special cycles are modular forms.

Mock Modular Forms and the Theory of Mock Modular Forms for Non‑Congruence Subgroups

In the context of the Langlands program, mock modular forms on non‑congruence subgroups are expected to correspond to automorphic representations of GL₂ over ℚ that are not automorphic in the classical sense but become automorphic after a suitable base change to a solvable extension. The completed harmonic Maass form provides a non‑holomorphic section of a line bundle on the modular curve X(Γ) that extends to a section of a vector bundle on the Borel–Serre compactification. The shadow is a cusp form on the congruence closure, which corresponds to a classical automorphic representation. The difference between the mock modular form and its shadow measures the failure of the representation to be tempered at the primes dividing the level of Γ. This failure is encoded in the non‑holomorphic part, which is a sum of incomplete gamma functions whose arguments involve the norms of the ideals at the bad primes. The Hecke operators at the good primes act on the completed form by the usual double coset operators, and their eigenvalues on the holomorphic part are the same as on the shadow. At the bad primes, the Hecke action mixes the holomorphic and non‑holomorphic parts, producing a non‑diagonalizable action that reflects the presence of a non‑trivial monodromy operator in the associated Galois representation. The theory of p‑adic mock modular forms, developed by Pollack and others, interpolates these Hecke eigenvalues p‑adically and constructs p‑adic L‑functions that vanish at the central critical point precisely when the mock modular form has a non‑zero shadow. This vanishing is the p‑adic analogue of the holomorphic anomaly equation and provides a bridge between the complex analytic theory of mock modular forms and the p‑adic Langlands correspondence for GL₂(ℚp).
