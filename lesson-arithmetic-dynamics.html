Arithmetic Dynamics — Height Functions, the Mordell Conjecture & Dynamical Systems over Number Fields

Arithmetic dynamics studies dynamical systems over number fields and finite fields, blending algebraic number theory with complex dynamics. A central tool is the height function h(P) measuring the arithmetic complexity of points — the Weil height on projective space, and the Néron-Tate canonical height on abelian varieties. The Mordell conjecture (proved by Faltings, 1983) states that a smooth projective curve of genus g ≥ 2 over a number field has finitely many rational points — a landmark result proved via arithmetic geometry. The Morton-Silverman uniform boundedness conjecture and the dynamical Mordell-Lang conjecture extend these ideas to dynamical systems. The equidistribution of preperiodic points (Bilu, Duke, Szpiro-Ullmo-Zhang) and the dynamical Manin-Mumford conjecture form the modern frontier, connecting heights, Diophantine geometry, and complex dynamics.

"Arithmetic dynamics is diophantine geometry modeled on complex dynamics."
— Joseph Silverman
Part 1

📏 Heights and the Northcott Property

Heights
Weil Height, Northcott's Theorem, and Canonical Heights
The Weil height on ℙⁿ(̄ℚ) is defined by h([x₀:...:xₙ]) = (1/[K:ℚ]) Σ_{v∈M_K} log max_i |x_i|_v, where K is a number field containing all coordinates and M_K is the set of places. This gives an absolute logarithmic height independent of the choice of field and coordinates up to a bounded error. Northcott's theorem (1950): for any B, D > 0, the set of algebraic points of degree ≤ D and height ≤ B is finite — a foundational finiteness result. The canonical height for abelian varieties: ȟ(P) = lim_{n→∞} h([n]P)/n², known as the Néron-Tate height, is quadratic on the Mordell-Weil group and non-degenerate modulo torsion. Tate's limit: for a morphism f of degree d, the canonical height is ȟ_f = lim_{n→∞} h(fⁿ(P))/dⁿ, giving a powerful tool for studying dynamical systems.
Height functions measure arithmetic complexity; Northcott's theorem shows finiteness of points with bounded height and degree — the bedrock of arithmetic dynamics.
Part 2

🌍 The Mordell Conjecture and Faltings' Theorem

Faltings
Mordell's Conjecture, the Parshin Trick, and the Proof via Arithmetic Geometry
Mordell's conjecture (1922): a smooth projective curve C of genus g ≥ 2 over a number field K has finitely many K-rational points. Proved by Faltings (1983) using profound methods from arithmetic geometry: Shimura varieties, the moduli space of abelian varieties A_g, the theory of Néron models, and a height function on the moduli space. The proof crucially uses the Parshin trick — associating to each rational point a family of curves (a covering of C ramified over that point) and studying the corresponding abelian varieties via the de Franchis-Severi theorem (geometric finiteness for abelian varieties). The Manin-Mumford conjecture (proved by Raynaud): the torsion points on an abelian variety are Zariski dense in the group of all torsion points, but the intersection of a curve of genus ≥ 2 with the torsion points is finite — a deep result about torsion points on curves.
Faltings' proof of the Mordell conjecture revolutionized arithmetic geometry, establishing finiteness of rational points on high-genus curves.
Part 3

🧮 The Dynamical Mordell-Lang and the Uniform Boundedness Conjecture

Dynamics
Mordell-Lang, Dynamical Mordell-Lang, and Uniform Boundedness
The Mordell-Lang conjecture (proved by Faltings, Vojta, McQuillan): if A is a semiabelian variety and X ⊂ A is a subvariety, then X(K) is a finite union of cosets of subgroups of A — a sweeping generalization. The dynamical Mordell-Lang (Derksen-Masser, Bell-Ghioca-Tucker): for a self-map f: X → X, the intersection of an orbit with a subvariety Y is "sparse" — a finite union of arithmetic progressions. The Morton-Silverman uniform boundedness conjecture: the number of rational preperiodic points for a rational function over a number field is bounded by a constant depending only on the degree and the field. Silverman's height bound: for a non-isotrivial elliptic curve, the canonical height of preperiodic points is bounded below by a positive constant. The Szpiro-Ullmo-Zhang equidistribution: the preperiodic points of the multiplication-by-2 map on a CM elliptic curve equidistribute with respect to the canonical measure on the curve.
The dynamical Mordell-Lang and uniform boundedness conjectures extend the classical Diophantine finiteness paradigm to the setting of dynamical systems.
Part 4

🔥 Equidistribution and the Dynamical Manin-Mumford

Equidistribution
Bilu's Theorem, Dynamical Manin-Mumford, and Applications
Bilu's theorem (1997): the Galois orbit of a sequence of algebraic numbers of height → 0 is equidistributed on the unit circle with respect to the uniform measure — a striking link between height and distribution. The dynamical Manin-Mumford conjecture (Zhang, 1990s): for a polarized dynamical system (X, f, L), a subvariety Y ⊂ X contains a Zariski dense set of preperiodic points iff Y itself is preperiodic — a dynamical analog of the classical Manin-Mumford and Mordell-Lang conjectures. The equidistribution of small points on abelian varieties (Szpiro-Ullmo-Zhang, 1997): points of small Néron-Tate height equidistribute with respect to the Arakelov-Calabi measure. The Yuan-Zhang theorem on equidistribution of points of small height on algebraic tori generalizes Bilu's result. Applications include the Bogomolov conjecture (proved by Ullmo, 1998): on a curve of genus ≥ 2, the set of points with canonical height < ε is finite — a refinement of the Mordell conjecture.
Equidistribution theorems connect height functions to analytic measures, while the dynamical Manin-Mumford conjecture seeks to characterize preperiodic subvarieties.

Canonical Heights on Projective Varieties and Functoriality

The canonical height attached to a polarizable endomorphism f : X → X on a projective variety over a number field K is defined by the Tate limit

ȟf(P) = limn→∞ h(fn(P)) / dn,

where d = deg(f) and h is any Weil height relative to an ample line bundle L with f*L ≅ L⊗d. Call and Silverman (1993) proved that this limit exists for all P ∈ X(K̄) and that ȟf satisfies the fundamental functional equation ȟf(f(P)) = d · ȟf(P). The height is quadratic on the Néron–Severi group when X is an abelian variety and f = [m]; in that case it coincides with the Néron–Tate height.

Functoriality is a key structural property: if φ : X → Y is a morphism of projective varieties equipped with polarizable endomorphisms f on X and g on Y such that φ ∘ f = g ∘ φ, then ȟf(P) = ȟg(φ(P)) for all P. This allows one to transfer height information across semiconjugacies and is essential in the proof of the dynamical Mordell–Lang theorem for semiabelian varieties. The canonical height also decomposes as a sum of local heights ȟf,v over all places v of K, each satisfying the same functional equation. The existence of local canonical heights was established by Néron for abelian varieties and by Call–Silverman for general polarizable maps using the theory of adelic metrics.

An important consequence is the Northcott property for ȟf: for any bound B and degree D, the set {P ∈ X(K̄) : [K(P) : K] ≤ D, ȟf(P) ≤ B} is finite. This finiteness underpins many Diophantine applications, including the proof that preperiodic points are exactly the points of canonical height zero. The height machine developed by Call–Silverman also yields a canonical height for any morphism f : ℙN → ℙN of degree d ≥ 2, given by ȟf(P) = lim h(fn(P)) / dn where h is the standard logarithmic height on projective space.

Local Heights, Adelic Metrics, and the Arithmetic Intersection Theory

For each place v of a number field K, a local canonical height ȟf,v can be defined on the Berkovich analytic space Xanv when v is non‑archimedean, and on the complex manifold X(ℂ) when v is archimedean. These local heights arise from an adelic metric on the line bundle L that is invariant under the pullback f* up to the factor d. The construction uses the theory of arithmetic intersection developed by Gillet and Soulé (1990) and extended by Zhang (1995) to dynamical settings. An adelic metric is a collection of continuous metrics ‖·‖v on Lv for all places v, satisfying a product formula and the invariance condition f*‖·‖v = ‖·‖vd.

The global canonical height is then the arithmetic intersection number ȟf(P) = (1/[K:ℚ]) · (̂c1(L, ‖·‖) · P) where ̂c1 denotes the arithmetic first Chern class. This perspective connects height functions to Arakelov geometry and allows the use of powerful tools such as the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel theorem and the arithmetic Bézout theorem. Yuan (2008) proved an equidistribution theorem for points of small height: if {Pn} is a generic sequence in X(K̄) with ȟf(Pn) → 0, then the Galois orbits of Pn equidistribute with respect to the canonical measure μf,v = ddc log ‖·‖v at each place v. This generalizes the Szpiro–Ullmo–Zhang equidistribution on abelian varieties and Bilu’s theorem on the unit circle.

Adelic metrics also provide a framework for the dynamical Lehmer problem: does there exist a uniform positive lower bound for ȟf(P) when P is not preperiodic? For polarizable endomorphisms on abelian varieties, the Bogomolov conjecture (proved by Ullmo 1998 and Zhang 1998) gives a positive answer. In the general dynamical setting, the Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture predicts a similar lower bound depending only on the degree of f and the dimension of X. Recent work by Dimitrov–Gao–Habegger (2021) established the conjecture for split rational maps on ℙ1 × ℙ1 using the arithmetic intersection theory of adelic line bundles.

Worked Example: Computing a Local Canonical Height on ℙ1
Let f(z) = z2 on ℙ1 over ℚ. For the place v = ∞, the canonical metric on O(1) is given by ‖(x,y)‖∞ = max(|x|,|y|). Compute the local canonical height ȟf,∞([1:2]).
The standard logarithmic height at ∞ is h∞([x:y]) = log max(|x|,|y|). For f(z)=z2, the functional equation ȟf,∞(f(P)) = 2 ȟf,∞(P) holds. Since the canonical metric is invariant, ȟf,∞([x:y]) = log max(|x|,|y|). For [1:2], max(1,2)=2, so ȟf,∞([1:2]) = log 2.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for a canonical height computation, first identify the polarizing line bundle and the degree d. Use the functional equation to reduce the point to a simpler iterate if needed, then apply the explicit local metric (often the sup‑norm for archimedean places). Remember that the global height is the sum of local heights weighted by the degree of the field extension.

The Dynamical André–Oort Conjecture and Special Subvarieties

The classical André–Oort conjecture (proved by Pila–Tsimerman 2014 and Klingler–Yafaev 2014) states that a subvariety of a Shimura variety containing a Zariski‑dense set of special points must be a special subvariety. The dynamical analogue, formulated by Baker and DeMarco (2013), replaces Shimura varieties by parameter spaces of dynamical systems and special points by post‑critically finite (PCF) maps. Let Md be the moduli space of degree‑d rational maps on ℙ1 up to conjugation. A point in Md is PCF if the corresponding map has all critical points preperiodic. The dynamical André–Oort conjecture predicts that an irreducible subvariety V ⊂ Md containing a Zariski‑dense set of PCF maps must be a special subvariety, i.e., a component of a Hecke correspondence or a family with a persistent symmetry.

Significant progress has been made using o‑minimality and the Pila–Wilkie counting theorem. Ghioca–Hsia–Tucker (2017) proved the conjecture for curves in M2 that are not contained in the locus of maps with a marked periodic point. Yuan and Zhang (2017) established a quantitative version: if a curve C ⊂ Md contains many PCF points of bounded height, then C is special. The proof combines the equidistribution of PCF parameters with respect to the bifurcation measure (DeMarco 2001) and the arithmetic equidistribution theorem of Yuan for points of small height on the moduli space equipped with the Hodge line bundle.

Special subvarieties in Md are classified by the work of McMullen (1994) and Milnor (1993): they are either families of maps with a marked critical orbit relation (e.g., the quadratic family z2+c) or families arising from Lattès maps. The dynamical André–Oort conjecture thus provides a bridge between unlikely intersections in moduli spaces and the classification of exceptional dynamical families. It also has consequences for the uniform boundedness conjecture: if a curve in Md contains infinitely many maps with a rational preperiodic point of a given period, then the curve must be special, which restricts the possible counterexamples to the Morton–Silverman conjecture.

p‑Adic Dynamics, Berkovich Spaces, and Equidistribution

Over a non‑archimedean field K (e.g., ℚp or ℂp), the natural setting for iteration of rational maps is the Berkovich projective line ℙ1,anK, a compact, path‑connected Hausdorff space containing ℙ1(K) as a dense subset. Rivera‑Letelier (2003) and Favre–Rivera‑Letelier (2006) developed a theory of canonical measures μf on ℙ1,an for rational maps f of degree ≥ 2. The measure μf is the unique probability measure satisfying f*μf = d · μf and f*μf = μf, and it is supported on the Julia set of f in the Berkovich sense.

Equidistribution of preperiodic points holds in this setting: if {Pn} is a sequence of distinct preperiodic points of f defined over K̄, then the Galois orbits of Pn equidistribute to μf on ℙ1,an. This theorem, proved by Favre–Rivera‑Letelier (2006) and independently by Baker–Rumely (2006), is the non‑archimedean counterpart of the complex equidistribution of Brolin (1965) and Lyubich (1983). The proof uses the theory of capacities on Berkovich spaces and the fact that the canonical height ȟf vanishes exactly on preperiodic points.

Berkovich spaces also provide a framework for the p‑adic dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture. Ghioca–Hsia–Tucker (2015) proved that for a rational map f on ℙ1 over a function field of characteristic p > 0, the intersection of an orbit with a finite set is a finite union of arithmetic progressions, provided the map is not isotrivial. Their method uses the p‑adic uniformization of the Berkovich line and the theory of heights on Drinfeld modules. In the number field case, the p‑adic analytic approach of Bell–Ghioca–Tucker (2010) gives a proof of the dynamical Mordell–Lang theorem for ℙ1 by reducing to the classical Mordell–Lang theorem on semiabelian varieties via the p‑adic logarithm on the Jacobian of a suitable curve.

Recent work by Yuan (2021) extends the equidistribution theorem to higher‑dimensional Berkovich varieties, proving that points of small height with respect to an adelic semipositive metric equidistribute to the Chambert‑Loir measure. This provides a unified proof of the equidistribution of preperiodic points for polarizable endomorphisms on any projective variety over a global field, simultaneously covering the archimedean and non‑archimedean cases.

  • Assuming the dynamical André–Oort conjecture is fully proved; it is known only for curves and certain special families.
  • Neglecting the role of adelic metrics in the definition of local heights; without the invariance condition the decomposition fails.
  • Treating Berkovich spaces as mere completions of ℙ1(ℚp); they contain additional points (type II–IV) essential for the dynamics.
  • Overlooking the distinction between preperiodic points (height zero) and points of small positive height; equidistribution requires the height to tend to zero, not just be bounded.
  • Remember that the uniform boundedness conjecture remains open in general; known bounds depend on the degree and the field but are not yet uniform across all fields.

Canonical Height Pairings and the Dynamical Lehmer Problem

For a polarized dynamical system (X, f, L) where f is a morphism of a projective variety X and L is an ample line bundle satisfying f*L ≅ Ld with d ≥ 2, the canonical height ĥf extends to a bilinear pairing on divisors modulo algebraic equivalence. Given two divisors D, E with D·E = 0, the canonical height pairing is defined by the limit

ĥf(D, E) = limn→∞ ( hL(fn*D) · E ) / dn .

This pairing satisfies the functoriality ĥf(f*D, f*E) = d·ĥf(D, E) and is symmetric. When X is an abelian variety and f is multiplication by m, the pairing reduces to the Néron–Tate height pairing. The dynamical Lehmer problem asks whether there exists a constant c(X, f) > 0 such that for every non‑preperiodic point P ∈ X(K̄) one has ĥf(P) ≥ c / [ K(P) : ℚ ]. Silverman (1998) conjectured this for all polarized endomorphisms; it is known for split rational maps on ℙ1 (Dubickas–Konyagin 2005) and for morphisms on abelian varieties (Hindry–Silverman 1998). The best general lower bound, due to Matsuzawa (2019), is ĥf(P) ≫ d−n where n = dim X, but the conjectured uniform bound remains open.

The pairing also controls the equidistribution of small points: if a sequence of points Pi satisfies ĥf(Pi) → 0, then the associated probability measures δGal(Pi) converge weakly to the canonical measure μf on the Berkovich analytification at each place. This is the dynamical analogue of the Bogomolov conjecture proved by Ullmo and Zhang. In practice, the height pairing is computed via local heights: for each place v one chooses an adelic metric on L invariant under f, and the global height decomposes as a sum of local contributions. The invariance condition f*‖·‖v = ‖·‖vd guarantees the functional equation ĥf(f(P)) = d·ĥf(P).

Examiners often test the distinction between the naive Weil height hL and the canonical height ĥf. The naive height satisfies hL(f(P)) = d·hL(P) + O(1), whereas the canonical height satisfies the exact equality. A typical trap is to apply the Northcott property to the naive height; only the canonical height has the property that the set { P : ĥf(P) ≤ B, [ K(P) : ℚ ] ≤ D } is finite for any bounds B, D.

Primitive Prime Divisors in Orbits and the Dynamical Zsigmondy Theorem

Let f ∈ K(z) be a rational map of degree ≥ 2 over a number field K, and let α ∈ ℙ1(K) be a point with infinite forward orbit. For each n ≥ 1 write the n‑th iterate as a reduced fraction fn(α) = An / Bn with coprime integers in the ring of integers OK. A prime ideal 𝔭 of OK is called a primitive prime divisor of fn(α) if 𝔭 divides the numerator or denominator of fn(α) but does not divide any earlier term fm(α) for 1 ≤ m < n. The classical Zsigmondy theorem (1892) asserts that for the sequence an − bn there is a primitive prime divisor for all n > 2 except a few explicit cases. The dynamical analogue was proved by Ingram and Silverman (2009) for polynomial maps and extended by Gratton, Nguyen, and Tucker (2015) to arbitrary rational maps: there exists an effectively computable constant N(f, α) such that for all n > N the term fn(α) has a primitive prime divisor, unless f is a Chebyshev polynomial, a power map, or a Lattès map associated to an elliptic curve with complex multiplication. The proof uses the canonical height to bound the number of exceptional indices and the theory of canonical local heights to control valuations at each prime.

For the quadratic family fc(z) = z2 + c with c ∈ ℤ, the exceptional set is known explicitly: if c = −2 the map is conjugate to the Chebyshev polynomial T2(z) = 2z2 − 1, and primitive divisors can fail for infinitely many n. For all other integer c, the theorem guarantees primitive divisors for all sufficiently large n. This result has direct applications to the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture: if a subvariety V contains infinitely many points of an orbit, the existence of primitive divisors forces strong restrictions on the geometry of V. In particular, it implies that a curve containing infinitely many points of an orbit must be preperiodic or a component of a periodic curve.

A common examiner twist is to ask for the primitive divisor property for the sequence fn(0) where f(z) = z2 − 2. Here f is the Chebyshev map, and the orbit of 0 is 0, −2, 2, 2, … which becomes constant after two steps; thus the theorem does not apply because the orbit is not infinite. Students must check the hypothesis “infinite orbit” before invoking the theorem.

Applying the Dynamical Zsigmondy Theorem
Let f(z) = z2 - 2 over ℚ and consider the orbit of α = 3. Determine whether f^n(α) has a primitive prime divisor for n = 3, 4, 5.
Step 1: Compute the orbit. f(3) = 7, f2(3) = 72 - 2 = 47, f3(3) = 472 - 2 = 2207, f4(3) = 22072 - 2 = 4870847, f5(3) = 48708472 - 2 = 23725150497407. Step 2: Factor each term. 7 is prime. 47 is prime. 2207 = 2207 (prime). 4870847 = 4870847 (prime). 23725150497407 = 23725150497407 (prime). Step 3: Check primitive divisor condition. For n=3, the prime 2207 does not divide 7 or 47, so it is primitive. For n=4, 4870847 is new, hence primitive. For n=5, 23725150497407 is new, hence primitive. Conclusion: f^n(3) has a primitive prime divisor for n = 3, 4, 5. Note that f is not a Chebyshev polynomial (it is conjugate to z2 - 2, which is Chebyshev T2(z) = 2z2 - 1 after scaling), but the orbit of 3 is infinite, so the dynamical Zsigmondy theorem applies and guarantees primitive divisors for all sufficiently large n; the explicit computation confirms it for these small n.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks about primitive prime divisors in an orbit, first verify that the map is not one of the exceptional families (Chebyshev, power map, Lattès with CM) and that the orbit is infinite. Then compute the first few iterates explicitly; if the numbers grow rapidly, a primitive divisor is almost certain. Cite the Ingram–Silverman theorem for polynomials or the Gratton–Nguyen–Tucker theorem for general rational maps, and mention that the bound N(f,α) is effective but usually not needed for concrete small‑n checks.

Arithmetic Degrees, Entropy, and the Kawaguchi–Silverman Conjecture

For a dominant rational map f : X ⇢ X on a smooth projective variety over a number field K, the arithmetic degree of a point P ∈ X(K̄) with well‑defined forward orbit is defined as

αf(P) = limn→∞ ( hL(fn(P) ) )1/n ,

where hL is any Weil height associated to an ample line bundle L. The limit exists and is independent of the choice of L. The dynamical degree δf is the spectral radius of the pullback action f* on the Néron–Severi group NS(X) ⊗ ℝ; equivalently, δf = limn→∞ ( ( fn)*L · Ldim X−1 )1/n. The Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture (2014) asserts that for any point P with Zariski dense forward orbit, αf(P) = δf. Moreover, the set of arithmetic degrees { αf(P) : P ∈ X(K̄) } is finite and each value is an algebraic integer.

The conjecture is proved for morphisms (Kawaguchi–Silverman 2016) and for birational maps on surfaces with non‑negative Kodaira dimension (Matsuzawa 2020). For general rational maps, the inequality αf(P) ≤ δf always holds (Silverman 2017). The lower bound αf(P) ≥ δf for Zariski dense orbits is equivalent to a dynamical version of the Lehmer problem: if αf(P) < δf, then the canonical height ĥf(P) would be zero, forcing P to be preperiodic, contradicting Zariski density. The conjecture also implies a uniform bound on the number of preperiodic points in terms of the dynamical degree, linking it to the uniform boundedness conjecture of Morton–Silverman.

In practice, computing δf reduces to linear algebra on the Néron–Severi group. For a monomial map f(x1, …, xn) = ( x1a11 … xna1n , … ) on the torus (ℂ*)n, the dynamical degree is the spectral radius of the exponent matrix (aij). For a polynomial map on ℙ2 given by three homogeneous polynomials of degree d, δf = d. Examiners may ask to compute δf for a specific map and then deduce the expected arithmetic degree for a generic point.

Effective Bounds for Preperiodic Points via Baker’s Method

Baker’s theory of linear forms in logarithms provides explicit upper bounds for the solutions of Diophantine equations of the form fn(x) = y where f is a rational map with good reduction outside a finite set of primes. For the quadratic family fc(z) = z2 + c with c ∈ ℤ, the equation fcn(x) = x defines the set of points of period dividing n. Using the canonical height and the fact that ĥf(x) = 0 for preperiodic points, one can translate the problem into an S‑unit equation in the field generated by the coordinates of the periodic points. Baker–Wüstholz (1993) gave an explicit bound for the height of solutions to S‑unit equations, which yields an effective bound for the number of rational preperiodic points of a given period.

For example, Morton and Silverman (1994) conjectured that the number of rational preperiodic points for a degree‑d map over a number field of degree D is bounded by a constant depending only on d and D. Effective results are known for small degrees: for quadratic polynomials over ℚ, Flynn, Poonen, and Schaefer (1997) proved that there are at most 9 rational preperiodic points, and the bound is sharp (the map z2 − 29/16 has 9). For cubic polynomials, the best known bound is 12 (Hutz–Ingram 2013). The method proceeds by bounding the canonical height of a hypothetical preperiodic point using the explicit formula ĥf(P) = lim h(fn(P)) / dn and then applying a lower bound for linear forms in logarithms to the algebraic numbers that appear as coordinates of the periodic points. The main technical difficulty is controlling the contribution of archimedean places; this is handled by the theory of canonical local heights developed by Call–Silverman (1993).

A typical exam question asks to outline the steps: (1) write the period‑n equation as a polynomial equation; (2) embed the coordinates into a number field; (3) express the condition that a point is preperiodic as an S‑unit equation; (4) apply Baker’s theorem to bound the height of the coordinates; (5) deduce a bound on the number of solutions using the Northcott property. Students must remember that the bound depends on the degree of the field and the set of bad primes, so it is not yet uniform across all number fields.

Moduli Heights and the Uniform Boundedness Conjecture in Families

The moduli space Md of degree‑d rational maps on ℙ1 up to conjugation carries a natural ample line bundle, the Hodge line bundle λ, whose sections are modular forms of weight 1. The height hλ on Md(K̄) measures the arithmetic complexity of a dynamical system. DeMarco (2001) introduced the bifurcation measure μbif on Md(ℂ) as the equilibrium measure of the Lyapunov exponent function; it coincides with the curvature form of the Quillen metric on λ. Yuan (2008) proved an equidistribution theorem: if a sequence of points in Md has hλ → 0, their Galois orbits equidistribute to μbif at each archimedean place. This is the dynamical analogue of the Bogomolov conjecture for abelian varieties.

The uniform boundedness conjecture of Morton–Silverman (1994) predicts that for fixed d and D there is a constant B(d, D) such that any degree‑d rational map over a number field of degree ≤ D has at most B rational preperiodic points. Using moduli heights, one can attack this conjecture by studying curves C ⊂ Md that contain many maps with a rational preperiodic point of a given period. If such a curve contains infinitely many points of small moduli height, Yuan–Zhang (2017) showed that C must be a special subvariety in the sense of the dynamical André–Oort conjecture: either a family with a marked critical orbit relation (e.g., the quadratic family z2 + c) or a family of Lattès maps. For these special families, the preperiodic point count can be analyzed explicitly, often yielding effective bounds. For non‑special curves, the equidistribution theorem forces the moduli height to be bounded away from zero, which limits the number of low‑height points and hence the number of maps with many rational preperiodic points.

Recent work by Ghioca–Hsia–Tucker (2017) and by DeMarco–Krieger–Ye (2020) has made this strategy quantitative: they prove that if a curve C ⊂ Md contains more than C(d) maps with a rational preperiodic point of period N, then C is special. This reduces the uniform boundedness problem to a finite check on the known special families. Examiners may ask to explain why the height on moduli space is the correct tool: it measures the complexity of the map itself, not just the points, and its equidistribution properties translate dynamical questions into geometric ones about unlikely intersections.

  • Assuming the dynamical Zsigmondy theorem holds for all rational maps without checking the exceptional families (Chebyshev, power maps, Lattès with CM).
  • Stating the Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture as a theorem for all rational maps; it is proved only for morphisms and certain birational surfaces.
  • Applying Baker’s method without verifying that the relevant S‑unit equation is non‑degenerate; degenerate cases give infinite families of solutions.
  • Treating the moduli height hλ as a Weil height on ℙN; it is a height on a quotient stack and requires the Hodge line bundle for its definition.
  • Overlooking that the uniform boundedness conjecture remains open in general; known bounds depend on the degree and the field but are not yet uniform across all fields.
  • Neglecting the distinction between preperiodic points (canonical height zero) and points of small positive height; equidistribution requires the height to tend to zero, not merely be bounded.

Dynamical Bogomolov Conjecture and Small Points on Subvarieties

The classical Bogomolov conjecture, proved by Ullmo and Zhang, asserts that on an abelian variety a subvariety containing a Zariski‑dense set of points of small Néron–Tate height must be a translate of an abelian subvariety by a torsion point. In arithmetic dynamics the analogous statement replaces the Néron–Tate height by a canonical height attached to a polarized endomorphism f : X → X on a projective variety. The dynamical Bogomolov conjecture predicts that if a subvariety V ⊂ X contains a Zariski‑dense set of points P with canonical height ĥf(P) ≤ ε for arbitrarily small ε > 0, then V must be preperiodic under f; equivalently, some iterate fn(V) is contained in a proper f‑invariant subvariety. The conjecture is known for polarised endomorphisms of abelian varieties (Favre–Gauthier, 2018) and for split rational maps on products of projective lines (Ghioca–Ye, 2020). The proof combines equidistribution of small points with the arithmetic Bézout theorem of Yuan–Zhang. A key technical point is that the canonical height is functorial: ĥf(f(P)) = d·ĥf(P) where d = deg f. This scaling property forces any infinite set of small points to be concentrated on preperiodic subvarieties. Examiners often ask to explain why the conjecture fails if one replaces the canonical height by a naive Weil height: the naive height does not satisfy exact functoriality, so a sequence of points with bounded naive height need not equidistribute to the invariant measure, and counterexamples exist on ℙ1 × ℙ1 with the map (z, w) ↦ (z2, w2).

Uniform Boundedness for Preperiodic Points on Drinfeld Modules

Drinfeld modules provide a function‑field analogue of elliptic curves. Let A = 𝔽q[T] and let φ : A → End𝔽q(t)(𝔾a) be a Drinfeld module of rank r over a finite extension K/𝔽q(T). The torsion points φ[a] for a ∈ A play the role of preperiodic points. The uniform boundedness conjecture in this setting, formulated by Scanlon and proved by Poonen (2005) for rank 2 and by Ghioca–Tucker (2010) in general, states that for fixed rank r and fixed degree D = [K : 𝔽q(T)] there exists a constant B(r, D) such that #φ[a](K) ≤ B for all non‑zero a ∈ A. The proof uses the fact that the a‑torsion submodule is a finite flat group scheme of rank qr deg a and applies the Mordell–Lang conjecture for function fields (Hrushovski) to the semiabelian variety obtained by compactifying the Drinfeld module. A crucial step is the construction of a canonical height ĥφ on the Drinfeld module, which satisfies ĥφ(φa(P)) = |a|·ĥφ(P) where |a| = qdeg a. The Northcott property for this height yields finiteness of points of bounded height, and the uniform bound follows from a careful analysis of the moduli space of Drinfeld modules and the equidistribution of small points on its Berkovich analytification. In examinations, a typical question asks to outline why the bound depends on the degree D but not on the characteristic q: the height is defined using the absolute values of K, which are normalised independently of q, and the moduli height on the Drinfeld modular variety is a Weil height on a projective variety over 𝔽q, so its degree is bounded by D.

Worked Example: Bounding Torsion on a Rank‑2 Drinfeld Module
Let φ be a rank‑2 Drinfeld module over a field K of degree 3 over 𝔽5(T). Show that the number of K‑rational torsion points of φ is bounded by a constant depending only on the rank and the degree.
1. The canonical height ĥφ on φ satisfies ĥφ(φa(P)) = 5deg a·ĥφ(P). 2. Torsion points have canonical height 0. 3. By the Northcott property, the set of points of height 0 in φ(K) is finite. 4. The uniform boundedness theorem of Ghioca–Tucker gives a constant B(2,3) such that #φ[a](K) ≤ B for all a ≠ 0. 5. Since every torsion point lies in some φ[a], the total number of K‑rational torsion points is at most the sum over a of B(2,3), which is finite and bounded by a constant depending only on rank 2 and degree 3.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a uniform bound in a function‑field dynamical setting, first identify the relevant canonical height and its scaling property. Then invoke the Northcott property to deduce finiteness, and finally cite the appropriate uniform boundedness theorem (Poonen for rank 2, Ghioca–Tucker for general rank). Do not attempt to compute the constant explicitly; the exam only requires the logical structure of the argument.

Arithmetic Dynamics on Semiabelian Varieties and the Dynamical Mordell‑Lang Conjecture

A semiabelian variety G is an extension of an abelian variety A by a torus T ≅ 𝔾mr. Endomorphisms of G that respect the group structure give rise to dynamical systems where the canonical height is the sum of the Néron–Tate height on A and the standard logarithmic height on T. The dynamical Mordell‑Lang conjecture for semiabelian varieties, proved by Bell–Ghioca–Tucker (2010) using model‑theoretic methods, states: let φ : G → G be a regular self‑map defined over a field of characteristic 0, let V ⊂ G be a subvariety, and let α ∈ G. Then the set {n ∈ ℕ : φn(α) ∈ V} is a finite union of arithmetic progressions. The proof reduces to the classical Mordell‑Lang conjecture for semiabelian varieties (McQuillan) by considering the graph of the orbit and applying the Hrushovski–Zilber trichotomy. A key ingredient is the existence of a canonical height ĥφ on G satisfying ĥφ(φ(P)) = d·ĥφ(P) where d is the dynamical degree. When φ is a group endomorphism, d equals the algebraic degree of φ on the Lie algebra. The conjecture fails in positive characteristic without additional hypotheses (e.g., φ must be a Frobenius‑twisted endomorphism). Examiners frequently test the distinction between the dynamical Mordell‑Lang statement for arbitrary rational maps (open) and the proven case for group endomorphisms on semiabelian varieties. A common trap is to assume the result holds for all rational maps on ℙ1; the known results there require the map to be a morphism and the subvariety to be a curve.

Effective Height Bounds via the Subspace Theorem in Dynamical Systems

The Subspace Theorem of Schmidt (1972) and its quantitative refinements by Evertse–Schlickewei provide a powerful tool for bounding the number of solutions to S‑unit equations. In arithmetic dynamics, one applies it to the equation fn(x) = y where x, y are S‑integral points on ℙ1. The method, developed by Corvaja–Zannier (2002) and extended by Levin (2008), yields effective upper bounds for the canonical height of preperiodic points in terms of the degree of the map, the size of the set S, and the degree of the number field. Concretely, for a rational map f ∈ K(z) of degree d ≥ 2, if P ∈ ℙ1(K) is preperiodic then ĥf(P) = 0, and the Subspace Theorem gives an explicit bound for the logarithmic height h(P) in terms of d, [K : ℚ], and the number of places in S. The proof writes the condition that the orbit of P is finite as an S‑unit equation in the coordinates of the iterates, then applies the quantitative Subspace Theorem to bound the height of the solutions. This approach is effective but the constants are typically enormous (tower‑exponential in the degree). Recent work by Besson–Dujardin (2022) improves the dependence on d using the theory of canonical local heights and the arithmetic intersection theory of Yuan–Zhang. In examinations, a typical question asks to sketch the reduction of the preperiodic point problem to an S‑unit equation: (1) choose a coordinate z on ℙ1; (2) write the minimal polynomial of fn(P) over K; (3) express the equality of two iterates as a relation among S‑units; (4) apply the Subspace Theorem to bound the height of P. Students must remember that the method only works when the S‑unit equation is non‑degenerate; degenerate cases correspond to maps with a critical orbit relation (e.g., Chebyshev polynomials) and require separate treatment.

Zariski Density of Preperiodic Points on Algebraic Tori and the Dynamical Manin‑Mumford

Let T = 𝔾mg be an algebraic torus and let φ : T → T be a dominant endomorphism given by a matrix M ∈ GLg(ℤ) with no eigenvalue a root of unity. The preperiodic points of φ are exactly the torsion points of T, i.e., points whose coordinates are roots of unity. The dynamical Manin‑Mumford conjecture for tori, proved by Zhang (1998) using equidistribution of torsion points on the complex torus (ℂ×)g, states that a subvariety V ⊂ T contains a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points if and only if V is a translate of a subtorus by a torsion point. The proof uses the fact that the canonical height on T is the standard logarithmic height, and the set of points of height 0 is precisely the torsion subgroup. Equidistribution of Galois orbits of torsion points with respect to the Haar measure on the compact torus (S1)g forces any subvariety containing infinitely many torsion points to be a finite union of torsion translates of subtori. The result extends to semiabelian varieties by combining the toric case with the abelian case (Ullmo–Zhang). A subtle point is that the condition “no eigenvalue a root of unity” is necessary: if M has a root of unity eigenvalue, the map has a positive‑dimensional subtorus of periodic points, and the conclusion fails (e.g., the map (x, y) ↦ (x2, y) on 𝔾m2 has the whole subtorus {1} × 𝔾m fixed). In exams, a frequent question asks to prove that the set of preperiodic points of a toral endomorphism is exactly the torsion subgroup, and then to deduce the dynamical Manin‑Mumford statement using the equidistribution theorem of Bilu (1997) for sequences of points of small height on 𝔾m. The key step is to show that if a subvariety contains infinitely many torsion points, its Zariski closure is a finite union of torsion translates of subtori, which follows from the structure theorem for algebraic subgroups of 𝔾mg.

  • Assuming the uniform boundedness theorem for Drinfeld modules holds without the hypothesis that the rank is fixed; the bound depends on the rank.
  • Applying the dynamical Mordell‑Lang conjecture to arbitrary rational maps on ℙ1; it is proved only for group endomorphisms on semiabelian varieties.
  • Using the Subspace Theorem without checking non‑degeneracy of the associated S‑unit equation; degenerate cases give infinite families of solutions.
  • Overlooking the eigenvalue condition in the toral dynamical Manin‑Mumford theorem; a root‑of‑unity eigenvalue produces a positive‑dimensional fixed subtorus and invalidates the conclusion.
  • Treating the canonical height on a semiabelian variety as the sum of the Néron–Tate height and the logarithmic height without verifying that the endomorphism respects the group extension structure.
  • Stating that the effective bounds from the Subspace Theorem are polynomial in the degree; they are typically tower‑exponential.

Canonical Height on Abelian Varieties and the Néron–Tate Pairing

The canonical height on an abelian variety A defined over a number field K is the unique quadratic form ĥ : A(K) → ℝ≥0 that satisfies ĥ(nP) = n²ĥ(P) for all integers n and differs from any naive Weil height by a bounded function. Its construction uses the Néron–Tate pairing ⟨·,·⟩ : A(K) × Â(K) → ℝ, where Â is the dual abelian variety, and the height of a point P is ĥ(P) = ⟨P,P⟩. The pairing is bilinear, symmetric, and positive definite modulo torsion, which makes the canonical height a genuine quadratic form on the Mordell–Weil group A(K)/A(K)ₜₒᵣₛ. Functoriality holds: for any homomorphism φ : A → B of abelian varieties, ĥ_B(φ(P)) = ĥ_A(P) when φ is an isogeny of degree 1, and more generally ĥ_B(φ(P)) = (deg φ) ĥ_A(P) up to a bounded error that vanishes after passing to the canonical height. The Northcott property follows immediately: for any bound B there are only finitely many points P ∈ A(K) with ĥ(P) ≤ B, because the Mordell–Weil group is finitely generated and the height is a positive definite quadratic form on the free part.

Explicit formulas for the canonical height are available via the decomposition into local heights. For each place v of K one chooses a symmetric line bundle L on A representing the principal polarization, equips L with a v‑adic metric, and defines a local height λ_v(P) = −log‖s(P)‖_v for a rational section s of L. The sum over all places converges to a function differing from the naive height by O(1), and the limit ĥ(P) = limn→∞ h( nP ) / n² exists and equals the sum of the local canonical heights. The local canonical height λ̂_v satisfies the functional equation λ̂_v(φ(P)) = (deg φ) λ̂_v(P) for any isogeny φ, which is the key to proving the global functional equation. In examinations, a typical question asks to show that the canonical height is independent of the choice of symmetric ample line bundle: one uses the fact that any two such bundles differ by a translation-invariant line bundle, whose associated height is bounded on A(K).

The Néron–Tate pairing also controls the behavior of heights under the group law. For P,Q ∈ A(K) one has the parallelogram law ĥ(P+Q) + ĥ(P−Q) = 2ĥ(P) + 2ĥ(Q). This identity is equivalent to the bilinearity of the pairing and is often used to prove that the canonical height is a quadratic form. A common examiner trap is to confuse the canonical height on an abelian variety with the canonical height attached to a polarized dynamical system (f,L) on a projective variety; the former comes from a group structure, the latter from iteration of a morphism. The two notions coincide when the morphism is the multiplication-by‑m map on an abelian variety, but they diverge for general endomorphisms that are not group homomorphisms.

Dynamical Uniform Boundedness for Polynomial Maps on the Projective Line

The Morton–Silverman uniform boundedness conjecture predicts that for a fixed degree d ≥ 2 there exists a constant C(d) such that for any number field K and any morphism f : ℙ¹ → ℙ¹ of degree d defined over K, the number of K‑rational preperiodic points of f is bounded by C(d) independently of K and of the coefficients of f. The conjecture is known for d = 2 (quadratic polynomials) by work of Morton and Silverman (1994) and later refined by Flynn, Poonen, and Schaefer (1997) who gave an explicit bound of 9 preperiodic points over ℚ. For higher degrees the best unconditional results are due to Benedetto (2005) who proved a bound depending on the degree and the number of primes of bad reduction, and to Faber and Granville (2001) who showed that the number of rational preperiodic points for a polynomial of degree d over ℚ is at most d² + 1 under a technical hypothesis on the critical orbit. The proof strategy typically reduces the problem to an S‑unit equation by writing the condition fⁿ(P) = fᵐ(P) as a relation among S‑units in the coordinates of the iterates, then applying the quantitative Subspace Theorem. The constants obtained are effective but tower‑exponential in d, reflecting the ineffectivity of the Subspace Theorem.

A crucial distinction is between uniform boundedness for all number fields and boundedness for a fixed field. Over a fixed number field K, Northcott’s theorem already gives finiteness of preperiodic points for each individual map, but the bound may depend on the map. Uniform boundedness asks for a bound that works simultaneously for all maps of a given degree over all number fields of bounded degree. The conjecture is known to fail if one allows the degree of the number field to grow without bound: for each d there exist maps with arbitrarily many preperiodic points over fields of sufficiently large degree, constructed by taking Chebyshev polynomials or power maps and extending the base field to contain many roots of unity. In examinations, a frequent question asks to explain why the presence of a critical orbit relation (e.g., f(c) = c for a critical point c) leads to degenerate S‑unit equations and forces a separate treatment; the answer is that the associated S‑unit equation becomes identically satisfied, producing infinite families of solutions that the Subspace Theorem cannot control.

Recent progress uses the theory of canonical local heights and the arithmetic intersection theory of Yuan–Zhang to improve the dependence on d. Besson and Dujardin (2022) showed that for polynomial maps with good reduction outside a fixed finite set of places, the number of preperiodic points is bounded by a constant polynomial in d. Their method replaces the global Subspace Theorem by a local equidistribution argument on the Berkovich projective line, exploiting the fact that the canonical measure of a polynomial map is the equilibrium measure of its filled Julia set. A typical exam problem asks to sketch the reduction of the preperiodic point problem to an S‑unit equation: choose a coordinate z on ℙ¹, write the minimal polynomial of fⁿ(P) over K, express the equality of two iterates as a relation among S‑units, and apply the Subspace Theorem to bound the height of P. Students must remember to check non‑degeneracy of the S‑unit equation, which fails exactly when the map has a critical orbit relation.

Worked Example: Bounding Preperiodic Points for Quadratic Polynomials
Let f(z) = z² + c with c ∈ ℚ. Show that f has at most 9 rational preperiodic points.
The result of Flynn, Poonen, and Schaefer (1997) classifies all possible rational preperiodic portraits for quadratic polynomials over ℚ. One first notes that any rational preperiodic point must be either a fixed point, a point of period 2, or eventually mapped into a cycle of length 1 or 2. The fixed points satisfy z² + c = z, giving at most 2 rational solutions. Points of period 2 satisfy f²(z) = z but f(z) ≠ z; the equation f²(z) − z factors as (z² + c − z)·(z² + z + c + 1). The second factor yields at most 2 rational points. Preperiodic points that are not periodic must map into one of these cycles after finitely many steps; each step is a quadratic equation, so the total number of rational preimages of the at most 4 periodic points is bounded by 4·2 = 8. Adding the periodic points themselves gives at most 12, but a careful analysis of the possible trees of preimages shows that the maximum is actually 9, attained for c = −29/16 where the map has a rational 2‑cycle and a full binary tree of preimages of depth 1. The proof uses the fact that the discriminant of each quadratic preimage equation must be a rational square, which restricts the possible c values to a finite list.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a bound on preperiodic points, first identify whether the map is a polynomial or a general rational map, and whether the base field is fixed or allowed to vary. For polynomials over ℚ, recall the explicit bound 9 for degree 2 and the general d² + 1 bound under the critical orbit hypothesis. For uniform boundedness over all number fields, state the Morton–Silverman conjecture and mention that it is open for d ≥ 3. Always check non‑degeneracy of the associated S‑unit equation; if the map has a critical point that is periodic, the Subspace Theorem argument breaks down and the problem requires a separate classification.

Arithmetic Equidistribution of Small Points and the Yuan–Zhang Theorem

The Yuan–Zhang equidistribution theorem (2017) generalizes the classical equidistribution of points of small height on abelian varieties (Ullmo, Zhang 1998) to arbitrary polarized dynamical systems (X, f, L) over a number field. Let f : X → X be a morphism of degree d ≥ 2 on a projective variety X, and let L be an ample line bundle such that f*L ≅ L⊗d. The canonical height ĥ_f,L is defined as the limit of h_L(fⁿ(P))/dⁿ. A sequence of algebraic points {P_n} with ĥ_f,L(P_n) → 0 is called a sequence of small points. The theorem asserts that the Galois orbits of such a sequence become equidistributed with respect to the canonical measure μ_f = (dd^c ĥ_f,L)dim X on the Berkovich analytification Xan_v for every place v of the base field. In particular, for any continuous function φ on Xan_v, the average (1/[K(P_n):K]) Σσ φ(σ(P_n)) converges to ∫ φ dμ_f. The proof combines the arithmetic intersection theory of Gillet–Soulé with the non‑archimedean potential theory of Chambert‑Loir and Thuillier, and uses the fact that the canonical height is a continuous function on the Berkovich space.

A key application is the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture, which predicts that a subvariety V ⊂ X contains a Zariski‑dense set of small points if and only if V is preperiodic (i.e., fⁿ(V) = V for some n > 0). Yuan and Zhang proved this for étale morphisms and for polarizable endomorphisms on abelian varieties. The equidistribution theorem forces any subvariety containing infinitely many small points to have its generic point equidistributed, which by the uniqueness of the canonical measure implies that the subvariety must be invariant under f up to iteration. In examinations, a typical question asks to deduce the dynamical Manin–Mumford statement for a torus from the equidistribution of torsion points: the canonical height on 𝔾_m^g is the standard logarithmic height, torsion points have height 0, and Bilu’s equidistribution theorem (1997) shows that their Galois orbits equidistribute to the Haar measure on the compact torus (S¹)^g; any subvariety containing infinitely many torsion points must therefore be a finite union of torsion translates of subtori.

The theorem also has a p‑adic version: for a non‑archimedean place v, the canonical measure μ_f,v is supported on the Berkovich Julia set of f, and equidistribution holds for any sequence of points with ĥ_f,L(P_n) → 0 that are defined over extensions of the completion K_v. This p‑adic equidistribution is the main tool in the proof of the p‑adic uniform boundedness results of Benedetto and of the dynamical André–Oort conjecture for Drinfeld modules. A common examiner trap is to confuse the canonical measure μ_f with the measure of maximal entropy on the complex Julia set; they coincide for polynomial maps on ℙ¹ but differ in higher dimension or for non‑polynomial maps. Students should remember that the canonical measure is defined as the wedge product of the curvature form of the canonical metric on L, and it is functorial under morphisms that respect the polarization.

The Dynamical Lehmer Problem and Lower Bounds for Canonical Heights

The dynamical Lehmer problem asks whether there exists a constant c(f) > 0 depending only on the polarized dynamical system (X, f, L) such that for every non‑preperiodic point P ∈ X(K̄) one has ĥ_f,L(P) ≥ c(f) / [K(P):K]. This is the dynamical analogue of Lehmer’s conjecture on Mahler measures, which predicts a positive lower bound for the logarithmic height of non‑torsion points on algebraic tori. For abelian varieties, the Néron–Tate height satisfies the classical Lehmer bound (proved by David and Hindry for abelian varieties of dimension ≥ 2, and by Silverman for elliptic curves with complex multiplication). In the dynamical setting, the problem is wide open for general rational maps on ℙ¹. The best known results are due to Silverman (2007) who proved a lower bound of the form ĥ_f(P) ≥ c / (ddeg f · [K(P):K]) for polynomial maps with a critical point that is not preperiodic, and to Ingram (2012) who obtained a bound depending on the number of primes of bad reduction. The constant c(f) is expected to be related to the minimal dynamical degree of f and to the geometry of the Julia set.

A major obstacle is the existence of maps with arbitrarily small positive canonical heights. For example, the family f_c(z) = z² + c contains parameters c for which the critical point 0 is strictly preperiodic (Misiurewicz parameters); near such parameters the canonical height of the critical value can be made arbitrarily small while the point remains non‑preperiodic. This shows that any uniform lower bound must depend on the map, not just on the degree. The dynamical Lehmer conjecture is known to hold for polarized endomorphisms of abelian varieties (where it reduces to the classical Lehmer problem) and for certain product maps on tori. In examinations, a typical question asks to explain why the classical Lehmer conjecture implies the dynamical Lehmer conjecture for the multiplication‑by‑m map on an abelian variety: the canonical height for [m] is m² times the Néron–Tate height, so a lower bound for the Néron–Tate height translates directly into a lower bound for the dynamical height.

Recent work by Ghioca, Tucker, and Zieve (2020) connects the dynamical Lehmer problem to the existence of primitive prime divisors in orbits. They show that if a point P has a primitive prime divisor for all sufficiently large iterates, then its canonical height cannot be too small relative to its degree. This gives a new approach to lower bounds via Zsigmondy‑type theorems for dynamical sequences. A common examiner trap is to assume that the dynamical Lehmer constant c(f) can be taken independent of the number field K; the conjecture explicitly allows the bound to depend on the degree of the field of definition, and counterexamples over varying fields show that a field‑independent bound is false in general. Students should also distinguish between the dynamical Lehmer problem (lower bound for canonical height) and the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture (characterization of subvarieties with dense small points); the former is a quantitative statement about individual points, the latter a qualitative statement about subvarieties.

p‑Adic Uniform Boundedness and the Morton–Silverman Conjecture

The p‑adic analogue of the Morton–Silverman conjecture asks whether, for a fixed prime p and a fixed degree d ≥ 2, there exists a constant C_p(d) such that any rational map f ∈ ℚ_p(z) of degree d has at most C_p(d) preperiodic points in ℙ¹(ℚ_p). Benedetto (2001) proved this for polynomial maps with good reduction, showing that the number of ℚ_p‑rational preperiodic points is bounded by a constant depending only on d and the residue characteristic p. The proof uses the structure of the Berkovich projective line and the fact that the filled Julia set of a polynomial with good reduction is a finite union of closed disks; preperiodic points must lie in the intersection of the Julia set with ℚ_p, which is a finite set. For rational maps with bad reduction, the bound may depend on the number of primes of bad reduction, and the best known results are due to Rivera‑Letelier (2003) who gave a bound polynomial in d for maps with at most one prime of bad reduction.

The p‑adic uniform boundedness conjecture is known to fail if one allows the degree of the base field extension to grow: for each d there exist maps over finite extensions of ℚ_p with arbitrarily many preperiodic points, constructed by taking power maps z ↦ z^d and extending the field to contain many roots of unity. This mirrors the global situation where uniform boundedness over all number fields fails without a bound on the field degree. In examinations, a frequent question asks to explain why good reduction is essential for a uniform bound independent of the map: good reduction implies that the reduction map on the Berkovich line is a contraction on the Julia set, forcing preperiodic points to lie in a bounded number of residue classes. When reduction is bad, the Julia set can be a Cantor set of positive Haar measure, allowing many more rational preperiodic points.

Recent advances by Favre and Rivera‑Letelier (2010) use the theory of canonical measures on the Berkovich line to prove equidistribution of preperiodic points for maps with good reduction, and deduce a uniform bound on the number of preperiodic points in any fixed finite extension of ℚ_p. Their method replaces the global Subspace Theorem by a p‑adic version of the Yuan–Zhang equidistribution theorem. A typical exam problem asks to sketch the proof that a polynomial with good reduction has at most d+1 preperiodic points in the residue field: the reduction map sends preperiodic points to preperiodic points of the reduced map over the finite residue field, and a polynomial of degree d over a finite field has at most d+1 preperiodic points because the functional graph of a map on a finite set has at most one cycle per connected component and each component has at most d+1 vertices. Students must remember that this argument only counts points with distinct reductions; points that reduce to the same residue class are handled by a separate analysis of the disks in the Berkovich space.

  • Treating the dynamical Lehmer constant as independent of the number field; the conjecture allows dependence on the field degree.

  • Mixing up the canonical measure μ_f with the measure of maximal entropy; they agree for polynomials on ℙ¹ but differ in general.

Canonical Heights for Correspondences and Multi‑Valued Dynamics

A correspondence on a projective variety X over a number field K is given by a closed subvariety C ⊂ X × X such that both projections π₁, π₂ : C → X are finite and surjective. The associated multi‑valued map sends a point x to the finite set π₂(π₁⁻¹(x)). Canonical heights for such correspondences were constructed by Silverman and later generalized by Kawaguchi and Silverman. One fixes an ample line bundle L on X and a positive integer d such that (π₁, π₂)*L ≅ L⊗ d on C. The canonical height ĥC(x) is then defined as the limit ĥC(x) = limn→∞ d−n hL(Cn(x)), where hL is a Weil height and Cn denotes the n-fold composition of the correspondence. This limit exists and satisfies the functoriality ĥC(C(x)) = d ĥC(x) and the Northcott property: for any bound B and degree D, the set {x ∈ X(K̄) : ĥC(x) ≤ B, [K(x) : K] ≤ D} is finite. Important examples include Hecke correspondences Tp on modular curves X₀(N), where the canonical height coincides with the Petersson norm of the associated modular form, and Lattès correspondences arising from endomorphisms of elliptic curves. The theory also yields an equidistribution theorem for sequences of points of small canonical height with respect to the canonical measure μC = limn→∞ d−n (π₂)_*(π₁^* ωLdim X), where ωL is a smooth (1,1)-form representing c₁(L). A key technical point is that the correspondence must be polarizable, i.e. there exists an ample L with the above scaling property; non‑polarizable correspondences (e.g. certain non‑algebraic multi‑valued maps) do not admit a canonical height with good functoriality.

Dynamical Zilber–Pink Conjecture for Mixed Shimura Varieties

The classical Zilber–Pink conjecture predicts that for a mixed Shimura variety S and a subvariety V ⊂ S, the intersection of V with the union of all proper special subvarieties of S is not Zariski dense in V unless V itself is contained in a proper special subvariety. The dynamical analogue replaces special subvarieties by preperiodic subvarieties for a polarized dynamical system (f, L) on S. A subvariety Y ⊂ S is called preperiodic if its forward orbit under f is finite. The dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture then asserts: let (f, L) be a polarized dynamical system on a mixed Shimura variety S defined over a number field, and let V ⊂ S be an irreducible subvariety. If the set of points in V that lie on a preperiodic subvariety of codimension at least one is Zariski dense in V, then V itself is preperiodic. This conjecture simultaneously generalizes the dynamical André–Oort conjecture (where preperiodic subvarieties are replaced by special subvarieties) and the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture (where S is an abelian variety and f is multiplication by an integer). Recent progress includes work of Gao, Habegger, and others who proved the conjecture for split semiabelian varieties and for certain families of abelian varieties using o‑minimality and the Pila–Wilkie counting theorem. The proof strategy typically combines a height inequality (showing that points on preperiodic subvarieties have small canonical height) with an equidistribution argument (Yuan–Zhang) to force the subvariety to be preperiodic. A crucial technical hypothesis is that the dynamical system is polarized by an ample line bundle L with f*L ≅ L⊗ d, d > 1, which guarantees the existence of a canonical height with the Northcott property. Without polarization, the set of preperiodic points can be too large (e.g. for translations on abelian varieties) and the conjecture fails.

Worked Example: Dynamical Zilber–Pink Implies Dynamical André–Oort
Explain why the dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture for a mixed Shimura variety S with a polarized dynamical system (f, L) implies the dynamical André–Oort conjecture for the same variety.
In the dynamical André–Oort setting one considers special subvarieties of S (Shimura subvarieties) and asks whether a subvariety V containing a Zariski‑dense set of special points must be special. Every special subvariety is preperiodic for any polarized dynamical system that respects the Shimura structure (e.g. a Hecke correspondence), because Hecke correspondences map special subvarieties to special subvarieties and the collection of special subvarieties is finite up to Hecke orbit. Hence the set of points lying on a proper special subvariety is a subset of the set of points lying on a proper preperiodic subvariety. If V contains a Zariski‑dense set of special points, it certainly contains a Zariski‑dense set of points on proper preperiodic subvarieties. By the dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture, V must then be preperiodic. In the context of mixed Shimura varieties, a preperiodic subvariety that is also a Shimura subvariety is necessarily special. Therefore V is special, which is exactly the conclusion of the dynamical André–Oort conjecture.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to relate two dynamical conjectures, identify the inclusion of the relevant collections of subvarieties (special ⊂ preperiodic, torsion ⊂ preperiodic, etc.). State clearly which conjecture assumes the larger collection and deduce the implication. Remember that polarization (f*L ≅ L⊗ d, d>1) is the standard hypothesis that guarantees a canonical height with Northcott; without it the conjectures are false in general.

Arithmetic Dynamics over Function Fields: Positive Characteristic and the Function Field Mordell Conjecture

Let K = F_q(C) be the function field of a smooth projective curve C over a finite field F_q. A rational map f : ℙ¹ → ℙ¹ defined over K gives a dynamical system on the Berkovich analytic space associated to each place of K. Heights are defined via degrees of divisors: for a point x ∈ ℙ¹(K) represented by homogeneous coordinates [x₀ : x₁] with x₀, x₁ ∈ F_q[C] coprime, the naive height is h(x) = max{deg(x₀), deg(x₁)}. The canonical height ĥ_f(x) = limn→∞ d−n h(fⁿ(x)) exists and satisfies the same functoriality and Northcott properties as in the number field case. The function field Mordell conjecture, proved independently by Manin and Grauert in the 1960s, states that a curve of genus ≥ 2 over K has only finitely many K-rational points unless it is isotrivial (i.e. becomes constant after a finite base change). In dynamics, the analogous statement is the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture in positive characteristic, which asks: given a semiabelian variety G over K, an endomorphism Φ : G → G, a subvariety V ⊂ G, and a point α ∈ G(K), the set {n ∈ ℕ : Φⁿ(α) ∈ V} is a finite union of arithmetic progressions. This was proved by Hrushovski, Moosa, and Scanlon using model‑theoretic methods (the theory of difference fields) and later by Ghioca and Tucker using p‑adic analytic techniques. A major difference from characteristic zero is the presence of the Frobenius endomorphism, which creates new periodic phenomena (e.g. the map x ↦ x^p on 𝔾_m has every point preperiodic). Uniform boundedness for preperiodic points in families over function fields has been established by Baker and DeMarco for polynomial maps, and by Ghioca, Tucker, and Zieve for more general rational maps, using the fact that the moduli space of rational maps of degree d is of general type for large d, so the set of maps with many preperiodic points is not Zariski dense.

Critical Orbit Relations, Exceptional Maps, and the Classification of Lattès, Chebyshev, and Power Maps

A rational map f ∈ K(z) of degree d ≥ 2 is called exceptional (or post‑critically finite) if its postcritical set PC(f) = ⋃n≥1 fⁿ(Crit(f)) is finite, where Crit(f) is the set of critical points. Over ℂ, a theorem of Thurston (rigidity) implies that every exceptional map is either a Lattès map, a Chebyshev polynomial (up to conjugation), or a power map z ↦ z±d. Lattès maps arise from endomorphisms of an elliptic curve E: if φ : E → E is an isogeny and π : E → ℙ¹ is the quotient by the involution x ↦ −x, then f = π ∘ φ ∘ π⁻¹ is a rational map on ℙ¹ with degree (deg φ)². Chebyshev polynomials T_d(z) satisfy T_d(z + z⁻¹) = z^d + z⁻ᵈ and are conjugate to z ↦ z^d on ℙ¹ via the map z ↦ z + z⁻¹. Power maps z ↦ z^d are the simplest examples. These three families are precisely the maps for which the canonical height ĥ_f can vanish on an infinite set of algebraic points (the torsion points on E for Lattès, roots of unity for Chebyshev and power maps). Consequently, they are the only maps that can violate the dynamical Lehmer lower bound ĥ_f(x) ≥ c(f) / [K(x) : K] for a constant c(f) > 0 independent of x. In the classification of maps with a critical orbit relation (a polynomial relation among the critical orbits), the same three families appear, together with a few sporadic examples in low degree. The proof uses the fact that a critical orbit relation forces the map to be post‑critically finite, and then Thurston's rigidity theorem classifies all such maps over ℂ. Over number fields, the same classification holds after base change to ℂ, and the arithmetic properties (canonical height, Galois action on preperiodic points) are governed by the underlying group structure (elliptic curve, multiplicative group). This classification underpins many results: the uniform boundedness conjecture is known for all maps except possibly these exceptional families; the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture holds for non‑exceptional maps; and the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture reduces to the classical Manin–Mumford for the associated group.

Dynamical Manin–Mumford Conjecture for Subvarieties of Abelian Varieties

Let A be an abelian variety over a number field K, equipped with a polarized dynamical system given by the multiplication‑by‑m map [m] : A → A for some integer m ≥ 2, and an ample symmetric line bundle L with [m]*L ≅ L⊗ m². The preperiodic points of [m] are exactly the torsion points of A. The dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for A predicts: if V ⊂ A is an irreducible subvariety that contains a Zariski‑dense set of torsion points, then V is a translate of an abelian subvariety by a torsion point. This is precisely the classical Manin–Mumford conjecture (proved by Raynaud), but the dynamical formulation emphasizes that the same statement holds for any polarized dynamical system on A whose preperiodic points are the torsion points (e.g. any isogeny with no non‑trivial fixed abelian subvariety). For more general polarized dynamical systems (f, L) on A where f is not a group endomorphism, the set of preperiodic points is not a group, and the conjecture becomes: if V contains a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points, then V is preperiodic. This stronger statement is known as the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for abelian varieties. Partial results include work of Zhang (using equidistribution of small points), Ghioca and Tucker (using p‑adic analytic methods), and Xie (using the arithmetic Bézout theorem). The proof strategy typically combines the Yuan–Zhang equidistribution theorem (preperiodic points equidistribute with respect to the canonical measure μ_f) with a Diophantine approximation argument: if V contains many preperiodic points, the restriction of μ_f to V must be the canonical measure of a preperiodic subvariety, forcing V itself to be preperiodic. A key technical condition is that f is polarized by an ample line bundle with f*L ≅ L⊗ d, d > 1, which ensures the canonical height has the Northcott property and the canonical measure is non‑zero. Without polarization, counterexamples exist (e.g. translations on A have every point preperiodic).

  • Confusing the canonical height for a correspondence with the height for its underlying graph; the scaling factor d must come from a polarizing line bundle, not from the degree of the projections.
  • Assuming the dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture holds for all dynamical systems; it requires a polarized system on a mixed Shimura variety and fails for non‑polarized maps such as translations.
  • Overlooking the role of the Frobenius endomorphism in positive characteristic; it creates infinite preperiodic sets (e.g. x ↦ x^p) that violate uniform boundedness unless the map is isotrivial.
  • Treating Lattès, Chebyshev, and power maps as generic examples; they are exactly the exceptional (post‑critically finite) maps and are the only ones where the dynamical Lehmer lower bound can fail.
  • Believing the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for abelian varieties is identical to the classical Manin–Mumford; the dynamical version applies to any polarized system, not only multiplication‑by‑m, and its proof uses equidistribution rather than Raynaud's theorem.
  • Neglecting the polarization hypothesis f*L ≅ L⊗ d (d>1) when invoking equidistribution theorems; without it the canonical measure may be zero and the Northcott property fails.
  • Mixing up the function field Mordell conjecture (finiteness of rational points on curves of genus ≥2) with the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture (structure of intersections of orbits with subvarieties); they are distinct statements, though both hold in positive characteristic.

The Dynamical Lang–Vojta Conjecture and Integral Points in Orbits

The classical Lang–Vojta conjecture predicts that for a smooth projective variety X over a number field K, a divisor D with normal crossings, and an ample line bundle L, the set of S-integral points on X D is not Zariski dense unless X D contains a rational curve. In arithmetic dynamics one replaces the ambient variety by the forward orbit Of(α) = {fn(α) : n ≥ 0} of a rational map f : PN → PN defined over K. The dynamical Lang–Vojta conjecture asserts that if f is polarised by an ample line bundle L with f*L ≅ Ld, d > 1, and α is a wandering point (i.e. its orbit is infinite), then for any proper closed subvariety V ⊂ PN the set of indices n such that fn(α) ∈ V is finite unless V is preperiodic. Equivalently, the orbit meets any non‑preperiodic subvariety in only finitely many points.

This statement strengthens the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, which only concerns intersections with a fixed subvariety defined over K. The Lang–Vojta formulation also incorporates an integral‑point condition: one fixes a finite set of places S and asks for points of the orbit that are S-integral with respect to a divisor D disjoint from the orbit. Silverman proved that for morphisms f : P1 → P1 of degree ≥ 2, if α is not preperiodic then the set of S-integral points in the orbit is finite. The proof uses the canonical height ĥf and the fact that ĥf(fn(α)) = dnĥf(α) grows exponentially, while the height of an S-integral point relative to D is bounded by a constant depending only on S and D. In higher dimension the conjecture remains open in general. Partial results exist for split polynomial maps f(x₁,…,xN) = (f₁(x₁),…,fN(xN)) where each fi is a one‑variable polynomial of degree ≥ 2; here the orbit is a product of one‑dimensional orbits and the problem reduces to the known one‑dimensional case via a sum‑of‑heights argument.

A key technical tool is the Subspace Theorem in its quantitative form (Evertse–Schlickewei). It allows one to bound the number of solutions to linear equations in S-units, which translates into a bound for the number of indices n with fn(α) lying in a given hyperplane. For general subvarieties one uses a combination of the Subspace Theorem with the theory of canonical heights on projective varieties (Call–Silverman) and the equidistribution of small points (Yuan–Zhang). The main obstruction to a full proof is the lack of a uniform lower bound for the canonical height of wandering points — the dynamical Lehmer problem — which would give a quantitative gap between the exponential growth of ĥf(fn(α)) and the height of points on a fixed subvariety.

If the dynamical Lang–Vojta conjecture holds, it implies a strong form of the uniform boundedness conjecture for integral points: the number of S-integral points in any orbit of a polarised morphism is bounded by a constant depending only on the degree of f, the dimension N, the degree of D, and the size of S. This would unify many finiteness results in Diophantine geometry under a single dynamical principle.

The Dynamical abc Conjecture and Height Inequalities for Orbits

The classical abc conjecture of Masser–Oesterlé states that for coprime integers a, b, c with a + b = c, the radical rad(abc) is usually not much smaller than max{|a|,|b|,|c|}. In arithmetic dynamics an analogous statement compares the canonical height of a point with the height of its image under a rational map. Let f : PN → PN be a morphism of degree d ≥ 2 defined over a number field K, polarised by an ample line bundle L with f*L ≅ Ld. For a point P ∈ PN(K) define the dynamical radical radf(P) as the product over all places v of the maximum of the local heights of the coordinates of P, taken with respect to a fixed adelic metric on L. The dynamical abc conjecture (Silverman, 2007) predicts that for any ε > 0 there exists a constant C(ε, f) such that for all P ∈ PN(K) with infinite orbit,

ĥf(f(P)) ≤ (1 + ε) ĥf(P) + C(ε, f) · log radf(P).

Equivalently, the canonical height of the image is at most the canonical height of the point plus a small multiple of the logarithmic radical. This inequality is a dynamical analogue of the abc bound c ≤ rad(abc)1+ε. When f is the multiplication‑by‑m map on an elliptic curve, the conjecture reduces to the classical Szpiro conjecture for elliptic curves, which is known to be equivalent to abc.

The conjecture has several important consequences. First, it implies a strong form of the dynamical Lehmer lower bound: there exists a constant c(f) > 0 such that ĥf(P) ≥ c(f) for every wandering point P. Indeed, if ĥf(P) were arbitrarily small, applying the abc inequality repeatedly along the orbit would force radf(fn(P)) to grow slower than dn, contradicting the Northcott property. Second, it yields effective bounds for the number of preperiodic points in families: if a family of polarised maps has a section with small canonical height, the abc inequality forces the section to be preperiodic, giving a uniform bound on the number of parameters for which the section is preperiodic. Third, it provides a new approach to the uniform boundedness conjecture for rational maps on P1: the number of preperiodic points of a degree‑d map is bounded by a constant depending only on d and the degree of the field of definition.

Partial results are known in special cases. For power maps f(x) = xd on P1, the dynamical abc conjecture is equivalent to the classical abc conjecture for the field K. For Chebyshev maps and Lattès maps, the conjecture follows from the known lower bounds for canonical heights on tori and elliptic curves (David–Hindry). For general polynomial maps of degree ≥ 2, the best unconditional result is a weak inequality with an extra logarithmic factor, proved by Ingram using the Subspace Theorem. The full conjecture remains open and is considered one of the central problems linking Diophantine approximation with arithmetic dynamics.

Equidistribution of Small Points on Berkovich Spaces and the Chambert‑Loir Measure

Over a non‑archimedean field the classical complex equidistribution theorems of Bilu, Szpiro–Ullmo–Zhang, and Yuan–Zhang have analogues on Berkovich analytic spaces. Let K be a field complete with respect to a non‑trivial non‑archimedean absolute value, and let f : PNK → PNK be a polarised morphism of degree d ≥ 2 with f*L ≅ Ld. The Berkovich projective space PN,anK is a compact, path‑connected Hausdorff space containing PN(K) as a dense subset. Chambert‑Loir constructed a canonical probability measure μf on PN,anK satisfying f*μf = d·μf and f*μf = μf. This measure is the non‑archimedean counterpart of the equilibrium measure on ℂ.

The equidistribution theorem (Favre–Rivera‑Letelier for N = 1; Chambert‑Loir, Thuillier, Yuan for general N) states: if {Pn} is a sequence of distinct algebraic points in PN(Kalg) such that the canonical heights ĥf(Pn) → 0, then the Galois orbits of Pn equidistribute with respect to μf. In other words, for any continuous function φ on PN,anK,

(1 / [K(Pn) : K]) Σσ φ(σ(Pn)) → ∫ φ dμf.

The proof uses the theory of adelic metrics on line bundles over Berkovich spaces and the fact that the canonical height can be expressed as an integral of the Green function against the measure. A crucial ingredient is the continuity of the Green function on the Berkovich space, which follows from the polarisation hypothesis. Without polarisation the canonical measure may be zero and equidistribution fails (e.g. translations on an abelian variety).

Applications include a non‑archimedean proof of the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for tori (Ghioca–Tucker) and a new approach to the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture: if a subvariety V contains a Zariski‑dense set of small points, the restriction of μf to the Berkovich analytification of V must be the canonical measure of a preperiodic subvariety, forcing V to be preperiodic. The theorem also yields effective bounds for the number of preperiodic points in families via the quantitative version of equidistribution (Favre–Rivera‑Letelier). In positive characteristic, the Frobenius endomorphism creates infinite preperiodic sets (e.g. x ↦ xp) that violate uniform boundedness unless the map is isotrivial; the Berkovich framework clarifies this by showing that the canonical measure of the Frobenius map is the Dirac mass at the Gauss point, so equidistribution does not produce a contradiction.

The Dynamical Manin–Mumford Conjecture for Subvarieties of Algebraic Tori

Let G = GmN be an algebraic torus over a number field K, and let f : G → G be a polarised endomorphism given by a matrix M ∈ GLN(ℤ) with all eigenvalues of absolute value > 1 (so f is expanding). The preperiodic points of f are exactly the torsion points of G. The dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for G predicts: if an irreducible subvariety V ⊂ G contains a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points of f, then V is a translate of a subtorus by a torsion point. This is precisely the classical Manin–Mumford theorem for tori (proved by Laurent), but the dynamical formulation emphasises that the same conclusion holds for any expanding endomorphism, not only the multiplication‑by‑m map.

The conjecture is known in full generality for tori. The proof combines the equidistribution of torsion points with respect to the Haar measure on the complex torus (or the Chambert‑Loir measure on the Berkovich torus) and a Diophantine approximation argument using the Subspace Theorem. If V contains many torsion points, the restriction of the canonical measure to V must be the Haar measure of a subtorus, which forces V to be a torsion translate of a subtorus. An alternative proof by Ghioca–Tucker uses p‑adic analytic uniformisation and the fact that the p‑adic logarithm maps torsion points to a lattice of rank equal to the dimension of the subtorus.

A subtle point is that the polarisation condition f*L ≅ Ld with d > 1 is essential. For a translation map τa(x) = a·x on G, every point is preperiodic (indeed periodic of order dividing the order of a), so any subvariety contains a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points without being a torsion translate. The expanding hypothesis excludes translations and guarantees that the canonical height has the Northcott property.

The result extends to semiabelian varieties (extensions of an abelian variety by a torus) under the additional assumption that the dynamical system respects the group structure. For a general polarised endomorphism of a semiabelian variety that is not a group homomorphism, the set of preperiodic points is not a group and the conjecture becomes: if V contains a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points, then V is preperiodic. This stronger statement is open and is the subject of the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for semiabelian varieties.

Uniform Boundedness for Endomorphisms of Projective Space in Higher Dimension

The uniform boundedness conjecture of Morton–Silverman (1994) asserts that for a fixed integer d ≥ 2 and a number field K of degree D, there exists a constant B(d, D) such that any morphism f : PN → PN of degree d defined over K has at most B(d, D) preperiodic points in PN(K). For N = 1 the conjecture is known for many families (polynomials, critically finite maps) but remains open in general. In higher dimension N ≥ 2 the problem is even less understood.

Partial results exist for special classes of maps. For split endomorphisms f(x₁,…,xN) = (f₁(x₁),…,fN(xN)) with each fi a one‑variable polynomial of degree di ≥ 2, the preperiodic points are exactly the product of the preperiodic points of the coordinate maps. Hence the number of K-rational preperiodic points is bounded by the product of the one‑dimensional bounds, giving a uniform bound depending only on the degrees di and D. For monomial maps f(x) = (x₁a₁₁⋯xNa₁N, …, x₁aN1⋯xNaNN) with integer matrix A = (aij) having all eigenvalues of absolute value > 1, the preperiodic points are the torsion points of the torus GmN. The uniform boundedness then follows from the classical result of Laurent on torsion points on subvarieties of tori, giving a bound depending only on N and D.

For general endomorphisms of PN, the best known result is due to Benedetto–Ingram–Jones–Levy (2013): if f is a morphism of degree d ≥ 2 with good reduction outside a finite set of places S, then the number of S-integral preperiodic points is bounded by a constant depending only on d, N, D, and |S|. The proof uses the theory of canonical heights on projective varieties, the Northcott property, and a quantitative version of the Subspace Theorem to bound the number of points of small height. The good‑reduction hypothesis is essential; without it one can construct families with arbitrarily many preperiodic points by varying the coefficients at a place of bad reduction.

A major open problem is whether the uniform bound can be made independent of the field degree D. For N = 1, it is conjectured that the number of preperiodic points of a degree‑d rational map over any number field is bounded by a constant depending only on d (the uniform boundedness conjecture over ℚ). In higher dimension, even the dependence on D is not known to be removable. Recent work of DeMarco–Krieger–Ye shows that for certain families of Lattès maps on P2 the number of preperiodic points grows with the degree of the field, suggesting that any uniform bound must depend on D. The interplay between the geometry of the critical locus, the dynamics on the Berkovich space, and the arithmetic of the field of definition remains a central theme in the search for a general uniform boundedness theorem.

  • Assuming the dynamical abc conjecture holds for all polarised maps; it is known only for exceptional maps (power, Chebyshev, Lattès) and remains open for generic polynomial maps.
  • Neglecting the polarisation hypothesis f*L ≅ L⊗d (d>1) when invoking equidistribution on Berkovich spaces; without it the canonical measure may be zero and the theorem fails.
  • Treating the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for tori as identical to the classical Manin–Mumford; the dynamical version requires the map to be expanding, and the proof uses equidistribution rather than the structure theorem for torsion points alone.
  • Believing uniform boundedness for endomorphisms of PN is known in full generality; it is only proved for split maps, monomial maps, and maps with good reduction outside a fixed set of places.
  • Overlooking the role of the Frobenius endomorphism in positive characteristic; it creates infinite preperiodic sets that violate uniform boundedness unless the map is isotrivial.
  • Mixing up the canonical height for a correspondence with the height for its underlying graph; the scaling factor d must come from a polarising line bundle, not from the degree of the projections.

The advanced topics above illustrate how height functions, equidistribution, and Diophantine approximation intertwine to shape the current frontier of arithmetic dynamics.

Uniform Boundedness for Preperiodic Points on Abelian Varieties

The uniform boundedness conjecture for preperiodic points on abelian varieties asks whether, for a fixed polarised endomorphism f : A → A of degree d ≥ 2 on an abelian variety A of dimension g defined over a number field K of degree D, the number of K-rational preperiodic points can be bounded by a constant depending only on g, d and D. The canonical height ĥ_f attached to the symmetric ample line bundle giving the polarisation satisfies ĥ_f(f(P)) = d·ĥ_f(P) and the Northcott property holds because ĥ_f is a quadratic form on A(K) ⊗ ℝ. A theorem of Hindry and Silverman (2000) proves the conjecture when f is a multiplication-by-m map (i.e. an isogeny) and A has no complex multiplication; the bound then follows from Merel’s uniform bound for torsion points on elliptic curves and its higher‑dimensional analogues due to Parent and others. For a general polarised endomorphism the problem is open. Partial results exist when f is a product of one‑dimensional maps on a split abelian variety A = E₁ × … × E_g; then preperiodic points are products of preperiodic points on each factor and the bound reduces to the one‑dimensional uniform boundedness for each elliptic curve. In the non‑split case, work of Ghioca, Tucker and Zhang (2011) shows that if f has good reduction outside a fixed finite set of places S, the number of S-integral preperiodic points is bounded by a constant depending on g, d, D and |S|. The proof combines the canonical height machinery, the Subspace Theorem and a quantitative version of the Mordell–Weil theorem for the group of S-integral points on A. A major obstacle to removing the good‑reduction hypothesis is the existence of families of polarised endomorphisms with arbitrarily many preperiodic points obtained by varying coefficients at a place of bad reduction, a phenomenon already observed for P¹ by Morton and Silverman.

Equidistribution of Small Points on Projective Varieties over Non‑Archimedean Fields

Let X be a smooth projective variety of dimension N over a complete non‑archimedean field K (e.g. ℚ_p or ℂ_p) and let f : X → X be a polarised endomorphism of degree d > 1 with respect to an ample line bundle L. The associated Berkovich analytic space X^an carries a canonical invariant probability measure μ_f constructed by Chambert‑Loir (2006) as the Monge‑Ampère measure of the canonical metric on L. The equidistribution theorem of Yuan (2008) and Favre–Rivera‑Letelier (2010) states that if {P_n} is a sequence of distinct algebraic points in X(K) with canonical heights ĥ_f(P_n) → 0, then the Galois orbits of P_n equidistribute towards μ_f on X^an. The proof uses the non‑archimedean analogue of the Brolin–Lyubich construction: one shows that the discrete measures supported on the Galois conjugates of P_n converge weakly to μ_f by testing against continuous functions and invoking the product formula for the canonical height. A key technical point is the continuity of the canonical metric on L in the Berkovich topology, which follows from the fact that f is polarised and the associated dynamical Green function is continuous. The theorem fails if the polarisation condition f^*L ≅ L⊗d is dropped; for a non‑polarised map the canonical measure may be zero and small points need not equidistribute. Applications include a dynamical Bogomolov conjecture for subvarieties of X (proved by Ghioca–Yuan in the toric case) and a proof of the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for subvarieties of algebraic tori over non‑archimedean fields. The method also yields effective bounds for the number of points of height below a given threshold via the quantitative equidistribution results of Yuan–Zhang (2017).

The canonical height for f(z)=z2 is ĥ_f(P) = log⁺|P|_p where |·|_p is the p-adic absolute value normalised so that |p|_p = p-1. For a primitive p^n-th root of unity ζp^n we have |ζp^n|_p = 1, hence ĥ_f(ζp^n) = 0. The Galois conjugates of ζp^n are all primitive p^n-th roots of unity; their Berkovich embeddings lie on the circle of radius 1 around the Gauss point. As n → ∞ these circles shrink to the Gauss point in the Berkovich topology. For any continuous function φ on P¹an, the average (1/φ(p^n)) Σ φ(σ(ζp^n)) tends to φ(ζ_G) because the support of the discrete measures converges to {ζ_G}. Thus the Galois orbits equidistribute to the Dirac mass at ζ_G, which is exactly μ_f.

The Dynamical Zsigmondy Theorem for Elliptic Curves and Drinfeld Modules

The classical Zsigmondy theorem asserts that for a sequence a^n − b^n with coprime integers a > b > 0, all but finitely many terms possess a primitive prime divisor — a prime dividing the term but not any earlier term. In arithmetic dynamics the analogue concerns primitive prime divisors in the orbit of a point under a morphism. For an elliptic curve E/K and a point P ∈ E(K) of infinite order, the elliptic divisibility sequence x([n]P) (the x-coordinate of the multiples of P) satisfies a dynamical Zsigmondy property: for all sufficiently large n, the denominator of x([n]P) contains a prime ideal of K that does not divide any earlier denominator. This was proved by Everest, McLaren and Ward (2004) using the canonical height ĥ_E and the fact that ĥ_E([n]P) = n² ĥ_E(P). The proof reduces to showing that the set of primes dividing the denominators of x([n]P) has natural density one, which follows from a lower bound for the canonical height of [n]P away from the set of primes of bad reduction. For Drinfeld modules φ : A → End_K(𝔾_a) over a function field K = 𝔽_q(t), the orbit of a point α ∈ K under a non‑isotrivial Drinfeld module also enjoys a Zsigmondy property. Work of Hsia and Silverman (2009) shows that if φ has rank r ≥ 2 and α is not preperiodic, then all but finitely many iterates φa_n(α) have a primitive prime divisor in the ring A. The argument uses the canonical height ĥ_φ on the Drinfeld module, which satisfies ĥ_φ(φ_a(α)) = |a|^r ĥ_φ(α), and a function‑field version of the Subspace Theorem to control the set of primes dividing the iterates. The result fails for isotrivial Drinfeld modules because the canonical height is identically zero and the orbit can be contained in a finite set of primes. These theorems illustrate how height growth forces the appearance of new prime divisors, a dynamical counterpart of the classical Zsigmondy phenomenon.

The Dynamical Bogomolov Conjecture for Subvarieties of Tori

Let G = 𝔾_m^N be an algebraic torus over a number field K and let f : G → G be an endomorphism given by a matrix A ∈ M_N(ℤ) with all eigenvalues of absolute value > 1 (an expanding map). For a subvariety V ⊂ G defined over K, the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture predicts that if V contains a Zariski‑dense set of points of small canonical height ĥ_f, then V must be a translate of a subtorus by a preperiodic point. The canonical height on G is the quadratic form ĥ_f(x) = ⟨x, Q x⟩ where Q is the positive‑definite matrix solving A^T Q A = d Q with d = |det A|. The conjecture is a dynamical analogue of the classical Bogomolov conjecture (proved by Ullmo and Zhang) which concerns the Néron–Tate height on abelian varieties. For tori, the dynamical version was proved by Ghioca, Tucker and Zhang (2012) using equidistribution of small points on the Berkovich analytic space Gan at all places. The proof proceeds by showing that if V contains a sequence of points P_n with ĥ_f(P_n) → 0, then the Galois orbits of P_n equidistribute to the Haar measure on the compact subtorus of Gan corresponding to the unit circle in each coordinate. The limit measure is supported on the maximal compact subgroup of Gan, which forces V to be a union of cosets of a subtorus. The expanding hypothesis on A is essential: if some eigenvalue has absolute value ≤ 1, the canonical height is not positive definite and the equidistribution argument breaks down. The result has been extended to semiabelian varieties by combining the toric case with the abelian case via the Raynaud extension. A quantitative version gives an effective lower bound for ĥ_f(P) for points P ∈ V(K) outside a proper subvariety, which is a dynamical Lehmer‑type estimate.

The Dynamical Mordell–Lang Conjecture for Endomorphisms of Projective Space

Let f : P^N → P^N be a morphism of degree d ≥ 2 defined over a number field K, and let V ⊂ P^N be a subvariety defined over K. The dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture predicts that the intersection of the forward orbit O_f(P) = {f^n(P) : n ≥ 0} of a point P ∈ P^N(K) with V(K) is a finite union of arithmetic progressions in the index n. In other words, the set {n ≥ 0 : f^n(P) ∈ V} is a union of sets of the form {a + b k : k ≥ 0}. This conjecture generalises the classical Mordell–Lang conjecture (proved by Faltings for abelian varieties and by McQuillan for semiabelian varieties) to the dynamical setting. For N = 1 the conjecture is known for polynomial maps and for rational maps with a totally ramified fixed point, by work of Bell, Ghioca and Tucker (2010) using p-adic analytic methods and the Skolem–Mahler–Lech theorem. In higher dimension, the conjecture is open in general. Partial results exist when f is a split map f(x1,…,x_N) = (f1(x1),…,f_N(x_N)) with each f_i a one‑variable polynomial; then the orbit coordinates evolve independently and the problem reduces to the one‑dimensional case for each coordinate, yielding a finite union of arithmetic progressions. For monomial maps f(x) = (x1a11⋯x_Na1N, …, x1aN1⋯x_NaNN) with expanding matrix A, the conjecture follows from the classical Mordell–Lang theorem for tori because the orbit lies in the torus 𝔾_m^N and the condition f^n(P) ∈ V translates into a linear recurrence in the exponents. The main difficulty for a general f is the lack of a group structure on P^N; one approach is to embed P^N into a suitable algebraic group via a compactification of the dynamical system, but no universal construction is known. The conjecture is also related to the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture: if V contains a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points, then V must be preperiodic, which is a special case of the dynamical Mordell–Lang statement when the orbit is preperiodic.

  • Confusing the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture for projective space with the classical Mordell–Lang for semiabelian varieties; the former has no group law and requires different p-adic techniques.
  • Assuming the dynamical Zsigmondy theorem holds for all Drinfeld modules; it fails for isotrivial modules where the canonical height vanishes.
  • Neglecting the expanding eigenvalue condition in the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture for tori; without it the canonical height is not positive definite and equidistribution fails.
  • Treating the equidistribution measure on Berkovich spaces as the Haar measure for every polarised map; the measure is the Chambert‑Loir measure, which coincides with Haar only for toric or abelian varieties with the standard polarisation.
  • Believing uniform boundedness for preperiodic points on abelian varieties is known in full generality; it is only proved for multiplication‑by‑m maps and for products of one‑dimensional maps.
  • Overlooking the role of the Subspace Theorem in the proof of the dynamical Zsigmondy theorem for Drinfeld modules; the function‑field version is essential to control prime divisors.
  • Mixing up the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for tori with the classical Manin–Mumford for abelian varieties; the dynamical version requires an expanding endomorphism and uses equidistribution rather than the structure theorem for torsion points.

These advanced topics illustrate the deep interplay between height functions, equidistribution, and Diophantine approximation that defines the current frontier of arithmetic dynamics.

Canonical Height Theory for Rational Maps on Weighted Projective Spaces

Weighted projective spaces P(a0,…,a_N) arise naturally when studying rational maps that are not morphisms on ordinary projective space. A rational map f:P(a0,…,a_N)dashrightarrowP(a0,…,a_N) of algebraic degree d≥ 2 admits a canonical height h_f satisfying the functional equation h_f(f(P))=dh_f(P) for all points P where both sides are defined. The construction follows the usual telescoping limit h_f(P)=limn→∞(h(f^n(P)))/(d^n) where h is a Weil height associated to an ample Q-Cartier divisor on the weighted projective space. Because the Picard group of a weighted projective space is Z generated by the class of O(1), any two Weil heights differ by a bounded function, so the limit is independent of the initial choice.

The Northcott property holds for h_f on weighted projective spaces: for any bound B and degree D there are only finitely many points PinP(a0,…,a_N)(K) with [K(P):K]≤ D and h_f(P)≤ B. This follows from the fact that the underlying coarse moduli space is a projective variety and the canonical height is a height on that variety. Functoriality with respect to finite morphisms between weighted projective spaces is also available: if π:P(a0,…,a_N)toP(b0,…,b_M) is a finite morphism compatible with the dynamics, then hf'(π(P))=deg(π)h_f(P) for the induced map f'.

Preperiodic points are exactly those of canonical height zero. The set of preperiodic points is therefore a set of bounded height, and by Northcott it is a set of bounded degree over the ground field. In the weighted setting, the classification of maps with a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points (the dynamical Manin–Mumford problem) is more subtle because the automorphism group of a weighted projective space is larger than PGLN+1. Recent work of Kawaguchi and Silverman shows that if a rational map on a weighted projective space has a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points, then the map is isotrivial in the sense that it becomes a morphism on a suitable blow‑up and its canonical height vanishes identically.

Local heights at a place v are defined using adelic metrics on the line bundle O(1) that are invariant under the dynamics. The product formula for the canonical height then reads h_f(P)=Σ_v λf,v(P) where each local height λf,v satisfies the same functional equation. This decomposition is essential for equidistribution arguments on the Berkovich analytification of the weighted projective space, where the Chambert‑Loir measure is replaced by the Monge‑Ampère measure of the invariant metric.

Applications include the study of Lattès maps on weighted projective spaces arising from endomorphisms of abelian varieties with a finite group action. The canonical height on the quotient coincides with the Néron–Tate height on the abelian variety divided by the order of the group, providing a bridge between classical abelian dynamics and the weighted projective setting.

Arithmetic Dynamics on Moduli of Polarized Varieties: Height of the Bifurcation Locus

The moduli space M_d of degree d polarized endomorphisms of P^N (or more generally of polarized varieties) carries a natural height function hmod induced by the determinant of cohomology line bundle on the universal family. For a family f_t parameterized by a curve C over a number field, the bifurcation locus Bif(f)⊂ C(C) consists of parameters where the dynamics is not structurally stable. The height of the bifurcation divisor can be expressed as an integral of the Lyapunov exponent against the equilibrium measure, yielding the formula hmod(Bif(f))=∫_C chi(f_t) dμeq(t) where chi is the sum of positive Lyapunov exponents.

DeMarco and others proved that the bifurcation current Tbif=dd^c chi is a positive closed (1,1)-current on the moduli space, and its mass on a curve equals the height of the bifurcation locus. This identifies the bifurcation height with an arithmetic intersection number on a suitable compactification of the moduli space. In particular, if a curve C is contained in the bifurcation locus, then the canonical height of the family restricted to C vanishes, which forces the family to be isotrivial by a dynamical version of the Bogomolov conjecture.

The equidistribution of parameters of small height towards the bifurcation measure is a dynamical analogue of the Yuan–Zhang theorem. If {t_n}⊂ C(K) is a sequence of parameters with hmod(t_n)→ 0, then the Galois orbits of t_n equidistribute to the measure Tbifwedge [C]. This result has been used to prove that a curve in the moduli space containing infinitely many post‑critically finite maps must be a special subvariety (a dynamical André–Oort statement).

Effective bounds for the height of the bifurcation locus have been obtained using the Subspace Theorem in the function field setting. For a non‑isotrivial family over a function field K=F_q(t), the height of the bifurcation divisor is bounded below by a positive constant depending only on the degree of the family and the genus of the base curve. This yields a function field analogue of the dynamical Lehmer problem for families.

Recent work of Gauthier and Vigny extends these ideas to higher dimensional bases, constructing a bifurcation current of higher codimension and proving equidistribution of small points on subvarieties of the moduli space. The main technical tool is the theory of super‑potentials for positive closed currents, which allows one to define intersection products without assuming smoothness of the ambient space.

Worked Example: Height of a Bifurcation Divisor
Let f_c(z)=z2+c be the quadratic family over Q. The bifurcation locus in the parameter space A1 is the boundary of the Mandelbrot set. Compute the modular height of the bifurcation divisor intersected with the line c=0 using the formula hmod(Bif∩{c=0})=∫_{{c=0}} chi(f_c) dμeq.
The line c=0 corresponds to the map f0(z)=z2, which is post‑critically finite (the critical point 0 is fixed). For a post‑critically finite parameter the Lyapunov exponent chi(f0)=log 2 and the equilibrium measure on the parameter line is the Dirac mass at c=0. Hence the integral equals log 2. By the general theory, the modular height of the bifurcation divisor restricted to this line is exactly log 2. This matches the known fact that the canonical height of the critical point for f0 is zero, so the bifurcation height measures the failure of the critical orbit to be preperiodic.

When a question asks for the height of a bifurcation divisor on a curve, first identify whether the curve contains a post‑critically finite parameter. If it does, the height equals the Lyapunov exponent at that parameter; otherwise use the integral formula with the equilibrium measure. Remember that the bifurcation current is dd^c of the Lyapunov exponent, so its mass on a curve is the height.

The Dynamical Uniform Boundedness Conjecture for Endomorphisms of Products of Projective Lines

Let X=(P1)^r be a product of r copies of the projective line over a number field K. An endomorphism f:X→ X of degree d≥ 2 is given by a tuple of rational maps (f1,…,f_r) each of degree d_i with Π d_i = d. The dynamical uniform boundedness conjecture predicts that there exists a constant B(r,d,[K:Q]) depending only on the dimension r, the degree d, and the degree of the field, such that any preperiodic point of f has at most B distinct K-rational preperiodic points. In other words, the number of K-rational preperiodic points is uniformly bounded across all such endomorphisms and all number fields of bounded degree.

For r=1 this is the classical Morton–Silverman conjecture, known for polynomial maps and for rational maps with a totally ramified fixed point. For r≥ 2 the problem is largely open. Partial results exist when the endomorphism splits as a product of one‑variable maps: then the set of preperiodic points is the Cartesian product of the preperiodic sets of each coordinate, and the bound reduces to the product of the one‑dimensional bounds. When the endomorphism is a skew product f(x1,…,x_r)=(f1(x1),f2(x1,x2),…,f_r(x1,…,x_r)), the projection onto the first coordinate gives a map on P1 and the fibers are again P1; uniform boundedness for the base and for the fiber maps implies a bound for the total space.

The canonical height on X is the sum of the pullbacks of the canonical heights on each factor: h_f(P)=Σi=1^r hf_i(π_i(P)) where π_i is the projection. This additive structure makes the Northcott property immediate. However, the lack of a group law on X prevents a direct application of the classical Mordell–Lang technique. The main approach to uniform boundedness uses the Subspace Theorem to bound the number of S-integral preperiodic points, combined with a specialization argument that reduces the problem to a family over a curve.

Recent work of Doyle, Poonen, and others shows that if the endomorphism is defined over a function field of characteristic zero, then the number of preperiodic points in a given fiber is bounded by a constant depending only on the genus of the base curve and the degree of the family. This function field result is a crucial step towards the number field conjecture via the standard specialization technique of Silverman.

A quantitative version of the conjecture would give an explicit bound such as B(r,d,D)≤ C(r) drD for some constant C(r). Such bounds are known for split maps and for certain skew products, but the general case remains a major open problem in arithmetic dynamics.

The Dynamical Zilber–Pink Conjecture for Semiabelian Varieties

The Zilber–Pink conjecture in its classical form predicts that a subvariety of a mixed Shimura variety containing a Zariski‑dense set of special points must be a special subvariety. The dynamical analogue replaces special points by preperiodic points of a polarised endomorphism f:A→ A on a semiabelian variety A (an extension of an abelian variety by a torus). A subvariety V⊂ A is called preperiodic if its forward orbit under f is finite. The dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture asserts that if V contains a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points, then V is a finite union of translates of semiabelian subvarieties that are themselves preperiodic under f.

When A is an abelian variety and f is the multiplication‑by‑m map, the conjecture reduces to the classical Manin–Mumford theorem (proved by Raynaud). For a general polarised endomorphism, the canonical height h_f is a quadratic form on A(K) and the set of preperiodic points is exactly the kernel of h_f. The equidistribution of Galois orbits of small points towards the Haar measure on the Berkovich analytification of A (the Chambert‑Loir measure) provides the main tool. If V contains a sequence of points P_n with h_f(P_n)→ 0, then the Galois orbits equidistribute to the Haar measure on the maximal compact subgroup of Aan. The support of this measure is a finite union of cosets of a subtorus, forcing V to be a union of such cosets.

The semiabelian case requires combining the toric equidistribution (as in the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture for tori) with the abelian equidistribution. The Raynaud extension 0→ G_m^t→ A→ B→ 0 of the semiabelian variety over the valuation ring allows one to lift the dynamics to the universal cover and apply the product equidistribution theorem of Yuan and Zhang. The expanding hypothesis on the eigenvalues of the induced action on the character group of the torus part is essential; if some eigenvalue has absolute value ≤ 1, the canonical height is not positive definite and the equidistribution argument fails.

Quantitative versions give effective lower bounds for h_f(P) for points P∈ V(K) outside a proper subvariety, which are dynamical Lehmer‑type estimates. These bounds depend on the degree of V, the degree of the field, and the minimal expansion factor of f on the toric part. The conjecture is known for split semiabelian varieties (products of an abelian variety and a torus) and for certain non‑split extensions where the dynamics respects the extension structure.

An important application is to the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture for semiabelian varieties: if V intersects a given orbit O_f(P) in a Zariski‑dense set, then V must contain a translate of a semiabelian subvariety that is invariant under f. This follows by applying the dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture to the subvariety V and the orbit closure.

Heights on the Space of Rational Maps and the Moduli Height of Dynamical Systems

The space Rat_d^N of degree d rational maps on P^N is a quasi‑projective variety defined by the non‑vanishing of the resultant. A height function hmod on this space can be constructed using the determinant of cohomology of the universal family F→ Rat_d^N. Concretely, for a map f given by homogeneous polynomials (F0:…:F_N) of degree d with coefficients in a number field K, one defines hmod(f)=(1)/([K:Q])Σv∈ M_K log \| Res(F0,…,F_N) \|_v where Res is the resultant and \|·\|_v are the standard absolute values normalized to satisfy the product formula. This height is invariant under the action of PGLN+1 by conjugation, so it descends to the moduli space M_d^N=Rat_d^N/PGLN+1.

The modular height controls the arithmetic complexity of the dynamical system. For a family f_t parameterized by a curve C, the height of the parameter t equals the modular height of the fiber f_t up to a bounded error. This allows one to translate statements about heights of points on C into statements about the dynamics of f_t. In particular, the bifurcation height of a family is the intersection of the modular height divisor with the curve, and the equidistribution of parameters of small modular height towards the bifurcation measure follows from the general Yuan–Zhang theorem applied to the line bundle of modular height.

Silverman proved that the modular height satisfies a Northcott property: for any bound B and degree D there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of rational maps of degree d on P^N defined over a number field of degree ≤ D with modular height ≤ B. This finiteness is a cornerstone for many uniform boundedness arguments, because it reduces the problem to a finite set of maps after a height bound is established.

The modular height also appears in the dynamical Lehmer problem: one asks whether there exists a constant c(d,N)>0 such that for any non‑preperiodic rational map f the canonical height of a wandering point satisfies h_f(P)≥ c(d,N) hmod(f). This inequality would imply that maps of small modular height have very few points of small canonical height, strengthening the uniform boundedness conjecture. Partial results are known for polynomial maps on P1 where the modular height is essentially the height of the coefficients.

In positive characteristic, the modular height must be replaced by a height on the moduli stack of Frobenius‑twisted maps. The theory of canonical heights for Drinfeld modules provides a parallel construction, where the modular height is the height of the associated Drinfeld module on the Drinfeld modular curve. The uniform boundedness conjecture for preperiodic points on Drinfeld modules is then equivalent to a Northcott property for this modular height.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the modular height on Rat_d^N with the canonical height of a point; the former measures complexity of the map, the latter measures complexity of a point under a fixed map.
  • Assuming the dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture holds for all semiabelian varieties without the expanding eigenvalue condition on the toric part.
  • Treating the bifurcation current as a measure on the whole moduli space; it is a current of codimension one, and its intersection with a curve gives a measure on that curve.
  • Believing uniform boundedness for preperiodic points on products of projective lines follows directly from the one‑dimensional case; the product structure gives a bound only for split maps.
  • Neglecting the role of the resultant in the definition of modular height; the resultant vanishes exactly on the locus where the map is not well defined, so its height captures the arithmetic of the bad reduction locus.
  • Mixing up the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for semiabelian varieties with the classical Manin–Mumford for abelian varieties; the dynamical version requires a polarised endomorphism and uses equidistribution of small points.
  • Overlooking that the Northcott property for modular height is proved using the fact that the moduli space is a quasi‑projective variety, not a proper variety; compactification issues are essential in the proof.

The Dynamical Manin–Mumford Conjecture for Subvarieties of Semiabelian Varieties

The classical Manin–Mumford theorem asserts that a subvariety of an abelian variety containing a Zariski‑dense set of torsion points must be a translate of an abelian subvariety. In arithmetic dynamics one replaces torsion points by preperiodic points of a polarised endomorphism f on a semiabelian variety G. A polarised endomorphism is a homomorphism f: G → G such that some multiple of the identity minus f is an isogeny; this condition guarantees the existence of a canonical height ĥf with the Northcott property. The dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture then states: if a subvariety V ⊂ G contains a Zariski‑dense set of f‑preperiodic points, then V is a translate of a semiabelian subvariety that is invariant under f. The conjecture is known for tori (Ghioca–Tucker, 2009) and for abelian varieties (Zhang, 1998; later refined by Ghioca–Hsia–Tucker). For general semiabelian varieties the expanding eigenvalue condition on the toric part is essential; without it counterexamples exist where a curve contains infinitely many preperiodic points without being a translate of a subtorus. The proof strategy combines equidistribution of small points (Yuan–Zhang) with a p‑adic analytic argument on the Berkovich skeleton of the toric part. In the split case G = A × T the conjecture reduces to the product of the abelian and toric cases, but non‑split extensions require a careful analysis of the extension class and its interaction with the canonical height. Recent work of Dimitrov–Gao–Habegger (2022) establishes the conjecture for semiabelian varieties whose toric part has rank one, using a combination of o‑minimality and the Pila–Zannier method. The conjecture remains open in full generality, and it is a central target for the dynamical Zilber–Pink programme.

The Dynamical Uniform Boundedness Conjecture for Endomorphisms of Semiabelian Varieties

Uniform boundedness asks whether the number of preperiodic points of a polarised endomorphism f on a semiabelian variety G defined over a number field K can be bounded by a constant depending only on dim G, deg f, and [K:ℚ]. Morton and Silverman proved the first such result for morphisms of ℙ¹; Hindry and Silverman extended it to abelian varieties using the Néron–Tate height and the fact that the canonical height is a quadratic form. For semiabelian varieties the situation is more delicate because the canonical height is no longer quadratic on the toric part. The conjecture predicts a bound B(dim G, deg f, [K:ℚ]) for #Preper(f, G(K)). In the split case G = A × T the bound follows from the product of the abelian and toric bounds, but the toric bound itself depends on the rank of T and the eigenvalues of f on the character lattice. For non‑split extensions the canonical height acquires a bilinear cross term, and the Northcott property must be proved for the associated height pairing on the extension class. Ghioca and Tucker (2011) obtained a bound for semiabelian varieties of dimension two under the expanding eigenvalue hypothesis. More recently, Dimitrov–Gao–Habegger (2023) proved a uniform bound for all semiabelian varieties whose toric part has rank one, using a modular height on the moduli stack of semiabelian varieties with polarised endomorphism. The modular height controls the arithmetic complexity of the pair (G, f) and satisfies a Northcott property (Silverman). The conjecture is equivalent to the statement that the set of isomorphism classes of polarised semiabelian varieties with a given bound on the number of preperiodic points is finite. This finiteness is a cornerstone for many dynamical Diophantine applications, including the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture for semiabelian varieties.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: "Explain why the uniform boundedness conjecture for preperiodic points on a semiabelian variety does not follow immediately from the product case." Provide a concise answer.
The product case only covers split semiabelian varieties G = A × T. A general semiabelian variety is an extension of an abelian variety by a torus, and the extension class introduces a non‑trivial bilinear term in the canonical height. This cross term prevents a direct factorisation of the preperiodic set, and the eigenvalues of f on the toric part interact with the abelian part. Consequently, a bound for the product does not control the non‑split case; one must analyse the height pairing on the extension and use modular heights on the moduli stack.
Exam‑day strategy
When a problem mentions uniform boundedness on semiabelian varieties, first identify whether the variety is split or a non‑trivial extension. Cite the expanding eigenvalue condition on the toric part and the role of the modular height on the moduli stack. This distinction is a frequent source of partial credit.

The Dynamical André–Oort Conjecture for Shimura Varieties

The classical André–Oort conjecture (proved by Pila–Tsimerman for ℋⁿ and by many hands for general Shimura varieties) states that the Zariski closure of a set of special points in a Shimura variety is a special subvariety. The dynamical analogue replaces special points by points that are both special and preperiodic under a polarised endomorphism f (or more generally a Hecke correspondence) on the Shimura variety S. A special point corresponds to a CM abelian variety (or a CM point on a modular curve); a point is f‑preperiodic if its forward orbit under f is finite. The dynamical André–Oort conjecture asserts: if a subvariety V ⊂ S contains a Zariski‑dense set of points that are simultaneously special and f‑preperiodic, then V is a special subvariety invariant under f. The conjecture is known for Hilbert modular varieties (Gao–Habegger, 2020) and for Siegel modular varieties of genus two (Daw–Ren, 2022). The proof combines the Pila–Zannier o‑minimal counting method with equidistribution of Galois orbits of special points (the work of Yuan–Zhang on small points) and the Ax–Lindemann theorem for the uniformisation map. A key technical point is that the endomorphism f must be polarised, i.e. it comes from a morphism of the underlying PEL‑type Shimura datum, so that the canonical height is well defined and the set of f‑preperiodic special points has bounded height. Without polarisation the conjecture fails: there exist non‑polarised correspondences for which the intersection of a curve with the set of special preperiodic points is infinite but the curve is not special. The dynamical André–Oort conjecture is a special case of the dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture for mixed Shimura varieties, and its resolution would provide a powerful tool for studying unlikely intersections in dynamical systems.

The Dynamical Bogomolov Conjecture for Subvarieties of Abelian Varieties

The classical Bogomolov conjecture (proved by Ullmo and Zhang) states that a subvariety of an abelian variety containing a generic sequence of points of small Néron–Tate height must be a translate of an abelian subvariety. In the dynamical setting one replaces the Néron–Tate height by the canonical height ĥf associated to a polarised endomorphism f on an abelian variety A. A generic sequence of small points is a sequence of distinct points {Pn} ⊂ V(K̄) such that ĥf(Pn) → 0 and the points are not contained in any proper subvariety of V. The dynamical Bogomolov conjecture then predicts: if V ⊂ A contains a generic sequence of f‑small points, then V is a translate of an abelian subvariety invariant under f. Zhang (1998) proved the conjecture for polarised endomorphisms using the equidistribution of small points (the Yuan–Zhang theorem) and the fact that the canonical height is a quadratic form. Ghioca–Tucker (2010) extended the result to the case where f is a polarised isogeny on a semiabelian variety, but the abelian case remains the most complete. The conjecture is closely related to the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture: preperiodic points have canonical height zero, so a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points is a special case of a generic sequence of small points. Conversely, the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture implies the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for abelian varieties. Recent work of Habegger (2021) gives an effective version: there exists an explicit constant c(A, f) > 0 such that any subvariety not a translate of an invariant abelian subvariety contains only finitely many points with ĥf < c. This effective bound is crucial for algorithmic applications and for proving uniform boundedness results via the modular height on the moduli space of polarised abelian varieties.

The Dynamical Zilber–Pink Conjecture for Shimura Varieties

The Zilber–Pink conjecture for mixed Shimura varieties (formulated by Pink and refined by Zilber) predicts that the intersection of a subvariety with the set of special points is a finite union of special subvarieties, unless the subvariety itself contains a positive‑dimensional special subvariety. The dynamical version incorporates a polarised endomorphism f (or a Hecke correspondence) on a Shimura variety S. One considers the set SPf(S) of points that are both special and f‑preperiodic. The dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture states: for any subvariety V ⊂ S, the intersection V ∩ SPf(S) is a finite union of translates of special subvarieties that are invariant under f, unless V contains a positive‑dimensional special subvariety invariant under f. This conjecture simultaneously generalises the dynamical André–Oort conjecture (where V is a curve) and the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture (where S is a semiabelian variety viewed as a mixed Shimura variety). The known cases are extremely limited: Gao–Habegger (2022) proved it for products of modular curves with a product endomorphism, using the Ax–Lindemann theorem for the uniformisation map ℋⁿ → (ℙ¹)ⁿ and the Pila–Zannier method. For general Shimura varieties the conjecture is wide open. A major obstacle is the lack of a good height theory for Hecke correspondences that are not polarised endomorphisms; the canonical height is only defined for polarised maps, and the equidistribution of small points (Yuan–Zhang) requires a polarised dynamical system. Another difficulty is the presence of atypical intersections coming from the dynamics of the correspondence on the boundary of the Baily–Borel compactification. The conjecture is expected to be a central organising principle for unlikely intersections in arithmetic dynamics, linking the dynamical Mordell–Lang, dynamical Manin–Mumford, and dynamical André–Oort conjectures under a single framework.

Canonical Heights on Drinfeld Modules and the Function Field Analogue

In positive characteristic the role of elliptic curves is played by Drinfeld modules, and the theory of canonical heights transfers with striking fidelity. Let K be a global function field of characteristic p and let φ be a Drinfeld module of rank r ≥ 2 defined over K. For a point P ∈ K̄ the naive height h(P) is defined via the degree of the divisor of poles of a coordinate function. The canonical height ĥφ(P) is obtained by the usual telescoping limit ĥφ(P) = limn→∞ h(φn(P)) / qrn, where q is the size of the constant field. Denis (1992) proved that ĥφ is a quadratic form on the Mordell–Weil group of the Drinfeld module and satisfies the Northcott property: for any bound B the set { P ∈ K̄ : ĥφ(P) ≤ B } is finite. The function field Mordell conjecture, proved by Manin (1963) and later refined by Grauert, implies that the set of K-rational preperiodic points is finite. Ghioca and Tucker (2008) extended the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture to Drinfeld modules, showing that the intersection of a subvariety with a φ‑orbit is a finite union of translates of φ‑invariant subvarieties. A key technical tool is the product formula for the canonical local heights λv(P) at each place v of K, which mirrors the Néron–Tate theory but uses the Carlitz exponential instead of the Weierstrass ℘‑function. The equidistribution of small points on the Berkovich analytic space associated to the Drinfeld module was established by Baker and Rumely (2010), yielding a function‑field analogue of the Yuan–Zhang theorem. These results demonstrate that the arithmetic dynamics of Drinfeld modules provides a complete parallel to the classical theory over number fields, with the added feature that the underlying geometry is rigid‑analytic rather than complex‑analytic.

Equidistribution of Galois Orbits on Berkovich Analytic Spaces

Let f be a polarised endomorphism of degree d ≥ 2 on a projective variety X defined over a number field K. For each place v of K one can associate a Berkovich analytic space Xanv and a canonical invariant probability measure μf,v (the equilibrium measure) constructed by Favre and Rivera‑Letelier (2006) for X = ℙ1 and by Chambert‑Loir (2006) in higher dimension. The fundamental equidistribution theorem states: if { Pn } is a generic sequence of distinct algebraic points with ĥf(Pn) → 0, then for every place v the Galois orbits of Pn equidistribute towards μf,v on Xanv. The proof uses the continuity of the canonical local height λf,v on the Berkovich space and the fact that the Laplacian of λf,v equals μf,v minus the Dirac mass at the Gauss point. A crucial ingredient is the product formula ∑v λf,v(P) = ĥf(P), which forces the local contributions to vanish in the limit. Yuan (2008) and Zhang (1998) independently proved the archimedean case using pluripotential theory; the non‑archimedean case follows from the work of Favre–Rivera‑Letelier and Chambert‑Loir. The theorem has immediate applications: it yields a dynamical Bogomolov conjecture for abelian varieties (Zhang), a dynamical Manin–Mumford theorem for tori (Ghioca–Tucker–Zhang), and a proof of the dynamical André–Oort conjecture for ℙ1 × ℙ1 (Baker–DeMarco). Moreover, the equidistribution of Galois orbits provides a bridge between arithmetic dynamics and the theory of heights on moduli spaces: the bifurcation measure on the moduli space of rational maps is exactly the pushforward of μf,v under the natural map from the parameter space to the Berkovich space. This perspective underlies recent progress on the uniform boundedness conjecture in families (DeMarco–Krone).

Applying the Equidistribution Theorem to Compute Canonical Heights
Let f(z) = z² + c be a quadratic polynomial over ℚ with c chosen so that f has a superattracting fixed point at ∞ and no other preperiodic points in ℚ. Suppose { P_n } is a sequence of distinct algebraic points with ĥ_f(P_n) → 0. Show that for the archimedean place v = ∞ the Galois orbits of P_n equidistribute with respect to the equilibrium measure μ_f,∞ on the Berkovich projective line ℙ¹_ℂ^an.
The canonical height ĥ_f is non‑negative and vanishes exactly on preperiodic points. Since ĥ_f(P_n) → 0 and the points are distinct, the sequence is generic. By the product formula ĥ_f(P_n) = ∑_v λ_f,v(P_n), the sum of local heights tends to 0. Each local height λ_f,v is non‑negative, so λ_f,∞(P_n) → 0. The equilibrium measure μ_f,∞ is the Laplacian of the continuous function λ_f,∞ on ℙ¹_ℂ^an. For any continuous test function φ on ℙ¹_ℂ^an, the average (1/[K(P_n):ℚ]) ∑σ φ(σ(P_n)) equals ∫ φ dδP_n, where δP_n is the probability measure supported on the Galois orbit. The convergence λ_f,∞(P_n) → 0 implies that the potentials of δP_n converge to the potential of μ_f,∞ in the sense of distributions. By the uniqueness of the equilibrium measure, δP_n converges weakly to μ_f,∞. Hence the Galois orbits equidistribute with respect to μ_f,∞.

When a question asks for equidistribution of Galois orbits, first verify that the sequence is generic and that the canonical height tends to zero. Then invoke the product formula to deduce vanishing of the relevant local height. Cite the appropriate theorem (Favre–Rivera‑Letelier for ℙ¹, Chambert‑Loir for higher dimension, Yuan–Zhang for the archimedean case). Do not attempt to reprove the Laplacian identity; state it as a known fact and apply the weak convergence argument.

The Dynamical Uniform Boundedness Conjecture for Rational Maps on Weighted Projective Spaces

Weighted projective spaces ℙ(a₀,…,aₙ) provide a natural setting for rational maps that are not morphisms on ordinary projective space. A rational map f : ℙ(a) ⇢ ℙ(a) of degree d ≥ 2 induces a canonical height ĥ_f on the set of preperiodic points, defined via a suitable adelic metric on the tautological line bundle O(1). The dynamical uniform boundedness conjecture in this context predicts that the number of preperiodic points in ℙ(a)(K) is bounded by a constant depending only on the degree d, the dimension n, the weight vector a, and the degree [K:ℚ]. Silverman (2007) proved the conjecture for morphisms on ℙⁿ, and the case of weighted projective spaces was addressed by Hutz (2015) for monomial maps and by Ingram (2018) for a class of Lattès maps arising from multiplication on abelian varieties with a weighted projective embedding. The main obstruction is the presence of orbifold singularities, which forces the canonical height to be defined on the smooth locus and extended by continuity. A key tool is the functoriality of canonical heights under finite morphisms π : ℙ(a) → ℙ(b) that respect the dynamics; this allows reduction to the classical case when π is a Galois cover. The conjecture is known for all polynomial maps on ℙ(1,1,2) (the weighted projective plane) by work of Doyle–Faber–Krumm (2020), who used a combination of the Subspace Theorem and a careful analysis of the critical orbit relations. For general rational maps the problem remains open; the best known bound is exponential in the height of the coefficients, obtained via the dynamical Lehmer inequality of Silverman–Tate.

Height Inequalities for Iterated Correspondences and the Dynamical abc Conjecture

The dynamical abc conjecture, formulated by Silverman (2007), asserts that for a rational map f : ℙ¹ ⇢ ℙ¹ of degree d ≥ 2 defined over a number field K, there exists a constant C(f,K,ε) such that for any point P ∈ ℙ¹(K) which is not preperiodic, the inequality h(fⁿ(P)) ≤ (1+ε) dⁿ ĥ_f(P) + C holds for all n ≥ 1. This is a dynamical analogue of the classical abc conjecture and implies strong uniform bounds for integral points in orbits. When f is replaced by a correspondence C ⊂ ℙ¹ × ℙ¹ of bidegree (d₁,d₂), the iterates Cⁿ define a multi‑valued dynamical system. The canonical height ĥ_C is defined as the limit of h(π₂(Cⁿ(P))) / (d₁d₂)ⁿ, where π₂ is the projection to the second factor. Kawaguchi and Silverman (2016) proved a height inequality for correspondences: ĥ_C(P) ≤ (1+ε) h(P) + O(1) for all P outside a proper Zariski closed set, assuming C is polarised (i.e., d₁ = d₂). The dynamical abc conjecture for correspondences would give a reverse inequality, yielding a precise asymptotic for h(Cⁿ(P)). Recent work of Matsuzawa (2021) established the conjecture for Lattès correspondences coming from multiplication by m on an elliptic curve, using the theory of canonical heights on abelian varieties and the classical abc conjecture for number fields (which is known conditionally on the Szpiro conjecture). The inequality also has consequences for the dynamical Zsigmondy theorem: if the abc conjecture holds, then for any non‑preperiodic point P the orbit Cⁿ(P) contains a primitive prime divisor for all sufficiently large n, generalising the result of Ingram–Silverman for single maps.

Effective Methods for Preperiodic Points via the Subspace Theorem and Baker's Method

Effective determination of preperiodic points for a given rational map f over a number field K is a central algorithmic problem. Two complementary approaches have yielded explicit bounds. The Subspace Theorem method, pioneered by Corvaja and Zannier (2002) and refined by Bugeaud, Corvaja, and Zannier (2003), applies to maps with a totally ramified fixed point (e.g., polynomials). It produces an explicit upper bound for the canonical height of any preperiodic point in terms of the height of the coefficients of f and the degree [K:ℚ]. The bound is of the form ĥ_f(P) ≤ C₁ (log H(f))C₂ where H(f) is the naive height of the coefficients and C₁, C₂ are effective constants depending only on the degree d and the dimension. Baker's method, based on linear forms in logarithms, gives a different type of bound: if P is preperiodic of exact period N, then N ≤ C₃ (log H(f))C₄ with effective C₃, C₄. This was carried out by Benedetto (2005) for unicritical polynomials and by Ghioca, Tucker, and Zieve (2011) for certain rational maps with good reduction outside a prescribed set. The two methods can be combined: the Subspace Theorem controls the height of the point, while Baker's method controls the period. In practice, one first computes a finite set of candidate preperiodic points by enumerating all points of height up to the Subspace Theorem bound, then checks each candidate for periodicity using reduction modulo primes. This algorithm has been implemented for quadratic polynomials over ℚ (Doyle–Krumm, 2019) and for cubic polynomials over quadratic fields (Faber–Hutz, 2022). The main limitation is that the constants C₁,…,C₄ are extremely large, making the bounds impractical for high degree maps; however, they are theoretically effective and provide the only unconditional finiteness proofs for preperiodic points in families.

Canonical Height Theory for Endomorphisms of Products of Projective Lines

Let X = P1 × P1 and let f = (f1, f2) : X → X be an endomorphism where each f_i is a rational map of degree d_i ≥ 2. The naive height on X is the sum of the standard Weil heights on each factor, h(P) = hP1(π1(P)) + hP1(π2(P)). The canonical height h_f is defined by the limit h_f(P) = limn→∞ (h(f^n(P)))/(d1^n d2^n) when the dynamical degrees coincide, i.e. d1 = d2 = d. In the polarised case the limit exists for all P ∈ X(overline{Q}) and satisfies the functoriality h_f(f(P)) = d2 h_f(P). When the degrees differ, one uses the dynamical degree δ = max(d1,d2) and the limit lim h(f^n(P))/δ^n still converges after a suitable modification of the height by a bounded function, as shown by Kawaguchi and Silverman (2016). The Northcott property holds: for any bound B the set {P ∈ X(overline{Q}) : h_f(P) ≤ B, [Q(P):Q] ≤ D} is finite. This finiteness underpins the uniform boundedness conjecture for preperiodic points on products of projective lines. The canonical height also admits a decomposition into local heights h_f = Σ_v λf,v where each local height is a limit of local Weil functions; the local theory on Berkovich spaces yields the equidistribution of small points toward the invariant measure μ_f = μf1 × μf2. Recent work of Favre and Gauthier (2018) established a precise formula for the height of the bifurcation locus in the moduli space of such maps, linking the canonical height to the Lyapunov exponent of the dynamical system.

The Dynamical Mordell–Lang Conjecture for Endomorphisms of Products of Projective Lines

The classical Mordell–Lang conjecture, proved by Faltings for abelian varieties and by McQuillan for semiabelian varieties, asserts that the intersection of a subvariety with a finitely generated group is a finite union of translates of subgroups. In arithmetic dynamics the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture replaces the group by the forward orbit of a point under a polarised endomorphism. For f = (f1,f2) on P1 × P1 with d1 = d2 = d, let V ⊂ X be a subvariety defined over a number field K and let α ∈ X(K) be a point with Zariski dense orbit. The conjecture predicts that the set { n ≥ 0 : f^n(α) ∈ V } is a finite union of arithmetic progressions. Ghioca, Tucker and Zieve (2011) proved this for split maps where f1 and f2 are both polynomials or both Chebyshev maps, using the theory of canonical heights and the Subspace Theorem. The proof proceeds by showing that if the set of return times is infinite then the canonical height of α restricted to V must vanish, forcing α to be preperiodic, contradicting Zariski density. For non‑split maps the conjecture remains open; the main obstruction is the lack of a general equidistribution theorem for the invariant measure on the product when the two factors have different dynamical degrees. A conditional result of Xie (2020) shows that assuming the dynamical André–Oort conjecture for the moduli space of such maps, the dynamical Mordell–Lang holds for all polarised endomorphisms. The conjecture also implies strong uniform bounds for the number of preperiodic points on subvarieties, linking it to the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture.

Examiner's trap
Let f = (z2, z2) on P1 × P1 and let α = (2,3). Determine whether the set { n ≥ 0 : f^n(α) ∈ V } with V = {(x,y) : x = y} is a finite union of arithmetic progressions, and justify your answer using the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture.
The map f is polarised with degree d=2. The subvariety V is the diagonal, defined by x=y. The orbit of α is (22^n, 32^n). Equality 22^n = 32^n holds only if 2=3, impossible. Hence the return set is empty, which is a finite union (zero) of arithmetic progressions. The dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture predicts exactly this behaviour: because the canonical height of α is positive (both coordinates have positive height), the intersection with a proper subvariety must be a finite union of arithmetic progressions, here the empty set.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks to apply the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture, first check whether the map is polarised (equal dynamical degrees) and whether the point has a Zariski dense orbit. If the orbit is contained in a proper subvariety, the return set is either all n or a finite union of progressions; if the orbit is dense, the conjecture forces the return set to be a finite union of progressions. Use canonical height positivity to rule out infinite return sets unless the point is preperiodic.

Arithmetic Dynamics on Moduli of Abelian Varieties: Height of the Bifurcation Locus

Let A_g be the moduli space of principally polarised abelian varieties of dimension g over overline{Q}. For a family of endomorphisms f_t : A_t → A_t parametrised by t ∈ A_g, the bifurcation locus Bif(f) consists of parameters where the dynamical behaviour is not structurally stable. Baker and DeMarco (2011) introduced a height function hbif on A_g by integrating the Lyapunov exponent of the family against the natural Weil–Petersson form. They proved that hbif(t) = hf_t(crit(f_t)) where crit(f_t) denotes the critical points of f_t. This identity links the arithmetic height of the bifurcation locus to the canonical height of critical orbits. In the case g=1 (elliptic curves) the bifurcation locus for the multiplication‑by‑m map is empty, reflecting the fact that the family is isotrivial. For g≥ 2 the bifurcation locus is a proper analytic subset of A_g and its height is positive for non‑isotrivial families. Recent work of Gauthier and Vigny (2022) showed that the equidistribution of parameters of small bifurcation height holds: if {t_n} is a sequence in A_g(overline{Q}) with hbif(t_n) → 0, then the Galois orbits of t_n equidistribute toward the bifurcation measure μbif = dd^c hbif. This result is a dynamical analogue of the André–Oort conjecture for Shimura varieties and provides a bridge between arithmetic dynamics and the geometry of moduli spaces.

p‑Adic Equidistribution of Small Points on Berkovich Spaces and the Chambert‑Loir Measure

Let K be a number field and v a non‑archimedean place. The Berkovich analytic space P1,anK_v carries a canonical probability measure μf,v attached to a polarised rational map f of degree d≥ 2. Chambert‑Loir (2006) constructed this measure as the limit of the discrete measures (1)/(d^n) Σf^n(x)=x δ_x and proved that for any sequence of algebraic points {P_n} with h_f(P_n) → 0, the Galois orbits of P_n equidistribute toward μf,v. The proof uses the theory of adelic metrics on line bundles and the fact that the canonical height decomposes as h_f = Σ_v λf,v. The local height λf,v is a continuous function on P1,anK_v whose Laplacian is μf,v - δ∞. Equidistribution follows from the energy minimisation principle: the probability measures supported on Galois orbits have energy converging to the minimal energy, which is uniquely attained by μf,v. This theorem has been extended to higher dimensional projective spaces by Favre and Rivera‑Letelier (2010) and to polarised endomorphisms of abelian varieties by Yuan and Zhang (2017). A key application is the proof of the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture for subvarieties of tori: if a subvariety contains a Zariski dense set of points of small canonical height, then it must be a torsion translate of a subtorus. The p‑adic equidistribution also underlies the proof of the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for semiabelian varieties by Ghioca, Hsia and Tucker (2015).

The Dynamical Zsigmondy Theorem for Higher Dimensional Varieties

The classical Zsigmondy theorem states that for integers a>b>0 the sequence a^n-b^n has a primitive prime divisor for all sufficiently large n. In arithmetic dynamics, Ingram and Silverman (2009) proved a dynamical analogue for rational maps on P1: if f is a rational map of degree d≥ 2 over a number field K and P∈ P1(K) is not preperiodic, then the orbit f^n(P) contains a primitive prime divisor for all sufficiently large n. The proof uses the canonical height and the fact that the set of primes dividing the numerator of f^n(P) grows like d^n h_f(P). For higher dimensional varieties, the statement must be formulated in terms of prime divisors on the variety. Let X be a smooth projective variety over K and f:X→ X a polarised endomorphism. A prime divisor p of K is called primitive for f^n(P) if p divides the pullback of a fixed ample divisor under f^n but does not divide any earlier iterate. Matsuzawa (2021) proved the dynamical Zsigmondy theorem for Lattès maps on abelian varieties, using the canonical height on the abelian variety and the classical abc conjecture. For general polarised endomorphisms on P^N with N≥ 2, the theorem is open; the main difficulty is controlling the height of the critical locus and the possible accumulation of prime divisors on subvarieties of the critical set. A conditional result of Xie (2022) shows that assuming the dynamical abc conjecture for correspondences, the dynamical Zsigmondy theorem holds for all polarised endomorphisms on projective space. The theorem has strong consequences for the uniform boundedness conjecture: if every orbit has a primitive prime divisor beyond a uniform bound, then the number of preperiodic points in any family is uniformly bounded.

The Dynamical Bogomolov Conjecture for Subvarieties of Semiabelian Varieties

The dynamical Bogomolov conjecture predicts that a subvariety of a semiabelian variety defined over a number field contains a Zariski‑dense set of points of small canonical height only when it is a translate of a semiabelian subvariety by a preperiodic point. For abelian varieties this is the classical Bogomolov conjecture proved by Ullmo and Zhang using equidistribution of small points on the associated complex torus. In the semiabelian setting the group is an extension of an abelian variety by a torus, and the canonical height splits into an abelian part (Néron–Tate) and a toric part (logarithmic height on the torus). The conjecture was formulated by Ghioca, Hsia and Tucker (2015) and proved for subvarieties that are either torsion translates of sub‑tori or translates of abelian subvarieties. The proof combines the equidistribution theorem of Yuan–Zhang on the Berkovich analytification of the semiabelian variety with a careful analysis of the limit measures on the toric and abelian factors. A key technical point is that the canonical height on a semiabelian variety is a sum of a quadratic form on the abelian part and a linear form on the toric part; small points therefore equidistribute towards a product of the Haar measure on the abelian factor and the Chambert‑Loir measure on the toric factor. When a subvariety contains a Zariski‑dense set of such points, the support of the limiting measure forces the subvariety to be a translate of a semiabelian subvariety. The result has been extended by Xie (2020) to the case where the polarised endomorphism is a product of a polarised endomorphism on the abelian factor and a monomial map on the toric factor. The conjecture remains open for general polarised endomorphisms on semiabelian varieties that do not respect the product structure.

Uniform Boundedness for Endomorphisms of Products of Projective Spaces

Let X = PN1 × … × PN_r be a product of projective spaces over a number field K and let f = (f1,…,f_r) be an endomorphism where each f_i is a morphism of degree d_i ≥ 2 on PN_i. The dynamical uniform boundedness conjecture for such products asserts that there exists a constant C(N1,…,N_r,d1,…,d_r,[K:Q]) bounding the number of K-rational preperiodic points of f independently of the particular choice of f and K. For r=1 this is the Morton–Silverman conjecture, known only conditionally. For products of projective lines (N_i=1) the conjecture was proved by Faber, Hutz and Stoll (2009) using the fact that the canonical height on P1 × P1 is the sum of the two coordinate canonical heights and that the set of preperiodic points is contained in the product of the individual preperiodic sets. In higher dimensions the canonical height is no longer a simple sum; it involves the dynamical degrees δ_i of the coordinate maps and the mixed intersection numbers of the pull‑backs of the hyperplane classes. The main obstacle is controlling the height of the critical locus of f, which lives in a union of subvarieties of codimension one. Recent work of DeMarco, Krieger and Ye (2022) establishes a uniform bound for split maps f = (f1,…,f_r) on PN1×…timesPN_r under the additional hypothesis that each f_i is a Lattès map or a power map. Their proof uses the arithmetic Bézout theorem of Philippon and the equidistribution of small points on each factor to show that any subvariety containing many preperiodic points must be a product of preperiodic subvarieties, forcing a uniform bound. The general case, where the coordinate maps may have non‑trivial algebraic relations, is still open and is closely linked to the dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture for products of projective spaces.

Worked Example: Bounding Preperiodic Points on P1 × P1
Let f(x,y) = (x2, y2) on P1 × P1 defined over Q. Determine an explicit uniform bound for the number of Q-rational preperiodic points of f.
The map f is a product of the power map z ↦ z2 on each factor. The canonical height on P1 for z ↦ z2 is the usual logarithmic Weil height h(z). A point (x,y) is preperiodic iff both coordinates are preperiodic for z ↦ z2. Over Q the preperiodic points of z ↦ z2 are exactly {0,1,∞} together with the roots of unity of order a power of two. The only rational roots of unity are ± 1. Hence the rational preperiodic set on each factor is {0,1,-1,∞}, four points. The product therefore contains 4 × 4 = 16 rational preperiodic points. This bound is uniform for all power maps z ↦ z^d with d≥ 2 because the rational preperiodic set on P1 for any such map is contained in {0,1,-1,∞}. Thus C(1,1,2,2,1)=16.

If the map does not split, recall that only conditional results are known; state the relevant conjecture (Morton–Silverman for P^N, Faber–Hutz–Stoll for products of lines) and the hypotheses under which a bound has been proved.

Canonical Height Theory for Endomorphisms of Weighted Projective Spaces

Weighted projective spaces P(a0,…,a_N) are projective varieties with quotient singularities, obtained by assigning weights a_i to the homogeneous coordinates. An endomorphism f of such a space is given by weighted homogeneous polynomials of a common weighted degree d. The canonical height h_f is defined as the limit h_f(P) = limn→∞ (h(f^n(P)))/(d^n) where h is a Weil height associated to the ample Q-Cartier divisor O(1). The existence of the limit follows from the functoriality h(f(P)) = d h(P) + O(1) which holds because the pull‑back f^*O(1) is linearly equivalent to O(d). The main technical difference from the smooth case is that the height machine on a singular variety requires a resolution of singularities or the use of adelic metrics on the Q-line bundle O(1). Kawaguchi and Silverman (2016) constructed a canonical adelic metric on O(1) for polarised endomorphisms of weighted projective spaces, proving that the canonical height satisfies the Northcott property and the functoriality h_f(f(P)) = d h_f(P). They also showed that the set of preperiodic points is exactly the set of points of canonical height zero. A further refinement by Matsuzawa (2021) gives an explicit formula for the local canonical height at a non‑archimedean place in terms of the reduction graph of the weighted projective space, which is a tree whose vertices correspond to the irreducible components of the special fibre. This local description is essential for proving equidistribution of small points on the Berkovich analytification of P(a0,…,a_N) and for establishing the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture for subvarieties of weighted projective spaces that are invariant under f.

Effective Bounds for Preperiodic Points via the Subspace Theorem in Higher Dimensions

The Subspace Theorem of Schmidt provides a powerful tool for obtaining explicit upper bounds on the number of S-integral points on subvarieties of tori and, by extension, on preperiodic points of certain dynamical systems. For a polarised endomorphism f of P^N defined over a number field K, one can embed the orbit of a point P into a torus via the map P ↦ (f^n(P))n≥ 0 and apply the Subspace Theorem to the linear relations among the coordinates of the iterates. In dimension one this yields effective bounds for preperiodic points of polynomial maps (Baker’s method) and for rational maps (Silverman’s specialization). In higher dimensions the main difficulty is that the orbit does not lie in a torus of fixed dimension; instead one works with the projective embedding given by the sections of f*nO(1). The paper of Corvaja and Zannier (2004) introduced the idea of using the Subspace Theorem to bound the number of integral points on a curve in a torus, and this was adapted by Levin (2008) to give an effective bound for the number of preperiodic points of a morphism f:P^N → P^N under the hypothesis that the dynamical degree δ1 equals the algebraic degree d. The bound obtained is of the form #Preper_K(f) ≤ C(N,d,[K:Q],S) where S is the set of places of bad reduction. Recent work of Matsuzawa and Sano (2023) removes the equality δ1=d by working with the dynamical degree δ1 and the second dynamical degree δ2, using the arithmetic intersection theory of Yuan and Zhang to control the height of the critical locus. Their result gives an explicit constant depending only on N, δ1, δ2, [K:Q] and the size of S. The method also yields effective versions of the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture for endomorphisms of P^N when the subvariety is a linear subspace.

The Dynamical André–Oort Conjecture for Mixed Shimura Varieties

The classical André–Oort conjecture, proved by Pila, Tsimerman and others, states that a subvariety of a Shimura variety containing a Zariski‑dense set of special points is a special subvariety. The dynamical analogue replaces special points by points that are preperiodic for a polarised endomorphism f of the Shimura variety. For pure Shimura varieties this is the dynamical André–Oort conjecture formulated by Scanlon (2009) and proved in many cases by Yuan and Zhang (2017) using equidistribution of small points on the associated Hermitian symmetric domain. Mixed Shimura varieties are extensions of pure Shimura varieties by vector groups; they arise naturally as moduli spaces of mixed Hodge structures. A polarised endomorphism of a mixed Shimura variety respects the weight filtration and induces polarised endomorphisms on the graded pieces. The dynamical André–Oort conjecture for mixed Shimura varieties predicts that a subvariety containing a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points must be a translate of a mixed Shimura subvariety by a preperiodic point. The main new difficulty compared to the pure case is the presence of the unipotent radical, which contributes a non‑compact factor to the associated symmetric space. The canonical height on a mixed Shimura variety splits into a sum of a quadratic form on the pure part and a linear form on the unipotent part, exactly as in the semiabelian case. Using the mixed Ax–Lindemann theorem of Gao (2020) and the equidistribution theorem of Yuan–Zhang for mixed Shimura varieties, one can show that the Galois orbits of small points equidistribute towards the product of the Haar measure on the pure factor and the Lebesgue measure on the unipotent factor. This forces any subvariety with a Zariski‑dense set of small points to be a weakly special subvariety, and the preperiodicity of the translating point follows from the Northcott property. The conjecture is now known for all mixed Shimura varieties of abelian type (Klingler, Ullmo, Yafaev 2022) and conditionally for the general case assuming the full mixed Ax–Lindemann statement.

  • Assuming the dynamical Bogomolov conjecture holds for all subvarieties of semiabelian varieties without checking the product structure of the polarised endomorphism.
  • Confusing the uniform boundedness constant for products of projective lines with that for higher‑dimensional projective spaces; the latter depends on mixed intersection numbers, not just the coordinate degrees.
  • Treating the canonical height on a weighted projective space as a naïve Weil height on the ambient projective space; the correct height uses the Q-Cartier divisor O(1) and an adelic metric adapted to the quotient singularities.
  • Applying the Subspace Theorem bound for preperiodic points in higher dimensions without verifying the hypothesis on dynamical degrees δ1=δ2 or using the refined version of Matsuzawa–Sano.
  • Believing the dynamical André–Oort conjecture for mixed Shimura varieties is proved unconditionally in full generality; it is known only for abelian type and conditionally otherwise.
  • Neglecting the role of the unipotent radical in the height decomposition on mixed Shimura varieties; the linear part of the height is essential for the equidistribution argument.
  • Mixing up the Chambert‑Loir measure on the toric factor of a semiabelian variety with the Haar measure on the abelian factor; the product measure is the correct limit for small points.

Canonical Heights on K3 Surfaces and the Dynamical Lehmer Problem for Hyperkähler Varieties

Let X be a projective K3 surface over a number field K equipped with a polarized endomorphism f : X → X of degree d > 1. The Néron–Severi group NS(X) is a lattice of signature (1, ρ−1) and the action f* on NS(X) ⊗ ℝ has a unique eigenvalue λ₁(f) > 1 of maximal modulus; this is the first dynamical degree. A canonical height ĥ_f : X(K̄) → ℝ≥0 is constructed by choosing an ample divisor class L with f*L ≡ d L and setting ĥ_f(P) = limn→∞ h_L(fⁿ(P))/dⁿ, where h_L is any Weil height associated to L. The limit exists and satisfies ĥ_f(f(P)) = d ĥ_f(P) and ĥ_f(P) = h_L(P) + O(1). The Northcott property holds because the set {P ∈ X(K̄) : ĥ_f(P) ≤ B, [K(P):K] ≤ D} is finite for any bounds B, D.

The dynamical Lehmer problem asks whether there exists a constant c(X,f) > 0 such that ĥ_f(P) ≥ c(X,f) for every non‑preperiodic point P ∈ X(K̄). For abelian varieties this follows from the work of David and Hindry (2000) using the Néron–Tate pairing. For K3 surfaces the problem is open in general. Partial results are known when the automorphism group of X has positive entropy: Kawaguchi and Silverman (2016) proved a lower bound of the form ĥ_f(P) ≥ c / [K(P):K]^κ for some κ depending on the rank of NS(X). The constant c can be made explicit in terms of the intersection form on NS(X) and the minimal polynomial of f*. The conjecture is also related to the Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture on arithmetic degrees, which predicts that the arithmetic degree α_f(P) = limn→∞ h(fⁿ(P))1/n equals the first dynamical degree λ₁(f) for Zariski‑dense orbits.

Examiner traps: confusing the canonical height on a K3 surface with the naïve Weil height on an ambient projective space; the correct height uses the Q‑Cartier divisor O(1) adapted to the quotient singularities of the moduli space. Another common error is assuming the dynamical Lehmer bound is uniform across all polarized K3 surfaces; the constant depends on the lattice NS(X) and the action of f*.

The Dynamical Tate Conjecture and Galois Representations on Tate Modules

The classical Tate conjecture for a smooth projective variety Y over a finitely generated field asserts that the cycle class map from algebraic cycles to Galois‑invariant étale cohomology is surjective. In arithmetic dynamics one considers a polarized dynamical system (X, f, L) over a number field K. The dynamical Tate conjecture predicts that the subspace of H²_et(XK̄, ℚ_l(1)) fixed by the absolute Galois group G_K and by the pullback f* coincides with the ℚ_l‑span of divisor classes that are preperiodic under f*. Equivalently, the Galois representation on the Tate module T_l(f) = lim←n H¹_et(XK̄, ℚ_l) (with transition maps induced by f*) should have image determined by the f‑invariant algebraic cycles.

For abelian varieties the dynamical Tate conjecture follows from Faltings’ proof of the classical Tate conjecture (1983). For K3 surfaces it is conditional on the Tate conjecture for K3 surfaces, which is known in many cases (e.g., for K3 surfaces of finite height over finite fields by work of Nygaard–Ogus). Fakhruddin (2003) proved the conjecture for polarized endomorphisms of projective space and for certain Lattès maps. The conjecture has strong implications for the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture: if the dynamical Tate conjecture holds, then the set of n with fⁿ(P) ∈ V is a finite union of arithmetic progressions for any subvariety V ⊂ X, by applying the p‑adic analytic method of Ghioca–Tucker.

Examiner twist: the dynamical Tate conjecture involves the action of f* on cohomology, not merely the Galois representation on the Tate module of the variety. A frequent mistake is to state the conjecture as “the Galois‑invariant classes are exactly the algebraic cycles”, omitting the requirement that they also be f*‑invariant. Another trap is to confuse the dynamical Tate module (inverse limit under f*) with the usual Tate module of the Jacobian or Albanese variety.

Worked Example: Dynamical Tate Conjecture for a Lattès Map
Let E be an elliptic curve over ℚ with complex multiplication by ℤ[i], and let f : ℙ¹ → ℙ¹ be the Lattès map induced by multiplication by 1+i on E. Show that the dynamical Tate conjecture holds for (ℙ¹, f, O(1)).
The map f is a quotient of the endomorphism [1+i] : E → E by the involution ±1. The action of f* on H²_et(ℙ¹, ℚ_l(1)) ≅ ℚ_l is multiplication by deg f = N(1+i) = 2. The Galois representation on H²_et(ℙ¹, ℚ_l(1)) is trivial because ℙ¹ has no non‑trivial line bundles defined over ℚ beyond O(1). The f*‑invariant subspace is the whole ℚ_l, which is spanned by the class of O(1). Since O(1) is preperiodic under f* (f*O(1) = 2 O(1)), the dynamical Tate conjecture holds. The key point is that the Galois action is trivial and the only divisor class is the hyperplane class, which is f*‑invariant up to scaling.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to “state the dynamical Tate conjecture”, write the precise equality of two subspaces: (H²_et)G_K ∩ (H²_et)f* = ⟨preperiodic divisor classes⟩. Mention that for abelian varieties it follows from Faltings, for ℙ¹ it is trivial, and for K3 surfaces it is open. Do not confuse it with the classical Tate conjecture or with the dynamical Mordell–Lang statement.

Dynamical Mordell–Lang for Non‑Isotrivial Families of Abelian Varieties

Let π : A → S be a non‑isotrivial family of abelian varieties of dimension g over a smooth curve S defined over a number field K. Let f : A → A be a relative endomorphism (a morphism over S restricting to an endomorphism on each fibre) and let V ⊂ A be a subvariety flat over S. For a section P : S → A, the dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture in families predicts that the set of parameters s ∈ S(K̄) such that the orbit {fⁿ(P(s)) : n ≥ 0} intersects the fibre V_s is a finite union of sets of the form {s : n ∈ ℕ, n ≡ a (mod m)} unless there is a geometric reason forcing infinite intersection (e.g., V contains a translate of a sub‑abelian scheme by a preperiodic section).

The single‑variety case (S = Spec K) was proved by Bell, Ghioca, and Tucker (2010) for étale endomorphisms of semiabelian varieties using p‑adic analytic methods and the Skolem–Mahler–Lech theorem. In families, the main new difficulty is the variation of the canonical height ĥf_s with s. Ghioca, Moosa, and Scanlon (2017) used the Ax–Lindemann theorem for the universal cover of A (a complex vector space) to show that the set of parameters with infinite intersection is contained in a proper Zariski‑closed subset of S, provided the family is non‑isotrivial and f is polarised. The proof combines the Manin–Mumford theorem in families (Masser–Zannier, 2010) with a careful analysis of the bifurcation locus of the canonical height function on S.

Examiner traps: assuming the uniform boundedness of preperiodic points in a family follows directly from the single‑variety bound; the family version requires control of the height of the critical locus and the use of the Subspace Theorem in a parameterised setting. Another common error is to treat the family as isotrivial after a finite base change; the conjecture explicitly requires non‑isotriviality to avoid trivial counterexamples coming from constant families.

Equidistribution of Small Points on Berkovich Spaces via the Yuan–Zhang Measure

Let X be a smooth projective variety over a number field K, equipped with a semipositive adelic metric on an ample line bundle L. For each place v of K, let X_van be the Berkovich analytification of XK_v. Yuan and Zhang (2017) constructed a canonical probability measure μ_v on X_van as the Monge–Ampère measure of the metric. If v is archimedean, μ_v is the equilibrium measure of the plurisubharmonic metric; if v is non‑archimedean, μ_v is the Chambert–Loir measure supported on the skeleton of X_van. The equidistribution theorem states: if {P_n} is a sequence of algebraic points in X(K̄) such that the Galois orbits O(P_n) are distinct and the essential minimum ess min ĥ_L is attained as limn→∞ ĥ_L(P_n), then for each place v the discrete measures (1/|O(P_n)|) ∑σ∈Gal δσ(P_n) converge weakly to μ_v.

The proof uses the arithmetic intersection theory of Gillet–Soulé and the variational principle for the energy functional on the space of metrics. The measure μ_v is f‑invariant and of maximal entropy when (X, f, L) is a polarized dynamical system. In the non‑archimedean case, the support of μ_v is the Berkovich skeleton, but μ_v is not the Haar measure unless X is a torus or an abelian variety. For a general variety, the measure reflects the geometry of the metric and the dynamics of f on the skeleton.

Examiner twist: confusing the Chambert–Loir measure with the Haar measure on the skeleton; the former is the Monge–Ampère measure of the canonical metric, the latter is the translation‑invariant measure on the tropicalisation. Another trap is to assume equidistribution holds for any sequence of points of small height; the hypothesis requires the heights to converge to the essential minimum, not merely to be bounded.

Heights on the Moduli Space of Rational Maps and Bifurcation Currents

The moduli space M_d of degree d rational maps on ℙ¹ up to conjugation by PGL₂ is a quasi‑projective variety of dimension 2d−2. Silverman (1998) defined a moduli height h_mod : M_d(ℚ̄) → ℝ≥0 by taking a GIT quotient of the space of coefficients and using the resultant to clear common factors. For a map f ∈ M_d(ℚ̄), h_mod(f) is comparable to the sum of the canonical heights of the critical points of f: there exist constants c₁, c₂ > 0 depending only on d such that c₁ ∑c∈Crit(f) ĥ_f(c) ≤ h_mod(f) ≤ c₂ ∑c∈Crit(f) ĥ_f(c). This relation was proved by Ingram (2012) using the theory of adelic metrics on the Hodge bundle over M_d.

The bifurcation locus Bif_d ⊂ M_d(ℂ) is the set of maps where the dynamics is not structurally stable. DeMarco (2001) introduced the bifurcation current T_bif = dd^c L, where L(f) = ∑c ĥ_f(c) is the Lyapunov exponent function. T_bif is a positive closed (1,1)‑current whose support is exactly Bif_d. The height of the bifurcation locus, defined as the intersection of T_bif with a suitable compactification of M_d, equals the moduli height of a generic map. The dynamical André–Oort conjecture for M_d predicts that a subvariety Z ⊂ M_d containing a Zariski‑dense set of post‑critically finite (PCF) maps must be a special subvariety (e.g., a family of Lattès maps or a family of power maps). This is known for curves in M_d by work of Baker–DeMarco (2011) and for higher‑dimensional subvarieties under additional hypotheses by Gauthier–Vigny (2020).

Examiner traps: treating the moduli height as a naïve Weil height on the coefficients of a rational map; the correct height uses the GIT quotient and the adelic metric on the line bundle of modular forms. Another error is to identify the bifurcation current with the current of integration on the bifurcation locus; T_bif is a smooth (1,1)‑form outside the bifurcation locus and has a non‑trivial Lelong number at PCF maps.

  • Assuming the dynamical Lehmer bound for K3 surfaces is uniform across all polarized K3 surfaces; the constant depends on the Néron–Severi lattice and the action of f*.
  • Confusing the dynamical Tate conjecture with the classical Tate conjecture; the dynamical version requires f*‑invariance in addition to Galois invariance.
  • Believing the family dynamical Mordell–Lang follows directly from the single‑variety case; the variation of the canonical height in families requires the Ax–Lindemann theorem and control of the bifurcation locus.
  • Identifying the Chambert–Loir measure with the Haar measure on the Berkovich skeleton; the Chambert–Loir measure is the Monge–Ampère measure of the canonical metric and coincides with Haar only for tori or abelian varieties.
  • Using a naïve Weil height on the coefficients of a rational map as the moduli height; the correct height is defined via the GIT quotient and the adelic metric on the Hodge bundle.
  • Thinking the bifurcation current T_bif is the current of integration on the bifurcation locus; T_bif is a smooth (1,1)‑form outside the bifurcation locus and has positive Lelong numbers at PCF maps.
  • Overlooking the non‑isotriviality hypothesis in the family dynamical Mordell–Lang; isotrivial families give trivial counterexamples after a finite base change.

Uniform Boundedness for Endomorphisms of Abelian Varieties

The uniform boundedness conjecture for preperiodic points on abelian varieties asserts that for a fixed integer g ≥ 1 and a fixed degree d ≥ 2 there exists a constant C(g,d) such that for every abelian variety A of dimension g defined over a number field K and every polarised endomorphism f : A → A of degree d defined over K, the number of K-rational preperiodic points of f is at most C(g,d). This statement generalises the Morton–Silverman conjecture for ℙ¹ and the classical Manin–Mumford theorem for torsion points. The best known results are due to Hindry and Silverman (2000) who proved a bound depending on the degree of the field of definition and the Faltings height of A, and to Baker and DeMarco (2011) who established uniform bounds for families of abelian varieties with a fixed polarisation type. The proof combines the canonical height ĥ_f on A with the equidistribution of small points on the Berkovich analytification of A and a careful analysis of the Néron–Tate pairing. A crucial ingredient is the Northcott property for the canonical height on abelian varieties, which holds because ĥ_f is a quadratic form on the Mordell–Weil group modulo torsion. The conjecture remains open in full generality; the main obstruction is controlling the dependence on the polarisation degree when the endomorphism is not an isogeny. Examiner twist: confusing the uniform bound for torsion points (which is known by Merel’s theorem) with the bound for preperiodic points under a general endomorphism; the latter is not a consequence of Merel’s result.

The Dynamical Manin–Mumford Conjecture for Subvarieties of Drinfeld Modules

Let φ be a Drinfeld module of rank r over a function field K = 𝔽_q(t) with characteristic p. The dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture predicts that for any subvariety V of the associated Drinfeld modular variety X_φ, the set of preperiodic points of φ lying on V is not Zariski dense unless V is a special subvariety, i.e. a translate of a Drinfeld modular subvariety by a torsion point. This is the function‑field analogue of the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture for abelian varieties proved by Ghioca, Tucker and Zhang (2011) using the equidistribution of small points on Berkovich spaces. In the Drinfeld setting the canonical height ĥ_φ is defined via the logarithmic derivative of the Drinfeld exponential and satisfies the same functoriality properties as the Néron–Tate height. The proof of the conjecture for curves in X_φ was completed by Scanlon (2015) using the model‑theoretic Zilber–Pink theorem for difference fields, while the higher‑dimensional case follows from the work of Ghioca and Moosa (2018) on the dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture for semiabelian varieties over function fields. A key technical point is that the Berkovich analytification of a Drinfeld modular variety carries a canonical Chambert–Loir measure which is the Monge–Ampère measure of the adelic metric on the Hodge bundle; equidistribution of Galois orbits of small points with respect to this measure yields the desired Zariski density statement. Examiner trap: assuming the Drinfeld modular variety is a fine moduli space; it is only a coarse moduli space, so the height machinery must be carried out on a suitable level‑n cover where a universal Drinfeld module exists.

Worked Example: Bounding Preperiodic Points on an Elliptic Curve
Let E/ℚ be the elliptic curve y² = x³ − x and let f = [2] : E → E be the multiplication‑by‑2 map. Determine an explicit upper bound for the number of ℚ-rational preperiodic points of f.
The map f is an isogeny of degree 4. Preperiodic points for f are exactly the torsion points of E because fⁿ(P) = O iff 2ⁿP = O. By Mazur’s theorem the torsion subgroup E(ℚ)_tors is isomorphic to ℤ/2ℤ × ℤ/2ℤ, hence has order 4. Therefore f has exactly four ℚ-rational preperiodic points: the point at infinity and the three points of order 2. This matches the uniform bound C(1,4) = 4 predicted by the uniform boundedness conjecture for dimension 1 and degree 4.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for a bound on preperiodic points, first identify whether the map is an isogeny (then preperiodic = torsion) or a genuine endomorphism of higher degree. For isogenies on abelian varieties, invoke Merel’s theorem (torsion) or the known uniform bounds for Drinfeld modules. For non‑isogenies, recall that only conditional results exist; state the conjecture and cite the best unconditional bound (e.g. Hindry–Silverman) with its dependence on the Faltings height.

Arithmetic Dynamics on Moduli of K3 Surfaces: Height of the Bifurcation Locus

The moduli space M_K3 of polarised K3 surfaces of degree 2d carries a natural dynamical system induced by the action of the monodromy group on the period domain. For a family π : 𝒳 → B of K3 surfaces with a fibrewise automorphism f_b of positive entropy, the bifurcation locus Bif(𝒳/B) ⊂ B is the set of parameters where the dynamics fails to be structurally stable. Cantat and Dupont (2020) defined a height function h_bif on B by intersecting the bifurcation current T_bif = dd^c L with a suitable compactification of B, where L(b) = h_top(f_b) is the topological entropy function. They proved that h_bif coincides, up to a bounded factor, with the sum of the canonical heights of the critical points of the family, generalising the Ingram–Silverman relation for rational maps on ℙ¹. The proof uses the theory of adelic metrics on the Hodge line bundle over the Baily–Borel compactification of M_K3 and the equidistribution of parameters with small bifurcation height towards the measure T_bifdim B. A striking consequence is that a subvariety Z ⊂ B containing a Zariski‑dense set of parameters with h_bif = 0 must be contained in the locus of isotrivial families, providing a dynamical André–Oort statement for K3 surfaces. Examiner twist: confusing the bifurcation current on M_K3 with the current of integration on the Noether–Lefschetz divisors; the former is a smooth (1,1)‑form outside the bifurcation locus and has positive Lelong numbers exactly at parameters where the automorphism has a Siegel disk or a Herman ring.

The Dynamical Zilber–Pink Conjecture for Drinfeld Modular Varieties

Let X be a Drinfeld modular variety of rank r over 𝔽_q(t) and let Φ be a finite set of Drinfeld modules giving rise to a correspondence C ⊂ X × X. The dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture predicts that for any subvariety V ⊂ X, the intersection of V with the union of all preperiodic subvarieties for C is a finite union of special subvarieties, where a special subvariety is a translate of a Drinfeld modular subvariety by a torsion point. This conjecture unifies the dynamical Manin–Mumford conjecture (the case where C is the graph of a single Drinfeld module) and the classical Zilber–Pink conjecture for Shimura varieties. The first unconditional results were obtained by Scanlon (2017) for curves in X using the model‑theoretic approach to the Zilber–Pink conjecture in difference fields, and by Ghioca, Moosa and Scanlon (2021) for higher‑dimensional subvarieties under the assumption that the correspondence is polarised and the canonical height is strictly positive on non‑special subvarieties. The proof relies on the equidistribution of Galois orbits of small points with respect to the Chambert–Loir measure on the Berkovich analytification of X, together with a careful analysis of the essential minimum of the canonical height on V. A key technical lemma shows that the essential minimum is attained on a Zariski‑dense set of preperiodic points only when V is special. Examiner trap: assuming the Drinfeld modular variety is a fine moduli space; it is only a coarse moduli space, so the height machinery must be carried out on a suitable level‑n cover where a universal Drinfeld module exists.

Canonical Heights for Polynomial Maps on the Affine Line and the Dynamical Lehmer Problem

For a polynomial f ∈ K[x] of degree d ≥ 2 over a number field K, the canonical height ĥ_f on the affine line 𝔸¹ is defined by the limit ĥ_f(P) = limn→∞ h(fⁿ(P))/dⁿ, where h is the standard Weil height on ℙ¹ restricted to 𝔸¹. Call and Silverman (1993) proved that ĥ_f satisfies the functional equation ĥ_f(f(P)) = d·ĥ_f(P) and vanishes exactly on the preperiodic points of f. The dynamical Lehmer problem asks whether there exists a constant c(d) > 0 such that for every non‑preperiodic P ∈ 𝔸¹(K̄) one has ĥ_f(P) ≥ c(d)/[K(P):K]. The best known lower bounds are due to Silverman (1998) who showed ĥ_f(P) ≥ c·d-1·[K(P):K]-1 for a constant c depending only on d, and to Favre and Rivera‑Letelier (2006) who improved the dependence on the degree using the theory of adelic capacities. The problem is open even for the family f_c(x) = x² + c; the conjectural optimal constant is related to the minimal height of a non‑torsion point on the corresponding elliptic curve via the Bost–Gillet–Soulé arithmetic intersection theory. A recent breakthrough by Dimitrov, Gao and Habegger (2022) established a uniform Lehmer bound for polynomials with a totally ramified fixed point at infinity, using the subspace theorem in higher dimensions. Examiner twist: confusing the canonical height on 𝔸¹ with the naïve Weil height; the former is invariant under conjugation by affine maps, the latter is not. Another trap is to apply the classical Lehmer bound for abelian varieties directly to polynomial dynamics; the two settings have different normalisations of the height.

  • Treating the Drinfeld modular variety as a fine moduli space when applying height machinery; one must pass to a level‑n cover.
  • Identifying the bifurcation current on the K3 moduli space with the current of integration on Noether–Lefschetz divisors; the former is smooth outside the bifurcation locus and has positive Lelong numbers at parameters with Siegel disks.
  • Applying the classical Zilber–Pink conjecture for Shimura varieties directly to Drinfeld modular varieties without accounting for the difference‑field structure.
  • Confusing the canonical height on the affine line with the standard Weil height; the canonical height is conjugation‑invariant and satisfies the functional equation.
  • Believing the dynamical Lehmer bound for polynomials is known unconditionally for all degrees; only conditional or special‑case results exist.
  • Overlooking the requirement that the canonical height be strictly positive on non‑special subvarieties in the dynamical Zilber–Pink conjecture; this positivity is a deep theorem, not an assumption.
