Arithmetic Dynamics — Height Functions, the Mordell Conjecture & Dynamical Systems over Number Fields

Arithmetic dynamics studies dynamical systems over number fields and finite fields, blending algebraic number theory with complex dynamics. A central tool is the height function h(P) measuring the arithmetic complexity of points — the Weil height on projective space, and the Néron-Tate canonical height on abelian varieties. The Mordell conjecture (proved by Faltings, 1983) states that a smooth projective curve of genus g ≥ 2 over a number field has finitely many rational points — a landmark result proved via arithmetic geometry. The Morton-Silverman uniform boundedness conjecture and the dynamical Mordell-Lang conjecture extend these ideas to dynamical systems. The equidistribution of preperiodic points (Bilu, Duke, Szpiro-Ullmo-Zhang) and the dynamical Manin-Mumford conjecture form the modern frontier, connecting heights, Diophantine geometry, and complex dynamics.

"Arithmetic dynamics is diophantine geometry modeled on complex dynamics."
— Joseph Silverman
Part 1

📏 Heights and the Northcott Property

Heights
Weil Height, Northcott's Theorem, and Canonical Heights
The Weil height on ℙⁿ(̄ℚ) is defined by h([x₀:...:xₙ]) = (1/[K:ℚ]) Σ_{v∈M_K} log max_i |x_i|_v, where K is a number field containing all coordinates and M_K is the set of places. This gives an absolute logarithmic height independent of the choice of field and coordinates up to a bounded error. Northcott's theorem (1950): for any B, D > 0, the set of algebraic points of degree ≤ D and height ≤ B is finite — a foundational finiteness result. The canonical height for abelian varieties: ȟ(P) = lim_{n→∞} h([n]P)/n², known as the Néron-Tate height, is quadratic on the Mordell-Weil group and non-degenerate modulo torsion. Tate's limit: for a morphism f of degree d, the canonical height is ȟ_f = lim_{n→∞} h(fⁿ(P))/dⁿ, giving a powerful tool for studying dynamical systems.
Height functions measure arithmetic complexity; Northcott's theorem shows finiteness of points with bounded height and degree — the bedrock of arithmetic dynamics.
Part 2

🌍 The Mordell Conjecture and Faltings' Theorem

Faltings
Mordell's Conjecture, the Parshin Trick, and the Proof via Arithmetic Geometry
Mordell's conjecture (1922): a smooth projective curve C of genus g ≥ 2 over a number field K has finitely many K-rational points. Proved by Faltings (1983) using profound methods from arithmetic geometry: Shimura varieties, the moduli space of abelian varieties A_g, the theory of Néron models, and a height function on the moduli space. The proof crucially uses the Parshin trick — associating to each rational point a family of curves (a covering of C ramified over that point) and studying the corresponding abelian varieties via the de Franchis-Severi theorem (geometric finiteness for abelian varieties). The Manin-Mumford conjecture (proved by Raynaud): the torsion points on an abelian variety are Zariski dense in the group of all torsion points, but the intersection of a curve of genus ≥ 2 with the torsion points is finite — a deep result about torsion points on curves.
Faltings' proof of the Mordell conjecture revolutionized arithmetic geometry, establishing finiteness of rational points on high-genus curves.
Part 3

🧮 The Dynamical Mordell-Lang and the Uniform Boundedness Conjecture

Dynamics
Mordell-Lang, Dynamical Mordell-Lang, and Uniform Boundedness
The Mordell-Lang conjecture (proved by Faltings, Vojta, McQuillan): if A is a semiabelian variety and X ⊂ A is a subvariety, then X(K) is a finite union of cosets of subgroups of A — a sweeping generalization. The dynamical Mordell-Lang (Derksen-Masser, Bell-Ghioca-Tucker): for a self-map f: X → X, the intersection of an orbit with a subvariety Y is "sparse" — a finite union of arithmetic progressions. The Morton-Silverman uniform boundedness conjecture: the number of rational preperiodic points for a rational function over a number field is bounded by a constant depending only on the degree and the field. Silverman's height bound: for a non-isotrivial elliptic curve, the canonical height of preperiodic points is bounded below by a positive constant. The Szpiro-Ullmo-Zhang equidistribution: the preperiodic points of the multiplication-by-2 map on a CM elliptic curve equidistribute with respect to the canonical measure on the curve.
The dynamical Mordell-Lang and uniform boundedness conjectures extend the classical Diophantine finiteness paradigm to the setting of dynamical systems.
Part 4

🔥 Equidistribution and the Dynamical Manin-Mumford

Equidistribution
Bilu's Theorem, Dynamical Manin-Mumford, and Applications
Bilu's theorem (1997): the Galois orbit of a sequence of algebraic numbers of height → 0 is equidistributed on the unit circle with respect to the uniform measure — a striking link between height and distribution. The dynamical Manin-Mumford conjecture (Zhang, 1990s): for a polarized dynamical system (X, f, L), a subvariety Y ⊂ X contains a Zariski dense set of preperiodic points iff Y itself is preperiodic — a dynamical analog of the classical Manin-Mumford and Mordell-Lang conjectures. The equidistribution of small points on abelian varieties (Szpiro-Ullmo-Zhang, 1997): points of small Néron-Tate height equidistribute with respect to the Arakelov-Calabi measure. The Yuan-Zhang theorem on equidistribution of points of small height on algebraic tori generalizes Bilu's result. Applications include the Bogomolov conjecture (proved by Ullmo, 1998): on a curve of genus ≥ 2, the set of points with canonical height < ε is finite — a refinement of the Mordell conjecture.
Equidistribution theorems connect height functions to analytic measures, while the dynamical Manin-Mumford conjecture seeks to characterize preperiodic subvarieties.

Canonical Heights on Projective Varieties and Functoriality

The canonical height attached to a polarizable endomorphism f : X → X on a projective variety over a number field K is defined by the Tate limit

ȟf(P) = limn→∞ h(fn(P)) / dn,

where d = deg(f) and h is any Weil height relative to an ample line bundle L with f*L ≅ L⊗d. Call and Silverman (1993) proved that this limit exists for all P ∈ X(K̄) and that ȟf satisfies the fundamental functional equation ȟf(f(P)) = d · ȟf(P). The height is quadratic on the Néron–Severi group when X is an abelian variety and f = [m]; in that case it coincides with the Néron–Tate height.

Functoriality is a key structural property: if φ : X → Y is a morphism of projective varieties equipped with polarizable endomorphisms f on X and g on Y such that φ ∘ f = g ∘ φ, then ȟf(P) = ȟg(φ(P)) for all P. This allows one to transfer height information across semiconjugacies and is essential in the proof of the dynamical Mordell–Lang theorem for semiabelian varieties. The canonical height also decomposes as a sum of local heights ȟf,v over all places v of K, each satisfying the same functional equation. The existence of local canonical heights was established by Néron for abelian varieties and by Call–Silverman for general polarizable maps using the theory of adelic metrics.

An important consequence is the Northcott property for ȟf: for any bound B and degree D, the set {P ∈ X(K̄) : [K(P) : K] ≤ D, ȟf(P) ≤ B} is finite. This finiteness underpins many Diophantine applications, including the proof that preperiodic points are exactly the points of canonical height zero. The height machine developed by Call–Silverman also yields a canonical height for any morphism f : ℙN → ℙN of degree d ≥ 2, given by ȟf(P) = lim h(fn(P)) / dn where h is the standard logarithmic height on projective space.

Local Heights, Adelic Metrics, and the Arithmetic Intersection Theory

For each place v of a number field K, a local canonical height ȟf,v can be defined on the Berkovich analytic space Xanv when v is non‑archimedean, and on the complex manifold X(ℂ) when v is archimedean. These local heights arise from an adelic metric on the line bundle L that is invariant under the pullback f* up to the factor d. The construction uses the theory of arithmetic intersection developed by Gillet and Soulé (1990) and extended by Zhang (1995) to dynamical settings. An adelic metric is a collection of continuous metrics ‖·‖v on Lv for all places v, satisfying a product formula and the invariance condition f*‖·‖v = ‖·‖vd.

The global canonical height is then the arithmetic intersection number ȟf(P) = (1/[K:ℚ]) · (̂c1(L, ‖·‖) · P) where ̂c1 denotes the arithmetic first Chern class. This perspective connects height functions to Arakelov geometry and allows the use of powerful tools such as the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel theorem and the arithmetic Bézout theorem. Yuan (2008) proved an equidistribution theorem for points of small height: if {Pn} is a generic sequence in X(K̄) with ȟf(Pn) → 0, then the Galois orbits of Pn equidistribute with respect to the canonical measure μf,v = ddc log ‖·‖v at each place v. This generalizes the Szpiro–Ullmo–Zhang equidistribution on abelian varieties and Bilu’s theorem on the unit circle.

Adelic metrics also provide a framework for the dynamical Lehmer problem: does there exist a uniform positive lower bound for ȟf(P) when P is not preperiodic? For polarizable endomorphisms on abelian varieties, the Bogomolov conjecture (proved by Ullmo 1998 and Zhang 1998) gives a positive answer. In the general dynamical setting, the Kawaguchi–Silverman conjecture predicts a similar lower bound depending only on the degree of f and the dimension of X. Recent work by Dimitrov–Gao–Habegger (2021) established the conjecture for split rational maps on ℙ1 × ℙ1 using the arithmetic intersection theory of adelic line bundles.

Worked Example: Computing a Local Canonical Height on ℙ1
Let f(z) = z2 on ℙ1 over ℚ. For the place v = ∞, the canonical metric on O(1) is given by ‖(x,y)‖∞ = max(|x|,|y|). Compute the local canonical height ȟf,∞([1:2]).
The standard logarithmic height at ∞ is h∞([x:y]) = log max(|x|,|y|). For f(z)=z2, the functional equation ȟf,∞(f(P)) = 2 ȟf,∞(P) holds. Since the canonical metric is invariant, ȟf,∞([x:y]) = log max(|x|,|y|). For [1:2], max(1,2)=2, so ȟf,∞([1:2]) = log 2.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for a canonical height computation, first identify the polarizing line bundle and the degree d. Use the functional equation to reduce the point to a simpler iterate if needed, then apply the explicit local metric (often the sup‑norm for archimedean places). Remember that the global height is the sum of local heights weighted by the degree of the field extension.

The Dynamical André–Oort Conjecture and Special Subvarieties

The classical André–Oort conjecture (proved by Pila–Tsimerman 2014 and Klingler–Yafaev 2014) states that a subvariety of a Shimura variety containing a Zariski‑dense set of special points must be a special subvariety. The dynamical analogue, formulated by Baker and DeMarco (2013), replaces Shimura varieties by parameter spaces of dynamical systems and special points by post‑critically finite (PCF) maps. Let Md be the moduli space of degree‑d rational maps on ℙ1 up to conjugation. A point in Md is PCF if the corresponding map has all critical points preperiodic. The dynamical André–Oort conjecture predicts that an irreducible subvariety V ⊂ Md containing a Zariski‑dense set of PCF maps must be a special subvariety, i.e., a component of a Hecke correspondence or a family with a persistent symmetry.

Significant progress has been made using o‑minimality and the Pila–Wilkie counting theorem. Ghioca–Hsia–Tucker (2017) proved the conjecture for curves in M2 that are not contained in the locus of maps with a marked periodic point. Yuan and Zhang (2017) established a quantitative version: if a curve C ⊂ Md contains many PCF points of bounded height, then C is special. The proof combines the equidistribution of PCF parameters with respect to the bifurcation measure (DeMarco 2001) and the arithmetic equidistribution theorem of Yuan for points of small height on the moduli space equipped with the Hodge line bundle.

Special subvarieties in Md are classified by the work of McMullen (1994) and Milnor (1993): they are either families of maps with a marked critical orbit relation (e.g., the quadratic family z2+c) or families arising from Lattès maps. The dynamical André–Oort conjecture thus provides a bridge between unlikely intersections in moduli spaces and the classification of exceptional dynamical families. It also has consequences for the uniform boundedness conjecture: if a curve in Md contains infinitely many maps with a rational preperiodic point of a given period, then the curve must be special, which restricts the possible counterexamples to the Morton–Silverman conjecture.

p‑Adic Dynamics, Berkovich Spaces, and Equidistribution

Over a non‑archimedean field K (e.g., ℚp or ℂp), the natural setting for iteration of rational maps is the Berkovich projective line ℙ1,anK, a compact, path‑connected Hausdorff space containing ℙ1(K) as a dense subset. Rivera‑Letelier (2003) and Favre–Rivera‑Letelier (2006) developed a theory of canonical measures μf on ℙ1,an for rational maps f of degree ≥ 2. The measure μf is the unique probability measure satisfying f*μf = d · μf and f*μf = μf, and it is supported on the Julia set of f in the Berkovich sense.

Equidistribution of preperiodic points holds in this setting: if {Pn} is a sequence of distinct preperiodic points of f defined over K̄, then the Galois orbits of Pn equidistribute to μf on ℙ1,an. This theorem, proved by Favre–Rivera‑Letelier (2006) and independently by Baker–Rumely (2006), is the non‑archimedean counterpart of the complex equidistribution of Brolin (1965) and Lyubich (1983). The proof uses the theory of capacities on Berkovich spaces and the fact that the canonical height ȟf vanishes exactly on preperiodic points.

Berkovich spaces also provide a framework for the p‑adic dynamical Mordell–Lang conjecture. Ghioca–Hsia–Tucker (2015) proved that for a rational map f on ℙ1 over a function field of characteristic p > 0, the intersection of an orbit with a finite set is a finite union of arithmetic progressions, provided the map is not isotrivial. Their method uses the p‑adic uniformization of the Berkovich line and the theory of heights on Drinfeld modules. In the number field case, the p‑adic analytic approach of Bell–Ghioca–Tucker (2010) gives a proof of the dynamical Mordell–Lang theorem for ℙ1 by reducing to the classical Mordell–Lang theorem on semiabelian varieties via the p‑adic logarithm on the Jacobian of a suitable curve.

Recent work by Yuan (2021) extends the equidistribution theorem to higher‑dimensional Berkovich varieties, proving that points of small height with respect to an adelic semipositive metric equidistribute to the Chambert‑Loir measure. This provides a unified proof of the equidistribution of preperiodic points for polarizable endomorphisms on any projective variety over a global field, simultaneously covering the archimedean and non‑archimedean cases.

  • Confusing the canonical height with a naive Weil height; the canonical height satisfies the exact functional equation, the naive height only up to bounded error.
  • Assuming the dynamical André–Oort conjecture is fully proved; it is known only for curves and certain special families.
  • Neglecting the role of adelic metrics in the definition of local heights; without the invariance condition the decomposition fails.
  • Treating Berkovich spaces as mere completions of ℙ1(ℚp); they contain additional points (type II–IV) essential for the dynamics.
  • Overlooking the distinction between preperiodic points (height zero) and points of small positive height; equidistribution requires the height to tend to zero, not just be bounded.
  • Remember that the uniform boundedness conjecture remains open in general; known bounds depend on the degree and the field but are not yet uniform across all fields.
