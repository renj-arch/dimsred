Condensed Mathematics — Condensed Sets, Solid Modules & the Analytic Patching

Condensed mathematics is a new foundation for analytic geometry developed by Dustin Clausen and Peter Scholze. It replaces topological spaces with condensed sets — sheaves on the site of profinite sets — creating a "convenient category" that contains all topological spaces while allowing free constructions, quotients, and internal Hom objects that fail in the topological category. The site ProFin consists of profinite sets (totally disconnected compact Hausdorff spaces) with finite families of jointly surjective maps as coverings. A condensed set is a sheaf on this site. Every topological space T determines a condensed set via S ↦ Cont(S, T) (continuous maps from a profinite set S to T). Unlike topological spaces, the category Cond is a topos — it is cartesian closed, has all limits and colimits, and admits a well-behaved notion of abelian group objects. From this foundation, Clausen and Scholze build solid modules (the condensed analog of complete topological vector spaces) and liquid modules (an analytic generalization over ℝ), reconstructing large parts of functional analysis without the usual pathologies (non-closed sums of subspaces, failures of the open mapping theorem, etc.). The theory culminates in the Fargues-Scholze geometric local Langlands correspondence for GL_n over ℚ_p, a landmark proof using the Fargues-Fontaine curve and the theory of shtukas, where condensed mathematics provides the language for the stack of G-bundles as a small v-stack.

Part 1

📏 Condensed Sets

Foundations
ProFin, Sheaves, and the Topos Cond
The site of profinite sets ProFin has objects profinite sets (cofiltered limits of finite sets, equivalently totally disconnected compact Hausdorff spaces) and coverings given by finite families of surjections {S_i → S} that are jointly surjective. A condensed set X is a sheaf on ProFin: a functor X: ProFin^op → Set such that for any surjection S' → S of profinite sets, the diagram X(S) → X(S') ⇉ X(S' ×_S S') is an equalizer. Equivalently, condensed sets are pyknotic sets (Barwick-Haine) — the difference is a set-theoretic size condition. Every topological space T gives a condensed set via T(S) = Cont(S, T), the set of continuous maps from S to T. This functor is fully faithful when T is compactly generated Hausdorff, so condensed sets generalize such spaces. The category Cond is a topos — it has finite limits, is cartesian closed (admits internal Hom objects), and has a subobject classifier. This solves classical problems: in Top, the quotient of a topological space by an equivalence relation does not always have the correct universal property; in Cond, quotients and colimits are always well-behaved. The internal Hom Hom_Cond(X, Y) is a condensed set defined by Hom_Cond(X, Y)(S) = Hom_Cond(X × S, Y). The existence of the internal Hom allows the construction of mapping spaces, loop spaces, and free condensed abelian groups that are impossible in the ordinary topological setting.
Condensed sets replace topological spaces with sheaves on profinite sets, yielding a topos where quotients, internal Homs, and free constructions exist — resolving the foundational pathologies of general topology.
Part 2

🌍 Condensed Abelian Groups and Solid Modules

Solid
Condensed Abelian Groups, Solidification, and the Derived Category
A condensed abelian group is a sheaf of abelian groups on ProFin — equivalently, an abelian group object in Cond. The category Cond(Ab) is a Grothendieck abelian category with enough projectives. Its derived category D(Cond(Ab)) is well-behaved. A condensed abelian group M is called solid if for every profinite set S = lim← S_i (written as a cofiltered limit of finite sets), the natural map (ℤ[S]^∧) ⊗ M → M(S) is an isomorphism, where ℤ[S]^∧ = lim← ℤ[S_i] is the completed free condensed abelian group on S. Equivalently, M is solid iff for every profinite set S, the map from the free condensed abelian group ℤ[S]^∧ to M is an isomorphism. The solidification functor M ↦ M^■ is the left adjoint to the inclusion Solid(Ab) → Cond(Ab). Solid modules form a full abelian subcategory closed under limits, colimits, and extensions. The key theorem (Scholze): the derived category D(Solid) is equivalent to the derived category of ℤ-vector spaces — D(Solid) ≅ D(ℤ). This is remarkable: it says that the homological algebra of solid modules reduces to ordinary homological algebra of ℤ-modules. Solid modules are the "correct" category for analytic geometry in the p-adic world, analogous to how complete topological vector spaces behave well in functional analysis. The condensed version of Banach spaces and the condensed approach to locally convex vector spaces eliminates the classic pathologies: the sum of two closed subspaces is always closed in the condensed sense, and the open mapping theorem holds universally.
Solid modules are the condensed analogue of complete topological vector spaces; their derived category reduces to D(ℤ), making them the ideal setting for p-adic analytic geometry.
Part 3

🧮 Analytic Rings and Liquid Modules

Analytic
Analytic Rings, Liquid Modules, and the Reconstruction of Functional Analysis
An analytic ring (A, A_■) is a condensed ring A together with a solid A-module structure A_■ — the "completion" functor — satisfying axioms that generalize the solid/liquid dichotomy. For the real numbers ℝ, the liquid module theory provides a category of "complete locally convex topological vector spaces" over ℝ equipped with the liquid condition: for each real number p ≥ 1, the liquid ℝ-module ℝ_{L^p spaces, Sobolev spaces, Schwartz distributions, and the theory of nuclear spaces all arise naturally as solid or liquid modules. The pathologies of topological vector spaces disappear: in the condensed/liquid framework, the sum of two closed subspaces is always closed, the open mapping theorem holds for all liquid Banach spaces, and the projective and injective tensor products are well-behaved. The condensed Hochschild homology and condensed cyclic homology define a cohomology theory for analytic rings that specializes to classical de Rham cohomology for smooth algebras and to p-adic Hodge theory for perfectoid rings. The analytic rings thus provide a unified framework for archimedean and non-archimedean geometry, bridging the gap between complex analysis and p-adic analysis.
Liquid modules over ℝ provide a pathology-free foundation for functional analysis, unifying archimedean and non-archimedean geometry through the concept of analytic rings.
Part 4

🔥 Applications: Fargues-Fontaine Curve and the Local Langlands

Langlands
The Fargues-Fontaine Curve, G-Bundles, and the Geometric Local Langlands
The Fargues-Fontaine curve X is a complete algebraic curve (over ℚ_p) that classifies untilts of a perfectoid field. Its remarkable property: the category of quasi-coherent sheaves on X is equivalent to the category of (φ, Γ)-modules over the Robba ring, realizing p-adic Galois representations geometrically. The Fargues-Scholze geometric Langlands correspondence (2021-2024) is a geometric version of the local Langlands program for GL_n(ℚ_p) using the Fargues-Fontaine curve and shtukas. Clausen and Scholze use condensed mathematics to define the stack of G-bundles Bun_G on the Fargues-Fontaine curve as a "condensed stack" — a small v-stack. The key theorem: the category of Weil representations of the Weil group of ℚ_p is equivalent to the category of certain perverse sheaves (with ℚ̅_ℓ-coefficients) on Bun_G. The derived Satake equivalence for the Fargues-Fontaine curve in the condensed setting provides the geometric input: the category of equivariant perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian is equivalent to the category of representations of the Langlands dual group. The Fargues-Scholze theorem constructs the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n(ℚ_p) purely geometrically, recovering and reinterpreting the classical local Langlands correspondence (Harris-Taylor, Henniart) through the geometry of the Fargues-Fontaine curve and the condensed formalism. This application demonstrates the power of condensed mathematics: it provides the necessary language to treat infinite-dimensional moduli spaces (like Bun_G) as algebraic geometric objects while retaining the analytic completeness properties essential for p-adic geometry.
The Fargues-Scholze geometric local Langlands correspondence uses condensed mathematics to give a purely geometric proof of the local Langlands correspondence for GL_n, via the Fargues-Fontaine curve and the stack of G-bundles as a condensed v-stack.
