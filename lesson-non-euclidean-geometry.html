Non-Euclidean Geometry — Hyperbolic Space, Geodesics & the Geometry of Negative Curvature

Non-Euclidean geometry arises from the denial of Euclid's parallel postulate — through a point not on a line there are infinitely many lines that do not intersect the given line. This leads to hyperbolic geometry, a world of constant negative curvature where triangles have angle sum less than π, the area of a triangle is proportional to its angle defect, and the space is unbounded yet infinite in extent. The field encompasses the Poincaré disk and upper half-plane models, the classification of surfaces by curvature (the uniformization theorem), the Gauss-Bonnet theorem linking curvature to topology, and the rich moduli theory of Riemann surfaces (Teichmüller space, Fenchel-Nielsen coordinates). Hyperbolic geometry is the geometry of surfaces of genus g ≥ 2 and of 3-manifolds in the Thurston geometrization program.

"In the hyperbolic plane, the angles of a triangle sum to less than π."
— János Bolyai
Part 1

📏 The Parallel Postulate and the Birth of Hyperbolic Geometry

Postulates
Euclid's Fifth, the Angle Defect, and the First Non-Euclidean Geometry
Euclid's five postulates of plane geometry: (1) a straight segment can be drawn joining any two points; (2) a segment can be extended indefinitely; (3) a circle can be drawn with any center and radius; (4) all right angles are equal; (5) the parallel postulate: if a line intersects two lines forming interior angles summing to less than π, the two lines meet on that side. For two millennia mathematicians tried to prove the parallel postulate from the first four. Girolamo Saccheri (1733) studied quadrilaterals with two right base angles (Saccheri quadrilaterals) and deduced contradictions he called "repugnant to the nature of a straight line" — actually the first theorems of hyperbolic geometry. Johann Heinrich Lambert (1766) proved that the area of a triangle in a "third-angle" geometry is proportional to its angle defect Δ = π − (α+β+γ). János Bolyai (1832) and Nikolai Lobachevsky (1829) independently discovered that a consistent geometry exists where through a point not on a line there are infinitely many parallels — the first non-Euclidean geometry. Eugenio Beltrami (1868) gave the first models: the pseudosphere (a surface of constant negative curvature K = −1 obtained by revolving a tractrix), showing that hyperbolic geometry is locally realized as a surface in ℝ³. The angle defect formula for a hyperbolic triangle: area = π − (α+β+γ) = Δ. Thus in hyperbolic geometry, area is bounded (≤ π) and directly measures the failure of a triangle to be Euclidean. Negative curvature K < 0 means geodesics diverge, triangles are thin, and the area grows exponentially with radius — space is "more voluminous" than Euclidean space.
Denying the parallel postulate yields a consistent geometry — hyperbolic geometry — where angle defect measures area, triangles are thin, and space is exponentially larger than Euclidean space.
Part 2

🌍 Models of the Hyperbolic Plane

Models
Poincaré Disk, Upper Half-Plane, Klein Model & Hyperbolic Isometries
The Poincaré disk model: ℍ² = {z ∈ ℂ : |z| < 1} with metric ds² = 4(dx²+dy²)/(1−|z|²)². It is conformal (angles measured Euclidean), and geodesics are arcs of circles orthogonal to the boundary ∂ℍ² = S¹. The Poincaré upper half-plane model: ℍ² = {z = x+iy : y > 0} with metric ds² = (dx²+dy²)/y². This is conformal; geodesics are vertical lines (x = constant) and semicircles centered on the real axis. The hyperbolic distance between points p,q in the upper half-plane: d(p,q) = arccosh(1 + |p−q|²/(2 Im(p) Im(q))). Isometries of ℍ² are given by Möbius transformations: z ↦ (az+b)/(cz+d) with a,b,c,d ∈ ℝ, ad−bc = 1 — i.e., the group PSL(2,ℝ). These preserve the hyperbolic metric and the boundary. The Klein-Beltrami model (projective): ℍ² = {x ∈ ℝ² : |x| < 1} with metric ds² = (dx·dx)/(1−|x|²) + (x·dx)²/(1−|x|²)². Here geodesics are straight chords (not conformal). The three models are isometric — they represent the same hyperbolic plane in different coordinates. Key geometric objects: horocycles (curves where all normals meet at a point on the boundary — circles tangent to ∂ℍ² in the disk model), equidistant curves (curves at constant distance from a geodesic — arcs of circles intersecting the boundary at the same points as the geodesic). Hyperbolic distance formula in the disk model: d(z,w) = arctanh(|z−w|/|1−z̄w|).
Three classic models of the hyperbolic plane — Poincaré disk, upper half-plane, and Klein disk — give different perspectives on the same negatively curved geometry, with Möbius transformations as isometries.
Part 3

🧮 Hyperbolic Geometry in Higher Dimensions

Higher
Hyperbolic n-Space ℍⁿ, the Hyperboloid Model, and the Ideal Boundary
Hyperbolic n-space ℍⁿ is the simply connected n-dimensional Riemannian manifold of constant sectional curvature K = −1. The hyperboloid (Minkowski) model: consider ℝ^{n,1} — Minkowski space with quadratic form Q(x,t) = x₁²+...+xₙ² − t². Then ℍⁿ = { (x,t) ∈ ℝ^{n,1} : Q(x,t) = −1, t > 0 } with the induced metric from ds² = dx₁²+...+dxₙ² − dt². This is a two-sheeted hyperboloid; the upper sheet (t > 0) is the hyperboloid model. Geodesics are intersections of the hyperboloid with 2-planes through the origin. The distance between points p,q is given by cosh d(p,q) = −⟨p,q⟩_{Mink} = −(p₁q₁+...+pₙqₙ − p_{n+1}q_{n+1}). The upper half-space model: ℍⁿ = {(x₁,...,x_{n−1},y) : y > 0} with metric ds² = (dx₁²+...+dx_{n−1}²+dy²)/y². Geodesics are vertical lines and semicircles orthogonal to the boundary y=0. The ideal boundary ∂ℍⁿ is the set of asymptotic equivalence classes of geodesic rays — topologically an (n−1)-sphere S^{n−1}. In the half-space model, ∂ℍⁿ = ℝ^{n−1} ∪ {∞}; in the disk model, ∂ℍⁿ = S^{n−1} (the unit sphere). Busemann functions measure distance to the boundary: for a geodesic ray γ(t) with γ(∞) = ξ, B_ξ(x) = lim_{t→∞} (d(x,γ(t)) − t). Level sets of Busemann functions are horospheres (spheres tangent to ∂ℍⁿ). A totally geodesic subspace of ℍⁿ is an isometrically embedded ℍ^k (k < n) — intersections of ℍⁿ with (k+1)-dimensional planes through the origin in the hyperboloid model. Hyperbolic manifolds are quotients M = ℍⁿ / Γ where Γ ⊂ Isom(ℍⁿ) is a discrete, torsion-free group of isometries (a Kleinian group when n=3). Such M are complete, locally isometric to ℍⁿ, and their fundamental group π₁(M) ≅ Γ. In dimension 3, the Thurston geometrization conjecture (proved by Perelman) asserts that every closed 3-manifold decomposes into pieces each admitting one of eight geometries, the most common being hyperbolic geometry.
Hyperbolic n-space ℍⁿ is the constant negative curvature analogue of ℝⁿ — it has an ideal boundary, horospheres, and discrete quotients yielding hyperbolic manifolds that dominate 3-dimensional topology.
Part 4

🔥 Classification of Surfaces and the Gauss-Bonnet Theorem

Classification
Gauss-Bonnet, Uniformization, Teichmüller Space & Hyperbolic Surfaces
The Gauss-Bonnet theorem: for a compact oriented Riemannian surface M (possibly with boundary), ∫_M K dA + ∫_{∂M} k_g ds = 2π χ(M), where K is Gauss curvature, k_g geodesic curvature of the boundary. For closed surfaces: ∫_M K dA = 2π χ(M). The Euler characteristic χ(M) = 2 − 2g for a surface of genus g. Thus: negative Euler characteristic (g ≥ 2) forces the total curvature to be negative, so every oriented surface of genus g ≥ 2 admits a hyperbolic metric (constant curvature K = −1). The uniformization theorem: every simply connected Riemann surface is holomorphically equivalent to exactly one of: the Riemann sphere ℂ̂ (curvature +1), the complex plane ℂ (curvature 0), or the unit disk 𝔻 (curvature −1). Consequently every Riemannian surface has a conformal metric of constant curvature +1, 0, or −1. Teichmüller space T_g is the space of marked hyperbolic (or complex) structures on a surface of genus g — it is homeomorphic to ℝ^{6g−6} (for g ≥ 2). A point in T_g specifies a hyperbolic metric up to isotopy. The moduli space M_g = T_g / Mod_g, where Mod_g is the mapping class group, is the space of Riemann surfaces up to biholomorphism — an orbifold of complex dimension 3g−3. The Weil-Petersson metric is a natural Kähler metric on Teichmüller space, with negative Ricci curvature and geodesic convexity of the space of filled geodesics. Fenchel-Nielsen coordinates parametrize T_g via 3g−3 length parameters ℓ_i > 0 (lengths of simple closed geodesics in a pants decomposition) and 3g−3 twist parameters τ_i ∈ ℝ (twists along those curves), giving global coordinates T_g ≅ ℝ_{+}^{3g−3} × ℝ^{3g−3}. The Wolpert formula expresses the Weil-Petersson symplectic form as ω_{WP} = Σ dℓ_i ∧ dτ_i. Hyperbolic surfaces of genus g ≥ 2 have a rich geometric structure: they contain infinitely many simple closed geodesics, the systole (shortest closed geodesic) is bounded above, and the length spectrum (set of lengths) encodes the shape of the surface via the Selberg trace formula.
The Gauss-Bonnet theorem links curvature directly to topology: surfaces of genus ≥ 2 naturally carry hyperbolic metrics, and Teichmüller space parametrizes their deformation — the moduli space of Riemann surfaces is one of the deepest objects in modern mathematics.
