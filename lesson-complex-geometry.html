Complex Geometry — Holomorphic Bundles, Kähler Manifolds & Hodge Theory

Complex geometry studies manifolds equipped with a complex structure — a way to locally identify neighborhoods with ℂⁿ such that transition maps are holomorphic. A complex manifold M has a complex structure J (an endomorphism with J² = −Id) that is integrable (vanishing Nijenhuis tensor). A Kähler manifold is a complex manifold with a Hermitian metric whose associated 2-form ω is closed (dω = 0) — this gives a rich interaction between Riemannian, symplectic, and complex geometry. The Hodge decomposition Hᵏ(M, ℂ) = ⊕_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(M) for compact Kähler manifolds is a powerful tool. The Calabi conjecture (proved by Yau) shows the existence of Ricci-flat Kähler metrics on compact Kähler manifolds with c₁ = 0 — these are Calabi-Yau manifolds central to string theory. Holomorphic vector bundles, Chern classes, and the Kodaira vanishing theorem complete the picture.

"Complex geometry is the meeting point of algebra, analysis, and topology."
— Phillip Griffiths
Part 1

📏 Complex Manifolds and Almost Complex Structures

Foundations
Complex Coordinates, Almost Complex Structures, and Integrability
A complex manifold of dimension n is a topological manifold covered by charts U_α → ℂⁿ with holomorphic transition maps. Equivalently, an almost complex structure J: TM → TM with J² = −Id. The Nijenhuis tensor N_J(X,Y) = [X,Y] + J[JX,Y] + J[X,JY] − [JX,JY] vanishes iff J is integrable (Newlander-Nirenberg theorem). The tangent bundle splits T_ℂM = T^{1,0}M ⊕ T^{0,1}M into holomorphic and antiholomorphic parts: vectors of type (1,0) satisfy JX = iX; type (0,1) satisfy JX = −iX. Holomorphic functions are those with ∂̄f = 0. The Dolbeault complex (Ω^{p,q}, ∂̄) computes Dolbeault cohomology H^{p,q}_∂̄(M). The Kodaira vanishing theorem: on a compact Kähler manifold with positive canonical bundle, H^q(M, K_M ⊗ L) = 0 for q > 0 when L is ample.
Complex manifolds are locally modeled on ℂⁿ with holomorphic transition maps; the integrability condition ensures the almost complex structure comes from actual holomorphic coordinates.
Part 2

🌍 Holomorphic Vector Bundles and Chern Classes

Bundles
Holomorphic Vector Bundles, Hermitian Metrics, and Chern Classes
A holomorphic vector bundle E → M is a complex vector bundle whose transition functions are holomorphic. The canonical bundle K_M = Λ^n T^{1,0}M^* is the top exterior power of the holomorphic cotangent bundle. A Hermitian metric h on E gives a unique Chern connection — the unique connection compatible with both the holomorphic structure and h. Its curvature Θ ∈ Ω^{1,1}(M, End(E)) is of type (1,1). The Chern classes c_k(E) ∈ H^{2k}(M, ℤ) are defined via c_k = [i/(2π) Tr(Θ^k)] (up to normalization). The Chern character ch(E) = Σ e^{x_i} (in terms of Chern roots) satisfies ch(E⊕F) = ch(E) + ch(F) and ch(E⊗F) = ch(E)·ch(F). Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch: χ(M, E) = ∫_M ch(E)·Td(TM). The Kodaira embedding theorem: a compact complex manifold with a positive holomorphic line bundle embeds into projective space.
Holomorphic vector bundles combine complex analysis with topology; Chern classes are topological invariants that measure the curvature of a bundle.
Part 3

🧮 Kähler Manifolds and Hodge Theory

Kähler
Kähler Metrics, Hodge Decomposition, and Lefschetz Theorems
A Kähler manifold (M, g, J, ω) has a Riemannian metric g compatible with J and a closed Kähler form ω(·,·) = g(·, J·) with dω = 0. The Kähler identities relate the operators ∂, ∂̄, and their adjoints. The Hodge decomposition for compact Kähler: Hᵏ(M, ℂ) = ⊕_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(M) with H^{p,q} ≅ H^{q,p} (conjugation) and H^{p,p}(M) ≠ 0. The Hodge numbers h^{p,q} = dim H^{p,q} satisfy h^{p,q} = h^{q,p} = h^{n-p,n-q} (Serre duality). The Lefschetz hyperplane theorem: if X ⊂ ℙ^N is a smooth projective variety, the inclusion i: Y ↪ X of a hyperplane section induces isomorphisms i_*: π_k(Y) → π_k(X) for k < n−1. The Hard Lefschetz theorem: cup product with ω^{n−k} is an isomorphism Hᵏ(M, ℝ) → H^{2n−k}(M, ℝ). The Hodge-Riemann bilinear relations impose positivity constraints on the intersection form.
Kähler geometry is the ideal setting where Riemannian, symplectic, and complex structures harmonize — leading to powerful Hodge theory constraints on topology.
Part 4

🔥 Calabi-Yau Manifolds and Mirror Symmetry

Calabi-Yau
The Calabi Conjecture, Ricci-Flat Metrics, and Mirror Symmetry
The Calabi conjecture (proved by Yau, 1977): on a compact Kähler manifold with c₁ = 0, there exists a unique Ricci-flat Kähler metric in each Kähler class. These manifolds are Calabi-Yau manifolds — they have SU(n) holonomy. K3 surfaces are the only compact Calabi-Yau 2-folds; in dimension 3, there are thousands of distinct topological types (e.g., the quintic hypersurface in ℙ⁴). Mirror symmetry (proposed by string theorists): for a Calabi-Yau 3-fold M, there exists a mirror M̂ such that h^{1,1}(M) = h^{2,1}(M̂) and h^{2,1}(M) = h^{1,1}(M̂) — the Hodge diamonds are flipped. The Mirror symmetry conjecture relates the complex structure moduli space of M to the Kähler moduli space of M̂ and predicts equality of Gromov-Witten invariants and period integrals. The Batyrev's construction: toric hypersurfaces provide a rich source of mirror pairs. Candelas-de la Ossa-Green-Parkes (1991) predicted the number of rational curves on the quintic using mirror symmetry — a landmark verification.
Calabi-Yau manifolds are Ricci-flat Kähler spaces central to string theory; mirror symmetry reveals deep dualities between a priori distinct geometric worlds.

Deformation Theory of Complex Manifolds and the Kodaira–Spencer Map

The study of how complex structures vary in families is governed by deformation theory. For a compact complex manifold X, the infinitesimal deformations are classified by the first cohomology group H¹(X, Θ_X), where Θ_X denotes the sheaf of holomorphic vector fields (the tangent sheaf). The Kodaira–Spencer map associates to a first‑order deformation of X an element of this cohomology group. Concretely, given a family π: 𝒳 → Δ over a small disc Δ with central fibre X, the derivative of the complex structure at the central point yields a class κ ∈ H¹(X, Θ_X). The map κ is linear and its image is the space of infinitesimal deformations. Obstructions to extending a first‑order deformation to higher order lie in H²(X, Θ_X). If H²(X, Θ_X) = 0, every infinitesimal deformation integrates to a genuine family; this is the case for Calabi–Yau manifolds by the Bogomolov–Tian–Todorov theorem. The Kuranishi space provides a local universal deformation space: it is a germ of an analytic space whose tangent space at the origin is H¹(X, Θ_X) and whose defining equations are given by the obstruction map. The dimension of the Kuranishi space equals the number of essential moduli of the complex structure. For a K3 surface, H¹(X, Θ_X) has dimension 20, matching the 20‑dimensional period domain. For a quintic threefold in ℙ⁴, H¹(X, Θ_X) has dimension 101, reflecting the 101 complex structure moduli. The Kodaira–Spencer map is functorial: a morphism of families induces a map on cohomology compatible with the deformation classes. This functoriality underlies the construction of the period map from the moduli space to a classifying space of Hodge structures.

The Bogomolov–Tian–Todorov Theorem and Unobstructed Deformations

The Bogomolov–Tian–Todorov (BTT) theorem asserts that the deformation functor of a compact Calabi–Yau manifold is unobstructed. A Calabi–Yau manifold is a compact Kähler manifold with trivial canonical bundle K_X ≅ 𝒪_X and Hⁱ(X, 𝒪_X) = 0 for 0 < i < dim X. The theorem was proved independently by Bogomolov (1978) and by Tian and Todorov (1987) using different methods. Tian’s approach uses the ∂̄‑operator and the fact that the Kodaira–Spencer map takes values in H¹(X, Θ_X) ≅ Hn‑1,1(X) via the isomorphism Θ_X ≅ Ωn‑1_X given by contraction with the holomorphic volume form. The key analytic ingredient is the existence of a Ricci‑flat Kähler metric (Yau’s theorem) which provides a Hodge decomposition and a Green’s operator for the ∂̄‑Laplacian. One then shows that the obstruction class in H²(X, Θ_X) vanishes because it can be represented by a ∂̄‑exact form after applying the ∂̄‑Hodge star operator. Todorov’s algebraic proof uses the fact that the differential graded Lie algebra controlling deformations is quasi‑isomorphic to an abelian one. The consequence is that the Kuranishi space of a Calabi–Yau manifold is smooth of dimension hn‑1,1(X). For a quintic threefold, this gives a smooth 101‑dimensional moduli space. The BTT theorem also applies to hyperkähler manifolds (e.g., K3 surfaces) and more generally to any compact Kähler manifold with c₁ = 0 and trivial canonical bundle. It fails for manifolds with non‑trivial canonical bundle; for instance, the deformation space of a general hypersurface of degree d > n+1 in ℙⁿ is obstructed.

Worked Example: Obstruction Vanishing for a Quintic Threefold
Let X ⊂ ℙ⁴ be a smooth quintic threefold. Show that the obstruction space H²(X, Θ_X) vanishes, so the Kuranishi space is smooth of dimension 101.
The canonical bundle of X is K_X ≅ 𝒪_X(d‑5) with d = 5, hence K_X ≅ 𝒪_X. By adjunction, X is a Calabi–Yau threefold. The Bogomolov–Tian–Todorov theorem states that for any compact Calabi–Yau manifold, H²(X, Θ_X) = 0. Therefore the obstruction space vanishes. The tangent space to the Kuranishi space is H¹(X, Θ_X). Using the isomorphism Θ_X ≅ Ω²_X given by the holomorphic volume form, we have H¹(X, Θ_X) ≅ H2,1(X). For the quintic, h2,1 = 101. Hence the Kuranishi space is a smooth germ of dimension 101.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks about unobstructedness of Calabi–Yau deformations, cite the BTT theorem and the isomorphism Θ_X ≅ Ωn‑1_X. Remember that the theorem requires compact Kähler, trivial canonical bundle, and vanishing intermediate Hⁱ(𝒪_X). Do not confuse it with the Kodaira–Spencer map, which only gives the tangent space.

The Hodge Conjecture: Known Cases and Open Problems

The Hodge conjecture predicts that on a smooth projective complex variety X, every rational Hodge class of type (p,p) — i.e., every class in H2p(X, ℚ) ∩ Hp,p(X) — is a ℚ‑linear combination of classes of algebraic cycles of codimension p. The conjecture is known in several important cases. For p = 1, the Lefschetz (1,1) theorem proves that every Hodge class of type (1,1) is the class of a divisor; this is equivalent to the statement that the Néron–Severi group NS(X) ⊗ ℚ equals H²(X, ℚ) ∩ H1,1(X). For p = dim X ‑ 1, the hard Lefschetz theorem and Poincaré duality reduce the statement to the case p = 1, so the conjecture holds for classes of codimension one. For abelian varieties, the conjecture is true for all p because the Hodge structure is generated by divisor classes; this was proved by Weil and later by Murre. For K3 surfaces, the conjecture holds for all p because the only non‑trivial Hodge numbers are h2,0=h0,2=1 and h1,1=20; the transcendental lattice has no (1,1) classes, so every (1,1) class is algebraic. For general hypersurfaces of high degree, the conjecture is wide open. The case of codimension two on a fourfold (p = 2, dim X = 4) is equivalent to the statement that every Hodge class in H⁴ is algebraic; this is known for complete intersections by work of Zucker and later by Voisin using degeneration techniques. The conjecture is also known for certain Shimura varieties and for varieties with a motive of abelian type. A major open problem is the case of a general quintic threefold: it is not known whether there exist non‑algebraic Hodge classes in H³ (which would be of type (2,1)+(1,2) and thus not covered by the conjecture) or in H⁴ (type (2,2)). The Hodge conjecture is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems; a proof or counterexample would have profound implications for the relationship between topology and algebraic geometry.

The Strominger–Yau–Zaslow Conjecture and Special Lagrangian Fibrations

The Strominger–Yau–Zaslow (SYZ) conjecture, proposed in 1996, gives a geometric interpretation of mirror symmetry for Calabi–Yau threefolds. It asserts that a Calabi–Yau threefold M admits a special Lagrangian torus fibration π: M → B with a section, and that the mirror manifold M̂ is obtained by dualizing the fibres (i.e., replacing each torus fibre T³ by its dual torus T̂³). A special Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ M is a real three‑dimensional submanifold calibrated by the real part of the holomorphic volume form Ω: Re(Ω)|_L = vol_L and Im(Ω)|_L = 0. The base B is a real three‑dimensional manifold, often with singularities corresponding to degenerate fibres. The SYZ conjecture explains the exchange of Hodge numbers h1,1(M) = h2,1(M̂) and h2,1(M) = h1,1(M̂) by identifying the complex structure moduli of M with the Kähler moduli of M̂ via the geometry of the fibration. The conjecture has been verified in several toric examples using the Batyrev construction, where the base B is a polytope and the fibration is given by the moment map. In the non‑toric setting, the existence of special Lagrangian fibrations is a deep problem in geometric analysis; it is related to the existence of solutions to the Monge–Ampère equation on the base with prescribed singularities. The SYZ picture also predicts that the Fukaya category of M (with objects special Lagrangian branes) is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on M̂, providing a categorical formulation of mirror symmetry. Recent work by Gross, Siebert, and others has developed a program to construct mirror pairs via toric degenerations and logarithmic geometry, giving a rigorous foundation for many aspects of the SYZ conjecture.

The Weil–Petersson Metric on the Moduli Space of Calabi–Yau Manifolds

The moduli space ℳ of complex structures on a Calabi–Yau manifold carries a natural Kähler metric called the Weil–Petersson metric. It is defined on the tangent space T_[X]ℳ ≅ H¹(X, Θ_X) by the Hodge inner product: for κ₁, κ₂ ∈ H¹(X, Θ_X), the metric is gWP(κ₁, κ₂) = ∫_X (κ₁ ⌟ Ω) ∧ (κ̄₂ ⌟ Ω̄) / (Ω ∧ Ω̄), where Ω is the holomorphic volume form normalized so that ∫_X Ω ∧ Ω̄ = 1. This metric is Kähler, its Kähler form is the Weil–Petersson form ωWP. The metric is incomplete: geodesics can reach the boundary of the moduli space in finite time, corresponding to degenerations of the Calabi–Yau manifold. The curvature of the Weil–Petersson metric is negative in the sense of Griffiths; more precisely, the holomorphic sectional curvature is bounded above by a negative constant. This negativity has strong consequences for the geometry of ℳ: it implies that ℳ is a Kobayashi hyperbolic space, and that any holomorphic map from ℂ to ℳ is constant. The Weil–Petersson metric also appears in the physics of string theory as the kinetic term for the complex structure moduli fields. Its completion, the Weil–Petersson completion, adds boundary strata corresponding to stable degenerations; the metric extends as a singular Kähler metric on the Deligne–Mumford compactification for curves, and on the KSBA compactification for higher‑dimensional Calabi–Yau varieties. The volume of ℳ with respect to the Weil–Petersson metric is finite for moduli of curves, but for Calabi–Yau threefolds the volume is expected to be infinite due to the large number of boundary components. The study of the Weil–Petersson metric connects Hodge theory, geometric analysis, and the compactification of moduli spaces.

  • Confusing the Kodaira–Spencer map with the period map; the former lands in H¹(Θ_X), the latter in a Grassmannian of Hodge structures.
  • Assuming the Bogomolov–Tian–Todorov theorem applies to all Kähler manifolds with c₁ = 0; it requires trivial canonical bundle and vanishing intermediate Hⁱ(𝒪_X).
  • Stating the Hodge conjecture as proved for all smooth projective varieties; it is only known in low codimension, for abelian varieties, K3 surfaces, and some complete intersections.
  • Believing the SYZ conjecture guarantees a smooth special Lagrangian fibration on every Calabi–Yau threefold; singular fibres are expected and the existence is still conjectural in general.
  • Thinking the Weil–Petersson metric is complete on the moduli space of Calabi–Yau threefolds; it is incomplete and its completion involves stable degenerations.
  • Mixing up the Hodge numbers h1,1 and h2,1 under mirror symmetry; mirror symmetry exchanges them, it does not preserve them.
  • Overlooking that the Lefschetz (1,1) theorem only covers divisor classes (p = 1) and does not imply the full Hodge conjecture.
