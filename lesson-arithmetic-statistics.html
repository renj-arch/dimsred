Arithmetic Statistics — the Cohen-Lenstra Heuristics, Average Ranks & Random Matrix Models

Arithmetic statistics is the probabilistic study of arithmetic objects: class groups of number fields, Selmer groups of elliptic curves, and Tate-Shafarevich groups. The central philosophy, due to Cohen and Lenstra (1984), is that the p-part of the class group of an imaginary quadratic field behaves like a random finite abelian p-group weighted by the reciprocal of the size of its automorphism group. The Davenport-Heilbronn theorem (1971) gave the first rigorous average result: the average size of the 3-torsion in class groups of quadratic fields is 2 (imaginary) and 1 (real). This was vastly generalized by Bhargava and Shankar (2010–2015) who developed a method to compute average sizes of n-Selmer groups for elliptic curves over ℚ, using geometry of numbers to parametrize Selmer elements by binary cubic, quartic, and quintic forms. Their result: the average rank of elliptic curves is bounded. Parallel to this, the random matrix model for L-functions — originating in Montgomery's pair correlation conjecture (1972) for ζ(s) and systematized by Katz and Sarnak (1999) — predicts that the zero statistics of L-functions mirror the eigenvalue statistics of random matrices from classical compact groups. The Bhargava cube laws parametrize number fields of small degree by integral orbits on prehomogeneous vector spaces, enabling the counting of number fields by discriminant.

"The statistical patterns in number theory often follow those of random matrix theory."
— Peter Sarnak
Part 1

📏 The Cohen-Lenstra Heuristics

Class Groups
Distribution of the p-Part of Class Groups of Imaginary Quadratic Fields
For an imaginary quadratic field K = ℚ(√−D) with fundamental discriminant D > 0, the class group Cl(K) is a finite abelian group whose order is the class number h(D). The Cohen-Lenstra heuristics (1984) predict that for an odd prime ℓ, the ℓ-part Cl(K)_ℓ is distributed like a random finite abelian ℓ-group with probability proportional to 1/|Aut(G)|. More precisely, for a fixed finite abelian ℓ-group G, as D → ∞ over fundamental discriminants, we expect Prob(Cl(K)_ℓ ≅ G) = c · |G|^(−w)/|Aut(G)| for a certain normalization constant c and weight w. The weight w = 1 for imaginary quadratic fields (the "Cohen-Lenstra" weight) and the distribution is concentrated on groups with small ℓ-rank. The Davenport-Heilbronn theorem (1971) gave the first concrete evidence: the average size of the 3-torsion subgroup Cl(K)[3] for imaginary quadratic fields is exactly 2, and for real quadratic fields it is 1. This is proved by counting cubic fields via binary cubic forms — a precursor to the Bhargava method. The Bhargava-Varma result (2015) computes the mean 3-rank of class groups (the average of dim_{𝔽_3} Cl(K)[3]) for quadratic fields, confirming the Cohen-Lenstra predictions. The moments of class group sizes — the average of |Cl(K)|^k — satisfy subtle growth rates; the Cohen-Lenstra heuristics predict that all moments are finite and governed by the same distribution.
The Cohen-Lenstra heuristics are a paradigm-shifting probabilistic model: the p-part of the class group of a random imaginary quadratic field behaves like a random finite abelian p-group with a natural weight 1/|Aut(G)|.
Part 2

🌍 The Bhargava-Shankar Method and Average Ranks

Selmer Groups
Average Size of n-Selmer Groups and the Bounded Average Rank of Elliptic Curves
For an elliptic curve E/ℚ, the n-Selmer group Sel_n(E) fits into the exact sequence 0 → E(ℚ)/nE(ℚ) → Sel_n(E) → Ш(E)[n] → 0, where Ш(E) is the Tate-Shafarevich group. Bhargava and Shankar (2010–2015) computed the average size of Sel_n(E) for n = 2, 3, 4, 5 by parametrizing the elements of Sel_n(E) by integral orbits on prehomogeneous vector spaces: binary cubic forms (n = 2), binary quartic forms (n = 3), pairs of ternary quadratic forms (n = 4), and 5 × 5 skew-symmetric matrices (n = 5). Using geometry of numbers, they counted these orbits and found: the average size of Sel_2(E) = 3, Sel_3(E) = 4, Sel_4(E) = 7, and Sel_5(E) = 6. The key consequence: the average rank of elliptic curves over ℚ is bounded. More precisely, the limsup of the average rank is at most 0.885... (from the 5-Selmer bound). Moreover, the average rank of curves with a marked rational point is at least some δ > 0. The parity phenomenon: the rank of E(ℚ) has the same parity as the root number w(E) = ±1 (the sign of the functional equation of the L-function); curves with w(E) = +1 have even rank, w(E) = −1 have odd rank. The distribution of the 2-Selmer group was shown to match the cokernel of a random symmetric matrix over 𝔽_2 (the Poonen-Rains heuristic). The Bhargava-Shankar-Tsimerman (2015) work on the average size of the 6-Selmer group and beyond remains active.
Bhargava and Shankar's orbit-counting method dramatically advanced our understanding: the average rank of elliptic curves is bounded, and the Selmer groups exhibit distributional patterns consistent with random matrix models.
Part 3

🧮 Random Matrix Models for L-functions

L-functions
Montgomery Pair Correlation, Katz-Sarnak Philosophy, and Keating-Snaith Conjectures
Hugh Montgomery (1972) studied the pair correlation of nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function ζ(s). Assuming the Riemann hypothesis, he showed that for 0 < α < β, the proportion of zeros with spacing in [α, β] is ∫_α^β (1 − (sin πu/πu)^2) du — the same as the distribution of eigenvalue spacings of the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) of random Hermitian matrices. This Montgomery-Dyson pair correlation conjecture was the first link between number theory and random matrix theory. The Katz-Sarnak philosophy (1999) vastly generalizes this: for families of L-functions (e.g., Dirichlet L-functions, L-functions of elliptic curves, modular forms), the statistics of zeros near the critical line match the eigenvalue statistics of random matrices from classical compact groups. The symmetry type of the family (unitary U(N), symplectic USp(2N), orthogonal O(N), or even/odd orthogonal SO(2N)/SO(2N+1)) is determined by the sign of the functional equation and the structure of the family. For example, the family of quadratic Dirichlet L-functions has symplectic symmetry, while the family of elliptic curve L-functions has orthogonal symmetry. The Keating-Snaith conjectures (2000): the moments of L-functions at the critical point s = 1/2 can be computed using the characteristic polynomial of random matrices from the appropriate symmetry group. For ζ(s), Keating and Snaith predicted ∫_0^T |ζ(1/2 + it)|^{2k} dt ∼ a_k · G(k+1)^2/G(2k+1) · T (log T)^{k^2}, where G is the Barnes G-function and a_k is an arithmetic factor. These predictions have been verified numerically and motivate the search for the true distribution of L-values.
The Katz-Sarnak philosophy unifies the statistics of L-functions across all number theory: zeros and values of L-functions behave like eigenvalues of random matrices from a symmetry group determined by the functional equation.
Part 4

🔥 Parametrization of Number Fields

Number Fields
Bhargava's Cube Laws, Counting Number Fields, and Malle's Conjecture
Bhargava's revolutionary insight (2004) is that number fields of small degree can be parametrized by integral orbits on prehomogeneous vector spaces. The cube law: a 2 × 2 × 2 cube of integers parametrizes a pair of binary quadratic forms; the resulting ring is a quartic ring (a commutative ring of rank 4 over ℤ). This leads to the parametrization of quartic fields by pairs of ternary quadratic forms (for quintic fields) and more complex integral orbits for sextic fields. The key counting result: the number of number fields of degree d with discriminant < X satisfies a power law. Specifically, Bhargava proved that the number of quartic fields with discriminant < X is ∼ c_4 · X, and the number of quintic fields is ∼ c_5 · X, disproving previous heuristics. The Malle's conjecture (2004) predicts that for a fixed Galois group G ⊂ S_n, the number of extensions L/K of degree n with discriminant ≤ X grows like X^{a_n} (log X)^{b_n(G)}, where a_n = 1/a(G) for a certain constant a(G) depending on the exponent of G, and b_n(G) depends on the group structure. The work of Bhargava-Shankar-Venkatesh (2013) and Ellenberg-Venkatesh (2008) has made significant progress on counting number fields, connecting the problem to the geometry of numbers on prehomogeneous vector spaces. The Thue-Siegel principle adapted to this context gives a method for counting integral points on homogeneous spaces by combining upper bounds (from Siegel's mass formula and the geometry of numbers) with lower bounds (from the construction of many integral orbits). The applications extend to class groups (the Cohen-Lenstra heuristics), Selmer groups (via the Bhargava-Shankar parametrization), and the average size of the Tate-Shafarevich group of elliptic curves.
Bhargava's cube laws and the orbit-counting method have transformed arithmetic statistics, enabling precise counting of number fields and linking the distribution of Selmer groups to random matrix heuristics.
