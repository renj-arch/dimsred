Arithmetic Statistics — the Cohen-Lenstra Heuristics, Average Ranks & Random Matrix Models

Arithmetic statistics is the probabilistic study of arithmetic objects: class groups of number fields, Selmer groups of elliptic curves, and Tate-Shafarevich groups. The central philosophy, due to Cohen and Lenstra (1984), is that the p-part of the class group of an imaginary quadratic field behaves like a random finite abelian p-group weighted by the reciprocal of the size of its automorphism group. The Davenport-Heilbronn theorem (1971) gave the first rigorous average result: the average size of the 3-torsion in class groups of quadratic fields is 2 (imaginary) and 1 (real). This was vastly generalized by Bhargava and Shankar (2010–2015) who developed a method to compute average sizes of n-Selmer groups for elliptic curves over ℚ, using geometry of numbers to parametrize Selmer elements by binary cubic, quartic, and quintic forms. Their result: the average rank of elliptic curves is bounded. Parallel to this, the random matrix model for L-functions — originating in Montgomery's pair correlation conjecture (1972) for ζ(s) and systematized by Katz and Sarnak (1999) — predicts that the zero statistics of L-functions mirror the eigenvalue statistics of random matrices from classical compact groups. The Bhargava cube laws parametrize number fields of small degree by integral orbits on prehomogeneous vector spaces, enabling the counting of number fields by discriminant.

"The statistical patterns in number theory often follow those of random matrix theory."
— Peter Sarnak
Part 1

📏 The Cohen-Lenstra Heuristics

Class Groups
Distribution of the p-Part of Class Groups of Imaginary Quadratic Fields
For an imaginary quadratic field K = ℚ(√−D) with fundamental discriminant D > 0, the class group Cl(K) is a finite abelian group whose order is the class number h(D). The Cohen-Lenstra heuristics (1984) predict that for an odd prime ℓ, the ℓ-part Cl(K)_ℓ is distributed like a random finite abelian ℓ-group with probability proportional to 1/|Aut(G)|. More precisely, for a fixed finite abelian ℓ-group G, as D → ∞ over fundamental discriminants, we expect Prob(Cl(K)_ℓ ≅ G) = c · |G|^(−w)/|Aut(G)| for a certain normalization constant c and weight w. The weight w = 1 for imaginary quadratic fields (the "Cohen-Lenstra" weight) and the distribution is concentrated on groups with small ℓ-rank. The Davenport-Heilbronn theorem (1971) gave the first concrete evidence: the average size of the 3-torsion subgroup Cl(K)[3] for imaginary quadratic fields is exactly 2, and for real quadratic fields it is 1. This is proved by counting cubic fields via binary cubic forms — a precursor to the Bhargava method. The Bhargava-Varma result (2015) computes the mean 3-rank of class groups (the average of dim_{𝔽_3} Cl(K)[3]) for quadratic fields, confirming the Cohen-Lenstra predictions. The moments of class group sizes — the average of |Cl(K)|^k — satisfy subtle growth rates; the Cohen-Lenstra heuristics predict that all moments are finite and governed by the same distribution.
The Cohen-Lenstra heuristics are a paradigm-shifting probabilistic model: the p-part of the class group of a random imaginary quadratic field behaves like a random finite abelian p-group with a natural weight 1/|Aut(G)|.
Part 2

🌍 The Bhargava-Shankar Method and Average Ranks

Selmer Groups
Average Size of n-Selmer Groups and the Bounded Average Rank of Elliptic Curves
For an elliptic curve E/ℚ, the n-Selmer group Sel_n(E) fits into the exact sequence 0 → E(ℚ)/nE(ℚ) → Sel_n(E) → Ш(E)[n] → 0, where Ш(E) is the Tate-Shafarevich group. Bhargava and Shankar (2010–2015) computed the average size of Sel_n(E) for n = 2, 3, 4, 5 by parametrizing the elements of Sel_n(E) by integral orbits on prehomogeneous vector spaces: binary cubic forms (n = 2), binary quartic forms (n = 3), pairs of ternary quadratic forms (n = 4), and 5 × 5 skew-symmetric matrices (n = 5). Using geometry of numbers, they counted these orbits and found: the average size of Sel_2(E) = 3, Sel_3(E) = 4, Sel_4(E) = 7, and Sel_5(E) = 6. The key consequence: the average rank of elliptic curves over ℚ is bounded. More precisely, the limsup of the average rank is at most 0.885... (from the 5-Selmer bound). Moreover, the average rank of curves with a marked rational point is at least some δ > 0. The parity phenomenon: the rank of E(ℚ) has the same parity as the root number w(E) = ±1 (the sign of the functional equation of the L-function); curves with w(E) = +1 have even rank, w(E) = −1 have odd rank. The distribution of the 2-Selmer group was shown to match the cokernel of a random symmetric matrix over 𝔽_2 (the Poonen-Rains heuristic). The Bhargava-Shankar-Tsimerman (2015) work on the average size of the 6-Selmer group and beyond remains active.
Bhargava and Shankar's orbit-counting method dramatically advanced our understanding: the average rank of elliptic curves is bounded, and the Selmer groups exhibit distributional patterns consistent with random matrix models.
Part 3

🧮 Random Matrix Models for L-functions

L-functions
Montgomery Pair Correlation, Katz-Sarnak Philosophy, and Keating-Snaith Conjectures
Hugh Montgomery (1972) studied the pair correlation of nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function ζ(s). Assuming the Riemann hypothesis, he showed that for 0 < α < β, the proportion of zeros with spacing in [α, β] is ∫_α^β (1 − (sin πu/πu)^2) du — the same as the distribution of eigenvalue spacings of the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) of random Hermitian matrices. This Montgomery-Dyson pair correlation conjecture was the first link between number theory and random matrix theory. The Katz-Sarnak philosophy (1999) vastly generalizes this: for families of L-functions (e.g., Dirichlet L-functions, L-functions of elliptic curves, modular forms), the statistics of zeros near the critical line match the eigenvalue statistics of random matrices from classical compact groups. The symmetry type of the family (unitary U(N), symplectic USp(2N), orthogonal O(N), or even/odd orthogonal SO(2N)/SO(2N+1)) is determined by the sign of the functional equation and the structure of the family. For example, the family of quadratic Dirichlet L-functions has symplectic symmetry, while the family of elliptic curve L-functions has orthogonal symmetry. The Keating-Snaith conjectures (2000): the moments of L-functions at the critical point s = 1/2 can be computed using the characteristic polynomial of random matrices from the appropriate symmetry group. For ζ(s), Keating and Snaith predicted ∫_0^T |ζ(1/2 + it)|^{2k} dt ∼ a_k · G(k+1)^2/G(2k+1) · T (log T)^{k^2}, where G is the Barnes G-function and a_k is an arithmetic factor. These predictions have been verified numerically and motivate the search for the true distribution of L-values.
The Katz-Sarnak philosophy unifies the statistics of L-functions across all number theory: zeros and values of L-functions behave like eigenvalues of random matrices from a symmetry group determined by the functional equation.
Part 4

🔥 Parametrization of Number Fields

Number Fields
Bhargava's Cube Laws, Counting Number Fields, and Malle's Conjecture
Bhargava's revolutionary insight (2004) is that number fields of small degree can be parametrized by integral orbits on prehomogeneous vector spaces. The cube law: a 2 × 2 × 2 cube of integers parametrizes a pair of binary quadratic forms; the resulting ring is a quartic ring (a commutative ring of rank 4 over ℤ). This leads to the parametrization of quartic fields by pairs of ternary quadratic forms (for quintic fields) and more complex integral orbits for sextic fields. The key counting result: the number of number fields of degree d with discriminant < X satisfies a power law. Specifically, Bhargava proved that the number of quartic fields with discriminant < X is ∼ c_4 · X, and the number of quintic fields is ∼ c_5 · X, disproving previous heuristics. The Malle's conjecture (2004) predicts that for a fixed Galois group G ⊂ S_n, the number of extensions L/K of degree n with discriminant ≤ X grows like X^{a_n} (log X)^{b_n(G)}, where a_n = 1/a(G) for a certain constant a(G) depending on the exponent of G, and b_n(G) depends on the group structure. The work of Bhargava-Shankar-Venkatesh (2013) and Ellenberg-Venkatesh (2008) has made significant progress on counting number fields, connecting the problem to the geometry of numbers on prehomogeneous vector spaces. The Thue-Siegel principle adapted to this context gives a method for counting integral points on homogeneous spaces by combining upper bounds (from Siegel's mass formula and the geometry of numbers) with lower bounds (from the construction of many integral orbits). The applications extend to class groups (the Cohen-Lenstra heuristics), Selmer groups (via the Bhargava-Shankar parametrization), and the average size of the Tate-Shafarevich group of elliptic curves.
Bhargava's cube laws and the orbit-counting method have transformed arithmetic statistics, enabling precise counting of number fields and linking the distribution of Selmer groups to random matrix heuristics.

The Cohen-Lenstra-Martinet Heuristics for Real Quadratic Fields

The original Cohen-Lenstra heuristics (1984) address the p-part of the class group of imaginary quadratic fields, where the unit group is finite (only roots of unity). For real quadratic fields ℚ(√d) with d > 0, the unit group has rank 1 by Dirichlet's unit theorem, generated by a fundamental unit ε_d > 1. This infinite unit group introduces a regulator R_d = log ε_d into the class number formula h_d R_d = √d L(1, χ_d) / 2 (for d ≡ 1 mod 4) or h_d R_d = √d L(1, χ_d) (for d ≡ 2,3 mod 4). The presence of the regulator means the naive Cohen-Lenstra weighting by 1/|Aut(G)| must be modified. Cohen and Martinet (1990) proposed that for a fixed odd prime p, the probability that the p-part of the class group Cl(ℚ(√d))[p^∞] is isomorphic to a finite abelian p-group G is proportional to 1/(|G| · |Aut(G)|). The extra factor 1/|G| accounts for the regulator: heuristically, the regulator R_d behaves like a random positive real number, and the class number formula suggests h_d ≈ √d L(1, χ_d) / R_d. Since L(1, χ_d) has a limiting distribution (related to the value distribution of L-functions), and R_d is "independent" of the class group structure, the probability that p^k divides h_d gains a factor 1/p^k, leading to the 1/|G| weighting. For p = 2, the situation is more subtle because the 2-rank of the class group is governed by genus theory: the 2-rank equals t − 1 where t is the number of distinct prime factors of the discriminant. The Cohen-Lenstra-Martinet heuristics for the odd part of the class group of real quadratic fields remain conjectural, but extensive numerical data (e.g., computations by Jacobson, Ramachandran, and Williams up to d < 108) show remarkable agreement. The heuristics also predict that the average size of the p-torsion subgroup Cl(ℚ(√d))[p] is 1 + 1/p for imaginary quadratic fields and 2 for real quadratic fields (the extra 1 coming from the 1/|G| factor summing to 2). This distinction between imaginary and real quadratic fields is a hallmark of the Cohen-Lenstra philosophy: the distribution of class groups is not universal but depends on the unit rank of the field.

The Poonen-Rains Model for Selmer Groups and the Cokernel of Random Alternating Matrices

The Bhargava-Shankar work on the average size of the 2-Selmer group of elliptic curves over ℚ showed that the 2-Selmer group Sel2(E/ℚ) is distributed like the cokernel of a random symmetric matrix over 𝔽2. Poonen and Rains (2012) generalized this to a comprehensive heuristic for the distribution of the p-Selmer group Sel_p(E/ℚ) for any prime p, and more generally for the Selmer group of any abelian variety. Their model replaces the symmetric matrix with a random alternating matrix over ℤ_p (or 𝔽_p for the p-torsion). The key insight is that the p-Selmer group fits into an exact sequence 0 → E(ℚ)/pE(ℚ) → Sel_p(E/ℚ) → Ш(E/ℚ)[p] → 0, and the Cassels-Tate pairing on Ш(E/ℚ)[p] is alternating and non-degenerate. Poonen and Rains model Sel_p(E/ℚ) as the cokernel of a random alternating matrix M over ℤ_p, conditioned on the dimension of the maximal isotropic subspace (which corresponds to E(ℚ)/pE(ℚ)). For p = 2, the alternating condition is equivalent to symmetric with zero diagonal, matching the Bhargava-Shankar result. For odd p, the model predicts that the probability Sel_p(E/ℚ) ≅ G (as a finite abelian p-group with an alternating pairing) is proportional to 1/(|G| · |Aut(G, ⟨·,·⟩)|), where Aut(G, ⟨·,·⟩) is the group of automorphisms preserving the alternating pairing. This weighting by the automorphism group of the pairing is the natural generalization of the Cohen-Lenstra 1/|Aut(G)|. The Poonen-Rains heuristic makes precise predictions for the average size of Sel_p(E/ℚ): it is p + 1 for all p. For p = 2, this gives average size 3, matching Bhargava-Shankar. For p = 3, the average size is predicted to be 4, and Bhargava-Shankar-Tsimerman (2015) proved the average size of the 3-Selmer group is 4. For p = 5, the average size is predicted to be 6, and recent work by Bhargava, Shankar, and others has made progress toward this. The model also predicts the distribution of the rank: the probability that rank E(ℚ) = r is the probability that a random alternating matrix over ℤ_p has cokernel with a maximal isotropic subspace of dimension r. This yields the prediction that 50% of elliptic curves have rank 0, 50% have rank 1, and 0% have rank ≥ 2 (in the sense of natural density). The parity conjecture (the rank has the same parity as the root number) is built into the model via the sign of the functional equation, which determines the symmetry type of the random matrix ensemble.

Worked Example: Average Size of p-Selmer Group
Using the Poonen-Rains heuristic, compute the predicted average size of the 5-Selmer group of an elliptic curve over ℚ. Explain why this average size differs from the average size of the 5-torsion in the class group of an imaginary quadratic field.
The Poonen-Rains heuristic models the p-Selmer group as the cokernel of a random alternating matrix over ℤ_p. For any prime p, the expected size of the cokernel of a random n × n alternating matrix over 𝔽_p, as n → ∞, is p + 1. This can be derived by computing the probability that the cokernel is a given finite abelian p-group G with an alternating pairing, weighted by 1/(|G| · |Aut(G, ⟨·,·⟩)|), and summing |G| over all such G. The sum evaluates to p + 1. For p = 5, the predicted average size of the 5-Selmer group is 5 + 1 = 6. In contrast, the Cohen-Lenstra heuristic for the p-torsion in the class group of an imaginary quadratic field weights by 1/|Aut(G)| (no alternating pairing, no extra 1/|G| factor). The expected size of the p-torsion under Cohen-Lenstra is 1 + 1/p. For p = 5, this is 1 + 1/5 = 6/5 = 1.2. The difference arises because the Selmer group carries an alternating pairing (the Cassels-Tate pairing) and sits in an exact sequence with E(ℚ)/pE(ℚ) and Ш(E/ℚ)[p], while the class group of an imaginary quadratic field has no such pairing and no regulator correction. The extra 1/|G| factor in the Poonen-Rains model (coming from the "random maximal isotropic subspace" conditioning) increases the average size from 1 + 1/p to p + 1.
Exam Strategy: Distinguishing Heuristic Models
When a question asks for the "average size" of a p-torsion or p-Selmer group, identify which heuristic applies: Cohen-Lenstra (imaginary quadratic class groups) → average size = 1 + 1/p. Cohen-Lenstra-Martinet (real quadratic class groups, odd p) → average size = 2. Poonen-Rains (p-Selmer of elliptic curves) → average size = p + 1. Bhargava-Shankar (2-Selmer) → average size = 3 (special case of Poonen-Rains). Memorize these four numbers and their domains; examiners often test whether you confuse the class group average with the Selmer group average.

Bhargava's Higher Composition Laws: Quintic Rings and the 5×5×5 Cube

Bhargava's 2004 PhD thesis introduced a series of "higher composition laws" generalizing Gauss's composition of binary quadratic forms (which parametrizes quadratic rings). The first new law uses a 2 × 2 × 2 cube of integers to parametrize pairs of ternary quadratic forms, which in turn correspond to quartic rings (commutative rings of rank 4 over ℤ). The second law uses a 2 × 3 × 3 box to parametrize pairs of a binary quadratic form and a ternary quadratic form, corresponding to quintic rings. The third law uses a 3 × 3 × 3 cube to parametrize triples of ternary quadratic forms, corresponding to sextic rings. The most striking is the parametrization of quintic rings: a 2 × 3 × 3 box of integers (18 integers total) encodes a pair (f, g) where f is a binary quadratic form and g is a ternary quadratic form. The invariants of this pair (the discriminant of the quintic ring, the resolvent sextic, etc.) are polynomial functions of the 18 integers. The group action is GL2(ℤ) × SL3(ℤ) × SL3(ℤ) acting on the box by slicing transformations. The integral orbits of this action are in bijection with isomorphism classes of quintic rings. This parametrization allowed Bhargava to count quintic fields by discriminant: the number of S5-quintic fields with absolute discriminant ≤ X is asymptotic to c X for an explicit constant c > 0 (proved by Bhargava in 2010). The constant c involves a product of local densities and the volume of a fundamental domain for the group action. For the connection to Selmer groups: the 2 × 3 × 3 box also parametrizes the 2-Selmer group of a certain genus-2 curve (or the 4-Selmer group of an elliptic curve). More directly, the 2 × 2 × 2 cube parametrizes the 2-Selmer group of an elliptic curve (as used by Bhargava-Shankar), and the 2 × 3 × 3 box parametrizes the 3-Selmer group. The 3 × 3 × 3 cube parametrizes the 5-Selmer group. This pattern — degree d+1 composition law parametrizes the d-Selmer group — is part of a broader philosophy: the geometry of numbers on prehomogeneous vector spaces controls both the counting of number fields and the distribution of Selmer groups. The invariants of the box (discriminant, resolvent polynomials) correspond to the invariants of the Selmer element (the associated homogeneous space). The group action corresponds to the equivalence of Selmer elements under the action of the rational points of the Jacobian.

Ellenberg-Venkatesh-Westerland: Homological Stability and Function Field Analogues

The Cohen-Lenstra heuristics were originally formulated for number fields. In the 2010s, a geometric proof of the function field analogue was achieved by Ellenberg, Venkatesh, and Westerland (2016), using homological stability of Hurwitz spaces. Over a finite field 𝔽_q, the class group of a quadratic extension 𝔽_q(t)(√D) (where D is a squarefree polynomial) is a finite abelian group. The Cohen-Lenstra heuristics predict that as q → ∞ (with the degree of D fixed), the distribution of the p-part of the class group (for p ≠ char 𝔽_q) converges to the Cohen-Lenstra distribution. Ellenberg-Venkatesh-Westerland proved this by interpreting the class group as the homology of a certain Hurwitz space (the moduli space of branched covers of the projective line). The key tool is homological stability: as the genus of the cover grows, the homology groups of the Hurwitz space stabilize, and the limiting distribution matches the Cohen-Lenstra prediction. The proof uses the Grothendieck-Lefschetz trace formula to relate the number of points on the Hurwitz space over 𝔽_q to the action of Frobenius on its étale cohomology. The stable homology is identified with the homology of the infinite symmetric group, and the Cohen-Lenstra measure emerges as the limiting distribution of the cokernel of a random matrix over ℤ_p (the "random matrix" here is the action of Frobenius on the first homology of the cover). This geometric approach also yields the function field analogue of the Poonen-Rains heuristic for Selmer groups of elliptic curves over 𝔽_q(t), proved by Ellenberg-Venkatesh-Westerland and independently by Erman-Wood. The function field setting is more tractable because the moduli spaces are algebraic varieties over finite fields, and the Weil conjectures give precise control over point counts. The number field case remains conjectural, but the function field proofs provide a blueprint: the Cohen-Lenstra measure is the limiting distribution of the cokernel of a random matrix arising from the monodromy action on homology. This connects arithmetic statistics to the topology of moduli spaces and the representation theory of symmetric groups.

Malle's Conjecture, Discriminant Counting, and the Inverse Galois Problem

Malle's conjecture (2004) refines the classical problem of counting number fields by discriminant. For a fixed transitive subgroup G ⊂ S_n, let N(G, K, X) be the number of extensions L/K of degree n with Galois group G and absolute discriminant NK/ℚ(disc(L/K)) ≤ X. Malle conjectured that N(G, K, X) ∼ c(G, K) Xa(G) (log X)b(G, K) - 1 as X → ∞, where a(G) = 1 / ind(G), ind(G) = ming∈G, g≠1 (n - number of cycles of g) is the minimal index of a non-identity element of G, and b(G, K) is the number of conjugacy classes in G of elements achieving this minimal index, divided by the order of the centralizer (with adjustments for the base field K). For G = S_n, ind(S_n) = 2 (achieved by transpositions), so a(S_n) = 1/2. The number of S_n-extensions of ℚ with discriminant ≤ X is predicted to grow like X1/2 (log X)b_n - 1. For n = 3, b3 = 1, giving X1/2 (no log factor), matching Davenport-Heilbronn. For n = 4, b4 = 1/2? Actually for S4, the minimal index is 2 (transpositions), there are 6 transpositions, centralizer size 4, so b = 6/4 = 3/2? The precise formula for b(G, K) involves the number of conjugacy classes of minimal index elements that are "realizable" over K. Malle's conjecture has been proved for abelian G (by Wright, 1989), for G = S3 (Davenport-Heilbronn), S4 (Bhargava, 2005), S5 (Bhargava, 2010), and for certain other groups using the geometry of numbers on prehomogeneous vector spaces. The conjecture connects to the inverse Galois problem: if Malle's conjecture holds with a positive constant c(G, ℚ), then there are infinitely many extensions with Galois group G, so G occurs as a Galois group over ℚ. The constant c(G, K) is a product of local factors: c(G, K) = τ(G) ∏_v c_v(G, K_v), where τ(G) is the Tamagawa number of a certain algebraic group associated to G, and c_v are local densities. The work of Bhargava-Shankar-Venkatesh (2013) and Ellenberg-Venkatesh (2008) developed the "Thue-Siegel principle" for counting integral points on homogeneous spaces, giving upper bounds of the form Xa(G)+ε and lower bounds of the form Xa(G) (log X)b(G)-1 for many groups G. The exponent a(G) = 1/ind(G) is sharp: Klüners (2003) gave a counterexample to the original form of Malle's conjecture for G = C3 ≀ C2 (wreath product) over ℚ, where the exponent is larger than 1/ind(G) due to "bad" ramification. The corrected conjecture (Malle's conjecture with "Malle's correction factor") is an active area of research.

  • Forgetting that the Cohen-Lenstra heuristics apply only to the odd part of the class group; the 2-part is governed by genus theory and has a completely different distribution (2-rank = t − 1).
  • Assuming the Poonen-Rains model predicts average rank 1/2 for all primes; it predicts 50% rank 0, 50% rank 1, 0% rank ≥ 2, but this is conditional on the parity conjecture and the random matrix model.
  • Mixing up the parametrization: 2×2×2 cube → quartic rings / 2-Selmer; 2×3×3 box → quintic rings / 3-Selmer; 3×3×3 cube → sextic rings / 5-Selmer. The degree of the ring is one more than the dimension of the smallest slice.
  • Stating Malle's conjecture as X1/2 for all S_n; the exponent is always 1/2 for S_n (since ind(S_n)=2), but the log exponent b(G) varies with n and the base field.
  • Believing the function field proofs (Ellenberg-Venkatesh-Westerland) directly imply the number field conjectures; they provide a geometric framework but the number field case lacks the algebraic geometry of moduli spaces over finite fields.
  • Overlooking the role of the regulator in the Cohen-Lenstra-Martinet heuristic: the extra 1/|G| factor comes from the regulator R_d = log ε_d in the class number formula, which has no analogue for imaginary quadratic fields.
