Condensed Matter Physics — Crystal Structure, Band Theory, Semiconductors & Superconductivity

Condensed matter physics explains the collective behaviour of large numbers of interacting particles. The central insight is that new phenomena — bands, phonons, superconductivity, topological states — emerge from the organisation of atoms and electrons, even though the underlying laws are those of quantum mechanics and electromagnetism.

“More is different.”
Philip W. Anderson
Part 1

Crystal Structure, Reciprocal Space and Diffraction

Crystals
Bravais lattices, Miller indices and Bragg’s law

A crystal is a periodic arrangement of atoms generated by a Bravais lattice and a basis. The lattice is specified by primitive vectors a₁, a₂, a₃; every lattice point is R = n₁a₁ + n₂a₂ + n₃a₃. The seven crystal systems and fourteen Bravais lattices classify the possible translational symmetries in three dimensions. Planes are labelled by Miller indices (hkl), and the interplanar spacing for an orthogonal lattice is

dhkl = 1/√(h²/a² + k²/b² + l²/c²).

Diffraction occurs when the path difference between waves scattered from adjacent planes is an integer number of wavelengths — Bragg’s law:

nλ = 2d sin θ.

The reciprocal lattice is the Fourier transform of the direct lattice; diffraction peaks correspond to reciprocal-lattice vectors G. The structure factor Fhkl = Σj fj exp(iG·rj) determines the intensity and can produce systematic absences, revealing the basis and symmetry.

Part 2

Band Theory: Bloch Electrons and the Fermi Surface

Electrons in Solids
Bloch’s theorem, bands and the Fermi energy

In a periodic potential, Bloch’s theorem states that electronic eigenstates have the form

ψnk(r) = eik·runk(r),

where unk has the periodicity of the lattice and k is the crystal momentum. The energy eigenvalues En(k) form bands; gaps open where Bragg reflection mixes degenerate plane waves. The tight-binding model, in which electrons hop between localised orbitals, gives a simple cosine dispersion, E(k) = E₀ − 2t cos(ka), and captures the essential physics of many solids.

At zero temperature, electrons fill states up to the Fermi energy EF. The Fermi surface separates occupied from empty states in k-space. Metals have a partially filled band and a finite density of states at EF; insulators and semiconductors have a filled valence band separated from an empty conduction band by a gap Eg. The Fermi–Dirac distribution f(E) = 1/(e(E−μ)/kBT + 1) controls thermal occupation.

The density of states near a parabolic band edge behaves as g(E) ∝ √(E − Ec) in three dimensions, and the effective mass m* = ħ²(∂²E/∂k²)⁻¹ encodes the band curvature.

Part 3

Semiconductors: Doping, p–n Junctions and Devices

Semiconductors
Carriers, junctions and the transistor

Semiconductors such as silicon and germanium have gaps of about 1.1 eV and 0.67 eV at room temperature. Thermal excitation creates electron–hole pairs; the intrinsic carrier concentration is ni ∝ T3/2 exp(−Eg/2kBT). Doping with group-V donors (P, As) adds electrons and produces an n-type material; group-III acceptors (B, Al) add holes and produce p-type material. Charge neutrality requires n + NA⁻ = p + ND⁺, and in equilibrium np = ni².

At a p–n junction, diffusion of carriers leaves a depletion region with a built-in electric field and a contact potential. Forward bias lowers the barrier and injects carriers, producing exponential current–voltage behaviour; reverse bias widens the depletion region and allows only a small leakage current. The same physics underlies diodes, bipolar transistors, solar cells, photodetectors and light-emitting diodes.

At the nanoscale, quantum confinement modifies the density of states, and heterostructures can create two-dimensional electron gases. These systems support the quantum Hall effect and are platforms for modern optoelectronics and quantum devices.

Part 4

Superconductivity and the Quantum Hall Effect

Quantum Phases
BCS pairing, vortices and quantised Hall conductance

Below a critical temperature Tc, many metals enter a superconducting state with zero electrical resistance and perfect diamagnetism (the Meissner effect). In the BCS theory, electrons near the Fermi surface form Cooper pairs through an effective attraction mediated by lattice vibrations. The pairs condense into a coherent ground state with an energy gap Δ; the excitation spectrum is Ek = √(ξk² + Δ²), where ξk = εk − EF.

Type-I superconductors expel the magnetic field completely; Type-II superconductors allow it to penetrate as quantised vortices above a lower critical field Hc1, up to an upper critical field Hc2. Josephson junctions, in which a thin insulating barrier separates two superconductors, exhibit a DC current at zero voltage and an AC current under bias; these effects are used in SQUIDs and superconducting qubits. High-temperature cuprates and iron-based superconductors remain an active research frontier.

In a two-dimensional electron gas at low temperature and high magnetic field, the Hall conductance becomes quantised: σxy = νe²/h, where ν is the filling factor. The integer quantum Hall effect follows from Landau levels and disorder-localised states; the fractional quantum Hall effect arises from strong electron correlations and supports quasiparticles with fractional charge and statistics. These phases exemplify topological order, where global properties are robust against local perturbations.
