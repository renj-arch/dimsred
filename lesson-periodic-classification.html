Periodic Classification of Elements — Periodic Trends, s/p/d/f Blocks & Properties

The periodic table is the master chart of chemical elements, arranging them in order of increasing atomic number and grouping them by similar electronic configurations and chemical properties. Mendeleev's periodic law: properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic masses. Moseley's modern periodic law: properties are a periodic function of atomic number. The long-form periodic table has 18 groups and 7 periods. Elements are classified into four blocks: s-block (groups 1–2, valence ns¹⁻²), p-block (groups 13–18, valence ns² np¹⁻⁶), d-block (groups 3–12, valence (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns¹⁻²), and f-block (lanthanides and actinides, valence (n−2)f¹⁻¹⁴). Periodic trends: atomic radius decreases across a period (increased nuclear charge) and increases down a group (more shells). Ionization energy — energy required to remove the outermost electron — increases across a period and decreases down a group. Electronegativity — the tendency of an atom to attract bonded electrons — increases across a period and decreases down a group. Electron affinity — energy released when an electron is added to a neutral atom — generally becomes more negative across a period and less negative down a group.

Part 1

📜 Historical Development and the Modern Periodic Table

Evolution
From Döbereiner's Triads to Mendeleev's Table and Moseley's Modern Law
Döbereiner's triads (1829): groups of three elements with similar properties where the atomic mass of the middle element is approximately the average of the other two (e.g., Li, Na, K; Ca, Sr, Ba). Newlands' law of octaves (1864): every eighth element has similar properties — analogous to musical octaves — but only worked up to calcium. Mendeleev's periodic law (1869): the properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic masses. Mendeleev left gaps for undiscovered elements and correctly predicted their properties (e.g., eka-aluminium → Ga, eka-silicon → Ge). He reversed some pairs (e.g., Te and I) to keep them in the correct group — an inconsistency resolved later. Moseley's law (1913): the frequency of X-rays emitted from an element is proportional to the square of its atomic number (√ν = a(Z − b)). This established atomic number, not atomic mass, as the fundamental ordering principle — the modern periodic law. The long-form periodic table has 18 groups and 7 periods. The periodic law: the physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers. The position of an element in the periodic table determines its electronic configuration and chemical behavior — metals on the left, non-metals on the right, metalloids along the staircase (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te).
The periodic table evolved from empirical observations (triads, octaves) through Mendeleev's predictive genius to Moseley's atomic-number-based foundation — now the organizing principle of all chemistry.
Part 2

🧬 Electronic Configuration and Block Classification

Blocks
s-block, p-block, d-block, f-block Elements — Configuration, Groups, and Periodicity
s-block elements (groups 1 and 2): valence electrons in the ns orbital (ns¹⁻²). Group 1 (alkali metals): Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr — highly reactive, soft, low melting points, form +1 ions. Group 2 (alkaline earth metals): Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra — less reactive than alkali metals, form +2 ions. p-block elements (groups 13–18): valence electrons in np orbitals (ns² np¹⁻⁶). Includes metals, metalloids, and non-metals. Group 18 (noble gases): completely filled ns² np⁶ — very low reactivity. d-block elements (groups 3–12): the transition metals, with valence in (n−1)d orbitals (1–10 electrons) and ns orbitals (1–2 electrons). They exhibit variable oxidation states, colored compounds, paramagnetism, and catalytic activity. f-block elements: lanthanides (Ce to Lu, 4f¹⁻¹⁴) and actinides (Th to Lr, 5f¹⁻¹⁴). Lanthanides show lanthanide contraction (steady decrease in ionic radius with increasing atomic number). Periods: Period 1 (2 elements, 1s), Period 2–3 (8 elements each, s + p), Period 4–5 (18 elements each, s + d + p), Period 6 (32 elements, s + f + d + p), Period 7 (incomplete). Periodicity: the repetition of similar properties after regular intervals due to similar valence electron configurations.
The s, p, d, f block classification directly reflects the filling order of atomic orbitals and explains the periodicity of chemical properties across the table.
Part 3

📊 Periodic Trends: Atomic Radius, Ionization Energy, and Electron Affinity

Trends
Variation of Atomic Radius, Ionic Radius, Ionization Enthalpy, and Electron Gain Enthalpy
Atomic radius: half the distance between two bonded atoms. Trends: decreases across a period (nuclear charge increases, electrons added to same shell — stronger pull) and increases down a group (new shells added, shielding increases). Ionic radius: cations are smaller than their parent atoms (lost electrons → less repulsion, increased effective nuclear charge); anions are larger (added electrons → more repulsion). Ionization enthalpy (IE): the minimum energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom. First IE generally increases across a period and decreases down a group. Exceptions: IE of N > O (half-filled p³ stability) and IE of Be > B (fully filled s² is stable). Successive IEs increase sharply (IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃) — the large jump indicates the number of valence electrons. Electron gain enthalpy (Δ_eg H): the energy change when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom. Becomes more negative across a period (higher attraction). Group 17 (halogens) have the most negative values. Group 18 (noble gases) have large positive values due to stable octet. The second electron gain enthalpy is always positive (repulsion of already present negative charge). Trend anomalies: IE of Mg > Al (s² p¹ — Al has one electron in a higher-energy p orbital, easier to remove).
Atomic size decreases across a period and increases down a group; ionization energy and electron affinity follow predictable trends with exceptions explained by orbital stability (half-filled, fully filled).
Part 4

⚖️ Electronegativity, Valence, and Periodic Applications

Bonding
Electronegativity Scales, Oxidation States, Periodic Applications, and Anomalies
Electronegativity (χ): the tendency of an atom in a molecule to attract shared electrons toward itself. The Pauling scale (most common, χ_F = 4.0): χ_A − χ_B = 0.102 √Δ, where Δ is the extra bond energy in kcal/mol. The Mulliken scale: χ = (IE + EA)/2 (average of ionization energy and electron affinity). The Allred-Rochow scale: based on effective nuclear charge and covalent radius. Trend: electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group. F is the most electronegative element (4.0), Cs and Fr are the least (~0.7). Oxidation states: the number of electrons an atom uses for bonding. s-block: +1 (group 1), +2 (group 2). p-block: variable — group 13 (+3), group 14 (±4), group 15 (−3 to +5), group 16 (−2 to +6), group 17 (−1 to +7). d-block: variable (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, Cu⁺/Cu²⁺, Mn²⁺ to Mn⁷⁺). Periodic applications: predicting bond type (ionic if Δχ > 1.7, covalent if Δχ < 1.7), explaining chemical reactivity (alkali metals are strong reductants, halogens are strong oxidants), understanding anomalous properties of second-period elements (Li differs from other group 1 due to small size), and the diagonal relationship (Li with Mg, Be with Al, B with Si — due to similar charge/radius ratio). Inert pair effect: the reluctance of s-electrons in heavier p-block elements to participate in bonding (e.g., Tl⁺ more stable than Tl³⁺, Pb²⁺ more stable than Pb⁴⁺).
Electronegativity determines bond polarity and chemical reactivity; periodic anomalies like the inert pair effect and diagonal relationship reveal nuances in periodicity.

Lanthanide and Actinide Contraction: Causes, Consequences, and Comparative Analysis

The steady decrease in ionic radii across the lanthanide series, known as the lanthanide contraction, arises from the poor shielding of the nuclear charge by the 4f electrons. As the atomic number increases from cerium (Z = 58) to lutetium (Z = 71), each added electron enters the 4f subshell, which is radially contracted and does not effectively screen the increasing nuclear charge. Consequently, the effective nuclear charge experienced by the outer 5d and 6s electrons rises, pulling them closer to the nucleus and reducing the ionic radius by roughly 0.2 Å across the series. The same phenomenon occurs in the actinide series (5f contraction), but the effect is amplified because the 5f orbitals are more diffuse and relativistic effects become significant for the heavier actinides.

Consequences of the lanthanide contraction are far‑reaching. The near‑identical radii of the 4d and 5d transition metals (e.g., Zr ≈ Hf, Nb ≈ Ta, Mo ≈ W) cause their chemical properties to be remarkably similar, making separation of these pairs industrially challenging. In the post‑lanthanide elements, the contraction leads to higher ionisation energies and smaller atomic radii than would be predicted by simple periodic trends, influencing the stability of oxidation states — for example, the +4 state of hafnium is more stable than that of zirconium. The actinide contraction similarly affects the chemistry of the trans‑uranium elements, contributing to the increasing stability of the +3 oxidation state across the series and the difficulty of isolating higher oxidation states for the later actinides.

Anomalous Behavior of First Row Transition Metals: Oxidation States, Complex Formation, and Magnetic Properties

The first transition series (Sc to Zn) displays a rich variety of oxidation states that deviate from the simple group‑valence expectation. Scandium and titanium commonly exhibit +3 and +4, respectively, but vanadium shows +2, +3, +4, +5; chromium is most stable as +3 and +6; manganese spans +2 to +7; iron favors +2 and +3; cobalt +2 and +3; nickel +2; copper +1 and +2; zinc only +2. The multiplicity arises because the (n‑1)d and ns orbitals are close in energy, allowing electrons to be removed from either set. The extra stability of half‑filled (d⁵) and fully filled (d¹⁰) configurations creates pronounced peaks: Mn²⁺ (d⁵) and Fe³⁺ (d⁵) are unusually stable, while Cu⁺ (d¹⁰) is favored over Cu²⁺ in many soft‑ligand environments.

Complex formation is governed by crystal‑field stabilization energy (CFSE). Octahedral complexes of d⁴–d⁷ ions can be high‑spin or low‑spin depending on the ligand field strength. Strong‑field ligands (CN⁻, CO) produce low‑spin configurations with larger CFSE, often stabilizing higher oxidation states (e.g., [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ vs. [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺). The spectrochemical series orders ligands by field strength, a concept examiners frequently pair with magnetic moment calculations. The spin‑only magnetic moment μₛₒ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons, provides a quick check: high‑spin d⁵ (Fe³⁺) gives 5.92 BM, low‑spin d⁵ (Fe³⁺ in strong field) gives 1.73 BM. Discrepancies between measured and spin‑only values signal orbital contribution, a classic trap.

Magnetic properties also reveal anomalies. The Curie‑Weiss law describes paramagnetic susceptibility, but many first‑row complexes show temperature‑independent paramagnetism (TIP) due to low‑lying excited states. For example, Ni²⁺ (d⁸) in octahedral geometry is typically paramagnetic with two unpaired electrons, yet square‑planar Ni²⁺ complexes (e.g., [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻) are diamagnetic because the dₓ²₋ᵧ² orbital is raised far above the others, forcing electron pairing. Such geometry‑dependent magnetism is a favorite examination topic, often framed as “predict the magnetic moment of [NiCl₄]²⁻ versus [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻”.

Worked Example: Oxidation State and Magnetic Moment Prediction
A complex [M(CN)₆]³⁻ is known to be low‑spin and diamagnetic. Identify the metal M from the first transition series and give its oxidation state.
CN⁻ is a strong‑field ligand, so the complex will be low‑spin. Diamagnetism means zero unpaired electrons. In an octahedral low‑spin d⁶ configuration (t₂g⁶ e_g⁰) all electrons are paired. The only first‑row metal that can achieve d⁶ in a +3 oxidation state is Fe (Fe³⁺ is d⁵, not d⁶). However, Fe²⁺ is d⁶. The overall charge of the complex is –3; each CN⁻ contributes –1, total –6. Let the metal oxidation state be x. Then x + (–6) = –3 → x = +3. This gives Fe³⁺ (d⁵), which is not diamagnetic. The next candidate is Co³⁺ (d⁶). Co³⁺ with six CN⁻ gives [Co(CN)₆]³⁻, low‑spin d⁶, diamagnetic. Therefore M = Co, oxidation state +3.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the magnetic moment of a transition‑metal complex, first determine the d‑electron count from the oxidation state, then decide high‑spin vs. low‑spin using the ligand’s position in the spectrochemical series. Write the spin‑only formula μ = √[n(n+2)] and state the number of unpaired electrons explicitly; this earns partial credit even if the final numeric value is mis‑calculated.

Periodic Trends in Metallic Character, Melting Points, and Density Across Blocks

Metallic character — the tendency to lose electrons and form a delocalised electron sea — decreases across a period and increases down a group. In the s‑block, the large atomic radii and low ionisation energies of Group 1 and 2 metals give them the highest metallic character. Across the p‑block, the transition from metals (Al, Ga, In, Tl) to metalloids (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te) to non‑metals (C, N, O, F, Ne) mirrors the rise in electronegativity and ionisation energy. The d‑block metals retain strong metallic bonding throughout the series because the (n‑1)d electrons contribute to the conduction band, but the melting points peak near the middle of each series (Cr, Mo, W) where the d‑band is half‑filled, maximising covalent‑metallic bond strength. The f‑block metals (lanthanides) show relatively low melting points (≈ 800–1100 °C) compared with the 5d transition metals, reflecting the localized nature of the 4f electrons which do not participate significantly in metallic bonding.

Melting‑point trends can be rationalised by the number of unpaired d‑electrons available for metallic bonding. In the first transition series, the melting points rise from Sc (1541 °C) to a maximum at Cr (1907 °C) and then fall to Zn (420 °C). The half‑filled d⁵ configuration of Cr provides the greatest number of unpaired electrons for metallic bonding, while the filled d¹⁰ of Zn contributes none. A similar pattern appears in the 4d and 5d series, with Mo (2623 °C) and W (3422 °C) as the respective peaks. The anomalously high melting point of tungsten is further enhanced by relativistic contraction of the 6s orbital, strengthening the metallic bond.

Density trends follow atomic mass and packing efficiency. The s‑block metals are light (Li = 0.53 g cm⁻³, Na = 0.97 g cm⁻³) because of low atomic mass and open‑packed structures. The d‑block metals are dense (Os = 22.59 g cm⁻³, Ir = 22.56 g cm⁻³) due to high atomic masses and close‑packed hexagonal or face‑centred cubic lattices. The lanthanides show a steady increase in density from La (6.15 g cm⁻³) to Lu (9.84 g cm⁻³) as the lanthanide contraction reduces volume while mass rises. Actinides are even denser (U = 19.1 g cm⁻³, Np = 20.4 g cm⁻³) because the 5f electrons are more extended and contribute to a more compact metallic lattice.

These trends are often combined in data‑interpretation questions: a table of melting points, densities, and atomic radii for a set of elements is given, and the candidate must identify the block, period, or group of an unknown element. Recognising that a high melting point coupled with high density and a relatively small atomic radius points to a 5d transition metal (e.g., W, Re, Os) is a rapid diagnostic.

Relativistic Effects on Heavy Element Chemistry: Gold, Mercury, and the Superheavy Elements

For elements with Z > 70, the inner‑shell electrons attain velocities approaching a significant fraction of the speed of light. Relativistic mass increase contracts the s and p₁/₂ orbitals (direct relativistic effect) and expands the d and f orbitals (indirect effect). The most celebrated consequence is the colour and inertness of gold. The 6s orbital contraction lowers its energy, while the 5d orbitals expand, reducing the 5d–6s gap. This shifts the plasma frequency into the visible range, giving gold its characteristic yellow colour, and raises the ionisation energy, making Au⁺ and Au³⁺ unusually stable compared with Ag⁺ and Cu⁺.

Mercury’s liquid state at room temperature is another hallmark. The 6s² electrons are strongly stabilised and contracted, weakening the metallic bonding that normally arises from delocalised s‑electrons. The resulting low cohesive energy yields a melting point of –38.8 °C. Relativistic effects also explain the high volatility of copernicium (element 112), predicted to be a gas or very volatile liquid, and the unexpected stability of the +2 oxidation state in flerovium (element 114) despite its position in Group 14.

In the superheavy region (Z ≥ 104), relativistic effects dominate chemistry. The 7s orbital is heavily contracted, the 7p₁/₂ is stabilised, and the 6d and 5f orbitals are destabilised. Calculations predict that element 112 (Cn) will behave more like a noble gas than a transition metal, while element 118 (Og) may exhibit measurable reactivity due to the destabilised 7p₃/₂ orbital. These predictions are tested indirectly through gas‑phase adsorption experiments on single atoms, a frontier topic that appears in advanced competitive papers as “explain why the chemistry of element 112 deviates from that of its lighter homologues”.

Key quantitative indicators: the relativistic contraction of the 6s orbital in gold is ≈ 0.2 Å; the spin‑orbit splitting of the 7p orbital in oganesson is ≈ 10 eV. Candidates should be able to link these numbers to observable properties — colour, melting point, oxidation‑state stability — without needing to perform the Dirac‑Fock calculations themselves.

Application of Periodic Trends in Predicting Reaction Mechanisms and Catalytic Activity

Periodic trends provide a framework for rationalising catalytic cycles. The Sabatier principle states that optimal catalysts bind reactants neither too weakly nor too strongly. Across the d‑block, the d‑band centre relative to the Fermi level correlates with adsorption strength: early transition metals (Ti, V) bind too strongly, late transition metals (Cu, Ag) bind too weakly, while the middle (Fe, Co, Ni, Ru, Rh, Pd, Pt) sit near the optimum. This volcano‑plot relationship is a staple of heterogeneous catalysis questions.

In homogeneous catalysis, the oxidation‑state flexibility of a metal centre dictates the feasible elementary steps. For example, the Wilkinson catalyst RhCl(PPh₃)₃ operates via Rh(I)/Rh(III) redox cycling. The ease of accessing Rh(III) from Rh(I) is governed by the relatively low third ionisation energy of rhodium and the stabilising effect of strong‑field phosphine ligands. Similarly, the Ziegler‑Natta polymerisation uses Ti(IV)/Ti(III) interconversion; the small size and high charge density of Ti⁴⁺ (a consequence of the lanthanide contraction on the 4d series) favour coordination of the olefin monomer.

Periodic trends also guide the design of oxidative addition/reductive elimination steps in cross‑coupling reactions. Palladium(0) complexes (d¹⁰) undergo oxidative addition to aryl halides because the Pd(II) product (d⁸) gains CFSE in a square‑planar geometry. The analogous Ni(0)/Ni(II) cycle is faster but less selective, reflecting nickel’s smaller atomic radius and higher ionisation energy. Examiners often ask: “Why does Pd(PPh₃)₄ catalyse Suzuki coupling more efficiently than Ni(PPh₃)₄?” The answer hinges on the balance between oxidative‑addition rate (favoured by Ni) and reductive‑elimination rate (favoured by Pd), both traceable to periodic properties.

Finally, the inert‑pair effect in heavy p‑block elements (Tl, Pb, Bi) influences catalytic redox cycles involving +1/+3 or +2/+4 couples. The reluctance of the 6s² electrons to ionise makes Tl⁺ a stable, soft Lewis acid useful in certain Friedel‑Crafts‑type reactions, while Pb²⁺ is preferred over Pb⁴⁺ in lead‑based oxidants. Recognising these nuances allows candidates to predict the viability of a proposed catalytic mechanism solely from the position of the metal in the periodic table.

  • Assuming that all transition metals exhibit the same range of oxidation states; the first row is far more variable than the second and third rows.
  • Confusing the lanthanide contraction with the actinide contraction and attributing identical magnitude to both series.
  • Using the spin‑only magnetic moment formula without checking for orbital contribution, leading to incorrect μ values for ions like Co²⁺ (high‑spin d⁷) or Ni²⁺ (square‑planar).
  • Neglecting relativistic effects when explaining the properties of gold, mercury, or superheavy elements, treating them as simple extrapolations of lighter congeners.
  • Applying the Sabatier volcano plot without considering that the d‑band centre shifts with ligand environment, not just the metal identity.
  • Overlooking the inert‑pair effect in p‑block catalysis, assuming that the highest oxidation state is always the most reactive.
  • Misidentifying the block of an unknown element from melting‑point and density data by ignoring the distinct peak at the half‑filled d‑shell.
