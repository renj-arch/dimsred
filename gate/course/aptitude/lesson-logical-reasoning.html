What is Logical Reasoning?

Logical Reasoning in GATE General Aptitude tests your ability to identify patterns, analyse relationships, and draw valid conclusions. Topics include analogies, syllogisms, puzzles, blood relations, direction sense, and number/letter series. GATE typically has 2-3 logical reasoning questions in the aptitude section.

Key Concepts

1. Analogies: Relationships include synonym/antonym, cause-effect, part-whole, worker-tool, product-material, animal-young one, degree of intensity. For letter analogies, use positional values (A=1 to Z=26). Number analogies use arithmetic operations.

2. Syllogisms: Statement types: All A are B (circle A inside B), No A is B (separate circles), Some A are B (overlapping circles), Some A are not B (partial overlap). A conclusion follows only if true in ALL possible Venn diagrams.

3. Blood Relations: Build a family tree step by step. Key: 'son of' or 'daughter of' indicates parent-child relationship. 'Brother/sister of' indicates sibling relationship. 'Husband/wife of' indicates marital relationship.

4. Direction Sense: Track movement step by step using a grid. Draw arrows for each movement. Right turn = +90° (clockwise from current direction). Left turn = -90° (anticlockwise). Use Pythagoras for net displacement.

5. Number & Letter Series: Number series: check differences, ratios, squares/cubes, alternating patterns, Fibonacci. Letter series: check positional value differences — often involves consistent addition/subtraction or a pattern of operations.

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Analogy)
Find the missing term: Engineer : Blueprint :: Architect : ?

a) Building b) Design c) Construction d) Site
Solution: b) Design. An engineer creates a blueprint. Similarly, an architect creates a design. This is a worker-product relationship.
Example 2 (Blood Relation)
Pointing to a man, Priya said, 'He is the son of the only daughter of my father.' How is the man related to Priya?

a) Brother b) Son c) Cousin d) Nephew
Solution: b) Son. Priya's father has only one daughter — that must be Priya herself (since Priya is a female name). The man is the son of that only daughter (Priya). Therefore, the man is Priya's son.
Example 3 (Number Series)
Find the next term: 3, 8, 15, 24, 35, ?

a) 42 b) 44 c) 48 d) 49
Solution: c) 48. Pattern: 1×3=3, 2×4=8, 3×5=15, 4×6=24, 5×7=35, 6×8=48.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For syllogisms in GATE, the Venn diagram method is most reliable. Draw all possible configurations before checking conclusions.
  • In blood relations, carefully track gender-specific terms (only 'mother' and 'sister' indicate female; 'father', 'brother', 'son' indicate male).
  • For series questions, examine differences first — if differences are constant or follow a pattern, you've found it. If not, check ratios or alternate terms.
  • In direction problems, draw a diagram for each movement — mental tracking often leads to errors in multi-step problems.
  • For analogy questions, identify the exact relationship in the first pair before checking options — multiple options may have partial similarity.
Pro Tip
GATE logical reasoning questions are straightforward and test basic aptitude. Do not overthink — if the answer seems too complex, you are likely on the wrong track. Practice 3-5 questions daily to maintain speed. These questions are easy to score if you identify the pattern quickly.

Advanced Applications

Modern exams feature questions combining multiple quantitative concepts. Master these by practicing previous year papers.

Shortcut Methods

Percentages: Use fraction table (12.5%=1/8, 20%=1/5, 25%=1/4). SI/CI: For 2 years, CI-SI = P(r/100)^2. Time Work: Use LCM of time periods. Average Speed: 2ab/(a+b) for equal distances.

Categorical Syllogisms: Structure, Mood, and Figure

A categorical syllogism consists of three propositions: two premises and a conclusion, each containing a subject, a predicate, and a copula. The four standard forms are A (All S are P), E (No S are P), I (Some S are P), and O (Some S are not P). The mood of a syllogism is the sequence of these letters for the major premise, minor premise, and conclusion (e.g., AAA, EIO). The figure is determined by the position of the middle term (M) in the premises: Figure 1 (M‑P, S‑M), Figure 2 (P‑M, S‑M), Figure 3 (M‑P, M‑S), Figure 4 (P‑M, M‑S). Only 24 of the 256 possible mood‑figure combinations are valid under the traditional rules of distribution, existential import, and the prohibition of illicit major/minor terms.

The distribution rule states that a term distributed in the conclusion must be distributed in at least one premise. In an A proposition the subject is distributed; in an E both terms are distributed; in an I none are distributed; in an O the predicate is distributed. The middle term must be distributed at least once, otherwise the syllogism commits the fallacy of undistributed middle. Additionally, no conclusion may be drawn from two negative premises, and a negative premise requires a negative conclusion. These constraints reduce the valid forms to the classic 15 moods across the four figures (Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio in Figure 1; Cesare, Camestres, Festino, Baroco in Figure 2; etc.).

Modern examinations often test the ability to recognise invalid forms quickly. A common shortcut is to convert each premise into a Venn diagram with three overlapping circles (S, M, P). Shade the area representing “No S are P” or “All S are P” as required, then place an “×” for particular statements. If the conclusion’s diagram is already forced in every possible shading, the syllogism is valid. This visual method bypasses memorisation of the 15 valid moods and works uniformly for all figures.

Exceptions arise when existential import is assumed for universal statements. Classical logic treats “All S are P” as implying the existence of at least one S, whereas modern Boolean interpretation does not. In exam questions that explicitly state “Assume existential import”, the valid moods expand to include forms such as AAI‑1 (Barbari) and EAO‑1 (Celaront). Without that assumption, these forms are invalid because the conclusion “Some S are P” cannot be guaranteed from universal premises alone.

Practical tip: when a question presents a syllogism in natural language, first identify the three terms, assign them to S, M, P, then rewrite each premise in standard form. Check the mood and figure against the valid list or draw the three‑circle Venn diagram. This two‑step process eliminates the most frequent error — mis‑identifying the middle term — and yields a reliable answer within 30 seconds.

Analogical Reasoning: Classification of Relations and Inference Rules

Analogies test the ability to perceive a structural similarity between two pairs of concepts. The relationship in the first pair (A : B) must be reproduced exactly in the second pair (C : D). Standard relation categories include: (1) Worker‑Product (carpenter : table), (2) Tool‑Function (scalpel : incise), (3) Cause‑Effect (rain : flood), (4) Part‑Whole (wheel : car), (5) Degree‑Intensity (warm : hot), (6) Symbol‑Referent (dove : peace), (7) Synonym/Antonym (joy : sorrow), (8) Classification (rose : flower), (9) Sequential (seed : sprout), and (10) Mathematical/Operational (2 : 4 :: 3 : 6). Recognising the precise category is essential because many distractors share a superficial similarity but belong to a different category.

A systematic approach: (a) articulate the relationship in a full sentence (“A carpenter makes a table”), (b) test each option by inserting it into the same sentence frame (“An architect makes a …”), (c) discard options that change the grammatical role (e.g., “building” is the product, not the act of designing), and (d) verify that the direction of the relation is preserved (worker‑product, not product‑worker). When multiple options survive, look for a secondary constraint such as gender, number, or domain specificity (e.g., “architect : blueprint” vs. “architect : design” — both are worker‑product, but “blueprint” is the technical document an architect produces, making it the tighter fit).

Advanced analogies may involve multi‑step relations: A : B :: C : D where the link from A to B is “A is the cause of B” and the link from C to D is “C is the prevention of D”. In such cases the relation type flips (cause vs. prevention) and the correct answer must mirror the logical inversion. Another twist is the “double analogy” where two independent relations hold simultaneously (e.g., “pen : ink :: brush : paint” — tool‑medium and instrument‑material). The examinee must identify the dominant relation that is common to both pairs.

Letter‑based analogies follow the same principle but operate on positional values (A=1, B=2, …). Common patterns: constant addition (A : C :: D : F, +2), alternating addition/subtraction, mirror positions (A : Z :: B : Y), or prime‑index mapping. The solver should compute the numeric difference for the first pair, then apply the identical arithmetic operation to the third term. If the operation yields a value outside 1‑26, wrap‑around (mod 26) is usually implied unless the question states otherwise.

Practice with a taxonomy table of relation types accelerates pattern recognition. Write the ten categories on a reference sheet, list two canonical examples per category, and rehearse converting a given pair into a sentence. Over time the classification becomes automatic, reducing the average solution time from 45 seconds to under 15 seconds.

Double Analogy Resolution
Select the pair that best matches the relationship in the given pair:  Catalyst : Reaction :: ? : ?  Options: (a) Enzyme : Metabolism, (b) Fuel : Engine, (c) Key : Lock, (d) Teacher : Student.
Step 1: Express the stem relation. A catalyst accelerates a reaction without being consumed; it is an agent that enables a process. Step 2: Test each option. (a) Enzyme : Metabolism — an enzyme catalyses metabolic reactions, mirroring the agent‑process link. (b) Fuel : Engine — fuel is consumed, not a reusable agent. (c) Key : Lock — key opens a lock, a tool‑object relation, not an enabling agent. (d) Teacher : Student — teacher instructs, a human‑role relation. Only (a) preserves the exact logical structure: reusable agent : process it enables. Answer: (a).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When you encounter an analogy, write the stem relation in a single clause on the rough sheet (e.g., “X enables Y”). Then read each option aloud, substituting into the clause. The option that yields a grammatically and logically coherent sentence is the answer. This verbalisation prevents silent mis‑reading and costs only a few seconds.

Complex Puzzle Architectures: Multi‑Variable Linear and Circular Arrangements

Puzzles that combine several attributes — such as person, colour, profession, and floor — require a matrix or grid rather than a simple line diagram. The standard technique is to create a table with rows representing entities (e.g., five persons) and columns for each attribute. Clues are of three types: direct assignment (“A lives on floor 3”), relative ordering (“B is immediately above C”), and conditional (“If D is a doctor, then E likes red”). Process direct clues first, filling cells definitively. Next, translate ordering clues into positional constraints (e.g., “B above C” means row(B) = row(C) − 1 in a bottom‑to‑top numbering). Conditional clues are handled by branching: assume the antecedent true, propagate consequences, and check for contradictions; if a contradiction appears, the antecedent must be false.

Circular arrangements add rotational symmetry. Fix one reference point arbitrarily (e.g., place “P” at the 12 o’clock position) to break symmetry, then apply clockwise/anticlockwise clues. “X sits third to the left of Y” translates to moving three steps anticlockwise from Y. When multiple circular tables interact (e.g., two concentric circles with facing pairs), draw both circles and map facing relations as vertical links. The same matrix method works: each seat becomes a row, attributes become columns, and facing pairs share a column for “opposite”.

Advanced puzzles often embed a “unique solution” requirement. After populating all certain cells, count the remaining empty cells. If the number of free permutations exceeds one, re‑examine clues for hidden exclusivity (e.g., “Only one person likes blue”). Such uniqueness clues convert a partially filled grid into a fully determined one. Another hidden constraint is the “pigeonhole principle”: if there are four colours and five persons, at least one colour repeats; a clue stating “All colours are different” would be contradictory, signalling a mis‑interpretation.

Time management: allocate a maximum of 2 minutes per puzzle. If after 90 seconds the grid has more than three ambiguous cells, flag the question, move on, and return later. The mental cost of holding a large incomplete matrix is high; a fresh look often reveals a missed direct clue.

Practice regimen: solve at least three multi‑attribute puzzles daily, varying the number of entities (4‑7) and attributes (3‑5). Use a spreadsheet template to automate row/column creation, allowing focus on logical deduction rather than drawing.

Blood‑Relation Coding: Generational Mapping and Gender Inference

Blood‑relation problems are essentially graph traversals on a family tree where nodes are individuals and edges encode parent‑child, sibling, or marital links. The most reliable representation is a generational diagram: place the oldest known generation at the top, draw vertical lines for parent‑child, horizontal lines for marriage, and bracket siblings. Gender is indicated by symbols (△ for male, ○ for female) or by pronouns in the statement. Every “son of” or “daughter of” fixes a child‑parent edge and the child’s gender; “brother of” or “sister of” fixes a sibling edge and the subject’s gender; “husband of” or “wife of” fixes a marital edge and the gender of both spouses.

A systematic decoding algorithm: (1) Identify the anchor person (usually the speaker). (2) Parse each relational phrase sequentially, updating the diagram. (3) When a phrase contains “only” (e.g., “only daughter of my father”), it creates a uniqueness constraint that often identifies the speaker’s gender and eliminates siblings. (4) After the diagram is complete, answer the target question by tracing the path between the two persons and counting generational steps (up = parent, down = child, across = sibling/spouse). The relationship name follows standard nomenclature: 0 generations up/down = self; 1 up = parent; 1 down = child; 2 up = grand‑parent; 2 down = grand‑child; 1 up + 1 down = uncle/aunt/nephew/niece depending on gender; same generation across marriage = brother‑in‑law/sister‑in‑law.

Common pitfalls: (a) Assuming “brother” implies male speaker — the speaker could be female referring to her brother. (b) Ignoring “only” which collapses multiple possible siblings into a single individual. (c) Mis‑reading “father’s brother” as “uncle” without checking whether the father has multiple brothers; the phrase “the brother” implies uniqueness. (d) Overlooking marital links that change generational level (e.g., “wife of my brother’s son” is a daughter‑in‑law, not a niece).

Speed technique: encode each relation as a vector (Δgen, Δgender, Δmarital). For example, “son” = (+1, male, none), “wife” = (0, female, married). Summing vectors along the described chain yields the net generational offset and gender of the target relative. This algebraic method reduces a verbal puzzle to arithmetic and eliminates diagramming errors under time pressure.

Direction‑Sense and Series Integration: Composite Displacement and Pattern Fusion

Direction‑sense questions track a moving point on a plane. The standard coordinate system uses North = +Y, East = +X. A right turn adds 90° clockwise to the current heading; a left turn subtracts 90°. After a sequence of moves (distance, turn, distance, …) the net displacement vector (ΔX, ΔY) is obtained by summing the component vectors of each leg. The straight‑line distance from the origin is √(ΔX² + ΔY²). The bearing from the origin is given by arctan(ΔY/ΔX) with quadrant correction. In exams, distances are usually integers, so the final distance often simplifies to an integer or a simple radical (e.g., 5√2).

Series questions frequently combine arithmetic, geometric, and positional patterns. A composite series may interleave two independent sub‑series (odd positions follow one rule, even positions another). Detect interleaving by writing terms with indices: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 … If differences of odd‑indexed terms form a constant sequence while even‑indexed terms follow a ratio, the series is interleaved. Another fusion is “difference of differences”: compute first differences, then second differences; constant second differences indicate a quadratic pattern (an² + bn + c). When letters appear, convert to positional values (A=1 … Z=26) and apply the same numeric analysis.

Hybrid problems present a direction‑sense walk where each step length is drawn from a numeric series. Example: “Start facing North. Move 3 m, turn right, move 5 m, turn left, move 8 m, turn right, move 13 m …” The step lengths 3, 5, 8, 13 follow a Fibonacci‑like recurrence (each term = sum of previous two). The solver must first generate the required number of terms, then apply the turn sequence to compute the final coordinate. This tests both series generation and vector addition in a single item.

Shortcut for hybrid items: write the heading after each turn as a unit vector (N = (0,1), E = (1,0), S = (0,−1), W = (−1,0)). Multiply each step length by its heading vector and accumulate. Because headings repeat every four turns, the heading sequence can be pre‑computed for the given turn pattern (e.g., R, L, R, L …). This reduces the problem to a dot‑product sum that can be evaluated mentally for small sequences.

Practice plan: create a spreadsheet that generates random turn strings (length 4‑8) and random series (arithmetic, geometric, Fibonacci, quadratic). Compute the final coordinate automatically, then solve manually to verify. Repeat until the combined reasoning becomes fluid — typically 15‑20 hybrid drills.

  • Assuming a universal statement carries existential import when the question follows Boolean interpretation.
  • Mis‑identifying the middle term in a syllogism, leading to an incorrect figure assignment.
  • Choosing an analogy option that shares a superficial category but reverses the direction of the relation.
  • Neglecting the “only” qualifier in blood‑relation statements, which collapses multiple possible siblings.
  • Forgetting to fix a reference point in circular arrangements, causing rotational ambiguity.
  • Treating a right turn as anticlockwise when the current heading is South or West.
  • Overlooking interleaved sub‑series and forcing a single pattern on the whole sequence.

Conditional Syllogisms and Hypothetical Reasoning

Conditional syllogisms extend categorical logic with implication statements (“If P then Q”). The two valid forms are modus ponens (P, If P then Q ⊢ Q) and modus tollens (¬Q, If P then Q ⊢ ¬P). Common fallacies are affirming the consequent (Q, If P then Q ⊢ P) and denying the antecedent (¬P, If P then Q ⊢ ¬Q). In exam items, antecedent and consequent are often embedded in passages; isolate the conditional core first. Rewrite statements symbolically, then apply a truth‑table check for the valid forms. When conditionals chain (If P then Q, If Q then R), hypothetical syllogism yields If P then R. This chaining is tested by presenting “if‑then” rules and asking which conclusion follows necessarily. Categorical and conditional premises may be mixed; solve the categorical part with Venn diagrams and the conditional part with propositional rules, then combine results. A conditional does not assert its antecedent; any conclusion assuming the antecedent without support is invalid.

Analogy Types: Functional, Causal, and Structural Mapping

Analogies are classified by the relation between pair terms. Functional analogies link object to purpose (knife : cut :: pen : write). Causal analogies link cause to effect (rain : flood :: spark : fire). Structural analogies link items with similar internal arrangement (atom : solar system :: cell : organism). Recognising the type guides the search for the answer. Systematic approach: (1) Express the stem relation concisely (“A is used to B”, “A causes B”, “A is a part of B”). (2) Test each option by substituting into the same phrase. (3) Discard options where the phrase fails or reverses. Distractors often share a superficial category but violate relational direction (tool : user vs. user : tool). “Double analogy” items require two independent relations simultaneously (e.g., “glove : hand :: sock : foot” and “glove : leather :: sock : cotton”); the correct option must satisfy both. Building a personal taxonomy of 30‑40 stem relations with verbal templates reduces cognitive load during the test.

Worked Example: Double Analogy
Select the pair that best matches the relationship in the stem: “Thermometer : Temperature :: Barometer : ?” Options: (A) Pressure (B) Altitude (C) Humidity (D) Wind
The stem shows an instrument‑to‑measurement relation: a thermometer measures temperature. The first term is the device, the second the quantity measured. Applying the same template: a barometer measures pressure. Option (A) Pressure fits. Option (B) Altitude is measured by an altimeter. Options (C) and (D) are unrelated. The correct answer is (A) Pressure.
Exam‑Day Strategy
For analogy clusters, write the relational phrase on the rough sheet before scanning options. This prevents selecting a categorically similar but relationally reversed pair. Allocate roughly 30 seconds per analogy; if no option fits, flag and return later.

Multi‑Layered Puzzle Solving: Constraint Propagation and Search Pruning

Complex arrangement puzzles (linear, circular, matrix) often involve 6‑10 entities with multiple attributes (colour, profession, floor, vehicle). An efficient method is constraint propagation: represent each entity as a node with a domain of possible values per attribute. Apply direct clues first (“A lives on floor 3”) to reduce domains. Then use relational clues (“B is immediately above C”) to enforce binary constraints, eliminating impossible pairs. When a domain collapses to one value, propagate that assignment to connected constraints. This iterative narrowing continues until a unique solution emerges or a branching point is reached. At a branch, choose the variable with the smallest remaining domain (minimum remaining values heuristic) and try values recursively, backtracking on contradiction. Maintain arc consistency after each assignment; if any domain empties, backtrack immediately. In timed conditions, solve the deterministic core on paper, then sketch a decision tree for the ambiguous sub‑puzzle. Questions often ask for a single attribute (e.g., “Who owns the red car?”), allowing you to stop once that attribute is fixed across viable branches. Practice with random constraint sets; aim to complete a typical 7‑entity, 4‑attribute puzzle in a few minutes.

Blood‑Relation Networks: Graph Representation and Cycle Detection

Blood‑relation problems can be modelled as a directed graph: vertices are individuals; edges encode parent‑child (directed upward), sibling (undirected), and marital (undirected, labelled “spouse”). Gender is a vertex attribute. A query (“How is X related to Y?”) becomes a shortest‑path problem with edge weights for generational steps (parent = +1, child = –1, sibling = 0, spouse = 0). The relationship name derives from net generational offset and target gender. Cycles appear when marital links connect branches (e.g., cousins marrying); they create multiple paths. The correct relationship follows the path with the smallest absolute generational sum, unless a unique route is specified (“the only brother”). Algorithmically, run breadth‑first search from the source, tracking cumulative offset and gender. On reaching the target, compute the label via a lookup table: offset 0 + same gender = brother/sister; offset 0 + opposite gender = sister/brother; offset +1 = father/mother; offset –1 = son/daughter; offset +2 = grandfather/grandmother; offset –2 = grandson/granddaughter; offset +1 with sibling edge = uncle/aunt; offset –1 with sibling edge = nephew/niece. Marital edges add the “in‑law” suffix without changing offset. This graph method helps avoid diagramming errors and handles “only” qualifiers by pruning sibling edges when a vertex has degree one in the sibling subgraph.

Series Fusion: Polynomial, Exponential, and Recursive Hybrid Patterns

Advanced series questions fuse independent patterns into one sequence. Common types: (1) Interleaved sub‑series – odd positions follow a quadratic rule (an²+bn+c), even positions a geometric progression (arⁿ⁻¹). Detect by separating indices and computing first/second differences per subsequence. (2) Recursive‑exponential blend – each term equals previous term times a constant plus a polynomial increment (Tₙ = k·Tₙ₋₁ + p(n)). Solve by iteration or characteristic equations. (3) Polynomial‑exponential hybrid – terms are sums of polynomial and exponential components (Tₙ = an²+bn+c + d·rⁿ). Identify by checking if successive ratios approach a constant while residuals follow a polynomial trend. Practical shortcut: write 8‑10 terms, compute successive ratios and differences in parallel columns. If ratios stabilise, isolate the exponential part by dividing by the estimated base; the remainder should reveal a polynomial. For letter series, convert to positional values (A=1…Z=26) first. Hybrid direction‑series items (step lengths from a fused series, turns from a fixed pattern) are solved by generating series terms, then applying vector addition. Recognising component “signatures” is key: constant second differences → quadratic; constant ratio → geometric; constant first differences → arithmetic; alternating signs → alternating series.

  • Assuming a conditional asserts its antecedent, leading to affirming the consequent.
  • Choosing an analogy option that matches category but reverses relational direction.
  • Neglecting to propagate constraints after domain reduction, leaving hidden contradictions.
  • Ignoring marital edges in blood‑relation graphs, causing incorrect generational offsets.
  • Forcing a single pattern on an interleaved series and missing alternating sub‑series.
  • Watch for these traps to avoid losing marks.

Quantifier Logic and Venn Diagram Techniques

Quantifier logic extends categorical syllogisms by making the scope of each term explicit. The four standard forms — All S are P (A), No S are P (E), Some S are P (I), Some S are not P (O) — carry distinct existential import. In the modern Boolean interpretation, universal statements (A and E) do not assert the existence of their subject, whereas particular statements (I and O) do. This distinction determines whether a Venn diagram region must be shaded (empty) or marked with an × (non‑empty). When solving a multi‑premise problem, draw three overlapping circles for the three terms. Shade regions dictated by universal premises first; then place × marks for particular premises, choosing a region that is not already shaded. If a particular premise forces an × into a shaded region, the premise set is inconsistent. After all premises are rendered, read the conclusion by checking whether the required region is universally shaded (for a universal conclusion) or contains an × (for a particular conclusion). Conversion, obversion, and contraposition are valid immediate inferences only when they preserve truth‑value under the chosen interpretation; for example, the converse of an A statement (All P are S) is not valid unless the subject class is known to be non‑empty. Mastering these rules lets you decide validity in seconds without constructing full truth tables.

When more than three terms appear, use a series of three‑circle diagrams linked by shared terms, or adopt the Euler‑circle method where each premise adds a containment or exclusion relation. The key is to keep the diagram minimal: each new premise should either shade a previously unshaded region or place an × in a region that remains possible. If a premise adds no new information, it is redundant. Practice with mixed‑quantifier sets such as “All A are B; Some B are C; No C are D” to see how the × from the second premise propagates through the chain and may be eliminated by the third. This visual propagation mirrors the algebraic resolution of quantified formulas and is the fastest route in timed conditions.

Argument Structure: Premises, Assumptions, and Inference Evaluation

Every logical argument consists of a set of premises and a conclusion. The premises provide the evidential base; the conclusion is the claim the arguer wishes to establish. An assumption is an unstated premise that must be true for the inference to hold. Identifying assumptions is the core of “strengthen/weaken” questions: a statement that, if added, makes the conclusion more likely (strengthener) or less likely (weaker) is typically an assumption or a direct support/attack on the inference link. To evaluate an argument, first isolate the conclusion — often signaled by “therefore”, “thus”, “hence”, or a question stem asking “which of the following follows?”. Next, list the explicit premises. Then ask: what must also be true for the premises to guarantee the conclusion? That missing link is the assumption. Common assumption types include causal necessity (X causes Y, so X must be present), representativeness (the sample reflects the population), and sufficiency (the cited reason alone suffices for the outcome).

Inference evaluation uses two complementary tests: the “necessary condition” test (if the assumption is false, does the conclusion collapse?) and the “sufficient condition” test (if the assumption is true, does the conclusion follow?). A strong argument survives both; a weak argument fails at least one. Fallacies arise when the inference relies on illicit patterns — affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, equivocation, false dilemma, or hasty generalization. Recognizing the fallacy name is less important than spotting the structural flaw: does the argument treat a correlation as causation? Does it shift the burden of proof? Does it appeal to an unqualified authority? In exam items, the correct answer often restates the assumption in a neutral form, while distractors either repeat a premise, introduce irrelevant information, or present a contrary claim. Practicing with real‑world editorials and official sample papers trains the eye to locate the inference gap instantly.

Assumption Identification
Argument: “The city’s new traffic‑management system reduced average commute time by 12 % in the first month. Therefore, the system should be adopted nationwide.” Which of the following is an assumption on which the argument depends?
The conclusion extrapolates a local, short‑term result to a national, long‑term policy. The hidden assumption is that the conditions producing the 12 % reduction (traffic volume, road geometry, driver compliance, weather) are representative of the nation as a whole and will persist. Option A (“The system works equally well in all weather conditions”) directly addresses persistence of conditions. Option B (“Other cities have similar traffic volumes”) addresses representativeness. Option C (“The system’s cost is lower than current methods”) is irrelevant to the causal claim. Option D (“Drivers will obey the new signals”) is a necessary condition for the system to function, but the argument already assumes the system functioned in the trial. The most comprehensive assumption is B, because if other cities differ significantly in traffic volume, the result may not replicate. Hence B is the correct assumption.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for an assumption, first write the conclusion in your own words. Then ask “What must be true for this conclusion to be unavoidable?” Eliminate any choice that merely restates a premise or introduces a new claim not required for the inference. The correct assumption is usually the most general statement that bridges the gap without adding extra content.

Alphanumeric Coding and Pattern Decoding Strategies

Alphanumeric coding questions present a string of letters, numbers, or both, transformed by a consistent rule. The rule set is finite: positional shift (Caesar cipher), reverse alphabet mapping (A↔Z, B↔Y), prime/composite index substitution, alternating arithmetic progressions, and mixed‑base conversions. The first step is to write the alphabet positions (A=1 … Z=26) alongside the given code. Compute differences between successive code values; a constant difference indicates a simple shift. If differences alternate (e.g., +2, −3, +2, −3), the rule is alternating. When numbers appear, test whether they represent letter positions, ASCII codes, or arithmetic results (sum of adjacent letters, product of positions). Prime‑index coding replaces letters at prime positions (2,3,5,7,11,13,17,19,23) with their position numbers while leaving others unchanged. Composite‑index coding does the opposite. A frequent pattern is “skip‑one” where every second letter is advanced by a fixed amount while the others stay.

For decoding, reverse‑engineer by applying the inverse operation to the code. If the code is “C E G I” and the plain text is “A C E G”, the rule is +2 shift. If the code contains numbers like “1 3 5 7” mapping to “A C E G”, the rule is direct position mapping. Mixed series often interleave two independent sub‑series: odd positions follow a letter shift, even positions follow a numeric progression. Separate the series by parity, solve each, then recombine. When a question supplies a coded word and asks for the code of another word, apply the discovered rule to the new word letter‑by‑letter. Speed comes from recognizing the “signature” of each rule: constant difference → shift; constant ratio → multiplicative; alternating signs → alternating; presence of 0 or 27 → wrap‑around. Practice with a catalogue of 30‑40 rule types until identification becomes automatic.

Input‑Output Machine Logic and Stepwise Transformation

Input‑output machines rearrange a given sequence of words or numbers through a fixed number of steps, each step applying a single deterministic operation. Typical operations include: moving the first element to the end (rotation), swapping adjacent pairs, sorting a subset alphabetically or numerically, replacing each element by its successor/predecessor, or inserting a delimiter. The machine description usually shows the initial input and the final output after a known number of steps, sometimes with intermediate steps displayed. To crack the rule, compare successive steps and note which positions change. If only the first and last elements exchange, the operation is “swap ends”. If the whole list shifts right by one, it is a rotation. When multiple operations appear to occur simultaneously, the machine may apply them in a fixed order (e.g., first sort the first half, then reverse the second half). Write the indices of each element across steps; a consistent index mapping reveals the permutation.

Once the step rule is known, you can predict any future step or reconstruct a missing intermediate step. For “find the step number where a given arrangement appears”, simulate forward until the arrangement matches or a cycle repeats. The maximum cycle length for n distinct items under a single permutation is the order of that permutation, computable via least common multiple of cycle lengths. In exams, n ≤ 8, so manual simulation of up to 10 steps is feasible. A shortcut: if the final output is the reverse of the input, the machine likely performs a full reversal in one step or a series of adjacent swaps (bubble‑sort style). Count the number of inversions in the final output relative to the input; each adjacent swap reduces inversions by one, giving the minimum steps. When numbers are involved, arithmetic operations (add constant, multiply, digit sum) may accompany rearrangement. Treat the numeric transformation independently from the positional permutation. Mastery comes from solving 20‑30 varied machine puzzles and noting the recurring operation families.

  • Assuming a universal premise has existential import when the Boolean interpretation is intended.
  • Confusing a necessary assumption with a sufficient condition in strengthen/weaken items.
  • Applying a single shift rule to an interleaved alphanumeric series without separating parity.
  • Overlooking wrap‑around at Z→A or 26→1 in Caesar‑type codes.
  • Treating a multi‑step machine as a single permutation and missing intermediate re‑ordering.
  • Ignoring the possibility that a numeric transformation and a positional permutation operate on different cycles.
  • Failing to verify that a decoded word satisfies all given coded examples before selecting an answer.
