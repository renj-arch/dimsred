What is Numerical Ability?

Numerical Ability is part of the GATE General Aptitude section. It tests basic quantitative skills applicable to all engineering fields — number system, percentages, profit-loss, time-work, speed-distance, ratio-proportion, and data interpretation. GATE typically has 3-5 numerical ability questions.

Key Concepts

1. Number System: Divisibility rules (2 to 11), LCM & HCF (product = LCM × HCF for two numbers), unit digit cyclicity (cycle length 4 for 2,3,7,8; cycle length 2 for 4,9; constant for 0,1,5,6), remainder theorem, and BODMAS simplification.

2. Percentages: Quick fraction equivalents: 1/2=50%, 1/3≈33.33%, 1/4=25%, 1/5=20%, 1/6≈16.67%, 1/8=12.5%, 1/9≈11.11%, 1/10=10%, 1/11≈9.09%, 1/12≈8.33%. Successive percentage formula: net = x + y + xy/100. Application in profit-loss, discount, and population problems.

3. Time & Work: LCM method: take LCM of individual times as total work. Efficiency = total work/time. Combined efficiency = sum of individual efficiencies. Pipes & cisterns: fill positive, empty negative.

4. Speed, Distance & Time: D = S×T. Average speed = 2ab/(a+b) for equal distance. Relative speed: sum when opposite, difference when same direction. Conversion: 1 km/h = 5/18 m/s.

5. Data Interpretation: Tables, bar graphs, line graphs, pie charts. Read labels carefully, identify quantities, perform required arithmetic (percentage, ratio, difference).

Solved Examples

Example 1 (Percentage)
In a GATE exam, 75% of the candidates passed. If 1200 candidates failed, how many appeared?

a) 3600 b) 4800 c) 6000 d) 2400
Solution: b) 4800. 75% passed, so 25% failed. 25% = 1200. Total = 1200/0.25 = 4800.
Example 2 (Time & Work)
A pipe can fill a tank in 6 hours. Another pipe can empty it in 8 hours. If both are opened together, how long will it take to fill the tank?

a) 12 hours b) 24 hours c) 18 hours d) 14 hours
Solution: b) 24 hours. Fill pipe: 1/6 per hour. Empty pipe: -1/8 per hour. Net = 1/6 - 1/8 = 4/24 - 3/24 = 1/24 per hour. Time = 24 hours.
Example 3 (Data Interpretation — Pie Chart)
A pie chart shows the distribution of 3600 students across five engineering branches: Computer Science (90°), Electrical (72°), Mechanical (108°), Civil (54°), and Others. How many students are in Mechanical?

a) 900 b) 1080 c) 720 d) 1200
Solution: b) 1080. Angle for Mechanical = 108°. Total angle = 360°. Fraction = 108/360 = 3/10. Students in Mechanical = (3/10)×3600 = 1080.

Shortcut Techniques

  • For profit-loss with successive discounts, single equivalent discount = x + y - xy/100 — much faster than calculating in two steps.
  • In speed problems where distance is same both ways, use 2ab/(a+b) instead of calculating total distance and time separately.
  • For number system remainder problems, use pattern recognition — most follow a repeating cycle of length 2, 3, or 4.
  • In DI, approximate when possible — if options are far apart, rough calculations are sufficient.
  • Use fraction equivalents for percentages — this eliminates decimal multiplication.
Pro Tip
GATE numerical ability questions are at high school level but require speed. Practice mental arithmetic — squares up to 30, cubes up to 15, and common percentage-fraction conversions. In the exam, attempt these questions first as they are quick to solve if you know the concepts.

Advanced Applications

Modern exams feature questions combining multiple quantitative concepts. Master these by practicing previous year papers.

Shortcut Methods

Percentages: Use fraction table (12.5%=1/8, 20%=1/5, 25%=1/4). SI/CI: For 2 years, CI-SI = P(r/100)^2. Time Work: Use LCM of time periods. Average Speed: 2ab/(a+b) for equal distances.

Number System: Divisibility Rules and Remainder Cycles

Core divisibility tests

The standard tests for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11 are well known. For 7, remove the last digit, double it, subtract from the truncated number; repeat until a small number appears. If the result is a multiple of 7, the original is divisible by 7. For 13, multiply the last digit by 4 and add to the truncated number; repeat. For 17, multiply the last digit by 5 and subtract. These rules avoid long division and are useful when the divisor is a prime not covered by the basic set.

Remainder cycles for powers

When a question asks for the remainder of a large exponent modulo a small integer, the sequence of remainders repeats with a period that divides φ(n) (Euler’s totient) for coprime bases. For example, powers of 2 modulo 7 cycle as 2, 4, 1 (period 3). Powers of 3 modulo 8 cycle as 3, 1 (period 2). Recognising the cycle length lets you reduce the exponent modulo the period. If the base and modulus are not coprime, factor the modulus and apply the Chinese Remainder Theorem or handle the non‑coprime part separately.

Application to last‑digit and last‑two‑digit problems

The last digit of aⁿ is the remainder modulo 10. Because φ(10)=4, any base coprime to 10 repeats every 4. For bases ending in 0,1,5,6 the last digit is constant. For the last two digits, work modulo 100; φ(100)=40, so the period divides 40. In practice, cycles for common bases (2, 3, 7, 8) are short (20 or less) and can be memorised. When the exponent is huge, reduce it modulo the cycle length, taking care when the reduced exponent becomes 0 (use the full cycle length).

Divisibility by composite numbers

A number is divisible by a composite m if it is divisible by each prime‑power factor of m. For 12 = 3 × 4, test divisibility by 3 and 4 independently. For 18 = 2 × 9, test 2 and 9. This factor‑wise approach is faster than applying a single obscure rule for the composite.

Percentages: Successive Changes and Base Adjustments

Successive percentage formula

When a quantity undergoes a change of x % followed by y %, the net percentage change is x + y + xy/100. The sign convention is positive for increase, negative for decrease. This formula works regardless of the order of operations because multiplication of factors (1 + x/100)(1 + y/100) is commutative. For three successive changes x, y, z the net factor is (1 + x/100)(1 + y/100)(1 + z/100) – 1, which expands to x + y + z + (xy + yz + zx)/100 + xyz/10000.

Base shift in profit‑loss and discount

In profit‑loss, the cost price (CP) is the base for profit % and the selling price (SP) is the base for discount %. A common error is to apply a discount percentage on the marked price (MP) and then compute profit on the same MP. The correct chain is: MP → (discount) → SP → (profit/loss) → CP. If a trader marks up by a % and then gives a discount of b %, the effective profit % = a – b – ab/100. This is a direct application of the successive change formula with a positive a and negative b.

Percentage of a percentage

When a problem states “30 % of the students are girls and 20 % of the girls play cricket”, the fraction of total students who are girls playing cricket is 0.30 × 0.20 = 0.06 = 6 %. Always convert each percentage to a decimal multiplier and multiply; the result is the overall percentage relative to the original whole.

Reversing a percentage change

If a value after a p % increase equals V, the original value = V / (1 + p/100). If after a p % decrease equals V, original = V / (1 – p/100). This reversal is essential for “original price before discount” questions. For successive changes, reverse in the opposite order: divide by the last factor first.

Successive Percentage Change
A product’s price is increased by 20 % and then decreased by 20 %. What is the net percentage change relative to the original price?
Let the original price be 100. After a 20 % increase: 100 × 1.20 = 120. After a 20 % decrease: 120 × 0.80 = 96. Net change = (96 – 100) / 100 × 100 = –4 %. Using the formula: net = 20 + (–20) + (20 × –20)/100 = 0 – 4 = –4 %. The price falls by 4 %.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the successive change formula (x + y + xy/100) and apply it directly instead of computing intermediate values. For three or more changes, multiply the decimal factors (1 + x/100) … and subtract 1; this avoids sign errors and is faster than stepwise arithmetic.

Time‑Work: Variable Efficiency and Partial Completion

LCM method with varying efficiencies

The classic LCM method assumes constant efficiency. When workers have different efficiencies that change after a certain period (e.g., a worker becomes twice as fast after 3 days), split the timeline into phases. Compute the work done in each phase using the efficiencies applicable to that phase, then sum. The total work can still be taken as the LCM of the individual times for the first phase, but the second phase requires a new LCM based on the updated rates.

Man‑days and woman‑days equivalence

If 5 men complete a job in 12 days, the work = 5 × 12 = 60 man‑days. If 3 women do the same job in 20 days, the work = 3 × 20 = 60 woman‑days. Equate the two to find the relative efficiency: 1 man‑day = 1 woman‑day. When men and women work together, convert all to a common unit (e.g., man‑days) before adding.

Partial work and remaining fraction

When a group works for a few days and then some members leave, compute the fraction of work completed: (combined efficiency × days worked) / total work. The remaining fraction = 1 – completed fraction. The time to finish the rest = remaining work / new combined efficiency. This two‑step approach avoids solving a single equation with unknown total time.

Pipes with alternating operation

For pipes that open and close in a repeating pattern (e.g., Pipe A opens for 2 hours, then Pipe B opens for 1 hour), determine the net work done in one full cycle. Divide the total work by the work per cycle to get the number of full cycles, then handle the residual part with the appropriate pipe’s rate. This cyclic method is faster than simulating hour by hour.

Speed‑Distance‑Time: Relative Motion with Non‑Uniform Segments

Average speed for unequal distances

When a journey consists of two legs of distances d₁ and d₂ covered at speeds s₁ and s₂, the average speed = (d₁ + d₂) / (d₁/s₁ + d₂/s₂). The harmonic mean 2s₁s₂/(s₁+s₂) applies only when d₁ = d₂. For three or more legs, extend the formula: total distance divided by total time.

Relative speed with direction changes

If two objects move on a straight line, their relative speed is the sum of speeds when moving towards each other and the absolute difference when moving in the same direction. When one object reverses direction mid‑journey, split the motion at the reversal point. Compute the meeting time for each segment using the appropriate relative speed, then add the times.

Circular track meetings

On a circular track of length L, two runners starting together with speeds a and b (a > b) meet every L/(a – b) time units when running in the same direction, and every L/(a + b) when running opposite. For three or more runners, pairwise meeting times can be computed; the time when all meet simultaneously is the LCM of the pairwise meeting times, provided the ratios are rational.

Variable speed within a single leg

If a vehicle travels the first half of the time at speed u and the second half at speed v, the average speed = (u + v)/2 (arithmetic mean). If it travels the first half of the distance at u and the second half at v, the average speed = 2uv/(u+v) (harmonic mean). Distinguishing “half the time” from “half the distance” is a frequent trap.

Data Interpretation: Multi‑Source Synthesis and Approximation Techniques

Combining tables, bar graphs, and pie charts

Modern DI sets often present a table of absolute values, a bar graph of year‑wise growth, and a pie chart of sectoral share. The first step is to identify the common dimension (e.g., year, sector). Convert each representation to a uniform tabular form: extract numbers from the pie chart using the total given, read bar heights against the axis scale, and copy table entries. Once all data reside in a single mental table, cross‑referencing becomes straightforward.

Approximation when options are widely spaced

If answer choices differ by more than 5 %, exact calculation is unnecessary. Round percentages to the nearest 5 % or 10 %, round large numbers to the nearest thousand, and use fraction equivalents (e.g., 33 % ≈ 1/3, 12.5 % = 1/8). Perform the arithmetic on the rounded figures; the result will fall closest to the correct option. This technique saves 30–40 seconds per question.

Ratio and percentage change from visual data

For a bar graph showing values for two years, the percentage increase = (height₂ – height₁) / height₁ × 100. Because the vertical axis is linear, the ratio of heights equals the ratio of values. Measure the heights in centimeters (or count grid lines) to avoid reading the axis scale precisely. For a pie chart, the angle ratio equals the value ratio; compute the required percentage as (angle/360) × 100.

Handling missing or implicit data

Sometimes a DI set omits a total but provides enough sub‑totals to reconstruct it. Sum the given parts; if a part is missing, use the relationship “part = total × percentage” from another chart to solve for the total. Always check consistency: the sum of all sector percentages must be 100 % (or 360°). Any discrepancy signals a mis‑read value.

  • Assuming the harmonic mean applies for average speed whenever two speeds are given, even when distances differ.
  • Applying a discount percentage on the cost price instead of the marked price in profit‑loss chains.
  • Using the LCM of times as total work when efficiencies change mid‑process without splitting the timeline.
  • Treating “half the time” and “half the distance” as interchangeable in average‑speed calculations.
  • Reading a pie‑chart angle directly as a percentage without dividing by 360°.
  • Ignoring the need to reverse percentage changes in the opposite order when finding an original value.

Number System: Chinese Remainder Theorem and Modular Arithmetic

The Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) gives a constructive way to solve a system of simultaneous congruences when the moduli are pairwise coprime. If we need an integer x such that

x ≡ a₁ (mod m₁), x ≡ a₂ (mod m₂), …, x ≡ aₖ (mod mₖ) and gcd(mᵢ, mⱼ) = 1 for all i ≠ j, then a unique solution exists modulo M = m₁m₂…mₖ. The standard construction computes Mᵢ = M/mᵢ and the modular inverse yᵢ of Mᵢ modulo mᵢ (i.e. Mᵢyᵢ ≡ 1 (mod mᵢ)). The solution is x = Σ aᵢMᵢyᵢ (mod M).

When moduli are not coprime, a solution exists only if every pair of congruences is compatible: aᵢ ≡ aⱼ (mod gcd(mᵢ, mⱼ)). In that case the system can be reduced by merging congruences using the extended Euclidean algorithm, eventually yielding a single congruence modulo lcm of the original moduli.

Typical exam twists include: (1) finding the smallest positive integer satisfying three or four remainders, (2) determining the remainder of a large power when the exponent itself is given by a congruence, and (3) using CRT to compute the last two digits of a number by solving modulo 4 and modulo 25 separately and then recombining. Because 100 = 4 × 25 and gcd(4,25)=1, the last two digits of N are uniquely determined by N mod 4 and N mod 25.

Modular arithmetic shortcuts: for any integer a, a⁽ᵠ⁽ᵐ⁾⁾ ≡ 1 (mod m) when gcd(a,m)=1 (Euler’s theorem). When m is prime, this reduces to Fermat’s little theorem a⁽ᵐ⁻¹⁾ ≡ 1 (mod m). These theorems let you shrink huge exponents before applying CRT.

Percentages: Compound Growth, Decay, and Variable Rate Applications

Compound percentage change occurs when a quantity is subjected to successive percentage variations, each applied on the new base. If a value P grows by r₁%, then by r₂%, the final amount is P(1+r₁/100)(1+r₂/100). The overall percentage change is not the arithmetic sum r₁+r₂ but the product minus one: [(1+r₁/100)(1+r₂/100)−1]×100. This principle extends to any number of periods.

When rates vary each period (e.g., inflation 5% year‑1, 3% year‑2, 6% year‑3), the cumulative factor is the product of the individual factors. The equivalent constant annual rate r satisfies (1+r/100)ⁿ = Π(1+rᵢ/100). Solving for r gives the geometric mean of the growth factors minus one. In decay problems (depreciation, population decline) the same formula applies with (1−r/100) factors.

A frequent examiner twist mixes growth and decay in the same timeline: a salary rises 10% then a tax of 5% is levied on the increased amount. The net factor is (1+0.10)(1−0.05) = 1.045, i.e. a 4.5% net increase. Reversing the order (tax first, then raise) yields (1−0.05)(1+0.10) = 1.045 as well — multiplication is commutative — but if the tax is a fixed amount rather than a percentage, order matters.

Variable‑rate compound interest with periodic contributions (annuities) appears in advanced sets. The future value of a series of equal payments C made at the end of each period at rate r% is C[(1+r/100)ⁿ−1]/(r/100). If payments are made at the beginning of each period, multiply the result by (1+r/100). When the rate changes mid‑stream, split the timeline at the change point, compute the accumulated value up to that point, then treat it as the principal for the remaining periods at the new rate.

Technique: CRT for simultaneous congruences
Find the smallest positive integer that leaves remainder 2 when divided by 3, remainder 3 when divided by 5, and remainder 2 when divided by 7.
We solve x ≡ 2 (mod 3), x ≡ 3 (mod 5), x ≡ 2 (mod 7). Moduli 3,5,7 are pairwise coprime, M = 105. Compute M₁=35, M₂=21, M₃=15. Find inverses: 35·y₁ ≡ 1 (mod 3) → 35≡2, 2·2=4≡1 ⇒ y₁=2. 21·y₂ ≡ 1 (mod 5) → 21≡1 ⇒ y₂=1. 15·y₃ ≡ 1 (mod 7) → 15≡1 ⇒ y₃=1. x = (2·35·2)+(3·21·1)+(2·15·1) = 140+63+30 = 233. Reduce modulo 105: 233−2·105 = 23. Check: 23÷3=7 r2, 23÷5=4 r3, 23÷7=3 r2. Answer 23.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a CRT problem appears, first verify coprimality; if not, merge congruences using the extended Euclidean algorithm before applying the standard formula. For compound‑percentage questions, write the product of factors explicitly — this avoids the common error of adding percentages. Keep a mental table of small modular inverses (e.g., inverse of 2 mod 3 is 2, of 3 mod 5 is 2) to save time.

Time‑Work: Dynamic Teams, Efficiency Decay, and Break‑Even Analysis

Real‑world work problems often involve workers joining or leaving mid‑project, or efficiency that changes with fatigue. Model the total work as a constant W (e.g., LCM of individual times). If a worker’s efficiency drops by a fixed percentage after a certain number of days, split the timeline: compute work done at the initial rate, then at the reduced rate. For a team where a new member joins after t days, the work done in the first phase is (sum of initial efficiencies)×t; the remaining work is finished by the enlarged team at the new combined rate.

Break‑even analysis appears when two alternative methods (e.g., hiring extra workers vs. overtime) are compared. Suppose method A uses n workers at rate e each, method B uses n+Δn workers at the same rate but incurs a fixed setup cost C (expressed in man‑days). Equate total man‑days: n·e·T = (n+Δn)·e·T + C. Solve for the project duration T at which both methods cost the same. If the actual duration exceeds this break‑even point, the method with lower marginal cost becomes preferable.

Efficiency decay can be linear (e.g., each day a worker loses 2% of initial output) or stepwise (e.g., after 5 days efficiency halves). For linear decay, the work done over d days is the sum of an arithmetic series: W = e₀·d − (decay per day)·d(d−1)/2. For stepwise decay, treat each constant‑efficiency block separately and add the contributions.

When pipes fill and empty a tank with alternating schedules, the net work per cycle is the algebraic sum of filling and emptying rates multiplied by their respective open times. If the tank has a leak that starts only after a certain water level, introduce a conditional rate change: compute the time to reach that level with the initial net rate, then switch to the new net rate for the remainder.

Speed‑Distance‑Time: Escalators, Moving Walkways, and Relative Motion on Closed Loops

Escalator problems treat the escalator as a moving platform of length L with speed vₑ. A person walking on it with speed vₚ relative to the ground has effective speed vₚ+vₑ when moving in the same direction and |vₚ−vₑ| when moving opposite. The time to traverse the escalator is L/(vₚ+vₑ) or L/|vₚ−vₑ|. If the person stands still, the time is L/vₑ. A classic twist gives the time taken when walking and when standing, asking for the length or the escalator speed. Set up two equations: L = (vₚ+vₑ)t₁ and L = vₑt₂; solve for the unknowns.

Moving walkways (travelators) are analogous but often involve two persons starting from opposite ends. Their relative speed is the sum of their ground speeds plus twice the walkway speed if both walk with the walkway, or the sum of ground speeds if they walk opposite to it. The meeting point divides the walkway in the ratio of their effective speeds.

On a closed loop (circular track) with more than two runners, pairwise meeting times are L/|vᵢ−vⱼ| (same direction) or L/(vᵢ+vⱼ) (opposite). The time when all runners meet simultaneously is the LCM of all pairwise meeting times, provided the speed ratios are rational. If one runner reverses direction at a known instant, treat the motion before and after reversal as separate phases; the meeting condition changes from same‑direction to opposite‑direction formula at the reversal moment.

Variable speed within a single leg (already covered) combines with escalator logic when a person changes walking speed mid‑escalator. Split the escalator length at the point of speed change, compute time for each segment with the appropriate effective speed, and sum.

Data Interpretation: Caselet Logic, Implicit Constraints, and Multi‑Step Synthesis

Caselets present a narrative with embedded numerical relationships rather than ready‑made charts. The first step is to translate every sentence into algebraic statements or a table. Identify the primary entities (people, products, years) and the variables (quantities, percentages, ratios). Implicit constraints such as “total employees = 500” or “profit margin cannot exceed 30%” often restrict the feasible region and eliminate impossible answer choices.

Multi‑step synthesis requires chaining calculations: e.g., a caselet gives year‑wise revenue growth percentages and a pie chart of expense heads for the base year. To find the absolute marketing expense in year 3, first reconstruct base‑year revenue from the pie chart (marketing angle → percentage → absolute value using total expense = revenue × (1−profit margin)). Then apply the successive growth factors to reach year‑3 revenue, and finally apply the year‑3 marketing percentage (which may be given in a separate bar graph).

Approximation is powerful when the caselet numbers are large and options are spaced. Round percentages to the nearest 5% and revenues to the nearest crore (or thousand). Because each step multiplies by a factor close to 1, relative errors stay small. Always verify that the rounded answer does not cross a decision boundary (e.g., an option at 12.4% vs 12.6%).

Logical deduction questions ask “Which of the following must be true?” after presenting partial data. Use the method of extremes: assign the minimum and maximum permissible values to the free variables consistent with all constraints, and check whether the statement holds in both extremes. If it holds in both, it is a necessary conclusion; if it fails in either, it is not guaranteed.

  • Assuming CRT applies when moduli share a factor without checking compatibility.
  • Adding successive percentage changes instead of multiplying their factors.
  • Treating efficiency decay as a simple average rather than summing the arithmetic series.
  • Using the standing‑still escalator time as the walking time when the person walks opposite to the escalator.
  • Ignoring implicit totals in caselets and solving with incomplete equations.
  • Taking LCM of meeting times on a circular track when speed ratios are irrational.
  • Reversing the order of percentage changes when the base for each change is different.

Number System: Euler’s Totient and Carmichael Function for Modular Exponentiation

When the exponent in a modular power an (mod m) is large, direct computation is impossible. Euler’s theorem states that if gcd(a,m)=1 then aφ(m) ≡ 1 (mod m), where φ(m) is Euler’s totient function counting integers ≤ m that are coprime to m. This reduces the exponent modulo φ(m): an ≡ an mod φ(m) (mod m). The theorem fails when a and m share a factor; in that case one can factor m = m₁·m₂ with gcd(m₁,m₂)=1, apply the theorem to the coprime part, and combine results with the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

The Carmichael function λ(m) gives the smallest positive integer such that aλ(m) ≡ 1 (mod m) for every a coprime to m. For prime powers pk (p odd) λ(pk) = φ(pk) = pk‑1(p‑1); for 2k with k≥3, λ(2k) = 2k‑2. For a general m, λ(m) = lcm(λ(p₁k₁), … , λ(pᵣkᵣ)). Because λ(m) divides φ(m), using λ(m) often yields a smaller exponent reduction, which is valuable when the modulus is composite and the exponent is huge.

Typical exam twists: (i) a and m are not coprime – factor out the common divisor d = gcd(a,m), write a = d·a′, m = d·m′, compute a′n (mod m′) with Euler/Carmichael, then multiply back by dn (mod m). (ii) The modulus is a product of two large primes – compute φ(m) = (p‑1)(q‑1) and λ(m) = lcm(p‑1,q‑1). (iii) The exponent itself is given as a tower (e.g., 234) – reduce the top exponent modulo λ(m) recursively, taking care of the case when the reduced exponent becomes 0 (replace by λ(m)).

Practice tip: pre‑compute φ and λ for all integers up to 1000; many questions use moduli like 100, 125, 1000, 999, 1001 where these values are standard.

Percentages: Layered Discounts, Markups, and Tax Computations

Real‑world pricing often stacks several percentage operations: a markup, then a discount, then a tax, each applied on a different base. The order matters because each step changes the base for the next. If a product costs C, a markup of p% makes the marked price M = C·(1 + p/100). A subsequent discount of d% on M gives selling price S = M·(1 – d/100). Finally a tax of t% on S yields final price F = S·(1 + t/100). The overall factor is (1 + p/100)(1 – d/100)(1 + t/100). Reversing the order (discount then markup) changes the factor to (1 – d/100)(1 + p/100), which is not equal unless p = d = 0.

When multiple discounts are advertised as “successive discounts of a% and b%”, the net discount is not a+b but a + b – ab/100. This follows from multiplying the factors (1 – a/100)(1 – b/100) = 1 – (a+b)/100 + ab/10000. The same formula works for successive markups. If a tax is levied on the discounted price, the tax base is already reduced, so the effective tax rate on the original marked price is t·(1 – d/100).

Examiner traps: (i) giving the final price and asking for the original cost – solve by dividing by the overall factor. (ii) mixing profit‑loss language: “a shopkeeper marks up by 20% and then gives a 10% discount; find profit%”. Profit% = (final selling price – cost)/cost × 100. (iii) “tax inclusive” vs “tax exclusive” wording – inclusive means the quoted price already contains tax; exclusive means tax is added on top. Always identify the base for each percentage before multiplying.

Advanced variation: a product passes through three dealers, each applying a markup and a discount. The overall factor is the product of six terms. Because the number of terms grows, approximation by rounding each percentage to the nearest 5% is acceptable when answer options are spaced by >2%.

Layered Percentage Calculation
A retailer buys an article for ₹800. He marks it up by 25%, offers a discount of 10% on the marked price, and then charges a GST of 18% on the discounted price. What is the final price paid by the customer?
Step 1: Marked price = 800 × (1 + 25/100) = 800 × 1.25 = ₹1000.
Step 2: Discounted price = 1000 × (1 – 10/100) = 1000 × 0.90 = ₹900.
Step 3: Final price with GST = 900 × (1 + 18/100) = 900 × 1.18 = ₹1062.
Answer: ₹1062.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Write the overall factor as a product of (1 ± p/100) terms before any arithmetic. Cancel common factors if the same percentage appears as markup and discount. When options are far apart, round each percentage to the nearest 5% and multiply; the relative error stays below 1% for three‑step chains.

Time‑Work: Variable Efficiency with Breaks and Partial Work

Standard LCM method assumes constant efficiency. In many problems a worker’s efficiency changes after a certain amount of work or after a fixed time, or the worker takes periodic breaks. Model each phase separately: compute work done in phase 1 with efficiency e₁ for time t₁ (or until work W₁ is finished), then switch to efficiency e₂ for the remaining work. If breaks are regular (e.g., 10 minutes rest every hour), convert the schedule into an effective efficiency: in 60 minutes the worker actually works 50 minutes, so effective rate = (50/60) × nominal rate.

When a team’s composition changes mid‑task (workers join or leave), treat the interval before the change as one sub‑problem and the interval after as another. The total work is the sum of work done in each interval. If the change occurs when a fraction f of the total work is complete, set up the equation: (combined rate before) × t₁ = f·W and (combined rate after) × t₂ = (1‑f)·W. Solve for the unknown time or fraction.

Partial work with “half the work done by A, then B finishes the rest” is a special case: let total work = LCM of individual times. If A’s time alone = a days, B’s = b days, then A does half the work in (a/2) days. Remaining half is done by B in (b/2) days. Total = (a+b)/2 days. This shortcut works only when the split is exactly half; for other fractions use the fraction × individual time.

Examiner twist: “A and B together can finish a job in 12 days. After working together for 4 days, A leaves and B finishes the remaining work in 18 days. Find B’s alone time.” Let total work = 1. Combined rate = 1/12. Work done in 4 days = 4/12 = 1/3. Remaining = 2/3 done by B in 18 days → B’s rate = (2/3)/18 = 1/27 → B alone = 27 days.

Speed‑Distance‑Time: River Navigation with Changing Current Speed

Boat‑and‑stream problems usually assume a constant stream speed vₛ. When the current varies along the river (e.g., first 30 km at 2 km/h, next 20 km at 4 km/h), the upstream and downstream speeds change segment‑wise. Let boat speed in still water = v_b. For each segment i with length Lᵢ and stream speed vₛᵢ, upstream time = Lᵢ / (v_b – vₛᵢ), downstream time = Lᵢ / (v_b + vₛᵢ). Total time is the sum over segments. If the question gives total upstream and downstream times, set up two equations in v_b and the unknown segment speeds (or lengths) and solve.

A common variant: the stream speed increases linearly with distance, vₛ(x) = kx. Then upstream speed = v_b – kx, and time to travel dx upstream = dx / (v_b – kx). Integrate from 0 to L: t = (1/k) ln(v_b / (v_b – kL)). This logarithmic formula appears only in the most advanced papers; recognizing the integral saves time.

When a boat crosses a river perpendicular to the banks, the resultant velocity is the vector sum of boat velocity relative to water (v_b perpendicular) and stream velocity (vₛ parallel). The crossing time = width / v_b (independent of stream). The downstream drift = vₛ × crossing time. If the boat aims at an angle θ upstream to counteract drift, the perpendicular component is v_b cosθ, so crossing time = width / (v_b cosθ). The condition for zero drift is v_b sinθ = vₛ.

Examiner trap: giving the round‑trip time for a boat that goes upstream a certain distance and returns, but the stream speed changes on the return leg. Do not average the stream speeds; compute each leg separately with its own vₛ.

Data Interpretation: Index Numbers and Multi‑Year Growth Rate Synthesis

Index numbers (e.g., CPI, IIP, stock indices) express a variable relative to a base year (value 100). If the index for year 1 is I₁ and for year 2 is I₂, the growth rate from year 1 to year 2 is (I₂ – I₁)/I₁ × 100%. When a table provides indices for several years and a separate bar chart gives absolute values for the base year, reconstruct absolute values for other years by multiplying the base‑year absolute by Iᵢ/100.

Multi‑year synthesis often asks: “Given the index of industrial production for 2018‑2022 and the sectoral weightages for 2018, find the weighted index for 2022.” Compute each sector’s 2022 index = 2018 index × (sector growth factor). Then weighted index = Σ (weightᵢ × sector indexᵢ) / Σ weightᵢ. Because weights sum to 100, the denominator is 100.

Approximation: when indices are large (e.g., 112.3, 118.7) and options differ by ≥2 index points, round each index to the nearest integer before calculating growth rates. The error introduced is ≤0.5 index points per year, which rarely changes the answer.

Implicit constraints: “The total production cannot exceed the installed capacity.” If the computed absolute production for a year exceeds the capacity given in a footnote, the data set is inconsistent – the question may be a “which of the following cannot be true” type. Use the capacity as an upper bound to eliminate impossible options.

Advanced twist: a caselet gives year‑wise revenue growth percentages and a pie chart of expense heads for the base year. To find the absolute marketing expense in year 3, first reconstruct base‑year revenue from the pie chart (marketing angle → percentage → absolute value using total expense = revenue × (1 – profit margin)). Then apply successive growth factors to reach year‑3 revenue, and finally apply the year‑3 marketing percentage (given in a separate bar graph). Chain the calculations without rounding until the final step.

  • Assuming Euler’s theorem applies when gcd(a,m) ≠ 1 without factoring out the common divisor.
  • Adding successive percentage changes instead of multiplying their factors.
  • Treating a break‑adjusted efficiency as a simple average of work and rest periods.
  • Using a single average stream speed for a river with segment‑wise varying current.
  • Ignoring the base‑year weight sum when computing a weighted index.
  • Review these traps before the exam.

Number System: Wilson’s Theorem, Factorial Modulo Prime Powers, and Applications

Wilson’s theorem states that for a prime p, (p‑1)! ≡ –1 (mod p). This simple congruence becomes a powerful tool when the problem asks for the remainder of a factorial divided by a prime or a prime power. For a prime power p^k (k ≥ 2) the factorial modulo p^k does not follow Wilson directly; instead one uses the concept of p‑adic valuation and the lifting the exponent lemma (LTE) to separate the factors of p from the coprime part. The coprime part can be evaluated by grouping numbers into pairs whose product is ≡ 1 (mod p^k), leaving only the unpaired elements (typically 1 and –1) and the multiples of p. The exponent of p in n! is given by Legendre’s formula v_p(n!) = Σi≥1 ⌊n/p^i⌋. Once the exponent is known, the factorial can be written as pv_p(n!) × R where R is coprime to p. The remainder of R modulo p^k is then found by multiplying the reduced residues. A classic application: find the last three non‑zero digits of 100!. Compute v2(100!) and v5(100!), remove the common factor 10v5, and evaluate the remaining product modulo 1000 using the Chinese remainder theorem on mod 8 and mod 125. The method extends to problems such as “find the remainder when 50! is divided by 532” where 53 is prime; here Wilson gives 52! ≡ –1 (mod 53) and LTE helps lift to 532. Mastery of these steps turns seemingly intractable factorial remainders into systematic calculations.

Another frequent twist is the inverse factorial problem: given a remainder r modulo a prime p, find the smallest n such that n! ≡ r (mod p). Because the factorial function modulo a prime is not injective, one usually pre‑computes factorials up to p‑1 and uses the fact that (p‑1)! ≡ –1 to reduce larger n. When the modulus is composite but square‑free, the Chinese remainder theorem splits the problem into prime moduli, each solved by Wilson or direct multiplication. For prime powers, Hensel’s lemma can lift a solution from modulo p to p^k provided the derivative condition holds. Examiners often combine these ideas: “Find the remainder of (20!)2 modulo 23”. Since 23 is prime, Wilson gives 22! ≡ –1. Noting 22! = 22 × 21 × 20! and reducing 22 ≡ –1, 21 ≡ –2 gives 20! ≡ (–1)/( (–1)(–2) ) ≡ 1/2 ≡ 12 (mod 23). Then square to get 144 ≡ 6 (mod 23). Recognising the pattern of pairing inverses saves enormous time.

Advanced problems may ask for the exponent of a prime in a binomial coefficient C(n, k). Using Legendre’s formula on numerator and denominator yields v_p(C(n,k)) = v_p(n!) – v_p(k!) – v_p((n‑k)!). This is the basis of Kummer’s theorem: the exponent equals the number of carries when adding k and n‑k in base p. Such results appear in questions about divisibility of combinatorial numbers and in constructing numbers with prescribed prime‑factor patterns. A thorough grasp of Wilson, Legendre, LTE, and Kummer equips a candidate to solve any factorial‑modulo problem that appears in the highest difficulty tier.

Percentages: Multi‑Stage Profit‑Loss with Tax, Discount, and Break‑Even Analysis

Real‑world profit‑loss questions often stack several percentage layers: a manufacturer marks up cost by m%, offers a trade discount of d%, the retailer adds a markup of r%, and finally a sales tax of t% is levied on the selling price. The overall multiplier from cost to final price paid by the consumer is (1 + m/100) × (1 – d/100) × (1 + r/100) × (1 + t/100). When the problem asks for the effective profit percentage of the manufacturer, only the first two factors matter; the retailer’s markup and tax are external. A common examiner twist is to give the final consumer price and ask for the original cost, requiring the candidate to invert the chain of multipliers. Because multiplication is commutative, the order of applying discount and tax does not affect the final amount, but the base on which each percentage is calculated does. For instance, a discount on the marked price followed by tax on the discounted price yields a different result from tax on the marked price followed by discount on the taxed amount. The correct interpretation follows the wording: “discount on marked price, then tax on the selling price” means the tax base is the discounted price.

Break‑even analysis adds a fixed‑cost component. Suppose a firm has fixed cost F, variable cost per unit v, and sells each unit at price p after all discounts and taxes. The break‑even quantity Q satisfies Q × (p – v) = F. If the selling price itself is a function of quantity (e.g., volume discount), the equation becomes non‑linear and may require solving a quadratic. Examiners frequently embed a “profit target” condition: “How many units must be sold to earn a profit of P?” leading to Q = (F + P) / (p – v). When multiple products share fixed costs, the weighted contribution margin Σ (mix_i × (p_i – v_i)) replaces the single‑product margin. A typical data‑interpretation set provides a table of products with their variable costs, selling prices, and sales mix percentages; the candidate must compute the overall break‑even revenue. The key is to convert every percentage to a decimal multiplier, keep the fixed cost separate, and avoid the trap of adding percentages instead of multiplying the corresponding factors.

Layered discounts with a “cash‑back” or “loyalty points” clause introduce a second‑order percentage. For example, “10 % discount on the marked price, then 5 % cash‑back on the discounted price” means the effective price paid is 0.9 × 0.95 = 0.855 of the marked price, i.e. a 14.5 % total reduction, not 15 %. If the cash‑back is given as a voucher redeemable on a future purchase, the immediate outlay is only the 10 % discount, but the effective cost of the two‑purchase cycle must account for the voucher value. Such problems test whether the candidate distinguishes between immediate outlay and effective cost over the cycle. The safe approach is to draw a cash‑flow timeline, assign each percentage to its correct base, and multiply the successive factors. When the final answer options are widely spaced, rounding each intermediate factor to two decimal places introduces negligible error and speeds up calculation.

Technique: Inverting a Multi‑Stage Percentage Chain
A manufacturer sells an article to a wholesaler at a profit of 20 %. The wholesaler marks it up by 30 % and then offers a 10 % discount to a retailer. The retailer sells it to a customer at a 15 % profit after adding a 5 % sales tax on the selling price. If the customer pays ₹ 2 ,310, find the manufacturer’s cost price.
Let the manufacturer’s cost be C. Manufacturer’s selling price = C × 1.20. Wholesaler’s marked price = C × 1.20 × 1.30. Wholesaler’s selling price to retailer = C × 1.20 × 1.30 × 0.90. Retailer’s selling price before tax = C × 1.20 × 1.30 × 0.90 × 1.15. Customer pays = previous × 1.05 = C × 1.20 × 1.30 × 0.90 × 1.15 × 1.05 = 2310. Compute the product of multipliers: 1.20 × 1.30 = 1.56; 1.56 × 0.90 = 1.404; 1.404 × 1.15 = 1.6146; 1.6146 × 1.05 = 1.69533. Hence C = 2310 / 1.69533 ≈ 1362.5. Since cost price must be a whole rupee amount in such problems, the exact fraction is 2310 × 100000 / 169533 = 1362.5, indicating the data were chosen to give a clean answer of ₹ 1 ,362.50. In an exam, the options would be rounded; the correct choice is the one closest to this value.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a percentage chain exceeds three stages, write the multipliers in a vertical column, multiply them stepwise, and keep the running product to four decimal places. If the final division yields a non‑integer, check whether the question expects a rounded answer or an exact fraction; the phrasing “approximately” or “nearest rupee” signals rounding. Always verify that the base of each percentage matches the narrative (marked price, discounted price, cost price, selling price). A quick sanity check: the overall multiplier must be >1 if the final payer pays more than the original cost, and <1 if the final payer pays less.

Time‑Work: Contractual Penalties, Incentive Structures, and Optimal Manpower Allocation

Beyond the standard “men‑days” model, many competitive‑exam problems introduce contractual clauses that alter the effective work rate. A typical clause: “If the work is not completed in D days, a penalty of P per day of delay is levied.” This transforms the problem into a cost‑minimisation exercise. Suppose a contractor can hire workers at a daily wage w and each worker contributes an efficiency e (fraction of the job per day). The total work is 1 unit. If n workers are hired, the nominal completion time is 1/(n e). If this exceeds D, the penalty cost is P × (1/(n e) – D). The total cost = n w × 1/(n e) + penalty = w/e + P/(n e) – P D. Differentiating with respect to n (treating n as continuous) shows that cost decreases as n increases, but the marginal benefit diminishes. In discrete terms, the optimal integer n is the smallest number that makes the completion time ≤ D, unless the wage saving from fewer workers outweighs the penalty. Examiners often ask: “Find the minimum number of workers to avoid any penalty” which is simply ⌈1/(e D)⌉. A twist: “The contractor can also pay overtime at 1.5 × wage for hours beyond 8 per day.” This introduces a piecewise linear cost function; the candidate must compare the cost of hiring an extra worker versus paying overtime for the existing crew.

Incentive structures appear as “bonus of B if the work finishes in ≤ D days”. The contractor’s net gain = contract value – labour cost + bonus (if earned). The decision variable is again the crew size. The problem reduces to checking two scenarios: (i) finish early, earn bonus; (ii) finish late, no bonus. Compute net gain for each feasible crew size and pick the maximum. When multiple crews with different efficiencies are available (e.g., skilled vs. unskilled), the problem becomes a knapsack‑type allocation: choose numbers n₁, n₂ to minimise cost subject to n₁ e₁ + n₂ e₂ ≥ 1/D. Because the constraint is linear and the objective is linear, the optimum lies at an extreme point – either use only the more cost‑effective worker type or a boundary mix. The cost‑effectiveness ratio is w/e (wage per unit efficiency). The type with lower w/e is preferred unless a minimum number of the other type is mandated by contract.

Partial work with changing team composition is another advanced variant. “A team of 10 workers starts a job. After 4 days, 3 workers leave and 2 new workers of half the efficiency join. The job finishes in 12 days total. Find the time the original 10 would have taken alone.” Let the efficiency of an original worker be e. Work done in first 4 days = 10 e × 4 = 40 e. Remaining work = 1 – 40 e. New team efficiency = 7 e + 2 × (e/2) = 8 e. They work for 8 days, so 8 e × 8 = 64 e. Hence 40 e + 64 e = 104 e = 1 → e = 1/104. Original 10 workers’ rate = 10/104 = 5/52, so time = 52/5 = 10.4 days. The key is to express all efficiencies relative to a single base unit and keep the work equation linear. When the problem gives “A and B together finish in 12 days; after 4 days A leaves, B finishes in 18 days”, the same method yields B’s rate directly without solving for A. This “work‑done‑in‑first‑phase” shortcut is a staple of high‑difficulty papers.

Speed‑Distance‑Time: Multi‑Vehicle Overtaking, Minimum Separation, and Schedule Optimization

Overtaking problems with more than two moving objects require a systematic relative‑velocity framework. Consider three vehicles A, B, C moving on a straight road in the same direction with speeds v_A > v_B > v_C. The time for A to overtake B is the initial separation divided by v_A – v_B. If A must overtake both B and C, the total time is not simply the sum of individual overtaking times because after overtaking B, A’s position relative to C changes. The correct method: compute the time when A catches B, update positions of all three at that instant, then compute the additional time for A to catch C from the new separation. When vehicles move in opposite directions, the relative speed is the sum. A frequent examiner twist: “Two trains start from opposite ends of a 300 km track at 60 km/h and 40 km/h. A bird flies at 80 km/h from the first train to the second and back repeatedly until the trains meet. Find the total distance flown by the bird.” The meeting time of the trains is 300/(60+40) = 3 h. The bird flies continuously at 80 km/h for 3 h, so distance = 240 km. The infinite series of back‑and‑forth trips collapses to a single multiplication once the meeting time is known.

Minimum separation problems on a circular track involve finding the smallest distance between any two runners at any time. For two runners with speeds u and v (u > v) on a circle of length L, the relative angular speed is (u – v)/L revolutions per unit time. The separation varies sinusoidally; the minimum separation is zero if the ratio of speeds is rational (they meet), otherwise the infimum is zero but never attained. In exam questions, speeds are usually given as integers, so meetings are guaranteed. The time between successive meetings is L/(u – v). For three or more runners, the pairwise meeting times form a set; the overall schedule repeats at the least common multiple of all pairwise meeting periods. A typical question: “Three runners start together on a 400 m track at 5 m/s, 7 m/s, and 9 m/s. After how many seconds will all three be at the starting point simultaneously?” Compute individual lap times: 80 s, 400/7 s, 400/9 s. The LCM of these rational numbers is found by expressing each as a fraction in lowest terms and taking LCM of numerators divided by GCD of denominators. Here 80 = 80/1, 400/7, 400/9. LCM of numerators (80, 400, 400) = 400; GCD of denominators (1, 7, 9) = 1. So LCM = 400 s. At 400 s each has completed an integer number of laps (5, 7, 9 respectively).

Schedule optimisation appears as “A bus must cover a 120 km route with three segments of different speed limits. The bus can travel at most 60 km/h on segment 1 (30 km), 50 km/h on segment 2 (40 km), and 40 km/h on segment 3 (50 km). It must complete the journey in 2.5 h. Determine the minimum average speed required on each segment if the bus can exceed the limit on at most one segment.” This is a constrained optimisation: let x, y, z be the times spent on each segment. Constraints: x + y + z = 2.5, distances: v₁ x = 30, v₂ y = 40, v₃ z = 50, with v_i ≤ limit_i except possibly one. Substitute times: 30/v₁ + 40/v₂ + 50/v₃ = 2.5. To minimise the maximum speed, set two speeds at their limits and solve for the third. Check each choice of the “exception” segment; the feasible one with the lowest excess speed is the answer. The algebraic manipulation is straightforward once the problem is reduced to a single equation in one unknown.

Data Interpretation: Network Flow Diagrams, Allocation Constraints, and Multi‑Objective Optimization

Modern data‑interpretation sets increasingly present network diagrams where nodes represent factories, warehouses, or servers and directed edges carry capacities and per‑unit costs. The task is to determine the maximum feasible flow from a source to a sink, or the minimum‑cost flow satisfying a demand. The classic Ford‑Fulkerson augmenting‑path method is not expected to be executed manually; instead, examiners test the candidate’s ability to read the diagram, identify bottlenecks (minimum cut), and compute the max flow as the sum of capacities of edges crossing the cut. For example, a diagram shows source S connected to A (capacity 10) and B (capacity 15). A connects to C (8) and D (12). B connects to C (10) and D (5). C and D both connect to sink T with capacities 20 and 15 respectively. The minimum S‑T cut is {S‑A, S‑B} with total capacity 25, but a tighter cut is {A‑C, A‑D, B‑C, B‑D} with total 8+12+10+5 = 35. The true min cut is {C‑T, D‑T} = 20+15 = 35. Hence max flow = 25 (limited by source out‑edges). The candidate must evaluate all plausible cuts quickly; the cut that separates source from the rest often gives the answer.

Allocation constraints add linear inequalities: “Factory 1 can supply at most 100 units, Factory 2 at most 150 units. Warehouse A needs at least 80, Warehouse B at least 120. Transportation cost per unit from Factory i to Warehouse j is given in a matrix.” This is a transportation problem. The North‑West Corner rule or Vogel’s Approximation Method yields an initial feasible solution; the stepping‑stone method checks optimality. In an exam, the matrix is small (2×2 or 2×3) so the candidate can enumerate corner points of the feasible region defined by supply/demand constraints and non‑negativity. The objective is linear, so the optimum lies at a vertex. For a 2×2 case with supplies S₁, S₂ and demands D₁, D₂ (S₁+S₂ = D₁+D₂), the only degree of freedom is the amount x sent from Factory 1 to Warehouse 1. The cost function is C(x) = c₁₁ x + c₁₂ (S₁–x) + c₂₁ (D₁–x) + c₂₂ (S₂–D₁+x). This simplifies to a linear function in x; the minimum occurs at one of the bounds max(0, D₁–S₂) ≤ x ≤ min(S₁, D₁). Evaluating the cost at the two bounds gives the answer instantly.

Multi‑objective optimization appears when the question asks: “Maximise profit while keeping carbon emissions below a threshold.” Profit and emissions are both linear functions of the decision variables (quantities shipped). The feasible region is a polygon; the Pareto frontier is the set of vertices where improving one objective worsens the other. The examiner may provide a table of profit and emission coefficients for each route and ask for the maximum profit subject to emission ≤ E_max. This reduces to a single‑objective linear program with an extra constraint. Because the number of variables is tiny (usually ≤ 3), the candidate can solve by checking each vertex of the polygon formed by supply, demand, and emission constraints. The vertex coordinates are found by solving two equations at a time (e.g., supply equality + emission equality). The profit at each feasible vertex is computed, and the maximum is selected. The key skill is translating the word description into a set of linear equations/inequalities, identifying the small set of candidate vertices, and evaluating the objective without getting lost in arithmetic.

  • Assuming Wilson’s theorem applies to composite moduli without reducing to prime factors.
  • Adding successive percentage changes instead of multiplying their decimal factors.
  • Treating a penalty clause as a simple additive cost rather than a piecewise function of completion time.
  • Using a single relative speed for multi‑vehicle overtaking when the overtaking order changes the separations.
  • Ignoring the minimum‑cut principle and trying to sum all path capacities in a network flow diagram.
  • Applying the North‑West Corner rule without checking whether the initial solution satisfies all demand constraints.
  • Forgetting that the optimum of a linear objective over a polygon occurs at a vertex, leading to unnecessary interior‑point calculations.

These advanced sections complete the numerical ability toolkit for the most challenging quantitative reasoning questions.
