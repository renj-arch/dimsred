Analytical Chemistry — Qualitative/Quantitative Analysis, Chromatography, Spectroscopy & Titration

Deep dive into Analytical Chemistry — qualitative analysis (systematic identification of cations via group separation with HCl, H₂S, NH₄OH, and (NH₄)₂CO₃ reagents forming precipitates of distinct solubility, confirmatory tests like the brown ring test for NO₃&supmin;, flame tests producing characteristic colors from alkali and alkaline earth metals, and anion analysis for CO₃²&supmin;, SO₄²&supmin;, Cl&supmin;), quantitative analysis (gravimetric analysis by precipitation and ignition to constant mass, volumetric analysis using primary standards and titrant standardization, error analysis with accuracy vs precision, absolute and relative error, standard deviation, confidence intervals, calibration curves and the method of standard addition and internal standards), chromatography (partition and adsorption principles, paper chromatography with Rf values, thin-layer chromatography TLC with silica gel stationary phase, gas chromatography GC with thermal conductivity and flame ionization detectors, high-performance liquid chromatography HPLC with reverse-phase C18 columns and UV-Vis detection, column chromatography and size-exclusion chromatography), and spectroscopy and titration (UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy following the Beer-Lambert law A = εbc, infrared spectroscopy identifying functional groups via molecular vibrations, nuclear magnetic resonance NMR exploiting the Zeeman splitting of spin-½ nuclei in a magnetic field with chemical shift and J-coupling, mass spectrometry with electron ionization and electrospray producing mass-to-charge ratio spectra, and titrimetric methods including acid-base titrations with phenolphthalein and methyl orange indicators, redox titrations with KMnO₄ and I₂/thiosulfate, complexometric titrations with EDTA, and precipitation titrations by Mohr and Volhard methods).

“Analytical chemistry is what chemists do to figure out what’s there and how much of it there is.”
— Izaak Kolthoff
Part 1

🔬 Qualitative Analysis

Identification
Cation and Anion Analysis, Flame Tests, and Confirmatory Tests
Qualitative analysis identifies the components of a sample without measuring quantities. Cation analysis uses group separation based on solubility differences. Group I (Ag⁺, Hg₂²⁺, Pb²⁺): precipitate as chlorides with dilute HCl. Group II (Cu²⁺, Cd²⁺, Hg²⁺, As³⁺, Sb³⁺, Bi³⁺, Sn²⁺): precipitate as sulfides in acidic H₂S. Group III (Fe²⁺/³⁺, Al³⁺, Cr³⁺): precipitate as hydroxides with NH₄OH + NH₄Cl buffer. Group IV (Co²⁺, Ni²⁺, Mn²⁺, Zn²⁺): precipitate as sulfides in basic H₂S. Group V (Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺): precipitate as carbonates with (NH₄)₂CO₃. Group VI (Mg²⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺): the remaining cations. Flame tests: Na⁺ gives intense yellow (589 nm), K⁺ lilac (768 nm), Ca²⁺ brick red (622 nm), Sr²⁺ crimson red, Ba²⁺ apple green (554 nm), Cu²⁺ blue-green. Anion analysis tests for CO₃²&supmin; (effervescence with acid, limewater turns milky), SO₄²&supmin; (precipitate with BaCl₂ insoluble in acid), Cl&supmin; (white precipitate with AgNO₃ soluble in NH₄OH), NO₃&supmin; (brown ring test: FeSO₄ + conc. H₂SO₄ forms brown [Fe(H₂O)₅NO]²⁺ at the interface). Confirmatory tests distinguish within groups, e.g., K₄[Fe(CN)₆] test for Fe³⁺ (Prussian blue), dimethylglyoxime for Ni²⁺ (scarlet red precipitate).
Qualitative analysis systematically exploits differences in solubility and specific chemical reactions to identify unknown ions through a logical separation scheme.
Part 2

⚖️ Quantitative Analysis

Measurement
Gravimetric Analysis, Volumetric Analysis, Error Analysis, and Calibration
Quantitative analysis determines the exact amount of analytes. Gravimetric analysis isolates an analyte by precipitation, filtration, and weighing to constant mass. The analyte is converted to a highly insoluble precipitate (low solubility product, large particle size for easy filtration), ignited to a known composition (weighing form), and the mass is related to the original analyte via the gravimetric factor: GF = (MWanalyte × a) / (MWprecipitate × b). Example: chloride determined as AgCl. Volumetric analysis (titration) measures the volume of a solution of known concentration (titrant) required to react completely with the analyte. Primary standards (Na₂CO₃, KHP, K₂Cr₂O₇, oxalic acid) are stable, pure, and used to standardize secondary standards (NaOH, KMnO₄, HCl). The equivalence point is determined by an indicator color change or instrumentally. End point should match equivalence point. Error analysis: accuracy (closeness to true value) vs precision (reproducibility). Absolute error = measured − true. Relative error = (absolute error / true) × 100%. Systematic errors (instrumental, method, personal) are reproducible and can be corrected. Random errors follow a Gaussian distribution, quantified by standard deviation s = [Σ(xi − x̄)² / (n − 1)]¹⁄² and relative standard deviation RSD = (s / x̄) × 100%. Calibration: external standard method (calibration curve plotting signal vs concentration), standard addition (for matrix-matched quantification), internal standard (corrects for instrument drift and injection volume variations). The limit of detection (LOD) = 3.3 σ / slope; limit of quantification (LOQ) = 10 σ / slope.
Quantitative analysis provides accurate and precise measurements through careful gravimetric, volumetric, and instrumental methods, with rigorous error analysis and calibration ensuring reliability.
Part 3

🧪 Chromatography

Separation
Paper, TLC, GC, HPLC, and Column Chromatography
Chromatography separates mixture components based on differential partitioning between a stationary phase and a mobile phase. The partition coefficient K = Cs / Cm; components with larger K spend more time in the stationary phase and elute later. The retention factor Rf = distance moved by spot / distance moved by solvent front in planar chromatography. Paper chromatography: stationary phase is water adsorbed on cellulose paper; mobile phase is an organic solvent or mixture. Used for amino acids, sugars, pigments. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC): stationary phase is a thin layer of silica gel or alumina on a glass or plastic plate. Uses include reaction monitoring, identity checks, and preparative separation. Detection by UV fluorescence quenching, iodine staining, or specific reagents (ninhydrin for amino acids). Gas chromatography (GC): mobile phase is an inert carrier gas (He, N₂), stationary phase is a liquid coated on a fused silica capillary column. Suitable for volatile, thermally stable compounds. Detectors: flame ionization detector (FID) measures carbon content (universal for organics), thermal conductivity detector (TCD) universal but less sensitive, mass spectrometer (GC-MS) provides structural identification. Retention time tR = tM + k′tM; capacity factor k′ = (tR − tM) / tM. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC): high-pressure pumps drive liquid mobile phase through packed columns (typically 3-5 µm particles). Reverse-phase HPLC (most common): nonpolar C18 or C8 stationary phase, polar mobile phase (water/methanol/acetonitrile). Compounds elute in order of decreasing polarity. Normal-phase HPLC: polar stationary phase (silica), nonpolar mobile phase. Detectors: UV-Vis (diode array DAD), refractive index, fluorescence, evaporative light scattering (ELSD). Column chromatography: gravity-driven or flash chromatography on silica gel columns for preparative separations. Size-exclusion chromatography (SEC): separates by molecular size (largest molecules elute first).
Chromatography exploits differential partitioning between phases to achieve high-resolution separation; the choice of stationary phase, mobile phase, and detection method determines applicability.
Part 4

📊 Spectroscopy & Titration

Analysis
UV-Vis, IR, NMR, MS, and Acid-Base, Redox, Complexometric Titrations
Spectroscopy measures the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter. UV-Vis spectroscopy (200-800 nm) probes electronic transitions (σ → σ*, n → π*, π → π*, d-d in transition metals). The Beer-Lambert law: A = εbc, where A is absorbance (log₁₀I₀/I), ε is molar absorptivity (M&supmin;¹ cm&supmin;¹), b is path length (cm), c is concentration (M). Deviations from linearity occur at high concentration, stray light, and chemical equilibria. Infrared (IR) spectroscopy (4000-400 cm&supmin;¹) probes molecular vibrations (stretching and bending). Key group frequencies: O−H (broad 3200-3600 cm&supmin;¹), N−H (sharp 3300-3500), C=O (strong 1680-1750), C≡N (2200-2260). Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy uses a strong magnetic field to split nuclear spin energy levels. ¹H NMR provides information on: number of protons (integration), electronic environment (chemical shift δ in ppm, relative to TMS at 0 ppm), neighboring protons (J-coupling splitting patterns n+1 rule for multiplicity), and exchangeable protons (O−H, N−H). 13C NMR: decoupled spectra show single lines per chemically distinct carbon; DEPT distinguishes CH, CH₂, CH₃. Mass spectrometry (MS) ionizes molecules and sorts ions by mass-to-charge ratio m/z. Ionization methods: electron ionization (EI) produces molecular ion M⁺· and fragment ions; electrospray ionization (ESI) for polar, large molecules (proteins) producing multiply charged ions; MALDI for biomolecules. Analyzers: quadrupole, time-of-flight (TOF), ion trap, orbitrap. Titrimetric methods: acid-base titrations determine analyte concentration via strong/weak acid-base reactions; indicators change color at pH near their pKa (phenolphthalein pH 8.3-10.0, methyl orange pH 3.1-4.4). Redox titrations: KMnO₄ (self-indicating, purple to colorless) in acidic medium for reducing agents; I₂/thiosulfate for oxidizing agents (starch indicator, blue to colorless). Complexometric titrations: EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) forms stable 1:1 complexes with di- and trivalent metal ions (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe³⁺, Zn²⁺); Eriochrome Black T indicator changes from wine red to blue at equivalence point.
Spectroscopic methods probe molecular structure through radiation-matter interactions; titrations exploit stoichiometric reactions for precise quantification, each technique with characteristic sensitivity and selectivity.
