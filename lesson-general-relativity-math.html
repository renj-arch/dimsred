General Relativity — Lorentzian Geometry, Einstein's Equation & Singularities

Deep dive into the Mathematics of General Relativity — the geometric theory of gravitation. Topics covered include Lorentzian manifolds (a smooth manifold M with a metric g of signature (−,+,+,+)), causal structure (timelike, null, and spacelike vectors, the light cone, time orientation, and global hyperbolicity), the Levi-Civita connection for Lorentzian metrics, the Riemann curvature tensor R(X,Y)Z = ∇_X ∇_Y Z − ∇_Y ∇_X Z − ∇_{[X,Y]} Z, the Ricci tensor Ric(X,Y) = Tr(Z → R(Z,X)Y), the scalar curvature R = Tr_g Ric, the Einstein field equations Ric − ½R g + Λ g = 8π T (where T is the stress-energy tensor and Λ is the cosmological constant), the Schwarzschild metric ds² = −(1−2M/r) dt² + (1−2M/r)⁻¹ dr² + r² dΩ² (the unique spherically symmetric vacuum solution by Birkhoff's theorem), the Kerr metric (the rotating black hole solution with angular momentum a), the Penrose–Hawking singularity theorems (showing that under physical conditions (the null energy condition, existence of a trapped surface, and a non-compact Cauchy surface), singularities are generic), the ADM formulation of general relativity (a Hamiltonian description foliating spacetime into spatial slices with lapse N and shift vector β), the initial value problem (the Einstein equations split into constraint equations (the Gauss and Codazzi equations) and evolution equations), the Bel–Robinson tensor and the positive mass theorem (Schoen–Yau, Witten), and the Petrov classification of the Weyl tensor.

Part 1

📏 Lorentzian Geometry and Causality

Spacetime
Lorentzian Manifolds, Causal Structure, and Global Hyperbolicity
A Lorentzian metric g on a smooth manifold M of dimension n is a symmetric (0,2)-tensor field with signature (1, n−1). A vector v ∈ T_p M is timelike if g_p(v, v) < 0, null (or lightlike) if g_p(v, v) = 0, and spacelike if g_p(v, v) > 0. The light cone at p is the set of null vectors. A time orientation is a continuous timelike vector field X (choosing the future direction). A causal curve is a piecewise smooth curve whose tangent vector is everywhere timelike or null. The chronological future I⁺(p) is the set of points reachable from p by a future-directed timelike curve; the causal future J⁺(p) adds null curves. A Lorentzian manifold is globally hyperbolic if it is causal (no closed causal curves) and the sets J⁺(p) ∩ J⁻(q) are compact for all p, q. A Cauchy surface Σ is a spacelike hypersurface that every inextendible causal curve intersects exactly once — global hyperbolicity is equivalent to the existence of a Cauchy surface. The Geroch splitting theorem: a globally hyperbolic spacetime is diffeomorphic to ℝ × Σ with the metric ds² = −N² dt² + g_t, where N is the lapse function and g_t is a Riemannian metric on Σ depending on t. The domain of dependence D(Σ) = {p : every inextendible causal curve through p meets Σ} — within D(Σ), the evolution is uniquely determined by data on Σ. The strong cosmic censorship conjecture (Penrose): generically, a globally hyperbolic spacetime is inextendible — the future cannot be predicted beyond a Cauchy horizon. The weak cosmic censorship: singularities are hidden behind event horizons — no naked singularities form from generic initial data.
Lorentzian geometry replaces Riemannian geometry in relativity: the causal structure (light cones, Cauchy surfaces, global hyperbolicity) is the mathematical foundation for deterministic evolution in general relativity.
Part 2

⚡ The Einstein Field Equations

Equations
The Einstein Tensor, the Stress-Energy Tensor, and the Einstein–Hilbert Action
The Einstein tensor: G = Ric − ½R g where Ric is the Ricci tensor and R is the scalar curvature. The Bianchi identity for the curvature tensor implies ∇·G = 0, so the Einstein equations G + Λg = 8πT are compatible with energy conservation ∇·T = 0. The Einstein–Hilbert action: S_EH = (1/16π) ∫_M R √{−det(g)} d⁴x. Varying with respect to the metric gives Ric − ½R g = 0 in vacuum (when Λ = T = 0). Adding the cosmological constant: S = (1/16π) ∫_M (R − 2Λ) √{−det(g)} d⁴x + S_matter. The stress-energy tensor T_{μν} is defined via the variation of the matter action: T_{μν} = −(2/√{−det(g)}) δS_m/δg^{μν}. For perfect fluids: T_{μν} = (ρ + p) u_μ u_ν + p g_{μν} where ρ is the energy density, p is the pressure, and u is the 4-velocity. The weak energy condition: T_{μν} v^μ v^ν ≥ 0 for all timelike v (energy density is non-negative). The null energy condition: T_{μν} k^μ k^ν ≥ 0 for all null k (used in singularity theorems). The dominant energy condition: energy flows no faster than light. The Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metric: ds² = −dt² + a(t)² (dr²/(1−kr²) + r² dΩ²) describes a homogeneous, isotropic universe. The Friedmann equations: (ȧ/a)² = 8πρ/3 − k/a² + Λ/3 and ä/a = −4π(ρ+3p)/3 + Λ/3, derived from the Einstein equations for the FLRW metric. The cosmological constant Λ can be interpreted as dark energy driving the accelerated expansion of the universe.
The Einstein field equations couple the geometry of spacetime (the Einstein tensor G) to the matter content (the stress-energy tensor T) — the Bianchi identity ensures that energy-momentum conservation is geometrically encoded.
Part 3

🌌 Black Hole Solutions and the Kerr Metric

Black Holes
The Schwarzschild Solution, the Kerr Metric, and the Penrose Diagram
The Schwarzschild metric (1916): ds² = −(1 − 2M/r) dt² + (1 − 2M/r)^{-1} dr² + r²(dθ² + sin²θ dφ²). This is the unique spherically symmetric vacuum solution of the Einstein equations (Birkhoff's theorem). The event horizon at r = 2M is a null hypersurface — the Schwarzschild radius. Inside the horizon (r < 2M), the roles of t and r swap: r becomes timelike, and the singularity at r = 0 is inescapable. The Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates extend the Schwarzschild metric through the horizon: U = −e^{(r−2M)/(4M)} cosh(t/(4M)), V = e^{(r−2M)/(4M)} sinh(t/(4M)) for r > 2M, giving the maximal extension with a white hole and a parallel universe. The Kerr metric (1963): ds² = −(1 − 2Mr/ρ²) dt² − (4Mar sin²θ/ρ²) dt dφ + (ρ²/Δ) dr² + ρ² dθ² + (r² + a² + 2Ma²r sin²θ/ρ²) sin²θ dφ², where ρ² = r² + a² cos²θ and Δ = r² − 2Mr + a². The Kerr metric describes a rotating black hole with mass M and angular momentum a. The ergosphere: the region between the outer event horizon r_+ = M + √(M²−a²) and the static limit surface where g_{tt} = 0 — inside the ergosphere, all observers are forced to rotate with the black hole (frame-dragging). The Penrose process: energy can be extracted from a Kerr black hole by dropping a particle into the ergosphere. The Penrose diagram (or Carter–Penrose diagram) is a compactified representation of the conformal structure of a spacetime showing its causal infinity (future null infinity ℐ⁺, past null infinity ℐ⁻, timelike infinity i⁰, and spacelike infinity i⁰). The Hawking radiation (Hawking, 1974): quantum effects near the event horizon cause black holes to emit thermal radiation with temperature T = ℏ/(8πM) (in units G=c=1). The No-hair theorem: a stationary black hole is completely characterized by its mass M, charge Q, and angular momentum J — the Kerr–Newman family covers all possibilities.
Exact black hole solutions (Schwarzschild, Kerr, Kerr–Newman) are the simplest non-trivial solutions of the Einstein equations — their geometry reveals event horizons, singularities, ergospheres, and the deep connection between gravity and thermodynamics.
Part 4

🔥 Singularity Theorems and the Cauchy Problem

Singularities
The Penrose–Hawking Singularity Theorems, the ADM Formulation, and the Positive Mass Theorem
The Penrose singularity theorem (1965): a spacetime (M, g) satisfying (i) the null energy condition (Ric(k,k) ≥ 0 for null k), (ii) a non-compact Cauchy surface, and (iii) the existence of a trapped surface (a closed spacelike 2-surface where both families of null geodesics orthogonal to it are converging) is geodesically incomplete — there exists an inextendible null geodesic of finite affine length. This implies that gravitational collapse inevitably produces a singularity. The Hawking singularity theorem: if the strong energy condition (Ric(v,v) ≥ 0 for timelike v) holds and the universe is expanding (the Hubble parameter is positive), then the universe is geodesically incomplete in the past (the Big Bang). The Raychaudhuri equation: dθ/dτ = −(1/3) θ² − σ_{μν} σ^{μν} + ω_{μν} ω^{μν} − R_{μν} k^μ k^ν for a congruence of null geodesics with tangent k, where θ is the expansion, σ is the shear, and ω is the twist. Under energy conditions, R_{μν} k^μ k^ν ≥ 0, so θ → −∞ in finite affine length → geodesic incompleteness (a singularity). The ADM formulation (Arnowitt–Deser–Misner, 1962): the Einstein equations recast as a Hamiltonian system. The spacetime is foliated by spacelike hypersurfaces Σ_t. Let g_{ij} be the induced metric on Σ_t and K_{ij} be the extrinsic curvature (second fundamental form). The Einstein equations split into the constraint equations: (1) the Hamiltonian constraint: R^{(3)} + (Tr K)² − K_{ij} K^{ij} = 16πρ, and (2) the momentum constraints: ∇_j (K^{ij} − (Tr K) g^{ij}) = 8πJ^i. The evolution equations: ∂_t g_{ij} = −2N K_{ij} + ℒ_β g_{ij} and ∂_t K_{ij} = −∇_i ∇_j N + N (R_{ij}^{(3)} − 2 K_{ik} K^k_j + (Tr K) K_{ij}) + ℒ_β K_{ij} − 8πN (S_{ij} − ½(S − ρ) g_{ij}), where N is the lapse and β is the shift vector. The positive mass theorem (Schoen–Yau, 1979; Witten, 1981): the total ADM mass of an asymptotically flat spacetime satisfying the dominant energy condition is non-negative, and zero only for Minkowski spacetime. Witten's proof uses spinors and the Dirac operator in the spacetime harmonic spinor approach: M = (1/4π) ∫_S ∇_a ψ̄ γ^a ψ dS where ψ is a solution to the Dirac equation on the spacelike slice.
The singularity theorems show that singularities are generic in general relativity under reasonable physical conditions, while the ADM formulation and the positive mass theorem provide the rigorous foundation for the initial value problem and the stability of Minkowski spacetime.

Cosmic Censorship and the Structure of Singularities

The singularity theorems guarantee the existence of incomplete geodesics under generic conditions, but they do not specify whether the resulting singularities are hidden behind event horizons or visible to distant observers. The cosmic censorship conjecture, proposed by Penrose in 1969, addresses this gap. The weak cosmic censorship conjecture asserts that singularities arising from gravitational collapse of generic, asymptotically flat initial data are always clothed by an event horizon, so that no causal signal from the singularity can reach future null infinity ℐ⁺. The strong cosmic censorship conjecture goes further: it claims that the maximal Cauchy development of generic initial data is inextendible as a regular Lorentzian manifold, meaning that the spacetime cannot be extended beyond the Cauchy horizon in a physically reasonable way. In the Kerr and Reissner–Nordström solutions, the inner Cauchy horizon is known to be unstable under perturbations (mass inflation), providing evidence for strong censorship. However, counterexamples exist in highly symmetric settings, such as the self‑similar collapse of a scalar field (Christodoulou, 1994) where a naked singularity forms for a set of initial data of codimension one. The conjecture remains open in full generality; its resolution is tied to the well‑posedness of the Einstein equations and the behavior of the Weyl curvature near singularities. The Bel–Robinson tensor and the Christodoulou–Klainerman stability proof for Minkowski space provide tools to study the asymptotic structure, while numerical relativity simulations of binary black hole mergers consistently produce horizons that satisfy weak censorship. Understanding the precise conditions under which censorship holds or fails is a central problem linking global Lorentzian geometry, PDE theory, and the physical interpretation of singularities.

Gravitational Waves and the Linearized Theory

Far from strong sources the metric can be written as gμν = ημν + hμν with |hμν| ≪ 1. In the Lorenz gauge ∂μ h̄μν = 0, where h̄μν = hμν − ½ ημν h, the linearized Einstein equations reduce to the wave equation □ h̄μν = −16π Tμν. The solution in the far zone is h̄ij(t, x) ≈ (2/r) d²Iij/dt² evaluated at retarded time t − r, where Iij = ∫ ρ xi xj d³x is the mass quadrupole moment of the source. This quadrupole formula predicts that a binary system of masses m₁, m₂ in a circular orbit of separation a radiates power P = (32/5) (m₁ m₂)² (m₁ + m₂) / a⁵ (in units G = c = 1). The first direct detection by LIGO in 2015 (GW150914) matched the waveform from a binary black hole inspiral, merger, and ringdown, confirming the linearized prediction for the inspiral phase and the full nonlinear dynamics for the merger. Higher‑order post‑Newtonian corrections incorporate tail effects, spin‑orbit coupling, and radiation reaction, extending the analytic description to comparable‑mass binaries. The effective‑one‑body formalism resums the post‑Newtonian series and maps the two‑body problem to a test particle in a deformed Kerr background, providing accurate templates for data analysis. Gravitational waves also carry information about the polarisation content of gravity: in general relativity only the plus and cross tensor modes propagate, while alternative theories may allow scalar or vector modes. The observation of the binary neutron star merger GW170817 with an electromagnetic counterpart constrained the speed of gravity to equal the speed of light to within 10⁻¹⁵, a stringent test of Lorentz invariance in the gravitational sector.

Worked Example: Quadrupole Power from a Binary
Two equal masses m orbit each other in a circle of radius R (separation a = 2R). Compute the leading‑order gravitational‑wave luminosity using the quadrupole formula.
The reduced mass is μ = m/2 and total mass M = 2m. The quadrupole moment tensor for a circular orbit in the x‑y plane is Ixx = μ R² cos²(ωt), Iyy = μ R² sin²(ωt), Ixy = μ R² cos(ωt) sin(ωt). The third time derivative of Iij yields d³Iij/dt³ ∼ μ R² ω³. The luminosity is P = (1/5) ⟨d³Iij/dt³ d³Iij/dt³⟩. Using Kepler’s law ω² = M/a³ = 2m/(8R³) = m/(4R³), one finds P = (32/5) (m²)² (2m) / (2R)⁵ = (32/5) m⁵ / (32 R⁵) = (1/5) m⁵ / R⁵. Restoring G and c gives P = (32/5) G⁴ m⁵ / (c⁵ a⁵). This matches the standard binary‑inspiral power formula.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the gravitational‑wave power from a binary, first write the quadrupole formula P = (1/5) ⟨…⟩, then express the quadrupole moment in terms of the reduced mass and orbital separation. Use Kepler’s law to eliminate the angular frequency. Keep factors of G and c explicit until the final step; dimensional analysis is a quick check that the powers of G and c are correct.

Quantum Field Theory on Curved Spacetime and Hawking Radiation Derivation

Quantum field theory on a fixed curved background treats the metric as classical while quantizing matter fields. For a free scalar field φ satisfying (□ − m²) φ = 0, the field operator is expanded in mode functions that are positive‑frequency with respect to a chosen time coordinate. In a stationary spacetime with a Killing vector ξ = ∂/∂t, the natural vacuum is defined by modes e−iωt with ω > 0. The Schwarzschild geometry possesses a timelike Killing vector outside the horizon, but it becomes null on the horizon and spacelike inside. An observer at infinity uses the asymptotic time t to define the Boulware vacuum, which is singular on the horizon. A freely falling observer crossing the horizon naturally uses Kruskal coordinates (U, V) and defines the Unruh vacuum, which is regular on the future horizon but not on the past horizon. The Hartle–Hawking vacuum is regular on both horizons and corresponds to a thermal state at temperature T = ℏ/(8πM). The Bogoliubov transformation between the in‑modes (defined on past null infinity ℐ⁻) and the out‑modes (defined on future null infinity ℐ⁺) yields a thermal spectrum: the expectation value of the number operator in the out‑region is ⟨Nω⟩ = 1/(e8πMω − 1). This is the Hawking flux. The derivation relies on the exponential redshift of outgoing modes near the horizon, which translates the vacuum fluctuations at ℐ⁻ into a thermal distribution at ℐ⁺. The greybody factors Γℓ(ω) account for the back‑scattering of modes by the curvature potential, modifying the spectrum observed at infinity. The semiclassical Einstein equations Gμν = 8π ⟨Tμν⟩ incorporate the renormalized stress‑energy tensor of the quantum field, leading to black‑hole evaporation and the information‑loss puzzle. Recent developments include the Page curve from replica wormholes in the gravitational path integral, suggesting unitary evolution of the radiation.

The Initial Value Problem and Well‑Posedness in General Relativity

The Einstein equations are a system of quasi‑linear hyperbolic partial differential equations for the metric components. The ADM formulation splits spacetime into spacelike slices Σt with induced metric gij and extrinsic curvature Kij. The constraint equations (Hamiltonian and momentum) must hold on each slice; they are elliptic equations for the initial data (gij, Kij). The evolution equations are first‑order in time for gij and Kij but contain second spatial derivatives through the Ricci tensor of Σt. Choosing the lapse N and shift βi as gauge functions turns the system into a symmetric hyperbolic system. The harmonic gauge (□ xμ = 0) yields a reduced system that is manifestly hyperbolic: the Einstein equations become □ gμν = Fμν(g, ∂g). The Choquet‑Bruhat theorem (1952) proves local existence and uniqueness of solutions for smooth initial data satisfying the constraints. The maximal Cauchy development is unique up to diffeomorphism. Global existence for small perturbations of Minkowski space was established by Christodoulou and Klainerman (1993) using the vector‑field method and the Bel–Robinson energy. For large data, the cosmic censorship conjecture is equivalent to the statement that the maximal development is inextendible as a C² Lorentzian metric. Numerical relativity solves the initial‑value problem by constructing constraint‑satisfying initial data (e.g., Bowen–York extrinsic curvature for spinning black holes) and evolving with high‑resolution shock‑capturing methods for matter coupled to the BSSNOK formulation of the ADM equations. The well‑posedness theory guarantees that the numerical solution converges to the true solution as resolution increases, provided the gauge conditions are chosen to avoid coordinate singularities.

Exact Solutions Beyond Kerr: Vaidya, Robinson–Trautman, and Cosmological Models

The Kerr family exhausts stationary, asymptotically flat, vacuum black‑hole solutions, but many physically relevant spacetimes are non‑stationary or contain matter. The Vaidya metric describes a spherically symmetric spacetime with a null dust fluid: ds² = −(1 − 2m(v)/r) dv² + 2 dv dr + r² dΩ², where v is the advanced time and m(v) is the mass function. It models radiating stars (outgoing Vaidya, m decreasing) or accreting black holes (ingoing Vaidya, m increasing). The Robinson–Trautman solutions are a class of algebraically special (Petrov type II) vacuum metrics that admit a shear‑free, twist‑free null geodesic congruence. They describe gravitational radiation from a bounded source relaxing to a Schwarzschild black hole; the mass decreases monotonically according to the Bondi mass‑loss formula. In cosmology, the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metrics are the unique maximally symmetric spatial solutions with a perfect fluid source. The scale factor a(t) satisfies the Friedmann equations: (ȧ/a)² = (8π/3) ρ − k/a² and ä/a = −(4π/3)(ρ + 3p). Exact inhomogeneous cosmological models include the Lemaître–Tolman–Bondi (LTB) dust solutions, which are spherically symmetric but not homogeneous, and the Szekeres solutions, which have no Killing vectors. These models are used to study backreaction, void formation, and the effect of inhomogeneities on the observed acceleration. The Kasner metric ds² = −dt² + t²p₁ dx² + t²p₂ dy² + t²p₃ dz² with Σ pi = Σ pi² = 1 describes anisotropic vacuum cosmologies and appears as the local asymptotic behavior near spacelike singularities in the Belinski–Khalatnikov–Lifshitz (BKL) conjecture. Understanding the global structure of these exact solutions informs the stability problem and the classification of singularities in general relativity.

  • Applying the quadrupole formula to systems with strong internal gravity (e.g., comparable‑mass black holes) without post‑Newtonian corrections.
  • Assuming the Boulware vacuum is the physical vacuum for a black hole; it is singular on the horizon, whereas the Unruh or Hartle–Hawking vacua are regular.

The Positive Mass Theorem and its Proof Sketch

The positive mass theorem asserts that for any complete, asymptotically flat Riemannian 3‑manifold (Σ, g) with non‑negative scalar curvature R ≥ 0, the ADM mass mADM is non‑negative. Moreover, mADM = 0 if and only if (Σ, g) is isometric to Euclidean space. In the Lorentzian setting the theorem applies to an initial data set (Σ, g, K) satisfying the dominant energy condition μ ≥ |J|, where μ = (R + (tr K)² − |K|²)/16π and J = (∇·K − d(tr K))/8π. The ADM mass is defined by the flux integral at spatial infinity mADM = (1/16π) limr→∞ ∫Sr (∂jgij − ∂igjj) ni dA.

Two landmark proofs exist. Schoen and Yau (1979) used minimal hypersurface techniques: they considered a stable minimal surface Σ0 in a conformally related manifold and derived a contradiction from the stability inequality if mADM < 0. The argument relies on the Jang equation to reduce the Lorentzian problem to the Riemannian one. Witten (1981) gave a spinorial proof: on a spin manifold one solves the Dirac equation Dψ = 0 with asymptotic boundary condition ψ → constant spinor. Integration by parts yields mADM = ∫Σ (|∇ψ|² + (R/4)|ψ|²) dV ≥ 0, with equality forcing ψ to be a covariantly constant spinor, which implies flatness. Both proofs require the dominant energy condition and asymptotic flatness; counterexamples such as the negative‑mass Schwarzschild slice show the necessity of these hypotheses.

Extensions include the Riemannian Penrose inequality mADM ≥ √(A/16π) for time‑symmetric data with an outermost minimal surface of area A, proved by Huisken–Ilmanen (inverse mean curvature flow) and Bray (conformal flow). The positive mass theorem also underpins the stability of Minkowski space: Christodoulou and Klainerman (1993) used a vector‑field method and the Bel–Robinson energy to show that small perturbations of Minkowski data exist globally and have non‑negative Bondi mass.

The Penrose Inequality and Quasi‑Local Mass

The Penrose inequality provides a lower bound on the total mass of an isolated gravitating system in terms of the area of its outermost apparent horizon. For a time‑symmetric, asymptotically flat initial data set (Σ, g) containing a closed minimal 2‑sphere S of area A, the inequality reads mADM ≥ √(A/16π). Equality holds exactly for a spatial slice of the Schwarzschild solution. The proof for the time‑symmetric case was completed independently by Huisken and Ilmanen (1997) using inverse mean curvature flow, and by Bray (1997) using a conformal flow of metrics. Both arguments exploit the monotonicity of the Hawking mass mH(S) = √(A/16π) (1 − (1/16π) ∫S H² dA) along the flow, where H is the mean curvature.

Quasi‑local mass attempts to assign a mass to a finite 2‑sphere without reference to infinity. The Hawking mass is the simplest candidate; it is monotone under inverse mean curvature flow and reduces to the ADM mass at infinity. The Brown–York mass mBY(S) = (1/8π) ∫S (k0 − k) dA compares the mean curvature k of S embedded in Σ with the mean curvature k0 of an isometric embedding into Euclidean space. Liu and Yau (2003) defined a mass using the mean curvature vector in spacetime, proving positivity under the dominant energy condition. Wang and Yau (2009) introduced a mass depending on an isometric embedding into Minkowski space together with a time function, which is also positive and recovers the ADM and Bondi masses in appropriate limits. These quasi‑local quantities are essential for formulating the Penrose inequality in the non‑time‑symmetric case, where the conjectured bound involves the area of a marginally outer trapped surface and the ADM mass.

Applying the Penrose Inequality
Given a time‑symmetric, asymptotically flat initial data set with an outermost minimal 2‑sphere of area 64π, compute the lower bound on the ADM mass and state the rigidity condition.
The Penrose inequality for time‑symmetric data reads mADM ≥ √(A/16π). Substituting A = 64π gives √(64π / 16π) = √4 = 2. Hence mADM ≥ 2. Equality holds if and only if the initial data set is isometric to a spatial slice of the Schwarzschild solution with mass parameter m = 2, i.e. the data is exactly the t = constant slice of Schwarzschild with ADM mass 2.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the Penrose inequality formula mADM ≥ √(A/16π) and the two main proofs (inverse mean curvature flow and conformal flow). Distinguish the time‑symmetric case (proved) from the general case (conjectural). Know the definitions of Hawking, Brown–York, Liu–Yau, and Wang–Yau quasi‑local masses and their positivity theorems.

Black Hole Thermodynamics and the Laws of Black Hole Mechanics

The four laws of black hole mechanics, formulated by Bardeen, Carter and Hawking (1973), mirror the laws of thermodynamics. The zeroth law states that the surface gravity κ of a stationary Killing horizon is constant over the horizon. For a Kerr–Newman black hole κ = (r+ − r−)/(2(r+² + a²)), where r± = M ± √(M² − a² − Q²). The first law relates variations of mass M, horizon area A, angular momentum J and electric charge Q: δM = (κ/8π) δA + Ω δJ + Φ δQ, where Ω = a/(r+² + a²) is the angular velocity and Φ = Q r+/(r+² + a²) the electrostatic potential. The second law, Hawking’s area theorem, asserts that the area of a future outer trapping horizon cannot decrease (δA ≥ 0) provided the null energy condition holds. The third law states that κ cannot be reduced to zero in any finite sequence of physical processes; extremal black holes (κ = 0) are unattainable.

Hawking’s discovery of black hole radiation (1974) identified the temperature T = κ/2π and entropy S = A/4 (in Planck units). The generalized second law (GSL) posits that the sum of black hole entropy and ordinary matter entropy never decreases. Quantum corrections lead to logarithmic terms in the entropy and to the Page curve describing the entanglement entropy of Hawking radiation. The thermodynamic analogy extends to black hole phase transitions: in anti‑de Sitter space, large black holes are thermodynamically stable while small ones are not, leading to a Hawking–Page transition. The laws also constrain the possible end‑states of gravitational collapse and underpin the holographic principle.

The Cauchy Horizon Instability and Strong Cosmic Censorship

In the maximal analytic extensions of the Reissner–Nordström and Kerr solutions, the inner horizon r = r− is a Cauchy horizon: beyond it the evolution of generic initial data is not uniquely determined. Linear perturbation theory shows that infalling radiation experiences an infinite blueshift at the Cauchy horizon, causing the energy density measured by a freely falling observer to diverge. Poisson and Israel (1990) demonstrated that nonlinear backreaction leads to mass inflation: the interior mass parameter grows exponentially while the area radius remains finite, producing a null singularity with finite tidal distortion but divergent curvature scalars.

Strong cosmic censorship (SCC) conjectures that for generic asymptotically flat initial data the maximal Cauchy development is inextendible as a regular Lorentzian manifold. Christodoulou (1991) proved SCC for the spherically symmetric Einstein–scalar field system: the Cauchy horizon is either spacelike (hence a singularity) or the solution is exactly Reissner–Nordström. Dafermos and Luk (2017) established C⁰‑inextendibility for the interior of Kerr black holes under linearized perturbations, while recent work by Luk and Oh (2021) shows that the metric cannot be extended as a C² solution. The distinction between C⁰ and C² extendibility is crucial: C⁰ extensions may exist (weak null singularities) but C² extensions are ruled out, preserving determinism in the sense of classical general relativity. The instability of the Cauchy horizon thus provides a mechanism for SCC, though the full conjecture for generic vacuum data remains open.

The Newman–Penrose Formalism and Spin‑Coefficient Equations

The Newman–Penrose (NP) formalism rewrites the Einstein equations using a null tetrad (l, n, m, m̄) with l·n = −1, m·m̄ = 1, all other inner products zero. The twelve complex spin coefficients (κ, σ, ρ, τ, π, λ, μ, ν, α, β, γ, ε) encode the connection components. The Weyl tensor is represented by five complex scalars Ψ₀ … Ψ₄; Ψ₀ and Ψ₄ describe transverse gravitational radiation, Ψ₂ the Coulomb‑like part, while Ψ₁ and Ψ₃ are longitudinal. The NP field equations split into the Ricci identities (giving evolution equations for the spin coefficients) and the Bianchi identities (governing the Weyl scalars). The peeling theorem states that along outgoing null geodesics Ψ₀ = O(r⁻⁵), Ψ₁ = O(r⁻⁴), Ψ₂ = O(r⁻³), Ψ₃ = O(r⁻²), Ψ₄ = O(r⁻¹), so Ψ₄ carries the radiative degrees of freedom at null infinity.

For perturbations of the Kerr background, the Teukolsky master equation separates the spin‑weighted field equations for Ψ₀ and Ψ₄, enabling mode analysis and the computation of quasinormal modes. In numerical relativity, the NP formalism is used to extract gravitational waveforms (Ψ₄) from simulations and to construct characteristic initial data. The Geroch–Held–Penrose (GHP) formalism further exploits the boost and spin weight symmetries, simplifying calculations in algebraically special spacetimes. Mastery of the NP spin‑coefficient equations is essential for analytic work on gravitational radiation, black hole perturbation theory, and the classification of exact solutions via Petrov types.

  • Confusing ADM mass with Bondi mass; ADM is total mass at spatial infinity, Bondi is mass at null infinity and decreases due to radiation.
  • Assuming the Penrose inequality holds for all initial data without the dominant energy condition.
  • Treating the surface gravity κ as a coordinate‑dependent quantity; it is invariant for Killing horizons.
  • Neglecting the role of the dominant energy condition in the positive mass theorem; counterexamples exist if violated.
  • Misapplying the area theorem to non‑stationary horizons; the theorem requires the null energy condition and a future outer trapping horizon.
  • Overlooking that the Newman–Penrose formalism requires a null tetrad adapted to the geometry; a poor choice obscures the physical content.
  • These traps highlight the most frequent conceptual errors in advanced general relativity examinations.
