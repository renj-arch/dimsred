General Relativity — Lorentzian Geometry, Einstein's Equation & Singularities

Deep dive into the Mathematics of General Relativity — the geometric theory of gravitation. Topics covered include Lorentzian manifolds (a smooth manifold M with a metric g of signature (−,+,+,+)), causal structure (timelike, null, and spacelike vectors, the light cone, time orientation, and global hyperbolicity), the Levi-Civita connection for Lorentzian metrics, the Riemann curvature tensor R(X,Y)Z = ∇_X ∇_Y Z − ∇_Y ∇_X Z − ∇_{[X,Y]} Z, the Ricci tensor Ric(X,Y) = Tr(Z → R(Z,X)Y), the scalar curvature R = Tr_g Ric, the Einstein field equations Ric − ½R g + Λ g = 8π T (where T is the stress-energy tensor and Λ is the cosmological constant), the Schwarzschild metric ds² = −(1−2M/r) dt² + (1−2M/r)⁻¹ dr² + r² dΩ² (the unique spherically symmetric vacuum solution by Birkhoff's theorem), the Kerr metric (the rotating black hole solution with angular momentum a), the Penrose–Hawking singularity theorems (showing that under physical conditions (the null energy condition, existence of a trapped surface, and a non-compact Cauchy surface), singularities are generic), the ADM formulation of general relativity (a Hamiltonian description foliating spacetime into spatial slices with lapse N and shift vector β), the initial value problem (the Einstein equations split into constraint equations (the Gauss and Codazzi equations) and evolution equations), the Bel–Robinson tensor and the positive mass theorem (Schoen–Yau, Witten), and the Petrov classification of the Weyl tensor.

Part 1

📏 Lorentzian Geometry and Causality

Spacetime
Lorentzian Manifolds, Causal Structure, and Global Hyperbolicity
A Lorentzian metric g on a smooth manifold M of dimension n is a symmetric (0,2)-tensor field with signature (1, n−1). A vector v ∈ T_p M is timelike if g_p(v, v) < 0, null (or lightlike) if g_p(v, v) = 0, and spacelike if g_p(v, v) > 0. The light cone at p is the set of null vectors. A time orientation is a continuous timelike vector field X (choosing the future direction). A causal curve is a piecewise smooth curve whose tangent vector is everywhere timelike or null. The chronological future I⁺(p) is the set of points reachable from p by a future-directed timelike curve; the causal future J⁺(p) adds null curves. A Lorentzian manifold is globally hyperbolic if it is causal (no closed causal curves) and the sets J⁺(p) ∩ J⁻(q) are compact for all p, q. A Cauchy surface Σ is a spacelike hypersurface that every inextendible causal curve intersects exactly once — global hyperbolicity is equivalent to the existence of a Cauchy surface. The Geroch splitting theorem: a globally hyperbolic spacetime is diffeomorphic to ℝ × Σ with the metric ds² = −N² dt² + g_t, where N is the lapse function and g_t is a Riemannian metric on Σ depending on t. The domain of dependence D(Σ) = {p : every inextendible causal curve through p meets Σ} — within D(Σ), the evolution is uniquely determined by data on Σ. The strong cosmic censorship conjecture (Penrose): generically, a globally hyperbolic spacetime is inextendible — the future cannot be predicted beyond a Cauchy horizon. The weak cosmic censorship: singularities are hidden behind event horizons — no naked singularities form from generic initial data.
Lorentzian geometry replaces Riemannian geometry in relativity: the causal structure (light cones, Cauchy surfaces, global hyperbolicity) is the mathematical foundation for deterministic evolution in general relativity.
Part 2

⚡ The Einstein Field Equations

Equations
The Einstein Tensor, the Stress-Energy Tensor, and the Einstein–Hilbert Action
The Einstein tensor: G = Ric − ½R g where Ric is the Ricci tensor and R is the scalar curvature. The Bianchi identity for the curvature tensor implies ∇·G = 0, so the Einstein equations G + Λg = 8πT are compatible with energy conservation ∇·T = 0. The Einstein–Hilbert action: S_EH = (1/16π) ∫_M R √{−det(g)} d⁴x. Varying with respect to the metric gives Ric − ½R g = 0 in vacuum (when Λ = T = 0). Adding the cosmological constant: S = (1/16π) ∫_M (R − 2Λ) √{−det(g)} d⁴x + S_matter. The stress-energy tensor T_{μν} is defined via the variation of the matter action: T_{μν} = −(2/√{−det(g)}) δS_m/δg^{μν}. For perfect fluids: T_{μν} = (ρ + p) u_μ u_ν + p g_{μν} where ρ is the energy density, p is the pressure, and u is the 4-velocity. The weak energy condition: T_{μν} v^μ v^ν ≥ 0 for all timelike v (energy density is non-negative). The null energy condition: T_{μν} k^μ k^ν ≥ 0 for all null k (used in singularity theorems). The dominant energy condition: energy flows no faster than light. The Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metric: ds² = −dt² + a(t)² (dr²/(1−kr²) + r² dΩ²) describes a homogeneous, isotropic universe. The Friedmann equations: (ȧ/a)² = 8πρ/3 − k/a² + Λ/3 and ä/a = −4π(ρ+3p)/3 + Λ/3, derived from the Einstein equations for the FLRW metric. The cosmological constant Λ can be interpreted as dark energy driving the accelerated expansion of the universe.
The Einstein field equations couple the geometry of spacetime (the Einstein tensor G) to the matter content (the stress-energy tensor T) — the Bianchi identity ensures that energy-momentum conservation is geometrically encoded.
Part 3

🌌 Black Hole Solutions and the Kerr Metric

Black Holes
The Schwarzschild Solution, the Kerr Metric, and the Penrose Diagram
The Schwarzschild metric (1916): ds² = −(1 − 2M/r) dt² + (1 − 2M/r)^{-1} dr² + r²(dθ² + sin²θ dφ²). This is the unique spherically symmetric vacuum solution of the Einstein equations (Birkhoff's theorem). The event horizon at r = 2M is a null hypersurface — the Schwarzschild radius. Inside the horizon (r < 2M), the roles of t and r swap: r becomes timelike, and the singularity at r = 0 is inescapable. The Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates extend the Schwarzschild metric through the horizon: U = −e^{(r−2M)/(4M)} cosh(t/(4M)), V = e^{(r−2M)/(4M)} sinh(t/(4M)) for r > 2M, giving the maximal extension with a white hole and a parallel universe. The Kerr metric (1963): ds² = −(1 − 2Mr/ρ²) dt² − (4Mar sin²θ/ρ²) dt dφ + (ρ²/Δ) dr² + ρ² dθ² + (r² + a² + 2Ma²r sin²θ/ρ²) sin²θ dφ², where ρ² = r² + a² cos²θ and Δ = r² − 2Mr + a². The Kerr metric describes a rotating black hole with mass M and angular momentum a. The ergosphere: the region between the outer event horizon r_+ = M + √(M²−a²) and the static limit surface where g_{tt} = 0 — inside the ergosphere, all observers are forced to rotate with the black hole (frame-dragging). The Penrose process: energy can be extracted from a Kerr black hole by dropping a particle into the ergosphere. The Penrose diagram (or Carter–Penrose diagram) is a compactified representation of the conformal structure of a spacetime showing its causal infinity (future null infinity ℐ⁺, past null infinity ℐ⁻, timelike infinity i⁰, and spacelike infinity i⁰). The Hawking radiation (Hawking, 1974): quantum effects near the event horizon cause black holes to emit thermal radiation with temperature T = ℏ/(8πM) (in units G=c=1). The No-hair theorem: a stationary black hole is completely characterized by its mass M, charge Q, and angular momentum J — the Kerr–Newman family covers all possibilities.
Exact black hole solutions (Schwarzschild, Kerr, Kerr–Newman) are the simplest non-trivial solutions of the Einstein equations — their geometry reveals event horizons, singularities, ergospheres, and the deep connection between gravity and thermodynamics.
Part 4

🔥 Singularity Theorems and the Cauchy Problem

Singularities
The Penrose–Hawking Singularity Theorems, the ADM Formulation, and the Positive Mass Theorem
The Penrose singularity theorem (1965): a spacetime (M, g) satisfying (i) the null energy condition (Ric(k,k) ≥ 0 for null k), (ii) a non-compact Cauchy surface, and (iii) the existence of a trapped surface (a closed spacelike 2-surface where both families of null geodesics orthogonal to it are converging) is geodesically incomplete — there exists an inextendible null geodesic of finite affine length. This implies that gravitational collapse inevitably produces a singularity. The Hawking singularity theorem: if the strong energy condition (Ric(v,v) ≥ 0 for timelike v) holds and the universe is expanding (the Hubble parameter is positive), then the universe is geodesically incomplete in the past (the Big Bang). The Raychaudhuri equation: dθ/dτ = −(1/3) θ² − σ_{μν} σ^{μν} + ω_{μν} ω^{μν} − R_{μν} k^μ k^ν for a congruence of null geodesics with tangent k, where θ is the expansion, σ is the shear, and ω is the twist. Under energy conditions, R_{μν} k^μ k^ν ≥ 0, so θ → −∞ in finite affine length → geodesic incompleteness (a singularity). The ADM formulation (Arnowitt–Deser–Misner, 1962): the Einstein equations recast as a Hamiltonian system. The spacetime is foliated by spacelike hypersurfaces Σ_t. Let g_{ij} be the induced metric on Σ_t and K_{ij} be the extrinsic curvature (second fundamental form). The Einstein equations split into the constraint equations: (1) the Hamiltonian constraint: R^{(3)} + (Tr K)² − K_{ij} K^{ij} = 16πρ, and (2) the momentum constraints: ∇_j (K^{ij} − (Tr K) g^{ij}) = 8πJ^i. The evolution equations: ∂_t g_{ij} = −2N K_{ij} + ℒ_β g_{ij} and ∂_t K_{ij} = −∇_i ∇_j N + N (R_{ij}^{(3)} − 2 K_{ik} K^k_j + (Tr K) K_{ij}) + ℒ_β K_{ij} − 8πN (S_{ij} − ½(S − ρ) g_{ij}), where N is the lapse and β is the shift vector. The positive mass theorem (Schoen–Yau, 1979; Witten, 1981): the total ADM mass of an asymptotically flat spacetime satisfying the dominant energy condition is non-negative, and zero only for Minkowski spacetime. Witten's proof uses spinors and the Dirac operator in the spacetime harmonic spinor approach: M = (1/4π) ∫_S ∇_a ψ̄ γ^a ψ dS where ψ is a solution to the Dirac equation on the spacelike slice.
The singularity theorems show that singularities are generic in general relativity under reasonable physical conditions, while the ADM formulation and the positive mass theorem provide the rigorous foundation for the initial value problem and the stability of Minkowski spacetime.
