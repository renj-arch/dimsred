Rough Path Theory — Controlled Paths, the Signature & the Lyons' Universal Limit Theorem

Rough Path Theory, developed by Terry Lyons, provides a deterministic pathwise framework for differential equations driven by irregular signals. A rough path is a path equipped with its iterated integrals up to a specified order, satisfying Chen's algebraic identity and analytic regularity (finite p-variation). The signature of a path — the full collection of iterated integrals — lives in the tensor algebra and serves as a universal feature map for path-space functionals. The Universal Limit Theorem (Lyons, 1998) states that the solution map of a rough differential equation depends continuously on the driving rough path, enabling the approximation of stochastic differential equations by smooth paths. Controlled rough paths (Gubinelli, 2004) give a tractable approach to integration against rough paths using a derivative-like expansion. The theory underpins Hairer's regularity structures, the signature method in machine learning, and applications in finance, medical time series, and handwriting recognition.

Part 1

📏 Paths, Iterated Integrals, and the p-variation

Foundations
p-variation, Iterated Integrals, Chen's Identity, and the Signature
The p-variation of a path X: [0,T] → ℝ^d is defined as ||X||_p = (sup_{division} Σ_i |X_{t_{i+1}}−X_{t_i}|^p)^{1/p}. For Brownian motion, p = 2 (finite quadratic variation). The iterated integral of order k is X^{(k)}_{s,t} = ∫_{s < t_1 < ... < t_k < t} dX_{t_1} ⊗ ... ⊗ dX_{t_k}, defined for smooth paths and extended by continuity. Chen's identity: for s < u < t, X^{(k)}_{s,t} = Σ_{i=0}^k X^{(i)}_{s,u} ⊗ X^{(k-i)}_{u,t} — this multiplicative property encodes path concatenation in the tensor algebra. The step-N signature is the truncated tensor series S_N(X)_{s,t} = (1, X^{(1)}_{s,t}, ..., X^{(N)}_{s,t}) ∈ T^N(ℝ^d). The full signature S(X)_{s,t} = (X^{(k)}_{s,t})_{k≥0} ∈ T((ℝ^d)). A homogeneous p-rough path is a path in the reduced tensor algebra satisfying Chen's identity and having Hölder-type regularity with exponent (1/p)-Hölder scaling for each level. The Lévy area (the antisymmetric part of the second iterated integral) distinguishes the Itô and Stratonovich lifts of Brownian motion.
The p-variation measures the roughness of a path; iterated integrals up to order ⌊p⌋ are needed to define a consistent rough path lift; Chen's identity is the algebraic backbone of the theory.
Part 2

🌍 Rough Differential Equations and the Universal Limit Theorem

RDEs
Rough Differential Equations and the Universal Limit Theorem (Lyons, 1998)
A rough differential equation (RDE) takes the form dY_t = V(Y_t) dX_t where X is a p-rough path and V = (V_1, ..., V_d) is a collection of sufficiently smooth vector fields on ℝ^m. The solution Y is the unique path satisfying dY = V(Y) dX, interpreted via the rough integral — a limit of Riemann-Stieltjes sums augmented by corrections involving higher-order iterated integrals (the area term). The Universal Limit Theorem (Lyons, 1998): the solution map (X, V) → Y is continuous in the rough path topology. If X^n → X in the p-variation rough path metric, then Y^n → Y in the q-variation metric for any q > p. This uniform continuity is the central analytic result: it allows the approximation of a rough path (e.g., Brownian motion) by smooth paths (piecewise linear approximations, mollifications) and guarantees the convergence of the corresponding solutions. The Stratonovich lift of Brownian motion includes the Lévy area process (the stochastic area integral), while the Itô lift does not satisfy the required geometric property (the Itô integral lacks the area correction, leading to different limiting behavior). The theory extends to branched rough paths (using the Butcher group) for non-geometric driving signals.
The Universal Limit Theorem ensures stability of RDE solutions under approximation of the driving signal — a pathwise analogue of the Wong-Zakai theorem in stochastic analysis.
Part 3

🧮 Controlled Rough Paths and Gubinelli's Approach

Gubinelli
Controlled Rough Paths, Gubinelli Derivatives, and the Hairer-Kelly Reconstruction Theorem
Gubinelli's controlled rough paths (2004) provide a streamlined approach: a path Y is controlled by X if for every s, t we can expand Y_t − Y_s = Y'_s (X_t − X_s) + R_{s,t} where the remainder R satisfies |R_{s,t}| ≤ C |t−s|^{p^{−1}+ε} for some ε > 0. The Gubinelli derivative Y'_s (an ℝ^{m×d}-valued process) is not a true derivative but a "local approximation coefficient." The rough integral ∫ Y_s dX_s is defined as the limit of Σ_i [Y_{t_i}(X_{t_{i+1}}−X_{t_i}) + Y'_{t_i} A_{t_i,t_{i+1}}] where A denotes the Lévy area (the second-order iterated integral). This extra correction term is essential for convergence when the driving path has finite p-variation with p ≥ 2. Davie's approach shows that RDEs can be solved via the Euler method using this controlled structure. The Hairer-Kelly reconstruction theorem (2014) generalizes the controlled path framework: given a "distribution-like" object (a modelled distribution) on a metric space, one can reconstruct a function whose increments match prescribed local expansions. This theorem is the cornerstone of Hairer's regularity structures (2014), which solved the KPZ equation and established a general theory for singular stochastic PDEs. The reconstruction theorem replaces the explicit Taylor expansion of controlled paths with an abstract "model" acting on a graded vector space.
Controlled rough paths turn the rough integral into a tractable calculus; the reconstruction theorem is the conceptual core of Hairer's regularity structures for singular SPDEs.
Part 4

🔥 Signature Methods and Machine Learning

Signature
The Signature as a Universal Feature Map, Log-Signature, and Applications
The signature of a path X over [s,t] is the infinite sequence S(X)_{s,t} = (1, X^{(1)}_{s,t}, X^{(2)}_{s,t}, ...) ∈ T((ℝ^d)). It is invariant under reparametrization: S(X∘φ) = S(X) for any smooth increasing φ. The signature is universal: every continuous function of the path (under mild conditions) can be approximated arbitrarily well by a linear functional of the signature (a consequence of the Stone-Weierstrass theorem in the tensor algebra). The log-signature — the formal logarithm of the signature in the tensor algebra — gives a reduced, non-redundant representation lying in the free Lie algebra. Applications of the signature in machine learning: the signature kernel (a positive-definite kernel on path space enabling kernel methods for sequential data), the signature as a deep learning layer (the signature transform maps a multivariate time series to a fixed-dimensional feature vector that can be fed into a neural network), and the signature in financial mathematics (portfolio optimization, volatility modeling, limit order book analysis). In handwriting recognition and medical time series (e.g., gait analysis, ECG), the signature provides a compact, informative feature set. The Expected Signature of a stochastic process (the signature of the law) relates to moment problems and can be computed via a controlled ODE. The Neural ODE perspective treats the signature transform as solving a linear controlled differential equation — connecting rough path theory to modern deep learning architectures for irregularly sampled time series.
The signature is a universal, reparametrization-invariant feature map for paths — bridging rough path theory, stochastic analysis, and machine learning.

Geometric and Non‑Geometric Rough Paths

The distinction between geometric and non‑geometric rough paths lies at the heart of the extension theorem. A geometric rough path of roughness p (1 ≤ p < 3) is a pair X = (X¹, X²) where X¹ is a continuous path of finite p‑variation and X² is a two‑parameter map satisfying Chen’s relation X²s,u = X²s,t + X²t,u + X¹s,t ⊗ X¹t,u and the symmetry condition X²s,t + X²t,s = 0. The symmetry encodes the fact that the second level is the iterated integral of a genuine path, i.e. the Lévy area. Lyons’ extension theorem (1998) states that any geometric rough path of finite p‑variation admits a unique lift to a full rough path in the tensor algebra T⁽⌈p⌉⁾(ℝᵈ) preserving the p‑variation norm. Non‑geometric rough paths relax the symmetry requirement; they arise naturally when the driving signal is a limit of non‑smooth approximations that do not preserve the area, such as the Itô lift of Brownian motion. The Itô lift satisfies Chen’s relation but its second level is not antisymmetric; instead X²s,t + X²t,s = [X¹, X¹]s,t, the quadratic covariation. This asymmetry forces the solution map of an RDE driven by an Itô rough path to differ from the Stratonovich solution by a correction term involving the derivative of the vector fields. In practice, the choice between geometric and non‑geometric lifts determines whether the limiting stochastic integral is interpreted in the Stratonovich or Itô sense. The universal limit theorem holds for both classes, but the solution map is continuous only when the driving rough paths are equipped with the appropriate topology (p‑variation for geometric, a slightly stronger metric for non‑geometric). Understanding this dichotomy is essential when approximating stochastic processes by piecewise linear or mollified paths, because the limit of the approximations may be geometric (Stratonovich) or non‑geometric (Itô) depending on the discretisation scheme.

The Sewing Lemma and Its Generalisations

The sewing lemma, introduced by Gubinelli (2004) in the context of controlled rough paths and independently by Feyel and de La Pradelle (2006), provides a deterministic criterion for defining integrals against irregular signals. Given a two‑parameter map As,t satisfying the approximate additivity condition |As,u − As,t − At,u| ≤ C |u−s|θ with θ > 1, there exists a unique path I_t such that |I_t − I_s − As,t| ≤ C' |t−s|θ. The lemma is the engine behind the definition of the rough integral ∫ Y dX when Y is controlled by X: one sets As,t = Y_s X¹s,t + Y'_s X²s,t and checks the required bound using the p‑variation of X and the regularity of the remainder. The original lemma works for θ = 2/p > 1, i.e. p < 2. For p ≥ 2 the exponent drops to ≤ 1 and the simple sewing lemma fails. Gubinelli’s generalised sewing lemma replaces the single remainder estimate by a family of remainders indexed by the level of the rough path, allowing the construction of integrals for any p < 3. Further extensions by Hairer and Kelly (2015) embed the sewing lemma into the framework of regularity structures, where the model plays the role of A and the reconstruction operator replaces the limit I. In numerical analysis, the sewing lemma underpins the convergence proof of the Euler scheme for RDEs: the local truncation error is exactly the sewing remainder, and the global error inherits the same p‑variation bound. A practical consequence is that any approximation of the driving signal that preserves the algebraic relations (Chen’s relation, symmetry) up to an error of order |t−s|θ with θ > 1 yields a convergent numerical method. This insight guides the design of higher‑order schemes such as the Milstein‑type method for rough paths, where the second level of the rough path is approximated by a discrete Lévy area.

Worked Example: Verifying the Sewing Condition for a Controlled Path
Let X be a geometric rough path of finite p‑variation with 2 ≤ p < 3. Let Y be controlled by X with Gubinelli derivative Y' and remainder R satisfying |Rs,t| ≤ C |t−s|2/p. Define As,t = Y_s X¹s,t + Y'_s X²s,t. Show that A satisfies the sewing condition with exponent θ = 2/p > 1.
We compute the defect Δs,t,u = As,u − As,t − At,u. Using the controlled expansion Y_u = Y_t + Y'_t X¹t,u + Rt,u and Chen’s relation X¹s,u = X¹s,t + X¹t,u, X²s,u = X²s,t + X²t,u + X¹s,t ⊗ X¹t,u, we obtain after cancellation Δs,t,u = Rt,u X¹s,t + Y'_t X¹s,t ⊗ X¹t,u + (Y'_s − Y'_t) X²t,u. The first term is bounded by C |u−t|2/p |t−s|1/p ≤ C |u−s|(2+1)/p = C |u−s|3/p. Since p < 3, 3/p > 1. The second term is bounded by ‖Y'‖∞ |t−s|1/p |u−t|1/p ≤ C |u−s|2/p. The third term uses the regularity of Y': |Y'_s − Y'_t| ≤ C' |t−s|1/p, giving bound C' |t−s|1/p |u−t|2/p ≤ C' |u−s|3/p. All bounds are of order |u−s|θ with θ = min(2/p, 3/p) = 2/p > 1. Hence the sewing lemma applies and the rough integral exists.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to prove existence of a rough integral, first identify the roughness p of the driver. If p < 2, the classical sewing lemma with θ = 2/p suffices. For 2 ≤ p < 3, explicitly write the controlled expansion and verify the generalised sewing estimate; the key is to track the three remainder terms shown in the worked example. Remember that the exponent θ must be strictly greater than 1; this is why the theory stops at p = 3.

Branched Rough Paths and the Butcher Group

Branched rough paths extend the geometric theory to driving signals that do not satisfy the shuffle product relations, such as the Itô lift of Brownian motion or the canonical lift of a Markov chain. The algebraic structure replaces the tensor algebra T((ℝᵈ)) by the Connes–Kreimer Hopf algebra of rooted trees H, whose characters form the Butcher group B. A branched rough path of order N is a map X: Δ_T → B satisfying the multiplicative property Xs,u = Xs,t ⋆ Xt,u (where ⋆ is the convolution product) and a p‑variation bound on each tree component. The Butcher group encodes the combinatorics of Runge–Kutta methods; each tree corresponds to an elementary differential. Lyons and Victoir (2007) proved an extension theorem for branched rough paths: any branched rough path of finite p‑variation admits a unique lift to a full branched rough path in the projective limit of B. The universal limit theorem holds verbatim for RDEs driven by branched rough paths, with the solution expressed as a series over trees. In machine learning, the branched signature (the character of the path in H) provides a feature map that captures non‑commutative and non‑shuffle information, useful for time series where the order of events matters but the underlying noise is not geometric. The log‑signature in the branched setting lives in the Lie algebra of primitive elements of H, giving a reduced representation analogous to the classical log‑signature. Recent work by Chevyrev and Oberhauser (2022) shows that the expected branched signature of a stochastic process characterises its law under mild conditions, opening the door to moment problems for non‑geometric noises.

Regularity Structures: Modelled Distributions and Reconstruction

Hairer’s theory of regularity structures (2014) generalises controlled rough paths to a setting where the “Taylor expansion” is replaced by an abstract model (Π, Γ) acting on a graded vector space T = ⊕α∈A T_α. A modelled distribution f assigns to each point x an element f(x) ∈ T<γ such that the local consistency condition ‖f(x) − Γxy f(y)‖_α ≤ C |x−y|γ−α holds. The reconstruction operator ℛ maps a modelled distribution to a genuine distribution ℛf on the underlying space, satisfying ‖ℛf − Π_x f(x)‖_α ≤ C |x−y|γ. This operator is the analytic heart of the theory; it replaces the sewing lemma and guarantees existence and uniqueness of solutions to singular SPDEs such as the KPZ equation, Φ⁴₃, and the stochastic quantisation equation. The model (Π, Γ) plays the role of the rough path: Π_x τ is the “lift” of the abstract symbol τ at point x, and Γxy encodes the recentering of expansions. For a geometric rough path of order ⌈p⌉, one can build a regularity structure where T is the truncated tensor algebra, Π_x X⊗k = X(k)x,·, and Γxy implements Chen’s relation. In this sense, controlled rough paths are a special case of modelled distributions with a very simple model. The reconstruction theorem is proved by a multiscale argument using wavelets; the key estimate is a bound on the wavelet coefficients of ℛf in terms of the modelled distribution norm. This machinery also yields a robust fixed‑point argument for SPDEs: the solution map is locally Lipschitz in the modelled distribution topology, and the renormalisation procedure corresponds to a continuous transformation of the model (the BPHZ renormalisation). Understanding the interplay between the algebraic structure (the rule for building T), the analytic structure (the model), and the reconstruction operator is essential for tackling exam questions on singular SPDEs.

Numerical Schemes for Rough Differential Equations

Convergent numerical methods for RDEs must respect the algebraic structure of the driving rough path. The simplest scheme is the Euler method: given a partition 0 = t₀ < t₁ < … < t_N = T, set Yti+1 = Yt_i + f(Yt_i) X¹t_i,ti+1 + Df(Yt_i) f(Yt_i) X²t_i,ti+1. Davie (2007) proved that if the discrete driver (X¹, X²) satisfies Chen’s relation and the p‑variation bound uniformly in the mesh size, then the Euler approximations converge to the true solution in q‑variation for any q > p. The order of convergence is 1/p in the p‑variation metric. Higher‑order schemes require approximations of higher‑level iterated integrals. The Milstein‑type scheme for p ∈ [2,3) adds the term ½ D²f(Yt_i)(f(Yt_i), f(Yt_i)) X³t_i,ti+1, where X³ is the third level of the rough path. For p ≥ 3 one needs a full Taylor expansion up to level ⌈p⌉. In practice, the Lévy area X² is not available analytically for most stochastic processes; it must be approximated. The Wong–Zakai approximation uses piecewise linear interpolation of the driving path, which yields a geometric rough path whose second level is the discrete Lévy area (the signed area of the linear interpolation). This approximation converges to the Stratonovich lift. For the Itô lift, one must add a correction term involving the quadratic variation. Recent work by Foster, Lyons, and Oberhauser (2021) develops a “signature‑based” numerical method: the solution is approximated by truncating the signature of the driving path and solving a linear controlled ODE. This method achieves spectral accuracy for smooth vector fields and avoids the explicit computation of higher‑order iterated integrals. Error analysis relies on the continuity of the Itô–Lyons map in the p‑variation topology and on bounds for the truncated signature in terms of the p‑variation norm. When implementing these schemes, one must ensure that the discrete driver satisfies the algebraic constraints (Chen’s relation, symmetry for geometric case) up to machine precision; otherwise the convergence proof breaks down.

  • Confusing the geometric lift (Stratonovich) with the non‑geometric Itô lift; the symmetry of the second level is the decisive test.
  • Applying the classical sewing lemma when p ≥ 2; the exponent θ = 2/p ≤ 1 violates the lemma’s hypothesis.
  • Forgetting that the Gubinelli derivative is not a true derivative but a local approximation coefficient; it need not be the pathwise derivative of Y.
  • Assuming the universal limit theorem holds for any topology; continuity of the Itô–Lyons map requires the p‑variation rough path metric (or a stronger one for non‑geometric paths).
  • Neglecting the algebraic constraints (Chen’s relation, shuffle product) when constructing discrete approximations for numerical schemes; violation leads to divergence.
  • Mixing branched and geometric rough path terminology; the Butcher group replaces the tensor algebra, and the signature lives in the Hopf algebra of trees.
  • Overlooking the role of the reconstruction operator in regularity structures; it is not merely a sewing lemma but a multiscale wavelet argument that yields a genuine distribution.

Higher‑Order Signatures and the Shuffle Algebra

The signature of a path is the infinite sequence of its iterated integrals. For a path X of finite p‑variation with p ∈ [1,2) the first level X¹ is the increment, the second level X² is the Lévy area, and higher levels encode progressively finer algebraic information. The algebraic structure of the signature is governed by the shuffle product: for any two words I, J in the tensor algebra, the product of the corresponding iterated integrals equals the sum over all shuffles of I and J. This identity, S(I) S(J) = Σshuffles S(shuffle(I,J)), holds pathwise for smooth paths and extends by continuity to geometric rough paths. The shuffle algebra is the free commutative algebra on the alphabet of coordinate indices; its dual is the space of linear functionals on the tensor algebra, which can be identified with formal power series. The signature map X ↦ S(X) is injective modulo tree‑like equivalence, a result known as the uniqueness of signature (Hambly–Lyons 2010). In practice, truncating the signature at level N yields a feature vector of dimension Σk=1..N dk for a d‑dimensional path. The shuffle relations imply that the truncated signature lives in a proper subspace of the full tensor space; exploiting this reduces dimensionality and improves statistical efficiency in learning tasks. The Hopf algebra structure (concatenation product, deconcatenation coproduct, antipode) provides a systematic way to compute the signature of a concatenated path: S(X ⊔ Y) = S(X) ⊗ S(Y). This factorisation underpins the “signature kernel” where the inner product of two signatures equals the expected value of the product of their iterated integrals under a suitable measure. Understanding the interplay between the shuffle product, the tensor algebra grading, and the p‑variation bounds is essential for proving convergence of signature‑based numerical schemes and for deriving error estimates in machine‑learning pipelines.

Rough Path Lifts of Stochastic Processes: Stratonovich versus Itô

Every continuous semimartingale admits canonical rough path lifts, but the lift depends on the chosen stochastic integral. The Stratonovich lift yields a geometric rough path: its second level is symmetric and satisfies Chen’s relation exactly. For a d‑dimensional Brownian motion B, the Stratonovich lift BStrat = (B, 𝔸) has 𝔸i,js,t = ∫st (Biu − Bis) ∘ dBju. The symmetry 𝔸i,j = 𝔸j,i follows from the Stratonovich chain rule. The Itô lift, by contrast, is non‑geometric: its second level 𝔸Itô = 𝔸Strat − ½ [Bi, Bj] is antisymmetric in the quadratic variation part. For Brownian motion the correction is ½ δi,j (t−s). The Itô lift satisfies a modified Chen relation with an extra term involving the quadratic variation. In rough path theory the Itô–Lyons map is continuous on the space of geometric rough paths equipped with the p‑variation metric; the Itô lift does not belong to this space for p ≥ 2 because its second level fails the symmetry required for geometricity. However, one can embed the Itô lift into the larger space of branched rough paths (Butcher group) where the algebraic constraints are relaxed. The Wong–Zakai theorem states that piecewise linear approximations of Brownian motion converge in rough path topology to the Stratonovich lift. For the Itô lift one must add the Lévy area correction explicitly. In numerical practice, simulating the Lévy area is the main bottleneck; the Lévy area can be sampled exactly for Brownian motion using the Lévy stochastic area formula, or approximated via the Milstein scheme. The choice of lift determines the limiting solution of a rough differential equation driven by the noise: Stratonovich lift gives the Stratonovich SDE solution, Itô lift gives the Itô SDE solution. This distinction is crucial when translating models from physics (where Stratonovich is natural) to finance (where Itô is standard).

Worked Example: Identifying the Geometric Lift
Given a two‑dimensional Brownian motion (B¹, B²) and its piecewise linear interpolation on a partition of mesh size δ, compute the discrete second level 𝔸1,2tₖ,tₖ₊₁ and show that it converges to the Stratonovich Lévy area.
For the linear interpolation on [tₖ, tₖ₊₁], the path is a straight line from Btₖ to Btₖ₊₁. The iterated integral ∫tₖtₖ₊₁ (B¹u−B¹tₖ) dB²u equals ½ ΔB¹ ΔB². The discrete Lévy area is the antisymmetric part: 𝔸1,2 = ½(ΔB¹ ΔB² − ΔB² ΔB¹). This is the signed area of the triangle with vertices (0,0), (ΔB¹,0), (ΔB¹,ΔB²) — generally non‑zero. As δ → 0, these discrete areas converge in probability (in p‑variation rough path metric for any p > 2) to the Stratonovich Lévy area ∫ B¹ ∘ dB² − ∫ B² ∘ dB¹. The limit is symmetric and satisfies Chen’s relation, confirming that the Wong–Zakai approximation yields the geometric (Stratonovich) rough path.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to distinguish between Stratonovich and Itô lifts, first check the symmetry of the second level. If the second level is symmetric, the lift is geometric (Stratonovich). If it contains an explicit quadratic‑variation correction term, it is the Itô lift. Remember that the Wong–Zakai theorem only guarantees convergence to the Stratonovich lift; any scheme that claims to converge to the Itô solution must incorporate the correction ½ [X, X] explicitly.

Lyons' Extension Theorem and Uniqueness of Geometric Rough Paths

Lyons' extension theorem states that a geometric rough path of order ⌈p⌉ is uniquely determined by its truncated signature up to level ⌈p⌉. More precisely, if two geometric rough paths X and Y have identical levels 1,…,⌈p⌉, then they coincide as elements of the rough path space. The proof uses the fact that the signature of a geometric rough path satisfies the shuffle relations, which allow reconstruction of higher levels from lower ones via the shuffle algebra. For p ∈ [2,3) the second level (Lévy area) together with the first level determines the third level uniquely because the third level can be expressed as a shuffle product of the first and second levels. In general, the tensor algebra truncated at level ⌈p⌉ carries a Hopf algebra structure; the set of group‑like elements (those satisfying the shuffle relations) forms the free nilpotent Lie group of step ⌈p⌉. The extension theorem identifies the space of geometric p‑rough paths with this Lie group equipped with the Carnot–Carathéodory metric induced by the p‑variation norm. Uniqueness fails for non‑geometric rough paths: the Itô lift of Brownian motion has the same first level as the Stratonovich lift but a different second level, and there is no algebraic rule to recover the Itô second level from the first level alone. This is why the Itô lift lives in the larger branched rough path space where the algebraic constraints are relaxed. The extension theorem underpins the continuity of the Itô–Lyons map: the solution map of an RDE depends only on the truncated signature up to level ⌈p⌉, and small perturbations in the p‑variation metric of the driving rough path produce small changes in the solution. In numerical analysis, the theorem justifies truncating the signature at level ⌈p⌉ when implementing signature‑based schemes; higher levels are algebraically determined and need not be simulated independently.

Signature Kernel Methods in Machine Learning

The signature kernel is a positive‑definite kernel on the space of paths defined by the inner product of their signatures: k(X, Y) = ⟨S(X), S(Y)⟩ℋ, where ℋ is the Hilbert space completion of the tensor algebra with respect to a suitable weighting (e.g., factorial decay). Because the signature is a universal feature map for paths modulo tree‑like equivalence, the signature kernel induces a metric that captures the full geometric information of the path. In practice the infinite‑dimensional inner product is approximated by truncating at level N and using the shuffle product to compute the kernel efficiently via dynamic programming (the “signature kernel trick”). The computational complexity is O(N d²) per pair of paths, where d is the dimension. The kernel can be combined with standard kernel methods (SVM, Gaussian processes, kernel ridge regression) for classification and regression on time‑series data. Recent work shows that the signature kernel is characteristic: the embedding of the law of a stochastic process into the reproducing kernel Hilbert space is injective, enabling two‑sample testing and maximum mean discrepancy estimation. For irregularly sampled or noisy data, one first constructs a rough path lift (e.g., via linear interpolation or a learned lift) and then computes the signature. The choice of lift influences the kernel; the Stratonovich lift yields a geometric kernel, while the Itô lift requires a branched kernel. Regularisation is typically achieved by weighting higher levels with factorial decay 1/k! or by adding a diagonal term. The signature kernel has been applied to healthcare time series, financial tick data, and human activity recognition, often outperforming handcrafted features and recurrent neural networks when data are scarce.

Rough Path Estimates for Fractional Brownian Motion

Fractional Brownian motion (fBm) with Hurst parameter H ∈ (0,1) is a Gaussian process with stationary increments and covariance E[BHt BHs] = ½ (|t|2H + |s|2H − |t−s|2H). For H > ¼ the sample paths have finite p‑variation for any p > 1/H, and a canonical geometric rough path lift exists. The lift is constructed via the Stratonovich‑type integral ∫ BH ∘ dBH, which can be defined as the limit of Riemann sums because the integrand has sufficient regularity (Young integration for H > ½, rough integration for H ∈ (¼, ½]). The second level (Lévy area) is well‑defined as a limit of compensated Riemann sums and satisfies the required p‑variation bounds. For H ≤ ¼ the p‑variation explodes and no rough path lift exists in the classical sense; one must resort to regularity structures or paracontrolled distributions. The Lyons–Victoir extension theorem provides an explicit construction of the geometric rough path for fBm with H > ¼ using the Gaussian rough path framework: the covariance of the lifted process is determined by the covariance of fBm and the Lévy area covariance, which can be expressed in terms of the Hurst parameter. The p‑variation norm of the fBm rough path scales like TH for the first level and T2H for the second level. These scaling relations are crucial for proving well‑posedness of RDEs driven by fBm and for deriving error bounds in numerical schemes. In particular, the Euler scheme for RDEs driven by fBm converges with order H in the p‑variation metric when H > ⅓. For H ∈ (¼, ⅓] one needs a second‑order scheme that includes the Lévy area. The Lévy area of fBm can be simulated exactly using the Cholesky decomposition of its covariance matrix on a dyadic grid, or approximated via the Fourier series representation. Understanding the interplay between the Hurst parameter, the p‑variation regularity, and the algebraic level required for the rough path lift is a standard exam topic.

  • Confusing the shuffle product with the concatenation product; the shuffle product is commutative and corresponds to the product of iterated integrals, while concatenation is non‑commutative and corresponds to path concatenation.
  • Assuming the Itô lift is geometric; its second level is not symmetric, so it lives in the branched rough path space, not the geometric group.
  • Forgetting that Lyons' extension theorem only holds for geometric rough paths; non‑geometric lifts require additional data beyond the truncated signature.
  • Using the signature kernel without truncation or weighting, leading to infinite‑dimensional feature vectors and numerical overflow.
  • Applying Young integration bounds to fractional Brownian motion with H ≤ ½; Young theory requires H > ½, while rough path theory covers H > ¼.
  • Neglecting the quadratic‑variation correction when converting a Stratonovich RDE to an Itô RDE; the correction term is ½ Df(Y) f(Y) d[X, X].
  • Believing that the Wong–Zakai approximation converges to the Itô lift; it converges to the Stratonovich lift unless an explicit correction is added.

Rough Differential Equations on Manifolds and Geometric Integration

When the solution of a rough differential equation (RDE) takes values in a smooth manifold M rather than a vector space, the classical vector‑space formulation must be replaced by a geometric one. The driving rough path X still lives in a tensor algebra over ℝd, but the vector fields Vi become sections of the tangent bundle TM. The solution is defined as a rough path Y in the manifold‑valued sense introduced by Cass, Litterer and Lyons (2007) and independently by Davie (2007). The key idea is to lift the vector fields to the frame bundle FM and solve the RDE there; the projection onto M then yields the manifold‑valued solution. This construction preserves the geometric nature of the Stratonovich integral: if X is the Stratonovich lift of a semimartingale, the solution coincides with the Stratonovich SDE on M. For the Itô lift, a correction term involving the curvature of the connection appears, exactly as in the classical Itô–Stratonovich conversion on manifolds.

The Lyons–Victoir extension theorem guarantees that any geometric rough path of finite p‑variation with p∈[2,3) can be extended to a full geometric rough path, which is essential for defining the solution on the frame bundle. The solution map X↦Y is locally Lipschitz in the p‑variation topology, a result proved by Friz and Victoir (2010) using controlled rough paths on manifolds. Numerical integration on manifolds benefits from this geometric viewpoint: the Euler–Heun scheme on the frame bundle, projected back via the exponential map, respects the manifold structure and achieves the same convergence order as in the Euclidean case. Applications include stochastic filtering on Lie groups, attitude estimation for rigid bodies, and the simulation of molecular dynamics with rough noise.

Rough Volatility Models and the Rough Bergomi Process

Rough volatility emerged from the empirical observation that the implied volatility surface of equity options exhibits a power‑law behaviour at short maturities, with an exponent close to 0.1. Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum (2014) proposed the rough Bergomi model, where the instantaneous variance vt is driven by a fractional Brownian motion (fBm) BH with Hurst parameter H≈0.1. The variance process is defined as vt=ξ0(t) exp( η ∫0t (t−s)H−½ dBHs − ½ η2 t2H ), where the integral is a Wiener integral with respect to fBm. Because H<½, the fBm paths have finite p‑variation for any p>1/H, and a canonical geometric rough path lift exists for H>¼. The rough path framework allows one to define the stochastic integral ∫ Y dX for the log‑price process Y driven by the rough volatility path X = (BH, 𝔸), where 𝔸 is the Lévy area of the fBm. The resulting rough differential equation for the log‑price is dYt = −½ vt dt + √vt ( ρ dBHt + √(1−ρ2) dWt ), where W is an independent Brownian motion. The rough path lift of the joint driver (BH, W) is constructed via the Gaussian rough path theory of Friz and Victoir (2010). Calibration of the rough Bergomi model to market data uses the signature of the volatility path: the expected signature of the fBm lift can be computed analytically via the covariance structure, enabling fast pricing through the signature‑based expansion of the solution. Recent extensions include the rough Heston model (El Euch and Rosenbaum, 2019) where the variance follows a rough CIR process, and multi‑factor rough volatility models that capture the term structure of the volatility skew.

Rough Partial Differential Equations Driven by Spatial Rough Noise

Rough path theory extends naturally to partial differential equations where the noise is rough in the spatial variable. Consider the stochastic heat equation on the torus 𝕋d: ∂tu = Δu + f(u) + g(u) ξ, where ξ is a Gaussian noise white in time and with spatial covariance given by a kernel Q(x,y) that is sufficiently regular to admit a geometric rough path lift in the spatial direction. Deya, Gubinelli and Tindel (2012) developed a theory of rough PDEs by treating the spatial variable as a parameter and applying the sewing lemma in the Banach space of spatial functions. The solution is constructed as a controlled rough path in the spatial variable: for each fixed time t, the map x↦u(t,x) is a controlled rough path with Gubinelli derivative ∂xu. The key estimate is a commutator bound of the form ‖u(t,·)‖𝒞α ≤ C ‖ξ‖𝒞α−1, where α is the spatial regularity exponent of the noise. When the noise is a fractional Brownian field with Hurst index H>½ in space, the equation can be solved pathwise using Young integration; for H∈(⅓,½] one needs the full rough path machinery. The theory also covers the stochastic wave equation and the KPZ equation in the rough regime, providing an alternative to regularity structures that stays within the controlled rough path framework. Numerical schemes for such rough PDEs combine a finite‑element discretisation in space with a rough path integrator in time; the convergence rate depends on the spatial regularity of the noise and the order of the rough integrator.

Higher‑Order Numerical Schemes for Rough Differential Equations: Milstein and Beyond

The Euler scheme for an RDE dY = V(Y) dX with p‑variation p∈[2,3) converges with order 1/p in the p‑variation metric. To achieve higher order one must incorporate the Lévy area of the driving rough path. The Milstein‑type scheme introduced by Davie (2007) and analysed by Friz and Victoir (2010) reads Ytk+1 = Ytk + V(Ytk) Xtk,tk+1 + ½ DV(Ytk) V(Ytk) 𝔸tk,tk+1, where 𝔸 is the second level of the rough path. This scheme achieves order 2/p for p∈[2,3). For p∈[3,4) one needs the third level of the rough path and the corresponding third‑order term involving the shuffle product of vector fields. The general higher‑order scheme is built from the truncated signature of the driving rough path and the iterated Lie brackets of the vector fields, exactly as in the classical Taylor expansion for ODEs. The Lyons–Victoir extension theorem provides a way to construct a geometric rough path with prescribed truncated signature, which is used to simulate the higher levels when only the first level is available (e.g., for fractional Brownian motion). The computational cost grows factorially with the order, but for low dimensions and moderate order (up to 3) the schemes are practical. Error analysis uses the sewing lemma to bound the local truncation error in terms of the p‑variation of the remainder. Recent work by Bayer, Friz and Riedel (2018) shows that a randomised Milstein scheme, where the Lévy area is approximated by a suitable random variable with the correct first two moments, retains the same strong convergence order while avoiding the exact simulation of the area.

Gaussian Rough Paths Beyond Fractional Brownian Motion

The theory of Gaussian rough paths, developed by Friz and Victoir (2010) and extended by Cass, Friz and others, provides a canonical lift for any centred Gaussian process X whose covariance R(s,t) satisfies a 2D ρ‑variation condition with ρ∈[1,2). The condition is ‖R‖ρ‑var;[0,T]2 = supΠ (∑[u,v]∈Π |R(v,v)−R(v,u)−R(u,v)+R(u,u)|ρ)1/ρ < ∞. When this holds, the Lévy area 𝔸s,t can be defined as the L2 limit of compensated Riemann sums, and the pair X = (X, 𝔸) is a geometric rough path of finite p‑variation for any p>2ρ. Examples include Brownian motion (ρ=1), fractional Brownian motion with H>¼ (ρ=1/(2H)), the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, and the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with H>¼. The covariance of the Lévy area is given by the Lévy‑area covariance formula Cov(𝔸s,t, 𝔸u,v) = ½ ∫st∫uv ( ∂1R ∂2R − ∂2R ∂1R ) dx dy, where the derivatives are understood in the distributional sense. The Lyons–Victoir extension theorem then yields a full geometric rough path from the second‑level data. This framework is essential for solving RDEs driven by Gaussian signals that are not semimartingales, and for statistical inference on the signature of Gaussian processes, where the expected signature can be expressed in terms of the covariance kernel.

Rough Paths and Malliavin Calculus: Integration by Parts and Density Estimates

The synthesis of rough path theory with Malliavin calculus, developed by researchers including Cass, Friz, and Hairer, provides a framework for proving regularity of probability laws of solutions to rough differential equations. For a Gaussian rough path X = (X, 𝔸) with Cameron–Martin space H, the solution map Φ : X ↦ YT is Malliavin differentiable. The Malliavin derivative DYT satisfies a linear rough differential equation driven by the same rough path, with coefficients given by the derivatives of the vector fields V. The integration by parts formula takes the form E[∂if(YT)H] = E[f(YT)δ(ui)] where δ is the Skorokhod integral and ui solves the adjoint equation. This yields the Bouleau–Hirsch criterion: if the Malliavin covariance matrix σT = ⟨DYT, DYT⟩H is almost surely invertible, then YT has a smooth density. For fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter H > 1/4, a Norris-type lemma for rough paths gives quantitative lower bounds on the smallest eigenvalue of σT, leading to Gaussian-type heat kernel estimates for the transition density. The key technical tool is the controlled rough path structure of the Malliavin derivative, which allows the sewing lemma to be applied pathwise to the linearised equation.

Rough Paths and Optimal Transport: Wasserstein Geometry of Rough Path Spaces

The space of geometric rough paths GΩp(ℝd) equipped with the p-variation metric is a complete metric space but not a length space. Research by Bonnier, Oberhauser, and others develops optimal transport geometry on the space of laws of rough paths. For two probability measures μ, ν on GΩp(ℝd), the Wasserstein-p distance Wp(μ, ν) is defined via couplings of the underlying rough paths. A fundamental result is that the signature map S : GΩp(ℝd) → T((ℝd)) is a homeomorphism onto its image, and the pushforward measures S#μ, S#ν on the tensor algebra inherit a natural optimal transport structure. The signature kernel k(X, Y) = ⟨S(X), S(Y)⟩ defines a maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) that metrizes weak convergence of laws on the rough path space. For Gaussian rough paths, the expected signature E[S(X)] can be computed explicitly from the covariance kernel, and the MMD between two Gaussian rough path laws reduces to a Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the difference of their covariance operators. This framework enables statistical two-sample testing for rough path data and provides a geometric interpretation of the signature kernel as a distance on the space of probability measures.

Worked Problem: Malliavin Covariance for a Linear RDE
Let X be a geometric rough path lift of a d-dimensional Brownian motion. Consider the linear RDE dY = A Y dX, Y0 = y0 ∈ ℝe, where A ∈ ℝe×e×d is a constant tensor. Compute the Malliavin derivative DYT and the Malliavin covariance matrix σT. Under what condition on A is σT almost surely invertible?
The solution is YT = S(X) y0 where S(X) is the signature of X acting via the linear vector fields. The Malliavin derivative DsYT satisfies the linear RDE d(DsY) = A (DsY) dX with initial condition DsYs = A Ys. By the flow property, DsYT = S(X)s,T A Ys where S(X)s,T is the signature from s to T. The Malliavin covariance matrix is σT = ∫0T DsYT ⊗ DsYT ds = ∫0T S(X)s,T A Ys Ys⊤ A⊤ S(X)s,T⊤ ds. Since S(X)s,T is invertible (it lies in the general linear group), σT is almost surely invertible if and only if the matrix ∫0T A Ys Ys⊤ A⊤ ds is almost surely invertible. By the support theorem for Brownian rough paths, this holds if the Lie algebra generated by the vector fields {A1, …, Ad} spans ℝe at y0 (Hörmander's condition).

Rough Paths in Nonlinear Filtering: The Rough Zakai Equation

Nonlinear filtering with rough observation noise leads to the rough Zakai equation, an infinite-dimensional rough differential equation on the space of unnormalised conditional densities. Let the signal process X satisfy dX = b(X) dt + σ(X) dW and the observation process Y be given by dY = h(X) dt + dZ, where Z is a rough path with p-variation p ∈ [2,3). The unnormalised conditional density ρt satisfies the rough Zakai equation dρ = ℒ*ρ dt + ρ h⊤ dY, where ℒ* is the adjoint of the signal generator. This is an RDE in the Banach space of distributions, driven by the rough path Y. The solution theory uses the controlled rough path framework in the dual of a nuclear Fréchet space. The key estimate is the sewing lemma in the space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators, which requires the observation function h to be sufficiently regular. The normalised filter πt(φ) = ρt(φ) / ρt(1) satisfies the rough Kushner–Stratonovich equation, a nonlinear RDE. For fractional Brownian motion observations with H > 1/2, the filter is well-posed and the innovation process is a rough path. The particle filter approximation for rough observations uses the rough path lift of the empirical measure process, and the convergence rate depends on the p-variation of the driving noise.

Rough Paths and Optimal Control: The Pontryagin Maximum Principle for RDEs

The optimal control of systems driven by rough paths extends the classical Pontryagin maximum principle to the rough setting. Consider the controlled RDE dY = V0(Y) dt + ∑i=1d Vi(Y) dXi + ∑j=1m Bj(Y) uj dt, where X is a given rough path and u is the control. The cost functional is J(u) = ∫0T L(Yt, ut) dt + Ψ(YT). The Hamiltonian is H(y, p, u) = ⟨p, V0(y) + ∑ Bj(y) uj⟩ + L(y, u). The adjoint process P satisfies the backward rough differential equation dP = −∂yH(Y, P, u) dt − ∑ ∂yVi(Y)⊤ P dXi, with terminal condition PT = ∂Ψ(YT). This is a linear RDE for P driven by the same rough path X. The maximum principle states that for an optimal control u*, the Hamiltonian is maximised pointwise: H(Yt, Pt, u*t) = supu H(Yt, Pt, u). The proof uses the needle variation technique adapted to rough paths: the variation of the state is a controlled rough path, and the sewing lemma controls the remainder terms. For geometric rough paths, the adjoint equation preserves the geometric structure, and the maximum principle holds without correction terms. For non-geometric (branched) rough paths, additional terms appear corresponding to the area anomaly.

Rough Paths and Quantum Stochastic Calculus: Hudson–Parthasarathy Equations as RDEs

The Hudson–Parthasarathy quantum stochastic calculus, which describes the evolution of open quantum systems, can be formulated as a rough differential equation on the Fock space. The quantum stochastic integrals ∫ L dA†, ∫ M dA, ∫ N dΛ are driven by the fundamental quantum noises: the creation process A†, the annihilation process A, and the gauge process Λ. These processes, when restricted to the vacuum state, have sample paths that are rough paths of finite p-variation for p > 2. The quantum Itô table dA dA† = dt, dΛ dA† = dA†, etc., corresponds to the shuffle product structure of the signature of the driving rough path. The Hudson–Parthasarathy equation dU = {L dA† − L† dA − (½ L†L + iH) dt} U is an RDE on the unitary group of the Fock space, driven by the rough path X = (A, A†, Λ). The solution Ut is the quantum stochastic exponential, which coincides with the signature of X evaluated on the Lie algebra of the unitary group. The quantum filtering equation (Belavkin equation) for the conditional state given homodyne detection observations is a nonlinear RDE on the space of density matrices. The rough path formulation provides a pathwise solution theory that does not rely on the vacuum expectation, enabling the study of quantum trajectories for non-Gaussian states. The signature of the quantum noise rough path encodes all multi-time correlation functions, and the expected signature gives the vacuum expectation values of time-ordered products.

Rough Paths and Large Deviations: The Freidlin–Wentzell Theory for Rough Differential Equations

The classical Freidlin–Wentzell theory establishes large deviation principles (LDPs) for solutions of stochastic differential equations driven by Brownian motion, with the rate function given by the action functional of the associated deterministic control problem. In the rough path setting, this extends to LDPs for the solution map of RDEs driven by Gaussian rough paths, most notably fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter H > 1/4. The key insight is that the Itô–Lyons solution map Φ : X ↦ Y is continuous from the rough path space (with p-variation topology, p ∈ [2, 3)) to the solution space. When the driving noise is scaled as εX, the family {Y^ε} satisfies an LDP with speed ε² and good rate function I(φ) = ½ ‖h‖²H where φ = Φ(h) for h in the Cameron–Martin space H of the Gaussian rough path, and I(φ) = ∞ otherwise. For fractional Brownian motion with H > 1/2, the Cameron–Martin space consists of functions whose fractional derivative of order H + ½ lies in L², and the rough path lift of h is the canonical geometric lift. The proof uses the contraction principle: the map ε ↦ εX satisfies an LDP in the rough path space (Friz and Victoir, 2010), and continuity of Φ transfers it to the solution. For H ≤ 1/2, the situation is more delicate because the Cameron–Martin space does not embed into the space of geometric rough paths; one must work with the weaker topology of the rough path metric or use the Lyons–Victoir extension theorem to lift Cameron–Martin paths. The rate function for the rough path LDP is I(X) = ½ ‖X‖²H where ‖·‖H is the reproducing kernel Hilbert space norm of the Gaussian rough path. This theory underpins importance sampling for rare events in rough volatility models and provides the mathematical foundation for the Freidlin–Wentzell exit problem for RDEs: the expected exit time from a domain D scales as exp(infφ∈∂D I(φ)/ε²).

Rough Paths and Statistical Inference: Parameter Estimation for Rough Differential Equations

Statistical inference for RDEs addresses the problem of estimating the vector fields V = (V₀, V₁, …, V_d) from discrete observations of the solution Y_t. Unlike classical SDEs where the likelihood is often intractable, the pathwise nature of RDEs enables likelihood-free methods based on the signature. The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) for the drift parameter in dY = V₀(Y)dt + ∑ V_i(Y)dX^i, when X is a known rough path, can be derived by discretising the RDE via an Euler scheme. For a parametric family V^θ, the contrast function is the p-variation distance between the observed path and the simulated path: θ̂_n = argmin_θ ‖Y^obs − Y^θ‖p-var. Under identifiability conditions (the map θ ↦ V^θ is injective modulo the flow) and regularity of V^θ, the estimator is typically consistent and asymptotically normal; the rate is n1/2 for p < 2 but deteriorates to n1/p for p ≥ 2 due to the roughness of the driver. A more robust approach uses the expected signature: the truncated signature S^N(Y) = (1, ∫ dY, ∫ dY ⊗ dY, …) up to level N provides a feature map that linearises the RDE. The moment equations E[S^N(Y)] = Ψ(θ) can be solved by generalised method of moments (GMM). For fractional Brownian motion with unknown Hurst parameter H, the quadratic variation of the area process ∫ Y ⊗ dY identifies H through the scaling relation E[|𝔸s,t|²] ∼ |t−s|4H. The Fisher information matrix for θ involves the Malliavin derivative of the solution with respect to the driving noise, which exists for Gaussian rough paths and can be computed via the linearised RDE for the Jacobian J_t = ∂Y_t/∂θ. Bayesian inference often approximates the rough path likelihood via an Euler–Maruyama scheme on the lifted path, regularising the prior with the p-variation norm of the residual. Recent work shows that signature-based estimators can achieve parametric rates even when the observation frequency is low, because the signature captures the entire trajectory information.

Worked Example: Identifiability in a Linear RDE
Consider the linear RDE dY_t = A Y_t dt + B Y_t dX_t on ℝ², where X is a fixed geometric rough path with non-trivial area, and A, B ∈ ℝ2×2 are unknown. Suppose we observe Y at times t_k = kΔ for k = 0,…,n. Show that (A, B) is identifiable from the signature of Y up to level 2 if and only if the Lie algebra generated by {A, B} acts transitively on ℝ².
The solution is Y_t = Φ_t Y0 where Φ_t solves dΦ = A Φ dt + B Φ dX, Φ0 = I. The level-2 signature of Y is S2(Y)0,t = (Y_t − Y0, ∫0^t (Y_s − Y0) ⊗ dY_s). Using the RDE, dY = A Y dt + B Y dX, so the second level is ∫ (Y_s − Y0) ⊗ (A Y_s ds + B Y_s dX_s). For small t, Y_s ≈ Y0 + A Y0 s + B Y0 X_s. Substituting and keeping terms up to order t² and area 𝔸0,t = ∫0^t X_s ⊗ dX_s, we find the signature depends on A, B, and the commutator [A, B] = AB − BA through the area term. Specifically, the coefficient of 𝔸0,t in the second level is Y0 ⊗ (B² Y0) + (B Y0) ⊗ (A Y0) + … which involves B² and BA. If the Lie algebra 𝔤 = Lie{A, B} is not transitive, there exists a non-zero vector v such that v^⊤ M = 0 for all M ∈ 𝔤. Then v^⊤ Y_t is deterministic (no noise dependence), and the signature cannot distinguish different B that agree on the quotient. Conversely, if 𝔤 acts transitively, the Chen–Fliess series for Φ_t shows that the signature coefficients span the tensor algebra generated by A and B, which separates parameters by the Chow–Rashevskii theorem for rough paths. Hence identifiability holds iff Lie{A, B} = 𝔤𝔩(2) or a transitive subalgebra.

Rough Paths and Neural Controlled Differential Equations: The Continuous-Time Limit of Deep Residual Networks

Neural Controlled Differential Equations (Neural CDEs) provide a continuous-time formulation of deep residual networks, where the hidden state evolves according to dZ_t = f_θ(Z_t) dX_t, with X_t being the input path (e.g., time-augmented data) and f_θ a neural network. This is precisely an RDE driven by the input path X, where the vector fields are parameterised by θ. The solution at time T, Z_T = Φ_θ(X), defines the model output. The adjoint method for gradient computation in Neural CDEs mirrors the rough path Pontryagin maximum principle: the gradient ∂L/∂θ is obtained by solving the backward RDE for the adjoint state a_t = ∂L/∂Z_t, with da = −(∂f_θ/∂z)^⊤ a dX, a_T = ∂L/∂Z_T. This is a linear RDE driven by the same input path X, and its solution exists pathwise for any X of finite p-variation. The signature of the input path provides a natural feature map: by the universal nonlinearity property, any continuous path-to-path functional can be approximated by a linear functional on the signature. Neural CDEs exploit this by learning the linear functional on the signature implicitly through the vector field f_θ. The depth of the equivalent discrete network corresponds to the number of steps in a numerical scheme for the RDE (e.g., Euler or log-ODE method). For irregular inputs (missing data, irregular sampling), the rough path lift of the input (e.g., linear interpolation lifted to level 2) provides a canonical continuous-time model, and the Neural CDE solution is robust to the mesh size. The memory cost is O(1) via the adjoint method, unlike RNNs which require O(T) memory for backpropagation through time. Theoretical guarantees suggest that if f_θ is Lip-γ with γ > p−1, the Itô–Lyons map is locally Lipschitz, and generalisation error typically scales with the p-variation of the input paths. The signature kernel k(X, Y) = ⟨S(X), S(Y)⟩ provides a positive definite kernel on path space, and Neural CDEs can be viewed as learning in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space of the signature kernel with a data-dependent feature map.

Rough Paths and Homogenisation: Averaging Principles for Multiscale Rough Differential Equations

Homogenisation for RDEs studies the limit ε → 0 of the slow component Y^ε in the multiscale system dY^ε = (1/ε) V₀(Y^ε, Z^ε) dt + ∑ V_i(Y^ε, Z^ε) dX^i_t, dZ^ε = (1/ε²) W₀(Y^ε, Z^ε) dt + (1/ε) ∑ W_j(Y^ε, Z^ε) dX^j_t, where X is a rough path and the fast process Z^ε is ergodic for each fixed y. The classical Freidlin–Wentzell averaging for SDEs extends to the rough setting under the assumption that the fast vector fields W_j satisfy the Hörmander condition uniformly in y, ensuring that the fast process has a unique invariant measure μ_y for each y. The averaged vector fields are V̄_i(y) = ∫ V_i(y, z) μ_y(dz). A key technical tool is a rough path analogue of the Khasminskii time-discretisation lemma: on time intervals of length δ = ε^α (α ∈ (0, 1)), the fast process equilibrates, and the slow process is approximated by the averaged RDE dȲ = ∑ V̄_i(Ȳ) dX^i. The error estimate ‖Y^ε − Ȳ‖p-var ≤ C ε^β holds for some β > 0 depending on the mixing rate of the fast process and the p-variation of X. For Gaussian rough drivers (e.g., fBM), the mixing rate relates to the spectral gap of the fast diffusion's generator, yielding convergence rates β that depend on H and p. The proof uses the sewing lemma to control the remainder terms arising from the rough integral, and the Lyons–Victoir extension theorem to lift the averaged vector fields to a geometric rough path when the original driver is geometric. If the fast process is driven by a different rough path X' independent of X, the cross-variation terms vanish in the limit, and the averaged equation is driven by X alone. For non-geometric drivers, the area anomaly in the fast process contributes a correction term to the averaged drift, analogous to the Itô–Stratonovich correction in SDE homogenisation. This theory underpins model reduction for rough volatility models where the volatility process has multiple time scales.

Rough Paths and Topological Data Analysis: Persistent Homology of Signature Paths

The signature of a path X, S(X) = (1, X1, X2, …), lives in the tensor algebra T((ℝ^d)). The truncated signature S^N(X) up to level N provides a finite-dimensional feature map that is invariant under time reparameterisation and tree-like equivalence. Topological Data Analysis (TDA) applies persistent homology to the filtration of sublevel sets of the signature norm or to the point cloud of signatures from a dataset of paths. The key result is that the signature map X ↦ S^N(X) is injective modulo tree-like pieces (Hambly–Lyons, 2010), so the persistent homology of the signature point cloud captures the topological structure of the path space modulo tree-like equivalence. For a dataset of paths {X_i}, the Vietoris–Rips complex built on the signature distance d_N(X, Y) = ‖S^N(X) − S^N(Y)‖ has persistent homology barcodes that are stable under the p-variation metric: if ‖X − Y‖p-var < ε, then the bottleneck distance between barcodes is bounded by C_N ε. Such stability results enable statistical inference on path spaces. The expected signature E[S(X)] for a stochastic process X is the moment generating functional of the process, and its logarithm (the log-signature) lies in the free Lie algebra. The persistent homology of the log-signature point cloud detects non-Gaussianity: for Gaussian rough paths, the log-signature is determined by the first two levels (mean and covariance), so higher-level persistent homology is trivial. For non-Gaussian processes (e.g., rough volatility, Lévy-driven RDEs), non-trivial bars in degree ≥ 2 appear, corresponding to higher-order cumulants. The signature kernel k(X, Y) = ⟨S(X), S(Y)⟩ induces a metric d_k(X, Y) = √(k(X,X) + k(Y,Y) − 2k(X,Y)) that is a Hilbertian metric, and the persistent homology of the kernel distance filtration is computable via the Nyström approximation. Applications include classification of EEG signals (often modeled with p ≈ 2.5), anomaly detection in financial tick data, and shape recognition in computer vision where the path is the boundary curve.

Rough Paths and Banach‑Space Valued Drivers: Integration and RDEs in Infinite Dimensions

When the driving signal takes values in a separable Banach space B, the definition of p-variation and the construction of rough integrals require only minor modifications of the finite‑dimensional theory. A path X : [0, T] → B has finite p-variation if

‖X‖p‑var;[0,T] = supΠ ( Σ[u,v]∈Π ‖Xv − Xu‖Bp )1/p < ∞ .

The tensor algebra is replaced by the projective tensor product B⊗k, and the signature lives in the completed tensor algebra T((B)). Controlled rough paths are defined analogously: a path Y is controlled by X if there exists a Gubinelli derivative Y′ with values in L(B, V) such that the remainder RYs,t = Yt − Ys − Y′s(Xt − Xs) satisfies a 2/p‑variation bound in V. The sewing lemma extends verbatim because it relies only on the triangle inequality and the p-variation norm, both valid in Banach spaces.

For a rough differential equation dY = V(Y) dX with V : V → L(B, V) of class C⌈p⌉+1, the solution map (Itô–Lyons map) is locally Lipschitz from the p-variation rough path metric on X to the p-variation metric on Y. This yields existence and uniqueness for RDEs driven by cylindrical Brownian motion, fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H > 1/3, or more general Gaussian rough paths in Hilbert spaces. The theory underpins the well‑posedness of stochastic PDEs written as RDEs in infinite dimensions, for instance the stochastic heat equation with multiplicative noise or the 2D stochastic Navier–Stokes equations driven by rough noise. A key technical point is that the vector fields must be sufficiently regular in the Fréchet sense; the required number of derivatives grows with p exactly as in the finite‑dimensional case.

Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Sheets: Multi‑Parameter Extensions

A rough sheet is a map X : [0, T]2 → ℝd (or a Banach space) equipped with iterated integrals over rectangles. For a rectangle R = [s1, t1] × [s2, t2] the second level is defined as

𝕏R = ∫R (Xu,v − Xs1,v − Xu,s2 + Xs1,s2) ⊗ dXu,v .

Chen’s identity generalises to an additive property over partitions of rectangles: if a rectangle is split horizontally or vertically, the signature of the whole equals the tensor product of the signatures of the pieces. The p-variation of a sheet is measured by a double sum over partitions in both parameters, and the sewing lemma holds for two‑parameter integrands provided the remainder satisfies a suitable 2D p-variation bound. The signature of a rough sheet takes values in the tensor algebra of ℝd and characterises the sheet up to tree‑like equivalence in each parameter separately.

Applications appear in the analysis of stochastic PDEs driven by space‑time white noise, where the noise is naturally a rough sheet with spatial regularity Hx < 1/2 and temporal regularity Ht < 1/2. The rough sheet framework also provides a pathwise formulation of the stochastic heat equation with multiplicative noise, avoiding the need for Itô calculus in the spatial variable. Recent work extends the Lyons–Victoir extension theorem to sheets, showing that any geometric rough sheet of finite p-variation can be lifted to a full geometric rough path in the sense of the tensor algebra over the product parameter set.

Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Differential Equations with Non‑Lipschitz Drift: Existence, Uniqueness, and Regularisation by Noise

Classical RDE theory requires the vector fields Vi to be Lipschitz (or C⌈p⌉+1) to guarantee a unique solution via Picard iteration in the space of controlled paths. When the drift is merely bounded and measurable, the deterministic ODE may have multiple solutions. However, the addition of a rough noise can restore well‑posedness — a phenomenon known as regularisation by noise. In the rough path setting, Catellier and Gubinelli proved that if the driving rough path X is a geometric p‑rough path with p ∈ [2,3) and the drift b : ℝd → ℝd belongs to the Besov space Bα∞,∞ with α > 1 − 2/p, then the RDE dY = b(Y) dt + σ(Y) dX has a unique solution for every initial condition, provided σ is sufficiently regular and non‑degenerate.

The proof uses a Zvonkin‑type transformation: one solves the Poisson equation L u = −b where L is the second‑order differential operator associated with the noise covariance. The transformed equation has a Lipschitz drift, and the rough integration theory applies. The required regularity α > 1 − 2/p matches the scaling of the rough driver: for Brownian motion (p = 2) one needs α > 0, i.e. any bounded measurable drift works; for rougher drivers (p > 2) the drift must be slightly more regular. This result extends to infinite‑dimensional RDEs and to branched rough paths, showing that the regularisation effect is robust across the various flavours of rough path theory.

Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Flows: Diffeomorphism Groups and the Chen‑Strichartz Estimates

A rough flow is a map Φ : [0, T] × ℝd → ℝd such that for each initial point x the trajectory t ↦ Φ(t, x) solves the RDE dY = V(Y) dX, and for each fixed t the map x ↦ Φ(t, x) is a diffeomorphism of ℝd. The collection {Φ(t, ·) : t ∈ [0, T]} forms a flow in the group Diff∞(ℝd) of smooth diffeomorphisms. The Chen‑Strichartz estimates quantify the regularity of the flow map in terms of the p‑variation of the driving rough path. For a geometric p‑rough path X with p ∈ [2,3) and vector fields V ∈ C⌈p⌉+1, one has

‖Φ(t, ·) − Id‖Ck ≤ C ‖X‖p‑var;[0,t] , k ≤ ⌈p⌉ − 1 .

Moreover, the inverse flow Ψ(t, ·) = Φ(t, ·)−1 satisfies the backward RDE dΨ = −V(Ψ) dX, and the same estimates hold. These bounds are essential for geometric numerical integration: a numerical scheme that preserves the group structure (e.g. a Runge–Kutta–Munthe‑Kaas method lifted to the Lie algebra of vector fields) inherits the Chen‑Strichartz estimates and therefore converges with the same order as the underlying rough Taylor expansion. The theory also connects to the study of stochastic flows of diffeomorphisms driven by Brownian motion, where the rough path lift is the Stratonovich lift, and to the analysis of the Euler equations with rough transport noise.

Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Paths for Lévy‑Driven Signals: Jumps, Truncation, and the Marcus Integral

Lévy processes with jumps are not of finite p‑variation for any p < 2, but their truncated versions are. For a Lévy process L with Lévy measure ν, define the truncated process Lε by removing jumps larger than ε. Then Lε has finite 1‑variation and its canonical lift (the piecewise linear interpolation of (Lε, ∫ Lε ⊗ dLε)) converges in the Skorokhod topology to a limit rough path L as ε → 0, provided ∫|x|>1 |x|2 ν(dx) < ∞. The limit is a branched rough path whose second level contains the Marcus correction: the antisymmetric part of the second level equals ½ ∫|x|≤1 x ⊗ x ν(dx). This correction distinguishes the Marcus integral (the rough integral against the lifted Lévy process) from the Itô integral; the Marcus integral obeys the classical chain rule, while the Itô integral acquires an extra term involving the Lévy measure.

Rough differential equations driven by such Lévy rough paths are well‑posed under the same regularity assumptions on the vector fields as for continuous drivers. The solution coincides with the solution of the Marcus SDE dY = V(Y) ⋄ dL, where ⋄ denotes the Marcus integral. This framework unifies the treatment of jump diffusions and continuous rough drivers, and it provides a pathwise interpretation of the stochastic exponential for Lévy processes. Recent extensions cover Lévy processes in Banach spaces and the construction of rough path lifts for Lévy white noise, enabling the analysis of SPDEs with jump noise via rough path methods.

Rough Paths and Rough Differential Equations with Delay

Delay rough differential equations (delay RDEs) extend the classical RDE framework by allowing the vector fields to depend on the past trajectory of the solution. Formally, for a given rough path X of finite p-variation with p ∈ [2,3), a delay RDE takes the form

dYt = V(Yt, Yt-τ) dXt, t ∈ [0,T],

with a fixed delay τ > 0 and an initial history function η ∈ C([-τ,0]; ℝd). The solution is constructed on the extended interval [-τ,T] by first solving a standard RDE on [0,τ] with the frozen history η, then iterating the Lyons–Itô map on successive intervals of length τ. The key analytical tool is the delayed controlled rough path space, introduced in recent work, where the Gubinelli derivative of the solution at time t depends on both Yt and Yt-τ. Existence and uniqueness hold under the same Lipschitz and boundedness assumptions on V as for non‑delay RDEs, provided V is of class C⌈p⌉+1 in both arguments. The solution map (η, X) ↦ Y is locally Lipschitz continuous from C([-τ,0]) × 𝒞p-var into C([-τ,T]), which yields stability with respect to the driving rough path and the initial history. Applications include modelling of systems with memory in finance (rough volatility with delayed leverage effect) and in biology (gene regulatory networks with transcriptional delays). Numerical approximation follows the Euler scheme on each subinterval, with convergence order 1/p in p‑variation, and the scheme preserves the delay structure by storing the discrete history.

Rough Paths and Rough Integration for Young‑type Integrals and the Sewing Lemma in Higher Order

Young integration provides a pathwise integral ∫ Y dX when Y has finite q‑variation and X has finite p‑variation with 1/p + 1/q > 1. The sewing lemma, originally developed by Feyel and de la Pradelle and later refined by Gubinelli, gives a systematic way to construct such integrals by controlling the remainder of a two‑parameter additive functional. In the rough path setting, the lemma is lifted to higher order: given a controlled rough path (Y, Y′) with respect to a geometric p‑rough path X, one defines the approximate integral on a partition π by

Iπ = Σ[u,v]∈π ( Yu Xu,v1 + Y′u Xu,v2 ),

where X1 and X2 are the first and second levels of X. The sewing lemma guarantees that the limit exists in p‑variation provided the remainder Ru,v = Yv − Yu − Y′u Xu,v1 satisfies a bound of order |v−u|2/p. The higher‑order version, developed in recent work by Tindel, Torrecilla and others, replaces the second level by the full truncated signature up to level ⌈p⌉ and requires the remainder to be controlled by the (⌈p⌉+1)-st level. This yields a unified construction of the rough integral for any p ≥ 1 and provides the analytic backbone for the definition of branched rough paths and for the solution theory of RDEs driven by non‑geometric drivers. The lemma also underpins the convergence analysis of numerical schemes: the local truncation error of a k‑step Taylor scheme is exactly the sewing remainder at level k+1, so the global error inherits the p‑variation bound from the sewing estimate.

Rough Paths and Rough Differential Equations on Metric Measure Spaces

The extension of rough path theory to metric measure spaces (mm‑spaces) allows one to drive RDEs by signals taking values in non‑Euclidean state spaces such as Wasserstein spaces, Alexandrov spaces, or Carnot groups. Foundational work by Cass, Litterer and Lyons defines a rough path on an mm‑space (M, d, μ) as a pair (X, 𝕏) where X is a continuous path in M with finite p‑variation and 𝕏 is a “second order” object taking values in a suitable tensor‑like product of the tangent cone at each point. Because M may lack a linear structure, the tensor product is replaced by the metric tensor product constructed via optimal transport plans: for x, y ∈ M, the increment Xs,t is represented by a geodesic, and the area term 𝕏s,t is a measure on the space of geodesic triangles satisfying a Chen‑type identity. The solution of an RDE dY = V(Y) dX is then defined via the rough flow on M, which is a map Φ: [0,T] × M → M such that for each initial point x the curve t ↦ Φ(t,x) solves the RDE in the sense of rough integration against the lifted driver. Existence and uniqueness hold when the vector fields V are Lipschitz in the sense of the metric derivative and the driver has finite p‑variation with p < 3. The theory has been applied to rough differential equations on the Wasserstein space P2(ℝd) driven by rough paths in ℝm, yielding a pathwise formulation of the McKean–Vlasov equation with rough noise. Numerical approximation on mm‑spaces uses the metric Euler scheme, where the update step follows a geodesic in the direction of the vector field evaluated at the current point, and convergence is proved under a non‑positive curvature assumption on M.

Rough Paths and Rough Path Lifts for Gaussian Processes with Non‑Stationary Covariances

Classical rough path lifts exist for Gaussian processes with stationary increments, notably fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H > 1/4. For non‑stationary Gaussian processes, the covariance function R(s,t) = 𝔼[Gs Gt] may not satisfy the scaling homogeneity required by the Kolmogorov continuity criterion in its standard form. Recent work by Friz, Shekhar and others developed a lift construction based on the covariance regularity condition: there exists α ∈ (1/3, 1/2] such that the 2D ρ‑variation of R is finite for ρ = 1/(2α). Concretely, one requires Σi,j |R(ti, tj) − R(ti−1, tj) − R(ti, tj−1) + R(ti−1, tj−1)|ρ < ∞ uniformly over partitions. Under this condition, the canonical lift of the piecewise linear interpolation of G converges in p‑variation for any p > 1/α, and the limit is a geometric rough path. The second level of the limit is given by the Lévy area defined via the Itô–Stratonovich correction: 𝔼[∫st (Gu − Gs) ⊗ dGu] = ½ (R(t,t) − R(s,s) − 2R(s,t)) Id + antisymmetric part determined by the covariance. This framework covers a wide class of processes, including Gaussian processes with time‑dependent Hurst index, Volterra‑type processes, and solutions of stochastic PDEs with rough noise. The lift is unique in law and the solution map for RDEs driven by such lifts is continuous, enabling statistical inference for the covariance parameters from observed rough trajectories.

Rough Paths and the Skorokhod Problem: Reflected Rough Differential Equations

The Skorokhod problem asks for a pair of paths (Y, K) such that Y = X + K, Y takes values in a closed convex domain D ⊂ ℝd, and K is a finite‑variation process that pushes Y back into D only when Y hits the boundary ∂D, with K increasing in the direction of the inward normal. When the driver X is a rough path of finite p‑variation with p ∈ [2, 3), the solution map X ↦ (Y, K) can be constructed pathwise using the Lyons–Itô map for RDEs with reflection. The key observation is that the reflection term K is itself a controlled rough path: its Gubinelli derivative is the normal vector field n(Y) on ∂D, and the remainder satisfies the required bound because the local time accumulated at the boundary is of order p‑variation. Existence and uniqueness hold when D is a convex domain with C2 boundary and the vector fields Vi are Lipγ for γ > p. The solution Y satisfies the reflected RDE dY = V(Y) dX + n(Y) dK, where the integral against dK is a Young integral because K has finite 1‑variation. The continuity of the solution map in the p‑variation topology follows from the continuity of the Lyons–Itô map and the Lipschitz property of the Skorokhod map in the uniform topology, lifted to p‑variation via the sewing lemma. Applications include reflected rough volatility models where the volatility process is constrained to stay positive, and queueing networks driven by rough noise where the workload process is reflected at zero. The numerical approximation uses a projected Euler scheme: Yk+1 = ΠD(Yk + V(Yk) Xk,k+1 + 𝕍(Yk) 𝕏k,k+1), where ΠD is the Euclidean projection onto D. Convergence of order 1/p in p‑variation is proved under a non‑degeneracy condition on the normal cone.

Rough Paths and Cadlag Rough Drivers: Integration Against Jump Processes

Classical rough path theory assumes continuous drivers, but many applied signals — limit order book data, insurance claims, neuronal spike trains — exhibit jumps. The extension to càdlàg rough paths replaces the p‑variation metric with the Skorokhod J1‑metric or the p‑variation metric on the space of càdlàg paths equipped with a suitable truncation of large jumps. A càdlàg rough path of roughness p ∈ [2, 3) is a pair (X, 𝕏) where X is càdlàg with finite p‑variation and 𝕏 is a two‑parameter process satisfying Chen's identity on intervals not containing jumps, and at a jump time τ the second level satisfies 𝕏τ−,τ = ½ ΔXτ ⊗ ΔXτ for geometric rough paths, or a more general antisymmetric part for non‑geometric lifts. The integral ∫ Y dX is defined by the sewing lemma on each continuity interval, with the jump contribution added as Yτ− ΔXτ + Y'τ− 𝕏τ−,τ, where Y' is the Gubinelli derivative. The solution of an RDE dY = V(Y) dX with càdlàg driver is constructed by solving the RDE on each interval between jumps and updating the state at jump times via the Marcus map: Yτ = exp(V(·) ΔXτ) Yτ−, where exp denotes the flow of the vector field V. This coincides with the Stratonovich interpretation for semimartingale drivers. The Lyons–Itô map is continuous in the p‑variation topology on the space of càdlàg rough paths equipped with the truncated p‑variation norm ‖X‖p‑var;[0,T] = supΠ (∑ |Xti+1 − Xti|p 1{|ΔX| ≤ 1})1/p. Large jumps are treated separately via the Marcus mapping, which preserves the geometric structure. Applications include rough volatility models with jumps (rough Bergomi with jumps), and the pathwise formulation of Lévy‑driven SDEs where the Lévy process is lifted to a càdlàg rough path using the Lévy–Itô decomposition and the truncation of small jumps. The numerical scheme combines the Euler scheme on continuity intervals with the exact Marcus update at jump times, achieving convergence of order 1/p in probability.

Worked Example: Reflected RDE on the Positive Half‑Line
Consider the reflected RDE on D = [0, ∞) driven by a geometric rough path X of finite p‑variation, p ∈ [2, 3): dY = σ(Y) dX + dK, Y ≥ 0, K non‑decreasing, ∫0T Yt dKt = 0, with σ ∈ Lipγ, γ > p, and σ(0) = 0. Let X be the lift of a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index H = 0.4 (so p = 1/H = 2.5). Show that the solution Y is a controlled rough path with Gubinelli derivative Y' = σ(Y) and compute the remainder RYs,t in terms of the remainder of X and the reflection term K.
Since Y = X + K and K has finite 1‑variation, Y is controlled by X with derivative Y' = σ(Y). The remainder is RYs,t = Yt − Ys − σ(Ys) Xs,t. Using the RDE, Yt − Ys = ∫st σ(Yu) dXu + Kt − Ks. The rough integral expands as σ(Ys) Xs,t + σ'(Ys) σ(Ys) 𝕏s,t + R∫s,t, where R∫ is the sewing remainder of order 3/p. Thus RYs,t = σ'(Ys) σ(Ys) 𝕏s,t + R∫s,t + Kt − Ks. The term Kt − Ks is of finite 1‑variation, hence of order |t−s|1 which is o(|t−s|2/p) since p < 3. The sewing remainder R∫ is of order |t−s|3/p. Therefore the dominant remainder term is σ'(Ys) σ(Ys) 𝕏s,t of order |t−s|2/p, and Y is indeed a controlled rough path with the claimed derivative. The reflection term does not affect the Gubinelli derivative because it is of lower order in the p‑variation scale.
Exam Strategy: Identifying the Correct Reflection Term
In reflected RDE questions, always check whether the domain D is convex with C2 boundary. If not, the Skorokhod problem may have multiple solutions or none. The reflection term K is a controlled rough path only when the normal vector field is Lipschitz; for non‑smooth boundaries (e.g., polyhedral domains), K may have higher p‑variation and the solution map may fail to be continuous. Rough Paths and Functional Rough Differential Equations: Path‑Dependent Vector Fields

Functional RDEs extend the classical RDE framework to vector fields that depend on the entire past trajectory: dYt = V(Y·∧t) dXt, where Y·∧t denotes the path segment {Ys : s ≤ t}. This arises naturally in systems with delay, hereditary effects, or path‑dependent volatility. The state space is the Banach space C([−τ, 0]; ℝd) of continuous history segments, and the driver X is a rough path in ℝm. The solution is a path Y in the space of continuous paths, and its lifted history segment (Yt, Y't) must be a controlled rough path in the history space. The Gubinelli derivative Y' takes values in the space of linear operators from ℝm to the history space, and the remainder satisfies a bound in the p‑variation norm on the history space. The key technical tool is the sewing lemma in the Banach space of history segments, which requires the vector field V to be Lipγ in the sense of Fréchet differentiability on the history space with γ > p. The solution map is continuous from the p‑variation topology on the driver to the p‑variation topology on the solution path, provided the initial history segment is fixed in the p‑variation topology. This is a stricter requirement than uniform convergence of initial data. Applications include rough volatility models with long memory (e.g., the rough Heston model where the variance process depends on its entire past), and neural controlled differential equations where the hidden state evolves according to a path‑dependent vector field. The numerical approximation uses a discrete history buffer: at each step, the vector field is evaluated on the piecewise linear interpolation of the stored history, and the Euler update is performed. Convergence of order 1/p in p‑variation is proved under a uniform Lipschitz condition on V with respect to the supremum norm on history segments.

Rough Paths and Singular Rough Differential Equations: Regularisation by Noise

Singular RDEs involve vector fields that are not Lipschitz, for example V(y) = |y|α−1 y with α ∈ (0, 1) (Bessel‑type drift) or distributional drifts such as white noise potential. The classical Lyons–Itô theory requires Lipγ vector fields with γ > p, but recent work shows that the rough noise itself can regularise the equation, allowing solutions for drifts below the Lipschitz threshold. The mechanism is analogous to the regularisation by noise for SDEs: the rough driver X provides enough "roughness" to compensate for the singularity of V. For a geometric rough path X of finite p‑variation, p ∈ [2, 3), and a drift V ∈ Cβ with β > 1 − 2/p, the RDE dY = V(Y) dt + dX has a unique solution in the sense of controlled rough paths. The proof uses the sewing lemma with a modified remainder estimate that exploits the cancellation between the singular drift and the rough driver. The solution map is continuous in the p‑variation topology on X and the uniform topology on the initial condition. For distributional drifts V ∈ Cβ with β < 0, one needs to renormalise the equation using the theory of regularity structures or paracontrolled distributions; the rough path lift of the driver provides the necessary commutator estimates. Applications include the pathwise formulation of the stochastic quantisation equation for Φ4d models, and the regularisation of sticky Brownian motion by rough noise. The numerical scheme uses a tamed Euler method: Yk+1 = Yk + Vε(Yk) Δt + Xk,k+1, where Vε is a mollified drift, and the mollification parameter ε is chosen proportional to the mesh size to balance the regularisation error and the discretisation error.

Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Differential Equations on Lie Groups: Geometric Numerical Integration

When the state space of an RDE is a Lie group G, the solution evolves on the group manifold and the vector fields are left‑invariant. The RDE dY = V(Y) dX with Y ∈ G and Vi ∈ 𝔤 (the Lie algebra) can be solved by lifting the driver to a rough path on the group via the Magnus expansion or the Chen–Strichartz formula. The solution is given by the rough exponential map: Yt = exp★(∫0t AdYs−1 V dXs) Y0, where exp★ is the group exponential and Ad is the adjoint representation. The signature of the solution path lies in the tensor algebra over 𝔤, and the shuffle product corresponds to the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula. Geometric numerical integrators preserve the group structure exactly: the Munthe‑Kaas method uses the Lie group exponential to update Yk+1 = exp(Ωk) Yk, where Ωk ∈ 𝔤 solves the truncated Magnus expansion driven by the discrete increments of the rough path. The Crouch–Grossman method uses a commutator‑free expansion with multiple evaluations of the vector fields, achieving the same order without computing Lie brackets explicitly. These schemes are essential for applications in rigid body dynamics driven by rough noise, quantum control on SU(n), and stochastic geometry on homogeneous spaces. The error analysis uses the Chen–Strichartz estimates for the difference between the exact and numerical flows in the Carnot–Carathéodory metric on the group.

Rough Paths and the Signature Inversion Problem: Reconstructing Paths from Signatures

The signature of a path, defined as the collection of all iterated integrals, faithfully represents the path up to tree-like equivalence. The signature inversion problem asks: given a sequence of tensors S = (1, S(1), S(2), …) in the tensor algebra T((ℝd), does a finite p-variation path X with signature S exist, and how can it be reconstructed? The signature map is injective on paths modulo tree-like pieces (Chen–Hambly–Lyons). A sequence S is the signature of a geometric rough path iff it satisfies the shuffle relations S(m) ⧢ S(n) = S(m+n) and the group-like property ΔS = S ⊗ S in the tensor Hopf algebra. For a truncated signature up to level N, reconstruction is ill-posed: small signature perturbations can cause large path changes. Stability holds only for paths with bounded complexity, e.g., factorial decay ‖S(k)‖ ≤ Ck k! (C > 0). Inversion algorithms include the log-signature method (using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula to recover the path from its log-signature in the free Lie algebra) and moment-problem methods. In machine learning, signature inversion underlies the signature kernel and signature transform for time-series classification. Uniqueness up to tree-like equivalence means any algorithm must fix a parametrisation (e.g., constant speed) or accept the ambiguity. For p ∈ [2, 3), the second-level signature 𝕏 determines the area process, and the path is recovered from (X, 𝕏) via the sewing lemma. Practical inversion is often regularised by penalising the p-variation of the reconstructed path, yielding a well-posed optimisation in the space of controlled rough paths.

Rough Paths and Expected Signatures: Moment Problems and Characteristic Functions

The expected signature 𝔼[Sig(X)] of a stochastic process X is the sequence of expected iterated integrals 𝔼[∫0<t1<…<tk<T dXt1 ⊗ … ⊗ dXtk]. It acts as a moment generating function for the path law. For Brownian motion B in ℝd, 𝔼[Sig(B)0,T] = exp(½ T ∑i=1d ei ⊗ ei), since the Lévy area has zero expectation. For a general Gaussian process with covariance R(s, t), the expected signature solves a linear rough differential equation driven by the covariance operator. It characterises the law among processes with the same finite-dimensional distributions if the moment problem is determinate. The moment problem for signatures asks: given tensors M = (M(k))k≥0, does a random rough path X exist with 𝔼[Sig(X)(k)] = M(k)? Necessary and sufficient conditions are the shuffle relations M(m) ⧢ M(n) = M(m+n) and positivity: 𝔼[⟨P, Sig(X)⟩2] ≥ 0 for all polynomials P in the tensor algebra. In practice, expected signatures are estimated via Monte Carlo or by solving moment equations. The truncated expected signature up to level N provides an injective feature map for sufficiently large N under mild conditions. The characteristic function φ(ℓ) = 𝔼[exp(⟨ℓ, Sig(X)⟩)] for ℓ in the dual tensor algebra satisfies a rough PDE and is used for parameter estimation and hypothesis testing. For a p-rough path, the decay bound ‖𝔼[Sig(X)(k)]‖ ≤ Ck k!1/p holds.

Worked Example: Expected Signature of Brownian Motion
Let B = (B1, B2) be a two-dimensional Brownian motion. Compute the expected signature 𝔼[Sig(B)0,T] up to level 3 and verify the shuffle relations.

Step 1: Level 0 and Level 1. Level 0 is 1. Level 1 is 𝔼[∫0T dBt] = 0 (zero mean).

Step 2: Level 2. The level-2 signature is 𝕏i,j = ∫0<t1<t2<T dBit1 dBjt2. For i = j, Itô's formula gives ½(BiT)2; expectation is ½T. For i ≠ j, it is the Lévy area, a zero-mean martingale. Thus 𝔼[𝕏] = ½T I2.

Step 3: Level 3. Triple iterated integrals. Shuffles of level-1 and level-2 vanish because level-1 expectation is zero. Remaining terms (Lyndon words) are integrals of odd polynomials in Gaussian variables, hence zero. So 𝔼[∫0<t1<t2<t3<T dBit1 dBjt2 dBkt3] = 0 for all i, j, k.

Step 4: Verify shuffle relations. The expected signature up to level 3 is S = 1 + 0 + ½T ∑i ei ⊗ ei + 0. Then S(1) ⧢ S(1) = 0, but S(2) = ½T ∑ ei ⊗ ei ≠ 0. Exam Strategy: Expected Signature vs Random Signature
Do not confuse the shuffle relations for the random signature (group-like almost surely) with the algebraic properties of the expected signature. The expected signature is not group-like; it satisfies a linear rough differential equation driven by the covariance. In multiple-choice questions, "The expected signature of Brownian motion satisfies the shuffle product" is false. The correct statement: "The random signature of Brownian motion satisfies the shuffle product almost surely." The expected signature equals the exponential of the covariance tensor only for zero-mean Gaussian processes with vanishing expected Lévy area.

Rough Paths and Rough Differential Equations on Sub-Riemannian Manifolds: Hypoellipticity and the Hörmander Condition

When an RDE evolves on a sub-Riemannian manifold (M, H, g) with bracket-generating horizontal bundle H ⊂ TM and metric g, the vector fields V1, …, Vd take values in H. The driving noise directly excites only horizontal directions; non-horizontal directions are reached through Lie brackets, a phenomenon called hypoellipticity. The Hörmander condition requires that the Lie algebra generated by {Vi} spans TyM at every y ∈ M. Under this condition, the transition density exists and is smooth despite degenerate noise. For rough paths, hypoellipticity extends classical Malliavin calculus. The Malliavin covariance matrix is σt = ∑i=1d ∫0t J0,s Vi(Ys) ⊗ J0,s Vi(Ys) ds, where J0,s is the flow Jacobian. For geometric rough paths with p < 3, the Jacobian satisfies a linear RDE, so σt is defined pathwise. The rough Norris lemma bounds the probability that ‖σt−1‖ is large, enabling density estimates. The Carnot–Carathéodory distance dCC(x, y) is the infimum length of horizontal curves. A support theorem holds: the law of Yt has full support in the sub-Riemannian ball of radius proportional to the p-variation of X. Small-time density asymptotics are governed by sub-Riemannian geodesics and the nilpotent approximation. For the Heisenberg group with horizontal fields X = ∂x − ½y∂z, Y = ∂y + ½x∂z, the RDE has an explicit solution via the group exponential and a closed-form density. The rough path framework handles non-semimartingale drivers, e.g., fractional Brownian motion with H > ¼, where classical Malliavin calculus fails but rough path Malliavin calculus succeeds.

Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Differential Equations with Non-Lipschitz Diffusion Coefficients: Yamada–Watanabe Theory for RDEs

The classical Yamada–Watanabe theorem for SDEs states that if the diffusion coefficient σ satisfies ∫0+ ρ(u)−2 du = ∞ for a concave increasing ρ with ρ(0) = 0, and the drift b is Lipschitz, then pathwise uniqueness holds for dY = b(Y) dt + σ(Y) dB. For RDEs dY = V(Y) dX with geometric rough path X of finite p-variation, the analogous condition involves the p-variation and vector field regularity. If Vi are γ-Hölder with γ > p, Lyons' theory gives existence and uniqueness. When γ ≤ p, the solution map is not standard. The RDE Yamada–Watanabe theory uses a one-sided Osgood condition: ⟨V(y) − V(z), y − z⟩ ≤ ρ(‖y − z‖2) with ∫0+ ρ(u)−1 du = ∞. For p ∈ [2, 3), the critical regularity is γ > p − 1. If Vi ∈ Cγ with γ ∈ (p − 1, p], solutions are defined via the sewing lemma with a modified remainder exploiting cancellation in the rough integral. The proof uses a rough Bihari–LaSalle inequality. The condition ∫0+ ρ(u)−1 du = ∞ is sharp: for ρ(u) = u |log u| the integral diverges (uniqueness); for ρ(u) = u |log u|1+ε it converges (non-uniqueness possible). Weak existence holds for continuous vector fields with linear growth (as a measure on controlled rough paths). Pathwise uniqueness plus weak existence implies strong existence and uniqueness in law. This theory is essential for RDEs with discontinuous or degenerate coefficients, e.g., in stochastic filtering with non-smooth observations or sticky-boundary models.

Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Differential Equations with State-Dependent Noise: The Stratonovich Correction in Rough Paths

In classical stochastic calculus, the Stratonovich integral ∫ Y ∘ dB differs from the Itô integral ∫ Y dB by ½ ∫ Y' dt. For RDEs driven by a rough path X, the Itô/Stratonovich distinction is encoded in the rough path lift. A semimartingale X has two canonical geometric rough path lifts: the Itô lift (Itô iterated integrals) and the Stratonovich lift (Stratonovich iterated integrals). They differ by the quadratic variation: 𝕏Strat = 𝕏Itô + ½ [X, X]. For a general rough path not from a semimartingale, the "Stratonovich correction" is not defined a priori. With state-dependent noise Vi(y), the lift choice affects the solution. The rough path formulation naturally corresponds to the Stratonovich interpretation (Wong–Zakai principle: limit of smooth approximations). To solve an RDE corresponding to an Itô SDE, use the Itô lift or add a drift correction. The Itô SDE dY = b(Y) dt + σ(Y) dB is equivalent to the Stratonovich RDE dY = (b(Y) − ½ ∑i Dσi(Y) σi(Y)) dt + σ(Y) ∘ dB. In rough path terms, the Itô RDE driven by the Itô lift of B equals the Stratonovich RDE driven by the Stratonovich lift with modified drift. For general X with p ∈ [2, 3), the correction for state-dependent σ(y) is ½ ∑i,j Dσi(Y) σj(Y) 𝕏i,j, well-defined as a rough integral since Y is controlled by X and 𝕏 has finite p/2-variation. The lift choice determines the physical noise interpretation: Stratonovich corresponds to the Wong–Zakai limit of smooth noises; Itô corresponds to non-anticipative discrete-time approximations. For fractional Brownian motion with H ≠ ½, there is no classical Itô/Stratonovich distinction (quadratic variation is zero or infinite), but the rough path lift still requires choosing the area process. The canonical lift is the Stratonovich-type lift from smooth approximations, yielding a geometric rough path for H > ¼.

Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Differential Equations with Reflection and State Constraints

The Skorokhod problem for rough differential equations extends the classical reflection mapping to drivers with finite p-variation, p ∈ [2, 3). Given a domain D ⊂ ℝ^d with C3 boundary and a rough path X, the reflected RDE seeks a pair (Y, K) where Y_t ∈ D̄, K is a non-decreasing process increasing only when Y_t ∈ ∂D, and dY_t = V(Y_t) dX_t + n(Y_t) dK_t with n the inward normal. Existence and uniqueness hold for geometric rough paths when V is Lip^γ with γ > p and the domain satisfies a uniform exterior sphere condition. The solution map (X, Y0) ↦ (Y, K) is locally Lipschitz in the p-variation topology. For non-geometric rough paths, the reflection term must be interpreted via the Gubinelli derivative: K is controlled by X with Gubinelli derivative Y' = V(Y), and the rough integral ∫ n(Y) dK is defined through the sewing lemma. The key estimate is the rough Gronwall inequality for reflected paths: ‖Y‖p-var ≤ C(1 + ‖X‖p-var^p) where C depends on the domain geometry and vector field regularity. Applications include constrained stochastic control, queueing networks with rough arrivals, and financial models with regulatory boundaries. The reflected rough path lift of a semimartingale reflected at a boundary converges to the Stratonovich reflected SDE under Wong–Zakai approximations, but the Itô reflected SDE requires a correction involving the local time and the second fundamental form of ∂D.

A central class is the rough paths lifted from stationary processes with finite p-variation, p ∈ [2, 3), whose covariance structure satisfies a sector condition: |Cov(X_s, X_t)| ≤ C|t-s|2H for some H > 1/p. Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Differential Equations on Manifolds with Boundary

Extending rough differential equations to manifolds with boundary requires a geometric formulation of the reflection map. Let M be a compact Riemannian manifold with C3 boundary ∂M, and let X be a geometric rough path in the tangent bundle TM. The reflected RDE on M seeks a path Y in M and a reflection process K taking values in the inward normal bundle N+∂M such that the anti-development of Y against the rough driver X satisfies the Euclidean reflected RDE in local coordinates. The solution is constructed via the Eells–Elworthy–Malliavin method: lift the problem to the orthonormal frame bundle OM, solve the reflected RDE on OM with a reflection term that keeps the frame tangent to the boundary, then project down to M. For a Stratonovich RDE dY = V_i(Y) ∘ dX^i on M with reflection, the generator is ½ ∑ V_i2 + V0 with Neumann boundary condition ∂_n u = 0. Rough Paths and the Theory of Rough Differential Equations with Discontinuous Vector Fields

Classical rough path theory assumes vector fields V ∈ Lip^γ with γ > p. When V is discontinuous, the solution concept must be relaxed. A natural framework is the Filippov convexification: for a measurable bounded vector field V, define the Filippov set-valued map F[V](y) = ⋂δ>0 ⋂N: μ(N)=0 co{V(B_δ(y) N)}. A Filippov solution to the RDE dY = V(Y) dX is an absolutely continuous path Y such that its derivative (in the rough sense) lies in F[V](Y) dX almost everywhere. For rough drivers, the derivative is replaced by the Gubinelli derivative Y' which must satisfy Y'_t ∈ F[V](Y_t) for all t. Existence of Filippov solutions holds for any bounded measurable V and any geometric rough path X with p < 3, using the Sewing Lemma on the convexified integral. Uniqueness fails in general; the solution set is a compact connected subset of the p-variation space. A selection principle picks the "physical" solution as the limit of solutions to regularised RDEs dY^n = V_n(Y^n) dX where V_n → V uniformly on compacts. For piecewise smooth V with a smooth discontinuity surface Σ, the selected solution satisfies a rough version of the Filippov sliding mode condition: when Y_t ∈ Σ, the Gubinelli derivative Y'_t lies in the convex hull of the limiting vector fields from both sides, projected onto the tangent space of Σ. The rough integral ∫ V(Y) dX is then defined as the limit of the regularised integrals, which exists and is independent of the regularisation sequence if V has bounded variation in the sense of Hardy–Krause. This theory applies to RDEs with dry friction, relay hysteresis, and piecewise affine control systems driven by rough noise.

  • Treating Filippov solutions for discontinuous vector fields as unique. The solution set is generally a non-singleton compact set; the physical solution is selected by a regularisation limit, not by the differential inclusion alone.
  • Applying the elliptic Hörmander condition (span of V_i alone) to degenerate RDEs. The correct condition involves the drift V0 and iterated brackets; a system can be hypoelliptic even if the diffusion vector fields do not span, provided the drift fills the missing directions through brackets.
  • Assuming the expected signature of a degenerate diffusion determines its law without checking the moment problem on the Carnot group. The expected signature lives in the universal enveloping algebra of the Lie algebra; the moment problem is determinate only if the Carleman condition holds for the projected moments.
  • Overlooking that the Carnot–Carathéodory distance in the support theorem scales with the p-variation of the driver, not the Euclidean distance. The support is contained in the horizontal directions generated by the vector fields, and the distance between two points is the infimum of the p-variation of controls steering the system between them.
