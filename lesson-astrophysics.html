Part 1

⭐ Stellar Structure and Evolution

Stellar Evolution
Protostars, the Main Sequence, and Post-Main-Sequence Evolution
A star begins as a protostar formed by gravitational collapse of a giant molecular cloud. The Virial theorem 2K + U = 0 relates the kinetic (thermal) energy K to the gravitational potential energy U, dictating that half the gravitational energy released goes into heating the core. The hydrostatic equilibrium equation dP/dr = −G M(r) ρ(r) / r² balances pressure against gravity. On the main sequence, hydrogen burns via the pp chain (dominant for M < 1.3 M_⊙) or the CNO cycle (dominant for M > 1.3 M_⊙), with energy generation rate ε ∝ ρ T^4 (pp) or ε ∝ ρ T^17 (CNO). The mass-luminosity relation L ∝ M^3.5 for main-sequence stars follows from homology relations. When core hydrogen is exhausted, the star becomes a red giant: the core contracts and heats, while the envelope expands. The Schönberg-Chandrasekhar limit (M_core / M_total ≈ 0.1) sets the maximum mass of an isothermal helium core. Helium burning via the triple-alpha process (3α → ¹²C) ignites at T ≈ 10^8 K, followed by carbon and oxygen burning in massive stars. The Urca process and neutrino cooling dominate in later stages. The final fate depends on the initial mass: M < 8 M_⊙ yields a white dwarf; M > 8 M_⊙ yields a core-collapse supernova leaving a neutron star or black hole.
The initial mass of a star is the single most important parameter determining its evolution, lifespan, and ultimate fate — from a cooling white dwarf to a black hole.
Part 2

🔥 Nucleosynthesis: Forging the Elements

Nucleosynthesis
The pp Chain, CNO Cycle, Triple-Alpha, s-Process, and r-Process
Primordial nucleosynthesis in the first three minutes after the Big Bang produced H, He, and traces of Li — no heavier elements. All heavier elements are forged in stars. Hydrogen burning fuses H to He via the pp chain (p + p → d, d + p → ³He, ³He + ³He → ⁴He + 2p; with branches pp-I, pp-II, and pp-III) or the CNO cycle (¹²C(p,γ)¹³N(e⁺ν)¹³C(p,γ)¹⁴N(p,γ)¹⁵O(e⁺ν)¹⁵N(p,α)¹²C). The CNO bottleneck is the slow ¹⁴N(p,γ)¹⁵O reaction. In the triple-alpha process, three ⁴He nuclei fuse via the unstable ⁸Be resonance to form ¹²C (the Hoyle state at 7.65 MeV). The α-process builds ¹⁶O, ²⁰Ne, ²⁴Mg, ²⁸Si. Beyond iron, fusion is endothermic. The s-process (slow neutron capture) in asymptotic giant branch stars produces about half the elements heavier than iron via neutron captures on seed nuclei, with β-decays between captures (the path follows the valley of stability). The r-process (rapid neutron capture) in supernovae and neutron star mergers produces the other half, with captures much faster than β-decays, pushing far from stability toward the neutron drip line. The r-process path proceeds via (n,γ) reactions at rates ≈ 10²³ cm³/mol/s, with subsequent β-decays back to stability. The p-process (proton capture) and νp-process in supernovae produce the rare proton-rich isotopes. The cosmic chemical evolution tracks the enrichment of the interstellar medium across generations of stars.
Every atom in our bodies heavier than helium was synthesized inside a star or in a supernova explosion — we are literally stardust.

🌀 Compact Objects: White Dwarfs, Neutron Stars, and Black Holes

Compact Objects
Chandrasekhar Limit, Neutron Star Structure, the Kerr Metric, and Accretion Physics
White dwarfs are supported by electron degeneracy pressure. The equation of state is P = K ρ^{5/3} (non-relativistic) or P = K ρ^{4/3} (relativistic). The Chandrasekhar mass limit M_Ch ≈ 1.44 M_⊙ = (3π/2)^{1/2} (ℏ c / G)^{3/2} (μ_e m_p)^{-2} follows from the relativistic degenerate Fermi gas. Above this mass, electron pressure cannot support gravity. Neutron stars are supported by neutron degeneracy pressure and the strong nuclear force. The Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff (TOV) equation dP/dr = −G (ε + P/c²) (M(r) + 4πr³P/c²) / (r² − 2G M(r) r / c²) is the general relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium. The TOV limit is about 2–3 M_⊙ (uncertain due to nuclear equation of state). Pulsars are rotating neutron stars with magnetic fields B ≈ 10^8–10^15 G, emitting beams of radiation from the magnetic poles; the lighthouse model explains the periodic pulses. Black holes are described by the Kerr metric for rotating black holes: ds² = −(1 − 2GMr/ρ²) dt² − (4GMar sin²θ/ρ²) dt dφ + (ρ²/Δ) dr² + ρ² dθ² + (r² + a² + 2GMa²r sin²θ/ρ²) sin²θ dφ², where ρ² = r² + a² cos²θ, Δ = r² − 2GMr + a². The event horizon is at r₊ = GM + √(G²M² − a²). The ergosphere lies between r₊ and the static limit r₀ = GM + √(G²M² − a² cos²θ), where frame-dragging forces all objects to co-rotate. The Penrose process extracts rotational energy from the black hole. Accretion disks around compact objects follow the Shakura-Sunyaev α-disk model: viscosity ν = α c_s H, where c_s is sound speed, H is scale height, and α ≈ 0.01–0.1. The Eddington luminosity L_Edd = 4π G M c / κ (where κ is opacity) sets the maximum accretion rate. The innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) for a Schwarzschild black hole is at r = 6GM/c²; for a maximally rotating Kerr black hole it is at r = GM/c² (co-rotating).
Compact objects are the ultimate laboratories of extreme physics — where general relativity, quantum mechanics, and nuclear physics converge in regimes inaccessible on Earth.

🌌 Galaxies, Dark Matter, and Active Galactic Nuclei

Galactic Astrophysics
Galaxy Rotation Curves, Dark Matter Halos, AGN Unification, and Large-Scale Structure
The galaxy rotation curve v(r) = √(G M(r)/r) from Keplerian expectations predicts v ∝ 1/√r beyond the visible disk. Observations (Rubin, Ford, 1970s) show flat rotation curves v ≈ const far beyond the visible matter, implying the existence of dark matter halos. The Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile ρ(r) = ρ₀ / [ (r/r_s) (1 + r/r_s)² ] describes the density of cold dark matter halos from N-body simulations. The MOND (Modified Newtonian Dynamics) alternative posits a modified acceleration law a = a_N / μ(a/a₀) where a₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10^{-10} m/s², but fails to explain cluster dynamics. Active galactic nuclei (AGN) are powered by supermassive black holes (10^6–10^10 M_⊙) accreting gas. The unified model attributes different AGN types (Seyfert 1/2, quasars, blazars) to viewing angle relative to a dusty torus. The broad-line region (BLR) with velocities ∼ 10^4 km/s and the narrow-line region (NLR) with ∼ 500 km/s arise at different radii from the central engine. The M-sigma relation M_BH ∝ σ^4 links the black hole mass to the bulge velocity dispersion. Galaxy clusters are the largest gravitationally bound structures, with total masses 10^14–10^15 M_⊙ dominated by dark matter (∼85%) and hot intracluster gas (∼12%), with galaxies contributing only ∼3%. The Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect — the inverse-Compton scattering of CMB photons by hot cluster electrons — provides a redshift-independent measure of cluster mass. The large-scale structure of the universe follows a cosmic web of filaments, voids, and nodes traced by galaxies, emerging from primordial density fluctuations.
Dark matter dominates the mass budget of galaxies and clusters, yet its particle nature remains one of the deepest unsolved problems in physics.
