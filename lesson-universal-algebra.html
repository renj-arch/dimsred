Universal Algebra — Varieties, Equational Logic & Birkhoff's Theorem

Universal algebra studies algebraic structures in full generality — groups, rings, lattices, modules, and more are all instances of algebras with finitary operations. An algebra A = (A, F) consists of a carrier set A and a set F of finitary operations on A. The similarity type (or signature) is the sequence of arities of the operations. A homomorphism preserves all operations. Congruences — equivalence relations compatible with operations — form the lattice Con(A) and are precisely the kernels of homomorphisms. The Birkhoff representation theorem (1944) shows every algebra is a subdirect product of subdirectly irreducible algebras. A variety is a class of algebras of the same type closed under homomorphic images (H), subalgebras (S), and direct products (P). Birkhoff's HSP theorem (1935) characterizes varieties as precisely the equationally definable classes — those axiomatized by identities (equations between terms). Mal'cev conditions link the existence of certain terms to congruence properties like permutability and modularity. The commutator [α,β] for congruences generalizes the group-theoretic commutator and classifies abelian and nilpotent algebras. Tame congruence theory (Hobby-McKenzie) classifies finite algebras into five types via the structure of minimal congruences, with profound consequences for the constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) and the algebraic dichotomy conjecture (proved by Bulatov and Zhuk).

"Universal algebra is the grammar of algebraic structures."
— George Gratzer
Part 1

📏 Algebras, Homomorphisms, and Subdirect Products

Foundations
Algebras, Congruences, Subdirect Irreducibility, and the Birkhoff Representation
An algebra A = (A, F) is a pair consisting of a non-empty set A (the carrier) together with a set F of finitary operations, each operation f ∈ F having a fixed arity n = ar(f) ≥ 0 (nullary operations are constant elements). The similarity type (or signature) τ is the list of arities. A homomorphism φ: A → B between algebras of the same type is a function preserving each operation: φ(f^A(a₁,…,a_n)) = f^B(φ(a₁),…,φ(a_n)). A congruence θ on A is an equivalence relation compatible with all operations: if a_i θ b_i for each i, then f(a₁,…,a_n) θ f(b₁,…,b_n). Congruences are precisely the kernels of homomorphisms. The set Con(A) of all congruences on A forms a complete lattice (the congruence lattice), ordered by inclusion, with meet = intersection and join = the transitive closure of the union. A subdirect product is a subalgebra A ⊆ Π_{i∈I} A_i such that the projection π_i: A → A_i is surjective for each i. An algebra A is subdirectly irreducible if whenever A is embedded as a subdirect product of algebras {A_i}, at least one projection A → A_i is an isomorphism — equivalently, A has a unique minimal non-trivial congruence μ (the monolith). Birkhoff's theorem (1944): every algebra is isomorphic to a subdirect product of subdirectly irreducible algebras. For a variety V, the subdirectly irreducible algebras in V determine V completely. The Jónsson lemma: if V is congruence-distributive (i.e., Con(A) is a distributive lattice for every A ∈ V), then every subdirectly irreducible algebra in V belongs to the class HS(κ) for some finite generating set κ. The subdirect representation provides the algebraic analogue of the prime ideal decomposition in rings.
Every algebra can be built from subdirectly irreducible building blocks — the "prime" algebras that serve as the fundamental components of the variety.
Part 2

🌍 Varieties and Birkhoff's HSP Theorem

Varieties
Equational Classes, Free Algebras, Term Algebras, and the HSP Theorem
A variety (or equational class) V is a class of algebras of the same type closed under the three operators: H (homomorphic images), S (subalgebras), and P (direct products). A term in a signature τ over a set X of variables is defined inductively: each variable x ∈ X is a term; if t₁,…,t_n are terms and f ∈ F has arity n, then f(t₁,…,t_n) is a term. The term algebra T(X) has the set of all τ-terms over X as its carrier, with operations interpreted syntactically: f^{T(X)}(t₁,…,t_n) = f(t₁,…,t_n). It is the free algebra over X in the class of all τ-algebras. An identity (or equation) is a pair of terms s ≈ t. An algebra A satisfies s ≈ t if for every assignment of variables to elements of A, the two terms evaluate to the same element. Birkhoff's HSP theorem (1935): a class K of algebras of the same type is a variety iff K = HSP(K). Equivalently, K is a variety iff there exists a set Σ of identities such that K = Mod(Σ) — the class of all algebras satisfying Σ. The equational logic (Birkhoff's deductive system) consists of five rules for deriving identities from a set Σ: (1) reflexivity: t ≈ t; (2) symmetry: if s ≈ t then t ≈ s; (3) transitivity: if r ≈ s and s ≈ t then r ≈ t; (4) substitution: if s ≈ t then s(θ) ≈ t(θ) for any substitution θ of variables by terms; (5) replacement: if s_i ≈ t_i for i=1,…,n, then f(s₁,…,s_n) ≈ f(t₁,…,t_n). The free algebra F_V(X) in V over X is the quotient T(X)/θ_V where θ_V is the congruence generated by all identities of V — it has the universal mapping property: any map X → A ∈ V extends uniquely to a homomorphism F_V(X) → A. The Mal'cev-Post correspondence: the word problem for free algebras in a variety is solvable iff the variety has a decidable equational theory. Finite axiomatizability: a finitely based variety is one that can be defined by finitely many identities (e.g., groups by group axioms); many varieties are not finitely based (e.g., the variety of all modular lattices, by a result of Ralph McKenzie).
Varieties are the bridge between syntax (equations) and semantics (algebraic structures) — the HSP theorem shows closure under H, S, P is exactly equivalent to being definable by equations.
Part 3

🧮 Mal'cev Conditions and Congruence Identities

Mal'cev
Mal'cev Terms, Congruence Permutability, Modularity, and the Commutator
A Mal'cev term for a variety V is a ternary term p(x,y,z) satisfying the identities p(x,x,z) ≈ z and p(x,z,z) ≈ x. Mal'cev's theorem (1954): V has a Mal'cev term iff every algebra in V is congruence-permutable — i.e., for any congruences α, β on A ∈ V, α∘β = β∘α (equivalently, α∨β = α∘β). Examples: groups have p(x,y,z)=xy⁻¹z; rings and modules have p(x,y,z)=x−y+z; Heyting algebras have p(x,y,z)=((x→y)→z)∧((z→y)→x). Congruence-distributive varieties: those where Con(A) is distributive for every A ∈ V. The Day condition (1970): V is congruence-distributive iff there exist ternary terms d_i(x,y,z) for i=0,…,n satisfying the Day identities: d_0(x,y,z)=x; d_n(x,y,z)=z; d_i(x,x,y)=d_{i+1}(x,x,y) for even i; d_i(x,y,y)=d_{i+1}(x,y,y) for odd i; and d_i(x,y,x)=x for all i. Congruence-modular varieties: Gumm's theorem (1981) provides a characterization via the shifting lemma and the existence of a Gumm term: there exists a ternary term q and a 4-ary term r such that q(x,x,z)=z, q(x,z,z)=r(x,z,z,z) with r satisfying certain absorption identities. The commutator [α,β] for congruences α,β ∈ Con(A) generalizes the group commutator: it is the smallest congruence γ such that for every term f and every pair of tuples (a,b) ∈ α and (c,d) ∈ β, we have f(a,c) ≡ f(a,d) (mod γ) whenever f(b,c) ≡ f(b,d) (mod α∧β). An algebra is abelian if [1_A,1_A] = 0_A. A variety is congruence-permutable iff it has a Mal'cev term; it is congruence-modular iff it satisfies the Shifting Lemma (Taylor, 1973): for congruences α,β,γ with α∧β ≤ γ, if [α,β] ≤ γ then certain shifting properties hold. The Freese-McKenzie commutator theory (1987) developed the full machinery of the commutator for modular varieties, extending the group-theoretic and ring-theoretic commutator to general algebraic structures.
Mal'cev conditions express purely syntactic (term existence) properties that are equivalent to deep lattice-theoretic properties of congruence lattices — syntactic conditions with semantic consequences.
Part 4

🔥 Tame Congruence Theory and Applications

Tame
Tame Congruence Theory, the Five Types, CSP Dichotomy, and the Finite Basis Problem
Tame congruence theory (Hobby & McKenzie, 1988) provides a classification of finite algebras based on the structure of their congruences. For a finite algebra A, consider a minimal congruence α (a cover of 0_A in Con(A)) and a trace — a minimal set of elements where α distinguishes points. The induced algebra on a trace is locally embeddable into one of five types: Type 1 (unary type): the induced structure is essentially unary; Type 2 (affine type): the trace is polynomially equivalent to a module over a finite ring; Type 3 (boolean type): the trace is polynomially equivalent to a boolean algebra; Type 4 (lattice type): the trace is polynomially equivalent to a distributive lattice; Type 5 (semilattice type): the trace is polynomially equivalent to a semilattice. The omitting types theorem: a variety V omits certain types depending on its Mal'cev conditions — e.g., congruence-permutable varieties omit types 1 and 5; congruence-distributive varieties omit types 1, 2, and 5; congruence-modular varieties that are not distributive omit type 1. The constraint satisfaction problem (CSP): given a relational structure B (the template), does a given instance A admit a homomorphism to B? The algebraic dichotomy conjecture (Bulatov-Jeavons-Krokhin, 2000s): every CSP is either NP-complete or polynomial-time solvable, characterized by the presence of a Taylor operation — a term t satisfying t(y,x,x,…,x) ≈ t(x,y,x,…,x) ≈ … ≈ t(x,x,…,x,y) (idempotent identities). This was proved independently by Bulatov (2017) and Zhuk (2020), now the Bulatov-Zhuk theorem. The finite basis problem: does a given finite algebra have a finite basis for its identities? Oates-Powell theorem (1964): every finite group has a finite basis of identities. Perkins (1969) gave a counterexample: a six-element semigroup (the "Perkins semigroup") that has no finite basis — it is inherently non-finitely based. Tarski's High School Problem (1960s): are the usual identities of exponentiation (x·y)·z=x·(y·z), x·y=y·x, x+0=x, x+(y+z)=(x+y)+z, x·(y+z)=x·y+x·z, x^1=x, x^{y+z}=x^y·x^z, (x·y)^z=x^z·y^z, (x^y)^z=x^{y·z} sufficient to prove all true identities of positive integers? Wilkie (1980) answered negatively: the identity (a+1)^a·(a+1)^{(a+1)} = ((a+1)^a)^{(a+1)}·(a+1)^{(a+1)^a} holds in ℕ but is not derivable from the High School identities — and in fact the variety generated by ℕ is not finitely based. Graph algebras (Kiss-Németi-Pálfy, 1983) encode directed graphs as algebras, transferring algebraic properties to graph-theoretic ones.
Tame congruence theory provides the most refined classification of finite algebras to date — with striking applications to computational complexity (the CSP dichotomy) and pure algebra (the finite basis problem).
