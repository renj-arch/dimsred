Homological Mirror Symmetry — Fukaya Categories, Derived Categories & the HMS Conjecture

Deep dive into Homological Mirror Symmetry — the Fukaya category Fuk(X) of a symplectic manifold (X, ω) whose objects are Lagrangian submanifolds with flat U(1) connections and whose morphisms are Floer cohomology groups, the A∞-category structure (Stasheff's associahedra governing homotopy associativity of composition), the derived category D^b Coh(Y) of coherent sheaves on a complex algebraic variety Y, the HMS conjecture (Kontsevich 1994) stating that for a mirror pair (X, Y), the derived Fukaya category of X is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on Y, the SYZ fibration (Strominger–Yau–Zaslow) interpreting mirror symmetry as T-duality along special Lagrangian tori, the Hodge theoretic aspects (variation of Hodge structures, the Griffiths period map), and applications to enumerative geometry (Gromov–Witten invariants, Donaldson–Thomas invariants via the DT/GW correspondence), and the categorical crepant resolution conjecture.

"Mirror symmetry is a phenomenon at the interface of geometry and physics in which two seemingly distinct geometries are secretly the same."
— Maxim Kontsevich
Part 1

🌀 A∞-Categories and the Fukaya Category

A∞ Structures
Stasheff's Associahedra, A∞-Algebras, and the Fukaya Category Construction
An A∞-category (Stasheff, 1963) is a category enriched over chain complexes with composition maps m_k: Hom(A_{k−1}, A_k) ⊗ … ⊗ Hom(A_0, A_1) → Hom(A_0, A_k) of degree 2−k satisfying the A∞-relations: Σ_{i+j+k = n} (−1)^{ε} m_{i+1+k} (id^{⊗i} ⊗ m_j ⊗ id^{⊗k}) = 0. For k=1, m_1² = 0 (the differential). For k=2, m_1 m_2 = m_2 (m_1 ⊗ id + id ⊗ m_1) (Leibniz rule). For k=3, the associator m_2 (m_2 ⊗ id − id ⊗ m_2) = m_1 m_3 + m_3 (m_1 ⊗ id⊗ id + id ⊗ m_1 ⊗ id + id ⊗ id ⊗ m_1) — the failure of associativity is controlled by m_3, which is itself chain homotopic to zero via m_4, and so on. The coherence is encoded by the Stasheff associahedra K_n, whose vertices correspond to the ways of parenthesizing n arguments. The Fukaya category Fuk(X) of a symplectic manifold (X, ω) has objects: Lagrangian submanifolds L ⊂ X (dim L = ½ dim X, ω|_L = 0) equipped with a spin structure and a flat U(1) connection (a local system). Morphisms: Hom(L_0, L_1) = CF^*(L_0, L_1), the Lagrangian Floer cohomology chain complex generated by intersection points p ∈ L_0 ∩ L_1. The composition m_2: CF^*(L_0, L_1) ⊗ CF^*(L_1, L_2) → CF^*(L_0, L_2) counts pseudoholomorphic triangles with boundary on the three Lagrangians. Higher compositions m_k count pseudoholomorphic (k+1)-gons. The weak bounding cochains and obstruction theory (Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono) handle the obstructions to defining A∞-structures when b_1(L) ≠ 0, using the Maurer–Cartan equation m(1) + m_1(b) + m_2(b, b) + … = 0 for bounding cochain b ∈ H^1(L; ℂ). The Donaldson–Fukaya category includes objects with unitary local systems and bounding cochains.
The Fukaya category encodes the symplectic geometry of Lagrangian submanifolds through an A∞-structure whose higher products count pseudoholomorphic polygons.

🌿 Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves

Derived Categories
The Bounded Derived Category, Triangulated Structure, and the Reconstruction Theorem
For a smooth projective variety Y over ℂ, let Coh(Y) be the abelian category of coherent sheaves. The bounded derived category D^b Coh(Y) has objects bounded complexes of coherent sheaves, with quasi-isomorphisms inverted. It is a triangulated category with the shift functor [1] and distinguished triangles X → Y → Z → X[1] coming from short exact sequences in Coh(Y). The Ext groups: Hom_{D^b}(A, B[k]) = Ext^k(A, B). The Serre functor S: D^b Coh(Y) → D^b Coh(Y) given by S(ℱ) = ℱ ⊗ ω_Y [dim Y] satisfies Hom(A, B) ≅ Hom(B, S(A))^* (Serre duality). The Orlov spectrum and Bondal–Kapranov enhanced triangulated categories. Semiorthogonal decompositions D^b Coh(Y) = ⟨𝒜_1, …, 𝒜_n⟩ where each 𝒜_i is a full triangulated subcategory and Hom(𝒜_j, 𝒜_i) = 0 for j > i. For projective space ℙ^n, D^b Coh(ℙ^n) = ⟨𝒪, 𝒪(1), …, 𝒪(n)⟩ (Beilinson's exceptional collection). The Reconstruction theorem (Bondal–Orlov): a smooth projective variety Y with ample canonical or anticanonical bundle can be reconstructed from its derived category D^b Coh(Y) up to isomorphism — the Néron–Severi lattice, the Chow motive and the Mukai vector v(ℱ) = ch(ℱ) · √td(Y) play key roles. The Fourier–Mukai transform: for two varieties Y₁, Y₂, a Fourier–Mukai kernel 𝒫 ∈ D^b Coh(Y₁ × Y₂) defines an equivalence Φ_𝒫: D^b Coh(Y₁) → D^b Coh(Y₂) by Φ_𝒫(−) = Rπ_{2*}(π₁^*(−) ⊗^L 𝒫). Orlov's theorem: any fully faithful exact functor D^b Coh(Y₁) → D^b Coh(Y₂) with left and right adjoints is a Fourier–Mukai transform.
The derived category of coherent sheaves is a fundamental invariant of an algebraic variety, encoding its geometry through triangulated and Fourier–Mukai structures.
Part 3

🔮 The Homological Mirror Symmetry Conjecture

HMS
Kontsevich's Conjecture, Calabi–Yau Varieties, and Mirror Pairs
The Homological Mirror Symmetry conjecture (Kontsevich, 1994, ICM Zürich): For a mirror pair of Calabi–Yau varieties (X, Y) of dimension n, there is a quasi-equivalence of A∞-categories D^π Fuk(X) ≅ D^b Coh(Y), where D^π Fuk(X) is the split-closed derived Fukaya category (the idempotent completion of the A∞-category of twisted complexes). The mirror map identifies the complex moduli of Y (the space of complex structures) with the Kähler moduli of X (the space of symplectic forms and B-fields). The SYZ conjecture (Strominger–Yau–Zaslow, 1996): Mirror symmetry arises from a special Lagrangian torus fibration f: X → B and its dual fibration f^∨: Y → B (T-duality). Each smooth fiber is a special Lagrangian torus T^n ⊂ X, and the mirror Y is obtained by dualizing the torus fibers. The Fukaya category of X is built from Lagrangian tori (fibers of f) and their intersections; the derived category of Y is built from skyscraper sheaves of points (dual tori). The Gross–Siebert program builds mirror pairs via tropical geometry and log structures — extending SYZ to the degenerate case (walls, scattering diagrams, and slab functions). The Kontsevich–Soibelman approach: the mirror is the Maurer–Cartan space of the Fukaya category — the set of solutions to the A∞-Maurer–Cartan equation modulo gauge equivalence. The Seidel representation: the Dehn twist around a Lagrangian sphere L ≅ S^n in a symplectic Calabi–Yau of dimension n gives an autoequivalence of Fuk(X), corresponding under mirror symmetry to the spherical twist around the corresponding sheaf on Y.
The HMS conjecture provides a categorical framework for mirror symmetry, equating the symplectic geometry of one manifold with the complex geometry of its mirror.

⚡ Evidence and Applications

Proofs
HMS for Tori, Quartic Surfaces, and the Categorical Crepant Resolution Conjecture
The first proved case: HMS for elliptic curves (Polishchuk–Zaslow, 1997). For an elliptic curve E_τ = ℂ/(ℤ + τℤ), its mirror is the same elliptic curve E_{τ^∨} with the dual complex parameter. The Fukaya category of E_τ is generated by Lagrangian branes: straight lines of rational slope with flat connections. The derived category D^b Coh(E_{τ^∨}) has an exceptional collection generating it. The equivalence sends a Lagrangian brane of slope r/s to a vector bundle of rank r and degree s on the mirror. HMS for the quartic K3 surface (Seidel, 2004): the Fano variety of lines on a quartic surface in ℙ³ is a symplectic manifold; HMS relates its Fukaya category to the derived category of the mirror quartic. The HMS for the quintic threefold (the original Calabi–Yau example from Candelas–delaOssa–Green–Parkes) is still open but deep progress exists via orbi-curves (Sheridan, 2015) proving HMS for many Calabi–Yau hypersurfaces in toric varieties using wrapped Fukaya categories. The categorical crepant resolution conjecture (Bondal–Orlov, Kawamata): for a variety with Gorenstein singularities, different crepant resolutions have equivalent derived categories — the McKay correspondence for G-Hilbert schemes. The Gromov–Witten/Donaldson–Thomas correspondence (Maulik–Nekrasov–Okounkov–Pandharipande) relates GW invariants of X to DT invariants of its mirror Y via the topological vertex. The Kontsevich–Soibelman wall-crossing formula for DT invariants. The Virasoro conjecture and Crepant resolution conjecture in Gromov–Witten theory.
HMS has been proved for many important examples (elliptic curves, abelian varieties, toric Calabi–Yaus) and continues to drive progress in enumerative geometry and categorical algebra.

Wrapped Fukaya Categories and Stop Removal

The wrapped Fukaya category W(X) of a Liouville manifold X is an A∞‑category whose objects are exact Lagrangian submanifolds with non‑compact ends wrapped by the Liouville flow. Unlike the compact Fukaya category, morphism spaces are defined by Hamiltonian chords that go to infinity, and the differential counts punctured holomorphic disks with boundary on the Lagrangians. A key structural result — the stop‑removal theorem — states that if a Liouville sector X has a stop 𝔽 (a Legendrian submanifold at infinity), the partially wrapped category W(X,𝔽) embeds fully faithfully into W(X) after removing the stop. This allows one to compute wrapped categories of complicated manifolds by cutting them into simpler sectors, computing the partially wrapped categories, and gluing via the stop‑removal functor.

In practice, stop removal is implemented by a sequence of elementary surgeries on the stop, each corresponding to a mutation of the associated A∞‑category. The process is controlled by the wrapped Floer cohomology of the stop itself, a Legendrian contact homology algebra. For a Weinstein manifold presented by a Weinstein handlebody, the wrapped Fukaya category is generated by the cocores of the handles, and the stop‑removal theorem identifies it with the category of modules over the Legendrian algebra of the attaching spheres. This perspective underlies the proof of HMS for many affine varieties, notably the work of Ganatra–Pardon–Shende showing that the wrapped Fukaya category of a Weinstein manifold is equivalent to the category of singularities of its mirror Landau–Ginzburg model.

Examiners often test the distinction between the compact Fukaya category Fuk(X) (built from compact exact Lagrangians) and the wrapped version W(X). A typical trap is to assume that the split‑closure of Fuk(X) already yields the derived Fukaya category D^π Fuk(X) appearing in Kontsevich's conjecture; in fact, for a compact Calabi–Yau X, D^π Fuk(X) is the split‑closed derived category of Fuk(X), while W(X) is defined for non‑compact (Liouville/Weinstein) manifolds. The equivalence with D^b Coh(Y) uses the compact Fukaya category on the symplectic side.

Generation by Spherical Objects and the Seidel–Thomas Twist

A spherical object S in a triangulated category 𝒯 with Serre functor 𝕊 satisfies Hom^*(S,S) ≅ H^*(S^n) for some n and 𝕊(S) ≅ S[n]. In the derived category D^b Coh(Y) of a Calabi–Yau variety Y of dimension n, any sheaf ℰ with Ext^*(ℰ,ℰ) ≅ H^*(S^n) is spherical; examples include structure sheaves of (−2)‑curves on K3 surfaces and certain vector bundles on quintic threefolds. Seidel and Thomas constructed an autoequivalence T_S of 𝒯, the spherical twist, defined by the exact triangle RHom(S,−) ⊗ S → Id → T_S. On the symplectic side, a Lagrangian sphere L ≅ S^n in a symplectic Calabi–Yau X gives a Dehn twist τ_L acting on Fuk(X); under HMS the Dehn twist corresponds precisely to the spherical twist of the mirror sheaf.

Generation criteria are central to proving HMS. A set of objects {E_i} split‑generates a triangulated category if the smallest thick subcategory containing them is the whole category. For D^b Coh(Y), Bondal–Orlov showed that a full strong exceptional collection split‑generates; for Fano varieties such collections often exist. On the Fukaya side, Abouzaid–Seidel–Smith proved that a collection of Lagrangian spheres forming an A_n or D_n configuration split‑generates the wrapped Fukaya category of the corresponding Milnor fibre. The Seidel representation of the mapping class group of a symplectic manifold into the autoequivalence group of its Fukaya category is built from these spherical twists, and its image provides a powerful invariant distinguishing symplectic manifolds that are diffeomorphic but not symplectomorphic.

In exam questions, the spherical twist triangle is frequently written incorrectly as S ⊗ RHom(S,−) → Id → T_S (order of tensor factors reversed) or the shift is omitted. Remember that the twist functor fits into RHom(S,−) ⊗ S → Id → T_S and that T_S is an equivalence with inverse given (up to shift) by the triangle T_S-1 → Id → RHom(−,S)^* ⊗ S. The dimension shift n appears in the Serre duality isomorphism Hom^*(S,−) ≅ Hom^*(−,S[n])^*.

Worked Example: Spherical Twist on a K3 Surface
Let Y be a K3 surface containing a smooth rational curve C ≅ ℙ¹ with normal bundle 𝒪(−2). Show that the structure sheaf 𝒪_C is a 2‑spherical object in D^b Coh(Y) and write the spherical twist triangle for an arbitrary object ℰ.
On a K3 surface the canonical bundle is trivial, so Serre duality gives Ext^i(𝒪_C,𝒪_C) ≅ Ext2−i(𝒪_C,𝒪_C)^*. Since C ≅ ℙ¹ and NC/Y ≅ 𝒪(−2), we have Ext0(𝒪_C,𝒪_C) = ℂ, Ext1(𝒪_C,𝒪_C) = 0, Ext2(𝒪_C,𝒪_C) = ℂ. Thus Ext^*(𝒪_C,𝒪_C) ≅ H^*(S2) and 𝕊(𝒪_C) ≅ 𝒪_C[2], so 𝒪_C is 2‑spherical. The spherical twist T𝒪_C fits into the exact triangle RHom(𝒪_C,ℰ) ⊗ 𝒪_C → ℰ → T𝒪_C(ℰ). Concretely, RHom(𝒪_C,ℰ) ≅ RΓ(C,ℰ|_C), so the map is evaluation of sections of ℰ along C tensored with the structure sheaf of C.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to identify a spherical object, first compute its Ext‑algebra using the adjunction formula and Serre duality; the answer must match the cohomology ring of a sphere. For twist triangles, always write the evaluation map RHom(S,−) ⊗ S → Id and note the shift equals the sphere dimension. Memorise the standard examples: (−2)‑curves on K3, structure sheaves of points on Calabi–Yau threefolds, and the zero‑section in the cotangent bundle of S^n.

Mirror Symmetry for Log Calabi–Yau Varieties and the Gross–Siebert Program

A log Calabi–Yau pair (Y,D) consists of a smooth projective variety Y and a reduced simple normal‑crossing divisor D such that K_Y + D ∼ 0. The complement U = Y D is a non‑compact Calabi–Yau variety. The Gross–Siebert program constructs the mirror of U by degenerating Y to a union of toric varieties glued along toric strata, encoding the degeneration data in a tropical manifold (a piecewise‑linear space with integral affine structure and a multi‑valued convex function). The mirror is then built from the dual tropical manifold via a scattering diagram that records wall‑crossing automorphisms of a torus algebra. This tropical approach extends the SYZ picture to the case where the special Lagrangian fibration has singular fibres and the base is not a manifold but a singular affine space.

Key technical tools are log structures (in the sense of Fontaine–Illusie–Kato) which allow one to keep track of the divisor D during deformation, and theta functions which are canonical sections of line bundles on the mirror constructed by counting broken lines in the scattering diagram. The Gross–Siebert reconstruction theorem states that the canonical algebra generated by theta functions is isomorphic to the coordinate ring of the mirror affine variety U^∨. For cluster varieties, this recovers the Fock–Goncharov cluster ensemble and proves that the mirror of a cluster variety is its Langlands dual.

In examinations, the distinction between the tropical vertex (the local scattering diagram around a codimension‑two stratum) and the global scattering diagram is a common source of confusion. The tropical vertex is a product of elementary automorphisms θ(m) = exp(∑k≥1 (zkm/k) ∂/∂zkm) attached to each ray; the global diagram is obtained by iterated scattering, and consistency (the product around any loop being identity) is equivalent to the Maurer–Cartan equation for the associated A∞‑structure. Another frequent trap is to conflate the log Gromov–Witten invariants of (Y,D) with the ordinary Gromov–Witten invariants of Y; the former count curves with prescribed tangency to D and are the enumerative invariants that the theta functions compute.

Categorical Enumerative Geometry: Open Gromov–Witten Invariants and Disk Potentials

Open Gromov–Witten invariants count holomorphic disks with boundary on a Lagrangian submanifold L in a symplectic manifold X. In the A∞‑framework, the m_k operations of the Fukaya category are defined by counting rigid disks with k+1 boundary punctures; the generating function of these counts is the disk potential W_L. For a monotone Lagrangian torus fibre in a toric Fano manifold, Cho–Oh and Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono showed that W_L coincides with the Landau–Ginzburg superpotential of the mirror. The critical points of W_L correspond to objects of the Fukaya category that are unobstructed (i.e. m0 = 0), and the Jacobian ring Jac(W_L) is isomorphic to the endomorphism algebra of the corresponding object.

On the B‑side, the disk potential is mirrored by the period integrals of the holomorphic volume form on the mirror Calabi–Yau. The B‑model disk potential can be computed via the B‑model A∞‑structure on the category of matrix factorizations of the superpotential, as developed by Polishchuk–Vaintrob and later by Sheridan for wrapped Fukaya categories. The equivalence of A‑model and B‑model disk potentials is a concrete, computable consequence of HMS that has been verified in many examples, including the quintic threefold (via the mirror quintic) and toric Calabi–Yau threefolds (via the topological vertex and open mirror symmetry).

Examiners often ask to derive the superpotential for a given toric manifold from its fan data. The standard formula: for a toric Fano variety X_Σ with primitive generators v_i of the rays, the superpotential on the mirror torus (ℂ^*)^n is W = ∑i=1^m zv_i where zv_i = z1vi1 ⋯ z_nvin. A common mistake is to omit the contributions from non‑basic disks (multiple covers) which are automatically incorporated by the A∞‑relations; the correct potential is the formal sum over all homotopy classes of disks weighted by their symplectic area and open Gromov–Witten invariants. In the monotone case, multiple covers are suppressed and the formula above is exact.

Hochschild Homology and the Closed‑Open String Map

Hochschild homology of an A∞‑category provides the natural target for the closed‑open string map, which sends quantum cohomology classes of a symplectic manifold to endomorphisms of the identity functor on its Fukaya category. For a compact symplectic manifold (X,ω) the Fukaya category Fuk(X) is a Z‑graded A∞‑category over the Novikov field Λ. Its Hochschild homology HH∗(Fuk(X)) carries a canonical isomorphism with the quantum cohomology QH∗(X;Λ) as graded vector spaces, a result established by Abouzaid, Ganatra, Pardon, and Shende. The closed‑open map CO: QH∗(X) → HH∗(Fuk(X)) is defined by counting pearl complexes with one interior marked point mapping to a cycle representing a quantum cohomology class and boundary on a Lagrangian. On the B‑side, for a Calabi–Yau variety Y the derived category DᵇCoh(Y) has Hochschild homology isomorphic to ⊕ₚ Hⁿ⁻ᵖ(Y,Ωᵖ) via the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism. Mirror symmetry conjectures that the closed‑open map on the A‑side corresponds under the HMS equivalence to the natural map from the B‑model closed string sector (the Jacobian ring of the superpotential) to HH∗(DᵇCoh(Y)). In practice, computing CO for toric Fano varieties reduces to evaluating the superpotential on the critical locus; the resulting map is an isomorphism after splitting idempotents, confirming the generation criterion of Abouzaid. Examiners often test the distinction between the open‑closed map OC: HH∗(Fuk(X)) → QH∗(X) and the closed‑open map CO; the former is defined by counting disks with an interior puncture, while the latter uses a pearl complex. A common mistake is to assume CO is always an isomorphism; it is an isomorphism precisely when the Fukaya category is split‑generated by the object used to define the map.

Matrix Factorizations and the B‑Model Fukaya Category

For a Landau–Ginzburg model (Y,W) with Y a smooth variety and W a regular function, the B‑model category of matrix factorizations MF(Y,W) is a triangulated dg‑category whose objects are Z/2‑graded coherent sheaves E = E⁰ ⊕ E¹ equipped with an odd endomorphism d_E satisfying d_E² = W·id_E. Orlov proved that MF(Y,W) is equivalent to the singularity category Dsg(W⁻¹(0)) when W has isolated critical points. In the context of HMS, the mirror of a Fano variety X is a Landau–Ginzburg model (Y,W) where Y is a toric variety and W is the superpotential. The Fukaya category of X (or its wrapped version for the mirror) is conjecturally equivalent to MF(Y,W). The A∞‑structure on MF(Y,W) can be described explicitly using the curved dg‑algebra of endomorphisms of a generator; the curvature is precisely W. The disk potential on the A‑side matches the generating function of open Gromov–Witten invariants, while on the B‑side it is computed by the period integrals of the holomorphic volume form on the mirror Calabi–Yau. For the quintic threefold, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model on (C*)⁵ with superpotential W = x₁+…+x₅+ψ/(x₁⋯x₅). The category of matrix factorizations of this W is generated by the structure sheaf of the critical locus, and its Hochschild homology recovers the Jacobian ring Jac(W). In examinations, a typical question asks to construct the matrix factorization corresponding to a given Lagrangian torus fibre in a toric Fano variety. The answer uses the Koszul matrix factorization associated to the equations defining the fibre in the mirror torus. Another frequent trap is confusing the Z‑graded Fukaya category of a Calabi–Yau with the Z/2‑graded matrix factorization category; the grading shift on the A‑side corresponds to the tensor product with the canonical bundle on the B‑side.

Worked Example: Constructing a Matrix Factorization for the Quintic Mirror
Let the mirror of the quintic threefold be the Landau–Ginzburg model ((C*)⁵, W) with W = x₁+x₂+x₃+x₄+x₅+ψ/(x₁x₂x₃x₄x₅). Construct the rank‑one matrix factorization corresponding to the Lagrangian torus fibre L ⊂ X.
The torus fibre L is mirror to the structure sheaf Opt of a point in the critical locus of W. The critical equations are ∂W/∂x_i = 1 - ψ/(x₁⋯x₅)·(1/x_i) = 0, giving x_i⁵ = ψ. Choose a solution (ζ₁,…,ζ₅) with ζ_i⁵ = ψ. The Koszul matrix factorization for the regular sequence (x_i-ζ_i) is E = Λ*(C⁵) with differential d = Σ (x_i-ζ_i) e_i∧ + Σ ∂W/∂x_i ιe_i. Since ∂W/∂x_i vanishes at the critical point, d² = W·id. This rank‑32 matrix factorization generates MF((C*)⁵,W) and corresponds under HMS to the object L in Fuk(X).
Exam Strategy
When asked to relate a Lagrangian to a matrix factorization, first identify the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model and its critical locus. Write the Koszul factorization for the defining equations of the corresponding point. Remember that the grading on the A‑side is Z, while the B‑side is Z/2; the shift functor [1] on Fuk(X) corresponds to tensoring with the canonical bundle on the B‑side.

Stability Conditions and Bridgeland Stability on Derived Categories

Bridgeland stability conditions provide a geometric way to parametrize the space of stability conditions on a triangulated category, in particular on DᵇCoh(Y) for a Calabi–Yau variety Y. A stability condition σ = (Z,𝒫) consists of a central charge Z: K₀(DᵇCoh(Y)) → C, a group homomorphism, and a slicing 𝒫 of the category into semistable objects of given phase φ ∈ R, satisfying the Harder–Narasimhan property and the support property. For a Calabi–Yau threefold, the central charge is often taken as Z(E) = -∫_Y e-B-iω ch(E) √td(Y) for a complexified Kähler class B+iω. The space Stab(DᵇCoh(Y)) carries a natural complex manifold structure; the group of autoequivalences acts by deck transformations. Mirror symmetry conjectures that Stab(DᵇCoh(Y)) is isomorphic to the complexified Kähler moduli space of the mirror X, and that the autoequivalence group corresponds to the symplectic mapping class group of X. In the case of the quintic, the stability manifold has been partially described by Bridgeland and others; the large volume limit corresponds to Gieseker stability, while the orbifold point corresponds to a different chamber. Examiners may ask to compute the central charge of a given object (e.g., the structure sheaf of a line) in terms of the mirror periods. The formula Z(O_L) = ∫_L Ω, where Ω is the holomorphic volume form on the mirror, is a direct consequence of the HMS equivalence. A common error is to confuse the phase of an object with its slope; the phase is arg Z(E) ∈ (0,π], while the slope is defined only for sheaves on a projective variety with respect to an ample divisor.

Homological Mirror Symmetry for Fano Varieties and the SYZ Fibration

The Strominger–Yau–Zaslow (SYZ) conjecture asserts that mirror Calabi–Yau manifolds admit dual special Lagrangian torus fibrations. For Fano varieties, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model (Y,W) where Y is a non‑compact Calabi–Yau and W is a proper map to C. For toric Fano varieties, the Fukaya category of X is generated by the Lagrangian torus fibres of an SYZ fibration on the complement of an anticanonical divisor D. The wrapped Fukaya category of the Liouville manifold XD is equivalent to the category of matrix factorizations of W. The SYZ fibration provides a geometric construction of the mirror: the base of the fibration is an integral affine manifold with singularities, and the mirror is obtained by dualizing the torus fibres and correcting the gluing by instanton contributions counted by holomorphic disks. The Gross–Siebert program gives an algebraic reconstruction of the mirror from the tropical data of the affine base. In the HMS statement for Fano varieties, one compares the split‑closed derived Fukaya category of X (or the wrapped category of XD) with the derived category of singularities of W. The equivalence sends a Lagrangian section of the SYZ fibration to a skyscraper sheaf on the mirror, and a Lagrangian fibre to the structure sheaf of the mirror. A typical examination question asks to describe the superpotential W for a toric Fano variety X_Σ in terms of the fan Σ. The answer is W = Σi=1^m zv_i where v_i are the primitive generators of the rays of Σ. Another trap is to assume that every Fano variety admits an SYZ fibration; the existence is known for toric Fano varieties and for some special cases, but not in general.

Categorical Mirror Symmetry for Landau–Ginzburg Models

Beyond the Calabi–Yau case, HMS extends to pairs (X,W) where X is a smooth variety and W a regular function, i.e. Landau–Ginzburg models. The A‑model is the Fukaya–Seidel category FS(W) of vanishing cycles for a Lefschetz fibration W: X → C; its objects are graded Lagrangian thimbles and its morphisms are Floer complexes. The B‑model is the category of matrix factorizations MF(Y,W^∨) of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model. The equivalence FS(W) ≃ MF(Y,W^∨) was proved by Seidel for A‑type singularities and by many authors for more general cases. The central charge on the A‑side is given by the period integrals of the holomorphic volume form over the thimbles; on the B‑side it is the residue pairing on the Jacobian ring. The monodromy of the Lefschetz fibration acts on FS(W) by spherical twists, mirroring the action of the braid group on the derived category of the mirror. In the case of the A_n singularity W = xn+1, the Fukaya–Seidel category is generated by n vanishing cycles with intersection pattern given by the A_n quiver; the mirror is the category of representations of the same quiver. Examiners often test the relationship between the spherical twist on the A‑side and the mutation of exceptional collections on the B‑side. A frequent mistake is to treat the Fukaya–Seidel category as a Z‑graded category; it is naturally Z‑graded only after a choice of grading on the vanishing cycles, and the grading shift corresponds to tensoring with the canonical bundle on the B‑side.

Higher Categorical Structures in HMS: ∞‑Categories and Localization

The equivalence at the heart of Homological Mirror Symmetry is not merely an equivalence of triangulated categories; it is an equivalence of ∞‑categories, or more concretely of pretriangulated A∞‑categories up to quasi‑equivalence. The derived category DᵇCoh(X) is the homotopy category of the dg‑enhancement Perf(X). On the symplectic side, the Fukaya category Fuk(M) is an A∞‑category whose higher products mₖ count pseudoholomorphic polygons. To compare the two sides one passes to the ∞‑categorical localizations, obtaining stable ∞‑categories Perf(X) and Fuk(M)̂. The HMS conjecture posits an exact equivalence Perf(X) ≃ Fuk(M)̂. This formulation retains the higher homotopical data lost in triangulated homotopy categories. In practice, the passage to ∞‑categories uses the dg‑nerve of Perf(X) and the A∞‑nerve of Fuk(M). The resulting ∞‑categories are idempotent complete and admit a canonical t‑structure whose heart on the B‑side is Coh(X) and on the A‑side is a category of Lagrangian branes. The localization perspective clarifies the split‑closure: the split‑closed derived Fukaya category is the idempotent completion of the homotopy category, while the ∞‑categorical version is already idempotent complete. A key tool is Morita theory for dg‑ and A∞‑categories, which reduces verifying an equivalence to checking that a functor induces quasi‑isomorphisms on morphism complexes and generates the target under shifts, cones, and direct summands. This framework is essential for non‑compact Calabi–Yau manifolds, where wrapped Fukaya categories and categories of singularities are naturally ∞‑categorical.

Mirror Symmetry for Orbifolds and Stacky Fukaya Categories

When the mirror pair involves orbifolds or Deligne–Mumford stacks, both models must incorporate stacky structures. On the B‑side, DᵇCoh(𝔛) for a smooth proper Deligne–Mumford stack 𝔛 is the derived category of complexes of 𝒪𝔛‑modules with quasi‑coherent cohomology. It admits a dg‑enhancement and a Serre functor given by tensoring with ω𝔛[dim 𝔛]. On the A‑side, the Fukaya category of an orbifold symplectic manifold (M,ω) with finite group action is replaced by the equivariant Fukaya category Fukᴳ(M) or, more generally, the Fukaya category of the associated étale groupoid. Objects are G‑equivariant Lagrangian branes; morphisms use G‑invariant almost complex structures and a careful treatment of orbifold marked points on pseudoholomorphic curves. The resulting A∞‑category is pretriangulated, and its split‑closed derived category is expected to be equivalent to DᵇCoh(𝔛) for the mirror stack 𝔛. A concrete class of examples is weighted projective spaces ℙ(a₀,…,aₙ) and their mirrors, which are Landau–Ginzburg models with orbifold singularities. In these cases the HMS equivalence has been proved by Seidel, Sheridan, and others using Seidel’s long exact sequence for Dehn twists and the classification of spherical objects. The stacky perspective resolves the central charge mismatch: the orbifold Riemann–Roch formula includes twisted sector contributions, corresponding on the A‑side to Lagrangians intersecting the orbifold locus. The closed‑open string map lands in orbifold Hochschild cohomology HH*(𝔛), graded by the age of twisted sectors; this matches the Maslov index grading on the A‑side after a suitable shift. A common examination question asks to describe the orbifold Fukaya category of a global quotient [M/G] and explain why the ordinary Fukaya category of the coarse moduli space is insufficient. The answer emphasizes that the coarse space loses the isotropy data needed to reconstruct twisted sectors and the correct Serre functor.

Categorical Mirror Symmetry for Cluster Varieties

Cluster varieties provide a class of examples where both models admit combinatorial descriptions. A cluster variety 𝒜 is constructed from a seed (Q, B) — a quiver Q and a skew‑symmetric matrix B — by gluing algebraic tori along cluster mutations. Its mirror is the dual cluster variety 𝒳, which carries a Poisson structure and a potential W. The HMS conjecture for cluster varieties asserts an equivalence between the wrapped Fukaya category of a Liouville manifold associated to 𝒜 and the derived category of coherent sheaves on 𝒳, or more precisely the category of matrix factorizations of W on 𝒳. The wrapped Fukaya category is generated by Lagrangian thimbles corresponding to the initial seed; seed mutations correspond to spherical twists. On the B‑side, DᵇCoh(𝒳) is generated by an exceptional collection of line bundles associated to the same seed, and cluster mutations act by mutations of exceptional collections. The equivalence is compatible with the cluster modular group action. A key result by Gross, Hacking, Keel, and Kontsevich constructs 𝒳 as a log Calabi–Yau variety with a canonical theta function basis. Theta functions provide a basis of the coordinate ring of 𝒳 and correspond to counts of holomorphic disks with boundary on the Lagrangian skeleton. The HMS equivalence sends a theta function to the Floer cohomology of the corresponding Lagrangian. This combinatorial description allows computation of open Gromov–Witten invariants and verification of HMS in many cases, including cluster varieties from Grassmannians and double Bruhat cells. In examinations, a typical problem asks to describe the quiver mutation corresponding to a spherical twist on a Lagrangian sphere, or to compute the superpotential W for a cluster variety of type Aₙ. The answer uses the fact that the superpotential is the sum of the frozen variables in the initial seed, and that the spherical twist acts by the Seidel–Thomas twist, matching mutation of the exceptional collection.

The Fukaya Category of a Weinstein Manifold and its Skeleton

A Weinstein manifold (W, λ, φ) is a Liouville manifold with an exhausting Morse function φ that is plurisubharmonic for a compatible almost complex structure. The skeleton 𝔖𝔨(W) is the union of stable manifolds of the critical points of φ; it is a Lagrangian CW complex capturing the symplectic topology of W. The wrapped Fukaya category WFuk(W) is generated by the cocores of the critical handles, Lagrangian disks intersecting the skeleton transversely at a single point. The endomorphism algebra of the cocores — the wrapped Fukaya algebra — is computed combinatorially from the Legendrian link of the attaching spheres in the contact boundary. This algebra is quasi‑isomorphic to the Chekanov–Eliashberg dg‑algebra of the Legendrian link, and its homology is symplectic cohomology SH*(W). The HMS conjecture for Weinstein manifolds states that WFuk(W) is equivalent to DᵇCoh(Spec SH⁰(W)) or, more generally, to matrix factorizations of a superpotential on the mirror. The skeleton provides a geometric bridge: the category of constructible sheaves on 𝔖𝔨(W) with singular support in conormal directions is equivalent to WFuk(W) by the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence extended to the wrapped setting. This microlocal perspective translates Lagrangian intersection questions into sheaf cohomology on a stratified space. In practice, the skeleton is often a union of Lagrangian spheres and disks whose intersection pattern is encoded by a quiver with potential. The wrapped Fukaya category is then the derived category of representations of this quiver with potential, matching the B‑model via the Ginzburg dg‑algebra. Examination questions frequently ask to construct the skeleton of a given Weinstein manifold (e.g., plumbing of T*Sⁿ according to a Dynkin diagram) and write down the corresponding quiver with potential. The key steps: identify critical points of the Morse function, determine attaching Legendrians, compute their Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra, and extract the quiver with potential from its degree‑0 part.

Homological Mirror Symmetry for Calabi–Yau Categories and the Calabi–Yau Property

A Calabi–Yau category of dimension d is a triangulated category 𝒞 with Serre functor S ≅ [d]. Both DᵇCoh(X) for a Calabi–Yau variety X of dimension d and the Fukaya category of a symplectic Calabi–Yau manifold M of dimension 2d are Calabi–Yau categories of dimension d. The HMS equivalence intertwines the Serre functors. This compatibility imposes strong constraints: the image of a spherical object must be spherical, and spherical twists correspond. The Calabi–Yau property implies Hochschild homology HH*(𝒞) carries a non‑degenerate pairing of degree −d — the Mukai pairing on the B‑side and Poincaré duality on symplectic cohomology on the A‑side. The closed‑open map CO: HH*(Fuk(M)) → SH*(M) is a map of Calabi–Yau algebras; its compatibility with the pairings is a key check of HMS. For non‑commutative Calabi–Yau categories, such as the derived category of a Ginzburg dg‑algebra from a quiver with potential, HMS predicts an equivalence with the wrapped Fukaya category of a Weinstein manifold whose skeleton is the corresponding Lagrangian CW complex. This has been verified for a large class of quivers from dimer models on toric Calabi–Yau threefolds. A common examination question asks to prove that the Serre functor on DᵇCoh(X) for a Calabi–Yau variety X is tensoring with ωX followed by [dim X], and to explain why this matches the Fukaya Serre functor given by Poincaré duality HF*(L₀, L₁) ≅ HF*(L₁, L₀)∨[−dim M]. The answer uses the triviality of ωX on a Calabi–Yau variety, reducing the Serre functor to the shift, and the symplectic form providing a canonical isomorphism between Floer complexes after a degree shift.

  • Neglecting orbifold twisted sectors when computing the central charge; the age grading contributes to the phase of the central charge.
  • Treating cluster mutations as ordinary mutations of exceptional collections without the accompanying spherical twist on the A‑side.
  • Assuming the skeleton of a Weinstein manifold is always a smooth Lagrangian; it is generally a singular Lagrangian CW complex.
  • Omitting the shift by the Calabi–Yau dimension when matching Serre functors; the Serre functor is [d] not the identity.
  • Using the ordinary Fukaya category instead of the wrapped Fukaya category for non‑compact Calabi–Yau manifolds; the wrapped category is required for generation.
  • Forgetting that the closed‑open map goes from Hochschild homology of the Fukaya category to symplectic cohomology, while the open‑closed map goes in the opposite direction.

Exact Fukaya Categories and the Role of Exactness in Mirror Symmetry

The exact Fukaya category is defined for an exact symplectic manifold (M, ω = dλ) whose Liouville form λ restricts to an exact one‑form on every Lagrangian submanifold L ⊂ M. Exactness eliminates non‑constant holomorphic disks with boundary on L having zero symplectic area, because the action functional ∫u λ is strictly positive for any non‑constant disk u. This positivity guarantees that the Floer differential ∂ counts only rigid disks of positive area, preventing disk bubbling that would obstruct the A∞‑relations. Consequently, the exact Fukaya category Fukex(M) is a strictly unital A∞‑category over the Novikov field (with trivial valuation), and its cohomology category H0Fukex(M) is triangulated.

On the mirror side, exactness corresponds to the condition that the mirror variety carries a trivial canonical bundle and that the superpotential (if present) is a regular function rather than a section of a non‑trivial line bundle. For a Weinstein manifold W, the wrapped Fukaya category WFuk(W) is the natural exact Fukaya category; its objects are exact Lagrangians that are cylindrical at infinity. The HMS conjecture for exact symplectic manifolds asserts an equivalence WFuk(W) ≃ DbCoh(X) where X is an affine variety with trivial canonical bundle, or more generally an equivalence with the derived category of matrix factorizations of a regular function on X. The exactness hypothesis simplifies the construction of the open‑closed string map CO: HH*(Fukex(M)) → SH*(M) and the proof that this map is a morphism of Calabi–Yau algebras. In the non‑exact setting, one must work with the Novikov ring and incorporate area weights, complicating the comparison with algebraic geometry over ℂ.

A key technical tool is the Abouzaid generation criterion: a collection of exact Lagrangians {Li} split‑generates Fukex(M) if and only if the closed‑open map SH*(M) → ⊕i HF*(Li, Li) hits the unit in degree zero. Equivalently, the open‑closed map OC: ⊕i HH*(CF*(Li, Li)) → SH*(M) hits the unit 1 ∈ SH0(M). This criterion translates the geometric problem of generation into an algebraic computation in symplectic cohomology. Under HMS, it corresponds to the statement that the mirror objects generate DbCoh(X). The exactness assumption also simplifies the Fukaya category of a Liouville sector, where the stop is a Legendrian submanifold in the contact boundary; stop removal corresponds on the B‑side to localization with respect to a divisor. These features make exact Fukaya categories the primary testing ground for HMS proofs, notably for toric varieties, plumbings of cotangent bundles, and log Calabi–Yau surfaces.

Fukaya–Seidel Categories of Lefschetz Fibrations and their Mirrors

A Lefschetz fibration π: E → ℂ with exact symplectic total space E and generic fiber a Liouville manifold gives rise to the Fukaya–Seidel category FS(π). Its objects are graded, exact Lagrangian thimbles (vanishing cycles) obtained by parallel transport of a vanishing cycle in the fiber along a path from a critical value to infinity. Morphism spaces are Floer cochain complexes CF*(L0, L1) computed in the total space E, and the A∞‑structure is defined by counting holomorphic polygons with boundary on the thimbles and with one interior puncture mapping to the fiber at infinity. The category FS(π) is independent of the choice of paths up to quasi‑equivalence, and it carries a natural action of the monodromy group of the fibration.

On the mirror side, a Lefschetz fibration corresponds to a Landau–Ginzburg model (Y, W) where Y is a smooth variety and W: Y → 𝔸1 is a regular function with isolated critical points. The derived category of singularities Dsg(W-1(0)) or, equivalently, the category of matrix factorizations MF(Y, W) is expected to be equivalent to FS(π). This equivalence is a relative version of HMS: the fiber of π is mirror to the generic fiber of W, and the vanishing cycles correspond to spherical objects in MF(Y, W) whose spherical twists generate the monodromy action. Seidel established this equivalence for the An Milnor fibration, where the Fukaya–Seidel category is generated by a chain of Lagrangian spheres intersecting according to the An Dynkin diagram, and the mirror is the category of matrix factorizations of the An singularity. The proof uses the fact that the endomorphism algebra of the distinguished basis of vanishing cycles is the Ginzburg dg‑algebra of the corresponding quiver with potential.

For more general Lefschetz fibrations, the Fukaya–Seidel category can be described combinatorially via the directed Fukaya category of the fiber with stop given by the vanishing cycles. The stop removal operation corresponds to passing from the wrapped Fukaya category of the fiber to the Fukaya–Seidel category of the total space. This perspective connects HMS for Lefschetz fibrations to the wrapped Fukaya category of the fiber and to categorical wall‑crossing formulas for stability conditions. In particular, the monodromy of the fibration acts on the derived category of the mirror by a sequence of spherical twists, and the HMS equivalence intertwines this action with the geometric monodromy on the Fukaya–Seidel category. Examination questions often ask to construct the quiver with potential for a given Lefschetz fibration (e.g., the plumbing of T*S2 according to a Dynkin diagram) and to verify that the Ginzburg dg‑algebra of that quiver is quasi‑isomorphic to the endomorphism algebra of the distinguished vanishing cycles.

Microlocal Sheaf Theory and the Nadler–Zaslow Correspondence Beyond Weinstein Manifolds

The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence establishes an equivalence between the derived category of constructible sheaves on a real analytic manifold M with singular support contained in the conormal bundle of a stratification, and the Fukaya category of the cotangent bundle T*M. In its original form, the correspondence applies to exact Lagrangians in T*M that are conormal to submanifolds of M. The extension to wrapped Fukaya categories replaces constructible sheaves with sheaves on the compactification of T*M with microsupport in the conormal directions at infinity. This microlocal perspective translates Lagrangian intersection problems into sheaf‑theoretic computations: the Floer cohomology HF*(L0, L1) is isomorphic to the Ext groups between the corresponding sheaves.

Beyond cotangent bundles, the microlocal approach has been generalized to arbitrary Weinstein manifolds via the skeleton 𝔖𝔨(W). The category of constructible sheaves on 𝔖𝔨(W) with singular support in the conormal directions to the strata is equivalent to the wrapped Fukaya category WFuk(W). This equivalence, proved by Ganatra–Pardon–Shende, uses the fact that the skeleton is a Lagrangian CW complex whose cells are the stable manifolds of the critical points of the Morse function. The sheaf‑theoretic description provides a combinatorial model for WFuk(W): the category is generated by the costandard sheaves on the cells, and the morphisms are computed by the cohomology of the links of the strata. This model has been used to prove HMS for plumbings of cotangent bundles of spheres according to Dynkin diagrams, where the skeleton is a union of Lagrangian spheres intersecting according to the diagram.

The microlocal framework also clarifies the relationship between the wrapped Fukaya category and the category of modules over the Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra of the Legendrian link at infinity. The Legendrian link is the boundary of the skeleton, and its Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra is the endomorphism algebra of the cocore disks in WFuk(W). The sheaf‑theoretic counterpart is the category of modules over the dg‑algebra of constructible sheaves on the Legendrian link. This duality underlies the proof that the wrapped Fukaya category of a Weinstein manifold is invariant under Weinstein homotopy, and it provides a practical algorithm for computing WFuk(W) by analyzing the Legendrian contact homology of the boundary. Examination questions may ask to describe the skeleton of a given Weinstein manifold (e.g., the plumbing of T*Sn for a Dynkin diagram) and to write down the corresponding quiver with potential from the Chekanov–Eliashberg algebra of the Legendrian link.

Generation Criteria, Split‑Generation, and the Abouzaid Criterion in Fukaya Categories

A set of objects {Li} in a triangulated A∞‑category 𝒞 split‑generates 𝒞 if the smallest full triangulated subcategory containing the Li and closed under direct summands is equivalent to 𝒞. In the Fukaya category, split‑generation is the correct notion of generation because the category is only idempotent complete after passing to the derived category. Abouzaid's generation criterion gives a practical test: a collection of exact Lagrangians {Li} split‑generates Fukex(M) if and only if the open‑closed string map OC: ⊕i HH*(CF*(Li, Li)) → SH*(M) hits the unit 1 ∈ SH0(M). Equivalently, the closed‑open map CO: SH*(M) → HH*(Fuk(M)) composed with the projection to ⊕i HF*(Li, Li) hits the unit in degree zero. This criterion translates the geometric problem of generation into an algebraic computation in symplectic cohomology.

The criterion has been extended to the wrapped Fukaya category of a Liouville sector with stop 𝔣. In that setting, the stop removal functor WFuk(W, 𝔣) → WFuk(W) is fully faithful, and its essential image consists of objects whose endomorphism algebras are annihilated by the stop. Generation by a set of objects in WFuk(W, 𝔣) can be detected by the non‑vanishing of the corresponding classes in the relative symplectic cohomology SH*(W, 𝔣). On the B‑side, the mirror statement is that a collection of objects in DbCoh(X) generates if and only if the induced map on Hochschild homology HH*(X) → ⊕ HH*(End(Ei)) hits the identity in degree zero. This is a categorical version of the classical fact that a set of vector bundles generates the derived category if and only if their Chern characters span the cohomology.

In practice, one often uses a directed set of Lagrangians (e.g., a distinguished basis of vanishing cycles) and checks that the endomorphism algebra of the direct sum is formal and that its Hochschild homology maps onto SH*(M). For the An Milnor fiber, the distinguished basis of n Lagrangian spheres split‑generates the Fukaya–Seidel category, and the endomorphism algebra is the Ginzburg dg‑algebra of the An quiver. The Abouzaid criterion then reduces to verifying that the unit in SH*(M) lies in the image of the open‑closed map, which follows from the fact that the Euler characteristic of the Milnor fiber is n+1 and the unit corresponds to the sum of the identity morphisms of the spheres. Examination questions may ask to state the Abouzaid criterion precisely, to explain why split‑generation is stronger than generation, and to apply the criterion to a concrete Lefschetz fibration such as the plumbing of T*S2 according to the E6 diagram.

Categorical Donaldson–Thomas Invariants and their Mirror Symmetric Interpretation

Categorical Donaldson–Thomas (DT) invariants were introduced by Kontsevich and Soibelman to categorify the numerical DT invariants counting stable objects in a 3‑Calabi–Yau category. Given a 3‑Calabi–Yau triangulated category 𝒞 with a stability condition, the numerical DT invariant is an element of the quantum torus associated to the Grothendieck group K0(𝒞). The categorification replaces the numerical invariant by a cohomological Hall algebra (CoHA) or, in the framework of Joyce and Song, by a perverse sheaf on the moduli stack of objects whose hypercohomology recovers the numerical DT invariants. The HMS conjecture predicts that the categorical DT invariants of the derived category DbCoh(X) for a Calabi–Yau threefold X should match the categorical DT invariants of the Fukaya category Fuk(M) of the mirror symplectic manifold M.

On the symplectic side, the categorical DT invariants are expected to be computed by the wrapped Fukaya category of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model. For a toric Calabi–Yau threefold X, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model (Y, W) where Y = (ℂ*)3 and W is a Laurent polynomial. The Fukaya–Seidel category of W is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on X, and the categorical DT invariants of X are captured by the cohomology of the moduli space of objects in FS(W). The wall‑crossing formula for DT invariants corresponds, under HMS, to the mutation of exceptional collections in the Fukaya category, which is implemented by spherical twists around Lagrangian spheres. This provides a symplectic interpretation of the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula as a sequence of mutations in the Fukaya category.

Recent work by Bousseau, Brini, and others has established correspondences between the categorical DT invariants of the resolved conifold and the open Gromov–Witten invariants of its mirror, which are computed by the disk potential in the Fukaya category. The disk potential is a generating function of open Gromov–Witten invariants with boundary on a Lagrangian brane, and its critical points correspond to objects in the Fukaya category that mirror stable sheaves on the conifold. The HMS equivalence identifies the disk potential with the superpotential of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model, and the categorical DT invariants are recovered from the BPS structure on the Fukaya category. Examination questions may ask to explain how the wall‑crossing formula for DT invariants translates into mutations of exceptional collections in the Fukaya category, or to describe the role of the disk potential in computing open Gromov–Witten invariants and its relation to the superpotential on the mirror.

  • Assuming the Fukaya–Seidel category of a Lefschetz fibration is independent of the choice of distinguished basis of vanishing cycles; it is independent up to quasi‑equivalence, but the specific A∞‑structure depends on the basis.
  • Neglecting the grading shift when matching spherical twists on the A‑side with spherical twists on the B‑side; the Seidel–Thomas twist on the Fukaya category corresponds to the spherical twist on the derived category only after a degree shift by the Calabi–Yau dimension.

  • Applying the Abouzaid generation criterion to non‑exact Lagrangians without passing to the wrapped setting; the criterion uses the open‑closed map to symplectic cohomology, which is defined for exact or wrapped categories.

Homological Mirror Symmetry for the Quintic Threefold and the Candelas–de la Ossa–Green–Parkes Mirror

The quintic threefold X ⊂ ℙ⁴ defined by a generic degree‑5 polynomial is the archetypal compact Calabi–Yau threefold. Its mirror X̌ was constructed by Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes (1991) as a crepant resolution of the quotient of the Fermat quintic {∑i=0⁴ zi⁵ = 0} by the group (ℤ/5ℤ)³ acting by phase multiplications with product 1. The HMS conjecture for this pair asserts an equivalence between the derived Fukaya category Fuk(X) (with appropriate gradings and spin structures) and the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves DᵇCoh(X̌). On the A‑side, the Fukaya category is generated by a single Lagrangian 3‑sphere L (the vanishing cycle of a Lefschetz pencil), whose endomorphism algebra is the Clifford algebra Cl(5). On the B‑side, DᵇCoh(X̌) admits a full exceptional collection of length 5 (or a tilting object) whose endomorphism algebra is the path algebra of the A₄ quiver with relations. The equivalence identifies the monodromy of the Gauss–Manin connection on the A‑side with the action of the spherical twist around L on the Fukaya category, and on the B‑side with the autoequivalence given by tensoring with the line bundle O(1) followed by a shift. The genus‑0 Gromov–Witten invariants of X (the famous instanton numbers 2875, 609250, …) are recovered from the period integrals on X̌ via the mirror map, and the HMS equivalence lifts this numerical correspondence to an isomorphism of Hochschild cohomology rings HH*(Fuk(X)) ≅ HH*(X̌) ≅ H*(X̌, ∧*TX̌). Seidel (2003) established the HMS equivalence for the quintic by constructing a directed A∞‑subcategory of the Fukaya category generated by a distinguished basis of vanishing cycles and showing it is quasi‑equivalent to the derived category of modules over the A₄ quiver, which in turn is equivalent to DᵇCoh(X̌) via Orlov’s theorem. The proof uses the fact that the Fukaya–Seidel category of the Lefschetz fibration W : ℂ⁵ → ℂ given by the Fermat polynomial is formal and its cohomology category is the derived category of the A₄ quiver. The closed‑open string map identifies the quantum cohomology QH*(X) with the Hochschild cohomology of the Fukaya category, and the mirror map identifies this with the Jacobian ring of the superpotential on the B‑side. Examination questions may ask to describe the role of the A₄ quiver in the HMS equivalence for the quintic, or to explain how the spherical twist on the Fukaya category corresponds to the monodromy of the mirror family.

The Fukaya Category of a Symplectic Manifold with a Stop and Partially Wrapped Fukaya Categories

Let (M, ω) be a Liouville manifold with contact boundary ∂M. A stop 𝔣 ⊂ ∂M is a closed Legendrian submanifold (or more generally a sectorial co‑Legendrian) that bounds a Liouville sector. The partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔣) is the A∞‑category whose objects are exact Lagrangian submanifolds L ⊂ M with boundary on ∂M 𝔣, cylindrical at infinity, and whose morphism spaces are given by wrapped Floer cochains where the wrapping Hamiltonian is required to be zero near the stop. When 𝔣 = ∅, one recovers the fully wrapped Fukaya category W(M); when 𝔣 = ∂M, one recovers the compact Fukaya category Fuk(M). The stop removal theorem of Ganatra–Pardon–Shende (2018) established that for a Liouville sector (M, 𝔣) there is a localization sequence of A∞‑categories W(M, 𝔣) → W(M) → W(M, ∂M 𝔣), where the second map is the stop‑removal functor and the first is the inclusion of the subcategory of objects disjoint from the stop. This sequence is a Karoubi‑exact sequence of A∞‑categories. On the mirror side, a stop corresponds to a divisor D in a Landau–Ginzburg model (Y, W) or a boundary condition in a B‑model category of matrix factorizations. The partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔣) is expected to be equivalent to the category of singularities of the mirror with support on the divisor mirror to 𝔣. For a toric Calabi–Yau threefold X with a toric divisor D, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model ((ℂ*)³, W) and the stop is the tropicalization of D. The partially wrapped Fukaya category of the mirror symplectic manifold with this stop is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on X supported on D. The stop‑removal sequence categorifies the exact sequence of K‑groups K₀(D) → K₀(X) → K₀(X D). The sectorial cover construction associates to a stop 𝔣 a Liouville manifold M̃ with a free ℤ‑action such that W(M, 𝔣) is equivalent to the ℤ‑equivariant wrapped Fukaya category of M̃. This provides a geometric realization of the orbit category construction for the monodromy autoequivalence. Examination questions may ask to state the stop‑removal exact sequence and explain its mirror interpretation, or to describe how a sectorial cover recovers the partially wrapped category as an equivariant category.

Worked Example: Stop Removal for the Cotangent Bundle of the Circle
Let M = T*S¹ ≅ S¹ × ℝ with the standard Liouville form λ = p dq. The contact boundary is two circles ∂M = S¹ × {±∞}. Take the stop 𝔣 to be one point on each boundary component (so 𝔣 consists of four points). Describe the partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔣) and the stop‑removal sequence.
Step 1: Identify the Liouville sector. Removing the stop 𝔣 from ∂M leaves four open intervals. The Liouville sector (M, 𝔣) has four corners. Its objects are exact Lagrangians with boundary on the complement of 𝔣, i.e., arcs connecting the four boundary intervals. Up to Hamiltonian isotopy, there are two non‑isomorphic objects: the zero section S¹ × {0} (which is compact, hence an object of the compact Fukaya category) and a non‑compact arc L connecting the two positive ends (or the two negative ends). Step 2: Compute morphisms. hom(L, L) is generated by the minimum and maximum of the Hamiltonian used for wrapping; since wrapping is not allowed across the stop, the wrapped Floer complex is finite‑dimensional. In fact W(M, 𝔣) is quasi‑equivalent to the derived category of the A₂ quiver (two vertices, one arrow). Step 3: The fully wrapped category. W(M) is the wrapped Fukaya category of the cylinder, which is equivalent to the category of ℤ‑graded modules over the algebra k[x, x⁻¹] (the group algebra of ℤ). Step 4: The complementary stop. ∂M 𝔣 consists of four intervals; the category W(M, ∂M 𝔣) is the compact Fukaya category of the cylinder with stops at the four points, which is equivalent to the derived category of the A₁ quiver (a single object with no morphisms). Step 5: The stop‑removal sequence. The sequence W(M, 𝔣) → W(M) → W(M, ∂M 𝔣) becomes Dᵇ(A₂) → Mod‑k[ℤ] → Dᵇ(A₁). The first functor sends the two objects of A₂ to the two standard modules k[ℤ] and k[ℤ][1] (shifted). The second functor is the quotient by the subcategory generated by these two modules, leaving the trivial module. This is a Karoubi‑exact sequence of dg‑categories.
Exam Strategy: Stops and Sectorial Covers
When a question asks about stops, first identify whether the stop is on the symplectic side (Legendrian in the contact boundary) or the B‑side (a divisor or support condition). The stop‑removal exact sequence is a Karoubi‑exact sequence of A∞‑categories, not merely a Verdier sequence of triangulated categories — the A∞‑structure matters. For sectorial covers, remember that the group action is ℤ (not ℝ) and the equivalence is between the partially wrapped category and the ℤ‑equivariant wrapped category of the cover. A common examiner trap is to confuse the partially wrapped category with the compact Fukaya category of the sector; they differ because objects in W(M, 𝔣) are allowed to wrap away from the stop, whereas compact Fukaya objects cannot wrap at all.

Categorical Mirror Symmetry for Toric Varieties and the Hori–Vafa Mirror Construction

Let X be a smooth projective toric variety defined by a fan Σ in a lattice N ≅ ℤⁿ. The Hori–Vafa mirror (2000) is a Landau–Ginzburg model ((ℂ*)ⁿ, W) where the superpotential W = ∑ρ ∈ Σ(1) zvρ is a sum of monomials indexed by the rays of the fan, with vρ ∈ M = Hom(N, ℤ) the primitive generator of the dual ray. The HMS conjecture for toric varieties asserts an equivalence between the derived Fukaya category Fuk((ℂ*)ⁿ, W) (or the Fukaya–Seidel category of W) and the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves DᵇCoh(X). Abouzaid (2006, 2009) established this for toric varieties by constructing a family of Lagrangian torus fibers Lu = {|zi| = eui} in (ℂ*)ⁿ and showing that the directed A∞‑subcategory generated by these fibers (with appropriate bounding cochains solving the Maurer–Cartan equation) is quasi‑equivalent to the derived category of the toric variety. The key technical tool is the tropical Lagrangian correspondence: the moment map μ : (ℂ*)ⁿ → ℝⁿ sends the Lagrangian torus fibers to points u ∈ ℝⁿ, and the superpotential W tropicalizes to a piecewise‑linear function on ℝⁿ whose Newton polytope is the fan polytope of X. The critical points of W correspond to the torus‑fixed points of X, and the thimbles of the Lefschetz fibration W correspond to the toric divisors. The equivalence identifies the line bundle O(D) on X with the Lagrangian brane given by the fiber over the point u dual to D, equipped with a bounding cochain determined by the open Gromov–Witten invariants of the fiber. The closed‑open string map identifies the quantum cohomology QH*(X) with the Jacobian ring Jac(W) = ℂ[z₁±¹, …, zₙ±¹] / (∂W/∂zi), which is the mirror of the Batyrev presentation of the quantum cohomology ring. For a Fano toric variety, the superpotential W is a Laurent polynomial with positive coefficients, and the Fukaya–Seidel category is generated by the vanishing cycles of the critical points of W. The HMS equivalence extends to toric stacks and to toric Calabi–Yau varieties, where the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model on a non‑compact toric variety. Examination questions may ask to write down the Hori–Vafa superpotential for a given toric variety (e.g., ℙ² or the Hirzebruch surface F₁), or to explain how the tropicalization of W recovers the fan of X.

Homological Mirror Symmetry for K3 Surfaces and the Autoequivalence Group of the Derived Category

A K3 surface S is a simply connected compact complex surface with trivial canonical bundle. Its derived category DᵇCoh(S) is a 2‑Calabi–Yau triangulated category. The mirror of a K3 surface is another K3 surface Š (or the same surface with a different complex structure) such that there is a Hodge isometry H²(S, ℤ) ≅ H²(Š, ℤ) exchanging the algebraic and transcendental lattices. The HMS conjecture for K3 surfaces predicts an equivalence Fuk(S) ≃ DᵇCoh(Š) where the Fukaya category is taken with respect to a symplectic form in the Kähler cone of S. The autoequivalence group Aut(DᵇCoh(S)) is generated by shifts, tensoring with line bundles, and spherical twists around spherical objects (objects E with Ext*S(E, E) ≅ H*(S², ℂ)). By the Mukai–Orlov theorem, any Fourier–Mukai partner of S is either S itself or a moduli space of stable sheaves on S. The spherical twists correspond on the A‑side to Dehn twists around Lagrangian 2‑spheres in S. The HMS equivalence identifies the action of the autoequivalence group on the numerical Grothendieck group N(S) ≅ H*(S, ℤ) with the action of the symplectic mapping class group on the Fukaya category. The stability manifold Stab(DᵇCoh(S)) has a connected component isomorphic to the space of complexified Kähler forms on the mirror K3 surface, and the action of the autoequivalence group on Stab is properly discontinuous with quotient the moduli space of marked K3 surfaces. The categorical Torelli theorem (Huybrechts, 2006) states that two K3 surfaces have equivalent derived categories if and only if they are Fourier–Mukai partners, which is equivalent to the existence of a Hodge isometry between their transcendental lattices. On the symplectic side, Seidel (2015) showed that the Fukaya category of a K3 surface determines the symplectic manifold up to symplectomorphism, and the autoequivalence group of the Fukaya category is the symplectic mapping class group. The HMS equivalence thus identifies the derived autoequivalence group with the symplectic mapping class group, providing a categorical proof of the global Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces. Examination questions may ask to describe the generators of the autoequivalence group of DᵇCoh(S) for a K3 surface, or to explain how the spherical twist on the B‑side corresponds to the Dehn twist on the A‑side under HMS.

The Relative Fukaya Category and Homological Mirror Symmetry for Pairs (X, D)

Let X be a smooth projective variety and D ⊂ X a smooth divisor. The pair (X, D) has a mirror which is a Landau–Ginzburg model (Y, W) with a distinguished fiber W⁻¹(0) mirror to D. The relative Fukaya category Fuk(X, D) is the A∞‑category whose objects are Lagrangian submanifolds L ⊂ X D that are cylindrical near D (i.e., they intersect a tubular neighborhood of D in a product L ∩ N(D) ≅ L ∩ D × [0, ∞)), and whose morphisms are Floer cochains with Hamiltonian perturbations that preserve the cylindrical condition. There is a localization sequence Fuk(D) → Fuk(X) → Fuk(X, D) where the first functor is the pushforward of a Lagrangian in D to X, and the second is the restriction to the complement. On the B‑side, the relative derived category DᵇCoh(X, D) is the Verdier quotient DᵇCoh(X) / DᵇDCoh(X) where the subcategory consists of complexes supported on D. The relative HMS conjecture asserts an equivalence Fuk(X, D) ≃ DᵇCoh(X, D). For a toric variety X and a toric divisor D, the mirror is the Hori–Vafa Landau–Ginzburg model ((ℂ*)ⁿ, W) with the stop given by the tropicalization of D. The partially wrapped Fukaya category W((ℂ*)ⁿ, 𝔣D) is equivalent to DᵇCoh(X, D). The relative Fukaya category admits a relative closed‑open string map QH*(X, D) → HH*(Fuk(X, D)) where the relative quantum cohomology is the quantum cohomology of the pair. The relative HMS equivalence identifies this with the map from the relative Hochschild cohomology of X to the Hochschild cohomology of the quotient category. When D is an anticanonical divisor, the pair (X, D) is a log Calabi–Yau pair, and the relative Fukaya category is a Calabi–Yau category of dimension dim X − 1. The Gross–Siebert program constructs the mirror of a log Calabi–Yau pair by tropical geometry, and the relative HMS equivalence is expected to be compatible with the tropical localization of the Fukaya category. Examination questions may ask to state the localization sequence for the relative Fukaya category and its B‑side counterpart, or to explain how the relative HMS equivalence for a toric pair reduces to the stop‑removal sequence for the partially wrapped Fukaya category.

  • Confusing the partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔣) with the compact Fukaya category of the Liouville sector; the former allows wrapping away from the stop, the latter does not.
  • Assuming the stop‑removal sequence is a Verdier sequence of triangulated categories; it is a Karoubi‑exact sequence of A∞‑categories, and the A∞‑structure is essential for the equivalence with the B‑side.
  • Treating the Hori–Vafa superpotential as a polynomial rather than a Laurent polynomial; the exponents are in the dual lattice M and can be negative, reflecting the non‑compactness of the torus fibers.
  • Neglecting the bounding cochain when identifying a toric line bundle with a Lagrangian torus fiber; the fiber is not unobstructed without a bounding cochain solving the Maurer–Cartan equation.
  • Confusing the spherical twist on a K3 surface with the Dehn twist on the mirror; they correspond under HMS only after a degree shift by the Calabi–Yau dimension (which is 2 for K3).
  • Assuming the relative Fukaya category Fuk(X, D) is the same as the Fukaya category of the complement X D; the former uses cylindrical Lagrangians near D, the latter uses compact Lagrangians in the complement.
  • Forgetting that the categorical Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces requires a Hodge isometry of the transcendental lattices, not just an isomorphism of the full Hodge structures.

Homological Mirror Symmetry for Elliptic Curves and the SYZ Fibration

The elliptic curve E = ℂ/(ℤ ⊕ τℤ) with Im(τ) > 0 provides the first non‑trivial verification of the Homological Mirror Symmetry conjecture. Its mirror is another elliptic curve E∨ = ℂ/(ℤ ⊕ (−1/τ)ℤ), and the SYZ fibration is the projection E → S¹ given by the argument of the holomorphic coordinate. The Fukaya category Fuk(E) has objects given by graded Lagrangian circles with local systems; morphisms are Floer cochain complexes whose differential counts holomorphic strips. The derived category DᵇCoh(E∨) has objects given by complexes of coherent sheaves; morphisms are Ext groups. Polishchuk and Zaslow (1998) gave an explicit A∞‑equivalence by identifying a Lagrangian circle with slope p/q (coprime integers) with the stable vector bundle of rank q and degree p on E∨. The equivalence sends the skyscraper sheaf 𝒪x to the Lagrangian fiber over x ∈ S¹, and the structure sheaf 𝒪E∨ to the zero‑section. The A∞‑structure on Fuk(E) is formal: all higher products mk for k ≥ 3 vanish after a suitable gauge transformation, reflecting the fact that the mirror is a commutative variety. The closed‑open string map QH*(E) → HH*(Fuk(E)) is an isomorphism, and the Serre functor on both sides is the shift by 2, matching the Calabi–Yau dimension. Examination questions often ask to compute the Floer cohomology HF*(L1, L2) for two Lagrangian circles with slopes p₁/q₁ and p₂/q₂ and to identify the corresponding Ext group on the mirror.

The Fukaya Category of a Cotangent Bundle and the Nadler–Zaslow Correspondence

Let M be a smooth manifold and T*M its cotangent bundle equipped with the canonical Liouville structure. The wrapped Fukaya category W(T*M) is equivalent to the derived category of constructible sheaves Shℂ(M) on M with complex coefficients — this is the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence. Objects of W(T*M) are exact, properly embedded Lagrangian submanifolds L ⊂ T*M that are conical at infinity, equipped with a grading and a spin structure. Morphisms are wrapped Floer cochains CW*(L₀, L₁) = limH→∞ CF*(L₀, φH(L₁)), where the limit is taken over Hamiltonians that are quadratic at infinity. The correspondence sends a Lagrangian L to the microlocal sheaf μL on M, whose stalk at x ∈ M is the Floer cohomology of L with the cotangent fiber T*xM. The zero‑section M ⊂ T*M corresponds to the constant sheaf ℂM. A conormal bundle T*NM of a submanifold N ⊂ M corresponds to the constant sheaf on N extended by zero. The equivalence is monoidal: the convolution product of sheaves corresponds to the Lagrangian correspondence product in the Fukaya category. For M = S¹, W(T*S¹) is generated by the zero‑section and the cotangent fiber, and the equivalence recovers the HMS for the elliptic curve via the SYZ transform. The Nadler–Zaslow correspondence extends to singular Lagrangians and to the relative setting: for a pair (M, ∂M), the partially wrapped Fukaya category of T*M with stop at ∂M is equivalent to Shℂ(M, ∂M). Examination questions may ask to describe the microlocal sheaf of a Lagrangian given by the graph of an exact 1‑form df, or to explain how the wrapped Floer differential encodes the Morse differential of f.

Worked Computation: Microlocal Sheaf of a Graph Lagrangian
Let M = ℝ and L = graph(df) ⊂ T*ℝ where f(x) = x²/2. Compute the microlocal sheaf μL and identify the corresponding object in Shℂ(ℝ).
The Lagrangian L = {(x, ξ) | ξ = x} is the graph of the exact 1‑form df with f(x) = x²/2. The microlocal sheaf μL is defined by its stalks: for x ∈ ℝ, (μL)x = HF*(L, T*xℝ). The cotangent fiber T*xℝ = {(x, ξ) | ξ ∈ ℝ} intersects L transversely at the single point (x, x). The Floer complex is generated by this intersection point. Since L and the fiber are exact, there are no holomorphic disks, so the differential vanishes. Thus HF*(L, T*xℝ) ≅ ℂ concentrated in degree 0 (the grading comes from the Maslov index, which is 0 for this transverse intersection). The microlocal sheaf is therefore the constant sheaf ℂ on ℝ. On the B‑side, the graph of df is Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero‑section via the flow of the Hamiltonian H(x, ξ) = f(x), so it corresponds to the same object as the zero‑section, namely the constant sheaf ℂℝ. This matches the computation: μL ≅ ℂℝ.
Exam Strategy: Recognizing the Nadler–Zaslow Dictionary
When a question asks for the sheaf corresponding to a Lagrangian in T*M, first check if the Lagrangian is the zero‑section (constant sheaf), a conormal bundle T*NM (constant sheaf on N extended by zero), or a graph of df (constant sheaf, since it is Hamiltonian isotopic to the zero‑section). For a general exact Lagrangian, the microlocal sheaf is constructible with singular support contained in the projection of the Lagrangian. The key computational tool is the stalk formula (μL)x = HF*(L, T*xM). Remember that wrapped Floer cohomology with a cotangent fiber computes the Morse cohomology of the generating function when the Lagrangian is a graph.

Homological Mirror Symmetry for Grassmannians and Homogeneous Spaces

The Grassmannian Gr(k, n) of k‑planes in ℂn is a Fano variety whose mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model ((ℂ*)n−1, W) with superpotential W given by the Givental–Hori–Vafa formula. Homological mirror symmetry for Grassmannians has been established by work of several authors (including Rietsch, Katzarkov–Kontsevich–Pantev, and others), constructing an equivalence between the derived category DᵇCoh(Gr(k, n)) and the Fukaya–Seidel category FS((ℂ*)n−1, W). The superpotential for Gr(k, n) is a Laurent polynomial on the torus whose exponents lie in the cocharacter lattice; for Gr(1,n) = ℙn−1 it takes the form W = x₁ + ⋯ + xn−1 + q/(x₁⋯xn−1). The exceptional collection of vector bundles on Gr(k, n) given by the Kapranov collection corresponds under HMS to a collection of Lagrangian thimbles in the Fukaya–Seidel category. The thimbles are the stable manifolds of the gradient flow of Re(W) with respect to a suitable Kähler metric. The quantum cohomology QH*(Gr(k, n)) is isomorphic to the Jacobian ring of W, and the closed‑open string map identifies this with the Hochschild cohomology of the Fukaya–Seidel category. For the quadric hypersurface Qn ⊂ ℙn+1, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model on a double cover of (ℂ*)n, and HMS has been proved in this case as well. For general homogeneous spaces G/P, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model on the Langlands dual group G∨, and the HMS conjecture is closely related to the geometric Langlands program. Examination questions may ask to write the superpotential for Gr(2, 4) and to describe the Lagrangian thimbles corresponding to the Kapranov exceptional collection.

Categorical Mirror Symmetry for Flag Varieties and the Geometric Langlands Program

The flag variety G/B for a reductive group G has a mirror that is a Landau–Ginzburg model on the Langlands dual group G∨. The derived category DᵇCoh(G/B) is expected to be equivalent to the Fukaya–Seidel category of this mirror, providing a categorical incarnation of the geometric Langlands correspondence. Bezrukavnikov and Finkelberg (2004) constructed an equivalence identifying DᵇCoh(G/B) with a category of G∨‑equivariant perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian GrG∨. On the symplectic side, the Fukaya category of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model is related to the category of G∨‑local systems on the punctured disk. The superpotential W on G∨ is the restriction of a canonical function on the dual group to a regular semisimple conjugacy class. The HMS equivalence for flag varieties is connected to the Kazhdan–Lusztig equivalence between representations of the quantum group at a root of unity and perverse sheaves on the affine flag variety. For G = SL2, the flag variety is ℙ¹, and the mirror is the Landau–Ginzburg model (ℂ*, W = z + q/z). The Fukaya–Seidel category is generated by two thimbles corresponding to the critical points of W, and the equivalence with DᵇCoh(ℙ¹) sends them to 𝒪 and 𝒪(1). The monodromy of the Fukaya–Seidel category around the critical values gives the braid group action, which corresponds to the action of the spherical twist on DᵇCoh(ℙ¹). Examination questions may ask to state the HMS conjecture for a flag variety G/B and to explain how the braid group action on the Fukaya–Seidel category corresponds to the action of the Weyl group on the derived category.

The Fukaya Category and Lagrangian Cobordism

Lagrangian cobordism provides a geometric framework for understanding exact triangles in the Fukaya category. Let L₀, L₁ be exact Lagrangian submanifolds in a Liouville manifold M. An exact Lagrangian cobordism from L₀ to L₁ is an exact Lagrangian submanifold V ⊂ M × ℂ such that V ∩ (M × {t}) = L₀ for t ≪ 0 and L₁ for t ≫ 0, and V is cylindrical outside a compact set. The existence of such a cobordism implies that L₁ is in the split‑closure of the cone of a morphism L₀ → L₁ in the Fukaya category. Conversely, every exact triangle in the Fukaya category can be realized by a Lagrangian cobordism (Biran–Cornea). The surgery exact triangle L₀ → L₁ → L₀ # L₁ → L₀[1] corresponds to a cobordism given by the trace of Lagrangian surgery. The wrapped Fukaya category W(M) admits a localization sequence associated to a Lagrangian cobordism: if V is a cobordism from L₀ to L₁, then there is a Karoubi‑exact sequence W(M V) → W(M) → W(M, V) where the last term is the partially wrapped category with stop at V. On the B‑side, this corresponds to the localization sequence for the derived category of a divisor. The cobordism group of Lagrangian submanifolds, modulo exact Lagrangian cobordisms, is isomorphic to the Grothendieck group of the Fukaya category. For the cotangent bundle T*M, the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence identifies Lagrangian cobordisms with exact triangles in Shℂ(M). Examination questions may ask to construct the Lagrangian cobordism corresponding to the exact triangle 𝒪 → 𝒪(1) → 𝒪p → 𝒪[1] on ℙ¹ under HMS, or to explain how the surgery exact triangle arises from a cobordism.

  • Assuming the Nadler–Zaslow correspondence holds for the compact Fukaya category of T*M; it holds for the wrapped Fukaya category, and the compact Fukaya category corresponds to compactly supported sheaves.
  • Treating the Kapranov exceptional collection on Gr(k, n) as a full strong exceptional collection without verifying the Ext‑vanishing conditions; it is strong and full, but the dual collection is not the same as the original.
  • Neglecting the grading on Lagrangian thimbles in the Fukaya–Seidel category; the grading is determined by the phase of the critical value of the superpotential, and a shift in grading corresponds to a shift in the derived category.
  • Confusing the geometric Langlands correspondence for G with HMS for the flag variety G/B; the former relates G‑local systems to G∨‑equivariant sheaves, the latter relates DᵇCoh(G/B) to the Fukaya–Seidel category of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model on G∨.
  • Assuming every exact triangle in the Fukaya category comes from a Lagrangian surgery; surgery gives a specific class of exact triangles, but general exact triangles correspond to general Lagrangian cobordisms which may not be surgeries.
  • Forgetting that the superpotential for the Grassmannian mirror is a Laurent polynomial, not a polynomial; the exponents lie in the cocharacter lattice of the maximal torus, and negative exponents reflect the non‑compactness of the torus fibers.

Homological Mirror Symmetry for Hypersurfaces in Toric Varieties and the Batyrev Mirror Construction

The Batyrev mirror construction associates to a Calabi–Yau hypersurface X in a toric variety V defined by a reflexive polytope Δ a mirror Calabi–Yau hypersurface X∨ in the toric variety defined by the dual polytope Δ∨. On the B‑side the derived category DᵇCoh(X) admits a semi‑orthogonal decomposition generated by the restriction of an exceptional collection on V together with the derived category of the ambient toric variety. On the A‑side the Fukaya category Fuk(X∨) is generated by Lagrangian spheres obtained as vanishing cycles of a Lefschetz fibration coming from the toric moment map. Sheridan proved that the HMS equivalence for the quintic threefold extends to all Calabi–Yau hypersurfaces in toric Fano varieties: there is an A∞‑equivalence Fuk(X∨) ≃ DᵇCoh(X) which sends the Lagrangian spheres to the spherical objects in DᵇCoh(X) corresponding to the exceptional collection. The superpotential on the mirror is the restriction of the Hori–Vafa Landau–Ginzburg potential to the hypersurface, and its critical points correspond to the toric fixed points. The HMS functor respects the monoidal structures given by the quantum product on Fuk(X∨) and the tensor product on DᵇCoh(X). Examination questions often ask to describe the Batyrev mirror of a given hypersurface, to write down the Hori–Vafa superpotential, and to explain how the spherical twist on the B‑side matches the Dehn twist on the A‑side.

Categorical Mirror Symmetry for Hypertoric Varieties and Symplectic Duality

Hypertoric varieties are hyperkähler analogues of toric varieties, constructed as hyperkähler quotients of a flat hyperkähler space by a torus action. They admit a natural symplectic form in each complex structure, and their mirror symmetry is governed by symplectic duality: the category of coherent sheaves on a hypertoric variety Y in complex structure I is equivalent to the Fukaya category of the mirror hypertoric variety Y∨ in complex structure J. The equivalence is constructed via the work of Braden, Licata, Proudfoot, and Webster, who identify both categories with the category of modules over a certain Koszul dual pair of algebras. The derived category DᵇCoh(Y) carries a natural action of the spherical twists associated to the exceptional collection given by the toric line bundles; on the symplectic side these correspond to Dehn twists along the Lagrangian spheres that form the core of Y∨. The HMS functor exchanges the natural t‑structure on DᵇCoh(Y) with the perverse t‑structure on the Fukaya category induced by the Morse–Bott function given by the moment map. A key computational tool is the hypertoric category 𝒪, a highest‑weight category whose simple objects correspond to the torus‑fixed points; its Koszul dual is the category of constructible sheaves on the core of the mirror. Examination questions may ask to state the symplectic duality conjecture for hypertoric varieties, to describe the Koszul dual algebra pair, or to explain how the spherical twist on DᵇCoh(Y) matches the monodromy of the Fukaya–Seidel category of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model.

State the HMS equivalence for the quintic threefold X ⊂ ℙ⁴ and its mirror X∨. Identify the generating objects on both sides and the correspondence of the superpotential critical points.

The Fukaya Category of a Symplectic Manifold with Contact Boundary and the Contact Fukaya Category

This functor fits into a localization sequence: the kernel consists of Lagrangians that are compactly supported in M, i.e. the compact Fukaya category Fuk(M). Equivariant Fukaya Categories and Homological Mirror Symmetry for Hamiltonian Group Actions

The equivariant Fukaya category FukG(M) of a symplectic manifold (M, ω) equipped with a Hamiltonian action of a compact Lie group G and a moment map μ: M → 𝔤* is constructed by replacing the usual Floer chain complex with a complex that incorporates the G‑action. Objects are G‑equivariant Lagrangian branes (L, ∇, b) where L is a Lagrangian submanifold invariant under G, ∇ is a G‑equivariant flat connection, and b is a bounding cochain satisfying the equivariant Maurer–Cartan equation. Morphism spaces are defined as the G‑invariant part of the Floer cochain complex CF*(L₀, L₁) ⊗ H*(BG), where H*(BG) is the cohomology of the classifying space. The A∞‑operations are obtained by counting G‑equivariant pseudoholomorphic disks with appropriate insertions of equivariant cohomology classes. This construction, developed by Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono and refined by Seidel, yields a triangulated A∞‑category over the Novikov ring Λ.

On the mirror side, the B‑model is the G‑equivariant derived category DbGCoh(X) of coherent sheaves on the mirror variety X, or more generally the derived category of G‑equivariant matrix factorizations when X carries a superpotential. The equivariant HMS conjecture asserts an equivalence FukG(M) ≃ DbGCoh(X) compatible with the monoidal structures. For toric varieties, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model (X, W) where X = (ℂ*)ⁿ and W is the Hori–Vafa superpotential; the G‑action corresponds to the torus action on the base of the SYZ fibration. Abouzaid proved the equivalence for compact toric manifolds using family Floer theory; Braden–Licata–Proudfoot–Webster established it for hypertoric varieties. In the case of the moduli space of Higgs bundles, the G‑action is the circle action rotating the Higgs field, and the equivariant HMS is closely related to the geometric Langlands correspondence.

Key technical tools include the equivariant localization theorem for the virtual fundamental class of the moduli space of pseudoholomorphic disks, the quantum Kirwan map QK: H*G(M) → QH*(M//G) which relates the equivariant quantum cohomology of M to that of the symplectic quotient, and the Seidel representation of the fundamental group of the Hamiltonian diffeomorphism group on the Fukaya category. Examination questions often ask to state the definition of a G‑equivariant Lagrangian brane, to describe how the moment map enters the equivariant Floer equation, or to explain the role of the quantum Kirwan map in constructing the mirror functor.

Non‑Exact Fukaya Categories and the Novikov Ring

When the symplectic form ω is not exact, the action functional on the loop space is multi‑valued and the energy of a pseudoholomorphic disk is not bounded below by a topological constant. To define Floer cohomology and the Fukaya category in this setting one works over the universal Novikov ring Λ = {∑i=0∞ ai Tλi | ai ∈ ℂ, λi ∈ ℝ, λi → ∞}. The formal variable T records the symplectic area: a disk of area A contributes a factor TA. The A∞‑operations mk are defined as sums over homotopy classes of disks weighted by Tarea. Because the area is non‑negative and the sum is over a well‑ordered set of exponents, the series converge in the T‑adic topology of Λ. Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono carried out this construction in full generality; Seidel treated the exact case and its extension to the non‑exact setting via the Novikov ring. The result is a unital A∞‑category Fuk(M) over Λ.

The Novikov ring is a field (the universal Novikov field) when one allows arbitrary real exponents; its valuation v(∑ ai Tλi) = min λi gives a filtration that controls the energy of disks. The Fukaya category is then a Λ‑linear A∞‑category, and its cohomology category H⁰Fuk(M) is triangulated. On the mirror side, the B‑model is the derived category of coherent sheaves on a formal deformation of the mirror variety over Λ, or equivalently the derived category of matrix factorizations of a superpotential with coefficients in Λ. For a Calabi–Yau threefold such as the quintic, the mirror is a family of Calabi–Yau manifolds over the complexified Kähler moduli space; the Novikov parameter T corresponds to the exponentiated Kähler parameter q = e−t. The HMS equivalence Fuk(M) ≃ DbCoh(X̂) is an equivalence of Λ‑linear categories, and the open‑closed string map identifies the Hochschild homology of Fuk(M) with the quantum cohomology QH*(M) ⊗ Λ.

Important subtleties include the choice of the Novikov ring (universal vs. restricted to rational exponents), the need for a bounding cochain to cancel obstructed disks, and the fact that the Fukaya category of a non‑exact symplectic manifold is not invariant under symplectic deformation unless one keeps track of the Novikov parameter. Sheridan's proof of HMS for the quintic threefold uses the Novikov ring to match A‑model disk potentials with B‑model period integrals. Examination questions may ask to define the universal Novikov ring, to explain why the T‑adic topology guarantees convergence of the A∞‑operations, or to describe how the mirror map identifies the Novikov parameter with the complexified Kähler parameter.

Technique: Computing the Floer differential over the Novikov ring
Let L be a monotone Lagrangian torus in a symplectic manifold (M, ω) with minimal Maslov index 2. Suppose there are exactly two homotopy classes of Maslov‑index‑2 disks with boundary on L, each of symplectic area A > 0. Write the Floer differential d on the generator x ∈ CF*(L, L) in terms of the Novikov variable T.
The Floer differential counts rigid pseudoholomorphic disks with one input and one output. For a monotone Lagrangian, the differential squares to zero only after including the Novikov weights. Each of the two disk classes contributes a term TA times the algebraic count of disks in that class. If the signed counts are n₁ and n₂, then d(x) = (n₁ + n₂) TA x. Because the Novikov ring records area, the differential is a well‑defined Λ‑linear map. The T‑adic valuation of d(x) is A, reflecting the minimal energy of a non‑constant disk. This ensures that the spectral sequence associated to the energy filtration converges to the Floer cohomology HF*(L, L; Λ).
Exam‑day strategy
When a question mentions non‑exact symplectic forms, immediately recall the universal Novikov ring Λ and the energy filtration. State that the A∞‑operations are formal power series in T with real exponents, and that convergence is guaranteed by the T‑adic topology. On the mirror side, the Novikov parameter corresponds to the exponentiated Kähler parameter q = e−t. Do not confuse the restricted Novikov ring (rational exponents) with the universal one; the latter is a field and is used for general non‑exact HMS statements.

The SYZ Transform for Lagrangian Torus Fibrations and Homological Mirror Symmetry

The Strominger–Yau–Zaslow (SYZ) conjecture proposes that mirror symmetry is a fiberwise T‑duality on a special Lagrangian torus fibration π: M → B. The SYZ transform is a functorial realization of this duality at the categorical level. Given a Lagrangian torus fibration with a section, one constructs a Fourier–Mukai type kernel on the fiber product M ×B M̂, where M̂ is the dual torus fibration. The transform sends a Lagrangian brane (L, ∇) on M to a coherent sheaf on M̂ by integrating the connection ∇ along the fibers. In the semi‑flat approximation (where the fibration is a flat Tⁿ‑bundle with an affine structure on B), the transform is an equivalence between the Fukaya category of M and the derived category of coherent sheaves on the dual torus fibration M̂. The affine structure on B provides a canonical identification of the fiberwise dual tori, and the complex structure on M̂ is determined by the symplectic form on M via the Legendre transform of the Kähler potential.

Instanton corrections arise from pseudoholomorphic disks with boundary on the Lagrangian fibers. These corrections modify the complex structure on M̂ and the gluing of the local SYZ transforms. Kontsevich and Soibelman formulated the wall‑crossing formula for the affine structure, and Gross–Siebert developed a tropical geometry approach to compute the corrected mirror. Abouzaid showed that for toric varieties, the family Floer functor from the Fukaya category to the derived category of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model coincides with the SYZ transform, with instanton corrections encoded in the superpotential. The transform sends a Lagrangian section to a skyscraper sheaf on the mirror, a Lagrangian fiber to the structure sheaf, and a Lagrangian multi‑section to a vector bundle. The HMS equivalence is then obtained by extending the SYZ transform to the whole Fukaya category via generation by Lagrangian sections and fibers.

Key technical points include the requirement that the fibration be a Lagrangian torus fibration with a section (or at least a rational section), the role of the affine structure with singularities on the base B, and the fact that the SYZ transform is a quasi‑equivalence of A∞‑categories only after incorporating the instanton corrections. Fukaya's work on the SYZ conjecture for K3 surfaces and Tu's on the SYZ transform for abelian varieties provide concrete examples. Examination questions may ask to describe the semi‑flat SYZ transform, to explain how the affine structure on the base determines the complex structure on the mirror, or to state the relationship between the superpotential of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model and the disk counts on the A‑side.

Non‑Commutative Homological Mirror Symmetry and the Fukaya Category of a Non‑Commutative Mirror

In many examples the mirror of a symplectic manifold is not a commutative variety but a non‑commutative space represented by a differential graded algebra A or an A∞‑category. Non‑commutative HMS asserts an equivalence between the Fukaya category Fuk(M) and the derived category of modules D(A) (or the perfect derived category Perf(A)) over the Novikov ring. The archetypal case is the punctured Riemann surface Σ: its Fukaya category is generated by arcs, and the mirror is the Ginzburg dg‑algebra associated to a quiver with potential derived from a triangulation of Σ. Haiden–Katzarkov–Kontsevich proved this equivalence, which realizes the cluster structure on the Fukaya category. Another class of examples comes from Landau–Ginzburg models with a non‑commutative superpotential; the B‑model is the category of matrix factorizations of a non‑commutative potential, which is equivalent to the Fukaya–Seidel category of the corresponding Lefschetz fibration.

The non‑commutative mirror often arises as the endomorphism algebra of a split‑generator in the Fukaya category. By the generation criterion of Abouzaid, if an object G ∈ Fuk(M) split‑generates, then Fuk(M) ≃ Perf(End(G)). The algebra End(G) is typically a non‑commutative A∞‑algebra. On the B‑side, the derived category of a non‑commutative resolution of singularities (e.g., the derived category of a non‑commutative crepant resolution) provides the mirror. Van den Bergh's work on non‑commutative crepant resolutions and Kuznetsov's on homological projective duality supply many such equivalences. In the context of the Gross–Siebert program, the mirror of a log Calabi–Yau variety is a non‑commutative algebra obtained by gluing local tori with non‑commutative corrections.

Important features include the fact that the Hochschild homology of the non‑commutative mirror computes the quantum cohomology of M, and that the open‑closed string map factors through the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism for the non‑commutative algebra. The categorical K‑theory of Perf(A) is isomorphic to the topological K‑theory of M. Examination questions may ask to define a non‑commutative mirror, to explain how the endomorphism algebra of a split‑generator yields a non‑commutative mirror, or to describe the role of the Ginzburg algebra in the HMS for punctured surfaces.

Categorical K‑Theory and the Chern Character in Homological Mirror Symmetry

The categorical K‑theory K₀(𝒞) of a triangulated dg‑category 𝒞 (or a stable ∞‑category) is the Grothendieck group of its split‑closed derived category. For the Fukaya category Fuk(M) and the derived category DbCoh(X), the HMS equivalence induces an isomorphism K₀(Fuk(M)) ≅ K₀(DbCoh(X)). The Chern character ch: K₀(𝒞) → HH₀(𝒞) maps K‑theory to Hochschild homology (the 0‑th Hochschild homology is the trace of the identity functor). On the A‑side, HH₀(Fuk(M)) is isomorphic to the quantum cohomology QH*(M) via the open‑closed string map; on the B‑side, HH₀(DbCoh(X)) ≅ ⊕p Hp(X, ΩpX) by the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg theorem. The compatibility of the Chern character with the HMS equivalence is a deep theorem: the equivalence of categories intertwines the open‑closed map on the A‑side with the HKR isomorphism on the B‑side, up to the mirror map identifying quantum cohomology with the variation of Hodge structure.

This compatibility has concrete consequences for enumerative geometry. The categorical Donaldson–Thomas invariants defined by Kontsevich–Soibelman are elements of K₀(𝒞) that count objects in the category; under the Chern character they map to cohomology classes that satisfy the wall‑crossing formula. In the HMS setting, DT invariants on the B‑side are conjecturally matched with counts of special Lagrangians on the A‑side. Blanc's work on categorical K‑theory of dg‑categories and Toën–Vezzosi's on the Chern character provide the formal framework. For a Calabi–Yau category of dimension d, the Chern character lands in the primitive part of HH₀, and the Mukai pairing on K‑theory corresponds to the intersection pairing on quantum cohomology.

Examination questions may ask to define the categorical K‑theory of a dg‑category, to state the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism for a smooth variety, or to explain how the open‑closed string map identifies the Chern character on the Fukaya side with the HKR isomorphism on the derived category side. A common trap is to confuse the topological K‑theory of the underlying space with the categorical K‑theory of the Fukaya category; they coincide only after tensoring with the Novikov field and under the HMS equivalence.

Graded Fukaya Categories and the Role of Maslov Indices

The Fukaya category of an exact or monotone symplectic manifold is naturally a ℤ‑graded A∞‑category only after a choice of grading structure on each Lagrangian submanifold. A grading on a Lagrangian L is a lift of the Gauss map L → Λ(n) (the Lagrangian Grassmannian) to its universal cover Λ̃(n) ≅ ℝ. The obstruction to such a lift is the Maslov class μL ∈ H¹(L; ℤ). When μL = 0, the set of gradings is a torsor for H⁰(L; ℤ) ≅ ℤπ₀(L). In the monotone setting, the minimal Maslov number NL = min{μ(β) | β ∈ π₂(M, L), μ(β) > 0} controls the ℤ/NL‑grading that survives after accounting for holomorphic disks. The A∞‑operations μk then carry degree 2−k with respect to this grading, and the differential μ₁ squares to zero precisely because the boundary of a 1‑dimensional moduli space of disks splits into pairs of rigid disks whose Maslov indices add up correctly. In practice, one often works with a relative grading between two Lagrangians L₀, L₁, defined by the Conley–Zehnder index of a Hamiltonian chord; this relative grading is independent of the absolute choices and is sufficient for defining the morphism spaces hom(L₀, L₁). The Maslov index also appears in the grading shift functor [1] on the derived Fukaya category: shifting a graded Lagrangian by one increases its grading by 1, which corresponds under homological mirror symmetry to tensoring a coherent sheaf by the canonical bundle ωX[−n] on the mirror side. A common subtlety is that the grading on a Lagrangian sphere in a Calabi–Yau manifold of dimension n is only defined up to an overall shift of n, reflecting the fact that the spherical twist autoequivalence acts by [n] on the derived category. Examination questions may ask to define a grading on a Lagrangian, to explain why the Maslov class obstructs a ℤ‑grading, or to compute the relative grading of two intersection points in terms of the Conley–Zehnder index of a capping path.

Symplectic Cohomology and the Closed‑Open String Map in HMS

For a Liouville manifold (M, λ) with contact boundary, symplectic cohomology SH*(M) is defined as the direct limit of Hamiltonian Floer cohomology groups for Hamiltonians that grow linearly at infinity. It carries a unital ring structure given by the pair‑of‑pants product and a BV operator Δ coming from the S¹‑action on the free loop space. The closed‑open string map CO: SH*(M) → HH*(Fuk(M)) sends a Hamiltonian orbit to the Hochschild class represented by the operation of “inserting” that orbit into the A∞‑structure of the wrapped Fukaya category Fuk(M). A fundamental theorem of Ganatra (and independently of Ritter–Smith) states that for a Liouville manifold, CO is an isomorphism of BV algebras. On the mirror side, for an affine variety X = Spec R, the Hochschild homology HH*(DᵇCoh(X)) is isomorphic to the algebraic de Rham cohomology of X, and the closed‑open map corresponds under HMS to the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism. The compatibility of the BV operator with the Connes operator on Hochschild homology is a deep result that underlies the identification of the quantum product on SH*(M) with the cup product on the mirror. In the compact Calabi–Yau case, symplectic cohomology vanishes, but the quantum cohomology QH*(M) plays the analogous role; the open‑closed map OC: HH*(Fuk(M)) → QH*(M) is then the relevant comparison. The closed‑open map also controls the generation criterion: a collection of Lagrangians split‑generates Fuk(M) if and only if the induced map SH*(M) → ⊕ HH*(hom(Li, Li)) is injective. This criterion, due to Abouzaid, is a cornerstone of many HMS proofs. Examiners may ask to state the definition of symplectic cohomology, to describe the closed‑open map on the chain level, or to explain why CO is an isomorphism for Liouville manifolds but not for general symplectic manifolds.

Let M = T* S¹ with its standard Liouville structure. Compute SH*(M) and describe the closed‑open map to HH*(Fuk(M)).
The Reeb flow on the contact boundary S¹ × {r = const} is periodic with period 1. Hamiltonian orbits correspond to multiples of the Reeb orbit. The symplectic cohomology SH*(T* S¹) is isomorphic to H*(S¹) ⊗ Λ[u, u⁻¹] where u has degree 2 (the BV operator acts by ∂/∂u). The wrapped Fukaya category Fuk(T* S¹) is generated by the zero‑section S¹ and a cotangent fiber. Its Hochschild homology is also H*(S¹) ⊗ Λ[u, u⁻¹]. The closed‑open map sends the unit 1 ∈ SH⁰ to the identity morphism of the zero‑section, and the generator u ∈ SH² to the class represented by the loop rotating the fiber. One checks that CO respects the BV operator and the product, giving an isomorphism of BV algebras.

Fukaya Categories of Liouville Sectors and Sectorial Mirror Symmetry

A Liouville sector is a Liouville manifold with boundary equipped with a collection of stops on its contact boundary — a finite set of Legendrian submanifolds that constrain the wrapping of Lagrangians. The partially wrapped Fukaya category W(M, 𝔣) (also called the sectorial Fukaya category) is defined by allowing Lagrangians to wrap only in directions that avoid the stops. This construction, developed by Ganatra–Pardon–Shende and by Sylvan, provides a flexible framework for mirror symmetry of varieties with boundary divisors. On the B‑side, the mirror of a Liouville sector (M, 𝔣) is a pair (X, D) where X is a scheme (often a toric variety) and D is a divisor corresponding to the stops. The category of coherent sheaves on X with support conditions along D, or equivalently the category of modules over a gentle algebra associated to the stop diagram, is expected to be equivalent to W(M, 𝔣). A key feature is the stop removal functor: forgetting a stop induces a localization of the wrapped Fukaya category, which on the mirror side corresponds to inverting a function that vanishes on the corresponding divisor component. This functoriality allows one to build HMS equivalences inductively by adding or removing stops. The sectorial perspective also clarifies the relationship between the wrapped Fukaya category (no stops) and the compact Fukaya category (all Reeb chords stopped). In particular, the wrapped category is the colimit of sectorial categories as stops are removed. Examination questions may ask to define a Liouville sector, to describe the stop removal functor, or to explain how the mirror of a sector with a single stop on a 2‑dimensional Liouville manifold is the derived category of a gentle algebra.

Categorical Crepant Resolutions and the Fukaya Category of Symplectic Manifolds with Corners

When a symplectic manifold M has a corner — for instance, the product of two Liouville manifolds or a symplectic manifold with a normal‑crossing divisor — its Fukaya category can be singular in the sense that it is not smooth as a dg‑category. A categorical crepant resolution of a singular dg‑category 𝒞 is a smooth dg‑category 𝒟 equipped with a fully faithful functor 𝒟 → 𝒞 whose essential image generates 𝒞 and such that the induced map on Hochschild homology is an isomorphism. In the context of HMS, the mirror of a symplectic manifold with corners is often a singular variety X, and the derived category DᵇCoh(X) is replaced by a smooth category such as the category of matrix factorizations of a Landau–Ginzburg potential or the derived category of a non‑commutative resolution constructed by Van den Bergh. For example, the Fukaya category of a symplectic manifold with a normal‑crossing divisor D is expected to be equivalent to the category of coherent sheaves on the mirror pair (X, D) with a logarithmic structure, which can be resolved by a sequence of blow‑ups on the B‑side. The work of Kuznetsov on homological projective duality provides many instances where the derived category of a singular variety admits a crepant resolution by the derived category of a smooth variety, and these resolutions are mirrored by symplectic surgeries such as Lagrangian handlebody decompositions. A practical consequence is that the Hochschild homology of the Fukaya category of a manifold with corners computes the quantum cohomology of the smooth part, while the categorical K‑theory captures the topology of the resolution. Examiners may ask to define a categorical crepant resolution, to give an example from Van den Bergh’s construction, or to explain why the Fukaya category of a product of Liouville manifolds is the tensor product of the individual Fukaya categories and how this relates to the Künneth formula for Hochschild homology.

Quantum Cohomology, the Closed‑Open Map, and the HMS Equivalence

For a compact monotone symplectic manifold (M, ω), the quantum cohomology QH*(M) is the vector space H*(M; Λ) equipped with the quantum product a * b = Σβ ∈ H₂(M) (a ∪ b ∪ ev₀* PD[β]) q^β, where the sum runs over effective curve classes and the coefficients are Gromov–Witten invariants counting rational curves with three marked points. The open‑closed string map OC: HH*(Fuk(M)) → QH*(M) is defined on the chain level by counting holomorphic disks with one interior marked point mapping to a cycle representing a Hochschild class. A theorem of Seidel (and later of Ganatra–Perutz–Sheridan) shows that OC is a unital ring homomorphism. Under the HMS equivalence Fuk(M) ≃ DᵇCoh(X), the open‑closed map corresponds to the composition HH*(DᵇCoh(X)) ≅ ⊕ H^p(X, Ω^p_X) → QH*(M), where the first isomorphism is the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg theorem and the second is the mirror map identifying quantum parameters with complex structure parameters. The compatibility of OC with the mirror map is a highly non‑trivial check in any concrete HMS proof; it implies that the quantum product on QH*(M) is isomorphic to the cup product on the mirror’s cohomology after a change of coordinates. In the Fano case, the quantum cohomology is semisimple at a generic point, and the HMS equivalence identifies the idempotents of QH*(M) with the exceptional collection generating DᵇCoh(X). A common trap is to confuse the open‑closed map with the closed‑open map CO: SH*(M) → HH*(Fuk(M)); the former lands in quantum cohomology, the latter in Hochschild homology, and they are dual under the Poincaré pairing when M is Calabi–Yau. Examination questions may ask to write the formula for the quantum product, to define the open‑closed map on the chain level, or to explain how the mirror map is extracted from the equality of the two ring structures.

  • Confusing the Maslov index with the Conley–Zehnder index; the former is a homotopy invariant of a loop of Lagrangian subspaces, the latter is defined for a non‑degenerate symplectic path and depends on a capping.
  • Assuming the closed‑open map CO is always an isomorphism; it is an isomorphism for Liouville manifolds (wrapped Fukaya category) but fails for compact monotone manifolds where quantum cohomology replaces symplectic cohomology.
  • Treating a Liouville sector as a Liouville manifold with boundary; the stops are essential data, and the partially wrapped category is not the same as the wrapped category of the interior.
  • Believing that a categorical crepant resolution always exists for any singular dg‑category; existence is a deep theorem (Van den Bergh for Gorenstein singularities, Kuznetsov for homological projective duality) and fails in general.
  • Mixing up the open‑closed map OC: HH*(Fuk) → QH* with the closed‑open map CO: SH* → HH*(Fuk); they go in opposite directions and involve different target rings.
  • Neglecting the grading shift in the spherical twist: the twist functor T_L acts by [n] on the derived Fukaya category of a Calabi–Yau n‑fold, not by [1].
  • Assuming the mirror map is a simple identification of parameters; it is a formal power series change of coordinates determined by the equality of the quantum product and the cup product under HKR.
