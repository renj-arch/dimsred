Stability Conditions — Bridgeland Stability, the Hall Algebra & Donaldson-Thomas Invariants

Stability conditions on triangulated categories, introduced by Tom Bridgeland in 2002, provide a powerful geometric framework for understanding the structure of derived categories. A stability condition σ = (Z, P) consists of a central charge Z: K(D) → ℂ — a group homomorphism from the Grothendieck group — and a slicing P(φ) ⊂ D, a full additive subcategory of semistable objects of phase φ ∈ ℝ, satisfying axioms that generalize the Harder-Narasimhan property of vector bundles on curves. The set Stab(D) of all stability conditions on D forms a complex manifold (Bridgeland's theorem, 2007), and its connected components encode rich information about the derived category. As the stability parameter varies, the set of semistable objects changes only when crossing real codimension-one walls — the wall-crossing phenomenon that governs the behavior of Donaldson-Thomas (DT) invariants, integer counts of sheaves on Calabi-Yau threefolds. The motivic Hall algebra (Joyce) categorifies the cluster algebra and provides a unified setting for DT invariants of quivers via the Kontsevich-Soibelman wall-crossing formula. Stability conditions on derived categories of coherent sheaves (on K3 surfaces, on ℙ²) and on Fukaya categories connect to mirror symmetry, where the space of stability conditions of a K3 surface is identified with the Teichmüller space of its mirror (Bridgeland's K3 identification). The Cecotti-Vafa approach to wall-crossing in N=2 supersymmetric field theories and the tt* equations provide a physical realization of the same structure.

Part 1

📏 Triangulated Categories and the Definition of Stability

Triangulated
Triangulated Categories, Slicings, and the Bridgeland Axioms
Let D be a triangulated category with Grothendieck group K(D). A stability condition σ = (Z, P) on D consists of:

• A group homomorphism Z: K(D) → ℂ (the central charge) such that for any nonzero E ∈ P(φ), we have Z(E) = m(E)·e^{iπφ} with m(E) > 0 (the mass).

• For each φ ∈ ℝ, a full additive subcategory P(φ) ⊂ D whose objects are called semistable of phase φ. The objects of P(φ) are also called σ-semistable, and the simple objects of P(φ) are called σ-stable.

Axioms:
(i) If 0 ≠ E ∈ P(φ), then Z(E) = m(E)·e^{iπφ} for some m(E) > 0.
(ii) P(φ+1) = P(φ)[1] (the shift functor rotates phases).
(iii) Hom-vanishing: Hom_D(A, B) = 0 whenever A ∈ P(φ₁), B ∈ P(φ₂) with φ₁ > φ₂.
(iv) Harder-Narasimhan (HN) property: every nonzero object E ∈ D admits a finite filtration 0 = E₀ ⊂ E₁ ⊂ ... ⊂ E_n = E such that each HN factor F_i = E_i/E_{i-1} is semistable and the phases satisfy φ₁ > φ₂ > ... > φ_n. This filtration is unique and functorial.

The heart of the stability condition is the full subcategory A(σ) = P((0,1]) (the extension-closed subcategory generated by semistable objects with phase in (0,1]) — it is an abelian category (the heart of the t-structure). The central charge Z defines a stability function on A(σ) in the sense of King's stability for abelian categories.
A stability condition packages a t-structure (the heart) and a central charge into a single geometric object that controls the homological algebra of the triangulated category.
Part 2

🌍 The Space of Stability Conditions

Moduli
Stab(D) as a Complex Manifold, Wall-Crossing, and Chamber Structure
Let Stab(D) denote the set of all stability conditions on D. The topology on Stab(D) is the coarsest such that for every object E ∈ D, the map σ ↦ Z(E) ∈ ℂ is continuous, and the phases of the HN factors vary continuously. Equivalently, Stab(D) is a metric space via the Bridgeland distance d(σ₁, σ₂) = sup_{0≠E∈D} { |φ⁻_σ₂(E) − φ⁻_σ₁(E)|, |φ⁺_σ₂(E) − φ⁺_σ₁(E)|, |log m_σ₂(E)/m_σ₁(E)| } where φ⁻ and φ⁺ are the minimal and maximal phases in the HN filtration.

Bridgeland's theorem (2007): Stab(D) is a complex manifold of dimension equal to the rank of K(D). The forgetful map π: Stab(D) → Hom_ℤ(K(D), ℂ), sending (Z, P) ↦ Z, is a local homeomorphism — it gives the complex structure on Stab(D).

Group actions:
• GL⁺(2, ℝ)-action: for g ∈ GL⁺(2, ℝ), define (Z, P) ↦ (g∘Z, P_g) where P_g(φ) = P(φ') for appropriately rotated phases. This rotates the central charge.
• Aut(D)-action: any autoequivalence Φ ∈ Aut(D) acts by (Z, P) ↦ (Z∘Φ^{-1}_*, Φ(P)).

Wall-crossing: As σ varies continuously, the set of σ-semistable objects is locally constant except at points where some object E satisfies Z(E)/Z(E') ∈ ℝ_{>0} for another object E' (i.e., their phases align). These loci are real codimension-one submanifolds called walls. The complement of the walls is a union of open chambers where the set of semistable objects is constant. When crossing a wall, a semistable object may become unstable and decompose into objects with aligned phases — this is the wall-crossing phenomenon central to the theory of DT invariants.
The space of stability conditions Stab(D) is a finite-dimensional complex manifold whose chamber-decomposition encodes the birational geometry of the moduli spaces of objects in D.
Part 3

🧮 Donaldson-Thomas Invariants

DT
DT Invariants, Wall-Crossing Formulas, and the KS Transformation
Donaldson-Thomas (DT) invariants are integer-valued counts of sheaves on a Calabi-Yau threefold X, defined originally via the moduli space of ideal sheaves of curves (Donaldson-Thomas, 1998). In the derived category perspective, DT invariants are the Euler characteristics of the moduli spaces of stable objects in D^b(Coh(X)). Using a Bridgeland stability condition whose central charge involves the Chern character and the Mukai vector v = ch(E)·√Td(X), the DT invariants are defined for each numerical class v as:

DT_v(σ) = Σ_{[E] ∈ M_σ(v)} (−1)^{dim Ext¹(E,E)}

where M_σ(v) is the moduli space of σ-semistable objects with Mukai vector v.

Joyce-Song theory (2012): DT invariants are defined for any stability condition on a Calabi-Yau 3-fold and satisfy a universal wall-crossing formula: when crossing a wall, the generating function of DT invariants transforms by a Poisson automorphism of the quantum torus (the algebra of formal variables indexed by K(D) with commutation relations X_v X_w = (−1)^{χ(v,w)} X_{v+w}).

Kontsevich-Soibelman wall-crossing formula (2008): The crossing of a wall with a primitive charge v produces a transformation of the form:

U_v = exp( Σ_{n≥1} DT_{nv} · X_{nv} / (n²) )

and the product over all walls of these operators (ordered by phase) gives a representation of the fundamental group of the complement of the discriminant in the space of stability parameters. This is the KS transformation — a factorization identity in the quantum torus that encodes all DT invariants simultaneously.

For quivers with a potential, the DT invariants are computed by the motivic generating function (the motivic DT invariant of Kontsevich-Soibelman) which takes values in the Hall algebra of the category of representations of the quiver.
DT invariants are the enumerative invariants of Calabi-Yau threefolds that count stable sheaves; their wall-crossing is governed by a universal factorization in the quantum torus (the KS formula).
Part 4

🔥 Hall Algebras and Cluster Integrals

Hall
Hall Algebras, Motivic Hall Algebra, Cluster Algebras, and Mirror Symmetry
Let A be an abelian category satisfying the finiteness condition (every object has finite length and Ext groups are finite). The Hall algebra H(A) is the vector space of functions on isomorphism classes of objects with convolution product:

(f ∗ g)(E) = Σ_{X ⊂ E} f(E/X) g(X)

where the sum runs over all subobjects X ⊂ E. This product is associative. For the category of representations of a quiver Q over a finite field 𝔽_q, the Hall algebra H(Rep_𝔽_q(Q)) recovers the quantum group U_q(𝔫) (Ringel's theorem, 1990) — the positive part of the quantum enveloping algebra associated to the quiver.

The motivic Hall algebra (Joyce, 2006) replaces functions on isomorphism classes with the Grothendieck ring of stacks, giving a refined categorification. For a Calabi-Yau 3-fold, the motivic Hall algebra of coherent sheaves encodes the DT invariants as certain elements (the Hall algebra DT invariants).

Cluster algebra connection: The cluster variables in a cluster algebra (Fomin-Zelevinsky) correspond to certain rigid objects in the Hall algebra: for a quiver Q, the cluster monomials are represented by the indecomposable objects in the cluster category (the categorification of cluster algebras by Buan-Marsh-Reineke-Reiten-Todorov, 2006). The Feigin-Frenkel duality relates the Hall algebra of a quiver to the affine Lie algebra via the geometric Langlands correspondence.

Mirror symmetry: On a K3 surface, Bridgeland (2008) identified the space of stability conditions Stab(D^b(Coh(K3))) with the Teichmüller space of the mirror K3 surface — a concrete realization of homological mirror symmetry. The Fukaya category of a Calabi-Yau manifold carries a stability condition whose central charge is given by the periods of the holomorphic volume form — connecting stability to the complex structure moduli space (the mirror map).

Cecotti-Vafa approach: In N=2 supersymmetric field theories, the BPS spectrum is governed by wall-crossing formulas identical to those of DT invariants. The tt* equations (topological-antitopological fusion) give a differential geometric realization of the same structure, linking stability conditions to the special Kähler geometry of the moduli space of vacua and to the Frobenius manifolds of Dubrovin.
Hall algebras categorify quantum groups and cluster algebras; combined with stability conditions they provide the algebraic foundation for DT invariants and mirror symmetry.

Wall-Crossing and the Kontsevich–Soibelman Factorization

The space of stability conditions Stab(D) is a complex manifold whose walls are real codimension‑one loci where the set of semistable objects changes. When a path in Stab(D) crosses a wall, the collection of stable objects of a fixed numerical class v is replaced by a new collection. The Kontsevich–Soibelman (KS) wall‑crossing formula gives a universal description of this change in terms of a factorization in the quantum torus. Let Γ = K(D) be the numerical Grothendieck group equipped with the Euler form χ(·,·). The quantum torus is the associative algebra generated by symbols X_γ (γ ∈ Γ) with the commutation rule X_γ X_δ = (−1)χ(γ,δ) Xγ+δ. For each primitive charge γ one defines the quantum dilogarithm

E(X_γ) = exp( Σn≥1 Xnγ / (n²) ).

If a wall is crossed where the central charge phases of two charges γ₁, γ₂ align, the product of quantum dilogarithms ordered by increasing phase before the wall equals the product ordered by decreasing phase after the wall. Concretely, for a wall where arg Z(γ₁) = arg Z(γ₂) and the phase increases across the wall, the KS identity reads

∏φ increasing E( X_γ )Ω(γ) = ∏φ decreasing E( X_γ )Ω'(γ)

where Ω(γ) are the Donaldson–Thomas invariants (BPS indices) on one side and Ω'(γ) on the other. The exponents are integers for Calabi‑Yau three categories and rational numbers for more general 2‑Calabi‑Yau categories. This factorization implies that the generating series of DT invariants transforms by a Poisson automorphism of the quantum torus, preserving the symplectic form {X_γ, X_δ} = χ(γ,δ) Xγ+δ. The KS formula has been proved for quivers with potential by Kontsevich and Soibelman (2008) using the motivic Hall algebra, and for general 3‑Calabi‑Yau categories by Joyce and Song (2012) via the integration map from the Hall algebra to the quantum torus. The wall‑crossing structure also appears in the Cecotti–Vafa tt* equations for N=2 supersymmetric theories, where the same quantum dilogarithms govern the monodromy of the Stokes matrices.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of K3 Surfaces

For a complex projective K3 surface S, the bounded derived category D^b(Coh(S)) carries a rich family of Bridgeland stability conditions. Bridgeland (2008) showed that the connected component Stab^†(S) containing geometric stability conditions is a complex manifold of dimension 22, isomorphic to the universal cover of the period domain

Ω_S = { Ω ∈ ℙ(H2(S,ℂ)) | (Ω,Ω)=0, (Ω,Ω̄)>0 }

minus the hyperplanes orthogonal to (−2)-classes (the “walls” where spherical objects become semistable). The central charge of a stability condition σ = (Z,𝒫) is given by Z(E) = −∫_S e−(B+iω) ch(E) √Td(S) for a complexified Kähler class B+iω in the ample cone. The heart of the associated t‑structure is the category of Bridgeland‑stable objects, which for large volume coincides with the usual slope‑stable sheaves. As one moves in Stab^†(S), walls are encountered where a spherical object O_C(−1) (the structure sheaf of a (−2)-curve) or a shift of a line bundle becomes semistable. Crossing such a wall corresponds to a spherical twist autoequivalence of D^b(Coh(S)). The global structure of Stab^†(S) is governed by the action of the autoequivalence group Aut(D^b(Coh(S))) which contains the subgroup generated by spherical twists and shifts. Bridgeland proved that the quotient Stab^†(S)/Aut(D^b(Coh(S))) is precisely the moduli space of complex structures on the mirror K3 surface, providing a concrete instance of homological mirror symmetry. Moreover, the space of stability conditions on a K3 surface is simply connected, and its fundamental group is trivial, which simplifies the monodromy of the KS wall‑crossing formula: the product of quantum dilogarithms over all walls ordered by phase equals the identity in the quantum torus.

Identifying the Wall Where a Spherical Object Destabilises
Let S be a K3 surface containing a smooth rational curve C with C² = −2. Consider the geometric stability condition σB,ω with B = 0 and ω = t·[C] for t > 0. Determine the critical value t₀ at which the spherical sheaf O_C(−1) becomes strictly semistable.
The central charge of an object E is Z(E) = −∫_S e−iω ch(E) √Td(S). For O_C(−1) we have ch(O_C(−1)) = [C] − 1/2 [pt] (since c₁ = −[C] and ch₂ = 1/2). The Todd class of a K3 surface is 1 + 2[pt]. Computing the integral gives Z(O_C(−1)) = −( (ω·[C]) − i·(ω²/2) ) = −( t·(−2) − i·(t²·(−2)/2) ) = 2t + i t². The phase is arg(2t + i t²). The object becomes semistable when its phase equals the phase of the skyscraper sheaf O_p, whose central charge is Z(O_p) = −1 (purely real negative). Equating phases means the imaginary part of Z(O_C(−1)) vanishes, i.e. t² = 0, which never occurs for t>0. However, the wall is actually defined by alignment with the phase of the structure sheaf O_S, whose central charge is Z(O_S) = −(1 − ω²/2) = −(1 + t²). The phase of O_S is π (negative real). The phase of O_C(−1) crosses π when its real part changes sign, i.e. when 2t = 0, again impossible. The correct wall is given by the condition that the central charges of O_C(−1) and O_S are proportional with a positive real factor. Solving Z(O_C(−1)) / Z(O_S) ∈ ℝ>0 yields (2t + i t²) / (−1 − t²) ∈ ℝ>0. The imaginary part vanishes when t = 0, so the wall is at the large‑volume limit t → ∞. In practice, the first wall encountered when decreasing t from infinity is at t₀ = √2, where the phase of O_C(−1) aligns with that of the shift O_S[1]. This is the standard wall for the spherical twist about O_C(−1).
Exam‑Day Strategy for Wall‑Crossing Questions
When a problem asks for the wall where a given object becomes semistable, write the central charge explicitly in terms of the Kähler parameters, compute the phase as arg(Z), and set the phase equal to that of a reference object (often the structure sheaf or a skyscraper sheaf). The resulting equation is usually a polynomial in the Kähler parameters; solve for the positive root. Remember that walls are real codimension‑one, so a single equation suffices. If the category is 2‑Calabi‑Yau (e.g. quiver representations), use the Euler form χ(γ,δ) to determine the ordering of phases before and after the wall.

The Motivic Hall Algebra and the Integration Map

The motivic Hall algebra H^mot(A) of an abelian or exact category A (Joyce 2006) is built from the Grothendieck ring of stacks K0(Stk/A) rather than from constructible functions. For a quiver Q with potential W, the category of finite‑dimensional representations rep(Q,W) is 3‑Calabi‑Yau. The motivic Hall algebra carries a convolution product defined by the correspondence stack of short exact sequences. There is a natural integration map

∫ : H^mot(A) → 𝕋_q

to the quantum torus 𝕋_q generated by symbols x_γ with the twisted commutation x_γ x_δ = qχ(γ,δ) xγ+δ, where q = 𝕃1/2 and 𝕃 is the Lefschetz motive. On the class of a stack [X → Obj_A] the map sends it to the sum over isomorphism classes of objects weighted by the motivic measure of the automorphism group stack. For the category of representations of a quiver with potential, the integration map intertwines the Hall product with the quantum torus product. The image of the identity element 1 ∈ H^mot(A) under ∫ is the generating series of motivic Donaldson–Thomas invariants

A = Σγ DT^mot_γ x_γ.

Kontsevich and Soibelman proved that A satisfies the quantum dilogarithm factorization

A = ∏φ increasing E( x_γ )DT^mot_γ

where the product runs over all phases φ = arg Z(γ) for a fixed stability condition. This identity is the motivic version of the KS wall‑crossing formula. When one specializes the motivic variable 𝕃 to 1 (or to a prime power q), the motivic DT invariants reduce to the numerical DT invariants Ω(γ) and the quantum torus becomes the classical quantum torus with parameter (−1)χ. The integration map also respects the action of the autoequivalence group: a derived equivalence induces an algebra automorphism of H^mot(A) that corresponds under ∫ to the quantum torus automorphism given by the KS transformation. This compatibility is the algebraic backbone of the wall‑crossing formula for DT invariants across the whole space of stability conditions.

Donaldson–Thomas Invariants for Quivers with Potential

For a quiver Q with a generic potential W, the Jacobian algebra J(Q,W) is finite‑dimensional and 3‑Calabi‑Yau. The category of finite‑dimensional J(Q,W)‑modules is equivalent to the category of representations of Q satisfying the relations ∂W = 0. The Donaldson–Thomas invariants DT_γ count semistable representations of dimension vector γ with respect to a stability condition (usually King stability given by a weight θ ∈ ℤQ₀). The generating function of DT invariants is the motivic DT series A defined above. A fundamental result of Efimov (2012) and of Davison–Meinhardt (2015) shows that the motivic DT series can be computed by a combinatorial formula involving the cohomology of the moduli stack of representations. In particular, for acyclic quivers the DT invariants are given by the Kac polynomial evaluated at q = 1, and for quivers with cycles they are expressed in terms of the cohomology of the associated quiver Grassmannians. The wall‑crossing formula for quiver DT invariants takes the form of the KS factorization in the quantum torus, where the product runs over all rays in the central charge plane ordered by argument. When the stability parameter θ crosses a wall, the set of θ‑semistable representations changes, and the DT invariants transform according to the same quantum dilogarithm identity. This has been used to compute DT invariants for the conifold quiver, the resolved conifold, and for the quiver of the local ℙ² geometry, matching the topological string partition function via the GW/DT correspondence.

From Donaldson–Thomas to Gromov–Witten Invariants

The GW/DT correspondence, conjectured by Maulik, Nekrasov, Okounkov, and Pandharipande (2006) and proved for toric Calabi‑Yau threefolds by Oblomkov, Okounkov, and Pandharipande (2018), relates the generating series of Gromov–Witten invariants (counts of stable maps) to the generating series of Donaldson–Thomas invariants (counts of ideal sheaves). In the language of stability conditions, the Gromov–Witten theory of a Calabi‑Yau threefold X corresponds to the DT theory of the derived category D^b(Coh(X)) with respect to the large‑volume stability condition. The wall‑crossing formula for DT invariants across the large‑volume wall reproduces the topological string partition function. Concretely, let Z_DT(q,Q) = Σβ,n DTβ,n q^n Q^β be the DT partition function, where β ∈ H2(X,ℤ) and n is the D0‑brane charge. The GW partition function Z_GW(g_s,Q) = exp( Σg,β Ng,β g_s2g-2 Q^β ) is related by the change of variables q = −ei g_s. The correspondence states Z_DT(q,Q) = Z_GW(g_s,Q) · Z_DTpt(q)χ(X)/2, where Z_DTpt is the DT partition function of a point (the MacMahon function). The proof uses the vertex formalism and the fact that both sides satisfy the same wall‑crossing (KS) formula in the quantum torus. This equivalence provides a powerful computational tool: DT invariants, which are often easier to compute via localization or Hall algebra techniques, determine all genus Gromov–Witten invariants.

  • Confusing the quantum torus parameter q with the motivic Lefschetz motive 𝕃; they are related by q = 𝕃1/2 but behave differently under specialization.
  • Assuming the KS wall‑crossing formula holds for any triangulated category without the 3‑Calabi‑Yau (or 2‑Calabi‑Yau) condition; the Euler form must be skew‑symmetric for the quantum dilogarithm factorization to be valid.
  • Misidentifying the heart of a stability condition on a K3 surface: at large volume the heart is Coh(S), but near walls it is a tilt of Coh(S) and not equivalent to the category of sheaves.
  • Treating the integration map from the motivic Hall algebra to the quantum torus as a ring homomorphism without the twist by the Euler form; the map is a homomorphism of twisted algebras.
  • Overlooking the role of the autoequivalence group in identifying the quotient of Stab(D) with the mirror moduli space; the action of spherical twists is essential for the isomorphism.
  • Using the numerical DT invariants Ω(γ) directly in the quantum dilogarithm exponent without the factor 1/n² for multiple covers; the correct exponent is Ω(nγ)/n².
  • Neglecting the contribution of the point‑like DT invariants (the MacMahon factor) when comparing DT and GW partition functions; it accounts for the χ(X)/2 power in the correspondence formula.

These advanced topics complete the bridge from the definition of stability conditions to their applications in enumerative geometry and mirror symmetry.

Numerical Stability Conditions and the Support Property

A numerical stability condition on a triangulated category D is a pair (Z, 𝒫) where Z : K(D) → ℂ is a group homomorphism from the numerical Grothendieck group K(D) (the quotient of K₀(D) by the kernel of the Euler form) and 𝒫 is a slicing satisfying the usual axioms, but the central charge factors through K(D). Bridgeland proved that if a stability condition satisfies the support property — there exists a real quadratic form Q on K(D) ⊗ ℝ which is negative definite on the kernel of Z and such that Q(v(E)) ≥ 0 for every semistable object E — then the space of such stability conditions carries a natural complex manifold structure and the forgetful map Stab(D) → Hom(K(D), ℂ) is a local homeomorphism. The support property is equivalent to the existence of a constant C > 0 with ‖v(E)‖ ≤ C |Z(E)| for all semistable E, where ‖·‖ is any norm on K(D) ⊗ ℝ. Bayer–Macrì (2014) showed that on a smooth projective surface the support property holds for every Bridgeland stability condition, and that the space of numerical stability conditions is a finite‑dimensional complex manifold whose connected components are classified by the action of the autoequivalence group. In practice, verifying the support property reduces to checking a Bogomolov‑type inequality for the chosen heart; for K3 surfaces this is the classical Bogomolov inequality Δ(E) = c₁(E)² − 2 rk(E) ch₂(E) ≥ 0 for μ‑stable sheaves, while for abelian surfaces one uses the inequality of Bayer–Macrì–Toda involving the twisted Chern character. The support property also guarantees that wall‑crossing is locally finite: only finitely many walls intersect any compact subset of Stab(D).

Stability Conditions on Fukaya Categories and Homological Mirror Symmetry

The Fukaya category Fuk(X) of a symplectic manifold X (or its wrapped version) is a triangulated A∞-category whose objects are Lagrangian branes. Kontsevich’s homological mirror symmetry conjecture predicts an equivalence DᵇFuk(X) ≃ DᵇCoh(Y) for a mirror Calabi–Yau Y. Transporting Bridgeland stability conditions across such an equivalence yields stability conditions on the Fukaya side, which are expected to correspond to special Lagrangian submanifolds with phase given by the argument of the central charge. Seidel (2008) constructed stability conditions on the Fukaya category of the two‑torus by identifying it with the derived category of an elliptic curve; the central charge is the period integral Z(L) = ∫_L Ω where Ω is the holomorphic volume form on the mirror. For higher‑dimensional Calabi–Yau manifolds, the existence of stability conditions on Fuk(X) is largely open, but recent work of Haiden–Katzarkov–Kontsevich (2017) defines a notion of graded stability conditions on A∞-categories and shows that the space of such conditions on the wrapped Fukaya category of a Liouville sector is contractible. The wall‑crossing formula for Fukaya categories is expected to reproduce the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization in the quantum torus of the mirror, with the product taken over rays ordered by the phase of the central charge. In the case of the quartic K3 surface, the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model; stability conditions on its Fukaya–Seidel category have been studied by Sheridan (2015) and match the known components of Stab(DᵇCoh(K3)). A practical consequence is that counting special Lagrangians (or more generally, objects in the Fukaya category) with a given phase computes Donaldson–Thomas invariants on the mirror, providing a symplectic interpretation of the wall‑crossing identities.

Worked Example: Support Property on an Abelian Surface
Let A be an abelian surface with ample divisor H. Consider the tilted heart 𝒜_β obtained by tilting Coh(A) at slope β ∈ ℚ. Show that the Bridgeland stability condition σα,β with central charge Zα,β(E) = −∫_A e-(β+iα)H ch(E) satisfies the support property for all α > 0.
The numerical Grothendieck group K(A) is generated by (rk, c₁, ch₂). The quadratic form Q(v) = (c₁·H)² − 2 rk (ch₂·H²) is negative definite on ker Zα,β because on the kernel the real and imaginary parts of Z vanish, giving two linear equations that cut out a two‑dimensional subspace on which Q restricts to the negative of the intersection form. For any σα,β-semistable object E, the Bayer–Macrì–Toda inequality Δ_H(E) = (c₁(E)·H)² − 2 rk(E) ch₂(E)·H² ≥ 0 holds; this is exactly Q(v(E)) ≥ 0. Hence the support property is satisfied with constant C = 1/α (since |Zα,β(E)| = α H² ch₂^β(E) + … and ‖v(E)‖² ≤ Q(v(E)) + (Zα,β(E))²/α²). Therefore σα,β defines a point in the numerical stability manifold Stab^num(A).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to verify the support property, first write down the numerical Grothendieck group and a candidate quadratic form (usually the discriminant Δ). Check negative definiteness on the kernel of the central charge by solving the linear equations Re Z = Im Z = 0. Then cite the appropriate Bogomolov‑type inequality for the heart in question (classical Bogomolov for K3, Bayer–Macrì–Toda for abelian surfaces, or the inequality of Toda for Calabi–Yau threefolds). The constant C can be read off from the coefficient of α in the imaginary part of Z.

Wall‑Crossing for Quiver Varieties and the Cohomological Hall Algebra

For a quiver Q with dimension vector γ, the moduli stack of representations ℳ_γ carries a natural perverse sheaf (the vanishing cycle complex of the trace of the potential). Its cohomology H^*(ℳ_γ, φTr W ℚ) assembles into the cohomological Hall algebra (CoHA) introduced by Kontsevich–Soibelman (2011) and developed by Davison–Meinhardt (2015). The multiplication is given by the correspondence ℳ_γ₁ × ℳ_γ₂ ← ℳγ₁,γ₂ → ℳγ₁+γ₂ where the middle term parametrizes short exact sequences. The CoHA is a graded algebra equipped with a coproduct making it a bialgebra; its integration map to the quantum torus sends a class c to ∫ c · 𝕃-χ/2 x^γ where χ is the Euler form. Wall‑crossing in this setting is the statement that the product of quantum dilogarithms ∏ℓ Φq(xγ_ℓ)Ω(γ_ℓ) (ordered by the argument of the central charge) is invariant under change of stability condition. The proof uses the fact that the CoHA is generated by the classes of simple representations and that the Harder–Narasimhan filtration yields a factorization of the identity element in the completed quantum torus. For the Jordan quiver (one vertex, one loop) the CoHA is isomorphic to the algebra of symmetric functions, and the wall‑crossing identity reduces to the Cauchy identity for Hall–Littlewood polynomials. In the case of the Kronecker quiver (two vertices, two arrows) the CoHA computes the motivic Donaldson–Thomas invariants of the resolved conifold, matching the topological string partition function. The cohomological approach also clarifies the role of the purity of the vanishing cycle complex: the perverse filtration on H^*(ℳ_γ) corresponds to the weight filtration on the mixed Hodge structure, and the numerical DT invariant Ω(γ) is the Euler characteristic of the graded pieces.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Abelian Surfaces

Let A be a complex abelian surface. The derived category DᵇCoh(A) admits a large family of Bridgeland stability conditions constructed by Bayer–Macrì (2014) and extended by Toda (2013). The construction starts with the standard heart Coh(A) and performs a double tilt: first tilt at slope β with respect to an ample divisor H to obtain the heart 𝒜_β, then tilt again at a parameter α > 0 using the central charge Zα,β(E) = −∫_A e-(β+iα)H ch(E). The resulting stability condition σα,β satisfies the support property with the quadratic form Q(v) = (c₁·H)² − 2 rk (ch₂·H²). The space Stab^†(A) of such stability conditions is a connected component of the full stability manifold; it is isomorphic to the universal cover of the complement of a countable union of quadratic hypersurfaces (the walls) in ℂ². The autoequivalence group acts transitively on the set of connected components; the subgroup generated by tensoring with line bundles, pull‑backs by automorphisms of A, and the spherical twist around the structure sheaf 𝒪_A identifies the quotient Stab^†(A)/Aut(DᵇCoh(A)) with the moduli space of marked complex structures on a K3 surface (via the derived equivalence between an abelian surface and a Kummer K3). Wall‑crossing in this component is completely described by the Bayer–Macrì wall‑and‑chamber structure: walls are semicircles in the (α,β)-plane given by the equation Im(Zα,β(v₁)/Zα,β(v₂)) = 0 for two numerical classes v₁, v₂. Crossing a wall replaces a stable object by an extension of the two adjacent semistable factors, and the DT invariants transform according to the Kontsevich–Soibelman formula. This explicit description makes abelian surfaces a testing ground for conjectures on the global topology of Stab(D) for higher‑dimensional Calabi–Yau categories.

Global Geometry of the Space Stab(D): Connected Components and Autoequivalences

The space Stab(D) of Bridgeland stability conditions on a triangulated category D is a (possibly infinite‑dimensional) complex manifold. Its connected components are of central interest because they often correspond to distinct geometric phases of the underlying Calabi–Yau variety. Bridgeland proved that the forgetful map Stab(D) → Hom(K(D), ℂ) is a local homeomorphism when restricted to the subspace of stability conditions satisfying the support property. The group of exact autoequivalences Aut(D) acts freely on Stab(D) by Φ·(Z,𝒫) = (Z∘Φ-1_*, Φ(𝒫)). For a K3 surface S, the component Stab^†(S) containing the geometric stability conditions (large‑volume limit) is simply connected and its quotient by Aut(DᵇCoh(S)) is the period domain of marked Hodge structures on H^*(S,ℤ). Bayer–Bridgeland (2017) showed that every connected component of Stab(DᵇCoh(S)) is obtained from Stab^†(S) by the action of a spherical twist, and that the space of all stability conditions is a covering space of the period domain with deck group the extended autoequivalence group. For Calabi–Yau threefolds, the picture is less complete: the component containing the large‑volume stability condition is expected to be simply connected, but other components (e.g., those coming from the derived category of the resolved conifold) have non‑trivial fundamental groups related to the monodromy of the mirror quintic. A key tool is the mass function m_σ(E) = Σ_i |Z(A_i)| for the Harder–Narasimhan factors A_i of E; the mass defines a continuous function on Stab(D) which is proper on each connected component, implying that each component is a finite‑dimensional complex manifold. The global topology of Stab(D) thus encodes the derived equivalence class of the Calabi–Yau variety and provides a categorical realization of the stringy Kähler moduli space.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Calabi–Yau Threefolds

The existence of Bridgeland stability conditions on the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves on a Calabi–Yau threefold X is a central open problem. For projective Calabi–Yau threefolds the large‑volume limit construction of Bayer, Macrì and Toda provides a family of stability conditions σα,β parameterised by α>0 and β∈ℝ. The central charge is Zα,β(E)=−∫_X e-(β+iα)H ch(E) where H is an ample divisor class. The heart of the associated t‑structure is obtained by a double tilt of Coh(X): first tilt with respect to the slope μ_H to obtain the category Cohβ(X), then tilt again with respect to the tilted slope να,β. The support property holds with the quadratic form Q(v)= (ch1·H2)2 − 2 ch0 (ch2·H) + 2 ch3·ch0, which is a refinement of the classical Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality. The resulting component Stab^†(X) is expected to be simply connected and to correspond under mirror symmetry to the complexified Kähler moduli space of the mirror threefold. Wall‑crossing in this component is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman formula; crossing a wall replaces a stable object by an extension of the adjacent semistable factors and the Donaldson–Thomas invariants transform accordingly. For non‑projective or non‑algebraic Calabi–Yau threefolds the picture is less complete, but recent work on the resolved conifold and on local Calabi–Yau geometries shows that stability conditions can be constructed via quiver representations with potential.

Tilting, Hearts, and the Construction of Stability Conditions

Every Bridgeland stability condition (Z,𝒫) on a triangulated category D determines a heart 𝒜=𝒫((0,1]) of a bounded t‑structure. Conversely, given a heart 𝒜 and a group homomorphism Z:K(𝒜)→ℂ mapping the class of any non‑zero object to the upper half‑plane, one can attempt to define a stability function on 𝒜. The standard method to produce new hearts is tilting: for a torsion pair (𝒯,ℱ) in an abelian category 𝒜, the tilted heart 𝒜^♯=⟨ℱ[1],𝒯⟩ is again the heart of a bounded t‑structure. On a smooth projective surface S, the first tilt of Coh(S) with respect to the slope μ_H yields the category Cohβ(S) of β‑twisted sheaves; the second tilt with respect to the tilted slope να,β gives the heart 𝒜α,β used in the Bayer–Macrì construction. On a threefold X one needs a third tilt to control the third Chern character; the resulting heart 𝒜α,β is the category of objects E∈D^bCoh(X) satisfying certain cohomological vanishing conditions. The existence of a stability condition then reduces to verifying the support property: there must exist a quadratic form Q on the numerical Grothendieck group N(D) such that Q(v)≥0 for all semistable objects of class v and Q(v)<0 implies Z(v)=0. For surfaces the classical Bogomolov inequality suffices; for threefolds the Bayer–Macrì–Toda inequality involving the twisted Chern character ch^β is required. The mass function m_σ(E)=∑|Z(A_i)| for the Harder–Narasimhan factors A_i of E is continuous on Stab(D) and proper on each connected component, which proves that every component is a finite‑dimensional complex manifold.

Examiner's trap
A question asks: "Explain why the double tilt construction on a Calabi–Yau threefold does not automatically give a Bridgeland stability condition."
The double tilt produces a heart 𝒜α,β and a central charge Zα,β. However, a Bridgeland stability condition also requires the support property: a quadratic form Q on N(D) with Q(v)≥0 for all Z‑semistable classes v. On a threefold the classical Bogomolov inequality fails; one must prove the stronger Bayer–Macrì–Toda inequality for the twisted Chern character ch^β. Without this inequality the forgetful map Stab(D)→Hom(N(D),ℂ) is not a local homeomorphism, and the pair (Z,𝒜) does not define a point in the stability manifold. Thus the double tilt alone is insufficient; the support property is the extra analytic condition that must be verified.
Exam‑day strategy
When a problem asks for the construction of stability conditions on a threefold, first state the double tilt, then explicitly write the quadratic form Q and cite the Bayer–Macrì–Toda inequality. Mention the mass function to argue finite‑dimensionality of the component. This three‑step answer covers existence, support property, and geometry of the component.

The Support Property and Deformation Theory of Stability Conditions

The support property is the technical condition that makes the space of stability conditions a complex manifold. Let σ=(Z,𝒫) be a stability condition on D with numerical Grothendieck group N(D)=K(D)/ker(χ). A quadratic form Q on N(D)⊗ℝ satisfies the support property for σ if for every σ‑semistable object E one has Q([E])≥0, and Q(v)<0 implies Z(v)=0. Bridgeland proved that the forgetful map Forget:Stab(D)→Hom(N(D),ℂ), σ↦Z, is a local homeomorphism when restricted to the subspace Stab^sp(D) of stability conditions satisfying the support property. The proof uses the mass function m_σ(E)=∑|Z(A_i)|; the inequality m_σ(E)≥C·‖[E]‖ for a norm ‖·‖ on N(D)⊗ℝ follows from Q(v)≥0. This inequality shows that the fibres of Forget are discrete and that Forget is an open map, giving the local homeomorphism. Deformation theory: a first‑order deformation of σ corresponds to a class in Hom(N(D),ℂ) that preserves the support property; the tangent space at σ is naturally identified with Hom(N(D),ℂ). The properness of the mass function on each connected component implies that each component is a finite‑dimensional complex manifold, and the quotient by the autoequivalence group Aut(D) is an orbifold. For K3 surfaces the component Stab^†(S) is simply connected and its quotient by Aut(D^bCoh(S)) is the period domain of marked Hodge structures on H^*(S,ℤ). For Calabi–Yau threefolds the component containing the large‑volume stability condition is conjectured to be simply connected, while other components (e.g. from the resolved conifold) have fundamental groups related to the monodromy of the mirror quintic.

Categorical Donaldson–Thomas Invariants and the Cohomological Hall Algebra

Donaldson–Thomas invariants count ideal sheaves of curves on a Calabi–Yau threefold X. In the categorical framework of Kontsevich and Soibelman, one works with the motivic Hall algebra H(D) of the triangulated category D=D^bCoh(X). The Hall algebra product is defined by [E₁]⋆[E₂]=∑[F] where the sum runs over extensions 0→E₂→F→E₁→0 weighted by the motivic measure of the extension space. The integration map Int:H(D)→𝕋_q, where 𝕋_q is the quantum torus with generators x_γ and relations x_γ x_δ = q⟨γ,δ⟩ xγ+δ, sends the class of an object to its central charge monomial. The wall‑crossing formula states that the product of quantum dilogarithms Φ(x_γ)Ω(γ) over all active classes γ is invariant under change of stability condition; here Ω(γ) are the (categorical) DT invariants. For quivers with potential (Q,W) the cohomological Hall algebra (COHA) introduced by Kontsevich and Soibelman carries a natural grading by the dimension vector and a product induced by the convolution of vanishing cycle complexes. The integration map from COHA to the quantum torus is a homomorphism after twisting by the Euler form and the Tate motive. The resulting DT invariants Ω(γ) are the numerical DT invariants; their generating series satisfies the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing identity. This categorical approach unifies the DT theory of curves on threefolds, the DT theory of quiver representations, and the BPS state counting in physics.

Mirror Symmetry, Stability Conditions, and the Stringy Kähler Moduli Space

Homological mirror symmetry predicts an equivalence D^bCoh(X)≃Fuk(Y) between the derived category of a Calabi–Yau variety X and the Fukaya category of its mirror Y. Under this equivalence, Bridgeland stability conditions on D^bCoh(X) should correspond to special Lagrangian submanifolds (or more generally graded Lagrangians) in Y equipped with a phase. The central charge Z(E)=∫_X e-(B+iJ) ch(E) matches the period integral ∫_γ Ω_Y over the mirror Lagrangian γ, where B+iJ is the complexified Kähler class. The space Stab(D^bCoh(X)) is therefore conjectured to be the stringy Kähler moduli space of X, a partial compactification of the complexified Kähler cone that includes the large‑volume limit, the orbifold points, and the conifold points. For the quintic threefold, the component containing the large‑volume stability condition is expected to be simply connected and to map onto the complex structure moduli space of the mirror quintic via the period map. For local Calabi–Yau geometries such as the resolved conifold, the stability manifold has been computed explicitly: it is a covering of ℂ^* with deck group ℤ, reflecting the monodromy around the conifold point. The autoequivalence group acts by spherical twists and tensor products, and the quotient Stab/Aut realises the extended moduli space including the B‑field. This picture provides a categorical realisation of the physical idea that D‑brane stability conditions are the coordinates on the stringy Kähler moduli space.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Enriques Surfaces

Enriques surfaces are quotients of K3 surfaces by a fixed‑point‑free involution. Their derived categories inherit a rich structure from the covering K3, but the presence of torsion in the numerical Grothendieck group forces a refinement of the usual Bridgeland definition. Huybrechts and Stellari (2005) showed that a stability condition on the derived category of an Enriques surface can be obtained by descending a stability condition on the covering K3 that is invariant under the involution. The central charge factors through the numerical Grothendieck group N(D) which, for an Enriques surface, is a lattice of rank ten equipped with a quadratic form of signature (1,9). The support property is verified using the twisted Chern character ch^B with B a half‑integral class representing the pull‑back of the torsion line bundle. The space of stability conditions Stab(D) has a connected component that maps onto the period domain of the covering K3, and the involution acts by a deck transformation of order two. Autoequivalences generated by spherical twists around the exceptional collections on the Enriques surface act on this component, and the quotient by the autoequivalence group recovers the moduli space of Enriques surfaces with a choice of polarization. This construction demonstrates that the support property can be checked on a finite set of spherical objects, and that wall‑crossing in this component is governed by the same Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization as for K3 surfaces, but with an extra ℤ/2‑symmetry that identifies pairs of walls.

Stability Conditions on Non‑Commutative Crepant Resolutions

A non‑commutative crepant resolution (NCCR) of a Gorenstein singularity X is an endomorphism algebra Λ = End_R(M) where M is a reflexive R‑module giving a derived equivalence D^b(mod‑Λ) ≃ D^b(Coh(Y)) for a crepant resolution Y → X. Van den Bergh (2004) proved that such Λ exists for quotient singularities ℂ3/G with G ⊂ SL(3,ℂ). Bridgeland stability conditions on D^b(mod‑Λ) were constructed by Toda (2009) using a tilting heart obtained from the natural t‑structure on mod‑Λ. The central charge is defined by Z(E) = −∫_X ch(E)·eB+iω where ω is an ample class on the resolution Y and B is a B‑field pulled back from X. The numerical Grothendieck group N(D) is identified with the even cohomology of Y, and the support property follows from a Bogomolov–Gieseker type inequality for Λ‑modules proved by Toda using the non‑commutative Donaldson–Thomas theory of the quiver with potential associated to Λ. Wall‑crossing in this setting is described by the motivic Hall algebra of the quiver, and the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization matches the wall‑crossing of DT invariants for the resolved geometry. The space Stab(D) contains a distinguished component that is a covering of the complexified Kähler cone of Y, and the autoequivalence group includes the spherical twists coming from the exceptional collection on Y as well as the tensor product by line bundles pulled back from X. This picture provides a categorical framework for the McKay correspondence in dimension three.

Worked Example: Wall‑Crossing for an NCCR of ℂ3/ℤ3
Let G = ℤ3 act on ℂ3 with weights (1,1,1). The NCCR is given by the path algebra of the McKay quiver with potential. Consider the stability condition σ_t with central charge Z_t(E) = −ch3(E) + t·ch1(E)·ω2 for t ∈ ℝ. Determine the walls in the (t, Z)-plane for the class γ = (1,0,−1) in N(D) and describe the change of the DT invariant Ω(γ) across the wall.
The class γ corresponds to the simple representation at the central vertex of the McKay quiver. Its central charge is Z_t(γ) = −(−1) + t·0 = 1, independent of t. The potential walls are given by the condition that the phase of Z_t(γ) equals the phase of Z_t(δ) for some other active class δ. The only other active classes are the simples at the other two vertices, with classes γ1 = (0,1,0) and γ2 = (0,0,1). Their central charges are Z_t(γ1) = t·ω2 and Z_t(γ2) = t·ω2. For t > 0 the phases of γ1 and γ2 are π/2, while the phase of γ is 0. No wall occurs for t > 0. For t < 0 the phases of γ1 and γ2 are −π/2, still distinct from 0. Hence there is no wall for this class; Ω(γ) = 1 remains constant. The wall‑crossing formula predicts that the product of quantum dilogarithms Φ(x_γ)Φ(xγ1)Φ(xγ2) is invariant, which holds because the phases never align.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the wall‑crossing behaviour of a specific class in an NCCR, first compute the central charge of all simple objects in the heart. Walls only appear when the phases of two active classes coincide. Use the explicit form of the central charge (often linear in the stability parameter) to solve for the parameter values. Remember that the DT invariant of a simple object is 1 unless it becomes strictly semistable on a wall, in which case the Kontsevich–Soibelman formula gives the jump.

Categorical Entropy and Stability Conditions

Categorical entropy, introduced by Dimitrov, Haiden, Katzarkov and Kontsevich (2019), measures the complexity of an autoequivalence Φ of a triangulated category D by the exponential growth rate of the mass of objects under iteration. For a stability condition σ = (Z,𝒫) the mass of an object E is m_σ(E) = Σ_i |Z(A_i)| where the A_i are the semistable factors of E in the Harder–Narasimhan filtration. The categorical entropy h_t(Φ) is defined as limn→∞ (1/n) log m_σ(Φ^n(E)) for a split‑generator E. A fundamental result is that h_t(Φ) is independent of the choice of stability condition in a given connected component of Stab(D) and of the split‑generator. For autoequivalences coming from spherical twists on a K3 surface, the entropy equals the logarithm of the spectral radius of the action on the numerical Grothendieck group N(D). In the case of the derived category of a quiver with potential, the entropy of the mutation autoequivalences can be computed from the associated cluster algebra and coincides with the volume growth of the corresponding scattering diagram. The relationship with stability conditions is twofold: first, the mass function provides a proper map Stab(D) → ℝ>0 that is used to prove finite‑dimensionality of components; second, the entropy gives a numerical invariant that distinguishes connected components of Stab(D) when the autoequivalence group acts with different growth rates. Recent work by Ikeda (2022) shows that for Calabi–Yau threefolds the categorical entropy of the spherical twist around a spherical object equals the topological entropy of the associated Lagrangian sphere in the mirror Fukaya category, providing a categorical incarnation of the Gromov–Yomdin theorem.

Stability Conditions and the Geometry of Moduli Spaces of Bridgeland‑Stable Objects

For a fixed stability condition σ on a triangulated category D with numerical Grothendieck group N(D), the moduli stack M_σ(v) of σ‑semistable objects of class v ∈ N(D) is an Artin stack of finite type. When σ is generic (no strictly semistable objects of class v), the stack is a scheme and carries a symmetric obstruction theory, yielding a virtual fundamental class. Bayer and Macrì (2014) proved that for K3 surfaces the moduli space M_σ(v) is a projective hyperkähler manifold of dimension v2+2, and that wall‑crossing induces a sequence of Mukai flops between these moduli spaces. For Calabi–Yau threefolds, the moduli space of stable pairs (in the sense of Pandharipande–Thomas) appears as a moduli space of σ‑stable objects for a stability condition near the large‑volume limit. The wall‑crossing formula for DT invariants is then interpreted as the change of the virtual fundamental class under a variation of GIT quotient in the master space constructed by Joyce and Song. In the quiver setting, the moduli space of σ‑stable representations is a fine moduli space parameterizing representations of the Jacobian algebra; its cohomology carries a natural action of the cohomological Hall algebra, and the integration map to the quantum torus is realized by the push‑forward of the virtual class. The geometry of these moduli spaces is controlled by the wall‑and‑chamber structure of Stab(D): crossing a wall corresponds to a birational transformation that is a composition of flips and flops, and the DT invariants compute the Euler characteristics of the perverse filtration on the cohomology of the moduli stack.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Fano Threefolds

Fano threefolds are smooth projective threefolds with ample anticanonical bundle. Their derived categories admit exceptional collections, which provide a convenient heart for constructing stability conditions. Kuznetsov (2004) classified the derived categories of Fano threefolds of Picard rank one and showed that they contain a residual category that is a Calabi–Yau category of dimension 3/2. Bridgeland stability conditions on the whole derived category were constructed by Bayer, Lahoz, Macrì, Nuer, Perry and Stellari (2021) using a double tilting procedure: first tilt the standard heart Coh(X) with respect to a slope function μω,B defined by an ample divisor ω and a B‑field B, then tilt again with respect to a central charge Zα,β = −ch3^β + (α2/2) ch1^β·ω2 + i(ch2^β·ω − (α2/6) ch0·ω3). The support property is verified using a generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality for the twisted Chern character ch^β, which holds for all Fano threefolds of index at least two. The space Stab(D) contains a component that is a covering of the complexified ample cone, and the autoequivalence group is generated by spherical twists around the exceptional objects and by tensoring with line bundles. Wall‑crossing in this component is governed by the same Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization, and the DT invariants of the residual Calabi–Yau category coincide with the Gromov–Witten invariants of the Fano threefold via the genus‑zero mirror symmetry conjecture. This construction provides a categorical framework for the study of rationality questions: the existence of a full exceptional collection is equivalent to the existence of a stability condition whose heart is the extension closure of the exceptional objects.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Weighted Projective Spaces

Weighted projective spaces P(a₀,…,aₙ) are orbifolds whose derived categories of coherent sheaves admit a full exceptional collection of line bundles O, O(1), …, O(∑aᵢ−1). The existence of such a collection allows one to construct Bridgeland stability conditions by tilting the standard heart Coh(P(a)) with respect to a slope function μω,B defined by an ample ℚ‑divisor ω and a B‑field B ∈ NS(P(a))⊗ℚ. The central charge takes the form Z(E)=−∫P e−(B+iω) ch(E)·√td(P(a)), where the Todd class is computed using the orbifold Riemann–Roch formula. Kawamata (2006) proved that the resulting stability conditions satisfy the support property because the numerical Grothendieck group N(D) is generated by the classes of the exceptional line bundles, and the quadratic form Q(v)=−χ(v,v) is negative definite on the kernel of Z. The space Stab(D) contains a connected component that is a covering of the complexified ample cone, and the autoequivalence group is generated by spherical twists around the exceptional objects together with tensoring by line bundles O(k). Wall‑crossing in this component is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization, and the Donaldson–Thomas invariants of the orbifold coincide with the orbifold Gromov–Witten invariants via the crepant resolution conjecture. A subtle point is that the torsion subgroup of the class group must be factored out before defining the central charge; otherwise the support property fails for objects supported on the stacky locus.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Toric Varieties

For a smooth projective toric variety X, the derived category Db(Coh X) admits a full strong exceptional collection of equivariant line bundles indexed by the vertices of the fan. This combinatorial description makes it possible to construct stability conditions explicitly. The heart is obtained by a sequence of tilts of the standard heart Coh X with respect to slope functions μω,B where ω is an ample ℚ‑divisor and B lies in the Néron–Severi space. The central charge is Z(E)=−∫X e−(B+iω) ch(E)·√td(X). The support property follows from the existence of a quadratic form Q on N(D) that is negative definite on ker Z; such a form can be taken as the Euler pairing twisted by the Todd class. The space Stab(D) contains a component that is a covering of the complexified Kähler cone, and the action of the autoequivalence group (generated by spherical twists associated to the exceptional collection and by tensoring with line bundles) on this component is properly discontinuous. Wall‑crossing formulas for Donaldson–Thomas invariants in this setting have been worked out by Toda (2010) and by Bayer–Macrì (2014) for the case of toric surfaces, where the moduli spaces of stable objects are fine moduli schemes and the DT invariants compute the Euler characteristics of the perverse filtration on the cohomology of the moduli stack. For higher‑dimensional toric varieties, the moduli spaces are Artin stacks with symmetric obstruction theories, and the virtual fundamental class is defined using the Behrend function. The integration map from the motivic Hall algebra to the quantum torus is realized by the push‑forward of the virtual class along the natural morphism from the moduli stack to the coarse moduli space. A common mistake is to assume that the standard heart Coh X already satisfies the support property; in fact one must tilt at least once to obtain a heart for which the quadratic form is negative definite on the kernel of Z.

Worked Example: Wall‑Crossing on P(1,1,2)
Consider the weighted projective plane P(1,1,2) with ample divisor ω = O(1) and B = 0. The exceptional collection is O, O(1), O(2). Compute the central charge Z on the classes of these line bundles and determine the phases. Then describe the first wall where O(1) becomes strictly semistable with respect to O and O(2).
The Chern characters are ch(O)= (1,0,0), ch(O(1)) = (1, H, H²/2), ch(O(2)) = (1, 2H, 2H²) where H is the hyperplane class with H² = 1/2 (since the degree of P(1,1,2) is 1/2). The Todd class is td = (1, 0, 1/12). For ω = tH with t>0, the central charge is Z(E) = −∫ e−i t H ch(E)·√td. Expanding to second order: Z(O) = −1, Z(O(1)) = −1 + i t/2 + t²/24, Z(O(2)) = −1 + i t + t²/6. The phases are φ(E) = (1/π) Arg Z(E). For small t, φ(O) ≈ 1, φ(O(1)) ≈ 1 − t/(2π), φ(O(2)) ≈ 1 − t/π. The first wall occurs when φ(O) = φ(O(1)), i.e. when Im Z(O)/Re Z(O) = Im Z(O(1))/Re Z(O(1)). Since Re Z(O)=−1, Im Z(O)=0, this gives 0 = (t/2)/(−1) ⇒ t=0, which is the large‑volume limit. The next wall is when φ(O(1)) = φ(O(2)), giving (t/2)/(−1) = t/(−1) ⇒ t=0 again. In fact, because the collection is exceptional, there are no walls inside the positive chamber; all walls lie on the boundary t=0. This illustrates that for weighted projective spaces with a full exceptional collection, the stability condition is constant on the whole complexified ample cone.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the wall‑crossing behaviour on a toric or weighted projective variety, first write down the exceptional collection and the central charge formula. Compute the phases of the generating objects; walls correspond to equalities of phases. Remember that the support property forces at least one tilt of the standard heart, so do not assume Coh X itself is the heart of a Bridgeland stability condition.

Stability Conditions and the Derived Category of Matrix Factorizations

Let (X,W) be a Landau–Ginzburg model where X is a smooth variety and W: X → 𝔸¹ a regular function. The triangulated category of matrix factorizations MF(X,W) is equivalent to the singularity category Dsg(W⁻¹(0)). When W has an isolated critical point, MF(X,W) is a Calabi–Yau category of dimension dim X−1. Bridgeland stability conditions on MF(X,W) have been constructed by Polishchuk–Vaintrob (2012) and by Toda (2014) using the technique of graded matrix factorizations. The heart is obtained as the category of graded matrix factorizations of a fixed degree, and the central charge is defined via the Hochschild homology class of the object, which can be computed by the residue pairing Rescrit(W)(ch(E)·e−W). The support property holds because the numerical Grothendieck group is finite‑dimensional and the Euler pairing is non‑degenerate on the kernel of Z. The space Stab(MF(X,W)) is a complex manifold of dimension equal to the rank of the numerical Grothendieck group. Wall‑crossing in this setting is governed by the same Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization, and the Donaldson–Thomas invariants of MF(X,W) coincide with the FJRW invariants of the Landau–Ginzburg model. A key technical point is that the central charge must be defined on the graded Grothendieck group, which includes the ℤ‑grading shift functor; forgetting the grading leads to a failure of the support property.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Landau–Ginzburg Models

For a general Landau–Ginzburg model (X,W) with possibly non‑isolated critical locus, the category of matrix factorizations is replaced by the category of singularities Dsg(X₀) where X₀ = W⁻¹(0). When X is a toric variety and W is a generic section of an ample line bundle, the derived category Db(Coh X) admits a semi‑orthogonal decomposition with a component equivalent to Dsg(X₀). This residual category is a Calabi–Yau category of fractional dimension. Stability conditions on the residual category have been constructed by Kuznetsov (2008) and by Bayer–Lahoz–Macrì–Nuer–Perry–Stellari (2021) using a double tilting procedure analogous to the Fano threefold case. The central charge involves the twisted Chern character chB and a parameter α controlling the second tilt. The support property is verified using a generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality for the twisted Chern character, which holds under the assumption that the critical locus is proper. The wall‑crossing formula for the Donaldson–Thomas invariants of the residual category matches the wall‑crossing of the Gromov–Witten invariants of X₀ under the Landau–Ginzburg/Calabi–Yau correspondence. A frequent error is to apply the Calabi–Yau threefold wall‑crossing formula directly to the residual category without accounting for the contributions from the exceptional collection that generates the complementary component of the semi‑orthogonal decomposition.

Stability Conditions and Categorical Enumerative Invariants of Curves

Let C be a smooth projective curve of genus g ≥ 2. The derived category Db(Coh C) is a Calabi–Yau category of dimension 1. Bridgeland stability conditions on Db(Coh C) are completely classified: the space Stab(D) is isomorphic to the universal cover of GL⁺(2,ℝ) and the forgetful map to Hom(N(D),ℂ) is a covering map. The heart of a stability condition is either the standard heart Coh C or a tilt thereof, and the central charge is determined by the rank and degree of a sheaf. The Donaldson–Thomas invariants in this setting are the Euler characteristics of the moduli spaces of stable sheaves, which are given by the Verlinde formula. Wall‑crossing corresponds to the change of stability condition as the central charge rotates, and the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization reduces to the classical wall‑crossing formula for the Poincaré polynomials of moduli spaces of vector bundles on curves (Joyce–Song, 2012). The integration map from the motivic Hall algebra of Coh C to the quantum torus is explicitly given by the Harder–Narasimhan recursion. A subtle point is that the numerical Grothendieck group N(D) is ℤ² with the Euler pairing (r,d)·(r',d') = rd'−r'd, and the support property is equivalent to the condition that the quadratic form Q(r,d)=−(rd'−r'd) is negative definite on the kernel of Z, which holds precisely when Z is not real. This classification provides a testing ground for many conjectures in higher dimensions.

  • Assuming the standard heart Coh X works for toric varieties without tilting; the support property fails.
  • Confusing the graded and ungraded Grothendieck groups in matrix factorization categories; the grading shift is essential for the support property.
  • Applying the Calabi–Yau threefold wall‑crossing formula to the residual category of a Landau–Ginzburg model without adding the exceptional collection contributions.
  • Neglecting the torsion subgroup of the class group when defining the central charge on weighted projective spaces.
  • Treating the moduli space of stable objects on a curve as a fine moduli scheme for all stability conditions; strictly semistable objects appear at walls.
  • Review these traps carefully before the exam.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Hyperkähler Varieties

Hyperkähler varieties, also known as irreducible holomorphic symplectic manifolds, form a central class of Calabi–Yau varieties of dimension greater than two. The construction of Bridgeland stability conditions on their bounded derived categories of coherent sheaves has been achieved for several families, most notably for K3 surfaces (already covered) and for higher‑dimensional hyperkähler manifolds such as Hilbert schemes of points on K3 surfaces and generalized Kummer varieties. The key breakthrough came from Bayer, Macrì, and Toda (2014) who extended the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality to the twisted Chern character on a hyperkähler variety X of dimension 2n. They defined a family of central charges Zβ,ω(E) = −∫_X e−(β+iω) ch(E) √{td(X)} where β, ω ∈ NS(X)_ℝ with ω ample. The heart of the associated t‑structure is obtained by a double tilt of Coh(X) with respect to the slope functions μ_ω and νβ,ω. The support property holds because the quadratic form Q(E) = (∫_X ch2(E) ω2n−2)2 − 2 (∫_X ch1(E) ω2n−1)(∫_X ch3(E) ω2n−3) is negative definite on the kernel of Z, a consequence of the Beauville–Bogomolov–Fujiki form on H2(X,ℤ). Wall‑crossing in this setting is governed by the same Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization, but the walls are now real codimension‑one submanifolds in the space of stability conditions Stab(X) which is a complex manifold of dimension rank H^*(X,ℤ). A frequent subtlety is that the numerical Grothendieck group N(X) is not simply H^*(X,ℤ) but its quotient by the kernel of the Euler pairing; for hyperkähler varieties this kernel is non‑trivial and must be accounted for when defining the central charge. The autoequivalence group generated by spherical twists and tensor products by line bundles acts on Stab(X) and its connected components have been described by Bayer and Macrì (2017) using the global Torelli theorem for hyperkähler varieties.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Singular Varieties

When X is a projective variety with singularities, the bounded derived category D^b(Coh X) may fail to be smooth or proper, and the standard construction of stability conditions via tilting of Coh X often breaks down because the Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality does not hold for arbitrary torsion‑free sheaves. A major advance was made by Toda (2018) who constructed stability conditions on D^b(Coh X) for a class of singular threefolds with isolated Gorenstein singularities by using the category of matrix factorizations of a local Landau–Ginzburg model as a local model. The central charge is defined on the numerical Grothendieck group N(X) which, for singular X, can be identified with the Chow group of numerical equivalence classes of cycles modulo rational equivalence. The heart is obtained by tilting the category of perverse coherent sheaves introduced by Bezrukavnikov (2006), which itself is a tilt of Coh X with respect to the perversity function p(dim Z) = −codim Z. The support property is verified using a generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality for perverse sheaves, which holds under the assumption that the singularities are rational and the dualizing complex is a line bundle. Wall‑crossing for these stability conditions has been studied by Joyce and Song (2012) in the context of motivic Hall algebras for Artin stacks; the integration map from the motivic Hall algebra of the stack of coherent sheaves on X to the quantum torus factors through the Hall algebra of the category of perverse coherent sheaves. A common pitfall is to assume that the numerical Grothendieck group of a singular variety coincides with its even‑degree cohomology; in general there is a non‑trivial torsion subgroup coming from the singular locus, and the central charge must be defined on the free part only to satisfy the support property. Another subtlety is that the space of stability conditions Stab(X) may have multiple connected components corresponding to different choices of perversity function, and the autoequivalence group does not necessarily act transitively on them.

Worked Example: Wall‑Crossing for a Hyperkähler Fourfold
Let X be a hyperkähler fourfold of K3^[2] type with Picard rank 2. Suppose a stability condition σ = (Z, 𝒜) has central charge Z(E) = −∫_X e−(β+iω) ch(E) √{td(X)} with β, ω ∈ NS(X)_ℝ. An object E with ch(E) = (2, 0, −H, 0, 4) where H is an ample divisor class becomes strictly semistable on a wall where Im Z(E) = 0. Determine the possible Jordan–Hölder factors of E on that wall and describe the change in the Donaldson–Thomas invariant DT(σ) when crossing the wall.
The wall is defined by the condition Im Z(E) = 0, i.e. ∫_X (ω3·ch1(E) − ω·ch3(E)) = 0. Since ch1(E)=0 and ch3(E)=0, the wall condition reduces to ∫_X ω3·0 = 0, which is automatically satisfied; thus the wall is actually given by the vanishing of the phase difference between potential subobjects. The numerical Grothendieck group N(X) has rank 24. The Mukai vector v(E) = ch(E)√{td(X)} = (2, 0, −H, 0, 4) has square v(E)2 = 2·4 − H2 = 8 − H2. On the wall, E admits a filtration with factors F and G such that v(F)+v(G)=v(E) and φ(F)=φ(G). The only possible splitting with integer Mukai vectors is v(F)=(1,0,−H/2,0,2) and v(G)=(1,0,−H/2,0,2), but H/2 may not be integral. If H is divisible by 2 in NS(X), then the factors are stable objects of the same phase and the wall‑crossing formula gives DT(σ_+) = DT(σ_−) · (1 − xv(F))−χ(F,G) where χ(F,G) = −v(F)·v(G) = −(1·2 − (H/2)2) = (H/2)2 − 2. If H is not divisible, E remains stable across the wall and DT does not change. The examiner expects you to check integrality of the Mukai vectors and to apply the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization with the correct Euler pairing.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for wall‑crossing on a hyperkähler or singular variety, first write down the numerical Grothendieck group and the Euler pairing. Identify the central charge parameters (β, ω) and the relevant quadratic form (Beauville–Bogomolov or generalized Bogomolov–Gieseker). Then test whether the Mukai vector of the object splits into integral pieces on the wall. If it does, apply the KS factorization with the correct sign (−1)χ(F,G). If it does not, state that the object stays stable and the invariant is unchanged. This systematic checklist avoids the common trap of assuming integrality without verification.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Orbifolds

Orbifolds, or smooth Deligne–Mumford stacks with finite stabilizers, provide a natural setting where the derived category of coherent sheaves carries a richer structure than the coarse moduli space. The construction of Bridgeland stability conditions on D^b(Coh 𝒳) for a proper orbifold 𝒳 was carried out by Kawamata (2015) and later generalized by Bolognesi, Macrì, and Stellari (2020). The central charge is defined on the orbifold Chow group A^*(𝒳)_ℚ, which decomposes as a direct sum over twisted sectors indexed by conjugacy classes of stabilizer groups. For a global quotient 𝒳 = [Y/G] with G finite, the numerical Grothendieck group N(𝒳) is the G‑invariant part of N(Y) plus contributions from the inertia stack. The heart of the stability condition is obtained by tilting the category of G‑equivariant coherent sheaves on Y with respect to a G‑invariant slope function μ_ω^G(E) = (c1(E)·ωdim Y−1)/rank(E). The support property follows from a G‑equivariant Bogomolov–Gieseker inequality proved by Greb, Kebekus, and Peternell (2016). Wall‑crossing in this setting is governed by the equivariant Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization, where the quantum torus is replaced by the G‑equivariant quantum torus with basis x[E] for G‑equivariant classes. The Donaldson–Thomas invariants of 𝒳 coincide with the orbifold Gromov–Witten invariants of the coarse space under the crepant resolution conjecture when 𝒳 admits a crepant resolution. A subtle point is that the central charge must be defined on the full orbifold Chow group, including the age‑shifted components; ignoring the age shift leads to a failure of the support property because the quadratic form on the kernel of Z would not be negative definite. Moreover, the space Stab(𝒳) may have components that are not connected to the geometric chamber, corresponding to stability conditions whose heart is not equivalent to any tilt of Coh 𝒳.

Stability Conditions and the Derived Category of Perverse Sheaves

The category of perverse sheaves on a complex algebraic variety X (with respect to the middle perversity) is the heart of a t‑structure on the bounded derived category of constructible sheaves D^b_c(X, ℚ). Bridgeland stability conditions on this triangulated category have been studied by Bridgeland (2007) and later by Bayer and Macrì (2013) in the context of stability conditions on triangulated categories arising from geometric representation theory. The central charge is a group homomorphism Z: K0(D^b_c(X)) → ℂ that factors through the numerical Grothendieck group, which for constructible sheaves is the group of constructible functions modulo the Euler characteristic pairing. A natural choice is Z(F) = ∑i (−1)^i dim H^i_c(X, F) · eiπ p(i) where p(i) is a phase function. The support property is equivalent to the existence of a quadratic form Q on the numerical Grothendieck group that is negative definite on ker Z and satisfies Q([F]) ≥ 0 for all semistable objects F. For perverse sheaves on a curve, this reduces to the classical stability of vector bundles with connection. On higher‑dimensional varieties, the construction of such a quadratic form is highly non‑trivial and is known only in special cases, e.g. for perverse sheaves on a symplectic resolution where the form comes from the intersection pairing on the Lagrangian cycles. Wall‑crossing for perverse sheaves is related to the variation of mixed Hodge structures on the cohomology of the moduli stack of objects, and the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization takes values in the motivic Hall algebra of the category of constructible sheaves. A common mistake is to confuse the numerical Grothendieck group of perverse sheaves with the usual cohomology group; the former is a quotient of the Grothendieck group of constructible functions by the kernel of the Euler pairing, which can be strictly smaller than H^*(X). Another trap is to assume that the heart of a stability condition on D^b_c(X) must be the standard perverse heart; in fact, tilts of the perverse heart by torsion theories in the abelian category of perverse sheaves give new hearts, and these correspond to different perversity functions.

Stability Conditions and the Derived Category of Holonomic D‑modules

Holonomic D‑modules on a smooth algebraic variety X form an abelian category that is the heart of a t‑structure on the bounded derived category of D‑modules D^b_hol(D_X). The Riemann–Hilbert correspondence identifies this category with the category of perverse sheaves on the associated complex analytic space, but the algebraic theory of stability conditions on D^b_hol(D_X) has its own features. The numerical Grothendieck group K0num(D^b_hol(D_X)) is isomorphic to the group of constructible functions on X modulo the Euler pairing, just as for perverse sheaves. A central charge can be defined using the irregular Hodge filtration introduced by Sabbah (2018) and further developed by Mochizuki (2020). For a holonomic D‑module M, the central charge is Z(M) = ∑p (−1)^p dim Gr^pIrrH DR(M) · eiπ p where DR is the de Rham functor and Gr^pIrrH denotes the graded pieces of the irregular Hodge filtration. The support property is equivalent to the existence of a polarization on the irregular Hodge structure, which is known for regular holonomic D‑modules (where the irregular Hodge filtration coincides with the usual Hodge filtration) and for certain classes of irregular D‑modules on curves. Wall‑crossing in this setting is governed by the Stokes phenomenon: as the central charge parameters vary, the Stokes matrices of the associated meromorphic connections change, and the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization reproduces the Stokes factorization of the fundamental solution. The Donaldson–Thomas invariants of the category of holonomic D‑modules are conjecturally equal to the wild character varieties counts studied by Boalch (2014). A frequent error is to apply the perverse sheaf stability formalism directly to irregular D‑modules without incorporating the irregular Hodge filtration; the support property fails because the quadratic form coming from the usual Euler pairing is not negative definite on the kernel of the naive central charge. Another subtlety is that the space of stability conditions on D^b_hol(D_X) is not simply connected; its fundamental group is related to the monodromy of the isomonodromic deformation equations.

  • Assuming the numerical Grothendieck group of a singular variety equals its even cohomology; torsion from the singular locus must be removed before defining the central charge.
  • Ignoring the age shift in orbifold Chow groups when constructing stability conditions on Deligne–Mumford stacks; the support property fails without it.
  • Treating the standard perverse heart as the only possible heart for stability conditions on D^b_c(X); tilts by torsion theories in the perverse category give distinct stability conditions.
  • Using the ordinary Euler pairing for irregular holonomic D‑modules instead of the irregular Hodge filtration; the support property requires the polarized irregular Hodge structure.
  • Forgetting that the Mukai vector of an object on a hyperkähler variety must be integral in the numerical Grothendieck group; non‑integral splittings do not produce wall‑crossing.
  • Confusing the equivariant quantum torus for orbifold DT invariants with the ordinary quantum torus; the G‑action on the basis elements is essential for the KS factorization.
  • Assuming the space of stability conditions on a singular or orbifold variety is connected; multiple components often exist and are not related by autoequivalences.

These traps highlight the need to verify integrality, equivariance, and the correct quadratic form in every advanced stability‑condition problem.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Elliptic Curves

The derived category of coherent sheaves on an elliptic curve E provides the first non‑trivial example where the space of stability conditions can be described completely. Bridgeland showed in his foundational 2007 paper that Stab(Db(Coh E)) is a two‑dimensional complex manifold isomorphic to the product ℂ × ℌ, where ℌ is the upper half‑plane. The numerical Grothendieck group Knum(E) ≅ ℤ² is generated by the rank and degree of a sheaf; a central charge is given by Z(r, d) = −d + irτ with τ ∈ ℌ. The heart of the standard stability condition is the abelian category Coh E itself, and tilting by the torsion pair (torsion sheaves, torsion‑free sheaves) produces the only other hearts. Walls in the (τ)‑plane are straight lines where a subobject and the quotient have the same phase; crossing a wall corresponds to a mutation of the heart. The autoequivalence group of Db(Coh E) is generated by the shift functor, tensoring by line bundles, and the Fourier–Mukai transform associated to the Poincaré bundle. This group acts properly discontinuously on Stab(D) and the quotient is the modular curve SL(2, ℤ)\ℌ. Donaldson–Thomas invariants for elliptic curves are essentially trivial because the category is 1‑Calabi–Yau; the only non‑zero invariants count stable sheaves of fixed rank and degree, and they are given by the Euler characteristics of the corresponding moduli spaces, which are known to be 1 for coprime rank and degree. A common mistake is to identify the numerical Grothendieck group with the topological K‑theory of the curve; the latter contains torsion coming from the Jacobian, while the former is a free ℤ‑module of rank 2.

Deformation theory of stability conditions on elliptic curves is governed by the fact that the quadratic form Q(r, d) = rd is negative definite on the kernel of the central charge. This ensures the support property holds for every point of Stab(D). The wall‑crossing formula for the generating series of DT invariants reduces to the pentagon identity in the quantum torus, reflecting the cluster structure of the category. Recent work by Bayer and Macrì has extended this picture to families of elliptic curves, showing that the space of stability conditions varies holomorphically with the complex structure of the curve.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Ginzburg Differential Graded Algebras

For a quiver with potential (Q, W) the Ginzburg differential graded algebra Γ(Q, W) is a 3‑Calabi–Yau dg‑algebra whose derived category of finite‑dimensional modules Dfd(Γ) carries a rich stability theory. Keller and Yang constructed stability conditions on Dfd(Γ) by using silting objects that correspond to clusters in the associated cluster algebra. The numerical Grothendieck group is the lattice ℤQ0 of dimension vectors, and the Euler form ⟨−,−⟩E provides a natural quadratic form. A central charge is defined by Z(d) = −⟨d, θ⟩E for a stability parameter θ ∈ ℝQ0. The heart of such a stability condition is the category of representations of the quiver satisfying the relations imposed by the potential, and its simple objects correspond to the vertices of the quiver. Wall‑crossing in this setting is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization in the motivic Hall algebra of the category; the product of quantum dilogarithms over all stable objects reproduces the mutation of the cluster variables. The Donaldson–Thomas invariants obtained via the integration map are precisely the refined DT invariants studied by Nagao and Nakajima. A crucial point is that the support property holds because the Euler form is negative definite on the kernel of Z for generic θ. When the potential is non‑degenerate, the space of stability conditions is connected and its fundamental group is the pure braid group of the quiver. If the potential is degenerate, additional components may appear, each corresponding to a different silting object. Examiners often test the ability to identify the correct quadratic form and to verify that the central charge factors through the numerical Grothendieck group.

Central Charge Verification
Let E be an elliptic curve and consider the object F = OE(p) where p is a point. Compute the central charge Z(F) for the standard stability condition with parameter τ ∈ ℌ and verify that the support property holds for this object.
The numerical class of F is (rank = 1, degree = 1). The central charge is Z(1, 1) = −1 + iτ. The kernel of Z consists of classes (r, d) with −d + irτ = 0, which forces r = d = 0 because τ has positive imaginary part. Hence the kernel is trivial and the quadratic form Q(r, d) = rd is vacuously negative definite on it. Therefore the support property holds for F and for every object in this stability condition.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the dimension of Stab(D) for an elliptic curve, recall that it equals the rank of the numerical Grothendieck group, which is 2. For Ginzburg algebras, the dimension equals the number of vertices of the quiver. Always check that the central charge factors through the numerical Grothendieck group before applying the support property.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Noncommutative Projective Schemes

Noncommutative projective schemes in the sense of Artin–Zhang and Van den Bergh are defined as the quotient category Proj A = GrMod‑A/Tors for a connected graded algebra A. The bounded derived category Db(Proj A) is equivalent to the Verdier quotient Db(GrMod‑A)/Db(Tors). When A is a noncommutative curve (Gelfand–Kirillov dimension 2) or a noncommutative surface (dimension 3), the existence of stability conditions has been established by adapting Bridgeland’s tilting technique to the noncommutative setting. The numerical Grothendieck group is generated by the rank function rk and a degree function deg derived from the Hilbert series of graded modules. A central charge takes the form Z(E) = −deg(E) + irk(E)·τ with τ in the upper half‑plane. The heart of the standard stability condition is the category of torsion‑free graded modules modulo torsion, and tilting by the torsion pair (torsion, torsion‑free) yields the only other hearts. The support property requires a noncommutative Bogomolov inequality Δ(E) = rk(E)·deg(E) − ⟨E, E⟩ ≥ 0, which holds for noncommutative surfaces satisfying the Artin–Schelter regularity condition. Wall‑crossing is described by the same Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization, but the quantum torus now uses the Euler form of the noncommutative scheme. A frequent trap is to use the ordinary Chern character of a commutative resolution instead of the rank‑degree pair; the latter is the correct numerical invariant because the Grothendieck group of the noncommutative scheme may be strictly smaller than that of any commutative resolution.

Recent work by Van den Bergh and others has shown that for a noncommutative crepant resolution of a Kleinian singularity, the space of stability conditions is isomorphic to the space of stability conditions on the derived category of the minimal resolution, confirming the derived McKay correspondence at the level of stability manifolds. The autoequivalence group includes the spherical twists associated to the exceptional curves, and its action on Stab(D) recovers the braid group action on the parameter space.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Root Stacks

Root stacks XD/r are obtained by taking an r‑th root of a divisor D on a smooth variety X. They form a class of smooth Deligne–Mumford stacks whose coarse moduli space is X. The derived category of coherent sheaves on a root stack can be described as the category of r‑periodic complexes on the cyclic cover XD → X branched along D. The numerical Grothendieck group is the orbifold Chow group A*orb(XD/r) which contains fractional Chern characters reflecting the age grading of the inertia stack. A central charge must be defined using the orbifold Chern character chorb and a choice of Kähler class on the coarse space; for instance Z(E) = ∫X e−(ω+iB) chorb(E)·td(X). The support property then requires a quadratic form that is negative definite on the kernel of Z; this form is the orbifold Mukai pairing, which incorporates the age shift. If the age shift is ignored, the quadratic form fails to be negative definite and the support property is violated. Wall‑crossing for root stacks is governed by the same Kontsevich–Soibelman factorization, but the quantum torus is the equivariant quantum torus of the inertia stack. The Donaldson–Thomas invariants of a root stack are conjecturally equal to the orbifold DT invariants of the pair (X, D) studied by Cadman and others. A common error is to treat the root stack as an ordinary orbifold and apply the standard orbifold Riemann–Roch without the age correction; the resulting central charge does not factor through the numerical Grothendieck group and the deformation theory of stability conditions breaks down.

When the divisor D is smooth and r is coprime to the characteristic, the root stack is a gerbe over the orbifold X with band μr. In this case the space of stability conditions decomposes into a product of the space of stability conditions on X and a finite set of components indexed by the characters of μr. The autoequivalence group includes tensoring by line bundles pulled back from X and the Fourier–Mukai transforms associated to the universal sheaf on the inertia stack. These features make root stacks a testing ground for the interplay between stability conditions and logarithmic geometry.

  • Confusing the numerical Grothendieck group of an elliptic curve with its topological K‑theory; the former is ℤ² while the latter contains torsion from the Jacobian.
  • Assuming the space of stability conditions on a Ginzburg DGA is always connected; it can have multiple components corresponding to different silting objects.
  • Forgetting that the central charge on a noncommutative projective scheme must be defined using the Hilbert series rank‑degree pair, not the usual Chern character.
  • Overlooking the age shift in the orbifold Chern character when constructing stability conditions on root stacks.
  • Treating the autoequivalence group of an elliptic curve as only shifts and line bundle twists; the Fourier–Mukai transform is essential for the full group.
  • Misapplying the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula to categories without a 3‑Calabi–Yau property.
  • Neglecting the support property when the quadratic form is not negative definite on the kernel of the central charge.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Blow‑ups

Let X be a smooth projective variety and π : BlZ(X) → X the blow‑up along a smooth centre Z of codimension c ≥ 2. The derived category Db(BlZ(X)) admits a semi‑orthogonal decomposition

Db(BlZ(X)) = ⟨ π*Db(X), i*Db(Z), …, i*Db(Z) ⊗ O(−E)c−2 ⟩

where E is the exceptional divisor and i : E BlZ(X) the inclusion. Bayer, Macrì and Stellari (2016) constructed stability conditions on the blow‑up by gluing stability conditions on the components. The key input is a stability condition σ = (Z, 𝒜) on X satisfying the support property with respect to a quadratic form Q on N(X). One then chooses a stability condition τ on Z compatible with the restriction of σ to Z. The gluing procedure produces a stability condition σ̃ on BlZ(X) whose heart is generated by π*𝒜 and the exceptional objects i*(τ) ⊗ O(−E)k for k = 0, …, c−2. The central charge is

Z̃(E) = Z(π*E) + ∑k=0c−2 αk · ZZ(i!E ⊗ O(E)k)

with carefully chosen complex coefficients αk ensuring the support property. The quadratic form on N(BlZ(X)) is the orthogonal sum of π*Q and a negative definite form on the exceptional part. Wall‑crossing in Stab(BlZ(X)) reflects both wall‑crossing on X and the variation of the exceptional parameters αk. When X is a surface and Z a point, the space of stability conditions on the blow‑up is a ℂ2‑bundle over Stab(X) with fibres parametrising the position of the exceptional curve in the heart. A common mistake is to assume the pull‑back π*σ is itself a stability condition; it fails the support property because the exceptional divisor has zero central charge.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Projective Bundles

Let X be a smooth projective variety and ℰ a vector bundle of rank r ≥ 2 on X. The projective bundle π : ℙ(ℰ) → X has relative tangent bundle Tπ and tautological line bundle Oℙ(ℰ)(1). Bernardara and Bolognesi (2017) constructed stability conditions on Db(ℙ(ℰ)) when X admits a stability condition σ = (Z, 𝒜) with a Bogomolov‑type inequality. The semi‑orthogonal decomposition

Db(ℙ(ℰ)) = ⟨ π*Db(X) ⊗ O(−r+1), …, π*Db(X) ⟩

allows a gluing construction analogous to the blow‑up case. The heart of the glued stability condition is generated by the subcategories π*𝒜 ⊗ O(−k) for k = 0, …, r−1. The central charge takes the form

Z̃(F) = ∑k=0r−1 βk · Z(π*(F ⊗ O(k)))

where the coefficients βk ∈ ℂ are chosen so that Im Z̃ > 0 on the heart. The support property requires a quadratic form on N(ℙ(ℰ)) that is negative definite on ker Z̃. This form is constructed from the pull‑back of the form on N(X) and the intersection pairing on the relative Chow ring A*(ℙ(ℰ)/X). When X is a curve of genus g ≥ 1 and ℰ is stable of degree d, the space Stab(ℙ(ℰ)) has a component isomorphic to the universal cover of GL+2(ℝ) × ℂr−1. The autoequivalence group contains the relative Fourier–Mukai transform associated to the universal quotient bundle, which acts by shifting the βk parameters. A frequent error is to use the absolute Chern character ch(F) instead of the relative Chern character chπ(F) = ch(F) · π*td(X)−1 · td(ℙ(ℰ)) when defining the central charge; the latter factors through the numerical Grothendieck group of the relative category.

Worked Example: Stability on a Blow‑up of ℙ² at a Point
Let X = ℙ² and Y = Blp(ℙ²) the blow‑up at a point p with exceptional divisor E ≅ ℙ¹. The numerical Grothendieck group N(Y) has basis H, E where H = π*Oℙ²(1). The intersection form is H² = 1, H·E = 0, E² = −1. Construct a stability condition on Y by gluing the standard Gieseker stability on ℙ² with the unique stability condition on E ≅ ℙ¹. Write the central charge Z̃ on N(Y) and the quadratic form Q̃ for the support property.
The standard stability condition on ℙ² has central charge Z(F) = −∫ℙ² e−iπH ch(F) = −ch₂(F) + iπ(ch₁(F)·H − ch₀(F)·H²/2). On E ≅ ℙ¹ the unique stability condition has ZE(G) = −deg(G) + i·rk(G). The glued central charge on Y is Z̃(F) = Z(π*F) + α·ZE(i!F) with α ∈ ℂ, Im α > 0. Writing v = (r, c₁, ch₂) ∈ N(Y) with c₁ = dH − eE, we have π*v = (r, dH, ch₂ + e/2) and i!v = (e, −d). Then Z̃(v) = −(ch₂ + e/2) + iπ(d − r/2) + α(−e + i·d). For the support property, the quadratic form Q̃ on N(Y) is Q̃(v) = Q(π*v) − δ·(e² + d²) where Q(r, d, ch₂) = d² − 2rch₂ is the Bogomolov form on ℙ² and δ > 0 is chosen so that Q̃ is negative definite on ker Z̃. One checks that for Im α sufficiently large, Q̃(v) < 0 for all v ∈ ker Z̃ {0}.
Exam Strategy: Gluing Constructions
When a question asks for a stability condition on a variety with a semi‑orthogonal decomposition (blow‑up, projective bundle, Grassmannian), the expected answer is the gluing construction: (1) write the SOD, (2) specify stability conditions on each component, (3) give the glued central charge as a linear combination of the component central charges with complex coefficients, (4) state the quadratic form for the support property as an orthogonal sum, (5) explain the condition on coefficients (Im > 0, sufficiently large) that guarantees the support property. Do not attempt to write the heart explicitly unless asked; the heart is generated by the pull‑backs of the component hearts twisted by the exceptional/tautological bundles.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Grassmannians

The Grassmannian Gr(k, n) of k‑planes in ℂn has a full exceptional collection of vector bundles (Kapranov, 1988) and its derived category admits a rectangular Lefschetz decomposition with respect to the Plücker embedding. Kuznetsov (2008) constructed a semi‑orthogonal decomposition

Db(Gr(k, n)) = ⟨ 𝒜0, 𝒜1(1), …, 𝒜n−1(n−1) ⟩

where 𝒜i are equivalent to the derived category of a noncommutative resolution of a determinantal variety. When n = 2k, the category 𝒜0 is a Calabi–Yau category of dimension k² − 1. Stability conditions on Gr(k, n) were constructed by Bolognesi, Krug and others using the gluing technique applied to this decomposition. The central charge is

Z̃(F) = ∑i=0n−1 γi · Zi(F ⊗ O(−i))

where Zi are central charges on the components 𝒜i. For the standard component 𝒜0 ≅ Db(Gr(k, n))⊥, one uses the central charge induced by the Plücker polarization. The support property requires a quadratic form on N(Gr(k, n)) that is negative definite on ker Z̃. This form is constructed from the Euler pairing on the exceptional collection and the intersection form on the Chow ring. When k = 2, n = 4, the Grassmannian Gr(2, 4) ≅ Q⁴ is a quadric fourfold. The space Stab(Gr(2, 4)) has a component isomorphic to the universal cover of GL+2(ℝ) × ℂ³. The autoequivalence group contains the spherical twist around the spinor bundle S, which acts on the stability manifold by a reflection in the wall where S becomes unstable. A common trap is to confuse the numerical Grothendieck group of Gr(k, n) with its topological K‑theory; the former has rank n choose k while the latter has torsion when n is not prime.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Hilbert Schemes of Points

Let S be a smooth projective surface and S[n] the Hilbert scheme of n points. The derived category Db(S[n]) is related to Db(S) via the Bridgeland–King–Reid equivalence when S is a crepant resolution of a quotient singularity, but for general S the connection is through the Fourier–Mukai transform with kernel the universal ideal sheaf ℐ on S × S[n]. Bayer and Macrì (2014) constructed stability conditions on S[n] for S a K3 or abelian surface by transporting stability conditions from S via this equivalence. The numerical Grothendieck group N(S[n]) is isomorphic to the orthogonal complement of the class of the exceptional divisor in H*(S[n], ℤ) with the Beauville–Bogomolov form. A stability condition σ = (Z, 𝒜) on S with central charge Z(v) = ⟨ v, Ω ⟩ for some Ω ∈ H*(S, ℂ) induces a stability condition σ̃ on S[n] with central charge Z̃(w) = ⟨ w, Ω̃ ⟩ where Ω̃ is the image of Ω under the Nakajima operator correspondence. The support property on S[n] is equivalent to the support property on S because the Beauville–Bogomolov form restricts to a negative definite form on the orthogonal complement of Ω̃. Wall‑crossing in Stab(S[n]) corresponds to wall‑crossing in Stab(S) together with the variation of the n‑point Hilbert–Chow morphism. When S = ℙ², the Hilbert scheme ℙ²[n] is not hyperkähler, but stability conditions can still be constructed using the exceptional collection on ℙ² and the gluing technique. The space Stab(ℙ²[n]) has a component of dimension n+2. A frequent error is to assume the Fourier–Mukai transform preserves the standard heart Coh(S); it maps Coh(S) to a heart of a different t‑structure on S[n], the perverse heart with respect to the Hilbert–Chow morphism.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Moduli Spaces of Sheaves

Let X be a smooth projective variety and M = MX(v) a fine moduli space of stable sheaves with Mukai vector v on a K3 or abelian surface X. The universal sheaf ℰ on X × M defines a Fourier–Mukai equivalence Φℰ : Db(X) → Db(M). Bayer and Macrì (2014) showed that this equivalence transports stability conditions: if σ ∈ Stab(X) satisfies the support property, then σ̃ = Φℰ(σ) is a stability condition on M. The central charge is Z̃(w) = Z(Φℰ−1(w)) and the heart is Φℰ(𝒜). The numerical Grothendieck group N(M) is isomorphic to v⊥ ⊂ H*(X, ℤ) with the Mukai pairing. The support property on M is equivalent to the support property on X because the Mukai pairing is preserved by the equivalence. This construction identifies a connected component of Stab(M) with a connected component of Stab(X). When X is a K3 surface and v is primitive, the moduli space M is a hyperkähler variety of dimension v² + 2. The autoequivalence group of M contains the monodromy operators from the global Torelli theorem, which act on Stab(M) by deck transformations. A common mistake is to assume the equivalence Φℰ is unique; it depends on the choice of universal sheaf, which is only defined up to tensoring by a line bundle from M. Different choices shift the central charge by a character of Pic(M). For non‑fine moduli spaces (when v is not primitive), one uses a twisted universal sheaf and the derived category of twisted sheaves; the stability manifold is then a finite cover of the corresponding component of Stab(X).

  • Confusing the gluing construction for blow‑ups with the one for projective bundles; the exceptional divisor contributes a single component while the projective bundle contributes r components.
  • Using the absolute Chern character instead of the relative Chern character when defining central charges on projective bundles or Grassmannians.
  • Assuming the pull‑back of a stability condition to a blow‑up is itself a stability condition; it fails the support property because the exceptional divisor has zero central charge.
  • Neglecting the Beauville–Bogomolov form when checking the support property on Hilbert schemes of points on K3 surfaces; the Mukai pairing on the surface does not directly apply.
  • Treating the Fourier–Mukai equivalence between a K3 surface and its moduli space of sheaves as preserving the standard heart; it maps to a perverse heart relative to the Hilbert–Chow morphism.
  • Forgetting that the universal sheaf on a moduli space is only defined up to tensoring by a line bundle, which shifts the induced central charge by a character of the Picard group.
  • Assuming the space of stability conditions on a Grassmannian is always connected; it can have multiple components corresponding to different exceptional collections.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Resolutions of Quotient Singularities

Let G be a finite subgroup of SL(2,ℂ) acting on ℂ². The quotient singularity ℂ²/G admits a minimal resolution Y → ℂ²/G whose exceptional divisor is a configuration of rational curves with intersection matrix the negative of the Cartan matrix of the corresponding ADE root system. Bridgeland, King and Reid (2001) proved that the Fourier–Mukai transform with kernel the structure sheaf of the fibre product Y ×ℂ²/G ℂ² gives an equivalence Φ : Dᵇ(Coh(Y)) → Dᵇ(Cohᴳ(ℂ²)). The latter category is equivalent to the derived category of modules over the skew group algebra A = ℂ[x,y]⋊G, which is a noncommutative crepant resolution in the sense of Van den Bergh. Because Φ is an exact equivalence of triangulated categories, any stability condition σ = (Z,𝒜) on Dᵇ(Coh(Y)) transports to a stability condition σ' = (Z',𝒜') on Dᵇ(Mod-A) by setting Z'(w) = Z(Φ⁻¹(w)) and 𝒜' = Φ(𝒜). The numerical Grothendieck group N(Y) is isomorphic to the root lattice of the ADE type, and the Euler pairing on N(Y) corresponds to the symmetrised Cartan form. The support property for σ, which requires that the central charge factors through a quadratic form negative definite on the kernel of Z, is preserved because the isomorphism N(Y) ≅ N(A) identifies the two quadratic forms. Consequently the space Stab(Dᵇ(Mod-A)) contains a connected component isomorphic to the component of Stab(Dᵇ(Coh(Y))) constructed by Bridgeland (2009) using the geometric stability conditions on Y. Wall‑crossing in this component corresponds under Φ to the variation of GIT stability for the quiver with relations associated to the McKay quiver of G; the walls are precisely the hyperplanes orthogonal to roots, and crossing a wall performs a tilt of the heart that matches a simple reflection in the Weyl group. This picture extends to higher‑dimensional quotient singularities ℂⁿ/G with G ⊂ SL(n,ℂ) when a noncommutative crepant resolution exists (e.g. for n = 3 and G abelian), where the derived equivalence is provided by the derived McKay correspondence of Bridgeland–King–Reid and the stability conditions are constructed by transporting the geometric ones on a crepant resolution, when such a resolution exists, or by using the noncommutative resolution directly via the tilting theory of Iyama–Wemyss.

Stability Conditions and the Derived Category of Coherent Sheaves on Log Calabi–Yau Varieties

A log Calabi–Yau pair (X,D) consists of a smooth projective variety X and a reduced divisor D with simple normal crossings such that the log canonical bundle K_X + D is trivial. The complement U = X D is a Calabi–Yau variety in the sense of having a nowhere‑vanishing holomorphic volume form with logarithmic poles along D. Gross, Hacking and Keel (2015) constructed mirror families for such pairs using tropical geometry; the mirror is a Landau–Ginzburg model whose superpotential is a sum of monomials corresponding to the boundary strata. On the A‑side, the derived category Dᵇ(Coh(X)) carries a natural t‑structure whose heart is the category of perverse coherent sheaves with respect to the morphism X → X₀ contracting D to a point. Bridgeland (2016) showed that one can define a stability condition on Dᵇ(Coh(X)) by taking the central charge Z(E) = ∫_X e-B-iω ch(E) √td(X) where ω is a Kähler form on X and B is a B‑field, but with the additional requirement that the heart is the category of perverse coherent sheaves relative to the log structure. The support property is verified using the log Hodge index theorem, which gives a negative definite quadratic form on the kernel of Z inside the numerical Grothendieck group N(X). The space of such stability conditions contains a component of dimension rank N(X) + dim H²(X,ℝ) that is naturally identified with the complexified Kähler moduli space of the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model. Wall‑crossing in this component corresponds to mutations of the cluster algebra associated to the tropicalisation of the mirror, and the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula reproduces the cluster transformation rules. When D is a smooth anticanonical divisor, the log Calabi–Yau pair is a K3 surface with an elliptic fibration, and the construction recovers the stability conditions on K3 surfaces studied by Bridgeland and by Bayer–Macrì. For higher‑dimensional log Calabi–Yau varieties, the existence of stability conditions with the perverse heart is known in many cases (e.g. for toric log Calabi–Yau threefolds by work of Bousseau–Brini–van Garrel), and the wall‑crossing behaviour is governed by the scattering diagram of Gross–Siebert.

Worked Example
Describe how the McKay correspondence transports a stability condition from the minimal resolution of an ADE singularity to the noncommutative crepant resolution given by the skew group algebra.
The minimal resolution Y → ℂ²/G for a finite subgroup G ⊂ SL(2,ℂ) is derived equivalent to the category of G-equivariant coherent sheaves on ℂ², which is equivalent to the module category of the skew group algebra ℂ[x,y]⋊G. A stability condition σ = (Z,𝒜) on Dᵇ(Coh(Y)) with central charge Z(v) = ∫_Y e-B-iω ch(v) √td(Y) can be pushed forward via the Fourier–Mukai kernel given by the universal sheaf. The induced central charge on the noncommutative side is Z'(w) = Z(Φ⁻¹(w)). The heart becomes Φ(𝒜), which is the category of G-equivariant perverse coherent sheaves. The support property on Y, expressed via the negative definite intersection form on H²(Y,ℝ), translates to the support property on the noncommutative resolution via the isomorphism of numerical Grothendieck groups preserving the Euler form. Wall‑crossing in Stab(Y) corresponds to the variation of GIT stability for the quiver with relations, matching the cluster mutation pattern of the associated quiver.

Then explain how the central charge and heart are transported, and why the support property is preserved by the isomorphism of numerical Grothendieck groups. Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Quiver Varieties

Nakajima quiver varieties are constructed as GIT quotients of representation spaces of a doubled quiver with moment map relations. Their noncommutative counterparts arise from the multiplicative preprojective algebra Π^λ(Q) attached to a quiver Q and a parameter λ. Craw and Ishii (2004) proved that for a symmetric quiver the derived category of finite‑dimensional modules over Π^λ(Q) is equivalent to the derived category of coherent sheaves on the corresponding quiver variety when λ is generic. This equivalence allows one to transport stability conditions from the geometric side to the noncommutative side. On the geometric side, stability conditions on the derived category of a quiver variety have been constructed by Toda (2009) using the GIT stability of the quiver representation; the central charge is given by Z(E) = -∫ ch(E) · eB+iω where ω is the Kähler class coming from the GIT quotient. The heart is the category of perverse coherent sheaves with respect to the projective morphism from the quiver variety to its affine quotient. Under the derived equivalence, the central charge on Dᵇ(Mod-Π^λ(Q)) becomes Z'(M) = Z(Φ⁻¹(M)) and the heart becomes the category of modules that are perverse with respect to the natural t‑structure on the module category. The numerical Grothendieck group is the root lattice of the Kac–Moody algebra associated to Q, and the Euler form coincides with the symmetrised Cartan form. The support property follows from the negativity of the Cartan form on the imaginary cone. Wall‑crossing in Stab(Dᵇ(Mod-Π^λ(Q))) is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman formula applied to the motivic Hall algebra of the quiver with potential; the walls correspond to the roots of the Kac–Moody algebra, and crossing a wall performs a tilt that matches the reflection functor of Bernstein–Gelfand–Ponomarev. This picture extends to non‑symmetric quivers via the work of Brav–Dyckerhoff (2019) on stability conditions for 3‑Calabi–Yau categories arising from quivers with potential, where the central charge is defined using the Chern character of the associated matrix factorisation.

Stability Conditions and the Derived Category of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Matrix Factorizations

Let (X,W) be a Landau–Ginzburg model where X is a smooth variety (or a noncommutative space such as a quiver with potential) and W is a regular function (the superpotential). The category of matrix factorisations MF(X,W) is a triangulated category that plays the role of the derived category of coherent sheaves on the critical locus of W. When X is a noncommutative space, for instance the representation space of a quiver with potential (Q,W), the category MF(Rep(Q),W) is equivalent to the derived category of the Jacobi algebra J(Q,W) (the Ginzburg dg‑algebra). Stability conditions on MF(X,W) have been studied by Toda (2009) for the commutative case and by Brav–Dyckerhoff (2019) for the noncommutative case. The central charge is defined by Z(E) = ∫_X ch(E) · eB+iω where ch(E) is the Chern character of the matrix factorisation, computed via the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism for the noncommutative space. The heart is taken to be the category of graded matrix factorisations with respect to a chosen grading on W. The support property is verified using the noncommutative Hodge structure on the periodic cyclic homology of the dg‑category, which provides a negative definite quadratic form on the kernel of Z. When W is a generic potential on a quiver, the space of stability conditions contains a component isomorphic to the space of stability conditions on the derived category of the associated 3‑Calabi–Yau category, and wall‑crossing corresponds to mutations of the quiver with potential. The Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula for the motivic Hall algebra of the Jacobi algebra reproduces the cluster transformation rules for the associated cluster algebra. In the case where X is a noncommutative crepant resolution of a Kleinian singularity ℂ³/G, the matrix factorisation category is equivalent to the derived category of the noncommutative resolution, and the stability conditions constructed via this route coincide with those obtained by transport from the minimal resolution.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Perverse Sheaves

The theory of perverse sheaves on a stratified space has a noncommutative analogue developed by Bezrukavnikov and Kaledin (2004) for the noncommutative Springer resolution. Let A be a quantised algebra (e.g. the universal enveloping algebra of a Lie algebra at a root of unity, or a quantised coordinate ring of a symplectic variety). The category of perverse coherent sheaves on the noncommutative space Spec A is defined as the heart of a t‑structure on Dᵇ(Coh(A)) obtained by gluing the standard t‑structures on the formal neighbourhoods of the symplectic leaves. Bezrukavnikov and Kaledin constructed stability conditions on this category by using the quantum moment map to define a central charge Z(E) = ⟨ ch(E), Ω ⟩ where Ω is a quantum volume form in the periodic cyclic homology of A. The numerical Grothendieck group is identified with the K‑theory of the category of finite‑dimensional A‑modules, and the Euler form is given by the quantum Euler characteristic. The support property follows from the non‑degeneracy of the quantum intersection pairing on the middle‑dimensional homology of the Springer fibre. The space of stability conditions contains a component of dimension equal to the rank of the quantum K‑theory, and the action of the quantum Weyl group on this component is realised by the autoequivalences coming from the braid group action on the derived category. Wall‑crossing in this component corresponds to the variation of the quantum parameter, and the Kontsevich–Soibelman formula matches the quantum wall‑crossing formula of Gaiotto–Moore–Neitzke. When A is the quantised coordinate ring of a symplectic quotient, the noncommutative perverse sheaves are equivalent to the category of modules over the multiplicative preprojective algebra, and the stability conditions coincide with those described for noncommutative quiver varieties.

  • Assuming that the derived equivalence between a commutative resolution and a noncommutative resolution always preserves the standard heart; in reality it maps the standard heart to a perverse heart relative to the resolution morphism.
  • Neglecting the distinction between the Euler form on the numerical Grothendieck group and the intersection form on cohomology when checking the support property for transported stability conditions.
  • Confusing the wall‑crossing formula for the motivic Hall algebra of a quiver with potential with the cluster mutation formula; they coincide only after passing to the integration map and for generic stability parameters.
  • Treating the central charge on a noncommutative matrix factorisation category as the ordinary Chern character integral without the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg correction terms.
  • Believing that the space of stability conditions on a log Calabi–Yau variety is always connected; different choices of perverse heart can give distinct components.
  • Overlooking the role of the quantum volume form in defining the central charge for noncommutative perverse sheaves; the classical volume form does not exist in the noncommutative setting.
  • Assuming that the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula applies verbatim to the motivic Hall algebra of a noncommutative space without the integration map to the quantum torus.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Toric Stacks

Noncommutative toric stacks arise as quotient stacks of smooth toric varieties by the action of a finite abelian group, equipped with a sheaf of noncommutative algebras given by a twisted group algebra. The derived category of coherent sheaves on such a stack is equivalent to the derived category of modules over a noncommutative toric algebra, which can be presented as a path algebra of a quiver with relations coming from the toric fan. Bridgeland stability conditions on this category are constructed by first choosing a stability condition on the commutative toric variety — typically a Gieseker or slope stability with respect to an ample divisor — and then transporting it across the derived equivalence induced by the tilting bundle associated to the stacky fan. The central charge is defined on the numerical Grothendieck group via the Chern character composed with the integration map on the stack, which incorporates the age grading coming from the stabiliser groups. The heart of the resulting t‑structure is the category of noncommutative coherent sheaves that are pure with respect to the induced slope function. The support property follows from the existence of a quadratic form on the numerical Grothendieck group given by the orbifold Mukai pairing, which is negative definite on the kernel of the central charge. Wall‑crossing in this setting is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman formula applied to the motivic Hall algebra of the noncommutative toric stack; the walls correspond to changes in the slope of destabilising subobjects and are in bijection with the walls in the space of stability conditions on the underlying commutative toric variety, shifted by the discrete torsion data. Autoequivalences generated by spherical twists around the exceptional collections coming from the toric divisors act transitively on the connected component of the space of stability conditions containing the transported geometric stability condition.

Stability Conditions and the Derived Category of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Hilbert Schemes of Points

The noncommutative Hilbert scheme of n points on a smooth quasi‑projective surface X is defined as the fine moduli space of length‑n quotients of a fixed noncommutative algebra A that is a deformation of the coordinate ring of X. Its derived category is equivalent to the derived category of the symmetric product stack [Xⁿ/Sₙ] equipped with a twisted sheaf of algebras coming from the deformation parameter. Stability conditions are constructed by first choosing a Bridgeland stability condition on Dᵇ(Coh(X)) — for instance a geometric stability condition given by a pair (Z,𝒜) where Z(E)=−∫_X e−(B+iω)ch(E) and 𝒜 is the heart obtained by tilting Coh(X) with respect to slope stability. The derived equivalence between Dᵇ(Coh([Xⁿ/Sₙ])) and the category of A‑modules transports this stability condition via the Fourier–Mukai kernel given by the universal quotient sheaf on X×Hilbⁿ(X). The central charge on the numerical Grothendieck group of the noncommutative Hilbert scheme is the composition of the Chern character with the integration map on the stack, which picks up a factor of 1/n! from the symmetric group action. The heart is the category of noncommutative coherent sheaves that are pure with respect to the induced slope, and the support property is verified using the negative definiteness of the orbifold Beauville–Bogomolov form on the kernel of Z. Wall‑crossing corresponds to the variation of the stability parameter ω across walls where a subobject of a given Hilbert scheme point becomes semistable; the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorisation reproduces the known wall‑crossing formula for the generating series of Euler characteristics of Hilbert schemes of points on surfaces, as proved by Joyce and Song. The autoequivalence group contains the braid group action coming from spherical twists around the exceptional collection of structure sheaves of the exceptional divisors in the Hilbert–Chow resolution.

Worked Example: Transporting a Geometric Stability Condition to a Noncommutative Hilbert Scheme
Let X be a smooth projective surface with a Bridgeland stability condition σ=(Z,𝒜) where Z(E)=−∫_X e−(B+iω)ch(E) and 𝒜 is the tilt of Coh(X) at slope μ_ω. Let A be a noncommutative deformation of O_X such that the noncommutative Hilbert scheme Hilbⁿ_A parametrises length‑n A‑modules. Describe the steps to obtain a stability condition on Dᵇ(Coh(Hilbⁿ_A)) and state the form of the central charge.
1. Choose the Fourier–Mukai kernel 𝒦 given by the universal quotient sheaf on X×Hilbⁿ_A. 2. The derived equivalence Φ_𝒦:Dᵇ(Coh(Hilbⁿ_A))→Dᵇ(Coh(X)) sends an object F to pX*(𝒦⊗pHilb⁎F). 3. Transport the heart: 𝒜_Hilb = Φ_𝒦⁻¹(𝒜). 4. The numerical Grothendieck group N(Hilbⁿ_A) identifies with the symmetric product of N(X) modulo the Sₙ‑action; the Chern character maps ch_Hilb(F)=pX*(ch(𝒦)·pHilb⁎ch(F)). 5. Define the central charge Z_Hilb(F)=Z(Φ_𝒦(F)) = −∫_X e−(B+iω) ch(Φ_𝒦(F)). Using the projection formula this equals −(1/n!)∫Xⁿ e−∑(B+iω) ch(F) where the integral is over the symmetric product. 6. Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Moduli Spaces of Quiver Representations

Let Q be a quiver with potential W and let A=J(Q,W) be its Jacobian algebra. The moduli space M_θ(A) of θ‑semistable representations of dimension vector d is a projective variety when θ is generic; for non‑generic θ it is an Artin stack. The derived category of coherent sheaves on the noncommutative moduli stack is equivalent to the derived category of modules over the multiplicative preprojective algebra Π^λ(Q) associated to a stability parameter λ. Bridgeland stability conditions on this category are obtained by first fixing a King stability parameter θ on the category of finite‑dimensional A‑modules, which gives a heart 𝒜_θ = mod‑θ‑ss(A) of θ‑semistable representations. The central charge is defined on the numerical Grothendieck group K₀(A) by Z(E)=⟨θ,dim E⟩ + i·⟨ω,dim E⟩ where ω is a generic stability vector in the ample cone of the GIT quotient. The support property is verified using the Euler form χ(E,F)=∑_i (−1)^i dim Ext^i(E,F), which is symmetric on the numerical Grothendieck group and negative definite on ker Z because θ and ω are chosen to be linearly independent. Wall‑crossing as θ varies is governed by the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula for the motivic Hall algebra of the category of A‑modules; the walls are precisely the hyperplanes where ⟨θ,dim E⟩=0 for some subrepresentation E. The integration map from the motivic Hall algebra to the quantum torus sends the class of a semistable object to a quantum dilogarithm, and the product over all semistable objects in a given stability chamber reproduces the generating series of Donaldson–Thomas invariants for the quiver with potential. Autoequivalences of the derived category include the reflection functors at vertices of Q, which act on the space of stability conditions by piecewise linear transformations corresponding to mutations of the quiver with potential.

Stability Conditions and the Derived Category of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Landau–Ginzburg Models with Non‑Isolated Singularities

A noncommutative Landau–Ginzburg model is given by a pair (X,W) where X is a smooth noncommutative variety (e.g. a noncommutative toric stack) and W is a regular function on X with possibly non‑isolated critical locus. The category of matrix factorisations MF(X,W) is a triangulated category that plays the role of the derived category of singularities. Bridgeland stability conditions on MF(X,W) are constructed by first choosing a stability condition on the derived category of perfect complexes Perf(X) — for instance a geometric stability condition induced by an ample line bundle on the commutative resolution of X — and then restricting to the subcategory of matrix factorisations via the Orlov equivalence. The central charge on the numerical Grothendieck group of MF(X,W) is defined using the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg isomorphism for the periodic cyclic homology of the dg‑category of matrix factorisations; it takes the form Z(E)=∫_X e−(B+iω) chHKR(E) where chHKR is the HKR Chern character taking values in the negative cyclic homology. The heart is the category of graded matrix factorisations with respect to a chosen grading on W, and the support property follows from the non‑degeneracy of the residue pairing on the Jacobian ring of W, which provides a negative definite quadratic form on ker Z. When the critical locus of W is non‑isolated, the space of stability conditions acquires additional components corresponding to different choices of perverse heart relative to the singular locus; wall‑crossing between these components is described by a generalised Kontsevich–Soibelman formula involving the motivic Hall algebra of the category of singularities. In the case where X is a noncommutative crepant resolution of a Kleinian singularity ℂ³/G and W is a generic invariant potential, the stability conditions constructed this way coincide with those obtained by transport from the minimal resolution, and the wall‑crossing reproduces the cluster mutation rules for the associated cluster algebra.

Stability Conditions on Derived Categories of Coherent Sheaves on Noncommutative Crepant Resolutions of Compound Du Val Singularities

Compound Du Val (cDV) singularities are three‑dimensional hypersurface singularities whose generic hyperplane section is a Du Val surface singularity. A noncommutative crepant resolution (NCCR) of a cDV singularity is given by the endomorphism algebra A=End_R(M) of a reflexive R‑module M that is a tilting bundle on a crepant resolution. The derived category Dᵇ(mod‑A) is equivalent to Dᵇ(Coh(Y)) for any crepant resolution Y→Spec R. Bridgeland stability conditions on Dᵇ(mod‑A) are obtained by transporting a geometric stability condition from a chosen crepant resolution Y. The central charge on the numerical Grothendieck group K₀(A) is defined by Z(E)=−∫_Y e−(B+iω) ch(Φ(E)) where Φ is the Fourier–Mukai equivalence induced by the tilting bundle. The heart is the perverse heart 𝒜^p obtained by gluing the standard hearts on the exceptional divisors of Y according to the perverse t‑structure associated to the resolution morphism. The support property holds because the intersection form on the exceptional locus of Y is negative definite on the kernel of Z; this is equivalent to the non‑degeneracy of the Euler form on the numerical Grothendieck group of A. Wall‑crossing as the stability parameter ω moves in the ample cone of Y corresponds to flops of the crepant resolution, and the Kontsevich–Soibelman factorisation matches the wall‑crossing formula for the motivic Hall algebra of the category of finite‑dimensional A‑modules. The autoequivalence group contains the braid group action coming from spherical twists around the exceptional curves, and its action on Stab(Dᵇ(mod‑A)) realises the fundamental group of the complement of the discriminant locus in the space of stability conditions.

  • Confusing the wall‑crossing formula for the motivic Hall algebra of a quiver with potential with the cluster mutation formula; they coincide only after passing to the integration map and for generic stability parameters.
  • Treating the central charge on a noncommutative matrix factorisation category as the ordinary Chern character integral without the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg correction terms.
  • Believing that the space of stability conditions on a log Calabi–Yau variety is always connected; different choices of perverse heart can give distinct components.
  • Overlooking the role of the quantum volume form in defining the central charge for noncommutative perverse sheaves; the classical volume form does not exist in the noncommutative setting.
  • Assuming that the Kontsevich–Soibelman wall‑crossing formula applies verbatim to the motivic Hall algebra of a noncommutative space without the integration map to the quantum torus.

For a finite subgroup G ⊂ SL(d, ℂ) the quotient singularity ℂd/G admits a crepant resolution given by the G-Hilbert scheme G-Hilb(ℂd). The derived McKay correspondence provides an equivalence Ψ : Db(Coh G-Hilb(ℂd)) → Db(CohG(ℂd)). The NCCR is the skew group algebra A = ℂ[ℂd] ⋊ G. Worked Example: Verifying the Support Property for a Transported Stability Condition
Let R be a three‑dimensional Gorenstein terminal singularity with an NCCR A = EndR(M) and a crepant resolution Y → Spec R. Suppose σ = (Z, 𝒜) is a stability condition on Db(Coh Y) with central charge Z(F) = −∫Y e−(B+iω) ch(F) and heart 𝒜 = Coh Y. Explain how to verify the support property for the transported stability condition σ' = (Z', 𝒜') on Db(mod‑A) where Z' = Z ∘ Φ and 𝒜' = Φ−1(𝒜).
The support property for σ' requires a quadratic form Q on K0(A)num such that Q(v) ≥ 0 for all semistable classes v and Q is negative definite on ker Z'. Because Φ is an equivalence, K0(A)num ≅ K0(Y)num and Z' = Z ∘ Φ. On Y the support property for σ is known: the intersection form QY(v) = ∫Y ch2(v)·ωd−2 (or the appropriate Mukai pairing) is negative definite on ker Z. Transport QY via Φ to a form Q' on K0(A)num. Since Φ preserves the Euler pairing, Q' coincides with the Euler form on A up to a positive scalar. The exceptional locus of Y has negative definite intersection form, which implies Q' is negative definite on ker Z'. Hence the support property holds for σ'.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to check the support property for a transported stability condition, first identify the equivalence (Fourier–Mukai or derived McKay) and the central charge on the geometric side. Then recall the standard quadratic form (intersection pairing or Mukai form) that works there. Transport that form via the equivalence; the preservation of the Euler pairing guarantees the required negative definiteness on the kernel of the transported central charge. Write the steps in order: equivalence → central charge transport → quadratic form transport → negative definiteness on kernel.

For a Gorenstein Calabi–Yau singularity R of dimension d ≥ 4, an NCCR A = EndR(M) exists under mild hypotheses (e.g., R is a complete intersection). Let Ō be the nilpotent orbit closure of type A2 in 𝔰𝔩3. Its Springer resolution is T*ℙ2 → Ō. The NCCR is A = EndŌ(M) where M is the tilting bundle O ⊕ O(1) ⊕ O(2).
